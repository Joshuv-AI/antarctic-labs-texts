Rethinking Pulmonary Embolism Segmentation: A Study of Current Approaches and Challenges with an Open Weight Model
Yixin Zhang, Ryan Chamberlain, Lawrence Ngo, Kevin Kramer, Maciej A. Mazurowski

Abstract
Pulmonary embolism (PE) is a life-threatening condition where accurate and timely detection is essential. Automated segmentation of emboli in CT pulmonary angiography (CTPA) scans can greatly aid in clinical workflows; however, current research is hindered by limitations such as small, inconsistent datasets, lack of reproducible baselines, and limited comparative evaluation of the models. In this study, we curated a densely annotated in-house dataset comprising 490 CTPA scans. Using this dataset, we systematically evaluated nine widely used segmentation architectures from both the CNN and Vision Transformer (ViT) families, initialized with either pretrained or random weights, under a unified testing framework as a performance audit. Our study leads to several important observations: (1) 3D U-Net with a ResNet encoder remains a highly effective architecture for PE segmentation; (2) 3D models are particularly well-suited to this task given the morphological characteristics of emboli; (3) CNN-based models generally yield superior performance compared to their ViT-based counterparts in PE segmentation; (4) classification-based pretraining, even on large PE datasets, can adversely impact segmentation performance compared to training from scratch, suggesting that PE classification and segmentation may rely on different sets of discriminative features; (5) different model architectures show a highly consistent pattern of segmentation performance when trained on the same data; and (6) while central and large emboli can be segmented with satisfactory accuracy, distal emboli remain challenging due to both task complexity and the scarcity of high-quality datasets. Besides these findings, our best-performing model achieves a mean Dice score of 0.7131 for segmentation. It detects 181 emboli with 49 false positives and 28 false negatives from 60 in-house testing scans. Its generalizability is further validated on public datasets. The model architectures and weights can be accessed at: https://github.com/mazurowski-lab/PulmonaryEmbolismSegmentation



1
Introduction

Pulmonary embolism (PE) is a life-threatening vascular condition with substantial morbidity and mortality, causing an estimated 30,000 or more deaths annually in the United States alone
[
26
,
28
]
.
A serious long-term complication is chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension (CTEPH)
[
1
,
20
,
22
]
, a progressive and often under-diagnosed form of pulmonary hypertension resulting from unresolved emboli. Early and accurate identification of emboli is critical for improving patient outcomes, enabling timely treatment, risk stratification, and monitoring.

Computed tomography pulmonary angiography (CTPA) is the clinical gold standard for diagnosing pulmonary embolism. While classification and detection methods have shown promising progress, voxel-level segmentation provides more detailed information by precisely identifying both the location and morphology of emboli.
This enables the quantification of clot burden, which can support radiologists in more efficiently assessing disease severity and making better-informed treatment decisions.
However, despite advancements in PE classification, detection, and general medical segmentation, progress in PE segmentation specifically has lagged behind.
Three persistent challenges limit advancement in this area:

1.

Limited Public Data:
As of 2025, only three public datasets for PE segmentation exist: READ (40 scans)
[
9
]
, CAD-PE (91 scans)
[
12
]
, and FUMPE (35 scans)
[
19
]
. However, inconsistent annotation standards (e.g., variable treatment of sub-segmental emboli or inconsistent labeling quality) make it difficult to use these datasets for training or cross-study comparison.

2.

Evaluation Pitfalls:
Many prior works report metrics such as slice-level ROC–AUC, which are of limited clinical relevance. Others suffer from methodological flaws like data leakage (e.g., including the same patient’s scans in both training and test sets) or even report performance on training data, inflating results and impeding fair comparison.

3.

Lack of Reproducibility:
Despite increasing interest in PE analysis, reproducibility remains a concern. Few works publicly released their code, and none have made model weights available.

These issues make it challenging to assess the current state of the art and to build upon prior work in a rigorous and reproducible manner.
To address these gaps, we present a systematic study of PE segmentation using deep learning. Our contributions are:

1.

Open-Source Baselines:
We develop and release the first open-weight PE segmentation models trained on a large, high-quality in-house dataset of 490 manually annotated CTPA scans (430 for training, 60 for testing), with each scan from a unique patient.

2.

Rigorous Benchmarking:
We implement and evaluate nine segmentation architectures, including U-Net variants, transformer-based models, and hybrid designs, under a consistent training and evaluation pipeline. Our evaluation employs clinically meaningful metrics such as Dice Similarity Coefficient (DSC) and embolus-level false positive/negative counts, using a strictly patient-disjoint train-test split to avoid data leakage. We also assess generalization performance on three public datasets with differing annotation standards to evaluate robustness under real-world variability.

3.

Comprehensive Empirical Analysis:
We conduct a detailed error analysis to identify key failure modes and inform future research directions. CNN-based models consistently outperform transformer-based architectures across all metrics.Additionally, patient-level error patterns are highly consistent across architectures, suggesting that the ability of a dataset to capture rare and challenging cases remains a key bottleneck in PE segmentation. Aside from the distinction between 2D and 3D architectures, model design appears to be a less significant factor in overall performance on this task. We further analyze embolus-level failure cases in both our in-house and public datasets, highlighting limitations in current public data and model generalizability.

2
Related Work

Research on automated PE analysis was developed along three main lines: classification, weakly supervised learning, and voxel-level segmentation. A summary of representative studies, including datasets, metrics, and our remarks, is provided in Table
1
.

Classification-based PE Analysis:

Most existing literature focuses on patient- or slice-level classification of CTPA scans. Huhtanen et al.
[
14
]
showed that recurrent models trained on weak labels could achieve exam-level accuracies exceeding 0.90. Islam et al.
[
16
]
extended this line of work using CNNs and Transformers trained on large-scale datasets, demonstrating the value of transfer learning and vessel-centric representations for reducing false positives. While these methods show strong performance, they often require additional human input for downstream clinical tasks such as clot burden estimation.

Fully Supervised Segmentation:

Fully supervised voxel-level segmentation has become a central focus in PE analysis. Cano-Espinosa et al.
[
3
]
proposed a multi-slice, multi-axial model, achieving per-embolus sensitivity of 0.68 at approximately one false positive per scan, evaluated on 20 cases from a public dataset. Cheng et al.
[
6
]
introduced a feature-enhanced, adversarial semi-supervised network based on HRNet, demonstrating performance gains with unlabeled data. Djahnine et al.
[
10
]
presented a 3D pipeline that jointly performed clot segmentation and Qanadli score estimation, reporting
R^{2}=0.72
for severity prediction. Liu et al.
[
18
]
proposed CAM-WNet with coordinate attention and pyramid pooling, while Tang et al.
[
27
]
incorporated CBAM attention; both reported high Dice scores on small in-house datasets (
n\leq 25
) but suffered from test-train leakage due to overlapping patients. Liu et al.
[
17
]
reported high AUC scores on several hundred cases but did not include Dice metrics, limiting assessment of segmentation quality. Doğan et al.
[
11
]
applied Mask R-CNN and achieved a Dice of 0.94, but restricted evaluation to large emboli near the pulmonary trunk.

Weakly Supervised Segmentation:

Several weakly supervised strategies have also been proposed to reduce PE annotation costs. Pu et al.
[
23
]
trained a CNN on high-confidence embolus candidates derived from vascular segmentation. Yang et al.
[
31
]
used graph-cut pre-segmentation to produce pseudo-labels, while Condrea et al.
[
8
]
applied explainability maps to iteratively refine labels from classification outputs. Although these methods are creative, their performance tends to degrade when encountering difficult cases such as distal emboli or anatomically ambiguous regions, which limits their clinical utility in the long run.

Given persistent issues with dataset quality, evaluation protocols, and reproducibility in prior work, our method emphasizes rigorous benchmarking and weight-sharing, patient-disjoint train-test splits, evaluation with clinically meaningful metrics, and detailed post-hoc analysis of model failure modes.

3
Method

3.1
In-House Dataset for PE Segmentation

Our study utilized 490 de-identified CTPAs (one per patient) with confirmed PE. Each case included voxel-level embolus annotations produced by board-certified radiologists, who identified thrombus regions based on filling defects within the pulmonary arteries.
The scans were collected from multiple institutions and encompassed a variety of scanner models, contrast agents, and clinical protocols. Patient age was available in the de-identified metadata (mean
61.3\pm 16.1
years).
All CTPAs were converted from DICOM to NIfTI format with RAS orientation, and a research assistant visually verified alignment between images and annotations.
The dataset was split into 430 scans for training/validation and 60 scans for a held-out test set. Due to the data usage agreement with the data providers, this dataset will not be released publicly; however, the weights of models trained on this dataset will be released for educational and research purposes.

3.1.1
Public Datasets for Detection Validation

To complement evaluation on our internal test set, we performed external validation using three publicly available pulmonary embolism (PE) datasets: FUMPE, READ, and CAD-PE. To the best of our knowledge, these represent all currently available public resources for PE segmentation.

•

FUMPE
: 35 CT pulmonary angiography scans with embolus annotations. The annotation masks are relatively coarse, often covering larger vessel regions and including limited cases of sub-segmental emboli.

•

READ
: 40 CT scans with embolus annotations. Annotations in this dataset tend to be more sensitive, with dark arterial regions frequently marked as emboli.

•

CAD-PE
: 91 CT scans. Unlike the other datasets, CAD-PE labels vessels containing segmental emboli rather than the emboli themselves, which can result in broader annotation masks. It also occasionally marks non-PE dark arterial regions as emboli.

Because these datasets differ substantially in annotation standards, we restricted our external evaluation to
detection performance only
, measured by the number of true positives (TP), false positives (FP), and false negatives (FN) across a range of detection thresholds.

3.2
Evaluation Metrics

3.2.1
Mean Dice Similarity Coefficient (mDSC)

Segmentation performance of all trained models was evaluated per CTPA using the Dice Similarity Coefficient:

DSC=\dfrac{2TP}{2TP+FP+FN}

where
TP
,
FP
, and
FN
are voxel counts. The final metric was the mean DSC across all test CTPAs.

3.2.2
Embolus-level detection analysis

In addition to computing voxel-level DSC, we also performed embolus-level detection analysis.
Many prior studies define a successful detection using only a one-pixel overlap between prediction and ground truth. We consider this criterion uninformative, as it can exaggerate detection performance by crediting trivial overlaps.
Instead, we adopt a stricter, volume-aware criterion. Specifically, we first apply morphological operations to identify individual emboli in both the human annotations and model predictions. Then, two stages of screening under a detection threshold
X\%
are applied to characterize the FPs and FNs.

In the first stage, each predicted embolus is checked for its overlapping with emboli from the annotation. If less than
X\%
of its volume overlaps with true emboli, the prediction is marked as false positive (FP). In contrast, predictions meeting the detection criteria are considered true positives (TPs). In the second stage, each annotated embolus is verified. If less than
X\%
of its volume is covered by TPs it will be considered a missed embolus (FN).
This approach deprecates both incidental ”touches” and excessive over-segmentations, providing a more robust evaluation.

3.3
Data Preprocessing and Augmentation

For 2D models, each CTPA volume is sliced along the axial plane without spatial resampling. For 3D models, all CTPAs were resampled to a consistent voxel spacing of (0.7373mm, 0.7373mm, 1.0mm) per voxel. The image intensity was clipped to between -195 HU and 310 HU (Hounsfield Units), centered, and normalized following nnU-Net conventions. During training, the data augmentation included affine scaling (0.8–1.25), random rotations (
\pm 20^{\circ}
and
90^{\circ}
), cropping (
384\times 384
), and horizontal/vertical flips.

3.4
Model Implementation and Training

We compared a range of 2D and 3D architectures from both the convolutional neural network (CNN) and vision transformer (ViT) families, with their performance summarized in Table
4
. The model selection was designed to cover both CNN- and ViT-based approaches across 2D and 3D settings, including generic architectures as well as those specifically tailored for PE segmentation.
For hyperparameters and architectural details, we followed the configurations provided in the original publications or official repositories whenever available (e.g., Chen et al.
[
5
]
). When such references were not available, we re-implemented the models and trained them with reasonable adjustments to ensure stable convergence using AdamW (learning rate
8\times 10^{-4}
, weight decay
1\times 10^{-5}
) optimizer with a One-Cycle learning rate scheduler using 10% of the training epochs for linear warm-up. A combination of DiceLoss computed over the batch and CrossEntropyLoss was used as the loss function.

4
Experiments

4.1
Model Comparison under Unified Framework

We benchmarked 9 distinct segmentation architectures covering both CNN- and ViT-based segmentation networks, covering both 2D and 3D settings, under a unified training and evaluation framework.
This unified setup not only enables a fair assessment of architectural differences, but also establishes a reproducible baseline for future PE segmentation research.

4.2
Effects of Using Classification as Pretraining

To investigate whether large-scale classification tasks can benefit voxel-level PE segmentation, we explored a pre-training strategy using the RSNA Pulmonary Embolism dataset (RSNA-PE)
[
7
]
, which contains 7,929 CTPA volumes with slice-level PE labels. We trained classification models using these labels, then transferred their weights to initialize segmentation models.

The classification models shared the same encoder and decoder structure as the segmentation networks. The only architectural change was at the output: voxel-wise logits from the output layer were aggregated using global max-pooling to generate slice-level predictions. This design allowed us to reuse the learned weights for segmentation network weight initialization without structural modifications.

After training the classification models to convergence, we initialized the corresponding segmentation networks with the pre-trained weights and trained them on subsets of our in-house PE segmentation dataset with different sizes. We also trained identical segmentation architectures from random initialization as the control group.

5
Results

5.1
Model Performance under Unified Framework

Table
4
reports the mean Dice Similarity Coefficient (mDSC) and standard deviation across the test set for all models evaluated under our unified framework. Several key observations emerge:

First
, 3D models consistently outperformed their 2D counterparts. This trend holds for both CNN- and ViT-based architectures, with an average improvement of approximately 0.05 in mDSC. This performance gain is expected, as emboli often span multiple slices and may be difficult to identify if they are thin and positioned perpendicularly to the axial plane.

Second
, CNN-based models outperformed transformer-based models in both 2D and 3D settings. Among 2D models, Inception-UNet and CAM-WNet surpassed SegFormer-B5 and SCUNet++, while in 3D, nnUNet3D achieved substantially higher mDSC than both MedNeXt and Swin-UNETR. These results suggest that, under the current dataset scale and characteristics, convolutional architectures remain more effective than transformers for voxel-level PE segmentation—likely due to their stronger local feature modeling and better inductive biases for medical imaging.

Third
, training strategies such as batch-level loss aggregation and warm-up schedules can have a significant impact on performance. For example, the original SCUNet++ yielded an mDSC of only 0.3963 (potentially due to the FUMPE dataset’s omission of smaller emboli), whereas our modified version improved the mDSC to 0.5726. This highlights that some performance differences reported in prior literature may stem more from improved optimization or tuning strategies than from architectural innovations alone.

Finally
, models within the same spatial dimensionality and architectural family tended to perform similarly. For instance, 2D models such as nnUNet2D, CAM-WNet, ResAttUNet, and Inception-UNet produced tightly clustered mDSC scores, as did SCUNet++ (modified) and SegFormer-B5, and 3D MedNeXt and Swin-UNETR.

6
Discussion

Our study provides a comprehensive evaluation of pulmonary embolism segmentation models across architectures, training paradigms, and datasets, and introduces the first set of open-weight baselines for out-of-box usage. Several patterns emerge from this analysis, offering both practical takeaways and guidance for future research.

3D models consistently outperform 2D counterparts, likely due to the volumetric nature of PE.
Many emboli are small and span multiple slices, and their visibility on a single axial plane is often limited. Access to inter-slice context therefore improves segmentation accuracy.
In addition, CNN-based models outperformed ViT-based architectures across both 2D and 3D configurations. This performance gap can be attributed to the task’s emphasis on fine-grained, localized features. CNN-based models, with their strong inductive biases and lower data requirements
[
32
]
, appear better suited for this task than ViT-based counterparts.
Beyond the 2D versus 3D and CNN-based versus ViT-based comparisons, models within the same architectural family often performed similarly. For example, all 2D CNN-based models yielded tightly clustered Dice scores, as did the 3D ViT-based variants. This suggests that under standardized training and evaluation framework, efforts of architecture-level changes may lead to much less returns as compared to improving data quality and representativeness.

Across all architectures, patient-level segmentation performance was highly correlated. This indicates that dataset difficulty and representativeness, rather than model capacity or initialization, are the dominant factors influencing prediction quality.
Such a finding can also be translated to a practical implication: lightweight models with sufficient capacity may be used as a proxy to identify difficult or underrepresented cases, while running at much lower computation cost than more complicated models.
Since they tend to share the same success and failure patterns, focusing on collecting difficult or under-represented data for the simpler model when expanding the dataset may be more effective than increasing sample size indiscriminately.

Failure analysis revealed that both false positives and false negatives were concentrated among the smallest emboli across all models and datasets. There is also a non-technical foundation for this problem. Zhou et al.
[
33
]
showed that even experienced radiologists struggle to detect sub-segmental PEs and are prone to FP in their annotating.
Such challenges are then reflected in the variation in annotation strategies: FUMPE excludes sub-segmental emboli to prioritize precision, whereas READ and CAD-PE adopt broader labeling to favor sensitivity.
Our in-house dataset aims to provide a radiologically grounded and internally consistent balance, but like all medical datasets, its annotations remain subject to limitations in image quality, inter-observer variability, and clinical interpretation. We hence acknowledge the potential presence of annotation errors as a limitation of our study.

Pretraining segmentation models on the RSNA-PE classification task did not lead to performance gains. In fact, randomly initialized models slightly outperformed those with pretraining.
One possible explanation is that classification tasks encourage the learning of holistic image-level features that are not well aligned with the fine-grained, voxel-level representations required for segmentation. Given the localized nature of PE, more targeted pretext tasks may be necessary to better leverage large-scale resources such as RSNA-PE.

These findings point to several directions for future progress.
First, there is a need for larger and clinically reliable datasets with validated annotations for small or distal PEs.
Second, method-focused studies should adopt evaluation protocols that are data-leakage-free and reflect clinical usability.
Third, reproducibility must be prioritized. Releasing code, pretrained weights, and standardized benchmarks is essential for fair comparison and sustained advancement for future studies.

7
Conclusion

This study summarizes the current gaps hindering PE segmentation studies and presents the first open-weight baselines for pulmonary embolism segmentation.
The model is trained on a large, multi-institutional dataset with expert voxel-level annotations.
By releasing both code and pretrained models, we address the reproducibility gap that has hindered progress in this domain and provide a practical foundation for future research.
