Bio-Polymers Based Functionalized Hydrogel Carriers for Targeted Oncology Therapies: Current Progress and Future Perspectives
Authors: Shengchun Qiu, Xiaoyan Wen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChallenges in cancer therapy remain in terms of lack of tumor selectivity, unwanted side effects, and inefficacy of current treatment strategies. Over the past few years, functionalized bio-polymer gels have become attractive alternatives for cancer therapy, as they are biocompatible and can be tailored to provide local drug delivery. Here, we critically assess recent advances in the design and use of these hydrogels, including polymer choice, cross-linking strategies, ligand-based tumor targeting and responsiveness to the tumor microenvironment. Multifunctional elements enable temporal and spatial regulation of drug release for a broad range of therapeutic approaches, including chemotherapy, immunotherapy, gene therapy, and phototherapy. Furthermore, the application of injectable, implantable, and 3D-bioprinted hydrogels for clinical benefits is also explored. While significant progress has been made, issues of reproducibility, scalability and clinical translation still need to be addressed. Future strategies such as artificial-assisted design, patient-specific drug delivery systems and multi-responsive hydrogels will likely address these challenges. In summary, functionalized hydrogels based on bio-polymers are a promising and disruptive approach for next-generation precision oncology, with a great potential for future clinical applications.

Introduction

Cancer is one of the major and increasing public health issues globally. According to the latest GLOBOCAN 2024 estimates, 20.6 million new cancer cases and 9.8 million cancer-related deaths took place in the world in 2024; this number may hit around 34.4 million and 17.5 million by 2050, respectively.1 Cancer progression occurs when a series of molecular and microenvironmental processes are involved and not just one. These are: sustained proliferative signalling, loss of growth suppressors, resistance to apoptosis, genomic instability, epigenetic reprogramming, metabolic adaptation, angiogenesis, invasion and metastasis, immune evasion and phenotypic plasticity.2,3 The tumour microenvironment also plays a role in driving the disease, characterised by hypoxia, extracellular-matrix remodelling, inflammatory cytokines, cancer-associated fibroblasts, tumour-associated macrophages and abnormal vasculature, and cancer stem cells are associated with recurrence and treatment resistance. These mechanisms result in acidic, hypoxic, enzyme rich, redox altered and immunosuppressive conditions that can be utilized as therapeutic targets.4,5 While chemotherapy remains a staple treatment for cancer, systemic drug administration is associated with low selectivity, dose-limiting side effects, short drug half-life and multi-drug resistance. This reduces efficacy and leads to poor quality of life.6–8 So, there has been a growing focus on developing targeted or local drug delivery systems to enhance accumulation within tumors while reducing systemic toxicity.

Nanotechnology-based drug delivery systems were originally developed to exploit the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect to passively target tumors. But there is increasing clinical and preclinical evidence that the EPR effect is highly variable and often inadequate for drug delivery to human tumors.9,10 Recent findings indicate that the transport of nanoparticles into solid tumors is more likely to be driven by active, trans-endothelial transport than leakage, which has long been assumed to be the case.11 This evidence suggests that other biomaterial-based delivery systems are needed to allow local, sustained and responsive drug delivery to tumors, regardless of vascular permeability.

In particular, hydrogels have attracted interest in this field due to their crosslinked, three-dimensional structure, high water content, and tunable properties, which are similar to the extracellular matrix (ECM).12–15 These properties enable the encapsulation of a variety of therapeutic drugs, such as small molecules, proteins, nucleic acids and nanoparticles, and their sustained release at the tumor site. Furthermore, injectable and in situ gelling hydrogels enable minimally invasive administration and conform to the shape of the tumor, making them particularly suitable for postsurgical treatments and intratumoral administration. These local systems increase drug retention in the tumor microenvironment (TME) and limit systemic toxicity and side effects.16

Biopolymer-based hydrogels have gained increasing attention relative to synthetic polymer-based hydrogels because of their inherent biocompatibility, biodegradability and bio-activity. Biopolymers such as chitosan, alginate, hyaluronic acid, gelatin, collagen and silk fibroin have similar structural elements to the ECM and can interact with cells.17,18 For instance, hyaluronic acid can interact with CD44 receptors expressed in many cancers for targeted drug delivery, and chitosan is pH-sensitive and can release drug in the acidic TME. Moreover, the degradation products of these bio-polymers are typically non-toxic, and can be metabolized via conventional processes in the body, which is advantageous for therapeutic use.

There are extensive reviews on the design and applications of hydrogels for drug delivery, but the majority of them are limited to the use of synthetic polymers, general biomedical applications, or non-targeted drug delivery. There remains a need for detailed examination of the connection between bio-polymer choice, functionalization strategies, responsiveness to tumor microenvironment, and therapy-specific performance for targeted cancer treatment. Moreover, key translational aspects such as structure property performance, therapy-specific design principles and manufacturing are often overlooked. This hampers the design of next-generation hydrogels for targeted cancer therapies.19

Here, we provide a comprehensive and critical analysis of bio-polymer-derived functionalized hydrogel carriers for targeted cancer therapy focusing on the interplay between structure and biological function. The synergistic evaluation of functionalised biopolymer hydrogels, material chemistry, targeting mechanism, therapeutic modality, and clinical translation is the novelty of this review. We provide a critical overview of the design principles of hydrogel systems, the comparison of main biopolymers, and advanced functionalization strategies such as ligand targeting, multi-stage delivery and stimulus-responsive release. We highlight responsive hydrogel systems that interact with the tumor microenvironment, injectable and implantable hydrogels, and combination therapies, such as chemo-immunotherapy and phototherapy. We also discuss quantitative performance metrics, design principles and tumor models for assessing therapeutic efficacy to bridge bench-to-bedside translation. The review also emphasizes the recent platforms like DNA hydrogels, MXene-responsive networks, cellulose ionogels, prodrug nanoassemblies, liquid-crystal elastomers, and 3D/4D-printed constructs. Comparative analysis of different therapeutic techniques, including chemotherapy, immunotherapy, gene therapy, and photothermal therapy is performed. Finally, we also discuss current challenges in reproducibility, scalability, regulatory approval and batch-to-batch variability, and emerging trends in design, personalization and theranostics, including the use of artificial intelligence.

Review Methodology

A structured approach to literature-review was followed to recognize the studies dealing with functionalised biopolymer based hydrogel carriers for targeted cancer therapy. The search terms on biopolymers, hydrogels, tumour targeting, stimuli-responsive delivery, chemotherapy, immunotherapy, gene therapy, photothermal therapy, and photodynamic therapy were combined to search the relevant databases. Only those studies that met the selection criteria were included: relevance, material, treatment and availability of experimental results. The information on polymer structure, crosslinking strategy, functionalisation, payload, cancer indication, model type, loading efficiency, release kinetics, therapeutic efficacy, toxicity and translational limitations were extracted. All quantitative results were only considered paired if the experimental conditions were deemed sufficiently close and all other evidence was critically evaluated and ranked according to its mechanistic relevance, reproducibility, biosafety, scalability and clinical readiness.

Design Principles of Bio-Polymeric Hydrogels

Hydrogel carriers made from bio-polymers are highly dependent on the network architecture, crosslinking strategy and the relationship between the physicochemical properties of the network and the biological system. Manipulation of these parameters allows us to design and control drug loading, release, retention in tumors, and degradation, which are key to successful local cancer therapy.

Network Architecture and Crosslinking Strategies

Hydrogels are three-dimensional networks of polymers that are crosslinked to form a mesh, which provides mechanical properties and responsiveness to the surrounding environment. Physical crosslinks are formed through non-covalent interactions, including hydrogen bonding, electrostatic interactions, hydrophobic interactions and chain entanglement. Such interactions allow reversible gelation and injectability without the need for potentially toxic crosslinkers.20,21 These are particularly ideal for minimally invasive intratumoral injections because of their shear thinning and self-healing properties.

Chemically crosslinked hydrogels, on the other hand, involve covalent interactions and offer superior mechanical properties and stability. Crosslinking strategies such as Schiff base, Michael addition, click chemistry and photo-crosslinking are often used to form bio-polymer networks with tunable degradation and drug release kinetics.22,23 Crosslinking chemistry plays a critical role in determining the rate of gel formation, gel uniformity and stability in vivo.24

Enzymatic crosslinking has become a biocompatible and bio-orthogonal method of gel formation in the body. Enzymatic reactions, such as horseradish peroxidase and transglutaminase, enable fine-tuning of the gelation process while maintaining the activity of encapsulated bioactive molecules and cells.25 Likewise, bio-orthogonal reactions like strain-promoted azide-alkyne cycloaddition and thiol-ene chemistry allow for rapid gelation in biological environments without interfering with native biochemical pathways, and are therefore well suited to injectable cancer therapies.26 The crosslinking approach used to form a hydrogel network is critical to network stability and drug delivery. Crosslinking Approaches for Bio-Polymeric Hydrogels and Its Influence on Physical Properties and Efficacy in Cancer Therapy

Structure Property Performance Relationship

A key consideration for advanced hydrogel design for cancer treatment is to develop structure-performance correlations. The degree of crosslinking affects the mesh size of the hydrogel, which in turn affects drug diffusion, release and penetration of biomolecules. Highly crosslinked hydrogels with smaller pores have low drug diffusion and release, and potentially longer retention times at the tumor site, while loosely crosslinked hydrogels have higher diffusion and degradation.26

The molecular weight and polymer concentration also influence degradation and mechanical properties. Higher molecular weight biopolymers exhibit higher crosslinking density and slower degradation, and are suitable for sustained release of drugs. By contrast, low molecular weight biopolymers form weaker networks that degrade rapidly and are suitable for short-term drug delivery.37,38 These trends are helpful in designing hydrogels for a particular cancer and drug delivery approach.

Physicochemical Properties Governing Therapeutic Outcome

Hydrogels that swell play an important role in drug delivery. Fully swollen hydrogels offer more rapid drug diffusion, whereas moderately swollen hydrogels allow for slower and sustained release.20 Hydrogel strength is also crucial, as it allows the hydrogel to maintain its shape after implantation, and resist physiological forces, especially when used after surgery. The degradation rate should be tailored to the drug’s treatment schedule. Biopolymers degrade via hydrolysis or enzymatic action, and degradation times can be controlled by changing the crosslinking density, polymer composition or including cleavable linkers.39 Controlled degradation allows full drug release without drug trapped in the system or premature drug release.

Biological Performance Requirements

Beyond physical and chemical considerations, hydrogel systems need to overcome biological issues in the tumor environment. Tumor penetration requires consideration of porosity, degradation and responsiveness to conditions such as low pH and high reactive oxygen species.40 Cellular uptake of the drug can be facilitated by the inclusion of targeting ligands or other active ingredients. Biocompatibility is also essential as implanted materials can induce an immune response, making the treatment less effective. Biopolymers such as hyaluronic acid and gelatin can act to modulate immune responses, such as macrophage activity, to enhance treatment.41

Design Criteria for Injectable vs Implantable Oncology Hydrogels

Injectable hydrogels should rapidly transform from sol to gel upon injection, be shear-thinning and safe to gel under physiological conditions. Thermally, pH, or ionically responsive systems are often used for intratumoral administration as they can gel to fit the shape of the tumor.35 The selection of injectable vs implantable systems is often based on tumor site, surgical considerations and treatment time. Tailoring these design criteria allows for the creation of hydrogels tailored to specific cancer treatments.

Biopolymers Used in Functional Hydrogel Systems

Biopolymers have been identified as valuable hydrogels for drug delivery due to their inherent biocompatibility, biodegradability and ECM-like structure. Biomaterials derived from natural sources possess functional groups that can be modified and used for crosslinking, as well as for drug attachment or the addition of targeting ligands.12 These properties facilitate the creation of hydrogels with multiple functions, including controlled drug release, environmental responsiveness, and enhanced selectivity. Biopolymers used in cancer-related research include polysaccharide-based materials such as chitosan, alginate and hyaluronic acid, as well as protein-based polymers such as gelatin and silk fibroin. These materials have different physicochemical properties that influence the formation, degradation, mechanical properties, and biological interactions of the resulting hydrogels. As such, their unique features and characteristics need to be understood in order to design multifunctional hydrogel systems for delivery of targeted cancer therapy.20

Polysaccharide-Based Systems

Hydrogels derived from polysaccharides are probably the most investigated biomaterials for drug delivery. Their natural abundance, diverse structure and functional groups make them good candidates for designing new delivery systems.

Chitosan

Chitosan is a positively charged polysaccharide derived from chitin, and one of the most valuable biopolymers for biomedical applications. With amine and hydroxyl groups in its structure, it can be modified and crosslinked to create injectable and responsive hydrogels.42 Chitosan is positively charged in acidic conditions, allowing it to bind to negatively charged cell membranes and improve cellular targeting and delivery.43 These characteristics have made it a popular choice for delivering drugs via hydrogels for local chemotherapy, gene therapy and immunotherapy. Studies have demonstrated that chitosan-based hydrogels can be responsive to the tumor environment, which is typically acidic and has elevated levels of specific enzymes, allowing for controlled release of the drug at the site of the tumor and reducing side effects. Apart from its targeting properties, chitosan is biodegradable and nonimmunogenic, making it an attractive candidate for clinical applications. Recent studies also suggest that chitosan-based systems can enhance drug pharmacokinetics and efficacy by increasing drug loading and providing sustained release.44

Alginate

Alginate is an anionic copolymer consisting of M-blocks, G-blocks, and alternating blocks of β-D-mannuronic acid and α-L-guluronic acid. Carboxylate groups form chelating bonds with Ca2+ and other divalent ions to form egg-box junctions. The block composition regulates the gel stiffness, porosity, swelling and diffusion and degradation and/or functionalisation allows for degradation, conjugation and stimulus controlled drug release profiles.45,46 Alginate hydrogels are highly hydrated and resemble soft tissues, making them an excellent carrier for therapeutic agents such as antineoplastic drugs, proteins and nanoparticles. Furthermore, alginate systems can be modified to provide controlled drug release through changes in crosslinking and/or blending with other polymers. As such, they are promising materials for sustained drug delivery within tumors, as well as for treatment of post-surgical cavities.47 However, alginate hydrogels are generally weak and lack cell binding ability.

Hyaluronic Acid

Hyaluronic acid (HA) is a naturally found linear glycosaminoglycan composed of D-glucuronic acid (GlcUA) and N-acetyl-D-glucosamine (GlcNAc) disaccharides. HA-based hydrogels have attracted much interest in cancer research. Carboxyl and hydroxyl groups are sites for chemical modification and hydrogel crosslinking. The tumour-cell recognition is achieved by its binding to CD44 and RHAMM, while hyaluronidase-mediated cleavage triggers selective degradation of the network, disassembly of the complex, and intracellular or in situ release of the loaded therapeutics in the tumour.48 HA hydrogels have been recently developed as injectable materials that are responsive to the tumor microenvironment, including pH and enzymes, for controlled drug delivery. For instance, HA-based injectable hydrogels show high biocompatibility and responsiveness to several stimuli and can deliver anti-cancer drugs to the site of tumors.18 HA’s interaction with tumor-related receptors and its role in regulating signaling pathways also indicate its potential multifunctional role in cancer treatment.

Protein-Based Systems

And, in addition to polysaccharides, protein-based biomaterials including gelatin and silk fibroin are also promising candidates for biomaterial development due to their natural bioactivity and structure.

Gelatin

Gelatin is a denatured polypeptide derived from collagen which contains glycine-rich sequences, accessible amino and carboxyl groups, integrin-binding RGD motifs and matrix-metalloproteinase-sensitive sites. It can be made into thermoreversible helices or into covalently crosslinked networks. These features can promote cell adhesion, enzyme responsive degradation, electrostatic drug binding, sustained release and functionalisation with targeting ligands or therapeutic nanomaterials in tumours.49,50 Gelatin hydrogels are commonly used in biomedical applications due to their good biocompatibility and versatility. They have also been widely investigated for drug delivery for cancer treatment as a carrier for numerous types of drugs.51

Silk Fibroin

Silk fibroin, a protein found in silkworm cocoons, is also being used as a hydrogel due to its excellent mechanical strength and ability to degrade. Physically crosslinked hydrogels of silicon fibroin can be formed by β-sheet conversion with tunable strength, porosity and degradation. The hydrophobic and hydrogen-bonding interactions entrap drugs, and the chemical modification allows local responsive release and combination therapy.52 Silk fibroin hydrogels have been demonstrated to be an effective drug delivery vehicle for sustained drug release and may also be used to support tissue regeneration while delivering an anti-cancer agent.53 What’s more, silk has low inflammatory response and good biocompatibility, which makes it ideal for long-term implantable applications.

Peptide

Over the past few years, biomaterials engineering has allowed for the development of new peptide- and nucleic acid-based systems. Likewise, DNA-based hydrogels are attractive options because of their programmable and predictable base pairing interactions. This enables tailored structure and drug delivery of the hydrogel. While peptide- and nucleic acid-based hydrogels are still in their infancy, they have tunable structures and a capacity to target, making them potential materials of the future.54

Functionalization Strategies for Tumor Targeting

Hydrogel-based drug carriers have been functionalized as a strategy to enhance the efficacy of local drug delivery systems in cancer treatment. While traditional hydrogels have benefits such as high drug loading and retention, their inert nature can hinder effective targeting of tumors and uptake by cells. Through functionalization, hydrogels can acquire new functions such as molecular targeting, responsiveness to environmental stimuli, and adaptability to the conditions within the TME.58 This facilitates better interactions with specific tumor tissues, enhances their penetration, and controls drug release. This allows functional hydrogels to offer enhanced spatial and temporal control of therapeutic efficacy.59

The latest developments have involved the design of hydrogels incorporating targeting via ligand-receptor interactions, multi-step adaptive behavior, and stimuli-responsive drug release. These versatile systems are increasingly being considered as important components of precision cancer therapies, in which therapies are directed at specific tumor characteristics, and combination therapies that combine multiple therapeutic approaches.60 Through specific targeting and controlled release, our goal is to improve the efficacy and efficiency of conventional cancer therapies.

Ligand-Mediated Active Targeting

Targeted drug delivery relies on decorating drug carriers with ligands that bind to receptors overexpressed on cancer cells. Ligand–receptor recognition facilitates receptor-mediated internalization, leading to increased internalization of therapeutic material into the cancer cells. This approach increases selectivity for the tumor and decreases off-target side effects when compared to passive targeting.61

For example, one of the most widely used targeting ligands is folic acid, which targets the folate receptors overexpressed in cancers, including ovarian, breast, and lung tumors. Folic acid-conjugated hydrogels have shown enhanced tumor targeting and efficacy as a result of binding and internalization. Another highly selective approach is antibody-based targeting.62 Hydrogels containing antibodies targeting receptors such as HER2, EGFR and PD-L1 have been used to deliver chemotherapeutic and immunotherapeutic drugs to the tumor site.

Aptamers are also promising alternatives to antibodies. Aptamers are small, highly specific and non-immunogenic, and can target tumor proteins while providing enhanced tissue penetration. Aptamer-functionalised hydrogels can target cancer cells specifically to deliver drugs.63 Also, tumor-targeting peptides, such as RGD peptides, bind to integrins expressed on tumor blood vessels and cancer cells. These peptides have been shown to increase tumor penetration and increase the delivery of drug-loaded hydrogels into tumors.64 However, ligand targeting is limited by the heterogeneity of tumors and the presence of different receptors. Tumor cells may not universally express the receptor of interest. Some recent strategies include using ligand targeting in combination with other adaptive mechanisms to enhance targeting efficiency and consistency. Folate receptor- and integrin-based ligands are still commonly used because of their high tumor expression levels. Although antibodies are highly specific, they can be costly to produce and may induce an immune response. Alternatively, smaller ligands such as peptides and aptamers can be used to avoid steric hindrance and increase penetration, and are considered ideal for delivery via hydrogels (Table 3). Multistage and Environment-Adaptive Targeting

One-stage targeting delivery systems can face several biological barriers, such as transport in the bloodstream, diffusion into the tumor tissue, and cell uptake. To overcome these barriers, multi-stage targeting systems have been designed to react to various conditions along the drug delivery process.73 One such approach is charge-reversible systems. Here, the hydrogel carriers start off neutral or negatively charged to prevent non-specific interactions in the bloodstream. They then become positively charged to improve their interaction with negatively charged cell membranes and increase uptake.74 There are also size-switching systems that involve the release of smaller particles or drug molecules from larger hydrogel carriers, allowing them to more easily diffuse into the tumor. This can overcome the barrier posed by the extracellular matrix.75

Ionic liquid-based cellulose ionogels are materials in which the ionic liquids are trapped in a network of hydrogen-bonded cellulose that act as a multifunctional matrix for multimodal oncology. Ionogels were re-constructed by molecular-network re-construction and displayed mechanical stability (tensile strength 3.5 MPa), electrochemical stability (electrochemical window 3.0 V) and ion transport (conductivity 14.3 mS cm−1) conducive to electrically monitored or stimulus-controlled implants.76 Cellulose scaffolds can co-localize chemotherapeutics with photoactive or catalytic nanomaterials and cellulose-nanocrystal hydrogels containing carbon dots and Fe3O4 enabled combined photothermal, photodynamic, and chemodynamic therapy under 660 nm irradiation, for cancer treatment. Controlled leaching, biodegradation and chronic-toxicity assessment is required for translation biocompatible ionic liquids.77

Stimuli-Responsive Systems

Smart systems are stimuli-responsive hydrogels that release drugs in response to particular triggers. They exploit the distinct biochemical characteristics of tumor tissues to deliver drugs in a selective and controlled manner.78 Internal stimuli such as pH, enzymes, redox conditions and reactive oxygen species are often changed in tumors.79 For instance, pH-sensitive hydrogels have acid-labile bonds which cleave at the slightly acidic pH of the tumor microenvironment, releasing the drug. Enzyme-sensitive hydrogels can break down when exposed to enzymes such as matrix metalloproteinases, which are typically present at high levels in tumors.80 Redox-responsive hydrogels react to the elevated levels of glutathione present in cancer cells, breaking down disulfide bonds and releasing drugs. Drug release can also be triggered by external stimuli such as light, heat, magnetic fields and ultrasound. These systems can provide on-demand drug delivery and are commonly used in photothermal therapy and ultrasound drug delivery.81 New advances have led to hydrogels with multiple stimuli-responsive capabilities, enabling sophisticated and versatile drug release in the dynamic tumor microenvironment.

Liquid crystal elastomers (LCEs) are stimuli-responsive networks, which combine liquid-crystal anisotropy with elastomeric elasticity and which can change shape reversibly in response to thermal, optical, electrical, magnetic or chemical stimuli. They can be dynamically actuated, are soft, and are mechanically adaptable, which is beneficial for dynamic scaffolds, artificial muscles, minimally invasive devices, biosensors, and controlled drug delivery systems. Combined with 4D printing, LCEs can be used to print patient-specific constructs that change their shape after printing and are subsequently used for surgery to enhance the spatial and temporal control of the therapy.82,83

Passive vs Active Tumor Microenvironment Modulation

Typical hydrogel systems react to conditions in the tumor microenvironment. But some recent approaches seek to change the tumor environment to enhance treatment outcomes.84 This can involve modulating several factors that lead to poor therapeutic outcomes, including hypoxia, acidity and oxidative stress. For instance, oxygen-generating hydrogels contain elements that release oxygen into the hypoxic tumor environment.85 This can boost the effectiveness of therapies such as photodynamic therapy and radiotherapy.

The enhanced permeability and retention effect is exploited in passive targeting where the long-circulating nanoscale hydrogel carriers can be passively trapped within the tumour as a result of inefficient lymphatic drainage and the leakiness of the tumour blood vessels. But, accumulation may be limited by a number of factors such as heterogeneous perfusion, stromal density and patient variability. Active targeting is achieved by ligand functionalisation of carriers, which upon tumour access will bind to receptors, overexpressed in these tumours, which will cause receptor mediated endocytosis. Examples of interactions are hyaluronic acid – CD44, folate – folate receptor, RGD – integrin αvβ3, and transferrin – transferrin receptor. Therefore, active targeting, in general, enhances the cellular recognition and internalisation and is not a substitute for passive delivery. Selectivity might be improved and off-target uptake and systemic toxicity might be reduced by optimisation of size, charge, circulation time and ligand density.86,87

Alternatively, pH-buffering hydrogels can help neutralize an acidic tumor microenvironment and enhance drug efficacy.88 Other examples include hydrogels that scavenge reactive oxygen species to modulate oxidative stress and inflammation in tumors. Nanocomposite Functional Hydrogels

The introduction of nanomaterials has further enhanced the capabilities and applications of hydrogels. Nanocomposite hydrogels leverage the structural benefits of hydrogels and the distinctive features of nanoparticles to improve drug delivery.94 A typical strategy is to load drugs into nanoparticles and incorporate these into hydrogels for sustained or targeted release. These nanocomposite systems provide improved drug stability, targeting and controlled release.95 Other formulations involve the incorporation of liposomes into hydrogels to allow the delivery of both polar and non-polar drugs and to increase drug loading.

Hydrogels based on metal-organic frameworks (MOFs) have also been developed on account of their large specific surface area and tunable pore sizes, facilitating drug loading and release. They can also offer multifunctional therapeutic effects, such as photothermal, photodynamic, and immunotherapy. However, there are challenges. These include making sure nanoparticles are homogenously dispersed, are stable and have safe and complete biodegradability.96

Prodrugs Based Nanoassembly

In this type of carrier-minimized nanoassembly, the anticancer drugs are covalently modified to allow self-assembly through hydrophobic, π–π and electrostatic interactions, with the goal of increasing the drug loading and reducing the toxicity associated with excipients. Tumour-responsive disulfide, hydrazone, boronate or enzyme cleavable linkers are used to selectively activate in reductive, acid, ROS-rich or enzyme-rich tumour microenvironments.97,98 Feng et al created fatty-alcohol–doxorubicin nanoassemblies of 60–70 nm, which were able to achieve a 14.2-fold increase in AUC when compared to free dye, and a final 4T1 tumour volume reduction of 144.39 ± 36.77 mm3 while maintaining tolerability with the use of the C16 derivative.99 The inclusion in a biopolymer hydrogel would make it possible to achieve local retention with sequential release and combined space - molecule targeting.

MXene-Based Responsive Hydrogels

The two-dimensional transition-metal carbides/nitrides (MXenes) and the hydrated polymer networks are the components of responsive hydrogels, specifically MXene-based responsive hydrogels. They possess rich surface groups –OH, –O and –F, which have favorable dispersion/hydrogen bonding, drug absorption and network reinforcement properties, while the near-infrared absorption can help to convert into photothermal. This makes these composites light-, pH-, temperature-, enzyme- or electrically responsive, which can be exploited for on-demand drug release, photothermal ablation and combination therapy. Doxorubicin release from a gellan-gum/MXene hydrogel was triggered by 808 nm light, and maintained drug cytotoxicity. But the long term retention and surface chemistry/biosafety that depends on the batch are still important issues for clinical oncology.100,101

DNA Hydrogels-Based Intracellular Delivery Strategy

Stimuli responsive, protective and recognition programmable DNA hydrogels also offer important advantages. Recognition and activation by mRNA, pH, ATP, glutathione or nucleases with aptamers, antisense sequences, DNAzymes and linkers. Yao et al have developed DNA hydrogels for imaging of mRNA of c-myc. Structural units were 10.2, 8.2 and 6.1 nm with the intermediate construct being found to have a detection limit of 67.6 pM and a response time of less than 30 min and uptake twice that of the largest construct. These systems have the potential to deliver siRNA, chemotherapeutics, proteins or imaging probes to tumours, but would benefit from optimisation with regard to nuclease stability, endosomal escape and reproducibility.102,103

Drug Loading and Release Mechanisms

Effective targeting is not the only crucial factor to the therapeutic performance of hydrogel-based drug delivery systems because of the ability of drugs to be incorporated and released by the system.104 Hydrogels consist of an interlocking polymer system that is three-dimensional and capable of entrapping a broad scope of therapeutic agents such as chemotherapeutic drugs, proteins, nucleic acids, and nanoparticles.105 This property of the hydrogel facilitates that hydrogels can serve as localized depots of drugs to achieve a high concentration of drugs at the tumors with minimal systemic exposure and toxicity. Hydrogel-based systems offer long-term and site-targeted drug releases, which is more efficient than conventional systemic chemotherapy and reduces side effects.106

Drug Incorporation Strategies

There are various ways in which drugs can be introduced into the hydrogel system, which depends on both the drug properties and the nature of the hydrogel system.107 Physical entrapment is the most widespread technique in which the drug molecules are trapped in the hydrogel during the formation process. Here, the hydrogel is porous and therefore the drug molecules are trapped by the porous structure and hence diffuse into the surrounding tissues slowly after the administration. The method is popular due to the fact that no chemical modification of the drug is required, and the method maintains the biological activity of the drug.96 Electrostatic interactions are another strategy that is important. In this case, the oppositely charged groups of the hydrogel network interact with charged drug molecules. As an example, negatively charged molecules such as nucleic acids or proteins can be bound by positively charged polymers such as chitosan.108 This interaction can be used to enhance drug loading efficiency as well as give a more controlled and sustained release profile.109

More developed methods employ covalent conjugation, in which the drugs are linked to the backbone of the hydrogel via degradable linkers.110,111 Figure 3 explains some important incorporation and controlled release methods of drugs using hydrogel systems such as reservoir system, matrix system and stimuli-responsive system. The methods allow accurate control of drug delivery, release rates and delivery to site. These design factors play an essential role in the optimization of therapeutic effect in cancer therapy. Diffusion- vs Degradation-Controlled Release

The two processes that mainly determine drug release in hydrogels include diffusion and degradation although the two frequently occur concurrently depending on the system design. Diffusion-controlled systems the drug molecules diffuse slowly across the hydrogel network because of concentration differences. Diffusion rate is determined by the size of pores, cross linking density, and the swelling characteristic of hydrogel. Large pores of the hydrogel tend to release the drug faster, whereas increased crosslinking of the hydrogel slows diffusion and delays release.106 Drug release in degradation-controlled systems takes place as the hydrogel matrix degrades with time. This degradation may occur in the form of hydrolysis or enzymatic degradation or environmental triggers as changes in pH. With degradation of the polymer network, the drugs trapped are released in tissues around the area. The method is especially applicable to the applications that demand prolonged and extended drug delivery.115

On-Demand and Programmable Delivery

Also passive release mechanisms are being supplemented with more sophisticated hydrogel systems which are currently being produced to deliver drugs on demand whereby the drug is only released under particular stimuli.116 This will enable more control over the time of drug release and treatment can be matched to the progression of the disease. One of such systems is stimuli-responsive hydrogels. These materials are sensitive to internal cues, including pH, temperature, enzyme activity, or reactive oxygen species, which are typical in the tumor microenvironment.117

As an example, pH-sensitive hydrogels have the ability to deliver drugs faster in acidic tumor environment than normal tissues. pH-responsive carriers take advantage of the pH difference for control of payload retention and release. Protonation and swelling, bond cleavage or disassembly of the network of polymers containing ionisable carboxyl groups, amine groups, acetal groups, hydrazone or imine groups, can vary as a function of pH. The acidic environment in the cell (including acidic tumour and endo/lysosomal environment) can be utilized to release drugs within the cell, while minimizing/avoiding drug leakage. The use of alginate is beneficial as it is compact at gastric pH, but expands and disintegrates at intestinal pH.118,119 Bhagat et al formulated alginate microcapsules which have nanominerals (Zn, Cu and Mn) with pH responsible release for targeting delivery to the intestine and improved bioavailability.120

Co-Delivery of Multi-Therapeutics

There are several biological pathways that involve cancer, and this usually renders it less effective under treatment with single drugs. Hydrogel systems are a convenient platform through which various therapeutic agents can be delivered together creating synergistic treatment plans.121

One example is the use of hydrogel to deliver chemotherapeutic drugs, immunotherapeutic agents together, which boosts immune response and directly attacks cancer cells. On the same note, integrating chemotherapy with gene therapy in the same hydrogel system can enhance better treatment of tumors because of addressing genetic and cellular mechanisms of tumor development.122

Such applications are particularly fitting to nanocomposite hydrogels, since the nanoparticles incorporated can be further loaded with other drugs or therapeutic molecules. This permits the release of several agents in a controlled and localized manner with high concentrations of drugs remaining at the tumor location.123 Recent studies on representative hydrogel-based drug delivery platforms, including their drug loading behavior and therapeutic outcome are summed in Table 4. Drug Delivery Using Hydrogel Based Representative Systems Applied in Cancer Treatment

Applications in Targeted Cancer Therapy

Delivery systems based on hydrogel have emerged as a powerful technique of localized and targeted treatment of cancer, particularly due to their capability to entrap drugs of interest, provide therapeutic drug levels at the target site, and release them in a regulated fashion. In contrast to conventional systemic chemotherapy, these hydrogel depots can be delivered to tumor tissues or post-surgical cavities, which enables to mitigate systemic side effects and enhance the overall treatment efficacy.129,130 Recent findings have indicated that there are a variety of therapeutic strategies in which hydrogel systems can be utilized, such as chemotherapy, immunotherapy, gene therapy, and phototherapy. Moreover, the flexibility of responsive polymers and nanomaterials has had a huge impact on the functionality of such systems, which now permit multimodal approaches to treatment based on the type of tumor being targeted.96

Chemotherapy

Chemotherapy is one of the most often used methods in the treatment of cancer, but its systemic administration is commonly associated with severe side effects because of the lack of specificity in the delivery of drugs. An alternative that is more focused is hydrogel-based systems, which allow them to deliver chemotherapeutic drugs locally and in a sustained manner. Drug depots in tumors can also be injectable hydrogels that slowly deliver drugs to different tumors like doxorubicin, paclitaxel, cisplatin, etc, keeping the active levels of the drugs in the tumor tissues constantly and with the least access to normal organs.131 As an example, the application of thermosensitive hydrogels has been greatly studied after excising the tumor. These hydrogels in the case of glioblastoma treatment can be injected in the surgical cavity and can be loaded with anticancer drugs to prevent tumor recurrence. Preclinical investigations have demonstrated enhanced tumor management and prolonged existence with such localized systems more than with conventional chemotherapy.132

The mechanism of action of each of the drugs used for chemotherapy is different: doxorubicin inhibits topoisomerase II, paclitaxel stabilizes microtubules, cisplatin induces cross linking of DNA, 5-fluorouracil inhibits thymidylate synthase, and gemcitabine blocks DNA synthesis. The benefit of these agents is that they have depots, which increase the exposure to the tumour and decrease the toxicity. The pH-responsive doxorubicin/FOK hydrogel was fabricated with high efficiency (over 95%) and the released doxorubicin exhibited a trend of higher amount after 48 h (58.62%), which decreased the tumour size from 109.65 to 81.50 mm3, meanwhile, free doxorubicin caused mass reduction from 18.8 to 16.9 g. Data on the pharmacokinetics, release, efficacy and toxicity of each agent should be tabulated.130,133

Immunotherapy

Immunotherapy is aimed at stimulating the immunity of the body to kill cancer cells. Nonetheless, immunotherapeutic agents, including immune checkpoint inhibitors, can be administered systemically with serious side effects of immunity. Hydrogel delivery systems are delivery systems that offer the method to deliver these agents locally and minimize systemic toxicity and maximize therapeutic outcomes.134 Hydrogel based injectable vectors have been made so as to deliver cytokines, immune checkpoint blocks and cancer vaccines to tumor locations or lymphoid tissues. They serve as local immune stores that release immunostimulatory molecules gradually, which enhances prolonged immune response and greater anti-tumor effect. It has been demonstrated that anti programmed cell death protein 1 (anti-PD-1) or anti programmed death-ligand 1 (anti-PD-L1) antibodies incorporated into hydrogel structures can be very effective in promoting immune response and tumor regression in melanoma, among other cancer models.135,136 Liu et al conceived a hydrogel containing alginate and bioactive-glass that could be applied for the release of Mn2+ ions to activate cGAS–STING and also a near-infrared hyperthermia agent to suppress the recurrence and to generate immune memory against melanoma in combination with anti-PD-1 blockade.137

Gene Therapy

Gene therapy provides a great approach to treating cancer whereby the nucleic acid like siRNA, mRNA, or plasmid DNA are introduced into tumor cells to modify their gene expression.138 Nevertheless, they are very unstable in the biological conditions and may be easily broken down by the enzymes, and that is why they do not work very well. Hydrogels could be utilized as bioprotective vectors to deliver genes to the tumor location by trapping nucleic acids and protecting them against degradation in addition to releasing them on the tumor site (on demand).139 As an illustration, hydrogel-based carriers made of nanoparticles have been used to deliver siRNA that suppresses oncogenes that cause tumor growth. These systems are able to offer prolonged gene control and better therapeutic outcomes than the traditional delivery procedures.140 In vivo, a thermosensitive hydrogel made of a poly(N-isopropylacrylamide)-poly(ethylene glycol)-poly(N-isopropylacrylamide) allowed to maintain the siRNA–STAT3 polyplexes for 7 days, and delayed the onset of ovarian-tumours up to day 56 for gene-therapy applications.141

Photothermal Therapy

Photothermal therapy Photothermal therapy (PTT) involves the use of materials capable of transforming the near-infrared light into heat, which then destroys tumor cells locally. Hydrogels have extensively been utilized as photothermal carriers of gold nanorods, graphene oxide, and polydopamine nanoparticles.142 This series of nanomaterials-loaded hydrogels produce heat when subjected to near-infrared light which can selectively destroy cancerous cells with little harm to the surrounding normal tissues. Besides the direct tumor ablation, photothermal hydrogels can also be useful in enhancing drug delivery due to the fact that it can increase the permeability of blood vessels and increase the deep penetration of therapeutic agents.143 A γ-polyglutamic-acid/polydopamine hydrogel has demonstrated a photothermal conversion of 45.6% and 82.3% tumour inhibition.144

Photodynamic Therapy

Photodynamic therapy (PDT) is a technique of using photosensitizers which form reactive oxygen species when activated with light and cause cell death in tumors. Nonetheless, conventional PDT has certain drawbacks including ineffective retention of photosensitizers at the tumor location, and low light penetration.145 Hydrogel-based systems are used to resolve such problems by offering sustained release and enhanced localization of photosensitizers. The active agents are highly concentrated at the tumor site and increase the efficacy of treatments by these hydrogels. Also, there is the development of oxygen-generating hydrogel to manage tumor hypoxia which tends to decrease the effectiveness of PDT.146 The therapeutic systems described in recent studies using hydrogel as a representative of a therapeutic system, the type of therapy and cancer model applied are summarized in Table 5. Targeted Cancer Therapy Based on Representative Hydrogel Platforms

Tumor Microenvironment-Responsive Hydrogels

The tumor microenvironment (TME) is a set of special biochemical and physiological parameters which characterize the cancerous tissues as opposite to the healthy tissues. These are acidic PH, enzyme over-expression, increased concentration of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and hypoxia.152 Smart hydrogel that reacts to these internal stimuli has become an innovative place of the site-specific delivery of drugs, where the therapeutic agents can be released selectively and in controlled amounts directly into the tumor tissues.153,154

pH-Responsive Hydrogels

The slightly acidic pH of the tumor microenvironment (usually between 6.5 and 6.8) is one of the most frequently used ones and is caused by the enhanced activity of glycolytic processes and the low blood perfusion.155 Hydrogels that respond to pH are known to be specially designed to change their structures or chemical properties in this acidic environment thus releasing drugs faster when they arrive at tumor sites.

Such systems usually utilize acid-sensitive linkages, including hydrazone, imine, or acetal bonds, that are usually stable at normal physiological conditions, but disintegrate in acidic environments. These bonds are torn when the tumor microenvironment is introduced and the hydrogel network becomes loose or degraded to release the drug it is encapsulated in. Though pH-responsive systems offer a comparatively easy method of targeting, pH difference between tumor and normal tissues is not so vast that it limits its specificity. This is why pH-responsible hydrogel is increasingly utilized together with other types of responsive gel to enhance selectivity and overall therapy efficacy.156

Enzyme-Responsive Hydrogels

Increased expression of particular enzymes, eg, matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), hyaluronidase, and other proteases, involved in tumor progression and metastasis, is also common in tumor tissues. The principle of enzyme-responsive hydrogels is based on the ability to introduce enzyme-cleavable linkers in the structure of polymer gels.157

Upon exposure of these hydrogels to target enzymes, the linkers are cleaved selectively causing degradation of the hydrogel structure and the release of drugs in a localized manner. Enzyme-responsive hydrogels are more specific than pH-responsive systems since the concentration of an enzyme is significantly higher in tumor tissues than in normal tissues. Nevertheless, variation in the enzyme expression between tumor types and even within patients may alter consistency and reproducibility.158

ROS-Responsive Hydrogels

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) are known to be high in tumor environment as a result of the increased metabolic rate and oxidative stress.159 Hydrogels that are ROS-responsive are supposed to react to these conditions by experiencing a change in structure that will lead to the release of the drug. ROS-sensitive linkages, eg thioketal or boronic ester, are usually found in these systems and cleaved under the influence of ROS.160 This enables drugs to be discharged selectively within tumor tissues and also remain stable under normal physiological conditions. The latter makes ROS-responsive hydrogels especially applicable to combination therapy, where ROS can be further augmented by other therapies, such as photodynamic or photothermal therapy.161 The over-induction and scavenging of ROS by strategies should however be closely regulated since they can disrupt oxidative based treatments.162 ROS-responsive hydrogels incorporate oxidant-cleavable thioketal, boronic-ester, diselenide or peroxalate linkages that can cause degradation of the network and release of payloads when exposed to oxidants present within the tumour.163 Ye et al constructed a ROS-responsive depot co-delivering FK866 (NAMPT inhibitor) and C188-9 (STAT3 inhibitor) that triggered ferroptosis as well as facilitating the infiltration of CD8+ T cells into the tumour.164

Hypoxia-Responsive Hydrogels

Low oxygen levels, which are also known as hypoxia, is a characteristic feature of most solid tumors and can be linked to resistance to treatment and adverse clinical outcome. Hypoxia-responsive hydrogels are a type of hydrogels that are engineered to use these low-oxygen environments to release a drug or activate therapeutic agents.165 Figure 4 emphasizes the combination of several TME-sensitive agents such as pH, ROS, and enzymatic inducers, which allow managing the release of drugs accurately and selectively. Multifunctional hydrogels are useful in increasing therapeutic efficacy because the scaffold adapts to the dynamic tumor microenvironment. Such systems usually have hypoxia sensitive moieties, including nitroimidazole derivatives, which are bioreduced in low oxygen conditions. The result of this process is the modification of the hydrogel structure or release of prodrugs present inside the tumor microenvironment. These systems are especially useful in the treatment of tumor areas that are resistant to conventional therapy. Nevertheless, it is a problem to obtain adequate and steady responsiveness in heterogeneous tumor settings.166 Table 6 summarizes representative examples of TME-responsive hydrogel systems, their activation mechanisms and therapeutic outcomes. Hydrogel-Based Representative TME-Responsive Systems for Targeted Therapy of Cancer

Injectable, Implantable and 3D/4D-Bioprinted Systems

The clinical application of the hydrogel-based drug delivery systems is highly dependent on the method of administration and the degree to which they could be located at the target site. Injectable, implantable, and 3D-bioprinted hydrogel platforms are three complementary strategies that can be used to treat locally and also address various clinical requirements in oncology. They are particularly applicable in the treatment of solid tumours and post surgical cavities, where the conventional systemic treatments are not effective or are accompanied with severe toxicity.151

Injectable in situ Forming Hydrogels

There has been a lot of interest in injectable hydrogels that create gels in the specific location of administration because of their minimally invasive characteristics and the capability to conform to the shape of irregular tumors. Such systems are usually programmed to react to physiological cues like temperature, pH, or ionic strength, so that the state of the liquid can be changed to gel upon injection.174 Injectable hydrogels are actively studied in cancer treatment, both in intratumoral and post-surgical. Indicatively, thermosensitive hydrogels may be introduced in the form of liquid preparations and subsequently solidified to form stable depository gel at the body temperature to allow slow drug delivery within the tumor. Although these have their benefits, other issues like the rate of gelation and the distribution of drugs evenly are still major problems especially in the tumor environment that are complex and heterogeneous.175 Figure 5 shows how injectable in situ forming hydrogels can be used to treat localized and minimally invasive cancer. Such systems allow site-specific drug delivery, sustained release and increased therapeutic efficacy especially in postoperative and immunotherapy. Implantable Drug Depots

The implantable hydrogel systems are more stable and long-term drugs delivery systems than injectable systems. These hydrogels are created in advance and implanted at the cancer location or inside the surgical resection beds.179 This is because their structure is stable and can be controlled and release the drug at a longer period and this is especially helpful in reducing the recurrence of tumors.180 These systems have demonstrated positive results in the treatment of aggressive cancers such as glioblastoma where local delivery is essential because of obstacles such as the blood brain barrier. Nevertheless, their more general clinical application is constrained by the necessity to undergo surgical operations and the possible dangers of implantation. As such, therapeutic benefit of implantable systems should be offset by the invasiveness of the procedure.181

3D-Bioprinted Hydrogel Platforms

The recent advancements of 3D bioprinting have allowed the fabrication of the hydrogel structures with a strict spatial order and with the composition that can be controlled. These systems enable the assembly of cells, medicines and biomolecules into customizable constructs that are highly similar to the tumor microenvironment.182

The 3D-bioprinted hydrogels are becoming powerful tumor models in cancer studies to screen drugs, as well as personalized medicine. They are more representative of the conventional in vitro systems because they have the ability to replicate tumor heterogeneity and cell matrix interactions. Nevertheless, the difficulties in providing appropriate vascularization, preserving their stability over time, and scaling production persist in restricting their clinical use.183

4D-Bioprinted Platforms

Injectable and implantable hydrogels are not the only hydrogels in the family of 4D printing, as time-dependent shape, mechanics and therapy are also added properties. These changes are achieved by employing stimuli-responsive materials that can be applied with minimal invasiveness and can be activated by temperature, pH, light, magnetic field, enzyme, or tumor associated stimuli to control the spatiotemporally delivery of the therapy.184 An example of such is the 4D printed sandwich structured metamaterial intestinal stent for palliative treatment of colorectal tumours. The device consists of three parts: the outer part is Janus layer that has synergistic photothermal and drug-mediated antitumour effects, the middle part is biomimetic gradient-metamaterial that provides structure and coordinated deformation, and the inner part is hydrophobic layer that can prevent the occurrence of restenosis. This architecture will facilitate malignant intestinal obstruction, preserve anatomy and local treatment.185 While intended for cardiac repair, Wang et al have presented a reconfigurable 4D-printed patent foramen ovale (PFO) occluder that also features transferable design concepts for shape-memory deployment, local drug delivery, bioabsorption, antithrombotic activity and accelerated tissue integration.186 For the biopolymer based hydrogel carriers, the analogous multilayer constructs could be employed to spatially separate incompatible agents, two in one for mechanical and therapeutic purposes, and for sequential and/or stimulus triggered release. However, for clinical translation, reproducible printing, biocompatible actuation, degradation control, sterility compatibility and in vivo evaluations should be performed.184

Image-Guided and Minimally Invasive Hydrogel Delivery

New technologies that involve the use of hydrogel delivery and imaging like MRI, CT, and ultrasound allow the therapeutic depots to be specifically placed. The methods permit the localization of the hydrogel in real-time, which guarantees the delivery of drugs to the target tumor site.187

There is also the minimally invasive approach, such as catheter-based and ultrasound-guided injections, which also enhances clinical feasibility because it does not require a highly invasive surgical procedure. These methods are especially suitable in the treatment of tumors that are deep-seated as precise targeting is essential in this case. Nevertheless, the concept of imaging compatibility into hydrogel design has been a difficult one but proper attention has to be paid to the material properties and appropriate contrast agents.188

Clinical Translation and Manufacturing Considerations

Although hydrogel-based drug delivery has made a great breakthrough in cancer treatment, their clinical application has not yet been realized. A huge disparity between preclinical success and clinical performance is one of the challenges. A lot of hydrogel systems tested successfully in animal models have not yet yielded similar results in human beings, in large part because of variations in tumor complexity, immune response and overall physiological state.189

One also needs scalability and reproducibility. Hydrogels made of bio-polymers, particularly natural ones, may vary in batches with respect to molecular weight, level of modification and mechanical performance. These differences can have a direct impact on drug loading, release characteristics and therapeutic performance. Thus, it is important to set uniform production procedures and rigorous quality check in order to pass the regulatory test.190

There are other challenges associated with sterilization and storage. Some of the hydrogels are also vulnerable to the usual methods of sterilization (heat or radiation) that can change the structure and functionality of the gel. A long-term stability that does not affect the biological activity is also of critical importance when used in the clinic.191 Resultatively, hydrogel systems have generally been treated as a combination product since they consist of drug and device components. This complicates the approval process, where they have to comply with the pharmaceutical regulations as well as the medical device regulations. The lack of standard testing and evaluation procedures also makes them slow to be adopted clinically.192 Real world manufacturing issues like cost, scalability, and usability should be met. Intravenously deliverable hydrogels are beneficial in delivering low-invasive implants, but need careful control of gelation kinetics and biomechanical characteristics. Effective yet possibly constrained by surgery procedures and patient willingness, implantable systems can be viewed as a possible way to overcome the problem of traditional systems. These issues will have to be addressed in order to bring hydrogel-based therapies to regular clinical practice.

Challenges, Limitations and Future Perspectives

Although a lot of progress has been made in hydrogel-based platforms in cancer therapy targeting, there are a number of pertinent issues that are restricting their effective commercialization into clinical applications. The most chronic problem is the phenomenon of initial burst release, which is the release of a significant part of the drug in a short period after delivery. This does not only lower the overall therapeutic efficacy of the therapy in the long term but it can also cause a local toxicity. Even though techniques like covalent drug conjugation and multi-network hydrogel designs have been studied to resolve this problem, a significant challenge is to have a fine and consistent control over the kinetics of drug release.193

The other issue of importance is the reproducibility especially in bio-polymer-based hydrogels. Polymer source, molecular weight, and functionalization degree can produce polymer variability that leads to inconsistency in physicochemical properties and consequently, drug loading and release profile. The problems are further complicated by the problem of storage and stability because most hydrogel systems are environmentally sensitive and subject to sterilization, which may change their structure and functionality.194 Individual variability of patients is also an important factor in the influence of the efficacy of hydrogel systems. Variations in tumor microenvironment, immune response and metabolic activity may result in differences in therapeutic responses. Such flexibility complicates the standardization of treatment procedures and demonstrates the necessity of more flexible and individualized delivery systems. Moreover, large-scale production issues like costs, reproduction and regulatory measures are still impediments to commercialization of these technologies.195

The next step in the evolution of future development is to transition from the concept of broadly smart materials, to one that is closed loop, spanning from tumour characterisation to formulation design, treatment delivery, measurement of response and adaptation of treatment. AI and machine learning can be used to model nonlinear relationships among polymer composition, crosslinking density, polymer rheology, polymer degradation, drug loading, drug release kinematics and biological impacts. Bayesian optimisation already enabled simultaneous optimisation of multiple properties of alginate based hydrogels and in a data-driven study 180 initial formulations were used to train models, resulting in generated hydrogels with over 1 MPa adhesive strength.196,197 These results show that although this technology is feasible, standardised datasets will be required which include material properties, but also tumour pH, levels of reactive oxygen species, receptor expression, stromal density, pharmacokinetics and toxicity for different types of cancer. However, the small size of many of the data sets, differences in the endpoints of experiments, change in the domain from animal to human tumours and lack of interpretability of the models, are all factors that currently limit reliable clinical prediction.198

Personalisation should be done at three levels: (1) Payload selection (tumour genomic, proteomic and immune biomarkers); (2) Material programming (stimulus thresholds, degradation rate, ligand density, release sequence); (3) Anatomical customisation (injectable constructs or constructs that can be 3D/4D printed that match the tumour geometry or the geometry of the resection cavity). Organoids can, for example, be kept alive in a mesoscale collagen-gelatin hydrogel to preserve their viability, histology, mutational characteristics and drug sensitivity, allowing their use for patient-specific drug testing. However, organoid-assisted selection of the hydrogel will still have to be correlated with clinical outcome and clinically acceptable manufacturing times will still need to be achieved.199,200 In the same manner, a theranostic hydrogel should not be simply a passive imaging tag but should be capable of providing feedback in terms of depot localization, payload release, tumour response, hypoxia, or pH or inflammatory biomarkers. The formulation that contained microbubbles and iodine showed dual ultrasound and X-ray/computed-tomography visualisation, and formulations containing < 1% microbubbles were able to discriminate from the surrounding liver tissue with minimal acoustic artefacts. The systems might help verify that the treatment volume has been covered and identify leakage before adjusting the dose, but the imaging signal would have to be quantitatively correlated with the drug concentration and therapeutic response at a local level.187,201,202

Conclusion

The advent of bio-polymer-based functionalized hydrogel systems has made a great change towards the targeted cancer therapy. These materials are biocompatible, structurally flexible and are able to incorporate various functional elements in one platform. Some of the strategies that have facilitated the spatial and temporal specificity of drug delivery are ligand-mediated targeting, stimuli-responsive behavior, and nanocomposites design. Also, multifunctional hydrogel that can be used to support chemotherapy, immunotherapy, gene therapy and phototherapy is being developed, thus pointing out their promise as the next generation therapeutic system. Although such improvements have been made, a consistent performance in a complex biological environment is a major challenge. The unique advantage of bio-polymer based functionalised hydrogel carriers is that the molecular composition, crosslinking density and porosity, degradation behaviour and surface chemistry can all be tailored to control drug loading, tumour retention, cellular uptake and therapeutic release, all of which are relevant for targeted oncology applications. The functional groups of polysaccharides and proteins like chitosan, alginate, hyaluronic acid, cellulose, dextran, gelatin, and DNA are chemically accessible to be conjugated to ligands, incorporated into nanomaterials, and also to be used as a stimulus-sensitive network. All these properties enable the passive accumulation of these properties in the tumour, as well as the receptor mediated targeting including hyaluronic acid – CD44, folate – folate receptor, RGD – integrin and transferrin – transferrin receptor.

Clinically, the hydrogel-based systems provide a powerful alternative to the traditional systemic therapies through the localized delivery of the drugs and reduction in systemic toxicity. They are also clinically important in the treatment of tumors especially in the post-surgical procedures to avert the recurrence of the tumors. Nevertheless, in order to achieve success in translating into clinical practice, it is necessary to surmount significant obstacles, such as reproducibility, scalability, complexity of regulation, and variability in patients. The discrepancy between the preclinical to clinical outcomes also highlights the importance of more realistic tumor models and standard evaluation procedures. The solution of these problems will be the key to the full potential of hydrogel-based therapies in cancer. The future of hydrogel-based cancer therapy will be advanced material design and precision medicine. Predictive optimization with the help of artificial intelligence and the creation of multi-responsive and personalized hydrogel systems should become faster in its progression. Real-time monitoring and adaptive treatment strategies will be made possible by other innovations including bio-orthogonal crosslinking and theranostic platforms. Bio-polymer-based hydrogel can play a transformational role in the creation of effective and patient-specific cancer therapies with further cooperation in the materials science, oncology, and biomedical engineering fields.
