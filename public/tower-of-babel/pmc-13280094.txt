Lactate-associated transcriptomic alterations and malignant phenotypes in colorectal cancer
Authors: Shujuan Li, Shiwei Feng, Xuanna Li, Ran Su, Jinhua Deng, Sijing Cheng, Yujie Hou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLactate, an energy source and metabolic by-product, has been implicated in cancer progression, but its role in colorectal cancer (CRC) remains incompletely understood. This study investigated the clinical significance, biological effects, and transcriptomic responses of CRC cells to lactate. In human CRC specimens, lactate levels were positively associated with advanced clinical stage and poorer disease-free survival. Functional assays showed that lactate promoted malignant cellular behaviors in both SW480 and HCT116 cells, while pH-control experiments suggested that these effects were not merely due to extracellular acidification alone. RNA sequencing in SW480 cells identified 1,418 differentially expressed genes after lactate treatment. GO and KEGG analyses revealed alterations in multiple metabolic and signaling pathways. qRT-PCR validated the alterations of representative genes, including HK2, VEGFA, JUNB, CCNB1, MAPK4, and COX2. In addition, flow cytometry showed activation of NF-κB and HIF-1α signaling following lactate treatment, and pharmacological inhibition of either pathway significantly attenuated the lactate-induced malignant phenotypes. Together, these findings provide transcriptomic and functional evidence that lactate promotes malignant phenotypes in CRC cells and offer exploratory mechanistic insights into the involvement of NF-κB and HIF-1α signaling.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-026-05147-1.Keywords: Lactate, Transcriptomics, Colorectal cancer

Introduction

The overwhelming majority of malignant tumors are genetically different. However, what pathogenic mechanism has in common for them are activation of oncogenic factors and inactivation of tumor suppressor factors. To meet the needs of tumor cells occurrence, development and metastasis, mutations in certain oncogenes or tumor suppressor genes are more likely to induce vigorous metabolism including glucose and lactate in the tumor microenvironment (TME). Even if there was sufficient oxygen, glycolysis rather than oxidative phosphorylation was significantly increased and mainly for energy supply in cancer cells, which was called “Warburg effect” [1]. That’s why the glycolytic activity and accumulation of lactate were increasing [2].

Lactate acidified TME contributes to migration, invasion, angiogenesis and immune evasion [3, 4]. This was established in a number of studies that the higher levels of lactate were associated with more metastases and shorter overall survival [5], [6], [7], such as pancreatic cancer [8], gastric cancer (GC) [9], renal cell carcinoma (RCC) [10], and prostate cancer [11]. Further, a preclinical study confirmed that the ability of tumor to metastasize observably decreased if the glycolysis and lactate metabolism were inhibited [12]. The levels of lactate elevated significantly with increasing malignancy and even more than 4-fold in metastatic colorectal cancer (mCRC) [13]. Nevertheless, surprisingly little work has been performed on the exact mechanism of lactate in the context of CRC.

In this study, by integrating lactate quantification, clinical prognostic analysis, transcriptomic profiling, functional validation in CRC cells, pH modulation experiments, and pharmacological inhibition of NF-κB and HIF-1α, we explored the associations between lactate levels and clinical outcome and further characterized the biological programs associated with lactate in CRC.

Materials and methods

Cell lines and cultures

The human CRC cell lines SW480 and HCT116 were purchased from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). All of the cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s (DMEM) (Gibco, NY, USA) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Gibco, NY, USA) and grown at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere supplemented with 5% CO2. For lactate treatment, L-lactate was added to the culture medium at a final concentration of 5 mM. For pharmacological inhibition experiments, cells were pretreated with the NF-κB inhibitor BAY 11–7082 (Selleck Chemicals, Cat# S2913) at a final concentration of 5 µM for 60 min, or with the HIF-1α inhibitor PX-478 dihydrochloride (Selleck Chemicals, Cat# S7612) at a final concentration of 20 µM for 24 h, before lactate stimulation and subsequent functional assays.

Patients and tissue samples

The 12 pairs of freshly frozen CRC and normal adjacent samples were obtained from patients during surgery at the Sixth Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University, Guangzhou, China. The matched adjacent normal tissues were collected at least 3 cm away from the tumor. None of the patients had received chemotherapy or radiotherapy before sampling. On the basis of the tumor-node-metastasis (TNM) classification system published by the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) and Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) (Edition 8), the clinical stages of tumors were determined. Written informed consent was priorly maintained from each patient. All specimens were acquired using protocols approved by the Institutional Review Board of The Sixth Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University.

Cell proliferation assays

Cell growth was evaluated by soft agar colony formation and Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; Sigma) assays. For anchorage-independent growth analysis, 500 cells were suspended in DMEM containing 10% FBS and 0.3% low-melting-point agarose, and then overlaid onto 6-well plates precoated with 0.66% agarose. Colony development was followed on a weekly basis, and colonies were documented by microscopy after 3 weeks. For the CCK-8 assay, cells were plated into 96-well plates (Corning, USA) at 5 × 103 cells per well in 100 µL complete medium. After 48 h of culture, 10 µL CCK-8 reagent was added to each well, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 2 h. Absorbance was then recorded at 450 nm using a microplate reader (Tecan Infinite, Switzerland). Cell viability was expressed relative to the control group after normalization of absorbance values.

Cell migration and invasion assays

Cell migratory and invasive capacities were assessed using Transwell inserts with an 8-µm pore size (Corning, USA) placed in 24-well plates. For migration assays, inserts were used without Matrigel coating, whereas for invasion assays, the upper chambers were precoated with Matrigel. SW480 and HCT116 cells were prepared in serum-free medium, and 5 × 104 cells in 200 µL were added to each upper chamber. The lower chambers were filled with culture medium supplemented with 10% FBS as a chemoattractant. Following incubation for 48 h at 37 °C, cells that had traversed the membrane were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Sangon, China) for 15 min and then stained with crystal violet (Sangon, China) for 10 min. Images were captured and the numbers of migrated or invaded cells were quantified under an Olympus microscope (Japan).

pH modulation experiments

SW480 and HCT116 cells were treated with lactate at the indicated concentrations and for the indicated times. To distinguish lactate-dependent effects from those caused by extracellular acidification, parallel pH modulation experiments were performed. In the neutralized lactate group, the pH of lactate-containing medium was adjusted by dropwise addition of sterile 1 N NaOH to the indicated value. In the acidification control group, lactate-free medium was adjusted with sterile 1 N HCl to the corresponding pH of the lactate-treated condition. The final pH values were verified using a calibrated pH meter before application to cells. Cells were then cultured under these conditions and subjected to the indicated functional assays.

qRT-PCR

Total RNA was isolated from cells and subjected to reverse transcription with the ReverTra Ace qPCR RT Kit (Toyobo Biochemicals, Kita-ku, Osaka, Japan) in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, 2000 ng of RNA was reverse-transcribed in a final volume of 20 µL. Real-time quantitative PCR was subsequently carried out using SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA) on an Applied Biosystems 7500 Sequence Detection System following the manufacturer’s recommended SYBR Green protocol. Each sample was analyzed in triplicate in 10 µL reaction mixtures. Relative mRNA expression levels of target genes were determined by the 2−△△CT method after normalization to ACTIN, and standard curves were generated for quantification. The primer sequences were as follows: HK2, forward 5′-GAGCCACCACTCACCCTACT-3′ and reverse 5′-CCAGGCATTCGGCAATGTG-3′; VEGFA, forward 5′-GAGATGAGCTTCCTACAGCAC-3′ and reverse 5′-TCACCGCCTCGGCTTGTCACAT-3′; JUNB, forward 5′-ACGACTCATACACAGCTACGG-3′ and reverse 5′-GCTCGGTTTCAGGAGTTTGTAGT-3′; CCNB1, forward 5′-AAACTTTGGTCTGGGTCGGC-3′ and reverse 5′-TGCTGCAATTTGAGAAGGAGG-3′; MAPK4, forward 5′-TACGGGGAGAATGCTCTTTG-3′ and reverse 5′-CAGGGATGGTCTCCAGGAT-3′; COX2, forward 5′-TGAGCAACTATTCCAAACCAGC-3′ and reverse 5′-GCACGTAGTCTTCGATCACTATC-3′; and ACTIN, forward 5′-CTGGAACGGTGAAGGTGACA-3′ and reverse 5′-AAGGGACTTCCTGTAACAATGCA-3′.

Flow cytometry analysis

Cells were collected after the indicated treatments, washed with cold PBS, and processed for intracellular flow cytometry. For detection of NF-κB phosphorylation, cells were fixed and permeabilized using BD Phosflow™ Fix Buffer I (BD Biosciences, Cat# 557870) and BD Phosflow™ Perm Buffer III (BD Biosciences, Cat# 558050). For detection of HIF-1α expression, cells were fixed and permeabilized using an intracellular fixation/permeabilization buffer set (Thermo Fisher/eBioscience, Cat# 00–5523-00) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cells were then incubated with fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies against phospho-NF-κB p65 (Ser536) (Thermo Fisher/Invitrogen, Cat# MA5-37157), phospho-NF-κB p65 (Ser529) (Thermo Fisher/eBioscience, Cat# 12–9863-42), phospho-NF-κB p65 (Ser468) (Proteintech, Cat# CL647-82335), and HIF-1α (Thermo Fisher/eBioscience, Cat# 12–7528-82) for 30 min at 4 °C in the dark. After washing, cells were resuspended in staining buffer. Fluorescence signals were detected and recorded by flow cytometry using a BD Biosciences instrument (New Jersey, USA), and the raw data were further analyzed and visualized using FlowJo software (v10.0.7).

RNA sequencing

For RNA sequencing analysis, cells were collected after lactate exposure to capture relatively stable transcriptomic alterations rather than acute transcriptional responses. Total RNA was extracted from all the cells we cultured. The RNA quality was examined by Agilent 2200 (Agilent Technologies, USA). RNA was processed with RiboMinus eukaryotic kit (QIAGEN, Valencia, CA, USA) to delete ribosomal RNA and construct cDNA library. Hisat2 was used as the RNA mapping algorithm to compare the filtered clean data to the reference genome corresponding to the sequencing species (NCBI Taxonomy ID: 9606).

RNA quality assessment

The quality of sequencing data was evaluated as a whole using Fast-QC software, including base quality value distribution, quality value location distribution, GC content, PCR duplication content, kmer frequency, etc. The RAW FASTQ files and FPKM values of RNA-Seq have been uploaded to the NCBI online database, and the accession number is GSE180948.

Identification of differentially expressed genes (DEGs)

In order to demonstrate the similarity and differences between the two groups, the RNA-seq data was standardized by using the software package of DEseq2 based on fragments per kilobase million reads (FPKM), and two-dimensional principal component analysis (PCA) by maptools and hierarchical clustering was carried out. In the screening, the expression of gene showing fold changes (FC)>1.5 with a false discovery rate (FDR)<0.05 were selected. Namely, gene was identified as an upregulated gene with log2FC>0.585 while was considered downregulated with log2FC<−0.585. Ggplot2 software package was used to build the volcano map in R software, and the heatmaps of DEGs were conducted by the software packages of hclust, pcaPP and pheatmap.

GO and KEGG enrichment analysis

All related genes were mapped to GO terms in the GO database. For one thing, the GO terms were reflected in the main branches of biological process (BP), molecular function (MF) and cellular component (CC); for another, the selected DEGs were annotated based on the KEGG database to obtain all the biological pathway terms, such as the metabolism and the environmental information processing, etc. GO terms and KEGG pathways with p<0.05 were considered significantly enriched by differential expressed genes. The heatmaps of GO terms and KEGG enrichment pathways were plotted by http://www.bioinformatics.com.cn, a free online platform for data analysis and visualization.

Statistics

The data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Survival curves were generated using the Kaplan-Meier method, and differences between groups were assessed using the log-rank test. All statistical analyses were performed by GraphPad Prism GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA) and SPSS 23.0 (IBM, SPSS, Chicago, IL, USA). The correlations were analyzed using Pearson’s correlation coefficients. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All of the experiments were performed at least three times.

Results

Clinical implication of lactate in CRC tissues

To investigate the role of lactate in CRC, we determined the levels of lactate in paired fresh tumor tissues and normal adjacent samples isolated from a set of 12 patients with CRC. Examined by the lactate assay kit, the levels of lactate were 2.7 ± 2.9-fold higher in CRC tissues than corresponding adjacent non-tumor samples (Fig. 1A). We next correlated the levels of lactate with the clinicopathological status among 134 patients with stage I-III CRC. Results showed the levels of lactate increased with more advanced TNM stages (Fig. 1B). When stratified into two lactate categories using a cut point of the median lactate levels, patients with high levels of lactate showed a higher recurrence rate in the analyzed cohort. Recurrence at 3 years was seen in 15 (22.4%) patients with a high level of lactate, and in 5 (7.5%) patients with a low high level of lactate (Fig. 1C). Likewise, patients with a high level of lactate were associated with shorter disease-free survival (DFS) (HR, 2.5; 95% CI, 1.1 to 5.6; p = 0.03) (Fig. 1D) in this single-center cohort. DFS at 3 years was recorded for 51 (76.1%) patients with a high level of lactate, and 60 (89.6%) patients with a low high level of lactate.

Contribution of lactate to the malignant behaviors of CRC cells

Next, we further investigated the biological functions of lactate in CRC. To this end, the MTS assay, colony formation experiment and Transwell assay were performed to determine the effects of lactate on cell growth. As shown in Fig. 2A, the MTS assay indicated that the growth of SW480 cells was significantly increased after treatment with lactate. Soft agar colony formation further confirmed the effect of lactate on CRC cell proliferation (Fig. 2B, C). Moreover, the Transwell assay demonstrated that treatment with lactate significantly promoted the migrative abilities of SW480 cells (Fig. 2D, E). Similar trends were observed for the migrative and invasive abilities of SW480 cells (Fig. 2F, G). Taken together, these findings indicate that lactate is associated with pro-tumorigenic phenotypes in SW480 cells.

To exclude the possibility that the observed effects were limited to a single CRC cell line, we repeated the functional assays in HCT116 cells. Lactate treatment produced results comparable to those observed in SW480 cells, with similarly enhanced malignant cellular behaviors (Fig. 3A, B). We next asked whether these effects could be explained by lactate-induced extracellular acidification. Correction of the pH decrease by NaOH titration did not abolish the effects of lactate (Fig. 3C-F). By contrast, HCl-induced acidification alone, adjusted to the corresponding pH, did not phenocopy the effects of lactate (Fig. 3C-F). These results argue that the observed phenotypes are not simply due to extracellular acidification.

General transcriptome profiling

Hierarchical clustering was performed with the data sets of all genes. In this case, Spearman correlation found clear support that biological replicates of the same condition clustered with high similarity (Fig. 4A). Moreover, to visualize the distances and reliability of the tested samples by DEGs, PCA scores were analyzed as follows: PC1 = 86.5%, PC2 = 5.2% (Fig. 4B). As a result, the groups of Lactate from SW480 exhibited significantly distinct gene expression profiles compared with the groups of PBS and revealed a good clustering effect. As has been stated, these findings indicate that the RNA-seq results reliably identified potential genes in the tumor-supportive role under lactate stress. The genetic composition of the samples in the exons, introns and intergenic regions was described (Fig. 4C). We noticed that abundant RNA transcripts were detected in both exons and introns, suggesting the presence of numerous non-coding RNA (ncRNA).

From the results obtained so far, 1418 DEGs were detected from the groups of PBS and Lactate (Fig. 5A). In total, 897 genes were upregulated and 521 genes were downregulated (Fig. 5B). To determine which biological processes can be regulated by lactate in CRC cells, GO enrichment analysis was performed on both control and experimental groups (Fig. 6). According to the GO annotations of the DEGs, 53 of the significantly overrepresented GO terms (22 BP, 15 CC, 16 MF) in the biological process category (Table S1). Among these functions, 48, 22, and 34 DEGs were annotated to inflammatory response (GO:0006954), positive regulation of angiogenesis (GO:0045766), and extracellular matrix (GO:0031012), respectively. When compared with the control groups of PBS, it was identified that for biological process, DEGs were significantly enriched in ‘positive regulation of cell population proliferation’ and ‘cytokine-mediated signaling pathway’ (FDR < 0.01); for cell component, DEGs were significantly enriched in ‘cytoplasm’, ‘chromatin’ and ‘cytosol’ (FDR < 0.01); for molecular function, DEGs were significantly enriched in ‘transcription factor binding’ and ‘growth factor activity’ (FDR < 0.01).

Without doubt, KEGG is a relatively complete database of biological systems that integrates genomic, chemical and systemic functional information.[14] To further understand the biological functions of genes, we performed a KEGG pathway enrichment analysis. Taken together, a total of 81 KEGG pathways were identified, where 68 were upregulated while 13 were downregulated (Table S2). Another promising finding was that dozens of significant KEGG pathways are divided to environmental information processing, cellular processes, organismal systems and human diseases (Fig. 7A). Validation of transcriptomic findings and functional involvement of NF-κB and HIF-1α signaling

To further validate the transcriptomic findings, we performed qRT-PCR analysis of several representative differentially expressed genes identified by RNA-seq, including HK2, VEGFA, JUNB, CCNB1, MAPK4, and COX2. The results confirmed that all of these genes were significantly dysregulated following lactate treatment, consistent with the transcriptomic data (Fig. 8A). We next examined the activation status of the NF-κB and HIF-1α pathways. Flow cytometric analysis showed that both NF-κB and HIF-1α signaling were activated in response to lactate treatment (Fig. 8B, C). To further assess their functional involvement, pharmacological inhibitors targeting NF-κB or HIF-1α were applied. Inhibition of either pathway significantly attenuated the lactate-induced enhancement of malignant cellular behaviors (Fig. 8D-G), supporting the functional contribution of NF-κB and HIF-1α signaling to the biological effects of lactate.

Discussion

Tumor-related metabolites, such as glucose metabolites, lipid metabolites and amino acids, can regulate key signaling pathways through mechanisms such as competitive inhibition of epigenetic regulatory enzymes or post-translational modification of proteins to affect the development of tumors [15, 16]. In particular, lactate accumulation has been found in hematological malignancies such as leukemia, lymphoma, and multiple myeloma, as well as solid tumors, suggesting that it may also participate in tumor progression [17, 18]. TME is a special environment, composed of multiple cell types, extracellular matrix components, and cytokines that support tumor cell growth [19]. In order to meet the needs of the rapid proliferation, tumor cells often show abnormally increased glucose uptake, enhanced glycolysis and a large amount of lactate accumulation, forming a TME characterized by hypoxia, nutrient deprivation, and acidity [19]. Although previous reports have shown that, as an important signaling molecule, lactate not only affects surrounding cells, but also plays a vital part in tumor immune escape, angiogenesis, and metabolic regulation, the definite mechanisms remain unclear.

High intratumoral lactate accumulation represents a feature of metabolic alterations in CRC and may serve as a potential prognostic indicator, although current evidence remains preliminary. Elevated lactate levels reflect enhanced glycolytic flux and correlate with tumor aggressiveness, recurrence, and shorter DFS. This association suggests that lactate may reflect a more aggressive tumor phenotype and could potentially be explored as a prognostic biomarker in CRC. Clinically, lactate quantification in tumor tissue or plasma could complement existing prognostic parameters and help stratify patients for risk-adapted treatment. In the present study, patients were stratified into high- and low-lactate groups using the median lactate level as the cut-off. This approach was adopted to provide an unbiased and distribution-independent stratification in the context of an exploratory, single-center cohort with a limited sample size, and to avoid potential overfitting associated with data-driven optimization. We acknowledge that the median-based cut-off does not necessarily represent an optimal clinically actionable threshold. More rigorous outcome-oriented methods, such as receiver operating characteristic curve analysis, as well as validation in independent cohorts, will be required to determine clinically meaningful cut-off values for lactate.

Based on the results of RNA-seq, differentially expressed genes associated with lactate-related metabolic alterations were identified. Given that tumor metabolic phenotypes are shaped by key glycolytic regulators, such as LDH and HK, we sought to understand the molecular players governing the tumor-supportive role of lactate in CRC cells. To this end, we further validated selected lactate-responsive genes by qRT-PCR. Notably, HK2 and VEGFA were among the genes upregulated at the transcriptomic level following lactate exposure. Previous studies have shown that dysregulation of these genes is associated with activation of tumor-promoting pathways such as NF-κB, PI3K-Akt, EGF, IGF-I, and HIF-1α [20]. Previous studies have reported that lactate may enhance HIF-1α-dependent transcriptional programs, including VEGF expression, potentially through Akt-related signaling pathways [21]. These observations are consistent with the pathway enrichment patterns identified in our transcriptomic analysis. Importantly, the pro-tumorigenic effects of lactate were further confirmed in both SW480 and HCT116 cells, indicating that the observed phenotype was not restricted to a single CRC cell line. Moreover, pH modulation experiments showed that these effects could not be explained simply by extracellular acidification, and pharmacological inhibition of NF-κB or HIF-1α attenuated lactate-induced malignant cellular behaviors. Together, these results extend the transcriptomic observations by providing functional support for the involvement of NF-κB/HIF-1α signaling in lactate-driven CRC phenotypes.

GPR81 is widely present in colon cancer, breast cancer, lung cancer, hepatocellular carcinoma, salivary gland cancer, cervical cancer and pancreatic cancer cell lines, and admittedly, the high expression of GPR81 in pancreatic cancer patients reaches 94% [22]. Previous studies have suggested that lactate may signal through GPR81 in certain cancer types, contributing to metabolic adaptation and therapy resistance [23]. What’s more, GPR81 can regulate the expression of MCT1 and MCT4 [24]. As the important lactate transporters, MCT1/4 play an integral role in metabolic adaptation, metabolic symbiosis, and maintenance of the metabolic phenotype of tumor cells [25–28]. Clearly, upregulation of MCT1/4 has been found in tumor cells of various human malignancies such as lung cancer, breast cancer, renal cancer and melanoma, which may be related to the regulation of ERK-MAPK/AMPK signaling pathway [29, 30].

GO enrichment analysis showed that HK2 participates in the HIF-1α signaling pathway. HIF-1α and HIF-2α can activate a variety of genes in tumor tissues, such as HK2 and promote the conversion of glucose into pyruvate [31, 32], leading to the transformation of tumor metabolism to the Warburg effect [33], finally resulting in the production of high concentrations of lactate in the extracellular matrix around growing tumors. Inevitably, the expression level of HIF-1α is significantly correlated with the depth of tumor invasion, lymph node metastasis, clinical stage, degree of differentiation and distant metastasis [34]. HIF-1α signaling pathway has been implicated in the regulation of numerous processes, including chemokines and tumor angiogenesis [35]. Because tumor angiogenesis depends on the activation and migration of endothelial cells, anti-tumor therapy targeting angiogenesis has emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy. VEGF plays a key role in the process of tumor angiogenesis. To illustrate, a study retrospectively analyzed 152 gastric cancer patients of different stages and detected that LDH5 overexpression, an independent predictor of poor prognosis, was significantly associated with upregulation of HIF-1α and VEGF [36]. In our study, HIF-1α emerged as a particularly relevant pathway. Consistent with the transcriptomic enrichment results and the upregulation of genes such as HK2 and VEGFA, pharmacological inhibition of HIF-1α with PX-478 significantly attenuated the lactate-induced enhancement of malignant cellular behaviors in CRC cells. These findings support the notion that HIF-1α is not merely associated with lactate-related transcriptional changes, but functionally contributes to the pro-tumorigenic effects of lactate in CRC.

Treatment with lactate altered the expression of TNFSF and TNFRSF family genes, such as TNFSF14, TNFSF10, TNFRSF1B, TNFRSF11A and TNFRSF10D. It is proved that TNFSF/TNFRSF members regulate cell differentiation, survival and programmed death, but their most critical functions are related to the immune system [37]. In other words, both innate and adaptive immune cells are controlled by TNFSF/TNFRSF members in a manner that is critical for coordinating the various mechanisms that drive co-stimulation or co-suppression of immune responses [38]. In addition, our transcriptomic analyses revealed enrichment of NF-κB and HIF-1α signaling pathways in lactate-treated CRC cells. Together with the finding that BAY 11–7082 and PX-478 attenuated lactate-induced malignant phenotypes, these data provide functional support for the involvement of NF-κB- and HIF-1α-associated signaling in mediating lactate-driven effects in CRC cells.

Lactate accumulation has been widely reported to play important roles in tumor progression across multiple cancer types, including breast, lung, and melanoma, mainly through metabolic adaptation, immune modulation, and hypoxia-associated transcriptional programs. In contrast, the role of lactate in colorectal cancer remains comparatively less well characterized, particularly with respect to integrated analyses linking clinical outcomes with transcriptome-level alterations. In this context, our study provides CRC-specific evidence by integrating clinical prognosis, in vitro phenotypic assays, and transcriptomic profiling, together with validation in an additional CRC cell line, pH modulation experiments, and pathway inhibition assays, thereby extending observations from other malignancies into CRC. Collectively, these findings provide clinical, transcriptomic, and functional support for further exploration of therapeutic strategies targeting lactate metabolism in CRC.

Several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. First, the clinical analyses were based on a single-center cohort with a limited sample size, which may reduce statistical power and limit generalizability. Independent validation in larger, multi-center cohorts will be required to confirm the prognostic relevance of lactate in CRC. Due to the limited sample size, multivariate survival analyses were not performed in the present study, and the prognostic associations observed should therefore be interpreted as exploratory. Second, although the inclusion of an additional CRC cell line together with pH-control and pathway-inhibition experiments has strengthened the robustness of our findings, the study is still mainly based on in vitro evidence. Further in vivo validation and investigation in clinically relevant models are needed before the therapeutic implications of targeting lactate-related pathways in CRC can be fully established. Taken together, these limitations highlight the need for future studies integrating larger clinical cohorts and more extensive biological validation.

In summary, lactate accumulation is associated with CRC progression, poor prognosis, and broad transcriptomic changes. Additional validation in SW480 and HCT116 cells confirmed that lactate promotes malignant cellular behaviors. pH modulation experiments showed that these effects were not merely due to extracellular acidification, and pharmacological inhibition further supported the involvement of NF-κB and HIF-1α signaling. Together, these findings support lactate as an active contributor to CRC progression and a potential biomarker and therapeutic target.
