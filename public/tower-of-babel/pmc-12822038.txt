Effect of anesthetic nasal mask on hypoxemia in elderly patients undergoing gastroscopy with deep sedation: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Huan Liu, Huisheng Wu, Lili Tang, Yangxi Zhao, Yunping Shi, Sichen Zhang, Quan Fang, Yinguang Fan, Xuesheng Liu, Peipei Guo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe risk of hypoxemia is increased in elderly patients who are sedated during gastroscopy. This can be attributed to the physiological changes associated with aging, such as reduced oxygen reserves and an increased sensitivity to sedatives. This study aimed to evaluate whether an anesthetic nasal mask is more effective than a traditional nasal cannula at preventing hypoxemia during gastroscopy in elderly patients.Methods300 participants were randomly allocated into traditional nasal cannula group or anesthetic nasal mask group (150 patients in each group). Deep sedation was achieved with intravenous propofol, titrated to maintain spontaneous breathing. The primary outcome was the incidence of hypoxemia (defined as SpO₂ ≤ 92%). Secondary outcomes included the incidence of severe hypoxemia (defined as an intraoperative SpO₂ of ≤ 75% at any time or a hypoxemia duration of ≥ 60 s), duration of hypoxemia, minimum intraoperative SpO₂, incidence of emergency airway interventions, incidence of gastroscopy interruption, procedure duration, recovery time, total propofol dose, satisfaction scores from endoscopists, anesthesiologists and patients, and other adverse events.ResultsCompared with traditional nasal cannulas, using an anaesthetic nasal mask could significantly reduce the incidence of hypoxemia (45.0% vs. 15.5%, p < 0.001). It also increased the minimum intraoperative SpO₂ (from 94.0% to 99.0%; P < 0.001) and reduced the need for jaw thrust (from 37.6% to 13.5%; P < 0.001). Satisfaction levels among endoscopists and anesthesiologists were significantly greater in the anesthetic nasal mask group (P < 0.01). However, there were no significant differences between the two groups in terms of the incidence of severe hypoxemia.ConclusionsElderly patients undergoing gastroscopy with deep sedation experienced a significant reduction in the incidence of hypoxemia when anesthetic nasal masks were used.Trial registrationThe trial was registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (https://www.chictr.org.cn, ChiCTR2100053388, registered on 20/11/2021).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12876-025-04543-1.Keywords: Gastroscopy, Nasal mask, Hypoxemia, Airway management, Sedation

Background

Gastrointestinal endoscopy (GIE) is an important method for diagnosing and treating gastrointestinal diseases [1, 2]. With ongoing economic development and increasing health awareness, the number of patients undergoing GIE has been steadily increasing each year. For example, around 17.7 million GIE procedures are carried out each year in the United States, with approximately 98% of these being performed under sedation [3, 4]. The pursuit of patient comfort and rapid recovery has made sedation without endotracheal intubation a popular choice. This approach reduces patient discomfort, thereby improving cooperation and overall satisfaction. It also provides optimal working conditions for the endoscopist and facilitates rapid patient recovery [5–7].

Hypoxemia is common during gastroscopy with deep sedation [8, 9]. This phenomenon can be attributed to several factors. Firstly, dose-dependent respiratory depression can be caused by anesthetic drugs such as propofol. Upper airway collapsibility may be increased by the relaxation of the pharyngeal muscles and a reduction in airway tone [10]. Secondly, insertion of the gastroscope through the oropharynx into the esophagus and stomach may cause upper airway obstruction and compression of the trachea [11]. Furthermore, the presence of the gastroscope in the oropharynx makes it more difficult for anesthesiologists to manage the airway. Preventing hypoxemia is crucial, as severe episodes may necessitate emergency airway intervention and result in respiratory or cardiac arrest [12–14].

To minimize the risk of hypoxemia, supplemental oxygen can be administered via a variety of devices during sedated gastroscopy [8, 15, 16]. Face masks are predominantly used during colonoscopy, as they interfere with gastroscopy procedures. Although nasopharyngeal airways can reduce the incidence of hypoxemia in obese patients, their use carries a risk of airway injury [17]. Although high-flow nasal cannula (HFNC) and bilevel positive airway pressure (BiPAP) ventilation are effective, their use remains limited by cost and equipment availability [16, 18]. Consequently, the nasal cannula—being minimally invasive and cost-effective—remains the most commonly used oxygen delivery method during gastroscopy.

However, the nasal cannula delivers only low-flow oxygen and lacks the capacity to provide positive pressure, limiting its effectiveness in patients prone to upper airway collapse during sedation. In contrast, anesthetic nasal masks form a seal around the nasal cavity and can be connected to a standard anesthesia breathing circuit, allowing the delivery of high-concentration oxygen and mild positive pressure ventilation when necessary. This dual function enables rapid correction of hypoventilation or mild airway obstruction without interrupting the endoscopic procedure.

Elderly patients are particularly prone to developing hypoxemia during deep sedation, primarily because of age-related physiological alterations. These alterations include decreased lung compliance, increased closing volume, and reduced respiratory muscle strength [19]. At present, clinical evidence on the use of anesthetic nasal masks during gastroscopy with deep sedation in elderly patients at risk of hypoxemia remains limited. Therefore, we designed this study to compare the clinical effectiveness of anesthetic nasal masks versus traditional nasal cannula oxygen delivery in elderly patients undergoing gastroscopy with deep sedation. We hypothesise that, among elderly patients undergoing gastroscopy with deep sedation, using an anaesthetic nasal mask significantly decreases the incidence of hypoxemia compared with using a traditional nasal cannula. We also evaluated several secondary outcomes: the incidence of severe hypoxemia, the need for airway interventions or procedure interruptions, as well as satisfaction scores from anesthesiologists, endoscopists, and patients.

Materials and methods

Patients

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Anhui Medical University (approval number: PJ2022-01-10). Before patient enrollment, the trial was registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2100053388, https://www.chictr.org.cn; registered on November 20, 2021) and conducted following the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guidelines, with written informed consent acquired from all participants prior to their enrollment.

This prospective, randomized controlled trial was conducted at the First Affiliated Hospital of Anhui Medical University. Although the registered protocol included participants across different age groups (young, middle-aged, and elderly), the present analysis focused exclusively on elderly patients (> 65 years). Eligible elderly patients scheduled for gastroscopy with deep sedation were recruited between February 15, 2022, and December 31, 2024. Inclusion criteria were: (1) over 65 years of age; (2) American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status I–III; (3) scheduled for gastroscopy with deep sedation; and (4) capacity and willingness to provide informed consent. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) severe cardiovascular or pulmonary disease (as defined in Table S1); (2) known allergies to anesthetic agents; (3) nasal disorders; (4) acute pharyngitis, tonsillitis, or severe obstructive sleep apnea syndrome; (5) myocardial infarction or unstable angina within the past six months; (6) procedures requiring extensive water injection or lasting > 1 h; and (7) refusal to participate.

Anesthesia management

Randomization was conducted after confirmation of patient eligibility and informed consent but before entry into the endoscopy suite. A computer-generated randomization list assigned patients in a 1:1 ratio to the anesthetic nasal mask group or the nasal cannula group. Sequentially numbered, sealed, opaque envelopes were used to ensure allocation concealment. These were opened by the attending anesthesiologist immediately before the procedure. The randomization personnel remained independent of the study design, patient recruitment and data collection processes. During the procedure, anesthesiologists recorded SpO₂ and perioperative events in real time using paper case report forms. Although anesthesiologists, endoscopists, and patients could not be blinded to group allocation, all participating clinicians received standardized training on the study protocol, including sedation procedures, patient monitoring, and proper use of the anesthetic nasal mask or nasal cannula. Data analyses were conducted by independent assessors who were blinded to group allocation and had no access to the case report forms, thereby minimizing potential bias.

Anesthesia management

All patients fasted and abstained according to guideline before the procedure. Upon arriving in the procedure room, the patient was positioned in accordance with standard endoscopy protocols. An intravenous line was established, and standard vital signs were continuously monitored using a multiparameter monitor (iPM7, Mindray Bio-Medical Electronics Co., Ltd., Shenzhen, China). All anesthetic procedures were conducted by a deputy chief anesthesiologist with substantial clinical experience. Gastroscopy procedures were performed by endoscopists with a minimum of five years of experience. A standardized sedation regimen was applied, involving intravenous administration of 5 µg of sufentanil (Yichang Humanwell Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., Yichang, China), followed two minutes later by a propofol (Fresenius Kabi Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) injection at a dose of 1–2 mg/kg. The depth of sedation was assessed using the Modified Observer’s Assessment of Alertness/Sedation (MOAA/S) scale, which ranges from 0 to 5 points [20]. The gastroscope was inserted once achieving the target sedation depth, which is indicated by loss of the eyelash reflex and an MOAA/S score of ≤ 1 [21, 22]. During the procedure, intermittent boluses of propofol (10–30 mg per dose) were administered to maintain a target MOAA/S score of ≤ 2 until completion of the examination. Intraoperative hypotension (mean arterial pressure < 70 mmHg) was managed with fluid resuscitation and 5 mg intravenous ephedrine, while bradycardia (a heart rate of less than 50 beats per minute) was intravenous atropine 0.5 mg. Before induction of sedation, both groups received 1 min of preoxygenation via a traditional nasal cannula (Nantong Aohua Medical Supplies Co., Ltd., Nantong, China) at an oxygen flow rate of 5 L/min. After sedation was initiated, patients in the observation group were switched to an anesthetic nasal mask for oxygen delivery (oxygen flow rate: 5 L/min) (Fig. 1). Hypoxemia was managed according to a standardized stepwise protocol. When a decrease in oxygen saturation was detected, the oxygen flow was first increased. If the hypoxemia was not reversed, the upper airway obstruction was relieved by performing a jaw thrust maneuver. If this maneuver failed to restore adequate oxygenation, mask-assisted ventilation was initiated. Endotracheal intubation was performed if the above measures did not reverse hypoxemia.

Anesthetic nasal masks (Jiangsu Nhwa Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd, Xuzhou, China) were offered in a range of sizes to fit different body types: small, medium, and large. Selecting the appropriate size ensures an effective seal over the nostrils.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome was the incidence of perioperative hypoxemia. Although hypoxemia is typically defined as SpO₂ < 90%, a threshold of 92% was adopted based on previous studies [16, 23].

The secondary outcome measures included the following: (1) Incidence of severe hypoxemia, defined as SpO₂ ≤ 75% at any time or hypoxemia lasting ≥ 60 seconds during gastroscopy; (2) Duration of hypoxemia; (3) Intraoperative minimum SpO₂; (4) Incidence emergency airway interventions (jaw thrust, mask-assisted ventilation, or tracheal intubation); (5) The interruption rate of endoscopic procedures; (6) Gastroscopy duration and anesthesia recovery time (time to achieve a MOAA/S score of 5 after the gastroscopy); (7) The total propofol dose (mg); (8) Adverse events were assessed by an independent investigator who was blinded to group allocation (coughing, hiccups, and intraoperative body movement were recorded during the procedure, while epistaxis, nasal dryness, sore throat, nausea, vomiting and dizziness were observed in the recovery room); (9) Satisfaction was assessed by three parties: endoscopists and anesthesiologists immediately after the procedure, and patients before discharge. These were conducted using a 100-point visual analogue scale. A score of ≥ 90 representing ‘complete satisfaction’ [24].

Sample size

Research indicates that approximately 40% of elderly patients experience hypoxemia during traditional nasal cannula oxygenation [16, 23]. Assuming that the use of an anesthetic nasal mask would reduce this incidence to 20%, α was set at 0.05, power at 0.9, and the dropout rate at 20%. In a two-sided test, patients were randomized. The calculated sample size was 274 participants, as determined using PASS software (version 11.0, NCSS, LLC, Kaysville, Utah, USA). To allow for variation in circumstances, we ultimately enrolled 300 patients, with 150 allocated to the anesthetic nasal mask group and 150 to the nasal cannula group.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 23.0. Quantitative data that was normally distributed was expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD), and differences between groups were compared using a Student’s t-test. Data from a skewed distribution was presented as using the median (P25, P75), and groups were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Qualitative data were presented as frequencies. Groups were compared using a chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test. Ranked data groups were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test.

A sensitivity analysis using a stricter definition of hypoxemia (SpO₂ < 90%) was performed to verify the robustness of the findings. A binary logistic regression analysis was performed to control for potential confounders. Independent variables included group (anesthetic nasal mask vs. traditional nasal cannula), gender, age, BMI, Mallampati classification (1–2 vs. 3–4), and procedure time. Adjusted odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated, with a P value < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Primary, sensitivity analysis, and secondary outcomes

The incidence of hypoxemia was significantly lower in the anesthetic nasal mask group than in the nasal cannula group (15.5% vs. 45.0%; 95% CI, 4.44 [2.56 to 7.69]; P < 0.001; Table 2). A sensitivity analysis based on the standard definition of hypoxemia (SpO₂ <90%) confirmed the reliability of the primary finding (10.1% vs. 31.5%; 95% CI, 4.09 [2.16 to 7.72]; P < 0.001). No significant differences were observed between the groups in terms of the incidence of severe hypoxemia (4.7% vs. 9.4%; 95% CI, 2.09 [0.82 to 5.33]; P = 0.12) or duration of hypoxemia (30.0 vs. 25.0 s; 95% CI, 3.0 [− 6.0 to 13.0]; P = 0.487). A significantly higher minimum intraoperative SpO₂ was recorded in the anesthetic nasal mask group than in the nasal cannula group (99.0% vs. 94.0%; 95% CI, 3.0 [2.0 to 5.0]; P < 0.001; Table 2).

The need for jaw thrust was significantly lower in the anesthetic nasal mask group than in the nasal cannula group (13.5% vs. 37.6%; 95% CI, 3.85 [2.17 to 6.86]; P < 0.001; Table 2). Fewer patients in the nasal mask group required increased oxygen flow to treat hypoxemia than in the nasal cannula group (15.5% vs. 45.0%; 95% CI, 4.44 [2.56 to 7.69]; P < 0.001). Multivariate analysis

In the multivariate logistic regression analysis, using a nasal mask was still linked to a lower risk of hypoxemia than a nasal cannula (OR = 5.52, 95% CI, 5.52 [3.04 to 10.01]; P < 0.001). The female gender, higher BMI, and longer procedure duration were all significantly increased the risk of hypoxemia, whereas age and Mallampati classification did not prove to be significant predictors (Fig. 3).

Discussion

In the setting of deep sedation, the reduction in hypoxemia observed with the anesthetic nasal mask is particularly consequential. Deep sedation inherently produces dose-dependent respiratory depression, reduced pharyngeal muscle tone, and a heightened propensity for upper airway collapse [25, 26]. These physiologic vulnerabilities are particularly pronounced in elderly patients, who often exhibit diminished functional residual capacity and limited compensatory respiratory reserve [19]. Under these conditions, even brief episodes of hypoventilation or obstructive apnea can precipitate rapid and clinically significant hypoxemia. By establishing a secure nasal seal, the anesthetic nasal mask ensures delivery of a higher and more consistent inspired oxygen concentration, even when partial airway obstruction occurs. More importantly, its connection to the anesthesia breathing circuit enables the immediate application of manual positive-pressure ventilation without interrupting the endoscopic procedure. This feature provides a distinct advantage during deep sedation, where the interval between the onset of hypoventilation and critical hypoxemia is short and conventional nasal cannula oxygenation offers no means of ventilatory support. The combination of stable oxygen delivery and the ability to provide prompt, transient ventilatory assistance likely accounts for the significantly higher minimum SpO₂ values recorded in the mask group.

The application of anesthetic nasal masks correlates with a significant decrease in emergency airway interventions. Simple airway management strategies like increasing oxygen flow and jaw thrusts effectively improve oxygenation, reducing the need for mask-assisted ventilation and interruptions to gastroscopy procedures. The reduced incidence of airway interventions and procedural interruptions likely contributed to higher satisfaction levels among both endoscopists and anesthesiologists in the anesthetic nasal mask group. There was no significant difference in the incidence of adverse events between the two groups, and the use of an anesthetic nasal mask did not increase the incidence of postoperative nasal discomfort complications. The findings indicate that the anesthesia nasal mask is effective and safe for oxygen delivery to elderly patients undergoing gastroscopy under deep sedation.

Multivariate logistic regression analysis revealed that female patients were at an increased risk of hypoxemia, consistent with prior observations under deep sedation protocols [27]. Previous studies have shown that women have higher baseline respiratory rates and lower functional residual capacity, as well as a slower metabolism of sedatives and opioids. These factors may increase their susceptibility to sudden drops in oxygen saturation during apnoea or hypoventilation [28, 29]. These findings suggest that sex may modulate the risk of hypoxemia, highlighting the importance of incorporating sex-specific physiological differences into sedation strategies to enhance patient safety.

HFNC is increasingly used to deliver oxygen during sedation for gastrointestinal endoscopy, as it provides heated, humidified oxygen at high flow rates and generates mild positive airway pressure to alleviate hypoxemia [30, 31]. However, its use necessitates specialized equipment, trained personnel, and incurs additional costs, and some patients may experience nasal discomfort or dryness [32]. In contrast, anesthetic nasal masks are compatible with standard anesthesia breathing circuits, delivering both oxygen and mild positive pressure ventilation when necessary. This makes them a practical alternative for improving oxygenation and facilitating airway management during deep sedation for gastrointestinal endoscopy.

Although a formal cost-effectiveness analysis was not conducted in this study, the practical economic benefits of the anesthetic nasal mask merit consideration. The cost of this device falls between that of traditional nasal cannulas and HFNC. The results of this study show a significant reduction in hypoxemia incidence and the need for emergency airway interventions. This suggests the anesthetic nasal mask is a cost-effective option. The higher initial cost of the anesthetic nasal mask, compared to a traditional nasal cannula, could be offset by the prevention of adverse events and improved procedural efficiency. Thus, this device provides a practical and potentially cost-effective solution to enhance patient safety during gastroscopy with sedation.

The incidence of hypoxemia in our study was higher than that reported in previous studies [16, 23], likely due to differences in patient populations and definitions. Our participants were elderly, which increases their susceptibility to hypoxemia. Additionally, we used a more sensitive definition of hypoxemia (SpO₂ ≤ 92%), whereas previous studies often defined hypoxemia as SpO₂ < 90% [33, 34]. This study determined the hypoxemia threshold based on two main criteria: First, this threshold provides higher sensitivity for detecting clinically significant oxygenation impairment. Second, it enables early intervention for hypoxic conditions in elderly patients, thus reducing the risk of perioperative complications.

The anesthetic nasal mask used in this study was equipped with end-tidal carbon dioxide (EtCO₂) monitoring capability, which could theoretically reduce the incidence of hypoxemia by facilitating the early detection of hypoventilation [35, 36]. However, due to equipment limitations in the procedure room, this function could not be utilized in the present study. This limitation may have resulted in underdetection of early hypoventilation episodes, as changes in SpO₂ typically occur later than those in EtCO₂ [37]. Consequently, the incidence of mild or transient hypoventilation may have been underestimated, potentially affecting the accuracy of respiratory event assessment. Future studies should specifically evaluate the clinical feasibility of integrating EtCO₂ monitoring into this anesthetic nasal mask design.

This study has several limitations. Firstly, small sample size means that our findings cannot be generalised and must be confirmed in larger, multicenter studies. Secondly, sedation depth was assessed solely using the MOAA/S score, which may have introduced observer bias. The absence of objective monitoring, such as the bispectral index (BIS), limited the accuracy of sedation assessment and may have influenced the interpretation of associated physiological responses. Thirdly, due to the significant visual differences between the anesthetic nasal mask and nasal cannula, it was not feasible to blind the anesthesiologists, endoscopists, or data recorders. To minimise bias, the analysis of objective data was carried out by independent assessors who were unaware of the group allocation. However, subjective outcomes such as satisfaction scores may still have been influenced by the lack of blinding, despite being these assessments were performed by investigators who were not involved in anesthesia management or aware of the study hypotheses. Fourthly, no standardized postoperative assessments of gastric distension or aspiration-related complications were conducted. Although no clinical aspiration events were observed in the recovery room, the absence of structured postoperative evaluation precluded a comprehensive assessment of potential risks associated with nasal mask ventilation, such as gastric insufflation or aspiration. Future studies incorporating postoperative imaging or symptom scoring are warranted to characterize these safety outcomes more thoroughly. Therefore, clinicians should exercise caution when applying this device in high-risk patients. Finally, potential selection bias should be acknowledged, as patients with severe cardiovascular, pulmonary, or other systemic comorbidities were excluded from this study. This could affect generalisability to higher-risk populations, who may experience different oxygenation and airway challenges.

This study employed a deep sedation protocol under continuous anesthesiologist supervision and airway preparation, which differs from the guideline-recommended moderate sedation for routine gastroscopy. Nevertheless, recent randomized controlled trials have demonstrated that deep sedation can be safely performed under adequate monitoring, supporting the rationale for our protocol [16, 38]. Although our findings suggest that anesthetic nasal masks are valuable for optimising oxygen delivery and enhancing patient safety in clinical practice, they may not immediately change current guideline recommendations. Nevertheless, this discovery provides supporting evidence for considering alternative oxygenation strategies, particularly for high-risk populations, and further research is needed to determine the most effective approach. Future multicenter studies comparing anesthetic nasal masks with guideline-endorsed oxygen delivery methods could inform subsequent updates to clinical practice guidelines. Based on our results, the anesthetic nasal mask may be regarded as an intermediate oxygen delivery modality between the traditional nasal cannula and HFNC systems, offering an optimal balance of efficacy, safety, and practicality for elderly or high-risk patients undergoing deep sedation.

Conclusions

The use of an anesthetic nasal mask significantly reduces the incidence of hypoxemia in elderly patients undergoing gastroscopy with deep sedation, without increasing adverse events, and enhances endoscopist and anesthesiologist satisfaction.
