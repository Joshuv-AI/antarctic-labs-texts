The effect of the web-based Barkley behavioral training model on the caregiving burden and resilience of mothers of children with attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Parisa Rahmani, Naghmeh Razaghi, Sakineh Soltani Kouhbanani, Fatemeh Moharrari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMothers of children with attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) often face considerable challenges in communicating with their children, which increases their caregiving burden. Resilience, as an important psychological resource, can help mitigate the negative effects of these challenges. Barkley’s behavioral parent training, especially when delivered web-based with easy access and flexible scheduling, may provide valuable support for these mothers. This study examined the impact of a web-based behavioral training program based on Barkley’s model on caregiving burden and resilience in mothers of children with ADHD.MethodsIn this randomized controlled trial, 56 mothers of children with ADHD were randomly allocated to the intervention and control groups (28 participants in each group). The intervention group received eight web-based multimedia training sessions based on the Barkley model. Demographic, resilience, and caregiving burden questionnaires were completed at baseline, post-intervention, and one-month follow-up. Four participants from each group withdrew, and statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 26.ResultsDemographic characteristics did not differ significantly between the intervention and control groups. At baseline, no significant between-group differences were observed in caregiving burden (p = 0.425) or resilience (p = 0.897). Immediately after the intervention, a significant increase was found only in resilience in the intervention group (p = 0.015, d = 0.73), while the between-group difference in caregiving burden remained non-significant (p = 0.063). One month after the intervention, significant between-group differences were observed in both caregiving burden (p = 0.001, d = 1.01) and resilience (p = 0.002, d = 0.97) in favor of the intervention group. Overall analyses showed significant main effects of time and group, and a significant time × group interaction, for both outcomes.ConclusionThese findings suggest that low-cost, accessible, and flexible web-based training can support mothers in managing stress, improving coping skills, and enhancing mental health, and may serve as a practical alternative to in-person parent training. Larger studies with longer follow-up are recommended to confirm these results.Clinical trial registrationThis randomized controlled trial was registered in the Iranian Registry of Clinical Trials under the code IRCT20250322065141N on May 15th, 2025 (https://en.irct.ir/trial/82591).Keywords: Caregiving burden, Resilience, Parent-training, Web-based intervention, ADHD

Introduction

Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) is one of the most common behavioral disorders in children [1], characterized by hyperactivity, impulsivity, distractibility, restlessness, talkativeness, difficulties in task organization, and leaving tasks unfinished, leading to significant functional impairments [2]. According to the National Health Survey (2020–2022), its prevalence among children aged 5–17 years was 11.3%, higher in boys (14.5%) than girls (8.0%) [3]. Iranian studies report a prevalence of 9–15% [4], while a survey in Shiraz found 5.8%, and 30–40% of children referred to counseling centers in Iran were diagnosed with ADHD [2, 5].

Children with ADHD face multiple challenges: 30–50% experience learning difficulties, 52% have problems with peer relationships, they are 2.8 times more likely to have speech difficulties than other children, and 25–50% suffer from emotional and sleep-related disorders [6–10]. Compared with their peers, they report lower health-related quality of life and significant impairments in physical, emotional, social, and academic functioning [11]. The impact of ADHD extends beyond the child and affects family functioning and the well‑being of caregivers [12]. Families often experience considerable psychological, social, and financial strain, which may lead to physical and emotional exhaustion and increased conflict in family interactions due to the child’s behavioral difficulties [1, 2, 13]. These pressures contribute to caregiving burden, defined as the negative physical, psychological, social, and economic consequences of caregiving stress [14]. Among caregivers, mothers generally experience higher levels of stress and worry than fathers because they spend more time with the child and take greater responsibility for managing the condition [5, 15].

In Iranian society, where cultural expectations place primary responsibility for childcare on mothers, this role often leads to additional psychological strain and role conflict [16, 17]. As a result, mothers of children with ADHD report higher caregiving burden, greater anxiety, lower perceived competence and resilience, and higher levels of psychological distress, often with limited family support [1, 5, 15, 18].

Resilience refers to an individual’s ability to maintain biopsychological balance in the face of adversity [19]. It is a key concept in positive psychology and represents the dynamic process of effectively adapting to difficult or traumatic experiences [4]. Baruth and Carroll’s resilience framework views resilience as a dynamic, multifactorial process encompassing both internal factors (e.g., coping skills and self‑efficacy) and environmental factors (e.g., social support and family functioning), and the application of this theory in the present study is appropriate [20]. As a positive coping mechanism, resilience enables individuals, particularly mothers caring for a child with ADHD, to manage stress [4]. Studies indicate that families of children with ADHD show low and vulnerable levels of resilience [21]. The prevalence of mood disorders among these mothers is estimated at 36%, including 27% with chronic depression and 9% with bipolar disorder [5]. These difficulties can negatively affect marital relationships, parent–child interactions, and overall family well‑being [22].

Previous research has shown that challenging ADHD‑related behaviors in children are associated with elevated caregiving burden and reduced resilience. Behavioral parent-training programs have demonstrated positive effects on parenting style and stress outcomes by improving parent‑child interaction and enhancing parents’ coping self‑efficacy [23, 24]. Accordingly, in this study, we selected Barkley’s behavioral parent-training, as it specifically targets the behavioral mechanisms most related to parental burden, and evidence suggests its potential in reducing stress and enhancing parental competence [25].

The Barkley behavioral parent training program, developed in 1997 for parents of children aged 2–12 years with ADHD, is implemented by parents in the child’s natural home environment [26]. Barkley’s model conceptualizes ADHD primarily as a disorder of self-regulation, executive functioning, and behavioral inhibition, emphasizing observable child behaviors that disrupt family functioning [27, 28]. By equipping parents with structured behavior management strategies that can be consistently applied in the child’s natural home environment, this approach aims to reduce child noncompliance and negative behaviors, thereby alleviating parental stress and caregiving burden and supporting resilience. Recent attention to digital delivery formats further supports the applicability of this intervention by increasing accessibility and facilitating the practical implementation of evidence-based parenting strategies in everyday family contexts [2, 26]. In recent years, delivery of parent-training through digital platforms has also received increasing attention [29].

Web-based learning delivers educational content online, offering flexibility and accessibility [30]. However, reduced interaction, low motivation, need for self-discipline, and limited access to devices or the internet can hinder participation [31–33]. Despite these challenges, the web‑based Barkley behavioral parent-training was chosen for its practical advantages, including accessibility, flexibility, and scalability, while maintaining effectiveness comparable to in-person programs [34, 35].

Although the Barkley intervention has been shown to reduce mothers’ anxiety and depression, increase parental awareness, and enhance motivation for behavioral change [36, 37], as well as improve children’s ADHD symptoms [38], the effects of web-based delivery on caregiving burden and resilience have not yet been studied in the Iranian socio-cultural context. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of a web-based Barkley-based behavioral parent-training program on caregiving burden and resilience among mothers of children with ADHD.

Methods

Study design and setting

In this randomized clinical trial, 56 mothers of children aged 6 to 9 years diagnosed with ADHD were initially enrolled; however, due to attrition during the study, the final analysis was conducted on 48 participants who completed the intervention. The diagnosis was confirmed by a child psychiatrist using the Conners Parent Rating Scale. Participants were recruited from clients referred to the pediatric clinic of Ibn Sina psychiatric hospital, Mashhad, Iran, based on predefined inclusion criteria, and were assigned to the intervention or control group using block randomization with blocks of four.

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the School of Nursing and Midwifery Ethics Committee at Mashhad University of Medical Sciences. The trial was registered in the Iranian registry of clinical trials. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the CONSORT guidelines for reporting clinical trials and describing interventions [39, 40]. Data collection was performed between June and August 2025, and statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS software (version 26.0) in September 2025.

Assessments were conducted using self-report questionnaires completed by mothers at three time points: baseline: before the intervention (in person), post-intervention: immediately after completing the intervention (web-based via the ePoll-based assessment platform), and one-month follow-up: one month after completion (web-based via the same platform).

Participants and inclusion criteria

Participants were mothers of children aged 6–9 years with a confirmed diagnosis of ADHD, formally diagnosed by a child psychiatrist or clinical child psychologist based on DSM-5 criteria or other validated diagnostic tools. Eligible children were required to have the combined type of ADHD, exhibiting both hyperactivity and attention deficits, as confirmed by the same professional and documented in their medical records. Children were included if they: Were between 6 and 9 years of age (This age range was selected because it corresponds to the typical school-entry period, when ADHD-related behaviors and attention difficulties are more clearly observable and diagnosable. At this age, children’s cognitive and social development allows better understanding of and adherence to parental guidance, making behavioral training for mothers more efficient, and they had a confirmed diagnosis of ADHD without comorbid disabling conditions (e.g., physical or intellectual disability, or autism spectrum disorder) and were receiving medication as prescribed by their child psychiatrist. To rule out autism, all children were screened with the Gilliam Autism Rating Scale (GARS), and negative results were confirmed for each participant.

Eligible mothers were required to: be the child’s primary caregiver and fully responsible for their care; have sufficient physical and mental health to care for the child; provide written informed consent to participate; be literate in Persian and able to communicate effectively; and own and be able to operate a smartphone. Mothers were excluded if they had a history of substance use or dependence on psychoactive drugs, had previously participated in similar educational programs, withdrew their consent, were absent from more than two educational sessions, or developed physical or psychological problems that prevented continued participation.

Sample size calculation

The sample size was estimated using G*Power software (version 3.1) [41], based on the study by Haji Seyed Javadi et al. (2014) [42]. Given that the primary objective was to compare the means of two independent groups (intervention and control groups), an independent-samples t-test was used as the basis for the power analysis [41]. Assuming a significance level of α = 0.05, a statistical power of 1-β = 0.80, and an effect size of Cohen’s d = 0.98, the required sample size was calculated to be 36 participants (18 per group). The selected effect size was derived from the findings of Haji Seyed Javadi et al. (2014), who reported large between-group effects following a comparable parent training intervention [42]. Considering a potential attrition rate of up to 50%, the final sample size was increased to 56 participants (28 per group), ensuring sufficient statistical power to detect significant differences between groups. Randomization and blinding

Participants were randomly assigned to either the intervention group (n = 28) or the control group (n = 28) using block randomization generated through the Random Allocation Software. To maintain balance across potential confounding variables (e.g., child’s age and gender), participants were stratified into blocks of four, which were then randomly allocated to each group. Each block was numbered, and the sequence of allocation ensured group balance. Upon enrollment, one block was randomly selected, and participants were assigned according to the predetermined order. Randomization was conducted by an independent researcher who was not involved in recruitment or data collection. Outcome assessors were blinded to group allocation to minimize bias (single masked design).

Ethical considerations

All participants were informed about the study objectives, procedures, potential risks, and benefits. Written informed consent was obtained from each participant before inclusion. Confidentiality and anonymity were ensured throughout the study, and participants were free to withdraw at any time without consequences.

Instruments and data collection tools

The data collection instruments included three standardized questionnaires and an informed consent form. The questionnaires consisted of a demographic questionnaire, the Zarit Burden Interview (ZBI), and the Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale (CD-RISC).

Informed consent form

After selecting eligible mothers according to the inclusion criteria, the researcher provided oral and written explanations of the study’s objectives, procedures, and ethical considerations. Participants were assured of the anonymity of their responses and the confidentiality of all collected data. They were encouraged to answer all questions carefully and honestly. Written informed consent was obtained before data collection.

Demographic questionnaire

The demographic questionnaire included nine items related to maternal characteristics such as age, education level, marital status, employment status, and place of residence, as well as child-related characteristics including age, gender, birth order, and family history of ADHD.

Zarit Burden Interview (ZBI)

The Zarit Burden Interview was initially developed by Zarit et al. (1986) to assess caregiving burden. It consists of 22 self-report items completed by mothers of children with ADHD. Each item is rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (“never”) to 4 (“always”). The total score ranges from 0 to 88, with higher scores indicating a greater caregiving burden [43].

Luppa, Zarit, Smith, Mintz, and Lieberman (2003) confirmed the validity and reliability of the ZBI [44]. In Iran, Pahlevanzadeh, Novidian, and Yazdani (2010) reported a test-retest reliability coefficient of 0.94 and established construct validity through significant positive correlations with the Hamilton Anxiety Scale (r = 0.88) and the Beck Depression Inventory (r = 0.67) [45].

In the present study, the Cronbach’s alpha for the ZBI was 0.78 across 22 items, indicating acceptable internal consistency.

Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale (CD-RISC)

The Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale was developed by Connor and Davidson (2003) based on research conducted between 1979 and 1991. It consists of 25 items rated on a 5-point Likert scale from 0 (“not true at all”) to 4 (“true nearly all the time”). The total score ranges from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating greater resilience [46]. The CD-RISC measures five subscales: personal competence, tolerance of negative affect and stress, individual adequacy, positive acceptance of change and self-control, and spiritual influences. In a recent Iranian study, Sharifnia et al. (2023) confirmed the validity and reliability of the Persian version, reporting Cronbach’s alpha values above 0.70 [47]. In the current study, all subscales demonstrated Cronbach’s alpha coefficients above 0.73. The Cronbach’s alpha values for the subscales were as follows: perception of competence = 0.79; trust in one’s instincts and tolerance of negative affect = 0.77; positive acceptance of change and secure relationships = 0.88; control = 0.74; and spiritual influences = 0.73, confirming acceptable internal consistency.

Intervention

Initially, the researcher met with the administrative and clinical staff of the Pediatric Clinic at Ibn Sina Psychiatric Hospital to explain the study objectives and obtain approval for collaboration. Participants who met the inclusion criteria and provided written informed consent completed the baseline demographic questionnaire and the caregiving burden and resilience questionnaires in person.

Control group

Mothers in the control group received the routine care provided by the pediatric psychiatric clinic for their children, which included medication management, periodic physician visits, and access to standard educational pamphlets.

Intervention group

The mothers in the intervention group (n = 28) were divided into two subgroups of 14 participants each, and an educational group was established on the Iranian messaging platform Eitaa, which provided a secure, culturally appropriate environment for virtual learning and group interaction. The group members consisted of a pediatric nurse with a master’s degree, a child psychologist with a PhD, and a child psychiatrist with a subspecialty in child psychiatry, all of whom had professional experience working with children.

The intervention was based on Barkley’s behavioral parent-training model and was delivered over eight 45-minute sessions spanning four weeks. Each week, two educational sessions were provided to mothers as audio files (podcasts), accompanied by written transcripts in PDF format. At the end of each week, mothers were assigned homework based on the two sessions conducted that week. They submitted their assignments as images, audio files, or written chats to the pediatric nurse. The nurse, in turn, confidentially shared the assignments with the child psychologist and the child psychiatrist to ensure the training’s effectiveness and to provide key feedback to the mothers.

To enhance engagement and participation, two content-based competitions were held during the intervention period, with winners receiving mobile data packages as prizes. One-month follow-up phase

A follow-up phase was conducted for one month after the completion of the intervention [37]. During the one-month follow-up period, both groups remained active in their respective Eitaa application groups; however, some participant attrition occurred in each, and no structured interactions or intervention-related content were provided in the web-based groups. In the control group, four participants were lost to follow-up: two withdrew voluntarily for personal reasons, and two were unreachable despite repeated phone calls and messages. In the intervention group, four participants did not complete the follow-up; one withdrew voluntarily for personal reasons, and three were excluded due to inconsistent engagement in the intervention sessions.

Due to limited opportunities for in-person contact and the wide geographic dispersion of participants, outcome assessments were conducted exclusively at the end of the follow-up period using the web‑based ePoll platform to ensure consistency in data collection. This approach enabled comprehensive and timely data collection without geographical constraints. Data obtained from participants who completed the study were included in the per-protocol analysis and subsequently analyzed using SPSS version 26. Participant flow and reasons for attrition are presented in the CONSORT flow diagram (Fig. 1).

Data analysis

Upon completion of data collection, all data were entered into SPSS version 26 for analysis, with statistical significance set at 0.05. Descriptive statistics, including absolute and relative frequency distributions, measures of central tendency, and measures of dispersion, were used to categorize and summarize the findings. To achieve the primary objectives of the study, inferential statistical analyses were conducted using appropriate tests, including the independent t-test, chi-square test, repeated measures analysis of variance, and Bonferroni post-hoc test, to compare data both between and within groups.

Normality assumptions were evaluated using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov and Shapiro-Wilk tests. Effect sizes were calculated using Cohen’s d for pairwise comparisons and partial eta squared (η2 p) for repeated‑measures ANOVA. According to standard guidelines, Cohen’s d values of 0.2, 0.5, and 0.8 represent small, medium, and large effects, respectively [49, 50]. For η2 p, values of 0.01, 0.06, and 0.14 were interpreted as small, medium, and large effect sizes [51].

Results

Demographic and clinical characteristics

At baseline, the demographic and clinical characteristics of participants were examined to ensure homogeneity between the intervention and control groups. Statistical analysis was conducted according to the per‑protocol approach on the 48 participants who completed the intervention (24 in the intervention group and 24 in the control group), while those who did not complete the study were excluded from the final analysis (Fig. 1). As shown in Table 2, the mean age of mothers in the intervention group was 34.35 ± 3.24 years, and in the control group, 36.00 ± 3.61 years. This difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.113). The mean age of children in the intervention group was 7.38 ± 1.14 years, and in the control group, 7.50 ± 1.18 years, showing no significant difference between the two groups (p = 0.709). Similarly, the distribution of variables such as child gender, marital status, maternal employment, maternal education level, place of residence, birth order, and family history of hyperactivity showed no significant differences between groups, indicating that the two groups were homogeneous in baseline characteristics.

Caregiving burden and resilience

As presented in Table 3 and Fig. 2, no significant between‑group differences were observed at baseline in the mean scores of caregiving burden (p = 0.425, d = 0.23) or resilience (p = 0.897, d = 0.03). Immediately after the intervention, the between‑group difference in caregiving burden remained non‑significant (p = 0.063, d = 0.55), whereas resilience showed a significant improvement in the intervention group (p = 0.015, d = 0.73), corresponding to a medium‑to‑large effect. At the one‑month follow‑up, significant and large between‑group differences emerged in both caregiving burden (p = 0.001, d = 1.01) and resilience (p = 0.002, d = 0.97).

Trends in the mean scores of caregiving burden and resilience among participants over time

Within‑group analyses showed a significant reduction in caregiving burden across time in the intervention group (p = 0.001, η2p = 0.305), while no meaningful change was observed in the control group (p = 0.396). Bonferroni post‑hoc comparisons indicated significant reductions from baseline to post‑intervention (A) and from post‑intervention to follow‑up (B). The RM‑ANOVA revealed significant main effects of time (F (1.929, 46) = 10.144, p = 0.001, η2p = 0.181), group (F (1, 46) = 4.516, p = 0.031, η2p = 0.097), and a significant time × group interaction (F (1.929, 46) = 4.927, p = 0.013, η2p = 0.089).

A similar pattern was observed for overall resilience. The intervention group demonstrated significant improvement across the three assessments (p = 0.002, η2p = 0.324), with Bonferroni post‑hoc analyses confirming significant increases from baseline to immediate post‑intervention (A) and from post‑intervention to follow‑up (B). No significant change was found in the control group (p = 0.415). RM‑ANOVA detected significant effects of time (F (1.026, 47.197) = 9.660, p = 0.001, η2p = 0.174), group (F (1, 46) = 4.875, p = 0.012, η2p = 0.129), and time × group interaction (F (1.026, 47.197) = 3.003, p = 0.025, η2p = 0.082).

Across all resilience subscales, no baseline differences were detected. In the personal competence subscale, the intervention group exhibited significant within‑group improvement (p = 0.048, η2p = 0.148), which Bonferroni tests confirmed for baseline to post‑intervention (A). Significant between‑group differences were also present at both post‑intervention assessments (p = 0.001; d = 0.97 and d = 1.07). RM‑ANOVA identified a significant main effect of group (F (1, 46) = 18.394, p = 0.001, η2p = 0.286), while the interaction effect was not significant (p = 0.216).

For trust in one’s instincts and tolerance of negative affect, the intervention group demonstrated significant improvement over time (p = 0.010, η2p = 0.225). Bonferroni post‑hoc results indicated significant increases from baseline to post‑intervention (A) and from post‑intervention to follow‑up (B). However, none of the between‑group comparisons reached significance. RM‑ANOVA showed a significant main effect of time (F (1.802, 82.870) = 7.674, p = 0.001, η2p = 0.143), with no significant group or interaction effects.

In the positive acceptance of change and secure relationships subscale, the intervention group showed significant within‑group improvement (p = 0.019, η2p = 0.183), with Bonferroni analysis confirming a significant increase from baseline to post‑intervention (A). However, no significant between‑group differences were detected at any time point (p = 0.109–0.554), and RM‑ANOVA did not reveal significant effects of time, group, or interaction.

No significant changes were observed in the control subscale in either group (intervention: p = 0.453; control: p = 0.736), and all between‑group comparisons remained non‑significant (p = 0.673–1.000). RM‑ANOVA likewise found no significant main or interaction effects. In contrast, significant improvement was found in the spiritual influences subscale within the intervention group (p = 0.037, η2p = 0.154). Bonferroni tests indicated significant improvement from baseline to follow‑up (A/B). The control group showed no change (p = 0.987). Between‑group differences were significant at both post‑intervention assessments (p = 0.030, d = 0.66; and p = 0.001, d = 1.08). RM‑ANOVA also demonstrated a significant main effect of group (F (1, 46) = 11.108, p = 0.002, η2p = 0.195), with non‑significant time and interaction effects.

Discussion

This study examined the effect of a web-based Barkley behavioral training model on caregiving burden and resilience among mothers of children with ADHD. The findings showed reductions in caregiving burden and improvements in resilience in the intervention group, both at post‑intervention and at one‑month follow‑up, compared with the control group. Given the chronic nature of ADHD and its wide‑ranging impact on families, these changes are clinically relevant.

The reduction in caregiving burden suggests that the Barkley-based training helped alleviate some pressures associated with caring for a child with ADHD. The structured sessions, focus on understanding the disorder, provision of concrete behavior‑management strategies, and attention to mothers’ self‑regulation may have enhanced mothers’ perceived control and confidence in their parenting role. Quantitatively, caregiving burden scores decreased after the intervention and remained stable at follow‑up. This is consistent with Friedman et al. (2017), who reported that pharmacological treatment alone does not sufficiently address caregiving burden and highlighted the need for complementary non‑pharmacological interventions [52], and with Amro and Tantillo (2025), who emphasized the importance of supportive parenting programs [53].

Previous research by Mousavi et al. (2019), Lohrasbi et al. (2023), Behzadi et al. (2022), and Reisi et al. (2021) similarly indicates that web-based educational interventions can reduce caregiving burden among caregivers of individuals with chronic illnesses or parents of ill children [54–57]. This convergence of evidence indicates that the effects of web-based interventions extend beyond the ADHD domain and that virtual-training can serve as an effective and accessible strategy for supporting caregivers across contexts. Together with the current results, this body of evidence suggests that structured web-based programs may reduce caregiving stress by increasing knowledge, fostering a sense of control, and decreasing feelings of isolation.

Some studies have reported less favorable results for remote interventions. For example, Tse et al. (2015) found that remote interventions were less effective than in‑person programs in reducing caregiving burden [58], and Söderqvist et al. (2017) reported no significant reduction in caregiving burden [59], which was likely due to the indirect participation of caregivers in intervention sessions. Our study did not compare in‑person and remote formats, but the web-based Barkley intervention was associated with significant reductions in maternal caregiving burden. Differences in intervention content, delivery, and target populations may partly explain discrepancies with these studies.

The intervention also increased resilience among mothers in the intervention group. Participation in the sessions was associated with improved understanding of the child’s behavior, more effective management of difficult situations, and higher perceived self‑efficacy. Quantitatively, the “perception of competence” and “spiritual influences” subscales improved significantly, while “trust in one’s instincts and tolerance of negative affect” showed a non‑significant trend toward improvement. The large effect size observed for “spiritual influences” at one‑month follow‑up (d = 1.07) should be interpreted cautiously. It may reflect cumulative benefits of ongoing practice and coping strategy use, as well as sample‑specific characteristics, but the study was not designed to isolate mechanisms underlying this subscale, and the finding requires replication before firm conclusions can be drawn.

Our results are consistent with Sasmarrini et al. (2024), Zabihollahzadeh et al. (2020), Bahrami and Khalifi (2021), and Khoshnoud et al. (2023), who reported positive effects of psychological and educational interventions on the resilience of parents and caregivers [60–63]. In contrast, Heydarian et al. (2023) found that the effects of virtual interventions on specific resilience dimensions were unstable [64]. Differences in intervention structure, intensity, and opportunities for skill practice may contribute to these mixed findings.

The persistence of intervention effects at one‑month follow‑up is another notable finding. Mothers continued to show lower caregiving burden and higher resilience scores compared with baseline. The one-month follow‑up phase, which involved active participation in analyzing hypothetical scenarios in web-based groups (e.g., via the Eitta platform), may have supported continued application of learned skills. This pattern is in line with Kuhlthau et al. (2020) [65], who found that the effects of virtual resilience interventions persisted for up to three months, and with Luo et al. (2021), who reported maintained benefits of mobile‑based resilience training for up to six months [66]. However, the present study included only a one‑month follow‑up; longer follow‑up periods are needed to determine the durability of these effects.

The potential mechanisms underlying the observed effects of this intervention could be characterized as follows: the Barkley model provides mothers with a structured, skill-oriented framework that helps them better understand their child’s behaviors, apply more effective behavioral management strategies, and ultimately experience greater competence and efficacy in their parenting role. This process, in turn, reduces caregiving burden, enhances the quality of the mother-child relationship, and fosters increased resilience.

Limitations and recommendations

This study is subject to several limitations. The findings may have been influenced by respondent bias, recall errors, or social desirability tendencies. Moreover, the absence of objective assessment tools such as direct behavioral observations of mothers or biological indicators (e.g., stress hormone levels) may have limited the validity of the findings, as incorporating such measures could have provided more robust evidence. In addition, the use of both in-person and web-based data collection methods may have introduced response-related bias due to differences in assessment conditions. Future studies are recommended to adopt a uniform data collection approach, either entirely in-person or entirely web-based, to minimize potential bias.

Another limitation is that the study was initially designed using a per‑protocol approach, meaning that the data required for a comprehensive intention‑to‑treat (ITT) analysis were not collected. This may have increased the risk of attrition bias and affected the estimation of the intervention’s effectiveness. Therefore, future research is recommended to employ an ITT analytical framework. Considering the methodological aspects of the study design, including the sample size, short follow-up period, and reliance on self-report measures, the findings should be interpreted with caution and regarded as preliminary. Additionally, the relatively small sample size limited the statistical power and generalizability of the findings. The sample size in this study was estimated using an independent t-test in G*Power. Future studies could benefit from sample size estimation methods based on ANOVA. Providing internet data packages as prizes may have introduced motivational bias, and future studies could consider using other alternative incentives. Environmental factors, including natural changes in the child’s condition and variations in family support, might also have contributed to the observed outcomes.

In light of these limitations, future research is recommended to examine the effects of this intervention on other family members, including fathers and siblings of children with ADHD, to achieve a more comprehensive understanding of its effectiveness. Conducting longer-term follow-up assessments at three, six, and twelve months would help determine the durability of intervention effects, and comparative studies evaluating the Barkley model against other educational or therapeutic approaches, such as positive parenting programs, mindfulness-based parent training, neurofeedback, and yoga, could further advance the generalizability and applicability of the findings. Additionally, future studies should investigate the effect of the Barkley training model across diverse populations of children and parents, accounting for variables such as child age, gender, and parental characteristics, including occupation and educational level, to strengthen the external validity of the results.

Conclusion

Overall, the findings suggest that the web-based Barkley behavioral training model can provide effective support for mothers of children with ADHD. The results highlight the importance of low-cost, non-pharmacological educational interventions and demonstrate that web-based training can serve as a practical alternative to in-person sessions under time or location constraints, as well as a scalable approach for expanding educational programs in schools, counseling centers, and specialized clinics. Moreover, this training model represents a promising pilot that requires confirmation in larger trials with longer follow-up periods and may serve as a foundation for future research aimed at integrating family-centered interventions.
