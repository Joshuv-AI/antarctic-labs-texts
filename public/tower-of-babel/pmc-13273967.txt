Cost of implementing tailored motivational interviewing training in youth HIV clinics
Authors: Karen Kolmodin MacDonell, Kit N Simpson, Lisa Todd, Amy Lee Hall, Sylvie Naar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMotivational interviewing (MI) may improve HIV outcomes in adolescents and emerging adults, but competence in delivering MI is critical. Although providers may receive introductory exposure to MI through workshops or didactic sessions, achieving competence requires more rigorous, structured training. Tailored Motivational Interviewing (TMI), is a set of targeted implementation strategies integrated into MI training to tailor it for adolescent HIV care providers and improve competence.MethodsUsing a retrospective analysis of program training, personnel, and performance records, the cost to train beyond beginner MI competency was calculated using a practice perspective, and tested by sensitivity analysis.ResultsThe cost of training a staff member to intermediate or expert MI competence was estimated at $6,286 for the base model.ConclusionsTMI had per-provider costs comparable to other MI training models, supporting feasibility and sustainability for youth HIV clinic implementation.Trial registrationThe main study is registered at clinicaltrials.gov in the National Library of Medicine with the ID NCT03681912. (Registration date: 2018-09-07).Keywords: Implementation science, Motivational interviewing, Cost analysis, HIV, Staff training

Background

Young people ages 13–24 are considered a key HIV population in the U.S. with the poorest outcomes along the HIV prevention and care continua [1]. Behavioral intervention research has increasingly focused on behavior change for primary and secondary HIV prevention, but the full benefits that should be possible for young people have yet to be realized. Multiple behavioral interventions targeting adherence, retention in care, and risk reduction among youth with HIV have demonstrated efficacy under controlled conditions, yet challenges remain in translating these approaches into routine clinical practice [2–9]. One reason for this is efficacious, evidence-based interventions have not been successfully implemented in real-world settings like youth HIV clinics. There are considerable barriers to the effective implementation of these interventions in these settings, including the need to balance flexibility (adaptation to context of the setting) and fidelity to the intervention (provider adherence and competence).

Motivational interviewing (MI) is a collaborative, goal-oriented style of communication that includes specific strategies to elicit intrinsic motivation and reinforce the language of change [10]; moreover, MI is the only behavioral intervention for youth to show success across the HIV prevention and care cascades [11]. There is growing evidence that MI competence is linked to positive patient outcomes [12]. Although providers may attain introductory exposure to MI through brief workshops or didactic sessions, most do not achieve intermediate or advanced competence without structured training that includes skill rehearsal, performance feedback, and ongoing coaching [13]. Thus, implementation science studies to test workforce development approaches to improve MI competency are a high priority.

Our team developed, Tailored Motivational Interviewing (TMI), a set of implementation strategies integrated into MI training to tailor it for adolescent HIV clinics. The goal was to improve MI competence in HIV youth care providers and address HIV-related target behaviors in adolescents and emerging adults ages 13–24. The study (ATN 146) utilized a type 3 hybrid implementation-effectiveness trial to examine the effect of TMI on fidelity to MI, using a dynamic wait-listed design [14] with an average of 15 youth HIV care providers at each of the 10 HIV clinical sites (subject recruitment venues) in the United States for a total of 150 providers [7]. On average, each provider saw 100 patients. Within the stepped wedge design, provider MI competence during the TMI implementation period was compared with pre-training baseline assessments and demonstrated significant improvement, with smaller reductions observed during the sustainment window [15].

While the primary trial [15] focused on implementation-effectiveness and provider competence outcomes, understanding the financial investment required to achieve and sustain competence is critical for broader dissemination. The current study therefore examines the cost of implementing TMI using a provider training model in youth HIV clinics. This analysis contributes to the limited literature on the cost of implementing evidence-based interventions in clinical settings, particularly the cost of training HIV care providers to MI competence relative to other structured training approaches.

Methods

Study design and setting

This manuscript reports the cost analysis component of a broader hybrid type 3 implementation effectiveness trial, the primary outcomes of which have been reported previously [15]. Participants (N = 151) were youth HIV medical providers, psychologists or social workers, or other providers (e.g., health educator or peer counselor) across 10 clinics that were part of the Adolescent Medicine Trials Network for HIV/AIDS Interventions (ATN). All procedures were approved by a university institutional review board. Participants reviewed an information sheet and provided verbal consent to enroll in the study. Clinics received a $3,000 incentive to be used at their discretion and individual participants received a $10 gift card for completing role plays to assess MI competence. In the stepped wedge design, 10 clinics were randomly assigned to receive TMI in clusters. All participants completed a 12-month implementation period with assessments every 3 months for 36 months in total. The duration of the follow-up and sustainment periods varied by randomization status.

Training components

All providers completed training in TMI during the implementation phase. Training consisted of in-person 10-hour group workshops delivered by a member of the Motivational Interviewing Network of Trainers. Participants completed 2 mandatory 1-hour individual coaching (remote) sessions immediately following the workshop, as well as 4 quarterly assessments (15–20 min each) using a standard patient role play and a tailored feedback report.

MI competence

The primary outcome, MI competence, was assessed using the Motivational Interviewing Coach Rating Scale [16], a 12-item measure rated on a 4-point scale (beginner, novice, intermediate and advanced) that can be used by MI coaches and researchers to rate real or simulated clinical encounters. Participants who scored in the “Beginner” or “Novice” range completed a mandatory coaching session that was optional for those who scored in “Intermediate” or “Advanced” levels.

Implementation support

Throughout the study, local implementation teams (“iTeams”) provided support for TMI implementation. Following the implementation and follow-up periods, clinics no longer received assessment feedback, coaching, or iTeam facilitation. During this sustainment period, clinics received funded support for internal activities to promote the program.

Costing approach

The present study used a retrospective analysis of program training, personnel, and performance records. The cost perspective is that of a clinic or health system, using resource counts extracted from the programmatic records to calculate the amount of human and other resources used in the preparation for training, actual training meetings, and for the measurement of performance and fidelity. Cost weights were calculated from national wage levels for the actors and combined those with time estimates for the training and performance tasks to estimate the mean cost per trained person as well as the mean cost per unit of improvements. Our study data come from 10 clinical sites that were part of the implementation intervention, which improves the external validity of our approach. The wage and other cost weights are nationally representative and based on data from the National Bureau of Labor and Statistics (NBLS; https://www.bls.gov/). Fringe benefit rates were not available at the NBLS so we used an average rate of 30% base pay to calculate weighted hourly costs for each type of provider. The effects of variations in costing assumptions were tested in a sensitivity analysis using Excel modeling. A copy for the model is available on request from the first author.

Results

Youth HIV care providers and clinics

Providers were categorized into medical providers (n = 58), mental health providers (n = 26) or other (health educators/community outreach; n = 67) with an average of 15.1 enrollees per clinic. Hourly pay with fringe was estimated as $107.92 for medical providers, $37.76 for mental health providers, and $27.12 for other providers. Each of the ten clinics received a $3,000 incentive for participating, totaling $30,000 in clinic incentive payments. After completing all required role plays, each provider also received a $10 gift card for a total of $1,510.

Training staff cost

Staff time costs reflected personnel involvement across preparation, workshop delivery, coaching, assessment, and sustainment activities. Costs for training were estimated as staff costs based on mean length hours and mean attendees (Table 1). Twenty-five pre-workshop iTeam meetings at 1.5 h each with 5 attendees cost an estimated $11,247. Baseline role plays (n = 549) were estimated at 0.5 h of staff time, totaling $16,466. The TMI workshop staff time (156 staff in total) was estimated to cost $112,292. The two mandatory coaching sessions were completed by 130 and 87 participants, respectively, with accompanying costs totaling $5,849 and $2,609. Implementation roles plays (n = 463) were estimated to take 30 min of staff time and total $13,887. Post-workshop iTeam meetings (n = 71 meetings) with an average of 5 participants cost $21,295. Finally, sustainment role plays (n = 390) totaled $11,697. The total estimated cost for training staff was $195,341.

Trainer cost

Costs for compensating MI trainers and coders were also calculated. Coach compensation was estimated to total $23,468, workshop registration cost $46,644, and travel cost $3,900. The hourly wage for the TMI coach was $100, while the average base salary for a MI Coach was estimated at (Glass Door) $69,326 or $45.06 per hour. Roleplay scoring (n = 853) cost $25 per session or $21,325. Trainer and coder costs totaled an estimated $116,067.

Cost per staff trained

The total cost of implementing the TMI program was estimated at $342,919. This equates to $6,286 cost per staff member to train to intermediate or expert MI competence level. The costing approach and Excel model used for estimating TMI costs per staff member can easily be applied to other training needs. The Excel model is available from the primary author by request. Several cost components, including trainer travel and workshop registration, represent fixed costs that may not vary substantially by clinic size. In contrast, staff time costs scale proportionally with the number of providers trained. Sensitivity analysis showed that increasing the mean hourly staff cost by 10% increased the Cost per Trainee-Year by 6%. An increase of 10% of the Trainer Staff components increased the Cost per Trainee-Year by 3%, and a 10% increase in clinic incentives increased Cost per Trainee-Year by 1%.

Discussion

A limited number of behavioral HIV treatment intervention studies with youth have included cost or cost-effectiveness analysis. Thus, it is difficult to determine if the TMI approach was more economical than other clinic-based behavioral interventions for youth with HIV. We can assume that improving HIV medication and treatment adherence, a primary aim of the TMI intervention program, will reduce lifetime costs for patient HIV care. The annual cost of initial antiretroviral treatment regimens for most people with HIV are estimated to cost $36,080 to $48,000 in the United States, with costs far outpacing the overall inflation rate even after the availability of generic medication options [17]. However, cost is driven by not only by the price of medication, but also adverse events and comorbidity treatment costs cumulated due to longer survival times from diagnosis [18]. There is evidence that despite improvements in available HIV treatments, lifetime HIV costs are substantially increasing. Long-term cost projections are influenced by cumulative medication exposure, treatment interruptions, and comorbidity burden over the life course. Patterns of stopping and restarting antiretroviral therapy have been documented among some populations and contribute to increased long-term healthcare utilization and cost [19, 20]. As HIV treatments continue to evolve and life expectancy with HIV increases, it is even more critical that young people with HIV remain adherent to HIV treatment regimens over the long-term.

Evidence from other settings suggests that MI skills can be retained over time when structured training, feedback, and ongoing support are provided. For example, a one-year follow-up study of lay HIV/AIDS counsellors in South Africa demonstrated that changes in MI competence resulting from a brief training course were maintained and in some cases enhanced one year later, indicating longer-term retention of training effects [21]. Meta-analytic evidence further shows that training gains tend to erode over time among participants who receive only workshop training, whereas inclusion of post-workshop feedback and coaching is associated with sustained MI skills over follow-up periods [22]. In the current study, although some attenuation in competence was observed during the sustainment period, reductions were smaller than initial gains, consistent with partial maintenance of skills over time. Taken together, these findings support the premise that structured implementation strategies that combine initial training with follow-up coaching and feedback may facilitate longer-term MI skill acquisition in clinical settings.

The TMI program cost $6,286 per staff member to train to intermediate or expert MI competence level. TMI is comparably priced to other MI training programs for clinicians, yet goes one step beyond to train beyond beginner competence level in MI. Much of the cost studies in MI we can use for comparison are from addiction treatment programs. For example, Olmstead, Carroll [23] evaluated cost and cost-effectiveness of three strategies for training community programs clinicians in MI to provide addiction treatment. They found total costs ranging from $6,141 for self-study (clinicians received materials only), to $28,020 for expert led and $49,217 for a train-the-trainer approach. The latter two approaches included skill-building workshops and three monthly supervision sessions. Self-study was the most cost-effective strategy, but expert-led was the most costly but also the most effective to reach a pre-determined training competency threshold. It cost an estimated $3,250 to train a clinician to meet MI performance standards using the expert approach. However, this was based on 2006 dollars. Converting to 2024 US dollars (https://www.usinflationcalculator.com/), the cost would be approximately $4,972.

TMI represents a clinic level training investment with costs comparable to other structured MI training approaches reported in the literature. It has been shown to be cost-effective when considering patient costs for HIV care, particularly for non-adherent youth, with costs that are comparable to other MI-training programs for clinicians [12]. However, there are several limitations to the current study. Cost of the TMI program was based on estimates for salary levels and other assorted costs and could vary by geographical region and/or time. Because competence was reassessed during scheduled quarterly assessments rather than immediately following coaching sessions, we cannot determine the short-term impact of individual coaching encounters on provider skill improvement. Additionally, though MI competence has been linked with better patient outcomes, patient- or clinic-level HIV-related outcomes (e.g., reductions in viral load) were outside the scope of the present study. As a result, we cannot assess if TMI implementation by MI-competent health providers resulted in improved patient outcomes. Moreover, the costs estimated in this study reflect training and implementation expenditures incurred at the clinic or system level rather than direct patient out-of-pocket costs. Potential downstream cost offsets associated with improved adherence would primarily accrue to healthcare systems and payers.

Conclusions

MI is an evidence-based communication approach associated with improved behavioral outcomes among youth with HIV. There is growing evidence that MI competence is linked to positive patient outcomes [12], but quality of MI of often inconsistent in practice. In the present study, we trained multidisciplinary HIV care providers to intermediate or advanced MI competence at a cost comparable to other structured MI training models. These cost estimates may inform planning and budgeting for implementation efforts in youth HIV clinic settings. Overall, TMI may be a feasible and sustainable approach to implementing high-quality MI in youth HIV clinics towards the goal of better patient outcomes.
