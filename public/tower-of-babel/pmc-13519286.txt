Experiences of Integration of HIV, Hepatitis, and Sexually Transmitted Infection Services Across Different Countries Worldwide: A Scoping Review
Authors: Parya Jangipour Afshar, Vahid Yazdi‐Feyzabadi, Zahra Abdolahinia, AliAkbar Haghdoost, Reza Goudarzi, Katayoun Tayeri, Babak Eshrati, Hamid Sharifi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsIntegrating services for diseases appears to improve healthcare access and outcomes. This scoping review aimed to evaluate how different countries implement the integration of HIV, viral hepatitis, and sexually transmitted infection (STI) services.MethodsThis scoping review follows Arksey and O'Malley's five‐step framework. We searched PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science until April 2025, with no limitations in languages and time. Background

Integrated healthcare services aim to streamline service delivery by coordinating two or more health programs while maintaining distinct operational functions, such as staffing, service delivery, or location [1]. Hofman et al. defined integration as “a formalized, collaborative process among service systems to decrease fragmentation of care and improve coordination. Integration is comprehensive and flexible, enabling decisions and actions that are responsive over time to the political climate, shifts in resources, budgets, population dynamics, and emerging public health trends” [2]. “Integrated care is often contrasted to fragmented and episodic care, and it is used synonymously with terms like coordinated care and seamless care, among others” [3]. Although integration is widely promoted as a means to improve healthcare delivery, there is no universally accepted definition, as it encompasses various models such as coordinated care, seamless care, and service consolidation.

Using integrated services is a common approach in different countries. Previous studies have examined the integration of noncommunicable diseases and HIV care, exploring models that combine HIV services with cardiovascular disease, hypertension, and diabetes management [4, 5]. Additionally, different countries' health systems, especially low‐income settings, have integrated priority health, nutrition interventions, and management of noncommunicable diseases [6, 7]. Based on the experiences in different nations, they concluded that integration positively or negatively impacts both the client and the health provider. Evidence suggested that integration can improve service quality [8] and reduce wait times [9, 10]. It can be cost‐effective [11, 12] and improve access to comprehensive care [2, 12, 13]. However, it may also increase the workload of healthcare providers, particularly in resource‐limited settings [14].

Given the similarities in transmission routes, treatment protocols, and target populations, integrating services for HIV, Hepatitis B virus, Hepatitis C virus (HBV and HCV), and sexually transmitted infections (STIs) has been recommended by the World Health Organization (WHO) [15, 16]. It would have been better if there had been more emphasis in the introduction on the fact that integration impacts different aspects of a service and that studies and evidence have assessed these dimensions. However, there is experience of how integration has been carried out and the strategies used. Still, it is scattered, and limited research systematically assesses how different countries implement these integration strategies.

Integration in healthcare can occur across multiple dimensions, including organizational, managerial, clinical, and service‐delivery levels. In practice, integration strategies may involve co‐location of services, coordinated screening programs, referral and linkage systems, shared service delivery platforms, or formal organizational collaboration. Because the terminology and implementation of integration vary substantially across settings, there is no universally accepted operational classification framework.

In this scoping review, we adopted a pragmatic service‐delivery perspective to classify integration models reported in the literature. Horizontal integration refers to the coordination or delivery of related services at the same level of care, such as co‐located or bundled HIV, HBV, HCV, and STI services within a single healthcare platform. However, vertical integration refers to the linkage of services across different levels or functions of care, including screening, referral, diagnosis, treatment, and follow‐up pathways across primary, secondary, or specialized healthcare services. When studies described mixed or overlapping approaches, classification was based on the predominant integration mechanism reported by the authors.

Although integration of HIV, HBV, HCV, and STI services has been recommended by the WHO because of shared transmission routes, overlapping target populations, and similarities in prevention and treatment approaches [15, 16], evidence regarding how integration is operationalized across countries remains fragmented. This scoping review aimed to systematically map global integration approaches, identify the types of integration strategies implemented, and assess how countries have operationalized integrated service delivery models.

Methods

Scoping reviews are systematic literature reviews encompassing various studies with diverse methodologies and designs [17]. For this scoping review, we followed the Arksey and O'Malley framework, which consists of five stages: (1) identifying the research question, (2) identifying relevant studies, (3) selecting studies, (4) charting the data, and (5) collating, summarizing, and reporting the results. The reporting of this scoping review was guided by the preferred reporting items for systematic reviews and meta‐analyses extension for scoping reviews (PRISMA‐ScR).

A comprehensive search was conducted up to April 2025 in PubMed, Web of Science, and Scopus without time or language restrictions. In addition, gray literature sources, including reports and documents from recognized organizations and institutional websites (e.g., WHO, UNAIDS, ministries of health, and governmental or international health agencies), were searched. Manual searches of reference lists were also performed to identify additional relevant studies. The search strategy incorporated keywords related to integration, HIV/AIDS, Hepatitis B and C, STIs, and their synonyms. The complete search strategy for the PubMed database is presented in Appendix S1.

Eligible sources included randomized controlled trials, observational studies, peer‐reviewed reports, and letters to the editor containing relevant original data on the integration of HIV, HBV, HCV, and STI services at any level of healthcare delivery. Non‐peer‐reviewed materials without sufficient methodological details or primary data, study protocols, books, dissertations, review articles, and articles without accessible full texts were excluded.

After removing duplicates, two reviewers (P.J.A., Z.A.) independently screened studies according to predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Screening was conducted in two stages: first by title and abstract, and subsequently through full‐text review of potentially eligible articles. Studies were excluded if they: (1) did not address the integration of HIV, HBV, HCV, or STI services; (2) lacked relevant original data; (3) focused on unrelated health conditions or services; (4) were review articles, study protocols, books, dissertations, or conference abstracts without sufficient methodological details; or (5) had inaccessible full texts despite attempts to retrieve them. If the title and abstract did not provide sufficient information to determine eligibility, the full text was assessed. Disagreements between reviewers were resolved through discussion among the authors. The exclusion of inaccessible full texts was considered a potential source of selection bias and has been acknowledged as a study limitation.

The data extraction process was carried out systematically using a standardized data‐charting form developed by the authors and refined iteratively during the review process. Key details extracted from each study included author(s), year of study completion, publication year, country, study design, type of intervention, community participation components (if applicable), main outcome(s), and type of integration (Appendix S2).

For classification purposes, integration models were categorized using an operational service‐delivery framework developed by the authors. Horizontal integration referred to coordinated or co‐located delivery of related services at the same level of care, whereas vertical integration referred to linkage or coordination across different levels or functions of care (e.g., screening, referral, treatment, and follow‐up). In studies with overlapping or mixed integration approaches, classification was based on the predominant integration mechanism described in the article. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion among the reviewers. No formal protocol was developed or registered for this scoping review.

Results

After eliminating duplicates in databases, 11,790 records remained. We assessed the remaining articles based on their titles and abstracts. Of those, 11,099 articles were discarded, while 691 proceeded to the full‐text review stage. An additional 15 documents were found through reference searches of the included documents. After carefully reviewing the full texts in accordance with the study's goals and criteria, we selected 78 relevant studies (Figure 1).

PRISMA flowchart for selection of documents about the experiences of integrating services of HIV, HBV, HCV, and STIs across different countries worldwide.

The findings indicated that most integration efforts in the fields of HIV, HBV, HCV, and STIs have been primarily vertical, meaning that a service has been supplemented to an existing service (93.5%). In comparison, only 6.5% have been horizontal. Integrated diseases HIV, Hepatitis B, and C, and STIs services based on the continents and countries.

North America

United States

From 1997 to 2005, STI clinics offered viral hepatitis preventive services, including HBV vaccinations [18]. In San Diego, California, in 1998, all clients received HIV counseling and testing (C&T), as well as HBV vaccination and testing, and HCV testing, at the STI clinic [19]. From 1998 to 2003, an HBV vaccination program was added in STI clinics for men who have sex with men (MSM), people who inject drugs (PWID), and other high‐risk groups [20]. In Marin County, California, HCV services were added to the existing HIV center from 1998 to 2002 because they did not have the budget for a separate center to provide hepatitis services and could use the infrastructure and equipment of the HIV center [21]. During the 1999–2002 period in California, HIV, viral hepatitis, STIs, and tuberculosis were integrated into a drug rehabilitation program for at‐risk groups [22].

In Illinois, viral hepatitis prevention was integrated into STI clinics from 1999 to 2005 [23]. In 2000, HIV and HCV services were combined. This included treatment, checking for drug side effects, and following up with patients [24]. Several integration models emerged in the United States during 2000–2002, where the HCV program was incorporated into the HIV program. This was implemented in different states to provide hepatitis services and be cost‐effective for them [25]. Between 2000 and 2005, Texas was one of the first states to address hepatitis C actively, establishing an education and prevention program for HCV and including it in all HIV, STD, and substance addiction programs [26]. In addition, in 2001, HBV services were integrated into STI clinics [27]. In 2003, the California Department of Health Services started to study the integration of HIV and HCV services to attract more PWID to HIV services. Evidence indicated that PWID were more interested in HCV testing than HIV testing [28].

In 2003, in Maricopa County, there was a distinction between STD and HIV prevention programs. Patients who needed HIV testing were referred to a separate HIV C&T program. Then, patients make HIV testing a standard component of medical care, especially in settings where patients are already being treated for STDs [29]. From 1999 to 2005, hepatitis was integrated into STI clinics for high‐risk populations [30], and during 2000–2004 in New York, the HIV site was merged with STI centers that provided free exams, testing, and treatment for STDs (e.g., syphilis, chlamydia, gonorrhea, cervical cancer screening) for IDUs [14]. From 2005 to 2007, people in North Carolina could get tested for TB, HIV, and syphilis at certain spots in the community. These spots were chosen using geographic information system. People were invited to get tested through advertising and offering incentives [31]. In Massachusetts, US, between 2009 and 2011, HIV and HCV prevention and screening services and liver health services for prisoners were integrated [32].

In Guyana, because co‐infection with HIV, HCV, and HBV was common, HCV and HBV testing were added to the HIV service for soldiers in 2011 [33]. In 2012–2014, in Philadelphia, Pennsylvania. All PHMC clinics routinely test for HCV in addition to HIV [34], and in the United States during 2012–2013, HIV and HCV services were integrated for high‐risk groups [35]. In 2012, an urban STD clinic added HCV point‐of‐care testing to its standard procedures [36]. In 2012, a screening program for syphilis, hepatitis B, and HIV was implemented in antenatal services for rural Guatemalan women [37]. From 2012 to 2015, Durham County made HCV screenings available at STI clinics. In addition, they were added to prisons and clinics that provided services to the homeless [38]. From 2014 to 2016, the Mississippi program added HIV testing to its regular syphilis testing services. This was done to find more cases of HIV among people who had syphilis [39]. HIV, STI, viral hepatitis, and TB were merged in services such as prevention, testing, treatment, and case management in Massachusetts from 2014 to 2018 [40].

From 2014 to 2017, King County, Washington, launched a committee for HIV/AIDS and STD prevention and introduced HIV pre‐exposure prophylaxis (PrEP), a notable achievement in HIV prevention measures for high‐risk populations, including MSM [41] during this period, Alabama and Mississippi introduced culturally suitable programs to increase Black men's awareness and access to HIV/HCV testing and care [42]. In Jackson, Mississippi, integrating HIV‐related activities into STDs during 2016–2018 [43]. In New Haven, the HIV center added HCV services to the package of services for patients from 2014 to 2018 [44]. In Baltimore, Maryland, between 2016 and 2018, people living with HIV (PLHIV) who received treatment in HIV care but not HCV care began HCV treatment [9]. From 2017 to 2018, HIV and HCV screening were performed on high‐risk populations and PWIDs [45]. In 2018, a cross‐disciplinary model between HIV, Hepatitis, STIs, and TB was implemented to strengthen both the testing and the services [46]. In 2018, the United States proposed implementing universal HBV vaccinations in STI clinics [47].

Canada

In 2021, syringe service programs in Miami, FL, and Montréal, Québec, Canada, provided integrated HIV PrEP and HCV treatment for PWID [48]. In 2002–2003, British Columbia combined HIV and STD testing and counseling [49].

Jamaica

In 1993, Jamaica Family Planning Services developed an integrated HIV and STI prevention program aimed at encouraging changes in sexual behavior, promoting condom use, and enhancing screening and treatment [50].

Asia

China

In 2004–2009, in Shandong, HIV testing was integrated into clinical STI services for monitoring, allowing female sex workers (FSWs) to have better access to high‐quality HIV/AIDS treatment, care, and support [51]. From 2009 to 2011, a program was implemented to integrate comprehensive structural STI/HIV intervention among FSWs in six cities in South China [52]. In 2010, syphilis and HIV testing were added to the services offered by STI clinics in Hong Kong [53]. Vertical integration of HIV, HBV, and syphilis for pregnant women was completed in 2003–2010, 2013, and 2014 [54, 55]. In China, HIV/Syphilis testing was included in STI clinics between 2009 and 2014 [56, 57]. In 2017, China's National Health Commission began a pilot program in Zhejiang, Guangdong, and Yunnan provinces to improve access to services for HBV and syphilis regarding mother‐to‐child transmission [58].

Vietnam

In 2012–2014, the Vietnam Ministry of Health approved a National Action Plan for integrating HIV, HBV, and syphilis vertically in pregnant women [59]. In 2021, HBV/HCV services were integrated with HIV services for key populations [60].

Myanmar

In 2017, integrated services for the diagnosis and treatment of HCV, HBV, and HIV among key populations were implemented in Myanmar [61]. In 2019–2020, a pilot program was conducted to examine the feasibility and acceptability of integrating HBV and HIV services [62].

India

In India, HCV testing and counseling were included in HIV services for PWID in 2019 [63].

Iran

In 2019, Iran established a unified protocol for people living in temporary residential centers (camps and shelters) for up to 6 months. This protocol serves as a framework for diagnosis, treatment, care, and follow‐up after discharge [64].

Indonesia

In 2018, the Indonesian Ministry of Health introduced its Elimination of Mother‐to‐Child Transmission program. The program involves offering HIV, syphilis, and hepatitis B tests to pregnant women seeking healthcare for the first time at clinics [65]. In 2019–2020, HIV co‐infection with HCV was highlighted as a significant health issue that requires the HCV continuum of care for PLWH treated at an HIV clinic after a free direct‐acting antivirals program [66].

Cambodia

In 2019, the vertical integration of HIV, HBV, and syphilis for pregnant women was done [67].

Africa

Uganda

Uganda initiated a pilot program in 2018 to investigate the feasibility of combining HBV and HIV services [68]. In 2018, the previously distinct services for HIV and HBV, which were offered at two separate centers, were integrated into a single center to deliver comprehensive care for both diseases to patients [69]. In 2020, the West Nile region of Uganda (Arua and Koboko) combined HBV and HIV services [70].

Kenya

In 2017, combining STIs and HIV testing resulted in increased detection of seroprevalence and improved access to care for patients with coinfection [10].

Nigeria

In 2012, participants in Nigeria received an integrated HIV‐STI prevention program [71].

Zambia

STI and HIV/AIDS services were merged in Zambia in 2006 to increase their uptake [72].

Other Countries in Africa

In South Africa, the program for integrating HIV and syphilis testing and treatment into antenatal care services began in 2005–2006 [73]. In Burkina Faso, from 2009 to 2011, an integrated HIV and STIs program was implemented for young FSW aged 18–25. The intervention consisted of teaching sessions, condom distribution, psychological support, and general medical treatment for HIV‐positive and STI patients [74]. In 2017, Africa performed simultaneous HIV, HCV, and HBV detection, emphasizing the importance of early screening for HBV and HCV as part of global health strategies targeted towards eliminating viral hepatitis as a major public health problem [75]. In 2018, STI clinics in Africa performed simultaneous HIV, HCV, and HBV testing [76]. In Gaborone, Botswana, STI testing and treatment were integrated into routine HIV care by 2019 [77]. HIV, hepatitis, and syphilis will be combined in Gaborone, Botswana, in 2021 to improve sexual reproductive health among youth [78].

Europe

France

In 2013, France faced a major health challenge, which was related to the high prevalence of HBV and HCV cases, and they were unaware of their viral status, so HBV and HCV testing were integrated into the HIV services for better screening for hepatitis [13]. People without healthcare coverage like immigrants and people with a high risk of infection, were tested for HBV, HCV, and HIV [79].

United Kingdom

In 2016–2017, Integrated Screening Programs for Migrants for latent TB, HIV, HBV, and HCV as part of their health check upon registration were implemented [80].

Netherlands

From 2009 to 2010, a new program was started. The program was called “Integrated Care Models.” It was a way to see how combining public health care with hospital care for STI/HIV care for HIV patients would work [8]. From 2013 to 2015, a screening project was started in Amsterdam, Gelderland. This project offered voluntary HBV, HCV, TB, and HIV screenings to migrants [81]. In 2018, the program is designed for sexual health issues such as chlamydia, gonorrhea, hepatitis B, syphilis, and HIV [82].

Spain

In Spain, from 2016 to 2017, efforts were made to implement a combined HIV and HCV screening program [83]. In 2018–2019, Lithuania, Romania, and Spain began testing for HCV, HBV, STIs, and HIV in high‐risk populations such as those with alcohol dependency and chemsex. Later, these tests were integrated into healthcare settings to improve coverage [84].

Italy

Between 2010 and 2016, Italy implemented a strict collaboration to improve STI screening and monitoring. Adopted the Unified Diagnose Protocol, which included screening for HIV, HBV, HCV, and syphilis [85].

Belgium

In 2002, STI services in Belgium were connected with other reproductive health and HIV prevention and treatment programs targeting the general population, as well as those at high risk and challenging to reach, such as sex workers and youth [86].

South America

Chile

In 2013, in Santiago, Chile, an online program for young Chilean women was launched for HIV and STI prevention that gave information on these infections, as well as high‐risk behavior [87].

Oceania

Australia

In 2020, in Australia, HIV and STIs (chlamydia, gonorrhea, syphilis) were merged in a Sexual Health Centre [88].

Multiregional

Between 2006 and 2009, in Canada, China, European countries, United States, the pilot program offered many services, including health education, referrals to social services, condom distribution, HIV testing, and self‐collected vaginal swabs for STI testing. It also provided pre and posttest counseling and follow‐up care for community sex workers [89]. From 2014 to 2018, people in Spain, the United States, and California received HIV and HCV care at the same clinics [90].

Discussion

The findings of this review highlighted that in recent years, the integration of HIV, HBV, HCV, and STIs has gained significant attention, with a notable trend towards vertical integration across countries. The most common forms of integration reported were related to HIV and STIs, followed by the integration of HIV and HCV. Furthermore, our results indicated that most integration experiences have been observed in North America and Asia, especially in China. However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously. The higher number of published integration experiences from the United States and China may partly reflect differences in research capacity, publication practices, indexing coverage, and availability of documented health‐system evaluations rather than the actual global distribution of integration initiatives. Similarly, countries with limited research infrastructure or lower representation in indexed databases may have implemented integration activities that remain underreported in the scientific literature.

A prominent trend revealed by this study is the widespread use of vertical integration strategies. Previous reviews on health systems integration have documented a shift from horizontal to vertical integration, alongside a transition from acute‐ or institution‐centered models to community‐based health and social services coordination [91]. This policy shift aims to enhance patient protection and ensure comprehensive service delivery, including physician and hospital services, to patients [92, 93]. Additionally, vertical integration is associated with improved health outcomes, better care, and enhanced system efficiency [91]. In particular, a review of the US healthcare system has found that vertical integration is linked to higher quality and performance [93], as well as better access to continuous healthcare services [94].

The United States and China have reported the most integration programs. While the US healthcare system is one of the most expensive in the world, it faces challenges in providing adequate access, quality, and overall health outcomes [95]. US policymakers and payers, like their counterparts in other countries, are increasingly turning to integrated care models to improve healthcare quality and efficiency [96]. In China, the government has prioritized public health improvement since its establishment in 1950. Initial efforts included education campaigns and enhanced screening and treatment for STIs [97], even before the identification of the first HIV case in 1992. By the 1990s, joint programs began, with significant progress made in integrating Prevention of Mother‐to‐child transmission services with antenatal care in 2003 [51, 54]. Research indicated that these programs have been effective [55]. These developments may partly explain the relatively high number of documented integration initiatives reported from China and the United States.

Our findings also underscore that the integration most frequently involved HIV and STIs, with subsequent integration of HIV and HCV. It is well‐established that STIs play a crucial role in the transmission of HIV. For example, syphilis has been shown to increase the risk of HIV transmission by at least threefold [98], while infections such as Neisseria gonorrhoeae and Chlamydia trachomatis also elevate the risk of HIV transmission [99, 100]. Early sexual activity is a key risk factor for both STIs and HIV, contributing to significant challenges in sexual health and fertility [101]. The rise in these infections poses a threat to the global goal of ending the AIDS epidemic by 2030 [102]. Methods to prevent HIV transmission and STIs are widely recognized and necessitate joint interventions [103], such as screening, education, treatment, and vaccination [51]. Research has shown that integrating sexual health services into existing HIV care programs has led to positive outcomes, including increased condom use, greater use of contraceptives, enhanced STI screening, and improved overall service quality [104, 105].

HCV and HIV share common risk factors and transmission routes, with a high prevalence of coinfection [83]. HCV has been identified as an opportunistic coinfection in PLWH, which can significantly accelerate the progression of HIV [106, 107]. Early diagnosis of HCV in PLWH offers numerous benefits, including a reduction in severe liver complications, such as cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma, that can result from chronic HCV infection [108]. The adoption of a combined screening approach has emerged as a cost‐effective strategy, enabling the use of existing resources to address both epidemics simultaneously and improve access to healthcare for individuals infected with HCV and HIV [35].

The interpretation and implementation of integration models should also consider contextual differences across health systems, including financing structures, governance mechanisms, workforce capacity, infrastructure, and sociopolitical conditions. Therefore, although integrated approaches appear promising, no single integration model is likely to be universally applicable across all settings. Future studies should place greater emphasis on implementation barriers, facilitators, and long‐term effectiveness across diverse healthcare contexts.

Limitations

First, such studies may have limited access to information about the details of these integration processes, which cannot be retrieved through search engines and databases. Second, the limitations of integration concerning the issue of HIV or other infections have some characteristics, such as the fact that in some countries, they are just in the beginning, and that integration is still a new topic for them. Third, integration is influenced by the characteristics of the health system, dispersion, structural characteristics, etc. It has political, structural, technical, and operational aspects, as well as implementation feasibility. These may not be present in many low‐income countries. Fourth, some potentially relevant studies could not be included because their full texts were inaccessible despite attempts to retrieve them. This may have introduced selection bias and resulted in the omission of relevant evidence.

Conclusions

This scoping review suggests that integrated approaches to HIV, HBV, HCV, and STI services have attracted growing attention in recent years, with most published experience originating from high‐income countries. Nevertheless, the evidence highlights ongoing gaps in implementation and evaluation in many low‐ and middle‐income settings, despite the continued high burden of these infections in those contexts. Given the descriptive nature of this review, these findings should be interpreted cautiously. While integrated service delivery approaches appear promising in improving coordination of care and service delivery, their effectiveness is likely to depend on context‐specific health system capacities and implementation conditions. Future efforts may benefit from further strengthening and expanding integration initiatives, particularly in resource‐limited settings, alongside more implementation‐focused research to better understand feasibility, barriers, and outcomes across diverse contexts.
