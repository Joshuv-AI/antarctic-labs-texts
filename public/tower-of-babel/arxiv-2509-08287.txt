Thermodynamic Diagnostics for Complex Langevin Simulations: The Role of Configurational Temperature
Anosh Joseph, Arpith Kumar

Abstract
The complex Langevin method (CLM) is a promising approach to tackle the sign problem in quantum field theories with complex actions. However, it can converge to incorrect results even when simulations appear stable, thus underscoring the need for robust diagnostics. Existing criteria, such as monitoring the drift distribution or the Langevin-time operator, are valuable, but they remain indirect. In this work, we propose a complementary reliability test based on the configurational temperature. It is constructed from the gradient and Hessian of the complex action. Unlike drift-based checks, this estimator directly probes thermodynamic consistency and thus offers a physically interpretable cross-check of CLM dynamics. To demonstrate that the estimator reproduces the input temperature with high precision, we use one-dimensional PT-symmetric models as a controlled benchmark. We show that the estimator also sensitively detects algorithmic errors, step-size artifacts, and incomplete thermalization. While tested in low-dimensional systems, the method is readily extensible to higher-dimensional scalar and gauge theories, where scalable approximations make its implementation practical. Since temperature is tied to the bare coupling in many lattice theories, configurational monitoring can also serve as an independent check on coupling-dependent observables. Our results suggest that configurational temperature can be integrated into a hybrid diagnostic framework, thus enhancing the robustness of CLM across a wide range of applications, including future studies of lattice QCD at finite density.

Introduction

We can systematically study the nonperturbative aspects of quantum field theories (QFTs) through lattice regularizations of the path integral.
Monte Carlo methods provide a robust framework for extracting physical observables in such systems, where the central idea is to generate field configurations with probabilities proportional to the Euclidean weight,
e^{-S}
, and to evaluate observables by statistical averaging over this importance-sampled ensemble.

Difficulties arise, however, when the action becomes complex.
In such cases, the weight factor can no longer be interpreted as a probability distribution, giving rise to the so-called
sign problem
.
This issue appears in many theories of broad physical interest: QCD at finite baryon density, where the fermion determinant acquires a complex phase; QCD with a topological
\theta
-term; Chern-Simons gauge theories with complex couplings; and certain classes of chiral gauge theories.
The inability to directly apply standard Monte Carlo importance sampling in these contexts hampers our ability to probe their nonperturbative dynamics.

The complex Langevin method (CLM)
Klauder:1983nn
;
Klauder:1983zm
;
Klauder:1983sp
;
Parisi:1984cs
;
Damgaard:1987rr
was proposed as a strategy to circumvent the sign problem.
The method extends the framework of stochastic quantization, formulated initially for real actions, to systems with complex actions.
In practice, this involves a complexification of the original dynamical variables of the path integral and the introduction of a stochastic process governed by a Langevin equation with complex drift.
Expectation values of observables in the original path integral are then recovered as equilibrium averages over this stochastic process.
For recent reviews of the method, see
Berger:2019odf
;
Joseph:2025tfw
.

Over the past decade, CLM has been applied successfully to a variety of models
Berges:2005yt
;
Berges:2006xc
;
Berges:2007nr
;
Bloch:2017sex
;
Aarts:2008rr
;
Pehlevan:2007eq
;
Aarts:2008wh
;
Aarts:2009hn
;
Aarts:2010gr
;
Aarts:2011zn
, including relativistic Bose gases at finite chemical potential, low-dimensional QCD-like systems, and spin models at nonzero density.
It has also been employed in the study of supersymmetric matrix models
Ito:2016efb
;
Ito:2016hlj
;
Anagnostopoulos:2017gos
;
Joseph:2019sof
;
Joseph:2020gdh
;
Kumar:2022fas
;
Kumar:2022giw
;
Kumar:2023nya
, and in large-
N
unitary matrix models where it has been used to explore Gross–Witten–Wadia transitions
Basu:2018dtm
;
Gross:1980he
;
Wadia:2012fr
;
Wadia:1980cp
.
Applications to lattice QCD at finite density remain a long-term goal, as reliable diagnostics for CLM are essential to ensure convergence to the correct physical results in such challenging theories.

Despite these advances, it is well known that CLM does not always converge to the correct result: simulations can appear stable while still producing systematically wrong answers.
This has motivated the development of practical reliability criteria, such as monitoring the distribution of the drift term or checking the action of the Langevin-time evolution operator on observables.
While useful, these diagnostics are insufficient, particularly in models with many degrees of freedom or subtle algorithmic instabilities.
In this work, we propose an alternative and complementary diagnostic criterion based on the configurational temperature, which directly tests thermodynamic consistency.

This paper is organized as follows.
In Sec.

we briefly review the complex Langevin method and summarize its main limitations.
In Sec.
3
we introduce the concept of a configuration-based thermometer, while Sec.
4

We demonstrate the effectiveness of this estimator in one-dimensional PT-symmetric models in Sec.

Finally, in Sec.

2
Complex Langevin Method and Its Limitations

The central idea of stochastic quantization is that expectation values of observables can be obtained as equilibrium values of a stochastic process.
In Langevin dynamics, this is realized by evolving the system in a fictitious time direction,
\theta
, under the influence of stochastic noise.
When the action is complex, one can attempt to extend this framework, although in such cases the field variables necessarily become complexified during the Langevin evolution, since the gradient of the action, the
drift term
, is itself complex.

The complex Langevin equation in Euler discretized form reads

\phi(\theta+\Delta\theta)=\phi(\theta)-\Delta\theta\,\frac{\delta S[\phi]}{\delta\phi(\theta)}+\sqrt{\Delta\theta}\,\eta(\theta),

(1)

where
\Delta\theta
is the Langevin time step, and
\eta(\theta)
is a Gaussian noise satisfying

\langle\eta(\theta)\rangle=0,\qquad\langle\eta(\theta)\eta(\theta^{\prime})\rangle=2\delta_{\theta,\theta^{\prime}}.

(2)

In practice, real Gaussian noise is often employed to suppress large excursions in the imaginary directions of the fields
Aarts:2009uq
;
Aarts:2011ax
;
Nagata:2015uga
.

For a general operator
\mathcal{O}
, the noise-averaged expectation value is

\big\langle\mathcal{O}[\phi(\theta)]\big\rangle_{\eta}=\int d\phi\,P[\phi(\theta)]\,\mathcal{O}[\phi],

(3)

where the probability distribution
P[\phi(\theta)]
evolves according to the Fokker–Planck equation

\frac{\partial P[\phi(\theta)]}{\partial\theta}=\frac{\delta}{\delta\phi(\theta)}\left(\frac{\delta}{\delta\phi(\theta)}+\frac{\delta S[\phi]}{\delta\phi(\theta)}\right)P[\phi(\theta)].

(4)

For real actions, the stationary solution is
P[\phi]\propto e^{-S[\phi]}
, ensuring convergence to the correct equilibrium distribution as
\theta\to\infty
.
For complex actions, however, the situation is far more subtle: the drift is complex and the fields evolve into a complexified manifold
\phi=\mathrm{Re}\,\phi+i\,\mathrm{Im}\,\phi
. Rigorous proofs of convergence to the desired complex measure remain elusive
Parisi:1984cs
;
Klauder:1985kq
;
Klauder:1985ks
;
Gausterer:1986gk
.

It has long been known that while Langevin dynamics with real actions generally yield correct results, the Complex Langevin Method (CLM) can converge to incorrect limits.
Two main obstructions have been identified.
First, the
excursion problem
: complexified variables can drift far into the imaginary directions, invalidating the integration by parts argument underlying the method
Aarts:2009uq
;
Aarts:2011ax
.
Second, the
singular drift problem
: if the drift term has poles, the dynamics may approach them frequently, spoiling the proof of correctness
Nishimura:2015pba
.
These insights clarified why CLM may appear stable yet yield wrong answers.

As a result, much effort has gone into formulating practical correctness criteria.
One proposal
Aarts:2009uq
introduces the Langevin-time evolution operator
L
acting on observables, and demands
\langle L\mathcal{O}\rangle=0
in the long-time limit.
While useful in simple models, this suffers from large statistical fluctuations in systems with many degrees of freedom and cannot guarantee correctness.
A sharper criterion was later proposed by Nagata
et al.

Nagata:2016vkn
, who showed that correct convergence requires the probability distribution of the drift term to decay exponentially (or faster) at large magnitude.
This condition is necessary and sufficient, provided additional assumptions such as ergodicity are met
Aarts:2017vrv
;
Seiler:2017wvd
.
Since the drift is computed at every Langevin step, this diagnostic is inexpensive to monitor.

In summary, current reliability criteria for CLM rely mainly on properties of the drift distribution.
While powerful, they remain indirect and may be challenging to apply in large or complicated theories.
This motivates the search for complementary diagnostics more closely tied to physical observables.
In this work, we propose an alternative: a configurational temperature estimator constructed from the gradient and Hessian of the complex action.
As we will demonstrate, this criterion directly probes thermodynamic consistency and provides a sensitive and independent cross-check of CLM simulations.

3
Configuration-Based Thermometer

In molecular dynamics simulations, conserved quantities such as energy or momentum often serve as valuable indicators of algorithmic correctness.
While conservation does not by itself guarantee validity, violations typically signal programming or numerical errors that are straightforward to identify.

In canonical Monte Carlo simulations, such exact conservation laws do not exist. This makes the validation more challenging.
Traditionally, one can assess the accuracy by comparing measured observables with known thermodynamic benchmarks.
However, this approach becomes impractical when exploring new theories or parameter regimes where no reference data exist.

Rugh provided a breakthrough by deriving a geometric expression for the temperature in the microcanonical ensemble
PhysRevLett.78.772
.
His formulation relates temperature to the curvature of constant-energy hypersurfaces in phase space, thereby offering a purely dynamical definition.
Building on this idea, Butler
et al.
introduced a
configurational temperature
suitable for canonical ensembles
10.1063/1.477301
.
This estimator depends only on gradients and curvatures of the potential (or action), making it especially attractive for Monte Carlo simulations that do not sample momenta.
The conceptual foundations can be traced back to Landau and Lifshitz’s
Statistical Physics

Landau1952
, Tolman’s
Principles of Statistical Mechanics

Tolman1979
, and later clarified in historical accounts such as
Hoover2007
.

Subsequent studies established the generality and robustness of the approach.
Jepps
et al.
showed that arbitrary phase-space vector fields can yield valid temperature estimators under suitable conditions, with supporting molecular dynamics simulations
PhysRevE.62.4757
.
Numerical tests confirmed its accuracy in diverse settings, including Lennard–Jones fluids
10.1063/1.477301
and the two-dimensional XY model using cluster algorithms
PhysRevE.94.062113
.
One can find broader theoretical extensions and further molecular dynamics applications in
1998JPhA…31.7761R
;
PhysRevE.62.4757
;
10.1063/1.1348024
;
10.1063/1.480995
.

Although initially proposed for thermostat design and system control, the configurational temperature was quickly recognized as a powerful diagnostic tool.
Because it probes thermodynamic consistency using only configurational information, it can reveal sampling inconsistencies or numerical instabilities independently of momentum-based definitions.

This feature makes the estimator especially valuable in lattice field theory.
Here, temperature is typically introduced via the temporal lattice extent or inverse coupling. However, discretization artifacts can distort physical observables, shifting transition points at finite lattice spacing.
Such deviations vanish only in the continuum limit, making verification of the intended simulation temperature a nontrivial task.
By providing an independent measurement of temperature that can be compared directly with the input parameter, the configurational estimator offers a stringent consistency check.
In the context of the complex Langevin method, where algorithmic subtleties may obscure convergence, this diagnostic can play a crucial role in validating that the system is sampling from the correct thermal distribution.

4
Derivation of the Configuration-Based Thermometer

The starting point is the thermodynamic relation

\frac{1}{T}=\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial E}\right)_{V},

(5)

which identifies inverse temperature with the change of entropy under an infinitesimal energy shift at fixed volume.
In the microcanonical ensemble, the entropy is proportional to the phase-space volume enclosed by a constant-energy shell,

S(E)=k_{B}\ln\Omega_{\Gamma}(E),\qquad\Omega_{\Gamma}(E)=\int_{\mu C(E)}d\vec{\Gamma},

(6)

where
\vec{\Gamma}=(q_{1},\dots,q_{N};p_{1},\dots,p_{N})
denotes positions and momenta, such that,
\mu C(E)\equiv\{\vec{\Gamma}\mid H(\vec{\Gamma})\leq E\}
for Hamiltonian
H(\vec{\Gamma})
.

Following Rugh’s geometric construction
PhysRevLett.78.772
, one introduces a displacement of each configuration along a phase-space vector field
\vec{n}(\vec{\Gamma})
that increases the energy by
\Delta E
.
Choosing

\vec{n}(\vec{\Gamma})=\frac{\vec{\nabla}_{\vec{q}}H}{\vec{\nabla}_{\vec{q}}H\cdot\vec{\nabla}_{\vec{q}}H},

(7)

ensures, to leading order, that
H(\vec{q}+\Delta E\,\vec{n})=H(\vec{q})+\Delta E
.
The associated Jacobian yields the entropy shift

\Delta S\approx k_{B}\,\Delta E\,\vec{\nabla}_{\vec{q}}\cdot\vec{n}(\vec{q}),

(8)

so that in the limit
\Delta E\to 0
,

\frac{1}{T}=k_{B}\big\langle\vec{\nabla}_{\vec{q}}\cdot\vec{n}(\vec{q})\big\rangle.

(9)

For simulations without explicit momenta (as in canonical Monte Carlo), one can set
H(\vec{q})=\Phi(\vec{q})
, the potential energy or action.
This leads to the configurational estimator

\frac{1}{k_{B}T}=\left\langle\vec{\nabla}_{\vec{q}}\cdot\frac{\vec{\nabla}_{\vec{q}}\Phi}{|\vec{\nabla}_{\vec{q}}\Phi|^{2}}\right\rangle+\mathcal{O}(1/N),

(10)

which is valid in the canonical ensemble by ensemble equivalence in the thermodynamic limit
N\to\infty
.

It is convenient to rewrite this in terms of the gradient and Hessian of
\Phi
.
Let

\vec{g}=\vec{\nabla}_{\vec{q}}\Phi,\qquad\mathbb{H}=\vec{\nabla}_{\vec{q}}\vec{\nabla}_{\vec{q}}^{T}\Phi.

(11)

Then

\frac{1}{k_{B}T}=\vec{\nabla}_{\vec{q}}\cdot\left(\frac{\vec{g}}{|\vec{g}|^{2}}\right)=\frac{\mathrm{Tr}(\mathbb{H})}{|\vec{g}|^{2}}-2\frac{\vec{g}^{T}\mathbb{H}\vec{g}}{|\vec{g}|^{4}}.

(12)

Equation (
12
) is the compact and numerically practical form of the configurational temperature estimator: it depends only on local derivatives of the action.

In high-dimensional systems, the gradient
\vec{g}
typically accumulates contributions from many directions, reducing its alignment with the curvature.
As a result, the directional (second) term in Eq. (
12
) often becomes subdominant, leaving the trace term as the leading contribution.
Nonetheless, retaining the whole expression is essential in lower-dimensional or highly anisotropic systems where directional effects are non-negligible.

5
One-dimensional PT-symmetric theories

It has long been observed
Bender:1998ke
;
Bender:1998gh
that quantum mechanical theories with PT-symmetric but non-Hermitian Hamiltonians nevertheless possess real, positive-definite spectra.
A simple but nontrivial example is provided by the one-dimensional (0+1D) scalar field theory with potential

V(\phi)=-\frac{g}{N}\left(i\phi\right)^{N},

(13)

where we take
g>0
and
N=2+\delta
with
\delta>0
following Ref.
Bender:1999ek
.
The Euclidean action is an integral over the thermal circle, with Euclidean time (
\tau
) compactified to circumference
\beta\equiv 1/(k_{B}T)
:

S=\int_{0}^{\beta}d\tau\,\left[\tfrac{1}{2}\Big(\tfrac{\partial\phi}{\partial\tau}\Big)^{2}+V(\phi)\right].

(14)

This model exhibits features that are inaccessible to standard perturbative methods.
For instance, the one-point function
G_{1}\equiv\langle\phi\rangle
in the PT-symmetric vacuum is nonzero and purely imaginary, a result that cannot be obtained from Feynman rules but can be derived systematically using Schwinger–Dyson truncations
Bender:1999ek
;
Pehlevan:2007eq
.
The exact values for selected cases with
g=1
are

\displaystyle G_{1}^{\rm exact}

\displaystyle=-i\,0.5901\quad(\delta=1),

(15)

\displaystyle G_{1}^{\rm exact}

\displaystyle=-i\,0.8669\quad(\delta=2),

(16)

while the full energy spectrum remains real and bounded below.
These features make the model a valuable nontrivial benchmark.

Because the potential is unbounded on the real axis, the path integral must be defined along a complex contour. Complex Langevin simulations have been shown to reproduce the corresponding contour solutions
Pehlevan:2007eq
.
Multiple stationary distributions are possible depending on the initial conditions, but restricting initial fields to the lower half-plane selects the PT-symmetric solution
Bernard:2001wh
.

On a lattice with
N_{\tau}
sites, the discretized action takes the form

S=\sum_{n=0}^{N_{\tau}-1}\left[\frac{(\phi_{n+1}-\phi_{n})^{2}}{2}-\frac{g}{2+\delta}\,(i\phi_{n})^{2+\delta}\right],

(17)

with periodic boundary conditions.
Here, the dimensionless field is related to the physical one by
\phi=\phi_{\rm phys}/\sqrt{a}
, with
a
the lattice spacing, while the coupling scales as
g=a^{2+\delta/2}g_{\rm phys}
.
The inverse temperature is
\beta=N_{\tau}a
.

We compute the equal-time correlation functions

G_{k}\equiv\langle\phi^{k}\rangle,\quad k=1,\dots,4,

(18)

for
\delta=1,2
.

We considered a lattice with
N_{\tau}=128
and
a=1
.
Simulations were performed with
\phi_{\rm initial}=0.0-i1.0
, adaptive Langevin step size
\epsilon\leq 0.001
, thermalization steps
N_{\rm therm}=10^{4}
and generation steps
N_{\rm gen}=10^{6}
.
Measurements were taken after every
10
steps.

5.1
Temperature estimator

We now evaluate the configurational temperature estimator in this PT-symmetric model.
For each configuration, we compute

\hat{\beta}=\frac{\sum_{n}h_{nn}}{\sum_{n}g_{n}^{2}}-\frac{2\sum_{nm}g_{n}g_{m}h_{nm}}{\left(\sum_{n}g_{n}^{2}\right)^{2}},

(19)

which is the lattice discretization of Equation (
12
), with the gradient and Hessian defined as

g_{n}=\frac{1}{\beta}\frac{\partial S}{\partial\phi_{n}},\qquad h_{nm}=\frac{1}{\beta}\frac{\partial^{2}S}{\partial\phi_{n}\partial\phi_{m}}.

(20)

Explicitly,

\displaystyle g_{n}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{\beta}\Big[(\phi_{n}-\phi_{n-1})+(\phi_{n}-\phi_{n+1})-g\,i^{2+\delta}\phi_{n}^{1+\delta}\Big],

(21)

\displaystyle h_{nn}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{\beta}\Big[2-g(1+\delta)i^{2+\delta}\phi_{n}^{\delta}\Big],

(22)

\displaystyle h_{nm}

\displaystyle=-\tfrac{1}{\beta}\big(\delta_{m,n-1}+\delta_{m,n+1}\big),

(23)

where
\delta_{m,n}
denotes the Kronecker delta, and periodic boundary conditions are imposed.
The measured inverse temperature is then
\beta_{M}\equiv\langle\hat{\beta}\rangle
.

6
Temperature Estimator as a Reliability Criterion

One of the most appealing features of the configurational temperature estimator is its direct sensitivity to algorithmic errors and numerical artifacts.
Since it probes thermodynamic consistency, any deviation of the measured inverse temperature
\beta_{M}
from the input
\beta
provides an immediate signal of potential problems.
In this section, we illustrate its utility in three contexts: detecting algorithmic errors, assessing Langevin step size dependence, and monitoring thermalization.

6.1
Detecting algorithmic errors

As a first test, we deliberately introduce an error into the noise normalization of the discretized Langevin equation.
Specifically, we replace the correct Gaussian correlation

\langle\eta(\theta)\eta(\theta^{\prime})\rangle=2\,\delta_{\theta\theta^{\prime}}

(24)

with

\langle\eta(\theta)\eta(\theta^{\prime})\rangle=\sigma\,\delta_{\theta\theta^{\prime}},

(25)

where
\sigma>0,\sigma\neq 2
, represents a mis-scaling of the noise term. In stochastic quantization, the mis-scaling, analogous to the rescaling of the diffusion coefficient in Brownian motion
Uhlenbeck:1930zz
;
Kubo:1966fyg
, breaks the fluctuation-dissipation balance, thereby modifying the stationary distribution of the associated Fokker–Planck equation from
e^{-S}
to
e^{-(2/\sigma)S}

Damgaard:1987rr
. In this setup, the white-noise normalization
\sigma
effectively sets the thermostat; departures from the canonical value
\sigma=2
imply that the dynamics samples at an effective inverse temperature
\beta_{\rm eff}=(2/\sigma)~\beta
, which our configuration-based thermometer is designed to register.

As shown in Table
3
, this modification is clearly detected by the configurational estimator:
\beta_{M}
shifts systematically away from the input value in a manner proportional to
\sigma
.
Even modest changes, such as
\sigma=1.0
, produce a measurable deviation.
Thus, the estimator serves as a robust diagnostic for errors that might otherwise remain hidden.

6.4
Langevin operator on observables

A correctness criterion for the CLM is based on the Langevin operator acting on observables
Aarts:2011ax
;
Aarts:2009uq
;
Aarts:2013uza
.
For an observable
{\cal O}_{i}[\phi,\tau]
at site
i
, we have

\frac{\partial{\cal O}_{i}[\phi,\tau]}{\partial\tau}=L_{i}\,{\cal O}_{i}[\phi,\tau],

(26)

where the operator
L_{i}
is defined as

L_{i}\equiv\Big(\frac{\partial}{\partial\phi_{i}}-\frac{\partial S}{\partial\phi_{i}}\Big)\frac{\partial}{\partial\phi_{i}}.

(27)

In equilibrium, the integration by parts argument implies that

C_{{\cal O}_{i}}\equiv\big\langle L_{i}{\cal O}_{i}[\phi]\big\rangle=0,

(28)

which can be used as a diagnostic of correctness.

We apply this criterion to the one-point function
G_{1}\equiv\langle\phi\rangle
.
Due to translational symmetry,
LG_{1}
can be averaged over all sites.
Figure
4
shows the Langevin-time history of
LG_{1}
, and Table
5
summarizes the results.
Within errors,
LG_{1}
is consistent with zero, suggesting that this criterion does not flag problems in the simulations considered here.

6.5
Decay of the drift terms

A second criterion, introduced by Nagata
et al.

Nagata:2016vkn
;
Nagata:2018net
, requires the probability distribution of the drift magnitude to decay exponentially or faster at large values.
This ensures that integration by parts is valid and convergence is correct.

The magnitude of the mean drift is defined as

u\equiv\sqrt{\frac{1}{N_{\tau}}\sum_{n=0}^{N_{\tau}-1}\left|\frac{\partial S}{\partial\phi_{n}}\right|^{2}}.

(29)

Figure
5
shows the drift distribution
P(u)
for several noise variances
\sigma
.
For
\delta=1
, exponential suppression holds for all tested
\sigma
.
For
\delta=2
, exponential decay is observed at
\sigma=2,4,8
, but breaks down at
\sigma=16
, indicating unreliable dynamics in that case.

6.6
Comparing the three correctness criteria

We now compare the three available correctness criteria side by side:

(i.)

Criterion 1 (this work):
Configurational temperature estimator, requiring
\beta_{M}=\beta
.

(ii.)

Criterion 2:
Langevin operator on observables,
\langle L{\cal O}\rangle=0
.

(ii.)

Criterion 3:
Exponential decay of the drift distribution.

We probe these criteria by introducing algorithmic errors via controlled noise mis-scaling.
The results are summarized in Table

.
We find that:

(i.)

Criterion 1
is highly sensitive: deviations in noise normalization are immediately reflected in
\beta_{M}\neq\beta
.

(i.)

Criterion 2
is satisfied in all cases, but its insensitivity means it fails to flag algorithmic errors.

(i.)

Criterion 3
is generally satisfied, but breaks down only at extreme mis-scalings (e.g.
\sigma=16
for
\delta=2
).

Thus, the configurational temperature emerges as a sharper diagnostic, capable of detecting subtle errors that remain invisible to existing criteria.
Its thermodynamic interpretation further distinguishes it as an independent and complementary test of CLM reliability.

The configurational temperature estimator offers a straightforward yet powerful reliability criterion.
It can identify noise mis-scaling, expose step-size artifacts, and track thermalization, providing information complementary to existing drift-based diagnostics while remaining easy to implement in practice.

7
Discussion and Outlook

In this work, we introduced a configurational temperature estimator as a
thermodynamically grounded reliability diagnostic
for the complex Langevin method (CLM).
Our results in one-dimensional PT-symmetric models demonstrate that this estimator faithfully tracks the input temperature, reacts promptly to algorithmic deviations, and complements existing drift-based diagnostics.
Importantly, it provides an
independent, physically interpretable cross-check
of CLM dynamics, thus, bridging algorithmic stability with thermodynamic consistency.

While the present study focused on one-dimensional PT-symmetric systems, we note that these models serve as a
controlled and nontrivial benchmark
where correctness criteria can be evaluated against exact or well-established results.
This allows us to isolate algorithmic effects in a clean setting before addressing the complexities of higher-dimensional theories.
Nevertheless, we emphasize that the configurational approach is
not limited to low-dimensional models
.
The required quantities, gradients and Hessians of the action, are already accessible in typical lattice simulations, and scalable approximations exist to make the estimator practical in large-volume or gauge-theory settings.

The broader motivation would be to establish configurational monitoring as part of a
hybrid diagnostic toolkit
for CLM, alongside drift analysis, gauge cooling, and operator-based checks.
In particular:

\bullet

In lattice QCD at finite density, where reliability remains the key obstacle to progress, configurational temperature could provide a stringent cross-check of thermodynamic consistency.

\bullet

In matrix models and supersymmetric theories, the method can be implemented with modest computational overhead, offering a complementary probe of phase structure and spontaneous symmetry breaking.

\bullet

Beyond diagnostics, we think that configurational monitoring may also provide a
sensitive indicator of phase transitions
, offering a thermodynamic lens on complex-action systems.

In summary, configurational temperature strengthens the link between
numerical correctness
and
physical consistency
thus providing a compact and conceptually distinct tool that can enhance the trustworthiness of CLM across a broad spectrum of applications.
We view this work as the
first step
toward integrating configurational criteria into mainstream complex-action simulations, with the ultimate goal of enabling reliable explorations of gauge theories and QCD at finite density.

A further direction arises in lattice field theories where temperature is not just a thermodynamic parameter but is directly linked to the bare coupling through the temporal lattice extent.
In such cases, deviations between the measured configurational temperature and the target value may signal not only algorithmic instabilities but also discretization artifacts that shift effective couplings.
This opens up the possibility of using configurational monitoring as a
nonperturbative cross-check of coupling-observable relations
thus providing an additional layer of validation in studies of thermal transitions, phase structure, and continuum extrapolations.
Such an application would broaden the role of configurational temperature beyond reliability diagnostics, positioning it as a versatile probe of thermodynamic consistency in lattice gauge theories.
