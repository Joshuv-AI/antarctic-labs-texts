School transparency as a predictor of organizational trust
Authors: Erdal Meriç, Hamza Öz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Today, the gradual weakening of trust in institutions leads to serious social and administrative problems in a wide range of areas, from public administration to the private sector, from non-governmental organizations to digital platforms, and in this context, the concept of transparency stands out as one of the important elements affecting trust. Transparency is the state where the actions and practices of a person, institution, or system are clear, understandable, and traceable, while trust is the state where an individual willingly allows themselves to be vulnerable based on their belief that others will act in good faith. Transparency and trust are important concepts for both the individual and the organization, and the fundamental and first step toward achieving organizational trust is establishing a transparent organizational culture. This research aims to determine the relationship between public school teachers’ perceptions of school transparency and their levels of organizational trust in the context of social exchange theory. The study group, which was structured with a correlational survey study, consists of a total of 595 teachers working in public schools in one province of Türkiye, selected by convenience sampling method from seven different geographical regions. Data was collected using the “School Transparency Scale” and the “Organizational Trust Scale.” Parametric tests were used to compare demographic variables in the study, and multiple linear regression analysis was conducted to determine the predictive value of school transparency on organizational trust. As a result of the research, it was determined that teachers’ perceptions of overall school transparency and school transparency in the dimensions of participation in decision-making and information flow were “high”, while their perceptions of school transparency in the dimension of accountability were “medium”; and their levels of overall organizational trust and organizational trust in the three sub-dimensions were “high”. The research also concluded that there was a positive, significant, and high-level relationship between school transparency and organizational trust. It was found that classroom teachers’ perceptions of overall school transparency and school transparency in the dimensions of participation in decision-making and accountability were higher than that of subject matter teachers; that teachers with bachelor’s degrees had higher perceptions of school transparency than that of postgraduate graduates; and that teachers working in primary and secondary schools had higher perceptions of school transparency than that of high school teachers. The research also found that classroom teachers’ trust in the institution was higher than that of subject matter teachers; that teachers with bachelor’s degrees had higher general organizational trust and higher levels of institutional trust than that of postgraduate graduates; and that teachers working in primary and secondary schools had higher organizational trust levels than that of high school teachers. In the context of the limitations of the study, various suggestions were made to increase school transparency and organizational trust.

Introduction

Today, the gradual weakening of trust in institutions leads to serious social and administrative problems across a wide spectrum, from public administration to the private sector, from civil society organizations to digital platforms. Lack of trust, particularly in public administration, reduces participation in decision-making processes, undermines the legitimacy of institutions, and diminishes their capacity for collaboration. Therefore, institutions’ trust-building strategies have become a critical area of ​​research both in academia and in practice. In this context, the concept of transparency stands out as one of the key elements affecting trust. Furthermore, with the structural changes experienced in education systems in recent years, due to factors such as a school-based management approach, increased accountability, and a diversity of stakeholder expectations, the concepts of organizational trust and school transparency have begun to gain importance in educational institutions. Research on building organizational trust focuses particularly on school transparency [1, 2]. Therefore, it is crucial to investigate organizational trust and transparency practices, which have become strategic factors for school administration, within the school context.

Trust is the willingness of an individual to be vulnerable based on the belief that others will act in good faith [3, 4]. In other words, it involves belief in and commitment to the behavior or intentions of a group or individual, expectations of fair and constructive behavior based on moral principles, and consideration of the rights of others [5]. Organizational trust, on the other hand, is viewed as individuals’ positive expectations regarding the intentions and behaviors of multiple organizational members based on organizational roles, relationships, experiences, and interdependence [6]. It refers to employees’ belief in the competence of their colleagues and managers, as well as their tolerant, fair, and ethical behavior [7].

In the context of teachers, organizational trust refers to the trust teachers have in their schools as the institutions in which they work, their administrators, their colleagues, and the education system in general. According to Li et al. [8], it is reasonable to expect that teachers in schools with higher organizational trust will experience higher levels of job satisfaction.

Transparency is the state in which the actions and practices of a person, institution, or system are clear, understandable, and traceable, and is a highly important concept from both an individual and organizational perspective. Berggren and Bernshteyn [9] stated that transparency provides numerous benefits to organizations and plays a significant role in reducing inefficiency and ensuring sustained performance. According to Distaso and Messner [10], organizational transparency is the degree to which an organization shares the information needed for its stakeholders to make informed decisions. Therefore, transparency facilitates access to organizational decisions and information about the organization.

School transparency means providing clear and accessible information on matters such as the school’s operations, decision-making mechanisms, resource utilization, and students’ academic achievement. This transparency in practices allows parents, students, and teachers to have greater trust in school administration. Consequently, when school stakeholders know how decisions are made, trust in the school and the education system in general increases.

Research in the literature has mentioned many factors that affect teachers’ organizational trust levels, and organizational trust is: organizational justice [11–14], organizational commitment [15], organizational commitment [16], organizational culture [17], organizational health [18, 19], job satisfaction [20, 21], self-efficacy beliefs and teacher autonomy [22], hope and motivation [23], academic identity [24], organizational citizenship behavior [11, 13, 17, 25], executive leadership behaviors [26–28], cynicism [29], workplace isolation [30], and favoritism [31] have been examined. It has been stated that organizational trust is affected by both individual and organizational factors, but no research has been found in the literature to determine the relationship between teachers’ perceptions of school transparency and their levels of organizational trust in the context of the reciprocity principle of social exchange theory. This research will fill this gap in the literature and will also contribute to determining the relationship between teachers’ perceptions of school transparency and their levels of organizational trust. Furthermore, a study by Dai et al. [32] suggests that studies on organizational trust should be examined in different cultural settings and with different variables. A study by Tosun and Çelik [33] stated that organizational transparency is a prerequisite for improving organizational health, while a study by Kahveci and Babayigit [2] indicated the need for studies examining the interaction between employees’ psychological effects of organizational transparency and school success.

Consequently, the main problem in this study was to determine the relationship between public school teachers’ perceptions of school transparency and their levels of organizational trust within the context of social exchange theory. It is believed that, along with individual and organizational factors related to organizational trust, teachers’ perceptions of school transparency may also influence their levels of organizational trust. Teachers’ positive perceptions that their schools are managed transparently will increase their trust in schools. Consequently, transparent school management will increase teachers’ trust in school administrators, colleagues, and the school organization. Based on this assumption, it is deemed valuable to investigate the relationship between teachers’ perceptions of school transparency and their levels of organizational trust within the context of the reciprocity principle of social change theory.

The education system in Türkiye is based on a centralized structure, and all schools are administered by the Ministry of National Education (MEB). Within this structure, school administrators operate under established regulations and adhere to central regulations to a significant extent in decision-making processes. This directly impacts perceptions of transparency, accountability, and organizational trust in schools. It is known that high accountability systems are being implemented in the Turkish education system. Specifically, within the scope of information transparency, information is shared with teachers, students, and parents via the internet and various social media applications. Within the scope of participation transparency, teachers and parents take an active role in the management process through parent and board meetings. Within the scope of accountability, external auditing is active, and parents have easy access to external evaluations. Parents and other stakeholders’ full and easy access to external evaluations increases the level of confidence in the evaluations made when choosing schools.

Theoretical framework

Organizational trust

Organizational trust is a concept that has been extensively researched in disciplines such as psychology, management, and organizational behavior [34]. Organizational trust, which has a highly complex social and psychological structure, has been addressed in different ways by different scholars from various perspectives [35]. Robinson [36] defines organizational trust as employees’ belief in the sincerity and trustworthiness of the organization or leadership and argues that this trust reflects employees’ identification with the organization and their desire to establish a long-term relationship. Podsakoff et al. [37], on the other hand, adds an interpersonal dimension to this concept, explaining organizational trust as the degree of trust employees have in their managers and coworkers. When employees perceive a benefit to themselves from the organization’s or leader’s behavior, they respond positively [38].

Organizational trust is defined as employees’ willingness to be vulnerable to the organization’s actions [39] and is viewed as their belief in the organization’s consistency in commitments and behaviors under conditions of uncertainty or risk [40]. Organizational trust is defined as the positive expectations individuals have about the intentions and behaviors of organizational members [41]. Organizational trust is categorized as trust in colleagues, trust in the manager, and trust in the organization [42]. The first step in establishing organizational trust is trust in the administrator [43]. Accordingly, administrators are one of the primary determinants of teachers’ perception of trust in the organization. Trust in colleagues is defined as the existence of a shared sense of trust in the attitudes, behaviors, and discourses of teachers toward one another within the organization, and the existence of mutual goodwill and honesty [44]. Trust in the institution is defined as the totality of teachers’ belief that the organization they are in will achieve its intended success and that this success will be sustained [45]. Teachers’ trust in the organization is shaped by the administration’s treatment of employees and their perception of the organization. Positive opinions about the organization follow a similar pattern in employee-organizational relationships; organizations can achieve their goals more quickly [46]. Employees’ trust at one level can influence their trust at other levels [47]. For example, employees’ trust in a manager can influence their trust in the organization. Conversely, a lack of trust in managers can lead to negative behaviors.

School transparency

Transparency refers to the degree to which information flows freely between managers and employees within an organization and among stakeholders [48]. Transparency is essentially the principle that decisions are made and implemented in accordance with legal regulations, that those affected by these decisions have access to information, and that this information is accessible, understandable, and concrete [49]. As in all organizations, transparency in schools, which are educational organizations, is critical to achieving organizational goals. School transparency refers to a school’s open, understandable, and accessible sharing of its educational activities, administrative processes, financial status, and relationships with all stakeholders, especially teachers, students, and parents. Transparent school management facilitates collaboration with the school by providing parents and students with more information about the school and fosters an environment of trust, improving the quality of education.

School transparency is particularly defined by participation in decision-making, the flow of information, and accountability [50]. By being open and transparent with school stakeholders in the operation of educational environments, sharing information without being secretive about any issue, and encouraging stakeholders’ opinions and participation in decision-making, when necessary, trust will be naturally built in schools. According to Kalkan and Alparslan [51], transparency is seen as crucial for ensuring and increasing the success of the institution, while Koçak et al. [52] consider it necessary for school administrations to exercise their delegated authority and be held accountable. Ertan-Kantos and Balcı [53] emphasized that all school administrators must be open and transparent. While accountability is considered a prerequisite for transparency, transparency is considered complementary to it.

Public reporting of school performance and effectiveness data is becoming increasingly common in many places, with the aim of ensuring accountability through transparency, facilitating school development, raising standards, and providing parents with information to make school-related choices for their children. A study by Tosun and Çelik [33] concluded that organizational transparency is a significant predictor of school effectiveness. According to LaForett and Mendez [54], transparency not only includes the clear presentation of information about school finances and programs but also fosters mutual trust between school leaders and teachers.

Social exchange theory

The foundation of organizational trust and school transparency is based on the social exchange theory developed by Blau [55]. This theory has been proposed as the most effective conceptual paradigm for explaining human behavior in social exchanges and understanding workplace behavior [56]. Çetin and Şentürk [57] argue that a positive behavior toward another person creates an unspecified obligation to respond with a positive behavior. This norm of reciprocity is considered the most fundamental principle of social exchange theory.

Blau [55] stated that only social exchanges elicit feelings of personal obligation, gratitude, and trust, while economic exchanges do not. He also noted that the benefits of social exchange do not necessarily have a price, suggesting that social exchanges create enduring social patterns. It is important to note that in social interaction theory, gains are based on mutual trust and involve uncertainty. At this point, the fundamental assumption is to ensure mutual trust and affection through greater support rather than economic gain [58, 59]. Cropanzano and Mitchell [56] stated that social exchange relationships will yield positive outcomes in work environments. School transparency will foster positive work attitudes within these relationships. In this context, school stakeholders who see schools being managed transparently are likely to respond positively to this action. As perceived school transparency increases, employees’ perceptions of organizational trust and performance also increase. Similarly, as perceptions of organizational justice increase, job satisfaction, organizational trust, and commitment also increase.

Organizational trust and school transparency in the context of social exchange theory

Social Exchange Theory argues that individuals build their organizational relationships on the principles of reciprocity, trust, and mutual benefit. According to the theory developed by Blau [55], employees exhibit positive attitudes and behaviors in response to the positive conditions provided by the organization. Transparency in the school context provides teachers with a psychological and professional “trust signal” through elements such as information sharing, openness, accountability, and clear execution of processes. These positive practices increase employees’ trust in the organization. Therefore, school transparency acts as an “investment” within the social exchange relationship, and in return, it is expected to increase organizational trust.

Transparency of organizational practices-adequate explanations of decisions, reliable information flow, timely feedback, and accurate and frank communication-increases employees’ trust in the organization [6]. This is because the fundamental and first step toward achieving trust is establishing a transparent organizational culture [48]. As social beings, people need to be together and work together to achieve both their personal and organizational goals. It’s important that this togetherness extends beyond sharing the physical environment and also encompasses cognitive and affective domains. Trusting each other is essential for achieving these goals, especially in partnerships formed to achieve common goals [60]. This is because a lack of mutual trust negatively impacts collaborative work toward shared goals.

When employees trust their organization, they feel more comfortable and will focus their energy on achieving organizational goals rather than protecting themselves [61]. Conversely, when employees lose trust in each other and the organization, it not only damages interpersonal relationships within the organization but can also threaten the organization’s very existence [62]. A review of the literature reveals no concrete research findings demonstrating that transparency always positively impacts trust. It should also be noted that transparency can have complex effects on trust, as it can make risks and errors visible in some cases. Therefore, the relationship between the two concepts is not linear, universal, or context-independent. This study stems from the need to identify the conditions under which the relationship between transparency and trust strengthens and weakens. Understanding the dynamics of this relationship is essential for institutions to develop effective governance models, establish sustainable relationships with stakeholders, and establish social trust. Therefore, this research aims to examine the dynamics of the relationship between transparency and trust with a holistic approach within the context of social change theory, to reveal the factors that determine this relationship, and to contribute to institutions’ development of strategies to strengthen trust. Thus, it aims to both make a conceptual and empirical contribution to academic literature and to produce findings that provide guidance for practitioners.

Research Model

This research, conducted using quantitative methods, was structured using a correlational survey study. The correlational survey study aims to determine the existence or degree of covariation between two or more variables [63].

Study Group

The study group consisted of a province from seven different geographical regions of Türkiye, selected by convenience sampling method to ensure geographical representation, and a total of 595 teachers (Adana: 70, Aydın: 71, Gaziantep: 78, Istanbul: 85, Konya: 76, Ordu: 94, Van: 121) who work in public schools in these provinces and who accepted to participate in the research voluntarily. 265 (44.5%) of the study group were female, 330 (55.5%) were male, 473 (79.5%) were married, and 122 (20.5%) were single. In terms of field, 203 (34.1%) of the participants were classroom teachers, 392 (65.9%) were branch teachers, and when examined in terms of educational background, 478 (80.3%) had a bachelor’s degree, and 117 (19.7%) teachers had a postgraduate degree. When looking at the education levels they work at, 217 (36.5%) of the teachers work in primary school, 177 (29.7%) in secondary school, and 201 (33.8%) in high school. Of the teachers, 147 (24.7%) have 1–10 years of professional seniority, 179 (30.1%) have 11–20 years, and 269 (45.2%) have 21 years or more of professional seniority. Regarding the length of service at their current school, 267 (44.9%) have 1–5 years, 193 (32.4%) have 6–10 years, and 135 (22.7%) have 11 years or more. Teachers with less than one year of service at their current school were not included in the study.

Data Collection Tools

The study data were collected using two scales, and the details of the scales used are provided below.

Organizational Trust Scale (OTS)

The “Organizational Trust Scale” (OTS), developed by Çalışkan [42], was used to determine teachers’ organizational trust levels. The OTS consists of 17 items and three sub-dimensions, and its dimensions are “Trust in Colleagues” (7 items), “Trust in the Administrator” (5 items), and “Trust in the Institution” (5 items). The scale items are designed as a five-point Likert-type scale and are scored from “Strongly disagree” (1) to “Strongly agree” (5). High scores indicate that teachers have high levels of organizational trust.

The reliability of the OTS was tested by Çalışkan [42] through separate analyses using data obtained from three different sample groups consisting of healthcare, education, and industrial workers. In the analysis conducted with data obtained from 455 educators, Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the overall OTS was calculated as 0.93. Cronbach’s alpha values for the sub-dimensions were found to be 0.92, 0.95, and 0.94, respectively. Similarly, analyses conducted with data obtained from healthcare and industrial workers revealed Cronbach’s alpha values ranging from 0.86 to 0.94. To test the three-dimensional structure resulting from an EFA conducted separately with data obtained from two different sample groups of healthcare and education workers for the validity of the scale, the researcher conducted a CFA with data obtained from a separate sample group of industrial workers. The CFA determined that the three-dimensional structure of the OTS was preserved and that the model demonstrated a good overall fit.

In this study, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient calculated for the reliability of the OTS was 0.95 for the overall scale and 0.91, 0.93, and 0.92 for the sub-dimensions, respectively. CFA was performed for the construct validity of the OTS, and it was determined that the item loading values varied between 0.58 and 0.92, the three-dimensional structure of the scale was preserved, and the model generally showed an excellent fit level (X2/sd = 2.98, CFI = 0.98, NFI = 0.96, GFI = 0.94, AGFI = 0.92, TLI = 0.97, IFI = 0.98, RMSEA = 0.06, SRMR = 0.03, RMR = 0.03).

School Transparency Scale (STS)

Teachers’ perceptions of school transparency were determined using the “School Transparency Scale” developed by Tosun and Çelik [50]. The STS consists of 16 items and three sub-dimensions, and its dimensions are “Participation in Decisions” (4 items), “Information Flow” (6 items), and “Accountability” (6 items). The scale is designed as a five-point Likert-type scale, with items ranging from “I completely disagree” (1) to “I completely agree” (5). High scores indicate that teachers have high perceptions of school transparency. Items 3 and 10 of the scale were reverse-coded before the analyses.

To test the reliability of the STS, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the overall scale was calculated as 0.95 in the analyses conducted by Tosun and Çelik [50]. Cronbach’s alpha values for the subscales were found to be 0.89, 0.90, and 0.91, respectively. To test the three-dimensional structure revealed by the EFA for the scale’s validity, the researchers conducted a CFA on a different study group. The CFA determined that the three-dimensional structure of the STS was preserved and that the model demonstrated an acceptable level of fit overall. In this study, Cronbach’s alpha coefficient calculated for the STS’s reliability was 0.96 for the overall scale and 0.92, 0.91, and 0.94 for the subscales, respectively. CFA was performed for the construct validity of the STS, and it was determined that the item loading values varied between 0.58 and 0.90, the three-dimensional structure of the scale was preserved, and the model generally showed an excellent fit level (X2/sd = 2.79, CFI = 0.98, NFI = 0.97, GFI = 0.95, AGFI = 0.93, TLI = 0.98, IFI = 0.98, RMSEA = 0.06, SRMR = 0.03, RMR = 0.03).

Data Collection and Analysis

Ethics committee approval was obtained for the study from the Ordu University Educational Research Ethics Committee, dated March 29, 2024, and numbered 2024/43.

To reduce self-report bias, the research data was collected electronically (Google Form). Participants were informed that their identities would be kept strictly confidential and that their responses would not be linked to their identities. Care was taken to ensure that the questions were formulated in a non-judgmental manner so as not to create normative pressure. To prevent time pressure, participants were given sufficient time to complete the scale forms after they were sent to them. Furthermore, the inclusion of two reverse-scored items in the scale also contributed to reducing self-report bias.

Scale forms completed by 611 teachers who volunteered to participate in the study were transferred to SPSS. For one-sided outliers, eight scales with z-scores below and above ± 3 were considered outliers and removed from the data set. For multi-sided outliers, the Mahalanobis distance was first examined, and four scales with p <.001 were removed from the data set. The Cook distance was also calculated to assess the presence of outliers, and four scales with values greater than 1.00 were considered outliers and removed from the data set. Analyses were conducted with the remaining 595 scales.

SPSS and AMOS programs were used to analyze the research data, and the significance levels for statistical analyses were set at 0.01 and 0.05. Skewness and kurtosis values were examined to check the univariate normality of the data set, and scatter plot matrices were examined to test multivariate normality. For univariate normality, skewness and kurtosis values were calculated for the total score and sub-dimensions of school transparency and organizational trust. For the STS and its sub-dimensions, skewness values ranged from − 0.747 to − 0.258, and kurtosis values ranged from − 0.626 to 0.258; for the OTS and its sub-dimensions, skewness values ranged from − 0.328 to − 0.119, and kurtosis values ranged from − 0.440 to 0.100. According to George and Mallery [64], skewness and kurtosis values within ± 1 indicate that the data set is normally distributed. Because the skewness and kurtosis values were within normal limits and the scatter plots were elliptical, parametric tests were used for the analyses.

The “Independent Samples t-Test” and “One-Way Analysis of Variance (One-Way Anova)” were used to compare variables. When comparing groups as a result of the t-test and ANOVA, homogeneity of variances was examined. In case of a difference between groups as a result of the ANOVA, Post Hoc tests were run to determine the source of the difference. The Tukey test was used in groups where variances were homogeneous, and the Games-Howell test was used in groups where variances were not homogeneous. In this study, which aimed to determine the extent to which school transparency predicts organizational trust, the three sub-dimensions of school transparency were determined as predictor (independent) variables, and organizational trust as the predicted (dependent) variable. Multiple linear regression analysis was conducted to determine the predictive level of school transparency on organizational trust. Before the regression analysis, the basic assumptions of the model were checked, and examination of the scatter diagram matrix revealed that the data met multivariate normality, that there was a linear relationship between the independent variables and the dependent variable, and that there was no multicollinearity problem (VIF < 10; Tolerance > 0.10; CI < 30) or autocorrelation (1.5 < DW = 2.16 < 2.5) among the independent variables. Arithmetic means and standard deviation values were also included in the data analysis. The scores obtained from the scales were evaluated as 1.00–1.79.00.79 (Very low), 1.80–2.59 (Low), 2.60–3.39 (Medium), 3.40–4.19 (High), and 4.20–5.00.20.00 (Very high). Score ranges on the scales were determined according to the grouped score formula, and absolute cutoff scores were used.

Looking at Table 2 reveals that teachers’ perceptions of school transparency in both general school transparency and participation in decision and accountability dimensions differed significantly by field (2.80 < t < 3.44, p <.01), while classroom teachers’ (3.50 < M < 3.77) perceptions of school transparency in both general school transparency and participation in decision and accountability dimensions were higher than subject teachers’ (3.20 < M < 3.57). When the effect size coefficients are examined, it is seen that the difference between the groups in both the general school transparency and the participation in decisions and accountability sub-dimensions is at a low level. Teachers’ perceptions of school transparency in the information flow dimension did not differ significantly by field (t(593) = 0.88, p >.05).

It was found that the teachers’ general organizational trust levels and the organizational trust levels in the dimensions of trust in colleagues and trust in the administrator did not differ in terms of field (1.05 < t < 1.83, p >.05), but the trust in the institution of class teachers (M = 3.69) was significantly higher than that of branch teachers (M = 3.54) (t(593) = 2.05, p <.05). The effect size coefficient shows that the difference between the groups in the sub-dimension of trust in the institution is at a low level.

Looking at Table 3 reveals that teachers’ perceptions of both overall school transparency and three sub-dimensions of school transparency differed significantly across educational background (2.01 < t < 2.89, p <.05). Teachers with a bachelor’s degree (3.36 < M < 4.11) had higher perceptions of both overall school transparency and three sub-dimensions of school transparency than teachers with a master’s degree (3.06 < M < 3.92). When the effect size coefficients are examined, it is seen that the difference between the groups in both general school transparency and the three sub-dimensions is at a low level.

Teachers’ overall organizational trust levels and institutional trust levels differed significantly across educational background (t(593) = 2.21, p <.05). Teachers with a bachelor’s degree had higher levels of both overall organizational trust (M = 3.58) and institutional trust (M = 3.63) than teachers with a master’s degree (3.41 < M < 3.44). When the effect size coefficients are examined, it is seen that the difference between the groups in both the general organizational trust and the trust in the institution sub-dimensions is at a low level. It was observed that the levels of trust in teachers’ colleagues and in their administrators did not differ according to their educational background (1.72 < t < 1.93, p >.05).

Looking at Table 4 reveals that teachers’ perceptions of both overall school transparency and three sub-dimensions of school transparency differ significantly depending on the level of education they work in (7.44 ≤ F ≤ 19.22, p <.01). A Tukey test conducted to determine the source of the difference revealed that teachers working in primary schools (3.51 ≤ M ≤ 4.11) and middle schools (3.47 ≤ M ≤ 4.17) had higher perceptions of both overall school transparency and three sub-dimensions of school transparency than those working in high schools (2.96 ≤ M ≤ 3.91). The means indicate that high school teachers’ perceptions of school transparency were lower than those working in other levels of education. When the effect size coefficients are examined, it is seen that the difference between the groups is at a low level in the general school transparency and information flow sub-dimensions, and the difference between the groups is at a moderate level in the participation in decisions accountability sub-dimensions.

Teachers’ overall organizational trust and three sub-dimensions of organizational trust levels differed significantly based on their educational level (7.51 ≤ F ≤ 12.42, p <.01). Tukey and Games-Howell tests were conducted to determine the source of the difference. Results indicated that teachers working in elementary schools (3.53 ≤ M ≤ 3.71) and middle schools (3.58 ≤ M ≤ 3.71) had higher levels of overall organizational trust and three sub-dimensions of organizational trust than those working in high schools (3.19 ≤ M ≤ 3.46). The means indicate that high school teachers have lower levels of organizational trust than teachers working in other educational levels. When the effect size coefficients are examined, it is seen that the difference between the groups is low in both general organizational trust and the three sub-dimensions.

The study also found that teachers’ perceptions of school transparency and organizational trust levels did not differ significantly based on gender, marital status, total length of service, or length of service at the current school.

Discussion and conclusion

This study, conducted to examine the relationship between teachers’ perceptions of school transparency and their levels of organizational trust, found positive relationships between school transparency and the variables and their sub-dimensions of organizational trust. The study also found that teachers’ overall perceptions of school transparency, as well as their perceptions of school transparency in the dimensions of participation in decisions and information flow, were “high,” while their perceptions of school transparency in the dimension of accountability were “medium.” These findings regarding school transparency are directly consistent with the research results of Atay and Argon [1]. Numerous studies in the literature support these findings, indicating that teachers’ perceptions of school transparency range from moderate [65] to high [66, 67]. According to the qualitative research conducted by Kahveci and Babayiğit [2], teachers believe that the flow of information in their schools is effective and transparent, and that information transfer is detailed, accurate, and reliable. Teachers also emphasized that decisions in schools are made jointly by school administrators and teachers, and that these decisions are communicated to all teachers.

It was found that teachers’ perceptions of school transparency in both general school transparency and participation in decisions and accountability differed significantly in terms of field, while classroom teachers’ perceptions of school transparency in both general school transparency and participation in decisions and accountability were higher than branch teachers. The higher perceptions of school transparency among classroom teachers compared to branch teachers may be due to the fact that classroom teachers, having been at school longer and being responsible for both the academic and social development of their students, communicate more frequently and directly with the administration, leading to greater involvement in school administration decision-making processes. Contrary to these results, a study conducted by Gökçe and Levent [67] found no significant difference between classroom teachers’ and branch teachers’ perceptions of school transparency. The study found that teachers’ perceptions of school transparency in the information flow dimension did not differ significantly across field. Similarly, a study conducted by Gökçe and Levent [67] concluded that teachers’ perceptions of information transparency did not differ across field.

It has been determined that teachers’ general organizational trust levels and their levels of organizational trust in the dimensions of trust in colleagues and trust in administrators do not differ by field. In support of this result, studies by Altunay [15] and Balıkçı [26] also found that teachers’ organizational trust levels did not differ by field. Similarly, in the study conducted by Akpolat and Oğuz [23], no significant difference was found between the trust levels of classroom teachers and branch teachers in the dimensions of trust in administrators and trust in colleagues.

The study found that classroom teachers’ trust in the institution was significantly higher than branch teachers. This difference may be due to the fact that classroom teachers’ sense of belonging to the school environment may have increased due to working mostly in a fixed classroom and structure, while branch teachers’ sense of belonging and trust may have weakened due to their assignments to different classrooms and sometimes to multiple schools.

Teachers’ perceptions of both overall school transparency and three sub-dimensions of school transparency differed significantly by educational background. It was found that teachers with bachelor’s degrees had higher perceptions of both overall school transparency and three sub-dimensions of school transparency than teachers with master’s degrees. Contrary to these results, studies by Atay and Argon [1] and Elekoğlu [66] concluded that teachers’ perceptions of school transparency did not differ by educational background.

It has been found that teachers’ general organizational trust levels and their trust in the institution differ significantly in terms of educational background, and that teachers with bachelor’s degrees have higher levels of both general organizational trust and institutional trust than teachers with postgraduate degrees. Similarly, a study by Teyfur et al. [28] found that teachers with bachelor’s degrees have higher levels of organizational trust than those with postgraduate degrees. The low organizational trust of teachers with postgraduate degrees may be due to the fact that these teachers generally want to advance in their careers or specialize, but when these expectations are not met within the current system, their trust in the organization decreases. Contrary to these results, another study conducted by Gündüz and Saraç [27] found that teachers with bachelor’s and master’s degrees had significantly higher general organizational trust than teachers with associate degrees. It was observed that teachers’ levels of trust in their colleagues and trust in their administrators did not differ by educational background. Similarly, Akpolat and Oğuz’s [23] study found no significant difference between undergraduate and graduate teachers’ trust in colleagues and trust in administrators. Similarly, a study by Balıkçı [26] found that teachers’ organizational trust levels did not differ based on educational background. Generally speaking teachers’ educational backgrounds do not lead to a difference in their trust in colleagues and administrators. However, the decrease in teachers’ overall organizational trust levels as their educational background increases is a situation that requires further investigation.

Teachers’ perceptions of school transparency and organizational trust levels differed significantly across the total score and all sub-dimensions based on the level of education they served. It was determined that primary and secondary school teachers had higher levels of both school transparency and organizational trust than those in high schools. The lower perceptions of school transparency and organizational trust among high school teachers compared to those in primary and secondary schools may be due to the more competitive environment in high schools, particularly due to the university entrance exam preparation process, where decisions are more pressured and made from above, resulting in a decrease in teacher participation in the process. In other words, while academic success is generally the focus in high schools, transparency and trust may be overlooked. Contrary to these results, a study by Akpolat and Oğuz [23] found no significant difference between the trust levels of primary, secondary, and high school teachers in the dimensions of trust in administrators and trust in colleagues.

The study also found that teachers’ perceptions of school transparency and their levels of organizational trust did not differ significantly in terms of gender, marital status, total length of service, or length of service at the current school. These results are consistent with the results of many studies in literature. Some of these studies have found that teachers’ general perceptions of school transparency did not differ in terms of gender [1, 67], marital status [67], total length of service [66, 67], or length of service at the current school [1]. Similarly, some studies in literature have found that teachers’ organizational trust levels do not differ according to variables such as gender [20, 68], total length of service [15, 26), and length of service at the current school [15]. Although there are studies in the literature indicating that teachers’ trust in their administrators [15, 23, 27] and trust in colleagues [15] do not differ in terms of gender, which is consistent with the results of this study, there are also studies showing that male teachers’ perceptions of organizational trust are higher than females in the dimensions of trust in colleagues [23, 27] and trust in stakeholders [27].

The study found a positive, significant, and high-level relationship between school transparency and organizational trust, with the sub-dimensions of school transparency collectively accounting for approximately 61% of the variance in organizational trust. It is important for school administrators to be transparent in their practices and encourage teachers’ participation in decision-making processes, as this increases teachers’ trust in the organization and contributes to their more effective and efficient performance of their duties. In this regard, Tosun and Çelik [33] stated that organizational transparency is an important antecedent of organizational trust, while Gökçe and Levent [67] stated that transparency in school administration contributes to the establishment of an environment of trust in schools. Numerous studies in literature support this finding, demonstrating that school transparency positively impacts teachers. These studies have identified positive relationships between school transparency and organizational commitment [69], school effectiveness [33, 66], family involvement [70], leadership styles [66], management by values [1], and attitudes toward supervision [65]. Similarly, Karaevli and Levent [71], in a study with teachers at different career stages, concluded that transparency in school administration contributes to the professional development of teachers and has positive implications for the healthy functioning of mentoring activities, effective communication, and the establishment of a sustainable environment of trust. A study by Gökçe and Levent [67] found a negative relationship between teachers’ levels of school transparency and their levels of organizational cynicism. In other words, as the level of school transparency increases, teachers’ levels of organizational cynicism decrease.

From a Social Exchange Theory perspective, the findings of the study indicate that leaders’ supportive, trust-based approaches in their social interactions with members foster a sense of reciprocity in employees toward the organization, thus strengthening their motivation to offer constructive ideas about organizational processes [72, 73]. The findings suggest that school transparency behaviors can be considered a socially significant determinant in developing and maintaining perceptions of organizational trust, and in this sense, they support previous studies in the literature [74, 75].

Theoretical and Practical Implications

The findings of this study demonstrate that transparent administrative behaviors contribute to the promotion of the principle of reciprocity among teachers and strengthen the formation of trust. In this context, our research expands social change theory in the educational context by demonstrating that transparency is not only a means of information sharing but also a psychological change factor, and that organizational trust is a reciprocal behavior that emerges as a result of transparency. Furthermore, it reveals that the centralized school structure in Türkiye affects the relationship between transparency and trust due to structural factors such as a hierarchical management structure, centralized authority, and limited accountability at the school level. School administrations can increase transparency and organizational trust by holding regular informational meetings and involving teachers and other stakeholders in school-related decision-making processes.

This study suggests that improvements should be made based on the finding that the importance placed on transparency in schools can contribute to the development of organizational trust. Furthermore, based on the finding that the perception of accountability in the context of school transparency is weaker than other dimensions, it can be concluded that in-school audit processes should be made more visible and feedback mechanisms should be established. To strengthen this perception, internal audit and independent external audit practices can be developed in schools; the results of regular feedback from students, parents, and teachers can be used to inform school improvement efforts and stakeholders. Similarly, the low level of organizational trust among high school participants can be considered a warning sign, suggesting the need to develop support mechanisms to reduce the impact of exam pressure on management processes. The low level of trust among graduates may be attributed to the lack of institutional career opportunities. In this context, career paths can be made more predictable.

This research is limited to the opinions of teachers working in seven different geographical regions across Türkiye. Future research could conduct cross-cultural comparisons with the participation of teachers from different countries. Another limitation of this research is that it is based solely on quantitative data. The relationship between school transparency and organizational trust could be explored more in depth through qualitative studies.
