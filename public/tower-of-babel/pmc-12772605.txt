Mapping the evidence on mHealth interventions for cardiovascular event care in Africa: a scoping review
Authors: Sintieh Nchinda Ngek Ekongefeyin, Guisilla Dedino, Cervantée Wild
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe burden of cardiovascular events in Africa is projected to rise significantly in the coming decade, placing additional strain on already overburdened healthcare systems. Mobile health (mHealth) technologies present a promising approach to addressing these challenges. This scoping review mapped the utilisation of mHealth interventions in cardiovascular event care across Africa.MethodsA systematic search of peer-reviewed and grey literature published between 2013 and 2025 was conducted. Studies were included if they focused on mHealth interventions for cardiovascular event care in Africa. Data were extracted and synthesised, categorising studies by geographical distribution, intervention type and target cardiovascular condition.ResultsA total of 26 studies were identified, comprising small-scale pilots, randomised controlled trials and process evaluations. Geographically, most studies were conducted in Eastern and Western Africa. The majority focused on stroke care (20/26) rather than myocardial infarction (5/26) and predominantly addressed post-event care. The most common technologies used were smartphone applications (11/26) and SMS/phone call interventions (7/26), with one study incorporating artificial intelligence for arrhythmia screening. While positive outcomes were frequently reported, significant challenges included health system constraints, low digital literacy and financial barriers.ConclusionmHealth technologies demonstrate potential in improving cardiovascular event care in Africa, particularly for stroke care. However, their widespread adoption is hindered by systemic and contextual challenges. Addressing these barriers through targeted health system strengthening, digital literacy initiatives and the design of mHealth interventions that are cost-effective, user-friendly and aligned with existing workflows is essential to leveraging mHealth in cardiovascular care.

Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) are the most prevalent non-communicable diseases, accounting for 17.9 million deaths annually.1 Common cardiovascular events include strokes and myocardial infarctions (MIs), claiming about 7.4 and 6.7 million lives due to coronary heart disease and stroke, respectively.2 The burden of CVDs has doubled in Africa,3 where the impact of CVDs is profound. These conditions contribute to 38.3% of non-communicable disease mortality and 22.9 million disability-adjusted life years.4 Despite this escalating burden, the region faces a shortage of personnel and facilities for the diagnosis and management of CVDs.5 To address these challenges, the WHO advocates for the adoption of digital health technologies, including mobile health (mHealth), to mitigate the impact of these shortages.6

mHealth refers to the use of mobile technology in healthcare to improve health outcomes.1 While more advanced technologies are implemented in high-income countries (HICs),7 simpler interventions, such as SMS and phone calls, remain underused in Africa.8 The increasing usage of mobile networks and internet access in the region offers significant opportunities for leveraging mHealth to improve cardiovascular care.9 Although its effectiveness is well-documented in HICs, evidence from Africa remains limited.

Despite the significant impact of cardiovascular events on individuals, health systems and economies in Africa, the use of mHealth in cardiovascular event care is sparsely reported in the region. To our knowledge, no review has scoped the evidence on the use of mHealth in cardiovascular event care in Africa. Existing reviews have predominantly focused on the use of digital health technologies in healthcare more generally,10 in public health policy11 and among vulnerable populations.12

This review aims to understand and map the utilisation of mHealth technologies in cardiovascular event care across Africa. Specifically, we aimed to identify the types of mHealth technology employed, their applications, their placement within the healthcare system and the associated benefits and challenges.

Methods

We conducted a scoping review from 10 January 2023 to 15 May 2025, following the PRISMA-ScR (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Extension for Scoping Reviews) guidelines.13

Search strategy and inclusion criteria

The eligibility criteria (box 1) were developed using the Population-Concept-Context framework.14 We included studies published between 2013 and 2025 that examined mHealth interventions for the prevention, diagnosis or management of cardiovascular events, involving patients or healthcare providers in Africa. Eligible study designs included experimental, observational and qualitative research. We included grey literature from non-governmental organisations like the WHO and the African Society of Cardiologists. However, we did not identify any studies (reports) meeting the inclusion criteria.

We systematically searched MEDLINE, Embase, Africa Index Medicus and Google Scholar, adhering to the PRESS (Peer Review of Electronic Search) checklist V.20. The databases were queried using the following search terms: (“cardiovascular*” OR “heart*” OR “myocardial infarct*” OR “stroke” OR “cerebrovascular” OR “heart failure”) AND (“mHealth” OR “mobile health” OR “telehealth” OR “telemedicine” OR “remote monitoring” OR “wearable*” OR “SMS” OR “text messaging” OR “app” OR “application*”) (online supplemental file S1). We screened the reference lists of included sources to identify additional relevant studies.

For this review, we define mHealth as the delivery of health services (in our case, cardiovascular event care) via mobile communication devices, including but not limited to mobile phones, smartphones, tablets and wearable devices.15 This encompasses a range of applications, including: SMS, mobile applications, unstructured supplementary service data, wearable sensors and devices and mobile phone calls.

Data analysis

The articles generated from all databases were exported to an Excel sheet. Two reviewers (SNNE and GD) independently assessed the articles for eligibility and resolved any discrepancies through consensus, guided by the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) guideline14 and the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria.

To assess the quality of the included studies, we conducted a critical appraisal using the 2018 version of the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT).16 The tool assessed the criteria as ‘Yes’, ‘No’ or ‘can’t tell’. For primary studies employing mixed methods, adherence to at least three out of five quality criteria for each component was required. The AMSTAR 2 (A Measurement Tool to Assess Systematic Reviews) was used to appraise systematic reviews.17

We employed a thematic approach for data analysis and synthesis. Descriptive statistics, including frequencies and percentages, were used to summarise the general characteristics of the extracted data, such as settings, populations, years of publication and study designs for each group. Then, we grouped our findings based on the type of mHealth technology used and whether it was before or after a cardiovascular event.

There was no specific patient and public involvement in the conduct of this review.

The critical appraisal revealed overall high quality of the evidence. 12 studies achieved a perfect MMAT score of 5/5, 7 studies exhibited high quality with a rating of 4/5 and 6 studies were graded 3/5. The systematic review was assessed as good quality, meeting 70% of the AMSTAR 2 criteria (online supplemental file S3).

The articles comprised eight feasibility studies and seven evaluation studies. Three studies used mixed methods approaches, combining feasibility assessment and gathering perspectives from participants and researchers. Notably, 80% of the studies focused on stroke, with only 20% addressing MI, with none covering both cardiovascular events (table 2). We grouped studies by regions using the United Nations geoscheme18 for Africa (figure 2) and by care type (figure 3). Most of the studies were from Eastern and Western Africa, with most of the studies from Ghana and were focused on post-cardiovascular event care. Some studies also highlighted the involvement of family members or caregivers in facilitating the use of digital healthcare services.19,23 However, there was limited research examining acute care and preventive measures for these events. Notably, the current evidence is derived from small-scale studies conducted in urban settings within a single country.

Range of technologies used in cardiovascular event care in Africa

mHealth solutions in cardiovascular event care ranged from basic SMS and phone calls to advanced smartphone applications, social media platforms and web-based tools. Smartphone applications were the most used (40%), followed by text messaging and phone calls (table 2)(figure 4). Some studies explored telemetry and social media platforms like WhatsApp.24,26

Types of technologies.

In stroke care, relatively simple technologies were used. South Africa employed telephone calls for private emergency services in the Stroke Face-Arm-Speech-Time (FAST) Assessment.25 In Ghana, multiple studies focused on Phone-Based Intervention under Nurse Guidance after Stroke (PINGS), using an SMS and a smartphone application connected to a Bluetooth blood pressure monitoring device.19 20 27 28 WhatsApp was incorporated in some PINGS evaluations.20 29 30 Feasibility studies on mobile phone-supported family-centred interventions (F@ce) among stroke survivors and their families employed phone calls and SMS.21,2331 In Benin, a mobile application ‘WalkWithMe’ was piloted to improve mobility among stroke survivors.32 An Egyptian study implemented a comprehensive stroke relay network using the Egyptian Stroke Network app (ESN-App) and a web-based platform.33 Other studies employed phone calls and SMS in stroke rehabilitation and risk assessment.34,36

For MI care, relatively advanced technology was employed. A feasibility study used an artificial intelligence (AI) application, AliveCor Kardia Mobile ECG, to detect heart anomalies.37 In Zimbabwe, mobile cardiac echography and ECG were piloted, incorporating email communication between primary care physicians in Zimbabwe and the USA.38 Similarly, in South Africa, telemetry supported by telephone Bluetooth was used to connect cases with ST-segment elevation MI (STEMI) to the nearest intervention centre using a prehospital mobile ECG.24 In Ivory Coast, the feasibility of tele-ECG in the diagnosis of STEMI was assessed retrospectively39

Where, when and how are mHealth applications used in cardiovascular event care within healthcare systems in Africa?

Fewer studies focused on pre-cardiovascular events. For the studies on MI, a feasibility study demonstrated that screening for cardiovascular event risk factors was possible using the AliveCor Kardia Mobile ECG.37 In Zimbabwe, a study used a mobile ECG and echocardiography to screen populations at high risk of STEMI and other cardiovascular events.38 In South Africa, a study aimed to demonstrate the feasibility of a prehospital multicentric randomised controlled trial (RCT) using Bluetooth telemetry 12-lead ECG for prehospital identification of STEMI.24 Another study in Egypt used WhatsApp to reduce transfer time between home, ambulance, emergency department and percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) centres.26 For stroke, an RCT in Nigeria and Ghana compared standard care with health education and an Afrocentric riskometer app for adults at risk of stroke.36 Also, a retrospective analysis in South Africa examined the use of FAST Assessment phone calls for individuals suspected of having a stroke.25,40Stokes et al found that three RCTs reported moderate blood pressure reduction as an outcome of the technology-based interventions.40 The ESN-App demonstrated how a connected network of facilities could streamline referral, triage and acute management of acute ischaemic stroke.33 The ongoing African Rigorous Innovative Stroke Epidemiological Surveillance (ARISES) study aims to provide further evidence in this area.35 Survivors of subacute and chronic stroke were supported for ten weeks with the WalkWithMe app. The app provided support to participants for 10 weeks by allowing them to log in their steps.32

For post-cardiovascular event, stroke studies reported findings from the perspectives of healthcare providers, patients and caregivers, testing the feasibility of lifelong support. Occupational therapists’ views on mobile phone support following stroke were described in Uganda.34 Feasibility studies were conducted for the F@CE app among patients who had a stroke, evaluating the experiences of caregivers and patients.21 31 The PINGs study in Ghana, which included an app and nurse support, aimed to involve up to 500 stroke survivors and their families in a Phase III trial.19 28 29 PINGs have also been piloted in Nigeria.19 Additionally, studies in Ethiopia reported the design and implementation of an outREACH system for upper limb rehabilitation,41 42 and a telerehabilitation application was piloted among stroke survivors in Ghana.27 In STEMI care, a Tele-ECG network supported health facilities without a cardiologist in the diagnosis and treatment of STEMI. Here, six health facilities were linked to a tertiary centre where the cardiologists provide support in real-time via a secured network to the facilities in need.39

mHealth tools and cardiovascular event outcomes

The outcomes varied across different phases of care and types of events. Pre-cardiovascular event studies mostly demonstrated positive findings. The use of prehospital 12-lead ECG telemetry in South Africa showed a reduction in PCI times for patients with STEMI.25 Similarly, the use of WhatsApp to streamline referrals between hospitals and PCI centres in Egypt significantly reduced PCI times by 20-fold.26 Feasibility studies demonstrated that healthcare professionals could screen for cardiovascular risk factors using mHealth tools.37,39 Likewise, stroke studies’ outcomes were promising. The RCT in Nigeria and Ghana using the Afrocentric riskometer app demonstrated improved global stroke risk scores, stroke awareness and reduction in systolic blood pressure in the intervention group compared with the control group.36 The use of the ESN-App in the stroke network reduced reperfusion time and improved outcomes at 90 days.33 The ongoing ARISES study in Nigeria pilots a community-based mHealth mapping initiative to track and report stroke cases.35

Post-cardiovascular event stroke studies showed positive outcomes. The F@CE App study in Uganda, which aimed to provide a family-centred activity of daily living (ADL) intervention, demonstrated improved performance and self-efficacy metrics in the intervention group compared with the control group.21 31 The PING studies in Ghana showed improved adherence to treatment and ADL for stroke survivors, increased support from nurses and increased uptake of the intervention.20 28 29 36 Tele-rehabilitation studies using smartphone apps showed improved motor benefits and rehabilitation outcomes for stroke survivors.27 41 42 For post-STEMI care, a cardiologist could remotely provide care for patients in facilities without a cardiologist. Diagnosis could be achieved within 12 hours, and up to 50% of patients receive the appropriate care faster.39

Implementation challenges and lessons learnt

A key challenge identified was the division between the costly private sector and the more affordable public sector, limiting the implementation to the private sector and excluding uninsured patients relying on the public sector.24 This is because of the additional cost required to have these technologies, which public hospitals often do not have. Obsolete technology also posed a challenge;24 26 28 however, sometimes existing equipment in host facilities was able to be used, which reduced costs and the need for extensive training on new equipment.19 20 24

Healthcare providers faced challenges with the use of SMS-supported interventions for rehabilitation following a stroke.34 They reported a lack of incentives, scepticism among colleagues and the need for more context-specific messages. However, despite these challenges, the interventions ultimately facilitated their work, enhanced their scientific knowledge and fostered a positive work culture.20 34 The importance of providing refresher courses for long-term studies was also emphasised.20 28 29 For example, overall, the PINGs study was well-received by healthcare providers.29

From the health users’ perspective, challenges were identified during the implementation of the F@CE app and the PINGs study.28 43 44 Users reported issues such as lengthy assessments, incomprehensible text messages and a short study duration that led them to revert to old habits once the study was completed.20 21 29 31 The need for education on prehospital research and mobile phone usage was emphasised,24 and concerns regarding the cost of new phones and internet subscriptions were raised. Even when phones and data subscriptions were provided, some users used the credits for other purposes.19 20 27 Caregivers to stroke survivors reported decreased life satisfaction and increased care burden.22

Discussion

This review highlights the potential of mHealth technologies to improve cardiovascular event care in Africa, with predominantly positive outcomes reported across studies. However, significant infrastructural and sociocultural challenges must be addressed to ensure the effective and equitable adoption of these innovations. Such challenges include limitations within local health systems, factors related to health personnel and barriers faced by patients and their families, for example, low digital literacy, cost, increased care burden and lengthy studies. It is recommended to adapt these innovations to the local context, promote digital literacy and support behavioural change.

Our findings align with previous research in the region, focusing on infectious disease and maternal and child health.45 Furthermore, they align with studies from HICs across diverse healthcare settings.46 47 This suggests that mHealth implementation and uptake are feasible and positive outcomes are potentially achievable when adapted to the context. The focus should be on integrating existing technologies into cardiovascular care and testing novel ones, such as AI and machine learning algorithms. It is noteworthy that SMS/phones have been widely tested,21 25 31 34 35 and by building on the lessons learnt from those experiences, these novel strategies can be piloted in under-researched areas of care, such as long-term care for MIs and the prevention of cardiovascular events.

Our findings suggest the increased use of sophisticated and recent technology, regardless of the type of cardiovascular event or whether it is a pre-cardiovascular or post-cardiovascular event. These findings are consistent with previous studies that emphasised the potential of mHealth in healthcare.48 49 Literature shows the existence of simpler mHealth approaches such as SMS/text and phone calls, along with an increasing number of smartphone applications and the emergence of AI and social media apps like WhatsApp for pilot testing.2124 25 27,29 31 Interestingly, an ongoing study is implementing Voice-over-Internet protocols.35 One approach is to use consumer products from HICs by healthcare professionals in Africa to serve a larger population, as demonstrated by Evans et al with the AliveCor App.37 This aligns with global goals from the WHO and the WHF to achieve a 30% reduction in cardiovascular deaths by 2030.

The focus in Africa has predominantly been on stroke care compared with MI. One possible explanation for this is the higher burden of strokes compared with MIs50 and the relative scarcity of healthcare facilities equipped to diagnose and manage MIs compared with stroke.24 25 However, recent statistics suggest an increasing adoption of technology for MI care.51 In this paper, studies on MIs used more sophisticated technology than those on strokes. This could suggest the possibility of testing and implementing mHealth technologies for MI care and not just stroke care.

The included studies demonstrated successful implementation and positive outcomes predominantly in post-cardiovascular event care. Studies addressing poststroke care have shown successful pilots in promoting adherence to secondary stroke prevention, behavioural change and rehabilitation.19 20 28 31 34 For MIs, successful implementations include screening for cardiovascular risk factors, streamlining referrals, reducing PCI time and remote diagnosis and coagulation therapy.2637,39 Although poststroke care and the prevention of MIs have shown evidence of success, further research is needed in these areas, as well as in post-MI care and prestroke care, where evidence is limited.

Importantly, we do recognise that technologies do not operate on their own. Our study has identified the successful uptake of mHealth technologies by healthcare providers and users, resulting in the positive outcomes reported. Notably, reduced PCI times,26 efficient screening for cardiovascular risk,37 improved post-event outcomes,25 33 quicker rehabilitation2027,29 31 34 36 and increased patient–provider satisfaction have been demonstrated.2027,29 31 34 36 41 42 Healthcare professionals have acknowledged their benefits in enhancing stroke care but have emphasised the importance of contextualisation and support for success.19 20 Caregivers and family members report increased care burden and decreased overall life satisfaction,22 highlighting the need to include these in the use of mHealth interventions in care.

While we expected more research on the utilisation of mHealth in Africa, given the current surge of technology in this setting,9 it is important to acknowledge that these studies were conducted on a small scale and were limited to urban areas and tertiary centres. Generalising these findings should be applied with caution, because larger-scale studies are needed to support and validate these innovative interventions.

The scarcity of data can be partly explained by several challenges. Infrastructural limitations, such as outdated technology and disparities between public and private healthcare systems, hinder the seamless integration of mHealth tools. Additionally, sociocultural barriers, including low digital literacy, cost constraints and the time required for training or assessments, affect the adoption of these technologies. Healthcare professionals also often lack sufficient incentives, such as financial remuneration or clear roles, to engage with mHealth interventions fully, which can limit their widespread use. For interventions to be sustainable, ongoing training, regular follow-up and incorporating user feedback are essential.43 Successful implementation depends on adapting mHealth solutions to local contexts, considering the technological infrastructure and healthcare resources.

Strengths and limitations

To our knowledge, this is the first review to map the evidence on mHealth interventions for cardiovascular event care in Africa. Moreover, we employed a rigorous quality appraisal process, ensuring the reliability and validity of the included studies. Lastly, the review captures recent research from the past decade, making the findings timely and relevant for the current state of digital health.

However, it is possible that relevant studies were missed, particularly in French literature, where no studies were identified. Additionally, given the rapid advancements in digital health, newer studies on emerging technologies may not have been available at the time of this review. There remains a risk of publication bias, with the underreporting of negative or inconclusive pilot studies potentially exaggerating positive outcomes. While we conducted a comprehensive search of the published literature, the lack of relevant grey literature identified may represent a limitation of this review. It is possible that relevant findings exist in unpublished reports or other sources not captured by our search.

Conclusion

This review has highlighted a modest body of small-scale studies, predominantly focused on post-cardiovascular event care, employing smartphone applications and text messaging interventions. The findings emphasise the critical need for healthcare systems in Africa to invest in modern technological infrastructure, including reliable internet connectivity and widespread access to mobile devices, to enable the effective implementation of mHealth solutions. Addressing disparities in technological proficiency will require the development and integration of digital literacy programmes for both healthcare providers and users. While the reviewed studies generally reported positive outcomes, they also underscored persistent challenges, including slow adoption rates, high costs, complex healthcare systems and technological barriers. Although some adoption has occurred in specific contexts, this should not be taken as evidence of broad feasibility without addressing these systemic obstacles. Large-scale trials are needed to rigorously evaluate the efficacy, safety and cost-effectiveness of mHealth interventions in diverse African contexts. Adapting these technologies to local needs, fostering digital literacy and supporting behaviour change initiatives will be pivotal for ensuring both effectiveness and scalability.
