PD-L1+ neutrophils trigger pulmonary endothelial pyroptosis via an oxidative phosphorylation-dependent mechanism in sepsis
Authors: Chenchen Ma, Lei Wang, Xihui Wang, Xuejiao Zhu, Yi Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSepsis-induced acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) is characterized by microvascular dysfunction, uncontrolled inflammation, and pulmonary edema, leading to high morbidity and mortality. Despite their clinical importance, targeted therapies are lacking. Neutrophils play a critical role in sepsis-induced lung injury, but the specific contributions of PD-L1+ neutrophils remain poorly understood. The aim of this study was to elucidate the role of PD-L1+ neutrophils in endothelial injury and the underlying mechanisms during sepsis.MethodsWe employed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) to analyze neutrophil heterogeneity in septic lungs and identified a distinct PD-L1 + neutrophil subpopulation. Using a murine model of sepsis induced by cecal ligation and puncture (CLP), we isolated PD-L1+ neutrophils and assessed their effects on pulmonary vascular endothelial cells (ECs) through coculture experiments. In vivo, PD-L1 was systemically neutralized using a neutralizing antibody to assess the impact of PD-L1 on lung injury and inflammatory responses.ResultsscRNA-seq revealed a unique PD-L1+ neutrophil subpopulation that infiltrated the lungs during sepsis. These neutrophils exacerbated endothelial pyroptosis, leading to vascular barrier dysfunction and increased inflammatory cytokine release. Mechanistically, PD-L1+ neutrophils exhibited a metabolic shift from oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) to glycolysis, which amplified their proinflammatory effects. The systemic neutralization of PD-L1 significantly reduced pulmonary endothelial dysfunction, inflammatory responses, and lung injury in septic mice, as evidenced by decreased vascular permeability, reduced inflammatory cytokine levels, and improved histopathological injury.ConclusionThis study demonstrated that PD-L1+ neutrophils play pivotal roles in driving endothelial pyroptosis and vascular injury during sepsis. The metabolic reprogramming of these neutrophils, characterized by a shift from OXPHOS to glycolysis, underlies their proinflammatory effects. Targeting PD-L1 or modulating metabolic pathways may offer novel therapeutic strategies to improve the clinical prognosis of sepsis-induced ARDS patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12931-025-03412-5.

Introduction

Sepsis-induced acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) is characterized by increased microvascular permeability, dysregulated inflammatory responses, and pulmonary edema, leading to significant morbidity and mortality [1–3]. To date, no targeted therapy exists, and clinical management remains limited to supportive and symptomatic interventions [4, 5]. Consequently, further in-depth research is essential to elucidate the underlying pathophysiological mechanisms of sepsis-induced acute lung injury.

Acute lung injury involves multiple pathological processes, including the loss of vascular integrity, the infiltration of neutrophils, and the massive accumulation of protein-rich fluid in the airspace of the lungs [6]. Neutrophil-mediated inflammation and vascular endothelial cell (EC) injury are unifying factors in the pathogenesis of ARDS caused by sepsis [7, 8]. Polymorphonuclear neutrophils (PMNs) are essential effector cells in host defense and tissue inflammatory responses. Inflammation initiates chemokine-driven neutrophil activation, followed by the neutrophil-derived production of reactive oxygen species and proteases, thus exacerbating lung damage [9]. Additionally, neutrophils directly release various proinflammatory cytokines and chemokines that bind to the vascular endothelium, resulting in an inflammatory cascade that disrupts vascular endothelial permeability, causing capillary leaks that characterize ARDS [10].

PD-L1 is a coinhibitory molecule expressed by antigen-presenting cells and lymphocytes that induces self-tolerance and immune function regulation by interacting with PD-1, negatively regulating T cell activation, and functioning as an immune checkpoint [11]. PD-L1 deficiency can protect mice from death caused by experimental polymicrobial sepsis [12]. Neutrophils isolated from septic patients can upregulate the expression of PD-L1 via an interferon-gamma (IFN-γ)-dependent process, which induces the apoptosis of T cells [12]. Additionally, PD-L1+ neutrophils can increase susceptibility to damage-induced infections via TGF-β-dependent signaling [13].

Given the important regulatory role of neutrophil‒endothelial interactions in sepsis-induced lung injury, we identified a subpopulation of neutrophils specifically marked with PD-L1 that extensively infiltrated damaged lung tissue after sepsis. We found that the systemic blockade of PD-L1 reduced endothelial barrier dysfunction in the lungs and protected animals from infection. These findings could pave the way for the development of more precise neutrophil modulation methods in the pursuit of better sepsis immunotherapy.

Materials and methods

Animals

Male C57BL/6 J mice (6–8 weeks) were obtained from the Hangzhou Institute of Medical Sciences, Chinese Academy of Sciences (Zhejiang, China). All experimental mice were housed and maintained in the specific pathogen-free (SPF) animal facilities. Animal ethics and all experimental protocols were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Hangzhou Institute of Medical Sciences, Chinese Academy of Sciences, Zhejiang, China.

Sepsis model

The cecum ligation and puncture technique (CLP) proposed by Rittirsch et al. to construct the sepsis-ALI model has become a universally accepted modality for modeling [14]. Briefly, mice were anesthetized with 2% isoflurane and fixed in the supine position. After routine shaving and iodine sterilization of the mid-abdominal region, an incision of approximately 1–2 cm was made, and gently pull the ileocecum out of the abdominal cavity through the incision and push the contents of this segment towards the cecum end to fill it up, while ligating it with suture. A 22G needle was then used to create a penetrating wound in the middle and lower 1/3, avoiding blood vessels. Subsequently, the cecum was put back into the abdominal cavity.

According to the manufacturer's requirements, 200 μg of PD-L1 in vivo neutralizing antibody (BioXcell, Cat. No.BE0101) and control antibody were injected into the tail vein of mice within 1 h prior to modeling to mimic PD-L1-deficient and non-deficient mice, respectively. The experimental observation time was 16 h postoperatively based on PD-L1+ PMN intrapulmonary infiltration time and peak inflammation.

Cell culture

Mouse-derived primary lung microvascular endothelial cells (PMVECs) were purchased from MasonCell Biotechnology (Hangzhou, China, Cat. No. CTCC-A001-MIC). Cells were cultured in endothelial cell-specific medium (ECM, ScienceCell, Cat. No. 1001) in a wet incubator at 37℃ with 5% CO2. The cells were passaged every 2 day, and the planting density had to be maintained above 60% of the field of view. The batch of cells received is the 1 st generation, the primary cells can only be passaged 3–4 times.

Isolation of PD-L1+ neutrophils from the bone marrow

Immunocompetent male 4–6 week C57BL/6 mice were established with CLP model. After 2–4 h of modeling, the mice were executed and tibias and femurs were stripped into pre-cooled DMEM medium and then irrigated using a 2 mL syringe (Irrigating medium: RIPM 1640 medium + 0.5 M EDTA + 1% P/S) in the bone marrow lumen. Neutrophils were initially isolated from bone marrow cells using a separation gradient method (TBD, Cat.No.TBD2013NM), and then the purification of neutrophils was continued in accordance with the manufacturer's requirements (Biolgend, cat. No. 480057) and combined with the use of a biotin-coupled-PD-L1 antibody (Biolgend, cat. No.124305) to isolate negative and positive neutrophils. After isolation, neutrophils were stabilized in complete medium(RIPM 1640 medium + 10% FBS + 1% P/S) for 1 h and then cellular experiments were performed.

Flow cytometry

To determine the number of neutrophils and PD-L1+neutrophils, peripheral blood, spleen and lung tissues were collected. Peripheral blood and spleen tissues were prepared as single cell suspensions using mouse lymphocyte isolate (Dakewe, cat. No. DKW33-R0100). Lung tissue samples were first cut into smaller pieces of tissue before being immersed in digestive enzyme solution (DMEM medium + 0.2% Collagenase I/IV + 25 U/10 ml Dispersing enzyme + 0.1 mg/ml DNAse I + 5 mM EDTA), and digested homogeneously at 37 °C for 45 min. According to the manufacturer's (Biolegend) instructions, single-cell suspensions of peripheral blood, spleen, and lung tissues were stained (4 °C, protected from light, 20–30 min) with antibodies against CD45 (APC-Cy7, Cat. No.157203), CD11b (BV421, Cat. No.101236), Ly6g/Gr-1 (APC, Cat. No.108411) and CD274 (PE, Cat. No.124307) and washed after staining with 500ul of pre-cooled PBS. Flow cytometry analysis was performed with BD LSRFortessa™ (BD, USA).

Immunofluorescence

The lung tissues were gently removed and then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, dehydrated twice with 30% sucrose, embedded in an embedding agent, and then sliced on a freezing microtome (Thermo, cat. No. NX70) and finally stained at 6 μm slice thickness. The slices were permeabilized with 0.5% TritonX-100 reagent for 10 min, and blocked with BSA to prevent the primary antibody from binding nonspecifcally. After sealing, the slices were incubated overnight at 4℃ with the following primary antibodies: Ly6g (Abcamab, cat. No. 307167), PD-L1 (Thermo, cat. No. 14–5982,), Next, the sample was incubated with a fuorescent dye-conjugated secondary antibody, which bound to the primary antibody. Finally,the samples were sealed using DAPI fluorescent (SouthernBiotech). And photographs were recorded using confocal microscopy (Nikon,cat. No. A1 HD25).

Lung histopathological damage

The lung samples were gently and completely removed and subsequently fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 48 h and then dehydrated by ethanol solution with different concentrations for 40 min. Following paraffin embedding, the samples were preserved for the subsequent experiments. The sections were sliced into 6 μm sections and placed in xylene, anhydrous ethanol, and alcohol to dewax in sequence. Hematoxylin–eosin (HE) was utilized to stain the lung samples. The histological photographs were observed by optical microscopy (Nikon, Japan).

Evans blue–albumin pulmonary transvascular flux measurements

Mice were injected with 0.5% (0.5 mg/ml) Evans Blue 200ul in the tail vein within 1 h prior to sampling, anesthetized with 1.25% tribromoethanol(0.2 ml/10 g). Exposing the thorax and cutting open the left auricle, and the lungs were perfused through the right ventricle (Pre-cooled PBS/500 ml + 0.5 M EDTA/5 ml) until the outflow from the left ventricle was clear fluid. Subsequently, the irrigated lung tissue was photographed, weighed, and added to pre-cooled PBS (V ul = lung tissue weight mg) for homogenization(TissueLyser II). After that, Formamide (V = twice PBS) was added and the reaction was carried out in a water bath at 60℃ for 18 h. The supernatant was centrifuged at 12,000 g for 30 min, and measuring the absorbance (OD) at 620 nm on an Microplate Reader. Finally, it is necessary to calculate the percentage of Evans Blue in the lung tissue (μg/g) [15, 16].

Bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF)

Mice were anesthetized and the trachea was exposed by blunt dissection. Tracheotomy was performed using ophthalmic scissors, and an intravenous indwelling cannula needle was inserted into the incision and secured with the surgical suture. A 1 ml syringe was connected for irrigation, and the procedure was repeated twice (the first time 0.7 ml; the second time 0.8 ml) for a total of 1.5 ml, each time with a 10 s dwell time and a slow withdrawing. The collected lavage fluid was centrifuged (1000 rpm, 10 min, 4 °C) to separate the supernatant and cellular precipitate. The supernatant was used to determine the protein concentration using a BCA kit, and the cell precipitate was used for cell counting using a cell counter.

ELISA assay

The expression levels of inflammatory factors and chemokines, such as TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6 and MCP-1 were detected using a commercialized ELISA kit (MultiSciences, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Fluorescein isothiocyanate-dextran (FITC-dextran) assay

PMVECs were planted in 24-well transwell plates at a density of 1 × 105 cells/mL. After the cells were observed to grow to confluence, the cells were co-cultured with neutrophils (1:10). After 12 h cultivation, all groups were treated by adding 40 kDa FITC-dextran solution (0.5 mg/mL) for 1 h with light protection in a 5% CO2 incubator at 37℃. The fluorescence values were measured by Multimode Microplate Reader (Tecan Spark 10 M). The permeability coefficient as Pa% = (Pa value of the lower chamber fluorescence + Pa value of the upper chamber fluorescence)/Pa value of the lower chamber fluorescence × 100%.

Lactate and ATP assay

PMVECs were seeded at a density of 3 × 103 cells/well in 96-Well White Opaque Plates and requested by experimental group whether or not to co-cultivate with neutrophils. Finally, the kit instructions (DOJINDO Laboratories, Cat. No.G270) were followed to measure lactate in the supernatant and ATP in the cells.

Quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted from lung tissues and cells using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen Life Technologies) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (Real-time qPCR) was subsequently realized by Taq Pro Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix(Vazyme, cat. no. Q712-02) using a Roche Light-Cycler 4800II real-time PCR system (Roche). The primer sequences are detailed in Supplementary Table S1.

Protein lysate (Containing a complete protease inhibitor cocktail) was utilized to extract total protein from lung tissue and endothelial cells, and then the protein concentrations were determined using a BCA protein assay kit (Pierce, USA). Briefly, the proteins were separated by SDS‒PAGE with a suitable concentration gradient and transferred to PVDF (Polyvinylidene-Fluoride) membranes (Hybond-P, GE Healthcare, Singapore). The membranes were incubated with 5% non-fat milk for 1 h and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies, including ZO-1 (Proteintech, cat. No. 21773–1-AP), ICAM-1 (ABclonal, cat. No. 12505S). cat. no. 12507 s)/cleaved (CST, cat. no. 63124 s), NLRP3 (Beyotime, cat. no. AF2155). Membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h. Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed by using Graphpad Prism 9.4 and the data are shown as means ± standard deviation (SD). Groups were compared by using the one-way ANOVA or the T test. p < 0.05 was considered a statistically significant difference.

Results

scRNA-seq reveals highly heterogeneous neutrophils in the septic lung

To explore the transcriptional changes associated with sepsis-induced lung damage at the single-cell level, we sorted cells from lung tissues from mice approximately 24 h after CLP or sham surgery (Fig. S1a), generating a thorough map of the 18 most distinctive transcriptome subpopulations (Fig. S1b). Moreover, we found that neutrophils from the lungs of septic mice presented increased contact with other cell types, including monocytes, endothelial cells, macrophages and fibroblasts, compared with neutrophils from the lungs of sham mice (Fig. S1c). These results suggest that the increased contact of neutrophils with other cells, such as monocytes, fibroblasts and endothelial cells, may play an important role in the development and progression of sepsis-induced lung injury.

To investigate the detailed transcriptional changes in highly heterogeneous neutrophils after sepsis, we identified six distinct neutrophil subclusters, i.e., Neu0–Neu5 (Fig. 1a), which all expressed canonical neutrophil genes, including S100a8, S100a9 and Chil1 (Fig. 1b). We defined the above Neu0 to Neu5 subclusters using Ccrl2, Cd274, Malat1, Fabp5, Arf2, and Smc6, respectively (Fig. 1d). Sepsis dramatically altered the proportion of neutrophil subclusters. In the sham group, 9% of the neutrophils were Neu0, 34% were Neu2, 32% were Neu3, and 3% were Neu4; in septic mice, 34% of the neutrophils were Neu0, 10% were Neu2, 8% were Neu3 and 25% were Neu4 (Fig. 1c). These distinct gene expression patterns suggested that each neutrophil cluster exhibited a previously undefined transcriptional profile. We performed Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analyses of neutrophils, and the results revealed that most microglial subclusters after stroke presented increased cell‒cell adhesion, immune system processes and leukocyte migration (Fig. 1e). In the Neu0 subcluster, we identified upregulated genes involved in oxidative phosphorylation and ATP metabolic processes. The Neu5 subcluster was enriched in ribosomal biogenesis and RNA splicing (Fig. 1e-f). Identification of PD-L1+ neutrophil subclusters associated with sepsis-induced lung injury

PD-L1+ neutrophils exacerbate pulmonary vascular endothelial pyroptosis in septic mice

To assess the influence of PD-L1+ neutrophils on pulmonary vascular endothelial function, we isolated PD-L1+ Neu and PD-L1− Neu cells by immunomagnetic bead separation (IMS) technology and cocultured them with pulmonary microvascular endothelial cells (PMVECs) stimulated with 500 ng/mL LPS. We measured the expression of inflammatory factors (IL-1β, IL-6 and TNF-α), adhesion molecules (CD62E and VCAM-1), and the chemokine CXCL2 in PMVECs at various time points. Compared with cocultured PD-L1- Neu/PMVECs, cocultured PD-L1 + Neu/PMVECs presented greater proinflammatory properties at 4 h (Fig. 3a). Subsequently, endothelial barrier permeability was assessed by measuring permeability to FITC-labeled dextran. The permeability coefficient of FITC-dextran was significantly greater in the PD-L1+Neu group than in the PD-L1−Neu group (Fig. 3b). The immune system has evolved multiple mechanisms to restrict infection and regulate inflammatory responses. Cell death is an important mechanism of immunity in the host defense against pathogens [17]. Specifically, pyroptosis, a newly identified mode of programmed cell death that is distinct from cell necrosis and apoptosis, is the main consequence of sepsis-induced vascular endothelial cell injury [18]. To investigate whether the exacerbation of endothelial injury by PD-L1+ neutrophils is associated with pyroptosis, the expression levels of pyroptosis-related proteins were detected. As shown in Fig. 3d, the expression of the key pyroptosis proteins NLRP3, GSDMD, cleaved GSDMD, IL-1β, and cleaved IL-1β was significantly greater in the PD-L1+ neutrophil coculture group than in the control group. Collectively, these results show that PD-L1+ neutrophils aggravate pulmonary EC barrier dysfunction and induce vascular endothelial pyroptosis during sepsis.

Systemic PD-L1 neutralization reduces pulmonary endothelial dysfunction and inflammatory responses after sepsis

Given that PD-L1+ neutrophils aggravate pulmonary EC injury during sepsis, we subsequently investigated the effects of PD-L1 on pulmonary endothelial function and the inflammatory response in vivo. We blocked PD-L1 via the intravenous injection of a neutralizing antibody, and PD-L1+ neutrophils in the lung were almost completely absent (Fig. S3a-b). Compared with control antibody treatment, anti-PD-L1 treatment significantly improved lung injury, as reflected by decreased intra-alveolar and interstitial edema (Fig. 6a). PD-L1 deletion significantly alleviated CLP-stimulated pulmonary edema, as evidenced by cell confluence and protein leakage in BALF (Fig. 6b-c). We also utilized ELISAs to measure the levels of inflammatory cytokines and chemokines in serum. The CLP group presented elevated levels of IL-1β, IL-6, MCP-1 and TNF-α in BALF; however, these levels decreased after the administration of a PD-L1 neutralizing antibody (Fig. 6d). Parallel changes in the levels of inflammatory cytokines were observed in the lung tissues (Fig. S4a). Furthermore, CLP damaged the pulmonary vascular endothelium, as evidenced by an increased degree of EBA extravasation; however, these effects were also reduced by PD-L1 blockade (Fig. 6e). We also observed downregulated VE-cadherin and ZO-1 expression. However, upon PD-L1 blockade, the expression of these adhesion molecules increased at both the gene and protein levels (Fig. 6f&S4b). Discussion

Sepsis-induced ARDS is a life-threatening condition characterized by microvascular dysfunction, uncontrolled inflammation, and pulmonary edema [19]. During sepsis, neutrophils are the first immune cells recruited to sites of infection or injury [20]. While neutrophils are essential for host defense, their excessive activation can lead to collateral tissue damage, particularly to the vascular endothelium [10]. Our study focused on the interaction between PD-L1+ neutrophils and endothelial cells and revealed that these neutrophils play a critical role in driving endothelial pyroptosis. This process exacerbates vascular injury, disrupts endothelial barrier function, and amplifies systemic inflammation, ultimately contributing to the high morbidity and mortality associated with sepsis-induced ARDS. These findings underscore the importance of neutrophil‒endothelial interactions in sepsis pathogenesis and highlight potential therapeutic targets to mitigate endothelial damage.

PD-L1, a key immune checkpoint molecule, is known to interact with its receptor PD-1 to suppress T cell activation and promote immune tolerance [21]. In sepsis, PD-L1 expression is upregulated in various immune cells, including monocytes, macrophages, and neutrophils, as well as in nonimmune cells, such as endothelial cells [22]. Neutrophils can also inhibit T cell activation, promote T cell differentiation, and increase the number of regulatory T cells (Tregs) through direct contact [23]. In addition, PD-L1+ neutrophils have been show to have the potential to predict and diagnose sepsis, and an anti-PD-L1 antibody has been shown to protect mice from inflammation [13]. In our study, single-cell RNA sequencing analysis revealed a distinct subpopulation of PD-L1-expressing neutrophils that demonstrated marked activation under septic conditions, suggesting that PD-L1 is a promising therapeutic target for alleviating sepsis-associated acute lung injury.

Vascular endothelial injury is a central event in the pathogenesis of sepsis [24]. During sepsis, endothelial cells are exposed to pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) and damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), leading to the activation of inflammatory signaling pathways, the upregulation of adhesion molecule expression, and increased vascular permeability, which collectively contribute to microvascular dysfunction and organ injury [25]. Importantly, the fate of endothelial cells whether they undergo apoptosis, necrosis, or pyroptosis plays a critical role in determining the severity of sepsis [26]. Pyroptosis, in particular, is a highly inflammatory form of cell death characterized by cell membrane rupture and the release of intracellular contents, which further amplifies the inflammatory cascade [27, 28]. Our findings indicate that PD-L1+ neutrophils exacerbate endothelial pyroptosis, thereby perpetuating vascular injury and systemic inflammation in sepsis. These findings underscore the pivotal role of endothelial cell fate in the progression of sepsis and suggest that targeting endothelial cell death pathways could be a promising therapeutic strategy.

Emerging evidence suggests that metabolic reprogramming, particularly the shift from oxidative phosphorylation to glycolysis, plays a crucial role in determining cell fate during sepsis [29]. Under physiological conditions, endothelial cells primarily rely on oxidative phosphorylation for energy production. However, during sepsis, the inflammatory microenvironment induces a metabolic shift toward glycolysis, a phenomenon known as the "Warburg effect" [30–32]. This shift is driven by the upregulation of hypoxia-inducible factor 1-alpha (HIF-1α) expression and the activation of mTOR signaling, which promote glycolytic enzyme expression and suppress mitochondrial function [33]. While glycolysis provides a rapid energy supply, it also leads to the accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and lactate, creating a pro-oxidant environment that exacerbates cellular stress. Importantly, this metabolic reprogramming has been linked to the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome, a key mediator of pyroptosis [34]. Our data demonstrate that PD-L1+ neutrophils exhibit increased glycolysis and impaired oxidative phosphorylation, which not only amplifies their inflammatory potential but also promotes endothelial pyroptosis. These findings highlight the intricate connection between metabolic reprogramming and cell fate in sepsis, particularly the role of glycolysis in driving pyroptosis.

In this study, a mouse model of sepsis was induced by abdominal infection. Although CLP-induced abdominal infection is a standard model for sepsis research, we did not employ other models of sepsis induced at other infection sites, which is a limitation of this study. Moreover, a notable limitation of our study is the lack of conditional knockout mice to validate the function of PD-L1 in neutrophils during sepsis. While our single-cell RNA sequencing data strongly suggest the involvement of PD-L1-expressing neutrophils in sepsis-induced lung injury, the lack of neutrophil-specific PD-L1 knockout mice prevents us from establishing a definitive causal relationship. The development of S100a8-Cre- or Mrp8-Cre-mediated PD-L1 conditional knockout mice would enable a more precise investigation of neutrophil-specific PD-L1 functions in future studies. These technical limitations highlight the need for more sophisticated genetic tools to fully elucidate the cell type-specific functions of PD-L1 in sepsis pathogenesis.

In summary, our study highlights the pivotal role of PD-L1+ neutrophils in driving endothelial pyroptosis during sepsis, providing a deeper understanding of the cellular and molecular mechanisms underlying sepsis-induced ARDS. The metabolic shift from oxidative phosphorylation to glycolysis in both neutrophils and endothelial cells has emerged as a critical factor in promoting pyroptosis and exacerbating vascular injury. Future research should focus on developing therapeutic interventions aimed at modulating PD-L1 expression, metabolic pathways, and pyroptosis to protect endothelial integrity and improve outcomes in sepsis patients.
