Correlated Growth of Causal Networks
Jiazhen Liu, Kunal Tamang, Dashun Wang, Chaoming Song

Abstract
The study of causal structure in complex systems has gained increasing attention, with many recent studies exploring causal networks that capture cause-effect relationships across diverse fields. Despite increasing empirical evidence linking causal structures to network topological correlations, the mechanisms underlying the emergence of these correlations in causal networks remain poorly understood. In this work, we propose a general growth framework for causal networks, incorporating two key types of correlations: causal and dynamic. We analytically demonstrate that degree correlations emerge as a consequence of marginal dependencies on these correlations. Our theoretical predictions align quantitatively with empirical data from four large-scale innovation networks. Our theory not only sheds light on the origins of topological correlations but also provides a general framework for understanding correlated growth across causal systems.

Theoretical Framework.

Consider a causal network model that starts with
n_{0}
events at time
t=0
. As the network evolves, each newly added event
i
connects to
m_{i}
preexisting events introduced at earlier times, yielding a DAG that ensures causality. Additionally, each event
i
is assigned a state
\mathbf{s}_{i}
, which may include both discrete and continuous attributes. For instance, in a social network,
\mathbf{s}_{i}
could encode the type of interaction (discrete) alongside its intensity or duration (continuous). To capture a wide range of scenarios, we divide
\mathbf{s}_{i}
into two subspaces: an observed component
\mathbf{s}_{\mathrm{obs}}
, reflecting publicly accessible properties, and a latent component
\mathbf{s}_{\mathrm{lat}}
, encoding hidden variables that influence network evolution.

Once an event with state
\mathbf{s}
is created at time
t
, its causal relationships can continue to grow. We capture this individual-level growth via a Green’s function
G_{\mathbf{s}}(k,\tau|k^{\prime},\tau^{\prime})
, which gives the probability that an event with
k^{\prime}
connections at age
\tau^{\prime}
grows to
k
connections at a later age
\tau
. Here,
\tau^{\prime}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=t^{\prime}-t_{i}
and
\tau\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=t-t_{i}
, measured from the event’s creation time
t_{i}
. The corresponding mean accumulative connection,
\overline{k}(\mathbf{s},\tau)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\sum_{k}kG_{\mathbf{s}}(k,\tau|0,0)
, then tracks how an event’s expected connections evolve over its lifetime. As an example, a Poisson process with rate
\lambda
yields
G_{\lambda}(k,\tau|k^{\prime},\tau^{\prime})=\frac{\left[\lambda(\tau-\tau^{\prime})\right]^{k-k^{\prime}}e^{-\lambda(\tau-\tau^{\prime})}}{(k-k^{\prime})!}
, and
\overline{k}(\mathbf{s},\tau)=\lambda\tau
. However, for a process with temporal correlation, the corresponding Green’s function
G
may exhibit a fat tail rather than an exponential decay. In our framework,
G
remains a general input to accommodate different growth processes.

Global network growth arises from the collective growth of individual events. To describe this process, we focus on
n(\mathbf{s},t)
, the rate at which new events with state
\mathbf{s}
appear. The directed edges from newly introduced vertices to existing ones encode causal correlations; for example, an “unfollow” action can only occur after a corresponding “follow.” To capture these correlations, we employ a mean-field causal kernel
K(\mathbf{s},t|\mathbf{s}^{\prime},t^{\prime})
, which gives the probability that a vertex created at time
t
has state
\mathbf{s}
given that it links to a vertex with state
\mathbf{s}^{\prime}
created at an earlier time
t^{\prime}<t
. The event-creation rate then satisfies

\displaystyle n(\mathbf{s},t)=n_{0}(\mathbf{s})\delta(t)+

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\langle k\rangle}\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\int\mathrm{d}\mathbf{s}^{\prime}\;n(\mathbf{s}^{\prime},t^{\prime})K(\mathbf{s},t|\mathbf{s}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\partial_{t}\overline{k}(\mathbf{s}^{\prime},t-t^{\prime}),

(1)

where
\langle k\rangle
is the average degree. For simplicity, in the discussion below, we consider the case of a fixed outdegree,
m_{i}=m
, so that
\langle k\rangle=m
. In more general cases,
m_{i}
can be incorporated into the state
\mathbf{s}_{i}
.

To solve Eq. (
1
), we focus primarily on its stationary solution. In this regime,
n(\mathbf{s},t)
factorizes asymptotically as
n(t)\psi(\mathbf{s})
, where
\psi(\mathbf{s})
is the stationary state distribution, and

n(t)\equiv\int n(\mathbf{s},t)\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{s}\;=\;\frac{\mathrm{d}N(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}

is the total growth rate of the network size. One can show that the existence of such a stationary solution requires the growth process to exhibit time-translation invariance (TTI), i.e.
K(\mathbf{s},t|\mathbf{s}^{\prime},t^{\prime})=K(\mathbf{s}|\mathbf{s}^{\prime},t-t^{\prime})
and
n(t)/n(t^{\prime})\sim f(t-t^{\prime})
, so that only time differences matter (see SM Section 1.2). Under these conditions, we predict an exponential growth

n(t)\,\sim\,e^{rt},

(2)

where
r
is the network growth rate. The analysis above also implies the asymptotic state-age distribution,

\Psi(\mathbf{s},\tau)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{n(\mathbf{s},t-\tau)}{\int_{0}^{t}n(\mathbf{s},t-\tau)d\tau}=\psi(\mathbf{s})re^{-r\tau},

(3)

factorizes into the product of the state and age distributions.

Substituting Eq. (
2
) into Eq. (
1
) and taking
t\to\infty
yields leads the stationary master equation

\psi(\mathbf{s})=\frac{1}{\langle k\rangle}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\tau\int\mathrm{d}\mathbf{s}^{\prime}K(\mathbf{s}|\mathbf{s}^{\prime},\tau)\partial_{\tau}{\overline{k}(\mathbf{s}^{\prime},\tau)}e^{-r\tau}\psi(\mathbf{s}^{\prime}),

(4)

subject to
\int\mathrm{d}\mathbf{s}\,\psi(\mathbf{s})=1
. Given the causal kernel
K
and the individual growth Green’s function
G
, which defines
\overline{k}
, this self-consistent equation determines both
r
and
\psi(\mathbf{s})
in the thermodynamic limit, along with all physical observables to be discussed.

Network Characteristics.
Once
\psi(\mathbf{s})
and
r
are obtained from Eq. (
4
), the state-age distribution
\Psi(\mathbf{s},\tau)
can be determined using Eq. (
3
), enabling an analytical computation of various network characteristics. We now outline the computation of the degree distribution
P(k)
, which represents the probability of finding a vertex with in-degree
k
, and the joint degree distribution
P(k,k^{\prime})
, which gives the probability of finding an edge from a vertex with in-degree
k
to a vertex with in-degree
k^{\prime}
. The latter is a fundamental measure that characterizes network correlations.

A key quantity in our framework for understanding these network characteristics is the joint state-age distribution,

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Psi}(\mathbf{s},\tau;\mathbf{s}^{\prime},\tau^{\prime})\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\frac{1}{\langle k\rangle}K(\mathbf{s}|\mathbf{s}^{\prime},\tau^{\prime}-\tau)\partial_{\tau^{\prime}}{\overline{k}(\mathbf{s}^{\prime},\tau^{\prime}-\tau)}\Psi(\mathbf{s}^{\prime},\tau^{\prime}),

which quantifies the probability of finding an edge from a vertex with state
\mathbf{s}
and age
\tau
to a vertex with state
\mathbf{s}^{\prime}
and age
\tau^{\prime}
(
\tau^{\prime}>\tau
). Using Eq. (
4
), one obtains the marginal probabilities at either of these two vertices:

\int\mathbf{\Psi}(\mathbf{s},\tau;\mathbf{s}^{\prime},\tau^{\prime})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{s}^{\prime}\mathrm{d}\tau^{\prime}=\Psi(\mathbf{s},\tau)

and

\int\mathbf{\Psi}(\mathbf{s},\tau;\mathbf{s}^{\prime},\tau^{\prime})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{s}\mathrm{d}\tau=\overline{k}(\mathbf{s}^{\prime},\tau^{\prime})\Psi(\mathbf{s}^{\prime},\tau^{\prime})/\langle k\rangle.

Since
G_{\mathbf{s}}(k,\tau|0,0)
gives the probability that a vertex with state
\mathbf{s}
and age
\tau
attains in-degree
k
, the degree distribution is given by

P(k)=\int\mathrm{d}\mathbf{s}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\tau\;G_{\mathbf{s}}(k,\tau|0,0)\Psi(\mathbf{s},\tau),

which can be interpreted as the averaged Green’s function,

P(k)=\left\langle G_{\mathbf{s}}(k,\tau|0,0)\right\rangle,

(5)

where the average
\left\langle\ldots\right\rangle\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\int\ldots\mathbf{\Psi}(\mathbf{s},\tau;\mathbf{s}^{\prime},\tau^{\prime})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{s}\mathrm{d}\tau\mathrm{d}\mathbf{s}^{\prime}\mathrm{d}\tau^{\prime}
is taken over the joint state-age distribution. Similarly, one finds (see SM Section 1.3)

\displaystyle P(k,k^{\prime})=\left\langle\frac{k^{\prime}}{\overline{k}(\mathbf{s}^{\prime},\tau^{\prime})}G_{\mathbf{s}^{\prime}}(k^{\prime},\tau^{\prime}|0,0)G_{\mathbf{s}}(k,\tau|0,0)\right\rangle,

(6)

where
k^{\prime}
represents the number of edges connecting to the vertex, and
\overline{k}(\mathbf{s}^{\prime},\tau^{\prime})
ensures that
\frac{k^{\prime}}{\overline{k}(\mathbf{s}^{\prime},\tau^{\prime})}G_{\mathbf{s}^{\prime}}(k^{\prime},\tau^{\prime}|0,0)
is properly normalized. Equation (
6
) implies that
k
and
k^{\prime}
are conditionally independent given the state and age, with their overall correlation mediated by the marginal dependencies in the joint state-age distribution. In other words, degree correlation is entirely encoded in the state-age correlation within
\mathbf{\Psi}
.

Empirical Validation.

To validate our theory against empirical data, we applied it to the scientific innovation system, which can be understood as a causal network, where the creation of innovations causally depends on existing knowledge. We used the Web of Science (WOS) dataset spanning 1970–2014, comprising 44M publications and 800M citations. For simplicity, the causal network considered here is the citation network, focusing on events of paper publication with a single type of causal relationship
\mathbf{s}_{\text{obs}}=
“cite”, which links new knowledge to its intellectual predecessors. Using the WOS dataset, we extracted four citation networks across different disciplines, including biology, chemistry, mathematics, and physics, comprising 13M, 9M, 3M, and 7M vertices, respectively (see SM Section 2.1).

Existing studies suggest that the growth process at the individual level follows a reinforced Poisson process (RPP), incorporating both preferential attachment and aging
[

\Lambda{(k,\tau)}=\lambda\phi(\tau)(k+k_{0})

\lambda

\phi(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma t}\exp\left[-\frac{(\ln{t}-\mu)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right]

\overline{k}(\lambda,t)=k_{0}\left(\exp\left(\lambda\int_{0}^{t}\phi(t^{\prime})\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\right)-1\right),

(7)

which implies a finite ultimate degree
\overline{k}_{\infty}(\lambda)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\overline{k}(\lambda,\infty)=k_{0}\left(e^{\lambda}-1\right)

7

\lambda

k_{0}
,
\mu
, and
\sigma
to each paper, leading to a large number of free parameters that are experimentally determined. In particular, the number of parameters scales with the number of vertices,

Our theory addresses the above drawbacks by treating individual parameters as latent states,

4

4

K

\displaystyle K(\lambda|\lambda^{\prime},t)=\frac{1}{\overline{\lambda}(\lambda^{\prime},t)}\mathcal{K}\left(\frac{\lambda}{\overline{\lambda}(\lambda^{\prime},t)}\right).

Here, the mean fitness

Discussion.
Our study demonstrates that topological correlations in causal networks naturally originate from fundamental causal and dynamical correlations during network growth. Analytical results explicitly reveal that these correlations induce marginal dependencies, which in turn generate observable degree correlations. We validate our theoretical framework by applying it to empirical citation graphs, which serve as representative real-world examples.

Compared to existing fitness-based models, which assign a separate fitness value to each vertex and require
O(N)
parameters for a network with
N
vertices, these models often suffer from overfitting, thereby limiting predictive power. In contrast, our model introduces causal dependencies between fitness values, encoded in a causal kernel, which reduces the number of free parameters from
O(N)
to
O(1)
. This structured approach mitigates overfitting while improving both explanatory and predictive capabilities. In this way, our method addresses notable limitations of existing models
[
12
,
11
]
, demonstrating that causal dependencies provide a more compact and generalizable framework for modeling real-world networks.

While our theory successfully captures key causal dependencies, several open questions remain. One major challenge is the incorporation of time-varying state distributions, particularly in causal networks exhibiting non-stationary growth. If Equation (
4
) admits no solution, stationarity breaks down. This can occur when the causal kernel has sufficiently broad tails. Consequently, the network undergoes super-exponential growth, violating TTI. This scenario is closely related to the “winner-takes-all” phase, where a small fraction of vertices accumulate a disproportionate share of influence, analogous to Bose-Einstein condensation
[
25
]
. Since this behavior does not arise in the empirical data we analyze, we defer its detailed investigation to future work. Another important direction is to explore topological correlations beyond the nearest-neighbor level. Higher-order structural dependencies may reveal additional causal mechanisms governing network formation, further enriching our understanding of dynamical correlations in complex systems.

Despite these open challenges, our findings provide a robust foundation for understanding correlations in causal networks. By incorporating causal correlation kernels, our framework applies to a wide range of systems, including social media, biological evolution, and economic growth. This approach offers new insights into the interplay between causal structure and network topology, with broad implications across physics, economics, social sciences, and biology.
