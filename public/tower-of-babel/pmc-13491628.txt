Enhancing the degradation of cellulose and hemicellulose in chili pepper straw waste using Cellulomonas iranensis 7–12, which was isolated from naturally decayed chili pepper straw
Authors: Mengting Chen, Zhiye Tian, Jiaya Chen, Xiaoping Li, Yongjun Wu, Xilong Shen, Shuoqiu Tong, Jin Jin, Cen Li, Meng Zhao, Liangcai Xiong, Sana Gul, Lintao Ren, Lincheng Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The sustainable valorization of agricultural waste, such as chili pepper straw, is often challenged by the absence of effective microbes that can degrade cell wall components. In this study, metagenomic analysis found that Pseudomonadota was the dominant phylum in the carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC)-enriched microbial communities. In addition, a cellulolytic bacterial strain, designated as Cellulomonas iranensis 7–12, was isolated from naturally decayed chili pepper straw and identified by colony morphology, Gram staining, 16 S rRNA gene sequencing, and genome-based average nucleotide identity (ANI) analysis. Within 30 h, C. iranensis 7–12 displayed robust cellulolytic activity, causing nearly complete disintegration of filter paper, a cellulose model substrate. In contrast, chili pepper straw, a structurally more complex lignocellulosic substrate, was only partially degraded, with dry-weight loss increasing from 11.98% in the uninoculated control to 32.63% after 4 d of fermentation with C. iranensis 7–12. C. iranensis 7–12 exhibited a predominantly extracellular cellulase–xylanase activity profile, with extracellular xylanase activity reaching 3.41 U/mL and exceeding the measured cellulase activities. Whole-genome sequencing of C. iranensis 7–12 identified a complete 3.79-Mb circular chromosome and a diverse CAZyme repertoire, including glycoside hydrolase families related to cellulose and hemicellulose degradation, carbohydrate-binding modules, carbohydrate esterases, and secretion-associated proteins. Moreover, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) examination revealed that the surface and internal microstructure of chili pepper straw were disrupted. Similarly, Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy analysis showed marked changes in the characteristic absorption bands associated with cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin-containing structures, indicating partial degradation of polysaccharide components and lignin-associated structural alteration. Collectively, C. iranensis 7–12 shows great potential for the bioconversion of chili pepper straw and the high-performance microbes will be further developed for the effective use of biomass resources.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-026-05520-8.

Introduction

Lignocellulosic agricultural residues are among the most abundant renewable biomass resources [1, 2]. However, the efficient utilization of lignocellulosic biomass through affordable and durable conversion technologies remains limited. Traditional disposal methods, including open-field burning and landfilling, result in the loss of valuable biomass resources and significantly contribute to environmental harm, such as greenhouse gas emissions and air pollution [2]. Thus, it has become an urgent global necessity to create economical and environmentally safe strategies for utilizing agricultural waste [3].

The extensive cultivation of chili pepper generates substantial quantities of chili pepper straw after harvest. However, the effective utilization of this residue remains challenging and comparatively understudied. The lignocellulosic material found in chili pepper straw presents a distinct structural complexity when compared to agricultural residues such as rice straw. Besides cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin, chili pepper straw has tightly packed vascular tissues and abundant cuticular components, which may reduce microbial attachment and limit enzyme access to internal polysaccharides [4]. Moreover, pepper plants accumulate capsaicinoids and other phenolic secondary metabolites as defensive substances, which have antimicrobial activity and may further restrict microbial colonization during natural decomposition [5]. Thus, chili pepper straw is a more intricate biomass substrate due to its physical barriers and chemical resistance, which makes its efficient microbial degradation particularly challenging. Therefore, microorganisms naturally present in decayed chili pepper straw may include populations adapted to these physical and chemical barriers, providing valuable microbial resources for the efficient conversion of chili pepper straw.

Using microbes to degrade lignocellulosic biomass has emerged as a promising biotechnological strategy, providing environmentally friendly and energy-efficient advantages [6, 7]. Recent studies have demonstrated that some lignocellulose-degrading bacteria can enhance the value of agricultural wastes by producing enzymes that degrade cellulose and hemicellulose. For instance, Fictibacillus sp. YS-26 has been reported to degrade lignocellulosic residues of banana straw by producing cellulolytic enzymes and xylanase [8]. Recently, Bacillus subtilis K35-1 was isolated from yak rumen fluid and reported to be a highly efficient cellulolytic bacterium, showing strong Congo red hydrolysis, high cellulase and hemicellulase activities, and substantial cellulose reduction in forage substrates [9]. Generally, cellulose degradation involves three essential enzymes: endoglucanases, exoglucanases, and β-glucosidases. Research has extensively focused on fungal cellulolytic systems, notably from Trichoderma and Aspergillus species, for their role in producing industrial enzymes. However, given their rapid growth, adaptability, and ease of cultivation, bacteria have received increasing attention for their ability to degrade cellulose and hemicellulose.

Cellulomonas, a genus classified under the family Cellulomonadaceae in the phylum Actinomycetota, is known for its potential to produce enzymes that degrade cell-wall polysaccharides of plants, including cellulases, hemicellulases, and lignin-modifying enzymes [7, 10]. Within this genus, Cellulomonas iranensis is gaining attention for its biotechnological capabilities. For instance, recent studies have shown that the Cellulomonas iranensis strain ZJW-6, isolated from paddy soil in central China, resulted in a remarkable 49.6% reduction in lignocellulose weight from rice straw. Notably, ZJW-6 strain was able to degrade cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin at the same time, achieving cellulose and hemicellulose loss rates of nearly 50% in four days and reducing lignin by approximately 30%. Significantly, ZJW-6 exhibited effective lignocellulose degradation under both aerobic and anaerobic conditions [11]. Moreover, comprehensive multi-omics and single-cell RNA sequencing analyses of Cellulomonas iranensis CI1, isolated from the termite gut microbiota, have revealed its potential for lignocellulose degradation and the underlying molecular pathways [12]. Whole-genome sequencing revealed a 4.0 Mb circular chromosome with 243 carbohydrate-active enzyme (CAZyme) genes, and proteomic analysis found nine CAZymes exclusively expressed during rice straw fermentation. During 14 d of rice-straw fermentation, strain CI1 reduced the lignin content from 32.9% to 29.2% of dry matter, representing an approximate 11.25% reduction [12]. Moreover, a microbial consortium composed of C. iranensis ZJW-6 and Acinetobacter DA-25 was reported to improve lignocellulose degradation through enzyme-activity complementation in fermentation of rice straw, especially by making up for the comparatively low β-glucosidase activity of C. iranensis ZJW-6. This finding indicates that the degradation efficiency of Cellulomonas-based systems is closely associated with cellulase and xylanase production as well as with the balance among different lignocellulolytic enzymes. Thus, C. iranensis has exhibited strong potential for degrading lignocellulosic materials in rice straw, but its effectiveness on chili pepper straw has yet to be examined.

In this study, C. iranensis 7–12, a cellulolytic bacterium, was isolated from naturally decayed chili pepper straw, representing a microbial resource derived from a unique and resistant biomass environment. The research evaluated its capacity to degrade chili pepper straw and explored its adaptive potential through physiological characterization, cellulase and xylanase analyses, structural modification assessment, and whole-genome sequencing. The key contribution of this study is linking the origin of C. iranensis 7–12 from naturally decayed chili pepper straw and its chili pepper straw-degradation phenotype with its xylanase-focused enzyme profile and extensive CAZyme repertoire. These findings provide a theoretical basis and a potential microbial resource for the efficient utilization of chili pepper straw and other agricultural wastes.

Materials and methods

Chili pepper straw materials

Naturally decayed line pepper (Capsicum annuum L.) straw and activated line pepper straw used for straw-degradation experiments were obtained from post-harvest residues at the Guizhou University Pepper Planting Base (26.449375° N, 106.655275° E), which is affiliated with the Institute of Pepper Industry and Technology, Guizhou University, Guiyang, Guizhou Province, China. Approximately 500 g of decayed straw was collected from each of five selected locations using a five-point sampling method. The samples were kept in sterile, self-sealing bags and transported to the laboratory. Subsequently, visible impurities were removed, and the straw was chopped into small fragments before being kept in sterile centrifuge tubes for future isolation and screening of cellulolytic bacteria. The activated line pepper straw was air-dried naturally, ground, passed through a 40-mesh sieve, and stored in sealed containers until required. No endangered or protected plant species were involved in this study, and the collection and use of plant materials complied with relevant institutional, national, and local guidelines.

DNA extraction and metagenomic sequencing of microorganisms from decayed chili pepper straw

Naturally decayed chili pepper straw samples were used to enrich microorganisms in liquid carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC) medium (CMC 5 g/L, (NH4)2SO4 0.79 g/L, K2HPO4 1 g/L, MgSO4·7H2O 0.5 g/L, KCl 1 g/L, yeast extract 0.5 g/L), with the initial pH adjusted to 7.0. Microbial biomass was subsequently obtained from the enrichment cultures and used for total DNA extraction with a DNA extraction kit from Omega Bio-tek, Inc. according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The enriched microbial communities designated T1 and T2 originated from naturally decayed chili pepper straw collected from the hilly and field sampling areas, respectively, within the Guizhou University Pepper Planting Base. Three biological replicates were prepared for each sample type.

Library preparation and metagenomic sequencing were subsequently conducted by Shanghai BIOZERON Biotech Co., Ltd. Briefly, 1 µg of DNA from each sample was randomly fragmented by ultrasonication. Afterward, libraries with an insert size of approximately 450 bp were constructed, and shotgun metagenomic sequencing was conducted on an MGI DNBSEQ-T7 platform in paired-end 150-bp (PE150) mode. The raw sequencing reads were quality-filtered to obtain clean reads for subsequent analyses. De novo assembly was performed using MEGAHIT [13] to generate contigs, followed by open reading frame (ORF) prediction and construction of a non-redundant gene catalog.

Taxonomic and functional annotations were performed using the whole-community metagenomic dataset. Briefly, gene abundance was quantified from reads mapped to a non-redundant gene catalog, corrected for gene length, and normalized to 10⁶ per sample using a transcripts-per-million-like approach. Phylum-level relative abundance was calculated by summing normalized gene abundances for each phylum and dividing by the total abundance in the phylum-level profile. The 10 most abundant named phyla across six samples were displayed separately, while unclassified and other phyla were grouped as “Others”. Functional annotation was performed using the KEGG [14] and eggNOG [15] databases.

Isolation of cellulose-degrading microorganisms

Cellulose-degrading strains were isolated following the procedure detailed in the literature [16, 17]. Briefly, a 10 g portion of each original naturally decayed straw material, along with a few sterile glass beads, was separately added to 90 mL of sterile saline and shaken at 30 °C and 160 rpm for 24 h to obtain bacterial suspensions. After settling for one hour, each suspension was serially diluted from 10⁻³ to 10⁻⁵, and 0.1 mL of each dilution was spread on CMC screening medium supplemented with 15 g/L agar, with the initial pH adjusted to 7.0. The plates were incubated at 30 °C for 72 h, after which distinct colonies were selected and repeatedly streaked on fresh CMC agar plates until uniform colonies were obtained. These purified bacterial isolates were maintained separately on CMC agar plates for subsequent evaluation. Each isolate’s cellulolytic potential was evaluated using the Congo red assay [16]. Briefly, 2 µL of each bacterial suspension was spotted onto the CMC agar plate and incubated at 30 °C for 3 d. Subsequently, the plates were stained with 0.1% Congo red for 15 min and destained with 1 mol/L NaCl for 15 min to visualize the hydrolysis zones. The ratio of the hydrolysis-zone diameter (D) to the colony diameter (d) was calculated as D/d to evaluate cellulose-degrading potential [17].

Screening and identification of cellulose-degrading microorganisms

Six purified isolates with the highest D/d values from the CMC–Congo red assay were further screened in a lignocellulose-degradation liquid medium using chili pepper straw as the substrate [18]. The fresh cultures were adjusted to an optical density at 600 nm (OD600) of 0.8 and individually inoculated into the medium at 1% (v/v), while a control (CK) contained the same medium without bacterial inoculation. Both setups were incubated at 30 °C and 160 rpm for 4 d. After fermentation, distilled water was added, and the suspension was gently mixed and allowed to stand until the residual straw had settled. The supernatant was carefully decanted, and the washing–settling–decanting procedure was repeated until the supernatant was clear. The recovered straw residue was then dried at 60 °C to constant weight.

Determination of cellulose and hemicellulose contents in chili pepper straw

The cellulose and hemicellulose contents of chili pepper straw were determined according to the Van Soest detergent fiber method [19, 20] with minor modifications [11]. The samples of chili pepper straw were ground and sieved through a 40-mesh sieve, then dried to a constant weight in an oven at 60 °C (denoted as W₀). For the determination of neutral detergent fiber (NDF), the dried sample was transferred to an iodine flask, and 50 mL of neutral detergent solution along with a few drops of decalin were added. The mixture was heated to 100 °C and refluxed for 1 h, then filtered using a funnel. The residue was repeatedly washed with hot water (≥ 90 °C) until the filtrate became neutral in pH, followed by 2–3 rinses with acetone. The treated residue was then dried to a constant weight at 60 °C (denoted as W₁). For the determination of acid detergent fiber (ADF), the dried NDF residue was transferred to an iodine flask, to which 50 mL of acid detergent solution and a few drops of decalin were added. The mixture was refluxed and boiled at 100 °C for 1 h and then filtered. The residue was washed with hot water until neutrality was achieved, rinsed with acetone 2–3 times, and dried to a constant weight at 60 °C (denoted as W₂). To obtain the acid-insoluble residue required for cellulose calculation, 5 mL of pre-cooled 72% sulfuric acid was added to the dried ADF residue. The mixture was hydrolyzed at room temperature for 3 h with intermittent stirring. Subsequently, 50 mL of distilled water was added to the mixture, which was then allowed to stand for 20 h. The residue was washed with distilled water to neutrality, filtered, and dried to a constant weight at 60 °C (denoted as W₃).

Identification of strain Cellulomonas iranensis 7–12

For the morphological characterization, Cellulomonas iranensis 7–12 (C. iranensis 7–12) was streaked onto CMC solid medium and incubated at 30 °C for 3 d, after which colony morphology was observed, recorded and photographed. Fresh bacterial cells were subjected to Gram staining and examined under a light microscope to determine cellular morphology and Gram reaction, following the procedure detailed in reference [21]. For the molecular identification, the target strain was identified by 16S rRNA gene sequencing, a widely used approach for bacterial taxonomic identification [22]. Genomic DNA of Cellulomonas iranensis 7–12 was extracted using the CTAB method and used as a template for PCR amplification. PCR products were then sequenced using the Sanger sequencing method. The obtained sequence was assembled and proofread using BioEdit software, and phylogenetic analysis was performed using sequences retrieved from NCBI, employing the neighbor-joining method implemented in MEGA 7.0 [23].

Biological characteristics of C. iranensis 7–12

To evaluate the effects of temperature and pH on bacterial growth, a fresh bacterial culture was inoculated into LB broth at 1% (v/v) and incubated at 160 rpm for 72 h under various temperatures (28, 31, 34, 37, and 40 °C) or initial pH values (6, 7, 8, 9, and 10). Bacterial growth was evaluated by measuring OD600, with uninoculated LB broth used as the blank control [24]. To assess the utilization of model cellulose by C. iranensis 7–12, filter paper (Whatman No. 1) strips were used as the sole carbon source. The filter paper was cut into 1 × 6 cm strips, weighed, and added to 50 mL of basal liquid medium. A fresh bacterial culture was inoculated at 1% (v/v), and the samples were incubated with shaking at 30 °C and 160 rpm for 30 h. The disintegration status of the filter paper in the inoculated and uninoculated control cultures was documented at 0, 6, 12, 18, 24, and 30 h. A fresh bacterial culture was similarly inoculated into the straw-based medium at 1% (v/v), and the cultures were incubated under identical conditions; culture appearance was recorded every 6 h. For both filter-paper and chili pepper-straw cultures, OD₆₀₀ was measured at each time point together with the corresponding uninoculated control containing the same amount of substrate and incubated under identical conditions. The substrate-background-corrected OD₆₀₀ was calculated by subtracting the control value from that of the inoculated culture. After 30 h of incubation, the residual straw was collected, washed, dried to a constant weight at 60 °C, and used to calculate the dry-weight loss rate.

Enzyme activity assays

The activities of endoglucanase, exoglucanase, β-glucosidase and xylanase were determined using standard cellulase activity and reducing-sugar assay methods, with minor modifications for chili pepper straw-based cultures [11, 25]. Crude enzyme fractions were prepared from a straw-based fermentation broth after 4 d of incubation. An uninoculated straw-medium control was processed in parallel using the same fractionation and enzyme-assay procedures. Briefly, 100 mL of the fermentation culture was centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 5 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected as the extracellular crude enzyme fraction (EC). The corresponding cell pellet was washed three times with sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), resuspended in 100 mL of sterile PBS, and disrupted in an ice bath using a JY98-IIIDN ultrasonic processor (Ningbo Xinzhi Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Ningbo, China), using 5-s sonication pulses separated by 5-s intervals for 5 min. After a 5-min cooling interval, the sonication cycle was repeated once, following the published protocol for C. iranensis ZJW-6 [11]. The resulting cell lysate was centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected as the intracellular crude enzyme fraction (IC). Endoglucanase activity was assessed using a 1% (w/v) carboxymethyl cellulose sodium salt solution as the substrate. The reaction mixture containing the crude enzyme solution, carboxymethyl cellulose, and phosphate–citric acid buffer (50 mM, pH 7.0) was incubated at 40 °C for 30 min, and the released reducing sugars were quantified at 540 nm using the 3,5-dinitrosalicylic acid (DNS) method [26]. Exoglucanase activity was assayed using a 1% (w/v) microcrystalline cellulose suspension as the substrate in 100 mM citric acid buffer (pH 5.0) at 50 °C for 30 min, followed by DNS color development and absorbance measurement at 540 nm. β-Glucosidase activity was determined using 0.8 mM p-nitrophenyl-β-D-glucopyranoside as the substrate. The reaction was performed in 50 mM acetate buffer (pH 5.0). After incubation at 50 °C for 30 min, the reaction was stopped with Na₂CO₃, and the released p-nitrophenol was measured at 405 nm. Xylanase activity was measured using a 1.0% (w/v) birchwood xylan suspension as the substrate in 50 mM sodium citrate buffer (pH 5.3) at 50 °C for 5 min, and the released reducing sugars were determined by the DNS method at 540 nm. One unit of enzyme activity was defined as the amount of enzyme required to release 1 µmol of the corresponding product per minute under the assay conditions [11]. All enzyme assays were performed in triplicate.

Structural analyses of chili pepper straw after microbial degradation

Chili pepper straw samples were collected after 4 d of fermentation with C. iranensis 7–12, while untreated straw served as the control. These samples were subsequently fixed in 2% glutaraldehyde overnight, followed by three washes with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). The samples were dehydrated using a series of ethanol concentrations and then dried with a carbon dioxide critical point dryer and sputter-coated with gold. Observations were conducted using SEM at an accelerating voltage of 10 kV and a current of 50 nA. Additionally, the samples of chili pepper straw were analyzed using Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy before and after being treated with C. iranensis 7–12. The samples were dried to a constant weight, mixed with KBr, and shaped into clear pellets. Subsequently, an infrared spectrometer was used to record spectra in the range of 4000 to 400 cm⁻¹.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as the mean ± standard error (SE) from three independent replicates. Differences between two groups were analyzed using Student’s t-test, whereas multiple-group comparisons were performed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison test. Differences were considered significant at p < 0.05. For metagenomic data, Shannon diversity and Bray–Curtis dissimilarity were calculated from species-level relative-abundance profiles. Differences in Shannon diversity were assessed using a two-sided exact Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Bray–Curtis PCoA was used for ordination, and group differences were evaluated using ANOSIM and PERMANOVA with 719 permutations. LEfSe was used as an exploratory analysis.

Whole-genome sequencing and analysis

The genome was assembled from PacBio HiFi reads using hifiasm, and assembly completeness was assessed using BUSCO [27]. Protein-coding genes were predicted with GeneMarkS, whereas tRNA, rRNA, and other non-coding RNA genes were annotated using tRNAscan-SE, rRNAmmer and Rfam, respectively. Functional annotation was performed against several databases, including the NCBI non-redundant protein database (NR), UniProtKB/Swiss-Prot, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), Clusters of Orthologous Groups (COG), Gene Ontology (GO), Transporter Classification Database (TCDB), and Protein families database (Pfam). CAZyme families and modules were identified using HMMER hmmscan against the dbCAN HMM database (dbCAN-HMMdb v13) with default parameters [28]. Genomic islands, prophage regions, clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeat (CRISPR) arrays, and secondary metabolite gene clusters were predicted using relevant public databases and bioinformatics tools. Signal peptides and transmembrane regions were predicted using SignalP and the transmembrane hidden Markov model (TMHMM), respectively. CAZyme-annotated proteins were cross-referenced with the SignalP and secreted-protein predictions to identify candidate extracellular CAZymes. Secondary metabolite biosynthetic gene clusters were predicted using antiSMASH. The circular genome map was generated using Circos. Genome-based taxonomic assignment was further evaluated by calculating the average nucleotide identity (ANI) between the complete genome of strain 7–12 and that of the type strain C. iranensis JCM 18110ᵀ (GenBank accession AP043850.1) using FastANI [29].

Results

Taxonomic and functional profiles of microbial communities

High-quality datasets were generated through metagenomic sequencing for the CMC-enriched microbial communities derived from the two decayed chili pepper straw sample groups. The Q20 values varied from 98.70% to 98.92%, Q30 values ranged from 97.61% to 97.92%, and the GC contents ranged from 55.76% to 56.66% (Table S1). In addition, gene prediction identified 85,427 to 180,437 open reading frames (ORFs) per sample, with average ORF lengths varying from 684.20 to 733.69 base pairs. Notably, the T1 samples showed numerically higher numbers of predicted ORFs than the T2 samples, indicating that more predicted coding sequences were recovered from T1 under the present sequencing and assembly conditions (Table S2). At the phylum level, Pseudomonadota predominated in both CMC-enriched communities, accounting for 98.17–98.85% and 96.27–96.66% of the relative abundance in T1 and T2, respectively (Fig. 1A). Actinomycetota, Bacteroidota, and Bacillota were detected at lower relative abundances. The mean Shannon index was numerically higher in T1 than in T2, but the difference was not statistically significant (Figure S1A). Species-level Bray–Curtis principal coordinate analysis showed visual separation between T1 and T2 along PC1, which explained 98.92% of the variation, whereas PC2 explained 0.89% (Figure S1B). Exploratory LEfSe analysis displayed taxa with differential LDA scores between the groups, but these patterns were treated as exploratory rather than as evidence of robust group-level differences (Fig. 1B).

KEGG functional annotation showed that the annotated genes were predominantly associated with metabolism-related categories, including global and overview maps, carbohydrate metabolism, amino acid metabolism, and energy metabolism (Fig. 1C). Membrane transport and signal transduction were also prominently represented. Examination of the top 20 KEGG pathways showed high representation of metabolic pathways, microbial metabolism in diverse environments, two-component systems, ABC transporters, carbon metabolism, and quorum sensing (Fig. 1D). These results indicated broad carbohydrate-utilization potential in the enriched communities, providing ecological and functional context for the culture-dependent screening of isolates from the original straw material.

Screening and identification of the cellulolytic isolate C. iranensis 7–12

Thirty-two morphologically distinct bacterial isolates were recovered as pure cultures from the original naturally decayed chili pepper straw materials, including 17 from the T1-associated source material and 15 from the T2-associated source material (Table S3). These distinct bacterial isolates were further analyzed via the Congo red assay, and the results revealed significant differences in their capabilities to hydrolyze CMC (Fig. 2A). Based on the hydrolysis zone diameter to colony diameter ratio (D/d), six isolates exhibiting relatively high D/d values were selected for further analysis. The Congo red plate images of the six selected isolates further confirmed their cellulose hydrolysis capacity (Figure S2). Among them, C. iranensis strain 7–12 showed the highest hydrolytic activity, with a D/d value of approximately 5.14. The Congo red staining distinctly highlighted a pronounced hydrolysis halo around strain 7–12 (Fig. 2B), supporting its cellulolytic potential. Subsequent straw-degradation assays showed that strain 7–12 produced the highest straw, hemicellulose, and cellulose degradation rates among the selected isolates after 4 d of fermentation (Figure S3). Therefore, strain 7–12 was selected for subsequent identification and degradation analyses.

After incubation on CMC solid medium, strain C. iranensis 7–12 produced circular, yellowish-green colonies characterized by slightly convex centers, smooth and moist surfaces, and entire margins (Fig. 2C). Additionally, Gram staining showed that the cells of strain 7–12 were short, rod-shaped with rounded ends, and stained Gram-positive, although partial decolorization was observed in some cells (Fig. 2D). Scanning electron microscopy further confirmed that strain 7–12 cells were short rod-shaped with rounded ends and relatively smooth surfaces (Fig. 2E). These morphological and staining characteristics provided preliminary phenotypic information for the identification of strain 7–12.

Alignment of the 16S rRNA gene sequence of strain 7–12 with reference sequences in the genus Cellulomonas showed the highest similarity (99%) to the type strain Cellulomonas iranensis JCM 18110ᵀ [30]. A phylogenetic tree constructed using the neighbor-joining method [23] showed that strain 7–12 clustered with C. iranensis JCM 18110ᵀ, with 99% bootstrap support (Fig. 2F). Pairwise ANI analysis showed that strain 7–12 shared 97.56% ANI with C. iranensis JCM 18110T, exceeding the generally accepted 95% species-level threshold [29]. Together, the 16S rRNA gene similarity, phylogenetic placement, and genome-based ANI result supported the assignment of strain 7–12 to C. iranensis.

Utilization of filter paper strips and chili pepper straw by C. iranensis 7–12

Growth assays in LB broth showed the highest OD₆₀₀ values at 34 °C and an initial pH of 9.0 (Figure S4). To evaluate the cellulolytic capacity of C. iranensis 7–12 for the model cellulose substrate, filter paper strips were employed as the sole carbon source in the basal medium [18]. During the 30 h incubation period, the filter paper strips gradually lost their structural integrity, showing swelling, softening, edge fraying and structural disruption, and were almost completely disintegrated by 30 h (Fig. 3A). Simultaneously, the culture’s turbidity increased, which indicated that bacteria were actively growing. Moreover, an increase in OD600 values over the 30 h incubation period was observed, further confirming that C. iranensis 7–12 could utilize filter paper cellulose as a carbon source (Fig. 3B). In contrast to the filter paper strips, when C. iranensis 7–12 was inoculated into the chili pepper straw medium (as the sole carbon source), visual changes were also observed in the culture solution (Fig. 3C). Compared with the control, the culture solution with inoculated C. iranensis 7–12 showed greater turbidity and partial disintegration of straw, and OD600 values increased, confirming active bacterial growth (Fig. 3D). These results indicate that C. iranensis 7–12 was able to utilize chili pepper straw components for growth. In addition, the dry-weight loss of chili pepper straw was significantly increased after 30 h of treatment with C. iranensis 7–12 compared with the uninoculated control (Figure S5).

Degradation of straw, cellulose, and hemicellulose

The initial cellulose and hemicellulose contents of untreated chili pepper straw were 45.12 ± 2.39% and 12.67 ± 0.66% of dry matter, respectively (Table S4). After 4 d of fermentation, the dry-weight loss of total straw increased from 11.98% in the uninoculated control to 32.63% in the C. iranensis 7–12 treatment (Fig. 4A). Inoculation with C. iranensis 7–12 also increased the hemicellulose and cellulose degradation rates from 21.60% to 47.66% and from 14.38% to 40.04%, respectively (Fig. 4A). These results demonstrate that C. iranensis 7–12 degraded the major structural carbohydrates of chili pepper straw. Moreover, the effects of temperature, initial pH, and peptone concentration on chili pepper straw degradation were further analyzed. In the separate single-factor experiments, the highest straw dry-weight loss rates within the tested ranges were observed at 34 °C, pH 9.0, and a peptone concentration of 10 g/L, with values of 35.64%, 34.77%, and 35.47%, respectively (Fig. 4B-D). These results identified the best-performing level of each individual factor within the tested ranges.

Cellulase and xylanase characteristics

To analyze lignocellulose degradation in chili pepper straw, the activities of four enzymes, including endoglucanase, exoglucanase, β-glucosidase, and xylanase, were measured after 4 d of straw fermentation with C. iranensis 7–12 [11, 25]. The results showed that the average extracellular activities of exoglucanase, endoglucanase, β-glucosidase, and xylanase were 0.242, 0.275, 0.030, and 3.410 U/mL, respectively, whereas the corresponding intracellular activities were only 0.113, 0.131, 0.009, and 0.605 U/mL (Fig. 5A–D). The findings showed that C. iranensis 7–12 had higher extracellular enzyme activities than intracellular enzyme activities, indicating a degradation strategy predominantly mediated by extracellular enzymes. Notably, extracellular xylanase showed the highest activity among the four enzymes, implying that C. iranensis 7–12 may have strong potential for chili pepper straw degradation by secreting xylanase.

Structural analysis for chili pepper straw degradation

To analyze the structural features of cellulose degradation, scanning electron microscopy was used to observe morphological evidence of structural disruption in chili pepper straw after treatment with C. iranensis 7–12. Before treatment, the straw had a relatively smooth surface, and its internal fibers were compactly arranged in ordered bundles with clear cell wall layers, which was consistent with the physical barrier formed by the cuticle and lignin–cellulose composite structure (Fig. 6A-B) [31]. Conversely, after 4 d of fermentation with C. iranensis 7–12, there were visible changes in the overall appearance of chili pepper straw (Fig. 6C-D). In detail, the straw’s surface showed roughness and fragmentation, with many cracks and fiber fragments, indicating the disintegration of the external protective lignocellulosic layer (Fig. 6C). Furthermore, the internal structural alterations were more significant, with disintegration of compact fiber bundles and the formation of pores and honeycomb-like cavities (Fig. 6D).

In addition, FTIR further identified chemical alterations in chili pepper straw after 4 d of treatment with C. iranensis 7–12. Spectral comparisons before and after degradation demonstrated a clear reduction in several characteristic absorptions (Fig. 6E). Additional SEM images from replicate samples showed relatively intact structures in untreated straw (Figure S6A–C) and comparable surface disruption, fiber disintegration, and pore formation after fermentation (Figure S6D–F). According to published peak assignments [32, 33] and the overview presented (Table S5), the reduced peaks included 897 cm⁻¹ (β-glycosidic bond vibration of cellulose), 1034 cm⁻¹ (C–O and C–O–C stretching in hemicellulose and cellulose), and 1740 cm⁻¹ (C = O stretching of hemicellulose and lignin). These results demonstrate that fermentation with C. iranensis 7–12 caused considerable structural alterations in chili pepper straw and support the degradation of its cellulose- and hemicellulose-related components.

Genome assembly and chromosome features

The genome of C. iranensis 7–12 was determined to be a single circular chromosome comprising 3,787,319 bp with a GC content of 75.16%, and no plasmid-homologous sequence was detected. BUSCO analysis indicated 100.0% genome completeness, with all 124 conserved markers recovered as complete single-copy genes (Table S6). The chromosome was found to contain 3,519 coding sequences (CDSs), which constitute 90.54% of the genome. Additionally, it harbors 46 tRNA genes, 6 rRNA genes and 1 small RNA-encoding gene. The presence of thirteen genomic islands, two prophage regions, and two CRISPR arrays indicates genomic plasticity and potential defense mechanisms against foreign genetic elements (Table S6). The circular genomic map showed a continuous distribution of CDS, broad coverage of COG categories, non-coding RNA loci, and variation in local GC content/GC skew (Fig. 7).

Functional annotation and Carbohydrate-Active Enzymes (CAZy) repertoire

Genome annotation assigned 3,320 genes to KEGG, 2,551 to COG, 2,194 to GO, 334 to TCDB and 262 genes to CAZy categories (Table S7). In the COG profile, carbohydrate transport and metabolism was the largest category (category G, 295 genes), followed by transcription, general function prediction, amino acid metabolism, translation and signal transduction (Fig. 8A). KEGG pathway annotation highlighted carbohydrate metabolism (192 genes), especially the pathways involved in starch/sucrose metabolism, amino sugar and nucleotide sugar metabolism, glycolysis/gluconeogenesis and the pentose phosphate pathway (Fig. 8B). GO terms were predominantly associated with metabolic processes, cellular processes, catalytic activity, binding and transporter activity (Fig. 8C). In addition, the dbCAN HMM analysis identified 315 CAZy family/module hits in 262 genes, including 132 glycoside hydrolase (GH) hits, 84 glycosyltransferase (GT) hits, 74 carbohydrate-binding module (CBM) hits, 20 carbohydrate esterase (CE) hits, 4 auxiliary activity (AA) hits, and 1 polysaccharide lyase (PL) hit (Fig. 8D; Table S8). The cellulose degradation and utilization repertoire included GH5, GH6, GH9, GH48, GH1, GH3, and GH94. For example, GH5 and GH9 may contribute to cellulose-chain cleavage, whereas GH6 and GH48 are associated with the degradation of insoluble and crystalline cellulose. GH1, GH3, and GH94 were closely related to the downstream utilization of cellobiose and cello-oligosaccharides. Hemicellulose-related families included GH10, GH11, GH43, GH51, GH62, GH67, and GH74. Moreover, the presence of abundant CBM2, CBM13, CBM22, CBM91, CE families and AA10 lytic polysaccharide monooxygenases (LPMOs) suggests a system of binding, hydrolysis, debranching and oxidative mechanisms for deconstruction of chili pepper straw polysaccharides (Table S9). Significantly, the abundance of carbohydrate-active enzymes in the genome, particularly those related to xylan GH families, was consistent with the observed fiber disruption and high extracellular xylanase activity, suggesting that hemicellulose may be an important target for C. iranensis 7–12. To place these genomic features in a broader context, the genomic and lignocellulose-degradation characteristics of C. iranensis 7–12 were compared with those of representative lignocellulose-degrading bacteria (Table S10).

Transport, secretion, and other genomic features

PHI annotation detected 336 homologs distributed across several phenotype categories, with classes 7 and 8 accounting for the largest proportions (Fig. 9A). CARD analysis identified four antibiotic-resistance homologs, while VFDB matched 212 homologs. TCDB annotation revealed 334 transporters, dominated by primary active and electrochemical potential-driven systems, including ATP-binding cassette transporters and Major Facilitator Superfamily transporters, which facilitate the uptake of soluble hydrolysis products (Fig. 9B). Predictions regarding secretion- and membrane-associated features identified 986 transmembrane proteins, 174 SignalP-positive proteins, and 100 predicted secreted proteins (Fig. 9C; Table S11). The analysis of the secreted-protein predictions with the CAZy annotations showed that the predicted secretome included GH5 and GH9 cellulase/endoglucanase candidates, a GH3 β-glucosidase candidate, GH10 and GH11 xylanases, and an AA10 lytic polysaccharide monooxygenase (LPMO) (Table S9). Analysis of secondary metabolism revealed clusters associated with type III polyketide synthase, terpenes, sactipeptides, non-ribosomal peptide synthetase-independent siderophores and class-III lanthipeptide (Fig. 9D; Table S12), suggesting accessory traits for nutrient acquisition, competitive interactions and stress adaptation.

Discussion

The use of lignocellulosic biomass is a critical strategy for promoting sustainable agriculture, with the main challenge being the tough barrier formed by cellulose, hemicellulose and lignin [34]. Microbial degradation exhibits significant potential for the treatment of crop straw due to its low energy requirements and environmental benefits [35]. In natural ecosystems, fungi are considered one of the most effective microbial groups for lignocellulose degradation, among which white-rot, brown-rot, and soft-rot fungi have been extensively studied. However, the extensive industrial application of fungi is constrained by limitations, including the possible pathogenic nature of some strains, for instance, Aspergillus [36]. Conversely, bacteria have the benefits of a rapid reproduction cycle, robust environmental adaptability, extensive acid and base tolerance, and simple genetic manipulation [35].

At the phylum level, Pseudomonadota was the predominant phylum in the CMC-enriched communities of T1 and T2 (Fig. 1A; Table S13). Actinomycetota, Bacteroidota, and Bacillota were less abundant. However, the presence of the Streptophyta assignments likely resulted from residual plant DNA, not bacterial community members. Pseudomonas/Pseudomonadaceae and Enterobacteriaceae are known to thrive in lignocellulose-adapted microbial consortia, with some Pseudomonadota aiding in lignocellulosic biomass conversion and others utilizing released carbohydrates [37, 38]. Borjigin et al. also identified Pseudomonas in similar groups, highlighting carbohydrate metabolism as crucial, aligning with the current study’s findings of carbohydrate and carbon metabolism, ABC transporters, and related pathways [37]. Under aerobic CMC conditions, microorganisms that grow quickly and efficiently use CMC or its breakdown products were favored, likely leading to the dominance of Pseudomonadota and similar functional profiles. These results reflect enrichment effects rather than the original community structure during natural straw decomposition. Interestingly, from naturally decayed chili pepper straw, 32 morphologically distinct bacterial isolates were obtained (Table S3). Through Congo red and straw degradation assays, strain 7–12 was identified as the most potent cellulolytic candidate among them. The 16S rRNA gene similarity (99%), phylogenetic placement (Fig. 2F), and genome-based ANI value (97.56%) supported the classification of strain 7–12 as C. iranensis. Genome annotation identified 315 CAZy family/module hits in 262 genes, including cellulolytic and hemicellulolytic GH families (Fig. 8D; Tables S8 and S9). These genomic features, including CBMs, CEs, and AA10 LPMOs, indicate a comprehensive system for polysaccharide deconstruction. The presence of 334 transporters and 100 predicted secreted proteins further supports the capacity of strain 7–12 for lignocellulose utilization (Fig. 9B–C; Table S11).

In this study, we identified a cellulose- and hemicellulose-degrading bacterium, C. iranensis 7–12, from naturally decayed chili pepper straw using morphological observation, Gram staining, 16S rRNA gene phylogeny, and genome-based ANI analysis (Fig. 2). In addition, chili pepper straw dry-weight loss increased from 11.98% in the uninoculated control to 32.63% in the C. iranensis 7–12 treatment after 4 d, with hemicellulose and cellulose degradation rates reaching 47.66% and 40.04%, respectively (Fig. 4A). Previous research reported a 49.6% reduction in rice-straw lignocellulose weight by C. iranensis ZJW-6 (Table S10). Notably, ZJW-6 strain was isolated from paddy soil in central China and evaluated using rice straw and a different medium, whereas C. iranensis 7–12 was isolated from naturally decayed chili pepper straw and evaluated using chili pepper straw. Moreover, the cellulase-producing Fusarium equiseti LJ-10, which was isolated from chili pepper straw, was reported to degrade this substrate. Additionally, Dickeya sp. WS52, a bacterial strain, was found to decompose sweet pepper stalk, although it was originally isolated from soft-rotted tobacco and had a CAZyme repertoire enriched in function for degrading pectin and hemicellulose [39, 40]. These studies suggest that microbial and enzymatic strategies could be used to degrade chili pepper straw biomass. At the genome level, C. iranensis 7–12 has a 3.79-Mb chromosome containing 262 CAZyme-annotated genes, compared with the 4.0-Mb genome containing 243 CAZyme genes in C. iranensis CI1 (Fig. 8D; Table S10) [12]. Cellulose-degrading bacteria, including Bacillus subtilis K35-1 and Fictibacillus sp. YS-26, also possess carbohydrate-degrading functions, although they differ in taxon, substrate, culture conditions, and analytical methods [8, 9] (Table S10).

The effective degradation of lignocellulose is dependent on the synergistic activity of multiple hydrolases. The cellulase system primarily consists of endoglucanase, exoglucanase and β-glucosidase. Endoglucanase randomly cleaves internal β-1,4-glycosidic bonds in the amorphous regions of cellulose, generating new chain ends. Additionally, exoglucanase progressively cleaves cellulose chains from the reducing or non-reducing ends to release cellobiose as the major product, while β-glucosidase further hydrolyzes cellobiose and cello-oligosaccharides into glucose [2, 7]. The degradation of hemicellulose also requires the involvement of various enzymes, especially xylanase and other hemicellulases [35]. Research demonstrated that cellulase and xylanase operate synergistically during the hydrolysis of natural lignocellulosic substrates, supporting the coordinated roles of these enzymes in lignocellulose degradation [41]. The removal or hydrolysis of xylan can reduce the shielding effect of hemicellulose around cellulose microfibrils, thereby improving cellulase accessibility to cellulose. Therefore, the xylanase-biased extracellular enzyme profile of strain 7–12 may represent an important enzymatic advantage for deconstructing the hemicellulose-rich matrix of chili pepper straw. In this research, the extracellular enzyme activities of C. iranensis 7–12, including endoglucanase, exoglucanase, β-glucosidase and xylanase, were found to be 0.275, 0.242, 0.030, and 3.410 U/mL, respectively, which were higher than the corresponding intracellular enzyme activities (Fig. 5). These results indicate that C. iranensis 7–12 mainly depends on its extracellular enzyme system for lignocellulose degradation. Previous studies on C. flavigena and C. fimi have described their ability to aerobically break down cellulose using extracellular enzymes and a diverse range of glycoside hydrolases and carbohydrate-binding proteins, with secretomes that respond to substrates like CMC or xylan [42, 43]. In addition, C. flavigena PR-22 has also been applied to the production of cellulase and xylanase alongside sugarcane-bagasse breakdown [44]. Similarly, C. iranensis 7–12, which is phylogenetically related to C. flavigena (Fig. 2), exhibited predominantly extracellular enzyme activities and encoded predicted secreted CAZymes from the GH5, GH9, GH10, GH11, and AA10 families, suggesting a shared extracellular polysaccharide-deconstruction strategy among these Cellulomonas species. Genome annotation of C. iranensis 7–12 showed that carbohydrate transport and metabolism was the largest COG functional category. Notably, the CAZy annotation highlighted an abundance of glycoside hydrolases and other enzymes involved in carbohydrate processing. In the genomics analysis of C. iranensis 7–12, it is noteworthy that several enzymes annotated by the carbohydrate-active enzymes (CAZy) database were identified among the 100 predicted secreted proteins. These include candidates for GH5 and GH9 cellulase/endoglucanase, a GH3 β-glucosidase candidate, GH10 and GH11 xylanases, and an AA10 LPMO (Table S9). The AA-family enzymes were specifically classified as AA10 LPMOs, which are associated with oxidative cleavage of polysaccharides. However, traditional lignin-modifying CAZy families, such as AA1 and AA2, were absent in the current annotation. Moreover, the genome’s significant repertoire of carbohydrate-active enzymes, particularly those related to xylan GH families, was consistent with the observed fiber disruption and high extracellular xylanase activity, suggesting that hemicellulose may be an important target for C. iranensis 7–12.

In plant cell walls, lignin and hemicellulose are covalently cross-linked to form lignin-carbohydrate complexes, which surround cellulose microfibrils and create a physical barrier that prevents enzyme access to the substrate [34]. Thus, the degradation of lignocellulosic biomass generally requires coordinated modification of the lignin–carbohydrate matrix, followed by the hydrolysis of hemicellulose and cellulose. Studies have shown that the hemicellulose and cellulose degradation rates of rice straw fermented by C. iranensis ZJW-6 were 47.8% and 43.6% [11]. However, compared with rice straw, chili pepper straw may possess a more complex lignocellulosic structure. After 4 days of fermentation with C. iranensis 7–12, the hemicellulose degradation rate was 47.66%, slightly higher than the cellulose degradation rate of 40.04% (Fig. 4A).

Scanning electron microscopy provided visual evidence for the degradation of chili pepper straw by C. iranensis 7–12. Before treatment, the outer surface of the straw was relatively smooth, and compact, orderly fiber bundles with distinct cell wall layers were observed on the inner surface. This morphology of cell wall represents a typical physical barrier, formed by the cuticle and lignin-cellulose composite structure of natural straw [12]. Following fermentation with C. iranensis 7–12, the outer surface of chili pepper straw appeared rough and fragmented, displaying numerous cracks and fiber fragments, indicating disruption of the external protective lignocellulosic layer. The internal structural changes were more pronounced, as the initially compact fiber bundles disintegrated and a large number of pores and honeycomb-like cavities were formed (Fig. 6C-D). Previous studies have shown that microbial degradation can lead to structural collapse and increased porosity in straw, which is consistent with these observations [45].

FTIR analysis further confirmed chemical changes in chili pepper straw at the molecular level (Fig. 6E). The intensities of characteristic bands at 897 cm⁻¹ (β-glycosidic bonds of cellulose), 1034 cm⁻¹ (C-O and C-O-C stretching vibrations of hemicellulose and cellulose), and 1740 cm⁻¹ (C = O stretching vibrations of hemicellulose and lignin) were weakened after treatment (Table S5). These results suggest partial degradation and structural changes of cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin-related components [32]. Together, the results from SEM and FTIR indicated that C. iranensis 7–12 was able to disrupt the compact lignocellulosic structure of chili pepper straw. Consistent with these structural changes, straw dry-weight loss was significantly higher in the C. iranensis 7–12 treatment than in the uninoculated control after 4 d of fermentation (Fig. 4A). In future studies, the soluble hydrolysis products found in the chili pepper straw fermentation broth should be chemically identified.

Conclusions

The cellulolytic bacterium C. iranensis 7–12, isolated from naturally decayed chili pepper straw, was evaluated for its cellulose- and hemicellulose-degrading performance. C. iranensis 7–12 almost entirely disintegrated filter paper in 30 h and increased chili pepper straw dry-weight loss from 11.98% in the uninoculated control to 32.63% after 4 d of fermentation. Enzymatic assays revealed predominantly extracellular enzyme activities, with xylanase activity reaching 3.41 U/mL and exceeding the measured cellulase activities, suggesting a xylanase-biased deconstruction strategy. In addition, whole-genome sequencing revealed a diverse repertoire of carbohydrate-active enzymes (CAZymes), including glycoside hydrolases, carbohydrate-binding modules, and carbohydrate esterases, supporting its polysaccharide-degrading potential. Moreover, fermentation with C. iranensis 7–12 altered the microstructure of chili pepper straw and reduced FTIR absorption bands associated with cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin-containing structures. Collectively, C. iranensis 7–12, isolated from naturally decayed chili pepper straw, exhibits robust adaptability and enhances the degradation of cellulose and hemicellulose in chili pepper straw.
