Monitoring of alkali–silica-reaction (ASR) in cementitious concretes by microwave non-resonant waveguide measurements
Authors: Ugur Cem Hasar, Huseyin Korkmaz, Vahid Nayyeri
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe length expansion measurements implemented in the literature as the reference methodology (the ASTM C1293 standard) for alkali-silica-reaction (ASR) formation analysis requires measurements at laboratory and thus is not appropriate for the field site, rendering new non-destructive evaluation tools for detection and quantification of the ASR formation. In the present study, we apply microwave reflection and transmission measurements for the first time in the literature for detection of ASR formation in ordinary Portland cement (OPC) concretes. It is observed that microwave transmission measurements are more sensitive than reflection measurements to ASR formation in OPC concretes. A correlation curve is established for the first time as an evaluation and prediction tool for the ASR formation in OPC concretes. The outcome of the presented study shows a high potential of microwave signals as a field evaluation tool for ASR formation at the field site.Subject terms: Electrical and electronic engineering, Composites

Introduction

Concrete is a mixture of cement, water, fine aggregates, and coarse aggregates. Today, ordinary Portland cement (OPC), as the oldest type of cement in use today, is produced by a complex and long-lasting process involving crushing, milling, mixing, and annealing of mainly calcium and silica materials in certain levels1. Generally considered as the filling materials having no interaction with the cementitious matrix, fine and coarse aggregates, bond by a cementitious matrix, constitute main components of concretes involving approximately 75% of overall mass, indicating their importance in construction industry2. However, some aggregates involving amorphous silica are active and interact with hydroxyl and alkali (e.g. potassium and sodium) ions in the cement pore solution, eventually resulting in the alkali-silica reaction (ASR)2.

ASR, which augments in a high temperature and sufficiently moisture environment, is a critical reaction with the potential of producing various microcracks throughout concretes, yielding after all internally irreversible hazards in concretes when these microcracks come together due to ASR expansion3. It is one of the main durability issues in concretes affecting their service lives and maintenance costs2,3. Therefore, a comprehensive analysis of its adverse effects plays a key role in minimizing the adverse effects of ASR by proper material selection and devising evaluation tools for interrogation of existing structures4. Such evaluation tools can provide much benefit if they could be implemented at the field site, assessing the severity and progress of any potential damage at the first instant that ASR occurs. Although non-destructive, the length expansion test, utilized as the general procedure for evaluating the ASR formation, is not much appropriate for field evaluation analysis of ASR because it relies on length expansion measurements of specially prepared samples ( prisms) at laboratory conditions over one year according to the ASTM C1293 standard5. This circumstance calls for new non-destructive evaluation tools for ASR detection in concretes.

There are various non-destructive evaluation techniques used to examine and evaluate ASR within concrete structures. These techniques can be grouped into qualitative and quantitative ones6. Visual inspection as a qualitative technique can be used to evaluate any surface cracking and gel exudation from a damaged structure. Although simple, it does not give information about internal integrity of an examined structure because it is simply based on an external evaluation. In addition, it can only detect the ASR only after the ASR has some progression, ultimately ending up with a structural damage. In this sense, such a technique is not convenient for early detection and monitoring ASR-oriented damage over time, producing a limitation for repair or maintenance in the end6. This is really a key issue because currently there is no cure for existing concrete structures affected by ASR7, expect for mitigating its adverse effects by means of waterproofing and confinement5. On the other hand, quantitative techniques can eliminate the aforementioned drawbacks of qualitative methods by providing an opportunity of examining internal integrity as well as allowing a check-up feasibility about the structural health. There are various quantitative techniques, each having distinctive features, to fill in this gap such as physical length (deformation) measurement8, the ultrasonic waves (e.g., ultrasonic pulse velocity, imaging arrays)6,9,10, thermal imaging8, ground-penetrating radar (GPR)11, and the microwave techniques4,12–15 can be utilized as evaluation tools to qualitatively examine any possible ASR. Physical length measurement technique relying on expansion measurement of concrete with a characteristic expansion of the ASR gel is considered to be a reference technique for ASR analysis. Such a test, however, requires precise instruments to detect minute changes in length expansion on the order of 0.01% with the threshold value of 0.04%, making these measurements vulnerable to measurement errors. This circumstance calls for other non-destructive techniques to eliminate this limitation. Besides, linear or non-linear ultrasonic techniques are widely used in the literature based on measurement of ultrasound pulse propagation through the examined structure. Nonetheless, they require a close or complete coupling the probes to the examined concrete structure with a possible undesired errors for challenging surface conditions. In addition, embedded transducers along with (fiber-optic) sensors could be simultaneously employed to examine ASR expansion and potential oriented ASR-induced cracks. Additionally, GPR techniques can evaluate ASR by measuring the reflected high-frequency radio waves from the examined structure. Nevertheless, their accuracy can be compromised by non-ideal environmental conditions and its effectiveness diminishes in highly conductive concrete samples. On the other hand, microwave techniques, which are our main concern in the present study, could be utilized for ASR analysis. They have the following unique features: i) they have a relatively good resolution16, ii) they have a high potential for penetrating into non-metallic structures17,18, iii) they could be specialized for and are highly suited to field evaluation19, and vi) they are highly sensitive to chemical reactions (e.g., hydration) associated with water and physical/mechanical changes (e.g., compressive strength) correlated to electrical properties4,12–15,20–32. Microwave techniques utilize reflection and transmission properties (or amplitude and phase information) or resonance frequency shift or amplitude decay properties of microwave signals emitted to an examined sample. These techniques are especially suitable for the analysis of aqueous samples due to high absorption of microwave energy with water, and therefore they have been implemented for numerical applications ranging from agriculture33, food engineering34, civil engineering35, and medical treatment36. Although microwave techniques have been applied effectively for these applications over a long time, they have recently been specialized, as a non-destructive evaluation tool, to ASR detection4,12–15. Although being effective and demonstrating a high potential of microwave signals, these studies4,12–15 were restricted to OPC mortars only. In the present work, we extend these studies and examine, for the first time in the literature, the potential of microwave signals for ASR detection in OPC concretes. Additionally, recent studies4,12–14 were restricted in the sense that there was no link between microwave measurements and reference measurements. In the present study, as another contribution, we establish a correlation curve from which microwave non-destructive measurements could be utilized to predict the ASR formation (and its severity) through transmission measurements. Although such a curve needs additional future work, it demonstrates high-potential of microwave signals for an accurate ASR detection and prediction.

The rest of the manuscript is organized as follows. First, a theoretical background about material microwave interrogation is presented in Sect. 2.1. Next, the setup used for microwave reflection and transmission measurements together with its calibration are discussed in Sect. 2.2. Then, the procedure for preparation of concrete samples using different aggregate types (reactive and non-reactive) and the curing conditions they are stored before each measurement are given in Sect. 2.4. After, results of length expansion measurements and microwave non-resonance reflection and transmission measurements are presented in Sects. 3.1 and 3.2. In addition, as supportive of these measurements, SEM micrograph and EDS measurements are shown in Sect. 3.3. Afterwards, preliminary results of correlation procedure devised to quantify and predict ASR level in OPC concrete samples relying on expansion values and microwave measurements are given in Sect. 3.4. In addition, limitation of the proposed microwave measurements for ASR analysis and its applicability for onsite measurements are discussed in Sect. 3.5. Finally, all important points are summarized in Sect. 4.

ASR is a chemical reaction occurring between sodium and potassium alkalis in OPC and non-crystalline amorphous silica present in some certain aggregates13. If not mitigated or eliminated, this chemical reaction causes a gel formation, which can turn into crack formation and eventual deterioration, limiting the service lives of concrete structures. ASR is a hygroscopic process taking place around and inside aggregates, resulting with a gel expansion or swelling after imbibiting with surrounded water. This expansion can create microcracks once its pressure exceeds the tensile capacity of hardened paste around it (elevated by moisture and temperature). These microcracks not only continue to grow but also come together to increase concrete permeability, resulting with a structural impairment in the end. As a consequence, ASR can be considered a chemical reaction elevated with moisturize and temperature producing cracks and permeability inside OPC concretes with reactive aggregates.

Microwave measurement setup and its calibration procedure

In our present study, microwave reflection and transmission properties of OPC concrete samples with different aggregate properties were measured by the fundamental waveguide measurements. Because these measurements are partially band-limited, in our current study, the waveguide measurement setup having the lowest frequency band of 2.60–3.95 GHz (S-band or WR-284) was selected for evaluation any possible ASR formation in OPC concrete samples in line with the studies4,12, because lower frequencies are more preferable for water reaction analysis such as the ASR formation in mortars13. Besides, it is noteworthy that because the utilized waveguide has the cross section of (a cutoff frequency of 2.078 GHz), it is highly desirable for reflection and transmission property analysis of microscopically inhomogeneous structures.

Figure 2 illustrates a photo of the setup used for microwave non-destructive waveguide measurements. This setup includes a vector network analyzer (VNA), two coaxial cables, two waveguide adapters, a waveguide measurement cell, and two additional waveguide straights. The VNA performs complex and measurements from which and can be extracted. It has an operating frequency band up to 26.5 GHz, a wider dynamic range of approximately 90 dB, and a directivity value greater than 32 dB. The energy transfer from VNA to the measurement cell was carried out by utilizing the two coaxial cables (nearly 100 cm) which are phase-stable, thus ensuring accurate microwave measurements. Two waveguide adapters were used for transforming an efficient energy transfer from coaxial cables to the measurement cell. This cell was constructed by a cascade connection of two waveguide straights with lengths of 80 mm and 30.46 mm. Two additional waveguide straights, each of which is approximately 200 mm, were connected between the measurement cell and waveguide adapters to ensure stable microwave measurements by suppressing any overmode signals.

The microwave non-resonant waveguide measurement setup for ASR detection in OPC samples with reactive and non-reactive aggregates.

The thru-reflect-line (TRL) calibration technique was utilized to calibrate the waveguide measurements because it is one of the most accurate two-port calibration techniques used for sexless connectors42. The setup was calibrated for 1001 frequency points in the S-band. For the line standard, a waveguide section of approximately 30.46 mm was utilized while a highly reflective metal plate (without no need to know its reflectivity) was used as the reflect standard. Before beginning waveguide measurements of OPC concrete samples, the performance of the calibration was tested by measuring and of the line standard. It was observed that was smaller than dB while was around dB over the entire frequency band for the thru connection. In addition, it is noted that the measured phase of is approximately at the mid-frequency ( GHz) for the line connection. These metrics are considered to be sufficient requirements to validate the accuracy of the TRL procedure42.

SEM micrographs and EDS measurements

To examine any possible ASR formation inside the produced samples, their scanning electron microscopy (SEM) micrographs were taken. After one year of curing period, the surfaces of all concrete samples were coated with Au/Pd after special adhesive application, and SEM-EDS images were taken by Zeiss Gemini 500 model Field emission scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM) at Erciyes University Technology Research and Application Center (TAUM). Samples were photographed with this device at various scales up to 20k magnification by operating at 5kV. The same instrument was used to obtain one EDS mapping and one SEM image with low-magnification from the high-magnification SEM image group. Optimum acceleration voltage, magnification factor, and image size were all arranged by the operator to capture best results43.

Results and discussions

Results of length expansion test

In order to establish a correlation between expansion test and microwave measurements, physical length measurements, as a general procedure used for testing the ASR formation, were performed according to the ASTM C1293 standard5. Length measurements were performed with a high precision dial gauge (Model number BGE-651/2) from BESMAK Materials Testing Machines with a precision of 0.01 mm out of 10.0 mm. Fig. 4 shows ASR length expansion (in percentage) of the prepared OPC concrete prisms ( ) with reactive and nonreactive aggregates, averaged from three individual measurements for each aggregate type. The maximum allowable limit for the reactive ASR expansion is 0.04% based on this standard for concrete samples. If the ASR expansion is higher than this specific threshold value at the measurement day of 360, then the OPC concrete sample under test can be referred to as a reactive ASR concrete sample based on the ASTM C1293 standard5.

Average length expansion (E) values of the OPC concretes with reactive and non-reactive aggregates (three sets for each) over one year (Error bars are presented at specific measurement days).

ASR results in Fig. 4 point out that ASR expansion values increase with time for both sets. The OPC concretes involving non-reactive limestone aggregates exhibited low ASR expansion values, and no significant ASR expansion was obtained until the completion of the examined duration of 360 days. On the other hand, OPC concretes with reactive quartz aggregates exhibited high ASR expansion values reaching around 0.18% at the day of 360, surpassing the allowable ASR limit of 0.04% given in the ASTM C1293 standard. Such a difference is attributed to the reactions in between the CaO from binder materials and the silica coming from the quartz aggregate, as seen from Table 1. This result is in complete agreement with the findings of the previous investigation44, which states that a high silica amount reduces the Ca/Si ratio of C-S-H in the system and improves the absorption of the alkalis and formation of the ASR gel, leading to the ASR expansion.

As observed from (10), this is mainly due to the effect of different losses within the reactive and non-reactive OPC concrete samples which, along with L, decreases eventually the P value and introduces attenuation within the samples. It is noteworthy that the advantage of using measurements over measurements is that measurements are not only associated with surface characteristics but also mainly related to a change in velocity of wave propagation, which is closely interrelated with a change in , due to hygroscopic process or hygric swelling, osmotic pressure, and crystallization pressure, as detailed in Sect. 2.1.

More information about measurements of the reactive and non-reactive OPC concrete samples and thereby ASR formation inside them can be gained by monitoring temporal variation of at a given frequency (e.g., the mid frequency) provided that measurements have relative uniformity over frequency, which is evident from the results in Figs. 5b and 6b. Fig. 7 illustrates the temporal variation (over Day 1 – Day 365) of measurements of the reactive and non-reactive OPC concrete samples at nearly 3.3 GHz. The following remarks are deduced from the dependencies in Fig. 7. First, measurements for both samples increase over time. This is mainly because cement reacts with free water in the pore solution (hydration), resulting in more bound water. Second, because both types of samples were subjected to the same hydration process and water ingress from the sealed container environment, the difference between measurements of the reactive and non-reactive OPC concrete samples is expected to be associated with ASR gel formation, just in the same manner the difference between measurements of the reactive and non-reactive OPC mortar samples in the study12. Furthermore, measurements in Figs. 5a and 6a support this point; that is, measurements of the non-reactive OPC concrete sample are greater measurements of the reactive OPC concrete sample, heavily interrelated with the difference between electromagnetic properties of free-water and bound-water (bound-water has a value (or tan value) lower than the value (or tan value) of free-water). Third, the increasing trend of measurements of the reactive OPC concrete sample over time is mainly associated with the transformation of free-water to bound-water while ASR gel receives more free-water from the sealed container environment12. However, different from measurements of the non-reactive OPC mortar sample in the study12, which stays fairly constant after few days of curing in the bath environment, measurements of the non-reactive OPC concrete sample continue to increase. This is in good agreement with the length expansion measurements in Fig. 4.

Limitation of microwave non-resonant waveguide measurements and their applicability for onsite applications

It is important to point out the following limitations of ASR detection and quantification using microwave non-resonant waveguide measurements. First, microwave measurements can indirectly be utilized to examine ASR formation through a correlation curve established by reflection and transmission measurements of the OPC concrete samples under examination. In this sense, application of the established calibration curve is partly limited to microwave non-resonant waveguide measurements at S-band (2.6–3.95 GHz) and/or OPC concrete samples with specific aggregate properties. For instance, the 3.7 dB difference between values of OPC concretes with the tested reactive and non-reactive aggregates may vary for another reactive and non-reactive aggregate set. As a result, this can render a drawback for the establishment of correlation curve in Fig. 11 since this offset is not identical to the value in Table 4. To eliminate this limitation, a database of correlation curves could be established considering microwave non-resonant waveguide measurements at other frequency bands or considering OPC concrete samples with different aggregate properties. Second, the value at one specific day can be slightly greater or smaller than the fitted dependence in Fig. 11, leading to inaccurate decision for the ASR formation for a value around the critical value 0.04 according to the ASTM C1293 standard5. To remove this disadvantage, measurement inaccuracies in and (e.g., a margin of 10%) should be accounted in the analysis, and needs to be observed for different test days to be on the safe side before determining the ASR activeness of a concrete sample.

On the other hand, the length expansion measurements were selected as reference measurements5 in the establishment of a possible correlation between these measurements and microwave measurements. However, sulfate attack and free-thaw damage are also associated with these length expansion measurements47,48. While this poses a favorable situation that microwave signals could be utilized to detect sulfate attack and free-thaw damage in a concrete structure, which are considered as future studies, it also produces a challenge that length expansion measurements could be mistakenly correlated with ASR while the main mechanism for length expansion could be sulfate attack or free-thaw damage or both. This drawback could be overcome by following a multi-physics approach (microwave signals supplemented with nonlinear acoustic measurements) by combining measurement tools with different working principles to discern the main cause of length expansion14,49. Besides, while sulfate attack results from chemical reactions forming expansive crystals, which cause bulk swelling and cracking, freeze-thaw damage is primarily caused by internal pressure from ice formation, leading to scaling or spalling, with only minor expansion. On the other hand, ASR involves gel swelling around aggregates, leading to map cracking and progressive expansion. Therefore, expansion in OPC concretes should be regarded as the primary failure mode associated with ASR.

Finally, the measurement setup in its present form requires the sample be positioned into its measurement cell. Besides, although it involves a portable VNA with a weight of approximately 3.0 kg only, it is highly expensive for onsite measurements. A microwave measurement setup constructed by inexpensive measurement apparatus could increase the potential of microwave evaluation tool for ASR analysis in OPC concretes at onsite measurements.

Conclusion

Microwave non-resonant reflection and transmission waveguide measurements over the S-band are implemented for the first time in the literature to relate the ASR chemical reaction with electromagnetic properties of OPC concretes. From the reflection and transmission properties of prepared OPC concrete samples with reactive and non-reactive aggregates, the following main results are noted. First, while the difference between reflection measurements of reactive and non-reactive OPC concretes is around 0.4 dB over the entire frequency band, that between transmission measurements of reactive and non-reactive OPC concretes is approximately 2.5 dB over the entire frequency band, indicating the sensitivity of transmission measurements to the ASR analysis. This indicates that microwave measurements based on a change in propagation factor are more appropriate for ASR analysis. Second, values at around mid-frequency (3.3 GHz) for both OPC concrete samples with reactive and non-reactive aggregates increase with time. This is attributed to transformation of free-water in pore solution to bound-water. Third, given that both OPC concrete samples with reactive and non-reactive aggregates are exposed to the same environmental conditions (hydration and water ingress), is chiefly linked with ASR gel formation. Fourth, the temporal trend of the increase in of the OPC concrete with reactive aggregate, due to transformation of free-water to bound-water, is in parallel with the temporal trend of increase in its expansion measurements. On the other hand, it is shown that a correlation curve, having a behavior of an exponential decaying form, established for the first time in the literature by using length expansion measurements and microwave measurements could be utilized as an evaluation and prediction tool for ASR formation in OPC concretes, meaning that microwave signals have a high potential as a field evaluation tool for ASR analysis.
