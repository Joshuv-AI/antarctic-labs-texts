Critique-Guided Distillation: Improving Supervised Fine-tuning via Better Distillation
Berkcan Kapusuzoglu, Supriyo Chakraborty, Chia-Hsuan Lee, Sambit Sahu

Abstract
Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) using expert demonstrations often suffer from the imitation problem, where the model learns to reproduce the correct responses without understanding the underlying rationale. To address this limitation, we propose Critique-Guided Distillation (CGD) , a novel multi-stage framework that integrates teacher model generated explanatory critiques and refined responses into the SFT process. A student model is then trained to map the triplet of prompt, teacher critique, and its own initial response to the corresponding refined teacher response, thereby learning both what to imitate and why . Using entropy-based analysis, we show that CGD reduces refinement uncertainty and can be interpreted as a Bayesian posterior update. We perform extensive empirical evaluation of CGD , on variety of benchmark tasks, and demonstrate significant gains on both math (AMC23 +17.5%) and language understanding tasks (MMLU-Pro +6.3%), while successfully mitigating the format drift issues observed in previous critique fine-tuning (CFT) techniques.



1
Introduction

Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) is a foundational technique for teaching large language models (LLMs) to perform diverse downstream tasks by mimicking expert-annotated outputs
[
39
,
27
]
. Despite its success, vanilla SFT has notable limitations: it increases model’s tendency to hallucinate
[
12
]
, exhibits limited out-of-distribution generalization
[
5
]
, and struggles to generalize to harder problem instances
[
33
,
32
]
. These shortcomings raise fundamental questions about SFT’s capacity for robust, and complex reasoning.

An alternative approach to improve reasoning leverages
critique
and
revision
at inference time: a model generates an initial answer, critiques it, then refines its output based on that critique
[
16
,
22
,
29
,
28
]
. While effective, these multi-pass prompting methods incur high computational cost and latency during deployment.

To integrate critique signals without extra inference costs, recent works has moved these steps into training. Rejection Sampling Fine-Tuning (RFT)
[
45
]
trains the model on its own generated outputs that are verified or ranked by a reward model, thus incorporating value-based feedback. Critique Fine-Tuning (CFT) instead trains a student model to reproduce teacher-generated critiques
[
37
]
. Although CFT outperforms vanilla SFT on several math benchmarks, prolonged CFT can induce output-format drift—overfitting to critique patterns rather than stable answer structures and its gains are sensitive to the quality of the critiques provided.

In this work, we introduce
Critique-Guided Distillation (CGD)
(Fig.
1
), a novel multi-step fine-tuning paradigm in which a student model learns to
transform
its own initial outputs into high-quality refinements—rather than merely generating critiques. Concretely, we condition a teacher model to produce both critiques and corresponding corrected answers, and train the student to map its raw response to the teacher’s refined version by conditioning it on the critique. By internalizing not only how to identify errors but what a polished response looks like,
CGD
closes the loop between diagnosis and correction, yielding more robust formatting comparing to CFT as shown in Fig.
2
, and significantly lower inference overhead than multi-pass critique methods.

By conditioning answer generation on the critique,
CGD
avoids format drift (the model continues to generate answers, not critiques) and ensures feedback is explicit and grounded. From a probabilistic perspective, each critique acts as evidence in a Bayesian update, the original model distribution
P(y|x)
(where
y
is an answer and
x
a query) is refined to a posterior
P(y|x,c)\propto P(c|x,y)P(y|x)
when conditioning on the critique
c
. By the properties of conditional entropy, adding this evidence guarantees a reduction in uncertainty under critique guidance. Thus,
CGD
’s distillation systematically reduces the model’s uncertainty in line with the critique guidance. We empirically validate
CGD
on mathematical reasoning and broad knowledge benchmarks, observing +17.5% and +15.0% absolute accuracy gains over SFT and CFT respectively on the challenging AMC23 dataset. Similarly, on OlympiadBench, CGD achieves a +12.9% gain over SFT and +8.0% over CFT, further reinforcing its effectiveness on hard math reasoning. Figure
3
visualizes this performance trend, showing that
CGD
consistently improves over strong baselines like Distilled SFT and CFT across all evaluation tasks. These gains are particularly notable given that all methods are trained on the same 100K WebInstruct samples with access to the same teacher model (LLaMA3.3-70B Instruct), suggesting that it is the structured use of critiques—not just data quantity or teacher strength—that drives performance. This supports our hypothesis that critique-conditioned refinement helps student models internalize not just what to imitate but why, yielding more robust and generalizable reasoning behavior.

.

Our contributions are as follows
:

•

We introduce
CGD
, a critique-conditioned distillation framework that preserves answer-generation format and integrates explanatory critiques.

•

We provide a theoretical analysis and a Bayesian interpretation of
CGD
, showing that each critique acts as evidence that narrows the model’s output distribution (reducing entropy), thus formalizing why
CGD
produces more confident, accurate predictions.

•

We empirically validate
CGD
’s effectiveness on mathematical reasoning and knowledge benchmarks, showing significant gains over SFT and CFT while mitigating format drift. We report all our results using a LLaMA3.1-8B Instruct student model, and LLaMA3.3-70B Instruct teacher model.

2
Related Work

We organize prior work under the following broad categories.

Supervised Fine-Tuning Limitations

Standard supervised fine-tuning (SFT) trains LLMs to mimic expert demonstrations, but it often induces the imitation problem, where models reproduce outputs without internalizing reasoning processes. Previous study shows that fine-tuning language model on new knowledge increases model’s tendency to hallucinate
[
12
]
. Furthermore, fine-tuned models exhibit poor out-of-distribution performance
[
5
]
, and gains on familiar data often come at the cost of reliability on unseen distributions
[
18
]
.

Recent work has also shown that SFT on reasoning trajectories can substantially boost mathematical problem-solving with only a few thousand examples
[
23
,
42
]
. Nonetheless, vanilla SFT still struggles to generalize to harder problem instances, leaving open the question of its limits on complex reasoning
[
33
,
32
]
. These limitations motivate integrating critique-and-correction mechanisms beyond naive answer imitation to achieve more robust reasoning and improved downstream performance.

Self-Correction and Critiques

Prior work has shown that LLMs can critique their own outputs and then refine those outputs based on self-generated feedback, yielding stronger reasoning and improved downstream performance
[
16
,
22
,
28
]
. However, these methods depend on multi-pass prompting with LLMs and incur substantial inference-time overhead.

To address this, several fine-tuning approaches have been proposed to endow smaller-scale models with self-correction capabilities
[
30
,
44
]
.
Shridhar et al. 2023
leverage a large LLM to generate initial predictions, feedback, and refined results, while
Yu et al. 2024b
further mitigate train–test misalignment by having the student model produce its own initial outputs. Nevertheless, all of these methods still require a separate critique and refinement pass at inference time, adding unwanted latency.

More recently, Critique Fine-Tuning (CFT)
[
37
]
trains a student model to generate teacher-generated critiques, enabling single-pass answer generation and greatly reducing inference cost. However, because CFT focuses on producing critique tokens, it is prone to output-format drift. In contrast,
CGD
directly fine-tunes the student to generate the final refined answer, preserving format consistency and outperforming CFT on reasoning tasks.

Feedback Mechanisms for Self-Refinement

To study the impact of granularity of feedback on self-refinement, DeCRIM breaks high-level instructions into fine-grained constraints to guide targeted corrections
[
8
]
. LLMRefine leverages human-defined error categories to produce pinpointed feedback, enabling precise adjustments to model outputs
[
41
,
25
]
. DCR further modularizes this pipeline by separating error detection, critique generation, and final refinement into distinct stages
[
36
]
.

Other approaches enhance correction accuracy by incorporating external tools, such as code executors for programming tasks
[
2
,
4
]
, formal proof assistants for mathematical reasoning
[
9
]
, and search engines for factual validation
[
11
,
13
]
to supply auxiliary information. In contrast, our method is self-contained: we rely solely on a medium-scale LLM (Llama70B) to provide general-purpose feedback without any external tooling.

Pedagogical and Explanation-Guided Feedback

Insights from education research stress the importance of actionable, theory-grounded feedback. Importantly, the quality of feedback is critical: if critiques are vague or generic, they may not guide learning. As the FELT framework
[
1
]
emphasizes, feedback must be actionable and specific rather than generic to be effective. Explanation-Guided Active Distillation (ELAD)
[
47
]
actively selects high-uncertainty examples via explanation-step uncertainties, reducing annotation cost while preserving student performance. Other work uses LLMs to supply real-time explanatory feedback to human tutors
[
19
]
and employs sequence labeling to highlight desired versus undesired response components for targeted improvement
[
20
]
. These works collectively underscore the power of explanation-rich feedback, motivating our
CGD
framework’s explicit incorporation of critiques as conditioning signals to enable models to learn both
what
is correct and
why
revisions matter.

3
Critique-Guided Distillation (CGD)

In this section we describe
CGD
and provide analysis of its objective.
We denote the parameterized student and teacher models as
S_{\theta}
and
T_{\phi}
respectively. We use a labeled dataset with samples
(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}
. As described earlier,
CGD
proceeds in three stages.

Initial Answer Generation:
We denote
y^{\prime}\sim S_{\theta_{init}}(y^{\prime}|x)
as the noisy response to input prompts
x\sim\mathcal{D}
using the student model.
S_{\theta_{init}}
is the student model before any fine-tuning is performed.

Critique Generation:
We generate critique
c\sim T_{\phi}(c|x,y^{\prime})
corresponding to the noisy response using the teacher model.

Refined Response Generation:
Finally,
\hat{y}\sim T_{\phi}(\hat{y}|x,y^{\prime},c)
is the refined response sampled using the teacher model.

CGD
performs maximum likelihood estimation using labeled dataset
((x,y^{\prime},c),\hat{y})
by minimizing the following loss

\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{x,y^{\prime},c,\hat{y}}[-\log S_{\theta}(\hat{y}|x,y^{\prime},c)]

(1)

as summarized in Algorithm
1
.

3.1
Analysis

We begin by stating the following general lemma on reducing KL divergence through conditioning.

Lemma 1
.

Let
X,Y,Z
and
C
be random variables with marginal distribution
P(X)
and conditional distributions
Q(Y|Z)
and
Q(Y|Z,C)
. The KL divergence satisfies the following inequality:

\displaystyle KL(P(X)||Q(Y|Z))\geq KL(P(X)||Q(Y|Z,C))

(2)

Proof.

We are comparing KL divergence between the marginal distribution
R(X)
with conditional distributions
Q(Y|Z)
(before conditioning on
C
) and
Q(Y|Z,C)
(after conditioning on
C
).

\displaystyle KL(R(X)||Q(Y|Z))

\displaystyle=\sum_{x}R(x)\log\frac{R(x)}{Q(y|z)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x}R(x)\log R(x)-\sum_{x}R(x)\log Q(y|z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x}R(x)\log R(x)-\sum_{x}R(x)\log\sum_{c}Q(y|z,c)Pr(c|z)

(3)

Similarly, we can expand the other KL term conditioned on
C
as

\displaystyle KL(R(X)||Q(Y|Z,C)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x}R(x)\sum_{c}Pr(c|z)\log\frac{R(x)}{Q(y|z,c)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x}R(x)\log R(x)-\sum_{x}R(x)\sum_{c}Pr(c|z)\log Q(y|z,c)

(4)

Observing that
-\log(x)
is a convex function, we apply Jensen’s inequality to the second term in Eqn. (
3
) and get

\displaystyle-\log\left(\sum_{c}Q(y|z,c)Pr(c|z)\right)

\displaystyle\leq-\sum_{c}Pr(c|z)\log Q(y|z,c)

\displaystyle\log\left(\sum_{c}Q(y|z,c)Pr(c|z)\right)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{c}Pr(c|z)\log Q(y|z,c)

(5)

Using Eqn. (
5
), we can easily show that

\displaystyle KL(R(X)||Q(Y|Z))

\displaystyle\geq KL(R(X)||Q(Y|Z,C)

(6)

∎

Figure 4
:
Visualization capturing the intuition behind improved performance of
CGD
. Traditional fine-tuning uses maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) with data
\mathcal{D}
to optimize the student model and arrive at
\theta_{mle}
. In
CGD
, there is additional interaction with a teacher model which takes as input the prompt, and noisy responses,
y^{\prime}
, from the student model, to generate critique,
c
, and refined responses,
\hat{y}
, (shown in blue). This additional context reduces the effective search space (shown as the blue box in the student model) over which with to perform MLE and obtain
\theta_{cgd}
.

To show why
CGD
starts from a better optimization point, we follow
[
35
]
, and formulate any fine-tuning task as

\theta^{*}=\argmin_{\theta\in\Theta}KL(P_{\mathcal{D}}(y|x)||S_{\theta}(y|x))

(7)

where the KL term measures how likely samples
(x,y)
from
\mathcal{D}
are under the learned student model
S_{\theta}
. Under
CGD
, Eqn. (
7
) changes to

\displaystyle\theta^{*}=\argmin_{\theta\in\Theta}KL(P_{\mathcal{D}}(y|x)||S_{\theta}(\hat{y}|x,y^{\prime},c))

(8)

Given Lemma
1
, and comparing the objectives in Eqns. (
7
) and (
8
), we can show that

\displaystyle KL(P_{\mathcal{D}}(y|x)||S_{\theta}(y|x))

\displaystyle\geq KL(P_{\mathcal{D}}(y|x)||S_{\theta}(\hat{y}|x,y^{\prime},c))

(9)

This allows
CGD
to start optimizing the model from a better point, leading to gains on tasks. Figure
4
uses a visualization to capture this intuition.

3.2
Bayesian Interpretation

Combining Lemma
1
with Bayes’ rule shows that conditioning on the teacher’s critique is equivalent to re
-
weighting the student’s prior beliefs by a likelihood term, i.e., forming the posterior in Eq. (
10
). We can view
CGD
through a Bayesian lens by treating the student’s refined output
\hat{y}
as a posterior distribution that integrates both its own prior belief and the teacher’s critique:

\underbrace{S_{\theta}(\hat{y}\mid x,y^{\prime},c)}_{\text{Student posterior}}\;\propto\;\underbrace{T_{\phi}\bigl(c\mid x,y^{\prime},\hat{y}\bigr)}_{\text{Teacher likelihood}}\;\times\;\underbrace{S_{\mathrm{init}}(\hat{y}\mid x,y^{\prime})}_{\text{Student prior}}

(10)

Here,
S_{\mathrm{init}}(\hat{y}\mid x,y^{\prime})
is the student’s original (prior) distribution over responses, and
T_{\phi}(c\mid x,y^{\prime},\hat{y})
acts like a likelihood term, up-weighting those
\hat{y}
values that align well with the teacher’s critique. In practice, one simply minimizes the KL divergence between the student’s posterior and the teacher-defined target distribution, which directly implements Eq. (
10
) in training via Algorithm
1
.

4.2
Baseline and Training Settings

We choose Llama3.1-8B Instruct as the
student model
and Llama3.3-70B Instruct as the
teacher model
. We compare our
CGD
to three supervised fine-tuning baselines: (i)
Standard SFT:
fine-tunes the student model to generate gold answers conditioned on the input prompt (ii)
Distilled SFT:
fine-tunes the student to reproduce the teacher’s refined answers, where each refinement is obtained by prompting the teacher with the input prompt, the student’s initial answer, and the teacher-generated critique. (iii)
Critique Fine-Tuning (CFT):
fine-tunes the student to generate the teacher-provided critiques conditioned on the input prompt and the student’s initial answer.

All experiments are trained on 16 Nvidia A100 GPUs for 30 minutes, amounting to a total of 8 A100 GPU-hours per experiment, using identical data splits and hyperparameters across methods.

Critique Fine-Tuning (CFT) yields substantial gains on models with strong pre-existing reasoning behaviors. For example, Qwen-2.5—which naturally employs backtracking, verification, and subgoal planning—achieves +4–10% improvements on math benchmarks with minimal impact on general capabilities

Conclusion

We have introduced
Critique-Guided Distillation (CGD)
, a simple yet powerful extension to supervised fine-tuning that teaches models not only
what
the correct answer is but also
why
it is correct. By conditioning the student on its own initial answer and a teacher’s explanatory critique, our framework aligns with a Bayesian view of evidence updating and provably reduces output uncertainty. Empirically,
CGD
substantially outperforms standard SFT, distilled SFT, and CFT on Llama3.1-8B Instruct model across five math and three reasoning benchmarks—achieving a 5.4% average gain over CFT without any additional inference-time cost (Table
1
). Beyond improved accuracy,
CGD
preserves the answer-generation format and avoids the computational overhead of inference-time correction methods. We believe that embedding explanatory feedback directly into the fine-tuning loop is a promising direction for developing more robust, interpretable, and efficient LLMs.

Limitations and Future Work

While our method enhances supervised fine-tuning (SFT) by incorporating explanatory critiques, it introduces additional computational overhead due to the multi-stage generation process involving initial student answers, teacher critiques, and refined responses. The effectiveness of our approach is contingent on the quality and relevance of the generated critiques; suboptimal critiques may lead to less effective refinements. Our experiments are limited to specific datasets, and further evaluation is necessary to assess generalizability across diverse tasks and domains. Additionally, the current framework does not explicitly address potential biases in critique generation, which could affect the fairness and safety of the refined outputs.

Future work could explore methods to ensure the critiques are not only informative but also aligned with ethical considerations, potentially mitigating issues related to hallucination and misalignment.
Since critiques are inherently explainable, they also offer a promising avenue for guiding models toward safer, more aligned, and less hallucination-prone outputs, especially when designed to flag factuality or ethical violations. Investigating how to systematically engineer such critiques for safety-critical domains remains an open and possibly impactful direction.

Example CGD Training Data Sample

To better understand how Critique-Guided Distillation (CGD) enables improved response quality without format drift, we present a representative CGD training data sample. The CGD training pipeline involves three components: a student’s initial answer, a teacher-generated critique that identifies specific flaws and explains the reasoning behind them, and a refined answer that incorporates the critique to correct the initial output. In the example, the student incorrectly assumes the number of electrons per nitrogen atom when calculating the mass of a nitride anion. The critique explicitly identifies this conceptual error, and the refined answer provides the correct computation with an explanation of the correct electronic configuration of the ion. This structured format teaches the model both the nature of the mistake and the reasoning needed to arrive at a correct solution, demonstrating how CGD effectively enhances model performance while preserving answer format consistency.
