Immediate Treatment Versus Watchful Waiting in Mycobacterium avium Complex Pulmonary Disease: A Propensity Score Matched Analysis
Authors: Areum Hwang, Hyeontaek Hwang, Dong-Hyun Joo, Ye Jin Lee, Byoung Soo Kwon, Yeon Wook Kim, Jong Sun Park, Nakwon Kwak, Jae-Joon Yim, Hyung-Jun Kim, Jae Ho Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe optimal timing for initiating antibiotic therapy in Mycobacterium avium complex pulmonary disease (MAC-PD) remains controversial. While previous studies advocate for “watchful waiting” in some cases, specific criteria to prioritize immediate treatment over observation are not well defined.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 592 treatment-naïve patients with MAC-PD diagnosed between 2003 and 2021. Propensity score matching was used to balance baseline characteristics between patients receiving immediate treatment (initiated ≤ 2 months [60 days] of diagnosis) and those undergoing watchful waiting. Primary outcomes were all-cause and respiratory mortality; secondary outcomes included culture conversion and adverse events.ResultsAfter matching, 102 pairs were analyzed. Immediate treatment was associated with significantly faster culture conversion [hazard ratio (HR) 3.07; 95% CI 1.68–5.61] but a higher incidence of adverse events (64.7% vs. 41.2%; P = 0.001). In the overall matched population, immediate treatment did not improve all-cause (HR 0.92; 95% CI 0.53–1.61) or respiratory mortality (HR 0.47; 95% CI 0.19–1.14). However, in the subgroup of patients with moderate-to-severe disease (BACES score ≥ 2), immediate treatment was significantly associated with reduced all-cause (HR 0.53; 95% CI 0.30–0.96) and respiratory mortality (HR 0.35; 95% CI 0.14–0.90).ConclusionsImmediate treatment accelerates culture conversion but increases AEs without improving overall survival, supporting watchful waiting for mild cases. However, patients with a BACES score ≥ 2 derive significant survival benefit from immediate therapy, suggesting the BACES score is an effective tool for guiding treatment timing. Regardless of the initial treatment decision, close and regular monitoring remains essential, as some patients will ultimately require treatment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s40121-026-01433-1.Keywords: Mycobacterium avium complex, Propensity score, Treatment outcome, Watchful waiting, Mortality, BACES score

Key Summary Points

Although antibiotic therapy is the standard for Mycobacterium avium complex pulmonary disease (MAC-PD), immediate treatment is not always required as some patients remain stable for extended periods without intervention.In this propensity score matched cohort, immediate treatment significantly accelerated culture conversion compared to watchful waiting but did not improve overall survival in the total population.Notably, subgroup analysis revealed that immediate treatment was associated with a significant reduction in both all-cause and respiratory mortality specifically among patients with a BACES score ≥ 2.These findings emphasize the importance of an individualized treatment approach and shared decision-making, where the BACES score can help identify patients who may derive the greatest clinical benefit from immediate therapy. Regardless of the treatment decision, close and regular monitoring remains essential for all patients with MAC-PD.

Introduction

Non-tuberculous mycobacterial pulmonary disease (NTM-PD) is a chronic respiratory infection caused by various species of non-tuberculous mycobacteria (NTM). NTM-PD has gained increasing attention as a significant public health concern due to its rising incidence and prevalence worldwide, especially among individuals with underlying lung conditions, such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and bronchiectasis, as well as those with immunocompromised status [1, 2].

Among the diverse NTM species, Mycobacterium avium complex (MAC), including Mycobacterium avium and Mycobacterium intracellulare, is the most common cause of NTM-PD worldwide [3]. Mycobacterium avium complex pulmonary disease (MAC-PD) presents a spectrum of disease severity, ranging from slowly progressing nodular bronchiectatic disease to more aggressive fibrocavitary forms, and its prevalence has been rising globally [4, 5].

The 2020 ATS/IDSA guidelines recommend treatment initiation based on symptom severity, radiological findings, and microbiological criteria, rather than solely on a positive culture [1]. The clinical course of NTM-PD is highly variable: while some patients progress and require treatment within a few years, others remain stable over long periods without intervention [6]. The treatment approach for NTM-PD varies depending on the causative species, but macrolide-based multidrug therapy is the standard regimen for the most common form, MAC-PD [3, 5]. Treatment requires prolonged administration and is often associated with significant adverse events (AEs), including gastrointestinal disturbances, hepatotoxicity, and ototoxicity [1, 5]. Given these challenges, initiating treatment versus adopting a watchful waiting approach requires careful consideration [1, 4].

The timing of treatment initiation remains a key clinical issue in managing MAC-PD. A study reported that 23.6% of untreated patients with stable MAC-PD remained clinically stable for at least 3 years, with 51.6% achieving spontaneous sputum culture conversion [6]. However, delaying therapy in progressive cases can lead to radiographic deterioration and increased treatment complexity [6, 7]. A meta-analysis reported a 60% treatment success rate, but noted that 16% of patients failed to complete treatment, with 6.4% discontinuing specifically due to AEs [8]. Given these complexities, the 2020 ATS/IDSA guidelines emphasize an individualized approach, integrating clinical, radiological, and microbiological factors through shared decision-making [1, 9].

This study evaluated the key clinical outcomes of immediate treatment and watchful waiting in patients undergoing MAC-PD. We assessed the culture conversion, mortality, and AEs using a propensity-matched cohort. By comparing these approaches, we sought to provide insights for optimizing treatment strategies and guiding clinical decision-making in patients diagnosed with MAC-PD.

Methods

Study Design and Patient Selection

This retrospective, single-center cohort study was conducted at a tertiary referral center in South Korea and included patients diagnosed with MAC-PD between May 1, 2003, and October 31, 2021. Adult patients (aged ≥ 18 years) were identified from electronic medical records and included if they met the diagnostic criteria for NTM-PD outlined in the 2020 ATS/IDSA guidelines, which require clinical, radiological, and microbiological confirmation. Only patients with MAC were analyzed.

Patients were excluded if they had previously received treatment for MAC-PD, had incomplete baseline data [e.g., missing chest computed tomography (CT) scans within 1 year of diagnosis, sputum smear/culture results, or body mass index (BMI) data], or were lost to follow-up within 2 months (60 days) of diagnosis. The date of diagnosis was defined as the earliest date when MAC was confirmed by (1) the date of the second consecutive positive sputum culture, (2) a positive culture from a bronchoscopy specimen, or (3) microbiological confirmation from a biopsy. The earliest date among the diagnostic approaches was used for patients diagnosed using multiple methods. The last follow-up date was determined as the date of the most recent outpatient visit or death.

The patients were categorized into two groups based on the timing of treatment initiation. The immediate treatment group included patients who were initiated on antibiotic therapy within 2 months of diagnosis, whereas the watchful waiting group consisted of patients who either remained untreated or started treatment more than 2 months after diagnosis if the disease progressed. We selected the cut-off of 2 months to reflect real-world practice, as it typically takes approximately 2 months to identify the species and drug susceptibility of cultured NTM. Once assigned to either group, all patients were retained in their original group regardless of subsequent changes in treatment status or duration, consistent with an intention-to-treat approach. Patient data, including demographic characteristics, smoking history, symptoms, comorbidities, and chest CT findings, were retrieved from the electronic medical records. Additional information, such as the presence of cavitary lesions, sputum smear results, and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), was also collected.

Group Classification and Propensity Score Matching

To minimize the potential confounding factors in this observational study, we applied propensity score matching to compare outcomes between the patients who were either in the immediate treatment group or watchful waiting groups. Propensity scores were calculated based on covariates potentially influencing treatment decisions and outcomes, including age, sex, BMI, the presence of a cavity ≥ 2 cm on CT [10], ESR, and the use of bronchoscopy as a diagnostic method [11]. These covariates were selected based on their established relevance to MAC-PD prognosis and treatment initiation [1].

Matching was performed at a 1:1 ratio using the nearest-neighbor method with a caliper width of 0.2 standard deviations to ensure comparability between the groups. After matching, the distribution of covariates was reassessed to confirm the balance between the groups. Additional variables with potential missing or inaccurate data, including mycobacterial species, history of tuberculosis, and smear positivity, were compared after matching [1, 12, 13].

To evaluate disease severity and predict clinical outcomes, BACES scores were calculated for all patients at baseline. The BACES score incorporates five variables: BMI < 18.5 kg/m2, age ≥ 65 years, presence of cavities on chest CT, elevated ESR (men > 15 mm/h, women > 20 mm/h), and male sex [14, 15]. Each variable is assigned one point, yielding a total score range of 0 to 5. Higher BACES scores indicate greater disease severity and worse prognosis. This score has been previously validated as a useful predictor of mortality and other outcomes in NTM-PD [15]. The BACES score has been used to categorize patients into mild (0–1), moderate (2–3), and severe (4–5) disease severity groups.

Outcome Measures

Time-to-culture conversion was defined as the achievement of at least three consecutive negative sputum cultures obtained at one-month intervals, as per the Nontuberculous Mycobacteria Network consensus definition proposed by Van Ingen et al. [16]. The date of the first culture-negative sputum sample collection was considered the day of culture conversion [15]. Due to the retrospective design, there was no predefined interval for sputum cultures regardless of the patient’s method of diagnosis.

We evaluated both all-cause mortality and mortality due to respiratory causes or infections. The date and primary cause of death were retrieved from death certificates completed by attending physicians and cross-referenced with data from the Statistics Korea database, classified according to the Korean Standard Classification of Diseases, 8th edition, as of December 31, 2024. Due to the lack of a consistent definition of mortality directly attributable to NTM-PD, we referenced the approach used in a previous study [15]. Table S1 provides further information on the causes of respiratory mortality. In the watchful waiting group, the proportion of patients who eventually initiated treatment and the time interval between diagnosis and treatment initiation were also outcomes of interest. Given the retrospective nature of this study, treatment initiation was not based on predefined criteria but was instead determined by the attending physician’s clinical judgment, considering individual patient symptoms and radiographic findings.

Adverse Events (AEs)

AEs were assessed in all matched patients during the study period; in the watchful waiting group, AEs were captured among those who eventually initiated antibiotic therapy. Symptoms reported during treatment, such as gastrointestinal discomfort, skin reactions, and dizziness, were documented. For auditory and visual disturbances, both objective findings (e.g., confirmed hearing loss or optic neuritis, which are known AEs of NTM-PD medications) and subjective symptoms reported by patients (e.g., tinnitus, blurred vision) were included. AEs were identified based on physician documentation in medical records, including symptoms reported during clinical visits and objective findings such as laboratory results and audiological or ophthalmological assessments where available. Data on new prescriptions or treatment interruptions were not systematically reviewed.

Exploratory Analyses

To identify potential subgroups of patients who may benefit from immediate treatment, we performed several exploratory analyses: (1) defining the waiting period as 6 months (early treatment vs. longer waiting), rather than 2 months (immediate treatment vs. watchful waiting); (2) analyzing patients with positive sputum smears or large cavities (≥ 2 cm); (3) stratifying by species (M. avium or M. intracellulare); and finally, (4) analyzing patients with moderate-to-severe MAC-PD defined as a BACES score ≥ 2. We performed the propensity score matching analyses using the original cohort, applying the same methodology as the main analysis.

Statistical Analysis

Standardized mean differences (SMDs) were calculated to assess the covariate balance before and after propensity score matching, with an SMD of < 0.1 indicating minimal imbalance and < 0.2 indicating acceptable imbalance. Continuous variables were summarized as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR) and compared using the Mann–Whitney U test for non-parametric data due to skewed distributions. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages and compared using Fisher’s exact test or the chi-square test. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was performed to estimate time-to-event variables while accounting for censored data. To further estimate the hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), Cox regression for matched pairs was employed, accounting for the paired nature of the propensity score-matched data.

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Seoul National University Bundang Hospital (IRB No: B-2307-840-102), and the requirement for informed consent was waived because of the retrospective nature of the study and the use of anonymized data. This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

Propensity Score Matching and Patient Characteristics

During the study period, 1276 patients diagnosed with MAC-PD at a tertiary referral center were screened. Of these, 684 patients were excluded because of missing or incomplete baseline data, including missing ESR data within 1 year of diagnosis (n = 420), lack of BMI data (n = 79), and missing CT data within 1 year of diagnosis (n = 50). After exclusion, 592 patients were eligible for the analysis. Patients were classified into two groups: the immediate treatment group, which included patients who initiated antibiotic therapy within 2 months of diagnosis (n = 102), and the watchful waiting group, which included patients who either remained untreated or initiated treatment for more than 2 months after diagnosis (n = 490). Propensity score matching resulted in 102 matched pairs (n = 204) (Fig. 1).

Clinical Course and Adverse Events

Follow-up sputum examinations were performed with a median interval of 6.4 months (IQR 2.9–14.6), which was comparable regardless of treatment group (immediate treatment 6.7 months vs. watchful waiting 5.8 months, P = 0.821). Patients in the immediate treatment group achieved culture conversion significantly faster than those in the watchful waiting group (HR with immediate treatment, stratified by matched pairs 3.07, 95% CI, 1.68–5.61; P < 0.001) (Fig. 2a). In the subgroup of patients with moderate-to-severe disease (BACES score ≥ 2), immediate treatment was associated with significantly faster culture conversion compared to watchful waiting (HR, 4.75; 95% CI, 1.62–13.96; P = 0.005) (Fig. 2b). The culture conversion rate was 45.1% (46/102) in the immediate treatment group and 26.5% (27/102) in the watchful waiting group. The median time to culture conversion from diagnosis was 3.27 months (IQR, 2.10–4.07) in the immediate treatment group and 9.23 months (IQR, 3.98–41.18) in the watchful waiting group. AEs occurred more frequently in the immediate treatment group than in the watchful waiting group (64.7% vs. 41.2%; P = 0.001). The most common AE was gastrointestinal discomfort, occurring in 38.2% of the immediate treatment group and 22.5% of the watchful waiting group (P = 0.022). Visual disturbances were also significantly more frequent in the immediate treatment group (14.7% vs. 2.9%, P = 0.005). Other AEs, including hepatotoxicity, skin reactions, and dizziness, were not significantly different between the groups (all P > 0.05) (Table 3).

In the watchful waiting group, 55 of 102 patients (53.9%) eventually initiated treatment during the follow-up period, with a median time to treatment initiation of 4.89 months (IQR, 2.86–22.54). The remaining 47 patients (46.1%) were managed without treatment for a median follow-up duration of 5.78 years (IQR, 2.73–11.42) (Figure S2).

Among patients who received antibiotic therapy, 69 of 102 (67.6%) in the immediate treatment group and 38 of 55 (69.1%) in the watchful waiting group who eventually initiated treatment were treated for at least 6 months. Of these, 65 (94.2%) and 33 (86.8%) received standard macrolide-based triple therapy (clarithromycin or azithromycin, ethambutol, and rifampicin) for at least 6 months. The remaining patients who did not receive standard triple therapy were all treated with macrolide and ethambutol-containing regimens, with rifampicin omitted or alternative agents added.

Mortality

There were no significant differences in overall survival between the immediate treatment and watchful waiting groups. The HR for all-cause mortality, stratified by matched pairs and comparing the immediate treatment group to the watchful waiting group, was 0.92 (95% CI, 0.53–1.61; P = 0.777) (Fig. 3a). Similarly, respiratory or infection-related mortality also showed no significant difference, with an HR of 0.47 (95% CI, 0.19–1.14; P = 0.096) (Fig. 3b). Mortality showed no difference within the whole cohort of 592 patients before propensity score matching (Figures S3a and S3b).

Exploratory Analyses

Several exploratory analyses were performed. First, we explored the longer waiting period of 6 months, defined as “longer waiting”. Within the whole unmatched population, patients who received early treatment showed significantly worse respiratory/infection-related mortality, without a significant difference in all-cause mortality (Figures S3c and S3d). However, after propensity score matching, early treatment within 6 months was neither associated with all-cause mortality (HR, 1.00; 95% CI, 0.66–1.53; P > 0.999) nor respiratory or infection-related deaths (HR, 1.12; 95% CI, 0.58–2.15; P = 0.739) (Figure S4; Table S2). Adverse reactions were significantly more common with early treatment compared to longer waiting (Table S3).

The subgroup of patients with positive sputum smear or large cavities (≥ 2 cm) also did not benefit from immediate treatment compared to watchful waiting, in terms of both all-cause mortality (HR, 1.00; 95% CI, 0.45–2.23; P > 0.999) and respiratory or infection-related deaths (HR, 1.25; 95% CI, 0.34–4.65; P = 0.739) (Figure S5; Table S4). The subgroup of patients with either M. avium or M. intracellulare revealed similar findings (Figure S6), although adequate propensity score matching was not achievable in patients with M. avium (Tables S5 and S6).

Notably, patients with moderate-to-severe MAC-PD, defined as a BACES score ≥2, demonstrated significantly better survival with immediate treatment compared to watchful waiting, in terms of both all-cause mortality (HR, 0.53; 95% CI, 0.30–0.96; P = 0.035) and deaths by respiratory cause or infection (HR, 0.35; 95% CI, 0.14–0.90; P = 0.028) (Table S7; Fig. 3c and d). The incidence of treatment-associated AEs was more common with immediate treatment within this subgroup as well (62.5% vs. 44.4%, P = 0.028) (Table S8).

Discussion

This study used propensity score matching to compare immediate treatment (initiated within 2 months of diagnosis) with watchful waiting in patients with MAC-PD. The results showed that immediate treatment significantly shortened the time to culture conversion at the cost of increased AEs but did not improve survival in the overall population. However, exploratory analyses confirmed that patients with moderate-to-severe disease, defined as a BACES score ≥ 2, demonstrated better survival with immediate treatment compared to watchful waiting. These findings suggest that a risk-stratified approach regarding the decision to start treatment can be beneficial for MAC-PD management.

The clinical course observed in our cohort aligns with previous findings. Although antibiotic therapy is the cornerstone of its management, emerging evidence indicates that immediate treatment is not universally required for MAC-PD because some patients remain clinically stable for extended periods without therapy. Our study shows that nearly half (46.1%) of patients in the watchful waiting group remained stable without medical treatment for a median follow-up of 5.78 years. In previous studies, spontaneous culture conversion has been observed in 41–47% of untreated patients, particularly those with lower disease severity and limited radiographic involvement [7, 17]. Moreover, elderly patients (≥ 75 years) without cavitary lesions or sputum smear positivity often maintain long-term stability, supporting a watchful waiting approach in select cases [18]. Treatment algorithms now incorporate clinical symptoms, radiographic findings, microbiological burden, and underlying bronchiectasis to guide therapy decisions, including observation in appropriate patients [19]. Furthermore, prior research has found no significant association between treatment delay and all-cause mortality [20].

Our study is the first to identify a specific subgroup of patients who can benefit from immediate treatment compared to watchful waiting, i.e., those with a BACES score of ≥ 2. Although this scoring system was originally developed to predict all-cause mortality in patients with NTM-PD, it has been reported to be associated with disease progression rates, spontaneous culture conversion, and health-related quality of life [15, 17, 21]. Regarding treatment efficacy, it is known that achieving microbiological cure at treatment completion, and prolonged therapy (≥ 18 months) can improve survival [20, 22, 23]. Moreover, post-treatment radiographic improvement significantly predicts better long-term survival, independent of microbiological culture conversion [24]. In addition, patients with untreated MAC-PD often experience worsening radiographic features over time [25]. Consistent with these findings, in the BACES ≥2 subgroup, the survival curves demonstrated the most pronounced separation between 2 and 5 years after diagnosis, suggesting that the survival benefit of immediate treatment in moderate-to-severe MAC-PD is mediated through long-term disease modification rather than short-term microbiological control alone. Furthermore, timely antibiotic initiation has been associated with reduced healthcare utilization and better lung function in those with mild but progressive disease [26, 27]. Additionally, antibiotic treatment has been shown to improve health-related quality of life, particularly in patients with severe symptoms [28]. These findings indicate that, although immediate treatment may not be necessary for all, it could benefit patients at risk of deterioration, reinforcing the importance of an individualized approach rather than a universal immediate treatment strategy.

This study has several strengths in addressing the critical gap in the current management strategies for MAC-PD. Previous studies have predominantly included mild MAC-PD cases [17, 20, 27]. In contrast, our cohort included a greater proportion of moderate-to-severe cases, making our findings particularly relevant for those requiring more complex treatment decisions. The BACES score distribution was well balanced between the treatment groups, confirming that baseline disease severity did not primarily drive outcome differences. Furthermore, the consistency of macrolide-based treatment regimens across both groups strengthens the internal validity of our comparison, as differences in treatment outcomes are less likely to be attributable to variations in drug selection. Consistent with previous research, immediate therapy accelerated culture conversion, potentially leading to better survival outcomes in a subgroup of patients.

Another key finding was the high incidence of AEs in the immediate treatment group (64.7%), a concern widely recognized in the literature [29, 30]. While previous studies have reported AE frequency, our analysis further examined the distribution of AE types, offering additional insight into the clinical presentation of MAC-PD. Notably, gastrointestinal disturbances (e.g., nausea and diarrhea) and ocular toxicity were among the most frequently observed AEs, which can influence treatment adherence and overall patient outcomes. Additionally, we separately analyzed respiratory infection-related mortality beyond all-cause mortality, providing a more nuanced perspective on treatment effects. Although some patients with cavitary lesions ≥ 2 cm in the watchful waiting group did not show a statistically significant increase in respiratory-related mortality, this finding should not be interpreted as evidence supporting observation in this high-risk subgroup. Cavitary MAC-PD carries the potential for progressive and irreversible lung destruction [10, 25], and the absence of significance likely reflects the small sample size rather than a true lack of risk. Patients with cavitary lesions ≥ 2 cm warrant timely treatment consideration and rigorous follow-up, and dedicated prospective studies in this subgroup are needed.

These findings have significant clinical implications. Immediate antimicrobial therapy may not be mandatory for all patients diagnosed with MAC-PD. Specifically, a 2-month period of watchful waiting after diagnosis is feasible for most patients, as this timeframe typically allows for species identification and drug susceptibility testing. This can significantly impact real-world clinical practice, emphasizing that empiric antibiotics against NTM should not be administered unless the species and drug susceptibilities are known. Rather than adopting a universal immediate treatment strategy, therapy initiation should be guided by shared decision-making, incorporating disease severity, patient preferences, and potential risks and benefits [9]. We suggest that the BACES score with a cut-off of ≥ 2 may be a useful guide for treatment initiation.

Regardless of the initial treatment decision, close and regular monitoring remains essential for all patients with MAC-PD, as disease course can be unpredictable and a subset of patients will ultimately require treatment. Clinicians should maintain vigilance throughout the observation period to detect disease progression early and intervene in a timely manner.

Despite its strengths, this study has several limitations. This was a single-center retrospective study, and many patients were excluded due to missing data, specifically ESR required for BACES score calculation, which may have introduced selection bias. Residual confounding factors cannot be entirely excluded despite propensity score matching, as unmeasured factors such as frailty or contraindications to multidrug therapy may have contributed to the higher proportion of high BACES score patients (4–5) in the watchful waiting group prior to matching. AE data were obtained solely from medical records based on physician documentation. As data on new prescriptions or treatment interruptions were not systematically reviewed, mild or transient adverse reactions may have been underestimated. This study spans nearly two decades (2003–2021). Although diagnostic criteria and standard antibiotic regimens remained largely consistent throughout the study period at our institution, temporal shifts in physician practice patterns toward watchful waiting in later years represent a potential source of historical bias [1, 19]. Additionally, advances in CT imaging technology over this period may have influenced the detection of cavitary lesions, representing a further potential source of historical bias. Our subgroup analysis focused on cavitary disease and smear positivity, but other high-risk features warrant further investigation. Finally, we were unable to assess whether patients in the watchful waiting group who eventually initiated treatment had experienced disease progression at the time of treatment initiation, which limits conclusions regarding the potential disadvantages of delayed treatment in initially mild disease. Prospective studies with serial disease severity assessments are needed to address this important clinical question.

Conclusion

Our study demonstrates that immediate treatment accelerates culture conversion in MAC-PD at the expense of increased AEs but does not improve survival in the overall population. However, patients with moderate-to-severe MAC-PD defined as a BACES score ≥ 2 may benefit from immediate treatment, given the observed survival gain. A substantial proportion of watchful waiting patients remain untreated, supporting an individualized treatment approach. These findings emphasize the importance of shared decision-making based on disease severity, microbiological burden, and patient preferences. Future research should refine the treatment selection criteria by integrating clinical severity scores with emerging biomarkers, ultimately identifying patients who would benefit most from immediate therapy while minimizing treatment-related risks.
