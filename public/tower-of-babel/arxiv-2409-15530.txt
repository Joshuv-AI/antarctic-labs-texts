Identifying Elasticities in Autocorrelated Time Series Using Causal Mechanism Graphs
Lion Hirth, Jonas Peters, Oliver Ruhnau, Jorge Sánchez Canales, Felix Schur, Raffaele Sgarlato, Silvana Tiedemann

Abstract
Instrumental variable (IV) methods are widely used to estimate the price elasticity of demand from observational data. However, naive IV estimators may be inconsistent in settings with autocorrelated time series. We argue that causal graphs taking into account time structure can simplify IV identification and help select consistent estimators. More precisely, we propose to model equilibrium conditions using causal mechanism time graphs. These (acyclic) graphs allow us to maintain the assumption of a simultaneous determination of prices and quantities and come with an interpretable notion of intervention. They further allow us to exploit recent advances in graphical inference to derive valid IV estimators that are consistent under weak assumptions. Finally, we argue that observing significant differences between the estimates of presumably valid estimators can help to reject false model assumptions, thereby improving our understanding of underlying economic dynamics. We apply our approach to the German electricity market, estimating the price elasticity of demand from simulated and real-world data. The findings underscore the importance of accounting for structural autocorrelation in IV-based analysis.



Keywords:
instrumental variables; elasticity of demand; directed acyclic graphs; simultaneous equations; structural causal model; autocorrelation; causal inference

Introduction

Estimating the price elasticity of demand from observational time series is a fundamental problem in econometrics.
Observable prices and quantities are determined simultaneously through the equilibrium of the underlying economic relationships, the demand and supply equations.
Identifying the price elasticity of demand from data, therefore, requires non-trivial methodology, such as using credible supply-shifting instrumental variables (IVs)
(
Wright, 1928
;
Tinbergen, 1940
;
Angrist et al., 2000
)
.
Time dependencies in the underlying relationships pose an additional challenge to identification.
IV estimators that do not account for this are, in general, inconsistent and
the resulting bias can be large: in
our simulation, an estimator that neglects the time dynamics is off by a large margin (see, e.g., Figure
1
).

The statistics and computer science communities have developed a framework based on structural and graphical causal models
(
Pearl, 2009
;
Spirtes et al., 2000
)

that
can help to find consistent estimators for causal effects.
Starting from a set of assumptions encoded in a structural causal model (SCM) and its associated graph, graphical criteria based on d-separation (see Definition
1
) can determine which estimators are valid;
this approach applies, for instance, to covariate adjustment or conditional instrumental variable regression
(
Brito & Pearl, 2002
;
Henckel et al., 2024
)
.
Recently,
Thams et al. (2024)
have extended the graphical criteria for the validity of conditional IV (CIV) estimators to infinite causal time series graphs
(
Peters et al., 2013
)
.
The application of the
graphical framework to economic problems has, however, remained limited.
The reason is that graphical criteria require acyclic causal graphs, and it is not obvious how a system in which prices and quantities are determined jointly should be represented as an acyclic graph
(
Imbens, 2020
)
.

In this paper, we extend the causal graphical framework to such economic settings with market equilibrium.
Specifically, we introduce mechanism-augmented SCMs and causal mechanism graphs, and extend them to the time series setting.
These constructions
include the demand and supply functions as additional mechanism nodes. This yields an acyclic graph of the system while maintaining the assumption that prices and quantities are determined
in equilibrium.
We discuss that the global Markov property continues to hold in the linear setting we consider (Appendix
A.3
), and
that the graphical criteria apply unchanged.
Including mechanism nodes explicitly
makes interventions in the system easier to interpret.
The idea of representing mechanisms as nodes has been discussed before – although, to the best of our knowledge, without SCMs, in different graphs, in non-time-series settings and without formal introduction
(see, e.g.,
Imbens, 2020
, Figure 12)
.
We mostly study linear models but extend our framework to nonlinear models in Appendix
D
.

Applying the same graphical criteria, it is generally possible to derive several valid CIV estimators for a given model.
This can be done by
blocking paths via conditioning (this
recovers several known estimators, such as the lag-augmented local projection IV estimator
(
Stock & Watson, 2018
;
Montiel & Plagborg-Møller, 2021
;
Montiel et al., 2024
)
)
or, see

Thams et al. (2024)
,
via estimating nuisance effects,
which
simultaneously estimates and later disregards effects that are not of primary interest.
We argue that deriving several valid estimators allows us to reject false assumptions.
If the researcher’s assumptions encoded in the structural model are correct, two valid estimators should yield comparable results (up to finite-sample errors).
Conversely, a statistically significant difference (such as non-overlapping confidence intervals) between estimates of two estimators that were considered valid should lead the researcher to reject her model assumptions, thereby improving our understanding of economic dynamics.

We apply this methodology to the price elasticity of demand in the electricity market, using wind power generation as an instrument, both on simulated data (Section
4
) and on real-world data (Section
5
).
Electricity is a suitable case because demand is inherently dynamic at high frequencies.
Many electricity-consuming activities involve technological, physical, or behavioral constraints that create intertemporal dependence. Industrial production processes, household appliances, and other electricity uses cannot generally adjust instantaneously to price changes (‘demand inertia’) and may require balancing upward and downward adjustments over time (‘load shifting’).
As a result, current demand may depend not only on current prices but also on past consumption decisions and other state variables.
Moreover, the case is empirically relevant: estimates of this elasticity are widely used in energy policy and market design, yet the identification strategies on which they rest rarely account for these dynamics (see the related literature below).
Which form these dynamics take – inertia, load shifting, or both – is not known a priori, and we therefore study three models:
Model I represents demand inertia;
Model II captures heterogeneous inertia and exposure to real-time prices;
and Model III represents load shifting through a stock of energy services.
Each model implies a different set of valid estimators.

Our analysis on real data
shows that disregarding the time dynamics matters: the naive IV estimator understates the demand response by a factor of two relative to our benchmark estimator, with non-overlapping confidence intervals.
The estimators that are valid across the largest model class agree on a linear demand response of approximately
-200
MW/(EUR/MWh), corresponding to a log-log elasticity of
-0.1
.
The pattern of
overlapping intervals
allows us to reject Models I and II, whereas Model III cannot be rejected.
By construction, our method can only falsify assumptions, so
the results are in line with the existence of
load shifting in electricity markets but do not provide strong evidence.

Related literature.

There is an ongoing discussion about whether (and if so, how) causal graphs can help solve problems in economics.
Proponents argue that causal graphs representing structural causal models allow researchers to derive valid non-parametric estimators transparently
(
Spirtes et al., 2000
;
Pearl, 2009
;
Pearl & Mackenzie, 2020
;
Hünermund & Bareinboim, 2023
)
, as exemplified by
Wright (1928)
, who derived the instrumental variable estimator for the price elasticity of demand for flaxseed oil via graph-based path analysis (see
Cunningham (2021)
for a historical account).
Opponents point out that the utility of causal graphs is limited to problems that can be expressed in terms of acyclic graphs and argue that this precludes a broad class of econometric problems represented by simultaneous equations, such as textbook examples of supply and demand.
They further argue
(see, e.g.,
Imbens, 2020
)
that, so far, using causal graphs does not solve problems that could not be solved with alternative frameworks such as the potential outcomes framework
(
Neyman, 1923
;
Holland, 1986
;
Angrist et al., 2000
;
Rubin, 2005
;
Angrist & Pischke, 2009
)
.
Despite the debate, graphs representing structural equations are not alien to econometrics

(see
Griliches & Mason, 1972
, for an early example)
, especially not to identification in time series:
Tinbergen (1940)
and
Wold (1964)
graphically represented causal dependencies between variables in economic models over time, using what they call an ‘arrow scheme’.
Arguably, these representations were not widely adopted because they were tied to what
Wold (1964)
termed the ‘causal chain model’, which posits that economic processes are recursive, and thus conflicted with the prevailing notion that economic systems are generally in equilibrium and should be modeled using simultaneous equations
(see, e.g.,
Christ, 1994
)
.
This paper engages directly with this debate by proposing a method to represent equilibrium in acyclic graphs, specifically by including the demand and supply functions as additional mechanism nodes.
We believe that this paper demonstrates the value of using graphs in econometrics.

Several papers study causal representations of equilibrium or constrained systems.
Blom et al. (2020)
retain equilibrium relations as causal constraints, whereas we assume a uniquely solvable equilibrium and encode its solution as an acyclic structural assignment.
Blom et al. (2021)
derive causal and conditional-independence graphs directly from systems of equations; in our construction, the demand and supply functions instead appear explicitly as function-valued mechanism nodes.
Beckers et al. (2023)
introduce constraints and a separate disconnection intervention, while we use standard interventions on either realized variables or mechanisms.
Our specialized construction yields graphs from which one can directly derive valid conditional IV estimators for autocorrelated time series.

Causal time graphs also speak to the literature on identifying dynamic causal effects in macroeconomic time series.
Macroeconomic applications often focus on estimating causal effects at different horizons
h
, known as impulse response functions: the response
of a (macro)economic system at time
t+h
to a shock at time
t
.
One can use frameworks such as local projections and SVARs
(
Jordà et al., 2015
;
Ramey & Zubairy, 2018
;
Stock & Watson, 2018
)
.
We provide a detailed comparison to our approach in Appendix
C

and summarize the most important points here.
The two frameworks target different causal estimands: our estimand is the demand response to a hypothetical intervention on the price, whereas SVAR analysis targets the response of equilibrium quantities to shocks, which generally depends on the supply slope as well.
We show that the former problem is strictly easier and
comes with different identifiability conditions (there are settings in which our estimand is identifiable but the SVAR estimand is not).
Arguably closest in spirit is the Proxy-VAR and local projection IV literature.
The lag-augmented local projection IV estimator
(
Stock & Watson, 2018
;
Montiel & Plagborg-Møller, 2021
;
Montiel et al., 2024
)

can be represented using the conditional IV (CIV) notation developed in this paper
(see estimator
\#4
in Section
3
).
More precisely, lag-augmented local projection IV can be viewed as a sequence of CIV estimators for the effects
P_{t}\rightarrow D_{t}
,
P_{t}\rightarrow D_{t+1}
,
\ldots
,
P_{t}\rightarrow D_{t+h}
at successive horizons.
Lag-augmented local projection IV estimators are not always valid;
our framework provides a means to assess their validity and to construct alternative valid estimators
(see Section
3.2
).
Furthermore, graphical analysis provides a straightforward explanation for recent findings in this literature, such as the importance of lead–lag exogeneity of the instrument
(
Stock & Watson, 2018
)
and the robustness of lag-augmented local projections to model misspecification
(
Montiel & Plagborg-Møller, 2021
;
Montiel et al., 2024
)
.

Historically, electricity demand was considered inelastic, and power plants adjusted supply
to whatever demand occurred
(
Borenstein, 2002
;
Cramton et al., 2013
)
. Liberalization, digitalization, and decarbonization
have since increased the scope of demand response, making the short-run price elasticity of electricity demand an important object to study. A substantial empirical literature estimates this elasticity using high-frequency electricity market data

(e.g.,
Lijesen, 2007
;
Bönte et al., 2015
;
Knaut & Paulus, 2017
;
Fabra et al., 2021
;
Hirth et al., 2024
;
Herrmann et al., 2026
)
.
Academics and practitioners routinely use the resulting estimates for model-based assessments
of system reliability
(e.g.,
Bushnell, 2005
;
Hogan, 2005
;
ACER, 2020
)
, market power
(e.g.,
Borenstein et al., 1999
)
, renewable energy policies
(e.g.,
Abrell et al., 2019
;
Reguant, 2019
)
, and geographic market configurations
(e.g.,
ENTSO-E, 2025
)
.
As electricity prices become more volatile with growing market shares
of intermittent wind and solar power, the price elasticity of electricity demand may become even more important
(
Joskow, 2019
;
Ciarreta et al., 2020
;
Maniatis & Milonas, 2022
;
Hosius et al., 2023
;
Ruhnau, 2022
)
.

2
Causal Representation of
Autocorrelated Equilibrium Systems

This section links the economic equilibrium model of autocorrelated price and demand with structural causal models
(
Pearl, 2009
;
Bongers et al., 2021
)
and (causal) graphical models
(
Lauritzen, 1996
;
Spirtes et al., 2000
;
Pearl, 2009
)
in the context of time series data
(e.g.,
Peters et al., 2022
)
.We propose
causal mechanism graphs, which can be used to represent an extension of the SCMs in whichconstraints,
such as the requirement that demand equals supply for the market to clear, form part of the structural assignments defining the model.
Specifically,
for our example,
we
propose to
include the structural equations for demand (and supply) as explicit yet unobserved
mechanism nodes that jointly determine the market-clearing price and, evaluated at that price, the realized quantities.
The
corresponding causal mechanism graph is
a
directed acyclic graph (DAG) representation of the system at equilibrium, in which the simultaneity of price and demand appears as confounding by the demand mechanism.
The SCM serves both as an observational model and as an interventional model
(
Peters et al., 2016
)
, and allows us to distinguish two types of interventions: interventions on realized variables and interventions on the mechanisms generating them (see Appendix
A.3
for details).

We introduce these concepts in the context of
three stylized dynamic demand models.
Each
model takes the form of a classic econometric
single-equation dynamic model used to approximate economic time series
(see
Hendry, 1995
, chapter 7)
.
Each of these models captures a qualitatively distinct pattern of dynamic behavior
plausible in electricity markets:
Model I captures physical inertia in electricity consumption; Model II captures heterogeneous inertia and exposure to real-time prices;
and Model III captures intertemporal substitution through storage or load shifting.
Although all three models generate autocorrelated demand series (see Section
4
), they imply different causal structures and
induce different sets of valid
conditional IV estimators (see Section
3
).
Appendix
F
relates these demand models to the behavior of a forward-looking, utility-maximizing consumer.
We introduce our models assuming linearity. Appendix
D
extends our results to nonlinear settings.

2.1
Equilibrium System with Inertial Demand (Model I)

Our starting point is an adaptation of SCMs
(
Pearl, 2009
;
Peters et al., 2017
;
Bongers et al., 2021
)

which allows for time series models
(
Peters et al., 2013
;
Peters et al., 2022
)
.
In short, here, an SCM is a set of structural equations

X^{j}_{t}\coloneqq f^{j}(\mathrm{PA}(t,j),\epsilon^{j}_{t})
,
j\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
,
where
\mathrm{PA}(t,j)
are the

parents

of
X^{j}_{t}
and

\epsilon^{j}_{t}
,
t\in\mathbb{Z},j\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
, are jointly independent random error variables. We provide a formal mathematical introduction to structural causal models for time series in Appendix
A.1
.

We model demand
D_{t}

as depending
structurally on price
P_{t}
, past demand
D_{t-1}

(we refer to this dependence as

(direct) structural autocorrelation
),
and an i.i.d. exogenous shock

U_{t}^{D}
.
We introduce
this type of
structural autocorrelation to model demand inertia: electricity consumption often involves multi-hour processes, so that feasible consumption in hour
t
depends on past operating decisions.
We further assume linearity, so that the demand equation reads

\textit{Model~I:}\qquad D_{t}\coloneqq M_{t}^{D}(P_{t},D_{t-1})\coloneqq D^{0}+\beta^{P}P_{t}+\beta^{D1}D_{t-1}+U^{D}_{t};

(2.1)

here,

M_{t}^{D}(\cdot,\cdot)

denotes the
mechanism of demand, which is a random function
due to the noise term;

\beta^{P}
is the slope of the demand equation.
Equation (
2.1
) is structural, so it models how
D_{t}
reacts to an intervention on
P_{t}
and
D_{t-1}
(formally defined in Appendix
A.3
);
for example,
\beta^{P}
corresponds to the effect of a hypothetical change
in the price
P_{t}
on the quantity demanded
D_{t}
,
understood as willingness to consume at that price
(see also Section
5
).
We take
\beta^{P}
as the causal (or structural) parameter of interest throughout the paper.
We return to the identification of
\beta^{P}

in Section
3
.

Regarding supply
S_{t}
, for all
t\in\mathbb{Z}
we assume:

S_{t}\coloneqq M_{t}^{S}(P_{t},W_{t})\coloneqq S^{0}+\gamma^{P}P_{t}+\gamma^{W}W_{t}+U^{S}_{t},

(2.2)

where
W_{t}
is exogenous,
U_{t}^{S}
is an i.i.d. exogenous shock,
and
\gamma^{P}
is the slope of the supply equation.
The structural equation of
W_{t}
does not depend on
P_{t}
and follows an AR(1) process:

W_{t}\coloneqq\beta^{W}W_{t-1}+U_{t}^{W}
,
where

U_{t}^{W}
is i.i.d. and
(U_{t}^{W})_{t}
,
(U_{t}^{S})_{t}
, and
(U_{t}^{D})_{t}
are jointly independent.

It is this interventional effect,

,

.
Such an extended potential outcome framework, however, does not lend itself to a straightforward graphical

2.2
Alternative Demand Equations

We now present two alternative models that also generate autocorrelated time series
but with different structural dependencies in time.
First, Model II (‘Partially responsive demand’)
assumes that aggregate demand consists of two components: the first,
A_{t}
, is
price-exposed but not directly structurally dependent on its own past, and the second,
B_{t}
, is non-price-exposed and exhibits direct structural autocorrelation.
In electricity markets, part of demand, such as retail consumers without real-time pricing, is not exposed to high-frequency price fluctuations.
The corresponding structural equations read

\begin{split}\textit{Model~II:}\qquad D_{t}&\coloneqq M_{t}^{D}(P_{t},B_{t-1})\\
&\coloneqq A_{t}+B_{t}=(A^{0}+\beta^{P}P_{t}+U^{A}_{t})+(B^{0}+\beta^{B1}B_{t-1}+U^{B}_{t})\\

The electricity system must always be in balance for physical reasons. The primary mechanism to equalize demand and supply is the day-ahead auction of the wholesale market. This is where generators, retail suppliers, and large industrial consumers submit their price-volume bids for each hour of the following day.
12
12

12

The bid volume does not correspond to aggregate demand, and the bid curves do not correspond to the demand equation. This is because market participants can submit net bids (pool-based bidding) and have the option of trading on other marketplaces. Hence, the demand elasticity is not observable but must be estimated. However, the clearing price is informative about the equilibrium price
(
Knaut & Paulus, 2017
)
.

Because electricity storage is expensive and limited in capacity, wholesale electricity prices are much more volatile than those of other commodities
(
Ciarreta et al., 2020
;
Maniatis & Milonas, 2022
;
Hosius et al., 2023
)
.Our Models I–III abstract from the day-ahead auction and model separate market clearings for each hour.

Identification.

To overcome the endogeneity problem induced by the equilibrium condition, we use wind power generation as an exogenous time series.

The assumption that the structural equation of wind power generation does not depend on the price of electricity is based on the argument that renewable generators have marginal costs close to zero. Additionally, most German wind power generators receive a subsidy

Furthermore, we include other covariates to increase the precision of the estimate, namely commodity prices (natural gas, coal, and emission allowances), solar PV generation, and other calendar controls, see details in Appendix

Models of electricity demand.

The literature identifies two main dynamic mechanisms: demand inertia and load shifting (for a comprehensive classification of demand response in the electricity market, see
Albadi & El-Saadany, 2008
).
First,
processes that run for extended periods, such as multi-hour industrial or appliance cycles, make demand at
t
depend on past load, as in Model I.
Second, electricity-consuming processes can be shifted in time to exploit (high-frequency) power price differentials.
Load shifting may arise whenever electricity is used to build up an inventory of energy services, such as charge in an electric vehicle or thermal mass in a building, as captured in
Model III.
Our alternative demand Model II assumes that demand can be divided into two components: one that is price-exposed and reacts instantaneously, and one that is inertial
and not exposed to price variations, motivated by retail consumers who lack real-time pricing.

6
Summary, Conclusion and Future Work

This paper introduces
causal mechanism graphs and
demonstrates the advantages of using
them to estimate the price elasticity of demand in a setting with autocorrelated instruments.
These acyclic graphs
are able to represent equilibrium constraints (whereby prices and quantities are determined simultaneously) and
include demand and supply functions as mechanism nodes, which allows for a straightforward definition of interventions, both on quantities and on mechanisms.
We provide a reduction of
causal mechanism graphs to a known class of graphs,
which allows us to exploit existing graphical CIV criteria.
Causal mechanism
graphs allow us to express structural assumptions transparently,
understand biasing dynamics,
derive multiple valid estimators, and test the validity of assumptions about structural dynamics.

One version of a lag-augmented local projection IV estimator appears as a special case, estimator
\#4
.
In the Proxy-VAR and linear local-projection settings,
it is used as part of a procedure for
impulse response analysis (see Appendix
C.4
).Furthermore, our framework allows us to
determine when this estimator is valid (and consistent)
and
provides a large family of related estimators that may be valid when lag-augmented IV is not.

We have applied the above framework to estimate the own-price elasticity of electricity demand under three competing structural assumptions: a model in which demand exhibits inertia, a model in which consumption accumulates into an inventory of energy services, and a model of heterogeneous response.
We have shown through simulations that each model can produce identical levels of observed demand autocorrelation, illustrating that the observed correlation alone cannot predict potential bias.
Several estimates disagree when conditional IV estimators are applied to German electricity demand. First, the widely used naive IV estimator disagrees with all other estimators, and thus seems unable to identify the own-price elasticity in this setting. Second, the comparison lets us reject
Models I and II.
All estimators that are valid for Model III agree with each other.
The procedure can be applied to other models too by going through the same four steps: structural equations, mechanism graph, valid estimators, falsification.

In this paper, we have introduced causal mechanism graphs in a linear setting.
However, the graphical criteria apply to more general settings. Under nonlinearity, the IV strategies identify a weighted-average estimand
(
Angrist et al., 2000
)
, which we validate in the basis-expansion case
(see Appendix
D
).

Looking ahead,
besides linearity, our analysis assumes that the structural coefficients are constant over time, a restrictive assumption in economics: preferences, technology, and beliefs may change, and these determine
\beta^{P}
(see Appendix
F
).
The graphical conditions (CIV1)–(CIV2), however, concern causal structure alone and can be combined with recent developments on nonstationary causal effects to define the relevant estimands.
Where coefficients vary smoothly, local kernel-weighted IV estimation may offer a route forward under additional smoothness and local-relevance assumptions
(
Dahlhaus, 1997
;
Giraitis et al., 2021
)
;
where the effect is genuinely period-specific, the time-series potential-outcome frameworks of
Bojinov & Shephard (2019)
and
Rambachan & Shephard (2019)
define estimands accordingly.

Causal mechanism graphs can help strengthen the intuition behind recent results in this literature.
Local projection IV estimators identify the same impulse response function as a vector autoregressive model with the instrument ordered first
(
Plagborg-Møller & Wolf, 2021
)
, which our CIV estimators recover at
h=0
(Appendix
C.4
).
The robustness of local projection IV to misspecification
(
Montiel et al., 2024
)

might admit similar graphical explanations.
Finally, it would be interesting to understand whether non-Gaussianity-based identification strategies
(e.g.,
Shimizu et al., 2006
;
Hoyer et al., 2008
)
can be applied to mechanism-augmented SCMs.

We consider
the graphical approach a powerful addition to the toolkit of every economist working with data structured as a time series, with many applications yet to be explored.

Appendix A
Structural Causal Models for Equilibrium Time Series

First, we give a formal definition of structural causal models (SCMs), followed by an introduction to directed acyclic graphs (DAGs) for time series. We then formalize the mechanism-augmented SCMs we propose in this article. Finally, we introduce the concept of marginalization, which is a way to simplify the representation of graphs.

Structural Causal Models for Time Series

Let
\{U_{t}^{j}\}_{t\in\mathbb{Z},j\in\{1,\dots,d\}}

random error variables.
The index
t

For all

(A.1)

Intervening in the system corresponds to

More precisely, we assume that

)

We now introduce some standard graph terminology

Lauritzen, 1996

Friedman & Koller, 2003

Pearl, 2009

Let
V\subseteq\mathbb{Z}\times\{1,\dots,d\}
. A
directed graph

\mathcal{G}=(V,E)
is composed of nodes
V
(also called vertices) and edges
E\subseteq V\times V
with the restriction that
for all
u,v\in V
either
(u,v)\not\in E
or
(v,u)\not\in E
or both. We sometimes use the notation

(v,u)\in E
.
A

is a sequence of distinct vertices
v_{1},\ldots,v_{m}
,
such that an edge connects each consecutive pair of vertices.
If
v_{k}\to v_{k+1}

we say that the path is
directed from
v_{1}
to
v_{m}
. A directed path
v_{1}\to...\to v_{m}
such that
(v_{m},v_{1})\in E
is called a
cycle
.
A directed graph
\mathcal{G}
is classified as a

if it has no cycles.

We also consider marginalized graphs, which are acyclic directed mixed graphs (ADMGs), containing both directed
(\rightarrow)
and bidirected

edges. A path in an ADMG is a sequence of distinct vertices in which each consecutive pair is joined by an edge of either type, and we define
v

v
(e.g.,

(

We say

A.3
Mechanism-augmented Structural Causal Models

We use an augmented version of structural causal models in which the structural mechanisms are represented explicitly as nodes, which we call mechanism-augmented SCM. In the SCM of Section
A.1
, a variable
X_{t}^{j}
is generated by the structural assignment (
A.1
), where the function
f^{j}
is part of the background model.
In the mechanism-augmented representation, we instead introduce a mechanism node
M_{t}^{j}
, a random function defined by
M_{t}^{j}(\cdot)\coloneqq f^{j}(\cdot,U_{t}^{j})
, and generate the realized variable by evaluating it:
X_{t}^{j}\coloneqq\operatorname{ev}(M_{t}^{j},\mathrm{PA}(t,j))=M_{t}^{j}(\mathrm{PA}(t,j))
, where
\operatorname{ev}\colon(f,x)\mapsto f(x)
is the evaluation map. We therefore extend the parent set of
X_{t}^{j}
to include
M_{t}^{j}
and change the structural equation
f^{j}
of
X_{t}^{j}
to
\mathrm{ev}
.
The mechanism-augmented SCM induces a graph in the sense of Section
A.2
with an extended node and edge set: in addition to the realized variables, each mechanism is a node,
V^{\mathrm{aug}}\coloneqq\{(t,s)\ |\ t\in\mathbb{Z},s\in\{1,\dots,d\}\}\cup\{(t,M^{s})\ |\ t\in\mathbb{Z},s\in\{1,\dots,d\}\}
and we extend the edge set to include edges
(t,M^{s})\to(t,s)
for all
t\in\mathbb{Z}
and
s\in\{1,\dots,d\}
.
For equilibrium systems, we additionally specify one scalar constraint
per time point,

C(p,\mathbf{M}_{t},Z_{t})=0,

where
\mathbf{M}_{t}
collects the mechanisms entering the constraint and
Z_{t}
collects the additional inputs to the mechanisms, such as

(D_{t-1},W_{t})
in Model I. We assume that, for admissible mechanisms and inputs, this equation
has a unique solution and that the solution operator
\Phi
is measurable.
We then define the structural assignment

P_{t}:=\Phi(\mathbf{M}_{t},Z_{t}).

The graph includes an edge into
P_{t}
from each argument on which
\Phi

depends.
For market clearing, the constraint is

C(p,\mathbf{M}_{t},Z_{t}):=M_{t}^{D}(p,Z_{D,t})-M_{t}^{S}(p,Z_{S,t})=0.

Importantly, interventions on the original parents
\mathrm{PA}(t,j)
induce the same interventional distributions in the original and mechanism-augmented SCM formulations. The mechanism-augmented SCM additionally allows interventions that set a mechanism node to a given function. Replacing a structural equation is the standard notion of intervention in an SCM (Section
A.1
); what the mechanism nodes add is that such an intervention becomes an intervention on a node of the graph, so that the graphical criteria apply to it, and that the equilibrium constraint is re-solved with the modified mechanism.
Thus, for interventions on the original variables, the augmentation leaves the
induced interventional distributions unchanged. Its additional role is to represent
the structural mechanisms explicitly as nodes, which can themselves be targeted
by interventions.
Although we present the construction for time series to match our setting, the same augmentation applies to static, cross-sectional SCMs.

This representation of an SCM as a mechanism-augmented SCM is useful for equilibrium systems. Consider realized demand
and supply variables
D_{t}
and
S_{t}
, with mechanisms

M_{t}^{D}(p)\coloneqq f^{D}(p,U^{D}_{t}),\qquad M_{t}^{S}(p)\coloneqq f^{S}(p,U^{S}_{t}),

i.e., here, there are no inputs other than price,
and suppose that an equilibrium variable
P_{t}
is determined by the market-clearing condition
D_{t}=S_{t}
. We can write

P_{t}\coloneqq\Phi(M_{t}^{D},M_{t}^{S}),

where
\Phi
returns the unique
p
satisfying
19
19

19

Throughout, we assume that the equilibrium exists and is unique, so that
\Phi
is well defined.

M_{t}^{D}(p)=M_{t}^{S}(p).

The realized variables are then

D_{t}\coloneqq\operatorname{ev}(M_{t}^{D},P_{t})=M_{t}^{D}(P_{t}),\qquad S_{t}\coloneqq\operatorname{ev}(M_{t}^{S},P_{t})=M_{t}^{S}(P_{t}).

The corresponding causal mechanism graph is acyclic: the mechanism nodes
M_{t}^{D}
and

M_{t}^{S}
are upstream of
P_{t}
, while
D_{t}
and
S_{t}
are realized only after

P_{t}
has been determined, see Figure
2
but without wind.
This distinction is important for interventions. An intervention on realized
demand,

\mathrm{do}(D_{t}=d),

overwrites the realized outcome after the equilibrium mechanism has operated.
It does not change the demand function
M^{D}_{t}
, and therefore does not make the
equilibrium price
P_{t}
adjust. By contrast, an intervention on the mechanism,

\mathrm{do}(M_{t}^{D}=m),

changes the demand function that enters the equilibrium problem. The equilibrium
price is then recomputed using the modified mechanism. In particular, an
equilibrium-preserving intervention that fixes demand at
d
can be written as

\mathrm{do}^{\mathrm{eq}}(D_{t}=d)\coloneqq\mathrm{do}(M^{D}_{t}=c_{d}),

where
c_{d}
is the constant function
c_{d}(p)=d
for all

. Thus,

\mathrm{do}(D_{t}=d)
is an after-the-fact outcome intervention, whereas

\mathrm{do}^{\mathrm{eq}}(D_{t}=d)
changes the mechanism generating demand
before the system re-equilibrates.
The construction extends directly to the dynamic models of the main text: there, the mechanisms take additional state arguments, for instance
M_{t}^{D}(p,D_{t-1})
and
M_{t}^{S}(p,W_{t})
in Model I.
In the linear setting considered in the main text, this representation can be
reduced to an ordinary finite-dimensional linear SCM. In particular, write

f^{j}(x,u)=a_{j}^{\top}x+c_{j}+b_{j}u

and define
m_{t}^{j}\coloneqq c_{j}+b_{j}U_{t}^{j}
. Then

M_{t}^{j}(x)=a_{j}^{\top}x+m_{t}^{j}.

Since
a_{j}
is fixed,
M_{t}^{j}
and
m_{t}^{j}
are in one-to-one
correspondence. Under this identification, the evaluation map

\mathrm{ev}(M,x)=M(x)
is represented by

\widetilde{\mathrm{ev}}_{j}(m,x)=a_{j}^{\top}x+m,

which is linear in
(m,x)
. Hence the structural assignment

X_{t}^{j}=\mathrm{ev}(M_{t}^{j},\mathrm{PA}(t,j))
becomes

X_{t}^{j}=a_{j}^{\top}\mathrm{PA}(t,j)+m_{t}^{j}.

Moreover, the linear equilibrium constraints considered in this article are
assumed to have a unique solution, and solving them yields linear structural
assignments for the equilibrium variables as functions of their parents.
Consequently, after replacing each
M_{t}^{j}
by its coordinate
m_{t}^{j}
and
solving the equilibrium constraints, all structural assignments of the
mechanism-augmented SCM are linear. The resulting finite-dimensional
representation therefore forms a linear structural time-series SCM with
jointly independent exogenous variables and an acyclic full time graph,
possibly containing additional directed edges between variables at the same
time point.

Importantly, replacing
M_{t}^{j}
by
m_{t}^{j}
is only a node-wise one-to-one
reparametrization. In particular,

\sigma(M_{t}^{j})=\sigma(m_{t}^{j})
,
so the function-valued and finite-dimensional representations induce the same
conditional-independence relations and the same graph up to relabeling of the
mechanism nodes.
Theorem 1 of
Thams et al., 2024
establishes the global Markov property for the corresponding infinite full time graphs without instantaneous effects. The same argument extends to the present setting: acyclicity allows the nodes within each time point to be arranged in a topological order, after which the finite-window argument of
Thams et al., 2024
applies unchanged. Hence, for finite disjoint sets of nodes
A
,
B
, and
C
,

A\perp\!\!\!\perp_{\mathcal{G}}C\mid B\quad\Longrightarrow\quad A\perp\!\!\!\perp C\mid B.

Finally, marginalizing the mechanism nodes produces the corresponding latent-confounding edges; for example,
M_{t}^{D}\to P_{t}
and
M_{t}^{D}\to D_{t}
induce
P_{t}\leftrightarrow D_{t}
. The resulting marginalized graph is therefore of the type considered in
Thams et al., 2024
, see Section
3.2
.

A.3.1
Relation to Imbens (2020)

The causal mechanism representation above can be understood as a response to the concern raised
by
Imbens (2020, Section 4.3)
that standard directed acyclic graphs do not
naturally represent equilibrium systems. In a textbook supply–demand model, the primitive
economic objects are not only the realized equilibrium price and quantity. Rather, they are
the demand and supply functions. For each possible price
p
, the functions
q^{D}(p)
and

q^{S}(p)
describe the quantities that would be demanded and supplied at that price. The
observed price is then determined by the market-clearing condition
q^{D}(p)=q^{S}(p)
.
Imbens points out that this creates a difficulty for ordinary DAGs: the natural nodes in the
graph would be the functions
q^{D}(\cdot)
and
q^{S}(\cdot)
, but these are not ordinary realized
variables.

The mechanism-augmented SCM formalizes precisely this idea.
The mechanism nodes
M^{D}
and
M^{S}
are
upstream of the equilibrium price
P
, while realized demand and supply are downstream of

P
. In this sense, the representation keeps the economic structure emphasized by Imbens
but embeds it in a graphical SCM language.
The
causal mechanism
graphs that we propose deviate from the graph suggested by
Imbens (2020, Fig. 12(b))
. We still use the variables supply and demand instead of a single quantity variable. This way we can allow, for example, interventions on demand (not the mechanism) that would yield an inequality between
demand and supply.
Furthermore,
unlike
Imbens (2020)
,
we
propose to add an edge from price to demand, for example,
which is important for
intervention effects and
identification after marginalization.

The mechanism-augmented SCM therefore answers Imbens’s representational question by making the
function-valued economic primitives explicit. Once these primitives are represented as
mechanism nodes, the equilibrium system can be written as an acyclic graph, and graphical
reasoning can be applied without abandoning the standard economic view that prices and
quantities are jointly determined in equilibrium.

A.4
Graph Marginalization

We make use of the graph marginalization
technique called

(see, e.g.,
Verma & Pearl, 1991
)
. The main idea
is to marginalize out nodes
while keeping the same d-separation statements over the retained nodes.
In the context of this paper, we, for example, marginalize out the supply variable
S_{t}
and mechanism
M_{t}^{S}
(see Remark
1
).
A formal definition of a marginalized time graph is also given in
Thams et al. (2024)
, which, too, considers graphs with infinitely many nodes.

Definition 2
.

Consider a
full time graph

over nodes

V=\{(t,s)\ |\ t\in\mathbb{Z},s\in\{1,\dots,d\}\}
.
Let
\mathcal{R}\subseteq V
be a set of nodes, which we call the
retained
nodes.
The
marginalized time graph

where for all

there is:

1.

a directed edge

if and only if
i\rightarrow j
in
\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{FT}}
or
there exist

v_{1},\ldots,v_{k_{1}}\notin\mathcal{R}
and a directed path

\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{FT}}
, and

2.

a bidirected edge
i\leftrightarrow j
if and only if there exist

w_{1},\ldots,w_{k_{2}}

\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{FT}}

U\to i
respectively

Latent projections are purely graphical operations. We use them for identifying parameters in the original graph. (We do not perform interventions in marginalized graphs.)

Remark 1

(Exchanging the role of supply and demand)

The mechanism-augmented SCM treats demand and supply in a parallel way: it represents both mechanisms and models interventions on either. The marginalized graphs used in the main text do not: they are purpose-built for one target parameter, the price elasticity of electricity demand. Marginalizing out the supply side (as in Figure

marginalizing out the demand side yields a graph that, given a demand-side instrument, supports identification of the supply slope

Appendix C
Comparing Graph-based CIV Identification with SVAR Approaches

The identification of causal effects in dynamic equilibrium systems has long been a central concern in applied econometrics. Structural vector autoregressions (SVARs), introduced in macroeconometrics by
Sims (1980)
, have become a standard framework for analyzing the propagation of structural shocks through impulse response functions (IRFs)
(
Kilian & Lütkepohl, 2017
;
Ramey, 2016
)
. In contrast, applied microeconometrics has increasingly adopted the potential outcomes framework
(
Angrist et al., 2000
;
Imbens & Rubin, 2015
)
and
related graphical approaches based on directed acyclic graphs (DAGs)
(e.g.,
Pearl, 2009
;
Hernán & Robins, 2020
;
Cunningham, 2021
)
to discuss and formalize identification strategies; the framework proposed in our work falls into the latter category.

Although these two traditions employ different representations and identification strategies, both seek to recover causal information from observational data generated by underlying economic processes, which may admit multiple causal representations.
In this paper, we use graphical methods to analyze a dynamic equilibrium system that admits an SVAR representation.
This appendix compares
our framework with the SVAR framework
and makes the following three points.

First, the two frameworks target different causal estimands.
Our conditional instrumental variable (CIV) estimators target
\beta^{P}
, a structural parameter of the demand equation (Section
C.1
).
SVAR analysis targets
structural impulse response functions (IRFs),
that is,
the responses of the observables to structural shocks; these include the effects of market clearing (Section
C.2
).

Second,
the identifiability conditions differ.
Both estimands are linked in that
a fully identified SVAR recovers the structural parameters; conversely, only the complete set of structural parameters reconstructs the IRFs.
In Section
C.3
, we provide systems in which the structural IRFs are not identified while
\beta^{P}
is still recovered via SVAR analysis (e.g., Model III), and one system in which SVAR
identifies neither IRFs nor
\beta^{P}

but a CIV estimator identifies
\beta^{P}
.

Third,
in our view, the closest connection between the two frameworks lies in
the Proxy-VAR literature
(
Stock & Watson, 2018
)
. There, similarly to our framework,
identification
relies
on exogenous variation supplied by an instrument and
targets a single structural shock. With a supply-side instrument, it recovers an impact response that equals
\beta^{P}
.
However, the two sets of assumptions are not equivalent. Section
C.4
introduces the Proxy-VAR estimator and gives a system in which no proxy constructed from
W_{t}
is admissible while a CIV (nuisance) estimator remains valid.

Finally, Section
C.5
compares the two approachesin simulation
and Section
C.6
adds a discussion.

is possible under strictly weaker conditions than
(P1)–(P5).
We
give
three systems in which at least
one of (P2)–(P4) (i.e.,
a premise of the SVAR framework and not (P5), the identification conditions of
Rubio-Ramírez et al. (2010)
) fails: in the first two, (
C.11
) still identifies
\beta^{P}
; in the third, it does not (but the nuisance IV estimator
\#8
remains valid).

We first turn to the identifiability of
\beta^{P}
.
Since
\varepsilon_{t}=B_{0}V_{t}
, the rows of
A_{0}
in (
C.6
) express the shocks in terms of the reduced-form innovations. Restrictions (i) and (ii) leave
V^{W}_{t}=\varepsilon_{t,W}
, and restriction (iii) leaves
V_{t}^{D}=\varepsilon_{t,Q}-\beta^{P}\varepsilon_{t,P}
.
Writing
\sigma_{ij}
,
i,j\in\{W,P,Q\}
, for the entries of
\Sigma_{\varepsilon}
,
the condition
\mathbb{E}[V_{t}^{D}V_{t}^{W}]=0
then gives

\beta^{P}=\frac{\sigma_{QW}}{\sigma_{PW}}.

(C.11)

Recovering
\beta^{P}
this way
does not require all of
(P1)–(P5):
it neither requires (P2), nor (P3) beyond its wind row (restrictions (i) and (ii), which give
\varepsilon_{t,W}=V^{W}_{t}
), nor (P4) beyond the single condition
\mathbb{E}[V^{D}_{t}V^{W}_{t}]=0
. This is an internal instrument setting
(
Plagborg-Møller & Wolf, 2021
)
.
The condition
\mathbb{E}[V_{t}^{D}V_{t}^{W}]=0
is the moment restriction that (CIV1) certifies:
in our system,
it holdsbecause

W_{t}
is d-separated from
D_{t}
in
\mathcal{G}_{P_{t}\not\to D_{t}}
given
\{W_{t-s},P_{t-s},D_{t-s}\}_{s=1}^{p}
, the conditioning set supplied by the projection on the past in (
C.8
). If, instead, there was an unblocked confounding path between wind and demand
the condition would generally fail
and (
C.11
) would generally be inconsistent for
\beta^{P}
.
Neither framework identifies
\beta^{P}
in that case.

We now discuss
the three systems.
In the first
system, (P4) fails and
\beta^{P}
is still identified.
To define the system,
suppose the data are generated by a variant of (
C.1
)–(
C.4
) in which the demand and supply mechanisms share an unobserved contemporaneous factor, so that the shocks are no longer mutually independent and
c\coloneqq\mathrm{Cov}(V^{D}_{t},V^{S}_{t})\neq 0
. The structural form (
C.6
),
and with it
\varepsilon_{t}=B_{0}V_{t}
, is unchanged, but
\Sigma_{V}
is no longer diagonal.
Imposing
\mathbb{E}[V^{S}_{t}V^{D}_{t}]=0
when it is false returns
(\gamma^{P}\sigma_{D}^{2}-c\,\beta^{P})/(\sigma_{D}^{2}-c)
in place of
\gamma^{P}
, so the supply row of
A_{0}
, and with it
B(L)
, is not recovered.
\beta^{P}
is identified because
\mathbb{E}[V^{D}_{t}V^{W}_{t}]=0
still holds in this system.

In the second system, defined by Model III (
2.7
)–(
2.8
),
(P2) fails and
\beta^{P}
is still identified. Model III makes the inventory
K_{t-1}
a geometric sum of past demand by (
2.9
); eliminating it, by applying
(1-(1-\delta^{K})L)
to (
2.7
), leaves a finite-lag equation in
D
and
P
whose error is the moving-average
U^{D}_{t}-(1-\delta^{K})U^{D}_{t-1}
.
The system is therefore VARMA, no finite-order (
C.8
) represents it exactly, and (P3) fails at any finite
p
. Since
0<1-\delta^{K}<1
, the moving-average polynomial
1-(1-\delta^{K})L
is invertible, so a reduced form of infinite order does exist, and its innovation covariance is the
\Sigma_{\varepsilon}
that (
C.11
) refers to here.
Wind nevertheless follows an exogenous AR(1), so residualizing on lags leaves the wind innovation whatever the truncation, and estimator
\#4
(shown in Section
C.4
to coincide with (
C.11
)) is valid under Model III.

In the third system, (P3) fails in its wind row and the SVAR approach identifies neither
\beta^{P}
nor
B(L)
.
To define the system,
consider the confounded system described in Section
3.2
, where, starting from Model I, hidden confounding links
W_{t}
with
D_{t-1}
and
D_{t-1}
with
D_{t}
. This makes
D_{t-1}
a non-collider on
W_{t}\leftrightarrow D_{t-1}\to D_{t}
and a collider on
W_{t}\leftrightarrow D_{t-1}\leftrightarrow D_{t}
: not conditioning on it leaves the first path open, which invalidates estimator
\#2
, while conditioning on it, which is what estimator
\#4
and the projection in (
C.8
) do, opens the second.
More precisely, the
contemporaneous condition
\mathbb{E}[V^{D}_{t}V^{W}_{t}]=0
remains intact, but
the innovation
\varepsilon_{t,W}
is no longer
V^{W}_{t}
, because (
C.8
) conditions on the collider
D_{t-1}
, and this correlates
\varepsilon_{t,W}
with the constructed demand shock
\varepsilon_{t,Q}-\beta^{P}\varepsilon_{t,P}
. Applied to
\varepsilon_{t}
, the rows of
A_{0}
therefore do not return the structural shocks, and (
C.11
) does not identify
\beta^{P}
either. In the second system, the projection on the past still delivered the wind innovation; here it does not, and no admissible control set restores it (see Section
C.4
).
The nuisance IV estimator
\#8
remains valid (Section
3.2
).

Recovering
\beta^{P}
when
B(L)
is not identified is a different question from the one
Rubio-Ramírez et al. (2010)
ask, and closer to the Proxy-VAR approach
(
Stock & Watson, 2018
)
we turn to next.

C.4
Comparing Proxy-VAR and CIV Identification

Stock & Watson (2018)
translated the IV identification strategy from microeconometrics to SVAR and local projection models.
Rather than recovering the full impact matrix from covariance restrictions, a Proxy-VAR uses an external variable
z_{t}
, correlated with one target shock and uncorrelated with the others, to trace that shock alone. The procedure has two steps. A reduced-form VAR (
C.8
) is estimated by least squares and its residuals
\hat{\varepsilon}_{t}
retained, which removes the influence of the past; the impact vector is then obtained, up to scale, as the covariances of the proxy
z_{t}
with those residuals, normalized so that the price response is one, and propagated through the estimated reduced-form moving-average matrices
\hat{C}_{h}
,

\hat{b}=\frac{\widehat{\mathbb{E}}\big[z_{t}\,\hat{\varepsilon}_{t}\big]}{\widehat{\mathbb{E}}\big[z_{t}\,\hat{\varepsilon}_{t,P}\big]},\qquad\hat{\theta}_{h}=\hat{C}_{h}\,\hat{b},\qquad h=0,1,2,\ldots

(C.12)

Here
\widehat{\mathbb{E}}
denotes the sample average,
\hat{\varepsilon}_{t,j}
the
j
th entry of
\hat{\varepsilon}_{t}
, and
C(L)=\Pi(L)^{-1}
collects the
reduced-form moving-average
matrices, with
C_{0}=I
.
The first step is the projection on lags used in (
C.8
); the second is what makes this an IV procedure, since the proxy supplies the exogenous variation and (
C.12
) is a ratio of reduced-form covariances.

Take the wind innovation as the proxy,
z_{t}=\hat{\varepsilon}_{t,W}
, and set
h=0
. Then
\hat{\varepsilon}_{t,Q}
and
\hat{\varepsilon}_{t,P}
are
Q_{t}
and
P_{t}
residualized on
\{W_{t-s},P_{t-s},Q_{t-s}\}_{s=1}^{p}
, and
z_{t}
is
W_{t}
residualized on the same set, so by the Frisch–Waugh–Lovell theorem

\hat{\theta}_{Q,0}=\mathrm{CIV}\big(W_{t}\mid P_{t}\to D_{t}\mid\{W_{t-s},P_{t-s},D_{t-s}\}_{s=1}^{p}\big),

where
\hat{\theta}_{Q,0}
is the entry of
\hat{\theta}_{0}
that corresponds to quantity. This expression is estimator
\#4
of Table
1
(with
L=p
and given that in equilibrium
Q_{t}=D_{t}
), the

lag-augmented local projection IV estimator
at
h=0

(
Montiel & Plagborg-Møller, 2021
;
Plagborg-Møller & Wolf, 2021
)
. It is also (
C.11
), since both are
\sigma_{QW}/\sigma_{PW}
. The fully identified SVAR, the Proxy-VAR, and CIV estimator
\#4
thus yield the same
estimate
for
\beta^{P}
in this system. The equivalence is exact at finite
p
here because Model I’s reduced form is a VAR(1)
(
Plagborg-Møller & Wolf, 2021
)
.

The assumptions
required by Proxy-VAR and CIV are closely related. Both cover relevance and exogeneity, but they are stated differently and are not equivalent. For a target shock
V^{1}_{t}
and proxy
z_{t}
,
Stock & Watson (2018, Section 1.3)
require

\underbrace{\mathbb{E}[z_{t}V^{1}_{t}]\neq 0}_{\text{relevance}},\qquad\underbrace{\mathbb{E}[z_{t}V^{j}_{t}]=0\ \ \forall j\neq 1}_{\text{contemporaneous exogeneity}},\qquad\underbrace{\mathbb{E}[z_{t}V_{t+k}]=0\ \ \forall k\neq 0}_{\text{lead--lag exogeneity}},

(C.13)

where
V^{j}_{t}
is the
j
th entry of
V_{t}
and
V^{1}_{t}
the targeted shock, together with regularity conditions ensuring well-defined covariances. They also allow these conditions to hold only after residualizing on control variables
(Condition LP-IV
⟂
of
Stock & Watson, 2018
, Section 1.4)
. The CIV criteria (CIV1)–(CIV3) are given in Section
3.1
.
From here, we draw three comparisons.

First, our CIV framework and
Stock & Watson (2018)
are closely related.
Consider wind in levels as the proxy. Strict lead–lag exogeneity
fails:
\mathbb{E}[W_{t}U^{W}_{t-s}]=(\beta^{W})^{s}\sigma_{W}^{2}\neq 0
for
s\geq 1
, which is why the naive IV estimator
\#1
is inconsistent
(Section
3.2
).
Both frameworks agree on the remedy: conditioning on
\{W_{t-s}\}_{s=1}^{L}
, as estimator
\#2
does, residualizes wind on its own past and leaves the wind innovation, for which lead–lag exogeneity holds.
This works because wind’s serial correlation comes from its own innovation alone. In general,
Stock & Watson (2018, Theorem 1, Section 2.2.3)
show that adding lagged observables as generic controls restores lead–lag exogeneity if and only if the corresponding VAR is invertible.

Second, the CIV criteria admit designs that neither (
C.13
) nor its control-variable version admits.
The two frameworks depart when lead–lag exogeneity cannot be restored by residualization, that is, when no admissible control set exists.
In the confounded system of Section
3.2
,
D_{t-1}
is a collider on one path from
W_{t}
to
D_{t}
and a non-collider on another (Section
C.3
), so neither conditioning on it nor omitting it leaves
\hat{\varepsilon}_{t,W}
a valid proxy. The nuisance IV estimator
\#8
is nevertheless valid.

Third, using a graph
makes the estimand explicit.
A supply-side instrument moves the equilibrium price along the demand function, so the ratio of its impact effects on quantity and on price is
\beta^{P}
; conversely, a demand-side instrument moves it along the supply function, so that ratio is
\gamma^{P}
.
More precisely, with
z_{t}=\hat{\varepsilon}_{t,W}
the vector
\hat{b}
estimates the wind column of
B_{0}
normalized to a unit price impact. At
h=0
the rescaling divides out
\gamma^{W}/(\beta^{P}-\gamma^{P})
and leaves
\beta^{P}
; at
h>0
it leaves the propagation of the instrument’s own shock through the system, not the response of
Q_{t}
to a shock in prices. In the graph, by contrast, the estimator names the edge
P_{t}\to D_{t}
(not
P_{t}\to Q_{t}
), so which effect is being estimated is part of its specification. Under CIV we claim identification of the structural coefficient
\beta^{P}
only, not of a dynamic response.

C.6
Discussion

We have established that the two frameworks target different estimands, that
\beta^{P}
can be identified under strictly weaker conditions than those that identify
B(L)
,
and that the CIV criteria admit designs that the Proxy-VAR conditions do not.

The CIV criteria characterize the set of valid
(\mathcal{I},\mathcal{X},\mathcal{B})
configurations for a given graph, and typically several can be valid, of which Table
1
lists a few.
Disagreement among estimators that are valid under the same graph is evidence against the assumptions encoded in the model (Section
3.2
), and in the application this allows us to reject Models I and II (Section
5
).
A just-identified SVAR scheme (a set of zero restrictions or a single proxy) only yields a single estimator of
\beta^{P}
and cannot directly be used in a similar way.

Still, we do not think that either
approach strictly dominates the other.
The same equilibrium system admits multiple causal representations, each suited to a different question and to the methods a given setting allows. Researchers interested in equilibrium propagation may prefer SVAR methods; those interested in recovering the primitive structural relationships
and in obtaining several different estimators
may benefit from graphical identification.

Appendix D
Extension to Nonlinear Causal Effects

The main text assumes that all structural equations are linear.
In the classical (i.e., non-equilibrium) nonlinear IV literature,
identifiability
has been studied
(e.g.,
Amemiya, 1974
;
Newey & Powell, 2003
;
Imbens & Newey, 2009
;
Newey, 2013
;
Christiansen et al., 2022
)
; from a more practical perspective,
several estimators have been proposed
(
Newey & Powell, 2003
;
Hartford et al., 2017
;
Racine & Hayfield, 2018
;
Chen & Christensen, 2018
;
Singh et al., 2019
;
Christiansen et al., 2022
)
, many of which have not yet been extended to the conditional case.
This appendix relaxes the linearity assumption for equilibrium systems: we restate the
system in nonlinear form and define the estimand
(Appendix
D.1
), ask what an IV analysis identifies and
connect
our
graphical formulation
to the average-derivative interpretation of

Angrist et al. (2000)

(Appendix
D.2
), develop the tractable case of structural
equations that are linear after a known feature transformation
(Appendix
D.3
), validate
our results
in simulation
(Appendix
D.4
), and
discuss how the graphical view is helpful in the nonlinear case, too
(Appendix
D.5
).

D.1
Nonlinear Equilibrium Systems and the Causal Estimand

We return to the equilibrium system of Section
2.1
,
now without the linearity assumption. Taking Model I as the running example,
the structural equations for demand, supply, and the instrument are

\displaystyle D_{t}

\displaystyle\coloneqq f^{D}(P_{t},D_{t-1},U^{D}_{t}),

(D.1)

\displaystyle S_{t}

\displaystyle\coloneqq f^{S}(P_{t},W_{t},U^{S}_{t}),

(D.2)

\displaystyle W_{t}

\displaystyle\coloneqq f^{W}(W_{t-1},U^{W}_{t}),

(D.3)

with mutually independent i.i.d. shocks
U^{D}_{t},U^{S}_{t},U^{W}_{t}
, each with mean
zero. The alternative demand structures (Models II and III) are obtained by
changing the arguments of
f^{D}
exactly as in
Section
2.2
; the discussion below covers all three.

We assume that
f^{D}
is continuously differentiable and strictly decreasing in
its price argument, that
f^{S}
is continuously differentiable and strictly
increasing in its price argument, and that
(D_{t},P_{t},W_{t})
is strictly
stationary and ergodic with finite second moments. Under these conditions the
market-clearing condition

f^{D}(P_{t},D_{t-1},U^{D}_{t})=f^{S}(P_{t},W_{t},U^{S}_{t})

(D.4)

admits, for every realization of
(D_{t-1},W_{t},U^{D}_{t},U^{S}_{t})
, a
solution for the equilibrium price, which we write as

P_{t}=f^{P}(D_{t-1},W_{t},U^{D}_{t},U^{S}_{t})
. The monotonicity of
f^{D}
and
f^{S}
in
price guarantees the uniqueness of this solution;
it is
the nonlinear counterpart of the requirement
\beta^{P}\neq\gamma^{P}
in the
linear model, and coincides with the structural monotonicity conditions of

Angrist et al. (2000, Lemma 1)
.
Under the further assumption that the curves cross at one point, this
ensures the existence of the solution.
As in the main text the equations
are structural in
(P_{t},D_{t-1})
and
(P_{t},W_{t})
; in potential-outcome
notation we write
D_{t}(p)\coloneqq f^{D}(p,D_{t-1},U^{D}_{t})
and

S_{t}(p,w)\coloneqq f^{S}(p,w,U^{S}_{t})
for the demand and supply that would be
realized at price
p
(and instrument
w
).

In the linear model the causal effect of interest is the slope
\beta^{P}
. For a
general
f^{D}
the natural causal object is not a single number but a function –
the
average structural function
(ASF)
(e.g.,
Imbens & Newey, 2009
)
, i.e., the average dose–response of
demand to an intervention on price,

m^{*}(p)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}\!\left[D_{t}\mid\mathrm{do}(P_{t}=p)\right]=\mathbb{E}\!\left[f^{D}(p,D_{t-1},U^{D}_{t})\right].

(D.5)

Because the intervention is on
P_{t}
alone, the expectation in (
D.5
) is
over the stationary (observational) distribution of
(D_{t-1},U^{D}_{t})
, which the
intervention leaves unchanged. When
f^{D}
is linear,
m^{*}(p)=\tilde{D}^{0}+\beta^{P}p

with
\tilde{D}^{0}\coloneqq D^{0}+\beta^{D1}\mathbb{E}[D_{t-1}]+\mathbb{E}[U^{D}_{t}]
, so
the main-text parameter
\beta^{P}

is exactly
the (constant) slope
m^{*\prime}
.
When
f^{D}
is nonlinear,
m^{*}
is curved and is no longer captured by a single
slope.

The shape of
m^{*}
also pins down what the edge
P_{t}\to D_{t}
represents when the
shock does not enter additively. Suppose, for example, that
U^{D}_{t}
moderates the
price response,

f^{D}(P_{t},D_{t-1},U^{D}_{t})=D^{0}+\beta_{0}^{P}P_{t}+\beta^{D1}D_{t-1}+\theta^{D}\,P_{t}U^{D}_{t}+U^{D}_{t}.

(D.6)

Then
m^{*}(p)=\tilde{D}^{0}+(\beta_{0}^{P}+\theta^{D}\,\mathbb{E}[U^{D}_{t}])\,p
, so the slope of
interest is the
total
price effect
\beta_{0}^{P}+\theta^{D}\,\mathbb{E}[U^{D}_{t}]

– the average response of demand to an intervention on price – rather than the
conditional slope
\beta_{0}^{P}
that holds at a fixed value of
U^{D}_{t}
(though in the case where
\mathbb{E}[U^{D}_{t}]=0
this is the same). We take this
total effect (equivalently, the slope of
m^{*}
) to be the estimand throughout this appendix.

D.2
Identifiability and Identification under Nonlinear Structural Equations

We now ask what an instrument that shifts supply identifies about the average
structural function (
D.5
). To separate the role of nonlinearity from
the autocorrelation studied in the main text, we suppress the lagged arguments
and write
f^{D}(P_{t},U^{D}_{t})
; lagged parents are handled exactly as in
Section
3
, by including them in the conditioning set

\mathcal{B}
.

Let
m^{*}(p)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}[D_{t}\mid\mathrm{do}(P_{t}=p)]=\mathbb{E}[D_{t}(p)]

denote the average structural function (ASF) of
Appendix
D.1
, and define the residual

e_{t}\coloneqq D_{t}-m^{*}(P_{t}),\qquad\text{so that}\qquad D_{t}=m^{*}(P_{t})+e_{t}.

(D.7)

The decomposition (
D.7
) holds by construction.
If, in addition,

\mathbb{E}[e_{t}\mid W_{t}]=0,

(D.8)

then
m^{*}
solves the conditional moment restriction

\mathbb{E}[D_{t}-m(P_{t})\mid W_{t}]=0
and is identified under a standard
completeness condition
(
Newey & Powell, 2003
)
.
We now discuss whether
the
ASF satisfies the instrument moment condition (
D.8
).

Crucially, (
D.8
) is, in general,
not
implied by the graphical validity of
W_{t}
as an
instrument for linear SCMs (conditions (CIV1) and (CIV2)). A graphically valid instrument
implies
exogeneity,

W_{t}\perp\!\!\!\perp U^{D}_{t}
, and the exclusion restriction that
W_{t}
does not enter
f^{D}
;
these properties constrain how
W_{t}
relates to the structural shock

U^{D}_{t}

but say nothing about
how that shock enters
f^{D}
. In fact, (
D.8
) holds automatically if the shock is additively separable,
that is,

D_{t}=m^{*}(P_{t})+U^{D}_{t}.

(D.9)

Under (
D.9
) the residual coincides with the structural shock,

e_{t}=U^{D}_{t}
, and (
D.8
)
coincides with
instrument exogeneity

\mathbb{E}[U^{D}_{t}\mid W_{t}]=0
. Note that (
D.9
) does not
require
m^{*}
to be linear: the price response may be arbitrarily curved, as long
as the shock does not interact with price.

When the shock is not additively separable,
e_{t}
ceases to equal
U^{D}_{t}
and
inherits a price-dependent, confounded component. For the moderating
specification (
D.6
), the ASF slope is

\beta^{P}=\beta_{0}^{P}+\theta^{D}\mathbb{E}[U^{D}_{t}]
and the residual contains the term

\theta^{D}P_{t}(U^{D}_{t}-\mathbb{E}[U^{D}_{t}])
. Because the equilibrium
price (
D.4
) depends on
U^{D}_{t}
, the price
P_{t}
is correlated
with the shock, so
\mathbb{E}[e_{t}\mid W_{t}]
does not vanish in general.

The decisive point is that additive separability is a restriction on the
functional form of
f^{D}
, not a property of the causal graph. Conditioning on a
set
\mathcal{B}
, as in the criteria (CIV1)–(CIV3), can restore a violated
exogeneity or exclusion restriction by blocking a path, but it cannot, in general, turn a
nonseparable structural equation into a separable one. Note that nonseparability does not preclude identification per se:
Imbens & Newey (2009)
, for example, identify the ASF in a nonseparable triangular model by exploiting a scalar-monotone first stage to construct a control variable, together with an appropriate common-support condition.

D.2.1
The Estimator-side reading.

The same phenomenon has a complementary description on the side of the estimator,
due to
Angrist et al. (2000)
. Rather than asking whether the ASF
satisfies the moment condition, they fix the linear IV estimator – the ratio of
the covariance of the outcome and the instrument to the covariance of price and
the instrument – and ask what it identifies when demand is heterogeneous.
Casting the simultaneous system in terms of the potential outcomes
D_{t}(p)
,

S_{t}(p,w)
rather than functional-form-specific residuals, they show, under four
assumptions,

(i)

independence
:
W_{t}\perp\!\!\!\perp\{D_{t}(p),S_{t}(p,w)\}_{p,w}
(given
covariates), the instrument is as good as randomly assigned;

(ii)

exclusion
:
W_{t}
does not enter demand;

(iii)

relevance
:
W_{t}
has a non-trivial effect on the
equilibrium price;

(iv)

monotonicity
: the equilibrium price is monotone in
W_{t}
;

that the IV estimand equals a weighted average of the derivative of the demand
function,

\beta^{\mathrm{IV}}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{\partial D_{t}}{\partial p}(p)\,\middle|\,p\in\mathcal{P}_{t}\right]\omega(p)\,\mathrm{d}p,\qquad\omega\geq 0,\quad\int\omega(p)\,\mathrm{d}p=1,

(D.10)

where the weights
\omega(p)
are proportional to the number of periods whose
price the instrument shifts across
p
, and
\mathcal{P}_{t}
is the
corresponding price interval. Monotonicity (iv) makes these
weights non-negative; in the linear constant-coefficient model it holds
automatically,
and otherwise it has to be assumed. Related recent work by
Kolesár & Plagborg-Møller (2025)
establishes
weighted-average causal interpretations for linear local projections and VARs
based on observed shocks or proxies under nonlinear data-generating processes.

The two assumptions on the shock map onto the two regimes above. When demand is
identical across periods up to an additive shift,
D_{t}(p)=m^{*}(p)+U^{D}_{t}
– the
separable case (
D.9
) – the estimand (
D.10
) is a weighted
average of the slope of the population-average demand curve, with no averaging
over heterogeneity
(
Angrist et al., 2000
, Cor. 3)
; this is the
regime in which
e_{t}=U^{D}_{t}
and IV recovers the slope of
m^{*}
(exactly so
when that slope is constant). When instead the price response is heterogeneous –
say
D_{t}(p)=a_{t}-b_{t}\,p
with random coefficients – (
D.10
) becomes a
price-gap–weighted average of the period-specific slopes
b_{t}
, which in general
differs from the average slope
\mathbb{E}[b_{t}]

(
Angrist et al., 2000
, Cor. 2)
. In this regime

\mathbb{E}[e_{t}\mid W_{t}]\neq 0
, and the weighted average in (
D.10
) is
precisely the object hidden inside the residual
e_{t}
.

These complications motivate the restriction to linear structural equations in
the main text. They also delimit a useful intermediate case: if the outcome
equation is linear and separable after a known, finite feature transformation of price, the
difficulties above disappear and the conditional-IV estimators of
Section
3
apply with only a change of variables. We
develop this case next.

D.3
Feature-Expansion without Interactions

Assume a structural causal model for time series as in
Appendix
A.1
, and suppose the structural equation for the
outcome
Y
is linear in a known feature map of the endogenous variables,

X_{Y}=\beta^{\top}\phi(X_{\mathcal{X}})+\gamma^{\top}X_{K}+\epsilon^{Y},

(D.11)

where
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{X}|}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{\phi}}
is a feature map
and the shock
\epsilon^{Y}
enters additively. A feature map is a fixed,
known transformation of the endogenous variables – for instance

\phi(p)=(p,p^{2})
for a quadratic price response, or a spline or
piecewise-linear basis – chosen by the analyst rather than estimated. Two points
about the assumptions in (
D.11
) are worth making
explicit. Treating
\phi
as known is what keeps the problem inside the linear
identification theory of the main text: once the features are fixed,

\phi(X_{\mathcal{X}})
is an ordinary (vector-valued) regressor and no
non-parametric estimation of the functional form is required. The requirement
that the shock

\epsilon^{Y}
enters (
D.11
) additively is the separability
condition of Appendix
D.2
: it is what lets the
coefficient
\beta
keep its causal reading as the (feature-transformed) average
derivative, rather than the heterogeneity-weighted average of (
D.10
).

We extend the conditional-IV condition to the feature-expanded setting by
replacing (CIV3) with the following relevance condition:

(CIV3
′
)

there exists a known feature map
\psi
such that

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathrm{cov}\!\left(\phi(X_{\mathcal{X}}),\,\psi(X_{\mathcal{I}})\mid X_{\mathcal{B}}\right)\right]

has full row rank, i.e., rank equal to
d_{\phi}
.

Assume that (CIV1), (CIV2), and (CIV3
′
) are satisfied, that the time series is
strictly stationary and ergodic, and that it has finite second moments. Then,
following the same arguments as in
Thams et al. (2024)
,
\beta
is the
unique vector satisfying

\mathrm{cov}\!\left(X_{Y}-b^{\top}\phi(X_{\mathcal{X}}),\,\psi(X_{\mathcal{I}})\mid X_{\mathcal{B}}\right)=0,

and

\hat{\beta}\coloneqq\arg\min_{b}\left\|\widehat{\mathrm{cov}}\!\left(X_{Y}-b^{\top}\phi(X_{\mathcal{X}}),\,\psi(X_{\mathcal{I}})\mid X_{\mathcal{B}}\right)\right\|_{F}

is a consistent estimator of
\beta
.
Operationally,

is the
conditional-IV estimator of Section
3
applied to the
transformed regressors
\phi(X_{\mathcal{X}})
and instruments

\psi(X_{\mathcal{I}})

– the choice of conditioning set, the nuisance-effect construction, and the
estimator-discrepancy diagnostic – carries over unchanged, now identifying the
feature-transformed effect
\beta

exactly those that enter the outcome equation additively and do not interact with
the shock; price-by-shock interactions of the form (

outside this class and return us to the general problem of

D.2

D.5
The Graphical View Remains Useful under Nonlinearity

Graphical IV validity alone does not in general
identify the causal effect in nonlinear equilibrium systems. Additional functional,
distributional, or support assumptions may be required

(e.g.,
Newey & Powell, 2003
;
Chesher, 2003
;
Matzkin, 2008
;
Imbens & Newey, 2009
;
Matzkin, 2015
;
Torgovitsky, 2015
)
. Nevertheless, the graphical part of the
identification argument remains useful:
Consider the
standard IV setting (instrument
Z
, endogenous regressor
X
, outcome
Y
) with
an additional
observed
confounder
V
(
V\to X
,
V\to Y
) and a
homogeneous first stage. Whether
V
must enter the conditioning set is governed
entirely by
f^{Y}
and the graph. If
Y
is linear in
X
– even with
V

modifying the slope,
Y=\beta(V)X+\dots
– the instrument induces variation
in
X
that is orthogonal to
V
and so averages over
V
automatically, and IV
identifies the ASF without conditioning on
V
. If instead
Y
is nonlinear in

X
with a
V
-dependent shape, e.g.,
Y=\beta X+\theta\,VX^{2}+\dots
, the
term
VX^{2}
couples the confounder to the regressor; ignoring
V
biases the
estimated ASF, and conditioning on
V
restores it. The contrast depends only on

f^{Y}
and the edges
V\to X
,
V\to Y
, not on the structural equations of
X
,

Z
, or
V
. The graph together with the form of
f^{Y}
thus still determines the
conditioning set that targets the ASF; what nonlinearity removes is the
automatic equivalence between a valid instrument and a single scalar effect, not
the graphical reasoning itself.

Appendix F
Microfoundations for the Dynamic Demand Models

In the main text we introduce the demand equations as single-equation dynamic models in the econometric tradition
(
Hendry, 1995
, chapter 7)
: the relevant intertemporal dependence is captured directly in one structural equation, and the equation is motivated by a behavioral mechanism.
This appendix makes the connection to optimizing behavior explicit by stating the assumptions under which each demand equation coincides with the decision rule of a utility-maximizing consumer.
Our analysis in the main text does not depend on these microfoundations: the identification results in Section
3
require only the structural equations as introduced in Section
2
.
Yet the derivation serves three purposes. First, it delivers sign restrictions on the coefficients of the structural equations. Second, it shows that consumers’ price expectations are an identification assumption: consumers must not use the instrument to forecast future prices.
Third, it separates the short-run price response we estimate from the response to a change in the price process itself.

, and

F.2
Model III

We now turn to Model III, the stockpiling model inspired by
Hendel & Nevo (2006)
.
We maintain the belief restriction (
F.5
) in the affine form (
F.7
) with
\bar{p}>0
, since the level to which prices are expected to revert is what determines whether the consumer stockpiles. Only the one-step-ahead forecast will enter below, unlike in Model I. Purchases are unrestricted, so the consumer reaches her preferred inventory in every period. This keeps the problem in closed form but leaves the inventory with no dynamics of its own, as we discuss at the end of the subsection.

Let the consumer value consumption
s_{t}
drawn from the inventory of energy services
K_{t}
. The consumer chooses purchases
d_{t}
and consumption
s_{t}
:

\max_{\{d_{t+j},s_{t+j}\}_{j\geq 0}}\quad\mathbb{E}_{t}\sum_{j\geq 0}\left[\eta^{j}\left((\alpha+u_{t+j})s_{t+j}-\frac{c}{2}s_{t+j}^{2}-p_{t+j}d_{t+j}-\frac{\kappa}{2}K_{t+j}^{2}\right)\right]

(F.9)

\text{s.t.}\quad K_{t}=(1-\delta^{K})\,K_{t-1}+\delta^{D}d_{t}-s_{t},

(F.10)

with
\alpha,c,\kappa>0
;
\delta^{K}\in(0,1)
is the depreciation rate of the inventory, and
\delta^{D}>0
the conversion rate of electricity to energy services.
We do not impose
K_{t}\geq 0
: in the linear-quadratic formulation (
F.9
),
K_{t}
measures how far the inventory sits above or below its usual level, around which holding costs are symmetric; the constant
K^{0}
in (
2.9
) is what centers it. Here, a negative
K_{t}
is, for instance, an electric vehicle charged less than usual. This is standard in linear-quadratic inventory models
(see
Ramey & West, 1999
)
.

To solve this problem we introduce the value function
V_{t}(K)
, the best expected discounted payoff achievable from period
t
onward given that the consumer enters
t
with inventory
K
; the time subscript carries the dependence on
p_{t}
and
u_{t}
, which the consumer takes as given. It satisfies

V_{t}(K_{t-1})=\max_{\{d_{t},s_{t}\}}\quad\left\{\underbrace{(\alpha+u_{t})s_{t}-\tfrac{c}{2}s_{t}^{2}-p_{t}d_{t}-\tfrac{\kappa}{2}K_{t}^{2}}_{\text{payoff at $t$}}+\underbrace{\eta\mathbb{E}_{t}[V_{t+1}(K_{t})]}_{\text{value of future payoffs}}\right\}.

(F.11)

Substituting the law of motion into (
F.11
) and writing
\mu_{t}\coloneqq\eta\mathbb{E}_{t}[V_{t+1}^{\prime}(K_{t})]
for the discounted marginal value of the inventory carried from
t
to

and

are

-p_{t}-\kappa K_{t}\delta^{D}+\mu_{t}\delta^{D}=0\qquad\text{and}\qquad(\alpha+u_{t})-cs_{t}+\kappa K_{t}-\mu_{t}=0.

(F.12)

Both conditions equate the net marginal value of the inventory,
\mu_{t}-\kappa K_{t}
, to a margin: the consumer buys until the marginal cost of adding to the inventory,
p_{t}/\delta^{D}
, equals it, and withdraws until the marginal utility of energy services,
\alpha+u_{t}-c\,s_{t}
, equals it. Substituting the first condition into the second gives optimal consumption

s_{t}^{*}=\tfrac{1}{c}\bigl(\alpha+u_{t}-p_{t}/\delta^{D}\bigr).

(F.13)

Differentiating (
F.11
) with respect to the state
K_{t-1}
and applying the envelope theorem,
V_{t}^{\prime}(K_{t-1})=(1-\delta^{K})(\mu_{t}-\kappa K_{t})=(1-\delta^{K})p_{t}/\delta^{D}
, using the first condition in (

V_{t+1}^{\prime}(K_{t})=(1-\delta^{K})p_{t+1}/\delta^{D}
and

\mu_{t}=\eta(1-\delta^{K})\frac{\mathbb{E}_{t}[p_{t+1}]}{\delta^{D}}.

(F.14)

Note that

does not depend on

Substituting (
F.14
), the law of motion, the belief rule (
F.7
) and

into the first condition in (

) and rearranging gives the consumer’s decision rule for demand,

\begin{split}d_{t}&=\frac{-1}{\kappa(\delta^{D})^{2}}\Bigl(p_{t}+\kappa\delta^{D}(1-\delta^{K})\,K_{t-1}-\tfrac{\kappa\delta^{D}}{c}\bigl(\alpha+u_{t}-p_{t}/\delta^{D}\bigr)\\
&\hskip 72.00008pt-\eta(1-\delta^{K})\bigl((1-\rho)\,\bar{p}+\rho\,p_{t}\bigr)\Bigr)\\
&=\frac{\alpha}{c\delta^{D}}+\frac{\eta(1-\delta^{K})(1-\rho)\,\bar{p}}{\kappa(\delta^{D})^{2}}-\frac{1+\kappa/c-\eta(1-\delta^{K})\rho}{\kappa(\delta^{D})^{2}}\,p_{t}\\
&\hskip 72.00008pt-\frac{1-\delta^{K}}{\delta^{D}}\,K_{t-1}+\frac{u_{t}}{c\delta^{D}},\end{split}

(F.15)

which maps to (

) as

\displaystyle D^{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{c\delta^{D}}+\frac{\eta(1-\delta^{K})(1-\rho)\,\bar{p}}{\kappa(\delta^{D})^{2}},

\displaystyle\qquad\beta^{P}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1+\kappa/c-\eta(1-\delta^{K})\rho}{\kappa(\delta^{D})^{2}},

\displaystyle\beta^{K}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1-\delta^{K}}{\delta^{D}},

\displaystyle\qquad U_{t}^{D}

\displaystyle=\frac{u_{t}}{c\delta^{D}}.

The mapping delivers the sign restrictions the main text assumes and decomposes each coefficient into primitives:

•

Sign of the price response.
Since
\eta(1-\delta^{K})\rho<1
, the numerator of
\beta^{P}
exceeds
\kappa/c>0
, so
\beta^{P}<0
. In this model
\beta^{P}
bundles preferences (
c
), technology (
\kappa,\delta^{D},\delta^{K}
), beliefs (
\rho
), and discounting (
\eta
), but it still has the interpretation of the short-run demand response to prices.

•

Sign of the inventory coefficient. The parameter
\beta^{K}<0
is purely technological, driven by the conversion and depreciation rates of the inventory.

•

Anticipation. The term
\eta(1-\delta^{K})\rho
makes
|\beta^{P}|
smaller when prices are persistent: a high price today signals a high price tomorrow, weakening the incentive to respond to prices (e.g., to postpone purchases). The sign is opposite to that of Model I, where anticipation raises
|\beta^{P}|
by (
F.8
).

•

Stockpiling. Combining (
F.12
) with (
F.14
) gives the inventory the consumer chooses to carry,

K_{t}=\frac{\eta(1-\delta^{K})(1-\rho)\,\bar{p}-\bigl(1-\eta(1-\delta^{K})\rho\bigr)p_{t}}{\kappa\,\delta^{D}},

(F.16)

which is positive if and only if
p_{t}<\eta(1-\delta^{K})(1-\rho)\bar{p}/\bigl(1-\eta(1-\delta^{K})\rho\bigr)
, a threshold that approaches
\bar{p}
as
\eta\to 1
and
\delta^{K}\to 0
. The consumer builds up the inventory when the current price is low relative to what she expects and runs it down when it is high. More precisely, carrying an inventory forward is worthwhile only when the discounted, depreciated price she expects to pay next hour exceeds the price she pays now. At
\eta=0
the threshold is zero, so a myopic consumer never stockpiles; but given the hourly setup,
\eta
is close to one.
K_{t}
does not depend on
K_{t-1}
: the inherited inventory changes how much she must buy, not the level she wants to hold.

•

Consumption from the inventory. Optimal consumption
s_{t}^{*}
is not constant but varies with
p_{t}
and
u_{t}
; the law of motion in the main text (
2.9
) replaces it by its mean
K^{0}=\mathbb{E}[s_{t}^{*}]
.

•

Short- and long-run responses. The same three objects separate here. Under the main text’s law of motion (
2.9
) the long-run response is
\delta^{K}\beta^{P}
; under (
F.10
), which keeps
s^{*}_{t}
, it is larger in absolute value by
(1-\delta^{K})/(c(\delta^{D})^{2})
, since a permanently higher price also lowers consumption drawn from the inventory. A change in the price process itself could change
\rho
, and with it
\beta^{P}
, or
\bar{p}
, and with it

.

•

Inventory dynamics. The linear-quadratic problem reproduces the demand equation (
2.7
), its sign restrictions and its decomposition into primitives, but not the inventory dynamics of Model III. Because the value function is affine, the inventory the consumer carries (
F.16
) is affine and strictly decreasing in the current price, so along the optimal path
K_{t-1}
is redundant given
p_{t-1}
and (
F.15
) reduces to a distributed lag in current and lagged prices. A cost of adjusting purchases, or the non-negativity constraint that leads
Hendel & Nevo (2006)
to solve their model numerically, would make the value function quadratic and
K_{t-1}
a genuine state, at the cost of coefficients that no longer have closed form.
