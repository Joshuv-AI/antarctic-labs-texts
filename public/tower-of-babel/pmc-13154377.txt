Short‐Term Effect of Ambient Air Pollution on Daily Mortality in Three Jordanian Cities: A Time‐Series Study
Authors: Murad S. Ramadan, Fayez A. Abdulla, Wael Al‐Delaimy, Aurelio Tobías, Massimo Stafoggia, Heba Safi, Mazen Malkawi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study involved a multi‐city time‐series analysis (2015–2019) of daily all‐cause (non‐accidental) mortality and particulate matter with an aerodynamic diameter less than 10 μm (PM10), nitrogen dioxide (NO2), and sulfur dioxide (SO2) in Jordan's three largest cities (Amman, Irbid, Zarqa). City‐specific quasi‐Poisson models estimated percentage increase of risk per 10‐unit increase across single‐day lags (0–6 days) and averaged windows as lag (0–1), lag (2–5), and lag (0–5). Multi‐pollutant models adjusted the primary pollutant for co‐pollutants at lag (0–1). PM10 means were 68.9, 49.0, and 78.7 μg/m3 in Amman, Irbid, and Zarqa, respectively. Amman's average concentration was close to the national annual limit of 70 μg/m3 set by Jordanian air quality standards (JS 1140/2006), while Zarqa's mean level exceeded it. In Amman, PM10 was positively associated with mortality (lag (0–1): 0.59% [0.22–0.96]; lag (0–5): 0.98% [0.40–1.57]); Irbid and Zarqa showed no consistent PM10 associations, and NO2/SO2 were generally null across cities. Adjustment for NO2 and SO2 did not attenuate Amman's PM10 effect (0.60%–1.19%). Stratified analyses indicated short‐lag PM10 and mortality associations were significant among males and older adults in Amman, and cumulative PM10 associations among females in Zarqa. This first national time‐series study for Jordan identifies an acute mortality risk from particulate pollution in Amman and suggests inter‐city heterogeneity, likely driven by differences in particle composition, sources, exposure, and standards exceedances. These findings support particulate‐focused control measures and enhanced risk communication for vulnerable groups.

Introduction

Air pollution is widely recognized as one of the leading public health risks worldwide (Mao et al., 2024). According to the World Health Organization (WHO), air pollution is responsible for an estimated 7 million annual premature deaths, which makes it the single largest environmental health risk globally (WHO, 2019). The Lancet Commission on Pollution and Health conducted a comprehensive analysis that reinforced this alarmingly high burden and estimated that pollution (in all forms) accounted for one in six deaths worldwide in 2015 (Fuller et al., 2022). Moreover, their analysis revealed that ambient air pollution was responsible for a large portion of that toll, with 4.2–4.5 million annual deaths. Air pollution‐related mortality has broad causes, including ischemic heart disease, stroke, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, lung cancer, and acute respiratory infections (WHO, 2021).

In the Eastern Mediterranean Region (EMR), alarming statistics reveal that approximately 90% of the population is exposed to air pollution levels that surpass the guideline limits set by WHO, leading to an estimated annual toll of 500,000 premature deaths (Malkawi et al., 2021). Satellite and ground‐based data show that the EMR region has the highest particulate concentrations of all WHO regions (WHO EMRO, 2014). In the Middle East, it was estimated that there is an excess of 745 deaths per 100,000 people each year from air pollution exposure, which makes air pollution exposure risk on par or exceeding well known risks such as high cholesterol or tobacco smoking (Osipov et al., 2022). Although many EMR countries are classified as low‐middle‐income countries, where WHO reports the highest burden of air pollution‐related deaths in 2019 (WHO, 2019), the focus of research and meta‐analyses are often directed toward high‐income countries (Lu et al., 2015; Vodonos et al., 2018; West et al., 2016). In conclusion, this region is navigating a dilemma between high air pollution exposure and limited research attention; however, it has recently started to gain more academic focus (Khader, 2020).

Jordan exemplifies these regional air quality challenges. Ambient air pollution is a growing concern given the rapid urbanization and increasing energy demands (Cimate Action, 2016). Air pollution public health impact assessment has been difficult due to gaps in national monitoring program (Ahmad et al., 2019). Jordan's Ministry of Environment did establish an air quality network around 2014. It focuses on regulated pollutants like PM10 (Particulate Matter with aerodynamic diameter less than 10 μm), Nitrogen dioxide (NO2) and Sulfur dioxide (SO2), which leaves fine particulate matter (PM2.5), the most health relevant pollutant, unmonitored until 2021. This situation isn't unique in the region, Kuwaiti authorities historically struggled to evaluate pollution health effects because of limited monitoring networks and lack of historical air quality data (Alahmad et al., 2023). Consequently, epidemiological evidence on air pollution and health in Jordan has been scarce, which forced policymakers to rely on risk estimates of studies from other regions (Faridi et al., 2023). This raises concerns, because factors like climate, population characteristics, and pollution composition probably will differ between countries. Experts have stated that underserved populations disproportionately affected by air pollution are not sufficiently represented in current health impact studies (Vilcassim & Thurston, 2023). Therefore, managing Jordan's public health risks requires generating local evidence.

The present study addresses this gap by investigating short‐term associations between ambient PM10, NO2, and SO2 and all‐cause (non‐accidental) mortality across Jordan's three largest cities, Amman, Irbid, and Zarqa, which together capture the country's principal urban, traffic, and industrial environments. Specifically, it aims to examine variations in pollution‐mortality relationships across demographic groups, explore lag structures of pollutant effects, and assess the influence of co‐pollutant adjustment on these associations.

Data and Methods

Population and Study Area

This study was conducted in Jordan's major governorates, Amman, Irbid, and Zarqa, over a 5‐year period from 1st of January 2015, to 31st of December 2019. Jordan has an arid to semi‐arid climate, with a population around 9.5 million in 2015 (Department of Statistics, 2022). The governorates of interest are the most populous in the country, together accounting for about 75% of the total population; Amman had about 4 million inhabitants, while Irbid and Zarqa had roughly 1.8 and 1.4 million, respectively.

Data Sources

Ambient Temperature Data

Air temperature at 2‐m height from the surface was retrieved from European Centre for Medium‐Range Weather Forecasts CAMS ERA5 reanalysis data set (Hersbach et al., 2020). This data set provides hourly temperature estimates on a 0.25° × 0.25° grid, it was temporally averaged to be daily. Data for each governorate was clipped to its administrative boundaries to yield a representative daily mean temperature for Amman, Irbid, and Zarqa. The resulting temperature time series were complete with no missing days.

Air Quality Data

Daily ambient air pollution data for PM10, NO2, and SO2 were acquired from the Jordanian Ministry of Environment (MoEnv). Figure 1 shows air quality stations' locations, names, and coordinates. Within the three Governorates, a total of 12 monitoring stations were active during 2015–2019 (seven in Amman, two in Irbid, and three in Zarqa), and each station measured all three pollutants. PM10 is measured using Beta attenuation, while for NO2 and SO2 Chemiluminescence and UV‐Fluorescence are used, respectively. Any station that had less than 75% data completeness over the study period was omitted from the analysis to ensure reliability of city averages. When a station had missing values for a given day, they were imputed using the mean of the other stations for that day, adjusted by a scaling factor equal to the ratio of the annual mean of the missing station to the annual mean of the remaining stations (Katsouyanni et al., 2001). If data from all stations were missing on a given day, the daily value was left as missing. Daily city‐level average concentration for each pollutant was derived by averaging measurements across all valid stations in the city, resulting values represent the average daily PM10, NO2, and SO2 levels for each city.

Air quality stations' locations map.

Mortality Data

Daily mortality data for all natural causes in Amman, Irbid, and Zarqa were obtained from the Ministry of Interior (MoI) of Jordan for the period 2015–2019. The data set included information on sex, age, date of birth, date of death, and city of residency, and was used to derive aggregated daily counts of non‐accidental deaths happened at age greater than 30 years (excluding external causes such as injuries and accidents). Data were stratified by sex (female and male) and by age (<65 and ≥65 years). Only anonymized, aggregated data were analyzed; no identifiable individual‐level information was used, and therefore, ethical approval was not required.

Statistical Analysis

Time‐series regression models were applied to investigate the short‐term associations between daily air pollutant concentrations and daily non‐accidental mortality in Amman, Irbid, and Zarqa. Analyses were carried out using generalized linear models (GLMs) with a quasi‐Poisson distribution to account for overdispersion in daily mortality counts (Bhaskaran et al., 2013). Long‐term and seasonal trends were controlled for by including a natural cubic spline of calendar time. The appropriate degrees of freedom (df) per year for the time spline were determined through sensitivity analyses based on quasi‐Akaike information criterion (qAIC), Ljung‐Box test for residual autocorrelation, and the stability of pollutant coefficients across different df specifications (Bhaskaran et al., 2013). The final df per year was chosen as the most parsimonious option balancing model fit and residual diagnostics.

Temperature was included as the only meteorological confounder. Instead of using one term to control for temperature, two natural splines were used to account for cold and hot temperatures (Stafoggia et al., 2013). A natural cubic spline for cold temperatures across lag 1–6 days and one internal knot at the 25th percentile of the temperature distribution was added to the model. For hot temperatures, a natural spline and a shorter lag of 0–1 day was used, with two internal knots at the 75th and 90th percentiles of the temperature distribution for hot days. By separating temperature into cold and hot components, the model will account for temperature extremes on mortality count. Models were also adjusted for day of the week.

The lagged effect of air pollution on mortality was examined, assuming that exposure on previous days can influence current mortality. The lag structures that were examined are single day lags (lag 0 up to lag 6) and average lags (immediate: lag 0–1, delayed: lag 2–5, prolonged: lag 0–5). As a sensitivity assessment, the minimum detectable effect (MDE) per 10‐unit increase for each pollutant was computed in each city, which represent the smallest effect that could be reliably detected given the city‐specific mortality counts and exposure variability (Bloom, 1995; Mair et al., 2020). To evaluate whether the assumption of a linear exposure–response was appropriate, sensitivity analysis was conducted by refitting all pollution models using penalized spline terms for PM10, NO2, and SO2 (natural cubic splines with 3 degrees of freedom). Spline‐based models were compared with their linear counterparts using quasi‐likelihood F‐tests. If the F‐test p‐value is greater than 0.05, then it can be concluded that there is no statistical evidence that non‐linearity fits the data better than linear model, thus non‐linearity is rejected. To reduce the influence of collinearity and assess independent effects, multi‐pollutant models were built using the multiday lag with the lowest qAIC value. The same single‐pollutant model structures were applied for multi‐pollutant analysis, but with additional adjustment for co‐pollutant(s). City‐specific Pearson correlation matrices were computed to assess pollutant co‐variation and inform multi‐pollutant model interpretation.

Lastly, a separate stratified analysis was conducted for subgroups to explore the vulnerable populations, focusing on sex (male/female) and age (<65/≥65). Estimates are presented as percentage increased risks (i.e., IR = (RR‐1) × 100) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) per 10‐unit increase in pollutant concentrations.

Data assimilation, preprocessing, and visualization were performed in Python (version 3.8.19). Statistical analyses were conducted in R (version 4.3.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria, https://cran.r‐project.org/) using the “mgcv” (mixed GAM computation vehicle) package.

Results

All three cities used 4 df/year for the calendar time spline, selected based on sensitivity analyses using qAIC, Ljung‐Box tests, and coefficient stability as shown in (Figures S1–S3 of Supporting Information S1). Table 1 shows the descriptive statistics of the input variables throughout the study area and period. 46,071 mortality cases were analyzed in this study. From 2015 to 2019, daily mortality averaged 15.8 (SD 4.6) deaths/day in Amman (total 28,924), 4.1 (2.1) in Irbid (7,414), and 5.3 (2.4) in Zarqa (9,733). PM10 averaged 68.9 μg/m3 in Amman (near the 70 μg/m3 annual Jordanian standards (JS 1140/2006) limit, with peaks well above the 120 μg/m3 24‐h cap), 49.0 μg/m3 in Irbid (generally below the annual limit, though episodically >120 μg/m3), and 78.7 μg/m3 in Zarqa (exceeding the annual limit with frequent potential 24‐h exceedances). NO2 means were 22.7, 18.3, and 18.5 ppb (Amman/Irbid/Zarqa), and SO2 means 8.7, 8.9, and 8.2 ppb, respectively—each city well below JS annual (50 ppb NO2, 40 ppb SO2) and 24‐h (80 ppb NO2, 140 ppb SO2) standards. Seasonal variation and time‐series plots are present in Figures S4 and S5 of Supporting Information S1. Overall, particulate pollution burden was highest and most persistent in Zarqa, near‐threshold with pronounced peaks in Amman, and comparatively lower in Irbid, while gaseous pollutants remained within national limits across cities.

Figure 2 shows the effects of air pollutants on total mortality in single‐pollutant models. Across Amman, Irbid, and Zarqa (2015–2019), time‐series GLMs indicated a small but statistically robust short‐lag association between PM10 and total mortality only in Amman (per 10 μg/m3 increase: lag (0–1) (0.59%, 0.22–0.96), lag (2–5) (0.48%, 0.00–0.96), lag (0–5) (0.98%, 0.40–1.57). In Irbid and Zarqa, PM10 estimates were small and did not reach statistical significance across examined single‐day and average lags. Spline‐based exposure–response models did not materially improve model fit relative to linear specifications. Across all pollutants and cities, quasi‐likelihood F‐tests did not indicate statistically significant departures from linearity (Table S1 in Supporting Information S1). These results suggest no strong evidence of non‐linearity, supporting the use of linear pollutant terms in the main models. To contextualize statistical power across cities, we computed the MDE for each pollutant and lag window. Amman exhibited the smallest MDEs (≈0.05%), indicating high power to detect even modest associations. Irbid and Zarqa required larger true effects (>0.15% and >0.1%, respectively) to reach statistical significance. These results are presented in Tables S2–S4 of Supporting Information S1. For NO2 and SO2, city‐specific estimates fluctuated around the null and were not statistically significant across lags or cumulative windows. Overall, the mortality signal is confined to short‐lag PM10 in Amman, with no consistent evidence for associations in the other cities or for NO2/SO2.

Stratified Analysis

In sex‐stratified analyses shown in Figure 4, statistically significant positive associations were confined to specific city–pollutant–lag combinations. In Amman, PM10 among males was associated with total mortality at lag (0–1) (0.73%, 0.25–1.21), and lag (0–5) (1.14%, 0.38–1.91). In Zarqa, PM10 among females was significant at lag (2–5) (2.12%, 0.53–3.73), and lag (0–5) (2.56%, 0.62–4.54). No other sex–pollutant–cumulative lag combinations yielded statistically significant effects.

In age‐stratified analyses, shown in Figure 3, statistically significant associations were limited to a few city–pollutant–age–lag combinations. In Amman, among the older (≥65) group, PM10 was associated with total mortality at lag (0–1) (0.59%, 0.16–1.02); no significant associations were observed for younger (<65) residents or for NO2/SO2. In Irbid, among the younger (<65 y) group, NO2 showed an immediate association at lag (0) (9.26%, 0.74–18.50). In Zarqa, no significant associations were observed among either younger or older groups. No other age–pollutant–cumulative lag combinations yielded statistically significant effects.

Multi‐Pollutant

The cumulative lag 0–5 was selected as the primary exposure window for multi‐pollutant analysis, given that it provided the lowest qAIC for all pollutant–city combinations. City‐specific correlations among pollutants were generally modest (Figure S6 in Supporting Information S1). In Amman, PM10 showed weak correlation with NO2 (r = 0.19) and moderate correlation with SO2 (r = 0.45). Table 2 shows multi‐pollutants analysis results. In Irbid, PM10 was moderately correlated with NO2 (r = 0.36) and weakly with SO2 (r = 0.24). In Zarqa, PM10 was essentially uncorrelated with both NO2 (r = 0.08) and SO2 (r = 0.02), while NO2 and SO2 displayed a strong correlation (r = 0.72). Overall, these patterns indicate limited multicollinearity affecting PM10 estimates, with the exception of a strong NO2 – SO2 relationship in Zarqa. Consequently, multi‐pollutant adjustment resulted in modest shifts that did not change the overall interpretation of city‐specific effects. In Amman, the PM10 estimate remained consistently positive, increasing slightly from 0.98% in the single‐pollutant model to 1.07% with NO2 adjustment and to 1.19% when SO2 or NO2 + SO2 were included, indicating that the PM10 association is robust and not explained by co‐pollutant confounding. By contrast, NO2 and SO2 estimates in Amman were unstable and imprecise (e.g., SO2 rising to 0.70% with NO2 adjustment but with wide confidence intervals). In Irbid, PM10 shifted between 0.72% and 1.19% across models, and NO2 attenuated from 4.47% to as low as 0.24%, reflecting imprecision and shared variability rather than clear pollutant‐specific effects. In Zarqa, PM10 remained null across all specifications (−0.43% to −0.22%), while NO2 and SO2 fluctuated widely (e.g., NO2 from −1.48% to −5.88%) with no consistent pattern. Overall, the multi‐pollutant models reinforce a robust PM10 association only in Amman, with mixed and imprecise estimates for NO2 and SO2 and persistent null findings in Irbid and Zarqa.

Discussion

This study provides the first Jordan‐based time‐series evidence linking short‐term changes in ambient air pollution with daily mortality. A modest but precise PM10 effect was identified only in Amman (+0.47% per 10 μg/m3 at lag 1; slightly larger for lag 0–1), while Irbid and Zarqa showed no consistent associations, and NO2 and SO2 effects were generally null. Using multi‐year mortality and air‐quality data with harmonized models that account for seasonality and temperature, the analysis produced robust, city‐specific estimates. The findings are consistent with international evidence (Liu et al., 2019; Orellano et al., 2020) and regional studies (Amini et al., 2019), highlighting particulate matter as the dominant driver of acute mortality in this context. By addressing the evidence gap emphasized by WHO/EMRO (Tobías et al., 2025), this work offers a foundation for evaluating Jordan's Ambient Air Quality Standards (JS 1140/2006) and informs targeted strategies to reduce particulate exposure during high‐pollution events.

Differences across cities likely reflect pollution composition and sources (traffic/urban mix in Amman vs. dust/industry in Zarqa and more natural dust in Irbid), exposure measurement (denser monitoring and larger population, hence greater power, in Amman), and standards exceedances (Amman near, and Zarqa above, the PM10 annual limit under JS 1140/2006, with episodic 24‐h exceedances). Differences in MDE sizes help explain the inter‐city heterogeneity. Amman had small MDEs and thus sufficient power to detect modest PM10–mortality associations. In contrast, Irbid and especially Zarqa had larger MDEs, indicating that only relatively large effects could be detected. Hence, the absence of significant associations in these cities is compatible with limited statistical power rather than evidence of no effect. Regional commentary suggests differences in health risks may arise depending on the composition and sources of particulate matter, such as dust versus combustion‐related particles (Tobías et al., 2025). This perspective aligns with the observed city‐level contrasts, where Zarqa exhibited the highest mean PM10 concentration (78.7 μg/m3) but no detectable acute mortality association, while Amman (68.9 μg/m3) showed a significant association. These differences may reflect variation in source contributions, including a relatively greater influence of traffic‐related emissions in Amman. Although the literature on dust episodes reports short‐term impacts on health services in Mediterranean settings (Middleton et al., 2008), the extent to which such effects translate into acute mortality outcomes likely depends on particle characteristics and context. Although stratified findings varied, they generally pointed to greater vulnerability among older adults, in line with broader evidence (Wang et al., 2023), with some sex‐specific heterogeneity across cities. Overall, these results support prioritizing particulate control, especially in Amman, while recognizing that sustained PM burdens in Zarqa raise chronic health concerns even when short‐term mortality signals are not detected.

Pollutant correlations were generally modest across cities, reflecting differing emission profiles. In Amman, PM10 showed weak‐to‐moderate correlations with NO2 and SO2 (r = 0.19–0.45), while in Irbid PM10 was moderately correlated with NO2 (r = 0.36) and weakly with SO2. In Zarqa, PM10 was essentially uncorrelated with both gases (r < 0.10), whereas NO2 and SO2 were strongly correlated (r = 0.72), consistent with industrial co‐emissions. The weak PM10–SO2 relationship in Amman also explains the increase in the PM10 estimate after SO2 adjustment, reflecting negative confounding rather than multicollinearity. Overall, these patterns indicate minimal collinearity affecting PM10 estimates and support the robustness of the multi‐pollutant models.

Our findings imply that reducing particulate pollution would yield measurable health benefits in Jordan's cities. Even a small short‐term risk increment (∼0.5% higher daily mortality per 10 μg/m3 PM10 in Amman) translates to non‐trivial numbers at the population scale; applied to Amman's ∼5,800 non‐accidental deaths/year, a sustained 10 μg/m3 reduction could avert on the order of a few dozen deaths annually. Policy implications include aligning national standards and practice with contemporary evidence. Jordan's JS 1140/2006 limits for PM10 (70 μg/m3 annual; 120 μg/m3 24‐h) are less stringent than the 2021 WHO guideline values, and Amman (near the annual limit) and Zarqa (above it) experienced episodic 24‐h exceedances; tightening particulate standards is therefore warranted. Priority measures should target PM sources (traffic and road dust, construction, and industrial emissions), complemented by public advisories during dust/storm episodes—an approach echoed in Eastern Mediterranean commentary on risk communication (Tobías et al., 2025).

Beyond differences in exposure levels and particulate composition, variation in healthcare access may also contribute to the observed heterogeneity between cities. Amman hosts the largest concentration of tertiary and specialized hospitals in Jordan, with greater availability of emergency and intensive care services, whereas Irbid and Zarqa rely more heavily on a smaller number of public and regional hospitals. Differences in proximity to acute care, emergency response capacity, and crowding in public facilities could influence short‐term survival following pollution‐related cardiovascular or respiratory events, potentially modifying observed pollution–mortality associations. Although we did not directly quantify healthcare infrastructure or access in this study, these contextual factors are important to consider when interpreting inter‐city differences and warrant dedicated investigation in future work.

This study shares the typical limitations inherent to time‐series air pollution research. City‐wide averages from a limited number of monitors can misclassify individual exposure, especially in Irbid and Zarqa, despite reasonable imputation, and we only observed PM10, NO2, and SO2 (unmeasured PM2.5 and ozone could confound or share effects). By design, we estimate acute (short‐term) risks, not long‐term impacts; because results were limited to adults aged ≥30 years and cause‐of‐death information was unavailable, the findings may not be generalizable to younger populations or deaths from external causes. Model choices (splines for time/temperature, lag windows) follow standard practice in the field (Dominici et al., 2002; Gasparrinia et al., 2010), yet residual seasonality, socio‐temporal events, or incomplete control of autocorrelation could modestly influence estimates. As with any observational design, associations cannot prove causality; some mortality displacement (“harvesting”) is possible.

A further limitation is that we lacked detailed data on healthcare infrastructure, emergency care capacity, and individual‐level access to treatment across cities. Our discussion of healthcare system differences as a possible contributor to inter‐city heterogeneity is, therefore, speculative and should be interpreted as a hypothesis‐generating perspective rather than a causal explanation.

Scope and context also matter. The three largest cities may not represent smaller or rural settings in Jordan's arid, dust‐influenced climate; nevertheless, our estimates broadly cohere with international evidence. The study period (2015–2019) predates COVID‐19, so findings serve as a baseline unaffected by lockdown‐era anomalies. Future work in Jordan and the Eastern Mediterranean should expand pollutant coverage (especially PM2.5 and ozone), refine exposure with denser networks or high‐resolution models, analyze cause‐specific mortality, and complement time‐series designs with cohort studies. Multi‐city collaborations in the region, explicitly encouraged by Tobías et al. (2025), would improve precision and comparability, helping to confirm and refine the patterns we report.

Conclusion

In this first national time‐series study from Jordan, we found a small but robust association between short‐term increases in PM10 and all‐cause mortality in Amman—most evident at same/next‐day lags and persisting in multi‐pollutant models—while Irbid and Zarqa showed no consistent PM10 effects, and NO2/SO2 were generally null across cities. The inter‐city heterogeneity likely reflects differences in particle composition and sources, exposure measurement and statistical power, and the frequency of exceedances of national standards. Stratified analyses indicated greater susceptibility among older adults, with some sex‐specific patterns across cities. These results support prioritizing particulate‐focused controls (traffic/road dust, construction, and industrial emissions), strengthening compliance with, and reconsideration of the current standards, and issuing targeted health advisories during high‐PM events. Continued investment in monitoring, refined exposure assessment, and cause‐specific analyses will help clarify mechanisms and guide interventions. Overall, our Jordan‐specific risk estimates provide an evidence base for health impact assessment and practical air‐quality actions, particularly in Amman, to reduce avoidable deaths.
