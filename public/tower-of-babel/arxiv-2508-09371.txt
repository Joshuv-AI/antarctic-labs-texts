Discovery of energy landscapes towards optimized quantum transport: Environmental effects and long-range tunneling
Maggie Lawrence, Matthew Pocrnic, Erin Fung, Juan Carrasquilla, Erik M. Gauger, Dvira Segal

Abstract
Carrier transport in quantum networks is governed by a variety of factors, including network dimensionality and connectivity, on-site energies, couplings between sites and whether they are short- or long-range, and environmental effects. In this work, we identify classes of quasi one-dimensional chains with energy profiles that optimize carrier transport under such influences. Specifically, we optimize on-site energies using Optax’s optimistic gradient descent and AdaMax algorithms, enabled by the JAX automatic differentiation framework. Focusing on steady-state transport, we study the system’s behavior under combined unitary and nonunitary (dephasing and dissipative) effects using the Lindblad quantum master equation. After validating our optimization scheme on short chains, we extend the study to larger systems where we identify systematic patterns in energy profiles. Our analysis reveals that different types of energy landscape enhance transport, depending on whether inter-site tunneling couplings in the chain are short- or long-range, the existence of environmental interactions, and the temperature of the environment. Our classification and insights of optimal energy landscapes offer guidance for designing efficient transport systems for electronic, photovoltaic and quantum communication applications.

Specifically, we focus on quasi one-dimensional tight-binding chains and consider the transfer of a single carrier from an entry site to a designated collection site. Our work offers guidelines for the type of configuration that optimizes transfer efficiency. Moreover, we gain insights into what makes certain structures optimal and what the underlying transport mechanisms are in different parameter regimes.

II
Model and Dynamics

We focus on quantum transport in quasi-one-dimensional systems that are coupled to local environments (see Fig.
1
). In order to focus on the essence of the problem, we make it more tractable by introducing several simplifying assumptions.
First, we consider the presence of only a single carrier within the quantum system, physically corresponding to a single charge or excitation.
Second, we include the environment in a phenomenological manner, using Lindblad dissipators
Breuer and Petruccione 2007
. The advantage of this description is that it applies to different environments, such as from
intra- or inter-molecular vibrations
Sarkar and Dubi 2020
;
Juhász and Árpád I. Csurgay 2018
, phonons
Eastham et al. 2013
;
Jäger et al. 2022
, photons
Giri and Schatz 2025
or electrons
Rosati et al. 2014
.
We refer to our models as “quasi-one-dimensional” since we test both short-range and long-range tunneling-coupling scenarios, where in the latter case there is essentially an all-to-all connectivity in the system. In what follows, we work with units of
\hbar\equiv 1
and
k_{B}\equiv 1
.

In the site-local basis, the Hamiltonian of the chain is given by a tight-binding model,

\hat{H}_{S}=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\varepsilon_{n}\ket{n}\bra{n}+\sum_{n\neq m}J_{|n-m|}\ket{n}\bra{m}.

(1)

Here,
\varepsilon_{n}
is the energy of site
n
, and
J_{|n-m|}
is the tunneling energy between site
m
to site
n
.
For simplicity, these tunnelings are chosen to be real and positive.
We use a power-law function to describe the tunneling energies,

J_{|n-m|}=\frac{J_{max}}{|n-m|^{\alpha}},

(2)

with
J_{max}
as the magnitude of the nearest-neighbors tunneling. In simulations, we use
\alpha=1
to represent long-range coupling, while
\alpha=3
supports shorter-range tunnelings. We often refer here to the two tunneling models as the long-range tunneling model and the short-range tunneling model, referring to the magnitude of tunneling elements beyond nearest neighbors.

The carriers interact with environments— typically phonons
Zerah-Harush and Dubi 2018
;
Zerah-Harush and Dubi 2020
, photons
Coates et al. 2023
;
Wu et al. 2024
, local impurities
Manninen et al. 2017
, and other carriers
Rosati et al. 2014
.
Microscopically, an environment can be captured in the harmonic approximation by a collection of harmonic modes of frequency
\omega_{k}
,

\hat{H}_{B}=\sum_{k}\omega_{k}\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{k}\hat{b}_{k}.

(3)

Here,
\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{k}
and
\hat{b}_{k}
are the creation and annihilation operators for the boson mode of the bath. The interaction between the system and bath is typically given in a bipartite form,

\hat{H}_{I}=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\hat{L}_{n}\sum_{k}g_{nk}\otimes\left(\hat{b}_{k}^{\dagger}+\hat{b}_{k}\right).

(4)

Here,
g_{nk}
, assumed real valued without loss of generality, are the coupling energies between the
k^{\text{th}}
bath mode and a local operator of the system on site
n
,
\hat{L}_{n}
.

We consider two models for system-bath couplings and specify the operators of the system that couple to the baths,
\hat{L}_{n}
. The first case, OQS Model I, concerns local dephasing effects, as described by trapped-ion experiments
Maier et al. 2019
.
In OQS Model II, a finite temperature environment couples to a local charge density, a scenario relevant for electron-phonon coupled systems
Fruchtman et al. 2016
.

II.1
OQS Model I

We assume that the environment acts locally and in an uncorrelated manner on each site to dephase the state. The local Lindblad QME is given by

\dot{\rho}=-i\left[\hat{H}_{S},\rho\right]+\sum_{n=1}^{N}\Gamma_{n}\left(\hat{L}_{n}\rho\hat{L}_{n}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\left\{\hat{L}_{n}^{\dagger}\hat{L}_{n},\rho\right\}\right).

(5)

Here, the Lindblad jump operators are given by
\hat{L}_{n}=\ket{n}\bra{n}
,
\Gamma_{n}
are the dephasing rate constants, and the dynamics evolve in the site-local basis.
In this case, the microscopic information from Eqs. (
3
) and (
4
) is not explicitly used, but one can associate
\Gamma_{n}
with a microscopic model of the bath
Coates et al. 2021
. It should be noted that in the energy basis, the local dephasing model corresponds to an infinite temperature bath
Turkeshi and Schiró 2021
.

We assume that all sites suffer the same dephasing rate,
\Gamma_{n}\equiv\Gamma
.
Regarding the range of values for
\Gamma
: theory, computations, and experiments
Fassioli et al. 2014
;
Maier et al. 2019
;
Blach et al. 2025
point out that environmental noise-assisted quantum transport is most pronounced when
\Gamma/J_{max}\sim 1
.
As such, we work in this regime and set these parameters to be comparable.

We comment that it is common to employ the Lindblad QME in the site basis and with local dephasing effects in order to capture local dynamical noise. Examples include recent studies on relaxation timescales in Lindbladian dynamics
Popkov and Presilla 2021
;
Zhou et al. 2021
, transport in networks
Catalano et al. 2023
, and quantum simulations on quantum hardware
Guimarães et al. 2024
.
In such studies, the dephasing rates are taken over a broad range of values compared to the system energy scale.

II.2
OQS Model II

In OQS Model II, the environment still acts locally and independently on each site, but we consider a finite-temperature bath and solve the problem in the energy basis of the system. That is, we employ the global Lindblad QME (also referred to as the exciton-basis QME in the context of exciton transport). In the global basis, transition rates between different eigenstates of the system depend
on the temperature of the environment and the energy difference between those states.
We write down the global Lindblad QME as

\dot{\rho}=-i\left[\hat{H}_{S},\rho\right]+\sum_{a\neq b}W_{ab}\left(\hat{F}_{ab}\rho\hat{F}_{ab}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\left\{\hat{F}_{ab}^{\dagger}\hat{F}_{ab},\rho\right\}\right).

(6)

Here, the
\hat{F}_{ab}
jump operators describe transitions between eigenstates
a
and
b
of the system’s Hamiltonian,

\hat{F}_{ab}=\ket{\phi_a}\bra{\phi_b}
,
where
\ket{\phi_a}
is the
a^{th}
eigenstate of
\hat{H}_{S}
, with eigenenergy
\omega_{a}
.
The transition rates
W_{ab}
in Eq. (
6
) are given by
Coates et al. 2023

W_{ab}=\sum_{n=1}^{N}S_{ab}\bra{\phi_a}\hat{L}_{n}\ket{\phi_b}\bra{\phi_b}\hat{L}_{n}\ket{\phi_a},

(7)

where
\hat{L}_{n}=|n\rangle\langle n|
are the site-local operators and

S_{ab}
corresponds to the spectral function of the bath,
calculated at the specific transition,

S_{ab}=\Gamma_{0}\left|\omega_{ab}\right|\left(n_{BE}\left(|\omega_{ab}|\right)+\Theta\left(\omega_{ba}\right)\right).

(8)

Here, we assume that the baths are characterized by ohmic spectral functions.
The above factor depends on the temperature of the bath;

n_{BE}(\omega)
is the Bose-Einstein occupation factor
n_{BE}(\omega)=\frac{1}{e^{\omega/T}-1}
. Other parameters are

\Gamma_{0}
as a dimensionless constant that dictates
the strength of system-bath coupling,
\omega_{ab}=\omega_{a}-\omega_{b}
, and

\Theta(\omega)
as the Heaviside step function.
Eq. (
8
) imposes the detailed balance relation, thus ensuring that transitions to lower energy states are more probable than transitions to higher-energy state; if

\omega_{ab}>0
, then

S_{ab}=\Gamma_{0}\omega_{ab}\frac{e^{\omega_{ab}/T}}{e^{\omega_{ab}/T}-1}
,

S_{ba}=\Gamma_{0}\omega_{ab}\frac{1}{e^{\omega_{ab}/T}-1}
.

The dynamics imposed by Eq. (
6
) decouples the population and coherences by using the so-called secular approximation. It relies on the assumption that energy levels are sufficiently separated from each other such that internal coherent dynamics have timescales shorter than the overall excitation decay time.

II.3
Steady state and measure for flux

In the literature, different measures have been suggested to quantify the ability of a network or a conductor to efficiently transfer carriers from an initial site to a target point. This includes the mean first passage time and other transient measures
Mohseni et al. 2008
;
Rebentrost et al. 2009
;
Maier et al. 2019
;
Ueno and Cooper 2025
;
Zhang et al. 2024
, as well as steady-state (or quasi-steady-state) measures, such as a rate constant and flux
Zerah-Harush and Dubi 2018
;
Zerah-Harush and Dubi 2020
;
Coates et al. 2021
;
Kalantar and Segal 2019
;
Dutta and Cooper 2020
;
Dutta and Cooper 2021
;
Coates et al. 2023
.
Here, we set the system under steady-state conditions, and our measure for transfer is the population flux out of a site of interest.

The steady state is constructed by enforcing a constant flux of carriers entering site
1
and leaving site
N
. These sites were chosen with the aim of achieving transport over the the longest distance allowed given a specified number of sites. Different combinations of injection and leakage sites may lead to different transport mechanisms being observed, see Refs.
Maier et al. 2019
;
Dutta and Cooper 2021
. We consider both Eqs. (
5
) and (
6
), and formally write them as
\dot{\vec{\rho}}=M\vec{\rho}
, with the density matrix written in vectorized form.

To enforce the steady state, first, we add the following jump operator to the Lindblad QME (written here in the site basis),

\hat{L}_{l}=\ket{1}\bra{N}
,
along with a temperature-independent rate constant, denoted by
\gamma_{l}
. This term corresponds to a leak and injection (or trapping) process. Second, in the steady state, we have
\dot{\rho}=0
; we find the solution to this equation,
\rho_{SS}
, by replacing one of the dependent rows in
M
(in our case, the first row) with the population normalization condition and solving
\tilde{M}\vec{\rho}^{SS}=\vec{u}
, where
\vec{u}
is a vector of zeros besides the normalization value in the first row.
\tilde{M}
is the modified Liouvillian matrix after adding the leakage jump operator and the normalization equation. This allows us to obtain the steady state as a matrix inversion task, rather than by obtaining the eigenvectors of
M
, which presents difficulties with automatic differentiation (see Appendix
A
).

Our measure of transfer is the steady-state flux from the exit site
N
of the system. It is given by

\eta=\gamma_{l}\bra{N}\rho^{SS}\ket{N}.

(9)

Maximizing
\eta
directly implies that one tries to maximize the population at the exit site. It is upper-bounded by
\eta\leq\gamma_{l}
.

The central question addressed in this work is straightforward: which families of energy profiles,
\vec{\varepsilon}=(\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2},...,\varepsilon_{N})

maximize the flux? In particular, we investigate the role of long-range tunneling compared to short-range tunneling, and examine how environmental coupling and finite-temperature effects influence the optimal energy landscape.
Given that the dynamics incorporate both coherent and incoherent processes, we anticipate that the optimal profiles will reflect the interplay between these competing mechanisms.

II.4
Optimization

The maximized population flux is obtained using one of two optimization algorithms: optimistic gradient ascent (OGA) or AdaMax. In both cases, an initial set of on-site energies are iteratively updated toward improving performance.

For OGA
Mokhtari et al. 2019
, in step
k
, the site energies are updated according to

\vec{\varepsilon}_{k+1}=\vec{\varepsilon}_{k}+2h\nabla_{\varepsilon}\eta\left(\vec{\varepsilon}_{k}\right)-h\nabla_{\varepsilon}\eta\left(\vec{\varepsilon}_{k-1}\right),

(10)

and in AdaMax
DeepMind et al. 2020
;
Kingma and Ba 2017
, the site energies are updated according to

\displaystyle\vec{\varepsilon}_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\vec{\varepsilon_{k}}+hm_{k}/(v_{k}(1-\beta_{1}^{k}))

(11)

\displaystyle m_{k}

\displaystyle=\beta_{1}m_{k-1}+(1-\beta_{1})\nabla_{\varepsilon}\eta(\vec{\varepsilon}_{k})

\displaystyle v_{k}

\displaystyle=\textrm{max}(|\nabla_{\varepsilon}\eta(\vec{\varepsilon}_{k})+\tilde{\epsilon}|,\beta_{2}v_{k-1}).

Here,
\vec{\varepsilon}_{k}
is a vector consisting of the values of site energies after
k
iterations,
h
is the learning rate,
m_{k}
is an exponentially-decaying moving average of the gradient,
v_{k}
is an exponentially-decaying moving average of the square of the gradient,
\beta_{1}
is the decay rate of
m_{k}
,
\beta_{2}
is the decay rate of
v_{k}
, and
\tilde{\epsilon}
is a small constant used to prevent division by zero. The energy profile
\vec{\varepsilon}_{max}
that
maximizes the flux in equation (
9
) is the zero of the gradient taken with respect to the system energies,

\eta\left(\vec{\varepsilon}_{max}\right)=\max(\eta)\Longrightarrow\nabla_{\varepsilon}\eta\left(\vec{\varepsilon}_{max}\right)=\vec{0}.

(12)

Eq. (
12
) provides a stopping condition for the gradient ascent algorithm:
exit the loop if
\left|\nabla_{\varepsilon}\eta\left(\vec{\varepsilon}_{k}\right)\right|<\epsilon
,
where
\epsilon
is some small tolerance.
Python libraries JAX and Optax
DeepMind et al. 2020
are used to calculate the flux derivatives and perform the OGA and AdaMax optimization.

Gradient-based optimization algorithms are sensitive to their starting condition. We also expect population flux as a function of site energies to have several local optima. To cover the parameter space thoroughly and identify many maxima of interest,
we perform optimization runs starting from many initial energy profiles.
Since the system’s behavior depends only on energy differences and not on the absolute value of energy, the energy of the first site is fixed to zero.
Then, a “hypergrid” is constructed over the remaining energies
(\varepsilon_{2},...,\varepsilon_{N})
and the initial conditions are randomly sampled from this grid.

While for chains with few sites it is possible to exhaustively optimize over the entire hypergrid, this becomes computationally impractical, and generally unnecessary, for larger systems. In such cases, random initializations followed by gradient ascent optimization provide a sufficient strategy for exploring the landscape of possible energy configurations.

In Appendix
A
we compare the optimization results for a three-level system using different optimization algorithms. Ultimately, OGA and AdaMax were selected because they performed the best on our problems, which typically included a “ridge” of close-to-optimal solutions.

II.5
Parameters and transport mechanisms

Depending on the range of parameters, in the coherent case one can observe sequential tunneling, deep tunneling, or ballistic motion. Furthermore, nonzero long-range terms, such as
J_{2}
, lead to interference effects with, e.g., short-range tunneling terms,
J_{1}
. Such interference effects can be constructive or destructive (see Section
III
).
When environmental effects are included, they can enhance the flux by destroying the Anderson localization effect through level broadening, or suppress ballistic transport.
Strong environmental effects in the form of local dephasing are known to freeze dynamics, interpreted as the quantum Zeno effect
Rebentrost et al. 2009
;
Zerah-Harush and Dubi 2018
;
Maier et al. 2019
. Furthermore, the finite-temperature dissipation model (OQS II) can enact diffusion-like transport
Lee et al. 2015
.

In this study, we select parameters for the chains such that competing transport effects contribute. That is, we set
J_{max}
,
\gamma_{l}
, the dephasing rate constant
\Gamma
and the temperature
T
to have similar values, thus achieving transport where they all compete and contribute.

With our choice of
\hbar=k_{B}=1
, every physical parameter that we use effectively has units of energy and may be compared directly to the site energies that we find through optimization. This allows us to easily comment on the site energy scale that plays best with our chosen tunneling and environmental effects.
To convert these values into physical units, one would divide the tunneling elements or dephasing rates by
\hbar
to obtain parameters in units of inverse time, where
\hbar
is the reduced Planck’s constant given in the same energy units as the site energies. For example, a typical energy scale may be on the order of meV
Creatore et al. 2013
;
Blach et al. 2025
, in which case
\hbar=6.58\cdot 10^{-13}\text{meV}\cdot\text{s}
. The temperature in degrees K is obtained similarly by dividing by
k_{B}
, the Boltzmann’s constant expressed in the appropriate energy unit.

III
Optimal Energy Landscapes in three-site systems

We begin our analysis by testing the optimization procedure on a minimal model, a three-site system.
In this setup, the population leaves site 3 at a fixed rate constant
\gamma_{l}
and is injected back to site 1 at the same rate constant, achieving a steady state.
Without loss of generality, we set the first site energy,
\varepsilon_{1}
, to zero. We also fix the tunneling elements.
The parameters varied in our model are the site energies
\varepsilon_{2}
and
\varepsilon_{3}
; by tuning these, we seek to optimize the population flux defined in Eq. (
9
).
Since only two parameters are varied in the optimization process, we can visualize the flux landscape as a contour map. We also easily compare the results of the optimization algorithm with the true global and local maxima obtained via extensive brute-force simulations.

III.1
Coherent model

We begin with the coherent case, obtained by setting
\Gamma_{n}=0
in Eq. (
5
). Fig.
2
shows simulation results for the three-level system under two scenarios: a model with nearest-neighbor interactions only (left), and a model with an additional tunneling element between the first and third levels (right).
In both scenarios, we present a complete flux map as a function of the two energy parameters,
\varepsilon_{2}
and
\varepsilon_{3}
while keeping
\varepsilon_{1}=0
. The point marked with an “x” indicates the value obtained via the OGA optimization protocol; it clearly successfully identified the optimal energy landscape for the system.

From Fig.
2
, we draw the following observations:
(i) When only nearest-neighbor coupling is allowed, i.e.
J_{1}\neq 0
and
J_{2}=0
(left panels), the optimal energy landscape is nearly flat with
\varepsilon_{2,3}\approx\varepsilon_{1}
; see Fig.
2
(a)-(b).
Notably, a broad range of values for the intermediate level
\varepsilon_{2}
supports close to optimal transfer fluxes.
In contrast, when the next-nearest-neighbor coupling is introduced, i.e.
J_{1}\neq 0
,
J_{2}\neq 0
, a V-shaped energy profile leads to enhanced transport and is optimal; see Fig.
2
(d)-(e).
In this structure, the middle site (site 2) is essentially moved “out of the way”, since the transfer can be facilitated by a direct tunneling transition from site 1 to site 3.
Another notable observation is that the optimal configuration shows populations close to
1/3
. We recall that optimal flux corresponds to high population at the exit site (3), which in this system corresponds to a value approaching 1/3.

For the three-level model, one can solve the coherent scenario analytically in the steady-state limit. To simplify the expression, we consider the case
\varepsilon_{1}=\varepsilon_{3}=0
while varying only
\varepsilon_{2}
(in the simulations,
\varepsilon_{3}
was also optimized). For nearest-neighbor tunneling, this yields

\displaystyle\eta(J_{2}=0,\Gamma=0)=\frac{4\gamma_{l}^{2}J_{1}^{4}}{12\gamma_{l}J_{1}^{4}+\gamma_{l}^{3}(\varepsilon_{2}^{2}+2J_{1}^{2})}.

(13)

This simple result captures rich behavior: assuming that
\gamma_{l}>J_{1}
, that is, that the leakage rate is not the slower, rate-determining step, we can distinguish between the deep tunneling limit,
\varepsilon_{2}\gg J_{1}
, and the ballistic limit,
\varepsilon_{2}\ll J_{1}
,

\displaystyle\eta(J_{2}=0,\Gamma=0)=\begin{cases}\frac{4J_{1}^{4}}{\gamma_{l}\varepsilon_{2}^{2}}&{\rm for\,\,}\varepsilon_{2}\gg J_{1}\\
\frac{2J_{1}^{2}}{\gamma_{l}}&{\rm for\,\,}\varepsilon_{2}\ll J_{1}.\end{cases}

(14)

In the opposite limit of a small leakage rate,
\gamma_{l}\ll\varepsilon_{2}
and
\gamma_{l}\ll J_{1}
, we find
\eta=\gamma_{l}/3
, reflecting that the system reaches an infinite-temperature equal-population state, and that
the flux is dictated by the leakage rate, which is the slowest, thus rate-determining, step. However, in our simulations, the parameters
\gamma_{l}
and
J_{1}
are of comparable magnitude. As such, neither of the limiting cases discussed above fully captures the observed behavior.

We now examine the role of next-nearest-neighbor tunneling,
J_{2}\neq 0
.
The introduction of this tunneling element opens the door to interference effects. As before, we simplify the analysis by setting
\varepsilon_{1}=\varepsilon_{3}=0
, and neglecting environmental effects. In this case, the population flux is

\displaystyle\eta(\Gamma=0)=

\displaystyle\frac{4\gamma_{l}^{2}\left[J_{1}^{2}-J_{2}(\varepsilon_{2}+J_{2})\right]^{2}}{12\gamma_{l}\left[J_{1}^{2}-J_{2}(\varepsilon_{2}+J_{2})\right]^{2}+\gamma_{l}^{3}\left[\varepsilon_{2}^{2}+2\varepsilon_{2}J_{2}+2(J_{1}^{2}+J_{2}^{2})\right]}.

(15)

When
J_{2}=0
, the flux is an even function of
\varepsilon_{2}
; the energy of the intermediate level can therefore be either positive or negative, see Eq. (
13
).
However, once
J_{2}\neq 0
, this symmetry is broken. This is because the presence of
J_{2}
introduces interference effects due to competing tunneling paths, and as such, the sign of
\varepsilon_{2}
is influential.
This effect is most clearly seen in the numerator of Eq. (
15
), where the flux vanishes when

J_{1}^{2}=J_{2}(\varepsilon_{2}+J_{2})
. For example, choosing
J_{1}=0.2
and
J_{2}=0.1
, the flux drops to zero at
\varepsilon_{2}=0.3
.
Conversely, negative values for
\varepsilon_{2}
are expected to enhance the flux, as confirmed in Fig.

2
(d)-(e).
Once again we point out that our simulation parameters are such that both tunneling and leakage parameters are roughly of the same order, making transport mechanisms inherently complex and nontrivial to disentangle.

The analysis of the coherent case in Fig.
2
provides three key guidelines for optimizing transport:
(i) In the absence of environmental effects there are many solutions (energy profiles)
that are near optimal. These solutions cover a wide range of values for the intermediate level,
\varepsilon_{2}
.
(ii) When multiple pathways are allowed due to next-nearest-neighbor tunneling, destructive interference effects appear when all energies are near resonance. To suppress these interference effects and achieve high flux, the intermediate level needs to be detuned from the entry and exit sites. That is,

|\varepsilon_{2}|\gg|\varepsilon_{1}|
,
|\varepsilon_{3}|
promotes transport.
(iii) The existence of multiple pathways, together with the openness of the system due to leakage,
\gamma_{l}\neq 0
,
breaks the symmetry of the energy profile with respect to
\varepsilon_{2}
, see Fig.
2
(d).

III.2
OQS Model I

We now incorporate environmental effects into the chain, in the form of local dephasing as described in Sec.
II.1
.

presents results for the three-level system, comparing the nearest-neighbor tunneling case [panels (a)–(c)] with the scenario that includes next-nearest-neighbor tunneling [panels (d)–(f)].
When only nearest-neighbor tunnelings are allowed (left), the flux depends on the interplay of coherent and incoherent effects. With the present parameters, this still favors a nearly flat energy profile, similar to the
\Gamma=0

When next-nearest-neighbor tunneling is included (right), interference effects take place, again similarly to the

Comparing Fig.

we observe that transfer trends are similar, although in the latter case an asymmetry with respect to the energy

Next, we analyze the problem analytically by solving the Lindblad QME, Eq. (
5
) in the steady-state limit.
To simplify our analysis, we set
\varepsilon_{1}=\varepsilon_{3}=0
.
Excluding
J_{2}
, we derive the following result, which generalizes Eq. (
13

\displaystyle\eta(J_{2}=0)=

\displaystyle\frac{2\gamma_{l}J_{1}^{2}\left[4\Gamma^{3}+4\Gamma^{2}\gamma_{l}+\Gamma\left(\gamma_{l}^{2}+8J_{1}^{2}\right)+2\gamma_{l}J_{1}^{2}\right]}{\Gamma^{2}\left[12\gamma_{l}\left(\varepsilon_{2}^{2}+4J_{1}^{2}\right)+7\gamma_{l}^{3}\right]+\Gamma\left[4\gamma_{l}^{2}\left(2\varepsilon_{2}^{2}+5J_{1}^{2}\right)+\gamma_{l}^{4}+48J_{1}^{4}\right]+\gamma_{l}^{3}\left(\varepsilon_{2}^{2}+2J_{1}^{2}\right)+12\Gamma^{4}\gamma_{l}+8\Gamma^{3}\left(2\gamma_{l}^{2}+3J_{1}^{2}\right)+12\gamma_{l}J_{1}^{4}}.

(16)

When all parameters are of comparable magnitude, no single term dominates, making the full expression cumbersome and difficult to interpret.
To gain some understanding, we expand Eq. (
16
) in orders of
\Gamma

13
) is given by

\displaystyle\eta(J_{2}=0)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\eta(J_{2}=0,\Gamma=0)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\Gamma\frac{2\gamma_{l}^{2}(\gamma_{l}^{2}\varepsilon_{2}^{2}J_{1}^{2}-8\varepsilon_{2}^{2}J_{1}^{4}-12J_{1}^{6})}{(\gamma_{l}^{2}\varepsilon_{2}^{2}+2\gamma_{l}^{2}J_{1}^{2}+12J_{1}^{4})^{2}}+O(\Gamma^{2})

Once again, we distinguish between the deep tunneling limit (
\varepsilon_{2}\gg J_{1}
) and the ballistic regime (
\varepsilon_{2}\ll J_{1}
), resulting in

\displaystyle\eta(J_{2}=0)\approx\begin{cases}\frac{4J_{1}^{4}}{\gamma_{l}\varepsilon_{2}^{2}}+2\Gamma\frac{J_{1}^{2}}{\varepsilon_{2}^{2}}&{\rm for\,\,}\varepsilon_{2}\gg J_{1}\\
\frac{2J_{1}^{2}}{\gamma_{l}}-\Gamma\frac{\gamma_{l}^{2}}{6J_{1}^{2}}&{\rm for\,\,}\varepsilon_{2}\ll J_{1}.\end{cases}

(18)

As expected, environmental effects assist transport in the deep tunneling regime, but reduce the flux when transfer occurs ballistically.

We now take into account both environmental effects and next-nearest-neighbor tunneling.
For simplicity, we focus on the resonance case,
\varepsilon_{1,2,3}=0
.
We derive corrections to the flux up to second order in
J_{2}
and first order in
\Gamma

\displaystyle\eta

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\frac{4\gamma_{l}(J_{1}^{2}-J_{2}^{2})^{2}}{12(J_{1}^{2}-J_{2}^{2})^{2}+2\gamma_{l}^{2}(J_{1}^{2}+J_{2}^{2})}-\Gamma\frac{\gamma_{l}^{2}J_{1}^{2}}{(\gamma_{l}^{2}+6J_{1}^{2})^{2}}

(19)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle 2\Gamma\gamma_{l}^{2}J_{2}^{2}\frac{2\gamma_{l}^{4}+23\gamma_{l}^{2}J_{1}^{2}-42J_{1}^{4}}{J_{1}^{2}(\gamma_{l}^{2}+6J_{1}^{2})^{3}}

From the second term, we note that the effect of dephasing is to suppress the short-range (
J_{1}
) ballistic transport.
The last term is proportional to
J_{2}^{2}\Gamma

The sign of this last term depends on the relative magnitude of
\gamma_{l}
and
J_{1}

\gamma_{l}\ll J_{1}

However, it is important to remember that Eq. (

assumes a flat energy profile, and as such does not capture the full complex picture that we present in
Fig.

when

This discussion is presented here to provide an appreciation of the range of transport regimes that appear in a system as short as three sites.

III.3
OQS Model II

The local dephasing model, OQS Model I, can be regarded as an infinite temperature setup since the jump operators do not differentiate between energy excitation and relaxation processes in the energy basis.
This is reflected in the steady-state solution, which reaches an equal population of
1/N
across all sites for an
N
-site chain once
\gamma_{l}\to 0
, regardless of the energy profile.

To probe temperature-induced effects, we next study the three-site model at finite temperature, as described by OQS Model II, Eq. (
6
). Results are presented in Fig.
4
, and we make the following observations:
(i) When
J_{2}=0
, the optimal energy profile is no longer flat. Instead, there is a preference for a structured energy profile, with
\varepsilon_{2}
elevated above the other two sites and
\varepsilon_{3}<\varepsilon_{1}
, see panels (a)-(b). Although at infinite temperature raising the intermediate level provides no advantage, at finite temperature this strategy enhances the flux. One way to rationalize this landscape is to point out that since the intermediate level is high in energy, its steady-state population is low (c), leaving more population available at the exit site 3.
(ii) When
J_{2}\neq 0
, the strategy identified in Fig.
3
was to suppress destructive interference by lowering
\varepsilon_{2}
.
However, this approach is less effective at intermediate temperatures because it causes carrier accumulation at the middle site (site 2) rather than at the exit site.
As such, we find that the structure in Fig.
4
(d)-(e)
balances interference effects with thermalization, yielding an optimal profile where
\varepsilon_{2}\sim\varepsilon_{3}<\varepsilon_{1}
.

IV
Optimal energy landscapes in long chains

Our goal is to develop physical intuition and offer practical guidelines for identifying energy profiles that enhance carrier transport in quasi-one-dimensional chains. We examine two central factors: (i) the interplay between environmental effects and coherent dynamics, and (ii) the role of long-range tunneling relative to short-range tunneling.

In Sec.
III
, we demonstrated that the optimistic gradient ascent approach successfully identified the optimal energy profile for a three-site toy model. Building on this success, we now extend our analysis to longer chains. Instead of explicitly defining nearest- and next-nearest-neighbor tunnelings, we employ a power-law tunneling scheme, as described in Eq. (
2
).
Two coupling regimes are studied:
\alpha=1
and
\alpha=3
, corresponding to, respectively, “long-range” and “short-range” coupling.

In the following, we present results for nine- and ten-site systems. These cases were selected to investigate possible even-odd effects in the chain length. For completeness, Appendix
B
provides parallel simulation results for intermediate chain lengths of five and six sites.

As a reminder, for an
N

N-1
energy levels relative to site 1, which is fixed as a reference point to zero energy. Other parameters are kept constant. We typically set the maximum tunneling energy to
J_{\text{max}}=0.2
and the leak rate to
\gamma_{l}=0.1
.
In OQS Model I, the dephasing rate is set to
\Gamma=0.1
, while in OQS Model II we use a temperature of
T=0.2
and the dimensionless system-bath coupling
\Gamma_{0}=0.1
.

IV.1
Coherent Model

Fig.
5
(a)-(b) show the energy profiles that optimize transport along the chain in the coherent limit.
For short-range tunneling (
\alpha=3
) the profiles remain nearly flat, reflecting a uniform landscape. In contrast, the long-range tunneling case (
\alpha=1
) dramatically reshapes the energy landscape by “pushing” intermediate energy levels away from resonance with both the entrance and exit sites, creating a more complex pattern.

This contrast is echoed in the population distributions shown in Fig.
5
(c)-(d).
Under short-range tunneling, populations are evenly spread across the chain, pointing to ballistic transport. Meanwhile, under long-range tunneling, intermediate sites generally hold low populations, acting as transient bridges, though in the 10-site chain some intermediate levels surprisingly maintain high populations.
Generally though, the chain population exemplifies the competition between the classically-required population gradient for transport (see the explanation of ENAQT in
Zerah-Harush and Dubi 2018
;
Zerah-Harush and Dubi 2020
), and our problem-specific requirement that the last site population be large so that the population flux is large.

To uncover the underlying transport mechanisms at play, we analyze in more detail the steady-state populations and coherences under both coupling models. The steady-state populations for the short-range tunneling case (
\alpha=3
), shown in pink in Fig.
5
(c)-(d), offers insight into why the corresponding energy profile is optimal. The population distribution exhibits a shallow gradient across the chain, indicative of near-ballistic transport, where the carriers flow with minimal scattering or localization.
In contrast, the steady-state populations for the long-range tunneling case (
\alpha=1
), shown in green in Fig.
5
(c)-(d), reveal a strikingly different transport strategy. Here, population accumulates predominantly at the first and last sites, with suppressed occupation of the intermediate sites. This suggests that the optimal energy profile minimizes the occupation of intermediate sites by energetically detuning them. This configuration helps avoid destructive interference effects, which are most pronounced when levels are near resonance.

The density matrix maps in Fig.
5
(e)-(h) corroborate the proposed transport mechanisms, with short-range tunneling (
\alpha=3
) facilitating length-independent ballistic motion. In contrast, a direct tunneling between the entry and exit sites, or possibly assisted by some intermediate levels, is shown in the long range (
\alpha=1
) case.
The energetically displaced levels exhibit strong coherence with each other, suggesting that they act as a “bridge” that helps to preserve the carrier’s coherence throughout the process.
However, we note that the steady-state populations in the coherent long-range model are highly sensitive to small variations in site energies. We suspect that the energy and population profiles depicted in Fig.
5
correspond to very sharp maxima in the population flux landscape, reflecting narrow “sweet spots” where transport flux peaks.

A natural question that arises is whether the optimized structures we obtain are constrained by our choice of initial optimizer conditions. In other words, are we truly finding the global maximum within the parameter space, or could there be even better solutions hidden beyond our current sampling?
In Figs.
6

A
for details on the optimization procedure.
Each column in any panel is a single profile, and the leftmost columns are those plotted in Fig.
5
(a)-(b).
Although the optimization was repeated with many randomized initial “guesses” for optimal profiles,
from Fig.
6

we find that in the short-range case, many initial conditions reach the same optimal solution or closely similar solutions and, correspondingly, a similar flux. In contrast, for the long-range model, Fig.

6

show that the parameter space apparently supports many local maxima. We identify several highly efficient solutions alongside a variety of sub-optimal ones. Notably, the best-performing solutions consistently share a key feature: the energies of the intermediate sites are strongly detuned from those of the entry and exit sites.

Fig.
13
in Appendix
B
shows that five- and six-site chains exhibit similar patterns in energy profiles, population distribution, and coherence behaviors for both short- and long-range tunneling regimes.
For short-range tunneling, the energy profile remains nearly uniform across the chain, hovering close to zero under our site 1 energy convention. In contrast, the long-range tunneling scenario favors setting the entrance and exit sites near resonance, while energetically detuning the intermediate sites. Interestingly, the most efficient solutions typically feature intermediate sites shifted to negative energies.
However, once again solutions under the long-range model are sensitive; small modifications in parameters can drastically alter the optimal energy landscape. This sensitivity gives rise to many locally-optimal energy profiles with intermediate sites detuned to positive energies, shown in Fig.

We summarize our findings on optimizing carrier transport in chains isolated from environmental effects. We refer to this as the “Coherent model” (CM) design rules: For short-range tunneling, the optimal strategy favors an almost uniform energy landscape that supports near-ballistic transport. In contrast, when long-range tunneling plays a significant role, the optimal energy profile leverages these extended couplings while simultaneously suppressing destructive interference. This results in a non-uniform, corrugated energy landscape that energetically detunes intermediate sites to facilitate more direct transfer between the chain’s endpoints.

In contrast, the long-range tunneling cases (Figs.

Taken together with the steady-state populations, these findings suggest that, much like in the coherent case, long-range tunneling favors an optimal energy profile that promotes direct transport from the initial to the terminal site. By effectively bypassing the intermediate sites, this configuration enhances long-range transfer and boosts the overall flux.

In Appendix

For models with short-range tunneling, we see nearly flat energy profiles and a constant gradient population. In contrast, long-range tunneling produces non-monotonic, corrugated energy landscapes, with population profiles exhibiting a clear transition from a decaying trend to an almost constant distribution along the chain.

We summarize our key findings on optimizing carrier transport in chains subject to local dephasing with the following OQS I design rule:
When local dephasing is strong and comparable in scale to the tunneling energies, the short-range tunneling model favors classical-like conduction with population decaying with length. This is achieved through nearly flat energy landscapes with only mild detuning from the entry and exit sites. In contrast, when long-range tunneling is allowed, optimal transport arises when the system successfully establishes ballistic-like conduction, even if only partial, as reflected by the steady-state population profiles. This transport regime is enabled by a highly nontrivial energy landscape that suppresses interference and promotes efficient end-to-end transfer.

The observation of a relatively flat population profile at some sites away from the entrance (
n=4
in Fig.
7
(c)-(d) and Fig.
15
(c)-(d)) under long-range tunneling is particularly striking. It demonstrates that ballistic-like transport can emerge even in chains with highly non-uniform energy landscapes, provided that the energy structure is carefully balanced with the long-range tunneling amplitudes and environmental effects.

IV.3
OQS Model II

We continue our investigation of transport, optimizing structures at finite temperatures using OQS Model II, as described by Eq. (

Unlike the Coherent Model and OQS Model I, we identify here several consistent trends across both short- and long-range tunneling chains. In both cases, the optimal energy profile takes on a ramp-like steadily decreasing form. This configuration can be rationalized: at moderate temperatures, it promotes forward transfer from the entrance to the exit site while effectively suppressing backflow.
For short-range tunneling, we also observe the emergence of high-energy levels above both the entrance and exit sites. Similarly to the three-site models shown in Figs.

Interestingly, after the first one or two sites, the population in the chain

towards the exit site, indicating on the accumulation of carriers towards the end of the chain due to the ramp potential.
Compared to optimal structures identified in OQS Model I, coherences in the finite-temperature model are more localized, extending only to about three neighboring sites for both short- and long-range tunneling cases. In Appendix

We summarize the key design principle for OQS Model II as follows: At moderate temperatures (
T\approx J_{max}

\Gamma_{0}T\ll 1

V
Conclusions

We identified distinct classes of energy landscapes that maximize population flux along quasi-one-dimensional chains. Hand-in-hand with the optimization, the analysis of the resulting steady state allowed us to uncover transport mechanisms.
Towards the objective of optimized transport, we specifically compared (i) the role of the environment and (ii) the impact of short-range vs. long-range tunneling on chain design. The interplay of these incoherent and coherent effects leads to complex transport behavior. Optimized transport in the models required these factors to cooperate.

We modeled the chain’s interaction with its environment using Lindblad jump operators. In OQS Model I, we incorporated local Lindblad dephasing, which, when viewed in the energy eigenbasis, corresponds to coupling with an infinite-temperature bath. In contrast, OQS Model II represents a finite-temperature environment that drives both excitation and relaxation processes between energy eigenstates, while satisfying the detailed balance condition.

We identified the following design rules for optimizing transfer, summarized in Table I.

(i) Coherent model: For short-range tunneling, the optimal configuration features an almost uniform energy landscape, enabling near-ballistic transport.
When long-range tunneling contributes, the optimal energy profile is non-uniform and corrugated, with intermediate sites energetically detuned. This facilitates a more direct transfer between the chain’s endpoints, as evidenced by strong steady-state coherences linking those sites. Long-range
coherent
tunneling systems are highly sensitive to variations in parameters near optimal energy landscapes.

(ii) OQS Model I: When local dephasing is significant and comparable in magnitude to the tunneling energy, models with short-range tunneling favor nearly flat energy landscapes, with only slight detuning from the entry and exit sites to facilitate conduction. In contrast, under long-range tunneling, a corrugated energy landscape allows the system to cross over to ballistic-like conduction away from the entrance site by suppressing interferences and facilitating efficient end-to-end transfer.

(iii) OQS Model II: At moderate temperatures (
T\approx J_{max}
), ramp-like energy profiles with a total gap of the order of
J_{max}
are preferred, regardless of tunneling range, as they support directional transport and improve the collection of carriers at the exit site. In these systems, transport proceed in an almost localized manner with small coherences.

The optimization process posed challenges at times, especially for the coherent model and OQS Model II. In the coherent model, particularly with long-range tunneling, the steady-state populations were highly sensitive to small changes in energy levels, resulting in complex, rugged flux landscapes with numerous local maxima that complicated optimization. For OQS Model II, the need to re-diagonalize the system Hamiltonian at each energy iteration further increased computational demands, making the optimization considerably slower compared to OQS Model I under the same parameters.

Long-range steady-state coherences, such as those found under OQS Model I in Fig.
7
(f)-(h)
have been shown to lead to robust entanglement
Streltsov et al. 2017
;
Dutta and Cooper 2020
;
Kim and Lee 2022
. As such, the energy profiles we obtain under long-range tunneling [purple in Fig.
7
(a)-(b)]
may find applications in quantum information processing and quantum metrology
Dutta and Cooper 2020
;
Schindler et al. 2013
;
Katsumi et al. 2025
.

In future work, we aim to extend our study of carrier transfer to situations involving multiple carriers, such as transport in spin systems modeled by Heisenberg-type chains with long-range exchange interactions.
Another promising direction is the investigation of excitation transfer in molecular aggregates or disordered materials coupled to an optical cavity
Mandal et al. 2023
;
Schachenmayer et al. 2015
;
Xu et al. 2023
;
Wu et al. 2024
.
Such cavity coupling can enable long-range excitation transfer. Joint optimization of both the molecular structure and its interaction with the cavity may yield new design principles for efficient carrier transport in quantum networks.

Appendix A
Details of the optimization process

Although OGA and AdaMax were used to perform the optimizations, other gradient-based optimization algorithms were also considered. Some demonstrations of this with three sites are shown in Fig.
12
, with their default Optax parameters — each found the true optimal values
(\varepsilon_{2}=-0.292,\varepsilon_{3}=-0.017)
for most initial energies. Ultimately, OGA and AdaMax were chosen because the optimizations terminated successfully in a relatively small number of steps. In Fig.
12
(a), the grey and green trajectories failed to find a maximum within the allowed range of energies, but the other trajectories took between 361 steps (brown) and 862 steps (red). The algorithms in Fig.
12
were tested with their default Optax hyperparameters except the learning rates, which are given in the caption. In theory, the default hyperparameters could be tuned as well, potentially leading to better convergence trends.

Many gradient descent algorithms used in machine learning/neural networks have a “momentum” term incorporated into their update step to help them cross “barren plateaus” where gradients are small. However, for many of our systems, our target maxima can be long ridges, which led most algorithms with momentum to find solutions farther along the ridge than they needed to. However, we found that AdaMax was better at finding maxima in complex flux landscapes where OGA failed due to its momentum term; comparing Fig.
12
(c) with (a), it is clear that AdaMax manages to explore the landscape more broadly.

Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) and Noisy SGD algorithms were also tested with three sites, the results of which are shown in Figs.
12
(b) and
12
(d), respectively. Both found the same optima as OGA and AdaMax, but with more steps: SGD took between 890 and 17,700 steps, and with the default noise levels, Noisy SGD took between 19,000 and 28,000 steps.

Differentiation through eigendecomposition in non-hermitian matrices is not implemented in JAX. Therefore, for OQS Model I, it was more practical to solve for the steady-state density matrix as described in Section
II.3
, rather than by diagonalizing the matrix
M
and taking the eigenvector corresponding to the eigenvalue equal to zero, as described in
Dey 2022
. For OQS Model II, where the Lindblad QME is expressed in the Hamiltonian eigenbasis, the eigendecomposition function in the Python Library FMMAX
Schubert and Hammond 2023
was used due to its compatibility with JAX’s automatic differentiation. The steady-state density matrix was still solved for in the manner described in Section
II.3
to slightly accelerate calculations.

JAX’s functions are multithreaded by default, making them incompatible with other Python parallelization libraries such as Python’s native multiprocessing library, or Joblib
Joblib Development Team 2020
. We had good success using JAX’s vectorization function, vmap, to vectorize our optimization loop over all initial sets of site energies instead.
