Global, Interdisciplinary Research to Guide a Secure and Sustainable Expansion of Mineral Supply Chains
Authors: Margaret G. O’Connell, Doug Aitken, Robin Bullock, Giuseppe Buscarnera, Jean-François Gaillard, Matthew A. Grayson, Ashty S. Karim, Richard LaDouceur, Kimberly R. Marion Suiseeya, Steven Micklethwaite, Pablo Pasten, I. Daniel Posen, Rajesh Sankaran, Avner Vengosh, Alvaro Videla, Nigel Wight, Jennifer B. Dunn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Interdisciplinary, international research collaborations are essential to expanding resilient global mineral supply chains sustainably and responsibly. We identify key research needs to achieve these objectives that address mining water consumption and contamination through tailing management and upstream process innovations. We also explore means of reducing solid waste from mines by repurposing it as a construction material and recovering minerals from aqueous waste streams. Introducing new technologies that improve water efficiency and reduce waste generation will bring trade-offs, including potentially increasing energy consumption. Accordingly, systems analysis is an essential tool for exploring these trade-offs and identifying innovations that can minimize them. These technologies will potentially influence how communities adjacent to mines experience social and environmental effects. Developing and implementing sensor networks to support community-based monitoring of the changes that adoption of these technologies will bring can support community-mine dialogue. Finally, energy system models can explore the potential regional or global effects of these changes on overall mining sector environmental effects. Innovation in the mining sector therefore requires collaboration among engineers, scientists, social scientists, policy experts, and others across academia, industry, governments, and nongovernmental organizations. Developing a secure, resilient, mineral supply chain that maintains or restores environmental quality while meeting global mineral demand requires supporting and sustaining interdisciplinary collaborations among nations.

Introduction

Critical minerals, strategic minerals, critical raw materials, and other terms broadly refer to several metals, elements, and other materials essential to many areas of economic and technological development. Here, we use the term “critical minerals” to describe these materials. Demand for critical minerals continues to grow. − The climate crisis is one demand driver given the urgent need to decarbonize the energy and transportation sectors that currently comprise nearly half of global greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions. From copper in electricity infrastructure, rare-earth elements (REE) in electric vehicles and wind turbines, and lithium in energy storage technologies, critical minerals underpin many decarbonization technologies. Some estimate that >3 billion tons of minerals are required to achieve climate goals, with 3-fold, nearly 7-fold, and 2-fold increases in copper, lithium, and REE production, respectively, required to meet demand for decarbonization technologies alone. Other sectors that play into this demand include communications and defense and electrolytic hydrogen and fuel cells, as well as the booming growth in data centers and the grid expansion necessary to provide them electricity. However, satisfying the increased demand for critical minerals by expanding traditional mining poses risks to water quality and water availability and can generate large volumes of wastes. These risks are generally the basis of community concerns that lead to communities with households, businesses, agricultural sites, and other infrastructure near mines to slow or halt new mining projects through protests and resistance, lobbying, and lawsuits. These actions have halted or delayed new supplies of REE, copper, and lithium, among other minerals (Table S1). Examples include the Kvanefield project in Greenland that would provide approximately 9% of global demand for rare-earth oxides. , The Cobre Panama mine (Panama), if restarted, and the Twin Metals Mine in Minnesota, if it obtains its permits, would meet approximately 1.4% and 2% of the world’s copper demand, respectively. − As a point of comparison, the world’s largest copper mine, Escondinda in Chile, provides about 5% of the world’s copper supply. Finally, if the Jadar Project lithium mine in Serbia were operating, it would supply about 5% of the world’s production. , Clearly, advances in the ability of mines to limit water pollution, reduce water consumption, and cut waste generation are important steps to improving mine–community relationships, which could advance the world’s ability to increase mineral production while protecting the health and well-being of people and ecosystems near mines.

We note that recycling will play a vital role in sustainable critical mineral supply, including for REE, potentially meeting 40% of mineral demand in 2050. − Mining, however, will continue to play a primary role in meeting minerals demand. It is therefore urgent to identify and pursue the following: technologies that reduce environmental and health risks, data monitoring infrastructure that addresses community concerns, and systems analysis methodologies that provide insights into mining trade-offs. Together, these tools can support effective decision making for areas where mining of critical minerals is envisioned. To meet these goals, a strong global interdisciplinary research effort is required.

Here, we propose a global, holistic, and interdisciplinary approach to addressing these challenges in the mining sector that builds upon past work that has addressed mine tailings management, mine water treatment, critical mineral recovery, and the potential for environmental quality data to enhance mining–community relationships largely as separate topics when they are intimately interrelated. − Drawing on our diverse and international expertise as engineers, geoscientists, environmental scientists, data scientists, and social scientists, the following sections will explore ongoing efforts in environmental impact mitigation, mining waste valorization, community-informed data monitoring, and the development of modeling tools capable of informing decision making across scales. We propose a globally integrated research agenda to bring about an important vision: a secure, resilient mineral supply chain that maintains or restores environmental quality while meeting global mineral demand.

Global Challenges and Emerging Solutions

The challenges faced by the mining industry and society more broadly in building a sustainable mineral supply chain are numerous, complex, and interconnected. Existing technologies and operational approaches embedded in traditional mineral supply chains will not be adequate to meet growing mineral demands in a sustainable and responsible manner. Achieving this transition will require both the development and deployment of new technologies and, critically, their integration into operational systems in ways that are appropriate to local geological, environmental, and societal contexts. This section focuses on water consumption and waste generation as two of mining’s most pressing challenge areas, while recognizing that similar approaches will also be required across related dimensions of the mineral supply chain, including, for example, energy consumption.

Water Consumption and Contamination

The energy–water nexus refers to the interconnected nature of energy and water systems, as water is an integral component of energy generation. Energy is also required to treat, distribute, and, in processes such as desalination, produce water for human and industrial use. Critical minerals mining, especially to support the energy transition, lies at the heart of this nexus: energy is needed for dewatering mining sites and operating wastewater treatment plants, while water supports processes such as flotation and dust suppression. Given that roughly 16% of critical mineral mines globally are in high water stress regions, a sustainable energy transition must employ technologies that minimize water use. Mining can exacerbate water insecurity through both consumption and contamination. At mine sites, the weathering of residual materials contained in mine tailings induces the leaching of hazardous elements and the contamination of nearby water resources. Ore type determines effluent composition; for example, sulfide minerals such as chalcopyrite (responsible for 70% of global copper production) are a major source of critical minerals. When exposed to air, these ores generate acid mine drainage (AMD), leading to watershed acidification. In aquatic systems, such acidic conditions mobilize toxic elements, − reaching ecosystems and humans through multiple exposure pathways beyond mining districts. These pollutants threaten drinking water, food production, and downstream ecosystems. Although this discussion focuses on tailings, managing other water streams, including dewatering and adit water, is also important.

Tailings Management

Tailings management consistently emerges as a key indicator of exposure risk. Globally, mining generates approximately 14 billion tons of tailings per year. The exposure of tailings to air and water can lead to risks such as AMD, toxic element dissolution and transport, dust contamination, and landslide risks. , Tailings storage facilities contain tailings and associated waters. Often involving the use of a tailings dam, these structures serve as disposal systems for mining waste and are built with residues prone to erosion, dispersal, and leaching. Tailings dams are vulnerable to geophysical hazards such as climate-driven extremes and seismic activity. Though their historical failure rates have been relatively low, recent evidence indicates a troubling increase in the number, frequency, and severity of tailing dam failures. The likelihood of catastrophic events stems from the detrimental role of excessive water content within this class of waste disposal systems. ,

Tailings dewatering therefore presents an important opportunity to reduce the risk of catastrophic tailings dam failures and the ensuing water contamination. The solids concentration of tailings can be increased through advanced thickening, such as high-rate thickeners, and the use of vacuum or pressure filters, the combination of which can increase water recovery rates by up to 85%. , Dry stacking tailings management entails mechanically dewatering tailings, moving them by truck or conveyer to a storage location, and layering and compacting them. Implementing these technologies can be more costly and energy-intensive than basic dewatering; , however, they offer substantial water recovery potential. Furthermore, tailings dewatering increases deposited-material strength and viscosity, reducing liquefaction and runout risks and thereby lowering both failure likelihood and potential impacts. Tailings dewatering also presents potential cost savings via reduced tailings dam footprints and higher value postclosure mine sites. Managing tailings conventionally remains the most common method of tailings management (e.g., 90% of Canadian mines as of 2017 deposited slurries in conventional facilities). Examples of dry stacking, however, exist globally, including at the Cerro Lindo mine in Peru.

Upstream Process Innovations

Beyond tailings dewatering, upstream measures can generate additional reductions. Preconcentration and ore-sorting technologies can remove barren material with minimal mineral loss; one study reports a removal of 20–30% removal of low-concentration ore with almost no reduction in copper recovery. Preconcentration approaches like these can support a proportional reduction in downstream water use. Coarse particle flotation can further enhance water recovery by producing tailings with improved drainage properties, making sedimentation and filtration more effective and reducing the energy demand of the process. Finally, mines are using more desalinated seawater to eliminate freshwater use, but the expense of this approach can be limiting. Including other water users and, potentially, other water sources into an integrated network can minimize costs, energy use, and environmental impacts per unit of water while increasing local water resilience. This approach also creates cost-effective and resilience-enhancing opportunities to store water and energy.

Maximizing Value Recovery from Traditional Byproducts or Wastes

Technology innovations should ideally reduce water consumption and contamination while increasing mineral recovery. Recovery of high-quality minerals will only become more difficult as demand levels push mining operations toward lower ore grades. Streams traditionally considered mining byproduct streams offer enticing opportunities to increase product recovery and reduce risks associated with waste storage and management. For example, the U.S. could eliminate its reliance on copper imports by recovering just half of the copper in domestic mining byproducts. Further, 16 tailings deposits, traditionally considered wastes, in the Antofagasta region of Chile contain enough REE to supply over 70% of the projected primary supply requirements in 2035. , Developing cost-effective minerals recovery strategies from these unconventional sources is therefore a pivotal area for future research. These sources, however, could contain potentially toxic and radioactive substances that may complicate or eliminate these strategies. −

Recovering Minerals from Waste Streams

Pathways to reduce mining waste through the extraction of valuable materials from tailings and water streams are under development. For example, chemical and electrochemical separation processes associated with primary resource extraction can be used for the recovery of critical minerals from tailings and other secondary resources. Leaching and chemical precipitation, adsorption, ion exchange, membrane separations, solvent extraction, and electrowinning are all commonly used, ,, but the complexity of process flowcharts increases greatly with the number of minerals targeted for recovery. Furthermore, the presence of unwanted impurities hinders efficient separations, resulting in additional costly neutralization steps prior to metal extraction. Nonetheless, examples of recovering minerals from waste streams have emerged. For example, Freeport–McMoRan implemented an initiative at the Morenci mine to recover residual copper from stockpiles from past mining operations. ,

Biological approaches to valorizing mining waste (i.e., biomining; extensively reviewed ,− ) are attractive because they offer the potential to manage selective separations from dilute streams and mitigate the environmental impact of traditional valorization methods. Biomining occurs through the processes of bioleaching, in which the microbes solubilize the metal of interest, and biooxidation, in which a mineral sulfide matrix is oxidized to extract the metal of interest. Bioleaching has been used at scale since at least the 18th century in Spain, where miners unknowingly were aided by microorganisms that solubilized the metals from mines and ore deposits. The first intentional biomining operation in 1951 used Thiobacillus ferrooxidans to dissolve pyrite in acidic solutions. Other bacteria like Desulfovibrio and Cupriavidus strains have been used to recover palladium from industrial waste because of their highly active enzymes to nucleate the metal. In addition, fungal Penicillum strains have been used to extract REE from solid material by citric acid production to maintain low pH. The choice of bacteria, fungi, or microbial consortia depends on the form of the waste stream (e.g., liquid and solid), the metal to be recovered, and the mechanism needed for recovery. , Organisms that naturally biomine are often found in extreme conditions (e.g., low pH, high temperatures, and high ionic concentrations). These organisms have enzymatic networks that enable metal binding, and the intentional use of microbes for bioleaching offers a low capital and operational cost alternative for low-grade ores when valuable metals are in low abundance (<0.5%) and traditional separations are too expensive or incur environmental penalties. Moreover, approximately a third of all proteins in nature have evolved to be highly selective for specific metal ions to perform their functions, and proteins exist that can bind almost every metal found in mining waste, including nickel, manganese, cobalt, and REE. − These proteins have high binding affinities but can be variable in their degree of metal preference. Studying and engineering metal-binding proteins holds transformative potential to develop new platforms for resource recovery. Next-generation biomining solutions will require a better understanding of cellular toxicity and metabolic regulation of bioaccumulation, discovery and characterization of new extremophiles that can be harnessed for bioleaching, and engineering of biosystems to efficiently process materials found in complex mixtures.

Repurposing Mining Waste for Use in the Construction Sector

Mine tailings are attracting increasing interest as building materials, which reduces waste volumes. Mine tailings can be used as cemented paste backfilling, reintegrating the material into underground mining operations to fill abandoned tunnels. A promising reutilization of tailings is in the construction sector as bricks, ceramics, glass, or alkaline-activated materials, as industry aggregates such as ore sand, and as components of concrete such as fine aggregate or cement. − Notably, mine tailings contain compounds with potential pozzolanic properties and can decrease the amount of cement and fine aggregate used to produce concrete. The beneficial reuse of these waste materials would reduce the GHG impact of cement production, reduce the need for fine aggregate for concrete production, and reduce the long-term tailings storage environmental and safety impacts. Many research questions remain unanswered about the potential use of tailings as building materials including how the properties and makeup of tailings influence their performance as building materials. Another key issue is understanding the extent of potential human exposure to leached heavy metals upon building material degradation. Assessing interactions that arise from repurposing tailings with other water or minerals recovery technologies at mines is also essential.

Integrating Technology Innovations

Research into technologies that reduce water consumption and wastes and/or increase mineral recovery has generally not explored how these technologies might complement or adversely interact with each other. Understanding the trade-offs between energy intensity, cost, and human/environmental health impacts that emerge from technologies applied in isolation versus in combination requires (a) robust data monitoring to evaluate technology performance across myriad real-world conditions, (b) systems analysis methods that incorporate these data accordingly, and (c) avenues for integrated research across process design (Figure ).

Role of Data Monitoring and Systems Modeling in Guiding Innovation

Advancements in Data Monitoring to Serve Community, Regulatory, and Industry Needs

Technological innovations may remain limited if communities do not trust their implementation. One route to increased community trust can be the cocreation of real-time, local environmental quality data. Multiple examples of these programs exist in Latin America. In Michigan (United States), the Eagle Mine’s Community Environmental Monitoring Program is a partnership between the mine, the Superior Watershed Program, the Keweenaw Bay Indian Community, and the Marquette Community Foundation. Surface water properties such as sulfate concentration, air quality parameters including particulate matter concentrations, and metals content in plants like blueberries are all monitored. A Web site houses the data for ease of public access. Despite these examples, communities are overwhelmingly excluded from contributing insights and data into environmental monitoring. − They generally lack access and expertise to utilize advanced sensing and other data collection technologies and have limited capacity to implement monitoring programs, and local and traditional ecological knowledge is often dismissed by governments and industries as “unscientific” or anecdotal. As a result, community involvement often relies on industry or government data, which can limit trust in the permitting process and engender resistance, contributing to multiyear and even multidecade mine development time scales. Furthermore, the state of science regarding community-based monitoring (CBM) is limited and generally constrained to monitoring that involves human-based sampling of environmental media that is then processed in a laboratory. As it stands, data from CBM is often viewed with suspicion and regarded as lacking rigor and quality. There is a scientific gap regarding the efficacy of CBM via sensors equipped with algorithms that use artificial intelligence (AI) to process the dataan approach that is not addressed in recent literature reviews and summary reports. , This approach, however, could save communities time, could be standardized in a manner that satisfies industry and regulators, and could uniquely produce data that communities trust.

Edge computing is a promising approach for advancing environmental monitoring via sensor- and AI-enabled observation to provide data that is relevant, accessible, and trustworthy. Utilizing a distributed computing paradigm, edge computing processes data close to the point of generation instead of transferring data exclusively to centralized cloud resources, thereby reducing latency, conserving bandwidth, and improving system responsiveness. − Sage is an example of an edge computing cyberinfrastructure that can support a multitude of sensors and adaptable software to match evolving data needs and environmental conditions. , Sensing capabilities include audio sensors to link bird sounds to biodiversity, a fusion of remote sensing imagery with local sensing to understand water quality, , and other meteorological, acoustic, and imaging sensors. This edge-based approach also enables adaptive and event-driven sensing, in which new measurements are triggered based on outputs from other sensorsfor example, activating visual analytics to identify chemical runoff following the detection of intense precipitation. , Integrating local in situ measurements with remote sensing products enables data fusion and multiresolution environmental characterization. This integration supports improved attribution of observed phenomena, such as identifying upwind pollution sources, tracking watershed-scale water quality dynamics, and validating ground-based sensor observations against independent satellite-based measurements. − The capacity to tailor sensing and software design to diverse environments is integral for establishing baseline environmental data and developing monitoring practices that align with industry, community, and regulatory needs.

Ongoing advances in sensor hardware, embedded AI, and the integration of in situ observations with remote sensing products are reshaping what can be measured in environmental systems, at what spatial and temporal resolution, and at what cost. − These advances are expanding the design space for environmental monitoring networks well beyond what was feasible even a decade ago. Still, important challenges remain in translating these capabilities into reliable, decision-grade data, including managing calibration drift across distributed deployments, , sustaining model accuracy under nonstationary environmental conditions, harmonizing high-frequency in situ measurements with coarser-resolution remote sensing products, , and meeting regulatory data-quality expectations across heterogeneous sensing modalities. Nevertheless, improved sensing capabilities that provide accessible data tailored to stakeholder concerns can build trust and support the implementation of the technological innovations discussed in Section . CBM provides an opportunity for communities to engage with mining companies and regulators based on their needs and priorities, , informing not only the kinds of environmental impacts monitored but also a more site-specific understanding of social risks and impacts. Real-time monitoring of operations employing diverse technologies in diverse environmental and community settings can provide the cross-context comparisons needed to evaluate technology performance under different conditions and develop best practices.

Multiscale Systems Analysis to Guide Critical Minerals Decision Making

Environmental impact assessment, including risk assessments, is a widespread method used around the world that remains an essential tool for assessing local site impacts and risks associated with toxic compounds in waste streams; however, there is an increasing need for holistic tools that consider a range of impacts across the entire set of inputs, operations, and supply chains associated with mineral production. These burdens must be understood across a variety of scales, beginning at the technology level with process modeling. One application of process modeling is to disentangle the interrelationships between waste prevention, water efficiency, and energy consumption in a processing train. Process models also explore the potential of emerging technologies, such as those that recover minerals from waste streams, to play a role in mineral supply chains. Prominent recent examples include models built to explore the recovery of REE from waste streams. ,, Importantly, process models are the basis for techno-economic analysis, which can help to identify economically viable technologies, set targets for process performance (e.g., mineral recovery and selectivity), and guide laboratory research. Methods to project laboratory-scale technology performance to full-scale impacts in a mining operation remain challenging but are necessary to cross-compare emerging and incumbent technologies. −

The second scale of systems analysis is at the mine level. Life cycle assessment (LCA) is an example of a model at this scale. LCA can assess the environmental burdens (e.g., energy and water consumption, emissions to air and water, and GHG emissions) per unit of mine output. To date, LCAs of critical mineral mining, which, per our recent review, cover 10 critical minerals mined in over 20 countries, have inconsistent choices of system boundary and functional unit, which complicates comparing published results. To address these challenges, Trost et al. proposed an approach to harmonizing minerals mining LCA methodology. These LCAs also suffer from major data gaps. Notably, data pertaining to water quality and biodiversity effects of critical mineral mines, which are among the most important environmental effects of mines according to communities that live near them, are scarce. Importantly, environmental monitoring data from sensing networks (Section ) can help fill this gap.

Critically, improved modeling of locally specific impacts within LCA, ideally prioritized based on the needs and concerns of mining communities, is needed to guide industry and regulatory decision making nationally and internationally. , LCA adoption in policies aiming to limit adverse environmental effects of the mineral supply chain would follow in the footsteps of other policies that have used LCAs to incentivize improved environmental performance. − These models can account for different pollutant emissions standards and geopolitical contexts that influence mine operations at different locations along a mineral’s global supply chain. Additionally, commercial market-driven mechanisms use LCA as a differentiator of environmental performance. , Mining industry certification programs, however, do not yet use LCA. For example, the Initiative for Responsible Mining Assurance (IRMA) does not necessarily require real-time data monitoring and access, nor does it mandate quantitative, LCA-informed approaches for measuring impacts. Calling for CBM and data accessibility and embracing an LCA methodological approach, especially to account for effects beyond GHG emissions such as water consumption and pollution that may contribute to ecotoxicity impacts, can improve the transparency of such certification programs and their capacity to direct decision making.

Finally, national and global energy systems models, energy-economy models, and integrated assessment models are a valuable suite of tools for crafting decarbonization pathways and assessing how choices in one sector (e.g., mining) interact with other sectors. − When coupled with material flow analysis, these models can interrogate the role of minerals across technology sectors and evaluate required mining sector growth trajectories that, in turn, affect mining’s resource use and emissions. For example, various existing models project scenarios that explore potential growth of electric vehicles, the types of batteries they will use, and associated lifetimes that will affect demand for critical minerals, − and a growing literature quantifies the broader mineral requirements implied by energy transition scenarios. , To better capture internal feedbacks between the energy system and mineral supply and to improve their ability to guide sustainable supply chain development, global energy systems models must be refined in several ways to guide sustainable supply chain development. First, these models must be adapted to better represent the physical flows of critical minerals associated with the technologies they model. − For example, Shi et al. (2025) showed how critical mineral constraints could jeopardize emission goals by limiting wind and solar buildout in China but that integrated assessment models are capable of reconfiguring to propose better diversified pathways that account for mineral-supply limits. Second, these tools can better leverage emerging methods that explicitly couple LCA to energy models and that enable internally consistent projections across a broad suite of environmental impacts. , Third, they must better represent the mining sector, how it is connected as both a user of energy and supply chain to energy technologies, and how it will change given changing ore bodies, efforts to shift the supply chain, and the effects of climate change including water scarcity. ,− In addition, their treatment of mineral-dependent sectors must become more robust through better projections and more detailed technology representation, reinforcing the need for stronger technology-level process modeling.

Taken together, enhancing systems analysis models and tools is integral to the guidance of technology and policy development. Developing these models requires input from many disciplines and global perspectives to produce robust, actionable results.

Interdisciplinary and International Collaborations to Develop and Integrate Solutions

When there are clear long-term benefits to incorporating new, innovative technologies into mines, the industry is often more willing to invest in and adopt them. A recent example is the significant increase in investment in electric haulage vehicles that offer benefits in reduced operational costs and achievement of decarbonization goals. , Nevertheless, mining innovation has historically been strongly oriented toward productivity, cost reduction, and production processes, while innovation in socioenvironmental management has received attention more unevenly and often as a secondary or compliance-driven dimension of technological change. Given the criticality of these areas to the future development of the industry and their connection to productivity, there are significant opportunities where an increase in targeted innovation that integrates across areas could result in considerably positive social, environmental, and economic outcomes.

At the technology level, collaboration is required among researchers developing early-stage innovations, industry users, and systems analysts translating bench- and pilot-scale results into potential full-scale impacts. At the supply chain level, building sustainable and secure critical mineral supply chains mandates collaboration across academic disciplines, across stakeholders, and across countries (Box ).

Disciplines: Interdisciplinary research must blend physical, earth, and environmental sciences to understand and avoid detrimental ecological impacts, human health and social sciences to understand and avoid impacts on frontline communities, and engineering, business, and law to inform the technology and policy practices that underscore mining operation implementation.

Stakeholders: Universities, communities, industry, and government have a role to play in each of the key research areas discussed in sections and . Community and regulatory needs must drive technology development, which, in turn, is guided by industry needs and systems-analysis-informed economic, social, and environmental metrics. These metrics themselves must be informed by early, frequent, and transparent community engagement, CBM, and insights from industry. This collaboration pathway is not linear, requiring iterative discussions between stakeholders at each step (Figure ).

Role of community, university, industry, and government stakeholders in driving technology, environmental modeling, and systems analysis innovations. Underpinning the connections between research areas and stakeholders is a need for global collaboration.

Countries: Given the wide range of experiences with mining in different countries, international collaboration between nations with established mining sectors, such as Chile and Australia, and nations with ambitious targets to expand this sector, including the U.S. and Canada, is essential to solving challenges in the mining industry. Countries must coordinate to avoid injustices regarding the distribution of mining burdens and benefits, and collaboration on a global scale can inform best practices based on lessons already learned in mining-mature nations and future insights as mining evolves.

International, interdisciplinary research is challenging to fund and sustain, in part because geopolitics can complicate international cooperation in the minerals sector. The international authorship of this Global Perspective illustrates how science diplomacy, , even when challenging, can help to sustain technical collaboration on shared environmental and technological challenges. International, interdisciplinary research and development in other areas such as addressing the COVID-19 pandemic and nuclear energy provide a roadmap for collaboration on challenging topics. , Building the collaborations that can lead to successful technology development and implementation in the mining industry will require creating stable public–private partnerships. These partnerships ideally would include international metals associations. − This type of research endeavor also requires a new workforce with expertise in the technology, geopolitics, social, and environmental aspects of this complex sector. New models of interdisciplinary, international education for undergraduate, graduate, and professional students are therefore necessary to train future leaders. Implementing these models will require creativity at universities to build degree and certification programs that do not follow traditional single-institution, single-discipline pathways. Overall, joint leadership from industry, governments, academic institutions, and communities has the potential to create the conditions for a secure, resilient mineral supply chain that responsibly stewards human and ecosystem well-being.
