Exploring parental views and perceptions of cardiovascular health: a qualitative study
Authors: Darci Phillips, Rachel Zmora, Ileah Rios, Tiwaloluwa Ajibewa, Zecilly Guzman, Leonardo Barrera, Leopoldo Castillo, Norrina Allen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCardiovascular health (CVH) is a critical component of overall health and well-being; however, understanding parents’ views and opinions on heart health remains underexplored. Insights from parents are essential for designing interventions that promote intergenerational heart health. The purpose of this study was to examine parental perceptions of CVH and gain a deeper understanding of their perspectives on achieving CVH and the role it plays in lifelong health.MethodsWe recruited participants and conducted semi-structured interviews with 21 parents of children under 18 and 4 children. Data were analyzed using thematic coding grounded in the constant comparative method to identify major themes and subthemes. Child interviews were analyzed separately. These interviews provided additional context for understanding how youth conceptualize CVH and insights into family communication on the topic. Thematic saturation was achieved, supporting the sample size’s adequacy for qualitative analysis.ResultsEight overarching themes emerged, including interest in participating in the study, parents’ initial thoughts on CVH, definitions of heart health, information sources, family and community roles, heart health experiences, resources, and emotional health connections. Key findings revealed significant barriers to CVH, including limited access to fitness programs and healthy food due to cost and time constraints. While families supported CVH through shared activities, explicit discussions among family members were uncommon. Participants emphasized the impact of stress and negative emotions on heart health, highlighting a potential area for intervention.ConclusionsThese findings highlight the need for flexible, community-based interventions that support CVH by engaging families and incorporating mental health promotion strategies. Effective strategies include enhancing community fitness and nutrition, programs to reduce cost barriers, offering flexible options to accommodate family schedules, and addressing emotional well-being. Healthcare providers can play a key role in these efforts by actively encouraging these strategies during patient interactions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25764-9.

Introduction

Cardiovascular health (CVH) is a key component of public health, directly influencing morbidity and mortality rates across populations [1]. In the United States, cardiovascular disease (CVD) and stroke remain leading causes of death, with projections indicating that total CVD prevalence will rise from 11.3% in 2020 to 15.0% by 2050. Hypertension, a key risk factor, is expected to increase from 51.2% to 61.0%, while obesity rates may climb from 43.1% to 60.6%, underscoring the urgent need for highly effective prevention strategies [2]. Despite advancements in clinical understanding and preventive strategies—such as cholesterol-lowering medications and improved hypertension management—significant disparities in CVH persist [3]. Certain racial and ethnic groups, including Black, Hispanic, American Indian/Alaska Native (AI/AN), and some Asian American subpopulations, face disproportionately higher rates of cardiovascular risk factors, CVD, and stroke. Additionally, people living in poverty, those without insurance, and individuals in rural areas experience higher CVD rates and worse health outcomes [2]. Shared familial environments and neighborhood dynamics significantly contribute to intergenerational patterns of CVH, highlighting the need to engage parents as critical actors in the transmission and promotion of heart-healthy behaviors.

Parents’ perceptions of CVH, their sources of information, and the barriers they face in maintaining CVH within their families remain insufficiently explored. Previous research has primarily focused on clinical markers and lifestyle interventions without adequately addressing the social, emotional, and contextual factors that shape parental attitudes and behaviors. Qualitative studies that do explore parent perspectives tend to focus narrowly on one or two health behaviors or factors, commonly diet, physical activity, or obesity prevention, or they concentrate on a single ethnic group or specific community. For example, Taverno Ross et al. [4] explored Latino parents’ perceptions of healthy living but emphasized physical activity and diet. Similarly, Arcan et al. [5] conducted a community-based participatory study with Somali, Latino, and Hmong immigrant parents to examine strategies for childhood obesity prevention. While valuable, their narrow focus limits their applicability to broader, family-centered CVH promotion. By understanding how parents articulate and understand CVH, researchers can tailor interventions to make them more effective for families and communities.

This qualitative study aimed to investigate parents’ views on CVH, including their definitions of heart health, experiences with maintaining it, and perceived facilitators and barriers. Optional interviews were also conducted with a small number of children to provide additional insight into how youth conceptualize heart health and to explore potential gaps in family communication. Through semi-structured interviews, this study sought to identify opportunities for interventions that will play a key role in preserving CVH for both parents and their children.

Methods

Design and settings

We used a qualitative design with semi-structured interviews to explore parents’ views and opinions of CVH. We chose this design to facilitate an in-depth exploration of participants’ perceptions, experiences, and barriers to CVH, allowing flexibility to probe deeper into relevant topics as they emerged. All interviews were conducted via Zoom, making participation more accessible for busy schedules. The constant comparative approach, which is a method of applying grounded theory, was employed to analyze the data [6]. This analytic strategy was chosen over other qualitative methods because it allowed for an ongoing comparison of participant responses during coding, which helped us explore a range of perspectives and experiences of CVH. As part of this approach, iterative team discussions were conducted to refine, collapse, and re-aggregate themes, ensuring that the analysis captured the breadth and depth of participants’ perspectives.

In presenting the findings, we use the term "heart health" rather than "cardiovascular health (CVH)" when paraphrasing or describing participant responses. This choice reflects the language predominantly used by both interviewers and participants during the interviews as well as our intention to use terminology that is more conversational and widely understood.

Participants and recruitment

Inclusion criteria required participants to be parents or legal guardians of at least one child under 18 years old. Exclusion criteria included individuals who were not parents of a child under 18 or who did not speak English or Spanish, as those were the languages supported by the study team. Child interviewers were optional for children ages 9 to 17. Most interview participants contributing to this study resided in Illinois, primarily in Chicago and the surrounding suburbs. The interviewers on the study team were comprised of one Hispanic woman, one Hispanic white woman, one white woman, and one Black man, which brought diverse racial and cultural perspectives to the study. While this team brought racial and cultural diversity, they did not fully mirror the racial/ethnic identities of the sample. For example, Asian and multiracial participants were not represented among interviewers. Interviewers’ identities alongside their professional experience in public health may have influenced the interviews and the interpretation of data.

Recruitment occurred in two phases:Phase 1 (May to October 2022): Participants were recruited through flyer distribution in community locations such as a children’s hospital, elementary schools, parks, daycare centers, and coffee shops. We also posted on parent-focused Facebook pages and collaborated with another research study to include our flyers in their take-home folders. These efforts resulted in 4 parent participants and 1 child participant, who were interviewed between July and November 2022.Phase 2 (October to November 2023): We selected and invited participants from the Young Hearts Study cohort via email. Selection criteria ensured diversity in race, ethnicity, income, and country of origin to capture a broad range of perspectives on heart health. This phase yielded the remaining 17 parent participants and 3 child participants.

Initially, we aimed to recruit all participants directly from the community as described in Phase 1. However, despite distributing flyers across a variety of settings (e.g., hospitals, schools, shelters, parks), the response rate remained very low. To improve enrollment, we shifted to a targeted approach in Phase 2, sending email invitations to a demographically diverse subset of Young Hearts participants. This approach was far more effective, likely due to prior research engagement and the direct method of outreach. Due to the small sample size in Phase 1, we did not conduct formal comparisons between the two phases.

A REDCap eligibility screener was used to recruit both parents and children. It was accessed via QR code on flyers or through email invitations. On the screener, parents were asked whether they were interested in having their child(ren) also participate in the study. A 'yes' response prompted the study team to coordinate a time when both the parent and child would be available for separate interviews. Child participation was entirely optional and only conducted with parent consent and child assent. Children were not required for parental eligibility. Including children provided additional insight into how youth conceptualize CVH and allowed the study team to explore potential gaps in family communication around heart health, thereby enhancing the intergenerational perspective of the study.

Data collection

An interview guide was developed based on the study’s objectives, with questions designed to elicit detailed insights into how parents understand, articulate, and approach CVH within their families (see Supplementary File 1 for the full English-language parent interview guide). A separate, age-appropriate interview guide was used for child participants to explore their understanding of heart health in a developmentally relevant way (see Supplementary File 2 for the full English-language child interview guide). The guides were pilot tested with non-study participants to ensure clarity and relevance and were subsequently reviewed and approved by the full study team. The guide included open-ended questions aimed at exploring participants’ initial thoughts on heart health, their definitions of heart health for themselves and their children, and the perceived barriers and facilitators to maintaining CVH. While the guide did not include a single question explicitly asking about barriers, this topic was explored through participant reflections and probes, primarily within discussions of family roles, community support, and desired resources (see Questions 6–9 in Supplementary File 1). As part of the analysis, we examined how frequently participants discussed factors outlined in the American Heart Association’s Life’s Essential 8 (LE8), which defines key measures for maintaining cardiovascular health, including blood pressure, blood sugar, body weight, cholesterol, diet, physical activity, nicotine exposure, and sleep.

Data analysis

Interview recordings were professionally transcribed and subsequently de-identified to maintain participant confidentiality. Transcripts were then uploaded into Dedoose, a qualitative data analysis software, to facilitate coding and thematic analysis using a constant comparative approach. Coding was conducted using both deductive and inductive strategies. Deductive codes were derived from the study objectives and the American Heart Association’s Life’s Essential 8 (LE8) framework, allowing the team to track which components of CVH were discussed across interview questions. Inductive codes were developed as new patterns emerged from the data, including themes such as cold weather as a barrier and connections between heart health and emotional well-being. Each transcript was coded independently by a primary and secondary coder. Coders flagged any disagreements, which were then reviewed collaboratively in weekly team meetings. Reflexive practices, such as team discussions and consensus-building during data analysis, were utilized to ensure potential biases were minimized throughout the research process. For clarity and contextual understanding, participant quotes are labeled with a study ID, sex, and age (e.g., Participant 9, Female, 35).

Results

We conducted interviews with 21 parents of children under 18 years old and 4 children. The mean interview length for parents was 19 min and 4 min for children. Although the child interviews were brief, they were developmentally appropriate for the participants' ages, which ranged from 9 to 14 years old (mean age: 11 years). The child interview guide was designed to be concise and age appropriate. Despite their brevity, the interviews provided meaningful insight into how children conceptualize CVH and the extent to which it is discussed within families. Child transcripts were coded separately from parent interviews to ensure that their perspectives were not overshadowed by the longer, more complex narratives provided by parents. Among parent participants, 19 were women, and 2 were men. They ranged in age from 25 to 51 years old (with one missing age response). 6 participants identified as Hispanic or Latino. 12 participants identified as White, 3 as Black or African American, 5 as Asian, and 1 participant identified as more than one race. Regarding family composition, 8 parents had one child, 9 had two children, and 4 had three children. Eight overarching themes were identified from the interviews, reflecting participants' perceptions of CVH. These included: (1) interest in the study, (2) initial thoughts about heart health, (3) heart health definitions, (4) information sources, (5) roles, (6) heart health experiences, (7) resources, and (8) emotional health connections. These themes highlight participants' motivations, barriers, and facilitators to maintaining CVH, as well as potential areas for intervention. The following sections explore the key themes that emerged from the interviews.

Theme 1: interest in the study

Participants described various reasons for their interest in the study. These included personal and family experiences with heart health issues, a desire to contribute to research, and prioritizing health. Two participants were motivated to participate due to personal heart health conditions, and nine participants mentioned family members who had suffered heart attacks or other heart-related conditions. One participant shared: “Yeah. Well, my grandma just passed away this year on February 10, she had a heart attack. My father, he's 48 or 49, had a heart attack, but he survived it. Just different things like that. Then my oldest brother, he's about 34 years old and he's going to RUSH hospital trying to figure out what's wrong with his heart” (Participant 9, Female, 32). For these participants, seeing family members suffer from heart-related conditions likely heightened their awareness of cardiovascular disease risks and reinforced their motivation to learn more about prevention and potential interventions.

In addition to those who expressed interest in the study due to family or personal experiences with cardiovascular conditions, several participants referenced these issues later in their interviews. Across all interviews, 15 (71%) participants discussed either family history or a personal experience with cardiovascular or related chronic conditions. One participant noted that they had not thought much about heart health because they lacked a family history of such conditions. Collectively, these findings suggest that many participants were attuned to cardiovascular risk, potentially shaping their proactive views on preserving CVH.

Theme 2: initial thoughts about heart health

When asked about their initial thoughts on heart health, participants described chronic conditions such as congenital heart defects and diabetes, acute heart-related events like heart attacks and strokes, the importance of maintaining a healthy lifestyle, and the heart’s function in circulating blood and oxygen throughout the body. Some participants immediately associated heart health with conditions like heart attacks or strokes. As one participant stated, “Heart attacks—that’s the first thing. I’m not sure if anything else particularly comes to mind. Maybe, possibly a stroke, but heart attack” (Participant 3, Female, 33). This response suggests that, for some, CVH is primarily linked to life-threatening events rather than preventive care.

Others emphasized a healthy lifestyle, including exercise and diet, as crucial for maintaining heart health. Additionally, heart function was often highlighted as an essential component of heart health. For example, one participant said, “What I'm thinking it could be from a perspective of someone that has issues with their heart or that how can you prevent, to basically support that organ that is vital for obviously survival” (Participant 18, Male, 40). This statement reflects an understanding of the heart as a critical organ that requires care to maintain proper function and prevent disease.

Similarly, child participants recognized heart health as an important aspect of their overall well-being. The majority described heart health in relation to the physical function of the heart and how it contributes to overall health. Some mentioned that they think about physical activity as a way to protect or help their heart, reinforcing the connection between movement and heart health. “I think about how important your heart is. And how you can protect your heart. And how your heart does so many good things for you” (Child Participant 4). This response suggests that even at a young age, the heart is recognized as essential to one’s health. However, this understanding might be more general and less focused on specific risk factors or preventive behaviors.

Theme 3: heart health definitions

Participants defined heart health for themselves and their families through three main lenses: medical care, a functioning heart, and being healthy through diet and exercise. Many emphasized routine check-ups and preventive care, with one participant stating, “To me, it just means just trying to stay healthy and making sure again staying on top of my checkups” (Participant 30, Female, 43). This response highlights the belief that maintaining CVH requires proactive medical engagement, such as regular visits to healthcare providers.

Intentional daily choices, such as balanced meals and exercise, were also seen as integral, as another participant explained: “To me personally, I think that it's an everyday thing. I think it's something that it's not just come the new year you want to start working out. I think it's just, it's an ongoing process. It just becomes part of your routine to invest in your body to have longevity in life. So, I think it's just being very mindful every day of what you're doing. Making sure for instance like I'm eating a well-balanced meal. I'm exercising” (Participant 17, Female, 46). This response highlights the idea that CVH is not a one-time effort but rather a continuous commitment. It underscores the importance of ensuring that CVH interventions prioritize sustainable lifestyle changes rather than short-term modifications.

Family definitions often mirrored these views, with parents emphasizing the importance of modeling healthy behaviors for their children. For example, one participant said: “And I think for my family too, my husband also has seen that sort of thing and just being aware of his health and being active and that we're modeling that for our kids and encouraging them to either do swimming or volleyball or gymnastics or something to keep them moving and active. And starting some of those habits while they're really young” (Participant 16, Female, 37).

This response highlights the intergenerational transmission of CVH-promoting practices, emphasizing the role of parental modeling in fostering lifelong healthy behaviors in children.

Theme 4: information sources

Participants relied on multiple sources for heart health information, including healthcare providers, online platforms, and their communities. Physicians were the most trusted sources as participants frequently mentioned that they consult their primary care providers or pediatricians.

“I start with our doctors who we see who we trust. If it's for my kids, we're very comfortable with our pediatrician and sending messages if we have any questions about things. If I'm looking for information, I definitely try to look for what I consider trusted” (Participant 16, Female, 37).

“I start with our doctors who we see who we trust. If it's for my kids, we're very comfortable with our pediatrician and sending messages if we have any questions about things. If I'm looking for information, I definitely try to look for what I consider trusted” (Participant 16, Female, 37).

This response underscores the role of healthcare providers as trusted sources of CVH information. Online resources, such as WebMD and Mayo Clinic, were often used but some participants raised concerns about reliability. Finally, participants mentioned community programs and newsletters as sources of general health information.

On the other hand, children and adolescents reported receiving most of their heart health information from teachers. None of the child participants mentioned discussing heart health with their friends, and only one had ever discussed it with a parent. This suggests that formal educational settings likely play a critical role in shaping children’s early understanding of heart health. One participant said, “There's a program they bring some people in and talk at our school. It was about overall health, mental health, sexual health, all sorts of things. But heart health is part of it actually” (Child Participant 3). This response highlights the influence that school-based programs could have in shaping children's early understanding of CVH. The participant's mention of heart health as just one part of a larger curriculum suggests that while it is addressed, it may not be a primary focus.

Theme 5: roles

Family, community, and healthcare providers played a critical role in shaping participants’ CVH. Families often served as support systems, encouraging healthy behaviors like exercising together or preparing balanced meals. One participant recalled, “We do a lot of things; I'm thinking like my husband and my kids, we do a lot of things together. So, we go for walks around the neighborhood when it's nice outside. We try to go to the park and we play together. We try to really encourage the healthy eating at home” (Participant 16, Female, 37). This response highlights the role of family cohesion in supporting CVH. By engaging in shared physical activities and fostering healthy eating habits, parents create an environment where positive CVH behaviors are encouraged and normalized from an early age. However, family stressors, such as busy schedules, were also reported as a barrier for maintaining CVH. Community resources, such as fitness programs, park district activities, and nearby walkable areas such as bike trails, were valued when available. However, they were not accessible for all participants, and some were limited by cost.

Theme 6: heart health experiences

Participants reflected on times they felt good about their heart health and how it could be improved. Colder weather was a significant barrier, with participants noting decreased physical activity in winter months. One participant explained, “So again, I think it's just creating those routines, and having those routines will definitely have an impact on our health. And as we get into this colder months, it's important to stay active, and it's important just to mindful of or even your eating habits because I think it's very important. So that's kind of the only thing that I'll say about that” (Participant 18, Male, 40).

The participant acknowledges how seasonal changes, particularly colder months, present challenges to staying active and maintaining healthy eating habits. However, they also emphasize that being mindful and establishing routines can help mitigate these barriers and support CVH. In addition, participants commonly described positive behaviors, such as starting fitness routines or making dietary changes, as crucial for improving heart health.

Likewise, child participants primarily described engaging in physical activity when asked about moments that they felt good about their heart health. Two children also associated feeling good about their heart health with eating nutritious foods, showing that both movement and diet were recognized as important from an early age. For example, one participant said, “Over the summer I really started eating healthy and running on the treadmill and doing a mile every day and I felt really good that I was, getting it and giving it physical training and everything” (Child Participant 3). This response highlights how children perceive physical activity as a key component of maintaining CVH. Their sense of accomplishment shows how positive health behaviors can be motivating and rewarding from a young age.

Theme 7: resources

Participants identified a variety of resources that could impact their heart health, including at-home equipment, community programs, and financial support. Many acknowledged underutilizing resources that were already at their home, like exercise equipment, while others said they believed having such equipment at home could really benefit their heart health. For example, one participant said, “If I had a Peloton, yeah, because during the winter months it makes it difficult for me to get to the gym because of the cold” (Participant 12, Female, 46). Participants expressed a desire for a range of community resources, including affordable gym memberships, walking paths, virtual fitness classes, and indoor playgrounds. Cost was a recurring barrier, particularly for healthier food options and fitness activities.

Theme 8: heart health and emotional health

Participants commonly linked their emotional health to their heart health, describing stress, anxiety, and negative emotions as detrimental. For example, one participant shared how work stress and busy schedules made it difficult to maintain a healthy lifestyle.

“I have a daughter, husband and very busy lately doing a lot of work family and studying as well. I'm doing an MBA so the crazy [laughs] life. But, yeah, I don't exercise much anymore. I feel like since COVID I've been sitting a lot. I took some time to try to exercise and I did it for a little bit but then just stop for some reason and then it's just – it goes away completely. So, it's difficult. So I have very sedentary. Only when I go on vacation, I get more active. I should be more on vacation. That's the healthy side of things” (Participant 20, Female, 38). This participant’s reflection illustrates how the demands of work, family, and education can make it difficult to maintain an active lifestyle, leading to a more sedentary routine.

Other participants emphasized the importance of positive emotional states, with some noting that happiness and social engagement contribute to better heart health. One participant explained: “What heart health means to me? It means to me health and being active and in the TV or the media we see like people, heart health is related to happiness. And so I guess that is in my head, like it's somehow directly or indirectly related to happiness outside you” (Participant 19, Female 39). By associating heart health with happiness and media portrayals of active lifestyles, the participant suggests that maintaining a positive emotional state and engaging in enjoyable activities may contribute to better cardiovascular outcomes. Finally, some participants suggested that stress management strategies like meditation and physical activity could counterbalance negative emotions and ultimately improve heart health. Discussion

This study explored parental perceptions of CVH, providing valuable insights into barriers, facilitators, and opportunities for promoting intergenerational heart health. Findings revealed that parents emphasized exercise and diet as central to CVH, identified community and family support as critical, acknowledged the influence of emotional well-being on heart health, and may undervalue sleep as an important measure of heart health. Our findings align with and expand upon existing literature on perceptions of CVH. Although not intentionally guided by the socioecological model (SEM), the interview questions and themes align with the multilevel structure, capturing individual, family, community, and healthcare influences. This underscores that cardiovascular health behaviors are shaped by broader social and structural factors, beyond individual choice.

The interviews of children provided additional insights into how younger individuals conceptualize heart health. Children demonstrated an awareness of the heart’s role in overall health and frequently connected heart health to physical activity. However, discussions about heart health did not appear to take place within families, with most children reporting that they had never discussed heart health with their parents. Instead, teachers were the primary source of information. This suggests an opportunity for family-centered educational interventions that help bridge the gap between what children learn in school and what is reinforced at home. However, given the small number of participants and the short interview duration, these findings should be interpreted as exploratory.

Participants frequently cited financial constraints as barriers to maintaining a healthy diet and engaging in physical activity, reflecting broader findings in the literature. Research consistently shows that limited access to affordable, nutritious food is linked to poorer eating habits and a higher risk of diet-related diseases, such as cardiovascular disease and diabetes [7]. In addition, financial limitations are known to impede healthy eating [8] as well as access to opportunities for physical activity, including fitness facilities and structured exercise programs [1, 9].

Time constraints emerged as another significant barrier to cardiovascular health, with many participants describing their struggles to balance work, family responsibilities, and self-care. These findings align with prior research indicating that parents often experience higher stress levels than non-parents, which can negatively impact health behaviors, including reduced physical activity, inadequate sleep, and suboptimal dietary habits. According to a 2024 advisory by the U.S. Surgeon General, 48% of parents reported feeling completely overwhelmed most days, compared to 26% of non-parents [1, 10]. Parents often prioritize the needs of their children and families over their own health, which can lead to chronic stress and further exacerbate barriers to maintaining CVH [1, 11]. Addressing these time-related challenges through flexible, family-centered interventions—such as offering childcare at fitness facilities or promoting brief, at-home exercise routines—may help alleviate these barriers and improve health outcomes.

At the same time, interviews with children suggest that they already recognize key heart-healthy behaviors, particularly physical activity and diet. However, since few children reported discussing heart health with their parents, there may be a missed opportunity to strengthen intergenerational health habits. This disconnect is notable, as parents emphasized modeling healthy behaviors, but children did not report parallel conversations at home. Modeling alone may not ensure awareness without explicit communication. Additionally, children’s focus on physical activity and diet, with limited mentions of other CVH measures, may reflect developmental differences in how health concepts are understood at younger ages. Encouraging parent–child conversations about heart health and providing family-oriented resources could help bridge this gap, reinforcing and expanding children's existing knowledge while also fostering healthier behaviors within the entire family. Given that parents can have a positive influence on their children's health behaviors, supporting them with accessible tools and strategies may further enhance children's long-term cardiovascular health [12].

Strengths and limitations

This study provides novel insights into perceptions of CVH among parents and children, offering a unique contribution to the literature on intergenerational heart health. This study includes a racially and ethnically diverse sample of parents from across the Chicago metropolitan area, providing a broad range of perspectives that enhance the depth of the findings. Participant recruitment is linked to the Young Hearts Study, adding valuable context to the cohort by providing qualitative perspectives that complement existing quantitative data. Additionally, the use of a rigorous approach grounded in the constant comparative method strengthens the reliability of findings by ensuring that emergent themes were systematically examined.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. Because 19 of 21 parent participants identified as women, the findings primarily reflected maternal perspectives. As such, transferability to fathers or other caregivers may be limited. Although efforts were made to recruit an economically diverse sample, impressions based on participants' comments and the neighborhoods mentioned suggest that many may have had access to relatively greater resources. For example, while healthcare providers were consistently viewed as trusted sources of information, participants did not raise common structural barriers such as insurance limitations, language access issues, or provider availability. This absence suggests relatively high access to healthcare resources within the sample. This could potentially limit generalizability of our findings to lower-income families. Additionally, the study sample was recruited in and around Chicago, further limiting generalizability. Also, many participants referenced personal or family cardiovascular conditions; however, we did not conduct comparative analyses between those with and without such histories. As a result, potential differences in perspectives of CVH could not be assessed. Additionally, while recruitment in Phase 2 was stratified on key demographic criteria to support sample diversity, modifying the recruitment strategy between phases may have introduced selection bias. Findings from the youth interviews should also be interpreted with caution due to the small number of children interviewed and the brief length of those interviews. Additionally, not all identities represented in the participant sample were reflected in the study team. This could have led to some missed contextual cues when there was a mismatch in cultural background between the interviewer and the participant. Furthermore, interviewers’ training in public health may have shaped which responses were probed more deeply, potentially influencing data collection and interpretation. While the study sample was racially and ethnically diverse, we did not conduct subgroup analyses to explore potential differences in CVH perceptions across these groups, which limited our ability to detect culturally specific insights. However, thematic saturation was achieved, indicating that our study sample was sufficient. Finally, the interview guide could have been designed to more comprehensively explore cultural influences on CVH and specific health behaviors such as sleep. Future research should aim to include a broader socioeconomic spectrum and refine qualitative approaches to further investigate underexplored aspects of parental perspectives on CVH.

Conclusion

This study highlights the importance of addressing financial and time barriers to heart health while promoting the role of family and community supports. By integrating stress management strategies and raising awareness about overlooked factors like sleep, health promotion interventions can more effectively support families in achieving lifelong cardiovascular health. Future research should consider longitudinal or larger-scale mixed methods studies to address the limitations of this study and deepen our understanding of parent and child perspectives on CVH and how they evolve over time.
