Abstract
. Identifying individual animals within large wildlife populations is essential for effective wildlife monitoring and conservation efforts. Recent advancements in computer vision have shown promise in animal re-identification (Animal ReID) by leveraging data from camera traps. However, existing methods rely exclusively on visual data, neglecting environmental metadata that ecologists have identified as highly correlated with animal behavior and identity, such as temperature and circadian rhythms. To bridge this gap, we propose the Meta-Feature Adapter (MFA), a lightweight module designed to integrate environmental metadata into vision-language foundation models, such as CLIP, to enhance Animal ReID performance. Our approach translates environmental metadata into natural language descriptions, encodes them into metadata-aware text embeddings, and incorporates these embeddings into image features through a cross-attention mechanism. Furthermore, we introduce a Gated Cross-Attention mechanism that dynamically adjusts the weights of metadata contributions, further improving performance. To validate our approach, we constructed the Metadata Augmented Animal Re-identification (MAAR) dataset, encompassing six species from New Zealand and featuring paired image data and environmental metadata. Extensive experiments demonstrate that MFA consistently improves Animal ReID performance across multiple baseline models. Our code and dataset are available at https://anonymous.4open.science/r/Web4Good-8606/ .



1.
Introduction

The conservation of biological diversity, formally adopted at the United Nations Conference on Environment and Development in 1992
(
Herkenrath 2002
)
, is a cornerstone of global environmental policy and a key component of the United Nations Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) 15 within the 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development
(
Anastas et al. 2021
)
.
Despite these efforts, global biodiversity faces critical threats, with the International Union for Conservation of Nature (IJCN) reporting over 42,100 species currently at risk of extinction
(
International Union for Conservation of Nature 2023
)
.
Effective wildlife monitoring is essential for biodiversity conservation, providing insights into ecosystem functionality, community dynamics, population ecology, and animal behavior
(
Körschens et al. 2018
)
.
Moreover, it is crucial for tracking the spatial distribution and health of endangered species and managing predator populations
(
Li et al. 2020a
)
.
For instance, New Zealand presents a compelling case study of wildlife conservation challenges.
As the Department of Conservation (DOC) reported, an estimated 25 million native birds are killed annually by invasive predators like rats, stoats, and possums
(
Department of Conservation 2023
)
.
Globally, wildlife monitoring and conservation demand an estimated annual investment of $76.1 billion USD
(
McCarthy et al. 2012
)
.

Traditional monitoring methods employed by ecologists, such as tagging, scarring, branding, and DNA analysis from biological samples, while effective, present significant drawbacks, including labor-intensive processes for field researchers, susceptibility to sensor failure, challenges in scaling to large populations, and limitations in assessing animal-environment interactions
(
Li et al. 2020a
;
Schneider et al. 2019
)
.
Recent advances in computer vision have significantly advanced wildlife monitoring with animal re-identification (Animal ReID) techniques, leveraging data collected from unmanned aerial vehicles and camera traps
(
Beery 2023
)
.
Classic computer vision-based Animal ReID methods predominantly utilize convolutional neural networks (CNNs)
(
LeCun et al. 1998
)
to extract distinguishing features from images
(
Yang et al. 2021
)
.
However, CNNs often focus on small, potentially irrelevant image regions, leading to features that are neither robust nor highly discriminative
(
Liu et al. 2021
)
.
Vision-Transformers (ViTs)
(
Dosovitskiy et al. 2021
)
have emerged as a promising alternative, offering enhanced capabilities to capture long-range dependencies across images.
Despite these advancements, existing methods depend exclusively on visual data, neglecting environmental metadata that ecologists have shown closely linked to animal behavior and identities, such as temperature and circadian rhythms
(
Koger et al. 2023
)
.
This raises a critical question: How can environmental metadata be effectively integrated with visual data to improve the performance of Animal ReID models?

We propose the Meta-Feature Adapter (MFA), a simple yet effective lightweight module designed to integrate environmental metadata into vision-language foundation models such as CLIP to enhance performance in Animal ReID tasks.
Our approach begins by translating environmental data into natural language descriptions.
For instance, circadian rhythm metadata is represented as descriptors like “day" or “night," while temperature metadata is categorized as “freezing," “cold," “chilly," “cool," “warm," or “hot"
(
Gagge et al. 1967
)
.
These descriptors are then concatenated with text descriptions of the original image to create prompts for the text encoder, such as: “A photo of a stoat Bob in cold temperature, captured during the day."
The generated prompts are processed by the text encoder and MFA to produce metadata-aware text embeddings, which are then integrated with image features via a cross-attention mechanism.
Additionally, we employ a Gated Cross-Attention mechanism
(
Zhang et al. 2024
)
, which dynamically adjusts the weighting of metadata based on the visual data, further enhancing ReID performance.

To evaluate our approach, we developed the Metadata Augmented Animal Re-identification (MAAR) dataset, a benchmark featuring six species from New Zealand with paired image data and environmental metadata.
We evaluate our approach using two protocols: (1) Intra-species re-identification
(
Varghese et al. 2023
)
, where training and testing datasets consist of disjoint individuals within the same species, and (2) cross-species re-identification
(
Heiling et al. 2016
)
, where training and testing datasets involve different species.
Our contributions are as follows:

•

We demonstrate that translating environmental metadata into natural language and integrating it with visual data can enhance performance in Animal ReID tasks.

•

We propose the Meta-Feature Adapter (MFA), a lightweight module that seamlessly integrates into existing VLM-based Animal ReID methods, incorporating environmental metadata into visual data.

•

We create and release a new benchmark dataset for Animal ReID, comprising six species with paired image data and environmental metadata, to support and advance future research in this domain.

Experiments across multiple baselines and two evaluation protocols validate the effectiveness of MFA, highlighting its potential to drive innovation in Animal ReID research and wildlife monitoring.

Related Work

Vision-Language Model

(VLM) aims to build a cohesive alignment between images and languages to learn a shared embedding space encompassing both modalities.
Recent VLMs such as CLIP
(
Radford et al. 2021
)
and ALIGN
(
Jia et al. 2021
)
have integrated two modalities through joint learning of image and text encoders in an image-text pair alignment fashion.
This approach enables the recent VLMs to generate highly informative cross-modality representations, leading to exceptional performance across various downstream tasks, including few-shot and zero-shot visual recognition
(
Chen et al. 2024b
;
Chen et al. 2024a
)
.
Furthermore, by tailoring VLMs, semantic segmentation
(
Zhang et al. 2023b
;
Li et al. 2024
)
, object detection
(
Kuckreja et al. 2024
;
Zhang et al. 2023a
)
, and domain generalization
(
Huang et al. 2023
;
Chen et al. 2024c
;
Zhao et al. 2024
)
tasks have also achieved promising performance improvements.
Despite this, the application of VLM to Animal ReID remains under explored
(
Jiao et al. 2024
)
.
While the application of VLM to person and vehicle ReID tasks has been realized
(
Li et al. 2023
)
, the challenges of Animal ReID, including attitude changes and unconstrained environments, present additional barriers.
Our work harnesses the capabilities of VLM to seamlessly integrate metadata, expressed as natural language descriptions, into visual data representations.

Animal Re-Identification.

The rapid development of deep learning
(
Huang et al. 2024
;
Yang et al. 2024
)
has made convolutional neural networks (CNNs) widely adopted for wildlife re-identification, leveraging them for feature extraction
(
Ahmed et al. 2015
;
Li et al. 2014
)
and metric learning
(
Liao et al. 2015
)
.
While person ReID technology is well researched, wildlife ReID remains in an early stage.
We can divide them into the following categories.
(1) Global Feature Learning: Building on traditional person ReID methods, many approaches use whole animal images to extract distinctive features
(
He et al. 2023
)
.
(2) Species-Specific Feature Extraction: Some wildlife have distinctive patterns, and these methods crop these specific pattern areas for local identification.
These methods are adept
at extracting discriminative features from specific body parts in a range of animal species, such as tail features of whales
(
Cheeseman et al. 2022
)
, elephant ears
(
Weideman et al. 2020
)
.
However, significant variations in perspective and partial occlusions often occur in practical applications, making capturing clear local features for each individual challenging.
(3) Auxiliary Information Integration: Pose key point estimation was leveraged to segment tiger images into seven components, facilitating local feature learning
(
Li et al. 2020a
)
.
Following this, a simplified pose definition for either side of a yak’s head, serving as an auxiliary supervisory signal to improve
feature learning
(
Zhang et al. 2021
)
.
While existing methods for Animal ReID have demonstrated effectiveness, these methods rely solely on visual data, overlooking the potential contributions of environmental metadata.
Our work is among the first to incorporate environmental metadata, demonstrating its ability to substantially enhance the performance of Animal ReID methods.

Data Fusion for Deep Learning.

The integration of data from multiple modalities in deep learning has been widely explored across various fields
(
Duan et al. 2024
;
Li et al. 2022
)
.
The goal of data fusion is to enrich contextual information, thereby improving the decision-making capabilities of models
(
Fadhel et al. 2024
)
.
The multimodal data fusion, Gated Multimodal Unit (GMU), is a specialized architecture designed for movie genre classification, where it integrates two modalities: poster images and tabular metadata
(
Arevalo et al. 2017
)
.
In health domains, researchers have demonstrated that integrating patient metadata with medical images can improve predictive accuracy
(
Pacheco and Krohling 2021
;
Li et al. 2020b
)
.
For example, the Hyper Integration Module (HIM) incorporates user-generated hint maps alongside data fusion to enhance skin lesion segmentation
(
Bi et al. 2022
)
.
Despite these advancements, metadata fusion in Animal ReID remains unexplored.
Additionally, most existing approaches directly fuse multimodal data without addressing potential alignment mismatches between modalities.
By translating metadata into natural language descriptions and leveraging the VLM, we effectively address alignment issues between visual and metadata data, providing a robust solution for multimodal fusion.

We introduce the Gated Cross-Attention mechanism, which further enhances metadata integration into visual features.
We first translate environmental metadata into natural language descriptions, which are then transformed into metadata-aware text embeddings.

Furthermore, we adopt a pre-trained Contrastive Language-Image Pretraining (CLIP)-based ReID method as the backbone, leveraging its image-text contrastive learning to address the modality misalignment problem
(
Radford et al. 2021
)
.

3.1.
Meta-Feature Adapter

Metadata Transformation
.
In this work, we incorporate three key types of metadata that influence animal appearance and behavior: temperature conditions, circadian rhythms, and face orientation.
Since numerical values often lack intuitive contextual meaning, we encode raw numerical metadata into categorical descriptors.
Temperature conditions are encoded as [freezing, cold, chilly, cool, warm, hot]
(
Gagge et al. 1967
)
, while circadian rhythms are categorized as [day, night].
Similarly, face orientation is represented as [front, back, left, right].
For each image, we construct meta-augmented prompts using a unified template: “A photo of a Stoat {individual id} in {freezing, cold, chilly, cool, warm, hot} temperature, with face direction {front, back, left, right}, captured during the {day, night}."
Once the meta-augmented prompts are generated, the text encoder of CLIP converts them into metadata-aware text embeddings, which serve as semantic anchors encoding the environmental context in a format CLIP’s image encoder understands.

Cross-Attention

We utilize a cross-attention mechanism to integrate metadata-aware text embeddings with visual features.
Unlike conventional feature concatenation or fusion methods, cross-attention enables a dynamic, context-aware integration, allowing different components of the image features to attend to relevant aspects of the environmental metadata selectively.

3.4.
Gated Cross-Attention

While cross-attention facilitates the integration of metadata-aware text embeddings with visual features, it lacks the ability to dynamically adjust the contribution of metadata based on its relevance to the given image.
The importance of metadata can vary across images.
For example, when visual features are highly distinctive across images within an identity, the model may rely less on environmental context.
Conversely, environmental metadata becomes more important when visual features are very similar across images within an identity.
To address this limitation, we employ a Gated Cross-Attention mechanism, which dynamically controls the influence of metadata features based on their relevance to each image.
Given image embeddings
I

[0,1]

(11)

\gamma=\text{Gate}(I,T_{\text{M}}^{\prime})=\sigma(\text{MLP}([I;T_{\text{M}}^{\prime}]))

4.1.
Dataset Construction

The MAAR dataset was constructed through a carefully designed process to ensure high quality and suitability for Animal ReID tasks.
This process involved metadata integration, identity annotation, image preprocessing, and dataset splitting and organization.
The detailed procedure is described below.

Metadata Integration
.
A key innovation of our dataset is the systematic integration of environmental metadata extracted from camera trap images.
We focus on three metadata features: temperature, face orientation, and circadian rhythms.
These features were selected for their direct influence on the animal appearance and behavior
(
Cade et al. 2021
;
McVey et al. 2023
)
.
Temperature affects activity patterns and may alter visible characteristics, such as fur density or coloration
(
Cossins 2012
)
.
Face orientation provides geometric context essential for feature matching in the Animal ReID tasks (Figure
4
).
Circadian rhythms significantly affect image quality and the visibility of distinguishing features.
All metadata is standardized and stored in accompanying JSON files for each image.

Identity Annotation and Image Preprocessing
.
Annotating individual animal identities is challenging and essential for ReID tasks, particularly when ground truth labels are unavailable.
We employed a combination of temporal analysis and visual verification to assign identities within each species.
For temporal analysis, we utilized the time-stamped nature of camera trap images to track animals across sequential frames.
Animals captured within short intervals (
e.g.
, a few seconds) were likely to represent the same individual.
For visual verification, distinct physical characteristics such as markings, size, and shape were used to differentiate individuals.
Species with prominent features, such as the unique plumage patterns of the yellow-eyed penguin, allowed for more straightforward identification.
Approximately 3,000 images per species were annotated, with each divided into multiple individual identities.
On average, each identity comprises approximately 120 images, ensuring sufficient data for training and evaluation.

To focus on the animals and minimize background clutter, we trained a YOLO-based detection model to automatically generate bounding boxes around the animals.
Following this automated step, manual inspection was performed to ensure the accuracy of the bounding boxes, particularly in cases involving overlapping objects, occlusions, or false detections.
Using the validated bounding boxes, we cropped the images to isolate the animals, creating a dataset decoupled from the surrounding environment.
Each image was renamed using a structured format,

This systematic naming convention facilitates efficient data management and traceability.

Dataset Splitting and Organization

The dataset was organized according to standard ReID protocols, comprising 60% for training, 25% for the gallery, and 15% for the query sets.

Baselines.

Our approach is evaluated with the state-of-the-art ReID method, CLIP-ReID
(
Li et al. 2023
)
, which adapts CLIP to human and vehicle ReID tasks and surpasses all old methods with CNN and ViTs by a large margin.
We compare with ReID-AW
(
Jiao et al. 2024
)
, a recent universal Animal ReID model designed specifically for handling arbitrary wildlife categories through dynamic visual prompts and semantic knowledge from large language models.
Additionally, we assess a baseline variant, CLIP FineTuning (CLIP-FT), which only fine-tunes the CLIP’s image encoder without extra effort.
The zero-shot ReID capabilities of CLIP (CLIP-ZS) are also examined.

Evaluation Metrics.

To evaluate the performance, we utilize two commonly used metrics in ReID tasks: mean Average Precision (mAP)
(
Zheng et al. 2015
)
and Cumulative Matching Characteristic (CMC)
(
Wang et al. 2007
)

mAP evaluates the retrieval performance by calculating the average precision across all queries.
CMC measures the rate at which the correct match is within the
k
-nearest neighbors, revealing the number of images that have to be examined to achieve a desired level of performance.
In our evaluation, we report the accuracy of the CMC-1.
Performance measures are averaged over ten runs, along with their corresponding 95% confidence intervals.

Evaluation Protocol.

We evaluate our approach using two protocols: (1) Intra-species ReID
(
Varghese et al. 2023
)
, where training and testing datasets consist of disjoint individuals within the same species, and (2) cross-species ReID
(
Heiling et al. 2016
)
, where training and testing datasets involve different species.
While most existing Animal ReID studies focus solely on intra-species validation, the cross-species protocol is crucial for assessing real-world applicability.
In practical wildlife monitoring, resource constraints often make it prohibitively expensive and time-consuming to train specialized models for every additional species
(
Jiao et al. 2024
)
.
Additionally, cross-species evaluation offers deeper insights into the framework’s ability to capture fundamental features that transcend species-specific characteristics.

Reproducibility Details.

We implement MFA using PyTorch, with ViT-B/16 as the backbone network.
The models are trained using the Adaptive Moment Estimation (Adam) optimizer, configured with a momentum of 0.9 and a weight decay of
1\times 10^{-4}
.
The initial learning rate is set to
1\times 10^{-6}
, and the learning rate decays by a factor of 0.1 every 10 epochs.
Training is conducted for 50 epochs with a batch size of 16.
All images are resized to
256\times 128
.
Experiments are conducted on NVIDIA Tesla A100 GPUs.

Ablation Study

Impact of Fusion Strategies.

To examine how different strategies for integrating metadata with visual features affect ReID performance, we compare three fusion approaches.
The basic fusion (BF) simply concatenates the metadata embeddings with visual features followed by a linear projection layer.
Cross-attention (CA) enables feature interaction by computing attention weights between metadata and visual features.
Our proposed gated cross-attention (GCA) further implemented an adaptive gating mechanism to control the influence of metadata based on visual content.
Table
4
, shows that basic fusion provides modest improvements (
e.g.
, 1.5% mAP gain on Deer for CLIP-FT), but cross-attention strategy outperforms basic fusion by enabling selective feature integration, with particularly notable gains on datasets such as Penguin (0.3% and 1.3% mAP improvements for CLIP-FT and CLIP-ReID respectively).
The gated cross-attention mechanism further enhances performance across all datasets, achieving the best results with improvements of up to 2.5% mAP over basic fusion.
This pattern is also observed in the ReID-AW model, where GCA achieves substantial improvements over BF across all species (
e.g.
, 2.2% mAP gain on Deer and 2.8% on Penguin).
This performance can be attributed to its ability to adaptively adjust metadata influence based on visual content relevance.
For instance, when visual features are highly distinctive, the gate can reduce reliance on metadata while increasing metadata influence for visually ambiguous cases.

In order to investigate the contribution of each metadata feature and identify potential interactions between different metadata combinations, we progressively integrate three types of metadata: temperature (T), circadian rhythm (C), and face orientation (F) on CLIP-FT+MFA, CLIP-ReID+MFA, and ReID-AW+MFA models.
Table

presents the results.
The results show that temperature contributes most to ReID performance across all models, achieving the highest mAP.
This aligns with zoological research showing that temperature significantly influences animal behavior and appearance
(
Shermeister et al. 2024
)
.
As the temperature fluctuates, animals may exhibit variations in posture, movement patterns, and even fur characteristics, providing additional discriminative features for ReID.
Moreover, different individuals often show distinct temperature preferences and behavioral adaptations
(
Lagerspetz and Vainio 2006
)
, making temperature-related features valuable for ReID tasks.
When combining all three metadata types (T+C+F), we observe the best performance across all datasets, suggesting that each metadata type contributes complementary information.

6.
Conclusion

This paper proposed the Meta-Feature Adapter (MFA), a novel module designed to integrate environmental metadata with visual data, enhancing Animal ReID performance for wildlife conservation.
By transforming metadata into natural language descriptions and leveraging vision-language models, MFA effectively incorporates metadata into visual features.
Additionally, we developed a Gated Cross-Attention mechanism that dynamically adjusts the contribution of metadata, further improving re-identification performance.
To support and advance future research in Animal ReID, we curated the Metadata Augmented Animal Re-identification (MAAR) dataset, which includes six species with paired image data and environmental metadata.
We envisage that this research will benefit the Animal ReID research community and contribute to global wildlife conservation efforts.
As future work, we will explore how MFA can leverage the strong ability of independent large-scale image and language models, such as GPT, and reduce reliance on pre-trained multimodal models with image-text alignment.
