Promoter identity shapes splicing outcomes and fidelity
Authors: Shaked Shanas, Anton M. Schwartz, Noa Nesher, Avital Melnik, Martin Mikl
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Gene expression is a complex process subject to regulation at multiple functionally interconnected levels. One prominent example is the crosstalk between transcription and splicing regulation. Past work has shown that transcription can influence splicing in multiple ways, but a systematic investigation of this complex interplay is lacking. Here we employ massively parallel reporter assays of large combinatorial promoter-splice site libraries to dissect how promoter identity and transcription dynamics affect alternative splicing in human cells. We find that promoter identity, rather than expression level, exerts strong and highly context-specific effects on cassette exon inclusion, exceeding the effect of pharmacological inhibitors of transcription initiation or elongation. Groups of exons display coordinated promoter-dependent splicing behavior, and we identified predictive sequence and structural features underlying this sensitivity. Promoter and gene architecture also shape isoform diversity by modulating cryptic splice site usage. These findings present promoters as central regulators of splicing outcomes and fidelity.

Introduction

Eukaryotic gene expression is a complex process that is amenable to regulation and fine-tuning at multiple levels, but also prone to disruptions. The majority of disease-associated mutations are synonymous or lie in non-coding regions1 and might affect expression of the gene rather than its amino acid sequence. To understand the functional consequence of sequence changes in non-coding regions, we need a thorough understanding of the rules underlying gene expression. Systematic attempts to achieve this have mostly focused on regulatory layers in isolation. In the context of the functioning of a cell or an organism, however, all aspects of gene expression are interconnected and ultimately part of one process aimed at carrying out a defined function. Accumulating evidence suggests that different regulatory levels are functionally interconnected2–18 and that the molecular machineries catalyzing each reaction also interact physically19–27.

One prominent, but poorly understood example is the crosstalk between transcription initiation and splicing regulation. Transcription rate has been shown to influence splicing decisions6,28–31, but which aspects of transcriptional regulation affect splicing is still largely unknown. Promoter properties, and not simply promoter strength, have been shown to influence the level of exon inclusion5. Constitutive splicing efficiency was shown to increase with transcription rate, amplifying the expression of strongly transcribed genes12. Elongation speed of RNA polymerase II (Pol II) affects some alternative splicing events but not others32, as well as promoter proximal pausing2. Especially the C-terminal domain of Pol II has been shown to interact with the splicing machinery and recruit different trans-acting factors, in vivo20,22,23,26,27 and in vitro33,34. Alternative promoters can be associated with different alternative splicing patterns of the transcript3,7. Both GC content7 as well as transcriptional activators35,36 have been implicated in this differential effect on splicing. Splicing can also feed back to promoter usage8,9,37, demonstrating that the crosstalk between transcription and splicing regulation is bidirectional.

Large-scale testing of rationally designed or random sequence libraries, often in combination with machine learning approaches, has immensely contributed to elucidating the regulatory grammar of transcription38–42, splicing10,43–46, polyadenylation47,48, RNA stability49 and localization50–52, miRNA-mediated regulation53, non-canonical mechanisms of translation54,55, and RNA export18,56, demonstrating the power and universal applicability of such approaches. Although they have become an experimental pillar of studies in gene regulation, a dedicated high-throughput systematic attempt to dissect the interplay between transcription and splicing is still lacking.

Here, we take a systematic approach to assess the interplay between transcription and splicing, employing massively parallel splicing assays of libraries of synthetic mini-genes carrying designed promoter and splice site regions. Varying sequences regulating transcription initiation on the one hand and splicing on the other hand in a combinatorial and systematic manner allows identifying splicing events sensitive to promoter identity and sequence features underlying this regulation.

Results

Promoter identity affects exon inclusion

To systematically assess the effect of promoter identity and pharmacological interference with transcription on splicing outcomes, we built on a synthetic oligonucleotide library previously developed in the lab10. The sequences, 210 nt in length, contain an exon that may or may not be included and its flanking introns. They are based on 134 endogenous human cassette exons, 23–89 bp in length (Supplementary Fig. 1A) selected to span a wide range of exon inclusion rates (Supplementary Data 1) and contain either the wild -type sequences or systematic sequence variations (Supplementary Fig. 1B, Supplementary Data 2, Methods). These variations typically change larger parts of the sequence and include recoding of exonic sequences, replacing intronic and exonic parts, insertion of potential splicing factor binding sites, deletions, secondary structure variations and others. Consequently, the variants in the resulting sequence library are different from their corresponding wild type sequence in on average 22.5 (median = 11) positions (Supplementary Fig. 1C) out of 147 bp of variable sequence, depending on the library subset (Supplementary Fig. 1D). The sequence library was cloned into a GFP-expressing mini gene under the control of a strong human promoter (EF1-alpha (pEF1α)), embedded between constant upstream and downstream exonic and intronic regions taken from the MCL1 gene where they flank exon 2 that can be included or skipped, thus providing a sequence context that endogenously allows facultative inclusion or skipping of the exon in between (Fig. 1A, Supplementary Fig. 1E). A 12 nt barcode was part of each library variant and included to allow unambiguous identification of the library variant from a spliced mRNA. The resulting plasmid library was transfected into HEK293 cells, RNA was isolated, and reporter RNA was sequenced to determine the splicing outcome of each of the 9608 library cassette exons. To allow annotation-free mapping of the sequencing reads to the genome, we developed a computational pipeline based on demultiplexing of the reads based on the unique barcode of the 9608 exons tested, which is included in the sequencing output irrespective of the splicing outcome, followed by individual mapping using STAR to each library mini-gene in isolation to prevent mapping errors due to the presence of highly similar library sequences (see Methods). Based on the resulting processed data (Supplementary Data 3), exon inclusion rates were calculated as percent spliced in (PSI) for all library variants carrying a cassette exon (exon included and exon skipped isoforms, Methods). Like in the case of endogenous cassette exons57,58 and previous measurements of these cassette exon sequences in a different experimental system10, the distribution of PSI values was bimodal, with one splicing outcome dominating over the other in the majority of cases (Fig. 1B, Supplementary Fig. 2A). No significant correlation could be observed between expression level and PSI (Supplementary Fig. 2B). To determine the effect of impaired transcription initiation or elongation on cassette exon inclusion, we treated the cells 6 h after transfection with compounds affecting different aspects of transcription (the PolII inhibitor α-Amanitin (AMA), the DNA intercalating agents Actinomycin D (ActD) and Doxorubicin (DOXO) and the topoisomerase I inhibitor Camptothecin (CPT) which slows down Pol II transcription elongation and can affect alternative exon skipping59) and measured their effect on reporter splicing 18 h later as described above (Fig. 1A). We tested two different concentrations of each compound that would only partially affect cell viability (Supplementary Fig. 2C). A reduction in the percentage of GFP-positive cells was visibile in the case of AMA and ActD, two widely used transcription inhibitors (Supplementary Fig. 2D). Principal component analysis (PCA) of PSI values showed that AMA and ActD samples clustered closely to each other (Fig. 1C), while targeting transcription elongation using CPT showed the largest difference to the control.

To determine the effect of promoter identity and expression level on cassette exon inclusion, we cloned the splicing reporter library under the control of additional promoters frequently used for the expression of transgenes in human cells (Fig. 1A, Supplementary Data 4): the chicken beta actin promoter (pACTB) as well as the full CMV promoter including distal enhancers (pCMV-full) and two truncated versions of the CMV promoter, one containing only the proximal region without the distal enhancers (pCMV-short) and one containing only the minimal core promoter (min. pCMV). The shorter CMV variants were contained within pCMV-full, such that they did not differ in their sequence starting from the TATA box at the proximal end of the CMV promoter and consequently had similar transcription start sites (Supplementary Fig. 2E). Therefore, any differences in RNA processing can be expected to be due to differences in upstream promoter or enhancer sequences. The CMV promoter truncations showed reduced expression levels compared to pCMV-full (Fig. 1D), with pCMV-short resulting in expression levels comparable to pEF1α and pACTB. The five resulting plasmid libraries were transfected into HEK293 cells and samples were processed as described above and in the Methods. Following mapping and filtering steps for minimal number of reads per library sequence (>100 across all samples unless indicated otherwise), the resulting filtered datasets contained readouts for 7048–7803 splicing reporters, including 5653 variants for which we obtained measurements based on more than 100 reads in all 20 samples. Including the promoter variants of the splice site libraries in the principal component analysis showed that PSI values clustered predominantly based on promoter identity, with the different pharmacologial treatments clustering according to their promoter (pEF1α, Fig. 1E). To determine whether the observed clustering by promoter identity was due to indirect effects associated with underlying differences in the transcriptomic profiles, we also performed RNA-sequencing of poly-adenylated mRNA from the same 10 RNA samples (Supplementary Data 5; Methods). Correlations between transcript abundances were similar across samples (Supplementary Fig. 2F), and no clustering according to the transfected construct could be observed in RNA-seq data (Supplementary Fig. 2G) as opposed to the PSI readouts of the reporter libraries (Supplementary Fig. 2H), with significant differences between within-group (“inner”) compared to between-group (“outer”) distances in the PSI-based but not in the RNA-seq-based clustering (Supplementary Fig. 2I).

In concordance with the PCA results, analyzing the correlation between PSI values of all reporter constructs across experimental conditions showed higher concordance between samples sharing the same promoter driving expression of the splicing reporter mini-genes (Fig. 1F, Pearson’s r > 0.99 for all replicates, r = 0.950–0.987 across pharmacological treatments and r = 0.908–0.968 across promoter constructs). Comparing per-exon (Supplementary Fig. 3A) as well as mean PSI changes (ΔPSI, Fig. 1G) in the different drug treatments and promoter variants compared to the pEF1α promoter showed that changing the promoter was associated with larger differences in exon inclusion rates than the drug treatments tested here (4.9–9.3% mean absolute PSI changes in different promoter constructs compared to 0.3–4.6% in pharmacological treatments; corrected p < 10−55 (Mann-Whitney-U, Bonferroni) for all comparisons between promoters and pharmacological treatments except for pACTB vs. CPT). Since PSI changes are sensitive to the starting PSI value, we repeated this comparison using the log2 ratio of exon-included vs. skipped isoforms10 as well as applying a non-monotonic mutation effect analysis58,60, which largely confirmed the trends observed in the ΔPSI-based analysis (Supplementary Fig. 3B, Methods). Taken together, these data suggest that promoter identity can have a stronger effect on cassette exon inclusion rates than pharmacological perturbation of transcription.

The genomic environment and chromatin context can influence both transcription and alternative splicing29. Our splicing reporter constructs were expressed episomally by transient transfection, which does not resemble a native genomic environment. To test whether different promoters are associated with different splicing outcomes also when expressed from a genomic location, we constructed reporter libraries driven by pEF1α or the full-length CMV promoter and integrated them site-specifically into the AAVS1 locus in HEK293 cells (Methods). PSI values of the integrated reporter library driven by pCMV-full showed better correlation with the transiently transfected splicing reporters compared to the pEF1α reporters integrated into the same genomic location (Supplementary Fig. 4AB, Supplementary Data 3). In the PCA, the two promoter variants were separated along the first principal component accounting for 85.4% of the variance compared to the integrated-episomal divide along the second principal component (8.9% explained variance, Supplementary Fig. 4C). In conclusion, while the genomic environment will affect the readout of our assay, these results suggest that promoter identity can affect splicing outcomes also when expressed from a controlled genomic location.

Dynamically regulated cassette exons are sensitive to promoter identity

To specifically interrogate the effect of pharmacological treatments and promoter identity on wild-type cassette exon sequences not carrying any designed sequence alterations, we analyzed pairwise correlations between PSI values in this subset (Fig. 2A; 2 × 134 variants). Biological replicates of the same promoter showed minimal variability (Supplementary Fig. 5A; r = 0.99). ActD and AMA showed a trend of higher rates of inclusion across exons tested (Fig. 2A), while targeting elongation using CPT led to more heterogenous changes in splicing behavior (Fig. 2A). Different promoters generally had exon-specific effects on inclusion rates, with CMV promoter variants exhibiting more exon skipping (Fig. 2A). To gauge the experimental variability, we tested multiple library variants with the same sequence of the cassette exon and flanking introns, but varying barcodes (Supplementary Fig. 5B). Promoter-dependent differences in PSI were most apparent in native cassette exon reporters with intermediate inclusion levels. We therefore categorized potentially included cassette exons based on the distribution of PSI values into binary (PSI < 10% or >80%) vs. non-binary splicing events (10% < PSI < 80%). Median inclusion rates of wild-type non-binary cassette exons were lowest when expressed under the control of the pCMV-full promoter and highest when expressed from the pEF1α promoter (Fig. 2B). In line with the expectation that clear-cut (=binary) splicing outcomes are more hard-coded and less easily affected by additional regulatory inputs than intermediate splicing outcomes, we observed a larger variability in PSI values across promoters in exons classified as non-binary compared to those classified as binary (Fig. 2C). Furthermore, promoter identity only rarely led to flip-like changes in splicing behavior across all library variants (<0.25% of sequences for each pairwise comparison).

To test whether the sensitivity of non-binary splicing events to promoter identity was a consequence of the larger effect size of changes in splicing efficiency on intermediate PSI values58,60, we applied three alternative approaches to quantify the consequence of changes in promoter identity on binary vs. non-binary cassette exons: the absolute difference in PSI between two conditions, the abolute difference in the log ratio of included vs. skipped isoform abundance and a mutation effect analysis58,60. We compared these three approaches based on the experimental variability (i.e. the difference between pairs of biological replicates) in binary vs. non-binary exons, using all highly diverse sequence variants in the library (>50/147 positions different from the corresponding reference sequence): While delta PSI showed significantly higher values for non-binary exons (Supplementary Fig. 6A), the mutation effect analysis (log2(A)) showed the inverse trend, probably due to the larger technical variability associated with non-binary cases where, by definition, one isoform is rare and absolute changes in read numbers of the minor isoform will have a larger impact on the calculated effect size. Log2(exon included/exon skipped) showed no systematic differences between the two groups across technical replicates and independently on whether all highly variable sequences or only the subset of endogenous sequences was used (Supplementary Fig. 6A). We calculated the difference in log2(exon included/exon skipped) for all pairwise comparisons of promoter constructs separately for binary and non-binary exons and observed larger effects in non-binary exons for the comparison between pEF1α and CMV promoter variants, in particular min. pCMV (Fig. 2DE; Supplementary Fig. 6B, top). This effect was also observed on the whole library (Fig. 2F), when restricting the analysis to endogenous sequences (Fig. 2G) and in the effect size analysis (Fig. 2H, Supplementary Fig. 6B, bottom). This suggests that – at least in the case of some promoters – the effect of changes in promoter identity on cassette exon inclusion is stronger for non-binary, intermediate splicing events.

To test whether the different sensitivity of cassette exons to promoter identity is also reflected in the splicing behavior in the native context, we compared the endogenous PSI variability as the range between minimal and maximal PSI values across tissues (based on VastDB61). Cassette exons with higher variability between promoters in our assay (as determined by the range of PSI values; “high”: >50th percentile of the distribution) also exhibited higher variability between tissues in endogenous RNA-seq data (Fig. 2I, p = 0.031, Mann-Whitney-U test), with no difference in mean PSI (Supplementary Fig. 6C), suggesting that splicing events more sensitive to promoter identity are also more strongly affected by cell type-specific regulatory inputs.

Promoter identity can bias overall exon inclusion rates

To determine whether drug treatments and promoter identity had a general positive or negative effect on cassette exon inclusion rates, we computed the mean and median PSI across all cassette exons for which we obtained high-confidence measurements in all 20 experimental conditions (Fig. 3A, Supplementary Fig. 7A). All drug treatments led to higher rates of cassette exon inclusion on average (Fig. 3A, Supplementary Fig. 7AB). While the pACTB reporter constructs, which, like in the case of the pEF1α construct, contain an intron in the 5’UTR, did not substantially alter mean PSI compared to pEF1α, all three intronless constructs (pCMV-full, pCMV-short and min. pCMV) resulted in lower mean exon inclusion levels (Fig. 3A, Supplementary Fig. 7AB), suggesting that the presence of an upstream constitutive splicing event might shift splicing decisions to more exon inclusion. Accordingly, the mean effect size of replacing pEF1α with pACTB was lower than comparing any of the CMV truncation constructs (Fig. 1F). To directly test the effect of an additional upstream intron on inclusion rates of a downstream cassette exon, we constructed versions of the pCMV-full promoter which included either the same intron as the EF1α promoter construct or the beta-globin intron frequently used in transgene expression. We cloned our reporter library into these constructs and tested their splicing behavior in HEK293 cells as described above. The overall distribution of PSI scores (Supplementary Fig. 7C) and the mean PSI values (Fig. 3B) in reporter libraries driven by pCMV-full with an additional intron were not significantly different from the pCMV-full construct without an upstream intron. Comparison of PSI scores across the reporter library showed that the pCMV-full variants with and without additional upstream intron showed higher correlation among themselves (Fig. 3C, Supplementary Fig. 7D) and clustered together in a PCA plot (Supplementary Fig. 7E). The mean effect size of changing the promoter from pEF1α to one of the CMV variants with intron was higher than the one for pCMV-full without intron, including the variant containing the same intron as the pEF1α promoter (Fig. 3D). To identify more subtle trends shaping exon inclusion in the context of different promoter variants or pharmacological treatments, we binned the measurements by PSI value and compared the fraction of exons falling into each bin. We observed that most constructs showed peaks of PSI values between 80 and 90%, indicating that in these contexts cassette exon inclusion rates >90% are not commonly observed. In contrast, the PSI distribution of predominantly included exons driven by a minimal CMV promoter was shifted towards the highest bin (90-100% exon inclusion, Fig. 3E, Supplementary Fig. 7F). This behavior was phenocopied by ActD treatment (Fig. 3F, Supplementary Fig. 7F), suggesting that a low rate of transcription initiation could be beneficial to achieve more complete exon inclusion.

Analysis of cassette exon inclusion rates typically, and also in the case of this study, relies on steady-state measurements of isoform abundance. In principle, steady state levels are affected both by the rate of production and the rate of degradation. To test whether differential stability of the two isoforms – exon included and exon skipped – influenced our PSI measurements in particular in the case of drugs interfering with transcription, we used library variants with donor and acceptor splice site mutations as well as addition of exonic splice enhancer and silencer elements. These mutations strongly biased exon inclusion, but did not exhibit differences in expression levels (Supplementary Fig. 8). While differential stability could affect relative isoform abundances in specific cases, these results suggest that they do not constitute a systematic bias between isoforms with exon included vs. isoforms with exon skipped.

Groups of exons show similar dependencies on promoter identity

As an exploratory approach to identify common patterns of promoter-dependent splicing behavior, we clustered 5653 cassette exons for which we obtained measurements in all samples into six groups based on their PSI values across promoter constructs (Fig. 4A, Supplementary Fig. 9A) and confirmed that wild-type sequences resembled the clustering based on promoter (Supplementary Fig. 9B). Mean exon inclusion rates differed across clusters and promoters (Fig. 4B). Clustering exons based on all experimental conditions, including the pharmacological treatments, did not substantially change the clustering behavior, with the different promoter constructs being the dominant drivers of clustering (Supplementary Fig. 9C). Cassette exons in clusters 1 and 2 showed changed exon inclusion rates specifically when expressed from the minimal CMV promoter, such as in the case of the wild-type sequences for RBM10 exon 6 (Fig. 4C, top; cluster 1, higher rate of exon inclusion in min. pCMV compared to other promoters) and COMMD9 exon 4 (Fig. 4C, bottom; cluster 2, lower rate of exon inclusion in min. pCMV). Next, we asked whether the groups of exons identified by the clustering were associated with measurable sequence features and therefore represented biologically meaningful distinctions rather than arbitrary partitions of the data. We used gradient boosting decision trees (XGBoost) for multi-class classification of promoter-dependent splicing behavior based on exon and splice site features (Methods) and scored the ability of the model to discriminate each group from the rest (Fig. 4D). Clusters 1 and 2 showed the best performance, and the distinction between cluster 1 and 2 reached the highest prediction accuracy (ROC AUC = 0.86). The ability to predict the exon group from independent sequence features not used for defining the clusters indicates that the clustering captures reproducible differences in exon features rather than reflecting stochastic variation in PSI values. To confirm that sequence similarity did not bias the clustering and prediction, we repeated the training and testing of a classifier using only highly diverse sequence variants (>50/147 positions different from the corresponding reference sequence; ~20% of the library). The resulting prediction accuracies were similar compared to the prediction based on the full library (Supplementary Fig. 9E), with the distinction between clusters 1 and 2 again showing the best performance (ROC AUC = 0.87). Analysis of the feature importances driving the prediction revealed exon length as the most important feature for the classification, followed by cassette exon splice site strength and secondary structure features (Fig. 4E). Accordingly, exon lengths were also significantly different between endogenous cassette exon across the six groups (Fig. 4F, p = 0.022, Kruskal-Wallis H-test). In particular, exon length was a distinguishing feature between clusters 1 and 2, for which min. pCMV showed opposing effects on exon inclusion rates (corr. p = 0.023, Dunn’s test, Benjamini-Hochberg correction), which also included the examples shown in Fig. 4C (RBM10: 74 bp; COMMD9: 35 bp).

To specifically investigate the hypothesis arising from the clustering analyses that exon length contributes to promoter sensitivity, we took advantage of a designed library subset which includes systematic shortening of 23 exons by deleting 6 bp either at the center or towards the beginning or the end of an exon. Exon shortening typically led to a drop in PSI score (Fig. 4G), and this drop appeared more pronounced in min. pCMV compared to pEF1α. To quantify this effect and to account for the different sensitivity across the range of PSI values, we computed the effect size (log2(A)) of each such mutation in all five promoters (Fig. 4H). In line with the analysis of the clustering approach, the sensitivity to exon shortening varied across promoters (p = 0.023, Kruskal-Wallis), with splicing reporters driven by the minimal pCMV promoter exhibiting the strongest reduction upon exon shortening. Taken together, these results suggest that exon length can be a factor underlying the differential sensitivity of exons to promoter identity.

The effect of splicing factor motifs varies between promoters

To identify sequence features associated with the promoter-response patterns identified above, we analyzed enrichment of RBP binding motifs across clusters. We calculated cumulative RBP binding motif scores (Fig. 5A; normalized for sequence length, see Methods) separately for the upstream intronic, exonic, and downstream intronic regions of the variable sequence based on CISBP-RNA62. These binding scores are not binary values of presence or absence of the perfect fit of a motif, but quantitative measures of similarity to the motif. We observed that for the majority of exonic and intronic motifs, matches were found in almost all variant families (library sequences derived from a shared endogenous sequence; Supplementary Fig. 10A). We also confirmed that clustering did not simply lead to grouping based on the original endogenous sequence, and each cluster contained library sequences from multiple variant families (Supplementary Fig. 10B). 241 out of 294 location-specific motif scores showed significant differences in normalized motif scores between the clusters (Kruskal-Wallis, corr. p-value < 0.05 (Bonferroni)). In pairwise comparisons of motif enrichment between clusters, clusters 1 and 2 showed the highest number of differentially enriched motifs (Fig. 5B), with cluster 2 displaying the strongest enrichment across the largest number of motifs relative to other clusters (Supplementary Fig. 10C, top; Methods). Correlation with RBP binding scores was higher for the PSI values measured for the minimal CMV promoter compared to other promoter constructs (Fig. 5C, p < 0.05, Mann-Whitney-U), indicating that motif presence was more predictive of the splicing outcome in the case of the shortest and weakest promoter. We used the standard deviation of Spearman correlation coefficients across samples as a measure for the variability of the effect of each motif on PSI and compared this measure to the most significant enrichment between clusters. 33/43 motifs in the top 25 percent in both measures were in the upstream intronic region (Fig. 5D) and showed a stronger negative correlation between RBP motif scores and exon inclusion rates in the minimal CMV promoter compared to all others (Supplementary Fig. 10E), while most exonic motifs did not show differences in their effect on cassette exon inclusion across promoters. To minimize the potential for spurious correlations because of the presence of similar variants, we repeated this analysis for a subset of highly variable sequences (>50/147 positions different from the corresponding reference sequence; ~20% of the library). While the absolute number of motifs passing our statistical threshold was lower, also due to the lower number of sequences included, the overall pattern remained the same, with upstream intronic regions showing higher variability between promoters (Supplementary Fig. 10F). In the case of potential FXR1 and KHDRBS3 binding sites in the upstream intronic region, the correlation between binding motifs and exon inclusion levels showed opposite directions in min. pCMV compared to other promoter constructs (Supplementary Fig. 10E). To analyze the effect of these RBP motifs on cassette exon inclusion in the different promoter constructs, we computed mean motif scores for different bins of PSI levels. Applying this approach to RBPs with well-established effects on exon inclusion showed the expected association between high exonic SRSF2 with high inclusion levels and vice versa. An inverse relationship was observed for HNRNPC binding scores, illustrating the opposing effects of SR proteins and hnRNPs on exon inclusion (Fig. 5E, Supplementary Fig. 11A). In the case of FXR1 and KHDRBS3, high motif scores in the upstream intronic region were associated with intermediate exon inclusion levels (Fig. 5F), with a marked shift towards lower PSI score when the splicing reporters were expressed under the control of a minimal CMV promoter. This effect was not observed in the downstream intronic region and only weakly in the exon (Supplementary Fig. 11B).

To directly measure the promoter-dependence of the effect of insertion of a splicing factor binding motif, we analyzed a subset of our library which contained sequence variants in which motifs were inserted at different exonic and intronic positions in multiple endogenous sequence contexts (Fig. 5G, top; Methods). The effect size of motif insertions differed between the promoter constructs and was highest in the minimal CMV promoter (Fig. 5G), in line with the results of our correlation analysis (Fig. 5C). Investigating the effect of specific well-known splicing factors showed that insertion of hnRNPA1 motifs had the expected negative effect on exon inclusion rates, especially in the exon itself, with no difference between the promoters (Fig. 5H). Insertion of an SRSF1 or SRSF6 motif in the exon generally led to increased inclusion rates (Fig. 5IJ), with the exception of SRSF1 in pACTB. The strength of the negative effect when found in the surrounding introns varied between promoters. The effect of hnRNPG motifs strongly depended on insertion site and promoter identity (Fig. 5K), although most comparisons remained below the threshold for statistical significance, partially also because of variability in the effect based on the exact location of the insertion (Methods). Taken together, these results indicate that the activity of specific splicing regulatory motifs can depend on promoter identity.

Promoter identity affects splice site choice and splicing fidelity

Up to this point, our analysis focused on the difference in the rate of exon inclusion, yielding one of two outcomes: exon inclusion or exon skipping. However, the position of donor and acceptor sites could vary, leading to the production of more than two isoforms from the same reporter construct. Our mapping process was not limited to the expected splicing events but also allowed for the detection of cryptic splice sites. Consequently, more than two isoforms can be detected (as illustrated in the splicing maps shown in Fig. 6A, with each colored line representing the splicing events observed in a specific isoform). The number of exon sequences yielding more than two isoforms changed between promoters (Fig. 4B, p < 0.01 for 2, 3 and >3 isoform groups, chi-squared test). In particular, the full CMV promoter and the CMV promoter lacking distal enhancers (pCMV-short) were associated with a larger number of isoforms. Annotation-free mapping revealed a large number of cryptic splice sites both in the variable and constant regions upstream and downstream of the cassette exon (Fig. 6CD, Supplementary Fig. 13A). These cryptic splice sites resembled canonical splice site sequences (Supplementary Fig. 13B) and were located at local maxima of donor and acceptor splice site strength (based on MaxEntScan63, Supplementary Fig. 13C). We quantified the occurrence (Fig. 6E, left) and overall frequency (Fig. 6E, right) of cryptic splicing events in the different promoter constructs and observed marked differences in the frequency of cryptic splicing events between promoters and contexts. Cryptic splice sites around the constant upstream donor site (position 88) were used more frequently in the pCMV full and short promoters, specifically when the cassette exon was skipped (alt. upstream donor (CE included), Fig. 6E, F). In cases where the cassette exon was included, use of cryptic upstream donors was less common and not enhanced in specific promoter contexts (alt. upstream donor (CE skipped), Fig. 6E, Supplementary Fig. 14A). Retention of the first, shorter intron occurred more frequently under the control of the CMV full and short promoters (Fig. 6EG, top and middle panels) across sequence contexts (Supplementary Fig. 14B). In addition, under the control of the minimal CMV promoter, the isoform with the first intron retained accounted for a smaller fraction of the transcripts produced (Supplementary Fig. 14BC). Cryptic splice site usage at the downstream acceptor was rare in general, but occurred at higher frequency in the min. pCMV promoter context, irrespective of exon inclusion or skipping (alt. downstream acceptor, Fig. 6E, Supplementary Fig. 14D–F).

Splice site choice in and around the designed library sequence containing the cassette exon was particularly variable, and one cryptic donor site downstream of the expected splice site at position 529 was used in a large number of sequence contexts (Fig. 6C). The frequency of use of alternative cassette exon donor sites was strongly increased by retention of the upstream intron (alt. CE donor, Fig. 6E, right) and expression from pCMV full or short promoters. Upon retention of the first intron, the donor of the cassette exon becomes the first splice site on the transcript. The higher frequency of cryptic donor use observed here and at the upstream splice site (Fig. 6F) indicates that the fidelity of recognition of the first donor site on a transcript is compromised in the two CMV promoter variants lacking an upstream splicing event. In contrast, when expressed from a minimal CMV promoter, the frequency of cryptic splice site use is reduced compared to all other promoter constructs (Fig. 6EF), suggesting that low expression can counteract the low fidelity of “first donor”-recognition. To test if addition of an upstream intron could rescue the low fidelity of “first donor”-recognition, we analyzed our pCMV-full reporter constructs with or without an additional intron. We observed that in the CMV-full variants with an additional upstream intron, the percentage of isoforms with cryptic donor usage at the cassette exon decreased specifically when the first intron was retained, but not when it was spliced out (Supplementary Fig. 14G). In contrast, an upstream intron did not prevent cryptic splice site usage around the strong donor site in the upstream constant region (AAG-GUAAGG at position 88, Supplementary Fig. 14H). These results point at a specific vulnerability of weaker donor sites in the case of no upstream splicing when expressed from a strong (CMV) promoter.

Constant cryptic acceptor sites upstream of the variable sequence containing the cassette exon exhibited particularly strong dependence on promoter identity, with some positions showing widely different usage across promoter contexts (e.g. at position 313: Fig. 6G, middle, Fig. 6HI; or position 277: Fig. 6G, bottom, Fig. 6J; Supplementary Fig. 14I). Overall, acceptor choice was only weakly correlated with the strength of the canonical acceptor across the variable cassette exon sequences (Fig. 6K). In contrast, the strength of the downstream donor site was significantly correlated with canonical acceptor usage and anticorrelated with cryptic acceptor usage, suggesting that efficient recognition of a downstream donor site aided recognition of preceding acceptor sites in the context of our mini-genes, in line with regulation of inclusion or skipping via exon definition.

Endogenous human promoter sequences can bias splicing outcomes

More than 50% of human genes are expressed from multiple promoters64,65. To test whether features of promoter dependence of cassette exon inclusion rates can also be observed in endogenous human promoters, we selected four promoter regions from two human genes (Fig. 7A): two alternative promoters in the p73 gene66,67 (pTP73-1 and −2, Supplementary Fig. 15A), which have been shown to give rise to two isoforms with opposite function68 and two promoters in MYC67 (pMYC-1 and −2), with pMYC-1 overlapping both main tandem transcription start sites and pMYC-2, a weak alternative promoter region (Supplementary Fig. 15B), giving rise to a protein variant with a short N-terminal truncation. The promoter regions (430-1300 bp in length) were selected in a way that they would include the annotated transcription start site and CAGE peak (Supplementary Fig. 15AB), while avoiding any splice sites, and cloned in our reporter constructs upstream of the cassette exon library. We transfected the resulting reporter libraries into HEK293 cells and performed the experimental and computational pipeline to determine splicing outcomes as described above for the original set of promoters. To map the transcription start site, we performed 5’RACE and observed one major band for each promoter (Supplementary Fig. 15C). In the case of pMYC-1, which contains two potential transcription start sites, the length of the 5’RACE product corresponded to the proximal one. We measured the expression level of the different promoter constructs using flow cytometry and found that pMYC-1 showed the highest and pMYC-2 the lowest expression level among the endogenous promoter constructs (Fig. 7B), in accordance with the endogenous CAGE data (Supplementary Fig. 15AB). Cassette exon library variants expressed from the four endogenous promoters showed similar overall distributions of PSI values (Supplementary Fig. 15D) and similar mean PSI values (Supplementary Fig. 15E), which were higher than the (equally intron-less) CMV promoter variants. The expression level of the promoter did not show a correlation with mean PSI values across all ten promoters tested (Fig. 7C, p > 0.05). When restricting this analysis to CMV promoter variants, which span a wide range of expression levels while keeping the transcribed sequence and the region around the transcription start site constant, we did not observe significant differences between the mean PSI (corr. p > 0.05 for all comparisons, Mann-Whitney-U test). In conclusion, expression level alone does not have a strong biological or technical impact on mean exon inclusion level in our experimental setup.

Comparing PSI values between the four endogenous promoters tested showed evidence for an effect of promoter identity on splicing readouts, with larger variability in PSI values between promoter constructs compared to replicate experiments for the same promoter, both at the level of the entire library (Fig. 7D; Pearson r = 0.98–0.99 for biological replicates of the same promoter vs 0.91–0.93 between promoters) as well as the endogenous cassette exon sequences alone (Supplementary Fig. 15F; Pearson r = 0.98–0.99 for biological replicates of the same promoter vs 0.91-0.95 between promoters). Likewise, samples clustered based on promoter identity in a PCA (Fig. 7E).

Based on the set of synthetic promoters, we had identified promoter-specific sensitivities to the insertion of splicing factor binding motifs (Fig. 5). Repeating this analysis on the set of endogenous promoters showed that the effect size of splicing factor motif insertions differed between promoters in all three locations: the cassette exon itself as well as the upstream and downstream intron (Supplementary Fig. 16A). Analyzing the effect of insertion of specific splicing factor binding motifs confirmed that while in the case of hnRNPA1 motifs no difference could be observed between promoters (Supplementary Fig. 16B), insertion of SRSF6 and hnRNPG motifs showed promoter-specific differences in their effect on exon inclusion rates (Fig. 7FG). In contrast, we did not observe promoter-specific sensitivities to exon length as in the case of the minimal CMV promoter (Supplementary Fig. 16C).

Analyzing the full range of isoforms generated from the reporter constructs showed that the number of exon sequences yielding more than two isoforms changed also between the four endogenous promoters tested (Fig. 7H, p < 0.05 for 1 and p < 0.001 for 2, 3 and >3 isoform groups, chi-squared test), with expression from pMYC-2 resulting in more isoforms than the other promoters, including the full CMV promoter, despite it being the weakest one (Fig. 7B). pMYC-2 was associated with more partially spliced isoforms in which the first intron was retained (Fig. 7I). We observed differences in the rate of alternative cassette exon donor usage depending on the splicing state of the first intron (Fig. 7I, “alt. CE donor (1st intron spliced)” or “alt. CE donor (1st intron retained)”), with endogenous promoters showing less frequent use of alternative first donor sites as the full CMV promoter when the first intron was retained.

When comparing isoform frequencies generated from individual (endogenous) cassette exon sequences for which we had sufficiently deep coverage across all samples (>200 reads, with a minimum of 2 reads per isoform), we observed that in some cases the variability in isoform frequency observed across synthetic promoters could also be detected in the endogenous ones tested here, e.g. changes in the relative abundance of exon skipped and exon included in PTS (Fig. 7J; ~67% skipped vs. ~33% included in pCMV-full, pCMV-short, pMYC-1 and pMYC-2 compared to ~78% skipped vs. 22% included in min. pCMV, pTP73-1 and pTP73-2) and the increased use of an alternative cassette exon donor site at position 406 in COMMD9 (isoform 88^406-435^1214) especially in pACTB, pMYC-1 and pTP73-1 (Fig. 7J). In the case of RBM10, we obtained measurements for three library variants with identical variable sequence, which showed largely consistent results despite the different barcodes (Supplementary Fig. 16D). These results indicate that an influence of promoter identity on splicing outcomes can also be observed in native human promoters. In the future, a systematic screen of human promoters for their ability to affect splicing decisions could reveal biologically relevant functional links between promoter identity and alternative splicing.

Discussion

Using systematic, high-throughput functional genomics approaches, we provide evidence that promoter identity is a powerful determinant of alternative splicing outcomes and splicing fidelity, often having a greater impact than pharmacological interference with transcription itself. By combining combinatorial promoter and splice site variation in a massively parallel reporter assay, we expand the scope of research on promoter-dependent splicing regulation. As opposed to native genes, where each combination of promoter and splice site is found only once in the genome, this approach allowed us to assess the interplay between transcription initiation and splicing in a holistic way, while reducing the complexity to an experimentally feasible and computationally meaningful level.

Our findings show that promoter identity, not simply their strength or expression level, can influence splicing decisions across thousands of cassette exons. Remarkably, in our experimental system, promoter-dependent differences in splicing outcomes were more pronounced than those induced by pharmacological perturbation of transcription initiation or elongation, underscoring that the regulatory context set by promoters plays a major role in shaping splicing outcomes.

This study builds and expands on previous reports that elements such as promoter-proximal sequence features and transcription factor binding sites5,35,69,70 actively influence splicing outcomes, but also demonstrates that the effect of promoter identity on splicing outcome is highly splice site-specific. Changes in promoter identity rarely lead to switch-like changes between complete exon inclusion and skipping, and “non-binary” exons with intermediate inclusion rates typically are more sensitive to promoter variation. Importantly, cassette exons exhibiting higher sensitivity to promoter identity in our assay also displayed a higher range of inclusion rates across tissues in endogenous RNA-seq datasets. This suggests that splicing events most responsive to experimental promoter variation are similarly tuned by endogenous regulatory context in vivo.

Groups of exons showed characteristic dependencies on promoter identity. We identified distinctive features of these groups which allowed us to predict promoter dependence of a splicing event based on splice site strength, exon length and RNA secondary structure. The impact of RBP binding motifs on PSI values, especially in the upstream intronic region, changed between promoter constructs, sometimes even showing promoter-dependent reversal of their effect (e.g. FXR1, KHDRBS3). The minimal promoter lacking any known enhancer elements was associated with the most distinct groups of exons: The presence of RBP motifs showed stronger correlation with splicing outcome, and smaller exons were skipped more often, while longer exons showed a preference for inclusion in the case of the minimal CMV promoter compared to the other promoters. Both observations point to a stronger dependence on cis-regulatory features in a situation of limited space for regulatory inputs at the promoter region, e.g., a larger number of exonic splicing enhancers or other distinguishing features. While these results suggest specific regulatory principles underlying promoter-splice site crosstalk, in the future, the general applicability and relevance of these features will need to be experimentally validated, most importantly in native genomic sequence contexts.

Beyond the decision between exon inclusion and skipping, annotation-free mapping of targeted mRNA sequencing reads uncovered isoforms resulting from splicing at alternative positions and promoter-dependent regulation of splice site selection and cryptic splice site usage. Two CMV promoter variants were associated with increased production of aberrant isoforms through cryptic donor site usage and enhanced intron retention. A minimal CMV promoter on the other hand, while generating an identical transcript from the same transcription start site, reduced cryptic donor usage. These findings suggest that promoter identity can influence splicing fidelity, possibly by affecting the temporal coordination of transcription initiation events, splicing factor recruitment, and RNA polymerase processivity.

Extending our approach to alternative promoters from the human MYC and TP73 genes demonstrated that the features associated with promoter sensitivity of cassette exons are also evident in endogenous promoter contexts. Cassette exon inclusion levels differed between constructs driven by distinct endogenous promoters, and the effects of specific splicing factor binding motifs on exon inclusion varied across all promoters tested, with particularly pronounced promoter-dependent effects observed for hnRNPG-binding motifs. The endogenous promoter constructs also exhibited differences in splice-site selection and splicing fidelity, and several of the resulting isoform switches mirrored those observed among constructs driven by the EF1α, ACTB and CMV promoter variants. These findings indicate that promoter-dependent effects on splicing outcomes are not restricted to synthetic or heterologous promoters.

Presence of an upstream constitutive splicing event was associated with changes in cassette exon inclusion and the fidelity of weak splice site recognition. In contrast, the presence of a shared additional upstream intron did not mask the differences associated with promoter identity, highlighting the complexity of the role of gene architecture in splicing decisions. In general, other aspects of the layout of our mini-genes, such as the relatively short length of the cassette exons and flanking introns, could affect splicing outcomes. Additional systematic studies will be needed to disentangle the variability of promoter-dependent effects on splicing across different exon and intron lengths.

The ability to systematically interrogate the interplay of transcription and splicing by testing combinatorial sequence libraries entails a number of limitations, as the use of HEK293 cells and episomal expression of synthetic mini-gene reporters may not capture all facets of endogenous promoter-splicing crosstalk, especially those depending on specific cellular or genomic contexts. Integration of two reporter libraries with different promoters in the AAVS1 safe-harbor locus showed that promoter-dependent effects can also be observed when expressed from a genomic location. However, the splicing outcome might also be affected by the location of genomic integration. Therefore, the role of the specific chromatin context in shaping promoter-dependent splicing regulation remains to be elucidated.

These results provide starting points for developing mechanistic models regarding the individual contributions of different promoter-associated features to splicing regulation. Potential molecular mechanisms underlying the observed phenomena and supported by our results include binding of transcription and splicing factors at promoter sites and their effect on downstream splicing events either through their interaction with Pol II or directly through chromatin looping. In addition, the effect of promoters on splicing outcomes could be mediated through modulation of transcription kinetics, epigenetic marks, or nucleosome positioning.

Taken together, our results establish promoter identity as an important factor in determining both alternative splicing patterns and splicing fidelity. These insights suggest a new layer of regulatory grammar linking transcription initiation and RNA processing. Due to the importance of the use of alternative promoters in health and disease71, this has far-reaching implications for understanding disease-associated non-coding variants and splicing regulation as part of gene expression programs. Future studies dissecting the molecular mechanisms and disrupting them in vivo will further clarify the mechanistic underpinnings and physiological relevance of promoter-dependent splicing regulation.

Methods

Library construction

An oligonucleotide library containing 9608 cassette exons and their surrounding intronic sequences, previously designed, was used in this study. All the variants were composed of an 18 nt forward primer, 12 nt barcode sequence, BcuI and AatII sites separated by a 3 nt spacer, a 147 nt variable region, and 18 nt reverse primer sequences. Barcodes were designed to differ from any other barcode in the library in at least 3 nt. In the original cassette exon library, a constant upstream region (including a donor site) was cloned using the BcuI and AatII sites such that in the final library, the barcode was 288 nt away from the variable sequence, 58 nt from the constant donor site, and always part of the final reporter mRNA sequences, irrespective of the splicing outcome. The library contained native human sequences (2×134 variants) encompassing cassette exons with varying inclusion levels in endogenous RNA-seq data (based on Encode RNA-seq data, accession number ENCFF000HFA) as well as subsets with designed systematic sequence manipulations. For example, the SF motif subset contained sequence variants with 9 nt-long splicing factor binding motifs introduced into endogenous sequence contexts at positions −58: − 49, −49: − 40, −40: − 31, +5: + 14 or +14: + 23 relative to an acceptor and at positions −21: − 12, −12: − 3, +6: + 15 or +15: + 24 relative to a donor site and depending on the individual exon lengths. To create a subset of exon length variants, 23 endogenous cassette exon sequences were shortened by 6 nt, either close to the 5’ end (position +5), 3’ end (position −6) or the center of the exon. Depending on the length of the wild-type exon, this resulted in 9–24 variants per exon. More detailed information about synthetic library design can be found in Mikl et al., 201910.

Master plasmids for cloning the library were constructed from pcDNA3.1 (a gift from Andreas Moor, ETH Zurich, Switzerland) and visualized using Benchling72. A library integration site was added at the end of the GFP coding region before the stop codon by inserting a synthetic DNA cassette containing AscI and SbfI restriction sites using FastDigest Bsp1704I (Thermo Fisher). Promoter sequences were either PCR amplified from plasmids (pEF1α, pACTB, pCMV-short) or ordered as synthetic DNA sequences (IDT) and cloned using FastDigest BcuI and FastDigest EcoRI (Thermo Fisher). For the pCMV-full construct, the original promoter sequence of pcDNA3.1 was used.

For library cloning, the complete reporter construct containing upstream and downstream constant sequences was PCR amplified from the original plasmid library10 (a gift from Eran Segal, Weizmann Institute of Science, Israel) using Kapa HiFi ready mix and primers containing an SbfI or a BsaI site, creating the AscI-compatible overhang GCGC; for: ttttCCTGCAGGATGGGGTTCGGTATGCGC, rev: ttttGGTCTCtGCGCttattCAAAAGCCAGCAGCACATTC. 20 ul Kapa HiFi Ready Mix, 1.5 ul each primer (10 uM), 50 ng plasmid, water to 40 ul. PCR program: 98 deg 3 min, 15 cycles: 98 deg 20 sec, 65 deg 15 sec, 72 deg 20 sec; 72 deg 1 min. The resulting product was gel purified and digested using SbfI and BsaI (both NEB) and purified using Ampure beads (1.5x). The master plasmids containing different promoters were linearized with AscI and SbfI (both NEB), dephosphorylated using FastAP (Thermo Fisher), and cleaned using NucleoSpin Gel and PCR cleanup kit (Macherey-Nagel).

Ligation was performed using Electroligase (NEB) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. For high-efficiency transformation, E. cloni 10 G ELITE electrocompetent cells (Lucigen) were used. Each library cloning was performed in duplicate to account for biases introduced during library amplification and cloning. For each plasmid library, at least 300,000 colonies were obtained, and endotoxin-free plasmid DNA was obtained by Miniprep (Macherey Nagel).

Flow cytometry

Flow cytometric measurements of reporter constructs were performed on an Agilent NovoCyte equipped with two lasers (488 nm and 561 nm). Cells were seeded in a 96-well format and transfected using Polyjet (SignaGen) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. One day after transfection, cells were trypsinized, resuspended in 100 ul full medium, and analyzed by flow cytometry. Cells were selected based on forward and side scatter to filter out debris and aggregates, and the population corresponding to single cells was exported as fcs files and processed using custom Python scripts. After reading the fcs files using the fcsparser package, cells were filtered for minimal mCherry fluorescence, and log2 fold ratios of GFP to mCherry fluorescence were calculated for each cell, followed by calculation of the mean across all cells of a sample (=one well).

Library testing

HEK293 cells (a gift from Eran Segal, Weizmann Institute of Science, Israel) were grown in DMEM + 10%FBS+PenStrep at 37 degrees C and 5% CO2. Cells were routinely tested for Mycoplasma contamination and used at passage numbers <30. Plasmid libraries were transfected into HEK293 cells (2 million cells per sample) using Polyjet (SignaGen). 6 h after transfection, medium was replaced with DMEM/10%FBS/PenStrep for the experiment comparing different promoter constructs and with DMEM/10%FBS/PenStrep containing one of the following compounds at the indicated concentration: α-Amanitin (SC 2024400, Santa Cruze Biotechnology): 2.5 ug/mL and 5 ug/mL; Actinomycin D (SBR00013, Merck): 1 ug/mL and 5 ug/mL; Camptothecin (208925, Sigma-Aldrich): 1 uM and 5 uM; Doxorubicin (D2975000, Sigma-Aldrich): 50 and 100 nM.

24 hours after transformation, cells were harvested, with 1% being used for FACS analysis of cell count and fluorescence intensity on a Novocyte Flow Cytometer (Agilent) and the rest of the cells being pelleted and resuspended in 600 ul TRIzol. RNA was isolated using RNA Miniprep kits (Zymo) including DNase treatment according to the manufacturer’s protocol. cDNA was synthesized from up to 5 ug total RNA using Maxima H minus reverse transcriptase (ThermoFisher) according to the manufacturer’s protocol, with oligo-dT primers containing 18 Ts, a 6-12 nt unique molecular identifier (UMI), and a partial Illumina read 1 primer sequence.

For site-specific integration of the library into the human genome, we transferred our reporter construct to a pzDONOR vector containing homology arms for the AAVS1 locus and a neomycin resistance gene to select for integrants. 3 million HEK293 cells were transfected with a 20:1 mix of library plasmid and Cas9/sgRNA plasmid targeting the AAVS1 locus (PX459 (Addgene) with a AAVS1-specific sgRNA (GGGGCCACTAGGGACAGGAT) using Polyjet (SignaGen). 6 hours after transfection, medium was changed to DMEM + 10%FBS+PenStrep and 24 h after transfection, medium was again replaced with DMEM + 10%FBS+PenStrep supplemented with 2 μg/ml puromycin to select for transfection and expression of Cas9-T2A-Puro. After 2 days, medium was changed to DMEM + 10%FBS+PenStrep+G418 (500 ug/ml) and cells were grown in the presence of 2 μg/ml puromcyin for 10 days until a uniform mCherry-positive population was reached.

NGS library preparation

For library preparation, reporter cDNA was PCR amplified using reporter-specific forward (GAGTTCAGACGTGTGCTCTTCCGATCTATGGGGTTCGGTATGCGC) and reverse (TTCCCTACACGACGCTCTTCCGATCTNNNNNNNNNNNNGAGCGCACCCGTCCGAGC) primers, introducing partial Illumina Read1 and Read2 sequences, respectively. The identical primers were used for all experiments and all promoter constructs.

20 ul Kapa Hifi ready mix, 1.5 ul 10 uM reporter-specific forward primer (adding the Illumina TruSeq Read 2 sequence) and 1.5 ul reverse primer (adding the Illumina TruSeq Read 1), 4 ul cDNA, 13 ul water (2 reactions per sample). 98 deg 3 min, 15 cycles: 98 deg 20 sec, 65 deg 15 sec, 72 deg 20 sec; 72 deg 1 min. After cleaning up the reaction with 1x Ampure beads (Beckman-Coulter), Illumina sequencing adaptors (NEBNext Multiplex Oligos for Illumina, NEB #E7600S) were added in a second PCR reaction: PCR program: 98 deg 3 min, 12 cycles: 98 deg 20 sec, 65 deg 15 sec, 72 deg 20 sec; 72 deg 1 min. The reactions were cleaned up with Ampure beads (0.75 x), and the size of the product was verified using TapeStation (Agilent High Sensitivity D1000 ScreenTape). Sequencing was performed on one lane of a NovaSeq X plus with 2×150 cycles (Admera Health, New Jersey, USA).

3’ end mRNA sequencing

For 3′ end mRNA sequencing, total RNA was fragmented using the NEBNext Magnesium RNA Fragmentation Module (New England Biolabs). Briefly, RNA was incubated in 1× fragmentation buffer at 94 °C for 75 seconds, and the reaction was terminated with RNA Fragmentation Stop Solution. Fragmented RNA was purified using Agencourt AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter) at a 1.5x bead-to-sample ratio and analyzed using High Sensitivity RNA ScreenTape (Agilent Technologies). First-strand cDNA synthesis was performed using Maxima H Minus Reverse Transcriptase (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with an oligo(dT) primer containing the Illumina Read 1 sequence, a barcode for multiplexing, and a 6 bp UMI on the one hand and a template-switching oligonucleotide (TSO) containing the Illumina Read 2 sequence on the other. RNA, oligo(dT) primer, and dNTPs were first incubated at 65 °C for 5 min, followed by reverse transcription (42 °C for 90 min, 10 cycles of 2 min 50 °C and 2 min 42 °C, inactivation for 5 min at 85 °C). Resulting cDNA was purified using AMPure XP beads (1.5x ratio), quantified using a Qubit fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and amplified by PCR using KAPA HiFi polymerase (Roche) and indexed i5/i7 primers (NEB Next Multiplex Oligos for Illumina, NEB #E7600S). Standard amplification consisted of an initial denaturation at 98 °C for 3 min, followed by 14 cycles of 98 °C for 15 s, 65 °C for 15 s, and 72 °C for 20 s, with a final extension at 72 °C for 1 min. Amplified libraries were purified using AMPure XP beads (0.75x ratio), quantified by Qubit, and assessed for size distribution using the D1000 ScreenTape system (Agilent Technologies) prior to sequencing.

Mapping of 3’ mRNA sequencing reads

After demultiplexing of samples by Read 1 (reverse) using ultraplex according to barcodes included in the oligo(dT) primer and extraction of the 6 bp UMI, Read 2 (forward) was trimmed using cutadapt (parameter -a AAAAAAAAAAAA) and mapped against the human genome (GRCh38) using subread. The resulting bam files were sorted and indexed using samtools (1.22), demultiplexed using UMI-tools 1.1.6 (umi_tools dedup), not allowing multimappers and reads per gene were counted using featureCounts 2.0.8 (-t exon -g gene_id).

5’ Rapid Amplification of cDNA Ends (5’RACE)

Total RNA (1 µg) from HEK293 cells transfected with one of the reporter libraries (one sample per promoter) was subjected to reverse transcription. cDNA synthesis was mediated by Maxima H Minus reverse transcriptase (Thermo Fisher Scientific) using a combination of random hexamers and a template-switching oligonucleotide (TSO) primer carrying a partial Illumina Read 2 sequence (TCAGACGTGTGCTCTTCCGA TCTrGrGrG). The incubation time of the reaction mixture with reverse transcriptase was 1.5 hours at 42 °C, followed by 10 cycles at 50 °C for 2 min and 42 °C for 2 min. Subsequent PCR amplification was performed using DreamTaq polymerase (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with Read 2 (CAGACGTGTGCTCTTCCG) and EGFP-rev (TGCTTGTCGGCCATGATATAG) primers, which target the TSO and reporter gene sequences, respectively. PCR conditions included annealing at 62 °C for 15 s and elongation at 72 °C for 40 s. Amplicons were resolved by 2% agarose gel electrophoresis.

Unbiased mapping of splice sites from NGS data on reporter libraries

Pre-computation of MaxEnt Splice Site Scores

Prior to executing the main analysis pipeline, MaxEnt splice site strength scores were pre-computed for every nucldotide positions across all library variants. MaxEnt splice site scoring was performed using the maxentpy Python implementation of the Maximum Entropy Modeling algorithm to measure splice junction strength63.

Barcode demultiplexing and read assignment

Paired-end Illumina FASTQ files (R1 and R2) from multiplexed sequencing experiments were processed using a custom Python script (v3.12.2) built using the BioPython library (v1.83). Library variant information was loaded from a CSV reference file containing: Library index identifiers, complete sequence constructs, associated metadata including gene names, subset classifications, and annotated splice site locations. Read assignment to library variants employed a two-step barcode identification process: first, each read was scanned for the location of a constant 10-mer sequence CGGTATGCGC. This sequence represents the terminal portion of the constant region as the primer. Following the primer location, variant-unique 12-mers were extracted as the putative barcode sequence. After finding the barcode identity, the barcodes were matched against the pre-constructed barcode dictionary using exact string matching. Successfully matched reads were assigned to their corresponding library variants, with read identifiers stored in variant-specific lists.

The demultiplexing process tracked total read count processed, successfully mapped reads per library variant, unmapped reads due to absent constant regions, and unmapped reads with unrecognized barcodes. Read assignment results were serialized using Python’s pickle module (v 0.7.5) for processing reads per library variant.

Reference construction and read mapping per library variant

The reference construction and alignment were processed through a custom Python script. For each library variant, read identifiers were retrieved from the serialized pickle dictionary generated during barcode demultiplexing. Library variants with no assigned reads were flagged and excluded from further analysis. For variants with assigned reads, read identifiers were listed to plain text files with one identifier per line. Paired-end FASTQ files were subset to extract reads corresponding to each library variant using seqtk (v1.4). The listed read identifier files served as input to filter both forward and reverse read files, generating library variant-specific FASTQ files containing only reads assigned to that particular variant during the demultiplexing stage.

A synthetic reference sequence was constructed for each library variant by concatenating four sequence components in order. The first component consisted of nucleotides 0 through 30 from the library variant sequence, which includes the primer and unique barcode. The second component was a 288-nucleotide constant sequence containing the first exon and other regulatory elements, common to all library variants. The third component consisted of nucleotides 45 through the end of the library variant sequence, which includes the cassette exon and intronic regions flanking the cassette exon. The fourth component was an 800-nucleotide constant region containing the downstream constant intronic and exonic sequence. The complete synthetic reference sequence was formatted as a FASTA file with the library variant identifier as the sequence header.

Using STAR (v2.7.11b)73, individual STAR genome indices were generated for each library variant using its synthetic reference FASTA file. The genomeSAindexNbases parameter was set to 4 to optimize indexing for the relatively short reference sequences, which were 1,316 nucleotides in length. Genome generation utilized 8 CPU threads for parallel processing.

STAR alignment was performed in paired-end mode using the library variant-specific forward and reverse FASTQ files. The alignment process utilized 8 CPU threads for parallel processing and output unsorted BAM format files. The outSAMtype parameter was set to BAM Unsorted to generate binary alignment files.

The initial BAM output contained all aligned reads. To ensure high-quality alignment data, reads were filtered using SAMtools (v1.6) to retain only properly paired reads where both forward and reverse reads aligned concordantly. The samtools view command with the flag 0 × 2 selected reads with the proper pair bit set in the SAM flag field.

Comprehensive alignment statistics were collected for each library variant using samtools and command-line text processing tools. Each variant-specific alignment summary included the total number of aligned reads, the number of unmapped reads, the number of aligned reads containing splice junctions identified by N operations in the CIGAR string, the number of aligned reads without splice junctions, the number of reads with soft-clipping identified by S operations in the CIGAR string, and the number of reads without soft-clipping. These statistics were aggregated across all library variants in a summary CSV file for quality control assessment.

Properly paired BAM files were converted to BED12 format using bedtools (v2.31.1) bamToBed with multiple flags to preserve essential alignment information. The split flag created separate BED blocks for each continuous alignment region, effectively representing exons as separate blocks. The bed12 flag generated complete BED12 format output with all twelve standard fields, including library variant ID, start position, end position, read name, score, strand, thick start, thick end, color, number of blocks, block sizes, and block starts.

The BED12 output was filtered to retain eight key fields: chromosome identifier (i.e., library variant ID), start position of the leftmost block, end position of the rightmost block, read name, strand orientation, number of blocks, comma-separated block sizes, and comma-separated block start positions. This reduced format contained all information necessary for splice junction characterization while minimizing file size. Additional alignment information was extracted directly from the BAM files using samtools view. The CIGAR string describing the alignment operations and the actual read sequence were extracted and written to separate files. These files contained one line per read with tab-separated CIGAR string and sequence fields. The reduced BED12 format file and the CIGAR string and sequence file were merged using the paste command with tab delimiters. The resulting integrated file contained chromosome coordinates, block structure information, CIGAR strings, and read sequences in a single comprehensive record for each aligned read. This integrated format provided all necessary information for splice site detection and quantification.

Splice site detection and quantification

For splice site detection, a custom Python script was applied using pandas (v2.2.2) and numpy (v1.26.4). Since paired-end sequencing was used, forward and reverse read information was integrated by separating even-indexed forward and odd-indexed reverse records, joining paired records based on read identifiers, and combining block coordinate information from both reads.

For multi-block alignments indicating splice junctions, intron coordinates were calculated using block size and start position information. Intron start coordinates were calculated as the start of the left block plus the block size for that exon, and intron end coordinates were calculated as the start of left block plus the block start position for the subsequent exon. Up to two introns per transcript isoform were supported, accommodating sequences with up to three exons.

Multiple quality control filters were applied: Both forward and reverse reads required greater than 110 nucleotides of aligned sequence. Reads with more than 4 nucleotides of deletions indicated by CIGAR D operations were excluded. A maximum of 3 exons per isoform were allowed. Reads mapping with start positions greater than 600 nucleotides were excluded to eliminate mis-mapping artifacts.

Splice site strength was evaluated using MaxEnt algorithm scores. To correct the splice site locations that may be mislocated due to mapping errors, a correction was applied using MaxEnt scores. For each detected splice site, the algorithm searched a window, specifically plus or minus 3 nucleotides flanking the detected position, to find the maximum MaxEnt score. The highest maxent score in the region was assigned to be a corrected splice site position.

Splice sites were classified as canonical or non-canonical based on dinucleotide composition. Canonical donor sites were defined by GT dinucleotide; canonical acceptor sites were defined by AG dinucleotide.

Unique splice-isoforms were defined by their complete set of splice junction coordinates. Read counts were aggregated per isoform, and metrics were calculated including reads per isoform, total reads per library variant, relative isoform abundance, number of exons per isoform, and exon length measurements for multi-exonic isoforms.

Individual library variant results were concatinated using pandas DataFrame operations. Results were exported as comma-separated value (CSV) files containing one row per unique splice-isoform.

Computational infrastructure used

The pipeline was executed on the HIVE high-performance computing cluster at the University of Haifa with: Red Hat Enterprise Linux 9 operating system distribution, SLURM job scheduling for parallel processing (v23.02.4), Array jobs processing up to 9,608 library variants simultaneously.

Computational analysis

Data filtering

After the filtering steps on the raw data performed as part of the mapping pipeline and described above, data were loaded into python and generally filtered as follows: In general, library variants with less than 100 reads per sample were filtered out, unless specified otherwise in the figure legends. Isoforms covered by a single read in a sample were not included in any isoform-level analysis (in particular in Figs. 6 and 7). Depending on the specific analysis, more stringent filtering steps were applied, such as based on the number of reads for a library sequence or presence of sufficient sequencing reads per isoform and per library sequence across all samples. This information is included in the figure legends and the results.

PSI score calculation

PSI scores were calculated – after filtering out low-coverage isoforms and variants without evidence for the presence of a cassette exon – by dividing the number of reads with an exon included (=2 spliced-out introns in the sequenced region) by the number of all reads passing filtering for any given variant.

Log2(I/S) score calculation

Log2 exon inclusion scores were calculated – after filtering out low-coverage variants and isoforms – from the fraction of reads with an exon included (=2 spliced-out introns in the sequenced region) out of the number of all reads passing filtering for any given variant.

Mutation effect score calculation (log2(A))

Log2(A) scores were calculated from wild-type PSI (the PSI value measured for the wild-type sequence corresponding to each library variant) and ΔPSI (PSI of the variant – wild-type PSI) as described previously58,60.

Classification

Multiclass prediction was performed using sklearn (1.5.1) and xgboost (2.1.1), with the following parameters: objective = “multi:softprob”, num_class = num_classes, eval_metric = “mlogloss”, use_label_encoder = False, random_state = 42 and the default settings without hyperparameter tuning. Classes were predicted through five-fold stratified cross-validation where at each iteration, the model is trained on 80% of the data and evaluated on 20%. The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve was used as a measure for model performance.

Motif and kmer analysis

RBP motif databases were downloaded from meme.org, and the Homo_sapiens.dna_encoded.meme from CISBP-RNA was used for analysis. Position-specific scoring matrices were derived and used to obtain cumulative binding scores for each RBP in the variable sequence of each library sequence separately for the upstream intronic part, the exonic part, and the downstream intronic part. RBP-specific thresholds for binding were precomputed based on the distribution of binding scores expected under a random sequence background and based on a 5% false discovery rate using Biopython’s motifs package. To account for the differences in exon and intron length, cumulative scores were length-normalized. Kruskal-Wallis test was performed to identify RBPs with significantly different binding scores across groups of library sequences (exon clusters as defined in Fig. 5A). Pairwise comparisons between the groups were performed using Dunn’s post test.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis and visualization were performed in Python version 3.12.2 using numpy 1.26.4, pandas 2.2.2, scipy 1.13.1, statsmodels 0.14.2, scikit-posthocs 0.11.4, matplotlib 3.9.2, seaborn 0.13.2. Statistical tests used are indicated in the figure legends and the results and were chosen based on sample size, data distribution, and normality.
