Breathing carcinogens: PM2.5 air pollution and lung cancer in never-smokers, a narrative review
Authors: Ian G Christie, Adam C Soloff, Raul Caso
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectiveLung cancer in never-smokers (LCINS) disproportionately affects women and younger individuals, and its incidence has increased over recent decades. Environmental respiratory hazards, such as particulate matter ≤2.5 µm (PM2.5), are associated with lung cancer incidence and mortality, but the mechanisms by which PM2.5 increases lung cancer susceptibility remain incompletely understood. Introduction

Lung cancer is the leading cause of cancer-related mortality in the United States and worldwide (1). Despite advances in treatment modalities, lung cancer survival remains poor (2). Cigarette smoking is the predominant risk factor; however, in the last few decades there has been a marked increase in lung cancer among individuals who have never smoked. Approximately 10–25% of the 2.5 million annual cases of lung cancer worldwide occur in individuals who have never smoked tobacco (3,4). Importantly, lung cancer in never-smokers (LCINS) is more commonly diagnosed in women, younger populations, and Asian populations (5,6). Despite these trends, the current lung cancer screening guidelines in the U.S. are based on smoking history. As a result, never-smokers are more likely to present with advanced stage disease due to a lack of early detection (7,8).

Fine particulate matter ≤2.5 µm in diameter (PM2.5) is a particularly concerning environmental respiratory hazard (ERH) because it consists of a complex mixture of toxic compounds capable of penetrating deep into the distal airways and alveolar spaces. Despite the increasing incidence of LCINS, the mechanisms linking ERHs to lung carcinogenesis remain incompletely understood, and current lung cancer screening guidelines continue to exclude this growing population. This review summarizes the current evidence linking PM2.5 exposure to LCINS, with a focus on its epidemiologic associations, underlying biologic mechanisms, molecular alterations, and clinical implications, while highlighting key priorities for future research. We present this article in accordance with the Narrative Review reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-1264/rc).

Methods

A literature review was performed to summarize the current evidence regarding PM2.5 exposure and LCINS. Literature was identified through searches of PubMed/MEDLINE using combinations of the keywords “PM2.5”, “fine particulate matter”, “air pollution”, “lung cancer”, “never-smokers”, “environmental respiratory hazards”, “genomic alterations”, and “tumor microenvironment”. Original research articles, systematic reviews, meta-analyses, and landmark experimental studies published in English from 2000 through May 2026 were considered. Additional relevant publications were identified through manual review of reference lists. Studies were selected based on their relevance to the epidemiology, molecular mechanisms, clinical outcomes, and therapeutic implications of PM2.5-associated lung carcinogenesis. Environmental hazard exposure

Sources and composition of PM2.5

PM2.5 is of particular concern due to its ability to evade upper airway defenses and deposit within the distal bronchioles and alveolar spaces (9). Approximately half of PM2.5 pollution is attributable to fossil fuel combustion, including diesel, gasoline, coal, and natural gas (10). Additional sources include biomass burning such as wood combustion, noncombustion emissions such as roadway and construction dust, and agricultural activities including livestock production and fertilizer use. Chemical characterization studies provide further insight into the composition of PM2.5 and its sources. Analyses from South Korea demonstrated that secondary inorganic aerosols, including sulfate, nitrate, and ammonium, represent the dominant fraction of PM2.5 in this region and arise from atmospheric reactions and ammonia emissions from industrial activity and agriculture (11). Other significant contributors include trace metals such as iron, selenium, bromine, and zinc, which are associated with coal combustion, vehicular emissions, and metal smelting. Similar compositional patterns have been observed in urban Taiwan, where secondary aerosols, industrial emissions, and traffic related particles predominate (12). Collectively, these findings implicate fossil fuel combustion, industrial activity, and agricultural emissions as the principal drivers of global PM2.5 exposure. PM2.5 contains multiple established carcinogens, including polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs), arsenic, cadmium, hexavalent chromium, nickel compounds, and diesel-related elemental carbon, providing biologic plausibility for the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) classification of outdoor air pollution and particulate matter as Group 1 carcinogens.

Patterns of exposure and environmental disparities

Exposure to PM2.5 is not randomly distributed; rather, it is concentrated in densely populated urban areas, low-income neighborhoods, and communities located near industrial sources or highways (13). These disparities contribute to a higher cumulative burden of ERHs among vulnerable populations. Social drivers such as reduced access to lung cancer screening, lower clinical suspicion in never-smokers, and disparities in access to specialized oncologic care may contribute to delayed diagnosis and poorer outcomes in this population. These disparities suggest that socially patterned environmental exposures may contribute to increased lung cancer risk and exacerbate inequities in diagnosis, treatment, and survival among never-smokers.

Epidemiologic evidence linking PM2.5 to lung cancer

Lung cancer incidence

Multiple studies have demonstrated an association between PM2.5 exposure and lung cancer risk. Feng and colleagues conducted a retrospective national cohort study of 15,138,652 Medicare beneficiaries, including both smoking and non-smoking patients. PM2.5 exposure was estimated using geographically defined monitoring data (14). Over a median follow-up of nine years, each decile increase in PM2.5 exposure was associated with a 6.4% relative increase in lung cancer incidence. Pope and colleagues evaluated 1,184,233 participants in the Cancer Prevention Study II cohort, including smokers and non-smokers (15). Estimated exposure to fine particulate matter, derived from residential location and regional air quality data, demonstrated that each 10 µg/m3 increase in PM2.5 was associated with a 4% increase in all-cause mortality, a 6% increase in cardiopulmonary mortality, and an 8% increase in lung cancer specific mortality. Relative risk estimates of cardiopulmonary and lung cancer mortality related to fine particle pollution were increased among the subset of non-smokers.

Huang and colleagues performed a meta-analysis of 17 studies across North America, Europe, and Asia, demonstrating that each 10 µg/m3 increase in PM2.5 exposure was associated with an 8% increase in lung cancer incidence and an 11% increase in lung cancer mortality (16).

Studies focusing on never smokers have also reported a strong association between PM2.5 and elevated lung cancer risk. Notably, a Taiwanese study of 12,011 cases and 48,044 controls reported increased odds of lung adenocarcinoma with cumulative PM2.5 exposure among women and never smokers (17). In a large United States cohort of 188,699 lifelong never-smokers followed for 26 years, a 15–27% increase in lung cancer mortality was observed in association with each 10 µg/m3 increase in PM2.5 concentration (18). LCINS: distinct biology and clinical implications

LCINS is increasingly recognized as a distinct clinicopathologic entity with epidemiologic, molecular, and immunologic features that differ from tobacco-associated lung cancer (19). The drivers of LCINS are multifactorial and remain incompletely understood, with ERHs, including fine particulate matter (PM2.5), emerging as important contributors to lung carcinogenesis. Compared with tobacco-associated lung cancer, LCINS is characterized by a lower tumor mutational burden, an absence or marked reduction of tobacco-associated mutational signatures (particularly SBS4), and a higher prevalence of actionable oncogenic alterations, including EGFR mutations and ALK, ROS1, RET, NTRK, HER2 (ERBB2), and MET exon 14 skipping alterations. In contrast, smoking-associated lung cancers more frequently harbor KRAS, TP53, STK11, and KEAP1 alterations. These molecular differences support the hypothesis that PM2.5 acts predominantly as a tumor promoter rather than a direct mutagen, facilitating the expansion of pre-existing oncogenic clones rather than inducing widespread genomic damage.

These molecular differences have important clinical implications. Patients with LCINS are more likely to harbor targetable genomic alterations and benefit from precision oncology approaches, while the lower tumor mutational burden and relative lack of neoantigens, together with a distinct tumor-immune microenvironment decrease responsiveness to immunotherapy. The biology and clinical management of LCINS cannot simply be extrapolated from tobacco-associated lung cancer and underscore the importance of investigating environmental risk factors, including PM2.5, that contribute to disease biology.

Mechanisms of PM2.5-induced lung carcinogenesis

PM2.5-induced inflammatory signaling

Exposure to ERHs can induce pulmonary inflammation, and repeated exposure to ERHs aggravates the initial injury and promotes chronic inflammation with cellular proliferation and extracellular reorganization (20). Chronic inflammation is a prerequisite of lung cancer required to establish a permissive microenvironment for tumor initiation (21). Pulmonary macrophages (pMacs), consisting of alveolar, interstitial, and inflammatory/recruited macrophages, are resident phagocytes of the lung and constitute the “first line of immune defense” because they are directly exposed to inhaled toxins (22). pMacs are described as either classically activated “M1-like” which possess pro-inflammatory and tumoricidal capabilities or alternatively activated “M2-like” which promote tissue repair, angiogenesis, and cell survival (23,24). The lung cancer tumor microenvironment (TME) promotes alternatively activated characteristics, such as VEGF and interleukin-10 (IL-10) production, which facilitate tumor initiation, immune evasion, and the subsequent metastatic cascade (25-28). Such responses may be sustained through epigenetic regulation (e.g., chromatin remodeling, DNA methylation) which translates environmental stimuli into gene expression and cellular function. These heritable epigenetic adaptations are maintained within self-renewing tissue-resident macrophages, long-lived pMacs, or their progenitors, with the result being that ERH exposure may lead to a long-lasting state of maladaptive immunity. Ultimately, reprogramming of pMacs following ERH exposure may play a role in LCINS; however, the biology behind this needs further investigation.

Experimental models have provided key mechanistic insights into how PM2.5 promotes carcinogenesis through inflammatory signaling pathways (29). Chao and colleagues evaluated the effects of PM2.5 exposure in both in vitro non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) cell lines and in vivo murine models (12). In exposed mice, histopathologic analysis demonstrated lung injury characterized by neutrophilic infiltration and interstitial fibrosis. This inflammatory phenotype was accompanied by upregulation of pro inflammatory cytokines, including tumor necrosis factor alpha, IL-6, and IL-17A, in both serum and lung tissue. In vitro studies showed that PM2.5 exposure enhanced cancer cell proliferation and invasive capacity, supporting a direct role in tumor progression.

Metabolic reprogramming and tumor progression

PM2.5 also induces metabolic reprogramming that favors malignant transformation and tumor progression. Experimental studies demonstrate that PM2.5 exposure promotes a shift toward aerobic glycolysis through activation of HIF-1α and other stress-responsive pathways, resulting in increased glucose uptake, lactate production, and enhanced cellular proliferation. In addition to altered glucose metabolism, PM2.5 has been associated with dysregulated lipid metabolism and increased glutamine utilization, metabolic adaptations that support biosynthesis, redox homeostasis, and sustained tumor growth (29).

One mechanism underlying this metabolic shift was demonstrated by Chen and colleagues (29), who showed that PM2.5 promotes glycolytic reprogramming through translational and transcriptional upregulation of dihydrolipoamide S-acetyltransferase (DLAT), a core component of the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex. PM2.5-induced DLAT expression increased glycolytic flux, cellular proliferation, and tumor growth in experimental models and was associated with poorer clinical outcomes in patients with NSCLC. Collectively, these findings suggest that PM2.5 promotes lung carcinogenesis by rewiring cellular metabolism to favor proliferation and survival.

Molecular landscape of PM2.5-associated lung cancer

Molecular and genomic alterations

A study by the TRACERx Lung Program showed that increasing PM2.5 levels were associated with higher estimated incidence of EGFR-driven lung cancer (30). Whole-genome sequencing showed that PM2.5 exposure does not increase mutational burden, however most mutations were attributable to aging-related signatures. This finding supports the hypothesis that PM2.5 functions primarily as a tumor promoter rather than a direct mutagen.

Additional studies have shown that tumors arising in patients with higher PM2.5 exposure have a greater prevalence of TP53 mutations,31 suggesting that particulate exposure may influence the genomic landscape of NSCLC through both tumor-promoting and mutagenic mechanisms (31). Long-term PM2.5 exposure has been shown to activate the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) pathway, which results in persistent EGFR activation, cell proliferation, and tumor growth in a mouse model (32). The same study reported PM2.5 exposure and subsequent AhR activation resulted in cancer progression.

Epigenetic and mitochondrial alterations

PM2.5 exposure contributes to mitochondrial dysfunction. Data from the Nurses’ Health Study demonstrated that higher exposure is associated with decreased leukocyte mitochondrial DNA copy number (33). Consistent with this, the Normative Aging Study demonstrated that long-term PM2.5 exposure is linked to accelerated DNA methylation age, partially mediated by reduced mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) copy number, supporting a mitochondrial pathway underlying pollution-related aging and disease (34).

These studies underscore the unique pathobiology of PM2.5 exposures and their mechanistic link to lung cancer. Additionally, they highlight the importance of molecular profiling in patients that are never-smokers with significant environmental exposure, as these patients may be more likely to harbor targetable genomic alterations.

Lung cancer outcomes

Impact on survival following lung resection

PM2.5 exposure has also been associated with adverse clinical outcomes among patients with NSCLC (35). Kosanpipat and colleagues evaluated 587 patients undergoing resection for NSCLC. The study included 23% non-smoking patients; all had clinically resectable disease, and pathologic stage was 35% stage I, 25% stage II, 34% stage III, 7% stage IV (all with isolated brain metastasis). Patients were stratified according to PM2.5 exposure (high- vs. low-exposure groups) (36). Annual mean PM2.5 exposure was estimated using patients’ residential PM2.5 concentrations and patients were categorized as exposed (≥25 µg/m3) or unexposed (<25 µg/m3). Higher PM2.5 exposure was associated with an increased risk of long-term mortality [adjusted hazard ratio (HR) 1.44, 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.08–1.89] and greater in-hospital postoperative (2.9% vs. 1.0%) secondary to respiratory failure. Tumor recurrence was not significantly associated with PM2.5 exposure (HR 1.12, 95% CI: 0.82–1.51), suggesting that reduced long-term survival may be driven, at least in part, by underlying cardiopulmonary comorbidity rather than increased cancer recurrence.

Impact on disease progression and stage at presentation

Li and colleagues evaluated 398 patients with NSCLC, with external validation in an additional 49 patients, to examine the relationship between pre-diagnostic air quality and clinical outcomes (37). PM2.5 exposure during the six months preceding diagnosis was estimated using residential air quality monitoring data. Elevated PM2.5 exposure was independently associated with worse overall survival (HR 1.53, 95% CI: 1.13–2.07), while higher Air Quality Index was independently associated with presentation with stage III–IV disease (odds ratio 2.61, 95% CI: 1.35–5.24). A study of 169 lung cancer patients in Taiwan residing in 4 districts with differing air quality reported a higher prevalence of advanced-stage lung adenocarcinoma among patients residing in regions with greater air pollution (38). Exposure was estimated using data from the Taiwan Environmental Protection Administration air quality monitoring network. Of note, the association appeared to be driven more by elevated NO2 levels than by particulate matter, highlighting that gaseous air pollutants, in addition to fine particulate exposure, may influence NSCLC characteristics. Conclusions

Although cigarette smoking remains the predominant risk factor for lung cancer, the incidence of LCINS has increased substantially over recent decades. ERHs, particularly PM2.5, are increasingly recognized as important contributors to lung carcinogenesis through chronic inflammation, tumor promotion, metabolic reprogramming, and molecular alterations. Emerging evidence also suggests that PM2.5 exposure is associated with more advanced disease and worse survival following lung cancer treatment. Collectively, these findings underscore the importance of environmental exposures in the biology of lung cancer and highlight the need to better recognize LCINS as a distinct and increasingly important clinical entity.

Despite growing evidence linking PM2.5 exposure to lung cancer, important knowledge gaps remain regarding its role in tumor progression and response to systemic therapies among never-smokers. Lung cancer screening in this population remains a major unmet need, as current screening recommendations are based largely on cumulative smoking exposure and therefore exclude nonsmokers at risk from ERHs. Individualized risk prediction integrating environmental exposures with demographic information and family history may identify high-risk never-smokers. Prospective evaluation of benefit and cost-effectiveness of screening this group will be essential before they can be incorporated into guidelines. Investigation of the molecular pathways activated by PM2.5 exposure may identify novel therapeutic targets and facilitate more personalized treatment strategies (39). Given the distinct molecular landscape of LCINS, comprehensive molecular profiling may be particularly important in never-smokers with significant ERH exposure to identify targetable genomic alterations. Ultimately, advancing our understanding of environmentally mediated lung carcinogenesis will be critical to developing more inclusive strategies for prevention, early detection, and personalized treatment of LCINS.
