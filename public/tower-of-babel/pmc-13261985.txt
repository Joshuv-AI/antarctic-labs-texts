Do softgel manufacturing or gastric conditions break down carrageenan into poligeenan?
Authors: Soo Ah Jin, Qi Fang, Karunakar Sukuru
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Carrageenan (CGN) is a high molecular weight (typically 200–800 kDa) polysaccharide derived from red seaweed. It is used as a food additive and as a functional excipient in pharmaceutical and nutraceutical products. In softgels, CGN can offer advantages over gelatin, including pH tolerance up to 12, thermal stability during processing (70–90 °C), and resistance to cross-linking. However, under extreme conditions, such as prolonged exposure to strong acid and temperatures exceeding 100 °C, CGN may degrade into low-molecular-weight fragments (<20 kDa), known as poligeenan, which has been linked to toxicological effects. This study investigates whether conditions used in softgel manufacturing or simulated gastrointestinal digestion could cause CGN to degrade into poligeenan or similar low-molecular-weight species. Iota carrageenan from three different batches used in food, nutraceutical, and pharmaceutical products was subjected to controlled stress conditions, including high temperature and low pH. Molecular weight distributions were measured using gel permeation chromatography (GPC). Carrageenan exhibited excellent thermal stability at 95 °C and 105 °C without significant molecular weight reduction when not exposed to acid. Under gastric-simulating conditions (pH 1.2 at 37 °C), moderate hydrolysis occurred, but fragments remained above the 20 kDa threshold. In contrast, combined acid and high-temperature treatment induced substantial degradation, yielding fragments under 20 kDa. No low-molecular-weight fragments consistent with poligeenan (<20 kDa) were observed under relevant conditions, reinforcing carrageenan's safety profile for use as a functional excipient in controlled-release dosage forms and dietary supplements.

Introduction

Carrageenan (CGN) is a high-molecular-weight, sulfated polysaccharide derived from red seaweed and commonly used as a multifunctional food additive and excipient in pharmaceutical and nutraceutical products [1]. It serves as a gelling, thickening, and stabilizing agent, with broad applicability in food, pharmaceutical and other industries. One of the applications of carrageenan is in softgel capsules and controlled-release systems. Carrageenans have also been reported to exhibit a range of biological activities, including antiviral, immunomodulatory, and antimicrobial effects, and have been investigated for biomedical applications such as tissue engineering and biomaterials development [2].

CGN exists in three primary forms: kappa (κ), iota (ι), and lambda (λ), which differ in sulfate content and gelling properties (Fig. 1). The functionality and safety of CGN are closely linked to its molecular weight distribution, which typically ranges from 200 to 800 kDa. Analytical studies, including gel permeation chromatography (GPC) coupled with inductively coupled plasma (ICP) detection, have confirmed that commercial food-grade or pharmaceutical-grade CGN lacks significant quantities of low-molecular-weight fragments associated with toxicity [1].Fig. 1Types of carrageenan. The three primary types [kappa (κ), iota (ι), and lambda (λ)] differ in sulfate content and presence of 3,6-anhydro-d-galactose. κ-Carrageenan contains one sulfate group and forms strong, rigid gels with potassium ions. ι-Carrageenan has two sulfate groups and forms soft, elastic gels with calcium ions. λ-Carrageenan, with three sulfate groups and no 3,6-anhydro bridge, does not gel but imparts viscosity. These structural features define their solubility and functional behavior in pharmaceutical and food formulations.

Despite its broad functionality, the safety of CGN has been debated due to its potential to degrade into low-molecular-weight components under the combined conditions of strong acidity and high temperature. Of particular concern is poligeenan (PGN), a chemically distinct degradation product with molecular weights below 20 kDa, formed through extended exposure of CGN to strong acid hydrolysis at elevated temperatures [3]. Poligeenan is not approved for food or pharmaceutical use due to its demonstrated pro-inflammatory and toxic effects [3,4].

Poligeenan (PGN) is not produced biologically and is not present in native carrageenan. Rather, it is generated through extensive acid hydrolysis of carrageenan under harsh, non-physiological conditions, typically at very low pH (about 0.9-1.3) and elevated temperatures above 80 °C for several hours. These conditions are substantially more severe than those encountered in the gastrointestinal tract or during standard softgel manufacturing. In humans, gastric pH under fasting conditions is generally about 1.3-3 and rises transiently after food intake before gradually returning toward baseline as gastric emptying proceeds. In addition, gastric residence time is limited, typically on the order of 3-4 h. Taken together, these conditions are not generally sufficient to produce the extensive hydrolysis required to convert carrageenan into poligeenan.

Toxicological concern around poligeenan arises from its well-documented pro-inflammatory activity in experimental systems. Unlike high-molecular-weight carrageenan, poligeenan has been associated with gastrointestinal injury, activation of innate immune signaling, disruption of epithelial barrier integrity, oxidative stress, and alterations in gut microbial composition [5]. Proposed mechanisms include activation of Toll-like receptor-mediated pathways and downstream NF-kB signaling, resulting in increased expression of pro-inflammatory mediators such as IL-8, TNF-a, and IL-6. Poligeenan has also been reported to reduce tight junction protein expression, which may increase intestinal permeability, and to promote oxidative and microbial changes that can further amplify inflammatory responses.

Confusion surrounding CGN's safety has been exacerbated by the historical use of the term “degraded carrageenan,” which led to toxicological findings derived from poligeenan being misattributed to CGN [3]. Although regulatory authorities such as the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA), the Joint FAO/WHO Expert Committee on Food Additives (JECFA), and the European Commission have concluded that CGN is safe under intended use conditions [3,5,6], concerns persist regarding its stability during digestion or processing. Specifically, questions remain as to whether human gastrointestinal conditions or standard softgel manufacturing processes could induce hydrolytic degradation of CGN to poligeenan-like fragments.

Numerous pharmacokinetic studies have demonstrated that CGN is not systemically absorbed and is excreted unchanged in feces following oral administration in rats, guinea pigs, and primates [3,[7], [8], [9], [10], [11], [12], [13]]. Even in models colonized with human gut microbiota, no significant biotransformation or molecular weight reduction has been demonstrated [3,10]. Nonetheless, in vitro studies using highly sulfated λ-carrageenan (which contains three sulfate groups, compared to two in ι-carrageenan and one in κ-carrageenan) have reported inflammatory responses in cultured epithelial cells. However, these effects have not been consistently replicated in in vivo models or human studies [6,11,12].

A review of the literature revealed no studies directly examining whether carrageenan undergoes molecular weight degradation under actual softgel manufacturing or gastrointestinal physiological conditions, or whether such conditions could lead to the formation of poligeenan.

To address this gap, the present study aimed to assess whether iota-carrageenan degrades into low-molecular-weight fragments, specifically those consistent with poligeenan (<20 kDa), under physiologically relevant or softgel processing conditions. For this reason, gel permeation chromatography (GPC) was selected as the primary analytical method because it directly measures molecular weight distribution and enables detection of low-molecular-weight degradation products. Three commercial lots of iota-carrageenan were subjected to controlled stress conditions involving elevated temperature, acidic pH, and their combinations. Molecular weight distributions were measured using gel permeation chromatography (GPC), a sensitive technique for detecting polymer fragmentation. This study focused on qualitative comparisons of molecular weight distribution profiles, reporting observed shifts in peak molecular weight (Mp) and distribution patterns as indicators of degradation trends across treatment conditions and time points.

Materials and methods

Design of experiment

Three batches of commercially available iota-carrageenan (GENUTINE® Carrageenan, supplied by CP Kelco) were utilized to assess the variability of this naturally derived material. These samples were not standardized, meaning no dextrose was added to alter their gel strength and viscosity. Dextrose can appear as low molecular weight fractions in tests, possibly being mistaken for poligeenan. To evaluate its stability, carrageenan samples from three different lots underwent three distinct treatment conditions: 1) elevated temperature alone; 2) acid treatment at physiological temperature; and 3) acid treatment at elevated temperature. Analytical procedure for molecular weight distribution

Gel permeation chromatography (GPC) was used to assess the molecular weight distribution of carrageenan samples following various stress conditions. A refractive index (RI) detector measured the concentration of eluting polymer species, generating chromatograms that plot RI response (y-axis) against logarithmic molecular weight (log Mw, x-axis). In these chromatograms, larger molecular weight species elute earlier (left side of the x-axis), while smaller degraded fragments elute later (right side). Thus, a left-shift in peak position indicates intact, high-molecular-weight carrageenan, whereas the appearance or intensification of right-shifted peaks signals polymer degradation. The RI response at a given molecular weight reflects the relative abundance of molecules within that range; it is not a direct measure of absolute concentration or molecular weight. This analytical approach is particularly suited for identifying poligeenan, a degradation product of carrageenan defined by its molecular weight <20 kDa. In this study, any emergence of signal in the Log Mw ∼4.3–4.5 region (corresponding to Mw ≤ 20 kDa) was used as an indicator of potential poligeenan formation.

Sample preparation

Carrageenan is used widely in food and pharmaceutical and products as a gelling or viscosity modifying agent. The typical usage level is less than one percentage (1.0%). Thus, one percent (1.0%) iota-carrageenan solution was selected for the experiments based on the usage levels in most commercial food and pharmaceutical products. A bulk solution of 0.1 M NaNO3 was prepared and filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane prior to use. Carrageenan powder was dissolved in this mobile phase by heating to 90 °C with continuous stirring until a clear solution was obtained. To reduce the sample viscosity, the solution was then diluted to 0.2% (w/w) using the same mobile phase and aliquoted into glass vials (∼20 g per vial) for analytical testing. Gel permeation chromatography method

A Gel Permeation Chromatography system (Model 1260 Infinity, Agilent Technologies) was utilized to run the GPC experiments. A TSKgel® GMPWxl column (30 cm × 7.8 mm) with a particle size of 13 μm was utilized to determine the molecular weight distribution of the samples. A calibration curve was created using various molecular weights of polyethylene oxide as standards. The mobile phase consisted of 0.1 M NaNO3 and elution was performed with the filtered mobile phase at a flow rate of 0.45 mL/min. An injection volume of 80 μL was used for the samples and all measurements were conducted in triplicates to ensure reproducibility.

Discussion

This study evaluated the molecular weight stability of iota-carrageenan under conditions simulating food and pharmaceutical manufacturing conditions and human gastrointestinal physiology. Gel permeation chromatography (GPC) was used to characterize the degradation profiles of three commercial carrageenan lots following exposure to (1) acidic conditions representative of the human gastrointestinal physiology, (2) elevated temperatures mimicking softgel processing, and (3) combined acid and heat stress to assess worst-case degradation scenarios.

Across all conditions of simulating human gastric exposure (pH 1.2 at 37 °C), carrageenan exhibited partial hydrolysis, reflected by the emergence of a second molecular weight peak. However, the resulting fragments remained well above the 20 kDa threshold used to define poligeenan (by more than 4-fold). These findings are consistent with in vivo pharmacokinetic data showing minimal gastrointestinal degradation and near-complete fecal recovery of intact carrageenan [3,8,10]. Our results provide in vitro confirmation that physiologically relevant acidity and exposure time do not result in meaningful molecular weight fragmentation.

Under thermal processing conditions representative of carrageenan gel preparation and softgel encapsulation (typically 80–95 °C), carrageenan remained chemically stable, with molecular weights consistently well above the threshold for poligeenan formation. Observed shifts in molecular weight were minimal and did not yield secondary peaks or low-molecular-weight tails. These results support previous literature asserting carrageenan's thermal resilience in neutral pH environments [3,5].

By contrast, combined exposure to acidic pH (1.2 and 3.0) and elevated temperatures (95 °C) induced significant degradation across all three carrageenan lots. Molecular weight distributions collapsed into low-kDa fragments, with Mp values falling well below the 20 kDa poligeenan threshold after just 1 h. These conditions represent a known pathway for generating poligeenan in laboratory settings and are not representative of either gastrointestinal or softgel manufacturing environments [1,3]. Therefore, poligeenan formation is not associated with carrageenan used in softgel encapsulations.

Due to the scope and design of this study, we employed a qualitative evaluation of molecular weight trends rather than inferential statistical comparisons. While data were collected in triplicate to confirm reproducibility, variability in sample complexity and inherent limitations of GPC, including resolution constraints in the low-molecular-weight range and dependence on calibration standards, limited statistical power. Interpretation of chromatographic peak shifts was therefore performed qualitatively. Nevertheless, the clear and consistent separation of degradation profiles, particularly under combined acid and heat stress, supports the robustness of our conclusions. This study also did not include viscosity or rheological characterization as a complementary measure of polymer chain integrity. While rheological properties can be sensitive to changes in polymer size, conformation, and intermolecular interactions, such measurements provide indirect evidence of degradation and cannot directly confirm whether low-molecular-weight fragments fall within the poligeenan range. Because the primary research question centered on the presence or absence of fragments below the 20 kDa threshold, GPC was considered the most appropriate method for this work. Another limitation is the absence of confirmatory structural techniques (e.g., FTIR, NMR) to chemically validate poligeenan formation. Although confirmatory structural analyses were not performed, the substantial molecular weight reductions observed, consistent fragmentation across acid types, and alignment with established degradation mechanisms strongly support the conclusion that significant hydrolysis, potentially yielding poligeenan, occurs only under extreme combined acid and heat conditions, not under physiological gastric conditions or during softgel manufacturing.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated that iota-carrageenan maintains its molecular integrity under conditions that closely simulate both human gastric physiology and standard industrial thermal processing. When exposed to simulated gastric pH (1.2) at 37 °C for up to 120 min, carrageenan exhibited limited hydrolysis with no evidence of fragmentation into poligeenan-range molecular weights. Similarly, exposure to elevated temperatures (95–105 °C) under neutral pH conditions produced only minor reductions in molecular weight without formation of low-molecular-weight species.

In contrast, carrageenan subjected to the combination of low pH and high temperature underwent extensive degradation. The resulting molecular weight distributions shifted significantly, with major peaks collapsing into fragments well below the 20 kDa threshold. These conditions are consistent with known poligeenan-generating processes and are not representative of human physiology or routine softgel manufacturing process conditions.

Together, these findings support the chemical stability of iota-carrageenan under realistic use scenarios and provide further evidence that poligeenan is not formed during digestion or typical pharmaceutical manufacturing processes. This reinforces carrageenan's continued use as a safe and functional excipient in oral and thermally processed dosage forms where its unique benefits (outlined in the introduction) can be fully leveraged. Future studies may explore degradation kinetics and expand to other carrageenan types to further refine our understanding of excipient behavior under controlled stress conditions as well as actual manufacturing and use conditions.
