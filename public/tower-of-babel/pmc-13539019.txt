Full-Construct Biomechanical Evaluation of Femoral Cortical Suspensory Fixation for Anterior Cruciate Ligament Reconstruction in a Porcine Knee Model
Authors: Tomas F. Vega, Juan Bernardo Villarreal Espinosa, Elizabeth Shewman, Zeeshan A. Khan, Harsh Singh, Brady T. Williams, Thomas Alter, Ron Gilat, Safa Gursoy, Andrew S. Bi, Jorge Chahla
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Although recent studies show no clinical outcome differences between cortical button suspensory fixation devices for anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction (ACLR), biomechanical mechanisms of elongation and failure remain unclear for adjustable-loop devices (ALDs) and fixed-loop devices (FLDs).Purpose:To compare biomechanical outcomes of ALDs with button-locking device (BLD), suture-locking device (SLD), and dual-locking device (DLD) mechanisms relative to an industry-standard FLD.Study Design:Controlled laboratory study.Methods:Five femoral cortical suspension devices (n = 11 each; total = 55 constructs) were tested using porcine femurs and bovine tendons simulating doubled hamstring grafts: EndoButton (FLD), Infinity Button (BLD), ProCinch (SLD1), UltraButton (SLD2), and TightRope (DLD). Constructs underwent cyclic loading simulating early (10-250 N, 500 cycles) and late (10-400 N, 500 cycles) rehabilitation, followed by pull-to-failure at 50 mm/min. Outcomes included excursion over simulated early and late rehabilitation and the cumulative total of both loading protocols. Also assessed were maximum load, stiffness, energy to failure, and failure mode, categorized as bone, button, suture loop rupture, tendon, or locking mechanism failure.Results:No significant differences were observed in cyclic loading at 500 cycles of 10 to 250 N or across the cumulative 1000 loading cycles between any ALD and the FLD. During cyclic loading at 10 to 400 N specifically, the BLD (3.34 ± 1.71 mm) and DLD (3.63 ± 1.18 mm) demonstrated greater excursion than the FLD (1.49 ± 0.36 mm; both P < .01). SLD1 (1270.5 ± 204.7 N) and SLD2 (1232.7 ± 185.2 N) withstood higher maximum loads than the BLD (1011.4 ± 95.8 N) and DLD (1018.4 ± 63.1 N; P < .05), although no differences were observed between the FLD and all ALDs combined. Stiffness did not differ across groups. BLD and DLD constructs most often failed by suture rupture, whereas the FLD and SLDs failed by button failure. Suture rupture was associated with greater excursion during cyclic loading at 10 to 400 N, lower maximum load, and reduced energy absorption compared with button failure (P < .05).Conclusion:All constructs exceeded forces expected during early and total rehabilitation after ACLR, confirming adequate initial biomechanical strength across ALDs and FLDs. Differences in excursion and load to failure were associated with locking mechanism design and resultant failure mode, although clinical implications remain uncertain.Clinical Relevance:Although mechanical behavior varied by device mechanism, all systems provided fixation above physiologic loads, supporting that design-specific differences influence how devices fail but not their ability to provide adequate early postoperative stability in ACLR.

Anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) injuries are extremely common, with more than 150,000 ACL reconstructions (ACLRs) performed annually. 1 The success of ACLR can depend on surgical technique, graft type, and the mechanical properties of graft fixation devices. 2 Graft fixation devices play an important role in ensuring graft tensioning is maintained until the graft has integrated with host bone, with a wide variety of fixation constructs such as interference screws, suture anchors, transfixion pins, screw and washer, and cortical button suspensory fixation devices. 2 With cortical suspensory fixation in particular, increases in the construct length during the early postoperative period have been shown to cause graft micromotion as well as clinical failure. 2 These cortical button suspensory fixation buttons have undergone continued development, with both fixed-loop device (FLD) and numerous adjustable-loop device (ALD) options. 2

Advantages of FLDs include robust fixation that limits the initial elongation during graft incorporation; however, FLDs require precise measurements to confirm accurate bone tunnel lengths to prevent graft-tunnel mismatch. 2 Conversely, the main advantage of ALDs includes intraoperative flexibility in tunnel length creation, as well as the ability to retension throughout the procedure.8,16,20,21,23,24,32 However, there is no consensus regarding the potential for loop elongation observed with ALDs compared with FLDs. Furthermore, ALDs have distinct locking mechanisms with biomechanical differences but unclear clinical implications.4,13,24 Biomechanical studies have demonstrated both equivalent elongation between ALDs and FLDs, as well as increased elongation potential with ALDs compared to FLDs, but they have been limited by small sample sizes and by in vitro testing conditions that resemble single-device testing rather than full tissue-device construct testing.5,16,29 As such, the biomechanical differences in ACLR using ALD and FLD for femoral-sided graft fixation remain unclear.

The purpose of this study was to compare biomechanical outcomes between a current industry standard FLD, the EndoButton (Smith & Nephew), and 4 ALDs, including the Infinity Button (ConMed Linvatec), TightRope (Arthrex), UltraButton (Smith & Nephew), and ProCinch (Stryker), using a full-construct in vitro model with porcine knees and bovine tendons. We hypothesized that no significant differences would be observed in terms of loop elongation and maximum load to failure when comparing the 5 devices.

Methods

Five femoral cortical suspension devices were selected to be biomechanically tested in an in vitro model with porcine femur and extensor digitorum bovine tendons (n = 11 specimens per device for a total of 55 constructs tested). Specimens were prepared by a board-certified orthopaedic surgeon (A.S.B.).

Cortical Suspensory Devices

The 4 ALDs included were the Infinity Button (ConMed Linvatec), ProCinch (Stryker), TightRope (Arthrex), and UltraButton (Smith & Nephew). The FLD, which served as the control, was the EndoButton (Smith & Nephew), consisting of a 15-mm fixed polyester loop. ALDs differed in their locking mechanisms; in accordance with the framework proposed by Bachmaier et al, 4 these were classified based on their primary mechanism of loop fixation. The Infinity Button was designated a button-locking device (BLD) because the free suture limb is spliced through the opposite limb and frictionally trapped against the cortical button under tension. The TightRope was categorized as a dual-locking device (DLD), employing both a Chinese finger-trap-style constricting splice and frictional wedging against the button under tension. The ProCinch and UltraButton were considered suture-locking devices (SLDs), in which fixation is achieved through internal suture loop knot-based cinching or distribution of friction through multiple internal button channels, respectively. Hereafter, the devices will be referred to by their stabilization mechanism, as FLD (EndoButton), BLD (Infinity Button), DLD (TightRope), SLD1 (ProCinch), and SLD2 (UltraButton). Specimen Preparation

Fresh-frozen skeletally mature porcine stifle joints and bovine extensor digitorum tendons were obtained from an animal product supplier (Animal Technologies). Bovine extensor digitorum tendons have been shown to have similar viscoelastic properties to human hamstring tendons. 9 The tendons were stored at –20°C and thawed in saline at room temperature for several hours before testing. Porcine knees were chosen due to previous use in similar biomechanic studies as well as similarities to human bone and uniformity of bone quality/density across specimens.2,25 The knees were stored at –20°C and thawed for 24 hours prior to dissection. Multiple freeze-thaw cycles were avoided for both porcine knees and bovine tendons. Overlying soft tissues were removed, and tibias were disarticulated. Care was taken to preserve a sufficient remnant of the ACL to allow for footprint identification. Disarticulated femurs were subsequently potted in 2-inch polyvinyl chloride pipe using dental acrylic (Isocryl; Lang Dental). Two perpendicularly oriented screws, 1 anterior-posterior and 1 medial-lateral, were passed through the femoral diaphysis to ensure adequate fixation prior to potting. Potted specimens were allowed to sit for a minimum of 12 hours prior to testing to allow the acrylic to fully cure.

Tunnel Preparation

Due to the narrowed porcine femoral notch, adequate access and visualization of the lateral wall required removal of the medial femoral condyle, which was accomplished with an oscillating saw. The ACL footprint was subsequently identified by the ACL remnant, which was sharply transected at its footprint using a scalpel. The appropriate industry-specific beath pin for each cortical suspensory button was then inserted, starting along the anterior border of the ACL footprint and exiting to a point 6 cm proximal to the distal-most aspect of the lateral femoral condyle. Due to the substantial periosteum of the porcine femurs, a scalpel and periosteal elevator were used to prep the cortical surface to ensure each subsequently passed button would sit flush with the cortex. The femoral tunnel was then created with a 9-mm full-radius reamer, creating a closed-socket tunnel, preserving an 8-mm bone bridge with the outer cortex in all specimens. A smaller-diameter tunnel was then passed through the lateral cortex over the beath pin to allow for button passage in accordance with manufacturer instructions. These tunnel diameters were 4.5 mm (EndoButton and UltraButton), 4.0 mm (TightRope and ProCinch), and 3.5 mm (Infinity Button).

Graft Preparation

Grafts were prepared using doubled bovine extensor tendons. The tendons were sharply trimmed to a length of 180 mm, and 30 mm of each end was prepared with a No. 2 ultra-high-weight polyethylene braided suture in a running locking fashion. Grafts were subsequently doubled over and sized to fit through a 9-mm sizing block. The end result was a 90-mm doubled graft, with 2 tails of 30 mm of whipstitched length and 60 mm of free graft.

The looped end of the doubled-over graft was prepared using the designated cortical suspension device, which was then deployed through the femoral socket against the lateral femoral cortex per manufacturers’ recommendations, while the whipstitched free ends of the graft were connected to the tensile testing machine’s (Insight 5; MTS Systems) actuator via a custom soft tissue clamp such that only the whipstitched portions were contained within the clamp.

For testing, the prepared femurs were secured in a custom jig to the base of a tensile testing machine such that the femoral ACLR tunnel was vertically oriented and in line with the actuator of the tensile testing machine (Figure 1). The clamped graft was then axially displaced by the testing device. To evaluate for slippage from the soft tissue clamp, the tendon was marked with a surgical marking pen at the portion exiting the clamp. The free ends of the adjustable loop devices were subsequently marked to be able to monitor for possible locking mechanism slippage and elongation during cyclic testing. In addition, the gauge length from the tunnel aperture to the tendon grip was measured with a digital micrometer and recorded.

Biomechanical Testing

Biomechanical testing was initiated with precyclic conditioning for 10 cycles between 10 and 50 N at 0.1 Hz. This was followed by 500 cycles of load between 10 and 250 N at 0.75 Hz to represent the simulated “early” rehabilitation phase following ACLR. 33 The 250 N load was chosen to simulate nonweightbearing passive range of motion during physical therapy.5,9,25,28 To simulate late postoperative rehabilitation, involving active range of motion and weightbearing ambulation, the construct underwent an additional 500 cycles of testing with a load between 10 and 400 N at 0.75 Hz.5,16,25 Pull-to-failure testing was then performed at 50 mm/min to measure maximum load.

The primary outcome variable from the cyclic testing consisted of excursion and was calculated for each phase of cyclic testing as the tensile testing machine actuator’s axial displacement in millimeters at 10 N of the final cycle of each respective loading phase, minus the displacement at 10 N of the final cycle of the previous loading phase. Thus, values for excursion during simulated early rehabilitation and late rehabilitation phases were calculated. In addition, total excursion was reported, which was measured as the total displacement from the early and late phases of rehabilitation combined. Failure outcomes included maximum load to failure, construct stiffness, energy to maximum load, and failure mode. Construct failures were categorized as bone failure, button failure, device suture loop breakage, tendon failure, and device-locking mechanism failure based on posttesting qualitative assessment. Button failures specifically involved sinking, flipping, or pulling through the lateral femoral cortical bone.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were presented as means and standard deviations. Categorical variables were presented as percentages. The Shapiro-Wilk test of normality was used to determine whether data were normally distributed. Levene’s statistic was used to determine the homogeneity of variances. Parametric continuous variables were analyzed by paired t test or analysis of variance, while nonparametric data were analyzed by Mann-Whitney U or Kruskal-Wallis test. Chi-square analysis was used to analyze categorical variables. A prior α level was set at .05 to indicate statistical significance. Bonferroni corrections were applied to post hoc tests. For significant parameters, a total of 6 post hoc tests were performed to compare graft constructs, using a Bonferroni-corrected significance level of P < .0083 (0.05/6). All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (v. 26; IBM). Based on prior research, the number of devices/specimens per group has ranged from 8 to 11 for isolated device testing and combined device-graft-construct testing, respectively, using a porcine bone/bovine tendon model.1,17 To sufficiently power (80%, α = .05) the proposed study to detect a 1-mm difference in cyclic displacement and with group standard deviations of 0.8 mm, 11 device-graft constructs per group were required.

Results

Cyclic Loading

All specimens tested survived the cyclic loading tests without failure. During cyclic loading between 10 and 250 N, simulating early rehabilitation testing, no significant differences were detected in excursion (P = .609) when comparing the buttons (FLD: 3.95 ± 1.33 mm, BLD: 4.25 ± 0.65 mm, SLD1: 4.67 ± 1.69 mm, DLD: 4.33 ± 0.94 mm, SLD2: 4.90 ± 2.28 mm) (Figure 2A). During cyclic loading between 10 and 400 N, simulating late rehabilitation testing, when comparing all buttons, the BLD (3.34 ± 1.71 mm vs 1.49 ± 0.36 mm; P = .01) and DLD (3.63 ± 1.18 mm vs 1.49 ± 0.36 mm; P = .002) had significantly larger excursions compared to the FLD (Figure 2B). However, there were no significant differences in total excursion between any of the buttons (Figure 2C).

Failure Testing

With respect to maximum load, the SLD1 (1270.5 ± 204.7 N) and SLD2 (1232.7 ± 185.2 N) demonstrated a greater maximum load when compared to the BLD (1011.4 ± 95.8 N; P = .001 and P = .009) and DLD (1018.4 ± 63.1 N; P = .002 and P = .013), respectively. However, when the FLD was directly compared to the ALDs, there were no statistically significant differences in maximum load (Figure 3A). Analysis of energy to maximum load by graft construct indicated that the FLD (7635.0 ± 2190.4 N*mm) had a greater energy to maximum load than the DLD (3976.3 ± 625.5 N*mm; P < .001) and BLD (5058.5 ± 998.4; P = .007). In addition, the SLD1 (6641.5 ± 2071.8 N*mm) had a greater energy to maximum load than the DLD (P < .005), but the SLD2 (6046.0 ± 1875.3 N*mm) had no significant differences when directly compared to other button constructs. (Figure 3B). When investigating stiffness of the constructs, there were no significant differences between any of the constructs (Figure 4).

The FLD failed mainly due to a button mechanical failure, whereas the ALDs failed mainly due to suture loop or button failure (Table 2). Discussion

The primary findings of this study were that all constructs exceeded forces expected during early rehabilitation after ACLR, confirming sufficient time-zero strength across devices. Adjustable-loop devices incorporating button-based locking mechanisms (both BLD and DLD) exhibited greater excursion during simulated late rehabilitation (10-400 N, 500 cycles) and more frequent suture loop failures than fixed-loop devices, while SLD ALDs (SLD1 and SLD2) demonstrated higher maximum loads and tended to fail through button failure. Despite these differences in mechanical behavior, simulated early rehabilitation (10-250 N) and cumulative (10-250 N and 10-400 N, 1000 cycles) excursion did not differ significantly between groups, and no failures occurred due to locking mechanism release. These results highlight that differences in locking mechanism design primarily influence how devices fail, rather than whether they provide clinically adequate initial fixation strength. Additionally, differences in suture material may potentially play a role, but these were not explicitly investigated in the present study.

Maturation of the ACL graft involves early incorporation into bone and later remodeling between 4 and 12 weeks, followed by intra-articular ligamentization that takes between 5 and 12 months.7,17,18 During this time, the ACL graft requires adequate fixation to withstand loads during early and late rehabilitation prior to full incorporation.7,14,16,26,27,30,31 Multiple studies have reported loads across the ACL in humans during the early rehabilitation process to be approximately 150 to 590 N, with walking estimated to produce up to 411 N.9-13 Although the maximum loads reached in all construct types tested in this study were higher than the projected ACL loads mentioned, the SLD1 and SLD2 demonstrated significantly higher maximum loads compared to the BLD and DLD. When Smith et al 29 compared biomechanical outcomes in a porcine knee model between the TightRope, UltraButton, EndoButton, GraftMax, and RetroButton, the authors reported the TightRope to have the highest pull to failure (958 ± 40 N; P < .030 compared to all other buttons). In our study, the average maximum load experienced by the TightRope (DLD) constructs was 1018.4 ± 63 N, a small and likely clinically insignificant difference that may be attributable to differences in biomechanical testing protocols. The constructs in the study by Smith et al 29 underwent 1000 cycles of position- and force-controlled dynamic loading forces of 100 to 400 N. Our study’s constructs also underwent 1000 total cycles, but these were separated into 2 different 500-cycle loading protocols at forces lower than those reported by Smith et al. 29 These were at 10 to 250 N and 10 to 400N to simulate an early and delayed postoperative rehabilitation process, respectively. 29 The lower overall strain on the construct may have contributed to the TightRope (DLD) in our study withstanding higher maximum loads.

FLDs have been previously hypothesized to demonstrate less displacement in comparison to ALDs. 5 Houck et al 15 investigated fixed- versus adjustable-loop femoral suspensory fixation in a systematic review and meta-analysis of 6 studies, finding the EndoButton (FLD) to have the least cyclic displacement when compared to ALDs. In hypothesizing there to be significant differences in peak cyclic displacement between ALDs and FLDs, Johnson and colleagues 16 biomechanically compared the EndoButton (FLD) with the Rigidloop (J&J), XO Button (ConMed), and TightRope (DLD). The authors reported the EndoButton to have significantly less peak cyclic displacement when compared to the other buttons. 16 Similarly, Chang and colleagues 6 compared the TightRope (DLD) and EndoButton (FLD) using device-only testing and a porcine knee model. When testing with a porcine knee model, the authors reported the EndoButton (FLD) to have a significantly higher load to failure compared to the TightRope (DLD). 6 Eguchi et al 10 also investigated the EndoButton (FLD) and TightRope (DLD) under isolated device testing and similarly reported the EndoButton (FLD) to have a significantly higher load to failure. Our results demonstrated the EndoButton (FLD) to have a higher average maximum load compared with those that involved friction-based locking at the button (BLD and DLD); however, this was not a significant difference (P = .07). Although our results show some ALDs to be statistically superior compared to others in terms of maximum load, we found all devices to have an ultimate maximum load above peak forces predicted during early rehabilitation after ACLR in humans.19,22,27

Nevertheless, cyclic displacement after ACLR may be the most clinically relevant concern, as this can lead to graft micromotion that impedes tendon-bone integration and eventual failure.5,16 In our study, cyclic displacement was measured after 500 cycles of testing in 2 phases: simulated early (10-250 N) and late (10-400 N) rehabilitation. There were no significant differences between devices in simulated early rehabilitation, although significant differences between buttons were seen during simulated late rehabilitation. The BLD and DLD demonstrated significantly higher displacements than the FLD, with the DLD showing the most displacement of all constructs. The BLD and DLD also had greater late excursion compared with the SLD1 and SLD2; however, this difference was not statistically significant. The clinical significance of this finding in vivo remains to be seen, as at the postoperative timeline of late rehabilitation, the ACL graft has likely undergone some aspect of ligamentization and incorporation, minimizing the effects of cyclic excursion seen in the in vitro model.

Despite demonstrating more cyclic displacement during simulated late rehabilitation, cumulative displacement across the entire cyclic loading testing (both simulated early [10-250 N] and late [10-400 N] rehabilitation, 500 cycles of each, 1000 total) was not significantly different between any devices. Furthermore, translational evidence in recent years has shown that in vitro displacement of ALDs has not been associated with poorer outcomes, potentially because in vitro studies represent time zero, whereas in vivo graft integration and ligamentization provide additional fixation. The upper range of forces of 250 N and 400 N for cyclic loading in simulated early and late rehabilitation phases were consistent with most reported literature, while our lower range of 10 N was in line with parameters used in studies by Noonan et al 20 and Barrow et al 5 that evaluated relative “unloading” of the knee joint. Glasbrenner et al 12 previously reported that ALDs relying on finger-trap locking mechanisms tend to elongate when unloaded, as tension is released from the finger-trap loop. 13 Other potential sources for increased displacement at lower loads include graft elongation due to slack within the constructs or, alternatively, weak or soft cortical porcine bone. Additional biomechanical studies have found that differences in ALDs’ displacement may be mitigated through knot-tying after deployment and before cycling or retensioning after cyclic loading.5,21 These findings indicate that ALD elongation is phase-specific, emerging under later, higher-load cycles rather than accumulating across the full rehabilitation simulation, which is consistent with clinical studies reporting comparable laxity and outcomes.3,11

The results also demonstrated that construct failures due to suture loop tearing had significantly higher late excursion than failures due to button failure. These differences in excursion likely highlight that progressive elongation of the suture during simulated late rehabilitation contributed to differences between BLD and DLD, which failed mostly due to suture loop tearing, compared to the FLD and SLD, which failed due to button failure. In a systematic review and meta-analysis of biomechanical studies comparing ALDs and FLDs, the authors reported that suture loop failure was significantly higher than all other failure mechanisms.15,23,28 However, there was no report on comparisons of biomechanical outcomes based on failure modes. In our study, the material property of sutures tended to be the primary failure mode for ALDs, highlighting the sutures themselves as the weakest point in the constructs, rather than the locking mechanism.

Suture material may impact the biomechanical properties of cortical suspensory devices, with thicker FLD polyester or ultra-high molecular weight polyethylene (UHMWPE) and larger-caliber sutures, such as the No. 5 UHMWPE of SLD1 and BLD, demonstrating greater rigidity, while the thinner No. 2 UHMWPE of DLD was more prone to elongation under cyclic loading. However, these biomechanical differences have not consistently translated into clinical outcomes, and retensioning after cyclic loading or knot-tying of adjustable-loop devices can yield fixation comparable to that of FLDs.5,20,21 Surgical technique and device design, including button and suture material, all contribute to graft stability in ACL reconstruction. Recent comparative studies emphasize overall clinical similarity between ALDs and FLDs; our failure-mode analysis likely reflects that material fatigue (suture) rather than locking slippage is the dominant ALD constraint at time zero.

Limitations

This study has considerable strengths, including linking failure modes (eg, suture rupture vs button failure) to quantitative differences in elongation and energy absorption to identify where each design class is biomechanically vulnerable. However, this study is not without limitations, perhaps the most important being that the devices used may not represent the latest commercially available models. Specifically, the contemporary commercially available DLD at the time of publication employs suture tapes rather than No. 2 UHMWPE, which may behave differently from the iteration of the device tested in our study. An in vitro porcine knee model using bovine extensor tendons was used to simulate a full ACLR graft–cortical button construct. Although studies have reported the similarities between human knees and porcine knees, their inherent material differences cannot be ignored. 1 Variability in supplied porcine legs’ cortical bone density may have contributed to differing failure modes, as softer or thinner cortical bone can fail at the implant-bone interface, leading to the button flipping into the tunnel. In addition, this time-zero in vitro biomechanical study does not factor in any postoperative healing that might occur, which in turn may mitigate late excursion and residual knee laxity. Clinical studies are required to further support the comparisons assessed in this study.

Conclusion

All constructs tested withstood forces exceeding those expected during early and total rehabilitation after ACLR, confirming adequate initial biomechanical strength across both ALDs and FLDs. Differences in excursion and maximum load were associated with the type of locking mechanism and resultant failure mode. These findings suggest distinct mechanical behaviors between device classes, although it remains uncertain whether these differences translate into clinically meaningful outcomes.
