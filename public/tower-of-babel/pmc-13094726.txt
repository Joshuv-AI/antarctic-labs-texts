Overlaps in mammalian iron and manganese homeostasis: recent advances
Authors: Milankumar Prajapati, Lauren Chiu, Jaydyn Akpengbe, Christos Costeas, Thomas B. Bartnikas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Iron and manganese are essential nutrients yet toxic in excess. Given this, levels of these metals are carefully regulated in the body by specific molecular mechanisms. In this review, we discuss recently reported overlaps in iron and manganese homeostasis in mammalian systems, with a focus on intestinal absorption and gastrointestinal excretion. We begin with the current understanding of iron and manganese homeostasis, then present causes and consequences of imbalances in levels of these metals in the body. Notably, while manganese is best known as a neurotoxicant, multiple recent studies have reported that variations in manganese levels correlate with a wide variety of parameters of health and disease. We then highlight deficiency in the manganese transport protein SLC30A10, the first reported inherited cause of manganese excess, and recent studies of SLC30A10 from our group and others that demonstrate three intriguing overlaps between iron and manganese homeostasis. First, intestinal iron transporters DMT1 and ferroportin are essential for manganese absorption and overload in SLC30A10 deficiency. Second, intestinal SLC30A10 downregulates manganese absorption when pathways of iron absorption are upregulated. Third, manganese excess promotes SLC30A10 expression by perturbing regulation of hypoxia-inducible factors, transcription factors that are essential for the cellular response to iron imbalance. We also briefly review SLC39A14 and SLC39A8 deficiency, two other inherited diseases of manganese imbalance, and the current understanding of the function of SLC39A14 and SLC39A8. We conclude with a discussion of active, unresolved questions in need of further investigation that will enhance our understanding of the interplay between iron and manganese homeostasis in mammalian systems.

Iron homeostasis

Iron is a dietary nutrient and metal that is essential for health yet toxic in excess (Charlebois and Pantopoulos 2023). It is needed for key biological processes including oxygen delivery, mitochondrial function, and DNA repair. At toxic levels, iron catalyzes formation of reactive oxygen species, resulting in oxidative stress. Given the essential-yet-toxic dual nature of this metal, iron levels are carefully regulated in the body (Galy et al. 2024). Dietary absorption is a key determinant of body iron levels. Iron is absorbed in two forms, heme and non-heme. Our understanding of non-heme iron absorption exceeds that of heme iron absorption. Dietary non-heme iron is imported into enterocytes by divalent metal transporter 1 (DMT1, SLC11A2) (Fig. 1). Iron is then trafficked across the enterocyte cell body and exported from the enterocyte into blood by ferroportin (SLC40A1). It then circulates bound to the serum protein transferrin for distribution to bone marrow and other sites in the body, with transferrin internalized into cells by transferrin receptor 1 (TFR1)-mediated endocytosis (Bartnikas 2012). Under conditions of excess, iron is also present in the blood in a pathological form known as non-transferrin-bound iron (NTBI). The biochemical nature of NTBI has yet to be firmly established, although NTBI import into liver and other tissues is dependent upon SLC39A14 (ZIP14), a transporter that imports manganese and zinc under physiological conditions (Aydemir and Cousins 2018). SLC39A14-dependent import of NTBI into hepatocytes is an essential prerequisite for biliary excretion, although the full mechanism and relevance of biliary excretion to iron homeostasis has yet to be established (Prajapati et al. 2021). Dietary iron absorption is highly regulated. One key regulator is hepcidin, a hormone produced largely by the liver. Hepcidin inhibits the activity and induces degradation of ferroportin, thereby limiting iron export from enterocytes into blood (Galy et al. 2024). Hepcidin can also negatively regulate DMT1 (Yamaji et al. 2004; Mena et al. 2006, 2008; Brasse-Lagnel et al. 2011; Bergamaschi et al. 2017). Hepcidin is abundantly expressed in states of iron excess and inflammation, inhibiting iron absorption. Hepcidin expression is suppressed by iron deficiency and erythropoietic drive, stimulating iron absorption. In addition to hepcidin, hypoxia-inducible factors (HIFs) and iron regulatory proteins (IRPs) regulate iron absorption (Galy et al. 2024). If hepcidin can be considered a key regulator of systemic iron homeostasis, HIFs and IRPs can be viewed as key regulators of cellular iron homeostasis.

HIFs are transcription factors composed of a regulated α subunit (HIF1A, HIF2A/EPAS1, or HIF3A) and a constitutively expressed β subunit (HIF1B/ARNT1) (Xu et al. 2017). In iron deficiency, α subunits are spared from hydroxylation by iron-dependent prolyl hydroxylases (PHDs) and subsequent proteasomal degradation, resulting in increased HIF activity. HIFs play critical but tissue-specific roles in regulation of iron homeostasis, particularly at the level of iron absorption. Intestinal HIF2 is essential for upregulation of intestinal DMT1 and ferroportin levels in mice on iron-deficient diets (Shah et al. 2009; Mastrogiannaki et al. 2009; Taylor et al. 2011; Anderson et al. 2013; Schwartz et al. 2019). Hepatic HIF2 also impacts iron absorption indirectly by driving expression of erythropoietin, which stimulates suppression of hepatic hepcidin by erythroferrone and other bone marrow-derived factors (Peyssonnaux et al. 2007; Mastrogiannaki et al. 2011; Srole and Ganz 2021).

IRPs (IRP1/ACO1; IRP2/IREB2) are RNA-binding proteins that bind to iron-responsive elements (IREs) in untranslated regions (UTRs) of mRNAs, including HIF2, inhibiting their translation or preventing their degradation (Kühn 2015). In iron deficiency, IRP1 loses an iron-sulfur cluster essential for its activity as an aconitase and gains RNA-binding activity; IRP2 is spared from iron-dependent degradation and accumulates. IRPs contribute prominently to regulation of iron absorption. An IRE in the 3’ UTR of DMT1 mRNA is essential for upregulation of intestinal DMT1 in iron-deficient mice (Qatato et al. 2022). Most ferroportin mRNAs possess a 5’ UTR IRE, but enterocytes also express an IRE-free isoform which is spared from IRP-driven repression so that ferroportin expression is not suppressed in iron deficiency (Zhang et al. 2009; Galy et al. 2013). HIF2 is also an IRP target. IRP1 can block translation of HIF2A mRNA via a 5’ UTR IRE, limiting HIF2A-mediated DMT1 expression (Sanchez et al. 2007; Anderson et al. 2013; Ghosh et al. 2013; Wilkinson and Pantopoulos 2013). However, intestinal HIF2 upregulates intestinal DMT1 and ferroportin in iron-deficient mice, indicating that IRP-driven HIF downregulation is insufficient to minimize HIF activity in iron deficiency (Shah et al. 2009; Mastrogiannaki et al. 2009; Taylor et al. 2011; Anderson et al. 2013; Schwartz et al. 2019). This may reflect the fact that PHDs are iron-dependent and thus less active in iron deficiency. The key impact of IRPs on iron absorption may involve ferritin, a multi-subunit iron storage protein composed of light and heavy chains. The mRNAs encoding both ferritin chains contain 5’ UTR IREs. Intestinal deficiency in both IRP1 and IRP2 increases intestinal ferritin levels and impairs iron absorption in mice, which is attributed to iron sequestration by ferritin within enterocytes (Galy et al. 2013). Intestinal IRP2 deficiency increases intestinal iron and ferritin levels in mice, indicating that IRP2 deficiency is sufficient to perturb enterocyte iron and ferritin levels (Ferring-Appel et al. 2009).

Even with multiple molecular mechanisms in place to maintain iron at sufficient but non-toxic levels, iron excess and deficiency are still prevalent conditions that compromise health (Charlebois and Pantopoulos 2023). Iron overload is typically due to hereditary hemochromatosis or chronic transfusions for the treatment of transfusion-dependent anemias. Hereditary hemochromatosis is characterized by a common pathway of hepcidin deficiency. While most cases arise from the C282Y mutation in HFE, the disease can also be caused by rare mutations in hepcidin itself (HAMP) or the signaling proteins required for its hepatic expression, such as hemojuvelin (HJV) and transferrin receptor 2 (TFR2) (Brissot et al. 2018). Much more common than iron overload, iron deficiency impacts 1–2 billion people in the world and 10–15% of individuals in the United States (Pasricha et al. 2021; Tawfik et al. 2024). It can be caused by inadequate intake, impaired absorption, and increased demand during development and pregnancy (Kumar et al. 2022). Iron deficiency can also be due to blood loss, physiologic or otherwise. Persistent hepcidin upregulation in the setting of inflammation and infection can also drive iron deficiency. Iron deficiency has multiple consequences. When severe enough, it leads to anemia and impairs neurocognitive development and function (Pivina et al. 2019; Schildroth et al. 2022; Gao et al. 2023). Iron deficiency also perturbs cardiac function and contributes to cardiovascular disease (Lakhal-Littleton 2019; Sawicki et al. 2023; Cirovic et al. 2025). Furthermore, iron deficiency also causes blood, brain, and liver manganese excess and increases dietary manganese absorption as discussed below (Ye et al. 2017; Bjørklund et al. 2020).

Manganese homeostasis

Like iron, manganese is a dietary nutrient and metal that is essential yet toxic in excess (Chen et al. 2018; Erikson and Aschner 2019; Baj et al. 2023). It is critical for key processes including antioxidant defense and protein glycosylation. Manganese toxicity is attributed to oxidative and ER stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and other defects. To minimize toxicity, manganese levels are carefully regulated largely by hepatobiliary excretion, although intestinal excretion can also contribute. Manganese undergoes enterohepatic circulation, but mechanisms are not well understood. No mechanisms for regulating manganese absorption in response to changes in systemic manganese status have been identified to date. For example, we have observed that manganese absorption does not differ in mice on manganese-deficient, -sufficient, or -rich diets (Prajapati et al. 2025). However, manganese absorption is impacted by iron status, as discussed below.

Our understanding of manganese homeostasis advanced significantly since 2012 with the discoveries of the first known inherited causes of manganese excess (SLC30A10 and SLC39A14 mutations) and deficiency (SLC39A8 mutations) (Anagianni and Tuschl 2019). SLC30A10 (ZNT10), SLC39A14 (ZIP14), and SLC39A8 (ZIP8) play distinct yet essential roles in manganese homeostasis (Fujishiro and Kambe 2022). These proteins are membrane-bound metal transporters expressed in hepatocytes, enterocytes, neurons, and other cell types. While their full roles in manganese homeostasis are an active area of research, they play critical roles in manganese absorption, distribution, and excretion. SLC39A8 contributes to dietary manganese absorption, although unidentified factors must contribute as intestinal SLC39A8 deficiency only partially abrogates manganese absorption and does not prominently impact manganese levels in mice (Choi et al. 2024b; Wu et al. 2025) (Fig. 2). DMT1 is a potential candidate, but intestinal DMT1 deficiency does not inhibit manganese absorption in wild-type mice (Shawki et al. 2015). Once in enterocytes, manganese is trafficked across the cell body then released into the blood, but proteins essential for intracellular manganese trafficking and basolateral manganese export have yet to be identified. Like iron, manganese can bind to transferrin in the blood, but transferrin is not essential for manganese homeostasis (Bu and Bartnikas 2015). Manganese is excreted largely by hepatobiliary excretion (Chen et al. 2018; Erikson and Aschner 2019). Manganese is imported from liver sinusoids into hepatocytes by SLC39A14, then exported into bile by SLC30A10 (Xin et al. 2017; Aydemir et al. 2017; Jenkitkasemwong et al. 2018; Mercadante et al. 2019; Fung and Zhao 2022; Hutchens et al. 2023). SLC39A8 can reclaim bile manganese back into hepatocytes (Lin et al. 2017). Manganese can also be excreted via the intestines, although this route is viewed as secondary to hepatobiliary. In intestinal excretion, manganese is imported from blood into enterocytes by SLC39A14, then exported into the lumen of the gastrointestinal tract by SLC30A10 (Scheiber et al. 2019; Mercadante et al. 2019; Aydemir et al. 2020; Hutchens et al. 2023). Overall, SLC30A10, SLC39A14, and SLC39A8 are key regulators of systemic manganese homeostasis, but also serve more region-specific roles in organs such as the brain (Taylor et al. 2019, 2023; Liu et al. 2023; Choi et al. 2024a).Fig. 2Model of manganese (Mn) transport. While SLC30A10 transports manganese, both SLC39A14 and SLC39A8 can transport iron as well as other metals. As mentioned above, SLC39A14 is essential for import of NTBI into tissues in states of iron excess (Jenkitkasemwong et al. 2015; Yu et al. 2020). SLC39A14 is expressed in enterocytes, but its role in iron absorption or enterocyte iron homeostasis has yet to be delineated. SLC39A8 can also transport iron. Endothelial SLC39A8 plays a minor role in regulating expression of bone morphogenetic protein 6 (BMP6) in mice with iron excess, where BMP6 is a potent stimulator of hepcidin expression (Fisher et al. 2024). SLC39A8 transports iron from the airway into lung tissue in mice (Zhang et al. 2023). Overall, while SLC39A14 plays a critical role in iron homeostasis by transporting NTBI into tissues, SLC39A8 has not yet been demonstrated to play a pivotal role in systemic iron homeostasis.

Given that SLC30A10, SLC39A14, and SLC39A8 are key determinants of manganese homeostasis, it is not surprising that regulation of these transporters is an active area of research. Regulation of SLC30A10, SLC39A14, and SLC39A8 expression is a highly complex process involving diet, inflammation, and metals. With regard to diet, high fructose intake disrupts manganese homeostasis by activating carbohydrate-responsive element binding protein (ChREBP) in the liver, which binds directly to the SLC30A10 gene, increases its expression, and decreases liver manganese levels (Shi et al. 2023). Dietary manganese upregulates SLC30A10 and SLC39A14 and downregulates SLC39A8 in the liver to facilitate hepatic clearance of manganese (Felber et al. 2019; Yu and Zhao 2023). In the intestine, SLC30A10 expression is controlled by the vitamin D receptor (VDR). Vitamin D3 acts as a transcriptional activator via vitamin D response elements (VDREs) in the SLC30A10 promoter, with recent genomic analyses revealing that these effects are distinct from calcium-regulating pathways (Claro da Silva et al. 2016; Li et al. 2020; Aita et al. 2022). The secondary bile acid lithocholic acid also acts as a potent VDR ligand and activates SLC30A10 expression (Ahmad et al. 2020).

With regard to inflammation, SLC39A14 is upregulated by interleukin-6 (IL-6) and nitric oxide in liver, lung, and neuronal cells to sequester serum zinc in tissues (Besecker et al. 2008; Aydemir et al. 2012; Fujishiro et al. 2014; Gálvez-Peralta et al. 2014). Similarly, SLC39A8 is induced by interferon-γ (IFN-γ) in intestinal epithelial cells and by tumor necrosis factor α (TNF-α) and interleukin-1β (IL-1β) in nucleus pulposus cells (Melia et al. 2019; Ding et al. 2021). SLC39A8 is also induced in mice under a variety of inflammatory contexts, leading to decreased liver manganese and increased biliary manganese levels (Sunuwar et al. 2023). Upregulation of SLC39A14 and SLC39A8 by inflammatory signals not only results in sequestration of metals from pathogens but also modulation of immune signaling. In monocytes and macrophages, nuclear factor κB (NF-κB) induces SLC39A8 to import zinc, which then inhibits IκB kinase, creating a negative feedback loop that restrains excessive inflammation (Liu et al. 2013; Pyle et al. 2017). Conversely, in adaptive immunity, SLC39A8 expression in T cells exacerbates collagen-induced arthritis by enhancing pathogenic cytokine responses (Kang et al. 2021). However, pathogens can hijack this system, as Dengue virus induces SLC39A8 to expand the intracellular zinc pools required for viral replication (Panwar et al. 2021).

Beyond transcriptional control, these transporters also respond to metals. Cellular iron loading increases cell-surface SLC39A8 and SLC39A14 expression (Wang et al. 2012; Zhao et al. 2014). Accordingly, SLC39A14 protein levels are upregulated in mouse models of iron-loading diseases including hypotransferrinemia and hereditary hemochromatosis, where the transporter facilitates NTBI uptake into tissues (Nam et al. 2013; Sterling et al. 2017). SLC39A14 is also regulated under iron deficiency, where it undergoes glycosylation-dependent proteasomal degradation (Zhao et al. 2014). Manganese triggers lysosomal degradation of SLC39A14 to minimize manganese uptake under conditions of excess (Thompson and Wessling-Resnick 2019). Cadmium induces SLC39A8 expression via NF-κB and c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK) pathways (Fujie et al. 2022). Lastly, in brain microvascular endothelial cells, calcium positively regulates trafficking of SLC39A8 and SLC39A14 to the plasma membrane, enhancing manganese uptake (Steimle et al. 2022).

HIFs also regulate SLC30A10, SLC39A8, and SLC39A14. Under conditions of manganese excess, manganese inhibits PHD2 (Liu et al. 2021; Gurol et al. 2024). PHD2 inhibition leads to increased HIF levels, which drive SLC30A10 expression and increased manganese excretion. This represents a homeostatic mechanism by which manganese excess stimulates manganese excretion. HIFs also upregulate SLC39A8 and SLC39A14 in inflammatory synoviocytes and chondrocytes, but the in vivo implications of these cell culture findings have yet to be reported (Lee et al. 2016; Hatano et al. 2021). The full impact of HIF-dependent regulation of manganese transporters is unknown and is particularly salient to our understanding of manganese-iron interactions, as discussed further below.

As mentioned above, manganese is critical for health but toxic in excess. In contrast to iron deficiency, manganese deficiency is rare, with no published reports of acquired cases. SLC39A8 deficiency results in manganese deficiency, glycosylation defects, and developmental dysfunction (Anagianni and Tuschl 2019). Much more common than deficiency, manganese overload can occur in multiple contexts (Chen et al. 2018; Erikson and Aschner 2019). Environmental exposure such as ingestion of contaminated water can result in increased manganese levels in the body, as does industrial exposure in mining and metal working where manganese-rich particulates or fumes are inhaled and cause a neurodegenerative condition known as manganism. Manganese excess can also be caused by impaired excretion due to liver dysfunction or rare genetic defects including deficiency in SLC30A10 and SLC39A14. Total parenteral nutrition can lead to increased manganese levels, as patients can be administered manganese at inappropriate levels, and such patients may have underlying disease impairing gastrointestinal excretion (Reinert and Forbes 2021). Finally, iron deficiency can also result in manganese excess (Ye et al. 2017; Bjørklund et al. 2020). This is attributed to upregulation of intestinal iron transporters leading to increased manganese absorption, although the underlying molecular mechanisms have never been established. Furthermore, the impact of manganese overload on the multiple consequences of iron deficiency has yet to be explored.

A well-established consequence of manganese overload is impaired neurocognitive development and function. However, multiple studies over the past 15 years have shown associations between increased manganese levels and aberrant markers/dysfunction in other systems. Manganese levels correlate with a variety of parameters of health and disease in the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) and other studies (Table 1). In the metabolic and hepatic domains, elevated blood manganese correlates with increased visceral adipose tissue mass (Tao et al. 2022), sarcopenia (Huang et al. 2024), hepatic steatosis, and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) (Zhang et al. 2022, 2024; Spaur et al. 2022; You and Chen 2025). Cardiovascular assessments have linked manganese levels to hypertension (Lee and Kim 2011), structural cardiac abnormalities (Sun et al. 2024), and cardiovascular mortality (Pu 2024). Manganese levels are also associated with respiratory and immune perturbations, including reduced lung function (Shu et al. 2021; Fan et al. 2023), increased systemic inflammation (Zhong et al. 2023), susceptibility to latent tuberculosis (Qi et al. 2024), as well as thyroid hormone disruptions (Guo et al. 2018; Corrales Vargas et al. 2022). Importantly, the cohorts summarized in Table 1 represent general populations, not patients with severe diseases like SLC30A10 or SLC39A14 deficiency. This suggests that even commonly occurring variations in physiological manganese levels are clinically relevant. These variations could reflect environmental factors such as varied diets and/or genetic polymorphisms. Notably, in otherwise healthy individuals, common, non-coding SLC30A10 single nucleotide polymorphisms are associated with blood Mn levels (up to 40% variance) and neurologic function (Ng et al. 2015; SLC30A10 deficiency

SLC30A10 is an apical manganese export protein found in hepatocytes, enterocytes, neurons, and other cells. SLC30A10 deficiency causes severe manganese excess, dystonia, cirrhosis, polycythemia, and increased erythropoietin levels (Anagianni and Tuschl 2019). Manganese overload is attributed to impaired gastrointestinal excretion, dystonia and cirrhosis to manganese toxicity, and polycythemia to increased erythropoietin levels. Patients are treated with chelators and oral iron. Chelators bind to manganese and promote its excretion from the body, while oral iron is believed to outcompete dietary manganese for absorption, resulting in decreasing dietary manganese uptake into the body. Studies from our group and others have shown that SLC30A10 is essential for manganese excretion because it exports manganese from hepatocytes into bile and from enterocytes into the lumen of the gastrointestinal tract (Leyva-Illades et al. 2014; Hutchens et al. 2017; Xia et al. 2017; Taylor et al. 2019; Mercadante et al. 2019). We have also demonstrated that erythropoietin excess is caused by upregulation of HIF2A in the liver, leading to HIF2-dependent erythropoietin synthesis by the liver (Prajapati et al. 2024). More recently, we explored the intriguing clinical observation that treatment of SLC30A10-deficient patients with oral iron can attenuate disease characteristics including dystonia and polycythemia. Given that DMT1 and ferroportin have been implicated in manganese transport (Knöpfel et al. 2005; Garrick et al. 2006; Thompson et al. 2007; Yin et al. 2010; Wang et al. 2011; Madejczyk and Ballatori 2012; Fujishiro et al. 2012; Illing et al. 2012; Kim et al. 2013; Seo and Wessling-Resnick 2015; Seo et al. 2016; Tai et al. 2016; Wolff et al. 2018; Choi et al. 2019), we hypothesized that these intestinal iron transporters mediate manganese absorption in mice with whole body SLC30A10 deficiency. While intestinal deficiency in DMT1 or ferroportin did not impact manganese levels in wild-type mice as shown previously by others (Shawki et al. 2015; Jin et al. 2019), it did decrease manganese levels and absorption in SLC30A10-deficient mice (Prajapati et al. 2025). Overall, DMT1 and ferroportin are essential for manganese absorption and excess in SLC30A10 deficiency, although the impact of DMT1 and ferroportin on manganese homeostasis in other contexts is contested.

If DMT1 and ferroportin contribute to manganese overload in SLC30A10 deficiency, why do other diseases of iron overload due to upregulated DMT1- and ferroportin-driven iron absorption not result in increased manganese levels? Hereditary hemochromatosis, caused by inherited defects in hepcidin expression, is one such disease in which aberrantly upregulated iron absorption leads to iron overload (Galy et al. 2024). We hypothesized that increased manganese absorption in this disease is attenuated by SLC30A10-dependent manganese export from enterocytes into the lumen of the gastrointestinal tract, resulting in no net increase in manganese absorption or levels. To test this, we employed a hemochromatosis mouse model due to deficiency in hemojuvelin, a BMP coreceptor essential for hepcidin expression. Intestinal SLC30A10 deficiency increased manganese levels and absorption without impacting iron excess in hemochromatosis mice, indicating that intestinal SLC30A10 attenuates manganese absorption and overload in this disease (Prajapati et al. 2025). Overall, our studies demonstrate that SLC30A10 mediates gastrointestinal manganese excretion under conditions of manganese excess and suppresses dietary manganese absorption when iron absorption pathways are upregulated.

SLC39A14 and SLC39A8 deficiency

As described above, SLC39A14 is a manganese transport protein found in hepatocytes, enterocytes, neurons, and other cells. It imports metal across the basolateral cell membrane. At the systemic level, SLC39A14 mediates biliary manganese excretion by importing manganese from blood into hepatocytes and intestinal manganese excretion by importing manganese from blood into enterocytes. From a biochemical perspective, SLC39A14 acts upstream of SLC30A10 (Fig. 2). Patients with SLC39A14 deficiency develop manganese excess and dystonia but not cirrhosis, polycythemia, and increased erythropoietin levels as observed in patients with SLC30A10 deficiency (Anagianni and Tuschl 2019). In SLC39A14 deficiency, manganese excess and dystonia are respectively attributed to impaired gastrointestinal excretion and manganese neurotoxicity. The absence of cirrhosis, polycythemia, and increased erythropoietin levels in SLC39A14 deficiency most likely reflects that the liver is spared from manganese overload in this disease. Patients with SLC39A14 deficiency are treated with chelators. Oral iron supplementation is unlikely to be as effective as it is for SLC30A10 deficiency, because increased dietary manganese absorption has not been documented in models of SLC39A14 deficiency nor is it likely to occur. We attribute increased dietary manganese absorption in SLC30A10 deficiency to liver manganese excess driving HIF-dependent erythropoietin expression, but the liver is spared from manganese overload in SLC39A14 deficiency.

Like SLC30A10 and SLC39A14, SLC39A8 is a manganese transport protein found in hepatocytes, enterocytes, and other cell types. It imports metal across the apical cell membrane. At the systemic level, SLC39A8 has a well-established role in attenuating biliary manganese excretion by importing manganese from bile into hepatocytes. A critical role in dietary manganese absorption for this transporter is less well established, as intestinal SLC39A8 deficiency only partially abrogates manganese absorption and does not prominently impact manganese levels in mice (Choi et al. 2024b; Wu et al. 2025). From a biochemical perspective, SLC39A8 performs the opposite function of SLC30A10. As such, SLC39A8 deficiency results in manganese deficiency (Anagianni and Tuschl 2019). Patients with SLC39A8 deficiency present early in life with a multitude of characteristics including failure to thrive, impaired growth, developmental delay, and neurologic deficits. They also exhibit impaired protein glycosylation, which is attributed to impaired activity of manganese-dependent galactosylation enzymes. Patients are treated with oral manganese supplementation.

Active questions

The past 15 years have seen impressive advances in our understanding of the molecular basis of manganese homeostasis, beginning with the first reports of patients with deficiency in SLC30A10, SLC39A14, and SLC39A8. Since then, our knowledge of overlaps between iron and manganese metabolism has expanded. One of the best examples of this is the fact that SLC39A14 imports manganese under conditions of health but iron under conditions of iron excess (Jenkitkasemwong et al. 2015). However, prominent questions remain.

How is manganese absorbed from the diet in conditions of health? And does this involve proteins that transport manganese exclusively or iron as well? We have shown that intestinal deficiency in DMT1 or ferroportin impairs manganese absorption and overload in SLC30A10-deficient mice, but SLC30A10 deficiency is an extreme form of pathophysiology. In wild-type mice, intestinal SLC39A8 deficiency only partially abrogates manganese absorption while intestinal DMT1 deficiency has no impact. Is there redundancy in that SLC39A8 and DMT1 can compensate for the other when one is deficient? Or is another transporter or process responsible for import of dietary manganese into enterocytes? The means by which manganese is exported from enterocytes into blood is also entirely unknown in conditions of health. Another prominent gap of knowledge is the mechanism by which manganese is trafficked intracellularly. Are there manganese chaperones, akin to the copper chaperone for copper/zinc superoxide dismutase (CCS) and polyC-binding protein 1 (PCBP1) which delivers intracellular iron to ferritin and other targets (Boyd et al. 2020; Philpott et al. 2020)? These chaperones minimize toxicity of unbound metal and ensure that metals needed for specific targets are not scavenged by intracellular detoxification mechanisms. If manganese chaperones do exist, they would presumably shuttle recently imported manganese to destinations throughout the cell including apo-proteins in need of manganese for activation and to membrane-associated proteins for transport into organelles and out of the cell.

We have shown that intestinal DMT1 and ferroportin mediate manganese absorption and overload in SLC30A10-deficient mice. Do DMT1 and ferroportin transport manganese in other pathophysiological conditions? Iron deficiency is the most common nutritional deficiency in the world and leads to increased manganese absorption and levels. We hypothesize that both intestinal DMT1 and ferroportin mediate manganese absorption and excess in iron deficiency, as they do in SLC30A10 deficiency. We predict that this reflects not only upregulation of DMT1 and ferroportin in response to iron deficiency but also less competition by iron for manganese transport by these proteins. Given that HIFs can stimulate SLC30A10 expression, we also speculate that activation of intestinal HIFs in iron deficiency leads not only to upregulation of DMT1 and ferroportin but also SLC30A10 and perhaps SLC39A14. Upregulation of SLC30A10 and SLC39A14 in the small intestine and liver would attenuate manganese absorption and excess observed in iron deficiency. Furthermore, impaired SLC30A10 and SLC39A14 expression/function would be expected to exacerbate manganese excess in iron deficiency. This could occur in iron-deficient individuals that carry common SLC30A10 single nucleotide polymorphisms that diminish SLC30A10 expression but do not cause complete SLC30A10 deficiency (Ng et al. 2015; Wahlberg et al. 2016, 2018; Lindner et al. 2022).

With our hypothesis that HIFs mediate the cellular response to iron deficiency but also attenuate development of manganese excess that results from iron deficiency, we propose that HIFs serve as critical regulators of the body’s response to iron deficiency and manganese excess (Fig. 3). With iron deficiency, manganese excess, or hypoxia, PHD-dependent HIF degradation would be impaired and HIFs would accumulate. HIFs would then upregulate intestinal iron transporters to increase iron absorption but also upregulate intestinal and hepatic manganese transporters to promote gastrointestinal manganese excretion. With this model, we must also include IRPs for several reasons. First, IRPs promote intestinal DMT1 expression, which would increase dietary iron and manganese absorption. IRPs can impair ferroportin expression, but enterocytes express an IRE-free ferroportin mRNA which should be spared from IRP-driven suppression. IRP1 can also block HIF2A expression, which would counteract any HIF2-dependent increases in DMT1, ferroportin, or SLC30A10. At this point, it is not clear if IRPs have a net positive or negative effect on manganese excess resulting from iron deficiency. Second, levels/activity of IRPs, like HIFs, increase not only with iron deficiency but also hypoxia (Mayank et al. 2019) and manganese excess. Manganese can promote IRP binding to target RNAs, although not all studies support this finding (Oshiro et al. 2002; Kwik-Uribe et al. 2003; Kwik-Uribe and Smith 2006; Wang et al. 2006). Nevertheless, it is possible that manganese could attenuate HIF expression, thereby counteracting HIF-dependent attenuation of manganese excretion. The impact of iron deficiency versus manganese excess on HIF-driven gene expression could be disentangled using a variety of approaches, but one of the simplest would be to compare the phenotypes of mice raised on diets deficient in iron or both iron and manganese—any differences between the two groups should reflect direct or indirect effects of manganese in the setting of iron deficiency. Another issue to consider with this model is that other metals may impact or be impacted by this pathway. For example, intestinal DMT1 imports copper in mice with iron-deficiency anemia, and other overlaps between copper and iron are well documented (Arredondo and Núñez 2005; Wang et al. 2018).
