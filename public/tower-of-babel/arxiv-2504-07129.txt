Near-Inertial Pollard Waves Modeling the Arctic Halocline
Christian Puntini

Abstract
. We present an explicit exact solution to the governing equations describing the vertical structure of the Arctic Ocean region centered around the North Pole. The solution describes a stratified water column with three constant-density regions: a motionless bottom layer, a top layer with uniform velocity and a middle layer - the halocline - described by nonhydrostatic, near-inertial Pollard waves.

1.
Introduction

The Arctic Ocean, located in the Northern Hemisphere and encompassing the North Pole, is a small ocean about
4000\,\mathrm{km}
long and
2500\,\mathrm{km}
wide, covering an area of around 10 million
\,\mathrm{km}^{2}
. It includes two main deep basins (around
4000\,\mathrm{m}
deep) separated by the underwater Lomonosov Ridge, the Amerasian Basin (divided into the Makarov and Canada basins), and the smaller Eurasian Basin (divided into the Amundsen and Nansen basins), and is surrounded by shallow seas (less than
400\,\mathrm{m}
deep): the Barents Sea, the Kara Sea, the Laptev Sea, the Siberian Sea, the Chukchi Sea and the Beaufort Sea (see
[
2
]
or
[
37
]
).

The primary oceanic inflows are from the Atlantic Ocean through the Fram Strait and the Barents Sea, and from the Pacific Ocean via the Bering Strait. Significant freshwater inflows also come from rivers in North America and Siberia. These inflows are major factors influencing the salinity and temperature variability of the water column, with salinity playing a key role in stratification, creating a halocline rather than a thermocline (which instead is typical of mid-latitudes and equatorial regions). As a matter of fact, one of the main feature of the Arctic Ocean is that it’s predominantly stratified by salinity (hence it is defined as a
\beta
-ocean) rather than by temperature (the so called
\alpha
-oceans) (see
[
2
]
or
[
3
]
).

Moreover, the central region of Arctic Ocean - the one centered around the North Pole and subject of our analysis - is entirely covered by a relatively thin (not exceeding
2\,\mathrm{m}
of thickness) layer of sea ice, which reduces in summer when temperature increases. Due to the global warming, the sea ice is rapidly declining, and the Arctic region is warming faster than the global average, due to the process known as Arctic amplification. This makes the Arctic highly vulnerable to climate change, potentially leading to an ice-free Arctic Ocean in summer and with thinner and more mobile sea ice in winter. The transition to a seasonally ice-free Arctic will profoundly affect Arctic oceanography, the marine ecosystems it supports, and the global climate (see
[
38
]
).

In addition to the atmospheric conditions, the Arctic sea ice is influenced by the sea water beneath it. The Arctic Ocean is stratified into a cold and fresh surface mixed layer (SML), with a depth between
5
to
100
meters, a halocline below the mixed layer with a base depth ranging from
40
to
200
meters, and a layer of warmer and saltier Atlantic Water (AW) (see
[
24
]
). The depths of the boundaries of this layers varies mainly according to temperature and presence of ice. In general, it can be said that in winter, with lower temperatures and increased ice presence, their depth increases, whereas in summer, these decrease (see
[
29
]
).

The halocline is a region of strong stratification, which prevents interaction of the ice cover with AW heat by the direct surface-generated mixing of the SML (see
[
32
]
and
[
18
]
for an in-detail description of the Arctic water column and its main physical processes). Consequently, the halocline layer, situated above the saltier and denser water, is fundamental for the formation of the ice cover (see
[
32
]
).

The presence of the permanent ice layer makes physical measurements particularly difficult. Hence, due to the lack of data from observation, the theoretical analysis of this region is particularly useful.

The physical processes in the mixed layer, mainly the ice-motion induced by wind and the Transpolar Drift Current (TDC) - the Arctic Ocean current transporting surface waters and sea ice from the Laptev Sea and the East Siberian Sea towards Fram Strait, with a speed of around
0.07\,\mathrm{ms^{-1}}
around the North Pole - induces a current with magnitude approximately
0.1\,\mathrm{ms^{-1}}
on the lower boundary of the mixed layer (see
[
18
]
and data therein).

The aim of this paper is to describe the halocline layer via an explicit and exact solution of the (approximated) nonlinear equations governing the ocean dynamics. Such a solution will represent nonlinear waves propagating in the direction of the Transpolar Drift Current. Just above the halocline we consider a uniform current, and the layer of Atlantic Water under the halocline is considered in hydrostatic state.

The solution in this study is constructed by adapting the Pollard’s solution, presented in 1970
[
31
]
for surface waves accounting for the effects of Earth’s rotation and extending the remarkable solution provided by Gerstner in
[

]

. Recently, Gerstner-like solution were used to describe equatorially-trapped waves

Even if within the halocline one could distinguish between the cold halocline layer in the Eurasian Basin, the Pacific Halocline Waters in the Amerasian Basin and the lower halocline water (see

The paper is structured as follow:

•

in Section 2 we derive the governing equations. More precisely, as we are investigating the fluid dynamics around the North Pole, where the classical spherical coordinates fail, we need to adopt the rotated spherical coordinates developed in
[

•

Section 3 is devoted to the main result of our analysis. We find an explicit solution, using the Lagrangian description of the flow, detailing the internal wave motion in the halocline by near-inertial Pollard waves propagating parallel to the Transpolar Drift Current. The dispersion relation for the nonlinear waves is obtained imposing the dynamic boundary condition (namely the continuity of the pressure across the two layers: the top and the bottom of the halocline).

In previous works
[
12
]
,
[
21
]
,
[
26
]
, with only one dynamic boundary condition, two modes of the wave motion were found, one fast mode standard to the theory of internal waves, and a second one, slow, with the period close to the inertial period of the Earth
T_{i}=\frac{2\pi}{f}
. Constantin & Monismith
[
12
]
refer to this slow-mode wave as inertial Gerstner wave.

In this work, imposing two dynamic conditions, one per boundary of the halocline, only the slow mode is show to be relevant. Henceforth, the waves will be near-inertial;

•

Section 4 is aimed at the review of some properties of the flow: vorticity, Lagrangian and Eulerian mean velocities, Stokes drift and mass flux;

•

in Section 5 we study the linearized version of the problem, still adopting the Lagrangian approach. The near-inertial slow mode is obtained again, and it is shown that the expression for the pressure in the linearized case is an approximation of the one in the nonlinear analysis;

•

finally, we conclude in Section 6 with a discussion on the results.

2.
Governing (approximated) equations

We consider the Earth to be a sphere of radius
R\approx 6371\,\mathrm{km}
. The classical spherical coordinate system is not suitable for the study of flows in regions centered around the North (and South) Pole, as longitude in undefined at the poles. This result is a consequence of the ‘hairy ball theorem’ (see
[
4
]
and
[
11
]

In this section, we review the construction of the rotated spherical coordinate system developed in
[
10
]
(see
[
11

Let us start by considering the standard Cartesian coordinate system
(X,Y,Z)
with basis
(\mathbf{e}_{1},\mathbf{e}_{2},\mathbf{e}_{3})

\vec{OE}
East,
\vec{ON}
North, respectively, and define the classical spherical coordinates
(\varphi,\theta,r)

\varphi\in[0,2\pi)
and
\theta\in[-\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2}]
being the angles of longitude and latitude respectively, and

the distance from Earth’s center. With respect to these coordinates, a point
P
has position vector

\vec{OP}:=\mathbf{r}=r\cos\theta\cos\varphi\mathbf{e}_{1}+r\cos\theta\sin\varphi\mathbf{e}_{2}+r\sin\theta\mathbf{e}_{3}.

(2.1)

Namely, the change of coordinates is given by

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}X=r\cos\theta\cos\varphi,\\
Y=r\cos\theta\sin\varphi,\\
Z=r\sin\theta,\end{array}\right.\qquad\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\varphi=\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{Y}{X}\right),\\
\theta=\sin^{-1}\left(\frac{Z}{\sqrt{X^{2}+Y^{2}+Z^{2}}}\right),\\

(2.2)

Now, let us define a new Cartesian coordinate system
(\mathbf{e}_{1}^{{\dagger}},\mathbf{e}_{2}^{{\dagger}},\mathbf{e}_{3}^{{\dagger}})
by permuting cyclically the first three Cartesian axes

\mathbf{e}_{1}^{{\dagger}}=\mathbf{e}_{3},\quad\mathbf{e}_{2}^{{\dagger}}=\mathbf{e}_{1},\quad\mathbf{e}_{3}^{{\dagger}}=\mathbf{e}_{2}.

(2.3)

The associated Cartesian coordinates to

Therefore, using (

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\cos\theta^{{\dagger}}\cos\varphi^{{\dagger}}=\cos\theta\sin\varphi,\\

&\sin\theta^{{\dagger}}=\cos\theta\cos\varphi.\end{aligned}\right.

(2.5)

In this new coordinate system the North Pole has coordinates
\varphi^{{\dagger}}=\frac{\pi}{2},\ \theta^{{\dagger}}=0
, and the solution of the system (

) for the Arctic Ocean is

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\varphi^{{\dagger}}=\cot^{-1}(\sin\varphi\cot\theta)\in(0,\pi),\\

(2.6)

The unit basis vectors in the rotated spherical coordinates

(\mathbf{e}_{\varphi}^{{\dagger}},\mathbf{e}_{\theta}^{{\dagger}},\mathbf{e}_{r})

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathbf{e}_{\varphi}^{{\dagger}}=-\sin\varphi^{{\dagger}}\mathbf{e}_{1}^{{\dagger}}+\cos\varphi^{{\dagger}}\mathbf{e}_{2}^{{\dagger}},\\
\mathbf{e}_{\theta}^{{\dagger}}=-\cos\varphi^{{\dagger}}\sin\theta^{{\dagger}}\mathbf{e}_{1}^{{\dagger}}-\sin\varphi^{{\dagger}}\sin\theta^{{\dagger}}\mathbf{e}_{2}^{{\dagger}}+\cos\theta^{{\dagger}}ec^{{\dagger}},\\

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\frac{D\boldsymbol{u}}{Dt}+2\mathbf{\Omega}\times\boldsymbol{u}+\mathbf{\Omega}\times(\mathbf{\Omega}\times\mathbf{r})=\frac{\mathbf{G}-\nabla p}{\rho},\\

2.1.
Standard approximations in geophysical fluid dynamics

2.1.1.
Tangent plane approximation

Let us start by fixing a point
P
on the Earth, with rotated spherical coordinates
(\varphi^{{\dagger}}_{0},\theta^{{\dagger}}_{0},R)
where
R
is the radius of the Earth. The relation between the coordinates
(X^{{\dagger}},Y^{{\dagger}},Z^{{\dagger}})
referred to the basis
(\mathbf{e}_{1}^{{\dagger}},\mathbf{e}_{2}^{{\dagger}},\mathbf{e}_{3}^{{\dagger}})
and rotated spherical coordinates
(\varphi^{{\dagger}},\theta^{{\dagger}},r)
referred to the basis
(\mathbf{e}_{\varphi}^{{\dagger}},\mathbf{e}_{\theta}^{{\dagger}},\mathbf{e}_{r}^{{\dagger}})
is given by

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}X^{{\dagger}}=r\cos\theta^{{\dagger}}\cos\varphi^{{\dagger}},\\
Y^{{\dagger}}=r\cos\theta^{{\dagger}}\sin\varphi^{{\dagger}},\\
Z^{{\dagger}}=r\sin\theta^{{\dagger}}.\end{array}\right.

(2.14)

It is well know that the region around
P
can be approximated by a tangent plane. The sphere of radius
R
is given by
\mathbf{S}^{2}_{R}=\{(X^{{\dagger}},Y^{{\dagger}},Z^{{\dagger}})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\,s.t.\,F(X^{{\dagger}},Y^{{\dagger}},Z^{{\dagger}}):=X^{{\dagger}^{2}}+Y^{{\dagger}^{2}}+Z^{{\dagger}^{2}}-R^{2}=0\}
, hence the normal vector to the sphere at
P
is given by (using (
2.14
))

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{n}}

\displaystyle=\left(\nabla F\right)(P)=2(X_{P}^{{\dagger}},Y_{P}^{{\dagger}},Z_{P}^{{\dagger}})=

(2.15)

\displaystyle=2R(\cos\theta_{0}\cos\varphi_{0},\,\cos\theta_{0}\sin\varphi_{0},\,\sin\theta_{0}).

The vector
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{n}}
is (up to normalizing) equal to the base vector
\mathbf{e}_{r}^{{\dagger}}(P)
(see equation (
2.7
)), and, since the tangent plane to the sphere at the point
P
is the geometric locus of points
M
such that
\vec{PM}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{n}}=\vec{PM}\cdot\mathbf{e}_{r}(P)=0
, it’s an immediate consequence that a basis for the tangent plane at
P
is
(\mathbf{e}_{\varphi}^{{\dagger}}(P),\mathbf{e}_{\theta}^{{\dagger}}(P))
.

On the tangent plane we define the following local coordinates

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\mathsf{x}=R\cos\theta^{{\dagger}}_{0}(\varphi^{{\dagger}}-\varphi^{{\dagger}}_{0}),\\
&\mathsf{y}=R(\theta^{{\dagger}}-\theta^{{\dagger}}_{0}),\\
\end{aligned}\right.

(2.16)

therefore, computing

\nabla\mathsf{x}=R\cos\theta^{{\dagger}}_{0}\ \mathbf{e}_{\varphi}^{{\dagger}}(P),\\
\qquad\nabla\mathsf{y}=R\ \mathbf{e}_{\theta}^{{\dagger}}(P),

(2.17)

we obtain the following basis

\mathbf{e}_{\mathsf{x}}=\frac{\nabla\mathsf{x}}{|\nabla\mathsf{x}|}=\mathbf{e}_{\varphi}(P),\qquad\mathbf{e}_{\mathsf{y}}=\frac{\nabla\mathsf{y}}{|\nabla\mathsf{y}|}=\mathbf{e}_{\theta}(P),

(2.18)

proving that
(\mathbf{e}_{\varphi}^{{\dagger}}(P),\mathbf{e}_{\theta}^{{\dagger}}(P))
is in fact a basis for the tangent plane at
P
.

The concept of tangent plane is always used implicitly for the horizontal variables when adopting a
f
-plane approximation.

The partial derivatives with respect to
(\mathsf{x},\mathsf{y})
are given by

\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathsf{x}}=\frac{1}{R\cos\theta_{0}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\varphi^{{\dagger}}},\qquad\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathsf{y}}=\frac{1}{R}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta^{{\dagger}}}.

(2.19)

2.1.2.
Traditional approximation

In this paragraph we review the ideas leading to the so-called traditional approximation, adapting them to the rotated spherical coordinates. Namely, we will construct this approximation starting from (
2.13
). Note that, even if the velocities
u^{{\dagger}},v^{{\dagger}},w^{{\dagger}}
are defined differently from the ones relative to the classical spherical coordinate system,
u^{{\dagger}},v^{{\dagger}}
are still the horizontal and
w^{{\dagger}}
the vertical components of the velocity vector
\boldsymbol{u}
, hence the observations about their magnitude, on which the traditional approximation is bases, still apply.

For a review of the traditional approximation of the governing equations in classical spherical coordinates we refer to
[
39
]
and
[
23
]
.

The first step involves neglecting metric terms

\frac{1}{r}\left(-u^{{\dagger}}v^{{\dagger}}\tan\theta^{{\dagger}}+u^{{\dagger}}w^{{\dagger}},\,u^{{\dagger}2}\tan\theta^{{\dagger}}+v^{{\dagger}}w^{{\dagger}},\,-u^{{\dagger}2}-v^{{\dagger}2}\right),

(2.20)

which represent the effect of curvature in spherical coordinates, in (
2.13
), and the terms in (
2.10
) and (
2.11
) which involves a velocity multiplied by
\frac{1}{r}
. Because of the thinnes of the atmosphere and ocean, vertical velocities (typically
\leq 0.01\,\mathrm{ms^{-1}}
) are much less than horizontal velocities by a factor of
10^{-4}
on average, therefore we may neglect the term involving
w^{{\dagger}}
in the horizontal components (namely the first two) of the Coriolis acceleration

2\mathbf{\Omega}\times\mathbf{u}=2\Omega\begin{pmatrix}-v^{{\dagger}}\sin\varphi^{{\dagger}}\cos\theta^{{\dagger}}-w^{{\dagger}}\sin\varphi^{{\dagger}}\sin\theta^{{\dagger}}\\
u^{{\dagger}}\sin\varphi^{{\dagger}}\cos\theta^{{\dagger}}-w^{{\dagger}}\cos\varphi^{{\dagger}}\\
u^{{\dagger}}\sin\varphi^{{\dagger}}\sin\theta^{{\dagger}}+v^{{\dagger}}\cos\varphi^{{\dagger}}\end{pmatrix}.

(2.21)

Moreover, in the atmosphere the typical scale of the horizontal velocities is
|u^{{\dagger}}|,\,|v^{{\dagger}}|\approx 10\,\mathrm{ms^{-1}}
, and even less in the ocean, giving that
|2\Omega(u^{{\dagger}}+v^{{\dagger}})|\approx 3\cdot 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{ms^{-2}}
, is negligible compared to gravity acceleration
g\approx 9.8\,\mathrm{ms^{-2}}
. Therefore, the Coriolis acceleration can be approximated as

2\mathbf{\Omega}\times\mathbf{u}\approx 2\Omega\begin{pmatrix}-v^{{\dagger}}\sin\varphi^{{\dagger}}\cos\theta^{{\dagger}}\\
u^{{\dagger}}\sin\varphi^{{\dagger}}\cos\theta^{{\dagger}}\\
0\end{pmatrix}.

(2.22)

These two approximations related to the Coriolis term
2\mathbf{\Omega}\times\mathbf{u}
, the first involving the two horizontal components of the equation, and the second involving the vertical component where the gravity acceleration is present, cannot be done independently of each other. Instead, they must be made together, otherwise the resulting equations fail to conserve energy
[
35
]
.

Lastly, as the ocean is shallow compared to Earth’s radius, we can write
r=R+\mathsf{z}
, with
R
being the Earth’s radius and
\mathsf{z}
increasing in the radial direction. This idea is fundamental for the so-called ‘thin-shell’ approximation (see e.g
[
10
]
). The coordinate
r
is then replaced by
R

\frac{1}{r^{2}}\frac{\partial r^{2}w^{{\dagger}}}{\partial r}
becomes
\frac{\partial w^{{\dagger}}}{\partial\mathsf{z}}
. With the above arguments, the Euler, mass conservation and incompressibility equations reduces to

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial t}+\frac{u^{{\dagger}}}{R\cos\theta^{{\dagger}}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\varphi^{{\dagger}}}+\frac{v^{{\dagger}}}{R}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta^{{\dagger}}}+w^{{\dagger}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathsf{z}}\right]\begin{pmatrix}u^{{\dagger}}\\
v^{{\dagger}}\\
w^{{\dagger}}\\

The simplified model we are considering features three constant densities,

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\rho_{1}a^{2}\frac{k^{4}\mathsf{c}^{2}}{k^{2}\mathsf{c}^{2}-f^{2}}\frac{k^{2}\mathsf{c}^{2}-f^{2}}{k^{2}}=\frac{1}{2}\rho_{1}a^{2}k^{2}\mathsf{c}^{2}>0,

where we used (
3.28
), (
3.29
) and (
3.30
).

Evaluating (
3.35
) at
s=\mathrm{s}_{+}(r)
and differentiating with respect to
r
gives

\mathrm{s}^{\prime}_{+}(r)=\frac{\rho_{0}f\mathsf{c}}{2mAe^{-2m\mathrm{s}_{+}(r)}+(\rho_{1}-\rho_{0})g}>0.

(3.39)

The result in (
3.39
) shows that the upper surface of the halocline reduces its depth as
r
increases (namely in the Eurasian Basin), and increases in depth as
r
decreases (namely in the Amerasian Basin).

3.3.2.
The lower surface of the halocline
\eta_{2}

For every
r\in[-\mathrm{r}_{0},\mathrm{r}_{0}]
, the lower surface of the halocline
\eta_{2}
(also called halocline base) is described by
s=\mathrm{s}_{-}(r)
.

Setting the expression for the pressure in (
3.32
) equal to the one in (
3.6
) at
s=\mathrm{s}_{-}(r)
, provides the fulfillment of the dynamic boundary condition at
\eta_{2}
. Therefore we get

\displaystyle P_{2}-\rho_{2}g(-d_{0}+\mathrm{s}_{-}-ae^{-m\mathrm{s}_{-}}\cos\tau)=

(3.40)

\displaystyle=P_{1}-\rho_{1}g\mathrm{s}_{-}+\frac{\rho_{1}}{2}(b^{2}k^{2}\mathsf{c}^{2}+fbdk\mathsf{c})e^{-2m\mathrm{s}_{-}}+\rho_{1}(bk\mathsf{c}^{2}+df\mathsf{c}+ga)e^{-m\mathrm{s}_{-}}\cos\tau,

which is equivalent to

\left\{\begin{aligned} &P_{2}-P_{1}=\frac{1}{2}\rho_{1}bk\mathsf{c}(bk\mathsf{c}+df)e^{-2m\mathrm{s}_{-}}+(\rho_{2}-\rho_{1})g\mathrm{s}_{-}-\rho_{2}gd_{0},\\
&bk\mathsf{c}^{2}=\frac{\rho_{2}}{\rho_{1}}ga-ga-df\mathsf{c}.\end{aligned}\right.

(3.41)

The halocline base
\eta_{2}
is determined by setting
s=\mathrm{s}_{-}(r)
at a fixed value of
r\in[-\mathrm{r}_{0},\mathrm{r}_{0}]
, where
\mathrm{s}_{-}(r)
is the unique solution of

P_{2}-P_{1}=\frac{1}{2}\rho_{1}bk\mathsf{c}(bk\mathsf{c}+df)e^{-2ms}+(\rho_{2}-\rho_{1})gs-\rho_{2}gd_{0}.

(3.42)

For every fixed
r\in[-\mathrm{r}_{0},\mathrm{r}_{0}]
, the function

s\longmapsto\frac{\rho_{1}}{2}(b^{2}k^{2}\mathsf{c}^{2}+bk\mathsf{c}df)e^{-2ms}+(\rho_{2}-\rho_{1})gs,

(3.43)

is strictly decreasing if
s<-\frac{1}{2m}\ln\left(\frac{B}{2mA}\right)
, where
B=(\rho_{2}-\rho_{1})g>0
and
A>0
as before, and strictly increasing if
s>-\frac{1}{2m}\ln\left(\frac{B}{2mA}\right)
, with a minimum at
\tilde{s}=-\frac{1}{2m}\ln\left(\frac{B}{2mA}\right)
.

We claim that
{B}>{2mA}
. As

\frac{B}{2mA}=\frac{1}{ma^{2}k^{2}\mathsf{c}^{2}}\left(\frac{\rho_{2}-\rho_{1}}{\rho_{1}}\right)g\approx\frac{1.77\cdot 10^{5}}{ma^{2}},

(3.44)

using the relation
k^{2}\mathsf{c}^{2}=\frac{f^{4}\mathsf{c}^{2}}{\mathfrak{g}^{2}}+f^{2}
, cf. (
3.46
) and (
3.48
), with values
\mathsf{c}\approx 0.1\,\mathrm{ms^{-1}}
,
\mathfrak{g}\approx 0.006\,\mathrm{ms^{-2}}
,
f=2\Omega\approx 1.5\cdot 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}
, and
\left(\frac{\rho_{2}-\rho_{1}}{\rho_{1}}\right)g\approx 0.004\,\mathrm{ms^{-2}}
. As a consequence,
\frac{B}{2mA}>1
true if
ma^{2}<1.77\cdot 10^{5}\mathrm{m^{2}}
. By (
3.17
),
a_{\mathrm{max}}=\frac{1}{m}
,
ma^{2}<\frac{1}{m}\lesssim\frac{10}{6}<2
due to (
6.3
), it follows that
\frac{B}{2mA}>1
, and this implies that (
3.43
) is a strictly increasing diffeomorphism from
(0,+\infty)
to
(A,+\infty)
. Therefore, by implicit function theorem there exist a unique (smooth) solution
\mathrm{s}_{-}(r)
. See Figure
3.1
.

3.3.3.
The dispersion relation

The last two equations in (
3.34
) and (
3.41
) are independent of
q,r,s
, therefore we can use them to write

\frac{\rho_{2}}{\rho_{1}}ga-ga-df\mathsf{c}=\left(\frac{\rho_{0}-\rho_{1}}{\rho_{1}}\right)(ga+f\mathsf{c}d)\Longrightarrow f\mathsf{c}d=\left(\frac{\rho_{2}-\rho_{0}}{\rho_{0}}\right)ga.

(3.45)

Defining the reduced gravity

\mathfrak{g}=\left(\frac{\rho_{2}-\rho_{0}}{\rho_{0}}\right)g,

(3.46)

we get

\mathsf{c}=\mathfrak{g}\frac{a}{fd},

(3.47)

that, making use of (
3.29
) and (
3.30
), gives the dispersion relation

f^{4}\mathsf{c}^{2}=\mathfrak{g}^{2}k^{2}\mathsf{c}^{2}-f^{2}\mathfrak{g}^{2},

(3.48)

which is equivalent to

\mathsf{c}^{2}=\frac{f^{2}\mathfrak{g}^{2}}{\mathfrak{g}^{2}k^{2}-f^{4}},

(3.49)

or

k^{2}=\frac{f^{4}}{\mathfrak{g}^{2}}+\frac{f^{2}}{\mathsf{c}^{2}}.

(3.50)

Observe that, (
3.49
) makes sense if and only if

\mathfrak{g}^{2}k^{2}-f^{4}>0\Longleftrightarrow k^{2}>\frac{f^{4}}{\mathfrak{g}^{2}},

(3.51)

as only
\mathsf{c}\in\mathbb{R}
is valid in the context of Pollard (and Gerstner) waves, which is obviously satisfied due to (
3.50
). As
f^{4}\approx 10^{-16}
, from (
3.49
) it follows that

\mathsf{c}^{2}\approx\frac{f^{2}}{k^{2}},

(3.52)

therefore, the modulus of the period of the wave
T=\frac{L}{\mathsf{c}}
is approximately
\frac{2\pi}{f}=T_{i}
, where
T_{i}
is the inertial period of the Earth, so the wave motion describing the halocline surfaces is essentially inertial.

To provide a qualitative value of
k
and consequently of the wavelength
L=\frac{2\pi}{k}
, we fix the reference parameters
\mathsf{c}\approx 0.1\,\mathrm{ms^{-1}}
,
\mathfrak{g}\approx 0.006\,\mathrm{ms^{-2}}
,
f=2\Omega\approx 1.5\cdot 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}
. The dispersion relation (
3.50
) gives an approximate value of the wave number
k\approx 1.5\cdot 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m^{-1}}
the wavelength
L\approx 4.2\,\,\mathrm{km}
.

4.
Other properties of the flow in the halocline layer

4.1.
Vorticity

In contrast to the other layers, where the flow is irrotational, in the halocline the vorticity vector

\omega=\left(\frac{\partial w}{\partial y}-\frac{\partial v}{\partial z},\frac{\partial u}{\partial z}-\frac{\partial w}{\partial x},\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}-\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}\right)

(4.1)

is given by

\displaystyle\omega^{T}=\frac{1}{1-m^{2}a^{2}e^{-2ms}}\begin{pmatrix}\frac{m^{2}af}{k}e^{-ms}\sin\tau\\
\mathsf{c}a(k^{2}-m^{2})e^{-ms}\cos\tau+\mathsf{c}ma^{2}(m^{2}+k^{2})e^{-2ms}\\
fma(\cos\tau+mae^{-ms})e^{-ms}\end{pmatrix},

(4.2)

due to the relations

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\partial(q,r,s)}{\partial(x,y,z)}\right)\left(\frac{\partial(u,v,w)}{\partial(q,r,s)}\right)=\left(\frac{\partial(u,v,w)}{\partial(x,y,z)}\right),

(4.3)

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\partial(q,r,s)}{\partial(x,y,z)}\right)=\left(\frac{\partial(x,y,z)}{\partial(q,r,s)}\right)^{-1},

with

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\partial(q,r,s)}{\partial(x,y,z)}\right)

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\partial q}{\partial x}&\frac{\partial r}{\partial x}&\frac{\partial s}{\partial x}\\
\frac{\partial q}{\partial y}&\frac{\partial r}{\partial y}&\frac{\partial s}{\partial y}\\
\frac{\partial q}{\partial z}&\frac{\partial r}{\partial z}&\frac{\partial s}{\partial z}\end{pmatrix}=

(4.4)

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1+kbe^{-ms}\cos\tau}{J}&-\frac{kde^{-ms}\sin\tau}{J}&-\frac{kae^{-ms}\sin\tau}{J}\\
0&1&0\\
-\frac{mbe^{-ms}\sin\tau}{J}&-\frac{mde^{-ms}\cos\tau-m^{2}ad\,e^{-2ms}}{J}&\frac{1-mae^{-ms}\cos\tau}{J}\end{pmatrix},

where
J=1-m^{2}a^{2}e^{-2ms}
is the determinant given in (
3.15
).

It is therefore evident that the flow is fully three-dimensional.

Observe that the velocity field
\boldsymbol{u}
described in (
3.19
) does not depend on the variable
r
, therefore, as

\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{u}}{\partial y}=\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{u}}{\partial q}\frac{\partial q}{\partial y}+\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{u}}{\partial r}\frac{\partial r}{\partial y}+\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{u}}{\partial s}\frac{\partial s}{\partial y},

(4.5)

making use of (
4.4
), it follows that
\boldsymbol{u}
does non depend on the coordinate
y
, namely

\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}=\frac{\partial v}{\partial y}=\frac{\partial w}{\partial y}=0.

(4.6)

Moreover,

\frac{\partial P}{\partial y}=\frac{\partial P}{\partial q}\frac{\partial q}{\partial y}+\frac{\partial P}{\partial r}\frac{\partial r}{\partial y}+\frac{\partial P}{\partial s}\frac{\partial s}{\partial y}=\frac{\partial P}{\partial r}=0,

(4.7)

where the last equality is due to (
3.26
), therefore also the pressure in independent of
y
in the halocline layer.

4.2.
Mean flow properties in the halocline layer

Following
[
20
]
, we examine the mean velocities, the Stokes drift, and the mass flux for the internal water waves in the halocline layer. The mean Eulerian velocity is the mean velocity of the fluid at a fixed point, while the mean Lagrangian velocity is the mean velocity following a selected fluid particle. The Stokes drift
\mathbf{U}^{S}
is the difference between the mean Lagrangian
\langle\boldsymbol{u}\rangle_{L}
and the mean Eulerian velocity
\langle\boldsymbol{u}\rangle_{E}
,

\mathrm{U}^{S}=\langle\boldsymbol{u}\rangle_{L}-\langle\boldsymbol{u}\rangle_{E},

(4.8)

and finally we recall that, as noted by Stokes (see
[
36
]
), the mass transport is function of the mean Lagrangian velocity rather than the mean Eulerian one.

From the expression of the velocities in (
3.19
), we can calculate the mean Lagrangian velocities by averaging over a period
T=\frac{L}{\mathsf{c}}
, obtaining

\displaystyle\langle u\rangle_{L}=\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}k\mathsf{c}b\,e^{-ms}\cos\tau\,dt=0,

(4.9)

\displaystyle\langle v\rangle_{L}=-\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}k\mathsf{c}d\,e^{-ms}\sin\tau\,dt=0,

\displaystyle\langle w\rangle_{L}=-\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}k\mathsf{c}a\,e^{-ms}\sin\tau\,dt=0.

In order to obtain the mean Eulerian velocity, we need to compute the average of the wave velocity over the period at any fixed depth. As a fixed depth
z_{0}
can be written, due to (
3.7
), as

z_{0}=-d_{0}+s-ae^{-ms}\cos\tau,

(4.10)

we can write the functional dependence
s=S(z_{0},\tau)
, where
W
is the Lambert
W
-function, and
S=z_{0}-W(am\cos\tau e^{mz_{0}})
(see
[
22
]
). Taking the derivative with respect to
q
of (
4.10
) with
s=S(z_{0},\tau)
, we get

\frac{\partial S}{\partial q}=-\frac{kae^{-ms}\sin\tau}{1+mae^{-ms}\cos\tau}.

(4.11)

In order to obtain the mean Eulerian
u
-velocity, we write, adding and subtracting
\mathsf{c}
:

\displaystyle\langle u\rangle_{E}(z_{0})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\mathsf{c}+u(x-\mathsf{c}t,y,z_{0})\,dt-\mathsf{c}=\frac{1}{L}\int_{0}^{L}\mathsf{c}+u(x-\mathsf{c}t,y,z_{0})\,dx-\mathsf{c}

(4.12)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{L}\int_{0}^{L}\left[\mathsf{c}+u(q-\mathsf{c}t,r,S(z_{0},\tau))\right]\frac{\partial x}{\partial q}\,dq-\mathsf{c}=

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{L}\int_{0}^{L}\mathsf{c}(1+ma\,e^{-mS}\cos\tau)\frac{1-m^{2}a^{2}\,e^{-2mS}}{1+mae^{-mS}\cos\tau}\,dq-\mathsf{c}=

\displaystyle=\mathsf{c}-\frac{m^{2}a^{2}\mathsf{c}}{L}\int_{0}^{L}e^{-2mS}\,dq-\mathsf{c}=-\frac{m^{2}a^{2}\mathsf{c}}{L}\int_{0}^{L}e^{-2mS}\,dq,

so the mean Eulerian
u
-velocity is opposite to the direction of propagation of the Pollard waves (parallel to the Transpolar Drift Current), whereas the other two Eulerian velocities are obtained by direct computations:

\displaystyle\langle v\rangle_{E}(z_{0})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}v(x-\mathsf{c}t,y,z_{0})\,dt=\frac{1}{L}\int_{0}^{L}v(x-\mathsf{c}t,y,z_{0})\,dx

(4.13)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{L}\int_{0}^{L}v(q-\mathsf{c}t,r,S(z_{0},\tau))\frac{\partial x}{\partial q}\,dq=

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{L}\int_{0}^{L}(-k\mathsf{c}d\,e^{-mS}\sin\tau)\frac{1-m^{2}a^{2}\,e^{-2mS}}{1+mae^{-mS}\cos\tau}\,dq=

\displaystyle=\frac{fma}{kL}\int_{0}^{L}(e^{-mS}\sin\tau)\frac{1-m^{2}a^{2}\,e^{-2mS}}{1+mae^{-mS}\cos\tau}\,dq,

\displaystyle\langle w\rangle_{E}(z_{0})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}w(x-\mathsf{c}t,y,z_{0})\,dt=\frac{1}{L}\int_{0}^{L}w(x-\mathsf{c}t,y,z_{0})\,dx

(4.14)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{L}\int_{0}^{L}w(q-\mathsf{c}t,r,S(z_{0},\tau))\frac{\partial x}{\partial q}\,dq=

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{L}\int_{0}^{L}(-k\mathsf{c}a\,e^{-mS}\sin\tau)\frac{1-m^{2}a^{2}\,e^{-2mS}}{1+mae^{-mS}\cos\tau}\,dq=

\displaystyle=-\frac{k\mathsf{c}a}{L}\int_{0}^{L}(e^{-mS}\sin\tau)\frac{1-m^{2}a^{2}\,e^{-2mS}}{1+mae^{-mS}\cos\tau}\,dq

where we have used (
3.7
), (
3.14
), (
3.29
) and the fact that

\frac{\partial x}{\partial q}=1+bm\frac{\partial S}{\partial q}e^{-mS}\sin\tau-kb\,e^{-mS}\cos\tau=\frac{1-m^{2}a^{2}\,e^{-2mS}}{1+mae^{-mS}\cos\tau},

(4.15)

whit the last equality coming from (
4.11
). The components of the Stokes drift, defined in (
4.8
), are therefore given by

\displaystyle U^{S}

\displaystyle=\frac{m^{2}a^{2}\mathsf{c}}{L}\int_{0}^{L}e^{-2mS}\,dq,

(4.16)

\displaystyle V^{S}

\displaystyle=-\frac{fma}{kL}\int_{0}^{L}(e^{-mS}\sin\tau)\frac{1-m^{2}a^{2}\,e^{-2mS}}{1+mae^{-mS}\cos\tau}\,dq,

\displaystyle W^{S}

\displaystyle=\frac{k\mathsf{c}a}{L}\int_{0}^{L}(e^{-mS}\sin\tau)\frac{1-m^{2}a^{2}\,e^{-2mS}}{1+mae^{-mS}\cos\tau}\,dq\,.

Finally, we compute the mass fluxes. As the motion of the water particles in the halocline in three-dimensional, we consider the flux through three orthogonal planes.

The mass flux through a plane
\Sigma
is defined as

\mathcal{M}=\int_{\Sigma}\rho\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{n}}\,d\Sigma,

(4.17)

where
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{n}}
in the normal vector to the surface
\Sigma
. As we are considering the halocline layer, we set constant density
\rho=\rho_{1}
.

We begin with the mass flux in the
x
-direction. Let us fix at
x=x_{0}
the plane
\Sigma^{x}=\left[\eta_{2},\eta_{1}\right]\times\left[y_{1},y_{2}\right]
, with
y_{1}<y_{2}
. The mass flux in the
x
-direction is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{x}

\displaystyle=\rho_{1}\iint_{\left[\eta_{2},\eta_{1}\right]\times\left[y_{1},y_{2}\right]}u(x_{0}-ct,y,z)\,dz\,dy

(4.18)

\displaystyle=\rho_{1}\int_{\mathrm{s_{-}}}^{\mathrm{s_{+}}}\int_{-\mathrm{r_{0}}}^{\mathrm{r_{0}}}u\det\begin{pmatrix}\partial_{r}y&\partial_{s}y\\
\partial_{r}z&\partial_{s}z\end{pmatrix}dr\,ds.

Having fixed
x=x_{0}
implies a functional relation

q=\beta(x_{0},s,t).

(4.19)

Taking the
s
-derivative of

x_{0}=q-be^{-ms}\sin\tau=\beta(x_{0},s,t)-be^{-ms}\sin\tau,

(4.20)

gives

\frac{\partial\beta}{\partial s}=-\frac{bme^{-ms}\sin\tau}{1-ma^{-ms}\cos\tau}.

(4.21)

From (
3.11
), we have

\frac{\partial y}{\partial r}=1,\quad\frac{\partial z}{\partial r}=0,

(4.22)

and, due to the relations

\left.\begin{aligned} z=-d_{0}+s-ae^{-ms}\cos\tau\\
q=\beta(x_{0},s,t)\\
\frac{\partial\beta}{\partial s}=-\frac{bme^{-ms}\sin\tau}{1-ma^{-ms}\cos\tau}\end{aligned}\right\}\Longrightarrow\frac{\partial z}{\partial s}=\frac{1-m^{2}a^{2}e^{-2ms}}{1-mae^{-ms}\cos\tau}.

(4.23)

Therefore, from (
4.18
), the mass flux in the
x
-direction is

\mathcal{M}^{x}=\rho_{1}\mathsf{c}ma\int_{\mathrm{s_{-}}}^{\mathrm{s_{+}}}e^{-ms}\cos\tau\frac{1-m^{2}a^{2}e^{-2ms}}{1-mae^{-ms}\cos\tau}\,ds.

(4.24)

As
\langle u\rangle_{L}=0
, we expect that the mass flux (
4.23
) is zero, when averaged over a wave period
T
. This assertion is in fact true: by differentiating with respect to
t
(
4.20
) one gets

\frac{\partial\beta}{\partial t}=-\frac{\mathsf{c}mae^{-ms}\cos\tau}{1-ma^{-ms}\cos\tau}

(4.25)

for the
T
-periodic function
t\mapsto\beta(x_{0},s,t)
, hence (
4.24
) reads as

\mathcal{M}^{x}=\rho_{1}\int_{\mathrm{s_{-}}}^{\mathrm{s_{+}}}\left(m^{2}a^{2}e^{-2ms}-1\right)\frac{\partial\beta}{\partial t}\,ds,

(4.26)

implying

\int_{0}^{T}\mathcal{M}^{x}\,dt=0.

(4.27)

Let us now compute the mass flux in the
y
-direction by fixing at
y=y_{0}
the plane
\Sigma^{y}=\left[\eta_{2},\eta_{1}\right]\times\left[0,L\right]
, where
L=\frac{2\pi}{k}
is the wave length. The mass flux in the
y
-direction is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{y}

\displaystyle=\rho_{1}\iint_{\left[\eta_{2},\eta_{1}\right]\times\left[0,L\right]}v(x-ct,y_{0},z)\,dz\,dx

(4.28)

\displaystyle=\rho_{1}\int_{\mathrm{s_{-}}}^{\mathrm{s_{+}}}\int_{0}^{L}v\det\begin{pmatrix}\partial_{q}x&\partial_{s}x\\
\partial_{q}z&\partial_{s}z\end{pmatrix}dq\,ds.

As the variables
x
and
z
are independent of
r
(see (
3.7
) and (
3.11
)), there is no need to write a functional relation for fixed
y=y_{0}
.
Consequently, the mass flux in the
y
-direction is

\mathcal{M}^{y}=\rho_{1}\frac{fma}{k}\int_{\mathrm{s_{-}}}^{\mathrm{s_{+}}}\int_{0}^{L}e^{-ms}\sin\tau\left(1-m^{2}a^{2}e^{-2ms}\right)\,dqds,

(4.29)

where me also used the relation (
3.29
). It is immediate to see that

\int_{0}^{T}\mathcal{M}^{y}\,dt=0,

(4.30)

as expected, since
\langle v\rangle_{L}=0
.

Finally, for the mass flux in the
z
-direction, let us fix at
z=z_{0}
the plane
\Sigma^{z}=[0,L]\times[y_{1},y_{2}]
, where
z_{0}
is chosen between the crest
\mathrm{z}_{-}
of the halocline lower surface
\eta_{2}
and the trough
\mathrm{z}_{+}
of the halocline upper surface
\eta_{1}
, that is
\mathrm{z}_{-}<z_{0}<\mathrm{z}_{+}
. We write the functional relation

s=\xi(z_{0},q,t).

(4.31)

Writing, according to (
3.7
)

z_{0}=s-ae^{-ms}\cos\tau,

(4.32)

we get

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\xi}{\partial q}

\displaystyle=-\frac{kae^{-m\xi}\sin\tau}{1+mae^{-m\xi}\cos\tau},

(4.33)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\xi}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=\frac{k\mathsf{c}ae^{-m\xi}\sin\tau}{1+mae^{-m\xi}\cos\tau}.

(4.34)

Equation (
4.33
) gives

\frac{\partial x}{\partial q}=\frac{1-m^{2}a^{2}e^{-2m\xi}}{1+mae^{-m\xi}\cos\tau},

(4.35)

and, since from (
3.11
) we have

\frac{\partial x}{\partial r}=0,\quad\frac{\partial y}{\partial r}=1,

(4.36)

the mass flux in the
z
-direction, defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{z}

\displaystyle=\rho_{1}\iint_{\left[0,L\right]\times[y_{1},y_{2}]}w(x-ct,y,z_{0})\,dz\,dx

(4.37)

\displaystyle=\rho_{1}\int_{0}^{L}\int_{-\mathrm{r_{0}}}^{\mathrm{r_{0}}}w\det\begin{pmatrix}\partial_{q}x&\partial_{r}x\\
\partial_{q}y&\partial_{r}y\end{pmatrix}dq\,dr,

is given by

\mathcal{M}^{z}=-2\rho_{1}\mathrm{r_{0}}\int_{0}^{L}k\mathsf{c}ae^{-m\xi}\sin\tau\frac{1-m^{2}a^{2}e^{-2m\xi}}{1+mae^{-m\xi}\cos\tau}\,dq.

(4.38)

Furthermore, as

\mathcal{M}^{z}=-2\rho_{1}\mathrm{r_{0}}\int_{0}^{L}\left(1-m^{2}a^{2}e^{-2m\xi}\right)\frac{\partial\xi}{\partial t}\,dq,

(4.39)

due to (
4.34
), and the function
t\mapsto\xi(z_{0},q,t)
being
T
-periodic, we get

\int_{0}^{T}\mathcal{M}^{z}\,dt=0,

(4.40)

as could be inferred by
\langle w\rangle_{L}=0.

In conlusion, this prove that the Pollard internal wave has no net wave transport over a wave period.

Comparison with the linearized Lagrangian version of the problem

Linearizing about the hydrostatic solution
x=q,\ y=r,\ z=s
the governing equations in Lagrangian form (see

]
and

]
), namely

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\left(\frac{\partial^{2}x}{\partial t^{2}}-f\frac{\partial y}{\partial t}\right)\frac{\partial x}{\partial q}+\left(\frac{\partial^{2}y}{\partial t^{2}}+f\frac{\partial x}{\partial t}\right)\frac{\partial y}{\partial q}+\frac{\partial^{2}z}{\partial t^{2}}\frac{\partial x}{\partial q}+g\frac{\partial z}{\partial q}=-\frac{1}{\rho}\frac{\partial P}{\partial q},\\
&\left(\frac{\partial^{2}x}{\partial t^{2}}-f\frac{\partial y}{\partial t}\right)\frac{\partial x}{\partial r}+\left(\frac{\partial^{2}y}{\partial t^{2}}+f\frac{\partial x}{\partial t}\right)\frac{\partial y}{\partial r}+\frac{\partial^{2}z}{\partial t^{2}}\frac{\partial x}{\partial r}+g\frac{\partial z}{\partial r}=-\frac{1}{\rho}\frac{\partial P}{\partial r},\\
&\left(\frac{\partial^{2}x}{\partial t^{2}}-f\frac{\partial y}{\partial t}\right)\frac{\partial x}{\partial s}+\left(\frac{\partial^{2}y}{\partial t^{2}}+f\frac{\partial x}{\partial t}\right)\frac{\partial y}{\partial s}+\frac{\partial^{2}z}{\partial t^{2}}\frac{\partial x}{\partial s}+g\frac{\partial z}{\partial q}=-\frac{1}{\rho}\frac{\partial P}{\partial s},\end{aligned}\right.

(5.1)

and

\frac{d}{dt}\left(\det\left(\frac{\partial(x,y,z)}{\partial(q,r,s)}\right)\right)=0,

(5.2)

which are the Euler equations and the incopressibility condition, respectively, yield to the linearized Euler equations

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\frac{\partial^{2}x}{\partial t^{2}}-f\frac{\partial y}{\partial t}+g\frac{\partial z}{\partial q}=-\frac{1}{\rho_{1}}\frac{\partial P}{\partial q},\\
&\frac{\partial^{2}y}{\partial t^{2}}+f\frac{\partial x}{\partial t}+g\frac{\partial z}{\partial r}=-\frac{1}{\rho_{1}}\frac{\partial P}{\partial r},\\
&\frac{\partial^{2}z}{\partial t^{2}}+g\frac{\partial z}{\partial s}=-\frac{1}{\rho_{1}}\frac{\partial P}{\partial s},\end{aligned}\right.

(5.3)

and the linearized incompressibility condition

\frac{\partial^{2}x}{\partial q\partial t}+\frac{\partial^{2}y}{\partial r\partial t}+\frac{\partial^{2}z}{\partial s\partial t}=0.

(5.4)

Remarkably, Pollard waves (
3.7
) are solutions also to the system of linearized equations (
5.3
) and (
5.4
). Namely, writing

\left\{\begin{aligned} &x=q-be^{-ms}\sin(k(q-\mathsf{c}t)),\\
&y=r-de^{-ms}\cos(k(q-\mathsf{c}t)),\\
&z=-d_{0}+s-ae^{-ms}\cos(k(q-\mathsf{c}t)),\end{aligned}\right.

(5.5)

the incompressiblity equation (
5.4
) is reduced to the condition (
3.14
):

b=\frac{am}{k}.

(5.6)

Rewriting (
5.3
) as

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\frac{\partial P}{\partial q}=-\rho_{1}\left[\frac{\partial^{2}x}{\partial t^{2}}-f\frac{\partial y}{\partial t}+g\frac{\partial z}{\partial q}\right],\\
&\frac{\partial P}{\partial r}=-\rho_{1}\left[\frac{\partial^{2}y}{\partial t^{2}}+f\frac{\partial x}{\partial t}+g\frac{\partial z}{\partial r}\right],\\
&\frac{\partial P}{\partial s}=-\rho_{1}\left[\frac{\partial^{2}z}{\partial t^{2}}+g\frac{\partial z}{\partial s}\right],\end{aligned}\right.

(5.7)

gives, for the pressure, the solution

P=\widetilde{P_{1}}-\rho_{1}gs+\rho_{1}\left[\frac{k^{2}\mathsf{c}^{2}a}{m}+ga\right]e^{-ms}\cos\tau,

(5.8)

with the conditions

k\mathsf{c}d+fb=0,

(5.9)

m^{2}=\frac{k^{4}\mathsf{c}^{2}}{\mathsf{c}^{2}k^{2}-f^{2}},

(5.10)

and where, again,

\tau=k(x-\mathsf{c}t).

(5.11)

We remark that the conditions (
5.6
), (
5.9
) and (
5.10
) are exactly those found in the nonlinear analysis: (
3.14
), (
3.24
) and (
3.25
).

Recalling the the dynamic boundary condition (namely the continuity of the pressure across the halocline top and bottom surfaces):

P=\begin{cases}P_{0}-\rho_{0}gz-\rho_{0}f\mathsf{c}y\quad\text{for}\ z=\eta_{1},\\
P_{2}-\rho_{2}gz\quad\qquad\text{for}\ z=\eta_{2},\end{cases}

(5.12)

we see that this is equivalent to

\left\{\begin{aligned} &P_{0}-\widetilde{P_{1}}=-(\rho_{1}-\rho_{0})g\mathrm{s}_{+}+\rho_{0}f\mathsf{c}r-\rho_{0}gd_{0},\\
&bk\mathsf{c}^{2}=\left(\frac{\rho_{0}-\rho_{1}}{\rho_{1}}\right)(ga+f\mathsf{c}d),\end{aligned}\right.

(5.13)

where
s=\mathrm{s}_{+}
represent the upper surface of the halocline
\eta_{1}
, while on the lower surface
\eta_{2}
represented by
s=\mathrm{s}_{-}
, it is given by

\left\{\begin{aligned} &P_{2}-\widetilde{P_{1}}=(\rho_{2}-\rho_{1})g\mathrm{s}_{-}-\rho_{2}gd_{0},\\
&bk\mathsf{c}^{2}=\frac{\rho_{2}}{\rho_{1}}ga-ga-df\mathsf{c},\end{aligned}\right.

(5.14)

therefore we recover again the dispersion relation (
3.49
):

\mathsf{c}^{2}=\frac{f^{2}\mathfrak{g}^{2}}{\mathfrak{g}^{2}k^{2}-f^{4}},

(5.15)

and we note that the pressure (
3.32
) of Pollard’s exact solution represent a higher order correction of the one related to the linear case just examined (
5.8
).

6.
Discussion

We conclude by presenting some qualitative and quantitative considerations for the solution (
3.7
).

Let us recall (
3.49
) and (
3.50
):

\mathsf{c}^{2}=\frac{f^{2}\mathfrak{g}^{2}}{\mathfrak{g}^{2}k^{2}-f^{4}},\qquad k^{2}=\frac{f^{4}}{\mathfrak{g}^{2}}+\frac{f^{2}}{\mathsf{c}^{2}},

(6.1)

where
\mathfrak{g}=\left(\frac{\rho_{2}-\rho_{0}}{\rho_{0}}\right)g
. As a consequence, the dispersion relation depends, in addition to
f
, only on
\rho_{2}
and
\rho_{0}
, namely on the density of the water above and below the halocline, and not on the density of its water
\rho_{1}
. However, this dependence is very marginal. Observe also that the the wavelength increases (very slightly) as
\mathfrak{g}
increases (i.e. when the density difference between the warmer and saltier Atlantic Water and the fresher and colder water of the Surface Mixing Layer increases). Fixing the parameters describing the flow under consideration,
\mathsf{c}\approx 0.1\,\mathrm{ms^{-1}}
,
\mathfrak{g}\approx 0.006\,\mathrm{ms^{-2}}
,
f=2\Omega\approx 1.5\cdot 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}
, due to relations (
3.30
) and (
3.50
), we can obtain an approximate value for

m^{2}=\frac{f^{4}}{\mathfrak{g}^{2}}+\frac{\mathfrak{g}^{2}}{\mathsf{c}^{4}}+2\frac{f^{2}}{\mathsf{c}^{2}}\approx\frac{\mathfrak{g}^{2}}{\mathsf{c}^{4}}=0.36\,\mathrm{m^{-2}},

(6.2)

giving

m\approx 0.6\,\mathrm{m^{-2}},

(6.3)

and
k\approx 1.5\cdot 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m^{-1}}
, as already seen in Section 3. From (
3.7
) it is immediate to see that as depth increases, the amplitude of the waves increases and the maximum oscillation of the wave will be less than
\frac{10}{6}\approx 1.67\,\mathrm{m}
, in view of (
3.17
), and, due to the relation (
6.2
), a variation in
\mathfrak{g}
will influence the wave amplitude more than the wave length.

Moreover, as the amplitude of the waves described in (
3.7
) increases when the depth of the halocline reduces, we can infer that in summer - with the decreasing of the depths of the halocline boundaries
[
29
]
- the amplitude of the oscillations will decrease, and vice-versa in winter. That said, due to the exponential factor
e^{-ms}
of (
3.7
), such increment/decrement of the amplitude in different seasons would hardly be detectable.

About the halocline’s upper surface, as anticipated, (
3.39
) shows that the upper surface of the halocline reduces its depth as
r
increases (namely in the Eurasian Basin), and increases in depth as
r
decreases (namely in the Amerasian Basin). This result matches the measurements made by oceanographers (see
[
32
]
), even if we infer that there should be other, physical processes influencing the halocline depth, such as water inflows from other basins, against these variations. In fact, from (
3.39
) we have that

\mathrm{s}^{\prime}_{+}(r)=\frac{\rho_{0}f\mathsf{c}}{2mAe^{-2m\mathrm{s}_{+}(r)}+(\rho_{1}-\rho_{0})g}<\left(\frac{(\rho_{1}-\rho_{0})g}{\rho_{0}}\right)^{-1}f\mathsf{c}\lesssim 0.019,

(6.4)

thus, an increase [decrease] in
r
of kilometers produces an decrease [increase] in the depth of the halocline’s upper surface of tens of meters. As depth variations of the halocline are less than a hundred meters in different basins (see
[
32
]
or
[
34
]
), we deduce that such variations must be controlled also by other physical processes.

Recalling that the particle motion in (
3.7
) describes trochoidal orbits, namely the path of a fixed point on a circle of radius
be^{-ms}
and centered at
(q,r,s-d_{0})
, rolling along the
x
-axis, in a plane that is at an angle
\arctan\left(-\frac{d}{a}\right)
with respect to the vertical axis, due to (
3.29
), we have that

\arctan\left(-\frac{d}{a}\right)=\arctan\left(\frac{m}{\frac{f^{3}\mathsf{c}}{\mathfrak{g}^{2}}+\frac{f}{\mathsf{c}}}\right)\lesssim\frac{\pi}{2},

(6.5)

and, using (
3.17
),

|d|<\frac{f}{k^{2}\mathsf{c}}\approx 667\,\mathrm{m}.

(6.6)

Namely, the throcoidal orbit described in (
3.7
) is almost horizontal with oscillations not exceeding
667\,\mathrm{m}
, describing small-amplitude, near-inertial, Pollard waves moving parallel to the Transpolar Drift Current.

As pointed out by Garret & Munk (see
[
14
]
and
[
15
]
), near-inertial waves are the most energetic ones, but are hard to detect, even without the presence of ice (e.g. using satellite methods
[
40
]
).

We point out the simple near-inertial dispersion relation obtained in (
3.49
), imposed by the two dynamic boundary conditions at the halocline surfaces, in contrast to other works - with only one dynamic boundary condition - having much more complicated dispersion relations, describing two wave modes (one slow, near inertial and one faster) whose formulae were obtained via a perturbative analysis of the dispersion relation, and not in closed form
[
12
]
,
[
25
]
,
[
26
]
.
