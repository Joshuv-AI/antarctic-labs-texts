Lobectomy results in a greater increase in the pulmonary artery to aorta ratio compared to sublobar resection: a retrospective study
Authors: Taketo Nakai, Yu Okubo, Takahiro Suzuki, Shigeki Suzuki, Kyohei Masai, Kaoru Kaseda, Yasunori Sato, Keisuke Asakura
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe pulmonary artery diameter-to-aortic diameter (PA/A) ratio on computed tomography correlates with PA pressure and is a simple tool for estimating the load on the right side of the heart. The purpose of this study was to investigate the change in the PA/A ratio in patients following lung resection according to the specific procedure used.MethodsPatients with pathological stage 0–I lung cancer who underwent lobectomy or sublobar resection between 2018 and 2020 were included in this study. The PA/A ratios were measured pre-operatively and at 1 and 3 years post-operatively (POY1 and POY3). Changes over time and factors associated with an increased PA/A ratio were analysed.ResultsOf 301 eligible patients, 210 underwent lobectomy and 91 underwent sublobar resection (35 segmentectomies and 56 wedge resections). Age, sex, body mass index, and smoking status were similar between the groups. Cardiac comorbidity and pathological stage 0 disease were more common in the sublobar resection group versus the lobectomy group (20.9% vs. 11.0%, 29.7% vs. 6.2%). Compared with the sublobar resection group, the lobectomy group showed a significantly higher ratio at POY3 (0.823 ± 0.131 vs. 0.786 ± 0.128) and a greater mean increase from baseline to POY1 (difference between groups: 0.039, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.023–0.05) and from baseline to POY3 (0.065, 95% CI 0.049–0.08). On multivariable analysis, lobectomy independently predicted a postoperative PA/A ratio increase (odds ratio 7.98, 95% CI 3.85–16.6).ConclusionsCompared with sublobar resection, lobectomy is associated with a significantly larger rise in the PA/A ratio, suggesting greater pulmonary-vascular loading after more extensive parenchymal removal. Surveillance of the PA/A ratios may help identify patients at risk of right-heart strain.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13019-026-04043-7.

Background

Pulmonary hypertension (PH) is the fifth known risk factor for cardiopulmonary complications following pulmonary resection [1]. It also serves as a poor prognostic factor [2]. Among all noncardiac surgeries, pulmonary resection has the most significant impact on cardiac function. One of the mechanisms by which pulmonary resection influences cardiac function is by altering cardiopulmonary haemodynamics. This procedure leads to a reduction in the pulmonary arterial bed, which increases the pulmonary artery (PA) pressure and may result in right ventricular impairment, especially in patients with potentially impaired cardiac function or PH [3]. Evaluating whether patients have PH pre-operatively is important; however, measuring PA pressure requires additional Doppler echocardiography or invasive cardiac catheterisation, which is associated with a certain rate of complications [4]. Owing to the cost and invasiveness, these examinations are impractical for all surgical candidates. On the other hand, computed tomography (CT) is an essential pre-operative examination for pulmonary resection. The PA diameter to aortic diameter (PA/A) ratio, which can be measured using CT, correlates with mean PA pressure [5–7] and has been reported as a simple tool for predicting PH [8].

The JCOG0802 trial comparing lobectomy and segmentectomy for small-sized peripheral non-small cell lung cancer showed that segmentectomy is superior to lobectomy in terms of overall survival (OS) [9]. The higher rate of death from other causes in the lobectomy group may have been the reason the segmentectomy group demonstrated a better OS despite a higher recurrence rate and a respiratory function advantage of only 3% in the forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1); however, the details are unclear. Although it is hypothesised that elevated PA pressure may be one of the causes of death in the lobectomy group, little is known about how the PA/A ratio changes according to the surgical technique [10]; furthermore, no report has addressed changes in the PA/A ratio over time. Therefore, we aimed to determine whether there was a difference in the post-operative PA/A ratios between lobectomy and sublobar resection.

Methods

The medical records of patients with pathological stage 0–I lung cancer who underwent lung resection at Keio University Hospital between January 2018 and December 2020 were retrospectively reviewed. Among these, only those who underwent lobectomy or sublobar resection and had follow-up CT images for 3 years without evidence of recurrence were included. Patients with a history of lung resection or multiple lesions were excluded from the study. Clinical and pathological data were extracted from electronic medical records and our databases. Pathological staging was based on the ninth edition of the Tumour Node Metastasis classification [11]. A manual review of radiological images was also conducted as part of this study.

This study was approved by the Medical Research Ethics Committee of Keio University Hospital (Institutional Review Board approval number 2023–1021) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The requirement for informed consent was waived owing to the retrospective nature of the study, as advised by the ethics committee.

Pre-operatively, most patients underwent whole-body CT, magnetic resonance imaging of the brain, 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography, electrocardiography, and spirometric pulmonary function tests. The pre-operative data obtained included the patients’ age at the time of surgery; sex; body mass index (BMI); smoking history; comorbidities, such as heart disease and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD); spirometric pulmonary function tests, including forced expiratory volume in 1 s predicted (%FEV1predicted) and forced expiratory volume % in 1 s (%FEV1); and pathological stage. Heart diseases included ischaemia, vascular diseases, and arrhythmias. Standard pulmonary resection was conducted using video-assisted thoracic surgery involving two skin incisions: an 8-cm incision used for manipulation and a 1.5-cm incision through which the utility port was placed, allowing access for the thoracoscope, staplers, and other instruments [12]. The decision to perform a lobectomy or sublobar resection for a lesion of suspected stage I lung cancer was made after discussion at a conference regarding factors such as patient risk, respiratory function, and tumour localisation.

Measurement of the PA/A ratio

Axial CT was used to examine the PA for enlargement. Two physicians (TN and YO) conducted the measurements while blinded to the patient’s post-operative course. Following the methodology of previous studies, we measured the diameter of the main PA at the level of its bifurcation and that of the ascending aorta at its widest point using the same imaging. The measurement for the main PA was at the rise of the bilateral pulmonary arteries [5–7, 13]. The rises of the main PAs are obvious on axial CT in most patients because the angles of the outline of the PAs change sharply at the points (Fig. 1). The PA/A ratio was calculated as an index of pulmonary arterial enlargement. Before study initiation, observers reviewed the measurements together to ensure a uniform and comprehensive understanding of the diameters to be measured. Diameters were measured based on pre-operative data and at 1 and 3 years post-operatively (POY1 and POY3, respectively).

Postoperative cardiopulmonary complications

Similar to our previous studies [14], in this study, postoperative cardiopulmonary complication (PCCs) of lung resection included one or more of the following: (1) pneumonia, defined as the appearance of pulmonary infiltrate on radiography, combined with fever and leucocytosis; (2) acute respiratory distress syndrome; (3) respiratory insufficiency requiring tracheostomy; (4) respiratory failure requiring mechanical ventilation; (5) atelectasis requiring bronchoscopic intervention; (6) hypoxemia requiring home oxygen therapy; (7) arrhythmias requiring medication; (8) coronary artery disease (angina pectoris and myocardial infarction); and (9) acute heart failure requiring medication. Complications related to surgical technique, such as prolonged air leaks, bronchopleural fistulas, and chylothorax, were not examined in this study.

Statistical analyses

Continuous variables were summarised as medians with ranges, and categorical variables, as frequencies with percentages. Baseline characteristics were compared between the lobectomy and sublobar resection groups and between patients with and without increased PA/A ratios; the Mann–Whitney U, chi-squared, or Fisher’s exact tests were used as appropriate.

Longitudinal changes in the PA/A ratio between the groups were evaluated using linear mixed-effects models. These models account for repeated measurements within each patient and allow for fixed effects (surgical group, time point, and their interaction) and random effects (patient-level variability). The interaction term between time and surgical group was used to assess whether the change in the PA/A ratio over time differed between the two groups.

Variables that were significant in univariate analyses were included in a multivariable logistic regression model to identify independent predictors of a post-operative increase in the PA/A ratio. All analyses were conducted using R (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) and SAS ver. 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Between January 2018 and December 2020, 406 patients underwent lung resection for pathological stage 0–I primary lung cancer at our institution. Among them, 301 patients met the inclusion criteria and were enrolled in the study cohort: 210 underwent lobectomy, and 91 underwent sublobar resection (35 segmentectomies and 56 wedge resections; Fig. 2).

Pre-operative and post-operative PA/A ratio

Figure 3 shows changes in the PA/A ratio over time. No significant difference in the PA/A ratio was observed between the groups pre-operatively (lobectomy vs. sublobar resection: 0.773 vs. 0.786, p = 0.3) or at POY1 (0.805 vs. 0.788, p = 0.4). However, at POY3, the lobectomy group showed a significantly higher PA/A ratio, compared with the sublobar resection group (0.823 vs. 0.786, p = 0.04).

Fig. 3PA/A ratio plots for the lobectomy and sublobar resection groups at pre-operative and post-operative years. The PA/A ratio was significantly higher in the lobectomy group than in the sublobar resection group at 3 years post-operatively (POY3) (0.823 vs. 0.786, p = 0.04), although no significant difference was found between pre-operative (0.773 vs. 0.786, p = 0.3) and 1-year post-operative (POY1) timepoints (0.805 vs. 0.788, p = 0.4). The change in the PA/A ratio from the pre-operative timepoint to POY1 was + 4.28% and + 0.25% and that by POY3 was + 6.70% and + 0.05% in the lobectomy and sublobar resection groups, respectively. A mixed-effects model analysis of the percent change in the PA/A ratio in both groups suggested that the lobectomy group had a significantly more pronounced increase at POY1 (p < 0.01) and POY3 (p < 0.01), compared with the sublobar resection group. Furthermore, in terms of temporal trends from pre-operative to POY3, the lobectomy group showed a large increase, particularly in the PA/A ratio from the pre-operative timepoint to POY1, followed by a consistent increase in the PA/A ratio up to POY3. The mean percent change of the PA/A ratio from the preoperative period to POY1 across the entire cohort was 3.1 ± 6.1%. The percent change from baseline to POY1 was + 4.28% in the lobectomy group and + 0.25% in the sublobar resection group; by POY3, the change was + 6.70% vs. +0.05%. A mixed-effects model revealed significantly greater increases in the PA/A ratio in the lobectomy group compared with the sublobar resection group from baseline to POY1 (difference between groups: 0.039, 95% CI 0.023–0.055, p < 0.01) and from baseline to POY3 (group difference: 0.065, 95% CI 0.049–0.081, p < 0.01). The lobectomy group exhibited the largest increase pre-operatively to POY1, followed by a continued gradual increase up to POY3. The percent change in PA/A ratio per lobe up to POY1 was greatest in the right lower lobe (+ 7.1 ± 6.5%), followed by the right upper lobe (+ 5.0 ± 5.6%), left lower lobe (+ 4.2 ± 6.0%), and right middle lobe (+ 2.8 ± 4.5%), generally correlating with the volume of lung resected. Contrarily, the left upper lobe showed a wide variation of + 1.4 ± 7.8% (Supplemental Fig. 1). The PA/A ratio changes following segmentectomy and wedge resection were also examined respectively by a mixed-effects model; however, no significant difference was observed between the two (difference between groups from baseline to POY1: -0.006, 95% CI -0.02–0.07, p = 0.36, difference between groups from baseline to POY3: -0.016, 95% CI -0.034–0.001, p = 0.07) (Supplemental Fig. 2).

Comparison of groups with and without increased PA/A ratio

Patients were then classified into either the PA/A increased group or the PA/A non-increased group based on whether the PA/A ratio at POY1 increased compared to the preoperative value. Table 3 shows a comparison of the clinical characteristics between those with and without increased PA/A ratio (PA/A increased and non-increased groups, respectively). Patients in the PA/A increased group were more likely to have pathological stage I disease (93.2% vs. 82.5%, p < 0.01) and to have undergone lobectomy, compared with those in the PA/A non-increased group (91.5% vs. 55.7%, p < 0.01). Table 4 shows the incidence of PCC, which was 5.9% in the PA/A increased group and 3.3% in the non-increased group, showing a tendency towards higher rates in the PA/A increased group; however, this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.4). Additionally, the incidence rate for atrial fibrillation, hypoxaemia, and pneumonia (but not atelectasis) was higher in the PA/A increased group compared to the non-increased group, although this difference was not significant.

Discussion

Lobectomy is assumed to lead to a greater load on the right side of the heart, compared with sublobar resection, owing to the loss of a larger pulmonary arterial bed; however, this has not been fully established. In this study, we found that the PA/A ratio, a measure of right heart load, was significantly higher after lobectomy than after sublobar resection and that lobectomy was an independent factor for increasing the PA/A ratio in the multivariable analysis.

As the PA/A ratio measured using CT correlates with the mean PA pressure [5–7], various studies have focused primarily on pre-operative PA/A ratios. We have previously reported that a pre-operative PA/A ratio of > 1 is an independent predictor of PCCs in patients with lung cancer [14]. Similarly, Lee et al. reported that a PA/A ratio of > 1 based on three-dimensional segmentation is a significant predictor of complications after lobectomy [15].

Regarding changes in the PA/A ratio before and after pulmonary resection, we found a significant increase in the PA/A ratio following pulmonary resection. However, knowledge concerning the differences in surgical techniques is limited. Nishikubo et al. reported a significantly higher PA/A ratio change at 3–12 months post-operatively for lower lobectomy than for segmentectomy of the lower lobe (104% vs. 102%) [16]. In the present study, the change in the PA/A ratio between the lobectomy and sublobar resection groups was compared, including segmentectomy of all lobes and wedge resection, over a long period, up to POY3; a significant increase was observed in the PA/A ratio at POY1 and POY3 in the lobectomy group versus the sublobar resection group. These results support the hypothesis that the PA/A ratio increases with increasing lung resection volume. The progression of PA/A ratio after lobectomy up to POY1 for each lobe generally correlated with the extent of lung resection. However, left upper lobectomy demonstrated a wider range of PA/A ratio changes compared to the other lobes (Supplemental Fig. 1). This variability may be explained by postoperative displacement of the great vessels following left upper lobectomy, altering the anatomical location where the PA/A ratio is measured.

The JCOG0802 trial showed non-inferiority and superiority of segmentectomy over lobectomy in OS, despite a higher recurrence rate and a respiratory function advantage of only 3% in %FEV1 [9]. The finding that deaths from other diseases were frequent, especially in the lobectomy group, suggests that the resection of a larger lung volume may negatively affect prognosis. Pulmonary resection leads to loss of the pulmonary vascular bed, which is directly associated with the load on the right heart. Elevated PA pressure induces PH, which is a predictor of post-operative complications in lung cancer [1] and a prognostic factor [2]. Similarly, the PA/A ratio, a measure of mean PA pressure, has been reported to be a predictor of post-operative complications [14, 15] and a prognostic factor [16]. In the present study, the increase in the PA/A ratio was more pronounced in the lobectomy group with larger lung resection volumes than in sublobar resections, which may have had a negative impact on prognosis. Patients with a high preoperative PA/A ratio, those with existing or suspected PH, or those considered to have high cardiopulmonary risk should be considered for sublobar resection rather than standard lobectomy. Additionally, if the preoperative PA/A ratio is already high or shows a significant postoperative increase, serum brain natriuretic peptide measurement and echocardiography should be added to routine follow-up examinations, with referral to cardiology as needed.

This study has some limitations. First, this is a single-centre retrospective study. Second, non-contrast CT was also used to measure the PA and aortic diameters, possibly resulting in slight measurement errors. Moreover, following left upper lobe resection, displacement of the great vessels may occur, leading to variability in PA/A ratio measurements. Third, the number of patients who underwent sublobar resection was relatively small, compared with those who underwent lobectomy. Fourth, we were unable to compare the PA/A ratio with clinical symptoms or the results of echocardiography or cardiac catheterization. Fifth, the relationship between the change in the PA/A ratio and prognosis could not be evaluated, as we included patients who had been monitored using CT for 3 years without recurrence to evaluate the long-term progression of the PA/A ratio for each surgical procedure. However, further large-scale trials are required to validate these findings.

Conclusions

Post-operatively, the PA/A ratio increased significantly more in the lobectomy group than in the sublobar resection group. As this ratio may reflect PH, sublobar resection should be considered for patients with high preoperative PA/A ratios and cardiopulmonary risk.
