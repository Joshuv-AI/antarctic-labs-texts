Toward a triadic approach in eating disorders: investigating the contribution of the reflexive, affective and interoceptive systems in a clinical data cohort
Authors: Valentin Flaudias, Corentin Gonthier, Aurélien Cornil, Maude Seneque, Clara Lakritz, Sylvain Iceta, Sébastien Guillaume
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEating disorders (ED) are disabling conditions increasingly conceptualized as addictive disorders. While food addiction (FA) is well documented in bulimia nervosa (BN) and binge-eating disorder (BED), its mechanisms in anorexia nervosa (AN) remain unclear. The triadic model of addiction—encompassing reflexive, affective, and interoceptive systems—offers a relevant framework to investigate FA across EDs. The current study aimed to test to what extent these three components of addiction contribute to FA in patients with ED.MethodsThe sample included 115 patients (mean age = 28.41 years, SD = 11.64; 91% female) diagnosed with either AN (restrictive: n = 38; binge-eating/purging type: n = 32), BN (n = 29), or BED (n = 16). Participants completed the Emotional Appetite Questionnaire, the interoceptive subscale of the Eating Disorder Inventory-2, the Detail and Flexibility Questionnaire, and the Yale Food Addiction Scale 2.0. In the present analyses, FA was operationalized using the continuous total score reflecting FA symptom severity. Additional analyses using the symptom-count and diagnostic scoring procedures yielded comparable results. Linear and multinomial logistic regressions were used to test the associations between FA and the three systems, and their ability to distinguish diagnostic groups.ResultsWhen considered separately, interoceptive difficulties (r = .54), negative emotions (r = .47), and cognitive inflexibility (r = .33) were all associated with higher FA (all ps < 0.001). However, in the joint model, only interoception (β = 0.39, p < .001) and negative emotions (β = 0.33, p = .005) remained significant predictors, whereas cognitive flexibility had no significant effect. These effects did not differ significantly between diagnostic groups. The affective system predicted diagnostic (χ²(3) = 21.78, p < .001), but the effect was inconsistent and the overall predictive value was modest (McFadden’s R² = 0.11, classification accuracy = 50%).ConclusionInteroceptive and affective systems appear to significantly predict FA across various ED; the reflexive system, as currently measured, seems to play a more limited role. These results highlight the need for more sensitive assessment tools and refined models of ED-related addictive dimensions.Trial Registration NCT03160443, first post 2017-05-03.Keywords: Eating disorders, Food addiction, Interoception, Emotion, Cognitive flexibility

Background

Eating disorders (ED) are particularly disabling [1], with a high and growing prevalence [2] (from 8.5 million in 1990 to 13.6 million in 2019). These disorders are now increasingly categorized within an addiction framework [3]. Indeed, there is growing evidence that bulimia nervosa (BN) and binge-eating disorder (BED) are associated with high levels of food addiction (FA) symptomatology and share several behavioral and neurocognitive features with addictive disorders (see for example [4]). The concept of FA has mainly been used and investigated for binge eating behaviors in BN and BED [3], but more recent studies have also reported evidence for FA in anorexia nervosa (AN), especially for binge-eating/purging-type AN (75% in [5], 74% in [6]) more than restrictive-type AN (54.2% in [5] and 26% in [6]). Overall, these findings militate in favor of bringing together addictive and EDs within the same conceptual framework, to improve their understanding.

FA offers a conceptual lens through which ED behaviors can be understood as forms of behavioral addiction. FA is characterized by an inability to reduce food intake, and by the perception and/or fear of a loss of control over eating [7]. FA has been conceptualized as a distinct form of behavioral addiction characterized by the compulsive consumption of palatable foods despite negative consequences [5]. It involves impaired inhibitory control, increased impulsivity, and heightened incentive salience for food cues - factors that are also present in substance addictions. Currently, the most commonly used measure of FA is the Yale Food Addiction Scale (YFAS 2.0) [6]. In this scale, the core criteria of substance dependence in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM) are adapted to assess addictive-like eating behaviors in relation to highly processed foods. It should be noted that the terminology surrounding addictive-like eating behaviors is evolving. In addition to the concept of food addiction, recent work has proposed more restrictive frameworks, such as ultra-processed food addiction (UPFA), which emphasize the addictive potential of highly processed foods rather than food per se [8]. However, these approaches remain under active debate, and food addiction as operationalized by the YFAS 2.0 remains the most widely used and validated framework for assessing addictive-like eating behaviors, particularly in eating disorder populations.

Current psychological models of EDs emphasize the role of cognitive and emotional difficulties, such as distortions of body image, inability to regulate emotions [3], and problems with interoception [9]. These same processes are central to recent models of addictive disorders. Recent work suggests that a triadic interplay between reflexive, emotional, and interoceptive systems may be involved in the development and maintenance of addiction, regardless of the object of this addiction. In particular, the triadic model of Noël et al. [10] is based on the observation that the activation of the insula is closely correlated with craving [11, 12]. The insula is richly interconnected with amygdala/striatal structures (particularly involved in the affective system) and with the prefrontal cortex, which is involved in the reflective system [12]. Noël et al. [10] accordingly suggested that the insula could exacerbate the activity of the “affective system” (an appetitive system supported by limbic structures and the reward system, involved in the impulsive processing of addiction-related stimuli and triggering automatic responses based on associative learning) and subvert the “reflective system” (a control and inhibitory system governed by frontal regions, involved in the cognitive processing of stimuli, relying on memory and executive functions and initiating controlled-deliberate responses). This prompts an in-depth reinterpretation of the dual-process models, postulating a third insular “interoceptive system” (which is a sensory system underlaid by insular cortex regions, involved in the perception and processing of internal body states, which triggers awareness of cravings).

The available evidence is rather compatible with this triadic model in the case of EDs, in showing difficulties for the three systems. Indeed, a growing body of research has documented the cognitive [13–15] and emotional regulation (e.g., see [16]) difficulties that are apparent in EDs. Some studies have also focused on the interoceptive difficulties of patients with EDs (e.g., see [17]). However, no study as of yet seems to have explored the interplay between these three factors in EDs and their relationship with FA. In other words, there has not been a dedicated test of the triadic model as a potential explanatory framework for EDs.

The current study aimed to test this triadic model in a sample of patients with EDs, including two subtypes of AN, BN, and BED, by exploring the relationships between cognitive difficulties, emotional difficulties, interoceptive difficulties, and FA. The primary goal was to investigate the hypothesis that the three systems predict the extent of FA, both separately (in simple regressions) and jointly (in multiple regressions modeling the unique effect of each system), in patients with ED. More precisely, we hypothesized that greater affective reactivity to negative emotions and greater interoceptive difficulties would be associated with higher levels of food addiction. We further hypothesized that deficits in the reflective system, operationalized through cognitive rigidity, would also be associated with food addiction, although potentially to a lesser extent.

A secondary goal was to test whether these relationships varied as a function of the type of ED. Finally, comparing the level of functioning of the three systems for patients with each ED could also be informative, which led to an exploratory analysis testing whether these systems could be used to predict diagnostic (e.g., are patients with a particularly dysfunctional interoceptive system more likely to present one type of ED than the others)?

Methods

Study design and participants

Eligible patients were all consecutive inpatients and outpatients with ED according to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders 5th edition criteria -DSM-5 (DSM-5; [18]) and who had undergone a psychiatric interview, which was conducted by the ED specialist team at the eating disorder unit in Montpellier, France, between May 2017 and September 2019. The current study only included all patients (n = 115) who were diagnosed with the following subtypes: AN – binge-eating/purging type, AN – restrictive type, BN and BED, and who provided complete data on the key measures. Nine patients had one missing data point for either the reflexive system or affective system: in these cases, the missing data were imputed using package mice for R based on all other variables. Another 64 patients visited the unit during the study timeframe but had missing data for key variables, such as food addiction or diagnostic, which could not reasonably be imputed: these patients were not included in the study. The data appeared missing completely at random, as none of the variables were associated with missingness, all ps > 0.125). The final simple included patients with the AN- binge-eating/purging type (n = 32), AN-restrictive type (n = 38), BN (n = 29), and BED (n = 16). Ethics statement

The data used for this analysis were from a broader study approved by the local Ethics Committee and all participants provided their consent for the use of these data (CPP: AU 1313; ID-RCB: 2017-A00269-44; Clinical Trial number: NCT03160443). The analyses reported here constitute secondary analyses and were not explicitly prespecified in the original trial registration.

Data collection and assessments

The following sociodemographic and clinical information were collected: age, sex, body mass index (BMI, measured on-site), and diagnosis (based on clinical interviews as described above). Data collection for the other measures took place on-site at the eating disorder unit, using self-report questionnaires (paper-based).

FA was assessed with the French version [19] of the YFAS 2.0 [6], which measures addictive-like eating behaviors related to highly processed foods. This scale has 35 items, each scored on a scale from 0 (never) to 7 (every day). For the primary analyses, a continuous total score (range: 0–245) was computed to provide a fine-grained measure of symptom severity, with higher scores indicating greater food addiction symptomatology. In addition, in line with recommended scoring procedures, two alternative operationalizations were derived from item-level responses. The symptom count score (ranging from 0 to 11) reflects the number of diagnostic criteria met. The diagnostic classification (presence vs. absence of food addiction) was defined by the presence of at least two symptoms in combination with clinically significant impairment or distress.

The affective system, was assessed with the Emotional Appetite Questionnaire - EMAQ [20] (22 items), which measures the tendency to eat in response to both positive and negative emotions and situations. The EMAQ provides two subscores: increased appetite with positive emotions (EMAQ-PA) and increased appetite with negative emotions (EMAQ-NA). Total scores range from 9 to 72 for the EMAQ-PA and from 14 to 129 for the EMAQ-NA. Higher scores indicate more emotional appetite.

The reflexive system, was assessed with the Detail and Flexibility Questionnaire - Dflex [21]. This questionnaire consists of two scales assessing cognitive flexibility (6 items) and attention to detail (10 items). The cognitive flexibility subscale assesses the ability to shift between thoughts and to consider alternatives. The attention to detail subscale assesses a tendency to focus on details rather than the big picture. As the two subscales were significantly correlated (r = .70) and had very similar correlations with other variables, only the results for total scores are detailed here. The two scores were summed, yielding total scores ranging from 16 to 160. Higher scores indicate higher cognitive difficulty.

Lastly, the interoceptive system was assessed with the interoception subscale of the Eating Disorder Inventory-2- EDI-2 [22]. This subscale (10 items) assesses the ability to recognize and respond to internal bodily sensations. Total scores range from 0 to 30. Higher scores indicate lower interoceptive consciousness.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were computed for all study variables, and one-way ANOVAs were used to examine differences across the four diagnostic groups (AN–restrictive, AN– binge-eating/purging, BN, and BED), except for gender.

To determine the relationships between food addiction and the three regulatory systems—affective (negative emotions related to eating), reflexive (cognitive flexibility), and interoceptive (interoceptive awareness)—a series of simple linear regressions were first conducted. Each predictor was tested separately for its association with food addiction using two continuous operationalizations of the YFAS 2.0: the continuous severity score and the symptom count score. Analyses were performed in the overall sample and within each diagnostic group. Cook’s distance was used to search for influential observations; the analyses were repeated without influential data points, and if this did not change the conclusions, the original results were reported.

In complementary analyses, food addiction was operationalized using the diagnostic classification (presence vs. absence of food addiction), and logistic regressions were conducted to examine whether the three systems predicted this binary outcome. As in the linear models, both simple and multiple logistic regressions were estimated, and interactions with diagnostic group were tested.

Multiple linear regressions were conducted subsequently to examine the joint contribution of the three systems in predicting food addiction scores. Interaction terms between each predictor and diagnostic group were tested to assess whether these associations differed across diagnostic categories.

Finally, a multinomial logistic regression was used to determine whether scores on the three regulatory systems could predict diagnostic group membership. The model included negative emotional responses to eating, interoceptive awareness, and cognitive flexibility as predictors. EMAQ positive emotion scores were excluded because of their lack of association with food addiction in preliminary analyses. Control variables (BMI, age, and gender) were included in analyses to assess the robustness of the findings; as this did not change any of the results, these analyses are not systematically reported here.

The significance threshold was set at p < .050 for all analyses. Data analysis was conducted using jamovi statistical software [23].

Results

The descriptive statistics for all the measures of interest are summarized in Table 1. All variables had reasonably normal distributions suitable for the use of linear models (all skewness coefficients < |1|, kurtosis < |1| except 2.60 for positive emotions). Measures of the three systems had mild correlations (the only significant correlations were Dflex and interoceptive consciousness: r = .49; Dflex and negative emotions related to eating: r = .33); multivariate models showed no problem of multicollinearity (all VIFs < 2.00, all tolerances > 0.65). Influence measures did not lead to removing any observation.

On average, the four diagnostic groups significantly differed in food addiction across all operationalizations (continuous severity score, symptom count, and diagnostic classification), negative emotions related to eating, and interoceptive consciousness, but not positive emotions related to eating or Dflex scores.

Regressions between food addiction (continuous score and symptom count) and each of the three systems

Simple regressions revealed that overall, food addiction (continuous score) was separately predicted by scores of each of the three systems: higher food addiction scores on the YFAS 2.0 were associated with more negative emotions related to eating (i.e. the affective system), more cognitive difficulty on the Dflex (i.e. the reflexive system), and less interoceptive consciousness on EDI-2 (i.e. the interoceptive system). These results are summarized in Table 2. The relationships were broadly consistent across diagnostic groups (although they did not reach significance at the p < .05 level for all groups, partly owing to smaller sample sizes). The interoceptive system was a significant predictor of food addiction in all groups, the reflexive system in all but AN- binge-eating/purging, and the affective system (i.e., negative emotions) only in AN-restrictive. A similar pattern of associations was observed when food addiction was operationalized using the YFAS 2.0 symptom count (see Table 3). Testing the interaction between each predictor and the diagnostic group via multiple regressions revealed that none of the effects were in fact significantly modulated by group, all ps > 0.535 for the continuous score (all ps > 0.220 for symptom count), suggesting that the three predictors functioned in broadly similar ways across groups.

Multiple regressions of food addiction (continuous score and symptom count) on the three systems

Jointly predicting FA based on scores for all three subsystems via multiple regressions revealed that overall, food addiction was predicted by a combination of interoceptive consciousness and negative emotions related to eating. A similar pattern of associations was observed when food addiction was operationalized using the YFAS 2.0 symptom count (see Table 5). Logistic regressions of binary food addiction on the three systems

When scoring the YFAS as a binary variable (addiction vs. no addiction) and analyzing the data with logistic regressions, the results were generally similar: food addiction was positively and significantly predicted by cognitive difficulty on the Dflex (p < .001), interoceptive consciousness on the EDI-2 (p < .001), and negative emotions (p < .001). Only cognitive difficulty and negative emotions retained a predictive value in a multiple regression (both ps < 0.02). All interactions with diagnostic group were non-significant, reflecting similar effects across groups (all ps > 0.178 in simple regressions, all ps > 0.211 in a multiple regression).

Prediction of diagnostic groups by the three systems

The final series of analyses tested whether the diagnostic group of a given patient (AN – binge-eating/purging, AN – restrictive, BN or BED) could be predicted on the basis of their scores on the three systems. This analysis used a multinomial logistic multiple regression, with the 4-modality diagnostic group as the dependent variable, and with the EDI-2 interoceptive scale, EMAQ negative emotions, and Dflex total score as predictors. EMAQ positive emotions were not included given the lack of relationship of this variable with other outcomes.

The analysis revealed a significant effect of EMAQ negative emotions, χ²(3) = 21.78, p < .001, but no significant effect of the EDI-2 interoceptive scale, χ²(3) = 6.15, p = .105, or Dflex total score, χ²(3) = 1.35, p = .718, suggesting that the affective system was the most suited to discriminating between the four possible diagnoses. This effect was driven by negative emotions associated with eating being lower in AN - restrictive than in AN - binge-eating/purging, and in turn, slightly lower in AN - binge-eating/purging than in either BN or BED. However, the three systems predicted only a modest part of the variance in diagnosis (McFadden’s R² = 0.11), and classification accuracy was poor (accuracy = 50.4%; see Table 6 for the confusion matrix). Inspecting contrasts between modalities revealed that none of the three systems were able to consistently differentiate among diagnostic groups (see Table 7), as even negative emotions related to eating did not significantly distinguish between BN and either AN- binge-eating/purging or BED.

Discussion

This study examined the triadic model of addictive behaviors — reflexive, affective, and interoceptive systems — in the context of FA across individuals diagnosed with AN, BN, and BED. The findings indicate that FA is more strongly associated with interoceptive difficulties and negative emotions related to eating than with deficits in cognitive flexibility. This pattern was observed consistently across diagnostic groups.

When considered separately, each of the three systems was associated with FA, suggesting the broad relevance of all three processes. However, only interoception and negative affect for eating retained unique predictive value in the joint model. The reflexive system, operationalized through a measure of cognitive flexibility (Dflex), was no longer a significant predictor after accounting for interoception and affect. This finding suggests that the reflexive system may contribute indirectly or be less central in explaining FA in EDs. In other words, despite the prominence of psychological and cognitive flexibility in the literature on EDs (24–26], these findings suggest that the reflexive system — measured through self-reported cognitive flexibility — may play a less central role in explaining food addiction (FA) than affective and interoceptive processes do.

While previous studies have highlighted associations between low flexibility and eating psychopathology, especially in AN, much of this evidence (including our own prior study) relies on self-report measures or is correlational in nature. Notably, a recent systematic review [27] reported inconsistent findings across studies assessing cognitive flexibility in AN patients. Although some impairments were observed in adults with acute AN, particularly in perceptual flexibility tasks, these impairments were not found consistently across all domains or patient subgroups. Adolescents with acute AN did not exhibit objective deficits despite reporting low flexibility, and differences between acute and recovered patients were limited. This raises questions about the extent to which cognitive flexibility represents a stable and specific deficit in EDs, and whether its role may have been overemphasized compared with the more consistent findings related to emotional dysregulation and interoceptive dysfunction. Furthermore, the current data indicate that once emotional and interoceptive factors are accounted for, cognitive flexibility no longer explains the unique variance in FA. These findings suggest that previous associations between cognitive flexibility and disordered eating may partly reflect a confounding role of underlying affective processes.

While cognitive flexibility is often emphasized theoretically in EDs, the current findings — alongside prior inconsistent results — raise concerns regarding the validity and sensitivity of the tools commonly used to assess this construct. The DFlex questionnaire, while widely employed, is a self-reported measure that may reflect subjective beliefs rather than actual cognitive performance. Similarly, traditional neuropsychological tests such as the Trail Making Test (TMT) have been criticized for their lack of specificity, as they involve multiple cognitive domains beyond flexibility. According to Pires et al. [28], many patients with AN perform within the normative range on such classical tasks, despite clinically observed rigidity. These findings suggest that current tools may be insufficiently sensitive to detect subtle but clinically meaningful impairments in this population. In response to this limitation, Pires and colleagues have proposed the Local-Global Task (LGT) as a more targeted and sensitive assessment of set-shifting abilities, showing encouraging results in case-based evaluations. This highlights the need for the field to adopt more precise (i.e., more sensitive and specific) instruments that can capture the specific cognitive profiles of ED patients, particularly those with restrictive subtypes. Without such refinement, the apparent lack of association between flexibility and clinical symptoms — such as FA in the current study — may reflect measurement limitations rather than a true absence of cognitive impairment.

In short, the current findings suggest that interoceptive dysfunction and emotional difficulties may represent core vulnerabilities across eating disorder diagnoses, as they consistently predict food addiction independently of diagnostic subtype. Taken together, these findings underscore the central role of interoceptive and affective processes in patterns of addiction across diagnostics, while also highlighting the conceptual and methodological challenges that complicate the interpretation of the contribution of cognitive flexibility.

Critically, the absence of significant interactions with diagnostic group indicates that the associations between the three systems and food addiction are broadly similar across eating disorder diagnoses. Although this null interaction does not exclude the possibility of more subtle diagnostic differences that could emerge in larger samples, it converges with the limited discriminative performance of the multinomial model (McFadden’s R² = 0.11). Together, these findings indicate that average differences between diagnostic categories are small for each of the three systems. Rather than supporting diagnosis-specific differentiation, this pattern is more consistent with a transdiagnostic, dimensional interpretation. Patients with more severe food addiction tend to exhibit greater dysfunction across systems, irrespective of eating disorder subtype, and the degree of impairment within any single system does not allow reliable prediction of diagnosis. These results are therefore compatible with a general triadic model of addiction, in which core cognitive, affective, and interoceptive processes cut across diagnostic boundaries.

Furthermore, the current finding that interoceptive and affective dysfunctions predict food addiction across different eating disorder (ED) diagnoses, without significant interaction effects with the diagnostic itself, aligns with emerging transdiagnostic models of psychopathology. This dimensional pattern of association supports the rationale behind the NIMH Research Domain Criteria (RDoC) initiative [29–31], which advocates for studying mental disorders through core neurobehavioral systems that cut across traditional diagnostic boundaries. In the RDoC framework, constructs such as interoception (nested under the arousal and regulatory systems domain) and negative valence (part of the negative valence systems) are conceptualized as fundamental processes implicated in various forms of psychopathology. The results of the present study indicate the potential for these systems to underlie addictive eating behaviors irrespective of the specific ED subtype, thereby emphasizing the importance of prioritizing dimensional constructs over nosological categories when conceptualizing the mechanisms of eating and addictive disorders.

In light of the present findings, recent proposals to restrict addictive-like eating behaviors to ultra-processed foods rather than food per se warrant consideration. Empirical work has shown that addictive-like eating behaviors are more strongly associated with ultra-processed foods than with minimally processed foods [32], a pattern that aligns with the strong associations observed here between food addiction, affective reactivity, and interoceptive dysfunction. Although the present study was not designed to distinguish between competing conceptual frameworks, these results are compatible with both food addiction and ultra-processed food addiction (UPFA) perspectives. In this sense, the present findings suggest that core addictive-like processes may operate regardless of whether the addictive target is defined broadly (food addiction) or more narrowly (UPFA). Future studies explicitly designed to compare these frameworks, using refined exposure and consumption measures, will be necessary to clarify their respective validity.

Several limitations should be acknowledged in interpreting these findings. First, important limitations should be acknowledged regarding the operationalization of the triadic model. First, all three systems were assessed using self-report instruments derived from routinely collected clinical data, which necessarily provide only partial proxies of the underlying neurocognitive processes. The reflective system was operationalized through self-reported cognitive rigidity and attentional style, without direct measures of inhibitory control or executive performance. Likewise, the affective system was assessed through subjective emotional appetite rather than experimental indices of emotional reactivity or reward sensitivity, and the interoceptive system through self-reported interoceptive awareness rather than objective physiological or behavioral measures. As a result, none of the three systems can be considered as exhaustively captured, and the relative contribution of each system to food addiction should be interpreted with caution. In particular, the absence of a unique effect of the reflective system in multivariate models may reflect measurement constraints inherent to self-report instruments, rather than a genuine lack of involvement of reflective control processes. These limitations are intrinsic to the use of care-based datasets and highlight the need for future studies specifically designed to test the triadic model in eating disorders, integrating objective and experimental measures for each system. Secondly, the sample size was limited, particularly for the BN and BED subgroups, which reduces statistical power for diagnostic-specific comparisons and increases the risk of type II error when examining group differences. Accordingly, subgroup-level results should be interpreted with caution. That said, the primary analyses relied on the full sample (N = 115) and tested moderation by diagnostic group within unified regression models, rather than on separate group-wise analyses. This approach allowed us to maximize statistical power and yielded consistent results across diagnostic categories, partially mitigating limitations related to small subgroup sizes. In addition, the cross-sectional design of the study precludes any inference regarding the temporal ordering or causal relationships between dysfunctions in the triadic systems and food addiction. Longitudinal designs will be required to determine whether these processes represent vulnerability factors, maintaining mechanisms, or consequences of addictive-like eating behaviors. Moreover, this study was based only on self-report questionnaires, which are explicit measures that are prone to participant bias. Implicit measures could be useful in these types of exploration, such as the water-load test [33] to evaluate interoception, the attentional network task for attention capacities [34], or the local global task for flexibility [35] for the reflexive system. The affective system could also be measured with experimental paradigms such as a dot probe task [36]. Finally, the absence of a healthy control group prevents a clear differentiation of which results are specific to a clinical population.

Accordingly, the present findings should not be interpreted as supporting direct clinical targeting of specific systems, but rather as contributing to a preliminary, transdiagnostic understanding of addictive-like processes in eating disorders.

Conclusions

The current study was among the first to explore a triadic model of addictions in the context of eating disorders. The findings support the relevance of interoceptive and affective disturbances in FA across eating disorder diagnoses. The reflexive system, as measured with a self-reported flexibility score, appeared to contribute less robustly, especially when considered alongside the other two systems. Understanding how these three systems jointly and differentially influence FA may help refine models of ED pathology and guide the development of targeted interventions.
