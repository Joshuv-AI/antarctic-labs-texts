Synchronization points: growth, asymptotics, congruences, and the synchronization zeta function
Alexander Fel’shtyn and Mateusz Slomiany

Abstract
. In this paper, we introduce the synchronization zeta function associated with a pair of self-maps of a topological space and investigate its properties. We also define the growth rate of synchronization points and derive an explicit formula in the setting of endomorphisms of compact, connected Abelian groups. In addition, we establish Gauss congruences and describe the asymptotic behavior for the sequence of numbers of synchronization points, under the assumption that the synchronization zeta function is rational. Further, we discuss connections with topological entropy and Reidemeister torsion.

1.
Introduction

Let
X
be a topological space and let
\alpha,\beta:X\to X
be two maps of
X
to itself.
We denote the set of
synchronization points of
n
-th iteration of
\alpha
and
\beta
by

S(\alpha^{n},\beta^{n}):=\left\{x\in X\ |\ \alpha^{n}(x)=\beta^{n}(x)\right\}.

If one of the maps is the identity map, say
\beta=\operatorname{Id}
, then
S(\alpha^{n},\operatorname{Id})
is the set of
fixed points of
\alpha^{n}
.
In topology the synchronization points are also known as
points of coincidence
.

We say that a pair of maps
\alpha,\beta
is
synchronously tame
if
the numbers of synchronization points
\#S(\alpha^{n},\beta^{n})
are finite for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

In the present paper we define the
synchronization zeta function
of a synchronously tame pair
\alpha,\beta
as

S_{\alpha,\beta}(z):=\exp\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{\#S(\alpha^{k},\beta^{k})}{k}z^{k}\right),

where
z
denotes a complex variable, and undertake its investigation.
When
\beta
or
\alpha
is the identity map, the synchronization zeta function becomes
the
Artin-Mazur zeta function
:

\zeta_{\alpha}(z):=\exp\left(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\#\operatorname{Fix}(\alpha^{n})}{n}z^{n}\right).

The synchronization zeta function can be
regarded as an analogue of the Hasse–Weil zeta function of an
algebraic variety over a finite field or the Artin–Mazur zeta
function of a continuous self-map of a topological space.
In the theory of dynamical systems, the synchronization zeta
function counts the synchronisation points of two maps, i.e. the
points whose orbits intersect under simultaneous iteration of two
endomorphisms; see
[
25
]
, for instance.
We define the
upper growth rate
,
lower growth rate

and
growth rate
(if it exists) of synchronization points of
\alpha
and
\beta
, to be

\displaystyle\begin{split}S^{\infty}_{+}(\alpha,\beta)&:=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\left(\#S(\alpha^{n},\beta^{n})\right)^{1/n},\\
S^{\infty}_{-}(\alpha,\beta)&:=\liminf_{n\to\infty}\left(\#S(\alpha^{n},\beta^{n})\right)^{1/n},\\
S^{\infty}(\alpha,\beta)&:=\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(\#S(\alpha^{n},\beta^{n})\right)^{1/n},\end{split}

respectively.
Due to Cauchy-Hadamard theorem, the radius of convergence of the synchronization zeta function
is the inverse
1/S^{\infty}_{+}(\alpha,\beta)
of the upper growth rate.

In this work we present, in analogy to works of
Bell, Miles, Ward
[
3
]
and Byszewski, Cornelissen
[
5
, §5]
and
[
15
]
results in support of a Pólya–Carlson dichotomy between
rationality and a natural boundary for the analytic behavior of the
synchronization zeta function.

In case of compact connected Abelian groups we give an explicit formula
for the growth rate
S^{\infty}(\alpha,\beta)
.
In case of some homeomorphisms of compact metric spaces we show a relationship between
the growth rate
S^{\infty}(\alpha,\beta)
and topological entropy.
We also show that a positive answer to the question rationality of the synchronization zeta function implies the Gauss congruences
for the sequence
\{\#S(\alpha^{n},\beta^{n})\}
.
We provide an observation on asymptotic behavior of the sequence
\{\#S(\alpha^{n},\beta^{n})\}
.
We also show a connection between special values of the synchronization zeta function and the Reidemeister torsion.

This work is organized as follows.

In section
2
, we remind definitions, facts and notation
from other theories which are of importance in course of the paper.
They regard topological entropy of a map, unitary dual of a group,
isolated lower central series of a nilpotent group, Markov partitions, and
p
-adic numbers.

In section
3
, we prove Pólya-Carlson dichotomy for the
synchronization zeta function of endomorphisms of compact connected Abelian groups
as well as rationality of this zeta function for dual maps to commuting automorphisms
of torsion-free virtually polycyclic groups, for commuting Axiom A diffeomorphisms
of
C^{\infty}
compact manifolds without boundary, and for commuting pseudo-Anosov homeomorphisms
of closed compact surfaces.
We also investigate the synchronization zeta function of maps of any finite set.

In section
4
, we prove the Gauss congruences for the sequence

\{\#S(\varphi^{n},\psi^{n})\}
of the numbers of synchronization points of the synchronously
tame pair
(\varphi,\psi)
of maps, under the assumption that the synchronization zeta function is rational.

In section
5
, we first discuss expansiveness and the so called
specification
property
for homeomorphisms, then we provide a relation between the growth rate of synchronization points
of a pair
\alpha,\beta
of commuting homeomorphisms of compact metric spaces and topological entropy
of
\beta^{-1}\alpha
, when the latter satisfies specification.

In section
6
, we study asymptotic behavior of the sequence

\{\#S(f^{n},g^{n})\}
of the numbers of synchronization points of the synchronously
tame pair
(f,g)
of maps of a compact metric space when the synchronization
zeta function is rational.

In section
7
, we show how the Reidemeister torsion arises as a special value
of the synchronization zeta function of dual maps to commuting automorphisms of finitely generated
torsion free nilpotent groups.

Preliminaries

2.1.
Reidemeister zeta function and Pólya-Carslon dichotomy

Let
G
be a group and
\varphi,\psi:G\rightarrow G
two endomorphisms.
Two elements
\alpha,\beta\in G
are said to be
(\varphi,\psi)
-conjugate

if and only if there exists
g\in G
with
\beta=\psi(g)\alpha\varphi(g^{-1})
.
The number of
(\varphi,\psi
)-conjugacy classes is called the

Reidemeister coincidence number
of endomorphisms
\varphi
and
\psi
,
denoted by
R(\varphi,\psi)
. If
\psi
is the identity map then the
(\varphi,\operatorname{Id})
-conjugacy
classes are the
\varphi
-conjugacy classes in the group
G
and
R(\varphi,\operatorname{Id})=R(\varphi)
,
where
R(\varphi)
is the usual Reidemeister number.

We call an endomorphism
\varphi

tame
if the Reidemeister numbers
R(\varphi^{n})

are finite for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. We call a pair
(\varphi,\psi)
of endomorphisms

tame
if the coincidence Reidemeister numbers
R(\varphi^{n},\psi^{n})
are finite
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

The
Reidemeister coincidence zeta function
of the pair
\varphi,\psi
is defined as

R_{\varphi,\psi}(z):=\exp\left(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{R(\varphi^{n},\psi^{n})}{n}z^{n}\right).

The
growth rate
of Reidemeister coincidence numbers, if it exists, is defined as

R^{\infty}(\varphi,\psi):=\lim_{n\to\infty}R(\varphi^{n},\psi^{n})^{1/n}.

The classical Pólya–Carlson theorem, as discussed in
[
26
, §6.5]
,
provides the following connection between the arithmetic properties of
the coefficients of a complex power series and its analytic behavior.
Suppose that an analytic function
F
is defined somehow in a region
D
of a complex plane.
If there is no point of the boundary
\partial D
of
D
over which
F
can be analytically continued,
then
\partial D
is called a
natural boundary
for
F
.

Theorem 1

(Pólya-Carlson, see
[
26
]
)
.

A power series with integer coefficients and radius of convergence
1
is either rational
or has the unit circle as a natural boundary.

It is convenient to introduce a notation

Z_{\varphi,\psi}(z):=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}R(\varphi^{n},\psi^{n})\cdot z^{n}.

for the generating series that enumerates directly the numbers of coincidence Reidemeister classes.
The following lemma shows in particular that, if
Z_{\varphi,\psi}(z)
has a natural
boundary at its radius of convergence, then so
does
R_{\varphi,\psi}(z)
; compare
[
3
]
.

Lemma 2
.

Let
\varphi,\psi:G\to G
be endomorphisms of a group.
If
R_{\varphi,\psi}(z)
is rational then
Z_{\varphi,\psi}(z)
is rational.
If
R_{\varphi,\psi}(z)
has an analytic continuation beyond
its circle of convergence, then so does
Z_{\varphi,\psi}(z)
too.
In particular, the existence of a natural boundary
at the circle of convergence for
Z_{\varphi,\psi}(z)
implies the
existence of a natural boundary for
R_{\varphi,\psi}(z)
.

Proof.

This follows from the fact that
Z_{\varphi,\psi}(z)=z\cdot R_{\varphi,\psi}(z)^{{}^{\prime}}/R_{\varphi,\psi}(z)
.
∎

For the proofs of the main theorems in this paper we rely on the
following key result of Bell, Miles and Ward.

Lemma 3

(
[
3
, Lemma 17]
)
.

Let
S
be a finite list of places of algebraic number fields and, for each
v\in S
,
let
\xi_{v}
be a non-unit root in the appropriate number field such that
|\xi_{v}|_{v}=1
.
Then the function

\displaystyle F(z):=\sum_{n\geq 1}f(n)z^{n},

where
f(n):=\prod_{v\in S}|\xi_{v}^{n}-1|_{v}
for
n\geq 1
,
has the unit circle as a natural boundary.

2.2.
p
-adics

In this section we remind basic definitions and prove facts about
p
-adic numbers needed later.
Let
p
be any prime number. For
a\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
we define the
p
-adic ordinal
\operatorname{ord}_{p}a

to be the highest power of
p
which divides
a
.
For a rational
q=a/b
we define
\operatorname{ord}_{p}q:=\operatorname{ord}_{p}a-\operatorname{ord}_{p}b
.
The
p
-adic norm of
q
is defined

\left|q\right|_{p}:=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{p^{\operatorname{ord}_{p}q}},\quad&q\neq 0,\\
0,\quad&q=0\end{cases}.

The norm is
non-Archimedean
which means that for all
x,y\in\mathbb{Q}

it satisfies
\left|x+y\right|_{p}\leq\max\left(|x|_{p},|y|_{p}\right)
.

We will make use of the following property:

(1)

\big(|x|_{p}<|y|_{p}\big)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad|x-y|_{p}=|y|_{p}.

The field
\mathbb{Q}
with the norm
|\cdot|_{p}
is not complete but if we consider the set
\mathbb{Q}_{p}

of equivalence classes of Cauchy sequences with regard to the relation

\{a_{i}\}\sim\{b_{i}\}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad|a_{i}-b_{i}|_{p}\to 0\ (i\to\infty)

(just as we actually do when we go from
\mathbb{Q}
to
\mathbb{R}
with the usual norm)
with the
p
-adic norm of a sequence defined as
\left|\{a_{i}\}\right|_{p}:=\lim|a_{i}|_{p}

then
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
turns out to be a complete field.
Moreover, from (
1
) and definition of Cauchy sequences we get
that in fact the sequence
\{|a_{i}|_{p}\}
of
p
-adic norms of elements of a Cauchy sequence
\{a_{i}\}

is constant for sufficiently large
i
.
The following remark will be important later.

Remark 4
.

Possible values for
|\cdot|_{p}
on
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
are in the set
\{p^{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\cup\{0\}
.
In particular the greatest possible value
<1
is
1/p
.

We are going to work with numbers algebraic over
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
.
Let
\mathbb{Q}_{p}(\xi)
be a finite extension of
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
generated by
\xi
which is a root of a monic polynomial

x^{n}+a_{1}x^{n-1}+\dots+a_{n-1}x+a_{n},\quad a_{i}\in\mathbb{Q}_{p}.

We define the
norm
of the extension as
\mathbb{N}_{\mathbb{Q}_{p}(\xi)/\mathbb{Q}_{p}}(\xi):=(-1)^{n}a_{n}
.
The extension of
p
-adic norm from
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
to
\mathbb{Q}_{p}(\xi)
is defined as

|\xi|_{p}:=\left|\mathbb{N}_{\mathbb{Q}_{p}(\xi)/\mathbb{Q}_{p}}(\xi)\right|_{p}^{1/n}.

There exist the algebraic closure
\overline{\mathbb{Q}_{p}}
and its completion
\Omega
(which is algebraically
closed as well) with further extensions of the norm but we will not need them in this work.
The subset
\mathbb{Z}_{p}:=\{x\in\mathbb{Q}_{p}\mid|x|_{p}\leq 1\}
is a ring
and its elements are called
p
-adic integers

There is the
p
-adic analog of logarithm defined for all
z\in\Omega
satisfying
|z|_{p}<1
:

\log_{p}(1+z):=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{m+1}}{m}z^{m}.

We are going to make use of the
p
-adic logarithm to prove a very specific inequality
which comes from
[
7
, Theorem 6.1]
.

Lemma 5

(
[
17
, Lemma 2.4]
)
.

Let
\xi
be an algebraic number over
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
such that
|\xi|_{p}=1
but
\xi
is not a root of unity.
Then there is a constant
C
such that for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
the inequality

(2)

\frac{1}{n}\leq C\cdot\left|\xi^{n}-1\right|_{p}

holds. The constant does not depend on
n
.

Proof.

For
|1|_{p}=1=|\xi|_{p}
, from (
1
) we have
|\xi^{n}-1|_{p}\leq 1
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
If for some
n
there is equality
|\xi^{n}-1|_{p}=1
then (
2
) holds for
C=1

Now consider
n
for which
|\xi^{n}-1|_{p}<1
.
Denote the degree
\left[\mathbb{Q}_{p}(\xi):\mathbb{Q}_{p}\right]=:k
.
From remark
4
and
the definition of the
p
-adic norm for algebraic numbers we have

(3)

|\xi^{n}-1|_{p}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt[k]{p}}.

Therefore we can compute the
p
-adic logarithm and

\displaystyle|n|_{p}\cdot|\log_{p}(\xi)|_{p}

\displaystyle=|\log_{p}(\xi^{n})|_{p}

\displaystyle=\left|(\xi^{n}-1)-\frac{(\xi^{n}-1)^{2}}{2}+\frac{(\xi^{n}-1)^{3}}{3}-\dots\right|_{p}

\displaystyle\leq\max_{m}\left(\left|\frac{(\xi^{n}-1)^{m}}{m}\right|_{p}\right)

Note that using (
3
) we get

0\leq\left|\frac{(\xi^{n}-1)^{m}}{m}\right|_{p}=p^{\operatorname{ord}_{p}m}\cdot\left|\xi^{n}-1\right|_{p}^{m}\leq m\cdot\left|\xi^{n}-1\right|_{p}^{m}\leq m\cdot\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt[k]{p}}\right)^{m}.

For a constant
q\in(0,1/\sqrt[k]{p}]
the derivative

\frac{\partial}{\partial x}xq^{x}=q^{x}(1+x\ln q)
vanishes only for

x=-1/\ln q
, is positive for smaller
x
and negative for greater ones.
Note that
-1/\ln q
is an increasing function therefore in our situation
-1/\ln q\ \leq\ k/\ln p
.
It is possible then that for a finite number of initial
m
s the terms over which we take the maximum
actually grow, but eventually there is a maximum
M=M(n)
and the sequence goes to
0
as
m\to\infty
.

In other words there exists
m_{0}=m_{0}(n)
satisfying
1\leq m_{0}\leq k/\ln p
, for which

\max_{m}\left(\left|\frac{(\xi^{n}-1)^{m}}{m}\right|_{p}\right)=|\xi^{n}-1|_{p}\cdot\frac{\left|\xi^{n}-1\right|_{p}^{m_{0}-1}}{|m_{0}|_{p}}

We can bound from above the right side similarly as above:

\displaystyle|\xi^{n}-1|_{p}\cdot\frac{\left|\xi^{n}-1\right|_{p}^{m_{0}-1}}{|m_{0}|_{p}}

\displaystyle=|\xi^{n}-1|_{p}\cdot\Big(p^{\operatorname{ord}_{p}m_{0}}\cdot\left|\xi^{n}-1\right|_{p}^{m_{0}-1}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq|\xi^{n}-1|_{p}\cdot\Big(m_{0}\cdot\left|\xi^{n}-1\right|_{p}^{m_{0}-1}\Big)

For
x\in[1,k/\ln p]
the derivative
\frac{\partial}{\partial q}xq^{x-1}=x(x-1)q^{x-2}\geq 0
so

\displaystyle|\xi^{n}-1|_{p}\cdot\Big(m_{0}\cdot\left|\xi^{n}-1\right|_{p}^{m_{0}-1}\Big)\ \leq\ |\xi^{n}-1|_{p}\cdot\left(m_{0}\cdot\frac{1}{(\sqrt[k]{p})^{m_{0}-1}}\right)

Note that

M^{*}:=\max_{1\ \leq\ m\ \leq\ k/\ln p}\left\{m\cdot p^{-(m-1)/k}\right\}\ \geq\ m_{0}\cdot p^{-(m_{0}-1)/k}

for all possible
m_{0}
, therefore
M^{*}
does not depend on
n
. We got

\frac{1}{n}\ \leq\ |n|_{p}\ \leq\ |\xi^{n}-1|_{p}\cdot\frac{M^{*}}{|\log_{p}(\xi)|_{p}}

and (
2
) holds for
C=M^{*}/|\log_{p}(\xi)|_{p}
.

In general (
2
) holds for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
regardless of actual values of
|\xi^{n}-1|_{p}

if we put
C:=\max\left\{1,M^{*}/|\log_{p}(\xi)|_{p}\right\}
.
∎

2.3.
Unitary dual

By
\widehat{G}
we denote the
unitary dual
of a group
G
, i.e.
the space of equivalence classes of
unitary irreducible representations of
G
, equipped with the

compact-open
topology.
If
\varphi:G\to G
is an automorphism,
it induces a dual map
\widehat{\varphi}:\widehat{G}\to\widehat{G}
,
\widehat{\varphi}(\rho):=\rho\circ\varphi
.
This dual map
\widehat{\varphi}
define a dynamical system on the unitary dual space
\widehat{G}
.

The unitary dual of any finitely generated abelian group
G
is an abelian group
\widehat{G}
which is,
by Pontryagin duality, the direct sum
of a Torus whose dimension is the rank of
G
, and of a finite abelian group.
In that case the dual map will be called the
Pontryagin dual map
.

We will need the following result.

Lemma 6
.

[
13
, Proposition 1]

Let
\varphi:G\to G
be an endomorphism of an Abelian group
G

and let
\widehat{\varphi}:\widehat{G}\to\widehat{G}
be the map induced by
\varphi

on the Pontryagin dual
\widehat{G}
of
G
.
Then

\operatorname{Ker}\widehat{\varphi}\ \cong\ \widehat{\operatorname{Coker}}\varphi.

Proof.

We construct the isomorphism explicitly.
Let
\chi\in\widehat{\operatorname{Coker}}\varphi
. In that case
\chi
is a homomorphism

\chi:\ \operatorname{Coker}\varphi=G/\operatorname{Im}\varphi\to U(1).

This homomorphism induces a map

\bar{\chi}:\ G\to U(1)

which is trivial on
\operatorname{Im}\varphi
.
This means that
\bar{\chi}\circ\varphi=\widehat{\varphi}(\bar{\chi})

is the identity element of
\widehat{G}
.
Hence
\bar{\chi}\in\operatorname{Ker}\widehat{\varphi}
.

If, on the other hand, we begin with
\bar{\chi}\in\operatorname{Ker}\widehat{\varphi}
,
then it follows that
\chi
is trivial on
\operatorname{Im}\varphi
, and so
\bar{\chi}

induces a homomorphism
\chi:G/\operatorname{Im}\varphi\to U(1)

and
\chi
is therefore in the dual of
\operatorname{Coker}\varphi
.
The correspondence
\chi\leftrightarrow\bar{\chi}
is clearly a bijection.
∎

2.4.
Markov partitions

Choose a natural number
N\geq 2
and consider the space

\Omega_{N}\ :=\ \left\{\omega=(\ldots,\omega_{-1},\omega_{0},\omega_{1},\ldots)\;|\;\omega_{i}\in\{0,1,\ldots,N-1\},i\in\mathbb{Z}\right\}.

The
left shift
in
\Omega_{N}
(sometimes called a
topological Bernoulli shift
) is the map

\sigma_{N}:\Omega_{N}\to\Omega_{n},\quad\sigma_{N}(\omega)=\omega^{\prime}=(\ldots,\omega_{0}^{\prime},\omega_{1}^{\prime},\ldots),

where
\omega_{n}^{\prime}=\omega_{n+1}
.

Let
A=(a_{ij})
be an
N\times N
matrix whose entries
a_{ij}\in\{0,1\}
.
Let

\Omega_{A}\ :=\ \left\{\omega\in\Omega_{N}\;|\;a_{\omega_{n}\omega_{n+1}}=1,n\in\mathbb{Z}\right\}.

One can think that the matrix
A
determines which symbols
0,1,\ldots,N-1
can be neighbors
in the sequence
\omega
.
The set
\Omega_{A}
is shift invariant.
The restriction
\sigma_{A}:=\left.\sigma_{N}\right|_{\Omega_{A}}
is a
subshift of finite type

(sometimes called a
topological Markov chain
).

Theorem 7
.

Number of periodic points of period
n\in\mathbb{N}
of a subshift of finite type
\sigma_{A}

\#\operatorname{Fix}(\sigma_{A}^{n})=\operatorname{tr}A^{n}.

Proof.

Fix two symbols
i,j\in\{0,\ldots,N-1\}
.
We will count the number
N_{ij}^{n}
of sequences
(\omega_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}
with
\omega_{0}=i
and
\omega_{n}=j
.
For
n=1
we have obviously
N_{ij}^{1}=a_{ij}
.
It is easy to see by induction that
N_{ij}^{n+1}=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}N_{ik}^{n}a_{kj}
,
it is just a sum of number of sequences with
\omega_{0}=i
and arbitrary
\omega_{k}
followed by
\omega_{k+1}=j
.
Observe, that if we write
a_{ij}^{n}
for the element in the
i
-th row and
j
-th column of
A^{n}
,
we have
N_{ij}^{n}=a_{ij}^{n}
.

Each sequence with
\omega_{0}=i=\omega_{n}
counted in
N_{ii}^{n}=a_{ii}^{n}
gives rise
to exactly one fixed point of
\sigma_{A}^{n}
, because for such a fixed point
\omega_{k}=\omega_{k+n}

and the terms
\omega_{0},\ldots,\omega_{n-1}
define it uniquely.
Summing over all
i=0,\ldots,N-1
finishes the proof.
∎

3.
Synchronization zeta function and growth of synchronization points

3.1.
Pólya-Carslon dichotomy

Our main result for the synchronization zeta function
S_{\alpha,\beta}(z)

in Theorem
9
below
is inspired by and proved in analogy with the following

Theorem 8

(
[
15
, Proposition 3.4]
)
.

Let
G
be a torsion-free abelian group of finite rank
d\geq 1
,
and let
(\varphi,\psi)
be a tame pair of endomorphisms for
G
.
Let
\varphi_{\mathbb{Q}},\psi_{\mathbb{Q}}\colon G_{\mathbb{Q}}\to G_{\mathbb{Q}}
denote the extensions of
\varphi,\psi
to the
divisible hull
G_{\mathbb{Q}}=\mathbb{Q}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}G\cong\mathbb{Q}^{d}

of
G
, and suppose that the endomorphisms

\varphi_{\mathbb{Q}},\psi_{\mathbb{Q}}
are simultaneously
triangularisable. Let
\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{d}
and

\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{d}
denote the eigenvalues of

\varphi_{\mathbb{Q}}
and
\psi_{\mathbb{Q}}
in a fixed algebraic
closure of the field
\mathbb{Q}
, including multiplicities,
ordered so that, for
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the eigenvalues of

\varphi_{\mathbb{Q}}^{\,n}-\psi_{\mathbb{Q}}^{\,n}
are

\xi_{1}^{\,n}-\eta_{1}^{\,n},\ldots,\xi_{d}^{\,n}-\eta_{d}^{\,n}
. Set

L=\mathbb{Q}(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{d},\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{d})
.

For each
p\in\mathbb{P}
, we fix an embedding

L\hookrightarrow\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}
, where

\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}
denotes the algebraic closure of

\mathbb{Q}_{p}
, and we write
\lvert\cdot\rvert_{p}
for the unique
prolongation of the
p
-adic absolute value
to
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}
; this is a way of choosing a particular
extension of the
p
-adic absolute value to
L
. Likewise, we
choose an embedding
L\hookrightarrow\mathbb{C}
and denote by

\lvert\cdot\rvert_{\infty}
the usual absolute value
on
\mathbb{C}
.

Then there exist subsets
I(p)\subseteq\{1,\ldots,d\}
, for

p\in\mathbb{P}
, such that the following hold.

(1)

For each
p\in\mathbb{P}
, the polynomials

\prod_{i\in I(p)}(X-\xi_{i})
and

\prod_{i\in I(p)}(X-\eta_{i})
have coefficients in

\mathbb{Z}_{p}
; in particular,

\lvert\xi_{i}\rvert_{p},\lvert\eta_{i}\rvert_{p}\leq 1
for

i\in I(p)
.

(2)

For each
n\in\mathbb{N}

(4)

&=\prod_{p\in\mathbf{P}}\prod_{i\in S^{*}(p)}\max\{\lvert\xi_{i}\rvert_{p},|\eta_{i}|_{p}\}\cdot\prod_{p\in\mathbf{P}}\,\prod_{i\in S(p)\setminus S^{*}(p)}\lvert\eta_{i}\rvert_{p}\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{d}\max\{|\xi_{i}|_{\infty},|\eta_{i}|_{\infty}\},\end{split}

G
is called
virtually polycyclic
if it has a subgroup of finite index which is polycyclic.
Every finitely generated nilpotent group is automatically polycyclic.

Theorem 14

(
[
18
, Theorem 7.8]
, Twisted Burnside-Frobenius Theorem)
.

Let
G
be a virtually polycyclic group and
\varphi:G\to G
its automorphism.
Then the Reidemeister number
R(\varphi)
is equal to the number of fixed points
of the map
\widehat{\varphi}
induced on the finite-dimensional part
\widehat{G}_{fin}

of the unitary dual of
G
.

Theorem 15

(
[
8
, Theorem 3.12]
)
.

Let
\varphi:\Gamma\to\Gamma
be a monomorphism of a virtually polycyclic group.
If
R(\varphi^{n})<\infty
for all integers
n>0
, then the group
\Gamma
must be virtually nilpotent.
In particular, the Reidemeister zeta function
R_{\varphi}(z)
of
\varphi
is rational
if the group
\Gamma
is additionally torsion-free.

Theorem 16
.

Let
G
be a torsion-free virtually polycyclic group
and
\varphi,\psi:G\to G
a synchronously tame pair of commuting automorphisms.
Denote the space of unitary irreducible representations of
G
by
\widehat{G}
,
its subspace of finite dimensional representations by
\widehat{G}_{fin}

and dual maps induced by
\varphi,\psi
on the subspace
\widehat{G}_{fin}
by
\widehat{\varphi}_{fin},\widehat{\psi}_{fin}
respectively.

Then
\#S(\widehat{\varphi}_{fin}^{n},\widehat{\psi}_{fin}^{n})=R(\varphi^{n},\psi^{n})
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}

and the synchronization zeta function
S_{\widehat{\varphi}_{fin},\widehat{\psi}_{fin}}(z)=R_{\varphi,\psi}(z)

is rational.

Proof.

For bijections
\widehat{\varphi}_{fin},\widehat{\psi}_{fin}

\displaystyle\rho\in S(\widehat{\varphi}_{fin}^{n},\widehat{\psi}_{fin}^{n})\

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\ \widehat{\varphi}_{fin}^{n}(\rho)=\widehat{\psi}_{fin}^{n}(\rho)\ \Leftrightarrow\ \left(\widehat{\psi}_{fin}^{-n}\circ\widehat{\varphi}_{fin}^{n}\right)(\rho)=\rho

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\ \left(\widehat{(\varphi^{n}\circ\psi^{-n})}_{fin}\right)(\rho)=\rho\ \Leftrightarrow\ \rho\in\operatorname{Fix}\left(\widehat{(\varphi^{n}\circ\psi^{-n})}_{fin}\right)

therefore, by commutativity of
\widehat{\varphi}_{fin},\widehat{\psi}_{fin}

and by Theorem
14

\displaystyle\#S(\widehat{\varphi}_{fin}^{n},\widehat{\psi}_{fin}^{n})

\displaystyle=\#\operatorname{Fix}\left(\widehat{(\varphi^{n}\circ\psi^{-n})}_{fin}\right)=R(\psi^{-n}\circ\varphi^{n})=R(\varphi^{n},\psi^{n}).

Observe that
\psi^{-n}\circ\varphi^{n}
is an automorphism, so in particular it is a monomoprhism,
therefore by Theorem
15
the group
G
must be virtually nilpotent
and because
G
is additionally by hypothesis torsion-free, the zeta function

\displaystyle S_{\widehat{\varphi}_{fin},\widehat{\psi}_{fin}}(z)=R_{\varphi,\psi}(z)=R_{\psi^{-1}\varphi}(z)

is rational.
∎

3.4.
Rationality of synchronization zeta function for Axiom A diffeomoprhisms

We recall definitions related to the Smale’s Axiom A as in
[
27
]
.
Let
M
be a compact manifold and
f\in\operatorname{Diff}(M)
be a diffeomorphism
such that the set
\operatorname{Fix}(f)
of its fixed points is finite.
A point
x\in M
is
wandering
if there is an open neighborhood
U
of
x

such that
\bigcup_{|m|>0}f^{m}(U)\cap U=\varnothing
.
A point is
nonwandering
if it is not wandering.
Nonwandering points form a closed invariant set which is denoted by
\Omega(f)
.

A linear automorphism
u:V\to V
of a finite dimensional vector space is
hyperbolic

if all its eigenvalues
\lambda_{i}
satisfy
|\lambda_{i}|\neq 1
,

contracting
if all its eigenvalues satisfy
|\lambda_{i}|<1
,
and
expanding
if all its eigenvalues satisfy
|\lambda_{i}|>1
.
A fixed point
p\in\operatorname{Fix}(f)
of a diffeomorphism is
hyperbolic

if the derivative of
f
at
p
,
Df(p):T_{p}(M)\to T_{p}(M)
is hyperbolic
as a linear automoprhism.

Let
\Lambda\subset M
be closed subset invariant under
f
.
The set
\Lambda
is
hyperbolic
(or has a
hyperbolic structure
)
if the tangent bundle of
M
restricted to
\Lambda
,
T_{\Lambda}(M)
has an invariant continuos
splitting
T_{\Lambda}(M)=E^{u}+E^{s}
such that
Df:E^{s}\to E^{s}
is contracting
and
Df:E^{u}\to E^{u}
expanding.
The sets
\Omega(f^{k})
for
k\in\mathbb{N}
are examples of hyperbolic sets.

We say that a diffeomorphism
f:M\to M
of a compact manifold
satisfies Axiom A

if: (a) the nonwandering set
\Omega(f)
is hyperbolic and
(b) the periodic points of
f
are dense in
\Omega(f)
.
If, additionally,
M=\Omega(f)
, then
f
is an
Anosov diffeomoprhism
.

Theorem 17

(
[
23
]
)
.

Let
f:M\to M
be a
C^{1}
diffeomorphism of a
C^{\infty}
compact manifold
M
without boundary.
If
f
satisfies Axiom A then the Artin-Mazur zeta function
is rational.

Observe that when
f,g
commute then

x\in\operatorname{Fix}\left((g^{-1}f)^{n}\right)\ \Leftrightarrow\ x\in S(f^{n},g^{n})\ \Leftrightarrow\ x\in\operatorname{Fix}\left((f^{-1}g)^{n}\right)

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}

and the corresponding Artin-Mazur and synchronization zeta functions are equal:

\zeta_{g^{-1}f}(z)\ =\ S_{f,g}(z)\ =\ \zeta_{f^{-1}g}(z).

Then Theorem
17
immediately implies the following

Theorem 18
.

Let
f,g:M\to M
be
C^{1}

commuting diffeomorphisms of a
C^{\infty}
compact manifold without boundary.
If
(g^{-1}f)
or
(f^{-1}g)
satisfies Axiom A, then the synchronization zeta function

S_{f,g}(z)
is rational.

Corollary 19
.

If
(g^{-1}f)
or
(f^{-1}g)
is Anosov diffeomorphism then the synchronization zeta function

S_{f,g}(z)
is rational.

3.5.
Rationality of synchronization zeta function for pseudo-Anosov homeomorphisms

A
measured foliation

(\mathcal{F},\mu)
on a closed compact manifold
M

is a foliation
\mathcal{F}
together with a transverse measure
\mu
invariant under holonomy.
Assume additionally that
M
is a surface, i.e.
\dim M=2
.
A homeomorphism
f:M\to M
is
pseudo-Anosov
if there are two
mutually transverse, invariant under
f
, measured singular foliations
(\mathcal{F}^{s},\mu^{s})

and
(\mathcal{F}^{u},\mu^{u})
, and a
\lambda>1
, such that

\displaystyle f(\mathcal{F}^{s},\mu^{s})

\displaystyle=(\mathcal{F}^{s},\frac{1}{\lambda}\mu^{s})

\displaystyle f(\mathcal{F}^{u},\mu^{u})

\displaystyle=(\mathcal{F}^{u},\lambda\mu^{u}).

We call
\mathcal{F}^{s},\mathcal{F}^{u}
the
stable
and
unstable
foliations, respectively.
Meaning of the equalities above is that
f
is contracting on the leaves of the stable foliation
and expanding on the leaves of the unstable foliation with the rate
\lambda^{-1}
and
\lambda
, respectively.

A subset
R\subset M
is an
(\mathcal{F}^{s},\mathcal{F}^{u})
-rectangle
, or
birectangle
,
if there exists an immersion
\iota:[0,1]\times[0,1]\to M
whose image is
R
and

(a)

f|_{(0,1)\times(0,1)}
is an embedding;

(b)

\forall t\in[0,1]
the image
\iota(\{t\}\times[0,1])
is included in a finite union
of leaves and singularities of
\mathcal{F}^{s}
;

(c)

\forall t\in[0,1]
the image
\iota([0,1]\times\{t\})
is included in a finite union
of leaves and singularities of
\mathcal{F}^{u}
.

A set of the form
\iota(\{t\}\times[0,1])
or
\iota([0,1]\times\{t\})
is an
\mathcal{F}^{s}
-fiber

or
\mathcal{F}^{u}
-fiber
, respectively.
A birectangle is
good
if
\iota
is an embedding.
By
\mathcal{F}(x,R)
we are going to denote the part of the
\mathcal{F}
-fiber
going through
x
and contained in the birectangle
R
.

A
Markov partition
for a pseudo-Anosov homeomorphism
f:M\to M
is a collection of birectangles

R=\{R_{1},\ldots,R_{k}\}
such that

(a)

\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}R_{i}\ =\ M
;

(b)

R_{i}
is a good rectangle;

(c)

\operatorname{Int}(R_{i})\cap\operatorname{Int}(R_{j})=\emptyset
for
i\neq j
;

(d)

if
x\in\operatorname{Int}(R_{i})
and
f(x)\in\operatorname{Int}(R_{j})
, then

f(\mathcal{F}^{s}(x,R_{i}))\subset\mathcal{F}^{s}(f(x),R_{j})\quad\text{and}\quad f^{-1}(\mathcal{F}^{u}(f(x),R_{j}))\subset\mathcal{F}^{u}(x,R_{i});

(e)

if
x\in\operatorname{Int}(R_{i})
and
f(x)\in\operatorname{Int}(R_{j})
, then

f(\mathcal{F}^{u}(x,R_{i}))\cap R_{j}=\mathcal{F}^{u}(f(x),R_{j})\quad\text{and}\quad f^{-1}(\mathcal{F}^{s}(f(x),R_{j}))\cap R_{i}=\mathcal{F}^{s}(x,R_{i});

Theorem 20

(
[
11
, Proposition 10.17]
)
.

A pseudo-Anosov diffeomorphism has a Markov partition.

Given a Markov partition
R=\{R_{1},\ldots,R_{k}\}
, we construct the subshift of finite type

\sigma_{A}:\Omega_{A}\to\Omega_{A}
letting
A
be the
k\times k
matrix defined by

\displaystyle a_{ij}=\begin{cases}1,&f(\operatorname{Int}R_{i})\cap\operatorname{Int}R_{j}\neq\emptyset\\
0,&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}.

If
(\ldots,b_{-1},b_{0},b_{1},\ldots)\in\Omega_{A}
then
\bigcap_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}f^{-i}(R_{b_{i}})

consists of a single point of
M
.
This gives us the projection map
p:\Omega_{A}\to M

satisfying
p\circ\sigma_{A}=f\circ p
.

Because birectangles
R_{i}
can meet on their boundaries, the map
p
is not injective in general.
Therefore, we cannot simply count fixed points of
f
by counting fixed points of
\sigma
.
To overcome this difficulties we can use Manning’s arguments from
[
23
]

(for details regarding pseudo-Anosov homeomorphisms see
[
12
, Lemma 27]
).
In this way we obtain finitely many subshifts of finite type
\sigma_{A_{i}},i=0,1,\ldots,m
,
where
A_{i}
is the transition matrix for
\sigma_{A_{i}}
,
such that

(14)

\#\operatorname{Fix}f^{n}\ =\ \sum_{i=0}^{m}\varepsilon_{i}\cdot\#\operatorname{Fix}(\sigma_{A_{i}}^{n})\ =\ \sum_{i=0}^{m}\varepsilon_{i}\cdot\operatorname{tr}A_{i}^{n},

where all
\varepsilon_{i}\in\{-1,1\}
.
The second equality is the result of Theorem
7
.
This leads us to the following

Theorem 21
.

Let
f:M\to M
be a pseudo-Anosov homeomorphism of a closed compact surface.
The Artin-Mazur zeta function is rational:

\zeta_{f}(z)\ =\ \prod_{i=0}^{m}\det(1-A_{i}z)^{-\varepsilon_{i}},

where
\epsilon_{i}\in\{-1,1\}
.

Proof.

We are going to use the following identities

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\operatorname{tr}A^{n}}{n}z^{n}=-\operatorname{tr}\big(\log(1-Az)\big),\quad\exp(\operatorname{tr}A)=\det(\exp A).

Direct computation using (
14
) provides us with the result:

\displaystyle\zeta_{f}(z)\

\displaystyle=\ \exp\left(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\#\operatorname{Fix}(f^{n})}{n}z^{n}\right)\ =\ \exp\left(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\sum_{i=0}^{m}\epsilon_{i}\cdot\operatorname{tr}A_{i}^{n}}{n}z^{n}\right)

\displaystyle=\ \exp\left(\sum_{i=0}^{m}\epsilon_{i}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\operatorname{tr}A_{i}^{n}}{n}z^{n}\right)\ =\ \prod_{i=0}^{m}\exp\left(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\operatorname{tr}A_{i}^{n}}{n}z^{n}\right)^{\epsilon_{i}}

\displaystyle=\ \prod_{i=0}^{m}\exp\Big(-\operatorname{tr}\big(\log(1-A_{i}z)\big)\Big)^{\epsilon_{i}}

\displaystyle=\ \prod_{i=0}^{m}\det\Big(\exp\big(\log(1-A_{i}z)\big)\Big)^{-\epsilon_{i}}\ =\ \prod_{i=0}^{m}\det(1-A_{i}z)^{-\varepsilon_{i}}.

∎

Similarly as for Axiom A diffeomorphisms, we can use this result to state

Theorem 22
.

Let
f,g:M\to M
be commuting homeomorphisms of a closed compact surface
M
.
If
(g^{-1}f)
or
(f^{-1}g)
is pseudo-Anosov then the synchronization zeta function
S_{f,g}(z)

is rational.

Proof.

Observe that when
f,g
commute then

x\in\operatorname{Fix}\left((g^{-1}f)^{n}\right)\ \Leftrightarrow\ x\in S(f^{n},g^{n})\ \Leftrightarrow\ x\in\operatorname{Fix}\left((f^{-1}g)^{n}\right)

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Therefore

\displaystyle S_{f,g}(z)\

\displaystyle=\ \exp\left(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\#S(f^{n},g^{n})}{n}z^{n}\right)\ =\ \exp\left(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\#\operatorname{Fix}\left((g^{-1}f)^{n}\right)}{n}z^{n}\right)

\displaystyle=\ \zeta_{g^{-1}f}(z)\ =\ \zeta_{f^{-1}g}(z).

Now if
(g^{-1}f)
or
(f^{-1}g)
is pseudo-Anosov, then Theorem
21

gives us rationality of
S_{f,g}(z)
.
∎

3.6.
Synchronization zeta function on finite sets

Theorem 23
.

Let X be a finite set and
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}:X\to X
two commuting bijections,
i.e. commuting permutations.
Then the synchronization zeta function
S_{\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}}(z)
is rational.

Proof.

For
\sigma_{1}^{n}(x)=\sigma_{2}^{n}(x)
iff
(\sigma_{2}^{-n}\circ\sigma_{1}^{n})(x)=x
, we have

\#S(\sigma_{1}^{n},\sigma_{2}^{n})=\#\operatorname{Fix}\left((\sigma_{2}^{-1}\sigma_{1})^{n}\right)
.
Let us denote the orbit of
x\in X
under
\sigma_{2}^{-1}\sigma_{1}
by
[x]
and its order by
\#[x]
.
Then

\#\operatorname{Fix}\left((\sigma_{2}^{-1}\sigma_{1})^{n}\right)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}[x]\\
\#[x]|n\end{subarray}}\#[x]

and

(15)

\displaystyle\begin{split}S_{\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}}(z)\ &=\ \exp\left(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\#S(\sigma_{1}^{n},\sigma_{2}^{n})}{n}z^{n}\right)\ =\ \exp\left(\sum_{[x]}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n=1\\
\#[x]|n\end{subarray}}^{\infty}\frac{\#[x]}{n}z^{n}\right)\\
&=\ \prod_{[x]}\exp\left(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\#[x]}{\#[x]n}z^{n}\right)\ =\ \prod_{[x]}\exp\left(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n}z^{\#[x]n}\right)\\
&=\ \prod_{[x]}\exp\left(-\log\left(1-z^{\#[x]}\right)\right)\ =\ \prod_{[x]}\frac{1}{1-z^{\#[x]}}.\end{split}

As
X
is finite, the product is finite as well, and
S_{\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}}(z)
is rational.
∎

Remark 24
.

Without the commutativity assumption the theorem is not true in general.
Let
X=\{0,1,2\}
and

\sigma_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&2\\
0&2&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad\sigma_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&2\\
1&0&2\end{pmatrix}.

We have
\sigma_{1}\left(\sigma_{2}(0)\right)=2\neq 1=\sigma_{2}\left(\sigma_{1}(0)\right)
.
Observe that

\displaystyle\#S(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2})=0,\quad\#S(\sigma_{1}^{2},\sigma_{2}^{2})=\#S(\operatorname{Id},\operatorname{Id})=\#X=3.

Therefore the synchronization zeta function

\displaystyle S_{\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}}(z)\

\displaystyle=\ \exp\left(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{3}{2}\cdot\frac{(z^{2})^{n}}{n}\right)\ =\ \exp\left(-\frac{3}{2}\log(1-z^{2})\right)

\displaystyle=\ \frac{1}{\sqrt{(1-z^{2})^{3}}}

is
algebraic
, not rational.

Theorem 25
.

Let
X
be a finite set and
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}:X\to X
two commuting bijections, i.e. commuting permutations.
The synchronization zeta function has the following functional equation

S_{\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}}\left(\frac{1}{z}\right)\ =\ (-1)^{p}z^{q}S_{\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}}(z)

where
q
is the number of periodic elements of
\sigma_{2}^{-1}\sigma_{1}

and
p
is the number of periodic orbits of
\sigma_{2}^{-1}\sigma_{1}
.

Proof.

Using (
15
) we write

\displaystyle S_{\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}}\left(\frac{1}{z}\right)\

\displaystyle=\ \prod_{[x]}\frac{1}{1-z^{-\#[x]}}\ =\ \prod_{[x]}\frac{z^{\#[x]}}{z^{\#[x]}-1}

\displaystyle=\ \prod_{[x]}\frac{-z^{\#[x]}}{1-z^{\#[x]}}\ =\ \prod_{[x]}-z^{\#[x]}\cdot\prod_{[x]}\frac{1}{1-z^{\#[x]}}

\displaystyle=\ \prod_{[x]}-z^{\#[x]}\cdot S_{\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}}(z).

The statement follows because
\sum_{[x]}\#[x]=q
.
∎

Theorem 26
.

Let
\sigma_{i},\sigma_{j}:X\to X
be two bijections, i.e. permutations of a finite set.
Then the following hold

(i)

The sequence of synchronization numbers
\{\#S(\sigma_{i}^{n},\sigma_{j}^{n})\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
is periodic.

(ii)

If
L
is the length of the period of the sequence of synchronization numbers
then for
k=1,\ldots,\lfloor L/2\rfloor

\#S(\sigma_{i}^{k},\sigma_{j}^{k})=\#S(\sigma_{i}^{L-k},\sigma_{j}^{L-k})

Proof.

(i) Let
k_{i},k_{j}
be ranks of
\sigma_{i},\sigma_{j}
in the group of permutations of
X
, i.e.

\sigma_{i}^{k_{i}}=\operatorname{Id}=\sigma_{j}^{k_{j}}.

Denote
L:=\operatorname{LCM}(k_{i},k_{j})
. This is the least natural number for which both
\sigma_{i}^{L}=\sigma_{j}^{L}=\operatorname{Id}
.
It is clear that
\#S(\sigma_{i}^{n},\sigma_{j}^{n})=\#S(\sigma_{i}^{n+L},\sigma_{j}^{n+L})
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

(ii) Observe that if
\sigma_{i}(x)=\sigma_{j}(x)
then

\sigma_{j}^{-1}\left(\sigma_{i}(x)\right)=\sigma_{j}^{-1}\left(\sigma_{j}(x)\right)=x=\sigma_{i}^{-1}\left(\sigma_{i}(x)\right)

therefore synchronization points of
\sigma_{i},\sigma_{j}
are in bijection with synchronization points
of
\sigma_{i}^{-1},\sigma_{j}^{-1}
and
\#S(\sigma_{i}^{n},\sigma_{j}^{n})=\#S(\sigma_{i}^{-n},\sigma_{j}^{-n})
.
The result follows from periodicity.
∎

Theorem 27
.

Let
\sigma_{i},\sigma_{j}:X\to X
be two bijections, i.e. permutations, of a finite set.
Then the logarithmic derivative of the synchronization zeta function
S_{\sigma_{i},\sigma_{j}}(z)

is rational and is equal

(16)

\displaystyle\left[\ln S_{\sigma_{i},\sigma_{j}}(z)\right]^{\prime}=\frac{S_{\sigma_{i},\sigma_{j}}^{\prime}(z)}{S_{\sigma_{i},\sigma_{j}}(z)}=\frac{\sum_{n=1}^{L}\#S(\sigma_{i}^{n},\sigma_{j}^{n})z^{n-1}}{1-z^{L}}.

Proof.

Direct computation gives us

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{S_{\sigma_{i},\sigma_{j}}^{\prime}(z)}{S_{\sigma_{i},\sigma_{j}}(z)}&=\frac{\exp\left(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\#S(\sigma_{i}^{n},\sigma_{j}^{n})}{n}z^{n}\right)\cdot\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\#S(\sigma_{i}^{n},\sigma_{j}^{n})z^{n-1}}{\exp\left(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\#S(\sigma_{i}^{n},\sigma_{j}^{n})}{n}z^{n}\right)}\\
&=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\#S(\sigma_{i}^{n},\sigma_{j}^{n})z^{n-1}.\end{split}

Since for
n=L+1,L+2,\ldots
we have
\#S(\sigma_{i}^{n},\sigma_{j}^{n})-\#S(\sigma_{i}^{n-L},\sigma_{j}^{n-L})=0
,
another direct check gives us

\displaystyle\begin{split}(1-z^{L})\cdot\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\#S(\sigma_{i}^{n},\sigma_{j}^{n})z^{n-1}&=\sum_{n=1}^{L}\#S(\sigma_{i}^{n},\sigma_{j}^{n})z^{n-1}+\sum_{n=L+1}^{\infty}\left(\#S(\sigma_{i}^{n},\sigma_{j}^{n})-\#S(\sigma_{i}^{n-L},\sigma_{j}^{n-L})\right)z^{n-1}\\
&=\sum_{n=1}^{L}\#S(\sigma_{i}^{n},\sigma_{j}^{n})z^{n-1}\end{split}

what, after dividing by
(1-z^{L})
, gives us (
16
).
∎

Theorem 28
.

Let
X
be a finite set and
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}:X\to X
two bijections, i.e. permutations.
Let
k_{1},k_{2}\in\mathbb{N}
be such that
\sigma_{1}^{k_{1}}=\operatorname{Id}=\sigma_{2}^{k_{2}}

and denote
L:=\operatorname{LCM}(k_{1},k_{2})
.
Let
\omega:=\exp(2\pi\iota/L)
be the
L
-th root of
1
and
\Re(\omega)
its real part.

Then the synchronization zeta function is of the form

(17)

\displaystyle S_{\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}}(z)=\begin{cases}(1-z)^{A_{0}}\cdot\prod_{k=1}^{\lfloor L/2\rfloor}\left(z^{2}-2\Re(\omega^{k})z+1\right)^{A_{k}},&2\!\not|\,L\\[4.30554pt]
(1-z)^{A_{0}}(1+z)^{A_{L/2}}\cdot\prod_{k=1}^{L/2-1}\left(z^{2}-2\Re(\omega^{k})z+1\right)^{A_{k}},&2\,|\,L\end{cases},

where all
A_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

When we consider the logarithmic derivative of the synchronization zeta function formally, we can write

\displaystyle\left[\ln S_{\sigma_{i},\sigma_{j}}(z)\right]^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\frac{S_{\sigma_{i},\sigma_{j}}^{\prime}(z)}{S_{\sigma_{i},\sigma_{j}}(z)}

\displaystyle\ln S_{\sigma_{i},\sigma_{j}}(z)

\displaystyle=\int\frac{S_{\sigma_{i},\sigma_{j}}^{\prime}(z)}{S_{\sigma_{i},\sigma_{j}}(z)}dz

\displaystyle S_{\sigma_{i},\sigma_{j}}(z)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\int\frac{S_{\sigma_{i},\sigma_{j}}^{\prime}(z)}{S_{\sigma_{i},\sigma_{j}}(z)}dz\right)

We will integrate the logarithmic derivative using the form of Theorem
27
.
For simplicity, denote the numerator
\sum_{n=1}^{L}\#S(\sigma_{i}^{n},\sigma_{j}^{n})z^{n-1}=:P(z)\in\mathbb{Z}[z]
.
The denominator has
L
different roots which are
L
-th roots of
1
, i.e.
\omega^{k},k=0,1,\ldots,L-1

therefore we factorize

\displaystyle z^{L}-1=(z-1)(z-\omega)\cdots(z-\omega^{L-1}).

Performing partial fraction decomposition we get

\displaystyle\frac{-P(z)}{z^{L}-1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{L-1}\frac{C_{k}}{z-\omega^{k}}

and, after reducing the right side to a common denominator and denoting by
\widehat{(z-\omega^{k})}

the missing term in the product,

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{L-1}\frac{C_{k}}{z-\omega^{k}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{k=0}^{L-1}C_{k}\cdot(z-\omega^{0})\cdots\widehat{(z-\omega^{k})}\cdots(z-\omega^{L-1})}{z^{L}-1}.

Observe that the derivative

\displaystyle(z^{L}-1)^{\prime}=\left[\prod_{k=0}^{L-1}(z-\omega^{k})\right]^{\prime}=\sum_{k=0}^{L-1}(z-\omega^{0})\cdots\widehat{(z-\omega^{k})}\cdots(z-\omega^{L-1})

therefore, comparing numerators and substituting the roots one by one, for
k=0,\ldots,L-1
we have

\displaystyle-P(\omega^{k})

\displaystyle=C_{k}\cdot(z^{L}-1)^{\prime}(\omega^{k})=C_{k}\cdot L\omega^{k(L-1)}

\displaystyle C_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{-P(\omega^{k})}{L\omega^{k(L-1)}}

We are going to show that all
C_{k}
are in fact real.
If
L
is even, then
\omega^{L/2}=\exp((2\pi\iota/L)\cdot(L/2))=-1

is a single real root which we will consider separately.
Regardless of parity of
L
,
1
always is a single real root.
We therefore have

\displaystyle C_{0}=\frac{-P(1)}{L}\in\mathbb{Q},\quad C_{L/2}=\frac{-P(-1)}{\pm L}\in\mathbb{Q}\text{ (for $L$ even)}.

Now consider
L
odd and
k
such that
\omega^{k}\notin\mathbb{R}
. The imaginary part

\displaystyle\operatorname{Im}C_{k}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Im}\left(-\frac{1}{L}\sum_{n=1}^{L}\#S(\sigma_{i}^{n},\sigma_{j}^{n})\omega^{k(n-1)+k(1-L)}\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{L}\sum_{n=1}^{L}\#S(\sigma_{i}^{n},\sigma_{j}^{n})\operatorname{Im}\omega^{kn-kL}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{L}\sum_{n=1}^{L}\#S(\sigma_{i}^{n},\sigma_{j}^{n})\operatorname{Im}\frac{\omega^{kn}}{\left(\omega^{L}\right)^{k}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{L}\sum_{n=1}^{L}\#S(\sigma_{i}^{n},\sigma_{j}^{n})\operatorname{Im}\left(\omega^{k}\right)^{n}

Note that
(\omega^{k})^{L}=1
and for
n=1,\ldots,\lfloor L/2\rfloor
the terms appears in complex
conjugate pairs
\operatorname{Im}(\omega^{k})^{n}=-\operatorname{Im}(\omega^{k})^{L-n}
so that we can rewrite the last sum

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{L}\sum_{n=1}^{\lfloor L/2\rfloor}\left[\#S(\sigma_{i}^{n},\sigma_{j}^{n})\operatorname{Im}\left(\omega^{k}\right)^{n}-\#S(\sigma_{i}^{L-n},\sigma_{j}^{L-n})\operatorname{Im}\left(\omega^{k}\right)^{n}\right]

Using Theorem
26
(ii) we get

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{L}\sum_{n=1}^{\lfloor L/2\rfloor}\#S(\sigma_{i}^{n},\sigma_{j}^{n})\left[\operatorname{Im}\left(\omega^{k}\right)^{n}-\operatorname{Im}\left(\omega^{k}\right)^{n}\right]

\displaystyle=0

therefore
C_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
is in fact real.

Because
z^{L}-1
is a polynomial with integer coefficients, all its complex roots appear in pairs
with their complex conjugates
\overline{\omega^{k}}=\omega^{-k}=\omega^{L-k}
.
Grouping conjugate complex roots we get

\displaystyle\frac{-P(z)}{z^{L}-1}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\frac{C_{0}}{z-1}+\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor L/2\rfloor}\left(\frac{C_{k}}{z-\omega^{k}}+\frac{\overline{C_{k}}}{z-\overline{\omega^{k}}}\right),&2\!\not|\,L\\[8.61108pt]
\frac{C_{0}}{z-1}+\frac{C_{L/2}}{z+1}+\sum_{k=1}^{L/2-1}\left(\frac{C_{k}}{z-\omega^{k}}+\frac{\overline{C_{k}}}{z-\overline{\omega^{k}}}\right),&2\,|\,L\end{cases}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\frac{C_{0}}{z-1}+\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor L/2\rfloor}\frac{2C_{k}z-2\Re(C_{k}\overline{\omega^{k}})}{z^{2}-2\Re(\omega^{k})z+1},&2\!\not|\,L\\[8.61108pt]
\frac{C_{0}}{z-1}+\frac{C_{L/2}}{z+1}+\sum_{k=1}^{L/2-1}\frac{2C_{k}z-2\Re(C_{k}\overline{\omega^{k}})}{z^{2}-2\Re(\omega^{k})z+1},&2\,|\,L\end{cases}

Let us take a closer look at the terms under the summation signs.
Since
C_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
we have
\Re(C_{k}\overline{\omega^{k}})/C_{k}=\Re(\omega^{k})

so

\displaystyle\frac{2C_{k}z-2\Re(C_{k}\overline{\omega^{k}})}{z^{2}-2\Re(\omega^{k})z+1}

\displaystyle=C_{k}\cdot\frac{2z-2\Re(\omega^{k})}{z^{2}-2\Re(\omega^{k})z+1}

\displaystyle C_{k}\int\frac{\left(z^{2}-2\Re(\omega^{k})z+1\right)^{\prime}}{z^{2}-2\Re(\omega^{k})z+1}dz

\displaystyle=C_{k}\cdot\ln\left(z^{2}-2\Re(\omega^{k})z+1\right)

Integrating term by term we get

\displaystyle\int\frac{S_{\sigma_{i},\sigma_{j}}^{\prime}(z)}{S_{\sigma_{i},\sigma_{j}}(z)}

\displaystyle=\int\frac{-P(z)}{z^{L}-1}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}C_{0}\ln(z-1)+\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor L/2\rfloor}C_{k}\ln\left(z^{2}-2\Re(\omega^{k})z+1\right),&2\!\not|\,L\\[8.61108pt]
C_{0}\ln(z-1)+C_{L/2}\ln(z+1)+\sum_{k=1}^{L/2-1}C_{k}\ln\left(z^{2}-2\Re(\omega^{k})z+1\right),&2\,|\,L\end{cases}

Taking exponent and setting
A_{k}:=C_{k}
provides us with the result.
∎

Corollary 29
.

If all
A_{k}
in (
17
) are integers then the synchronization zeta function
is rational, which is the case for example for
commuting
permutations
as proved in
Theorem
23
.
If all
A_{k}
are rational then the synchronization zeta function is algebraic,
which was illustrated in
Remark
24
.

In the most general situation
A_{k}
is real. Even if the synchronization zeta function is not rational or algebraic,
the equation (
17
) uses polynomials and real exponents and this
assures that the set of singularities of the synchronization zeta function does not have accumulation points,
hence, the synchronization zeta function itself does not have the natural boundary.

More generally, for maps of a finite sets that not necessarily are bijections,
we have similar yet more complicated results.

Lemma 30
.

Let
X
be a finite set and
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}:X\to X
two arbitrary maps. Then

(i)

The sequence of synchronization numbers of
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}
is periodic
from a certain place, i.e. there exist
n,L\in\mathbb{N}
such that

(18)

\displaystyle\#S(\sigma_{1}^{n+k+L},\sigma_{2}^{n+k+L})=\#S(\sigma_{1}^{n+k},\sigma_{2}^{n+k}),\quad\text{ for all }k=0,1,2,\ldots;

(ii)

We have the equality

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\#S(\sigma_{1}^{k},\sigma_{2}^{k})z^{k-1}=\frac{P(z)}{1-z^{L}},

where
P(z)\in\mathbb{Z}[Z]
is a polynomial of
\deg P=n+L-2
with integer coefficients.

Proof.

(i) Consider iterations
\sigma_{1}^{k}
for
k=1,2,\ldots
and let
x\in X
.
For
X
is finite, there is the first iteration
k_{x}
such that
\sigma_{1}^{k_{x}}(x)
is the same element
as
\sigma_{1}^{k_{x}-l_{x}}(x)
for some
l_{x}\in\mathbb{N}
. Denote
k_{x}-l_{x}=:n_{x}
.
If we denote
n_{1}:=\max_{x}n_{x},L_{1}:=\operatorname{LCM}\{l_{x}\;|\;x\in X\}
then

\displaystyle\sigma_{1}^{n_{1}+k}=\sigma_{1}^{n_{1}+k+L_{1}},\quad\text{ for all }k\in\mathbb{N}.

In the same way we obtain
n_{2},L_{2}
for
\sigma_{2}
.
We have (
18
) for
n:=\max(n_{1},n_{2}),L:=\operatorname{LCM}(L_{1},L_{2})
.

(ii) Direct computation gives us

\displaystyle(1-z^{L})\cdot\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\#S(\sigma_{1}^{k},\sigma_{2}^{k})z^{k-1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{L}\#S(\sigma_{1}^{k},\sigma_{2}^{k})z^{k-1}+\sum_{k=L+1}^{\infty}\left(\#S(\sigma_{1}^{k},\sigma_{2}^{k})-\#S(\sigma_{1}^{k-L},\sigma_{2}^{k-L})\right)z^{k-1}.

Observe that the second sum is in fact finite. Indeed, if
n
is as in (i)
then we have (
18
)
and from
k=n+L
all differences equal zero.
If we denote the right side by
P(z)
and divide both sides by
(1-z^{L})
then we get the result.
∎

Theorem 31
.

Let
X
be a finite set and
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}:X\to X
two arbitrary maps.

Denote by
L
the length of the period of the sequence of synchronization numbers and denote

\displaystyle\omega:=\exp(2\pi\iota/L).

Let
P(z)\in\mathbb{Z}[z]
be the polynomial with integer coefficients such that

\displaystyle\left(1-z^{L}\right)\cdot\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\#S(\sigma_{1}^{n},\sigma_{2}^{n})z^{n-1}=P(z)

as in
Lemma
30
(ii)
.
Let
Q(z),R(z)\in\mathbb{Z}[z]
be the polynomials fulfilling

\displaystyle P(z)=Q(z)\cdot(1-z^{L})+R(z),\quad\deg R<L.

Then the synchronization zeta function is of the form

\displaystyle S_{\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}}(z)=\exp\left(\int Q(z)dz\right)\cdot(1-z)^{A_{0}}\cdot T(z)

where

\displaystyle T(z)=\begin{cases}\prod_{k=1}^{\lfloor L/2\rfloor}\left(z^{2}-2\Re(\omega^{k})z+1\right)^{A_{k}}\cdot\left(\frac{-\Re(\omega^{k})+\iota\sqrt{1-\Re(\omega^{k})^{2}}\ +\ z}{\Re(\omega^{k})+\iota\sqrt{1-\Re(\omega^{k})^{2}}\ -\ z}\right)^{B_{k}\iota},&2\!\not|\,L\\[4.30554pt]
(1+z)^{A_{L/2}}\cdot\prod_{k=1}^{L/2-1}\left(z^{2}-2\Re(\omega^{k})z+1\right)^{A_{k}}\cdot\left(\frac{-\Re(\omega^{k})+\iota\sqrt{1-\Re(\omega^{k})^{2}}\ +\ z}{\Re(\omega^{k})+\iota\sqrt{1-\Re(\omega^{k})^{2}}\ -\ z}\right)^{B_{k}\iota},&2\,|\,L\end{cases}

where
A_{i},B_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
and
\iota^{2}=-1
.

Proof.

We are going to follow the same path as in Theorem
28
.
Two important differences are that we do not have the property of Theorem
26
(ii)
and
\deg P
can be larger than the length
L
of the period in the sequence of synchronization numbers.

Integrating term by term we get

\displaystyle\begin{split}S_{\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}}(z)&=\exp\left(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\#S(\sigma_{1}^{n},\sigma_{2}^{n})}{n}z^{n}\right)\\
&=\exp\left(\int\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\#S(\sigma_{1}^{n},\sigma_{2}^{n})z^{n-1}dz\right)\\
&=\exp\left(\int\frac{P(z)}{1-z^{L}}dz\right)\\
&=\exp\left(\int Q(z)dz\right)\cdot\exp\left(\int\frac{R(z)}{1-z^{L}}dz\right).\end{split}

Because
\deg R<L
we can perform partial fraction decomposition as in the proof
of Theorem
28
:

\displaystyle\frac{-R(z)}{z^{L}-1}=\sum_{k=0}^{L-1}\frac{C_{k}}{z-\omega^{k}},\quad\text{ where }C_{k}=\frac{-R(\omega^{k})}{L\omega^{k(L-1)}}\in\mathbb{C}.

In the sum, roots of unity appear in conjugate pairs, excluding
-1
for
L
even and
1
regardless of parity of
L
.
Grouping the complex conjugate roots we get (for
L
odd)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\sum_{k=1}^{L-1}\frac{C_{k}}{z-\omega^{k}}&=\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor L/2\rfloor}\frac{C_{k}(z-\overline{\omega^{k}})+\overline{C_{k}}(z-\omega^{k})}{z^{2}-2\Re(\omega^{k})z+1}\\
&=\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor L/2\rfloor}\frac{2\Re(C_{k})z-2\Re(C_{k}\overline{\omega^{k}})}{z^{2}-2\Re(\omega^{k})z+1}\\
&=\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor L/2\rfloor}\Re(C_{k})\frac{2z-2\Re(C_{k}\overline{\omega^{k}})/\Re(C_{k})}{z^{2}-2\Re(\omega^{k})z+1}.\end{split}

For fixed
k
we can write

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{2z-2\Re(C_{k}\overline{\omega^{k}})/\Re(C_{k})}{z^{2}-2\Re(\omega^{k})z+1}&=\frac{2z-2\Re(\omega^{k})+2\Re(\omega^{k})-2\Re(C_{k}\overline{\omega^{k}})/\Re(C_{k})}{z^{2}-2\Re(\omega^{k})z+1}\\
&=\frac{2z-2\Re(\omega^{k})}{z^{2}-2\Re(\omega^{k})z+1}+\left(2\Re(\omega^{k})-2\Re(C_{k}\overline{\omega^{k}})/\Re(C_{k})\right)\frac{1}{z^{2}-2\Re(\omega^{k})z+1}.\end{split}

In the first term the numerator is the derivative of the denumerator, therefore after integration
it will give the logarithm

\displaystyle\int\frac{2z-2\Re(\omega^{k})}{z^{2}-2\Re(\omega^{k})z+1}dz=\ln\left(z^{2}-2\Re(\omega^{k})z+1\right)

which will be canceled out after exponentiation.
The second term, if
\operatorname{Im}C_{k}\neq 0
, after integration gives rise to

\displaystyle\int\frac{1}{z^{2}-2\Re(\omega^{k})z+1}dz=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\Re(\omega^{k})^{2}}}\arctan\left(\frac{z-\Re(\omega^{k})}{\sqrt{1-\Re(\omega^{k})^{2}}}\right)

which, after using the known identity

\displaystyle\arctan(z)=\frac{\iota}{2}\ln\left(\frac{\iota+z}{\iota-z}\right),

gives us

\displaystyle\int\frac{1}{z^{2}-2\Re(\omega^{k})z+1}dz=\frac{\iota}{2\sqrt{1-\Re(\omega^{k})^{2}}}\ln\left(\frac{-\Re(\omega^{k})+\iota\sqrt{1-\Re(\omega^{k})^{2}}+z}{\Re(\omega^{k})+\iota\sqrt{1-\Re(\omega^{k})^{2}}-z}\right).

Hence, for a fixed
k
, we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}\exp\left(\int\frac{C_{k}}{z-\omega^{k}}dz\right)=\exp&\left[\Re(C_{k})\ln\left(z^{2}-2\Re(\omega^{k})z+1\right)\right]\\
&\cdot\exp\left[\frac{\Re(C_{k})\Re(\omega^{k})-\Re(C_{k}\overline{\omega^{k}})}{\sqrt{1-\Re(\omega^{k})^{2}}}\cdot\iota\cdot\ln\left(\frac{-\Re(\omega^{k})+\iota\sqrt{1-\Re(\omega^{k})^{2}}+z}{\Re(\omega^{k})+\iota\sqrt{1-\Re(\omega^{k})^{2}}-z}\right)\right].\end{split}

Summing over
k
and setting

\displaystyle A_{k}:=\Re(C_{k}),\quad B_{k}:=\frac{\Re(C_{k})\Re(\omega^{k})-\Re(C_{k}\overline{\omega^{k}})}{\sqrt{1-\Re(\omega^{k})^{2}}}

finishes the proof.
∎

Corollary 32
.

In analogy to the
Corollary
29
we observe
that the synchronization zeta function of two arbitrary maps of a finite set does not have a natural boundary.
That is because all terms can be written as real powers of polynomials and complex powers of rational functions,
hence, the set of singularities does not have accumulation points.

Observe that if all
B_{k}=0
, i.e. all
\mathbb{C}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
, then this general form collapses
to the form of
Theorem
28

with an additional exponent of an integral of a polynomial term.

4.
Gauss congruences for numbers of synchronization points

Let
\mu(n)
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, be the
Möbius function
, i.e.

\displaystyle\mu(n)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1,&\mbox{ if }n=1,\\
(-1)^{k},&\mbox{ if }n\mbox{ is a product of }k\mbox{ distinct primes,}\\
0,&\mbox{ if }n\mbox{ is not square-free.}\end{array}\right.

In number theory, the following Gauss congruence for integers holds:

\displaystyle\sum_{d\mid n}\mu(n/d)\cdot a^{d}\equiv 0\mod n

for any integer
a
and any natural number
n
. In the case of a prime power
n=p^{r}
, the Gauss congruence turns into the Euler congruence. Indeed, for
n=p^{r}
the Möbius function
\mu(n/d)=\mu(p^{r}/d)
is nonzero only in two cases: when
d=p^{r}
and when
d=p^{r-1}
. Therefore, from the Gauss congruence we obtain the Euler congruence

\displaystyle a^{p^{r}}\equiv a^{p^{r-1}}\mod p^{r}.

When
(a,n)=1,n=p^{r}
, these congruences are equivalent to the following classical Euler’s theorem:

\displaystyle a^{\varphi(n)}\equiv 1\mod n.

These congruences have been generalized from integers to some other mathematical invariants such as the traces of powers of all integer matrices
A
and the Lefschetz numbers of iterations of a map see
[
24
,
28
]
:

(19)

\displaystyle\sum_{d\mid n}\mu(n/d)\cdot\operatorname{tr}(A^{d})\equiv 0\mod n,

(20)

\displaystyle\operatorname{tr}(A^{p^{r}})\equiv\operatorname{tr}(A^{p^{r-1}})\mod p^{r}.

A. Dold in
[
9
]
(see also
[
21
, Theorem 3.1.4]
) proved by a geometric argument
the following congruences (
DL
) for the Lefschetz numbers of iterations of a map
f

on a compact ANR
X
and any natural number
n

(DL)

\displaystyle\sum_{d\mid n}\mu(n/d)\cdot L(f^{d})\equiv 0\mod n.

These congruences are now called the Dold congruences.
It is also shown in
[
24
]
(see also
[
28
, Theorem 9]
) that the above congruences (
19
),
(
20
) and (
DL
) are equivalent.

To prove Gauss congruences in case of rationality of the synchronization zeta function
we follow
[
2
, Theorem 2.1]
and
[
21
, Theorem 3.1.23, Proposition 3.1.12]
.

Lemma 33
.

S_{\varphi,\psi}(z)
is rational iff for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
we have

(21)

\#S(\varphi^{k},\psi^{k})=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\chi_{i}\lambda_{i}^{k}

where
r\in\mathbb{N},\ \chi_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
and
\lambda_{i}\in\mathbb{C}
are distinct algebraic integers.

Proof.

\Leftarrow
)
Let us split all the
\chi_{i}
into two sets according to their signs.
We have
r^{+}
positive
\chi_{i}^{+}=\chi_{i}
and
r^{-}
positive
\chi_{i}^{-}=-\chi_{i}
where
r^{+}+r^{-}=r
.
Denote
\lambda_{i}
as
\lambda_{i}^{+}
or
\lambda_{i}^{-}
with respect to the sign of corresponding
\chi_{i}
.
With this notation we have

\sum_{i=1}^{r}\chi_{i}\lambda_{i}^{k}\ =\ \sum_{i=1}^{r^{+}}\chi_{i}^{+}{\lambda_{i}^{+}}^{k}\ -\ \sum_{i=1}^{r^{-}}\chi_{i}^{-}{\lambda_{i}^{-}}^{k}.

Using this equality in (
21
), properties of
\exp

and the identity
\ln(1/(1-t))=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}t^{n}/n
we get

S_{\varphi,\psi}(z)=\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{r^{+}}\left(\exp\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{(\lambda_{i}^{+}z)^{k}}{k}\right)\right)^{\chi_{i}^{+}}}{\prod_{i=1}^{r^{-}}\left(\exp\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{(\lambda_{i}^{-}z)^{k}}{k}\right)\right)^{\chi_{i}^{-}}}=\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{r^{-}}\left(1-\lambda_{i}^{-}z\right)^{\chi_{i}^{-}}}{\prod_{i=1}^{r^{+}}\left(1-\lambda_{i}^{+}z\right)^{\chi_{i}^{+}}}

and the zeta function is rational.

\Rightarrow
)
Assume
S_{\varphi,\psi}(z)
is rational.
By definition
S_{\varphi,\psi}(0)=1\neq 0
therefore we can write

S_{\varphi,\psi}(z)=\frac{\prod_{i}(1-\beta_{i}z)}{\prod_{j}(1-\gamma_{j}z)}

where
\beta_{i},\gamma_{j}
are non-zero roots of polynomials and the products are finite.
Combine the roots into one sequence
\{\lambda_{i}\}
where for
i\neq j
there is
\lambda_{i}\neq\lambda_{j}
.
Taking multiplication into account we can write

(22)

S_{\varphi,\psi}(z)=\prod_{i}\left(1-\lambda_{i}z\right)^{\chi_{i}},\quad\chi_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}.

Let us consider the logarithmic derivative

(23)

S(z):=\frac{d}{dz}\ln S_{\varphi,\psi}(z)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\#S(\varphi^{k},\psi^{k})\cdot z^{k-1}.

From (
22
) we have

(24)

\displaystyle\begin{split}S(z)&=\frac{d}{dz}\left(\sum_{i}\chi_{i}\ln(1-\lambda_{i}z)\right)\ =\ \sum_{i}\chi_{i}\frac{-\lambda_{i}}{1-\lambda_{i}z}\\
&=\sum_{i}(-\chi_{i}\lambda_{i})\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\lambda_{i}^{k}z^{k}\ =\ \sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{i}-\chi_{i}\lambda_{i}^{k}\right)z^{k-1}.\end{split}

Comparing coefficients at
z^{k-1}
we get
\#S(\varphi^{k},\psi^{k})=\sum_{i}\chi_{i}\lambda_{i}^{k}

up to the sign of
\chi_{i}
,
the sum is finite,
\chi_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
and
\lambda_{i}
are algebraic numbers.

We are going to prove that
\lambda_{i}
are actually algebraic integers.
From (
23
) we know that
S(z)
is given by a power series with integer coefficients
and from (
24
) that it is a rational function.
Therefore
S(z)=u(z)/v(z)
, where
u,v\in\mathbb{Q}[z]
.
Fatou lemma (see
[
21
, Lemma 3.1.31]
) says that
u(z),v(z)
in this setting have the forms

u(z)=\sum_{i=0}^{s}a_{i}z^{i},\quad v(z)=1+\sum_{j=1}^{q}b_{j}z^{j}

where the coefficients
a_{i},b_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}
.
Comparing this with (
24
) we get

\frac{\sum_{i=0}^{s}a_{i}z^{i}}{1+\sum_{j=1}^{q}b_{j}z^{j}}\ =\ S(z)\ =\ \sum_{i}\frac{-\chi_{i}\lambda_{i}}{1-\lambda_{i}z}

and it is easy to check that all
\lambda_{i}
are roots of the polynomial

\tilde{v}(z)=z^{q}+\sum_{j=1}^{q}b_{j}z^{q-j}
.
Hence they are algebraic integers.
∎

Lemma 34
.

There exists a map
f:X\to X
of a compact euclidean neighborhood retract such that

L(f^{k})=\#S(\varphi^{k},\psi^{k})
for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
iff
the equality (
21
) holds for every
k\in\mathbb{N}

Proof.

\Rightarrow
)
Denote by
H_{i}(f):H_{i}(X;\mathbb{Q})\to H_{i}(X;\mathbb{Q})
the map induced on
i
-th homology space and put

A_{e}:=\bigoplus_{i-even}H_{i}(f),\quad A_{o}:=\bigoplus_{i-odd}H_{i}(f).

With this notation we can write

\#S(\varphi^{k},\psi^{k})=L(f^{k})=\operatorname{tr}A_{e}^{k}-\operatorname{tr}A_{o}^{k}.

Let
\lambda_{i}^{+},\lambda_{j}^{-}
be all distinct eigenvalues of
A_{e},A_{o}
respectively,
each of multiplicity
\chi_{i}^{+},\chi_{j}^{-}
respectively.
Then

\#S(\varphi^{k},\psi^{k})\ =\ \sum_{i}\chi_{i}^{+}{\lambda_{i}^{+}}^{k}\ -\ \sum_{j}\chi_{j}^{-}{\lambda_{j}^{-}}^{k},

where
\chi_{i}^{+},\chi_{j}^{-}\in\mathbb{Z}
and all
\lambda_{i}^{+},\lambda_{j}^{-}
are distinct algebraic integers
(as roots of characteristic polynomials of integer matrices).

\Leftarrow
)
Assume that for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
the equality

\#S(\varphi^{k},\psi^{k})=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\chi_{i}\lambda_{i}^{k}

where
r\in\mathbb{N},\ \chi_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
and
\lambda_{i}\in\mathbb{C}
are distinct algebraic integers, holds.
It turns out that if
\lambda_{i},\lambda_{j}
are algebraically conjugate then
\chi_{i}=\chi_{j}
.
Indeed, denote by
v(z)
an irreducible polynomial roots of which are
\lambda_{i},\lambda_{j}
.
Denote by
\Sigma
the field of
v(z)
and let
\sigma
be an element of the Galois group of
\Sigma
.
By definition
\sigma
is the identity function on
\mathbb{Q}
.
It is well-known that
\sigma
acts as a permutation of the set of roots of
v(z)
,
denote the induced permutation of indices by
\sigma
as well,
i.e.
\sigma(\lambda_{i})=\lambda_{\sigma(i)}
.
We get

\sigma\left(\#S(\varphi^{k},\psi^{k})\right)=\sigma\left(\sum_{i=1}^{r}\chi_{i}\lambda_{i}^{k}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\chi_{i}\sigma(\lambda_{i})^{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\chi_{i}\lambda_{\sigma(i)}^{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\chi_{\sigma^{-1}(i)}\lambda_{i}^{k}.

On the other hand a number of synchronization points is an integer
therefore
\sigma\left(\#S(\varphi^{k},\psi^{k})\right)=\#S(\varphi^{k},\psi^{k})

and for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
we get

\sum_{i=1}^{r}\chi_{i}\lambda_{i}^{k}\ =\ \sum_{i=1}^{r}\chi_{\sigma^{-1}(i)}\lambda_{i}^{k}.

This equality holds especially for
k=1,\dots r
, let us write it in a matrix form:

\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_{1}&\dots&\lambda_{r}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\lambda_{1}^{r}&\dots&\lambda_{r}^{r}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\chi_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\chi_{r}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_{1}&\dots&\lambda_{r}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\lambda_{1}^{r}&\dots&\lambda_{r}^{r}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\chi_{\sigma^{-1}(1)}\\
\vdots\\
\chi_{\sigma^{-1}(r)}\end{pmatrix}.

Since all
\lambda_{i}
are distinct, the matrix is invertible (as the Vandermonde matrix)
and
\chi_{i}=\chi_{\sigma^{-1}(i)}
for all

and for arbitrary
\sigma
.
We know that for every pair
\lambda_{i},\lambda_{j}
there is an automorphism
\sigma

such that
\sigma(\lambda_{i})=\lambda_{j}
.
It means that
\chi_{i}=\chi_{j}
whenever
\lambda_{i},\lambda_{j}
are algebraically conjugate.

Now let
v(z)=\prod_{i=1}^{r}(\lambda_{i}-z)
be a polynomial roots of which are all
\lambda_{i}
.
Let

\displaystyle v(z)=\prod_{\alpha}v_{\alpha}(z)=\prod_{\alpha}\left(z^{r_{\alpha}}+b_{1}^{(\alpha)}z^{r_{\alpha}-1}+\dots+b_{r_{\alpha}-1}^{(\alpha)}z+b_{r_{\alpha}}^{(\alpha)}\right),\quad b_{i}^{(\alpha)}\in\mathbb{Z}

be its decomposition into irreducible polynomials.
We split the set
A=A^{+}\cup A^{-}
of indices
\alpha
into two subsets according to the sign of
\chi_{\alpha}
.
We can write

(25)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\#S(\varphi^{k},\psi^{k})&=\sum_{\alpha\in A}\chi_{\alpha}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{r_{\alpha}}\left(\lambda^{(\alpha)}_{i}\right)^{k}\right)\\
&=\sum_{\alpha\in A^{+}}\chi_{\alpha}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{r_{\alpha}}\left(\lambda^{(\alpha)}_{i}\right)^{k}\right)-\sum_{\alpha\in A^{-}}|\chi_{\alpha}|\left(\sum_{i=1}^{r_{\alpha}}\left(\lambda^{(\alpha)}_{i}\right)^{k}\right).\end{split}

Consider the integer matrix

M_{\alpha}:=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&\dotsb&0&-b_{r_{\alpha}}^{(\alpha)}\\
1&0&\dotsb&0&-b_{r_{\alpha-1}}^{(\alpha)}\\
\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&\dotsb&0&-b_{2}^{(\alpha)}\\
0&0&\dotsb&1&-b_{1}^{(\alpha)}\\
\end{pmatrix}

Straightforward computation using the Laplace expansion shows that
\det(zI-M_{\alpha})=v_{\alpha}(z)

therefore
\{\lambda_{i}^{(\alpha)}\}
are the eigenvalues of

.
Continuing (
25
) we write

\#S(\varphi^{k},\psi^{k})=\sum_{\alpha\in A^{+}}\chi_{\alpha}\operatorname{tr}M_{\alpha}^{k}-\sum_{\alpha\in A^{-}}|\chi_{\alpha}|\operatorname{tr}M_{\alpha}^{k}

Denote

A_{e}:=\bigoplus_{\alpha\in A^{+}}\bigoplus_{i=1}^{\chi_{\alpha}}M_{\alpha},\quad A_{o}:=\bigoplus_{\alpha\in A^{-}}\bigoplus_{i=1}^{|\chi_{\alpha}|}M_{\alpha}.

Using this notation we finally have

\#S(\varphi^{k},\psi^{k})=\operatorname{tr}A_{e}^{k}-\operatorname{tr}A_{o}^{k}.

The thesis follows from the next Lemma

.

5.
The growth rate of number of synchronization points and topological entropy

Let
\alpha,\beta:X\to X
be homeomorphisms of a compact metric space to itself.
In this section we establish connection between the growth rate
S^{\infty}(\alpha,\beta)

and topological entropy for a class of pairs of homeomorphisms.

A homeomorphism
f
of a compact metric space
(X,d)
is
expansive
if there exists a constant
\epsilon>0

such that given any two distinct points
x,y\in X
, there exists
n\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
d(f^{n}(x),f^{n}(y))>\epsilon
.
We call such an
\epsilon
an
expansive constant
.

We will need also a notion of the Bowen’s
specification

[
4
]
.
The formal definition can seem vague at first so it is good to have the following intuition in mind.
We say that a self-map
f:X\to X
of a compact metric space satisfies
specification

if, given a precision
\epsilon>0
and a number of segments of orbits, one is able to find a periodic orbit
which, with the precision
\epsilon
, follows each one of them and is moving from one segment to another
in a fixed amount of time which depends only on
\epsilon
.

Formally, we say that a homeomorphism
f
satisfies
specification
if for each
\epsilon>0

there is an integer
p(\epsilon)
for which the following is true.
Given pairs
(x_{1},I_{1}),\dots,(x_{n},I_{n})
, where
x_{i}\in X
and
I_{i}
are intervals of integers
contained in
[a,b]
such that
d(I_{i},I_{j})\geq p(\epsilon)
for
i\neq j
,
there exists an
x\in X
for which
f^{b-a+p(\epsilon)}(x)=x

and
d(f^{k}x,f^{k}x_{i})<\delta
for all
k\in I_{i}
for all
i=1,\dots,n
.

Our result is based on
[
4
, Lemma 4]
which is far more general than we need here.
We follow logic of the proof but we tailor it to our purpose.
By
S(f,n,\epsilon)
we denote the order of the maximal
(n,\epsilon)
-separeted set.
The topological entropy of a homeomorphism
f
is denoted by
h(f)
.

Lemma 37

(cf.
[
4
, Lemma 1]
)
.

Let
f:X\to X
be an expansive homeomoprhism of a compact metric space.
For any sufficiently small positive
\epsilon,\delta
there is a constant
C_{\delta,\epsilon}

such that

S(f,n,\delta)\leq C_{\delta,\epsilon}\cdot S(f,n,\epsilon),\qquad\forall n\in\mathbb{N}.

Proof.

Let
2\epsilon
be an expansive constant.
There is an
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that if
d(f^{k}x,f^{k}y)\leq 2\epsilon
for
k=-N,\dots,N
,
then
d(x,y)\leq\delta
.
We choose
\alpha>0
so small that if
d(x,y)\leq\alpha
,
then
d(f^{k}x,f^{k}y)\leq\delta
for
k=-N,\dots,N
.
Denote by
M
the order of the maximal
\alpha
-net of the compact metric space
X\times X
.
The constant
C_{\delta,\epsilon}
will depend on
M
, which is independent of
n
.

Let
F
be a maximal
(n,\epsilon)
-separated set and
E
an
(n,\delta)
-separated set.
Because
F
is maximal, for each
x\in E
we can choose an
a(x)\in F
which is indistinguishable
from
x
during
n
iterations with the precision
\epsilon
, i.e.

d(f^{k}x,f^{k}a(x))\leq\epsilon,\qquad k=0,\dots n-1.

For each
a\in F
we denote the subset of
E
approximated in that manner by

E_{a}:=\left\{x\in E\mid a(x)=x\right\}.

If
x,y\in E_{a}
, then
d(f^{k}x,f^{k}y)\leq 2\epsilon
for
k=0,\dots,n-1
by the triangle inequality.
By the choice of
N
we have
d(f^{k}x,f^{k}y)\leq\delta
for
k=N,\dots,n-N
.

Now because
\{x,y\}\subset E
is
(n,\delta)
-separated there must be either
d(x,y)>\alpha

or
d(f^{n}x,f^{n}y)>\alpha
.
The set
\{(x,f^{n}x)\subset X\times X\ \mid\ x\in E_{a}\}
is therefore a subset
of an
\alpha
-net of
X\times X
, so
\#E_{a}\leq M
.
Hence

\sum_{x\in E}1\ \leq\ \sum_{a\in F}\#E_{a}\cdot 1\ \leq\ M\cdot S(f,n,\epsilon).

We get our result with
C_{\delta,\epsilon}=M
.
∎

Lemma 38

(cf.
[
4
, Lemma 2]
)
.

Let
f:X\to X
be an expansive homeomoprhism of a compact metric space satisfying specification.
For sufficiently small positive
\epsilon
and
n_{1},\dots,n_{k}\in\mathbb{N}

there are positive constants
E_{\epsilon},D_{\epsilon}
such that

\prod_{j=1}^{k}E_{\epsilon}\cdot S(f,n_{j},\epsilon)\ \leq\ S(f,n_{1}+\dots+n_{k},\epsilon)\ \leq\ \prod_{j=1}^{k}D_{\epsilon}\cdot S(f,n_{j},\epsilon)

Proof.

Let
E
be
(n_{1}+\dots+n_{k},\epsilon)
-separated set and, for each
j=1,\dots,k
,
let
F_{j}
be a maximal
(n_{j},\epsilon/2)
-separated set.
We construct a map
g:E\to F_{1}\times\dots\times F_{k}
by sending

x\mapsto g(x)=\left(g_{1}(x),\dots,g_{k}(x)\right)
so that

d\big(f^{n_{1}+\dots+n_{j-1}+i}(x),f^{i}g_{j}(x)\big)\ \leq\ \epsilon/2,\quad i=0,\dots,n_{j}.

It is possible by specification and by maximality of all
F_{j}
.
By triangle inequality
g
is an injection, hence
\#E\leq\#F_{1}\cdot\ldots\cdot\#F_{k}
.
Because
E
was arbitrary, we have the right-hand side inequality for
D_{\epsilon}=1

(27)

S(f,n_{1}+\dots+n_{k},\epsilon)\leq\prod_{j=1}^{k}S(f,n_{j},\epsilon).

Now, for
j=1,\dots,k
, let
E_{j}
be a maximal
(n_{j},3\epsilon)
-separated set.
Let
a_{j}:=n_{1}+\dots+n_{j-1}+(j-1)p(\epsilon)
where
p(\epsilon)
is as in the definition of specification.
Similarly as before, for each
z=(z_{1},\dots,z_{k})\in E_{1}\times\dots\times E_{k}

we can find an
x=x(z)\in X
such that

\displaystyle d\left(f^{a_{j}+i}(x),f^{i}(z_{j})\right)\ <\ \epsilon,\quad i=0,\dots,n_{j}.

It is again possible by specification.
By the triangle inequality the map
x(z)
is an injection and the image

E:=\{x(z):z\in E_{1}\times\dots\times E_{k}\}\subset X
is an
(m,\epsilon)
-separated set,
where
m:=n_{1}+\dots+n_{k}+(k-1)p(\epsilon)
.
Hence, for the order of a maximal
(m,\epsilon)
-separated set we can write

S(f,m,\epsilon)\ \geq\ \prod_{j=1}^{k}S(f,n_{j},3\epsilon).

By already proved (
27
) we have

S(f,m,\epsilon)\ \leq\ S(f,n_{1}+\ldots+n_{k},\epsilon)\cdot S(f,p(\epsilon))^{k-1}.

By the two last inequalities

S(f,n_{1}+\ldots+n_{k},\epsilon)\ \geq\ \frac{1}{S(f,p(\epsilon))^{k-1}}\prod_{j=1}^{k}S(f,n_{j},3\epsilon).

Finally, Lemma
37
gives us

S(f,n_{j},\epsilon)\leq C_{\epsilon,3\epsilon}\cdot S(f,n_{j},3\epsilon)
, therefore

S(f,n_{1}+\ldots+n_{k},\epsilon)\ \geq\ \frac{1}{S(f,p(\epsilon))^{k-1}C_{\epsilon,3\epsilon}^{k}}\prod_{j=1}^{k}S(f,n_{j},\epsilon)

which is actually the left-hand side inequality.
∎

Lemma 39

(cf.
[
4
, Lemma 3]
)
.

Let
f:X\to X
be an expansive homeomoprhism of a compact metric space satisfying specification.
For sufficiently small positive
\epsilon

\frac{1}{D_{\epsilon}}\exp\big(h(f)\cdot n\big)\ \leq\ S(f,n,\epsilon)\ \leq\ \frac{1}{E_{\epsilon}}\exp\big(h(f)\cdot n\big),\qquad\forall n\in\mathbb{N},

where
D_{\epsilon},E_{\epsilon}
are as in
Lemma
38
.

Proof.

By Lemma
38
we have
S(f,kn,\epsilon)\geq\big(E_{\epsilon}\cdot S(f,n,\epsilon)\big)^{k}
.
Therefore, from the definition of topological entropy

h(f)\ =\ \lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{1}{kn}\log S(f,kn,\epsilon)\ \geq\ \frac{1}{n}\log\big(E_{\epsilon}\cdot S(f,n,\epsilon)\big).

Now assume that
S(f,n,\epsilon)>\exp\big(h(f)\cdot n\big)/E_{\epsilon}
.
Then

\frac{1}{n}\log\big(E_{\epsilon}\cdot S(f,n,\epsilon)\big)\ >\ \frac{1}{n}\log\exp\big(h(f)\cdot n\big)\ =\ h(f).

The contradiction
h(f)>h(f)
finishes the proof of the right-hand side inequality.

The left-hand side inequality is provided similarly.
∎

Lemma 40

(cf.
[
4
, Lemma 4]
)
.

Let
f:X\to X
be an expansive homeomoprhism of a compact metric space satisfying specification.
There are positive constants
A,B
such that

(28)

A\cdot\exp\big(h(f)\cdot n\big)\ \leq\ \#\operatorname{Fix}(f^{n})\ \leq\ B\cdot\exp\big(h(f)\cdot n\big)

for sufficiently large
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

Let
\epsilon
be no greater than an expansive constant of
f
.
Let
x,y\in\operatorname{Fix}(f^{n})
.
If
d(f^{k}x,f^{k}y)\leq\epsilon
holds for
k=0,\dots,n-1
, then it holds for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}

and, by expansiveness,
x=y
.
The set
\operatorname{Fix}(f^{n})
is therefore
(n,\epsilon)
-separated.
Hence
\#\operatorname{Fix}(f^{n})\leq S(f,n,\epsilon)
and Lemma
39
gives us
B
.

Consider now
n\geq p(\epsilon)
, where
p(\epsilon)
is as in the definition of specification.
Let
E
be an
(n-p(\epsilon),3\epsilon)
-separated set.
By specification, for each
z\in E
there exists an
x(z)\in\operatorname{Fix}(f^{n})
such that

d(f^{k}z,f^{k}x(z))\leq\epsilon
for
k=0,\dots,n-p(\epsilon)
.
For

there is
x(z)\neq x(z^{\prime})
, therefore
\#\operatorname{Fix}(f^{n})\geq S(f,n-p(\epsilon),3\epsilon)
.
Using Lemma
39
we get

S(f,n-p(\epsilon),3\epsilon)\ \geq\frac{1}{D_{3\epsilon}\cdot\exp\big(h(f)\cdot p(\epsilon)\big)}\exp\big(h(f)\cdot n\big),

which gives us
A
.
∎

Theorem 41
.

Let
\alpha,\beta:X\to X
be a synchronously tame pair of commuting homeomorphisms of a compact metric space

Directly from the definition of

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\ \alpha^{n}(x)=\beta^{n}(x)\ \Leftrightarrow\ (\beta^{-1}\alpha)^{n}(x)=x\ \Leftrightarrow\ x\in\operatorname{Fix}\left((\beta^{-1}\alpha)^{n}\right).

We substitute

into (

provides us with the result.
∎

6.
Asymptotic behavior of the sequence
\{\#S(\varphi^{k},\psi^{k})\}

Assume that Synchronization zeta function is rational.
Then the equality (
21
)
\#S(f^{k},g^{k})=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\chi_{i}\lambda_{i}^{k}
holds
and from the proof of Lemma
33
we know
that
\lambda_{i}^{-1}
are zeros and poles of the zeta function.
Define

\lambda(f,g):=\max_{i}\{|\lambda_{i}|\},\qquad n(f,g):=\#\{i\mid|\lambda_{i}|=\lambda(f,g)\}.

In Theorem
9
we proved a formula for growth
of synchronization points.
Providing
S_{f,g}(z)

Babenko and Bogatyi stated the following theorem.

Theorem 44

(
[

, Theorem 2.6]
, see also
[
21
, Theorem 3.1.53]
)

If

.

(ii)

The set of limit points of the sequence
\left\{L(f^{k})\ /\ r(f)^{k}\right\}_{k=1}^{\infty}

contains an interval.

(iii)

The sequence
\left\{L(f^{k})\ /\ r(f)^{k}\right\}_{k=1}^{\infty}

as the periodic sequence
\left\{\sum_{\epsilon_{i}^{m}=1}A_{i}\epsilon_{i}^{k}\right\}_{k=1}^{\infty}

Fel’shtyn and Lee proved an analogous result for Nielsen numbers of a map

.
Inspired by these, by (

)
and by the duality expressed in (

)
we state the following

.

Let
f,g:X\to X
be a synchronously tame pair of maps of a compact metric space
X

and let the synchronization zeta function

be rational.

One of the three possibilities holds:

(i)

\#S(f^{k},g^{k})=0,k=1,2,\dots
.

(ii)

The sequence
\{\#S(f^{k},g^{k})\ /\ \lambda(f,g)^{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
has the same
limit points set as a periodic sequence
\{\sum_{j=1}^{n(\varphi,\psi)}\alpha_{j}\epsilon_{j}^{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}

where
\alpha_{j}\in\mathbb{Z},\ \epsilon_{j}\in\mathbb{C}
and
\epsilon_{j}^{q}=1
for some integer
q>0
.

(iii)

The set of limit points of the sequence
\{\#S(f^{k},g^{k})\ /\ \lambda(f,g)^{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}

contains an interval.

Proof.

The equality (
21
)
\#S(f^{k},g^{k})=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\chi_{i}\lambda_{i}^{k}

is a result of Lemma
33
.
Observe that if
\lambda(f,g)=0
then obviously
\#S(f^{k},g^{k})=0
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
On the other hand, if
\#S(f^{k},g^{k})=0
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
, then the synchrozniation zeta function

S_{f,g}(z)\equiv 1
. From the proof of Lemma
33
we know
that every
\lambda_{i}
is either a zero or a pole of the zeta function.
The constant function has neither zeros nor poles, therefore
\lambda(f,g)=0
.
This is the case (i).

Now assume that
\lambda(f,g)\neq 0
.
We factor every
\lambda_{i}
for which
|\lambda_{i}|=\lambda(\varphi,\psi)

in (

) as

\lambda_{i}=\lambda(\varphi,\psi)\exp(2\pi\iota\theta_{i}),

where
\iota^{2}=-1
is the imaginary unit.
Then

\#S(f^{k},g^{k})=\lambda(f,g)^{k}\left(\sum_{|\lambda_{i}|=\lambda(f,g)}\chi_{i}\exp(2\pi\iota k\theta_{i})\right)+\sum_{|\lambda_{i}|<\lambda(f,g)}\chi_{i}\lambda_{i}^{k}

and

\left|\frac{\#S(f^{k},g^{k})}{\lambda(f,g)^{k}}-\sum_{|\lambda_{i}|=\lambda(f,g)}\chi_{i}\exp(2\pi\iota k\theta_{i})\right|\leq\sum_{|\lambda_{i}|<\lambda(f,g)}|\chi_{i}|\cdot\left|\frac{\lambda_{i}}{\lambda(f,g)}\right|^{k}.

Right hand side goes to

when

therefore the sequences

\left\{\#S(f^{k},g^{k})\ /\ \lambda(f,g)^{k}\right\}_{k=1}^{\infty}\mbox{\quad and \quad}\left\{\sum_{|\lambda_{i}|=\lambda(f,g)}\chi_{i}\exp(2\pi\iota k\theta_{i})\right\}_{k=1}^{\infty}

Now we claim that the structure of the limit points set of the latter sequence

\theta_{i}

Assume that for all

we can write

Put

Whenever

This is the case (ii).

Now assume that some of

\mathcal{S}:=\left\{i_{1},\dots,i_{s}\right\}\subset\{1,\dots,n(f,g)\}

We can express the assumption as linear independence

\left(\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}\alpha_{i}\theta_{i}=\alpha,\quad\alpha_{i},\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}\right)\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad\forall_{i}\ \alpha_{i}=\alpha=0.

Then the Kronecker theorem (see

\left\{(k\theta_{i_{1}},\dots,k\theta_{i_{s}})\right\}_{k=1}^{\infty}

Consider the continuous function

h:\mathbb{T}^{n(f,g)}\to[0,\infty),\qquad h(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n(f,g)})=\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n(f,g)}\chi_{i}\exp(2\pi\iota\xi_{i})\right|.

We can consider the subtorus

\mathbb{T}_{\mathcal{S}}:=\left\{(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n(f,g)})\in\mathbb{T}^{n(f,g)}\mid\xi_{j}=0,\forall j\notin\mathcal{S}\right\}

For

is compact and the restriction

is continuous, there exists the maximum
m_{\mathcal{S}}:=\max_{\mathbb{T}_{\mathcal{S}}}h_{\mathcal{S}}(\xi)
.
Observe that the closure of the image of the dense sequence contains the interval

\overline{h_{\mathcal{S}}\big(\left\{k\theta_{i_{1}},\dots,k\theta_{i_{s}}\right\}_{k}\big)}\ \supset\ [0,m_{\mathcal{S}}].

This is the case (iii).

Connection with the Reidemeister Torsion

Lemma 46
.

Let
\phi,\psi:\Gamma\rightarrow\Gamma
are two automorphisms.
Two elements
x,y
of
\Gamma
are
\psi^{-1}\phi
-conjugate if and only if
elements
\psi(x)

are
(\psi,\phi)
-conjugate. Therefore the Reidemeister number

Proof.

Let

7.1.
The Reidemeister Torsion as a special value of the coincidence Reidemeister zeta function and the synchronization zeta function

Like the Euler characteristic, the Reidemeister torsion is algebraically defined.
Roughly speaking, the Euler characteristic is a
graded version of the dimension, extending
the dimension from a single vector space to a complex
of vector spaces.
In a similar way, the Reidemeister torsion
is a graded version of the absolute value of the determinant
of an isomorphism of vector spaces.
Let
d^{i}:C^{i}\rightarrow C^{i+1}
be a cochain complex
C^{*}

of finite dimensional vector spaces over
\mathbb{C}
with

C^{i}=0
for
i<0
and large
i
.
If the cohomology
H^{i}=0
for all
i
we say that

C^{*}
is
acyclic
.
If one is given positive densities
\Delta_{i}
on
C^{i}

then the Reidemeister torsion
\tau(C^{*},\Delta_{i})\in(0,\infty)

for acyclic
C^{*}
is defined as follows:

Definition 49
.

Consider a chain contraction
\delta^{i}:C^{i}\rightarrow C^{i-1}
,
ie. a linear map such that
d\circ\delta+\delta\circ d=id
.
Then
d+\delta
determines a map

(d+\delta)_{+}:C^{+}:=\oplus C^{2i}\rightarrow C^{-}:=\oplus C^{2i+1}

and a map

(d+\delta)_{-}:C^{-}\rightarrow C^{+}
.
Since the map

(d+\delta)^{2}=id+\delta^{2}
is unipotent,

(d+\delta)_{+}
must be an isomorphism.
One defines
\tau(C^{*},\Delta_{i}):=\mid\det(d+\delta)_{+}\mid

(see
[
20
]
).

Reidemeister torsion is defined in the following geometric setting.
Suppose
K
is a finite complex and
E
is a flat, finite dimensional,
complex vector bundle with base
K
.
We recall that a flat vector bundle over
K
is essentially the
same thing as a representation of
\pi_{1}(K)
when
K
is
connected.
If
p\in K
is a basepoint then one may move the fibre at
p

in a locally constant way around a loop in
K
. This
defines an action of
\pi_{1}(K)
on the fibre
E_{p}
of
E

above
p
. We call this action the holonomy representation

\rho:\pi\to GL(E_{p})
.

Conversely, given a representation
\rho:\pi\to GL(V)

of
\pi
on a finite dimensional complex vector space
V
,
one may define a bundle
E=E_{\rho}=(\tilde{K}\times V)/\pi
.
Here
\tilde{K}
is the universal cover of
K
, and

\pi
acts on
\tilde{K}
by covering tranformations and on
V

by
\rho
.
The holonomy of
E_{\rho}
is
\rho
, so the two constructions
give an equivalence of flat bundles and representations of
\pi
.

If
K
is not connected then it is simpler to work with
flat bundles. One then defines the holonomy as a
representation of the direct sum of
\pi_{1}
of the
components of
K
. In this way, the equivalence of
flat bundles and representations is recovered.

Suppose now that one has on each fibre of
E
a positive density
which is locally constant on
K
.
In terms of
\rho_{E}
this assumption just means

\mid\det\rho_{E}\mid=1
.
Let
V
denote the fibre of
E
.
Then the cochain complex

with coefficients in
E

can be identified with the direct sum of copies
of
V
associated to each
i
-cell
\sigma
of
K
.
The identification is achieved by choosing a basepoint in each
component of
K
and a basepoint from each
i
-cell.
By choosing a flat density on

we obtain a
preferred density
\Delta_{i}
on

. One defines the

to be
