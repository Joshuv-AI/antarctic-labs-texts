Cluster algebras and skein algebras for surfaces
Hiroaki Karuo, Han-Bom Moon, Helen Wong

Abstract
. We consider two algebras of curves associated to an oriented surface of finite type – the cluster algebra from combinatorial algebra, and the skein algebra from quantum topology. We focus on generalizations of cluster algebras and generalizations of skein algebras that include arcs whose endpoints are marked points on the boundary or in the interior of the surface. We show that the generalizations are closely related by maps that can be explicitly defined, and we explore the structural implications, including (non-)finite generation. We also discuss open questions about the algebraic structure of the algebras.



1.
Introduction

Let
\Sigma
be an oriented surface with a finite set of marked points, which we distinguish as boundary marked points or interior punctures. The main goals of this paper are to review and clarify relationships between several generalizations of the cluster algebra and the skein algebra of
\Sigma
, and to explore the structural implications of this relationship.

The
cluster algebra

\mathcal{A}(\Sigma)
of a surface are a large class of cluster algebras that are particularly well-studied (See
[
Wil14
]
for an excellent survey). Cluster algebras are commutative algebras whose generators (cluster variables) and relations (cluster seeds) satisfy mutation properties determined by certain types of combinatorial data
[
FZ02
]
. In the case of the cluster algebra of a surface with boundary marked points, the generators correspond to edges of an ideal triangulation, and mutation encodes the combinatorial changes seen when a diagonal edge of the triangulation is flipped
[
GSV05
,
FG06
]
. The definition was motivated by the similarity between the mutation formulas for cluster algebras and the Ptolemy relation for lengths of edges seen in Teichmüller theory, especially as in the work of Penner
[
Pen87
,
Pen12
]
. In the presence of interior marked points, one can also define the cluster algebra, but one needs to combinatorially extend the definition of a triangulation and the edge flip move
[
FST08
,
FT18
]
.

The cluster algebra for a surface has been found to be closely related to the Kauffman bracket
skein algebra

\mathcal{S}_{q}(\Sigma)
from quantum topology. The skein algebra was introduced in
[
Prz91
,
Tur91
]
as a generalization of the Jones polynomial
[
Jon85
,
Kau87
]
and appears prominently in the associated topological quantum field theory
[
BHMV95
]
. The skein algebra is generated by framed loops in
\Sigma\times(-1,1)
modulo the Kauffman bracket skein relations, and it is non-commutative except for a few small surfaces and except when
q=\pm 1
. The skein algebra holds a special place in quantum topology due to its connections to hyperbolic geometry and algebraic geometry. In particular, it is a deformation quantization of the
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
-character variety of the surface
[
Bul97
,
PS00
,
Tur91
]
, which contains the Teichmüller space of
\Sigma
.

Because of the common connection to Teichmüller theory, it is natural to expect that there is some compatibility between the cluster algebra
\mathcal{A}(\Sigma)
and the skein algebra
\mathcal{S}_{q}(\Sigma)
. Actually, because the cluster algebra is generated by edges of an ideal triangulation, a little thought shows that the compatibility should exist with versions of the skein algebra that include arcs between marked points on
\Sigma
. One such generalization appeared in work of Roger and Yang
[
RY14
]
, who wanted to identify their skein algebra as a deformation quantization of Penner’s decorated Teichmüller space for a punctured surface
\Sigma

[
Pen87
,
Pen12
]
. At around the same time, Muller
[
Mul16
]
defined a different skein algebra, coming from a quantum cluster algebra for a surface of boundary marked points. Since then, these definitions have been combined and extended to include decorations of
\pm
(called states) on the boundary marked points
[
Lê18
,
BKL24
]
. We aim to make a precise statement about their relationship with each other and with cluster algebras here.

We remark that similar compatibility results have been proved previously, with different levels of generality and different methods
[
Mul16
,
MQ23
,
MW24
]
. However, clear statements about the compatibility of the cluster algebra and skein algebra have been missing so far. The known compatibility results have treated only special cases (such as surfaces with only boundary marked points, or only with interior punctures) and only with certain variations. A goal of this paper is to provide general statements that incorporate what is known so far, both by providing references and by explaining how to extend existing proofs.

We begin with definitions of the various generalizations of the skein algebra that include arcs in Section
2
. Our primary interest is in the
Muller-Roger-Yang generalized skein algebra

\mathcal{S}_{q}^{\mathrm{MRY}}(\Sigma)
from
[
BKL24
]
. We also discuss the
Lê-Roger-Yang generalized skein algebra

\mathcal{S}_{q}^{\mathrm{LRY}}(\Sigma)
, which includes states at the boundary marked points. In Section
4
, we prove a striking common property, that the skein algebras are finitely generated. In contrast, as we discuss in Section
6.2
, the cluster algebra
\mathcal{A}(\Sigma)
is not always finitely generated.

Theorem A
.

The generalized skein algebras
\mathcal{S}_{q}^{\mathrm{MRY}}(\Sigma)
and
\mathcal{S}_{q}^{\mathrm{LRY}}(\Sigma)
are finitely generated.

It was shown that the original skein algebra
\mathcal{S}_{q}(\Sigma)
was finitely generated in
[
Bul99
]
, and the proof was extended in for specific cases
[
Mul16
,
BPKW16
,
MW24
]
. Our proof here applies to the most general case. It immediately follows that some variations of generalized skein algebras are also finitely generated (Corollary
4.2
and
4.5
).

In Section
5
, we define the cluster algebra for surfaces with both boundary marked points and interior punctures, as well its associated
upper cluster algebra

\mathcal{U}(\Sigma)
. We prove the compatibility of the cluster algebras and skein algebras in Section
6
. To do so, we make two modifications. First, observe that the set of boundary edges form a multiplicative subset, and so we may define a localization
\mathcal{S}_{q}^{\mathrm{MRY}}(\Sigma)[\partial^{-1}]
. Then we set
q=1
to obtain a commutative skein algebra
\mathcal{S}_{1}^{\mathrm{MRY}}(\Sigma)[\partial^{-1}]
, and we show that it contains a copy of the cluster algebra. Secondly, we define a certain subalgebra
\mathcal{S}_{q}^{\square}(\Sigma)
of
\mathcal{S}_{q}^{\mathrm{MRY}}(\Sigma)[\partial^{-1}]
that is generated by tagged arcs and loops; see Section
6
for the definition. We show that it is contained inside the upper cluster algebra
\mathcal{U}(\Sigma)
.

Theorem B
.

Let
\Sigma
be a triangulable marked surface.

(1)

There is an inclusion
\mathcal{A}(\Sigma)\subset\mathcal{S}_{1}^{\mathrm{MRY}}(\Sigma)[\partial^{-1}]
.

(2)

There is a subalgebra
\mathcal{S}_{q}^{\square}(\Sigma)
of
\mathcal{S}_{q}^{\mathrm{MRY}}(\Sigma)[\partial^{-1}]
(that is generated by tagged arcs and loops) so that there are inclusions

\mathcal{A}(\Sigma)\subset\mathcal{S}_{1}^{\square}(\Sigma)\subset\mathcal{U}(\Sigma).

We remark that the second part of Theorem
B
also appears in
[
MQ23
, Proposition 3.7]
. However, at least to the authors, the extension from an unpunctured surface case
[
Mul16
]
to punctured surfaces needs some verification. This is because there are no tagged arcs in
\mathcal{S}_{q}^{\mathrm{MRY}}(\Sigma)
, but there are vertex classes. We give some brief outline of the argument and references to relevant literature in Section
6
.

Due to limited scope and time, the authors decided to restrict discussion here to the generalized skein algebras
\mathcal{S}_{q}^{\mathrm{MRY}}(\Sigma)
and
\mathcal{S}_{q}^{\mathrm{LRY}}(\Sigma)
, and their variations. The research on the ordinary skein algebra
\mathcal{S}_{q}(\Sigma)
is both long and rich. The results for the
\mathcal{S}_{q}(\Sigma)
obviously inspired the theorems presented in this paper, as well as the open questions from Section
7
. Unfortunately, a full discussion and bibliography are outside the scope of our paper. We encourage interested readers to check the references, e.g.
[
LY21
,
PBIMW24
]
.

Definition 2.1

(2)

If

-tangle and a

-tangle are disjoint unions of framed loops on

4.
Finite generation of skein algebra

In this section, we prove that the skein algebras we consider are always finitely generated. In a way, this is rather surprising— as we will see later, the skein algebra contains a copy of a cluster algebra, but in many cases the cluster algebra is not finitely generated.
Here we give a skein theoretic proof, extending
[
Bul99
]
and
[
BKWP16
]
, where the finite generation of
\mathcal{S}_{q}(\Sigma)
and
\mathcal{S}_{q}^{\mathrm{RY}}(\Sigma)
were shown. Alternatively, one may prove the finite generation via quantum trace, as done in
[
BKL24
]
for
\overline{\mathcal{S}}_{q}^{\mathrm{LRY}}
.

Our strategy is based on the following observation. For a marked surface
\Sigma

5.
Cluster algebra of surface

We next consider the
cluster algebra

\mathcal{A}(\Sigma)
coming from the curves in a triangulable oriented surface
\Sigma
. In this section, we give a brief definition of
\mathcal{A}(\Sigma)
. A more detailed description and further properties of
\mathcal{A}(\Sigma)
can be found in
[
FST08
,
FT18
]
. In Section
6
, we will explain its relationship with the skein algebra.

5.1.
Informal description

Let
\Sigma=(\underline{\Sigma},V)
be a surface that admits an ideal triangulation
\Delta
, with the set of vertices
V
. Here an ideal triangulation always contains all boundary arcs. Let
E:=\{x_{i}\}
be the set of edges in
\Delta
. The cluster algebra
\mathcal{A}(\Sigma)
is defined as some subalgebra of the field of fractions
\mathbb{C}(E):=\mathbb{C}(x_{i}|_{i\in I})
generated by edges in
E
subject to geometrically motivated relations. Because the formal definition will require some additional structure on the edges, it may seem overly technical at first. We thus start with an intuitive description to give the flavor of the construction and worry about the technical details later.

The basic construction of the cluster algebra is based on the following idea. For a given triangulation
\Delta
and a choice of an internal diagonal edge
x
of a quadrilateral, we may flip the diagonal edge
x
in the triangulation to the other diagonal edge
x^{\prime}
of the quadrilateral. This move produces a new triangulation
\Delta^{\prime}
where all edges are the same as in
\Delta
except that
x
is replaced by a flipped edge
x^{\prime}
(Figure
9
).

In the cluster algebra, we relate the edges
x
and
x^{\prime}
using the
Ptolemy relation
, which comes from the geometry of relating the lengths of the edges of the quadrilateral with its diagonals.
More specifically, suppose that the quadrilateral has edges
x_{1}
,
x_{2}
,
x_{3}
, and
x_{4}
, and flipping the diagonal
x
of the quadrilateral gives a new diagonal
x^{\prime}
, as in Figure
9
. In this case, the Ptolemy relation between
x
and
x^{\prime}
is defined to be
xx^{\prime}=x_{2}x_{4}+x_{1}x_{3}
. Observe that the product
xx^{\prime}
is a ‘binomial’ with respect to the quadrilateral edges
x_{1}
,
x_{2}
,
x_{3}
, and
x_{4}
, and that
x^{\prime}
is a rational function with respect to edges in
\Delta
. As a result, we can think of the edges in the new triangulation
\Delta^{\prime}
as belonging in the field of fractions of the edges of the original triangulation
\Delta
. Moreover, the combinatorial changes between the edges of
\Delta
and
\Delta^{\prime}
can also be encoded algebraically by way of mutation of the adjacency matrices, and we will provide these equations later.

Figure 9.
Example of a flip of triangulation and the associated Ptolemy relation

The cluster algebra
\mathcal{A}(\Sigma)
we seek to define is very ‘close’ to the subalgebra of
\mathbb{C}(E)
generated by all the edges of all possible triangulations subject to the Ptolemy relation and mutation of adjacency matrices. Importantly, the resulting
\mathcal{A}(\Sigma)
can be regarded as an algebra of all arcs on
\Sigma
. Recall that, any embedded arc between two vertices belongs to some ideal triangulation on
\Sigma
. Moreover, any two ideal triangulations are connected by finitely many flips. In the algebra, each time we flip an edge in
\Delta^{\prime}
, we obtain a new triangulation with one new edge that is related to the old ones by the Ptolemy relation. Although the number of generators of the cluster algebra is a priori infinite, it turns out that any arc can be written in terms of the edges of the original triangulation
\Delta
. The upshot is that we can think of any embedded arc of
\Sigma
as belonging in
\mathcal{A}(\Sigma)
, and the cluster algebra
\mathcal{A}(\Sigma)
is an algebra of curves.

However, there are some technical issues that arise when aligning this intuitive construction of
\mathcal{A}(\Sigma)
with the standard definition of cluster algebras. In particular, all elements of a cluster algebra should be flippable. However, boundary edges are never diagonals of any quadrilaterial and hence never flippable. This can be accounted for by using a version of the cluster algebra where the boundary edges are
frozen variables
. In cluster algebra literature, it is common to include the multiplicative inverses of the frozen variables, so we will take the same convention.

A more serious issue comes up when there are interior punctures. In particular, consider the part of an ideal triangulation in Figure
10
. One may flip the top interior edge, to get a new triangulation with a self-folded triangle. However, now the bottom edge connecting the two bottom punctures (inside of the self-folded triangle) is no longer a diagonal of a quadrilateral and thus cannot be flipped.

Figure 10.
No flip for self-folded triangles

To rectify this issue, in
[
FST08
]
, Fomin, Shapiro, and Thurston introduced the additional structure of
tagged arcs
to account for the combinatorics of self-folded triangles. A tagged arc is a topological arc where each end can be decorated by one of two taggings, plain or notched. The notched end is denoted by drawing a small bowtie, and the plain end is unmarked. The three possible types of tagged arcs are illustrated in Figure
11
.

Figure 11.
Three types of tagged arcs

Triangulations can then be generalized to include tagged arcs, and every tagged arc can now be flipped as in Figure
12
. This new diagrammatic procedure for tagged arcs is called a
tagged flip
, and its combinatorics are designed to match that of ordinary flips along diagonals of quadrilaterals. For the details, see
[
FST08
, Section 7]
,
[
MW24
, Section 3]
.

Figure 12.
Tagged flip

We emphasize that a tagged arc can have a notched end only at an interior puncture. Boundary arcs and arcs connecting boundary marked points cannot have any tags. So, in the absence of interior punctures, the additional structure of tagging is not needed at all to define the cluster algebra of the surface.

Consider the subalgebra

x_{j}^{\pm 1}
simply maps to
\underline{x_{j}}^{\pm 1}
and gives a well-defined element in
\mathcal{S}_{1}^{\mathrm{MRY}}(\Sigma)[\partial^{-1}]
.

The slogan for
\rho
is: “A tag is a vertex class.” Whenever one has a notch on a tagged arc, replace the notched end by a vertex class.

Proof of Part (1) of Theorem
B
.

For each cluster seed, we have a well-defined homomorphism
\rho_{\Delta}
in (
18
). To prove the statement, we first show that these homomorphisms are compatible: If
\Delta
and
\Delta^{\prime}
are connected by a single flip, then for any element in

(19)

\mathbb{C}[x_{i}|_{i\in I_{\circ}},x_{j}^{\pm 1}|_{j\in I_{\partial}}]\cap\mathbb{C}[x_{i}^{\prime}|_{i\in I_{\circ}},x_{j}^{\prime\pm 1}|_{j\in I_{\partial}}],

the image of
\rho_{\Delta}
and
\rho_{\Delta^{\prime}}
coincide. Thus, we may ‘glue’ the two homomorphisms to get
\rho
.

One may describe all possible tagged flips and for each one, calculating the mutation explicitly. It was done in
[
MW24
, Proposition 4.3]
using puzzle pieces. The paper covered only surfaces without boundary, but note that we do not flip any boundary edge, so the same list of cases covers all possible cases here, too.

To give a flavor of the type of computation, we now reproduce the proof using puzzle pieces for one particular case. Consider the tagged flip
\Delta\to\Delta^{\prime}
described in Figure
15
. For this flip, the only nontrivial change is made for
x
, and by the exchange relation (
9
), we obtain
xx^{\prime}=x_{2}+x_{3}
. On the other hand, the puncture-skein relation in Definition
2.2
(after setting
q=1
) provides
v_{2}\underline{x}\underline{x^{\prime}}=\underline{x_{2}}+\underline{x_{3}}
. Therefore,

(20)

\rho(xx^{\prime})=v_{2}\underline{x}\underline{x^{\prime}}=\underline{x_{2}}+\underline{x_{3}}=\rho(x_{2}+x_{3}).

Figure 15.
A tagged flip of an edge of triangulation

With similar calculations on other cases, we obtain a well-defined morphism
\rho:\mathcal{A}(\Sigma)\to\mathcal{S}_{1}^{\mathrm{MRY}}(\Sigma)[\partial^{-1}]
. To finish the proof, we need to show that
\rho
is a monomorphism.

Fix an ordinary triangulation
\Delta
whose set of edges is
E=\{x_{i}\}
. We claim that both
\mathcal{A}(\Sigma)
and
\mathcal{S}_{q}^{\mathrm{MRY}}(\Sigma)[\partial^{-1}]
admit a morphism to the Laurent polynomials in
\{x_{i}\}
,

L_{\Delta}:=\mathbb{C}[x_{1}^{\pm 1},x_{2}^{\pm 1},\cdots,x_{n}^{\pm 1}].

For
\mathcal{A}(\Sigma)
, the Laurent phenomenon of Theorem
5.6
provides an inclusion
\mathcal{A}(\Sigma)\subset L_{\Delta}
. We denote the inclusion map by
\iota
. For
\mathcal{S}_{1}^{\mathrm{MRY}}(\Sigma)
, one needs to show that any vertices and loop classes are Laurent polynomials. The computation for the loop classes and general arc classes not necessarily in
\Delta
is well-known in skein theory literature. For instance, see
[
RY14
, Theorem 3.22]
. Applying puncture-skein relation, any interior puncture is also a Laurent polynomial in
E
. For instance, in Figure
15
,
v_{2}\underline{x}\underline{x^{\prime}}=\underline{x_{2}}+\underline{x_{3}}
.

Thus, we have the following commutative diagram of commutative algebras:

(21)

\textstyle{\mathcal{A}(\Sigma)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\rho}
\scriptstyle{\iota}
\textstyle{\mathcal{S}_{1}^{\mathrm{MRY}}(\Sigma)[\partial^{-1}]\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\iota^{\prime}}
\textstyle{L_{\Delta}.}

Since
\iota=\iota^{\prime}\circ\rho
is injective,
\rho
is injective, too.
∎

Remark 6.2
.

Note that the above proof does not claim that
\iota^{\prime}
is also injective. We believe it has to be shown separately. If any edge is not a zero divisor, then the localization map
\iota^{\prime}
is injective. But how do we know that the multiplication of an arc on
\mathcal{S}_{1}^{\mathrm{MRY}}(\Sigma)[\partial^{-1}]
is injective? One way to show it is using dimension theory from algebraic geometry. Apply an algebraic ring extension
\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}(\Sigma)
of
\mathcal{A}(\Sigma)
in
\mathbb{C}(E)
and construct an epimorphism
\widetilde{\rho}:\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}(\Sigma)\to\mathcal{S}_{1}^{\mathrm{MRY}}(\Sigma)[\partial^{-1}]
. Since
\widetilde{\rho}
is an epimorphism from an integral domain with the same dimension, its kernel is trivial
[
MW24
, Theorem 5.2]
. Thus,
\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}(\Sigma)\cong\mathcal{S}_{1}^{\mathrm{MRY}}(\Sigma)[\partial^{-1}]
and the latter is an integral domain, and any nonzero element is not a zero divisor.

Observe that the above proof tells us that any loop class in
\mathcal{S}_{1}^{\mathrm{MRY}}(\Sigma)[\partial^{-1}]
is a Laurent polynomial with respect to any fixed ordinary triangulation. The same Laurent polynomial formula also applies to tagged triangulation
[
MSW11
, Proposition 3.15]
. It thus follows that all loop classes are in
\mathcal{U}(\Sigma)
. However, even though each vertex class is a Laurent polynomial with respect to an ordinary triangulation, it is not with respect to a tagged triangulation. Therefore, it does not belong to
\mathcal{U}(\Sigma)
.

Definition 6.3
.

Let
\mathcal{S}_{q}^{\square}(\Sigma)
be a subalgebra of
\mathcal{S}_{q}^{\mathrm{MRY}}(\Sigma)[\partial^{-1}]
generated by:

(1)

loop classes;

(2)

all arc classes;

(3)

all formal inverses of boundary arc classes;

(4)

if
\beta
is an arc class connecting two (non-necessarily distinct) interior punctures
v
and
w
;
v\beta
,
w\beta
, and
vw\beta
;

(5)

if
\beta
is an arc class connecting an interior puncture
v
and a boundary marked point,
v\beta
.

Proof of Part (2) of Theorem
B
.

When
q=1
, we may define
\mathcal{S}_{1}^{\square}(\Sigma)
in a simpler way: It is a subalgebra of
\mathcal{S}_{1}^{\mathrm{MRY}}(\Sigma)[\partial^{-1}]
generated by the image of
\rho
and the loop classes. Since
\mathcal{S}_{1}^{\square}(\Sigma)
is generated by
\rho(\mathcal{A}(\Sigma))
and the loop classes, any element in
\mathcal{S}_{1}^{\square}(\Sigma)
is in
\mathcal{U}(\Sigma)
.
∎

We also obtain the following structural results:

Proposition 6.4
.

Let
\Sigma
be a marked surface.

(1)

\mathcal{S}_{q}^{\square}(\Sigma)
is finitely generated.

(2)

\mathcal{S}_{1}^{\square}(\Sigma)
is an integral domain.

Proof.

One may note that if one start with a generator in
\mathcal{S}_{q}^{\square}(\Sigma)
, the reduction process described in Section
4
makes elements in
\mathcal{S}_{q}^{\square}(\Sigma)
only. Thus, the same proof of the finite generation works for
\mathcal{S}_{q}^{\square}(\Sigma)
. This proves (1).

Item (2) is an immediate consequence of Theorem
B
because
\mathcal{U}(\Sigma)
is an integral domain.
∎

6.2.
Applications of Compatibility

There are a few applications to the structure of
\mathcal{A}(\Sigma)
.

In general, it has been observed that the ordinary cluster algebra
\mathcal{A}
may behave badly, but its upper cluster algebra has better structure. For instance, in many cases,
\mathcal{U}
is a finitely generated algebra, hence it defines an affine irreducible algebraic variety
\mathrm{Spec}\;\mathcal{U}
. (However, there are some infinitely generated examples
[
Spe13
,
GHK15
]
.)
Moreover,
\mathcal{U}
is integrally closed
[
Mul13
, Proposition 2.1]
, so
\mathrm{Spec}\;\mathcal{U}
is a normal variety, which means its singularity type is relatively mild (the singular locus is of codimension
\geq 2
, etc.). So one may ask if
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{U}
, for a given cluster algebra.

In the case of cluster algebra
\mathcal{A}(\Sigma)
of surfaces, if there is at least one boundary component, then
\mathcal{A}(\Sigma)=\mathcal{U}(\Sigma)

[
Mul13
,
MSW13
,
CLS15
,
MS16
]
. On the other hand, if
\Sigma
is a surface without boundary but with one interior puncture, then
\mathcal{A}(\Sigma)\neq\mathcal{U}(\Sigma)

[
Lad13
]
, which was shown by constructing an explicit element in
\mathcal{U}(\Sigma)\setminus\mathcal{A}(\Sigma)
.

An application of the compatibility between
\mathcal{A}(\Sigma_{g,n})
and
\mathcal{S}_{1}^{\mathrm{MRY}}(\Sigma_{g,n})
can be used to show that
\mathcal{A}(\Sigma_{g,n})\neq\mathcal{U}(\Sigma_{g,n})
when
g\geq 1

[
MW24
, Section 6]
. We summarize the argument here. Using techniques from invariant theory with
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
-coefficients, it was shown that
\mathcal{A}(\Sigma_{g,n})
with
g,n\geq 1
cannot be generated by finitely many elements
[
MW24
, Theorem C]
. By way of contradiction, assume
\mathcal{A}(\Sigma_{g,n})=\mathcal{U}(\Sigma_{g,n})
. Since
\mathcal{A}(\Sigma_{g,n})\subset\mathcal{S}_{1}^{\square}(\Sigma_{g,n})\subset\mathcal{U}(\Sigma_{g,n})
, this would imply that
\mathcal{A}(\Sigma_{g,n})=\mathcal{S}_{1}^{\square}(\Sigma_{g,n})
. But
\mathcal{S}_{1}^{\square}(\Sigma_{g,n})
is finitely generated (Proposition
6.4
), a contradiction.

In the case of
\Sigma_{0,n}
, the
n
-punctured sphere, it was a folklore conjecture that
\mathcal{A}(\Sigma_{0,n})
is finitely generated. The computation in
[
ACDHM21
]
indeed implies that
\mathcal{A}(\Sigma_{0,n})=\mathcal{S}_{1}^{\square}(\Sigma_{0,n})
. Therefore, the finite generation of
\mathcal{A}(\Sigma_{0,n})
follows. We do not know if
\mathcal{A}(\Sigma_{0,n})=\mathcal{U}(\Sigma_{0,n})
or not.

The finite generation problem for
\mathcal{A}(\Sigma)
itself is an interesting problem. As we mentioned earlier, it was shown that
\mathcal{A}(\Sigma_{g,n})
is not finitely generated when
g,n\geq 1

[
MW24
, Section 6]
.
Note that from Proposition
6.4
and Theorem
B
, when
\mathcal{A}(\Sigma)=\mathcal{U}(\Sigma)
, we immediately obtain the finite generation of
\mathcal{A}(\Sigma)
. The previous results and the discussion above show that if the given triangulated surface
\Sigma
has boundary or
\Sigma=\Sigma_{0,n}
,
\mathcal{A}(\Sigma)
is finitely generated. Therefore, we have a complete understanding on the finite generation problem for
\mathcal{A}(\Sigma)
.

Remark 6.5
.

The skein algebra
\mathcal{S}_{q}(\Sigma)
and its variations are non-commutative (except for some small surfaces), and one can think of them as ‘quantum’ versions of the commutative
\mathcal{S}_{1}(\Sigma)
. Similarly, one may wonder if we can obtain a ‘quantum’ analogue
\mathcal{A}_{q}(\Sigma)
of
\mathcal{A}(\Sigma)
. More generally, for a cluster algebra
\mathcal{A}
whose exchange matrix is of full-rank, its deformation quantization
\mathcal{A}_{q}(\Sigma)
was studied in
[
BZ05
]
and called the quantum cluster algebra. However, if the exchange matrix is not of full-rank, it is not possible to obtain
\mathcal{A}_{q}(\Sigma)

[
BZ05
, Proposition 3.3]
. It is also well-known that the exchange matrix
B
for
\Sigma
is not of full-rank if
\Sigma
has an interior puncture. Thus, for a surface with an interior puncture,
\mathcal{A}_{q}(\Sigma)
does not provide a desired deformation quantization.

On the other hand, note that
\mathcal{S}_{q}^{\mathrm{MRY}}(\Sigma)
can be defined for any oriented surface
\Sigma
and it is a quantization of
\mathcal{S}_{1}^{\mathrm{MRY}}(\Sigma)
. Therefore, from Theorem
B
, we may understand
\mathcal{S}_{q}^{\mathrm{MRY}}(\Sigma)
as a quantization of
\mathcal{A}(\Sigma)
.

7.
Open questions

In this last section, we leave a few open questions on the structures of cluster algebras and skein algebras of surfaces.

7.1.
Algebraic structure of skein algebras

By Theorem
A
,
\mathcal{S}_{q}^{\mathrm{MRY}}(\Sigma)[\partial^{-1}]
and its variations are all finitely generated algebras. However, numerous questions remain about its multiplicative structure. For example, a presentation of the skein algebra is currently known only for a few small surfaces.

Question 7.1
.

(1)

Compute a presentation of
\mathcal{S}_{q}^{\mathrm{MRY}}(\Sigma)[\partial^{-1}]
.

(2)

Find a minimal number of generators of
\mathcal{S}_{q}^{\mathrm{MRY}}(\Sigma)[\partial^{-1}]
.

To the best of our knowledge, the presentation has been computed for the once-puncture torus
\Sigma_{1,1}

[
BPKW16
]
and for the
n
-punctured sphere
\Sigma_{0,n}

[
ACDHM21
]
. Note that in these two cases, there is no boundary component, so the localization is not necessary. Even though it is not explicitly stated, the case of a disk with
n
boundary marked points can be obtained from
[
FZ03
, Section 12]
. The case of the annulus with one marked point on each boundary component appears in
[
Lê15
]
.

Recall that
D_{n}
is an
n
-punctured open disk. Note that when
q=1
, the commutative algebra
\mathcal{S}_{1}^{\mathrm{MRY}}(D_{n})
naturally appears in many different contexts, including in classical invariant theory, as the coordinate ring of the Grassmannian, and in tropical geometry. Consult
[
ACDHM21
, Remark 5.2]
for additional discussion.

Question 7.2
.

Identify
\mathcal{S}_{q}^{\mathrm{MRY}}(\Sigma)
for a simple surface
\Sigma
with other algebraic structures.

One may investigate other algebraic properties of
\mathcal{S}_{q}^{\mathrm{MRY}}(\Sigma)
. It has been known that
\mathcal{S}_{q}^{\mathrm{MRY}}(\Sigma)
is a domain
[
BKL24
]
as a corollary of an embedding into a quantum torus, and its center has been calculated when
q
is a primitive root of unity of odd order
[
KMW25
]
. A similar result was obtained for
Z(\mathcal{S}_{q}^{L+}(\Sigma))
in
[
Yu23a
]
and for
Z(\overline{\mathcal{S}}_{q}^{L+}(\Sigma))
in
[
Kor21
]
. From its connection to the cluster algebra, it has been conjectured that
\mathcal{S}_{q}^{\mathrm{MRY}}(\Sigma)
has a ‘positive basis,’ that is, a
\mathbb{Z}[q^{\pm 1/2}][v_{i}^{\pm 1}]
-basis whose multiplicative structure constants are all in
\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}[q^{\pm 1/2}][v_{i}^{\pm 1}]

[
Thu14
]
after we replace
\mathbb{C}
with
\mathbb{Z}[q^{\pm 1/2}]
in the ground ring. See related results in
[
DM21
,
MQ23
]
.

Question 7.3
.

(1)

Do the generalized skein algebras each admit a positive basis?

(2)

Compute the structure constants and describe them geometrically.

For a partial evidence for
{\mathcal{S}}_{1}^{\mathrm{RY}}(\Sigma)
, see
[
Kar24
]
.

7.2.
Upper cluster algebra

From Section
6
, we have that
\mathcal{A}(\Sigma)\subset\mathcal{S}_{1}^{\square}(\Sigma)\subset\mathcal{U}(\Sigma)
.
Currently, there are no known elements in
\mathcal{U}(\Sigma)
but not in
\mathcal{S}_{1}^{\square}(\Sigma)
. This suggests:

Question 7.4
.

Is it true that
\mathcal{S}_{1}^{\square}(\Sigma)=\mathcal{U}(\Sigma)
?

Between
\mathcal{A}(\Sigma)
and
\mathcal{U}(\Sigma)
, there is another algebra motivated by mirror symmetry, so-called the
mid cluster algebra

\mathcal{M}(\Sigma)

[
GHKK18
]
. It was shown that
\mathcal{S}_{1}^{\square}(\Sigma)=\mathcal{M}(\Sigma)

[
MQ23
, Theorem 1.3]
. It is not known whether or not
\mathcal{M}(\Sigma)
and
\mathcal{U}(\Sigma)
are equal.

Observe that if
\mathcal{S}_{1}^{\square}(\Sigma)=\mathcal{U}(\Sigma)
, Proposition
6.4
implies that
\mathcal{U}(\Sigma)
is finitely generated.

This compatibility of
\mathcal{A}(\Sigma)
and
\mathcal{S}_{q}^{\mathrm{MRY}}(\Sigma)
seems to be a part of more general phenomenon. One may understand our
\mathcal{A}(\Sigma)
as the theory with the structure group
\mathrm{SL}_{2}
, in the framework of higher Teichm̈uller theory in
[
FG06
]
. For some other structure groups of row rank, there are similar results
[
IOS23
,
IY23
]
. It suggests that for a higher structure group, where the skein theory is not clear, the skein algebra is expected to be ‘defined’ as the upper cluster algebra.

7.3.
Representation theory of skein algebra

A natural next step would be to study the representation theory of
\mathcal{S}_{q}^{\mathrm{MRY}}(\Sigma)
. There have been some investigations for quantum cluster algebras and localized Muller skein algebras with boundary edges
[
MNTY24
,
Kor21
,
Kor22
,
KK23
]
. However, less is known about the representation theory for variations of the skein algebra in the presence of interior punctures, as in the Roger-Yang skein algebra.

Based on techniques adapted from these earlier works,
[
KMW25
, Proposition 5.5]
showed that when
q
is an odd primitive root of unity,
\mathcal{S}_{q}^{\mathrm{MRY}}(\Sigma)
(hence
\mathcal{S}_{q}^{\mathrm{MRY}}(\Sigma)[\partial^{-1}]
too) is an
almost Azumaya algebra
. This means ‘most’ of irreducible representations can be identified with points in an open dense subset
U\subset\mathrm{MaxSpec}\;Z(\mathcal{S}_{q}^{\mathrm{MRY}}(\Sigma))
. See
[
KMW25
, Section 5]
for more details. The open set
U
is called the
Azumaya locus
of
\mathrm{MaxSpec}\;Z(\mathcal{S}_{q}^{\mathrm{MRY}}(\Sigma))
. The center
Z(\mathcal{S}_{q}^{\mathrm{MRY}}(\Sigma))
was calculated in
[
KMW25
, Theorem A]
. However, we do not yet have an explicit description of the representations of
\mathcal{S}_{q}^{\mathrm{MRY}}(\Sigma)
.

Question 7.5
.

(1)

Describe the Azumaya locus
U\subset\mathrm{MaxSpec}\;Z(\mathcal{S}_{q}^{\mathrm{MRY}}(\Sigma))
explicitly.

(2)

For each point
m\in U
, construct the corresponding finite dimensional irreducible representation
V
of
\mathcal{S}_{q}^{\mathrm{MRY}}(\Sigma)
geometrically.

The ordinary skein algebra
\mathcal{S}_{q}(\Sigma)
can be regarded as a quantization of the Teichmüller space of
\Sigma
, and its representations are closely related to hyperbolic geometric data of the surface
\Sigma
. Similarly, since
\mathcal{S}_{q}^{\mathrm{MRY}}(\Sigma)
is based on combinatorial data from the decorated Teichmüller space of
\Sigma
by extending the discussion in
[
RY14
,
Mul16
]
, it would be interesting to see how the representations of
\mathcal{S}_{q}^{\mathrm{MRY}}(\Sigma)
are related to the hyperbolic geometry of
\Sigma
as well.
