Introduction

Donald Knuth
[
4
]
introduced
whirlpool permutations
, which he defines in the following way: An
m\times n
matrix has
(m-1)(n-1)
submatrices of size
2\times 2
consisting of adjacent rows and columns. An
m\times n
whirlpool permutation is an
m\times n
matrix with entries the integers from 1 to
mn
, in which the relative order of the elements in each of those submatrices is a “vortex”—that is, it travels a cyclic path from smallest to largest, either clockwise or counterclockwise.

Knuth computed the number of
m\times n
whirlpool permutations for small values of
m
and
n
and found that the number
W_{n}
of
2\times n
whirlpool permutations seemed be given by sequence
A261683
of the On-Line Encyclopedia of Integer Sequences (OEIS)
[
7
]
, which counts permutations
a_{1}a_{2}\cdots a_{2n}
satisfying
a_{2i-1}<a_{2i}
if and only if
a_{2i}<a_{2i+1}
for
1\leq i\leq n-1
. He proved that this is indeed the case. For example, if
n=2
then there are eight such permutations and they satisfy either
a_{1}<a_{2}<a_{3}
or
a_{1}>a_{2}>a_{3}
. Note that there is no restriction on
a_{2n}
. Thus
W_{n}=2nU_{n}
, where
U_{n}
is the number of permutations of
\{1,2,\dots,2n-1\}
satisfying
a_{2i-1}<a_{2i}
if and only if
a_{2i}<a_{2i+1}
for
1\leq i\leq n-1
.

Knuth called permutations satisfying
a_{2i-1}<a_{2i}
if and only if
a_{2i}<a_{2i+1}

up-up-or-down-down permutations
since the ups and downs (often called
rises and falls
or
ascents and descents
) come in pairs. They may be described in a slightly different way: For a permutation
a_{1}a_{2}\cdots a_{m}
, an index

1<i<m
, is called a

if
a_{i-1}<a_{i}>a_{i+1}
, and
i
is called a

if
a_{i-1}>a_{i}<a_{i}
. Then the up-up-or-down-down permutations are those in which every peak and every valley is odd.

The numbers

that count up-up-or-down-down permutations of odd length are sequence

(1)

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}U_{n}\frac{x^{2n+1}}{(2n+1)!}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{2}\tanh(x/\sqrt{2})}{1-(x/\sqrt{2})\tanh(x/\sqrt{2})}=\frac{\sqrt{2}\sinh(x/\sqrt{2})}{\cosh(x/\sqrt{2})-(x/\sqrt{2})\sinh(x/\sqrt{2})},

\displaystyle=x+2\frac{x^{3}}{3!}+14\frac{x^{5}}{5!}+204\frac{x^{7}}{7!}+5104\frac{x^{9}}{9!}+10570416\frac{x^{13}}{13!}+\cdots,

, Zhuang

. A derivation of the generating function for

, which is

2.
The Proof

First we verify that Knuth’s recurrence (
2
) holds.

Lemma 1
.

The numbers
p_{n}(k)
satisfy the recurrence (
2
).

Proof.

Given an up-up-or-down-down permutation
a_{1}a_{2}\cdots a_{2n+3}
of
\{-(n+1),-n,\dots,n,n+1\}
with
a_{2n+3}=k
, we remove
a_{2n+2}
and
a_{2n+3}
and replace the remaining entries in an order-preserving way with
-n,\dots,n
, thus obtaining an up-up-or-down-down permutation
b_{1}b_{2}\cdots b_{2n+1}
of
\{-n,\dots,n\}
. How many such permutations
a_{1}a_{2}\cdots a_{2n+3}
are there with
b_{2n+1}=j
?
We consider two cases.

First, suppose that
a_{2n+1}<a_{2n+2}<a_{2n+3}=k
. In this case,
b_{2n+1}=a_{2n+1}+1
, so
a_{2n+1}=b_{2n+1}-1=j-1
and
j<k
. Then
a_{2n+2}
may be any of the
k-j
numbers
j,j+1,\dots,k-1
, and
a_{1}a_{2}\cdots a_{2n+3}
is uniquely determined by
b_{1}b_{2}\cdots b_{2n+1}
and the choice of
a_{2n+2}
, so there are
(k-j)p_{n}(j)
possibilities for
a_{1}a_{2}\cdots a_{2n+3}
.

In the second case, suppose that
a_{2n+1}>a_{2n+2}>a_{2n+3}
. In this case
b_{2n+1}=a_{2n+1}-1
, so
a_{2n+1}=b_{2n+1}+1=j+1
and
j>k
. Then
a_{2n+2}
may be any of the
j-k
numbers
k+1,k+2,\dots,j
, so as in the first case, there are
(j-k)p_{n}(j)
possibilities for
a_{1}a_{2}\cdots a_{2n+3}
.

Thus

p_{n+1}(k)=\sum_{j=-n}^{k-1}(k-j)p_{n}(j)+\sum_{j=k+1}^{n}(j-k)p_{n}(j)=\sum_{j=-n}^{n}|j-k|p_{n}(j).\qed

Next, we derive from (
2
) three identities for
p_{n}(k)
that are easier to work with then (
2
).

Lemma 2
.

For
n\geq 0
and
-n\leq k\leq n
we have
p_{n}(-k)=p_{n}(k)
.

Proof.

This follows easily by induction from (
2
).
∎

Lemma 3
.

For
n\geq 0
and
-n\leq k\leq n
we have

p_{n+1}(k+1)-2p_{n+1}(k)+p_{n+1}(k-1)=2p_{n}(k)
.

Proof.

By (
2
), we have

\qquad\quad p_{n+1}(k+1)-2p_{n+1}(k)+p_{n+1}(k-1)\\
=\sum_{j=-n}^{n}\bigl(\left|j-k-1\right|-2\left|j-k\right|+\left|j-k+1\right|\bigr)p_{n}(j).\qquad\quad

If
j>k
then

\left|j-k-1\right|-2\left|j-k\right|+\left|j-k+1\right|=(j-k-1)-2(j-k)+(j-k+1)=0.

If
j<k
then this sum is similarly 0. Finally, if
j=k
then

\left|j-k-1\right|-2\left|j-k\right|+\left|j-k+1\right|=1-2\cdot 0+1=2.\qed

Lemma 4
.

For
n\geq 1
we have
(n-1)p_{n}(n)=np_{n}(n-1)
.

Proof.

We prove the equivalent formula
np_{n+1}(n+1)=(n+1)p_{n+1}(n)
for
n\geq 0
.
Using the fact that
p_{n}(-j)=p_{n}(j)
, we have

\displaystyle p_{n+1}(n+1)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=-n}^{n}\left|j-n-1\right|p_{n}(j)

\displaystyle=(n+1)p_{n}(0)+\sum_{j=-n}^{-1}\left|j-n-1\right|p_{n}(j)+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left|j-n-1\right|p_{n}(j)

\displaystyle=(n+1)p_{n}(0)+\sum_{j=1}^{n}(n+j+1)p_{n}(j)+\sum_{j=1}^{n}(n-j+1)p_{n}(j)

\displaystyle=(n+1)p_{n}(0)+2(n+1)\sum_{j=1}^{n}p_{n}(j).

Similarly, we have

\displaystyle p_{n+1}(n)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=-n}^{n}\left|j-n\right|p_{n}(j)

\displaystyle=np_{n}(0)+\sum_{j=-n}^{-1}\left|j-n\right|p_{n}(j)+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left|j-n\right|p_{n}(j)

\displaystyle=np_{n}(0)+\sum_{j=1}^{n}(n+j)p_{n}(j)+\sum_{j=1}^{n}(n-j)p_{n}(j)

\displaystyle=np_{n}(0)+2n\sum_{j=1}^{n}p_{n}(j),

and the equality
np_{n+1}(n+1)=(n+1)p_{n+1}(n)
follows.
∎

Next we need two facts about “Seidel arrays.” (Cf. Dumont
[
2
]
and Dumont and Viennot
[
3
]
.)

Lemma 5
.

(a)

Let
a_{i,j}
be an array of numbers, indexed by nonnegative integers
i
and
j
, and satisfying
a_{i+1,j}-a_{i,j+1}=2a_{i,j}
for all nonnegative integers
i
and
j
.
Then

\sum_{i,j=0}^{\infty}a_{i,j}\frac{x^{i}}{i!}\frac{y^{j}}{j!}=e^{-2y}A(x+y),

where
A(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}a_{i,0}x^{i}/i!
.

(b)

Now suppose that in addition
a_{j,i}=(-1)^{i+j}a_{i,j}
for all
i
and
j
. Then

\sum_{i,j=0}^{\infty}a_{i,j}\frac{x^{i}}{i!}\frac{y^{j}}{j!}=e^{(x-y)}B(x+y),

where
B(x)
is a power series with only even powers of
x
,
and thus

\sum_{\textup{$i$+$j$ even}}a_{i,j}\frac{x^{i}}{i!}\frac{y^{j}}{j!}=\cosh(x-y)\,B(x+y)

and

\sum_{\textup{$i$+$j$ odd}}a_{i,j}\frac{x^{i}}{i!}\frac{y^{j}}{j!}=\sinh(x-y)\,B(x+y).

Proof.

Let

R(x,y)=\sum_{i,j=0}^{\infty}a_{i,j}\frac{x^{i}}{i!}\frac{y^{j}}{j!}.

Then the recurrence
a_{i+1,j}-a_{i,j+1}=2a_{i,j}
is equivalent to the differential equation

(3)

\frac{\partial R}{\partial x}(x,y)-\frac{\partial R}{\partial y}(x,y)=2R(x,y).

Since the entire array
(a_{i,j})
is determined by the recurrence and the values of
a_{i,0}
, it follows that
R(x,y)
is the unique formal power series solution of (
3
) satisfying
R(x,0)=A(x)
. Since
e^{-2y}A(x+y)
satisfies the differential equation and reduces to
A(x)
for
y=0
, it must be equal to
R(x,y)
.

The condition
a_{j,i}=(-1)^{i+j}a_{i,j}
is equivalent to
R(y,x)=R(-x,-y)
. If this holds then
e^{-2x}A(x+y)=e^{2y}A(-x-y)
. Setting
y=0
gives
e^{-2x}A(x)=A(-x)
so
B(x)=e^{-x}A(x)
satisfies
B(x)=B(-x)
, and
e^{-2y}A(x+y)=e^{(x-y)}B(x+y)
.
∎

We note that in part (b) of Lemma
5
, instead of the hypothesis
a_{j,i}=(-1)^{i+j}a_{i,j}
, it would be sufficient to have
a_{0,i}=(-1)^{i}a_{i,0}
.

We can now prove the main result.

Theorem
.

(4)

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{k=-n}^{n}p_{n}(k)\frac{x^{n+k}}{(n+k)!}\frac{y^{n-k}}{(n-k)!}=\frac{\cosh\Bigl(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(x-y)\Bigr)}{\cosh\Bigl(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(x+y)\Bigr)-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(x+y)\sinh\Bigl(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(x+y)\Bigr)}.

Proof.

Let

P=P(x,y)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{k=-n}^{n}2^{n}p_{n}(k)\frac{x^{n+k}}{(n+k)!}\frac{y^{n-k}}{(n-k)!}.

(The power of two is inserted to avoid factors of
\sqrt{2}
in many of our formulas.)
By Lemma
2
,
P(y,x)=P(x,y)
, and since every term in
P(x,y)
has even total degree, we have
P(-y,-x)=P(x,y)
.

Let
\mathscr{L}
be the linear operator on power series in
x
and
y
defined by

\mathscr{L}(G)=\frac{\partial G}{\partial x}-\frac{\partial G}{\partial y}.

Then the recurrence of Lemma
3
is equivalent to

\mathscr{L}^{2}(P)=4P.

Now let
Q=Q(x,y)=\frac{1}{2}\mathscr{L}(P)

and let
R=R(x,y)=P+Q
. Then

\mathscr{L}(R)=\mathscr{L}(P)+\tfrac{1}{2}\mathscr{L}^{2}(P)=2Q+2P=2R.

It is easy to check that since
P(-y,-x)=P(x,y)
, we have
Q(-y,-x)=Q(x,y)
.
Thus by part (b) of Lemma
5
, we have

(5)

R=e^{x-y}B(x+y),

where
B(x)
is a power series with only even powers of
x
. All we need to do now is determine
B(x)
.
From (
5
) we have

(6)

\displaystyle P(x,0)

\displaystyle=\cosh x\,B(x),

\displaystyle Q(x,0)

\displaystyle=\sinh x\,B(x).

Next, we want to show that

P(x,0)=1+xQ(x,0)
.
We have

P(x,0)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}2^{n}p_{n}(n)\frac{x^{2n}}{(2n)!}

and it follows from the definition of
Q(x,y)
that

Q(x,0)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}2^{n-1}\bigl(p_{n}(n)-p_{n}(n-1)\bigr)\frac{x^{2n-1}}{(2n-1)!},

so

\displaystyle xQ(x,0)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}x2^{n-1}\bigl(p_{n}(n)-p_{n}(n-1)\bigr)\frac{x^{2n-1}}{(2n-1)!}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}2^{n}n\bigl(p_{n}(n)-p_{n}(n-1)\bigr)\frac{x^{2n}}{(2n)!}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}2^{n}p_{n}(n)\frac{x^{2n}}{(2n)!}\quad\text{(by Lemma \ref{l-2})}

\displaystyle=P(x,0)-1.

Thus from (
6
) we obtain

(7)

\displaystyle P(x,0)

\displaystyle=\cosh x\,B(x)

\displaystyle P(x,0)

\displaystyle=1+x\sinh x\,B(x).

Solving for
B(x)
in (
7
) gives

B(x)=\frac{1}{\cosh x-x\sinh x}.

Thus

R(x,y)=e^{x-y}B(x+y)=\frac{e^{x-y}}{\cosh(x+y)-(x+y)\sinh(x+y)}

and
P(x,y)
is the sum of the terms in
R(x,y)
of even total degree, so

P(x,y)=\frac{\cosh(x-y)}{\cosh(x+y)-(x+y)\sinh(x+y)}.

Then (
4
) follows by replacing
x
with
x/\sqrt{2}
and
y
with
y/\sqrt{2}
.
∎

3.
Further remarks

We can derive the one-variable generating function (
1
) from (
4
) in two different ways. First, we may set
y=0
in (
4
), obtaining

(8)

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}p_{n}(n)\frac{x^{2n}}{(2n)!}=\frac{\cosh(x/\sqrt{2})}{\cosh(x/\sqrt{2})-(x/\sqrt{2})\sinh(x/\sqrt{2})}.

Now
p_{n}(n)
is the number of up-up-or-down-down permutations of
\{-n,-n+1,\dots,0,\dots n-1,n\}
that end with
n
. If we remove the final
n
, then for
n>0
we have an up-up-or-down-down permutation of
2n
numbers ending with an up. Therefore if we subtract 1 from (
8
) and multiply by 2, we will obtaining the exponential generating function for the numbers
W_{n}=2nU_{n}
as defined in the introduction. Dividing by
x
gives (
1
).

With a little more work, we can derive (
1
) by using (
4
) to evaluate
\sum_{k=-n}^{n}p_{n}(k)
. We replace
x
with
tx
and
y
with
(1-t)x
in (
4
) and integrate with respect to
t
from 0 to 1, using the beta integral

\int_{0}^{1}t^{n+k}(1-t)^{n-k}=\frac{(n+k)!\,(n-k)!}{(2n+1)!}.

We then multiply by
x
. The result is (
1
).

One might wonder why the generating function for
p_{n}(k)
is an exponential generating function in two variables, rather than an exponential generating function in one variable for the Laurent polynomials
\sum_{k=-n}^{n}p_{n}(k)t^{k}
. One way of combinatorially explaining the bivariate exponential generating function, which arises from a different approach and from other problems involving permutations where the last entry is fixed, is to restate the problem as counting permutations of a set
\{-m,-m-1,\dots,0,\dots,n-1,n\}
that end with 0. In these problems it is usually best to think of the positive and negative numbers as different sorts of labels, and of the final 0 as not a label at all, so the number of permutations in question of such a set will be multiplied by
\frac{x^{m}}{m!}\frac{y^{n}}{n!}
.

We give two examples. First, the bivariate generating function for alternating permutations (satisfying
a_{1}>a_{2}<a_{3}>\dots
) is

(9)

\frac{\cos y+\sin y}{\cos(x+y)}.

In other words, the coefficient of
\frac{x^{m}}{m!}\frac{y^{n}}{n!}
in (
9
) is the number of alternating permutations of the set
\{-m,\dots,0,\dots,n\}
that end with 0. The coefficients here are sequence
A008280
in the OEIS. They are usually called Entringer numbers, though they appear (without a combinatorial interpretation) in an 1877 paper of Seidel.

Second, let
A_{m,n}(t)
count by descents permutations
\{-m,-m+1,\dots,0,\dots,n\}
that end with 0. (So
A_{m,0}(t)
and
A_{0,n}(t)
are Eulerian polynomials.)
Then

\sum_{m,n=0}^{\infty}\frac{A_{m,n}(t)}{(1-t)^{m+n+1}}\frac{x^{m}}{m!}\frac{y^{n}}{n!}=\frac{e^{x}}{1-te^{x+y}}.
