Associations of Mean Corpuscular Hemoglobin with Survival and Cardiovascular Outcomes Among Patients with Hemodialysis in Japan Based on Real-World Data
Authors: Keita Teruya, Aya Maruyama, Yuko Mitobe, Hirokazu Honda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAnemia in chronic kidney disease increases mortality risk in patients with hemodialysis. Hemoglobin (Hb) is currently used as the primary biomarker for renal anemia management. Recently, mean corpuscular hemoglobin (MCH) has gained attention as an indicator for improving anemia care. Using a large real-world dataset, we assessed the associations of MCH with survival and cardiovascular outcomes in Japanese patients with hemodialysis.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study of 8266 patients receiving maintenance hemodialysis, using a nationwide electronic medical record database (Sept 2004–Apr 2023). MCH at the index date was categorized into quintiles. We examined the associations of MCH with all-cause mortality and major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE)-plus (comprising death, myocardial infarction, stroke, heart failure hospitalization, thromboembolism) using Cox proportional hazards models and restricted cubic spline analyses.ResultsOver a median follow-up of 2.8 years, 1414 deaths (17.1%) and 3537 MACE-plus events (42.9%) occurred. Low MCH (< 28.1 pg) was independently associated with increased all-cause mortality (adjusted hazard ratio [HR] 1.287; 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.012–1.637), while only a non-significant trend was observed for MACE-plus (p = 0.074). Spline analyses revealed that both low and high MCH were associated with increased mortality and MACE-plus. In patients with Hb 10.0–12.0 g/dL, low MCH (< 28.1 pg) was significantly linked to higher mortality (adjusted HR 1.432; 95% CI 1.007–2.038).ConclusionIn Japanese patients with hemodialysis, both low and high MCH levels may provide information for predicting mortality and cardiovascular risk. Incorporating MCH alongside Hb may enhance risk stratification and improve anemia management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12325-026-03602-7.Keywords: Anemia, Chronic kidney disease (CKD), Real-world data, Mortality, Major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), Erythrocyte indices

Introduction

Anemia frequently occurs in chronic kidney disease (CKD) and poses a significant mortality risk, particularly in patients with advanced CKD [1, 2]. The Kidney Disease Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) guidelines recommend a hemoglobin (Hb) target of 10.0–11.5 g/dL for patients with hemodialysis [3], while the Japanese guidelines for renal anemia recommend maintaining an Hb range of 10.0–12.0 g/dL using erythropoiesis-stimulating agents (ESAs) and iron supplements [4]. However, in CKD populations overseas, fluctuations in Hb that deviate from the recommended target levels have become a significant clinical concern [5]. Studies of patients with hemodialysis in Japan have suggested that unnecessary adjustments in ESA or iron supplement dosages contribute to Hb fluctuations, regardless of serum ferritin levels [6].

Mean corpuscular hemoglobin (MCH), an index representing the average Hb content per erythrocyte, has recently attracted attention for optimizing Hb management, and a novel treatment strategy for managing renal anemia has been proposed based on the relationship Hb = MCH × red blood cell (RBC) count [7]. Furthermore, MCH has been reported as a useful marker of iron-deficiency anemia [7–9], and studies of patients with hemodialysis have shown that MCH correlates with hepcidin, a protein involved in iron metabolism [6]. One notable advantage of using MCH for anemia management is its defined upper limit; most values fall within 26–33 pg/cell, thereby simplifying anemia control [7, 10].

To date, studies examining the relationship between anemia and clinical outcomes have focused on low Hb levels, Hb variability, and poor response to ESAs [1, 2, 5, 11]. However, a recent study overseas showed that increased mean corpuscular volume (MCV), an erythrocyte-related parameter, is associated with higher risks of all-cause or cardiovascular mortality in patients with CKD [12]. Furthermore, a study of patients with hemodialysis in Japan reported an association between low MCV and increased all-cause mortality, suggesting a potential role for iron deficiency [13]. In addition, among Japanese patients with hemodialysis with Hb levels of 10.0–12.0 g/dL, those with MCH < 30 pg and RBC count > 3.5 × 106/µL had significantly higher all-cause mortality compared with patients with 30 pg ≤ MCH < 35 pg and RBC ≤ 3.5 × 106/µL [14].

An association between MCH and survival outcomes in patients with hemodialysis has been suggested [14]; however, studies with large patient cohorts have not been reported. Moreover, the relationship between MCH and cardiovascular events remains insufficiently clarified in this population. Therefore, the objective of this study was to investigate the associations of MCH with mortality and cardiovascular events among patients with hemodialysis using a large Japanese real-world database. Furthermore, we aimed to assess the clinical utility of MCH as a prognostic marker for survival and cardiovascular risk in this population.

Methods

Study Design and Data Source

We conducted a retrospective cohort study using a database of Japanese medical institutions. The database was the Real-World Database (RWD) supported by Real World Data Co., Ltd. (Tokyo, Japan), a member of the Japanese Medical Data Center (JMDC) Group. This database contains electronic medical record information collected from over 200 medical institutions across Japan, including prescription data, examinations, test results, and diagnostic data based on the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) codes. As of March 2023, the RWD contained electronic medical records for over 26 million individuals, covering a wide range of age groups and geographical areas. Data between September 1, 2004 and April 30, 2023 were collected for this study.

We selected patients who met all of the following criteria: (1) had records of hemodialysis; (2) had non-dialysis-related medical procedures recorded between 60 and 7 days before their first dialysis date; (3) received dialysis on or after the index date (defined as 90 days after the first dialysis date); (4) had MCH measured within 30 days before or after the index date; and (5) had a record of renal anemia or had been treated with ESAs or hypoxia-inducible factor prolyl hydroxylase (HIF-PH) inhibitors before the index date. Patients with records of peritoneal dialysis between the start of dialysis and the index date were excluded. Additionally, we excluded patients diagnosed with gastrointestinal bleeding who were hospitalized or received a blood transfusion within 7 days prior to the index date.

Outcome Measures

Patient demographics, medication use, laboratory test results, comorbidities, and underlying CKD etiologies were collected from data prior to the index date. Use of ESAs, HIF-PH inhibitors, oral iron supplements, and intravenous iron preparations was assessed using data within 30 days before the index date. For each laboratory parameter, the value closest to the index date within a 30-day window before or after was designated as the index date value. ICD-10 codes for primary renal diseases and comorbidities are provided in the Supplementary Material (Tables S1 and S2).

Outcomes included all-cause death, myocardial infarction, stroke, hospitalization for heart failure, and thromboembolic events; only the first occurrence of each was counted as an event. Patients were followed until death, the first major cardiovascular event, withdrawal from the dataset, or the end of a 3-year observation period, whichever occurred first.

The primary endpoint was the association between categorized MCH levels and all-cause mortality risk. Secondary endpoints included (1) the association between categorized MCH levels and major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE)-plus risk; and (2) the nonlinear relationships between continuous MCH values and all-cause mortality or MACE-plus risk. MACE was defined as the first occurrence of any of the following events: all-cause mortality, myocardial infarction, or stroke. MACE-plus was defined as a composite of MACE and hospitalization for either heart failure or thromboembolism [15]. Events were identified using a combination of ICD-10 codes and corresponding procedure records; detailed definitions of MACE-plus are provided in the Supplementary Material (Table S3). As exploratory analyses, we assessed the associations of categorical and continuous MCH with all-cause mortality and MACE-plus risk among patients with index-date Hb levels of 10.0–12.0 g/dL, the range recommended by current guidelines for renal anemia [4]. We also evaluated the associations of categorical and continuous MCH with hospitalization for heart failure or the risks of vascular events, defined as a composite of myocardial infarction, stroke, and thromboembolic events. In addition, we examined the relationship between time-varying MCH and each of the clinical outcomes.

Statistical Analysis

Summary statistics were presented as frequencies and percentages for categorical variables, and as means ± standard deviations or medians (interquartile ranges, IQR) for continuous variables. MCH values at the index date were divided into quintiles to form five equal-sized groups: group 1 (MCH < 28.1 pg), group 2 (28.1 pg ≤ MCH < 29.6 pg), group 3 (29.6 pg ≤ MCH < 30.9 pg), group 4 (30.9 pg ≤ MCH < 32.2 pg), and group 5 (MCH ≥ 32.2 pg) (Table 1).

We used two types of Cox proportional hazards regression models—a baseline model and a time-dependent model—to assess the associations of MCH with each of the following outcomes: all-cause mortality, MACE-plus, hospitalization for heart failure, and vascular events.

In the baseline model, patient background characteristics recorded prior to the index date were treated as covariates. In the time-dependent model, laboratory parameters (including MCH) and prior treatment variables were incorporated as time-varying covariates, updated at 4-month intervals. In all Cox regression analyses, MCH group 3 served as the reference category. For both the baseline and time-dependent models, we conducted univariate and multivariate analyses. In the univariate model, only the MCH group was included as the independent variable. In the multivariate model, we adjusted for potential confounders, including age, sex, ESA administration, iron therapy, and laboratory values (serum ferritin, transferrin saturation [TSAT], C-reactive protein [CRP], and white blood cell [WBC] count). In addition, comorbidities, including diabetes, cardiovascular diseases, and cerebrovascular diseases, as well as primary CKD etiologies, including diabetic nephropathy, chronic glomerulonephritis, and nephrosclerosis, were each treated as independent covariates.

To explore the nonlinear relationship between event risk and MCH, we plotted restricted cubic spline (RCS) curves of hazard ratios (HRs). We placed four knots at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles of the index-date MCH values and used the median value (30.3 pg) as the reference. HRs and 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs) were estimated from the Cox proportional hazards model. All statistical analyses were performed using SAS® ver. 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA), and a two-sided significance level of 5% was applied.

Ethical Approval

Ethical approval and informed consent were waived as all patient data had already been anonymized and de-identified in accordance with Japan’s Ethical Guidelines for Medical and Biological Research Involving Human Subjects. This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments.

Results

Patient Characteristics

The patient flowchart is shown in Fig. 1. Of the 214,529 patients registered between September 1, 2004 and April 30, 2023, a total of 8764 met the inclusion criteria. After we applied the exclusion criteria, 8266 individuals were included in the analysis. The distribution across MCH groups 1–5 was as follows: group 1, n = 1611; group 2, n = 1568; group 3, n = 1770; group 4, n = 1601; and group 5, n = 1716.

Table 1 summarizes the baseline patient characteristics of each group. In total, the mean age was 69.5 years (standard deviation 12.2), 64.3% were male, 35.7% were female, and the median MCH was 30.3 pg. Hb levels were comparable across the five groups.

Lower MCH groups were characterized by younger age, lower TSAT and serum ferritin levels, higher total iron-binding capacity (TIBC), higher CRP levels, higher WBC and RBC counts, and a higher prevalence of diabetic nephropathy. The correlation coefficient between MCH and MCV was 0.91, and that between MCH and MCH concentration was 0.49.

Regarding medications, 29.2% of patients in the overall cohort received treatment for renal anemia, including ESAs and HIF-PH inhibitors. The proportion of patients receiving treatment was highest in group 1 (33.2%) and showed an overall downward trend, with minor fluctuations across groups 2–5, reaching 26.6% in group 5. Iron supplementation, including both oral and intravenous preparations as well as iron-containing phosphate binders, was prescribed to 27.4% of patients. The proportion of patients receiving oral iron, including iron-containing phosphate binders, increased with higher MCH.

Associations Between MCH and the Risks of All-Cause Mortality and MACE-Plus

We analyzed the association between MCH and the risk of all-cause mortality (Table 2). The median follow-up duration was 2.8 years (IQR 1.1–3.0 years). During the follow-up period, 1414 patients (17.1%) died. The numbers of deaths in groups 1–5 were 278 (17.3%), 238 (15.2%), 281 (15.9%), 272 (17.0%), and 345 (20.1%), respectively. In the baseline model, univariate analysis showed that only the highest MCH group (group 5) had a significantly increased risk of all-cause mortality (HR 1.336; 95% CI 1.142–1.564), whereas multivariate analysis revealed a significantly elevated risk of death in the lowest MCH group (group 1; HR 1.287; 95% CI 1.012–1.637). In the time-dependent model, univariate analysis showed that both the lowest (group 1) and highest (group 5) MCH groups were associated with a significantly increased mortality risk (group 1: HR 1.315; 95% CI 1.104–1.566; group 5: HR 1.265; 95% CI 1.080–1.480), and multivariate analysis confirmed that patients in the lowest MCH group (group 1) had a significantly higher risk of all-cause mortality (HR 1.649; 95% CI 1.302–2.088).

During the follow-up period, 3537 patients (42.9%) experienced MACE-plus. Specifically, MACE-plus occurred in 680 patients (42.3%) in group 1, 668 (42.7%) in group 2, 727 (41.1%) in group 3, 683 (42.7%) in group 4, and 779 (45.4%) in group 5. Details of the first MACE-plus events are presented in the Supplementary Material (Table S4).

In the baseline model, univariate analysis showed that only the highest MCH group (group 5) was significantly associated with an increased risk of MACE-plus (HR 1.150; 95% CI 1.040–1.273) (Table 2), whereas in the multivariate analysis, the lowest MCH group (group 1) showed a trend toward increased MACE-plus risk (HR 1.156), although the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.074). In the time-dependent model, univariate analysis showed that the lowest MCH group (group 1) had a significantly higher risk of MACE-plus (HR 1.158; 95% CI 1.035–1.296), and low MCH (group 1) remained an independent predictor of increased MACE-plus risk (HR 1.209; 95% CI 1.031–1.416) in the multivariate analysis.

We evaluated how the risks of all-cause mortality or MACE-plus varied across the spectrum of MCH values without assuming a linear relationship, using RCS analyses with Cox proportional hazards models (Fig. 2). In both the baseline and time-dependent models, the association between continuous MCH and the risks of all-cause mortality or MACE-plus exhibited a U-shaped pattern.

Associations Between MCH and the Risks of All-Cause Mortality and MACE-Plus in Patients with Hb Levels of 10.0–12.0 g/dL on the Index Date

In patients with Hb levels of 10.0–12.0 g/dL on the index date, low MCH (group 1) was significantly associated with an increased risk of all-cause mortality (HR 1.432; 95% CI 1.007–2.038), whereas no significant relationship was observed between MCH and the risk of MACE-plus (Table 3). In addition, RCS analyses showed that the relationship between continuous MCH and the risks of mortality or MACE-plus exhibited a U-shaped pattern (p = 0.007 and p = 0.012, respectively) (Fig. 3).

Regarding hospitalization for heart failure, no significant association with MCH was observed in the baseline model. Discussion

This study investigated the associations of MCH with survival, the composite outcome of MACE-plus, hospitalization for heart failure, and vascular events in patients with renal anemia receiving maintenance hemodialysis in Japan. We found that lower MCH was significantly associated with increased risks of all-cause mortality and MACE-plus. Furthermore, both lower and higher MCH values were associated with increased risks of hospitalization for heart failure in the time-dependent Cox regression model. In addition, RCS analyses showed that MCH exhibited U-shaped relationships with risks of both all-cause mortality and MACE-plus, and the association between MCH and the risk of hospitalization for heart failure was also significantly U-shaped in the time-dependent model. Notably, in patients with Hb levels of 10.0–12.0 g/dL on the index date, lower MCH remained associated with increased risks of all-cause mortality and MACE-plus.

In the present study, low MCH was significantly associated with an increased risk of all-cause mortality. MCH, which reflects the average Hb content per erythrocyte, rises in parallel with MCV, and the two parameters are strongly positively correlated [16]. A previous study in Japanese patients with hemodialysis by Honda et al. reported no significant association between elevated MCV and mortality, whereas low MCV was linked to poorer prognosis [13]. Consistently, our analysis showed that patients with low MCH had a higher mortality risk. By contrast, an international cohort study by Dratch et al. reported that high MCV is associated with increased mortality [12]. These results likely reflect differences in iron metabolism specific to each population. Furthermore, Honda et al. reported substantially lower TSAT and serum ferritin compared to those reported by Dratch et al. [12, 13]. Aligned with these results, the median TSAT and ferritin levels on the index date in our Japanese patient cohort were 20.7% and 64.0 ng/mL, respectively, both of which were lower than those observed in the study by Dratch et al. [12]. These findings may reflect the more restrictive iron supplementation practices commonly employed in Japanese patients with hemodialysis, compared to international cohorts [17]. In addition, differences in the degree of functional iron deficiency—driven by chronic inflammation, which promotes iron sequestration—may also play a role. This condition could be more pronounced in Western populations because of generally larger body size [18–21]. Such differences in iron deficiency and iron utilization may shift the distributions of MCV and MCH, ultimately leading to the observed association between low MCH and mortality risk in Japanese patients, in contrast to overseas reports.

Low MCH was significantly associated with an increased risk of the composite MACE-plus outcome, with hazard ratios exhibiting a U-shaped pattern. To account for differences in event incidence and underlying pathophysiology, we further explored the relationships between MCH and hospitalization for heart failure and vascular events. MCH demonstrated a U-shaped association with the risk of heart failure-related hospitalization. In contrast, no significant association was observed between MCH and vascular events, consistent with prior findings for MCV [13]. Given that all-cause mortality also showed a U-shaped relationship with MCH, the increased risk of MACE-plus appears to be driven predominantly by the elevated risks of death and heart failure hospitalization.

The previous study in Japanese patients with hemodialysis demonstrated that low MCV is associated with poor prognosis, an association attributed to iron deficiency [13]. In the present study, we used MCH, which reflects the amount of Hb per red blood cell, as an indicator. Patients in group 1 exhibited lower serum ferritin and TSAT levels compared with the other groups, indicating iron deficiency, and also had higher CRP levels. After adjustment for iron metabolism markers, CRP, and iron supplementation, low MCH remained an independent risk factor for both mortality and hospitalization for heart failure. One possible explanation for this independent association is that reduced MCH may impair oxygen transport capacity, thereby contributing to the development of clinical events. Additionally, although tissue iron levels were not measured in this study, depletion of tissue iron may also account for the role of low MCH as an independent risk factor [22, 23]. Patients in MCH group 1, characterized by iron deficiency, may also have concomitant depletion of tissue iron. Independent of iron stores or circulating bioavailable iron, reduced tissue iron may impair mitochondrial ATP production in myocardial tissue, thereby contributing to the development of adverse clinical outcomes such as heart failure [23].

On the other hand, MCH was not associated with vascular events; this may be attributable to the fact that the occurrence of vascular events is influenced by multiple risk factors beyond iron, including a history of cardiovascular disease [24, 25], and vascular calcification [26–28]. Although recent studies suggest that iron deficiency-induced transferrin elevation is related to thrombosis [29], this mechanism is likely different from the way tissue iron depletion contributes to heart failure. Therefore, the reduced oxygen transport capacity and tissue iron deficiency associated with low MCH may have had a limited impact on the risk of vascular events.

Among patients with Hb levels of 10.0–12.0 g/dL on the index date, low MCH was associated with an increased risk of all-cause mortality. This finding highlights the importance of considering MCH when aiming to reduce prognostic risk, even in patients within the target Hb range of 10.0–12.0 g/dL. A previous study reported that, among patients with hemodialysis with Hb levels of 10.0–12.0 g/dL, those with MCH < 30 pg and RBC > 3.5 × 106/μL had a significantly higher all-cause mortality rate compared to patients with 30 pg ≤ MCH < 35 pg and RBC ≤ 3.5 × 106/μL [14]. This suggests that a greater number of erythrocytes carrying less Hb per cell is associated with increased risk of death compared with fewer erythrocytes containing more Hb. Our findings are consistent with this previous report. Therefore, even when Hb levels are within the target range, reductions in MCH should be avoided.

In the present study, taking the RCS analysis results into account, elevated MCH levels were associated with increased risks of mortality, MACE-plus, and hospitalization due to heart failure. The reason elevated MCH acts as an independent risk factor remains unclear; one possible explanation is impaired oxygen delivery resulting from reduced erythrocyte deformability associated with increased intracellular Hb concentration [30, 31]. In our study, MCH was positively correlated with MCH concentration (r = 0.49), indicating that patients with higher MCH tended to have higher intracellular Hb concentrations. Consequently, these patients may have been more susceptible to adverse events due to impaired oxygen delivery.

The positive correlation between MCH and MCV (r = 0.91) observed in this study, together with the established association of MCV with macrocytic anemia caused by vitamin B12 or folate deficiency [32, 33], suggests that some patients with markedly elevated MCH may have had macrocytic anemia due to vitamin B12 or folate deficiency. However, the clinical laboratory data used in this study did not include measurements of vitamin B12 or folate; therefore, we were unable to adjust for these factors in the multivariable analysis, which may have influenced our findings.

The Cox regression models using categorical MCH variables demonstrated an increased risk only in the low MCH groups, whereas the RCS analysis treating MCH as a continuous variable revealed a U-shaped association, with elevated risk observed at both extremes—very low and very high MCH levels. This discrepancy may be attributable to differences in statistical modeling, such as the threshold values applied to categorical MCH groups and the number and placement of knots in the spline function for continuous MCH in the RCS analysis.

Low MCH was significantly associated with increased risks of MACE-plus and hospitalization for heart failure only in the time-dependent Cox models, suggesting that MCH may exert a short-term effect on clinical outcomes rather than a lasting influence.

The present study suggests that MCH should be taken into consideration in the management of renal anemia. MCH has been reported to be a useful indicator for iron-deficiency anemia [8, 9], and iron supplementation is one pharmacological intervention that influences MCH levels [6]. Consistently, patients in the lowest MCH group of this study exhibited significantly lower serum ferritin and TSAT levels. Furthermore, even among patients with index-date Hb levels of 10.0–12.0 g/dL, low MCH was associated with an increased risk of mortality. Therefore, in the management of renal anemia in patients with hemodialysis, it is recommended to appropriately use iron supplementation and anemia treatment agents, with consideration of both MCH and RBC levels. However, this study did not specifically investigate the association between iron supplementation and outcomes such as mortality, which warrants clarification through further research employing appropriate study design.

This study has several limitations. First, as it was exploratory and aimed at hypothesis generation, statistical testing adjusted for multiplicity was not performed. Second, the RWD used in this study was a medical institution-based database that did not permit the tracking of patient transfers; consequently, patients may have received medical care or diagnoses at other facilities. Third, only pre-structured data were collected and utilized; therefore, the underlying reasons for specific medical interventions were not clear, and inaccuracies may exist. Fourth, for certain laboratory parameters, such as iron indices, the number of measurements was extremely limited, which may have introduced selection bias into some analytical models. Lastly, the associations identified in this observational study cannot be interpreted as causal relationships.

Conclusions

In Japanese patients with renal anemia receiving maintenance hemodialysis, both low and high MCH levels were associated with increased risks of all-cause mortality and MACE-plus. Among cardiovascular events, the hospitalization for heart failure was elevated at both low and high MCH levels. Moreover, even in patients whose Hb levels were within the target range of 10.0–12.0 g/dL, our findings suggest that MCH should also be taken into consideration. Prospective clinical studies are warranted to further elucidate the relationship between MCH and clinical outcomes.
