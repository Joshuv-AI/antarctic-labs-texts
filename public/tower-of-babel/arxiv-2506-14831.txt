Recent Advances in Multi-Agent Human Trajectory Prediction: A Comprehensive Review
Céline Finet, Stephane Da Silva Martins, Jean-Bernard Hayet, Ioannis Karamouzas, Javad Amirian, Sylvie Le Hégarat-Mascle, Julien Pettré, Emanuel Aldea

Abstract
With the emergence of powerful data-driven methods in human trajectory prediction (HTP), gaining a finer understanding of multi-agent interactions lies within hand’s reach, with important implications in areas such as autonomous navigation and crowd modeling. This survey reviews some of the most recent advancements in deep learning-based multi-agent trajectory prediction, focusing on studies published between 2020 and 2024. We categorize the existing methods based on their architectural design, their input representations, and their overall prediction strategies, placing a particular emphasis on models evaluated using the ETH/UCY benchmark. Furthermore, we highlight key challenges and future research directions in the field of multi-agent HTP.



Keywords
Review, survey, human trajectory prediction, deep learning, robotics, autonomous navigation, crowd modeling

Introduction

Human Trajectory Prediction (HTP) focuses on forecasting the future movements of individuals or groups, given the observation of their past behaviors and application-dependent contextual data. In their influential 2020 survey,
[
1
]
have provided a comprehensive revision of existing research work in HTP, categorizing approaches into two main paradigms: data-driven models, which learn statistical patterns from historical data, and expert-driven models, which rely on physical principles and domain-specific rules. Today, just a few years after this landmark study, one can state that the transition from physics-based models to data-driven algorithms is nearly complete and represents a significant shift for HTP. Although physics-based models have provided foundational insight into pedestrian dynamics, data-driven approaches offer enhanced accuracy, scalability, and adaptability.

A key physics-based model has been the Social Force Model (SFM), introduced by Helbing and Molnár in the 1990s
[
2
]
. This model conceptualizes pedestrians as particles influenced by social forces that guide their movements, such as attraction to goals and repulsion from obstacles. Beyond SFM, other expert-driven models, such as velocity-based approaches, like Reciprocal Velocity Obstacles (RVO)
[
3
]
or Optimal Reciprocal Collision Avoidance (ORCA)
[
4
]
, have been developed for predicting human trajectories in crowds.

In this study, we have decided to focus only on data-driven approaches, due to their better performance compared to physics-based approaches and to the lack of new work on physics-based approaches. Since the publication of Rudenko’s survey, HTP has experienced rapid advances, particularly in data-driven models that utilize deep learning techniques. Among these advancements, multi-agent models have become a key area of focus, as they address the intricate interactions and dependencies between individuals in dynamic environments. Figure

As HTP embraces data-driven models, the availability of high-quality datasets becomes essential to learn intricate patterns of human motion and interactions. Consequently, datasets and benchmarks are not just tools but foundational elements that enable meaningful advancements in this domain. Among the most widely used datasets, the ETH/UCY benchmark

Many existing approaches still focus on single-agent prediction or fail to adequately address the complexities of modeling agent interactions in dynamic, multi-agent settings.

1.1
Terminology

An
agent
is any entity whose movement is modeled, tracked, and predicted within a specific environment. In the context of human trajectory prediction, agents typically refer to pedestrians.

The
environment
consists of spatial and contextual elements that influence agent movements. This refers to either static (e.g. obstacles) or dynamic (e.g. other agents)
elements that constrain or modify the trajectories of moving entities.

Mono-agent prediction
refers to forecasting a single pedestrian’s future trajectory based on its past behavior and the available context. It focuses exclusively on the individual agent’s movement and intentions without considering its interactions with other agents. On the opposite,
multi-agent prediction
forecasts the trajectories of multiple agents by considering their interactions, including the effects of social dynamics, shared environments, potential collisions or cooperation, making the task complex since it should capture the interdependencies in their behaviors.

Joint prediction
is a form of multi-agent prediction that predicts the trajectories of multiple pedestrians
simultaneously
while accounting for their mutual social interactions. Conversely,
marginal prediction
predicts the trajectory of an individual pedestrian while considering their social interactions with other agents.

Direct prediction
involves generating the entire trajectory in a single step, directly mapping past positions and contextual features into future coordinates. On the opposite,
auto-regressive prediction
predicts the trajectory recursively, generating one position at a time and using this output as input for the subsequent prediction.

Target-conditioned prediction
forecasts trajectories using long-term goals or intermediate waypoints as explicit latent variables within the trajectory inference process.

Prediction refinement
iteratively improves an initial coarse trajectory prediction with additional refinement steps.

1.4
Modular architectures of HTP solutions

This survey presents the various architectures proposed to tackle the HTP problem, following the precise definition outlined in the introduction. Forecasting pedestrian paths is an inherently complex challenge, as it involves accounting for multiple factors such as pedestrians’ intentions, social interactions, and interactions with the surrounding static or dynamic environment. To tackle this complexity, many proposed solutions have adopted
modular
architectures, with each module focusing on a specific aspect of the problem. Given this modularity, we have structured the discussion of architectures into three key sections illustrated in Figure
4
.

Section
2
examines how different models represent the environment and agents, focusing on both static and dynamic contextual information. We also highlight how graph-based representations and attention mechanisms are used to capture dynamic obstacles or static features in the environment.

Next, Section
3
explains the backbone architecture of the prediction models themselves. Here, we categorize the models according to their primary architectural choices, such as generative models, Transformers, and recurrent networks, and explain how these architectures interact with the contextual inputs to generate predictions.

Finally, Section
4
explores the methods used for trajectory creation, including prediction strategies, stochastic prediction, and the distinction between marginal and joint prediction methods. We also review how the predictions are evaluated using various metrics.

By organizing the review in this manner, we aim to provide a comprehensive understanding of the existing solutions for pedestrian trajectory estimation, highlighting the strengths and limitations of each architectural choice while explaining how they address the challenges inherent to the task. This structure facilitates a more thorough analysis of the different approaches, making it easier to understand how each solution contributes to the broader field of HTP research.

2.2
Dynamic contextual information

In many cases, the agent of interest for which we want to perform trajectory prediction is surrounded by dynamic obstacles, such as other agents, that may influence its trajectory through interactions such as social behaviors and spatial proximities. These implicit latent variables should be accounted for to improve prediction accuracy.
In most datasets, one has access to the positions of every agent at any given time and, when focusing on a specific agent, and can get a description of the agent’s dynamic surroundings. Social interactions are not explicitly described but can be inferred from the positions of all individuals recorded. In the following, we review the most frequent approaches to represent and tackle this neighborhood information. We divide them into
Pooling and Encoders
, which have been the first solutions to appear in the field. Then, we cover
Graph-based
solutions, which are the most prominent solutions now thanks to their ability to model complex interactions. Finally, we review other noteworthy approaches.

3.1
Sequence modeling

Sequence modeling is a cornerstone in HTP, offering the ability to handle sequential dependencies in pedestrian movement. Two prominent approaches for sequence modeling are
Recurrent Neural Networks
(RNNs) and
Transformers
, each bringing unique strengths and limitations to trajectory prediction tasks.

RNNs, particularly LSTMs, have been widely used to capture temporal dependencies in sequential data, making them suitable for short-term motion prediction. However, they may struggle with long-range dependencies due to vanishing gradient issues, which limit their performance with complex trajectories. In contrast, Transformers leverage attention mechanisms to overcome these limitations by enabling parallel processing and improving long-range dependency handling, making them effective in scenarios requiring the integration of social interactions and environmental contexts.
The following paragraphs explore how RNNs and Transformers have been adapted for HTP, highlighting their strengths and challenges in addressing their complexities.

3.1.1
RNNs.

The temporal dimension of human trajectories is crucial for understanding and predicting agent movement. Recurrent networks are particularly well-suited to this task, as their recurrent units are designed to process sequential data. These networks capture a specific type and size of memory related to the sequence that has just occurred, which allows them to consider past trajectories and contextual information when predicting an agent’s next move.

4.2
Stochastic trajectory prediction

Most HTP models embrace a stochastic approach rather than a deterministic one because human motion is fundamentally uncertain and shaped by a variety of factors, such as social interactions, environmental conditions, and individual intentions. Deterministic models offer a single predicted trajectory, which often fails to capture the natural variability seen in human behavior and may even fail catastrophically by producing “averaged” outputs when faced with inherently multimodal situations. For instance, pedestrians in similar situations may take different routes due to subtle variations in their intents or the context in which they find themselves. Stochastic methods sample multiple potential trajectories from the estimated predictive distributions,
enabling them to account for the inherent unpredictability and adaptability of human decision-making. This results in more robust and realistic predictions. Stochasticity applies both to agents’ future trajectories and their goals.
Indeed, if a
goal-conditioned
model samples a goal before predicting trajectories conditioned to this goal, then the trajectories become stochastic.
Generative models
handle this randomness using a latent space, as seen in VAEs and GANs, or through noise distributions processed via reverse diffusion, as in Diffusion models.

Goal conditioned.

Goal conditioned predictions estimate a distribution of goals and then sample from this distribution. In a way, this scheme introduces a form of interpretability to the model, by introducing an explicit latent variable, the goal. For instance, the CTSGI model
[
118
]
handles stochasticity through its Goal Point Estimation module, which leverages a CVAE to predict a distribution of possible goal points for each pedestrian and their neighbors.
BNSP-SFM
[
174
]
handles stochasticity by modeling the aleatoric uncertainty as random forces arising from social interactions (based on the social force model of
[
2
]
).
The model predicts distributions for three main factors: goal attraction, collision avoidance, and environment repulsion.
The Graph-TERN model
[
87
]
addresses stochasticity in pedestrian trajectory prediction through control point prediction. It estimates a set of control points that segment the future trajectory, with each control point serving as a potential intermediate destination. To represent the probability distribution of these points, a multivariate Gaussian Mixture Model is employed, capturing the uncertainty in pedestrian movement and enabling the generation of multiple plausible trajectories. The final trajectory endpoint is determined by linking the predicted control points, resulting in a set of possible destinations that reflect the multimodal nature of pedestrian movement. We can also mention
[
114
]
goal-conditioned part that operates by retrieving multi-modal goal candidates for test trajectories from an expert repository of training examples then fed into an end-to-end trajectory predictor.

The use of heatmaps and energy maps is another way to introduce stochasticity. For instance, STHGLU
[
124
]
generates a global scene heatmap based on training data to represent the likelihood of passage in various geographical areas. This heatmap reflects, for example, low passage probabilities in areas with obstacles. A local heatmap is extracted around each pedestrian at each timestep, capturing the evolving environmental information to probabilistically guide the agent’s future movements.
In SocialCVAE
[
126
]
, a similar approach employs a local interaction energy map to estimate interaction costs between pedestrians and their neighbors. Constructed using an energy-based interaction model, this map predicts the intensity of repulsion between agents and represents future neighborhood occupancy. It serves as a conditioning factor for the CVAE model, enabling it to learn socially plausible movement decisions over time.
These methods capture diverse and realistic behaviors by accounting for uncertainties from both social interactions and environmental factors.

Generative models.

Generative model-based HTP algorithms can naturally account for stochasticity in their output trajectories.
In the case of (C)VAE, the latent space introduces stochasticity.
The model is trained to minimize the difference between the prior and approximate posterior distributions of the latent variables using the Kullback-Leibler divergence loss while maximizing the expected likelihood of the ground-truth future trajectories. During inference, the model samples from the latent variable distribution to generate multiple plausible future trajectories. For instance, the ForceFormer model
[
151
]
leverages a CVAE framework to generate multiple possible predictions.
GANs also map variables from a latent space into trajectories, but the training is very different. The generator is trained to create realistic trajectories, while the discriminator is trained to distinguish real from generated trajectories. They are used in models such as DTGAN
[
69
]
, for example.
Diffusion models incorporate stochasticity in trajectory prediction by using a Markov chain with a Gaussian distribution (the diffusion process). This allows them to learn the underlying probabilistic structure of trajectories. For example, BCDiff
[
147
]
employs two coupled diffusion models to generate both unobserved historical trajectories and future trajectories, enhancing the accuracy and diversity of predictions, particularly in cases with limited observation time. We can also mention
[
173
]
model that combines distributional diffusion with bivariate Gaussian distributions to account for uncertainty in the future pedestrian locations.
Finally Gaussian Mixture Models (GMM) handle stochasticity by expressing uncertainty in a parametric way. PPNet
[
160
]
employs a GMM-based framework to generate probabilistic goals, ensuring that multiple possible outcomes are considered from the start. The framework is combined with more traditional generative approaches like continuous curve modeling.

4.3
Marginal vs. joint prediction

A key distinction among HTP models is whether the predictive model focuses on individual agents—yielding a
marginal
distribution over their trajectories—or considers the entire set of agents across consecutive frames, producing a
joint
distribution over their trajectories.
It should be clear that, for most applications such as monitoring or autonomous driving, reasoning
jointly
over the set of agents in the scene is critical to analyze the whole scene dynamics or to elaborate motion plans, because of the dependencies involved in the agents’ motion. Using marginal predictions could lead to producing tuples of individual predictions that contradict the joint predictive distribution.

Joint prediction is inherently more complex due to the high dimensionality of the problem and the technical challenges it presents, such as handling sequences of observations with varying lengths.
This may explain why most early HTP works
[
54
,
39
]
have focused on marginal predictions and why the benchmarking and evaluation efforts have tended to mainly emphasize marginal metrics (
\text{minADE}_{k}
,
\text{minFDE}_{k}
, see Section
4.4
).
Even in early HTP research, efforts were made to address joint predictive distributions. A notable example is SocialGAN
[
49
]
, which shares a single latent variable from a GAN’s generator of across all agent trajectory decoders in a scene. Since then, more joint approaches have emerged. Most of them employ stochastic strategies that simultaneously infer individual latent variables for all agents
[
48
,
148
]
and typically incorporate a coupling mechanism—such as graphs or pooling—to condition each latent posterior or prior distribution on the complete set of past and future trajectories.
Note that even if the predictive model is designed to be able to produce samples from the joint predictive distribution, the loss function used to train the system may not necessarily evaluate the quality of the joint outputs; it is a motivation in recent works such as
[
175
]
that applies simple modifications to the loss functions used in HTP to enforce the generation of better
joint samples
.

Finally, as we detail next,
new metrics have recently been proposed to evaluate the quality of the prediction beyond the marginal predictive distributions
[
175
]
.

4.4
Evaluation metrics

Evaluation metrics are paramount to assess the performance of various trajectory generation models through different perspectives. As such, a number of relevant metrics have been introduced by different communities, including computer vision, robotics, and computer graphics, seeking to quantify the accuracy and robustness of the predictions.

The majority of HTP models perform marginal predictions by focusing on predicting trajectories for individual agents and comparing them to the ground truth. In this setting, the most commonly used evaluation metrics for deterministic models are the
average displacement error
(ADE) and the
final displacement error
(FDE)
[
5
]
. ADE measures the average L2 distance between the predicted and ground truth positions of an agent’s trajectory over a sequence of time steps, while FDE measures the L2 distance at the final time step of the agent’s trajectory.

In the case of multimodal prediction, the ideal metric should involve the whole predictive distribution and its ground truth counterpart. Unfortunately, the latter is not available, as we just have one sample from the ground truth distribution.
Both the ADE and FDE can extend to multimodal prediction models by computing the
\text{minADE}_{k}
and
\text{minFDE}_{k}
,
i.e., the
minimum ADE
and
minimum FDE
, respectively, over
k
predictions from the generated distribution.
These metrics are also commonly referred to as
Top-k ADE
and
Top-k FDE
, with
typical values for
k
in the literature being
k
=3,
k
=5,
k
=10, and
k
=20.
However, we note that as
k
gets large,
minADE/minFDE
are rather biased, since a model that produces uniformly distributed samples can achieve a pretty low error
[
26
]
.
Nevertheless, the ADE/FDE differences in many state-of-the-art HTP models are only a few centimeters with simple linear models exhibiting rather competitive performance
[
112
,
176
]
, raising concerns about the relative rankings of such models in the presence of sensor noise and other types of uncertainty.

Another metric for multimodal prediction that focuses on the quality of the predictive distribution is the
negative log-likelihood
(NLL), that evaluates the likelihood of the ground truth trajectories
[
177
]
under an estimate of the predicted distribution, typically obtained by Gaussian kernel density estimation
[
178
]
.
More recently, the

Average Mahalanobis Distance
has also been introduced as a robust alternative to
minADE/minFDE
that combines the distance between generated trajectories and ground truth with the variance of the predictions
[
112
]
. The
Average Maximum Eigenvalue
of the covariance matrix of the predicted distribution has also been introduced as a complementary metric to evaluate the overall spread of the predictive trajectories, and thus the confidence of the predictions
[
112
]
.

Other metrics for joint prediction have been explored by different communities and can be used for assessing HTP models. Notable among these are the
Percentage/Rate of Collisions

of a given agent with its predicted neighboring agents
[
179
]
, either by providing a holistic evaluation by considering all
k
samples
[
26
,
180
,
175
]
or an optimistic evaluation by considering the sample with the minimum joint ADE
[
175
]
.
In the autonomous vehicle literature, the
Overlap rate

[
24
]
extends
the
Percentage/Rate of Collisions
to consider collisions with the static part of the environment, with additional metrics being inspired by object detection, including the

Mean Average Precision

[
24
]
and the
Miss Rate

[
181
,
24
]
that reports the percentage of predicted agents’ coordinates at each frame that lie outside of a threshold area defined around the corresponding ground truth coordinates.
In computer graphics, the
Entropy
of the distribution of errors between crowd predicted trajectories and ground truth either at a per-agent level
[
182
]
or collectively
[
183
]
has been proposed to assess crowd simulation models.
The
Entropy
metric
accounts for the noise inherently present in real-world datasets and the fact that such datasets are finite and cannot necessarily capture the all ground truth behaviors. However, its applicability has only been tested on physics-based models.
Similarly,
[
184
]
evaluate physics-based models by casting the problem as an anomaly detection problem, while
[
185
]
explored average likelihood and KL-divergence evaluation metrics.

5
Discussion

5.1
Progress in the Last 5 Years

Since the seminal survey by
[
1
]
, the field of human trajectory prediction (HTP) has undergone a significant transformation, driven by the adoption of deep learning approaches
While the shift from traditional physics-based models, such as the Social Force Model
[
2
]
, Reciprocal Velocity Obstacles
[
3
]
, and Optimal Reciprocal Collision Avoidance
[
4
]
, to data-driven methods began well before 2020, the last five years have seen a marked surge in advanced generative and context-aware techniques.
Generative models for multi-modal HTP—initially introduced through approaches like GANs and VAEs, such as SocialGAN
[
49
]
—have become increasingly prevalent in recent years.
Notably, the success of diffusion models
[
186
]
has further advanced the field by providing a robust probabilistic framework for generating diverse and realistic trajectories, as demonstrated in recent works such as
[
147
]
.

Contemporary models are now increasingly capable of capturing complex sequential and social interdependencies among agents by leveraging state-of-the-art architectures. In particular, transformer-based models—originally developed for natural language and image processing
[
187
]
—leverage self-attention mechanism to allow dynamic weighting of different time steps and interactions, enabling the capture of long-range temporal dependencies and intricate inter-agent relationships that traditional recurrent models often miss.
In parallel, the growing adoption of Graph Neural Networks has proven effective for modeling relationships among agents like in Grouptron
[
76
]
), complementing the temporal strengths of transformers—especially given the inherently graph-structured nature of HTP problems. These architectures also enable the iterative refinement of trajectory predictions by using additional contextual cues, such as social interactions and environmental constraints as in
[
55
,
79
,
38
]
.

5.2
Limitations and Challenges

Despite the aforementioned advancements, several challenges remain. One key limitation lies in current benchmarking datasets, such as ETH/UCY
[
5
,
6
]
, which are relatively small by modern machine learning standards and offer limited coverage of complex inter-agent and agent-environment interactions
[
26
,
188
]
, yet have become the de facto benchmark for evaluating HTP methods.
More generally, data quality and availability pose significant hurdles. High-quality, annotated trajectory datasets are often scarce, and the available data are frequently noisy and sparse. This scarcity hampers the ability of models to generalize to new or diverse environments, leading to performance degradation in real-world applications.

In terms of existing methodologies, while pooling methods help reduce computational complexity, they often sacrifice fine-grained details of individual behaviors. Graph-based models, such as GNNs and GCNs, enhance spatial modeling but typically treat nodes uniformly. This limits their ability to capture diverse social dynamics, a limitation that is somewhat addressed by GATs . Critical issues related to temporal dependencies and computational complexity persist. LSTMs and GRUs effectively capture short-term dependencies but struggle with vanishing gradients over long-range sequences
[
142
,
141
]
. Transformer-based architectures, while powerful in overcoming these gradient issues, introduce significant computational complexity due to their quadratic self-attention operations
[
187
]
.

Generative models also present challenges. GANs can be unstable during training, often experiencing mode collapse and vanishing gradients
[
144
]
. Meanwhile, diffusion models, although powerful, require extensive computational resources due to their iterative sampling processes
[
186
]
.

Another major challenge is the interpretability and explainability of complex models. As models become more sophisticated—integrating multiple modalities and deep architectures—their decision-making processes often become opaque. This “black-box” nature can be problematic for safety-critical applications, such as autonomous driving, where understanding and validating model predictions is essential for trust and effective error diagnosis. One possible idea to improve this aspect, developed in recent works
[
157
]
, is to rely on large language models, that may be queried to provide such explanations.

The choice of model and prediction strategies significantly impact the computational efficiency and accuracy of HTP. Trajectory generation strategies—whether direct, auto-regressive, target-conditioned, or refinement-based—each offer unique benefits and can be tailored to specific applications. At the same time, stochastic methods are crucial for capturing the inherent uncertainty in human motion, generating multiple plausible trajectories to reflect real-world variability.
The distinction between marginal and joint predictions is equally important: while joint predictions are more complex, they are crucial for modeling collective behavior, capturing scene dynamics, and better reflecting human-like understanding of social dynamics.

Current evaluation metrics, such as ADE, FDE, and their multimodal extensions (minADE, minFDE) quantify prediction accuracy but often fall short in assessing joint prediction performance and uncertainty
[
175
]
.
As HTP models advance, new metrics are needed to capture robustness and realism, addressing limitations in traditional evaluation methods while accounting for noisy input data and the inherent unpredictability of human decision-making.

5.3
Current Trends and Future Research Directions

To further advance multi-agent HTP, several research directions can be explored. One approach is to investigate hybrid models that combine graph-based reasoning with Transformer mechanisms, which could enhance the ability to capture complex social and temporal dynamics
[
48
,
100
]
. Furthermore, it is essential to improve the transparency and robustness of the model against sensor noise by incorporating explainable AI techniques
[
174
,
112
]
.

A promising direction in trajectory
prediction is the integration of large language models (LLMs), which offer strong generalization and semantic reasoning capabilities. TrajEvo
[
189
]
uses LLMs to automatically generate and refine prediction heuristics from trajectory data, improving generalization and interpretability. Traj-LLM
[
190
]
leverages LLMs to understand both agent behavior and scene context, while LMTraj
[
157
]
frames trajectory forecasting as a question-answering task, translating movement and visual data into natural language to support socially and contextually informed predictions. These works illustrate the growing potential of LLMs to advance trajectory prediction by enhancing flexibility, explainability, and adaptability.

Future research should also focus on the fusion of VAEs
[
166
]
, GANs
[
144
]
, and diffusion models
[
186
]
to achieve robust multimodal predictions while addressing training instability and reducing computational costs. Furthermore, there is a need to develop richer datasets that capture complex, social interactions between agents that go beyond simple collision avoidance and grouping
[
188
]
.
Recent advances in computer vision, graphics, and machine learning offer promising avenues for developing such datasets, from extracting accurate trajectories from monocular videos
[
191
,
192
]
to synthesizing complex social interaction scenarios using, e.g., physics-based simulators
[
193
,
194
]
, large foundational models
[
195
,
196
]
, and virtual reality
[
197
]
.

Lastly, given the limitations of ADE and FDE in assessing the predictive performance of HTP models
[
175
]
, there is also an increasing need for more sophisticated evaluation metrics.
Ideally, such metrics should: a) evaluate the joint prediction performance of all agents; b) account for noisy ground truth datasets; c) incorporate behavioral uncertainty; and d) be robust to out-of-distribution testing data. The development of more appropriate metrics will not only enhance the objective assessment of various HTP models but also provide critical insights for refining existing methods and guiding future innovations.

6
Conclusion

This survey provides a comprehensive overview of multi-agent human trajectory prediction, highlighting the significant progress made from 2020 to 2024. The field has transitioned from physics-based models, such as the Social Force Model
[
198
]
and the Optimal Reciprocal Collision Avoidance
[
4
]
, to data-driven, deep learning-powered approaches. These new methods include advanced sequence modeling, graph-based architectures, and generative models like VAEs, GANs, and diffusion models. This transition has led to substantial improvements in modeling the multimodal, uncertain, and interactive nature of human motion.

In spite of this progress, problems persist. Current methods still struggle to accurately capture complex social interactions and long-range temporal dependencies. Also, improvements are needed in evaluation methodologies and diversity in datasets, particularly due to the limitations associated with the current ETH/UCY benchmark datasets.
Future research should address these limitations by developing hybrid architectures, enhancing interpretability, and integrating emerging techniques such as large language models.

In summary, while significant progress has been made over the past five years, continued innovation in model design, evaluation metrics, and dataset development is crucial to fully harness the potential of multi-agent human trajectory prediction in real-world applications.
