Endogenous and/or exogenous origin of phenolic metabolites in humans (the ENDOPHENOL project): study protocol for a randomized, controlled, crossover trial
Authors: Laila Zeraik, Alice Rosi, Daniele Del Rio, Alan Crozier, Letizia Bresciani, Pedro Mena
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background(Poly)phenols are molecules with various structural complexities that are mostly present in fruits, vegetables, and some cereals, and are widely consumed daily. Following ingestion, the phenolic compounds that are not absorbed in the upper gastrointestinal tract—the majority—are metabolized in the colon to form low-molecular-weight phenolic catabolites (LMWP), which potentially make a substantial contribution to the biological activity of (poly)phenol-containing foods. However, the production of LMWP can also occur through the metabolism of the aromatic amino acids phenylalanine and tyrosine as well as via catecholamine pathways.MethodsA randomized, controlled, crossover trial in which healthy adults (n = 30) follow a very-low-(poly)phenol diet and restrict physical activity for 5 days is proposed. On the morning of the third day, subjects will consume either coffee (known source of (poly)phenols) or hot water (control).DiscussionThe main objective is the characterization of LMWP in biological samples through metabolomic techniques, attempting to distinguish the possible origin of these metabolites based on the different production of LMWP in the context of a controlled diet with or without a known source of (poly)phenols (primary outcome). Factors leading to interindividual variability and playing a role in phenolic metabolism, such as gut microbiota composition and genetic polymorphisms (secondary outcomes), will also be considered. Emerging evidence suggests that endogenous metabolism also contributes to circulating LMWP, which could lead to an overestimation of their attribution to dietary (poly)phenols. By implementing a long washout and using coffee as the test source, this study aims to capture background phenolic production and better disentangle dietary from endogenous contributions. These insights will support a more accurate understanding of the role of LMWP in nutrition and health.Trial registrationThis study was approved by the Territorial Ethics Committee (CET) of Area Vasta Emilia Nord (AVEN) (340/2023/SPER/UNIPR). Clinical Trials registration: NCT06028659. Registered on 23 August 2023.

Introduction

Background and rationale {9a}

Plant (poly)phenols consist of a broad number of organic molecules with phenyl units, varying in chemical structure complexity [1]. Produced as secondary plant metabolites, (poly)phenols are divided into two commonly accepted categories, namely flavonoids, which—in most cases—share a diphenyl structure linked by a heterocyclic pyran, and non-flavonoids, which comprise a varied set of compounds such as phenolic acids, phenolic alcohols, stilbenes, lignans, and hydrolysable tannins [2, 3]. They are widespread in fruits, vegetables, cereals and derived products, and plant-based beverages that constitute the human diet, mainly as glycosylated structures [4, 5] or, in the case of chlorogenic acids and related compounds, conjugated to quinic acid moieties, which are abundant particularly in coffee [6, 7]. In general, epidemiological studies have attributed many biological effects to (poly)phenol intake, and efforts have been made to elucidate their role in the health outcomes associated with a plant-rich diet [1, 8].

After ingestion, (poly)phenols follow the path of xenobiotics throughout the digestive system, with a portion being hydrolysed and absorbed before reaching the large intestine [5]. In the upper gastrointestinal tract, the smaller, more hydrophilic free forms of (poly)phenols are partially absorbed, while the most substantial fraction of the ingested (poly)phenols pass into the large intestine, where they are catabolized by gut microbiota enzymes before entering colonocytes [9]. Hence, the route of (poly)phenols from ingestion to the bloodstream results in their transformation into a plethora of typically smaller and phase II conjugated catabolites [10]. Most of these metabolites are C6-C1–3 phenolic acids or benzene derivatives known as low molecular weight phenolics (LMWP) [11], which have been linked to some of the reported biological activities of the ingested plant (poly)phenols [8, 9, 12, 13].

LMWP from dietary (poly)phenols potentially converge with metabolites derived from dietary aromatic amino acids and endogenous processes [14, 15]. Hippuric acid, for instance, is an abundant subproduct of the metabolism of many (poly)phenols, but it is also produced by the glycine deportation system, which uses benzoic acid as a means to excrete glycine in urine [16]. Benzoic acid is a common product of the breakdown of (poly)phenols, as are the aromatic amino acids phenylalanine and tyrosine, and, to a lesser degree tryptophan [11, 15]. Another converging pathway for LMWP production involves the metabolism of catecholamines: 3′,4′-dihydroxyphenylacetic acid is produced by the breakdown of dietary (poly)phenols by the gut microbiota. It is also a catabolite of dopamine and can undergo conversion to 4′-hydroxy-3′-methoxyphenylacetic acid, which is also a tyrosine catabolite and a microbiota-derived LMWP [17, 18].

In addition to the different possible sources of LMWP, there are factors of variability among individuals, such as age, sex, genetics, and microbiota diversity, which could lead to different concentrations and spectra of compounds in biological samples [19, 20]. Indeed, studies addressing the involvement of the gut microbiota in (poly)phenol metabolism indicate that varying microbial compositions can result in different (poly)phenol production profiles [21, 22]. Further complexity to the kinetic characteristics of LMWP is added by the host genetic fingerprint, influencing absorption and metabolism of these compounds. For instance, Haldar and collaborators have reported over 100 genetic tagging single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in candidate genes that are potentially related to the kinetics and metabolism of (poly)phenols [23].

Another variable to consider could be the rate of constant synthesis and breakdown of proteins for their renewal, namely, protein turnover, a dynamic process that can favour one direction or the other in protein and amino acid metabolism [24] influencing the endogenous production of LMWP. Among the various tissues contributing to protein turnover, the intestinal epithelium may be particularly significant due to its rapid renewal cycle, with complete cell turnover occurring every 3 to 5 days [25]. This high rate of epithelial cell turnover results in the continual release of proteins and peptides into the gut lumen, which can directly interact with and influence the gut microbiota and the metabolism of phenolic compounds. Despite its potential impact, the contribution of intestinal epithelial protein turnover to the pool of endogenous LMWP remains underexplored in studies on (poly)phenol metabolism and bioavailability.

In general, the current literature rarely includes a discussion on the origin of shared LMWP between endogenous and exogenous metabolism. Understanding the relative contributions of dietary (poly)phenols and host-derived proteins to circulating LMWP levels is essential for designing more effective dietary interventions aimed at modulating these bioactive metabolites. Taken together, the current evidence underscores the complexity of LMWP metabolism, where identical compounds may originate from both dietary (poly)phenols and the endogenous processes described above. The relative contribution of these sources, however, remains largely unknown. This study was therefore designed to disentangle exogenous and endogenous sources of LMWP under controlled very low-(poly)phenol dietary conditions, both at baseline and following coffee intake, while accounting for inter-individual variability linked to microbiota composition and host genetics, using targeted and untargeted metabolomics approaches. Here, targeted metabolomics refers to the quantitative analysis of a predefined set of LMWP known to occur in coffee [7] and, in some cases, also produced endogenously from amino acids or catecholamines [15], whereas untargeted metabolomics refers to the comprehensive profiling of phenolic and other compounds without prior selection [26] enabling the detection of metabolites whose origins are less well established.

Explanation for the choice of comparator {9b}

Coffee was chosen as the source of (poly)phenols since its phenolic profile consists of well-characterized and relatively small phenolic compounds, with its major compounds, namely chlorogenic acids, being catabolized into LMWP, the targeted molecules of our investigation [27–29]. Hot water was selected as the control drink.

Objectives {10}

The aim of this study is to characterize LMWP and assess their origin from exogenous (i.e. from dietary (poly)phenols and aromatic amino acids) and endogenous (from protein turnover and the amino acid pool, and catecholamines) production. To achieve these tasks, the objectives are as follows:i)To identify and quantify LMWP in biological samples in the context of a controlled very low-(poly)phenol diet.ii)To identify and quantify LMWP in biological samples after the intake of coffee in the context of a controlled very low-(poly)phenol diet.iii)To assess the ratio of exogenous/endogenous LMWP present in obtained samples.iv)To evaluate the influence of proteins on LMWP production considering the subject’s microbiota profile and genetic variation (SNP – analysis).

The primary aim of the study will be to discriminate between the exogenous (from dietary (poly)phenols and amino acids) and endogenous production of LMWP. A randomized crossover intervention study with human subjects will be carried out to achieve these goals. The other reported objectives are considered secondary objectives.

Methods: patient and public involvement and trial design

Patient and public involvement {11}

Patients and the public were not directly involved in the initial conceptualization or design of the ENDOPHENOL study. The research questions and outcome measures were primarily guided by existing scientific literature and the need to differentiate endogenous and exogenous sources of phenolic metabolites. While direct patient input was not sought in defining these objectives, the study aims to address broader concerns related to dietary (poly)phenol metabolism, which have potential implications for personalized nutrition and health research. The protocol was structured to minimize participant burden while maintaining scientific rigour, since participants were provided with comprehensive information regarding the duration of the dietary intervention, sample collection schedule, and lifestyle restrictions before consenting to the study. Public representatives did not assist in recruitment. Study findings will be shared with participants through accessible formats upon request. Key results will be disseminated through academic publications and conferences.

Volunteers’ flow during the study visits

Biological samples will be collected at selected timepoints. Subjects will be asked to collect all urine produced during the 60 h before the intervention, starting from the eve of the first day of diet (from 8:00 pm of day “S”—Fig. 2) and every 12 h until the morning of the intervention (–60 to –48 h,–48 to –36 h, –36 to –24 h, –24 to –12 h, –12 to 0 h, Fig. 2). On the morning of the intervention (visit 2, day 0), subjects will be requested to collect fasting spot urine and a single saliva sample for genotyping. Then, coffee or control will be provided along with a standardized very-low-(poly)phenol breakfast and water (ad libitum). Immediately after the intervention, subjects will be asked to continue the urine collection for the following 48 h (day 0, + 1 and + 2) over specified timepoints: 0–3; 3–6; 6–9; 9–12; 12–24; 24–36; and 36–48 h (Fig. 2). Finally, subjects will autonomously collect a faecal sample within 6 to 48 h after the intervention; volunteers will be provided with instructions to transfer an aliquot of the voided specimen into containers prepared for the microbiota and chemical analysis. Once the first arm is completed, subjects will resume habitual lifestyle for two weeks (wash-out), after which the crossover arm (another 4 days + 12 h) will start. Once handed over, urine and feces will be aliquoted and stored at –80 °C until chemical analyses. Saliva and fecal samples for genetic analyses will be kept under refrigeration.Fig. 2Biological sample collection time-points S1–S13: samples 01 to 13 indicating urine collected at specific timeranges; GN: single saliva sample for genetic profile; F: single fecal sample collected per arm). Methods: participants, interventions, and outcomes

Trial setting {13}

The ENDOPHENOL trial will take place at the Human Nutrition Unit facilities, Department of Food and Drug, University of Parma, and at the medical clinic of the “Division of Nutritional and Metabolic Sciences” of the Parma University Hospital (Parma, Italy).

Characteristics of the people who are needed for the trial

The characteristics of the subjects are described in the inclusion and exclusion criteria sessions.

Eligibility criteria for participants {14a}

Inclusion criteria

Apparently healthy adults, aged 20–40, with body mass index (BMI) between 18 and 28 kg/m2 and being regular coffee consumers (at least 1 cup a day).

Exclusion criteria

Clinical diagnosis of metabolic, cardiovascular, renal, or digestive diseases, immunodeficiency, or autoimmune diseases (other than well-compensated hypothyroidism), mental disorders, regular consumption of medication (except birth-control pills and hormone replacement therapies such as for hypothyroidism), use of antibiotics in the last month, food allergies or intolerances, pregnancy, or lactation.

Eligibility criteria for sites and those delivering interventions {14b}

The intervention study will be conducted at Parma University Hospital and University sites. Individuals responsible for delivering the intervention will include licensed medical professionals involved in participant enrollment, as well as trained University staff overseeing the implementation and monitoring of the dietary intervention.

Who will take informed consent? {32a}

During recruitment (visit 0), potential volunteers will be informed of the details of the protocol by the PI, or the staff designated by the PI, and of the risks associated with participation by the Medical Doctor involved in the study as a clinical member of the staff designated by the PI. Volunteers freely accepting to participate in the study will be asked for their written informed consent (Supplementary Material S1).

Additional consent provisions for collection and use of participant data and biological specimens {32b}

Participants of the study will also be asked to read all information regarding sample collection and data management, and sign forms for “Information and expression of consent to the processing of genetic data” (Supplementary Material S2) and “Information and expression of consent for the processing of personal data and biological samples” (Supplementary Material S3).

Intervention and comparator

Intervention and comparator description {15a}

Subjects will either receive three portions of decaffeinated coffee (50 mL each, totalizing 150 mL) or the same volume of hot mineral water, both containing the same amount of added sugar (5 g added through a commercially available single sachet), both to be consumed as a single serving of 150 mL. For the whole experiment duration, coffee servings will be prepared using the same coffee machine (Eletta Explore, De’Longhi Appliances S.r.l, Treviso, Italy), to standardize the brewing method and obtained volume, using commercially available coffee beans belonging to the same production, and the same brand of mineral water, thus avoiding variation in raw material.

Criteria for discontinuing or modifying allocated intervention/comparator {15b}

Volunteers will be free to withdraw from the study at any time regardless of motivation. The principal investigator and research staff conducting the study will have the right to remove volunteers from the study if there is proof of non-compliance with the protocol or for health-related reasons.

Strategies to improve adherence to intervention/comparator {15c}

In addition to receiving detailed information on the study objectives and procedures, participants will be asked to follow a controlled diet consisting of meals designed to require minimal preparation and effort. To monitor compliance with the study protocol, they will be asked to keep a food diary, recording the amount eaten and any side effects experienced for each meal consumed during the intervention, even if these are not expected to be causally related to the intervention.

Concomitant care permitted or prohibited during the trial {15d}

Participants will be instructed not to start new medications, dietary supplements (including probiotics, prebiotics, and high-dose vitamins), or change their dietary habits before stating the study. Regular medical treatments considered compatible with the protocol, such as oral contraceptives, thyroid hormone replacement, or use of paracetamol will be documented and recorded case by case.

Ancillary and post-trial care {34}

No ancillary or post-trial care is planned. The study will be covered by insurance in accordance with national regulations.

Outcomes {16}

Primary outcome

Urinary targeted metabolomics: Urine samples will be prepared for MS analysis as reported by Mena et al. [31] and will be subjected to targeted LC-MS analyses (quadrupole time-of-flight with ion mobility MS for identification and triple quadrupole MS for quantification purposes), to assess the pool of LMWP and to investigate differences between LMWP derived from dietary phenolics supplied through the controlled-low-(poly)phenol diet and the coffee treatment (intervention group), and LMWP produced from both dietary proteins contained in the controlled-low-(poly)phenol diet and endogenous production (control group) (primary outcome). Metabolites of interest will be identified and quantified using calibration curves of authentic standards, commercially available or already synthesized, when possible.

Secondary outcomes

Urinary untargeted metabolomics: Urine samples will also be prepared for untargeted LC–MS analyses using a quadrupole time-of-flight with ion mobility. This approach will allow us to investigate potential differences in metabolomes between samples collected within the control and the intervention group, shedding light on other metabolite changes beyond phenolic compounds. The analysis will be carried out as reported by Favari and collaborators [32]. This will enable the generation of a comprehensive picture of the impact of coffee (poly)phenol consumption on the human urinary metabolome.

Faecal (un)targeted metabolomics: The search for LMWP in subjects’ faeces will be carried out according to previous protocols [4, 33]. After extraction, faecal samples will be subjected to both targeted and untargeted LC-MS analyses to assess different profiles of produced but not fully absorbed LMWP in the context of the controlled diet with coffee or control. Metabolites of interest will be identified and quantified by LC-MS as previously described for (poly)phenol determination in urine samples.

Microbiota profiling: The analysis of faecal microbiota taxonomy at the species level will be performed according to published literature [34]. DNA from faecal samples will be extracted and targeted amplicon sequencing based on the internal transcribed spacer (ITS) protocol will be applied, targeting the entire spacer region between the 16S rRNA and 23 rRNA genes within the rRNA locus [35]. Data obtained will be processed and analyzed using QIIME [36] and appropriate databases for each experiment. Microbiota composition evaluation will be used to check for the influence of microbiota on LMWP production and to assess inter-individual variability in metabolite production.

Gene single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) analysis: Genotyping will be conducted on saliva samples using genome-wide, SNP array-based untargeted methodology. Commercially available SNP arrays (e.g. Illumina) detect gene variability across most of the genome using a tagging SNP approach. For our analysis, we will focus on genes known to be important in (poly)phenol bioavailability as a targeted approach, related to proteins involved in (poly)phenol absorption (e.g. LPH, SGLT1, GLUT2), transport/excretion (ABCB1, MRP1, BCRP, OAT1, etc.), and metabolism (UGT1A1, SULT1A1, SULF1, COMT, etc.). An untargeted pipeline will also be followed. Genetic differences will be studied as potential predictors of variation in exogenous/endogenous LMWP production and bioavailability.

Harms {17}

The very-low (poly)phenol diet will consist of a nutritionally balanced plan in terms of macronutrient and micronutrient content. Meals will be easily prepared by the volunteers using ready-to-eat commercially available food and dietary supplements, provided by the principal investigators. No harms are expected from the suggested diet.

The study will involve the consumption of water or coffee. No adverse effects are expected from water intake. However, due to the potential side effects of caffeine in sensitive individuals, decaffeinated coffee will be administered to minimize any risk.

Sample size {19}

Sample size calculation is an essential tool that contributes to the quality of study designs. However, in many metabolomics studies, exploratory approaches mean that the expected effect is often unknown, making sample size calculation a challenge. For this study, the sample size was estimated considering the urinary excretion of coffee-derived phenolic metabolites and the available literature on similar approaches [31, 37]. Stalmach et al. assessed the coffee-derived metabolites in urine samples of 11 subjects and detected compounds in nM concentrations from 0 to 24 h after 200 mL of coffee intake with a known concentration of chlorogenic acids [37]. To assess the urinary excretion of coffee-derived (poly)phenols in different doses, Mena et al. recruited 21 volunteers, also detecting metabolites at nM concentrations in urine considering the 24 h excretion period [31]. Thus, the number of 30 volunteers was established considering the accepted parameters for clinical trials (power set at 0.8 and α set at 0.05) [38], possible dropouts (15% drop-out rate), and exclusion of volunteers to maintain the sample power for statistical analysis.

Recruitment {20}

Volunteers will be recruited for the study in Parma (PR), using announcements placed in the university, hospital, and public places.

Assignment of interventions: randomization

Sequence generation: who will generate the sequence {21a}

To allocate subjects to each arm, a list will be generated—by a colleague not involved in subject enrolment—using a randomized block design using Random Number Generator Pro (Segobit Software).

Sequence generation: type of randomization {21b}

Restricted randomization will be adopted to achieve balanced 1:1 allocation between the coffee and control arms for the first study arm.

Allocation concealment mechanism {22}

The volunteers’ allocation list will be blind for PIs and volunteers, as it will be made by a colleague not involved in the subject enrolment and using a numbered sequence in sealed, opaque envelopes.

Implementation {23}

Information will be collected exclusively by a medical doctor, the principal investigator, or the specialized staff involved in the study. Recruited subjects will also be asked to sign an authorisation for the use of their personal information and data obtained.

Assignment of interventions: blinding

Who will be blinded {24a}

Until the day of intervention with coffee or control drinks, trial participants and the principal investigator will remain blinded.

How will be blinding be achieved {24b}

Envelopes will remain sealed and its content unavailable to all study participants and the principal investigator until the moment of preparation of the intervention drink (coffee or hot water). Due to the drink’s nature, it will not be possible to conceal the intervention in the second arm (V4, Fig. 1) once the first arm has been completed.

Procedure for unblinding if needed {24c}

A procedure for unblinding will not be needed.

Data collection and management

Plans for assessment and collection of outcomes {25a}

Personal data, including sex, age, ethnicity, smoking, alcohol consumption, health status for chronic diseases, food intolerances or allergies, use of medication or supplements, bowel function, menstrual irregularities, and information regarding factors potentially influencing gut microbiota composition of the volunteers will be collected through an enrollment questionnaire that has been submitted and approved by the Ethics Committee. Collected data will be recorded in a Data Collection Form submitted and approved by the Ethics Committee.

Plans to promote participant retention and complete follow-up {25b}

Participants will be contacted via email and/or phone 5–2 days before every visit to confirm appointment and participation in the next steps of the trial.

Data management {26}

Data will be stored in a computer with no internet connection dedicated to the function and protected with a password.

Confidentiality {33}

To assure privacy, participants will be pseudo-anonymized by the attribution of a personal alphanumeric code that will be used to identify all samples and data retrieved during the experiment.

Statistical methods

Statistical methods for primary and secondary outcomes {27a}

Data will be assessed using different softwares (SPSS, SIMCA, R, MetaboAnalyst, and others). Each analyzed variable will be checked for normal distribution to apply the most appropriate statistical strategy. For normally distributed data (which will be expressed as mean ± standard deviation), repeated measures t-test or ANOVA with post hoc comparisons will be performed. For non-parametric data (which will be reported as median and interquartile range), the Friedman test with post hoc pairwise comparisons will be performed. A p-value < 0.05 will be set for statistical significance. Multivariate approaches will be used to explore the inter-individual variability and will allow associations among data sets, following the procedures developed by our research group for targeted metabolomics analysis of phenolic metabolites [39, 40] or applying that proposed for untargeted metabolomics data [32].

Data from Tag SNPs and the microbial profiling will be analyzed following previous reports [41–43]. The relationship between patterns of LMWP and their putative determinants (age, sex, ethnicity, lifestyle, anthropometrics, body composition, genotype, and gut microbiome) will be explored using correlation tests and multivariate analyses. P-value < 0.05 will be regarded as statistically significant. Machine learning and data mining will be used [44–46] to integrate individual datasets.

Who will be included in each analysis {27b}

All participants who conclude the trial and deliver samples will be included in the analysis.

How missing data will be handled in the analysis {27c}

For specific time points where urine will not be collected by participants, data from those individuals will be excluded from the analysis for that particular time point.

Methods for additional analyses (e.g. subgroup analyses) {27d}

Unsupervised and supervised multivariate analysis (principal component analysis, PCA, and partial least squares–discriminant analysis, PLS-DA, respectively) will be used to identify and characterize the different urinary metabotypes, following the procedures developed by our research group for targeted metabolomics analysis of phenolic metabolites [22] or when using untargeted metabolomics data [47]. We will be using different clustering approaches (k-means, hierarchical, density-based, etc.) to better explore the existing variability. This will serve to establish the number of clusters (i.e. phenolic metabotypes), the number of participants forming each cluster and the similarities among metabotypes.

Discussion

Metabolic convergence of dietary (poly)phenols is an ongoing object of study, alongside the factors that lead to the reported differences in metabolite production and variability, such as food matrixes, the gut microbiota profile, intrinsic subject characteristics, available and chosen methodologies, and so on [15, 19]. Evidence on endogenous LMWP production, although limited, appears to suggest a significant contribution of these sources to the pool of bioavailable metabolites, implying plant (poly)phenols are not the sole source of these bioactive compounds [15, 48]. This could mean an overestimation of the concentration of LMWP attributed to the tested foods and beverages in the literature. Accordingly, steps must be taken to understand and report circulating LMWP species, concentrations, and possible sources, including the endogenous production and the contribution of dietary proteins, so we more accurately comprehend their role in nutrition and health. Following that premise, the Endophenol study was designed foreseeing a relatively long washout period, aiming to reach the decline and possible stabilization to the level of “background” phenolic production. The known source of phenolics is coffee, which means a more negligible food matrix effect with a characteristic set of LMWP with expected kinetics. Altogether, the study is, to the best of our knowledge, the first entirely devoted to the unveiling of phenolic metabolite sources. Further studies in the field may attempt to monitor physical activity in a more detailed way and assess, at the individual level, protein turnover at muscle level, intestinal epithelial protein turnover, and catecholamines levels.
