Utilizing 45 years of instrumental seismicity to investigate blind seismogenic faults in Southeastern Piedmont
Authors: Francisca Guiñez-Rivas, Sergio C Vinciguerra, Cesare Comina, Fabrizio Bosco, Guido Maria Adinolfi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn tectonically complex and data-limited regions, microseismicity offers a valuable tool for studying active fault structures and their activity. This study presents a robust methodology for investigating seismotectonics through instrumental seismicity, applied in Southeastern Piedmont, a region characterized by limited surface faulting and complex subsurface geometry. By integrating phase and parameter data from multiple seismic catalogues and applying rigorous quality-control and relocation procedures, we construct a high-resolution seismicity dataset spanning over 45 years. Focal-mechanism calculations and spatial-temporal clustering reveal a heterogeneous distribution of seismicity from the crust down into the lithospheric mantle (70 km), with strike-slip dominating focal mechanisms, influenced by the rheological and thermal structure of the lithosphere. The temporal distribution of seismicity reveals that earthquakes occur as mainshock–aftershock sequences repeatedly affecting the same volumes, indicating the presence of active and long-lived seismogenic structures within the Adria lithosphere. This study provides an updated analysis of seismicity in Southeastern Piedmont (Alessandria’s area), within the tectonic suture between the Alps and the Apennines, where inherited post-subduction structures control the segmentation into blind seismogenic faults. This work offers a robust framework for improving the understanding of post-collisional tectonic evolution and identifying hidden active faults.Subject terms: Natural hazards, Solid Earth sciences

Introduction

Accurately identifying and characterizing active seismogenic structures is fundamental for reliable seismic hazard assessment. Faults capable of generating earthquakes represent potential sources of future damaging events, and understanding their geometry, kinematics, and their activity is essential for informed risk reduction strategies1,2. However, in many regions, especially those with low to moderate seismicity rates, the identification of active fault systems remains a complex task3,4. This is particularly true when faults are simultaneously deeply buried and lack major seismic activity, making conventional geological and geophysical approaches limited or insufficient.

In structurally complex and data-limited environments, instrumental seismicity becomes the most valuable tool for imaging active fault structures and investigating their activity. Microearthquakes, although releasing small amounts of energy, may offer valuable information to identify active fault patches, and illuminate seismogenic sources at depth5–7. Their spatial distribution, temporal evolution, and focal mechanisms may reveal subtle fault interactions, cluster patterns, and stress orientations that cannot be identified via surface observations8. High-quality instrumental seismicity offers an independent dataset to complement geological and geophysical observations, enabling a more comprehensive understanding of the ongoing crustal deformation.

Southeastern Piedmont region (NW Italy; Fig 1) lies at the transition between the Western Alps, the Northern Apennines, and the Po Plain foreland basin, an intricate tectonic setting shaped by the interaction of crustal-scale thrust systems, inherited structural features and complex tectonic alignments3,4,9. Here, thick sedimentary cover and almost absent surface faulting hinder direct geological observations2,10,11 of potential fault sources. A detailed analysis of microseismicity using innovative methods in this area is therefore crucial to provide better insights into the number, dimensions, and kinematics of active faults in the region. Southeastern Piedmont hosts several urban centers and critical infrastructures, yet the level of knowledge regarding active faults remains incomplete.

In this work we aim to address this lack by constructing a 45 years comprehensive seismicity dataset of instrumental recordings. To achieve this, we integrate phase and parameter data from multiple seismic catalogues, apply robust quality-control procedures, and relocate events using both absolute and relative location methods, by using a 3D velocity model. The resulting dataset provides, for the first time, a high-resolution catalog of seismicity in the southeastern Piedmont area, offering unprecedented detail on its spatio-temporal distribution, seismogenic volumes activated at depth, and source characteristics.

Ultimately, the goal of this study is to improve the seismotectonic interpretation of the region and better relate the improved seismic catalog with the seismogenic sources. By enhancing the accuracy and quality of earthquake locations and source parameters, we contribute to a more reliable assessment of seismic hazard in a tectonically complex and densely populated area.

Seismotectonic context

Southern Piedmont lies within a complex tectonic transition zone where the Alpine and Apennine systems collide. The tectonic evolution of this region is primarily driven by the long-term convergence between the European and African plates, with Adria acting as a promontory or microplate of Africa. This convergence led to the closure of the Jurassic Ligurian–Piedmont Tethys ocean, followed by oceanic subduction and eventual continent–continent collision that built the Alpine orogen, including the Western Alps and the Piedmont sector of the Alps–Apennines junction3,13–15.

During the Tertiary, the crustal architecture of the Western Alps developed in response to the continued European–Adrian convergence and subduction4. The European continental margin acted as the lower subducting plate beneath the Adriatic upper plate, forming a subduction wedge composed of high-pressure metamorphic units, oceanic sediments, and fragments of continental crust from both margins4,9,14. Superimposed on these long-term processes is the anticlockwise rotation of the Adriatic plate3,14,16,17 which still continues to influence present-day deformation and seismicity patterns across the region18.

These combined processes have produced a marked lithospheric asymmetry across the Alps–Apennines transition3,4,14,15. The Alpine lithosphere is thick and mechanically strong, with the European Moho deepening eastward to > 70 km beneath Piedmont, whereas the Apennine lithosphere is thinner and weaker. Modern seismic and tomographic studies further document strong lateral variations in the geometry of the European slab along the Western Alpine arc, ranging from a well-preserved slab in the south to smooth crustal roots in the north3. A key structural feature in this context is the Ivrea Body, a high velocity and density lithospheric anomaly at the European–Adriatic boundary, which exerts a major influence on the distribution of seismicity and the orientation of the regional stress field3,4,13. Recent studies reveal a pronounced eastward deepening of the European Moho, consistent with the presence of a subducted slab beneath the overriding Adria plate4,9,13−15,16. In southwestern Piedmont, the Moho offsets rotate to become perpendicular to the belt, shifting from west to east as the Alpine arc merges into the thicker (> 45 km) Adria continental crust. Farther east, the European Moho continues to descend, reaching depths greater than 70 km4,14,19–25.

The Piedmont region today displays a complex present-day stress field. Regionally, deformation is dominated by strike-slip (transcurrent) regimes, with localized zones of extension in the internal Alpine arc and compression at the foreland margins3,13,14. This pattern reflects the combined influence of gravitational body forces driving orogen-perpendicular extension in the Alpine core and transpression along its flanks, as well as effects related to the anticlockwise rotation of the Adriatic plate relative to Europe3,13,14,17.

Although Southeastern Piedmont generally exhibits low seismicity, some moderate earthquakes (Md 4.2–4.8) have occurred, forming SW–NE clusters that are likely to reflect the activation of deep basement faults. The broader seismogenic framework of the region is thought to reflect the inheritance of the major pre-Burdigalian tectonic structures of the Tertiary Piedmont Basin, superimposed above the larger Alpine–Apenninic deformation field12,26. This includes the widespread basement involvement and thrust-related structures, with potential NW-verging structures extending beneath the Western Alps. Beneath the Po Plain, a NW–SE-trending fold with a short NNE-dipping limb has been interpreted as part of the Northern Apennines structural system27. The Monferrato area is underlain by the Tertiary Piedmont Basin resting a top the Ligurian and Sub-Ligurian basement, forming a complex framework shaped by reactivated Triassic–Jurassic extensional faults and later by compressional structures such as the Monferrato Arch27,28. This arch, mapped extensively between Turin and Alessandria, displays thrusting and detachment features that deform both Mesozoic carbonates and Cenozoic strata12,27,28. Owing to the structural complexity and the limited resolution of current subsurface models, the precise relationship between these geological features and the observed seismicity remains uncertain, as the thick (> 5 km)29,30–31 sedimentary fill of the Tertiary Piedmont Basin further obscures the underlying structural configuration and complicates the identification of active faults.

Historical seismicity in Southern Piedmont indicates low to moderate magnitudes but significant long-term activity. At least four earthquakes of Mw 5.5–6.5 have been documented over the last six centuries, the 1564 Lantosque (Mw 5.8), the 1808 Pinerolo (Mw 5.6), the 1828 Tortona (Mw 5.7), and the 1887 Diano Marina (Mw 6.3) events, although some of the seismogenic sources are likely to not belong to the region investigated32,1. Archival updates also reveal damaging sequences such as the 1771 Alba event (Io 6) and minor sequences in the Apennine foothills during 18781. Importantly, instrumental seismicity since 1985 shows spatial patterns consistent with historical macroseismic observations, supporting the reliability of both datasets32,1.

Instrumental seismicity provides additional constraints to the present tectonic deformation. Seismicity in the Western Alps and Piedmont is generally low to moderate (M < 4.5) and is mostly concentrated within the upper ~ 15 km in the internal Alpine domains, while locally deeper earthquakes (25–35 km) occur toward the Po Plain foreland3,13. The spatial distribution of focal mechanisms mirrors the regional tectonic pattern: dextral strike-slip motion predominates along the Alpine arc; discontinuous extensional faulting characterizes sectors such as the Briançonnais and Piémontais arcs; compressional regimes are largely confined to the Po Plain and parts of the western front.

Recent improvements in seismic monitoring, via dense networks have enhanced event detection and consequently focal mechanism resolution, confirming the dominance of transcurrent deformation combined with oblique extension in the Alps–Apennines transition. Recent seismic sequences near Genoa, such as the 2021–2022 Bargagli sequence, further highlight distributed strike-slip deformation in this transitional zone3,14,33–35.

These deep seismic alignments are consistent, according to several studies2,39,40 to strike-slip faulting and reactivated Mesozoic or Alpine–Apennine structures near the Monferrato area.

Results

We performed a cluster analysis on the temporal evolution of earthquakes in our catalogue employing the HDBSCAN (Hierarchical Density-Based Spatial Clustering of Applications with Noise) algorithm41 to test the geometry of the active faults and their temporal activation. From this analysis, we identified 12 clusters, with durations from 253 to 1487 days and containing between 54 and 133 events, shown on Fig. 3. Early activity (1980–1990 s) seems diffuse, not localised along the main structures or with a discernible pattern. From the 1990 s onward, clustering is observed, with multiple clusters activating over time. These include the clusters near Alessandria (clusters d, e, and f), Tortona (clusters f and j), Asti (clusters b and h), and along the north-western Apennine front (clusters g, h, i and j), consistently with the moderate-magnitude earthquakes documented in these areas. No single cluster dominates throughout the entire record; instead, Fig. 3 highlights a distributed pattern of intermittently reactivated fault systems.

Results of time-based clustering applied to the collected catalogue. Each panel represents a different cluster, with events belonging to the cluster shown in red and events outside the cluster shown in grey. Events with magnitudes above 3.5 within each cluster are highlighted with yellow stars.

Periods of increased activity, such as those identified in 2008, 2012–2013, 2020, and 2021, correspond to dense groupings of events in Fig. 3 (Clusters g, h, j, and k). These temporal peaks support the variability observed in the annual histogram (Fig. 2b).

Using the combined datasets, we extracted 1330 events within a 50-km radius of Alessandria and carried out earthquake relocation. Because S-wave arrivals generally exhibit larger uncertainties than P-wave picks, we applied a quality-control procedure based on the simil-Wadati criterion42, obtaining a Vp/Vs ratio of 1.7. Approximately 10% of the S-wave arrivals were discarded according to the quality threshold imposed.

The catalogue was then relocated using the NonLinLoc43 absolute location algorithm. To ensure robust solutions, we adopted a regional 3D velocity model44 that accounts for lateral variations in crustal structure not captured by standard 1D models28. This procedure yielded 796 well-located events, with a median RMS of 0.27 s and median horizontal and vertical uncertainties of 1.7 km and 2.5 km, respectively.

We applied double-difference (DD)45 relocation methods using the same 3D velocity model employed in the absolute location stage. After this refinement, 609 events retained high-quality and relatively stable locations. To ensure reliable depth estimations, we selected only those events with S-wave arrivals recorded at stations located close to the source relatively to the focal depth (twice its value). After applying these criteria, 421 earthquakes remained with well-resolved hypocentral depths, as shown in Fig. 4. From this refined dataset, we observe that most earthquakes occur at depths shallower than 20 km, with a subtle deepening trend toward the north. Seismicity in the southern part of the region (Tertiary Piedmont Basin and Western Alps) is the shallowest, with depths not exceeding 15 km. For the entire dataset, around 50% of seismicity falls between 10 and 20 km of depth, within the Adriatic basement46.

This dataset includes 40 focal mechanisms, calculated for events with magnitude above 2, which provide additional insights into the geometry and kinematic of the active faults as well as the stress regime of the area. Focal mechanisms were computed through a hybrid, non-conventional technique that combines traditional P-wave polarity readings with P/S low-frequency spectral-level ratios, significantly improving robustness, particularly for small-magnitude earthquakes40,47,48. Given the heterogeneous nature of the seismicity, both spatially and temporally, general conclusions on the mechanics of the whole structural systems affecting the region cannot be drawn. Instead, these patterns should be interpreted as the expression of multiple, but individual source zones, each activated episodically over time. In the central part of the study area strike-slip mechanisms prevail, consistently with a strike-slip stress regime previously identified in this area3,4,13,49. In contrast, the northwestern Apennines (eastern sector of our study) show a diverse range of focal mechanisms, including mostly normal-faulting solutions and occasional strike-slip and reverse faulting. Additionally, a limited number of normal-faulting mechanisms are observed in the western part of the region, indicating the mechanical variability across the study area.

To identify coherent seismogenic volumes and fault-aligned clusters, we carried out a spatial cluster analysis using HDBSCAN. A parametric analysis optimized the cluster quality, minimized noise, and it selected an appropriate resolution, allowing a more rigorous assessment of the spatial distribution of seismicity and its tectonic relationships. A total of 13 distinct spatial clusters were identified (Fig. 6), providing a framework for a more detailed examination of the active structures.

Discussion

The analysed seismicity is organized into 13 spatial clusters distributed over a wide depth range (4.1 to 70 km) within the Adriatic lithosphere, affecting both the lower and the upper crust, and it is characterized by moderate magnitudes (maximum Md 4.8). Focal mechanisms are predominantly strike-slip, although locally variable kinematic styles (62.5% strike-slip, 30% normal and 7.5% reverse) are also observed, indicating a strongly segmented and mechanically heterogeneous lithosphere in which deformation is efficiently partitioned across multiple structures acting at different depths. According to their spatial distribution and the main morpho-tectonic domains, the seismic clusters can be grouped into three principal areas: the Alessandria Basin, the Tertiary Piedmont Basin, and the Northern Apennines.

Alessandria Basin seismicity

The Alessandria Basin is a piggy-back basin within the Tertiary Piedmont Basin (TPB), mainly filled by Plio–Quaternary sedimentary successions. It represents one of the main depocenters of the TPB30,50. This region corresponds to the sector where a polarity change occurs between the Alpine subduction system, characterized by an east–northeastward-dipping slab, and the Apenninic subduction system, dipping toward the southwest, thus defining a suture zone marked by complex crustal- and lithospheric-scale structures15,16,35.

This basin hosts the most energetic seismic activity in our study area (six clusters in total, corresponding to numbers 1, 2, 4, 7, 8 and 11), with the region being home of the three largest earthquakes recorded (Md > 4). At the west of the city of Alessandria we see two clusters (fuchsia and blue) related to mainshock-aftershock sequences of the 2000 (Md 4.8) and 2001 (Md 4.2) earthquakes. As shown in Fig. 3, this structure exhibited no seismic activity prior to the 2000s. A detailed inspection of the hypocenters (Fig. 7) shows a shallower cluster between 5 and 15 km and exhibits a NE-trending structure. A deeper cluster occur between 15 and 25 km, showing a clear SE-dipping geometry. The only focal mechanism available comes from a ML 2.1 event from 2023, with strike-slip kinematics consistent with the results of Massa et al.26 for the 2000–2001 seismic sequences. In this framework, the SW-dipping faults identified at shallow depths within the Alessandria Basin by previous studies may represent the shallow expression of deeper seismogenic structures30,50.

Two seismic sequences occurred in the area near Tortona: the first related to the 2003 Md 4.5 earthquake (Fig. 3). The DD locations for this sequence are shown in Fig. 5. The yellow cluster (Fig. 6), located near Casale Monferrato, includes nine earthquakes occurring at depths of 60–70 km, which are aligned along a SW–NE trend.

In the Alessandria Basin, seismicity is predominantly distributed at intermediate to deep seismogenic volumes, mainly between ~ 10 and 40 km. In the central part of the basin, outside the main identified clusters, deep-seated earthquakes occur at depths of around 40 km, extending below the Adriatic Moho. Earthquake hypocentres delineate buried active structures with a prevailing SW–NE orientation and predominantly strike-slip kinematics. These structures concentrate and release the most energetic events of the studied area. Overall, hypocentral depths show a systematic increase toward the western and northern sectors of the study area, suggesting a structural control related to the geometry of the Adriatic lithosphere.

Tertiary Piedmont Basin-Western Alps seismicity

The Tertiary Piedmont Basin (TPB) is a syn-tectonic basin formed during the Neoalpine–Apennine evolution and filled by sedimentary successions from the Late Eocene–Early Oligocene to the Late Miocene15,30,51. It occupies an internal position between the Po Plain foredeep to the north and the Ligurian (Western) Alps to the south. The surface boundary between the Alpine and Apenninic domains is traditionally identified with the Sestri Voltaggio Zone, a km-wide, north–south–trending structural domain interpreted as a major tectonic boundary within the Alps–Apennines transition52,53.

In this area, three seismicity clusters can be identified (numbers 3, 9 and 10). The first two consist of a small cluster (brown) and a second cluster (dark blue; Fig. 3), for which focal mechanisms are available, with earthquake depths ranging from 15 to 22 km. The FMs display both strike-slip and normal kinematics.

At the southern edge of the study area, a red seismicity cluster is observed beneath the transition between the Tertiary Piedmont Basin (TPB) and the Western Alps. The seismicity is relatively high but distributed over a wide area and lacking a clear spatial trend. In this area seismicity has been repeatedly occurring over time (e.g., 1989–1992 and 1993–1999), without producing high-magnitude events or organized mainshock-aftershock sequences. Earthquakes are generally shallower than 15 km, consistent with upper-crustal seismicity. Five high-quality focal mechanisms, recorded between 2011 and 2021 (ML 2.0 and ML 3.1), show a consistent strike-slip kinematics and a SW–NE–oriented nodal plane, in agreement with the regional stress field and the overall hypocentral trend.

The depth distribution of earthquakes indicates that seismicity in the first two clusters occurs below the Cenozoic sedimentary cover, within Western Alpine, Ligurian, or Mesozoic carbonate units; whereas in the southernmost cluster it involves the exposed crystalline units of the Western Alps, as documented by geological cross-sections in previous studies52,54,55. Overall, these observations indicate that earthquakes are mainly generated by active structures rooted in the crust and hosted within mechanically strong and competent lithologies, rather than within the weaker sedimentary cover. This seismicity may be associated with strike-slip faults oriented orthogonally to the main compressional direction, consistently with structural models showing a complex subsurface architecture beneath the Northern Apennines characterized in this area by a NW–SE–oriented fold verging toward the NNE. Focal mechanism kinematics support this interpretation, showing steeply dipping nodal planes with a SW–NE orientation and a partial spatial alignment, indicating that these faults accommodate deformation at depth through brittle failure within brittle lithospheric domains.

No clear seismicity clusters can be identified in association with the Sestri–Voltaggio Fault Zone within the study area, indicating a lack of significant seismic activity along this structure during the analyzed period.

Northern Apennines seismicity

The Northern Apennines are characterized by a nappe stack topped by the Liguride Units, composed of fragments of the Ligurian–Piedmontese oceanic basin and its transition to the Adriatic continental margin54,55. These units, consisting mainly of Jurassic ophiolites and related sedimentary successions, were thrusts during the Oligocene–Miocene onto the external domains of the Adriatic margin. They record deformation related to oceanic closure and subsequent continental collision and are separated to the west from the HP/LT oceanic-derived units of the Voltri Group by the Sestri–Voltaggio Fault Zone. Villalvernia–Varzi Line is a major E–W–striking, steeply dipping fault zone that separates the External Ligurian and Epiligurian Units of the Northern Apennines from the Tertiary Piedmont Basin56–58. It played a key role in the Oligocene–Miocene tectono-sedimentary evolution of the area and in accommodating the westward indentation of the Adria microplate against the Western Alps.

Seismicity in the Northern Apennines shows a heterogeneous and strongly time-dependent behavior, with four main clusters identified (numbers 5, 6, 12 and 13). The first two clusters (blue and orange) include events that spatially follow the bending of the northernmost Apenninic arc between the Piedmont and Lombardy regions, to the north of the VVF. Earthquake depths generally are located between ~ 10 and 25 km, and seismicity is organized into distinct time intervals showing clear temporal migration patterns. These clusters, particularly the blue one, are characterized by many seismic events. Seismic activity migrated between the eastern and western sectors of the region, being concentrated in the east in 2008, shifting westward in 2012–2013, migrating again eastward in 2016–2018, and finally terminating back westward in 2019–2020. Focal mechanism solutions indicate that normal faulting with a preferential SSW–NNE orientation is the main mechanism taking place, consistently with the extensional regime of the Northern Apennines57–59. Strike-slip solutions are associated with E–W–trending structures, such as the Villalvernia–Varzi fault, which overlaps the southern part of the blue cluster, whereas reverse-faulting solutions observed in the northern sector remain controversial and may reflect localized thrusting.

Two seismicity clusters (brown and light green) are observed, south of the Villalvernia–Varzi fault, corresponding to the Ligurian Units. The brown cluster includes a limited number of events occurring at depths between ~ 12 and 15 km and it does not show distinctive features. In contrast, the light green cluster corresponds to a well-defined seismic sequence of about 30 events near Savignone, occurring between August and November of 2021, east of the Sestri–Voltaggio Fault, with a maximum recorded magnitude of ML 2.4 (Fig. 9). The focal mechanisms reveal SW-NE strike-slip kinematics, and the cross-sections indicate a well-defined SE-dipping structure extending between 6 and 8 km depth, showing a good agreement with the results of Eva et al., 202333.

From these results, it emerges that the brown and lime clusters are confined to the Ligurian Units33,53,60 and include shallow seismicity, with the lime cluster displaying strike-slip kinematics along a SW–NE trend. In contrast, the remaining clusters occur at significantly higher depths and they likely involve the Adria-derived units of the Tuscan domain and the external Umbria–Marche foreland, as well as the lower crust of the Adriatic lithosphere3,4,28. These deeper clusters are mainly characterized by transtensional deformation, consistently with the Apenninic extension, and follow the SW–NE trend of the external Northern Apenninic thrust front near Voghera15,28. Our analysis reveals that this volume does not appear to be currently active in compression.

Seismotectonic implications

The analyzed seismicity, together with its focal mechanism solutions, highlights the fundamental role of lithospheric-scale strike-slip deformation along this segment of the Adria–Europe plate boundary. Earthquake distribution and kinematic patterns indicate that deformation is mostly localized along active hidden strike-slip faults, where seismogenic volumes originate by the interplay of the stress field with contrasting rheological properties in agreement with previous studies56. Within this complex lithospheric architecture, earthquakes are predominantly concentrated in the upper crust and controlled by the brittle stress regime; however, seismicity is also persistently recorded down to ~ 70 km depth within the lithospheric mantle beneath the Adriatic Moho3,4,9,23,24,49 where thermal contrasts, fluid overpressures and mineral phase transformations may occur in association with relict slab fragments from a no longer active subduction system.

In detail the analyzed seismicity is primarily located within the Adriatic accretionary wedge, where earthquakes mainly nucleate in the upper crust, consistently with our observations, but they also extend to greater depths within the lower crust and the underlying lithospheric mantle. This depth distribution indicates that lithospheric domains subjected to similar stress conditions may accommodate deformation through brittle failure. These observations are also consistent with thermally controlled models of seismicity, which propose that earthquakes are largely confined to upper crustal regions colder than ~ 300 °C (around 10 km of depth), while seismic rupture may also occur in lower crust domains if temperatures remain below ~ 600 °C (around 60–70 km depth under average thermal gradient)56,58,. The present results provide direct empirical support for these predictions. In this context, the mineral phase transformations such as mantle serpentinization represent an additional key factor, as they may promote brittle processes at depth and continuity of seismicity within deeper volumes.

Overall, these findings may have important implications for the active tectonic interpretation of collisional zones, including the orogenic belts formation. Our results underscore the controlling role of lithospheric architecture and the geometric relationship between deep seismicity and the subduction in shaping the seismic behaviour of post-collisional settings. This seismic pattern points to a deformation regime controlled by the slab dynamics and/or the reactivation of inherited mantle rigid indenters or geological structures. Shear-wave tomography reveals several rigid mantle bodies, in good agreement with inherited indenters, among which the Adriatic Lithospheric Mantle represents a main one. At the lithospheric scale, our results suggest that the mechanics of the indenter when overridden by weaker lithosphere may generate a positive tectonic flower structure4. The horizontal compression associated with mantle indentation may result in a regional deformation pattern dominated by strike-slip tectonics, possibly accompanied by Moho slicing or Moho duplication, and expressed across the Po Plain, the European foreland, and the Alps–Apennines junction.

Conclusions

This study provides a comprehensive and updated analysis of seismicity in southeastern Piedmont (Alessandria area), located within the tectonic suture between the Alps and the Apennines. Unlike most previous studies, which focus on the Western Alps, this work targets the relatively understudied flat areas of Southeastern Piedmont, where the geometry and activity of (hidden) seismogenic structures remain poorly constrained. By compiling and analyzing an extensive 45-year seismic database—including earthquake relocations, focal mechanism calculations, and spatio-temporal clustering—this work contributes to a better understanding of the stress acting in a region whose seismotectonic framework and seismogenic faults remain subject to ongoing debate.

Here we summarize the main findings.

Seismicity in Southeastern Piedmont is organized into distinct clusters related to active, segmented, and largely hidden seismogenic faults, dominated by lithospheric-scale strike-slip deformation3,4,13,14 (62.5% of focal mechanisms being strike-slip), with no clear evidence of compressive-related seismicity associated with the Alpine or Apenninic thrust fronts.

Materials and methods

We merged three independent seismic catalogues to build a comprehensive 45-year database of seismicity in Southeastern Piedmont . These catalogues provide both parametric event information and phase-arrival data recorded by stations belonging to several seismic networks with different levels of coverage. Integrating these datasets is essential to improve temporal consistency, enhance spatial completeness, and reduce biases associated with individual networks (Fig. 10a, b).

Representation of the collected database. (a) Histograms showing the number of events per year, coloured according to the catalogue (CLASS, RSNI, INGV). (b) Histograms showing the number of events per magnitude, with colours corresponding to the catalogue. (c) Simil-Wadati diagram for all phase arrivals collected from the three catalogues. The best-fit line, computed using the Huber fit, is shown as a dashed red line. Selected phase arrivals are depicted in blue, while discarded phase arrivals are shown in grey.

The first catalogue is CLASS36 (Catalogo delle Localizzazioni ASSolute), which includes a revised set of earthquakes located throughout the Italian peninsula using a probabilistic approach and a 3-D velocity model. The phase arrivals available within CLASS correspond to those published in CSI61 (Catalogo della Sismicità Italiana), covering events from 1981 to 2008. The second catalogue is the one published by the Regional Seismic Network of Northwestern Italy (RSNI38), managed by the University of Genoa, which provides event parameters and phase-arrival information from 2012 to the present. Finally, we included the INGV37 (Istituto Nazionale di Geofisica e Vulcanologia) seismic catalogue, containing events and phase arrivals from 1980 to the present.

Using these combined datasets, we selected all earthquakes occurring within a circular area of 50-km radius centred on the city of Alessandria. A total of 1330 events were identified within this region. When duplicate events were present across catalogues, we adopted a hierarchical priority scheme that reflects network density and expected data quality: RSNI was assigned highest priority (given its dense instrumental coverage within the study area), followed by CLASS, and finally INGV. After merging, we extracted information from 355 seismic stations belonging to various networks. The same priority was assigned when merging phase arrival times from the different catalogues.

We applied a quality-control procedure based on the simil-Wadati criterion42, to assess the reliability of both P- and S-arrival times. For each event, we computed the time differences (tsi -tsj) vs. (tpi - tpj) for all station pairs, thus building a simil-Wadati diagram. The central trend of this relationship, corresponding to the regional Vp/Vs ratio, was estimated using a Huber robust regression62. The Huber estimator behaves like a least-squares (quadratic) fit for small residuals but transitions to a linear penalty for large residuals, reducing the influence of outliers without discarding data points outright.

The resulting Vp/Vs ratio obtained from the full dataset was 1.70. These results are in line with the values known for this region and with those presented in the literature3644,. Using the fitted trend, we used an automated iterative procedure to remove all arrivals whose residual distance from the regression line exceeded 2 s. This filtering ensures mutual consistency among phase arrivals and reduces the impact of mispicked S phases. After applying this criterion, approximately 10% of all S-wave arrivals were discarded, ending up with a total of 21,022 P-arrivals and 13,156 S-arrivals (Fig. 10c).

The merged catalogue was relocated using NonLinLoc43, which implements a fully non-linear, probabilistic approach for absolute earthquake locations. To ensure high-quality solutions, we adopted a regional 3D velocity model44, allowing us to account for lateral variations in crustal structure that cannot be captured by standard 1D models28, especially considering the tectonic complexity of the region. A total of 167 seismic stations contributed to this step, and station corrections were estimated during the inversion. This procedure resulted in 796 well-located events, with a median RMS of 0.27 s and median horizontal/vertical uncertainties of 1.7 and 2.5 km, respectively.

To further refine event locations, we applied the double-difference relocation algorithm HypoDD45. HypoDD uses differential arrival times between pairs of nearby earthquakes recorded at the same station to reduce path-dependent biases and significantly improve relative hypocentral precision. The method simultaneously minimizes residuals of observed and predicted differential times while holding the velocity structure fixed. For the DD procedure, we consistently employed the 3D velocity model for both ray tracing and the estimation of station-specific arrival times. The DD earthquake-location inversion was performed using 5 iterations grouped into 5 sets, each solving the linearized equations via a least-squares approach. We used only phase-arrival times with uncertainties smaller than 0.5 s. To compute the travel time differences, the maximum hypocentral distance was set to 20, the maximum number of neighbors was set to 10, the minimum number of links was set to 8, and the minimum and maximum number of observations per event pair was set to 8 and 20 respectively.

After relocation, 609 events retained high-quality, stable relative locations.

Estimating earthquake depth is generally more challenging than constraining epicentral coordinates. Depth resolution is strongly controlled by the S–P time differences at stations located close to the epicenter. Only stations at short hypocentral distances provide a sufficiently steep raypath geometry to resolve depth, whereas distant stations yield nearly horizontal raypaths that contribute little vertical constraint63,64.

To obtain accurate depth estimates, we applied a filter based on the ratio of depth to the distance of the nearest station with an S-wave arrival. We retained only those events for which at least one S-wave was detected at a station located at a distance similar to the event’s focal depth (up to a maximum of twice the depth). This criterion ensures that only events with well-constrained depths, based on station geometry, are included. After applying this filter, 421 earthquakes remained with well-resolved hypocentral depths.

Focal mechanisms were computed using a hybrid method that combines P-wave polarity readings with P- and S-wave spectral-level ratios. P- wave polarity readings were obtained automatically by analyzing waveform windows around the P arrival and extracting waveform features. These features were subsequently classified using UMAP65, a dimension reduction technique, and HDBSCAN, a density-based clustering algorithm, which enabled robust identification of up/down polarities through waveform similarity.

Spectral levels for both P and S waves were computed using TESLA47, an automated procedure that fits the low-frequency amplitude level in the Fourier domain. The spectral inversion was conducted in the frequency band 0.5–40 Hz, yielding stable and internally consistent P/S amplitude ratios.

Focal mechanisms were obtained through a fully Bayesian grid-search approach implemented through BISTROP41. The inversion began with a uniform prior distribution over strike, dip, and rake. A full grid search was performed with a 2° angular step, and for each parameter combination, the likelihood was computed based on the joint fit to polarity data and spectral ratios. The final mechanism corresponds to the Maximum A Posteriori (MAP) solution.

Events used for focal mechanism computations were selected based on magnitude (M > = 2), good quality DD-locations with a good relation of depth vs. nearest station, and good azimuthal coverage (station gap < 200°). In the end, a total of 400 polarities and 193 spectral level ratios were used for inversion, yielding a total of 45 focal mechanisms with a median of 7 polarities and 3 spectral level ratios per event.

To evaluate solution stability, we examined the distribution of Kagan Angles (KA) between the MAP focal mechanism and all solutions with posterior probability > 90%. The Kagan Angle measures the smallest rotation needed to align the principal axes of two mechanisms, providing a quantitative assessment of their similarity. Following the criteria of Adinolfi et al. (2022)66, this threshold balances robustness and resolution, ensuring that only the most probable solutions contribute to the stability assessment. The median Kagan Angle of this distribution was used as a measure of internal consistency: focal mechanisms with median KA < 30° were classified as well constrained.

Using this framework, we obtained 40 high-quality focal mechanisms for earthquakes within the study region, discarding 5 fault plane solutions. These results are shown in Figs. 11 and 12. It is worth noting that using the traditional approach of only polarities, only 35 focal mechanisms could be computed, from which only 29 had a KA median < 30°.

To investigate the geometry of the seismogenic structures and their activation through time, we applied a cluster analysis to both the spatial distribution and temporal evolution of earthquakes in our catalogue. The methodology follows a density-based approach which employs the HDBSCAN algorithm due to its ability to identify clusters of arbitrary shape, adapt to variable event densities, and separate noise from meaningful seismic patterns67,68–69.

The selection of the min_samples and min_cluster_size parameters in HDBSCAN was evaluated through a grid-search approach, exploring different parameter combinations within predefined ranges. To streamline the choice of the optimal configuration and improve clustering performance, we followed a Clustering Optimization Index (COI) that integrates three metrics: the Calinski–Harabasz Index70, the number of noise points, and the number of identified clusters (Fig. 13). This composite index prioritizes parameter sets that maximize clustering quality, minimize noise, and produce an appropriate number of clusters. The COI functions as a weighted average designed to optimize these outcomes simultaneously.

Evaluation of HDBSCAN clustering performance across different min_cluster_size and min_samples parameter combinations for the time cluster analysis. The four panels display various evaluation metrics: Calinski-Harabasz Index (a), number of identified clusters (b), number of noise points (c), and the custom metric developed in this study, the Combined Optimality Index (COI) (d). Contour lines are overlaid on each panel to better illustrate the variation of metric values across the parameter space. In panel (d), the 90th and 75th percentiles are highlighted with red and orange lines, respectively.

For the spatial cluster analysis, earthquake hypocenters were transformed into a uniform metric coordinate system and used to compute a Euclidean distance matrix, allowing the algorithm to delineate coherent seismogenic volumes and fault-aligned clusters. We systematically varied the min_cluster_size and min_samples parameters from 3 to 100 in steps of 3. The COI was used as a guide to identify the most suitable parameter combinations, by jointly evaluating cluster compactness, the proportion of noise points, and the resulting number of clusters. This approach allowed us to balance sensitivity to small clusters with the preservation of meaningful structural features.

The temporal evolution of seismic activity was analyzed by providing HDBSCAN with a pre-computed matrix of absolute time differences between event origin times, enabling the identification of temporally correlated sequences and activation phases. For the temporal clustering, min_cluster_size and min_samples were varied between 10 and 1000 in increments of 10, and the COI was used to select the optimal configuration. The index evaluates temporal cluster coherence, noise, and cluster count, allowing the identification of settings that most effectively reveal seismicity bursts and their evolution.
