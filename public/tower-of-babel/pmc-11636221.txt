Curve-on-curve technique is more reliable than free-floating technique for tibial components positioning in posterior stabilized total knee arthroplasty using symmetrical tibial components in varus osteoarthritis
Authors: Mohamed Elkabbani, Ahmed Mostafa Saied, Bassam Ali Abouelnas, Apostu Dragos, Amr Osman, Samih Tarabichi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
IntroductionMalrotation of the tibial components in total knee arthroplasties is a common cause of pain and functional impairment. There are multiple methods used to establish the tibial component rotation, but there is still no consensus which method is the best.The objective of this study was to compare two of the most commonly used techniques, that is the use of self-alignment during passive range of motion (free-floating technique) versus the anterior cortex referencing method (Curve-on-curve technique).Materials and methodsTwenty-eight consecutive patients with advanced varus-type osteoarthritis scheduled for posterior stabilized total knee replacement with symmetrical tibial baseplate were included in the study. We set the location of the tibial component trial using the method of self-alignment during passive range of motion and compared it to the location of the tibial component trial when referenced to the anterior cortex. The distance between the two locations was independently measured by two experienced surgeons.ResultsIn all of the cases, the tibial component centre was located more laterally on the anterior tibial cortex in the anterior cortex referencing technique when compared to the self-alignment technique [range 3–19 mm].ConclusionThe tibial components placed using the anterior referencing technique (Curve-on-curve technique) are more externally rotated as compared to those placed using the self-alignment technique (free-floating technique) in posterior stabilized total knee arthroplasties using symmetrical tibial components.

Introduction

Total knee arthroplasty (TKA) is a commonly performed surgery used to treat advanced knee osteoarthritis, with overall good results in terms of pain reduction and function improvement [1]. There are many factors which influence the result of a total knee replacement, including implant design, the surgical technique and the postoperative rehabilitation. The rotational malalignment is one of the causes which can produce pain and functional impairment [2, 3].

While there is consensus about the transepicondylar axis as a reference for the femoral rotational alignment, there is no consensus on ideal tibial component rotation [4]. The goal of tibial component rotational alignment in primary TKA is to achieve parallelism between the femoral trans epicondylar axis and the medio lateral axis of the tibial component on the coronal plane.

Several techniques have been mentioned for intraoperative tibial component positioning, including anterior cortex referencing (curve on curve technique), medial third of tibial tubercle referencing, and self-alignment of the trial during passive range of motion (free floating technique) [5–7]. The advantage of the self-alignment technique during passive motion could be represented by considering the femorotibial kinematics. On the other hand, a disadvantage of this technique is that it can misplace the tibial component in the case of a deficient femoral component rotation.

In this study, we compared two commonly used techniques to set the tibial component rotational while performing TKA, the anterior cortex referencing and the self-alignment techniques. Our hypothesis is that the anterior technique allows for more externally rotated tibial components when compared to the self-alignment technique.

Methods

Twenty-eight consecutive patients with advanced varus-type osteoarthritis scheduled for posterior stabilized total knee replacement using cemented components and symmetrical tibial components were included in the study. We excluded patients with history of previous knee surgeries or trauma. All of the patients underwent informed consent prior to surgery.

All of the patients had perioperative management consisting in 1.5 g of cefuroxime and 1 gram of tranexamic acid at the induction of spinal anesthesia. A tourniquet was inflated to 100 mmHg above the systolic blood pressure before skin incision. The surgery was performed according to the standard surgical technique for cemented posterior stabilized total knee arthroplasties in a high-volume arthroplasty institution. All surgeries were done by one high-volume arthroplasty surgeon, using the subvastus approach and a cemented posterior stabilized TKA (NexGen; Zimmer Biomet, Warsaw, Indiana). A second surgeon would assist during the surgery. This system has symmetrical tibial components.

After performing femoral notch cuts, soft tissue balancing including sequential medial releases was performed and then the properly sized trial components and insert were used. In each patient, both the self-alignment and the anterior cortex referencing techniques were used. For the self-referencing technique, the knee is then brought into two full cycles of flexion and extension to allow the tibial trial component to be freely positioned on the cut tibial surface. Then the center of the trial tibial component is marked with diathermy on the anterior tibial border.

All trial components are then removed, and with the knee flexed and tibial translated anteriorly, the same sized tibial trial component is placed following the anterior tibial border without any anterior overhang, according to the anterior tibial cortex referencing technique [8, 9] and fixed with pins. The center of the tibial trial component is then marked with diathermy again in its new position and the distance between the two markings is measured using a measuring device.

The distance between the two diathermy markings were then measured by two senior orthopedic surgeons with a ruler (Fig. 1). In all cases, the anterior cortex referencing method was used for final positioning and cementing of the tibial component. The surgeon would administer ropivacaine at the level of the wound and in the surrounding soft tissues. The patients were performed a femoral nerve block postoperatively. The patients underwent a standard rehabilitation protocol which included passive range of motion, muscle strength training, active range of motion and walking training. (Fig. 1)

Results

The study included 19 females (68%) and 9 males (32%). Their mean age was 60.7 ± 6.9 years (range: 49–73 years). The right side was done for 18 cases (64,3%) and the left side for 10 cases (35,7%). The size of femoral component was size D in 7 cases, size E in 13 cases and size F in 8 cases. The size of tibial tray was size 3 in 11 cases, size 4 in 8 cases and size 5 in 9 cases. The height of polyethylene insert was size 10 in 14 cases, 12 in 11 cases and size 14 in 4 cases.

The mean distance between the centers of the trial tibial components positioned through the anterior cortex referencing and self-alignment referencing was 12.33 ± 4.15 mm (range: 3–19 mm). After the lead surgeon positioned the tibial trial, both surgeons performed the above measurements independently for both the anterior cortex referencing and self-alignment technique. The Pearson correlation test between the two surgeons’ measurements of the distance between the centers of the trial tibial components being positioned through the anterior cortex referencing technique and self-alignment referencing technique was 0.98. In all cases, the centers of the trial tibial components being placed using the anterior cortex referencing technique were located more laterally on the anterior tibial border rather than the centers of the trial tibial components being placed through the self-alignment technique. As the same sized trial tibial component was used in each case by its own, as well as no lateral overhang was accepted in any case during measurement of either methods, then any difference in measurements between the two methods can only be explained by rotational rather than translational differences. This means that the anterior cortex referencing involves more external rotation of the tibial component compared to self-alignment technique.

The mean difference was lower in female patients, compared to male patients (8.63 [±4.05] vs. 11.6 [±3.77]; p = 0.06). No correlations were observed between mean distance and femur sizes, tibia sizes and age. (Fig. 2)

Discussion

Malrotation of tibial and femoral components can lead to patellofemoral maltracking, increased wear, decreased range of motion and an increased incidence of revision surgery [10–13]. Tibial component malrotation occurs more frequently than femoral component malrotation and is even more debilitating -especially in internal rotation - compared to femoral malrotation with subsequent unexplained pain, stiffness, and patellar complications [14, 15]. Moreover, the design of the tibial implant plays an important role in the rotational alignment [16]. The anatomic design of the tibial plate has a more accurate rotational alignment and a better bone coverage compared to symmetric and asymmetric tibial implants [16].

Moreover, the malrotation of the tibial component is hard to diagnose using clinical and x-ray examinations. More advanced imaging including computed tomography (CT) scan are needed [17]. Isolated tibial component malrotation alters patellar tracking and/or polyethylene loading in cadavers with some implants, including those with mobile bearing inlays [18]. A study of painful knees after TKA found that 44% had significant rotational errors of the tibial tray [8]. Some studies show a range of tibial component malpositioning exceeding 30° [19].

The Study Group of European Knee Associates showed that an internal rotation of more than 3° causes a higher patellar contact stress, an internal rotation of more than 5° causes anterior knee pain and an internal rotation of more than 10° can lead to stiffness [20]. A combined maltracking of the femoral and tibial components has an increased rate of anterior knee pain, synovitis, and revision surgery [21].

Revision surgeries are an important socioeconomic burden on the patient and medical care. Revision surgeries are generally complex, high-cost and result in a lower functional outcome compared to primary total knee replacements.

Internally rotated tibial component is associated with an anteriorly displaced posterolateral corner, leading to a limitation of the posterior femoral roll back mechanism which occurs in the lateral compartment, especially in the case of an intact posterior cruciate ligament. A study showed that internal malrotation of the tibial component reduces the ability of the tibia to externally rotate related to the femur, thereby limiting full knee extension and increasing the risk of flexion contracture in total knee replacements [22]. The self-alignment technique after knee flexion-extension cycles can transfer the femoral rotational error to the tibial component, especially when the posterior condylar axis is used [23]. The transepicondylar axis is a more reliable referencing compared to the posterior condylar axis, thus limiting the malrotation of the femoral component and tibial component respectively, when a self-alignment technique is used [23]. Another study showed that this technique is more efficient compared to the tibial tubercle method for a more consistent reproduction of tibial component rotation for less experienced surgeons [24]. There is conflicting data about the clinically tolerable limits of tibial component internal malrotation. (6°- 9°) [25, 21]. A cutoff point for revision of mal rotated TKA components has not yet been identified [19].

As tibial component external rotation is more tolerated rather that tibial component internal rotation, it is better intraoperatively to opt for external rather than internal rotation of the tibial component if optimal rotation is not guaranteed [8, 26]. Furthermore, tibial component internal rotation must be excluded whenever unexplained knee pain is present [27].

In this study, we compared the self-alignment and the anterior cortex referencing technique, but another method is by using the Akagi line. This line connects the middle of the posterior cruciate ligament to the medial border of the patellar tendon. Nevertheless, the reproducibility of the Akagi line intraoperatively is poor, as stated by Kawaguchi et al. [28]. Other landmarks such as the medial third of the tibial tubercle was found to be less reliable than anterior cortex referencing [9]. Our goal was not to indicate which one of the two techniques is better in obtaining ideal rotation, but we were aiming to see the difference between the center of rotation of the two methods. Moreover, due to the emerging understanding of the importance of tibial component rotation in TKA, it is very important not only to identify the ideal tibial component placement technique, but also to do it reproducibly. As in all cases, the anterior cortex referencing technique resulted in more external rotation values for the tibial components compared to the self-alignment tibial techniques, we would suggest this technique for less experienced surgeons or during their learning curve for total knee arthroplasty as tibial component external rotation is more tolerated rather that tibial component internal rotation.

Limitations of the study include small sample size, inclusion of only varus-type osteoarthritis, as well as lack of preoperative evaluation of the tibia and femur’s rotation resulting in inability of correlating their rotation to the resulting mean distance created. Intraoperatively, only symmetrical tibial baseplates were used; therefore, we cannot extend our results to asymmetrical or anatomical tibial base plates. In addition, using a ruler - instead of a caliper - can reduce the accuracy of intraoperative measurements. Moreover, the fact that a single lead surgeon performed all the surgeries can lead to biased intraoperative measurements which can further affect the study’s results. Finally, we did not perform an analysis of the cost and intraoperative time, which would be helpful in determining the overall efficiency and effectiveness of each approach.

The study compares two commonly used techniques in determining the tibial component rotation. Future research including controlled clinical trials including larger sample size should be performed to compare the use of both techniques in terms of postoperative patient reported outcomes and clinical follow-up. Moreover, clinical studies that compare the intraoperative measurements with postoperative computed tomography measurements would offer important benefits for a better understanding of the tibial component rotation.

Conclusion

Curve on curve with anterior cortex referencing technique places the tibial components into more external rotation as compared to the self-alignment free floating technique in posterior stabilized total knee arthroplasties for varus osteoarthritis. The anterior cortex referencing is more reliable, and we recommend its use especially amongst training surgeons.
