SMOC2 promotes peritoneal metastasis of gastric cancer involving Wnt/β-catenin pathway modulation and inducing angiogenesis
Authors: Chaoqun Li, Luxi Yin, Duqin Zhao, Yuchao Jiang, Xinnuo Li, Qian Yin, Jiangli Xu, Yihui Huang, Jiaojiao Ni, Jieer Ying
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractGastric cancer (GC) is a leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide, with peritoneal metastasis commonly seen in advanced cases. Based on RNA-sequencing data from GEO dataset, we focused on the role of SMOC2, SPARC-related modular calcium-binding protein 2, in regulating GC progression and peritoneal metastasis. Our analysis revealed that high SMOC2 expression was associated with poor survival in the population with peritoneal metastases. Functional assays demonstrated that SMOC2 promotes the migration and invasion of GC cells in vitro. In vivo experiments using a mouse peritoneal metastasis model showed that SMOC2 promoted the peritoneal metastasis of GC cells. Additionally, in a co-culture system of GC cells and endothelial cells, SMOC2 overexpression in GC cells promoted the proliferation, migration, and tube formation capability of endothelial cells. Mechanistically, SMOC2 knockdown reduced GSK-3β phosphorylation and nuclear β-catenin levels, accompanied by decreased expression of downstream target genes. These results indicate that SMOC2 promotes GC peritoneal metastasis involving Wnt/β-catenin pathway modulation. In conclusion, our findings highlight SMOC2's significant role in GC progression, potentially involving Wnt/β-catenin pathway modulation and angiogenesis. These results position SMOC2 as a potential prognostic biomarker and therapeutic target for GC peritoneal metastasis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10238-025-01914-6.Keywords: Gastric cancer, SMOC2, Peritoneal metastasis, Angiogenesis, Wnt/β-catenin signaling

Introduction

Gastric cancer (GC) is one of the most common malignancies and a leading cause of cancer mortality worldwide, ranking as the fifth most prevalent neoplasm and the fourth most deadly tumor [1]. In metastatic cases, direct seeding into the peritoneal cavity represents the most frequent route [2, 3]. The prognosis for these patients is very poor, with an untreated 5-year survival rate of only 2%, and chemotherapy is typically the primary treatment [4, 5]. However, its efficacy is often limited by inadequate drug delivery and complications such as intestinal obstruction and abdominal distension. Ongoing research has shed light on the mechanisms of peritoneal metastasis in gastric cancer and led to emerging treatment strategies, yet effective therapies remain limited and clinical needs unmet [5, 6]. Therefore, it is crucial to investigate the genes involved in GC peritoneal metastasis and to design corresponding targeted interventions.

Peritoneal metastasis involves multiple steps: detachment of cancer cells from the primary tumor, survival in the peritoneal microenvironment, adhesion to peritoneal mesothelial cells, invasion of the basement membrane, and the growth of metastatic lesions [7, 8]. A crucial aspect of this process is angiogenesis, which provides the necessary nutrients for the establishment and growth of peritoneal metastatic nodules through the formation of new blood vessels. In the peritoneal microenvironment, various cellular components contribute to pro-angiogenic signaling that specifically promotes peritoneal metastasis. Multiple mechanisms have been identified, including anoikis-resistant cancer cells secreting PDGFB to activate MAPK/ERK signaling, omental adipocytes promoting angiogenesis through the CXCL2-VEGFA axis, and molecules like ESM1 binding to c-Met on endothelial cells to upregulate VEGFA expression [9–11]. The critical role of angiogenesis in peritoneal metastasis is further demonstrated by studies showing that anti-angiogenic agents can effectively inhibit peritoneal metastasis by reducing microvessel density and VEGF levels in peritoneal metastatic nodules [12]. Hence, exploring the angiogenic mechanisms involved in GC peritoneal metastasis is of significant importance.

In preliminary research, we identified the potential role of SPARC-related modular calcium-binding protein 2 (SMOC2) in GC peritoneal metastasis by analyzing the GSE62254 database. SMOC2, a glycoprotein from the SPARC family, exhibits a calcium-dependent conformation and is expressed in various tissues, including the lungs and aorta [13–15]. Recent studies have linked SMOC2 to several pathological conditions, including tumors [16], vascular calcification [17], and endocrine disorders [18]. Specifically, its role appears to vary across different cancers. For example, SMOC2 upregulation can activate epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) through the integrin β3, focal adhesion kinase (FAK), and paxillin pathway, thereby enhancing renal tumor proliferation and metastasis [19], while high levels suppress growth and migration in colorectal cancer [20]. However, the few existing studies have suggested tumor-suppressive functions of SMOC2 in GC [21, 22], while the specific functions and mechanisms of SMOC2 in GC peritoneal metastasis remain largely unexplored.

Numerous studies have shown that aberrant activation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway accelerates GC progression [23, 24]. In the classical Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, β-catenin acts as a key signaling molecule, translocating to the nucleus to bind LEF/TCF transcription factors and activate downstream gene transcription. When Wnt signaling is inactive, β-catenin forms a complex with APC, Axin, and GSK-3β, leading to its phosphorylation and subsequent degradation. Some studies have indicated a potential involvement of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway in peritoneal metastasis, but further studies are needed to clarify this connection [25, 26].

In this study, we found that SMOC2 is highly expressed in GC peritoneal metastasis patients and is closely correlated with poor prognosis. SMOC2 positively regulates the migration and peritoneal metastasis of GC cells, and activates vascular endothelial cells. Additionally, our findings indicate that SMOC2 promotes GC progression involving Wnt/β-catenin pathway changes. These results suggest that SMOC2 is a potential target for drug development aimed at peritoneal metastasis.

Materials and methods

Clinical samples

This study recruited 136 patients who underwent surgery from Zhejiang Cancer Hospital between January 2013 and December 2017. We collected demographic information and clinicopathological characteristics to investigate the association between these variables and SMOC2 expression. These patients had not received anti-tumor treatment before surgery, nor did they have other types of malignant tumors. Formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) GC tissues and matched adjacent non-tumor (NT) tissues from these patients were collected for tissue microarray construction and immunohistochemistry (IHC) analysis. Each tumor's pathological diagnosis was confirmed through independent assessments by two pathologists. Patients were given standardized chemotherapy shortly after surgery based on postoperative pathological results. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Zhejiang Cancer Hospital (IRB-2021-431, IRB-2024-178) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

The gene expression omnibus (GEO) data analysis

RNA-sequencing data and clinical information of 300 GC patients with recurrence were obtained from the GEO dataset GSE62254. Recurrence sites included peritoneum, ascites, liver, intra-abdominal lymph nodes, distant lymph nodes, bone, and other organs. Patients were divided into peritoneal metastasis (n = 54) and non-peritoneal metastasis (n = 246) groups based on the first site of recurrence. Additional comparisons were performed across individual recurrence sites to evaluate SMOC2 specificity. For survival analysis, peritoneal metastasis patients were stratified into high and low SMOC2 expression groups using an optimized cutoff value.

Cell culture

The human GC cell lines AGS, MGC-803, HGC, and MKN-45 were purchased from the cell bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). GC cells were maintained in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin at 37 °C and 5% CO2. Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) were purchased from Gibco and were used after two to five passages. HUVECs were maintained in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM, Gibco) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin at 37 °C and 5% CO2.

Preparation of conditioned medium (CM) for GC cells

GC cells in logarithmic growth phase were trypsinized, centrifuged, and seeded in 6-well plates at 3 × 105 cells per well for both control and SMOC2 shRNA groups. After 24 h of incubation in complete DMEM medium, cells were rinsed and cultured in 2 ml serum-free DMEM for 48 h. The culture medium was then collected, centrifuged at 1500 r/min for 5 min, and the supernatant was harvested as conditioned medium (CM). CM was either used immediately or stored at − 20 °C.

Tumor cell transwell assay

For migration assays, MGC-803 (10 × 104 cells/100 µl) and AGS (5 × 104 cells/100 µl) cells were digested, resuspended in serum-free medium, and placed in the upper Transwell chamber (8.0 µm, Corning, NY) without any coating. For invasion assays, Transwell chambers were pre-coated with Matrigel. Briefly, Matrigel was diluted 1:10 with cold serum-free RPMI 1640 medium, and 50 μL of diluted Matrigel was applied to each Transwell membrane. After incubation at 37 °C for 4–6 h to allow gelification, excess unpolymerized matrix was removed. MGC-803 (1.5 × 105 cells/100 µl) and AGS (1.0 × 105 cells/100 µl) cells suspended in serum-free medium were then seeded into the Matrigel-coated upper chambers. The bottom chamber contained 700 µl of 1640 medium with 10% FBS for both assays. After 24–48 h, cells in the upper chamber were removed with cotton swabs, while cells that migrated to the lower chamber were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, stained with crystal violet, photographed, and counted using ImageJ software.

GC cell-HUVEC cell co-culture system

For the co-culture system, GC cells (AGS and MGC-803) were seeded in the upper chamber of a Transwell plate (0.4 µm, Corning 3450, NY), while HUVEC cells were cultured in the lower chamber until 80–90% confluent. The upper chamber containing GC cells was then inserted into the lower chamber with HUVECs. The culture medium consisted of 50% RPMI 1640 and 50% DMEM supplemented with 10% serum. This non-contact co-culture was maintained at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ for 24–48 h. After co-culture, HUVECs were collected for subsequent phenotypic experiments.

HUVECs biological experiments

HUVECs were collected after 24–48 h of either co-culture with GC cells in the Transwell system or treatment with GC cell-conditioned medium. The collected HUVECs were then used for cell proliferation, migration, and tube formation assays. Detailed experimental protocols are provided in the supplementary information.

Quantitative reverse transcriptase PCR

Total RNA was extracted using Trizol reagent (Invitrogen) and reverse transcribed with Hifair® III 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis System (Yeasen). The cDNA was amplified using Hieff UNICON® Universal Blue qPCR SYBR Master Mix (Yeasen) with specific primers. The PCR cycling parameters were as follows: 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 40 cycles of 15 s at 95 °C and 1 min at 60 °C. Gene expression levels were normalized to the β-Actin housekeeping gene, and quantification was performed using the ΔΔCt calculation method. The primer sequences are provided in Table S1.

Western blotting

Cells were lysed in RIPA buffer (Beyotime, China) with proteinase inhibitors (TargetMol, USA). Proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore, USA). The membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies: SMOC2 (Cat No.31063-1-AP); p-β-catenin (Ser33, Cat No.80067-1-RR); β-catenin (Cat No.51067-2-AP); p-GSK3β (Ser9, Cat No.67558-1-Ig); GSK3β (Cat No.22104-1-AP); and GAPDH (Cat No.60004-1-Ig). After washing with 0.1% TBST, membranes were incubated with a horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-linked secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature. Signals were detected using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL), and GAPDH expression levels were used to confirm equal protein loading. All antibodies were from Proteintech (Wuhan, China).

Transfection and establishment of stable cell lines

Silencing MGC-803 and AGS cells were seeded in a 6-well dish and transfected with 80 pmol of either non-targeting siRNA control or SMOC2 siRNA (Tsingke) using Lipofectamine 3000 Reagent (Thermo Fisher), following the manufacturer’s protocol for 6 h.

Knockdown MGC-803 and AGS cells were transfected with short hairpin (sh)RNA lentiviruses (pLVX-IRES-Puro) targeting SMOC2 (5’-GCATGGACATTGACCCTTATA-3’) or control lentiviruses (Genechem). HiTransG (Genechem) was employed to transduce these lentiviruses into GC cells. Subsequently, the cells were subjected to selection for stably transfected clones with puromycin (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific) over a span of 2 weeks. Stable clones were picked out and assessed for SMOC2 knockdown efficiency via Western blot.

Overexpression MGC-803 and AGS cells were transfected with 1 µg of pcDNA3.1-SMOC2 (Tsingke) or an empty pcDNA3.1 vector using Lipofectamine 3000 Reagent (Thermo Fisher) in a 6-well dish, following the manufacturer’s protocol for 24–72 h for transient overexpression. For stable cell line generation, MKN-45 cells were transfected with either control or SMOC2 overexpressing lentivirus (Genechem) at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 10. One day after transfection, cells were treated with 1.25 µg/ml puromycin for 2–3 weeks until the survival clones proliferated.

Subcellular protein separation

Adherent GC cells were washed with 1 × PBS and harvested by cell scraping. The cells were then pelleted by centrifugation, and nuclear and cytoplasmic proteins were extracted using the Nuclear Protein and Cytoplasm Protein Extraction Kit (Beyotime, P0028) according to the manufacturer's protocol. For Western blot analysis, Lamin B1 and GAPDH served as reference markers for nuclear and cytoplasmic fractions, respectively.

Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining

Tumor tissues from GC patients and the mouse models were fixed in 10% formaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned. After dewaxing, rehydration, and antigen retrieval, sections were blocked with 10% goat serum (37 °C, 30 min) and incubated overnight (4 °C) with primary antibodies against p-β-catenin (Ser33, Cat No.80067-1-RR), β-catenin (Cat No.51067-2-AP), p-GSK3β (Ser9, Cat No.67558-1-Ig), and AXIN2 (Cat No.20540-1-AP). All antibodies were from Proteintech (Wuhan, China). HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies and DAB substrate were used for visualization, followed by hematoxylin counterstaining. Staining was scored using the H-score system.

Analysis of IHC results

Two pathologists blindly evaluated all IHC results based on staining intensity and percentage of positive cells. For clinical samples, we used the Immunoreactive Score system. The Immunoreactive Score (IRS) was calculated by multiplying the staining intensity score (IS: 0–3) by the percentage of positive cells score (AP: 0–4). Patients were classified into SMOC2 high and low expression groups according to the IRS score. For animal tissues, the analysis was performed using Visiopharm software, with the entire tissue region selected for H-score evaluation. The H-score was calculated using the following formula: H-score = ∑ (pi × i) where pi represents the percentage of positive cells, and i represents the intensity score assigned to positive cells: Negative (no staining) is scored 0, weak positivity (light yellow) is scored 1, moderate positivity (brownish–yellow) is scored 2, and strong positivity (brown) is scored 3. The H-score is a value ranging from 0 to 300, with higher scores indicating greater overall positivity intensity.

Peritoneal metastasis model in mice

To establish the peritoneal metastasis model, a suspension of 2.0 × 106 cells (AGS cells with stable SMOC2 knockdown or control, and MKN-45 cells with stable SMOC2 overexpression or control) was injected intraperitoneally into 4-week-old male NSG mice, with five mice per group. Tumor growth and metastasis were monitored through weekly live animal fluorescence imaging. After 4–6 weeks, the mice were sacrificed and systematically examined for gross metastatic deposits in major organs including lungs, liver, spleen, kidneys, and other abdominal organs. Their abdominal cavities were photographed to document tumor spread. Tumors were then harvested and weighed for comprehensive statistical analysis. All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Experimental Ethics Committee of Zhejiang Cancer Hospital (2024-10-007) and were performed under the guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals.

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software, USA). A Chi-square test was used to analyze the association between SMOC2 expression and GC clinicopathological features. Survival curves were generated using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared using the log-rank test. Differences between two groups were analyzed by Student's t-test, while multiple group comparisons used one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Correlation analyses were conducted using Spearman's test. Data are presented as means ± SD, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Elevated SMOC2 in GC with peritoneal metastasis indicates poor prognosis

To identify molecular markers for GC peritoneal metastasis and explore potential treatment targets, we analyzed clinical data from 300 GC patients in the GSE62254 database, dividing them into peritoneal metastasis (n = 54) and non-peritoneal metastasis (n = 246) groups based on the first site of recurrence (Fig. 1A). This comparison identified 419 significantly upregulated genes in the peritoneal metastasis group (P < 0.01, fold change > 1.2). KEGG pathway analysis showed that these upregulated genes were enriched in pathways related to angiogenesis, matrix adhesion, and growth factor response (Fig. 1B). Notably, we identified seven common genes in these three pathways, including SMOC2 (Fig. 1C).

Elevated SMOC2 in GC with peritoneal metastasis indicates poor prognosis. A–C Screening of differentially expressed genes, pathway enrichment analysis, and screening of overlapping genes of peritoneal metastasis of GC in GSE62254 database. D The expression of SMOC2 in GC patients with non-peritoneal metastasis and peritoneal metastasis in GSE62254 database. E–F Disease-free survival and overall survival analysis of SMOC2 in GC patients with peritoneal metastasis in GSE62254 database. G The expression of SMOC2 in paired primary gastric cancer tumors and peritoneal metastases (n = 9). Note Data presented as mean ± SD. *, P < 0.05; ****, P < 0.0001

In the GSE62254 database, SMOC2 expression was significantly higher in tumor tissues of patients with peritoneal metastasis compared to those without peritoneal metastasis (Fig. 1D). Analysis across individual recurrence sites showed that SMOC2 elevation was specific to peritoneal metastasis and ascites patients, with no significant differences in other metastatic sites such as liver and lymph nodes (Fig. S1). Survival analysis revealed a trend toward worse disease-free and overall survival in peritoneal metastasis patients with high SMOC2 expression (Fig. 1E, F). Additionally, SMOC2 expression was significantly elevated in peritoneal metastatic lesions compared to paired primary gastric tumors (Fig. 1G). Furthermore, our retrospective cohort study of 136 GC patients assessed SMOC2 expression in tissue microarrays using immunohistochemical (IHC) staining. Analysis revealed significant correlations between SMOC2 expression and Lauren type (P = 0.016), CA19-9 levels (P = 0.005), HER2 status (P = 0.089), and CA72-4 levels (P = 0.013) (Table S2).

SMOC2 promotes GC cell migration, invasion, and metastasis in vivo

To select appropriate cell lines for functional studies, we first examined endogenous SMOC2 expression levels. Western blot analysis revealed high SMOC2 expression in AGS and MGC-803 cells, while MKN-45, HGC-27, and normal gastric epithelial cells (GES-1) showed relatively low expression (Fig. S2). Based on these profiles, high-expressing lines were used for knockdown experiments and low-expressing lines for overexpression studies.

We next investigated SMOC2's role in GC cell migration and invasion using knockdown approaches. Transient SMOC2 knockdown using siRNA in high-expressing MGC-803 and AGS cells significantly inhibited migration and invasion compared to controls (Fig. 2A, B). Similarly, stable SMOC2 knockdown using lentiviral systems markedly decreased cell migration in both cell lines (Fig. 2C, D). Additionally, intraperitoneal implantation of AGS cells in NSG mice showed that SMOC2 knockdown tended to reduce peritoneal nodule formation compared with control cells (P = 0.09, Fig. 2E, F).

Loss of SMOC2 inhibits GC cell migration, invasion, and metastasis in vivo. A Analysis of migration and invasion in MGC-803 cells transfected with either control or SMOC2 siRNA. B Migration and invasion analysis of AGS cells treated with control or SMOC2 siRNA. C Migration analysis of MGC-803 cells infected with control or SMOC2 knockdown lentivirus. D Migration analysis of AGS cells infected with control or SMOC2 knockdown lentivirus. E Control and SMOC2 knockdown AGS cells were intraperitoneally injected into nude mice. F The number of tumors in each group is presented. Note Data presented as mean ± SD. *, P < 0.05

Conversely, we transiently overexpressed SMOC2 in MGC-803 and AGS cells, which led to enhanced cell migration and invasion (Fig. 3A, B). For in vivo validation, MKN-45 cells stably overexpressing SMOC2 were intraperitoneally injected into NSG mice. Bioluminescent imaging revealed that SMOC2 overexpression markedly promoted peritoneal metastasis (Fig. 3C), as confirmed by representative images of peritoneal tumors (Fig. 3D) and increased tumor weights at experiment endpoint (Fig. 3E). No gross metastatic deposits were observed in distant organs. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that SMOC2 functions as a positive regulator of migration, invasion, and metastasis in GC cells.

Quantification analysis of bioluminescent images showing peritoneal metastases is presented (n = 5). D Representative images of peritoneal tumors in both groups. E Tumor weights were measured at the end of the experiment. Note Data presented as mean ± SD. *, P < 0.05

SMOC2 promotes tumor angiogenesis in GC

The previous studies have shown that SMOC2 stimulates endothelial cell proliferation and migration, thereby regulating angiogenic activity both in vivo and in vitro [27–29]. Angiogenesis, a critical process in tumor growth and metastasis, is tightly regulated by growth factors and stromal cell proteins. Research has shown that SMOC2 overexpression in HUVECs not only stimulates DNA synthesis but also produces synergistic effects with VEGF, suggesting SMOC2 functions as an angiogenesis enhancer [28, 29].

To validate our KEGG pathway analysis findings showing significant upregulation of angiogenesis in the peritoneal metastasis group, we established a co-culture model of GC cells and HUVECs. Following SMOC2 knockdown in GC cells, we observed significant reductions in endothelial cell migration (Fig. 4A, B), proliferation (Fig. 4C), and tube formation capacity (Fig. 4D, E). These findings collectively indicate that SMOC2 promotes angiogenesis during GC peritoneal metastasis.

D–E The number of junctions and total branching length were also quantified to characterize the tube formation capability of HUVECs. Note Data presented as mean ± SD. *, P < 0.05; **, P < 0.01; and ***, P < 0.001

SMOC2 correlates with Wnt/β-catenin signaling changes in GC cells

Various signaling pathways, including AKT/mTOR, EMT [30], Hippo-YAP [31], and Wnt/β-catenin [24], contribute to peritoneal metastasis development. Quantitative PCR analysis revealed that SMOC2 knockdown in AGS and MGC-803 cells significantly reduced expression of Wnt/β-catenin target genes such as SOX2, MMP7, Cyclin D1, and CD44 (Fig. 5A, B). Conversely, SMOC2 overexpression in HGC-27 and MKN-45 cells significantly increased expression of these target genes (Fig. 5C, D).

SMOC2 regulates Wnt/β-catenin signaling in GC cells. A-D Quantitative PCR analysis of the expression level of Wnt/β-catenin pathway target genes in GC cells with different SMOC2 expression levels. E–H Western blots for p-β-catenin (Ser33), β-catenin, p-GSK3β (Ser9), and GSK3β in GC cells with SMOC2 knockdown or overexpression. I–L The cytoplasmic and nuclear expression levels of β-catenin were detected by Western blot in GC cells after treatment with SMOC2 lentivirus and the SMOC2 overexpression plasmid. Note Data presented as mean ± SD. *, P < 0.05; **, P < 0.01; and ***, P < 0.001

In the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, GSK3β phosphorylates β-catenin at Ser33, promoting its degradation [32]. When GSK3β is phosphorylated at Ser9, it becomes inactive, allowing β-catenin stabilization and nuclear translocation to activate downstream genes [33]. Western blotting showed that SMOC2 knockdown in GC cells was accompanied by reduced p-GSK3β (Ser9) levels while total GSK3β remained stable, decreased β-catenin expression, and increased p-β-catenin (Ser33) levels (Fig. 5E, F). Conversely, SMOC2 overexpression produced opposite results (Fig. 5G, H).

Further analysis demonstrated that SMOC2 knockdown decreased nuclear localization of β-catenin in AGS and MGC-803 cells, while SMOC2 overexpression increased nuclear β-catenin in MKN-45 and HGC cells (Fig. 5I, L). These findings suggest that SMOC2 may contribute to peritoneal metastasis partly through modulation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway.

In vivo and clinical validation of SMOC2, Wnt/β-catenin pathway, and angiogenesis

In vivo experiments provided additional evidence consistent with SMOC2's involvement in Wnt/β-catenin pathway modulation, aligning with our in vitro findings. In MKN-45 xenograft tumor sections, SMOC2 overexpression significantly increased nuclear β-catenin localization, AXIN2 expression, and p-GSK3β (Ser9) levels, while reducing p-β-catenin (Ser33) (Fig. 6A, B). This observation supports the potential involvement of SMOC2 in Wnt/β-catenin signaling modulation during GC peritoneal metastasis.

A-B Representative IHC images and H-score of p-β-catenin (Ser33), β-catenin, p-GSK3β (Ser9), and AXIN2 in MKN45 xenograft tumor sections. Scale bars = 50 µm. C IHC staining of CD31 and quantification of microvessel density in GC tissues with high and low SMOC2 expression. D Relative expression of CD31, LEF1, and CD44 in GC tissues with and without peritoneal metastasis in GSE62254 database. E Correlation between SMOC2 expression and CD31, LEF1, and CD44 expression levels in peritoneal metastasis group in GSE62254 database. Note Data presented as mean ± SD. *, P < 0.05; **, P < 0.01; and ****, P < 0.0001

IHC staining revealed significantly higher microvessel density (CD31 positive) in patient tissues expressing high SMOC2 compared to those with low SMOC2 expression, supporting its role in promoting angiogenesis (Fig. 6C). Analysis of the GSE62254 database showed significantly enhanced expression of CD31 (angiogenesis marker), LEF1, and CD44 (Wnt/β-catenin pathway target genes) in tumor tissues from GC patients with peritoneal metastasis compared to those without (Fig. 6D). Correlation analysis demonstrated that SMOC2 expression was significantly positively associated with CD31 (r = 0.4221, p = 0.0015), LEF1 (r = 0.4864, p = 0.0002), and CD44 (r = 0.1336, p = 0.3355) expression in the peritoneal metastasis group (Fig. 6E). Together, these findings suggest that SMOC2 contributes to GC peritoneal metastasis through angiogenesis promotion and potential Wnt/β-catenin signaling involvement, indicating its potential as both a therapeutic target and diagnostic biomarker.

Discussion

GC ranks as the second most commonly diagnosed malignancy. Peritoneal metastasis is common in GC, but its mechanisms remain poorly understood and effective treatments are limited [34, 35]. In this study, we identified SMOC2 as a key player in GC peritoneal metastasis and revealed its correlation with poor prognosis. Further experiments revealed that SMOC2 promotes GC cell migration, invasion, peritoneal metastasis, and endothelial cell activation with associated changes in the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway.

Several studies have reported heterogeneous effects of SMOC2 across different tumor types [36–38]. In thyroid cancer, low SMOC2 expression correlates with decreased survival rates [36]; while in hepatocellular carcinoma, SMOC2 enhances proliferation by facilitating cell cycle progression [37]; in gallbladder cancer, SMOC2 is stabilized by HDAC2 to promote metastasis and stemness [38]. In GC, some reports suggest that SMOC2 correlates with the intestinal stem cell marker LGR5 and may indicate improved survival [21]. Additionally, Xu et al. [22] demonstrated that microRNA-19a-3p promotes GC progression by targeting and suppressing SMOC2. However, our findings contrast with these previous studies, demonstrating that SMOC2 is specifically highly expressed in GC patients with peritoneal metastases and correlates with poor prognosis in this subgroup. Importantly, our analysis across different recurrence sites revealed that SMOC2 elevation is specifically restricted to peritoneal cavity involvement (peritoneal metastasis and ascites) with no association with liver, lymph node, or bone metastases.

This specificity may reflect context-dependent roles of SMOC2, where it acts as a tumor suppressor in primary tumors but promotes metastatic dissemination through angiogenesis and Wnt/β-catenin pathway activation in the peritoneal microenvironment. In our study, SMOC2 positively regulates the migratory and invasive capabilities of GC cells in vitro and promotes peritoneal nodule formation in vivo, suggesting SMOC2 contributes significantly to GC peritoneal metastasis.

Additionally, our research revealed that SMOC2 expression in GC cells positively correlates with endothelial cell migration, proliferation, and tube formation. These findings align with the previous literature, as studies have shown that SMOC2 can cooperate with growth factors to stimulate angiogenesis [29]. This mechanism is particularly relevant because peritoneal metastases development has been demonstrated to rely on endothelial cell-mediated angiogenesis [39, 40]. Furthermore, the microvascular density and VEGF expression in GC tissues were found to be closely associated with peritoneal metastasis [41]. Taken together, these observations not only confirm our findings but also suggest SMOC2 as a potential therapeutic target for inhibiting angiogenesis in GC peritoneal metastasis.

Mechanistically, we demonstrated that SMOC2 correlates with Wnt/β-catenin pathway changes in GC, including altered p-GSK3β and p-β-catenin levels, and changes in nuclear β-catenin localization. In vivo, SMOC2 overexpression enhanced nuclear β-catenin localization and altered the expression of key pathway components in mouse GC tissues. Clinically, SMOC2 expression correlated with CD31, LEF1, and CD44 in GC patients with peritoneal metastasis, supporting its association with Wnt signaling and angiogenesis.

Despite these findings, our study has limitations, including the need for validation in larger clinical cohorts and deeper mechanistic insights. Future work may investigate the therapeutic potential of targeting SMOC2, alone or in combination with anti-angiogenic agents. A study in endometrial cancer showed that SMOC2 interacts with Wnt receptors (e.g., Fzd6 and LRP6), enhancing canonical Wnt ligand binding and pathway activation [42]. Whether similar mechanisms operate in gastric cancer peritoneal metastasis warrants further investigation.

In summary, our study demonstrates that SMOC2 enhances GC peritoneal metastasis, potentially involving the Wnt/β-catenin pathway and promoting tumor angiogenesis. These findings advance our understanding of GC peritoneal metastasis mechanisms and identify SMOC2 as a promising therapeutic specifically for peritoneal metastasis, with potential implications for patient stratification and targeted therapeutic approaches.
