Protocol for modeling biliary cell activation in human precision-cut liver slices
Authors: Beatrice Anfuso, Manuela Mastronardi, Silvia Palmisano, Giovanni Sorrentino
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryPersistent ductular reaction is a hallmark of chronic liver injury, characterized by the proliferation and pro-inflammatory activity of reactive biliary epithelial cells. Here, we present a protocol for modeling biliary cell activation in human precision-cut liver slices. We describe steps for collecting samples, generating, and culturing precision-cut liver slices, and activating biliary cells ex vivo while preserving native architecture and multicellular interactions. Biliary cell activation can be quantified by EdU incorporation, supporting mechanistic studies and translational drug-screening applications.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Anfuso et al.1

Before you begin

The protocol below describes the generation of an ex vivo organotypic model to study biliary epithelial cells (BEC) activation within a complex native microenvironment that preserves both the hepatic cellular ecosystem and the native extracellular matrix architecture. To recapitulate regenerative processes, precision-cut liver slices (PCLS) are cultured in niche-specific media2,3 and embedded in a basement membrane-based extract (BME) matrix.4 PCLS can be established from human liver tissue within a few hours, provided that fresh, viable samples are available. Importantly, PCLS can be derived from healthy livers and from livers with diverse pathological conditions (e.g., steatosis, cholestatic injury, fibrosis), thereby enabling investigation of BEC propagation in a disease-specific context. BEC reactivity can be quantified using EdU incorporation followed by immunofluorescence and digital histology-based analysis. The preserved multicellular environment further allows interrogation by advanced omics approaches, including single-cell or single-nucleus RNA sequencing, spatial transcriptomics, and spatial proteomics.

Innovation

PCLS have been widely used to study hepatocyte metabolism and drug toxicity. However, their application to model ductular reaction (DR) within a preserved multicellular microenvironment has remained limited. This protocol introduces key methodological adaptations that enable reproducible induction and quantification of BEC activation, a hallmark of DR ex vivo.

First, the use of niche-specific expansion media,2,3 combined with BME embedding,4 supports the activation and proliferation of reactive BEC while preserving the native extracellular matrix architecture and spatial organization of portal regions. This enables the study of BEC activation within an intact tissue context that preserves epithelial, stromal, and immune cell interactions.

Second, the protocol integrates automated digital histology-based phenotyping using artificial intelligence–assisted segmentation to quantify proliferating BEC and other cell populations in an unbiased manner. This approach improves reproducibility and scalability compared to manual counting.

Third, the platform is compatible with compound screening, enabling functional interrogation of pathway dependencies directly in organotypic tissue. In the original study, this strategy revealed a dependence of reactive BEC on mevalonate metabolism.1

Finally, the method is applicable to both murine and human liver tissues, in healthy and diseased conditions, thereby bridging mechanistic investigation and patient-relevant biology.

Together, these innovations extend standard PCLS methodology into a versatile organotypic platform for studying BEC activation, multicellular interactions, and pharmacological modulation in a physiologically relevant context.

Expected outcomes

When cultured in expansion medium, PCLS typically exhibit progressive activation of BEC over time. In our experience, once placed in culture, slices retain the ability to support biliary epithelial cell activation for up to 8 days, although the duration and magnitude of the response may vary according to the characteristics of the primary tissue. Robust activation of proliferating BECs can occur at different times depending on disease stage. Proliferating BECs are generally enriched in periportal regions and display an irregular, migratory morphology (Figure 5).

Importantly, although BEC proliferation represents the primary quantitative readout described in this protocol, this organotypic platform enables investigation of additional cellular components involved in DR. For example, proliferation of mesenchymal cells, such as Vimentin+ fibroblasts or α-SMA+ myofibroblasts, can be assessed using EdU incorporation or Ki67 staining. The preservation of multicellular architecture further enables analysis of spatial interactions among epithelial, stromal, and immune compartments and is compatible with spatial proteomics approaches, such as multiplex immunofluorescence, which simultaneously interrogates multiple cell types within the same tissue section. The platform is also compatible with advanced transcriptomic approaches, including single-cell or single-nucleus RNA sequencing, enabling characterization of cell-state transitions, pathway activation, and cell–cell communication within the preserved microenvironment (Figure 6). Importantly, the platform also preserves the native extracellular matrix, allowing quantitative investigation of its structural organization by second harmonic generation (SHG) microscopy for morphometric analysis, as well as characterization of its mechanical properties using atomic force microscopy (AFM) or nanoindentation-based approaches.Figure 6Multilevel analytical applications enabled by the human PCLS platformOverview of downstream applications supported by PCLS cultured in expansion medium. Limitations

This protocol models biliary epithelial cell activation in an ex vivo organotypic context while preserving native tissue architecture. However, some limitations should be considered.

First, diffusion gradients of oxygen and nutrients may develop within 260 μm slices during culture. Although this thickness is widely used to preserve viability, regional differences in metabolic activity or hypoxia may occur, particularly in prolonged cultures.

Second, substantial inter-individual variability is observed in human PCLS, potentially influenced by age, metabolic status, degree of steatosis, and underlying inflammatory conditions, as well as tissue ischemic time. Each donor should therefore be considered an independent biological replicate, and a sufficient sample size is required for statistical robustness.

Third, embedding in BME provides structural support but may introduce exogenous matrix components and residual growth factors that could influence cellular behavior. Alternative matrix systems, such as gelatin methacryloyl (GelMA)- or polyethylene glycol (PEG)-based hydrogels may require empirical optimization.

Finally, while EdU incorporation provides a robust measure of cumulative proliferation during the labeling window, it does not distinguish between differences in cell-cycle kinetics or proliferative burst dynamics. Complementary markers such as Ki67 or phospho-Histone H3 may provide additional insight when required.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

Low slice viability or absence of BEC activation. Related to steps 3 and 23 [Collection of human samples].

Potential solution

Ensure that tissue is transferred immediately into ice-cold basal medium and processed within 1 hour of resection to minimize ischemic time. Maintain all samples on ice during slicing. We recommend assessing the viability of a small subset of PCLS from each slice preparation by ATP quantification (e.g., CellTiter-Glo® Luminescent Cell Viability Assay, Promega) or by a metabolic assay such as MTS before proceeding with treatments.

Problem 2

Irregular, incomplete, or uneven slices during cutting. Related to steps 16 and 19 [Preparation of precision-cut liver slices (PCLS)].

Potential solution

Adjust arm and blade speed according to tissue consistency. Modify agarose concentration as needed, increasing it if the tissue is particularly soft. For firmer samples, reduce cutting speed. The Alabama MD6000 slicer can also be used with the dedicated tissue holder to cut cores without agarose embedding when appropriate.

Problem 3

Weak or inconsistent EdU signal. Related to steps 33 and 37 [BEC proliferation assessment using EdU incorporation assay].

Potential solution

Optimize EdU pulse duration (typically 16–24 h, depending on proliferation kinetics) and concentration. Avoid prolonged fixation (>16 h), as over-fixation can reduce EdU signal intensity. Apply an autofluorescence quenching step prior to imaging if background fluorescence is high. Prepare EdU working solutions fresh immediately before use and aliquot stock solutions to avoid repeated freeze–thaw cycles.

Problem 4

High background or inaccurate digital phenotyping in QuPath. Related to step 45 and 51 [Digital quantification of proliferating BECs].

Potential solution

Ensure high-quality image acquisition; repeat staining if signal intensity is low or if tissue sections are folded or disrupted. Carefully select representative training regions that include both positive and negative cells across a range of fluorescence intensities. Use multiple regions during classifier training when necessary. Visually verify nuclear segmentation accuracy before applying batch analysis to the full dataset.

Problem 5

High variability between replicates or between human samples. Related to steps 31 [BEC proliferation assessment using EdU incorporation assay].

Potential solution

Distribute consecutive slices evenly among treatment groups to minimize positional bias along the tissue core. Increase the number of slices per condition when possible. When working with human PCLS, treat each patient as an independent biological replicate and avoid pooling slices across donors.
