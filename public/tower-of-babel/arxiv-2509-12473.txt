Cox Regression on the Plane
Yael Travis-Lumer

Abstract
The Cox proportional hazards model is the most widely used regression model in univariate survival analysis. Extensions of the Cox model to bivariate survival data, however, remain scarce. We propose two novel extensions based on a Lehmann-type representation of the survival function. The first, the simple Lehmann model, is a direct extension that retains a straightforward structure. The second, the generalized Lehmann model, allows greater flexibility by incorporating three distinct regression parameters and includes the simple Lehmann model as a special case. For both models, we derive the corresponding regression formulations for the three bivariate hazard functions and discuss their interpretation and model validity. To estimate the regression parameters, we adopt a bivariate pseudo-observations approach. For the generalized Lehmann model, we extend this approach to accommodate a trivariate structure: trivariate pseudo-observations and a trivariate link function. We then propose a two-step estimation procedure, where the marginal regression parameters are estimated in the first step, and the remaining parameters are estimated in the second step. Finally, we establish the consistency and asymptotic normality of the resulting estimators.



keywords:

Bivariate survival function; Pseudo-observations; Generalized estimating equations; Hazard rates; Multivariate failure times.

1
Introduction

The Cox proportional hazards model
(
Cox 1972
)
is the most widely used regression model for univariate survival analysis, where the goal is to quantify covariate effects on a single failure time. Extending this model to handle bivariate failure times allows for the analysis of correlated survival outcomes, enabling joint estimation of covariate effects on both event times. Our motivating example concerns estimating the joint survival distribution of the times to enucleation of both eyes in children with retinoblastoma, a rare ocular cancer originating in the retina. However, direct generalization of the Cox model to the bivariate setting is not straightforward, and usually requires some assumptions about the dependency structure linking the two failure times.

Over the years, several extensions of the Cox model to bivariate survival data have been proposed. In his original paper,
Cox 1972
briefly outlined a possible generalization involving four univariate hazard functions, two marginal and two conditional, but practical implementation remains unclear, and the conditional hazards rely on only a small subset of the data.
Clayton and
Cuzick 1985
introduced a shared frailty approach, modeling each marginal hazard via a Cox model conditional on a common frailty with a single association parameter; however, this framework does not accommodate covariate effects on the dependence structure. More recently,
Marra and
Radice 2020
extended this by incorporating regression models for both the marginals and the association parameter within a parametric copula framework. While flexible, such parametric assumptions may limit applicability in practice. Furthermore, for the frailty and copula models discussed above, the impact of covariates on the joint survival distribution is less straightforward to interpret than in the univariate setting.

An alternative and interesting extension of the Cox model is based on specifying three proportional hazards models: two for the single-failure hazards and one for the double-failure hazard.
Pons
et al. 1992
(Section 4) briefly proposed this approach with two marginal Cox models and an additional model for the joint failure rate.
Tien and
Sen 2002
proposed a similar framework to bivariate interval-censored data, restricting estimation to time points where the two event times coincide (
t_{1}=t_{2}
). More recently,
Prentice and
Zhao 2021
extended these multiplicative hazard models also to multivariate failure times. A key limitation of these approaches is that the resulting covariate-adjusted hazard models do not correspond to a valid bivariate survival distribution, a point we will demonstrate explicitly in Section
3.3.1
.

In this paper, we propose a semi-parametric extension of the Cox model to bivariate survival data; this extension is based on a generalization of the Lehmann alternative representation of the Cox model in terms of the survival function. We consider both a simple extension and a further generalization of the model, and derive the corresponding regression formulations for the three bivariate hazard functions. Unlike copula or frailty models, our framework does not assume a parametric form for the bivariate distribution, making it fully semi-parametric in the original sense of the Cox model, with the only parametric assumption on the covariate effects. Moreover, the effects of covariates on the joint survival distribution are relatively easier to interpret. With respect to multivariate hazard modeling approaches, our approach has the advantage of corresponding to a valid bivariate survival distribution
and thus enables prediction of joint survival events. For both proposed models, we estimate model parameters using a generalization of the pseudo-observations approach
(
Andersen 2003
)
, and establish the consistency and asymptotic normality of the resulting estimators.

2
Preliminaries

Let
(T_{1},T_{2})
denote a bivariate random vector representing the survival times of interest, and let
(C_{1},C_{2})
denote the corresponding bivariate censoring times. For
j=1,2
, we define the observed time as
Y_{j}=\min(T_{j},C_{j})
, and the event indicator as
\Delta_{j}=I(T_{j}\leq C_{j})
. The observed data thus consist of
n
independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) copies
\{(Y_{1i},\Delta_{1i},Y_{2i},\Delta_{2i},Z_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
,
where
Z_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
denotes the covariate vector for the
i
th subject.
Throughout this work, we assume that the survival times are continuous. For estimation, we further impose that
(C_{1},C_{2})
is independent of both
(T_{1},T_{2})
and
Z
. This condition is the natural bivariate extension of the independent censoring assumption, which is standard in the univariate pseudo-observations literature.

We denote by
S_{T_{1},T_{2}}(t_{1},t_{2})=P(T_{1}>t_{1},T_{2}>t_{2})

the joint survival function of
(T_{1},T_{2})
at
(t_{1},t_{2})
. For brevity, we write
S(t_{1},t_{2})
instead of
S_{T_{1},T_{2}}(t_{1},t_{2})
. The marginal survival functions of
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
are denoted by
S_{T_{1}}(t_{1})
and
S_{T_{2}}(t_{2})
, respectively.

Throughout, for infinitesimally small
dt_{1}
and
dt_{1}
, we adopt the shorthand notation

P(T_{1}\in dt_{1},T_{2}\in dt_{2}):=P(t_{1}\leq T_{1}\leq t_{1}+dt_{1},\,t_{2}\leq T_{2}\leq t_{2}+dt_{2}).

The instantaneous risk of a joint failure is defined as

\Lambda_{11}(ds,dt)=P\!\left(T_{1}\in ds,T_{2}\in dt\mid T_{1}\geq s,T_{2}\geq t\right)=\frac{P(T_{1}\in ds,T_{2}\in dt)}{P(T_{1}\geq s,T_{2}\geq t)}=\frac{S(ds,dt)}{S(s^{-},t^{-})}.

Similarly, the instantaneous risks of single failures are

\Lambda_{10}(ds,t)=P\!\left(T_{1}\in ds,T_{2}>t\mid T_{1}\geq s,T_{2}>t\right)=\frac{P(T_{1}\in ds,T_{2}>t)}{P(T_{1}\geq s,T_{2}>t)}=\frac{P(T_{1}\in ds\mid T_{2}>t)}{P(T_{1}\geq s\mid T_{2}>t)},

and

\Lambda_{01}(s,dt)=P\!\left(T_{1}>s,T_{2}\in dt\mid T_{1}>s,T_{2}\geq t\right)=\frac{P(T_{1}>s,T_{2}\in dt)}{P(T_{1}>s,T_{2}\geq t)}=\frac{P(T_{2}\in dt\mid T_{1}>s)}{P(T_{2}\geq t\mid T_{1}>s)}.

Thus,
\Lambda_{10}(ds,t)
and
\Lambda_{01}(s,dt)
can be interpreted as conditional univariate hazards, given
T_{2}>t
and
T_{1}>s
, respectively. We denote the bivariate cumulative hazard vector by

\Lambda(s,t)=\big(\Lambda_{10}(s,t),\,\Lambda_{01}(s,t),\,\Lambda_{11}(s,t)\big).

When
S(t_{1},t_{2})
admits a density
f(t_{1},t_{2})
(the setting considered in this paper), we write

\Lambda_{11}(ds,dt)=\lambda_{11}(s,t)\,ds\,dt,\quad\Lambda_{10}(ds,t)=\lambda_{10}(s,t)\,ds,\quad\Lambda_{01}(s,dt)=\lambda_{01}(s,t)\,dt,

where

\displaystyle\lambda_{11}(s,t)

\displaystyle=\lim_{h_{1},h_{2}\to 0}\frac{1}{h_{1}h_{2}}\,P\!\left(T_{1}\in[s,s+h_{1}],T_{2}\in[t,t+h_{2}]\mid T_{1}\geq s,T_{2}\geq t\right)=\frac{f(s,t)}{S(s^{-},t^{-})},

\displaystyle\lambda_{10}(s,t)

\displaystyle=\lim_{h_{1}\to 0}\frac{1}{h_{1}}\,P\!\left(T_{1}\in[s,s+h_{1}]\mid T_{1}\geq s,T_{2}>t\right)=\frac{\int_{t}^{\infty}f(s,v)\,dv}{S(s^{-},t)},

\displaystyle\lambda_{01}(s,t)

\displaystyle=\lim_{h_{2}\to 0}\frac{1}{h_{2}}\,P\!\left(T_{2}\in[t,t+h_{2}]\mid T_{1}>s,T_{2}\geq t\right)=\frac{\int_{s}^{\infty}f(u,t)\,du}{S(s,t^{-})}.

Finally, the marginal hazard functions
\lambda_{T_{1}}(\cdot)
and
\lambda_{T_{2}}(\cdot)
can be expressed in terms of the single-failure hazards:

\lambda_{T_{1}}(t_{1})=\lambda_{10}(t_{1},0),\qquad\lambda_{T_{2}}(t_{2})=\lambda_{01}(0,t_{2}).

Define
A(t_{1},t_{2})=\log(S(t_{1},t_{2}))
. Then, as in
Dabrowska 1988
, for
(t_{1},t_{2})\in[0,\tau_{1}]\times[0,\tau_{2}]
such that
S(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})>0
, we have

\displaystyle S(t_{1},t_{2})

\displaystyle=\exp\{A(t_{1},t_{2})\}=\exp\left\{\int_{0}^{t_{1}}\int_{0}^{t_{2}}A(du,dv)+A(t_{1},0)+A(0,t_{2})\right\}

(1)

\displaystyle=\exp\{A(t_{1},0)\}\exp\{A(0,t_{2})\}\exp\left\{\int_{0}^{t_{1}}\int_{0}^{t_{2}}A(du,dv)\right\}

\displaystyle=S_{T_{1}}(t_{1})S_{T_{2}}(t_{2})\exp\left\{\int_{0}^{t_{1}}\int_{0}^{t_{2}}A(du,dv)\right\}.

For further information, see Appendix
A
. Thus, any bivariate survival function can be expressed as the product of the two marginal survival functions and an additional multiplicative term

\exp\left\{\int_{0}^{t_{1}}\int_{0}^{t_{2}}A(du,dv)\right\}
. This term captures the dependence between
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
, and can equivalently be represented in terms of the bivariate cumulative hazard vector (see Appendix
B
).

3
The two models

3.1
The simple Lehmann model

A natural bivariate extension of the Lehmann alternative representation of the Cox model is

S(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z)=\left[S^{0}(t_{1},t_{2})\right]^{\exp(b^{T}Z)},

(2)

where
S^{0}(t_{1},t_{2})
denotes a baseline bivariate survival function and
b\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
is a vector of regression coefficients. We refer to (
2
) as the
simple Lehmann model
, since it directly extends the univariate Cox model when expressed in terms of the survival function.

For comparison, in the univariate setting with failure time
T
, the Cox model specifies

\lambda_{T}(t\mid Z)=\lambda_{T}^{0}(t)\,\exp(\beta^{T}Z),

which admits the equivalent survival representation

S_{T}(t\mid Z)=\left[S_{T}^{0}(t)\right]^{\exp(\beta^{T}Z)},

where
S_{T}^{0}(t)=\exp\{-\Lambda_{T}^{0}(t)\}=\exp\{-\int_{0}^{t}\lambda_{T}^{0}(u)\,du\}
is the baseline survival function corresponding to the baseline hazard
\lambda_{T}^{0}(t)
. This representation is commonly referred to as the Lehmann alternative form of the Cox model.

The simple Lehmann model in (
2
) admits a proportional log-survival interpretation. For a univariate covariate
Z
,

\log S(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z=z+1)=e^{b}\,\log S(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z=z).

Since
\log S(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z)<0
, a positive
b
decreases the survival probability, while a negative
b
increases it.

For a
p
-dimensional covariate vector
Z
, let
z^{*}
be obtained from
z
by increasing its
j
th component by one unit. Then,

S(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z=z^{*})=\{S(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z=z)\}^{\exp(b_{j})},

where
b=(b_{1},\ldots,b_{p})^{T}
. Thus,
b_{j}>0
reduces survival, while
b_{j}<0
increases it.
Intuitively, the regression coefficients stretch or compress the joint survival function on the log scale: positive coefficients accelerate its decline, and negative ones slow it down.

Finally, it is important to emphasize that the simple Lehmann model in (
2
) is not strictly a proportional hazards model: in particular, the joint (double-failure) hazard does not take a multiplicative form, as discussed in Section
3.3
. Moreover, specifying three proportional hazards models (two single-failure hazards and one double-failure hazard) does not, in general, correspond to a valid bivariate survival distribution, as explained in Section
3.3.1
.

3.2
The generalized Lehmann model

In some applications, the effects of covariates on the two marginal survival times and on the dependence structure between them may differ. To accommodate this, we require a more flexible model that allows separate regression coefficients for each marginal and for the joint component. At the same time, we aim to retain a semi-parametric structure in the original sense of Cox, leaving all baseline quantities unspecified. We refer to this formulation as the
generalized Lehmann model
, which we present in detail below.

By Equation (
1
), the baseline bivariate survival function
S^{0}(t_{1},t_{2})
can be written as

S^{0}(t_{1},t_{2})=S_{T_{1}}^{0}(t_{1})\,S_{T_{2}}^{0}(t_{2})\,\exp\Big\{\int_{0}^{t_{1}}\int_{0}^{t_{2}}A^{0}(du,dv)\Big\},

for all
(t_{1},t_{2})\in[0,\tau_{1}]\times[0,\tau_{2}]
such that
S^{0}(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})>0
,
where
S_{T_{1}}^{0}(t_{1})=S^{0}(t_{1},0)
and
S_{T_{2}}^{0}(t_{2})=S^{0}(0,t_{2})
are the marginal baseline survival functions, and
A^{0}(t_{1},t_{2})=\log(S^{0}(t_{1},t_{2}))
.

Consequently, for all
(t_{1},t_{2})\in[0,\tau_{1}]\times[0,\tau_{2}]
, the simple Lehmann model in Equation (
2
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle S(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z)

\displaystyle=\left[S^{0}(t_{1},t_{2})\right]^{\exp(b^{T}Z)}

\displaystyle=\left[S_{T_{1}}^{0}(t_{1})S_{T_{2}}^{0}(t_{2})\exp\Big\{\int_{0}^{t_{1}}\int_{0}^{t_{2}}A^{0}(du,dv)\Big\}\right]^{\exp(b^{T}Z)}

\displaystyle=\left[S_{T_{1}}^{0}(t_{1})\right]^{\exp(b^{T}Z)}\left[S_{T_{2}}^{0}(t_{2})\right]^{\exp(b^{T}Z)}\left[\exp\Big\{\int_{0}^{t_{1}}\int_{0}^{t_{2}}A^{0}(du,dv)\Big\}\right]^{\exp(b^{T}Z)}

\displaystyle=S_{T_{1}}(t_{1}\mid Z)S_{T_{2}}(t_{2}\mid Z)\exp\Big\{\int_{0}^{t_{1}}\int_{0}^{t_{2}}A(du,dv;Z)\Big\},

where
S_{T_{1}}(t_{1}\mid Z)
and
S_{T_{2}}(t_{2}\mid Z)
correspond to univariate Cox models (using the same regression parameter
b
), and
\int_{0}^{t_{1}}\int_{0}^{t_{2}}A^{0}(du,dv)\,e^{b^{T}Z}
defines a Cox-like model for
\int_{0}^{t_{1}}\int_{0}^{t_{2}}A(du,dv;Z)
, with
A(t_{1},t_{2};Z)\equiv\log(S(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z))
.

Hence, the simple Lehmann model can equivalently be obtained by multiplying the following three sub-models:

\displaystyle S_{T_{1}}(t_{1}\mid Z)

\displaystyle=\left[S_{T_{1}}^{0}(t_{1})\right]^{\exp(b^{T}Z)},

\displaystyle S_{T_{2}}(t_{2}\mid Z)

\displaystyle=\left[S_{T_{2}}^{0}(t_{2})\right]^{\exp(b^{T}Z)},

\displaystyle\exp\Big\{\int_{0}^{t_{1}}\int_{0}^{t_{2}}A(du,dv;Z)\Big\}

\displaystyle=\left[\exp\Big\{\int_{0}^{t_{1}}\int_{0}^{t_{2}}A^{0}(du,dv)\Big\}\right]^{\exp(b^{T}Z)},

all sharing the same regression parameter
b\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
.

Assuming the same regression coefficient for
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
may be reasonable, for example, when
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
are exchangeable, as in our survival of eyes example. However, requiring
\int_{0}^{t_{1}}\int_{0}^{t_{2}}A(du,dv;Z)
to share the same coefficient is more restrictive. A natural generalization is

\displaystyle S_{T_{1}}(t_{1}\mid Z)

\displaystyle=\left[S_{T_{1}}^{0}(t_{1})\right]^{\exp(\alpha^{T}Z)},

(3)

\displaystyle S_{T_{2}}(t_{2}\mid Z)

\displaystyle=\left[S_{T_{2}}^{0}(t_{2})\right]^{\exp(\beta^{T}Z)},

\displaystyle\exp\Big\{\int_{0}^{t_{1}}\int_{0}^{t_{2}}A(du,dv;Z)\Big\}

\displaystyle=\left[\exp\Big\{\int_{0}^{t_{1}}\int_{0}^{t_{2}}A^{0}(du,dv)\Big\}\right]^{\exp(\gamma^{T}Z)},

corresponding to

S(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z)=\left[S_{T_{1}}^{0}(t_{1})\right]^{\exp(\alpha^{T}Z)}\left[S_{T_{2}}^{0}(t_{2})\right]^{\exp(\beta^{T}Z)}\left[\exp\Big\{\int_{0}^{t_{1}}\int_{0}^{t_{2}}A^{0}(du,dv)\Big\}\right]^{\exp(\gamma^{T}Z)}.

The case
\alpha=\beta=\gamma
recovers the simple Lehmann model. We refer to (
3
) as the
generalized Lehmann model
.

This model can also be expressed in terms of derivatives of the log survival function:

\displaystyle\lambda_{T_{1}}(t_{1}\mid Z)

\displaystyle=\lambda_{T_{1}}^{0}(t_{1})\,e^{\alpha^{T}Z},

\displaystyle\lambda_{T_{2}}(t_{2}\mid Z)

\displaystyle=\lambda_{T_{2}}^{0}(t_{2})\,e^{\beta^{T}Z},

\displaystyle a(t_{1},t_{2};Z)

\displaystyle=a^{0}(t_{1},t_{2})\,e^{\gamma^{T}Z},

where

•

\lambda_{T_{1}}(t_{1}\mid Z)=-\frac{\partial\log S(t_{1},0\mid Z)}{\partial t_{1}}
,

•

\lambda_{T_{2}}(t_{2}\mid Z)=-\frac{\partial\log S(0,t_{2}\mid Z)}{\partial t_{2}}
,

•

a(t_{1},t_{2};Z)=\frac{\partial^{2}\log S(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z)}{\partial t_{1}\partial t_{2}}
.

That is, the generalized Lehmann model specifies three multiplicative models for the derivatives of the log survival function.
While only the first two are proportional hazards models, the third specifies a
proportional derivative of the log survival
. This formulation naturally includes the univariate Cox model as a special case:

-\frac{d\log S_{T}(t\mid Z)}{dt}=\lambda_{T}(t\mid Z)=\lambda_{T}^{0}(t)e^{\beta^{T}Z},

as the derivative of the log survival function specifies a multiplicative model.

The interpretation of the generalized Lehmann model is more complex than that of the simple Lehmann model in (
2
). For a univariate
Z
, increasing the covariate by 1 unit yields

\displaystyle S(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z=z+1)

\displaystyle=[S_{T_{1}}(t_{1}\mid Z=z)]^{e^{\alpha}}[S_{T_{2}}(t_{2}\mid Z=z)]^{e^{\beta}}\left[\frac{S(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z=z)}{S_{T_{1}}(t_{1}\mid Z=z)S_{T_{2}}(t_{2}\mid Z=z)}\right]^{e^{\gamma}}

\displaystyle=[S_{T_{1}}(t_{1}\mid Z=z)]^{e^{\alpha}-e^{\gamma}}[S_{T_{2}}(t_{2}\mid Z=z)]^{e^{\beta}-e^{\gamma}}[S(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z=z)]^{e^{\gamma}}.

Consequently:

1.

If
e^{\alpha}-e^{\gamma}<1
,
e^{\beta}-e^{\gamma}<1
, and
e^{\gamma}<1
, then
S(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z=z+1)>S(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z=z)
.

2.

If
e^{\alpha}-e^{\gamma}\geq 1
,
e^{\beta}-e^{\gamma}\geq 1
, and
e^{\gamma}\geq 1
, then
S(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z=z+1)\leq S(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z=z)
.

3.

Other combinations can lead to mixed effects on marginal versus joint survival.

Finally, an additional generalization allows different covariate vectors for each component:
Z_{1}
for the first failure,
Z_{2}
for the second, and
Z_{3}
for the joint term, with
Z=(Z_{1}^{T},Z_{2}^{T},Z_{3}^{T})^{T}
. The three sub-models in (
3
) can then be used with some regression parameters set to zero accordingly.

3.3
The corresponding hazard functions

In the bivariate setting, the dependence structure between the two survival times implies that the joint distribution is characterized not just by marginal hazards, but also by a double-failure hazard. Unlike the univariate case, where the hazard uniquely determines the survival function, bivariate survival functions require three hazard functions: two single-failure hazards and one double-failure hazard.

Assuming continuity of the survival times, and similarly to the product integral representation of the bivariate survival function
(
Dabrowska 1988
)
, the covariate-adjusted bivariate survival function
S(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z)
can be expressed in terms of these three hazard functions:

\displaystyle S(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z)

\displaystyle=\exp\Bigg\{-\Lambda_{10}(t_{1},0\mid Z)-\Lambda_{01}(0,t_{2}\mid Z)

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t_{1}}\int_{0}^{t_{2}}\underbrace{\left[\lambda_{11}(u,v\mid Z)-\lambda_{10}(u,v\mid Z)\lambda_{01}(u,v\mid Z)\right]}_{a(u,v;Z)}dudv\Bigg\}.

The corresponding hazard functions satisfy:

\displaystyle\lambda_{10}(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z)

\displaystyle=\frac{-\frac{\partial S(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z)}{\partial t_{1}}}{S(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z)}=\lambda_{10}(t_{1},0\mid Z)-\int_{0}^{t_{2}}a(t_{1},v;Z)dv,

\displaystyle\lambda_{01}(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z)

\displaystyle=\frac{-\frac{\partial S(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z)}{\partial t_{2}}}{S(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z)}=\lambda_{01}(0,t_{2}\mid Z)-\int_{0}^{t_{1}}a(u,t_{2};Z)du,

\displaystyle\lambda_{11}(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z)

\displaystyle=\frac{\frac{\partial^{2}S(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z)}{\partial t_{1}\partial t_{2}}}{S(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z)}=a(t_{1},t_{2};Z)+\lambda_{10}(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z)\lambda_{01}(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z).

The hazard functions for the two types of Lehmann models are summarized in Proposition
1
.

Proposition 1
.

Simple Lehmann model:

\displaystyle\lambda_{10}(u,v\mid Z)

\displaystyle=\lambda_{10}^{0}(u,v)e^{b^{T}Z},

(4)

\displaystyle\lambda_{01}(u,v\mid Z)

\displaystyle=\lambda_{01}^{0}(u,v)e^{b^{T}Z},

\displaystyle\lambda_{11}(u,v\mid Z)

\displaystyle=\lambda_{11}^{0}(u,v)e^{b^{T}Z}-\lambda_{10}^{0}(u,v)\lambda_{01}^{0}(u,v)\left[e^{b^{T}Z}-e^{2b^{T}Z}\right].

Generalized Lehmann model:

\displaystyle\lambda_{10}(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z)

\displaystyle=e^{\alpha^{T}Z}\lambda_{10}^{0}(t_{1},0)-e^{\gamma^{T}Z}\int_{0}^{t_{2}}a^{0}(t_{1},v)dv,

(5)

\displaystyle\lambda_{01}(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z)

\displaystyle=e^{\beta^{T}Z}\lambda_{01}^{0}(0,t_{2})-e^{\gamma^{T}Z}\int_{0}^{t_{1}}a^{0}(u,t_{2})du,

\displaystyle\lambda_{11}(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z)

\displaystyle=\lambda_{10}(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z)\lambda_{01}(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z)+e^{\gamma^{T}Z}a^{0}(t_{1},t_{2}),

where
a^{0}(u,v)=\lambda_{11}^{0}(u,v)-\lambda_{10}^{0}(u,v)\lambda_{01}^{0}(u,v)
.

The proof follows from the relationships between the hazard functions and derivatives of the log bivariate survival function, and can be found in Appendix
C
.

When
\alpha=\beta=\gamma=b
, the generalized Lehmann model reduces to the simple Lehmann model. In this case, the corresponding hazard models coincide, yielding the identities

-\int_{0}^{t_{2}}a^{0}(t_{1},v)\,dv\;=\;\lambda_{10}^{0}(t_{1},t_{2})-\lambda_{10}^{0}(t_{1},0),\quad-\int_{0}^{t_{1}}a^{0}(u,t_{2})\,du\;=\;\lambda_{01}^{0}(t_{1},t_{2})-\lambda_{01}^{0}(0,t_{2}).

Equivalently,

-\frac{\partial\lambda_{01}^{0}(t_{1},t_{2})}{\partial t_{1}}=a^{0}(t_{1},t_{2})=-\frac{\partial\lambda_{10}^{0}(t_{1},t_{2})}{\partial t_{2}}.

These identities follow from the continuity of
S^{0}(t_{1},t_{2})
, which implies equality of mixed partial derivatives:

\frac{\partial}{\partial t_{2}}\left(\frac{\partial\log S^{0}(t_{1},t_{2})}{\partial t_{1}}\right)=\frac{\partial}{\partial t_{1}}\left(\frac{\partial\log S^{0}(t_{1},t_{2})}{\partial t_{2}}\right).

The same identities hold for any covariate
Z
:

-\frac{\partial\lambda_{01}(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z)}{\partial t_{1}}=a(t_{1},t_{2};Z)=-\frac{\partial\lambda_{10}(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z)}{\partial t_{2}}.

3.3.1
Three proportional hazards models are not valid

At first sight, it seems natural to extend the Cox proportional hazards model to bivariate survival data by specifying three bivariate proportional hazard models:

\displaystyle\lambda_{10}(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z)

\displaystyle=e^{\alpha^{T}Z}\lambda_{10}^{0}(t_{1},t_{2}),

\displaystyle\lambda_{01}(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z)

\displaystyle=e^{\beta^{T}Z}\lambda_{01}^{0}(t_{1},t_{2}),

\displaystyle\lambda_{11}(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z)

\displaystyle=e^{\gamma^{T}Z}\lambda_{11}^{0}(t_{1},t_{2}),

where
\lambda_{10}^{0}
,
\lambda_{01}^{0}
, and
\lambda_{11}^{0}
are valid baseline bivariate hazard functions.

However, these models are generally invalid. The continuity constraint
-\frac{\partial\lambda_{10}(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z)}{\partial t_{2}}=a(t_{1},t_{2};Z)=-\frac{\partial\lambda_{01}(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z)}{\partial t_{1}}
, together with
a(t_{1},t_{2};Z)=\lambda_{11}(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z)-\lambda_{10}(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z)\lambda_{01}(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z)
, means that any valid hazards model needs to satisfy

\displaystyle\lambda_{10}(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z)

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{t_{2}}a(t_{1},v;Z)dv+C_{1}(t_{1},Z)

\displaystyle\lambda_{01}(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z)

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{t_{1}}a(u,t_{2};Z)du+C_{2}(t_{2},Z)

\displaystyle\lambda_{11}(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z)

\displaystyle=a(t_{1},t_{2};Z)+\lambda_{10}(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z)\lambda_{01}(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z),

where
C_{1}(t_{1},Z)=\lambda_{10}(t_{1},0\mid Z)=\lambda_{1}(t_{1}\mid Z)
and
C_{2}(t_{2},Z)=\lambda_{01}(0,t_{2}\mid Z)=\lambda_{2}(t_{2}\mid Z)
are the two marginal hazard functions. Specifically, if we use two marginal Cox models such that
C_{1}(t_{1},Z)=\lambda_{1}(t_{1}\mid Z)=\lambda_{T_{1}}^{0}(t_{1})e^{\alpha^{T}Z}
and
C_{2}(t_{2},Z)=\lambda_{2}(t_{2}\mid Z)=\lambda_{T_{2}}^{0}(t_{2})e^{\beta^{T}Z}
, then a consistent model for the double-failure rate must satisfy

\lambda_{11}(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z)=a(t_{1},t_{2};Z)+\left[\lambda_{T_{1}}^{0}(t_{1})e^{\alpha^{T}Z}-\int_{0}^{t_{2}}a(t_{1},v;Z)dv\right]\left[\lambda_{T_{2}}^{0}(t_{2})e^{\beta^{T}Z}-\int_{0}^{t_{1}}a(u,t_{2};Z)du\right]

which does not admit a multiplicative form such as
\lambda_{11}(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z)=e^{\gamma^{T}Z}\lambda_{11}^{0}(t_{1},t_{2})
. Thus, the three proportional hazards models proposed in
Pons
et al. 1992
;
Tien and
Sen 2002
;
Prentice and
Zhao 2021
do not correspond to a valid bivariate survival distribution.

3.4
Dependency measures

Dependency between two failure times can be quantified using the cross ratio function, which directly relates to the bivariate hazard functions in our models. Intuitively, the bivariate hazard
\lambda_{11}(t_{1},t_{2})
captures the instantaneous joint risk, while the product
\lambda_{10}(t_{1},t_{2})\lambda_{01}(t_{1},t_{2})
represents the risk under independence. The ratio of these quantities forms the basis for classical and covariate-adjusted dependency measures, linking our model formulation to well-established concepts in bivariate survival analysis.

Clayton 1978
,
Oakes 1989
, and
Hsu and
Prentice 1996
define the
cross ratio function
by

c(t_{1},t_{2})=\frac{S(dt_{1},dt_{2})S(t_{1},t_{2})}{S(dt_{1},t_{2})S(t_{1},dt_{2})}.

The value of
c(t_{1},t_{2})
indicates positive association (
>1
), no association (
=1
), or negative local association (
<1
) between the two failure times at
(t_{1},t_{2})
.

For continuous survival times, the cross ratio function can also be expressed in terms of the bivariate hazard functions:

c(t_{1},t_{2})=\frac{\frac{\partial^{2}S(t_{1},t_{2})}{\partial t_{1}\partial t_{2}}S(t_{1},t_{2})}{\frac{\partial S(t_{1},t_{2})}{\partial t_{1}}\frac{\partial S(t_{1},t_{2})}{\partial t_{2}}}=\frac{\lambda_{11}(t_{1},t_{2})}{\lambda_{10}(t_{1},t_{2})\lambda_{01}(t_{1},t_{2})}.

We propose the
covariate-adjusted cross ratio function
:

c(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z)=\frac{\frac{\partial^{2}S(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z)}{\partial t_{1}\partial t_{2}}S(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z)}{\frac{\partial S(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z)}{\partial t_{1}}\frac{\partial S(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z)}{\partial t_{2}}}=\frac{\lambda_{11}(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z)}{\lambda_{10}(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z)\lambda_{01}(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z)}=1+\frac{a(t_{1},t_{2};Z)}{\lambda_{10}(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z)\lambda_{01}(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z)},

where
a(t_{1},t_{2};Z)=\lambda_{11}(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z)-\lambda_{10}(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z)\lambda_{01}(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z)
.

Proposition 2
.

Assume either Lehmann model. The value of the covariate-adjusted cross ratio function
c(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z)
is determined from the sign of
a^{0}(t_{1},t_{2})
:

1.

If
a^{0}(t_{1},t_{2})>0
, then
c(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z)>1
.

2.

If
a^{0}(t_{1},t_{2})=0
, then
c(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z)=1
.

3.

If
a^{0}(t_{1},t_{2})<0
, then
c(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z)<1
.

The proof of Proposition
2
follows directly from the definition of the covariate-adjusted cross ratio term and from the fact that
a(t_{1},t_{2};Z)=a^{0}(t_{1},t_{2})e^{b^{T}Z}
in the simple Lehmann model and
a(t_{1},t_{2};Z)=a^{0}(t_{1},t_{2})e^{\gamma^{T}Z}
in the generalized Lehmann model.

The corresponding weighted average of the covariate-adjusted cross ratio term is

C(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z)=\frac{\int_{0}^{t_{1}}\int_{0}^{t_{2}}\lambda_{11}(s_{1},s_{2}\mid Z)ds_{1}ds_{2}}{\int_{0}^{t_{1}}\int_{0}^{t_{2}}\lambda_{10}(s_{1},s_{2}\mid Z)\lambda_{01}(s_{1},s_{2}\mid Z)ds_{1}ds_{2}},

(6)

which is a special case of the general weighted class

D(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z)=\frac{\int_{0}^{t_{1}}\int_{0}^{t_{2}}\phi\{c(s_{1},s_{2}\mid Z)\}w_{Z}(ds_{1},ds_{2})}{\int_{0}^{t_{1}}\int_{0}^{t_{2}}w_{Z}(ds_{1},ds_{2})},

by setting
\phi(c)=c
and

w_{Z}(ds_{1},ds_{2})=\frac{S(ds_{1},s_{2}\mid Z)S(s_{1},ds_{2}\mid Z)}{[S(s_{1},s_{2}\mid Z)]^{2}}=\lambda_{10}(s_{1},s_{2}\mid Z)\lambda_{01}(s_{1},s_{2}\mid Z)ds_{1}ds_{2}.

Prentice and
Zhao 2021
proposed a similar weighted average:

\tilde{C}(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z)=\frac{\int_{0}^{t_{1}}\int_{0}^{t_{2}}S(s_{1},s_{2}\mid Z)\lambda_{11}(s_{1},s_{2}\mid Z)ds_{1}ds_{2}}{\int_{0}^{t_{1}}\int_{0}^{t_{2}}S(s_{1},s_{2}\mid Z)\lambda_{10}(s_{1},s_{2}\mid Z)\lambda_{01}(s_{1},s_{2}\mid Z)ds_{1}ds_{2}},

which is a special case of
D(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z)
using
\phi(c)=c
and weights

w_{Z}(ds_{1},ds_{2})=\frac{S(ds_{1},s_{2}\mid Z)S(s_{1},ds_{2}\mid Z)}{S(s_{1},s_{2}\mid Z)}=S(s_{1},s_{2}\mid Z)\lambda_{10}(s_{1},s_{2}\mid Z)\lambda_{01}(s_{1},s_{2}\mid Z)ds_{1}ds_{2}.

Remark 1
.

The weighted average
D(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z)
with

can be re-written in terms of the function

D(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z)=1+\frac{\int_{0}^{t_{1}}\int_{0}^{t_{2}}\frac{a(s_{1},s_{2};Z)}{\lambda_{10}(s_{1},s_{2}\mid Z)\lambda_{01}(s_{1},s_{2}\mid Z)}w_{Z}(ds_{1},ds_{2})}{\int_{0}^{t_{1}}\int_{0}^{t_{2}}w_{Z}(ds_{1},ds_{2})}.

) becomes

.

Assume either Lehmann model. The value of the covariate-adjusted weighted average

.

.

3.5
The validity of the proposed models

Here we check if and when the proposed models correspond to a valid bivariate survival function. A valid bivariate survival function of a pair of non-negative random variables needs to satisfy

1.

S(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z)\geq 0
,
\forall t_{1},t_{2}\geq 0
,

2.

S(0,0\mid Z)=1
,

3.

S_{T_{1}}(t_{1}\mid Z)=S(t_{1},0\mid Z)
and
S_{T_{2}}(t_{2}\mid Z)=S(0,t_{2}\mid Z)
,

4.

\lim_{t_{1},t_{2}\rightarrow\infty}S(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z)=0
,
\lim_{t_{1}\rightarrow\infty}S(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z)=0
, and
\lim_{t_{2}\rightarrow\infty}S(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z)=0
,

5.

S(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z)
is monotonically decreasing with respect to each coordinate,

6.

S(b,d\mid Z)-S(b,c\mid Z)-S(a,d\mid Z)+S(a,c\mid Z)\geq 0
for
a\leq b
and
c\leq d
.

Properties (1)–(3) are relatively easy to check in these generalized Lehmann models. Property (4) holds trivially for the simple Lehmann model; the generalized Lehmann model is only defined on a rectangle
[0,\tau_{1}]\times[0,\tau_{2}]
where the factorization in Equation (
1
) holds. Hence, it only needs to satisfy the validity conditions locally on this rectangle, and does not need to satisfy Property (4) globally. This means that the survival function is valid for all times within the range where the model is defined, which is sufficient for practical applications where the observation window is finite.

Property (5) holds when

\frac{\partial S(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z)}{\partial t_{1}},\ \frac{\partial S(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z)}{\partial t_{2}}<0,

and Property (6) holds when

f(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z)=\frac{\partial^{2}S(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z)}{\partial t_{1}\partial t_{2}}\geq 0.

Consequently, it is enough to check the derivatives of
S(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z)
. Specifically, we need to verify that

1.

f(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z)=\frac{\partial^{2}S(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z)}{\partial t_{1}\partial t_{2}}\geq 0
(positivity over rectangles),

2.

\frac{\partial S(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z)}{\partial t_{1}},\ \frac{\partial S(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z)}{\partial t_{2}}\leq 0
(monotonicity with respect to each coordinate).

Note that verifying that the bivariate density function
f(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z)
is non-negative is equivalent to verifying that the double-failure rate

\lambda_{11}(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z)=\frac{f(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z)}{S(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z)}

is non-negative. Similarly, due to the relations

\lambda_{10}(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z)=-\frac{\partial S(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z)/\partial t_{1}}{S(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z)},\quad\lambda_{01}(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z)=-\frac{\partial S(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z)/\partial t_{2}}{S(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z)},

checking that the two partial derivatives of the survival function are non-positive is equivalent to checking that
\lambda_{10}(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z)
and
\lambda_{01}(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z)
are both non-negative. That is, for our model to be valid, we need to verify that

\lambda_{10}(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z)\geq 0,\quad\lambda_{01}(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z)\geq 0,\quad\lambda_{11}(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z)\geq 0,

for all
(t_{1},t_{2})
where our models are defined. Below we present conditions that guarantee the validity of both the simple and generalized Lehmann models.

Proposition 4

(Conditions for the simple Lehmann model)
.

Let
a^{0}(t_{1},t_{2})=\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t_{1}\partial t_{2}}\log S^{0}(t_{1},t_{2})
denote the cross-partial derivative of the log baseline survival function. If either of the following conditions holds:

1.

a^{0}(u,v)\geq 0
for all
u,v\geq 0
, in which case there are no restrictions on the regression parameters or covariate values;

2.

\color[rgb]{0,0,0}b^{T}Z\geq 0
for all covariate vectors
Z
, in which case any valid baseline bivariate survival distribution can be used, regardless of its dependence structure.

Then the simple Lehmann model (
2
) defines a proper bivariate survival function.

The Proof of Proposition
4
can be found in Appendix
E
. Proposition
4
covers two broad scenarios.
Condition (1) requires that the cross-partial derivative of the log survival be non-negative. This condition implies that the baseline survival function is log supermodular, and includes many familiar examples, such as the bivariate Gaussian distribution with non-negative correlation, the Marshall–Olkin bivariate exponential distribution, and Archimedean copulas with positive association parameters. In this case, there are no restrictions on the regression parameters.
Condition (2), on the other hand, restricts the regression index
b^{T}Z
to be non-negative for all covariate values, but then any valid baseline bivariate survival function may be used. Note that covariates can always be shifted by a constant to ensure non-negativity; such a shift affects only the intercept, not the slopes, so the interpretation of regression parameters remains unchanged.

Proposition 5

(Sufficient conditions for the generalized Lehmann model)
.

Let
a^{0}(t_{1},t_{2})=\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t_{1}\partial t_{2}}\log S^{0}(t_{1},t_{2})
denote the cross-partial derivative of the log baseline survival function. If either of the following conditions holds:

1.

For all covariate vectors
Z
,

\gamma^{T}Z\leq\min\{\alpha^{T}Z,\beta^{T}Z\},

and
a^{0}(u,v)\geq 0
for all
(u,v)\in[0,\tau_{1}]\times[0,\tau_{2}]
;

2.

For all covariate vectors
Z
,

0\leq\gamma^{T}Z\leq\min\{\alpha^{T}Z,\beta^{T}Z\},

and
S^{0}(t_{1},t_{2})
is a valid baseline bivariate survival function;

then the three generalized Lehmann models (
3
) defines a proper bivariate survival function.

The proof of Proposition
5
can be found in Appendix
E
. Proposition
5
again highlights two sufficient regimes.
Condition (1) requires both that the covariate index
\gamma^{T}Z
does not exceed the smaller of
\alpha^{T}Z
and
\beta^{T}Z
, and that the baseline distribution satisfies
a^{0}(u,v)\geq 0
over the relevant time domain.
Condition (2) requires that
\gamma^{T}Z
lies between
0
and
\min\{\alpha^{T}Z,\beta^{T}Z\}
for all covariate values. Note that this condition can be interpreted as meaning that the magnitude of covariate effects on the dependency structure is smaller than the effect of covariates on each failure-time separately. For example, consider the effect of cigarette smoking on lung cancer and heart attack; this condition implies that the effect of smoking on each disease separately is larger than its effect on the dependency between the two diseases, which is reasonable. In this case, validity of the generalized Lehmann model is ensured for any baseline dependence structure. As in the simple Lehmann case, covariates can always be shifted by a constant to ensure non-negativity; such a shift affects only the intercepts, not the slopes, so the interpretation of regression parameters remains unchanged. An advantage of the semi-parametric models (
3
) is that the unspecified baseline components offer valuable flexibility, while parametric assumptions enter only through the covariate effects.
Finally, note that the conditions stated in Proposition
5
are sufficient but not necessary. For example, the Gumbel–Barnett survival copula, given by
C(u,v)=uv\exp(-\theta\ln(u)\ln(v))
, which specifies negative orthant dependence (NOD), together with
\gamma=\alpha+\beta
, defines a valid generalized Lehmann model even though it does not satisfy the conditions in Proposition
5
. Finding an explicit characterization of necessary conditions remains a topic for future research.

4
Estimation of model parameters

Estimation in the bivariate Cox-type setting is considerably more challenging than in the univariate case, primarily because the dependence structure introduces additional nuisance components and the bivariate hazard functions do not admit a multiplicative form. Ideally, one would like to employ a likelihood-based approach. Following
Cox 1972
, it would be desirable to define a partial likelihood function and estimate the model parameters by maximizing it. In the univariate Cox model, the partial likelihood depends only on the regression parameter
\beta
, as the nuisance parameter (the baseline hazard) cancels out under the proportional hazards assumption. In the bivariate setting, it is possible to define an analogous partial likelihood based on the covariate-adjusted double-failure rate
\lambda_{11}(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z)
. However, under both generalizations of the Cox model,
\lambda_{11}(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z)
no longer admits a multiplicative structure, and the baseline hazards do not cancel. As a result, the corresponding partial likelihood would involve additional nuisance parameters, complicating estimation. To circumvent this difficulty, we instead propose to estimate the model parameters using a generalization of the pseudo-observations approach
(
Andersen 2003
)
. We begin in Section
4.1
with a short introduction to the pseudo-observations approach for bivariate survival data. Sections
4.2
–
4.3
then detail the estimation procedures for the simple and generalized Lehmann models. Finally, Sections
4.4
–
4.5
address variance estimation and graphical methods for assessing goodness-of-fit, thereby completing a coherent framework for inference and model evaluation in the bivariate Cox-type setting.

4.1
Bivariate pseudo-observations

To estimate the parameters of our bivariate models, we employ an extension of the pseudo-observations approach for bivariate survival data
(
Travis-Lumer et al. 2025
)
. The key idea is to construct pseudo-observations for a suitably chosen functional of the bivariate survival times, which can then be used in a regression framework.

Let
f(\cdot,\cdot)
be a function of the bivariate survival times
(T_{1},T_{2})
, and let
\theta
be a univariate parameter defined by

\theta=E[f(T_{1},T_{2})].

In this paper, we primarily consider indicator functions of the form

f(T_{1},T_{2})=I(T_{1}>t_{1}^{0},T_{2}>t_{2}^{0}),

so that

\theta=E[I(T_{1}>t_{1}^{0},T_{2}>t_{2}^{0})]=P(T_{1}>t_{1}^{0},T_{2}>t_{2}^{0})=S(t_{1}^{0},t_{2}^{0}),

i.e., the bivariate survival probability at the fixed time point
(t_{1}^{0},t_{2}^{0})
.

Suppose we have
n
independent and identically distributed observations

\{(Y_{1i},\Delta_{1i},Y_{2i},\Delta_{2i},Z_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n},

and that a well-behaved estimator
\hat{\theta}
of
\theta
exists (a plug-in estimator of a smooth estimating functional).

Definition 1

(
Andersen 2003
)
.

The
pseudo-observation
for the
i
th observation is defined as

\hat{\theta}_{i}=n\hat{\theta}-(n-1)\hat{\theta}^{-i},

where
\hat{\theta}^{-i}
is the leave-one-out estimator obtained by removing the
i
th observation from the sample.

Following
Travis-Lumer et al. 2025
;
Andersen 2003
, the pseudo-observations
\hat{\theta}_{i}
serve as responses in a generalized linear model (GLM). Specifically, letting
g
denote a link function, the regression coefficients can be estimated from the GLM

g\big(E[f(T_{1},T_{2})\mid Z]\big)=\beta_{0}+\sum_{j=1}^{p}\beta_{j}Z_{j}.

(7)

This approach allows direct modeling of covariate effects on bivariate survival functionals, avoiding the complications of full likelihood-based estimation in the bivariate Cox-type setting

4.2
Fitting the simple Lehmann model

The simple Lehmann model introduced in Section
3.1
can be expressed as a semi-parametric regression model using the complementary log-log (cloglog) link:

\log\big(-\log S(t_{1},t_{2}\mid Z)\big)=\sum_{j=1}^{p}b_{j}Z_{j}+\underbrace{\log\big(-\log S^{0}(t_{1},t_{2})\big)}_{b_{0}(t_{1},t_{2})},

where
(b_{1},\ldots,b_{p})^{T}
is the vector of regression coefficients of interest, and
b_{0}(t_{1},t_{2})
is an infinite-dimensional nuisance parameter representing the link-transformed baseline bivariate survival function.

For a functional of the form
f(T_{1},T_{2})=I(T_{1}>t_{1}^{0},T_{2}>t_{2}^{0})
, the model can be written as a GLM using the inverse cloglog link:

E[f(T_{1},T_{2})\mid Z]=S(t_{1}^{0},t_{2}^{0}\mid Z)=\exp\Big(-\exp\big(b_{0}(t_{1}^{0},t_{2}^{0})+\sum_{j=1}^{p}b_{j}Z_{j}\big)\Big),

(8)

where
b_{0}(t_{1}^{0},t_{2}^{0})=\log(-\log S^{0}(t_{1}^{0},t_{2}^{0}))
is the link-transformed baseline survival probability at
(t_{1}^{0},t_{2}^{0})
and is treated as the intercept.

Equation (
8
) involves
p+1
regression parameters: the
p
-dimensional slope vector and a single intercept. When considering
K
bivariate time points
\{(t_{1}^{j},t_{2}^{j})\}_{j=1}^{K}
, each time point has its own intercept, while the slope vector remains the same, resulting in a total of
p+K
regression parameters. These parameters can be estimated using generalized estimating equations (GEE)
(
Liang and
Zeger 1986
)
:

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial b}g^{-1}(b^{T}Z_{i}^{*})\right)^{T}V_{i}^{-1}\left(\hat{\theta}_{i}-g^{-1}(b^{T}Z_{i}^{*})\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}U_{i}(b)\equiv U(b)=0,

(9)

where
b=(b_{01},\ldots,b_{0K},b_{1},\ldots,b_{p})^{T}
,
\hat{\theta}_{i}=(\hat{\theta}_{i1},\ldots,\hat{\theta}_{iK})^{T}
are the pseudo-observations for the
i
th subject,
g^{-1}(y)=\exp(-\exp(y))
is the inverse of the cloglog link,
V_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times K}
is a working covariance matrix (commonly chosen as the identity), and
Z_{ij}^{*}=(0,\ldots,1,\ldots,0,Z_{i}^{T})^{T}
has the
1
in the
j
th position. See Section 2.4 of
Travis-Lumer et al. 2025
for further details.

4.2.1
Asymptotic theory

Travis-Lumer et al. 2025
established the consistency and asymptotic normality of the estimator
\hat{b}
, defined as the solution to the GEE (
9
), for any general link function
g
. For completeness, we summarize the main result for a single bivariate time point (
K=1
). Assume that the GLM in Equation (
8
) holds for the true, but unknown parameter vector
b^{*}=(b_{0}^{*},b_{1}^{*},\ldots,b_{p}^{*})^{T}
.

Theorem 1
.

Let
\hat{b}
be the solution to the generalized estimating equations (
9
), where the pseudo-observations
\hat{\theta}_{i}
are based on the Dabrowska estimator
(
Dabrowska 1988
)
of the bivariate survival function. Assume that the censoring times are independent of both the failure times and the covariates. Then, under suitable regularity conditions
(
Overgaard
et al. 2017
, Theorem 3.4)
,

\hat{b}\xrightarrow{p}b^{*},\quad\sqrt{n}(\hat{b}-b^{*})\xrightarrow{d}N(0,M^{-1}\Sigma M^{-1}),

where
M=N(b^{*})
with

N(b)=E\Big[\big(\frac{\partial}{\partial b}g^{-1}(b^{T}Z_{i})\big)^{T}V_{i}^{-1}\frac{\partial}{\partial b}g^{-1}(b^{T}Z_{i})\Big],

and
\Sigma
is defined in Web Appendix A of
Travis-Lumer et al. 2025
.

4.3
Fitting the generalized Lehmann-type model

Here, we extend the pseudo-observations approach to estimate the regression parameters
\alpha,\beta,\gamma
. To this end, we present the generalized Lehmann model as a trivariate GLM, focusing on the bivariate survival probability at a fixed time point
(t_{1}^{0},t_{2}^{0})

The three generalized Lehmann models presented in Equation (
3

\displaystyle\log(-\log(S_{T_{1}}(t_{1}^{0}\mid Z)))

\displaystyle=\log(\Lambda_{T_{1}}^{0}(t_{1}^{0}))+\alpha^{T}Z,

A trivariate regression model

v\\

4.3.2
Estimating parameters in the trivariate regression model

Let
\hat{\theta}
denote the Dabrowska estimator of
\theta
:

\hat{\theta}=\begin{pmatrix}\hat{S}_{T_{1}}(t_{1}^{0})\\
\hat{S}_{T_{2}}(t_{2}^{0})\\
\hat{S}_{T_{1},T_{2}}(t_{1}^{0},t_{2}^{0})\end{pmatrix},

where the marginals of the Dabrowska estimator are equivalent to the Kaplan-Meier estimators
(
Kaplan and
Meier 1958
)
of the two univariate survival functions.

We define the trivariate pseudo-observations by

\hat{\theta}_{i}=n\hat{\theta}-(n-1)\hat{\theta}^{-i},\quad i=1,\dots,n,

with components

\displaystyle\hat{\theta}_{1i}

\displaystyle=n\hat{S}_{T_{1}}(t_{1}^{0})-(n-1)\hat{S}_{T_{1}}^{-i}(t_{1}^{0}),

\displaystyle\hat{\theta}_{2i}

\displaystyle=n\hat{S}_{T_{2}}(t_{2}^{0})-(n-1)\hat{S}_{T_{2}}^{-i}(t_{2}^{0}),

\displaystyle\hat{\theta}_{3i}

\displaystyle=n\hat{S}_{T_{1},T_{2}}(t_{1}^{0},t_{2}^{0})-(n-1)\hat{S}_{T_{1},T_{2}}^{-i}(t_{1}^{0},t_{2}^{0}).

We adopt a 2-step estimation procedure:

1.

Estimate the marginal parameters
\xi_{1}=(\alpha_{0},\alpha^{T},\beta_{0},\beta^{T})^{T}
via univariate pseudo-observations with generalized estimating equations (GEEs):

U_{1}(\xi_{1})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\frac{\partial m_{i}(\xi_{1})}{\partial\xi_{1}}\right)^{T}V_{i}^{-1}\left(X_{i}-m_{i}(\xi_{1})\right)=0,

where
X_{i}=(\hat{\theta}_{1i},\hat{\theta}_{2i})^{T}
,
m_{i}(\xi_{1})=\tilde{g}^{-1}(\alpha_{0}+\alpha^{T}Z_{i},\beta_{0}+\beta^{T}Z_{i})^{T}
, and
\tilde{g}^{-1}(u,v)=(\exp(-\exp(u)),\exp(-\exp(v)))^{T}
.

2.

Estimate the remaining parameters
\xi_{2}=(\gamma_{0},\gamma^{T})^{T}
by regressing

Y_{i}=\frac{\hat{\theta}_{3i}}{\hat{S}_{T_{1}}(t_{1}^{0}\mid Z_{i})\hat{S}_{T_{2}}(t_{2}^{0}\mid Z_{i})}

on
Z_{i}
using the mean function
\mu_{i}(\xi_{2})=g_{3}^{-1}(\gamma_{0}+\gamma^{T}Z_{i})
:

U_{2}(\xi_{2})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\frac{\partial\mu_{i}(\xi_{2})}{\partial\xi_{2}}\right)^{T}W_{i}^{-1}(Y_{i}-\mu_{i}(\xi_{2}))=0.

Remark 3
.

U_{2}(\xi_{2})=U_{2}(\hat{\xi}_{1},\xi_{2})
depends on the estimated
\hat{\xi}_{1}
because
\hat{S}_{T_{1}}(t_{1}^{0}\mid Z_{i})=\exp(-\exp(\hat{\alpha}_{0}+\hat{\alpha}^{T}Z_{i}))
and
\hat{S}_{T_{2}}(t_{2}^{0}\mid Z_{i})=\exp(-\exp(\hat{\beta}_{0}+\hat{\beta}^{T}Z_{i}))
are functions of
(\hat{\alpha}_{0},\hat{\alpha})
and
(\hat{\beta}_{0},\hat{\beta})

Remark 4
.

Consider the modified responses

Y_{i}=\frac{\hat{\theta}_{3i}}{\hat{S}_{T_{1}}(t_{1}^{0}\mid Z_{i})\,\hat{S}_{T_{2}}(t_{2}^{0}\mid Z_{i})},\quad i=1,\ldots,n.

The mean of
\{Y_{i}\}
determines the form of the link function
g_{3}
. Specifically, if

\bar{Y}=\tfrac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}>1
then

g_{3}(x,y,z)=\log\!\big(\log(z/(xy))\big)
,
whereas if

\bar{Y}=\tfrac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}<1
then

g_{3}(x,y,z)=\log\!\big(-\log(z/(xy))\big)
.
Thus, the link function
g_{3}
is selected empirically at no additional computational cost and without requiring knowledge of the baseline dependence structure.

If the pseudo-observations are constructed at
K
bivariate time points

\{(t_{1}^{1},t_{2}^{1}),\ldots,(t_{1}^{K},t_{2}^{K})\}
, then for the
j
th time point
(t_{1}^{j},t_{2}^{j})
,
j=1,\ldots,K
, we evaluate the mean of

Y_{ij}=\frac{\hat{\theta}_{3ij}}{\hat{S}_{T_{1}}(t_{1}^{j}\mid Z_{i})\,\hat{S}_{T_{2}}(t_{2}^{j}\mid Z_{i})},\quad i=1,\ldots,n,

and denote it by
\bar{Y}_{j}
. If the logarithms of all mean values
\bar{Y}_{1},\ldots,\bar{Y}_{K}
have the same sign, as may occur when the baseline distribution is either positive orthant dependent (POD) or negative orthant dependent (NOD), then a single link function can be used across all time points. If, however, the signs differ across time points, the analyst faces a choice:

1.

Common link
— adopt one link function based on the overall direction of the mean values, implicitly assuming that the data arise from a distribution with a consistent dependency structure (POD or NOD) across time;

2.

Time-specific links
— allow the link function to vary with
(t_{1}^{j},t_{2}^{j})
, thereby permitting the data to exhibit POD behavior at some time points and NOD behavior at others.

In practice, the analyst may also rely on domain knowledge to guide this decision: in some applications a consistent dependency direction may be more plausible (e.g., for paired organ failure times), whereas in others a mixed dependency pattern across time points may better reflect the underlying scientific mechanism.

4.5
Graphical goodness-of-fit

For univariate survival data,
Perme and
Andersen 2008
and
Andersen and
Pohar Perme 2010
propose to use pseudo-residuals and pseudo-scatterplots for checking model assumptions. Specifically, They consider the case where
f(T)=1(T>t_{0})\in\{0,1\}
and
\theta=E[f(t)]=S_{T}(t_{0})
. They then calculate the pseudo-observations
\hat{\theta}_{i}
, and treat them as the outcome. Denote by
\hat{S}_{T}(t_{0}|Z_{i})
the predicted value that is based on the model. The raw pseudo-residual are
\hat{\theta}_{i}-\hat{S}_{T}(t_{0}|Z_{i})
, and the standardized
(Pearson) residuals are

\frac{\hat{\theta}_{i}-\hat{S}_{T}(t_{0}|Z_{i})}{\sqrt{\hat{S}_{T}(t_{0}|Z_{i})(1-\hat{S}_{T}(t_{0}|Z_{i}))}}.

For the bivariate setting, consider the function
f_{3}(T_{1},T_{2})=1(T_{1}>t_{1}^{0},T_{2}>t_{2}^{0})\in\{0,1\}
and
\theta_{3}=E[f_{3}(T_{1},T_{2})]=S(t_{1}^{0},t_{2}^{0})
. For the pseudo-observations
\hat{\theta}_{3_{i}}
and the predicted probabilities
\hat{S}(t_{1}^{0},t_{2}^{0}|Z_{i})
, the raw pseudo-residual are
\hat{\theta}_{3_{i}}-\hat{S}(t_{1}^{0},t_{2}^{0}|Z_{i})
, and the standardized
(Pearson) residuals are

\frac{\hat{\theta}_{3_{i}}-\hat{S}(t_{1}^{0},t_{2}^{0}|Z_{i})}{\sqrt{\hat{S}(t_{1}^{0},t_{2}^{0}|Z_{i})(1-\hat{S}(t_{1}^{0},t_{2}^{0}|Z_{i}))}}.

We shall use these residuals as a graphical diagnostic tool to evaluate the model’s fit. If the model fits the data well, no trends should be seen in the residuals when we plot
them against a covariate. In practice, we plot the residuals
against the covariate at a few chosen time points
t_{0}\in\{t_{1},\ldots,t_{K}\}
(in which case the pseudo-values are
K
-dimensional). Finally, for the simple Lehmann model, one can also define pseudo-scatterplots where the cloglog link-transformed pseudo-values and predicted survival probabilities are both plotted against a covariate value. However, for the generalized Lehmann model there is no single link function that when applied to the pseudo-values should return a linear combination of the covariate.

5
Discussion

In this paper we proposed two novel extensions of the Cox regression model to bivariate survival data. The simple Lehmann model is easy to understand and interpret, whereas the generalized Lehmann model offers greater flexibility. Both models extend the univariate Cox framework in the sense that derivatives of the log survival function are proportional, while leaving all baseline quantities unspecified. We also derived the corresponding hazard models and identified the conditions required for these to define a valid bivariate distribution. In fact, to the best of our knowledge, this is the first work to state explicitly the relations that must hold among the two single-failure rate models and the double-failure rate model.

To estimate model parameters, we extended the pseudo-observations approach and, for the first time, applied a two-step pseudo-value–based estimation strategy. Theoretical results establish the consistency and asymptotic normality of the regression parameter estimators under mild regularity conditions. Importantly, the two-step procedure for the generalized Lehmann model ensures tractability while retaining desirable asymptotic properties. Our results further highlight that variance estimation must account for dependence between steps, and we provided explicit formulas for the asymptotic covariance structure. A further practical advantage of the pseudo-observations approach is its robustness, as model assumptions are required only at the specific time points of interest rather than across the entire time scale. One practical drawback of the pseudo-observations framework is its computational load: recalculating the base survival estimator
n+1
times can be demanding for large samples. A promising direction for future research is to replace the current pseudo-observations with infinitesimal jack-knife pseudo-values, which retain the same asymptotic properties while being much faster to compute
(
Parner
et al. 2023
)
. Nonetheless, the current approach remains tractable in moderate-sized studies and benefits from straightforward parallelization. Together, these guarantees strengthen the case for pseudo-observations as a versatile tool in complex multivariate survival settings.

From a practical perspective, the pseudo-observations framework enables straightforward implementation using standard GEE software, making the proposed models accessible to applied researchers. The regression coefficients also retain interpretable links to survival probabilities at user-specified time points, which may be more directly relevant for practitioners in medicine and epidemiology than hazard-based parameters. This aligns with a growing trend in survival analysis toward probability-based interpretations that are transparent for clinical and policy decision-making.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, efficiency may be lost relative to likelihood-based methods under correct model specification, although the robustness of the GEE approach partly mitigates this drawback. Developing likelihood-based estimation procedures for the proposed models would therefore be an important direction for future research, with the potential to improve efficiency and also allow for time-dependent covariates. Second, the current framework assumes independent censoring, as is standard in the pseudo-observations literature. Relaxing this assumption, for example via inverse probability of censoring weighting (IPCW) estimators of the joint survival function such as that of
van der
Laan et al. 2002
, may be a promising direction. More generally, one could explore alternative estimators of the joint survival function within the pseudo-observations framework, as well as the use of shrinkage or regularization methods for high-dimensional covariates.

Future research could proceed along several lines. A natural extension would be to develop a two-step estimation procedure in which the first-step estimates are based on the Cox partial likelihood, potentially improving efficiency while retaining tractability. Another important direction is the estimation of covariate-adjusted dependence measures, which would further enhance interpretability in applied contexts.

Finally, extending our models to trivariate or higher-dimensional survival data would provide valuable tools for studies involving multiple organs or family data. While the simple Lehmann model extends naturally to higher dimensions, the generalized Lehmann model does not scale as easily. Specifically, because it requires a separate regression parameter for each derivative of the
d
-dimensional log survival function, estimation would involve
2^{d}-1
parameter vectors—already seven distinct vectors for trivariate survival—which quickly becomes infeasible.

In conclusion, our study establishes a new class of Lehmann-type semi-parametric regression models for bivariate survival data, together with a tractable estimation framework based on pseudo-observations. These models extend the Cox paradigm to the multivariate setting while preserving interpretability through proportionality in the derivatives of the log survival function. Beyond their immediate applicability, they highlight several open methodological challenges, including likelihood-based estimation, necessary conditions for model validity, higher-dimensional extensions, and efficient treatment of dependent censoring, providing a roadmap for future methodological developments.

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}A_{1}(\tilde{\xi}_{1})

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{n}\left[\frac{\partial U_{1}(\xi_{1})}{\partial\xi_{1}}\Bigr|_{\xi_{1}=\tilde{\xi}_{1}}\right]=-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[\frac{\partial U_{1i}(\xi_{1})}{\partial\xi_{1}}\Bigr|_{\xi_{1}=\tilde{\xi}_{1}}\right]

converges almost surely to
M_{1}
. Consequently,

\sqrt{n}(\hat{\xi}_{1}-\xi_{1}^{*})\xrightarrow{d}N\left(0,\left[M_{1}\right]^{-1}\Sigma_{1}\left[M_{1}\right]^{-T}\right),

where we replaced the matrix
\frac{1}{n}A_{1}(\tilde{\xi}_{1})
with
M_{1}
.

Next, a first-order Taylor expansion of
U_{2}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})
around the estimated regression parameters gives

\displaystyle U_{2}(\xi_{1}^{*},\xi_{2}^{*})=\underbrace{U_{2}(\hat{\xi}_{1},\hat{\xi}_{2})}_{0}+\frac{\partial U_{2}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})}{\partial\xi_{1}}\Bigr|_{(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})=(\bar{\xi}_{1},\bar{\xi}_{2})}(\xi_{1}^{*}-\hat{\xi}_{1})+\frac{\partial U_{2}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})}{\partial\xi_{2}}\Bigr|_{(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})=(\bar{\xi}_{1},\bar{\xi}_{2})}(\xi_{2}^{*}-\hat{\xi}_{2}),

where
(\bar{\xi}_{1},\bar{\xi}_{2})
is between
(\hat{\xi}_{1},\hat{\xi}_{2})
and
(\xi_{1}^{*},\xi_{2}^{*})
. Denote
B_{1}=-\frac{\partial U_{2}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})}{\partial\xi_{1}}\Bigr|_{(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})=(\bar{\xi}_{1},\bar{\xi}_{2})}
and
B_{2}=-\frac{\partial U_{2}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})}{\partial\xi_{2}}\Bigr|_{(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})=(\bar{\xi}_{1},\bar{\xi}_{2})}
. Then,
U_{2}(\xi_{1}^{*},\xi_{2}^{*})=B_{1}(\hat{\xi}_{1}-\xi_{1}^{*})+B_{2}(\hat{\xi}_{2}-\xi_{2}^{*})

and thus,

\displaystyle\hat{\xi}_{2}-\xi_{2}^{*}=[B_{2}]^{-1}\left(U_{2}(\xi_{1}^{*},\xi_{2}^{*})-B_{1}(\hat{\xi}_{1}-\xi_{1}^{*})\right).

Using Equation (
18
), we can write this also in terms of the estimating function
U_{1}
such that

\displaystyle\hat{\xi}_{2}-\xi_{2}^{*}=[B_{2}]^{-1}\left(U_{2}(\xi_{1}^{*},\xi_{2}^{*})-B_{1}[A_{1}(\tilde{\xi}_{1})]^{-1}U_{1}(\xi_{1}^{*})\right).

Consequently,

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}(\hat{\xi}_{2}-\xi_{2}^{*})

\displaystyle=n[B_{2}]^{-1}\left(n^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}U_{2}(\xi_{1}^{*},\xi_{2}^{*})-B_{1}[A_{1}(\tilde{\xi}_{1})]^{-1}n^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}U_{1}(\xi_{1}^{*})\right)

\displaystyle=\left[\frac{1}{n}B_{2}\right]^{-1}\left(\underbrace{n^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}U_{2}(\xi_{1}^{*},\xi_{2}^{*})}_{N(0,\Sigma_{2})}-B_{1}[A_{1}(\tilde{\xi}_{1})]^{-1}\underbrace{n^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}U_{1}(\xi_{1}^{*})}_{N(0,\Sigma_{1})}\right)

is asymptotically equivalent to the sum of two dependent MVN random variables. Specifically,
\sqrt{n}(\hat{\xi}_{2}-\xi_{2}^{*})
converges in distribution to
W_{1}-W_{2}
, where
W_{1}\sim N\left(0,\left[\frac{1}{n}B_{2}\right]^{-1}\Sigma_{2}\left[\frac{1}{n}B_{2}\right]^{-T}\right)
,
W_{2}\sim N\left(0,\left[\frac{1}{n}B_{2}\right]^{-1}B_{1}[A_{1}(\tilde{\xi}_{1})]^{-1}\Sigma_{1}[A_{1}(\tilde{\xi}_{1})]^{-T}B_{1}^{T}\left[\frac{1}{n}B_{2}\right]^{-T}\right)
, and

\displaystyle\text{Cov}(W_{1},W_{2})

\displaystyle=\left[\frac{1}{n}B_{2}\right]^{-1}\text{Cov}\left(n^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}U_{2}(\xi_{1}^{*},\xi_{2}^{*}),n^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}U_{1}(\xi_{1}^{*})\right)[A_{1}(\tilde{\xi}_{1})]^{-T}B_{1}^{T}\left[\frac{1}{n}B_{2}\right]^{-T}

\displaystyle=\left[\frac{1}{n}B_{2}\right]^{-1}\Sigma_{21}[A_{1}(\tilde{\xi}_{1})]^{-T}B_{1}^{T}\left[\frac{1}{n}B_{2}\right]^{-T}.

In summary,
\sqrt{n}(\hat{\xi}_{2}-\xi_{2}^{*})
converges in distribution to
W_{1}-W_{2}\sim N(0,var(W_{1}-W_{2}))
, where

\displaystyle var(W_{1}-W_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\frac{1}{n}B_{2}\right]^{-1}\left(\Sigma_{2}+B_{1}[A_{1}(\tilde{\xi}_{1})]^{-1}\Sigma_{1}[A_{1}(\tilde{\xi}_{1})]^{-T}B_{1}^{T}-\Sigma_{21}[A_{1}(\tilde{\xi}_{1})]^{-T}B_{1}^{T}-B_{1}[A_{1}(\tilde{\xi}_{1})]^{-1}\Sigma_{21}^{T}\right)\left[\frac{1}{n}B_{2}\right]^{-T},

and each of these matrices can be estimated quite easily, where we will be replacing the intermediate values
\tilde{\xi}_{1},\bar{\xi}_{1},\bar{\xi}_{2}
with the estimated values
\hat{\xi}_{1},\hat{\xi}_{2}
.
∎
