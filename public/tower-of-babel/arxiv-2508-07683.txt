TAR-TVG: Enhancing VLMs with Timestamp Anchor-Constrained Reasoning for Temporal Video Grounding
Chaohong Guo, Xun Mo, Yongwei Nie, Xuemiao Xu, Chao Xu, Fei Yu, Chengjiang Long

Abstract
Temporal Video Grounding (TVG) aims to precisely localize video segments corresponding to natural language queries, which is a critical capability for long-form video understanding. Although existing reinforcement learning approaches encourage models to generate reasoning chains before predictions, they fail to explicitly constrain the reasoning process to ensure the quality of the final temporal predictions. To address this limitation, we propose Timestamp Anchor-constrained Reasoning for Temporal Video Grounding (TAR-TVG), a novel framework that introduces timestamp anchors within the reasoning process to enforce explicit supervision to the thought content. These anchors serve as intermediate verification points. More importantly, we require each reasoning step to produce increasingly accurate temporal estimations, thereby ensuring that the reasoning process contributes meaningfully to the final prediction. To address the challenge of low-probability anchor generation in models (e.g., Qwen2.5-VL-3B), we develop an efficient self-distillation training strategy: (1) initial GRPO training to collect 30K high-quality reasoning traces containing multiple timestamp anchors, (2) supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on distilled data, and (3) final GRPO optimization on the SFT-enhanced model. This three-stage training strategy enables robust anchor generation while maintaining reasoning quality. Experiments show that our model achieves state-of-the-art performance while producing interpretable, verifiable reasoning chains with progressively refined temporal estimations.

Introduction

Temporal Video Grounding (TVG) is a fundamental task in video understanding that requires models to precisely localize temporal segments in untrimmed videos corresponding to natural language queries
(
Anne Hendricks et al. 2017
;
Gao et al. 2017
;
Krishna et al. 2017
;
Lin et al. 2023
;
Zhang et al. 2023b
)
. Imaging in smart home environments, TVG can enable AI assistants to accurately locate moments like “when the child entered the kitchen” from hours of footage
(
Yang et al. 2025
;
Grauman et al. 2022
;
Sigurdsson et al. 2016
)
. This capability is critical for numerous real-world applications, including video surveillance
(
Tian et al. 2009
)
, intelligent video retrieval
(
Anne Hendricks et al. 2017
;
Caba Heilbron et al. 2015
)
, and human-computer interaction systems
(
Zhou et al. 2023
)
.

Previous TVG methods can be broadly categorized into three paradigms: (1) Vision Language Pretraining (VLP) approaches leverage pretrained visual encoders in combination with specialized feature fusion and grounding modules
(
Jang et al. 2023
;
Mu, Mo, and Li 2024
;
Zhu et al. 2023
;
Zhang et al. 2021a
;
Zeng et al. 2020
)
. Although effective, these methods suffer from error accumulation due to disjoint feature extraction and grounding steps
(
Wang et al. 2025b
)
. (2) Recent methods have proposed using VLMs to directly predict the start and end timestamps of the target segment
(
Qu et al. 2024
;
Ren et al. 2024
;
Huang et al. 2024
;
Zeng et al. 2025
)
, bypassing any intermediate reasoning processes and therefore lacking interpretability. (3) Reasoning-Enhanced Models, such as works like TimeZero
(
Wang et al. 2025c
)
, Time-R1
(
Wang et al. 2025b
)
and VideoChat-R1
(
Li et al. 2025
)
, inspired by DeepSeek-R1
(
Guo et al. 2025
)
, adopt chain-of-thought prompting where models first generate
<think> ...</think>
reasoning chains before producing
<answer> ...</answer>
tags.

Although reasoning-enhanced models demonstrate promising results, they share a critical flaw: the generated reasoning processes lack explicit constraints to ensure their relevance to final predictions. These methods employ GRPO (Group Relative Policy Optimization)
(
Guo et al. 2025
)
with two primary rewards: Format reward for structured output, and IoU reward for final timestamp accuracy
(
Wang et al. 2025c
;
Wang et al. 2025b
;
Li et al. 2025
)
. However, this framework does not provide direct supervision over the reasoning content itself. Although free-form reasoning enables flexible exploration, it fails to effectively constrain the model’s reasoning behavior, thereby missing a valuable opportunity to enhance model capability through guided reasoning.

To guide the reasoning process, we introduce Timestamp Anchor-constrained Reasoning (TAR), which enforces explicit supervision through intermediate temporal anchors. The motivation stems from the fact that human temporal reasoning naturally proceeds via progressive refinement (e.g., “first half of video”
\rightarrow
“around 2:00-2:45”
\rightarrow
“2:28-2:32”). Current models lack this gradual verification capability. To mimic this behavior, our model inserts
<timestamp>...</timestamp>
anchors within the
<think>...</think>
thinking process (see Figure
1
). These anchors serve as checkpoints that enable explicit evaluation of intermediate reasoning steps. More importantly, we constrain that early anchors establish coarse temporal boundaries and subsequent anchors progressively narrow or correct the range. This mechanism introduces two key advantages over previous approaches
(
Wang et al. 2025c
;
Wang et al. 2025b
;
Li et al. 2025
)
. First, the anchor system provides interpretable verification points: we can directly assess whether each reasoning step contributes meaningfully to the final prediction. Second, it imposes an inductive bias that mirrors human-like temporal reasoning patterns, where initial estimates are refined through successive approximations. Ultimately, the proposed timestamp anchors transform the reasoning process from an unconstrained ”black box” into a transparent, verifiable chain of temporal refinements.

However, models such as Qwen2.5-VL-3B/7B struggle to generate reasoning traces with valid timestamp tags even when explicitly prompted. Our experiments reveal that in 76% cases, these models either fail to insert timestamp tags entirely or produce temporally inconsistent sequences. Without such tags, the proposed anchor-based constraints become inapplicable, severely degrading training efficiency. To address this, we introduce a three-stage training strategy. (1)
Initial GRPO Training
: We first apply our GRPO-based reinforcement learning algorithm, which incorporates timestamp anchor constraints. Although this stage infrequently yields perfect outputs, it occasionally generates usable reasoning traces. We collect these rare high-quality samples (where timestamp tags exhibit progressive accuracy improvements) to curate a 30K Chain-of-Thought (CoT) dataset. (2)
Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)
: The curated dataset is used to fine-tune the model via SFT, significantly improving its ability to generate timestamp-anchored reasoning. (3)
GRPO Re-training
: The SFT-enhanced model undergoes further refinement using GRPO with anchor constraints, leveraging its improved initialization.
This GRPO
\rightarrow
SFT
\rightarrow
GRPO strategy enhances training efficiency and ultimately produces models with superior reasoning capabilities.

In summary, the key contributions of this paper are: (1) We propose TAR-TVG, a new reinforcement learning method that introduces timestamp anchors into the model’s reasoning process. These anchors enable step-by-step verification and enforce progressive refinement constraints. This approach effectively guides the model to better understand temporal grounding in videos. (2) We present an efficient training strategy that automatically extracts high-quality reasoning traces during model training, eliminating the need for manual dataset creation. (3) Extensive experiments demonstrate the superiority of our method. On Charades-STA, TAR-TVG achieves new state-of-the-art performance, with the best mIoU (61.1) and the highest R1@0.7 (50.2), outperforming existing approaches.

Related Works

Temporal Video Grounding.

Significant advances in video understanding have driven the development of tasks such as video captioning
(
Lin et al. 2024
;
Zhao et al. 2023
)
, video question answering
(
Xiao et al. 2021
;
Zhang et al. 2023a
)
, and video-text retrieval
(
Lin et al. 2022
;
Miech et al. 2019
)
. The task of Temporal Video Grounding
(
Gao et al. 2017
;
Krishna et al. 2017
)
precisely locates text-related segments from videos through text queries. This field can be divided into three main categories:

The first category involves Vision-Language Pre-training (VLP) models. These methods
(
Zhang et al. 2021b
;
Lei 2021
;
Lin et al. 2023
;
Cao et al. 2025
)
utilize pre-trained visual and text encoders
(
Radford et al. 2021
;
Wang et al. 2024b
)
to extract features, which then undergo cross-modal fusion. Finally, a task-specific head is used to output the final results. These methods typically suffer from error accumulation and heavily rely on the pre-trained models.

The second category consists of large language models (LLMs) with fine-tuned visual encoders, such as LLaMA
(
Touvron et al. 2023
)
and the LLaVA Series
(
Liu et al. 2023
;
Liu et al. 2024a
)
, or fine-tuned open-source large-scale vision-language models like Qwen2-VL
(
Wang et al. 2024a
)
and Qwen2.5-VL
(
Bai et al. 2025
)
. However, these approaches
(
Qu et al. 2024
;
Huang et al. 2024
;
Zeng et al. 2025
;
Ren et al. 2024
)
rely on token-level alignment, lack interpretability, and exhibit limited temporal awareness.

Along with the development of reasoning models and reinforcement learning (RL) in LLMs
(
Jin et al. 2025
;
Wu et al. 2025
)
, TimeZero
(
Wang et al. 2025c
)
proposed a reasoning-driven Large Vision-Language Model (LVLM) for video grounding, achieving new state-of-the-art results. Subsequent work, Time-R1
(
Wang et al. 2025b
)
, used more data while optimizing the reward function. VideoChat-R1
(
Li et al. 2025
)
expanded video tasks to spatio-temporal perception tasks such as video captioning, video question answering, and object tracking. However, these RL-based methods do not apply explicit constraint on the reasoning process, while our method fill this gap.

Vision-Language models Reasoning and Reward Optimization.

Recent advances in reasoning with large language models (LLMs)
(
Achiam et al. 2023
)
have demonstrated outstanding performance in mathematics
(
Liu et al. 2024b
)
and coding
(
Gao et al. 2024
)
tasks, as exemplified by models such as OpenAI’s o1 and DeepSeek’s R1
(
Guo et al. 2025
)
. Subsequent efforts have extended reasoning capabilities to the visual domain, initially focusing on images. More recently, increasing attention has been given to reasoning over videos, for example, Video-R1
(
Feng et al. 2025
)
, which enhances reinforcement learning for video through T-GRPO, and Long-RL
(
Chen et al. 2025
)
, which targets reasoning over long videos.
These methods are all based on task-specific metrics as rewards and use rule-based approaches. Some methods also employ additional reward models
(
Wang et al. 2025a
)
which evaluates the reasoning process of the model’s output.
However, such rule-based approaches often result in a disconnect between the reasoning process and the final answer, while purely model-based rewards can be computationally expensive, especially when the rewards need to be assigned to the content of the reasoning itself. To strike a balance, we adopt a rule-based method that rewards intermediate steps by introducing timestamps during the thinking process as intermediate verification points, effectively combining the advantages of both approaches.

Method

Given a video
V
, Temporal Video Grounding (TVG) can be formulated as
[\hat{t}_{s},\hat{t}_{e}]=f(V,p,q)
, where
[\hat{t}_{s},\hat{t}_{e}]
denotes the predicted start and end timestamps of the target segment,
p
is the used prompt, and
q
is the original query. The ground-truth temporal segment is represented as
[t_{s},t_{e}]
.

Figure 2 illustrates our reinforcement learning framework employing the GRPO algorithm. Given video, query and prompt inputs, the Large Vision-Language Model (LVLM, specifically Qwen-2.5VL-3B/7B in this work) is prompted to generate structured outputs comprising: (1) timestamp predictions
\hat{t}_{s},\hat{t}_{e}
enclosed in
<answer>...</answer>
tags, and (2) reasoning traces within
<think>...</think>
block containing intermediate
<timestamp>…</timestamp>
predictions. For these outputs, we compute three reward functions: (1) Format Reward ensures structural compliance of response templates, (2) Soft IoU Reward measures temporal overlap between predicted segments and ground truth, and (3) Timestamp Anchor-based Reward (our core innovation) constrains the reasoning process by requiring multiple progressive
<timestamp>
predictions within
<think>
blocks, and monotonically improving accuracy across successive timestamps.

The remainder of this section first details the GRPO framework, then elaborates on each reward function, and concludes with our three-stage training strategy.

Preliminary of GRPO

Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)
(
Guo et al. 2025
)
is a variant of Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)
(
Schulman et al. 2017
)
in reinforcement learning. In the GRPO framework, given a query
q
, the policy model
\pi_{\theta}
(i.e. the LVLM) generates
G
distinct candidate responses
o=\{o_{1},o_{2},\dots,o_{G}\}
. A reward function, which is a group of rule-based rewards in our method, assigns a reward score to each response, yielding
\{r(o_{1}),r(o_{2}),\dots,r(o_{G})\}
. Based on the recently optimized state of the policy model
\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}
, a weighted sum reward is calculated, formulated as:

R(o)=\sum_{i=1}^{G}\frac{\pi_{\theta}(o_{i})}{\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(o_{i})}\cdot\frac{r(o_{i})-\mathrm{mean}(\{r(o_{i})\}_{i=1}^{G})}{\mathrm{std}(\{r(o_{i})\}_{i=1}^{G})}.

(1)

To prevent large deviations from the original model
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
, a KL-divergence penalty with a scaling coefficient
\alpha
is added to the training objective:

\max_{\pi_{\theta}}\mathbb{E}_{o\sim\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(p)}[R(o)-\alpha\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_{\theta}\|\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})].\\

(2)

Format Reward

We prompt (see the prompt in the supplemental material) the model to output in the following structure:

<think> ...

<timestamp> ... </timestamp> ... <timestamp> ... </timestamp> ...

</think>

<answer> ... </answer>
.

This is different from TimeZero
(
Wang et al. 2025c
)
, Time-R1
(
Wang et al. 2025b
)
, and VideoChat-R1
(
Li et al. 2025
)
that do not insert timestamps into the think block.

In our
<think>
block, the model first provides an initial analysis of the video, then outputs a preliminary answer in the first
<timestamp>
block. It then continues to analyze this initial answer in combination with its earlier reasoning and the video content, producing a refined or corrected answer in the second
<timestamp>
block, and so on until it wants to stop. In the
<answer>
block, the model provides its final answer, which we set as the same in the last timestamp tag in the think block. We use regular expressions to verify whether the model’s output conforms to this required format. If the format is correct, we give a score of 3 to the model, otherwise 0:

r_{\mathrm{Format}}(o)=\begin{cases}0,\mathrm{if}\ o\mathrm{\ does\ not\ match\ format}\\
3,\mathrm{if}\ o\mathrm{\ matches\ format}\end{cases}

(3)

Soft IoU Reward

In previous work, e.g.,
(
Wang et al. 2025c
)
, they usually adopt the following IoU loss:

r_{\mathrm{IoU}}(o)=\frac{\max\left(0,\min(t_{e},\hat{t}_{e})-\max(t_{s},\hat{t}_{s})\right)}{\max(t_{e},\hat{t}_{e})-\min(t_{s},\hat{t}_{s})}.

(4)

In this paper, we propose a slightly different soft IoU to compute the reward for the model’s output:

r_{\mathrm{sIoU}}(o)=\frac{\min(t_{e},\hat{t}_{e})-\max(t_{s},\hat{t}_{s})}{\max(t_{e},\hat{t}_{e})-\min(t_{s},\hat{t}_{s})}.

(5)

The only difference is that there is no max operation. Soft IoU can measure the distance between the predicted and ground-truth segments even when there is no overlap which the previous IoU cannot achieve, which enables faster and more stable training. For example, in the early stages of training, if the model predicts a segment from 15.4s to 18.0s while the ground truth is from 3.4s to 12.2s, soft IoU yields a score of -0.21, whereas standard IoU gives 0. As a result, the standard IoU is not sensitive to the degree of misalignment in the predictions, leading to slower convergence.

Timestamp Anchor-based Reward

As shown in Figure
1
and
2
, previous approaches generate plain text in-between
<think>
tags while we generate multiple
<timestamp>
tags within the reasoning content in the
<think>
block.

Let
s
be the the number of
<timestamp>
tags. We extract the time durations in-between them, and compute three parts of Timestamp Anchor-based Reward. First, we compute the weighted sum of the soft IoUs between the time ranges in
<timestamp>
tags and the ground truth:

\text{TAR}_{1}(o)=\sum_{i=1}^{s}i\ \text{sIoU}_{i}(o),

(6)

where
\text{sIoU}_{i}(o)
computes the soft IoU score for the
i^{th}
timestamp of output
o
, and
i
is the weight, with more recent timestamps having greater weights, requiring them to be more accurate.
Second, we apply a penalty to
s
if it deviates from 2 to prevent reward hacking of generating too many
<timestamp>
tags:

\text{TAR}_{2}(o)=(s-2)^{2}.

(7)

Finally, we explicitly require that later timestamps be more accurate than earlier ones, achieving progressive refinement. The progressive refinement reward for the
i^{th}
timestamp of output
o
can be formalized as:

\delta_{i}(o)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }\text{sIoU}_{i}(o)>\text{sIoU}_{i-1}(o),\\
-1&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(8)

Based on this,
\text{TAR}_{3}
is defined as:

\text{TAR}_{3}(o)=\sum_{i=2}^{s}\delta_{i}(o).

(9)

Finally, our timestamp anchor-based reward function is defined as:

r_{\text{TAR}}(o)=\text{TAR}_{1}(o)-\beta\ \text{TAR}_{2}(o)+\gamma\ \text{TAR}_{3}(o),

(10)

where
\beta
=5,
\gamma
=1 are hyperparameters. The overall reward of the whole reinforcement learning is formulated as:

r(o)=r_{\mathrm{Format}}(o)+r_{\text{sIoU}}(o)+r_{\text{TAR}}(o).

(11)

GRPO-SFT-GRPO training strategy. Stage 1 is initial GRPO training process to collect 30K CoT data which are used to perform SFT in stage 2. Finally, GRPO is re-applied based on SFT-improved model in stage 3.

GRPO-SFT-GRPO Training Strategy

During the training of the reinforcement learning model illustrated in Figure
2
, we found it particularly difficult to train. The reason is that, despite designing various prompts to guide the model to output timestamp tags containing results in the reasoning process, the model consistently struggled to generate these tags successfully. A simple statistical analysis revealed that in 76% of cases, the model failed to produce the desired reasoning process. With such a high failure rate, training the model proved extremely challenging.

To address this issue, we propose a three-step training strategy called GRPO-SFT-GRPO (as shown in Figure
3
). First, we directly execute the reinforcement learning process depicted in Figure
2
, implemented by GRPO. Given a video
V
, a prompt
p
, and a query
q
, the GRPO algorithm generates multiple outputs
o
and computes a reward
r
for each, ultimately optimizing the model based on the group advantages
A
. During this process, we accumulate 186K outputs
o
and filter them down to 30K. The filtering criteria were:
r
must be greater than 6.4, the number of generated timestamp tags must be at least 2, the sIoU of the first timestamp must exceed 0.5, and the second one must exceed 0.7. The core idea of this step is that, although the probability is low, some high-quality data can still be generated. This approach allows us to collect a high-quality dataset without any manual intervention.

Second, using the 30K dataset, we perform supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on the model to increase the likelihood of inserting timestamps during the reasoning process.

Finally, we re-apply the GRPO reinforcement learning training (as in Figure
3
) on the SFT-improved model. At this stage, the training efficiency is improved significantly.

Experiments

Experimental Setup

Benchmarks.
We evaluate our model on four video grounding datasets: Charades-STA
(
Gao et al. 2017
)
(6,672 indoor activity videos, 30.6s avg., 12,408 train/3,720 test clip-query pairs), QVHighlights
(
Lei 2021
)
(4,855 train/1,020 val video-text pairs), ActivityNet-Caption
(
Caba Heilbron et al. 2015
)
(37,421 train/17,505 val/17,031 test samples), and TVGBench
(
Wang et al. 2025b
)
(800 samples). The latter aggregates test sets from ActivityNet-Caption, Charades-STA and three others as a comprehensive benchmark, evaluating 11 query types with balanced video-query distribution for fair assessment.

Implementation details.

We use Qwen2.5-VL-3B/7B as our base models. We first generate and filter the 30K CoT data, and then train an SFT model using this data. We then fine-tune the models for 3 epochs on the Charades-STA training set, followed by 1 epoch of training on a subset of the QVHighlights training set. The SFT stage for both the 3B and 7B models is conducted using 4
\times
NVIDIA A100 40GB GPUs and is implemented via LoRA fine-tuning with a rank
r=16
and scaling factor
\alpha=32
. For GRPO training, the 3B model is trained with 8
\times
A100 40GB GPUs, and the 7B model is trained with 16
\times
A100 40GB GPUs.

Number of Timestamps.

We conduct extensive experiments on the number of timestamps (anchor points), as shown in Table
4
. The best performance (61.1 mIoU) is achieved when the number of timestamps is 2. In practice, using too many timestamps leads to slower convergence and worse evaluation results during training.

Impact of Three-Stage Training Strategy.

As shown in Table
6
, incorporating our high-quality CoT data for supervised fine-tuning significantly improves the model’s temporal grounding ability during GRPO training. Training with reinforcement learning alone yields only 45.9 R1@0.7, and using chain-of-thought (CoT) supervision alone in supervised fine-tuning (SFT) still limits the model’s ability to understand videos. However, when reinforcement learning is further applied on top of the SFT-trained model, both reasoning ability and video comprehension improve, leading to the final performance of 50.2 R1@0.7 and 61.1 mIoU.

Discussion about Thinking Length.

We recorded the thinking lengths of models of different sizes and analyzed the impact of performing SFT on both thinking length and performance metrics. Figure
5
show that the 3B model generally produces longer thinking sequences compared to the 7B model. For example, when only GRPO is applied, the 3B model generates on average 10.1 (186.1-175.0) more tokens than the 7B model. After SFT, all models show improved performance. However, the thinking length of the 3B model remains nearly unchanged. For the 7B model, the thinking length decreases from 175 tokens to 158.4. These results indicate that stronger models require less thinking to achieve better performance.

Conclusion

We propose TAR-TVG, a Timestamp Anchor-constrained Reasoning framework for temporal video grounding. We address the limitations of existing methods in supervising the think process by introducing a progressive, step-by-step timestamp refinement mechanism leading to increasingly accurate predictions. Our contributions include: (1) TAR-TVG, a progressive reasoning framework designed for the TVG task; (2) an efficient training strategy which can automatically generate high-quality datasets during training; (3) state-of-the-art performance on Charades-STA, along with improved results on other TVG benchmarks. Our method achieves the best reported mIoU of 61.1 on the Charades-STA dataset.
