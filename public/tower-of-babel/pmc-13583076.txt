Mental illness among persons with and without dementia in continuing care: A multi-jurisdictional, repeated cross-sectional study
Authors: Joey Champigny, David B. Hogan, Ruth Ann Marrie, Jin Luo, Xueyi Chen, Okechukwu Ekuma, Randy Walld, Erind Dvorani, James M. Bolton, Zahra Goodarzi, Ping Li, Dallas Seitz, Andrea Gruneir, Matthias Hoben, Nathan Herrmann, Luke Mondor, Colleen J. Maxwell, on behalf of the EMHLTH-CC Research Team
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLimited epidemiologic data exists for mental illnesses among at-risk home care (HC) and residential long-term care (LTC) recipients.ObjectiveTo estimate the annual prevalence (April 1, 2012-March 31, 2023) of various mental illnesses among HC and LTC populations (versus matched comparators) with and without dementia in the Canadian provinces of Ontario, Alberta, and Manitoba.MethodsParallel repeated cross-sectional studies were conducted in each province using linked health administrative data. HC and LTC recipients aged ≥18 years were matched to comparators on demographics, dementia and comorbidity. Prevalence estimates were derived using validated case definitions for any mental illness, mood/anxiety disorders, depression and anxiety (with/without drug claims), schizophrenia, bipolar disorder and suicide attempt. Prevalence ratios (95% CIs) were estimated with modified Poisson regression models.ResultsIncluded were 682,466 HC clients and 233,499 LTC residents. Mental illnesses were common in HC and LTC settings across all three provinces. Prevalence estimates were typically higher among care recipients versus matched comparators and among persons with versus without dementia. Up to 70% of HC clients and 80% of LTC residents with dementia had any mental illness; in both settings, 40% had mood/anxiety disorders, 4% bipolar disorders, and between 2%-6% schizophrenia. Suicide attempts were rare (0.5–0.6%). Prevalence varied by province and was higher for case algorithms including drug claims.ConclusionsThe high prevalence of mental illness and its common co-occurrence with dementia in HC and LTC recipients illustrates the complexity and challenges of care in these populations and raises concerns about potential unmet mental health needs.

Introduction

Mental illnesses are common and associated with significant disability and morbidity among older populations globally.1,2 A recent systematic review and meta-analysis reported an overall prevalence of 19.2% (95%CI: 13.0–27.5%) for depressive and 16.5% (95%CI: 11.1–22.8%) for anxiety disorders in older adults. 3 Dementia is also prevalent in older populations, 4 and many individuals with dementia experience mental illness.5–7 Differentiating between the behavioral and psychological symptoms of dementia and a separate mental illness can be challenging.8–10 Masking, misattribution, or exacerbation of one condition by the other may occur.11,12 Regardless of the underlying relationship, the co-occurrence of dementia and mental illness symptoms may significantly affect the health, healthcare use, quality of care and life of older adults.13–15

Comprehensive estimates of mental illness prevalence among persons with dementia receiving home care (HC) or living in residential long-term care (LTC) settings (including nursing homes and assisted living) are scarce, particularly for conditions other than depression.16–18 This is a significant knowledge gap as many HC and LTC recipients have dementia19,20 or other health and functional impairments21,22 that pose a risk for mental illness and unmet mental health care needs. 1 A retrospective cohort study of nursing home residents with dementia in the U.S. reported that 68.5% had a psychiatric disorder and 47.9% had more than one type of disorder. 7 Among those with a psychiatric disorder, depression (78%) and anxiety (49%) were the most prevalent, though 9.7% had schizophrenia and 6.7% a bipolar disorder. In U.S. assisted living settings, Temkin-Greener and colleagues found that one-third of residents had co-occurring dementia and major depression, 11.3% had dementia and schizophrenia, and 8.1% had dementia and bipolar disorder. 23 An investigation of neuropsychiatric symptoms among a small U.S. sample of HC clients with dementia reported prevalence estimates of 51% for depression/dysphoria and 59% for anxiety. 16 Canadian studies of mental illness in HC and LTC settings have largely focused on the overall prevalence of significant depressive symptoms, rather than prevalence of specific disorders, reporting modestly higher estimates in LTC (24–27% overall, 23% with both depressive symptoms and cognitive impairment)22,24 than in HC (20%). 24

The use of validated case definitions for mental illness based on administrative health data offers opportunities to generate population-based and timely estimates of a diverse range of mental illnesses among those with (and without) dementia receiving HC and LTC in Canada. Utilizing this approach, our study objectives were to: (i) estimate the annual prevalence, from April 1, 2012 to March 31, 2023, of various mental illnesses among adults receiving HC services or residing in LTC, stratified by the presence/absence of dementia, in three Canadian provinces (Ontario, Alberta, Manitoba), and (ii) contrast the prevalence estimates and ratios (comparing HC versus matched community comparators; LTC versus matched HC comparators) for persons with and without dementia, across settings and provinces for the most recent fiscal year. We hypothesized that prevalence estimates would be higher among persons with dementia than without, and among our study populations versus matched comparator groups.

Methods

Study design and setting

This study comprised parallel, repeated cross-sectional investigations of adults aged ≥18 years receiving publicly funded HC or LTC, and their matched comparator groups, from Ontario (ON), Manitoba (MB), and Alberta (AB). Specifically, HC referred to the provision of scheduled health or personal support services in one's home and LTC to a residential setting providing unscheduled 24 h/day, 7-days/week nursing and personal care (i.e., nursing homes). The study accrual period spanned April 1, 2012 (2013 for AB) to March 31, 2023. In all three jurisdictions, access to publicly funded HC and LTC is centrally coordinated through provincial continuing care services. These provinces were selected because of the availability of comparable linked administrative databases and to permit exploration of regions of different sizes, urbanicity, ethno-cultural composition and healthcare practices in Canada, 25 all factors relevant to mental illness epidemiology.

In each province, publicly funded health insurance provides universal coverage for medically necessary hospital and physician services, most prescription medications (with variation in coverage by age group) and select health and supportive care services in HC and LTC. However, continuing care policies, services and delivery differ across provinces,21,26 including access to and utilization of mental health care. 27 Further specific details on provincial differences in select demographic and health system characteristics are summarized in Supplemental Table 1.

Data sources

Each province holds prospectively collected, population-level health service use data. As privacy regulations prevent individual-level data from leaving each province, comparable data sources and harmonized definitions were used to identify the study populations and measures, with analyses occurring separately in each province. These databases have been widely used to investigate health care and outcomes in HC15,27,28 and LTC.29–31 Databases (measures) examined included: Home Care and Long-Term Care (service and Resident Assessment Instrument (RAI) assessment dates); Population Registry (demographics, dates of birth, death & health insurance coverage, postal code linked to CENSUS data for urban/rural residence and area-based socioeconomic measures); the Discharge Abstract Database (DAD) (hospital admission/discharge dates and diagnoses using International Classification of Diseases & Related Health Problems, 10th Revision, Canada [ICD-10-CA] codes and procedures using Canadian Classification of Health Interventions [CCI] codes); the Ontario Mental Health Reporting System (OMHRS) (admission/discharge dates and diagnoses using International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision, Clinical Modification [ICD-9-CM] or ICD-10-CM codes for those in designated adult inpatient mental health beds in general/specialty facilities); the National Ambulatory Care Reporting System (NACRS) (admission/discharge dates and diagnoses using ICD-10-CA and procedures for all emergency department and day surgery visits to hospital); Physician Services Claims (dates and setting for physician services and a single diagnosis code [up to 3 in AB though typically only 1 available] using ICD-9-CM [AB, MB] or a modified version of ICD-9 [ON], with shadow-billing for physicians on alternative payment plans); Outpatient Prescription Drug Claims (drug type & dispense dates); and Vital Statistics (dates of death). Supplemental Table 2 provides province-specific details; MB data were restricted to the Winnipeg Regional Health Authority that included ∼57% of the total MB population in 2023.

In ON, these databases were linked using unique encoded identifiers and analyzed at ICES. 32 In AB, databases were linked using health care numbers and analyzed within Alberta Health Services (AHS) with support by the Alberta Strategy for Patient-Oriented Research Support Unit (AbSPORU). 33 In MB, databases were accessed through the Manitoba Population Research Data Repository at the Manitoba Centre for Health Policy (MCHP) and were linked using an encrypted unique provincial identifier and analyzed at the MCHP. 34

This study received ethics clearance from the University of Waterloo Human Research Ethics Committee (#46451), University of Alberta Health Research Ethics Board (pSite00000181), University of Calgary Conjoint Health Research Ethics Board (#REB24-0793), and University of Manitoba Health Research Ethics Board (#HS26489). Approval for data access was obtained from ICES, AHS, and the Manitoba Health Information Privacy Committee. At ICES, the use of Ontario data for this study was authorized under section 45 of Ontario's Personal Health Information Protection Act.

Study populations

In each province, we identified all HC clients and LTC residents aged ≥18 years with a RAI assessment completed between April 1, 2012 (2013 for AB) and March 31, 2023, and selected the first assessment in this period as the index assessment (assessment reference date = index date). In HC, the RAI-HC (or newer interRAI-HC) is completed at admission and every 6–12 months thereafter; in LTC, full assessments with the RAI-MDS 2.0 are completed at admission, annually and following a significant health status change. We excluded individuals with invalid sex, registration number, or death date preceding index date, aged >110 years (given delays in death date validation), ineligible for provincial health insurance at index date or for ≥180 days (continuous) within 5 years before index date, and those with an index date in fiscal 2012 (2013 for AB) who had a RAI assessment in the prior year (to focus on relatively recent admissions to care).

The distribution of HC client index dates was used to generate the pool of possible community comparators and to assign pseudo-index dates. Each HC client was then matched 1:1 to a comparator (individual residing in the community not receiving HC/LTC in 3.5 years before their matched HC client's index date) by age ± 3 years, sex, and first two digits of postal code at index date, index date (± 3 years), dementia diagnosis (yes/no), and comorbidity level (low = 0,1, moderate = 2, high = ≥3 conditions) based on the sum of seven chronic conditions (hypertension, ischemic heart disease, diabetes, multiple sclerosis, epilepsy, parkinsonism, stroke) included in the Canadian Chronic Disease Surveillance System (CCDSS). 35 If multiple eligible comparators were identified, the final selection was random. Dementia and the other chronic conditions were identified using validated CCDSS case definitions and assessed during the 5 years before subjects’ index date (Supplemental Table 3).36,37

Using the existing HC study group as the pool of possible comparators, a similar though modified process was used to match each LTC resident 1:1 (by the same set of measures as above) to an individual receiving home care in the 3.5 years before their matched LTC resident's index date, selecting the home care comparator closest to this index date if more than one match was identified.

For both study populations (HC, LTC) the matched comparators allowed for consideration of relevant secular trends and confounding variables in interpretating mental illness prevalence.

Mental illness measures

First, we created an index period defined as 6-months pre- and post-index date and second, applied validated case definitions for mental illnesses with administrative data (Supplemental Table 4). These included four health service use measures currently assessed by the CCDSS (health service use for any mental illness, mood/anxiety disorders and schizophrenia; and schizophrenia diagnosis)35,36 and six other mental illness measures (depression - specific definition, depression with relevant drug claims used for diagnosis, anxiety, anxiety with relevant drug claims used for diagnosis, bipolar disorders, suicide attempt) with validated case definitions derived for populations with comorbidities.38–40 For selected analyses among HC clients, a second bipolar disorder case algorithm that required relevant dispensed medications along with physician claims to meet the definition 38 was explored given preliminary findings of a higher prevalence for the original case algorithm in Alberta than expected (potentially related to the common use of ICD-9-CM code ‘296’ [any episodic mood disorder) in physician claims for bipolar disorders in AB).

Other characteristics

We examined subjects’ age, sex (female/male, assigned at birth), urban/rural residence at index date, and four area-based deprivation measures from the Canadian Index of Multiple Deprivation (CIMD) 41 : residential instability, ethno-cultural composition, economic dependency, and situational vulnerability. Each measure was dichotomized to define those as high (most deprived; quintiles 4,5) versus low (less deprived, quintiles 1–3 & missing). The CIMD dimensions aim to illustrate potential inequalities in measures of socioeconomic characteristics for geographical areas across Canada. 41 The number of non-mental health physician visits in the year before index date was included to reflect healthcare access and utilization.

Analyses

Within each province, the characteristics of HC clients and LTC residents and their matched comparators were summarized using means (standard deviations [SD]), medians (interquartile range [IQR]) and frequency (percentage) distributions. Standardized differences of >0.10 defined meaningful differences for group comparisons (i.e., HC versus matched comparator or LTC versus matched comparator) 42 among those with and without dementia.

We estimated the crude prevalence of mental illness by fiscal year for HC clients and LTC residents relative to their matched comparators, for those with and without dementia. For fiscal 2022, we compared the prevalence of each mental illness for HC clients and LTC residents (versus their matched comparators) by dementia strata for each province and derived prevalence ratios with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) (for HC versus comparator; LTC versus comparator) using modified Poisson regression models with a robust variance estimator, 43 accounting for clustering by matched pair (with unstructured correlation matrix to allow flexibility in modeling). These prevalence ratios reflect adjustment for variables used in matching (i.e., age, sex, region of residence, dementia and comorbidity level).

All analyses were conducted with SAS Enterprise Guide 8.3 (ON) and SAS version 9.4 (AB, MB), with p < 0.05 defined as the level of statistical significance.

Results

The flow diagrams depicting the creation of matched study groups for each province are provided in Supplemental Figures 1–3. For ease of interpretation, the following results primarily focus on HC and LTC recipients with dementia with reference to material in the Supplemental Material for persons without dementia in these care settings.

Baseline characteristics

Included were 682,466 adults receiving HC (ON = 551,926, AB = 99,120, MB = 31,420) and 233,499 in LTC (ON = 181,883, AB = 36,211, MB = 15,405). There were 16,102, 2353, 2711 HC clients and 70,768, 14,770, 6454 LTC residents with dementia in ON, AB, and MB, respectively. For HC clients with dementia, the mean (SD) age was 75.7 (11.2) years in ON, 71.8 (12.6) years in AB and 78.5 (10.4) years in MB. The percentage female was 52.3% (ON), 48.5% (AB), and 59.3% (MB) (Table 1). For LTC residents with dementia, the mean (SD) age was 82.8 (8.0) years in ON, 83.1 (8.6) years in AB and 81.6 (7.7) years in MB. The percentage female was 58.1% (ON), 57.2% (AB), and 61.2% (MB). For both HC and LTC, those with dementia primarily resided in urban settings (highest for MB), with some provincial variation evident in the CIMD dimensions (e.g., higher proportion in MB residing in areas with high residential instability and situational vulnerability and lower proportion in AB residing in areas with high ethnocultural composition [HC only] and economic dependency).

Annual prevalence of mental illnesses in HC

The most prevalent mental illnesses among HC clients with dementia (shown below for earliest – latest fiscal years reported) were: any mental illness (ON: 44.0%-43.9%, AB: 59.2%-70.0%, MB: 50.4%-67.7%), mood/anxiety disorders (ON: 35.2%-31.3%, AB: 39.7%-42.6%, MB: 25.6%-35.3%), depression-specific (ON: 34.2%-29.4%, AB: 47.6%-51.1%, MB: 31.7%-37.0%) and anxiety (ON: 32.8%-27.5%, AB:26.6%-37.2%, MB: 17.7%-26.5%) (Figure 1.A1-C1). In all provinces, case algorithms that included drug claims resulted in higher prevalence estimates of depression (ON: 44.0%-39.8%, AB: 55.5%-60.1%, MB: 39.2%-44.5%) and anxiety (ON: 44.3%-38.1%, AB: 37.8–45.7%, MB: 26.6%-40.3%). Prevalence estimates were relatively higher in AB and sometimes MB than ON (e.g., any mental illness, depression) and rose slightly for several conditions around 2020/2021, though not always consistently across provinces.

Annual prevalence of mental illness for HC clients and matched comparators with dementia, Ontario (A1, A2), Alberta (B1, B2) & Manitoba (C1, C2), f2012–2022.

For the less common conditions, prevalence estimates among HC clients with dementia were bipolar disorders (ON: 5.3%-2.9%, AB: 15.3%-13.5%, MB: 7.5%-7.1%), schizophrenia (ON: 4.5%-2.4%, AB: 7.9%-3.1%, MB: 2.6%-2.9%), healthcare use for schizophrenia (ON: 4.1%-2.4%, AB: 7.6%-5.8%, MB: 1.9%-2.9%) and suicide attempt (ON: 0.85%-0.56%, unable to estimate accurately for AB & MB). The notably higher estimates for bipolar disorders in AB (and to a lesser extent MB) were more comparable with ON when derived with the revised case algorithm including drug claims (AB: 5.8%-3.2%, MB: 3.3%-3.8%).

Throughout the study period, prevalence estimates for several mental illnesses were generally higher in HC clients with dementia than their matched community comparators (Figure 1.A2-C2).

Among HC clients without dementia, mental illness prevalence estimates were generally lower relative to clients with dementia (Supplemental Figures 4, 6, 8), though consistently higher than their matched community comparators.

Annual prevalence of mental illness in LTC

Prevalence estimates for the commoner mental illnesses in LTC residents with dementia were any mental illness (ON: 31.0%-44.7%, AB: 56.2%-73.0%, MB: 67.9%-80.1%), mood/anxiety disorders (ON; 23.2%-29.2%, AB: 33.3%-37.5%, MB: 32.3%-40.1%), depression-specific (ON: 28.7%-30.1%, AB: 44.3%-46.6%, MB: 39.4%-45.7%) and anxiety (ON: 25.6%-27.3%, AB: 22.0%-27.1%, MB: 16.8%-32.2%) (Figure 2A1-C1). Prevalence estimates of depression (ON: 42.2%-44.9%, AB: 54.2%-58.4%, MB: 52.4%-53.0%) and anxiety (ON: 33.9%-35.7%, AB: 31.1%-38.2%, MB: 30.4%-41.1%) were higher when derived from case algorithms that included drug claims. For several common conditions, prevalence was relatively higher for AB and MB than ON and rose slightly around 2020/2021.

Annual prevalence of mental illness for LTC residents and matched comparators with dementia, Ontario (A1, A2), Alberta (B1, B2) & Manitoba (C1, C2), f2012–2022.

For the less common conditions among LTC residents with dementia, prevalence estimates were bipolar disorders (ON: 3.9%-3.3%, AB: 8.9%-14.2%, MB: 4.0%-9.3%), schizophrenia (ON: 3.1%-3.2%, AB: 3.5%-4.3%, MB: 2.3%-1.7%), healthcare use for schizophrenia (ON:2.4%-3.0%, AB: 3.2%-4.6%, MB: 1.8%-2.1%), and suicide attempt (ON: 0.19%-0.4%, AB: 0.29%-0.46%, unable to estimate accurately for MB). The higher estimates for bipolar disorders in AB were more comparable with ON when derived with the revised case algorithm including drug claims (AB: 2.2%-4.0%).

Prevalence estimates for selected common conditions were generally higher for LTC residents with dementia than their matched comparators (Figure 2A2-C2), though some differences were relatively modest (e.g., mood/anxiety disorders).

Among LTC residents without dementia, prevalence estimates were generally lower than observed for LTC residents with dementia, though some differences were small depending on province (Supplemental Figures 5, 7, 9). The prevalence of mental illnesses was generally higher among LTC residents without dementia versus their matched comparators, with some variation across provinces.

Prevalence and prevalence ratios for April 1, 2022-March 31, 2023

Prevalence estimates for mental illnesses in the most recent fiscal year were generally higher for those in HC or LTC than their matched comparators and higher among those with dementia (Figure 3) than without (Supplemental Figure 10). Prevalence ratios for HC clients (versus matched comparators) with dementia ranged between 1.3 to 2.6 (Figure 4A.1) and for LTC residents (versus matched comparators) with dementia between 1.1 to 2.7 (Figure 5A.1). Exceptions were noted for select conditions in MB (e.g., no statistically significant differences for HC versus matched comparators with dementia for depression, anxiety and bipolar disorders) and for some conditions in AB (e.g., no statistically significant differences for LTC versus matched comparators with dementia for mood/anxiety disorders and anxiety).

Discussion

This large descriptive epidemiologic study in three Canadian provinces provides novel contemporary data illustrating the considerable burden of mental illness among HC and LTC recipients. Prevalence estimates for most conditions were generally higher in the care setting relative to the matched comparator groups and higher among individuals with (versus without) dementia. Among persons with dementia, up to 7 in 10 HC clients and 8 in 10 LTC residents had any mental illness, including 4 in 10 with mood/anxiety disorders in both settings. Our findings indicate that about 4% of individuals with dementia in both settings have bipolar disorders and between 2%-6% have schizophrenia, while suicide attempts were rare (0.5%-0.6%). Including persons with and without dementia, our prevalence estimates suggest there are 400,000 HC clients and 160,000 LTC residents affected by a mental illness in these provinces that collectively represent ∼55% of the Canadian adult population. Important interprovincial variation was evident with relatively higher estimates for several mental illnesses in care settings in Alberta and Manitoba relative to Ontario.

Our findings are consistent with previous studies demonstrating high psychiatric comorbidity in persons with dementia receiving HC or residential LTC services.7,16,17,22,23 In a large retrospective cohort study of U.S. nursing home residents with dementia, 68.5% had any psychiatric disorder, 53.4% depression, 33.6% anxiety, 6.6% schizophrenia and 4.6% bipolar disorders. 7 In a small sample receiving home health care, the prevalence of depression was 51%. 16 These U.S. estimates are comparable with the upper range observed for persons with dementia in our Canadian care settings. Concerns have been raised about recent increases in the prevalence of serious mental illness in U.S. nursing homes (including schizophrenia, with estimates as high as 12%).44,45 It is unclear if this is also occurring in Canadian LTC settings, but our findings of the absolute numbers of persons in HC and LTC living with mental illnesses represents an important first step toward understanding the resources, services, and appropriate treatments required to ensure quality of care and life for those affected across care settings.45,46

Recent Canadian studies examining the full range of mental illness in these care settings are scarce.17,27 Publicly available estimates from the Canadian Institute for Health Information (CIHI) for 2022–2023, derived from the RAI clinical assessment rather than administrative data, indicate that about 27% of HC clients have any psychiatric diagnosis (individual diagnoses are not available). 47 For LTC residents, the prevalence of individual diagnoses (for ON, AB, MB) were reported as: depression (32.1%, 36.4%, 29%), anxiety (17.4%, 17.4%, 20.9%), bipolar disorder (2.8%, 2.5%, 1.7%) and schizophrenia (4.0%, 3.2%, 3.6%). 48 Except for schizophrenia, these estimates are lower than we observed for persons with dementia in the three study provinces, and somewhat lower or similar for selected diagnoses (e.g., depression) among our sample of LTC residents without dementia. Combining both RAI and health administrative data, Poss et al., reported a prevalence of 53% for any psychiatric diagnosis in HC clients from Manitoba and Nova Scotia. 27

The higher prevalence of some mental illnesses observed for care recipients in AB and MB (relative to ON), is consistent with general population studies illustrating higher estimates of mental health conditions in the Western provinces compared to ON. 49 The reasons underlying these findings require further investigation and may include provincial differences in the actual prevalence of mental illness (influenced by regional variation in the social determinants of health, population demographics, health characteristics, climate conditions), healthcare seeking behaviors, and in the availability, accessibility and cost of care services. Mental health coverage under provincial public plans is generally limited to services provided by physicians (including psychiatrists) or in the hospital and important gaps in access to care for those in need exist across provinces,50,51 including for those receiving HC 27 or LTC. 50 Provincial differences in coding practices should also be considered as evident for the original bipolar disorder case algorithm in our study.

The prevalence of depression and anxiety among persons with (and without) dementia in HC and LTC was considerably higher for the case algorithms that included drug claims. For HC clients with dementia, the case algorithms with drug claims resulted in a ∼10% absolute increase in the prevalence of depression (versus depression-specific) and 11–14% absolute increase in the prevalence of anxiety. For LTC residents with dementia, there was an absolute increase of 12–15% for depression and 8–14% for anxiety. How these findings should be interpreted is unclear. Some differences would be expected (e.g., lower prevalence of depression when using a specific definition); however, the potential for both mental illness under-treatment50–52 and off-label use of antidepressants/antianxiety agents (e.g., for agitated behaviors or insomnia in persons with dementia even in the absence of mental illness symptoms)7,8,53 in these care settings warrants careful consideration and further investigation.

Implications and future directions

The high prevalence and diversity of mental illnesses evident in our HC and LTC recipients raises serious concerns and questions about how well prepared our healthcare systems are for meeting current and future mental health care needs in these populations. Responding to the already increasing clinical complexity evident in these settings, 50 along with the unique challenges imposed by co-occurring mental illness, will be daunting. The shortage in geriatric and mental health providers,54,55 relative absence of appropriate educational and support programs, and inadequate public funding of well-integrated mental, health and social care services for older adults, even in high-income countries like Canada, are important barriers to the timely identification, diagnosis, treatment and management of mental illnesses.50–52 This imbalance between the specialty care needed by HC and LTC clients at risk for mental illness, many of whom face prejudice or discrimination related to age, disability and/or mental illness, 52 and the care they are likely receiving translates to poorer health and quality of life for these care recipients.8,56 Our findings also speak to the need for ongoing surveillance and additional validation of measures of mental illness as well as mental well-being in HC and LTC to facilitate resource planning and allocation.

Study strengths and limitations

This study provides current prevalence estimates for a diverse range of mental illnesses among at-risk and understudied care populations across three Canadian provinces that vary in sociodemographic and ethnocultural composition. The study size and use of comparable linked health administrative databases, validated case definitions, harmonized measures, and matching to relevant comparator populations are also key study strengths. A key limitation is the potential for under-estimates of mental illness as administrative data only capture persons who access care and are appropriately recognized and diagnosed. Factors affecting healthcare use/transfers and the recognition/diagnosis of mental illness are likely to differ across HC and LTC settings and for individuals with and without dementia. 8 Provincial comparisons should be interpreted cautiously as misclassification of some mental illnesses may arise because of differences in healthcare availability, accessibility and use, coding practices, and accuracy of case algorithms in these care settings. Caution is also needed in interpreting the slight rise in prevalence around 2020/2021 as this may reflect the adverse mental health consequences of the COVID-19 pandemic and/or changes in the clinical characteristics or healthcare use of persons who remained in HC or LTC during this period. 57 We were unable to generate stable estimates for suicide attempts in all provinces and we acknowledge that this measure is not an illness but rather a potential outcome of mental illness. With available data, it is not possible to identify the key factors (including health conditions) that contributed to the need for HC or LTC services; these factors may be important to understanding mental illness prevalence. We could not examine the impact of HC or LTC length of stay on mental illness, though by design none were receiving regularly scheduled HC or LTC for at least a year before their index date. Finally, we did not examine sex differences, other mental illnesses important to these populations (including post-traumatic stress disorder and substance use disorders),50,52,58 or measures of mental well-being and resilience.

Conclusions

This large multi-jurisdictional Canadian study showed a high prevalence of various mental illnesses among adults receiving HC and LTC, particularly evident for persons with dementia. It further highlights the potential for high unmet mental health needs in these clinically complex and at-risk populations. While important steps have been made to address these needs with the recent publication of national standards for mental health and well-being in continuing care,59,60 further efforts will be needed to ensure these standards are broadly implemented to improve the availability, accessibility, integration and appropriateness of mental health care and supports for all adults (and their family/friend caregivers) receiving care in HC or LTC settings.
