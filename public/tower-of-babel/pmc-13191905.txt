Moral injury and influencing factors among palliative care nurses : a cross-sectional study
Authors: Yanan Xu, Xiaomei Ji, Li Zhang, Yujie Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMoral injury has emerged as an important ethical and psychosocial concern in healthcare. Palliative care nurses frequently encounter ethically challenging situations and sustained emotional demands, yet evidence on the extent of moral injury and its associated factors remains limited.MethodsA cross-sectional, descriptive and correlational survey. Convenience sampling was used to recruit palliative care nurses working in palliative care services in Anhui and Jiangsu provinces, China, between December 2025 and February 2026. Participants completed a demographic/work-related questionnaire, the Moral Injury Symptom Scale–Health Professional (MISS-HP), and the Emotional Labor Scale. Data were analysed using t-tests/ANOVA, Pearson correlation, and stepwise multiple linear regression.ResultsOf 361 nurses approached, 349 provided valid responses (96.7%). The mean MISS-HP total score was 48.96 ± 17.35, suggesting that moral injury scores among palliative care nurses were relatively elevated. In the multivariable model (adjusted R²=0.386), higher moral injury was associated with being male, being unmarried, having a higher education level, experiencing major ethical dilemmas more frequently in the past year, lower perceived support from leaders/colleagues, and higher emotional labour (all p < 0.05).ConclusionThe findings suggest that moral injury symptoms among palliative care nurses were relatively elevated. Emotional labour was positively correlated with moral injury, indicating that nurses with higher emotional labour tended to report greater moral injury symptoms. In addition, demographic characteristics and work-related factors, including gender, marital status, educational attainment, frequency of major ethical dilemmas, and perceived organisational support, were significantly associated with moral injury. These findings highlight the importance of strengthening organisational support and developing strategies to manage emotional labour in order to mitigate moral injury and support nurses’ well-being.

Introduction

Moral injury refers to the psychological, spiritual, and social distress that can arise when individuals experience, witness, or are unable to prevent events that transgress deeply held moral values [1, 2]. Unlike transient emotional upset, it is often characterised by persistent guilt, shame, anger, loss of trust, and erosion of meaning, and may even extend to spiritual struggle and interpersonal withdrawal [3]. In recent years, moral injury has garnered increasing attention in healthcare due to the prevalence of value-laden clinical decisions, competing professional obligations, and institutional constraints in practice [4–6]. Palliative care is a specialized service for patients with life-limiting illness, aiming to maximise comfort, preserve dignity, and relieve suffering at the end of life [7], yet this context is marked by profound emotional strain and recurring moral dilemmas. While caring for dying patients, nurses witness intense physical suffering and psychological distress of family members, which heightens their emotional load and moral conflict [8]. In end-of-life decision-making [9], they must navigate complex trade-offs between respecting patient autonomy, responding to the needs of patients and families, and upholding their own professional and personal values [10]. For instance, decisions regarding opioid analgesia escalation, support for forgoing life-prolonging treatment, or responses to requests for potentially non-beneficial interventions may conflict with nurses’ moral beliefs and professional ideals, thereby increasing their risk of moral injury [11, 12].

Evidence indicates that palliative care nurses may experience guilt, helplessness, and anxiety when confronting patient death or perceiving an inability to provide optimal care [13]. Such reactions not only threaten their psychological well-being but also contribute to emotional withdrawal, compassion fatigue, and professional burnout [14]. Over time, sustained empathic engagement alongside limited control over care outcomes may foster a sense of ineffectiveness in relieving suffering, eroding nurses’ professional meaning and self-worth [15]. This cumulative emotional burden can lead to mental exhaustion, diminished work engagement, and compromised quality and consistency of care [16]; furthermore, prolonged emotional labour amid death, suffering, and perceived powerlessness may trigger lasting psychological distress, with downstream negative impacts on patients’ comfort and the overall effectiveness of end-of-life care [17]. Conversely, adequate organisational support and effective emotional coping strategies can strengthen nurses’ capacity for compassionate presence, ethical decision-making, and therapeutic communication [18]. Such positive emotional regulation enhances the quality of nurse-patient-family interactions, facilitates shared decision-making, and ultimately improves patient comfort and dignity, as well as family satisfaction during end-of-life care [19]. Therefore, understanding and addressing moral injury in palliative care nurses is essential not only for protecting nurses’ professional well-being but also for cultivating care environments that promote positive experiences for patients and their families.

Although moral conflict and occupational stress among healthcare workers have been widely examined [20], research specifically addressing moral injury among palliative care nurses remains scarce. Compared with nurses in other clinical settings, palliative care nurses operate in highly demanding and emotionally intensive environments, frequently encountering death, feelings of helplessness, and complex ethical dilemmas [21]. Consequently, examining moral injury within this population is of considerable theoretical and practical importance. Understanding the prevalence and associated factors of moral injury may provide an empirical basis for alleviating nurses’ psychological burden and guiding the development of targeted interventions and organisational support. Ultimately, such insights may contribute to improving the quality of palliative care services while promoting nurses’ job satisfaction and psychological well-being [22]. Therefore, this study aims to assess the level of moral injury among palliative care nurses and to identify its associated factors.

Methods

Design and participants

This descriptive, cross-sectional study was conducted between December 2025 and February 2026. Using convenience sampling, palliative care nurses working in palliative care services in Anhui and Jiangsu provinces were recruited.

Inclusion criteria were: (1) holding a valid nursing license; (2) currently working in hospital-based palliative care units providing inpatient palliative care, with at least 1 year of experience. Exclusion criteria were: (1) nurses rotating through clinical departments or undertaking further training; (2) nurses on sick leave or maternity leave.

Sample size was estimated as 5–10 participants per independent variable for regression analysis [23]. Considering 18 candidate predictors and a 20% invalid response rate, the estimated final sample size ranged from 113 to 223 palliative care nurses.

Measures

General information questionnaire

The general information questionnaire was developed by the researcher based on a review of the relevant literature [24] and the aims of the present study. It included demographic and work-related variables that have been reported or considered relevant to moral injury among nurses, including gender, age, marital status, education level, religious belief, years of palliative care work, professional title, type of hospital, number of palliative care training sessions per year, number of end-of-life patients encountered per week, experience of work-related moral dilemmas or ethical conflicts, experience of occupational burnout or emotional exhaustion in the past year, number of major ethical dilemma events experienced in the past year, level of support from supervisors and colleagues, and number of night shifts per week.

Moral injury symptom scale for healthcare professionals

The Moral Injury Symptom Scale for Healthcare Professionals (MISS-HP) was developed by Mantri et al. [25] based on the original military version of the scale and adapted to the healthcare context to assess moral injury symptoms among healthcare workers. The Cronbach’s α coefficient of the English version of the scale was 0.75. The scale contains 10 items covering 10 dimensions of moral injury symptoms: betrayal, guilt, shame, moral concern, loss of trust, loss of meaning, difficulty forgiving, self-condemnation, faith struggle, and loss of faith. Items 5, 6, 7, and 10 are positively worded and therefore require reverse scoring. Each item is rated on a 10-point scale ranging from 1 (“completely disagree”) to 10 (“completely agree”), with a total score ranging from 10 to 100. Higher total scores indicate more severe moral injury symptoms. A total score of ≥ 50 suggests noticeable moral injury symptoms, mainly reflected in increased feelings of guilt, shame, and sadness, which may affect well-being through a reduced sense of purpose and decreased inner peace [26]. Wang et al. [27]translated the scale into Chinese. The Chinese version showed good reliability and validity, with a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.714 [27]. In the present study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient of this scale was 0.838.

Emotional labor scale

The Emotional Labor Scale was developed by Grandey [28]and adapted and revised into Chinese for nursing contexts by Luo Hong et al. [29]. It includes three dimensions: surface acting, deep acting, and emotional expression, with a total of 14 items. Specifically, surface acting consists of 7 items and represents displaying emotions that are not genuinely felt; deep acting consists of 3 items and represents adjusting inner feelings to match required emotional expressions; and emotional expression consists of 4 items and represents the genuine expression of appropriate emotions at work. Each item is rated on a 5-point Likert scale, with responses ranging from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree” and scored from 1 to 5. The total score ranges from 14 to 70, with higher scores indicating higher levels of emotional labor and more frequent experiences reported by the respondents. The Cronbach’s α coefficients for the total scale and the three dimensions were 0.811, 0.711, 0.826, and 0.872, respectively. In this study, the overall Cronbach’s α coefficient of the scale was 0.892.

Data collection and quality control

Before data collection, the principal investigator contacted the nursing departments of participating hospitals to standardize instructions regarding study aims, eligibility criteria, and survey procedures. After institutional agreement, an electronic questionnaire link/QR code was distributed via nursing department channels to unit-based nurse WeChat groups. Participation was voluntary, and informed consent was obtained on the first page of the survey. The survey was anonymous, all items were mandatory, and each account/device/IP address was allowed to submit only once to reduce missing data and duplicate responses. Data collection ended when no new valid responses were received for one consecutive week. Two researchers reviewed and cross-checked the dataset, excluding questionnaires that exhibited obvious response patterns (e.g., identical or nearly identical responses across all items), logical inconsistencies, or abnormal completion. A total of 361 questionnaires were distributed, and 349 valid questionnaires were included (valid response rate: 96.7%).

Data analysis

Data were analysed using SPSS version 25.0. To assess the normality of the data distribution, both the Kolmogorov-Smirnov and Shapiro-Wilk tests were applied using multiple normality assessment methods. Results indicated that the main continuous variables (MISS-HP total score, emotional labor total score and its dimensions) were normally distributed. Categorical variables were summarised as frequencies and percentages, whereas continuous variables were described using means and standard deviations. Group differences for normally distributed continuous variables were tested using the independent-samples t-test or one-way analysis of variance, as appropriate. The relationship between moral injury and emotional labour was assessed using Pearson’s correlation analysis. Variables that reached statistical significance in the univariate analyses (P < 0.05) were subsequently entered into a stepwise multiple linear regression model to identify factors associated with moral injury. Statistical significance was defined as a two-tailed P value < 0.05 [30].

Ethical consideration

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Nanjing Drum Tower Hospital, affiliated with Nanjing University Medical School (Ethics Approval No.: 2024-853-02). The research was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, adhering to relevant guidelines and regulations. All participants consented to participate in the study through the online survey.

Discussion

The results of the study showed that the moral injury scores of palliative care nurses were relatively elevated.

The mean MISS-HP score observed in this study was similar to the findings reported in studies of oncology and emergency nurses [31, 32], indicating that moral injury is also an important issue among palliative care nurses. This may reflect the fact that moral injury is common among nurses working in emotionally demanding and ethically challenging clinical settings. In palliative care, nurses are continuously involved in bedside care, symptom management, and communication with patients and families, and they may experience moral injury when they recognise what would best serve patients’ interests but are constrained by institutional rules, limited resources, or treatment decisions made by others [33]. These experiences can affect nurses’ well-being and the quality of care provided: unaddressed moral distress may reduce emotional availability, limit therapeutic communication, and constrain compassionate support. Conversely, organisational support, ethical consultation, and team-based communication can help nurses maintain resilience and professional engagement. Strengthening these capacities can improve patient comfort, ensure consistent symptom management, and support dignified end-of-life care. Families benefit through clearer communication, better understanding of care decisions, enhanced emotional support, and reduced anxiety or guilt [34]. Integrating moral injury into risk management-including screening, early identification, ethics and bereavement support, supervision, and communication training, may thus promote compassionate, patient-centred care and a more supportive, informed experience for patients and their families.

The results of this study indicated that gender was associated with moral injury, with male nurses reporting higher levels of moral injury symptoms than female nurses. This finding is consistent with previous research suggesting that gender differences may influence psychological responses to workplace stress and ethical challenges [35]. Male nurses working in palliative care may face additional pressure related to professional identity, social expectations, and workplace integration. Traditional norms of masculinity may also encourage emotional suppression and avoidance coping strategies rather than seeking social or emotional support [36]. Prolonged emotional suppression may increase internal conflict and psychological strain, thereby heightening vulnerability to moral injury [37]. In contrast, women may be more attuned to the needs and emotions of others, making them more likely to experience moral resonance. When confronted with ethical dilemmas, women often emphasize interpersonal connection and relational engagement, which may enhance their capacity to navigate moral challenges in clinical practice and reduce the risk of moral injury [38]. Nursing managers should acknowledge these gender-related differences, implement tailored interventions for male nurses, and foster inclusive participation in ethical decision-making by addressing stereotypes, strengthening professional identity, and promoting supportive team practices.

This study found that unmarried nurses and those reporting lower levels of support from leaders and colleagues experienced higher levels of moral injury symptoms, which is consistent with the findings of Wang et al. [39]. Unmarried nurses may have relatively limited sources of emotional and social support, which may reduce their psychological resources when coping with ethical dilemmas and repeated exposure to patient death. As a result, they may be more vulnerable to persistent negative emotions and self-blame.In addition, end-of-life care is often accompanied by resource constraints and decision-making conflicts. When nurses lack emotional and instrumental support from colleagues, managers, and healthcare organisations, it may become more difficult to resolve moral dilemmas through team communication, ethical consultation, or workload adjustment, thereby increasing the risk of moral injury [40].Therefore, nursing managers should strengthen supportive cultures within palliative care teams and create open and psychologically safe environments that encourage ethical dialogue and emotional expression.

The results of this study showed that moral injury scores were highest among nurses with a master’s degree or above, which is consistent with the findings of Cao et al. [41]. A possible reason is that postgraduate-level nursing education places greater emphasizes evidence-based thinking, ethical reflection, and research capabilities, leading to higher expectations for professional values such as quality of care, preservation of dignity, and humanistic care [42]. However, once they enter frontline palliative care, they often have to work in situations with limited resources, procedural constraints, differing family demands, and uncertainty in end-of-life decisions [43]. In addition, postgraduate nursing education often places greater emphasis on the development of theoretical knowledge and research skills, while relatively less attention may be given to clinical practical training. As a result, when faced with highly complex communication, conflict resolution, and crisis management in palliative care settings, nurses with postgraduate education may be more likely to experience a mismatch between their preparation and clinical role demands [44]. Therefore, nurse managers could establish phased competency development pathways for highly educated nurses, using mentorship, scenario-based case reviews, and communication training to improve their practical competence in palliative care settings, enhance interdisciplinary coordination and conflict communication skills, and increase their sense of professional value through participation in quality improvement and care process optimization.

The frequency of major ethical dilemmas in the past year was positively associated with moral injury, suggesting a cumulative effect of repeated ethical challenges. In palliative care settings, nurses frequently encounter ethically sensitive situations such as balancing symptom control with sedation decisions, navigating disagreements among family members about treatment goals, and managing situations in which patients are unable to clearly express their preferences. According to the concept of “moral residue,” [45] unresolved ethical conflicts may leave lasting emotional traces that accumulate over time. When such experiences occur repeatedly without adequate support or reflection, they may gradually erode nurses’ psychological resilience and contribute to the development of moral injury. Hospitals should therefore consider establishing mechanisms for identifying and responding to high-impact ethical events, including structured debriefings, ethics consultation services, and opportunities for team reflection following ethically challenging cases.

Emotional labour was significantly associated with moral injury in this study. The mean total emotional labour score was 45.21 ± 8.84, and the item mean was 3.23 ± 0.63, indicating that palliative care nurses experienced a considerable level of emotional labour. Among the three dimensions, deep acting and the expression of naturally felt emotions scored higher than surface acting, suggesting that palliative care nurses tend to engage more in internal emotional regulation and genuine emotional expression than in superficial emotional display. This may reflect the nature of palliative care, in which nurses are expected not only to manage their own emotions but also to provide sustained emotional support to patients and families [46]. A similar finding was reported by Pehlivan et al. [47], who suggested that the efforts made by palliative care nurses to meet patients’ needs while overcoming their own negative emotions may be greater than the emotional burden associated with suppressing their true feelings. If nurses provide care grounded in genuine empathy and a sense of professional meaning, and if healthcare institutions offer organisational support such as training, ethical consultation, bereavement counselling, and protected time off, this may help sustain their emotional caregiving capacity and therapeutic engagement [48]. In turn, this may contribute to better symptom management and clearer communication with families. It may also help nurses provide targeted psychological reassurance and emotional support, and guide families to better understand and participate in end-of-life decision-making [49]. Addressing moral injury and emotional labour among nurses may therefore not only alleviate their psychological stress, but also improve patient comfort, family satisfaction, and the overall quality of end-of-life care.

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations. First, because of the cross-sectional design, the causal relationships among the variables could not be determined. Second, the data were collected using self-reported questionnaires, which may have introduced reporting bias. Third, the participants were recruited from a limited geographical area, and the sample may not fully represent palliative care nurses in other regions. Therefore, caution is needed when extending the findings to different cultural or healthcare settings. Further research should explore more organisational and psychological factors and develop targeted interventions to reduce moral injury among palliative care nurses.

Conclusion

In this study, moral injury scores among palliative care nurses were relatively high. Moral injury was significantly associated with demographic characteristics such as gender, marital status, and education level, as well as with emotional factors, particularly emotional labour, the frequency of major ethical dilemmas in the past year, and perceived support from leadership and colleagues. Specifically, higher emotional labour and more frequent major ethical dilemmas were associated with more severe moral injury, whereas greater perceived support from leaders and colleagues was associated with lower levels of moral injury. Nurses with continuous emotional regulation demands when caring for distressed patients and their families were more vulnerable to moral injury. This finding suggests that emotional labour may contribute to moral injury by consuming psychological resources and increasing sensitivity to ethically challenging situations. At the same time, insufficient support from leaders and colleagues may weaken nurses’ ability to cope with emotional strain and moral conflict, thereby further aggravating moral injury. Nursing leadership should recognise moral injury as an important ethical and psychological concern in palliative care, implement routine screening for both moral injury and emotional strain, and provide targeted interventions through peer support, ethical guidance, and psychological counselling to alleviate moral distress, strengthen emotional resilience, and reduce exhaustion.
