Does Distal Fusion Increase Pain—or Reflect Baseline Pain Risk in Adolescent Idiopathic Scoliosis With Spondylolysis?
Authors: Ercan Yılmaz
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

We read with great interest the study by Shobode et al evaluating lowest instrumented vertebra (LIV) selection in adolescents with idiopathic scoliosis and concomitant spondylolysis or low-grade spondylolisthesis. 1 The authors found greater follow-up low-back pain when fusion extended below L3 and when fewer than three mobile segments were preserved, concluding that stopping proximal to L3—and preferably at L2 when feasible—may minimize postoperative pain. 1 This conclusion has direct surgical implications, because preserving an additional lumbar motion segment must be balanced against deformity correction and distal stability. However, we believe that the principal pain finding cannot be attributed to LIV selection without accounting for preoperative pain. Follow-up VAS was the primary outcome, yet preoperative VAS values were neither reported by LIV group nor incorporated into the regression models. 1 This omission is particularly consequential because baseline pain is one of the strongest established predictors of postoperative pain after AIS fusion. In a multicenter registry of 1744 patients, Hwang et al identified preoperative pain as the most consistent predictor of postoperative pain. 2 Similarly, Charalampidis et al found that preoperative VAS pain and mental-health scores independently predicted persistent postoperative back pain. 3 Most importantly, Mimura et al evaluated preoperative pain, radiographic parameters, number of fused vertebrae, and LIV simultaneously; preoperative pain remained independently associated with 2-year postoperative pain, whereas LIV did not. 4

This distinction is especially important in the present retrospective cohort, in which LIV was not randomly assigned. Patients requiring fusion to L4 may have differed before surgery in distal curve morphology, symptomatic pars pathology, spondylolisthesis severity, or pain burden—all factors that could influence both LIV selection and subsequent pain. 1 With only 7 patients fused to L4 versus 22 ending at or above L3, even modest baseline imbalance could materially alter the reported 5.0 versus 3.0 VAS difference. The cohort also included substantial baseline psychosocial heterogeneity, including pre-existing mental-health disorders and opioid dependence, yet their distributions across LIV groups were not reported. 1 An analysis of follow-up pain adjusted for baseline pain and relevant preoperative clinical and radiographic characteristics is therefore essential before attributing the observed difference to fusion level. There is an additional inferential concern: the study directly compared L4 with a heterogeneous group ending at L3 or more proximally, but did not provide a direct L2-versus-L3 pain comparison; thus, the proposal that L2 may be the “more optimal” LIV extends beyond the observed group comparison. 1 Until baseline outcome imbalance and treatment-selection effects are addressed, the data support an association between distal LIV and follow-up pain, but not the conclusion that proximal fusion itself minimizes pain. This distinction could materially alter surgical recommendations in adolescents with concomitant pars defects.
