How floods impact health systems: a scoping review of Australian research
Authors: Nicole Dorfer, Jodie Bailie, Christine Ahern, Rebecca McNaught, Karen Scott, Veronica Matthews, Geoffrey Gerard Morgan, Kanchana Ekanayake, Ross Bailie
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesAlthough the health impacts of floods are well described, there is limited research on how flooding affects health systems, services and the health workforce—despite their central role in mitigating and responding to these impacts. This scoping review examines the nature and extent of existing research evidence on the impact of flooding events on Australia’s health system.DesignA scoping review following the Johanna Briggs Institute methodology.Data sourcesMEDLINE, Embase, CINAHL, Scopus, Web of Science, ProQuest Central and PsycINFO were searched through to 22 October 2024. Reference lists of included publications were screened for additional publications.EligibilityWe included studies that reported any health system or health service disruption associated with flooding in Australia. Disruptions encompassed impacts on hospitals, primary care, health information systems, infrastructure, public health and health promotion activities, and the health workforce. We included peer-reviewed publications, including original research, commentaries, perspectives, editorials, letters to the editor, modelling studies and reviews. Grey literature was excluded.Data extraction and synthesisScreening of full texts and data extraction were completed by two independent reviewers. A health system disruption analytical framework was iteratively developed and was used to categorise the findings.ResultsOur search identified 6687 publications, of which 28 were included in the final review. 13 publications were original research publications and 15 were commentaries or reviews, with the majority published in the past ten years. Of the publications included, most focused on disruptions to hospital services and transport systems, including a reduction in health workforce availability, primarily due to the latter. Less than one-third reported impacts on health services for socially vulnerable populations. Floods affect multiple levels of the health system, intersecting with impacts across three key domains: infrastructure and health information systems, access to healthcare and the health workforce.ConclusionsOriginal research on how floods impact Australia’s health system, its services and workforce has been limited, particularly in relation to general practice, allied health and the differential impacts on socially vulnerable populations. Further research is needed to inform targeted disaster preparedness and response strategies and to understand the complex and intersecting impacts. The analytical framework developed in this review provides a way to conceptualise how floods disrupt different components of the health system and offers a foundation for future research and policy development to strengthen system resilience in the face of increasing flood risk.Keywords: Health Services, Climate Change, Primary Health Care

Introduction

Floods are among the most frequent and destructive weather-related disasters world-wide,1 accounting for 43% of all weather-related disasters between 1994 and 2013.2 As the climate warms and the hydrological cycle intensifies, floods are projected to become both more frequent and more severe.3 Global assessments of flood mortality show that the communities with the highest number of flood days per year are located in the Mississippi basin in the USA, the Pacific coast of Latin America, Lake Victoria and the Volta basin in Africa, South-East Asia, coastal China and eastern Australia.4

In Australia, flooding has become the nation’s most costly weather-related disaster, averaging US$8.8 billion per year (as of 2017).5 Since 2010, the country has experienced multiple catastrophic flooding events,6 including the devastating 2022 and 2023 floods that inundated many communities along the east coast. The Australian National Climate Risk Assessment identifies flooding as a key and escalating climate hazard, with substantial implications for health—particularly for mental health and community well-being.7

The health impacts of flooding are well documented, encompassing immediate and long-term physical and mental health impacts.48,12 A scoping review by Bailie et al12 identified 69 Australian publications spanning a 70-year period from 1951 to 2023, with most studies examining post-traumatic stress disorder, psychological distress and child developmental outcomes.12 However, evidence on the broad range of impacts of floods on health systems remains limited.13

International and national frameworks—including the Sendai Framework for Disaster Risk Reduction 2015–2030,14 and the Australian National Climate Risk Assessment7—emphasise the importance of strengthening health system resilience in the face of disasters. Floods place acute and sustained pressure on health systems.15 16 Previous studies have explored disaster impacts more broadly on the health system from specific perspectives, such as individual professional groups (ie nurses, disaster managers),17 18 within specific localities,13 19 focusing on workforce planning,16 20 or broader disaster response contexts.1521,24 A global scoping review by Dewi et al24 synthesised 135 studies published up to 2023 on the impacts of extreme weather events on health outcomes and healthcare utilisation in rural and remote areas. Legg et al23 identified 22 publications from 2014 to 2023 examining how the Australian health system is adapting to the risks associated with extreme weather events more generally. Recent reviews22,25 mainly focus on the impacts of climate change-related events more broadly, with one examining how these events affect health outcomes and the utilisation of primary care in rural and remote Australia.24 International rapid reviews22 25 describe the health workforce impacts of climate-related disasters, including staff shortages and psychological effects.

However, to date, no comprehensive synthesis has specifically examined the impacts of flooding on health systems. This represents a critical evidence gap, as floods are among the most frequent and costly weather-related disasters worldwide. Resilient health systems are essential to mitigating and responding to such events, both in Australia and globally. This review therefore aimed to describe the nature and extent of the available evidence on the impacts of flooding events on Australia’s health system. In doing so, it introduces an analytical framework developed through this review, which may assist in conceptualising and understanding the scope of health-system impacts associated with floods.

Methods

A scoping review methodology was selected to describe the nature and extent of available evidence on health system disruption from flooding in Australia.26 27 Scoping reviews are well-suited to mapping key concepts, clarifying conceptual boundaries, identifying the types of evidence available and highlighting knowledge gaps.26 27 This study followed scoping review methodology based on JBI (formerly known as the Johanna Briggs Institute) guidelines28,30 and adhered to a published a priori protocol.31 Reporting followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews.32 As the aim is to provide a broad overview rather than evaluate intervention effectiveness, critical appraisal and risk of bias assessment were not conducted, in line with JBI scoping review methodology.26 30

Inclusion criteria

The scoping review parameters were defined using the ‘Population, Concept, Context’ framework outlined in the JBI guidelines.28 The population of interest included individuals in flood-affected areas whose health or access to healthcare may be compromised. All flood types were considered: fluvial, pluvial and coastal (storm surges associated with cyclones).33 Publications presenting aggregated results across multiple exposures (not specific to flooding) were excluded.

The concept of interest for the review was 'health systems,’ defined by the WHO as all organisations, people and actions aimed at promoting, restoring or maintaining health,34 consistent with more recently proposed health system frameworks.35 It covered disruptions to healthcare delivery due to floods, including impacts on hospitals, primary care, health information systems, infrastructure, public health, health promotion and the health workforce. A broad search was conducted using terms related to health impacts and healthcare personnel.

The context was Australia, including international publications with specific data on the impact of floods on Australian health systems. We included peer-reviewed publications reporting original research. To broaden the scope and capture additional conceptual or experiential insights, we also included peer-reviewed commentaries, perspectives, editorials, modelling studies and reviews (scoping, rapid and systematic). Grey literature, books, book chapters, conference abstracts, proceedings and theses were excluded.

Search strategy

We searched Medline, Embase, PsycINFO, CINAHL, Scopus, Web of Science and ProQuest Central for peer-reviewed publications from each database’s earliest date to 22 October 2024. The reference lists of included sources were also screened for additional studies. The full search strategy is in online supplemental Appendix 1.

Study selection

Citations were uploaded into Covidence36 and duplicates removed. After a pilot test, one reviewer (JB) screened titles and abstracts, with an independent review of 25% by another reviewer (ND, RM). For full-text screening, reviewers (ND, JB, RM) independently assessed all publications against the inclusion criteria. Disagreements were resolved through discussion or with an additional reviewer (RB).

Data extraction

A data extraction tool was developed in Covidence, covering variables like methodology, study design, study setting and health system disruptions. It was piloted on two papers by independent reviewers (JB, ND) and refined before applying to the rest. Two reviewers (ND, JB) independently extracted data, with disagreements resolved through discussion or an additional reviewer (RB). The remaining publications were extracted by one reviewer (ND) and checked by another (JB).

Data synthesis and presentation

After extraction, data were exported from Covidence to Excel and categorised as either ‘original research’ or ‘commentaries/reviews,’ the latter including expert commentaries, personal reflections, letters to the editor and reviews. We have presented the results of these separately. Original research provides empirical evidence on health system disruptions during floods, while commentaries and reviews provide a record of personal experiences, expert interpretation and broader contextual and conceptual insights. Presenting them separately in the results allows for clearer synthesis and avoids conflating empirical findings with opinion-based or secondary analyses.

As we progressed with data analysis and synthesis, it became clear that a structured approach would assist in organising the findings and in showing where within the health system impacts occurred, at what level, and the intersecting types of impact. We reviewed several existing frameworks designed to strengthen system resilience in disasters.37 38 None suited the specific aims of our scoping review. We therefore used an inductive approach to develop a tailored analytical framework (figure 1). This framework was developed during the synthesis phase, rather than in the process of searching or extracting data. We used the framework to organise, interpret and visually represent the intersecting relationships between types and levels of health system impacts identified in the publications, informed by our broader understanding of health systems literature. ND and JB iteratively categorised data into the framework, refining it with RB’s input and feedback from other authors. Throughout this process, we repeatedly returned to the full texts to confirm and adjust data extraction in light of the evolving framework.

Analytical framework showing the impacts of floods on health systems, including health services and workforce.

The analytical framework identifies two main categories of flood-related health system impacts: ‘clinical services’ and ‘public health, health promotion and prevention.’ Within ‘clinical services,’ there are three subcategories: hospitals (including emergency, dialysis and outpatient services), primary healthcare (general practice, allied health, pharmacies and community-based care) and ‘general/other’ (unspecified health services). Three cross-cutting impacts affect these levels: ‘infrastructure and health information systems’ (power, telecommunications, building, road and system damage); ‘people unable to access care’ (eg, cut-off by flooded roads or being too distressed/overwhelmed) and ‘workforce impacts’ (eg, decreased availability due to home damage, transport disruptions, increased workload and stress).

Patient and public involvement

No patients or members of the public were involved in the design, analysis or reporting of this study.

Clinical services

Hospital impacts were the most reported disruption to clinical services, although most were commentaries or reviews. Eight commentaries reported on dialysis service disruptions,5354 56 58 59 61,63 mainly linked to the 2011 QLD floods and Cyclone Yasi-related floods. Other hospital impacts included evacuations,52 55 60 63 relocation of inpatient and emergency services,63 increased emergency presentations due to flood-related injuries53 64 and cancellations of elective clinics and surgeries.62

Primary healthcare disruptions were the second most reported,4041 43,45 47 including medication access issues in both original research and commentaries or reviews.40 43 45 47 57 61 64 65 Original research attributed this disruption to accessing medications,47 pharmacy and hospital damage,40 43 47 including power outages,40 43 inaccessible electronic records,47 staff shortages due to road closures47 and supply chain disruptions.44 Commentaries and reviews identified flooded roads, lost belongings (including medications) and stock shortages as barriers to accessing care.57 61 64 65

Public health/health promotion and prevention

Three original research papers and four commentaries examined the impact of flooding on public health, health promotion and prevention. Research publications highlighted issues such as lack of running water, disrupted emergency services due to flooded roads, communication failures, power outages, medication shortages and workforce strain.44 45 One paper proposed a mathematical model to optimise hospital evacuations amid transport disruptions and staff shortages.51 Commentaries discussed the impacts on public health including infrastructure and transport damage, staff shortages, disrupted communications, the threat of infectious diseases from flood waters, lack of running water and functional sewage systems and limited access to health guidelines due to power outages.52 53 55 65

Infrastructure and health information systems

The most common disruption to infrastructure, for original research papers, was damage to transport systems,43,4751 with flooded and damaged roads impeding access to hospitals and pharmacies for staff, patients and medical supplies. This was followed by disruptions to telecommunications,44 46 47 including loss of phone coverage and internet, resulting in uncontactable clinicians and patients, disrupting continuity of care. Commentaries and reviews also reported on transport disruptions5355 58 59 61,65 and telecommunication damage.54 56 65 Health information system disruptions were noted in eight papers, with three original research papers43 47 49 and five commentaries54 56 60 63 64 reporting on damaged or inaccessible medical records.

People unable to access care

The most common disruption reported on for clients/patients was impediments to accessing medications, in both original research40 43 45 47 and commentaries,57 61 64 65 with key barriers detailed in section ‘clinical services’ . Four commentaries cited flooded roads as a barrier to dialysis services.56 58 61 62 Disruptions to general health services were noted in three research papers39 48 50 and one review,13 attributed to disrupted support networks for people with disability and their carers,39 communication system failures preventing contact with health services,44 power outages limiting access to healthcare and flooded roads necessitating aerial food and medical supply drops.55

Health workforce impacts

Flooding impacts on the health workforce were identified in seven original research papers and eleven commentaries or reviews. The most common impact in original research publications was reduced health workforce availability,41,4446 47 51 attributed to transport disruptions such as damaged roads, preventing staff from accessing workplaces43 46 47 and exacerbating existing staff shortages in rural areas.47 Some healthcare staff and medical students had to evacuate their homes,41 42 46 preventing them from working. Increased workloads for those who could work41 43 led to mental health impacts,41 46 including fatigue, exhaustion and trauma.

Commentaries and reviews identified decreased staff availability as the most common workforce impact, mainly due to transport disruptions,53,5562 concerns for family and property53 54 62 and direct flood water impacts.13 53 58 They also reported increased workloads during and after flooding,59 60 62 64 due to staffing shortages and higher hospital demand. One publication raised concerns regarding the mental health of staff facing these extra demands on the health workforce.65

Impacts of flooding on health services for socially vulnerable populations

Despite socioeconomically disadvantaged people experiencing poorer access to health services during and after flooding,13 less than one-third of publications referred to impacts of flooding on socially vulnerable populations (three original research papers and six commentaries/reviews). One original research paper highlighted that people with disabilities and their carers were more likely to report disrupted access to health and social care compared with others.39 Another focused on transgender people disrupted access to regular hormone replacement therapy due to limited access to hospitals and/or pharmacies40 and power outages affecting the storage of hormones that require specific temperatures.40 Children and adolescents, identified as vulnerable after the 2011 QLD floods, required additional mental health support from existing services that were strained due to staff being personally impacted and fatigued.41 Three commentaries identified Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people as a significant proportion of dialysis patients affected by Cyclone Yasi.54 56 61 Two commentaries discussed extreme flooding exacerbating already limited health service availability for people living in rural and remote areas.57 65

Preparation or response strategies and recommendations

Most publications discuss or identify preparation or response strategies to minimise the impact of flooding events on the health system, including ten original research publications and eleven commentaries or reviews. Five highlighted strategies that were successful, including two original research papers41 42 and three commentaries.56 58 62 From original research papers, these success strategies included a well-established hospital disaster plan with designated evacuation coordinators, communication channels, patient documentation for transfers, battery-powered equipment and sufficient41 or even additional staffing levels.42 Commentaries discussed the success of established dialysis disaster plans,56 58 an adequately staffed field hospital at evacuation centres56 and strong communication between clinical teams.62

Several recommendations were also made, aimed at minimising flooding impacts on the health system (table 3). For original research, the two most common recommendations were: (1) increased eHealth implementation (electronic prescriptions, telehealth, online health records) to ensure continuous care43 44 47 and (2) improved communication, including public health messaging and clinical team coordination.41 44 45 For commentaries or reviews, the most common recommendation was to address the needs of socially vulnerable populations in planning and policy,13 54 57 64 65 followed by promoting interdisciplinary collaboration in research and response.13 61 64 65

Discussion

To our knowledge, this scoping review is the first to map the published literature on health system disruptions caused by flooding events in Australia. Floods are reported to affect multiple levels of the health system. Hospital services were most frequently reported to be impacted, including evacuations, temporary closures and disruptions to dialysis, emergency, outpatient and elective services. Primary healthcare—particularly pharmacies and medication supply—was commonly reported as disrupted. These impacts stemmed from damage to buildings, transport, communication and health information infrastructure, which compounded workforce challenges such as staff shortages, increased workloads and psychological strain, collectively making it more difficult for people to access care.

Our review illustrates how system disruptions can intersect with changes in health care-seeking behaviour. People may defer or avoid seeking care, either prioritising immediate safety and recovery or being unable to reach services due to damaged or blocked roads. Breakdowns in infrastructure (including transport and supply chains), and increased strain on health workers, together with hazardous conditions that may restrict access to health facilities for patients and staff, may lead to reduced presentations even as healthcare need increases. These combined disruptions to system functioning and care-seeking contribute to the complex patterns of healthcare use observed during floods. For example, Hart et al66 found no measurable increase in all-cause hospitalisations associated with flood exposure in rural NSW, whereas O’Driscoll et al67 reported increased psychiatric admissions but shorter lengths of stay following the 2022 Lismore floods. The complexity of impacts highlights the need for further research to better understand service use and underlying factors and to strengthen health system responses that extend beyond the initial emergency phase.

A key finding was the limited amount of original, peer-reviewed research. Of the 28 publications identified, less than half (13) were original studies; the rest were commentaries or reviews. We found that most original research has been published in the past decade, with nearly one-quarter of publications focused on the Northern Rivers region of NSW—an area that has experienced repeated and severe flood events.

Another important finding was the limited research examining the impact of flooding on allied health or general practice, despite their central role in Australia’s health system.68 Following the 2022 Northern Rivers floods, the Royal Australian College of General Practitioners reported that 58 out of 70 primary care providers (nearly 80%) experienced some degree of flood damage.69 These findings raise concerns that primary care services may be ill-prepared for disaster response and recovery, despite being the first point of contact for communities and a critical link in maintaining continuity of care during disasters.24 Broadly, there is a clear need to investigate which types of health services are most vulnerable to disruption and where these disruptions occur. Mapping the specific services affected—such as primary care, hospital-based care, mental health support or community outreach—and identifying the regions most at risk would enable more targeted and effective mitigation and adaptation strategies.7

While it is well established that floods expose and exacerbate existing social inequalities,39 70 our review identified a clear gap in Australian research, with fewer than one-third of publications in this review examining how health system disruptions during floods affected socially vulnerable populations. Previous reviews of the health impacts of flooding in Australia12 and Northern NSW13 similarly noted limited attention to the disproportionate effects on socially vulnerable groups. In our review, a quarter of publications specifically called for greater focus on the needs and experiences of socially vulnerable populations. Given the documented disproportionate impacts of floods on socially vulnerable populations, it is critical that Australian flood research investigates these differential impacts and how to strengthen health systems as a result. Doing so will better inform policymakers and practitioners in developing targeted strategies to reduce impacts on priority populations.

Most publications discussed preparation and response strategies aimed at minimising the impact of flooding on the health system; however, none systematically reported or evaluated interventions or adaptations designed to strengthen system preparedness. Further research is needed to develop and evaluate adaptation strategies across multiple levels of the health system—local, regional and national.7 With floods becoming more frequent and severe, there is now an urgent need to move beyond calls for action towards systematically developing and evaluating adaptation measures that enhance the resilience of health systems.7 71

The analytical framework developed for this review provided a useful structure for mapping the diverse and intersecting impacts of flooding on health systems and provides a base for further refinement. Developed inductively from our review and existing knowledge of health systems, it will benefit from validation through application in empirical studies and engagement with health system practitioners and policymakers. Future refinement could also involve testing its applicability across different disaster types, geographic contexts and health and social care system settings. Iterative development of the framework will be useful for guiding both future research and policy responses to health system disruptions caused by weather-related disasters.

Strengths and limitations

Strengths include an a priori protocol, no date limits to the search (which enabled a complete picture of published research), independent full-text review by two reviewers, and 86% of data extraction done independently, with a search strategy developed with an experienced academic librarian and field experts. By including both original research and commentaries, we were able to gain a complete picture of the scope of research reporting on health system disruption due to flooding in Australia, as published in peer-reviewed journals.

Review limitations include our focus on peer-reviewed publications, potentially missing relevant evidence from grey literature, books or conference proceedings. Due to resource constraints, only 20% of the titles and abstracts were screened by two independent reviewers. The limited amount of evidence points to a general gap in research on this topic, as well as specific areas requiring further investigation—consistent with the aims of a scoping review. We acknowledge that different types of flooding—pluvial, fluvial and coastal—may affect health systems in distinct ways. Although our search terms captured all flood types, the limited number of eligible studies precluded analysis by flood type. Additionally, while our definition of the healthcare system is broad along with broad search terms, we did not include specific search terms related to institutional settings such as resident and aged care facilities or social services. It is possible that our search may have missed some research on impacts in these types of specific settings. These limitations highlight important areas for future research.

This review is timely given the recent launch of Australia’s first National Health and Climate Strategy,72 73 and National Adaptation Plan,74 both aiming to build climate-resilient health systems. It provides a foundation for future work to map and compare health system impacts of floods across different contexts internationally. Conclusions

This scoping review synthesises the current literature on the impact of floods on Australia’s health system. The review is pertinent as Australia faces an increasing frequency and intensity of flooding events, especially given the minimal research to date on the impacts of these events. We identified a significant lack of research into the disruptions to general practice and allied health services, which is often people’s first point of contact to Australia’s healthcare system. Further research is required to develop focused preparation and response strategies for future healthcare system resilience in the face of disasters caused by a warming climate. The analytical framework for categorising health system disruption may be useful for guiding and contextualising further research in this area.
