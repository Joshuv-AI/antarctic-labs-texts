Strichartz estimates for higher order Schrödinger equations with Partial regular initial data
Vishvesh Kumar, Shyam Swarup Mondal, Iswarya Sitiraju, Manli Song

Abstract
. In this paper, we establish refined Strichartz estimates for higher-order Schrödinger equations with initial data exhibiting partial regularity. By partial regularity, we mean that the initial data are not required to have full Sobolev regularity but only regularity with respect to a subset of the spatial variables. As an application of these estimates, we investigate the well-posedness of nonlinear Schrödinger equations with power-type nonlinearities. In addition, we extend our analysis to the Dunkl–Schrödinger equations under partial regularity, defined with respect to two distinct root systems. This extension poses significant challenges, mainly due to the lack of a suitable stationary phase method in the Dunkl setting. To overcome this difficulty, we develop a new result that provides an adaptation of the stationary phase method to the framework of Dunkl analysis.

1.
Introduction and main results

This paper aims to investigate Strichartz estimates and their application for a class of dispersive equations associated with the Laplacian and the Dunkl Laplacian on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with power-type nonlinearities. Let us begin our discussion by considering a class of dispersive equations on
\mathbb{R}^{d}:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} i\partial_{t}u(t,x)+\Phi(D)u(t,x)&=F_{p}(u)(t,x),\quad(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d},\\
u(0,x)&=f(x),\end{aligned}\right.

(1.1)

where
\Phi(D)
denotes the Fourier (or Dunkl-Fourier) multiplier operator associated with the real-valued function (also known as multiplier)
\Phi
. The function
\Phi
is assumed to satisfy certain specific, yet sufficiently general conditions that encompass several important dispersive equations discussed below. Regarding the nonlinearity
F_{p}
, we assume that it belongs to the class
C^{1}
and satisfies the growth condition with exponent
p>1
:

\left|F_{p}(u)\right|\lesssim|u|^{p}\quad\text{ and }\quad|u|\left|F_{p}^{\prime}(u)\right|\sim\left|F_{p}(u)\right|.

(1.2)

Typical examples of such nonlinearities include the standard forms
\pm|u|^{p-1}u
and
\pm|u|^{p}
.

Our initial focus will be on equation (
1.1
) in the Euclidean setting, where
\Phi(D)
denotes a Fourier multiplier on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We will revisit and extend the discussion to the Dunkl framework in a subsequent section. Equation (
1.1
) serves as a unifying framework that includes several fundamental dispersive equations, depending on the specific choice of the Fourier multiplier
\Phi
. For instance, when
\Phi(\xi)=|\xi|^{2}
, the solution
u
to (
1.1
) corresponds to the classical Schrödinger evolution
e^{-it\Delta}f
. If
\Phi(\xi)=|\xi|
, the equation models the linear wave propagation through the operator
{}^{it\sqrt{-\Delta}}f
. More generally, for
\Phi(\xi)=|\xi|^{m}
with
m>1
, the solution describes the fractional Schrödinger flow given by
e^{it(-\Delta)^{m/2}}f
. These examples illustrate the flexibility of the formulation in capturing a broad class of dispersive dynamics through appropriate choices of
\Phi
.

It is well known that equation (
1.1
) with initial data
f\in\dot{H}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, when coupled with the nonlinearities
\pm|u|^{p-1}u
or
\pm|u|^{p}
, exhibits scaling invariance due to the homogeneity of the symbol
\Phi
. This invariance under suitable scaling transformations reflects the balance between the linear dispersive term and the nonlinear interaction, a feature that plays a crucial role in the study of criticality and well-posedness. Indeed, we first note that if
u(t,x)
is a solution of (
1.1
), then so is

u_{\delta}(t,x)=\delta^{\frac{m}{p-1}}u(\delta^{m}t,\delta x),\quad\delta>0,

with rescaled initial data
f_{\delta}(x)=u_{\delta}(0,x)
. Now, we observe that

\|f_{\delta}\|_{\dot{H}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}=\delta^{\frac{m}{p-1}+s-\frac{d}{2}}\|f\|_{\dot{H}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}.

(1.3)

The scaling relation (
1.3
) plays a key role in identifying the critical exponent
p
(or, equivalently, the scale-invariant Sobolev space
\dot{H}^{s_{c}}
), given by

p=p_{c}(d,s)=1+\frac{2m}{d-2s},\quad\text{alternately}\,\,s_{c}=\frac{d}{2}-\frac{m}{p-1}.

This critical exponent marks the threshold at which the interplay between the linear dispersion and the nonlinearity changes. It distinguishes between different regimes of solution behavior: when
p>p_{c}(d,s)
, the problem is said to be supercritical; when
p<p_{c}(d,s)
, it is subcritical. The critical exponent serves as a dividing line between the existence of global-in-time Sobolev solutions (in the subcritical and critical cases) and the potential for finite-time blow-up of weak solutions, even with small initial data, in the supercritical case.

The subcritical regime
p<p_{c}(d,s)=1+\frac{2m}{d-2s}
reveals that higher regularity of the initial data, corresponding to larger Sobolev indices
s
, permits a wider range of admissible nonlinear exponents
p
. This reflects the enhanced control over the nonlinearity afforded by smoother initial profiles.

Our primary prototype for the symbol
\Phi
is given by
\Phi(\xi)=|\xi|^{m}
with
m>1
. In this case, equation (
1.1
) reduces to the higher-order Schrödinger equation, specifically the Cauchy problem:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}i\partial_{t}u(t,x)+(-\Delta)^{m/2}u(t,x)=F_{p}(u)(t,x),\\
u(0,x)=f(x),\end{cases}

(1.4)

for
(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}.
Here
(-\Delta)^{m/2}
denotes the pseudo-differential operator given by

(-\Delta)^{m/2}f(x):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{ix\xi}|\xi|^{m}\widehat{f}(\xi)\,d\xi

with the Euclidean Fourier transform
\widehat{f}(\xi):=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-ix\xi}f(x)dx.

The higher-order Schrödinger equation with
m>2
has garnered significant interest in mathematical physics. In particular, when
m=4
, equation (
1.4
) arises in the study of the formation and propagation of intense laser beams in bulk media; see
[
25
,
26
]
. Fractional-order cases with

are also of considerable importance, especially in light of the development of fractional quantum mechanics by Laskin
[
30
]
, where the fractional Schrödinger equation was introduced as a generalization of the classical model. This formulation stems from an extension of the Feynman path integral from Brownian-like to Lévy-like quantum mechanical paths (see
[
30
,
31
]
). In this framework, it is conjectured that certain quantum phenomena may be accurately captured only within these fractional regimes. Additionally, the fractional Schrödinger equation appears in water wave models (see
[
22
]
), further highlighting its interdisciplinary relevance. When

, the nonlinear fractional Schrödinger equation generalizes both the standard nonlinear Schrödinger equation (
m=2
) and the fourth-order nonlinear Schrödinger equation (
m=4
); see, for example,
[
35
]
.

In the classical case
m=2
, equation (
1.4
) reduces to the standard nonlinear Schrödinger equation on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, a model that arises naturally in quantum mechanics. A central aspect in the analysis of such equations is understanding the space-time behavior of solutions to the associated free linear problem,

. These estimates form the backbone of well-posedness and scattering theory for nonlinear Schrödinger equations; see

]

For the higher-order Schrödinger equation (
1.4

[

established that for

\displaystyle\left\|e^{it(-\Delta)^{\frac{m}{2}}}f\right\|_{L_{t}^{q}\left(\mathbb{R};L_{x}^{r}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)\right)}\lesssim\|f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}.

(1.5)

Such estimates are indispensable in extending the classical theory of nonlinear Schrödinger equations to higher-order and more general settings. Strichartz estimates for the higher-order in different frameworks and their application in the nonlinear PDEs can be found in
[
29
,
26
,
25
,
7
]
. Based on the Strichartz estimate (
1.5
), the local well-posedness of (
1.4
) has been discussed in
[

,
20
,
10

with initial data from Sobolev spaces
H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
\dot{H}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).
Particularly in the subcritical case, that is
p<p_{c}(d,s)=1+\frac{2m}{d-2s}
, one could see that the more one assumes regularity s of initial data, the wider a possible range of
p
in the power type nonlinearity.

The main objective of this paper is to investigate the local well-posedness of the nonlinear Schrödinger equation (
1.4
) associated with higher-order operators on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, when the initial data does not possess full regularity in the classical Sobolev space
H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, but instead lies in a partially regular space of the form
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d-k};H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{k}))
for some
1\leq k\leq d
. Recent progress in this direction was made by Koh, Lee, and Seo
[
28
]
, who studied local well-posedness of nonlinear Schrödinger and wave equations under partial regularity assumptions. Certain nonlinear Schrd̈inger equations exhibit partial off-axis variations, which naturally motivates the consideration of initial data with only partial regularity in the analytical study of such models; see
[
1
,
2
]
.
Let us briefly explain the motivation behind this type of study.

We decompose the spatial variable
X\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
as
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{d-k}\times\mathbb{R}^{k}
, and assume that the initial data belongs to the mixed Sobolev space
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d-k};{\dot{H}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{k}))}
rather than the full space
\dot{H}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Under this assumption, one can still observe the same scaling behavior as in the fully regular case. More precisely, for a rescaled initial datum
f_{\delta}
, we have

\|f_{\delta}\|_{L_{x}^{2}\dot{H}_{y}^{s}}=\delta^{\frac{m}{p-1}+s-\frac{d}{2}}\|f\|_{L_{x}^{2}\dot{H}_{y}^{s}},

where the scaling exponent
\frac{m}{p-1}+s-\frac{d}{2}
is identical to that in the full regularity setting (
1.3
), independent of the choice of
k
.

This observation suggests that the critical exponent
p
for well-posedness remains unchanged even under partial regularity assumptions. Consequently, it motivates the development of a well-posedness theory in lower-regularity frameworks, thereby extending the classical theory to a broader class of initial data. It is worth noting that
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d-k};H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{k}))
is bigger than that of
H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
in the sense that
1\leq k_{1}<k_{2}<d
,

H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\subsetneq L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d-k_{2}};H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{k_{2}}))\subsetneq L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d-k_{1}};H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{k_{1}})).

We refer to
[
28
]
for more details on this topic.

As a natural first step toward developing a well-posedness theory for the nonlinear Schrödinger equation (

More generally, we derive refined Strichartz estimates for the generalized dispersive propagator

(1.6)

under the following assumptions
(A)
on

The following theorem about refined Strichartz estimates for the generalized dispersive propagator
e^{it\Phi(D)}
is one of the main results of this paper.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
d\geq 1
and
1\leq k\leq d
. Let
\Phi
be a real-valued smooth function satisfying Assumptions (A). If

\displaystyle\frac{2}{q}\leq(M-k)\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{r}\right)+k\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{\widetilde{r}}\right),\quad 2\leq\widetilde{r}\leq r<\infty,\quad 2<q\leq\infty.

Then, we have

\displaystyle\left\|e^{it\Phi(D)}f\right\|_{L_{t}^{q}\left(\mathbb{R};L_{x}^{r}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d-k};L_{y}^{\tilde{r}}\left(\mathbb{R}^{k}\right)\right)\right)}\lesssim\|f\|_{\dot{H}_{x,y}^{s}},

(1.7)

under the scaling condition

\displaystyle\frac{{{m}}}{q}=-s+(d-k)\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{r}\right)+k\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{\widetilde{r}}\right).

These refined Strichartz estimates enable us to leverage varying integrability and regularity properties across different spatial directions, thereby making it possible to formulate a well-posedness theory under the assumption of only partial regularity in the initial data.

We remark that Theorem

In the special case where

Now, we turn our attention toward the Strichartz estimates of the Dunkl-Schrödinger equations and well-posedness theory for nonlinear Dunkl-Schrödinger equations. Over the last four decades, an alternative to classical Fourier analysis, known as
(rational) Dunkl theory
, has been developed in Euclidean spaces. This framework builds upon the structure of a root system
\mathcal{R}
, its associated reflection group (Coxeter group)
G
, and a multiplicity function
\kappa:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
.
Let
h^{2}_{\kappa}
and
E_{\kappa}
denote the weight function and Dunkl kernel associated with the root system
\mathcal{R}
, respectively. Let
N:=d+2\gamma
denote the homogeneous dimension. We refer to Section
5
for detailed definitions and unexplained notations.

For
\xi\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
and for a positive multiplicity function
\kappa,
Dunkl
[
13
]
in 1989 introduced a family of first-order differential-difference operators
T_{\xi}:=T_{\xi}(\kappa)
by

\displaystyle T_{\xi}(\kappa)f(x):=\partial_{\xi}f(x)+\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{R}^{+}}\kappa_{\alpha}\langle\alpha,\xi\rangle\frac{f(x)-f\left(r_{\alpha}x\right)}{\langle\alpha,x\rangle},\quad f\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),

(1.8)

where
\partial_{\xi}
denotes the directional derivative corresponding to
\xi
. These differential-difference operators, now known as Dunkl operators, extend the classical notion of partial derivatives. Dunkl operators have found numerous applications across mathematics and mathematical physics. For instance, they play a significant role in the integrability of quantum many-body systems of the Calogero–Moser–Sutherland type and in the analysis of certain probabilistic processes
[
40
,
19
]
. Moreover, they have profoundly influenced the theory of special functions and orthogonal polynomials in both one and several variables; see
[
14
]
.

Let
\{\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\cdots,\xi_{n}\}
be an orthonormal basis of
(\mathbb{R}^{d},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)
. Then the Dunkl Laplacian operator
\Delta_{\kappa}
is defined as

\Delta_{\kappa}:=\sum_{j=1}^{n}T^{2}_{\xi_{j}}(\kappa).

Now, for
m>0,
we consider the nonlinear Dunkl-Schrödinger equations

\begin{cases}i\partial_{t}u+(-\Delta_{\kappa})^{m/2}u=F_{p}(u),\\
u(0,x)=f(x),\end{cases}

(1.9)

where the nonlinearity
F_{p}\in C^{1}
with
p>1
satisfies

|F_{p}(u)|\lesssim|u|^{p}\quad\text{and}\quad|u||F^{\prime}_{p}(u)|\sim|F_{p}(u)|.

(1.10)

Here
(-\Delta_{\kappa})^{m/2}
denotes the Dunkl-Fourier multiplier operator with symbol
|\xi|^{m}
given by

(-\Delta_{\kappa})^{m/2}f(x):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\xi|^{m}\mathcal{F}_{\kappa}{f}(\xi)E_{\kappa}(ix,\xi)h_{\kappa}(\xi)d\xi

with the Dunkl Fourier transform

\mathcal{F}_{\kappa}{f}(\xi):=\frac{1}{c_{\kappa}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x)E_{\kappa}(x,-i\xi)h_{\kappa}(x)dx.

Our aim is to investigate Strichartz estimates and local well-posedness for the Dunkl–Schrödinger equation (
5.16
) within the framework of partial regularity. Since Dunkl analysis is intrinsically connected to root systems, it is essential to first establish a suitable setting for formulating partial regularity on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
in this context.
Let
\mathcal{R}_{1}
and
\mathcal{R}_{2}
be root systems in
\mathbb{R}^{d-k}
and
\mathbb{R}^{k}
, respectively, with associated reflection groups
G_{1}
and

, where

.
Then,

\mathcal{R}:=\mathcal{R}_{1}\times(0)_{k}\cup(0)_{d-k}\times\mathcal{R}_{2},

where
(0)_{j}=(0,0,\ldots,0)\in\mathbb{R}^{j}
, is a root system on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with reflection group
G=G_{1}\times G_{2}
. Let
\kappa_{1}
and
\kappa_{2}
be multiplicity functions for
\mathcal{R}_{1}
and
\mathcal{R}_{2}
respectively. Then we define the multiplicity function
\kappa:\mathcal{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
the root system
\mathcal{R}
is given by

\begin{array}[]{ll}\kappa(\alpha,0)=\kappa_{1}(\alpha)&\text{ for all }\alpha\in\mathcal{R}_{1}\\
\kappa(0,\beta)=\kappa_{2}(\beta)&\text{ for all }\beta\in\mathcal{R}_{2}.\end{array}

In fact, for any
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, let

Note that, if

is a solution of (

In addition, the Sobolev norm of the rescaled initial data

This scaling suggests the critical exponent

for (

p=p_{c}(d,s)=1+\frac{2m}{d+2\gamma-2s},\quad\text{alternately}\,\,s_{c}=\frac{d+2\gamma}{2}-\frac{m}{p-1}.

Theorem 1.5

Let

2.
Preliminaries

This section is devoted to presenting basic facts, function spaces, and preliminary tools that will be used throughout the paper.

As discussed in the introduction, we denote
X=(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{d-k}\times\mathbb{R}^{k}
for
1\leq k\leq d
, with the understanding that when
k=d
, the
y
-variable does not appear. We make use of mixed Lebesgue spaces of the form
L^{p}_{x}(\mathbb{R}^{d-k},L^{q}_{y}(\mathbb{R}^{k}))
, which we also denote by
L^{p}_{x}L^{q}_{y}(\mathbb{R}^{d-k}\times\mathbb{R}^{k})
or simply
L^{p}_{x}L^{q}_{y}
. These spaces are equipped with the norm:

\|f\|_{L^{p}_{x}L^{q}_{y}}:=\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d-k}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}|f(x,y)|^{q}\,dy\right)^{\frac{p}{q}}dx\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}.

As usual, we define the Fourier transform of a function
f=f(x,y)
as

(\mathcal{F}f)(\xi,\eta):=\widehat{f}(\xi,\eta):=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x,y)e^{-i(x.\xi+y.\eta)}\,dx\,dy.

On the other hand, the partial Fourier transform with respect to the
y
-variable is defined as

(\mathcal{F}_{y}f)(x,\eta):=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{k}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}f(x,y)e^{-iy.\eta}\,dy.

In a similar fashion, the partial Fourier transform with respect to
x
-variable will be denoted by
\mathcal{F}_{x}.

Next, we will introduce the mixed Sobolev-type spaces
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}_{x}^{d-k},H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{k}_{y}))
of order
s\in\mathbb{R}
via the following norm

\|f\|_{L^{p}_{x}H^{s}_{y}}:=\|(1-\Delta_{y})^{\frac{1}{2}}f\|_{L^{p}_{x}L^{2}_{y}}=\|(1+|\eta|^{2})^{\frac{s}{2}}f\|_{L^{p}_{x}L^{2}_{y}}.

Furthermore, for any interval
I\subset\mathbb{R},
we will make use of the mixed space-time space
L^{q}_{t}(I,L^{p}_{x}(\mathbb{R}^{d-k},H^{s}_{y}(\mathbb{R}^{k})))
which will be briefly denotes by
L^{q}_{t}(I)L^{p}_{x}H_{y}^{s}
or simply
L^{q}_{t}L^{p}_{x}H_{y}^{s}.
These spaces are eqquiped with the norm

\|F\|_{L^{q}_{t}L^{p}_{x}H_{y}^{s}}:=\left(\int_{I}\|F(t)\|^{q}_{L^{p}_{x}H^{s}_{y}}\,dt\right)^{\frac{1}{q}}.

For suitable functions
f
and
g
defined on
\mathbb{R}^{d-k}\times\mathbb{R}^{k},
we denote by
f*_{y}g
the convolution of
f
and
g
with respect to
y
-variable defined as

f*_{y}g(x,y):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}f(x,y-y^{\prime})g(x,y^{\prime})\,dy^{\prime}.

Let
\psi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow[0,1]
be a radial smooth cut-off function supported in

\{(\xi,\eta)\in\left.\mathbb{R}^{d-k}\times\mathbb{R}^{k}:\frac{1}{2}\leq|(\xi,\eta)|\leq 2\right\}

such that

\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\psi\left(2^{-j}\xi,2^{-j}\eta\right)=1.

For
j\in\mathbb{Z}
, the Littlewood-Paley operator
P_{j}
is defined as follows:

\widehat{P_{j}f}(\xi,\eta)=\psi_{j}(\xi,\eta)\hat{f}(\xi,\eta),

(2.1)

where
\psi_{j}(\xi,\eta):=\psi\left(2^{-j}\xi,2^{-j}\eta\right)
and its support is the set
\left\{(\xi,\eta):2^{j-1}\leq|(\xi,\eta)|\leq 2^{j+1}\right\}
.The Littlewood-Paley theorem on mixed Lebesgue spaces
L_{x}^{r}L_{y}^{\widetilde{r}}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d-k}\times\mathbb{R}^{k}\right)
; for
1<r,\widetilde{r}<\infty
,
is given by

\displaystyle\|f\|_{L_{x}^{r}L_{y}^{\tilde{r}}}\lesssim\left\|\left(\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\left|P_{j}f\right|^{2}\right)^{1/2}\right\|_{L_{x}^{r}L_{y}^{\tilde{r}}}.

(2.2)

The Littlewood-Paley operators
P_{j}
satisfy the following important properties.

•

Identity relation:
f=\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}P_{j}f.

•

Quasi-orthogonal relations:
P_{j}P_{k}=0
if
|j-k|>1.

•

There exist constant
C>0
such that

C^{-1}\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\left\|P_{j}f\right\|_{L^{2}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)}^{2}\leq\|f\|_{L^{2}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)}^{2}\leq C\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\left\|P_{j}f\right\|_{L^{2}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)}^{2}.

•

Sobolev norm:

\|f\|_{H^{s}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)}\sim\left\|\{2^{js}\left\|P_{j}f\right\|_{L^{2}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)}\}_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\right\|_{\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})}.

Moreover, there exist constant
C>0
such that

C^{-1}\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}2^{2js}\left\|P_{j}f\right\|_{L^{2}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)}^{2}\leq\|f\|_{H^{s}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)}^{2}\leq C\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}2^{2js}\left\|P_{j}f\right\|_{L^{2}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)}^{2}.

To establish one of the main results of this paper, we make use of the following classical lemma from the stationary phase method (see
[
37
, VIII, Section 5, B]
). For convenience, we denote the Hessian matrix
\left(\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\xi_{i}\partial\xi_{j}}\right)
by
H
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\psi
be a compactly supported smooth function on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and let
\phi
be a smooth function satisfying rank
H\phi\geq M
on the support of
\psi
. Then, for
(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1},
we have

\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{i(x,t)\cdot(\xi,\phi(\xi))}\psi(\xi)d\xi\right|\leq C(1+|(x,t)|)^{-M/2}.

3.
Strichartz estimates for the dispersive semigroup
e^{it\Phi(D)}

In this section, we focus on establishing Strichartz estimates for the Schrödinger propagator
e^{it\Phi(D)}
associated with the operator
\Phi(D)
in the framework of partial regularity. Before presenting the main result, we first recall a few preparatory lemmas. The following lemma concerns the fixed-ime decay estimate of the Schrödinger propagator
e^{it\Phi(D)}
on mixed-norm Lebesgue spaces.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
d\geq 2
and
1\leq k\leq d
. Let
\Phi\in C^{\infty}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\backslash\{0\}\right)
be a real-valued function satisfying Assumptions (A). Assume that
2\leq\widetilde{r}\leq r\leq\infty
. Then, for
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{d-k}\times\mathbb{R}^{k},
we have the following estimate:

\displaystyle\left\|e^{it\Phi(D)}P_{0}f\right\|_{L_{x}^{r}L_{y}^{\tilde{r}}}\lesssim(1+|t|)^{-\beta(r,\widetilde{r})}\|f\|_{L_{x}^{r^{\prime}}L_{y}^{\tilde{r}^{\prime}}},

(3.1)

where
P_{0}
is the Littlewood-Paley operator given in (
2.1
) and

\beta(r,\widetilde{r}):=(M-k)\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{r}\right)+k\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{\widetilde{r}}\right).

Proof.

In order to get the estimate (
3.1
), we will apply the Riesz-Thorin interpolation theorem. Thus, it is enough to obtain estimate (
3.1
) for the following three cases:

Case
(a)

r=\widetilde{r}=2
,

Case
(b)

r=\widetilde{r}=\infty
,

Case
(c)

r=\infty
and
\tilde{r}=2
.

It is clear that
Case
(a) follows directly from the Plancherel theorem of the Fourier transform.

For
Case
(b), that is, when
r=\widetilde{r}=\infty
, considering Fourier transform and its inversion formula, we can write

\displaystyle e^{it\Phi(D)}P_{0}f(x,y)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d-k}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}K\left(x-x^{\prime},y-y^{\prime},t\right)f\left(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}\right)dy^{\prime}dx^{\prime}

\displaystyle=(K*f)(x,y),

where
P_{0}
is given in (
2.1
) and the kernel
K
is given by

\displaystyle K(x,y,t)=\frac{2}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d-k}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}e^{i(x,y)\cdot(\xi,\eta)}e^{it\Phi(\xi,\eta)}\psi(\xi,\eta)d\eta d\xi.

(3.2)

Using Young’s inequality, we get

\displaystyle\left\|e^{it\Phi(D)}P_{0}f\right\|_{L_{x,y}^{\infty}}=\left\|K*f\right\|_{L_{x,y}^{\infty}}\leq\left\|K\right\|_{L_{x,y}^{\infty}}\|f\|_{L_{x,y}^{1}}.

(3.3)

Now, we will estimate
L_{x,y}^{\infty}
norm of the kernel
K
. From our assumption on
\Phi
, rank
H\Phi(\xi,\eta)\geq M
on the support
\left\{(\xi,\eta)\in\mathbb{R}^{d-k}\times\mathbb{R}^{k}:1/2<|(\xi,\eta)|<2\right\}
of
\psi
in (
3.2
), by Lemma
2.1
, (
3.2
) can be estimate in the following way:

\displaystyle\left|K(x,y,t)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d-k}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}e^{i(x,y,t)\cdot\left(\xi,\eta,\Phi(\xi,\eta)\right)}\psi(\xi,\eta)d\eta d\xi\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim(1+|(x,y,t)|)^{-\frac{M}{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim(1+|t|)^{-\frac{M}{2}}.

Then from the inequality (
3.3
), we get

\left\|e^{it\Phi(D)}P_{0}f\right\|_{L_{x,y}^{\infty}}=\left\|K*f\right\|_{L_{x,y}^{\infty}}\lesssim(1+|t|)^{-\beta(\infty,\infty)}\|f\|_{L_{x,y}^{1}}

with
\beta(\infty,\infty)=M/2\geq 0
and this concludes
Case
(b).

For
Case
(c), that is, when
r=\infty
and
\tilde{r}=2
. Using Minkowski’s inequality and Plancherel’s theorem with respect to
y
variable, we get

\displaystyle\left\|e^{it\Phi(D)}P_{0}f\right\|_{L_{x}^{\infty}L_{y}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\left\|\|K*f\|_{L_{y}^{2}}\right\|_{L_{x}^{\infty}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d-k}}\|K\left(x-x^{\prime},\cdot\right)*_{y}f\left(x^{\prime},\cdot\right)\|_{L_{y}^{2}}dx^{\prime}\right\|_{L_{x}^{\infty}}

\displaystyle=\left\|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d-k}}\|\widetilde{K}\left(x-x^{\prime},\cdot\right)\tilde{f}\left(x^{\prime},\cdot\right)\|_{L_{\eta}^{2}}dx^{\prime}\right\|_{L_{x}^{\infty}},

(3.4)

where
\tilde{f}=\mathcal{F}_{y}(f(x,\cdot))
denotes the Fourier transform of
f
with respect to the
y\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
variable. Let us denote
\tilde{K}=\mathcal{F}_{y}(K(x,\cdot))
. Then
\tilde{K}
can be written explicitely as

\widetilde{K}(x,\eta,t)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d-k}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d-k}}e^{i(x,t)\cdot(\xi,\Phi(\xi,\eta))}\psi(\xi,\eta)d\xi.

Again applying Lemma
2.1
with phase functions
\Phi(\xi,\eta)
for fixed
|\eta|\leq 2
with our assumption that rank
H{\Phi}(\xi,\eta)\geq M-k,
we get

\displaystyle\sup_{|\eta|\leq 2}\left|\widetilde{K}(x,\eta,t)\right|\lesssim(1+|(x,t)|)^{-\frac{M-k}{2}}.

(3.5)

Coming back to the inequality (
3
), using the estimate (
3.5
) and Young’s inequality, we obtain

\displaystyle\left\|e^{it\Phi(D)}P_{0}f\right\|_{L_{x}^{\infty}L_{y}^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d-k}}\|\widetilde{K}\left(x-x^{\prime},\cdot\right)\tilde{f}\left(x^{\prime},\cdot\right)\|_{L_{\eta}^{2}}dx^{\prime}\right\|_{L_{x}^{\infty}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d-k}}(1+|(x-x^{\prime},t)|)^{-\frac{M-k}{2}}\|\tilde{f}\left(x^{\prime},\cdot\right)\|_{L_{\eta}^{2}}dx^{\prime}\right\|_{L_{x}^{\infty}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\|(1+|(\cdot,t)|)^{-\frac{M-k}{2}}*_{x}\|\tilde{f}\|_{L_{\eta}^{2}}\right\|_{L_{x}^{\infty}}

\displaystyle\lesssim(1+|t|)^{-\frac{M-k}{2}}\left\|\|\tilde{f}\|_{L_{\eta}^{2}}\right\|_{L_{x}^{1}}

\displaystyle\leq(1+|t|)^{-\frac{M-k}{2}}\|f\|_{L_{x}^{1}L_{y}^{2}}

\displaystyle=(1+|t|)^{-\beta(\infty,2)}\|f\|_{L_{x}^{1}L_{y}^{2}}

with
\beta(\infty,2)=\frac{M-k}{2}\geq 0
. This completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

Next we assuming the above decay estimates, we have the following frequency localized estimates

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\Phi\in C^{\infty}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\backslash\{0\}\right)
be a real-valued function satisfying Assumptions (A). Assume that
d\geq 1,1\leq k\leq d
, and

\displaystyle\frac{2}{q}\leq(M-k)\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{r}\right)+k\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{\widetilde{r}}\right),\quad 2\leq\widetilde{r}\leq r<\infty,\quad 2\leq q\leq\infty.

Then, we have

\displaystyle\left\|e^{it\Phi(D)}P_{0}f\right\|_{L_{t}^{q}L_{x}^{r}L_{y}^{\tilde{\tau}}}\lesssim\|f\|_{L_{x,y}^{2}}.

(3.6)

Proof.

By a standard
TT^{*}
argument, the required estimate (
3.6
) is equivalent to

\left\|\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{i(t-\tau)\Phi(D)}P_{0}g(\tau,\cdot)d\tau\right\|_{L_{t}^{q}L_{x}^{r}L_{y}^{\tilde{r}}}\lesssim\|g\|_{L_{t}^{q^{\prime}}L_{x}^{r^{\prime}}L_{y}^{\tilde{r}^{\prime}}}.

Thus our aim is to prove the above estimate. By the estimate (
3.1
) in Lemma
3.1
, we obtain

\displaystyle\left\|\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{i(t-\tau)\Phi(D)}P_{0}g(\tau,\cdot)d\tau\right\|_{L_{t}^{q}L_{x}^{r}L_{y}^{\tilde{r}}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\int_{\mathbb{R}}\|e^{i(t-\tau)\Phi(D)}P_{0}g(\tau,\cdot)\|_{L_{x}^{r}L_{y}^{\tilde{r}}}d\tau\right\|_{L_{t}^{q}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\|\int_{\mathbb{R}}(1+|t-\tau|)^{-\beta(r,\widetilde{r})}\|g(\tau,\cdot)\|_{L_{x}^{r^{\prime}}L_{y}^{\tilde{r}^{\prime}}}d\tau\right\|_{L_{t}^{q}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\|(1+|\cdot|)^{-\beta(r,\widetilde{r})}*_{t}\|g\|_{L_{x}^{r^{\prime}}L_{y}^{\tilde{r}^{\prime}}}\right\|_{L_{t}^{q}}.

From the notation of
\beta(r,\tilde{r})
, the inequality
\frac{2}{q}\leq(M-k)\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{r}\right)+k\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{\widetilde{r}}\right)
reduces to
2/q\leq\beta(r,\tilde{r})
. We now consider three cases.

•

When
2/q<\beta(r,\tilde{r})
and
2\leq q\leq\infty
: Then Young’s inequality yields

\displaystyle\left\|(1+|\cdot|)^{-\beta(r,\widetilde{r})}*_{t}\|g\|_{L_{x}^{r^{\prime}}L_{y}^{\widetilde{r}}}\right\|_{L_{t}^{q}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\|(1+|\cdot|)^{-\beta(r,\widetilde{r})}\right\|_{L_{t}^{q}}\|g\|_{L_{t}^{q^{\prime}}L_{x}^{r^{\prime}}L_{y}^{\widetilde{r}}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|g\|_{L_{t}^{q^{\prime}}L_{x}^{r^{\prime}}L_{y}^{\widetilde{r}^{\prime}}}.

•

When
2/q=\beta(r,\tilde{r})
with
2<q<\infty
: Applying one-dimensional Hardy-Littlewood Sobolev inequality, we get

\displaystyle\left\|(1+|\cdot|)^{-\beta(r,\tilde{r})}*_{t}\|g\|_{L_{x}^{r^{\prime}}L_{y}^{\tilde{r}^{\prime}}}\right\|_{L_{t}^{q}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\||\cdot|^{-\beta(r,\tilde{r})}*_{t}\|g\|_{L_{x}^{r^{\prime}}L_{y}^{\tilde{r}^{\prime}}}\right\|_{L_{t}^{q}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|g\|_{L_{t}^{q^{\prime}}L_{x}^{\tau_{x}^{\prime}}L_{y}^{\tilde{r}^{\prime}}}.

•

When
q=\infty
and
\beta(r,\widetilde{r})=0
: This implies that
r=\widetilde{r}=2
. In this case, the required estimate (
3.1
) holds trivially by Plancherel’s theorem. This completes the proof of the lemma.

∎

Now we are in a position to prove Strichartz estimates in the partial regularity framework.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
\Phi\in C^{\infty}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\backslash\{0\}\right)
be a real-valued function satisfying Assumptions (A).
Let
d\geq 1,1\leq k\leq d,2\leq\widetilde{r}\leq r<\infty
and
2<q\leq\infty
. If
q,r,\tilde{r}
satisfy

\displaystyle\frac{2}{q}\leq(M-k)\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{r}\right)+k\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{\widetilde{r}}\right).

(3.7)

Then we have

\displaystyle\left\|e^{it\Phi(D)}f\right\|_{L_{t}^{q}\left(\mathbb{R};L_{x}^{r}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d-k};L_{y}^{\tilde{r}}\left(\mathbb{R}^{k}\right)\right)\right)}\lesssim\|f\|_{\dot{H}_{x,y}^{s}}

(3.8)

under the scaling condition

\displaystyle\frac{{{m}}}{q}=-s+(d-k)\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{r}\right)+k\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{\widetilde{r}}\right),

(3.9)

where
m\geq 1
is the homogeneous degree of
\Phi.

Proof.

We begin the proof by substituting
e^{it\Phi(D)}f
into the estimate (
2.2
) as follows

\|e^{it\Phi(D)}f\|_{L_{x}^{r}L_{y}^{\tilde{r}}}\lesssim\left\|\left(\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\left|P_{j}e^{it\Phi(D)}f\right|^{2}\right)^{1/2}\right\|_{L_{x}^{r}L_{y}^{\tilde{r}}}.

Next, using the Minkowski inequality, we can conclude that

\displaystyle\left\|e^{it\Phi(D)}f\right\|_{L_{t}^{q}L_{x}^{r}L_{y}^{\tilde{r}}}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\|\left(\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\left|P_{j}e^{it\Phi(D)}f\right|^{2}\right)^{1/2}\right\|_{L_{t}^{q}L_{x}^{r}L_{y}^{\tilde{r}}}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\left\|e^{it\Phi(D)}P_{j}f\right\|_{L_{t}^{q}L_{x}^{r}L_{y}^{\tilde{r}}}^{2},

(3.10)

where in the last step, we used the fact that
P_{j}
commutes with the semigroup
e^{it\Phi(D)}
. Now, applying the Fourier transform and inversion, we can write

\displaystyle e^{it\Phi(D)}P_{j}f(x,y)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{i\left(x,y\right)\cdot(\xi,\eta)+it\Phi(\xi,\eta)}\psi_{j}(\xi,\eta)\hat{f}\left(\xi,\eta\right)d\eta d\xi

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{i\left(x,y\right)\cdot(\xi,\eta)+it\Phi(\xi,\eta)}\psi(2^{-j}\xi,2^{-j}\eta)\hat{f}\left(\xi,\eta\right)d\eta d\xi.

By the change of variables
2^{-j}\xi\rightarrow\xi
and
2^{-j}\eta\rightarrow\eta
and using the homogeneity property of
\Phi
as given in the Assumption (A), we get

\displaystyle e^{it\Phi(D)}P_{j}f(x,y)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{i\left(2^{j}x,2^{j}y\right)\cdot(\xi,\eta)+it\Phi(2^{j}\xi,2^{j}\eta)}\psi(\xi,\eta)\hat{f}\left(2^{j}\xi,2^{j}\eta\right)2^{jN}d\eta d\xi

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{i\left(2^{j}x,2^{j}y\right)\cdot(\xi,\eta)+it\Phi(2^{j}(\xi,\eta))}\psi(\xi,\eta)\hat{f}\left(2^{j}\xi,2^{j}\eta\right)2^{jN}d\eta d\xi

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{i\left(2^{j}x,2^{j}y\right)\cdot(\xi,\eta)+i2^{mj}t\Phi(\xi,\eta)}\psi(\xi,\eta)\hat{f}\left(2^{j}\xi,2^{j}\eta\right)2^{jN}d\eta d\xi

\displaystyle=e^{i2^{jm}t\Phi(D)}P_{0}f_{j}\left(2^{j}x,2^{j}y\right)

where
f_{j}(x,y):=f\left(2^{-j}x,2^{-j}y\right)
. Now using the localized estimate (
3.6
) given in Lemma
3.2
and the above scaling estimates, we can write

\displaystyle\left\|e^{it\Phi(D)}P_{j}f\right\|_{L_{t}^{q}L_{x}^{r}L_{y}^{\tilde{r}}}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim 2^{j\left(-\frac{{m}}{q}-\frac{d-k}{r}-\frac{k}{\tilde{r}}\right)}\left\|e^{it\Phi(D)}P_{0}f_{j}\right\|_{L_{t}^{q}L_{x}^{r}L_{y}^{\tilde{r}}}

\displaystyle\lesssim 2^{j\left(-\frac{{{m}}}{q}-\frac{d-k}{r}-\frac{k}{\tilde{r}}\right)}\left\|f_{j}\right\|_{L_{x,y}^{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim 2^{-\frac{jd}{2}}2^{js}2^{j\left(-s-\frac{{{m}}}{q}-\frac{d-k}{r}-\frac{k}{\tilde{r}}+\frac{d}{2}\right)}\left\|f_{j}\right\|_{L_{x,y}^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{js}\|f\|_{L_{x,y}^{2}},

(3.11)

where we used the scaling condition (
3.9
) in the last inequality.

Now, from the quasi-orthogonal relations
P_{j}P_{k}=0
if
|j-k|>1
and the identity relation
f=\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}P_{j}f
, if we write
P_{j}f=P_{j}\widetilde{P}_{j}f
with
\widetilde{P}_{j}=\sum_{k:|j-k|\leq 1}P_{k}
, then from (
3
) and (
3
), we obtain

\displaystyle\left\|e^{it\Phi(D)}f\right\|_{L_{t}^{q}L_{x}^{r}L_{y}^{\tilde{r}}}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\left\|e^{it\Phi(D)}P_{j}f\right\|_{L_{t}^{q}L_{x}^{r}L_{y}^{\tilde{r}}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\left\|e^{it\Phi(D)}P_{j}\widetilde{P}_{j}f\right\|_{L_{t}^{q}L_{x}^{r}L_{y}^{\tilde{\tau}}}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}2^{2js}\left\|\widetilde{P}_{j}f\right\|_{L_{x,y}^{2}}^{2}\lesssim\|f\|_{\dot{H}_{x,y}^{s}}^{2},

completing the proof of the theorem.
∎

By choosing specific forms of the function
\Phi
, we arrive at particular Strichartz estimates for partially regular initial data. To start, we look at the Strichartz estimate for the free Schrödinger propagator, through which we recover
[

.

Corollary 3.4
.

Let
d\geq 1,

1\leq k\leq d
and

\displaystyle\frac{2}{q}\leq(d-k)\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{r}\right)+k\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{\widetilde{r}}\right),\quad 2\leq\widetilde{r}\leq r<\infty,\quad 2<q\leq\infty.

Then, we have the following Strichartz inequality

\displaystyle\left\|e^{-it\Delta}f\right\|_{L_{t}^{q}\left(\mathbb{R};L_{x}^{r}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d-k};L_{y}^{\tilde{r}}\left(\mathbb{R}^{k}\right)\right)\right)}\lesssim\|f\|_{\dot{H}_{x,y}^{s}},

under the scaling condition

\displaystyle\frac{{{2}}}{q}=-s+(d-k)\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{r}\right)+k\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{\widetilde{r}}\right).

Proof.

By choosing
\Phi(\xi)=|\xi|^{2}
, we can verify that it satisfies Assumption (A) for
m=2,M=d.
Then, the result immediately follows from Theorem
3.3
.
∎

We show that our result yields the Strichartz estimate for the wave propagator, which was already established in
[

.

Corollary 3.5

Let

Our next result concerns the Strichartz estimates for the biharmonic operator with partially regular initial data. This particular case appears to be new in the literature.

Corollary 3.6
.

Let
d\geq 1,

1\leq k\leq d
and

\displaystyle\frac{2}{q}\leq(d-k)\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{r}\right)+k\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{\widetilde{r}}\right),\quad 2\leq\widetilde{r}\leq r<\infty,\quad 2<q\leq\infty.

Then we have

\displaystyle\left\|e^{-it\Delta^{2}}f\right\|_{L_{t}^{q}\left(\mathbb{R};L_{x}^{r}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d-k};L_{y}^{\tilde{r}}\left(\mathbb{R}^{k}\right)\right)\right)}\lesssim\|f\|_{\dot{H}_{x,y}^{s}},

\displaystyle\frac{{{4}}}{q}=(d-k)\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{r}\right)+k\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{\widetilde{r}}\right)-s.

Proof.

To derive this result, we choose
\Phi(\cdot)=|\cdot|^{4}.
We write
\Phi(\xi,\eta)=(|\xi|^{2}+|\eta|^{2})^{2}
, where
\xi=(\xi_{1},\cdots,\xi_{d-k})\in\mathbb{R}^{d-k}

In this case the Hessian matrix

\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
16\xi_{d-k}\eta_{1}&\cdots&8(|\xi|^{2}+|\eta|^{2})+16\eta_{k}^{2}\end{pmatrix}.

Observe that the rank of
H\Phi(\xi,\eta)=d
for all
(\xi,\eta)\neq 0.

Again, if we consider
\Phi_{\eta}(\xi)=(|\xi|^{2}+|\eta|^{2})^{2}
for fixed
|\eta|\leq 2
. Then Hessian matrix

H\Phi_{\eta}(\xi)=\begin{pmatrix}4(|\xi|^{2}+|\eta|^{2})+8\xi_{1}^{2}&\cdots&8\xi_{1}\xi_{d-k}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
8\xi_{d-k}\xi_{1}&\cdots&4(|\xi|^{2}+|\eta|^{2})+8\xi_{d-k}^{2}\end{pmatrix}.

in this case rank of
H\Phi_{\eta}(\xi)=d-k
for fixed
|\eta|\leq 2
.
Thus, Assumptions (A) is verified by
\Phi
by taking
m=4,M=d
, and for any
1\leq k\leq d.
Then, this corollary follows as a direct application of Theorem
3.3
.
∎

(3.13)

4.
Application: Local well-posedness of higher order nonlinear Schrödinger equations

In this section, we prove the local well-posedness theorem for the higher order nonlinear Schrödinger equation (
1.4
) with partially regular initial data
f\in L_{x}^{2}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d-2};H_{y}^{s}\left(\mathbb{R}^{2}\right)\right)
. We begin by describing our approach to prove the local wellposedness of the nonlinear problem (
1.1
) for
\Phi(\xi)=|\xi|^{m}
, that is, equation (
1.4
).

Philosophy of our approach:
First, we note that the solution of the linear equation (
1.4
) with
F=0
is given by
e^{it(-\Delta)^{\frac{m}{2}}}f
. By Duhamel’s principle, the solution map of the nonlinear equation (
1.4
) can be written as

u(t,x)=e^{it(-\Delta)^{\frac{m}{2}}}f(x)-i\int_{0}^{t}e^{i(t-\tau)(-\Delta)^{\frac{m}{2}}}F_{p}(u)(t,x)\;d\tau.

For
0<s\leq 1
and suitable values of
T,A>0
, we introduce the evolution space

X(T)=\left\{u\in C\left([0,T];L_{x}^{2}H_{y}^{s}\right)\cap L_{t}^{q}\left([0,T];L_{x}^{r}W_{y}^{s,\widetilde{r}}\right):\|u\|_{L_{t}^{q}\left([0,T];L_{x}^{r}W_{y}^{s,\tilde{r}}\right)}\leq A\right\}

(4.1)

with the distance

d(u,v)=\|u-v\|_{L_{t}^{q}\left([0,T];L_{x}^{r}L_{y}^{\tilde{r}}\right)}.

We define the operator
N:X(T)\to X(T)
defined by

Nu(t,x):=e^{it(-\Delta)^{\frac{m}{2}}}f(x)-i\int_{0}^{t}e^{i(t-\tau)(-\Delta)^{\frac{m}{2}}}F_{p}(u)(\tau,x)\;d\tau.

We aim to prove the local existence and uniqueness of small data Sobolev solutions of low regularity to the equation (
1.4
) with
the help of the Banach’s fixed point theorem argument. We find a unique fixed point (say)
u^{*}
of the operator
N
, that means,
u^{*}=Nu^{*}\in X(T)
for all positive
T
. More precisely, to find such a unique fixed point, we will establish two crucial inequalities of the form

\displaystyle\|Nu\|_{X(T)}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\|f\right\|_{L_{x}^{2}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d-2};H_{y}^{s}\left(\mathbb{R}^{2}\right)\right)}+\|u\|_{X(T)}^{p}

(4.2)

and

\displaystyle d(u,v)=\|Nu-Nv\|_{L_{t}^{q}\left(I;L_{x}^{r}L_{y}^{\tilde{r}}\right)}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|u-v\|_{X(T)}\left[\|u\|_{X(T)}^{p-1}+\|v\|_{X(T)}^{p-1}\right],

(4.3)

for any
u,v\in X(T)
.

The following result is related to the local in-time wellposedness for the higher order nonlinear Schrödinger equation (
1.4
) with partially regular initial data
f\in L_{x}^{2}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d-2};H_{y}^{s}\left(\mathbb{R}^{2}\right)\right)
.

Theorem 4.1
.

For
0<s\leq 1,m>2
and
d\geq 3
, let
1<p<1+\frac{2m}{d-2s}
and
f\in

L_{x}^{2}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d-2};H_{y}^{s}\left(\mathbb{R}^{2}\right)\right)
. Further, assume that

(q,r,\tilde{r})
with
2\leq\widetilde{r}\leq r<\infty
and
2<q\leq\infty
satisfies

\frac{{{m}}}{q}=(d-2)\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{r}\right)+2\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{\widetilde{r}}\right).

Then there exists
T>0

u\in C_{t}\left([0,T];L_{x}^{2}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d-2};H_{y}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\right)\right)\cap L_{t}^{q}\left([0,T];L_{x}^{r}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d-2};W_{y}^{s,\widetilde{r}}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\right)\right)

that satisfies the higher order nonlinear schrödinger equation (
1.4
).

Proof.

In order to prove the existence of a local solution to the nonlinear equation (
1.4
), it is enough to show that the map

N:X(T)\to Nu(t,x):=e^{it(-\Delta)^{\frac{m}{2}}}f(x)-i\int_{0}^{t}e^{i(t-\tau)(-\Delta)^{\frac{m}{2}}}F_{p}(u)(\tau,x)\;d\tau

is defines a contraction map on
X(T)
defined in (
4.1
). To prove
N
is a contraction map on
X(T),
by denoting
\mathcal{I}=[0,T]
, our first step is to show that

\displaystyle\left\|\left\langle\nabla_{y}\right\rangle^{s}F_{p}(u)\right\|_{L_{t}^{q_{1}^{\prime}}\left(\mathcal{I};L_{x}^{r_{1}^{\prime}}L_{y}^{\tilde{r}_{1}^{\prime}}\right)}\leq CT^{\beta(N,p,s)}\|u\|_{L_{t}^{q_{1}}\left(\mathcal{I};L_{x}^{r_{1}}W_{y}^{s,\tilde{r}_{1}}\right)}^{p}

for
0<s\leq 1
and
1<p<1+\frac{2m}{d-2s}
and for some
\beta(N,p,s)>0
.

For
d\geq 3
,
0<s\leq 1
and for a fixed exponent
p
with
1<p<1+\frac{2m}{d-2s}
, we choose
\varepsilon>0
and a pair
\left(q_{1},r_{1},\widetilde{r}_{1}\right)
such that it satisfies the following conditions

\frac{(1-s)(p-1)}{2}<\varepsilon<\min\left\{\frac{d-2}{m^{2}}\left(1+\frac{2m}{d-2}-p\right),\frac{p-1}{2}\right\}

and

\frac{1}{r_{1}}=\frac{1}{p+1},\quad\frac{1}{\widetilde{r}_{1}}=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\epsilon}{p+1},\quad\frac{1}{q_{1}}=\frac{d-2}{2m}-\frac{d-2}{m(p+1)}+\frac{m\varepsilon}{2(p+1)}.

(4.4)

Note that above
\left(q_{1},r_{1},\widetilde{r}_{1}\right)
also satisfies the admissible condition
\frac{{{m}}}{q_{1}}+\frac{d-2}{r_{1}}+\frac{2}{\widetilde{r}_{1}}=\frac{d}{2}.

Using (
1.2
) and the Hölder inequality with (
4.4
), we get the estimate

\displaystyle\left\|F_{p}(u)\right\|_{L_{t}^{q_{1}^{\prime}}\left(\mathcal{I};L_{x}^{r_{1}^{\prime}}L_{y}^{\tilde{r}_{1}^{\prime}}\right)}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\|\chi_{\mathcal{I}}(t)|u|^{p-1}|u|\right\|_{L_{t}^{q_{1}^{\prime}}\left(\mathcal{I};L_{x}^{r_{1}^{\prime}}L_{y}^{L_{1}^{\prime}}\right)}

\displaystyle\leq CT^{1-\frac{p+1}{q_{1}}}\|u\|_{L_{t}^{q_{1}}\left(\mathcal{I};L_{x}^{r_{1}}L_{y}^{(p-1)(p+1)/2\varepsilon}\right)}^{p-1}\|u\|_{L_{t}^{q_{1}}\left(\mathcal{I};L_{x}^{r_{1}}L_{y}^{\tilde{r_{0}}}\right)}.

(4.5)

In a similar manner, for
0<s\leq 1
, using the following fractional chain rule for Laplacian on
\mathbb{R}^{d}

\||\nabla|^{s}F(u)\|_{L^{c}}\leq\||F^{\prime}(u)\|_{L^{a}}\||\nabla|^{s}F(u)\|_{L^{c}},

where
\frac{1}{c}=\frac{1}{a}+\frac{1}{b},
along with the Hölder inequality, we obtain

\displaystyle\left\|\left|\nabla_{y}\right|^{s}F_{p}(u)\right\|_{L_{t}^{q_{1}^{\prime}}\left(\mathcal{I};L_{x}^{r_{1}^{\prime}}L_{y}^{\tilde{r}_{1}}\right)}\leq CT^{1-\frac{p+1}{q_{1}}}\|u\|_{L_{t}^{q_{1}}\left(\mathcal{I};L_{x}^{r_{1}}L_{y}^{(p-1)(p+1)/2\varepsilon}\right)}^{p-1}\|u\|_{L_{t}^{q_{1}}\left(\mathcal{I};L_{x}^{r_{1}}W_{y}^{s,\tilde{r}_{1}}\right)}.

(4.6)

Using the fact that
W^{s,\widetilde{r}_{1}}\subseteq L^{\widetilde{r}_{1}}
for
s\geq 0
and by the Sobolev embedding in two dimensions

W^{s,\widetilde{r}_{1}}\hookrightarrow W^{1-\frac{2\varepsilon}{p-1},\widetilde{r}_{1}}\hookrightarrow L^{\frac{(p-1)(p+1)}{2\epsilon_{1}}},

the inequalities (
4
) and (
4.6
) further reduces to

\displaystyle\left\||\nabla_{y}|^{s}F_{p}(u)\right\|_{L_{t}^{q_{1}^{\prime}}\left(\mathcal{I};L_{x}^{r_{1}^{\prime}}L_{y}^{\vec{r}^{\prime}}\right)}\leq CT^{\beta_{1}(d,p,s)}\|u\|_{L_{t}^{q_{1}}\left(\mathcal{I};L_{x}^{r_{1}}W_{y}^{s,\bar{r}_{1}}\right)}^{p}

(4.7)

with
1\geq s\geq 0
and
\beta_{1}(d,p,s)=1-\frac{p+1}{q_{1}}=\frac{d-2}{2m}\left(1+\frac{2m}{d-2}-p\right)-\frac{m\varepsilon}{2}>0.

Thus using the inequalities (
4
) and (
4.7
), we can write

\displaystyle\left\|\left\langle\nabla_{y}\right\rangle^{s}F_{p}(u)\right\|_{L_{t}^{q_{1}^{\prime}}\left(\mathcal{I};L_{x}^{r_{1}^{\prime}}L_{y}^{\tilde{r}_{1}^{\prime}}\right)}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\|F_{p}(u)\right\|_{L_{t}^{q_{1}^{\prime}}\left(\mathcal{I};L_{x}^{r_{1}^{\prime}}L_{y}^{\tilde{r}_{1}}\right)}+\left\|\left|\nabla_{y}\right|^{s}F_{p}(u)\right\|_{L_{t}^{q_{1}^{\prime}}\left(\mathcal{I};L_{x}^{r_{1}^{\prime}}L_{y}^{\tilde{r}_{1}^{\prime}}\right)}

\displaystyle\lesssim T^{\beta_{1}(d,p,s)}\|u\|_{L_{t}^{q_{1}}\left(\mathcal{I};L_{x}^{r_{1}}W_{y}^{s,\bar{r}_{1}}\right)}^{p}.

(4.8)

In a similar way, we can have

\displaystyle\left\|F_{p}(u)-F_{p}(v)\right\|_{L_{t}^{q_{1}^{\prime}}\left(\mathcal{I};L_{x}^{r_{1}^{\prime}}L_{y}^{\tilde{r_{1}}^{\prime}}\right)}

\displaystyle\leq CT^{\beta_{1}(d,p,s)}\left(\|u\|_{L_{t}^{q_{1}}\left(\mathcal{I};L_{x}^{r_{1}}W_{y}^{s,\tilde{r}_{1}}\right)}^{p-1}+\|v\|_{L_{t}^{q_{1}}\left(\mathcal{I};L_{x}^{r_{1}}W_{y}^{s,\tilde{r}_{1}}\right)}^{p-1}\right)\|u-v\|_{L_{t}^{q_{1}}\left(\mathcal{I};L_{x}^{r_{1}}L_{y}^{\tilde{r}_{1}}\right)},

(4.9)

by using the following elementary properties for the nonlinearity
F_{p}(u):

|F_{p}(u)-F_{p}(v)|\leq C(|u|^{p-1}+|v|^{p-1})|u-v|.

(4.10)

Now our aim is to establish (
4.2
) and (
4.3
).
By Plancherel’s theorem, a dual version of the Strichartz inequality (
3.13
) with
s=0
, and from the inequality (
4
), one can see that

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in\mathcal{I}}\|Nu\|_{L_{x}^{2}H_{y}^{s}}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{t\in\mathcal{I}}\|e^{it(-\Delta)^{\frac{m}{2}}}f\|_{L_{x}^{2}H_{y}^{s}}+\sup_{t\in\mathcal{I}}\left\|\int_{0}^{t}e^{i(t-\tau)(-\Delta)^{\frac{m}{2}}}F_{p}(u)\;d\tau\right\|_{L_{x}^{2}H_{y}^{s}}

\displaystyle\leq C\|f\|_{L_{x}^{2}H_{y}^{s}}+C\sup_{t\in\mathcal{I}}\left\|\left\langle\nabla_{y}\right\rangle^{s}\int_{0}^{t}e^{-i\tau(-\Delta)^{\frac{m}{2}}}F_{p}(u)d\tau\right\|_{L_{x}^{2}L_{y}^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq C\|f\|_{L_{x}^{2}H_{y}^{s}}+C\left\|\left\langle\nabla_{y}\right\rangle^{s}F_{p}(u)\right\|_{L_{t}^{q_{1}^{\prime}}\left(\mathcal{I};L_{x}^{r_{1}^{\prime}}L_{y}^{\tilde{r}_{1}^{\prime}}\right)}

\displaystyle\leq C\|f\|_{L_{x}^{2}H_{y}^{s}}+CT^{\beta_{1}(d,p,s)}\|u\|_{L_{t}^{q_{1}}\left(\mathcal{I};L_{x}^{r_{1}}W_{y}^{s,\bar{r}_{1}}\right)}^{p},

for some
\left(q_{1},r_{1},\widetilde{r}_{1}\right)
that satisfies
\frac{{{m}}}{q_{1}}+\frac{d-2}{r_{1}}+\frac{2}{\widetilde{r}_{1}}=\frac{d}{2}.
Thus for
u\in X
, it follows that

\sup_{t\in\mathcal{I}}\|Nu\|_{L_{x}^{2}H_{y}^{s}}\leq C\|f\|_{L_{x}^{2}H_{y}^{s}}+CT^{\beta_{1}(d,p,s)}A^{p}.

(4.11)

Let us now proceed to prove our next aim to show inequality of the form (
4.3
). Now using
(
3.15
) and (
4
), we get

\displaystyle d(Nu,Nv)

\displaystyle=\|Nu-Nv\|_{L_{t}^{q}\left(I;L_{x}^{r}L_{y}^{\tilde{r}}\right)}

\displaystyle=\left\|\int_{0}^{t}e^{i(t-\tau)(-\Delta)^{\frac{m}{2}}}\left(F_{p}(u)-F_{p}(v)\right)d\tau\right\|_{L_{t}^{q}\left(\mathcal{I};L_{x}^{r}L_{y}^{\tilde{r}}\right)}

\displaystyle\leq C\left\|F_{p}(u)-F_{p}(v)\right\|_{L^{q_{1}^{\prime}}\left(\mathcal{I};L_{x}^{r_{1}^{\prime}}L_{y}^{\bar{r}_{1}^{\prime}}\right)}

\displaystyle\leq CT^{\beta_{1}(d,p,s)}\left(\|u\|_{L_{t}^{q_{1}}\left(\mathcal{I};L_{x}^{r_{1}}W_{y}^{s,\tilde{r}_{1}}\right)}^{p-1}+\|v\|_{L_{t}^{q_{1}}\left(\mathcal{I};L_{x}^{r_{1}}W_{y}^{s,\tilde{r}_{1}}\right)}^{p-1}\right)\|u-v\|_{L_{t}^{q_{1}}\left(\mathcal{I};L_{x}^{r_{1}}L_{y}^{\tilde{r}_{1}}\right)}

\displaystyle\leq 2CT^{\beta_{1}(d,p,s)}A^{p-1}d(u,v)

(4.12)

for
u,v\in X(T)
. By choosing
A=2C\|f\|_{L_{x}^{2}H_{y}^{s}}
and taking sufficiently small
T>0
such that

2CT^{\beta_{1}(d,p,s)}A^{p-1}\leq\frac{1}{2}.

Then (
4.11
) and (
4
) reduces to

\displaystyle\|Nu\|_{L^{q}\left(\mathcal{I};L_{x}^{r}W_{y}^{s,\bar{r}}\right)}\leq A

(4.13)

and

\displaystyle d(Nu,Nv)\leq\frac{1}{2}d(u,v),

(4.14)

respectively for
u,v\in X(T).
Thus from (
4.13
), we see that
Nu\in X(T)
, i.e.,
N
maps
X(T)
into itself.
Also from (
4.14
), it follows that the map
N
is a contraction mapping on the ball
\mathcal{B}(R)
around the origin in the Banach space
X(T)
. Therefore, by Banach’s fixed point theorem, there exists a uniquely determined fixed point
u^{*}
of the operator
N
, which means
u^{*}=Nu^{*}\in X(T)
. This fixed point
u^{*}
will be our mild solution to (
1.4
) on
[0,T]
. This implies that there exists a local small data Sobolev solution
u^{*}
of the equation
u^{*}=Nu^{*}
in
X(T)
, which also gives the solution to the equation (
1.4
) and this completes the proof of the local well-posedness theorem.
∎

5.1.
Dunkl (differential-difference) operators

Root systems together with finite reflection groups form the fundamental building blocks of the theory of Dunkl operators. We begin this section by recalling the definition of a root system.

Let
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the standard Euclidean scalar product in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. For
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we denote
|x|
as
|x|=\langle x,x\rangle^{1/2}
.
For
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\backslash\{0\},
denote
r_{\alpha}
as the reflection with respect to the hyperplane
\langle\alpha\rangle^{\perp}
, orthogonal to
\alpha
and is defined by

r_{\alpha}(x):=x-2\frac{\langle\alpha,x\rangle}{|\alpha|^{2}}\alpha,\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

A finite set
\mathcal{R}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}\backslash\{0\}
is said to be a root system if the following holds:

(1)

r_{\alpha}(\mathcal{R})=\mathcal{R}
for all
\alpha\in\mathcal{R}
,

(2)

\mathcal{R}\cap\mathbb{R}\alpha=\{\pm\alpha\}
for all
\alpha\in\mathcal{R}
.

For a given root system
\mathcal{R}
, the subgroup
G\subset O(n,\mathbb{R})
generated by the reflections
\left\{r_{\alpha}\mid\alpha\in\mathcal{R}\right\}
is called the finite Coxeter group associated with
\mathcal{R}
. The dimension of
span\mathcal{R}
is called the rank of
\mathcal{R}
. For a detailed study on the theory of the finite reflection groups, we refer the reader to
[
21
]
. For some
\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\backslash\bigcup_{\alpha\in\mathcal{R}}\langle\alpha\rangle^{\perp}
, let
\mathcal{R}^{+}:=\{\alpha\in\mathcal{R}:\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle>0\}
be a fix positive root system.

Typical examples of such systems include the Weyl groups, for instance, the symmetric group
S_{n}
corresponding to the root system of type
A_{n-1}
and the hyperoctahedral group associated with type
B_{n}
. In addition,
H_{3},H_{4}
(icosahedral groups) and,
I_{2}(n)
(symmetry group of the regular
n
-gon) also belong to the class of Coxeter groups.

A multiplicity function associated with

G
is a function
\kappa:\mathcal{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
that remains constant on the
G
-orbits. Setting
\kappa_{\alpha}:=\kappa(\alpha)
for
\alpha\in\mathcal{R},
from the definition of
G
-invariant, we have
\kappa_{g\alpha}=\kappa_{\alpha}
for all
g\in G
. We say
\kappa
is non-negative if
\kappa_{\alpha}\geq 0
for all
\alpha\in\mathcal{R}
. The
\mathbb{C}
-vector space of non-negative multiplicity functions on
\mathcal{R}
is denoted by
\mathcal{K}^{+}
. We also denote
\gamma
as
\gamma=\gamma(\kappa):=\sum\limits_{\alpha\in\mathcal{R}^{+}}\kappa(\alpha)
. For simplicity of notation, we will use the notation
N:=d+2\gamma
to denote the homogeneous dimension throughout the paper.

In 1989, Dunkl introduced, for
\xi\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
and
\kappa\in\mathcal{K}^{+},
a family of first-order differential–difference operators
T_{\xi}:=T_{\xi}(\kappa)
defined by

\displaystyle T_{\xi}(\kappa)f(x):=\partial_{\xi}f(x)+\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{R}^{+}}\kappa_{\alpha}\langle\alpha,\xi\rangle\frac{f(x)-f\left(r_{\alpha}x\right)}{\langle\alpha,x\rangle},\quad f\in C^{1}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right),

(5.1)

where
\partial_{\xi}
denotes the directional derivative corresponding to
\xi
. The operator
T_{\xi}
defined in (
5.1
) and commonly referred to as the Dunkl operator, represents one of the most significant advances in the theory of special functions linked to root systems
[
12
]
. These operators commute with each other and are skew-symmetric relative to the
G
-invariant measure
h_{\kappa}(x)dx
, where the weight function is given by

h_{\kappa}(x):=\prod\limits_{\alpha\in\mathcal{R}^{+}}|\langle\alpha,x\rangle|^{2\kappa_{\alpha}}

is of homogeneous degree
2\gamma
. Because the multiplicity function is
G
-invariant the definition of the Dunkl operator does not depend on the particular choice of the positive subsystem
\mathcal{R}^{+}
. In
[
13
]
, it is shown that for any
\kappa\in\mathcal{K}^{+}
, there is a unique linear isomorphism
V_{\kappa}
(Dunkl’s intertwining operator) on the space
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
of polynomials on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that

(1)

V_{\kappa}\left(\mathcal{P}_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\right)=\mathcal{P}_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
for all
m\in\mathbb{N}
,

(2)

V_{\kappa}|_{\mathcal{P}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}=id
,

(3)

T_{\xi}(\kappa)V_{\kappa}=V_{\kappa}\partial_{\xi}
,

where
\mathcal{P}_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
denotes the space of homogeneous polynomials of degree
m
. Rösler in
[
36
]
proved that, for any finite reflection group
G
and any
k\in\mathcal{K}^{+}
, there exists a unique positive Radon probability measure
\rho_{x}^{\kappa}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that

V_{\kappa}f(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(\xi)d\rho_{x}^{\kappa}(\xi).

(5.2)

The measure
\rho_{x}^{k}
depends on
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and its support is contained in the ball
B\left(\|x\|\right):=\{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\|\xi\|\leq\|x\|\}
. Owing to the Laplace-type representation (
5.2
), Dunkl’s intertwining operator
V_{\kappa}
admits an extension to a broader class of function spaces.

Let
\{\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\cdots,\xi_{d}\}
be an orthonormal basis of
(\mathbb{R}^{d},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)
. Then the Dunkl Laplacian operator
\Delta_{\kappa}
is defined as

\Delta_{\kappa}=\sum_{j=1}^{d}T^{2}_{\xi_{j}}(\kappa).

The definition of
\Delta_{\kappa}
is independent of the choice of the orthonormal basis of
\mathbb{R}^{d}.
In fact, one can see that the operator
\Delta_{\kappa}
also can be expressed as

\Delta_{\kappa}f(x)=\Delta f(x)+\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{R}^{+}}\kappa_{\alpha}\left\{\frac{2\langle\nabla f(x),\alpha\rangle}{\langle\alpha,x\rangle}-|\alpha|^{2}\frac{f(x)-f(r_{\alpha}x)}{\langle\alpha,x\rangle^{2}}\right\},\quad f\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),

where
\nabla
and
\Delta
are the usual gradiant and Laplacian operator on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, respectively. Note that, for
\kappa\equiv 0
, coincides with the classical Euclidean Laplacian
\Delta
.

For every
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the system

\begin{cases}T_{\xi}u(x,y)=\langle y,\xi\rangle\,u(x,y),\quad\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\\
u(0,y)=1,\end{cases}

admits a unique analytic solution on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, which we denote as
E_{\kappa}(x,y)
, which is generally referred to as the Dunkl kernel. The kernel has a unique holomorphic extension to
\mathbb{C}^{n}\times\mathbb{C}^{n}
and is given by

E_{\kappa}(x,y)=V_{\kappa}(e^{\langle\cdot,y\rangle})(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{\langle\xi,y\rangle}d\rho_{x}^{\kappa}(\xi),\quad\forall x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

When
\kappa\equiv 0
, the Dunkl kernel
E_{\kappa}(x,y)
reduces to the exponential
e^{\langle x,y\rangle}
. For
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
z,w\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
, the Dunkl kernel satisfies the following properties:

(i)

E_{\kappa}(z,w)=E_{\kappa}(w,z)
and
E_{\kappa}(\lambda z,w)=E_{\kappa}(z,\lambda w),\quad\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
;

(ii)

for any
\nu\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
, we have

|D_{z}^{\nu}E_{\kappa}(x,z)|\leq|x|^{|\nu|}e^{|Re(z)||x|},

where
D_{z}^{\nu}=\frac{\partial^{|\nu|}}{\partial{z_{1}}^{\nu_{1}}\cdots{z_{n}}^{\nu_{n}}}
and
|\nu|=\nu_{1}+\cdots+\nu_{n}
. In particular, for any
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

|E_{\kappa}(x,iy)|\leq 1;

(iii)

E_{\kappa}(ix,y)=\overline{E_{\kappa}(-ix,y)}
and
E_{\kappa}(gx,gy)=E_{\kappa}(x,y),\quad g\in G
.

5.2.
The Dunkl transform

For
1\leq p<\infty
, let
L_{\kappa}^{p}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)
be the space of
L^{p}
-functions on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with respect to the weight
h_{\kappa_{1}}(x)
with the
L_{\kappa}^{p}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)
-norm

\|f\|_{L_{\kappa}^{p}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)}=\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|f(x)|^{p}h_{\kappa_{1}}(x)dx\right)^{\frac{1}{p}},

and
\|f\|_{L_{\kappa}^{\infty}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)}=ess\sup\limits_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}|f(x)|
.
The Dunkl transform is defined on
L_{\kappa}^{1}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)
by

\mathcal{F}_{\kappa}f(y)=\frac{1}{c_{\kappa}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x)E_{\kappa}(x,-iy)h_{\kappa_{1}}(x)dx,

(5.3)

where
c_{\kappa}
is the Mehta-type constant defined by

c_{\kappa}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-\frac{|x|^{2}}{2}}h_{\kappa_{1}}(x)dx.

When
\kappa\equiv 0
,the Dunkl transform reduces to the classical Fourier transform. With
N=2\gamma+n
, here we list some basic properties of the Dunkl transform.

(i)

For all
f\in L_{\kappa}^{1}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)
, we have

\|\mathcal{F}_{\kappa}f\|_{L_{\kappa}^{\infty}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)}\leq\frac{1}{c_{\kappa}}\|f\|_{L_{\kappa}^{1}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)}.

(ii)

For any function
f
in the Schwartz space
\mathcal{S}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)
, we have

\mathcal{F}_{\kappa}(T_{\xi}f)(y)=i\langle\xi,y\rangle\mathcal{F}_{\kappa}f(y),\quad y,\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

In particular, it follows that

\mathcal{F}_{\kappa}(\Delta_{\kappa}f)(y)=-|y|^{2}\mathcal{F}_{\kappa}f(y),\quad y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(iii)

For all
f\in L_{\kappa}^{1}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)
, we have

\mathcal{F}_{\kappa}(f(\cdot/\lambda))(y)=\lambda^{N}\mathcal{F}_{\kappa}f(\lambda y),\quad y\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\lambda>0.

(iv)

The Dunkl transform
\mathcal{F}_{\kappa}
is a homeomorphism of the Schwartz space
\mathcal{S}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)
and its inverse is given by
\mathcal{F}_{\kappa}^{-1}g(x)=\mathcal{F}_{\kappa}g(-x),
for all
g\in\mathcal{S}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)
. In addition, for all
f\in\mathcal{S}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)
, it satisfies

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|f(x)|^{2}h_{\kappa_{1}}(x)dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\mathcal{F}_{\kappa}f(y)|^{2}h_{\kappa}(y)dy.

(v)

For every
f\in L_{\kappa}^{1}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)
such that
\mathcal{F}_{\kappa}f\in L_{\kappa}^{1}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)
, we have the inversion formula

f(x)=\frac{1}{c_{\kappa}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathcal{F}_{\kappa}f(y)E_{\kappa}(ix,y)h_{\kappa}(y)dy,\quad a.e.\;x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(vi)

If
f
is a radial function in
L_{\kappa}^{1}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)
such that
f(x)=\tilde{f}(|x|)
, then

\mathcal{F}_{\kappa}f(y)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(N/2)}\int_{0}^{\infty}\tilde{f}(r)\frac{J_{\frac{d-2}{2}}(r|y|)}{\left(r|y|\right)^{\frac{d-2}{2}}}r^{N-1}dr,

(5.4)

where
J_{\nu}
denotes the Bessel function of order
\nu>-\frac{1}{2}
.

(vii)

For
1\leq p\leq\infty
and
s\in\mathbb{R}
, the homogeneous Dunkl-Sobolev space
\dot{H}_{\kappa}^{p,s}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)
is the set of tempered distributions
u\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)
such that
\mathcal{F}^{-1}_{\kappa}\left(|\cdot|^{s}\mathcal{F}_{\kappa}f\right)\in L_{\kappa}^{p}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)
and is equiped with the norm

\|u\|_{\dot{H}_{\kappa}^{p,s}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)}=\left\|\mathcal{F}^{-1}_{\kappa}\left(|\cdot|^{s}\mathcal{F}_{\kappa}f\right)\right\|_{L_{\kappa}^{p}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)}.

In particular, when
p=2
, we denote
\dot{H}_{\kappa}^{p,s}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)
by
\dot{H}_{\kappa}^{s}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)
in short and
\dot{H}_{\kappa}^{0}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)=L_{\kappa}^{2}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)
.

(viii)

For any
1\leq p_{1}\leq p_{2}\leq\infty
and
s_{1}\leq s_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
s_{1}-\frac{d}{p_{1}}=s_{2}-\frac{d}{p_{2}}
, we have

\dot{H}_{\kappa}^{p_{1},s_{1}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\subseteq\dot{H}_{\kappa}^{p_{2},s_{2}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

(5.5)

5.3.
Dunkl convolution operator

For given
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the Dunkl translation operator
f\mapsto\tau_{x}f
is defined on
\mathcal{S}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)
by

\mathcal{F}_{\kappa}(\tau_{x}f)(y)=E_{\kappa}(x,iy)\mathcal{F}_{\kappa}f(y),\quad y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Currently, an explicit formula for the Dunkl translation operator is available only in two cases. When
f(x)=\tilde{f}(|x|)
is a continuous radial function in
L_{\kappa}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, the Dunkl translation operator is represented by (see
[
36
,
9
]
)

\tau_{x}f(y)=V_{\kappa}[\tilde{f}\left(\sqrt{|y|^{2}+|x|^{2}-2\langle y,\cdot\rangle}\right)](x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\tilde{f}\left(\sqrt{|y|^{2}+|x|^{2}-2\langle y,\xi\rangle}\right)d\rho^{\kappa}_{x}(\xi).

(5.6)

The Dunkl translation operator can likewise be extended to the space of tempered distributions
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)
.Extending key results from classical Fourier analysis to the framework of the Dunkl transform proves to be quite challenging. A major difficulty arises from the fact that the Dunkl translation operator is, in general, not positive. Even
L_{\kappa}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
-boundedness of
\tau_{x}
is not established in general. On the other hand, if
f
is a radial function in
L_{\kappa}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
1\leq p\leq\infty
, the following holds (see
[
16
,
39
]
)

\|\tau_{x}f\|_{L_{\kappa}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq\|f\|_{L_{\kappa}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}.

Using the Dunkl translation operator, the Dunkl convolution of functions
f,g\in\mathcal{S}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)
is defined as

f*_{\kappa}g(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\tau_{x}f(-y)g(y)h_{\kappa}(y)dy,\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

The Dunkl convolution enjoys the following properties.

(1)

\mathcal{F}_{\kappa}(f*_{\kappa}g)=\mathcal{F}_{\kappa}(f)\mathcal{F}_{\kappa}(g)
,
\mathcal{F}^{-1}_{\kappa}(f*_{\kappa}g)=\mathcal{F}^{-1}_{\kappa}(f)\mathcal{F}^{-1}_{\kappa}(g)
, and
f*_{\kappa}g=g*_{\kappa}f
.

(2)

Young’s inequality: Let
1\leq p,q,r\leq\infty
such that
1+\frac{1}{r}=\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}
. If
f\in L^{p}_{\kappa}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
g
is a radial function of
L_{\kappa}^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, then
f*_{\kappa}g\in L_{\kappa}^{r}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and we have

\|f*_{\kappa}g\|_{L_{\kappa}^{r}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq\|f\|_{L_{\kappa}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\|g\|_{L_{\kappa}^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}.

(5.7)

Since the Dunkl transform of radial functions is explicitly expressed by the integral related to Bessel functions, we first list some properties of Bessel functions.

Let
J_{\nu}
be the Bessel function of order
\nu>-\frac{1}{2}

Lemma 5.1
.

For
r>0

\mathcal{R}_{1}

\mathcal{R}:=\mathcal{R}_{1}\times(0)_{k}\cup(0)_{d-k}\times\mathcal{R}_{2}

(0)_{j}=(0,0,\ldots,0)\in\mathbb{R}^{j}
, is a root system on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Let
\kappa_{1}:\mathcal{R}_{1}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
be a multiplicity function for
\mathcal{R}_{1}
and
\kappa_{2}:\mathcal{R}_{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}

\mathcal{R}_{2}

\kappa:\mathcal{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}

\begin{array}[]{ll}\kappa(\alpha,0)=\kappa_{1}(\alpha)&\text{ for all }\alpha\in\mathcal{R}_{1}\\

This defines
\kappa
on all of
\mathcal{R}
. For this choice of the multiplicity function, partial counterparts of the Dunkl objects will actually become coordinate wise product of the corresponding linear objects. In fact, for any
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, let
h_{\kappa_{1}}(x)
and
h_{\kappa_{2}}(y)
are the

\mathcal{R}_{1}
and
\mathcal{R}_{2}
with homogeneous degree

and
2\gamma_{2}

is the weight on
\mathcal{R}
with homogeneous degree

d\mu_{\kappa}\left(x,y\right)=d\mu_{\kappa_{1}}(x)d\mu_{\kappa_{2}}(y).

E_{\kappa_{1}}(x_{1},y_{1})

and
\kappa_{2}
. Then the kernel

on
\mathbb{R}^{d}

•

1\leq p<\infty
, let

•

L_{\kappa_{1},x}^{p}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d-k}\right)

Since the root system
\mathcal{R}
is the union of two orthogonal root system, we can define Dunkl operator on
\mathcal{R}

\displaystyle T_{j}(\kappa)f(x):=\partial_{j}f(x)+\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{R}^{+}}\kappa_{\alpha}\langle\alpha,\xi\rangle\frac{f(x)-f\left(r_{\alpha}x\right)}{\langle\alpha,x\rangle},\quad f\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

\Delta_{\kappa}
is defined as

\Delta_{\kappa}=\sum_{j=1}^{d}T^{2}_{j}(\kappa).

•

We define the Dunkl gradient of a complex valued function
f
as the operator valued operator

\nabla_{\kappa}f=\left(T_{1},T_{2}f,\cdots,T_{d}f\right)

and modulus by

|\nabla_{\kappa}f|(x)=\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d}|T_{j}(\kappa)f(x)|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

•

The homogeneous fractional operator
|\nabla_{\kappa}|^{s}
is defined as

|\nabla_{\kappa}|^{s}f:=\mathcal{F}^{-1}_{\kappa}\left(|\cdot|^{s}\mathcal{F}_{\kappa}f\right).

•

For
1\leq p\leq\infty
and
s\in\mathbb{R}
, the homogeneous Dunkl-Sobolev space
\dot{H}_{\kappa}^{p,s}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)
is defined as

\dot{H}_{\kappa}^{p,s}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)=\left\{u\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right):\|u\|_{\dot{H}_{\kappa}^{p,s}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)}=\left\|\mathcal{F}^{-1}_{\kappa}\left(|\cdot|^{s}\mathcal{F}_{\kappa}f\right)\right\|_{L_{\kappa}^{p}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)}<\infty\right\}.

5.5.
Littlewood-Paley projections

Let
\psi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow[0,1]
be a radial smooth cut-off function supported in

\{\xi\in\left.\mathbb{R}^{d}:\frac{1}{2}\leq|\xi|\leq 2\right\}

such that

\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\psi\left(2^{-j}\xi\right)=1.

For
j\in\mathbb{Z}
, the Littlewood-Paley operator
\mathcal{P}_{j}
is defined as follows:

\mathcal{F}_{\kappa}(\mathcal{P}_{j}f)(\xi,\eta)=\psi_{j}(\xi,\eta)\mathcal{F}_{\kappa}(f)(\xi,\eta),

where
\psi_{j}(\xi,\eta):=\psi\left(2^{-j}\xi,2^{-j}\eta\right)
and its supporst is the set
\left\{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:2^{j-1}\leq|(\xi,\eta)|\leq 2^{j+1}\right\}
.The Littlewood-Paley theorem on mixed Lebesgue spaces
L^{r}_{{\kappa_{1},x}}L^{\tilde{r}}_{\kappa_{2},y}(\mathbb{R}^{d-k}\times\mathbb{R}^{k})
; for
1<r,\tilde{r}<\infty
,
is given by

\|f\|_{L^{r}_{{\kappa_{1},x}}L^{\tilde{r}}_{\kappa_{2},y}}\lesssim\left\|\left(\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\left|\mathcal{P}_{j}f\right|^{2}\right)^{1/2}\right\|_{L^{r}_{{\kappa_{1},x}}L^{\tilde{r}}_{\kappa_{2},y}}.

(5.15)

The Littlewood-Paley operators
\mathcal{P}_{j}
also satisfy the following important properties.

•

Identity relation:
f=\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathcal{P}_{j}f.

•

Quasi-orthogonal relations:
\mathcal{P}_{j}\mathcal{P}_{k}=0
if
|j-k|>1.

•

There exists a constant
C>0
such that

C^{-1}\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\left\|\mathcal{P}_{j}f\right\|_{L_{\kappa}^{2}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)}^{2}\leq\|f\|_{L_{\kappa}^{2}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)}^{2}\leq C\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\left\|\mathcal{P}_{j}f\right\|_{L_{\kappa}^{2}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)}^{2}.

•

Sobolev norm:

\|f\|_{H_{\kappa}^{s}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)}\sim\left\|(2^{js}\left\|\mathcal{P}_{j}f\right\|_{L_{\kappa}^{2}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)})\right\|_{\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})}.

Moreover, there exists a constant
C>0
such that

C^{-1}\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}2^{2js}\left\|\mathcal{P}_{j}f\right\|_{L_{\kappa}^{2}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)}^{2}\leq\|f\|_{H_{\kappa}^{s}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)}^{2}\leq C\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}2^{2js}\left\|\mathcal{P}_{j}f\right\|_{L_{\kappa}^{2}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)}^{2}.

Also, in the Dunkl partial frame, all the properties mentioned in Subsections
5.2
and
5.3
holds.

5.6.
Strichartz estimates for higher order Dunkl-Schr̈odinger semigroups

Consider the nonlinear Schrödinger and wave equations

\begin{cases}i\partial_{t}u+(-\Delta_{\kappa})^{\frac{m}{2}}u=F_{p}(u),\\
u(0,x)=f(x),\end{cases}

(5.16)

where
m>0,

(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
and the nonlinearity
F_{p}\in C^{1}
with
p>1
satisfies same condition as in (
1.10
).
If
u(t,x)
is a solution of (
5.16
), then so is

u_{\delta}(t,x)=\delta^{\frac{m}{p-1}}u(\delta^{m}t,\delta x),\quad\delta>0.

In addition, the Sobolev norm of the rescaled initial data
f_{\delta}(x)=u_{\delta}(0,x)
is given in terms of the original
f
as

\|f_{\delta}\|_{\dot{H}_{\kappa}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}=\delta^{\frac{m}{p-1}+s-\frac{N}{2}}\|f\|_{\dot{H}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}.

(5.17)

On the other hand

\|f_{\delta}\|_{L_{\kappa_{1},x}^{2}\dot{H}_{\kappa_{2},y}^{s}}=\delta^{\frac{m}{p-1}+s-\frac{d+2(\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2})}{2}}\|f\|_{L_{\kappa_{1},x}^{2}\dot{H}_{\kappa_{2},y}^{s}}=\delta^{\frac{m}{p-1}+s-\frac{N}{2}}\|f\|_{L_{\kappa_{1},x}^{2}\dot{H}_{\kappa_{2},y}^{s}},

where
\dot{H}_{\kappa_{2},y}^{s}
denotes the partial Dunkl-Sobolev space with respect to the
y
variable.
Therefore, the same range of
p
is guaranteed even when the regularity is imposed only partially, which naturally suggests the possibility of developing a new well-posedness theory under weaker regularity assumptions. The first step in this direction is to study Strichartz estimates for the free Dunkl–Schrödinger propagator with partially regular initial data. The following theorem, which constitutes the main result of this section, addresses this issue.

Theorem 5.3
.

Let
d\geq 1,

1\leq k\leq d,

2\leq\widetilde{r}\leq r<\infty,m\in(0,\infty)\backslash\{1\}
and
2<q\leq\infty
. If
q,r,\tilde{r}
satisfy

\displaystyle\frac{2}{q}\leq(d+2\gamma_{1}-k)\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{r}\right)+(k+2\gamma_{2})\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{\widetilde{r}}\right).

(5.18)

Then we have

\displaystyle\left\|e^{it(-\Delta_{\kappa})^{\frac{m}{2}}}f\right\|_{L_{t}^{q}\left(\mathbb{R};L_{{\kappa_{1},x}}^{r}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d-k};L_{{\kappa_{2},y}}^{\tilde{r}}\left(\mathbb{R}^{k}\right)\right)\right)}\lesssim\|f\|_{\dot{H}_{\kappa}^{s}}^{2},

(5.19)

under the scaling condition

\displaystyle\frac{{{m}}}{q}=-s+(d+2\gamma_{1}-k)\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{r}\right)+(k+2\gamma_{2})\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{\widetilde{r}}\right).

(5.20)

Now, we obtain the following result, which can be applied in various contexts.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
\psi
be any smooth function on
\mathbb{R}
supported in
[\frac{1}{2},2]
and let
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{+}\to\mathbb{R}
be a smooth function.

•

If
\phi^{\prime}(r)\neq 0,

\forall r\in[\frac{1}{2},2]
, for
(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
, we have

\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}E_{\kappa}(ix,\xi)e^{-it\phi(|\xi|)}\psi(|\xi|)h_{\kappa}(\xi)d\xi\right|\lesssim_{\phi,\psi}(1+|(x,t)|)^{-\frac{N-1}{2}}.

•

If
\phi^{\prime}(r),\phi^{\prime\prime}(r)\neq 0,

\forall r\in[\frac{1}{2},2]
, for
(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
, we have

\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}E_{\kappa}(ix,\xi)e^{-it\phi(|\xi|)}\psi(|\xi|)h_{\kappa}(\xi)d\xi\right|\lesssim_{\phi,\psi}(1+|(x,t)|)^{-\frac{N}{2}}.

Proof.

Let us denote

\displaystyle I_{\kappa}(t,x):

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}E_{\kappa}(ix,\xi)e^{-it\phi(|\xi|)}\psi(|\xi|)h_{\kappa}(\xi)d\xi.

Then it is easy to see that

\left|I_{\kappa}(t,x)\right|\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\psi(|\xi|)|h_{\kappa}(\xi)d\xi=\|\psi\|_{L^{1}_{\kappa}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\lesssim_{\psi}1.

Hence, it suffices to prove the following statement:

•

If
\phi^{\prime}(r)\neq 0
for all
r\in[\frac{1}{2},2]
, we have

\left|I_{\kappa}(t,x)\right|\lesssim_{\phi,\psi}|(x,t)|^{-\frac{N-1}{2}};

(5.21)

•

In addition, if
\phi^{\prime\prime}(r)\neq 0
for all
r\in[\frac{1}{2},2]
, we have

\left|I_{\kappa}(t,x)\right|\lesssim_{\phi,\psi}|(x,t)|^{-\frac{N}{2}}.

(5.22)

Now from (
5.4
) and (
5.12
), we see that

\displaystyle I_{\kappa}(t,x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Gamma(N/2)}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-it\phi(r)}\psi(r)\frac{J_{\frac{N-2}{2}}(r|x|)}{\left(r|x|\right)^{\frac{N-2}{2}}}r^{N-1}dr

\displaystyle=C\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(e^{i(-t\phi(r)+r|x|)}h(r|x|)+e^{i(-t\phi(r)-r|x|)}\overline{h(r|x|)}\right)\psi(r)r^{N-1}dr

\displaystyle=C\sum_{\pm}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{i\left(\pm r|x|-t\phi(r)\right)}\psi(r)H(r|x|)r^{N-1}dr.

where
H=h
or
\overline{h}
. We divide our discussion in two cases.

•

Case 1:

|x|\geq 2\max\limits_{r\in[1/2,2]}|\phi^{\prime}(r)||t|
or
|x|\leq\frac{1}{2}\min\limits_{r\in[1/2,2]}|\phi^{\prime}(r)||t|
. In this case, we have

\left|\frac{d}{dr}\left(\pm r|x|-t\phi(r)\right)\right|=\left|\pm|x|-t\phi^{\prime}(r)\right|\gtrsim_{\phi}|(x,t)|.

For any
M\in\mathbb{N}
, by repeated integration by parts
M
-times and (
5.14
), we obtain

\left|\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{i\left(\pm r|x|-t\phi(r)\right)}\psi(r)H(r|x|)r^{N-1}dr\right|\lesssim_{\phi,\psi,M}\frac{1}{|(x,t)|^{M}}.

Therefore

\left|I_{\kappa}(t,x)\right|\lesssim_{\phi,\psi,M}\frac{1}{|(x,t)|^{M}}.

(5.23)

•

Case 2:

\frac{1}{2}\min\limits_{r\in[1/2,2]}|\phi^{\prime}(r)||t|\leq|x|\leq 2\max\limits_{r\in[1/2,2]}|\phi^{\prime}(r)||t|
, i.e.,
|x|\sim|t|
. We see that

I_{\kappa}(t,x)=\mathcal{F}^{-1}_{\kappa}(e^{-it\phi}\psi(|\cdot|))(x).

From the proof of Theorem 1.1 in
[
32
]
, we can get

\left|I_{\kappa}(t,x)\right|=\|\mathcal{F}^{-1}_{\kappa}(e^{-it\phi}\psi(|\cdot|))\|_{L_{\kappa}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\lesssim_{\psi}\frac{1}{|t|^{\frac{N-1}{2}}}\lesssim_{\psi}\frac{1}{|(x,t)|^{\frac{N-1}{2}}}.

(5.24)

Finally, combining (
5.23
) and (
5.24
), we prove (
5.21
). Moreover, if
\phi^{\prime\prime}(r)\neq 0
for all
r\in[\frac{1}{2},2]
, from the proof of Theorem 1.1 in
[
32
]
, using Van der Corput lemma, when
\frac{1}{2}\min\limits_{r\in[1/2,2]}|\phi^{\prime}(r)||t|\leq|x|\leq 2\max\limits_{r\in[1/2,2]}|\phi^{\prime}(r)||t|
, we have

\left|I_{\kappa}(t,x)\right|=\|\mathcal{F}^{-1}_{\kappa}(e^{-it\phi}\psi(|\cdot|))\|_{L_{\kappa}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\lesssim_{\phi,\psi}\frac{1}{|t|^{\frac{N}{2}}}\lesssim_{\phi,\psi}\frac{1}{|(x,t)|^{\frac{N}{2}}}.

(5.25)

Combining (
5.23
) and (
5.25
), we prove (
5.22
) and this completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

As an consequence of above result we get the following lemma, which will serve as a useful tool in estimating the certain integrals in our main result.

Corollary 5.5

(Fractional Schrödinger case)
.

Assume
\alpha>0
. Let
\psi
be any smooth function on
\mathbb{R}
supported in
[\frac{1}{2},2]
. Then, for
(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}

\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}E_{\kappa}(ix,\xi)e^{-it|\xi|^{\alpha}}\psi(|\xi|)h_{\kappa}(\xi)d\xi\right|\lesssim_{\psi}\begin{cases}(1+|(x,t)|)^{-\frac{N}{2}},\alpha\neq 1,\\
(1+|(x,t)|)^{-\frac{N-1}{2}},\alpha=1.\end{cases}

To establish the above result, we first prove the following lemmas, which are crucial for extending localized frequency estimates to the global setting.

Lemma 5.6
.

Let
d\geq 1
,
1\leq k\leq d
and
m\in(0,\infty)\backslash\{1\}
. Assume that
\leq\widetilde{r}\leq r\leq\infty
. Then for
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{d-k}\times\mathbb{R}^{k}
we have

\displaystyle\left\|e^{it(-\Delta_{\kappa})^{\frac{m}{2}}}\mathcal{P}_{0}f\right\|_{L_{\kappa_{1},x}^{r}L_{\kappa_{2},y}^{\tilde{r}}}\lesssim(1+|t|)^{-\beta(r,\widetilde{r})}\|f\|_{L_{\kappa_{1},x}^{r^{\prime}}L_{\kappa_{2},y}^{\tilde{r}^{\prime}}}

(5.26)

where

\beta(r,\widetilde{r})=(d+2\gamma_{1}-k)\Big(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{r}\Big)+(k+2\gamma_{2})\Big(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{\widetilde{r}}\Big).

Proof.

By the Riesz-Thorin interpolation theorem, we only need to obtain (
5.26
) for the three cases:
(a)
r=\widetilde{r}=2
,
(b)
r=\widetilde{r}=\infty
,
(c)
r=\infty
and
\tilde{r}=2
.

The case (a) directly follows from the Plancherel theorem. For the case (b), i.e., when
r=\widetilde{r}=\infty
, considering the Dunkl transform and its inversion formula, we can write

\displaystyle e^{-it\Delta_{\kappa}}P_{0}f(x,y)

\displaystyle=K*_{\kappa}f(x,y),

where the kernel
K
is given by

\displaystyle K(x,y,t)=\frac{1}{{c_{\kappa_{1}}c_{\kappa_{2}}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d-k}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}E_{\kappa}(i(x,y),(\xi,\eta))e^{it(|\xi|^{2}+|\eta|^{2})^{\frac{m}{2}}}\psi(\xi,\eta)h_{\kappa_{1}}(\xi)h_{\kappa_{2}}(\eta)d\eta d\xi.

(5.27)

Using Young’s inequality, we get

\displaystyle\left\|e^{-it\Delta_{\kappa}}\mathcal{P}_{0}f\right\|_{L_{\kappa}^{\infty}}=\left\|K*_{\kappa}f\right\|_{L_{\kappa}^{\infty}}\leq\left\|K\right\|_{L_{\kappa}^{\infty}}\|f\|_{L_{\kappa}^{1}}.

(5.28)

Now, our aim is to estimate the kernel
K
. By Corollary
5.5
, (
5.27
) can be estimated in the following way:

\displaystyle\left|K(x,y,t)\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{{c_{\kappa_{1}}c_{\kappa_{2}}}}\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d-k}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}E_{\kappa}(i(x,y),(\xi,\eta))e^{it(|\xi|^{2}+|\eta|^{2})^{\frac{m}{2}}}\psi(\xi,\eta)h_{\kappa_{1}}(\xi)h_{\kappa_{2}}(\eta)d\eta d\xi\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim(1+|(x,y,t)|)^{-\frac{N}{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim(1+|t|)^{-\frac{N}{2}}.

(5.29)

Then from the inequality (
5.28
), we get

\left\|e^{it(-\Delta_{\kappa})^{\frac{m}{2}}}\mathcal{P}_{0}f\right\|_{L_{\kappa}^{\infty}}=\left\|K*_{\kappa}f\right\|_{L_{\kappa}^{\infty}}\lesssim(1+|t|)^{-\beta(\infty,\infty)}\|f\|_{L_{\kappa}^{1}}

with
\beta(\infty,\infty)=N/2\geq 0
and this concludes part (b).

For the case
(c)
i.e., when
r=\infty
and
\tilde{r}=2
, using Minkowski’s inequality and Plancherel’s theorem with respect to
y
variable, we get

\displaystyle\left\|e^{it(-\Delta_{\kappa})^{\frac{m}{2}}}\mathcal{P}_{0}f\right\|_{L_{\kappa_{1},x}^{\infty}L_{\kappa_{2},y}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\left\|\|K*_{\kappa_{2}}f\|_{L_{\kappa_{2},y}^{2}}\right\|_{L_{\kappa_{1},x}^{\infty}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d-k}}\|K\left(x-x^{\prime},\cdot\right)*_{\kappa_{2},y}f\left(x^{\prime},\cdot\right)\|_{L_{\kappa_{2},y}^{2}}h_{\kappa_{1}}(x^{\prime})dx^{\prime}\right\|_{L_{\kappa_{1},x}^{\infty}}

\displaystyle=\left\|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d-k}}\|\widetilde{K}\left(x-x^{\prime},\cdot\right)\tilde{f}\left(x^{\prime},\cdot\right)\|_{L_{\kappa_{2},\eta}^{2}}h_{\kappa_{1}}(x^{\prime})dx^{\prime}\right\|_{L_{\kappa_{1},x}^{\infty}},

(5.30)

where
\tilde{f}=\mathcal{F}_{{\kappa_{2},y}}(f(x,\cdot))
denotes the Dunkl transform of
f
with respect to the
y\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
variable and similarly
\tilde{K}=\mathcal{F}_{{\kappa_{2},y}}(K(x,\cdot))
, which is given by

\widetilde{K}(x,\eta,t)=\frac{1}{{c_{\kappa_{1}}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d-k}}E_{\kappa}(i(x,y),(\xi,\eta))e^{it(|\xi|^{2}+|\eta|^{2})^{\frac{m}{2}}}\psi(\xi,\eta)h_{\kappa_{1}}(\xi)d\xi.

Again, applying Corollary
5.5
for fixed
|\eta|\leq 2
, we get

\displaystyle\sup_{|\eta|\leq 2}\left|\widetilde{K}(x,\eta,t)\right|\lesssim(1+|(x,t)|)^{-\frac{d+2\gamma_{1}-k}{2}},

(5.31)

where
(d-k)+2\gamma_{1}
is the homogeneous dimension of
\mathbb{R}^{d-k}.

Using the estimate (
5.31
) and Young’s inequality, from (
5.6
), we obtain

\displaystyle\left\|e^{it(-\Delta_{\kappa})^{\frac{m}{2}}}\mathcal{P}_{0}f\right\|_{L_{\kappa_{1},x}^{\infty}L_{\kappa_{2},y}^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d-k}}\|\widetilde{K}\left(x-x^{\prime},\cdot\right)\tilde{f}\left(x^{\prime},\cdot\right)\|_{L_{\kappa_{2},\eta}^{2}}h_{\kappa_{1}}(x^{\prime})dx^{\prime}\right\|_{L_{\kappa_{1},x}^{\infty}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d-k}}(1+|(x-x^{\prime},t)|)^{-\frac{d+2\gamma_{1}-k}{2}}\|\tilde{f}\left(x^{\prime},\cdot\right)\|_{L_{\kappa_{2},\eta}^{2}}h_{\kappa_{1}}(x^{\prime})dx^{\prime}\right\|_{L_{\kappa_{1},x}^{\infty}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\|(1+|(\cdot,t)|)^{-\frac{d+2\gamma_{1}-k}{2}}*_{\kappa,x}\|\tilde{f}\|_{L_{\kappa_{2},\eta}^{2}}\right\|_{L_{\kappa_{1},x}^{\infty}}

\displaystyle\lesssim(1+|t|)^{-\frac{d+2\gamma_{1}-k}{2}}\left\|\|\tilde{f}\|_{L_{\kappa_{2},\eta}^{2}}\right\|_{L_{\kappa_{1},x}^{1}}

\displaystyle\leq(1+|t|)^{-\frac{d+2\gamma_{1}-k}{2}}\|f\|_{L_{\kappa_{1},x}^{1}L_{\kappa_{2},y}^{2}}

\displaystyle=(1+|t|)^{-\beta(\infty,2)}\|f\|_{L_{\kappa_{1},x}^{1}L_{\kappa_{2},y}^{2}}

with
\beta(\infty,2)=\frac{d+2\gamma_{1}-k}{2}\geq 0
. This completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

Lemma 5.7
.

Let
d\geq 1,1\leq k\leq d
,
m\in(0,\infty)\backslash\{1\}
, and

\displaystyle\frac{2}{q}\leq(d+2\gamma_{1}-k)\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{r}\right)+(k+2\gamma_{2})\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{\widetilde{r}}\right),\quad 2\leq\widetilde{r}\leq r<\infty,\quad 2\leq q\leq\infty,

then

\displaystyle\left\|e^{it(-\Delta_{\kappa})^{\frac{m}{2}}}\mathcal{P}_{0}f\right\|_{L_{t}^{q}L_{\kappa_{1},x}^{r}L_{\kappa_{2},y}^{\tilde{\tau}}}\lesssim\|f\|_{L_{\kappa}^{2}}.

(5.32)

Proof.

By the standard
TT^{*}
argument, the required estimate (
5.32
) is equivalent to

\left\|\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{i(t-\tau)(-\Delta_{\kappa})^{\frac{m}{2}}}\mathcal{P}_{0}gd\tau\right\|_{L_{t}^{q}L_{\kappa_{1},x}^{r}L_{\kappa_{2},y}^{\tilde{r}}}\lesssim\|g\|_{L_{t}^{q^{\prime}}L_{\kappa_{1},x}^{r^{\prime}}L_{\kappa_{2},y}^{\tilde{r}^{\prime}}}.

Now by the estimate (
5.26
) in Lemma
5.6
, we have

\displaystyle\left\|\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{i(t-\tau)(-\Delta_{\kappa})^{\frac{m}{2}}}\mathcal{P}_{0}gd\tau\right\|_{L_{t}^{q}L_{\kappa_{1},x}^{r}L_{\kappa_{2},y}^{\tilde{r}}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\int_{\mathbb{R}}\|e^{i(t-\tau)(-\Delta_{\kappa})^{\frac{m}{2}}}\mathcal{P}_{0}g\|_{L_{\kappa_{1},x}^{r}L_{\kappa_{2},y}^{\tilde{r}}}d\tau\right\|_{L_{t}^{q}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\|\int_{\mathbb{R}}(1+|t-\tau|)^{-\beta(r,\widetilde{r})}\|g\|_{L_{\kappa_{1},x}^{r^{\prime}}L_{\kappa_{2},y}^{\tilde{r}^{\prime}}}d\tau\right\|_{L_{t}^{q}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\|(1+|\cdot|)^{-\beta(r,\widetilde{r})}*_{t}\|g\|_{L_{\kappa_{1},x}^{r^{\prime}}L_{\kappa_{2},y}^{\tilde{r}^{\prime}}}\right\|_{L_{t}^{q}}.

From the notation,
\frac{2}{q}\leq(d+2\gamma_{1}-k)\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{r}\right)+(k+2\gamma_{2})\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{\widetilde{r}}\right)
reduces to
2/q\leq\beta(r,\tilde{r})
. We now consider two cases.

•

When
2/q<\beta(r,\tilde{r})
and
2\leq q\leq\infty
: Young’s inequality yields

\displaystyle\left\|(1+|\cdot|)^{-\beta(r,\widetilde{r})}*_{t}\|g\|_{L_{\kappa_{1},x}^{r^{\prime}}L_{\kappa_{2},y}^{\widetilde{r}}}\right\|_{L_{t}^{q}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\|(1+|\cdot|)^{-\beta(r,\widetilde{r})}\right\|_{L_{t}^{q}}\|g\|_{L_{t}^{q^{\prime}}L_{\kappa_{1},x}^{r^{\prime}}L_{\kappa_{2},y}^{\widetilde{r}}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|g\|_{L_{t}^{q^{\prime}}L_{\kappa_{1},x}^{r^{\prime}}L_{\kappa_{2},y}^{\widetilde{r}^{\prime}}}.

•

When
2/q=\beta(r,\tilde{r})
with
2<q<\infty
: applying the Hardy-Littlewood-Sobolev inequality for one-dimension, to get

\displaystyle\left\|(1+|\cdot|)^{-\beta(r,\tilde{r})}*_{t}\|g\|_{L_{\kappa_{1},x}^{r^{\prime}}L_{\kappa_{2},y}^{\tilde{r}^{\prime}}}\right\|_{L_{t}^{q}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\||\cdot|^{-\beta(r,\tilde{r})}*_{t}\|g\|_{L_{\kappa_{1},x}^{r^{\prime}}L_{\kappa_{2},y}^{\tilde{r}^{\prime}}}\right\|_{L_{t}^{q}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|g\|_{L_{t}^{q^{\prime}}L_{\kappa_{1},x}^{\tau_{x}^{\prime}}L_{\kappa_{2},y}^{\tilde{r}^{\prime}}}.

•

When
q=\infty
and
\beta(r,\widetilde{r})=0
: this implies that
r=\widetilde{r}=2
. In this case, the required estimate (
5.26
) holds trivially by Plancherel’s theorem and hence completes the proof of the lemma.

∎

Now we are in a position to prove Strichartz estimates for the higher order Dunkl-Schrödinger equation in the partial regularity framework.

Proof of Theorem
5.3
.

Susbtituting
e^{it(-\Delta_{\kappa})^{\frac{m}{2}}}f
into (
5.15
) and using Minkowski inequality along with the that fact that
P_{j}
can commute with
e^{it(-\Delta_{\kappa})^{\frac{m}{2}}}
, we conclude that

\displaystyle\left\|e^{it(-\Delta_{\kappa})^{\frac{m}{2}}}f\right\|_{L_{t}^{q}L_{\kappa_{1},x}^{r}L_{\kappa_{2},y}^{\tilde{r}}}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\|\left(\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\left|\mathcal{P}_{j}e^{it(-\Delta_{\kappa})^{\frac{m}{2}}}f\right|^{2}\right)^{1/2}\right\|_{L_{t}^{q}L_{\kappa_{1},x}^{r}L_{\kappa_{2},y}^{\tilde{r}}}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\left\|e^{it(-\Delta_{\kappa})^{\frac{m}{2}}}\mathcal{P}_{j}f\right\|_{L_{t}^{q}L_{\kappa_{1},x}^{r}L_{\kappa_{2},y}^{\tilde{r}}}^{2}.

(5.33)

Using the Dunkl transform and its inversion formula, we can write

\displaystyle e^{it(-\Delta_{\kappa})^{\frac{m}{2}}}\mathcal{P}_{j}f(x,y)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}E_{\kappa}(i(x,y),(\xi,\eta))e^{it(|\xi|^{2}+|\eta|^{2})^{\frac{m}{2}}}\psi_{j}(\xi,\eta)\mathcal{F}_{\kappa}{f}(\xi,\eta)h_{\kappa_{2}}(\eta)h_{\kappa_{1}}(\xi)d\xi d\eta

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}E_{\kappa}(i(x,y),(\xi,\eta))e^{it(|\xi|^{2}+|\eta|^{2})^{\frac{m}{2}}}\psi(2^{-j}\xi,2^{-j}\eta)\mathcal{F}_{\kappa}{f}(\xi,\eta)h_{\kappa_{2}}(\eta)h_{\kappa_{1}}(\xi)d\xi d\eta.

By the change of variables
2^{-j}\xi\rightarrow\xi
and
2^{-j}\eta\rightarrow\eta
, we get

\displaystyle e^{it(-\Delta_{\kappa})^{\frac{m}{2}}}\mathcal{P}_{j}f(x,y)

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{jN}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}E_{\kappa}(i(2^{j}x,2^{j}y),(\xi,\eta))e^{it(|2^{j}\xi|^{2}+|2^{j}\eta|^{2})^{\frac{m}{2}}}\psi(\xi,\eta)\mathcal{F}_{\kappa}{f}(2^{j}\xi,2^{j}\eta)h_{\kappa_{2}}(2^{j}\eta)h_{\kappa_{1}}(2^{j}\xi)d\xi d\eta

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{jN}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}E_{\kappa}(i(2^{j}x,2^{j}y),(\xi,\eta))e^{i2^{mj}t(|2^{j}\xi|^{2}+|2^{j}\eta|^{2})^{\frac{m}{2}}}\psi(\xi,\eta)\mathcal{F}_{\kappa}{f}(2^{j}\xi,2^{j}\eta)h_{\kappa_{2}}(\eta)h_{\kappa_{1}}(\xi)d\xi d\eta

where
f_{j}(x,y):=f\left(2^{-j}x,2^{-j}y\right)
. Using (
5.32
) and the above scaling estimates, we can write

\displaystyle\left\|e^{it(-\Delta_{\kappa})^{\frac{m}{2}}}\mathcal{P}_{j}f\right\|_{L_{t}^{q}L_{\kappa_{1},x}^{r}L_{\kappa_{2},y}^{\tilde{r}}}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim 2^{j\left(-\frac{{m}}{q}-\frac{d+2\gamma_{1}-k}{r}-\frac{k+2\gamma_{2}}{\tilde{r}}\right)}\left\|e^{it(-\Delta_{\kappa})^{\frac{m}{2}}}\mathcal{P}_{0}f_{j}\right\|_{L_{t}^{q}L_{\kappa_{1},x}^{r}L_{\kappa_{2},y}^{\tilde{r}}}

(5.34)

where in the last line we used the scaling condition (
5.20

P_{j}P_{k}=0
if
|j-k|>1
