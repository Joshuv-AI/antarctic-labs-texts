Disease burden of ATTR amyloidosis based on the SF-36® health survey
Authors: Vaishali Sanchorawala, Alexandra Greatsinger, Andrew Staron, John L. Berk, Lisa M. Mendelson, Tracy Joshi, Natasha J. Burke, Nadia Tabatabaeepour, Jess Smith, James Signorovitch
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Transthyretin (ATTR) amyloidosis is a progressive systemic disease associated with substantial morbidity and mortality. Although health-related quality of life (HRQoL) assessments are increasingly incorporated into clinical trials, data in ATTR amyloidosis remain limited. We conducted a retrospective observational study of patients with confirmed ATTR amyloidosis evaluated at the Boston University Amyloidosis Center between 1992 and 2022. Patients who completed the 36-Item Short Form Health Survey (SF-36®) at baseline were included. Participants were categorized as wild-type ATTR (ATTRwt), V122I-variant ATTR (V122I-ATTRv), or non-V122I-ATTRv. SF-36® domain scores were summarized into norm-based Physical Component Summary (PCS) and Mental Component Summary (MCS) scores (U.S. population mean 50, SD 10).Among 997 patients evaluated, 836 (83.9%) completed the SF-36®. Of these, 407 (48.7%) had ATTRwt, 336 (40.2%) non-V122I-ATTRv, and 93 (11.1%) V122I-ATTRv. Mean age was 66.9 years, ranging from 55.8 years in non-V122I-ATTRv to 75.3 years in ATTRwt. The V122I-ATTRv cohort was predominantly Black (91.4%). PCS scores were markedly reduced across all groups compared with population norms, with the greatest impairment in V122I-ATTRv (33 ± 11), followed by ATTRwt (36 ± 11) and non-V122I-ATTRv (37 ± 13). The most affected domains were role limitations due to physical health and vitality. In contrast, MCS scores were near population averages across all groups (49–51), suggesting preserved mental and social well-being.ATTR amyloidosis is associated with substantial physical HRQoL impairment, particularly in patients with the V122I variant, comparable to chronic heart failure. These findings suggest the importance of routine HRQoL assessment and support the development of amyloidosis-specific patient-reported outcome measures.

Background

Transthyretin (ATTR) amyloidosis is a progressive, systemic disease marked by the extracellular deposition of misfolded transthyretin proteins in the form of amyloid fibrils. These deposits can infiltrate vital organs and tissues, particularly the heart, nerves, and soft tissues, leading to a wide range of clinical manifestations that significantly compromise function and quality of life. ATTR amyloidosis is categorized into two major forms: wild-type (ATTRwt), typically presents in older adults and predominantly manifests as a cardiomyopathic phenotype, and hereditary or variant ATTR (ATTRv), which results from mutations in the transthyretin gene. Among these, the V122I (p.Val142Ile) variant is common in individuals of African ancestry and primarily associated with cardiomyopathy, while other non-V122I variants often present with mixed cardiac and neurological features [1].

Despite improved recognition through advances in imaging and genetic testing, ATTR amyloidosis is often diagnosed late, contributing to high morbidity and mortality [2]. While clinicians are increasingly aware of the physical burden of the disease, there is limited data on how patients perceive its impact on their health and functional well-being. Health-related quality of life (HRQoL) assessments are thus becoming more relevant in both clinical trials and real-world care settings to measure the subjective burden of disease and the effectiveness of treatments.

The 36-Item Short Form Health Survey (SF-36®) is a validated, patient-reported instrument that evaluates HRQoL across eight domains spanning physical, emotional, and social well-being [3]. While this tool has been validated in light chain (AL) amyloidosis, its use in ATTR amyloidosis populations remains underexplored [4–7]. This study seeks to address that gap by analyzing SF-36® data collected from patients with ATTR amyloidosis at a large referral center.

Methods

We performed a retrospective observational study using data from the Boston University Amyloidosis Center, where patients with confirmed ATTR amyloidosis were evaluated between 1992 and 2022. Inclusion required completion of the SF-36® Health Survey at the time of baseline clinical evaluation. Baseline clinical evaluation refers to the patient’s first comprehensive assessment at the Boston University Amyloidosis Center, during which diagnostic confirmation, clinical evaluation, and completion of patient-reported outcome instruments were performed. This evaluation did not necessarily coincide with the initial diagnosis of ATTR amyloidosis, and some patients may have received prior evaluation or treatment before referral. Patients were stratified into three groups based on transthyretin genotype: ATTRwt, V122I-ATTRv, and non-V122I-ATTRv. Demographic and clinical information was collected, including age at diagnosis, sex, race, and educational attainment.

The SF-36® Health Survey includes 36 questions spanning eight health domains: physical functioning, role limitations (both physical and emotional), bodily pain, general health, vitality (i.e., energy/fatigue), social functioning, and mental health. Each domain is scored from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating better health. These are summarized into two standardized scores: the Physical Component Summary (PCS) and the Mental Component Summary (MCS). The SF-36® (SF-36) scoring algorithm was used, applying standard norm-based scoring procedures in which domain and summary scores are standardized to a mean of 50 and standard deviation of 10 based on a U.S. general population reference dataset. For all scales and summary measures, higher scores represent more favorable functioning [8]. Missing responses were handled according to standard SF-36 scoring guidelines, whereby scale scores were calculated if at least half of the items within a domain were completed.

We descriptively compared mean PCS and MCS scores across ATTR subgroups and to general population norms. This analysis was an exploratory and hypothesis-generating analysis, without formal hypothesis testing.

Results

Of 997 patients evaluated for ATTR amyloidosis, 836 (83.9%) had completed the SF-36® Health Survey at baseline and were included in the analysis. Among them, 407 (48.7%) had ATTRwt, 336 (40.2%) had non-V122I-ATTRv, and 93 (11.1%) had V122I-ATTRv amyloidosis. The overall mean age was 66.9 years (Table 1), with the ATTRwt group being the oldest (75.3 years), followed by V122I-ATTRv (70.6 years) and non-V122I-ATTRv (55.8 years). Most patients with V122I-ATTRv amyloidosis were Black (91.4%), whereas > 94% of patients with ATTRwt and non-V122I-ATTRv amyloidosis were White.

Discussion

This study evaluates health-related quality of life (HRQoL) in patients with ATTR amyloidosis using the validated SF-36® Health Survey. Our findings highlight the substantial physical burden of the disease, particularly in those with the V122I variant, who scored lowest across several physical domains. These findings align with clinical experience, as this variant is often associated with more advanced cardiac involvement and worse functional status at diagnosis.

Of note, mean PCS scores in all ATTR subtypes, particularly in V122I-ATTRv (mean PCS score 33), approach those seen in large cohorts of patients with chronic heart failure. In a pooled analysis of 71,025 Medicare beneficiaries with heart failure (54% female, 16% Black), the mean PCS was 29.5 [9]. While this comparison should be interpreted cautiously given differences in study populations and methodology, it provides useful context suggesting that the physical health burden experienced by patients with ATTR amyloidosis may be comparable to that observed in large cohorts of patients with chronic heart failure. The substantial reduction in PCS scores may also reflect the fact that many patients are evaluated at referral centers after a prolonged diagnostic journey. Delayed recognition of ATTR amyloidosis may therefore contribute to the degree of functional impairment observed at the time of initial evaluation. Despite this, MCS scores remained near normative values across all ATTR groups. This pattern mirrors findings in the heart failure literature, where the mean MCS was 47.9 in the same Medicare cohort [9]. MCS scores were closer to population norms, although this finding should be interpreted cautiously. Generic instruments such as the SF-36® may not fully capture disease-specific psychological burdens, including uncertainty regarding prognosis, progressive functional decline, or the impact of chronic symptoms.

The differences observed between subgroups also reflect broader issues of health equity. The V122I-ATTRv cohort, primarily composed of Black patients, reported the most profound deficits in physical HRQoL. This may stem from both genetic predisposition to the disease and structural factors, including delayed diagnosis, reduced access to specialty care, and underrepresentation in research [10].

The ATTR subgroups differed substantially in baseline demographic characteristics, including age, race, and educational attainment. These factors are known to influence health-related quality of life measures and may contribute to differences observed between subgroups independent of disease subtype. Because the present analysis was exploratory and descriptive in nature, we did not perform adjusted analyses to account for these baseline differences, and subgroup comparisons should therefore be interpreted cautiously.

The Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire (KCCQ) has been widely used in ATTR cardiomyopathy clinical trials and provides valuable heart failure–specific insights into physical limitations, symptom burden, and quality of life. However, its relevance may be more limited in patients with ATTR amyloidosis who may have predominant neuropathic manifestations (ATTR-PN), as it was designed primarily to assess cardiomyopathy-related symptoms.

In contrast, the SF-36®, though not disease-specific, is well-validated in AL amyloidosis and provides a broader view of physical and emotional well-being [6]. Our findings support its use as a complementary tool in the care of patients with ATTR amyloidosis, both in clinical practice and research. Future efforts should focus on developing and validating amyloidosis-specific HRQoL instruments that capture the full spectrum of disease-related impairments, including neuropathy, fatigue, and social isolation—factors that may be underrecognized by existing tools like the SF-36® or KCCQ.

Several efforts have recently focused on developing ATTR-specific patient-reported outcome instruments that better capture the multidimensional burden of the disease. These include tools designed to assess symptoms and quality-of-life domains unique to transthyretin amyloidosis, such as neuropathy-related disability, autonomic symptoms, and the functional limitations associated with restrictive cardiomyopathy [11, 12]. Emerging tools like the Transthyretin Amyloidosis – Quality of Life Questionnaire (ATTR–QOL), developed with patient input and multidisciplinary expert consensus, offer promise as an ATTR-specific PRO measure. However, the ATTR–QOL still requires psychometric validation in prospective studies [13].

Our study has several important implications. First, the findings support the routine integration of validated HRQoL instruments into clinical practice, not only to monitor disease burden but also to guide shared decision-making. Second, they highlight the need for targeted interventions to improve function and well-being, especially in vulnerable subgroups like those with the V122I variant. Finally, they emphasize the importance of critically evaluating commonly used outcome measures in amyloidosis trials to ensure they are meaningful, interpretable, and validated for this unique patient population.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, this study includes only patients evaluated at a tertiary referral center who completed the SF-36® at baseline assessment, which introduces potential referral and selection bias. Patients referred to specialty centers may represent a population with more advanced or complex disease, potentially leading to an overestimation of functional impairment. Conversely, patients who were too ill to complete the survey or who died prior to referral would not be captured, introducing potential survivor bias. In addition, the study spans three decades (1992–2022), during which awareness, diagnostic tools, and treatment options for ATTR amyloidosis evolved substantially, raising the possibility of era effects. Another limitation is the lack of standardized objective measures of cardiac disease severity (such as ejection fraction, arrhythmia burden, or other imaging parameters) that could be systematically correlated with patient-reported HRQoL scores. Because this cohort spans three decades, such parameters were not consistently captured in a uniform manner at the time of baseline SF-36® assessment. Prospective studies integrating HRQoL instruments with objective cardiac markers would help clarify the relationship between physiologic disease severity and patient-perceived functional impairment.

Conclusions

ATTR amyloidosis imposes a significant physical HRQoL burden, most notably among patients with the V122I variant, while mental health scores were closer to population norms. Longitudinal studies are needed to track how HRQoL changes over disease course and with treatment. These findings support ongoing efforts to develop and validate amyloidosis-specific patient-reported outcome measures, including emerging ATTR-specific instruments designed to better capture the unique symptom burden and functional limitations of this population.
