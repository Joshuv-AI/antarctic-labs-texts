Circulating 16 micronutrients concentration and risk of abdominal aortic aneurysm: a two-sample mediation Mendelian randomization analysis
Authors: Wenpeng Zhao, Yiliang Hu, Xiya Yan, Zhihan You, Weimin Zhou, Shichen Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAbdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA) is a severe cardiovascular disease involving various genetic and metabolic factors. Micronutrients play critical roles in cardiovascular health, yet their specific impacts on AAA remain unclear. This study aims to explore the effects of micronutrients calcium and zinc on AAA risk mediated by plasma proteins using the two-sample Mendelian Randomization (MR) approach.MethodsUtilizing large-scale GWAS data from the UK Biobank; FinnGen databases and Decode database, we assessed the causal relationships between 16 micronutrients concentration and AAA risk and analyzed potential mechanisms mediated by plasma proteins. Strict statistical methods were used to select genetic instrumental variables, and multivariable MR analyses were conducted to dissect direct and indirect effects.ResultsGenetically predicted calcium and zinc levels showed nominally significant associations with AAA risk (IVW Ca: OR 2.32, 95% CI 1.04–5.18, p = 0.04; IVW Zn: OR 1.24, 95% CI 1.05–1.47, p = 0.01). Proteome-wide MR analyses identified several AAA-associated plasma proteins. Among the zinc-related analyses, inter-alpha-trypsin inhibitor heavy chain H2 (ITIH2) was identified as a partial and preliminary mediating factor for the zinc-AAA association. The mediation analysis suggested a modest mediated proportion through ITIH2 (4.65%, 95% CI 0.701%-8.59%). These findings should be interpreted cautiously because the zinc analysis was based on two instrumental variables and the primary micronutrient screen was exploratory.ConclusionsThis study provides suggestive genetic evidence that calcium and zinc may be associated with AAA risk. In addition, ITIH2 may act as a partial and preliminary mediator of the zinc-AAA association, although the mediated proportion was modest and the evidence requires validation in larger datasets. These results highlight the need for further investigation of micronutrients, plasma proteins, and AAA biology before clinical translation.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13019-026-04593-w.

Introduction

Abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA) represents a life-threatening vascular condition marked by the dilatation of the abdominal aorta, which carries the risk of arterial rupture [1, 2]. The etiology of AAA is multifactorial, encompassing genetic predispositions, metabolic imbalances, and inflammatory processes [3, 4]. Recent advancements in cardiovascular genomics and molecular biology have highlighted the importance of micronutrients, such as metal ions and vitamins, in maintaining the structural integrity and functional homeostasis [5–7].

Micronutrients are vital for cellular signaling, enzymatic regulation, and immunological defenses [8–10]. Empirical research suggests strong correlations between dysregulation of these minerals and cardiovascular pathology [9]. Micronutrients regulate various metabolic and biological pathways by influencing gene expression, which may influence vascular health and the development of AAA [10]. At the same time, micronutrient deficiency is related to vascular dysfunction, which may lead to the degradation and remodeling of vascular wall materials, thus promoting the formation and expansion of AAA. Calcium deficiency can cause vascular smooth muscle cell dysfunction [11], while decreased zinc impairs antioxidative and anti-inflammatory cellular defenses [12, 13]. In addition, abdominal aortic aneurysms (AAA) are primarily attributed to increased expression and activity of matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), which lead to the depletion of medial vascular smooth muscle cells and degradation of the extracellular matrix (ECM). Critically, MMP activity is highly sensitive to external environmental factors, including metal ions, pH value, and temperature, suggesting that metal ion concentration could be a crucial factor in AAA development. However, the causal relationship between micronutrients and AAA remains contentious, and the mechanisms through which they affect AAA pathogenesis require further elucidation.

Mendelian randomization has been increasingly used in cardiovascular research because genetic variants fixed at conception can provide evidence less susceptible to confounding and reverse causation than conventional observational studies. For example, MR approaches have been applied to identify genetic risk factors for atrial fibrillation and to support primary prevention strategies [14]. MR has also been used as a pharmacological tool for genetically proxied drug-target validation, providing in silico evidence for therapeutic efficacy and safety before clinical trials [15]. These applications support the relevance of MR for investigating potential causal factors and molecular pathways in cardiovascular disease.

By integrating comprehensive genetic scrutiny with sophisticated biostatistical approaches, our work not only augments the understanding of micronutrients in cardiovascular pathology but also posits novel molecular targets for AAA prevention and therapy. Additionally, the methodologies and insights gleaned from this study serve as a valuable resource for the genetic epidemiology of other complex diseases.

Methods

Research design

Leveraging large-scale genomic data from Genome-Wide Association Studies (GWAS) [16, 17], this investigation employs Mendelian Randomization (MR) to interrogate the genetic underpinnings of the influence exerted by micronutrients on AAA through plasma proteins, thereby shedding light on their underlying biological functions. This approach mitigates confounders inherent to observational studies, thus furnishing more substantiated causal assertions.

In our study, we utilize publicly available large-scale GWAS data for a two-sample Mendelian Randomization analysis [18] to explore the genetic relationship between micronutrients such as metal ions and vitamins, and AAA. The overall effect of any exposure on the outcome can be decomposed into direct and indirect effects. After adjusting for mediating factors through multivariable MR [19], the direct effects of micronutrients on AAA were obtained. The indirect effects are mediated by plasma proteins, also referred to as mediating effects. We conducted a two-step MR study to investigate whether plasma proteins mediate the effect of micronutrients on AAA. First, we examined the causal effect of micronutrients on the outcome (AAA). Next, we explored the causal effect of plasma proteins on AAA. Then, we further investigated the causal effect of micronutrients on plasma proteins. Finally, by identifying potential mediating factors (plasma proteins) through multivariable MR, we determined the direct effects of exposure on the outcome. The indirect effects (mediating effects) were estimated by subtracting the direct effects from the total effects.

Sources of MR analysis data

The summary statistics data for metal ions and vitamins were sourced from the UK Biobank GWAS [20], which encompasses data from up to 500,000 participants of European ancestry, collected between 2006 and 2010, and includes extensive assessments of various micronutrients (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/gwas/home). Clinical trial number: not applicable.

The summary statistics for AAA GWAS were provided by FinnGen Biobank [21], which includes 353,087 individuals of European ancestry (case/control: 3548/349,539). FinnGen is a large-scale cooperative project aimed at collecting and analyzing genetic and health data from participants of Finnish biobanks (https://www.finngen.fi/en). The summary statistics for 4,907 plasma proteins[22] were obtained from the Decode database (https://www.decode.com/), within a 1 MB range of the cis pQTL transcription start sites (p < 5 × 10^−8, r2 < 0.001). The R2 and F-statistics were calculated for each SNP, and SNPs with an F-statistic greater than 10 were selected to ensure sufficient strength of the genetic instrumental variables and mitigate weak instrument bias. Subsequently, the p-values derived from the Mendelian Randomization (MR) analysis were adjusted using the False Discovery Rate (FDR) method to control false positives resulting from multiple testing. Furthermore, heterogeneity and pleiotropy tests performed to evaluate the consistency and reliability of the results, and SNPs demonstrating significant heterogeneity or pleiotropic effects were excluded. This study conforms to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Our study does not require ethical approval as it uses already published or publicly available GWAS summary data that have been approved by the respective ethical and institutional review boards. The MR and sensitivity analysis between micronutrients and AAA

To identify SNPs linked to various trace nutrients, we utilized resources like the FinnGen database (https://www.finngen.fi/en) and the GWAS catalog, focusing on the most extensive studies available from European populations [24]. When selecting GWAS for specific nutrients, we prioritized studies with the largest number of participants. Additionally, we filtered for SNPs with a minor allele frequency greater than 0.01 from the outcome datasets and removed SNPs associated with potential confounders to reduce confounding effects.

We identified SNPs associated with seven essential minerals (serum calcium, copper, iron, magnesium, phosphorus, toenail selenium, and zinc) and seven vitamins (25-OH Vitamin D, Vtamin A1, B6, B9, B12, C, and E). Specifically for iron status, we selected three SNPs known to affect serum iron, ferritin, transferrin, and their saturation, consistently influencing overall iron status. All GWAS measured the levels of trace nutrients in serum, except for selenium, which was measured in toenails or blood. Due to the lack of GWAS reporting genome-wide significant SNPs, potassium, sodium, and vitamins B1, B2, and K were excluded [25]. The final MR-IVs used are listed in the (Supplementary Table 1).

Mediation analysis and MR analysis

To evaluate the overall effect of micronutrients on AAA, a two-sample MR was conducted. The primary micronutrient-AAA screen was treated as an exploratory analysis and initially evaluated using a nominal p < 0.05 threshold across 16 micronutrient exposures; therefore, associations from this screen were interpreted cautiously rather than as definitive discoveries after stringent correction. For the proteome-wide MR analyses, p-values were adjusted using the False Discovery Rate (FDR) method to reduce false-positive findings across the larger number of plasma-protein tests. The total impact of any exposure on the outcome can be broken down into indirect and direct effects [26]. The indirect effects mediated through plasma proteins were calculated using the formula beta1 x beta2, where beta1 is the MR effect of micronutrients on the mediating factor, and beta2 is the MR effect of the mediating factor on AAA, adjusting for genetically determined micronutrients [27]. We estimated the proportion of mediating effect (E%) using the following formula:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$E(\% ) = \frac{{\sum\nolimits_{k = 1}^{k} \beta 1 \times \beta 2k}}{{\sum\nolimits_{k = 1}^{k} \beta 3 + \beta 1 \times \beta 2k}}$$\end{document}

β3 is the MR effect of micronutrients on AAA adjusted for genetically determined potential mediating factors.

To obtain MR analysis results, inverse-variance weighted (IVW) meta-analysis of each Wald Ratio was performed, although for some micronutrients, the number of available SNPs was insufficient for an IVW test, requiring alternative MR analysis methods [28]. These Wald Ratios are considered the most reliable when there are no signs of directional pleiotropy. Additionally, supplementary methods such as the weighted median method and MR-Egger were employed to substantiate the MR results. In MR-PRESSO analysis, SNPs causing excess heterogeneity beyond expectations were excluded from the causal effect assessment to reduce heterogeneity, and it was used for outlier testing and calibration.

Sensitivity analysis

We analyzed potential pleiotropic effects of selected SNPs as IVs using the MR-Egger intercept when at least three independent instrumental variables were available, because MR-Egger regression cannot validly estimate an intercept with fewer than three IVs [29, 30]. For zinc, only two independent SNPs satisfied the prespecified selection criteria; therefore, MR-Egger, weighted median, weighted mode, and related sensitivity analyses requiring more instruments were not applied to the zinc-AAA analysis. Additionally, Cochran Q was calculated where applicable, but its interpretability is limited when the degrees of freedom are very small, as in the two-SNP zinc analysis. Moreover, we conducted a leave-one-out sensitivity assessment when the number of instruments allowed this analysis. Statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.0.5 and packages such as TwoSampleMR and MR-PRESSO [31]. The threshold for statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 unless otherwise specified.

Results

Selection of instrumental variables (IVs) For micronutrients

Our MR study investigated the causal relationship between metal ions, vitamins, and AAA. We initially identified SNPs associated with the exposure factors (micronutrients) that met the criteria of genome-wide significance (p < 5 × 10^−8, r2 < 0.001, and within 10 Mb). The following SNPs were used as instrumental variables: Calcium (7 SNPs), Copper (2 SNPs), Iron (2 SNPs), Magnesium (6 SNPs), Phosphorus (3 SNPs), Toenail and blood selenium (2 SNPs), Zinc (2 SNPs), Vitamin A1 (retinol) (2 SNPs), Vitamin B6 (1 SNP), Vitamin B9 (folic acid) (2 SNPs), Vitamin B12 (7 SNPs), Vitamin C (9 SNPs), 25-OH vitamin D (6 SNPs), Vitamin E (alpha-tocopherol) (3 SNPs), Glutathione peroxidase 7 levels (3 SNPs), and Ubiquinone (3 SNPs) (Supplementary Table 1). Because zinc had only two eligible IVs, zinc-related sensitivity analyses that require three or more IVs were not performed.

Overall effects of metal ions and vitamins on AAA

After MR analysis, zinc was nominally associated with a 24% increased risk of AAA (IVW: OR = 1.24, 95% CI 1.05–1.47, p = 0.01) (Fig. 1). Genetically predicted calcium was also nominally associated with increased AAA risk (IVW: OR = 2.32, 95% CI 1.04–5.18, p = 0.04), with results generally consistent across available methods (Fig. 2, Supplementary Table 2). Vitamin E (alpha-tocopherol) showed a large point estimate (IVW: OR = 15.22, 95% CI 4.35–53.26, p = 0.01); however, this estimate was based on only three SNPs and had a very wide confidence interval, indicating analytical instability. Therefore, the vitamin E result was considered exploratory and was not emphasized as a robust finding. To further verify the causal relationship between calcium, zinc, and AAA, we conducted a reverse Mendelian Randomization analysis. Using AAA as the exposure factor, SNPs were selected under specified criteria and compared with the outcomes for calcium and zinc. The findings indicated no causal relationship when AAA served as the exposure factor with outcomes for calcium and zinc (p = 0.552; p = 0.422). These results support a unidirectional positive correlation between serum calcium, zinc, and AAA (Supplementary Table 3).

Identification of AAA-related plasma proteins

The current study highlights the relationship between plasma proteins and cardiovascular diseases; however, due to limitations in sample size and technological capabilities, a comprehensive analysis of plasma proteins potentially contributing to abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA) development remains unperformed. Published large-scale Genome-Wide Association Studies (GWAS) provide new opportunities to identify plasma proteins implicated in AAA. We specifically focused on cis-pQTLs, as they are considered to have more direct and specific biological effects on proteins. After screening and harmonizing (p = 5e-8, r2 = 0.001), 1310 plasma proteins were included as exposure factors in the MR analysis for AAA.

The MR analysis between plasma proteins and AAA

Furthermore, after conducting a functional enrichment analysis of 28 plasma proteins associated with AAA outcomes, we found that these proteins are involved in processes such as positive regulation of endocytosis, wound healing, extracellular matrix composition, immune activation, and the inhibition of metal channel activity (Fig. 3B). Intriguingly, existing studies suggest that fluctuations in metal ion concentrations can modify these cellular functions, indicating potential interactions between calcium, zinc, and plasma proteins.

The causal effects of metal ions on plasma proteins

Previous analyses suggested nominal associations of serum zinc and calcium with AAA risk and identified plasma proteins associated with AAA. We therefore examined whether zinc or calcium may influence AAA-related plasma proteins. Among the 28 AAA-associated plasma proteins evaluated, SNP-driven serum zinc was positively associated with ITIH2 (IVW: OR 1.084, 95% CI 1.023–1.148, p = 0.006). In contrast, we did not identify a significant calcium-protein pathway suitable for mediation analysis under the current screening framework. Discussion

In the current field of cardiovascular disease research, exploring how biomarkers interact with genetic factors to influence disease progression has become an increasingly important research direction [32]. Our findings suggested positive ORs for both zinc and calcium, indicating that genetically predicted higher concentrations of these micronutrients may be associated with increased AAA risk. However, these results should be interpreted as suggestive because the primary micronutrient analysis was exploratory and the zinc result was based on only two independent instrumental variables.

Zinc, as an essential trace element, regulates various biological processes, including immune responses and cell apoptosis. Some research showed that zinc can regulate endothelial cell functions, reduce inflammatory responses, and prevent oxidative stress, thereby protecting cardiovascular protection [33, 34]. In addition, zinc appears to significantly reduce various cardiovascular risk factors and improve antioxidant capacity [35, 36]. However, Yanagisawa et al. shows that excessive zinc can lead to increased oxidative stress caused by exogenous superoxide radicals, resulting in increased blood pressure and decreased renal function. Other studies have also reported the same results—serum zinc is positively correlated with blood pressure [37–39].

At the molecular level, zinc may influence AAA development through its direct regulation of MMP activity. MMPs are zinc-dependent endopeptidases whose catalytic activity requires a zinc ion coordinated within the active site [36]. This catalytic zinc ion is essential for substrate binding and peptide bond hydrolysis, while additional structural zinc and calcium ions stabilize the enzyme’s tertiary structure. Alterations in extracellular zinc availability may therefore affect MMP activation efficiency, catalytic kinetics, or substrate specificity. Moreover, excessive zinc may facilitate the activation of latent pro-MMPs by disrupting the cysteine–zinc interaction within the pro-domain (the “cysteine switch” mechanism), thereby enhancing extracellular matrix degradation within the aneurysmal wall [38].

Beyond its direct enzymatic role, zinc may also modulate MMP expression at the transcriptional level by influencing redox-sensitive signaling pathways, including NF-κB and MAPK signaling, which are known regulators of MMP gene expression in macrophages and vascular smooth muscle cells [41]. These combined mechanisms provide a plausible molecular explanation for the observed positive association between elevated zinc levels and AAA risk.

Unlike serum zinc, serum calcium has been identified as a risk factor for vascular diseases in a longitudinal study that spanned 30 years and involved hundreds of thousands of participants. Multiple prospective studies have linked circulating calcium to myocardial infarction, coronary artery disease, stroke incidents, and increased mortality [40–43]. These studies have also observed a significant correlation with the development of aortic calcification and carotid artery plaques [44, 45]. However, until now, no comprehensive analysis has directly examined the relationship between circulating calcium levels and AAA. Our study provides initial genetic evidence supporting a causal role of elevated circulating calcium in AAA susceptibility.

At the molecular level, calcium ions are critical regulators of MMP structure and activity. Calcium-binding sites within MMPs stabilize the enzyme’s tertiary structure and protect it from autolytic degradation. Increased extracellular calcium concentrations may enhance MMP stability and prolong enzymatic activity within the vascular wall. In parallel, calcium signaling regulates intracellular pathways that control MMP synthesis and secretion, particularly through calcium-dependent kinases and transcription factors such as AP-1 and NFAT.

We propose that circulating calcium may contribute to AAA development through two complementary mechanisms: (1) regulation of intracellular calcium homeostasis and calcium-dependent signaling pathways, which influence vascular smooth muscle cell contraction, phenotypic switching, and hemodynamic stress, thereby compromising vascular wall integrity [6, 46, 47]; and (2) modulation of platelet activation through calcium-sensing receptors, promoting intraluminal thrombus formation. Notably, intraluminal thrombi serve as reservoirs for inflammatory cells and proteolytic enzymes, including MMPs, which can further accelerate extracellular matrix degradation and aneurysm expansion.

Taken together, zinc and calcium may act synergistically to regulate MMP activity at multiple levels, including catalytic activation, structural stabilization, transcriptional control, and spatial localization within the aneurysmal wall. This multi-layered regulation highlights how modest alterations in metal ion homeostasis may exert amplified effects on extracellular matrix remodeling and AAA progression.

From a clinical perspective, these findings suggest that micronutrient balance may represent a modifiable factor in AAA risk management. While zinc and calcium are widely consumed as dietary supplements, particularly among older adults, our results raise the possibility that excessive intake may have unintended adverse effects on aneurysm progression. Rather than universal supplementation, maintaining micronutrient levels within an optimal physiological range may be more appropriate for individuals at high risk of AAA. Future clinical studies are warranted to determine whether monitoring circulating zinc and calcium levels could improve risk stratification or guide personalized dietary recommendations in AAA-prone populations.

Moreover, the dual role of zinc in influencing enzyme activity and cellular stress responses may further contribute to AAA development by altering protein expression and secretion profiles within the vascular microenvironment. Our findings regarding the plasma protein ITIH2 suggest that zinc-associated molecular networks may intersect with thrombo-inflammatory pathways relevant to AAA pathophysiology. The presence of hypervariable polymorphisms within the ITIH2 gene [48] implies that genetic variability may modulate its functional role in cardiovascular inflammation and thrombosis [49], Previous studies have demonstrated that plasma proteins such as lipocalins, fibrinogen, and apolipoproteins play critical roles in maintaining vascular integrity, regulating inflammatory mediator transport, and controlling thrombus formation [49–52]. During AAA progression, sustained inflammation is a defining feature, and alterations in plasma protein composition may reflect—and potentially reinforce—local inflammatory and proteolytic activity within the aneurysmal wall [53] [54].

Investigating the interactions between micronutrients, plasma proteins, and proteolytic enzymes across different cardiovascular diseases may provide a more comprehensive understanding of these complex biological networks. Such insights could ultimately facilitate the development of targeted preventative strategies or adjunctive therapeutic approaches aimed at modulating metal ion homeostasis or MMP activity in AAA and related vascular disorders.

Study limitations

Although our study provides novel insights into the potential roles of calcium and zinc in abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA) risk, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the zinc analysis relied on only two independent instrumental variables. This prevented valid use of MR-Egger regression and limited the interpretability of Cochran Q heterogeneity testing. Therefore, the zinc-AAA association and the downstream zinc-ITIH2 mediation result should be interpreted as preliminary and require validation in larger or updated zinc GWAS datasets with more independent instruments. Second, the primary micronutrient-AAA screen involved 16 exposures and was interpreted using nominal statistical significance. Although the proteome-wide analysis used FDR correction, the calcium and zinc associations from the micronutrient screen may be vulnerable to false-positive inflation and should be viewed as hypothesis-generating. Third, the vitamin E estimate showed a very large OR with a wide confidence interval based on a small number of IVs, indicating potential analytical instability; we therefore did not interpret it as robust evidence for a causal effect.

Second, all genome-wide association study (GWAS) datasets used in this analysis were derived from populations of European ancestry. In addition, the exposure, outcome, and proteomic datasets were drawn from different European cohorts, including UK Biobank, FinnGen, and deCODE. Although this design reduces broad ancestry mismatch, differences in linkage disequilibrium structure, allele frequencies, cohort ascertainment, and population-specific genetic architecture may still introduce bias during SNP harmonization and instrumental-variable selection. This cross-cohort integration issue may affect the transferability and precision of causal estimates, particularly for exposures with a limited number of instruments. Future studies incorporating multi-ethnic cohorts and harmonized individual-level datasets are therefore necessary to validate the generalizability and robustness of these associations.

Third, although we applied stringent criteria for instrumental variable selection and conducted multiple sensitivity analyses to minimize bias, Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis relies on several key assumptions, including the absence of horizontal pleiotropy and a linear relationship between exposure and outcome. Violations of these assumptions cannot be completely excluded and may influence the magnitude or direction of the observed causal estimates. In addition, MR analyses are less well suited to capturing nonlinear or threshold effects, which may be particularly relevant for micronutrients such as calcium and zinc, where both deficiency and excess may exert adverse biological effects.

Third, our study focuses on genetically predicted circulating levels of calcium and zinc, which may not fully reflect short-term fluctuations or long-term dietary intake patterns observed in clinical practice. As a result, direct translation of these findings into specific dietary recommendations or supplementation strategies is not yet warranted. Prospective cohort studies and interventional clinical trials are needed to determine whether modifying micronutrient intake or circulating levels can meaningfully influence AAA development or progression in at-risk populations.

Fourth, although our findings suggest potential clinical implications—such as the use of circulating micronutrient levels for risk stratification or as adjunctive biomarkers in AAA surveillance—we did not directly evaluate clinical endpoints such as aneurysm growth rate, rupture risk, or response to medical therapy. Future studies integrating genetic data with longitudinal imaging, biochemical measurements, and clinical outcomes will be essential to establish the translational relevance of these associations and to explore whether targeting micronutrient balance or downstream pathways, such as MMP activity, could represent viable therapeutic or preventative strategies.

Finally, the mediation proportion of ITIH2 was modest (4.65%) and its confidence interval approached zero. Thus, most of the putative zinc effect on AAA may operate through other biological pathways not captured in the current analysis. Future proteomic and experimental studies are needed to determine whether ITIH2 has a reproducible mechanistic role in AAA pathogenesis and to identify additional mediators of zinc-related vascular effects.

Conclusion

In this systemic MR study, we found suggestive evidence that genetically predicted calcium and zinc levels may be associated with AAA risk. We also identified ITIH2 as a preliminary and partial mediator of the zinc-AAA association, with a modest mediated proportion. Because the zinc analysis was based on two instrumental variables and the primary micronutrient screen was exploratory, these findings require confirmation in larger GWAS datasets and functional studies before clinical or therapeutic application.
