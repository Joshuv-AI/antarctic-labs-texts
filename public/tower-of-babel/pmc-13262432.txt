Subclinical but significant: A multi-informant comparative study on early socio-emotional difficulties in preterm preschoolers
Authors: Rowena Van den Broeck, Lisa Gistelinck, Bieke Bollen, Els Ortibus, Gunnar Naulaers, Bart Boets
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChildren born preterm face increased risks of neurodevelopmental difficulties, comprised within the “preterm behavioral phenotype”. This study examined behavioral and socio-emotional development in preterm preschoolers using a multi-informant approach to characterize behavioral profiles, mental health outcomes, and their associations with gestational age, birth weight, and early medical experiences.Methods102 five-year-old children (70 preterm, 32 full-term) were assessed using standardized measures, including parent and teacher questionnaires (Child Behavior Checklist, Teacher Report Form, Social Responsiveness Scale, ADHD Questionnaire) and direct clinical observation (Autism Diagnostic Observation Schedule-2). Analyses examined group differences and associations with gestational age, birth weight and neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) parameters, including early procedural pain, respiratory support and kangaroo care.ResultsPreterm children demonstrated a predominantly subclinical phenotype, characterized by elevated difficulties across domains. Marked informant discrepancies emerged: parents reported more internalizing symptoms, attention deficits, social-communication difficulties, and autism-related traits in the preterm group, whereas teacher ratings showed stronger associations with gestational age, birth weight, and early medical procedures. Early NICU experiences were linked to later outcomes: greater exposure to skin-breaking procedures predicted higher reported behavioral problems, whereas more kangaroo care was associated with fewer reported social difficulties.ConclusionsPreterm preschoolers display subtle yet meaningful socio-emotional vulnerabilities that manifest below clinical thresholds, but still warrant continued surveillance. Divergence between parent and teacher perspectives underscores the importance of multi-informant assessment across contexts. Associations between early procedural pain and later socio-emotional outcomes identify a modifiable risk factor, emphasizing the importance of optimized neonatal pain management and minimizing medical intensity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13034-026-01082-9.

Background

Preterm (PT) birth, defined as birth before 37 completed weeks of gestation, affects approximately 11% of births worldwide and represents a significant public health concern [1, 2]. While advances in neonatal care have improved survival rates, PT children continue to face elevated risks for enduring neurodevelopmental challenges. These range from overt physical and neurological impairments to more subtle but persisting difficulties in learning, emotion regulation and social interaction [3–7]. Neurobehavioral outcomes show a strong inverse relationship with gestational age (GA), with earlier birth predicting greater impairment [6, 8, 9]. However, adverse effects are not confined to the most premature infants: moderate-to-late PT children (GA 33-36w) also demonstrate poorer neurodevelopmental outcomes than their full-term (FT) peers [10–12]. Across development, PT populations consistently show elevated behavioral problems [4, 13], particularly in attention and internalizing domains such as social withdrawal and anxiety, while group differences in externalizing problems are typically smaller [4, 14–18]. This constellation of traits has been conceptualized as the “preterm behavioral phenotype” [6], characterized by heightened inattention, hyperactivity, anxiety, emotion dysregulation, and social difficulties. This phenotype is observed across children born before 37 weeks of gestation, but varies considerably in severity, with some experiencing severe difficulties that lead to clinical diagnosis, whereas most PT individuals show subclinical traits. These are mild symptoms that do not warrant diagnosis or treatment, yet may still exert a significant impact on daily functioning.

While symptoms in PT children are often mild and remain below diagnostic thresholds [19], PT birth increases the lifetime risk for psychiatric conditions by approximately 3.5-fold [5, 7, 20, 21]. Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD) is most prevalent, followed by Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) and anxiety disorders [4, 13, 20–24]. However, clinical presentations often differ from those in FT populations [6, 25–28], and while not meeting full diagnostic criteria they may cause significant impact on daily life (e.g., difficulties with picking up on subtle social cues) [6, 29–31]. These outcomes are influenced by multiple factors beyond prematurity itself [18], including structural brain alterations [32–35], early stress exposure induced by intensive neonatal medical care [36–40], disrupted parent-infant bonding, parental mental health [41, 42], altered early parent-child interactions [43, 44], as well as socioeconomic factors and sex, with some studies suggesting greater vulnerability in boys and in socioeconomically disadvantaged families, though findings are not entirely consistent [45–47]. Not only are many factors at play, a recent review reports that cognitive outcomes are very heterogenous across PT populations, but shows that the observed variance is due to true heterogeneity rather than sampling errors [21]. This complexity likely contributes to mixed findings across studies, which vary depending on informant, assessment method, or developmental stage.

The present study examines behavioral and socio-emotional development in a cohort of healthy PT preschoolers, followed since birth and hospitalization at the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) at University Hospital Leuven. While moderate-to-late PT children (GA 33-36w) account for the majority of PT births, they remain underrepresented in developmental follow-ups [4]. Furthermore, the subclinical difficulties that characterize the preterm behavioral phenotype, and their manifestation across different contexts, remain poorly understood, particularly at age five when children transition into formal schooling and behavioral challenges may become more apparent in structured educational settings. In addition to GA, previous evidence suggests that early NICU experiences also contribute to altered development [7, 48, 49]. For example, within the Resilience Cohort, low birth weight (BW) and a high number of skin breaking procedures predicted poorer executive functioning at two years, whereas kangaroo care served as a protective factor [49]. Building on this earlier work, the present study addresses these gaps by examining behavioral and socio-emotional profiles of five-year-old PT children across a broad gestational range using multi-informant assessments comprising parent and teacher reports and direct clinical observation. Specifically, we examined: (1) group differences between PT and FT children in behavioral, socio-emotional and attentional functioning, (2) associations between GA and BW, and socio-emotional outcomes within the PT group, and (3) the influence of both risk factors (e.g. procedural pain, ventilation duration) and potentially protective factors (e.g. parental visits, kangaroo care) on later socio-emotional functioning. While we acknowledge that GA and BW are highly correlated variables, previous research suggests that they might exert distinct influences on developmental outcomes [50–53], making it important to investigate how both GA and BW relate to socio-emotional development. We hypothesized that PT children would demonstrate greater difficulties particularly in attention and internalizing domains, with effects modulated by the degree of prematurity and early NICU experiences.

Materials and methods

Participants

A total of 102 five-year-old children and their biological parent(s) participated. All children were native Dutch speakers, enrolled in Kindergarten, and had at least one native Dutch-speaking parent. The sample consisted of 70 PT children, recruited from the Resilience Cohort [49, 54–56], a prospective longitudinal study following PT children born between 2016 and 2018 at University Hospital Leuven (Belgium) from birth through early childhood. PT participants had a GA below 37 weeks and BW below 2500 g. Of these, 37 were born moderate-to-late preterm (MPT; GA 33-36w), 21 very preterm (VPT; GA 29-32w), and 12 extremely preterm (EPT; GA ≤ 28w). The PT group included several multiples: 15 pairs of twins and 2 triplets. Exclusion criteria were severe cerebral lesions (grade III-IV intraventricular hemorrhage and periventricular leukomalacia) and neurological disorders (i.e., cerebral palsy or epilepsy). The comparison group included 32 FT children, recruited via local schools, and represented a similar age group and sex ratio (Table 1). There were no multiples in the FT group. Inclusion criteria for FT children were GA between 37 and 42 weeks and BW above 2500 g. Exclusion criteria for the FT group were a formal diagnosis of ASD, ADHD or a first-degree relative with either condition. None of the FT children were reported to have stayed at NICU or had any significant perinatal complications warranting invasive medical procedures. Ethical approval was obtained from the Medical Ethical Committee of UZ/KU Leuven (S58807). Parents and teachers provided written informed consent, in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Data for the current study were collected as part of the Resilience Cohort’s 5-year follow-up assessment, which compromised three laboratory sessions. The current analyses focus on data from the third session, which included parent-report questionnaires and assessments of intelligence and autistic traits. Teacher-report questionnaires were obtained during the same period via e-mail. The other lab sessions included frequency-tagging EEG experiments and face-to-face interactions with their biological mother, and will not be discussed further in this report.

Questionnaire data

All questionnaires were collected through REDCap (https://project-redcap.org/). Parent-reported questionnaires were filled in by the children’s biological mother or father, whereas teacher-reported questionnaires were filled in by the Kindergarten teacher. All measures have been validated for use in Flemish or Dutch-speaking populations. The CBCL, TRF, and SRS-2 utilize Flemish norms [57–59], the WPPSI-IV-NL uses Flemish subtest norms [60, 61], and the ADOS-2 employs internationally validated norms [62]. The ADHD-Q uses Dutch norms, widely applied in Flemish settings [63].

Child Behavior Checklist (CBCL)

Emotional and behavioral problems were assessed using the Dutch version of the Child Behavior Checklist (preschool form; CBCL; parent report; NPT = 66, NFT = 32) [64]. The questionnaire is a widely used measure with high test-retest reliability (0.81–0.95) [65]. The 100-item questionnaire assesses behaviors that children might display, rated on a 3-point Likert scale. The CBCL comprises seven empirically based subscales (Emotionally Reactive, Anxious/Depressed, Somatic Complaints, Withdrawn, Sleep Problems, Attention Problems, and Aggressive Behavior) and five DSM-oriented scales (Affective Problems, Anxiety Problems, Pervasive Developmental Problems, Attention Deficit/Hyperactivity Problems, and Oppositional Defiant Problems). Composite T-scores for Internalizing (reflecting inward-directed problems such as anxiety, depression, withdrawal, and somatic complaints; e.g., “Clings to adults or too dependent”), Externalizing (reflecting outward-directed problems such as aggression and rule-breaking behaviors; e.g., “Demands must be met immediately”), and Total Problems were calculated. Higher scores indicate more emotional or behavioral problems. T = 64 was used as clinical threshold, in accordance with CBCL scoring guidelines.

Caregiver-teacher report form (TRF)

Teacher-reported emotional and behavioral problems were assessed using the Dutch version of the Caregiver-Teacher Report Form (TRF; NPT = 62, NFT = 30) [65]. Similar to the CBCL, the TRF comprises 100 items rated on a 3-point Likert scale, but includes six empirically based subscales (excluding Sleep Problems). Composite T-scores for Internalizing, Externalizing, and Total Problems were calculated, with higher scores reflecting greater difficulties. T = 64 was used as clinical threshold, as indicated by the TRF.

ADHD-Questionnaire (ADHD-Q)

Attention problems were identified using the ADHD-questionnaire (ADHD-Q; parent report) (NPT = 64, NFT = 32). The ADHD-Q shows excellent internal consistency with Cronbach’s α of 0.96 for the total scale and 0.89–0.94 for the subscales [63]. Its 18 items reflect hyperactivity (e.g., “Has difficulty remaining seated when required”), impulsivity (e.g., “Engages in physically dangerous activities without considering consequences”), and inattention (e.g., “Is easily distracted”), rated on a 4-point scale. Higher total and subscale scores indicate more ADHD-related difficulties.

Social-responsiveness scale (SRS-2)

Social responsiveness was assessed using the Dutch version of the Social-Responsiveness Scale (SRS-2) using parent (NPT = 64, NFT = 32) and teacher report (NPT = 61, NFT = 30) [59, 66]. The SRS-2 has demonstrated excellent internal consistency (α = 0.94–0.96 across age groups; α = 0.95 in clinical samples) with strong consistency across gender, age, and clinical subgroups, and test-retest reliability ranging from 0.88 to 0.95 [67]. The 65-item questionnaire assesses interpersonal behavior (e.g., “Plays appropriately with peers”), communication (e.g., “Has difficulty following the flow of a conversation”), and restricted and repetitive behaviors (such as stereotyped movements and inflexible adherence to routines, e.g., “Has an unusually narrow range of interests”). Higher total T-scores indicate poorer social responsiveness. The SRS-2 categorizes T-scores as within normal limits (≤ 59), mild (60-65), moderate (66-75), or severe (≥ 76), with scores ≥ 60 suggesting clinically elevated autistic traits.

Behavioral measures

Autism traits

Autism traits were assessed using the Autism Diagnostic Observation Schedule, 2nd Edition (ADOS-2; Module 3), administered by a trained and certified clinical researcher [62]. Before administering the ADOS-2, the clinician verified that all children were verbally fluent in order to complete Module 3. The ADOS-2 demonstrates strong psychometric properties, with high internal consistency for the Social Affect domain (α = 0.87–0.92 across modules) and moderate internal consistency for the Restricted and Repetitive Behavior domain (α = 0.51–0.66), as well as test-retest reliability ranging from 0.68 to 0.92 [68]. Total scores ≥ 8 indicate clinically significant autism symptomatology, scores between 5 and 7 indicate moderate symptomatology, and scores ≤ 4 suggest minimal to no autism traits. The ADOS-2 yields two subscales: Social Affect (SA), reflecting deficits in social-emotional reciprocity, communication, and nonverbal interaction, and Restricted and Repetitive Behaviors (RRB), reflecting stereotyped behaviors, restricted interests, and unusual sensory responses.

Intellectual ability

Intellectual ability was measured using an abbreviated version of the Dutch Wechsler Preschool and Primary Scale of Intelligence (WPPSI-IV-NL) [60, 61, 69, 70]. The Verbal Comprehension Index (VCI) was estimated using the Information and Similarities subtests, Fluid Reasoning Index (FRI) using the Matrix Reasoning and Picture Concepts, and the Working Memory Index using the Picture Memory and Zoo Locations.

Statistical analysis

Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test and visual inspection of histograms. Group comparisons used Welch’s t-test or Mann-Whitney U test, depending on data normality. Sex ratios were compared using chi-square tests. Associations with prematurity indices (GA and BW) and NICU environmental factors (parental visits, ventilation duration, kangaroo care and skin breaking procedures during the first 5 days) were assessed in the PT group using Spearman correlations. Given the multiple comparisons conducted, a dual approach was adopted to control for Type I error inflation. For correlational analyses, Bonferroni correction was applied (α = 0.05/24 = 0.002). For group comparisons, rather than applying stringent correction, which may obscure clinically meaningful patterns in a modestly sized sample, we evaluated the consistency of findings across conceptually related measures (e.g., convergent results across empirical and DSM-oriented subscales). Isolated significant findings lacking corroborating evidence from related measures were interpreted with caution.

ADHD-questionnaire

ADHD-related symptoms assessed through the parent-reported ADHD-Q revealed a nuanced pattern of attentional difficulties. Mann-Whitney U tests showed no significant group difference on the ADHD-Q total score \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\left(W=825,\:p=0.12\right)$$\end{document} (Fig. 2).

Analysis of the ADHD-Q subscales revealed selective attention difficulties in the PT group. PT children scored significantly higher than FT peers on the Attention Deficit subscale \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\left(W=772,\:p=0.05\right)$$\end{document}, indicating greater difficulties with sustained attention, concentration, and task completion. In contrast, no significant group differences emerged for Hyperactivity \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\left(W=942,\:p=0.53\right)$$\end{document} or Impulsivity \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\left(W=797,\:p=0.07\right)$$\end{document}, although PT children showed numerically higher scores on Impulsivity with a trend toward significance. This pattern suggests that PT children’s ADHD-related difficulties are characterized by inattentive rather than hyperactive or impulsive symptoms.

Social-responsiveness scale-2

SRS-2 parent report

Autistic traits assessed through the parent-reported SRS-2 revealed significant social difficulties in the PT group. Mann-Whitney U tests showed a significant group difference on the SRS-2 total score \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\left(W=615,\:p=0.001\right)$$\end{document}, with PT children scoring higher than FT controls, indicating elevated autism-like difficulties (Fig. 3, left). Analysis of the SRS-2 subscales confirmed elevated autistic traits across both domains in the PT group. PT children scored significantly higher than FT peers on the Social Communication and Interaction subscale \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\left(W=672,\:p=0.006\right)$$\end{document}, indicating greater difficulties with social reciprocity, verbal and nonverbal communication. Similarly, PT children showed significantly elevated scores on the Restricted Interests and Repetitive Behaviors subscale \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\left(W=729,\:p=0.02\right)$$\end{document}, suggesting increased stereotyped behaviors and sensory sensitivities. Lastly, sensitivity analyses excluding children of mothers with elevated autism traits (SRS-A scores T≥60) yielded substantively identical results for SRS-2 parent measures (see Supplementary Table S1).

Discussion

Parents and clinicians recognize the classical preterm behavioral phenotype

In this study, we examined behavioral and socio-emotional functioning in five-year-old children born between 23 and 36 weeks of gestation, comparing them with age- and sex-matched FT peers using comprehensive assessment tools and multiple informants. Our findings reveal a subclinical behavioral phenotype in PT children, characterized by elevated socio-emotional difficulties. However, the presence of group differences and their associations with GA and BW varied systematically across informants.

Parents of PT children reported significantly more behavioral problems than parents of FT children, with elevated internalizing but not externalizing problems, more anxious and depressive symptoms, inattention, and autism-related characteristics. Autism-related differences were most pronounced in the social communication domain rather than in restricted and repetitive behaviors, and were further supported by clinical observations, including higher ADOS-2 Social Affect scores. Despite these group differences, 80% of PT children scored within normative ranges, and difficulties typically remained below clinical cutoffs.

These findings align with the “preterm behavioral phenotype” described by Johnson & Marlow (2011) [6], underscoring the heightened vulnerability of PT children even in the absence of overt neurological impairments. Although these subclinical difficulties may not meet diagnostic criteria, they may still impact daily functioning and highlight the need for continuous developmental monitoring.

In contrast, teachers did not corroborate the parent- or clinician-reported difficulties. Teacher ratings revealed no significant group differences in internalizing or externalizing problems, anxiety, depressive symptoms, attentional difficulties, or autism-related traits. This informant discrepancy suggests that behavioral and socio-emotional difficulties in PTs may be context-specific, less observable in structured classroom settings, or more salient to parents due to their awareness of the child’s PT history. Such patterns reinforce the importance of integrating multiple informants when evaluating developmental outcomes in PT populations.

Teachers report fewer but more prematurity-sensitive behavioral problems in preterm children

Turning to associations between behavioral outcomes and neonatal/NICU characteristics, we identified a highly intriguing and seemingly counterintuitive pattern. At the group level, parents of PT children reported substantially higher emotional and behavioral difficulties compared with parents of the FT children. Yet, these parent-reported differences did not covary with prematurity indices such as GA or BW, or with NICU-related stressful or supportive experiences. In contrast, teacher reports did not differentiate between PT and FT groups overall. However, within the PT group, individual variability in teacher-reported difficulties was strongly predicted by individual differences in GA, BW, and NICU experiences. Children who were born more prematurely or who experienced more invasive medical procedures were consistently rated by teachers as showing greater behavioral and socio-emotional difficulties at age five.

How can these findings be reconciled? One explanation is informant bias. Parents typically know their child’s prematurity history and may generalize concerns from the NICU period into their current perceptions, regardless of the child’s actual degree of biological vulnerability [71]. Moreover, parental mental health difficulties - more common following PT birth [72, 73] - can shape perceptions of child behavior [74, 75], potentially contributing to elevated but prematurity-insensitive ratings.

Teachers, on the other hand, are usually unaware of children’s birth histories, and evaluate behavior relative to a classroom norm rather than a medical narrative. This may yield more objective assessments that better reflect biological gradients of vulnerability. Teachers’ ratings may therefore be less inflated by background expectancy but more sensitive to actual prematurity-related neurodevelopmental variability.

A second explanation involves differences in the types of difficulties each informant observes. The elevations reported by parents primarily involved internalizing symptoms and subtle social-communication difficulties, domains consistently underreported by teachers [76, 77]. Emotional symptoms can be more visible at home, where children express distress more freely, whereas teachers must distribute attention across an entire class group and may miss low-intensity or internal experiences [76]. Similarly, for autism-related behaviors, children often show more adaptive or masked functioning in school relative to home [78, 79], which may attenuate teacher-reported difficulties at the group level. It is possible that late PT children succeed more in masking their difficulties than VPT children in the classroom, while such a need for masking is not needed in the safe home environment.

A third explanation concerns context-dependent expression of difficulties. School environments impose more structured, social, and attentional demands than home environments. These demands may amplify subtle regulatory or socio-emotional vulnerabilities linked to prematurity, making them more detectable by teachers when comparing children within the same classroom but less prominent when comparing PT to FT groups overall. Thus, while teachers may rate PT and FT children similarly on average, the degree of prematurity uniquely predicts which PT children struggle most in the school context.

Together, these explanations suggest that parents and teachers provide distinct yet complementary perspectives. Parents identify broad, subclinical socio-emotional difficulties, whereas teachers detect prematurity-related gradients that emerge under classroom demands. Both viewpoints are essential for a comprehensive understanding of behavioral and socio-emotional outcomes following PT birth.

ADHD symptoms: Selective attention difficulties without hyperactive/impulsive characteristics

Although group differences were absent for externalizing problems and overall ADHD symptom scores, PT children showed significantly higher parent-rated scores on the Attention Deficit subscale of the ADHD-Q. This points to specific difficulties with sustained attention rather than a broader hyperactive-impulsive phenotype. Despite ADHD being the most prevalent psychiatric outcome in PT populations [6, 13, 20, 22], we found no overall group differences in ADHD-related symptoms aside from this attentional component. Similar patterns have been documented elsewhere, with several studies reporting no clear increase in ADHD-prevalence in PT samples [15, 80] or suggesting that symptom expression may vary across developmental periods [19]. Many authors have suggested that PT children often display a ‘purer’ or predominantly inattentive ADHD profile, sometimes described as akin to ADD, without the conduct or oppositional problems more typical of combined-type ADHD [6, 26, 28, 81]. This distinct symptom structure may explain why overall group differences were absent in our study.

An alternative explanation is that our PT group, which included children without major neurological injuries or disability, simply did not exhibit elevated ADHD-symptoms compared to FT peers. This interpretation aligns with evidence that increased ADHD prevalence in PT populations is often driven by specific perinatal complications rather than GA per se [28].

Additionally, ADHD symptoms often become more prominent as children transition into increasingly structured and demanding school environments [82, 83]. This developmental shift may help explain why variability in teacher-reported ADHD symptoms, despite the absence of overall group-level differences, was strongly related to GA and BW, and negative NICU experiences.

Early medical experiences have lasting impact on neurodevelopmental outcomes

Within the PT group, early NICU experiences were correlated with teacher-reported behavioral outcomes. While these exploratory correlational findings cannot establish causation, they align with evidence linking early pain and stress exposure to neurodevelopmental outcomes [84, 85]. Specifically, children who underwent a higher number of skin-breaking procedures during their first five days of life were later rated by teachers as showing more behavioral problems and autistic traits. Similarly, longer duration of respiratory support was associated with greater teacher-reported behavioral difficulties, although this association did not remain significant after controlling for GA. Together, these findings suggest that multiple aspects of early medical intensity may exert long-term effects on socio-emotional development.

These findings are consistent with evidence that repeated pain and physiological stress during critical developmental periods can alter nervous system functioning and stress-regulation systems, with downstream consequences for self-regulation and later executive functioning [84, 85]. Interestingly, these associations emerged in teacher, but not parent, ratings. Again, this pattern may reflect the structured and socially demanding nature of classroom settings, where subtle regulatory vulnerabilities become more apparent, and where teachers, through experience with large groups of same-aged peers, may more readily detect small deviations from typical behavior.

Kangaroo care and parental visitation appear to have protective benefits for the socio-emotional development of PT children, as we found a significant correlation between average kangaroo care duration per day and parent-reported social difficulties on the SRS-2. Also the correlation between parental visitation and ADOS-2 scores approached significance, with more parental presence being mildly associated with lower ADOS-2 scores and thus fewer autism-related traits. While this did not reach conventional statistical significance, it may represent a meaningful trend worthy of investigation in larger samples. This aligns with a recent review by Pineda and colleagues (2023) who described kangaroo care as the sensory intervention with the strongest evidence for benefiting infant development, noting its positive effects on physical growth, parent-infant bonding, and reductions in parental stress, anxiety, and depression [86]. Importantly, these benefits may even persist long-term: a longitudinal follow-up study demonstrated lasting social and behavioral protective effects of kangaroo care into adulthood [87]. Our findings add to this evidence, suggesting early skin-to-skin contact may support social development.

Methodological considerations

This study has several notable strengths. We used well-validated instruments and recruited PT and FT groups that were comparable on key demographic variables, increasing the internal validity of group comparisons. The sample also covered the full spectrum of prematurity, from extremely PT (GA ≤ 28w) to the often understudied moderate-to-late PT range (GA 33-36w), enhancing the ecological validity and representativeness of findings. Moreover, the multi-informant design allowed us to capture context-dependent differences in behavior, as evidenced by systematic discrepancies between parent and teacher reports and the modest cross-informant agreement on the CBCL/TRF scales. This design provides a more nuanced understanding of how PT behavioral characteristics manifest across settings.

Several limitations should also be acknowledged. First, the sample size limited our ability to conduct adequately powered subgroup analyses, particularly for GA or BW subgroups. However, our primary research question focused on identifying distinct behavioral phenotypes within the broader PT population as a clinically relevant risk group, rather than examining dose-response effects of GA. We acknowledge this as a limitation. Future studies with larger, sufficiently powered samples should examine whether distinct phenotypes emerge across GA subgroups, particularly to isolate risks unique to the moderate-to-late PT population. Second, the cross-sectional design restricts inferences about developmental trajectories and prevents us from examining how environmental influences between NICU discharge and school entry (e.g., caregiver interactions, early education, peer relationships) interact with prematurity-related vulnerabilities to shape the behavioral outcomes observed at age 5. Third, the exploratory analyses linking early NICU experiences to later outcomes require replication in larger, prospectively designed cohorts. Fourth, groups were matched on age and sex but not on socioeconomic status (SES). Family demographic characteristics (Supplementary Table S4) show some differences in maternal education and family income between groups, which may influence developmental outcomes. Additionally, we were unable to exclude PT children with a potential family history of psychiatric conditions such as ASD and ADHD, whereas this was a stringent exclusion criterion for the FT sample. PT birth itself is associated with increased rates of neurodevelopmental difficulties, making familial vulnerability difficult to disentangle from prematurity-related risk. Excluding PT children with any family history would result in a highly selected, non-representative sample that does not reflect the broader PT population we aimed to characterize. Furthermore, emerging evidence suggests that ADHD symptoms in preterm children may arise through prematurity-specific pathways independent of familial genetic risk [21, 88]. In line with this, a recent study on the “preterm behavioral phenotype” within a PT school-aged population still found elevated socio-communicative difficulties in line with the phenotype while specifically excluding PT children with a formal autism diagnosis [89]. To account for this limitation, we performed an additional sensitivity analysis excluding children of mothers with elevated autism traits, which did not alter our findings. Next, while Bonferroni correction minimized Type I error, this conservative approach may have limited detection of meaningful associations. Future studies with larger samples and multivariate modeling may help identifying additional independent predictors. Furthermore, while the current study provides a comprehensive descriptive characterization of behavioral difficulties across informants and domains, person-centered analyses represent a valuable future direction that can build upon this foundational characterization to identify meaningful subgroups. Finally, despite efforts to recruit a representative cohort, participation bias cannot be fully excluded, particularly among families of children with more pronounced difficulties.

Clinical and research implications

Clinical assessment of PT children should recognize that difficulties are predominantly subclinical yet still meaningful, warranting careful monitoring of functional outcomes even in the absence of formal diagnoses. Notably, parents of moderate-to-late PT children also reported elevated internalizing symptoms compared with parents of FT peers, difficulties that may go unnoticed in classroom settings yet still warrant monitoring. This suggests that structured developmental follow-up should be extended to include children born between 33 and 36 weeks gestational age, rather than restricting routine follow-up to those born ≤ 32 weeks, as is currently standard practice in Flanders, Belgium.

The strong associations between GA, BW, early NICU experiences, and teacher-reported behavioral difficulties suggest that educational settings may provide particularly valuable contexts for detecting subtle regulatory, socio-emotional and attentional challenges. Teachers’ observations, made in highly structured and socially demanding classroom settings, may capture difficulties that are less apparent at home and can therefore play a role in early identification and referral processes.

The associations between early procedural pain and later behavioral and socio-emotional outcomes underscore the critical importance of optimizing NICU practices. These results reinforce the need for comprehensive pain management protocols, minimizing avoidable stressors, and balancing necessary medical interventions with strategies that support neurodevelopmental protection.

Given the specific pattern of socio-communicative difficulties over restricted behaviors, autism screening in PT children should prioritize social domains. Additionally, the dissociation between parent and teacher reports highlights the importance of using multi-informant, multi-method assessment strategies to capture context-specific manifestations of the “preterm behavioral phenotype”.

For research, our results highlight the importance of dimensional approaches that examine within-group variability rather than relying solely on categorical PT versus FT comparisons. Longitudinal studies are needed to track how these subclinical patterns evolve across development, identify sensitive periods for intervention, and clarify which early medical and environmental factors most strongly shape trajectories. Finally, replication in larger and more diverse cohorts will be essential for testing the robustness and generalizability of the associations observed here.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that PT preschoolers present subtle but meaningful behavioral and socio-emotional vulnerabilities, reflected predominantly in subclinical elevations but with clear group differences in internalizing problems, attention difficulties, and autism-related traits. The consistent informant discrepancies, particularly the stronger correlations between GA and BW, and teacher reports, underscore the importance of considering both contextual demands and potential reporting biases when evaluating PT children’s functioning. A novel contribution of this work is the evidence linking early procedural pain exposure to later behavioral and socio-emotional difficulties, highlighting the lasting impact of early NICU experiences and reinforcing the importance of comprehensive pain management and minimal necessary medical intervention in NICU. Overall, these findings emphasize the value of nuanced, multi-informant assessment strategies that recognize the complex interplay between biological vulnerability, environmental context, and informant perspective. Such approaches are essential for accurately characterizing the “preterm behavioral phenotype” and for guiding targeted, developmentally sensitive monitoring and support across early childhood.
