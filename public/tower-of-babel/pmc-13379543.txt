Advancing Lost Productivity Measurement in Economic Evaluations: Evidence Priorities from a Targeted Literature Review and Expert Workshop
Authors: R Brett McQueen, Benjamin Cohen, Caroline Kacergis, Eileen Kennedy, Katherine L Rosettie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundProductivity losses from illness, treatment, and informal caregiving impose a substantial economic burden on patients, caregivers, employers, and society in the United States (US), but measurement remains inconsistent and often omits key domains, such as presenteeism and unpaid non-market work productivity. These gaps limit the integration of productivity into economic evaluations and policy and may lead to undervaluation of interventions that improve health and reduce disease burden.ObjectiveThe aim was to identify evidence priorities and methodological improvements for measuring and valuing productivity in US economic evaluations.MethodsA targeted literature review (TLR) of US-based studies published from 2020 to 2024 was conducted to evaluate measurement of absenteeism, presenteeism, or unpaid non-market work productivity. Definitions, instruments, valuation methods, and reporting practices were extracted. Findings informed a virtual workshop with eight stakeholders, who completed prioritization exercises and participated in facilitated discussions to refine evidence priorities and methodological recommendations.ResultsThirty studies met the TLR inclusion criteria. Absenteeism was assessed in 77%, unpaid non-market work productivity in 67%, and presenteeism in 37%. The Work Productivity and Activity Impairment questionnaire was used in 40%. Only 30% assessed informal caregiving-related productivity losses, 23% included fringe benefits, and 37% used longitudinal designs. In the workshop, stakeholders prioritized four predefined domains for future evidence generation and value assessment: (1) measuring longitudinal fluctuations in productivity by disease severity; (2) valuing unpaid non-market work productivity; (3) measuring presenteeism; and (4) disaggregating productivity by job types and wfork environments. Recommendations included transparent, perspective-specific valuation; capturing total compensation in absenteeism costs; disaggregating time-based and monetary outcomes; and developing instruments suitable for remote, hybrid, and gig work.ConclusionsProductivity losses are an underrecognized component of disease burden in the US. More consistent use of validated instruments, particularly for measuring longitudinal and unpaid non-market work productivity, may improve the reliability and policy relevance of productivity estimates.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s41669-026-00654-x.

Key Points for Decision Makers

Productivity losses from illness, treatment, and informal caregiving are a major burden in the United States, but current studies measure these losses inconsistently and often overlook important domains such as presenteeism and unpaid non-market work productivity.This review and expert workshop highlighted key gaps, including the need for longitudinal tracking by disease severity and treatment response, improved approaches for valuing unpaid non-market work productivity, and better instruments for measuring presenteeism.Standardizing productivity measurement would improve consistency in economic evaluations and better capture the benefits of health interventions.

Background

Lost productivity from illness, treatment, and informal caregiving imposes a significant burden on employees, employers, and society, yet it is often measured incompletely [1–4]. In the United States (US), employer-incurred productivity losses due to worker illness and injury are substantial. For example, a 2019 estimate that included both absenteeism, defined here as missed workdays due to illness or health-related issues, and presenteeism, defined here as attending work while ill and experiencing impaired job performance, placed total employer costs above $500 billion [5]. By comparison, an earlier estimate from 2015 attributed approximately $225.8 billion in employer costs to absenteeism alone [6]. These estimates predate major labor market shifts following the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic and may underestimate the current magnitude and distribution of productivity losses associated with worker illness and informal caregiving demands.

When individuals cannot participate in paid market work, defined here as productivity associated with formal employment and earnings, or unpaid non-market work productivity, defined here as productive activities outside of the labor market that generate economic value but are not compensated through wages, such as informal caregiving, volunteering, or household production [8–10], the resulting productivity losses can have substantial economic consequences for families, employers, and society [1, 3]. Informal caregiving, defined here as unpaid assistance provided by family members or friends to individuals with health-related needs, may increase as diseases progress, often prompting caregivers to reduce paid work hours or exit the workforce entirely, which further amplifies overall productivity losses [1]. When comprehensively measured, productivity losses can exceed direct healthcare costs, particularly in chronic or disabling conditions or those leading to premature death [3].

Measuring lost productivity is challenging because of inconsistent definitions, variable data sources, and the underutilization of standardized measurement instruments. These challenges leave productivity losses both undermeasured and undervalued in health policy and economic evaluations [1, 3, 4], further obscuring the societal burden of disease and weakening incentives to invest in interventions that enhance or preserve productivity. A recent systematic review identified inconsistent utilization of productivity loss measures, underscoring the need for standardized methods to capture the full value of interventions, especially those that improve functioning and reduce disease burden over time [4]. Measurement is particularly inconsistent for presenteeism and unpaid non-market work productivity, which are not routinely captured [1, 4, 8–10]. As a result, the total value of health interventions and the economic losses they prevent are often underestimated [8, 11, 12].

Despite its substantial economic impact, productivity remains inconsistently integrated into US healthcare and policy decisions [1, 4, 8]. Even within the societal perspective, which is intended to capture productivity losses experienced by patients, employers, and informal caregivers, stakeholders in the US, including the Institute for Clinical and Economic Review (ICER), academic researchers, and payers, vary in their methodological approaches [4, 13]. Accounting for productivity can meaningfully shift cost-effectiveness. A recent analysis found that including productivity reduced the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio by up to 27% [14], indicating that traditional evaluations miss significant value. Standardized, comprehensive measurement of lost productivity is therefore critical to accurately reflect the real-world value of health interventions and to inform formulary, reimbursement, and coverage decisions.

Given that productivity losses are inconsistently measured and often omitted or underused in economic evaluations [15], the objective of this analysis was to identify evidence-generation priorities and methodological improvements for the measurement and valuation of productivity losses within the US context.

Methods

We used a two-part approach consisting of a targeted literature review (TLR) and a virtual expert workshop to examine current practices in measuring and valuing lost productivity. Findings from the TLR were used to develop preliminary priorities and methodological recommendations. These initial recommendations were then presented during a virtual expert workshop, where stakeholders discussed and refined them. The workshop discussions were structured to directly inform the study objective by identifying priorities and methodological improvements related to (1) the measurement of productivity losses, defined here as quantifying time associated with reduced participation in paid market and unpaid non-market activities, such as absenteeism, presenteeism, or hours of informal caregiving, and (2) the valuation of productivity losses, defined here as assigning a monetary value to that measured time, such as valuing paid market work using gross employee compensation, including wages, salaries, and employer-paid benefits, or valuing unpaid non-market work using proxy wage rates [16].

Targeted Literature Review

We conducted a TLR to examine how productivity losses are measured and valued in US-based economic evaluations. In this analysis, “costs of productivity losses” refers to the monetized value of productivity losses associated with illness, treatment, or informal caregiving, including absenteeism, presenteeism, employment disruption, and unpaid non-market work productivity.

We searched PubMed for studies published between February 2020 and December 2024 and reviewed grey literature, including policy papers, reports from health economic and value assessment organizations, workplace and human resource data, and survey sources [17–23] (Supplemental Table 1).

The search strategy included terms related to lost productivity, economic evaluation, and relevant populations, such as informal caregivers and individuals in the workforce. Terms were grouped into four main domains: (1) productivity concepts; (2) population groups; (3) methodological elements; and (4) disease or condition areas with a high expected burden of lost productivity (Supplemental Table 2). To be included in the final review, sources had to be US specific, report at least one productivity-related measure, such as absenteeism, presenteeism, work loss, or unpaid non-market work productivity, and address chronic, disabling, or high-burden conditions. A total of 181 studies were identified through the TLR, of which 151 studies were excluded after screening (Supplemental Table 3). Most excluded studies either did not provide quantitative or qualitative assessments of productivity losses in the context of illness, treatment, or informal caregiving or were not conducted in US populations. We extracted information on productivity domains measured, measurement instruments, valuation methods, and whether outcomes were disaggregated or linked to clinical data (Table 1).

Workshop to Refine Recommendations

We convened a 3-h virtual expert workshop on May 5, 2024, with eight stakeholders from multiple sectors, including academia (N = 7), industry (N = 5), and the federal government (N = 1). Sector categories were not mutually exclusive because several stakeholders had experience across multiple sector roles, which accounts for overlap across categories. Among stakeholders, seven specialized in both health economics and policy, and one specialized in health economics alone. Stakeholders were selected based on demonstrated expertise in productivity measurement, valuation, and economic evaluation, including peer-reviewed publications, senior leadership roles in policy or methodological development, and direct experience informing health economic decision-making or guidelines.

Over the past 10 years, the seven stakeholders acknowledged in this article have collectively authored 50 peer-reviewed publications related to productivity loss or societal perspective economic evaluations. Including the stakeholder who did not respond for acknowledgement, these professionals have collectively authored 62 such publications during this period. These counts do not include other thought leadership activities, such as value assessment framework development, policy contributions, or expert panel participation.

All selected stakeholders held master’s or PhD degrees and collectively represented nearly 200 years of professional experience across research, policy, and applied economic evaluation. Stakeholders discussed evidence priorities, valuation strategies, and recommendations for improving data collection related to productivity losses.

After reviewing the TLR findings and identifying evidence gaps, stakeholders engaged in two rounds of an interactive coin-allocation exercise, adapted from established group-based preference elicitation techniques [16]. In the first round, stakeholders prioritized four predefined domains by allocating a fixed number of coins (100 coins per stakeholder) across them: (1) measuring longitudinal fluctuations in productivity by disease severity; (2) valuing unpaid non-market work productivity; (3) measuring presenteeism; and (4) disaggregating productivity by job types and work environments, such as salaried versus hourly work, remote versus onsite roles. This exercise served as a structured elicitation method to capture stakeholder perspectives on priorities for future data collection, measurement tool development, or methodological refinement.

In the second round, stakeholders were asked to prioritize the same four domains based on their perceived importance in future value assessments, regardless of current data availability. This second exercise was intended to identify areas where investment could have the greatest impact on decision-making and emphasized domains requiring methodological innovation or novel measurement instruments, paralleling prior exercises designed to capture difficult-to-quantify value elements [24, 25].

Stakeholders then engaged in facilitated discussions to identify priorities for improving the measurement of productivity losses, including definitional distinctions, data infrastructure needs, and strategies for standardizing future measurement practices and valuation approaches, including related methodological limitations. Discussions were facilitated using a semi-structured guide aligned with the study objectives (Supplemental Table 4). However, the conversation often extended beyond the prepared questions as stakeholders discussed themes that emerged during the voting exercise and highlighted the issues they considered most important. Workshop notes were reviewed by the project team following the session, and key themes and recommendations were identified and refined through iterative review to ensure accurate representation of stakeholder input.

Results

Targeted Literature Review

We identified 30 US-based studies published between 2020 and 2024 that reported productivity-related outcomes, such as absenteeism, presenteeism, or unpaid non-market work productivity, and pertained to chronic, disabling, or high-burden conditions, such as Alzheimer’s disease [1, 11, 12, 26–52].

Table 2 summarizes productivity elements across four perspectives: patients, employers, society, and informal caregivers. Table 3 details the number and proportion of studies assessing specific productivity components, including absenteeism, presenteeism, unpaid non-market work productivity, and inclusion of fringe benefits. Overall, the TLR revealed substantial heterogeneity in how productivity losses were measured, valued, and reported across studies. These findings informed the topics explored during the expert workshop.

Measurement

Although 77% of studies (23 of 30) assessed absenteeism [1, 11, 12, 26–30, 32–34, 37, 40, 43–52] and 37% (11 of 30) assessed presenteeism [27, 29, 30, 33, 41, 43, 45, 47, 48, 50, 52], definitions and measurement approaches varied substantially across studies. Absenteeism was more consistently measured using self-reported time-based metrics (e.g., number of hours or days absent), standardized survey instruments, and employer records or administrative data sources (e.g., disability claims) [1, 11, 12, 26–30, 32–34, 37, 40, 43–52]. Presenteeism was typically measured using self-reported reductions in productivity [26, 33, 48, 50, 52].

A range of instruments were used to measure productivity impact across multiple domains, including absenteeism, presenteeism, and unpaid non-market work productivity. The most frequently cited standardized instrument was the Work Productivity and Activity Impairment (WPAI) questionnaire, which captures absenteeism, presenteeism, and activity impairment related to unpaid non-market work productivity. Despite being a validated and widely used measure, the WPAI appeared in only 40% of studies (12 of 30), indicating limited adoption of standardized measurement approaches [1, 20, 28, 31, 35, 36, 38–41, 43, 47]. Other commonly used instruments included the Work Limitations Questionnaire, which focuses exclusively on presenteeism through on-the-job work limitations; the Health and Work Performance Questionnaire, which measures both absenteeism and presenteeism; and the Health-Related Productivity Loss Inventory, which has the broadest scope, capturing absenteeism, presenteeism, and two aspects of unpaid non-market work productivity: informal caregiver time and household production [43]. In addition to self-reported instruments, several studies also used administrative data sources, such as short-term disability claims, payroll records, and health surveillance systems, as well as national surveys like the Medical Expenditure Panel Survey and the American Productivity Audit [43].

Unpaid non-market work productivity was measured in 67% of studies (20 of 30), most commonly using surveys or other self-reported measures [12, 26, 28, 30–41, 43, 45, 47, 50, 51]. These assessments primarily captured household production and volunteer activities. In contrast, informal caregiving-related impacts on unpaid non-market work productivity were assessed in only 30% of studies (9 of 30) and were assessed less frequently [26, 30, 32, 33, 35, 37, 47, 50, 51].

Only 43% of studies (13 of 30) considered employment status changes or job loss [12, 26, 27, 30, 32, 33, 37, 39, 43, 45, 47, 50, 51]. Disability claims data were included in 53% of studies (16 of 30) [1, 11, 12, 26, 27, 29, 30, 33, 43–47, 49–51]. Longitudinal assessment of productivity in relation to clinical outcomes and disease progression was less common than point-in-time assessments, with longitudinal analyses occurring in only 37% of studies (11 of 30) [11, 26–29, 34, 41, 42, 46, 48, 49].

Valuation

Costs of productivity losses were assessed in 80% of studies (24 of 30), but valuation methods varied substantially [1, 11, 26, 27, 29, 30, 32–35, 37–40, 42–51]. Most studies reported aggregate monetary values rather than disaggregated results, often using average wage data from the US Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) or privately owned claims databases [46, 53]. While aggregate costs of productivity losses predominated, some studies explicitly differentiated productivity losses into short-term disability, long-term disability, and workers' compensation. The remaining 20% (6 of 30) cited methodological and data challenges as reasons for not quantifying productivity loss costs [12, 28, 31, 36, 41, 51]. These included difficulties linking health events with productivity outcomes and insufficient granularity to capture job-type heterogeneity. Only 23% (7 of 30) accounted for fringe benefits, potentially leading to systematic underestimation of employer-incurred productivity costs [32, 43, 44, 46, 47, 49, 52]. Valuation of unpaid non-market work productivity was described less consistently than valuation of paid market work, consistent with the evidence gap identified in both the TLR and workshop.

Absenteeism was commonly calculated by multiplying the number of missed workdays by average daily earnings, derived from self-reported income and national datasets, such as the BLS data. Some studies stratified wages by demographics (e.g., age, gender) or included broader cost components such as fringe benefits, employment loss, or forgone volunteer time [53, 54]. Presenteeism was typically converted into monetary terms by applying partial productivity loss to estimates of average wages [26, 33, 40, 48, 52].

Together, these findings show persistent inconsistency in productivity measurement and valuation. Inconsistent use of standardized instruments, limited consideration of informal caregiving, and omission of fringe benefits all contribute to the underestimation of total productivity losses. These gaps informed the expert workshop.

Workshop

Stakeholder Priorities: Voting Results

The coin allocation exercise asked stakeholders to prioritize four predefined domains related to future evidence generation and value assessment for lost productivity. Measuring longitudinal fluctuations in productivity by disease severity ranked highest in both exercises (evidence generation: 53%; value assessment priorities: 48%), emphasizing the need to capture dynamic, real-world productivity changes over the course of disease. Valuing unpaid non-market work productivity ranked second in both exercises (25%, 28%), highlighting the economic importance of productive activities outside of the labor market. Stakeholders ranked measuring presenteeism third in both exercises (16%, 22%) due to concerns over the lack of standardized methods for measuring and valuing presenteeism relative to absenteeism. Disaggregating productivity by job types and work environments was deprioritized in both exercises (6%, 3%), though stakeholders acknowledged relevance given evolving labor markets (Figs. 1, 2).

Stakeholder Discussions

Among the four predefined domains considered during the coin-allocation exercise, stakeholders identified three priorities for improving the measurement and valuation of productivity losses in economic evaluations. These priorities, ranked by importance during the exercise, were (1) measuring longitudinal fluctuations in productivity by disease severity; (2) valuing unpaid non-market work productivity; and (3) measuring presenteeism (Table 4). Stakeholders emphasized that addressing persistent evidence gaps and methodological limitations across both traditional and non-traditional job environments will be critical to advancing these priorities.

To support the measurement of longitudinal fluctuations in productivity by disease severity, stakeholders emphasized the need to standardize how productivity losses are measured and valued across the disease or treatment course. Recommended instruments should reflect both job and disease characteristics and integrate self-reported and objective data (e.g., clinical milestones). Measurement approaches should reflect evolving labor markets, including remote and hybrid work, gig employment, and occupations influenced by artificial intelligence and automation.

Stakeholders also highlighted improved valuation of unpaid non-market work productivity as a priority, particularly for informal caregivers, retirees, and populations outside formal employment. When disease-specific data are lacking, proxy methods, such as estimating productivity losses as a function of age and quality of life, may provide interim solutions [14].

Stakeholders further discussed challenges related to measuring and valuing presenteeism, which remains difficult to quantify in economic evaluations. While stakeholders agreed that absenteeism should be valued using total compensation, including fringe benefits, opinions diverged regarding the appropriate approach for valuing presenteeism, with some supporting wage-multiplier methods and others favoring empiric adjustment approaches based on observed work patterns and context.

For all priorities, stakeholders recommended transparent, perspective-specific valuation methods to enhance applicability across decision contexts, including reporting disaggregated productivity loss estimates by job type, disease severity, or other relevant characteristics when those factors influence how productivity losses are measured or observed. For example, the economic impact of missed work due to illness may appear differently depending on the structure of work. For hourly workers, whose output is closely tied to hours worked, missed time may directly translate to lost output, whereas for salaried employees, some tasks may be redistributed within a team, potentially masking short-term productivity impacts. Stakeholders also highlighted equity considerations, supporting the use of age-specific wage estimates but opposing adjustments by race or sex to avoid reinforcing systemic inequities. Stakeholders emphasized that disaggregating productivity loss estimates by population characteristics may improve transparency, but should not be used to assign different value to individuals or groups.

Finally, stakeholders recommended presenting results using both friction cost and human capital approaches, as the two methods offer complementary perspectives. However, this methodological debate was not a primary focus of the workshop, as the objective was not to compare frameworks for valuing productivity losses. Overall, the workshop emphasized the need for clear and decision-relevant approaches to measure and value productivity losses to better inform economic evaluations and healthcare decision-making in the US (Table 4).

Discussion

Findings from the TLR and expert workshop show that, despite longstanding recognition of its importance, lost productivity remains inconsistently measured, valued, and integrated into US value assessments. Our findings extend recommendations from the Second Panel on Cost-Effectiveness in Health and Medicine, which emphasized incorporating productivity impacts when adopting a societal perspective [25].

The TLR revealed substantial methodological inconsistencies and limited integration of productivity data across US economic evaluations. Varied definitions and heterogeneous measurement approaches curb standardization in both measurement and valuation. Limited adoption of standardized instruments further reduces comparability and contributes to incomplete assessment of domains such as presenteeism and unpaid non-market work productivity.

Stakeholders reaffirmed the Second Panel’s recommendations, noting that productivity losses, particularly absenteeism and presenteeism, represent major yet frequently omitted components of disease burden [25]. Three priority domains emerged for future evidence generation and methodological improvement in economic evaluations: (1) measuring longitudinal fluctuations in productivity by disease severity; (2) valuing unpaid non-market work productivity; and (3) measuring presenteeism. Persistent methodological limitations across both traditional and non-traditional employment contexts highlight the need for improved measurement and valuation approaches.

Consistent with the priorities identified during the workshop, stakeholders highlighted the need to develop instruments tailored to disease areas, informal caregiving settings, and job types (e.g., remote, hybrid, gig). They also encouraged the use of the impact inventory, a checklist developed by the Second Panel to promote systematic inclusion of non-health consequences in economic evaluations [25]. This tool can help standardize reporting of lost earnings, informal caregiving impacts, and productivity losses. Implementing these recommendations will require measurement and valuation frameworks that reflect real-world labor trends and emerging employment models to ensure relevance in both economic and policy contexts [56].

Consistent with the gaps identified in the TLR and priorities raised during the workshop, productivity losses should be reported as both time-based outcomes and monetized values that include total compensation, especially for absenteeism estimates. Unpaid non-market work productivity, which is often omitted, should be more systematically captured using standardized benchmarks and disaggregated by activity type and relevant population characteristics (e.g., age group, employment status, or caregiving role) when these factors influence the type or amount of unpaid work performed. Reporting such disaggregated estimates can improve transparency and ensure that different forms of unpaid non-market work productivity are more accurately represented when productivity losses are interpreted in decision contexts (e.g., formulary review) [30, 57].

Applying productivity estimates across diseases and subpopulations requires careful consideration. Wage assumptions should prioritize age-based parameters and avoid sex- or race-based differentials to prevent perpetuating bias. Productivity losses may disproportionately affect marginalized populations, underscoring the need to consider both health and economic equity in value frameworks. While distributional cost-effectiveness analyses address health equity, economic equity, such as differences in earnings, career trajectories, and labor force participation, remains underexplored. Incorporating economic equity, together with downstream effects such as changes in tax revenue and labor force participation, may improve the societal relevance of value-based decision-making.

Overall, the workshop findings clarify evidence priorities, highlight methodological gaps, and identify areas where valuation approaches could be improved.

Opportunities for Future Research and Next Steps

Future research should prioritize validated, disease- and context-specific instruments for measuring presenteeism and unpaid non-market work productivity. Longitudinal real-world data linking productivity trends to disease severity and treatment response across domains are needed.

Further work is also needed to refine valuation methods with explicit attention to equity. This includes promoting equitable wage assumptions (e.g., avoiding sex-based wage disaggregation), estimating economic equity impacts of interventions, and systematically incorporating unpaid non-market work productivity to better reflect the experiences of informal caregivers and lower-income populations.

Pilot studies comparing alternative valuation methods could generate more decision-relevant evidence. For example, varying presenteeism assumptions in Prescription Drug Affordability Board reviews could show how productivity inputs affect incremental cost-effectiveness ratios and related decisions. Additional pilots could include longitudinal studies of productivity in hybrid and gig employment settings and development of disease-specific value thresholds that incorporate absenteeism, presenteeism, and unpaid non-market work productivity effects.

Limitations

Several limitations should be noted. First, the TLR relied on a single bibliographic database, which may have limited the comprehensiveness of study identification despite efforts to capture relevant evidence. Second, the TLR covered only the past 5 years and focused on high-burden conditions, potentially excluding less-studied diseases. Third, the expert workshop sample was purposively selected and excluded some stakeholder groups (e.g., payers, employers). Fourth, the review included only studies that explicitly measured productivity, which may underestimate the extent of implementation gaps. In addition, some methodological areas remained unresolved, particularly adjusting willingness-to-pay thresholds, valuing unpaid non-market work productivity (e.g., average versus federal wage rates), and how absenteeism and presenteeism should be weighted. Some stakeholders suggested valuing both equally, whereas others proposed that 1 day of presenteeism equates to half a day of absenteeism. Finally, discussions comparing existing frameworks for valuing lost productivity (e.g., human capital approach versus friction costs) were not included, as they did not align with the workshop objectives.

These limitations highlight the need for additional work to improve measurement, valuation, stakeholder alignment, and practical application of productivity data in economic evaluation. The recommendations may not be generalizable to all populations or decision-making contexts.

Conclusions

These findings highlight the need for more consistent, longitudinal measurement of absenteeism, presenteeism, and unpaid non-market work productivity in US economic evaluations. Future studies should test the feasibility and decision relevance of incorporating these recommendations into clinical and real-world research. Greater methodological consistency, together with explicit attention to equity in valuation, may strengthen the policy relevance of productivity estimates.
