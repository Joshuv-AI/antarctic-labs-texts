Pulmonary diffusion abnormalities in patients with inflammatory bowel disease: A longitudinal study
Authors: Hugo Maisonneuve, Benedicte Caron, Anne Guillaumot, Mathias Poussel, David Sorel, François Chabot, Bruno Ribeiro Baptista, Laurent Peyrin-Biroulet, Ari Chaouat, Simon Valentin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundImpaired diffusing capacity of the lung for carbon monoxide (DLCO) has been frequently described among patients with inflammatory bowel diseases, but longitudinal data of DLCO impairment and its association with IBD activity remain unclear.ObjectivesTo describe the longitudinal report of prevalence and clinical characteristics of chronic DLCO impairment among patients with IBD reporting respiratory symptoms.DesignWe conducted a prospective single-center study including consecutive patients with confirmed IBD and respiratory symptoms.MethodsPatients underwent longitudinal evaluation with chest CT scans and pulmonary function tests.ResultsAmong 75 patients with DLCO measurements, 12 (16%) showed persistently reduced DLCO over a median follow-up of 26.9 [IQR 24.9–30.6] months. Median DLCO was 63.4% of predicted value. All patients had mild or inactive IBD and were on biologics. Imaging abnormalities were mild and did not explain the DLCO reduction.ConclusionsChronic DLCO impairment was observed in 16.0% of IBD patients and no obvious association with clinically active IBD was observed in this selected symptomatic cohort.Keywords: inflammatory bowel diseases, lung physiology, pulmonary function test

Introduction

Respiratory involvement in inflammatory bowel diseases (IBD) has historically been considered a rare extra-intestinal manifestation (EIM), potentially affecting different levels of the bronchial tree from the glottis to the small airways, at any stage of the disease course. 1 While respiratory symptoms (i.e., dyspnea, wheezing and cough) and pulmonary function test (PFTs) abnormalities in patients with IBD are well described, the prevalence and underlying mechanisms of diffusing capacity of the lungs for carbon monoxide (DLCO) impairment remain insufficiently studied and poorly understood, particularly in the era of biologic therapies. DLCO impairment may be observed in more than 25% of IBD patients with respiratory symptoms. 2 However, its exact determinants and clinical implications remain unclear. Few retrospective studies with small sample size have reported reduced DLCO, in patients with IBD.3,4 Some of them suggested a potential association between DLCO impairment and IBD activity, but the literature remain inconsistent, and robust data in the biologic era are lacking, precluding firm conclusions on this point.1,5 Moreover, longitudinal data confirming the persistence DLCO impairment are scarce. We hypothesized that a substantial proportion of patients present with chronic DLCO impairment that cannot be fully explained by established causes of reduced DLCO. The objectives of this study were therefore to determine prevalence and clinical characteristics of chronic DLCO impairment in patients with IBD reporting respiratory symptoms.

Methods and results

A prospective, single-center study was conducted at Nancy University Hospital. All consecutive patients from February 1, 2019, to February 28, 2021 with a confirmed diagnosis of IBD and reported respiratory symptoms (i.e., using a validated questionnaire, the “European Community Respiratory Health Survey questionnaire”) were referred for a pulmonology consultation and systematic PFTs, as previously described. 2 These patients were all symptomatic patients (i.e., dyspnea, cough, sputum, respiratory exacerbations), suspected to have a pulmonary disease. In case of DLCO impairment (i.e., defined by DLCO lower than lower limit of normal (LLN)) at the time of first pulmonology visit, a follow-up consultation was performed at least six months later with repeated PFTs, a thin-slice chest CT scan with contrast injection in the prone position, as well as a transthoracic echocardiography. Spirometry, lung volumes, and DLCO, measured by the single breath technique were performed on a Sensor Medics. Predicted values were obtained using the updated Global Lung Function Initiative reference equations. The study design has been reviewed and approved by the ethical board of the Nancy University Hospital, registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT04225650), and performed in accordance with ethical standards. All patients gave informed written consent prior to inclusion in the study. The reporting of this study conforms to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement. 6 All quantitative variables are shown as the median and interquartile range [IQR, 25-75th percentile] and qualitative variables as number and frequency. Non-parametric tests were used given the sample sizes of less than 30 subjects: Wilcoxon matched-pairs signed rank test for paired groups. All the graphs, calculation and statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software version 10.0 for Mac (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA).

Of the 75 patients who had PFTs with DLCO measurement corrected for hemoglobin level, 20 (26.7 %) had a DLCO below the LLN. Among them, four were lost to follow-up. After a median interval of 26.9 [IQR 24.9–30.6] months between the two pulmonary function tests, impaired DLCO was confirmed in 12 patients (16.0 %) (see flow chart of the study in Figure 1). Characteristics of patients with a chronic impaired DLCO versus patients who had a normal DLCO at the second pulmonary evaluation are presented in Table 1. The median age of these 12 patients was 54.0 [IQR 50.8–60.0] years. Seven of them had Crohn’s disease (CD) and five had ulcerative colitis (UC). Among patients with UC four patients were in remission according to the partial Mayo activity score, and one had mild disease. For patients with CD, according to the Harvey Bradshaw Index, three had a mild activity and four were in remission. Eight patients (66.7 %) were active or former smokers, with a median consumption of 20.0 [IQR 10.5–30.0] pack-years.

The median DLCO measured was 63.4% [IQR 56.5–70.7%] of predicted value. The evolution of DLCO measurements between first and second consultation is depicted on Figure 2. Patients with a confirmed DLCO impairment showed a median evolution of -6.5 [IQR -14.7 to +1.6] % of DLCO, whereas the four remaining patients exhibited an increase of 13.2 [IQR 0.8 – 25.6] %, bringing all of the latter above the LLN.

All twelve patients had respiratory functional signs (dyspnoea n=10, chronic cough n=8, wheezing n=2) with normal pulse oxygen saturation (median of 97.5 [IQR 95.8 – 98.5] %). Two patients had an obstructive ventilatory disorder (with significant tobacco exposure and emphysema), leading to a diagnosis of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and one patient had restrictive ventilatory disorder with no obvious etiology. Regarding the etiologies of DLCO impairment, six patients (50.0 %) had mild emphysema (median quantification at 17.0 [IQR 8.0–18.0] %), one patient had early interstitial involvement, and one patient was diagnosed with pulmonary hypertension with right heart catheterization. None had acute pulmonary embolism.

Median IBD duration was 18.2 [12.1 – 22.9] years for these twelve patients. Seven patients (58.3%) had at least one EIM at first visit (six with articular and one with hepatic disease). IBD treatments included 5-aminosalicylic acid in 8 (66.7 %) patients, methotrexate in 4 (33.3 %), thiopurines in 6 (50.0 %), and biologics in 12 (100.0 %) patients (Table 1).

There were no differences in terms of respiratory symptoms between patients with pulmonology consultation and those without, except for cough, slightly more frequently observed in patients with pulmonology consultation (Supplemental table 1).

Discussion

In this prospective cohort study, we found chronic alteration of DLCO in 16.0 % of IBD patients reporting respiratory symptoms. DLCO impairment, often of uncertain etiology after a comprehensive respiratory assessment, occurred exclusively in patients treated with biologics for IBD.

These findings are consistent with previous studies reporting DLCO impairment between 13% and 44% of patients with IBD both in children and adults.7,8 Some of these studies did not take into account the correction of DLCO with hemoglobin or the Global Lung Function Initiative reference equations, which are now mandatory in order to discuss DLCO impairment using appropriate methodology. By using Global Lung Function Initiative reference values that consider age, sex, height, and ethnicity, the methodology we used provides a more accurate assessment of respiratory abnormalities and helps to avoid false-positive detection. We did not observe any link between disease activity and reduced DLCO, since the majority of patients with chronic DLCO impairment were in remission from their IBD. The data in the literature are highly inconsistent on this subject, based on small patient cohorts and often published before the advent of biologics.9–11 This prospective study provides new information because it is based on a large, recent cohort and does not observe a link between IBD activity and DLCO impairment. However, it is possible that the alteration in DLCO occurs at the time of IBD flare-up and persists secondarily.

Longitudinal data from our patients suggest a change in DLCO over time. In some patients, it improved significantly and returned to normal, which may indicate a transient impairment possibly linked to a respiratory condition (i.e., an undiagnosed lower respiratory tract infection or acute pulmonary embolism) that has been cured, without functional sequelae. Most other patients experienced a slow deterioration in DLCO, without a recurrence of IBD activity, suggesting the possibility of an underlying respiratory disease that is sub-radiological. Respiratory monitoring of these patients must therefore be carried out closely.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to address this issue since the advent of advanced therapies in the treatment of IBD, in patients reporting respiratory symptoms. One of the key points is that all patients with this chronic DLCO impairment received treatment with biologics, whereas 61.2 % of patients of a previous report had been treated in this way. 2 Interestingly, several biologic agents used in our cohort have previously been associated with the development of interstitial lung disease. 12 This would be consistent with our preliminary hypothesis of a sub-radiological respiratory disease, and chest CT follow-up of these patients will be necessary to confirm it. However, because of the small sample size and the lack of an appropriate comparator group, no causal or associative relationship can be inferred from this observation, which should be viewed as exploratory and hypothesis-generating. Moreover, other contributors to reduced DLCO were also present in some patients (i.e., emphysema due to smoking exposure, thromboembolic history, and pulmonary hypertension in one patient). Finally, biologic therapy was highly prevalent at baseline (93.8% of patients with impaired DLCO at baseline), reflecting the highly selected nature of our tertiary referral cohort. Consequently, referral bias and disease severity may have substantially influenced this finding. Radiological quantification of emphysema revealed only mild disease. Thus, the severity of DLCO impairment in our patients seems disproportionate to the mild emphysema or interstitial disease present in seven of them. However, as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease is a frequent cause of DLCO impairment, this diagnosis should be systematically excluded when evaluating patients with reduced DLCO.

The main limitation of our study is that this study did not include a comparator group with patients not treated with biologics, which requires cautious interpretation of the results. A mild reduction in DLCO has been reported in smokers despite otherwise normal pulmonary function tests and no evidence of emphysema. However, this reduction remains markedly smaller than that observed in the present study. On the other hand, the strengths of the study lie in the reproducibility of DLCO which has been well validated in the literature during the follow-up of patients with chronic respiratory disease (such as interstitial lung disease), and in the fact that all measurements were performed on the same device within the same center, limiting possible measurement biases.

In conclusion, in this prospective cohort of IBD patients with respiratory symptoms, persistent DLCO impairment was observed in 16.0 % of cases. This finding should be interpreted in the context of a symptomatic and selected population. Importantly, no obvious association of DLCO impairment with clinically active IBD was observed in this selected symptomatic cohort. While this study is only observational, it might reflect early sub-radiological changes that could be suggestive of early interstitial lung disease, although any potential link with biologic therapy remains uncertain. Further longitudinal studies are needed to clarify its natural history and potential implications for respiratory monitoring and IBD management.
