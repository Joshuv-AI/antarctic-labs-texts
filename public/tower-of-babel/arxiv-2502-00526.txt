Dirichlet’s Lemma in Number Fields
Franz Lemmermeyer

Abstract
. Dirichlet’s Lemma states that every primitive quadratic Dirichlet character χ \chi can be written in the form χ ⁡ ( n ) = ( Δ n ) \chi(n)=(\frac{\Delta}{n}) for a suitable quadratic discriminant Δ \Delta . In this article we define a group, the separant class group SCl ​ ( F ) \mbox{\rm SCl}(F) , that measures the extent to which Dirichlet’s Lemma fails in general number fields F F . As an application we will show that over fields with trivial separant groups, genus theory of quadratic extensions can be made as explicit as over the rationals.



In the first part of this article we will introduce the separant class
group of a number field
F
as a finite
2
-group that is trivial if
and only if every quadratic Dirichlet character can be realized as a
Kronecker symbol. The second part is devoted to applications.

1.
Genus Theory of Quadratic Number Fields

We begin by reviewing a few classical results concerning quadratic
number fields, the factorization of discriminants into prime discriminants,
and connections with genus theory.

Let
\Delta
denote the discriminant of a quadratic number field
K
.
Quadratic discriminants form a group
{\operatorname{Disc}\,}
with respect to the following
multiplication: if
\Delta_{1}
and
\Delta_{2}
are two quadratic
discriminants, then
\Delta_{1}*\Delta_{2}=\Delta_{3}
, where
\Delta_{3}

is the discriminant of the extension
{\mathbb{Q}}(\sqrt{\Delta_{1}\Delta_{2}}\,)
.
The neutral element is the discriminant
1
of the trivial
extension
{\mathbb{Q}}/{\mathbb{Q}}
. Observe that
\Delta*\Delta=1

(the group
{\operatorname{Disc}\,}
is an elementary abelian
2
-group) and that,
e.g.,
(-8)*(-4)=8
. If
\Delta_{1}
and
\Delta_{2}
are
coprime, then clearly
\Delta_{1}*\Delta_{2}=\Delta_{1}\Delta_{2}
.

Prime discriminants are discriminants having only one prime factor, namely

•

the discriminants
-4
,
\pm 8
of
{\mathbb{Q}}(\sqrt{-1}\,)
and

{\mathbb{Q}}(\sqrt{\pm 2}\,)
;

•

the discriminant
p
of
{\mathbb{Q}}(\sqrt{p}\,)
for primes
p\equiv 1\bmod 4
;

•

the discriminant
-q
of
{\mathbb{Q}}(\sqrt{-q}\,)

for primes
q\equiv 3\bmod 4
.

It is well known and easy to show that every discriminant
\Delta
can
be written uniquely as a product of prime discriminants:

\Delta=\Delta_{1}\cdots\Delta_{r}\Delta_{r+1}\cdots\Delta_{t},

where we assume that
\Delta_{j}<0
for
j\leq r
and
\Delta_{j}>0
for

j>r
. In this case,

K_{\operatorname{gen}}^{+}={\mathbb{Q}}(\sqrt{\Delta_{1}},\ldots,\sqrt{\Delta_{t}}\,)

is the genus class field of
K
in the strict sense, that is, the
maximal abelian extension (necessarily an elementary abelian

2
-extension) of
{\mathbb{Q}}
that is unramified at all finite places over
K
.
The ordinary genus field
K_{\operatorname{gen}}
of
K
, i.e., the maximal unramified
extension of
K
that is abelian over
{\mathbb{Q}}
, is then given by

K_{\operatorname{gen}}={\mathbb{Q}}(\sqrt{\Delta_{1}\Delta_{2}},\ldots,\sqrt{\Delta_{1}\Delta_{r}},\sqrt{\Delta_{r+1}},\ldots,\sqrt{\Delta_{t}}\,)

if
\Delta>0
(for negative discriminants,
K_{\operatorname{gen}}=K_{\operatorname{gen}}^{+}
).

Example 1.

The quadratic number field
K={\mathbb{Q}}(\sqrt{210}\,)
has discriminant

\Delta=840
, and the factorization of
\Delta
into prime discriminants is

\Delta=8\cdot(-3)\cdot 5\cdot(-7),

hence the genus field of
K
in the usual and in the strict sense are given by

K_{\operatorname{gen}}^{+}={\mathbb{Q}}(\sqrt{2},\sqrt{-3},\sqrt{5},\sqrt{-7}\,)\quad\text{and}\quad K_{\operatorname{gen}}={\mathbb{Q}}(\sqrt{2},\sqrt{5},\sqrt{21}\,).

The genus field
K_{\operatorname{gen}}
in the usual sense is the maximal real subfield
of
K_{\operatorname{gen}}^{+}
, which in our case is

K_{\operatorname{gen}}={\mathbb{Q}}(\sqrt{2},\sqrt{5},\sqrt{21}\,).

A quadratic number field has odd class number (in the usual sense)
if and only if
K_{\operatorname{gen}}=K
. This condition is obviously necessary;
for sufficiency observe that if
K
has even class number, then its
Hilbert
2
-class field can be constructed in central steps, i.e., there
exists a central unramified quadratic extension of
K
, which necessarily
is elementary abelian over
{\mathbb{Q}}
and thus contained in the genus class field
of
K
(for details, see
[
5
]
). The explicit description of the genus
field then immediately implies the following classification of quadratic
number fields with odd class number:

Theorem 1.1
.

The quadratic extensions
{\mathbb{Q}}(\sqrt{m}\,)
with odd class number are

•

{\mathbb{Q}}(\sqrt{-1}\,)
,
{\mathbb{Q}}(\sqrt{-2}\,)
, and
{\mathbb{Q}}(\sqrt{-q}\,)

for primes
q\equiv 3\bmod 4
in the complex quadratic case;

•

{\mathbb{Q}}(\sqrt{p}\,)
for primes
p
and
{\mathbb{Q}}(\sqrt{pq}\,)
,
for primes
p
and
q
, where
p=2
or
p\equiv 3\bmod 4

and
q\equiv 3\bmod 4
.

Genus characters

Given an ideal class
c\in\operatorname{Cl}^{+}(K)
, choose an ideal
{\mathfrak{a}}
coprime to

\Delta
. Then
\chi_{j}(c)=(\frac{\Delta_{j}}{N{\mathfrak{a}}})
defines a quadratic
character on the ideal class group
\operatorname{Cl}^{+}(K)
called a genus character.
The main result concerning genus characters is the

Theorem 1.2

(Principal Genus Theorem)
.

An ideal class
c\in\operatorname{Cl}^{+}(K)
is a square if and only if

\chi_{j}(c)=1
for
j=1,\ldots,t
.

Work by Goldstein
[
6
]

and Sunley
[
12
,
13
]
(see also Davis
[
3
]
) shows that,
for a base field
F
, unique factorization into prime discriminants holds
if and only if
F
is totally real and has class number
1
in the strict
sense. Replacing discriminants by separants we will be able to generalize
these results to fields with trivial separant class group, i.e., to totally
real fields with odd class number in the strict sense. The notion of
separants goes back to the author’s dissertation
[
8
]
, where it was
used to generalize Rédei’s construction of unramified cyclic quartic
extensions.

2.
Dirichlet’s Lemma in
{\mathbb{Q}}

In this article we will study Kronecker symbols
(\frac{\Delta}{p})
, which
describe the splitting of primes in the quadratic number field with
discriminant
\Delta
, and quadratic Dirichlet characters defined modulo
m
:
these are homomorphisms from
({\mathbb{Z}}/m{\mathbb{Z}})^{\times}
to the (multiplicatively written)
group
{\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}}
. We identify Dirichlet characters modulo
m
and modulo
n

if they agree on all integers coprime to
mn
.

A quadratic Dirichlet character defined modulo
m
is called primitive if
it cannot be defined for any proper divisor of
n
. The three nontrivial
quadratic Dirichlet characters defined modulo
8
are

\begin{array}[]{c|rrrr}\raisebox{0.0pt}[11.19443pt][4.30554pt]{\rule{0.0pt}{8.61108pt}}&1&3&5&7\\
\hline\cr\raisebox{0.0pt}[11.19443pt][4.30554pt]{\rule{0.0pt}{8.61108pt}}\chi_{8}&+1&-1&-1&+1\\
\raisebox{0.0pt}[11.19443pt][4.30554pt]{\rule{0.0pt}{8.61108pt}}\chi_{-8}&+1&+1&-1&-1\\
\raisebox{0.0pt}[11.19443pt][4.30554pt]{\rule{0.0pt}{8.61108pt}}\chi_{-4}&+1&-1&+1&-1\end{array}

Their values at positive integers agree with
(\frac{2}{p})
,
(\frac{-2}{p})

and
(\frac{-1}{p})
, respectively. The characters
\chi_{8}
and

\chi_{-8}
are primitive, the character
\chi_{-4}
is not as it is already
defined modulo
4
.

The smallest defining modulus of a character is called its conductor;
for Kronecker characters
\kappa_{a}=(\frac{a}{\cdot})
, the conductor
agrees with the discriminant of the quadratic number field
{\mathbb{Q}}(\sqrt{a}\,)
.

The fact that Kronecker symbols
(\frac{\Delta}{p})
for primes
p>0

only depend on the residue class of
p
modulo
\Delta
is essentially
equivalent to the quadratic reciprocity law. This observation assigns a
primitive quadratic Dirichlet character to each Kronecker symbol, and
Dirichlet’s Lemma states that every primitive quadratic Dirichlet
character arises in this way (see below for a precise statement).

The same construction works for any algebraic number field, but it
turns out that, in general, not every primitive quadratic Dirichlet
character comes from a Kronecker symbol. For measuring this defect
we introduce the separant class group
\mbox{\rm SCl}(F)
of a number field
F
,
and we will determine its structure.

We start by recalling Dirichlet’s Lemma
1
1

1

The name “Dirichlet’s
Lemma” was coined by Cohn
[
2
]
.
, which goes back to Dirichlet’s
proof that there are infinitely many primes in arithmetic progressions.
In this proof, Dirichlet invented the characters that now bear his
name (strictly speaking Dirichlet provided the tools, and Dedekind
came up with the abstract concept of a character when he edited
Dirichlet’s lectures). Dirichlet characters are group homomorphisms

\chi:({\mathbb{Z}}/m{\mathbb{Z}})^{\times}\longrightarrow{\mathbb{C}}^{\times}
. Dirichlet’s main problem was showing
that L-series

L(s,\chi)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\chi(n)n^{-s}

associated to Dirichlet characters
\chi
have the property

(1)

\lim_{s\to 1}L(s,\chi)\neq 0

for all characters
\chi
different from the principal character.
This was not difficult except for quadratic characters
\chi
, i.e.,
characters that attain only the values
+1

and
-1
. Eventually, Dirichlet realized that the limit in (
1
)
could be evaluated directly by showing that every primitive quadratic
Dirichlet character
\chi
has the form
\chi(n)=(\frac{\Delta}{n})

for some quadratic discriminant
\Delta
, which allowed him to show
that the limit in question is a product of nonzero constants and the
class number and the regulator of the quadratic number field
{\mathbb{Q}}(\sqrt{D}\,)
.
Again this is only true cum grano salis, as Dirichlet worked with
quadratic forms instead of fields.

Let us call a character
\kappa
of the form
\kappa(n)=(\frac{\Delta}{n})

a Kronecker character. A Kronecker character
\kappa
is called
modular

if there exists a modulus
N>0
such that
\kappa(n)=\kappa(n+N)
for
all natural numbers
n
. It is easy to see that if
\kappa
is modular with
respect to
N_{1}
and
N_{2}
, then it is modular with respect to

\gcd(N_{1},N_{2})
. The minimal modulus is called the conductor of
\kappa
.

Similarly, a quadratic number field
K
is called modular if
there exists an integer
N>0
such that
K
is contained in the field

{\mathbb{Q}}(\zeta_{N})
of
N
-th roots of unity. Again it is easy to see that if

K
is modular with respect to
N_{1}
and
N_{2}
, then it is modular with
respect to
\gcd(N_{1},N_{2})
. The minimal modulus for
K
is called the
conductor of
K
.

The similarity between these two notions of modularity is not superficial:
the splitting of primes
p
in quadratic number fields
K
with discriminant

\Delta
depends on the Kronecker character
\kappa(p)=(\frac{\Delta}{p})
,
that in cyclotomic fields
{\mathbb{Q}}(\zeta_{N})
on the values
\chi(p)
for certain
Dirichlet characters
\chi
defined modulo
N
. The modularity of
K

can be shown to imply the modularity of
\kappa
, and in fact the
conductor of the Kronecker character
(\frac{\Delta}{\cdot})
is equal to
the conductor
N=|\Delta|
of the corresponding quadratic number field
K
.

Infinite Kronecker characters

Consider the Kronecker symbol
\kappa_{4}(n)=(\frac{-4}{n})
.
Then for all positive integers
n
we have

\kappa_{4}(n)=\begin{cases}+1&\text{ if }n\equiv 1\bmod 4,\\
-1&\text{ if }n\equiv 3\bmod 4.\end{cases}

There is, however, a problem with negative integers: although

-3\equiv 1\bmod 4
, we have
\kappa_{4}(-3)=\kappa_{4}(+3)=+1
.
To get around this problem we introduce an infinite Kronecker
symbol

\kappa_{\infty}(n)=\Big(\frac{\infty}{n}\Big)=\begin{cases}+1&\text{ if }n>0,\\
-1&\text{ if }n<0.\end{cases}

Then
\kappa_{4}(n)\kappa_{\infty}(n)=\chi(n)
for all nonzero integers

n
, where
\chi
is the nontrivial Dirichlet character modulo
4
.

Dirichlet showed that every Dirichlet character is a Kronecker
character; in fact, we even have

Dirichlet’s Lemma.

There is a bijection between primitive quadratic Dirichlet
characters modulo
N
and Kronecker characters with conductor
N
.

The fact that, conversely, Kronecker characters
(\frac{\Delta}{\cdot})

are Dirichlet characters defined modulo
|\Delta|
is equivalent to
Euler’s version of the quadratic reciprocity law (see
[
1
]
,
as well as, for a proof of Dirichlet’s Lemma,
[
10
]
.).

3.
Dirichlet’s Lemma in Number Fields

We now define the groups of Kronecker characters
{\operatorname{Kr}}(F)
and the
group of quadratic Dirichlet characters
{\operatorname{Dir}}(F)
in arbitrary
number fields
F
, observe that each Kronecker character is a Dirichlet
character, and define the separant class group as their quotient group.

Kronecker characters

Let
F
be a number field. Each element
\alpha\in F^{\times}

defines a Kronecker character
\kappa_{\alpha}=(\frac{\alpha}{})
.
We identify two Kronecker characters
\kappa_{\alpha}
and
\kappa_{\beta}

if they have the same values at all elements coprime to
\alpha
and
\beta
;
for example, we identify the Kronecker symbols
(\frac{-1}{\cdot})

and
(\frac{-4}{\cdot})
over
{\mathbb{Q}}
.

We will evaluate Kronecker characters
(\frac{\alpha}{\beta})
only
at integers
\beta
coprime to the relative discriminant
\operatorname{disc}\,(K/F)
,
where
K=F(\sqrt{\alpha}\,)
. For example, the Kronecker symbol

(\frac{3}{m})
is defined only for integers coprime to
6
.

Kronecker characters form a group
{\operatorname{Kr}}(F)
with respect to multiplication.
If
\alpha
is a square,
\kappa_{\alpha}
is the trivial character.
This observation implies that there is a homomorphism

\phi:F^{\times}/F^{\times\,2}\longrightarrow{\operatorname{Kr}}(F)
defined by

\phi(\alpha F^{\times\,2})=\kappa_{\alpha}
.
Different elements in
F^{\times}/F^{\times\,2}
do not necessarily
give rise to different Kronecker symbols; in other words:
\phi

is not necessarily injective. The next proposition determnines the
kernel of
\phi
:

Proposition 3.1
.

We have an exact sequence

\begin{CD}1@>{}>{}>E_{4}/E^{2}@>{\iota}>{}>F^{\times}/F^{\times\,2}@>{\phi}>{}>{\operatorname{Kr}}(F)@>{}>{}>1,\end{CD}

where
E
is the unit group of
{\mathcal{O}}_{F}
and
E_{4}
is the subgroup
of units that are congruent to a square modulo
4
.

Observe that if
\varepsilon\in E_{4}\setminus E^{2}
, then

F(\sqrt{\varepsilon}\,)/F
is a quadratic extension unramified at all the
finite primes; in particular, the class number of
F
in the strict sense
is even when such a unit exists. An explicit example of a unit in

E_{4}\setminus E^{2}
is given by the fundamental unit
\varepsilon=35+6\sqrt{34}

of
{\mathbb{Q}}(\sqrt{34}\,)
; since
2\varepsilon=(6+\sqrt{34}\,)^{2}
we have

F(\sqrt{\varepsilon}\,)=F(\sqrt{2}\,)
, and this is an unramified quadratic
extension of
F
.

Proof.

We only have to show that
\ker\phi=\operatorname{im}\iota
. Assume that

\kappa\in\ker\phi
; then
\kappa=(\frac{\alpha}{})
is the
trivial Kronecker character. Since the conductor of the
Kronecker symbol is the conductor of the quadratic extension

F(\sqrt{\alpha})/F
, this extension is unramified at all finite
primes; but this implies that either
\alpha
is a square and the
extension is trivial, or that
\alpha
is a unit congruent to a
square modulo
4
.
∎

Next we show that Kronecker characters are Dirichlet characters:

Proposition 3.2
.

Each Kronecker character
\kappa_{\alpha}=(\frac{\alpha}{\cdot})

is a primitive quadratic Dirichlet character with conductor

\operatorname{disc}\,(K/F)
, where
K=F(\sqrt{\alpha}\,)
.

Proof.

By Artin’s reciprocity law, the splitting behavior of a prime ideal in
a quadratic extension
K=F(\sqrt{\alpha}\,)
only depends on the coset
of
{\mathfrak{p}}
in the corresponding ideal group, and the conductor of this
group is the relative discriminant of
K/F
. In particular, for any

\beta\gg 0
in
F^{\times}
coprime to
2\alpha
we have

(\frac{\alpha}{\beta})=\chi(\beta)
for
some Dirichlet character with conductor
\operatorname{disc}\,(K/F)
. Since the
conductor is the minimal defining modulus,
\chi
is primitive.
∎

This result will allow us to show that certain Dirichlet characters
are not Kronecker characters: a necessary condition for the existence
if a Kronecker character with conductor
{\mathfrak{f}}
is the existence of
a quadratic extension
K/F
with relative discriminant

\operatorname{disc}\,(K/F)={\mathfrak{f}}
.

4.
Determination of the Separant Class Group

The separant class group
\mbox{\rm SCl}(F)={\operatorname{Dir}}(F)/{\operatorname{Kr}}(F)
of a number field
F

is by definition an elementary abelian
2
-group, and so it is sufficient
to determine its order, or its dimension as an
{\mathbb{F}}_{2}
-vector space. This
is accomplished by our main theorem:

Theorem 4.1
.

There is an isomorphism

\mbox{\rm SCl}(F)\simeq\operatorname{Cl}_{F}\{4\}/\operatorname{Cl}_{F}\{4\}^{2},

where
\operatorname{Cl}_{F}\{4\}
is the ray class group of
F
defined modulo
(4)
.
In particular we have
\#\mbox{\rm SCl}(F)=2^{\rho^{+}+s}
, where

2^{\rho^{+}}=\operatorname{Cl}_{2}^{+}(K)/\operatorname{Cl}_{2}^{+}(K)^{2}
denotes the
2
-part of the
class group of
F
in the strict sense, and
s
the number of pairs
of complex embeddings of
F
. Thus
F
has trivial separant class group
if and only if
F
is totally real with odd class number in the strict
sense.

The fact that
\#\mbox{\rm SCl}(F)=2^{\rho^{+}+s}
was proved in
[
9
]
.

Characters with conductor dividing
(4)

Our proof uses a few facts concerning Kronecker and Dirichlet characters
whose conductors divide
(4)
. Let
{\operatorname{Kr}}_{4}(F)
denote the group of
Kronecker characters with conductor dividing
(4)
,
{\operatorname{Dir}}_{4}(F)

the group of Dirichlet characters defined modulo
(4)
, and

M_{4}=({\mathcal{O}}_{F}/4{\mathcal{O}}_{F})^{\times}
the group of coprime residue classes modulo
4
.
For the proof of Thm.
4.1
we only need the fact that the homomorphism

\iota_{2}:M_{4}/M_{4}^{2}\longrightarrow{\operatorname{Dir}}_{4}(F)
is surjective.

Proposition 4.2
.

There is an isomorphism
\iota_{2}:M_{4}/M_{4}^{2}\longrightarrow{\operatorname{Dir}}_{4}(F)
. Both groups
have order
2^{n}
, where
n=(F:{\mathbb{Q}})
.

Proof.

Quadratic Dirichlet characters defined modulo
4
are homomorphisms
from
M_{4}
to the multiplicatively written group
{\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}}
. Since such
homomorphisms are trivial on squares, they induce homomorphisms

M_{4}/M_{4}^{2}\longrightarrow{\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}}
. Thus
{\operatorname{Dir}}_{4}(F)=\operatorname{Hom}(M_{4}/M_{4}^{2},{\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}})
,
which means that
{\operatorname{Dir}}_{4}(F)
is the dual of
M_{4}/M_{4}^{2}
(for example
as
{\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}}
-vector spaces); in particular,
{\operatorname{Dir}}_{4}(F)\simeq M_{4}/M_{4}^{2}

as abelian groups.

The isomorphism between
M_{4}/M_{4}^{2}
and
{\operatorname{Dir}}_{4}(F)
given above is not
canonical, but depends on the choice of a basis. In this case, however,
there is also a canonical isomorphism that we will now construct (and which
we need for making the diagram commutative).

Let
\alpha
represent a coprime residue class modulo
4
, and let

\kappa_{\alpha}
denote the corresponding Kronecker character.
Write
\kappa_{\alpha}=\chi_{0}\chi_{1}
as the product of a primitive
quadratic Dirichlet character
\chi_{0}
defined modulo
4
and
a Dirichlet character
\chi_{1}
defined modulo an odd ideal. Then set

\iota_{2}(\alpha E^{2})=\chi_{0}
.

The map
\iota_{2}
is well defined: If
\alpha\equiv\beta\bmod 4
,

\kappa_{\alpha}=\chi_{0}\chi_{1}
and
\kappa_{\beta}=\chi_{0}^{\prime}\chi_{1}^{\prime}
,
then
\kappa_{\alpha}\kappa_{\beta}=\kappa_{\alpha\beta}
is defined modulo
an odd ideal since
\alpha/\beta\equiv 1\bmod 4
. Thus
\chi_{0}=\chi_{0}^{\prime}
.

We observe that if
\alpha
is a unit,
then
\iota_{2}(\alpha E^{2})=\kappa_{\varepsilon}
; thus the upper left square of
the diagram is commutative.

We claim that
\iota_{2}
is injective. Assume that

\iota_{2}(\alpha M_{4}^{2})=\iota_{2}(\beta M_{4}^{2})
. Then
\kappa_{\alpha\beta}

has conductor coprime to
2
, hence
\alpha\beta\equiv\xi^{2}\bmod 4
,
which implies that
\alpha
and
\beta
generate the same class in

M_{4}/M_{4}^{2}
.

Since
M_{4}/M_{4}^{2}\simeq({\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}})^{n}
for
n=(F:{\mathbb{Q}})
(see

[
9
, p. 281]
; this follows by sending
\alpha\bmod 2
to

1+2\alpha\bmod 4
), both groups have order
2^{n}
.
∎

Kronecker characters with conductor dividing
(4)

Next we determine the number of Kronecker characters with conductor
dividing
(4)
. Recall that we have introduced the following groups in

[
9
]
:

•

\operatorname{Sel}(F)=\{\alpha\in F^{\times}:(\alpha)={\mathfrak{a}}^{2}\}/F^{\times\,2}

is the group of all nonzero elements of
F
that generate the square of
an ideal;

•

\operatorname{Sel}_{4}(F)=\{\alpha\in F^{\times}:(\alpha,2)=(1),(\alpha)={\mathfrak{a}}^{2},\alpha\equiv\xi^{2}\bmod 4\}/F^{\times\,2}

is the subgroup of
\operatorname{Sel}(F)
of elements with odd norm that
generate the square of an ideal and are congruent to a square modulo
4
.

Proposition 4.3
.

We have an exact sequence

\begin{CD}1@>{}>{}>\operatorname{Sel}_{4}(F)@>{\iota}>{}>\operatorname{Sel}(F)@>{\nu}>{}>{\operatorname{Kr}}_{4}(F)@>{}>{}>1\end{CD}

Proof.

The homomorphism
\iota
is an injection since
\operatorname{Sel}_{4}(F)\subseteq\operatorname{Sel}(F)
.
We set
\nu(\alpha F^{\times\,2})=\kappa_{\alpha}
. Then
\ker\nu

consists of the cosets
\alpha F^{\times\,2}
such that
\kappa_{\alpha}=1
;
this holds if and only if
\alpha\equiv\xi^{2}\bmod 4
, i.e., if

\alpha F^{\times\,2}\in\operatorname{Sel}_{4}(F)
.

Finally assume that
\kappa_{\alpha}\in{\operatorname{Kr}}_{4}(F)
. Then
(\alpha)={\mathfrak{a}}^{2}
,
hence
\kappa_{\alpha}\in\operatorname{im}\nu
.
∎

We know from
[
9
]
that
\#\operatorname{Sel}_{4}(F)=2^{\rho^{+}}
,
where
\rho^{+}
denotes the
2
-rank of
\operatorname{Cl}_{2}^{+}(F)
, and that

\#\operatorname{Sel}(F)=\rho+r+s
, where
\rho
denotes the
2
-rank of
\operatorname{Cl}_{2}(F)
,
and
r
and
2s
denote the number of real and complex embeddings of
F
.

Corollary 4.4
.

We have
\#{\operatorname{Kr}}_{4}(F)=2^{r+s-(\rho^{+}-\rho)}
.

In addition we see

Corollary 4.5
.

Every Dirichlet character defined modulo
4
is represented by
a Kronecker character if and only if
F
is totally real and every
totally positive unit in
E_{F}
is a square.

The groups
{\operatorname{Dir}}_{4}(F)
and
{\operatorname{Kr}}_{4}(F)
have equal order if and only if

n=r+s-(\rho^{+}-\rho)
. Since
n=r+2s
, this is equivalent to

s=-(\rho^{+}-\rho)
. This in turn holds if and only if
s=0
and

\rho^{+}=\rho
, i.e., if
F
is totally real and every totally positive
unit is a square.

Corollary 4.6
.

If
F
has odd class number, then every Kronecker character with
conductor dividing
4
has the form
\kappa_{\varepsilon}
for a unit
\varepsilon\in E_{F}
.

Proof.

If
F
has odd class number, then
\operatorname{Sel}(F)\simeq E/E^{2}
and

5.
Separants

Relative discriminants
\operatorname{disc}\,(K/F)
of quadratic extensions
K/F
of
number fields contain information about the ramified primes. Separants,
which we will define below, contain more information: they are (cosets of)
algebraic numbers whose square roots generate the quadratic extensions
in question. In this section we will define separants for number fields

F
with odd class number, and we will prove their most basic properties.

For quadratic extensions
K/F
, we let
\operatorname{Ram}(K/F)
denote the set
of ramified places of
F
, and
\operatorname{Ram}_{f}(K/F)
its subset of finite
ramified places, i.e., of prime ideals in
F
that are ramified in
K/F
.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
K/F
be a quadratic extension of number fields. If
F
has
odd class number and
K=F(\sqrt{\alpha}\,)
, then there
exists an integer
\delta\in F^{\times}
unique up to squares of units
such that

•

F(\sqrt{\alpha}\,)=F(\sqrt{\delta}\,)
;

•

\operatorname{disc}\,(K/F)^{h}=(\delta)
.

Proof.

Assume that
F
has odd class number
h
, and consider quadratic
extensions
K=F(\sqrt{\alpha}\,)
. The relative discriminant

{\mathfrak{d}}=\operatorname{disc}\,(K/F)
is an ideal, and we know that

4\mu=\operatorname{disc}\,(\sqrt{\mu}\,)={\mathfrak{a}}^{2}{\mathfrak{d}}
for some integral
ideal
{\mathfrak{a}}
. Raising everything to the
h
-th power we get

(4^{h}\mu^{h})=(\alpha^{2}\delta)
, where
{\mathfrak{a}}^{h}=(\alpha)
and

{\mathfrak{d}}^{h}=(\delta)
; moreover we can (and will) choose
\delta

in such a way that
F(\sqrt{\mu}\,)=F(\sqrt{\delta}\,)
.
∎

The coset
{\operatorname{sep}}(K/F)=\delta\cdot E_{F}^{2}
is called the separant of
the quadratic extension
K/F
. Separants form a group
\mbox{\rm Sep}(F)

with respect to the following multiplication: if

K_{1}=F(\sqrt{\alpha}\,)
and
K_{2}=F(\sqrt{\beta}\,)
are
quadratic extensions with separants
\delta_{1}E_{F}^{2}
and
\delta_{2}E_{F}^{2}
, let

K_{3}=F(\sqrt{\alpha\beta}\,)
be the third quadratic subfield of
K_{1}K_{2}/F
,
and set

\delta_{1}E_{F}^{2}*\delta_{2}E_{F}^{2}=\delta_{3}E_{F}^{2},

where
\delta_{3}E_{F}^{2}={\operatorname{sep}}(K_{3}/F)
. Observe that this is the usual
product if and only if the separants are coprime.
Over the field
F={\mathbb{Q}}
, a separant of a quadratic
number field is just its discriminant because
E_{\mathbb{Q}}^{2}=1
.

Example 2.

Consider
F={\mathbb{Q}}(\sqrt{-23}\,)
and
K=F(\sqrt{\mu}\,)
for

\mu=-5+2\sqrt{-23}
. Since
(\mu)={\mathfrak{p}}^{2}{\mathfrak{q}}
for the prime
ideals
{\mathfrak{p}}=(3,1-\sqrt{-23}\,)
and
{\mathfrak{q}}=(13,4+\sqrt{-23}\,)
,
we have
\operatorname{Ram}_{f}(K/F)=\{{\mathfrak{q}}\}
and
\operatorname{disc}\,K/F={\mathfrak{q}}
. Also we
find
{\mathfrak{q}}^{3}=(\delta)
for
\delta=-37-6\sqrt{-23}
, and

{\operatorname{sep}}(K/F)=\delta E_{F}^{2}
.

Separants that cannot be written as a product of separants are
called irreducible; separants
\delta\cdot E_{F}^{2}
for which

(\delta)
is a power of a prime ideal are called prime separants.

Example 3.

Consider the quadratic extension
K=F(\sqrt{1+2i}\,)
of
F={\mathbb{Q}}(i)
.
It has relative integral basis
\{1,\frac{1+\sqrt{1+2i}}{1+i}\}
, and
its relative discriminant is therefore the ideal
(1+i)^{2}(1+2i)
. The
generator
\delta=2i(1+2i)
of this ideal has the property that

K=F(\sqrt{\delta}\,)
, hence it is the separant of this extension:

{\operatorname{sep}}(K/F)=2i(1+2i)
. Since there is no quadratic extension of
F

with relative discriminant
{\mathfrak{d}}=(2)
, this separant cannot be factored
into prime separants.

The modulus associated to a separant
{\operatorname{sep}}(K/F)
is the conductor
of the extension
K/F
, including the infinite places; two separants
are called coprime if they are coprime as ideals, and strongly coprime
if their associated conductors are coprime.

Since the square root of the separant
{\operatorname{sep}}(K/F)
generates the quadratic
extension
K/F
, the splitting of primes in
K/F
is determined by
the quadratic Kronecker symbol
(\delta/{\mathfrak{p}})
.

6.
Unique Factorization into Prime Separants

We now explain how to generalize the prime discriminant factorization
considered by Goldstein and Sunley to fields with trivial separant
class group:

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
F
be an algebraic number field. Then the following assertions
are equivalent:

(1)

The separant class group of
F
is trivial:
\mbox{\rm SCl}(F)=1
.

(2)

F
is totally real, and its class number in the strict sense is odd.

(3)

F
has odd class number, and for every Dirichlet character
\chi

defined modulo
4
there is a unit
\varepsilon\in E_{F}
such that

\chi=(\frac{\varepsilon}{\cdot})
.

(4)

F
has odd class number, and for each prime ideal
{\mathfrak{p}}
with
odd norm there is a (unique) quadratic extension
K/F
with

\operatorname{Ram}_{f}(K/F)=\{{\mathfrak{p}}\}
.

(5)

Every separant in
\mbox{\rm Sep}(F)
can be written uniquely (up to order)
as a product of prime separants.

(6)

For every quadratic extension
K/F
we have

(K_{\operatorname{gen}}^{(2)+}:F)=2^{t}
, where
t=\#\operatorname{Ram}_{f}(K/F)
.

Proof.

(1)
\iff
(2) is a consequence of the formula
\#\mbox{\rm Sep}(F)=2^{\rho^{+}+s}
,
where
\rho^{+}={\operatorname{rk}\,}\operatorname{Cl}_{2}(F)
and
2s
is the number of complex
embeddings of
F
.

\Longrightarrow
(4): Let
{\mathfrak{p}}
be a prime ideal with odd norm. Since
F

has odd class number,
{\mathfrak{p}}^{h}=(\pi)
for some
\pi\in{\mathcal{O}}_{F}
. Since

\mbox{\rm SCl}(F)=1
, every element of
M_{4}/M_{4}^{2}
is reprsented by a unit;
thus there is a unit
\varepsilon
such that
\varepsilon\pi\equiv\xi^{2}\bmod 4
.
In the quadratic extension
K=F(\sqrt{\pi\varepsilon}\,)
, the only ramified
prime ideal is
{\mathfrak{p}}
.

(4)
\Longrightarrow

\chi
be a Dirichlet character defined modulo
4
.
Then
\chi
is in the image of

\kappa=(\frac{\alpha}{\cdot})
such that

\kappa=\chi\psi
, where
\psi
is defined modulo an odd ideal.
By assumption,
\psi
is a Kronecker character; but then so is
\chi
.
This implies the claim.

(3)
\Longrightarrow
(1): By Cor.
4.5

F
is totally real and

\rho^{+}=\rho=0

(1)
\Longrightarrow
(5): Let
\delta E_{F}^{2}
be a separant. The Kronecker

\kappa_{\delta}
can be written uniquely as a product of
primitive quadratic Dirichlet characters with prime power conductor:

\kappa_{\delta}=\chi_{1}\cdots\chi_{t}

\mbox{\rm Sep}(F)=1

\chi_{j}
is a Kronecker character
(\delta_{j}/\,\cdot\,)
.
Since the conductors of these Kronecker characters are coprime prime
ideal powers we must have
\delta=\delta_{1}\cdots\delta_{t}
.

We claim that this factorization is unique. To this end, assume that
there are two factorizations

\delta=\delta_{1}\cdots\delta_{t}=\delta_{1}^{\prime}\cdots\delta_{t}^{\prime}
of
\delta

into prime separants.

Since
K_{\operatorname{gen}}^{+}=F(\sqrt{\delta_{1}},\ldots,\sqrt{\delta_{t}}\,)

is the maximal elementary abelian extension of

K

at all finite primes, we must have
\sqrt{\delta_{j}^{\prime}}\in K_{\operatorname{gen}}^{+}
.
But since
\delta_{j}^{\prime}
and the
\delta_{j}
are prime separants, this is
only possible if
\delta_{j}^{\prime}E^{2}
is among the
\delta_{j}E^{2}
.

(5)
\Longrightarrow
(6): Let
K/F
be a quadratic extension with separant
\delta
.
Since
\mbox{\rm SCl}(F)=1
we can write
\delta=\delta_{1}\cdots\delta_{t}
as a
product of prime discriminants that are coprime except for infinite prime
factors. But then
L=F(\sqrt{\delta_{1}},\ldots,\sqrt{\delta_{t}}\,)/K

is unramified at all finite primes, hence equal to the genus field in
the strict sense.

(6)
\Longrightarrow
(4): We first claim that
F
has odd class number in the strict
sense. If not, then there is a quadratic extension
K/F
unramified at
all primes; in particular,
t=0
, and by assumption we have

K_{\operatorname{gen}}^{+}=F
: contradiction since
K\subseteq K_{\operatorname{gen}}^{+}
.

Now let
{\mathfrak{p}}
be a prime ideal with odd norm; choose a prime ideal
{\mathfrak{q}}

in the inverse class of
{\mathfrak{p}}
with odd norm in the ray class group

\operatorname{Cl}\{4\}
modulo
4
; then
{\mathfrak{p}}{\mathfrak{q}}=(\alpha)
for some

\alpha\equiv\xi^{2}\bmod 4
. The quadratic extension

K=F(\sqrt{\alpha}\,)

K_{\operatorname{gen}}^{+}=F(\sqrt{\delta}_{1},\sqrt{\delta_{2}}\,)
for coprime separants

\delta_{1}
,

7.
Genus theory

Genus theory of quadratic extensions
k/F
of number fields with trivial
separant class group can be made as explicit as over
{\mathbb{Q}}
: the factorization
of
\delta={\operatorname{sep}}(k/F)
into prime separants gives us the genus class field
as well as the genus characters.

Lemma 7.1
.

Let
F
be a number field with odd class number in the strict sense.
Let
K=F(\sqrt{\delta}\,)
and
L=F(\sqrt{\delta_{1}},\sqrt{\delta_{2}}\,)
,
were
\delta
,
\delta_{1}
and
\delta_{2}
are separants. Then
L/K
is
unramified at all finite primes if and only if
\delta=\delta_{1}\delta_{2}
.

Proof.

Let
k_{1}=F(\sqrt{\delta_{1}}\,)
,
k_{2}=k(\sqrt{\delta_{2}}\,)

and
K=F(\sqrt{\delta_{1}\delta_{2}}\,)=F(\sqrt{\delta}\,)
be the
three quadratic subextensions of
K/F
. By the conductor-discriminant
formula we have

\operatorname{disc}\,(L/F)=\operatorname{disc}\,(k/F)\cdot\operatorname{disc}\,(k_{1}/F)\cdot\operatorname{disc}\,(k_{2}/F).

Since
L/K
is unramified, we have
\operatorname{disc}\,(L/F)=\operatorname{disc}\,(k/F)^{2}
. This
implies
\operatorname{disc}\,(k_{1}/F)\cdot\operatorname{disc}\,(k_{2}/F)=\operatorname{disc}\,(k/F)
. Since
separants are
h
-th powers of the relative discriminants, we must
have
\delta_{1}\cdot\delta=\varepsilon\delta
. Since

k=F(\sqrt{\delta_{1}\delta_{2}}\,)=F(\sqrt{\delta}\,)
, the unit

\varepsilon
must be a square, and this implies that

\delta_{1}E^{2}\cdot\delta_{2}E^{2}=\delta E^{2}
as claimed.
∎

Let
F
be a number field with
\mbox{\rm SCl}(F)=1
. For a quadratic extension

K/F
with separant
{\operatorname{sep}}(K/F)=\delta E_{F}^{2}
let

\delta=\delta_{1}\cdots\delta_{t}

denote the factorization of
{\operatorname{sep}}(K/F)
into prime separants.
We already know that this factorization determines the genus field of
K/F
:

Proposition 7.2
.

Let
F
be a number field with trivial separant class group, and
let
K/F
be a quadratic extension with separant
\delta={\operatorname{sep}}(K/F)
.
If
\delta=\delta_{1}\cdots\delta_{t}
is its factorization into
prime separants, then its genus field in the strict sense is

K_{\operatorname{gen}}^{+}=F(\sqrt{\delta_{1}},\ldots,\sqrt{\delta_{t}}\,)
.

Proof.

Write
{\operatorname{sep}}(k/F)=\delta=\delta_{1}\cdots\delta_{t}
as a product of
prime separants. Then
K=F(\sqrt{\delta_{1}},\ldots,\sqrt{\delta_{t}}\,)

is an abelian extension of
F
unramified over
k
, hence contained
in the genus field. Since
(k_{\operatorname{gen}}:k)\leq 2^{t}
, we must have
K=k_{\operatorname{gen}}
.
∎

Now let
c\in\operatorname{Cl}(K)
be an ideal class, pick an ideal
{\mathfrak{a}}\in c
coprime to

\delta
. Then
\chi_{j}(c)=(\frac{\delta_{j}}{N_{K/F}{\mathfrak{a}}})
is a well
defined quadratic character on the class group. The characters
\chi_{j}

are called genus characters of
K/F
.

Theorem 7.3

(Principal Genus theorem)
.

Let
F
be a number field with trivial separant class group. Then
an ideal class
c\in\operatorname{Cl}^{+}(K)
is a square if and only if

\chi_{j}(c)=+1
for all genus characters
\chi_{j}
.

Proof.

Write
c=[{\mathfrak{p}}]
for some prime ideal
{\mathfrak{p}}
unramified in
k/F
.
Clearly
\chi_{j}(c)=1
for all genus characters is equivalent
to
{\mathfrak{p}}
splitting completely in the genus class field.
Since
F
has odd class number in the strict sense,
K_{\operatorname{gen}}^{+}
is
the maximal elementary abelian
2
-extension contained in the
Hilbert class field. By class field theory,
{\mathfrak{p}}
splits completely
in the maximal elementary abelian
2
-extension containd in the
Hilbert class field if and only if the ideal class
[{\mathfrak{p}}]
is
a square in the class group
\operatorname{Cl}_{2}^{+}(k)
.
∎

Example.

Consider the quadratic extension
k=F(\sqrt{93+50\sqrt{2}}\,)
of

F={\mathbb{Q}}(\sqrt{2}
. The separant

\delta={\operatorname{sep}}(k/F)=(107+55\sqrt{2}\,)E_{F}^{2}
has the factorization
into prime separants

107+54\sqrt{2}=(13+4\sqrt{2}\,)(7+2\sqrt{2}\,).

This shows that the genus class field of
k
is given by

k_{\operatorname{gen}}^{+}=k(\sqrt{13+4\sqrt{2}}\,).

Since
k_{\operatorname{gen}}^{+}=k_{\operatorname{gen}}
in this case, the
2
-class group of
k

must be cyclic.

The
2
-class group is generated by a prime ideal above
2
;
since
(\sqrt{2}\,)
does not split in
F(\sqrt{13+4\sqrt{2}}\,)
,
the ideal cass generated by the prime ideal above
2
is not a square,
and we deduce that
\operatorname{Cl}_{2}(k)\simeq{\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}}
.
