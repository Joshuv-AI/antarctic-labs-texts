Introduction

Osteosarcoma (OS), the most prevalent primary malignant neoplasm of the bone, is commonly localized within the metaphysis of long bones and exhibits high invasiveness and metastatic potential [1]. OS commonly happens in children and adolescents, and its most prominent symptoms are localized bone pain and swelling. Early symptoms of OS were relatively insidious, and some patients had already developed lung metastases when diagnosed in the late stage, which resulted in an extremely poor prognosis, with mortality rates as high as 80% [2]. Currently, the underlying pathogenesis of OS is still unclear, and the genetic factor is thought to be significantly associated with OS [3]. In addition, due to its high rate of disability, OS has become a public health problem of worldwide concern. Therefore, further investigation of the pathogenesis of OS and searching for ideal therapeutic strategies can help prolong the survival of OS patients.

Current therapeutic approaches for OS are multimodal, encompassing chemotherapy, neoadjuvant chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and surgery [4]. Surgical intervention remains a cornerstone in OS management, but some patients are still at risk of tumour recurrence and distal metastasis after surgery. The amalgamation of chemotherapy and surgical protocols has yielded noteworthy outcomes, culminating in 5-year survival rates of up to 70% among patients afflicted with non-metastatic OS [5]. However, those with metastatic or recurrent disease continue to face poor prognoses, with a 5-year survival rate of merely 20% [5]. Moreover, long-term chemotherapy makes OS patients highly resistant to drugs, with more adverse effects. Recently, immunotherapy has shown promise across various cancers, with PD-L1, a critical immunosuppressive molecule, playing a role in inhibiting T cell and NK cell activation [6]. In the tumour microenvironment (TME), high expression of PD-L1 inhibits T cell function, leading to tumour immune escape [7]. PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors partially delayed the malignant progression of OS in mice [8]. Relevant clinical trials are ongoing and haven't yet found the same therapeutic effects as animal study [10]. Hence, finding new therapeutic strategies to improve the prognosis of OS patients is urgently needed.

TME is a complex and rich environment composed of multiple tissue cells, immune cells, and tumour cells, which contribute significantly to tumour growth [9]. TME can help tumour cells evade immune surveillance and promote tumour inflammation and tumour angiogenesis, ultimately accelerating cancer progression [10]. Tumour cells within the TME exhibit significant heterogeneity, affecting their growth rate, invasion, metastatic, and drug sensitivity between different tumour cells [11]. T cells, as adaptive immune cells, are key regulators of anti-tumour activity. Suppression of T cell effector function in TME is one of the major reasons for promoting tumour development and the failure of immunotherapy [12]. T cell activation (such as CD8+ T cells and CD4+ T cells) can enhance the therapeutic efficacy of immunotherapy and perform anti-tumour functions [13]. Immunotherapies developed based on T cells have proven efficacy against numerous malignancies, but their effectiveness against OS is still unclear [14]. Further exploration of the molecular mechanisms of OS and T cell-related genes (TCRGs) makes targeted T-cell drug therapy a potentially effective therapeutic strategy for OS treatment.

Presently, the treatment of OS still suffers from high pulmonary metastasis rates, few effective therapeutic agents, high tumour heterogeneity, and few specific targets [15]. Considering this, we need to explore and develop new tumour therapeutic targets and personalized treatment strategies. With advancements in high-throughput sequencing and single-cell sequencing (scRNA-seq) technologies, a more nuanced understanding of OS pathophysiology is possible, paving the way for personalized treatment approaches. In the study, we explored the biological functions associated with OS from the perspective of scRNA-seq based on public datasets of OS. We identified TCRGs associated with OS, further constructed a T cell-related signature (TCRS), predicted the prognosis of OS, and finally validated the reliability of TCRS. Besides, we investigated differences in bio-functionality and cellular mutations between different risk groups. Lastly, we assessed immunotherapy response and drug sensitivity in different risk groups. The purpose of the study was to investigate new immunotherapy and targeted therapy strategies to provide novel insights to improve prognosis and personalize treatment for OS patients.

Materials and methods

Download and organization of data

The scRNA-seq data relevant to OS were sourced from the GEO database (GSE152048), encompassing 100,987 single cells originating from 11 OS samples (5 males and 6 females, aged between 11 and 38 years) [16]. Additionally, RNA-seq data, micro-array data, and clinical information associated with OS were gathered from various repositories, including TARGET-OS and several GEO datasets: GSE16091, GSE21257, and GSE39055 [17–19]. Following the removal of incomplete or duplicate entries, the TARGET-OS dataset contained 85 OS samples, while GSE16091 comprised 29 samples, GSE21257 included 53 samples, and GSE39055 retained 37 OS samples.

Processing of scRNA-seq data

To ensure robust data quality, we implemented established quality control measures based on previously established thresholds [16, 20]. Then, we performed the steps of normalization, dimensionality reduction, and clustering using a standardized process for single-cell data analysis. For cell annotation, we utilized the "SingleR" package, identified highly variable genes within each cell type, and employed the "FindAllMarkers" function (logFC ≥ 1 and p < 0.01) [21]. Lastly, we investigated inter-cellular communication patterns by integrating ligand-receptor information utilizing the "CellChat" framework. This approach facilitated the modeling of communication probabilities and enabled the identification of significant communication events [22].

Development of a T cell-related signature

To refine the predictive accuracy of our prognostic TCRS, we devised a framework centered on T cells that demonstrated prognostic significance. To pinpoint relevant TCRGs crucial for prognosis, we initially performed univariate Cox regression analysis on these genes. Subsequently, to streamline the selection process and identify the most pertinent TCRGs, we employed LASSO analysis coupled with tenfold cross-validation. Validation of the prognostic signature

To assess survival differences between risk groups, Kaplan–Meier analysis was employed. The predictive efficacy of both the TCRS and clinical characteristics was evaluated using time-dependent ROC curves, implemented via the "timeROC" R package [23]. Survival differences among diverse risk groups within each clinical subgroup were scrutinized to gauge the prognostic TCRS's adaptability across varying clinical profiles. The C-index served to quantify the discriminatory performance of the gene signature relative to conventional clinical variables. Furthermore, the study investigated the correlation between risk groups and clinical characteristics to ascertain the TCRS's clinical relevance. For enhanced clinical utility, a nomogram integrating the TCRS and clinical attributes was devised to prognosticate 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival probabilities for patients. We identified differentially expressed gene (DEGs) between these groups (|logFC|> 0.585 and FDR < 0.05), followed by Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analyses (p < 0.05) [24]. We also analyzed somatic mutations in different risk groups [25].

Identification of immunotherapy, chemotherapy, and targeted therapies

In parallel, we investigated immune cell infiltration patterns employing the CIBERSORT algorithm within different risk cohorts [26, 27]. Furthermore, we examined discrepancies in immune checkpoint genes (ICGs) expression levels between these groups utilizing the Wilcoxon test. To prognosticate response to immunotherapy within each risk group, we computed Tumour Immune Dysfunction and Exclusion (TIDE) scores [28]. Notably, this study employed the "oncoPredict" R package to forecast drug sensitivity utilizing data from the GDSC database [29].

Cell sources

The human OS cell line MG63 and the osteoblast cell line hFOB1.19 (#ZY60037/ZY60076; Zeye Biology, Shanghai, China) were used in this study. The cells were cultured in DMEM medium (10% fetal bovine serum, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin) (5% CO2, 37 °C).

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction assay

Quantitative reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) primers were designed and synthesized by Sheng Gong Company through DNAMAN software, and the primer sequences of each gene are listed in Table 1. After collecting the cells in each group, total RNA was extracted by using Trizol reagent (#ZY68369FA; Zeye Biology, Shanghai, China), and complementary DNA was obtained using a kit with reverse transcription reagent (#ZY6335PD; Zeye Biology, Shanghai, China). Total RNA was extracted using Trizol reagent (#ZY68369FA; Zeye Biology, Shanghai, China), and complementary DNA was obtained using a kit with reverse transcription (#ZY6335PD; Zeye Biology, Shanghai, China) under the following conditions: 42 ℃, 2 min, 50 ℃, 15 min, 85 ℃, 5 s, and 4 ℃, 10 min, for 1 cycle. The qRT-PCR was performed in a 7500 Real-time detector (ABI, USA), and the amplification procedure was divided into two steps: pre-denaturation at 95 ℃ for 10 min, denaturation at 95 ℃ for 15 s, and annealing and extension at 60 ℃ for 45 s. The relative expression of the final mRNA was calculated by the 2−ΔΔCt method with GAPDH as the internal reference.

Western blotting

MG63 and hFOB1.19 were lysed by cell RIPA lysis buffer (#ZY65747FA; Zeye Biology, Shanghai, China) with 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF). Equal proteins (25 μg) were electrophoresed on 10% SDS-PAGE, transferred to polyvi nylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes and blocked with skim milk, and incubated with the primary antibodies against CAMK4 (1:800), TSPYL4 (1;600), MYOM2 (1:500), IL2RA (1:600), and CDC14A (1:700) (#PA1-542; #PA5-114,055; #PA5-101,684; #PA5-116,978; #PA5-114,232; SCIENTIFIC, Shanghai, China) in tris-buffered saline Tween-20 (TBST). Then, the membranes were washed and incubated with an HRP-conjugated second antibody. Finally, the PVDF membranes were washed and reacted with the ECL kit (#ZY65787FA; Zeye Biology, Shanghai, China). Scanning densitometry was used to quantify the signals.

Results

Processing of scRNA-seq data

Figure 1 depicts the workflow employed in the present study. We conducted a comprehensive analysis of scRNA-seq data to investigate the tumour microenvironment in OS. Initial steps encompassed quality control procedures applied to the single-cell data, with the pre- and post-quality control outcomes delineated in Fig. S1A and B, respectively. Figure 2A showed the 3,000 highly variable genes, with the top 10 genes highlighted (MYLPF, MYL1, ACTC1, TNNC2, PLA2G2A, CXCL10, MYH3, FABP4, TNNT2, and SFRP2). Furthermore, Fig. 2B presents marker genes associated with the first four PCs in the OS. Utilizing the R package "clustree," we visually portrayed inter-cluster interactions across various resolutions, aiding in the selection of an optimal resolution for subsequent analyses (resolution = 1) (Fig. 2C). The tSNE graph illustrated the distribution of distinct clusters, with "SingleR" employed for cell annotation, and 18 clusters (0–17) and 9 cell types (chondrocytes, monocytes, MSCs, T cells, endothelial cells, macrophages, tissue stem cells, B cells, and neurons) were identified. (Fig. 2D and E). The heatmap showed the top 10 marker genes for each cell type based on average expression (Fig. 2F). Leveraging the "FindAllMarkers" function, we identified 13,095 highly variable genes across each cell type (logFC ≥ 1 and p < 0.01) (Table S1). Moreover, our analysis delved into interactions between T cells and other cell types, notably within the CCL, EGF, VEGF, MHC-II, ICAM, and IL4 signaling pathways (Fig. 3A–D).

Development of a T cell-related signature

In investigating the potential prognostic implications of distinct immune cell populations in OS patients, our study conducted a comprehensive survival analysis, revealing that scores of macrophages, B cells, and neurons were not significantly associated with patient prognosis, whereas high scores of T cells were significantly associated with poor prognosis (Fig. 4). Subsequently, we employed univariate Cox analysis to initially screen 14 candidate prognostic TCRGs (ITK, CAMK4, CDC14A, IL2RA, CYFIP2, CISH, MYOM2, HOXB2, RASGRP2, ZBTB40, TSPYL4, ABCB1, LLGL2, and SPAG1) (Fig. 5A). Further refinement of these candidates was achieved through LASSO analysis, effectively narrowing down the selection to 12 TCRGs of significant prognostic relevance (CAMK4, CDC14A, IL2RA, CYFIP2, CISH, MYOM2, HOXB2, RASGRP2, TSPYL4, ABCB1, LLGL2, and SPAG1) (Fig. 5B and C). Finally, employing multivariate Cox analysis, we established a predictive TCRS comprising five robust prognostic TCRGs (CAMK4, CDC14A, IL2RA, MYOM2, and TSPYL4) (Fig. 5D).

Validation of the T cell-related signature

Figures 6A–D delineated the differences in survival analysis, risk scores, survival status, and the expression profiles of the five TCRGs across distinct groups within the TARGET-OS (p < 0.001), GSE16091 (p = 0.014), GSE21257 (p = 0.049), and GSE39055 (p = 0.034) datasets, highlighting the robustness and transferability of the TCRS across diverse OS datasets. Remarkably, all AUC values associated with the TCRS exceeded 0.85, which was significantly better than the clinical features and showed good predictive performance (Fig. 7A and B). The consistent favorable survival outcomes observed in the low-risk group in different clinical subgroups (age, gender, metastatic, and site) underscored the generalizability and clinical relevance of the TCRS (Fig. 7C). Moreover, the C-index score of TCRS was markedly higher compared to that of traditional clinical features, underscoring its superior predictive capability (Fig. 8A). Additionally, the heatmap elucidated the relationship between the TCRS, clinical characteristics, and the five TCRGs; specifically, the risk score was significantly correlated with the expression levels of the five TCRGs, and more patients with metastases were found in the high-risk group (Fig. 8B). To streamline clinical implementation, we constructed a nomogram integrating the TCRS and clinical parameters, facilitating the prediction of OS patient survival probabilities at 1-, 3-, and 5-year intervals. Notably, the stratification based on risk scores emerged as the most influential prognostic factor (Fig. 8C).

Enrichment and mutation analysis

We identified a total of 21 DEGs across distinct groups, meeting stringent criteria (|logFC|≥ 0.585 and FDR < 0.05). To investigate the biomolecular functions and associated signaling pathways of these DEGs, we conducted GO and KEGG pathway analyses. The GO analysis unveiled diverse enriched terms associated with DEGs, encompassing inorganic compound metabolism, kinesin complex organization, histone deacetylase binding, carbohydrate phosphatase activity, and sugar-phosphatase activity (Fig. 9A and Table S2). Meanwhile, KEGG pathway analysis unveiled a spectrum of enriched pathways linked to DEGs, spanning mineral absorption, calcium signaling, Th17 cell differentiation, cholinergic synapse function, and oxytocin signaling pathways (Fig. 9B and Table S3). Mutation analysis unveiled a higher mutation frequency within the high-risk group, with TP53 mutations predominantly observed in the low-risk group, contrasting with TP53 and TTN mutations prevailing in the high-risk group (Fig. 9C and D). These findings underscored disparities in molecular mechanisms, signaling pathways, and gene mutations between high- and low-risk groups, potentially underpinning the divergent prognostic outcomes observed across distinct subgroups.

Identification of immunotherapy, chemotherapy, and targeted therapies

We employed the CIBERSORT method to assess immune cell infiltration within the OS samples (Fig. 10A). Notably, the low-risk group exhibited higher levels of CD4 memory resting T cells, contrasting with lower levels of macrophage M0 (Fig. 10B). Co-expression analysis revealed a negative correlation between the risk score and CD8 + T cell infiltration levels (Fig. 10C). Furthermore, the low-risk group exhibited significant up-regulation of ICGs, including CD274, LAG3, and TIGIT (Fig. 11A). Lower TIDE scores observed in the low-risk group suggest their potential suitability for immunotherapeutic interventions (Fig. 11B). The combination of TIDE score and risk score emerged as a robust predictor of patient prognosis (Fig. 11C). Beyond immunotherapy, chemotherapy and targeted therapy represent pivotal modalities in OS management. Finally, we identified 49 chemotherapeutic and targeted therapeutic agents, and the 50% inhibitory concentration (IC50) of the agents in the high-risk group were all smaller than those in the low-risk group, suggesting that patients in the high-risk group are more suitable for chemotherapy and targeted therapies (p < 0.05) (Fig. 12). Overall, our findings suggest that the low-risk group may exhibit heightened sensitivity to immunotherapy, while the high-risk group may demonstrate greater responsiveness to chemotherapy and targeted therapies.

Discussion

OS is a highly aggressive malignant bone tumour characterized by a dismal prognosis, predominantly affecting adolescents [30]. Despite advances in medical treatments, the five-year survival rate for OS remains disappointingly low, particularly in cases with distant metastatic dissemination [31]. Early diagnosis and effective treatment are essential to improving survival in OS patients. Advances in sequencing technology have made it possible to explore the mechanisms and changes in cancer pathology at the genetic level. Recent advancements in sequencing technologies have enabled a deeper exploration of cancer pathologies at the genetic level, while bioinformatics analyses provide insights into key mutated genes, tumor heterogeneity, and prognostic predictions [32].

T cells, as a core component of the immune system, exhibited substantial anti-cancer potential by recognizing and attacking cancer cells [33]. T cells recognized specific antigens on the surface of cancer cells and released cytotoxic substances to kill the cancer cells [34]. However, cancer cells can evade immune surveillance by altering the TME to suppress T cell activity [35]. CAR T-cell therapy showed a promising prognosis for OS patients [36]. Targeting the neovasculature improves T cell immunotherapy efficacy in OS [37]. Although T cells were thought to be involved in the pathological mechanisms of cancer, the understanding of their specific role and mechanisms in OS remains limited [38]. Therefore, we explored TCRGs in OS prognosis and immune response, offering vital clues in the search for novel therapeutic targets and immunological drugs.

The exploration of complex TME within tumours by scRNA-seq has become a popular direction for investigating tumour mechanisms [39]. First, we processed the scRNA-seq data of OS from the GEO database and performed cell annotation. We identified highly variable genes in each cell type using the "FindAllMarkers" function, explored the distribution of different clusters, and investigated inter-cellular communication patterns via the "CellChat" framework. In addition, we further investigated the interactions between T cells and other cell types. Further exploring the potential prognosis of different immune cell populations in OS patients, we found that T cells were significantly correlated with OS patient prognosis. Then, we used univariate Cox analysis and LASSO analysis to identify nine TCRGs associated with OS prognosis. And we used multivariate Cox analysis to screen five reliable prognostic TCRGs and construct a prognostic model for the TCRG characteristics of OS patients. TSPYL4 is a gene encoding a protein of the TSPY family, which is considered to play a potential role in cell cycle regulation and cancer biology [40]. TSPYL4 was aberrantly expressed in HNSCC and might be a potential prognostic biomarker for HNSCC [41]. Another study also discovered that TSPYL4 expression was significantly higher in pancreatic tumour tissues [42]. Our study also found that TSPYL4 may be strongly associated with the prognosis of OS. MYOM2 is an important gene for muscle structure and function, and may be involved in biological changes in tumours. The expression level of MYOM2 can significantly influence the disease progression and prognosis of hepatocellular carcinoma patients [43]. MYOM2 was observed as a mutant gene in primary central nervous system lymphoma [44]. Our analyses suggested that MYOM2 was probably a risk factor for the development of OS.

IL2RA is a receptor for IL-2, and IL-2 is a cytokine involved in T cell growth, proliferation, and differentiation [45]. IL2RA polymorphisms are significantly correlated with lung cancer risk [46]. It was also shown that IL2RA might be a prognostic indicator for pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma [47]. CDC14A is a gene encoding a dual-specificity phosphatase involved in regulating the cell cycle and DNA damage repair [48]. CDC14A had a crucial effect on sperm function and fertility in men [49]. Targeting CDC14A induced cell cycle arrest and enhances apoptosis in gastric cancer cells [50]. Interestingly, earlier studies have also demonstrated that targeting CDC14A effectively inhibits the growth and invasion of lung adenocarcinoma [51]. CAMK4 is a kinase involved in calcium signaling and the regulation of various cellular processes. CAMK4 is closely related to T cell differentiation, and dysregulated CAMK4 expression affects the TME and the immune system [52]. The CAMK4 level was abnormally increased in colorectal cancer, indicating a possible association with poor prognosis and aggressive tumour behaviour [53]. CAMK4 is a key gene involved in gastric cancer development, according to WGCNA analysis [54].

Then, we assessed survival differences between OS patients in different groups by KM analysis and ROC curves. Our results demonstrated a better survival prognosis for OS patients in the high-risk group in multiple OS cohorts. The ROC curve confirmed the reliability of the results, showing the stability of TCRS in different OS cohorts. Besides, we created a nomogram integrating TCRS and clinical attributes to predict OS patients survival probabilities. We also screened 21 DEGs and performed GO and KEGG analyses to explore underlying biological functions. The somatic mutation analysis revealed a higher probability of gene mutations in the high-risk group, with TP53 mutations predominantly in the low-risk group and TNN mutations predominantly in the high-risk group. The TP53 mutation was a major oncogenic driver of OS, leading to chromosomal instability in OS patients [55]. Abnormalities in the TP53 gene were linked to the sensitivity of OS patients to chemotherapy [56]. Immune cell infiltration is of critical importance in tumour development and prognosis [57]. We employed the CIBERSORT method to explore the composition of immune cells in different risk groups and found that the low-risk group exhibited higher levels of CD4 memory resting T cells, contrasting with lower levels of macrophage M0. The emergence of immune checkpoint inhibitors provided novel therapeutic methods for the treatment of various malignancies [58]. We observed that the low-risk group exhibited significant up-regulation of ICGs and lower TIDE scores, suggesting that they may be suitable for immunotherapy. In addition, chemotherapy and targeted therapy are essential treatment strategies for OS. We revealed that the high-risk group might display higher responsiveness to chemotherapy and targeted therapies by exploring drug sensitivities. Overall, our findings provide novel perspectives for personalized treatment of OS patients.

Nevertheless, several limitations were identified in this study. Firstly, the analysis was based on relatively small sample sizes, which may introduce biases into the results. Secondly, we only explored the expression of hub TCRGs in OS at the gene expression level, and further experiments are needed on the specific pathological mechanisms. Moving forward, it is crucial to validate our findings through comprehensive examination in large-scale clinical trials and fundamental investigations.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study identified TCRGs, constructed and validated a TCRS for OS, and assessed immune response and drug sensitivity in different risk groups of OS patients. These findings provide novel insights into personalized treatment strategies for OS, potentially guiding more effective therapeutic interventions.
