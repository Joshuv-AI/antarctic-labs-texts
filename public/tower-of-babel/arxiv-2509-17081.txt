Proposal for macroscopic delocalisation of a large mass in a RF trap
Martine Schut, Valerio Scarani

Abstract
Engineering coherent spatial superpositions of levitated large masses is an ongoing challenge. Borrowing from recent experimental work, we consider a charged mass of hundreds of nanometers size (“nanoparticle”) co-trapped with an ion in a Paul trap, and propose a scheme to manipulate its spatial state through the Coulomb interaction with the ion. We focus on the achievable delocalisation, only sketching the other challenges of the protocol (initial cooling, preservation of coherence for long-enough times, and detection). We prove that our scheme can displace coherently the nanoparticle by a few nanometers, with state-of-the-art parameters. Though smaller than the nanoparticle’s size, this is much larger than the wavefunction of the trap’s ground state. Thus the co-trapping scheme is in principle able to demonstrate macroscopic delocalisation of a charged nanoparticle.



I
Introduction

One of the core principles of quantum physics is that of superposition of states.
This principle is widely used in quantum technologies and quantum information, but is not observed in our everyday macroscopic world.
By creating quantum superpositions in increasingly large objects, we are pushing the boundary between quantum and classical physics.
We focus on the quantum superposition of two states localized at different positions, i.e. spatial delocalisation.
These spatial superpositions have been realised in small particles and have found applications in, for example, the Colella-Overhauser-Werner (COW) experiment for showing that the equivalence principle holds in the quantum limit
colella_observation_1975
, the challenge now is to have both a large mass and a large superposition size.
Increasing the mass is particularly important for applications related to gravity, which scales with mass.
A large spatial superposition (
0.54
m) of a very small particle
kovachy2015quantum
, and a small superposition of a heavy mass (
16\penalty\ \mu
g)
bild_schrodinger_2023
, have been reported; but the regime of heavy masses with large superpositions (at least attograms and micro/nanometers) has not yet been reached experimentally.
These macroscopic quantum systems have proposed applications in testing fundamental physics: they explore the boundary between classical and quantum physics and can test foundations of quantum mechanics (e.g., CSL and Diósi-Penrose models
Bassi_2013
;
howl2019exploring
).
They could also have multiple applications in sensing, such as for small force measurements such as from the gravitational interaction
bose2017spin
;
marletto2017gravitationally
or dark matter interaction
Riedel_2013
; and have been suggested for applications in geophysics, volcanology and seismology, since they can be used for sensing density shifts
stray2022quantum
.

We are particularly interested in levitated systems, since they are less connected to the environment and have potential for detecting superpositions of the gravitational field.
There are several platforms that work towards realising macroscopic quantum superpositions, such as in optical trapped nanoparticles or diamagnetically levitated systems.
The first one has proven successful for atomic and nanoparticles and in terms of cooling
delic2020cooling
;
gonzalez2021levitodynamics
but suffers a lot of decoherence due to heating from the trapping laser field.
In diamagnetically levitated spin-based platforms
margalit2021realization
;
Bose:2025qns
, good spin manipulation and readout have been achieved
Walsworth2020
.

In this paper, we focus on electromagnetically trapped charged systems, where a large electric dipole moment or a large number of charges on the test mass can be used to cool the system in three degrees of freedom
Dania2021cooling
;
Barker:2023
, and near the motional ground state for optically trapped nanoparticles
delic2020cooling
.
Creating large coherent cat states has been an active research area in the ion trapping community since the ’90s, both in single or multiple Paul/Penning traps
monroe_schrodinger_1996
;
schmidt2003realization
;
johnson_ultrafast_2017
.
The internal structure of the atomic particle can be manipulated with a series of laser pulses. After preparing a superposition of internal states, these laser pulses can be used to give the atomic particle a state-dependent kick and thus create a spatial superposition.
As particles increase in size, their internal structure becomes more complicated and, consequently, the direct application of these methods to large masses becomes challenging
arrangoiz2019resolving
.
Inspired by recent work recent work
bykov2024nanoparticle
, our proposal is based on a scheme where an ion and the nanoparticle are co-trapped in a dual frequency Paul trap.
One can first prepare a spatial superposition of the ion with standard methods, then let it induce a correlated displacement the nanoparticle via their mutual distance-dependent interaction. This approach avoids the problem of accessing the nanoparticle internal energy structure, while still having the cooling advantage of a charged nanoparticle.
This methodology is similar to the phonon-induced displacements in nanomechanical resonators such as in ref.
Pathak2017
.

In this work we show that there exists a parameter regime, in which sizeable spatial superpositions can in principle be prepared. The challenges related to the readout of the superposition and to the need to perform the protocol fast enough to avoid decoherence will be briefly mentioned, but their detailed study is left for future work.
We start by introducing our setup and give a brief background on linear Paul traps driven by two frequencies for trapping the nanoparticle-atomic ion system (section
II
). Section
III
presents the proposed protocol as well as exemplary parameters that could be used for experimental realisation. Finally, the induced nanoparticle superposition width is calculated and the results are discussed (section
IV
).

II.2
Couplings

The interaction potential

III
Proposed protocol

We now present a protocol in which the ion is manipulated to induce a superposition in the nanoparticle through the distance-dependent mutual interactions. In sec.
IV
we will give more details and find the expected superposition size of the nanoparticle.

III.1
Step-by-step protocol

1.

Prepare and cool the nanoparticle
.
Following e.g. the protocol described in ref.
bykov2024nanoparticle
where a nanoparticle can be loaded into the trap in a ultra-high vacuum and cooled. Increasing the number of charges on the nanoparticle then allows for a stiffer trap.

2.

Load the ion
.
With the off-set voltage, the nanoparticle can be moved away from the centre such that the ion can be loaded and cooled there. We assume the separation between the two particles is
>50\,$\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}$
such that their mutual interaction is negligible.

3.

Prepare the system
.
The DC voltages are adjusted such that the separation between the ion and nanoparticle is solely in the
z
-direction.

4.

Create ion cat state
.
With a
\pi/2
pulse on the ion, we create a superposition of two selected pseudo-spin states. Then, with a state-dependent kick (SDK) the motional states of the ion entangle with its internal spin states, creating a cat-state.

5.

Mutual interaction
.
By adjusting the end-cap voltages we reduce the distance between the two particles such that their mutual interaction increases. For some time the particles interact, resulting in an entangled cat state of the ion spin/motion with the nanoparticle.
After the interaction period, the separation is increased again.

6.

Measurement.

Obviously, one should check that the positions of the ion and the nanoparticle are correlated as expected. If this classical correlation is indeed present, to check how much coherence is preserved, instead of the position measurements, steps 5 and 4 are undone. This would transfer the remaining coherence back to the spin, where it can be readily measured.
The measurement scheme proposed in, e.g., ref.
BoseKim2011
, where the coupling to a qubit is used to reconstruct the nanoparticle characteristic function could also be applicable by co-trapping a second ion.

IV.1
Preparation

The ion’s motion is initialised by the Doppler cooling of step 2 to the average vibrational occupation number
\bar{n}_{0}\approx 0

The atomic ion considered here,

\ket{\downarrow}\equiv\ket{S_{1/2},M_J=1/2}\,,\quad\mbox{}\quad\ket{\uparrow}\equiv\ket{D_{5/2},M_J=3/2}\,.

(7)

\ket{\psi_0}=\ket{\downarrow}\ket{0}\,.

(8)

A

\ket{\psi_1}=\left(\ket{\downarrow}+\ket{\uparrow}\right)\ket{0}/\sqrt{2}\,.

The resulting evolution operator is a state-dependent displacement operator accompanied by a spin-flip:

\hat{U}=\hat{D}(\beta)\ket{\uparrow}\bra{\downarrow}+\hat{D}(-\beta)\ket{\downarrow}\bra{\uparrow}\,,

V
Conclusion & Discussion

We have studied the possibility of engineering a delocalised state of a charged particle of the size of hundreds of nanometers (“nanoparticle”) through the interaction with an ion, based on the co-trapping in a Paul trap demonstrated in ref.
bykov2024nanoparticle
. We found that the coherent displacement of the nanoparticle can be of the orders of a few nanometers, smaller than the size of the nanoparticle itself but much larger than the spread of its initial wavefunction.

The possibility of cooling the system makes this setup attractive.
Some level of cooling in all directions has already been demonstrated
bykov_3d_2023
and there are ideas on how to improve it
bykov2024nanoparticle
. We have not advanced any additional proposal for cooling in this work, but simply assumed that the trapped nanoparticle can be suitably cooled.

With respect to the reported experiment, our proposal needs additional laser beams along the
z
-direction for the spin-dependent displacement of the ion. Geometric constraints may require these beams to be slightly off-axis, introducing Coulomb forces on the nanoparticle in the radial plane, which have not been considered here.

Having established that a significant delocalisation is in principle achievable with reasonable parameters, the next step is a comprehensive analysis of the sources of decoherence. Coherence may be lost through interaction with the environment (blackbody radiation, scattering with air molecules, Coulomb interaction with remnant environmental charges…) or from unavoidable fluctuations of the controls (static and oscillating fields of the trap, geometric deviations from the ideal…).

\displaystyle a_{x}

\displaystyle=-\left[\frac{V_{\text{off}}}{r_{0}^{2}}+\frac{V_{\text{end}}}{z_{0}^{2}}\right]\frac{4Q}{m\Omega_{s}^{2}}\,,

(21)

\displaystyle q_{x}

\displaystyle=\frac{2QV_{s}}{r_{0}^{2}m\Omega_{s}^{2}}\,,\quad\mbox{}\quad p_{x}=\frac{2QV_{f}}{r_{0}^{2}m\Omega_{s}^{2}}\,,

The stability of these solutions depends on the triplet

Neglecting the fast voltage for the nanoparticle, the equations of motion become Matthieu-equations (i.e. Hill equations with a single RF frequency), which has well-understood solutions

mclachlan1947theory

Appendix B
State-Dependent Kick Derivation

We give a state-dependent kick (SDK) to our ion state using two counter-propagating lasers to drive Raman transitions between the
\ket{\uparrow}
and
\ket{\downarrow}
states via two-photon resonance.
In this appendix we first generically show how the laser fields can couple the ion spin and motional state and can displace the motional state. Then, we apply it to our specific trapped ion system.

For two correctly tuned counter-propagating lasers with orthogonal polarisations, the transitions addressed by the laser frequencies are driven via the electric dipole interaction and drive the different spin state via the different polarisations.
The external driving force from the laser fields causes a displacement of the ion motional state related to the momentum difference between the two lasers and detuning.
We will assume the Lamb-Dicke regime: the spread in the position of the ions from their equilibrium positions is much smaller than the optical wavelength of the lasers.
In the Lamb-Dicke regime we can neglect higher-state couplings because the coupling between the motional and qubit states is weak, using very short pulses can sometimes have the same effect even outside the Lamb-Dicke regime
GarciaRipoll:2003
.

Taking a generic oscillating field generated by the laser with momentum
\mathbf{k}
frequency
\omega
and phase
\phi

\mathbf{E}_{l}(\mathbf{r})=E_{l}(\mathbf{r})\cos(\mathbf{k}_l \cdot\mathbf{r} - \omega_l t - \phi_l)\varepsilon_{l}\,,

(30)

where
l=\alpha,\beta
and
\varepsilon_{l}
is the polarisation of each field, which determines which transition is driven.
The laser frequencies are as shown in fig.

\omega_{\beta}-\omega_{\alpha}
is detuned from the qubit splitting
\omega_{0}

The resulting driving motional force is thus determined by the detuning
\delta

The Bogoliubov transformation is given by:

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{a}\\
\tilde{a}^{\dagger}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\cosh(r_a)&\sinh(r_a)\\

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{b}\\

\displaystyle\text{with }\tanh(2r_a)=\frac{A_{1}}{A_{2}}\,\,\text{ and }\,\,\tanh(2r_b)=\frac{B_{1}}{B_{2}}

The frequencies are found to be

\tilde{\omega}_{a}=2\sqrt{A_{2}^{2}-A_{1}^{2}}\,,\quad\mbox{}\quad\tilde{\omega}_{b}=2\sqrt{B_{2}^{2}-B_{1}^{2}}

(48)

and the shifted coupling constant is:

\displaystyle\tilde{C}_{12}=C_{12}\sqrt{1-\frac{A_{1}^{2}}{A_{2}^{2}}}\sqrt{1-\frac{B_{1}^{2}}{B_{2}^{2}}}\left(1-\frac{A_{1}}{A_{2}}\right)\left(1-\frac{B_{1}}{B_{2}}\right)

(49)

Resulting in the transformed Hamiltonian:

\hat{H}=\tilde{\omega}_{a}\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}+\tilde{\omega}_{b}\hat{b}^{\dagger}\hat{b}+\tilde{C}_{12}\left(\hat{a}^{\dagger}+\hat{a}\right)\left(\hat{b}^{\dagger}+\hat{b}\right)+\bar{C}

(50)
