Introduction

Effective ventilatory treatments and prevention of RDS have dramatically increased survival of very preterm infants. Introduction of continuous distending pressure ventilation, surfactant supplementation and antenatal glucocorticoid (AGC) treatment have played major roles [1]. Despite minimizing the damaging treatment practices, progress in diminishing the chronic consequences of extreme prematurity has been slow [2]. Bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD) and particularly adverse neuro‐sensory problems affect the survivors [2, 3].

In randomised clinical trials (RCT), decreasing the target of arterial oxygen saturation led to a decrease in neonatal survival. Higher oxygen saturation led to survival at the expense of the increase in the rate of retinopathy of prematurity [4]. Non‐invasive ventilation and less‐invasive surfactant administration have decreased the risk of BPD and the survival without BPD has barely increased [5]. Early routine closure of patent ductus arteriosus (PDA) has failed to increase survival or to decrease serious morbidities [6, 7]. The efficacy of inhaled nitric oxide has been variable and its adverse effects to immature infants are likely. Early neonatal corticosteroid has increased survival without BPD but concerns about adverse consequences remain [8]. However, caffeine treatment decreases the outcome of BPD or death and decreases severe neurosensory problems, with evidence of improvement even at 11 years of age [9].

This review deals with some aspects of clinical‐translational research on management and causes of spontaneous preterm births and lung diseases of infants born extremely premature. The survival of borderline viable infants, born at 22–24 weeks of gestation, is increasing. However, acute and chronic diseases involving particularly the neuro‐sensory system have not decreased. The pathogenesis of diseases of prematurity includes multiple organs and complex pathways. Organs involved in the uptake of oxygen and nutrients are primarily exposed to noxious factors that result in activation and adaptation of defence mechanisms. Current challenges are to develop more effective and safe management practices for borderline viable infants and to decrease extremely premature births without adverse long‐term consequences. Present review contains preliminary and controversial data. Current challenge is to consolidate available treatment practices and to develop new approaches for prevention of life‐long diseases.

Innate Immunity and Other Defences in Immature Lungs

Learned Innate Immunity

The ancient innate immunity in developed organisms plays prominent roles in physiology and pathophysiology. Various patterns of epigenetic effects stemming from learned innate immunity have been described [10]. Major categories of learned innate immunity across species include differentiation, tolerance, trained immunity and priming (Figure 1). The duration of learned innate immune memory is variable and generally exceeds a few days. On the other hand, innate immune memory can last for years in haematopoietic stem cells or differentiated cells within healthy tissues.

The molecular biology of innate immunity and the epigenetic mechanisms of induction and reversal of innate immune responsiveness deal with a major evolving area of molecular research [10].

Innate Immunity and Hormone‐Induced Differentiation of Alveolar Cells

Inflammatory cytokines or surface components of microbes mediate epigenetic differentiation of functionally immature cells. An example is the differentiation of the immature alveolar cells, induced by introduction of inflammatory cytokines to the amniotic fluid [11]. This model study explains the decrease in the risk of RDS in chorioamnionitis (CA) associating with spontaneous premature birth [12]. Fetal breathing movements facilitate the availability of the inflammatory cytokines in future air spaces. IL‐1 induces synthesis of surfactant proteins, particularly SP‐A and SP‐B, as well as surfactant phospholipids in immature alveolar type II cells [11]. Other inflammatory cytokines and endotoxins of microbial origin have a similar effect in the acceleration of surfactant maturity [13].

The antenatal glucocorticoid (AGC) increases the resilience against immune insults to critical foetal organs and contributes to the synthesis of surfactant phospholipids. Both moderate inflammation and steroids accelerate lung maturity in extremely premature infants, thereby decreasing the risk of RDS and enhancing the resilience against multiorgan damage [14]. The very preterm foetus not exposed to moderate inflammation or AGC has functionally immature alveolar lining at birth. Moderate quantities of corticosteroid and inflammatory cytokines increase and maintain the metabolism and integrity of alveolar surfactant and their effects tend to be additive. Antenatal AGC also activates antioxidants and clearance of lung fluid, decreasing the severity of RDS [13, 14].

Spontaneous differentiation of immature alveolar type II cells is initiated at very premature birth when the inflammatory insult during the first breaths induces the inflammatory cytokines. The differentiation of the surfactant system proceeds during the first 2–5 neonatal days. Simultaneous pro‐inflammatory response by alveolar type II cells generates GM‐CSF [15], attracting monocytes to the alveolar space, where they eventually differentiate into alveolar macrophages. Alveolar type II cells and macrophages interact closely, regulating the turnover and pool size of the extracellular surfactant system and alveolar macrophages are prominent in the immune defence [13]. Both cell types take up inactivated extracellular surfactant (Figure 2).

In healthy neonates, the alveolar surfactant pool is substantial and its turnover is slow. Furthermore, after extremely premature birth, surfactant complex within the alveolar lining is exposed to inhibition and inactivation [13, 14]. Expectant cardiopulmonary management, particularly non‐invasive ventilation contributes to the maintenance of surface activity in the alveolar space. Surfactant collectins, SP‐A and SP‐D, support the functions of pulmonary alveolar macrophages and other immune cells, especially during infections [16, 17]. Notably, current commercial exogenous surfactants lack these highly species‐specific collectins.

Inflammatory Damage and Innate Immune Tolerance in Very Premature Transition

Intra‐amniotic inflammation induces differentiation of alveolar type II cells in very premature fetal lungs. On the other hand, extensive or repeated fetal inflammatory insults are damaging and disrupt successful perinatal adaptation. Although type II alveolar cells are resistant to inflammatory damage, the type I alveolar cells become severely damaged allowing inhibition and inactivation of alveolar surfactant. Alveolar damage involves early chemokine‐induced infiltration of granulocytes. On the other hand, developing tolerance and paresis of monocyte—macrophages render them inefficient in clearance of debris from the alveolar space. The developing neonatal acute respiratory failure (nARDS) is life‐threatening and often complicated by BPD [13].

A typical severe case involves life‐threatening early infections in very premature infants at birth, characterised by low serum CRP and low inflammatory cytokine levels in the most severe cases of nARDS. The cardiopulmonary system, pulmonary microcirculation, alveolar cells and terminal airways are susceptible to damage. Alveolar macrophages are functionally paralysed, while alveolar type II cells exhibit relative resistance against inflammatory injury. Nonetheless, the extracellular surfactant complex is inhibited by serum proteins, leaking to the alveolar lining and inactivated by cellular debris from granulocytes and type I alveolar cells. This perspective aligns with the transient and inadequate response of surfactant treatment in severe pneumonia or in full‐blown hyaline membrane disease.

Immune tolerance or paralysis, defined as the poor response or non‐responsiveness of innate immunity after repeating the exposure to endotoxins. It often develops within the alveolar lining of very preterm foetuses [18], in respiratory distress after birth [19] and in other tissues as well [10, 19]. In the early neonatal period following very premature birth, the immune tolerance of alveolar monocytes may protect against cytokine‐induced inflammatory damage while simultaneously heightening susceptibility to infections.

Severe nARDS following prolonged and severe intrauterine insult often include persistent foetal circulation, airway obstruction and hypoxemia shortly after very preterm birth. It mimics lung hypoplasia as it is often associated with prolonged premature rupture of membranes and lung compression. Proposed pathogenesis involves CA and extensive foetal inflammation, leading to immune paralysis. Most affected infants respond to high concentrations of inhaled nitric oxide at 20–25 ppm, facilitating acute weaning from oxygen and high airway pressures. Acute exposure to a high concentration of nitric oxide promotes recovery from immune paralysis [13, 20].

Recent model studies suggest that epigenetic‐metabolic reprogramming of alveolar immune cells may be NO‐centric and mitochondrial, potentially reinvigorating the poorly responsive or paralysed M2‐macrophages towards functionally active M1‐state. The process may hinge on an epigenetic transformation instigated by acute metabolic changes. These include accumulation of specific tricarboxylic acid cycle metabolites and redox change due to inhibition of the electron transport chain in mitochondrial inner membrane [20, 21].

The paralysis of innate immunity is associated with severe suppression of endogenous synthesis of NO. In severe respiratory failure, dramatic response to inhaled NO associates with acute decrease of pulmonary hypertension and obstruction of peripheral airways as well as a more gradual recovery from the immune paralysis [13]. Nitric oxide instantaneously reacts with iron‐containing proteins. This results in formation of both beneficial NO‐metabolites and highly reactive toxic radicals. Nitric oxide is a medical chameleon, necessitating careful use and the pursuit towards safer alternative treatments.

Extremely Premature Perinatal Transition

Epidemiological data have highlighted the beneficial effects of both primary and secondary prevention strategies of premature births [22, 23]. This important area falls outside the scope of the current discussion.

Research into the early biogenesis of the placenta and its appendices is crucial for understanding the maintenance of pregnancy. Genomic studies have identified multiple candidate genes associated with the length of gestation, with far fewer candidates linked to the arbitrary definition of spontaneous premature birth (SPTB). Several candidate gene variants either promote inflammation or diminish anti‐inflammatory capacity, as observed in studies of single cells from the endometrium or early decidua [24, 25, 26]. This aligns with the proposed analogy of the pathogeneses of spontaneous abortions and SPTBs.

Prevention of SPTB, With History of Failures and Emerging Success

Over the past 80 years, numerous efforts have been made to medically prevent SPTB or delay the premature labour, that is, tocolysis. Many of them, including stilbestrol, thalidomide, alcohol and indomethacin, have failed and had detrimental effects on foetuses, leading to adverse outcomes in newborn infants, children and adults.

Multidisciplinary translational research has been pivotal in advancing modern neonatal‐perinatal medicine from the late 1960s to the early 1990s, resulting in effective prevention and treatment of RDS. Since then, perinatal medicine has increasingly focused on either obstetrics of high‐risk mothers or neonatal medicine of extremely preterm infants. Progress has been made in both fields. Providing the mother with magnesium sulfate treatment shortly before very preterm birth decreases the risk of cerebral palsy (CP) [27]. Prophylactic progesterone treatment initiated before the second half of pregnancy in rare short cervix syndrome has been shown to decrease the risk of SPTB [28]. The treatment appears safe for both the fetus and newborn.

Calcium channel blockers (Nifedipine) and oxytocin antagonists (Atosiban) decrease strong uterine contractions without serious adverse effects, thereby extending the duration of labour and allowing more time for the beneficial effect of antenatal glucocorticoid. Indomethacin prolongs preterm pregnancies for several days but is associated with adverse effects, including constriction of the foetal ductus arteriosus and increased risks of BPD, necrotizing enterocolitis and intraventricular haemorrhage in infants born very premature [13].

A low dose of acetylsalicylic acid starting before the second half of the high‐risk pregnancies developing hypertension and proteinuria decreases the risk of severe pre‐eclampsia. Multiple obstetric treatments in the threat of severe preeclampsia is outside the topic of recent review [29].

Prediction of Premature Birth

Mothers at increased risk of SPTB due to previous premature births or imminent premature labour are evaluated for the risk. Methods such as cervical length measurement and biochemical tests, including foetal fibronectin (fFN), placental alpha‐macroglobulin‐1 (PAMG‐1) and phosphorylated IGF‐binding protein‐1 (phIGFBP‐1), are utilised for predicting SPTB [30]. Unfortunately, these and other tests are neither very sensitive nor specific in early prediction of SPTB. Multiple reports have highlighted variable success rates for serum, cervix, or fornix biomarkers and other physical or radiological evaluations in predicting SPTB [31].

SPTB is a syndrome that may be associated with ascending, hematogenous, or salpingeal infection/inflammation and often presents without apparent infection or inflammation. Therefore, a combination of biomarkers, alongside assessments of cervical and placental morphology, may eventually yield a more accurate prediction of SPTB. Risk of SPTB is affected by various acquired or genetic factors that either individually or in combination activate the labour and influence the length of pregnancy.

Antenatal Glucocorticoid (AGC) Therapy

Lung maturity prior to and during spontaneous term and near‐term delivery is accelerated by increasing foetal corticosteroid levels. This is due to an increase in foetal corticosteroid synthesis and an increase in placental corticosteroid permeability. Fluorinated glucocorticoids, such as betamethasone or dexamethasone, cross the placental barrier of immature foetuses. They are indicated before 34 weeks of gestation in spontaneous or elective preterm delivery. The duration of beneficial AGC effect is 1–7 days prior to anticipated delivery. According to meta‐analyses of past RCTs, AGC reduces the risk of severe intraventricular haemorrhage, RDS, necrotizing enterocolitis and neonatal mortality. AGC tends to decrease the severity of early neonatal infections and of BPD as well. A single dosage of AGC has not demonstrated detectable short‐ or long‐term adverse effects to infants born very preterm. However, infants of early term‐ and near‐term mothers treated with AGC exhibit an increased risk of hypoglycaemia [32]. AGC treatment is indicated before elective high‐risk term or near‐term delivery when amniotic fluid surfactant analysis indicates lung immaturity.

About 50% of births following AGC treatment occur either before or after the window of maximal efficacy in preventing neonatal morbidities. AGC administered within 12–24 h before very premature birth shows a small positive impact on neonatal adaptation. Among term births, 2%–5% of infants have been exposed to AGC and these children may face an increased risk of cognitive and psychological adverse effects during school age, even when adjusted for the morbidities of unexposed siblings. It remains unclear whether these adverse effects arise from imminent premature delivery or whether AGC is the cause [8].

Current AGC‐drugs, containing dexamethasone or betamethasone phosphate, result in an excessive transient increase in maternal glucocorticoid levels. Intramuscular betamethasone acetate is very slowly released to circulation and it suppresses endogenous maternal corticosteroid production for over a week. Retreatment with AGC decreases the risk of acute respiratory distress, without other benefits. In addition, AGC decreases dosage‐dependently the birth weight and head circumference [33]. Currently, no more than one repeat dose of AGC in imminent preterm birth is allowed before 32 weeks of gestation and more than 7 days after the initial AGC dose [8].

Ongoing studies aim to determine optimal AGC dosage and assess its long‐term safety [34, 35].

Towards Prevention of Very Early SPTB

The reproduction, including anatomy and regulation of labour onset, is species‐specific. During the first trimester of human pregnancy, the corpus luteum produces progesterone, which is later synthesised in the placental syncytiotrophoblast that develops during the late first trimester. Syncytiotrophoblast, the multinucleated outer layer of placental villi, grows and differentiates thereafter (Figure 3). Progesterone plays a prominent role in maintaining pregnancy. Serum progesterone levels gradually increase throughout gestation, even during preterm and term labour. The syncytiotrophoblast is involved in the delivery of oxygen, nutrients, micronutrients and immunoglobulins through maternal blood by diffusion, specific transporters, or vesicles. Nuclear progesterone receptors are prominent in stromal cells within the basal decidua. Progesterone binding to nuclear progesterone receptors increases anti‐inflammatory gene expression by epigenetic mechanism. IL‐1, a known trigger of SPTB, modifies the expression of nuclear progesterone receptors, diminishing their anti‐inflammatory effects [37].

Some factors influencing the onset of SPTB. Predisposing factors are mostly inflammatory, leading to activation of prostaglandin synthesis, elevation of oxytocin and activation of proteases. Although too early cervical ripening and strong uterine contractions eventually cause the SPTB, predisposing factors are multiple and the exact mechanisms regulating early onset of preterm labour is insufficiently understood. About one third of the predisposing factors are genetic and two thirds of them are acquired. Ascending infection is a well‐recognised factor that triggers SPTB. In idiopathic SPTB, neither ascending infection nor chorioamnionitis are evident. Placenta and particularly its attachments to uterus and foetal compartment are active areas of research. Normal Human Labour and Delivery

Human bipedalism has led to a narrowing of the pelvis, which may obstruct the passage of an enlarged fetal head [38]. This relatively recent evolutionary change could have influenced the duration of pregnancy and the regulation of labour onset, including the high risk of SPTB. During the term labour, a large intrauterine mass, combined with intermittent contractions and foetal movements, generates stress around the cervix and foetal membranes. These stressors induce immune mediators, including cytokines, which increase prostaglandin synthesis, enhancing uterine contractions, contributing to cervical dilatation and delivery of the foetus. Extensive uterine contractions facilitate detachment of the placenta and foetal membranes during the third stage of labour.

In multiple pregnancies, excessive intrauterine size may lead to increased risk of early immune activation, onset of near‐term delivery and accelerated lung maturity [39].

Phenotypes of Preterm Births

Several phenotypes of SPTB have been identified. However, the pathogenesis remains controversial and effective medical prevention is not available [36, 40]. Ascending, salpingeal and hematogenous pathways are possible entry routes of infection and inflammation (Figure 3). Events such as periodontal disease or neuro‐psychiatric stress are associated with an increased risk of SPTB.

About a third of SPTB cases are associated with ascending infection or inflammation. At least as many SPTB cases show little evidence of inflammation.

In CA, ascending inflammatory mediators disseminate through the foetal membranes into the amniotic fluid, airways, gastrointestinal tract and to other foetal compartments, triggering a foetal inflammatory response. The phenotype is heterogeneous. Outcomes of infections include foetal death, early neonatal septic shock and premature neonatal adaptation without serious lung disease. Conversely, in histological CA without placental defects, the extremely preterm affected newborns have a low incidence of RDS and tend to have a better neurological outcome at age two than the gestation controls [41]. In idiopathic SPTB affecting one third of very premature births, inflammatory biomarkers are typically not elevated, while abnormalities such as capillary bleeding in decidua or partial placental detachment may be observed.

Genetic predisposition accounts for 15 to 40% of total SPTB risk, with maternal genetic contribution of 80%–95% (Figure 3). Based on further interpretation of genome wide association studies (GWAS), most foetal SPTB‐predisposing alleles are maternal rather than paternal in origin [25]. Previous SPTB is linked to a 3–6‐fold increase in SPTB risk during subsequent pregnancies. The interaction between genetic and acquired factors influences the SPTB risk. However, the complex puzzle of gene–gene and gene–environment interactions is far from understood.

Genetic memory of acquired immunity in vertebrates plays a crucial role in enhancing resistance against specific infections. Acquired immunity, particularly relevant vaccinations, prevents specific infections, such as rubella that increased the risk of SPTB among the infections of the TORCH complex. Screening of the carriers, followed by prophylaxis of ascending Group B Streptococcus infections is another example of successful prevention of life‐threatening perinatal infection. However, antibiotics are not beneficial for overall prevention of preterm births. Genetic variants affecting the acquired immunity may influence susceptibility to premature birth. Particularly, the immune reactions between semi‐allogenic foetal and maternal tissues may predispose to prematurity [36]. Maternal anti‐foetal immune reactions are among proposed causes of SPTB, although there is still limited evidence.

In severe pre‐eclampsia, impaired adaptive T cell tolerance with Th imbalance has been reported [42]. This suggests that in preeclampsia, alloimmunity and dysregulation of adaptive immunity play a role in pathogenesis. Elective very preterm birth without labour in severe pre‐eclampsia often takes place due to foetal growth restriction or asphyxia. These cases are associated with abnormal early decidualisation [43]. Besides pre‐eclampsia, multiple other rare causes lead to elective, extremely premature deliveries. They are not included in the present discussion.

New Approaches for Prevention of SPTB

Maternal immunisation is an effective intervention for prevention of specific infections increasing the risk of SPTB. Recently COVID‐19 vaccination has decreased the risk of SPTB during the pandemic. Expanding immunisations against common pathogens during pregnancies is a feasible approach.

Current research on medical prevention of SPTB focuses on defenses against infections and inflammation. The aim is to characterise the molecular structure and function of critical tissues and individual cells potentially involved in the onset of SPTB [44]. In SPTB cases, defining the roles of excessive expression of proteins in various contexts is crucial. Toll‐like receptors are transmembrane receptors that recognise patterns of microbial structures and serve as the major pathway triggering the proinflammatory cytokine cascade involved in the onset of premature labour and delivery. Toll‐like receptors are expressed in immune cells, particularly in macrophages and natural killer cells and in several non‐immune cells. Silencing of proteins that promote SPTB may provide prevention of prematurity. Furthermore, defining specific deficiencies in critical intrauterine tissues in SPTB may identify factors that strengthen immunity against imminent SPTB.

Comparisons of proteomics between placentas from idiopathic SPTB and those from spontaneous term births or elective preterm births without labour reveal multiple differences. The chorionic plate of placenta at SPTB displays contrasting differences in protein composition, compared to elective preterm births without labour or to spontaneous term births. In the basal plate of placenta at SPTB (Figure 3), there are consistent differences in several proteins in both comparisons [45]. Study the exomes encoding these SPTB‐associating proteins for rare, SPTB‐associated alleles revealed among others HSPA5, which exhibited excessive expression in decidua at SPTB. Epigenetic silencing HSPA5 expression in a basal decidua cell line reduced inflammation‐related gene expression [46]. In contrast, SERPINA1 gene, which encodes α‐1‐antitrypsin, demonstrated a damaging exome variant in recurrent SPTB cases. Silencing SERPINA1 expression in a basal decidua cell line affects genes predisposing to SPTB [45]. Alfa‐1‐antitrypsin, a multifunctional serine protease inhibitor, plays additional roles in autoimmune disease protection and stress hormone regulation. Notably, a large GWAS study discovered that maternal SERPINA1 PI*Z variant significantly correlates with gestational length and increases the odds of SPTB [47]. In the animal model, α‐1‐antitrypsin, supplemented to pregnant α‐1‐antitrypsin‐deficient transgenic mice, was concentrated in placenta. It decreased lipopolysaccharide‐induced premature births, prolonged the pregnancies and maintained foetal growth in comparison to the transgenic animals exposed to placebo and lipopolysaccharide [47].

In human pregnancies, low α‐1‐antitrypsin content in the basal decidua in the majority of the SPTB cases is due to acquired rather than genetic deficiency [45]. Serum α‐1‐antitrypsin concentrations significantly increase during the late first trimester and remain increased during the pregnancy [48]. The α‐1‐antitrypsin deficiency in basal decidua at birth in SPTB may be causally related to premature birth [45, 47].

Alpha‐1‐antitrypsin augmentation therapy for delaying progressive lung emphysema due to genetic deficiency of α‐1‐antitrypsin is an accepted therapy in the USA. Analogous augmentation therapies may eventually become available for prevention of SPTB in extremely early pregnancies.

Management of Extremely Premature Newborn

During the past 60 years, effective respiratory treatments have vastly increased intact survival of premature infants. Most of these premature infants were born after 27 weeks of gestation [8]. Present review deals with selective aspects of perinatal transition of infants born before 28 weeks of gestation (ELGA) and particularly borderline viable infants, born during 22–24 weeks of gestation.

Medical, Societal and Family Factors

According to population‐based registers, mortality is edging lower among the ELGA infants. However, serious morbidities among borderline viable infants, including BPD, necrotizing enterocolitis, intraventricular haemorrhage, and chronic neuro‐sensory conditions, have barely decreased. The lowered incidence of CP has not been accompanied by lower rates of chronic neurosensory, behavioural and cognitive morbidities and they are often recognised first in school age. Important risk factors include short length of pregnancy, intrauterine growth retardation (IUGR) and male sex. In some countries, best efforts are made to maintain life regardless of the degree of immaturity, while others tend to have a higher gestation limit for active treatment.

Primary concern of mother is often the survival of her immature fetus and infant. Parents also need realistic information about the likely long‐term outcomes. The questions of the mother require open answers and her important roles, starting early after the delivery, are encouraged [49].

Delayed Cord Clamping

Delayed cord clamping, particularly the physiological cord clamping, is beneficial to the premature newborn [8]. Physiological cord clamping means a delay in cord clamping until spontaneous breathing is established. During the third stage of labour, simultaneous early circulatory and respiratory transition provides interactive benefits [50]. According to meta‐analyses, transfusion from the placenta during late cord clamping increases blood volume and haematocrit, promoting hemodynamic parameters and increasing survival of extremely premature infants [51]. The optimal duration of cord clamping and respiratory management during late clamping in extremely preterm infants is currently investigated [52]. This may be critical in borderline viability, since spontaneous breathing may fail or excessive blood may be introduced from the large placenta to the tiny newborn during the third stage of labour. At present, it is too early to confirm the potential benefits and risks of delaying cord clamping during perinatal transition of borderline viable infants.

Surfactant Treatment

Prophylactic surfactant at birth to extremely preterm infants was indicated based on RCTs during the early years of surfactant therapy. After most of these trials, AGC prophylaxis became commonly applied. Introduction of T‐piece resuscitation at birth allowed CPAP ventilation using more modest inspiratory pressures than during bagging. Introduction of early management using higher CPAP or end‐expiratory pressures is beneficial. However, this management may be insufficient in case the infant does not have surfactant in the alveolar lining [13].

Intubation for surfactant treatment followed by brief ventilation and rapid extubating (INSURE) became preferred management practice during the early 2000s [53]. A more recent meta‐analysis revealed that surfactant treatment early in RDS demonstrates less risk of chronic lung disease or deaths than prophylactic surfactant [54]. In this meta‐analysis, RCTs that defined the level of early CPAP were included. Furthermore, AGC treatment was common. The Surfactant, Positive Pressure and Oxygenation Trial (SUPPORT) [55] provided > 70% of all infants included in the meta‐analysis [54]. The infants in the SUPPORT trial were born at 24.0–27.9 weeks of gestation. They were randomised to prophylactic surfactant and selective surfactant treatment groups. After intubation at birth, prophylactic surfactant treatment was allowed until the age of 1 h after birth. The control infants received CPAP at a recommended 5 cm H2O from birth and early surfactant was given after the first hour of life in early RDS. However, infants requiring resuscitation and mechanical ventilation at birth received surfactant, regardless of the treatment strata [55]. According to the meta‐analysis, early CPAP and selective surfactant treatment in developing RDS was more beneficial than prophylactic surfactant [54].

More recently, the less invasive surfactant administration technique, not requiring intubation, has emerged as a popular strategy. This approach was first used by Lars Victorin. It was further developed by Angela Kribs to the Less Invasive Surfactant Administration (LISA) technique, involving surfactant administration through a catheter introduced into the trachea without intubation. The LISA technique, in comparison to intubation and surfactant administration, decreased the incidence of BPD and tended to increase survival without BPD compared to intubation and surfactant administration [56, 57]. LISA or other techniques not requiring intubation have become popular.

In borderline viability, less‐invasive surfactant administration and non‐invasive ventilation are demanding techniques that often fail after birth. According to recent reports, acute failure of non‐invasive ventilation is associated with a deterioration in neurological outcomes [58]. The benefits and risks of these non‐invasive ventilation and less‐invasive surfactant administration techniques have not been critically evaluated for borderline viable infants [59].

Managing Ventilation

Non‐invasive ventilation does not require intubation. To provide CPAP, non‐invasive mechanical ventilation techniques are currently given via nasal devices or using high‐flow cannulas [8]. Non‐invasive ventilation without exogenous surfactant is often successful. This is possible since many ELGA infants receive a full course of AGC and SPTB may additionally be complicated by CA. However, non‐invasive ventilation often fails among ELGA infants and may cause cardio‐pulmonary events. Brain injuries associated with these events were best predicted by a very short duration of pregnancy [60]. In trials evaluating the efficacy and safety of conservative management versus non‐invasive management, only a few infants are born before 26 weeks of gestation.

Challenging aspects in studies comparing different ventilation practices include lack of blinding and variable experience of the management teams. Of the modes of mechanical ventilation of intubated infants, volume‐targeted ventilation is recommended for extremely premature infants [8, 61]. High‐frequency ventilation is indicated for ventilation of stiff lungs with very low compliance. Acute treatment of severe respiratory distress using high‐frequency ventilation, combined with nitric oxide, may be effective [62], unless the respiratory tract is anatomically immature.

Neurally Adjusted Ventilatory Assist (NAVA) has gradually gained support in maintaining both invasive and non‐invasive mechanical ventilation for ELGA infants. NAVA takes into consideration the neuronal regulation of the strength of individual contractions of the diaphragm, adjusting the pressures of mechanical ventilation for individual breaths [63, 64].

Neonatal Corticosteroid

Early neonatal dexamethasone treatment improved cardiorespiratory adaptation and decreased the risk of BPD, but it was disputed when evidence on the increased risk of CP and cognitive problems during follow‐up became evident [65]. Despite a decrease in glucocorticoid dosage, administration of hydrocortisone using physiological stress dosage, synthetic steroids other than dexamethasone or combining corticosteroid with surfactant still raises concerns about efficacy and risks of acute intestinal perforations or long‐term adverse neurological effects [66].

A short course of pharmacologic corticosteroid, given multiple weeks after birth to extremely preterm‐born, ventilation and oxygen‐dependent infants, increases survival without BPD. When the estimated BPD risk is at least 50%, short corticosteroid treatment is expected to increase both weaning from ventilation and survival free of CP [67].

Managing Patent Ductus Arteriosus (PDA)

According to population‐based retrospective data, very preterm infants requiring closure of PDA have an increased risk of life‐threatening BPD and other serious morbidities [68]. Recent randomised trials have focused on early administration of either non‐steroidal anti‐inflammatory drugs, ibuprofen or indomethacin, that inhibit cyclo‐oxygenase [6], or paracetamol, that is an inhibitor of the peroxidase, required for prostaglandin synthesis [7]. The early administration of either ibuprofen or paracetamol increased early closure of PDA but failed to decrease the risk of death or serious neonatal diseases in extremely preterm infants [6, 7]. Therefore, the expectant management rather than mandatory early or prophylactic treatment of PDA is currently recommended.

Early administration of non‐steroidal anti‐inflammatory drug has multiple adverse effects, including oliguria, pulmonary ischemia and intestinal perforation. Recently prophylactic paracetamol was reported to increase the risk of cholestasis without hepatitis (6.4% vs. 2.6%, p < 0.05). The risk of cholestasis was not associated with either paracetamol dosage or borderline viability [7].

Hypothesis‐Generating Observations on Management of Borderline Viable Infants

Data on the management of borderline viable infants is limited. There are very few published prospective studies, randomised trials, or RCT strata focusing on this population [69]. Experience on management and outcomes of borderline viable infants has been documented [70]. In Japan, many immature infants are proactively treated and the survival rates are impressive [71]. Cognitive, psycho‐sensory and neurodevelopmental delays are commonly observed among infants born 22–24 weeks of pregnancy. Serious cognitive defects may become evident first during school age [72].

In Finland, extensive data has been prospectively collected on the first hospitalisation of all very premature infants born since 2004 by the National Medical Institute. The treatment is centralised in five university hospitals. Resuscitation at postconceptional weeks 22 may be performed only in selected cases. The overall survival of infants born at 22–23 weeks of pregnancy is poor. Over multiple years, a single centre has maintained stable neonatal outcomes for infants born between 23 and 25 weeks of gestation. The annual mortality rate during the initial hospitalisation for those born at 23 weeks ranges from 30% to 50%, while survival rates for those born at 24–25 weeks have ranged between 80% and 100%. Notably, the incidence of moderate to severe BPD among infants born at 23–25 weeks is low (10%–15%) and discharge from hospital takes place without home oxygen at 41 to 45 postconceptional weeks.

The protocol was developed by Dr. T. Saarela, head of neonatology team in Oulu university hospital. The cardio‐respiratory management protocol for these infants has remained stable for several years with the following features. A senior neonatologist supervises the resuscitation team at birth, regardless of the time of birth. All infants are intubated and surfactant is administered at birth. If necessary, surfactant treatment is repeated 1–3 times within the first 5 days. During the first 10–30 min after birth, manual ventilation is provided using a T‐piece, with CPAP. Short (< 0.5 s) intermittent pressure‐controlled breaths are given when deemed to be necessary. Stable mechanical ventilation is initiated at intensive care unit. Aim is to start neuronal control of inspiration pressures by NAVA ventilation, as soon as feasible after birth. High‐frequency ventilation and inhaled nitric oxide treatments are given in severe respiratory distress, unless anatomical immaturity is evident. In case the gas exchange improves, weaning from inhaled NO and high‐frequency ventilation is aimed within a few hours. Regardless of the response, prolonged inhaled NO treatment is not indicated. Fluid intake is limited during the first week of life (70–100 mL/kg, including saline washout), while minimizing insensible water loss. Early intestinal feeding, when possible, begins with maternal colostrum on day one. Early, low‐dose paracetamol is administered for 7–10 days to infants exhibiting signs of pain.

A report from Japan revealed some interesting details in management after birth at 22–23 weeks of gestation. For instance, the team standardises the volume of blood transfused from the umbilical cord to the newborn [71].

In a phase 2 RCT led by Dr. Aikio on ELGA infants, the use of early low‐dose paracetamol for 9 days was associated with early PDA closure [73]. Pharmacodynamic studies demonstrated that the increase in the dose of prophylactic paracetamol for 5 days improved PDA closure in ELGA infants [74]. In the following large stratified RCT, prophylactic paracetamol to most immature infants (23–26 weeks of gestation) tended to associate with a lower risk of necrotizing enterocolitis and a higher survival rate [7].

Discussion

The risks of chronic diseases among borderline viable infants born at 22–24 weeks of gestation are staggering. While mortality has been decreasing, chronic morbidities remain prevalent and many disorders of the nervous system become evident first in school age and even acute somatic diseases tend to become chronic [72]. Recently, less invasive management strategies for extremely premature infants are being investigated, yielding promising results in survival without BPD [8]. Management protocols for these infants are often based on RCTs on infants born later in gestation. Further prospective multicentre studies focusing on perinatal transition, neonatal management and long‐term outcomes of these vulnerable infants are indicated.

Efforts to prevent early deaths and serious chronic morbidities include extensive research aiming to prolong extremely short pregnancies. The progress has been slow. Besides vaccinations against perinatal infections, new inhibitors that prevent premature delivery are under investigation. Furthermore, interest is focusing on weaknesses in the immune defence functions, as well as on mechanisms how acquired and genetic variants interactively influence the length of pregnancy and foetal growth.

Basal plate of the placenta contains immune cells capable of triggering the onset of SPTB [36] and influencing the foetal growth [43]. Inflammation‐induced proteolysis is one possible mechanism. The low content of major protease inhibitor within the basal plate associates with SPTB [45] and the damaging SERPINA1 gene variant associates with increased odds of short duration of pregnancy [47]. In mice, on the other hand, α‐1‐antitrypsin supplementation to animals with deficient Serpina1 expression decreases inflammation‐induced preterm births [47]. In humans, α‐1‐antitrypsin augmentation delays the progression of chronic hereditary lung emphysema. The same natural human product may prove to be beneficial in very high‐risk pregnancies. Supplementation of a defect in the innate immunity may safely prolong extremely short human pregnancies.

Ongoing research aims to discover therapies that strengthen anti‐inflammatory defences against SPTB‐promoting pathways or inhibit SPTB‐inducing effects of the inflammatory pathways. The design inhibitors for inflammatory receptors involved in spontaneous premature labour and delivery have yielded encouraging results [75]. Allosteric inhibitors of the IL‐1 receptor, a key signalling pathway of SPTB, have demonstrated the ability to prevent acute inflammation‐induced preterm birth in animal models, with evidence of pharmacologic selectivity and no detectable adverse effects. Extensive studies are required for consolidating the anticipated efficacy and safety.

New strategies for SPTB prevention carry the potential of remarkable benefits and unforeseen adverse effects. Treatments aiming to restore critical deficiencies in intrauterine defence mechanisms, or to inhibit activities causing damaging inflammatory responses in maternal and foetal compartments, are promising. First extensive clinical trials with long term follow‐up may ultimately provide effective and safe management practices prolonging the extremely premature pregnancies. Prevention of all multiple causes of extremely premature births in the foreseeable future is unlikely. Therefore, the progress in available treatments and introduction of analogous immune‐active drugs in intensive care of extremely premature infants remain an essential part in perinatal medicine.

Conclusion

After the introduction of successful respiratory management of very preterm infants, progress in increasing intact neuro‐sensory and somatic survival of borderline viable infants has been slow and prevention of extremely preterm births has failed. This review emphasises the need for extensive multidisciplinary investigation, aiming towards enhancing perinatal viability and intact survival.
