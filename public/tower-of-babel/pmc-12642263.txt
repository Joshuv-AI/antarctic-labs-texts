A case of an attic lesion presenting with unilateral hearing loss, diagnosed as a meningioma: a case report and review of the literature
Authors: Farzin Davoodi, Zahra Babamohamadi, Mahdi Khajavi, Farhad Mokhtarinejad, Mohaddese Fallahi, Meysam Abdollahi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Meningiomas are among the most prevalent tumor types found within the central nervous system, specifically arising from arachnoid cap cells. These tumors account for approximately 24% to 30% of all primary intracranial tumors. Conversely, extra-cranial (ectopic) meningiomas have been documented in various anatomical sites. Such ectopic meningiomas are commonly situated near sensory organs within the head and neck region, including the orbit, skull, paranasal sinuses, and nasopharynx. Furthermore, they may arise along nerve distributions and adjacent to the spine. However, it is noteworthy that ectopic meningiomas are rarely found in the lungs or elbow joints [1].

Meningiomas are generally more common in females and have been linked to other malignancies in women. The explanation may lie in the presence of estrogen and progesterone receptors on the tumor surface [2].

Meningiomas are often seen as sessile or lenticular, well-defined extra-axial mass lesions that have a broad-based attachment to the dura. When viewed on noncontrast computed tomography (CT), these tumors typically show hyperdensity. Moreover, T1-weighted magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) reveals that meningiomas have iso- to hypointense characteristics, while T2-weighted images indicate they may appear iso- to hyperintense [3]. Meningothelial, fibrous, and transitional meningiomas can display varying intensities on diffusion-weighted imaging; therefore, the apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) values can vary considerably. Most of these tumors usually do not show any diffusion restriction or facilitation on diffusion-weighted images when compared with the adjacent brain tissue [4].

The histopathologic diagnosis is typically straightforward; however, challenges can arise when the tumor is located in unexpected areas. The histological characteristics of meningioma are primarily defined by the presence of spherical formations of meningothelial cells, which may undergo mineralization, resulting in the formation of psamomatous bodies. A notable feature is the reduction of central chromatin, alongside the occurrence of intranuclear cytoplasmic pseudoinclusions, which are defined by cytoplasmic invaginations within the nuclei. These histological traits, in conjunction with the absence of brain invasion and the infrequent presence of mitotic figures, delineate the typical histological pattern observed in subtypes of tumors classified as benign [5]. the differential diagnosis may include conditions such as paraganglioma [6], melanoma [7], schwannoma [8], lymphoma [9], and histiocytosis [10, 11], among others. Previous literature on meningiomas found in unusual locations is primarily limited to individual cases or small case series. This case report discusses a primary case of meningioma that presented with unilateral hearing loss.

Case report

A 48-year-old Iranian female patient presented to our tertiary care center with a 1.5-year history of left ear hearing loss and tinnitus. A thorough physical examination was conducted, revealing no extra-temporal findings. The tympanic membrane appeared intact. Results from the Rinne test were positive in both ears, while the Weber test demonstrated lateralization to the right side. The evaluation of cranial nerves yielded normal results. An audiogram indicated a sensorineural hearing loss of 45 decibels in the left ear (Fig. 1).

The PTA shows unilateral sensory neural hearing loss in the left ear

Imaging studies revealed a mass-like lesion located in the left epitympanum, which extended to the prosac and surrounded the malleus and incus, while maintaining the integrity of the ossicular chain (Fig. 2).

A: T1 + GAD coronal MRI indicates an enhanced lesion in the left attic. B: T2 coronal MRI indicates a hyper-intense mass in the left attic. C: hypo-dense mass in the left attic in the coronal CT scan. D: hypo-dense mass in the left attic in axial CT scan

A thorough clinical evaluation for sensorineural hearing loss was conducted. The results of the following diagnostic tests—antinuclear antibodies (ANA), antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA), double-stranded DNA (dsDNA), rheumatoid factor (RF), VDRL, and human immunodeficiency virus (HIV)—did not indicate any specific abnormalities.

Upon reviewing the results of physical examinations, imaging, and diagnostic tests, we identified an occupying lesion responsible for the observed hearing loss. The hyper-intensity noted in T2-weighted images, along with enhancement following gadolinium administration at T1, facilitates a differential diagnosis. Potential candidates include hemangioma or other vascular-occupying lesions, as the mass demonstrated significant enhancement with gadolinium. Another consideration is schwannoma, which similarly exhibits notable gadolinium enhancement. Additionally, meningioma remains a possibility; however, we did not observe the characteristic speckled hyper-dense appearance typically associated with this condition in the CT scan.

The patient was prepared for middle ear exploration surgery. The findings during the surgical procedure were consistent with the results obtained from imaging studies. A total mastoidectomy was performed, which facilitated exposure of the antrum. During the operation, a semi-vascular lesion was identified, occupying the antrum, attic, and aditus, with anterior extension toward the tensor tympani muscle. The lesion was carefully shaved from the ossicular chain. The defect observed in the tegmen tympani was less than 1 cm, through which the tumor penetrated into the middle ear. An effort was made to identify the attachment of the mass to the meningeal membrane. We resected the involved portion of the meningeal membrane and successfully excised the tumor in its entirety. Following this procedure, we assessed for cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leakage. As there was no evidence of leakage, we proceeded with primary closure of the post-auricular incision. The resulting specimen was submitted for pathological analysis.

Pathological findings revealed a low-grade meningioma, characterized by monomorphic neoplastic meningothelial cells displaying round nuclei and eosinophilic cytoplasm. Additionally, some cells exhibited intranuclear pseudoinclusions and a syncytial appearance, accompanied by lobulated architecture. The presence of bone involvement was also noted (Fig. 3).

D: IHC (positive diffuse nuclear staining for progesterone receptor (PR)). E: IHC (negative for chromogranin). F: IHC (patchy positive for epithelial membrane antigen (EMA))

Following a comprehensive histopathological analysis utilizing hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, several differential diagnoses were considered for this case, including paraganglioma, schwannoma, benign vascular lesion, and meningioma. To arrive at a definitive diagnosis, immunohistochemical analysis was requested.

In the initial phase of the immunohistochemistry (IHC) assessment, the pathologist examined the tissue sample for synaptophysin, chromogranins (specifically chromogranin A), and Ki-67. The results indicated that synaptophysin and chromogranins were negative, with Ki-67 showing a positivity of only 1%. Consequently, both paraganglioma and schwannoma were excluded from the differential diagnosis.

Upon further examination, the pathologist noted the presence of vascular spaces and subsequently requested CD31 staining, which returned a negative result, thereby ruling out a benign vascular lesion. In the concluding phase of the diagnostic process, progesterone receptor (PR) and epithelial membrane antigen (EMA) were assessed to confirm the diagnosis of meningioma.

Owing to the limited size of the tissue sample, further analysis was scheduled to evaluate the critical markers necessary for establishing the definitive diagnosis; however, STAT6 was not assessed. Additionally, claudin was not utilized, as it is typically more applicable in the context of malignant tumors (Fig. 3).

The patient has ultimately received a diagnosis of a meningioma located in the middle ear.

Follow-up indicated no recurrence or hearing deterioration in 6 months, and since the mass was completely excised without brain extension, radiotherapy was considered unnecessary. The patient is scheduled to undergo an MRI examination again in 6 months.

Discussion

Unilateral hearing loss may arise from a multitude of etiologies. Common causes encompass sensorineural hearing loss, acoustic neuroma [12], meningitis [13], and drug-induced toxicity [14], among others. For otolaryngologists, it is imperative to consider less frequent causes, including tumors that are typically not localized within the middle ear. Case reports have documented the occurrence of meningiomas within the middle ear, whether as primary tumors [15] or as extensions from adjacent anatomical locations [16].

In 2021, Liu Y.J. and colleagues published a case series involving seven patients who presented with unilateral moderate hearing loss over a course of 10 years. Initially, these cases were predominantly misdiagnosed as middle ear effusion; however, they were ultimately determined to be instances of unilateral middle ear meningioma. The authors noted that primary meningiomas located in the middle ear are typically unilateral, with the predominant complaint being moderate conductive hearing loss that may persist for a duration of 3 months to 5 years, generally occurring without any other symptoms during the initial consultation.

Biopsies must be performed with caution for specific pathological lesions, such as middle ear paragangliomas or facial nerve schwannomas, owing to the associated risks of significant hemorrhage and potential facial paralysis [12].

Nicolay et al. (2014) reported in a case study that intracranial meningiomas, which extend into the internal acoustic canal, frequently infiltrate the inner ear, leading to sensorineural hearing loss, tinnitus, vertigo, or facial nerve paralysis. Additionally, the direct spread of an intra-osseous meningioma through the tegmen tympani into the mastoid or middle ear cavity may occur. Such dissemination can result in symptoms including conductive hearing loss and chronic otitis media. These clinical manifestations are attributed to the tumor’s encroachment into the middle ear cavity, which obstructs the Eustachian tube. In certain instances, a secretory variant of meningioma, referred to as secretory meningioma (SM), may also be implicated in this condition. This rare benign subtype of meningioma, classified as World Health Organization Grade I, is characterized by eosinophilic, periodic acid-Schiff (PAS)-positive globular intracellularr pseudopsamomas that manifest as inclusions within intracytoplasmic lumina lined by microvilli [17].

In their case series in 2021, C.W. Lee et al. identified fifteen distinct causes of rapidly progressive hearing loss. These causes can be categorized into several etiological groups: intracranial conditions, which include meningioma, lymphoma, metastatic deposits, cavernous angioma, meningitis, and superficial siderosis; paraneoplastic syndromes associated with small cell lung carcinoma and thymoma; inflammatory or autoimmune disorders, such as autoimmune inner-ear disease, sarcoidosis, vasculitis, and Sjögren’s syndrome; infectious diseases, including syphilis and human immunodeficiency virus; as well as medication-induced factors [18].

Several reports have documented cases of middle ear meningioma that presented with middle ear effusion. In these instances, all patients exhibited conductive hearing loss, with the majority ultimately diagnosed with the secretory subtype of meningioma upon final pathological evaluation [19–21].

This case report presents an instance of an attic lesion that manifested as sensory hearing loss. No mass was detected over the intact tympanic membrane, and there was neither a history nor evidence of otorrhea. Radiologic evaluations revealed a hypo-dense occupying lesion in the left attic in the CT scan, with MRI scans indicating a hyper-intense mass in T2 that showed enhancement with gadolinium in T1 sequences. On the basis of these findings, a decision was made to perform a biopsy under general anesthesia. During the surgical procedure, the semi-vascular mass was completely excised, and the pathological examination confirmed the diagnosis of meningioma.

In this case report, we emphasize the significance of considering rare etiologies of hearing loss. In this context, there are several technical considerations related to surgical intervention. Firstly, in cases involving middle ear meningioma, it is crucial to ascertain the extent of the mass and its origin and attachment to the meningeal membrane to facilitate complete excision and minimize the risk of recurrence.

Furthermore, it is necessary to remove the affected portion of the meningeal membrane and to monitor for cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leakage during the procedure. In instances of CSF leakage, it is imperative to repair any resulting dural defects. Given the rarity of this pathology and the associated technical nuances, this report holds significant value.

Conclusions

Primary tympanic meningiomas, especially when the tympanic membrane is intact, are often at risk of misdiagnosis. They may present with symptoms such as hearing loss or persistent middle ear effusion. When these symptoms recur and resemble secretory otitis media, examining the tympanic membrane can provide valuable insights. It is crucial to conduct timely imaging—such as a computed tomography (CT) scan or MRI—to rule out any middle ear-occupying lesions and enable early intervention. Otolaryngologists should always consider uncommon causes of hearing loss to prevent misdiagnosis.
