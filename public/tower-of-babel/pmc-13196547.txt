Upright versus supine positioning for breast and lung radiotherapy: a pilot study on patient comfort, experience and lung function
Authors: S Boisbouvier, Anthony Criscuolo, Tracy Underwood, V Grégoire
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Overall, breast and lung cancer patients had less discomfort in upright compared to Supine positioning.•The majority of the patients preferred upright positioning psychologically, for physical comfort, and for normal breathing.•Tidal Volume and Inspiratory Capacity were larger in supine compared to upright positioning.•Vital capacity measurements were similar for upright and Supine positioning.•Deep Inspiration Breath Hold was feasible for this patient cohort, both upright and supine.Keywords: Radiotherapy, Supine positioning, Upright positioning, Comfort, Experience, Lung function parameters

Introduction

Lung and breast radiotherapy are conventionally delivered with patients in a supine position using a beam delivery system that rotates around them on a gantry. Patients are generally setup with bilateral arm abduction supported by an armrest or a personalized immobilization device, ensuring beam access, avoidance of the arms, reproducibility and stability [1], [2], [3], [4], [5]. Upright radiotherapy is attractive as it offers the opportunity to use fixed treatment beams, which are associated with more compact and cost-effective treatment rooms for particle therapy as well as photon therapy [6], [7].

Despite growing interest, the technological development of upright radiotherapy has progressed slowly, with upright patient positioners and CT scanners for radiotherapy only entering the commercial market in recent years. Clinical data on upright positioning remain sparse, largely originating from in-house systems in particle therapy and sometimes based on publications that are nearly 40 years old. For the thoracic area, upright positioning has been associated with an increase in average lung volume [8], [9], [10], [11], [12], and a decrease of cranio-caudal motion amplitude of the lung [10] compared to supine positioning. These results suggest that upright thoracic radiotherapy could reduce irradiated lung volumes, decreasing the mean lung dose and potentially decreasing lung toxicity [13]. Moreover, studies have shown general trends toward improved patient comfort and/or a preference for upright positioning compared with the supine position which also enables treatment of patients who cannot tolerate the supine position [9], [14], [15], [16], [17].

In 2023, we reported a proof of concept study on upright breast radiotherapy [16]. This study revealed that: (i) breast cancer patients preferred upright rather than supine positioning, (ii) perched position and specialized radiotherapy bras were effective in limiting or eliminating skin folds, (iii) arms up positioning should enable sufficient beam access, but would require specialized “arms-up” supports designed for upright positioning.

After the development of specific armrests for “arms-up” upright positioning, the present study aimed to compare the discomfort and the experience of lung and breast cancer patients between supine and upright positioning. This study also compared lung volumes and longest possible deep inspiration breath holds (DIBH) between upright and supine positions [18], [8], [9], [10], [11], [12].

Materials and methods

Ethical considerations

This prospective, single center pilot study obtained ethical approval from local institutional review board (R201-004-288) and all patients gave informed consent to participate.

Patient population

Eight breast cancer patients and 7 lung cancer patients were included between December 2023 and September 2024. Among the 7 lung cancer patients, there were 5 males and 2 females. All patients treated for breast cancer were females.

Patient workflow

Patients received conventional radiotherapy (supine), in the radiation oncology department of the Leon Berard Cancer Center (Lyon, France) on an Elekta® Linear Accelerator (Stockholm, Sweden). During their radiotherapy course, 5 additional research appointments were scheduled. During these 5 appointments, patients tested both supine and upright positions. In the supine position, patients were set up with their arms up using a breast board (Posiboard 2, CVICO, Kalona, IA) with an adapted head rest and a knee cushion (Fig. 1a). In the upright position, patients were set up on an upright positioner designed for radiotherapy (Eve® from Leo Cancer Care, Crawley, UK) (Fig. 1b) [15]. This upright positioner includes a backrest, a seat, a shin rest and a heel stop, which were adjusted individually. Based on previous studies, the backrest was inclined at 10° backward, the seat pan was tilted down 15° from horizontal, and custom “arms up” supports (designed specifically for upright radiotherapy) were used [16]. A vacuum cushion was molded for each patient and positioned to support their lower back and buttocks. The 5 appointments enabled repeated discomfort and spirometry measurements in both positions with approximately 15 min spent in each position. Nine patients started with upright positioning on the first appointment before moving to supine positioning, then started with supine positioning during appointment 2, alternating the starting position at each appointment. For the remaining six patients, the sequence reversed the order of the positions. Alternating body positions for participants and appointments mitigated against potential biases due to practice or fatigue.

Patient positions for spirometry tests in supine position (a) and in upright position (b).

At the start and the end of the first four appointments, patients were asked to report on any discomfort in their neck, shoulders, upper back, elbows, lower back, wrists/hands, hips/thighs, and knees. At the end of the first and the last appointments, patients also filled out a questionnaire (Supplementary material 1) designed to appraise their experience using 5-point Likert scales (between 1 completely disagree and 5 completely agree). Finally, at the end of the last appointment, patients were asked to report which body position they preferred. All discomfort assessments, experience and preference reports were collected after the tests, while patients were seated in a regular chair.

For the spirometry measurements, the recommendations of the American Thoracic Society and European Respiratory society were followed [19]. Patients breathed through a mouthpiece with a nose clip and a filter connected to the SDX spirometer (Dyn’R®, Aix en Provence, France). The protocol is included in Fig. 2.

Details of the spirometry tests performed in each position.

The tests began with a record of tidal Volume (Vt) for 2 min. Then patients were asked and encouraged to completely exhale and then completely inhale in a relaxed manner. The volume change between a complete expiration and full inspiration represents a Vital Capacity (VC) measurement [20]. To guarantee the reliability of the VC measurement, between three and five repetitions were performed, with an acceptance criterion of obtaining two VC measurements within 5% or 0.15 L of each other [19]. Between measurements, patients were given time to catch their breath and remove the mouthpiece and the nose clip if necessary. From the VC measurements, Expiratory Reserve Volume (ERV) and Inspiratory Capacity (IC) were calculated. ERV represents the amount of air that can be maximally exhaled from the end expiratory lung volume during tidal breathing and IC is the maximum amount of air inhaled after a normal expiration (Fig. 3a). A Deep Inspiratory Breath Hold (DIBH) target was then set at 80% of the maximum IC with a tolerance of +/- 0.20 L. Using visual digital glasses, patients were able to observe their respiratory trace and aim for their DIBH target band. Patients practiced holding their breath at this DIBH level for 10 s, before attempting to hold their breath within the DIBH target band for as long as possible. All these tests were repeated in both positions during each appointment.

a) Respiratory volumes acquired during a spirometry test in a breast cancer patient; b) box plots of the tidal Volume (Vt), the Vital Capacity (VC), the Expiratory Reserve Volume (ERV) and the Inspiratory Capacity (IC) calculated from tests performed in upright (UP) and in supine (SP) position (with means of the values in L).

Patient characteristics, including age, weight, height, smoking habits and surgical history, were recorded. BMI was calculated. Spirometry data was exported for each patient as CSV files. A Python (3.9) script was created to read the files, find the peaks and output the various volumes: Vt, VC, ERV and IC. The DIBH duration was collected by filtering the timestamps that met the condition previously mentioned (80% of the maximum IC with a tolerance of ±0.2 L).

Data analyses

Fisher’s exact test was used to compare (i) the proportion of uncomfortable body parts in each position, (ii) the results regarding the patient experience and (iii) the patient preference. The mean and standard deviations (SD) were calculated for Vt measurements. Mean data was then compared for the two body positions using a paired T-test. The consistency of the VC measurements and the longest breath-hold durations was also assessed.

Results

Patient characteristics

A summary of the patients’ characteristics is provided in Table 1. The median values for their age, height, and weight at the first research appointment were: 60 years (range 50–78 years), 167 cm (range 155–178 cm) and 62 kg (range 50–104 kg), respectively. Four patients (one woman and three men) were overweight with a BMI between 25 and 29.9, and two patients (one woman and one man) were obese with a BMI between 30 and 39.9. Three patients had previously undergone back surgery (including one with inserted stabilization material), two patients had previously undergone a lower limb surgery (including one with a prosthesis), and one patient had a previous upper limb surgery. For 14 patients, all tests were completed within two weeks, while for the remaining patient the appointments were scheduled over 22 days due to a COVID-19 infection.

Patient comfort and preference

The fifteen patients were asked to report any uncomfortable body part in the first four appointments. Before testing upright or supine positioning, they reported any pre-existing discomfort, as a baseline. At this stage, the shoulders were the most frequently reported area of discomfort. Of the 60 tests (15 patients, each with 4 appointments where comfort was recorded), patients reported no discomfort in 47 and 41 tests in upright and supine position, respectively (p > 0.05). In the supine position, the lower back and the shoulders were the body parts where discomfort was most frequently reported (lower back discomfort: 10% of tests compared to 3% in upright, p > 0.05, shoulder discomfort: 8% of tests compared to 3% in upright, p > 0.05). Discomfort in the knees was the most frequent issue in the upright position, reported in 5% of the tests, while no knee discomfort was reported for supine positioning (p > 0.05). Among patients with a history of back or lower-limb surgery, only one patient reported discomfort in both the upper and lower back while in the supine position.

Fig. 4 presents the results of the 10-item questionnaire. Results at the end of the fifth appointment broadly mirrored those obtained at the end of the first appointment. For the upright position, at least 13/15 patients completely disagreed when asked whether they experienced pain, vulnerability, difficulties in maintaining the position, dizziness, tingling or fatigue. For the supine position, between 4 and 13 patients completely disagreed with these statements. Statistically less pain was reported when standing up at the end of the test in the upright positioning (p < 0.05). No significant differences between body positions were found for patient comfort once in position, ease of breathing normally and ease of performing DIBH.

Responses to the 10-items questionnaire. The numbers represent the patients that agree/disagree with the statement (* represents a statically significant difference (p < 0.05) between the responses for the upright and the supine positioning).

For physical comfort, 9/15 (60%) patients (6 breast cancer patients and 3 lung cancer patients) preferred the upright position, 1 breast cancer patient preferred the supine position (6.7%), and 5 had no preference (33.3%) (p > 0.05). Psychologically and for normal breathing, 10 patients preferred the upright position (66.7%) while 2 breast cancer and 1 lung cancer patients preferred supine positioning (total n = 3, 20%). The remaining 2 patients (13.3%) had no preference (p > 0.05). For DIBH, 8 patients (53.3%) preferred upright positioning, 6 preferred supine positioning (40%) and 1 had no preference (6.7%) (p > 0.05). The sample size was insufficient to detect any correlation between BMI and the results of the patient discomfort and preference.

Respiratory volumes

For the supine position, the mean and SD values were Vt 0.720 ± 0.261 L, VC 3.028 ± 0.628 L, ERV 0.533 ± 0.207 L and IC 2.495 ± 0.571 L. For the upright position, the corresponding values (means and SDs) were Vt 0.665 ± 0.269 L, VC 3.104 ± 0.663 L, ERV 1.124 ± 0.440 L, IC 1.980 ± 0.598 L (Fig. 3b). A representative example of respiratory volume curves for a single patient positioned upright and in supine is presented in figure 3a. The mean Vt and IC values were statistically significantly smaller in upright compared to supine positioning (p < 0.05) (Fig. 3b). The mean of the ERV measurements was significantly larger in upright compared to supine positioning (p < 0.05) (Fig. 3b). There was no statistically significant difference in the mean VC between upright and supine (p = 0.17) (Fig. 3b). The sample size of this study was insufficient to detect any correlation between BMI and the results of the respiratory volumes. Graphical representations of VC measurements and longest DIBH at each appointment for each patient (Supplementary material 2) show greater consistency in the upright position and show no trend of decline or increase measurements over time for any position. One patient contracted COVID during the study; the VC measured the day before testing positive (i.e. most likely already contaminated), was 14% and 17% lower than his VC reference value in the upright and supine positions respectively. Fifteen days later, the VC measurements had returned to within 10% of the reference value.

DIBH length

All patients were able to perform clinically representative, 20 s DIBH tests in both positions. The maximum achieved DIBH durations varied substantially between patients. For supine positioning, the maximum durations ranged between 29.5 s and 122.2 s, whereas for upright positioning, the maximum durations ranged between 24.9 s and 92.2 s. No statistically significant difference was found between maximum achieved DIBH durations for the two positions (p = 0.10).

Discussion

This study compared (i) any reported discomfort, (ii) patient experiences and preferences, (iii) respiratory volumes and (iv) maximum DIBH durations amongst breast and lung cancer patients, for supine and upright radiotherapy setups.

Patients receiving supine radiotherapy, reported less back discomfort and less pain when standing up after 15-minute tests in the upright position, compared to the supine position. These results, obtained for positioning timeframes representative of a typical radiotherapy session, are consistent with previous data on various populations [15], [21]. These data emphasize the relationship between radiotherapy patient positioning and the experience of comfort [22]. Upright positioning therefore appears to be an opportunity to improve patients’ physical comfort with regards to back pain [21]. Moreover, our study showed a trend (not statistically significant) for reduced shoulder discomfort upright compared to supine, which could suggest that the armrests developed specifically for upright positioning offer improved comfort relative to the supine chest board. Improvement in this area is especially important for breast cancer patients, who benefit from the arms‑up position because it reduces the inframammary skin fold [23]. However, many of these patients have difficulty raising their arms after surgery [24]. In addition to improvements in physical comfort, our results highlight the influence of patient positioning on psychological comfort and on comfort associated with normal breathing. When Duister et al. reported that all their patients receiving palliative lung radiotherapy found the sitting position more comfortable than the supine position, this likely reflected not only improved physical comfort but also enhanced psychological comfort and greater respiratory comfort [9]. This could be due to the adoption of a more natural position without the need to change patients’ position when they arrive in the treatment room (i.e. patient walking to the chair and sitting up versus patient walking to the treatment couch and having to lying down). Although the patients enrolled in our study described less back discomfort, they reported slightly more knee discomfort, probably linked to the design of the shin rest. This shin rest is crucial for reproducibility of the lower half of the body [15], [16]. However, the design and material should fit with the external anatomy of the patient and provide enough comfort and support. This study also indicates that patients requiring lung and breast radiotherapy are likely to prefer the upright position rather than the supine position. This finding builds upon previous studies revealing that a majority of breast cancer patients treated in supine position, would have preferred to be treated in an upright position [16], [21]. Thus, while patients who cannot tolerate supine positioning may benefit from upright radiotherapy, the comfort benefits are likely to extend more generally to broader cohorts of patients requiring breast or lung radiotherapy [17]. Overweight and obese patients, who may experience discomfort, practical difficulties when lying flat, should be specifically included in future upright radiotherapy studies to assess whether they could benefit from this alternative treatment position [25], [26], [27]. At this stage, however, evidence is insufficient, and our study did not demonstrate any BMI‑related differences, likely due to its limited sample size. Overall, our results encourage further research to determine whether the benefit of upright radiotherapy is limited to patient comfort or can be extended to other aspects, such as improved protection of organs at risk.

Regarding lung volumes, we found significantly larger ERV values and smaller IC values upright, without changes in VC, suggesting more efficient respiration for upright positioning. Similar findings have also been reported elsewhere, for example amongst patients with chronic heart failure, or obese individuals [28], [29], [30], [31], [32], [33]. These studies used Pulmonary Functional Tests (PFTs) to objectively assess lung function, for which sitting or standing positions are recommended [34], [35]. In our study, repeated VC measurements showed greater consistency in the upright position, which may explain the recommendation for seated positions in pulmonary function test guidelines [36].

The similar VC values from PFTs upright and supine found in our results seemingly contradict results from CT or MRI studies showing increases in lung volume up to 26.9% for the upright position [10], [11], [12]. In PFTs, volume is measured through flow measurements, whereas volume is measured using voxels in CT and MR imaging [37], [38]. Regardless of the breathing status during image acquisition (i.e. free breathing or inhalation), CT or MRI measurements consistently include residual volume (RV), representing the air that remains in the lungs after maximum expiration. Plethysmographic or dilutional techniques are necessary to provide RV measurements [38]. Since such measurements were performed in a specialized cabin with a patient in seated position, only a few small studies have compared RV upright and supine. They reported similar values for the two positions, but provide limited methodological details and draw conclusions based upon small samples, resulting in low-quality evidence [31], [37], [39]. Literature suggests that gravity pulls the diaphragm and abdominal contents downward in the upright position, creating additional space in the thoracic cavity and resulting in larger lung volumes visible on CT or MR images [40], [41]. Whether this additional space contributes to increase the RV remains an open question that warrants further investigation.

Our results showed smaller Vt in upright positioning, suggesting that patients use a smaller part of their lungs when they breathe normally in the upright position than in the supine position. This potentially means a reduction of lung motion as also indicated by the 4D MRI study by Yang et al. [10]. In lung radiotherapy, Internal Target Volume (ITV) is commonly used to account for tumor motion due to respiration, and a reduction of lung motion may lead to a smaller ITV, offering greater protection to the healthy surrounding tissue.

As DIBH is also widely used in RT to reduce tumor motion and dose to organs at risk, its feasibility in upright positioning was assessed [42]. All our patients performed DIBH in the upright position for a minimum of 20 s, a clinically relevant duration for beam gating. However, DIBH may be easier in the supine position potentially due to a higher involvement of abdominal muscles in upright positions for postural control [43].

Several limitations of this study should be noted, including the small sample size of 15 patients from a single center, all treated in the supine position. Because upright radiotherapy positioning systems are not yet widely available, opportunities for multi‑center studies remain limited. To enhance data quality and limit the impact of fatigue or other clinical factors, 5 appointments were scheduled for each patient within 15 days, allowing repeated measurements over a short timeframe. Despite the small sample size, our findings align with those of several other studies reporting improved comfort, a preference for upright positioning, and no difference in VC between upright and supine positions [25]. In addition, the current literature is insufficient to clearly identify which patients would benefit most from upright positioning. Ongoing research in upright radiotherapy aims to determine the clinical contexts in which this approach offers advantages. Although patients unable to tolerate the supine position would intuitively benefit from upright radiotherapy, their inclusion in such studies remains challenging. Furthermore, the absence of fully configured upright treatment rooms has, to date, prevented in-depth studies of how patients might experience upright radiotherapy. Nevertheless, the results of this study motivate us to continue research with larger sample size, including the acquisition of CT scans in the upright and supine positions to compare internal anatomy and target volume motion.

Conclusion

This study indicates that changing from a supine to an upright radiotherapy position may improve comfort for patients with breast and lung cancer. Most patients expressed a preference for the upright position. No differences in vital capacity were found. However, the tidal volume data may suggest a reduced tumour motion in the upright position. A 20 s duration for gated DIBH beam delivery for breast and lung RT appeared feasible. These findings, however, require confirmation in larger patient cohorts.
