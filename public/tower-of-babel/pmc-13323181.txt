Fontan‐Related Lymphatic Complications: Incidence, Risk Factors, and Impact on Extracardiac Outcomes and Morbidity
Authors: Riley M Boyd, Christina Laternser, Karl Migally, Shivani G Patel, Rima Dababneh, Michael R Carr, Sheetal Patel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatients born with single ventricle anatomy palliated with the Fontan procedure are at risk of lymphatic complications (LC), including plastic bronchitis, protein losing enteropathy, and ascites. The mechanism of LC, associated risk factors, and impact on end organ dysfunction and mortality are not well understood.MethodsWe performed a single‐center, retrospective, cross‐sectional cohort study of patients with Fontan circulation seen at our center between 2009 and 2022. Univariate comparisons were completed comparing those with and without LC.ResultsA total of 314 Fontan patients were included with a mean age of 20.2±10.7 years. Of those patients, 83 (26.4%) had at least 1 LC, including ascites (21.4%), protein losing enteropathy (11.8%), and plastic bronchitis (2.9%). Patients with LC were older (23.5±12.6 versus 19.1±9.8 years, P=0.001) and had their Fontan operation at an older age (5.5 with LC versus 4.0 years without LC, P<0.001). The presence of LC was associated with renal dysfunction (P=0.004), thromboembolism (P=0.01), and arrhythmia (P<0.001). LC were also associated with higher mean Fontan pressures on catheterization (14.7±3.8 with LC versus 12.8±3.7 mm Hg without LC, P=0.003). Fontan patients with LC had higher rates of transplantation (25.3% versus 2.2%, P<0.001) and death (18.1% with LC versus 4.3% without LC, P<0.001).ConclusionsFontan‐related lymphatic complications develop in 1 out of 4 patients by 20 years of age, and they are associated with older age, renal dysfunction, thromboembolism, and arrhythmia. Patients with LC have higher rates of transplantation and death. Our findings indicate that elevated central venous pressure, rather than systolic cardiac or atrioventricular valve function, is associated with these complications.Keywords: ascites, Fontan procedure, plastic bronchitis, protein‐losing enteropathiesSubject Categories: Clinical Studies, Complications, Mortality/Survival, Congenital Heart Disease

Single ventricle congenital heart disease (SVCHD) refers to a heterogenous group where, simplistically, 1 of the ventricles is unable to provide adequate either systemic or pulmonary blood flow. Included in this group of cardiac lesions are those with a dominant left ventricle, a dominant right ventricle, or a biventricular heart that is unable to be septated to achieve biventricular circulation. Patients born with SVCHD often require staged surgical palliation, culminating with the Fontan operation. The goal of staged palliation is to ultimately re‐route blood flow to establish normal blood oxygen saturations while minimizing stress on the systemic ventricle. This operative approach has dramatically improved the lifespan of patients with SVCHD, a trend that has continued over multiple decades. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 Nonetheless, passive flow into the pulmonary circulation leads to systemic venous congestion and restricted cardiac output. 6 These features of Fontan circulation contribute to various complications, including protein losing enteropathy (PLE), plastic bronchitis (PB), liver disease, renal dysfunction, thromboembolism, arrhythmia, heart failure, need for orthotopic heart transplantation (OHT), and death. 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13

The lymphatic system has been increasingly implicated in various Fontan‐related complications, namely PLE, PB, and ascites. PLE is thought to be due to increased lymphatic production, at least in part due to systemic venous congestion, resulting in intestinal wall edema, inflammation, and lymphangiectasia. 14 , 15 When protein leaks into the intestinal lumen, patients may present with symptoms such as diarrhea. The mechanism of PB is not entirely known. 14 It has been suggested that systemic venous congestion results in impaired lymphatic drainage. 14 , 15 When the lymphatic barrier is overcome, protein can leak into the airways resulting in proteinaceous casts. 15 Patients with PLE have protein loss via the gastrointestinal tract that results in hypoalbuminemia and ascites. Ascites in patients with Fontan circulation can also result from venous congestion and hepatic dysfunction. 15 , 16 , 17 Additionally, patients with PLE and PB have increased morbidity and mortality. 18 , 19 , 20 Despite the growing understanding of complications, such as PLE and PB, there is still a need to better identify patients at risk for developing such complications and build upon the growing body of evidence regarding lymphatic complications (LC). This study aimed to evaluate the incidence of lymphatic‐related complications, including PLE, PB, and ascites, in SVCHD with Fontan circulation; identify clinical, echocardiographic and cardiac catheterization‐based hemodynamic risk factors; and assess the impact of these LC on outcomes, such as OHT or death.

METHODS

Study Design

This was a single center, retrospective, cross‐sectional cohort study including patients with SVCHD with surgical palliation to Fontan circulation. Study subjects' latest outpatient clinic note was reviewed, and age was calculated from the patients' latest echocardiogram. The Institutional Review Board at Ann & Robert H. Lurie Children's Hospital of Chicago reviewed this study and granted a waiver of consent (IRB #2022‐5310). Three of the authors (R. M. B., C. L., and S. P.) had full access to the data in the study and take responsibility for the integrity of the data and statistical analysis. Data will not be publicly available to maintain privacy.

Subjects

Inclusion criteria required subjects to be patients with SVCHD with completed Fontan palliation evaluated at our outpatient clinics between January 2009 and March 2022. Patients were excluded from the study if they were deemed to have inadequate follow‐up to assess long‐term outcomes; this typically occurred if patients were only seen at our institution for their general or heart failure cardiac care ≤1 times following their Fontan. For patients who underwent OHT, only pretransplant evaluations and data were included.

Clinical and Laboratory Data

Data were retrospectively collected from electronic medical records. The primary exposure was the presence of LC, defined by the diagnosis of PLE, PB, or ascites. The presence of PLE was defined by clinical documentation indicating a patient was diagnosed with PLE or elevated stool alpha‐1 antitrypsin >54 mg/dL. Plastic bronchitis was defined as a clinically documented diagnosis of PB or documented description of a patient producing pulmonary casts. Documentation of clinical examination and imaging reports were used to determine if a patient had ascites. In addition to the primary exposure, other variables and outcomes of interest were collected, including demographic characteristics, anthropometric data, cardiac morphologic diagnoses (eg, dominant ventricle type), surgical factors (eg, type of Fontan palliation), mortality, OHT, and the presence of other comorbidities, including arrhythmia, renal dysfunction, and thromboembolism. The Full Age Spectrum creatine‐based equation was used to estimate glomerular filtration rate. 21 Renal dysfunction was defined as an estimated glomerular filtration rate <90 mL/min per 1.73 m2 as this correlates with the estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) of stage 2 chronic kidney disease per the National Kidney Foundation. 22

Echocardiographic and Catheter‐Based Hemodynamic Data

Cardiac catheterization and echocardiographic data were retrospectively evaluated. There were no criteria for studies to be before or after onset of LC; patients' last echocardiogram and, if applicable, an echocardiogram within 6 months of cardiac catheterization were reviewed. Through chart review of the reading physicians' echocardiography report, qualitative evaluations of ventricular function and dominant atrioventricular valve regurgitation were collected. Hemodynamic data were collected via retrospective review of documented cardiac catheterization data.

Statistical Analysis

Demographic, anthropometric, echocardiographic, catheter‐based hemodynamics and outcome factors were compared between the overall cohort with LC and the cohort without LC using bivariate analyses. Categorical and continuous variables were compared using a chi‐square test or t test, respectively. Similar comparisons were completed for subgroups of children, defined as <18 years of age, and adults, defined by 18 years of age or older, to address potential bias from patient age. Subgroup analyses were also completed for patients with LC with PLE/PB versus those with isolated ascites; this subanalysis also included children and adult subgroup analyses. Similar statistical methods were used except when comparing categorical variables with any group in the analysis having N≤5; for comparisons of categorical variables with any group having N≤5, Fisher's exact test were used. In this study, P values were calculated for those observations for which we had data; missing data were not included in P value calculation. Some data were not presented to maintain patient confidentiality, for example, if N<5 or if an N of <5 can be deduced from related data.

Kaplan–Meier curves were used to estimate freedom from death or OHT, whichever occurred first, over time. Time zero was defined as the date of Fontan operation. Patients were censored at the date of their most recent echocardiogram if neither event had occurred. All analyses described were performed using Stata 19 (StataCorp, TX).

RESULTS

A total of 414 patients with Fontan circulation were seen at our center during the defined study period; 100 patients were excluded, primarily due to inadequate outpatient follow‐up. The remaining 314 patients had a mean age of 20.2±10.7 years (Table 1), with a male predominance (60.5%). There was a statistically significant difference in the frequencies of race or ethnicity between those with and without LC in the entire cohort (P=0.03), but in neither the pediatric or adult subgroups alone (Table 1). The average age at Fontan operation completion was 4.4 years. Of the 314 patients, 26.4% demonstrated at least 1 form of LC, including 22.6% of children and 29.8% of adults. The most common LC was ascites (21.4%) followed by PLE (11.8%) and PB (2.9%). Of those with LC, 65.1% had only 1 LC, 32.5% had a combination of 2, and 2.4% had all 3 LC. Isolated ascites (50.6%) was the most common LC. The presence of LC was associated with older age (23.5±12.6 with LC versus 19.1±9.8 years without LC, P=0.001) and greater weight (61.1±26.9 with LC versus 54.2±25.3 kg without LC, P=0.04) (Table 1). When separating children and adults, LC was only associated with older age in adult subgroup (31.8±8.3 adults with LC versus 26.5±7.6 years in adults without LC, P<0.001) but not in the pediatric subgroup (10.9±5.2 children with LC versus 11.3±4.0 years in children without LC, P=0.58). Those with PLE/PB were younger and shorter in height than those with isolated ascites (Table S1). However, this was not observed when those with isolated ascites were removed from the cohort (Table S4). There were no statistically significant differences between sex, height, or body mass index in the entire cohort or either age subgroup.

Cardiac Surgical Factors

LC were associated with Fontan completion at an older age (5.5 with LC versus 4.0 years without LC, P<0.001) (Table 2) in the entire cohort. Age at Fontan operation was statistically significant for adults only when separating by age (6.8 years in adults with LC versus 4.3 years in adults without LC, P<0.001). In the entire cohort, there were no statistically significant differences between dominant ventricle morphology or type of stage 1 surgery. However, there was a statistically significant association of LC with dominant right ventricle in the pediatric subset (66.7% children with LC versus 36.3% children without LC, P=0.002). Additionally, there was a statistical difference between type of stage 1 surgery when comparing PLE/PB and ascites subgroups; this was seen only in the adult subgroup (Table S2) and not observed with removal of patients with isolated LC (Table S4). Dominant right ventricle was more prevalent in those with PLE/PB when compared with isolated ascites, but this was not observed in the pediatric or adult subgroups (Table S2). Initial Fontan operation type was also statistically significant in the entire cohort and adult subset (Table 2); this was due to a predominance of atriopulmonary Fontan operations in the adults with LC.

Echocardiography and Cardiac Catheterization

Among our cohort, 51% (159/314) of patients had post‐Fontan cardiac catheterization data. Among these patients, mean Fontan pressure was higher in those with LC (14.7±3.8 with LC versus 12.8±3.7 mm Hg without LC, P=0.003). This difference was statistically significant only in the pediatric subgroup (14.9±3.6 children with LC versus 12.1±3.1 mm Hg children without LC, P=0.005), but there was a trend toward significance in adults (14.6±4.0 adults with LC versus 13.1±3.9 mm Hg adults without LC, P=0.07). Indexed pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR) was higher in children with LC (2.26±1.15 children with LC versus 1.53±0.73 children without LC, P=0.008) but not in the entire cohort or adults. There was no significant association of LC with moderate to severe ventricular dysfunction or moderate to severe atrioventricular valve regurgitation (Table 3).

End Organ Dysfunction and Outcomes

The presence of LC was associated with increased rates of renal dysfunction, as indicated by eGFR <90 mL/min per 1.73 m2, within the entire cohort (29.9% with LC versus 13.7% without LC, P=0.004) (Table 3). The presence of LC was associated with renal dysfunction in pediatric patients (40.9% children with LC versus 13.3% children without LC, P=0.006) but not in adults. Among patients with LC, eGFR was lower in patients with PLE/PB when compared with isolated ascites (Table S3). With removal of those with isolated ascites, eGFR was lower and rates of renal dysfunction were higher in those with PLE/PB than no LC. LC were associated with thromboembolism in the entire cohort and adults; LC were also associated arrhythmia requiring treatment in all groups. Rates of OHT (25.3% with LC versus 2.2% without LC, P<0.001) and mortality (18.1% with LC versus 4.3% without LC, P<0.001) were significantly higher in those with LC in the entire cohort and each subset. DISCUSSION

These results demonstrate the significant morbidity and mortality associated with LC in a relatively large cohort of children and adults with Fontan circulation. Over one quarter of our study group developed some form of LC within an average follow‐up period of 15.8 years from Fontan completion. Prior reports have estimated the prevalence of PLE to be between 2% to 13%. 2 , 4 , 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 Similarly, our study found that 11.8% of patients developed PLE during the follow‐up period. Plastic bronchitis is thought to be rarer than PLE. Sharma et al 20 found that PB occurred in <1% of patients from the ANZFR (Australian and New Zealand Fontan Registry). Conversely, studies using questionnaire‐based methods found that 6.8% to 24% had developed symptoms consistent with PB. 26 , 27 In our cohort, ~3% of patients developed PB, suggesting that this diagnosis may be slightly more prevalent than seen in the ANZFR, however, it is still quite rare. We reported high rates of ascites in our cohort, which may be early signs of more severe LC, such as PLE or PB. Direct comparisons between mortality rates among studies are difficult, as survival has continued to rise across each decade. 3 Additionally, there is significant heterogeneity between studies regarding timing of Fontan completion. Nonetheless, our mortality rate of 8.0%, with an average follow‐up time of 15.8 years after Fontan operation is similar to that reported elsewhere. 2 , 3 , 5 , 13 , 20 , 23

Renal dysfunction is not uncommon in patients with Fontan circulation. Van Belle and colleagues found that prior studies demonstrated a median of 10% of patients had an eGFR <90 mL/min per 1.73 m2. 28 Our reported rate of renal dysfunction at 18.3% is slightly higher than the reported median of 10% but still within the previously reported range. It has been shown that renal function declines more rapidly in patients with Fontan circulation compared with healthy controls. 29 Interestingly, we found that reduced eGFR was associated with LC in children but not adults. Additionally, patients with PLE/PB were more likely to have renal dysfunction than those with isolated ascites. Renal dysfunction, proteinuria, and albuminuria have been associated with elevated central venous pressure. 30 , 31 , 32 , 33 Conversely, evidence of renal dysfunction has not consistently been associated with decreased cardiac function 30 , 31 , 32 , 33 , 34 despite lower ejection fraction previously being associated with markers of renal dysfunction. 35 These data raise the question if early onset renal dysfunction may predispose patients to other complications and how childhood renal dysfunction may play a role in lifelong outcomes. Given these findings, further work investigating potential mechanisms of Fontan‐related renal dysfunction, including the role of venous congestion, and the role of renal dysfunction in other Fontan‐related outcomes is warranted.

Other complications, including arrhythmia, thromboembolism, OHT, and mortality, were also associated with LC. However, some findings were isolated to either adults or children. In instances where we observed statistical significance within the entire cohort, but only 1 of the subsets, it is reasonable to believe that the observed result in the entire cohort is driven by the findings within the subset and may not be applicable to the entire cohort. In children, there was a predominance of a dominant right ventricle and elevated indexed PVR. Understandably, elevated PVR results in worsening venous congestion, as indicated by elevated mean Fontan pressure in children, thereby contributing to lymphatic production and ultimately the development of LC, the need for OHT, and death. Additionally, right ventricular dominance has been shown to be associated with increased risk of PLE and PB. 20 , 36 We did not observe this association of dominant right ventricle and elevated PVR with LC in adults with Fontan palliation.

Older age at Fontan completion was observed in adults with LC, but not pediatric patients. Pundi et al showed a trend of increased mortality among those who had their Fontan operation at 5 to 16 years when compared with those at 2 to 4 years of age. 2 Fontan completion after 6 years old has also been associated with increased rates of PLE and mortality. 37 In our study, adults with LC had their Fontan operation at 6.8 years of age whereas those without LC had their operation at 4.3 years, which aligns with prior reports. We suspect the wide range of ages at the time of Fontan completion in adults reflects historical trends in practice where the Fontan operation was previously not consistently completed between 2 to 4 years of age. Another historical trend apparent in our data is that adults with LC had a greater proportion of atriopulmonary Fontan operations, a trend not seen in the pediatric subset as atriopulmonary Fontan has been replaced with cavopulmonary Fontan for the past few decades. However, it is of note that Sharma et al did not find that atriopulmonary Fontan was associated with increased risk of PLE, PB, OHT, or death. 20 Given these findings likely reflect historical trends, they support prior changes in clinical practice and may not affect current surgical care.

Older age, along with longer time since Fontan operation, was associated with LC in the entire cohort and adults. In adults, this finding may be because these patients have lived with elevated systemic venous pressures for a prolonged period, and therefore, potentially more time with chronic inflammation and elevated lymphatic production resulting in development of proteinaceous leak. The lack of similar findings in pediatric patients suggests that the association of age with LC may not apply to those <18 years of age despite this finding being seen in the entire cohort. These data, combined with our data regarding right ventricular dominance and PVR, may suggest that the development of LC within the adult and pediatric populations is be driven by different processes.

Our cohort of patients with LC is a complex, heterogenous group. When comparing isolated ascites and PLE/PB, those with isolated ascites were younger, shorter in height, and had operative differences possibly reflective of surgical era. Those with PLE/PB are more likely to have a dominant right ventricle and higher rates of renal dysfunction. This demonstrates that although we have grouped those with ascites, PLE, and PB, they represent somewhat distinct populations and our pooled LC analysis, at least in part, reflects a mixture of distinct phenotypes. Ascites cannot exclusively be associated with lymphatic abnormalities. Instead, the development of ascites in patients with Fontan circulation is multifactorial and likely reflects the interaction between lymphatic abnormalities, chronic elevated venous pressures, hepatic dysfunction, and, potentially, the loss of protein due to malabsorption. 15 , 16 , 17 Accordingly, when patients with isolated ascites were removed from our analysis, associations related to age and surgical era were attenuated, however, associations with more severe venous congestion and adverse outcomes were largely preserved. The differences observed between pooled LC analyses and ascites‐restricted analyses likely reflect the interaction of phenotype heterogeneity and shared pathophysiology rather than analytic instability. Recognizing heterogeneity within LC phenotypes may be important for future risk stratification and mechanistic studies in populations with Fontan circulation. However, our data demonstrate that regardless of phenotype, patients with Fontan circulation and LC are at risk of adverse outcomes.

Our study has several limitations. This was a retrospective, cross‐sectional study, therefore, we cannot deem causality between LC and our variables of interest. Multiple baseline characteristics, particularly age, between the group with versus without LC were also different. When analyzing pediatric and adult subgroups separately, some associations were statistically significant in only 1 subgroup, suggesting that some findings within the entire cohort are driven by a single subgroup or confounded by age. We also did not investigate the temporal relationship between LC and outcomes or variables, such as the development of renal or cardiac dysfunction. This limits our ability to further prognosticate the chronological relationship between these outcomes. Additionally, we found that LC are associated with OHT, however, it is important to note that PLE and PB are indications for OHT. Finally, we limited our analysis of cardiac catheterization data to patients with cardiac catheterization within 6 months of an echocardiogram. Consequently, this may introduce selection bias for patients with more complications, as these patients may have required additional evaluation due to changes in clinical status. Therefore, the hemodynamic data from cardiac catheterization may not be generalizable to all patients with Fontan circulation. Additionally, there may have been changes during the 6‐month period between echocardiogram and cardiac catheterization, which may affect results.

CONCLUSIONS

Patients with Fontan circulation are at high risk of developing LC, with over one quarter of the patients within our cohort developing them during follow‐up. Additionally, these patients are at higher risk of developing renal dysfunction. These complications may be driven by more severe systemic venous congestion, as demonstrated by cardiac catheterization data and the association with complications that are suspected to be the result of a similar physiologic process. Most importantly, patients with Fontan circulation who develop LC are at an increased risk of death. This highlights the need to better understand, identify, and improve care for those Fontan patients with LC. Future studies should consider further exploring the potential of childhood renal dysfunction and the differences in disease processes among pediatric and adult patients. Future studies should also consider evaluating the natural history of LC as they relate to other outcomes, with the goal of elucidating whether the onset of specific conditions may portend future complications.
