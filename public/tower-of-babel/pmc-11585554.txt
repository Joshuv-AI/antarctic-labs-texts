The intrahepatic bacterial metataxonomic signature of patients with hepatocellular carcinoma
Authors: Jie Li, Xuanpei Zhai, Changzhou Chen, Rong Zhang, Xiaowu Huang, Yifan Liu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractDysbiosis of the gut-liver axis increases the risk of bacterial and metabolite influx into the liver, which may contribute to the development of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). In this study, we compared the microbiomes in HCC tumors and adjacent tissues. We examined the HCC tumors and adjacent tissues from 19 patients diagnosed with HCC. We find that the liver tissues from HCC patients with capsule invasion presented higher alpha diversity at the genus level than those without. The bacterial compositions in liver tissues of HCC patients at stage II differed from those at stage I and Advanced, respectively. Metagenomic profiling revealed that order Actinomycetales was enriched in the HCC patients at advanced stages. Order Lactobacillales, family Veillonellaceae, genera Rhodobacter and Megasphaera are enriched in tumors of HCC patients, whereas genus Pseudochrobactrum is enriched in the adjacent tissues from HCC patients. An increased abundance of class Actinobacteria and order Actinomycetales is observed in the HCC patients with cirrhosis. In contrast, phylum Firmicutes, classes Clostridia and Betaproteobacteria, and order Clostridiales are enriched in those without cirrhosis. The presence of various types of bacterial 16S rRNAs in HCC tumors and adjacent tissues indicates the presence of various bacterial communities therein. Our study provides information about differentially abundant intrahepatic bacteria in patients with HCC. Introduction

Liver cancer is one of the leading causes of cancer-related deaths worldwide and remains a global health challenge1,2. The incidence of liver cancer has been estimated to exceed 1 million cases by the year 20253. Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is the most common type of primary liver cancer, accounting for approximately 90% of liver cancer cases3. HCC mainly occurs in patients with chronic liver diseases, such as cirrhosis caused by hepatitis B or C infection4.

Over the past decade, research has shown that the gut microbiota has emerged as a core factor affecting human health and disease. Substances from the gastrointestinal tract, including gut microbiomes and their metabolites, can be transported into the liver through the gut-liver axis via vascular and portal circulation systems5–7. Dysbiosis of this axis can lead to the portal influx of bacteria and their metabolites into the liver. It may thus cause or aggravate liver diseases, including HCC, via multiple mechanisms8. Lipopolysaccharides derived from the gut microbiota binding with Toll-like receptor 4 have been proposed to promote HCC8–11. The gut microbiota can mediate primary-to-secondary bile acid conversion, increase the hepatic natural killer T cells and heighten interferon-γ production upon antigen stimulation, thereby exerting anti-tumor effects in HCC12. In addition, gut bacterial metabolites, such as increased taurocholic acid and decreased 3-indole-propionic acid, may cause hepatic lipid accumulation, inflammation, and cell proliferation, ultimately leading to HCC8. Meanwhile, increased deoxycholic acid may induce HCC development by causing liver DNA damage, and increased trimethylamine-N-oxide may contribute to the development of HCC8,13Furthermore, decreased short-chain fatty acids may lead to the progression of HCC8,11,14. These data indicate that commensal bacteria are important in regulating liver inflammation, detoxification, and immune responses, and they may be associated with chronic diseases, fibrosis, and liver cancer. In a study of gut microbiota analysis in HCC patients, Ren et al. found that altered bacterial communities may play an important role in the occurrence and development of HCC, with a reduction in butyrate-producing bacteria that maintain bacterial energy metabolism and gut health and an increase in lipopolysaccharide-producing bacteria that promote liver inflammation and oxidative damage in early HCC, thereby promoting the development of HCC15.

Apart from the gut microbiota, tissue-enriched and intratumoral bacteria are also supposed to be associated with oncogenesis. Increasing evidence suggests that different types of human tumors possess distinct intratumoral bacterial compositions, and most of these intratumoral bacteria were found existing in cancer cells and immune cells, which has been indicated to influence tumor behaviors and patient outcomes independent of the tumor genomic composition16,17. Moreover, the composition of the tumor microbiome has been reported to be associated with the gut microbiome in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) and to affect host immune responses and the natural history of the disease17–19. Intracellular bacteria residing in tumors could promote breast cancer metastasis20. Further evidence suggests that the composition of the papillary thyroid cancer microbiome and its association with tumor invasion, the tumor microbiome may interact with thyroid hormones and autoimmune antibodies to regulate the tumor microenvironment, which may contribute to tumor invasion10. These findings indicate that intratumoral microbiota is heterogeneous in different cancer types and may differ from the related gut microbiota. The intratumoral microbiota may be associated with cancer development or immune responses via different mechanisms and signalling pathways. Recently, Xue et al. claimed that intratumoral microbiota is closely related to changes in the host metabolism, epigenetics, and gene-expression profiles, suggesting that microbiomes of hepatic tumors might provide novel diagnostic information21,22. By analyzing the intratumoral bacterial spectrum of normal human liver, peritumor, and HCC tissues, Huang et al. have identified specific bacterial signatures which could be used as potential diagnostic markers for HCC23. As indicated in the literature, intratumoral bacteria are supposed to be closer to the tumor cells and may play an important role in forming the tumor microenvironment, which may be relevant to the pathological process of HCC22–24. However, in contrast to the gut microbiota, the intratumoral microbiota of liver cancer remains still poorly understood and requires more investigation in future studies.

In this study, we designed and implemented a DNA-based method to profile the microbiomes of tumors and adjacent healthy tissues derived from HCC patients (n = 19) (Fig. 1). Total DNA was extracted from these samples and subjected to 16S rRNA gene sequencing, which allowed us to identify bacterial communities within both tumor and adjacent tissues. The analysis revealed that Actinobacteria, Firmicutes, and Proteobacteria were the three most dominant phyla detected in the liver tissues of these patients.

Study concept of intrahepatic microbiome analysis in patients with hepatocellular carcinoma. Paired HCC tumors and adjacent tissues were collected from each patient. 16S rRNA gene profiling of HCC tumors and adjacent tissues was performed using 16S rRNA sequencing targeting hypervariable V3-V4 regions. Bacterial analysis of paired samples was conducted based on bacterial OTUs clustered from 16S rRNA gene sequencing data.

Materials and methods

Patient recruitment, tissue Collection, and bacterial DNA extraction

According to strict selection criteria, 19 patients who had curative resection performed and were pathologically diagnosed with HCC at Zhongshan Hospital of Fudan University in June 2022 were enrolled in this study. The inclusion criteria were: (i) fresh tissues to ensure adequate DNA yield for 16S rRNA analysis; (ii) patients who had curative resection performed; (iii) Chinese patients; (iv) adult patients (age > 18 years). The exclusion criteria were: (i) patients who had received anti-tumor treatments before surgery; (ii) diagnosis of HCC 1 year prior to the study. Complete clinical characteristics of each participant, including age, sex, tumor stage (I, II, III, IV), cirrhosis (present or not), capsule invasion (present or not), hepatitis A virus (HAV) infection (present or not), hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection (present or not), hepatitis C virus (HCV) infection (present or not), and fatty liver (present or not) were listed in Table S1. Paired samples of fresh HCC tumor and adjacent tissue were collected from each patient, and their characteristics are summarized in Table 1.

Characteristics of HCC patients.

In this study, sterility was carefully maintained during tissue collection to minimize contamination. A portion of the surgical excision was selected as the experimental sample on the sterilized nurse’s operating table and directly transferred to a sterile 50 mL Falcon tube during the surgical procedure. The tube, pre-filled with sterile tissue preservation solution, was handled in a biosafety cabinet to ensure sterility. The samples were kept on ice until lysis. Both tumor and adjacent healthy tissues were collected in the same surgery following identical procedures. Therefore, we believe that the difference in bacterial signatures observed between tumor and healthy tissues should sufficiently rule out the possibility of environmental contamination.

Total DNA was extracted from fresh samples of HCC tumor and adjacent tissue using the FastPure Cell/Tissue DNA Isolation Mini Kit (Vazyme, DC102-01), following the manufacturer’s instructions with minor adaptations. Prior to and after the proteinase K digestion step, bead-beating was performed at full speed in a vortex mixer (Sinopharm, SCRC-VM-96 A) for 15 min using 200 µL of 0.1 mm zirconia/silica beads (Biosepc, 11079101z).

Written informed consent was obtained from all patients prior to their participation in the study. The Research Ethics Committee of Zhongshan Hospital (Num: B2021-020) and ShanghaiTech University (Num: Q2022-060) approved the study.

16S rRNA V3–V4 amplification and sequencing

Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification was performed using Phanta Max Super-Fidelity DNA Polymerase (Vazyme, P505-d1) and 16S universal primers targeting the hypervariable V3–V4 region of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene by following the recommendations of Illumina. The bacterial populations in the HCC tumors and adjacent tissues were characterized via next-generation sequencing of the V3–V4 variable regions of the 16S rRNA gene. A sequencing library was generated by adding sequencing adapters to each sample. The joint pair length was set to encompass an amplicon of 465 base pairs using a 2 × 250 paired-end NovaSeq SP 500 cycle kit. The V3–V4 regions were detected on a NovaSeq 6000 sequencing platform (Genewiz, Suzhou, China).

Bacterial profiling

Raw sequence reads were filtered and quality-trimmed using Trimmomatic 0.3925as follows: (1) trimming low-quality reads (quality score < 20); (2) removing reads < 50 bp; and (3) excising the sequencing adapters. Contaminating reads of human sequences were removed using Kneaddata with default parameters against the reference database GRCh38 p12. The bacterial communities in the HCC tumors and adjacent tissues were profiled using the QIIME2 Metagenomic Analysis Platform26. The trimmed sequence reads from the bacterial communities were imported into QIIME2 (version 2021.8) for de-multiplexing. The de-multiplexed paired-end reads were joined using VSEARCH27. Post merging the joined reads with the QIIME2 plugin VSEARCH, they were quality-controlled using the QIIME2 plugin q-score, followed by de-multiplexing with the QIIME2 plugin VSEARCH. Concurrently, the bacterial feature table and representative sequences were generated and clustered into OTUs using the QIIME2 plugin VSEARCH at 99% identity against the Greengenes 13_8 99% OTUs reference database. The resulting OTUs were then imported into the QIIME2 plugin Phylogeny for constructing a phylogenetic tree for phylogenetic diversity analyses28. Taxonomy was assigned using the q2-feature-classifier plugin in QIIME2 against the Greengenes 13_8 99% OTUs reference database, and a taxonomy-based phylogenetic tree was generated using Interactive Tree of Life (iTOL) v629.

Statistical analyses

The study utilised unrarefied sequence data, considering that bacterial analysis without rarefaction can yield more accurate and reliable results in bacterial diversity analyses. Multiple diversity indices were calculated using the QIIME2 plugin diversity alpha, including the Shannon diversity, Simpson diversity, Observed Features diversity, Chao1 diversity, and Pielou’s Evenness diversity. Subsequently, a pairwise Kruskal-Wallis test was executed to evaluate the alpha diversity within each categorized characteristic group using the QIIME2 plugin diversity, and the p-values were corrected using the Benjamini and Hochberg method. Any p-value less than 0.05 was deemed statistically significant in this study.

The unrarefied sequence reads were utilized to compute the Jaccard and Bray-Curtis distance using the QIIME2 plugin diversity beta. Furthermore, to determine the differences in beta diversity among different characteristic groups, a permutational analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) was performed in QIIME2 with 999 permutations. The p-values for multiple testing were corrected using the Benjamini and Hochberg method as a part of the pairwise PERMANOVA test, with a p-value less than 0.05 considered statistically significant. The differential patterns in intrahepatic bacterial communities across all the predefined characteristic groups were visualized using Principal Coordinates Analysis (PCoA).

The linear discriminant analysis (LDA) of effect size (LEfSe) analysis was performed in Galaxy version 1.0 for the detection of the differentially abundant taxa between/among predefined different characteristic groups. LEfSe helped in biomarker discovery and explaining the identified features that characterize the differences between each categorized characteristic group. A non-parametric factorial Kruskal-Wallis sum-rank test was performed to identify features with significant differential abundance in the group of interest, setting the alpha value as 0.05 and the threshold on the logarithmic LDA score for discriminative features as 2.0.

Results

Metataxonomic analysis of HCC

We performed 16S rRNA gene sequencing to assess the metataxonomic structure and abundance of the intrahepatic microbiota in HCC patients. In total, 40,069,070 sequence reads were imported into QIIME2 after they were treated with Kneaddata. Merging with VSEARCH resulted in 357,577 joined reads. After filtering based on the q-score, 357,248 joined reads remained. The remaining joined reads were then de-replicated, and the bacterial feature table and representative sequences were generated using VSEARCH. The generated feature table and representative sequences were clustered into OTUs using VSEARCH at 99% identity against the Greengenes 13_8 99% OTUs reference database. In total, 607 OTUs with a total frequency of 42,191 were detected in 38 samples (provided in supporting information).

Intrahepatic microbiome diversity

LEfSe analysis identified class Actinobacteria and order Actinomycetales as marker taxa for HCC cirrhosis (Fig. 2a and b & S1). Additionally, order Lactobacillales, family Veillonellaceae, genera Rhodobacter and Megasphaera were found to be enriched in HCC tumors, while genus Pseudochrobactrum was enriched in the adjacent tissues of HCC patients (Fig. 2c and d & S2). Relative taxa abundance analysis at the phylum level revealed that Proteobacteria was the most abundant phylum in cirrhotic and noncirrhotic groups, as well as in three different stages of HCC (Fig. S3).

To investigate the intrahepatic microbiome of patients with HCC, we compared alpha and beta diversity within/between defined groups: the tumor and the adjacent tissue, hepatic tissue with and without capsule invasion, with and without HBV infection, cirrhotic and noncirrhotic, as well as alpha and beta diversity within/among different tumor stages. We found that the bacterial diversity estimated by the Pielou’s Species Evenness index had no statistically significant differences within each predefined group. By calculation of beta diversity using the Jaccard coefficient of similarity, we have not detected any statistically significant difference in beta diversity between these defined groups: the tumor and the adjacent tissue, hepatic tissue with and without capsule invasion, with and without HBV infection, cirrhotic and noncirrhotic. Estimating the beta diversity based on Bray-Curtis similarity, no statistically significant differences were found between/among predefined groups. Indeed, no statistically significant differences were detected within/between the HCC tumors and adjacent tissues by diversity analysis in this work.

Abundance of certain bacteria altered in the HCC tumor microenvironment of patients with capsule invasion

By estimating the Shannon index and the Simpson index, we found that the hepatic tissues of patients with capsule invasion statistically significantly differed from those of patients without capsule invasion (Shannon index, p-value: 0.007, Fig. 3a; Simpson index, p-value: 0.028, Fig. 3b). A Venn diagram showed that 162 OTUs were shared between patients with and without capsule invasion (Fig. 3c). Notably, 89 of 607 OTUs were unique for liver tissues from HCC patients with capsule invasion. We then calculated beta diversity using Jaccard and Bray-Curtis. However, we could not detect any statistically significant differences in the intrahepatic bacterial composition between patients with and without capsule invasion. By analyzing the relative frequencies of bacterial compositions in liver tissues, Proteobacteria, Actinobacteria, and Firmicutes were the top three abundant phyla in liver tissues of HCC patients with capsule invasion and those without capsule invasion (Fig. S4).

Bacterial diversity analysis in HCC with/without capsule invasion. a Based on Shannon entropy, we consider the liver tissues from HCC patients with capsule invasion to have a higher feature richness with a p-value of 0.007. b Based on Simpson entropy, the bacterial evenness of liver tissues from HCC patients with capsule invasion is better than that of HCC without capsule invasion with a p-value of 0.028. c The Venn diagram shows the overlap of OTUs shared between HCC with/without capsule invasion.

The bacterial profile of HCC is statistically significantly different among various cancer stages

Since the amount of patients diagnosed with HCC stages III and IV was not comparable with stages I and II, we combined the patients with HCC stages III and IV as stage Advanced. By analyzing using the Observed Features index, the Shannon index and the Chao 1 index, alpha diversity of HCC stage II was statistically significantly higher than HCC stage I (the Observed Features index, p-value: 0.010, Fig. 4a; Shannon index, p-value: 0.015, Fig. 4b; Chao 1 index, p-value: 0.048, Fig. 4c). Estimating the alpha diversity by the Chao1 index and the Observed Features diversity index, statistically significant differences in bacterial composition were observed within HCC cancer stage II and stage Advanced (the Observed Features index, p-value: 0.010, Fig. 4a; Chao1 index, p-value: 0.009, Fig. 4c). Venn diagrams displaying the overlap between groups showed that 50 of the total 607 OTUs were shared among all stages, 115 of the total 607 OTUs were shared between stage I and II, 94 of the total 607 OTUs were shared between stage II and stage Advanced (Fig. 4d).

Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) of the binary_jaccard distance at the OTU level revealed that the intrahepatic bacterial compositions of the HCC stage II were statistically significantly different from those of the HCC stages I and Advanced respectively (II versus I, p-value: 0.007; II versus Advanced, p-value: 0.007; Fig. 4e and f). Especially nine liver tissues were shared among all stages, indicating their similarities in bacterial features.

To further explore the relationship between the differences in bacterial abundance and the prognoses of various stages of HCC, we performed LEfSe analysis with a logarithmic LDA score cutoff ≥ 2.0. The LEfSe analysis results confirmed that order Actinomycetales was the only enriched taxon in patients at HCC stage Advanced (Fig. 4g & Fig. S5).

Furthermore, we have generated a phylogenetic tree of the intrahepatic microbiome at the phylum level (Fig. 5a), which showed the evolutionary relationship between different intrahepatic bacteria based on DNA sequences. As revealed by 16S rRNA analysis, the bacterial phyla of Proteobacteria, Actinobacteria, and Firmicutes were the three dominant populations both in HCC tumors and the adjacent tissues (Fig. 5b).

Discussion

In this study, we characterized the intrahepatic microbiomes of a cohort of HCC patients by direct bacterial analysis in postoperative specimens, aiming to evaluate the bacterial composition in HCC tumors and adjacent tissues in an attempt to elucidate the link between dysbiosis and HCC, which in turn may indicate that alterations in bacterial compositions may be associated with the development of HCC. The main findings of this study include the identification of the dominant bacterial phyla Proteobacteria, Actinobacteria, and Firmicutes in the liver of HCC patients, and order Lactobacillales, family Veillonellaceae, genera Rhodobacter and Megasphaera were enriched in tumors. In contrast, genus Pseudochrobactrum was enriched in adjacent tissues. Class Actinobacteria and order Actinomycetales were revealed as marker taxa for cirrhosis. Upon exploring the relationship between intrahepatic microbiome profile and prognoses of various stages of HCC, we found that order Actinomycetales was the marker taxa for advanced HCC stages. Contrary to our expectations, we did not detect any relationship between intrahepatic microbiome profile and other characteristics, such as fatty liver and HBV infection, which may be due to the low sequencing depth and will be resolved in future studies.

A recent study employed an integrative approach by analyzing whole-transcriptome RNA-sequencing, 850k DNA methylation sequencing data, and liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry-based metabolomics data to investigate bacterial profiles in HCC patients22. This comprehensive analysis facilitated the identification of an association between the abundance of intratumoral bacteria and HCC. Moreover, the study could extend our analysis to a functional level, elucidating the interactions between specific intratumoral bacteria and metabolites as well as genetic alterations in HCC, despite a substantial variation at the taxonomic level. In this study, using the 16S rRNA sequencing methods, we validated the presence of bacteria in HCC, the association between intrahepatic microbiome and various stages of HCC, the presence of cirrhosis. These data suggest that the intrahepatic microbiome may play a role in cirrhosis in HCC patients, and intrahepatic microbiome analysis may assist in the diagnosis of HCC by predicting the advanced stages of the disease. It may serve toward new therapeutic possibilities in the future. Future research endeavors are necessitated to ascertain the precise relationship between altered intrahepatic microbiomes and capsule invasion in HCC patients. These investigations should incorporate analyses based on DNA, transcriptomics, methylation, and metabolomics. Additionally, examining the potential role of intrahepatic microbiomes in predicting stages of HCC is imperative. This information is poised to be pivotal in the development of novel diagnostic and therapeutic modalities for HCC. Furthermore, intrahepatic microbiome could affect the survival of patients with HCC after surgery as well, thereby it can serve as biomarkers for predicting patient outcomes30. In our current study, we did not delve into the association between the intrahepatic microbiome and postoperative outcomes for HCC patients. However, future research will prioritize this area, with the objective of pinpointing microbial biomarkers predictive of postoperative survival in HCC patients.

Most bacterial communities found in the tumoral environment are normally present in the gut microbiota, suggesting a bacterial translocation from the gut to the tumors10,31. In this work, three phyla are dominant in the liver tissues of patients with HCC, namely, Actinobacteria, Firmicutes and Proteobacteria, which have been observed as the main phyla in the gut32. We thus suspect that crosstalk might exist between the HCC microbiome composition and the gut microbiota33. The interplay of gut microbiome and liver immunology and disease has been well recognized33. The impairment in the gut-liver axis is believed to contribute to the onset of various diseases such as alcohol-associated liver disease (ALD), nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), and autoimmune liver disease (AILD)33,34. While the reported gut-liver communications are based on molecule lever, we suspect that cell-lever communication may exist. It means that bacterial cells, including cells among Actinobacteria, Firmicutes, and Proteobacteria, may migrate from the gut to the liver. Consequently, in unhealthy liver tissues such as tumors, these bacterial cells may escape the ill local immune systems34, which eventually leads to enrichment. A most recent study based on 16S rRNA sequencing and transcriptome analysis suggests that the bacterial 16S rRNA gene sequences identified in liver tissues of HCC and cirrhosis patients may not indicate the presence of live bacteria, but rather fragments of bacterial DNA that have been translocated into the liver35. While our current study does not delve into the gut microbiota of the same patients, we acknowledge the significance of this interplay. Further exploration is warranted to clarify the translocation of bacteria or bacterial DNA fragments from the gut to HCC. Animal models or in vitro functional analysis may be suitable in this situation.

Based on Fig. 4a-c, one can observe that bacterial diversity is elevated during the early stage of tumor progression (from stage I to II) but lowered back in the late stages (from stage II to advanced). We suspect that such a tendency in bacteria diversity along the tumor progression is accosted with the alternations in the tumor microenvironment during the process36,37. It has been elucidated that a dynamic and reciprocal relationship develops early in tumor growth, between cancer cells and components of the tumor microenvironment that supports cancer cell survival, local invasion, and metastatic dissemination38. To overcome a hypoxic and acidic microenvironment, the tumor microenvironment promotes angiogenesis to restore oxygen and nutrient supply and remove metabolic waste. During this process, the tumor microbiome could be enriched as well. In the late stages of the tumor, the competition of nutrients and oxygen among cancer cells becomes prominent, which may in turn lead to suppression of tumor microbiome39. It is also reported that the diffusivity of nanoparticles in the tumor microenvironment changes dynamically with the physical properties of the extracellular matrix40. This may also contribute to our findings, as matrix porosity is a governing factor limiting bacteria growth41.

Indeed, no statistically significant alpha diversity was detected in the HCC tumors and adjacent tissues, and no statistically significant beta diversity was observed comparing the bacterial composition of the HCC tumors versus the adjacent tissues and cirrhotic versus noncirrhotic in this work. Nevertheless, through employing the LEfSe analysis for comparison between the two groups, we discerned taxa with statistically significant differential abundance. This finding suggests that in microbiome analysis, the adoption of diverse analytical methodologies may yield more comprehensive results. Furthermore, it could be instrumental in unearthing underlying biological disparities. In this pilot study, we adopted 16S rRNA V3–V4 sequencing, which limited the resolution to the genus level. It is imperative to identify HCC-related bacteria at the strain level by using highly accurate metagenomic sequencing, which also offers functional information about the intrahepatic microbiota. However, our study did not attempt to define a universal bacterial signature in HCC tumors but to reinforce the concept that it is an altered bacterial balance that characterizes the HCC tumors versus the adjacent tissues in patients with HCC, which may increase the significance of the bacterial presence at the level of liver cell transformation. It is necessary to elucidate the impact of the intrahepatic microbiome on the development and progression of HCC both in animal models and human studies. It is imperative to concentrate on investigating the associations between microbiota, and the development and progression of HCC utilizing metagenomic sequencing approaches. Concurrently, elucidating the functionality of the intrahepatic microbiota through transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses is of paramount importance. Ultimately, microbiota-oriented diagnostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets for personalized treatment approaches of HCC may be identified. While further studies are needed to validate the results on the intratumoural microbiome, our current findings could enlighten studies on the potential clinical translation of intratumoural microbiome of HCC.

Although this study identified limited microbial differences between cancerous and adjacent tissues, as well as between cirrhotic and non-cirrhotic samples, these differences, even at higher taxonomic levels (such as the order level), may still play a role in the progression of HCC. While only a few genera were significantly enriched in cancerous tissues, previous studies suggest that these microbes could indirectly influence the tumor microenvironment by modulating immune responses or altering metabolic pathways16,22. The observed enrichment of Rhodobacter and Megasphaera in HCC tissues, though limited, might contribute to the pro-inflammatory environment often associated with HCC. However, further high-resolution studies are required to confirm these associations. Future research should employ metagenomic and metabolomic analyses to better identify and understand the roles of key microbes involved in liver cancer development.

Conclusions

This study provides new insights into the intrahepatic microbiome of patients with hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), revealing distinct bacterial compositions associated with various clinical features. Our findings indicate that the dominant bacterial phyla in HCC tumors and adjacent tissues are Proteobacteria, Actinobacteria, and Firmicutes. Specifically, the enrichment of genera such as Rhodobacter and Megasphaera in tumors, and Pseudochrobactrum in adjacent tissues, suggests potential roles of these bacteria in the tumor microenvironment and disease progression. The association of class Actinobacteria and order Actinomycetales with cirrhosis, as well as the enrichment of order Actinomycetales in advanced stages of HCC, highlights the relevance of these bacterial taxa in disease severity and progression.

Our study demonstrates that bacterial diversity is higher in HCC stage II compared to stage I and advanced stages, which may reflect changes in the tumor microenvironment as the disease progresses. Although we did not find statistically significant differences in alpha or beta diversity between tumors and adjacent tissues, or between cirrhotic and non-cirrhotic groups, the differential abundance of certain taxa, identified through LEfSe analysis, suggests that intrahepatic bacteria could play a role in HCC pathogenesis.
