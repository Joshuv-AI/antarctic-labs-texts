Rroid2 regulates effector-to-memory CD8+ T cell differentiation during infection in vivo
Authors: Julia Erber, Carmen Stecher, Valerie Plajer, Nina Braun, William Mallard, Loyal A. Goff, Iros Barozzi, Thomas Mohr, John L. Rinn, Richard A. Flavell, Dietmar Herndler-Brandstetter
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SignificanceLong noncoding RNAs (lncRNAs) have emerged as key regulators of numerous cellular processes. While thousands of lncRNAs have been identified, the function of most lncRNAs remains unknown. To explore their role in the immune response to infection, we analyzed lncRNA expression in cytotoxic T cells and deleted six lncRNAs in mice. Our findings reveal that loss of Rroid2 impairs cytotoxic T cell expansion, function, and effector-to-memory differentiation by fine-tuning key transcriptional regulators and signaling pathways. In summary, this study enhances our understanding of Rroid2-mediated immune regulation and establishes Rroid2 as a key regulator of cytotoxic T cell differentiation.

Approximately 80% of the genome is transcribed, with the majority of the transcriptome comprising noncoding RNAs, in particular long noncoding RNAs (lncRNAs) (1, 2). While initially dismissed as transcriptional noise, lncRNAs have emerged as key regulators in numerous cellular processes, and their abundance has been associated with developmental complexity (3–6). LncRNAs are defined as RNAs that do not code for protein and that are longer than 200 bp (7, 8). They can be categorized based on their genomic positioning relative to protein-coding genes into long intergenic noncoding RNAs, sense or antisense lncRNAs, intronic lncRNAs, bidirectional RNAs, and enhancer RNAs (9). LncRNAs show more restricted expression patterns than messenger RNAs (mRNAs), are often highly cell type–specific, and can act in cis on the genome and chromatin to fine-tune gene expression, enhance transcription, or mediate antisense regulation (10). Although thousands of lncRNAs have been identified, the function of the majority of lncRNAs is still unknown (11).

CD8+ T cells play a key role in recognizing and eliminating infected or malignant cells. Upon activation, CD8+ T cells differentiate into a variety of effector and memory cell types. Although the phenotypic and functional changes that occur during CD8+ T cell differentiation are well characterized (12), the impact of lncRNAs in fine-tuning the expression of transcription factors to regulate this highly orchestrated process is incompletely understood.

LncRNAs play a crucial role in the adaptive and innate immune system (9, 13), and subset-specific lncRNA expression patterns have been reported for CD4+ T cell helper lineages and CD8+ T cell subsets (14, 15). In CD8+ T cells, lncRNAs have been shown to regulate excessive T cell activation (16), modulate T cell differentiation (17–19), and influence survival and function (20–22). Although many lncRNAs are expressed in CD8+ T cells, only few have been experimentally validated for their biological significance in CD8+ T cell differentiation and function in vivo. Understanding which lncRNAs fine-tune the expression and/or activity of transcription factors to regulate CD8+ T cell differentiation offers insights into potential therapeutic strategies to modulate immune responses. By manipulating these regulatory pathways, it may be possible to enhance immunity against infections and cancer or to suppress autoimmune and inflammatory conditions.

In this study, we first characterize the lncRNA expression profile of CD8+ T cell subsets following infection with Listeria monocytogenes. We then select six candidate lncRNAs and generate lncRNA knockout (KO) mouse models to investigate the in vivo role of these lncRNAs during homeostasis and infection. This way, we identify a lncRNA, termed “regulatory region of Id2” (Rroid2). Rroid2 is absent in naïve CD8+ T cells but plays a critical role in effector CD8+ T cell proliferation and function as well as effector-to-memory CD8+ T cell. We then show that Rroid2 plays a key role in fine-tuning transcription factors like inhibitor of DNA binding 2 (Id2) and T-bet, thereby influencing the differentiation and function of CD8+ T cells.

Results

Expression and Regulation of lncRNAs in CD8+ T Cell Subsets during Infection.

To assess genome-wide expression of lncRNAs in CD8+ T cell subsets during infection, we used an ovalbumin-expressing Listeria monocytogenes (LM-OVA) model and performed deep RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) of purified OT-I cell subsets at different time points (SI Appendix, Fig. S1A). RNA-seq analysis confirmed a characteristic gene expression profile for naïve, short-lived effector cells (SLEC), memory precursor effector cells (MPEC), and memory CD8+ T cells using a set of 48 established key genes (23–25) (SI Appendix, Fig. S1B). Next, we identified 843 lncRNAs that were expressed in at least one of the four OT-I cell subsets (FPKM >1; Fig. 1A and Dataset S1). 5.58% of the expressed lncRNAs displayed a high expression level [fragments per kilobase of transcript per million fragments mapped (FPKM) >20], and this proportion was comparable between the four CD8+ T cell subsets (Fig. 1B). 667 lncRNAs were differentially expressed between the CD8+ T cell subsets (fold change ≥1.5) and were assigned to 23 different clusters based on their expression pattern (Fig. 1 C and D). To identify candidates for generating lncRNA KO mice, we identified 78 lncRNAs with a high expression (FPKM ≥20) in at least one of the four OT-I cell subsets (SI Appendix, Fig. S1C), of which 29 lncRNAs were differentially regulated between the CD8+ T cell subsets (Fig. 1 E and F and SI Appendix, Fig. S1D). Of those 29 lncRNAs, we selected six lncRNAs that displayed different expression profiles and that did not overlap with any coding or noncoding genes. LncRNA-117 and lncRNA-124 (cluster 8), were low in naïve but upregulated in day 7 effector CD8+ T cells, lncRNA-111 (cluster 12) was highly expressed in naïve and memory CD8+ T cells, whereas lncRNA-104, lncRNA-106, and lncRNA-128 (clusters 15 and 23) were highly expressed in naïve CD8+ T cells and down-regulated in effector CD8+ T cells.

Generation and Phenotypic Characterization of lncRNA KO Mice.

Before we generated lncRNA KO mice, we confirmed the expression profile of the six lncRNAs in OT-I cell subsets by using RNA samples from additional LM-OVA experiments and quantitative PCR (SI Appendix, Fig. S2A and Table S1). In addition, the analysis of lncRNA expression in 11 different tissues revealed that most of the six lncRNAs were expressed in the spleen, lymph node, and thymus (SI Appendix, Fig. S2B). To generate lncRNA KO mice, we used CRISPR/Cas9-mediated genome engineering and designed single-guide RNAs (sgRNAs) specific for each of the loci of the six lncRNA candidates (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 C–H and Tables S2 and S3). To investigate the in vivo role of the six lncRNAs, we crossed lncRNA+/− heterozygous mice with each other to obtain lncRNA wild-type (WT) and KO littermates. Deletion of the lncRNA-106 locus appeared to be embryonic lethal, since lncRNA-106+/− to lncRNA-106+/− breeding did not produce lncRNA-106−/− mice (SI Appendix, Fig. S2I). And lncRNA-111+/− mice displayed severe bilateral periocular alopecia with ocular discharge that had to be treated with antibiotic ophthalmic ointment (SI Appendix, Fig. S2J). LncRNA-111+/− mice were therefore not bred to generate homozygous KO mice. LncRNA-104−/−, lncRNA-117−/−, lncRNA-124−/−, and lncRNA-128−/− mice were born at the anticipated Mendelian frequency and did not show any obvious abnormalities.

We then analyzed the frequency of CD8+ T cell subsets in the blood and spleen of lncRNA-104−/−, lncRNA-111+/−, lncRNA-117−/−, and lncRNA-128−/− mice during homeostasis. No significant differences were detected in the frequencies of CD8+ T cell subsets among the mice (SI Appendix, Figs. S3 and S4 A and B). LncRNA-111 was also expressed in CD4+ T cells and Foxp3+ Treg cells (BioGPS) (26), however, lncRNA-111+/− mice did not display any differences in the frequency of CD4+ T cells or Foxp3+ Treg cells in the spleen (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 C and D). Following infection of WT, lncRNA-104−/−, and lncRNA-117−/− mice with Listeria monocytogenes, the frequency of CD8+ T cells, KLRG1+ CD44hi and CD8+ TEM cells in the spleen 7 d post infection was comparable between lncRNA KO and WT mice (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 E–G).

To investigate the impact of lncRNA-104 and lncRNA-117 deficiency in an antigen- and T cell–specific manner, we adoptively transferred WT and lncRNA KO naïve OT-I cells into C57BL/6 recipient mice followed by LM-OVA infection the next day (SI Appendix, Fig. S4H). On day 7 post infection, the percentage of lncRNA-104−/− and lncRNA-117−/− effector OT-I cells among CD8+ T cells was higher compared to WT OT-I cells, and lncRNA-104−/− and lncRNA-117−/− OT-I cells expressed KLRG1 at a higher percentage than WT OT-I cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S4I, data from one experiment). On day 30 post infection, the frequency of memory OT-I cells in the blood and spleen was comparable between WT and KO OT-I cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S4J). However, lncRNA-104−/− OT-I cells still had a higher percentage of KLRG1 expression than WT and lncRNA-117−/− OT-I cells. Functional analysis revealed no difference in the percentage of tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α) and CD107a expression between WT and lncRNA KO OT-I cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S4K).

To explore the spatial expression relationship of the six lncRNAs and their neighboring genes, we calculated the expression correlation between lncRNAs (104, 106, 111, 117, 124, and 128) and neighboring genes based on the expression levels in all four OT-I cell subsets from our deep RNA-seq dataset (SI Appendix, Fig. S1A). Our findings reveal both coregulated and antagonistic expression patterns between lncRNAs and their neighboring genes, highlighting potential target genes of the six lncRNAs (SI Appendix, Fig. S5A). For example, we found a strong coregulated expression pattern between lncRNA-104 and Ccm2, lncRNA-111 and Slc39a11, and lncRNA-124 and Id2 (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 A and B).

Rroid2 Regulates Foxp3+ Treg Cells during Homeostasis.

Similar to our approach with the other lncRNA KO mice, we then examined the effects of lncRNA-124 deficiency on immune cells under homeostatic conditions. Because lncRNA-124 (Gm36496) is located on chromosome 12, 90 kb upstream of the repressive transcription factor Id2 (SI Appendix, Figs. S1D and S2G), we termed the lncRNA regulatory region of Id2 (Rroid2). First, we isolated RNA from 13 innate and adaptive immune cell subsets from the spleen to quantify lncRNA Rroid2. Expression of Rroid2 was observed in effector-memory CD8+ and CD4+ T cells (TEM), CD4+CD25+ Treg cells, macrophages, NK cells, and NKT cells (Fig. 2A). The data also show that Rroid2 was efficiently deleted in immune cell subsets as well as in total splenocytes of Rroid2−/− mice (Fig. 2A and SI Appendix, Fig. S6A). To assess the frequency of immune cell subsets by flow cytometry, we developed different staining panels and the gating strategy is shown in SI Appendix, Fig. S3. No major differences in CD45+ cell number were observed in the spleen, bone marrow (BM), lymph node, and thymus of Rroid2−/− mice (SI Appendix, Fig. S5B). The number of CD8+ and CD4+ T cells, NK cells, NKT cells, and four myeloid cell subtypes in the spleen (Fig. 2B) and BM (SI Appendix, Fig. S6C) was comparable between WT and Rroid2−/− mice. Similarly, Rroid2 deficiency did not affect CD8+ and CD4+ T cell numbers in the lymph node, T cell development in the thymus, CD49a+ NK1.1+ innate lymphoid cells type 1 (ILC1s) and CD49b+ NK1.1+ NK cells in the liver, nor CD8+ and CD4+ T cell subsets in the spleen and BM (SI Appendix, Fig. S6 D–H). However, the percentage and number of Foxp3+ CD4+ Treg cells was increased in the spleen and BM of Rroid2−/− mice (Fig. 2C and SI Appendix, Fig. S6I). This increase was due to a higher number of CD44dim resting Treg cells, but not CD44hi activated Treg cells in spleen and BM of Rroid2−/− mice (Fig. 2D and SI Appendix, Fig. S6J). Foxp3+ CD4+ Treg cells were slightly increased in the thymus, which was due to both, recirculating mature Foxp3+ CD73+ Treg cells as well as newly produced Foxp3+ CD73− Treg cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S6 K and L).

Because Rroid2−/− mice had an increased frequency of Treg cells, we analyzed whether this may have any functional consequence, e.g., affecting tumor growth or the composition of tumor-infiltrating immune cells. We therefore challenged WT and Rroid2−/− mice subcutaneously with B16 melanoma (SI Appendix, Fig. S7A). Tumor growth and tumor weight 3 wk post injection was similar between WT and Rroid2−/− mice (SI Appendix, Fig. S7 B and C). We also did not notice any difference in the number of tumor-infiltrating CD45+ cells, Foxp3+ CD4+ Treg cells and 7 additional immune cells between WT and Rroid2−/− mice (SI Appendix, Fig. S7D).

Rroid2 Impairs Effector CD8+ T Cell Expansion and Effector-to-Memory CD8+ T Cell Differentiation Following Infection.

To explore the relevance of Rroid2 in CD8+ T cell differentiation, we adoptively cotransferred sorted naïve CD44lo CD62Lhi OT-I cells from Rroid2+/+ (CD45.1.2) and Rroid2−/− (CD45.2.2) at a ratio of 1:1 into WT recipient mice (CD45.1.1), and infected the mice with a sublethal dose of LM-OVA the following day (Fig. 3 A and B). Rroid2−/− OT-I cells showed a reduced expansion in the blood and spleen 7 d post infection compared to WT OT-I cells (Fig. 3 C and D and SI Appendix, Fig. S8 A–C). During the early contraction phase, the number of Rroid2−/− OT-I cells showed the greatest decrease, resulting in a 3.3-fold difference between Rroid2−/− and WT OT-I cells 14 d post infection (Fig. 3E). Paired analysis of cotransferred WT and KO OT-I cells revealed a decrease in SLECs and an increase in MPECs in Rroid2−/− OT-I cells in the blood and spleen 14 d post infection, whereas no difference was observed 30 d post infection (Fig. 3 F and G and SI Appendix, Fig. S8D). Similar to blood, the frequency of Rroid2−/− memory OT-I cells was severely reduced in the spleen and lymph nodes 30 and 60 d post infection (SI Appendix, Fig. S8 E and F), and Rroid2−/− memory OT-I cells showed an increased central-memory (TCM) and a decreased effector-memory (TEM) phenotype in the blood and spleen 30 d post infection (Fig. 3 H and I and SI Appendix, Fig. S8G).

Rroid2 Does Not Affect Early T Cell Activation or Activation-Induced Cell Death (AICD) In Vitro.

Next, we determined whether the decreased effector CD8+ T cell numbers at day 7 post infection is due to a decrease in early T cell activation or an increase in AICD. We therefore isolated splenocytes from WT and Rroid2−/− mice and stimulated them with anti-CD3 and anti-CD28 in vitro (SI Appendix, Fig. S9A). Four hours post stimulation, a similar number of WT and Rroid2−/− CD8+ T cells expressed the activation marker CD69, the differentiation marker CD44, and the apoptotic marker Annexin V (SI Appendix, Fig. S9 B–E). 48 h post stimulation, a similar frequency of cells expressed the activation marker CD25, CD44, and the proliferation marker Ki-67 (SI Appendix, Fig. S9 D and E). In addition, similar results were observed when stimulating purified WT and Rroid2−/− OT-I cells with anti-CD3 and anti-CD28 antibodies in vitro (SI Appendix, Fig. S9 F and G).

Rroid2-Deficient Effector CD8+ T Cells Display Transcriptomic and Functional Changes.

Next, we chose an unbiased approach to explore the underlying mechanism of how Rroid2−/− regulates CD8+ T cell differentiation, by performing RNA-seq of adoptively cotransferred WT and Rroid2−/− OT-I cell subsets 7 and 14 d post infection with LM-OVA. Principal component analysis revealed that the biological duplicates for each of the three WT and three KO OT-I cell subsets generally showed good consistency (SI Appendix, Fig. S10A). Whereas differentially expressed gene (DEG) analysis did not detect major differences between WT and Rroid2−/− SLECs 7 d post infection, 198 genes were downregulated in Rroid2−/− SLECs 14 d post infection (Fig. 4A and Dataset S2). We performed gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) using decoupleR (27) to determine pathways and transcription factors with differential activities in WT and Rroid2−/− SLECs at day 14 post infection. Our analysis revealed that the activity of ten pathways was downregulated in Rroid2−/− compared to WT SLECs 14 dpi, including the JAK-STAT (Pou2af1, Socs1, Socs3), NFκB (Nfkb2, Nfkbid, Relb), and MAPK pathways (Map4k2, Map3k9, Fosl1) (Fig. 4B). Accordingly, a lower score for seven transcription factors, including NFκB/RELA, ESR1, and CEBPA/B, was determined for Rroid2−/− SLECs at day 14 post infection (Fig. 4C). A heatmap of 32 genes, including previously described effector and memory genes (12) along with key genes identified by our pathway activity analysis, further highlights the pronounced changes observed between WT and Rroid2−/− SLECs 14 dpi (Fig. 4D). Because several genes associated with MAPK, EGFR, p53, and NFκB pathway signaling were downregulated in Rroid2−/− effector OT-I cells, we analyzed the proliferation marker Ki-67. Ki-67 protein expression was downregulated in circulating Rroid2−/− OT-I cells 7 dpi (Fig. 4E). This could explain the decreased expansion of Rroid2−/− effector OT-I cells in the blood and spleen at day 7 pi (Fig. 3C and SI Appendix, Fig. S8C). Both, KLRG1+ and KLRG1− effector OT-I cells showed a decrease in Ki-67 MFI on day 7 pi (Fig. 4F). As indicated by RNA-seq analysis, Id2 and T-bet proteins were also found to be downregulated (by 10 to 12%) in Rroid2−/− KLRG1+ and KLRG1− effector OT-I cells in the spleen 14 dpi (Fig. 4 G and H), but not 7 dpi (SI Appendix, Fig. S10 B–D). Accordingly, expression of the degranulation marker CD107a was decreased in KLRG1+ and KLRG1− effector OT-I cells in the spleen on day 14 pi (Fig. 4I), and expression of granzyme B (GZMB) was decreased in CD44+ CD8+ T cells of Rroid2−/− compared to WT mice 7 dpi with LM-OVA (SI Appendix, Fig. S10E). In contrast, Rroid2−/− deficiency did not affect the expression of interferon gamma (IFN-γ) and TNF-α in effector OT-I cells 14 dpi with LM-OVA (SI Appendix, Fig. S10F).

Next, we investigated the locus of Rroid2 located on chromosome 12, 90 kb upstream of the repressive transcription factor Id2. Published ChIP-seq (H3K27ac, H3K4me1, and H3K4me3) and DNase-seq data in spleen and thymus indicate that the Rroid2 locus shows a signature of a transcriptionally active enhancer (SI Appendix, Fig. S10 G and H) (28). Overlap with highly evolutionary conserved stretches of noncoding DNA (PhyloP basewise score) and with candidate cis-regulatory elements (cCRE; as predicted by ENCODE) further support a distal enhancer-like signature with conserved sequences within exon 1 and intron 1 of Rroid2 (SI Appendix, Fig. S10G) (29). Notably, lncRNA Rroid is located ~220 kb upstream of Id2, which regulates group 1 ILCs (30). To assess whether Rroid might also regulate OT-I cell subsets following infection with LM-OVA, we analyzed the mRNA expression of Rroid2 and Rroid. Our results showed that Rroid2 but not Rroid is expressed in effector and memory OT-I cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S10I). Additionally, we examined the subcellular localization of Rroid2 by performing cytoplasmic, nuclear, and chromatin fractionation followed by quantitative PCR. This revealed that Rroid2 is a nuclear lncRNA (Fig. 4J). As controls, we used the two lncRNAs Neat1 and Malat1, known to be enriched in the chromatin fraction (31), and Actb, which is abundant in the cytoplasmic and nuclear fraction.

To gain insight into putative regulatory elements within the Rroid2 locus, we performed locus overlap analysis (LOLA) to investigate T cell–specific transcription factor binding site (TFBS) enrichment at the Rroid2 genomic region. Our analysis revealed that binding motifs for specific transcription factors were enriched in exon 1 and intron 1 of the Rroid2 locus, including Jun (JunB, JunD), STATs (Stat3, Stat4, Stat5a, Stat5b, Stat6), Batf, Irf4, and Tbx21 (SI Appendix, Fig. S11 A–F and Dataset S3), which are known to be involved in T cell activation and effector T cell differentiation (32).

Lower Frequency of Rroid2-Deficient Memory CD8+ T Cells Limits Their Expansion upon Antigen Rechallenge.

Next, we investigated the responsiveness of WT and Rroid2−/− memory OT-I cells following rechallenge with LM-OVA 34 d after the primary infection (Fig. 5A). Analysis of OT-I cell frequency 4 d post rechallenge revealed a reduced expansion in Rroid2−/− compared to WT OT-I cells (Fig. 5 B–D). 61.5% (8/13) of Rroid2−/− memory OT-I cells compared to only 15% (2/13) of WT memory OT-I cells failed to expand (>0.05% of OT-I cells among CD45+ cells; Fig. 5C). No changes were observed in the proportion of SLEC, double positive effector cells (DPEC), and MPEC between WT and Rroid2−/− OT-I cells on day 4 post rechallenge (Fig. 5E).

To test the ability of memory OT-I cells in response to antigen reencounter on a per cell basis, we transferred the same number of WT and Rroid2−/− memory OT-I cells into naive recipient mice and challenged with LM-OVA (SI Appendix, Fig. S12A). No differences in weight loss and bacterial load in the spleen and liver were observed in recipient mice that received either WT or KO memory OT-I cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S12 B and C). Both WT and KO memory OT-I cells expanded to a comparable extent 6 d after LM-OVA challenge (SI Appendix, Fig. S12 D–F), and expressed similar amounts of Id2, T-bet, granzyme B, IFN-γ, and TNF-α (SI Appendix, Fig. S12 G and H). These results indicate that the lower frequency of Rroid2−/− memory CD8+ T cells limits their expansion upon antigen rechallenge, whereas the expansion and function of Rroid2−/− memory CD8+ T cells on a per cell basis is not impaired.

The Human lncRNA LINC01814 Shows Partial Conservation with Rroid2, Is Located Upstream of ID2 and Is Upregulated in Human Memory CD8+ T Cells.

By using PhyloP basewise conservation analysis, we show that Rroid2 exhibits strong evolutionary conservation (SI Appendix, Fig. S13A). BLAT homology analysis revealed that the lncRNA LINC01814, which is 140 kb antisense upstream of the human ID2, is the human orthologue of mouse Rroid2 (SI Appendix, Fig. S13B). Publicly available DNA methylation data from different human cell types revealed that Jurkat T cells showed very low DNA methylation at exon 1 of LINC01814, whereas fibroblasts, HEK293 cells, HepG2 cells, MCF7 cells, melanocytes, and osteoblasts showed very high DNA methylation (SI Appendix, Fig. S13C). This indicates that LINC01814 may be specifically transcribed in human T cells. Interestingly, LINC01814 mRNA showed low expression in human naïve CD8+ T cells but high expression in memory CD8+ T cells, similar to the mouse Rroid2 expression profile (SI Appendix, Fig. S13D). Publicly available ChIP-seq data from the Immunological Genome (ImmGen) project indicate that human memory CD8+ T cells, but not total CD8+ T cells, show H3K27ac and H3K4me1 peaks within LINC01814, indicating active enhancers (SI Appendix, Fig. S13E). The last exon of LINC01814-201 also shows highest homology with mouse Rroid2 and overlaps with a human ID2 enhancer. Together, these data suggest that LINC01814 is the human orthologue of mouse Rroid2 and may have a similar functional role given its proximity to human ID2 and its elevated expression in human memory CD8+ T cells.

Discussion

In this study, we characterized the lncRNA expression landscape of CD8+ T cells following bacterial challenge and investigated the in vivo role of six lncRNAs by generating KO mouse models. We demonstrated that Rroid2 deficiency impaired effector CD8+ T cell expansion and cytotoxic function as well as effector-to-memory CD8+ T cell differentiation. Rroid2 deficiency affected both KLRG1+ and KLRG1− effector CD8+ T cells, by fine-tuning the transcription factors Id2 and T-bet.

Of the six lncRNA KO mice, lncRNA-106, known as small nucleolar RNA host gene 12 (Snhg12), appeared to be embryonic lethal. Snhg12 is a ubiquitously expressed and evolutionary conserved lncRNA. It was shown that silencing of Snhg12 increased DNA damage and vascular senescence (33, 34), and promoted tumorigenesis by acting as a sponge for microRNAs (35). Genetic disruption of TATA-box binding protein associated factor 12 (Taf12) has also been shown to cause embryonic lethality in the mouse (36). Taf12 is located 13.5 kb upstream of Snhg12 and has an expression pattern antagonistic to Snhg12 in CD8+ T cell subsets (Pearson correlation coefficient > 0.9). Another explanation of the embryonic lethality phenotype of lncRNA-106 KO mice could be the concomitant deletion of snoRNAs (Snora16a, Snora44, Snora61, and Snord99) located within the introns of lncRNA-106. The loss of snoRNAs has been shown to cause developmental defects in other model organisms (37). LncRNA-111, known as 2610035D17Rik or Rason, was ubiquitously expressed in tissues and showed high expression in naïve and memory CD8+ T cells. Its expression profile in CD8+ T cell subsets correlated with the expression of its neighboring gene Slc39a11 (Pearson correlation coefficient > 0.95), a zinc transporter (38). LncRNA-111+/− mice did not show any differences in CD8+ T cell subsets, but KO of Rason in mouse embryonic fibroblasts inhibited Kirsten rat sarcoma virus (KRAS)-mediated tumor transformation (39) or LncRNA-111+/− mice displayed severe chronic inflammation of the eyes, which was not due to systemic changes in the frequency of Foxp3+ Treg cells. Due to the poor breeding performance and the severe inflammation of the eyes, we did not generate homozygous lncRNA-111 KO mice to further investigate the relevance of lncRNA-111 in CD8+ T cell biology.

The other four lncRNAs (lncRNA-104, lncRNA-117, lncRNA-124, and lncRNA-128) showed a more restricted tissue expression pattern and the corresponding KO mice were born at the anticipated Mendelian frequency and without obvious abnormalities. Although lncRNA-104−/− and lncRNA-117−/− mice did not display any differences in the frequency of CD8+ T cell subsets during homeostatic conditions, a higher frequency of effector OT-I cells and KLRG1 expression was evident 7 d post challenge with LM-OVA. This phenotype may be explained by a study demonstrating that human lncRNA SNHG15 is a negative regulator of inflammation (40). SNHG15 interfered with K63-linked ubiquitination of tumor necrosis factor receptor-associated factor 2 and repressed activation of the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK)/nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB) signaling pathways thereby preventing the production of proinflammatory cytokines.

Rroid2 (Gm36496) was expressed in the thymus, BM, and spleen (29) and was upregulated in effector and memory CD8+ T cells. We found a strong coregulated expression pattern in CD8+ T cell subsets between Rroid2 and Id2 (Pearson correlation coefficient > 0.95), and Rroid2-deficient effector OT-I cells displayed a 10 to 12% reduction in Id2 and T-bet protein expression. Id2 and T-bet are key transcription factors that regulate CD8+ T cell differentiation and function (12). Id2 promotes the survival and cytotoxicity of effector CD8+ T cells, and supports effector-to-memory CD8+ T cell differentiation (23). In particular, Id2−/− CD8+ T cells fail to induce Tbx21 expression, and show lower expression of the antiapoptotic molecules Bcl2 and Serpinb9 and higher expression of the proapoptotic molecule Bcl2l11 (Bim) (23). However, the effects observed in Id2−/− CD8+ T cells were not fully phenocopied by Rroid2−/− CD8+ T cells. Both Id2 and Rroid2 deficiency resulted in reduced cytotoxic capacity of effector CD8+ T cells (CD107a, Gzma, Gzmb, Serpinb9), reduced memory CD8+ T cell numbers, and a shift toward an effector-memory phenotype (23), which was also observed in T-bet-deficient CD8+ T cells (41). While Id2−/− CD8+ T cells proliferated normally in vivo (23) and were unable to generate KLRG1+ effector CD8+ T cells (42), Rroid2−/− CD8+ T cells displayed reduced proliferation and only a subtle decrease in KLRG1+ effector CD8+ T cells (SLEC and DPEC) 14 d post infection. The KLRG1+ phenotype was not sustained into the memory phase, likely due to the downregulation of Rroid2 in memory OT-I cells, which resulted in comparable Id2 levels in rechallenged WT and KO memory OT-I cells. Interestingly, Rroid2−/− as well as Id2−/− CD8+ T cells downregulated numerous killer cell lectin like receptor (KLR) genes, such as Klra3, Klrc1, Klrd1, and Klrk1 (43). In contrast to Rroid2−/− CD8+ T cells, Id2-deficient CD8+ T cells upregulated Id3, and high expression of Id3 was sufficient to inhibit the generation of KLRG1+ effector CD8+ T cells (42). Rroid2-deficient CD8+ T cells also did not show any regulation of Eomes, Tcf7, or Bcl2l11 (43). Instead, Bcl2l12, which inhibits p53-dependent DNA damage-induced apoptosis in CD4+ T cells (44), was downregulated in Rroid2−/− CD8+ T cells. Importantly, Rroid2 deficiency affected both, KLRG1+ and KLRG1− effector CD8+ T cells, and similarly reduced their expression of Ki-67, Id2, T-bet, and CD107a. Further analysis using RNA-seq revealed that Rroid2 deficiency downregulated 198 genes in SLECs 14 d post infection. Rroid2−/− SLECs displayed a lower JAK-STAT (Pou2af1, Socs1, Socs3, Stat3), NFκB (Nfkb2, Nfkbid, Relb) and MAPK (Map4k2, Map3k9, Fosl1) pathway activity. These results reinforce our observations of reduced CD8+ T cell proliferation and a diminished ability of effector cells to differentiate into memory CD8+ T cells (12, 45). Furthermore, lncRNA Snhg1 and Pou2af1, both of which promote memory T cells and recall responses (46, 47), were down-regulated in Rroid2−/− SLECs.

Apart from effector/memory CD8+ T cells, Rroid2 was also expressed in CD4+CD25+ Treg cells. During homeostatic conditions, Rroid2 deficiency led to an increase in Foxp3+ CD44dim resting Treg cells, which was due to an increase in circulating Foxp3+ CD73+ Treg cells as well as newly produced Foxp3+ CD73− Treg cells in the thymus. This phenotype may result from the role of the transcriptional regulator Id2 in maintaining the regulatory T cell pool (48). In particular, Treg-specific expression of Id2 in mice has been shown to reduce the Treg compartment (49). In addition, T-bet has been shown to promote survival and functional fitness of peripheral Treg cells (50).

We detected Rroid2 expression also in memory CD4+ T cells, NK cells, NKT cells, and macrophages. Because Id2 has been shown to regulate numerous immune cell lineages, e.g., promote NK cells, NKT cells, innate lymphoid cells (ILCs), and erythroid cell development, and impair the development of B cells and CD8α+ dendritic cells (51–54), we quantified immune cell subsets in Rroid2−/− mice, including Id2-dependent lineages. However, our analysis did not reveal any differences in the frequency of NK cells, NKT cells, and ILCs in Rroid2−/− mice. In contrast to Id2−/− mice, Rroid2−/− mice did not show gradually retarded growth, increased neonatal lethality, or a lack of lymph nodes (55). The fine-tuned downregulation of Id2 via Rroid2 may thus not be sufficient to affect the development and survival of NK cells, NKT cells, and ILCs. Alternatively, lncRNA Rroid2 may have more than one function and the biological significance of each of those functions may be dependent on the cellular context in which the lncRNA is expressed, as has been shown for the lncRNA Morrbid (22).

Notably, the locus upstream of Id2 encodes a series of lncRNAs, such as Gm17746, Gm36287, Gm36496 (Rroid2), Gm47705, Gm36441, Gm47713, Gm46343, and Gm36723 (Rroid). Of those lncRNAs, RNA-demarcated regulatory region of Id2 (Rroid), located 220 kb upstream of Id2, has been shown to be specifically expressed in ILC1s. The Rroid locus, but not the lncRNA itself, was required for lineage identity and function of ILC1s, while CD8+ T cells were not affected (30). Consistent with these findings, we did not detect lncRNA Rroid expression in our CD8+ T cell subsets. The locus upstream of Id2 also harbors a number of cCREs, some of which overlap with the lncRNAs. An enhancer located within Rroid2 activated a minimal SCP1 promoter by CapStarr-seq with weak activity in P5424 T cells and strong activity in NIH3T3 fibroblasts (28). A weak enhancer activity could explain the 10% decrease in Id2 protein in Rroid2−/− CD8+ T cells. The proximity of the human orthologue of Rroid2, lncRNA LINC01814, to human ID2, and the finding that the conserved region between Rroid2 and LINC01814 also overlaps with an ID2 enhancer, strengthen our findings that Rroid2 deficiency leads to a decrease in Id2 protein expression in effector OT-I cells, and suggests a similar mechanism in human CD8+ T cells. Accordingly, LINC01814 exhibited a low expression in human naïve CD8+ T cells but a high expression in memory CD8+ T cells, similar to Rroid2.

In summary, our results demonstrate that Rroid2 deficiency promotes Foxp3+ regulatory T cells but impairs CD8+ T cell proliferation and cytotoxicity. Rroid2 deficiency also leads to a decreased frequency of memory CD8+ T cells, in particular effector-memory cells. Mechanistically, Rroid2 leads to a fine-tuned downregulation of the transcription factors Id2 and T-bet in both KLRG1+ and KLRG1− effector CD8+ T cells, while also downregulating genes associated with the JAK-STAT and NF-κB signaling pathways. The human orthologue of Rroid2, LINC01814, also overlaps with an ID2 enhancer and shows a high expression in human memory CD8+ T cells. Thus, Rroid2 represents a key regulatory layer that controls both qualitative and quantitative aspects of CD8+ T cell responses to pathogens.

Materials and Methods

Human Peripheral Blood Samples.

Human peripheral blood samples were collected from systemically healthy volunteers (age 26 to 46 y, two women and one man). Informed written consent was obtained from all study participants and the study was approved by the ethics committee of the Medical University of Vienna (ECS #1874/2023). Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMC) were isolated by density gradient centrifugation using Ficoll-Paque PLUS (GE Healthcare). Viable (Zombie Aqua-negative) human naïve (CD45RA+CD62L+) and memory (CD45RA-) CD3+CD8+ T cells were sorted from PBMCs using a FACSMelody Cell Sorter (BD Biosciences).

Mice.

C57BL/6J (#000664), C57BL/6J CD45.1 (#002014), and OT-I mice (#003831) were obtained from The Jackson Laboratory, Janvier Labs or Charles River Laboratories. Rag2−/− mice (RAGN12) were obtained from Taconic Biosciences. Age- and sex-matched mice (littermates wherever possible) were used in all experiments. Primers used for genotyping are listed in SI Appendix, Table S3. Initial animal experiments were performed in compliance with Yale Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee protocols. All experiments were approved by the animal ethics committee of the Medical University of Vienna and the Austrian Federal Ministry of Education, Science and Research (66.009/0407-V/3b/2018 and 66.009/0408-V/3b/2018).

Generation of lncRNA KO Mice.

LncRNA KO mice were generated by C.S. and D.H.-B. in the lab of Richard A. Favell (Yale University, USA; MTA #20104) using CRISPR/Cas9-mediated genome engineering. sgRNAs and Cas9 mRNA were microinjected into fertilized one-cell embryos. The surviving zygotes were transplanted into pseudopregnant foster mothers. SgRNAs were designed using published sgRNA design tools (56, 57) and validated using NCBI Blast to avoid off-target effects. The sequence of the sgRNA oligos used to generate lncRNA KO mice is shown in SI Appendix, Table S2. The cutting efficiency of CRISPR/Cas9 sgRNAs was determined using the SURVEYOR Mutation Detection Kit (Integrated DNA Technologies). Sanger Sequencing of PCR amplicons was performed to confirm KO alleles. The PCR products were purified using a QIAquick PCR Purification Kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Forward sequencing primers (4 µM) were added to the purified amplicons and then they were sent for sequencing (Keck DNA Sequencing Core at Yale School of Medicine).

Adoptive OT-I Cell Transfer and Infection with Listeria Monocytogenes.

Splenocytes were isolated from Rag2−/− OT-I (CD45.1.2) and Rag2−/− OT-I lncRNA KO mice (CD45.2.2) and a single cell suspension was generated by mechanical disruption and filtering through a 70 μm cell strainer using R10 medium (RPMI-16040 supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% Penicillin/Streptavidin). Following centrifugation at 500 g for 5 min at 4 °C, red blood cell lysis was performed using an ammonium–chloride–potassium (ACK) lysis buffer [150 mM NH4Cl, 10 mM KHCO3, and 0.1 mM Na2 ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA)]. The cells were stained and naïve CD44lo CD62Lhi CD45+ CD8+ OT-I cells were sorted using a FACSMelody (BD Biosciences) or an Aria Cell Sorter (BD Biosciences). 2 × 105 WT (CD45.1.2) and Rroid2−/− naïve OT-I cells (CD45.2.2) were adoptively transferred at a ratio of 1:1 into C57BL/6J (CD45.1.1) recipient mice using distinct congenic markers. LM-OVA aliquots were thawed and cultured overnight at 37 °C in brain heart infusion (BHI) broth supplemented with 75 μg/mL erythromycin. On the next day, the bacteria were subcultured for 4 to 5 h in fresh BHI medium supplemented with 75 μg/mL erythromycin and the number of bacteria was determined by measuring OD600 on a spectrophotometer. One day after the transfer of OT-I cells, the mice were infected intravenously (i.v.) with 2 × 105 LM-OVA. For rechallenge experiments, the mice were infected i.v. with 2 × 106 LM-OVA.

Tumor Challenge.

B16-F10 melanoma cells were authenticated by STR profiling and grown in R10 medium at 37 °C and 5% CO2 until they reached 80% confluency. 1 × 105 B16 melanoma cells were injected subcutaneously into both flanks of 8 to 12 wk old WT and Rroid2−/− mice. Tumor growth was monitored using caliper measurement. Tumor volume was calculated using the following formula: 0.5 × length × width2. Tumor-infiltrating immune cells were isolated by cutting the tumor into small pieces and digesting it with 1 mg/mL Collagenase D for 30 min at 37 °C on a shaker. Thereafter, the tumor homogenate was filtered through a 70 µm cell strainer (Thermo Fisher Scientific), washed with R10 medium at 500 g for 5 min at 4 °C and taken up in 1 mL 10% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) in FBS for freezing. After the in vivo tumor experiments were completed, all tumor samples were thawed, washed, stained with fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies in 100 µL fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) buffer and measured on an LSR Fortessa X-20 flow cytometer (BD Biosciences) to determine the frequency of tumor-infiltrating immune cell subsets.

Isolation of Immune Cells from Lymphoid and Nonlymphoid Organs.

Peripheral blood was collected by retro-orbital bleeding or cardiac puncture and was immediately mixed with 100 µL phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) with heparin (250 U/mL). A single cell suspension from the spleen as well as axillary and brachial lymph nodes was generated by mechanical disruption and filtering through a 100 µm nylon mesh filter (Lactan) using R10 medium. Bone was isolated and BM cells were flushed out from the tibia and femur using FACS buffer and a syringe with a 27G needle. Cells from the liver were isolated by mechanical disruption and lymphocytes from the lung were isolated by cutting the lung into small pieces. The homogenates were digested for 1 h at 37 °C with 0.5 mg/mL Collagenase IV in R10 medium. Liver homogenates were centrifuged at 17 g for 3 min to remove hepatocytes and lung homogenates were filtered through a 70 µm cell strainer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and centrifuged at 300 g for 10 min, as has been described previously (58). Red blood cell lysis was performed using an ACK lysis buffer. Following centrifugation with 500 g for 5 min at 4 °C, the cells were resuspended in FACS buffer (1× PBS supplemented with 2% FBS) for staining with fluorophore-conjugated antibodies.

Flow Cytometry.

For immunofluorescence surface staining, fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies were added to single cell suspensions in 100 µL FACS buffer and incubated for 30 min at 4 °C protected from light. Following incubation, 1 mL of FACS buffer was added, the cells were centrifuged and then resuspended in 200 µL 4 °C FACS buffer for measurements at the flow cytometer. For intracellular staining of Foxp3, Ki-67, T-bet, Id2, and GZMB, the Foxp3/Transcription Factor Staining Buffer Set (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used. The stained cells were measured on an LSRFortessa X20 flow cytometer (BD Biosciences) using FACSDiva software (BD Biosciences). The antibodies used for flow cytometry and cell sorting are listed in SI Appendix, Table S4. Flow cytometry data were analyzed using FlowJo V10 software (BD Biosciences). Absolute numbers were calculated by cell counting with a Neubauer chamber and determining relative amounts using FlowJo V10.

Intracellular Cytokine Staining.

Splenocytes or purified OT-I cell subsets were stimulated with phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate and ionomycin (BioLegend, #423302) in the presence of 1× Brefeldin A (BioLegend, #420601) for 3 to 4 h. The cells were washed and surface staining was performed. Thereafter, the cells were fixed and permeabilized using the Cytofix/Cytoperm kit (BD Biosciences, #554722) and stained for IFN-γ and TNF-α. For the detection of the degranulation marker CD107a, cells were stimulated as described above but without Brefeldin A. CD107a fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC) was added to the cell culture well for the entire stimulation period.

In Vitro Activation Assay.

For the short-term activation of CD8+ T cells, 24-well plates were coated with anti-mouse CD3 antibody (1 µg/mL in PBS) and incubated in the dark at 4 °C for at least 6 h. The spleen of WT and Rroid2−/− mice were collected and a single cell suspension was generated. 2 × 106 splenocytes per mL were seeded in R10 medium supplemented with anti-CD28 (0.5 µg/mL) onto the anti-CD3-coated plates and activated for the indicated period. For the short-term activation of OT-I cells, WT and Rroid2−/− OT-I cells were seeded at 1 to 2 × 106 cells/mL in R10 medium with anti-CD28 (1 µg/mL) and IL-2 (20 ng/mL) onto the anti-CD3-coated plates (2 µg/mL) and activated for the indicated period. Annexin V was stained in the presence of 1× Annexin V binding buffer (10× buffer: 0.1 M 4-(2-hydroxyethyl)-1-piperazineethanesulfonic acid (HEPES) pH 7.4, 1.4 M NaCl, and 25 mM CaCl2).

RNA Isolation, cDNA Synthesis, and qPCR.

RNA was extracted from the spleen, axillary and brachial lymph nodes, liver, lung, heart, thymus, small intestine, kidney, BM, skin, and stomach of 8 to 10 wk old C57BL6/J mice. Tissue specimens were frozen in 500 µL TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Following thawing, tissue samples were mechanically disrupted using a Polytron PT 2100 homogenizer (Kinematica) and incubated for 5 min for complete nucleoprotein complex dissociation. Immune cell subsets from the spleen of WT and Rroid2−/− mice were sorted using a FACSMelody (BD Biosciences) either directly into TRIzol reagent (less than 150,000 cells) or into RPMI-1640 supplemented with 50% FBS, followed by centrifugation and resuspension in TRIzol reagent. The samples in TRIzol were then snap frozen. RNA isolation was performed using TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and the RNeasy Plus Mini kit (Qiagen, #74134) for tissue samples or the RNeasy Mini kit (Qiagen, #74104) for immune cell subsets. OT-I cell subsets were isolated from the spleen, enriched for CD8+ T cells using the MojoSort Mouse T Cell Isolation Kit (BioLegend, #480008) and then sorted into 700 µL RTL Plus lysis buffer supplemented with β-mercaptoethanol. RNA isolation was performed using the RNeasy Micro kit (Qiagen, #74004).

RNA from mouse tissues was reverse transcribed using a Superscript III Reverse Transcriptase (Invitrogen, #18080085). qPCR was performed using a KAPA SYBR FAST qPCR Master Mix (2×) with ROX low on a 7500 Fast Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems). RNA of sorted immune cell subsets from WT and Rroid2−/− mice was reverse transcribed using a RevertAid First Strand complementary DNA (cDNA) Synthesis Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). qPCR was performed using a Maxima SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific) on a CFX Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad Laboratories). The expression was normalized to a housekeeping gene (Actb) using the comparative threshold cycle method (2−∆Ct). Primers used for qPCR are listed in SI Appendix, Table S1.

In order to quantify bacteria in tissues after rechallenge, DNA was isolated from spleen and liver tissue pieces from mice 3 d post rechallenge with LM-OVA using the QIAamp DNA Micro Kit (Qiagen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. qRT-PCR was performed on a BioRad CFX96 Touch Real-Time PCR Detection System using Maxima SYBR Green + ROX master mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Primer oligonucleotides were synthesized by Sigma Aldrich. Relative expression values of LM-OVA genomic DNA were normalized to mouse genomic DNA using the comparative threshold cycle method (2−∆Ct). Primer sequences were 16s_LM-OVA (forward: CTTCCGCAATGGACGAAAGT, reverse: ACGATCCGAAAACCTTCTTCATAC) and C57BL/6 chromosome 6 (forward: CTGGTTTTCGCCTCTAAAGC, reverse: CAGAGCTGCGAGCCTTTC).

RNA-seq and Computational Analysis.

Naïve (CD62Lhi CD44lo), effector (SLEC: KLRG1+CD127-; DPEC: KLRG1+CD127+), and memory OT-I cells were isolated from the spleen (SI Appendix, Fig. S1A) and sorted into TRIzol using an Aria Cell Sorter (BD Biosciences). For each OT-I cell subset, the cells were pooled from 2 to 3 mice. Total RNA was isolated using the RNeasy Plus Mini kit (Qiagen, #74134). Deep RNA-seq was performed using a HiSeq 2000 System (Illumina). To detect lncRNAs, 75 bp paired-end sequencing was used to reach 100 million reads per OT-I cell subset. The sequencing reads were aligned to the Mus musculus reference genome version GRCm38 using TopHat2. Cufflinks was employed to calculate the fragments per kilobase of transcript per million fragments mapped (FPKM). To identify lncRNAs, the sequencing data were filtered based on the absence of protein domains (Pfam database) and open reading frames longer than 30 bp (PhyloCSF <100) to exclude transcripts with high coding potential. Cuffdiff was used for the analysis of differential expressed transcripts (q < 0.05) (59).

For bulk RNA-seq, naïve and SLEC/MPEC OT-I cells 7 and/or 14 dpi with LM-OVA were sorted into 700 µL RTL Plus lysis buffer supplemented with β-mercaptoethanol. RNA isolation was performed using the RNeasy Micro kit (Qiagen, #74004). RNA-seq libraries were prepared at the Genomics Core Facility (Medical University of Vienna) using the SMART-Seq Stranded Kit (#634442) and Unique Dual Index Kit 1 to 24 (#634756) according to the manufacturer’s protocol (Takara Bio). Libraries were QC-checked on a Bioanalyzer 2100 (Agilent) using a High Sensitivity DNA Kit for correct insert size and quantified using Qubit dsDNA HS Assay (Invitrogen). Pooled libraries were sequenced using a P3 flow cell on a NextSeq2000 instrument (Illumina) in 1 × 50 bp single-end sequencing mode. On average, 32 million reads per sample were generated. Reads in fastq format were generated using the Illumina bcl2fastq command line tool (v2.19.1.403) including trimming of the sequencing adapters. Reads in fastq format were aligned to the Mus musculus reference genome version GRCm38 with Gencode mV23 annotations (60) using STAR aligner (61) version 2.6.1a in 2-pass mode. Raw reads per gene were counted by STAR (61). Differential gene expression was analyzed using DESeq2 (62) version 1.43.5. Visualization was generated with ggplot2 package, heatmaps were created with ComplexHeatmap package (63), volcano plots with the EnhancedVolcano Package (64) and GSEA with decoupleR (27). Ctse, which was highly significantly regulated between WT and Rroid2−/− SLEC at days 7 and 14, was excluded in the volcano plots of SLEC d7 and d14, because Ctse, which is located close to Ptprc (CD45), has been shown to be regulated due to the genetic background of C57BL/6 CD45.1 mice (65). Additionally, small nuclear RNA Gm23680 was excluded in the volcano plot of SLEC d14.

Cell Fractionation.

To determine the subcellular localization of Rroid2, subcellular fractionation to obtain cytoplasmic, nucleoplasmic, and chromatin fractions was performed as described previously (66). 10 × 106 splenocytes from C57BL6/J mice were isolated and resuspended and vortexed in 380 µL ice-cold HLB (50 mM Tris-HCl pH7.4, 50 mM NaCl, 3 mM MgCl2, 0.5% NP-40, 10% glycerol), supplemented with 100U SUPERase·In RNase Inhibitor (Invitrogen, #AM2694). Cells were incubated at 4 °C for 30 min, vortexed, and centrifuged at 1,000 g, for 5 min at 4 °C. The supernatant contained the cytoplasmic fraction. The pellet was resuspended and vortexed in 380 µL ice-cold Modified Wuarin-Schibler buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.4, 4 mM EDTA, 0.3 M NaCl, 1 M urea, 1% NP-40) (67) supplemented with 100U SUPERase·In RNase Inhibitor (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Lysis was performed for 10 min at 4 °C, followed by vortexing and another 10 min of lysis. After centrifugation, the supernatant contained the nuclear fraction and the chromatin was pelleted. For RNA isolation, samples were taken up in 1 mL TRIzol reagent and RNA isolation and qPCR were performed as described above. Primers used for qPCR are listed in SI Appendix, Table S1.

TFBS Enrichment Analysis.

We performed LOLA using the LOLA R package (68) to investigate TFBS enrichment at the lncRNA Rroid2 genomic region. Genomic coordinates of Rroid2 were manually defined and separated into promoter (1 kb upstream), exons, and introns. These regions served as the user sets, while the user universe was defined using all TFBS from the ENCODE TFBS mm10 collection included in the LOLA region database (dbPath). The runLOLA() function was executed to test for enrichment of TFBS across each Rroid2 region compared to the genomic background. Enrichment results were filtered to retain only transcription factors active in T cells and classified into transcription factor families using an external annotation based on the JASPAR database (69). Enriched TFs were defined as those with a false discovery rate (FDR) < 1.

Quantification and Statistical Analysis.

Data are presented as mean ± SEM. A P value below 0.05 was considered significant. Two-tailed Student’s t test was used for comparing two groups. One-way ANOVA with Tukey’s multiple comparisons test was used for testing multiple groups. Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 10. No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size. PCR genotyping determined group allocation, mice were processed in a random order and multiple investigators were involved in data generation and analysis.
