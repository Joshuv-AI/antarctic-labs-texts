Loop Weierstrass Representation
Thomas Raujouan and Nick Schmitt and Jonas Ziefle

Abstract
. We introduce the Loop Weierstrass Representation for minimal surfaces in Euclidean space and constant mean curvature 1 surfaces in hyperbolic space by applying integral system methods to the Weierstrass and Bryant representations. We unify associated families, dual surfaces and Goursat transformations under the same holomorphic data, we introduce a simple factor dressing for minimal surfaces, and we compute and classify various examples.



Introduction

Minimal surfaces in Euclidean
3
-space and constant mean curvature
1
(CMC 1) surfaces in hyperbolic
3
-space can be produced from holomorphic data by the Weierstrass and Bryant representations, respectively
[
17
,
2
]
. In this article, we introduce the Loop Weierstrass Representation (LWR), a single framework that allows for the construction of both types of surfaces. It recovers the Weierstrass and Bryant representations as special cases of a wider class of holomorphic representations that arise by introducing a loop parameter (originating from associated families) and a gauge freedom in the holomorphic data.
Consequently, all questions posed for the Weierstrass and the Bryant representations – such as closing, end behavior, symmetries, total curvature – can be covered in the single framework of the LWR. This unification leads to some structural insights, technical advantages and new aspects that we expose in the present paper:

•

The LWR produces various families of related surfaces from the same holomorphic data. It covers the classical associated family and extends it to a 4-real-parameter family (figure
2
). It also covers the Goursat transformations
[
10
]
as well as the dual CMC 1 surfaces introduced by Umehara and Yamada
[
15
]
. In the LWR, all these transformations arise after solving one single ODE, whether the target space is Euclidean or hyperbolic.

•

The LWR provides a suitable framework for simple factor dressing, an integrable system transformation that produces new surfaces from old ones. It adds planar or horospherical ends to a given surface while preserving its periods (figures
1
,
3
,
4
and
5
).

•

In the LWR, catenoids and surfaces with similar ends can be constructed from potentials that are locally gauge equivalent to potentials with simple poles.
This brings the theory of Fuchsian systems into the construction of minimal and CMC 1 surfaces, a strategy that has been fruitful for the DPW representation
[
6
]
.

•

An LWR potential for a surface with symmetries can be pushed down by a covering map to a potential on a simpler surface. For example,
n
-noids with Platonic symmetries can be constructed with an LWR potential on a three-punctured
\mathbb{C}P^{1}
, and the Schwarz

Holomorphic Representation of Surfaces

The representation of surfaces as immersions is based on their first and second fundamental forms, encoding the intrinsic and extrinsic geometry of the surface, respectively. The compatibility of the fundamental forms is given by the Gauss-Codazzi equations. By Bonnet’s theorem, immersions can locally be constructed, provided the Gauss-Codazzi equations are satisfied.

When studying isometric constant mean curvature
H
(CMC
H
) immersions of surfaces into spaces of constant sectional curvature
k
, the Lawson correspondence
[
12
]
states that the Gauss-Codazzi equations for two immersions are the same if the quantity
H^{2}+k
is the same for both. Thereby minimal (i.e. CMC
0
) surfaces in Euclidean space
\mathbb{E}^{3}
(i.e.
k=0
) correspond to CMC
1
surfaces in hyperbolic space
\mathbb{H}^{3}
(i.e.
k=-1
).
In the Weierstrass and Bryant representations, the correspondence becomes locally explicit in terms of an
\mathrm{sl}_{2}\mathbb{C}
-valued holomorphic one-form
\eta
of rank
1
that encodes the first and second fundamental forms.
Considering two different integration procedures for
\eta
yields two different holomorphic null-curves. One represents a minimal immersion in
\mathbb{E}^{3}
and is obtained by solving the ODE
d\psi=\eta
for
\psi
in
\mathrm{sl}_{2}\mathbb{C}
, the other one gives a CMC
1
immersion in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
and is obtained by solving the ODE
d\Psi=\Psi\eta
for
\Psi
in
\mathrm{SL}_{2}\mathbb{C}
.

Motivation for the LWR

In the following we explain how the LWR appears as a natural extension of the Weierstrass and Bryant representation by introducing an associate family parameter and a gauge freedom.

Firstly, we note that scaling the Hopf differential of a CMC
1
immersion in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
by a complex parameter
\lambda
and the metric by
\left|\lambda\right|^{2}
gives new solutions of the Gauss-Codazzi equation. The corresponding family of CMC
1
immersions is called an associated family.
For the Bryant representation this amounts to scaling the potential

\eta\longmapsto\zeta_{\lambda}:=\lambda\eta

and considering for the associated family of null-curves the ODE

d\Psi_{\lambda}=\Psi_{\lambda}\zeta_{\lambda}.

Secondly, we observe that gauging the null-curve by an
\mathrm{SL}_{2}\mathbb{C}
-valued holomorphic function
z\mapsto g(z)
, that is

(0.1)

\Psi_{\lambda}\longmapsto\Phi_{\lambda}:=\Psi_{\lambda}g,

changes the potential by

\zeta_{\lambda}\longmapsto\xi_{\lambda}:=g^{-1}\zeta_{\lambda}g+g^{-1}dg.

We thus arrive at the ODE

d\Phi_{\lambda}=\Phi_{\lambda}\xi_{\lambda}

for a potential
\xi_{\lambda}:=\lambda\alpha+\beta
with
\alpha
an
\mathrm{sl}_{2}\mathbb{C}
-valued holomorphic one-form of rank one.
Provided that the initial value for
\Psi_{0}
has been set to the identity, then
\Phi_{0}=g
and, from (
0.1
), one can read off the null curve
\Psi
as

\Psi=\Phi_{1}\Phi_{0}^{-1}.

Furthermore, any frame of the form
{\Phi_{\lambda_{1}}\Phi_{\lambda_{0}}^{-1}}
is a holomorphic null-curve and therefore produces a CMC
1
surface.
We call this representation of CMC
1
immersions the “Loop Weierstrass Representation” (LWR) and
\Phi_{\lambda}
the “LWR frame”.
The LWR possesses a gauge freedom since gauging the LWR frame by
\Phi_{\lambda}\mapsto\Phi_{\lambda}h
leaves the null-curve unchanged.

Null-curves
\psi
for minimal surfaces in Euclidean space can be obtained from the LWR frame
\Phi_{\lambda}
by taking a logarithmic derivative

\psi:=(\dot{\Phi}\Phi^{-1})_{\mid\lambda=\lambda_{0}},

where the partial derivative with respect to
\lambda
is denoted by the dot. This is reminicent of the transition from the Lie group
\mathrm{SL}_{2}\mathbb{C}
to the Lie algebra
\mathrm{sl}_{2}\mathbb{C}
. As an interpretation for this formula, minimal surfaces in Euclidean space can be seen as a blow-up limit of CMC
1
surfaces in hyperbolic space (Proposition
4.7
).

1  The Loop Weierstrass Representation

In this section, we introduce the Loop Weierstrass Representation (LWR) for constructing minimal immersions into Euclidean space and CMC
1
immersions into hyperbolic space. LWR relies on the Lie group
\mathrm{SL}_{2}\mathbb{C}
and its Lie algebra
\mathrm{sl}_{2}\mathbb{C}
, so we first recall in section
1.1
how to describe Euclidean and hyperbolic spaces in a matrix model. We recall some differential geometry definitions in Section
1.2
and state the constructions of Weierstrass
[
17
]
and Bryant
[
2
]
that produce minimal and CMC
1
surfaces out of holomorphic null curves. In Section
1.3
, we come to the LWR itself and show how it can build families of null curves from a given holomorphic connection, and can thus induce families of minimal and CMC
1
surfaces.

1.1  Ambient spaces

Minkowski space
\mathbb{R}^{1,3}
is identified with the real 4-dimensional vector space
\mathcal{H}_{2}
of 2-by-2 Hermitian matrices equipped with the Lorentzian inner product

(1.1)

\left\langle x,y\right\rangle:=-\frac{1}{2}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{tr}(x\widehat{y}),\quad x,y\in\mathcal{H}_{2}

where the hat denotes the adjugate matrix, i.e.:

(1.2)

\widehat{x}=\begin{pmatrix}d&-b\\
-c&a\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{if}\quad x=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}.

The Lorentzian norm is then given by the determinant:

(1.3)

\lVert x\rVert^{2}=-\det x.

Under this identification, hyperbolic space is

\mathbb{H}^{3}=\left\{x\in\mathcal{H}_{2}\mid\det x=1,\quad\mathop{\mathrm{}}{tr}x>0\right\}=\left\{FF^{*}\mid F\in\mathrm{SL}_{2}\mathbb{C}\right\},

and Euclidean space is identified with the tangent space of
\mathbb{H}^{3}
at the identity matrix
\mathrm{I}
, which is nothing else but the trace-free 2-by-2 Hermitian matrices:

\mathbb{E}^{3}=T_{\mathrm{I}}\mathbb{H}^{3}=i\cdot\mathrm{su}_{2}.

An orientation of
\mathbb{E}^{3}
is chosen so that
(x,y,x\times y)
is positively oriented, where

(1.4)

x\times y:=-\frac{i}{2}\left[x,y\right],\quad x,y\in\mathbb{E}^{3}.

The group
\mathrm{SL}_{2}\mathbb{C}
acts isometrically and transitively on
\mathbb{H}^{3}
via

(1.5)

x\mapsto VxV^{*}

where
V\in\mathrm{SL}_{2}\mathbb{C}
and
x\in\mathbb{H}^{3}
. This action extends the orientation from
\mathbb{E}^{3}=T_{\mathrm{I}}\mathbb{H}^{3}
to the entire
\mathbb{H}^{3}
.
The group
\mathrm{SU}_{2}\ltimes\mathbb{E}^{3}
acts isometrically and transitively on
\mathbb{E}^{3}
via

x\mapsto UxU^{-1}+T

where
U\in\mathrm{SU}_{2}
,
T\in\mathbb{E}^{3}
and
x\in\mathbb{E}^{3}
.

1.2  Null curves and minimal or CMC
1
immersions

For a given function
f
defined on a Riemann surface
\Sigma
with local coordinate
z=x+iy
, we write
f_{x}
,
f_{y}
,
f_{z}
, and
f_{\overline{z}}
for the
x
,
y
,
z
and
{\overline{z}}
derivatives of
f
, respectively. In this paper, all the maps defined on a Riemann surface
\Sigma
are assumed to be real-analytic, but not necessarily holomorphic. Let
\mathbb{X}^{3}
denote Euclidean space
\mathbb{E}^{3}
or hyperbolic space
\mathbb{H}^{3}
.

Definition 1.1
.

An immersion
f\colon\Sigma\to\mathbb{X}^{3}
is
conformal
if
\left\langle f_{z},f_{z}\right\rangle=0
. In such case, the first fundamental form (or
metric
) reads

(1.6)

ds^{2}:=\left\langle df,df\right\rangle=2\left\langle f_{z},f_{{\overline{z}}}\right\rangle\left|dz\right|^{2}.

The
unit normal
vector
N
of a conformal immersion
f
at a point
p=f(z_{0})
is the unit vector such that
(f_{x},f_{y},N)
is a positively oriented orthogonal basis of
\mathrm{T}_{p}\mathbb{X}^{3}
.

The
Hopf differential

Qdz^{2}
and the
mean curvature

H
of a conformal immersion at a point are defined via the second fundamental form:

(1.7)

-\left\langle df,dN\right\rangle=:Qdz^{2}+Hds^{2}+{\overline{Q}}d{\overline{z}}^{2}.

A conformal immersion into Euclidean space is
minimal
if
H\equiv 0
.
A conformal immersion into hyperbolic space is
CMC
1
if
H\equiv 1
.
An
umbilic
of
f
is a point
z_{0}\in\Sigma
such that
Q(z_{0})=0
.
If
H
is constant and
Q
is constantly vanishing, the immersion is
flat
.
The
Gauss-Codazzi equations
for minimal surfaces in Euclidean space and CMC
1
surfaces in hyperbolic space are the same. Writing
ds^{2}=4e^{2\omega}\left|dz\right|^{2}
, they are

(1.8)

\left|Q\right|^{2}=4e^{2\omega}\omega_{z{{\overline{z}}}},\quad Q_{\overline{z}}=0.

Definition 1.2
.

A map
\psi\colon\Sigma\to\mathrm{sl}_{2}\mathbb{C}
(resp.
\Psi\colon\Sigma\to\mathrm{SL}_{2}\mathbb{C}
) is a
holomorphic null curve
if it is holomorphic with nowhere vanishing differential, and if
\det d\psi
(resp.
\det d\Psi
) is constantly vanishing.

Theorem 1.3

(Weierstrass
[
17
]
)
.

Let
\psi
be a holomorphic null curve into
\mathrm{sl}_{2}\mathbb{C}
. Then
\psi+\psi^{*}
is a conformal, minimal immersion into Euclidean space. Moreover, any conformal, minimal immersion can locally be obtained this way.

Theorem 1.4

(Bryant
[
2
]
)
.

Let
\Psi
be a holomorphic null curve into
\mathrm{SL}_{2}\mathbb{C}
. Then
\Psi^{*}\Psi
is a conformal, CMC
1
immersion into hyperbolic space. Moreover, any conformal, CMC 1 immersion can locally be obtained this way.

1.3  The Loop Weierstrass Representation

We first introduce the Loop Weierstrass Representation as an algorithm, and then show that it produces holomorphic null-curves (Lemma
1.6
), and hence CMC
1
surfaces in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
and minimal surfaces in
\mathbb{E}^{3}
(theorem
1.7
).

Definition 1.5
.

An
LWR potential

\xi=(\xi_{\lambda})_{\lambda\in\mathbb{C}}
is an affine linear family of meromorphic 1-forms on a Riemann surface
\Sigma
with values in
\mathrm{sl}_{2}\mathbb{C}
, whose linear term is nilpotent. Locally, away from its poles, for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
, it can be written as

(1.9)

\xi_{\lambda}=(A\lambda+B)dz

where
z\in U\subset\Sigma
is a local coordinate,
A\colon U\to\mathrm{sl}_{2}\mathbb{C}
is holomorphic, nilpotent, and
B\colon U\to\mathrm{sl}_{2}\mathbb{C}
is holomorphic.

An
LWR frame

\Phi=(\Phi_{\lambda})_{\lambda\in\mathbb{C}}
is a holomorphic family of maps
\Phi_{\lambda}\colon\Sigma\to\mathrm{SL}_{2}\mathbb{C}
such that
\Phi^{-1}d\Phi
is an LWR potential.

The
Loop Weierstrass Representation
(LWR) is the following algorithm.

(1)

Take an LWR potential
\xi
defined on some Riemann surface
\Sigma
.

(2)

Let
\widetilde{\Sigma}
be the universal cover of
\Sigma
and solve for
\Phi_{\lambda}\colon\widetilde{\Sigma}\to\mathrm{SL}_{2}\mathbb{C}
the following initial value problem:

(1.10)

\begin{cases}\Phi^{-1}d\Phi=\xi,\\
\Phi_{\lambda}(z_{0})=C_{\lambda}\end{cases}

where
z_{0}\in\widetilde{\Sigma}
and
C_{\lambda}\in\mathrm{SL}_{2}\mathbb{C}
is holomorphic with respect to
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
.

(3)

Let
\lambda_{0}\neq\lambda_{1}\in\mathbb{C}
and define
\psi\colon\widetilde{\Sigma}\to\mathrm{sl}_{2}\mathbb{C}
and
\Psi\colon\widetilde{\Sigma}\to\mathrm{SL}_{2}\mathbb{C}
as

(1.11)

\psi:=(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{0})(\dot{\Phi}\Phi^{-1})_{\lambda_{0}},\quad\Psi:=\Phi_{\lambda_{1}}\Phi_{\lambda_{0}}^{-1}

where the dot denotes the partial derivative with respect to
\lambda
.

(4)

Define
f^{\mathbb{E}}\colon\widetilde{\Sigma}\to\mathbb{E}^{3}
and
f^{\mathbb{H}}\colon\widetilde{\Sigma}\to\mathbb{H}^{3}
as

(1.12)

f^{\mathbb{E}}:=\psi+\psi^{*},\quad f^{\mathbb{H}}:=\Psi^{*}\Psi.

The input data
(\Sigma,\xi,\Phi,\lambda_{0},\lambda_{1})
is called
LWR data
. The ordered pair
(\lambda_{0},\lambda_{1})
are the
evaluation points
. We will sometimes write
f^{\mathbb{X}}=\mathop{\mathrm{LWR}}(\Sigma,\xi,\Phi,\lambda_{0},\lambda_{1})
to denote the immersion induced by LWR.

Lemma 1.6
.

Fix
\lambda_{0}\neq\lambda_{1}\in\mathbb{C}
. For any LWR frame
\Phi
, the maps
\psi
and
\Psi
defined in (
1.11
) are holomorphic null curves (possibly branched).
Moreover, any holomorphic null curve into
\mathrm{sl}_{2}\mathbb{C}
or
\mathrm{SL}_{2}\mathbb{C}
can locally be obtained this way.

Proof.

Let
\xi
be the LWR potential of
\Phi
.
We first show that
\psi
is a holomorphic null curve. The LWR frame
\Phi
is holomorphic, so
\psi
is holomorphic. Compute

\displaystyle d(\dot{\Phi}\Phi^{-1})

\displaystyle=d(\dot{\Phi})\Phi^{-1}-\dot{\Phi}\Phi^{-1}d{\Phi}\Phi^{-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\left(d\Phi\right)}{\partial\lambda}\Phi^{-1}-\dot{\Phi}\xi\Phi^{-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\left(\Phi\xi\right)}{\partial\lambda}\Phi^{-1}-\dot{\Phi}\xi\Phi^{-1}

\displaystyle=\Phi\dot{\xi}\Phi^{-1}.

Therefore, with
\xi_{\lambda}=(A\lambda+B)dz
,

(1.13)

d\psi=(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{0})\Phi_{\lambda_{0}}A\Phi_{\lambda_{0}}^{-1}dz.

By definition of
\xi
,
\det A=0
, so
\det d\psi=0
. Similarly,
\Psi
is holomorphic and

(1.14)

d\Psi=(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{0})\Phi_{\lambda_{1}}A\Phi_{\lambda_{0}}^{-1}dz

so
\Psi
is a holomorphic null curve.

We now prove that all null curves can be obtained this way. Let
\psi\colon D\to\mathrm{sl}_{2}\mathbb{C}
be a holomorphic null curve from a simply-connected neighborhood
D
of
z_{0}\in\mathbb{C}
. Let
\alpha_{\lambda}=\frac{\lambda-\lambda_{0}}{\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{0}}
and let
\xi_{\lambda}=\alpha_{\lambda}d\psi
. The potential
\xi
is an LWR potential on
D
because
\psi
is a holomorphic null curve and
\alpha
is linear in
\lambda
.
Define the LWR frame
\Phi
on
D
as the unique solution of
\Phi^{-1}d\Phi=\xi
with
\Phi({z}_{0})=\exp\left(\alpha\psi(z_{0})\right)
. Let
X=(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{0})(\dot{\Phi}\Phi^{-1})_{\lambda_{0}}
. A computation gives
X({z}_{0})=\psi(z_{0})
because
\dot{\alpha}=(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{0})^{-1}
. Moreover,
\alpha_{\lambda_{0}}=0
, so
X_{z}=\psi_{z}
. By the Picard-Lindelöf Theorem,
\psi=X
.
The case of
\Psi\colon D\to\mathrm{SL}_{2}\mathbb{C}
is covered similarly with
\xi=\alpha\Psi^{-1}d\Psi
and
\Phi({z}_{0})=\exp\left(\alpha\log\Psi(z_{0})\right)
, computing that
\Phi_{\lambda_{0}}=\mathrm{I}
and
\Phi_{\lambda_{1}}=\Psi
.
∎

As a direct consequence of theorems
1.3
,
1.4
and lemma
1.6
, we have:

Theorem 1.7
.

Fix
\lambda_{0}\neq\lambda_{1}\in\mathbb{C}
. For any LWR frame
\Phi
, the maps
f^{\mathbb{E}}
and
f^{\mathbb{H}}
defined in (
1.12
) are minimal and CMC 1 conformal immersions, respectively.
Moreover, any conformal, minimal (resp. CMC 1) immersion into
\mathbb{E}^{3}
(resp.
\mathbb{H}^{3}
) can locally be obtained this way.

2  Gauge freedom and standard forms of LWR potentials

Definition 2.1
.

An
LWR gauge
is a meromorphic map
g\colon~\Sigma\to\mathrm{SL}_{2}\mathbb{C}
, independent of
\lambda
.

Lemma 2.2
.

LWR gauges act on LWR frames and potentials without changing the induced immersions.

Proof.

Let
\Phi
be an LWR frame on
\Sigma
with LWR potential
\xi
and let
g\colon\Sigma\to\mathrm{SL}_{2}\mathbb{C}
be an LWR gauge. Define

\xi\cdot g:=g^{-1}\xi g+g^{-1}dg.

It is easy to check that
\Phi\cdot g:=\Phi g
is a frame for
\xi\cdot g
. The fact that
g
does not depend on
\lambda
implies that
\xi\cdot g
is linear in
\lambda
and that the linear factor has vanishing determinant. Therefore,
\xi\cdot g
is an LWR potential, and
\Phi g
is an LWR frame. The
\lambda
-independence of
g
also implies that the holomorphic null curves
\psi
and
\Psi
are unchanged after gauging. Therefore, the immersions themselves are preserved.
∎

In this section, we give two useful representatives in the gauge orbit of a given LWR potential. The first one (section
2.2
) is linear in
\lambda
with no constant term and is directly related to the Weierstrass and Bryant representations. The second one (section
2.3
) is off-diagonal and is uniquely determined by the Schwarzian derivative of a spinorial quantity that we define in section
2.1
.

2.1  Spinors

Definition 2.3
.

Let
A\in\mathrm{sl}_{2}\mathbb{C}
be a nilpotent matrix. A vector
x=(u,v)\in\mathbb{C}^{2}
is a
spinor
for
A
if

(2.1)

A=\begin{pmatrix}-uv&u^{2}\\
-v^{2}&uv\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}u\\
v\end{pmatrix}(-v\quad u)=:xx^{\perp}.

A spinor for
A
is uniquely determined up to a sign. We extend the definition and say that
x\colon\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{C}^{2}
is a spinor for an LWR potential
\xi
if
xx^{\perp}=A
where
\xi=(A\lambda+B)dz
on some simply connected domain
\mathbb{D}\subset\Sigma
.

Remark 2.4
.

The notation
x^{\perp}
has been chosen because for all
x=(u,v)\in\mathbb{C}^{2}
,

x^{\perp}x=(-v\quad u)\begin{pmatrix}u\\
v\end{pmatrix}=0.

We give some properties of
x^{\perp}
that can be directly computed:

Lemma 2.5
.

For all
x:=(u,v)\in\mathbb{C}^{2}
, writing
\lVert x\rVert^{2}:=\left|u\right|^{2}+\left|v\right|^{2}
,

(2.2)

\left\langle xx^{\perp},(xx^{\perp})^{*}\right\rangle=\lVert x\rVert^{2},

(2.3)

[xx^{\perp},(xx^{\perp})^{*}]=-\lVert x\rVert^{2}(\lVert x\rVert^{2}\mathrm{I}-2xx^{*}),

(2.4)

\lVert[xx^{\perp},(xx^{\perp})^{*}]\rVert=\lVert y\rVert^{4},

(2.5)

\left\langle yy^{*},(yy^{\perp})^{\prime}\right\rangle=-\frac{1}{2}\lVert y\rVert^{2}y^{\perp}y^{\prime},

(2.6)

\forall\Phi\in\mathrm{SL}_{2}\mathbb{C},\quad\forall x\in\mathbb{C}^{2},\quad(\Phi x)^{\perp}=x^{\perp}\Phi^{-1}.

Proposition 2.6
.

Let
\xi
be an LWR potential on
\Sigma
. Let
\Phi
be a local LWR frame for
\xi
and let
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
. Let
y:=\Phi_{\lambda}x
where
x
is a spinor for
\xi
. Then the function

(2.7)

q:=\det(y,y_{z})

is well-defined on
\Sigma
and only depends on the orbit of
\xi
under LWR gauging (not on
\Phi
, not on
\lambda
).

Proof.

We first show that
q
satisfies

(2.8)

q=\det(x,Bx+x_{z})

where
\xi=(A\lambda+B)dz
.
To do so, differentiate
y=\Phi_{\lambda}x
using
d\Phi=\Phi\xi
and recall that
x^{\perp}x=0
to get

\displaystyle dy

\displaystyle=\Phi\xi x+\Phi dx

\displaystyle=\Phi(\lambda Ax+Bx+x_{z})dz

\displaystyle=\Phi(\lambda xx^{\perp}x+Bx+x_{z})dz

\displaystyle=\Phi(Bx+x_{z})dz.

Therefore,

q=\det(\Phi x,\Phi(Bx+x_{z}))

and (
2.8
) follows from
\det\Phi=1
. This shows that
q
is well-defined on
\Sigma
and does not depend on
\Phi
nor does it depend on
\lambda
.

To show that
q
is invariant under LWR gauging, let
\tilde{\xi}:=\xi\cdot g
where
g
is an LWR gauge. Let
\tilde{\Phi}:=\Phi g
, let
\tilde{x}
be a spinor for
\tilde{\xi}
and let
\tilde{y}:=\tilde{\Phi}\tilde{x}
for some
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
. Then by (
2.6
),
\tilde{x}=g^{-1}x
, so
\tilde{y}=y
and
q=\det(\tilde{y},\tilde{y}_{z})
.
∎

Definition 2.7
.

An LWR potential is
degenerate
if
q
is constantly vanishing, and
non-degenerate
otherwise.

2.2  Weierstrass potential

Proposition 2.8
.

Let
\xi
be an LWR potential on
\Sigma
and let
\Phi
be an LWR frame for
\xi
on a simply-connected domain
\mathbb{D}\subset\Sigma
. With
\Phi_{0}:=\Phi|_{\lambda=0}
,

(2.9)

\xi\cdot\Phi_{0}^{-1}=\lambda\begin{pmatrix}g&-g^{2}\\
1&-g\end{pmatrix}\frac{qdz}{g_{z}}

where

(2.10)

g:=u/v,\qquad q:=uv_{z}-vu_{z}

and

(u,v):=\Phi_{0}x

where
x
is a spinor for
\xi
.

Proof.

Write
\xi=(A\lambda+B)dz
so that
xx^{\perp}=A
and let
y:=\Phi_{0}x
. By (
2.6
),

\Phi_{0}A\Phi_{0}^{-1}=\Phi_{0}xx^{\perp}\Phi_{0}^{-1}=yy^{\perp}.

Moreover,
d\Phi=\Phi\xi
, so

\Phi_{0}d(\Phi_{0}^{-1})=-d\Phi_{0}\Phi_{0}^{-1}=-\Phi_{0}B\Phi_{0}^{-1}dz.

Therefore,

\xi\cdot\Phi_{0}^{-1}=\lambda yy^{\perp}dz

and (
2.9
) follows from
y=(u,v)
and the definitions of
g
and
q
.
∎

Remark 2.9
.

Let
\xi_{1}
be the potential given by (

).

(1)

The functions
g
and

in

do not depend on the choice of sign for the spinor

, and the definition of

in proposition
2.8
agrees with its definition in proposition

.

(2)

Since

is only defined locally on

\xi_{1}
.

(3)

Since

is uniquely determined by

up to conjugation by

, so is the function

. However, proposition

shows that the function

is uniquely determined by

.

(4)

As we will see in section

, the potential

is directly related to the Weierstrass representation: if

, then

is the Gauss map of

and the hyperbolic Gauss map of

. Moreover, if

is the Hopf differential of

and

.

2.3  Schwarz potential

Definition 2.10
.

Let
g\colon\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
be a meromorphic function. The map

\mathcal{S}[g]=\left(\frac{g^{\prime\prime}}{2g^{\prime}}\right)^{2}-\left(\frac{g^{\prime\prime}}{2g^{\prime}}\right)^{\prime}

is the
Schwarzian derivative
of
g
. It is invariant under Möbius transformations of
g
. It acts as a second order operator under pre-composition: for any meromorphic
\phi\colon\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
,

(2.11)

\mathcal{S}[g\circ\phi]=(\mathcal{S}[g]\circ\phi)(\phi^{\prime})^{2}+\mathcal{S}[\phi].

The following lemma justifies our normalization for the Schwarzian derivative and is merely a computation.

Lemma 2.11
.

Let
u,v
be two holomorphic solutions of the equation

y^{\prime\prime}=Sy

where
S
is a meromorphic function. If
u/v
is not constant, then
S=\mathcal{S}[u/v]
.

Theorem 2.12
.

Let
\xi=(A\lambda+B)dz
be a non-degenerate LWR potential in the coordinate
z
. Then there exists an LWR gauge
k
, unique up to a sign, such that

\xi\cdot k=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
\lambda q+s&0\end{pmatrix}dz

for some functions
q
and
s
.

Moreover, if
(u,v):=\Phi x
where
x
is a spinor for
A
and
\Phi
is an LWR frame for
\xi
at
\lambda=0
, then

q=uv_{z}-vu_{z}\qquad\text{and}\qquad s=\mathcal{S}[u/v]|_{\lambda=0}.

Proof.

We first show existence. Gauge
\xi
with
k_{1}:=\Phi_{0}^{-1}
so that
\xi_{1}:=\xi\cdot k_{1}
is a Weierstrass potential as in proposition
2.8
. With
g:=(u/v)|_{\lambda=0}
, compute the successive LWR gauge transformations:

k_{2}:=\begin{pmatrix}1&g\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},\quad\xi_{2}:=\xi_{1}\cdot k_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}0&g^{\prime}\\
\lambda q/g^{\prime}&0\end{pmatrix}dz,

k_{3}:=\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{g^{\prime}}&0\\
0&\frac{1}{\sqrt{g^{\prime}}}\end{pmatrix},\quad\xi_{3}:=\xi_{2}\cdot k_{3}=\begin{pmatrix}h&1\\
\lambda q&-h\end{pmatrix}dz,\quad h:=\frac{g^{\prime\prime}}{2g^{\prime}},

k_{4}:=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
-h&1\end{pmatrix},\quad\xi_{4}:=\xi_{3}\cdot k_{4}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
\lambda q+s&0\end{pmatrix}dz,\quad s:=\mathcal{S}[g].

Therefore, with
k:=k_{1}k_{2}k_{3}k_{4}
, the gauged potential
\xi\cdot k
has the expected form.

We now show uniqueness. Let
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
be two LWR gauges such that

\eta_{1}:=\xi\cdot r_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
\lambda q_{1}+s_{1}&0\end{pmatrix}dz\quad\text{and}\quad\eta_{2}:=\xi\cdot r_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
\lambda q_{2}+s_{2}&0\end{pmatrix}dz.

Then
\eta_{2}=\eta_{1}\cdot r
with
r:=r_{1}^{-1}r_{2}
. This implies that

(2.12)

dr=r\eta_{2}-\eta_{1}r.

With

r:=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix},

(
2.12
) is equivalent to

\begin{pmatrix}a^{\prime}&b^{\prime}\\
c^{\prime}&d^{\prime}\end{pmatrix}=\lambda\begin{pmatrix}bq_{2}&0\\
dq_{2}-aq_{1}&bq_{1}\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}-c&a-d\\
ds_{2}-as_{1}&c\end{pmatrix}.

But
\eta_{1}
and
\eta_{2}
are gauge-equivalent, so by proposition
2.6
,
q_{1}=q_{2}=q
. Equating the
\lambda^{1}
-coefficients and recalling that
q
is not constantly vanishing because
\xi
is non-degenerate,
b=0
and
a=d
. We deduce that
c=0
and that
r=\pm\mathrm{I}
because
\det r=1
. Therefore,
r_{1}=\pm r_{2}
and
s_{1}=s_{2}
.
∎

Corollary 2.13
.

Let
\xi
be an LWR potential. Let
\Phi
be a frame for
\xi
, let
x
be a spinor for
\xi
and let
y:=\Phi x
. With
y=(u,v)
, the function
\mathcal{S}[u/v]
only depends on the orbit of
\xi
under LWR gauging and satisfies for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
,

(2.13)

\mathcal{S}[u/v]=\lambda q+s

where
q
and
s
are as in theorem
2.12
.

Proof.

We first show that
\mathcal{S}[u/v]
does not depend on the initial condition for
\Phi
. Let
\tilde{\Phi}:=C\Phi
where
C\in\mathrm{SL}_{2}\mathbb{C}
is holomorphic in
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
and let
\tilde{y}:=\tilde{\Phi}x
. Then
\tilde{y}=Cy
. With
\tilde{y}=(\tilde{u},\tilde{v})
,
\tilde{u}/\tilde{v}
and
u/v
differ by a
\lambda
-dependent Möbius transformation. Therefore, their respective Schwarzian derivative are the same.

We now show that
\mathcal{S}[u/v]
is invariant under LWR gauging. Let
g
be an LWR gauge and let
\tilde{\Phi}:=\Phi g
and
\tilde{\xi}:=\xi\cdot g
. Then, by (
2.6
),
\widetilde{x}:=g^{-1}x
is a spinor for
\tilde{\xi}
. Let
\tilde{y}:=\tilde{\Phi}\tilde{x}
. Then
\tilde{y}=\Phi x=y
, so
\mathcal{S}[u/v]
is invariant under LWR gauging.

We now prove (
2.13
) by assuming, without loss of generality, that
\xi
is given in its Schwarz form, as in theorem
2.12
.
Let
y=\Phi x
where
x
is a spinor for
\xi
and
\Phi
is an LWR frame associated with
\xi
. Considering the form of
\xi
, we have
x=i\sqrt{q}(0,1)
. Writing

\Phi=\begin{pmatrix}a&c\\
b&d\end{pmatrix},

we get

and

\frac{u}{v}=\frac{c}{d}.

Now differentiate the equation
d\Phi=\Phi\xi
componentwise in
z
to get

\begin{cases}c^{\prime\prime}=a^{\prime}=(\lambda q+s)c\\
d^{\prime\prime}=b^{\prime}=(\lambda q+s)d.\end{cases}

By hypothesis,
\xi
is non-degenerate, so by proposition
2.6
,
\det(y,y_{z})\neq 0
, therefore

is not constant. By lemma

.
∎

3.2  Gauss map

Let

be a CMC
1
conformal immersion and let
N
be the normal of
f
. For each

, the Riemannian exponential map at

in the direction of

defines a geodesic

and the linear plane spanned by

and

(3.11)

We identify

with the Riemann sphere via the stereographic projection

defined by

(3.12)

{\mathrm{St}}^{-1}(u/v):=\mathrm{I}-2\lVert\nu\rVert^{-2}\nu\nu^{*},\quad\nu:=(u,v).

.

The

map of a CMC 1 immersion is the map

immersions are related by a
Goursat transformation
if they have the same Hopf differential and if the Schwarzian derivative of their Gauss maps are equal.

Theorem 4.10
.

Holomorphic dressing induces a Goursat transformation of the induced immersion
f
. Moreover, any Goursat transformation of
f
can be obtained by holomorphic dressing, provided that
f
is not flat.

Proof.

Holomorphic dressing amounts to changing the initial condition
\Phi(z_{0})
in the initial value problem (
1.10
). Therefore, it does not change the potential
\xi
. By remark
3.2
, the Hopf differential is unchanged.
Moreover, by theorem
3.1
, the Gauss map
\widehat{G}
of the dressed immersion
\widehat{f}
reads

\widehat{G}=\frac{aG+b}{cG+d},\quad R_{\lambda_{0}}=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\in\mathrm{SL}_{2}\mathbb{C}

where
G
is the Gauss map of
f
. Therefore,
\widehat{G}
and
G
differ by a Möbius transformation, so they have the same Schwarzian derivative.

Conversely, let
\widehat{f}
be a Goursat transformation of
f
.
The immersions are not flat, so their respective potential can be gauged into a Schwarz potential (by theorem
2.12
). By corollary
2.13
, these Schwarz potentials agree because the respective Hopf differential and Schwarzian derivative of the Gauss map agree for a Goursat pair. Therefore, the gauged LWR frames agree, up to a choice of initial condition, and one can be obtained from the other via a holomorphic dressing.
∎

4.3  Rigid motions

Definition 4.11
.

Let
f\colon\Sigma\to\mathbb{X}^{3}
be an immersion. A

of

is an immersion
\widehat{f}\colon\Sigma\to\mathbb{X}^{3}
such that there exists an orientation-preserving isometry
\mathcal{J}
of
\mathbb{X}^{3}
satisfying
\widehat{f}=\mathcal{J}\circ f
.

Theorem 4.12
.

Let
f
be the immersion induced by the LWR frame
\Phi
at
(\lambda_{0},\lambda_{1})
and let
\hat{\Phi}=R\Phi
be a holomorphic dressing of
\Phi
.

•

In
\mathbb{E}^{3}
, if
R_{\lambda_{0}}\in\mathrm{SU}_{2}
, then the immersion induced by
\hat{\Phi}
is a rigid motion of
f
.

•

In
\mathbb{H}^{3}
, if
R_{\lambda_{1}}\in\mathrm{SU}_{2}
, then the immersion induced by
\hat{\Phi}
is a rigid motion of
f
.

•

Any rigid motion of
f
can be obtained this way, provided that
f
is not flat.

Proof.

The null curves
\hat{\psi}
and
\hat{\Psi}
induced by
\hat{\Phi}
read

\hat{\psi}=R_{\lambda_{0}}\psi R_{\lambda_{0}}^{-1}+(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{0})(\dot{R}R^{-1})_{\lambda_{0}}

and

\hat{\Psi}=R_{\lambda_{1}}\Psi R_{\lambda_{0}}^{-1}.

Therefore, if
R_{\lambda_{0}}\in\mathrm{SU}_{2}
, then the induced immersion in
\mathbb{E}^{3}
reads

(4.1)

\hat{f}^{\mathbb{E}}=R_{\lambda_{0}}f^{\mathbb{E}}R_{\lambda_{0}}^{-1}+T

where

(4.2)

T=c+c^{*},\quad c=(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{0})(\dot{R}R^{-1})_{\lambda_{0}}.

If
R_{\lambda_{1}}\in\mathrm{SU}_{2}
, then

(4.3)

\hat{f}^{\mathbb{H}}=(R_{\lambda_{0}}^{-1})^{*}f^{\mathbb{H}}R_{\lambda_{0}}^{-1}.

Therefore, in both spaces,
\hat{f}
is a rigid motion of
f
.

Conversely, let
\hat{f}
be a rigid motion of
f
. Rigid motions are a special case of Goursat transformations, and
f
is not flat. Therefore, by theorem
4.10
,
\hat{f}
can be obtained by dressing. Let
\hat{\Phi}=R\Phi
inducing
\hat{f}
. Because
\hat{f}
is a rigid motion of
f
, the corresponding metrics are the same. Therefore, in
\mathbb{E}^{3}
, by theorem
3.1
, with
y=\Phi x
,

\lVert y\rVert=\lVert R_{\lambda_{0}}y\rVert

constantly on
\Sigma
.
Using that
f
is not flat, we deduce that
R_{\lambda_{0}}\in\mathrm{SU}_{2}
in
\mathbb{E}^{3}
.
By the same argument,
R_{\lambda_{1}}\in\mathrm{SU}_{2}
in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
.
∎

Corollary 4.13

(Identity)
.

Let
f
and
\hat{f}
be two non-flat immersions obtained via the LWR at the same evaluation points
(\lambda_{0},\lambda_{1})
. Then
f=\hat{f}
if and only if the corresponding LWR frames satisfy

{\Phi}=R\hat{\Phi}g

for some LWR gauge
g
and some holomorphic dressing

such that:

•

in
\mathbb{E}^{3}

and

and
{R}_{\lambda_{1}}\in\mathrm{SU}_{2}

If
\hat{f}={f}
, then the two immersions have the same Hopf differential and the Schwarzian derivative of their Gauss map are the same. Therefore, the Schwarz form of their potential is the same. Thus, their respective potential belong to the same gauge class: there exists a gauge
g

\hat{\xi}=\xi\cdot g.

Solving the corresponding differential system yields

\hat{\Phi}=R\Phi g

for some
\lambda

, in
\mathbb{E}^{3}
,

and
\hat{f}
is given by (

). But

and the immersion is not flat, so

and

We end this section by exhibiting a group homomorphism between holomorphic dressings and rigid motions. This homomorphism will be useful in section

in order to distinguish rotations from general screw motions.

Proposition 4.14
.

Fix
(\lambda_{0},\lambda_{1})
. Let
G_{\lambda_{0}}
be the subgroup of
\lambda
-holomorphic maps

\phi\colon G_{\lambda_{0}}\to\mathrm{Iso}^{+}(\mathbb{E}^{3})

\phi(R)(X):=R_{\lambda_{0}}XR_{\lambda_{0}}^{-1}+\rho(R)+\rho(R)^{*}

\rho(X):=(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{0})(\dot{R}R^{-1})_{\lambda_{0}}.

Let

and
X\in\mathbb{E}^{3}
.

\phi(RS)(X)=(RS)_{\lambda_{0}}X(RS)_{\lambda_{0}}^{-1}+\rho(RS)+\rho(RS)^{*}.

6.1  Solution to the Gauss-Codazzi equations

Proposition 6.2
.

Let
f
be a minimal or CMC
1
intrinsic surface of revolution.

•

If
f
is flat, then there exist
a>0
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
such that the metric of
f
is

(6.1)

ds^{2}=a^{4}\left|z\right|^{4\alpha}\left|dz\right|^{2}.

•

If
f
is not flat, then there exist
a,b>0
,
\alpha<\beta\in\mathbb{R}
and
\nu\in[0,2\pi)
such that the metric and Hopf differential of
f
are

(6.2)

ds^{2}=\left(a^{2}\left|z\right|^{2\alpha}+b^{2}\left|z\right|^{2\beta}\right)^{2}\left|dz\right|^{2},

(6.3)

Qdz^{2}=e^{i\nu}ab(\beta-\alpha)z^{\alpha+\beta-1}dz^{2}.

Proof.

Write
z=re^{i\theta}
and
ds^{2}=4e^{2\omega}\left|dz\right|^{2}
with
\omega\colon\widetilde{\mathbb{C}^{*}}\to\mathbb{R}
.
By assumption on
f
, the function
\omega
only depends on

.
The Gauss-Codazzi equations (
1.8
) read

(6.4)

\omega_{rr}+r^{-1}\omega_{r}=\left|Q\right|^{2}e^{-2\omega},\quad Q_{{{\overline{z}}}}=0.

If
f
is flat,
Q
is constantly vanishing.
In this case, all the solutions to (
6.4
) are given by

\omega(r)=c_{1}+c_{2}\log(r),\quad c_{1},c_{2}\in\mathbb{R}.

With
a:=2e^{c_{1}}>0
and
\alpha:=c_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
, the metric reads as in (
6.1
).

If
f
is not flat, then
Q
is not constantly vanishing.
The Codazzi equation ((
6.4
)) implies that
Q
is holomorphic.
Therefore, there exists a domain
D\subset\widetilde{\mathbb{C}^{*}}
on which
Q
never vanishes.
On

, consider

and compute

(6.5)

h^{-1}h_{\theta}=ih^{-1}(zh_{z}-{\overline{z}}h_{{\overline{z}}})=i\left(zQ^{-1}Q_{z}-{\overline{zQ^{-1}Q_{z}}}\right).

The Gauss equation in (
6.4
) implies that
\left|Q\right|^{2}
only depends on
r
, so

and by (
6.5
),

zQ^{-1}Q_{z}={\overline{zQ^{-1}Q_{z}}}.

The left-hand side is holomorphic whereas the right-hand side is anti-holomorphic, so there exists
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
such that

zQ^{-1}Q_{z}=\gamma.

Solving this equation for
Q
yields for all

Q(z)=e^{i\nu}cz^{\gamma},\quad\nu\in\mathbb{R},\quad c>0.

By holomorphicity of

, this holds on
\widetilde{\mathbb{C}^{*}}
.
Going back to the Gauss equation in (

), we have

(6.6)

\omega_{rr}+r^{-1}\omega_{r}=c^{2}r^{2\gamma}e^{-2\omega}

and a two-parameter family of solutions is given by

\omega(r)=\log\left(\frac{1}{2}\left(a^{2}r^{2\alpha}+b^{2}r^{2\beta}\right)\right)

where
a>0
,

and

are defined by

\alpha=\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\gamma-\frac{c}{ab}\right),\quad\beta=\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\gamma+\frac{c}{ab}\right).

With such
\omega
, the metric and Hopf differential of

read as in equations (
6.2
) and (
6.3
).

Note that all solutions to (
6.6
) are given by (
6.7
).
Indeed, let
\omega
be given by (
6.7
). The function

\psi\colon\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}\to\mathbb{R}^{2},\quad(a,b)\mapsto(\omega(1),\omega^{\prime}(1))

is surjective, and the ODE is given by

∎

6.2  LWR potential for intrinsic surfaces of revolution

Let
a,b>0
,
\alpha<\beta\in\mathbb{R}

) induces an intrinsic surface of revolution with metric and Hopf differential as in (

At

also gives the Hopf differential as in (

Conversely, let

6.3  Enneper surfaces

Definition 6.4
.

A minimal or CMC
1
immersion is an

if it is a non-flat, intrinsic surface of revolution that descends from the universal cover of
\mathbb{C}^{*}
to a conformal immersion on
\mathbb{C}
.

Let
r>0
and
n\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 0}
. Consider the following LWR data on
\mathbb{C}
:

(6.9)

\xi=\begin{pmatrix}0&rz^{n}\\
\lambda&0\end{pmatrix}dz,\quad\Phi(0)=\mathrm{I},\quad(\lambda_{0},\lambda_{1})=\begin{cases}(0,1)\text{ in }\mathbb{E}^{3},\\
(1,0)\text{ in }\mathbb{H}^{3}.\end{cases}

Theorem 6.5
.

The LWR data (
6.9
) induces an Enneper surface. Moreover, any Enneper surface can be obtained this way, up to an isometry and coordinate change.

Proof.

We first show that the LWR data (
6.9
) yields an Enneper surface. Let

. One can compute explicitly:

(6.10)

\Phi_{0}(z)=\begin{pmatrix}1&\frac{rz^{n+1}}{n+1}\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}.

By theorem
3.1
, the metric of

is

(6.11)

ds^{2}=\left(1+\frac{r^{2}\left|z\right|^{2n+2}}{(n+1)^{2}}\right)^{2}\left|dz\right|^{2}.

The metric is rotationally invariant, so
f
is an Enneper surface. Note that the Hopf differential of

is

(6.12)

Qdz^{2}=(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{0})rz^{n}dz^{2}.

We now show that any Enneper surface
f\colon\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{X}^{3}
can be obtained this way.
By definition, the metric of
f
is radial and
f
is not flat.
By theorem
6.3
,
f
can be obtained with the LWR data (
6.8
).
The metric of
f
is given by

ds^{2}=\left({a}^{2}\left|z\right|^{2\alpha}+{b}^{2}\left|z\right|^{2\beta}\right)^{2}\left|dz\right|^{2}.

But

is conformal at

\alpha\beta=0
and
\alpha+\beta\geq 0

Because

, we have
\alpha=0
and

The Hopf differential is

The immersion

is well-defined on
\mathbb{C}

such that

Therefore, the metric and Hopf differential of

Qdz^{2}=e^{i\nu}ab(n+1)z^{n}dz^{2}.

The immersion is not flat, so

and

. After the coordinate change

w=e^{\frac{i\rho}{n+2}}a^{2}z

where

is defined by

and after letting

one can compute that the metric and Hopf differential of

read as equations (
6.11
) and (

) in the coordinate

∎

Corollary 6.6
.

Any Enneper surface admits an extrinsic rotational symmetry of order

.

Proof.

Let

be an Enneper surface. By theorem
6.5
,

is given by the LWR data (

). Let

,

and compute

so that

(6.13)

\tau^{*}\Phi=R\Phi g

where

is holomorphic in

and independent of

∎

6.4  Catenoids

In this section, we review catenoids in the framework of the LWR. We exhibit a family of Fuchsian potentials that induce catenoids and show that any catenoid can be obtained this way.

Definition 6.7
.

A minimal or CMC 1 conformal immersion
f\colon\widetilde{\mathbb{C}^{*}}\to\mathbb{X}^{3}
is a
catenoid
if it is a non-flat extrinsic surface of revolution: for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
,

\tau_{t}^{*}f=\mathcal{R}(t)\circ f

where
\tau_{t}
is a lift of
z\mapsto e^{it}z
and
\mathcal{R}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathrm{Iso}(\mathbb{X}^{3})
is a 1-parameter group of rotations.

Remark 6.8
.

We do not assume that all catenoids are closed on
\mathbb{C}^{*}
. However, noting that the group
\mathcal{R}
is compact, any catenoid closes on some angular sector in
\widetilde{\mathbb{C}^{*}}
. With
t_{0}:=\min\{t>0\mid\tau_{t}^{*}f=f\}
, we define the
wrapping number
of
f
as
r:=2\pi/t_{0}
.

Proposition 6.9
.

Let
\Phi
be an LWR frame inducing a catenoid at the evaluation points
(\lambda_{0},\lambda_{1})=(0,1)
. Up to an LWR gauge, the LWR potential of
\Phi
reads

(6.14)

\xi=Kz^{-1}dz,\quad K=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
q\lambda+p&0\end{pmatrix}

where
q\in\mathbb{R}^{*}
and
p>0
. Moreover, the Hopf differential of the catenoid is
qz^{-2}dz^{2}
and its wrapping number is
2\sqrt{p}
.

Proof.

Let
f\colon\widetilde{\mathbb{C}^{*}}\to\mathbb{X}^{3}
be the catenoid induced by
\Phi
. By assumption,
f
is not flat, so there exists a Schwarz potential inducing
f
locally (by theorem
2.12
), and up to a gauge,

\xi=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
Q\lambda+S&0\end{pmatrix}dz.

By corollary
2.13
,
Qdz^{2}
is the Hopf differential of
f
and
S
is the Schwarzian derivative of its Gauss map. Let
t\in\mathbb{R}
and
\tau(z)=e^{it}z
. Then

\tau^{*}\xi=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
\tau^{*}Q\lambda+\tau^{*}S&0\end{pmatrix}e^{it}dz.

We put this potential into its Schwarz form with a diagonal LWR gauge

:

\tau^{*}\xi\cdot g=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
e^{2it}(\tau^{*}Q\lambda+\tau^{*}S)&0\end{pmatrix}dz.

But
f
is a catenoid, so
\tau
induces a rotation of the surface. By theorems
5.2
and
5.4
, there exists an LWR gauge
\tilde{g}
such that
\tau^{*}\xi=\xi\cdot\tilde{g}
.
Therefore,
\xi
and
\tau^{*}\xi\cdot g
are Schwarz potentials that lie in the same gauge class.
By uniqueness of the Schwarz potential (theorem
2.12
), this implies that
\xi=\tau^{*}\xi\cdot g

(6.15)

\tau^{*}Q=e^{-2it}Q,\quad\tau^{*}S=e^{-2it}S.

But
Q
is holomorphic and not identically zero, so by (
6.15
)

never vanishes. Therefore there exists
q\in\mathbb{C}^{*}
such that
Q=qz^{-2}
. For the same reason, there exists
s\in\mathbb{C}
such that

.
The Schwarz potential reads

\xi=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
z^{-2}(q\lambda+s)&0\end{pmatrix}dz

and
\xi
extends meromorphically to
\mathbb{C}^{*}
. With
p:=s+1/4
,

(6.16)

\xi\cdot\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{z}&0\\
-\frac{1}{2\sqrt{z}}&\frac{1}{\sqrt{z}}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
q\lambda+p&0\end{pmatrix}\frac{dz}{z}.

Up to a gauge, we can therefore assume that
\xi=Kz^{-1}dz
where

K=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
\lambda q+p&0\end{pmatrix}

with
q\in\mathbb{C}^{*}
and
p\in\mathbb{C}
.

We now show that
q\in\mathbb{R}^{*}
and
p>0
. The frame
\Phi
satisfies
\Phi^{-1}d\Phi=Kz^{-1}dz
, so there exists
C=(C_{\lambda})_{\lambda\in\mathbb{C}}
independent of

such that
\Phi=Cz^{K}
. Therefore,
\tau^{*}\Phi=R\Phi
with

Recall that for all

,
\tau
induces a rotation of

with a fixed axis.

In

5.2

for all

. But this implies that

The eigenvalues of

and

In

The eigenvalues of

4.13

.
Using

Because

-parameter group of rotations. Consider

∎

In both cases, the LWR frame reads

For all

The metric is given by theorem

-noids

In this section, we give a standard form for LWR potentials that induce
n
-noids. We then show how to construct generic trinoids by solving a period problem that amounts to the unitarization of a monodromy representation.

Definition 7.1
.

A genus zero
n
-noid
is a conformal, minimal or CMC
1
immersion of the
n
-punctured sphere obtained from an LWR potential
\xi
which is

(1)

of Fuchsian type: around each puncture
p_{k}

g_{k}
such that
\xi\cdot g_{k}
has a simple pole at
p_{k}
, and

(2)

of catenoid type: the residue at
p_{k}
of
\xi\cdot g_{k}

\pm\sqrt{q_{k}\lambda+1/4}
. The parameter
q_{k}\in\mathbb{C}^{*}
is the

of the puncture
p_{k}
.

Our definition of a pole of catenoid type is justified by the following lemma.

Lemma 7.2
.

Let
\xi=Kz^{-1}dz
be an LWR potential on
\mathbb{C}^{*}
where the residue
K
is independent of
z
and satisfies

\det K=-(q\lambda+p)

for some
q\in\mathbb{C}^{*}
and
p\in\mathbb{C}
. Then there exists an LWR gauge
g
such that

\xi\cdot g=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
q\lambda+p&0\end{pmatrix}\frac{dz}{z}.

Proof.

Write
K=A\lambda+B
and let
x:=(u,v)
be a spinor for
A
(

does not depend on
z
).
Up to conjugation by

g_{0}=\begin{pmatrix}0&i\\
i&0\end{pmatrix},

one can assume that

.
Recall that
q\neq 0
and compute:

g_{1}:=\begin{pmatrix}v^{-1}&u\\
0&v\end{pmatrix},\quad\xi_{1}:=\xi\cdot g_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{p}&-q\\
-\lambda&-\sqrt{p}\end{pmatrix}\frac{dz}{z},

g_{2}:=\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{-q}&0\\
0&\frac{1}{\sqrt{-q}}\end{pmatrix},\quad\xi_{2}:=\xi_{1}\cdot g_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{p}&1\\
q\lambda&-\sqrt{p}\end{pmatrix}\frac{dz}{z},

g_{3}:=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
-\sqrt{p}&1\end{pmatrix},\quad\xi_{3}:=\xi_{2}\cdot g_{3}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\

.

of

7.2  Unitarizability on the three-punctured sphere

Definition 7.4
.

•

A subset
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathrm{SL}_{2}\mathbb{C}
(resp.
\mathrm{sl}_{2}\mathbb{C}
) is
reducible
if there exists
\ell\in\mathbb{C}P^{1}
which is an eigenline of
X
for all
X\in\mathcal{X}
.

•

A subset
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathrm{SL}_{2}\mathbb{C}
(resp.
\mathrm{sl}_{2}\mathbb{C}
) is
unitarizable
if there exists
C\in\mathrm{SL}_{2}\mathbb{C}
such that
CXC^{-1}\in\mathrm{SU}_{2}
(resp.
\mathrm{su}_{2}
) for all
X\in\mathcal{X}
.

•

\nu\in\mathbb{C}
is a
logarithmic eigenvalue
of
M\in\mathrm{SL}_{2}\mathbb{C}
if
e^{2\pi i\nu}
is an eigenvalue of
M
. The logarithmic eigenvalues of
M
are defined up to
\nu\mapsto-\nu
and
\nu\mapsto\nu+1
, and can be
normalized
uniquely so that
\mathop{\rm Re}\nu\in[0,1/2]
.

Theorem 7.5
.

Let
M_{0},M_{1},M_{2}\in\mathrm{SL}_{2}\mathbb{C}
satisfy
M_{0}M_{1}M_{2}=\mathrm{I}
. Let
\nu_{0},\nu_{1},\nu_{2}\in\mathbb{C}
be corresponding logarithmic eigenvalues. Then

•

\{M_{0},M_{1},M_{2}\}
is reducible if and only if
\nu_{0}\pm\nu_{1}\pm\nu_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}
for some choice of signs.

•

\{M_{0},M_{1},M_{2}\}
is irreducible and unitarizable if and only if
\nu_{0},\nu_{1},\nu_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
and, when normalized to
[0,1/2]
, satisfy the spherical triangle inequalities:

\nu_{0}<\nu_{1}+\nu_{2},\quad\nu_{1}<\nu_{0}+\nu_{2},\quad\nu_{2}<\nu_{0}+\nu_{1},\quad\nu_{0}+\nu_{1}+\nu_{2}<1.

Proof.

Let

t_{k}:=\frac{1}{2}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{tr}M_{k},\quad k=0,1,2,

and

\varphi:=1-t_{0}^{2}-t_{1}^{2}-t_{2}^{2}+2t_{0}t_{1}t_{2}.

By
[
8
]
,
\{M_{0},M_{1},M_{2}\}
is reducible if and only if
\varphi=0
. Moreover,
\{M_{0},M_{1},M_{2}\}
is irreducible and unitarizable if and only if
t_{0},t_{1},t_{2}\in(-1,1)
and
\varphi>0
.
Let
e_{k}:=e^{2\pi i\nu_{k}}
so that

t_{k}=\frac{e_{k}+e_{k}^{-1}}{2},\quad k=0,1,2.

Then

4e_{0}^{2}e_{1}^{2}e_{2}^{2}\varphi=(e_{0}e_{1}e_{2}-1)(e_{0}e_{1}-e_{2})(e_{0}e_{2}-e_{1})(e_{1}e_{2}-e_{0}).

So
\varphi=0
if and only if
\nu_{0}\pm\nu_{1}\pm\nu_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}
for some choice of signs. This proves the first point.

To prove the second point, note that
t_{k}\in(-1,1)
if and only if
\nu_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
. Assume that
\nu_{0},\nu_{1},\nu_{2}
are normalized. Then
\varphi=0
if and only if
(\nu_{0},\nu_{1},\nu_{2})
lies on the boundary of the tetrahedron
\mathcal{T}\subset[0,1/2]^{3}
defined by

(\nu_{0}=\nu_{1}+\nu_{2})\quad\text{or}\quad(\nu_{1}=\nu_{0}+\nu_{2})\quad\text{or}\quad(\nu_{2}=\nu_{0}+\nu_{1})\quad\text{or}\quad(\nu_{0}+\nu_{1}+\nu_{2}=1).

Using the continuity of
\varphi
, one can check that
\varphi>0
if and only if
(\nu_{0},\nu_{1},\nu_{2})
lies in the interior of
\mathcal{T}
, that is,
\nu_{0},\nu_{1},\nu_{2}
satisfy the spherical triangle inequalities.
∎

Theorem 7.6
.

Let
A_{0},A_{1},A_{2}\in\mathrm{sl}_{2}\mathbb{C}
satisfy
A_{0}+A_{1}+A_{2}=0
. Let
ia_{0},ia_{1},ia_{2}\in\mathbb{C}
be corresponding eigenvalues. Then

•

\{A_{0},A_{1},A_{2}\}
is reducible if and only if
a_{0}\pm a_{1}\pm a_{2}=0
for some choice of signs.

•

\{A_{0},A_{1},A_{2}\}
is irreducible and unitarizable if and only if
a_{0},a_{1},a_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
and, when normalized to
\mathbb{R}_{+}
, satisfy the Euclidean triangle inequalities:

{a_{0}}<{a_{1}}+{a_{2}},\quad{a_{1}}<{a_{0}}+{a_{2}},\quad{a_{2}}<{a_{0}}+{a_{1}}.

Proof.

Let

\varphi:=\det[A_{1},A_{2}].

We first show that
\{A_{0},A_{1},A_{2}\}
is reducible if and only if
\varphi=0
.
If
\{A_{0},A_{1},A_{2}\}
is reducible, then they share an eigenline and this eigenline is in the kernel of the commutator
[A_{1},A_{2}]
, which implies that
\varphi=0
. Conversely, if
\varphi=0
, then the commutator
[A_{1},A_{2}]
is nilpotent and can therefore be written
[A_{1},A_{2}]=xx^{\perp}
for some
x\in\mathbb{C}^{2}
. If
x=0
, then
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
commute, so they share an eigenline and
\{A_{0},A_{1},A_{2}\}
is reducible. Assume that
x\neq 0
. Considering the bilinear extension of equations (
1.1
) and (
1.4
), one has

0=\left\langle A_{1},[A_{1},A_{2}]\right\rangle=\left\langle A_{1},xx^{\perp}\right\rangle=\det(A_{1}x,x).

Therefore,
A_{1}x\in\left\langle x\right\rangle
and
x
is an eigenvector of
A_{1}
. Similarly,
x
is an eigenvector of
A_{2}
. Therefore
\{A_{0},A_{1},A_{2}\}
is reducible. We have proved that
\{A_{0},A_{1},A_{2}\}
is reducible if and only if
\varphi=0
.

Now a computation gives

\varphi=4(\left\langle A_{1},A_{1}\right\rangle\left\langle A_{2},A_{2}\right\rangle-\left\langle A_{1},A_{2}\right\rangle^{2}).

By the polarization identity, and using that
A_{1}+A_{2}=-A_{0}
,

\varphi=4\lVert A_{1}\rVert^{2}\lVert A_{2}\rVert^{2}-(\lVert A_{0}\rVert^{2}-\lVert A_{1}\rVert^{2}-\lVert A_{2}\rVert^{2})^{2},

where
\lVert A_{k}\rVert^{2}=\left\langle A_{k},A_{k}\right\rangle=-\det A_{k}
. But
\det A_{k}=a_{k}^{2}
, so

\varphi=(a_{0}+a_{1}+a_{2})(a_{0}+a_{1}-a_{2})(a_{0}-a_{1}+a_{2})(-a_{0}+a_{1}+a_{2}).

Hence,
\varphi=0
if and only if
a_{0}\pm a_{1}\pm a_{2}=0
for some choice of signs, and the first point is proved.

To prove the second point, we show that
\{A_{0},A_{1},A_{2}\}
is irreducible and unitarizable if and only if
a_{0},a_{1},a_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
and
\varphi>0
. Let

T:=\begin{pmatrix}\left\langle A_{1},A_{1}\right\rangle&\left\langle A_{1},A_{2}\right\rangle&0\\
\left\langle A_{1},A_{2}\right\rangle&\left\langle A_{2},A_{2}\right\rangle&0\\
0&0&\left\langle A_{1},A_{1}\right\rangle\left\langle A_{2},A_{2}\right\rangle-\left\langle A_{1},A_{2}\right\rangle^{2}\end{pmatrix}.

If
\{A_{0},A_{1},A_{2}\}
is irreducible and unitarizable, then
a_{0},a_{1},a_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
and
T
is real symmetric positive definite, so
\varphi>0
. Conversely, assume that
a_{0},a_{1},a_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
and that
\varphi>0
. Then
T
is real symmetric positive definite. Let
T=U^{T}U
be the Cholesky decomposition of
T
, with
U
real upper triangular. Let
S\in\mathrm{SO}_{3}\mathbb{C}
such that
U=SV
where
V=(A_{1},A_{2},A_{1}\times A_{2})
.
Let
C\in\mathrm{SL}_{2}\mathbb{C}
be a lift of
S
given by the double covering
\mathrm{SL}_{2}\mathbb{C}\to\mathrm{SO}_{3}\mathbb{C}
(see exercise 7.17 in
[
7
]
).
Then
C
is a unitarizer of
\{A_{0},A_{1},A_{2}\}
. Therefore,
\{A_{0},A_{1},A_{2}\}
is irreducible and unitarizable if and only if
a_{0},a_{1},a_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
and
\varphi>0
, which is equivalent to
a_{0},a_{1},a_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
and
\left|a_{0}\right|,\left|a_{1}\right|,\left|a_{2}\right|
satisfying the Euclidean triangle inequalities.
∎

Corollary 7.7

Any trinoid can be obtained from the LWR with the data

.

Proposition 7.8
.

Let
\Phi
be an LWR frame with LWR potential

7.7

be the monodromy of

(1)

(2)

(7.4)

Proof.

First note that these eigenvalues do not depend upon the choice of an initial condition for

Around each singularity, there exists a gauge

To compute the eigenvalues of

Therefore, with

Using that

Theorem 7.10
.

Irreducible trinoids are parametrized by their weights.

Proof.

We start with Euclidean space
\mathbb{E}^{3}
. Let
\mathcal{T}
be the set of irreducible minimal trinoids (modulo rigid motions) and let
\mathcal{Q}
be the set of triples
(q_{0},q_{1},q_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
whose absolute values satisfy the Euclidean triangle inequalities.
Let

be the map that associates to a trinoid its weights. We show that

is a bijection.

The map
\mathcal{W}
is well-defined: by corollary
7.7
, the weights of a given trinoid are determined by its Hopf differential, which is invariant under rigid motions.
Moreover, we need to show that

: let

be a trinoid given by corollary
7.7
and let
M_{0},M_{1},M_{2}
be the monodromies of
\Phi
around the poles

1
and
\infty

and by theorem

,

is irreducible and unitary. By proposition
7.8
, the eigenvalues of
(\dot{M}_{k})_{\lambda_{0}}
are
\pm 2\pi iq_{k}
. By theorem
7.6
,
(q_{0},q_{1},q_{2})\in\mathcal{Q}
. Note that the Hopf differential

given by corollary
7.7
has two distinct zeroes if and only if

but

.

The map
\mathcal{W}
is surjective: let

. Let
(\Sigma,\xi,\Phi,\lambda_{0},\lambda_{1})
be LWR data as in corollary

and theorem

ensure that

The map
\mathcal{W}
is injective: let

and

be two irreducible trinoids with the same weights. By corollary

,

and

and

8.1  Dressed frames

Definition 8.1
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}
and
\ell\neq m\in\mathbb{C}P^{1}
. The
simple factor

\mathrm{SF}(\alpha,\ell,m)
is the
\lambda
-family of 2-by-2 matrices

\mathrm{SF}(\alpha,\ell,m):=S\Delta S^{-1}

where

\Delta:=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda-\alpha&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}

and
S\in\mathrm{GL}_{2}\mathbb{C}
is independent of
\lambda
and has its first column vector in
\ell
and its second column vector in
m
.

Remark 8.2
.

•

Simple factors are well-defined: multiplying the columns of
S
by scalars amounts to multiplying
S
on the right by a diagonal matrix, which commutes with
\Delta
.

•

\mathrm{SF}
is injective because a 2-by-2 diagonalizable matrix is determined by its ordered pairs of eigenvalues and eigenlines.

•

Permuting the diagonal entries of
\Delta
in
\mathrm{SF}(\alpha,\ell,m)
defines the simple factor
\mathrm{SF}(\alpha,m,\ell)
. Thus, for all
\alpha
,
\ell
and
m
,

(8.1)

\mathrm{SF}(\alpha,\ell,m)^{-1}=(\lambda-\alpha)^{-1}\mathrm{SF}(\alpha,m,\ell).

Theorem 8.3
.

Let
\Phi
be an LWR frame and assume that
\Phi
is not flat. For any
z
-independent simple factor
g
, there exists a unique
z
-dependent simple factor
h
such that

(8.2)

g\#\Phi:=g\Phi h^{-1}

is an LWR frame.

Proof.

Write
g=\mathrm{SF}(\alpha,\ell,m)
.
We first show that the simple factor
h
is necessarily given by

(8.3)

h=\mathrm{SF}(\alpha,x,y),\quad x=\Phi_{\alpha}^{-1}\ell,\quad y=\mathop{\mathrm{}}{span}w.

where
w
is a spinor for
A
in the potential
\xi=(A\lambda+B)dz
of
\Phi
.

Let
h=\mathrm{SF}(\beta,x,y)
such that
\hat{\Phi}:=g\Phi h^{-1}
is an LWR frame. Then
\beta=\alpha
because
\det\hat{\Phi}=1
.
Moreover,
\hat{\Phi}
is holomorphic at
\lambda=\alpha
, so on the one hand,

(\lambda-\alpha)g\Phi h^{-1}\mathop{\longrightarrow}_{\lambda\to\alpha}0.

On the other hand, by (
8.1
),

(\lambda-\alpha)h^{-1}=\mathrm{SF}(\alpha,y,x)

so

(\lambda-\alpha)g\Phi h^{-1}x\mathop{\longrightarrow}_{\lambda\to\alpha}g_{\alpha}\Phi_{\alpha}x.

Therefore, in order for
\hat{\Phi}
to be holomorphic at
\lambda=\alpha
, it is necessary that
x=\mathop{\mathrm{Ker}}g_{\alpha}\Phi_{\alpha}
, i.e.
x=\Phi_{\alpha}^{-1}\ell
.
To determine
y
, let
\xi=\Phi^{-1}d\Phi
and
\hat{\xi}=\xi\cdot h^{-1}
. The frame
\hat{\Phi}
is LWR, so
\hat{\xi}
is an LWR potential. Writing
h=S\Delta S^{-1}
,
S
is
\lambda
-independent, so both
\tilde{\xi}:=\xi\cdot(S\Delta^{-1})
and
\xi\cdot S
are LWR potentials. But
\Delta
is
z
-independent, so

\tilde{\xi}=\Delta(\xi\cdot S)\Delta^{-1}.

With
\xi\cdot S=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&-a\end{pmatrix}
,

(8.4)

\tilde{\xi}=\begin{pmatrix}a&b(\lambda-\alpha)\\
c(\lambda-\alpha)^{-1}&-a\end{pmatrix}.

Therefore,
c_{\alpha}=0
. Differentiating at
\lambda=\alpha
gives

\dot{\tilde{\xi}}=\begin{pmatrix}\dot{a}_{\alpha}&b_{\alpha}\\
0&-\dot{a}_{\alpha}\end{pmatrix}.

This matrix is nilpotent, so
\dot{a}_{\alpha}=0
. By linearity of
\xi\cdot S
, one can write

(8.5)

\xi\cdot S=\begin{pmatrix}\hat{a}&\hat{b}\\
(\lambda-\alpha)\hat{c}&-\hat{a}\end{pmatrix}

with
\hat{a},\hat{b},\hat{c}
independent of
\lambda
. With
\xi=(A\lambda+B)dz
, this implies that

SAS^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
\hat{c}&0\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{and}\quad SBS^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}\hat{a}&\hat{b}\\
-\alpha\hat{c}&-\hat{a}\end{pmatrix}.

Therefore, with
A=ww^{\perp}
,
w\in y
, and this determines
h=\mathrm{SF}(\alpha,\Phi_{\alpha}^{-1}\ell,y)
uniquely.

To show existence, let
\Sigma
be the Riemann surface on which
\xi
is defined and let
\tilde{\Sigma}
be its universal cover, so that
\Phi
is defined on
\tilde{\Sigma}
. Let
h
as in (
8.3
). Then

is defined on
\tilde{\Sigma}\backslash\mathcal{S}
where

(8.6)

\mathcal{S}=\{z\in\tilde{\Sigma}\mid\Phi_{\alpha}w\in\ell\}.

By meromorphicity of
\Phi
and

,
\mathcal{S}
is either a set of isolated points or the entire
\tilde{\Sigma}
. Suppose that
\mathcal{S}=\tilde{\Sigma}
. Then

\det(\Phi w,(\Phi w)_{z})\mathop{\longrightarrow}_{\lambda\to\alpha}0

because
\ell
is constant in

. By theorem
3.1
, this implies that

, and that contradicts the non-flatness of
\Phi
. Therefore,
\mathcal{S}
is a set of isolated points in
\tilde{\Sigma}
, and

is well-defined on
\tilde{\Sigma}\backslash\mathcal{S}
. Therefore, the frame
\hat{\Phi}
defined by (

) is a frame on
\tilde{\Sigma}\backslash\mathcal{S}
, and the uniqueness part of this proof shows that it is an LWR frame.

Definition 8.4
.

by
g
. It descends, via the LWR, to a map on the induced immersions, also called simple factor dressing.

Corollary 8.5
.

Simple factor dressing preserves the Hopf differential.

Proof.

Let
Qdz^{2}
and
\tilde{Q}dz^{2}
be the Hopf differentials induced by
\Phi
and
\tilde{\Phi}:=g\#\Phi=g\Phi h^{-1}
respectively.
The LWR potential of
\tilde{\Phi}
is
\tilde{\xi}:=\xi\cdot h^{-1}
. By theorem
8.3
,
h
is a simple factor, so
h=S\Delta S^{-1}
. Therefore,
\tilde{\xi}=\xi\cdot(S\Delta^{-1}S^{-1})
, and we have

\hat{\xi}:=\tilde{\xi}\cdot S=(\xi\cdot S)\cdot\Delta^{-1}.

Let
\hat{\Phi}:=\tilde{\Phi}S
be an LWR frame for
\tilde{\xi}
and let
\hat{Q}dz^{2}
be the Hopf differential induced by
\hat{\Phi}
. On the one hand,
S
is an LWR gauge, so
\hat{Q}=\tilde{Q}
. On the other hand, assuming without loss of generality that
\xi\cdot S
is in its Schwarz form (theorem
2.12
), with
Qdz^{2}=(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{0})qdz^{2}
,

\hat{\xi}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
q\lambda+s&0\end{pmatrix}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}(\lambda-\alpha)^{-1}&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}dz=\begin{pmatrix}0&\lambda-\alpha\\
\frac{q\lambda+s}{\lambda-\alpha}&0\end{pmatrix}dz.

But
\hat{\xi}
is an LWR potential, so
q\alpha+s=0
. Therefore,

\hat{\xi}=\begin{pmatrix}0&\lambda-\alpha\\
q&0\end{pmatrix}dz.

By remark
3.2
,
\hat{Q}=Q
.
∎

Theorem 8.6
.

Let
\Phi
be a non-flat LWR frame with monodromy
M
. Let
g=\mathrm{SF}(\alpha,\l,m)
. If
\l
is an eigenline of
M_{\alpha}
, then the monodromy
\hat{M}
of
g\#\Phi
is

\hat{M}=gMg^{-1}.

Proof.

Let
\xi
be the potential of
\Phi
and let
w
be the associated spinor. Assume that
\xi
is defined on
\Sigma
and let
\tilde{\Sigma}
be its universal cover.
Let
h=\mathrm{SF}(\alpha,x,y)
with
x=\Phi_{\alpha}^{-1}\ell
and
y=\mathop{\mathrm{}}{span}w
so that the dressed frame reads
\hat{\Phi}=g\Phi h^{-1}
as in theorem
8.3
.
Note that
h
has no monodromy around the extras singularities defined by (
8.6
).
Let
\tau
be a deck transformation of
\tilde{\Sigma}\to\Sigma
and let
M
be the corresponding monodromy matrix for
\Phi
.
The potential
\xi
is well-defined on
\Sigma
, so
\tau^{*}y=y
. Moreover,

\tau^{*}x=\tau^{*}\Phi_{\alpha}^{-1}\ell=\Phi_{\alpha}^{-1}M_{\alpha}^{-1}\ell=\Phi_{\alpha}^{-1}l=x

because
\ell
is an eigenline of
M_{\alpha}
. Therefore
\tau^{*}h=h
, and because
g
is
z
-independent,

\tau^{*}\hat{\Phi}=g(\tau^{*}\Phi)h^{-1}=gM\Phi h^{-1}=gMg^{-1}\hat{\Phi}.\qed

8.2  Example: dressed catenoids

Theorem 8.7
.

For any catenoid, there exists a two-real-parameter family of simple factor dressings, each one admitting a discrete rotational symmetry and an extra end on every fundamental piece. The first parameter
u>0
determines the angle of the rotation, while the second parameter
\ell\in\mathbb{R}\mathrm{P}^{1}
determines the location of the end. The dressed surface closes over a cover of the catenoid’s domain if and only if
u\in\mathbb{Q}
. In this case,
u=n/r
where
r
is the wrapping number and
n
is the number of extra ends.

Proof.

We first study the Euclidean case. Let
f
be a catenoid in
\mathbb{E}^{3}
. By theorem
6.10
,
f
is obtained via the LWR with the data of theorem
6.10
. Thus,
\Phi=Cz^{K}
with
\det K=-(q\lambda+p)
with
q\in\mathbb{R}^{*}
and
p>0
. Let

u>0,\quad u\neq 1,\quad\ell\in\mathbb{R}\mathrm{P}^{1}.

Write
\det K_{\lambda}=-\mu_{\lambda}^{2}
with
\mu_{0}>0
. Let
\alpha:=p(u^{2}-1)/q\in\mathbb{R}^{*}
so that
\mu_{\alpha}=u\sqrt{p}>0
and
\mu_{\alpha}/\mu_{0}=u>0
. Let
g:=\mathrm{SF}(\alpha,\ell,m)
for any
m\in\mathbb{C}P^{1}
. Define

\hat{\Phi}:=\hat{g}\Phi h^{-1},\quad\hat{g}:=\sqrt{\det g_{0}}g_{0}^{-1}g.

where
h
is defined via
g\#\Phi=g\Phi h^{-1}
as in theorem
8.3
.
Let
\hat{f}
be the immersion induced by
\hat{\Phi}
at the evaluation points
(\lambda_{0},\lambda_{1})=(0,1)
.

To check that
\hat{f}
admits a discrete rotational symmetry, let
\tau\colon z\mapsto e^{i\pi/\mu_{\alpha}}z
and recall that
\tau^{*}\Phi=R(\pi/\mu_{\alpha})\Phi
with
R(t)=C\exp(itK)C^{-1}
. Compute
R_{\alpha}(\pi/\mu_{\alpha})=-\mathrm{I}
and deduce with (
8.3
) that
\tau^{*}x=x
.
Moreover,
\tau^{*}y=y
, so
\tau^{*}h=h
. We then have

\tau^{*}\hat{\Phi}=\hat{R}\hat{\Phi}

with

(8.7)

\hat{R}:=\hat{g}R(\pi/\mu_{\alpha})\hat{g}^{-1}.

Note that
\hat{R}=\tilde{g}R(\pi/\mu_{\alpha})\tilde{g}^{-1}
where
\tilde{g}:=g_{0}^{-1}g\in\mathrm{SL}_{2}\mathbb{C}
is holomorphic for
\lambda
in a neighborhood of
\lambda_{0}=0
and
\tilde{g}_{0}=\mathrm{I}
.
Moreover, the wrapping number of the catenoid is
2\mu_{0}
, so
R(\pi/\mu_{\alpha})
induces a rotation of angle
\frac{2\pi\mu_{0}}{\mu_{\alpha}}
. By proposition
4.14
,
\hat{R}
induces a rotation of the same angle. Therefore, a rotation of angle
2\pi/(2u\sqrt{p})
in the domain induces a rotation of angle
2\pi/u
in space.

One can compute explicitly the location of the singularities of
h
by solving for
z
with the notations of (
8.3
):

\Phi_{\alpha}^{-1}\ell=y\quad\iff\quad\Phi_{\alpha}w\in\ell\quad\iff\quad-\frac{1}{\mu_{0}}\cdot\frac{(\mu_{0}+\mu_{\alpha})z^{2\mu_{\alpha}}-(\mu_{0}-\mu_{\alpha})}{(\mu_{0}-\mu_{\alpha})z^{2\mu_{\alpha}}-(\mu_{0}+\mu_{\alpha})}=\frac{\ell_{1}}{\ell_{2}}.

Considering
z^{2\mu_{\alpha}}
as the unknown of the equation, it has exactly one solution. Therefore, the dressed surface admits one extra end on each fundamental domain of its rotational symmetry.
Note that the change

\begin{pmatrix}\ell_{1}\\
\ell_{2}\end{pmatrix}\mapsto\begin{pmatrix}\omega\ell_{1}\\
\ell_{2}\end{pmatrix},\quad\Phi\mapsto\begin{pmatrix}\omega^{1/2}&0\\
0&\omega^{-1/2}\end{pmatrix}\Phi\qquad(\omega\in\mathbb{S}^{1}),

induces a rotation of the surface, and justifies our normalization
\ell\in\mathbb{R}\mathrm{P}^{1}
. This parameter will determine the location
z_{1}
of the extra singularity in each fundamental domain. By (
3.1
), this singularity is an end because

\det h\mathop{\longrightarrow}_{z\to z_{1}}0\implies\lVert h^{-1}\rVert\mathop{\longrightarrow}_{z\to z_{1}}\infty\implies ds^{2}\mathop{\longrightarrow}_{z\to z_{1}}\infty

and the end is planar because the Hopf differential holomorphically extends to
z_{1}
by corollary
8.5
.

The proof in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
follows the same arguments with

u>0,\quad u\neq 1,\quad u\neq\frac{\sqrt{p+q}}{\sqrt{p}},\quad\ell\in\mathbb{R}\mathrm{P}^{1}

so that
\alpha\notin\{0,1\}
. Let
\hat{g}:=\sqrt{\det g_{1}}g_{1}^{-1}g
. Use proposition
4.15
to show that the surface admits a discrete rotational symmetry.
∎
