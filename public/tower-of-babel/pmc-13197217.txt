Protection of Hygroscopic Pharmaceutical and Nutraceutical Active Ingredients via Film Coating Technologies: A Review
Authors: Pitriani Pitriani, Aisyah Tri Mulyani, Yoga Windhu Wardhana, Anis Yohana Chaerunisaa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHygroscopicity poses a significant challenge in the formulation of pharmaceutical and nutraceutical products, as moisture absorption often leads to chemical degradation, physical instability, and compromised therapeutic efficacy. This narrative literature review, evaluated the comparative effectiveness of various polymeric systems and coating technologies as moisture barriers across diverse dosage forms, including tablets, powders, granules, and particulates. We analyzed a spectrum of barrier materials, including cellulose derivatives, polyvinylpyrrolidone, polyvinyl alcohol (PVA), methacrylates, and shellac, contrasting conventional solvent based techniques (fluid bed, spray drying, freeze drying coating) with advanced methodologies (hot melt coating, electrostatic dry coating, and Atomic Layer Deposition/ALD). Conventional coating systems utilizing HPMC and PVA were found to maintain moisture uptake below 1.5% during long-term storage (40°C/75% RH). However, more advanced strategies demonstrated superior protective capabilities, for instance, hotmelt coating employing lipid-based excipients (Precirol ATO 5) reduced moisture absorption by up to 85% at 75% RH. Furthermore, electrostatic dry coating showed a significant reduction in weight gain, decreasing from 6.5% to 3.3% compared to uncoated cores over a 48-hour period. Notably, ALD provided the most robust protection, preserving the amorphous stability of sensitive particles for up to two years under extreme conditions. This review highlights that film coatings not only mitigate moisture induced degradation but also enhance shelf life and improve the mechanical properties of dosage forms. These results underscore the versatility of coating technologies, enabling formulators to tailor strategies for highly moisture sensitive compounds.Keywords: coating, film coating, film-forming polymers, hygroscopic

Introduction

Hygroscopicity refers to the ability of a substance to absorb or retain moisture from its surrounding environment.1,2 Hygroscopicity is classified into four categories based on the percentage of weight gain at 25°C and 80% Relative Humidity (RH) over 24 hours: non hygroscopic (0–0.12% w/w), slightly hygroscopic (0.2–2% w/w), hygroscopic (2–15% w/w), and very hygroscopic (>15% w/w).3 In pharmaceutical and nutraceutical formulations, the presence of hygroscopic active ingredients poses a significant challenge due to their high sensitivity to humidity.4,5 Exposure to moisture can initiate undesirable chemical reactions, such as hydrolysis, which may compromise the therapeutic efficacy of the active compound and potentially lead to the formation of degradation products that pose risks to human health.6,7 Highly hygroscopic substances typically possess polar functional groups in their structure, such as hydroxyl (-OH) or carbonyl (-C=O), allowing them to form hydrogen bonds with water molecules. These interactions contribute to the hydrophilic nature of the substance, promoting water absorption from the humid atmosphere and causing the material to become damp or partially dissolve. Many pharmaceutical active ingredients with polar functional groups tend to absorb moisture.8,9 Various organic compounds and salts, such as carboxylic acids,10 amino acids,11 sugars,11 magnesium salts, such as carboxylic acids,12 exhibit varying levels of hygroscopicity, influenced by functional group structure, molecular weight, and water solubility.13

Increased hygroscopicity can affect active substances physicochemical properties, stability, and bioavailability.14,15 Physically, moisture absorption can induce clumping and deliquescence, leading to material degradation, discoloration, and transitions in crystalline structure.16–19 Chemically, absorbed moisture acts as a catalyst for hydrolytic reactions, thereby compromising the chemical integrity of the active compound, reducing its molar mass, and disrupting both the overall crystallinity and thermal stability of the formulation.20 To mitigate the adverse effects associated with high hygroscopicity, robust strategies are required to shield active ingredients from environmental moisture. Consequently, film coating technology has emerged as the most effective and widely adopted intervention for addressing these stability challenges within pharmaceutical formulations.21 Techniques for reducing moisture-induced instability include molecular changes like salt production and crystal engineering as well as external interventions like customized packaging. However, molecular changes run the danger of changing the active ingredient’s physicochemical characteristics, and if the patient breaks the main seal, package protection is jeopardized. As a result, film coating is a better option as it provides an inherent physical barrier that shields the dosage form for the duration of its life without changing the chemical structure of the active component.21,22

This technique aims to restrict water penetration into the core of the dosage form, thereby protecting the active ingredient from moisture-induced degradation. The solution diffusion model, which governs moisture permeability based on the solubility and diffusivity of water vapor inside the polymer matrix, explains why this protection goes beyond simple physical blockage. Film coatings offer several advantages, including superior mechanical properties, relatively fast processing, space efficiency, and adaptability to specific formulation requirements.23–25 Furthermore, the film layer acts as a barrier against moisture, light, and oxygen, providing comprehensive protection for moisture-sensitive actives. This barrier function not only prolongs product shelf life and enhances stability but also slows down degradation pathways such as hydrolysis and oxidation.26,27 However, the physicochemical characteristics of the chosen polymers as well as the particular coating technique used have a significant impact on how effective this protection is. Several film coating techniques have been developed, including fluid bed coating, pan coating, spray coating, freeze-dry coating, and advanced coating.28–31

One of the most implemented techniques in film coating technology is the solvent-based approach, widely applied in the pharmaceutical industry due to its efficiency and diagnostic capabilities in forming protective layers against moisture.32 The solvent-based film coating method involves depositing a thin polymer layer onto the surface of a tablet core through a spraying technique.24 The coating solution or suspension, which contains polymers and other ingredients such as pigments and plasticizers, is sprayed onto the rotating tablet cores within a container. The drying process is facilitated by the passage of hot air through the base of the tablets, enabling the evaporation of the solvent and leaving a thin film on the surface of each tablet core. The film’s formation depends on the polymer’s physicochemical properties. Plasticizers are added to lower the glass transition temperature and enhance the flexibility of the film, thus preventing the polymer film’s cracking or peeling. This is a crucial trade off in formulation, whereas plasticizers are necessary for mechanical integrity, their presence raises the free volume of the polymer, which may unintentionally promote greater rates of moisture transfer.26,33

Although solvent-based solutions are common, the industry is looking more and more into solvent-free options to improve formulation stability and process efficiency. Numerous coating techniques have been developed for solvent-based and solvent-free processes to enhance the efficiency of the coating process. However, each method has advantages and disadvantages, which must be considered when selecting the appropriate coating technology. Additionally, the coating layers uniformity and the formulations stability under varying storage conditions are critical factors in the coating process. Selecting appropriate polymer materials and applying suitable coating technologies are essential for producing stable, high-quality pharmaceutical products. Furthermore, in order to guarantee that the chosen moisture barrier retains its integrity for the duration of the product’s anticipated shelf life, these decisions must closely adhere to industrial regulatory frameworks, including ICH Q1A stability testing criteria. Previous literature reviews have discussed pharmaceutical film coating;25,34,35 however, a critical gap remains in the evaluation of specific moisture protection performance, particularly regarding the interplay between polymer hydrophilicity and coating methodologies. This review specifically evaluates the application of coating technologies across various pharmaceutical and nutraceutical solid oral dosage forms, including tablets, powders, granules, and microparticles. The goal of this methodical approach is to give formulators a useful, data-driven framework for choosing the best protection measures for extremely sensitive materials. The categorization of coating polymers, their methods for protecting against moisture, and a comparative analysis of the efficacy of modern coatings are covered in depth in the sections that follow. Methods

This study employs a narrative literature review by applying the identification strategy framework, which was then adapted into keywords for database searches. A comprehensive literature search was conducted using the PubMed, Scopus, Google Scholar, and ScienceDirect databases for the period of 2011–2025. These databases were chosen to encompass all peer-reviewed literature. The search filters applied included the terms “film coating” AND “hygroscopic” AND “moisture protection”. These topics were combined using the Boolean operators “AND” and “OR”.

The framework adopted in this review comprised four essential elements: Population (P): active pharmaceutical ingredients (APIs) and nutraceutical products characterized by high hygroscopicity and moisture sensitivity; Intervention (I): the application of various film coating technologies, encompassing conventional solvent-based methods as well as advanced techniques such as solvent-free coating; Comparison (C): a comparative analysis of the protective efficiency among different polymer types and a performance evaluation between traditional coating methods and emerging technologies; and Outcome (O): enhanced moisture barrier efficiency and a significant reduction in water absorption percentages.

The inclusion criteria for this review were as follows: (1) original research articles, (2) published between 2011 and 2025, (3) availability of the full-text version, (4) studies focusing on the use of film coating to protect hygroscopic substances, (5) investigations involving pharmaceutical or nutraceutical dosage forms, (6) availability of data regarding hygroscopicity parameters, and (7) articles published in English. Articles failing to meet these predefined criteria, including non-original research and duplicate publications, were excluded from the analysis.

The comprehensive search using PubMed, Scopus, and ScienceDirect resulted in 8937 articles covering the period from 2011 to 2025. Duplicate articles, titles, abstracts, and full-text screenings were manually sorted. Film Coating

Film coating is an effective strategy to reduce the hygroscopic properties of pharmaceutical or nutraceutical formulations.37,38 This technique works by forming a thin and uniform film layer on the surface of the solid core, thereby creating a moisture barrier from the surrounding environment. In addition to serving as a moisture barrier, film coating prevents interactions between the active ingredients and the external environment, masks undesirable taste and odor, and can control the release of active substances.21,39 The film coating method offers several advantages, including rapid processing, space efficiency in production, potential for automation, and the production of layers that generally possess outstanding mechanical properties without causing significant increases in tablet size. Additionally, it offers ease of adaptation to specific formulation needs. However, like other technologies, film coating also presents challenges. The coating process requires precise control to produce a uniform and consistent film. Imperfections in the coating can result in defects that may compromise its effectiveness as a moisture barrier and affect the stability of the pharmaceutical formulation.23,40,41 Therefore, it is essential to optimize the coating conditions, including the spray rate, inlet air temperature, pan speed, and the use of high-quality materials, to achieve uniformity and enhance the overall quality of the film coating process.42,43

Polymer Coating for Hygroscopic Substances

Polymer coatings used for moisture protection consist of water-soluble polymers and organic solvent-soluble polymers. These two types of polymers play a crucial role in ensuring the stability of active ingredients in pharmaceutical formulations, particularly for hygroscopic substances. Water-soluble polymers, such as cellulose, polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP) and polyvinyl alcohol (PVA), are increasingly favored for moisture barrier coatings due to their solubility in water and minimal impact on drug release.44 These polymers facilitate aqueous coating processes, thereby avoiding complications associated with organic solvents. Additionally, biodegradable water-soluble polymers reduce environmental risks.45 Organic solvent-soluble polymers exhibit promising properties for moisture protection coatings due to their ability to form films with low permeability. These polymers include methacrylate, shellac, and cellulose.46 Solutions or suspensions of these polymers in water or organic solvents are finely sprayed during fluid bed or pan coating procedures. The spray droplets form a thin layer, which is then evaporated.

Application of Polymers as Aqueous Solvent Systems for Protection of Hygroscopic Active Ingredients

In formulation, particularly in pharmaceutical and nutraceutical preparations, protection against degradation caused by the hygroscopicity of active substances is of paramount importance.63 Hygroscopicity can lead to thermodynamic destabilization, phase changes, chemical degradation, and detrimental modifications of physicochemical properties.64 A study conducted by Iggui et al65 on PHBV bio nanocomposites demonstrated that increased hygroscopicity can trigger hydrolytic reactions. Cellulose

Hydroxypropyl methylcellulose (HPMC) is a hydrophilic polymer derived from cellulose, modified by adding hydroxypropyl and methyl groups. It exhibits excellent mechanical properties, making it suitable for forming flexible and durable films, as demonstrated in the SF/HPMC mixed film.66,67 HPMC is widely used in pharmaceutical applications, particularly as a film-coating polymer for tablet formulations containing hygroscopic active ingredients.68 It can also form films that control drug release and mask undesirable tastes and odors. The solubility of HPMC in water and organic solvents across the biological pH range makes it an ideal candidate for film coating applications.69 Due to its hydrophilic nature, HPMC is widely utilized in film coating for its ability to form a continuous and viscous film layer. This layer serves as a robust physical barrier against moisture ingress, effectively protecting hygroscopic active ingredients in pharmaceutical formulations from environmental humidity.70,71

HPMC as Polymer Coating in Monolithic System

Abbas et al53 reported that glucosamine potassium chloride and diacerein tablets coated with HPMC polymer were able to control the increase in hygroscopicity. The study findings indicated that, during six months of storage at 40°C and 75% relative humidity, the HPMC-coated tablets exhibited water absorption of less than 1.5%, whereas the uncoated tablets underwent color changes and became sticky. These results demonstrate that HPMC provides effective moisture protection, making it an ideal choice for preserving the stability of hygroscopic active ingredients.

Li et al54 compared the use of HPMC and PVP coating polymers for tablets containing active ingredients from extract powders, aiming to protect the active compounds from moisture. The water absorption in tablets coated with HPMC was lower than that in tablets coated with PVP. In the study conducted by T. Y. Han et al55 HPMC polymer was also used to coat tablets containing the herbal active ingredient Xianqu, which is sensitive to moisture. The results indicated that the tablets coated with HPMC exhibited a moisture content of 6.412%, whereas the uncoated tablets had a moisture content of 1.783%. Research conducted by Li et al57 showed that tablet coatings with the polymers HPMC and silica, containing the active ingredient Zingiberis rhizoma extract, provided adequate moisture protection compared to uncoated tablets, with the moisture content of the coated tablets being 14.42%, while the uncoated tablets had 18.2%. This indicates that the HPMC polymer and silica effectively provide protection against moisture.

HPMC as Polymer Coating in Multiparticulate System

The type of herbal material being coated determined the extent of water absorption, with a lower absorption rate observed in certain materials. For instance, the moisture content of the powdered Zingiberis Rhizoma extracted with ethyl alcohol was 18.2%. In contrast, the composite particles coated with HPMC exhibited a moisture content of 15.1%, and those coated with PVP were 15.2%. Similarly, the powdered Ganoderma extracted with water showed a moisture content of 18.35%. The corresponding composite particles coated with HPMC had a moisture content of 17.6%, while those coated with PVP measured 17.9%.

HPMC Coating as Polymer Blend

Optimal moisture protection can be achieved by combining water-soluble and water-insoluble polymers provides a dual mechanism barrier. The hydrophilic polymer interacts with moisture to form a viscous shield, whereas the hydrophobic polymer minimizes overall water uptake by limiting permeability. Furthermore, the combination of these two types of polymers can be used to balance moisture protection and drug release profiles. However, this combination may not produce a smooth and continuous layer due to the polarity differences between hydrophilic and hydrophobic polymers, leading to an uneven mixture.72 For example, a study by Mabrouki and Kaukhova27 used HPMC and hydrophobic shellac to coat tablets containing the active ingredient Herniaria glabra extract. The study demonstrated adequate moisture protection compared to the core tablets, with the weight gain reduced from 16.1% for the core tablets to 5.7% at 75% RH and from 18.2% to 7.5% at 90% RH after approximately 110 hours. The data indicates that the combination of hydrophilic and hydrophobic polymers can provide moisture protection in pharmaceutical formulation applications.

A study conducted by Duyen et al58 compared tablet coatings using various polymers, including HPMC, Opadry (HPMC), and Eugrarite. When compared to other commercial polymers such as Eugrarite and Opadry, the tablets coated with HPMC exhibited a low moisture content of 3.22%. HPMC demonstrated superior performance in protecting the tablets from moisture, as it forms a film layer that spreads evenly, adheres firmly to the tablet surface, and dries stably, resulting in a thin film layer ideal for preventing moisture absorption.

Heinamaki et al59 utilized HPMC combined with Suberin Fatty Acids (SFA) as a film-forming agent to protect hygroscopic active substances. The research team found that adding SFA to HPMC films reduced water vapor permeability, enhancing moisture protection in pharmaceutical applications. Films with higher concentrations of SFA (up to 15%) exhibited the lowest water vapor permeability, indicating effective moisture barrier properties. Thermal analysis data also showed that the films glass transition temperature decreased with SFA, which suggested increased plasticization and better film formation compared to HPMC films without added Suberin Fatty Acids (SFA).

Research by Penhasi et al60 demonstrated that the addition of a Polymeric Surface-Active Agent (PSAA) to a mixture of hydrophilic Hydroxypropyl Cellulose (HPC) and lipophilic Stearic Acid (SA) could help achieve solid dispersion between two different phases, as observed in calcium chloride coatings. PSAA at specific concentrations helped form micelles that strengthened the bond between the phases, resulting in a uniform and continuous film layer. The combination of HPC/SA/PSAA in a 62:25:10 ratio effectively reduced water vapor permeability compared to single polymer use, with a lower water vapor transmission rate of 60 g/m2 per day, compared to 180 g/m2 per day for single HPC films. Moreover, this combination reduced water absorption, with only a 3.5% weight increase after 168 hours at 75% RH, compared to a 10% increase in uncoated tablets. These findings suggest that combining water-soluble and water-insoluble polymers could effectively address high water absorption.

Opadry is a coating mixture containing Hydroxypropyl methylcellulose as a film-forming agent. A study by Yunarto et al61 used Opadry AMB II (HPMC) as a polymer coating for tablets containing the active ingredient ethyl acetate fraction of Gambir, addressing its limitations, such as high sensitivity to moisture and light. The study found that tablets coated with Opadry AMB II were effective in addressing hygroscopicity issues. Opadry AMB II contains hydroxyl groups (-OH), enhancing polymer chain cohesion and forming a film layer. The resulting film has mechanical strength from these bonds, preventing moisture absorption. Coated tablets demonstrated physical and chemical stability during accelerated stability testing at 75% relative humidity and a temperature of 40°C ± 2°C, showing only minimal changes in catechin content, which remained at approximately 94.22% ± 0.50% in the first month and remained stable at 94.12% ± 0.14% after six months.

Another example of Opadry polymer use is demonstrated in research by Oza and Sagar,28 where they used Opadry II (HPMC) as a polymer coating for tablets containing the highly hygroscopic active ingredient glucosamine sulfate potassium chloride, which is prone to degradation upon exposure to moisture. The study revealed that the coated tablets absorbed less than 0.7% moisture. This finding suggests that the coating film is highly effective in controlling moisture and maintaining the physical and chemical stability of the solid dosage form.

Polyvinylpyrrolidone

PVP contains carbonyl groups, PVP exhibits excellent film forming properties, creating a regular and continuous layer. This film acts as a physical barrier that sequesters moisture, effectively shielding the pharmaceutical preparation from external humidity.73,74 Intrinsic Hygroscopicity and Difficulties with Technology PVP, or polyvinylpyrrolidone, is well known for its superior film-forming and binding properties. However, because of its inherent hygroscopicity, its use in moisture protection poses a unique technological problem. PVP thin films are easily able to absorb air moisture, which acts as a solvent and considerably reduces the polymer’s glass transition (Tg). The film changes from a rigid, glassy state to a more mobile, compliant state as a result of the plasticization and swelling of the polymer network caused by this moisture absorption, particularly under high humidity conditions. Higher diffusion rates inside the matrix may be made possible by this moisture induced flexibility, which might jeopardize the integrity of the barrier and serve as a moisture reservoir for the core.75 Recent research, however, shows that process optimization may be used to manage this constraint. For instance, Zheng et al62 used polyvinylpyrrolidone as a coating polymer for effervescent tablets containing citric acid and sodium bicarbonate. This coating was employed to address the limitations of the active ingredients, which exhibit high hygroscopicity and stickiness. The study demonstrated that effervescent tablets coated with PVP effectively tackled the problem of high hygroscopicity. Using the fluid bed coating technique, the coated tablets showed improved flow properties, reduced particle adhesion, and higher stability against moisture.

Polyvinyl Alcohol

PVA emulsifies various active ingredients, provides high tensile strength, and resists abrasion. Additionally, PVA acts as a barrier to oxygen, enhancing chemical stability by separating incompatible compounds in fixed-dose combination formulations and contributing to forming smooth and consistent tablet surfaces.76–78 The semi-crystalline structure of PVA forms a denser barrier against water diffusion compared to the amorphous network of HPMC or the hygroscopic, swelling prone PVP. For example, a study by Higuchi et al29 used PVA and mannitol as a coating for L-cysteine tablets to protect the tablets from moisture and mask unpleasant taste. The moisture absorption rate of the PVA/mannitol coating, which has a concentration ratio of 15:2.5–15:4 (w/w) and is applied to smooth tablet surfaces containing the natural antioxidant L-cysteine, was lower than that of both PVA-coated tablets and uncoated tablets. This study connected the moisture absorption rate of the coating to surface roughness, where smoother surfaces resulted in lower moisture absorption rates. The incorporation of mannitol, characterized by its low hygroscopicity, into the hydrophilic PVA matrix creates a composite film that functions as a robust barrier against moisture.29

Methacrylate

Eudragit is a polymer derived from polymethacrylate, widely used in pharmaceutical applications such as drug delivery systems and protection against moisture. Eudragit is used as a coating agent in pharmaceutical formulations, resistant to acids and soluble in basic environments. Eudragit is an anionic (Eudragit L 30D-55), cationic (Eudragit E PO), and neutral copolymer, containing various concentrations of methacrylic acid esters, alkyl methacrylate, and 2- (dimethyl amino) ethyl methacrylate. Generally, variations in the concentration of ionized and unionized groups are adjusted in their structure. It is commonly used as a material for coating oral tablet and capsule formulations for protection and to provide sustained-release dosing.88

The use of organic solvent-soluble polymers to protect hygroscopic active ingredients has been demonstrated in research by Mwesigwa and Basit82 which used Eudragit L 30D-55 as a coating polymer, showing effective moisture protection with low water absorption (0.19%) at a relative humidity (RH) of 75%. Eudragit L 30D-55 was found to be less hygroscopic compared to other commercial polymers, such as Opadry AMB and Sepifilm LP 014 (HPMC). A study by Wang et al83 combined Glycerin Monostearate and Eudragit L 30D-55 to provide effective moisture protection. This combination reduced moisture absorption to 0.53% at 75% RH, compared to an increase of 1.24% in uncoated tablet cores and 1.61% in tablet cores coated with Eudragit L 30D-55, indicating better stability of the coated tablets against moisture.

Tipduangta et al89 used Eudragit E as a coating polymer to protect active ingredients from instability due to moisture. Their study showed that Eudragit E effectively reduced moisture absorption by up to 50%, likely due to its intrinsic low water absorption. Another study by Y. C. Ng et al84 demonstrated that Eudragit E PO showed lower water absorption compared to other tested polymers like PVP K30 and Soluplus. In this study, Eudragit E PO exhibited the strongest inhibition of crystallization in amorphous drugs, as it prevented moisture absorption, which could otherwise cause drug degradation or crystallization.

The combination of polymers can serve as an alternative to single-polymer coatings. For example, the use of the commercial hydrophilic polymer combination of Eudragit E PO and Eudragit RLPO. A study by Patel et al85 employed the hydrophilic Eudragit E PO and Eudragit RLPO to coat moisture-sensitive ranitidine hydrochloride, used for the treatment of gastroesophageal reflux disease. The coated tablets demonstrated superior moisture protection compared to the marketed RANTEC 300 (Ranitidine hydrochloride) formulation, absorbing only 10–15% moisture after 170 hours at 75% RH, RANTEC 300 while absorbed 35–40%, highlighting the effectiveness of the hydrophilic polymer combination as a moisture barrier.

Even though methacrylate systems have been shown to be effective at protecting against moisture, their use poses unique processing and regulatory issues that need to be carefully handled. Historically, a lot of methacrylate coatings used organic solvents, which raise serious issues with regard to operator safety, environmental compliance, and the stringent residual solvent limits mandated by regulations.90–92 Formulators also have to balance the importance of barrier density and release kinetics thickening the coating to reduce water vapor permeability may unintentionally delay the dosage form’s breakdown and disintegration. In order to guarantee that the coating efficiently keeps out atmospheric moisture while being stored without impeding the active ingredient’s immediate or modified release upon administration, the polymer layer must be optimized by a precise balance.93–95

Shellac

Because of its safety and ability to create films, shellac, which is naturally generated from the resin of the female lac insect (Kerria lacca), is highly prized in the food and pharmaceutical sectors. However, inherent physicochemical restrictions frequently limit its use as a moisture barrier, requiring precise formulation techniques. The high brittleness of native shellac films increases the possibility of microcracks forming during handling, and its pH-dependent solubility limits its applicability to enteric applications.96,97 Additionally, when shellac ages, it is prone to polymerization, a process in which the network of polymers gets denser over time, which may result in longer rates of disintegration when stored. Recent research shows that, when tuned, it remains relevant despite these obstacles. For example, Huang et al’s research X. Huang et al31 reported that shellac, when used as a polymer coating, effectively protects active compounds from humidity exposure. Compared to uncoated microcapsules, those coated with shellac exhibited lower hygroscopicity and reduced water vapor adsorption rates, indicating that shellac serves as an effective moisture barrier. Furthermore, the study found that active compounds encapsulated with shellac maintained higher probiotic stability, particularly under conditions of low relative humidity and elevated temperatures. The effectiveness of shellac as a coating material was also examined in a study by Yin et al.30 The results indicated that microcapsules containing active compounds coated with shellac exhibited lower hygroscopicity and slower water vapor adsorption rates. These findings suggest that shellac serves as an effective moisture barrier. Moreover, the study demonstrated that shellac-based polymer coatings provided the highest level of protection for probiotics during storage, thereby enhancing the stability of sensitive bioactive compounds.

Cellulose

Reven et al86 investigated the use of Hydroxypropyl Methylcellulose phthalate (HPMC phthalate) as a polymer coating to protect glimepiride-containing tablets. The study demonstrated that HPMC phthalate provided effective moisture resistance. Compared to uncoated core tablets, which showed a 3% weight increase after 24 hours at 75% relative humidity (RH), the coated tablets exhibited only 1.7% water uptake under the same conditions. This moisture barrier property is attributed to the hydrophobic nature of the phthalate groups. As a result, HPMC phthalate coating enhances the tablet’s stability under humid conditions by reducing water absorption and preventing the degradation of the active pharmaceutical ingredient.

To protect tablet cores from excessive moisture absorption during storage, a gradual coating process was employed. The first step involved sub coating with Opadry I (HPMC), a hydroxypropyl methylcellulose-based coating system dissolved in organic solvents, applied until a 1% weight increase was achieved. The second coating was done with Opadry AMB, a coating system using an aqueous solvent. This second layer formed a barrier against water vapor and was applied until a 3% weight increase was reached. This double-layer coating effectively enhanced tablet stability against moisture, maintaining both the physical and chemical quality of the tablets during long-term storage, even in high RH conditions.80

A study conducted by Kazlauske et al81 used Surelease (ethyl cellulose), as a polymer to maintain the stability of moisture-sensitive theophylline tablets. The results showed that the theophylline tablets coated with Surelease remained stable at 50°C for over 15 days, indicating that the film formed by this polymer was resistant to humid conditions. The water permeability of the Surelease film remained consistent, showing that the coating was not significantly affected by moisture and had the ability to limit excessive water penetration.

Coating Methods for Hygroscopic Active Substances

Conventional Methods

Pan Coating

Pan coatings are widely used in the pharmaceutical industry to prevent increased hygroscopicity, improve stability, control release, and mask unpleasant odors and tastes. For instance, it has been effectively applied to coat the ethyl acetate fraction of Gambir,61 L-cysteine tablets,29 glucosamine and diacerein tablets,53 amlodipine tablets,58 aspirin tablets,83 glimepiride tablets,86 ranitidine hydrochloride tablets,85 and choline alfoscerate tablets.80 These various applications demonstrate the value of pan coating in creating a robust physical barrier against environmental moisture. This coating strategy is crucial in modern pharmaceutical manufacturing for improving the physicochemical stability and significantly extending the shelf life of a wide range of hygroscopic pharmaceutical active ingredients.

The process of film layer formation to prevent moisture absorption using pan coating begins by placing the solid core into the machine. As the machine rotates, the solid core spins within the container. Then, a coating solution that has been atomized is sprayed through a nozzle, ensuring the polymer coating layer is evenly distributed across the entire surface of the hygroscopic solid core. To support the formation of a homogeneous film layer and prevent agglomeration, hot air is circulated into the pan, passing over the surface of the solid core. This airflow accelerates the solvent evaporation process and gradually dries the coating film layer. Optimization of parameters such as rotation speed, spray rate, spray pressure, inlet air temperature, and solution volume is critical to ensure the integrity and consistency of the final moisture barrier42,98 (Figure 5. Fluid Bed Dryer

The fluid bed dryer method for coating is an efficient and versatile way to create protective layers on various solid dosage forms. This technique has proven particularly beneficial for hygroscopic active pharmaceutical ingredients (API) due to its ability to precisely control both drying and coating parameters. The reduction of moisture sensitivity has been widely documented in research. For instance, it has been effectively applied to coat glucosamine sulfate potassium chloride tablets,28 effervescent tablets containing citric acid,62 Xianqu herbal tablets,55 Zingiberis Rhizoma tablets,57 and Herniaria glabra tablets,27 calcium chloride tablets,60 theophylline tablets,81 aspirin tablets,82 and ibuprofen.100 The wide and diverse applications of this method collectively demonstrate the fluid bed dryer as a crucial technique for creating effective moisture barriers. This process significantly enhances the physicochemical stability and extends the shelf life of a broad range of hygroscopic pharmaceutical active ingredients.

Mechanistic Insight

Hygroscopicity of active substances is a long-standing yet crucial problem in pharmaceutical research that may be solved with the use of film coating technology, a proven and adaptable formulation technique. In contrast to exterior physical packaging methods that just shield the final product, film coating incorporates protection at the tablet or particle level, forming a micro-barrier that keeps the delicate core dry and protected from the elements. Beyond simple aesthetics or taste masking, the main goal of creating these coating systems is to maintain the drug’s physicochemical stability, stop hydrolysis, and ultimately guarantee that therapeutic efficacy is maintained until the patient is administered.

A thorough grasp of the relationship between polymers and moisture is essential for assessing the therapeutic potential of coating systems. A fascinating contrast between aqueous solvent-based and organic solvent-based coating material selection is revealed by the literature now under publication.

Barrier Film Formation

Though safe and ecologically benign, water-soluble polymers like HPMC and PVA work via a method in which the polymer hydrates to create a thick gel layer that prevents the flow of water vapor. Even while this layer works well for materials with a moderate sensitivity, research suggests that it might not be enough to withstand the rate of water vapor transfer in extreme conditions. In contrast, methacrylate derivatives (Eudragit) and Shellac, which are hydrophobic or soluble in organic solvents, provide better protection by creating an impenetrable wall that physically repels water molecules. These results highlight the need to specifically adjust polymer selection to the desired stability profile and the degree of hygroscopicity of the active pharmaceutical ingredient (API).

The idea of molecular barrier formation may be utilized to explain how film coating increases stability, as several studies have shown. Hydrogen bonds between the polymer’s hydroxyl groups and water molecules first form an aqueous matrix that “binds” moisture before it reaches the tablet core. A polymeric film’s inherent moisture vapor transfer rate, which is primarily determined by the tortuosity and availability of diffusion channels within the matrix, determines how effective the film will ultimately be.

Plasticizer Effect

Although localized hydrogen bonding briefly delays initial moisture infiltration in hydrophilic materials, the penetrating water molecules quickly have a significant plasticizing impact. By increasing the free volume between neighboring polymer chains and acting as a plasticizer, this absorbed water considerably lowers the glass transition temperature of the polymer. As a result, the mobility of the polymer chain rises, unintentionally expanding the internal diffusion channels and generating a positive feedback loop that speeds up more moisture penetration. On the other hand, a more advanced perspective is demonstrated by the application of methods such as hot-melted coating with lipid-based excipients (Precirol ATO 5). This technique vigorously thwarts moisture-induced plasticization due to the lipophilic nature of the covering substance. It greatly reduces moisture absorption by up to 85% by optimizing the tortuosity of the diffusion pathway, which totally eliminates water entrance routes rather than merely delaying diffusion.

Solvent Free Technologies

Due to their cost-effectiveness, traditional coating techniques like pan coating and fluid bed processing have dominated the market, but in order to overcome thermodynamic constraints and solvent residual hazards, a paradigm shift is occurring toward solvent-free technologies. The possibility of API degradation during the coating process itself is a major drawback of traditional techniques, especially when using aqueous solvents on extremely hygroscopic materials. Here, electrostatic dry coating and atomic layer deposition (ALD) provide a ground breaking new viewpoint. For example, under stress circumstances, ALD may deposit nano meter thin layers that preserve the amorphous stability of particles for up to two years.

Accordingly, the future of hygroscopicity protection may rely more on the accuracy and molecular density of the protective substance than on the thickness of the polymer layer. Although there are many effective coating techniques in the literature, it is crucial to distinguish between really innovative technical advancements and incremental formulation refinements. The majority of current research focuses on little steps forward, including combining hydrophilic and hydrophobic polymers to optimize moisture vapor transfer rates (MVTR) or varying the ratio of plasticizers in HPMC or PVA-based systems. Despite the fact that these optimizations are very useful to the industry and result in modest gains in processability and barrier function, they still primarily depend on traditional solvent-evaporation mechanics, which inevitably exposes processing to moisture. However, recent advancements in vapor-phase technologies, particularly Atomic Layer Deposition (ALD), and solvent-free electrostatic dry coating represent really significant paradigm changes instead. These advanced methods do more than simply enhance an existing barrier; they completely avoid the conventional thermodynamic limitations of solvent-based coating. These cutting-edge methods provide unparalleled stabilization for extremely sensitive compounds, such as amorphous solid dispersions and highly hydrolysis-prone APIs, where conventional and incremental approaches consistently fall short. They do this by directly applying nanoscale, conformal protection onto active particulate surfaces without the use of liquid solvents.

Despite the superiority of these cutting-edge technologies, there are still real barriers to commercial scaleup and formulation. The compatibility of the coating liquid and the hygroscopic tablet core is crucial from a formulation standpoint; solvent seeping into the core during spraying may cause premature disintegration before the protective layer is completely developed. Additionally, while natural polymers like Shellac work well as barriers, they frequently show substantial batch-to-batch variability as compared to synthetic polymers like Opadry or methacrylate derivatives, which can alter the uniformity of drug release. Formulators must strike a compromise between the highest level of moisture protection and a profile of quick disintegration upon ingestion.

From a manufacturing perspective, switching to more sophisticated techniques like hot-melt or electrostatic coating necessitates purchasing a lot of equipment and controlling process parameters (like inlet air temperature, spray rate, and electrical charge uniformity) much more strictly than with traditional techniques. Small changes in manufacturing parameters might result in coating flaws that seriously impair hygroscopic medication stability. To achieve proper droplet formation, careful optimization of the coating suspension is required, particularly regarding the solid-to-solvent ratio and plasticizer concentration. The barrier’s morphological integrity is determined by the interaction of heat conditions and spray rate. While avoiding very dry circumstances that may result in premature droplet evaporation and a porous, discontinuous layer, a closely controlled spray rate shields the highly hygroscopic core from localized over wetting and consequent deterioration. Monitor the final coating thickness and overall buildup. An overly thin layer can leave exposed micro-defects, while a coating that is too thick often leads to mechanical stress and delayed drug release. In order to ensure full coalescence, certain curing conditions must supply the necessary thermal energy for the polymer chains to surpass their glass transition temperature. By efficiently sealing any small gaps created during the solvent’s quick evaporation, this enhanced curing phase solidifies a consistent and strong physical barrier against moisture penetration. Furthermore, one of the main determinants is cost-effectiveness. ALD and electrostatic coating are expensive, and they have trouble getting regulatory approval. Clear, consistent pharmacopeial rules are already in place for conventional techniques like pan and fluid-bed coating. Universal monographs are still lacking for these new technologies, nevertheless. In order to meet GMP criteria, businesses must create their own intricate validation methods from the ground up before using them in commercial production. Strict Quality by Design (QbD) testing is required to demonstrate batch-to-batch consistency for these techniques, which eventually hinders their industrial acceptance. ALD offers better protection, but its operational costs might only be justified for biological active ingredients or high-value products. In the meantime, fluid bed methods optimization using hybrid composite polymers (hydrophilic hydrophobic) might still be the most cost-effective option for generic medications. There is great potential for more advancements in coating technology for hygroscopic active substances. To create coating methods that are both more economical and still efficient in offering long-term moisture protection, additional research is required. Further studies on the selection and optimization of coating polymers, along with the creation of new, more effective and inexpensive methods for scale up, will lead to new possibilities in pharmaceutical formulation.

Due to the absence of uniform testing, comparing this research is extremely difficult. Although moisture protection was assessed in all the trials, the methods used varied greatly exposure durations ranging from 24 hours to months, and humidity levels varied. This made it impossible to perform a straight statistical meta-analysis, and our comparisons only reveal relative trends. In order to increase the industry’s ability to use academic research, future studies must adhere to regulatory criteria such as the ICH Q1A recommendations. It is recommended that researchers use established accelerated stability tests, such 40 °C/75% RH for six months, rather than selecting random testing durations. A novel coating system’s viability for commercial scale up is nearly difficult to ascertain without these conventional testing.

As a result, rather than being absolute comparable results across various experimental setups, the comparisons in this analysis should be understood as relative trends within certain coating systems. A methodical approach is necessary to match the physicochemical restrictions of the API with the proper processing procedure in order to navigate the wide range of coating methods and materials addressed. Conclusion

This review highlights film coating as a highly efficient and versatile approach for protecting hygroscopic pharmaceutical and nutraceutical active ingredients. This process effectiveness mainly depends on a risk-based formulation approach, in which the polymer’s physicochemical properties and coating technique are carefully adapted to each active ingredient’s sensitivity level. According to the literature, traditional water-soluble polymers like HPMC applied by fluid-bed or pan coating are still practical and economical for moderately hygroscopic, thermally stable extracts; however, for formulations exposed to extreme humidity, hydrophobic barriers made of specialized methacrylate copolymers (Eudragit L30D-55) or lipid-based hot melt coatings (Precirol ATO 5) exhibit superior performance, successfully reducing moisture absorption by up to 75%. For the most sensitive formulations, like amorphous particles, new vapor-phase technologies such as Atomic Layer Deposition (ALD) provide unparalleled protection, preserving stability for up to two years under extreme conditions. Sophisticated solvent-free techniques like HPC based electrostatic dry coating effectively reduce the hydrolytic risks associated with aqueous solvents, limiting weight gain to as low as 3.3%, compared to 6.5% in uncoated cores. Despite these technological advancements, the effectiveness of these barrier systems is not absolute. Significant challenges still include batch-to-batch film uniformity, the scalability of solvent-free methods, and the complex interplay between process heat management and microstructural integrity. To bridge the existing methodological gaps and accurately estimate Critical Relative Humidity (CRH) thresholds, future studies must shift from static weight measurements to standardized methodologies. To turn these bench-scale discoveries into useful commercial uses, formulators must ultimately move toward stringent regulatory compliance. Future approaches must use Quality by Design (ICH Q8) and Risk Management (ICH Q9) frameworks to optimize processing parameters while closely adhering to ISO standardized controls and ICH Q1A stability protocols in order to guarantee consistent, long-term barrier integrity. Future studies should fill in a number of important formulation science gaps. The industry currently relies mostly on synthetic polymers. Although these natural materials still need to be optimized to meet synthetic moisture barriers, this raises a demand for sustainable, bio-based alternatives. Furthermore, hybrid coatings are still mostly unstudied. Formulators may be able to strike a compromise between moisture protection and desirable medication release characteristics by combining hydrophilic and hydrophobic ingredients.
