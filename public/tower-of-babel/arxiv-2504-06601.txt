Abstract
. Let X X be a random variable that takes its values in 1 q ​ ℤ \frac{1}{q}\mathbb{Z} , for some integer q ⩾ 2 q\geqslant 2 , and consider X X rounded to an integer, either downwards or upwards or to the nearest integer. We give general formulas for the characteristic function and moments of the rounded variable. These formulas complement the related but different formulas in the case that X X has a continuous distribution, which was studied by Janson (2006).



1.
Introduction

Let
X
be a random variable and consider also
X
rounded to an integer;
more precisely we may consider, for example,
\lfloor X\rfloor
(rounding downwards)
or
\langle X\rangle
(rounding to the nearest integer),
see Section
2
for precise definitions.

We gave in
[
2
]
general formulas for the characteristic function and
moments (in particular, the first and second moments)
of the rounded variable in the important case when
X
has a
continuous distribution; this was motivated by several examples that had
appeared as subsequence limits of integer-valued random variables in
different problems.

In the present paper we consider instead the case of
X
with a discrete
distribution.
More precisely, we suppose that there exists an integer
q\geqslant 2
such that

X
takes its values in
\frac{1}{q}\mathbb{Z}
, or equivalently that
qX\in\mathbb{Z}
a.s.
(For completeness, we allow also
q=1
below, but the results are trivial in
this case.)
Again, we give general formulas for the characteristic function and moments.

The results can be compared to those given in
[
2
]
for the case of a
continuous distribution; the results are similar but different.

Remark 1.1
.

Our setting is obviously equivalent to rounding an integer-valued
random variable to multiples of
q
, and the results can easily be
translated to that case.

2.
Notation

The characteristic function of a random variable
X
is denoted by

\varphi_{X}(t):=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}e^{\mathrm{i}tX}
.

The indicator function of an event
\mathcal{E}
is denoted
\boldsymbol{1}\{\mathcal{E}\}
.

For two integers
j
and
k
,
j\mid k
means that
j
is a divisor of
k
,
i.e., that
k\in j\mathbb{Z}
.

For a real number
x
, let
\lfloor x\rfloor
and
\lceil x\rceil

denote
x
rounded downwards and upwards, respectively, to the nearest integer.
Similarly,

\langle x\rangle
denotes
x
rounded to the nearest integer,
for definiteness choosing the larger one if there is a tie.
Thus

\lfloor x\rfloor\leqslant x<\lfloor x\rfloor+1
,

\lceil x\rceil-1<x\leqslant\lceil x\rceil

and

\langle x\rangle-\frac{1}{2}\leqslant x<\langle x\rangle+\frac{1}{2}
;
furthermore,

\displaystyle\lceil x\rceil

\displaystyle=-\lfloor-x\rfloor,

(2.1)

\displaystyle\langle x\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\lfloor x+\tfrac{1}{2}\right\rfloor.

(2.2)

Remark 2.1
.

Rounding downwards to
\lfloor X\rfloor
is obviously an asymmetric operation;
the mirror operation is to rounding upwards to
\lceil X\rceil
.
Since
\lceil X\rceil=-\lfloor-X\rfloor
by (
2.1
),
we will for simplicity consider only
\lfloor X\rfloor
and
\langle X\rangle
below;
results for
\lceil X\rceil
follow from the results

\lfloor X\rfloor
applied to
-X
. In particular, it is easily
seen that
(
3.5
) and (
4.1
) below hold for
\lceil X\rceil
if we replace

h_{q}(t)
by
h_{q}(-t)
.

Also rounding to
\langle X\rangle
is slightly asymmetric since we choose
the larger
value when there is a tie.
The alternative to choose the lower value is again the mirror operation
given by
-\langle-X\rangle
, and results follow from the results below,
now replacing
\widetilde{h}_{q}(t)
by
\widetilde{h}_{q}(-t)
.

Note that when
q
is odd and
qX
is integer-valued, there cannot be a tie
for the rounding
\langle X\rangle
, so the two versions coincide.
This explains the greater symmetry in some of the results for odd
q
, in
particular that
h_{q}(t)
in (
3.3
) then is a symmetric function.

\triangle

3.
The characteristic function

Let
q\geqslant 1
be a fixed integer.
Suppose that
X
is a random variable such that

qX
is integer-valued,
i.e.,

X\in\frac{1}{q}\mathbb{Z}
(a.s.).
Note that this assumption implies (and in fact is equivalent to)

\varphi_{X}
havíng period
2\pi q
.
We consider the rounded variables
{\lfloor X\rfloor}
and
{\langle X\rangle}
.

We first define two auxilliary functions. Let

\displaystyle h_{q}(t):=\frac{1}{q}\sum_{k=0}^{q-1}e^{-\mathrm{i}tk/q}=\frac{1-e^{-\mathrm{i}t}}{q(1-e^{-\mathrm{i}t/q})}=\frac{\sin\frac{t}{2}}{q\sin\frac{t}{2q}}e^{-\mathrm{i}\frac{q-1}{2q}t},

(3.1)

where the two last formulas are interpreted by continuity as 1 when the
denominator is 0 (i.e., when
t\in 2\pi q\mathbb{Z}
).
Furthermore, let, if
q
is even,

\displaystyle\widetilde{h}_{q}(t):=\frac{1}{q}\sum_{k=-q/2}^{q/2-1}e^{-\mathrm{i}tk/q}=e^{\mathrm{i}t/2}h_{q}(t)=\frac{\sin\frac{t}{2}}{q\sin\frac{t}{2q}}e^{\frac{\mathrm{i}t}{2q}},

(3.2)

and if
q
is odd

\displaystyle\widetilde{h}_{q}(t):=\frac{1}{q}\sum_{k=-(q-1)/2}^{(q-1)/2}e^{-\mathrm{i}tk/q}=e^{\mathrm{i}\frac{q-1}{2q}t}h_{q}(t)=\frac{\sin\frac{t}{2}}{q\sin\frac{t}{2q}}.

(3.3)

Note that these functions may be interpreted as characteristic functions.
Let
U_{q}
be a random variable that is uniformly distributed
on
\{\frac{k}{q}:k=0,\dots,q-1\}
, and let

{\widetilde{U}}_{q}
be a random variable that is uniformly distributed on

\{\frac{k}{q}:k=-\frac{q}{2},\dots,\frac{q}{2}-1\}
(
q
even)
or

\{\frac{k}{q}:k=-\frac{q-1}{2},\dots,\frac{q-1}{2}\}
(
q
odd).
Thus, these random variables are uniformly distributed
on the set of the
q
integer
multiples of
1/q
in
[0,1)
(
U_{q}
) and
[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2})

(
{\widetilde{U}}_{q}
).
Then
h_{q}(-t)
is the characteristic function
\varphi_{U_{q}}(t)
of
U_{q}

and
\widetilde{h}_{q}(-t)
is the characteristic function
\varphi_{{\widetilde{U}}_{q}}(t)
of
{\widetilde{U}}_{q}
;
equivalently,

\displaystyle h_{q}(t)=\varphi_{-U_{q}}(t),\qquad\widetilde{h}_{q}(t)=\varphi_{-{\widetilde{U}}_{q}}(t).

(3.4)

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
q\geqslant 1
be an integer, and
suppose that
X
is a random variable such that

qX
is integer-valued.
Then

\displaystyle\varphi_{\lfloor X\rfloor}(t)=\sum_{j=0}^{q-1}h_{q}(t+2\pi j)\varphi_{X}(t+2\pi j)

(3.5)

and

\displaystyle\varphi_{\langle X\rangle}(t)=\sum_{j=0}^{q-1}\widetilde{h}_{q}(t+2\pi j)\varphi_{X}(t+2\pi j).

(3.6)

Proof.

It is convenient to use the random variables
U_{q}
and
{\widetilde{U}}_{q}
above,
assuming as we may that they are independent of
X
.
Then, for any integer
k
,

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}(\lfloor X\rfloor=k)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{q-1}\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}\Bigl(X=k+\frac{j}{q}\Bigr)=q\sum_{j=0}^{q-1}\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}\Bigl(X=k+\frac{j}{q}\Bigr)\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}\Bigl(U_{q}=\frac{j}{q}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=q\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}\bigl(X-U_{q}=k\bigr).

(3.7)

Furthermore, if
Y
is any random variable with values in
\frac{1}{q}\mathbb{Z}
, then

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{q-1}\varphi_{Y}(t+2\pi j)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{q-1}\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}\sum_{\ell=0}^{q-1}\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}\bigl(Y=k+\ell/q\bigr)e^{\mathrm{i}(t+2\pi j)(k+\ell/q)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}\sum_{\ell=0}^{q-1}e^{\mathrm{i}t(k+\ell/q)}\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}\bigl(Y=k+\ell/q\bigr)\sum_{j=0}^{q-1}e^{2\pi\mathrm{i}j\ell/q}

\displaystyle=q\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}e^{\mathrm{i}tk}\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}\bigl(Y=k\bigr)

(3.8)

since the inner sum over
j
vanishes when
\ell\neq 0
and is
q
for
\ell=0
.
(This argument can be regarded as Fourier inversion on the finite group

\mathbb{Z}_{q}
).
Taking
Y:=X-U_{q}
, we obtain by combining (
3
) and (
3
)
and recalling (
3.4
),

\displaystyle\varphi_{\lfloor X\rfloor}(t)

\displaystyle=q\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}e^{\mathrm{i}tk}\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}\bigl(X-U_{q}=k\bigr)=\sum_{j=0}^{q-1}\varphi_{X-U_{q}}(t+2\pi j)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{q-1}\varphi_{-U_{q}}(t+2\pi j)\varphi_{X}(t+2\pi j)=\sum_{j=0}^{q-1}h_{q}(t+2\pi j)\varphi_{X}(t+2\pi j),

(3.9)

which shows (
3.5
).

We obtain (
3.6
) by the same argument, now using
{\widetilde{U}}_{q}
instead of
U_{q}
.
∎

Remark 3.2
.

The sums in (
3.5
) and (
3.6
) may be taken over any set of
j

that has exactly one element in each residue class mod
j
,
since the summands have period
q
in
j

(because
h_{q}
,
\widetilde{h}_{q}
, and
\varphi_{X}
all are
2\pi q
-periodic).
The same applies to (
4.1
) and (
4.2
) below.

\triangle

4.
Moments

Suppose that for some integer
r\geqslant 1
,

X
has a finite
r
th moment, i.e.,
\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}[|X|^{r}]<\infty
.
Then obviously
\lfloor X\rfloor
and
\langle X\rangle
also have finite
r
th moments.
Theorem
3.1
implies the following formulas.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
q\geqslant 1
be an integer, and
suppose that
X
is a random variable such that

qX
is integer-valued.
Then, for every integer
r\geqslant 1
such that
\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}|X|^{r}<\infty
,

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}\bigl({\lfloor X\rfloor}^{r}\bigr)=\mathrm{i}^{-r}\sum_{j=0}^{q-1}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{r}}{\mathrm{d}t^{r}}\bigl(h_{q}(t)\varphi_{X}(t)\bigr)\bigr|_{t=2\pi j}

(4.1)

and

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}\bigl({\langle X\rangle}^{r}\bigr)=\mathrm{i}^{-r}\sum_{j=0}^{q-1}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{r}}{\mathrm{d}t^{r}}\bigl(\widetilde{h}_{q}(t)\varphi_{X}(t)\bigr)\bigr|_{t=2\pi j}.

(4.2)

Proof.

These follow immediately by
differentiating (
3.5
) and (
3.6
)
r
times at
t=0
.
∎

In the following two sections, we derive more explicit formulas for the
first two moments.

5.
Mean

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
q\geqslant 1
be an integer, and
suppose that
X
is a random variable such that

qX
is integer-valued.
Suppose also
\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}|X|<\infty
.
Then

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}{\lfloor X\rfloor}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}X-\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2q}+\sum_{j=1}^{q-1}\frac{1}{q(1-e^{-2\pi\mathrm{i}j/q})}\varphi_{X}(2\pi j).

(5.1)

If
q
is even, then

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}{\langle X\rangle}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}X+\frac{1}{2q}+\sum_{j=1}^{q-1}\frac{(-1)^{j}}{q(1-e^{-2\pi\mathrm{i}j/q})}\varphi_{X}(2\pi j).

(5.2)

If
q
is odd, then

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}{\langle X\rangle}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}X+\sum_{j=1}^{q-1}\frac{(-1)^{j}}{q(e^{\pi\mathrm{i}j/q}-e^{-\pi\mathrm{i}j/q})}\varphi_{X}(2\pi j).

(5.3)

Proof.

By
taking
r=1
in (
4.1
),
or directly by taking the derivative of (
3.5
) at
t=0
,
we obtain

\displaystyle\mathrm{i}\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}{\lfloor X\rfloor}

\displaystyle=\varphi_{\lfloor X\rfloor}^{\prime}(0)=\sum_{j=0}^{q-1}h_{q}(2\pi j)\varphi^{\prime}_{X}(2\pi j)+\sum_{j=0}^{q-1}h_{q}^{\prime}(2\pi j)\varphi_{X}(2\pi j).

(5.4)

We have
\varphi_{X}^{\prime}(0)=\mathrm{i}\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}X
,
and (
3.1
) yields

\displaystyle h_{q}(0)=1\quad\text{and}\quad h_{q}(2\pi j)=0\text{ for }1\leqslant j\leqslant q-1.

(5.5)

Hence, (
5.4
) simplifies to

\displaystyle\mathrm{i}\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}{\lfloor X\rfloor}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{i}\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}X+\sum_{j=0}^{q-1}h_{q}^{\prime}(2\pi j)\varphi_{X}(2\pi j).

(5.6)

The first expression in (
3.1
) yields

\displaystyle h_{q}^{\prime}(0)=\frac{1}{q}\sum_{k=0}^{q-1}\frac{-\mathrm{i}k}{q}=-\mathrm{i}\frac{q(q-1)/2}{q^{2}}=-\mathrm{i}\frac{q-1}{2q}.

(5.7)

(Alternatively, by (
3.4
) this follows from, and is equivalent to,

\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}U_{q}=\frac{q-1}{2q}
, which easily is seen directly.)
Furthermore,
the second expression in (
3.1
) yields, noting that the numerator
there vanishes at
t=2\pi j
,

\displaystyle h_{q}^{\prime}(2\pi j)=\frac{\mathrm{i}}{q(1-e^{-2\pi\mathrm{i}j/q})},\qquad 1\leqslant j\leqslant q-1.

(5.8)

Consequently, (
5.6
) yields (
5.1
).

For
\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}\langle X\rangle
, we argue in the same way using (
3.6
) or (
4.2
).
We note that (
3.2
)–(
3.3
) and (
5.5
) yield

\displaystyle\widetilde{h}_{q}(0)=1\quad\text{and}\quad\widetilde{h}_{q}(2\pi j)=0\text{ for }1\leqslant j\leqslant q-1.

(5.9)

Hence, taking derivatives in (
3.6
) yields

\displaystyle\mathrm{i}\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}{\langle X\rangle}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{i}\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}X+\sum_{j=0}^{q-1}\widetilde{h}_{q}^{\prime}(2\pi j)\varphi_{X}(2\pi j).

(5.10)

Furthermore, (
3.2
) and (
3.3
)
together with (
5.5
) and (
5.7
)–(
5.8
)
imply that:

If
q
is even, then

\displaystyle\widetilde{h}_{q}^{\prime}(0)=h_{q}^{\prime}(0)+\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}h_{q}(0)=\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2q}

(5.11)

and

\displaystyle\widetilde{h}_{q}^{\prime}(2\pi j)=e^{\mathrm{i}\pi j}h_{q}^{\prime}(2\pi j)=\mathrm{i}\frac{(-1)^{j}}{q(1-e^{-2\pi\mathrm{i}j/q})},\qquad 1\leqslant j\leqslant q-1;

(5.12)

if
q
is odd, then

\displaystyle\widetilde{h}_{q}^{\prime}(0)=h_{q}^{\prime}(0)+\mathrm{i}\frac{q-1}{2q}h_{q}(0)=0

(5.13)

(which also follows from (
3.4
) since
\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}{\widetilde{U}}_{q}=0
when
q
is odd),
and

\displaystyle\widetilde{h}_{q}^{\prime}(2\pi j)=e^{\mathrm{i}\frac{q-1}{q}\pi j}h_{q}^{\prime}(2\pi j)=\mathrm{i}\frac{(-1)^{j}}{q(e^{\pi\mathrm{i}j/q}-e^{-\pi\mathrm{i}j/q})}=\frac{(-1)^{j}}{2q\sin(\pi j/q)},\qquad 1\leqslant j\leqslant q-1.

(5.14)

We obtain (
5.2
) and (
5.3
) by substituting
(
5.11
)–(
5.14
) into (
5.10
).
∎

6.
Second moment

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
q\geqslant 1
be an integer, and
suppose that
X
is a random variable such that

qX
is integer-valued.
Suppose also
\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}[X^{2}]<\infty
.
Then

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}\bigl[{\lfloor X\rfloor}^{2}\bigr]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}[X^{2}]+\frac{2q^{2}-3q+1}{6q^{2}}-\frac{q-1}{q}\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}X-2\sum_{j=1}^{q-1}\frac{\mathrm{i}}{q(1-e^{-2\pi\mathrm{i}j/q})}\varphi^{\prime}_{X}(2\pi j)

\displaystyle\hskip 40.00006pt-\sum_{j=1}^{q-1}\Bigl(\frac{1}{q(1-e^{-2\pi\mathrm{i}j/q})}+2\frac{e^{-2\pi\mathrm{i}j/q}}{q^{2}(1-e^{-2\pi\mathrm{i}j/q})^{2}}\Bigr)\varphi_{X}(2\pi j).

(6.1)

If
q
is even, then

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}\bigl[{\langle X\rangle}^{2}\bigr]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}[X^{2}]+\frac{1}{12}+\frac{1}{6q^{2}}+\frac{1}{q}\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}X-2\sum_{j=1}^{q-1}\mathrm{i}\frac{(-1)^{j}}{q(1-e^{2\pi\mathrm{i}j/q})}\varphi^{\prime}_{X}(2\pi j)

\displaystyle\hskip 40.00006pt+\sum_{j=1}^{q-1}\frac{(-1)^{j}}{2q^{2}\sin^{2}(\pi j/q)}\varphi_{X}(2\pi j).

(6.2)

If
q
is odd, then

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}\bigl[{\langle X\rangle}^{2}\bigr]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}[X^{2}]+\frac{1}{12}-\frac{1}{12q^{2}}-\sum_{j=1}^{q-1}\frac{(-1)^{j}}{q\sin(\pi j/q)}\varphi^{\prime}_{X}(2\pi j)

\displaystyle\hskip 40.00006pt+\sum_{j=1}^{q-1}\frac{(-1)^{j}\cos(\pi j/q)}{2q^{2}\sin^{2}(\pi j/q)}\varphi_{X}(2\pi j).

(6.3)

Proof.

By taking
r=2
in (
4.1
), or directly
by taking the second derivative of (
3.5
) at
t=0
,
we obtain,
using
\varphi_{X}^{\prime\prime}(0)=-\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}[X^{2}]
and (
5.5
),

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}({\lfloor X\rfloor}^{2})

\displaystyle=-\varphi_{\lfloor X\rfloor}^{\prime\prime}(0)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{j=0}^{q-1}h_{q}(2\pi j)\varphi^{\prime\prime}_{X}(2\pi j)-2\sum_{j=0}^{q-1}h_{q}^{\prime}(2\pi j)\varphi^{\prime}_{X}(2\pi j)-\sum_{j=0}^{q-1}h_{q}^{\prime\prime}(2\pi j)\varphi_{X}(2\pi j)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}[X^{2}]-2\sum_{j=0}^{q-1}h_{q}^{\prime}(2\pi j)\varphi^{\prime}_{X}(2\pi j)-\sum_{j=0}^{q-1}h_{q}^{\prime\prime}(2\pi j)\varphi_{X}(2\pi j).

(6.4)

We obtain from (
3.1
)

\displaystyle h_{q}^{\prime\prime}(0)=\frac{1}{q}\sum_{k=0}^{q-1}\frac{-k^{2}}{q^{2}}=-\frac{q(q-1)(2q-1)}{6q^{3}}=-\frac{2q^{2}-3q+1}{6q^{2}}

(6.5)

and, since
e^{2\pi j\mathrm{i}}=1
,

\displaystyle h_{q}^{\prime\prime}(2\pi j)=\frac{1}{q(1-e^{-2\pi\mathrm{i}j/q})}+2\frac{e^{-2\pi\mathrm{i}j/q}}{q^{2}(1-e^{-2\pi\mathrm{i}j/q})^{2}},\qquad j=1,\dots,q-1.

(6.6)

Consequently, using also (
5.7
)–(
5.8
),
\varphi_{X}^{\prime}(0)=\mathrm{i}\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}X
and

\varphi_{X}(0)=1
,
(
6.1
) follows from
(
6
).

Turning to
\langle X\rangle
, we obtain as above, from (
3.6
) or
(
4.2
),

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}({\langle X\rangle}^{2})=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}[X^{2}]-2\sum_{j=0}^{q-1}\widetilde{h}_{q}^{\prime}(2\pi j)\varphi^{\prime}_{X}(2\pi j)-\sum_{j=0}^{q-1}\widetilde{h}_{q}^{\prime\prime}(2\pi j)\varphi_{X}(2\pi j).

(6.7)

For even
q
,
we obtain from (
3.2
), (
6.5
)–(
6.6
),
(
5.7
)–(
5.8
), and (
5.5
),

\displaystyle\widetilde{h}_{q}^{\prime\prime}(0)=h_{q}^{\prime\prime}(0)+\mathrm{i}h_{q}^{\prime}(0)-\tfrac{1}{4}h_{q}(0)=-\frac{q^{2}+2}{12q^{2}}

(6.8)

and

\displaystyle\widetilde{h}_{q}^{\prime\prime}(2\pi j)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{j}h_{q}^{\prime\prime}(2\pi j)+2\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}(-1)^{j}h_{q}^{\prime}(2\pi j)

\displaystyle=2\frac{(-1)^{j}e^{-2\pi\mathrm{i}j/q}}{q^{2}(1-e^{-2\pi\mathrm{i}j/q})^{2}}=\frac{(-1)^{j+1}}{2q^{2}\sin^{2}(\pi j/q)},\qquad j=1,\dots,q-1.

(6.9)

For odd
j
, we obtain similarly
(or directly from the last formula in (
3.3
))

\displaystyle\widetilde{h}_{q}^{\prime\prime}(0)=-\frac{q^{2}-1}{12q^{2}}

(6.10)

and

\displaystyle\widetilde{h}_{q}^{\prime\prime}(2\pi j)

\displaystyle=\frac{(-1)^{j+1}\cos(\pi j/q)}{2q^{2}\sin^{2}(\pi j/q)},\qquad j=1,\dots,q-1.

(6.11)

The results (
6.1
) and (
6.1
) now follow from (
6.7
),
using (
5.11
)–(
5.14
) and (
6.8
)–(
6.11
).
∎

7.
Examples

Example 7.1
.

Consider the simplest case
q=2
, i.e., assume that
X
a.s. is an integer
or half-integer.
Then
\langle X\rangle=\lceil X\rceil
.
For the mean, we obtain from Theorem
5.1

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}\lfloor X\rfloor

\displaystyle=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}X-\frac{1}{4}+\frac{\varphi_{X}(2\pi)}{4},

(7.1)

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}\lceil X\rceil=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}\langle X\rangle

\displaystyle=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}X+\frac{1}{4}-\frac{\varphi_{X}(2\pi)}{4}.

(7.2)

(This can also easily be seen directly.)
Similarly,
for the second moment, from Theorem
6.1
,

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}\bigl[{\lfloor X\rfloor}^{2}\bigr]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}[X^{2}]+\frac{1}{8}-\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}X-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}\varphi_{X}^{\prime}(2\pi)-\frac{1}{8}\varphi_{X}(2\pi),

(7.3)

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}\bigl[{\lceil X\rceil}^{2}\bigr]=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}\bigl[{\langle X\rangle}^{2}\bigr]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}[X^{2}]+\frac{1}{8}+\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}X+\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}\varphi_{X}^{\prime}(2\pi)-\frac{1}{8}\varphi_{X}(2\pi).

(7.4)

Note that (
7.1
)–(
7.2
) and (
7.3
)–(
7.4
)
agree with the relation

\lceil X\rceil=-\lfloor-X\rfloor
.

\triangle

Example 7.2
.

Let
q\geqslant 1
, and let
X:=U_{q}
. This eaxmple is trivial, since obviously

\lfloor U_{q}\rfloor=0
; nevertheless it is interesting to see how this is reflected
in the formulas above. Recall that by (
3.4
), we now have

\displaystyle\varphi_{X}(t)=\varphi_{U_{q}}(t)=h_{q}(-t)=\overline{h_{q}(t)}.

(7.5)

In particular, (
5.5
) shows that

\displaystyle\varphi_{U_{q}}(2\pi j)=0\quad\text{for }1\leqslant j\leqslant q-1.

(7.6)

Hence the sum in (
5.1
) vanishes, and (
5.1
) reduces to

\displaystyle 0=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}\lfloor U_{q}\rfloor=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}U_{q}-\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2q}=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}U_{q}-\frac{q-1}{2q},

(7.7)

or

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}U_{q}=\frac{q-1}{2q},

(7.8)

as is easily seen
(and was observed after (
5.7
)).

The variance formula is more interesting.
The last sum in (
6.1
) vanishes, again because of (
7.6
).
For the first sum we have, by (
5.8
) and (
7.5
),

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{q-1}\frac{\mathrm{i}}{q(1-e^{-2\pi\mathrm{i}j/q})}\varphi^{\prime}_{U_{q}}(2\pi j)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{q-1}h_{q}^{\prime}(2\pi j)\overline{h_{q}^{\prime}(2\pi j)}=\sum_{j=1}^{q-1}\bigl\lvert h_{q}^{\prime}(2\pi j)\bigr\rvert^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{q-1}\frac{1}{\lvert 2q\sin(\pi j/q)\rvert^{2}}=\frac{1}{4q^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{q-1}\frac{1}{\sin^{2}(\pi j/q)}.

(7.9)

Hence (
6.1
) yields, using also (
7.8
),

\displaystyle 0=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}\bigl[{\lfloor U_{q}\rfloor}^{2}\bigr]=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}[U_{q}^{2}]+\frac{2q^{2}-3q+1}{6q^{2}}-\frac{(q-1)^{2}}{2q^{2}}-\frac{1}{2q^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{q-1}\frac{1}{\sin^{2}(\pi j/q)}.

(7.10)

Furthermore, by (
7.5
) and (
6.5
),

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}[U_{q}^{2}]=-\varphi_{U_{q}}^{\prime\prime}(0)=-h_{q}^{\prime\prime}(0)=\frac{2q^{2}-3q+1}{6q^{2}},

(7.11)

which equals the second term on the right-hand side of (
7.10
).
(See the proof of Theorem
6.1
.)
Hence (
7.10
) is equivalent to

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{q-1}\frac{1}{\sin^{2}(\pi j/q)}=\frac{2}{3}\bigl(2q^{2}-3q+1\bigr)-(q-1)^{2}=\frac{q^{2}-1}{3}.

(7.12)

This non-obvious formula can also be shown more directly by
applying Parseval’s formula to the restriction of
h_{q}^{\prime}
to

\{2\pi j:0\leqslant j<q\}
, regarded as a function
on the cyclic group
\mathbb{Z}_{q}
; this function is given
by (
5.7
)–(
5.8
), and it follows from (
3.1
) that its inverse
Fourier transform (also a function on
\mathbb{Z}_{q}
) is given by

-\mathrm{i}\frac{k}{q}
,
0\leqslant k<q
; calculations similar to (and related to) the
ones above then yield (
7.12
).

We obtain the same result (
7.12
) by similar calculations if we instead
take
X:={\widetilde{U}}_{q}
and consider
\langle{\widetilde{U}}_{q}\rangle=0
in Theorem
6.1
.

\triangle

Example 7.3
.

As a more complicated example, we consider the following problem;
we thank Seungki Kim for asking it, which was the original motivation for
the present paper.

Let
q
be odd, let
n\geqslant 1
, and let
\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n}
be i.i.d. with each
\xi_{k}
having the distribution of
{\widetilde{U}}_{q}
in Section
3
,
i.e.,
\xi_{k}
is uniformly distributed on

\{-\frac{q-1}{2q},-\frac{q-3}{2q},\dots,\frac{q-3}{2q},\frac{q-1}{2q}\}
;
in other words each
q\xi_{k}
is uniformly distributed on the set

\{-\frac{q-1}{2},-\frac{q-3}{2},\dots,\frac{q-3}{2},\frac{q-1}{2}\}

of integers in
(-\frac{q}{2},\frac{q}{2})
.
Let
s_{1},\dots,s_{n}
be positive integers, and
consider

\displaystyle X:=s_{1}\xi_{1}+\dots+s_{n}\xi_{n}.

(7.13)

Since
q
is odd we have
\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}\xi_{i}=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}{\widetilde{U}}_{q}=0
and,
using (
3.4
) and (
6.10
),

\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}\xi_{i}=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}[\xi_{i}^{2}]=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}\bigl[{\widetilde{U}}_{q}^{2}\bigr]=-\widetilde{h}_{q}^{\prime\prime}(0)=\frac{q^{2}-1}{12q^{2}}.

(7.14)

Consequently,

\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}X=\sum_{i=1}^{n}s_{i}^{2}\operatorname{Var}\xi_{i}=\frac{q^{2}-1}{12q^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}s_{i}^{2}.

(7.15)

Consider now the rounded variable
\langle X\rangle
, and in particular its
variance.
A natural approximation to
\operatorname{Var}[\langle X\rangle]
is
\operatorname{Var}X+\frac{1}{12}
.
(For any
X
. In statistics this is known as Sheppard’s correction
(for the variance) when dealing with grouped data,
see e.g.
[
1
, Section 27.9]
.
See also
[
2
]
for continuous
X
.)
How good is this approximation for the variable
X
in (
7.13
)?

We will in (
7.37
) below
give an upper bound of the error that is valid for all
n\geqslant 2
and

s_{1},\dots,s_{n}
;
it is presumably not sharp but it seems to be rather
good when the
s_{i}
are much smaller
than
q
. We leave it to the readers to find better bounds,
in particular for cases with larger
s_{i}
.

Each
\xi_{k}
has the characteristic function

\displaystyle\varphi_{\xi}(t)=\frac{1}{q}\sum_{j=-(q-1)/2}^{(q-1)/2}e^{\mathrm{i}jt/q}=\widetilde{h}_{q}(t)=\frac{\sin\frac{t}{2}}{q\sin\frac{t}{2q}},

(7.16)

see (
3.3
) and (
3.4
).
Consequently,
X
has the characteristic function

\displaystyle\varphi_{X}(t)=\prod_{k=1}^{n}\varphi_{\xi}(s_{k}t)=\prod_{k=1}^{n}\widetilde{h}_{q}(s_{k}t).

(7.17)

It follows from (
5.9
) and the fact that
\widetilde{h}_{q}
has period
2\pi q

that for every
j\in\mathbb{Z}
, we have

\varphi_{X}(2\pi j)\in\{0,1\}
, and, letting
\gcd(\dots)
denote the greatest
common divisor,

\displaystyle\varphi_{X}(2\pi j)\neq 0

\displaystyle\iff\varphi_{X}(2\pi j)=1\iff\varphi_{\xi}(2\pi js_{k})=1\;\forall k\leqslant n\iff q\mid js_{k}\;\forall k\leqslant n

\displaystyle\iff q\mid j\gcd(s_{1},\dots,s_{n},q).

(7.18)

Let

\displaystyle d

\displaystyle:=\gcd(s_{1},\dots,s_{n},q),

(7.19)

\displaystyle J

\displaystyle:=q/d.

(7.20)

Note that
d
and
J
are divisors of
q
, and thus odd positive integers with

d,J\leqslant q
. Furthermore, (
7.3
) can be written

\displaystyle\varphi_{X}(2\pi j)=\boldsymbol{1}\{q\mid jd\}=\boldsymbol{1}\{J\mid j\}.

(7.21)

Each
\xi_{k}
has a distribution that is symmetric:

-\xi_{k}\overset{\mathrm{d}}{=}\xi_{k}
. Thus the same holds for
X
, and thus also for
\langle X\rangle

(since
q
is odd, cf. Remark
2.1
).
In particular,

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}\langle X\rangle=0.

(7.22)

For the second moment, we use (
6.1
).
Consider first the last sum in (
6.1
).
By (
7.21
), we may sum over
j=\ell J
,
\ell=1,\dots,d-1
, only,
and thus the absolute value of the sum is

\displaystyle\left\lvert\sum_{j=1}^{q-1}\frac{(-1)^{j}\cos(\pi j/q)}{2q^{2}\sin^{2}(\pi j/q)}\varphi_{X}(2\pi j)\right\rvert\leqslant\sum_{\ell=1}^{d-1}\frac{1}{2q^{2}\sin^{2}(\pi\ell J/q)}=\frac{1}{2q^{2}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{d-1}\frac{1}{\sin^{2}(\pi\ell/d)}.

(7.23)

The final sum is
(d^{2}-1)/3
by (
7.12
).
Hence, (
7.23
) implies

\displaystyle\left\lvert\sum_{j=1}^{q-1}\frac{(-1)^{j}\cos(\pi j/q)}{2q^{2}\sin^{2}(\pi j/q)}\varphi_{X}(2\pi j)\right\rvert<\frac{d^{2}}{6q^{2}}\leqslant\frac{(\min_{k}s_{k})^{2}}{6q^{2}}.

(7.24)

For the first sum in (
6.1
), we first
define

\displaystyle X_{k}:=\sum_{i\neq k}s_{i}\xi_{i},

(7.25)

the sum (
7.13
) with the term
s_{k}\xi_{k}
omitted.
Then we
differentiate (
7.17
)
and obtain

\displaystyle\varphi_{X}^{\prime}(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}s_{k}\varphi^{\prime}_{\xi}(s_{k}t)\prod_{i\neq k}\varphi_{\xi}(s_{i}t)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}s_{k}\varphi^{\prime}_{\xi}(s_{k}t)\varphi_{X_{k}}(t).

(7.26)

Let

\displaystyle d_{k}

\displaystyle:=\gcd(\{s_{i}:i\neq k\}\cup\{q\}),

(7.27)

\displaystyle J_{k}

\displaystyle:=q/d_{k}.

(7.28)

Then (
7.21
) applied to
X_{k}
yields, for all
j\in\mathbb{Z}
,

\displaystyle\varphi_{X_{k}}(2\pi j)=\boldsymbol{1}\{q\mid jd_{k}\}=\boldsymbol{1}\{J_{k}\mid j\}.

(7.29)

Consequently,
for the first sum in (
6.1
),
using (
7.26
), (
7.16
), and (
5.13
)–(
5.14
),
and taking
j=\ell J_{k}

similarly to the argument in (
7.23
),

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{q-1}\frac{(-1)^{j}}{q\sin(\pi j/q)}\varphi^{\prime}_{X}(2\pi j)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{q-1}\frac{(-1)^{j}}{q\sin(\pi j/q)}\sum_{k=1}^{n}s_{k}\varphi^{\prime}_{\xi}(2\pi s_{k}j)\varphi_{X_{k}}(2\pi j)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}s_{k}\sum_{j=1}^{q-1}\frac{(-1)^{j}}{q\sin(\pi j/q)}\cdot\frac{(-1)^{s_{k}j}\boldsymbol{1}\{q\nmid s_{k}j\}}{2q\sin(\pi s_{k}j/q)}\boldsymbol{1}\{J_{k}\mid j\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{s_{k}}{2q^{2}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{d_{k}-1}\frac{(-1)^{(s_{k}+1)\ell}\boldsymbol{1}\{d_{k}\nmid s_{k}\ell\}}{\sin(\pi\ell/d_{k})\sin(\pi s_{k}\ell/d_{k})}.

(7.30)

Denote the inner sum on the last line by
S_{k}
.
Then the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality yields

\displaystyle S_{k}^{2}\leqslant\sum_{\ell=1}^{d_{k}-1}\frac{1}{\sin^{2}(\pi\ell/d_{k})}\cdot\sum_{\ell=1}^{d_{k}-1}\frac{\boldsymbol{1}\{d_{k}\nmid s_{k}\ell\}}{\sin^{2}(\pi s_{k}\ell/d_{k})}.

(7.31)

The first sum is
<d_{k}^{2}/3
by (
7.12
). For the second sum,
we note that by (
7.19
) and (
7.27
),

\displaystyle d=\gcd(d_{k},s_{k})

(7.32)

and thus

\displaystyle d_{k}\mid s_{k}\ell\iff d_{k}\mid\gcd(s_{k}\ell,d_{k}\ell)=d\ell\iff(d_{k}/d)\mid\ell.

(7.33)

Let
r_{k}:=d_{k}/d
(an odd integer by (
7.32
) and (
7.27
))
and
s_{k}^{\prime}:=s_{k}/d
(also an integer by (
7.32
)).
It follows that
\gcd(s_{k}^{\prime},r_{k})=1
; thus
s_{k}^{\prime}\ell
runs through the
equivalence classes modulo
{r_{k}}
once each as
\ell=1,\dots,r_{k}
,
and
d_{k}/r_{k}=d
times each as
\ell=1,\dots,d_{k}
.
Consequently, since
s_{k}\ell/d_{k}=s^{\prime}_{k}\ell/r_{k}
, using (
7.12
) again,

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=1}^{d_{k}-1}\frac{\boldsymbol{1}\{d_{k}\nmid s_{k}\ell\}}{\sin^{2}(\pi s_{k}\ell/d_{k})}=d\sum_{j=1}^{r_{k}-1}\frac{1}{\sin^{2}(\pi j/r_{k})}<\frac{dr_{k}^{2}}{3}=\frac{d_{k}^{2}}{3d}\leqslant\frac{d_{k}^{2}}{3}.

(7.34)

Hence, (
7.31
) yields
S_{k}^{2}\leqslant(d_{k}^{2}/3)^{2}
and thus
|S_{k}|\leqslant d_{k}^{2}/3
,
and (
7.3
) yields

\displaystyle\left\lvert\sum_{j=1}^{q-1}\frac{(-1)^{j}}{q\sin(\pi j/q)}\varphi^{\prime}_{X}(2\pi j)\right\rvert\leqslant\frac{1}{6q^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}s_{k}d_{k}^{2}.

(7.35)

Assume now
n\geqslant 2
. Then
d_{k}\leqslant s_{k+1}
(with the index taken modulo
n
),
and thus Hölder’s inequality (with exponents
3
and
3/2
) shows

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}s_{k}d_{k}^{2}\leqslant\Bigl(\sum_{k=1}^{n}s_{k}^{3}\Bigr)^{1/3}\Bigl(\sum_{k=1}^{n}d_{k}^{3}\Bigr)^{2/3}\leqslant\sum_{k=1}^{n}s_{k}^{3}.

(7.36)

Consequently,
(
6.1
) yields, using (
7.35
)–(
7.36
) and (
7.24
),

\displaystyle\left\lvert\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}\bigl[{\langle X\rangle}^{2}\bigr]-\bigl(\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}[X^{2}]+\tfrac{1}{12}\bigr)\right\rvert\leqslant\frac{1+\sum_{k=1}^{n}s_{k}^{3}+(\min_{k}s_{k})^{2}}{6q^{2}}\leqslant\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{n}s_{k}^{3}}{3q^{2}}.

(7.37)

\triangle
