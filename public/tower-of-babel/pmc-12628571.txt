Prognostic modeling and immune infiltration analysis in hepatocellular carcinoma using glutathione metabolism-associated genes
Authors: Fuqiang Ma, LiLi, Ziyi Xu, Yingda Xie, Yanpin Ma, Penghui Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGlutathione (GSH) plays a central role in multiple physiological processes, including the maintenance of intracellular redox homeostasis and the detoxification of xenobiotics. While previous studies have linked GSH metabolism to the pathogenesis and treatment of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), systematic analysis of the expression profiles of GSH metabolism-related genes in HCC and their consistent correlation with patient prognosis remains insufficiently addressed.MethodsIn this study, the ribonucleic acid sequencing (RNA-seq) data and clinical information data of HCC were downloaded from public databases. Following Cox regression and consensus clustering analyses, three GSH metabolism-related subtypes with differential survival probability and immune infiltration status were identified in patients with HCC. Next, CDCA8, KIF20A, TRNP1, and ADH4 were chosen to establish a prognostic model.ResultsCompared with those in the low-risk group, patients with high-risk scores exhibited poor survival probability, higher immune scores, decreased benefit from immunotherapy and poor drug sensitivity.ConclusionsIn summary, this study established a prognostic risk model for HCC based on GSH metabolism-related genes, which could predict the prognosis and characterize immune infiltration status of HCC. This study may contribute to the identification of potential prognostic targets and the development of new clinical management strategies for HCC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40001-025-03403-8.Keywords: Hepatocellular carcinoma, Glutathione metabolism, Prognostic model, Immune infiltration

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), which is the most common malignant tumor, is a major worldwide disease burden with a poor prognosis [1]. Globally, the risk factors of HCC vary according to the geographical location. The primary risk factors for HCC in sub-Saharan Africa and east Asia are chronic HBV infection and aflatoxin B1 exposure, while those in the United States and Europe are HCV infection and excessive alcohol consumption [2]. Otherwise, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease is an etiological factor for HCC in developed countries. Recent advances in interventional therapy, targeted drugs, and immunotherapy for HCC have improved clinical prognosis for patients, yet HCC remains the most prevalent cancer with the lowest survival rate worldwide [3–5]. Liver cancer is expected to contribute to 1.3 million deaths in 2040 [6]. Consequently, there is an urgent demand to establish a novel prognostic model to assess the risk of HCC.

GSH, a tripeptide formed by glutamate, cysteine, and glycine, functions as a major intracellular antioxidant and regulates cell signaling [7–10]. The two major forms of GSH, which is synthesized mainly in the liver, are reduced GSH and glutathione disulfide(GSSG) [11, 12]. Glutathione reductase (GR) can catalyze the interconversion of the two types of GSH. The coenzyme of GR is NADPH, which is generated in the pentose phosphate pathway. Under physiological conditions, reduced GSH accounts for most GSH and defends cells against oxidative stress induced by endogenous reactive oxygen species (ROS), which promotes cell damage and disrupts homeostasis [13–16]. In addition, GSH and its related enzymes take an essential part in the detoxification of drugs and intracellular toxic metabolites by binding to multiple endogenous electrophiles and xenobiotics. Previous researchers have found that alterations in GSH content contribute to hepatocyte apoptosis and necrosis, indicating the importance of GSH in the pathogenesis of hepatic disease [17, 18].

The GSH metabolism pathway modulates the carcinogenesis of HCC through different mechanisms. Compared with those in healthy livers, the levels of GSH, selenium-dependent GSH peroxidase, and GR are downregulated in HCC tissues [19]. A recent study reported that sirtuin-1 deficiency upregulates GSH metabolism, shaping a reductive environment in the liver and consequently preventing the occurrence of HCC in mice [20]. SLC27A5 is reported to heighten sorafenib-induced ferroptosis in HCC cells through downregulating the expression of GR [21]. This indicates that the combination regimen of sorafenib and GR inhibitors can be a potential treatment strategy for HCC drug resistance. A cascaded copper-based nanocatalyst exerts therapeutic effects on HCC by regulating the levels of GSH and cyclooxygenase-2 [22]. Clinically, GSH and antioxidants are recommended for the prevention of HCC in patients with chronic hepatic disease [23, 24]. However, the function of the GSH metabolic pathway in the development of liver cancer has not been elucidated.

Although GSH metabolism is closely associated with liver diseases, the expression profiles and molecular subtypes of GSH metabolism-related genes in HCC, as well as their relationships with prognosis and the immune microenvironment, still lack systematic investigation. Therefore, this study aims to elucidate the expression characteristics of these genes and their significance in HCC through comprehensive analysis. This study downloaded the RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) and clinical information data of HCC from public databases to comprehensively analyze the profiles of GSH metabolism-related genes in HCC [25]. A prognostic model on the basis of GSH metabolism-related genes was constructed, and its prognostic performance was verified. In addition, the immune infiltration status in different risk subgroups was characterized. Furthermore, the differential clinicopathological features, drug sensitivity, and immunotherapy benefits between different risk groups were evaluated. The findings of this study can improve the management strategies for patients with HCC.

Results

Molecular subtypes based on GSH metabolism-related genes in HCC

Univariate Cox analysis of 50 GSH metabolism-related genes in TCGA–LIHC data set revealed that 18 genes were significantly relevant to the prognosis of HCC. Of these, GSTZ1 was a protective factor, whereas the remaining 17 genes were risk factors (Fig. 1A). Next, the expression extents of the 18 genes in HCC and pericancerous tissues were examined. GSTZ1 was downregulated, whereas the other 17 genes, including G6PD, GGCT, and GGT7, were upregulated in HCC tissues (Fig. 1B).

Based on the 18 prognostic GSH metabolism-related genes, samples in TCGA–LIHC cohort were clustered using consensus clustering. The cumulative distribution function (CDF) curve and its delta area showed that the clustering results were steady when the cluster number was selected as 3 (Fig. 1C, D). Therefore, k = 3 was selected to obtain the following three molecular subtypes: S1, S2, and S3 (Fig. 1E). Similar results were obtained with the HCCDB18 data set (Fig. S1A–C). Principal component analysis (PCA) plot revealed good separation between the samples of the three subgroups in TCGA–LIHC data set, which was also verified in an independent data set HCCDB18 (Fig. 1F). Kaplan–Meier analysis suggested that the prognoses of the three subtypes were markedly different in patients with HCC in TCGA–LIHC and HCCDB18 data sets (Fig. 1G). The S1 subtype exhibited the worst prognosis, whereas the S2 subtype exhibited the best prognosis. In addition, the heatmap revealed the expression extents of 18 GSH metabolism-related genes in TCGA–LIHC data set between the three subtypes (Fig. 2A). Most genes were upregulated in the S1 subtype.

Based on the previously reported immune cell gene set, the immune infiltration status in different subtypes was examined using the single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) algorithm [26, 27]. The results found that the immune score of the S1 subtype was higher, and the same was true for innate immunity and acquired immunity score (Fig. 2B–D). Similar results were obtained with the HCCDB18 data set (Fig. S1D, E).

Identification of prognosis-related differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in TCGA–LIHC cohort

The DEGs between the following subtype pairs were analyzed using the limma package: S1 and S2; S1 and S3; S2 and S3 (Fig. 3A–C). The number of intersecting DEGs among the three groups was 39 (Fig. 3D). Univariate Cox regression analysis of 39 DEGs suggested that 30 genes were significantly associated with prognosis (25 risk genes and 5 protective genes) (Fig. 3E) (Additional file 3). Next, least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) Cox regression analysis was conducted with the 30 DEGs. When the lambda value was 0.0259, four genes (CDCA8, KIF20A, TRNP1, and ADH4) were obtained. These four genes were used as target genes for downstream analysis (Fig. 3F, G). The LASSO Cox coefficients of CDCA8, KIF20A, TRNP1, and ADH4 were calculated (Fig. 3H). Furthermore, we examined the mRNA expression level of the four genes in cell line HepG2 and LO2 by quantitative reverse transcription–polymerase chain reaction (qRT–PCR). The results found that, compared to LO2, ADH4 and TRNP1 were highly expressed in HepG2, while CDCA8 and KIF20A were lowly expressed in HepG2 (Fig. 3I).

Construction of a prognostic model based on GSH metabolism-related genes

A four-gene prognostic model was constructed and the risk score of each patient was calculated as follows: risk score = (0.228 × CDCA8 expression level) + (0.101 × KIF20A expression level) + (0.09 × TRNP1 expression level) − (0.051 × ADH4 expression level). Next, the patients were divided into high-risk and low-risk groups according to the optimal cutoff value of risk score. Kaplan–Meier analysis revealed that the survival probability of patients in the high-risk group was significantly lower than that in the low-risk group. To further assess the predictive performance of the model, ROC curves were generated to evaluate the accuracy of the risk score in predicting survival at different timepoints (Fig. 4A). The robustness of the novel prognostic model was validated using HCCDB18 (Fig. 4B) and GSE76427 (Fig. 4C) data sets, and the results indicated that the model exhibited good performance.

F, G Evaluation of the F risk score and G GLMS according to different clinicopathological characteristics in TCGA–LIHC cohort. *P < 0.05; **P < 0.01; ***P < 0.001, ****P < 0.0001

Correlation between risk score and different clinicopathological features

Furthermore, the risk scores among the three subtypes in TCGA–LIHC data set were comparatively analyzed. The risk score was high for the S1 subtype and low for the S2 subtype (Fig. 4D). In addition, each sample in TCGA–LIHC data set was assigned a “GSH metabolism” gene set enrichment score (GLMS) using ssGSEA. Comparative analysis of the GLMS revealed that the GLMS of the low-risk group was markedly higher than that of the high-risk group (Fig. 4E).

Next, the distribution of risk scores according to distinct clinicopathological features was examined. The risk scores varied according to tumor-node-metastasis (TNM) stage, tumor stage, and pathology grade in TCGA–LIHC cohort. The risk score was significantly and positively associated with advanced T stage, tumor stage, and pathology grade (Fig. 4F). The GLMS significantly varied according to T stage, TNM stage, and pathology grade (Fig. 4G).

Potential regulatory mechanisms and immune characteristics under different risk states

According to the pathways in “h.all.v7.4.symbols.gmt,” the two risk groups were compared using ssGSEA, which revealed significant differences in 41 pathways. The heatmap (Fig. 5A) revealed some hallmarks, such as “adipogenesis, oxidative phosphorylation, and bile acid metabolism” were significantly associated with the high-risk group, while hallmarks, such as “G2/M checkpoint, DNA repair, glycolysis, and PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling” were significantly correlated with the low-risk group.

C Comparison of innate immunity and acquired immunity scores. D Comparison of the activity of 29 immune-related pathways. E Analysis of the correlation between risk score and human gene signature score. F TIDE analysis according to different risk status. ssGSEA was performed to evaluate the immune characteristics under different risk states. In TCGA–LIHC data set, the immune, innate immunity, and acquired immunity scores were positively related to the risk grade (Fig. 5B–D). The scores of activated CD4+ T cells, activated dendritic cells, and type 2 T helper cells were significantly high in the high-risk group (P < 0.00005). The correlation between the gene signatures of 13 human-related pathways and risk score was analyzed using ssGSEA [28]. The risk score was positively associated with cell cycle, DNA damage response, DNA replication, mismatch repair, and homologous recombination (Fig. 5E).

Prediction of benefit from cancer immunotherapy

Next, tumor immune dysfunction and exclusion (TIDE) analysis of different risk states were performed. The TIDE, exclusion, and myeloid-derived suppressor cell scores were significantly upregulated in the high-risk group (Fig. 5F). The decreased TIDE score in the low-risk group suggested that the patients in this group can benefit from immunotherapy. Furthermore, the sensitivity of patients with HCC with different risk status to 19 traditional chemotherapy drugs was evaluated. The sensitivity of the high-risk group to 19 drugs, such as vinblastine, methotrexate, and docetaxel was lower than that of the low-risk group (Fig. 5G). The risk scores of patients receiving immunotherapy in GSE135222 (Fig. 6A), GSE78220 (Fig. 6B), and GSE91061 (Fig. 6C) data sets were calculated, and the Kaplan–Meier curve was drawn. The survival probability of patients in the low-risk group receiving immunotherapy was markedly higher than that of patients in the high-risk group. The incidence of progressive disease/stable disease (PD/SD) in the high-risk group was higher than that in the low-risk group, suggesting that the high-risk group received decreased benefits from immunotherapy. This was consistent with the results of TIDE analysis.

Analysis by combining clinicopathological features to further improve the prognostic model

The age, gender, T stage, N stage, M stage, stage, pathological grade, and risk grouping of patients with HCC were included to construct a decision tree (Fig. 7A). The group, T stage, and gender were retained in the decision tree, and five distinct risk clusters were determined. Group and T stage were powerful parameters. The survival probability markedly varied between the five risk clusters (Fig. 7B). The risk clusters C3, C4, and C5 comprised high-risk patients, while the C1 and C2 groups comprised low-risk patients (Fig. 7C). The survival status of patients markedly varied between different clusters (Fig. 7D). Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses suggested that the risk score was the most crucial prognostic factor (Fig. 7E, F).

C, D Comparative analysis of the C risk states and D survival outcomes in different risk clusters. E Univariate and F multivariate Cox regression analyses. G Nomogram model. Furthermore, a nomogram was established to quantify the risk evaluation and survival probability (Fig. 7G). The risk score was the most important component in predicting the survival rate. The calibration curve was adopted to assess the prediction performance of the model. The nomogram exhibited a good prediction effectiveness (Fig. 7H). DCA revealed that the benefits of the risk score method and the nomogram method were better than those of the extreme curve, indicating good reliability of the model (Fig. 7I).

Verification of the prognostic gene expression at the single-cell level

To verify the robustness of the prognostic model, data of 25 HCC samples were obtained from the GSE151530 data set and subjected to single-cell analysis. Based on the gene expression levels of canonical cell makers, the following six cell subpopulations were obtained: B cells, endothelial cells, fibroblasts, hepatocytes, myeloid cells, and T cells (Fig. 8A) [29–31]. The proportion of cell subpopulations in each sample is shown in Fig. 8B. Among the overall cell proportions calculated by integrating data from all samples, T cells account for the highest proportion, followed by hepatocytes. Comparative analysis of GLMSs in different cells revealed that the GLMS of hepatocytes was the highest and that the GLMSs significantly varied between different cell classifications (Fig. 8C). The expression levels of CDCA8, KIF20A, TRNP1, and ADH4 in different cell types are shown in Fig. 8D. The CDCA8, KIF20A, TRNP1, and ADH4 levels were upregulated in hepatocytes, indicating that they may have an important function in the progression of liver cancer. Further analysis of these four genes can improve our understanding of the pathogenesis of HCC.

Discussion

HCC is a global health challenge with high mortality rates. Several candidate biomarkers have been identified for predicting the prognosis of HCC. However, the heterogeneity and complexity of HCC have limited its effective clinical management [32, 33]. Therefore, novel prognostic models must be developed for HCC to guide clinical treatment. GSH is the most abundant antioxidant in cells and regulates the signaling cascade involved in intracellular redox homeostasis maintenance, cellular oxidative damage response, detoxication of xenobiotics, and immune response [34–38]. The liver, which is one of the organs with the highest GSH content, is involved in glutathione synthesis. The dysregulation of GSH homeostasis in the liver is reported to mediate the pathogenesis of various liver diseases due to the generation of ROS, altering the redox status of hepatocytes and affecting the signal transduction pathways in hepatocytes [36–38]. In this study, the relationship between the expression of GSH metabolism-related genes and the prognosis of HCC was examined. Furthermore, a prognostic model based on GSH metabolism-related genes was established for HCC. The prognostic model could effectively determine the immune infiltration status, immunotherapy response, and survival outcome of patients with HCC. The findings of this study can provide potential strategies for prognostic assessment and clinical management of HCC.

This study found that the expression of GSTZ1 was down-regulated in human HCC tissues, suggesting that GSTZ1 acts as a tumor suppressor in HCC. Previous study reported that GSTZ1 deficiency promotes the proliferation of HCC by downregulating GSH, upregulating ROS levels, enhancing lipid peroxidation, and activating the NRF2 signaling pathway [39]. Another study revealed that GSTZ1-1 deficiency promoted HCC progression by activating the NRF2/IGF1R axis through the accumulation of succinylacetone [40]. Wang and his colleagues proved that GSTZ1 overexpression enhanced the sensitivity of HCC cells to sorafenib-induced ferroptosis via suppressing the NRF2/GPX4 axis [41]. These findings indicated that GSTZ1 is a promising therapeutic target for HCC.

In the present study, the following four GSH metabolism-related genes were used to establish a prognostic model for HCC: CDCA8, KIF20A, TRNP1, and ADH4. CDCA8, an important component of the chromosome passenger complex, is involved in cell proliferation. Cui et al. reported that CDCA8 overexpression may cause poor survival of patients with HCC [42]. In addition, CDCA8 knockdown inhibited the proliferation of HCC cells. Jeon et al. revealed that silencing CDCA8 restores the ATF3/GADD34 tumor suppressor pathway and inactivates the AKT/β-Catenin signal, exerting growth-inhibitory effects and inhibiting HCC stemness [43]. KIF20A, a member of the kinesin superfamily, has a critical role in tumor progression [44, 45]. Previous research reported that KIF20A silencing inhibited cell proliferation, facilitated the chemosensitivity of HCC cells to cisplatin and sorafenib, and was related to better prognosis in HCC [46]. The dynamic modulation of TRNP1 is essential for the regulation of mammalian cerebral cortex expansion and self-renewal of neural stem cells [47]. Several studies have found that TRNP1 is associated with the prognosis of HCC [48–50]. The mechanism of TRNP1 in the development of HCC has not been elucidated. ADH4, a major member of the alcohol dehydrogenase family, metabolizes multiple substrates and is involved in tumorigenesis [51, 52]. Wei et al. found that the mRNA and protein expression levels of ADH4 were markedly down-regulated in HCC tissues [53]. The downregulated ADH4 expression was related to poor overall survival. In addition, Zhang et al. reported increased levels of B-cell infiltration in the low-ADH4 expression group, suggesting that ADH4 may be related to the immune microenvironment of HCC [54]. In this study, CDCA8, KIF20A, and TRNP1 were identified as risk factors for HCC prognosis, whereas ADH4 was considered a protective factor. The new prognostic model based on CDCA8, KIF20A, TRNP1, and ADH4 could predict the prognosis of patients with HCC and had clinical guiding value. Furthermore, the findings of this study indicated that CDCA8, KIF20A, TRNP1, and ADH4 are potential therapeutic targets for HCC. Further studies are needed to elucidate the molecular mechanisms of these genes in HCC progression.

The tumor microenvironment (TME) is closely relevant to the formation and metastasis of tumor tissues [55, 56]. The heterogeneous immune cell subsets of malignant hepatocytes have multiple functions in formation the immune microenvironment of HCC. Lu et al. described a single-cell atlas of HCC and identified seven subtypes based on the immune microenvironment, which can predict the survival of patients with HCC [57]. Wang et al. explored the intratumoral heterogeneity of HCC using spatial transcriptome technology and revealed the spatial expression pattern of the immune microenvironment [58]. The authors reported that CCL15 is significantly expressed in the core zone of HCC and promotes an immunosuppressive TME by recruiting and polarizing M2 phenotype macrophages. CCL19 and CCL21, which are concentrated in the enrichment area of immune infiltration, can suppress HCC growth through recruiting CD3+ T cells and CD20+ B cells. In this study, patients in the high-risk group with poor prognosis exhibited a high immune score and significantly increased proportions of activated CD4+ T cells and activated dendritic cells. TIDE score analysis indicated that the patients in the low-risk group could benefit from immunotherapy. Recently, immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have revolutionized the palliative therapy of HCC [59, 60]. The tumor and genomic characteristics of inflamed and noninflamed HCC are related to the response to ICIs [61, 62]. Therefore, the TME of HCC must be characterized to develop optimal immunotherapy regimens for patients with HCC.

This study aimed to classify patients with HCC into different subtypes on the basis of GSH metabolism-related genes, establish a prognostic prediction model, and link GSH metabolism-related genes to the risk classification of patients with HCC. Multi-level and multi-database verification of the prognostic model suggested good performance of the model. However, this study had several limitations. The RNA-seq and clinical data in this study were downloaded from public databases. The efficiency of the prognostic model was not verified in the local HCC cohort. The consensus clustering and LASSO model results could be influenced by analytical parameters, and research was limited to a small number of cell lines. In addition, functional in vivo and in vitro experiments were not performed to investigate the molecular mechanism of these genes in the progression of HCC. The risk model optimization and the elucidation of the functional mechanisms of genes will be the focus of our future studies. We plan to further validate and improve the model using additional clinical data, and explore the biological roles of these genes in HCC through relevant experiments as research progresses.

Conclusions

The novel risk model constructed based on GSH metabolism-related genes served as a prognostic predictor for HCC. In addition, the model was related to immune infiltration status, immunotherapy benefit, and drug sensitivity. A systematic study of GSH metabolism-related genes will contribute to understanding their function and prognostic value in HCC.

Materials and methods

Cell culture and qRT-PCR

The human hepatocyte line LO2 and the HCC cell line HepG2 were obtained from the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). The cells were cultured at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator and maintained in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Biological Industries, Israel). Total RNA was extracted from cells using a RNeasy mini kit (Qiagen, USA). The mRNA was reverse-transcribed into complementary DNA using PrimeScript™ RT master mix (Takara, Japan). qRT-PCR analysis was performed using an ABI 7500 Fast PCR instrument. GAPDH was used as an internal reference. The relative mRNA expression was quantified using the 2−ΔΔCt method. The primers used in this analysis are listed in Additional file 1.

Data collection

The mRNA expression profiles of HCC samples and adjacent non-neoplastic tissues, as well as corresponding clinical data, were obtained from The Cancer Genome Atlas–Liver Hepatocellular Carcinoma (TCGA–LIHC), Hepatocellular Carcinoma Database (HCCDB18), and Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) data sets (GSE76427, GSE91061, GSE78220, GSE135222, and GSE151530) [63, 64]. Patients with limited follow-up information and mRNA expression data were not included in further analysis. Besides, the GSH metabolism-related genes participating in the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway “glutathione metabolism” were obtained from the Molecular Signatures Database [65]. The details of GSH metabolism-related genes are listed in Additional file 2.

Screening prognostic GSH metabolism-related genes and constructing molecular subtypes

GSH metabolism-related genes associated with HCC prognosis were determined by univariate Cox regression analysis. Next, ConsensusClusterPlus package was adopted to carry out consensus clustering analysis of 18 genes that were significantly correlated with prognosis. In this clustering process, the partitioning around medoids (PAM) was used as the clustering algorithm, and Euclidean distance was applied as the distance metric. The CDF was used to identify disparate molecular subtypes. Moreover, PCA was conducted to observe the separation among different subtypes.

Establishing a prognostic model based on GSH metabolism-related genes

In TCGA–LIHC cohort, DEGs among three subtypes were screened using the “limma” package. The criterion for identifying DEGs were as follows: |log2 fold-change|≥ 1 and false discovery rate < 0.05.

To further narrow down the number of candidate prognostic DEGs, LASSO Cox regression analysis was performed by “glmnet” package [66, 67]. Next, the penalty parameter of the prognostic model, lambda, was determined through tenfold cross-validation. Moreover, the LASSO Cox coefficient of each prognostic gene was analyzed.

The optimum cutoff value was determined using the “surv cutpoint” function. Survival probability was analyzed using the Kaplan–Meier method. In addition, the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was plotted through the “timeROC” package to assess the efficiency of the prognostic model.

Immune characteristics analysis

ssGSEA was carried out through the R package “GSVA” to obtain the GLMS, immune score, and pathway score. In addition, the overall immune infiltration status of TCGA–LIHC cohort was assessed using the “Estimation of STromal and Immune cells in MAlignant Tumor tissues using Expression data” (ESTIMATE) algorithm [68].

Drug sensitivity analysis and immunotherapy benefit evaluation

The sensitivity of patients with HCC to traditional chemotherapy drugs was assessed using the R package “pRRophetic.” TIDE analysis was performed to assess the potential clinical effects of immunotherapy on patients with HCC [69]. Progressive disease (PD) indicates that the sum of lesion diameters exceeds 20% of the reference value or new lesions appear. Stable disease (SD) indicates that changes in lesion volume and number are between partial response and PD.

Construction and assessment of decision tree and nomogram

A decision tree model and a nomogram were created by combining the risk score and clinicopathological features of patients with HCC. The calibration curve was plotted to evaluate the predictive power of the improved prognostic model. The reliability of the predictive model was measured using decision curve analysis (DCA) [70].

Single-cell sequencing data

The Seurat package was used to perform single-cell analysis of 25 HCC samples based on the single-cell transcriptomic data set GSE151530. The data of each sample were standardized using logarithmic normalization. Highly variable features were screened using the FindVariableFeatures function and scaled using the ScaleData function. The FindNeighbors and FindClusters functions were used to identify cell subsets with dim = 10. The uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) plot was adopted to display the cell subpopulations.

Statistical analysis

Data pre-processing and statistical analysis were performed using R (V4.2) and GraphPad Prism (V9.2). The significance of continuous variables was tested through Wilcoxon test, while that of categorical variables was tested via chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Differences were considered significant at P < 0.05.
