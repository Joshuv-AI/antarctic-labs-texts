Environmental factors and chronic kidney disease: a case-control study
Authors: Mousa Ghelichi-Ghojogh, Mohammad fararouei, Mozhgan Seif, Maryam Pakfetrat
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractChronic kidney disease (CKD) is a non-communicable disease that includes a range of different physiological disorders causing abnormal renal function and progressive decline in the glomerular filtration rate (GFR). This study aimed to investigate the associations of several Environmental factors with CKD in the Iranian population. This is the second phase of a hospital-based case-control study, which was conducted on 700 participants (350 CKD cases and 350 controls, age and gender frequency matched). Multiple logistic regression was applied to measure the associations between the selected factors and CKD. Odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. All p-values were two-sided and the results were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05. The results suggested that environmental factors including proximity of residence to mines (OR yes/no=3.98, 95%CI: 2.20–7.21, p < 0.001), proximity of residence to mobile antenna (OR yes/no=2.20, 95%CI: 1.24–3.89, p = 0.006), and exposure to chemicals (OR chemical/no=4.40, 95%CI: 2.27–8.53, p < 0.001), were significantly associated with a higher risk of CKD. The present study covered a large number of factors in association with CKD and highlighted the importance of some environmental factors in development of CKD.‌ One of the main causes of heat in the work environment being a risk factor is dehydration caused by high heat. In order to reduce damage to the kidneys in jobs that deal with high heat, the following tips are recommended: drinking fluids, reducing working hours and shifts, proper ventilation in the workplace, using suitable clothes and heat protection.Keywords: Chronic kidney disease, Environmental factors, Case-controlSubject terms: Nephrology, Chronic kidney disease

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is a condition that disturbs the normal function of the kidneys. With gradual decline in glomerular filtration rate (GFR)1,2, CKD has five levels of kidney damage, from a small alteration to their function to a total failure3. People with advanced CKD are more likely to end up with kidney failure or even die before the final stage4,5. Reports suggest that there are 697. 5 million people with CKD around the world (about 9. 1% of the global population)6,7. Moreover, from 1990 to 2017 the global mortality rate of CKD increased by 41.5%8 and although in 2017 CKD was found to be the 12th most common cause of death in the world, the condition is expected to become the fifth most common cause of death by 20409. A study reported that about 11. 86% of people in Europe are suffering from CKD10. Regarding Iran, about 11.68% of Iranian adults are estimated to have CKD11 and about 2.9% of women and 1.3% of men in Iran are getting CKD each year12.

With regard to the etiology, different studies separately associated numerous factors, (e.g. nutritional, environmental and occupational parameters) with CKD13–16. For example, studies revealed that being exposed to high temperature may lead to dehydration and a reduction in kidney volume and even death from pre-existing chronic conditions17. For example, studies on the association of high environmental temperature and kidney function suggested that without other risk factors (e.g. diabetes, hypertension, or glomerulonephritis), repeated exposure to high temperature, intense physical activity, and insufficient rehydration can result in kidneys suboptimal function and CKD18,19. Despite valuable findings from previous studies, our understanding of the CKD’s etiology and its contributing factors is potentially biased due to not including certain potentially important confounders in the study as studies predominantly investigated the subject from only limited aspects (either nutritional, behavioral, or environmental).

The present study is the second phase of a hospital-based case-control study aimed to reveal the associations of several environmental factors with CKD in the Iranian population adjusted for several contributing factors from different aspects including nutritional and behavioral ones.

Methods

Settings

This is the second phase of a hospital-based case-control study, the participants in this study were either registered patients (cases) or visitors to the Faghihi and Motahari medical centers, which are the two biggest referral centers in South Iran and are situated in Shiraz, the provincial capital of Fars. This is the second phase of a previously published study. Sex and age were used to match cases and controls in terms of frequency. Employing the CKD-EPI formula, the GFR values were computed20,21.

Selection of cases

Patients from the country’s south receive medical care from the chosen clinics. Participants in this research included all CKD patients registered with the aforementioned centers between June 2020 and December 2020. A case involved a patient who had at least two GFR < 60 (ml/min/1.73 m2) events within a three-month period. The most recent version of the International Classification of Diseases (2010) states that patients with GFR values of 30–59 (ml/min/1.73 m2) and 15–29 (ml/min/1.73 m2), respectively, are assigned codes N18.3 and N18.422. A total of 350 patients were included in the study who received a CKD diagnosis from a nephrologist within the study period.

Selection of the controls

To prevent recall bias, hospital controls were employed. Patients admitted to orthopedic and general surgery wards between June and December 2020 (for reasons including hemorrhoids, varicose veins, appendicitis, intestinal obstruction, and hernias) were the source of the control subjects. GFR was estimated from the participants’ serum creatinine levels, and the controls (n = 350) were those who reported no history of CKD and had normal GFRs (ml/min/1.73 m2) (GFR > 60).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Individuals with a final diagnosis of CKD and a minimum age of 20 were considered eligible. Individuals in critical condition, those with acute kidney injury, those undergoing renal transplantation, and those with cognitive impairment were not allowed to participate.

Data collection

The required information from at least a year before interview was gathered via an interview-administered questionnaire and the participant’s medical records. Three experts made a targeted revision of previously designed and used questionnaire and evaluated the revised version of the questionnaire. Assessment was made following a pilot study with 50 cases and 50 controls. Using the test-retest method, the questionnaire’s reliability was assessed (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.75) and approved. A qualified public health nurse conducted the interview when participants were visiting the clinics.

The questionnaire was designed to define the factors’ usual status preceding at least a year before experiencing the first symptoms of CKD, in order to avoid measuring concurrent conditions that their association with CKD may be interpreted as reverse causation. Participants therefore disclosed their past environmental exposures including proximity of residence to a highway, high voltage electricity, a mine, a factory, a mobile antenna, a polluting source, and the heat of the living environment and the workplace, and exposure to pesticides and industrial chemicals. Demographic and health information including marital, employment, and educational status, place of residence, age, sex, as well as, history of diabetes, HTN, and cardiovascular diseases obtained from the hospital file of the participants.

The participants were asked to give their written informed consent or their legal guardians verbal informed consent, as most of them lacked formal education. The study protocol was examined and approved (approval number 1399.865) by the Shiraz University of Medical Sciences ethical committee. Also, the present study conforms to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations.

Sample size and statistical analysis

The sample size was determined to be able to identify a correlation as small as OR = 1.5 between CKD and the history of antibiotic use, one of our primary study factors21. With type one error of 0.05 (2-sided) and power of 0.80, the required sample size was estimated to be as large as 312 participants for each group. Test for unadjusted relationships between categorical variables and CKD was conducted using chi-square. Using unconditional logistic regression, we were able to derive both crude and adjusted odds ratios for the analysis as explained below.

Because in phase two, new variables were added to a large number of variables from phase one, we used 4 steps to built a model of determinants of CKD23. Accordingly, (1) we found factors linked to CKD with a P-value of 0.20 (or less) based on the univariate analysis. Using the variables selected in step 1, we fitted two independent models using backward selection method: model 1 contained data from phase one, and the other (model 2) included variables from phase two. In the third stage, we combined the two models and using backward and forward (reintroducing each variable that was previously excluded from model 1 and 2), we reached the final model (only the variables with a P-value of 0.05 or less) were retained in the final model. The results were deemed statistically significant at p < 0.05, and all p-values were two-sided. Stata version 14.0 (Stata Corporation, College Station, TX, USA) was used for all analyses. Due to the completeness of data collected in the face to face interviews, no particular action was made to handle missing data.

Results

Baseline characteristics

The analysis comprised a total of 350 cases and 350 age and sex-matched controls. 59.6 ± 12.4 and 58.9 ± 12.2, respectively, were the mean ages of the cases and controls (p = 0.83). Overall, 208 patients (59.4%) and 200 controls (57.1%) were male (p = 0.54). Also, 149 patients (42.6%) and 133 controls (38.0%) were illiterate or had elementary education (p = 0.001). The majority of cases (96.9%) and controls (95.7%) had married status (p = 0.42). The average GFR (ml/min/1.73 m2) for the CKD and control groups were 38.6 ± 11.4 and 78.3 ± 10.2, respectively.

Discussion

The results of the present study suggested that several variables including, proximity of residence to mines, proximity of residence to mobile antenna, proximity of residence to several polluting sources, the heat of the workplace and exposure to pollutants may affect the risk of CKD.

The proximity of the place of residence to mines was one of the environmental factors that was known as a risk factor for CKD. One of the reasons that mines are dangerous can be inhalation of dust from mines24. Also, the presence of various chemicals (zinc, silica, mercury) is the risk factor for various diseases such as diabetes, high blood pressure, and cardiovascular disease, which are also the risk factor for CKD25. In fact, considering the existence of all kinds of mines, including stone mines in Iran, and the fact that a comprehensive and complete study has not been conducted regarding the risk factor of the proximity of the place of residence to mines, and our study is the first analytical study that has been conducted, it is recommended that the studies More analysis should be done in this field.

Another risk factor for CKD was the proximity of the residence to the mobile phone antenna. Although there have been limited studies on the harmful effects of mobile phone waves and mobile phone antennas, some studies have investigated the harmful effects of mobile phone waves on the heart, liver, lungs, and reproductive system26–30. One of the reasons that mobile phone waves are dangerous is blood pressure fluctuations, so when a person talks on the phone, blood pressure increases slightly, while when the call is stopped, blood pressure returns to normal. By talking several times on the cell phone during the day and night, the blood pressure will also fluctuate, which can cause kidney damage31. Considering that no study has been conducted on the risk factor of mobile antenna for CKD in the country, and our study is the first study that has investigated this variable as a risk factor, and also considering the increase of the recommendation More analytical studies can be done in this regard and it is also recommended that the antennas of this variable can be considered as a new risk factor for CKD.

Studies also suggest that the temperature of the work environment is another risk factor for CKD. Accordingly, the risk of acquiring the condition in people whose work environment is hot is 3.32 times higher compared to people who work in an environment with normal/cold temperature, which is consistent with the results of other studies32–36. In a meta-analysis study, the prevalence of CKD among workers whose work environment was hot was reported as 15%37. In a longitudinal study in Saudi Arabia, the incidence of CKD was reported to be 18%32. One of the main causes of heat in the work environment being a risk factor is dehydration caused by high heat. In order to reduce damage to the kidneys in jobs that deal with high heat, the following tips are recommended: drinking fluids, reducing working hours and shifts, proper ventilation in the workplace, using suitable clothes and heat protection.

In the current study, the history of diabetes was associated with a higher risk of CKD. This finding is consistent with the results of other studies on the same subject22,38. It is not surprising that people with diabetes have an increased risk of CKD as diabetes is an important detrimental factor for kidney functioning as approximately, 40% of patients with diabetes develop CKD39.

The other variable that was associated with an increased risk of CKD was a history of hypertension. Our result is consistent with the results of several other studies38,40. It is reported that hypertension is both a cause and effect of CKD and accelerates the progression of the CKD to ESRD41.

The results of the study showed that exposure to heavy metals is another factor affecting CKD. Exposure to heavy metals such as lead, cadmium, arsenic, mercury and cadmium that enter the human body from various sources (contamination of food sources during processing, mines, lead and plastic industries, urban sewage and chemical toxins) can cause damage and decrease kidney function. In other studies, heavy metals have been identified in relation to the risk of CKD42–45. This is despite the fact that in the study conducted by Anupama et al., no relationship was reported between heavy metals and CKD46. According to the published conflicting results, it is suggested that more studies be done on the risk factor of heavy metals. In addition to heavy metals, chemical toxins were also risk factors that are in line with the findings of other studies regarding CKD43,47,48. Considering that there is a possibility of chemicals and poisons entering the body through inhalation, skin absorption or eating from various sources such as industry, agriculture, transportation, it is suggested to use laboratory coats, safety glasses and Face masks, breathing masks and gloves should be used to prevent body contact with chemicals.

Strengths and limitation

A sizable sample size was used in our research. Furthermore, the study comprised a notably substantial quantity of research variables. However, as a common issue in case-control studies, recall bias is to be considered when interpreting the results. Dealing with this issue, we used hospital controls and categorical or ordered answer questions to reduce recall bias in our study49. Using ordered or categorical questions can potentially reduce recall bias by providing participants with predefined response options49,50. This approach helps respondents to structure their responses and reduces reliance on memory alone51. Despite the fact that the current study’s design was unable to establish a causal relationship between CKD and the related variables the results could still be valuable and conveniently applied in the prevention of CKD due to the associated factors’ potential importance and modifiable nature.

Conclusions

This study supports the hypothesis that the proximity of the place of residence to mines and mobile antenna, may contribute CKD. In addition, work related exposure to heat and chemical pollutants are introduced as important CKD risk factors. Tight and effective control measures in work environment are needed to prevent CKD. Regulated fluids drinking, reducing working time in hot day hours, proper ventilation and air conditioning in the workplace or using specially designed clothes are among the possible solutions.
