‘Peripheral nerve blocks vs selective spinal anesthesia in patients with femur fracture’ - Uncertain evidence of superiority
Authors: Maja Kovac
Published: 2026

To the Editor,

I read with great interest and delight Coviello et al., ‘Peripheral nerve blocks vs selective spinal anesthesia in patients with femur fracture: a patient-, surgeon-, and assessor-blinded randomized controlled study’ [1]. The authors should be commended on addressing an interesting comparison of two regional anaesthetic techniques, particularly given the limited evidence comparing selective spinal anaesthesia (SSA) and peripheral nerve blocks (PNBs). Although there are some interesting findings, there are some methodological and interpretative issues to address.

The first concern regarding the methodology is the randomisation of the groups. The SSA and PNB groups were mostly comparable, as stated by the authors, including the number of patients in individual ASA categories. However, in regular clinical practice ASA 1–2 patients are often regarded as ‘mild anaesthetic risk’, whilst ASA 3–4 are regarded as ‘high anaesthetic risk’ [2]. It is noted that there is a significant disparity between the groups, with 80% SSA patients classed as ASA 3–4, comparing to only 56.7% in the PNB group. This higher comorbidity profile in the SSA group potentially contributed to the significantly greater number of hypotensive episodes identified by the authors. However, this was not acknowledged by the authors as a possible contributing factor.

The second methodological concern was regarding the regular intraoperative evaluation of motor and sensory blockade of sedated patients using the Bromage and Hollmen scales. The rationale for why this was done was not explained and requires clarification as blocks are not routinely examined once surgery has begun [3]. Furthermore, in sedated patients, neurological testing may be challenging as these tests require co-operative patients [4]. The authors haven’t described how this was done, why this was done and whether both limbs or only the operated limb was assessed, leading to lack of clarity and rationale for the reader.

The interpretation of results lacked convincing clinical implications. Whilst less hypotensive episodes were noted in the PNB group, all episodes were transient, corrected with fluids, and not associated with secondary complications. The key concerns of intraoperative hypotension are the risks of organ dysfunction [5], therefore, without evidence of reduction in these secondary outcomes, can we conclude that PNBs are safer alternatives to SSA? A greater sample of patients could provide more convincing data regarding secondary outcomes and reduce any effect of the disparity in the ASA categories.

Finally, although postoperative pain scores were statistically lower in the PNB group, the clinical relevance appears limited. Both groups demonstrated low overall pain scores, and notably, patients receiving PNBs experienced early breakthrough pain requiring more rescue analgesia. This breakthrough pain does not describe the established ‘rebound pain’ secondary to block offset [6]. The authors suggest that this breakthrough pain may be attributable to the slower onset of sensory blockade with PNBs. Yet, the achievement of an adequate intraoperative block suggests that effective sensory blockade (anaesthetic and analgesic) was established, therefore questions the validity of this explanation.

In summary, whilst the study contributes to an important anaesthetic question, some of the methodology and lack of significant clinical implications hinders the conclusion that either technique is superior. Significantly varying ASA scores of the groups may have significantly affected outcomes, and the addition of potentially unwarranted assessments of the patient could have contributed to the suggestion of PNB superiority. Finally, the interpretation of results lacked beneficial clinical implications and therefore cannot be utilised yet to support one technique over the other.
