Risk of Liver Cancer From Major Causes Compared to Smoking and Lung Cancer: A Systematic Review and Meta‐Analysis
Authors: Carolyn Wallace, Devin Razavi‐Shearer, Samantha Hall, Tarik Asselah, Cary James, Loreta A Kondili, Shyamasundaran Kottilil, Daniel Low‐Beer, Poonam Mathur, Junko Tanaka, Harrys A Torres, Homie Razavi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTLiver cancer is the second leading cause of premature cancer mortality globally, whose main causes—HBV, HCV, MASH and alcohol use—are preventable. Viral hepatitis' carcinogenicity is not as well‐known as the risk of lung cancer from smoking, leading to rising rates of liver cancer worldwide. The aim of this study was to compare the risk of liver cancer in HBV, HCV, HDV, MASH and alcohol consumption with the better‐known risk of lung cancer associated with smoking. We extracted the relative risk of liver cancer from HBV, HCV, HDV, MASH, and alcohol use and the relative risk of lung cancer from smoking from previous studies. We included cohort, case–control and nested case–control studies published as original articles. Odds ratios for each factor were pooled globally and by region to test for significant differences. One hundred and twenty‐six studies with 86,097,714 participants were included. Relative to the risk of lung cancer and currently smoking (OR 6.0), HBV (OR 15.2, p < 0.001), HCV (OR 12.8, p < 0.01) and HDV (OR 23.5, p < 0.01) were significantly associated with a higher risk of liver cancer, while MASLD (OR 2.6, p < 0.01) and alcohol use (OR 2.4, p < 0.001) were significantly associated with a lower risk of liver cancer. There was no statistically significant difference in liver cancer risk across regions. These findings highlight that viral hepatitis is a carcinogenic infectious disease. Better integration of HCC surveillance within the management of viral hepatitis is critical.Keywords: hepatocellular carcinoma, liver cancer, viral hepatitis

Liver cancer is the sixth most prevalent cancer worldwide. While its incidence is markedly lower than that of the most common cancer, lung cancer (866,000 compared to 2.5 million cases annually), liver cancer remains the third leading cause of cancer mortality, accounting for 760,000 deaths globally in 2022, and the second leading contributor to premature cancer mortality, behind lung cancer [1, 2]. As with lung cancer, the causes of liver cancer are largely preventable. The main risk factors for liver cancer are hepatitis B virus (HBV), hepatitis C virus (HCV), excessive alcohol use and metabolic‐associated steatohepatitis (MASH) [3]. Though less common, hepatitis D virus (HDV) also accounts for a notable amount of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) cases, with a global analysis estimating that 20% of HCC cases among the HBV+ population were attributable to HDV [4].

Unlike the well‐known causal relationship between smoking and lung cancer, patients, physicians and policymakers alike display significantly less awareness and action towards the direct link of hepatitis to liver cancer, which illuminates a deleterious threat to public health that has remained relatively shadowed and inconspicuous. In one large survey, 63% of participants from the general population identified alcohol use as a cause of liver cancer, but only 22%–23% identified hepatitis B or hepatitis C as a risk factor [5]. From a treatment perspective, multiple studies have documented that less than half of those who fall into the guidelines for recommended surveillance for HCC are screened appropriately [6, 7]. And, while 62% of countries have national breast cancer screening and 65% have national cervical cancer screening programmes [8], only four countries have a national HCC screening programme at present (China, Japan, South Korea and Taiwan) [9, 10].

Discovered over 60 years ago, the HBV vaccine was the first cancer‐preventing vaccination, yet this was not the primary focus of outreach campaigns and vaccination efforts. For instance, while the prevention of cancer is highlighted over 50 times in the human papillomavirus vaccine package insert, it is not mentioned once in HBV vaccination package inserts [11, 12, 13]. These perceptions are compounded with treatment guidelines that treat viral hepatitis solely as a liver disease rather than a cancer‐causing infectious disease, first manifesting in fibrosis staging restrictions for HCV treatment and continuing in HBV treatment guidelines. For HBV, effective treatment has been available for the last 25 years, yet upwards of 75% of individuals diagnosed with HBV remain treatment ineligible [14], while 34%–64% of cases of HCC among HBV+ treatment naïve patients were among patients who were outside of the treatment guidelines [15]. Further, patients are faced with the burden of required annual and semi‐annual follow‐ups to identify when they may become treatment eligible.

MASH is the fastest‐growing cause of HCC deaths [16] and has less favourable survival compared with hepatitis‐related HCC [17]; therefore, examining MASH merits attention. As the most common chronic liver disease worldwide [18], with rates rising in tandem with increasing rates of obesity, MASH is bound to play a more prominent role in contributing to HCC in the future. Yet, similar to viral hepatitis, there remains limited awareness and screening [19], and a widespread lack of national strategies for MASH compounds the likelihood of a growing burden [20].

The aim of this study was to compare the risk of liver cancer in HBV, HCV, HDV and MASH to the better‐known risks of liver cancer associated with alcohol consumption and lung cancer associated with smoking. Our intention is to bring awareness to the primary causes of liver cancer and the magnitude of the risk of inaction.

Methods

Data Sources and Criteria

A comprehensive literature search was conducted in PubMed for epidemiological studies published online before September 2024 that reported on the relationship between lung cancer and smoking, and liver cancer and alcohol use, HBV, HCV, HDV and metabolic dysfunction‐associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) in the general population. Key words included the exposure (smoking, alcohol use, HBV, HCV, HDV and MASLD/non‐alcoholic fatty liver disease [NAFLD]), outcome (lung cancer for smoking, HCC for all other exposures) and study design (cohort or case–control). Sources were cross‐referenced to identify additional potentially relevant studies. Only articles in English were included. Meta‐analyses were excluded to minimize double counting of studies. We included cohort, case–control and nested case–control studies published as original articles. One cross‐sectional study was included due to its large scope. Studies needed to report odds ratio (OR), relative risk (RR) or hazard ratio (HR) and confidence intervals (CIs) of the risk estimates to be included. A completed PRISMA 2020 checklist is provided in the Supporting Information.

Potentially eligible studies were reviewed independently by three epidemiologists, then full reports were reviewed to determine eligibility for inclusion in the meta‐analysis. Based on the inclusion criteria, studies with a high risk of bias were excluded. For each included study, we extracted the following: study design, country, RR estimates and CIs, and sample size. RR for combined genders was used when available; otherwise, RR by gender was used. For smoking, current smokers in the highest use group reported were included. For alcohol use, the highest use group reported was included. MASLD included data for MASLD and NAFLD, based on the nomenclature used at the time of the study. The nearly complete accordance between NAFLD and MASLD populations has been well established (including almost identical testing accuracy and clinical characteristics), allowing for the transfer of evidence on NAFLD to MASLD [21]. Since HCC attributed to MASLD only develops among those classified as MASH, all MASLD (and previously defined NAFLD) data were attributed to MASH. Hepatitis B surface antigen positivity was used for HBV, anti‐HCV positivity status was used for HCV, and anti‐HDV positivity was used for HDV/HBV co‐infection. Relative risk of HCC for anti‐HDV/HBV co‐infection relative to HBV mono‐infection was used, as there were very few studies comparing risk of HCC among anti‐HDV positive individuals to the general population. HCC diagnosed by all approaches was included (i.e., radiographic imaging, biopsy), and only studies reporting from record linkage were included. Studies were analyzed globally as well as by region (West, Asia and Africa) where available. The West region included studies from Europe, United States, Canada and Australia. Studies were grouped by region according to the study location, not the origin of the patient population (e.g., did not consider the impact of migration in each study), although most studies were based on national cohorts or databases, which should be reflective of the overall population of the study's country.

Statistical Methods

Since cancer is a rare disease, OR, RR and HR were assumed to be comparable [22]. The analysis included risk estimates adjusted for the greatest number of potential confounding factors when available.

Odds ratios for each factor were pooled by region using a random effects model, implemented with the rma function in R, to account for both within‐study and between‐study variability. The results were used to test for significant differences in the odds ratio of each factor and liver cancer relative to the odds ratio for smoking and lung cancer, and to assess whether regions were a statistically significant variable.

The adjusted odds ratio for HDV was derived by adding the log odds of HBV to the log odds of HDV by each region. This method accounts for the dependency of HDV on HBV infection, ensuring the estimates for risk of cancer are relative to the general population rather than the HBV‐infected population.

Population‐based weights, proportional to the regional study populations, were applied to the log‐transformed odds ratios. The weighted log odds ratios and their standard errors were combined to produce pooled global estimates. Final odds ratios and corresponding 95% confidence intervals were obtained by exponentiating the weighted log odds ratios and standard errors.

Meta‐analyses were conducted using the metafor package in R, applying random effects models with Restricted Maximum Likelihood (REML). This modelling approach was selected to account for potential differences across studies, including varying study design, wide variations in sample size and dissimilarities in populations (i.e., socio‐demographic and clinical characteristics, such as comorbidities), diagnostic criteria, exposure definitions (i.e., highest use group for smoking and alcohol use) and duration of follow‐up. Heterogeneity was evaluated using both the Q‐test and the I 2 statistic. The Q‐test results were statistically significant, and all analyses showed I 2 values > 50%, indicating considerable variation in effect sizes beyond what would be expected by chance. These findings justified the use of random effects models rather than fixed effects models.

Each criterion was considered collectively as well as by geographic region (West, Africa and Asia) for subgroup analysis. Excluding studies with potential overlap was not necessary, as the random effects model accounts for study precision through standard errors, and population‐based weighting minimizes impact on the results. Meta‐regression was conducted using region as a moderator to assess whether it contributed to the observed variation in effect sizes.

Ethical Statement

This study is a systematic review and meta‐analysis based exclusively on previously published studies. No new data were collected from human participants or animals; therefore, institutional review board approval and informed consent were not required. All included studies were conducted in accordance with their respective institutional ethical standards and applicable regulations.

Conclusion

The intent of this analysis was to compare the RR of liver cancer across various exposures. Our analysis found that the risk of HCC is similar for alcohol use and MASH. MASH‐related HCC develops in the absence of cirrhosis more commonly than other risk factors and is often diagnosed at a more advanced stage [23], most likely since non‐cirrhotic patients fall outside of surveillance guidelines. Predictive and cost‐effective diagnostics are essential to identify at‐risk individuals and broaden surveillance protocols. Presently, prevention of the development of MASH to HCC, and even the development of MASLD at all, via lifestyle interventions and the management of common comorbid conditions (e.g., obesity, diabetes) is imperative.

More remarkably, our analysis found that the risk of HCC from HBV and HCV is significantly higher statistically than the risk of lung cancer due to current smoking. HBV is the leading etiology of liver cancer worldwide and is responsible for one‐third of all cases, and HCV further propagates this landscape, accounting for over 20% of liver cancer cases [3].

With over 51 million HCV cases and nearly 249 million HBV cases currently globally [24], the importance of increased screening, prophylaxis (for HBV) and antiviral treatment cannot be overstated. If the status quo is maintained, HCC incidence and deaths are projected to increase by over 55% by 2040, with 1.4 million new cases and 1.3 million deaths in 2040 and cost INT$1.7 trillion globally through 2050 [25, 26].

Additionally, we found that individuals concurrently infected with HDV and HBV have the largest risk of developing HCC. While some HBV/HDV co‐infected patients have been shown to spontaneously recover, over three‐quarters of the super‐infected population progress to chronic disease [27], emphasizing the importance of global HBV prevention in order to eliminate HDV infection. Disease progression is accelerated in those with simultaneous HBV and HDV infection [27], with HCC manifesting at an earlier age in the HDV‐infected population than the HBV mono‐infected population [28], illuminating the importance of early vaccination against HBV. And, as with HCV and HBV, routine universal screening is critical for HDV, as risk‐based screening currently recommended by AASLD has shown to miss nearly 20% of HDV cases [29]. Overall rates of screening can be low, even in countries with sufficient resources [30, 31, 32], illustrating the benefit of reflex testing to facilitate earlier identification of anti‐HDV for appropriate care and the prevention of HCC.

The pooled RRs from our study are similar to meta‐analyses performed for each criterion and cancer outcome. Bagnardi et al. [33] reported a RR of 2.07 (1.7–2.6) for alcohol use and HCC. Petrelli et al. [34] reported a 2.19 RR (1.67–2.87) for MASLD and HCC. RRs ranged from 7.3 to 15.6 for smoking and lung cancer among global meta‐analyses [35, 36, 37, 38]. A meta‐analysis of HBV and HCC reported a RR of 13.5 (9.5–19.1) and for HCV and HCC a RR of 12.2 (9.2–16.2) [39]. Meta‐analyses comparing HDV with HBV mono‐infection reported RRs between 1.28 and 2.97 [40, 41, 42, 43]. The lower risk of lung cancer attributable to smoking in Asia compared with the West in our study has been previously established as well [44].

As this analysis depends upon other published studies, bias and confounding factors (i.e., obesity, diabetes, alcohol consumption and co‐existing liver diseases) in the original studies are not controlled for; however, pooled estimates may be influenced due to varying methods of adjustment across the original studies. The serological markers used in the published studies are not diagnostic of chronic viral infection. Further, the lack of uniformity among definitions of alcohol use and smoking is a limitation. To address methodological differences, region‐specific statistical models and population‐based weighting were used to account for variation across studies. Lung cancer and liver cancer involve different disease processes and arise from different populations, potentially limiting direct comparison; however, comparing odds ratios allows for comparison of the strength of association between smoking‐related lung cancer and viral hepatitis‐related HCC. Relatively few studies addressed the relative risk of HCC among the HDV+ population, leading to a larger confidence interval. Excluding the category of alcohol use, data were not controlled by the level of alcohol consumption. Although the risk of cancer is elevated with higher levels of alcohol consumption, a much larger portion of the population consumes lower levels of alcohol, leading to a similar number of overall cancer cases across levels of consumption [45], thus attenuating the effect in variation of alcohol consumption. The interaction between risk factors (criteria) were not studied in this analysis, although there is substantial evidence indicating the presence of two or more risk factors compound the risk of HCC.

This study in no way diminishes the high risk of lung cancer associated with smoking. Indeed, it has taken decades of global and national tobacco control policies to realize a decline in lung cancer mortality related to smoking [46], and lung cancer still claims more lives than any other form of cancer globally [2]. However, liver cancer is the second leading cause of years of life lost from cancer worldwide [10]; therefore, addressing it is an important public health goal as well. It does not serve us well to be complacent with health policy and communication because of the extended natural history of HCC any more than it does with lung cancer, and viral hepatitis vaccination, treatment and cure are some of the most direct interventions for preventing cancer.

Demonstrating that the risk of developing HCC from HBV or HCV exceeds the risk of developing lung cancer from smoking, our analysis emphasizes the significant contribution viral hepatitis makes to the number of cancer deaths globally. And, as rates of MASH rise worldwide, this insidious disease will also add to the burden. Cementing the connection between these diseases and HCC in the minds of patients, physicians and policy makers is the first step to salutary action.
