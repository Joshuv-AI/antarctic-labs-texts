Using transcriptome analysis and QTL mapping to decipher the genetic basis of heterosis in potato root system
Authors: Jing Yang, Xue Sun, Xianhua Hu, Tingyu Yin, Xueshuang Wang, Wenyi Wang, Jing Liu, Wei Tang, Canhui Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHeterosis is a crucial theoretical and technological foundation for modern crop improvement, and its utilization has significantly enhanced agricultural productivity. As the most important tuber crop, research on the mechanism of heterosis in potatoes (Solanum tuberosum L.) has just started, especially the research on root heterosis in diploid potato has not been reported yet.ResultsWe crossed two diploid materials HD5 and M9 with homozygous genomes to obtain the F1 hybrid HM with significant root heterosis. Transcriptome sequencing analysis was performed on the roots of 14-day-old hydroponically grown seedlings. A total of 2,156 non-additive expression genes, 5,306 dominant expression genes, 1,345 overdominant expression genes, and 3,246 allele-specific expression genes were obtained. Genetic effect analysis of heterosis showed that the dominant effect was the main mode of action during root development. GO and KEGG enrichment analysis of the above four gene sets indicated that the lignin biosynthesis pathway was involved in regulating the formation of root heterosis. Calculation of heterotic effects for 47 QTLs mapped using the F2 population showed that overdominance and complete-incomplete dominance constituted the primary heterotic effects of potato roots. Further integration of QTL mapping results with lignin pathway analysis identified six candidate genes related to root development heterosis: DM8C01G33280, DM8C02G28580, DM8C02G29270, DM8C03G31680, DM8C05G27310, and DM8C12G18910.ConclusionsThis study contributes to clarifying the molecular mechanisms of heterosis in potato root development and provides valuable targets for cloning and functional analysis of heterosis-related genes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07679-0.Keywords: Potato, Root, Heterosis, QTL, Transcriptome

Background

Heterosis refers to the phenomenon where F₁ hybrids derived from crossing two genetically distinct pure or inbred parental lines outperform both parents in traits such as vitality, adaptability, fertility, growth potential, stress resistance, yield, and quality, either individually or collectively [1]. Its large-scale application in crop breeding (e.g., maize and rice) has led to a significant increase in global grain production during the 20th century, ensuring global food security [2, 3]. Hybrid breeding has also become one of the most effective approaches for boosting crop yield [4]. Although heterosis has been extensively applied in practical production, the underlying molecular mechanisms driving heterosis are still not fully understood [5].

The formation mechanism of heterosis is very complex, and the classical hypothesis explaining its mechanism was proposed long ago, i.e. dominance, overdominance, and epistasis [6], and several studies have validated these hypotheses [7]. The dominance complementation hypothesis posits that dominant genes from one parent can mask deleterious recessive genes from the other parent [1, 8]. The overdominance hypothesis suggests that the heterozygous state of different alleles in plants generates stimulatory effects on growth and development [9, 10]. Epistasis proposes that heterosis arises not only from interactions between alleles in F1 hybrids but may also involve interactions between non-allelic genes at different chromosomal loci [11, 12]. In recent years, significant progress has been made in studying the mechanisms of heterosis, driven by the rapid development of high-throughput sequencing, quantitative trait locus (QTL) mapping, genome-wide association studies (GWAS), and multi-omics technologies. For instance, in rice, Huang et al. [4] systematically identified major gene loci controlling heterosis by measuring and analyzing field yield traits in over 1,000 hybrid rice varieties and 17 representative genetic populations, revealing that yield heterosis in rice primarily stems from incomplete dominance effects. In maize, Xiao et al. [13] conducted large-scale crossing experiments to generate over 40,000 hybrids; using GWAS, they demonstrated that dominance complementation in hybrids can alleviate inhibitory interactions between parental genes, thereby contributing to heterosis. Shao et al. [14] analyzed transcriptomic data from 4 conditions and 3 tissues of hybrid rice, finding that hybrids selectively express beneficial alleles under specific spatiotemporal conditions, leading to heterosis and proposing the “directional allelic-specific expression hypothesis”. Sun et al. [15] combined genomic and transcriptomic analyses to dissect the mechanisms of rice heterosis by examining hybrid pairing patterns, parental variation frequencies, haplotype combinations of key genes, and allelic-specific expression. The mining and identification of heterosis-related genes provide a genetic basis for efficient utilization of heterosis. Liu et al. [16] reported that the hybrid rice CY1000 harbors both the paternal salt-tolerant gene OsAAT30P and the maternal salt-tolerant gene OsWRKY72M. Through regulation by the OsWRKY72-OsAAT30/OsGSTU26 module, the hybrid exhibits overdominance effects in salt tolerance.

Potato (Solanum tuberosum L.) serves as a staple food for over 1.3 billion people in more than 120 countries [17]. However, due to the autotetraploid nature of most cultivated potato varieties and their primary reliance on clonal propagation, research on potato heterosis lags far behind that of seed crops such as maize and rice. Diploid hybrid breeding has emerged as a revolutionary breakthrough in potato breeding in recent years, offering advantages over traditional tetraploid breeding, including shorter cycles, higher efficiency, and precise genetic improvement. With the recent resolution of self-incompatibility and inbreeding depression issues [18–21], Zhang et al. [22] developed the F1 hybrid potato with prominent heterosis, marking the initiation of systematic studies on potato heterosis. Li et al. [23] explored the genetic and molecular mechanisms of heterosis during the seedling, flowering, and tuber stages by integrating multi-omics data (transcriptome, metabolome, and DNA methylation) from F1 and its two parents. Their findings revealed that dominance complementation of genes primarily drives heterosis establishment in diploid potatoes, with significant gene expression and functional complementation observed in traits such as flower color, fertility, and starch synthesis. Recently, the same team reported an F2 diploid potato population derived from crossing two highly homozygous inbred lines. Multi-omics data generated 20,382 traits and identified 25,770 quantitative trait loci (QTLs). The researchers found that positive heterotic effects of yield-related QTLs and negative heterotic effects of metabolite QTLs (mQTLs) contribute to yield heterosis. Additionally, the PME gene, exhibiting dominance heterosis, plays a critical role in heterosis related to male sterility [24].

The root system is a critical organ for plants to absorb water and nutrients, mediate plant-microbe interactions, and anchor plants in soil [25]. Potato is considered a typical shallow-rooted crop, with significant correlations between root growth and development, shoot biomass, and final tuber yield [26]. Thus, improving potato root traits to enhance the spatial utilization efficiency of water and nutrients-and consequently boost yield-holds significant potential. However, research on gene discovery and application related to potato root development has lagged significantly. To date, only StNAC262 [27] and StRFP2 [28] have been identified via genetic transformation as root development-related genes in potatoes. Additionally, the genetic mechanisms underlying root heterosis in potatoes remain uncharacterized to date. Overall, research on the heterosis of potato roots is urgently needed.

In this study, we utilized two homozygous diploid Solanum chacoense potato materials, HD5 and M9. F1 and F2 populations were obtained via crossing and selfing, respectively. The root phenotypes of F1 displayed significant heterosis, while the F2 population exhibited pronounced phenotypic segregation. Transcriptome sequencing analysis was performed on roots of F1 (HM) and its two parents, hydroponically cultured for 14 days. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were classified by expression patterns, and non-additively expressed (NAE) genes and allele-specifically expressed (ASE) genes associated with heterosis were identified. The regulatory pathways and potential heterosis mechanisms involving these genes were analyzed. Additionally, by integrating QTL mapping results of root traits from the F2 population derived from HD5 and M9, genes related to heterosis in potato root development were mined. These findings contribute to a deeper understanding of root heterosis in potatoes and provide key targets for functional studies and application of heterosis-related genes in root development.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and growth conditions

Two highly homozygous diploid potato (Solanum chacoense) parents (HD5 and M9) and their F1 hybrids (HM) were used in this study. M9 was developed through three generations of strict selfing from the progenitor line M6 [29]. HD5 is a naturally occurring, self-compatible homozygous mutant accession, both of which were maintained by the Joint Academy of Potato Science of Yunnan Normal University. Stem segments of each material were inoculated onto MS30 medium. All materials were cultured in a sterile environment at 20℃ with a 16-h light/8-h dark photoperiod. After 15 days, 36 uniform and robust sterile seedlings were selected from each of the two parents and the F1, and transplanted into Hoagland nutrient solution (10 mL/L). All hydroponic containers were placed in an open-air environment for continuous culture over 28 days. Non-destructive in situ root trait investigations were performed at five time points: 0, 7, 14, 21, and 28 days.

Root heterosis measurements

Hydroponic roots were evenly placed on transparent trays and scanned using a root scanner (Top Cloud Agriculture Plant Root Scanner GXY-A) to obtain clear root images. The images were analyzed with RhizoPheno software to measure parameters including total root length (TRL), root diameter (RD), root volume (RV), and root surface area (RSA); maximum root length (MRL) was measured with a ruler. Mean values of root traits were calculated for parents and F1. Mid-parent heterosis (MPH) and high-parent heterosis (HPH) of hybrids were calculated using the following formulas: MPH (%) = 100 × (F1 – MP)/MP and HPH (%) = 100 × (F1 – HP)/HP, where F1 is the mean value of hybrid traits, MP is the mean value of the two parents’ traits, and HP is the value of the high-parent trait. Duncan’s multiple comparisons were performed using Statistical Analysis System (SAS, version 8.01).

RNA isolation, sequencing, and transcriptome data analysis

Due to the significant heterosis of F1 hybrid HM observed at 14 days of hydroponic culture, root systems of parents and F1 were sampled at this time point, with whole roots collected and 3 individual plants per material used as biological replicates. Root tissues were placed in a mortar, ground thoroughly with liquid nitrogen until a fine powder was formed, and total RNA was extracted using a plant total RNA purification kit (Omega Plant RNA Kit) following the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA quality was assessed using two methods: (1) NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer for RNA purity and concentration; (2) Agilent 2100/LabChip GX for precise RNA integrity evaluation. After passing quality control, cDNA library construction was performed as follows: (1) eukaryotic mRNA was enriched using Oligo (dT)-attached magnetic beads; (2) mRNA was randomly fragmented with Fragmentation Buffer; (3) first- and second-strand cDNA were synthesized using mRNA as a template, followed by cDNA purification; (4) purified double-stranded cDNA underwent end repair, adenylation, and adapter ligation, with size selection performed using AMPure XP beads; (5) finally, cDNA libraries were obtained via PCR enrichment. Post-construction, libraries were preliminarily quantified using a Qubit 3.0 fluorometer, followed by insert size verification with a Qsep400 high-throughput analysis system. After confirming the insert size met expectations, the effective concentration of libraries was accurately quantified via Q-PCR to ensure quality. Finally, the libraries were sequenced using the PE150 mode on a high-throughput sequencing platform by Biomarker Technologies Co., Ltd.

After sequencing data were generated, adapter sequences and low-quality reads were removed to obtain high-quality clean data. Clean reads were then rapidly and accurately aligned to the reference genome DM8.1 (www.bioinformaticslab.cn/pubs/dm8/) using HISAT2 [30] software to obtain the positional information of reads on the reference genome. Aligned reads were subsequently assembled using StringTie [31] to reconstruct the transcriptome for subsequent analysis. Gene read counts were obtained based on the number of sequencing reads mapped to each transcript, and normalized using FPKM [32] (fragments per kilobase of transcript per million fragments mapped) as an indicator of transcript or gene expression levels.

Classification of gene expression patterns

Differential expression analysis was performed using edgeR software [33] based on the read counts mapped to genes. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified with the following criteria: FDR < 0.05 and |log2(fold change)| ≥ 1, with genes simultaneously meeting both criteria considered DEGs. Using this method, pairwise differential expression analysis was conducted among the two parents and F1. According to the method described by Huang et al. [34], genes were categorized into 12 expression pattern types.

Non-additively expressed genes identification

MPV (mid-parent value, defined as the mean expression value of the gene in parents) of gene expression levels was calculated. Progeny expression levels were tested for significant differences from MPV using EBSeq software [35]. Genes with significant differences were classified as non-additive expression genes, with thresholds set at fold change = 1.5 and p-value = 0.05.

Allele-specifically expressed genes identification

During the earlier genetic map construction, a total of 2,415,192 SNPs were identified, which were homozygous and inconsistent between the two parents (aa×bb) with sequencing depth exceeding 5X. Following the method described by Li et al. [23], RNA SNPs were called. Using DESeq2 software [36], we tested for significant differences in reads from different parental origins in the offspring at candidate SNPs (screening thresholds: fold change ≥ 1.5; FDR < 0.05). A locus was defined as an allele-specific expression (ASE) locus if significant differences were detected. Additionally, a gene was designated as an ASE gene when it harbored at least one ASE locus, and all ASE loci within this gene exhibited the same expression trend.

KEGG and GO enrichment analyses

Functional enrichment analyses of Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways and Gene Ontology (GO) terms, along with correlation analysis and principal component analysis, were performed using the Omicshare platform (Omicshare, http://www.omicshare.com/tools/).; Network visualization of the enrichment results was performed using Cytoscape software (version 3.6.1).

Evaluation of single-locus effects

Evaluation of single-locus effects: after the QTLs were identified, genetic effects were assessed through comparisons of both homozygous genotypes and the heterozygous genotypes. According to the estimates of dominance (short for d) effects with those of additive effects (short for a), the genetic model was further partitioned into five types using the following standards of Liu et al. [37]: overdominance (d/a >1.25), complete–incomplete dominance (0.25 < d/a ≤ 1.25), additive effect (− 0.25 < d/a ≤ 0.25), incomplete recessive (− 1.25 < d/a ≤ − 0.25) and underdominance (d/a ≤ − 1.25).

Validation of RNA-seq by qRT-PCR

RNA samples were reverse-transcribed to generate cDNA using a high-capacity cDNA synthesis kit (Applied Biosystems, USA). Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed with the AceQ qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix Kit (Vazyme Biotech) following standard protocols. Gene-specific RT-PCR primers were designed using the online tool Primer3Plus, and primer specificity was validated via NCBI Primer-BLAST (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/tools/primer-blast/). The sequences of primer pairs for the seven candidate genes are provided in Table S1. Additionally, RNA was reverse-transcribed to cDNA using the All-in-one First-strand Synthesis Master Mix (with dsDNse) from Yugong Biological. Gene expression levels were assessed on the StepOnePlus System (Applied Biosystems, USA), with three biological replicates per treatment group. Actin served as an endogenous reference gene for normalization of Ct values, and relative expression levels were calculated using the ΔΔCt method.

Results

Root system of potato hybrids exhibits significant heterosis

The dynamic root phenotypic traits of the male parent M9, female parent HD5, and hybrid F1 HM were investigated using a hydroponic method at 0, 7, 14, 21, and 28 days of cultivation (Fig. 1). Overall, the root growth of hybrid F1 HM was significantly faster than that of either parent, with HD5 showing faster growth than M9 among the parental lines. For HM, the total root length (RL), root volume (RV), root surface area (RSA), and maximum root length (MRL) were significantly higher than those of both parents across all five time points from 0 to 28 days. Additionally, root diameter (RD) was significantly greater than that of both parents at 7, 14, and 21 days.

Based on the reference genome and novel gene prediction results, a total of 42,261 genes were identified (Table S3). Further analysis was performed on the distribution characteristics of gene expression in each material. Using FPKM = 0 as the threshold, 66.79% of genes were expressed in HM, followed by 65.75% in M9 and 64.17% in HD5. Additionally, the number of genes with expression levels in the intervals of 0 < FPKM ≤ 1 and 1 < FPKM ≤ 20 was also the highest in the hybrid. Among the three materials, M9 exhibited the largest number of genes with expression levels > 20 FPKM, followed by HM and HD5 (Fig. 2C), though the overall differences in quantity were not significant. In summary, more genes were expressed in the hybrid, which may be related to the formation of heterosis in F1 roots.

In the case of significance level FDR < 0.05 and |log2(fold change)| ≥ 1, a total of 8,443 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified between the two parental lines, including 5,118 upregulated genes and 3,325 downregulated genes. Additionally, 5,705 DEGs were identified between HD5 and the hybrid, and 6,016 DEGs were found between M9 and the hybrid (Fig. 2D).

Identification and enrichment analysis of non-additively expressed genes

By comparing the gene expression levels between the hybrid and its two parents, we classified the gene expression patterns into two types: additive expression and non-additive expression (NAE). A gene was categorized as non-additively expressed if its expression level in the hybrid was significantly different from the average expression level of the two parents; conversely, it was classified as additively expressed if no significant difference was observed between the hybrid’s expression level and the parental mean. NAE genes are considered to be significantly associated with heterosis [16]. In this study, following filtering, among the 27,686 genes with detectable expression levels, we identified 25,530 additively expressed genes (accounting for 92.21% of all analyzed genes), while only a small subset of genes (2,156, 7.79%) exhibited non-additive expression patterns (Fig. 3A and Table S4). Among these NAE genes, 1,114 genes were highly expressed in the hybrid, and 1,042 genes were lowly expressed. Notably, the 2,156 non-additively expressed genes shared as many as 933 genes with the DEGs between the two parents (Fig. 3B).

A Proportion of non-additively expressed genes; B Venn diagram showing the overlap between non-additively expressed genes and DEGs between the two parents; C KEGG enrichment analysis and D GO enrichment analysis (Biological Process) of non-additively expressed genes

With a threshold of p < 0.05, KEGG enrichment analysis identified a total of 25 pathways, primarily including phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, phenylalanine metabolism, and plant hormone signal transduction, among others (Fig. 3C and Table S5). GO enrichment analysis of NAE genes revealed that, in terms of biological process, these genes were mainly involved in processes such as hydrogen peroxide catabolic process, single-organism catabolic process, and reactive oxygen species metabolic process. For molecular function, significant enrichment was observed in entries including oxidoreductase activity, tetrapyrrole binding, and heme binding. Regarding cellular component, the NAE genes were primarily enriched in the extracellular region, integral component of membrane, and intrinsic component of membrane (Fig. 3D and Table S5).

Expression pattern of differentially expressed genes

To further analyze the DEGs of hybrids and parents, pairwise differential expression analysis was performed among all materials (Fig. 4 A), and genes were classified into 12 categories of female parent-hybrid-male parent (F-H-M) expression patterns based on the method described by Shen et al. [38]. Genes in the P1 and P2 modes exhibited additive expression, totaling 1,079 genes; those in the P3-P6 modes showed dominant expression, totaling 5,306 genes; and genes in the P7-P12 modes displayed overdominant expression, totaling 1,345 genes (Fig. 4B and Table S6). Dominantly expressed genes accounted for 69% of all genes (Fig. 4 C), indicating that heterosis in potato root traits is primarily influenced by dominant genes.

Enrichment analyses were performed on dominant and overdominant genes, respectively. KEGG enrichment analysis of dominant genes identified 16 significantly enriched pathways (p < 0.05), primarily including plant-pathogen interaction, monoterpenoid biosynthesis, and phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, among others (Fig. 5A and Table S7). Within the biological process (BP) category of GO, dominant genes were primarily enriched in terms such as photosynthesis, hydrogen peroxide metabolic process, and hydrogen peroxide catabolic process (Fig. 5B). In terms of molecular function (MF), significant enrichment was observed in terms including oxidoreductase activity, tetrapyrrole binding, and peroxidase activity. Regarding the cellular component (CC) category, these genes were primarily enriched in terms such as integral component of membrane, intrinsic component of membrane, and membrane part (Fig. 5B and Table S7).

KEGG enrichment analysis was also performed on the overdominant genes, and 16 pathways were significantly enriched, mainly including phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, sesquiterpenoid and triterpenoid biosynthesis, and biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acids, etc. (Fig. 5C). In the biological process GO category, the overdominant genes were mainly enriched in entries such as zinc II ion transport, oxylipin metabolic process, and oxylipin biosynthetic process (Fig. 5D). In terms of molecular function, they were mainly enriched in entries such as peptidase inhibitor activity, peptidase regulator activity, and endopeptidase inhibitor activity. In terms of cell components, they were mainly enriched in entries such as integral component of membrane, intrinsic component of membrane, and extracellular region (Fig. 5D and Table S8).

Identification of allele-specific expression genes and their mechanistic role in root heterosis

We utilized 2,415,192 aa×bb-type SNPs between two parental lines to identify allele-specific expression genes (ASEGs) in the root system. This SNP set was filtered according to specified criteria, yielding 65,535 SNPs corresponding to 13,518 genes. Following subsequent differential analysis using DESeq2 software, a total of 7,098 differential SNPs were obtained, corresponding to 3,419 genes. After further removing 700 SNPs with inconsistent expression directions on the same gene (corresponding to 173 genes), we ultimately identified 3,246 ASEGs (Table S9 and Fig. 6 A). These genes accounted for only 7.68% of all genes, of which 1,640 were biased toward HD5 expression and 1,606 were biased toward M9 expression (Fig. 6B).

Identification and characterization analysis of ASEGs. A Identification process of ASEGs; B Proportion of ASEGs in total genes and direction of allele-specific expression; C, D Expression profile analysis of ASEGs in HD5 and M9; E KEGG enrichment analysis of ASEGs; F GO (biological process) enrichment analysis of ASEGs

To explore the mechanism of allele-specific expression, we first checked the expression level of ASEGs in the two parents. Interestingly, genes biased toward HD5 expression in the hybrid, on the whole, also exhibited higher expression levels in the parental line HD5; conversely, genes biased toward M9 expression in the hybrid, on the whole, exhibited higher expression levels in the parental line M9 (Fig. 6 C). To further explore the relationship between gene expression differences in parental lines and allele-specific expression (ASE) in hybrids, we analyzed the direction of ASE in hybrid F1 for three gene categories: HD5-highly expressed genes, M9-highly expressed genes, and genes with non-significant expression differences between parents. The results showed that among HD5-highly expressed genes, those biased toward HD5 expression in hybrid F1 were significantly more numerous than those biased toward M9 expression. Conversely, among M9-highly expressed genes, those biased toward M9 expression in hybrid F1 were far more abundant than those biased toward HD5 expression. For genes with non-significant expression differences between parents, the number of genes biased toward HD5 expression versus M9 expression in hybrid F1 showed no significant discrepancy (Fig. 6D). This suggested that the expression levels of most of these genes in the hybrids were inherited from the expression pattern in the parents. This result is consistent with the studies by Li et al. [23] in potato and Shao et al. [14] in rice.

To investigate the role of the ASEGs in heterosis, we performed Gene Ontology (GO) and KEGG enrichment analyses using all ASEGs. A total of 16 pathways were significantly enriched in KEGG (p < 0.05), primarily including ribosome, pyruvate metabolism, and phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, among others (Fig. 6E and Table S10). Among the GO-enriched biological processes, significant enrichment was observed in small molecule metabolic process, organonitrogen compound metabolic process, and oxoacid metabolic process (Fig. 6 F and Table S10). In terms of molecular functions, ASEGs were mainly involved in peroxidase activity, antioxidant activity, and catalytic activity. Regarding cellular components, significant enrichment was noted in cytoplasm, cytoplasmic part, and cytosol (Table S10).

The phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway is involved in the formation of heterosis in the potato root system

In the above study, enrichment analyses were performed using four gene sets: non-additive expressed genes, dominant expressed genes, overdominant expressed genes, and allele-specific expressed genes. We further constructed network Venn diagrams for both KEGG and GO enrichment analyses (Fig. 7A, B). The results revealed that the overdominant genes and non-additive expressed genes exhibited the highest overlap in enrichment results, with 10 shared pathways. This was followed by 6 overlapping pathways between dominant genes and non-additive genes, 5 between dominant genes and overdominant genes, 4 between non-additive expressed genes and allele-specific expressed genes, 2 between overdominant expressed genes and allele-specific expressed genes, and 1 between dominant genes and allele-specific expressed genes.

From the perspective of pathways, linoleic acid metabolism, alpha-Linolenic acid metabolism, and the MAPK signaling pathway-plant were co-enriched by three gene sets. Notably, phenylpropanoid biosynthesis was co-enriched by all four gene sets (Fig. 7A). In the biological processes of GO enrichment, reactive oxygen species metabolic process, hydrogen peroxide metabolic process, and hydrogen peroxide catabolic process were simultaneously enriched by all four gene sets. Intriguingly, the majority of genes enriched in these three pathways were also identified within phenylpropanoid biosynthesis (Fig. 7B). Based on these findings, we propose that phenylpropanoid biosynthesis participates in regulating root development, thereby mediating the formation of heterosis in potatoes.

To further validate this result, we analyzed the expression levels of 483 genes within the phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway. The results showed that the average expression level of genes was highest in F1, which was significantly higher than that in HD5; HD5, in turn, was highly significantly higher than M9 (Fig. 7C and Table S11).

Summary of enrichment results of four gene sets. KEGG (A) and GO (B) enrichment results of the four gene sets. C Average expression levels of 483 genes in the phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway in F1 and two parents. D Expression profiles of genes in the lignin biosynthesis pathway

Lignin primarily accumulates in the secondary cell walls of plants, providing mechanical support and participating in the formation of xylem vessels responsible for transporting water and mineral elements [39]. The development of Casparian strips in the endodermis of plant roots and the xylem of vascular bundles both require the synthesis and accumulation of lignin [40]. Lignin biosynthesis represents an important branch pathway within phenylpropanoid metabolism [41]. Therefore, we analyzed in detail the expression profiles of 141 genes within the four gene sets in the lignin biosynthesis pathway (Fig. 7D and Table S12).

The first three steps of the phenylpropanoid pathway constitute the general phenylpropanoid pathway, and the reactions within this pathway are catalyzed by phenylalanine ammonia lyase (PAL), cinnamic acid 4-hydroxylase (C4H), and 4-coumarate-CoA ligase (4CL) [41]. Here, PAL-encoding genes were primarily derived from the non-additive expression gene set, while C4H and 4CL genes mainly originated from the allele-specific expression gene set. Hydroxycinnamoyl-CoA shikimate/quinate hydroxycinnamoyl transferase (HCT), a bifunctional enzyme, catalyzes the retransfer of the caffeoyl moiety to CoA and directs the metabolic flux from the general phenylpropanoid pathway to monolignol biosynthesis. P-Coumaroyl shikimate 3′-hydroxylase (C3′H) catalyzes the hydroxylation of p-coumaroyl shikimate to form caffeoyl shikimate. Caffeoyl-CoA 3-O-methyltransferase (CCoAOMT) mediates the methyl-transfer reaction in monolignol biosynthesis, converting caffeoyl-CoA to feruloyl-CoA. Additionally, CCoAOMT is responsible for catalyzing the formation of sinapoyl CoA from 5-hydroxyferuloyl CoA. Caffeate/5-hydroxyferulate 3-O-methyltransferase (COMT) participates in the methylation of diverse phenylpropanoid metabolites during monolignol biosynthesis. Ferulate 5-hydroxylase (F5H) catalyzes the nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate- and O₂-dependent hydroxylation of various phenylpropanoid metabolites. Here, HCT genes primarily exhibited dominant expression. C3′H and CCoAOMT genes were predominantly derived from non-additive expression gene sets. Three COMT genes were derived from non-additive expression, dominant expression, and allele-specific expression gene sets. One F5H gene belonged to the non-additive expression gene set. Cinnamoyl-CoA reductase (CCR), the first enzyme catalyzing the monolignol biosynthesis pathway, mediates the reduction reaction to synthesize p-coumaraldehyde, caffealdehyde, coniferaldehyde, 5-hydroxyconiferaldehyde, and sinapaldehyde. The final step of monolignol biosynthesis is catalyzed by cinnamyl alcohol dehydrogenase (CAD), leading to the formation of p-coumaryl alcohol, caffeyl alcohol, coniferyl alcohol, 5-hydroxyconiferyl alcohol, and sinapyl alcohol. Most CCR- and CAD-encoding genes exhibited overdominant expression. The three basic units of natural lignin polymers—p-hydroxyphenyl (H), guaiacyl (G), and syringyl (S)—are derived from p-coumaryl alcohol, coniferyl alcohol, and sinapyl alcohol, respectively. After being transported to the cell wall, these monolignols are polymerized by peroxidases (POD) and/or laccases to form lignin. In our pathway, 77 POD-encoding genes were identified, with the vast majority originating from non-additive expression gene sets and exhibiting dominant expression.

Screening for heterosis candidate genes that root system by the integration of QTL Mapping and RNA-seq

The F2 population undergoes genetic segregation, containing both parental and heterozygous types, which confers advantages in the mapping of heterotic loci and evaluation of heterotic effects [37]. In our previous study, we derived an F2 population containing 170 lines from the cross between HD5 and M9, and constructed a high-density genetic map via whole-genome resequencing (Fig. 8B, unpublished). This map is of high quality, containing a total of 4,646 bin markers (Fig. 8C) with a total genetic distance of 1075.69 cM (Fig. 8D). Except for one large gap observed on each of chromosomes 5, 6, and 12, the marker density is relatively uniform across the remaining chromosomes (Fig. 8E).

In a previous study, we conducted hydroponic culture for the F2 population. Root traits, including total root length, root diameter, root volume, root surface area, and maximum root length, were investigated at 14 days, with fresh root weight added to the measurements at 28 days. Under three replicated environments, a total of 47 QTLs were identified by this genetic map (Fig. 8F and Table S13, unpublished). Following the method described by Liu et al. [37]. By comparing the heterozygous genotype with both homozygous genotypes, we employed the degree of dominance (d/a) of each QTL to estimate the single-locus heterotic effects. The results showed that overdominance and complete–incomplete dominance constituted the potato root primary heterotic effects (Fig. 8F).

To screen for candidate heterosis genes, we performed an intersection analysis between the genes within QTL intervals (Table S14) and genes in the lignin biosynthesis pathway (Table S12) based on the technical route (Fig. 8A). Notably, 15 candidate genes were identified within 11 genetic locus intervals (Fig. 9C). Further, based on gene annotation, six most promising candidate genes were selected: DM8C01G33280 (C3’H), DM8C02G28580 (CCoAOMT), DM8C02G29270 (POD), DM8C03G31680 (CCR), DM8C05G27310 (PAL), and DM8C12G18910 (4CL), all of which encode key enzymes in the lignin biosynthesis pathway. These genes were subjected to quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) analysis, and the results showed that the expression trends of all genes were consistent across the two analyses (Fig. 9D).

Discussion

Genetic basis of root system heterosis in potato

Heterosis is widely present in plants and frequently applied in crop breeding to improve crop yield [42]. However, there are few reports on heterosis in clonally propagated crops [23]. Especially in diploid potatoes, the self-incompatibility and inbreeding depression characteristics make it difficult to create inbred line materials with high genomic homozygosity [21], which has severely hindered the utilization of heterosis and research on its genetic mechanisms in potatoes. To date, only two reports have systematically analyzed heterosis in diploid potatoes. Specifically, Li et al. [23] integrated transcriptomic, metabolomic, and DNA methylation data from seedlings, flowers, and tubers of parents and hybrids, and found that the initial establishment of heterosis in diploid potatoes is mainly due to dominance complementation. They further developed the F1 into an F2 population to analyze the genetic basis of heterosis, revealing that positive heterotic effects of yield-related QTLs and negative heterotic effects of metabolite QTLs contribute to yield advantage formation [24]. In this study, we used two highly homozygous diploid materials, HD5 and M9, to create F1 and F2 populations for heterosis research. Although both parental materials belong to Solanum chacoense, our hybrid F1 HM exhibited strong heterosis throughout most of the growth period, particularly in yield, disease resistance, seedling vigor, and root systems (Fig. 1; Table 1). The F2 population showed significant segregation of root development-related phenotypes, making it an ideal material for analyzing the genetic basis of heterosis.

Current heterosis mechanism research still focuses more on yield [4, 7, 15, 37, 43–45] and abiotic stress resistance [16, 34, 46, 47]. As the “invisible half” of plants underground, roots are often neglected compared to aboveground parts, and studies on the heterosis mechanism of root development are even fewer. For example, Zhai et al. [48] performed comparative transcriptomic analysis on roots of the super rice hybrid combination “Xieyou” 9308 at tillering and heading stages using RNA-seq, and found that 66.59% of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between hybrids and parents were downregulated at the tillering stage, while 64.41% were upregulated at the heading stage. These DEGs were significantly enriched in pathways related to carbohydrate metabolism, phytohormone signal transduction, and other processes. Pi et al. [49] analyzed root heterosis in tobacco using transcriptomics and found a high proportion of overdominantly expressed genes. Enrichment analysis of these overdominantly expressed genes showed that “Plant hormone signal transduction”, “Phenylpropanoid biosynthesis”, “MAPK signaling pathway - plant”, and “Starch and sucrose metabolism” pathways were associated with root development. In this study, we found that the proportion of dominant genes was the highest, reaching 69%. In this study, we for the first time employed F1 and F2 populations to investigate root heterosis in diploid potatoes. Enrichment analysis of four gene sets related to root heterosis in potatoes showed that “Linoleic acid metabolism”, “alpha-Linolenic acid metabolism”, and “MAPK signaling pathway - plant” were co-enriched by three gene sets, while “Phenylpropanoid biosynthesis” was co-enriched by all four gene sets. Additionally, we calculated the heterotic effects of 47 QTLs mapped using the F2 population and found that the number of overdominant QTLs was the highest, followed by 16 QTLs with complete-incomplete dominance (Fig. 8F and Table S13). This result is inconsistent with the F1 transcriptomic analysis. We speculate that the main reason may be that QTLs involve single-gene levels, while F1 transcriptomic DEGs are more numerous. Overall, the two are not contradictory; both are due to expression-level complementation of genes leading to the formation of root heterosis.

The role of lignin biosynthesis in heterosis

Analyzing the relationship between gene expression and heterosis using transcriptome sequencing represents a critical direction in the study of heterosis genetic mechanisms. Non-additive expression genes, dominant expression genes, over-dominant expression genes, and allele-specific expression genes are all considered to be closely associated with the formation of heterosis. In previous studies, most investigations focused on one or two of these sets for in-depth analysis. In this study, we performed enrichment analysis on all four gene sets. Notably, KEGG and GO enrichment analyses of these four gene sets were all significantly enriched in the “phenylpropanoid biosynthesis” pathway. Detailed analysis of genes within this pathway found that these genes primarily originate from the key branch of lignin biosynthesis within the phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway. Thus, we propose that lignin biosynthesis is involved in mediating the formation of root heterosis in potatoes.

Lignin plays a crucial role in plant growth, development, and stress adaptation, primarily accumulating in secondary cell walls where it can constitute up to 30% of cell wall components. It is particularly abundant in tracheids, sclereids, interfascicular fibers, and xylem tissues [50]. Lignification is one of the hallmark events of root maturation, encompassing the formation of endodermal Casparian strips and xylem lignification in vascular bundles [40]. In wheat, TaANR1 and TaMAD25 coordinately regulate the expression of lignin biosynthesis-related genes and lignin accumulation in roots, thereby promoting root development [51]. Gao et al. [52] reported that six dirigent proteins directly participate in lignin biosynthesis, and mutants with complete loss of Casparian strip lignification exhibit severe growth retardation. Multiple enzymes play key roles in the lignin biosynthesis process. Notably, genes such as PAL [53], 4CL [54], CCR [55], CCD [55], HCT [56], C3’H [57], COMT [58], F5H [59], CSE [60, 61], and POD [62] have been demonstrated to be involved in lignin biosynthesis.

Using QTL mapping and pathway analysis, six most probable root heterosis-related genes were identified

Through analysis of the transcriptome sequencing data from parental lines and F1 hybrid, a total of 4 gene sets associated with heterosis were identified (Fig. 9A). These gene sets collectively comprised 10,109 genes (Fig. 9A, B). Using DM8.1 as the reference genome, 4,185 candidate genes were obtained within the QTLs and their flanking intervals were mapped in this study. By further integrating with 141 genes involved in the lignin biosynthesis pathway, 15 overlapping genes were screened out (Fig. 9C). Based on gene annotation, 6 final candidate genes were ultimately identified (Fig. 9D). These 6 genes were DM8C01G33280 (C3’H), DM8C02G28580 (CCoAOMT), DM8C02G29270 (POD), DM8C03G31680 (CCR), DM8C05G27310 (PAL), and DM8C12G18910 (4CL), respectively. C3’H is a cytochrome P450-dependent monooxygenase and catalyzes the formation of caffeoyl shikimate through hydroxylation of p-coumaroyl shikimate, was downregulated via RNA interference in transgenic hybrid poplar, resulting in reduced total lignin content [57]. CCoAOMT has been recognized as a key regulatory gene in the biosynthesis of guaiacyl (G) lignin monomers [63]. Bu et al. [64] identified the CCoAOMT gene family within the L. chinense genome, and overexpression of LcCCoAOMT8 in transgenic tobacco plants was shown to enhance CCoAOMT activity and lignin content, while promoting xylem development. Peroxidase is considered to be involved in the final stage of lignin biosynthesis [65]. Overexpression of POD increases the content of phenol and lignin in plants [62]. CCR and CAD are the last two enzymes that function in the monolignols synthesis pathway, while the disruption of their function also affects the lignin content [55]. In the Arabidopsis thaliana quadruple mutant (pal1/pal2/pal3/pal4) of PAL genes, lignin content exhibited a 20–25% reduction compared to the wild-type plants [53]. Gui et al. [54] reported that suppressing the Os4CL3 gene expression led to a notable decrease in both lignin content and plant height in rice. In conclusion, we propose that these genes are involved in root system lignin biosynthesis, thereby contributing to the regulation of heterosis in the root system of hybrid F1.

The mining and identification of heterosis-related genes serve as crucial evidence for in-depth analysis of the heterosis formation mechanism at the single-gene level and constitute the genetic basis for the efficient utilization of heterosis in the era of molecular design breeding. In rice, a large number of genes associated with traits that directly influence heterosis performance, such as yield, plant architecture, growth period, and fertility restoration, have been cloned. The complementation or interaction of elite alleles of these genes in hybrid combinations forms the molecular basis for heterosis formation [4]. Investigating the functions of the six candidate genes within the QTL intervals in this study will facilitate a deeper understanding of the molecular mechanism underlying heterosis. Furthermore, these six genes also provide direct targets for molecular design breeding aimed at utilizing root heterosis in potatoes. In conclusion, through traditional molecular marker-assisted selection breeding or gene knockout, insertion, or replacement using CRISPR/Cas technology, aim to achieve rapid pyramiding of elite alleles and enable efficient, customized improvement of important agronomic traits, thereby achieving the goal of fully utilizing heterosis.

Conclusions

This study systematically investigated the root transcriptomes of the diploid potato hybrid F1 HM and its parental seedlings using RNA-Seq technology. By applying diverse analytical methods, we characterized non-additively expressed genes, dominantly expressed genes, overdominantly expressed genes, and non-allele-specifically expressed genes, and compared these gene sets with root trait QTLs. These investigations deepened our understanding of the genetic mechanisms underlying heterosis in root development, and the identified genes provided valuable clues for elucidating the molecular basis of heterosis in potato roots.
