A novel pregabalin functionalized salicylaldehyde derivative loaded mesoporous silica nano scaffold: a prospective carrier for targeting inflammatory cytokine storm
Authors: Yasir Mehmood, Syeda Momena Rizvi, Musarrat Ijaz, Tamseela Shahzadi, Hira Shahid, Shabbir Ahmed, Akhtar Rasul, Javed Iqbal, Abdulrahman A Almehizia, Amir Bouallegue, Musaab Dauelbait, Esmael M Alyami
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractActive pharmaceutical ingredients (APIs) have poor efficacy and low bloodstream and target site concentrations due to their limited water solubility. Pregabalin is essential product to control pain and inflammation. We have synthesized novel pregabalin derivative called gabsali ((S, E)-3-(((2-hydroxybenzylidene)amino)methyl)-5-methylhexanoic acid. We have synthesized it by using salicylaldehyde to functionalize pregabalin (PG). The research was divided into two parts. The first part of the process was making a new kind of pregabalin called gabsali (GS). Second, MSNs were created using the sol-gel technique and then the synthesized GS was added to them. This study introduces a unique drug delivery method that improves bioavailability, stability, and anti-inflammatory activity by incorporating chemically modified pregabalin (GS) into engineered MSNs. This strategy has not been documented in the literature before. Zeta sizer, scanning electron microscopy (SEM), transmission electron microscopy (TEM), Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), differential scanning calorimetry (DSC), BET analysis, and high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) were some of the modern tools used to determine particle size, shape, drug release, and compatibility. Under all tested conditions, the inclusion compound showed a considerable improvement in dissolving rate relative to the crystalline medicine in in vitro dissolving assays. The produced MSNs had a particle size of about 500 nm and were nano-sized, spherical. The FTIR spectroscopy was used to examine the chemical GS. The medicine was successfully added into blank MSNs, resulting in a decrease in both their specific surface area (602.5 ± 0.7 m²/g) and pore width (5.9 nm). In order to identify the potential benefits of GS, this study evaluated a preclinical model of inflammation that was created using ovalbumin (OVA). In a comparison to pregabalin, its anti-inflammatory activity was tested at doses of 50, 75, and 100 mg/kg. Using reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR), the levels of inflammatory mediators (cytokines IL-2 and IL-6) in the blood were measured and discovered to be significantly reduced. Cell viability was evaluated using the MTT assay, which showed a higher IC50 compared to pregabalin, the unmodified medicine. According to in vivo pharmacokinetic studies, MSN-GS significantly increased bioavailability compared to the pure drug. Using MSN-GS, the aforementioned results clearly show biocompatibility, improved in-vivo bioavailability, and satisfactory in-vitro performance.Keywords: Pregabalin, Solubility, Bioavailability, Mesoporous silica nanoparticles

Introduction

Pregabalin, whose molecular formula is (S)-3-(aminomethyl)-5-methylhexanoic acid, is chemically similar to the inhibitory neurotransmitter GABA. With its 2004 European approval, this medicine has since been used to treat peripheral neuropathic pain and partial seizures in epileptic patients as an adjuvant treatment [1]. Although it differs in its effect, pregabalin is structurally similar to the naturally occurring neurotransmitter gamma-aminobutyric acid. As analgesic, anticonvulsant, and anxiety-relieving agent, it is highly effective [2, 3]. One of the key benefits of pregabalin is its reliability, ease of use, and high patient tolerance. Research strongly suggests that it can reduce heat hyperalgesia, chemical-induced nociception, and carrageenan or formalin-induced peripheral inflammation [4]. We created a novel pregabalin derivative in this study by coupling the amine group with salicylaldehyde via a straightforward imination procedure [5]. The pregablin derivative was made by reacting it with salicylaldehyde, a chemical that can produce imines. The new salicylaldehyde derivative gabsali (GS), which is functionalized with pregabalin, was formed as a consequence of this process. The study’s primary objective was to determine GS could be more useful as an anti-inflammatory or pain management drug by reducing inflammation, fever, and discomfort. The derivative produced in our work (GS) has substantially less water solubility than pregabalin itself, which is known to be highly water soluble (around 36 mg/mL). The pregabalin-based derivative’s decreased solubility presented a formulation problem, requiring the employment of an appropriate nanocarrier technology to improve its bioavailability and aqueous solubility. For this scientist has made several system which can use for enhancement of solubility. Recent breakthroughs in nanotechnology, a multidisciplinary scientific subject focused on the development of nanomaterials for diverse uses, including the efficient transport of medicinal molecules, have resulted in the creation of nanomedicines [2]. Nanomedicines primarily focus on drug delivery applications, emphasizing the applications and physicochemical properties of organic and inorganic nanocarriers, such as nanoemulsions [6–8], polymeric micelles [9, 10], nanoparticles [11, 12], nanotubes [11] and silica based nano carriers [13].

Scientists have recently become very interested in mesoporous silica nanoparticles (MSNs) because they offer appealing characteristics, including biocompatibility, non-toxicity, excellent cellular absorption, the ability to load drugs, and the ability to release drugs in a controlled manner [14, 15]. These favourable features make MSNs promising contenders for numerous usages, including drug delivery [16, 17], theranostic [18, 19], biocatalysis [20, 21] and tissue engineering [22, 23]. Likewise, one of the primary benefits of these materials is improvement in oral bioavailability of low water soluble medicine [24]. In MSNs, particle morphology [25], pore size [26–30], surface area [31], particle size, surface coating [32, 33], loading method [34–37] and drug/excipient ratio are the key features which control drug dissolution. A crucial element in promoting the application of MSNs (Mesoporous Silica Nanoparticles) in medicine and pharmaceuticals is the capacity to regulate their pore size. Only molecules with diameters less than 10 nanometers can be contained in MSNs [38]. This means that while selecting an encapsulating chemical, it is crucial to take into account not only the molecular size of the drug but also the pore size of the MSNs.

Knowing how well our MSN-GS formulation works therapeutically requires assessing inflammatory cytokines including interleukin-2 (IL-2) and interleukin-6 (IL-6) [39]. During inflammation, cytokines are essential for mediating and intensifying the immune response. A cytokine storm, which is the term used to describe the excessive or unregulated production of these pro-inflammatory cytokines, can cause significant tissue damage and accelerate the course of inflammatory disorders. Therefore, cytokine profiling was crucial in verifying that our formulation provides higher immunomodulatory control, which is a crucial prerequisite for treating illnesses caused by inflammation, in addition to increasing solubility and pharmacokinetics [40]. This enhances our work’s therapeutic value, particularly in conditions like neuroinflammation, chronic pain syndromes, and autoimmune illnesses that are marked by increased cytokine activity.

The structure of the chemical was validated by the utilization of a variety of spectroscopic methods. This pregabalin-salicylaldehyde derivative will be put into drug delivery systems (MSNs) in order to facilitate more precise drug release control. When the compound has been subjected to a number of physicochemical characterizations, it will then be tested for its anti-inflammatory activities in mice utilizing well-established testing methods.

Materials and methods

Chemicals

Dae-Jung Chemicals of Sasang-gu, Busan, South Korea, supplied both the surfactant or cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB, 99.0%), and the alkoxide precursor or tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS, 99.0%). Additionally, salicylaldehyde was acquired from Dae-Jung Chemicals, which is located in Sasang-gu, Busan, South Korea. Ameer & Adnan Pharmaceutical Pvt. Ltd., located in Lahore, Pakistan, was kind enough to supply us with pregabalin that had a purity of 99.8%. Acetonitrile with a purity level of at least 98% was acquired from Dae-Jung Chemicals, located in Sasang-gu, Busan, South Korea. Every additional substance that was utilized in this investigation was of an analytical grade, and it was utilized in its original form.

Synthesis of pregabalin derivative GS

A straightforward reaction between pregabalin and salicylaldehyde was executed in order to produce the pregabalin derivative known as GS, also known as GS. At the same time as a round-bottom flask was filled with a total volume of 20 milliliters of methanol, a minute quantity of sodium metal (0.018 g, 0.00078 moles) was added while the flask was continuously stirred. A total of 0.1 g (0.00063 mol) of pregabalin was added once the dissolution was finished, and the mixture was agitated at room temperature without being heated. After that, an equal amount of salicylaldehyde (0.00063 mol) was added to the flask, and it was covered with aluminum foil and agitated for a further twenty-four hours. The combination that was produced was allowed to dry at room temperature in open air. The paste that was formed was then washed with n-hexane and filtered ((three washes of 20 mL each at room temperature), and drying conditions (vacuum drying at 40 °C for 12 h), which resulted in the production of an amorphous yellow powder. Additional purification and characterization of the substance were carried out utilizing a variety of methods.

Preparation of MSN-GS

A few modest adjustments were made to the sol-gel process that was used to construct MSNs in an oil/water phase, as described in with a few minor changes [41, 42]. In order to make sol-gel, 500 mg of CTAB was dissolved in a combination of water and acetonitrile (1:1) at a temperature of 35 degrees Celsius. During the synthesis of blank MSNs, acetonitrile and water were utilized to maximize the sol-gel process; acetonitrile enhanced the dispersion and uniform pore creation, while water served as a hydrolysis medium for TEOS.

The pH was then corrected to 9.0 using 0.1 M sodium hydroxide. While the reaction was taking place, the solution was constantly agitated at a speed of 1500 revolutions per minute while a nitrogen flow was maintained. After a clear solution was formed, a certain quantity of TEOS, which was five milliliters, was added to the solution in a drop-by-drop fashion. Following the passage of a few minutes, the solution turned opaque, which served as an indication that the reaction had begun and that a gel was being formed. We used a 0.1 μm porous filter to filter the white gel, and then we washed it with deionized water. This process was carried out under nitrogen pressure. Following that, the gel was transformed into a fine powder and allowed to dry at room temperature for an entire night. After being calcined at 500for six hours, the powder was given the name MSN. This was done in order to entirely eliminate the surfactant. Additionally, GS was loaded into nano MSN carriers using the process of adsorption, which included stirring and soaking, as was previously reported [43]. A concentrated solution (1 mg/mL) of GS was made in methanol (500 mg/500mL) and ethanol 500 mg/500mL. After that, MSNs were added into this solution in a 1:1 (w/w) ratio (1 g:1 g) After being stirred for three hours continuously, the mixture was immersed for three days. Following that, a membrane filter (0.1 μm) was used to filter the particles, and 100 mL of methanol washed over them three times to remove the GS that had adhered to their surface. The samples underwent a second drying process in a vacuum oven at 50 °C after an initial night of drying at room temperature. The designation given to these charged particles was MSN-GS.

Low processing temperatures, adjustable porosity, and the capacity to encapsulate delicate substances without thermal deterioration are just a few benefits of the sol-gel approach for creating nanoparticles. Because of these characteristics, it is quite versatile for pharmaceutical applications, particularly for commercial scalability.

Drug loading and entrapment efficiency

A solution containing 100 mg of particles and 100 mL of methanol was agitated for one hour to ascertain the concentration of MSN-GS. The Agela C18 column (250 × 4.6 mm, 5 μm) and RP-HPLC (Shimadzu LC 20 A, 1000 pump, 2500 UV-VIS detector, Japan) were used for the quantitative measurement of the extracted GS, which was carried out at a wavelength of 270 nm. The area under the curve was computed to analyze the extracted GS. Additionally, the flow rate of the mobile phase was maintained at 1 milliliter per minute, and it was composed of acetonitrile, methanol, and ammonium acetate buffer in a ratio of 20:40:40 (volume to volume). We compared the peaks that were formed after each injection of 20 µL of analyte with the standard, which consisted of three individuals. What follows is the computation for drug loading and efficiency equivalences 1 and 2, respectively.

Microscopic analysis

The MSN-GS surface morphology and shape along with GS was investigated by SEM (VEGA3 TESCAN) after gold coating [33, 44]. The TEM pictures were captured using a field emission electron gun running at 200 kV on a JEOL JEM 2100 F analytical electron microscope. We used AFM to check the coatings’ morphology and homogeneity.

Dynamic light scattering (DLS)

Particle size and zeta potential of MSN-GS was measured by using Zetasizer Nano ZS at 25 °C using water as dispersant [45].

Nitrogen – adsorption/desorption

Following the degassing of both samples at a temperature of -200 for a period of twenty-four hours prior to the analysis, the Barrett-Joyner-Halenda (BJH) model was utilized to find out the pore size and pore volume distribution based on the desorption isotherm. Adsorption data was gathered at relative pressure (p/p°), and Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) was employed for the purpose of computing the BSSA. At a temperature of -196.15 the specific surface area (BSSA), pore volume, and size of blank MSNs and MSN-GS were measured using N2 adsorption/desorption analysis (Micromeritics, Gemini VII 2390 surface area analyzer) [44].

Fourier transform infra-red spectroscopy (FTIR)

FTIR spectra of drug pregabalin derivative (GS), blank MSNs and MSN-GS were obtained by utilizing FTIR spectrometer (Nicolet-IS7ATR-FTIR, Thermo Scientific-USA). With a resolution of 2 cm− 1, FTIR spectra were acquired over the 500–4000 cm− 1 spectral region [46].

Thermal analysis

Using a nitrogen purge of 40 mL/min and a heating rate of 10 °C/min, the GS, blank MSNs, and MSN-GS were analyzed by DSC and TGA in a Q1000, TA Instruments, USA [33, 44].

In-vitro dissolution

In-vitro dissolution was employed to examine the release profiles of pure pregabalin, GS, and MSN-GS. The conditions were maintained according to the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) guidelines, specifically at 75 rpm ± 1 and 37 °C. Pregabalin (100 mg), GS (100 mg), and MSN-GS (equivalent to 100 mg GS) were subjected to two distinct dissolution media with pH levels of 1.2 and 7.4 in a USP type II apparatus (Model-DT 120, Cure apparatus, Pakistan) for 180 min. The dissolving liquid was refilled with an equal volume at predetermined intervals after samples were extracted. The concentration of pure pregabalin, GS, and GS inside MSN was determined using a calibration curve that had already been developed [33].

Viability of cells

We used an assay called MTT to measure the cell viability. Each well in the 96-well plate was supplemented with 100 µL of medium after the HEP G2 cells were inoculated at a density of 1 × 10^4 cells per well. After that, the plates were left to incubate for an entire day. The cells were cultured for twenty-four hours in a controlled environment and subjected to either pure pregabalin or MSN-GS. The absorbance at 570 nm was measured using a multi-detection microplate reader (SynergyTM HT-BioTek-Instruments Inc., USA). We carried out the research in three separate instances, and the findings were reported in the form of a percentage of viable cells in both the treatment group and the baseline control group [47].

Determination of Pro-Inflammatory cytokines IL-2, IL-6 mRNA expression

Cytokines IL-2 and IL-6 that promote inflammation are mostly produced by macrophages that have been activated, and they play a role in the process of making inflammatory responses more intense [48, 49]. Pregabalin has been shown to suppress the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines like IL-2 and IL-6, which are known to promote inflammation. We have study to evaluate the IL-2, IL-6 in rats and compare GS effect against pregabalin. The Ethics Committee of Rashid Lateef Khan University in Pakistan gave its approval for the study (Study No: 19681 − 2024). We used twelve Sprague-Dawley rats, ranging in weight from 150 to 250 g, with three animals in each of the four groups. The animals were kept in a controlled environment at the RLKU animal home, where the temperature was 26 ± 2 °C and the humidity was 40–60%. Light and dark cycles of twelve hours each were also enforced. The rats had access to water and a standard food [50]. Ionomycin we have used to enhance inflammatory cytokines. Due to the toxicity and wide range of tissues that ionomycin, a calcium ionophore, can impact, there are few well-established dosing procedures [51]. Three groups were sensitized on day 0 with 0.1 mg/kg ionomycin dissolved in phosphate buffer saline (PBS) (1 mg/10mL). The rats were given an intraperitoneal (IP) injection challenge of 0.1 mg/kg of ionomycin up to 14 days. The rats in group I (Control) were given only PBS as a challenge. After this we have taken blood sample of each groups to observe inflammatory cytokines. When we observed in 3 groups which were taken injection have increase inflammatory cytokines we have started to give pregabalin and MSN-GS were administered orally 100 mg/70kg daily for 3 days. One group was diseased to which did not administered pregablin and one group was controlled.

Accelerated stability study

The goal of the expedited research was to find out how stable the medication molecules were in MSNs. Under conditions of forced degradation, the stability of MSN-GS was investigated [52]. MSN-GS particles were filled into hard gelatin capsules along with blister and were placed in stability chamber with 40 ± 2 °C and 70% relative humidity over a period of 45 days [53]. Marketed pregabalin capsules, GS within capsule form and MSN-GS capsules were analyzed after 45 days and investigated for drug contents via HPLC analysis.

Statistical analysis

The data was reviewed using the GraphPad Prism v.6 program and presented as the average plus or minus the standard deviation. One-way ANOVA was employed to assess quantitative variables. The post hoc Tukey’s test was employed to compare all groups with one another.

Dynamic light scattering

In accordance with the explanation provided by Powers et al., we utilized DLS in order to estimate the particle size, size distribution, and zeta potential of MSN-GS [56]. With regard to MSN-GS, the average particle size, PDI, and zeta potential were 486 nm, 0.237 (as shown in Fig. 3), and − 7.29mV, respectively [57]. Our SEM results, which were described in the previous section, are further supported by the fact that the low PDI of drug-loaded particles suggested that they were of uniform size. One important function of the MSNs’ negative surface charge (-7.29 mV) is to bind and attract medicament molecules, particularly those with positive or partially positive groups. The efficiency of drug adsorption and loading is enhanced by the electrostatic interaction between the negatively charged surface of the MSN and the positively charged drug molecules.

This charge can affect molecular interactions like as hydrogen bonding and van der Waals forces, which can improve medication retention even for pharmaceuticals that are neutral or mildly polar. Additionally, surface charge is essential for biological interactions as well as colloidal stability. In physiological settings, the modest electrostatic repulsion between particles caused by the moderately negative zeta potential (–7.29 mV) for MSN-GS helps to prevent aggregation and preserve dispersion stability. Furthermore, by electrostatic attraction, the negative surface charge improves the binding of positively charged or zwitterionic drug molecules, increasing the effectiveness of drug loading.

According to [54], zeta potential is also useful for predicting surface charge, stability, and the possibility of cellular contact with GS-loaded particles [58]. Nanocarriers that have a cationic charge are more likely to successfully interact with cellular membranes in comparison to those that have a negative charge [59]. Furthermore, charge on the particles prevents their aggregation and hence enhances their stability [60]. Furthermore, due to its optimal size, PDI, and charge, MSN-GS is suitable for drug administration while causing the least amount of toxicity to biological membranes.

Nitrogen adsorption/desorption studies

For the purpose of determining the BSSA, pore volume, and pore size distribution of MSN and MSN-GS, the nitrogen adsorption-desorption method was utilized. Prior to the loading of drugs, the MSN displayed a pore size of 5.9 ± 0.3 nm, a pore volume of 0.946 ± 0.045 cm³/g, and a BSSA of 602.5 ± 0.7 m²/g. It has been determined that this is an H1 hysteresis loop, which indicates that typical mesoporous channels are present [42, 55]. These parameters indicates mesoporous nature of particles as explained by [42, 61, 62]. Furthermore, an indication of its ability to host therapeutic molecules for a variety of drug delivery applications is provided by the high BSSA and pore volume. Following the loading of the antiviral medication BSSA, the pore volume and pore size decreased to 583.2 ± 4.8m2/g, 0.893 ± 0.029, and 5 ± 0.2 nm, respectively, as depicted in Figs. 4 and 5; Table 2. One possible explanation for this drop is that GS was loaded into pores. Our findings are supported by a large number of other research, such as Zhang et al., who loaded telmisartan into silica-based nanoparticles and showed a reduction in pore size and BSSA [33]. Acetonitrile and water were used to create a mixed solvent solution for the production of blank MSNs. The sol-gel procedure required water as a hydrolysis medium, and acetonitrile enhanced precursor dispersion and promoted the development of homogeneous, well-structured pores. The MSNs produced by this solvent combination had large pore volumes (0.982 ± 0.125 cm³/g) and high surface areas (619.5 ± 1.7 m²/g), both of which are essential for effective drug loading. Following GS loading, MSN-GS’s average pore size shrank from 3.8 to 2.7 nm, and its BET surface area and total pore volume dropped to 560.5 ± 3.8 m²/g and 0.795 ± 0.129 cm³/g, respectively. These decreases unequivocally show that GS was successfully encapsulated inside the MSN pores. Both the large surface area available for adsorption and the electrostatic interactions between the functional groups of GS and the negatively charged MSN surface promoted effective drug loading. All things considered, the solvent solution that was selected not only made it possible for mesoporous structures to develop, but it also improved their ability to efficiently hold drug molecules.

FTIR

The Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) study contains information not only about various chemical groups but also about the potential interactions between drug carriers. The FTIR spectra produced by salicylaldehyde, pregabalin, GS, MSNs, and MSN-GS are displayed in Fig. 6.

FTIR spectra of MSN and MSN-GS and pregablin, Salicylaldehyde, pregablin and Gabsali

In Fig. 6 Salicylaldehyde exhibited a broad peak at approximately 3400 cm⁻¹, which is correlated with the stretching vibration of the phenolic hydroxyl (-OH) group. Typical of phenols like salicylaldehyde, the broadness results from intramolecular or intermolecular hydrogen bonding. This peak verifies that the molecule has a hydroxyl group. An intense and distinct peak between 1660 and 1680 cm⁻¹ is thought to be caused by the stretching vibration of the aldehyde carbonyl (C = O). Because the bond order is significantly lower after conjugation with the aromatic ring, aldehyde C = O stretches tend to be less prominent than ketones. The primary peaks seen at 1434.26, 1686.23, 1626.93, 1508.11, and 2961.38 cm− 1 were observed in GS. Figure 6. The stretching vibration of O-H was shown by peaks at 2961.38 cm− 1. Likewise, the N-H bending vibration was shown by the typical peak at 1626.93 cm− 1. In addition, the C = C stretching and the C-H asymmetric stretching are corresponding to the maxima at 1434.26 and 1686.23 cm− 1, respectively.

At 797.97, 1053.78, 1636.88, and 3396.49 cm− 1, absorption bands of the silicate were detected in MSNs Fig. 6. In the instance of MSNs, a peak at 797.97 cm− 1 was observed, suggesting the existence of silicate absorption bands. Furthermore, the bending of Si-O-Si was shown by a peak at 1053.78 cm− 1 [63, 64]. A broader peak at 3396.49 cm− 1 indicated the silanol (Si-OH) symmetric stretching [65] and bending vibration at 1636 cm− 1 [42]. Pregablin showed O-H stretch peak at 3319.39 cm− 1, C-O stretch at 1744.55 cm− 1, C-H stretch at 2919.15 cm− 1 [66]. 1536.25 cm− 1 (N–O asymmetric stretch), 1459.85 cm− 1 (C–H bend), 1343.24 cm− 1 (N–O symmetric stretch), 1297.71 cm− 1 (C–O stretch), and 937.19 cm− 1 (O–H bend) peaks are specific for pregablin. According to the MSN spectrum, the FTIR spectrum of MSN-GS had a peak that was indicative of Si-O-Si bending at a frequency of 1063.34 cm− 1. In a similar manner, the MSN-GS exhibited distinct peaks at 1677.22, 1517.69, and 1478.19 cm− 1 due to the N-H bending vibration, the C = C-C aromatic ring stretching, and the C-H asymmetric stretching, respectively. These peaks revealed the existence of the drug in the MSN-GS that was generated, without any significant interaction.

When the drug is in a noncrystalline state, there is no possibility of detecting a decrease in the melting point of the drug [67, 68]. MSN-GS has an endothermic peak at 200.9 °C, which is the temperature at which it melts. This is because the GS on the surface of MSN has lost water. In MSN-GS, there is no endothermic peak at 50℃, which indicates that GS is in a non-evaporating state. However, a weak and broad endothermic peak develops at 100 °C in MSN-GS, which shows that the drug is in a noncrystalline condition when it is entrapped in MSN. The partial contact of the drug with the surface of mesoporous nanoparticles that are rich in silica groups may be responsible for the melting peak depression of the MSN-GS, which causes partial amorphization of GS [69].

In-vitro release studies

The release profiles of GS and MSN-GS particles was studied in two different dissolution media (pH 1.2) (pH 7.4) [70–74]. As shown in Fig. 9, the release rate of GS was rapid in 1.2 pH buffer (t50 2.12 h) and it was very slow in 7.4 pH buffer (t50 6.15 h).

Cell viability of HEP G2 cell line after exposure to MSN-GS for 24 h. Error bars indicate the standard deviation (n = 3)

The dissolution medium’s pH has a significant impact on how GS and MSN-GS release their contents. Because of its high solubility and basicity (pKb 13.5), GS releases 50% of its initial concentration in about 2 h when exposed to acidic circumstances (pH 1.2). At a neutral pH of 7.4, however, its release is much slower, taking six hours to achieve the same proportion of release. One recognized negative effect of using GS for an extended period of time is stomach irritation, which can be exacerbated by this pH-dependent release. At pH 1.2, the MSN-GS formulation showed a much slower release rate (t₅₀ = 2.5 h), suggesting that the silica matrix was protecting the formulation. The large surface area and organized pore structure of MSNs, which control drug release kinetics, as well as the confinement of GS inside their nanoscale pores, which serve as diffusion barriers, are probably the causes of this delay. In comparison to free GS, MSN-GS demonstrated a more steady and prolonged release at pH 7.4, indicating that it may be able to lessen burst release and extend drug availability.

Mesoporous silica nanoparticles (MSN) were loaded with GS to reduce this effect. A better controlled release profile is shown by MSN-GS. The release is 50% slower at pH 1.2 (2.5 h) than with GS, which is probably because the protective SiO2 matrix of MSN limits the amount of acidic environment. Its due to its confinement in nanoscale channels, and the large surface area of MSN, all of which improve dissolution and release kinetics. Drug release can be further modulated through electrostatic or covalent interactions, which are made possible by the dual-functional surfaces of MSN, which consist of inner pores and an outside surface. The dissolution enhancement is due to a number of factors, including: (a) the presence of the drug in amorphous form as a result of its interaction with the carrier; (b) the presence of the drug in confined channels, which reduces the size of the drug particles to the nanometer range, suppresses the crystallization of GS [67, 69, 75, 76]. MSN has two functional surfaces: an inner cylindrical surface that is produced by pores and an outer surface. Both surfaces can be selectively functionalized to increase loading and release properties, as well as to prevent release in acidic environments due to electrostatic binding or covalent bonding. As a result, these many elements work together to increase the pace at which the trapped medication dissolves. At both pH levels, the Fig. 9 clearly demonstrates that GS and MSN-GS have separate release trajectories, lending credence to these findings. With its protective function in mind, MSN-GS releases at a reduced rate at pH 1.2, but its enhanced dissolving capabilities are on full display at pH 7.4.

These characteristics are met or exceeded by our formulation, demonstrating that the MSN platform efficiently regulates GS release at different pH levels and maximizes its therapeutic potential while reducing gastrointestinal adverse effects.

Cell viability

Drug carriers intended to be used in human must be nontoxic, compatible [77] and stable in living system [78]. The human liver cell line was subjected to a 24-hour incubation period with different doses of MSN-GS, specifically 50, 100, 150, 200, and 400 µg/mL. This was done in order to ascertain the cell survival of drug loaded carriers. Following the completion of the incubation period, a volume of 10 µl of MTT reagent was introduced into every well, and the mixture was then subjected to additional incubation for a duration of two hours. Plates were read using a bio reader set to 570 nm after the incubation period had been completed. The cytocompatibility of drug-loaded particles was further demonstrated by our findings. As demonstrated in Fig. 8, MSN-GS had the highest level of cell survival up to 400 µg/mL. It was discovered that the LD 50 of particles was 448 µg/mL. It is evident that the viability of exposed cell lines reduced proportionally with the increase in the concentration of MSN-GS, which suggested that their behavior was dosage dependent. It is possible that this behavior is the result of being exposed to a larger concentration of drug-loaded particles and having direct physical contact with monolayer cell line [42, 79]. Thus our finding further confirmed previously reported finding about biocompatibility of silica particles [42, 78, 80, 81]. These findings suggest that MSN-GS does not cause appreciable acute cytotoxicity and is cytocompatible within a therapeutic range. Even though these short-term results are encouraging, it is crucial to take into account any possible long-term repercussions, especially when administering medication chronically for inflammatory disorders. The possibility of buildup in organs like the liver, spleen, or kidneys due to extended usage of silica-based carriers raises concerns since it may cause immunological reactions or delayed damage.

Stability of MSN-GS

The stability of MSN-GS particles was examined by measuring the amount of drug degradation in formulations. MSN-GS were kept in forced degradation settings (40 °C and 70% RH) for 45 days. The degradation of the GS drug was substantially lower than that of the GS drug (23% vs. <5%). On the other hand, standard capsules on the market caused a 6% reduction in the effectiveness of the medicine. Nanoparticles have been utilized extensively to prevent medication degradation caused by enzymes and environmental influences until the drug reaches its target site [82]. Oxidation and hydrolysis are the primary causes of drug degradation in areas with high temperatures and high humidity. Hydrolysis is even more likely to occur in dosage forms that have hydrophilic surfaces [83]. The prepared MSN-GS showed reduced degradation, and the loss of the medication was no more than 5%. As a result, these MSN-GS were able to give protection and release in a rigorous environment.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the study demonstrates the successful synthesis of the pregabalin derivative (GS) and its encapsulation within mesoporous silica nanoparticles (MSN-GS). The physicochemical characterization of MSN-GS indicated favorable properties such as uniform size, stability, and an amorphous drug state, which was confirmed by various analytical techniques like SEM, TEM, DLS, FTIR, and DSC. The loading of GS into MSN showed effective encapsulation with an efficiency of 37% and maintained the drug’s controlled release properties. The in-vitro release studies revealed a slower and controlled release of GS from MSN-GS, particularly in basic media, suggesting protection for the stomach, unlike the raw GS that exhibited rapid release. The biocompatibility of MSN-GS was also confirmed by cytotoxicity studies, with no significant toxicity observed up to 400 µg/mL. Additionally, the formulation reduced pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-2 and IL-6, highlighting its potential anti-inflammatory effects. The stability studies demonstrated that MSN-GS maintained its integrity and minimized drug degradation under harsh conditions. Molecular docking results indicated enhanced binding affinity of GS in its encapsulated form, providing insights into its improved biological activity compared to pregabalin. Overall, the MSN-GS formulation offers significant potential for drug delivery applications, combining stability, controlled release, and reduced toxicity for therapeutic use.

When combined, these results show that MSN-GS is a viable drug delivery method with benefits in therapeutic performance, stability, and controlled release. Future research should concentrate on pharmacodynamics and pharmacokinetic profile in bigger animal models, as well as long-term in vivo toxicity trials to evaluate chronic safety. The sol-gel-based MSN synthesis should also be scaled up for industrial production, with an emphasis on cost-effectiveness and process repeatability. In order to move this platform into clinical trials and therapeutic applications in chronic inflammatory disorders, these steps will be essential.
