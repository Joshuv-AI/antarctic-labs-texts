Abstract
We present an analysis of Si III absorption and its effect on the 1D Ly α \alpha forest power spectrum using the Sherwood-Relics hydrodynamical simulation suite. In addition to the well-understood oscillations arising from the Ly α \alpha –Si III cross correlation, we find an enhancement in small-scale power that has been ignored in previous studies. We therefore develop a new analytical fitting function that captures two critical effects that have previously been neglected: distinct Ly α \alpha and Si III line profiles, and a variable ratio for coeval Ly α \alpha and Si III optical depths. In contrast to earlier work, we also predict amplitudes for the Si III power spectrum and Ly α \alpha -Si III cross power spectrum that decrease toward lower redshift due to the hardening metagalactic UV background spectrum at z ≲ 3.5 z\lesssim 3.5 . The fitting function is validated by comparison against multiple simulated datasets at redshifts 2.2 ≤ z ≤ 5.0 2.2\leq z\leq 5.0 and wavenumbers k < 0.2 ​ s ​ km − 1 k<0.2\rm\,s\,km^{-1} . Our model remains in good agreement with earlier work at large scales ( k ≲ 0.06 ​ s ​ km − 1 k\lesssim 0.06\rm\,s\,km^{-1} ) and it has little effect on existing warm dark matter constraints from the Ly α \alpha forest when adopting a physically motivated prior on the silicon abundance. It will, however, be an essential consideration for future, high precision Ly α \alpha forest power spectrum measurements.



1
Introduction

The Ly
\alpha
forest, characterised by absorption features imprinted on the spectra of distant quasars, is produced when light from background quasars is absorbed by neutral hydrogen in the intergalactic medium (IGM). It stands as one of the most informative probes for studying the structure and evolution of the Universe
(
Meiksin 2009
;
McQuinn 2016
)
. As the light traverses regions of varying hydrogen gas density along the line of sight, a complex pattern of absorption emerges. This pattern reflects both the physical state of the IGM and the underlying dark matter distribution, making the Ly
\alpha
forest an essential tool for investigating a range of astrophysical and cosmological phenomena.

One of the most widely used summary statistics used for extracting information from the Ly
\alpha
forest is the one-dimensional Ly
\alpha
power spectrum, which quantifies the statistical distribution of absorption features as a function of spatial scale. Its study remains an important area of research
(
Palanque-Delabrouille et al. 2013
;
Iršič et al. 2017
;
Chabanier et al. 2019
;
Karaçaylı et al. 2022
;
Ravoux et al. 2025
, e.g.,)
. In particular, it plays a crucial role in constraining cosmological parameters
(
Croft et al. 2002
;
Viel et al. 2004
;
Seljak et al. 2005
;
McDonald et al. 2006
, e.g.,)
, dark matter models
(
Narayanan et al. 2000
;
Viel et al. 2013
;
Rogers & Peiris 2021
;
Hooper et al. 2022
;
Villasenor et al. 2023
;
Iršič et al. 2024
;
Garcia-Gallego et al. 2025
, e.g.,)
and primordial magnetic fields
(
Pavičević et al. 2025
)
, complementing observations from the cosmic microwave background, galaxy clustering, and weak gravitational lensing.

Current measurements of the 1D power spectrum are divided into two regimes. High-resolution spectroscopic surveys (e.g., Keck/HIRES, VLT/UVES;
R\gtrsim 20,000
, S/N
>10
) resolve small-scale features but have limited sample sizes (
\lesssim 100
QSOs), leading to large statistical uncertainties
(
Iršič et al. 2017
;
Boera et al. 2019
;
Karaçaylı et al. 2022
, e.g.,)
. Conversely, surveys like SDSS/eBOSS and DESI achieve larger samples (
\sim 10^{5}
QSO spectra
(
Chabanier et al. 2019
;
Ravoux et al. 2023
;
Ravoux et al. 2025
;
Karaçaylı et al. 2025
)
) at the cost of spectral resolution, limiting their sensitivity to wavenumbers beyond
k\sim 0.03\rm\,s\,km^{-1}
. Representing the first regime,
Boera et al. 2019
measured the power spectrum at
z=
4.2, 4.6, and 5.0 using fifteen high-resolution QSO spectra from Keck/HIRES and VLT/UVES, reaching scales down to
k\sim 0.2
s km
-1
with
\sim 15
per cent precision. For the second regime, the most recent public results come from SDSS/eBOSS
(
Chabanier et al. 2019
)
, measuring the power spectrum from
z=2.2
to 4.6 and extending to
k\sim 0.02
s km
-1
, and DESI DR1
(
Ravoux et al. 2025
;
Karaçaylı et al. 2025
)
, measuring from
z=2.2
to 4.2 and extending to
k\sim 0.026-0.039
s km
-1
depending on redshift.

Accurate modeling of the Ly
\alpha
forest power spectrum, however, faces challenges due to contamination from metal absorption lines. These lines are broadly categorised as either correlated or uncorrelated with Ly
\alpha
absorption. Correlated metal lines trace the Ly
\alpha
forest due to their spectral and redshift proximity within the same large-scale structures. The most significant contributor is Si
III
(
\lambda
1207), arising from doubly ionised silicon. Uncorrelated metal lines, conversely, do not align spatially or in redshift with the Ly
\alpha
forest and often trace distinct astrophysical environments, resulting in a stochastic relationship with Ly
\alpha
absorption. Prominent examples include C
IV
(
\lambda\lambda
1548, 1551), Si
IV
(
\lambda\lambda
1394, 1403), and Mg
II
(
\lambda\lambda
2796, 2804). These metal lines introduce systematic distortions that complicate cosmological measurements and IGM modelling using the Ly
\alpha
forest power spectrum. Consequently, improving models of their impact on the Ly
\alpha
forest power spectrum is essential.

In practice, the effect of uncorrelated metals on the 1D power spectrum can be reduced to negligible levels by estimating their power spectrum using sidebands – spectral regions devoid of Ly
\alpha
absorption that lie redward of the QSO Ly
\alpha
emission lines
(
Palanque-Delabrouille et al. 2013
;
Chabanier et al. 2019
;
Ravoux et al. 2023
;
Boera et al. 2019
;
Karaçaylı et al. 2022
, e.g.,)
. Contamination from correlated metals, however, is harder to isolate due to the fact they are blended in the Ly
\alpha
forest. The canonical modelling approach, first introduced by
McDonald et al. 2006
and widely adopted in many studies
(
Palanque-Delabrouille et al. 2013
;
Chabanier et al. 2019
, e.g.,)
, assumes that the transmitted flux contrast of Si
III
absorption can be obtained by a linear rescaling of the Ly
\alpha
flux contrast. This ansatz yields an expression for the Si
III
power spectrum in terms of the Ly
\alpha
power spectrum. At large scales, it is well-established that this model accurately describes the oscillatory behavior in the power spectrum induced by Si
III
absorption. Furthermore, the resulting systematic errors from the Si
III
oscillations are small compared to other uncertainties
(
McDonald et al. 2006
;
Palanque-Delabrouille et al. 2013
;
Chabanier et al. 2019
)
.

However, the
McDonald et al. 2006
ansatz will break down towards smaller scales as differences in individual absorption line profiles become significant. Furthermore, the redshift evolution of the Si
III
power spectrum is also uncertain. These limitations pose problems for high-resolution measurements of the power spectrum, particularly as statistical errors become smaller. To address this issue, this paper presents a revised approach for the impact of correlated Si
III
absorption on the Ly
\alpha
forest spectrum. We develop a physically motivated fitting function based on the Sherwood-Relics simulation suite
(
Bolton et al. 2017
;
Puchwein et al. 2023
)
, from which we directly compute Ly
\alpha
and Si
III
optical depths and construct mock QSO spectra.

The paper is organised as follows: Section
2
outlines our methodology for creating the mock QSO spectra and quantifying the power spectrum. Section
3
discusses the limitations of previous work and an analytical argument that helps build insight into our revised model. We test and validate our model against simulation datasets with varying redshifts, temperatures, and metallicities in Section
4
. Readers who are primarily interested in how to implement our model within parameter inference frameworks may wish to skip directly to Section
4.4
and Eq. (
21
). The implications of our model for warm dark matter constraints are discussed briefly in Section
5
. Finally, Section
6
summarises our results and conclusions.

We use a simulation from the Sherwood-Relics project
(
Puchwein et al. 2023
)
to construct Ly
\alpha
(
\lambda 1216
) and Si
III
(
\lambda 1207
) absorption spectra. Sherwood-Relics is an extensive series of high-resolution cosmological hydrodynamical simulations performed using a customised version of
P-Gadget-3

(
Springel 2005
)
. In this work we use a cosmological box of size
40h^{-1}\rm\,cMpc
with
2\times 2048^{3}
dark matter and gas particles. The dark matter particle mass is
M_{\rm dm}=5.37\times 10^{5}h^{-1}\,\rm M_{\odot}
and the gas particle mass is
M_{\rm gas}=9.97\times 10^{4}h^{-1}\,M_{\odot}
. The box size and mass resolution have been chosen to resolve the small-scale structure of the Ly
\alpha
forest
(
Bolton & Becker 2009
, see e.g.)
.

The initial conditions for the simulation are identical to those used in the earlier Sherwood simulation project
(
Bolton et al. 2017
)
. We assume a flat
\Lambda
CDM cosmology with
\Omega_{\Lambda}=0.692
,
\Omega_{\rm M}=0.308
,
\Omega_{\rm b}=0.0482
,
\sigma_{8}=0.829
,
n_{\rm s}=0.961
,
h=0.678
, and a primordial helium mass abundance of
Y=0.24
. A computationally efficient star-formation scheme is used, where gas particles are converted into collisionless star particles if they have gas overdensities
\Delta>10^{3}
and gas temperatures
T<10^{5}\rm\,K

(
Viel et al. 2004
)
. The simulation uses a spatially uniform ultraviolet (UV) background
(
Puchwein et al. 2019
)
and the thermal and ionisation state of the hydrogen and helium gas is computed using a non-equilibrium thermo-chemistry solver
(
Puchwein et al. 2015
)
.

Periodic sightlines are drawn through the simulation to extract the gas densities, temperatures, peculiar velocities and H
I
fractions needed to compute mock Ly
\alpha
absorption spectra. We use the interpolation scheme described by
Theuns et al. 1998
for this purpose. The sightlines are extracted at a redshift interval of
\Delta z=0.1
, and for each redshift we draw a total of
5000
such sightlines with
2048
pixels, giving a pixel size of
19.5h^{-1}\rm\,ckpc
. The Ly
\alpha
optical depths along each sightline are then computed by performing a convolution with the the Voigt line profile from
Tepper-García 2006
.

We use a similar approach to construct the Si
III
absorption spectra, although with some additional steps. Firstly, the Sherwood-Relics simulations do not use a sub-grid model for metal production and dispersal. Instead, we assume a fiducial silicon abundance model that follows the gas overdensity,
\Delta=\rho/\langle\rho\rangle

[{\rm Si/H}]=-2.70+0.08(z-3)+0.65(\log_{10}\Delta-0.5).

2.2
Mock quasar sightlines

Once the optical depths are extracted from the hydrodynamical simulations, we combine the individual sightlines drawn from the hydrodynamical simulation into mock quasar spectra. First, for a given quasar redshift
z_{\rm QSO}
, we construct a full-length Ly
\alpha
forest spectrum (i.e. from
\lambda_{\rm\alpha}(1+z_{\rm QSO})
to
\lambda_{\rm\beta}(1+z_{\rm QSO})
, where
\lambda_{\rm\alpha}
and
\lambda_{\rm\beta}
are the Ly
\alpha
and Ly
\beta
rest frame wavelengths) by combining the simulated sightlines at the required redshifts. These are stitched together so that the optical depth remains continuous; the periodic nature of the sightlines allows us to shift each segment to maintain continuous intersections. The same process is used to extract the coeval Si
III
absorption spectra over a matching length scale. The Ly
\alpha
absorption is furthermore normalised to match the effective optical depth measured by
Becker et al. 2013
at the appropriate redshifts. Finally, the Si
III
optical depths are shifted to align at the correct observed wavelengths and then added to the Ly
\alpha
optical depths, where we assume the rest frame wavelengths of the transitions are Ly
\alpha
(
\lambda
1216) and Si
III
(
\lambda
1207). An example of the predicted absorption in a noiseless spectrum at
z\sim 3
is displayed in the upper panel of Figure
2
.

2.3
Computing the power spectrum

We shall use the noiseless mock spectra to compute the 1D Ly
\alpha
forest power spectrum, both including and excluding the correlated Si
III
absorption. Following the approach commonly adopted in the literature
(
Palanque-Delabrouille et al. 2013
;
Chabanier et al. 2019
;
Ravoux et al. 2023
, e.g.)
, we compute the power spectrum,
P(k)
, using the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) approach, where

P(k)\propto|\tilde{\delta}(k)|^{2}.

(3)

Here
\tilde{\delta}(k)
is the Fourier transform of the flux contrast,
\delta(\lambda)
, which is given by

\delta(\lambda)=\frac{F(\lambda)}{\overline{F}(\lambda)}-1,

(4)

where
F(\lambda)
is the transmitted flux in a pixel at wavelength
\lambda
, and
\overline{F}(\lambda)
is the mean transmission of the intervening Ly
\alpha
forest. For the latter we assume the effective optical depth,
\tau_{\rm efff}
, reported by
Becker et al. 2013
, where
\overline{F}(\lambda)=e^{-\tau_{\rm eff}}
. We also assume the observer frame wavelength,
\lambda_{\rm obs}
, and the velocity are related by

\lambda_{\rm obs}=\lambda_{\rm rest}(1+z)\sqrt{\frac{1-v/c}{1+v/c}},

(5)

where
\lambda_{\rm rest}
is the rest frame wavelength of the transition.

It is well known that the correlations between metal line transitions with wavelengths similar to the Ly
\alpha

\alpha
forest power spectrum. For Si
III
, which is one of the most prominent correlated metals, a feature at
\Delta v=2271\rm\,km\,s^{-1}

\alpha
forest auto-correlation. This also manifests as an oscillatory feature in the 1D power spectrum with peak separations of
\Delta k=2\pi/\Delta v

(
McDonald et al. 2006
;
Palanque-Delabrouille et al. 2013
;
Chabanier et al. 2019
;
Ravoux et al. 2023
, see e.g.)
.

Hence, in order to obtain a good match between observations and models, the contribution of Si
III
to the power spectrum is usually treated as a nuisance parameter.
McDonald et al. 2006

III

\delta_{\rm Si\,\textsc{III}}

\alpha
forest transmitted flux contrast,
\delta_{\rm Ly\alpha}

a

\displaystyle\delta_{\rm tot}(v)

\displaystyle=\delta_{\rm Ly\alpha}(v)+\delta_{\rm Si\,\textsc{III}}(v),

(6)

\displaystyle=\delta_{\rm Ly\alpha}(v)+a\ \delta_{\rm Ly\alpha}(v+\Delta v).

\alpha

Å

. Although this retains the same oscillation period in both models, it has an amplitude that is scale dependent in the direct simulation, yet is constant in the rescaled model (i.e, from Eq. (
7
), we expect the ratio of the amplitudes for

and
P_{\rm Ly\alpha}(k)
to be
a/2
). This suggests that applying Eq. (
6
) to 1D Ly
\alpha
forest power spectrum constraints at wavenumbers larger than
k\sim 10^{-2}\rm\,s\,km^{-1}
will result in an underestimate of the contribution of correlated metals to the small scale power.

The physical origin of these differences arise from two effects missed in the ansatz made by Eq. (
6
). The first – as already discussed earlier – is that the Ly
\alpha
and Si
III
line profiles are different. In general, unless the gas is highly turbulent, the Si
III
line widths will be narrower than Ly
\alpha
(cf. Eq. (

III
power spectrum. In addition, the ratio of the coeval Si

and Ly
\alpha
optical depths is not constant. If ignoring redshift space effects by assuming the line profiles are Dirac delta functions, the ratio of the Si
III
to Ly
\alpha
optical depths is

\displaystyle\beta(\nu)

\displaystyle=\frac{\tau_{\rm Si\,\textsc{III}}(v)}{\tau_{\rm Ly\alpha}(v+\Delta v)},

(11)

\displaystyle=\frac{f_{\rm Si\,\textsc{III}}\lambda_{\rm Si\,\textsc{III}}}{f_{\rm Ly\alpha}\lambda_{\rm Ly\alpha}}\frac{x_{\rm Si\,\textsc{III}}(\Delta,T)}{x_{\rm HI}(\Delta,T)}10^{\rm[Si/H]+(Si/H)_{\odot}},

where
f_{\rm Si\,\textsc{III}}\lambda_{\rm SiIII}/f_{\rm Ly\alpha}\lambda_{\rm Ly\alpha}=3.88
is the constant ratio of the transition oscillator strengths and rest frame wavelengths
(
Morton 2003
)
. At any given redshift we therefore expect
\beta(v)
to have a pixel dependency arising from variations in the silicon abundance,
[\rm Si/H]
, and Si

, where the latter will depend on density,
\Delta

P_{\rm Si\,\textsc{III}}(k)
and
P_{\rm Ly\alpha-Si\,\textsc{III}}(k)

\delta_{\rm Si\,\textsc{III}}(v)\ \circledast\phi(v)=[a(1+\delta_{\beta}(v))\delta_{\rm Ly\alpha}(v+\Delta v)].

(12)

Here,
\circledast\phi(v)

b

\phi(v)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}\ b}\text{exp}\left(-\frac{v^{2}}{b^{2}}\right).

(13)

The perturbative term
\delta_{\beta}(v)
represents the pixel dependency implied by Eq. (

\delta_{\beta}(v)\simeq\frac{\beta(v)}{\bar{\beta}(v)}-1,

(14)

\tilde{\phi}(k)=\text{exp}\left(-\frac{b^{2}k^{2}}{4}\right),

(15)

\delta_{\rm Si\,\textsc{III}}(v)
can be obtained by applying a Fourier transform to both sides of Eq. (

\tilde{\delta}_{\rm Si\,\textsc{III}}(k)=a\text{exp}\left(\frac{b^{2}k^{2}}{4}+ik\Delta v\right)[\tilde{\delta}_{\rm Ly\alpha}(k)+\tilde{\delta}_{\epsilon}(k)].

(16)

For notational convenience we have written the convolution arising from the Fourier transform of
\delta_{\beta}(v)\delta_{\rm Ly\alpha}(v)

\tilde{\delta}_{\epsilon}(k)\equiv\tilde{\delta}_{\beta}(k)\circledast\tilde{\delta}_{\rm Ly\alpha}(k)

\alpha

P_{\rm Si\,\textsc{III}}(k)=a^{2}\exp\left(\frac{b^{2}k^{2}}{2}\right)[P_{\rm Ly\alpha}(k)+P_{\rm Ly\alpha-\epsilon}(k)+P_{\rm\epsilon}(k)],

(17)

where
P_{\rm Ly\alpha-\epsilon}(k)
is the power spectrum of the cross-correlation between
\delta_{\rm Ly\alpha}(v)
and
\delta_{\beta}(v)\delta_{\rm Ly\alpha}(v)

P_{\epsilon}(k)
is the power spectrum of
\delta_{\beta}(v)\delta_{\rm Ly\alpha}(v)

P_{\rm Ly\alpha-Si\,\textsc{III}}(k)=2a\cos(\Delta vk)\exp\left(\frac{b^{2}k^{2}}{4}\right)[P_{\rm Ly\alpha}(k)+P_{\rm Ly\alpha-\epsilon}(k)],

(18)

Next we consider the amplitude parameters,
a_{\rm Si\,\textsc{III}}
and
a_{\rm Ly\alpha-Si\,\textsc{III}}
. Following the pioneering work by
McDonald et al. 2006
, previous studies have typically imposed a redshift dependence

where
\bar{F}(z)
is the mean transmission in the Ly
\alpha
forest and
f
is a free parameter. Under this assumption the amplitude of the Si
III
power spectrum declines toward higher redshift as
\bar{F}
decreases. However, in this work we find the opposite trend in the simulation data; the amplitude increases with redshift at
z\lesssim 3.5
and remains relatively flat at higher redshifts, as shown by the redshift evolution of
a_{\rm Si\,\textsc{III}}
and
a_{\rm Ly\alpha-Si\,\textsc{III}}
in the lower panels of Figure
9

McDonald et al. 2006
ansatz to our simulations at
k<0.02\rm\,s\,km^{-1}

a_{\rm Si\,\textsc{III}}
and

are insensitive to thermal variations and their redshift evolution is driven almost entirely by the silicon abundance,
\rm[Si/H]
, and/or the Si
III
fraction,
x_{\rm Si\,\textsc{III}}
.

Overall, this implies the redshift evolution of
a_{\rm Si\,\textsc{III}}
and
a_{\rm Ly\alpha-Si\,\textsc{III}}

in our models. This is corroborated by the redshift evolution of
x_{\rm Si\,\textsc{III}}

1
, which exhibits a qualitatively similar trend to
a_{\rm Si\,\textsc{III}}
and
a_{\rm Ly\alpha-Si\,\textsc{III}}

III

[1-\bar{F}(z)]^{-1}

It is, nevertheless, useful to parameterise the redshift evolution of

,
a_{\rm Si\,\textsc{III}}
and

to facilitate marginalisation over multiple redshift bins. One possibility is to adopt the redshift scaling of our fiducial model in Table

Viel et al. 2013

5
Implications for warm dark matter constraints

What are the implications of this work for existing warm dark matter constraints from the 1D Ly
\alpha
forest power spectrum? To assess this we compare our best-fit fiducial models to the uncertainties on data. For large-scale measurements such as those from eBOSS or DESI, which cover wavenumbers below
\sim 0.05\rm\,s\,km^{-1}
, as already discussed the differences between our model and the
McDonald et al. 2006
ansatz are small. The main departure of our fiducial model is that we expect the amplitude of the Si
III
oscillations to increase toward higher redshift in the range
2\leq z\leq 3.5
and remain approximately constant thereafter.

However, this situation changes for high-resolution measurements that probe smaller scales (
k\gtrsim 0.1
s/km). We compare our model to the uncertainties on the
Boera et al. 2019
power spectrum measurement (grey shading) in Figure
10
. The contribution from Si
III
becomes increasingly prominent at small scales. The red and blue curves show, respectively, our best-fit fiducial models using
Method I
and
Method II
described in Section
4.4
. The black circles show the results from our hydrodynamical simulation with the same error bars displayed in Figure
6
. At scales
k\sim 0.2\rm\,s\,km^{-1}
, the small-scale power from the Si
III
power spectrum – which was not accounted for in earlier models – becomes comparable to the observational error bars.

It is thus interesting to investigate the impact of our model on existing warm dark matter (WDM) constraints from 1D Ly
\alpha
power spectrum measurements.
Iršič et al. 2024
recently used the measurement of
Boera et al. 2019
to set a stringent lower bound on the mass of a warm dark matter (WDM) thermal relic. Their best-fit WDM model showed excellent agreement at intermediate scales (

/dof = 20.4/20), giving a limit of

keV (at 95 per cent confidence). However, extending their analysis to smaller scales (
k<0.2
s km
-1
) resulted in a somewhat poorer fit (
\chi^{2}
/dof = 40.7/34) with a limit of
m_{\rm WDM}\geq 5.7
keV.
Iršič et al. 2024
noted the larger reduced
\chi^{2}
was due to additional small-scale power at
k>0.1
s km

– precisely where our study reveals substantial Si

driven enhancements to the small-scale power.

To quantify the contribution of correlated Si
III
absorption identified in this study, we incorporate our model (using
Method I

Iršič et al. 2024
pipeline and compare the best-fit models for the 1D Ly
\alpha
power spectrum with and without our Si

. We find that
k_{\rm ref}
and
a_{\rm Si\,\textsc{III}}
are prior-dominated, while
a_{\rm Ly\alpha-Si\,\textsc{III}}
remains unconstrained. This is due to the dominance of the small-scale enhancement from
P_{\rm Si\,\textsc{III}}(k)
over the cross-term oscillations for the wavenumbers considered here. When using physically motivated priors for
A
,
B
and
C
, as described in Section
4.4
, the

.

Note that because

Consequently, the goodness-of-fit remains similar and the constraint on the WDM mass slightly tightens from

keV to
m_{\rm WDM}\geq 5.8

6
Summary

We have used a hydrodynamical simulation drawn from the Sherwood-Relics simulation suite
(
Bolton et al. 2017
;
Puchwein et al. 2023
)
to construct an improved model for the impact of correlated Si
III
absorption on the 1D Ly
\alpha
forest power spectrum. We have identified a significant enhancement in the 1D power spectrum due to Si
III
at small scales (
k\gtrsim 0.06\rm\,s\,km^{-1}
s/km) that is not explicitly accounted for in previous work. To address this limitation, we have developed a physically motivated fitting function to extend the canonical framework for modelling the Ly
\alpha
-Si
III
correlation first introduced by
McDonald et al. 2006
. Our model takes into account two critical effects missed in the original ansatz made by
McDonald et al. 2006
– the fact that Ly
\alpha
and Si
III
line profiles are different, and that the ratio of coeval Ly
\alpha
and Si
III
optical depths are not constant.

Our improved model yields a three parameter fit (see Eq. (
21
)) where
k_{\rm ref}
controls the small-scale power enhancement, and
a_{\rm Si\,\textsc{III}}
and
a_{\rm Ly\alpha-Si\,\textsc{III}}
independently control the amplitudes of the Si
III
power spectrum and the Ly
\alpha
-Si
III
cross-correlation power spectrum, respectively. We validate this model by comparison to multiple simulated datasets at redshifts
2.2\leq z\leq 5.0
for wavenumbers
k\lesssim 0.2\rm\,s\,km^{-1}
. The absolute value of the fractional deviation between our fitting function and all tested datasets is within 2 per cent, suggesting good agreement between our fitting function and the simulation data across a variety of IGM thermal and metal enrichment histories. Incorporating this effect will be essential for future cosmological and astrophysical constraints using high resolution Ly
\alpha
forest data. We provide full guidelines for the implementation of the model within existing parameter inference frameworks in Section
4.4
.

Reassuringly, however, if only large scales (
k\lesssim 0.06
s km
-1
) are of interest, both the new fitting function presented here and the earlier
McDonald et al. 2006
approach provide excellent approximations to the Ly
\alpha
-Si
III
correlation. The simplicity of the
McDonald et al. 2006
model, given by Eq. (
7
) – (
9
), means this is still a good choice on these scales. However, we also find the redshift dependence typically assumed for the amplitude of the Si
III
and Ly
\alpha
–Si
III
cross power spectra – such that
a(z)\propto[1-\bar{F}(z)]^{-1}
where
\bar{F}(z)
is the mean transmission in the Ly
\alpha
forest and
f
is a free parameter – is not observed in any of our simulations. Under this assumption the amplitude of the Si
III
oscillations declines toward higher redshift as
\bar{F}
decreases. Instead, our empirically constrained simulations return the opposite trend; the amplitude increases with redshift at
2\leq z\leq 3.5
and remains approximately constant at higher redshifts. This is primarily due to the hardening of the metagalactic UV background spectrum at
z\lesssim 3.5
for photon energies around the Si
III
ionisation potential of
33.5\rm\,eV
, coupled with modest evolution (by a factor of
\lesssim 10
) evolution in the intergalactic silicon abundance at
2<z<5

(
Schaye et al. 2003
;
Aguirre et al. 2004
)
. The origin of this spectral hardening is the increasingly important contribution from quasar emission to the UV background
(
Khaire & Srianand 2019
;
Puchwein et al. 2019
, e.g)
.

Finally, our Si
III
absorption model has little impact on existing constraints on warm dark matter free streaming if adopting physically motivated priors on the IGM temperature and silicon abundance, but it may partially account for the otherwise difficult-to-fit small scale power enhancement at
k>0.1\rm\,s\,km^{-1}
in the
Boera et al. 2019
power spectrum measurements noted by
Iršič et al. 2024
. We anticipate that modelling this effect will become more important as the precision of the observational data improves. Our results also presents an opportunity to place novel constraints on the metal enrichment of the low density IGM using the 1D power spectrum. We intend to explore this possibility in future work.
