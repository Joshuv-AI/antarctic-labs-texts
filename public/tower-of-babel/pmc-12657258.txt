Evaluation of the Effect of Topical Vitamin A Eye Ointment on Pain Reduction and Accelerated Healing of Corneal Defect After Photorefractive Keratectomy: A Prospective Randomized Controlled Study
Authors: Maryam Hajizadeh, Nasrin Masihpour, Shima Shalil‐Ahmadi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsPhotorefractive keratectomy (PRK) is commonly used for refractive errors but is associated with postoperative pain and delayed epithelial healing. Vitamin A has been shown to support epithelial regeneration and corneal wound healing. This study aimed to evaluate the effect of topical vitamin A (retinol palmitate) on postoperative pain and corneal epithelial healing after PRK.MethodsA double‐blind, randomized controlled trial was conducted on 56 eyes of 28 myopic patients undergoing PRK at Imam Khomeini Hospital, Ahvaz, Iran. Participants were assigned to a vitamin A ointment group (250 IU/g in a petrolatum‐based ointment) or a control group. The primary outcome was corneal epithelial healing, and the secondary outcome was postoperative pain, measured using the visual analog scale (VAS).ResultsCorneal healing was significantly faster in the vitamin A group, with smaller epithelial defects at 24 h (19.32 ± 6.21 mm² vs. 26 ± 8.28 mm², p = 0.023), 2 days (10.64 ± 3.45 mm² vs. 16.17 ± 6.71 mm², p = 0.01), and 3 days (2.96 ± 2.47 mm² vs. 6.17 ± 3.99 mm², p = 0.016). However, by Day 4, the difference was no longer significant (p = 0.16). Postoperative pain, measured on the VAS, showed no significant differences between the two groups at any time point: 12 h (p = 0.26), 24 h (p = 0.08), 2 days (p = 0.17), and 3 days (p = 0.37). Both groups demonstrated a general decrease in pain over time, but no significant interaction effect was observed between group and time (F (3, 162) = 0.26, p = 0.36).ConclusionTopical vitamin A accelerated corneal epithelial healing after PRK, especially in the first 3 days post‐surgery. However, it did not significantly reduce postoperative pain. These results suggest vitamin A aids in wound healing, but its role in pain management after PRK remains unclear. Larger studies with longer follow‐up are needed to explore its effects further.

Introduction

Photorefractive keratectomy (PRK) is a well‐established and widely utilized surface laser procedure for correcting refractive errors including myopia, astigmatism, and hyperopia [1]. Renowned for its flapless approach and low complication rates, PRK has been a preferred choice for refractive surgery for several decades [2]. With ongoing advancements in surgical techniques and laser technology, the procedure's safety and efficacy have been further enhanced, contributing to its widespread global adoption [3]. Despite these improvements, PRK is not without its challenges, as it is often associated with postoperative pain, prolonged epithelial healing, and the risk of corneal stromal haze [4, 5]

The primary cause of postoperative pain in PRK is corneal epithelial damage induced by the laser ablation, which triggers the release of inflammatory mediators, including prostaglandins, histamines, and substance P [6, 7]. This inflammatory response not only contributes to discomfort and delayed healing but also heightens the risk of complications [6, 8]. Acute postoperative pain can lead to an increased heart rate, elevated blood pressure, patient dissatisfaction, prolonged recovery, higher treatment costs, and decreased treatment quality [9]. A prior investigation has revealed a significant relationship between inflammatory manifestations, such as lid edema, and the intensity of pain following PRK surgery. Additionally, it is noteworthy that patients experiencing greater pain severity tend to report increased ocular discomfort, particularly symptoms like foreign body sensation and tearing [10].

Traditional pain management strategies, including bandage contact lenses, NSAID eye drops, oral analgesics, and cold compresses, aim to alleviate these symptoms but may have limitations and side effects [11, 12]. These medications control the release of proinflammatory cytokines, which would otherwise impede the healing process [13].

Vitamin A, known for its role in epithelial growth and corneal wound healing, presents a promising adjunctive treatment for managing these challenges [14, 15]. Vitamin A deficiency is associated with corneal epithelial abnormalities, including keratinization and delayed healing [16, 17]. While studies have demonstrated the benefits of vitamin A in the treatment of ocular surface diseases, there is limited research on its efficacy in promoting epithelial healing and pain reduction following refractive surgery [18]. Only one clinical trial has reported faster epithelial healing with oral vitamin A and E supplementation post‐PRK. However, the role of topical vitamin A in this context remains unexplored [19]. Therefore, this randomized controlled study aims to evaluate the effect of topical vitamin A eye ointment on reducing pain and accelerating corneal epithelial healing after PRK surgery.

Materials and Methods

This single‑center, double‑blind controlled trial was conducted on 56 eyes of 28 myopic patients who underwent PRK surgery in the ophthalmology clinic of Imam Khomeini Hospital, affiliated to Ahvaz Jundishapur University of Medical Sciences (Ahvaz, Iran) from March 2024 to October 2024. Adult patients undergoing elective myopic PRK surgery were recruited.

Participants were excluded from the study if they had myopia greater than 8 diopters (D), astigmatism exceeding 4 D, keratometry more than 48 D, corneal thickness < 480 mm, a mesopic pupil size larger than 6 mm (to minimize the risk of post‐PRK complications such as night vision glare or halos), or any degree of hyperopia. Additionally, individuals with a history of keratoconus, herpes keratitis, corneal dystrophy, glaucoma, cataract, blepharitis, uveitis, pregnancy, or a previous medical history of dry eye disease were not included. Further exclusion criteria encompassed recent contact lens use within the past 2 months, a history of prior refractive surgery, wound healing disorders (e.g., keloids), as well as the presence of gastrointestinal conditions or systemic diseases, particularly diabetes, cardiovascular diseases, and coagulation disorders.

A standard comprehensive ophthalmic examination was conducted, which included slit‐lamp examination, biomicroscopy, manifest and cycloplegic refraction, assessment of uncorrected and best‐corrected visual acuity, measurement of intraocular pressure, corneal topography using the Keraton Optikon, corneal pachymetry with the Tomey TMS‐2, keratometry, and indirect fundoscopic evaluation.

Ethical Consideration

The study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Ahvaz Jundishapur University of Medical Sciences (IR.AJUMS.REC.1402.609) and registered in the Iranian Clinical Trials Registry (IRCT20240213060989N1). The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki for medical research. Informed consent was obtained from eligible individuals. The present research is reported based on Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) [20].

PRK Procedure

All surgical procedures were conducted by a single surgeon (M.H.). Before laser ablation, topical anesthesia consisting of 2% lidocaine was administered to each eye. To ensure a sterile environment, plastic sterile drapes were placed, and a lid speculum, along with Tegaderm, was used to provide adequate exposure of the operative field. An 8.5‐mm epithelial defect was then created using a blunt spatula following the application of a 17% alcohol solution for 15 s. Stromal ablation was subsequently performed using the Technolas 217‐p excimer laser (Technolas Perfect Vision GmbH, Munich, Germany). After the laser ablation, 0.02% mitomycin C (Kyowa Hakko Kogyo, Tokyo, Japan) was applied to the stromal surface for a duration of 10–35 s, depending on the patient's preoperative refraction, and was then thoroughly rinsed with 30 mL of cold balanced salt solution. The eye was focused and centered on the optical axis, and the treatment was delivered.

Postoperative Care and Follow‐Up

After the procedure, a single drop of 1% topical antibiotic (Clobiotic; Sina Darou, Tehran, Iran) was instilled into the eye, and a therapeutic soft contact lens (Air Optix; Alcon Laboratories Inc., Fort Worth, Texas, USA) was placed on both eyes.

All participants received a standardized postoperative medication regimen to promote healing and minimize complications. Preservative‐free artificial tears were administered hourly. Betamethasone 0.1% eye drops (Sina Darou, Tehran, Iran) were prescribed four times daily for 1 month, followed by a gradual tapering over the subsequent 2 months. Additionally, chloramphenicol 0.5% eye drops and Clobiotic 1% eye drops (Sina Darou, Tehran, Iran) were instilled every 4 h until the therapeutic contact lens was removed. Upon complete epithelial healing, betamethasone 0.1% eye drops were discontinued and replaced with fluorometholone eye drops (Sina Darou, Tehran, Iran).

The patients were randomly assigned to two groups as follows: Patients in the vitamin A group received retinol palmitate ophthalmic treatment, administered at a concentration of 250 IU/g in a petrolatum‐based ointment vehicle (Sina Darou, Tehran, Iran). The control group received an identical petrolatum‐based ointment without vitamin A (placebo vehicle). Both ointments were packaged in identical, masked tubes and were indistinguishable in appearance, texture, and smell. The ointment was applied four times daily, beginning on the day of surgery and continuing for 7 days postoperatively. Randomization codes were kept by the hospital pharmacy and revealed only after data analysis. The administration of the study medication and all postoperative examinations were performed by an independent investigator who was unaware of group allocation, ensuring double blinding of both patients and outcome assessors. Neither group received any oral supplements containing vitamin A (Figure 1).

The flowchart of the randomized clinical trial.

The administration of the vitamin A ointment and the follow‐up data collection were performed by an independent investigator who had no involvement in the surgical procedures. This approach was implemented to ensure blinding, minimizing potential biases in outcome assessment by preventing the investigators responsible for evaluating the results from being influenced by knowledge of the treatment allocation.

Primary Outcome: Corneal Healing After Surgery

The corneal wound healing response was assessed by measuring the horizontal diameter of the epithelial defect induced by laser ablation. The measurement was performed on the day of surgery and continued daily from postoperative Days 1–7, depending on the healing process, until complete re‐epithelialization (100%) was achieved, defined as the absence of fluorescein staining on slit‐lamp examination. To ensure accurate measurement, fluorescein dye was instilled, and the horizontal diameter of the de‐epithelialized area was recorded using a video slit‐lamp camera. The collected images were then analyzed using a computer‐based image analysis system (Adobe Photoshop 5), and the epithelial defect diameter was precisely measured using computer planimetry.

Secondary Outcomes: Postoperative Pain

Postoperative pain was assessed using the visual analog scale (VAS), with a range from 0 (no pain) to 5 (extreme pain). Before surgery, each patient received standardized verbal instructions and was shown a printed VAS sheet with evenly spaced numerical anchors (0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5) and descriptive labels (0 = no pain, 5 = worst imaginable pain). Patients were asked to indicate the number that best reflected their pain intensity at each assessment point. VAS pain assessment started 12 h after surgery and was continued at regular daily intervals until complete recovery, defined as a VAS score of 0 maintained for at least 24 consecutive hours.

Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 20.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Qualitative variables were summarized as frequencies and compared with the Chi‐square test, suitable for detecting differences in categorical distributions such as sex. Quantitative variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and compared between the two groups with the independent t‐test, appropriate for normally distributed continuous variables. To examine changes in corneal defect size and pain scores across repeated postoperative measurements and to test group‐by‐time interactions, one‐way repeated measures ANOVA was used. A two‐sided p‐value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Demographic Characteristics

A total of 28 patients (56 eyes) were included, with 14 in each group. The mean age was 30.5 ± 5.59 years in the vitamin A group and 28.2 ± 4.3 years in the control group (p = 0.23). Male distribution was 64.28% in the control group and 50% in the vitamin A group (p = 0.7) (Table 1).

Corneal Healing Process

The corneal defect size was significantly smaller in the vitamin A ointment group compared to the control group at 24 h (p = 0.02), 2 days (p = 0.01), and 3 days (p = 0.02) postoperatively. However, by Day 4, the difference between the groups was no longer statistically significant (p = 0.16). Furthermore, the One‐way repeated measures ANOVA demonstrated a significant interaction effect between treatment group and time (F (6,182) = 4.36, p < 0.001), indicating that the healing trend over time significantly differed between the vitamin A and control groups. A significant time effect was also observed (F (6,182) = 161.8, p < 0.001), confirming that corneal healing progressed significantly across the postoperative days. Additionally, a significant main effect of group (F (1,182) = 18.7, p < 0.001) suggests that the vitamin A ointment group had a significantly faster healing rate than the control group (Table 2 and Figure 2).

Postoperative Pain

VAS scores were similar between the vitamin A and control groups at all time points, with no statistically significant differences at 12 h (p = 0.26), 24 h (p = 0.08), Day 2 (p = 0.17), or Day 3 (p = 0.37). In the One‐way repeated measures ANOVA, a significant main effect of group was found (F (1, 54) = 10.843, p = 0.002), with the Routine Treatment group showing slightly lower pain scores across all time points. In the within‐subjects analysis, a significant effect of time on pain scores was observed (F (3, 162) = 62.85, p < 0.001), indicating that pain significantly decreased over the 3 days in both groups. However, the interaction between group and time was not significant (F (3, 162) = 0.26, p = 0.36), suggesting that while both groups experienced pain reduction over time, the rate or extent of pain reduction did not significantly differ between the groups (Table 2 and Figure 2).

Discussion

In this study, we sought to evaluate the efficacy of topical vitamin A (retinol palmitate) in corneal epithelial healing and postoperative pain following PRK. Our findings showed that the use of vitamin A significantly accelerated the healing of the corneal epithelium compared to the control group; no substantial effect was observed in terms of postoperative pain reduction. These findings bring important insights into the role of vitamin A in corneal wound healing and its potential therapeutic benefits in refractive surgery.

The loss of anterior stromal keratocytes due to apoptosis and the generation of free radicals following PRK surgery have been identified as initiating factors that could induce epithelial hyperplasia, eventually leading to haze formation and regression [21, 22]. Consequently, the degree of tissue injury likely reflects the interplay between oxidative stress and the local antioxidant defense mechanisms. Experimental data from animal studies indicate that specific pharmacological interventions effectively counteract oxygen radicals produced during PRK procedures, subsequently influencing corneal epithelial proliferation and differentiation [23, 24]. Despite having moderate antioxidant properties, vitamin A significantly contributes to epithelial growth, limbal stem cell differentiation, and enhanced corneal wound healing [25]. Evidence suggests vitamin A can modulate thrombospondin‐1 expression in corneal tissues, thereby accelerating re‐epithelialization processes in injured corneas [26].

Additionally, vitamin A and its metabolites, such as retinaldehyde and retinoic acids, are essential for forming visual chromophores and regulating gene expression critical to corneal and conjunctival health [27]. Deficiencies in vitamin A result in abnormal differentiation at the ocular surface, causing keratinization, scarring, squamous epithelial metaplasia, and conjunctival cell deficiency [16]. Given these therapeutic properties, topical retinoids have gained attention as potential pharmacological treatments for ocular surface disorders characterized by scarring, squamous metaplasia, or dry eye syndrome [28].

Following corneal injury, the corneal epithelium secretes various growth factors and cytokines, including TGF‐β, which bind to cell surface receptors and initiate multiple signal transduction pathways [29]. Among these, keratocyte conversion to myofibroblasts plays a crucial role in the contraction and closure of corneal abrasions and the re‐epithelialization of the surface [30]. However, it is also involved in several pathological processes, including the formation of corneal opacity [31]. Myofibroblasts contribute to wound repair through phagocytosis and by expressing transglutaminase (which cleaves the extracellular matrix) and matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) [32]. Additionally, the rapid degradation of the corneal stroma following corneal wounds appears to be mediated by these MMPs [32]. Studies have demonstrated that the absence of vitamin A following corneal abrasion is associated with increased expression of MMP‐9, leading to delayed corneal wound healing. On the other hand, the use of vitamin A reduces the expression of MMP‐9 and therefore accelerates wound healing in this group compared to the control group [33].

Our study confirmed that topical vitamin A promoted corneal epithelial healing, particularly during the initial stages after PRK. Specifically, by Day 3, we observed a significant improvement in the epithelial coverage compared to control groups, whereas conflicting findings have been observed in other studies [18]. Several factors might account for discrepancies across studies, including differences in the vitamin A molecule, formulation vehicles, associated molecular structures, and administered dosages. vitamin A encompasses various dietary unsaturated hydrocarbons such as retinol, retinal, retinoic acid, and multiple provitamin A carotenoids. Due to instability in its pure alcohol form, vitamin A is commercially available and administered as esters like retinyl palmitate or in alcohol form combined with petrolatum‐based vehicles. Johansen et al. conducted a study employing all‐trans retinoic acid (atRA) to assess its therapeutic impact on corneal epithelial abrasions [34]. Their findings revealed that twice‐daily application of 0.05% atRA significantly accelerated healing compared to a vaseline‐based vehicle. Moreover, the presence of additional molecules associated with vitamin A may also influence the healing efficacy. In a randomized controlled trial involving rabbit eyes, the combined application of vitamin A palmitate and basic fibroblast growth factor demonstrated substantial effectiveness in promoting repair of mechanical corneal epithelial defects and facilitating the establishment of intercellular junctions [35]

The route of administration may also influence the delivery of the molecule to corneal epithelial cells. Studies have shown that vitamin A is present in tears in the form of retinol [36]. This finding provides the rationale for treating corneal diseases with topical vitamin A and supports the preference for the topical route of administration. However, no significant effect has been observed with topical forms at specific low doses [37]. On the other hand, clinical trials have shown that oral supplementation of vitamin A and vitamin E after PRK results in faster epithelial healing [38]. Also, a study by Asena et al. indicated the effect of high‐dose vitamin A on accelerating the healing of corneal abrasions through re‐epithelialization [39]. This difference could be explained by variations in dosage, which makes direct comparisons challenging. Further research is needed to address the efficacy of different molecular forms of vitamin A, dosage, vehicle, and other potentially relevant molecules that could enhance the action of vitamin A on the repair of corneal mechanical epithelial defects and the establishment of intracellular junctions.

Topical vitamin A did not significantly reduce postoperative pain in our study. Postoperative pain after PRK typically reaches its peak within the first 12–24 h, with most patients reporting substantial discomfort during the first few days. This pain is mainly due to nerve exposure from epithelial removal, inflammation, and keratocyte apoptosis in the corneal stroma [40, 41, 42]. Systemic NSAIDs and opioid analgesics, topical NSAIDs, cold compresses, dressing lenses, and local anesthetics have also been reported to improve pain more than alternative strategies and make PRK‐related pain more tolerable [4]. None of the agents used to reduce pain after PRK surgery can eliminate postoperative pain [43]. Our findings align with those of Chelala et al. [18], who found that while topical vitamin A promoted corneal epithelial healing, it did not significantly alleviate pain compared to other more potent analgesic agents such as NSAIDs and local anesthetics. The lack of significant pain relief may be attributed to the mode of action of vitamin A, which primarily targets epithelial regeneration but does not directly intervene in nerve healing or inflammatory cytokine production that are pivotal in pain mechanisms. Additionally, the dose of retinol palmitate used in our study (250 IU/g) may have been insufficient to produce a meaningful analgesic effect.

This study has several limitations that should be considered. First, the sample size was relatively small, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. A larger, multicenter study with a more diverse patient population is recommended to validate these results. Second, although we used objective measures for corneal healing and pain assessment, subjective variations in pain perception among individuals may have influenced the VAS scores. Additionally, the follow‐up period was limited to the early postoperative phase, and the long‐term effects of topical vitamin A on corneal healing and haze formation were not assessed. Future research should include extended follow‐up periods to evaluate potential late‐stage benefits or complications. Lastly, other confounding factors, such as tear film stability and pre‐existing ocular surface conditions, were not extensively controlled, which could have affected healing outcomes.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that topical vitamin A ointment significantly accelerates corneal epithelial healing after PRK surgery, particularly within the first 3 postoperative days, as confirmed by statistical analysis. These findings suggest that vitamin A ointment can be a valuable adjunct therapy to enhance corneal wound healing in PRK patients, although its role in pain management remains inconclusive. Further large‐scale studies with extended follow‐up periods are needed to explore the long‐term benefits and potential effects on postoperative complications.
