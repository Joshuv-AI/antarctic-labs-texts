Influencing factors of knowledge, attitudes, and practices regarding physical restraints among pediatric ICU nurses: a multicenter survey
Authors: Rui Zhu, Jie Zhang, Kening Ma, Jiaojiao Zang, Nan Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPhysical restraint, as a medical adjunct, is commonly employed in intensive care units. Presently, most studies on physical restraint in China focus on adult populations, with limited research and small sample sizes concerning critically ill pediatric patients. This study aims to investigate the current knowledge, attitudes, and practices regarding physical restraint among pediatric ICU nurses through a multicenter survey, analyzing the factors influencing the use of physical restraint.MethodsA questionnaire survey was conducted among 825 pediatric ICU nurses in 25 children’s hospitals by convenience sampling method. Background

Physical restraint (PR) refers to the use of medical devices attached to or placed adjacent to a patient’s body to control movement or prevent free access to the body. In the intensive care units (ICUs), this method is frequently used as an adjunct to medical care. It facilitates comprehensive monitoring and protection of children by healthcare providers while effectively reducing the risk of adverse events such as falls from beds or accidental extubation.

Due to rapid changes in condition, multiple indwelling catheters, diverse monitoring devices, and complex treatment regimens, critically ill children often require physical restraints to limit movement and ensure safe and effective treatment [1]. Currently, the reported prevalence of physical restraint use in Chinese ICUs is 61.2% [2]. However, physical restraint use varies widely across countries and healthcare settings. According to a recent systematic review and meta-analysis [3], the prevalence of physical restraint in adult ICUs ranges from 16.6% to 56% in the United States and Canada and from 32.9% to 35.8% in Japan and Jordan in ICUs. In Europe, the rates vary from 0% in Britain and Portugal to 100% in Italy. The overall prevalence of patients exposed to PRs was 41.6%. The variation is attributed to differences in definitions, patient populations, unit culture, and regulatory frameworks. Such variability indicates that physical restraint are shaped by local contextual factors. Importantly, data from China, particularly for pediatric populations, remain limited compared with international standard. Given the high prevalence of restraint use in Chinese ICUs and the established harms of inappropriate restraint, understanding these effects in critically ill children is essential. Research indicates [4] that inappropriate physical restraints not only influence patients’ physiological functions but also cause multifaceted harm to their psychological state and physical safety. Specific manifestations include skin injuries, neurological impairment, cardiac dysfunction, and fractures.

Consequently, the standardized and rational implementation of physical restraints holds significant practical importance in the clinical care of critically ill children. The ‘Knowledge–Attitude–Practice’ (KAP) model is a well-established theoretical framework in health practice research. This model posits that knowledge forms the basis for practical change, attitudes provide the motivational impetus, and practice constitutes the practical outcome. According to this model, individuals who possess accurate knowledge are more likely to develop positive attitudes, which in turn promote the adoption of appropriate practices. This framework has been widely applied in nursing education and the evaluation of clinical practice, and has been recognized by the World Health Organization as a standardized method for health practice surveys. In the context of physical restraint, the KAP model suggests that nurses’ understanding of the indications for physical restraint, the associated risks and alternative options shapes their attitudes towards the use of physical restraint, thereby influencing their clinical practice. As the primary implementers of physical restraint, nurses’ knowledge and attitudes directly influence the appropriateness and safety of practices. Existing research indicates [5] that nurses’ cognitive level and attitudes toward physical restraints are key factors affecting their practices.

Currently, research on physical restraint in China predominantly focuses on adult patients, with limited exploration of critically ill children. Existing research on critically ill children remains limited, with issues such as small sample sizes and insufficient representativeness. Therefore, this study aims to conduct a multicenter survey to systematically understand pediatric ICU nurses’ current knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) level regarding physical restraint. It will also deeply analyze the cognitive factors influencing their physical restraint decisions. This research aims to provide evidence-based support for developing scientific and pediatric physical restraint management strategies, thereby enhancing patient safety and nursing quality.

Methods

Settings and type of study

In March 2025, we used convenience sampling method to investigate pediatric ICU nurses at 25 tertiary children’s specialty hospitals in China. Tertiary and lower-level general hospitals were not included because they lack specialized pediatric intensive care units, such as pediatric cardiac intensive care units and pediatric emergency intensive care units.

Participants and sampling method

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria were: (1) Possession of a valid nursing license; (2) At least one year of experience working in an intensive care unit; (3) Informed consent and voluntary participation in this study.

Exclusion criteria were: (1) Off-duty nurses during the survey period (e.g., on maternity/sick leave, attending off-site training or education programs); (2) Trainees and rotating nurses.

Data collection instruments

General information questionnaire

The survey questionnaire was independently designed by the research team and comprises 11 items, including gender, age, working years, ICU working years, professional title, educational background, hospital level, specialty/non-specialty nurse, and whether training has been received or not.

Pediatric ICU nurses’ knowledge, attitudes, and practices regarding physical restraints questionnaire

The Knowledge-Attitudes-Practices Questionnaire on Physical Restraints was translated into Chinese by Suen et al. [6] in 2006. This study adapted the ICU Pediatric Knowledge-Attitudes-Practices Questionnaire on Physical Restraints, revised in 2018 by Hu et al. [7] to incorporate characteristics of pediatric growth and development. The questionnaire contains three dimensions, totaling 36 items.

Knowledge Dimension. The knowledge dimension covers the conditions for applying physical restraints, post-restraint care, and relevant legal knowledge, comprising 11 items in total. Scoring uses a binary scoring system: “Agree” counts as 1 point, “Disagree” as 0 points. Items 2, 9, and 11 are reverse-scored questions. The total score is 11 points. Each correctly answered item is counted as a “correct response.” The accuracy rate for each item is calculated using the formula: (Number of correct responses / Total number of responses) × 100%. A higher total score indicates a higher level of knowledge among nurses regarding the use of physical restraints on children.

The total score ranges from 11 to 55 points. Higher scores indicate a greater tendency to use such measures in clinical practice.

It employs a 3-point Likert scale, with each item offering three response options: “Never,” “Sometimes,” and “Often,” scored as 1, 2, and 3 points respectively. The KAP questionnaire used in this study was taken directly from Hu et al., with no revisions to the items or structure; its content validity has already been validated in the original study. This study reports Cronbach’s α coefficients only for the attitude and practice dimensions; for the knowledge dimension, nurses’ level of knowledge mastery is described by the average correct response rate. The average correct answer rate for the knowledge dimension was 69.8%, the Cronbach’s α coefficient for the attitude dimension was 0.785, and the Cronbach’s α coefficient for the practices dimension was 0.772.

Procedure

This study used the Wen juan xing (a popular online survey platform in China) to design and generate an electronic questionnaire, collecting data via links and QR codes. Prior to implementation, approval and support were obtained from the responsible parties of the involved hospitals and relevant departments, with questionnaire access channels distributed through hierarchical channels. To enhance data quality, the Wenjuanxing Professional Edition feature is used to randomly shuffle all items and options, thereby controlling for order effects. The survey was conducted anonymously, with all items marked as mandatory. Each IP address was restricted to a single submission. The questionnaire’s cover page included standardized instructions clearly outlining the study’s purpose, significance, and completion requirements. Nurses completed and submitted the questionnaires independently online. After data collection, two researchers independently reviewed all submitted questionnaires. Invalid responses were defined according to the following criteria: (a) all items selected the same option (e.g., all “agree” or all “never”); (b) obvious logical contradictions (e.g., strongly inconsistent answers on reverse-scored items compared to the overall pattern); (c) suspected duplicate submissions (identical response patterns across all items with extremely close submission times but different IP addresses). A total of 850 questionnaires were distributed. After excluding 25 invalid responses (18 with all identical options, 4 with logical contradictions, and 3 suspected duplicates), 825 valid questionnaires were retained, yielding a valid response rate of 97.05%.

Statistical methods

This study employed SPSS 25.0 software for data analysis. The enumeration data were statistically described using frequency and percentage (rate). Quantitative data, tested and found to be normally distributed, were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Differences in KAP scores regarding physical restraint among pediatric ICU nurses with varying characteristics were compared using t-tests or analysis of variance (ANOVA). Multivariate linear regression analysis was further conducted to explore factors influencing their KAP levels. All statistical tests were performed at a significance level of α = 0.05. Given the hierarchical structure in which nurses are nested within hospitals, we first estimated an empty random intercept multilevel model to quantify potential clustering effects. The intra-class correlation coefficient (ICC) was calculated as the variance between hospitals(σ²u) divided by the total variance(σ²u + σ²e). The variance components obtained from the null model were: σ²u = 0.264, σ²e = 46.491. The calculated ICC was 0.006, indicating that only 0.6% of the total variation in outcomes could be attributed to differences between hospitals. This extremely low ICC value comes as no surprise, as all participating hospitals are tertiary pediatric specialist institutions that are comparable in terms of organizational structure, clinical standards and staff qualification requirements, this is likely to have minimized heterogeneity between hospitals. This value is well below the conventional threshold for a meaningful clustering effect (ICC ≥ 0.05). To further verify the robustness of this decision, we calculated the design effect (Deff) = 1 + (n̄ − 1) × ICC, where n̄ is the average cluster size (n̄ = 825/25 = 33). The calculated value of Deff was 1.19, which is well below the conventional critical value of 2.0, indicating that the clustering effect will not significantly increase the variance of the estimates. Given that the ICC for the null model is close to zero and Deff is well below 2.0, it is methodologically reasonable to expect that fitting a full multilevel model will yield fixed-effect estimates that are virtually identical to those from a single-level model. Consequently, the clustering effect is considered negligible, and no further adjustment for clustering was made in the main analysis.

Results

Pediatric ICU nurses’ demographic characteristics

This study included 825 pediatric ICU nurses. In terms of age, the majority were aged 31–40 (51.4%), followed by those aged 20–30 (37.2%). There were 80 nurses aged 41–50 (9.7%) and 14 nurses aged 50 or older (1.7%). In terms of gender, females constituted the overwhelming majority (94.2%), while males numbered 48 (5.8%). Educational background was as follows: bachelor’s degree holders (84.6%), associate degree holders (13.8%), master’s degree or higher (1.2%), and secondary vocational school graduates (0.4%). Professional titles were distributed as follows: Senior Nurse Practitioners (39.6%), Nurse Practitioners (39.2%), Associate Chief Nurse Practitioners (4.2%), Nurses (16.4%), and Chief Nurse Practitioners (0.6%). Marital status: Married 554 (67.2%), Unmarried 263 (31.9%), Divorced 7 (0.8%), Other 1 (0.1%). Working years predominantly ranged from 6 to 10 years (32.6%), followed by 11 to 15 years (28.2%), less than 5 years (20.7%), 16 to 20 years (9.3%), and over 20 years (9.1%). Additionally, 276 nurses (33.5%) held specialized certifications, while 549 nurses (66.5%) did not.

The total score of 825 pediatric ICU nurses on the knowledge-attitude-practice questionnaire regarding physical restraints was 75.67 ± 6.83. Scores for each dimension were as follows: Knowledge dimension (7.67 ± 1.45), Attitude dimension (30.00 ± 6.44), and Practice dimension (37.40 ± 3.29). Discussion

Current status of knowledge, attitudes and practices regarding physical restraints among pediatric ICU nurses

The results show that the total score for knowledge, attitude, and practice regarding physical restraints among pediatric ICU nurses is (75.67 ± 6.83), with a score of (7.67 ± 1.45) for the knowledge dimension, (30.00 ± 6.44) for the attitude dimension, and (37.40 ± 3.29) for the practice dimension, indicating an intermediate level, consistent with the findings of Pan et al. [8].

In the knowledge dimension, the three lowest‑scoring items are Item 2, 9, and 11. Item 2 (“Restraints should be used when close monitoring of the child is not possible”) received a low score, indicating misconceptions among pediatric ICU nurses regarding the indications and initiation principles of physical restraint. The erroneous inclusion of “inability to provide timely care” as a justification for restraint use violates the core principle of minimizing restraint and limiting its application to genuine safety emergencies, thereby posing a risk of over‑restraint in clinical practice. Item 9 (“Clinically, there are no alternatives to physical restraints”) was among the lowest‑scoring items, suggesting that nurses’ knowledge about non‑restraint alternative interventions and insufficient clinical application skills may be inadequate. Nearly 50% of the surveyed nurses lacked relevant knowledge of restraint alternatives, a finding that is consistent with the pattern of excessive restraint use in clinical nursing practice. Item 11 (“In emergencies, caregivers may apply restraints without prior physician orders”) received a low score, suggesting a poor grasp of compliance procedures for emergency restraint application. According to the Guidelines for the Care of Physical Restraints for Inpatients, nurses may initiate restraint interventions in emergency high‑risk situations but must subsequently complete retroactive documentation of the medical order and dynamically reassess the necessity of restraint within a specified timeframe. However, most nurses had a vague understanding of the procedural boundaries and compliance requirements for emergency restraints, posing potential risks to nursing safety and legal compliance.

Survey results revealed suboptimal nursing knowledge of patient physical restraint in China, attributable to inadequate specialized training, heterogeneous restraint management protocols among medical institutions at different tiers, and deficient clinical quality control execution [9]. Compared with China, international minimal-restraint governance developed at an earlier stage: Ontario, Canada legislated the Minimizing Patient Restraints Act in 2001 to legally enforce restraint reduction, forming a sophisticated and fully standardized management framework [10]. Whereas in China, the relevant national standard remains a recommended industry specification rather than compulsory regulation [11]. This regulatory gap, along with the scarcity of standardized clinical guidance, may partly explain the suboptimal knowledge levels observed in our study.

In the attitude dimension of this survey, Items 1, 7 and 11 yielded the lowest scores, where higher scores correspond to greater nurses’ preference for physical restraint application, and lower scores denote a positive stance toward restraint reduction. Item 7 (“I believe restraints reduce the risk of children falling out of bed”) obtained the minimum score, implying most nurses deny the fall-prevention effectiveness of physical restraints, inconsistent with the results reported by Zhu et al. [12]. Scientific restraint management systems lay the foundation for standardized clinical implementation. As pediatric ICU children commonly receive pharmacological sedation, comprehensive assessments of disease status, sedation depth and muscular strength enable nurses to avoid falls and redundant restraints fundamentally via diversified alternatives, including modified hospital beds, standardized pipelines and bed rails, reduced bed height, positioning cushions and family-centered care. Accordingly, dynamic whole-cycle assessment and routine specialized training focusing on restraint minimization and substitute interventions are recommended. Prioritized interventions should rectify nurses’ excessive dependence on restraints as reflected in Items 1 and 11, eliminate the flawed stereotype that restraints prevent falls, and promote evidence-based restraint reduction centered on pediatric patients’ optimal benefits.

Items 2, 10 and 11 presented the minimum scores for the practice dimension, with Item 10 (“Restraint is used more frequently when staffing levels are low”) showing notably lower values. This finding reveals a critical clinical drawback: insufficient staffing and excessive workload hinder regular patient rounds, forcing nurses to determine restraint initiation and discontinuation empirically and consequently elevating restraint prevalence. To mitigate such drawbacks, a pediatric physical restraint reduction management team consisting of multidisciplinary clinicians and nurses is proposed for pediatric ICUs. The team should formulate standardized restraint-reduction protocols to facilitate rational restraint administration and shorten restraint duration among critically ill children [13]. In addition, the absence of standardized restraint assessment tools in partial departments impedes quantitative restraint necessity evaluation and raises the incidence of restraint-associated complications. Accordingly, nursing administrators should popularize relevant industry specifications via diversified approaches to consolidate nurses’ theoretical proficiency and optimize restraint-related clinical practices.

Factors influencing pediatric ICU nurses’ knowledge, attitudes, and practices regarding physical restraints

Training

Multiple linear regression analysis indicates that restraint-related training was positively associated with nurses’ restraint practices (β = 0.133, P < 0.001). This finding is consistent with previous studies, which have suggested that systematic training may be associated with higher levels of nurses’ knowledge and self-reported performance in restraint-related scenarios [14–15]. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis of educational interventions in pediatric nursing further confirmed this association. The study synthesized evidence from 11 studies involving 1,322 nurses and reported that structured education was associated with nurses’ knowledge, attitudes and practice regarding pediatric pain management. However, it is important to note that the impact on practice outcomes was only moderate and markedly smaller than the impact on knowledge, this may indicate that the increase in knowledge resulting from training does not necessarily translate into changes in practice—a pattern that echoes the gap between attitudes and practice observed in the present study [16]. Furthermore, there is often a discrepancy between institutional restraint policies and their actual implementation; the majority of nurses remain unfamiliar with the relevant guidelines and fail to apply assessment criteria correctly. Such gaps in policy implementation stem from inadequate training, manifested specifically as poor relevance, interruptions in training and a lack of practicality, whilst an incomplete regulatory framework at hospital level further exacerbates this situation, with many medical centers lacking unified restraint guidelines and standardized specifications for assessment tools [17, 18].These comprehensive shortcomings in policy formulation and staff training may contribute to inconsistencies in restraint practices. As restraint management is a systemic issue closely linked to ward safety culture, quality control and training systems [19], merely extending the duration or increasing the frequency of training without simultaneously optimizing policies may be insufficient to achieve continuous improvement in restraint management.

International research evidence has demonstrated that structured training on physical restraints in intensive care units (ICUs) is highly effective, evidence-based, systematic training is consistently associated with nursing staff’s theoretical knowledge and practical skills [20]. Therefore, effective training may need to emphasize the internalization of knowledge and improvements in practice. As with the use of clinical assessment tools, the practical value of restraint assessment tools depends on standardized training for users [21]. Taking into account the findings of this study alongside existing published evidence, hospitals may consider refining their institutional policies on restraint management and developing systematic training programs that include regular refresher courses and post-training assessments [22]. At the same time, national professional consensus statements and industry standards regarding physical restraints could be more widely promoted, which may contribute to nurses’ professional understanding and optimize the quality of restraint care.

Attitude

Multiple linear regression analysis revealed a significant negative correlation between nurses’ attitude scores toward physical restraints and their restraint practices (β = −0.153, P < 0.001). It should be noted that, although the original scale was designed such that a higher score indicates a greater tendency to use restraints, the results of this study reveal the opposite pattern: nurses with higher attitude scores are, in fact, more opposed to the use of restraints. Within the clinical setting of this study, nurses’ negative perceptions of the risks associated with physical restraints were correlated with lower self-reported restraint use, which is consistent with the core tenets of the Knowledge-Attitude-Practice (KAP) model. Namely, that attitude may play a mediating role in the relationship between knowledge and clinical practice and is also consistent with the findings of the cross-sectional study by Köse et al. [23], which indicated that nurses with higher attitude scores were more opposed to the use of physical restraints.

It is worth noting that the original developers of the scale, Hu et al., reported in 2018, within the context of their original study, that high attitude scores were associated with a greater tendency to use restraints. This apparent discrepancy may be considered from the theoretical perspective of the “attitude-practice gap” theory, which suggests that the relationship between attitudes and practices may be influenced by a variety of situational factors, including workload, staffing ratios, the availability of alternative measures, and organizational safety culture [24]. Differences in clinical context between the period of the original scale development and our current study, particularly in terms of restraint management philosophy, promotion of alternatives, and standardized training, may offer a possible explanation for the divergent findings. This comparison does not imply a rejection of either set of findings, but rather highlights the crucial role of contextual factors in shaping the process by which attitudes are translated into practice.

Apart from specific attitudes towards restraint measures, research indicates that broader educational interventions have a more fundamental impact on the professional attitudes of pediatric nurses. A quasi-experimental study by Coşkun et al. demonstrated that family-centered care (FCC) training was associated with improvements in pediatric nurses’ attitudes towards collaborative practice with families [25]. This finding suggests that attitudes within pediatric settings may be positively influenced by structured education, and that such changes may extend beyond technical knowledge to encompass communication with families and shared decision-making. In the context of physical restraint, integrating family-centered care principles—particularly involving family members in discussions regarding alternatives to restraint and seeking their input on personalized care plans—represents a potential complementary approach that could be explored to influence nurses’ attitudes and, consequently, their practice.

Although there was a significant negative correlation between attitude and practice, the regression model with practice score as the dependent variable yielded an R² of 0.083 and an adjusted R² of 0.069 (F = 5.669, P < 0.001), suggesting that the included predictor variables could explain only 6.9% to 8.3% of the variance in practice. Such a low explanatory power indicates that nurses’ decisions regarding restraint are largely driven by contextual confounding factors not included in the model: even when nurses possess sound cognitive understanding of the need to reduce restraint, their actual practice remains heavily constrained by real-world conditions, representing a prominent practical dilemma in pediatric ICU settings. Given that the effect sizes are generally small and there is a significant amount of unexplained variance, the practical significance of statistically significant predictors should be viewed with caution, and they should not be overemphasized as key determinants guiding clinical practice. Some nurses regard restraint as the sole means of ensuring patients’ safety, whilst excessive workloads reinforce the use of restraint, further contributing to adverse physical and psychological outcomes for pediatric patients [26, 27]. The root cause of this contradiction may be partially explained by underlying ethical conflicts; resource shortages, inadequate staffing levels and institutional regulations may contribute to situations in which nurses use restraints contrary to their professional judgement, which may in turn contribute to moral distress [28]. This phenomenon is highly consistent with data from other reports [29] indicating widespread ethical conflicts among pediatric ICU nurses in their decision-making regarding the use of restraints. Furthermore, key unmeasured variables, such as ethical dilemmas, may account for a significant proportion of the variation not captured by the model, future research should prioritize the inclusion of these variables.

Currently, a variety of validated restraint assessment tools have been developed to support pre-implementation evaluations [30–33]. However, organizational culture and institutional processes play a critical role in translating attitudes into practice. A lack of standardized regulations may compromise restraint management standards [34], whereas a harm reduction-oriented culture can optimize practice [21]. Therefore, fostering a “minimal restraint” culture, establishing evidence-based guidelines, selecting appropriate assessment tools, and conducting regular quality improvement initiatives are essential [35].

Demographic factors: age and professional title

After adjusting for confounding factors, a multiple linear regression analysis revealed demographic variables that were independently associated with the total Knowledge-Attitude-Practice (KAP) score. Compared with nurses aged under 30, participants aged 31–40 (β = −0.111, P = 0.020) and those aged 50 and over (β = −0.078, P = 0.039) had significantly lower KAP total scores. These associations may be related to age groups in terms of knowledge updating, clinical proficiency and participation in training. Whilst younger nurses are able to acquire the latest knowledge more quickly, they lack practical experience; nurses in the middle age group achieve the optimal balance between theory and practice; whereas more senior nurses face issues of outdated knowledge and insufficient continuing professional development [36]. A cross-sectional study of 158 ICU nurses found that age was negatively correlated with attitude scores (r = − 0.229, p = 0.004), and that the longer the professional experience, the less supportive the attitude towards the use of physical restraints (r = − 0.174, p = 0.029) [37].

In terms of professional rank, compared with Assistant Registered Nurses, Registered Nurses performed better in both the practice dimension (β = 0.139, P = 0.004) and overall Knowledge, Attitude and Practice (KAP) scores (β = 0.124, P = 0.018), which is consistent with the possibility that career progression and accumulated experience may be associated with improved standardized restraint management [38]. No statistically significant difference was found between senior registered nurses and advanced registered nurses; this finding may be related to differences in clinical exposure and administrative responsibilities that may limit opportunities for direct bedside decision-making regarding patient restraint, although this interpretation requires further investigation. Factors such as the number of children and specialist nursing qualifications lost their univariate statistical significance after multivariate adjustment and they were not retained as statistically significant independent variables in the multivariate model.

Departmental factors: practice differences across ICU subspecialties

Multiple regression analysis revealed differences in knowledge and practice regarding physical restraints across the various subspecialties within intensive care units (ICUs).

Compared with the pediatric intensive care unit (PICU), the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) (β = 0.093, P = 0.007) and the respiratory intensive care unit (RICU) (β = 0.197, P < 0.001) had higher knowledge scores than the pediatric intensive care unit (PICU), whilst the emergency intensive care unit (EICU, β=−0.119, P = 0.001) and the coronary care unit (CCU, β=−0.086, P = 0.013) had lower scores. These differences may be related to variations in patient characteristics, the frequency of restraint use, and the training priorities of each unit. The fragility of neonates may be related to NICU nurses’ proactive acquisition of knowledge on reducing restraints, the frequent use of non-invasive ventilation in the RICU, meanwhile, may be associated with a need for standardized assessment of indications. In contrast, emergency trauma and post-operative cases in the EICU may involve greater emphasis on rapid, experience-based decision-making rather than theoretical knowledge, whilst established sedation protocols in the CCU may be associated with restraint training being a secondary priority.

In terms of practice outcomes, the EICU (β = −0.086, P = 0.013) and SICU (β = −0.101, P = 0.004) scored lower. A cross-sectional study by Wang et al. involving 1,086 pediatric patients in the PICU confirmed through logistic regression analysis that mechanical ventilation, indwelling catheters and sedation are key determinants of restraint [39]. Although SICU nurses possess the same level of knowledge, their restraint practices are more appropriate, which raises the possibility that this subspecialty may have a well-developed local restraint management program and may utilize a variety of alternative interventions.

It is recommended that targeted, differentiated training be provided for management staff to optimize restraint management. Clinical decision-making training may be considered for newly recruited nurses, whilst regular continuing professional development could be beneficial for senior nurses to update their specialist knowledge; the mentoring capabilities of senior nurses should be fully utilized to provide on-site clinical guidance. In addition, the differences between subspecialties may inform tailored optimization strategies: intensive, standardized restraint training may be beneficial in EICUs and CCUs, where knowledge is lacking, and the effective restraint minimization protocols used in SICUs could be considered for broader dissemination to other ICU departments.

Limitations

There are some limitations to this study. First, participants may not be representative of all pediatric ICU nurses due to the convenience sampling method used to select the study population. This study employed convenience sampling, with the entire sample drawn from 25 tertiary pediatric specialist hospitals. Whilst this approach ensured an adequate sample size and enhanced data collection efficiency, it may also have introduced selection bias. This primarily includes: (1) Institutional-level bias: Tertiary pediatric specialist hospitals typically exhibit systematic differences from non-tertiary hospitals in terms of medical resources, clinical standards, staffing levels and the complexity of patients’ conditions; consequently, the levels of knowledge, attitudes or practice among healthcare professionals reflected in this study may be higher than those found in primary care or relatively resource-poor non-specialist healthcare institutions. (2) Limited generalizability: Caution should be exercised when extrapolating the findings of this study to non-specialist hospitals or mixed-level hospital groups. The service models, training resources and patient demographics of these institutions differ from those of tertiary specialist hospitals, and the relationships between variables observed in this study may not be directly applicable. (3) Inherent bias in convenience sampling: Convenience sampling cannot guarantee randomness in terms of geographical distribution, hospital size or management willingness; unobserved selection factors may exist, thereby affecting the robustness of the results. It is strongly recommended that the study findings should not be over-generalized to secondary general hospitals with pediatric wards, primary care facilities without pediatric ICU facilities, or other institutions with differing resource levels. It is recommended that future studies employ stratified random sampling or multi-stage sampling to include healthcare institutions of different levels and types, thereby enhancing the external validity and generalizability of the conclusions. Second, self-assessment questionnaires may be subject to social desirability bias, as nurses may report practices they believe to be better than their actual practice. Although we have ensured anonymity and emphasized that there are no right or wrong answers, this bias cannot be entirely eliminated. Third, we acknowledge that the model’s explanatory power is limited. This may be due to the large sample size, which could have led to even weak effects reaching statistical significance, or to the omission of key independent variables. We have highlighted this limitation in the discussion section and suggested that future research include independent variables such as ethical dilemmas to enhance the model’s explanatory power. Fourth, Although this questionnaire was directly adopted from a previously validated scale without modifications, its factor structure has not been further tested in the current multicenter sample, which constitutes a limitation of this study. In addition, because our survey was a cross-sectional survey, only correlation, not causation, could be inferred. It is recommended that future studies employ a longitudinal design or experimental research to establish causality.

Conclusions

The total knowledge, attitudes, and practices scores for physical restraints among pediatric ICU nurses were (75.67 ± 6.83) scores, and this score was statistically associated with age, professional title, department, prior training in restraints, and attitudes toward restraint use. Currently, significant variations exist in restraint management practices across healthcare institutions. It is recommended that nursing administrators develop evidence-based, customized training programs tailored to their specific settings. They should promote existing national consensus guidelines and evidence summaries, implement tiered training initiatives for nurses, standardize restraint practices, and ensure patient benefit.
