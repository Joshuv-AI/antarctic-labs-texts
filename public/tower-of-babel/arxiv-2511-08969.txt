Hadamard products of dual Jacobi–Trudi matrices
Robert Angarone, Jang Soo Kim, Jaeseong Oh, Daniel Soskin

Abstract
. We study positivity properties of Hadamard products of Jacobi–Trudi matrices. Maló proved that the Hadamard (entrywise) product of two totally positive upper-triangular Toeplitz matrices whose Toeplitz sequences are the coefficient sequences of real-rooted polynomials with nonpositive zeros is again totally positive. Sokal conjectured that this result can be strengthened to total monomial positivity for the Hadamard product of Jacobi–Trudi matrices. In this paper we show that Temperley–Lieb immanants are Schur positive for Hadamard products of Jacobi–Trudi matrices given by ribbon-like skew shapes. In particular, we affirm Sokal’s conjecture for minors given by ribbon-like skew shapes. Moreover, we provide a manifestly positive Schur expansion for Temperley–Lieb immanants evaluated on the Hadamard product of Jacobi–Trudi matrices indexed by ribbons. In addition, for the ribbon case, we construct a corresponding representation, offering a representation-theoretic proof of the Schur positivity.



1.
Introduction

A matrix is said to be
totally positive
if all of its minors are nonnegative. Total positivity arises in various areas of mathematics and has wide-ranging applications. Originally studied by Gantmacher and Krein
[
4
,
5
]
in the context of classical analysis and numerical interpolation, total positivity has since become a central topic in representation theory, geometry, mathematical physics, and algebraic combinatorics (see the survey by Fomin
[
3
]
and references therein).

By the Cauchy–Binet theorem, it is immediate that the product

MN
of two totally positive matrices
M
and
N
is also
totally positive. However, their Hadamard (entrywise) product

M*N
need not be totally positive in general.
Nevertheless, there exists a class of matrices whose total positivity is preserved under the Hadamard product.
A
Toeplitz matrix
with
Toeplitz sequence
(a_{0},a_{1},\ldots)
is an infinite, upper-triangular matrix
of the form
(a_{j-i})_{i,j=0}^{\infty}
.
The
Laguerre–Pólya class
is the class of entire functions that arise as uniform limits of
univariate polynomials with nonnegative coefficients and real roots.
Maló
[
10
]
proved that the Hadamard (entrywise) product of two totally positive Toeplitz matrices remains totally positive, provided that their Toeplitz sequences are the coefficient sequences of series in the Laguerre–Pólya class.

For an entire function
p(t)=\prod_{i\geq 0}(1+\alpha_{i}t)
in the Laguerre–Pólya class, if we replace the nonnegative real numbers
\alpha_{i}\geq 0
by variables
x_{i}
, then the coefficient of
t^{k}
in
p(t)
becomes the
elementary symmetric function

e_{k}(\mathbf{x})
in the variables
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},\dots)
. In this way, the Toeplitz matrix whose entries are given by the coefficients of
p(t)
can be regarded as a special case of the following matrix:

M(\mathbf{x})=\bigl(e_{j-i}(\mathbf{x})\bigr)_{i,j\geq 0}.

The minors of this matrix are determinants of the
(dual) Jacobi–Trudi matrices

\mathcal{E}_{\lambda/\mu}(\mathbf{x}):=\bigl(e_{\lambda_{i}-\mu_{j}-i+j}(\mathbf{x})\bigr)_{i,j=1}^{\ell(\lambda)}.

Here
\lambda/\mu
is a skew shape, and
\ell(\lambda)
denotes the number of parts of the partition
\lambda
. By the Jacobi–Trudi identity and the Littlewood–Richardson rule, every minor of
M(\mathbf{x})
is Schur-positive, and hence monomial-positive. Schur positivity and monomial positivity are stronger notions of positivity than positivity of real numbers.
In connection with Maló’s theorem, one may then ask whether the total positivity of the Hadamard product of two Toeplitz matrices
M(\mathbf{x})
and
M(\mathbf{y})
, when
x_{i},y_{j}\geq 0
, can be strengthened to the monomial positivity of each minor. Very recently, Sokal
[
16
]
formulated the following conjecture on the monomial positivity of the Hadamard product of
M(\mathbf{x})
and
M(\mathbf{y})
for distinct sequences of variables
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},\dots)
and
\mathbf{y}=(y_{1},y_{2},\dots)
.

Conjecture 1.1

(Sokal’s conjecture)
.

The Hadamard product
M(\mathbf{x})*M(\mathbf{y})
is totally
monomial positive. In other words, every minor

\det(\mathcal{E}_{\lambda/\mu}(\mathbf{x})*\mathcal{E}_{\lambda/\mu}(\mathbf{y}))

is a multi-symmetric function in the variables
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}
with
nonnegative integer coefficients.

In the present work, we study a strengthening of Sokal’s conjecture along several axes. First, we allow products of Jacobi–Trudi matrices indexed by different skew shapes. In other words, we consider the Hadamard product of an arbitrary minor of
M(\mathbf{x})
with an arbitrary minor of
M(\mathbf{y})
. Second, we consider Hadamard products of
k
Jacobi–Trudi matrices, rather than just two. Lastly, we generalize the determinant to an arbitrary
Temperley–Lieb immanant
, which is a certain generalization of the determinant introduced by Rhoades and Skandera
[
12
]
.

Conjecture 1.2
.

Given any Temperley–Lieb immanant
\operatorname{imm}_{\tau}
and any family of
skew shapes

\lambda^{(1)}/\mu^{(1)},\ldots,\lambda^{(k)}/\mu^{(k)}
, the
multi-symmetric function

\operatorname{imm}_{\tau}\left(\mathcal{E}_{\lambda^{(1)}/\mu^{(1)}}(\mathbf{x}^{(1)})*\cdots*\mathcal{E}_{\lambda^{(k)}/\mu^{(k)}}(\mathbf{x}^{(k)})\right)

is monomial positive.

While
1.1
and our stronger
1.2
remain open, we prove that the conjectures hold in the special case when each
\lambda^{(i)}/\mu^{(i)}
is a skew shape not containing a
3\times 2
block of cells. In fact, in this scenario, we demonstrate that the expression in
1.2
is Schur positive.

Theorem 1.3
.

Suppose
\lambda^{(1)}/\mu^{(1)},\ldots,\lambda^{(k)}/\mu^{(k)}
is a collection of skew shapes each not containing a
3\times 2
block of cells. Then for any Temperley–Lieb immanant
\operatorname{imm}_{\tau}
, the multi-symmetric function

\operatorname{imm}_{\tau}\left(\mathcal{E}_{\lambda^{(1)}/\mu^{(1)}}(\mathbf{x}^{(1)})*\cdots*\mathcal{E}_{\lambda^{(k)}/\mu^{(k)}}(\mathbf{x}^{(k)})\right)

is Schur positive.

We focus on this restricted class of skew shapes for three key reasons. First,
1.1
and
1.2
will not always extend to Schur positivity if the skew shapes do not all avoid a
3\times 2
block. For example, the expression
\det(\mathcal{E}_{(2,2,2)}(\mathbf{x})*\mathcal{E}_{(2,2,2)}(\mathbf{y}))
is not Schur positive.

Second, the proof of
1.3
involves techniques introduced by Rhoades and Skandera in
[
12
]
. Said techniques can be viewed as a generalization of the sign-reversing involution normally used to prove the Lindström–Gessel–Viennot lemma.
However, we demonstrate that a similar sign-reversing involution argument cannot work to prove
1.1
or
1.2
when the collection of skew shapes is not
essentially
(3\times 2)
-avoiding
, a mild generalization of the condition in
1.3
. See
3.18
and the ensuing discussion.

Third, skew shapes avoiding a
3\times 2
block generalize the well-known
ribbons
, which are skew shapes avoiding a
2\times 2
block. In the special case when each
\lambda^{(i)}/\mu^{(i)}
is a ribbon, we can write the Schur expansion from
1.3
explicitly in terms of standard Young tableaux with restricted descent sets.
See
3.10
.

In particular, when taking the Hadamard product of a ribbon Jacobi–Trudi matrix with
itself, we obtain the following the following explicit formula.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
R
be a ribbon of size
m
. Then we have a manifestly positive Schur expansion

\det\left(\mathcal{E}_{R}(\mathbf{x})*\mathcal{E}_{R}(\mathbf{y})\right)=\sum_{\lambda,\mu\vdash m}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I,J\subseteq[m-1]\\
I\cap J=\operatorname{Des}(R^{\prime})\end{subarray}}f^{\lambda}(I)f^{\mu}(J)\,s_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x})s_{\mu}(\mathbf{y}),

where
s_{\lambda}
is the Schur function,
f^{\lambda}(I)

is the number of standard Young tableaux of shape
\lambda

with descent set
I
, and
\operatorname{Des}(R)
is the descent set of

R
.

We explore the latter result further by constructing an

\mathfrak{S}_{n}\times\mathfrak{S}_{n}
-module whose Frobenius
image equals the determinant in
1.4
, thereby establishing Schur positivity from the
representation-theoretic perspective. See
4.4
.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we review background material on multi-symmetric
functions, lattice paths, immanants, and the representation theory of products of symmetric groups.
Section
3
is devoted to the proofs of Theorems
1.3
and
1.4
.
Section
4
concerns the construction of the
\mathfrak{S}_{n}\times\mathfrak{S}_{n}
-module that realizes
1.4
.
Finally, Section
5
discusses some open problems and
directions for future research.

Let
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},\dots)
be an infinite sequence of

symmetric function
in the variables
\mathbf{x}

is a formal power series that is symmetric in the sense that for any

Let
\Lambda(\mathbf{x})
denote the graded ring of symmetric

\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{\ell})

where the summation is over all permutations

(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{\ell},0,0,\dots)
. The

and

associated with a partition

h_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x}):=h_{\lambda_{1}}(\mathbf{x})h_{\lambda_{2}}(\mathbf{x})\cdots\quad\text{and}\quad e_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x}):=e_{\lambda_{1}}(\mathbf{x})e_{\lambda_{2}}(\mathbf{x})\cdots,

respectively, where

h_{d}(\mathbf{x}):=\sum_{i_{1}\leq\cdots\leq i_{d}}x_{i_{1}}\cdots x_{i_{d}}\quad\text{and}\quad e_{d}(\mathbf{x}):=\sum_{i_{1}<\cdots<i_{d}}x_{i_{1}}\cdots x_{i_{d}}.

We define
h_{0}(\mathbf{x})=e_{0}(\mathbf{x})=1

.

One of the most prominent symmetric functions is the Schur function
s_{\lambda}
. The
(skew) Schur function

is defined via the (dual) Jacobi–Trudi identity:

s_{\lambda/\mu}(\mathbf{x})=\left(e_{\lambda^{\prime}_{i}-\mu^{\prime}_{j}-i+j}(\mathbf{x})\right)_{i,j=1}^{\ell(\lambda^{\prime})}.

When

, the function

is referred

indexed by
\lambda
.

We define
(dual) Jacobi–Trudi matrices

\mathcal{E}_{\lambda/\mu}(\mathbf{x})
by

\mathcal{E}_{\lambda/\mu}(\mathbf{x}):=\left(e_{\lambda_{i}-\mu_{j}-i+j}(\mathbf{x})\right)_{i,j=1}^{\ell(\lambda)}.

Note that, with this notation,

\det\mathcal{E}_{\lambda/\mu}(\mathbf{x})=s_{\lambda^{\prime}/\mu^{\prime}}(\mathbf{x})
.

Remark 2.1
.

Our definition of dual Jacobi–Trudi matrices
differs from the standard convention by a conjugation of
\lambda/\mu
.
When using the standard convention, the reader should take this change into account.
For example, results stated in terms of
(3\times 2)
-avoiding skew shapes
would instead concern
(2\times 3)
-avoiding skew shapes.

Note that each
\det\left(\mathcal{E}_{\lambda/\mu}(\mathbf{x})\right)
is a minor of the matrix

M(\mathbf{x})=(e_{i-j}(\mathbf{x}))_{i,j\geq 0}
introduced earlier,
and in fact all minors of
M(\mathbf{x})
arise this way.

where

, with

denoting the number of entries equal to

in

Each of the families

forms a basis for the space of symmetric functions

We extend the definition of the involution

\{b_{\lambda}\}
of

-positive

\lambda^{(1)},\dots,\lambda^{(k)}\in\operatorname{Par}
.

Suppose we are given a list of matrices

A^{(1)}=\left(a^{(1)}_{i,j}(\mathbf{x}^{(1)})\right)_{1\leq i,j\leq n}\,,\,\,\,\dots\,\,\,,\,A^{(k)}=\left(a^{(k)}_{i,j}(\mathbf{x}^{(k)})\right)_{1\leq i,j\leq n},

where the entries

.
We define the

A^{(1)}*\cdots*A^{(k)}
as the entrywise product:

A^{(1)}*\cdots*A^{(k)}=\left(a^{(1)}_{i,j}(\mathbf{x}^{(1)})\cdots a^{(k)}_{i,j}(\mathbf{x}^{(k)})\right)_{1\leq i,j\leq n},

whose entries lie in
\Lambda(\mathbf{x}^{(1)})\otimes\cdots\otimes\Lambda(\mathbf{x}^{(k)})
.

In

, we will consider Hadamard products of several Jacobi–Trudi matrices, possibly for skew shapes with different numbers of rows.
To that end, we define

\mathcal{E}_{\lambda/\mu}^{n}(\mathbf{x}):=\left(e_{\lambda_{i}-\mu_{j}-i+j}(\mathbf{x})\right)_{i,j=1}^{n}.

Note that, for any

, we have

\det\left(\mathcal{E}_{\lambda/\mu}^{n}(\mathbf{x})\right)=\det\left(\mathcal{E}_{\lambda/\mu}^{m}(\mathbf{x})\right),

which follows easily from the definition.
Hence, we write

\det\left(\mathcal{E}_{\lambda/\mu}(\mathbf{x})\right)

\det(\mathcal{E}_{\lambda/\mu}^{n}(\mathbf{x}))

.
More generally,
whenever

for all
i
, we have

\det\left(\mathcal{E}_{\lambda^{(1)}/\mu^{(1)}}^{n}(\mathbf{x}^{(1)})*\cdots*\mathcal{E}_{\lambda^{(k)}/\mu^{(k)}}^{n}(\mathbf{x}^{(k)})\right)=\det\left(\mathcal{E}_{\lambda^{(1)}/\mu^{(1)}}^{m}(\mathbf{x}^{(1)})*\cdots*\mathcal{E}_{\lambda^{(k)}/\mu^{(k)}}^{m}(\mathbf{x}^{(k)})\right).

So, we define any of these expressions to be

\det\left(\mathcal{E}_{\lambda^{(1)}/\mu^{(1)}}(\mathbf{x}^{(1)})*\cdots*\mathcal{E}_{\lambda^{(k)}/\mu^{(k)}}(\mathbf{x}^{(k)})\right)

without risk of confusion. The same is true when the determinant is replaced with any immanant
\operatorname{imm}_{f}
; see
Section

2.3
.

2.2.
Lattice paths and networks

The key idea for the proofs of
1.3
and
1.4
is to interpret the entries of Jacobi–Trudi matrices as generating functions for paths in directed graphs, in the spirit of the Lindström–Gessel–Viennot lemma.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be the directed graph with vertex set
\mathbb{Z}^{2}

and edges of the form
(a,b)\to(a,b-1)
and
(a,b)\to(a-1,b-1)
for all
(a,b)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
.
We call these edges
south
and
southwest
steps, respectively.
We will refer to paths in
\mathcal{L}
as
(south-southwest) lattice paths
.
For
A,B\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, we write
p:A\to B
to mean that
p
is a south-southwest lattice path
that begins at
A
and ends at
B
.
Suppose
p
is a south-southwest lattice path
and
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots)
is an infinite sequence of variables.
Then let
\operatorname{wt}_{\mathbf{x}}(p)
denote the
\mathbf{x}
-weight
of
p
,
which is the product of
x_{b}
for each southwest step
(a,b)\to(a-1,b-1)
in
p
.
See
Figure

1
for an example.

To model Jacobi–Trudi matrices, we allow certain infinite lattice paths.
We write
p:(a,\infty)\to B
to mean that,

the path

(a,M)\to(a,M-1)
for

.

Suppose
\lambda/\mu
is a skew shape with
\ell(\lambda)\leq n
.
Then, for each
1\leq i\leq n
, define

\begin{array}[]{ccc}A(\lambda)_{i}:=(\lambda_{i}+n-i,\infty)&\text{and}&B(\mu)_{i}:=(\mu_{i}+n-i,0).\end{array}

Observe that

\sum_{p:A(\lambda)_{i}\to B(\mu)_{j}}\operatorname{wt}_{\mathbf{x}}(p)=e_{\lambda_{i}-\mu_{j}-i+j}(\mathbf{x})=(\mathcal{E}^{n}_{\lambda/\mu}(\mathbf{x}))_{i,j}.

A
(\lambda,\mu)
-path family
is a tuple
\mathbf{p}=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{n})
of lattice paths with
p_{i}:A(\lambda)_{i}\to B(\mu)_{\sigma(i)}
for all
1\leq i\leq n
and some permutation
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
.
The permutation
\sigma
is called the
type
of
\mathbf{p}
and is denoted
\operatorname{type}(\mathbf{p})
. The
\mathbf{x}
-weight
of a path family is the product of the
\mathbf{x}
-weights of the individual paths in
\mathbf{p}
, and is denoted
\operatorname{wt}_{\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{p})
.
Two paths are said to be
nonintersecting
if they have no vertices in common.
A path family is said to be
nonintersecting
if its paths are pairwise nonintersecting.

With this notation in hand, we may express the determinant of a dual Jacobi–Trudi matrix as

(2.3)

\det\left(\mathcal{E}^{n}_{\lambda/\mu}(\mathbf{x})\right)=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}\sum_{\operatorname{type}(\mathbf{p})=\sigma}\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)\operatorname{wt}_{\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{p}),

where the inner sum ranges over all
(\lambda,\mu)
-path families
\mathbf{p}
.
Since the graph
\mathcal{L}
is planar, by the Lindström–Gessel–Viennot lemma, we have the following identity
in the case of dual Jacobi–Trudi matrices:

(2.4)

\det\left(\mathcal{E}^{n}_{\lambda/\mu}(\mathbf{x})\right)=\sum_{\mathbf{p}}\operatorname{wt}_{\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{p}),

where the sum ranges over all nonintersecting
(\lambda,\mu)
-path families
\mathbf{p}
.

As stated in the previous subsection,
\det(\mathcal{E}^{n}_{\lambda/\mu}(\mathbf{x}))

does not depend on the choice of
n\geq\ell(\lambda)
.
This can also be seen from (
2.4
): increasing the value of
n
corresponds to appending vertical paths with trivial weight to each path family
\mathbf{p}
.
Accordingly, in what follows, we sometimes omit the parameter
n
when discussing
(\lambda,\mu)
-path families.

2.3.
Temperley–Lieb Immanants

Given a function
f:\mathfrak{S}_{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
and an
n\times n
matrix
M
, the
f
-immanant
of
M
is

(2.5)

\operatorname{imm}_{f}(M):=\sum_{w\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}f(w)M_{1,w(1)}\cdots M_{1,w(n)}.

One particularly well-studied family of immanants are
ordinary immanants
.
Ordinary immanants are obtained by setting
f=\chi^{\lambda}
, which is the
irreducible character of
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
associated to the partition
\lambda\vdash n
.
Notably, the ordinary immanants with
f=\chi^{(1^{n})}
and
f=\chi^{(n)}
coincide with
the determinant and the permanent, respectively.

The study of immanants has significantly expanded the theory of
determinants. They have been investigated in contexts such as
semi-definite Hermitian matrices
[
14
]
, totally positive
matrices
[
21
]
, and Jacobi–Trudi matrices. Goulden
and Jackson
[
6
]
initiated the study of immanants of
Jacobi–Trudi matrices. They conjectured that ordinary immanants of
Jacobi–Trudi matrices are monomial positive. This conjecture was later
confirmed by Greene
[
7
]
.

In addition to ordinary immanants,
Temperley–Lieb immanants
have been studied for Jacobi–Trudi matrices. These immanants were introduced by Rhoades and Skandera
[
12
]
and can be defined via the combinatorics of nonintersecting matchings. Much like ordinary immanants, they include the determinant as a special case.
In
[
13
]
, Rhoades and Skandera showed the Schur positivity of Temperley–Lieb immanants of generalized Jacobi–Trudi matrices.
They did this by demonstrating that Temperley–Lieb immanants are a subclass of
Kazhdan–Lusztig immanants

[
13
, Proposition 5]
,
whose Schur positivity follows from the work of Haiman
[
8
, Theorem 1.5]
.
In their recent work
[
11
]
, Nguyen and Pylyavskyy used crystal operators to provide a combinatorial interpretation for the coefficients of Temperley–Lieb immanants of Jacobi–Trudi matrices when expanded in the Schur basis.

We now present the basic definitions underlying Temperley–Lieb immanants.
Given a formal parameter
\xi
, the
Temperley–Lieb algebra

\operatorname{TL}_{n}(\xi)
is the associative
\mathbb{C}
-algebra generated by

t_{1},\dotsc,t_{n-1}
and subject to the relations

\displaystyle t_{i}^{2}

\displaystyle=\xi t_{i},

\displaystyle\text{for }i=1,\dotsc,n-1,

\displaystyle t_{i}t_{j}t_{i}

\displaystyle=t_{i},

\displaystyle\text{if }|i-j|=1,

\displaystyle t_{i}t_{j}

\displaystyle=t_{j}t_{i},

\displaystyle\text{if }|i-j|\geq 2.

In the remainder of the paper, we assume
\xi=2
. In this case, there is an isomorphism
[
2
]

\operatorname{TL}_{n}(2)\cong\mathbb{C}[\mathfrak{S}_{n}]/(1+\mathfrak{s}_{1}+\mathfrak{s}_{2}+\mathfrak{s}_{1}\mathfrak{s}_{2}+\mathfrak{s}_{2}\mathfrak{s}_{1}+\mathfrak{s}_{1}\mathfrak{s}_{2}\mathfrak{s}_{1}).

Specifically, the isomorphism is given by the algebra homomorphism

(2.6)

\displaystyle\theta:\mathbb{C}[\mathfrak{S}_{n}]\rightarrow\operatorname{TL}_{n}(2)~\mbox{ with }~\theta(\mathfrak{s}_{i})=t_{i}-1.

One may interpret multiplication in
\operatorname{TL}_{n}(2)
visually.
To each algebra generator, we associate a
Kauffman diagram

2n

, we associate the Kauffman diagram in which the

are given in

2.4.
TL immanants of Jacobi–Trudi matrices

Generalizing (
2.3
), the
f
-immanant of a dual Jacobi–Trudi matrix can be written as

(2.7)

\operatorname{imm}_{f}\left(\mathcal{E}_{\lambda/\mu}(\mathbf{x})\right)=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}\sum_{\operatorname{type}(\mathbf{p})=\sigma}f(\sigma)\operatorname{wt}_{\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{p}),

where the inner sum ranges over all
(\lambda,\mu)
-path families
\mathbf{p}
.
Rhoades and Skandera
[
12
]
proved that Temperley–Lieb immanants of
path matrices for planar networks can be written as nonnegative expressions
in the associated network’s edge weights.
In the remainder of this subsection, we paraphrase the essential steps of their proof,
since some of the vocabulary developed by Rhoades and Skandera
will be useful in the proofs of our main theorems.
For simplicity, we phrase the results in terms of
the south-southwest lattice path graph
\mathcal{L}

and dual Jacobi–Trudi matrices,
although the setup generalizes to all planar networks.

For a skew shape
\lambda/\mu
,
a
(\lambda,\mu)
-subnetwork
is a multiset
H
of edges in
\mathcal{L}

that is equal to the disjoint union of all paths in some
(\lambda,\mu)
-path family
\mathbf{p}
.
In this case, we say that
\mathbf{p}

covers

H
.
We may also simply call
H
a
subnetwork
to mean it is a
(\lambda,\mu)
-subnetwork for some
\lambda/\mu
.
While the path family covering a given subnetwork
H
is not always unique,
the
\mathbf{x}
-weight of such a path family is unique.
As such, we write
\operatorname{wt}_{\mathbf{x}}(H)
to denote
the
\mathbf{x}
-weight of any path family that covers
H
.
Given a
(\lambda,\mu)
-subnetwork
H
with
\ell(\lambda)\leq n
,
the
path family generating function
for
H
is

\beta(H):=\sum_{\mathbf{p}}\operatorname{type}(\mathbf{p})\in\mathbb{C}[\mathfrak{S}_{n}],

where the sum is over all path families
\mathbf{p}
that cover
H
.

When computing the
f
-immanant of a path matrix, we extend the function
f:\mathfrak{S}_{n}\to\mathbb{R}
by linearity so that it is defined on all of
\mathbb{C}[\mathfrak{S}_{n}]
.
Then, we may collect the terms in (
2.7
) according to the
subnetwork covered by each path family.
For dual Jacobi–Trudi matrices, we thus obtain the expression

(2.8)

\operatorname{imm}_{f}\left(\mathcal{E}_{\lambda/\mu}(\mathbf{x})\right)=\sum_{H}f(\beta(H))\operatorname{wt}_{\mathbf{x}}(H),

where the sum ranges over all
(\lambda,\mu)
-subnetworks
H
.

Our goal in the remainder of this section is to
recall Rhoades and Skandera’s result,
which gives a manifestly positive interpretation of (
2.8
)
in the case of TL immanants.
The primary idea is to show that
f_{\tau}(\beta(H))\geq 0

for any Kauffman diagram
\tau
and any subnetwork
H
.

Two south-southwest lattice paths
p
and
q
are said to be
noncrossing
if there do not exist
(a_{1},b_{1}),\,(a_{2},b_{2})\in p
and
(c_{1},d_{1}),\,(c_{2},d_{2})\in q
such that

a_{1}<c_{1},\,b_{1}=d_{1},\quad\text{and}\quad c_{2}<a_{2},\,b_{2}=d_{2}.

Note that a pair of noncrossing paths may still intersect.
A path family is said to be
noncrossing
if its paths are pairwise noncrossing.
Observe that, for any subnetwork
H
, there is a unique noncrossing path family
\mathbf{p}
that covers
H
. See
Figure

7
for an example.

Let
\mathbf{p}=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{n})
be the unique noncrossing path family for
H
. An
essential intersection point
of
H
is a vertex in the intersection of two paths
p_{i}
and
p_{i+1}
with the property that the previous vertex in each path is not in the intersection of
p_{i}
and
p_{i+1}
.
Note that a pair of paths may have multiple essential intersection points.
An ordering
(u_{1},\ldots,u_{\ell})
of the essential intersection points of
H
is called
compatible
if there is no directed path in
H
from
u_{b}
to
u_{a}
whenever
a<b
.
Given a compatible ordering of the essential intersection points of
H
, an
intersection sequence
for
H
is
(i_{1},\ldots,i_{\ell})
, where
i_{k}=c
if
u_{k}
is in the intersection of
p_{c}
and
p_{c+1}
.
For example, the subnetwork in
Figure

4
has
2
essential intersection points and a unique intersection sequence
(1,2)
.

A
(\lambda,\mu)
-generalized wiring diagram
is a
(\lambda,\mu)
-subnetwork
H
covered by a path family in which no three paths share a vertex.
As with subnetworks, we call
H
a
generalized wiring diagram

if it is a
(\lambda,\mu)
-generalized wiring diagram for some
\lambda/\mu
.
Note that, if two path families
\mathbf{p}
and
\mathbf{p}^{\prime}
both cover
H
, then
\mathbf{p}
has three paths that share a vertex if and only if
\mathbf{p}^{\prime}
does.
This implies that whether a given subnetwork is a generalized wiring diagram is independent of the choice of the covering path family.
Combining the arguments in the proof of
[
12
, Lemma 2.5]
and
[
12
, p. 461]
,
we obtain the following lemma.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
H
be a subnetwork. If
H
is a generalized wiring diagram, then

\beta(H)=(1+\mathfrak{s}_{i_{1}})\cdots(1+\mathfrak{s}_{i_{\ell}}),

where
(i_{1},\ldots,i_{\ell})
is an intersection sequence for
H
.
If
H
is not a generalized wiring diagram, then
\beta(H)
is in the kernel of
\theta
.

Note that, if
H
is indeed a generalized wiring diagram, then by
2.3
,

(2.9)

\theta(\beta(H))=\theta((1+\mathfrak{s}_{i_{1}})\cdots(1+\mathfrak{s}_{i_{\ell}}))=t_{i_{1}}\cdots t_{i_{\ell}}.

Using the defining relations of
\operatorname{TL}_{n}(2)
, the latter expression can
be reduced to
2^{k}\tau
for some integer
k
and a Kauffman
diagram
\tau\in\mathcal{B}_{n}
. Note that
k
and
\tau
are
uniquely determined by
H
, because the element

\theta(\beta(H))\in\operatorname{TL}_{n}(2)
has a unique expansion in the basis

\mathcal{B}_{n}
.

Definition 2.4
.

For a generalized wiring diagram
H
, we define

\epsilon(H)
and
\psi(H)
as the unique integer and the
Kauffman diagram, respectively, satisfying

\theta(\beta(H))=2^{\epsilon(H)}\psi(H).

The Kauffman diagram
\psi(H)
is called the
Temperley–Lieb
type
of
H
.

We can compute
\psi(H)
and
\epsilon(H)
visually using the following method
[
12
, p. 462]
. An example is given in
Figure

5
.

(1)

Contract each doubly covered edge into a single vertex.

(2)

Split each vertex shared by two paths into two vertices—one incident to the two incoming edges and the other incident to the two outgoing edges.

(3)

If any closed loops are created during steps (1) and (2), remove them.

(4)

The number of loops removed in step (3) is
\epsilon(H)
, and the resulting graph is the Kauffman diagram
\psi(H)
.

2.5.
Representation theory of
\mathfrak{S}_{n}\times\mathfrak{S}_{n}

The irreducible representations of the symmetric group

\mathfrak{S}_{n}
correspond bijectively to the partitions

\lambda
of
n
. We denote by
S^{\lambda}
the
corresponding irreducible representation, known as the
Specht
module
. The
Frobenius characteristic map
is an isomorphism
between the space of (virtual)
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
-modules and the space of
symmetric functions of homogeneous degree
n
, defined as follows:
for a finite-dimensional
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
-module

V=\bigoplus_{\lambda\vdash n}(S^{\lambda})^{\oplus c_{\lambda}}
,

\operatorname{Frob}(V):=\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}c_{\lambda}s_{\lambda}.

The irreducible representations of the product

\mathfrak{S}_{n}\times\mathfrak{S}_{n}
of two symmetric groups are
indexed by the pairs
(\lambda,\mu)
of partitions of
n
,
with corresponding representations
S^{\lambda}\otimes S^{\mu}
.
The Frobenius characteristic map extends naturally to the
representations of
\mathfrak{S}_{n}\times\mathfrak{S}_{n}
. More
precisely, for a finite dimensional

\mathfrak{S}_{n}\times\mathfrak{S}_{n}
-module

V=\bigoplus_{\lambda,\mu\vdash n}\left(S^{\lambda}\otimes S^{\mu}\right)^{\oplus c_{\lambda,\mu}},

we define

\operatorname{Frob}_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}(V)=\sum_{\lambda,\mu\vdash n}c_{\lambda,\mu}s_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x})s_{\mu}(\mathbf{y}).

In particular, if
\operatorname{Frob}_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}(V)=f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})

for some
\mathfrak{S}_{n}\times\mathfrak{S}_{n}
-module
V
,
then
f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
is
s
-positive.

3.
Hadamard products of ribbon-like Jacobi–Trudi matrices

In
Section

3.1
, we prove
1.3
, i.e., that the Temperley–Lieb immanant

\operatorname{imm}_{\tau}\left(\mathcal{E}_{\lambda^{(1)}/\mu^{(1)}}(\mathbf{x}^{(1)})*\cdots*\mathcal{E}_{\lambda^{(k)}/\mu^{(k)}}(\mathbf{x}^{(k)})\right)

is
s
-positive for any
\tau\in\mathcal{B}_{n}
, provided that each
\lambda^{(i)}/\mu^{(i)}
is a skew shape that does not contain a
3\times 2
block of cells. We say that such shapes are
(3\times 2)
-
avoiding
.

If each of the skew shapes is in fact a
ribbon
, i.e., a connected skew shape not containing a
2\times 2
block, we can explicitly describe the expansion of this immanant into the Schur basis using standard Young tableaux with prescribed descent sets.
This is done in
Section

3.2
.

In
Section

3.3
, we discuss an extension of the result to a slightly larger class of collections of skew shapes. We then demonstrate that extending our main result to skew shapes containing a
3\times 2
block would require a new technique.

3.1.
Hadamard products of
(3\times 2)
-avoiding skew shapes

When computing an immanant of a single Jacobi–Trudi matrix, one first expands the immanant as a sum over
(\lambda,\mu)
-path families as in (
2.3
).
For Hadamard products of several Jacobi–Trudi matrices, we start in an analogous fashion.
Given skew shapes
\lambda^{(1)}/\mu^{(1)},\ldots,\lambda^{(k)}/\mu^{(k)}

with
\ell(\lambda^{(i)})\leq n
for all
i
, we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{imm}_{f}\left(\mathcal{E}_{\lambda^{(1)}/\mu^{(1)}}(\mathbf{x}^{(1)})*\cdots*\right.\vphantom{.}

\displaystyle\left.\vphantom{.}\mathcal{E}_{\lambda^{(k)}/\mu^{(k)}}(\mathbf{x}^{(k)})\right)

(3.1)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}\,\,\sum_{(\mathbf{p}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{p}^{(k)})}\,\,f(\sigma)\operatorname{wt}_{\mathbf{x}^{(1)}}(\mathbf{p}^{(1)})\cdots\operatorname{wt}_{\mathbf{x}^{(k)}}(\mathbf{p}^{(k)}).

Here the inner sum is over all tuples
(\mathbf{p}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{p}^{(k)})

such that each
\mathbf{p}^{(i)}
is a
\left(\lambda^{(i)},\mu^{(i)}\right)
-path family satisfying
\operatorname{type}(\mathbf{p}^{(i)})=\sigma
.

In the single matrix case, the next step is to collect path families according to their union, and reorganize the sum to be over subnetworks instead; see (
2.8
).
We now introduce the corresponding notions for Hadamard products, which will allow us to simplify the expression above.

Given tuples

\underline{\lambda}=(\lambda^{(1)},\ldots,\lambda^{(k)})\quad\text{ and }\quad\underline{\mu}=(\mu^{(1)},\ldots,\mu^{(k)})

of partitions satisfying
\mu^{(i)}\subseteq\lambda^{(i)}
for all
i
,
a
(\underline{\lambda},\underline{\mu})
-
multinetwork
is a tuple

\mathbf{H}=(H^{(1)},\ldots,H^{(k)})

in which each
H^{(i)}
is a
(\lambda^{(i)},\mu^{(i)})
-subnetwork.
Given such a multinetwork,
we define the corresponding
path family generating function
to be

(3.2)

\displaystyle\beta(\mathbf{H})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\underline{\mathbf{p}}}\operatorname{type}(\underline{\mathbf{p}})\in\mathbb{C}[\mathfrak{S}_{n}].

Here, the sum is over all tuples
\underline{\mathbf{p}}=(\mathbf{p}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{p}^{(k)})
in which each
\mathbf{p}^{(i)}
is a
(\lambda^{(i)},\mu^{(i)})
-path family covering
H^{(i)}
, and all of the path families
\mathbf{p}^{(i)}
are of the same permutation type. We denote this shared permutation type by
\operatorname{type}(\underline{\mathbf{p}})
.
In such a situation, we say that the tuple
\underline{\mathbf{p}}

covers

\mathbf{H}
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Suppose
\lambda/\mu
is a
(3\times 2)
-avoiding skew shape
with
\ell(\lambda)\leq n
. Let
H
be a

(\lambda,\mu)
-subnetwork, and let

\mathbf{p}=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{n})
be the unique noncrossing path
family covering
H
. Then the following hold:

(1)

H
is a generalized wiring diagram.

(2)

There exists a unique set
I\subseteq[n-1]
such that

\beta(H)=2^{\epsilon(H)}\mathfrak{p}(I)
. In particular,

\psi(H)=\tau(I)
.

(3)

The path
p_{i}
intersects
p_{i+1}
if and only if

i\in I
.

(4)

The essential intersection points of
p_{i}
with
p_{i+1}

always occur before the essential intersection points of
p_{i+1}

with
p_{i+2}
.

Proof.

For the first statement, we need to show that there is no vertex
v

contained in three of the paths in
\mathbf{p}
. Since
\mathbf{p}
is
a noncrossing path family, it suffices to show that no vertex
v
is
contained in three consecutive paths
p_{i},p_{i+1},
and
p_{i+2}
.
Suppose, for the sake of contradiction, that
v
is such a vertex.

Since the vertex
v=(x,y)
is contained in the south-southwest lattice path
p_{i}:A(\lambda)_{i}\to B(\mu)_{i}
, it must lie weakly to the right of
B(\mu)_{i}=(\mu_{i}+n-i,0)
in the plane, which implies
\mu_{i}+n-i\leq x.

Similarly, since
v
is contained in
p_{i+2}
, it must lie weakly to the left of
A(\lambda)_{i+2}=(\lambda_{i+2}+n-i-2,\infty)
, which implies
x\leq\lambda_{i+2}+n-i-2.

Together, these conditions imply
\mu_{i}+n-i\leq\lambda_{i+2}+n-i-2
, equivalently
\mu_{i}\leq\lambda_{i+2}-2
.
However,
since
\lambda/\mu
is
(3\times 2)
-avoiding, we have

\lambda_{i+2}-2<\mu_{i}
.
Thus we have obtained a contradiction. It follows that the paths
p_{i}
and
p_{i+2}
can never intersect, which implies that no three paths in
\mathbf{p}
can share a vertex.

For the remaining statements, we construct a compatible ordering

(u_{1},\ldots,u_{\ell})
of the essential intersection points of

H
for which the corresponding intersection sequence

(i_{1},\ldots,i_{\ell})
is weakly increasing. First, place all
essential intersection points of
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
, then all
essential intersection points of
p_{2}
and
p_{3}
, and so on. Each
time, place the vertices from the path
p_{i}
in the order in which
they appear in
p_{i}
. By construction, the corresponding sequence

(i_{1},\ldots,i_{\ell})
is weakly increasing, so we only need to
check that this sequence is compatible with
H
, i.e., that there
can never be a directed path in
H
from
u_{b}
to
u_{a}
for

a<b
. There are two cases to consider. If
u_{b}
and
u_{a}

both belong to the same path
p_{i}
, they have already been ordered
compatibly. Otherwise
u_{a}
lies in the intersection of paths

p_{i}
and
p_{i+1}
, while
u_{b}
belongs to the intersection
of
p_{j}
and
p_{j+1}
for
j\geq i+1
. In particular,

u_{b}
lies on a path
p_{j+1}
with
j+1\geq i+2
. Following a
similar argument as we did above, this implies
u_{b}
lies strictly
to the left of
u_{a}
in the plane, so there is no directed path from

u_{b}
to
u_{a}
.

Now we are ready to prove the remaining statements. Let

I\subseteq[n-1]
be the set of integers appearing in

(i_{1},\ldots,i_{\ell})
. Since
(i_{1},\ldots,i_{\ell})
is weakly
increasing, by
2.3
and the fact that

(1+\mathfrak{s}_{i})^{k}=2^{k-1}(1+\mathfrak{s}_{i})
, we have

\beta(H)=(1+\mathfrak{s}_{i_{1}})(1+\mathfrak{s}_{i_{2}})\cdots(1+\mathfrak{s}_{i_{\ell}})=2^{\epsilon}\prod_{i\in I}(1+\mathfrak{s}_{i})=2^{\epsilon}\mathfrak{p}(I),

where
\epsilon=\ell-|I|
. Since
i\in I
if and only if

p_{i}
and
p_{i+1}
intersect,
I
and
\epsilon
are
uniquely determined by
H
. Since

\theta(\beta(H))=\theta(2^{\epsilon}\mathfrak{p}(I))=2^{\epsilon}\tau(I)
,
we have
\epsilon=\epsilon(H)
and
\tau(I)=\psi(H)
. This
shows the second statement. The third and the fourth statements follow
immediately from the construction of the ordering

(u_{1},\dots,u_{\ell})
above.
∎

As mentioned in
Section

2.3

\epsilon(H)
is the
number of closed loops in
H

Lemma 3.3
.

Suppose

\lambda^{(1)}/\mu^{(1)},\ldots,\lambda^{(k)}/\mu^{(k)}
are

(3\times 2)
-avoiding skew shapes, with

\ell(\lambda^{(i)})\leq n
for all
i
. Let

\mathbf{H}=(H^{(1)},\ldots,H^{(k)})
be a tuple such that each

H^{(i)}
is a
(\lambda^{(i)},\mu^{(i)})
-subnetwork.
Then

\beta(\mathbf{H})=2^{\epsilon(\mathbf{H})}\mathfrak{p}(I_{1}\cap\cdots\cap I_{k}),

where

\epsilon(\mathbf{H})=\epsilon(H^{(1)})+\cdots+\epsilon(H^{(k)})
,
and for all
1\leq i\leq k
, the set
I_{i}\subseteq[n-1]
is the unique set such that

\beta(H^{(i)})=2^{\epsilon(H^{(i)})}\mathfrak{p}(I_{i})
.

Proof.

Let
\star
be the product in
\mathbb{C}[\mathfrak{S}_{n}]
defined by

u\star w=u\delta_{u,w}
for
u,w\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
and extended by
linearity. By the definition of
\beta(\mathbf{H})
in
(
3.2
), we have

\beta(\mathbf{H})=\beta(H^{(1)})\star\cdots\star\beta(H^{(k)}).

By
3.1
, for each
i\in[k]
, we have

\beta(H^{(i)})=2^{\epsilon(H^{(i)})}\mathfrak{p}(I_{i})
for a unique set
I_{i}\subseteq[n-1]
.
Thus, the above equation can be written as

(3.4)

\beta(\mathbf{H})=2^{\epsilon(H^{(1)})}\mathfrak{p}(I_{1})\star\cdots\star 2^{\epsilon(H^{(k)})}\mathfrak{p}(I_{k})=2^{\epsilon(\mathbf{H})}\left(\mathfrak{p}(I_{1})\star\cdots\star\mathfrak{p}(I_{k})\right).

Now observe that for any
I\subseteq[n-1]
,

\displaystyle\mathfrak{p}(I)

\displaystyle=\prod_{i\in I}(1+\mathfrak{s}_{i})=\sum_{A\subseteq I}\mathfrak{s}_{A},

where
\mathfrak{s}_{A}=\prod_{i\in A}\mathfrak{s}_{i}
. As before, products over
subsets are taken in increasing order from left to right.
For any sets

A,B\subseteq[n-1]
, we have
\mathfrak{s}_{A}=\mathfrak{s}_{B}
if and only if

A=B
. This can easily be seen from the observation that

\min(A)
is the smallest integer
i
such that

\mathfrak{s}_{A}(i)\neq i
. Thus, for any sets
I,I^{\prime}\subseteq[n-1]
, we have

(3.5)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{p}(I)\star\mathfrak{p}(I^{\prime})=\left(\sum_{A\subseteq I}\mathfrak{s}_{A}\right)\star\left(\sum_{B\subseteq I^{\prime}}\mathfrak{s}_{B}\right)=\sum_{C\subseteq I\cap I^{\prime}}\mathfrak{s}_{C}=\mathfrak{p}(I\cap I^{\prime}).

By (
3.4
) and
(
3.5
), we obtain the lemma.
∎

Theorem 3.5
.

Let

and suppose

.

Proof.

Using (

\mathbf{H}

3.2.
Schur expansion of ribbon Hadamard products

In this subsection, we provide a more explicit description of the Schur expansion from
3.6
in the case where all of the skew shapes
\lambda^{(i)}/\mu^{(i)}
are ribbons. The description is given in terms of standard Young tableaux with restricted descent sets.

We begin with the following lemma, which guarantees that the powers of

2
from
3.5
do not appear in the
ribbon case.

Lemma 3.7
.

Suppose
\lambda/\mu
is a ribbon.
Then any
(\lambda,\mu)
-subnetwork
H
satisfies
\epsilon(H)=0
.

Proof.

As in the proof of
3.1
, let

\mathbf{p}=(p_{1},\dots,p_{n})
be the unique noncrossing path family
covering
H
, where
n
is an integer with

\ell(\lambda)\leq n
. It suffices to show that each pair

(p_{i},p_{i+1})
of paths in
\mathbf{p}
has at most one essential
intersection point, because this implies that there are no loops in
H
.

To show this, first observe that because
\lambda/\mu
is a
ribbon, it satisfies
\mu_{i}=\lambda_{i+1}-1
for all

a\leq i\leq b-1
, where
[a,b]=\{i:\lambda_{i}>\mu_{i}\}
.
Then, let

v=(x,y)
be an essential intersection point of
p_{i}
and

p_{i+1}

for some
i\in[a,b-1]
.
Note that
i
lies in this range because the paths
p_{k}
for
k\leq a-1

or
k\geq b+1
are vertical paths consisting of south steps.
Since
v
lies on the path

p_{i}:A(\lambda)_{i}\to B(\mu)_{i}
, it must lie weakly to the right
of
B(\mu)_{i}=(\mu_{i}+n-i,0)
in the plane. Since
v

lies on the path
p_{i+1}:A(\lambda)_{i+1}\to B(\mu)_{i+1}
, it
must lie weakly to left of

A(\lambda)_{i+1}=(\lambda_{i+1}+n-i-1,\infty)
. The latter two
conditions together imply that

\lambda_{i+1}+n-i-1\leq x\leq\mu_{i}+n-i.

Since
\mu_{i}=\lambda_{i+1}-1
, this implies

x=\lambda_{i+1}+n-i-1=\mu_{i}+n-i
. Therefore all essential
intersection points of
p_{i}
and
p_{i+1}
must lie on a fixed
vertical line. Since both paths move only south and southwest in the
plane, there is at most one essential intersection point.
∎

We now establish some notation that will be useful in translating between Temperley–Lieb types and standard Young tableaux.
For a
ribbon
R
of size
m
, label the cells sequentially
from
1
to
m
, starting from the top-right cell. A

descent
of
R
is an integer
i\in[m-1]
such that the
cell labeled
i
is positioned immediately above the cell labeled

i+1
. We denote by
\operatorname{Des}(R)
the set of descents of
R
.
For a word
u=u_{1}\cdots u_{n}=(u_{1},\dots,u_{m})\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}^{m}
,
we also define
\operatorname{Des}(u)
to be the set of integers

i\in[m-1]
such that
u_{i}>u_{i+1}
, and we denote

\mathbf{x}_{u}=x_{u_{1}}\cdots x_{u_{m}}
.

Suppose that
R=\lambda/\mu
is a ribbon
with
\{i:\lambda_{i}>\mu_{i}\}=[a,b]
.
Then

(3.7)

\operatorname{Des}(R)=\left\{\sum_{j=1}^{i}(\lambda_{j}-\mu_{j}):i\in[a,b-1]\right\}.

For any set
I\subseteq\mathbb{N}
, we define

(3.8)

d(I,R)=\left\{\sum_{j=1}^{i}(\lambda_{j}-\mu_{j}):i\in I\right\}.

Note that
d([a,b-1],R)=\operatorname{Des}(R)
.
Lastly, we denote
\operatorname{NDes}(R):=[m-1]\setminus\operatorname{Des}(R).

Lemma 3.8
.

Suppose
R=\lambda/\mu
is a ribbon of size
m
with

\{i:\lambda_{i}>\mu_{i}\}=[a,b]
. Then, for any set

I\subseteq[a,b-1]
, there is a bijection between the set of

R
-subnetworks
H
with
\beta(H)=\mathfrak{p}(I)
and the set of words

u\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}^{m}
satisfying

\operatorname{Des}(u)=\operatorname{NDes}(R)\cup d(I,R)
.

Proof.

Suppose
\ell(\lambda)\leq n
. Let
X
be the set of

R
-subnetworks
H
with
\beta(H)=\mathfrak{p}(I)
and let
Y

be the set of words
u\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}^{m}
satisfying

\operatorname{Des}(u)=\operatorname{NDes}(R)\cup d(I,R)
. We need to find a bijection from

X
to
Y
.

Consider
H\in X
. Let
\mathbf{p}=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{n})
be the
unique noncrossing path family that covers
H
. Next, associate to
each path
p_{i}
a decreasing word
u(p_{i})
, consisting of
positive integers, recorded by tracking the
y
-coordinates of the
starting points of southwest steps in
p_{i}
, starting from the
top. We then define
u(\mathbf{p})\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}_{\geq 1}
as the
concatenation of the words
u(p_{1}),\dots,u(p_{n})
. We now show
that
\operatorname{Des}(u(\mathbf{p}))=\operatorname{NDes}(R)\cup d(I,R)
.

There are two types of descents in
\operatorname{Des}(u(\mathbf{p}))\subseteq[m-1]
to consider:

(i)

descents that come from one of the words
u(p_{i})
, or

(ii)

descents that occur when the last entry of
u(p_{i})
is greater than the first entry of
u(p_{i+1})
.

We claim that the descents of type (i) form the set
[m-1]\setminus\operatorname{Des}(R)=\operatorname{NDes}(R)
.
We also claim that the descents of type (ii) form the set
d(I,R)
.
These two claims together will demonstrate that

\operatorname{Des}(u(\mathbf{p}))=\operatorname{NDes}(R)\cup d(I,R)
,
as desired.

For the first claim, observe that since each

p_{i}:(\lambda_{i}+n-i,\infty)\to(\mu_{i}+n-i,0)
is a south-southwest
lattice path, each
u(p_{i})
is a decreasing word. Since the
x

coordinates of these points differ by
\lambda_{i}-\mu_{i}
, each

p_{i}
must take exactly
\lambda_{i}-\mu_{i}
southwest steps, so

u(p_{i})
is a word of length
\lambda_{i}-\mu_{i}
. Thus
u(p_{i})

contributes the descents to
u(\mathbf{p})
in positions

N_{i-1}+1,N_{i-1}+2,\dots,N_{i}-1
,
where
N_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{i}\left(\lambda_{j}-\mu_{j}\right)
.
Thus, by (
3.7
),
the set of descents of type (i) is

[m-1]\setminus\{N_{1},\dots,N_{n}\}=[m-1]\setminus\{N_{a},\dots,N_{b-1}\}=[m-1]\setminus\operatorname{Des}(R),

which shows the first claim.

For the second claim, suppose the last entry of
u(p_{i})
is greater
than the first entry of
u(p_{i+1})
. Since
p_{j}
is of length

\lambda_{j}-\mu_{j}
for all
j
, the corresponding descent in

u(\mathbf{p})
is at position

N_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{i}(\lambda_{j}-\mu_{j})
. To prove the claim, we
must argue that this occurs if and only if
i\in I
. Since
p_{i}

has destination
(\mu_{i}+n-i,0)
, the last southwest step of
p_{i}

terminates with
x
-coordinate
\mu_{i}+n-i
. Similarly, the first
southwest step of
p_{i+1}
originates with
x
-coordinate

\lambda_{i+1}+n-i-1
. Since
\mu_{i}=\lambda_{i+1}-1
, the
vertical lines
x=\lambda_{i+1}+n-i-1
and
x=\mu_{i}+n-i

are identical. The fact that the last entry of
u(p_{i})
is greater
than the first entry of
u(p_{i+1})
means that the last southwest
step of
p_{i}
originates strictly north of the first southwest step
of
p_{i+1}
. It follows that
p_{i}
and
p_{i+1}
intersect. By

3.1
, since
\beta(H)=\mathfrak{p}(I)
, such an
intersection occurs if and only if
i\in I
. This implies the
second claim.

By the two claims,
H\mapsto u
is a map from
X
to

Y
. To show that this is a bijection, we construct its inverse.
Observe that a south-southwest path
p
is determined by the
decreasing word
u(p)
defined earlier. Suppose

u=(u_{1},\dots,u_{n})\in Y
. First, let

p_{1}:A(\lambda)_{1}\to B(\mu)_{1}
be the unique path with

u(p_{1})=(u_{1},\dots,u_{N_{1}})
. Then define

p_{2}:A(\lambda)_{2}\to B(\mu)_{2}
to be the unique path with

u(p_{2})=(u_{N_{1}+1},\dots,u_{N_{2}})
, and so on. In this way, we
obtain a sequence
\mathbf{p}=(p_{1},\dots,p_{n})
of paths. Let
H

be the subnetwork covered by
\mathbf{p}
. It is easy to check that

u\mapsto H
is the desired inverse map.
∎

[a,b]=\{i:\lambda_{i}>\mu_{i}\}

\displaystyle=1+\mathfrak{s}_{a}+\mathfrak{s}_{a+1}.

In particular,
\operatorname{sgn}(\beta(\mathbf{H}))=\operatorname{sgn}(1+\mathfrak{s}_{a}+\mathfrak{s}_{a+1})=1-1-1=-1.

∎

The inequality
\operatorname{sgn}(\beta(\mathbf{H}))\geq 0
means that there exists an
injection from the set of path families covering
H
with negative
sign into the set of those with positive sign.
3.18
demonstrates that, outside the setting of

3.17
, there is no such injection.
Therefore, if one were to try to solve
1.1

or
1.2
by constructing a
sign-reversing injection of path families, the injection must not
always preserve the underlying multiset of edges used.

In addition, the required injection must depend on the directed graph structure specifically for south-southwest lattice paths.
Consider ordinary Jacobi–Trudi matrices,

\mathcal{H}_{\lambda/\mu}(\mathbf{x}):=(h_{\lambda_{i}-\mu_{j}+i-j}(\mathbf{x}))_{i,j=1}^{\ell(\lambda)}.

Note that

\det(\mathcal{H}_{\lambda/\mu}(\mathbf{x}))=s_{\lambda/\mu}(\mathbf{x})
.
Similarly to dual Jacobi–Trudi matrices, ordinary Jacobi–Trudi
matrices can be realized by a lattice path construction with
south
(0,-1)
and west
(-1,0)
steps. However, Hadamard
products of ordinary Jacobi–Trudi matrices do not always have
monomial-positive determinants. One can check, for example, that

\det(\mathcal{H}_{(2,2,2)}(\mathbf{x})*\mathcal{H}_{(2,2,2)}(\mathbf{y}))
is not
monomial-positive.

4.
Representation-theoretic interpretation

To provide further insight on Sokal’s conjecture, Stanley
[
17
]

introduced a ring homomorphism

\Phi_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}:\Lambda\to\Lambda(\mathbf{x})\otimes\Lambda(\mathbf{y})
defined by

\Phi_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}(e_{n})=e_{n}(\mathbf{x})e_{n}(\mathbf{y})
.
In
1.4
, we have seen that

\Phi_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}(s_{R})=\det(\mathcal{E}_{R^{\prime}}(\mathbf{x})*\mathcal{E}_{R^{\prime}}(\mathbf{y})).

is
s
-positive for any ribbon
R
.
This naturally leads
to the question of constructing an

\mathfrak{S}_{n}\times\mathfrak{S}_{n}
-module whose image
under the Frobenius characteristic map equals
\Phi_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}(s_{R})
.
In this section, we answer this question by constructing a
simplicial complex arising from the Boolean poset whose reduced homology
yields the desired
\mathfrak{S}_{n}\times\mathfrak{S}_{n}
-module.
To do this, we need some preparations.

From now on, we assume that
P
is a finite poset with a unique
minimal element
\hat{0}
and a unique maximal element
\hat{1}
,
and that every maximal chain in
P
has the same length, denote by

\ell(P)
. Here, the
length
of a chain is the number of
elements in the chain minus one. Let

r:P\to\{0,1,\dots,\ell(P)\}
denote the rank function of
P
.
Note that
r(\hat{0})=0
and
r(\hat{1})=\ell(P)
. For

A\subseteq[\ell(P)-1]
, the
rank-selected subposet

P_{A}

is defined as

P_{A}:=\{s\in P:r(s)\in A\}\cup\{\hat{0},\hat{1}\}.

The
order complex

\Delta(P)
of
P
is the simplicial
complex whose
k
-faces are the chains
x_{0}<x_{1}<\cdots<x_{k}

in
P\setminus\{\hat{0},\hat{1}\}
. We denote by

\tilde{H}_{i}(P):=\tilde{H}_{i}(\Delta(P))
the
i
-th reduced
simplicial homology group of
\Delta(P)
(over
\mathbb{C}
).
Recall that for any simplicial complex
\mathcal{K}
, we have

\tilde{H}_{-1}(\mathcal{K})=0
unless
\mathcal{K}
is the
empty simplex
\emptyset
. For
\mathcal{K}=\emptyset
, we
have
\tilde{H}_{-1}(\emptyset)=\mathbb{C}
, and

\tilde{H}_{i}(\emptyset)=0
for all
i\geq 0
.

We need two important results in poset topology. One is a result
by Stanley
[
18
]
on group actions on finite posets, and
the other is a result by Björner and Wachs
[
1
]
on
EL-shellable posets.

Suppose
G
is a group of automorphisms of a poset
P
with

\ell(P)=n
. For a subset
A\subseteq[n-1]
, the group

G
acts on the set of maximal chains of the rank-selected
subposet
P_{A}
. We denote by
\alpha_{A}
the
G
-module
induced by this action. Note that
G
also acts on each reduced
homology group
\tilde{H}_{i}(P_{A})
for
-1\leq i<|A|
. Hence, we
also consider
\tilde{H}_{i}(P_{A})
as a
G
-module. Define the
virtual
G
-module
\beta_{A}
by

(4.1)

\beta_{A}:=\sum_{i=-1}^{|A|-1}(-1)^{|A|-i-1}\tilde{H}_{i}(P_{A}).

Using a version of the Hopf–Lefschetz
fixed-point formula, Stanley proved the following theorem.

Theorem 4.1
.

[
18
, 1.1. Theorem]
Given a group
G
of
automorphisms of a poset
P
, the
G
-modules
\alpha_{A}

and the virtual
G
-modules
\beta_{A}
are related by

(4.2)

\displaystyle\alpha_{A}=\sum_{B\subseteq A}\beta_{B},\qquad\text{and}\qquad\beta_{A}=\sum_{B\subseteq A}(-1)^{|A|-|B|}\alpha_{B}.

Next, we recall the notion of EL-shellable posets. For a poset
P

with
\ell(P)=n
, let
E(P)
denote the set of edges in the
Hasse diagram of
P
. An
edge labeling
of a poset
P

is a function
L:E(P)\to K
, where
K
is a poset equipped
with a partial order
<_{K}
. Given such a labeling, we can
associate a sequence of labels to each maximal chain

(4.3)

c=\{\hat{0}=u_{0}\lessdot u_{1}\lessdot\cdots\lessdot u_{n}\lessdot u_{n+1}=\hat{1}\}

by defining its corresponding word as

L(c)=L(u_{0},u_{1})L(u_{1},u_{2})\cdots L(u_{n},u_{n+1}).

A maximal chain is said to be
increasing
if its labels form a strictly increasing sequence in
K
, meaning that

L(u_{0},u_{1})<_{K}L(u_{1},u_{2})<_{K}\dots<_{K}L(u_{n},u_{n+1}).

The
descent set
of the maximal chain
c
in (
4.3
) is
defined to be

\operatorname{Des}(c):=\{i\in[n]:L(u_{i-1}\lessdot u_{i})\geq_{K}L(u_{i}\lessdot u_{i+1})\}.

In particular, an increasing maximal chain
c
has an empty descent set.

The lexicographic order on these words induces an ordering on the
maximal chains of
P
. This induced ordering plays a crucial role
in defining a structural property of posets known as EL-shellability.

Definition 4.2
.

[
1
, Definition 2.1]
A bounded poset

P
is said to be
edge lexicographically shellable
(EL-shellable)
if there exists an edge labeling of
P
such
that, within every closed interval
[x,y]
of
P
, there is
a unique increasing maximal chain. Moreover, this chain must be
the lexicographically smallest among all maximal chains in
[x,y]
.

EL-shellability has significant implications for the topology of order
complexes. In particular, it allows us to describe the homotopy type
of rank-selected subposets.

Theorem 4.3
.

[
1
, Theorem 8.1]
Let
P
be a pure, EL-shellable
poset, and let
A\subseteq[\ell(P)-1]
. Then the order complex
of the rank-selected subposet
P_{A}
is homotopy equivalent to a
wedge of
(|A|-1)
-dimensional spheres.

We now define the main ingredients of the representation-theoretic
interpretation for the expression

\Phi_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}(s_{R})
.
Let
B_{n}
be the
Boolean poset of
[n]
, that is, the poset of all subsets of

[n]
ordered by inclusion. We define another poset

\tilde{B}_{n}
by

\tilde{B}_{n}:=\{(S,T)\in B_{n}\times B_{n}:|S|=|T|\},

where
(S,T)\leq(A,B)
in
\tilde{B}_{n}
if

S\subseteq A
and
T\subseteq B
. There is a natural action of
the symmetric group
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
on
B_{n}
, given by

\sigma\cdot S=\{\sigma(s):s\in S\}
. This action extends to
an
\mathfrak{S}_{n}\times\mathfrak{S}_{n}
-action on
\tilde{B}_{n}
,
defined by

(\sigma,\tau)\cdot(S,T)=(\sigma\cdot S,\tau\cdot T).

Moreover, the group
\mathfrak{S}_{n}\times\mathfrak{S}_{n}
also acts
on each reduced homology group
\tilde{H}_{i}((\tilde{B}_{n})_{A})
of
the rank-selected subposet
(\tilde{B}_{n})_{A}
. Thus, we can consider

\tilde{H}_{i}((\tilde{B}_{n})_{A})
as an

\mathfrak{S}_{n}\times\mathfrak{S}_{n}
-module. Now we state the main
result of this section.

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
R
be a ribbon of size
n
, and consider the

\mathfrak{S}_{n}\times\mathfrak{S}_{n}
-module

\tilde{H}_{|\operatorname{Des}(R^{\prime})|-1}((\tilde{B}_{n})_{\operatorname{Des}(R^{\prime})})

as described above. Then we have

\Phi_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}(s_{R})=\omega\left(\operatorname{Frob}_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}\left(\tilde{H}_{|\operatorname{Des}(R^{\prime})|-1}((\tilde{B}_{n})_{\operatorname{Des}(R^{\prime})})\right)\right).

Before proving
4.4
, we introduce a lemma and recall some relevant terminology.
A
composition

\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{k})
is a sequence of positive integers. The
size
of a composition is defined as
\alpha_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{k}
.
We define
h_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x})=h_{\alpha_{1}}(\mathbf{x})\cdots h_{\alpha_{k}}(\mathbf{x})

and
e_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x})=e_{\alpha_{1}}(\mathbf{x})\cdots e_{\alpha_{k}}(\mathbf{x})
.

Given a subset
A=\{a_{1}<\cdots<a_{k}\}\subseteq[n-1]
, we define the composition
\operatorname{comp}(A)
of size
n
by

\operatorname{comp}(A)=(a_{1},a_{2}-a_{1},\dots,a_{k}-a_{k-1},n-a_{k}).

An
ordered set partition
of
[n]
is a set partition whose parts are equipped with a total order. For example,
\pi=(\{2,4\},\{6\},\{1,3,5\})
is an ordered set partition of
[n]
with three parts. The
type
of an ordered set partition is the composition given by the sequence of sizes of its parts. In our running example, the type of
\pi
is
(2,1,3)
.

Lemma 4.5
.

For
A\subseteq[n-1]
, we have

\operatorname{Frob}_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}(\tilde{H}_{|A|-1}((\tilde{B}_{n})_{A}))=\sum_{B\subseteq A}(-1)^{|A|-|B|}h_{\operatorname{comp}(B)}(\mathbf{x})h_{\operatorname{comp}(B)}(\mathbf{y}).

Proof.

Consider the edge labeling
L:E(\tilde{B}_{n})\to\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}
of
\tilde{B}_{n}
defined by

L((A,B),(A\cup\{a\},B\cup\{b\}))=(a,b),

where, we regard
\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}
as a poset with the order relation

(a,b)<_{\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}}(c,d)\quad\text{if and only if}\quad a<c\text{ and }b<d.

It is straightforward to verify that
L
is an EL-labeling of

\tilde{B}_{n}
. Consequently, by applying Theorem
4.3
, we conclude that the reduced
homology
\tilde{H}_{i}((\tilde{B}_{n})_{A})
vanishes unless

i=|A|-1
, the highest possible degree. Thus, by (
4.1
),
we obtain the identity

\beta_{A}=\sum_{i=-1}^{|A|-1}(-1)^{|A|-i-1}\tilde{H}_{i}((\tilde{B}_{n})_{A})=\tilde{H}_{|A|-1}((\tilde{B}_{n})_{A}).

Hence, by
4.1
, we have

(4.4)

\tilde{H}_{|A|-1}((\tilde{B}_{n})_{A})=\sum_{B\subseteq A}(-1)^{|A|-|B|}\alpha_{B}.

Let
A=\{a_{1}<\cdots<a_{k}\}\subseteq[n-1]
, and
consider the rank-selected subposet
(B_{n})_{A}
of the Boolean poset

B_{n}
. The maximal chains in
(B_{n})_{A}
correspond to the
ordered set partitions of
[n]
into parts of sizes given by
\operatorname{comp}(A)
.
The symmetric group
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
acts on these chains by
permuting the underlying elements of
[n]
, and this action realizes
the permutation module

\mathbf{1}\uparrow^{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}_{\mathfrak{S}_{\operatorname{comp}(A)}},

induced from the trivial module of the Young subgroup

\mathfrak{S}_{\operatorname{comp}(A)}:=\mathfrak{S}_{a_{1}}\times\mathfrak{S}_{a_{2}-a_{1}}\times\cdots\times\mathfrak{S}_{n-a_{k}}.

Now consider the rank-selected subposet
(\tilde{B}_{n})_{A}
of the
poset
\tilde{B}_{n}
. The maximal chains in
(\tilde{B}_{n})_{A}

correspond to pairs
(\pi_{1},\pi_{2})
of ordered set partitions of

[n]
, each of a fixed type
\operatorname{comp}(A)
. The natural action of

\mathfrak{S}_{n}\times\mathfrak{S}_{n}
on such pairs induces the
tensor product
of
\mathbf{1}\uparrow^{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}_{\mathfrak{S}_{\operatorname{comp}(A)}}
, that is,

\alpha_{A}\cong\left(\mathbf{1}\uparrow^{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}_{\mathfrak{S}_{\operatorname{comp}(A)}}\right)\otimes\left(\mathbf{1}\uparrow^{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}_{\mathfrak{S}_{\operatorname{comp}(A)}}\right).

Under the Frobenius characteristic map, the module corresponds to a product of homogeneous
symmetric functions:

(4.5)

\operatorname{Frob}_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}(\alpha_{A})=h_{\operatorname{comp}(A)}(\mathbf{x})h_{\operatorname{comp}(A)}(\mathbf{y}).

By
(
4.5
), applying the Frobenius characteristic map to
(
4.4
) yields the desired identity.
∎

Finally, we are ready to prove
4.4
.

Proof of Theorem
4.4
.

By applying the inclusion-exclusion principle as in the proof of

[
19
, Proposition 7.19.1]
, we obtain that

(4.6)

s_{R}=\sum_{A\subseteq\operatorname{Des}(R^{\prime})}(-1)^{|\operatorname{Des}(R^{\prime})|-|A|}e_{\operatorname{comp}(A)}.

Therefore, the identity we need to show can be restated as

\operatorname{Frob}_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}\left(\tilde{H}_{|\operatorname{Des}(R^{\prime})|-1}((\tilde{B}_{n})_{\operatorname{Des}(R^{\prime})})\right)=\sum_{A\subseteq\operatorname{Des}(R^{\prime})}(-1)^{|\operatorname{Des}(R^{\prime})|-|A|}h_{\operatorname{comp}(A)}(\mathbf{x})h_{\operatorname{comp}(A)}(\mathbf{y}).

This follows from
4.5
.
∎

5.
Concluding Remarks

In this section, we review related conjectures and discuss potential
generalizations of our results.
To introduce the first conjecture, recall Schur’s classical result
[
15
]
:
for two real-rooted polynomials

f(x)=a_{0}+a_{1}x+\cdots+a_{n}x^{n}\quad\text{and}\quad g(x)=b_{0}+b_{1}x+\cdots+b_{m}x^{m},

their
factorial Hadamard product

f\mathbin{\mathchoice{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\displaystyle\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.77778}{$\displaystyle\bigcirc$}}}$\cr$\displaystyle\star$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\textstyle\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.77778}{$\textstyle\bigcirc$}}}$\cr$\textstyle\star$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptstyle\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.77778}{$\scriptstyle\bigcirc$}}}$\cr$\scriptstyle\star$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.77778}{$\scriptscriptstyle\bigcirc$}}}$\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\star$\cr}}}}g:=\sum_{i=0}^{n}i!\,a_{i}b_{i}\,x^{i}

is also real-rooted. In analogy with the way Sokal’s conjecture (
1.1
) generalizes Maló’s result, Sokal further proposed the following conjecture, which strengthens Schur’s theorem.

Conjecture 5.1
.

[
16
]
Let

\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n})
and

\mu=(\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{n})
be partitions with at most
n

parts satisfying
\mu\subseteq\lambda
. For any integers

k
and
r
with
0\leq r\leq k-1
, the following
determinant is
m
-positive:

\det\left(((\lambda_{i}-\mu_{j}-i+j)!)^{r}e_{\lambda_{i}-\mu_{j}-i+j}(\mathbf{x}^{(1)})\cdots e_{\lambda_{i}-\mu_{j}-i+j}(\mathbf{x}^{(k)})\right)_{i,j=1}^{n}.

Sokal’s conjecture (
1.1
) can be stated as

\Phi_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}(s_{\lambda/\mu})
being
m
-positive; see

Section

4
for the definition of the map

\Phi_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}
. Stanley extended Sokal’s conjecture to a
broader setting.

Conjecture 5.2
.

[
17
]

For any partition
\lambda
, the multi-symmetric function

\Phi_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}(m_{\lambda})
is
m
-positive.

Recall that Greene
[
7
]
proved that ordinary immanants of Jacobi–Trudi matrices are monomial-positive. Stembridge
[
22
]
proposed several conjectural
generalizations of Greene’s theorem. To state his conjectures, we
define the
monomial (virtual) character

\phi_{\lambda}
as
follows: For
\lambda\vdash n
and
w\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
,

\phi_{\lambda}(w):=\langle m_{\lambda},p_{\tau(w)}\rangle,

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
is the Hall inner product, and

p_{\tau(w)}
is the power sum symmetric function indexed by the
partition
\tau(w)
corresponding to the cycle type of
w
.
Among Stembridge’s conjectures are the following:

(1)

Ordinary immanants of Jacobi–Trudi matrices are
s
-positive.

(2)

Monomial immanants of Jacobi–Trudi matrices are
m
-positive.

(3)

Monomial immanants of Jacobi–Trudi matrices are
s
-positive.

It is worth noting that the last conjecture implies all the others.
Haiman
[
8
]
confirmed the first conjecture using
Kazhdan–Lusztig theory. The last conjecture remains an open problem
of significant importance. The positivity of the coefficient of

s_{(n)}
in this case is equivalent to the famous
Stanley–Stembridge conjecture
[
20
]
, which was recently
resolved by Hikita
[
9
]
.

Next, we propose a possible extension of Greene’s result and the
second conjecture mentioned above to our setting by considering the
monomial positivity of the immanants of the Hadamard product of
Jacobi–Trudi matrices.

Conjecture 5.3
.

Let
\lambda
,
\mu
,
\nu
, and
\rho
be partitions
of length at most
n
, satisfying
\mu\subseteq\lambda

and
\rho\subseteq\nu
. For any partition
\eta\vdash n
,

(1)

the ordinary immanant

\operatorname{imm}_{\eta}\left(\operatorname{JT}_{\lambda/\mu}(\mathbf{x})*\operatorname{JT}_{\nu/\rho}(\mathbf{y})\right)
is
m
-positive,

(2)

the monomial immanant

\operatorname{imm}_{\phi_{\eta}}\left(\operatorname{JT}_{\lambda/\mu}(\mathbf{x})*\operatorname{JT}_{\nu/\rho}(\mathbf{y})\right)

is
m
-positive.

Note that the second part of
5.3
implies the first.
1.1
is equivalent to the special case of the first part for

\eta=(1^{n})
.
