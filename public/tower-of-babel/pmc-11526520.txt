Validity of the self-reported number of teeth in independent older people in Japan
Authors: Yoshihiro Shimazaki, Mizuki Saito, Toshiya Nonoyama, Yoshinori Inamoto
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn questionnaire surveys, questions about self-reported number of teeth (SRNT) are often used as a measure oral health. This study investigated the validity of SRNT in older Japanese people.MethodsIn total, 4984 75- and 80-year-old patients who underwent dental examinations were enrolled. A self-administered questionnaire that asked about the number of teeth was used in the analysis. The percentage agreement and kappa value were calculated for the agreement between SRNT and observed numbers of teeth. To identify factors that affect the reliability of SRNT, a logistic regression analysis was performed using correctness of SRNT as the dependent variable.ResultsAmong the 3950 participants who responded as to whether they had ≥ 20 teeth, the degree of agreement was 92.9% (kappa value 0.856, p < 0.001) in an objective evaluation. Of the 2621 participants who reported their numbers of teeth, the SRNT and observed number of teeth matched in 57.5% (kappa value 0.559; p < 0.001). Observed number of teeth and annual dental checkup had a significant effect on the accuracy of SRNT. Multivariate logistic regression analysis, with the agreement between SRNT and the observed number of teeth (i.e. whether the number of teeth exceeded 20) as the dependent variable, showed that the observed number of teeth, use of interdental cleaning tools, and annual dental checkup were significantly associated with the agreement between SRNT and the actual number of teeth. In multivariate analysis with tooth number agreement (± 1 tooth) as the dependent variable, the observed number of teeth and use of interdental cleaning tools were significantly associated with the agreement between SRNT and the observed number of teeth.ConclusionAlthough SRNT did not perfectly match the observed numbers of teeth, the results of this study imply that the SRNT of older people is reliable and useful in epidemiological studies.

Background

Oral-health conditions, such as periodontal disease and number of teeth, are closely associated with systemic health [1, 2]. Among oral-health conditions, the number of teeth is one of the most important indicators, especially in older people. Clarifying the relationship between number of teeth and systemic health is important for extending healthy life expectancy as the population ages.

In epidemiological studies that use oral-health conditions as analysis items, oral-health indicators are typically evaluated by oral examinations performed by dentists. The oral condition objectively examined in epidemiological studies has often confirmed the reliability of multiple evaluators if the number of subjects is large and oral examinations are performed [3–6]. However, in epidemiological studies on oral health, oral examinations by dentists may not always be possible. Therefore, the relationship between oral health and systemic health has been investigated based on self-reported oral-health status [7–9]. The validity of a variety of self-reported oral-health conditions has been evaluated [10–14].

Among oral-health indicators, the validity of the self-reported number of teeth (SRNT) has been examined [15–22] for different countries and different age groups. For example, surveys have been conducted in Europe and the United States in middle-aged and older age groups [15, 16, 20–22]; each study demonstrated a high correlation between self-reported and observed numbers of teeth. However, the characteristics of those who did not answer the question about SRNT were not provided. In addition, the influence of factors on differences between the SRNT and observed number of teeth is unclear. In some surveys conducted in Japan, research on SRNT focused on middle-aged and older groups [17–19]; however, no study has focused solely on older individuals. The strength of the correlation between the SRNT and observed number of teeth has been shown for some factors; yet, it has been difficult to resolve the factors that lead to errors in the SRNT. A review article on diagnostic self-reported oral-health outcomes suggested the need for surveys of the validity of self-reported oral-health conditions in individual countries [12]. Together with age group, sex, and other characteristics, differences in the number of teeth may affect the SRNT. Epidemiological studies have analysed the SRNT based on the responses to a questionnaire on the number of teeth. Here, it is also important to understand the characteristics of people in the target group who do not answer the question. In future oral-health research for older people, it is possible that there will be more opportunities to conduct not only epidemiological surveys including dental checkups but also web questionnaire surveys and epidemiological surveys about oral health, based on questionnaire information. Therefore, a better understanding of the reliability of the SRNT is necessary going forward.

In each prefecture in Japan, dental checkups are conducted by medical insurance providers specialising in elderly care to evaluate the oral-health status of individuals aged ≥ 75 years based on national policies. This study investigated the validity of the SRNT and the factors that influence it using health examination data obtained from those participating in dental checkups conducted by medical-insurance providers for older people in one prefecture in Japan.

The aim of this study was to clarify the extent of agreement between the SRNT and actual numbers of teeth, as well as to compare the characteristics of individuals who did versus did not answer the SRNT questions. This study also attempted to identify the factors that influence SRNT responses and the extent of that influence.

Methods

Participants

This cross-sectional study was based on a single survey. The study enrolled subjects insured by the Medical Care System for the Elderly in Mie Prefecture, Japan. The insurer of the Medical Care System funds annual health checks for insured people. The government started supporting dental examinations for older people in 2014; since then, the insurer has been funding dental examinations for 75- and 80-year-olds. This study was conducted in consultation with the medical insurer, the university department, and the local dental association, with the aim of using the results of dental examinations set up by the medical insurer as part of their health projects for future health measures.

The insurer for the aged population in Mie Prefecture sent a ticket for an oral-health examination to individuals aged 75 or 80 years in September 2014 that included information on dental examinations. The examinations were performed at dental clinics belonging to the Mie Dental Association between October 1, 2014, and November 30, 2014. In 2014, there were 17,338 people aged 75 years and 16,040 people aged 80 years in Mie Prefecture. Ultimately, 2,865 (16.5%) and 2,119 (13.2%) 75- and 80-year-old individuals, respectively, underwent oral-health examinations [23]. Since this study did not target a specific disease, all participants who underwent the oral-health examination were included, and no exclusion criteria were set in advance. As the health examinations were not conducted for the survey but rather as periodic health checkups for the older population, consent was not obtained from the participants. The study obtained permission from the insurer to use de-identified data. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Aichi Gakuin University School of Dentistry (approval number 443) and was conducted in accordance with all relevant dictates of the Declaration of Helsinki of the World Medical Association.

Oral health examination

Dentists at each dental clinic conducted the oral-health examinations using detailed manuals created independently by the Mie Dental Association to standardise the assessment of oral-health status. The state of each tooth was recorded as sound, decayed (D), filled (F), or missing (M), and the total number of sound, decayed (including stump), and filled (including coping) teeth, including third molars, was calculated as the observed number of teeth. In this study, no calibration training for the oral examination was provided, and inter-examiner errors in the evaluation of the decayed, missing, and filled (DMF) index were not confirmed; however, this study did not examine the distinction between sound and carious teeth and evaluated only the presence versus absence of teeth, including the stump.

Mental health status

This study attempted to confirm the presence or absence of dementia as a psychological condition that may affect responses to the questionnaire. Regarding dementia, this study checked whether medical costs related to dementia had been claimed based on the International Statistics Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems (ICD-10) disease classification codes F01, F03 (vascular and unspecified dementia), and G30 (Alzheimer’s disease) in the year of the survey, in the health insurance claims data managed by the medical insurer for Mie Prefecture. The presence or absence of medical treatment for dementia was classified based on whether the medical expense receipt was calculated.

Questionnaire

A self-administered questionnaire was given to participants at the time of the dental checkup. The questionnaire was based on the “Dental Checkup Manual for Elderly People” created by the Ministry of Health, Labor and Welfare [24]. This study did not assess the physical or mental condition of the participants. However, because the participants visited a dental clinic and received a dental checkup, it is likely that many of them were independent and completed the questionnaire on their own. The questionnaire included items about subjective indicators of oral health (bite quality, appearance, pronunciation, bad breath, pain, difficulty swallowing, dry mouth, interpretation of dental treatment, and denture malfunction), denture use (use, have but do not use, or do not have), SRNT, subjective indicators of dental caries and periodontal disease (cold-water pain, bleeding when brushing teeth, and gum swelling), family dentist (have or do not have), history of systemic disease (diabetes, stroke, heart disease, cancer, lung disease, and osteoporosis), snacking habits (every day, sometimes, or never), smoking status (yes or no), oral-hygiene habits (brushing teeth before bed, use of fluoride toothpaste, and use of interdental cleaning tools), receiving oral-hygiene instruction (yes or no), annual dental checkup (yes or no), and symptoms related to oral dysfunction (chewing difficulty, swallowing difficulty, and dry mouth). The questionnaire results were used to determine the SRNT. A typical question would be: “How many teeth do you have? Include the following: treated teeth, post crowns, and teeth with only roots remaining.” The response to the first question was used to classify the number of teeth into one of two categories: ≥ 20 teeth or ≤ 19 teeth. In Japan, the 8020 campaign to maintain 20 teeth by age 80 years is underway, and whether an older person has 20 teeth is a commonly used indicator [25]. Next, the participants were asked to report their specific number of teeth. Among the questionnaire items, undergoing an annual dental checkup (yes, no, or no answer) was a factor that appeared to have a significant impact on the SRNT.

Statistical analysis

Regarding the question of whether the participants had 20 or more teeth, this study first classified the participants into responders and non-responders, and then divided the responders into two groups: those with ≥ 20 teeth and those with ≤ 19 teeth. Pearson’s chi-square test was used to assess differences in the responses to each question between groups distinguished according to the response regarding the question SNRT. Also, Cramer’s V was used as a measure of the effect size for the chi-square text. The relationships of items related to lifestyle habits and oral health habits with SRNT were also analysed. As a categorical variable, the observed numbers of teeth were categorised as ≥ 28, 20–27, 10–19, 1–9, or 0 (edentulous), according to the classification of tooth number in a previous study [26]. To analyse the agreement between the SRNT and observed number of teeth for ≥ 20 teeth, the percentage agreement and kappa value were calculated. In addition, the sensitivity and specificity of SRNT for predicting two categories of the number of observed teeth were calculated by defining ≤ 19 teeth as positive and ≥ 20 teeth as negative.

With regard to the questions about the specific number of teeth, the percentage agreement and the kappa value between the specific SRNT and the observed number of teeth were calculated among responders. Pearson’s correlation coefficient between the observed and SRNT was calculated, and its significance was analysed. To examine the factors influencing the response to the question about the specific number of teeth, participants were divided into two groups (responders and non-responders), and the responders were subdivided into two groups (difference between the SRNT and observed tooth number ≤ 1 or ≥ 2). Pearson’s chi-square test was used to assess differences in proportion between responders and non-responders to the above question, and in the proportion of responses for a specific SRNT according to each factor. And, Cramer’s V was used as a measure of the effect size for the chi-square test.

To examine factors influencing the agreement between SRNT and the actual tooth number, logistic regression analysis was performed, with the presence or absence of agreement in tooth number as the dependent variable. First, to examine the accuracy of responses to the question of whether the participants had ≥ 20 or ≤ 19 teeth (dependent variable), logistic regression analysis was conducted, and the odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated among responders. This study included factors thought to influence SRNT as independent variables. Age, sex, observed number of teeth, family dentist, smoking status, snacking habits, brushing teeth before bed, use of fluoride toothpaste, use of interdental cleaning tools, receiving oral hygiene instructions, annual dental checkup, and medical treatment for dementia were independent variables in the univariate analyses. Regarding the degree of agreement with the specific number of teeth, logistic regression analysis was performed, with a difference between the SRNT and actual number of teeth of ± 1 tooth defined as agreement (dependent variable). Age, sex, category of observed number of teeth, family dentist, smoking status, snacking habits, brushing teeth before bed, use of fluoride toothpaste, use of interdental cleaning tools, receiving oral hygiene instructions, annual dental checkup, and medical treatment for dementia were included in the univariate analysis as independent variables. In the multivariate analysis, age and sex were forced in, and variables that had a p-value < 0.2 for their association with SRNT in the univariate analyses were included in a logistic regression analysis with forward–backward stepwise selection.

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS v. 26.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). A value of p < 0.05 was considered indicative of statistical significance.

Results

Of the 4984 participants who underwent dental examinations, 3950 responded to the item asking whether they had ≥ 20 teeth (response rate 79.3%); the remaining participants did not answer this question. Figure 1 shows information about the study population and the people who obtained and analysed the data used in the study. The associations between the response status of the SRNT question asking whether they had ≥ 20 and each factor were assessed. Differences were found between responders and non-responders in age, sex, observed number of teeth, family dentist, smoking habits, snacking habits, brushing teeth before bed, use of fluoride toothpaste, use of interdental cleaning tools, receiving oral hygiene instructions, and annual dental checkup. The responders were younger (75 years old) and more likely to be male; those who answered the questions were more likely to give better answers on items related to oral hygiene habits and dental visits (Table 1). Of these variables, observed number of teeth and use of fluoride toothpaste had effect sizes greater than 0.1. When comparing responders with a SRNT ≥ 20 teeth with those with a SRNT ≤ 19 teeth, differences were observed in age, observed number of teeth, smoking habits, snacking habits, brushing teeth before bed, use of interdental cleaning tools, receiving oral hygiene instructions, and annual dental checkup. Those with ≥ 20 SRNT were more likely to be young (75 years old) and more likely to have good oral hygiene habits and to attend dental visits (Table 1). Of the 2129 participants with a SRNT ≥ 20, 95.8% had ≥ 20 teeth, and of the 1821 participants with a SRNT ≤ 19, 89.5% had ≤ 19 teeth (Table 1). Of these variables, age, observed number of teeth, use of interdental cleaning tools, and receiving oral-hygiene instruction had effect sizes greater than 0.1. Among those who did answer, the agreement between self-reported and objective evaluations was 92.9%, with a kappa value of 0.856 (p < 0.001). When SRNT ≤ 19 teeth was defined as positive and ≥ 20 teeth as negative, the sensitivity and specificity of the SRNT predicting the actual number of teeth were 94.8% and 91.4%, respectively.Fig. Of the 4984 participants who underwent dental examinations, 2621 answered regarding their specific number of teeth (response rate, 52.6%); the others did not answer the question. A scatter plot of the SRNT and observed number of teeth is shown in Fig. 2; the correlation coefficient was r = 0.96 (p < 0.001). The kappa value for agreement between the observed number of teeth and SRNT was 0.559 (p < 0.001). Figure 3 shows a histogram of the difference calculated by subtracting the SRNT from the observed number of teeth. The SRNT matched the number of teeth determined in dental examinations in 57.5% of the cases. The bilaterally symmetric distribution centred on zero, where both numbers coincided, is shown in the figure. Differences between the observed and self-reported numbers of teeth

The associations between the response to the question regarding the specific SRNT and each variable were analysed (Table 2). The response to the question about the specific number of teeth was associated with the observed number of teeth, use of fluoride toothpaste, use of interdental cleaning tools, receiving oral hygiene instructions, and annual dental checkup. There was a tendency that the greater the observed number of teeth, the higher the proportion who did not answer the question regarding specific number of teeth. When comparing the groups in which the difference between the observed number of teeth and SRNT was within ± 1 and those in which it was ± 2 or more, the proportion of participants with 0–9 teeth or ≥ 28 observed teeth was higher, and the proportion of participants with 10–27 observed teeth was lower, in the group in which the difference between the observed number of teeth and SRNT was within ± 1 (Table 2). Other factors were not found to be associated with the difference in the observed number of teeth and SRNT. For both response status to question about specific self-reported number of teeth and difference in numbers of teeth, only observed number of teeth had an effect size greater than 0.1. Figure 4 shows the distribution of the error in the numbers of teeth for each observed number of teeth. The error tended to be small when the observed number of teeth was < 10. Discussion

This study investigated the validity of SRNT among community-dwelling independent older people in Japan, and examined using SRNT as reported in a questionnaire survey, without conducting an oral-health examination. The results showed that the SRNT of older people was very reliable.

The strengths of this study include that it examined not only the validity of SRNT based on data obtained from a large population but also examined the characteristics of non-responders to the question about SRNT and factors that influence the agreement between SRNT and the actual number of teeth. On the other hand, a weakness of this study is that the participants were limited to 75- and 80-year-olds; thus, the results do not apply to young or middle-aged adults. Furthermore, since numbers of teeth are thought to vary depending on the health status of a country or region, the results may not apply to groups with significantly different health conditions, even among the elderly. Although studies on the validity of SRNT have been conducted in the past, the results of this study need to be considered in light of these strengths and weaknesses.

One of the study aims was to confirm the validity of SRNT for predicting actual numbers of teeth in independent older individuals in Japan. This study asked older adults whether they had ≥ 20 teeth and their specific number of teeth. Regarding the accuracy of SRNT, the percentage agreement and kappa values between SRNT and dental-examination results indicated a high degree of reliability among those having ≥ 20 teeth. For the specific number of teeth, the kappa value was 0.56, and 75.4% of the results agreed with those of dental examinations with ± 1 tooth as the margin. These results confirm the reliability of SRNT in older people. However, the results of the regression analyses used to examine the reliability of SRNT showed that the accuracy was significantly lower for 10–27 teeth than for ≥ 28 teeth. Therefore, there may be errors in SRNT for people who have lost teeth but still have many remaining teeth. In participants who did not undergo annual dental checkups, the presence or absence of an SNRT of 20 influenced the agreement with the actual number of teeth. In Japan, the 8020 movement has implemented various projects and measures to improve oral health since 1989. This movement has now become more widespread, not only among dental professionals but also among the general population [25]. For those undergoing dental checkups, having 20 or more teeth may serve as an indicator of oral health. In addition, the participants in this study who used interdental cleaning tools every day tended to provide an accurate SRNT. It is possible that those who are very conscious of oral hygiene are more concerned about the number of teeth they have and therefore have accurate knowledge thereof. By contrast, the other lifestyle habits examined in this study did not have a significant effect on the difference between the SRNT and observed number of teeth, which may constitute useful information for understanding the characteristics of SRNT data.

This study confirmed the reliability of using SRNT to predict actual numbers of teeth in Japanese individuals aged ≥ 75 years. The high reliability of SRNT has also been confirmed in studies targeting younger adult populations in Japan [17–19] and other countries [20, 21]. Although this study examined the agreement between SRNT and observed number of teeth, it was not possible to confirm the agreement between self-reported dental-treatment status and the prosthetic status of lost teeth. A previous study showed that SRNT inaccuracy increases with the number of restored teeth and fixed prostheses [22]. A study of individuals > 50 years of age showed that despite a high concordance between SRNT and actual numbers of teeth, self-reporting of the prosthetic status of missing teeth was not accurate [13]. It is important to keep in mind that treatment and prosthetic status may influence SRNT, and self-reported prosthetic status may be less reliable than SRNT.

The second aim of this study was to compare the characteristics of participants who did versus did not answer the SRNT questions in the questionnaire. Previous studies aiming to validate the reliability of SRNT have reported only the results of responders [15–22]. This study conducted a questionnaire survey among participants who underwent dental examinations, many of whom did not report their number of teeth. Approximately 20% of the participants did not answer whether they had ≥ 20 teeth, and about half did not provide their specific number of teeth. Based on the results of this study, reluctance to report a specific number of teeth seemed to increase with the number of teeth. Differences were also found in other factors between SRNT responders and non-responders, but the factor thought to have the greatest influence was the number of teeth. Although the rate of response to SRNT questions in other studies is unknown, SRNT showed poor concordance with actual numbers of teeth among people with more teeth [22]; this implies that people with more teeth may find it difficult to answer the SRNT question. The large number of non-responders among edentulous people may be because daily use of dentures makes them less conscious of their number of teeth. Additionally, because fewer edentulous than dentulous people visit the dentist [27], it is possible that edentulous people have a lower awareness of their number of teeth. Considering the results of this study, a questionnaire survey that asks about the category of number of teeth rather than a specific number of teeth yields a higher response rate and more reliable results. However, a simple question that categorises the number of teeth into two groups cannot be used as a continuous value, and there is a limit to the analytical power of analysing trends or dividing the findings into multiple categories. Depending on the investigators’ intentions, it is necessary to decide whether to prioritise the data-collection rate, accuracy, or analysability. In addition, the distribution of the responders’ SRNT may differ from that of a population of interest. In this study, evaluated the characteristics of those who did not respond to the SRNT questions, but their characteristics may have been influenced by other factors. A study of online questionnaires for pregnant women showed that factors such as older age and lower education level were associated with discontinuation of questionnaires [28]. As seen in the results, the characteristics of survey participants may also influence the response rate for questionnaires regarding participants’ numbers of teeth.

This study evaluated the SRNT was because using the SRNT in future epidemiological surveys could expand the feasibility of conducting epidemiological research, not just research based on oral-health examinations. Research based on SRNT seems effective, especially in studies targeting older individuals who have lost multiple teeth. In epidemiological studies on oral health, the oral condition of participants should be based on oral examinations by dentists [6]. In addition, to obtain reliable results, the population should be as large as possible [29]. By contrast, as health checkup and medical-expense receipt data have become digitised, dental studies based on big data such as national databases, which are not intended for epidemiological research, are being conducted [30–32]. If the reliability of the data can be ensured, self-reported health conditions will expand the range of research. When conducting epidemiological surveys, in many developing countries it is difficult to conduct dental checkups. To address this, the oral-examination manual of the World Health Organisation provides a questionnaire on numbers of teeth and subjective oral symptoms [33]. Because individuals’ number of teeth is influenced by their country [34], the factors that influence the accuracy of SRNT differ. Nevertheless, applying SRNT to epidemiological studies may be an important tool to clarify the effects of oral health on systemic health. In this study, the SRNT of independent (non-bedridden) older people was highly reliable, irrespective of the age group. Therefore, epidemiological research need not be hampered by an inability to perform dental examinations. However, as shown by the results of this study, it is possible that using SNRT in analyses may lead to errors in the results for those with more teeth.

Although this study presented some of the strengths and weaknesses above, other limitations should also be noted. The data used in this study were obtained 10 years ago. Although the number of teeth retained by older people in Japan is increasing, the rate of increase in tooth number is slower than it was more than 10 years ago, and the results of this study are still useful as a reference. Because the participants had undergone dental examinations at dental clinics, the results may not match those of individuals who did not undergo dental examinations in the same area. Additionally, because the study targeted an older population in a specific region of Japan, the generalisability of the results may be limited. This study targeted individuals who visited a dental clinic and received dental checkups, so most participants were independent older individuals; however, it was not possible to ascertain their cognitive status, including mild cognitive impairments. A previous study showed that SRNT concordance with actual tooth numbers was lower in older individuals with dementia [22]; thus, SRNT is more likely to be inaccurate in those with dementia. This study attempted to confirm the state of dementia from health insurance claims data, but it would be desirable if future studies aimed to ascertain intellectual status, including mild cognitive decline. This study did not confirm the validity of the questionnaire that includes SRNT. However, the government has proposed that this questionnaire be used in oral examinations of older people, and it is therefore used in other parts of Japan. In this study, dental checkups were performed according to the examination manual; however, no calibration training among examiners was done, and inter-examiner reliability was not confirmed. Nevertheless, because the presence or absence of teeth is obvious, discrepancies in teeth numbers are unlikely [5]. This study investigated the associations of items related to lifestyle and oral health habits with SRNT. Other factors, such as socioeconomic status, may also influence SRNT. In older Brazilian women, SRNT was found to be influenced by socioeconomic status [14]. This study evaluated the degree of agreement between SRNT and actual numbers of teeth; however, information on factors that influence SRNT was scarce. Further research is needed to identify factors that influence self-reported oral-health status in Japanese older people. The response rates for the specific tooth counts were low. When collecting data on SRNT, the ability to increase the response rate and the means to do so should be considered.

Conclusions

The SRNT of older people is reliable, particularly if they are asked if they have ≥ 20 teeth. Many people did not report their specific numbers of teeth; among those who did, there was little difference between the SRNT and actual numbers of teeth. Therefore, for independent older people in Japan, SRNT is very reliable.
