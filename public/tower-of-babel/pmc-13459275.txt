Synthesis and characterization of tungsten disulfide-graphene oxide nanocomposite
Authors: Riham M A El-Ghany, Mostafa Zedan, Gamal El-Ghannam, Walid Tawfik, Souad A Elfeky
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTwo-dimensional (2D) materials, such as tungsten disulfide (WS2) and graphene oxide (GO), have garnered significant attention. This is due to their unique physicochemical properties and potential applications in catalysis, electronics, and phototherapy. In this study, we report the synthesis and characterization of WS2 nanosheets, GO nanosheets, and a novel WS2-GO nanocomposite (NC). The NC was prepared via a simple wet-chemical method, which is low-cost and versatile, ensuring uniform dispersion and strong interfacial interactions between the WS2 and GO nanosheets. Functional group identification was done by Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FT-IR), while structural and morphological analysis was done using X-Ray Diffraction (XRD), Field Emission Scanning Electron Microscopy (FE-SEM), and Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM). Additionally, Energy Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (EDX) verified a homogeneous elemental distribution. FT-IR spectra of the NC reveal characteristic bands for carboxyl, hydroxyl, and alkoxy groups from GO, as well as W-S and S-S vibrational modes from WS2. XRD analysis confirms the crystalline nature of WS2-GO NC, displaying the characteristic GO peak alongside distinct diffraction peaks corresponding to the hexagonal phase of WS2, which verifies the successful formation of the hybrid. SEM and TEM imaging visually confirm the integration of WS2 onto the wrinkled GO surface with minimal aggregation and high crystallinity, as supported by selected area electron diffraction (SAED). Furthermore, the WS2-GO NC exhibits intensified ultraviolet absorption and broadened, quenched excitonic transitions in the visible range, indicating interfacial electronic coupling and efficient charge transfer. Together, these results demonstrate that the WS2-GO NC retains the functional properties of both components, making it a promising candidate for next-generation energy storage, catalysis, and biomedical applications.Keywords: WS2-GO NC, Tungsten disulfide (WS2), Graphene oxide (GO), Transition metal dichalcogenides, 2D nanomaterials

Introduction

2D nanomaterials represent an ultrathin class of layered materials characterized by high anisotropy and planar morphology [1–5]. Their unique architecture confers several advantages for biomedical applications, including robust mechanical strength, facile surface functionalization, exceptional surface-to-volume ratios, and intrinsic optical properties. Scaling bulk materials down to the nanoscale profoundly transforms their fundamental behavior, leading to the emergence of distinctive physicochemical properties due to their confinement to a single dimension. Specifically, 2D nanomaterials are defined by their confinement to a single dimension at the nanoscale, resulting in a planar, sheet-like configuration [6]. Since the groundbreaking isolation of graphene in 2004 [7], there has been global interest in developing diverse 2D structures for applications ranging from biotechnology to catalytic water splitting and energy storage [8–10]. Due to the quantum confinement of electrons within a 2D plane, these materials often exhibit superior electronic properties, such as significantly enhanced electron mobility and tunable optical opacity. Consequently, these properties hold significant promise for transforming biomedical fields, potentially unlocking novel avenues for the diagnosis and therapeutic intervention of various diseases [11, 12]. Beyond biotechnology, these emerging 2D materials exhibit remarkable potential across a broad spectrum of applications, including catalytic water splitting, advanced photoelectronics, and next-generation energy storage systems [8–10]. Furthermore, their exceptionally high surface area and ultrathin profiles make them ideal for applications where surface interactions are critical, such as targeted cancer therapy [6]. Among this class, graphene remains a standout carbon allotrope [13]. Its successful isolation proved that single-atom-thick layers could be engineered for extraordinary performance, fundamentally transforming our understanding of material design and opening new frontiers in nanotechnology [14]. The graphene family of materials has garnered significant interest due to its exceptional physical and chemical properties. Notably, graphene is the thinnest known material while maintaining extraordinary mechanical strength. Its thermal conductivity surpasses that of silicon by a factor of 25, and graphene-based materials possess an impressive theoretical surface area of 2630 m²/g. This versatile family primarily includes pristine graphene, GO, and reduced GO (rGO), each offering unique advantages for diverse applications [15]. Specifically, GO represents a distinct class of these materials, typically ranging from 1 to 100 nm in size. Its surface-functionalized oxygen groups enable the formation of stable colloidal suspensions, highlighting a hydrophilic character that facilitates biological interactions [16–18]. GO also responds to physical stimuli such as magnetic fields, ultrasound, and light enabling precise, on-demand payload release and facilitating combined therapeutic modalities, including photodynamic therapy (PDT) and photothermal therapy (PTT) [19]. Inspired by the pioneering work on graphene, a diverse array of other 2D nanomaterials has been engineered for anticancer applications. Notable examples include black phosphorus, graphitic-phase carbon nitride (g-C3N4), molybdenum disulfide (MoS2), and WS2 [6]. As a member of the transition metal dichalcogenides (TMDCs) family, WS2 exhibits a distinctive sandwich-like architecture, consisting of a hexagonal tungsten layer between two hexagonal sulfur layers [20]. This structural configuration endows WS2 with remarkable electrical, magnetic, thermal, and optical properties [21]. Consequently, WS2 nanomaterials have attracted substantial attention in medical research due to their high electrical conductivity, biocompatibility, and low toxicity. These attributes stem from their robust stability and capacity for facile functionalization with organic, inorganic, or biologically active molecules [22]. To date, various WS2 nanomaterials morphologies have been synthesized, including nanoparticles, nanocrystals [23, 24], nanotubes [25], hybrid nanomaterials, nanosheets [26], and quantum dots [27]. To provide a comprehensive perspective, it is essential to compare WS2 with other 2D systems, as the optimal material choice remains highly application dependent. Other TMDs like MoS₂ and WSe2 offer tunable bandgaps for electronics and energy applications [28, 29]. For instance, in gas sensing, sulfides such as MoS₂ and WS2 present distinct trade-offs in sensitivity and selectivity [29]. Direct comparative studies highlight WS2’s superior performance in specific domains: Riham et al. [21] demonstrated that WS2 exhibits enhanced nonlinear optical properties compared to MoS₂, featuring a 2.1× higher nonlinear absorption coefficient and a 1.8× greater nonlinear refractive index. These advantages, arising from stronger spin–orbit coupling and greater bandgap tunability, enable more efficient saturable absorption and light modulation in WS2-based systems. Beyond performance, biocompatibility is another key differentiator; WS2 maintains cell viability more than MoS₂ at the same concentration, a difference attributed to its lower generation of reactive oxygen species. Furthermore, the performance of such sulfides can be further enhanced by forming heterostructures and composites. For instance, molybdenum-based heterostructures, such as n-MoO₃@MoS₂, have been developed for efficient light harvesting, while CuS nanostructures have shown exceptional photosensitivity in photodetector applications. Furthermore, the incorporation of dopants into II-VI group semiconductors, such as CdS, has been a successful strategy for modulating their structural and optical properties in solar cells and photocatalytic technologies [30–34]. These studies highlight the versatility of sulfide systems and underscore the need for a comparative understanding of tungsten-based chalcogenides, which offer distinct electronic properties and high chemical stability. These modifications pave the way for WS2 formulations to play a pivotal role in cutting-edge innovations. While WS2 offers significant advantages, it faces limitations, including poor physiological stability and low cellular uptake. Integrating WS2 with GO addresses these challenges by enhancing stability and preventing agglomeration. To address these challenges, various surface modification strategies have been explored [35]. Following, WS2 is selected to integrate with GO to enhance the stability and durability of the composite. This choice is driven by WS2’s renowned chemical stability and resilience under harsh conditions [36, 37]. When combined, WS2 and GO exhibit synergistic effects, where the individual properties of each component enhance and complement the overall performance [38]. For instance, GO improves the dispersion of WS2 particles and prevents their agglomeration, while WS2 provides active catalytic sites. By adjusting the WS2-to-GO ratio and optimizing the synthesis process, the properties of the WS2-GO composite can be finely tuned, allowing for customization to meet the specific requirements of targeted applications [39, 40]. The unique integration of WS2 and GO enables the composite material to exhibit multifunctional capabilities, such as the ability to perform both photocatalysis and electrocatalysis simultaneously. This versatility makes the WS2-GO composite highly adaptable for a wide range of catalytic applications. Furthermore, the diverse oxygen-containing functional groups present in GO can introduce structural defects into the WS2 framework, generating additional active sites for catalytic reactions. This defect in engineering enhances the overall catalytic activity of the composite material, further broadening its utility in advanced catalytic processes [41]. While both WS2 and GO individually exhibit promising photothermal properties, their integration into a synergistic NC remains underexplored, particularly in the context of enhancing photothermal conversion efficiency for biomedical or energy applications. Existing studies predominantly focus on WS2 or GO as standalone photothermal agents, overlooking the potential benefits of combining WS2’s narrow bandgap and strong near-infrared absorption with GO’s high thermal conductivity and large surface area. Moreover, there is limited understanding of how interfacial interactions, structural compatibility, and functional group engineering in WS2-GO hybrids influence heat generation, light absorption, and stability under irradiation. These underexplored aspects open promising avenues for future research. Systematic optimization of WS2-GO NC through precise tuning of WS2-to-GO ratios, heterostructure interface engineering, and controlled defect modulation—holds significant potential for advancing their functional properties. Such efforts could focus on enhancing near-infrared absorption and photothermal conversion efficiency, thereby enabling transformative applications in deep-tissue photothermal therapy with minimized side effects, as well as in advanced catalytic and energy conversion systems. Thus, there is a critical need to design and systematically investigate WS2-GO NCs to unlock their full photothermal potential through optimized synthesis, interfacial engineering, and performance evaluation. This study aims to synthesize and characterize WS2-GO NC and evaluate their structural, chemical, and functional properties for catalytic and biomedical applications.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and materials

The following reagents were used as received: graphite fine powder (extra pure), sulfuric acid (H2SO4, 98%), orthophosphoric acid (H3PO4, 85%), hydrochloric acid (HCl fuming, 37%), and absolute ethanol (EMPLURA) were supplied by Millipore Merck. Potassium permanganate (KMnO4) and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) were procured from Advent India. Sodium tungstate dihydrate (Na2WO4·2H2O), thiourea (CH4N2S), hydroxylamine hydrochloride (NH₂OH·HCl), and cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) were acquired from Loba Chemie, India.

Synthesis of GO nanosheets

GO was prepared from graphite flakes using an improved Hummer method [42]. In a typical synthesis, graphite flakes (3.0 g) and KMnO₄ (18.0 g) were combined. A 9:1 v/v mixture of concentrated H₂SO₄/H₃PO₄ (360:40 mL) was added, inducing a slight exotherm to 35–40 °C. The reaction mixture was heated to 50 °C and stirred for 12 h. After cooling to room temperature, the mixture was quenched by pouring it onto ice (400 mL), followed by the slow addition of 30% H₂O₂ (3 mL) to reduce residual permanganate. The resulting suspension was isolated by centrifugation (10,000 rpm, 20 min), and the supernatant was discarded. The solid pellet was then subjected to a sequential washing regimen with water (200 mL), 30% HCl (200 mL), and ethanol (200 mL). Subsequent washes with deionized water were performed. The improved GO (IGO) synthesis method demonstrated superior efficiency, particularly during the initial purification. To separate the products, the mixture was filtered through a polyester mesh; the oxidized hydrophilic carbon material passed through, while under-oxidized hydrophobic fragments were retained due to their larger particle size and low solubility. The resulting purified GO was then dried under ambient conditions (Fig. 1a). Notably, this method yielded 0.7 g of IGO [42].

Synthesis of WS2 nanosheets

WS2 nanosheets were synthesized via a hydrothermal route using Na2WO4·2H2O and CH4N2S, as tungsten and sulfur precursors, respectively. In a typical procedure, Na2WO4·2 H₂O (0.005 M) was combined with CH4N2S, (0.02 M) and NH2OH·HCl (0.01 M) as a reducing agent in deionized water under vigorous stirring. CTAB (0.24 g) was introduced as a surfactant to direct the growth of nanosheets. After one hour of stirring, a white precipitate formed (pH 6.15). The mixture was sealed in a Teflon-lined stainless-steel autoclave and maintained at 180 °C for 24 h. Upon completion, the resulting product was cooled to room temperature, collected by centrifugation, and washed thoroughly with deionized water and absolute ethanol to remove impurities. The final product was dried in an oven at 30 °C for 4 h (Fig. 1b) [43]. Hydrothermal synthesis has emerged as an eco-friendly and scalable approach for fabricating diverse nanostructures, offering distinct advantages such as low-temperature processing, high yield, and straightforward synthesis conditions [44, 45].

Synthesis of WS2-GO NC

Following a green chemistry approach, the WS2-GO NC was synthesized via a wet chemical hydrothermal route using deionized water, as the sole solvent (Fig. 1c). This method avoids toxic organic solvents and corrosive media, utilizing water as a non-toxic, non-flammable, and abundantly available solvent, thereby aligning with the principles of environmentally friendly synthesis [46, 47]. In a typical procedure, a primary suspension was formed by dispersing 1 g of WS2 nanocrystals in 100 mL of deionized water via ultrasonication. To this suspension, 1 g of GO was added as a precursor, and the combined mixture was agitated for 10 min to ensure homogeneity. This mixture was then subjected to hydrothermal treatment in a 150 mL Teflon-lined autoclave at 60 °C for 6 h to facilitate composite formation [48]. The final product was isolated by filtration and dried under vacuum at 60 °C overnight, yielding the WS2-GO NC (1.7 g, 85%).

Characterization of samples

The morphology and atomic-scale structure of the synthesized materials were analyzed using electron microscopy. High-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) was conducted on a JEOL JEM-2100 microscope at an accelerating voltage of 200 kV. For TEM analysis, samples were prepared by drop-casting a colloidal suspension onto a copper grid (300-mesh, Formvar/carbon-coated) and drying it in air. Elemental composition was confirmed using energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) coupled with FE-SEM on a Thermo Scientific Quattro S system. XRD patterns were recorded on a Malvern Panalytical Empyrean instrument (Netherlands) with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.54060 Å) to determine crystallographic phase and structure. Scans were performed from 5° to 85° (2θ) with a step size of 0.04°. Molecular vibrational modes were investigated by attenuated total reflectance Fourier transform infrared (ATR/FTIR) spectroscopy using a Bruker Vertex 70-RAM II spectrometer, collecting spectra in the range of 400–4000 cm⁻¹. Optical absorption and diffuse reflectance spectroscopy (DRS) of the prepared material were determined via UV-Vis spectrophotometry (Cary Series UV-Vis-NIR, Australia).

Results and discussion

FT-IR

FT-IR spectroscopy is a powerful analytical technique used to identify and characterize chemical compounds based on their molecular vibrations. FT-IR spectra of the samples were recorded (Fig. 2) to study the functional groups present. The presence of WS2 nanosheets was evidenced by the W-S stretching band at 558 cm⁻¹ [43] and the S-S stretching bands at 974 and 432 cm⁻¹ [49]. The O-H (out-of-plane) bending at 1626 cm⁻¹ was also observed. Peaks detected at 1407 cm⁻¹ and approximately 2917 cm⁻¹ are attributed to C-H bending in CH₂ groups [50] and C-H stretching vibrations [51], respectively. These peaks may arise from organic residues, surface adsorbates, or functionalization agents used during synthesis or exfoliation (e.g., CTAB, organic solvents), which can leave residual CH₂ groups. Additionally, residual ethanol used in washing can contribute to the C-H signals. The FT-IR spectrum of GO shows characteristic peaks. In the spectrum, a shoulder at ~ 3616 cm⁻¹ corresponds to O-H stretching in edge hydroxyl groups [52]. Peaks at 1620 cm⁻¹ and 1727 cm⁻¹ indicate C = C bonds in aromatic rings [52, 53] and carboxyl (C = O) stretching [53], respectively. A peak at 1035 cm⁻¹ is assigned to C-O-C stretching in epoxy or alkoxy groups [54]. FT-IR analysis of WS2-GO nanosheets was carried out to investigate the interactions of WS2 with GO and to verify successful adsorption. The WS2-GO spectrum retained GO-related peaks at 3616 cm⁻¹, 1620 cm⁻¹, 1035 cm⁻¹, and 1727 cm⁻¹, and WS2 peaks at 558 cm⁻¹, 974 cm⁻¹, and 432 cm⁻¹ were also detected, confirming the incorporation of WS2 into the GO matrix. Notable changes in peak intensity and minor peak shifts suggest strong interfacial interactions between WS2 and GO, likely due to chemical bonding or electrostatic interactions. GO may serve as a stabilizing matrix for WS2, preventing agglomeration and enhancing dispersion. XRD

XRD analysis (Fig. 3) was used to investigate the crystal structure and the quality of crystallization of WS2, GO, and the WS2-GO samples across a 2θ range of 5.0°—85°. The WS2 nanosheet XRD pattern showed characteristic peaks at 2θ ≈ 14.5°, 28.8°, 29.8°, 38°, 45°, 50.4°, 58.9°, and 66°, corresponding to the (002), (004), (100), (103), (006), (110), and (114) planes, respectively [51, 55]. All the observed peaks are consistent with the hexagonal crystal structure of WS2 (JCPDS NO. 08–0237). The strongest diffraction peak at 14.5° (002 plane) indicates a layered stacking structure, characteristic of WS2 nanosheets [56]. The (002) interlayer spacing was determined to be 0.61 nm through application of Bragg’s law (nλ = 2dsinθ, where θ = 7.25° corresponds to the observed 2θ = 14.5° diffraction peak and λ = 1.54056 Å). The pronounced intensity of this (002) reflection relative to other diffraction peaks indicates strong preferential basal plane orientation, confirming the 2D nanosheet morphology of the WS2 samples. Additionally, the lattice parameters of the hexagonal WS2 phase (a = b ≠ c) were determined from the (110) diffraction plane. Refinement yielded unit cell dimensions of a = 3.156 Å and c = 12.78 Å, with a corresponding cell volume of 110.4 Å³. These values show good agreement with the standard JCPDS reference (No. 00-08-0237) for 2 H-WS2 (a = 3.1540 Å, c = 12.3620 Å, V = 106.50 Å³), confirming the phase purity of the synthesized nanosheets. The XRD diffraction pattern of the synthesized GO nanosheet exhibited a prominent peak at ≈ 10.03°, corresponding to the (001) crystal plane, with an interlayer spacing of 0.846 nm, indicating complete oxidation and exfoliation of GO [57]. The XRD spectrum of the WS2-GO NC exhibited peaks characteristic of both WS2 and GO, confirming the coexistence of both crystalline phases. Notably, several higher-angle WS2 diffraction peaks, particularly those at 50.4° (110), 58.6°, and 60°, exhibited a marked reduction in intensity or complete disappearance in the composite pattern. This phenomenon can be attributed to several factors. First, the strong preferential basal plane orientation of WS2 nanosheets, evidenced by the dominant (002) reflection, becomes even more pronounced when the nanosheets are deposited onto the GO surface. This texturing effect causes non-basal planes such as (110) and (114) to be oriented away from the Bragg diffraction condition, resulting in diminished or absent peaks [58]. Second, the incorporation of WS2 onto GO introduces interfacial strain and lattice distortions, as suggested by the observed change in c-axis spacing. Such non-uniform strain preferentially broadens higher-angle reflections, which are more sensitive to lattice imperfections, potentially causing them to merge with the background [59]. Third, the amorphous carbon matrix of GO contributes significant diffuse scattering, which can mask weaker diffraction peaks from WS2 [60]. Additionally, peaks at higher angles (50–60°) are intrinsically less intense than the primary (002) reflection and are therefore more susceptible to being obscured by the GO background signal [60].

SEM

The surface morphology and elemental composition of WS2, GO, and WS2-GO NC were analyzed using FE-SEM and TEM, along with SAED. These techniques provided insights into structural integrity, exfoliation efficiency, and NC formation. FE-SEM of WS2, GO, and the WS2-GO NC at different magnifications were studied, as seen in Fig. 4. The WS2 samples at different magnifications are displayed in Fig. 4a, b. As seen in Fig. 4a (low-magnification SEM image), the samples primarily consist of numerous wrinkled and overlapped nanosheets, exhibiting a morphology similar to that reported by Zhang et al. [61, 62]. To further examine the structure of the nanosheets, a high-magnification SEM image was obtained (Fig. 4b). The image reveals that the synthesized nanosheets are nearly monodisperse and loosely stacked [62]. Moreover, WS2 exhibited a layered structure showing flake and sheet-like morphology with sharp edges, which are characteristic of WS2 nanosheets, indicating their well-defined crystalline nature, indicative of controlled hydrothermal synthesis. SEM also identifies wrinkles and folds on the surface of WS2 nanosheets, which are common in 2D materials due to their flexibility and van der Waals interactions between layers. GO nanosheets display characteristic wrinkles and folds (Fig. 4c, d), confirming the successful exfoliation of graphite oxide into 2D GO nanosheets. Comparable results of FE-SEM images are reported in the literature [63]. This morphology enhances surface reactivity and dispersion, making GO a suitable template for WS2 integration. High-magnification FE-SEM images of WS2-GO (Fig. 4e, f) revealed a highly wrinkled WS2 surface upon conjugating with regularly distributed GO nanosheets. The uniform dispersion of GO prevents WS2 agglomeration, which enhances stability and surface area, beneficial for catalysis and electronic applications. These results confirm the successful integration of WS2 and GO, forming a stable NC.

TEM

TEM and HRTEM were employed to further investigate the morphology of GO and WS2 nanosheets. Figure 5a, b show TEM images of the as-synthesized WS2 nanosheet. The low-magnification TEM image (Fig. 5a) reveals that the samples consist primarily of thin, well-dispersed nanosheets [62]. Also, it appears randomly agglomerated, suggesting the presence of weak van der Waals forces, like previous reports [56, 62]. The high-magnification TEM image of WS2 (Fig. 5b) showed the structure more clearly. The structure of the as-prepared WS2 was further analyzed by SAED patterns (the inset in Fig. 5b) that display a ring with multiple diffraction spots [56]. The hexagonal symmetry of WS2 is clearly visible from the diffraction pattern in accordance with Singh et al. [64]. The SAED patterns provide complementary evidence to the XRD analysis of WS2. The measured d-spacings derived from XRD are approximately 0.61 nm for (002) at 2θ ≈ 14.5°, 0.31 nm for (004) at 28.8°, 0.30 nm for (100) at 29.8°, 0.24 nm for (103) at 38°, 0.20 nm for (006) at 45°, 0.18 nm for (110) at 50.4°, and 0.16 nm for (114) at 66°. These values match well with the SAED ring positions, confirming the hexagonal crystal structure. The strong intensity of the (002) ring highlights the preferential basal plane orientation, consistent with the dominant (002) reflection observed in XRD. Furthermore, the TEM images of the as-synthesized WS2 nanosheet (Fig. 5a, b) confirmed the successful synthesis of sheet-like WS2, which is consistent with the morphology as presented in the FE-SEM observations.

a TEM micrograph and b HRTEM image of WS2 nanosheet (the inset displays the SAED pattern). The images reveal thin, well-dispersed nanosheets with minimal aggregation. The inset SAED pattern shows hexagonal symmetry and multiple diffraction spots, confirming high crystallinity and the layered structure of WS2, consistent with its 2D morphology

TEM imaging (Fig. 6a) shows that the GO nanosheets possess a highly transparent and lamellar structure, adorned with the characteristic ripples and folds that are indicative of effective exfoliation [65]. This ultrathin, wrinkled morphology is a direct result of the oxidation process (using H₂SO₄ and KMnO₄) and sonication, which disrupts the van der Waals forces in the parent graphite. The high-quality crystalline order within the sheets is evidenced by the SAED pattern (Fig. 6b), which displays a clear hexagonal symmetry, a signature of GO that aligns with XRD data. The SAED pattern of GO exhibits a broad diffuse ring corresponding to the (001) plane with a d-spacing of ~ 0.846 nm, reflecting its oxidized layered structure.

a TEM micrograph and b SAED image of GO nanosheets. The GO sheets exhibit a transparent, lamellar structure with characteristic folds and ripples, indicative of successful exfoliation. The SAED pattern displays hexagonal symmetry, confirming the crystalline nature and structural integrity of GO

The TEM image of the WS2-GO NC in Fig. 7a reveals distinct structural features indicative of successful composite formation. Dark, shaped aggregates correspond to WS2 nanocrystals, whose pronounced contrast arises from the high atomic number of tungsten, resulting in strong electron scattering. These nanocrystals are anchored onto a faint, wrinkled background attributed to the GO substrate, which appears thin, flexible, and slightly crumpled due to its sheet-like morphology. The clustered arrangement of WS2 domains on GO suggests uniform dispersion and strong interfacial interactions, likely mediated by van der Waals forces, hydrogen bonding, or defect-assisted anchoring. The relatively uniform size distribution of the WS2 particles further supports controlled nucleation and minimal post-synthesis aggregation, underscoring the reproducibility and scalability of the hydrothermal synthesis method [66]. The SAED pattern of the WS2-GO NC reveals a well-defined crystalline structure, indicative of successful integration of WS2 nanocrystals within the GO matrix. The presence of concentric diffraction rings, along with discrete bright spots, confirms the polycrystalline nature of the WS2 domains. These rings correspond to specific lattice planes of hexagonal WS2, such as (002), (100), and (110), which are commonly observed in layered TMDCs. The symmetry and sharpness of the spots suggest high crystallinity and minimal structural disorder. The coexistence of crystalline WS2 and GO in the SAED pattern (Fig. 7b) supports the formation of a hybrid nanostructure with strong interfacial interactions. The WS2–GO composite shows a combination of both features: distinct WS2 rings indexed to the (002), (100), and (103) planes with their respective d-spacings, together with the diffuse (001) ring of GO. This coexistence of indexed diffraction features demonstrates that WS2 nanosheets retain their crystallinity while being successfully integrated with GO sheets. Functionally, this morphology offers several advantages: GO serves as a conductive scaffold enhancing electron mobility, while WS2 contributes semiconducting properties suitable for optoelectronic and sensing applications. The intimate contact between the two components promotes catalytic synergy through efficient charge separation and active site exposure, and the structural integration of flexible GO with rigid WS2 yields a mechanically robust composite with potential utility in advanced energy, environmental, or biomedical systems.

EDX

The elemental composition of WS2, GO nanosheets, and the WS2-GO NC was analyzed using energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX). The chemical elements present in WS2, GO nanosheets, and the WS2-GO NC include carbon (C), oxygen (O), nitrogen (N), tungsten (W), and sulfur (S). The EDX analysis for unmodified WS2 nanosheets, shown in Fig. 8, indicated a composition of 99.7% tungsten and 0.29% sulfur, with no detectable impurities. The significantly low sulfur content suggests a deficiency of sulfur in the WS2 nanosheets, which may be attributed to incomplete sulfurization during synthesis, loss of sulfur due to high-temperature processing, or formation of tungsten-rich phases or defects in the WS2 lattice. Such sulfur deficiencies are commonly reported in hydrothermal synthesis [67].

The EDX spectra of the as-prepared WS2 nanosheets

The EDX data for GO, depicted in the corresponding Fig. 9, shows a carbon content of 64.2% and an oxygen content of 35.8%. The EDX results confirm the successful synthesis of GO with a high oxygen content, validating the oxidation process. The C/O ratio of ~ 1.79 aligns with typical GO compositions [68], indicating the presence of oxygen-functionalized carbon sheets. The results suggest that the GO structure is pure, well-oxidized, and can be effectively used in NC formation or further chemical modifications.

The EDX spectra of the as-prepared GO nanosheets. The spectrum reveals 64.2% carbon and 35.8% oxygen, with a C/O ratio of ~ 1.79, confirming successful oxidation and the presence of oxygen-functionalized groups essential for composite formation

EDX analysis of the WS2-GO NC provides quantitative insights into its elemental composition and resulting structural features that underpin its functional properties (Fig. 10). The analysis reveals carbon (36.03 wt%), oxygen (25.20 wt%), tungsten (38.08 wt%), and sulfur (0.69 wt%) (Table 2), with low error margins for C, O, and W confirming reliable quantification, while the higher error for S (19.64%) indicates its concentration approaches the detection limit. The carbon-to-oxygen atomic ratio of approximately 1.9:1 aligns with the expected stoichiometry of GO synthesized via oxidative methods such as the Hummers approach [69]. This confirms the presence of abundant oxygen-containing functional groups, which enhance the hydrophilicity and serve as anchoring sites for the nucleation and growth of the inorganic WS2 phase [70]. The tungsten-to-sulfur atomic ratio of approximately 9.6:1 deviates significantly from the ideal 1:2 stoichiometry of pristine WS2, indicating substantial sulfur deficiency. Such sulfur vacancies are widely reported in 2D TMDs due to their high surface area and synthesis conditions [71], introducing mid-gap states and localized defect levels that strongly influence charge transport and optical properties [72, 73]. These defect states enable sub-bandgap optical transitions, enhancing absorption in the visible to near-infrared (NIR) region, beneficial for applications such as saturable absorbers or NIR photodetectors [74–76]. While also increasing exposed active sites for reactions like HER in catalytic and battery applications [71, 77]. In such applications, a moderate density of sulfur vacancies can enhance performance, though excessive deficiency may introduce recombination centers and compromise long-term structural stability [78, 79]. The close interfacial contact between defect-rich, sulfur-deficient WS2 (WS2₋ₓ) and the conductive GO support, supported by reliable tungsten quantification, facilitates the formation of a synergistic heterojunction capable of efficient charge separation and transport [80]. This integrated structure, combining controlled sulfur deficiency with the conductive GO network, is particularly advantageous for photocatalysis, sensing, and energy storage applications where defect-mediated mechanisms and efficient charge dynamics are critical.

DRS and UV-visible spectroscopy

The DRS of GO, WS2, and the WS2-GO NC (Fig. 11a) exhibit pronounced absorption features in the ultraviolet region (≈ 230–330 nm), which are attributed to high-energy electronic transitions (> 3.8 eV) inherent to their electronic structures. In GO, this absorption band is mainly associated with π–π* transitions of sp²-hybridized carbon domains [82], whereas in WS2 it originates from higher-energy interband transitions involving the valence and conduction bands. Notably, the WS2-GO NC shows a significantly enhanced absorption intensity in this region, indicating strong electronic coupling and efficient charge-transfer interactions between the GO sheets and WS2 layers similar to those observed in [83]. The complementary UV–Vis absorption spectra (Fig. 11b) further support these findings and confirm successful composite formation. GO exhibits a gradually decreasing absorption profile in the visible region, with weak features extending toward ~ 550–642 nm, which can be attributed to electronic transitions associated with residual sp² domains and oxygen-containing functional groups. In contrast, pristine WS2 displays two distinct absorption bands centered at ~ 550 and ~ 642 nm, corresponding to the B and A excitonic transitions arising from direct electronic transitions at the K point of the Brillouin zone. The A excitonic peak corresponds to a direct band-gap energy of approximately 1.93 eV, confirming the semiconducting nature of WS2 [84]. These excitonic features are retained in the WS2–GO NC, albeit with noticeable broadening and reduced intensity in the 580–630 nm range, indicating strong interfacial interaction between WS2 nanosheets and the GO matrix. The peak broadening and intensity suppression suggest efficient charge transfer and partial quenching of excitonic recombination due to the formation of interfacial electronic states. Building upon the versatility highlighted previously, our comparison reveals that WS2-GO NC exhibits a characteristic absorption profile consistent with the behavior of other nanostructured sulfides. Previous studies on doped CdS and CuS have reported significant blue or red shifts in the absorption spectra depending on the dopant concentration and nanoparticle size [32–34, 85]. Specifically, bandgap narrowing or widening in these systems is often attributed to the introduction of localized energy states or quantum confinement effects.

Conclusions

In this study, 2D WS2, GO, and a novel WS2-GO NC were synthesized using a facile wet-chemical approach. Comprehensive structural, compositional, and morphological characterization was performed using FTIR, XRD, SEM, TEM, EDX, and UV-Vis (DRS) analyses. FTIR spectroscopy revealed the presence of functional groups associated with both WS2 and GO, such as W-S, S-S bonds, and oxygen-containing groups (e.g., C = O, C-OH). XRD analysis confirmed the crystalline nature of WS2, GO, and their NC. Morphological analysis by SEM confirmed the uniform dispersion of WS2 nanosheets on the GO surface. TEM analysis clearly revealed the layered structure of WS2 and the ultrathin, transparent, and wrinkled morphology of 2D GO. SAED patterns further validated the hexagonal phase and high crystallinity of the WS2 component. Furthermore, TEM imaging revealed the ultrathin, transparent, and wrinkled sheet-like structure characteristic of the 2D nature of GO. The successful formation and enhanced properties of the NC were further corroborated by elemental and optical analyses. EDX analysis confirmed the presence of carbon, oxygen, tungsten, and sulfur, with the tungsten-to-sulfur ratio indicating substantial sulfur deficiency in the WS2. These sulfur vacancies are known to introduce mid-gap states, which can enhance visible to near-infrared absorption and increase active sites for catalytic reactions. Complementing the EDX findings, UV-Vis DRS analysis provided critical insights into the optical properties and electronic interactions within the NC. All samples exhibit pronounced absorption in the ultraviolet region, attributed to high-energy electronic transitions. Notably, the WS2-GO NC shows a significantly enhanced absorption intensity in this region, indicating strong electronic coupling and efficient charge-transfer interactions between the GO sheets and WS2 layers. Collectively, these findings confirm the successful synthesis of WS2, GO, and their nanohybrid, with enhanced structural integrity and strong interfacial interactions. The resulting structural and optical characteristics, including sulfur-vacancy-mediated absorption and efficient charge transfer, position the WS2-GO NC as a highly promising candidate for advanced applications such as photocatalysis and phototherapy. The safety and stability of the NC under physiological conditions and its actual cellular uptake efficiency should be investigated. This work provides a comprehensive understanding of WS2-GO NCs and lays a foundation for developing next-generation 2D material-based nanomaterials for advanced technological applications.
