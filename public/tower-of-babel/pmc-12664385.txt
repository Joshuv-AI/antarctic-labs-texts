Selective adsorption of metabolites by different size of TiO2 nano particles from tea infusion
Authors: Lu Li, Qian Wang, Mingchun Wen, Zekai Wang, Yuelin Song, Liang Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTea is rich in catechins, amino acids, and carbohydrates, which are the main flavor components of tea. However, excessive amount of catechins contribute to the bitter and astringent tastes of tea, thereby affecting the quality of tea. In this study, TiO2 was employed as an adsorbent material to investigate its adsorption properties for different structural types of main flavor compounds in tea infusion. The results found that anatase-type TiO2 had strong adsorption capacity for tea polyphenols, and its adsorption amount relied on the surface area of TiO2 particles. After the addition of 60 nm TiO2, the total catechins content in tea infusion decreased from 813.78 to 93.16 μg/mL. Meanwhile, the content of theanine and monosaccharides, the umami and sweet compounds of tea, was not affected. These results indicated that TiO2 could serve as an adsorbent to exclusively remove the bitter tasting component catechins from tea and enhance their taste profile.Keywords: Titanium dioxide, Catechins, Theanine, Monosaccharides, Adsorption

Introduction

Tea is one of the most popular beverages around the world. Usually, the fresh tea leaves (Camellia sinensis) are used as materials to produce various types of tea, such as green, black and oolong tea. In recent years, with the development of bottled tea beverages, various types of tea drinks are popular in the global market, such as pure tea drinks, flavored tea drinks, and ready-to-drink tea beverages. Tea resources are widely used in the beverage industry (Liang et al., 2021). Although tea leaves have always been used as raw materials to manufacture tea beverage, some issues still influence the quality of final tea product. In tea components, the comprehensive flavor profile is constructed by various taste compounds (Zhang et al., 2020), these flavor profiles significantly impact tea quality. For instance, in terms of taste of tea, low bitterness and high umami are key indicators of high-quality tea, which is also highly palatable to consumers. Theanine and monosaccharides are umami and sweet compounds that contribute to the high consumer acceptability of products (Zhang et al., 2020). High contents of polyphenols in tea trigger the bitter and astringent taste, lead to low consumer acceptance. (Yang & Lee, 2020; Ye, Ye, et al., 2022). In addition, the high content of theanine in fresh tea leaves usually contributes to the high-quality of final tea product (Jia et al., 2018). For example, the fresh leaves of white tea contain more theanine, which is often more than twice the content of ordinary tea leaves, resulting in a refreshing and umami taste (Xie et al., 2021).

Catechins are main polyphenol compounds of tea leaves and tea products. They are the main bioactive compounds of tea, possessing various biological activities, such as hypoglycemic, lipid-lowering, anti-inflammatory, antibacterial, and other health benefits (Lambert et al., 2007; Liu et al., 2022). Furthermore, catechins are also bitter and astringent compounds. With the maturing of tea leaves, the content of catechins in tea leaves continues to increase, resulting in a significantly higher bitter taste in summer and autumn tea compared to spring tea (Cao et al., 2019; Ye, Guo, et al., 2022). Therefore, due to bitter and astringent taste, a large amount of summer and autumn tea has not been fully utilized, resulting in a significant waste of resources. In order to fully utilize summer and autumn tea leaves, especially for their application in the tea beverage industry, it is necessary to reduce the content of catechins in tea infusion or tea extracts.

Typically, the methods for removing large amounts of catechins from tea are generally solvent extraction or chromatographic separation (Row & Jin, 2006; Zhang et al., 2023). However, these methods inevitably use organic solvents, which pose certain hazards and toxicity. Catechins could be classified into catechol-type and pyrogallol-type catechins, both of which contain phenolic hydroxyl groups in the B-ring of flavan-3-ols (Braicu et al., 2013; Hu et al., 2024). These phenolic hydroxyl groups have strong reactivity, especially easy to form complexes with transition metal ions (Chen et al., 2024; Holloway et al., 2012). When catechins are in a solution of variable valence metal ions, they are prone to undergo oxidation reactions to form oxidation products such as theaflavins (Yasuda et al., 2012). However, when catechins bind with inert metal ions, the catechol hydroxyl group can form a very strong covalent bond with the metal ion and be adsorbed onto it.

Titanium dioxide (TiO2) is commonly used as a food additive for whitening foods, and it also serves as a common adsorbent used in food processing (Berardinelli & Parisi, 2021; Weir et al., 2012). Owing to its higher surface reactivity compared to the rutile phase, anatase TiO2 is the predominant form utilized in food applications (Winkler et al., 2018). Meanwhile, TiO2 usually exists in the form of powder/granules, which maintains stable adsorption performance and can be removed by centrifugation after adsorption (Amano et al., 2010; Li et al., 2021; Zhou et al., 2019). Furthermore, TiO2 poses minimal health risks to humans when used as a food additive within recommended limits. Studies have indicated that TiO2 do not have a significantly effect on human gut microbiota (Dudefoi et al., 2017). Moreover, TiO2 particles of any size cannot penetrate the stratum corneum of the skin (Winkler et al., 2018).

The main purpose of this study is to use TiO2 as an adsorbent to investigate the adsorption of catechins and other secondary metabolites in tea infusion by different particle sizes of TiO2. The main taste compounds of tea were quantitatively determined before and after adsorption with TiO2, and the change of secondary metabolites in tea infusion was also qualitatively analyzed. In order to expect to find a suitable amount of TiO2 added, so that most of the catechins in the tea can be adsorbed at the same time to enhance the flavor quality of tea, this result not only provide a theoretical basis for the production of tea beverages, but also help mitigate the resource waste associated with summer and autumn tea.

Materials and methods

Materials and chemicals

Baihaoyinzhen, was purchased from Fuding Bainian Xibai Tea Industry Co., Ltd. (Ningde, Fujian, China). TiO2 with 25 nm, 40 nm, 60 nm, 100 nm, and 2–3 μm particle sizes (labeled as T-25, T-40, T-60, T-100, T-2) were purchased from Macklin Chemicals (Macklin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China).

Catechins standards of (+)-catechin (C), (−)-epicatechin (EC), (−)-gallocatechin (GC), (−)-epigallocatechin (EGC), (−)-epicatechin gallate (ECG), (−)-gallocatechin gallate (GCG), and (−)-epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG) were purchased from Shanghai Yuanye Biotechnology Company (Shanghai, China), and their purities were higher than 95 %. Monosaccharides standards of d-glucose (D-Glc), D-galactose (D-Gal), D-galacturonic acid (D-GalA), D-glucuronic acid (D-GlcA), D-mannose (D-Man), L-rhamnose (L-Rha), L-fucose (L-Fuc), L-arabinose (L-Ara), and D-xylose (D-Xyl) were purchased from the above company. Amino acid standards of L-aspartic acid (L-Asp), L-glutamic acid (L-Glu), and L-theanine (L-Thea) were purchased from Sigma Aldrich Chemicals (St. Louis, MO, USA), and their purities were higher than 98 %. HPLC-grade acetonitrile, formic acid, and triethylamine were purchased from TEDIA Company Inc. (Fairfield, OH, USA).

Sample extraction

1000 mg of white tea powder was weighed, then extracted with hot distilled water in water bath at 90 °C for 20 min. After cooling, the supernatant liquid was obtained by centrifugation in a refrigerated high-speed centrifuge. After repeating the above operation for two times, used a volumetric flask to fix the volume and obtained the extract (10 mg/mL).

Adsorption of extracts using TiO2 nanoparticles

TiO2 nanoparticles adsorption method has been studied similarly by previous researchers (Li et al., 2021; Li et al., 2022). The extracts were mixed with different concentrations of anatase TiO2 nanoparticles of different particle sizes (T-25, T-40, T-60, T-100, T-2), respectively. The blank control was the extract without added TiO2 nanoparticles. After the vortex, it was placed at room temperature for 24 h. The extracts without TiO2 nanoparticles were set as control. In addition, the extracts and extract-TiO2 mixture were passed through a 0.22 μm membrane for subsequent analysis after centrifugation. Three biological replicates were performed for each sample.

Determination of catechins using UHPLC

To determine the content of catechins in the samples, we quantified them with a UHPLC system (Thermo Scientific Dionex UltiMate 3000, Waltham, MA, USA) that consisted of a degasser, an auto-sampler (WPS-3000RS), a binary pump (HPG-3400RS), a column compartment (TCC-100), and a diode array detector (DAD-3000). The samples in Section 2.3 were analyzed with an Acquity UPLC® BEH Shield RP18 column (1.7 μm, 2.1 × 50 mm, Waters, Milford, MA, USA), the phases were water (contained 0.1 % formic acid, v/v, phase A) and acetonitrile (phase B). In addition, the parameters (such as injection volume, elution program, column oven temperature, and DAD detection wavelength) were carried out strictly in line with those we previously disclosed (Wen et al., 2025).

Determination of non-volatile metabolites using UPLC-ESI-Q/TOF-HRMS

We used an Agilent 1290 UHPLC system coupled with a time-of-flight mass spectrometer (Agilent 1290 Infinity II-6545 Q-TOF, Agilent Technologies, Palo Alto, CA, USA) to characterize non-volatile metabolites in the samples. The samples in Section 2.3 were analyzed with an Acquity UPLC® BEH Shield RP18 column (1.7 μm, 2.1 × 50 mm, Waters, Milford, MA, USA), the phases were water (contained 0.1 % formic acid, v/v, phase A) and acetonitrile (phase B). Scanning was performed in negative ionization mode over a full scan range of mass to charge (m/z) 100–1700 Da. In addition, the chromatographic and mass parameters (e.g., injection volume, elution program, column oven temperature, and DAD detection wavelength, etc.) have been reported in previous studies (Li et al., 2025).

In order to elucidated the structure of metabolites, the extracts were analyzed in negative targeted MS/MS mode, while the MS2 (MS/MS) scanning range was set at m/z 50–1500 Da. Then, the structure of metabolites was proposed according to their fragment ions, referring to tea metabolome database (Yue et al., 2014), authentic compounds, and previous studies (Li et al., 2025; Wen et al., 2025).

Determination of free amino acids using HPLC-QqQ-MS

The sample used for the determination of free amino acids is the same sample used in Section 2.3 after adsorption, except that the sample needs to be diluted by a factor of ten (1 mg/mL) before analysis. Using a triple-quadrupole linear ion trap mass spectrometer (API 5500 QTRAP™ MS/MS system from AB Sciex, Concord, ON, Canada) coupled to an ExionLC system (AB Sciex) for analysis. A comix column (250 × 4.6 mm, 2.7 μm, Comixshell CARP, Phenomenex, Torrance, CA, USA) was used for the compound separation. In addition, the specific parameters including the mobile phases, injection volume, elution program, column oven temperature, and mass spectrometer, etc. referred to previous study (Wang et al., 2024).

Quantification of monosaccharides in samples

Since free monosaccharides do not absorb at specific UV wavelengths, they cannot be detected directly by HPLC. Pre-column derivatization was used to convert the monosaccharides into derivatives with UV-absorbing properties, thus facilitating HPLC detection. Derivatization of samples from Section 2.3 using PMP, a 750 μL aliquot of the extract (10 mg/mL) was concentrated using a freeze-dryer, then redissolved in 200 μL solvent to yield a final concentration of 37.5 mg/mL, before proceeding with derivatization. Furthermore, after derivatization of the extracts, the sample was filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane to HPLC analysis.

In order to determine the content of free monosaccharides in the sample after derivatization, the analysis was performed using an Agilent 1260 HPLC system (Agilent Technologies, Palo Alto, CA, USA). The phases were water (contained phosphate and triethylamine, phase A) and acetonitrile (phase B). An Agilent C18 column (250 mm × 4.6 mm, 5 μm, Agilent Technologies, Palo Alto, CA, USA) was used for the compound separation. The specific parameters have been reported in our team's previous studies (Long et al., 2024).

Determination of titanium (Ti) using ICP-MS

According to Section 2.2, two groups of white tea extracts with equal volumes were obtained. Subsequently, in accordance with Section 2.3, two groups of extracts were prepared (the control group: white tea extract; the experimental group: extract treated with T-60). After being freeze-dried separately, they were diluted to a constant volume of 25 mL. The Ti content was determined using an ICP-MS (Agilent 7800, Agilent Technologies, Palo Alto, CA, USA), with the specific parameters as follows: pump rate 20 r/min, nebulizer flow 1 L/min, auxiliary gas 1 L/min, sample flush time 40 s, and RF power 1550 W (Kanrar et al., 2023).

Data analysis

Catechins, free monosaccharides and free amino acids were quantified using established standard curves, and the data were subjected to one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and post hoc testing by Fisher's protected least significant differences procedure (P<0.05) using SPSS software (version 26.0). The peak area values of the metabolites were Z-score normalized and then performed for difference display (heatmap analysis).

Results and discussions

Crystal structural property and specific surface area of TiO₂ nanoparticles with different particle sizes

Nanoscale TiO2 exhibits a predominantly tetragonal crystal structure (anatase or rutile phase), with titanium (Ti) and oxygen (O) atoms forming alternating structural units (Bourikas et al., 2014; Dambournet, 2022). The material exposes multiple crystallographic surfaces, including {101}, {110}, {001}, {103}, and others. Rutile nanoparticles primarily expose the {110} surface, whereas anatase nanoparticles predominantly expose the {101} surface (Bourikas et al., 2014). On the anatase {101} surface, both 5-fold- (Ti5C) and 6-fold-coordinated Ti atoms (Ti6C) coexist with 2-fold- (O2C) and 3-fold-coordinated O atoms (O3C) (Bourikas et al., 2014; Liu et al., 2014). Due to its high surface area at the nanoscale, the physical properties of TiO2 are different from those of crystalline materials with the same volume (Kim et al., 2007). In the present study, the physical properties of TiO2 with five particle sizes were determined, including crystallographic information and specific surface area. As depicted in X-ray diffraction spectrum (Fig. 1), the main characteristic peaks of five TiO2 appeared nearly at 25.3°, 37.8°, 48.0°, 54.0°, corresponding to the crystal face indices of {101}, {004}, {200}, and {105}. Among these peaks, the {101} peak was the strongest, confirming that these TiO2 were in the anatase phase (Bourikas et al., 2014). Furthermore, the {101} peak intensities of T-2 and T-100 were higher than those of other TiO2, indicating a higher degree of crystallinity in these TiO2. In contrast, the {101} peak of T-60 exhibited the lowest intensity and was wider than the {101} peaks of other TiO2, suggesting that T-60 had smaller crystallite sizes.

XRD patterns of TiO2 nanoparticles with different particle sizes.

Scanning Electron Microscope (SEM) is a microscopic instrument that employs a focused electron beam to scan a sample surface point-by-point, generating images by detecting signals produced through electron-sample interactions (Rosas-Arellano et al., 2025). It clearly reveals the microtopography of the sample surface (such as particles, pores, etc.) with a resolution reaching the nanoscale. The SEM results of TiO2 nanoparticles with different particle sizes were shown in Fig. 2. It was evident that all nanoparticles exhibited a certain degree of agglomeration, this has been commonly observed in previous studies (Li et al., 2010; Rachmaniar et al., 2024). The particle shapes of T-25 and T-40 were relatively similar (Fig. 2A&B), both presenting a hollow, ring-like structure. In contrast, the surface of T-60 particles was the roughest with the most irregular morphology (Fig. 2C), which was consistent with the XRD results. In addition, as shown in Fig. 2D&E, the particles of T-100 and T-2 were significantly larger than the other three, with smooth and distinct shapes. From the above results, it was inferred that the adsorption properties of T-60 might differ greatly from those of other TiO2 nanoparticles.

SEM images of TiO2 nanoparticle with different particle sizes under 500 nm (a) and 300 nm scale bar (b).

Additionally, the specific surface area and pore diameter distribution of these TiO2 were determined through nitrogen adsorption and de-adsorption test coupled with BET (Brunauer-Emmett-Teller) and BJH (Barrett-Joiner-Halenda) models. As shown in Fig. 3, the adsorption curves of TiO2 samples showed an upward convexity in the relative pressure range of 0.1–0.5, followed by a downward concavity at higher relative pressures (0.6–1). These characteristics were consistent with the IV-type isotherm defined in the IUPAC classification standard, indicating that these TiO2 samples might possess microporous or mesoporous structures (Al-Ghouti & Da'ana, 2020). Interestingly, T-60 shows the surprising adsorption properties at low relative pressures, which also suggesting that T-60 had smaller and “useful” crystallite sizes. The BJH calculation results (Fig. 4) further demonstrated that these TiO2 samples possessed a number of micropores, as evidenced by their pore volume being significantly higher in the range of 1–20 nm. However, there is no peak in pore size distribution of T-25, which suggest that the non-adsorption micropore (≤1 nm) lower the average size. Moreover, T-60′ pore size distribution show heterogeneity, which is also unhelpful to the property of adsorption. On the contrary, the T-60′ peak of pore size distribution is sharp and good homogeneity. However, the pore volume of T-25 within the range of 1–20 nm was markedly higher than that of T-60, its specific surface area (83.75 m2/g) was significantly lower than that of T-60 (294.52 m2/g), which was consistent with the adsorption difference to tea polyphenols (Table 1).

Metabolite difference analysis of white tea extract after treating with TiO₂ nanoparticles by UPLC-ESI-Q/TOF-HRMS

To reveal the effects of TiO2 nanoparticles of varying particle sizes on the metabolites in white tea extract, a combined UPLC-ESI-Q/TOF-HRMS and heatmap analysis was applied. The identification of all metabolites (including retention time, ion fragments, and other information) was presented in Table S1. As shown in Fig. 5, the addition of T-25, T-40, T-60, T-100, and T-2 reduced the levels of metabolites in white tea extract to varying extents, as evidenced by the decrease in MS peak intensity. Heatmap analysis further indicated that a large amount of catechins (e.g., EC, EGC, and GC), theasinensins (e.g., theasinensin A/D), B-type procyanidins, hydrolysable tannins, and flavone glycosides (such as kaempferol-3-O-(p-coumaroyl)-glucoside) were clearly detected in white tea extract. However, the concentrations of these compounds obviously decreased after adding T-25, T-40, and T-60, likely due to the binding of their phenolic hydroxyl groups to Ti5C atoms (Kurepa et al., 2014). In contrast, T-100 and T-2 addition exhibited minimal effects on the concentrations of these compounds. Only B-type dimeric procyanidins (ECG-ECG with A-C ring coupling), digalloyl-HHDP-glucose, and trigalloyl-glucose-1 showed significant reductions at a 1:4 mass ratio of white tea powder to TiO₂ nanoparticles.

The base peak chromatograms (A) and heatmap (B) of white tea extract with and without adding TiO2 nanoparticles by UPLC-ESI-Q/TOF-HRMS.

Heatmap analysis (Fig. 5B) also revealed that T-60 exhibited significantly higher adsorption efficiency for white tea metabolites (e.g., catechins, theasinensins) than T-25 and T-40 across all tested mass ratios of white tea powder to TiO2 nanoparticles (1,1 to 1:4). Notably, T-25, T-40, and T-60 showed no adsorption capacity for theanine; instead, they increased its concentration in the extract. This phenomenon may be attributed to competitive adsorption, where TiO2 nanoparticles preferentially bond water molecules over theanine during treatment (Wang et al., 2017). These results indicated that TiO2 might exhibit limited adsorption capacity for low-molecular-weight compounds lacking phenolic hydroxyl groups, such as amino acids and free monosaccharides.

Major component quantification of white tea extract with and without adding TiO2 nanoparticles

Content variation in catechins, purine alkaloids, and gallic acid

The HPLC spectra of white tea extract before and after treating with TiO2 nanoparticles (T-25, T-40, T-60, T-100, and T-2) were shown in Fig. 6. At a mass ratio of 1:4 (white tea powder/TiO2), all nanoparticle treatments reduced the peak intensities of metabolites (e.g., catechins, purine alkaloids, and gallic acid), with T-60 exhibiting the most pronounced adsorption effect. As shown in Table 2, the concentrations of these compounds in white tea extracts with and without TiO2 nanoparticles were presented. EGCG was the primary catechin in white tea extract with the highest concentration of 332.60 ± 1.53 μg/mL, followed by ECG, EC, C, and EGC with the concentration of 250.48 ± 1.16, 77.60 ± 0.31, 55.41 ± 0.47, and 55.13 ± 0.14 μg/mL, respectively. The addition of TiO2 nanoparticles resulted in dose-dependent reductions in catechin levels, with T-25 treatment significantly decreasing EGCG to 132.91 ± 2.66 μg/mL (p < 0.05) and T-60 treatment reducing EGCG, ECG, and EC to 15.53 ± 0.01, 16.97 ± 0.22, and 9.39 ± 0.33 μg/mL, respectively, across all tested mass ratios (1:1–1:4, white tea powder/TiO2). Moreover, T-60 showed the highest adsorption efficiency for catechin compared to other TiO2 nanoparticles, with the adsorption rates of total catechins reaching 78.89 %, 86.41 %, and 88.55 % at mass ratios of 1:1, 1:2, and 1:4 (white tea powder/TiO2), respectively. Overall, the adsorption capacity of TiO2 nanoparticles was in the order of T-60 > T-25 > T-40 > T-100 ≈ T-2 (especially at higher mass ratios of TiO2 to white tea powder), which was consistent with their difference in specific surface area.

Notably, T-60 exhibited varying adsorption efficiencies for different catechins, with the following order of adsorption rates: EGC (100 %) = GC (100 %) > EGCG (95.32 %) > ECG (90.03 %) > EC (78.29 %) > C (38.54 %) > GCG (24.76 %) at the mass ratio of 1:2 (white tea powder/TiO2). This result aligned well with previous studies (Li et al., 2021), indicating that catechins containing a pyrogallol structure (with the exception of GCG) displayed enhanced binding affinity to T-60. This inference was further verified by the fact that the adsorption rate of GA increased from 35.76 % to 100 % as the mass ratio of T-60 to white tea powder increased, since GA contains a pyrogallol structure. Furthermore, the stereochemical configuration at C-2/C-3 positions of catechins significantly influenced its interaction with T-60, where 2R,3R-catechins exhibited stronger binding affinity than their 2R,3S- or 2S,3R-counterparts. However, this trend did not extend to EGC and GC, potentially due to their relatively low concentrations in white tea extracts.

Adsorption of free amino acids in tea by T-60 at various ratios

Based on the above findings, among other particle sizes of TiO2 nanoparticles, T-60 had the best adsorption effect on catechins in tea extracts, so T-60 was selected as the experimental material for subsequent investigations into its effects on amino acids and reducing sugars. In addition, amino acids not only serve as key Maillard reaction substrates, but also play an important role in the umami (a crucial flavor attribute in tea infusion), and L-Asp, L-Glu and L-Thea are recognized as the amino acids that give tea its umami (Kaneko et al., 2006; Liu et al., 2023). As depicted in Table 3, the concentrations of L-Asp, L-Glu and L-Thea slight increased when the mass ratios (white tea powder/T-60) shifted from 1:0 to 1:1 or 1:2, and this finding was consistent with the results of UPLC-ESI-Q/TOF-HRMS analysis. However, at a mass ratio (white tea powder/T-60) of 1:4, except for the concentration of L-Thea in the tea extract, which was lower than that without adding TiO2, the concentration of the other two amino acids (L-Asp and L-Glu) was slightly higher than that without adding TiO2. The results suggested that the binding affinity of these free amino acids is not only lower than that of catechins but also lower than that of water molecules in tea infusion. Overall, T-60 exhibited minimal impact on the binding rates of umami free amino acids in tea infusion, particularly at mass ratios (white tea powder/T-60) of 1:1 and 1:2. However, T-60 significantly reduced tea polyphenol levels when the mass ratio was adjusted to 1:2. For instance, the total catechin concentration decreased by 86.41 % under these conditions (Table S2). This difference indicated that T-60 could selectively remove tea polyphenols from tea infusion, while it does not adsorb these umami free amino acids.

Adsorption of free monosaccharides in tea by T-60 at various ratios

As depicted in Fig. 7, the concentrations of L-Ara, L-Fuc, D-Gal, D-GalA, L-Rha, and D-Xyl exhibited a consistent trend: a slight increase as the mass ratio shifted from 1:0 to 1:1, followed by a significant decline when the mass ratio further increased to 1:2–1:4. Despite their structural hydroxyl groups, these monosaccharides showed lower binding affinities to T-60 compared with polyphenols and water molecules. However, T-60 might have limited capacity for binding to water molecules, a conclusion further supported by the reduction in the concentrations of these monosaccharides and most free amino acids as the T-60 dosage increased. In contrast, the concentrations of D-Glc, D-GlcA, and D-Man showed a continuous decrease as the T-60 dosage increased, indicating that their binding affinities to T-60 were higher compared with those to water molecules. At a mass ratio of 1:2, their binding rates ranged from 41.34 % to 66.33 %, remaining lower than those of most catechins (78.29–100 %), which suggested weaker binding affinities. These findings thus indicated that T-60 could selectively remove tea polyphenols from tea infusion, thereby enriching free amino acids and monosaccharides.

Determination of Ti element content in white tea extract with and without adding T-60

TiO2, a common food additive in China, is widely used to improve the color of foods (such as cakes, biscuits, and jellies, etc.) (Boutillier et al., 2022; Cao et al., 2024). According to GB 2760–2024 (National Standard for the Use of Food Additives), the maximum usage limit of TiO2 in different foods ranges from 0.2 to 10 g/kg. The Ti content in the tea extract was determined at the optimal TiO2 addition ratio (white tea powder: 60 nm TiO2 = 1:2, w/w). As shown in Table 4, it was found that after adding TiO2 nanoparticles, the Ti concentration in the tea extract increased by 1430.26 ng/mL, it was still within the addition range specified in the national standard, indicating a reasonable addition. Additionally, studies have shown that no toxic effects of TiO2 on human reproduction and development were observed even when the daily intake of TiO2 reached 1000 mg per kilogram of body weight (bw) (Liang et al., 2024; Younes et al., 2021).

Conclusion

To study the adsorption effect of TiO2 nanoparticles with different particle sizes on metabolites in white tea extract, we firstly determined the physical characterization of this TiO2, in addition, UPLC-ESI-Q/TOF-HRMS, HPLC-QqQ-MS, UHPLC and HPLC were used to detect the changes in metabolite concentrations in white tea extract before and after TiO2 nanoparticles adsorption.

It was hypothesized from the XRD and BET results that 60 nm TiO2 had the best adsorption effect for three main reasons: 1) It had the widest XRD peaks, which indicated that the crystals had greater uncertainty in the atomic positions, lack of ordering, and poorer crystallinity. 2) It displayed amazing adsorption properties at low relative pressures, suggesting that it had smaller crystallite sizes. 3) In addition, the particle size of the TiO2 also had the largest specific surface area.

As expected, 60 nm TiO2 nanoparticles showed excellent adsorption effect on polyphenols in white tea extract, in which the adsorption rate of total catechins was as high as 88.55 %. In addition, TiO2 nanoparticles of this particle size showed insignificant adsorption effects on free amino acids and free monosaccharides, and the results indicated that catechins in tea better adsorbed by TiO2 nanoparticles than free amino acids and free monosaccharides.

Finally, from our results that the best adsorption effect was achieved when the mass ratio of 60 nm TiO2 to tea powder was 1:2. In addition, we conducted elemental analysis on the tea extract treated at this ratio using ICP-MS, and the Ti content in the tea extract treated with TiO2 nanoparticles remained within the allowable addition range specified by the national standard. This ratio removed most of the catechins in the white tea extract, while most of the free amino acids and free monosaccharides were retained in it, of which umami amino acids were determined to be almost not adsorbed. So, it was hypothesized that the bitter and astringent taste of tea infusion after adsorption of this TiO2 was low and the umami-flavored attributes were revealed.

Therefore, subsequent research can continue to focus on this property to reduce the bitter and astringent flavor in tea infusion. This is expected to improve the utilization rate of summer and autumn tea, thereby providing a theoretical basis for enhancing the quality of summer and autumn tea and tea beverages. In addition, since both free amino acids and free monosaccharides are important substrates for the Maillard reaction, the effect of polyphenols in tea on its Maillard reaction can be removed by this method, so that the reaction mechanism between these two types of substrates can be explored in a real tea system.
