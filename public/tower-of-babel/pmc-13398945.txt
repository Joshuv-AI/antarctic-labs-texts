Research Progress on Upper Airway Cough Syndrome
Authors: Wan‐Ting Huang, Jia Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTUpper airway cough syndrome (UACS) has been identified as a significant contributor to chronic cough in various national guidelines. However, the complexity of its pathogenesis, the lack of clarity surrounding its diagnostic criteria, and its overlap with other etiologies of chronic cough present considerable challenges in both diagnosis and treatment. The utilization of nasal and laryngeal endoscopy is crucial in the diagnostic process, and the implementation of a multidisciplinary collaborative approach can markedly enhance diagnostic accuracy and treatment outcomes. This article provides a comprehensive review of the clinical characteristics, diagnostic and therapeutic approaches, and pathophysiological mechanisms associated with UACS, aiming to equip clinicians with a deeper understanding for improved management of this condition.Keywords: chronic cough, clinical features, diagnose, physiopathologic mechanism, UACS

Introduction

Chronic cough (CC) is defined in adults as a cough that persists for more than 8 weeks, without significant abnormalities detected during chest physical examinations or X‐rays, and its global prevalence is 9.6% [1]. One of the prevalent etiologies of CC is upper airway cough syndrome (UACS), which is characterized by the retrograde movement of secretions from upper respiratory tract conditions into the postnasal region and throat, with the potential for these secretions to reflux into the glottis or trachea, thereby inducing coughing. A considerable proportion of UACS are linked to nasal pathologies, such as allergic rhinitis and chronic sinusitis, which manifest clinically with cough as the predominant symptom [1]. While guidelines from various countries have recognized UACS as a primary contributor to CC, accounting for 18%–41% of adult cases [1, 2, 3, 4], the complexity of its pathogenesis, the lack of clarity in diagnostic criteria, and its overlap with other CC etiologies continue to pose significant challenges at the intersection of respiratory medicine and otolaryngology.

The purpose of this review is to synthesize the clinical characteristics, diagnostic approaches, therapeutic strategies, and underlying pathophysiological mechanisms associated with UACS.

Clinical Features

The American College of Chest Physicians (ACCP) indicates that the symptoms and signs associated with UACS are nonspecific, making it impossible to establish a definitive diagnosis based solely on patient history and physical examination [4]. The Chinese Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Management of Cough delineate the following criteria for diagnosing UACS [1]: (1) a CC that predominantly occurs during the day or following postural changes, with reduced coughing during sleep; (2) clinical manifestations and a medical history indicative of nasal and/or throat disorders; (3) corroborative diagnostic tests for nasal and/or throat conditions; and (4) a noticeable improvement in cough following treatment aimed at the underlying condition. Given that UACS can be further classified into allergic rhinitis, nonallergic rhinitis, chronic sinusitis, and other less common disorders, each requiring distinct treatment approaches, it is advisable to consult pertinent otolaryngology guidelines for more precise diagnostic evaluation when feasible [5, 6, 7].

According to the cough management guidelines established by the Japanese Respiratory Society, sinobronchial syndrome (SBS), instead of UACS, is identified as the predominant cause of CC within the country [8]. SBS is defined by a persistent cough resulting from chronic sinusitis, with key diagnostic criteria that include: (1) a productive cough lasting more than 8 weeks without accompanying wheezing; (2) symptoms indicative of postnasal drip and throat clearing, along with the presence of mucoid or mucopurulent secretions in the nasopharynx and cobblestone‐like alterations in the mucosal surface; (3) imaging studies, such as sinus X‐rays or CT scans, revealing fluid retention or mucosal thickening; (4) an elevation of neutrophils, excluding eosinophils, in nasal secretions; and (5) a notable improvement in cough following treatment with macrolide antibiotics. There is no consensus regarding the classification of SBS. Outside of Japan, SBS is not explicitly recognized in CC management guidelines [9]. In many other countries, CC attributed to chronic sinusitis is often regarded as analogous to CC resulting from rhinitis, and is thus categorized under UACS [1, 2, 4].

The European Respiratory Society (ERS) has introduced the concept of cough hypersensitivity syndrome (CHS) [10], positing that a significant proportion of patients with CC can be categorized within this syndrome [11]. Patients diagnosed with CHS typically exhibit one of three distinct phenotypes: (1) a nasal symptom‐dominant phenotype, such as UACS; (2) a Th2 cell‐dominant phenotype, which includes conditions like cough‐variant asthma or non‐asthmatic eosinophilic bronchitis; and (3) a phenotype characterized by symptoms of acid reflux and heartburn, commonly referred to as gastroesophageal reflux cough. Cough hypersensitivity is characterized by a diminished threshold for stimulus intensity necessary to provoke a cough or an exaggerated cough response to a consistent level of stimulus intensity. This heightened sensitivity of sensory nerves enables even minimal stimuli to elicit a cough, categorizing the condition as sensory neuropathy [12]. Research has indicated that the pathological alterations associated with cough hypersensitivity are linked to the upregulation of TRPV1 expression in sensory nerves, and the application of TRPV1 antagonists has been shown to effectively alleviate symptoms of CHS and diminish cough sensitivity [13, 14].

Diagnostic and Treatment Strategies

The Chinese cough guidelines advocate for the use of sinus CT as the preferred initial diagnostic modality for suspected cases of chronic rhinosinusitis (CRS) [1]. Conversely, the chronic sinusitis guideline frequently prioritize nasal endoscopy as the primary diagnostic approach [5], indicating that sinus CT should not be solely relied upon for the diagnosis of CRS. Sinus CT is recognized as an optimal imaging technique for evaluating nasal and sinus pathologies due to its high sensitivity and its ability to provide clear visualization of sinus anatomy [15]. This imaging modality facilitates a noninvasive evaluation of the maxillary sinus ostium and the deeper nasal sinus ostia [16]. Both nasal endoscopy and sinus CT demonstrate comparable efficacy in diagnosing chronic sinusitis; nasal endoscopy offers advantages in terms of cost‐effectiveness and reduced radiation exposure [17]. Also, it can identify areas that may be overlooked by CT or anterior rhinoscopy [18]. This procedure can be conducted under local anesthesia, thereby enabling prompt assessment and early diagnosis of the condition [15]. Scholars in Australia have posited that endoscopy is instrumental in the evaluation of cough [19], as it permits direct visualization of the nasal cavity, thereby enhancing the diagnostic accuracy for UACS. Laryngoscopy can also yield direct evidence of gastroesophageal reflux or laryngopharyngeal reflux. In conclusion, we advocate for the prioritization of nasal and laryngeal endoscopy in the diagnostic process for CC.

A multidisciplinary team comprising specialists from the respiratory, otolaryngology, and gastroenterology departments has been identified as an effective approach for the rapid diagnosis of patients with refractory chronic cough (RCC), achieving a diagnostic rate of 88.7% and demonstrating efficacy within approximately 2 weeks [20]. This collaborative model addresses the majority of etiologies associated with CC. During the consultation process, nasal and laryngeal endoscopy are employed to assess patients, facilitating accurate diagnoses while simultaneously minimizing costs and reducing the duration of examinations. Consequently, we advocate for the promotion of this integrated clinic model as a practical solution.

The primary therapeutic approaches for UACS exhibit a degree of uniformity across various nations, predominantly involving the use of antihistamines [9]. While official treatment guidelines for UACS display slight variations among countries, domestic protocols generally align closely with otolaryngological standards, albeit with some minor discrepancies [1, 5, 6, 7]. The treatment recommendations for allergic rhinitis and chronic sinusitis outlined in the Chinese cough guidelines align closely with those established in otolaryngology guidelines [1, 7]. According to the 2021 Chinese cough guidelines, first‐generation antihistamines and nasal decongestants are recommended for the treatment of nonallergic rhinitis [1]. However, the otolaryngological literature delineates nonallergic rhinitis into several distinct subtypes [6], each necessitating tailored treatment strategies. For instance, in cases of vasomotor rhinitis, nasal corticosteroids are favored for alleviating nasal congestion, whereas anticholinergic agents are preferred for managing rhinorrhea. In instances where both nasal congestion and rhinorrhea are present, antihistamines are advised. In the context of nonallergic rhinitis characterized by eosinophilia, nasal corticosteroids are identified as the first‐line therapeutic option.

Furthermore, as the understanding of cough mechanisms advances, clinical trials targeting specific cough pathways are currently in progress, including the investigation of TRPV1 antagonists and TRPV4 antagonists [21, 22]. P2X3 antagonists, such as gefapixant, sivopixant, and camlipixant, have demonstrated significant efficacy in patients with RCC [23, 24, 25]. Topical lidocaine has been utilized to manage coughing during bronchoscopy and is occasionally employed in clinical practice for RCC [26]. Additionally, certain traditional Chinese medicines have been explored for their potential in treating UACS [27].

Pathophysiological Mechanisms

There is substantial overlap between the pathophysiology of CC due to UACS and that of CHS. The three distinct phenotypes of CHS ultimately converge along similar terminal pathways, with the following key mechanism:

1.Activation of sensory nerves in the airwaysSensory nerve endings in the airways express a variety of transient receptor potential (TRP) channels—including TRPV1, TRPA1, TRPV4, and TRPM8—which respond to stimuli such as acid, heat, and osmotic changes [10, 14, 28, 29, 30, 31]. The heterogeneity in cough sensitivity can be explained by anatomical variations in the neural pathways mediating the cough reflex. An increased density of sensory nerves in the airway epithelium has been proposed as a pathological mechanism underlying cough hypersensitivity [10]. Considering that distinct cough receptors respond to specific types of stimuli, it is hypothesized that different phenotypes of cough hypersensitivity may involve particular patterns of airway epithelial neural remodeling [32].These phenotypic alterations in peripheral sensory neurons may, in turn, trigger inflammatory responses from immune cells—a process referred to as neurogenic inflammation. Released neuropeptides, such as substance P (SP) and calcitonin gene‐related peptide (CGRP), can induce local vasodilation [23], chemotaxis [33], immune cell activation, and promote type 2 helper T‐cell polarization [34]. This neuro‐immune interaction, particularly in the context of repeated sensory input (e.g., from viruses or allergens), can lead to a self‐perpetuating cycle of hypersensitivity [35].

2.Central sensitization of cough

Central sensitization of cough

Functional brain imaging studies have revealed altered central neuroprocessing in the cough regulatory network among patients with CC [36]. Laryngeal irritation following inhalation of tussive agents—often perceived as an urge to cough—is generally accompanied by activation across multiple brain regions, both in individuals with CC and in healthy controls. However, significant differences have been observed in the activation of three midbrain regions—the cuneiform nucleus, periaqueductal gray, and dorsal raphe—with markedly enhanced signals in patients with cough hypersensitivity, a phenomenon not seen in controls [37]. Another notable finding is the significantly reduced activation in brain regions associated with cough suppression, such as the dorsomedial prefrontal cortex and anterior midcingulate cortex, among cough hypersensitivity patients [37, 38].

Neural Reflex Activation

Sensitization of Nasal Afferent Nerves

Nasal afferent nerves are characterized by the expression of various ion channels that are capable of detecting a range of stimuli. The trigeminal afferent nerve endings that innervate the nasal mucosa are often designated as “gatekeepers,” as they serve as the initial detectors of substances entering the upper respiratory tract [39]. While direct stimulation of the nasal mucosa does not elicit a cough [40], it is evident that the cough reflex is influenced by distinct groups of nasal afferent nerves [41]. One prevailing hypothesis posits that the activation of nasal afferents may enhance the cough reflex as a protective mechanism to limit the dissemination of pathological processes from the nasal cavity to other regions of the respiratory tract [42]. The trigeminal afferent pathway has the capacity to facilitate coughing by reducing the threshold of the central cough pattern generator in response to vagal afferent input, thereby enabling even benign or physiological stimuli from the lower airways to provoke a cough [40]. Given that the trigeminal and vagal sensory nerves responsible for triggering coughing terminate in disparate regions of the brainstem, the sensitization of the trigeminal nasal sensory pathway in relation to coughing may involve more intricate mechanisms than those associated with vagally mediated sensitization [43]. Although primary sensory fibers originating from the nasal cavity predominantly synapse with second‐order neurons in the trigeminal sensory nucleus, these afferent signals are also linked to chemosensitive areas, such as the posterior brain area and the nucleus of the solitary tract (nTS), which may disrupt the regulatory input to the “cough generator.” [42] Furthermore, stimulation of the nasal trigeminal afferents has been shown to induce c‐fos expression in the nTS, suggesting a potential role for upper airway neurogenic inflammation in the central sensitization of the cough reflex [44].

Laryngeal Nerve Hyperresponsiveness

Patients with UACS, individuals with non‐coughing rhinitis or sinusitis, and healthy controls all exhibited a decrease in cough sensitivity following the application of topical anesthesia to the laryngeal nerve endings using lidocaine, and the extent of this reduction was consistent across groups [45]. These findings imply that although laryngeal cough receptors may play a role in the heightened cough sensitivity observed in UACS patients, the local sensitivity does not significantly exceed that of the other groups and is not the primary factor driving the cough reflex. Rather, it is posited that the activation of cough receptors located in the lower airways is the predominant contributor to the increased cough sensitivity in UACS patients [9].

The notion of laryngeal hyperresponsiveness (LHR) was introduced in the 1990s [46], describing a protective reflex in humans known as the laryngeal chemoreflex, which serves to prevent the aspiration of liquid substances into the lower airways. A positive diagnosis of this condition is indicated by a reduction of more than 25% in the maximum inspiratory flow rate following histamine inhalation. Research indicates that the prevalence of LHR among UACS patients is 76%, which is notably higher than that observed in non‐coughing patients [47]. These individuals exhibit heightened sensitivity in the pharyngeal and laryngeal regions, rendering them more susceptible to coughing in response to various stimuli.

The Pivotal Role of TRP Channels

Cough reflex is modulated by the vagus nerve and sensory afferent nerves that innervate the airways. The TRP family comprises a diverse array of ion channel proteins, several of which are localized to sensory nerve endings in the airways. The activation of these channels can trigger coughing [28].

TRPV1 is a calcium‐permeable, nonselective ion channel that functions as a sensor for noxious heat, becoming activated at temperatures exceeding 42°C. It can also be stimulated by a variety of exogenous and endogenous ligands, including capsaicin, low pH, resiniferatoxin, and endogenous mediators such as anandamide and hydroperoxyeicosatetraenoic acid [48]. In the airways, TRPV1 expression has been documented in vagal ganglia and jugular ganglion neurons [49], as well as in dorsal root and trigeminal ganglia, in addition to various non‐neuronal cells [50]. The activation of TRPV1 by ligands like capsaicin can elicit coughing and stimulate the vagus nerve [51, 52], primarily activating C fibers, although it has also been shown to activate Aδ fibers, which are more responsive to mechanical stimuli, potentially as an indirect consequence of bronchospasm [53].

TRPA1 is predominantly expressed in neuronal tissues, including nasal trigeminal nerves, vagal airway neurons, and spinal dorsal root ganglia [49, 54], with a primary localization on C fibers [55]. It can be activated by products of oxidative stress (e.g., reactive oxygen species [ROS]) [56], bacterial endotoxins (LPS) [57], and inflammatory mediators (e.g., prostaglandin E2 [PGE2] and bradykinin) [29], as well as through the activation of protease‐activated receptor 2 (PAR2) [58]. In contrast to TRPV1, TRPA1 has been shown to activate only C fibers [55], inducing coughing in both animal models and humans, without involvement of Aδ fibers [53, 55].

TRPV4 is a calcium‐permeable channel that is activated by moderate temperatures (> 24°C), osmotic changes, and derivatives of arachidonic acid [59, 60, 61]. It is primarily expressed in dorsal root ganglia and pulmonary sensory neurons [62, 63], with lesser expression in vagal and jugular ganglia, suggesting that its action on sensory neurons may occur through indirect mechanisms [31]. The activation of TRPV4 leads to the release of ATP, which subsequently acts on P2X3 receptors, resulting in calcium influx, vagal depolarization, and the discharge of Aδ fibers, culminating in coughing [31].

TRPM8 functions as a cold temperature sensor, becoming activated at lower temperatures (15°C–28°C) [64]. It is expressed in sensory neurons of the dorsal root and trigeminal ganglia [65], particularly within nasal trigeminal and jugular neurons [30, 66]. Menthol activates TRPM8 receptors, exhibiting antitussive properties, likely through a reflex mechanism involving nasal trigeminal afferents, although non‐TRPM8 pathways may also contribute [30]. Further investigation and data are warranted to elucidate these mechanisms.

Airway Inflammation Interaction

The Driving Role of Lower Airway Inflammation

Research indicates that airway inflammation in patients with CC who do not have asthma, including those with UACS, is primarily attributed to the infiltration of mast cells, neutrophils, and lymphocytes. This differs from the underlying causes associated with cough‐variant asthma and eosinophilic bronchitis [67]. Most national guidelines categorize UACS as CC resulting from rhinitis and chronic sinusitis; however, the underlying mechanisms of CC associated with these two conditions may differ. UACS patients with rhinitis demonstrate significantly heightened cough sensitivity compared to healthy controls, alongside elevated percentages of eosinophils in induced sputum and increased levels of CGRP, histamine, and PGE2 in supernatants [68]. This suggests a potential link between UACS due to rhinitis and mast cell activation [69]. Conversely, in patients with chronic sinusitis who present with cough, induced sputum reveals an increase in inflammatory cells, predominantly neutrophils [67, 70].

Neutrophils are recognized as significant contributors to the pathophysiology of CC; however, the precise mechanisms remain inadequately elucidated [71]. Potential functions of neutrophils in CC include the production of ROS, neutrophil extracellular traps, and various cytokines and chemokines (such as IL‐8 and TNF‐α), which may exacerbate airway inflammation and heighten cough reflex sensitivity [72]. Additionally, neutrophils may release proteases (e.g., NE) and cytokines that can lead to structural alterations in the airway, including thickening of the basement membrane, vascular proliferation, and hyperplasia of goblet cells [73]. Furthermore, the release of ROS, PGE2, ATP, and other mediators can activate TRPA1 and TRPV1 channels on sensory nerve fibers, thereby increasing cough reflex sensitivity [74, 75, 76]. Neuropeptides, such as SP, released by neutrophils, may also activate sensory nerve fibers, contributing to neurogenic inflammation and exacerbating cough [77]. The role of the airway microbiome in CC remains to be fully understood; however, some studies suggest a correlation between elevated neutrophil levels and the presence of specific bacteria, such as Hemophilus influenzae [77].

The Synergistic Effect of Upper Airway Inflammation

Research has demonstrated that levels of SP and CGRP in nasal secretions are significantly elevated in patients with CC compared to those without CC [78]. It is posited that stimulation of cough receptors by inflammatory mediators released from lower airway neurons activates neurogenic reflexes in response to inflammation of the nasal mucosa, thereby triggering coughing [70]. Furthermore, some studies indicate an increased number of neurons capable of producing neurogenic inflammatory mediators in the nasal mucosa of individuals with rhinitis [79]. Airway inflammation may manifest locally as a component of systemic inflammation [80], such as in rhinitis patients. The question of whether inflammation serves as a causative factor or a consequence of CC warrants further investigation in future studies.

Mucociliary Clearance Dysfunction

CC has historically been attributed to mechanical or chemical stimulation of afferent nerves innervating the pharynx, larynx, or lower airways, primarily due to postnasal drip [1, 4]. However, Current investigations indicate a lack of a definitive causal relationship between postnasal drip and coughing [81]. The mechanisms by which postnasal drip induces coughing may be more intricate than previously thought. The normal function of sinus mucociliary clearance serves as a critical host defense mechanism that eliminates pathogens, including bacteria, dust, and aerosols, that are inhaled into the upper respiratory tract [82]. In patients with chronic rhinitis, increased mucus viscosity can activate mechanoreceptors, while inflammatory mediators can stimulate chemoreceptors, both of which may lead to coughing [83]. Prolonged exposure to inflammatory cytokines, such as TNF‐α, may also disrupt normal ciliary function [84], resulting in increased mucus retention and local immune dysfunction, with these alterations being prevalent in both the upper and lower airways.

Conclutions

The pathophysiological mechanism underlying CC in UACS is characterized by a complex interplay within a “neural‐inflammatory‐mucus” triad. Despite advancements in diagnostic and therapeutic approaches facilitated by multidisciplinary collaboration, significant challenges persist in clinical practice due to the heterogeneity of underlying mechanisms and the lack of clarity in diagnostic criteria. We hope to develop a precise, stratified diagnostic and treatment framework informed by the expression profiles of TRP channels and the diversity of inflammatory cell populations. The ongoing evolution of artificial intelligence technologies presents opportunities to create intelligent diagnostic models that enhance the differentiation of UACS from other etiological factors. In conclusion, research on UACS must extend beyond conventional disciplinary confines, fostering an integration of fundamental research and clinical application, and the approach aspires to transition from “empirical treatment” to “precision medicine,” ultimately enhancing the long‐term prognosis and quality of life for patients suffering from CC.
