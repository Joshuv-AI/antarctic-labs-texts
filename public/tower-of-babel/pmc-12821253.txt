Deciphering the gonadal cell atlas of Monopterus albus and cell fate during sex reversal based on single-cell RNA sequencing
Authors: Lingbo Yi, Fuyun Zhu, Lun Liu, Runlong Zhao, Yanhong Luo, Shuqing Liang, Qingtao Yang, Jihua Ye, Haibo Yang, Li Wang, Yu Hou, Xueya Zhao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe Monopterus albus serves as a unique model for studying sex reversal, transitioning naturally from female to male. However, the origins of male germline stem cells (GSCs) and the roles of somatic cells during sex reversal remain poorly understood.MethodsWe performed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) on ovarian, ovotestis, and testicular tissues to construct a gonadal cell atlas. RNA fluorescence in situ hybridization (RNA-FISH) validated cell subpopulations.ResultsCell types of germ cells and somatic cells in gonads were identified, and their differentiation trajectories during sex reversal were depicted. Our results show that GSCswdr17+tep1− in the ovaries possess bipotential differentiation capacity and can transform into GSCswdr17+ tep1+ with the capability to differentiating into sperm during sex reversal. Two theca cell subpopulations cooperate to synthesize steroid precursors in ovaries, with Leydig cells in testes likely originating from theca cell transitions. Proliferative gdf9+ follicle cells promoted folliculogenesis and persisted in male gonads with reduced hsd17b1 expression. Stromal heterogen-eity analysis revealed bmp4+ mesenchymal stem cells (MSCsbmp4+) as potential precursors for Sertoli cells in testes.ConclusionsThis study provides a comprehensive cellular roadmap of gonadal cell dynamics during sex reversal in Monopterus albus. Our findings unveil the molecular mechanisms underlying germline and somatic cell fate determination, offering novel insights into vertebrate sex reversal and potential therapeutic strategies for disorders of sex development (DSD).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13287-025-04810-8.

Background

Organisms that reproduce sexually exhibit two sex types, female and male. In mammals, the sex chromosomes in the fertilized egg determine whether the individual will develop as female or male throughout its entire life cycle. However, in teleosts, mechanisms of sex determination are highly diverse. The ricefield eel (Monopterus albus), a novel model species for studies in evolution, genetics, and development, begins life as female and transitions through an intersex stage to become male. This unique reproductive strategy provides an ideal model for investigating mechanisms of sex determination [1, 2]. The mechanisms of sex determination represent a fundamental question in biology, as they impact not only individual reproductive health but also play crucial roles in evolution and development. A deeper understanding of the sex reversal process in the ricefield eel can help unveil the molecular mechanisms underlying sex determination and provide new therapeutic insights for human DSD.

Sex determination in higher animals is a complex process in which somatic cells and germ cells of the gonads determine the sexual status through the activation of a series of genes and the mutual communication between these cell types. Germ cells exhibit germ cell-specific molecular characteristics even before they migrate to the gonads during early embryonic development. However, at this stage, they do not yet display sexually differentiated features and are referred to as primordial germ cells (PGCs) [3]. The correct migration, positioning, and differentiation of PGCs are crucial for sex determination and gonadal development. In non-sex-reversing species, sex determination occurs after PGCs migrate to the gonadal region, where they respond to signals from somatic cells to undergo sex-specific differentiation. This process enables them to develop into oocytes in females and spermatozoa in males [4, 5]. Through the observation of tissue sections using immunohistochemistry for the primordial germ cell marker gene vasa, researchers have suggested that GSCs in the ovaries and testes of the ricefield eel may constitute two distinct cell populations [4, 6]. Additionally, it has been proposed that primordial GSCs might exist among the somatic cells of the ricefield eel ovaries [7]. Recent advances in scRNA-seq technology have provided more robust evidence for the presence of PGC subpopulations with bipotential differentiation capacity in the ricefield eel gonads. Using this technology, the research team identified subpopulations of germline cells with bipotential differentiation capacity in the intersex gonads of the ricefield eel [8]. However, due to the lack of gonadal samples spanning the life span, the early origin of male GSCs remains unresolved.

Somatic cells that constitute the testis or ovary play a pivotal role in sex determination. In mammals, theca cells synthesize androgens in response to luteinizing hormone (LH) stimulation, which are then converted into estrogens by granulosa cells. Leydig cells in the testis serve as the primary source of androgens, and share functional similarities with theca cells in hormone synthesis, as observed in zebrafish [9]. Upon activation, sex determining region of Y (SRY) induces the expression of SRY-box transcription factor 9 (SOX9), which further promotes the formation of Leydig and Sertoli cells, ultimately leading to testis development. Conversely, in the absence of SRY, the ovarian developmental pathway is activated, which relies on genes such as WNT family member 4 (WNT4) and R-spondin 1 (RSPO1) being crucial for normal ovarian development [10, 11]. Beyond the regulation of these core sex-determining genes, epigenetic modifications may serve as a key regulatory mechanism in animals lacking sex chromosomes. The epigenetic state of sex-determining genes is essential for initiating ovary- or testis-specific pathways [12, 13]. In recent years, advancements in omics technologies have enabled researchers to explore the molecular mechanisms of sex reversal in ricefield eel at a systems level. Milestone achievements include whole-genome sequencing and assembly, significantly enhancing our understanding of sex reversal mechanisms at the omics level [14]. In ricefield eel, the application of transcriptomics, methylomics, and modified proteomics has elucidated the relationship between epigenetic regulation and sex reversal [15–18]. The tissue transition from ovary to testis in sex-reversing animals involves functional changes and shifts in molecular expression patterns across various somatic cells. However, current research has yet to report the specific differentiation events of different types of somatic cells during the sex reversal process.

scRNA-seq technology offers unique advantages in defining cell identity and elucidating cell differentiation and development processes. In this study, we used scRNA-seq to characterize the complex cellular compositions of the ovary, ovotestis, and testis, and traced their cellular and molecular transitions during sex reversal. We validated these subpopulations using RNA-FISH to determine their distribution patterns within the gonads. Our findings provide insights into the fate transitions of germline and somatic cell lineages during sex reversal in ricefield eel, offering new perspectives on the mechanisms of sex determination and transformation in vertebrates.

Materials and methods

Sample collection

The ricefield eel were euthanized by decapitation and exsanguination. Gonadal tissues were dissected and the mid-section of the tissue was collected and temporarily stored in pre-chilled fish physiological saline at 4 °C.

Cryosectioning

Samples were embedded in OCT compound and sectioned using a cryostat. Sections were mounted on RNase-free slides and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) at 4 °C for 20 min. Post-fixation, sections were washed sequentially in 3x PBS (for several seconds), 3x PBS (5 min), 1x PBS (for several seconds), and 1x PBS (5 min). Dehydration was performed using a gradient ethanol series: 30%, 60%, 80%, 95%, and 100% ethanol for 2 min each. After drying, sections were stored at -80 °C. All solutions used during sample processing were prepared with DEPC-treated water and reagents.

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining

Samples were embedded in OCT compound and sectioned as described above. Sections were mounted on RNase-free slides and fixed in 4% PFA at 4 °C for 20 min, followed by two washes in PBS for 5 min each. Slides were stained with hematoxylin for 5 min, rinsed under running water for 1–3 min, differentiated in 1% hydrochloric acid alcohol for 2–3 s, then rinsed again for 10 min. Counterstaining was performed with eosin for 3 min. Dehydration was achieved using a gradient ethanol series as described previously. Sections were dried at room temperature and mounted with neutral resin. Sex identification was performed based on morphological criteria under an optical microscope.

Gene reannotation of ricefield eel

CDS sequences were downloaded from Gigadb. These sequences were aligned against the NR database using BLAST, with an e-value threshold of < 0.00001 to obtain corresponding gene symbols. Symbols for orthologous genes in teleosts were kept lowercase, while those for other species were uppercase. To ensure comprehensive annotation, additional information was obtained from multiple databases (Gigadb, NCBI, Ensembl). If multiple CDS sequences corresponded to the same gene symbol, they were distinguished as symbol–1, symbol–2, etc.

Preparation of single-cell suspensions

Approximately 100 mg of fresh gonadal tissue was collected per sample. Tissues were washed with ice-cold PBS to remove mature oocytes and free sperm cells. Under sterile conditions, tissues were minced and digested with 1 mg/mL collagenase IV (dissolved in serum-free RPMI 1640) at 37 °C for 20 min, with gentle mixing every 5 minutes. Digestion was stopped by adding RPMI 1640 containing 10% FBS. The cell suspension was filtered through a 40 μm cell strainer, and cell concentration was determined under a microscope. Cell viability was assessed using trypan blue staining, requiring a viability greater than 80% and a minimum concentration of 1 × 105 cells/µL.

Single cell RNA sequencing library construction and sequencing

scRNA-seq library construction and sequencing using the 10X genomics platform

The scRNA-seq data presented in this study were generated using two distinct sequencing platforms. The data from the five samples in the main figures (Dataset_1) were obtained using the 10X Genomics platform. For the supplementary validation dataset (Dataset_2), female and male samples were sequenced on the SeekOne platform, while the intersex sample was sourced from a publicly available dataset (GEO Accession Number: GSE153961) generated using the 10X Genomics platform.

The scRNA-seq libraries for the 10X Genomics platform were constructed using the 10X Genomics Chromium Single Cell 3’ Reagent Kit (v3) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, a single-cell suspension with a viability of > 90% was loaded onto a Chromium microfluidic chip along with the Master Mix and Partitioning Oil. Single cells, barcoded Gel Beads, and reverse transcription (RT) reagents were co-encapsulated into nanoliter-scale Gel Beads-in-emulsion (GEMs) using the Chromium Controller. Within the GEMs, cells were lysed, and the released poly-adenylated mRNA transcripts were barcoded with cell-specific barcodes and unique molecular identifiers (UMIs) during the reverse transcription reaction. Following RT, the GEMs were broken, and the barcoded cDNA was purified and amplified via PCR. The amplified cDNA was then enzymatically fragmented, end-repaired, and ligated to Illumina adapters and sample index sequences to construct the final sequencing library. Library quality was assessed using an Agilent Bioanalyzer, and quantification was performed using qPCR. The libraries were sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq X™ Ten platform (Illumina, USA) with PE150 read length according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

scRNA-seq library construction and sequencing using the SeekOne platform

The scRNA-seq libraries for the SeekOne platform were prepared using SeekOne® Digital Droplet Single Cell 3’ library preparation kit (SeekGene, Catalog No. K00202). Briefly, a suspension containing an appropriate number of cells was mixed with reverse transcription reagents and loaded into the sample well of a SeekOne® Chip S3. Barcoded Hydrogel Beads (BHBs) and partitioning oil were then dispensed into their respective wells on the chip. After droplet emulsion generation, reverse transcription was carried out at 42 °C for 90 min, followed by enzyme inactivation at 85 °C for 5 min. The resulting cDNA was purified from broken droplets and amplified by PCR. The amplified cDNA was subsequently cleaned, fragmented, end-repaired, A-tailed, and ligated to Illumina sequencing adapters. An indexing PCR was then performed to enrich for DNA fragments derived from the 3′ polyadenylated region of transcripts, each containing a cell barcode and a UMI. Final libraries were purified using VAHTS DNA Clean Beads, quantified with a Qubit fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Q33226), and assessed for fragment size distribution using a Bio-Fragment Analyzer (Bioptic, Qsep400). Sequencing was performed on a GENEMIND SURFSeq 5000 platform with a paired-end 150 bp read configuration.

Quality control, alignment, annotation, and expression quantification of the raw sequencing data from single cell RNA sequencing platform

The processing pipeline for raw sequencing data generated from the 10X Genomics and SeekOne platforms is described as follows. Single-cell data from the 10x Genomics platform were analyzed using Cell Ranger software (v 3.0.2). Initially, cellranger mkfastq, which incorporates Illumina’s bcl2fastq, was used to demultiplex samples and convert raw base call files into sample-specific FASTQ files. Subsequently, for each sample, cellranger count was executed to perform the core analysis. This involved aligning sequencing reads to the reference genome using STAR, processing cell barcodes and UMIs with 10X’s proprietary algorithms, and performing gene quantification to generate a filtered feature-barcode matrix. Putative cells were distinguished from ambient RNA background using an internal cell-calling algorithm. Finally, to enable a combined analysis across multiple samples, the individual libraries were normalized and aggregated using Cell Ranger to create a unified gene expression matrix for downstream comparative analysis.

Single-cell data from the SeekOne platform were analyzed using SeekOne® Tools. The detailed procedure was as follows: Raw sequencing reads were first processed using fastp to remove adapter sequences and low-quality bases. The cleaned reads, containing cell barcodes and UMIs, were then aligned to the Monopterus albus reference genome using STAR. Gene expression quantification was performed with featureCounts, wherein reads sharing identical barcodes and UMIs were considered duplicates originating from the same molecule and were thus collapsed to generate an initial expression matrix. Finally, employing a strategy consistent with Cell Ranger and EmptyDrops, the initial matrix was filtered to remove background noise and obtain a high-confidence cell-by-gene expression matrix for all subsequent analyses.

Sequencing, quality control, alignment, annotation, and expression quantification of the bulk RNA sequencing data

For the cell line samples, we established three technical replicates per group for both the gene-edited and control groups, whereas for the animal tissue samples, we collected five biological replicates for each type of gonad. Total RNA was extracted using the TRIzol® reagent (Invitrogen, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA integrity was assessed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, USA), and only samples with an RNA Integrity Number (RIN) greater than 7.0 were used for subsequent library construction. Sequencing libraries were prepared with the NEBNext® Ultra™ RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina® (NEB, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The qualified libraries were sequenced on the GENEMIND SURFSeq 5000 platform in PE150 mode. Raw sequencing reads were processed to remove adapters and low-quality bases using fastp with default parameters. The clean reads were then aligned to the Monopterus albus reference genome using the Hisat2 (v2.2.1). Gene expression levels were estimated and quantified using featureCounts (v2.0.1) to generate the raw count matrix. Downstream analyses such as differential expression analysis were performed in R using the DESeq2 package.

Clustering analysis and cell type identification based on single-cell sequencing data

Batch effect correction was performed separately within dataset_1 and dataset_2 using Canonical Correlation Analysis (CCA). Clustering analysis was performed using Seurat R package. Cells and genes were filtered using the criteria nFeature_RNA > 200 & nFeature_RNA < 4000 & percent.mt < 25. Following quality control and normalization of the single-cell RNA-seq data, a principal component analysis (PCA) was performed on the scaled expression matrix of highly variable genes to capture the main sources of transcriptional variation. The top 20 PCAs were selected as input for subsequent graph-based clustering. UMAP was used for dimensionality reduction and visualization to distinguish cell clusters. Known molecular markers were used to identify cell types within each cluster.

Cell type annotation via reference mapping

We performed cell type annotation for the query dataset (Dataset_2) by projecting it onto a reference-based UMAP model. Specifically, using the MapQuery() function from the Seurat package, cells from Dataset_2 were projected into the pre-established UMAP embedding of the reference dataset (Dataset_1). This approach allowed for the transfer of cell type labels from the reference to the query dataset and the visualization of both datasets within a unified UMAP space.

Identification of cluster specific marker genes

Cluster-specific marker genes were identified using the FindAllMarkers() function in the Seurat package, with thresholds set at p_value < 0.05 and log2FC > 0.25.

Construction of pseudotime trajectories

Pseudotime trajectories were constructed using the Monocle 2 package (v2.24.0) to infer differentiation trajectories and identify pseudotime-specific genes.

Cell cycle scoring and stemness scoring

Cell cycle scoring was performed using the CellCycleScoring function in Seurat package to assign G2M and S phase scores. Stemness scoring was conducted using SCENT R package (v1.0.3).

GO functional analysis and KEGG pathway analysis

Gene ontology (GO) analysis and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment were performed using the clusterProfiler R package (v4.7.1.1).

Cell culture

The mouse spermatogonial cell line GC-1 and mouse embryonic fibroblast cell line C3H/10T1/2 were cultured in high-glucose DMEM medium (Biosharp, BL304A) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Vazyme, F103-01), 0.1 mg/mL streptomycin, and 100 IU/mL penicillin (Biosharp, BL505A). Cells were maintained as adherent cultures at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ incubator. Subculturing was performed when the cell density reached approximately 80% confluence to ensure adequate nutrient supply and sustain logarithmic growth.

Cell transfection

For overexpression of the Bmp4 gene in C3H/10T1/2 cells, transfection was performed as follows: Cells in the logarithmic growth phase were seeded at a density of 5 × 10⁵ cells per well and transfected with 2.5 µg of either pcDNA3.1-BMP4 or the negative control plasmid pcDNA-NC (Youbao Biological, Changsha, China) using Lipo8000™ transfection reagent (Beyotime, C0533). After 48 h of incubation at 37 °C, cells were harvested to assess overexpression efficiency. Each experimental group was analyzed with six technical replicates. For knockdown of Nr3c1 and Mllt1 genes in GC-1 cells, the following procedure was used: GC-1 cells in the logarithmic growth phase were plated at 5 × 10⁵ cells per well and transfected with 100 pmol of Nr3c1 siRNA, Mllt1 siRNA, or a non-targeting negative control siRNA (Genepharma, Shanghai, China) using Lipo8000™ reagent (Beyotime, C0533). Cells were collected after 48 h of transfection at 37 °C for subsequent analysis of knockdown efficiency. Each experimental group was analyzed with six technical replicates.

RNA in situ hybridization

Probes were synthesized by Wuhan Servicebio Technology Co., Ltd. Frozen sections were removed from − 80 °C storage and brought to room temperature. Sections were circled using an immunohistochemistry pen, and permeabilization solution (0.3% Triton X-100 in PBS) was applied and incubated for 15 min, followed by three washes in PBS for 5 min each. Pre-hybridization solution (200 µL) was applied and incubated at 40 °C for 30 min. After removing the pre-hybridization solution, hybridization solution containing probes (1:10 dilution) was added and incubated overnight at 40 °C within a humid chamber. Post-hybridization washes were performed using 4xSSC, 2xSSC, 1xSSC, and 0.5xSSC at 40 °C for 10 min each. Branching probe hybridization solution (1:10 dilution) was added for 45 min at 40 °C, followed by additional washes. Signal probe hybridization solution (1:200 dilution) was added for 3 h at 40 °C, followed by further washes. DAPI staining was performed for 8 min in the dark, followed by three PBS washes for 5 min each. Sections were mounted with anti-fade mounting medium and imaged using a Nikon upright fluorescence microscope. Permeabilization and washing solutions were prepared with DEPC-treated water and reagents.

Results

Construction of the gonadal cell atlas in ricefield eel

To enhance the robustness and generalizability of our findings, we generated two independent single-cell transcriptomic datasets (Dataset_1 and Dataset_2), each comprising gonadal samples from female, intersex, and male ricefield eel. The specimens were collected from wild populations in the Yangtze River, Chongqing, China. Sex was determined histologically by H&E staining of cryosectioned gonadal tissues (Supplementary Fig. S1A-B). Dataset_1 included one female, three intersex, and one male specimen. Single-cell suspensions were prepared individually from each sample and sequenced using the 10x Genomics platform (Fig. 1A). After rigorous quality control and batch-effect correction using CCA, 9,715 high-quality cells were retained in Dataset_1 (Supplementary Fig. S1C-G; Supplementary Table S1, S15). Unsupervised clustering of these cells revealed 22 distinct clusters (Fig. 1B), which were annotated based on lineage-specific marker genes (Fig. 1C). Dataset_2 was established by sequencing libraries that were each pooled from three biological replicates per sex, using the SeekOne platform for subsequent validation.

Synaptonemal complex protein 1 (sycp1), which is involved in homologous chromosome pairing during meiosis, marked the germline cell lineage [19]. Steroidogenic acute regulatory protein (star) facilitates cholesterol transport from the cytoplasm to the inner mitochondrial membrane, and is predominantly expressed in theca cells of the ovaries and Leydig cells of the testes [20, 21]. Hydroxysteroid 17-beta dehydrogenase 1 (hsd17b1) marked follicle cells in the ovaries and their homologous counterparts in ovotestes and testes [22]. Collagen type I alpha 1 chain (col1a1) and Platelet derived growth factor subunit A (pdgfa) labeled stromal cell populations [23, 24]. Plasmalemma vesicle associated protein b (plvapb) primarily served as a marker for blood vessel cells [25]. Using molecular markers immunoglobulin light iota constant 1, s1 (igic1s1), CD247 antigen like (cd247l), lysozyme (lyz), and C1q and TNF related 9 (c1qtnf9), which are specific for B cells, T cells, neutrophils, and macrophages in zebrafish [26, 27], we identified corresponding cell populations in ricefield eel. Ultimately, we constructed a comprehensive cellular atlas of the ricefield eel gonads (Fig. 1D). Additionally, we identified more marker genes within various cell lineages (Fig. 1E and Supplementary Table S2).

The analytical findings were recapitulated in Dataset_2 to confirm their reliability. Annotation using lineage-specific markers identified consistent cell types, including germ cells, theca cells, follicle cells, stromal cells, blood vessels cells, and immune cells (Supplementary Fig. S1H-L). Finally, projecting Dataset_1 onto Dataset_2 confirmed a strong consensus in cellular classification (Supplementary Fig. S1M). Notably, differences between cell lineages appeared more significant than those arising from gender sources, with no cell cluster being exclusively derived from a specific sex. This suggests that different somatic cell populations within the gonads retain lineage homology during sex reversal. Comparative analysis of cell composition revealed that intersex gonads had a higher proportion of somatic cells compared to ovaries and testes (Fig. 1F), particularly an increased abundance of blood vessel cells, follicle cells, and stromal cells. This observation implies that during sex reversal, germ cell production may be less active in degenerating ovaries and immature testes.

Construction of the germ cell lineages in female and male ricefield eel

To elucidate the differentiation process of germ cells in the ricefield eel, we extracted and re-clustered the germ cell population from Dataset_1. Initial clustering identified 15 distinct cell populations (Supplementary Fig. S2A). Cluster 9 contained contaminating erythrocytes (Supplementary Fig. S2B-C), which were filtered out. Subsequent clustering of the remaining 5,885 viable germ cells yielded 10 distinct cell clusters (Fig. 2A). Cells from female, intersex, and male samples were distributed across all clusters (Fig. 2B), indicating that differences in meiotic stages, rather than sex-specific transcriptional profiles, primarily drove the transcriptional variations. DND microRNA-mediated repression inhibitor 1 (dnd1), essential for the survival and positioning of PGCs in vertebrates [28, 29], was specifically expressed in cluster 6 (Fig. 2C). Nucleophosmin 1 (npm1) and calreticulin (calr) are markers for teleost germ stem cells and spermatogonia [30].

The spermatocyte marker gene sycp1 was upregulated from the leptotene to zygotene [32, 33] and highly expressed in clusters 6, 2, and 4 [31, 32]. Deleted in azoospermia-like (dazl), a marker for primary pachytene spermatocytes [33], was predominantly expressed in clusters 3 and 8. Aurora kinase A (aurka), upregulated during the diplotene [32], was expressed in clusters 7 and 0, corresponding to the onset of metaphase I (Fig. 2D and Supplementary Fig. S2D). Nucleolar and spindle associated protein 1 (nusap1), involved in spindle assembly regulation in oocytes, was expressed during the transition from prophase I to metaphase I and from anaphase I to telophase I [34]. Anaphase Promoting Complex/Cyclosome subunit 3 (cdc23) plays a critical role in the metaphase-to-anaphase transition, thus cluster 5 represents the anaphase stage [35]. Cluster 1, positioned between metaphase and anaphase, was defined as diakinesis. Cluster 9, highly expressing fosab and exclusively originating from ovotestes, displayed elevated apoptosis scores (Supplementary Fig. S2E), suggesting it represents apoptotic germ stem cells in degenerating ovaries. Thus, we constructed a comprehensive germ cell atlas for ricefield eel gonads (Fig. 2E) and identified additional potential genes involved in germ cell lineage differentiation (Fig. 2F and Supplementary Table S3).

Pseudotime analysis revealed that as germ cells differentiated, they clearly segregated into two branches representing female and male lineages (Fig. 2G, H and Supplementary Fig. S2F). Intersex-derived germ cells were present in both branches, indicating coexistence of both sexes within intersex individuals. We further identified sex-specific meiotic-related genes in germ cells (Supplementary Fig. S2G), which exhibited sex-preferential expression at different stages of germ cell differentiation. Spermatogenesis associated 4 (spata4), specifically expressed in male germ cells in vertebrates [36], was also specifically expressed in spermatocytes of ricefield eel during the metaphase to anaphase of the meiosis I. Additionally, we identified additional sex-specific meiotic-related genes that regulate the progression of meiosis in male or female germ cells(Fig. 2I).

Recapitulating our findings, the independent Dataset_2 confirmed the robustness of the analysis. All meiotic germ cell stages defined in Dataset_1 were successfully recapitulated in Dataset_2 using the same stage-specific markers (Supplementary Fig. S2H-I). Moreover, the stage-specific gene expression patterns discovered in Dataset_1 were consistently observed in Dataset_2 (Supplementary Fig. S2J). Ultimately, we constructed a cellular atlas of gametogenesis in the ricefield eel, identifying potential key genes involved in this process, as well as differentiation-related genes specific to oogenesis and spermatogenesis.

GSCswdr17+tep− are germ stem cells retained in females with the potential to differentiate into male spermatogonia

Unlike mammals, which produce a limited number of oocytes only during embryogenesis, many teleost species maintain self-renewing GSCs that enable continuous oocyte production throughout adulthood [37, 38]. Our sequencing samples encompass gonads at all reproductive stages, facilitating comprehensive analysis of GSC dynamics and differentiation pathways across the entire sex reversal process.

Clustering analysis identified two GSC subpopulations from three types of gonads (Fig. 3A). These were distinguished by WD repeat domain 17 (wdr17) expression into GSCswdr17+ and GSCswdr17− (Fig. 3B). Both subpopulations were present in ovary, ovotestis, and testis (Fig. 3C and Supplementary Fig. S3A). GSCswdr17+ expressed lower levels of classical GSC markers like dnd1, higher stemness scores, and lower cycling scores (Fig. 3D-F). Functional enrichment analysis showed that GSCswdr17+ were enriched for “translation” and “rRNA metabolic processes”, while GSCswdr17− were enriched for “histone modification”, “chromatin organization” and “developmental processes involved in reproduction” (Fig. 3G and Supplementary Table S4). This suggests that GSCswdr17+ represent a quiescent state with high stemness potential, whereas GSCswdr17− are more differentiated. GO functional analysis of GSCs isolated from different sexes revealed that both female and male GSCs were primarily enriched for “translation” and “proteasomal protein catabolic processes”. In contrast, intersex GSCs were enriched for “chromatin organization”, “mRNA metabolic processes” and “histone modification”, suggesting chromatin remodeling as a potential mechanism in transitioning from female to male GSCs (Supplementary Fig. S3B and Supplementary Table S5).

Pseudotime trajectory analysis revealed a differentiation path of GSCs from female through intersex to male (Fig. 3H). Female-derived GSCs were classified into female state 1(Female_S1) and female state 2(Female_S2), while male-derived germ stem cells were divided into male state 1(Male_S1) and male state 2(Male_S2). Intersex GSCs exhibited dual characteristics of both sexes, following a trajectory from female to male differentiation. Female_S1 and Male_S1 were mainly composed of GSCswdr17+, whereas Female_S2 and Male_S2 were predominantly GSCswdr17− (Fig. 3I and Supplementary Fig. S3C). Analysis of female GSCs confirmed their heterogeneity based on wdr17 expression, with GSCswdr17+ showing higher stemness potential (Supplementary Fig. S3D-G). On the differentiation trajectory, these two subpopulations occupied distinct evolutionary states (Supplementary Fig. S3H), indicating that GSCswdr17+ differentiate into GSCswdr17− during female germ cell differentiation. In testes, Male_S1 exhibited higher stemness scores compared to Male_S2 (Supplementary Fig. S3I), suggesting that Male_S1 differentiates into Male_S2 (Fig. 3H). RNA-FISH experiments validated the presence of both GSCs wdr17+ and GSCswdr17− in the germinal cords of all three gonadal types (Fig. 3J).

The sex reversal process involves the transition from Female_S1 to Male_S1. Pseudotime trajectory analysis (Supplementary Fig. S3J) identified upregulated gene clusters enriched for “chromatin remodeling”, supporting its role in GSC sex reversal. The “Wnt signaling pathway” was also enriched in the sex reversal gene set (Supplementary Fig. S3K and Supplementary Table S6). Telomerase associated protein 1 (tep1), a component of the ribonucleoprotein complex responsible for telomerase activity, was specifically expressed in male GSCs (Supplementary Fig. S3L-M ). Higher telomerase activity supports long-term self-renewal and high proliferation rates in male GSCs [39]. RNA-FISH confirmed tep1 expression in male GSCs but not in female GSCs, with both female GSCsdnd1+tep1− and male GSCsdnd1+tep1+ coexist in ovotestis (Supplementary Fig. S3N).

Among the sex reversal-related genes, 100 were annotated as transcription factors (Supplementary Table S7). Super elongation complex subunit b (mllt1b), as a chromatin factor known for regulating progenitor cell differentiation in zebrafish [40], may play a crucial role in GSC sex reversal. Knockdown of nuclear receptor subfamily 3, group C, member 1 (nr3c1), a glucocorticoid receptor, impairs female zebrafish reproduction [41, 42], but its role in sex reversal remains unexplored. To investigate the potential roles of mllt1b and nr3c1 in regulating sex conversion within GSCs, we knocked down the expression of their mouse homologs, Mllt1 and Nr3c1, in the mouse spermatogonial cell line GC-1 (Fig. 3K-L). RNA-seq of the knockdown and control cells identified differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Pathway analysis of these DEGs showed that knockdown of Mllt1 or Nr3c1 upregulated pathways including “Cell cycle,” “Oxidative phosphorylation,” “Oocyte meiosis,” and “Progesterone-mediated oocyte maturation” (Fig. 3M-N), implying that both genes are required to maintain the male identity of spermatogonia. Conversely, the downregulation of critical signaling pathways like TGF-β, Notch, and Wnt is highly significant, as these pathways are deeply implicated in male sex determination, testis development, and spermatogonial stem cell maintenance (Fig. 3M-N) [43–45]. Our findings suggest that Mllt1 and Nr3c1 are pivotal in maintaining the male identity of germ stem cells, potentially through the coordinated regulation of a network of key signaling and metabolic pathways. Recapitulating our findings in the independent Dataset_2 confirmed the reproducibility of our analyses. Based on wdr17 expression levels, we again identified two distinct subpopulations within the GSCs: GSCswdr17+ and GSCswdr17− (Supplementary Fig. S3O- P). The GSCswdr17+ population exhibited a higher stemness score and a lower G2/M-phase score compared to the GSCswdr17− (Supplementary Fig. S3Q-R). Furthermore, the expression of tep1 in GSCs increased along the differentiation trajectory from female to intersex states (Supplementary Fig. S3S-U).

In summary, four distinct GSC subpopulations were identified based on the expression patterns of the marker genes wdr17 and tep1, including GSCswdr17+tep1−, GSCswdr17−tep1−, GSCswdr17+tep1+, and GSCswdr17−tep1+. Notably, the two tep1 negative subpopulations (GSCswdr17+tep1− and GSCswdr17−tep1−) were ovary-specific, whereas the two tep1 positive subpopulations (GSCswdr17+tep1+ and GSCswdr17−tep1+) were testis-specific. During sex reversal, the GSCswdr17+ constituted the sexually transitioning population, differentiating from the wdr17+tep1− state in females to the wdr17+tep1+ state in males. In contrast, both GSCwdr17− subpopulations are postulated to originate from GSCswdr17+ that have initiated differentiation into either spermatogonia or oogonia. These four GSCs coexist in ovotestis. Multiple transcription factors, including mllt1b and nr3c1, likely regulate the sexual reversal of GSCswdr17+ through a mechanism involving chromatin remodeling.

Collaborative steroidogenesis by two theca cell subpopulations and their potential transition into Leydig cells

Theca cells in ovary and Leydig cells in testis exhibit functional similarities. In females, theca cells are the primary sites for androgen synthesis (e.g., testosterone and androstenedione), using enzymes such as CYP11A1 and CYP17A1 to convert cholesterol into androstenedione or testosterone, which serve as androgen precursors for granulosa cells. Granulosa cells then convert inactive estrone (E1) to active estradiol (E2) via HSD17B1 [46]. In males, Leydig cells synthesize testosterone through similar enzymatic reactions [47]. Both cell types express common metabolic enzymes like STAR, CYP11A1, and CYP17A1, found in a same cell population in females and intersex samples (Supplementary Fig. S4A). Given their dual characteristics, we designated these theca-like cells in intersex individuals as “Theca-Leydig” cells.

Theca and theca-Leydig cells can be clustered into three groups (Fig. 4A). Clusters 0 and 1 represent two distinct theca cell subpopulations in females, while Cluster 2 is unique to intersex samples and may represent Leydig cells in males (Fig. 4B). Enzymes involved in steroidogenesis, including cytochrome P450 family members and hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase family genes, show differential expression among clusters. Cluster 0 highly expresses cytochrome P450 family 11 subfamily A member 1, tandem duplicate 2(cyp11a2, homologs of human CYP11A1), while Cluster 1 highly expresses cyp17a1. Cluster 2 shares a similar expression pattern with Cluster 1 but at lower levels overall (Fig. 4C). CYP11A1 converts cholesterol into pregnenolone, the first step in steroid hormone biosynthesis. CYP17A1 further converts pregnenolone or progesterone into dehydroepiandrosterone (DHEA) or androstenedione, which serves as a precursor for subsequent steroid hormones [48]. The steroid hormone synthesis reactions mediated by CYP11A1 and CYP17A1 typically occur within the same cell. However, in ricefield eel, cyp11a2 and cyp17a1 are expressed in two distinct theca cell subpopulations, suggesting a collaborative role in steroidogenesis. Using RNA-FISH probes for cyp11a2 and star, we identified cyp11a2+ and cyp11a2− theca cell subpopulations existing in female gonads (Fig. 4D). The heterogeneity of theca cells has been confirmed.

Analysis of the specific expression profiles of these clusters (Supplementary Fig. S4B and Supplementary Table S8) revealed that Cluster 2, unique to intersex gonads, specifically expresses JunB proto-oncogene, AP-1 transcription factor subunit b (junbb) (Fig. 4E), likely representing Leydig cells in testis. RNA-FISH confirmed the presence of junbb+theca-Leydig cells only in testis and ovotestis’ male regions (Fig. 4F), indicating that junbb+ cells are Leydig cells and probably derived from theca cells (Fig. 4G). Stemness analysis indicated that the two theca cell subpopulations in females have higher stemness scores compared to junbb+Leydig cells, suggesting that theca cells retain stem cell potential (Fig. 4H). As a key component of the AP-1 transcription factor complex, Junbb acts as a critical downstream effector of c-Jun, collectively playing a pivotal role in regulating key steps of steroid hormone synthesis in the testis. In fish, the c-Jun/AP-1 pathway directly regulates the star gene, thereby ensuring adequate androgen production and maintaining normal spermatogenic function [49]. Integrating our analytical results with existing evidence, we observed upregulated junbb expression during the sex reversal of theca cells in the ricefield eel. We hypothesize that this elevated junbb expression may enhance the transcription of the steroidogenic gene cyp17a1, consequently promoting hormone synthesis and facilitating the transformation of theca cells into Leydig cells.

Monocle2 trajectory analysis showed that Cluster 0 contains theca progenitor cells that differentiate into two theca cell subtypes expressing either cyp11a2 or cyp17a1. Male junbb+Leydig cells may originate from the differentiation of female theca cells (Supplementary Fig. S4C-D). The two states of junbb+Leydig cells on the trajectory suggest heterogeneity within Leydig cells in male gonads. The independent Dataset_2 confirmed the robustness of our subpopulation analysis. All three “theca-Leydig” cell clusters defined in Dataset_1 were recapitulated in Dataset_2 (Supplementary Fig. S4E-G). Notably, cell cluster 2, putatively identified as Leydig cells, showed an enrichment in male specimens, confirming our initial hypothesis (Supplementary Fig. S4H). The stemness scores for these subpopulations also correlated well between the two datasets (Supplementary Fig. S4I).

scRNA-seq data showed that Leydig cells have higher average expression levels of cyp17a1, whereas theca cells express higher levels of cyp11a2 (Fig. 4I). Re-annotating transcriptomes from public datasets GSE43649 and SRX1007627 (each sex derived from pools of 12 samples) revealed no annotation for cyp11a2, but all datasets showed significantly higher cyp17a1 expression in males (Fig. 4J), a finding that is consistent with the analysis of our single-cell transcriptomic data. To further validate our findings, we performed additional bulk transcriptome sequencing on five biological replicates each of female, intersex, and male gonads. In this batch of data, cyp17a1 similarly exhibited significantly higher expression in the male gonads (Supplementary Fig. S4J). This indicates that Leydig cells in males exhibit high cyp17a1 expression, facilitating increased testosterone synthesis.

In summary, we identified two distinct theca cell subpopulations within the ovaries of ricefield eel, which express cyp11a2 and cyp17a1, respectively. These subpopulations are involved in the cooperative process of converting cholesterol into androgens. Furthermore, our data suggest that Leydig cells in males likely originate from a transition of these female theca cell subpopulations.

Roles of gdf9+ follicle cells in oogenesis and apoptosis

HSD17B1 is predominantly expressed in granulosa cells of mammalian ovaries, where it converts E1 derived from theca cells into E2, the most active form of estrogen [50]. In fish, follicle cells perform similar functions of granulosa cells found in mammals. Through clustering analysis, four distinct follicle subpopulations were identified. Due to the absence or scarcity of follicle-like cells in testes, similar clusters were not observed in males (Fig. 5A, B). We analyzed hormone metabolism-related genes in these four subpopulations. Clusters 0, 1, 2, and 4 are involved in hormone synthesis and metabolism. Cluster 0 and 2 share high expression of cytochrome P450, family 24, subfamily A, polypeptide 1(cyp24a1), named cyp24a1hiFollicles. Cluster 1 expresses lower levels of hsd17b1 but higher levels of cytochrome P450, family 51(cyp51), termed cyp51hiFollicles (Fig. 5C). The protein lanosterol 14α-demethylase, encoded by the cyp51 gene, converts lanosterol into follicular fluid meiosis-activating sterol (FF-MAS), a cholesterol biosynthesis intermediate that accumulates in gonads and activates oocyte meiosis [51].

By extracting the specifically highly expressed genes from different cell populations (Supplementary Fig. S5A and Supplementary Table S9), we found that Cluster 4 is enriched in pathways such as “DNA replication”, “Cell cycle” and “Reproduction” (Supplementary Fig. S5B and Supplementary Table S10) and specifically expresses proliferation-related genes proliferating cell nuclear antigen (pcna) and growth differentiation factor 9 (gdf9) (Fig. 5E). Cell cycle analysis confirmed Cluster 4 consists of proliferative follicle cells (Supplementary Fig. S5C). Given the presence of GSCs and continuous oocyte formation in teleosts, follicle cells must proliferate continuously to support follicle maturation; thus, we named Cluster 4 Proliferation-Follicle cells. Cluster 3 is enriched in the “Apoptosis” pathway, representing apoptotic follicle cells, hence named Apoptosis-Follicle (Fig. 5D). To determine their distribution, we performed RNA-FISH using gdf9 as a marker. We found that gdf9+ follicle cells are located both surrounding mature follicles and within the reproductive ridge between follicles, which is the center of oogenesis. These cells have high proliferative capacity with high expression of hsd17b1 and gdf9, and promote follicle maturation. Surprisingly, gdf9+follicle-like cells were also observed in the male regions of ovotestis and in testis (Fig. 5F). Corroborating our analysis, the four follicle cell subpopulations were identified in the independent Dataset_2 (Supplementary Fig. S5D-E). Notably, while the markers gdf9 and pcna were absent in this dataset, the consistent expression of mki67 specifically in the putative proliferation-follicle cluster across both datasets confirmed its identity (Supplementary Fig. S5F), with platform differences being a likely cause for the missing markers. The proliferative nature of this cluster was further supported by cell cycle scoring (Supplementary Fig. S5G).

Besides converting E1 to E2, Hsd17b1 can oxidize testosterone to androstenedione. Given the lack of follicle-like cells in our single-cell data from males, a comparison of bulk transcriptome data from both our own sequencing efforts and public datasets revealed that hsd17b1 expression across three types of gonads was significantly lower in males (Fig. 5G, Supplementary Fig. S5H). The retained follicle-like cells from female gonads show markedly reduced hsd17b1 expression. However, low-level expression may help regulate testosterone concentration balance in testis. In summary, gdf9+follicle cells secrete hormones that promote follicular maturation. These cells can also be retained within the testes; however, their expression levels of hsd17b1 are significantly reduced.

Stromal heterogeneity and the role of bmp4+ cells as a source of Sertoli cells

In ricefield eel gonads, a population of cells highly expressing thbs1b exhibit stromal cell characteristics, including the expression of multiple collagen family genes (Supplementary Fig. S6A). These cells are crucial for maintaining tissue structure and facilitating intercellular signaling. Clustering analysis identified multiple stromal cell types in ricefield eel gonads (Fig. 6A). WT1 transcription factor (wt1) is pivotal in early gonad development, particularly in Sertoli cell differentiation and maintenance. In mature testes, wt1 is primarily expressed in clusters 0 and 1, suggesting these clusters may have Sertoli cell functions (Fig. 6B). Classical Sertoli cell markers follicle stimulating hormone receptor (fshr) and GATA binding protein 4 (gata4) were detected at low levels in single-cell data but showed high expression in testes in bulk RNA-seq data (Supplementary Fig. S6B). Interestingly, these markers were also expressed in follicle-like cell lineages (Supplementary Fig. S6C).

Cluster 1 expresses epithelial markers epcam and ovarian epithelial marker fdf24, indicating an ovarian surface epithelium (OSE) role in ovaries and potentially Sertoli-like cells in males (Epithelia-Sertoli like cells, ESLCs). Cluster 0 highly expresses fibroblast markers decorin (dcn) and collagen type I alpha 1 chain (col1a1), designated as Fibroblasts-Sertoli like cells (FSLCs). Clusters 2 and 3 express smooth muscle cell markers cald1 and acta2, identified as vascular smooth muscle cells (vSMCs). Cluster 5 expresses pericyte markers pdgfrb and notch3. Cluster 4 expresses bone morphogenetic protein 4 (bmp4), a marker for MSCs (Fig. 6B). Thus, we identified five distinct stromal cell populations in ricefield eel gonads (Fig. 6C), and characterized the molecular features and functions of the five identified cell types (Fig. 6D and Supplementary Tables S11, S12).

BMP4 plays a crucial role in sex determination by regulating multiple signaling pathways, thereby influencing the differentiation of the genital ridge and gonad formation. Notably, BMP4 is particularly important for the differentiation and functional maintenance of Sertoli cells [52]. Three bulk RNA-seq datasets show high bmp4 expression in male gonads (Fig. 6E, Supplementary Fig. S6D), suggesting that bmp4+ stromal cells might be a source of Sertoli cell differentiation during sex reversal. Chromatin remodeling activity scores across stromal cell populations in intersex individuals revealed that MSCsbmp4+ had the highest scores (Fig. 6F-G). This suggests chromatin remodeling is a universal regulatory mechanism in both germ cell and stromal cell sex reversal, with MSCsbmp4+ playing a critical role. All five stromal cell subtypes were successfully recapitulated using their respective marker genes in Dataset_2 (Supplementary Fig. S6E-F). The chromatin remodeling scores across these five subpopulations were highest in samples of intersex origin, with the MSCsbmp4+ derived from intersex gonads exhibiting the most pronounced score (Supplementary Fig. S6G).

To investigate the potential role of bmp4 in stromal cells during sex reversal, we overexpressed its mouse homolog, Bmp4, in the mouse embryonic fibroblast cell line C3H/10T1/2 (Fig. 6H-I). Transcriptome sequencing was subsequently performed on both the Bmp4-overexpressing group and the control group transfected with an empty vector. Differential expression gene (DEG) analysis, followed by functional and pathway enrichment analysis, revealed that Bmp4 overexpression led to the downregulation of pathways related to Cholesterol Biosynthesis and Metabolism and Beta Catenin Binding. Conversely, processes such as ‘Androgen Biosynthesis and Metabolic’ and ‘Testosterone Biosynthetic’ were upregulated (Fig. 6J). BMP4 signaling is a well-established, conserved pro-male determinant in vertebrate sex determination, known in mammals including humans and sheep to drive Sertoli cell differentiation and ensure testicular structural integrity [53, 54]. Our study now reveals its critical role in the natural sex reversal process of the ricefield eel. Based on the aforementioned results, we propose that Bmp4, expressed in MSCs, may promote a masculinizing microenvironment by reprogramming metabolic and signaling pathways.

Four immune cell types were identified in ricefield eel gonads: innate immune cells (macrophages and neutrophils) and adaptive immune cells (T cells and B cells) (Fig. 6K). Markers for these immune cell types were also identified (Fig. 6L and Supplementary Table S13). Consistent with our initial assessment, the immune cell profiling results were successfully recapitulated in the independent Dataset_2 (Supplementary Fig. S6H-I). Blood vessel cells displayed low heterogeneity and no significant differences in cell cycle characteristics (Supplementary Fig. S6J-K). Immune and vessel cells exhibit conserved biological functions and do not express key hormone synthesis genes (Supplementary Fig. S6L), suggesting they are not directly involved in sex reversal.

Given the implication from our prior analyses that chromatin remodeling may be a key regulatory mechanism underlying sex reversal in ricefield eel, we sought to investigate this further in Dataset_2. We assessed the global activity of four major chromatin-modifying events, including ‘DNA methylation’, ‘DNA demethylation’, ‘histone acetylation’ and ‘histone deacetylation’, across four cell types postulated to undergo identity transitions (GSCs, theca cells, proliferation-follicle cells, and MSCs) in female, intersex, and male gonads. Generally, histone acetylation and DNA demethylation promote an open chromatin state and facilitate gene transcription, whereas histone deacetylation and DNA methylation are associated with gene silencing. Our analysis revealed that intersex gonads exhibited higher levels of histone acetylation alongside lower levels of histone deacetylation and DNA methylation, collectively indicating a more transcriptionally active chromatin state (Supplementary Fig. S7A-D). Furthermore, we identified epigenetic regulators that were differentially expressed among the sexes (Supplementary Fig. S7E). These genes may orchestrate the transdifferentiation of various cell types within the gonad by broadly modulating chromatin accessibility and activating the transcription of downstream genes. The observed shift towards a more transcriptionally permissive state in intersex gonads strongly suggests that large-scale epigenetic reprogramming is necessary to facilitate the profound changes in cellular identity required for the female-to-male transition.

Discussion

In contrast to the chromosomal sex determination prevalent in most mammals, teleost fish exhibit remarkable diversity in their sex determining mechanisms, making them powerful models for studying sexual plasticity. The ricefield eel serves as a unique model of sex reversal, where germ cells with the same genome sequentially develop into both sexes. This provides valuable insights into sex determination and reversal. Using scRNA-seq, we achieved high-resolution dissection of the cellular composition of ricefield eel gonads and their changes during sex reversal. Our study elucidated the differentiation trajectories of germ and somatic cells and identified the bidirectional differentiation potential of GSCswdr17+, thereby providing a foundational resource for elucidating the cellular basis of vertebrate sex determination.

Our study provides novel insights into the cellular and molecular mechanisms of sex determination and reversal in fish. The bidirectional differentiation potential of GSCswdr17+ is reported here for the first time in ricefield eel, along with their differentiation trajectory during sex reversal. Furthermore, we identified key somatic cell subpopulations, such as two theca cell subpopulations that work synergistically in steroid hormone synthesis, and provided evidence for the origin of Leydig cells in testis. Proliferative gdf9+ follicle cells were found in all three gonadal types, and the importance of MSCs bmp4+ in testes formation was highlighted. We also preliminarily analyzed common regulatory mechanisms, such as chromatin remodeling, during sex reversal of germ and somatic cells. These findings complement existing knowledge and provide new directions for future research. Previous studies have primarily focused on sex determination mechanisms in zebrafish and other teleosts, while our study expands this field by revealing unique characteristics of GSCs and somatic cells in ricefield eel, aspects that have not been extensively explored.

The coordinated increase in histone acetylation and decrease in repressive marks (histone deacetylation and DNA methylation) likely creates a permissive environment for the activation of a genetic program essential for masculinization. This epigenetically “poised” state in intersex gonads would allow for the rapid and coordinated expression of genes involved in testicular development, steroidogenesis, and male germ cell differentiation that are otherwise silenced in the female state. This mechanism is consistent with studies in other species showing that epigenetic modifications can act as a primary switch in sex determination [55], especially in systems lacking rigid genetic sex chromosomes [13]. We propose that sex reversal in the ricefield eel is not solely driven by changes in transcription factor expression but is critically dependent on a preceding and pervasive alteration of the epigenetic landscape.

In human medicine, DSD are complex conditions involving abnormalities in chromosomes, gonads, or anatomical structures [56]. When an individual’s biological sex does not match their genetic sex, they are considered to have DSD. The incidence of DSD is approximately 1 in 4500–5500 live births, with complex etiologies and diagnostic challenges. Next-generation sequencing technologies promise to improve diagnostic rates [57]. Studying the sex reversal mechanisms in ricefield eel can provide valuable references for understanding and treating these diseases. Understanding how to regulate the differentiation pathways of germ and somatic cells could aid in developing new treatments, particularly for individuals with infertility or other reproductive health issues due to abnormal sex determination (such as gonadal dysgenesis). For example, regulating the expression of key genes like WDR17 and BMP4 could promote or inhibit specific types of gonad cell differentiation.

Despite significant progress, our study has some limitations. Limited sample sizes may result in incomplete gene expression pattern identification. Validating these findings in more species would enhance their generalizability. Due to insufficient cell capture numbers, certain low-abundance cell types or gene expression patterns may not have been fully captured. Future work incorporating larger cohort studies, spatial transcriptomics to contextualize cellular interactions, and functional genetic approaches (e.g., CRISPR/Cas9) in appropriate models will be crucial to validate the roles of key gene. Exploring the role of epigenetic modifications (e.g., DNA methylation and histone modification) in sex reversal is another important area for future investigation.

Conclusions

In summary, our single-cell atlas of Monopterus albus gonads reveals that natural sex reversal is driven by coordinated fate transitions in both germline and somatic cell lineages. We demonstrate the existence of bipotent ovarian GSCs capable of male differentiation, alongside the plastic transformation of somatic cell populations including theca cells and stromal precursors (Fig. 7). Our findings establish a paradigm of vertebrate sex determination as a process of systematic cellular reorganization, with direct implications for understanding disorders of cell fate regulation, such as DSDs.
