A prospective pharmacokinetic interaction study between rifampicin and fusidic acid for the treatment of staphylococcal infections
Authors: Callum Maggs, Laurens Manning, Okhee Yoo, Sam Salman, Madhu Page-Sharp, Craig Aboltins
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveFusidic acid with rifampicin is used for the treatment of severe staphylococcal infection, particularly prosthetic joint infections. Previous studies using twice daily fusidic acid showed rifampicin increases fusidic acid clearance, potentially causing sub-therapeutic concentrations. It is uncertain whether this occurs with three-times daily dosing. This study sought to re-evaluate this potential drug–drug interaction.MethodsIn this prospective, open-label drug–drug interaction population pharmacokinetic (PK) study, participants were randomized to receive fusidic acid or rifampicin for 24 hours, followed by combination therapy for the duration of treatment. Drug concentration assays used liquid-chromatography mass spectroscopy on dried blood spots. Population PK models were built for fusidic acid, rifampicin and 25-desacetyl rifampicin.ResultsTen participants were recruited. Inter-individual variability for both absorption (98.1%) and clearance (77.8%) were high for fusidic acid. A population pharmacokinetic model for fusidic acid revealed that early autoinhibition dominated over later rifampicin-mediated induction, resulting in a net decrease in fusidic acid clearance. The mean fusidic acid area under the curve during each dosing interval (AUCτ) at steady state was 1.76-fold [0.049, 34.918] higher relative to Day 1, despite high uncertainty.Large inter-individual (110%) variability in absorption was observed for rifampicin. There was no apparent effect on rifampicin metabolism by fusidic acid co-administration, however, fusidic acid decreased clearance of 25-desacetyl rifampicin.ConclusionIn patients treated with fusidic acid three times daily in combination with rifampicin, autoinhibition potentially counteracted rifampicin induction such that fusidic acid concentrations were not reduced. Rifampicin clearance was not affected by fusidic acid, but 25-desacetyl rifampicin clearance was decreased. There was large inter-individual variability in the observed concentrations and final parameter estimates.

Background

Rifampicin and fusidic acid are two important antibiotics used in clinical practice for the treatment of various bacterial infections. Rifampicin is a highly bioavailable agent with a broad spectrum of activity. It has good activity against biofilm-associated organisms and is considered an important tool for the treatment of peri-prosthetic joint infections (PJI) and other severe staphylococcal infections.1–3 However, staphylococci can rapidly develop resistance to rifampicin monotherapy, particularly in the presence of a biofilm.3,4 The addition of an appropriate companion antibiotic mitigates the emergence of rifampicin resistance.5 Methicillin-sensitive and resistant staphylococcal strains in most parts of the world remain sensitive to fusidic acid.6 Fusidic acid has shown clinical efficacy in combination with rifampicin for prosthetic device infections3 but has also been used for the treatment of several other bone, joint, skin and soft tissue infections.7,8,9

Both drugs have been used in Australia and Europe for more than four decades, but little is known about their pharmacokinetics (PK), pharmacodynamics and potential interactions when given as a combination. Two small studies suggest a possible bidirectional interaction between rifampicin and fusidic acid. In one study where PK data were available for six participants, a significant drug–drug interaction was assumed to have occurred on the basis that the concentrations of fusidic acid observed reduced over time and were significantly lower than observed in a previous trial of healthy volunteers.10 In this study, twice-daily dosing of fusidic acid was used, in contrast to three-times-daily dosing reported in other clinical studies7 and no formal PK modelling was performed. In a second, retrospective analysis of patients receiving rifampicin for osteoarticular infections, co-administration of fusidic acid was associated with increased rifampicin exposure.11 25-desacetyl rifampicin is the principal active metabolite of rifampicin, retaining antimicrobial activity and potentially contributing to efficacy and toxicity; measuring it provides a more complete assessment of exposure and drug–drug interactions. Given the scarce data, we sought to re-evaluate a possible drug–drug interaction for fusidic acid and rifampicin, including its metabolite 25-desacetyl rifampicin, when co-administered in patients managed for PJI and other severe staphylococcal infections.

Methods

Site

This study was performed at St Vincent’s Hospital Melbourne and Northern Health Melbourne, Victoria, Australia between January 2019 and June 2022.

Ethical approval

Ethical approval was obtained from the St Vincent’s Hospital Human Ethics and Review Committee (HREC 004/19; Project ID 47904). All participants provided written informed consent.

Eligibility

Patients with a presumed or confirmed staphylococcal infection, where a combination of rifampicin and fusidic acid was planned by their treating clinician, were eligible for recruitment. Potential participants were identified by the infectious diseases and pharmacy teams. At the time of recruitment, participants were receiving intravenous therapy with an anti-staphylococcal beta-lactam antimicrobial. Subjects were excluded from the study if they had a history of adverse reaction to rifampicin or fusidic acid, had a contraindication to these medications (pregnancy, interaction with other medications, liver failure), were unlikely to be on treatment with these medications for longer than 4 weeks or if they refused or were unable to provide informed consent. Owing to the potential for acquisition of resistance to either fusidic acid or rifampicin when used alone with intravenous anti-staphylococcal agents other than beta-lactams,12 patients receiving non-beta-lactams as the intravenous anti-staphylococcal agent were excluded.

Study design

This was a prospective open-label clinical drug–drug interaction study. The decision to treat with rifampicin and fusidic acid was determined independently by treating clinicians before study enrolment. The doses of the antibiotics of rifampicin 300 mg orally twice daily and fusidic acid 500 mg three times daily reflected the standard of care at our institutions. To attempt to determine the presence of early interactions, patients were randomized at enrolment (Day 0) to the ‘index’ study drug of either oral rifampicin or oral fusidic acid. The intravenous anti-staphylococcal beta-lactam was continued for the first 24 hours of the study period. After 24 hours (Day 1), the other combination antibiotic, the ‘secondary’ study drug (either rifampicin or fusidic acid) was started. At this point, the intravenous therapy was ceased. Antibiotics were administered strictly as per protocol under supervision in hospital and on the day of follow-up sampling. Missed doses post-discharge were tracked via medication diaries and no dose adjustments occurred during therapy. The total duration of antibiotic therapy was determined by the treating clinician. Randomization to the ‘index’ drug was performed using Microsoft Excel (version 15.35).

Clinical data

Demographic data on age, body mass index, serum creatinine, serum albumin and the site of staphylococcal infection. Follow-up data regarding medication tolerance, adherence and clinical outcome were assessed at 4 weeks and 3 months.

Sampling schedule

A minimum of four timed concentrations were collected on three study days (Day 1, Day 2 and Day 28 ± 7), with an additional venous sample on Day 1 for validation. Samples were collected pre-dose and at 30–40 minutes, 1.5–2 hours and 3.5–4.5 hours post-dose (Table 1).

Sample collection

Mixed capillary blood from a finger prick was taken and spotted onto filter paper.13 The dried blood spot (DBS) cards were air dried at room temperature for 1 to 2 h, placed into an airtight foil envelope with a single desiccant sachet and transported on dry ice before storage at −80°C.

Sample analysis

The methods to assay fusidic acid, rifampicin and des-acetylrifampicin from DBS and plasma have been published previously. The assay limits of quantification were 0.15, 0.015 and 0.014 µg/mL for fusidic acid, rifampicin and 25-desacetyl rifampicin, respectively.14 All pharmacokinetic modelling was performed using measured total drug concentrations.

Outcomes and sample size calculation

The primary outcome was evidence of a pharmacokinetic drug–drug interaction between rifampicin and fusidic acid, assessed by comparing estimated exposure (area under the concentration–time curve over the dosing interval, AUCτ) at steady state during combination therapy with exposure when each drug was administered alone. The interaction was summarized using the geometric mean ratio of AUCτ (combination/alone), with 90% confidence intervals.

Given the study’s nonlinear mixed-effects modelling framework, the within-participant comparison design and the limited existing data describing this interaction, a formal sample size calculation based on a single analytic formula was not feasible. Instead, recruitment targets were guided by simulation-based feasibility assessments using previously published population pharmacokinetic models for rifampicin and fusidic acid and Monte Carlo simulations implemented in NONMEM via Perl-Speaks-NONMEM.14 These simulations suggested that ∼8–12 evaluable participants would provide acceptable ability to detect moderate-to-large changes in exposure (∼≥30%) while supporting stable population PK model estimation.

Exploratory data analysis

The temporal relationships between the raw concentrations of the parent drugs fusidic acid and rifampicin, and the rifampicin metabolite, 25-desacetyl rifampicin, were visualized using R version 4.1.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Pharmacokinetic analysis

Concentrations were log-transformed and used to construct the PK model using NONMEM (v.7.5.1, ICON Development Solutions, Ellicott City, MD, USA) with a Gfortran v.4.6.0 compiler, supported by Perl-Speaks-NONMEM (PsN) and Pirana.15

Parameter estimation was conducted using the first-order conditional estimation method with interaction. Graphical presentation was facilitated using R (version 4.1.3, R Core Team 2022), with model diagnostics and graphical evaluation of results supported by the xpose4 package. Statistical significance for adding extra parameters to nested models was evaluated using the objective function value (OFV). A decrease in OFV (ΔOFV) of 3.84 between two nested models differing by a single parameter was considered statistically significant at the 5% level (P value = 0.05).

The base structural models included the volume of distribution of the central compartment (Vc), clearance (CL) and the absorption rate constant (Ka). One- and two-compartment models with first-order elimination from the central compartment were evaluated. Both zero-order and transit absorption models were tested. Once the structural model was finalized, inter-individual variability (IIV) was estimated for parameters where the data supported it. Allometric scaling based on body size was applied a priori, using exponents of 0.75 for clearance and 1.0 for volume of distribution parameters. Inter-occasion variability (IOV) on CL was assessed across different time points (Day 1, Day 2, Days 7–21 and Day 28). Co-variate effects were explored, including C-reactive protein (CRP), albumin, age and haematocrit (HCT) on Ka, and CRP, albumin and age on CL. Various residual error models were evaluated, including additive and combined additive/proportional models.

Model selection was further informed by graphical analyses of goodness-of-fit plots, simulation-based prediction-corrected visual predictive checks (VPC) and credible parameter estimates with satisfactory precision (Tables S1 and S2, available as Supplementary data at JAC Online).

Results

A total of 10 participants, including three females were recruited. The focus of infection included osteoarticular involvement of the axial skeleton (four), large joint septic arthritis (four, including two with PJI), one participant with endovascular infection and one with a small joint infection. The median (interquartile range) age was 59 (48–68) years. The median body mass index was 31 (29–35) kg/m2. At baseline the median creatinine, albumin and CRP concentrations were 78 (63–89) µmol/L, 27 (21–35) g/L and 44 (22–112) mg/dL, respectively.

Two participants experienced transient nausea and one transient dyspepsia while taking the study antimicrobials. There were no major adverse events, and all were able to continue with the prescribed medications for the study period. All participants demonstrated clinical improvement with treatment and there were no clinical treatment failures when assessed at 3 months after enrolment.

Four (40%) were randomized to receive fusidic acid as the index drug, while six (60%) were randomized to receive rifampicin. A total of 96 fusidic acid concentrations, 101 rifampicin concentrations and 96 25-desacetyl rifampicin concentrations were available for modelling.

All four participants receiving fusidic acid as the index drug (Figure 1, top row, teal lines) exhibited elevated plasma concentrations of fusidic acid on Day 2. At Week 4, presumed to represent steady-state conditions, two out of three evaluable participants continued to show elevated fusidic acid plasma concentrations; for these two individuals, the peak plasma concentration (Cmax) was approximately double the values observed on Day 1. Similarly, in the rifampicin index group (Figure 1, top row, red lines), plasma fusidic acid concentrations were elevated at Weeks 3 and 4 for five out of six participants, with one participant not exhibiting this trend. The mean raw observed Cmax for fusidic acid across all participants was 8.68 µg/ml [range 3.26–13.90] on Day 1, 11.97 µg/ml [4.15–30.0] on Day 2 and 21.15 µg/ml [1.23–42.0] at Week 3–4, suggesting a possible overall reduction in fusidic acid clearance. Conversely, in the rifampicin index group (Figure 1 middle row, red lines), slightly elevated rifampicin plasma concentrations were observed on Day 2 for all six participants. However, at steady state, no clear trend in rifampicin plasma concentrations was discernible in either index group (Figure 1, middle row). Plasma concentrations of des-acetylrifampicin, a primary metabolite of rifampicin, were elevated in both index groups (Figure 1, bottom row).

Population pharmacokinetic model for fusidic acid

A one-compartment model with first-order absorption yielded the best model fit. Inter-individual variability (IIV) on CL and Ka was also incorporated into the model. Given the primary hypothesis, IOV on CL was also explored for Day 0, Day 1, Day 7–21 and Day 28, but this did not improve model fit. Similarly, an interaction term was also explored on CL depending on co-administration of rifampicin. This modification improved model fit (ΔOFV −7.718) but led to a 60% reduction in estimated CL. This finding is counterintuitive, as rifampicin is a well-characterized enzyme inducer that would typically be expected to increase, rather than decrease, the clearance of a co-administered substrate such as fusidic acid.

The pharmacokinetic model was subsequently refined to describe the change in fusidic acid CL over time using a time-dependent parameter, rather than including rifampicin co-administration as a co-variate. This change was guided by observations from both the raw data and initial modelling explorations, which suggested a reduction in fusidic acid CL over time. The revised model featuring autoinhibition yielded more biologically plausible parameter estimates. Further model development involved an attempt to stabilize the model and explore potential delayed induction by adding an induction term for CL, proposed to commence after 1 week of treatment. Whereas this addition may have contributed to model stability, the induction term itself was not found to be statistically significant.

The final model for fusidic acid clearance incorporated two opposing, time-dependent effects: an early negative parameter (Day 2) representing autoinhibition and a later positive parameter (Week 1) representing rifampicin-mediated induction (Tables S1 and S2). Despite the late induction effect, the dominant autoinhibition resulted in a net decrease in overall fusidic acid clearance.

The final model population pharmacokinetic parameters are provided in Table 2. IIV variability for both absorption (98.1%) and clearance (77.8%) were high for fusidic acid. The model, which incorporated inhibition and induction parameters, predicted a 4.09-fold [95% CI 1.026, 25.641] increase in the AUC by Day 2. At steady state, this effect was reduced to a 1.76-fold [95% CI 0.049, 34.918] increase; however, this steady-state AUCτ ratio exhibited large variability. The model demonstrated good predictive performance at the individual level (Figure 2). However, at lower concentrations, the observed values aligned less well with the population predictions. The VPC for fusidic acid indicated that the model adequately captured the central tendency, although substantial variability was observed at steady state (Figure 3).

Discussion

In this prospective clinical drug–drug interaction study, we characterized the pharmacokinetics of rifampicin and fusidic acid when co-administered using doses commonly used for staphylococcal infections. In our study, where fusidic acid was dosed at 500 mg three times daily, concentrations of fusidic acid were not reduced by rifampicin, with our model suggesting time-dependent autoinhibition of fusidic acid clearance dominating over potential rifampicin-mediated induction, leading to stable or increased fusidic acid exposure. These findings contrast with the one previous study examining this potential PK interaction, where fusidic acid concentrations were reduced when co-administered with rifampicin.10 Although differences in study design, participants and analytic methods may have contributed to the contradictory findings, a plausible explanation could be the different fusidic acid doses, with a lower, twice-daily dosing used in the previous study. Accumulation with three-times daily dosing of fusidic acid compared with twice-daily dosing has been described previously in older studies investigating fusidic acid PK when used alone.10 In addition, this previous study used high initial loading doses, leading to high early concentrations that may have affected comparative assessment of subsequent concentration.

In our study, fusidic acid did not seem to influence rifampicin clearance at the doses studied. This is not consistent with the one previous study on this topic that we are aware of, a retrospective analysis of patients receiving rifampicin at a dose of 300 mg three times daily for osteoarticular infections. In this study, the use of fusidic acid was a significant co-variate in a population PK model where co-administration of fusidic acid was associated with decreased rifampicin clearance and volume of distribution with resulting increased rifampicin exposure. Again, while there are multiple potential explanations for the different findings between this study and ours, in the context of known nonlinear increase in exposure to rifampicin at higher doses, a potential PK interaction may only have been apparent at the higher doses used in this previous study and not ours.11 Previous studies, in which rifampicin has been used once daily for the treatment of tuberculosis, have demonstrated autoinduction of rifampicin metabolism with a reduction in AUC occurring over the first 2 weeks of treatment.16 It is also possible that autoinduction of rifampicin metabolism was counteracted by inhibition of rifampicin metabolism by fusidic acid, however, we were unable to determine this on the basis of our data.

In our study, fusidic acid was associated with a reduction in clearance of a major metabolite, 25-desacetyl rifampicin, through an unknown mechanism. Although this metabolite is less potent than the parent compound, it potentially contributes to overall antimicrobial activity and toxicity.

As has been seen in previous studies where these drugs have been used alone, we observed marked IIV in the pharmacokinetics of both fusidic acid and rifampicin, particularly in absorption and clearance.17 This variability probably reflects a combination of physiological factors including inflammation, genetic metabolic polymorphisms and underlying medical conditions. Although we attempted to explore these factors as covariates, the modest sample size in this study limited our ability to determine their influence. Regardless, the observed variability highlights that patient specific factors are likely to be as important as drug–drug interactions in determining drug exposure.

This study has several strengths, including the prospective design, rich sampling across multiple treatment phases and simultaneous modelling of both drugs and a primary metabolite. However, important limitations must be acknowledged. The modest sample size limited precision and particularly given the significant inter-individual variability, smaller clinically significant interactions cannot be excluded. In addition, the use of the same dose across all individuals prevented the direct exploration of dose-exposure relationships.

Understanding the pharmacokinetic profile of rifampicin and fusidic acid co-administration is crucial for optimizing treatment regimens and ensuring therapeutic efficacy in patients with staphylococcal infections. The clinical implications of the findings of our study are that when used in combination, three-times daily dosing of fusidic acid and twice-daily dosing of rifampicin appears appropriate. Although this study was not designed to examine clinical outcomes, the lack of clinical failures and good tolerance, along with the findings of previous clinical studies also suggest that this dosing combination is acceptable.7,18 Future studies should explore potential predictors of the observed inter-individual variation and the role of therapeutic drug monitoring as well as the implications of the findings of an interaction affecting 25-desacetyl rifampicin clearance.

In this study in patients with staphylococcal infections treated with rifampicin and fusidic acid, we found that fusidic acid autoinhibition of clearance potentially outweighed rifampicin-mediated induction, resulting in preserved fusidic acid exposure when administered three times daily. Rifampicin clearance was not significantly affected although clearance of a major metabolite was reduced. These findings support the pharmacokinetic compatibility of this clinically important antimicrobial combination.
