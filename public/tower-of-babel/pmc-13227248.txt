Node-level inference and score construction may overstate the diagnostic utility of cervical lymph node ultrasound in Alzheimer’s disease
Authors: Yaohui Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights (for review)•Node-level data were used to support patient-level AD discrimination.•This design raises a risk of pseudoreplication and inflated significance.•The Zone 5 score uses nested predictors and may double-count signals.•Hierarchical reanalysis and external validation are needed.keywords: Alzheimer's disease, Cervical lymph node ultrasound, Glymphatic-lymphatic system, Clustered data analysis, Diagnostic model validation

To the Editor,

We read with great interest the article by Kim et al. describing structural and vascular alterations of deep cervical lymph nodes in amyloid PET-positive Alzheimer’s disease (AD) using high-frequency color Doppler ultrasound [1]. The biological premise of this work is plausible because magnetic resonance imaging has demonstrated drainage from the human brain to cervical lymph nodes in vivo [2]. Experimental ligation of deep cervical lymph nodes has also been shown to aggravate amyloid deposition, neuroinflammation, and cognitive impairment in APP/PS1 mice [3]. More recently, neurodegeneration-related fluid biomarkers have been reported to be enriched in human cervical lymph nodes, further supporting the translational relevance of extracranial lymphatic structures in neurodegenerative disease [4].

However, we believe the current analytic structure may overstate the reported diagnostic utility. The principal concern is a unit-of-analysis mismatch: although the study enrolled 25 patients with AD and 25 cognitively normal controls, much of the inferential framework appears to rely on node-level observations, including 481 total lymph nodes and 153 Zone 5 nodes, to discriminate a patient-level outcome, namely AD status [1]. Multiple lymph nodes sampled from the same individual are clustered rather than statistically independent, and analyses that ignore such clustering can produce misleading inference [5]. In repeated-measures or correlated-data settings, failure to account for within-subject correlation can yield incorrect standard errors and significance testing [6]. Multilevel models and generalized estimating equation approaches are specifically recommended to account for clustering in clinical research settings of this kind [7].

A second concern relates to the proposed Zone 5 scoring system. The score combines nested predictors, including counts of oval-shaped lymph nodes, oval-shaped SMI-positive lymph nodes, and oval-shaped SMI-positive lymph nodes with SMI ≤ 7, so that the same observation can effectively contribute more than once to the final score [1]. Regression-type models that are developed and evaluated in the same small dataset are vulnerable to overfitting and overly optimistic apparent performance [8]. Internal validation procedures are needed to quantify this optimism rather than relying on the apparent performance of the derivation sample alone [9]. Even when internal validation is performed, external validation remains necessary before a prediction model can be considered generalizable across settings and populations [10].

Accordingly, the present findings are best interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than as evidence of a clinically deployable ultrasound biomarker for AD. Reanalysis at the participant level, or re-estimation within a hierarchical framework that explicitly accounts for nodal clustering within individuals, followed by independent validation, would substantially strengthen the translational credibility of this promising work [10].
