Scalable Differentially Private Sketches under Continual Observation
Rayne Holland

Abstract
Sketches are a fundamental tool in data stream analytics. They are notable for supporting both approximate frequency queries and heavy hitter detection with bounded trade-offs for error and memory. Importantly, on streams that contain sensitive information, sketches can be easily privatized with the injection of a suitable amount of noise. This process is efficient in the single-release model, where the output is released only at the end of the stream. In this setting, it suffices to add noise to the sketch once. In contrast, in the continual observation model, where the output is released at every time-step, noise needs to be added to the sketch before each release. This creates an additional computational overhead. To address this, we introduce Lazy Sketch, a novel differentially private sketching method, in the continual observation model, that employs lazy updates, perturbing and modifying only a small portion of the sketch at each step. Compared to prior work, we reduce the update complexity by a factor of O ⁡ ( w ) O(w) , where w w is the width of the sketch. Experiments demonstrate that our method increases throughput by up to 250× over prior work, making continual observation differential privacy practical for high-speed streaming applications. In addition, for heavy hitter detection, we present a new sketch-based algorithm that leverages lazy updates to achieve a per-update complexity of O ⁡ ( d ​ log ⁡ T w + log ⁡ w ) O(d\log\tfrac{T}{w}+\log w) , for sketches with dimension d × w d\times w and streams of length T T . This marks a significant improvement over prior approaches in the streaming continual observation model, which require recomputing frequency estimates for every item in the input domain at each time step.

Introduction

Frequency estimation and heavy hitter detection are foundational problems in data stream processing, where the goal is to summarize item frequencies and identify high-frequency items using limited memory and computational resources.
A widely adopted solution is based on
sketching
, a method that hashes items to a compact array of shared counters.
Sketching enables approximate frequency queries with strong space and accuracy guarantees.
Notable examples include Count-Min Sketch (
\mathsf{CMS}
)
Cormode and Muthukrishnan 2005
and Count Sketch (
\mathsf{CS}
)
Charikar et al. 2002
, which have become standard tools in a range of applications, such as network traffic measurement
Bu et al. 2010
;
Jang et al. 2020
;
Liu et al. 2016
;
Li et al. 2022
;
Roughan and Zhang 2006
;
Yu et al. 2013
.

In privacy-sensitive domains, such as the monitoring of network activity, it is essential to ensure that streaming data structures do not reveal sensitive information.
To ensure privacy in the analysis of data streams,

differential privacy
has emerged as the prevailing standard.
It guarantees that an observer analyzing the outputs of a differentially private algorithm is fundamentally limited, in an information-theoretic sense, in their ability to infer the presence or absence of any individual data point in the stream.

On data streams, differential privacy can be provided under two distinct models:
single release
or
continual observation
.
In the single release setting, the data structure is released only after processing the entire stream.
Sketch-based methods can be privatized efficiently in this model, by injecting calibrated noise into the sketch once, at the beginning of the stream
Pagh and Thorup 2022
;
Zhao et al. 2022
.
These approaches achieve strong privacy-utility trade-offs with minimal computational overhead.

In contrast, the continual observation model requires the algorithm to release the (private) data structure at
every
time step, preserving privacy across the entire output sequence.
This model reflects real-world scenarios where applications need real-time access to private statistics.
However, continual observation poses significant computational and privacy challenges.
To ensure differential privacy for sketch-based methods under continual observation, the current approach is to inject fresh noise into every counter in the sketch at each time step
Epasto et al. 2023
.
This leads to high per-update costs, proportional to the size of the sketch, and quickly becomes impractical for high-throughput or real-time systems.
For heavy hitter detection, the situation is even more acute: a recent method requires querying the entire universe of possible items at each time step to preserve DP
Epasto et al. 2023
.
Thus, existing techniques are infeasible for large input domains or rapid data streams, which are the target applications of sketching methods.

Our Approach

To address these challenges, and reduce this computational overhead, we propose a new sketch-based method for differentially private frequency estimation under continual observation.
Our key idea is to use
lazy updates
– a partial update strategy that modifies and injects noise into only a small, rotating subset of the output sketch at each time step, while leaving the rest of the sketch unchanged.
This amortizes the computational cost of noise injection over time, yielding dramatic improvements in update efficiency.
We also provide an implementation of our method and test it empirically against existing art

Preliminaries

2.1
Privacy

Differential privacy ensures that the inclusion or exclusion of any individual in a dataset has a minimal and bounded impact on the output of a mechanism.
Two streams
\mathcal{X}=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{T}\}
and
\mathcal{X}^{\prime}=\{x_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,x_{T}^{\prime}\}
are
neighboring
,
denoted
\mathcal{X}\sim\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
,
if they differ in one element.
Formally,
\mathcal{X}\sim\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
if there exists a unique
i
such that

x_{i}\neq x_{i}^{\prime}
.
The following definition of differential privacy is adapted from Dwork and Roth
Dwork et al. 2014
.

Definition 1

(
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-Differential Privacy)
.

Let
\mathcal{X},\mathcal{X}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{U}^{*}
be neighboring input streams (i.e., differing in at most one element). A randomized mechanism
\mathcal{M}
satisfies
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-differential privacy under a given observation model if for all measurable sets of outputs
Z\subseteq\mathtt{support}(\mathcal{M})
, it holds that:

\mathds{P}[\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{X})\in Z]\leq e^{\varepsilon}\cdot\mathds{P}[\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{X}^{\prime})\in Z]+\delta.

In streaming there are two types of observation model:
single release
and
continual observation
.
In the single release model, the mechanism processes the stream once and releases a single differentially private output after the final stream update has been observed.
Under continual observation, the mechanism releases an output at each time step, and differential privacy is required to hold for the sequence of outputs up to every time step.

The Gaussian mechanism is a fundamental technique for ensuring
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-differential privacy by adding noise calibrated to a function’s
\ell_{2}
-sensitivity.
Let
\triangle_{2}(f)=\max_{\mathcal{X}\sim\mathcal{X}^{\prime}}\lVert f(\mathcal{X})-f(\mathcal{X}^{\prime})\rVert_{2}
denote the
\ell_{2}
-sensitivity of the function
f
.

Lemma 1

(Gaussian Mechanism
Dwork et al. 2014
)
.

Let
f
be a function with
\ell_{2}
-sensitivity
\triangle_{2}(f)
. For any
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and
\delta\in(0,1)
, the mechanism

\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{X})=f(\mathcal{X})+\mathcal{N}\left(0,\sigma^{2}I\right),

where
\sigma\geq\frac{\triangle_{2}(f)\cdot\sqrt{2\ln(1.25/\delta)}}{\varepsilon}
, satisfies
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-differential privacy.

Fact 1

(Tail bound on the sum of Gaussian RVs)
.

Let
Y=\sum_{i=1}^{h}Y_{i}
, where
Y_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
are i.i.d. Gaussian random variables. Then, for any
\beta\in(0,1)
,

\Pr\left[|Y|\geq\sigma\sqrt{2h\ln(2/\beta)}\right]\leq\beta.

2.2
Sketching

Sketching algorithms enable approximate frequency queries, in small memory, by hashing items to a compact collection of shared counters.
We focus on the well known
\mathsf{CMS}
and
\mathsf{CS}
.
While they share a similar structure, they differ in their update and query procedures and offer different error trade-offs.

Count-Min Sketch

The
\mathsf{CMS}

Cormode and Muthukrishnan 2005
uses
d
hash functions
h_{1},\ldots,h_{d}:\mathcal{U}\rightarrow[w]
to maintain a
d\times w
matrix of counters
C\in(\mathrm{R}^{+})^{d\times w}
.
For each incoming item
x\in\mathcal{U}
with frequency increment
c_{x}
, the update rule is:

C[i,h_{i}(x)]\leftarrow C[i,h_{i}(x)]+c_{x},\quad\text{for all }i\in[d].

This update procedure is illustrated in Figure
1
.
To estimate the frequency of
x
,
\mathsf{CMS}
returns:

\hat{f}_{x}=\min_{i\in[d]}C[i,h_{i}(x)].

This estimator is biased upwards due to hash collisions with other items but offers strong probabilistic error guarantees.

(Approximation error for

\displaystyle\mathds{P}[\hat{f}_{x}-f_{x}>\eta F_{1}]\leq\beta,

Count Sketch

The
\mathsf{CS}

Charikar et al. 2002
modifies the
\mathsf{CMS}
by incorporating randomized signs to reduce bias.
In addition to hash functions
h_{1},\ldots,h_{d}:\mathcal{U}\rightarrow[w]
, it uses a second family of hash functions
g_{1},\ldots,g_{d}:\mathcal{U}\rightarrow\{-1,+1\}
that assign random signs.
For each
x\in\mathcal{U}
, with increment
c_{x}
, the update rule is:

C[i,h_{i}(x)]\leftarrow C[i,h_{i}(x)]+g_{i}(x)\cdot c_{x},\quad\text{for all }i\in[d].

To estimate the frequency of
x
,
\mathsf{CS}
returns:

\hat{f}_{x}=\mathrm{median}_{i\in[d]}\left(g_{i}(x)\cdot C[i,h_{i}(x)]\right).

This estimator is unbiased and has error bounded in terms of the second moment of the frequency vector.

Lemma 3

(Approximation error for
\mathsf{CS}

Charikar et al. 2002
)
.

For
x\in[n]
and any
\beta,\eta\in(0,1)

d=\lceil\ln(1/\beta)\rceil
and table width
\lceil w=3/\eta^{2}\rceil

\Pr\left[|\hat{f}_{x}-f_{x}|>\eta\sqrt{F_{2}/}\right]\leq\beta,

where
F_{2}=\sum_{y\in[n]}f_{y}^{2}
is the second moment of the frequency vector.

Differential Privacy in the Single Release Model

For private release, sketches must have similar distributions on neighboring streams.
In the single release model, we sample a random vector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times w}
independent of the data and release
C(X)+v

Pagh and Thorup 2022
;
Zhao et al. 2022
.
The parameters of the sampling distribution depend on the sensitivity of the sketch.
Since sketches are linear, for neighboring inputs
X\sim X^{\prime}
, we have
C(X)-C(X^{\prime})=C(X-X^{\prime})
.
As neighboring inputs have sensitivity
\Delta_{2}(X-X^{\prime})=1
,
a sketch
C
has sensitivity proportional to its number of rows.
Therefore,
\Delta_{2}(C)=\sqrt{2d}
and
C(X)+v
achieves
\varepsilon
-differential privacy for
v\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma)^{d\times w}
, with
\sigma=\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon}\sqrt{4d\ln(1.25/\delta)}
by Lemma
1
.

Heavy Hitter Detection

A heavy hitter is an item whose frequency significantly exceeds that of others.
Formally, a
(k,\ell_{p})
-heavy hitter is an element
x
such that
|f_{x}|\geq\|f\|_{p}/k
.
Sketches provide efficient methods for identifying heavy hitters under sublinear memory.
The
\mathsf{CMS}
is effective for detecting
\ell_{1}
-heavy hitters due to its additive error bounded in terms of
F_{1}
.
In contrast,
\mathsf{CS}
is suitable for identifying
\ell_{2}
-heavy hitters, as its estimation error scales with
\sqrt{F_{2}/w}
, making it more resilient to skewed or high-variance frequency distributions.

To efficiently maintain the approximate heavy hitters during a stream, a heap-based approach is adopted.
Here, a min-heap of size
k
is maintained over estimated frequencies
\hat{f}_{x}
from the sketch.
On each update, if
x
is in the heap, we update its value, and if not, we insert it if
\hat{f}_{x}
exceeds the current minimum.
Heavy hitters are reported as elements in the heap with
|\hat{f}_{x}|\geq\|f\|_{p}/k
.

The Gaussian Binary Mechanism

We introduce the Gaussian Binary Mechanism (
\mathsf{GaussBM})
, a variation of the standard binary mechanism that replaces Laplace noise with Gaussian noise.
This mechanism serves as the core tool for solving the
n
-counters problem – a generalization of the standard summing problem
Andersson and Pagh 2023
;
Fichtenberger et al. 2023
;
Henzinger et al. 2024
.
We will use the
n
-counters problem to formalize sketching in the continual observation model as a structured counting task.
Lastly, we derive concrete utility bounds, for the collection of counters, that will support our approach to identifying heavy hitters under continual observation.

In the continual observation model, differentially private counters must release estimates at every timestep while preserving privacy across all intermediate outputs.
Unlike the single release model, which injects noise only once, continual observation counters add noise at each update.
This introduces the challenge of minimizing the cumulative error over time.

A standard approach is the
binary mechanism

Chan et al. 2011
;
Dwork et al. 2010
, which organizes the stream of updates into a binary tree structure.
Each update is represented by a leaf node and internal nodes correspond to the intervals comprised of all descendant leaf nodes.
An illustration of this binary tree structure is given in Figure
2
.
A query for the value in the counter at time
t
can be constructed from the tree by aggregating the noisy counts of intervals that partition
[1,t]
.
This reduces noise relative to a naive approach that is equivalent to summing all the noisy counts in the leaf nodes.

The binary mechanism with Gaussian noise, detailed in Algorithm

c
at each time step
t
as a leaf node with fresh Gaussian noise.
It then merges tree nodes whose time intervals form complete binary intervals ending at
t

Heavy Hitters

A
\mathsf{LazySketch}
is
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-differentially private for
all

It can be released to the public at each time step and provide unlimited queries without breaking privacy.
Thus, it can be trivially used to construct a set of heavy hitters at each update by querying the frequency estimate of every item in the universe and outputting the items with the “heavy” estimates.
This is the approach adopted by
Epasto et al. 2023
.
However, if the universe is large, as is standard in streaming scenarios, the computational cost of an update is prohibitive.

To reduce the update cost, we are going to make two changes.
First, we introduce lazy updates and only change the set of heavy hitters periodically.
Second, we maintain a small set of
high frequency
candidates that are used to inform the output.
Maintaining the set of candidates, without breaking privacy, is a non-trivial task.
This is because the candidates influence the likelihood of the output.
For example, on neighboring streams
X
and
X^{\prime}
, which differ at
x_{l}\neq x_{l}^{\prime}

x_{l}
is in the candidates for
X
but not
X^{\prime}

to appear in the output.
This has the potential to break privacy.

To overcome this challenge, we construct a threshold that both admits heavy hitters and is large enough to suppress privacy leakage events with high probability.
Let
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}

X
and
X^{\prime}
.
The candidate sets are constructed such that an element

in the set difference must have a “small” frequency estimate on stream
X^{\prime}
.
Thus, if the threshold at the next update is high enough
y
will not appear as a heavy hitter on stream
X
.

We parameterize the threshold to allow a trade-off between performance and coverage.
For the
k
-heavy hitters problem, we introduce a parameter
\tilde{k}>k
that denotes the size of the candidate set in the data structure.
Intuitively, for a larger candidate set, items outside the candidate set are less likely to be true heavy hitters.
This allows us to the lower the threshold and increase coverage at the cost of storing and evaluating more candidates.

both the heavy hitters and the candidates require
\mathcal{O}(\tilde{k})
words of memory.
Thus, the memory cost is dominated by the allocation of the
\mathsf{LazyCMS}
.
This completes the proof.
∎

6
Experiments

In this paper, we have proposed a novel differentially private sketch, designed for the continual observation model.
It is aimed at improving throughput relative to existing art.
To evaluate this claim empirically, we have implemented a suite of data structures and performed a comprehensive analysis of their privacy, utility, and performance trade-offs.

6.1
Implemented Sketches

The following sketches are implemented and evaluated for frequency estimation:
\mathsf{CMS}
,
\mathsf{CS}
,
\mathsf{LazyCMS}
,
\mathsf{LazyCS}
,
\mathsf{PunctualCMS}
, and
\mathsf{PunctualCS}
.

•

\mathsf{CMS}
and
\mathsf{CS}
serve as non-private baselines.

•

The
Lazy
variants (
\mathsf{LazyCMS}
,
\mathsf{LazyCS}
) represent our proposed differentially private method.

•

The
Punctual
variants (
\mathsf{PunctualCMS}
,
\mathsf{PunctualCS}
) correspond to state-of-the-art differentially private sketches based on the Binary Mechanism.

All sketches are implemented with a fixed depth of
d=3
hash functions.
An array of Binary Mechanism counters is realized using a preallocated, fixed-size two-dimensional array, where each cell contains one node per level of the full binary tree, enabling logarithmic-time updates in the stream length.

For heavy hitter detection, we implement
\mathsf{LazyHH}
, which uses
\mathsf{LazyCMS}
to track heavy items.
We do not implement and test the heavy hitter algorithm of
Epasto et al. 2023
as it is computational infeasible and requires generating a frequency estimate for every item in the universe at each update.
As a baseline, we compare our approach with a non-private method that uses a
\mathsf{CMS}
and a heap to track heavy items.

6.2
Datasets and Metrics

Synthetic Data.

To evaluate frequency estimation, we generate synthetic data streams following Zipfian distributions with a varying skew parameter.
Each synthetic stream consists of
2^{20}
updates. The privacy parameter
\delta
is fixed to
0.001
across all experiments.

We vary three key parameters in our experiments:
The privacy parameter
\varepsilon
;
the sketch width and sketch depth (memory);
the skew of the input distribution (for synthetic data generation).
For evaluation we record:

•

Runtime: Average time taken to process all
2^{20}
stream updates.

•

Accuracy: Measured as
Average Relative Error (ARE)
for the top-
15
most frequent items in the stream:

\mathrm{ARE}=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{|f_{i}-\hat{f}_{i}|}{f_{i}}

where
f_{i}
is the true frequency and
\hat{f}_{i}
is the estimated frequency of the
i
-th heavy item.

Real-World Data.

For heavy hitter detection, we use anonymized traffic traces from the CAIDA 2019 dataset
3
3

3

https://www.caida.org/data/passive/passive_2019_dataset.xml
.
The data consists of approximately
26.6
million total source IP addresses and around
312{,}000
distinct source IPs.
For all experiments we set
k=128
.
We vary two key parameters:
memory, by changing
\tilde{k}
for
\mathsf{LazyHH}
and changing the width for the
\mathsf{CMS}
(and keeping
k
constant); and privacy, by changing
\varepsilon
.

For evaluation, we report:

•

ARE: As defined above, for heavy hitters returned by each sketch.

•

Precision: The proportion of reported items that are actual heavy hitters:

\mathrm{Precision}=\frac{|\text{Reported}\cap\text{True}|}{|\text{Reported}|}

•

\mathrm{Recall}=\frac{|\text{Reported}\cap\text{True}|}{|\text{True}|}

6

While this threshold is theoretically polylogarithmic in the stream length, in practical settings it is essential that it remains low enough to allow meaningful detection of frequent items.
Therefore, recall is a key metric in these experiments.

We begin by looking at frequency estimation for differentially private sketches.
A key contribution of our work is improving the throughput of existing work with the use of lazy updates.
We validate this contribution here, and observe additional benefits of our approach, such as improved utility.

Since the continual observation sketches implement the
\mathsf{GaussBM}

This results in significantly higher per-counter memory usage than in traditional sketches.
Consequently, for a fixed memory budget, relative to the non-private sketches, the sketch width (i.e., the number of columns) is lower for the private variants.
Furthermore, in the
\mathsf{LazySketch}

\mathsf{GaussBM}

T/w

This results in a smaller counter size compared with the
\mathsf{PunctualSketch}

Thus, it has a larger sketch width under the same memory budget.
For instance, with a 24KB memory allocation, the widths for
\mathsf{CMS}

\mathsf{LazyCMS}

\mathsf{PunctualCMS}
are 2000, 55, and 33, respectively.

\mathsf{PunctualSketch}

7
Prior Work

Differentially private sketches are motivated by the need to perform sensitive streaming analytics under formal privacy guarantees.
These methods adapt classical sketching techniques to meet the requirements of differential privacy.
In the 1-pass model, it is sufficient to inject a suitably calibrated noise into the sketch at the beginning of the stream.
Prior work has used Gaussian noise
Pagh and Thorup 2022
;
Zhao et al. 2022
and randomized response
Bassily et al. 2017
.
In the continual observation model, the current approach is to use a continual observation counter in each cell in the sketch
Epasto et al. 2023
.
However, to maintain privacy, this requires updating every cell in the sketch at each stream arrival.
Reducing this computational overhead is the main motivation for this work.

Recently, differentially private sketches have seen increasing application in a range of privacy-preserving data analysis tasks, including synthetic data generation
Holland et al. 2024
, quantile estimation
Zhao et al. 2022
, and heavy hitter detection
Bassily et al. 2017
.
These efforts demonstrate the importance of sketches as a foundation for scalable private analytics.

As an alternative to linear sketches, the
\mathsf{MisraGries}
algorithm
Misra and Gries 1982
can be privatized
Biswas et al. 2024
;
Chan et al. 2011
;
Lebeda and Tetek 2023
and used for frequency and heavy hitter approximation.

\mathsf{MisraGries}
is a counter-based method that maintains up to
w
key-value pairs
(x,c)
, tracking frequent items and their approximate counts.

Unlike sketch-based methods, privatizing
\mathsf{MisraGries}
is non-trivial: simply adding noise to counters is insufficient because the
identities
of tracked items can change between neighboring inputs, making the output distinguishable.
In the 1-pass model, this issue is formalized by Lebeda and Tetek
Lebeda and Tetek 2023
, who show that
\mathsf{MisraGries}
can expose up to two
isolated elements
(tracked items that differ between two neighboring streams).
This implies that while isolated elements exist, the instability is limited and can be obfuscated with noise.
Moreover, since these elements typically appear with very low counts (at most one), they can be suppressed using thresholding techniques (as in stability histograms), mitigating privacy leakage with high probability.

However, this idea cannot be easily extended to the continual observation setting.
The reason being that the identity of isolated elements can change as the stream progresses and an adversary (with access to neighboring datasets) can track these changes using the deterministic
\mathsf{MisraGries}
.
Thus, the sensitivity of the outputs cannot be bound in any useful way.

8
Conclusion

We have presented a new framework for sketch-based frequency estimation and heavy hitter detection that satisfies differential privacy under the challenging continual observation model.
Our approach is built around a novel
lazy update
mechanism, which selectively injects noise into a small, rotating portion of the sketch at each time step.
This design choice dramatically reduces per-update computational overhead while maintaining strong utility and privacy guarantees.

Through the design and implementation of the
\mathsf{LazySketch}
and
\mathsf{LazyHH}
algorithms, we demonstrate that it is possible to achieve both high throughput and high utility in privacy-sensitive streaming applications.
In particular, our experiments show that
\mathsf{LazySketch}
improves update throughput by a factor of 250 compared to the state-of-the-art, while also offering better or comparable accuracy across a wide range of parameter settings.
For heavy hitter detection,
\mathsf{LazyHH}
is the first differentially private sketching method under continual observation to achieve perfect recall on real-world data with modest memory and strict privacy constraints.
Thus, our results indicate that sketching under continual observation can be made efficient and accurate with principled algorithmic innovations.
