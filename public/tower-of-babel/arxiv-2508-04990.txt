Generalization of anomaly formula for time reversal symmetry in (2+1)D abelian bosonic TQFTs
Ippo Orii

Abstract
We study time-reversal symmetry in ( 2 + 1 ) (2+1) D abelian bosonic topological phases. Time-reversal anomalies in such systems are classified by ℤ 2 × ℤ 2 \mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2} symmetry-protected topological (SPT) phases in ( 3 + 1 ) (3+1) D, and can be diagnosed via partition functions on manifolds such as ℝ ​ ℙ 4 \mathbb{RP}^{4} and ℂ ​ ℙ 2 \mathbb{CP}^{2} . These partition functions are related by the anomaly formula Z ⁡ ( ℝ ​ ℙ 4 ) ​ Z ​ ( ℂ ​ ℙ 2 ) = θ ℳ , Z(\mathbb{RP}^{4})\,Z(\mathbb{CP}^{2})=\theta_{\mathcal{M}}, where θ ℳ \theta_{\mathcal{M}} is the Dehn twist phase associated with the crosscap state. Meanwhile, the existence of gapped boundaries is constrained by so-called higher central charges ξ n \xi_{n} , which serve as computable invariants encoding obstruction data. Motivated by the known relation Z ⁡ ( ℂ ​ ℙ 2 ) = ξ 1 Z(\mathbb{CP}^{2})=\xi_{1} , we propose a generalization of the anomaly formula that involves both the higher central charges ξ n \xi_{n} and a new time-reversal invariant η n \eta_{n} . Introducing a distinguished subset ℳ n ⊂ 𝒜 \mathcal{M}^{n}\subset\mathcal{A} of anyons, we establish the relation η n ⋅ ξ n = ∑ a ∈ ℳ n θ ​ ( a ) n | ∑ a ∈ ℳ n θ ​ ( a ) n | , \eta_{n}\cdot\xi_{n}=\frac{\sum_{a\in\mathcal{M}^{n}}\theta(a)^{n}}{\left|\sum_{a\in\mathcal{M}^{n}}\theta(a)^{n}\right|}, which generalizes the known anomaly formula. We analyze the algebraic structure of ℳ n \mathcal{M}^{n} , derive consistency relations it satisfies, and clarify its connection to the original anomaly formula.

4
Gapped boundary

4.1
Lagrangian subgroup

Strictly adhering to the axioms, one typically associates Hilbert spaces only to closed two-dimensional surfaces. Within this formalism, incorporating spacelike boundaries is subtle, as such boundaries do not naturally fit into the standard TQFT framework. Nonetheless, it is both natural and physically relevant to consider systems with spacelike boundaries and to investigate their properties. When the boundary itself admits a topological description—that is, when no gapless degrees of freedom appear along the boundary—we refer to it as a
gapped boundary
.

Determining whether a given topological phase admits a gapped boundary is one of central questions in the study of topological phases. For abelian systems, a necessary and sufficient condition for the existence of a gapped boundary is known
[
KS11
,
FSV13
]
:

A gapped boundary exists if and only if there exists a Lagrangian subgroup
L\subset\mathcal{A}
.

Here, a
Lagrangian subgroup
is defined as follows:

A subgroup
L\subset\mathcal{A}
satisfying:

•

\theta(a)=1
for all
a\in L
,

•

B(a,b)=1
for all
a,b\in L
,

•

|L|^{2}=|\mathcal{A}|
,

•

For every
a\in\mathcal{A}\setminus L
, there exists at least one
b\in L
such that
B(a,b)\neq 1
.

Roughly speaking, a gapped boundary can be viewed as an interface between a nontrivial TQFT and a trivial TQFT. A Lagrangian subgroup is a set of anyons whose condensation effectively trivializes the theory. Therefore, if the anyon system admits such a subgroup, one can obtain a gapped boundary by condensing the corresponding anyons in a subregion of spacetime.

Thus, verifying the existence of a gapped boundary reduces to identifying a Lagrangian subgroup. However, finding such a subgroup is generally nontrivial. Therefore, it is often useful to employ computable invariants as practical criteria for detecting the existence of gapped boundaries as we introduce in the next subsections.

\mathcal{M}=\mathrm{Im}(1+\mathsf{T}).

Then, we easily see that

\theta_{\mathcal{M}}=1

since for any
a\in\mathcal{A}
, we have

\displaystyle\theta_{\mathcal{M}}

\displaystyle=\theta((1+\mathsf{T})a)

\displaystyle=B(a,\mathsf{T}a)\,\theta(a)\,\theta(\mathsf{T}a)

\displaystyle=\eta((1+\mathsf{T})a)^{-1}

\displaystyle=1.

Here, we use the consistency condition
B
.

Now, define

L:=\mathrm{Im}(1+\mathsf{T})=\Ker(1-\mathsf{T})=\mathcal{M}.

Then, the first and second conditions for the lagrangian subgroup follow immediately from
\theta_{\mathcal{M}}=1
and the orthogonality
\Ker(1-\mathsf{T})\perp\mathrm{Im}(1-\mathsf{T})
.

The third condition follows from the identity

|\mathcal{A}|=|\Ker(1-\mathsf{T})|\cdot|\mathrm{Im}(1-\mathsf{T})|.

As for the fourth condition: for all
a\in\mathcal{A}\setminus\mathrm{Im}(1+\mathsf{T})=\mathcal{A}\setminus L
, there exists
b\in L=\Ker(1-\mathsf{T})
such that
B(a,b)\neq 1
. Otherwise, if
B(a,b)=1
for all
b\in\Ker(1-\mathsf{T})
, it would imply
a\in\Ker(1-\mathsf{T})^{\perp}=\mathrm{Im}(1+\mathsf{T})
, contradicting
a\notin\mathrm{Im}(1+\mathsf{T})
.

This completes the verification.

4.2
Chiral central charge and Higher central charge

We have already introduced the chiral central charge as part of the input data for abelian systems. It is also known to serve as an obstruction to the existence of a gapped boundary:

A necessary condition for the existence of a gapped boundary is that

c_{-}\equiv 0\pmod{8}.

In the presence of time-reversal symmetry, it is known that
c_{-}
must take values in
0
or
4\pmod{8}
. This can be seen as follows:

\displaystyle e^{2\pi ic_{-}/8}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{A}|^{1/2}}\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\theta(a)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{A}|^{1/2}}\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\theta(\mathsf{T}a)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{A}|^{1/2}}\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\overline{\theta(a)}

\displaystyle=e^{-2\pi ic_{-}/8},

which implies that

c_{-}\equiv 0\text{ or }4\pmod{8}.

This is a well-known constraint for the existence of time-reversal symmetry in bosonic systems.

7

7

For a detailed discussion of the chiral central charge in time-reversal invariant systems, see
[
Kob21
]
.

In particular, when
|\mathcal{A}|
is odd, it is evident that

c_{-}\equiv 0\pmod{8}

since such theories can be realized as gauge theories
[

]
.
However, the condition above is
necessary
, but not
sufficient
. Recently, a new computable invariant called the
Higher central charge
was introduced. This invariant was first defined in the mathematical literature
[
NSW19
,
Ng+22
]
, and later given a physical interpretation as a criterion for the existence of a gapped boundary in
[
Kai+22
]
, thereby providing a necessary and sufficient condition for the existence of a gapped boundary.

Its definition is as follows:

\xi_{n}\coloneqq\frac{\sum\limits_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\theta(a)^{n}}{\bigl|\sum\limits_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\theta(a)^{n}\bigr|},

where
n\in\mathbb{N}
satisfies

\gcd\left(n,\frac{2|\mathcal{A}|}{\gcd(n,2|\mathcal{A}|)}\right)=1.

The key result is the following:

An abelian TQFT admits a gapped boundary if and only if
\xi_{n}
is trivial for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
satisfying

\gcd\left(n,\frac{2|\mathcal{A}|}{\gcd(n,2|\mathcal{A}|)}\right)=1.

Since

\xi_{n}=\xi_{n+2|\mathcal{A}|},

only finitely many higher central charges need to be computed. These invariants thus provide a practical and computable alternative to the known criterion based on Lagrangian subgroups.
Later in this paper, we will denote

\mathbb{N}_{c}\coloneqq\left\{n\in\mathbb{N}\ \middle|\ \gcd\left(n,\frac{2|\mathcal{A}|}{\gcd(n,2|\mathcal{A}|)}\right)=1\right\},

That is, the higher central charge
\xi_{n}
is defined only for
n\in\mathbb{N}_{c}

5.1
Statement of formula

Recall the anomaly formula
[

]

Z(\mathbb{RP}^{4})\,Z(\mathbb{CP}^{2})=\theta_{\mathcal{M}}.

(5.1)

Here, both

and
Z(\mathbb{CP}^{2})
take values in
\{\pm 1\}
. In general, the time-reversal anomaly of 2+1-dimensional abelian bosonic systems is characterized by abelian bosonic SPT phases in 3+1-dimensional spacetime with time-reversal symmetry. These SPT phases are believed to be classified by

We can equivalently rewrite the left hand side of this equation as:

\frac{\sum\limits_{a\in\Ker(1-\mathsf{T})}\theta(a)\eta(a)}{\left|\sum\limits_{a\in\Ker(1-\mathsf{T})}\theta(a)\eta(a)\right|}\cdot\frac{\sum\limits_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\theta(a)}{\left|\sum\limits_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\theta(a)\right|}.

We now consider an analogue of the ordinary anomaly formula involving the higher central charge:

\xi_{n}\coloneqq\frac{\sum\limits_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\theta(a)^{n}}{\left|\sum\limits_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\theta(a)^{n}\right|},

and define

\eta_{n}\coloneqq\frac{\sum\limits_{a\in\Ker(1-\mathsf{T})}\big(\theta(a)\,\eta(a)\big)^{n}}{\left|\sum\limits_{a\in\Ker(1-\mathsf{T})}\big(\theta(a)\,\eta(a)\big)^{n}\right|}.

We then consider the product
\eta_{n}\cdot\xi_{n}
as the generalized anomaly formula. We find the following relation:

\eta_{n}\cdot\xi_{n}=\frac{\sum\limits_{a\in\mathcal{E}^{n}}\theta(a)^{n}}{\left|\sum\limits_{a\in\mathcal{E}^{n}}\theta(a)^{n}\right|}.

(5.2)

Here,
\mathcal{E}^{n}
is defined by

\mathcal{E}^{n}\coloneqq\text{$\frac{\Ima^{n}(1+\mathsf{T})}{\Ima(1+\mathsf{T})}$-torsor}.

satisfying

B(a,nb)=\eta(nb)\quad\text{for all }a\in\mathcal{E}^{n}.

Before arriving at the final formula, we first define
\mathcal{M}^{n}
as follows:

\mathcal{M}^{n}\coloneqq\left\{a\in\mathcal{A}\mid B(a,nb)=\eta(nb)\quad\text{for all }b\in\Ker(1-\mathsf{T})\right\}.

We will first derive a formula involving
\mathcal{M}^{n}
, and subsequently extract the crucial subset
\mathcal{E}^{n}
from it.

Here,
n
is taken from the set
\mathbb{N}_{c}
, which we introduced in
4.2
. Note that this generalized anomaly formula, like

, is defined for

.

5.2
Derivation of formula

As a preparation for the derivation, we will show the following three facts:

•

The quantity
\eta_{n}
takes the form

\eta_{n}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }n\text{ is even},\\
Z(\mathbb{RP}^{4})&\text{if }n\text{ is odd}.\end{cases}

(5.3)

•

\left|\sum_{a\in\Ker(1-\mathsf{T})}\big(\theta(a)\,\eta(a)\big)^{n}\right|=|\Ker(1-\mathsf{T})|.

(5.4)

•

The quantity
\widetilde{M}_{n,a}
, defined by

\widetilde{M}_{n,a}\coloneqq\sum_{b\in\Ker(1-\mathsf{T})}B(-a,nb)\eta(nb),

(5.5)

satisfies

\widetilde{M}_{n,a}=\begin{cases}|\Ker(1-\mathsf{T})|&\text{if }a\in\mathcal{M}^{n},\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(5.6)

(
5.3
) :

When
n
is even, (say
n=2n^{\prime}
):

\big(\theta(a)\eta(a)\big)^{2n^{\prime}}=\big(\theta(a)^{2}\eta(a)^{2}\big)^{n}=\big(\theta(a)\theta(\mathsf{T}a)\big)^{n}=1.

When
n
is odd, (say
n=2n^{\prime}+1
):

\big(\theta(a)\eta(a)\big)^{2n^{\prime}+1}=\big(\theta(a)\eta(a)\big)^{2n^{\prime}}\theta(a)\eta(a)=\theta(a)\eta(a).

Therefore, we conclude the proposition.

(
5.4
) :

When
n
is even, the expression reduces to

\left|\sum_{a\in\Ker(1-\mathsf{T})}\big(\theta(a)\,\eta(a)\big)^{n}\right|=\sum_{a\in\Ker(1-\mathsf{T})}1=|\Ker(1-\mathsf{T})|.

When
n
is odd, it reduces to

\left|\sum_{a\in\Ker(1-\mathsf{T})}\big(\theta(a)\,\eta(a)\big)^{n}\right|=\left|\sum_{a\in\Ker(1-\mathsf{T})}\theta(a)\,\eta(a)\right|=|\Ker(1-\mathsf{T})|\cdot|Z(\mathbb{RP}^{4})|=|\Ker(1-\mathsf{T})|.

(
5.6
) :

We use the following fact:

Define functions
\rho^{n}_{B,a}\colon\Ker(1-\mathsf{T})\to U(1)
and
\rho^{n}_{\eta}\colon\Ker(1-\mathsf{T})\to\{\pm 1\}
by

\displaystyle\rho^{n}_{B,a}(b)

\displaystyle\coloneqq B(a,nb),

\displaystyle\rho^{n}_{\eta}(b)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\eta(nb).

Then, these are homomorphisms and thus define one-dimensional representations (i.e., characters) of the finite abelian group
\Ker(1-\mathsf{T})
.

We can rewrite the expression for
\widetilde{M}_{n,a}
as

\widetilde{M}_{n,a}=\sum_{b\in\Ker(1-\mathsf{T})}\overline{\rho^{n}_{B,a}(b)}\,\rho^{n}_{\eta}(b).

Using the orthogonality of characters, we find:

\widetilde{M}_{n,a}=\begin{cases}|\Ker(1-\mathsf{T})|&\text{if }\rho^{n}_{B,a}=\rho^{n}_{\eta},\\
0&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(5.7)

which holds because the characters form an orthonormal basis under the standard pairing.

Recalling the definitions:

\rho^{n}_{B,a}(b)=B(a,nb),\qquad\rho^{n}_{\eta}(b)=\eta(nb),

we see that
\rho^{n}_{B,a}=\rho^{n}_{\eta}
if and only if

B(a,nb)=\eta(nb)\quad\text{for all }b\in\Ker(1-\mathsf{T}),

i.e.,
a\in\mathcal{M}^{n}
. Therefore, we obtain:

\widetilde{M}_{n,a}=\begin{cases}|\Ker(1-\mathsf{T})|&\text{if }a\in\mathcal{M}^{n},\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(5.8)

We now derive the following formula:

\eta_{n}\cdot\xi_{n}=\frac{\sum\limits_{a\in\mathcal{M}^{n}}\theta(a)^{n}}{\left|\sum\limits_{a\in\mathcal{M}^{n}}\theta(a)^{n}\right|}.

To derive this, we use the following two facts shown above:

•

\left|\sum_{a\in\Ker(1-\mathsf{T})}\big(\theta(a)\,\eta(a)\big)^{n}\right|=|\Ker(1-\mathsf{T})|

•

\widetilde{M}_{n,a}=\begin{cases}|\Ker(1-\mathsf{T})|&\text{if }a\in\mathcal{M}^{n}\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

We now compute
\eta_{n}\cdot\xi_{n}
explicitly:

\displaystyle\eta_{n}\cdot\xi_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum\limits_{a\in\Ker(1-\mathsf{T})}\big(\theta(a)\,\eta(a)\big)^{n}}{\left|\sum\limits_{a\in\Ker(1-\mathsf{T})}\big(\theta(a)\,\eta(a)\big)^{n}\right|}\cdot\frac{\sum\limits_{b\in\mathcal{A}}\theta(b)^{n}}{\left|\sum\limits_{b\in\mathcal{A}}\theta(b)^{n}\right|}

(5.9)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\Ker(1-\mathsf{T})|\cdot\left|\sum\limits_{b\in\mathcal{A}}\theta(b)^{n}\right|}\sum_{a\in\Ker(1-\mathsf{T})}\sum_{b\in\mathcal{A}}\big(\theta(a)\,\eta(a)\,\theta(b)\big)^{n}.

Now observe:

\displaystyle\sum_{a,b\in\mathcal{A}}\big(\theta(a)\,\eta(a)\,\theta(b)\big)^{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{a,b\in\mathcal{A}}\big(\theta(a+b)\,B(-a,b)\,\eta(a)\big)^{n}

(5.10)

\displaystyle=\sum_{a,c\in\mathcal{A}}\big(\theta(c)\,B(-a,c-a)\,\eta(a)\big)^{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{a,c\in\mathcal{A}}\big(\theta(c)\,B(a,a)\,B(-c,a)\,\eta(a)\big)^{n}.

Note that

\displaystyle B(a,a)^{n}

\displaystyle=B(a,na)

(5.11)

\displaystyle=B(a,\mathsf{T}na)

\displaystyle=B(a,\mathsf{T}a)^{n}

\displaystyle=\eta\big(-(1+\mathsf{T})a\big)^{n}

\displaystyle=\eta\big(-n(1+\mathsf{T})a\big)

since
a\in\Ker(1-\mathsf{T})
, i.e.,
\mathsf{T}na=na
.

Therefore, we obtain:

\displaystyle\sum_{a,b\in\mathcal{A}}\big(\theta(a)\,\eta(a)\,\theta(b)\big)^{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{a,b\in\mathcal{A}}\big(\theta(a+b)\,B(-a,b)\,\eta(a)\big)^{n}

(5.12)

\displaystyle=\sum_{a,c\in\mathcal{A}}\theta(c)^{n}B(-c,na)\eta(na)\eta\big(-n(1+\mathsf{T})a\big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{a,c\in\mathcal{A}}\theta(c)^{n}B(-c,na)\eta(-\mathsf{T}na)

\displaystyle=\sum_{c\in\mathcal{A}}\theta(c)^{n}\sum_{a\in\Ker(1-\mathsf{T})}B(-c,na)\eta(na)

\displaystyle=\sum_{c\in\mathcal{A}}\theta(c)^{n}\cdot\widetilde{M}_{n,c}

\displaystyle=|\Ker(1-\mathsf{T})|\sum_{c\in\mathcal{M}^{n}}\theta(c)^{n}

where we used the explicit form of
\widetilde{M}_{n,c}
from (
5.8
).

Substituting this back into (

), we conclude:

\displaystyle\eta_{n}\cdot\xi_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\Ker(1-\mathsf{T})|\cdot\left|\sum\limits_{b\in\mathcal{A}}\theta(b)^{n}\right|}\cdot|\Ker(1-\mathsf{T})|\sum_{c\in\mathcal{M}^{n}}\theta(c)^{n}

(5.13)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum\limits_{c\in\mathcal{M}^{n}}\theta(c)^{n}}{\left|\sum\limits_{b\in\mathcal{A}}\theta(b)^{n}\right|}.

Note that we have

\left|\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\theta(a)^{n}\right|=\left|\sum_{a\in\mathcal{M}^{n}}\theta(a)^{n}\right|

using this equation (
5.13
), we obtain

\eta_{n}\cdot\xi_{n}=\frac{\sum\limits_{a\in\mathcal{M}^{n}}\theta(a)^{n}}{\left|\sum\limits_{a\in\mathcal{M}^{n}}\theta(a)^{n}\right|}.

This completes half of the derivation.

5.3
Insight from Dimensions

Before proceeding to analyze the detailed structure of

]

\dim\left(V\big(\Sigma_{n}(a_{1},\dots,a_{k})\big)\right)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{A}|^{\frac{2-k}{2}}}\sum_{b\in\Ker(1-\mathsf{T})}B\big(-(a_{1}+\cdots+a_{k}),b\big)\,\eta(nb),

n

k

a_{1},\dots,a_{k}
.

If we impose the constraint

then the formula becomes

\dim\left(V\big(\Sigma_{n}(a_{1},\dots,a_{k})\big)\right)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{A}|^{\frac{2-k}{2}}}\sum_{b\in\Ker(1-\mathsf{T})}B(-a,nb)\,\eta(nb)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{A}|^{\frac{2-k}{2}}}\widetilde{M}_{n,a}.

From this point of view, we can identify
\mathcal{M}^{n}
as the subset

\mathcal{M}^{n}=\left\{a\in\mathcal{A}\;\middle|\;\dim\left(V\big(\Sigma_{n}(na)\big)\right)\neq 0\right\}.

In fact, we can generalize this statement slightly. Let us define
\Sigma_{n,m}(a_{1},\dots,a_{k})
as a non-orientable surface with
m
crosscaps and
k
punctures labeled by
a_{1},\dots,a_{k}
, subject to the constraints:

\displaystyle a_{1}+\cdots+a_{k}

\displaystyle=na,

\displaystyle n

\displaystyle\equiv m\mod 2.

Then, since
\eta(a)\in\{\pm 1\}
, it follows that

\left\{a\in\mathcal{A}\;\middle|\;\dim\left(V\big(\Sigma_{n}(a_{1},\dots,a_{k})\big)\right)\neq 0\right\}=\left\{a\in\mathcal{A}\;\middle|\;\dim\left(V\big(\Sigma_{n,m}(a_{1},\dots,a_{k})\big)\right)\neq 0\right\}.

(5.14)

Recall when

:

In our previous work
[

]
, we proved that

\dim\left(V\big(\Sigma_{1}(a)\big)\right)=\dim\left(V\big(\mathbb{RP}^{2}(a)\big)\right)\neq 0

, and established several results that we review in Sec. 2. In this sense, the set

The fact modulo 2:

The result stated in (

[

, two crosscaps can be replaced with a handle, provided that the overall non-orientability of the surface is preserved. For example,

\displaystyle\mathbb{RP}^{2}\#\mathbb{RP}^{2}\#\mathbb{RP}^{2}

5.4
Structure of
\mathcal{M}^{n}

Expression of
\mathcal{M}^{n}
:

Now, let us define
\Ima^{n}(1+\mathsf{T})

as

\Ima^{n}(1+\mathsf{T})\coloneqq\{a\in\mathcal{A}\mid na\in\Ima(1+\mathsf{T})\}.

Then we find that

the expression of
\mathcal{M}^{n}
is

\mathcal{M}^{n}\coloneqq\text{$\Ima^{n}(1+\mathsf{T})$-torsor}.

satisfying, for all
a\in\mathcal{M}^{n}

B(a,nb)=\eta(nb)\quad\text{for all }b\in\Ker(1-\mathsf{T}).

To prove this structure, we verify the following:

•

If
a\in\mathcal{M}^{n}
and
b\in\Ima^{n}(1+\mathsf{T})
satisfy
b+a=a
, then
b=0
.

•

For any
a,b\in\mathcal{M}^{n}
, there exists
c\in\Ima^{n}(1+\mathsf{T})
such that
a=b+c
, i.e.,
c=a-b
.

The first condition is trivial. For the second, we proceed as follows:

Assume
a,b\in\mathcal{M}^{n}
, so both satisfy

\begin{cases}B(a,nd)=\eta(nd)\\
B(b,nd)=\eta(nd)\end{cases}\quad\text{for all }d\in\Ker(1-\mathsf{T}).

Dividing the two equations gives:

B(a-b,nd)=B(n(a-b),d)=1\quad\text{for all }d\in\Ker(1-\mathsf{T}).

By non-degeneracy of
B
, this implies:

n(a-b)\in\Ker(1-\mathsf{T})^{\perp}=\Ima(1+\mathsf{T}),

so we define
c:=a-b\in\Ima^{n}(1+\mathsf{T})
, and get the conclusion as desired.

Constraint on addition :

From the fact that
\mathcal{M}^{n}
forms a torsor over
\Ima^{n}(1+\mathsf{T})
, we can easily derive the following constraint:

a+b\in\Ima^{n}(1+\mathsf{T})\quad\text{for all }a,b\in\mathcal{M}^{n}.

(5.15)

It suffices to show that
2a\in\Ima^{n}(1+\mathsf{T})
, since the torsor property implies that any difference
a-b\in\Ima^{n}(1+\mathsf{T})
, and hence the sum
a+b=2a+(b-a)\in\Ima^{n}(1+\mathsf{T})
if
2a\in\Ima^{n}(1+\mathsf{T})
.

To prove this, let
a\in\mathcal{M}^{n}
. Then for any
b\in\Ker(1-\mathsf{T})
, we have:

B(a,nb)^{2}=\eta(nb)^{2}=1,

since
\eta(nb)\in\{\pm 1\}
. On the other hand, using bilinearity of
B
, the left-hand side simplifies as:

B(a,nb)^{2}=B(2a,nb)=B(2na,b).

Therefore,

B(2na,b)=1\quad\text{for all }b\in\Ker(1-\mathsf{T}).

By non-degeneracy of
B
, this implies:

2na\in\Ker(1-\mathsf{T})^{\perp}=\Ima(1+\mathsf{T}),

i.e.,

2a\in\Ima^{n}(1+\mathsf{T})

as desired.

Constraint on
\mathsf{T}a
:

We can derive another proposition as follows:

\mathsf{T}a\in\mathcal{M}^{n}\quad\text{for all }a\in\mathcal{M}^{n}.

(5.16)

This follows easily since

\displaystyle B(\mathsf{T}a,nb)

\displaystyle=B(a,-\mathsf{T}nb)

(5.17)

\displaystyle=\eta(-\mathsf{T}nb)

\displaystyle=\eta(nb)\quad\text{for all }b\in\Ker(1-\mathsf{T}).

5.5
Topological spins of
\mathcal{M}^{n}

To fully understand the structure of
\mathcal{M}^{n}
, let us define the following:

Define

\mathcal{E}^{n}\coloneqq\text{$\frac{\Ima^{n}(1+\mathsf{T})}{\Ima(1+\mathsf{T})}$-torsor}.

satisfying

B(a,nb)=\eta(nb)\quad\text{for all }a\in\mathcal{E}^{n}.

then, we find that

\theta(a)^{n}
is well defined on
\mathcal{E}^{n}
.

(5.18)

We will use the following identities:

•

The compatibility condition between
B
and
\eta
(see
B
):

B(a,\mathsf{T}a)\,\eta((1+\mathsf{T})a)=1\quad\text{for all }a\in\mathcal{A}.

•

Anti-unitarity of
\mathsf{T}
:

\theta(a)\,\theta(\mathsf{T}a)=1\quad\text{for all }a\in\mathcal{A}.

•

Since
a\in\mathcal{M}^{n}
, we also have:

\eta(-n(1+\mathsf{T})c)\,B(a,n(1+\mathsf{T})c)=1\quad\text{for all }c\in\mathcal{A}.

Using these, we compute:

\displaystyle\frac{\theta(b)^{n}}{\theta(a)^{n}}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\theta(a+(1+\mathsf{T})c)}{\theta(a)}\right)^{n}

(5.19)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\theta(a)\,\theta((1+\mathsf{T})c)\,B(a,(1+\mathsf{T})c)}{\theta(a)}\right)^{n}

\displaystyle=\left(\theta((1+\mathsf{T})c)\,B(a,(1+\mathsf{T})c)\right)^{n}

\displaystyle=\left(\theta(c)\,\theta(\mathsf{T}c)\,B(c,\mathsf{T}c)\,B(a,(1+\mathsf{T})c)\right)^{n}

\displaystyle=\left(\eta(-(1+\mathsf{T})c)\,B(a,(1+\mathsf{T})c)\right)^{n}

\displaystyle=\eta(-n(1+\mathsf{T})c)\,B(a,n(1+\mathsf{T})c)

\displaystyle=1.

Therefore, we conclude the desired identity holds.

Furthermore, we can constrain the possible values of
\theta(a)^{n}
as follows:

\theta(a)^{n}\in\{\pm 1\}\quad\text{for all }a\in\mathcal{E}^{n}.

(5.20)

Combining equations (
5.16
) and (
5.18
), we obtain

\theta(a)^{n}=\theta\big(a-(1+\mathsf{T})a\big)^{n}=\theta(-\mathsf{T}a)^{n}=\theta(\mathsf{T}a)^{n}=\overline{\theta(a)^{n}},

which implies

\left(\theta(a)^{n}\right)^{2}=1.

Therefore, the claim follows.

5.6
Anomaly formula and higher central charge

Let us recall the expression of the formula and the well-defininedness of
\theta(a)^{n}
. Then the generalized anomaly formula simplifies to the following expression:

\eta_{n}\cdot\xi_{n}=\frac{\sum\limits_{a\in\mathcal{E}^{n}}\theta(a)^{n}}{\left|\sum\limits_{a\in\mathcal{E}^{n}}\theta(a)^{n}\right|}.

(5.21)

From the expression above and equation (

6
Conclusion and outlook

In this paper, we present a generalization of the anomaly formula incorporating time-reversal symmetry, which naturally accounts for the higher central charge. The resulting formula and its structure closely resemble the known original case, yet its direct physical interpretation remains unclear. Given that
\mathcal{M}^{n}
can be viewed as a subsystem that nontrivially contributes to the higher central charge, it is plausible that this subset plays an essential role in determining the existence of a gapped boundary.

As future directions, it would be valuable to elucidate the physical interpretation of the generalized formula and to extend the framework to non-abelian systems and spin-TQFTs.

Appendix A
Orthogonality of
\Ker(1-\mathsf{T})
and
\Ima(1+\mathsf{T})

In this section, we will prove the following equalities:

\Ker(1-\mathsf{T})=\left[\Ima(1+\mathsf{T})\right]^{\perp},\quad\Ker(1+\mathsf{T})=\left[\Ima(1-\mathsf{T})\right]^{\perp}.

This result was shown in
[
WL17
,
LT18
]
. For the reader’s convenience, we briefly review the derivation here.

We first note the basic identity:

B(\mathsf{T}a,b)=B(a,\mathsf{T}b)^{-1}.

(A.1)

This implies the following symmetry relation:

B((1+\mathsf{T})a,b)=B(a,(1-\mathsf{T})b).

(A.2)

As a consequence, we obtain the inclusions:

\Ker(1-\mathsf{T})\subset\left[\Ima(1+\mathsf{T})\right]^{\perp},\quad\Ker(1+\mathsf{T})\subset\left[\Ima(1-\mathsf{T})\right]^{\perp}.

(A.3)

Using the non-degeneracy of the bilinear form
B
, we obtain the inequalities:

|\Ker(1-\mathsf{T})|\leq\frac{|\mathcal{A}|}{|\Ima(1+\mathsf{T})|},\quad|\Ker(1+\mathsf{T})|\leq\frac{|\mathcal{A}|}{|\Ima(1-\mathsf{T})|}.

(A.4)

On the other hand, it is evident that

|\mathcal{A}/\Ker(1+\mathsf{T})|=|\Ima(1+\mathsf{T})|,\quad|\mathcal{A}/\Ker(1-\mathsf{T})|=|\Ima(1-\mathsf{T})|.

(A.5)

Combining equations (
A.4
) and (
A.5
), we conclude that the inclusions in (
A.3
) are in fact equalities:

\Ker(1-\mathsf{T})=\left[\Ima(1+\mathsf{T})\right]^{\perp},\quad\Ker(1+\mathsf{T})=\left[\Ima(1-\mathsf{T})\right]^{\perp}.

(A.6)
