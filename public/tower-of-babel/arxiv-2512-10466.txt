Geometric quantization on big line bundles
Siarhei Finski

Abstract




MnLargeSymbols’164
MnLargeSymbols’171

Abstract.

We extend several geometric quantization results to the setting of big line bundles.
More precisely, we prove the asymptotic isometry property for the map that associates to a metric on a big line bundle the corresponding sup-norms on the spaces of holomorphic sections of its tensor powers.
Building on this, we show that submultiplicative norms on section rings of big line bundles are asymptotically equivalent to sup-norms.
As an application, we show that any bounded submultiplicative filtration on the section ring of a big line bundle naturally gives rise to a Mabuchi geodesic ray, and the speed of this ray encodes the statistical invariants of the filtration.

Introduction

The main goal of this paper is to show that several geometric quantization results, which were previously established in the ample case, extend to the setting of big line bundles.

Here, we only mention that the resulting metric space is geodesically complete.
In the ample case, this theory was established through the works of Mabuchi
[

, Chen
[

]
, Darvas
[

For ample line bundles, Phong-Sturm in

To state our result, we define the
section ring

R(X,L)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\oplus_{k=0}^{\infty}H^{0}(X,L^{\otimes k}).

(1.9)

A graded norm

As a basic example, any bounded metric

The relevant equivalence relation is the following: two graded norms

\frac{1}{k}d_{p}(N_{k},N_{k}^{\prime})\to 0,\qquad\text{as }k\to\infty.

(1.11)

\operatorname{Ban}^{\infty}_{k}(h^{L}_{0})\leq N_{k}\leq\operatorname{Ban}^{\infty}_{k}(h^{L}_{1}),

,

Another application of Theorems

,

lies in the theory of submultiplicative filtrations.
Recall that a
decreasing -filtration

\mathcal{F}
of a vector space
V
is a map from to vector subspaces of

,
t\mapsto\mathcal{F}^{t}V
, verifying
\mathcal{F}^{t}V\subset\mathcal{F}^{s}V
for

, and such that
\mathcal{F}^{t}V=V
for sufficiently small

and
\mathcal{F}^{t}V=\{0\}
for sufficiently large

.
It is
left-continuous
if for any
t\in\real
, there is
\epsilon_{0}>0
such that
\mathcal{F}^{t}V=\mathcal{F}^{t-\epsilon}V
for any
0<\epsilon<\epsilon_{0}
.
A filtration
\mathcal{F}
on
R(X,L)
is a collection
(\mathcal{F}_{k})_{k=0}^{\infty}
of decreasing left-continuous filtrations
\mathcal{F}_{k}
on
H^{0}(X,L^{\otimes k})
.
We say that
\mathcal{F}
is

if for any

we have

\mathcal{F}^{t}_{k}H^{0}(X,L^{\otimes k})\cdot\mathcal{F}^{s}_{l}H^{0}(X,L^{\otimes l})\subset\mathcal{F}^{t+s}_{k+l}H^{0}(X,L^{\otimes(k+l)}).

(1.14)

In what follows, we sometimes omit the subscript from
\mathcal{F}_{k}
when it is clear from the context.

We say that
\mathcal{F}
is
bounded
if there is

such that for any

, we have

\mathcal{F}^{Ck}H^{0}(X,L^{\otimes k})=\{0\},\qquad\mathcal{F}^{-Ck}H^{0}(X,L^{\otimes k})=H^{0}(X,L^{\otimes k}).

(1.15)

Similarly to the remark we already made for norms, in the ample case, the usual definition of bounded filtrations does not require the upper bound from (
1.15
), as it follows from submultiplicativity and the finite generation of

, cf.

The theory of submultiplicative filtrations on rings was initiated by Samuel

]

]

[

In complex geometry, interest in submultiplicative filtrations emerged from their connection to the theory of canonical metrics, as highlighted in the study of constant scalar curvature Kähler metrics in
[

]

[

]

Remarkably, the Mabuchi geometry – originally developed to address the constant scalar curvature equation – now provides new insights and results concerning submultiplicative filtrations.
Moreover, submultiplicative filtrations have also found applications in other versions of canonical metrics, such as in the study of the Wess-Zumino-Witten equation
[

Let us explain how Mabuchi geometry arises in the study of submultiplicative filtrations.
In Section

, for any continuous metric
h^{L}
on

and a bounded submultiplicative filtration

,

We show that for any

is a geodesic ray with respect to all distances

In the ample setting, the above construction was carried out by Phong-Sturm
[

]
for finitely generated filtrations (i.e., those associated with a test configuration) and then by Ross-Witt Nyström
[

]
for arbitrary bounded submultiplicative filtrations.

Of course, the above construction is useful only if the resulting ray captures sufficient information about the filtration.
In our final result, we show that the speed of this ray encodes all of the statistical invariants of the filtration.
To explain the latter point in detail, we define the sequence of

\mu_{\mathcal{F},k}

\mu_{\mathcal{F},k}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\frac{1}{n_{k}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{k}}\delta\Big[\frac{e_{\mathcal{F}}(j,k)}{k}\Big],

(1.16)

where

e_{\mathcal{F}}(j,k)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\sup\Big\{t\in\real\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\dim\mathcal{F}^{t}H^{0}(X,L^{\otimes k})\geq j\Big\}.

(1.17)

Now, for the above geodesic ray

,

\mu_{\mathcal{F}}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\Big(-\frac{\dot{h}^{L}_{0}}{2}\Big)_{*}\Big(\frac{c_{1}(L,h^{L}_{0})^{n}}{{\rm{vol}}(L)}\Big).

When

is bounded, and the measure

As we assume that

is not necessarily bounded, and

In either case,

is a probability measure: when

Theorem 1.7

For any bounded submultiplicative filtration

1.7
in the ample case, relying on the analysis of the corresponding weighted Bergman kernel.
Extending this to the big setting is a natural direction for future work.

In conclusion, we briefly outline the main technical ideas underlying the proofs of Theorems
1.1
and
1.3
.
The key strategy in both cases is to reduce the problem to the ample setting.
This is achieved by constructing sufficiently large subrings of section rings of ample line bundles inside the section ring of the given big line bundle, and then showing that the distance between two norms on a vector space can be approximated by the distances between their restrictions to sufficiently large subspaces.
For Theorem
1.3
, one must moreover extend our previous characterization of submultiplicative norms in the ample case to singular varieties and, in order to analyze the sup‐norms associated with a possibly non-continuous metric
FS(N)_{*}
, introduce a refined notion of singularity class for plurisubharmonic functions that sharpens the classical notion of analytic singularities.

In Section

Section

contains the proof of Theorem

as well as several technical results required in later sections.
Section

is devoted to the proof of Theorem

.
In Section

and

2.1
Potentials with minimal and algebraic singularities

Positive metrics on ample line bundles form a bridge between complex and algebraic geometry.
When the line bundle is merely big but not ample, Kodaira’s theorem implies that the line bundle admits no positive metrics.
In this case, the role of positive metrics is taken over by Kähler currents, which will be the main focus of this section.

Similarly to Kodaira characterization of ample line bundles, it is known that a line bundle
L
is big if and only if there is a closed positive
(1,1)
-current in
c_{1}(L)
, moreover, a manifold carries a big line bundle if and only if it is Moishezon, see Bonavero
[
12
]
, Ji-Shiffman
[
49
]
, Demailly
[
11
]
, cf. also
[
54
, Theorem 2.3.28]
.
While an arbitrary Moishezon manifold is bimeromorphic to a projective manifold, if the Moishezon manifold carries a Kähler metric, it is automatically projective, cf.
[
54
, Theorem 2.2.26]
.

General Kähler currents are often difficult to analyze, but those with potentials having analytic singularities can be effectively studied using tools from algebraic geometry.
We will refer to such currents as
currents with analytic singularities
.
Recall that a function
\psi\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}X\to[-\infty,+\infty[
is said to have
analytic singularities
if, locally, it can be written as

\psi=\frac{c}{2}\cdot\log(\sum|f_{i}|^{2})+g,

(2.1)

where
c>0
,
g
is a locally bounded function, and the
f_{i}
are local holomorphic functions.
We say that
\psi
has
neat analytic singularities
if the function
g
above can be chosen to be smooth.
It is often more convenient to work with functions having
(neat) algebraic singularities
: these are functions with (neat) analytic singularities for which the constant
c
in (
2.1
) can be chosen rational.

We shall need the following basic result on these functions.

Proposition 2.1
.

Assume that
\psi_{i}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}X\to[-\infty,+\infty[
,
i=0,1
, have algebraic singularities.
Then
\max\{\psi_{0},\psi_{1}\}
also has algebraic singularities.

Remark 2.2
.

It is also immediate that a sum of two potentials with algebraic singularities has algebraic singularities.

Proof.

While the proof is classical, cf.
[
34
, Proposition 4.1.8]
, we reproduce it for the reader’s convenience and also because its arguments are employed in proving Proposition
4.6
, which refines Proposition
2.1
.
Since
\phi_{i}
,
i=0,1
, have algebraic singularities, for some
p_{i},q_{i}\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, local holomorphic functions
f_{i,j}
and bounded
g_{i}
, we can write
\psi_{i}=\frac{p_{i}}{q_{i}}\cdot\log(\sum|f_{i,j}|^{2})+g_{i}
.
Note that for any
a,b>0
, we have
\log(a+b)-\log(2)\leq\max\{\log(a),\log(b)\}\leq\log(a+b)
.
Hence for a certain bounded function

, we can write

\max\{\psi_{0},\psi_{1}\}=\frac{1}{q_{0}q_{1}}\log\Big(\big(\sum|f_{0,j}|^{2}\big)^{p_{0}q_{1}}+\big(\sum|f_{1,j}|^{2}\big)^{p_{1}q_{0}}\Big)+g_{2}.

(2.2)

Once we expand the sum under the exponent, the proof concludes.
∎

[
33
, Proposition I.4.24]
.
By
[
23
, Theorem 2.3]
, it suffices to show that
\int_{X}T^{\,n}\geq\int_{X}T_{i}^{\,n}
and that
\phi_{i}
converges to
\phi
in capacity, see
[
45
, Definition 4.23]
for the definition of the convergence in capacity.
The first condition follows from Theorem
2.6
and (
2.10
).

The proof that
\phi_{i}
converges to
\phi
in capacity is a minor modification of the usual argument showing that a uniformly bounded from above increasing sequence of psh functions converges to the upper semicontinuous regularization of its supremum in capacity, see
[
45
, Proposition 4.25]
.
Indeed, the only step of
[
45
, Proposition 4.25]
where monotonicity is used is the application of Dini’s lemma, but the latter remains valid for supermultiplicative sequences, cf.
[
36
, §A.2]
.
∎

2.2
Pluripotential theory with prescribed singularities

The main goal of this section is to recall some basic facts from the relative pluripotential theory developed by Ross-Witt Nyström in
[
69
]
,
[
68
]
and Darvas-Di Nezza-Lu in
[
24
]
,
[
22
]
,
[
23
]
.

We fix a smooth closed
(1,1)
-form
\theta
from a big class
[\theta]
.
Recall that we already defined the partial order
\preceq
on the space of
\theta
-psh functions in Section
2.1
.
Instead of considering the space of all
\theta
-psh functions, the relative pluripotential theory concerns solely
\theta
-psh functions which have singularities which are not better than those of a fixed
\psi\in\operatorname{PSH}(X,\theta)
.
More precisely, it deals with the following subset of
\operatorname{PSH}(X,\theta)
:

\operatorname{PSH}(X,\theta,\psi)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\{\phi\in\operatorname{PSH}(X,\theta)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\phi\preceq\psi\}.

(2.11)

Remark that this space depends solely on the singularity type
[\psi]
.

Let us now describe the analogue of the envelope construction in the relative setting.
First, given a non-empty subset
V\subset X
and a bounded function
f\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}V\to\mathbb{R}
, we define

P_{\theta,V}(f)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\sup\big\{\phi\in\operatorname{PSH}(X,\theta)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\phi\leq f\text{ on }V\big\}.

(2.12)

For brevity, we also note
P_{\theta}(f)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=P_{\theta,X}(f)
.
Then it is standard that if
V
is non-pluripolar, then
P_{\theta,V}(f)
is uniformly bounded from above, and hence
P_{\theta,V}(f)^{*}
is
\theta
-psh, cf.
[
45
, Theorem 9.17]
.
In particular, we can extend the definition
P_{\theta}(f)
for an arbitrary bounded from below function
f\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}X\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
, which is bounded from above on a non-pluripolar subset.
Indeed, if
V
is such a subset, then one can easily verify that for
M\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\sup P_{\theta,V}(f)
, we have

P_{\theta}(f)=P_{\theta}(\min\{f,M\}).

(2.13)

It is also immediate that the envelope behaves well under homotheties: for any
f
as above and any
t\in]0,+\infty[
, we have

P_{t\cdot\theta}(t\cdot f)=t\cdot P_{\theta}(f).

(2.14)

This property allows us to extend the definition of envelopes to metrics on
\mathbb{Q}
-line bundles.

Moreover, the envelope behaves well with respect to birational maps, cf.
[
6
, Proposition 2.9]
.
More specifically, if the map
\pi\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}Y\to X
is birational, then for any
f
as in (
2.13
), we have

P_{\pi^{*}\theta}(\pi^{*}f)=\pi^{*}P_{\theta}(f).

(2.15)

Note that we also immediately have
P_{\theta}(f)^{*}\leq f^{*}
, cf.
[
45
, Theorem 9.17]
, and so, if
f
is upper semicontinuous, then
P_{\theta}(f)
is also upper semicontinuous, and
P_{\theta}(f)\in\operatorname{PSH}(X,\theta)
.

Now, for any fixed
\psi\in\operatorname{PSH}(X,\theta)
, we define the envelope

P_{\theta}[\psi](f)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\lim_{C\to+\infty}P_{\theta}(\min\{\psi+C,f\}).

(2.16)

If
f
is bounded from above on some non-pluripolar subset, then by a similar argument as in (
2.13
),
P_{\theta}(\min\{\psi+C,f\})^{*}
is an increasing sequence of
\theta
-psh functions, which is uniformly bounded from above.
Hence, we have
P_{\theta}[\psi](f)^{*}\in\operatorname{PSH}(X,\theta)
.
Moreover, as for
M\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=-\sup_{x\in X}\psi(x)+\inf_{x\in X}f(x)
, we have
\psi+M\leq f
, we obtain
\psi+M\leq P_{\theta}[\psi](f)^{*}
.
In particular, for the singularity types, we have
[\psi]\preceq[P_{\theta}[\psi](f)^{*}]
.

Directly from (
2.13
), (
2.15
) and
[
68
, Remark 4.6]
,
[
69
]
, we obtain the following result.

Proposition 2.11
.

If
f
is bounded from above on some non-pluripolar subset and from below everywhere, then
[\psi]=[P_{\theta}[\psi](f)^{*}]
for every
\psi
with analytic singularities.

Let us now point out the following simple property of the above envelope construction.
Assume that
\psi_{1},\psi_{2}\in\operatorname{PSH}(X,\theta)
are such that
\psi_{2}\preceq\psi_{1}
.
Then for any
f
as in Proposition
2.11
,

P_{\theta}[\psi_{2}](f)=P_{\theta}[\psi_{2}](P_{\theta}[\psi_{1}](f)).

(2.17)

We will now fix
\psi\in\operatorname{PSH}(X,\theta)
with analytic singularities.
We use the notation
\hat{X}
,
\hat{\theta}
,
g
for the objects introduced in (
2.3
).
We define the order-preserving bijection between
\operatorname{PSH}(X,\theta,\psi)
and
\operatorname{PSH}(\hat{X},\hat{\theta})
as

\operatorname{PSH}(X,\theta,\psi)\ni u\mapsto\hat{u}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=(u-\psi)\circ\pi+g\in\operatorname{PSH}(\hat{X},\hat{\theta}).

(2.18)

For the proof of the fact that it is indeed a bijection, see
[
46
, Theorem 3.1]
.
Note that it implies that
u\in\operatorname{PSH}(X,\theta,\psi)
has the same singularity type as
\psi
if and only if
\hat{u}
has minimal singularities.

From the fact that (
2.18
) is an order-preserving bijection, it is immediate to see that for any
f
as in Proposition
2.11
, we have

(P_{\theta}[\psi](f)-\psi)\circ\pi+g=P_{\hat{\theta}}((f-\psi)\circ\pi+g).

(2.19)

By re-arranging the terms, taking the upper semicontinuous regularization and using the fact that
\psi\circ\pi-g
is upper semicontinuous, see (
2.3
), we deduce that under (
2.18
), we have

P_{\theta}[\psi](f)^{*}\mapsto P_{\hat{\theta}}((f-\psi)\circ\pi+g)^{*}.

(2.20)

The class
[\hat{\theta}]
is nef by Proposition
2.5
.
If we moreover assume that
\int(\theta+\mathrm{d}\mathrm{d}^{c}\psi)^{n}>0
, then by (
2.4
),
\int(\hat{\theta}+\mathrm{d}\mathrm{d}^{c}g)^{n}>0
, and the class
[\hat{\theta}]
is also big.
In particular, the relative pluripotential theory in a big class
[\theta]
associated with a potential
\phi
with analytic singularities verifying
\int(\theta+\mathrm{d}\mathrm{d}^{c}\psi)^{n}>0
, reduces to the pluripotential theory associated with a big and nef class
[\hat{\theta}]
.
This observation is crucial for the extension of Mabuchi geometry in the big setting, as we recall in the next section.

When
\psi
has neat analytic singularities, the correspondence described above can be refined so that its image consists of potentials associated with a semi-positive form.
Indeed, by Proposition
2.5
, the form
\hat{\theta}(g)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\hat{\theta}+\mathrm{d}\mathrm{d}^{c}g
is semi-positive.
Moreover, in complete analogy with (
2.18
), one obtains an order-preserving bijection between
\operatorname{PSH}(X,\theta,\psi)
and
\operatorname{PSH}(\hat{X},\hat{\theta}(g))
, defined as

\operatorname{PSH}(X,\theta,\psi)\ni u\mapsto\hat{u}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=(u-\psi)\circ\pi\in\operatorname{PSH}(\hat{X},\hat{\theta}(g)).

(2.21)

Although (
2.18
) and (
2.21
) introduce an apparent notational conflict, we will always specify which bijection is being used, so no ambiguity will arise.

2.3
Mabuchi geometry on ample and big classes

The main goal of this section is to recall the definition of the Mabuchi-Darvas distance on the space of Kähler forms within a given Kähler class
[\omega]
.
We then extend this definition to the space of bounded
\omega
-psh potentials, where
\omega
is a Kähler form representing
[\omega]
.
In this part, we follow Mabuchi
[
55
]
, Chen
[
21
]
, and Darvas
[
28
]
.
Building on these results, and following Di Nezza-Lu
[
35
]
, we explain how to extend this construction to big and nef classes
[\beta]
.
Finally, following Gupta
[
46
]
, we further extend the construction to the general big classes
[\theta]
.

Now, let us fix a Kähler form
\omega
on
X
in the class
[\omega]
.
On the space of Kähler potentials
\mathcal{H}_{\omega}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\{\phi\in\mathscr{C}^{\infty}(X)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\omega+\mathrm{d}\mathrm{d}^{c}\phi>0\}
, we define a collection of
L^{p}
-type Finsler metrics,
p\in[1,+\infty[
, as follows.
If
u\in\mathcal{H}_{\omega}
and
\xi\in T_{u}\mathcal{H}_{\omega}\simeq\mathscr{C}^{\infty}(X,\real)
, then the
L^{p}
-length of
\xi
is given by the following expression

\|\xi\|_{p}^{u}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\sqrt[p]{\frac{1}{\int[\omega]^{n}}\int_{X}|\xi(x)|^{p}\cdot(\omega+\mathrm{d}\mathrm{d}^{c}u)^{n}(x)}.

(2.22)

For
p=2
, this was introduced by Mabuchi
[
55
]
, and for
p\in[1,+\infty[
by Darvas
[
28
]
.

Extending the analysis of Chen
[
21
]
, Darvas in
[
28
]
studied the metric completions
(\mathcal{E}^{p}_{\omega},d_{p})
,
p\in[1,+\infty[
, of the path length metric structures
(\mathcal{H}_{\omega},d_{p})
associated with (
2.22
).
He proved that these completions are geodesic metric spaces and showed that they coincide with the space of finite energy classes previously introduced by Guedj-Zeriahi
[
44
]
.
The phrase path length metric structure above means that the distance between
\phi_{0}\in\mathcal{H}_{\omega}
and
\phi_{1}\in\mathcal{H}_{\omega}
is defined as the infimum of the length
l(\gamma)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\int_{0}^{1}\|\gamma^{\prime}(t)\|^{\gamma(t)}_{p}dt
, where
\gamma
is a piecewise smooth path in
\mathcal{H}_{\omega}
joining
\phi_{0}
and
\phi_{1}
.
What will be important for us is that the above distance is well-defined on
{\rm{PSH}}(X,\omega)\cap L^{\infty}(X)
, as
{\rm{PSH}}(X,\omega)\cap L^{\infty}(X)\subset\mathcal{E}^{p}_{\omega}
for any
p\in[1,+\infty[
, cf.
[
45
, Exercise 10.2]
.

Di Nezza-Lu
[
35
]
further extended this theory to the setting of big and nef classes
[\beta]
.
To explain how this was done, we fix a smooth closed
(1,1)
-form
\beta
in
[\beta]
.
Then for any
\epsilon>0
, while the smooth closed
(1,1)
-form
\beta+\epsilon\omega
is not necessarily positive, it lies inside of the class
[\beta+\epsilon\omega]
, which is Kähler.
For
f,g\in\mathscr{C}^{0}(X,\real)
, Di Nezza-Lu defined the distance between the envelopes
P_{\beta}(f),P_{\beta}(g)\in\operatorname{PSH}(X,\beta)
as follows

d_{p}(P_{\beta}(f),P_{\beta}(g))\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}d_{p}(P_{\beta+\epsilon\omega}(f),P_{\beta+\epsilon\omega}(g)).

(2.23)

Note that the right-hand side of (
2.23
) is well-defined, as
[\beta+\epsilon\omega]
is Kähler, and so
P_{\beta+\epsilon\omega}(f),P_{\beta+\epsilon\omega}(g)
are bounded.
For arbitrary
u,v\in\operatorname{PSH}(X,\beta)
with minimal singularities, the distance can then be defined through an additional approximation.
More specifically, we consider decreasing sequences
f_{i},g_{i}\in\mathscr{C}^{0}(X,\real)
,
i\in\mathbb{N}
, so that
\lim_{i\to\infty}f_{i}=u
and
\lim_{i\to\infty}g_{i}=v
(such sequences exist as
u
and
v
are upper semicontinuous).
Then we define

d_{p}(u,v)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\lim_{i\to\infty}d_{p}(P_{\beta}(f_{i}),P_{\beta}(g_{i})).

(2.24)

Di Nezza-Lu proved that this defines a distance function on the space of
\beta
-psh potentials with minimal singularities, independent of all auxiliary choices.

Gupta
[
46
]
further extended this theory to the setting of big classes
[\theta]
.
To do so, he proceeded by approximation of the space
\operatorname{PSH}(X,\theta)
with
\operatorname{PSH}(X,\theta,\psi_{k})
, for a certain sequence of
\psi_{k}\in\operatorname{PSH}(X,\theta)
with analytic singularities.
Within each
\operatorname{PSH}(X,\theta,\psi_{k})
, he defined the metrics using the above definition of Di Nezza-Lu.

Let us first explain how the second step is done and then proceed to the description of the first one.
For this, we fix
\psi\in\operatorname{PSH}(X,\theta)
with analytic singularities and so that
\int(\theta+\mathrm{d}\mathrm{d}^{c}\psi)^{n}>0
.
We describe how to endow with the distance the subspace of
\operatorname{PSH}(X,\theta,\psi)
consisting of
\theta
-psh potentials of the singularity type as
[\psi]
.
This is done through the use of the bijection (
2.18
), from which we use the notations below.
As we explained in Section
2.2
,
\theta
-psh potentials of the singularity type as
[\psi]
are in one-to-one correspondence with
\hat{\theta}
-psh potentials with minimal singularities.
Moreover, as we explained, the class
[\hat{\theta}]
is big and nef.
Hence, the distance can be imported from (
2.24
).
Gupta in
[
46
, Theorem 3.14]
proved that the resulting distance does not depend on the choice of the resolution
\pi\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\hat{X}\to X
.

Now, we describe the approximation of the space
\operatorname{PSH}(X,\theta)
by
\operatorname{PSH}(X,\theta,\psi_{k})
.
We fix an arbitrary
\psi\in\operatorname{PSH}(X,\theta)
with
\psi\leq-1
and analytic singularities, which is strictly
\theta
-psh (such
\psi
exists by Theorem
2.3
).
Gupta showed in
[
46
, §4]
that there exists an increasing sequence
\psi_{k}\in\operatorname{PSH}(X,\theta)
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
, with analytic singularities, satisfying
\psi_{k}\geq\psi
, and such that
\psi_{k}\to V_{\theta}
, as
k\to\infty
, outside a pluripolar set, where
V_{\theta}
is defined in (
1.2
).
We next show that the sequence
\psi_{k}
may be chosen to have algebraic singularities, and so that
\psi_{k}
is strictly
\theta
-psh.
To this end, we adapt Gupta’s construction with only minor modifications.

Note first that since
\psi\leq-1
, we have
\psi\leq V_{\theta}-1
.
Define
\varphi_{j}=\frac{1}{j}\psi+\frac{j-1}{j}V_{\theta}
.
Then
\varphi_{j}\leq V_{\theta}
and
\varphi_{j}\nearrow V_{\theta}
outside a pluripolar set.
Although
\varphi_{j}
do not necessarily have analytic singularities, each
\varphi_{j}
is strictly
\theta
-psh (as
\psi
is) and
\varphi_{j}\geq\psi
.
By Theorem
2.3
and a version of Dini’s theorem, we may choose a strictly
\theta
-psh potential with algebraic singularities
\phi_{j}
, verifying
V_{\theta}\geq\phi_{j}\geq\varphi_{j}
.
The problem now is that the sequence
\phi_{j}
,
j\in\mathbb{N}
, is not necessarily monotone.

Consider the envelope
P_{\theta}[\phi_{j}]
.
Since
\varphi_{j}\leq\phi_{j}
and
\phi_{j}\leq V_{\theta}
, we have
\varphi_{j}\leq P_{\theta}[\phi_{j}]^{*}\leq V_{\theta}
.
Because
\varphi_{j}\nearrow V_{\theta}
outside a pluripolar set, it follows that
P_{\theta}[\phi_{j}]^{*}\to V_{\theta}
pointwise outside a pluripolar set.
Moreover, since
\phi_{j}
has algebraic singularities and
[P_{\theta}[\phi_{j}]^{*}]=[\phi_{j}]
by Proposition
2.11
, the envelopes
P_{\theta}[\phi_{j}]^{*}
also have algebraic singularities.
Define

\psi_{k}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\max\{P_{\theta}[\phi_{1}]^{*},\dots,P_{\theta}[\phi_{k}]^{*}\}.

(2.25)

Then
\psi_{k}
has algebraic singularities by Proposition
2.1
, and
\psi_{k}\nearrow V_{\theta}
outside a pluripolar set.
Moreover, as
P_{\theta}[\phi_{j}]^{*}\geq\varphi_{j}\geq\psi
, we see that
\psi_{k}\geq\psi
.
Note that by considering
\frac{k-1}{k}\psi_{k}+\frac{1}{k}\psi
instead of
\psi_{k}
, without loosing the generality, we may further assume that
\psi_{k}
is strictly
\theta
-psh.

Note that by Theorem
2.6
, we have
\int(\theta+\mathrm{d}\mathrm{d}^{c}\psi_{k})^{n}\geq\int(\theta+\mathrm{d}\mathrm{d}^{c}\psi)^{n}>0
, and so each space
\operatorname{PSH}(X,\theta,\psi_{k})
falls into the mold of the considerations after (
2.18
).
Then, for any
f,g\in\mathscr{C}^{2}(X)
, Gupta in
[
46
, (7)]
defined the distance between
P_{\theta}(f)
and
P_{\theta}(g)
as follows

d_{p}(P_{\theta}(f)^{*},P_{\theta}(g)^{*})\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\lim_{k\to\infty}d_{p}(P_{\theta}[\psi_{k}](f)^{*},P_{\theta}[\psi_{k}](g)^{*}).

(2.26)

Remark that the distances on the right-hand side of (
2.26
) are well-defined.
This is because
\psi_{k}
has analytic singularities, and so
[P_{\theta}[\psi_{k}](f)^{*}]=[P_{\theta}[\psi_{k}](g)^{*}]=[\psi_{k}]
by Proposition
2.11
.

Gupta verified in
[
46
, Theorem 4.7]
that the distance (
2.26
) does not depend on the choice of
\psi_{k}
as long as this sequence verifies the two properties above:
\psi_{k}\in\operatorname{PSH}(X,\theta)
, is an increasing sequence with analytic singularities, and
\psi_{k}\to V_{\theta}
, as
k\to\infty
, outside a pluripolar set.
Further, Gupta in
[
46
, Definition 4.10]
defined
d_{p}(u,v)
for arbitrary
u,v\in\operatorname{PSH}(X,\theta)
with minimal singularities in the same way as in (
2.24
) and verified that this is well-defined.
In what follows, we will occasionally write
d^{\theta}_{p}(u,v)
in place of
d_{p}(u,v)
in order to emphasize the dependence on
\theta
; however, in most instances this will be clear from the context.

Following previous work of Trusiani
[
74
]
, done for
p=1
, Gupta in
[
46
, Theorem 7.3]
verified that for any
u,v\in\operatorname{PSH}(X,\theta)
with minimal singularities, and any
\psi\in\operatorname{PSH}(X,\theta)
with analytic singularities, the following contraction property is satisfied

d_{p}(P_{\theta}[\psi](u)^{*},P_{\theta}[\psi](v)^{*})\leq d_{p}(u,v).

(2.27)

We now proceed to establish the following statement using the results discussed above.

Lemma 2.12
.

For any
f,g\in\mathscr{C}^{2}(X)
,
\epsilon>0
, there is a strictly
\theta
-psh potential
\psi
with neat algebraic singularities, so that

d_{p}(P_{\theta}(f)^{*},P_{\theta}(g)^{*})-\epsilon\leq d_{p}(P_{\theta}[\psi](f)^{*},P_{\theta}[\psi](g)^{*})\leq d_{p}(P_{\theta}(f)^{*},P_{\theta}(g)^{*}).

(2.28)

Moreover, one can choose
\psi
so that

\int(\theta+\mathrm{d}\mathrm{d}^{c}\psi)^{n}\geq{\rm{vol}}(\theta)-\epsilon.

(2.29)

Proof.

We fix a sequence
\psi_{k}
of strictly
\theta
-psh potentials described after (
2.26
).
By (
2.26
), there is
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that

d_{p}(P_{\theta}(f)^{*},P_{\theta}(g)^{*})-\epsilon\leq d_{p}(P_{\theta}[\psi_{k}](f)^{*},P_{\theta}[\psi_{k}](g)^{*}).

(2.30)

Moreover, upon increasing
k
, one may further assume by Theorems
2.7
and
2.8
that

\int(\theta+\mathrm{d}\mathrm{d}^{c}\psi_{k})^{n}\geq{\rm{vol}}(\theta)-\epsilon,

(2.31)

and upon replacing
\psi_{k}
by
\psi_{k}-1
, we may further assume that
\psi_{k}\leq-1
.
Note that while both (
2.28
) and (
2.29
) are satisfied for
\psi\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\psi_{k}
, the resulting
\psi
is not with neat algebraic singularities.

To remedy this, we consider a sequence of strictly
\theta
-psh potentials
\phi_{l}
,
l\in\mathbb{N}
, with algebraic singularities decreasing towards
\psi_{k}
as in Theorem
2.3
.
Then by our assumption
\psi_{k}\leq-1
, a version of Dini’s theorem implies that we have
\phi_{l}\leq-1/2
, for sufficiently large
l
.
We fix such
l
from now on, and to simplify further exposition, denote
\phi\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\phi_{l}
.
In particular, by the construction (
2.25
), we can consider a sequence
\psi_{r}^{0}\in\operatorname{PSH}(X,\theta)
,
r\in\mathbb{N}
, with algebraic singularities satisfying
\psi_{r}^{0}\geq\phi
, and such that
\psi_{r}^{0}\to V_{\theta}
, as
r\to\infty
, outside a pluripolar set.
Then immediately from the fact that the definition (
2.26
) doesn’t depend on the choice of the approximation sequence, we conclude that

d_{p}(P_{\theta}(f)^{*},P_{\theta}(g)^{*})=\lim_{r\to\infty}d_{p}(P_{\theta}[\psi_{r}^{0}](f)^{*},P_{\theta}[\psi_{r}^{0}](g)^{*}).

(2.32)

From this, (
2.27
) and (
2.30
), we conclude that

d_{p}(P_{\theta}(f)^{*},P_{\theta}(g)^{*})-\epsilon\leq d_{p}(P_{\theta}[\psi_{k}](f)^{*},P_{\theta}[\psi_{k}](g)^{*})\\
\leq d_{p}(P_{\theta}[\phi](f)^{*},P_{\theta}[\phi](g)^{*})\leq d_{p}(P_{\theta}[\psi_{1}^{0}](f)^{*},P_{\theta}[\psi_{1}^{0}](g)^{*})\leq d_{p}(P_{\theta}(f)^{*},P_{\theta}(g)^{*})

(2.33)

In particular, we deduce that (
2.28
) is verified for
\psi\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\phi
.
By Theorem
2.6
and (
2.31
), we deduce that (
2.29
) is also verified for such a choice of
\psi
, finishing the proof.
∎

The extension of the Mabuchi-Darvas distance for arbitrary
u,v\in\operatorname{PSH}(X,\theta)
with minimal singularities is then done as follows.
As before, we consider decreasing sequences
f_{i},g_{i}\in\mathscr{C}^{2}(X,\real)
,
i\in\mathbb{N}
, so that
\lim_{i\to\infty}f_{i}=u
and
\lim_{i\to\infty}g_{i}=v
.
Then we define

d_{p}(u,v)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\lim_{i\to\infty}d_{p}(P_{\theta}(f_{i})^{*},P_{\theta}(g_{i})^{*}).

(2.34)

Gupta in
[
46
, Theorems 4.11, 4.13]
verified that it gives a well-defined distance on
\theta
-psh potentials with minimal singularities.
In
[
46
, Theorem 5.6]
, he also verified that his construction coincides with the one of Di Nezza-Lu if the class
[\theta]
is big and nef.

Lemma 2.13
.

Consider

Proof.

, as

We emphasize that the Mabuchi-Darvas distance between potentials depends only on the associated currents.
Namely, if

We summarize this as

d_{p}^{\theta}(u,v)=d_{p}^{\theta(g)}(u-g,v-g).

(2.41)

Note also that with our normalization of the Mabuchi distance, for any

with minimal singularities and any

Following previous work of Darvas

Let us now proceed to the description of geodesics associated with the above distances.
For this, we consider the strip

For any

and

.

It is immediate that if both

and

We define the

,

It is then immediate that for any

Moreover, for any

In the ample case, this was observed in

Indeed, we assume that

Any subgeodesic is convex in

Moreover, the above also shows that for any

2.4
Norms, metrics and envelopes

In geometric quantization, the correspondence between metrics on line bundles and norms on their spaces of holomorphic sections plays a central role.
The main goal of this section is to recall the basics of this correspondence.

We fix a holomorphic line bundle
L
over a compact complex manifold
X
.
We assume that the space
H^{0}(X,L^{\otimes k})
is non-empty for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
For any norm
N_{k}=\|\cdot\|_{k}
on
H^{0}(X,L^{\otimes k})
, we associate a singular metric
FS(N_{k})
on
L^{\otimes k}
as follows: for any
x\in X
,
l\in L^{\otimes k}_{x}
, we let

|l|_{FS(N_{k}),x}=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}s\in H^{0}(X,L^{\otimes k})\\
s(x)=l\end{subarray}}\|s\|_{k},

(2.47)

with the convention that the infimum equals
+\infty
if there is no
s\in H^{0}(X,L^{\otimes k})
such that
s(x)=l
.
Note that immediately from the definitions, for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

N_{k}\geq\operatorname{Ban}^{\infty}_{k}(FS(N_{k})^{\frac{1}{k}}).

(2.48)

Moreover,
N_{k}\mapsto FS(N_{k})
is a monotone procedure in the sense that for any norms
N_{k,0}
,
N_{k,1}
verifying
N_{k,0}\geq N_{k,1}
, we have

FS(N_{k,0})\geq FS(N_{k,1}).

(2.49)

When the line bundle
L^{\otimes k}
is very ample, the above metric can be alternatively described through the Kodaira embeddings

{\rm{Kod}}_{k}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}X\hookrightarrow\mathbb{P}(H^{0}(X,L^{\otimes k})^{*}).

(2.50)

which embeds
X
in the space of hyperplanes in
H^{0}(X,L^{\otimes k})
.
The evaluation maps provide the isomorphism

L^{\otimes(-k)}\to{\rm{Kod}}_{k}^{*}\mathscr{O}(-1),

where
\mathscr{O}(-1)
is the tautological bundle over
\mathbb{P}(H^{0}(X,L^{\otimes k})^{*})
.
We endow
H^{0}(X,L^{\otimes k})^{*}
with the dual norm
N_{k}^{*}
and induce from it a metric
h^{FS}(N_{k})
on
\mathscr{O}(-1)
over
\mathbb{P}(H^{0}(X,L^{\otimes k})^{*})
.
The reader will check that the resulting metric
FS(N_{k})
on
L^{\otimes k}
is the only metric verifying under the dual of the above isomorphism the identity
FS(N_{k})={\rm{Kod}}_{k}^{*}(h^{FS}(N_{k})^{*})
.
A version of the above identity also exists in the big setting, and we recall it in Section
3.1
.

For the latter use, we introduce the
base locus
of a holomorphic line bundle
L^{\prime}
on
X
as

Bs(L^{\prime})\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\Big\{x\in X\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\text{there is }s\in H^{0}(X,L^{\prime}),\text{ such that }s(x)\neq 0\Big\}.

(2.51)

The
stable base locus
is defined as

\mathbb{B}(L^{\prime})\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\cap_{k=1}^{+\infty}Bs((L^{\prime})^{\otimes k}).

(2.52)

The notion of the stable base locus extends naturally to
\mathbb{Q}
-line bundles, a fact we use to define the
augmented base locus
as

\mathbb{B}_{+}(L^{\prime})\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\mathbb{B}(L^{\prime}-\epsilon A),

(2.53)

where
A
is an arbitrary ample line bundle and
\epsilon
is a sufficiently small rational number.
From Noether’s finiteness result, the above definition doesn’t depend on
\epsilon>0
as long as it is very small.
From this, it is immediate to see that it doesn’t depend on the choice of the ample line bundle
A
.

Let us now recall that
FS(N_{k})
has a psh potential if
H^{0}(X,L^{\otimes k})\neq\{0\}
.
To see this, we fix an arbitrary smooth metric
h^{L}
on
L
, then the function
\phi_{k}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}X\to[-\infty,+\infty[
, defined so that
FS(N_{k})=(h^{L})^{k}\cdot\exp(-2k\cdot\phi_{k})
, can be described as

\phi_{k}(x)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\sup\Big\{\frac{1}{k}\log|s(x)|_{(h^{L})^{k}}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}s\in H^{0}(X,L^{\otimes k}),\|s\|_{N_{k}}\leq 1\Big\}.

(2.54)

Note that by the Poincaré-Lelong formula, the functions under the supremum in (
2.54
) are in
\operatorname{PSH}(X,\theta)
, where
\theta\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=c_{1}(L,h^{L})
.
Since the norm
N_{k}
is bounded from below by a sup-norm of some metric on
L
, we see that the functions under the supremum in (
2.54
) also form a uniformly bounded from above subset of functions, and hence
\phi_{k}^{*}\in\operatorname{PSH}(X,\theta)
.
However, it is immediate to see that
\phi_{k}
is continuous outside of the base-locus, where it takes
-\infty
values, and so
\phi_{k}^{*}=\phi_{k}
; see also (
3.10
) for an alternative proof of the last fact.
For Hermitian norms
N_{k}
, the above formula can be further simplified

\phi_{k}(x)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\frac{1}{2k}\log\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{n_{k}}|s_{i}(x)|^{2}_{(h^{L})^{k}}\Big).

(2.55)

Hence, if
N_{k}
is a Hermitian norm, then
\phi_{k}
has neat algebraic singularities.
Moreover, since general norms
N_{k}
can be bounded above and below by Hermitian norms, it follows that
\phi_{k}
always has algebraic singularities, see also the proof of Proposition
4.16
for a refinement of this result.

We can now study the convergence properties of Fubini-Study metrics.
Recall that in (

), for a continuous metric

on

.

.
By approximation and the fact that taking the envelope

is continuous on

.
It is also standard, cf.

Theorem 2.16

Let

be a continuous metric on

, then the sequence of singular metrics

Remark 2.17
.

When

is ample and

is smooth and positive, the rate of convergence can be analyzed more precisely, following the results of Tian
[
73
]
, Zelditch
[
80
]
, and others.
However, such uniform behavior should not be expected in the big case.
This is for the following reason.
We already explained that
FS(\operatorname{Ban}^{\infty}_{k}(h^{L}))^{\frac{1}{k}}
has a potential with algebraic singularities.
But

has a potential with minimal singularities.
As singularity class cannot change under the uniform convergence, the uniform convergence of potentials of

Proof.

The result follows immediately from

Using the standard terminology, Proposition

To extend this to a general density

Now, even though we initially defined the sup-norms

To see this, we consider such a metric
h^{L}
, and write
h^{L}=h^{L}_{0}\cdot\exp(-2\psi)
for some bounded metric
h^{L}_{0}
.
Then for
s\in H^{0}(X,L^{\otimes k})
, we define

\|s\|_{\operatorname{Ban}^{\infty}_{k}(h^{L})}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\exp(k\cdot\sup_{x\in X}(\phi-\psi)),

(2.58)

where
\phi\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\frac{1}{k}\log|s|_{(h^{L}_{0})^{k}}
.
As
\phi\in\operatorname{PSH}(X,\theta)
by Poincaré-Lelong, we deduce that the supremum on the right-hand side of (
2.58
) is finite if
\psi
is bounded from below by a potential with minimal singularities.
Hence, by the above, the right-hand side of (
1.13
) is well-defined.

From the above description it is also immediate, cf.
[
6
, Lemma 2.8]
, that for any metric
h^{L}
as in (
2.58
), we have the following identity

\operatorname{Ban}^{\infty}_{k}(h^{L})=\operatorname{Ban}^{\infty}_{k}(P(h^{L})),

(2.59)

where
P(h^{L})
is the envelope defined as in (
1.2
), see the discussion after (
2.62
) for a brief proof and Proposition
4.12
for a further refinement.

From the above, we see that for any
h^{L}
with a potential of minimal singularities, the norm
\operatorname{Ban}^{\infty}(h^{L})
is bounded.
Indeed, since
h^{L}
has a potential with minimal singularities and for any continuous
h^{L}_{0}
,
P(h^{L}_{0})
has a potential with minimal singularities, we deduce that there is
C>0
, such that
P(h^{L}_{0})\cdot\exp(-C)\leq h^{L}\leq P(h^{L}_{0})\cdot\exp(C)
.
It then follows from (
2.59
) that

\exp(-Ck)\cdot\operatorname{Ban}^{\infty}_{k}(h^{L}_{0})\leq\operatorname{Ban}^{\infty}_{k}(h^{L})\leq\exp(Ck)\cdot\operatorname{Ban}^{\infty}_{k}(h^{L}_{0}).

(2.60)

Consider now the following situation.
Assume that a big line bundle
L
can be written as
L=F\otimes\mathscr{O}(E)
, where
E
is an effective divisor and
F
is an arbitrary line bundle.
We fix a singular metric
h^{L}
on
L
, and introduce the following metric on
F
:
h^{F}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=h^{L}/h^{E}_{{\rm{sing}}}
, where
h^{E}_{{\rm{sing}}}
is the canonical singular metric on
\mathscr{O}(E)
.
It is immediate to verify that if the line bundle
F
is psh, and the potential of the metric
h^{L}
is bounded from below by a potential with minimal singularities, then the same holds true for the induced metric on
F
.
Moreover, the embedding

\iota_{k}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}H^{0}(X,F^{\otimes k})\to H^{0}(X,L^{\otimes k}),\qquad s\mapsto s\cdot s_{E}^{k},

(2.61)

is isometric, when
H^{0}(X,F^{\otimes k})
is endowed with the norm
\operatorname{Ban}^{\infty}_{k}(h^{F})
and
H^{0}(X,L^{\otimes k})
is endowed with the norm
\operatorname{Ban}^{\infty}_{k}(h^{L})
.

Let us establish the following result, generalizing (
2.59
).

Proposition 2.21
.

If the potential of the metric
h^{L}
is bounded from below by a potential with minimal singularities, then for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
\iota_{k}^{*}\operatorname{Ban}^{\infty}_{k}(h^{L})=\operatorname{Ban}^{\infty}_{k}(P(h^{F}))
.

Proof.

First of all, for any
s\in H^{0}(X,F^{\otimes k})
, we have

\|s\|_{\operatorname{Ban}^{\infty}_{k}(h^{F})}=\exp(k\cdot\sup_{x\in X}(\phi-\psi)),

(2.62)

where
\phi\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\frac{1}{k}\log|s|_{(h^{F}_{0})^{k}}
.
On the other hand, we have

\|s\|_{\operatorname{Ban}^{\infty}_{k}(P(h^{F}))}=\exp(k\cdot\sup_{x\in X}(\phi-P_{\theta}(\psi))).

(2.63)

Clearly, we have
P_{\theta}(\psi)\leq\psi
, giving us the inequality
\operatorname{Ban}^{\infty}_{k}(P(h^{F}))\geq\operatorname{Ban}^{\infty}_{k}(h^{F})
.
However, since
\phi\in\operatorname{PSH}(X,\theta)
by Poincaré-Lelong, we deduce

\phi-\sup_{x\in X}(\phi-\psi)\leq P_{\theta}(\psi).

(2.64)

By rearranging terms and taking the supremum, we obtain the reverse inequality, which finishes the proof of Proposition
2.21
.
∎

2.5
Quasi-distances on the space of norms and respective geodesics

In this section, we recall the definition of a family of distances on the space of Hermitian norms on finite-dimensional vector spaces, and explain how these distances extend to quasi-distances on the space of all norms.
We then discuss the construction of geodesics for these distances.

To begin, we fix two Hermitian norms
N_{i}
,
i=0,1
, associated with the scalar products
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{i}
on a vector space
V
of dimension
v
.
Define the transfer map
A\in\text{End}{(V)}
between
N_{0}
and
N_{1}
as the Hermitian operator, verifying
\langle A\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{0}=\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{1}
.
For any
p\in[1,+\infty[
, we define

d_{p}(N_{0},N_{1})=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt[p]{\frac{{\rm{Tr}}[|\log A|^{p}]}{v}}.

(2.65)

By
[
15
, Theorem 3.1]
, the above functions
d_{p}
,
p\in[1,+\infty[
, satisfy the triangle inequality.
Hence, they define distances on the space of all Hermitian norms.
It is then immediate to verify that the Hermitian norms
N_{t}
, associated with the scalar products
\langle A^{t}\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{0}
form a metric geodesic connecting
N_{0}
with
N_{1}
for any
d_{p}
,
p\in[1,+\infty[
.

While there are many motivations for the above definition of distances, we only mention that one can show that the space of Hermitian norms on
V
, endowed with the distance
d_{2}
, is isometric to the space
SL(V)/SU(V)
, equipped with the distance induced by the standard
SL(V)
-invariant metric, see
[
25
, Theorem 1.1]
.

Let
N_{0},N_{1},N_{2}
be Hermitian norms on
V
, ordered as
N_{0}\leq N_{1}\leq N_{2}
.
Lidskii inequality from
[
25
, Theorem 5.1]
states that for any
p\in[1,+\infty[
, we have

d_{p}(N_{1},N_{2})^{p}\leq d_{p}(N_{0},N_{2})^{p}-d_{p}(N_{0},N_{1})^{p}.

(2.66)

Next, we extend the construction of distances to non-Hermitian norms.
Let
N_{i}=\|\cdot\|_{i}
,
i=1,2
, be two norms on
V
.
We define the
logarithmic relative spectrum
of
N_{1}
with respect to
N_{2}
as a non-increasing sequence
\lambda_{j}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\lambda_{j}(N_{1},N_{2})
,
j=1,\cdots,v
, defined as

\lambda_{j}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}W\subset V\\
\dim W=j\end{subarray}}\inf_{w\in W\setminus\{0\}}\log\frac{\|w\|_{2}}{\|w\|_{1}}.

(2.67)

Directly from (
2.67
), for any norms
N_{1},N_{2},N_{3}
,
j=1,\cdots,v
, we have

\lambda_{j}(N_{1},N_{2})+\lambda_{\dim V}(N_{2},N_{3})\leq\lambda_{j}(N_{1},N_{3})\leq\lambda_{j}(N_{1},N_{2})+\lambda_{1}(N_{2},N_{3}),

(2.68)

and whenever
N_{1}\leq N_{2}\leq N_{3}
, we have

\lambda_{j}(N_{1},N_{2})\leq\lambda_{j}(N_{1},N_{3}).

(2.69)

We then define the following quantity

d_{p}(N_{1},N_{2})=\sqrt[p]{\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{v}|\lambda_{i}(N_{1},N_{2})|^{p}}{v}}.

(2.70)

It is then an immediate consequence of the min-max characterization of the eigenvalues that the definition (
2.70
) extends that of (
2.65
).

While the function from (
2.70
) separates points, it is not clear that it satisfies the triangle inequality, which is why we refer to them as quasi-distances and not distances.
We shall show below that it satisfies a relaxed triangle inequality.

By the John ellipsoid theorem, cf.
[
62
, p. 27]
, for any normed vector space
(V,N_{V})
, there is a Hermitian norm
N_{V}^{H}
on
V
, verifying

N_{V}^{H}\leq N_{V}\leq\sqrt{v}\cdot N_{V}^{H}.

(2.71)

Note that it is immediate from (
2.68
) and Minkowski inequality that if the norms
N_{1}
and
N_{2}
are such that for
C>0
, we have
N_{1}\cdot\exp(-C)\leq N_{2}\leq N_{1}\cdot\exp(C)
, then for any norm
N_{0}
on
V
,
p\in[1,+\infty[
, we have

\big|d_{p}(N_{0},N_{1})-d_{p}(N_{0},N_{2})\big|\leq C.

(2.72)

From this and the fact that the triangle inequality holds for metrics, it follows, cf.
[
41
, proof of Lemma 2.9]
for details, that for any norms
N_{1},N_{2},N_{3}
on
V
,

d_{p}(N_{1},N_{3})\leq d_{p}(N_{1},N_{2})+d_{p}(N_{2},N_{3})+6\log v.

(2.73)

Similarly to (
2.73
), Lidskii inequality (
2.66
) holds in the following weaker form: for any norms
N_{0},N_{1},N_{2}
on
V
, ordered as
N_{0}\leq N_{1}\leq N_{2}
, we have

d_{1}(N_{1},N_{2})\leq d_{1}(N_{0},N_{2})-d_{1}(N_{0},N_{1})+6\log v.

(2.74)

Let us now describe a relation between the
d_{1}
-distance and the volumes of the unit balls.
We fix a Hermitian norm
H
on
V
, which allows us to calculate the volumes
v(\cdot)
of measurable subsets in
V
.
Note that while such volumes depend on the choice of
V
, their ratio does not.

Lemma 2.22
.

For any two Hermitian norms
N_{0}
,
N_{1}
on
V
, verifying
N_{0}\leq N_{1}
, we have

d_{1}(N_{0},N_{1})=\frac{1}{v}\log\Big(\frac{v(B_{0})}{v(B_{1})}\Big),

(2.75)

where
B_{i}\subset V
,
i=0,1
, are the unit balls of the norms
N_{i}
.
If the norms
N_{0}
,
N_{1}
are not necessarily Hermitian, then we have

\Big|d_{1}(N_{0},N_{1})-\frac{1}{v}\log\Big(\frac{v(B_{0})}{v(B_{1})}\Big)\Big|\leq 6\log v.

(2.76)

Proof.

For Hermitian norms, the result is immediate: it simply says that the volume of an ellipse is proportional to the product of its principal axes.
For not necessarily Hermitian norms, the result follows from (
2.71
), (
2.73
) and (
2.75
).
∎

When working with general norms rather than Hermitian ones, it becomes essential to replace the geodesic construction with an alternative path between norms.
Complex interpolation appears to be the most suitable for this purpose.

Let us recall the definition of complex interpolation.
We fix two norms
N_{0}=\|\cdot\|_{0}
,
N_{1}=\|\cdot\|_{1}
on
V
.
Consider the strip
S=\{z\in\mathbb{C}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}0<\operatorname{Re}z<1\}
.
Define
\mathcal{F}(V)
to be the set of all functions
f\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}S\to V
such that:
f
extend continuously on
\overline{S}
, holomorphic on
S
, and the boundary functions
t\mapsto f(it)
and
t\mapsto f(1+it)
are bounded.

For
t\in]0,1[
, the
complex interpolation norm

N_{t}=\|\cdot\|_{t}
on
V
is defined by

\|v\|_{t}=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in\mathcal{F}(V)\\
f(t)=v\end{subarray}}\Big\{\max\big(\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}}\|f(iy)\|_{0},\;\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}}\|f(1+iy)\|_{1}\big)\Big\}.

(2.77)

To make a connection between the construction of geodesics and complex interpolation, we note that by the interpolation theorem of Stein-Weiss, see
[
4
, Theorem 5.4.1]
, a complex interpolation between two Hermitian norms is given by the geodesic connecting them.

From the above definition, it is immediate to see that complex interpolation is a monotone operation in the sense that if the complex interpolation between
N_{0}
and
N_{1}
is bigger than the complex interpolation between
N^{\prime}_{0}
and
N^{\prime}_{1}
for any norms verifying
N_{0}\geq N^{\prime}_{0}
and
N_{1}\geq N^{\prime}_{1}
.

2.6
Geometric quantization on ample line bundles

The main goal of this section is to recall some geometric quantization results in the ample setting.

We fix an ample line bundle
A
over
X
. Let
dV_{X}
be a smooth volume form on
X
. The following result was established by Chen-Sun
[
20
]
for
p=2
and by Berndtsson
[
10
]
for
p\in[1,+\infty[
, both under additional regularity assumptions on the metrics. These regularity assumptions were later relaxed by Darvas-Lu-Rubinstein
[
25
]
.
See also Darvas-Xia
[
27
, Lemma 2.10, Theorem 2.11]
for further developments.

Theorem 2.23
.

For any bounded metrics
h^{A}_{0}
,
h^{A}_{1}
on
A
with psh potentials, and any
p\in[1,+\infty[
,

d_{p}(h^{A}_{0},h^{A}_{1})=\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{d_{p}(\operatorname{Hilb}_{k}(h^{A}_{0},dV_{X}),\operatorname{Hilb}_{k}(h^{A}_{1},dV_{X}))}{k}.

(2.78)

Let us explain that Theorem
2.23
implies the following version of Theorem
1.1
.

Proposition 2.24
.

For any continuous metrics
h^{A}_{0}
,
h^{A}_{1}
on
A
with psh potentials, and any
p\in[1,+\infty[
, we have

d_{p}(h^{A}_{0},h^{A}_{1})=\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{d_{p}(\operatorname{Ban}_{k}(h^{A}_{0}),\operatorname{Ban}_{k}(h^{A}_{1}))}{k}.

(2.79)

Proof.

By Proposition
2.18
, together with (
2.59
) and (
2.72
), we see that

\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{d_{p}(\operatorname{Ban}_{k}(h^{A}_{0}),\operatorname{Ban}_{k}(h^{A}_{1}))}{k}=\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{d_{p}(\operatorname{Hilb}_{k}(h^{A}_{0},dV_{X}),\operatorname{Hilb}_{k}(h^{A}_{1},dV_{X}))}{k}.

(2.80)

We conclude by applying Theorem
2.23
.
∎

Since the envelope
P(h^{A})
is continuous for any continuous metric
h^{A}
on
A
by the discussion before Theorem
2.16
, we see that Proposition
2.24
and (
2.59
) imply that Theorem
1.1
holds for ample line bundles.

We also note that in
[
25
]
, in the setting of Theorem
2.23
the authors considered metrics with more singular potentials arising from the corresponding finite energy spaces.
It is reasonable to expect that analogous results should hold in the big setting as well.

Now, for any bounded metrics
h^{A}_{0}
,
h^{A}_{1}
on
A
with psh potentials, we denote by
h^{A}_{t}
,
t\in[0,1]
, the Mabuchi geodesic between them.
For any
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
t\in[0,1]
, we consider the geodesic
H_{k,t}
between
\operatorname{Hilb}_{k}(h^{A}_{0},dV_{X})
and
\operatorname{Hilb}_{k}(h^{A}_{1},dV_{X})
, as described after (
2.65
).
For regular metrics
h^{A}_{0}
and
h^{A}_{1}
, the following result was established by Berndtsson
[
10
]
, following the previous work of Phong-Sturm
[
59
]
, establishing a weaker form of convergence.
These regularity assumptions were later relaxed in
[
25
]
.

Theorem 2.25
.

For any
t\in[0,1]
, the sequence of metrics
FS(H_{k,t})^{\frac{1}{k}}
on
A
converges to
h^{A}_{t}
as
k\to\infty
, with respect to any
d_{p}
-distance, for
p\in[1,+\infty[
. Moreover, if
h^{A}_{0}
and
h^{A}_{1}
are continuous, then the convergence is uniform.

Let us explain that Theorem
2.25
implies the following version of Theorem
1.5
.

Proposition 2.26
.

For any continuous metrics
h^{A}_{0}
,
h^{A}_{1}
on
A
with psh potentials, in the notations of Theorem
1.5
, the sequence of metrics
FS(N_{k,t})^{\frac{1}{k}}
converges uniformly towards
h^{A}_{t}
, as
k\to\infty
.

Proof.

By the monotonicity properties of complex interpolation explained after (
2.77
), the fact that the metric geodesic
H_{k,t}
can be interpreted as complex interpolation between
\operatorname{Hilb}_{k}(h^{A}_{0},dV_{X})
,
\operatorname{Hilb}_{k}(h^{A}_{1},dV_{X})
, and Proposition
2.18
, we see that for any
\epsilon>0
, there is
k_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
, so that for any
k\geq k_{0}
,
t\in[0,1]
, we have

N_{k,t}\cdot\exp(-\epsilon k)\leq H_{k,t}\leq N_{k,t}\cdot\exp(\epsilon k).

(2.81)

The result now follows from Theorem
2.25
and (
2.49
).
∎

Finally, let us consider a graded submultiplicative norm
N=(N_{k})_{k=0}^{\infty}
,
N_{k}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\|\cdot\|_{k}
, on the section ring
R(X,A)
.
We assume that there is a bounded metric
h^{A}_{0}
on
A
such that
N_{k}\geq\operatorname{Ban}^{\infty}_{k}(h^{A}_{0})
for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
Then by the ample version of Lemma
2.19
from
[
41
, Lemma 3.3]
, the sequence of metrics
FS(N_{k})^{\frac{1}{k}}
converges pointwise, as
k\to\infty
, to a bounded metric
FS(N)
on
A
, and the lower semicontinuous regularization
FS(N)_{*}
of
FS(N)
has a psh potential.
The following statement is the main result of the previous work of the author
[
41
]
, which obviously refines Theorem
1.3
for ample line bundles.

Theorem 2.27
.

For any
p\in[1,+\infty[
, we have

N\sim_{p}\operatorname{Ban}^{\infty}(FS(N)_{*}).

(2.82)

If, moreover,
FS(N)
is continuous, then (
2.82
) can be strengthened as follows: for any
\epsilon>0
, there is
k_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for any
k\geq k_{0}
, we have

\operatorname{Ban}_{k}^{\infty}(FS(N))\leq N_{k}\leq\operatorname{Ban}_{k}^{\infty}(FS(N))\cdot\exp(\epsilon k).

(2.83)

Remark 2.28
.

See Theorem
4.1
for the generalization of (
2.83
) to singular spaces.

Approximations by section rings of ample line bundles

This section is devoted to one of the principal tools of this article: an approximation procedure that reduces the study of section rings of big line bundles to those of ample line bundles.
More precisely, in Section
3.1
, we describe several subring constructions in a section ring of a big line bundle.
In Section
3.2
, we describe how to relate distances between norms on a vector space and a subspace.
Finally, in Section
3.3
, we apply both of these techniques to prove Theorem
1.1
.

3.1
Subrings of section rings of big line bundles

A key technique in this paper is to reduce statements concerning big line bundles to ample line bundles.
To achieve this, we construct “sufficiently large” subrings inside of the section ring of a big line bundle, which can be realized as section rings of ample line bundles.

In this section we review two different constructions of such subrings.
The first construction is purely algebraic, whereas the second is more analytic.
Throughout this section we fix a complex projective manifold
X
, and a big line bundle
L
on
X
.

Construction 1
:
the subring generated by sections of a fixed degree
.

Fix
m\in\mathbb{N}
and consider the subring
[{\rm{Sym}}H^{0}(X,L^{\otimes m})]\subset R(X,L^{\otimes m})
generated by
H^{0}(X,L^{\otimes m})
.
Let
[{\rm{Sym}}^{k}H^{0}(X,L^{\otimes m})]
be the
k
-th graded component of
[{\rm{Sym}}H^{0}(X,L^{\otimes m})]
.

Theorem 3.1

(Lazarsfeld-Mustață
[
50
, Theorem D]
)
.

For any
\epsilon>0
, there is
m_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for any
m\geq m_{0}
, there is
k_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for
k\geq k_{0}
, we have

\frac{\dim[{\rm{Sym}}^{k}H^{0}(X,L^{\otimes m})]}{\dim H^{0}(X,L^{\otimes km})}\geq 1-\epsilon.

(3.1)

Below we give a proof of Theorem
3.1
, distinct from that in
[
50
]
. This is not only for completeness, but also because it relies on the observation that the ring
\oplus_{k=0}^{\infty}[{\rm{Sym}}^{k}H^{0}(X,L^{\otimes m})]
coincides in high degrees with the section ring of an
ample
line bundle over a
singular space
.
This will play an important role in our proof of Theorem
1.3
.

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

We denote by
Y_{m}
the Zariski closure of the image of the Kodaira map
X\dashrightarrow\mathbb{P}(H^{0}(X,L^{\otimes m})^{*})
.
As
L
is big, for
m\in\mathbb{N}
sufficiently large,
Y_{m}
is a complex analytic space of the same dimension as
X
, i.e.,
n
, cf.
[
51
, p. 139]
.
We consider the resolution of indeterminacies of the Kodaira map

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\hat{X}_{m}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Y_{m}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{P}(H^{0}(X,L^{\otimes m})^{*}).}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi_{m}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f_{m}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\rm{Kod}}_{m}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\iota_{m}}

(3.2)

The map
f_{m}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\hat{X}_{m}\to Y_{m}
is a birational modification, cf.
[
51
, p. 139]
.

If we denote by
E_{i}
,
i\in I
, the exceptional divisors on
\hat{X}_{m}
, then there are
b_{i}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for the divisor
E_{m}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\sum b_{i}\cdot E_{i}
, the evaluation map provides the isomorphism

\pi_{m}^{*}L^{\otimes(-m)}\otimes\mathscr{O}(E_{m})\to f_{m}^{*}\mathscr{O}(-1).

(3.3)

Over
\hat{X}_{m}
, we define the following line bundle

L_{m}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\pi_{m}^{*}L^{\otimes m}\otimes\mathscr{O}(-E_{m}).

(3.4)

We also denote by
A_{m}
the restriction of the hyperplane line bundle
\mathscr{O}(1)
on
Y_{m}
.
The isomorphism (

) induces the isomorphism

(3.5)

and birational invariance of the volume, cf.

, gives us

(3.6)

Also, for sufficiently large

, there is an isomorphism

[{\rm{Sym}}^{k}H^{0}(X,L^{\otimes m})]\overset{\sim}{\to}H^{0}(Y_{k},A_{m}^{\otimes k}).

(3.7)

To see this, first note that the restriction morphism from

to

induces a map
H^{0}(\mathbb{P}(H^{0}(X,L^{\otimes m})^{*}),\mathscr{O}(k))\to H^{0}(Y_{m},A_{m}^{\otimes k})
.
Using the standard identification

and the definition of

, this map factors through the evaluation map on

, which gives precisely the map (

).
That this map is an isomorphism for

sufficiently large follows from the fact that the restriction morphism is surjective for

sufficiently large, cf. the explanation after (

).

By (

) and (

), in order to prove (

3.2
Relating distances on a vector space and a subspace

In several parts of this article, we will need to compare the distances between two norms on a vector space and the distances between their restrictions to a subspace.
The following result, which constitutes the main result of this section, provides precisely this comparison.

Lemma 3.3
.

On a finitely dimensional vector space
V
, for any
p\in[1,+\infty[
, the
d_{p}
-distances between two norms
N_{0}
,
N_{1}
, are related with the respective
d_{p}
-distances between their restrictions
N_{0}|_{E}
,
N_{1}|_{E}
to a subspace
E\subset V
as

\Big|v\cdot d_{p}(N_{0},N_{1})^{p}-e\cdot d_{p}(N_{0}|_{E},N_{1}|_{E})^{p}\Big|\leq 2(v-e)\cdot C^{p}+20(1+\log v)v\cdot p\cdot C^{p-1}.

(3.18)

where
v\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\dim V
,
e\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\dim E
, and
C\geq 0
is such that
N_{0}\cdot\exp(-C)\leq N_{1}\leq N_{0}\cdot\exp(C)
.
In particular, if for some
\epsilon>0
, we have
e/v\geq 1-\epsilon
, then

\Big|d_{p}(N_{0},N_{1})^{p}-d_{p}(N_{0}|_{E},N_{1}|_{E})^{p}\Big|\leq 3\epsilon\cdot C^{p}+20(1+\log v)p\cdot C^{p-1}.

(3.19)

In order to prove Lemma
3.3
, we develop some notions from finite-dimensional functional analysis.
For two Hermitian norms
N_{0}
,
N_{1}
on
V
, we define the “rooftop” Hermitian norm
N_{0}\vee N_{1}
as follows: we choose a basis
e_{1},\ldots,e_{v}
of
V
,
v\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\dim V
, which is orthogonal for both
N_{0}
and
N_{1}
, and define
N_{0}\vee N_{1}
such that
e_{1},\ldots,e_{v}
is orthogonal for
N_{0}\vee N_{1}
, and normalized such that
\|e_{i}\|_{N_{0}\vee N_{1}}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\max\{\|e_{i}\|_{N_{0}},\|e_{i}\|_{N_{1}}\}
.

We stress out while we obviously have
N_{0}\leq N_{0}\vee N_{1}
and
N_{1}\leq N_{0}\vee N_{1}
, the space of Hermitian norms on
V
is not a lattice (unless
\dim V=1
), i.e., there is in general no minimal Hermitian norm which majorizes both
N_{0}
and
N_{1}
.
The reason for considering the norm
N_{0}\vee N_{1}
comes from the following Pythagorean formula

d_{p}(N_{0},N_{1})^{p}=d_{p}(N_{0},N_{0}\vee N_{1})^{p}+d_{p}(N_{0}\vee N_{1},N_{1})^{p}.

(3.20)

Unlike the space of Hermitian norms, the space of all norms does form a lattice.
Indeed, for any two norms
N_{0}
,
N_{1}
, the maximum
\max\{N_{0},N_{1}\}
gives a norm, which is also the least upper bound.
We claim that for Hermitian
N_{0}
,
N_{1}
, we have

\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}N_{0}\vee N_{1}\leq\max\{N_{0},N_{1}\}\leq N_{0}\vee N_{1}.

(3.21)

The second inequality in (
3.21
) is immediate, since
\max\{N_{0},N_{1}\}
is the smallest majorant of
N_{0}
and
N_{1}
among all possible norms, and
N_{0}\vee N_{1}
is a majorant.
The first inequality in (
3.21
) is a reformulation of the following elementary estimate: for any
a_{i},b_{i}>0
,
x_{i}\in\mathbb{C}
,
i=1,\ldots,v
,

\frac{1}{2}\sum\max\{a_{i},b_{i}\}\cdot|x_{i}|^{2}\leq\max\Big\{\sum a_{i}\cdot|x_{i}|^{2},\sum b_{i}\cdot|x_{i}|^{2},\Big\}.

(3.22)

Note that by the mean value theorem, for any
d>0
,
x,y\in\real
,
|x-y|\leq d
such that for some
C>0
,
|x|,|y|\leq C
, we have

||x|^{p}-|y|^{p}|\leq dp\cdot C^{p-1}.

(3.23)

By (
2.68
), (
3.20
), (
3.21
) and (
3.23
), we deduce that for arbitrary Hermitian norms
N_{0},N_{1}
on
V
, the following weak form of Pythagorean identity holds

\Big|d_{p}(N_{0},N_{1})^{p}-d_{p}(N_{0},\max\{N_{0},N_{1}\})^{p}-d_{p}(\max\{N_{0},N_{1}\},N_{1})^{p}\Big|\leq 10p\cdot C^{p-1}.

(3.24)

By (
2.71
), (
3.23
) and (
3.24
), we further see that for not necessarily Hermitian norms
N_{0},N_{1}
,

\Big|d_{p}(N_{0},N_{1})^{p}-d_{p}(N_{0},\max\{N_{0},N_{1}\})^{p}-d_{p}(\max\{N_{0},N_{1}\},N_{1})^{p}\Big|\leq 10(1+\log v)p\cdot C^{p-1}.

(3.25)

Proof of Lemma
3.3
.

Remark first that if we additionally assume that
N_{0}\leq N_{1}
, then we even have

\Big|v\cdot d_{p}(N_{0},N_{1})^{p}-e\cdot d_{p}(N_{0}|_{E},N_{1}|_{E})^{p}\Big|\leq(v-e)\cdot C^{p},

(3.26)

Indeed, from the definition of the logarithmic relative spectrum in (
2.67
), we see that for any
i=1,\cdots,e
, we have

\lambda_{i}(N_{0},N_{1})\geq\lambda_{i}(N_{0}|_{E},N_{1}|_{E})\geq\lambda_{i+v-e}(N_{0},N_{1}).

(3.27)

Since under the assumption
N_{0}\leq N_{1}\leq N_{0}\cdot\exp(C)
, we have
0\leq\lambda_{i}(N_{0},N_{1})\leq C
, for any
i=1,\cdots,v
, (
3.26
) follows immediately from (
2.70
) and (
3.27
).

By (
3.26
), using the fact that
\max\{N_{0},N_{1}\}|_{E}=\max\{N_{0}|_{E},N_{1}|_{E}\}
, for
i=0,1
, we obtain

\Big|v\cdot d_{p}(N_{i},\max\{N_{0},N_{1}\})^{p}-e\cdot d_{p}(N_{i}|_{E},\max\{N_{0}|_{E},N_{1}|_{E}\})^{p}\Big|\leq(v-e)\cdot C^{p}.

(3.28)

We obtain (
3.18
) by summing up the above equation for

, using the triangle inequality and (
3.25
) twice: for the pair
(N_{0},N_{1})
and for
(N_{0}|_{E},N_{1}|_{E})
.
∎

4

Characterization of submultiplicative norms

The main goal of this section is to characterize the submultiplicative norms as sup-norms, i.e., to show Theorem
1.3
.
More specifically, in Section
4.1
, we establish a version of Theorem
2.27
valid for singular varieties.
Then in Section
4.2
, we study the sup-norms associated with metrics whose potentials have minimal singularities.
Finally, in Section
4.3
, we establish Theorem
1.3
.

4.1
Submultiplicative norms on section rings of singular spaces

The main purpose of this section is to investigate submultiplicative norms on section rings of ample line bundles over singular spaces, thereby extending our previous work
[
41
]
to the singular setting.
This will be essential for the proof of Theorem
1.3
, where singular varieties naturally arise.

Our argument will largely parallel the one in
[
41
]
, with one notable difference: in
[
41
, §3.1]
we made use of the results from
[
36
]
, which concern
L^{2}
-norms rather than sup-norms.
In the singular case, however,
L^{2}
-norms are significantly harder to control due to the absence of the appropriate version of the Ohsawa-Takegoshi extension theorem, and it is not clear whether the results of
[
36
]
extend fully to singular varieties.
The novelty of our approach lies in avoiding the use of
[
36
]
altogether, and working instead directly with sup-norms.
This is feasible because, for sup-norms, Bost
[
13
]
and Randriambololona
[
63
]
established a version of the Ohsawa-Takegoshi extension theorem that remains valid in the singular setting; see Theorem
4.4
.

We fix a reduced compact complex space
(Y,\mathscr{O}_{Y})
endowed with an ample line bundle
A
, see
[
33
, §II.5]
for the basic introduction to analysis on complex spaces.
Below, whenever we refer to smooth functions on
Y
or smooth metrics on
A
, this is always understood as the restriction of a smooth function or metric defined in a local analytic chart.
The same applies for the positivity notion of a metric on a line bundle, and it can be seen that the resulting definition doesn’t depend on the choice of the analytic chart, see
[
57
, Lemma 4]
.

By Fekete’s lemma, cf. Lemma
2.19
, for any submultiplicative norm
N
on
R(Y,A)
, the sequence of metrics
FS(N_{k})^{\frac{1}{k}}
on
A
converges, as
k\to\infty
, to a (possibly only bounded from above and even null) metric on
A
, which we denote by
FS(N)
.
Throughout this section we assume that
FS(N)
is continuous.
In particular, it is a nowhere zero metric.
Immediately from (
2.10
) and (
2.48
), we see that this implies that
\operatorname{Ban}^{\infty}(FS(N))\leq N
, i.e., one side of the inequality required in the definition of a bounded norm is satisfied.
Another side of the inequality follows from the finite generation of the section ring; moreover, the following stronger statement holds.

Theorem 4.1
.

Assume that a graded norm
N=(N_{k})_{k=0}^{\infty}
over the section ring
R(Y,A)
of an ample line bundle
A
is submultiplicative and the metric
FS(N)
on
A
is continuous.
Then for any
\epsilon>0
, there is
k_{0}\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
such that for any
k\geq k_{0}
, we have

\operatorname{Ban}^{\infty}_{k}(FS(N))\leq N_{k}\leq\exp(\epsilon k)\cdot\operatorname{Ban}^{\infty}_{k}(FS(N)).

(4.1)

In the smooth case, Theorem
4.1
recovers one of the main results of
[
41
]
, see Theorem
2.27
.
Similarly to
[
41
]
, projective and injective tensor norms play a central role in our proof of Theorem
4.1
.
We therefore begin by recalling the definitions.

Let
V_{1},V_{2}
be two finite dimensional vector spaces endowed with norms
N_{i}=\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}_{i}
,
i=1,2
.
The
projective tensor norm

N_{1}\otimes_{\pi}N_{2}=\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}_{\otimes_{\pi}}
on
V_{1}\otimes V_{2}
is defined for
f\in V_{1}\otimes V_{2}
as

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert f\right\rVert}_{\otimes_{\pi}}=\inf\Big\{\sum\|x_{i}\|_{1}\cdot\|y_{i}\|_{2}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\quad f=\sum x_{i}\otimes y_{i}\Big\},

(4.2)

where the infimum is taken over different ways of partitioning
f
into a sum of decomposable terms.
The
injective tensor norm

N_{1}\otimes_{\epsilon}N_{2}=\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}_{\otimes_{\epsilon}}
on
V_{1}\otimes V_{2}
is defined as

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert f\right\rVert}_{\otimes_{\epsilon}}=\sup\Big\{\big|(\phi\otimes\psi)(f)\big|\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\quad\phi\in V_{1}^{*},\psi\in V_{2}^{*},\|\phi\|_{1}^{*}=\|\psi\|_{2}^{*}=1\Big\}

(4.3)

where
\|\cdot\|_{i}^{*}
,
i=1,2
, are the dual norms associated with
\|\cdot\|_{i}
.
Lemma below compares injective and projective tensor norms, see
[
70
, Proposition 6.1]
,
[
1
, Theorem 21]
for a proof.

Lemma 4.2
.

The following inequality between the norms on
V_{1}\otimes V_{2}
holds

N_{1}\otimes_{\epsilon}N_{2}\leq N_{1}\otimes_{\pi}N_{2}\leq N_{1}\otimes_{\epsilon}N_{2}\cdot\min\{\dim V_{1},\dim V_{2}\}.

(4.4)

One of the key technical insights in
[
41
]
is that, although the injective and projective tensor norms on the full tensor algebra of a fixed finitely dimensional vector space may differ substantially, their restrictions to the symmetric subalgebra are in fact equivalent, up to a subexponential factor.
We now recall this statement.

We first recall some constructions.
A norm
N_{V}=\|\cdot\|_{V}
on a finite-dimensional vector space
V
naturally induces the norm
\|\cdot\|_{Q}
on any quotient
Q
,
\pi\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}V\to Q
of
V
through

\|f\|_{Q}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\inf\big\{\|g\|_{V};\quad g\in V,\pi(g)=f\},\qquad f\in Q.

(4.5)

At times we denote the quotient norm by
[N_{V}]
, leaving the underlying quotient space implicit.

Recall that for any
k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, we have the
symmetrization
map
{\rm{Sym}}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}V^{\otimes k}\to{\rm{Sym}}^{k}(V)
.
Consider two norms
{\rm{Sym}}^{k}_{\epsilon}(N_{V})\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\|\cdot\|^{{\rm{Sym}},\epsilon}_{N_{V},k}
and
{\rm{Sym}}^{k}_{\pi}(N_{V})\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\|\cdot\|^{{\rm{Sym}},\pi}_{N_{V},k}
on symmetric tensors
{\rm{Sym}}^{k}(V)
, induced by the symmetrization map by the quotient construction, and the norms
N_{V}\otimes_{\epsilon}\cdots\otimes_{\epsilon}N_{V}
,
N_{V}\otimes_{\pi}\cdots\otimes_{\pi}N_{V}
on
V^{\otimes k}
.
Define the norm
{\rm{Sym}}^{k}_{{\rm{ev}}}(N_{V})\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\|\cdot\|^{{\rm{ev}}}_{N_{V},k}
on
{\rm{Sym}}^{k}(V)
as

\|P\|^{{\rm{ev}}}_{N_{V},k}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in V^{*}\\
\|v\|_{V}^{*}\leq 1\end{subarray}}|P(v)|,\qquad P\in{\rm{Sym}}^{k}(V).

(4.6)

Theorem 4.3

(
[
41
, Theorem 3.13]
)
.

For any
\epsilon>0
, there is
k_{0}\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
such that for any
k\geq k_{0}
, the following chain of inequalities is satisfied

\exp(-\epsilon k)\cdot{\rm{Sym}}_{\pi}^{k}(N_{V})\leq{\rm{Sym}}_{{\rm{ev}}}^{k}(N_{V})\leq{\rm{Sym}}_{\epsilon}^{k}(N_{V})\leq{\rm{Sym}}_{\pi}^{k}(N_{V}).

(4.7)

We shall also extensively rely on the following result due to Bost
[
13
, Theorem A.1]
and Randriambololona
[
63
, Théorème B]
, which can be seen as an asymptotic version of the sup-version of Ohsawa-Takegoshi extension theorem, cf.
[
58
]
,
[
32
]
.
See also
[
38
, Theorem 1.10]
for a more precise result in the smooth setting.

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
(Y,\mathscr{O}_{Y})
be a reduced compact complex space equipped with an ample line bundle
A
carrying a smooth positive metric
h^{A}
, and let
E
be a vector bundle on
Y
endowed with a smooth metric
h^{E}
.
For any reduced compact complex analytic subspace
\iota\colon(Z,\mathscr{O}_{Z})\hookrightarrow(Y,\mathscr{O}_{Y})
, the following statement holds.
For any
\epsilon>0
, there is
k_{0}\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
such that for any
k\geq k_{0}
,
s\in H^{0}(Z,\iota^{*}A^{\otimes k}\otimes\iota^{*}E)
, there is
\tilde{s}\in H^{0}(Y,A^{\otimes k}\otimes E)
such that
\iota^{*}\tilde{s}=s
and

\sup_{y\in Y}|\tilde{s}(y)|_{(h^{A})^{k}\cdot h^{E}}\leq\sup_{z\in Z}|s(z)|_{(h^{A})^{k}\cdot h^{E}}\cdot\exp(\epsilon k).

(4.8)

The preceding result admits a reformulation in the framework of quotient norms, a perspective that will be important for the arguments below.
To formulate it, for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
, consider the restriction map

{\rm{Res}}_{Z}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}H^{0}(Y,A^{\otimes k}\otimes E)\to H^{0}(Z,\iota^{*}A^{\otimes k}\otimes\iota^{*}E).

(4.9)

One easily sees that for sufficiently large
k
, the map (
4.9
) is surjective.
Indeed it follows immediately by Serre’s vanishing theorem and the long exact sequence in cohomology

\cdots\to H^{0}(Y,A^{\otimes k}\otimes E)\to H^{0}(Z,\iota^{*}A^{\otimes k}\otimes\iota^{*}E)\to H^{1}(Y,A^{\otimes k}\otimes E\otimes\mathcal{J}_{Z})\to\cdots,

(4.10)

associated with the short exact sequence of sheaves

0\to\mathcal{J}_{Z}\to\mathscr{O}_{Y}\to\iota_{*}\mathscr{O}_{Z}\to 0,

(4.11)

where
\mathcal{J}_{Z}
denotes the sheaf of holomorphic functions on
Y
vanishing along
Z
.
Hence, a norm on
H^{0}(Y,A^{\otimes k}\otimes E)
induces by (
4.9
) a norm on
H^{0}(Z,\iota^{*}A^{\otimes k}\otimes\iota^{*}E)
.
We denote by
[\operatorname{Ban}^{\infty}_{1}(Y,(h^{A})^{k}\cdot h^{E})]
the quotient norm on
H^{0}(Z,A^{\otimes k}\otimes E)
associated with the norm
\operatorname{Ban}^{\infty}_{1}(Y,(h^{A})^{k}\cdot h^{E})
on
H^{0}(Y,A^{\otimes k}\otimes E)
.
Theorem
4.4
then reformulates as follows: for any
\epsilon>0
, there is
k_{0}\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
such that for any
k\geq k_{0}
, we have

[\operatorname{Ban}^{\infty}_{1}(Y,(h^{A})^{k}\cdot h^{E})]\leq\operatorname{Ban}^{\infty}_{1}(Z,(h^{A})^{k}\cdot h^{E})\cdot\exp(\epsilon k).

(4.12)

Note that the following inverse inequality is immediate

\operatorname{Ban}^{\infty}_{1}(Z,(h^{A})^{k}\cdot h^{E})\leq[\operatorname{Ban}^{\infty}_{1}(Y,(h^{A})^{k}\cdot h^{E})],

(4.13)

as it simply says that the sup-norm of the restriction of a section is no bigger than the sup-norm of the section.

As a first application of the result above, we extend Theorem
4.4
from the case of the symmetric algebra to the more general setting of the section ring associated with an arbitrary ample line bundle.
For any
r\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
,
k;k_{1},\ldots,k_{r}\in\mathbb{N}
,
k_{1}+\cdots+k_{r}=k
, we define the multiplication map

{\rm{Mult}}_{k_{1},\cdots,k_{r}}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}H^{0}(Y,A^{k_{1}})\otimes\cdots\otimes H^{0}(Y,A^{k_{r}})\to H^{0}(Y,A^{\otimes k}),

(4.14)

as
f_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes f_{r}\mapsto f_{1}\cdots f_{r}
.
As
A
is assumed to be ample, it is standard that there is
k_{0}\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
such that for any
k_{1},\cdots,k_{r}\geq k_{0}
, the map
{\rm{Mult}}_{k_{1},\cdots,k_{r}}
is surjective, cf.
[
51
, Example 1.2.22]
.

Assume now that
k,l\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
are sufficiently large so that the multiplication map
{\rm Mult}_{k,l}
is surjective.
A straightforward verification shows that, using the notations of (
4.2
) and (
4.5
), the submultiplicativity condition can be reformulated in terms of inequalities between the norms on
H^{0}(Y,A^{\otimes(k+l)})
as follows:

N_{k+l}\leq[N_{k}\otimes_{\pi}N_{l}],

(4.15)

where the bracket
[\cdot]
above is interpreted with respect to
{\rm Mult}_{k,l}
.

For simplicity, we further assume that
A
is very ample and all the multiplication maps are surjective.
By the finite generation of the ring
R(Y,A)
, the above can always be satisfied upon considering
R(Y,A^{N})
a sufficiently large
N\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
.

Let
N_{1}
be a norm on
H^{0}(Y,A)
.
By the surjectivity of the multiplication maps, we endow
H^{0}(Y,A^{\otimes k})
with the norms
N_{k}^{\pi}=[N_{1}\otimes_{\pi}\cdots\otimes_{\pi}N_{1}]
and
N_{k}^{\epsilon}=[N_{1}\otimes_{\epsilon}\cdots\otimes_{\epsilon}N_{1}]
, where the tensor powers are repeated
k

According to (
4.15
), the norm
N^{\pi}=(N_{k}^{\pi})_{k=0}^{\infty}
is the biggest submultiplicative norm on
R(Y,A)
, extending
N_{1}
.
We now prove the following result, which may be regarded as an instance of Theorem
4.1
, specialized to
N^{\pi}
.

Proposition 4.5
.

For any
\epsilon>0
, there is
k_{0}\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
such that for any
k\geq k_{0}
, the following chain of inequalities is satisfied

\exp(-\epsilon k)\cdot N_{k}^{\pi}\leq\operatorname{Ban}^{\infty}_{k}(FS(N_{1}))\leq N_{k}^{\epsilon}\leq N_{k}^{\pi}.

(4.16)

Proof.

The proof follows that of
[
41
, Theorem 3.1]
, which is done in the non-singular setting with essentially one modification: instead of the semiclassical Ohsawa-Takegoshi extension theorem from
[
38
]
, we apply Theorem
4.4
.
For the convenience of the reader, we reproduce the argument below with minor simplifications.
First of all, the inequality

follows from the respective inequality on the level of tensor norms from Lemma

.
The inequality
\operatorname{Ban}^{\infty}(FS(N_{1}))\leq N^{\epsilon}
is also rather immediate.
Indeed, by (

\operatorname{Ban}^{\infty}_{1}(FS(N_{1}))\leq N_{1}
.
Hence, by the order-preserving nature of the tensor norms, we deduce

[\operatorname{Ban}^{\infty}_{1}(FS(N_{1}))\otimes_{\epsilon}\cdots\otimes_{\epsilon}\operatorname{Ban}^{\infty}_{1}(FS(N_{1}))]\leq N_{k}^{\epsilon}.

(4.17)

Note that in the notations of Theorem

\operatorname{Ban}^{\infty}_{1}(FS(N_{1}))\otimes_{\epsilon}\cdots\otimes_{\epsilon}\operatorname{Ban}^{\infty}_{1}(FS(N_{1}))={\rm{Sym}}_{\epsilon}^{k}(\operatorname{Ban}^{\infty}_{1}(FS(N_{1}))).

(4.18)

Immediately from the definitions, we see that for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

{\rm{Sym}}_{{\rm{ev}}}^{k}(\operatorname{Ban}^{\infty}_{1}(FS(N_{1})))=\operatorname{Ban}^{\infty}_{k}(\mathbb{P}(H^{0}(Y,A)^{*}),FS(N_{1}))),

(4.19)

where above by an abuse of notation, we denoted the metric on
\mathscr{O}(1)
induced by
N_{1}
as
FS(N_{1})
.

Let us consider the Kodaira embedding
{\rm{Kod}}_{1}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}Y\hookrightarrow\mathbb{P}(H^{0}(Y,A)^{*})

We denote by
{\rm{Res}}_{{\rm{Kod}}}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}R(\mathbb{P}(H^{0}(Y,A)^{*}),\mathscr{O}(1))\to R(Y,A)

The multiplication operator

4.2
Regularizable from above psh metrics and sup-norms

The primary aim of this section is to investigate the sup-norms associated with general metrics having potentials with minimal singularities instead of those given by the envelopes of continuous metrics.
The reason for presenting this study here is that the Fubini-Study metric corresponding to an arbitrary bounded norm is not in general given by an envelope of a continuous metric, cf.
[
41
, Proposition 3.8]
for an example.
Consequently, the methods from Section
4.1
cannot be used as-is to prove Theorem
1.3
.
The main goal of the section is to show that the asymptotic study of sup-norms associated with general metrics having potentials with minimal singularities reduces to the study of the sup-norms associated with the so-called
regularizable from above psh metrics
.

For ample line bundles, this phenomenon was already observed by Boucksom-Eriksson
[
15
]
and later developed by the author
[
41
]
.
Accordingly, the present section can be viewed as a generalization of those results to the big case.

The main technical challenge in extending the aforementioned works to the big setting arises from the need to identify a suitable substitute for the class of continuous metrics with plurisubharmonic potentials that is stable under taking minima.
This is a subtle issue, as due to singularities it requires working on the birational models of a given manifold rather than on the manifold itself – a difficulty that does not occur in the ample case.

Throughout this section, we fix a complex projective manifold
X
and a big line bundle
L
over
X
.
We say that a function
\psi\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}X\to[-\infty,+\infty[
has
weakly neat analytic singularities
if for a certain birational model
\pi\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\hat{X}\to X
, locally,
\psi\circ\pi
can be written as in (
2.1
), where

is continuous.
We say that a function
\psi\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}X\to[-\infty,+\infty[
has
weakly neat algebraic singularities
if in addition to the above condition,

is rational.

Proposition 4.6
.

Assume that
\psi_{i}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}X\to[-\infty,+\infty[
,

Then
\max\{\psi_{0},\psi_{1}\}
also has weakly neat algebraic singularities.

Remark 4.7
.

a) Since the maximum of two smooth functions is, in general, not smooth, it follows that the maximum of two potentials with neat algebraic singularities will not, in general, have neat algebraic singularities.
More strikingly, while the maximum of two continuous functions of course remains continuous, if, instead of weakly neat analytic singularities, one considers the smaller class of potentials for which the function

Indeed, as we know from analysis on Section

Proposition

We can now proceed to the main definition of this section.

Definition 4.8
.

We say that a metric
h^{L}
on
L
with a potential of minimal singularities is regularizable from above if there is a decreasing sequence of metrics
h^{L}_{k}
on
L
, the potentials of which are psh and have weakly neat algebraic singularities, and which converge, as
k\to\infty
, towards a metric
h^{L,0}
, so that
h^{L,0}\geq h^{L}\geq h^{L,0}_{*}
.
If
h^{L}
moreover has a psh potential, we say that
h^{L}
is a regularizable from above psh metric, and the above condition is equivalent to requiring that
h^{L}_{k}
converge, as
k\to\infty
, towards
h^{L}
almost everywhere.

We note that in the above definition, no upper semicontinuity assumption is imposed on the potential of
h^{L}
.
Nevertheless, from
[
33
, (I.4.23)]
, it follows that whenever
h^{L}
is regularizable from above, its lower semicontinuous regularization
h^{L}_{*}
is a regularizable from above psh metric.
Moreover, by
[
2
]
,
h^{L}=h^{L}_{*}
outside of a pluripolar subset.

From Proposition
4.6
and Remark
4.7
b), it is immediate that a class of regularizable from above metrics is closed under the formation of the minima and products.
We also point out that while every metric with a psh potential can be approximated from below by metrics with neat algebraic singularities by Theorem
2.3
, not every metric with a psh potential is regularizable from above.
Indeed, the discontinuity set of a regularizable from above metric is necessarily pluripolar by the Bedford-Taylor characterization of negligible sets
[
2
, §5]
; see also
[
3
]
for a converse statement.

While the above definition is, a priori, more general than the one used in the ample case, see
[
41
, Definition 1.5]
, the following proposition shows that they are equivalent.

Proposition 4.9
.

A metric
h^{L}
with a bounded potential on an ample line bundle
L
is regularizable from above if and only if there is a decreasing sequence of metrics
h^{L}_{k}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
, on
L
, which have continuous psh potentials and which converge towards
h^{L}
almost everywhere.

Proof.

One direction is immediate, and we only have to establish that if there is a decreasing sequence of metrics
h^{L}_{k}
on
L
, which have weakly neat algebraic singularities, then one can construct
h^{L}_{k,0}
which will be in addition continuous.
To see this, fix an arbitrary metric
h^{L}_{-1}
on
L
which has a continuous psh potential, and which verifies
h^{L}_{-1}\geq h^{L}
.
We claim that the sequence
h^{L}_{k,0}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\min\{h^{L}_{-1},h^{L}_{k}\}
verifies the above claim.
Indeed,
h^{L}_{k,0}
has a psh potential, as the maximum of two psh functions is psh.
The sequence
h^{L}_{k,0}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
, also converges towards
h^{L}
almost everywhere, as
h^{L}_{k}
does so.
Finally,
h^{L}_{k,0}
is continuous.
Indeed, the potential of
h^{L}_{-1}
is continuous, and by assumption the potential of
h^{L}_{k}
is continuous outside its polar set. The maximum of two such potentials is obviously continuous.
∎

In what follows, for any metric
h^{L}
with a potential of minimal singularities, we shall associate a certain regularizable from above metric.
This will be done through an envelope construction, replicating what was done in the ample case in
[

For this, we define

Q(h^{L})\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\inf\Big\{h^{L}_{0}\text{ with a psh potential}\\
\text{of weakly neat algebraic singularities verifying }h^{L}\leq h^{L}_{0}\Big\}.

(4.36)

Note that by Theorem
2.3
, there are metrics with neat algebraic singularities on
L
, and since
h^{L}
has a potential with minimal singularities, the above infimum is taken over a nonempty set.

Proposition 4.10
.

For any metric
h^{L}
with a potential of minimal singularities, the metric
Q(h^{L})
is regularizable from above.

Proof.

Choquet’s lemma, cf.
[
33
, (I.4.23)]
, implies that there is a sequence
h^{L}_{i,0}
,
i\in\mathbb{N}
, of metrics on

with psh potentials of weakly neat algebraic singularities such that

and

Q(h^{L})_{*}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\big\{\inf_{i\in\mathbb{N}}h^{L}_{i,0}\big\}_{*}.

(4.37)

By Proposition
4.6
, the sequence

h^{L}_{i}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\min\{h^{L}_{0,0},h^{L}_{1,0},\ldots,h^{L}_{i,0}\},

(4.38)

provides a decreasing sequence of metrics on
L
with psh potentials of weakly neat algebraic singularities.
We denote by
h^{L,0}
the pointwise limit of
h^{L}_{i}
, as

Note that since
h^{L}_{i}\geq h^{L}
, we have
h^{L}_{i}\geq Q(h^{L})
, and as a consequence
h^{L,0}\geq Q(h^{L})
.
However, by (

h^{L,0}_{*}=Q(h^{L})_{*}
, and so we have
h^{L,0}\geq Q(h^{L})\geq h^{L,0}_{*}
, which finishes the proof.
∎

.

For any metric
h^{L}
with a potential of minimal singularities, we have
Q(Q(h^{L}))=Q(P(h^{L}))=Q(h^{L})

Proof.

Note that we have the following inequalities

(4.39)

By the definition of

, we then have

Proof of Theorem
4.13
.

As an application of the above analysis, we establish the following result.

Proposition 4.15

For any metric
h^{L}
with a potential of minimal singularities, and any

\operatorname{Ban}^{\infty}(h^{L})\sim_{p}\operatorname{Ban}^{\infty}(Q(h^{L})_{*}).

Proof.

Remark first that if
h^{L}

.

Let us hence proceed to the proof of (

By Proposition
4.12
, it suffices to establish that

\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{d_{p}(\operatorname{Ban}^{\infty}_{k}(Q(h^{L})),\operatorname{Ban}^{\infty}_{k}(Q(h^{L})_{*}))}{k}=0.

4.13
, as both

.
∎

In the proof of Theorem

Lemma 5.1
.

Assume that

is a graded algebra such that

N_{t}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=(N_{k,t})_{k=0}^{\infty}
, composed from complex interpolations

between

and

is submultiplicative.

Proof.

The result follows immediately from the definition (
2.77

For completeness, let us provide an argument.
For given

,

Then in the notations of (

\|s_{1}\cdot s_{2}\|_{N_{k+l,t}}=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in\mathcal{F}(R_{k+l})\\
f(t)=s_{1}\cdot s_{2}\end{subarray}}\Big\{\max\big(\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}}\|f(iy)\|_{N_{k+l,0}},\;\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}}\|f(1+iy)\|_{N_{k+l,1}}\big)\Big\}.

(5.1)

Since we have
\mathcal{F}(R_{k})\cdot\mathcal{F}(R_{l})\subset\mathcal{F}(R_{k+l})
, the right-hand side of (
5.1
) increases if we replace the infimum over
f\in\mathcal{F}(R_{k+l})
verifying

by the infimum over
f_{1}\in\mathcal{F}(R_{k})
,
f_{2}\in\mathcal{F}(R_{l})
verifying

,
f_{2}(t)=s_{2}
.
Moreover, by the submultiplicativity of

,

and

\leq\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}}\max\big(\|f_{1}(iy)\|_{N_{k,0}},\;\|f_{1}(1+iy)\|_{N_{k,1}}\big)\\
\cdot\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}}\max\big(\|f_{2}(iy)\|_{N_{l,0}},\;\|f_{2}(1+iy)\|_{N_{l,1}}\big).

(5.2)

Combining all of the above bounds yields the bound
\|s_{1}\cdot s_{2}\|_{N_{k+l,t}}\leq\|s_{1}\|_{N_{k,t}}\cdot\|s_{2}\|_{N_{l,t}}
.
∎

5.2
Geodesic rays and submultiplicative filtrations

The main goal of this section is twofold: first, to extend the construction of the geodesic ray associated with a filtration from the ample case to the big case; and second, to show that the speed of this geodesic ray captures the statistical invariants of the filtration.

We begin with some necessary preliminaries from linear algebra: we discuss two different constructions of rays of norms associated with a filtration.

We fix a finitely-dimensional vector space
V
,
\dim V=v
, and a filtration
\mathcal{F}
of
V
.
We also fix a
Hermitian
norm
H_{0}
on
V
.
Consider an orthonormal basis
s_{1},\ldots,s_{v}
, of
V
, adapted to the filtration
\mathcal{F}
, i.e., verifying
s_{i}\in\mathcal{F}^{e_{\mathcal{F}}(i)}V

are defined as in (
1.17
).
We define the ray of Hermitian norms
H_{t}
,
t\in[0,+\infty[

by declaring the basis

(s_{1}^{t},\ldots,s_{v}^{t})\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\big(e^{te_{\mathcal{F}}(1)}s_{1},\ldots,e^{te_{\mathcal{F}}(v)}s_{v}\big),

(5.13)

to be orthonormal with respect to
H_{t}

It is immediate to verify that the resulting ray is geodesic with respect to the distances

(5.18)

However, immediately from the definitions, we see that
H_{k,t}
,
t\in[0,+\infty[
, is a metric geodesic for
d_{p}
, i.e., (
5.7
) holds under our new notations.
Using the weak triangle inequality (
2.73
) and the above estimate, we deduce

\Big|d_{p}(N_{k,t},N_{k,s})-d_{p}(H_{k,t},H_{k,s})\Big|\leq 50\log n_{k}.

(5.19)

The proof is now finished in exactly the same manner as in Theorem
1.5
.
∎

Recall that in the ample setting, for finitely generated filtrations, Phong-Sturm
[
61
]
proposed an alternative construction of geodesic rays using the geometric quantization of
L^{2}
-norms.
Later, still within the context of finitely generated filtrations, they
[
60
]
presented a construction based on solutions to homogeneous Monge-Ampère equations on the resolution of a test configuration, and demonstrated that this approach agrees with their earlier construction.

For arbitrary bounded submultiplicative filtrations in the ample setting, Ross-Witt Nyström
[
68
]
constructed geodesic rays using the language of test curves, and verified that for finitely generated filtrations, it recovers the construction from
[
61
]
.
Boucksom-Jonsson
[
17
]
later offered an alternative construction based on valuations and non-Archimedean geometry.

The construction given in Theorem
5.7
in the ample setting was carried out by the author in
[
41
]
, where it was also verified that it coincides with the constructions of Boucksom-Jonsson and Ross-Witt Nyström.
The equivalence between the latter two follows also from Darvas-Xia
[
27
]
.

One may reasonably expect that, in the big setting, in addition to the construction from Theorem
5.7
, all of the aforementioned constructions can also be performed, and that a similar equivalence statement holds.
We discuss one part of this story in Proposition
5.8
, but for brevity, we leave a formal statement and verification of the full claim to future work.

Proof of Theorem
1.7
.

The proof repeats verbatim the one given in the ample setting from
[
41
]
, the only difference is that one has to use the big versions of Proposition
2.23
and Theorem
2.27
.
For the convenience of the reader, we reproduce the argument below.

Let us first remark that it is enough to establish Theorem
1.7
in the special case when the filtration
\mathcal{F}
satisfies the additional assumption

\mathcal{F}^{0}R(X,L)=\{0\}.

(5.20)

To see this, remark that since
\mathcal{F}
is bounded, there is
C>0
, verifying
\mathcal{F}^{Ck}H^{0}(X,L^{\otimes k})=\{0\}
.
Consider now another filtration
\mathcal{F}_{0}
on
R(X,L)
, defined for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
\lambda\in\real
, as follows
\mathcal{F}_{0}^{\lambda}H^{0}(X,L^{\otimes k})=\mathcal{F}^{\lambda+Ck}H^{0}(X,L^{\otimes k})
.
Clearly,
\mathcal{F}_{0}
is submultiplicative and bounded whenever
\mathcal{F}
is submultiplicative and bounded.
An easy verification shows that establishing Theorem
1.7
for
\mathcal{F}_{0}
and
\mathcal{F}
is equivalent. We, hence, assume from now on that
\mathcal{F}
satisfies (
5.20
).

By (
5.13
), (
5.19
) and (
5.20
), we deduce that

\Big|d_{p}(N_{k,0},N_{k,1})-k\cdot\sqrt[p]{\int_{x\in\real}(-x)^{p}d\mu_{\mathcal{F},k}(x)}\Big|\leq 50\log n_{k}.

(5.21)

As in (
5.3
), from (
5.14
) and (
5.21
), we then deduce

d_{p}(FS(N_{0})_{*},FS(N_{1})_{*})=\lim_{k\to\infty}\sqrt[p]{\int_{x\in\real}(-x)^{p}d\mu_{\mathcal{F},k}(x)}.

(5.22)

Since
N_{k,t}\geq N_{k,0}
, we have
h^{L}_{t}\geq h^{L}_{0}
, and so
\dot{h}^{L}_{0}\leq 0
.
From Theorem
2.14
and (
5.22
), for any
p\in[1,+\infty[
, we then obtain

\int_{x\in\real}x^{p}d\mu_{\mathcal{F}}(x)=\lim_{k\to\infty}\int_{x\in\real}x^{p}d\mu_{\mathcal{F},k}(x).

(5.23)

Note that the measures
\mu_{\mathcal{F},k}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
, all have supports contained in the same compact subset.
As measures of compact support are characterized by their moments, (
5.23
) implies that
\mu_{\mathcal{F},k}
converge weakly, as
k\in\mathbb{N}
, towards
\mu_{\mathcal{F}}
, which finishes the proof.
∎

We conclude the paper by giving an alternative definition of
h^{L}_{t}
in the spirit of Phong-Sturm
[
61
]
and Ross-Witt Nyström
[
68
]
.
For this, for a continuous metric
h^{L}
on
L
and a smooth volume form
dV_{X}
on
X
, we denote by
H_{k,t}
,
t\in[0,1]
, the ray of Hermitian norms emanating from
\operatorname{Hilb}_{k}(h^{L},dV_{X})
and associated with
\mathcal{F}_{k}
as in (
5.13
).

Proposition 5.8
.

For any
t\in[0,+\infty[
, the sequence of metrics
FS(H_{k,t})^{\frac{1}{k}}
converges, as
k\to\infty
, towards a singular metric
h^{L}_{t,0}
, so that for the ray
h^{L}_{t}
from Theorem
5.7
, we have
h^{L}_{t}=(h^{L}_{t,0})_{*}
.

Proof.

By Proposition
2.18
and the reasoning as in (
5.18
), we obtain that for any
\epsilon>0
, there is
k_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
, so that for any

H_{k,t}\cdot\exp(-\epsilon k)\leq N_{k,t}\leq H_{k,t}\cdot\exp(\epsilon k).

(5.24)

The result now follows from the above estimate, Lemma
2.19
and (
