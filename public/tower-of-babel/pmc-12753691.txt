Coupled osteogenesis and angiogenesis on 3D-printed highly porous PCL/magnesium/laponite nanocomposite scaffold
Authors: Mahtab Namnabat, Elnaz Tamjid, Parvin Najafi, Elaheh Bazazzadeh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
3D-printed scaffolds are crucial in bone tissue engineering, providing a bioactive porous network for osteogenesis. However, their limited angiogenesis and mechanical strength poses challenges for effective bone repair. In this study, a 3D-printed scaffold based on polycaprolactone (PCL) containing magnesium particles (Mg) and laponite nanoparticles (LAP) loaded with pre-osteoblastic cells (MC3T3-E1) was developed to simultaneously improve mechanical properties, and exhibit coupled osteoblastic differentiation and vascularization. Adding Mg and LAP particles to PCL increased cell viability by 21% and 10% for scaffolds containing Mg (PM) and Mg-LAP (PML), respectively. The hierarchical 3D structure was investigated by scanning electron microscopy, while the surface properties and the structural analysis were studied by FTIR, DLS, XRD, and wettability tests. The PM and PML showed 20% and 60% enhancement in Young’s modulus as compared to the P scaffold, i.e., remarkably in the range of natural bone. Noticeably, adding Mg and LAP increased the alkaline phosphate enzyme activity of pre-osteoblastic cells by 28-fold and 20-fold in comparison with the P scaffolds and non-treated control samples. Moreover, PML printed scaffold showed significantly higher vascularization compared to P scaffolds in chorioallantoic membrane (CAM) assay. The 3D-printed scaffolds developed in this study demonstrated coupled osteogenesis and angiogenesis, paving the way for innovative biomaterials in bone tissue regeneration.

Introduction

Recent advancements in tissue engineering hold the promise to revolutionize regenerative medicine by developing various substitutes for restoring, maintaining or improving diverse hard and soft tissues1,2. Given that bone is the second most commonly transplanted tissue worldwide, this study focuses on advancing bone tissue engineering strategies, where scaffolds play a pivotal role by providing a bioactive porous substrate network for cellular activity via improved cellular connectivity, proliferation, differentiation, and facilitating the transmission of growth factors3 and molecular signals4,5.

Despite various traditional techniques for scaffold fabrication, including electrospinning, solvent casting, gas foaming, and solvent leaching, the 3D-printing technique is significantly advantageous for the development of hierarchical structures that enhance cellular proliferation and formation of new blood vessels6–11. While a wide range of biomaterials can be used through 3D-printing methods, the micro extrusion-based 3D printing technique has attracted much attention as it is beneficial in cell laden structures for personalized scaffolds with a tailored pore size, possessing favorable mechanical characteristics which are crucial in bone tissue engineering12–15. Bone tissue engineering research involves the utilization of a range of natural and synthetic polymers, for example, chitosan, linear aliphatic polyesters, aromatic polymers (polyhydroxyalkanoates), as well as, bioceramics and metals. Polycaprolactone (PCL) is certainly an interesting synthetic polymer with its notable biocompatibility, acceptable mechanical properties, low melting point, appropriate biodegradability, as well as reasonable versatility in utilization across various printing methods, which make it a promising candidate for bone tissue engineering scaffolds16,17. However, there are some shortcomings, such as its hydrophobic nature, low degradation rate, and brittle structure. To mitigate these issues, incorporating other biomaterials to complement PCL’s properties is a smart strategy18–20.

Magnesium (Mg) is an intracellular cation that contributes to bone regeneration through stabilization, growth, mineralization, and participating in many critical biochemical reactions as a cofactor for enzymes required for osteoblast proliferation21–23. Moreover, in vivo studies have shown that Mg promotes the osteogenic differentiation of osteoblasts, as well as the angiogenesis of human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVEC)24. Despite the great potential of Mg in bone tissue engineering, the main issue is its high biodegradation which is a drawback in in vivo applications25. In recent years, 3D-printed polymer matrix composite (PMC) scaffolds containing Mg have been recognized as a potential candidate due to their acceptable physicochemical and tunable mechanical properties. On the other hand, the mechanical properties of Mg (i.e., elastic modulus and tensile and compressive strengths) are extremely similar to that of cortical bone26–28. Thus, Mg is a promising candidate for bone tissue engineering29,30 which effectively contributes to simultaneous bone tissue regeneration and angiogenesis31.

Currently, there is a significant interest in the use of clay-based nanoparticles, specifically Laponite® (LAP), for biomedical and pharmaceutical applications. These nanomaterials are characterized by their two-dimensional nature and are composed of sheet structures exhibiting layered silicate properties32–35. LAP, with 1–2 nm thickness and 25–50 nm diameter, demonstrates a noteworthy characteristic in which a positive charge is distributed along the peripheries of its surface, while all of its facets possess a permanent negative surface charge36–40. Therefore, LAP can electrostatically interact with various chemical materials ranging from small molecules or ions to natural or synthetic polymers and different inorganic nanoparticles37,41–44. Furthermore, they are readily functionalized and can be easily degraded in the physiological environment, producing non-toxic and potentially bioactive products, such as Mg2⁺, Na⁺, and Si(OH)₄, which have been shown to enhance cell attachment, proliferation, and differentiation, supporting osteogenic and vascular tissue development45,46. Notably, recent studies have identified LAP as a promising material that can improves scaffold mechanical properties while promoting stem cell activity and tissue vascularization47.

In this study, a 3D printed scaffold composed of PCL integrated with Mg particles and LAP nanoparticles was developed through a micro-extrusion technique. This scaffold was then seeded with pre-osteoblastic cells (MC3T3-E1) to create a bioactive substitute that supports the coupled osteogenesis and angiogenesis while maintaining suitable mechanical strength for bone tissue engineering applications. The influence of Mg and LAP on the scaffold’s cell viability, attachment, differentiation, and angiogenic potential was analyzed and discussed.

Materials and methods

Materials

Polycaprolactone (PCL, Mw = 80,000, Sigma Aldrich, USA), Magnesium (Mg, micro-particles size about 60–300 µm, Merck, Germany), Dimethyl carbonate (DMC: Alfa Aesar, UK), Laponite RD (LAP Nanoparticles, 25 nm diameter, 1 nm thickness, BYK Additives, Germany), Phosphate buffered saline (PBS) tablets, Triton-X100 (Merck, Germany) fetal bovine serum (FBS), Dulbecco’s modified eagle’s medium (DMEM), trypsin/EDTA solution (0.25%), Penicillin–streptomycin 100 units/mL (Bioidea, Iran).

Scaffold preparation

A schematic image of the method used to prepare 3D scaffolds is presented in Fig. 1. To prepare highly porous scaffolds with controlled hierarchical structure, 3D printing technology was employed. At first, a computer-aided design (CAD) model of the scaffold was created using SolidWorks (version 2016, Dassault Systems, France)46. The model was circular with a diameter of 1 cm, a thickness of 0.6 mm, and a porosity of 90%. Then it was converted into an STL (Standard Tessellation Language) file and codes readable by a 3D printing machine (BiofabX2, Iran) by Simplify software. Three distinct groups of composite scaffolds were investigated, i.e., pure PCL, PCL/Mg, and PCL/Mg/LAP composite scaffolds (abbreviated as P, PM, and PML composite scaffolds). A PCL solution was initially formulated using a concentration of 10% w/v in DMC to prepare the printing ink. This was achieved through the addition of PCL to DMC solvent in a polymer to DMC ratio of 10:90, followed by the placement of the mixture on a magnetic stirrer (IKA, Germany) and stirring with the temperature of 50 °C for 40 min at a speed of 400 rpm until the PCL was dissolved completely in DMC48. Subsequently, 3% w/v of Mg particles, were added into the PCL solution. The concentration of Mg (3% w/v) was selected based on previous optimization studies, which demonstrated that this ratio provides favorable mechanical properties and biological performance in PCL-based scaffolds49. The PM composites were carried out for 2 h at a stirring speed of 400 rpm to ensure that the Mg particles were thoroughly distributed in the PCL matrix. Furthermore, the second composite was also meticulously formulated by adding 1% wt of LAP to the PM composite mixture. The LAP concentration (1% wt) was chosen following literature precedent, as it effectively enhances scaffold bioactivity and mechanical integrity without compromising printability42. To ensure the optimal distribution of LAP nanoparticles within the PM composite matrix, the resulting blend was subjected to continuous stirring at a velocity of 400 rpm for 4 h after adding LAP into the mixture. The prepared composites were placed in penicillin bottles and stored in a freezer at − 80 °C. To eliminate the solvent, followed by sublimation in a freeze dryer (Alpha 2–4 LD plus, Germany) under a pressure of 0.024 mbar and a temperature of − 80 °C for 24 h48. The composites were then printed by a micro-extrusion device (3D PL, BiofabX2, Omid Afarinan Mohandesi Ayandeh, Iran), at the temperature of 130 °C, and the printer nozzle speed of 5 m⋅s−1.Fig. 1Schematic diagram of the preparation process of 3D-printed scaffolds.

Particles characterization

The surface characteristics and morphology of the particles were investigated using a field emission scanning electron microscope (FESEM, TESCAN, MIRA3, Germany). Zeta potential measurement was conducted with zeta sizer (Malvern, Worcestershire, UK) to assess the homogeneity of Mg and LAP particle dispersion and determine their surface charge, respectively. A homogeneous suspension of particles in a water dispersant was prepared for this purpose50. To determine the uniformity of the particle size distribution, DLS analysis, a physical technique, was employed. Furthermore, the zeta potential assay was employed to measure the surface charge of the particles.

Scaffolds characterization

Field Emission Scanning Electron Microscope (FESEM, TESCAN, MIRA3, Germany) was applied to conduct topological and microstructural observations. Sputter coating of a thin layer of gold and a low acceleration voltage (5 kV) were utilized for imaging purposes. Elemental analysis was performed using Electron diffraction X-ray (EDX, TESCAN, Germany) spectroscopy. Additionally, for phase analysis comprehensive X-ray diffraction method (TESCAN, MIRA3, Germany) in a range of 2Θ = 20 to 70º, was employed using Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å) with a step size of 0.02º. For phase Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR, Spectrum1, PerkinElmer, UK) was used in the range of 400–4000 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${cm}^{-1}$$\end{document} to study the functional groups bonds as a “fingerprint” of the samples. The crystallinity percentage of the scaffolds was evaluated using X-ray diffraction (XRD, INTEL, EQUINOX 3000, USA). The evaluation of scaffold wettability was performed by a contact angle measurement device equipped with a camera (30ag20 Dino-Lite, Jikan, Iran), using 4 μl of deionized water dispensed onto the scaffolds via a syringe. Subsequently, a drop image was taken after a 30-s interval.

Mechanical properties

The scaffolds’ mechanical properties were assessed through compressive strength according to the D638-ASTM standard using a universal material testing machine (STM-1 SANTAM, Iran), using a load cell of 100 N, at a 1 mm/min speed and maximum strain of 40%51.

In vitro cell response

Cell viability

The cell viability of mouse fibroblast cells (L929, Iranian Biological Resources Center, Iran) using extracts derived from scaffolds according to ISO 10993–5 was evaluated. The cells were cultured in T25 cell culture flasks containing DMEM (Dulbecco′s Modified Eagle Medium, Bioidea, Iran) with a 10% FBS (Fetal Bovine Serum, Bioidea, Iran) concentration. The cell culture flasks were incubated at 37 °C under a 5% CO2 atmosphere. Then, L929 cells were transferred to a 96-well cell culture plate at a density of 8 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document} 103 cells per well. After 24 h, the culture medium was replaced with complete medium containing 40 µl of the extract from each sample. Following a 24- and 48-h cell culture, the supernatant was removed and 10 µl of tetrazolium bromide (Sigma-Alderich, USA) and 90 µl of culture medium were added to each well. The solution was incubated for 4 h, after which the culture medium was removed. Next, 100 µl of DMSO was added to each well, and the absorbance of the solutions of each well was measured using an ELISA microplate reader (Bio Tek ELX800, USA) at a wavelength of 570 nm52.

Cell adhesion

The scaffolds were sterilized in 70% alcohol for 24 h, followed by UV illumination for 25 min. A 100 µl aliquot of the medium containing \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${15\times 10}^{3}$$\end{document} of MC3T3-E1 mouse pre-osteoblast cells (Iranian Biological Resources Center (IBRC), Iran) were placed on the scaffolds, incubated for 4 h to secure the cell attachment to the surface. Then, the DMEM culture medium including 10% FBS and 1% antibiotics was added, and incubated overnight. To remove the non-adherent cells, the scaffolds were washed twice with PBS, and then 2.5% glutaraldehyde was added and washed with PBS after 1 h. Next, Triton x-100 was added for 4 min and then washed with PBS. Finally, SEM images were prepared to examine the attached cells on the scaffold53.

Alkaline phosphatase enzyme activity (ALP) assay

To evaluate osteogenic differentiation, alkaline phosphatase activity as an early osteogenesis marker in mineralization was determined on MC3T3-E1 cells cultured on the P, PM, and PML scaffolds on days 3, 7, and 14 post-cell culture. The seeded cells were lysed using 1% Triton X-100 with three freeze–thaw cycles and using ultrasound sonication bath (Soltec, Italy) during thawing54,55. The ALP assay kit (Pars Azmun, Iran) was utilized to calculate the level of ALP activity, with 20 μl of both cultured MC3T3-E1 supernatants and scaffold extracts (cell lysates) added to 1 mL of buffer reagent R1 and R2, according to the ALP kit protocol. Subsequently, the absorbance of the samples was measured at a wavelength of 405 nm using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer (Bio Tek Instrument, US).

Chick embryo chorioallantoic membrane (CAM) assay

To evaluate the angiogenic properties of P, PM, and PML scaffolds, the chick embryo chorioallantoic membrane (CAM) assay was implemented. Chicken fertilized eggs were incubated at 37.5 °C and 60% humidity for 48 h, while rocking every hour through 90°. To manipulate chick embryos, a surrogate eggshell system was used. Consequently, double-yolk eggshells were created as recipients of the cultured embryos. A laminar airflow hood was used to perform all operations after wiping eggs with 70% ethanol to prevent bacterial and fungal contamination. Initially, a tiny electric drill was used to remove the blunt end of recipient eggs that were 35–40 g heavier than the initial donor egg, creating a 42–45-mm window, and then the yolk and albumen were removed. The incubated eggs were carefully transferred into the recipient eggs using a 30–32-mm window. An insulin syringe was used to inject fast green dye under the embryos, and the embryos were sealed with plastic cling wrap and incubated in a forced-air incubator for 9 days at 37 °C and 60% humidity. Regular removing of dead or diseased embryos minimized future contamination. At embryonic day 9, after discarding the plastic cling wrap, sterile filter paper-shaped O-ring discs were placed on embryos and treated with experimental groups inside the rings. The embryos were transferred under the Olympus SZX16Wide Zoom Versatile Stereo Microscope (Yokohama, Japan), and images were taken as hour 0. The embryos were covered in plastic cling wrap and cultured for 72 h embryonic day 12 before being photographed for the feather mentioned above analysis. For five random photos in each treatment, the number of branches was computed and averaged56,57. All measurements and computations were done using WIMASIS (Wine Image Analysis System) image analysis (Version 3.1, WIMASIS, Germany).

Results and discussion

Field emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM) of Mg and LAP

Field emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM) images prepared from Mg and LAP particles are shown in Fig. 2. As observed in the images, the LAP particles predominantly exhibit nanoscale dimensions, while the Mg particles are mostly within the micrometer range. Specifically, most of the LAP particles appear to be smaller than 50 nm, while the Mg particles are primarily in the microscale range (approximately 60–300 µm), with the majority around 100 µm. This particle size distribution is consistent with the characteristics of the Mg powder used in this study. Although minor fragmentation may occur during scaffold preparation, our FESEM and DLS analyses confirm that most Mg particles remain microsized, in agreement with previous reports on Mg-based composites58,59. Such a polydisperse size distribution may positively influence the mechanical and biological performance of the scaffolds. However, it is important to note that the observed size distribution of LAP particles may not accurately represent their true nanoparticle nature. LAP, consisting of disk-shaped nanoparticles with diameters around 25 nm and thickness of about 1 nm, tends to form larger aggregates due to its high surface charge density. This aggregation, known as the “house of cards” structure, occurs when negatively charged faces of LAP disks interact with positively charged edges, resulting in the formation of larger structures observable in FESEM images. This phenomenon does not negate the inherent nanoscale properties of LAP, but rather demonstrates its unique self-assembly behavior60.Fig. 2FESEM images of LAP (a) and Mg (b) particles.

Zeta potential measurement

The determination of the surface charge of the particles was executed utilizing zeta potential measurements, to explore the protein-nanoparticles interaction. The data obtained demonstrates that LAP nanoparticles exhibit a zeta surface charge of − 9.03 ± 0.38 mV, while Mg particles present a zeta surface charge of − 10.6 ± 0.5 mV. This negative zeta potential of the Mg particles can be attributed to the formation of a Mg hydroxide (Mg(OH)2) layer on their surface, which occurs in the presence of moisture or CO2. The negatively charged hydroxide layer enhances the particles’ hydrophilicity and contributes to the observed zeta potential. This phenomenon, while seemingly contradictory to the positive surface charge expected for pure Mg, reflects the influence of the environmental conditions on the surface chemistry of the particles61. Negative zeta potential is indicative of the nanoparticles’ stability and resistance to electrostatic repulsion forces, with potential implications for the enhancement of bone healing62–64. Furthermore, according to the outcomes of DLS analysis, the measured hydrodynamic diameter (DH) of LAP nanoparticles was 114 ± 23 nm. It is important to note that the hydrodynamic diameter includes not only the physical core of the particle but also the surrounding electrical double layer, which forms due to the particle’s surface charge. Thus, the moderate negative zeta potential of LAP nanoparticles attributes to the formation of electrical double layer, thereby increasing the measured hydrodynamic size beyond the size observed at FESEM.

Water contact angle

As illustrated in Fig. 4a, the addition of Mg particles to the polymer matrix resulted in a notable increase in the surface wettability of the scaffold, which consequently led to a decrease in the contact angle from 106.5 ± 0.71° in P to 96.25 ± 0.92° in the PM scaffold. Additionally, integrating LAP nanoparticles into the scaffold’s structure further enhanced the wettability and decreased the contact angle of the water drop on the scaffold surface. The contact angle in the PML scaffold was determined to be approximately 81.55 ± 14.7°41. Similarly, Perumal et al.67, investigated the impact of Mg on the wettability of PCL nanofibers and observed that the incorporation of Mg into the hydrophobic polymer enhanced its hydrophilicity by forming hydroxyl groups on the surface, which facilitated hydrogen bonding with water molecules. This improved wettability behavior may contribute to the adhesion, proliferation, and bioactivity of cells on the scaffold. In a separate study, LAP was utilized to modify the physical and chemical properties of biodegradable kafirin nanocomposite films, and it was discovered that the addition of LAP to the composites significantly decreased the contact angle. This phenomenon was explained by the presence of hydroxyl (–OH) groups in the silicate clay chemical structure of LAP, which possesses hydrophilic properties68,69. X-ray diffraction (XRD)

The X-ray diffraction pattern of PCL, Mg, and LAP is shown in Fig. 4c. The XRD analysis of PCL demonstrates its semi-crystalline nature evidenced by two sharp crystal peaks, at angles of 21.5° and 23.7°, which correspond to the (110) and (200) planes, respectively75,76. On the other hand, Mg reveals weak diffraction peaks at around 27°, 34.5°, 36.5°, and 38 that can be attributed to (110), (002), (101), and (200) planes. These results are in agreement with a previous study77 indicating that the Mg particles were crystalline with a dense hexahedral structure (HCP). Sharp diffraction peaks of LAP at specific angles, including 34.8°, 35.5°, and 32°, correspond to (200), (311), and (005) planes78.

Additionally, a minor peak was detected at the angle of 30°, related to the (220) plane. These findings confirm the presence of a crystalline structure of LAP in the composite structure, which are inconsistent with the previous research conducted by Mahdavinia et al. confirming the crystal structure of LAP78. It is worthy to note that LAP as a synthetic material, is a 2:1 swelling phyllosilicate mineral with an octahedral MgO sheet sandwiched between two tetrahedral silica sheets79. Figure 4d demonstrates the XRD analysis of the PM and PML scaffolds, revealing diffraction peaks related to PCL, Mg, and LAP crystal planes within the range of 20° to 40°. A noticeable shift and sharpening of specific peaks, were observed, potentially due to chemical interactions between LAP and the PCL matrix. This observation is supported by the presence of sharper and more intense diffraction peaks in the PCL and LAP samples, which are indicative of a higher degree of crystallinity. The increase in crystallinity in the composite scaffold containing LAP nanoparticles may be attributed to the nucleating effect of LAP during fabrication. These nanoparticles promote the ordered alignment of polymer chains during the solidification process, thereby enhancing the overall crystallinity. A similar phenomenon was reported by Tabesh et al., where the presence of nanoparticles stimulated crystallization by acting as nucleating agents74,80.

Mechanical properties

Figure 5a displays the stress–strain curves for compression of P, PM, and PML scaffolds. The P scaffold has a compressive modulus of about 1.3 ± 0.03 MPa (Fig. 5b) which is lower than the Mg-containing scaffolds. The PM and the PML scaffolds have a modulus of 1.5 ± 0.06 MPa and 2.1 ± 0.13 MPa, respectively. An increase in Mg leads to an increase in the compressive modulus of the scaffold, as Mg acts as a reinforcing agent within the PCL matrix, enhancing the mechanical properties of the structure. This reinforcement helps distribute applied loads more effectively, resulting in higher compressive strength and elastic modulus. The mechanism discussed in this article suggests that the addition of Mg facilitates the formation of more ordered crystalline structures, as indicated by the X-ray diffraction results. These crystalline regions contribute significantly to the improvement of both compressive strength and elastic modulus. The size range of the magnesium particles used in this study was approximately 60–300 µm, with most particles around 100 µm. These particles play a crucial role in preventing the movement of polymeric fibers in the matrix. By interacting with the polymer, Mg particles effectively trap and entangle the polymeric fibers, restricting their movement. This mechanism is similar to the principles of the mixture law, as the addition of the reinforcing particles (Mg) alters the properties of the composite material (polymer matrix and particles), leading to improved mechanical characteristics81,82. As can be seen in the Fig. 5b, the PM scaffold exhibits superior strength when compared to the pure P scaffold. Dong et al. have reported that the presence of Mg in PCL scaffolds leads to an increase in compressive strength and modulus of elasticity due to Mg ions acting as reinforcing agents within the PCL matrix, providing additional strength and stiffness to the scaffold structure. Moreover, based on X-ray diffraction results, Mg addition leads to the formation of more ordered crystalline structures. These crystalline regions contribute to the overall mechanical properties of the scaffold, improving both compressive strength and elastic modulus. In the present study, the Mg particles were mostly around 100 µm in size, which facilitates reinforcement of the scaffold matrix by promoting more organized crystal growth49. Furthermore, the column diagram indicates that the PML scaffold has 0.5 MPa more Young’s modulus in comparison with PM scaffolds. It is revealed that adding LAP leads to a significant increase in Young’s modulus, enhancing the compressive strength by 20% as compared to other scaffolds. Although the measured Young’s modulus values of the scaffolds (ranging from 1.3 to 2.1 MPa) are substantially lower than that of native cancellous bone (typically between 0.1 and 2.0 GPa, depending on age, anatomical site, and health condition), the PML scaffold, in particular, approaches the lower bound of the physiological range. This improvement suggests that the scaffold possesses enhanced mechanical characteristics that are more bio-relevant, offering sufficient support for early-stage bone regeneration while potentially minimizing the risk of stress shielding83. Several research studies have confirmed that the incorporation of LAP in polymers leads to an increase in compressive strength and Young’s modulus. This enhancement in mechanical properties can be attributed to increased crosslink density due to electrostatic and hydrogen bond interactions as non-covalent LAP crosslinks between the polymer and LAP84–86. This finding has been previously reported in other studies that adding LAP nanoparticles to the polymer substantially improves the mechanical strength of nanocomposite layers87–89. In a separate investigation, Wang et al.90, incorporated LAP into PLGA scaffolds for urethra tissue engineering. In vitro cell response

MTT assay

The cell viability of the 3D scaffolds is presented in Fig. 6a. The P scaffold exhibited less cell proliferation, with a viability of 69% ± 5.96% and a statical significance of p *** < 0.001. This reduction was resulted from the degradation of PCL in the culture medium, which subsequently led to the acidification of the culture medium52,91,92. However, no significant difference was observed in the proliferation of cells on the control sample in comparison to the PM scaffold within the first 24 h. As compared to the P samples, the incorporation of Mg in PM scaffold resulted in improved cell growth, with a viability of 90.30% ± 3.75%. This phenomenon can be attributed to the promotive effect of Mg via the release of Mg2⁺ ions, which actively participate in cellular signaling pathways and promote osteogenic differentiation and cell proliferation. Mg ions modulate integrin-mediated adhesion, enhance the expression of bone-related genes (such as ALP and OCN), and activate MAPK and PI3K/Akt signaling pathways, which are crucial for cell survival and proliferation93,94. Furthermore, the release of Mg2⁺ ions, buffers the acidic degradation products of PCL due to their intrinsic alkalinity, maintaining the pH in a physiological range conducive to cell viability92. Cell attachment

Figure 6b exhibits the adhesion of the proliferated cells on P, PM, and PML scaffolds. The cells adhered to the scaffold pores using a light microscope after for 3, 7, and 14 days are depicted in Fig. 6b. Although the PML sample shows a slight cell attachment at day 3, a minimal level of adhesion is observed with only a limited number of cells attached to the rest of the scaffolds. However, on days 7 and 14, pure P scaffolds showed low cell attachment as compared to the other scaffolds. In contrast, the PM and PML scaffolds demonstrated excellent cell adhesion via pore filling and tissue formation. According to Dong’s research49, dead cells comprised about 30% the P scaffolds, while the ratio of living cells increased in composite scaffolds. The results are attributed to the lack of bioactive sites in the P scaffold. However, only a small proportion of cells adhere to the P scaffold without bioactive sites, which have unfavorable modes. Previous studies have shown that adding 3 wt% Mg to PCL increases cell adhesion, growth, and spread on the scaffolds49. The cell adhesion observation revealed that the cells are attached to the inner parts of the PML scaffold pores significantly faster than PM scaffold on the third day (Fig. 6b). This enhancement can be attributed to the synergistic effect of Mg and LAP incorporation into the PCL matrix, which improves surface hydrophilicity, roughness, and the availability of bioactive sites. The released ions, including Mg2⁺ and Si(OH)₄, contribute to enhanced cell adhesion by promoting integrin-mediated interactions, facilitating cytoskeletal reorganization, and activating downstream signaling pathways such as Focal adhesion kinase (FAK) and Wnt/β-catenin—both of which are involved in regulating cell attachment, proliferation, and survival66,95. Moreover, the adhesion mechanism appears to be influenced by the scaffold’s surface electrostatic charge, primarily resulting from the release of Mg2⁺ ions. These ions support the formation of electrostatically driven protein adsorption sites on the cell membrane, thus enabling more efficient cell-scaffold binding96. The effectiveness of LAP in promoting cell attachment and proliferation was further confirmed by experimental findings. It is hypothesized that LAP facilitates the generation of negatively charged silanol groups (Si–OH) on the scaffold surface, enhancing protein adsorption and cell-scaffold interactions15. Additionally, the gradual dissolution of Mg2⁺ ions into the culture medium is believed to promote the survival and activity of pre-osteoblast cells through the activation of signaling pathways such as Wnt/β-catenin, which plays a key role in regulating proliferation, differentiation, and cellular homeostasis18,46,97.

Alkaline phosphate (ALP) activity

The evaluation of osteogenic differentiation on PCL-based scaffolds was conducted through ALP activity. Figure 6c demonstrated that PM and PML scaffolds exhibited more significant ALP activity than the P scaffold. After 3 days, the samples did not show any significant changes compared to the control condition. The P scaffold showed very low ALP activity, attributable to the lack of bioactive sites, indicated by the cell attachment results. In other words, the minimal and negligible attachment of the cells to the P scaffold leads to reduce the proliferation and thus the differentiation49. On day 7, the control displayed the highest enzymatic activity level, which significantly differed from the PM and PML scaffolds. It can be deduced that the initial rate of cell growth in control well is higher; however, upon reaching high density, proliferation stops, and cell differentiation commences, leading to continued increase in ALP enzyme activity98,99. Generally, bone scaffolds have significantly impacted cellular osteogenic differentiation compared to control samples, with PM scaffolds displaying the highest differentiation rate within 14 days, amounting to about 80%. As a result, these scaffolds were able to promote in vitro bone formation without the need for osteogenic supplements components. In a study by W. Liu et al., it was found that Mg can effectively promote the osteogenic differentiation of MC3T3-E1 cells via the upregulation of platelet-derived growth factor-BB (PDGF-BB) expression and secretion. PDGF-BB subsequently enhances alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity and mineralization, thereby facilitating osteogenic differentiation100. According to a study by CC Hung et al., the activation of Wnt signaling by Mg2+ causes human bone marrow stromal cells (hBMSCs) to differentiate into osteoblasts101. Moreover, this result indicates that PML nanoparticles also boosted the activity of alkaline phosphatase enzyme, significantly effectuating differentiation. This is attributed to the impact of Mg2+ and Si4+ ions released from LAP64,102,103. Another research study revealed that the cells seeded on PCL/Carboxyl methyl cellulose (CMC)-LAP scaffolds exhibited elevated levels of ALP activity compared to the cells grown on PCL/CMC scaffolds. This increase in ALP activity can be attributed to the promotion of osteogenic differentiation in stem cells residing on polymeric scaffolds that incorporate LAP nanoparticles66. Despite these overall stimulatory effects, the relatively lower ALP activity observed in PML compared to PM can be attributed to the complex interplay of ions and divergent cellular signaling pathways. The Si(OH)₄ and Na⁺ ions released from LAP, along with Mg2⁺, can activate distinct and sometimes competing osteogenic signaling cascades such as MAPK/ERK, Wnt/β-catenin, TGF-β/BMP, and NF-κB pathways. This multifaceted modulation may shift cells toward alternative differentiation fates beyond classical ALP-associated osteogenesis, partially explaining the reduced ALP levels despite preserved or enhanced osteogenic potential104–107. Furthermore, enhanced cell adhesion and proliferation in PML scaffolds promote earlier cellular maturation and exit from the proliferative phase. This accelerated differentiation timeline may lead to a transient decrease in ALP expression measured at the sampling time point108,109. According to another study Cytoskeletal reorganization and actin filament dynamics, influenced by cell density and scaffold surface properties, also regulate ALP transcription and enzymatic activity via factors like serum response factor (SRF)110,111. Finally, the nucleating effect of LAP increases matrix crystallinity, facilitating more ordered mineral deposition processes that do not necessarily require parallel increases in ALP activity. Thus, the slight decrease in ALP does not reflect diminished osteogenic capacity but rather nuanced modulation by complementary osteogenic mechanisms. As they provide a 3D environment, 3D nanocomposite scaffolds offer more cell attachment surface compared to 2D cell culture in control samples, resulting in increased cell proliferation, higher cell ALP activity, and enhanced bone formation. Consequently, the scaffolds developed and utilized in this study has revealed significant increase in ALP activity.

In previous studies refs112,113, it has been shown that the introduction of Mg into materials and the subsequent release of Mg2+ led to an improvement in the process of angiogenesis and a decrease in inflammation. Mg promotes the secretion of platelet-derived growth factor-BB (PDGF-BB) by MC3T3-E1, which enhances osteoblasts’ osteogenic differentiation ability and vein endothelial cells angiogenic ability100,114. Specifically, a deficiency in Mg has been found to hinder the migration and growth of microvascular endothelial cells, while also promoting the synthesis of markers associated with inflammation. As a consequence of the stimulatory effects of Mg, elevated levels of this element promoted the proliferation of microvascular cells and rendered them more responsive to migratory signals, ultimately inducing angiogenesis115. In this investigation, we utilized LAP in conjunction with Mg as a bioactive agent within the scaffold. The results demonstrated that the inclusion of LAP in the PM scaffold increased the branching points and vascularization. This outcome can be attributed to LAP’s ability to stimulate the growth of blood vessels, such as VEGF116,117. Similarly, Xu et al.118 demonstrated that by using LAP-embedded PCL with suitable potential as an ideal bone tissue engineering scaffold can accelerate vascularized bone regeneration and be envisioned for bone defect regeneration. As demonstrated in previous studies, robust vascular networks are required for various large tissue defects as they provide access to nutrients, factors, oxygen to the cells, and to expel the metabolites to avoid tissue necrosis upon its implantation and therefore allow efficient bone formation119. The addition of Mg and LAP to P scaffolds could result in modifications of surface charge, roughness, or functional groups, which may improve the biological performance of P scaffolds. This includes enhancing the attachment, proliferation, and differentiation of osteogenic cells, which can ultimately lead to the initiation of mineral deposition on the scaffold surface and support vascularization93,120.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we have successfully fabricated a bioactive PM and PML composite scaffold by the micro-extrusion 3D printing method for bone tissue engineering applications. The nanocomposite scaffolds developed in this study demonstrated facile manipulation and improved formability, which is critical to filling defect sites with irregular shapes. The obtained porous scaffold presented suitable and tunable mechanical, and biological properties. Adding the LAP to the scaffold structure would synergistically increase the crystallite phase and hydrophilicity of the nanocomposite. PML showed enhanced mechanical behavior and improved bone regeneration. Interestingly, the PML scaffold demonstrated its superior efficiency through enhanced biocompatibility, proliferation, adhesion, migration, and angiogenesis. According to the obtained results, PML scaffold with acceptable osteogenic and angiogenic activity is a promising candidate for potential hard tissue engineering applications.
