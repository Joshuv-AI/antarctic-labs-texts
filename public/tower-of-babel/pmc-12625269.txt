Pregnancy and postpartum-related admissions to the intensive care unit at a tertiary centre in Northwest China: a 14-year retrospective study
Authors: Weiwei Yuan, Yanni Zheng, Xu Zhang, Tingting Li, Haiwang Sha, Zhiyan Hui
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe purpose of this study was to analyse the incidence of maternal morbidity and mortality of pregnant and postpartum women admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU).MethodsIn this study, we retrospectively analyzed all pregnant and postpartum patients admitted to the ICU of the intensive care unit at our tertiary center in northwest China between January 1, 2009 and December 31, 2022.ResultsA total of 528 patients with pregnancy and postpartum related morbidities were admitted to the ICU (5.16 per 1000 deliveries). It accounted for 11.73% of all adult admissions to the unit. The median age was 28 years, the median gestation time was 37 weeks, and 89.98% did not receive irregular prenatal care. 182 (34.47%) were primiparous. Most of admitting in postpartum (99.24%) and 48.67% were premature. The main reasons for admission included postpartum hemorrhage (PPH) (180/528), hypertensive disorders pregnancy (176/528), and cardiac diseases (69/528). The median length of ICU stay was four days (2–6), and the median APACHE II score was 12.5 points. 185 patients (35.04%) required invasive mechanical ventilation, 25 (4.73%) had non-invasive ventilation, 85(16.1%) required vasoactive drugs, 14 patients (2.65%) underwent renal replacement therapy, and 69 patients (13.07%) underwent hysterectomy. When comparing the rates of maternal mortality between 2009 and 2015 and 2016–2022, a decreasing trend was revealed (2.98% vs. 1.04%; P = 0.151), and stillbirth (19.64% vs. 20.83%, P = 0.743) did not decrease significantly, whereas the rates of ICU admission did (19.26% vs. 6.63%; p trends < 0.01). We reported 12 maternal deaths (2.27%; 11.7 per 100,0000 deliveries), and 9 maternal deaths due to obstetric factors. There were 106 cases of fetal death.ConclusionsThere are three main reasons for obstetric ICU admissions: postpartum hemorrhage, hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, and cardiac diseases. The majority of obstetric patients admitted to the ICU do not require multi-organ supportive therapy. The main cause of maternal mortality was obstetric factors. Pregnant women having a second child and diabetes are more likely to have stillbirth.Keywords: Intensive care unit, Maternal morbidity, Maternal mortality, Fetal outcomes

Introduction

Severe maternal morbidity (SMM) and maternal mortality rate (MMR) have been proposed globally as indicators to evaluate the quality of maternal health services [1–3]. Although the incidence rate is low in developed countries, SMM can cause catastrophic consequences and has a significant impact on maternal mortality and fetal outcomes [4]. Many studies focused on maternal near-miss (MNM) to prevent them from developing into maternal deaths [5, 6]. The World Health Organization (WHO) diagnoses MNM based on three criteria: disease-, intervention-and organ dysfunction-based [7]. Admission to the ICU is an intervention criteria and is considered an objective indicator of SMM [6–8]. The latest systematic review shows that the overall ICU admission rate for pregnant women is 1.6% (equivalent to 16 cases per 1,000 deliveries). However, there are significant differences between high- and low-income countries, with only 4 cases per 1,000 deliveries in the former, and as high as 28 cases per 1,000 deliveries low- and middle-income countries [9]. These variations mainly stem from differences in ICU admission standards, resource utilization, and regional disparities.

China is a developing country with a large population and vast territory. The current reported the admission rate is 2.8–5.6 cases per 1000 deliveries [10, 11], and its MMR has decreased from 1500 to 17.8 per 100, 000 live births between 1949 and 2019 [12], reflecting significant achievements in reducing maternal deaths. Nevertheless, regional disparities are prominent, with rural areas having 2–5 times higher MMR than urban areas [12], attributable to factors such as economic development, accessibility of healthcare resources, and educational attainment. Previous studies primarily focused on national or provincial levels, with rare data at the prefectural city or county level [10, 13, 14]. Some research suggested that analyzing the levels and trends of MMR at the local level is more helpful in highlighting successful experiences and identifying areas for improvement [12, 15].

Yan’an City, located in a remote area of northwestern China, with less developed economics, comprises 2 districts and 11 counties with a population of approximately 2 million. So far, there is no report in this region. This study aims to explore the epidemiology, case mix, and outcomes of MNM in Yan’an City from 2009 to 2022. As the largest regional referral center, data reflect the disease burden of high-risk pregnant women in this region and can inform continuous quality improvement in maternal health care.

Materials and methods

Patients

This was a retrospective cohort study conducted in Yan’an University Affiliated Hospital between January 1, 2009 and December 31, 2022. The hospital is a tertiary center with 1900-bed facility, which has a 10-bed capacity ICU and lacks a high-dependency unit (HDU). We included all pregnant and postpartum women admitted to the ICU at ≥ 24 weeks of gestation through 42 days postpartum, excluding women < 18 years or those with psychiatric disorders. In 2015, China implemented the “universal two child policy”, and we compared the data between the periods of 2009–2015 and 2016–2022.

Definitions

According to the WHO [7], MNM was defned as a woman who nearly died but survived a complication that occurred during pregnancy, childbirth, or within 42 days of termination of pregnancy. MMR : the number of women with MNM per 1000 live births. SMM : the number of maternal deaths per 100,000 live births. Maternal Death: a woman died during pregnancy, childbirth or within 42 days of termination of pregnancy. Preterm birth was defined as spontaneous or iatrogenic delivery at or gestation < 37 weeks. Stillbirth was defined as the term or preterm delivery of an infant who died in utero and was born with an Apgar score of 0/0/0.

Data collection

The following data were collected from the patients’ medical records by the electronic medical record system of hospital: (1) demographic information: age, medical history, education level, number of pregnancies, parity, cesarean history, number of prenatal examinations, gestational weeks, admission method; (2) clinical presentation: mode of delivery (vaginal or cesarean), complications (gestational diabetes, preeclampsia, gestational hypertension, uterine rupture, placental factors, and preterm birth), the main diagnosis for admission to the ICU, acute physiology and chronic health evaluation II (APACHE II) score during the ICU stay, the stay of ICU and hospital, ICU interventions (use of mechanical ventilation and non-invasive ventilation, vasoactive drugs, continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT); (3) perinatal outcomes: maternal death, perinatal data, stillbirths, whether a hysterectomy was performed. According to the WHO’s International Classification of Diseases-10 (ICD-10) [16], maternal causes of death were classified as obstetric or nonobstetric. Obstetric death refers to a series of events leading to death caused by complications related to the pregnant state (pregnancy, delivery, and the postpartum period), interventions, omissions, incorrect treatment, or any of the above reasons, such as obstetric hemorrhage, amniotic fluid embolism. Nonobstetric death refers to death caused by preexisting underlying diseases or diseases that develop during pregnancy and are not directly related to obstetrics but are exacerbated by the physiological effects of pregnancy, such as cardiovascular or respiratory disease. Every cause of-maternal death was retrospectively reviewed and extracted independently by two authors (Yuan and Zheng). The ICU admission rate, SMM, MMR, and stillbirth rate were calculated. The project was approved by the institutional review board of the Affiliated Hospital of Yan’an University (S-S20240007). The requirement to obtain informed consent was waived because of the retrospective design.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables are expressed as numbers (percentages), and continuous variables are presented as means ± standard deviations (SD) or medians (interquartile range), depending on whether they followed a normal distribution. Student’s t-test and the Mann–Whitney U test were used to compare normally and nonnormally distributed continuous variables, respectively. The chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test was used for comparisons of categorical variables. A p-value less than or equal to 0.05 was considered statistically significant. A univariate logistic regression was performed to identify maternal risk factors associated with stillbirth. Variables with statistically significant differences in univariate analysis were included in the multivariate logistic regression model, which was manually constructed. Odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. All statistical analyses were conducted using EmpowerStats software (www.empowerstats.com, X&Y Solutions, Inc., Boston, MA), which is based on the R statistical environment. EmpowerStats was primarily used for descriptive statistics and logistic regression analyses, while R software version 3.6.1 (http://www.r-project.org) was employed for data preprocessing (e.g., handling of missing values and recoding of variables), advanced visualization, and sensitivity analyses.

Results

Frequency of ICU utilization

During the 14-year study period (2009–2022), 102,419 deliveries occurred, and 528 women were admitted to the ICU during pregnancy or postpartum. The average rate of pregnancy-related ICU admissions was 5.16 per 1,000 deliveries, and it increased from 4.04 to 8.53 per 1,000 deliveries, and then gradually declined to 2.56 (p < 0.01) in 2009–2022. The rate peaked at 11.53 per 1,000 deliveries in 2010, and then decreased (Fig. 1). The proportion of ICU admissions was 0.52% (528/102,419), accounting for 11.73% (528/4,502) of all adult admissions of ICU.

Demographics of Pregnancy-Related ICU admissions

The median age of the women admitted to the ICU was 28 years, 13.83% were older than 35 years, 87.31% had a middle school education or lower (Table 1). Approximately 71.01% (375/528) of patients were admitted from emergency departments, and 26.33% were transferred from other lower-level hospitals. Nulliparous women comprised 34.47% of the ICU admissions, and women with a second child comprised 43.56%, and those with irregular prenatal care accounted for 89.98%. Among them, 4.73% (25/528) of patients had no prenatal care, the rate of premature deliveries was 48.67% (Table 1). Most of the patients were admitted during the postpartum period (99.24%), 16.48% had a history of cesarean section, and 90.15% had undergone cesarean deliveries. There were 106 fetal deaths in the study. The median gestation time was 37 weeks and the median birth weight was 2560 g. The median length of stay in the ICU was 4 days, and the hospital length of stay was 8 days. The median APACHE II score was 12.5 points.

Compared to 2009–2015, the later period showed a significantly higher median age (27 vs. 30 year, P < 0.001) and a substantially lower proportion of patients with lower-education (95.83% vs. 72.40%, P < 0.001). Referrals from lower-level hospitals demonstrated a marked increase (15.48% vs. 45.31%, P < 0.001), contrasting with a decline in emergency admissions (83.63% vs. 48.96%, P < 0.001) (Table 1). Notably, the proportion of patients with a history of cesarean delivery rose significantly (12.50% vs. 23.44%, P = 0.001) in the later period, while the cesarean section rate during the later period showed a concurrent decline (92.56% vs. 85.94%, p = 0.014).

Diagnoses associated with ICU admission

Over the 14-year study period, the main diagnoses at ICU admission were postpartum hemorrhage (PPH) (180/528), hypertensive disorders pregnancy (176/528), cardiac diseases (69/528), infection (35/528), amniotic fluid embolism (27/528), and organ failure (17/528). In the latter period, the ICU admission rate for pregnancy-related postpartum hemorrhage did not increase significantly (31.55%−38.54%, P = 0.103), whereas that for acute kidney injury increased significantly (3.87%−13.54%, P < 0.001). The ICU admission rate of patients with infection increased to a nearly significant level (5.06%−9.38%, P = 0.058) and decreased for patients with amniotic fluid embolism(6.55%−2.60%, P = 0.048); other diagnoses did not change significantly (Table 2). PPH (180/528; 34.09%) was the main reason for ICU admission (Table 2). Uterine atony (59/180) and placental abruption (55/180) accounted for 32.77% and 30.55% of the PPH cases, respectively. Among them, 27 patients (15.0%) experienced uterine rupture.

Complications and invasive operations after ICU admission

Approximately 38.45% of patients with APACHE II scores were greater than 15, including 83 cases of postpartum hemorrhage, 58 cases of severe preeclampsia, 20 cases of heart disease, 16 cases of amniotic fluid embolism, 12 cases of infection, 11 cases of organ failure, and 3 cases of acute fatty liver of pregnancy. The most common type of organ damage was 39 cases of acute renal injury in 39 patients (27.3%), 14 of whom were treated with continuous renal replacement therapy. There were 39 cases of heart failure and 17 cases of respiratory failure. A total of 185 patients (35.04%) required invasive mechanical ventilation, 25 patients (4.73%) had non-invasive ventilation, 85 patients (16.1%) had vasoactive drugs, 14 patients (2.65%) underwent renal replacement therapy, and 69 patients (13.07%) underwent hysterectomy. The tracheal intubation rate decreased significantly over the study period (38.99% vs. 28.13%; P = 0.038); in most cases, intubation was not performed because of respiratory failure but rather for general anesthesia in the operating room.

Maternal mortality rate and fetal outcomes

The overall maternal mortality rate was 2.27%, with 2.3 per 100 obstetric patients admitted to the ICU and 11.7 per 100,000 hospital deliveries. The fetal death was 20.08 per 100 births. From 2009 to 2015 to 2016–2022, the maternal mortality rate in the ICU showed a downwards trend, but there was no significant difference (2.98%−1.04%, P = 0.151). During the study period, 12 maternal deaths occurred, including 6 cases from our hospital and the other 6 from external hospitals. There were 9 deaths due to direct obstetric causes and 3 deaths because of nonobstetric causes (including one case of cerebral hemorrhage, one acute liver failure and one case of peripartum cardiomyopathy), as shown in Table 3.

Discussion

This study is the first 14-year analysis of obstetric ICU admissions at a prefecture-level tertiary hospital in underdeveloped region of northwestern China. In our study, obstetric admissions accounted for 0.52% of all deliveries patients (5.16/1000 deliveries) and 11.73% of the total admissions in our ICU. This finding is consistent with a previous multicentre study in China (5.16 vs. 5.6/1000 deliveries) [10]. Compared to other studies, the rate of 0.7–4.2 per 1000 deliveries increased [17–20], while it decreased compared to 9.9 per 1000 deliveries in Austria [20]. Possible reasons for this discrepancy may include differences in ICU admission criteria, the management of obstetrics in tertiary hospitals, and the availability of ICUs or HDUs in other local hospitals [19, 21]. These differences are of great significance for clinical practice and health policies, as they reflect the differences in obstetric critical care management among different regions and hospitals, which may affect resource allocation, monitoring levels, and prevention and treatment strategies for obstetric complications, thereby helping to develop more targeted and effective clinical guidelines and policy measures.

There are 53 ICU beds available in our city, with an ICU availability rate of 2.6 per 100,000 people, compared to 30.5 per 100,000 population in the United States, which is insufficient [22]. The majority of women admitted to the ICU were postpartum (99.24%), which is in accordance with the previous reports [23–26]. The length of ICU stay was 5 days, which was comparable to that reported in the published literature [27]. Prenatal care constitutes a cornerstone of preventive healthcare, facilitating early detection of potential complications and playing a critical role in mitigating maternal mortality risk. The WHO recommends a minimum of eight antenatal care visits prior to delivery [28]. In our cohort, however, 89.98% of patients received irregular prenatal care, and 87.31% of patients had less education. Educational level and low income are the main factors influencing prenatal care [6]. Women with lower education levels often have limited awareness of prenatal care, insufficient risk recognition skills, and tend to delay seeking medical attention, leading to a higher proportion of early emergency admissions. These issues may also be associated with poverty, difficulty accessing resources, insufficient opportunities for prenatal care, gender inequality perceptions, and environmental factors. The weak capacity of primary healthcare services, resource shortages, lack of understanding of the severity of diseases, and delays in referrals result in low early referral rates to lower-level hospitals.

Compared to earlier periods, the later research cohort exhibited an older population demographic, higher educational levels, and a greater proportion of patients transferred from lower-level hospitals. These changes suggest that significant improvements in education may be a key driving factor behind the positive developments. Enhanced prenatal care quality, improved capacity of primary healthcare facilities, and a more effective referral system have contributed to earlier and more timely interventions for patients, indirectly reducing the severity of diseases and the rate of preterm births. The proportion of patients with a history of cesarean Sect. (12.50% vs. 23.44%) has increased significantly, while the overall rate of cesarean deliveries has markedly decreased. This indicates a positive shift in obstetric practice, reflecting progress in the promotion of tiered diagnosis and treatment, improvements in perinatal care, and efforts to reduce unnecessary cesarean Sect [29].

The most common reasons for ICU admission in this study were postpartum hemorrhage (PPH) in 34.09%, hypertensive disorders of pregnancy in 33.33%, and cardiac disease in 13.07%, which are comparable to the published literature [10, 30, 31]. The most common causes of postpartum hemorrhage were uterine atony, placental abruption, and uterine rupture, which are also the main reasons for hysterectomy, comparable to previous studies [32, 33]. It is noteworthy that PPH not only is the primary cause of ICU admissions (34.09%) in this study, but also the main cause of maternal mortality (6/12, 50%). This aligns with the global consensus that PPH is the leading cause of maternal death worldwide (accounting for 27.1%) and the primary cause of severe obstetric complications [34]. The increasing proportion of advanced-aged pregnant women, higher incidence of pregnancy-related complications, and the rising number of women with a history of cesarean section may be significant factors contributing to the heightened risk of obstetric hemorrhage in China [15]. Multiple studies [20, 35, 36] suggested that enhanced prenatal management of pregnant women with cardiac diseases could potentially reduce ICU admission rates.

In our study, the ICU maternal mortality rate in the ICU was 2.27% (11.7 per 100,000 deliveries), which is similar to that in published reports in Beijing (2.27% vs. 2.0%) [10], and comparable to that in foreign reports of 12 per 100,000 livebirths in developed regions in 2015 [15]. This indicates that China has made tremendous efforts in maternal and child health care. Notably, the causes of maternal death are different. In this study, 75% (9/12) of maternal deaths were due to obstetric factors, while 75% of deaths were due to sepsis-related causes in Beijing [10]. In our study, 50% of the deceased patients were from other hospitals. Among them, 7 patients (58.33%) had stillbirths, and 9 patients underwent uterine hysterectomy, all of whom were postpartum to admitted the ICU. The ICU intervention time was relatively short (average 16.8 h), which may be related to economic backwardness and delayed seeking and arrival of ICUs. The median APACHE II score in this study was 12.5, indicating that pregnant women were sent to the ICU in a more critical condition. This suggests that we should improve antenatal education, strengthen perinatal management, and collaborate with grassroots hospitals and regional cooperation to improve maternal outcomes.

The APACHE II score is the most widely used tool for assessing the severity of illness and predicting mortality in critically ill obstetric patients [37]. Physiological changes during pregnancy, such as higher respiratory and heart rates and lower hematocrit and creatinine levels, may lead to higher APACHE II scores, causing falsely elevated predicted mortality rates, with conflicting results [38]. Thus the APACHE II score may not entirely be applicable to women in obstetric ICU settings. Some conditions, could not be accurately judged only from vital signs and were prone to false-negative results, such as pregnancies with congenital heart disease. Developing effective assessment tools for this population has challenges. Future efforts could integrate pregnancy-specific physiological adaptations, such as establishing dynamic gestational age-adjusted thresholds, or use obstetric variables.

There were 35 cases of infection in our study, accounting for 6.63%, and 10 cases of sepsis, accounting for 1.89%. There were no deaths among these patients. A possible reason is that obstetric sepsis often originates from a single source of infection (such as genital tract or intrauterine infection), and patients tend to be relatively young with fewer underlying health conditions, leading to better outcomes with early intervention. Wanderer et al.‘s study on the entire population of obstetric ICU admissions revealed that sepsis is common in obstetric ICU patients, but they did not find an association between sepsis and maternal mortality in the ICU [20]. In our study, pregnant women received less prenatal care and had less education. Maternal health awareness was weak, and some patients were admitted with stillbirths [36]. Early detection and intervention of maternal complications, interdisciplinary care, and efficient referral systems are crucial for preventing maternal deaths. Since the implementation of the two-child policy in 2015, China has established referral centres for critically ill pregnant women and newborns at the provincial, regional, and county levels [12]. According to the five-colour management program, risk factors for pregnant women were assessed. However, there are still some challenges, such as the uneven development of medical standards in different regions, insufficient awareness of bleeding risks, and inadequate skills to address bleeding risks, leading to delayed referrals and insufficient multidisciplinary collaboration. Therefore, it is necessary to improve the knowledge and skills of doctors in primary hospitals, strengthen the supervision of the quality of obstetric services, and establish standardized multidisciplinary teams to improve the quality of maternal health services [12].

There was a higher percentage of fetal deaths in our study, accounting for 19.45%(106/545). We found that women having a second child and diabetes mellitus were risk factors for fetal death, consistent with previous research findings [39]. A hyperglycemic environment damages endothelial function, leading to placental insufficiency and affecting the fetus’s oxygen and nutrient supply, thereby significantly increasing the risk of stillbirth [40]. In resource-limited or poorly managed areas, GDM screening, diagnosis, and standardized management are often inadequate. Earlier data indicate that mothers with lower education levels and poor prenatal care may experience insufficient nutrient supplementation, resulting in restricted fetal development. Inadequate prenatal care also contributes to a lack of early risk identification, weak primary service capacity, and delayed referral of high-risk patients, which are also reasons for intrapartum stillbirth. The key to preventing stillbirth lies in breaking this vicious cycle: strengthening local health education, improving the quality of prenatal care, enhancing risk screening, training primary healthcare personnel, and establishing efficient referral systems are particularly important. This requires multi-dimensional collaboration across medical, social, and policy sectors to improve maternal and fetal outcomes.

Limitations

This study has certain limitations: First, it is a retrospective design, with diagnoses obtained from the hospital electronic medical records system. This approach has inherent limitations, as the quality of record-keeping depends on the subjective judgment of clinicians, and confounding factors were not measured. Second, it is a single-center study, with participants from a regional tertiary referral center, which may limit the applicability of the results to other regions. Third, as an exploratory analysis, the raw variables were not adjusted for confounders. Finally, fetal death was based on prenatal ultrasound indicating intrauterine fetal demise or Apgar scores of 0/0/0 at birth. There was no specific statistical analysis for fetal deaths occurring before delivery or at birth, and no long-term postpartum follow-up was conducted.

Conclusions

There are three main reasons for obstetric ICU admissions: postpartum hemorrhage, hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, and cardiac diseases. The majority of obstetric patients admitted to the ICU do not require multi-organ supportive therapy. The main cause of maternal mortality is obstetric factors. Improving regional cooperation and referral systems, as well as strengthening maternal management at lower-level hospitals, could reduce unnecessary admission to the ICU, and improve maternal and fetal outcomes.
