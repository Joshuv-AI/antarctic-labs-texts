Health worker practices and challenges surrounding routine immunisation service delivery of measles-containing vaccine: a cross-sectional assessment of health facilities in Oromia region, Ethiopia – 2021
Authors: Tesfaye Deti Gelmessa, Abyot Bekele Woyessa, Monica P Shah, Almea M Matanock, Susan Nkengasong, Binyam Moges Azmeraye, Jeff Pan, Leuel Lisanwork, Getnet Yimer, Shu-Hua Wang, Qian An, Paulos Samuel, Bekana Tolera Gonfa, Birhanu Kenate, Abebe Bekele Hurissa, Getachew Wako, Amsalu Shiferaw, Yohannes Lakew Tefera, Melkamu Ayalew Kokebie, Tatek Bogale Anbessie, Habtamu Teklie Wubie, Aaron S Wallace, Ciara E Sugerman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEthiopia’s routine immunisation schedule has included the first dose of measles-containing vaccine (MCV1) since 1980, and MCV2 was introduced in 2019. However, challenges with MCV service delivery and frontline health worker (HW) practices persist.MethodsA cross-sectional assessment was conducted between February and March 2021 among HWs (n=72) and Expanded Program on Immunization (EPI) focal persons (n=29) from 18 randomly selected districts and 36 health facilities that provide vaccination in Oromia, Ethiopia. Participants were interviewed about their practices during vaccination sessions, the availability of updated reporting tools for MCV2, the cold-chain system, immunisation programme operations and vaccine supply monitoring. Data management and descriptive analyses were carried out using R.ResultsAmong HWs, 76% and 40% reported offering MCV at every static and outreach vaccination session, respectively, 72% needed at least five children to open a 10-dose MCV vial, 13% reported recent (past 12 months) cancellation of MCV sessions due to vaccine stock-outs and 29% regularly discussed with the performance monitoring team. The availability of updated reporting tools varied; 36% had updated immunisation registers, 40% had updated tally sheets, 63% had updated vaccination cards and 76% had updated monthly reporting forms. District EPI focal persons reported inconsistent power supply (68%) and at least one recent vaccine stock-out (17%).ConclusionsInconsistent delivery of MCV at all vaccination sessions and the reluctance of HWs to open the measles vaccine vial could lead to missed opportunities for measles vaccination. MCV coverage could be improved by switching to a five-dose MCV vial, re-enforcing HW training in open vial policies and session management, ensuring availability of updated monitoring tools and monitoring vaccine stock.

Introduction

Ethiopia has provided routine childhood vaccinations through the Expanded Program on Immunization (EPI) since 1980 to reduce mortality and morbidity from vaccine-preventable diseases. The EPI programme initially included six vaccine-preventable diseases: BCG, measles, polio, diphtheria, pertussis and tetanus.1 Since its introduction, the EPI programme in Ethiopia has been supported by legal frameworks, including the 1993 health policy, which promotes access to immunisation services and strengthened access and utilisation in all areas of the country.2 EPI remains a core component of subsequent health sector development and transformation plans to vaccinate all eligible children in their first year of life and beyond.3 4 As part of implementing immunisation policy and strategic documents, comprehensive multi-year plans have been developed and implemented nationwide, with the most recent one published in 2021.5 6

Ethiopia’s immunisation programme has been embedded into the health system structure at all levels, from the Federal Ministry of Health to the health post level. The programme is managed by a designated team in the maternal and child health directorate at the national level, integrated with the maternal and child health team at the regional level. It has one or two officers or focal persons at lower levels. There are in-service training packages and platforms for health workers (HWs) and EPI managers at different levels of the health system. The WHO’s Immunization in Practice and middle-level management training modules were adapted, accredited and provided to EPI focal persons and vaccinators. Furthermore, all essential components of the immunisation programme were included in integrated refresher training for the health extension workers from health posts where about 68% of infants received routine vaccinations.7

In Ethiopia, routine immunisation services are provided in 94% of public and 2% of private facilities,8 through one or more strategies (static, outreach and mobile), depending on the distance between the health facility (HF) and the community’s settlement.6 9 To ensure that all eligible children seeking immunisation services at HFs or outreach sites receive the vaccinations, EPI policy states that multidose vials should be opened for measles-containing vaccine (MCV) and BCG, irrespective of the number of children present during a vaccination session.10 Nationally, different evidence showed that 81% of HFs provided childhood immunisation services; however, only 12% of HFs offered routine immunisation services daily during weekdays.11 12 The remaining HFs offered static vaccination sessions less frequently, at either once per week (25%) or only once per month (40%).12 This limited service availability may result from several challenges, including concerns about vaccine wastage,13 conflict-related disruptions14 and inadequate cold-chain capacity,15 specially at health posts.

Since the launch of EPI, Ethiopia has introduced hepatitis B and Haemophilus influenzae type b vaccines in 2007, pneumococcal conjugate vaccine in 2011, rotavirus vaccine in 2013,16 human papillomavirus vaccine in 201817 and injectable polio vaccine in 2015.18 In February 2019, the second dose of MCV (MCV2) was introduced in the routine immunisation schedule for children 15–23 months to reduce measles morbidity and mortality and accelerate progress towards achieving global and regional measles elimination goals. While it is estimated that over 3.3 million children receive MCV2 annually in Ethiopia, the country has experienced low MCV coverage; shortly after the introduction of MCV2 and at the time of this assessment, WHO/UNICEF estimates of immunisation coverage in 2019 were 58% for MCV1 and 41% for MCV2,19 and estimates from a Mini Demographic and Health Survey between March and June 2019 were 59% for MCV1 and 9% for MCV2.20 As a result of inadequate MCV coverage, measles has remained a major public health challenge in Ethiopia, particularly in the Oromia region.21 22

Given these challenges with MCV coverage in Ethiopia, we conducted an assessment 2 years after the introduction of MCV2 to describe policies, practices and operational aspects of routine immunisation service delivery from the perspective of HWs and EPI focal persons at selected HFs and districts in Oromia Region. The assessment was undertaken in Oromia because it has among the lowest MCV1 coverage nationally (48.7%)20 and accounts for approximately 50% of Ethiopia’s zero-dose children.23

Methods

Study setting

The assessment was carried out in Oromia Region, Ethiopia, between 1 February and 18 March 2021. Oromia is expansive in its relative geographic area, sharing 34% of Ethiopia’s landmass,24 and is also the most populous region, with about 40 million people, representing an estimated 1.2 million births or 37% of the nation’s population.25 The region has 8622 public HFs, including 108 hospitals, 1399 health centres and 7115 health posts. All public HFs are tasked to provide routine immunisation services.

Study design

This assessment was part of a broader study to understand barriers and facilitators to MCV2 vaccination uptake and delivery, published elsewhere.26 The previous article examined vaccination coverage and caregiver-related factors, while this component focused on HW practices and challenges in delivering measles vaccine services. A cross-sectional assessment was conducted among HWs and district/zonal-level EPI focal persons using structured questionnaires in 36 HFs from 18 randomly selected districts in the Oromia Region. The districts and HFs were randomly selected after initial stratification by rural/urban settlement and the MCV1 baseline administrative coverage. In each selected woreda, two health centres were randomly chosen using a lottery method. Following this, one health post linked to each selected health centre was also randomly selected, applying the same method. The detailed selection criteria and sampling strategy are described elsewhere.26 One EPI focal person was interviewed at each district/zone, while one or two HWs who provided routine immunisation services were interviewed at each selected HF, depending on availability. The survey team also observed immunisation recording tools and cold chains to verify the responses of participants.

Data collection, management and analysis

The survey team was comprised of 20 data collectors and four supervisors. HWs were interviewed and information related to experiences with vaccine administration, type and frequency of vaccination sessions, vaccine cold chain management and vaccination recording practices were collected using structured questionnaires and observations (online supplemental material: Questionnaire for Health Care Workers). Information on immunisation performance monitoring and supervision at each level was also collected by interviewing EPI focal persons and reviewing meeting minutes and supervision feedback. EPI focal persons at the selected districts were also interviewed on vaccination microplanning, vaccine stock, source of power supply for vaccine cold chain management, supportive supervision schedules, vaccination defaulter tracking system and availability of budget for cold chain equipment maintenance. All questionnaires were pre-programmed with logic checks and skip patterns using Open Data Kit and field tested prior to data collection. Data were collected electronically on Android tablets and stored on a secure cloud-based server. Data cleaning and statistical analyses were carried out using R statistical software.27 Descriptive analyses were conducted and frequencies were reported to summarise the findings. Data were presented for all areas combined and stratified by rural and urban settings.

Results

HW practices related to routine immunisation and MCV service delivery

Among 72 HWs interviewed, 80.6% (94.4% rural; 66.7% urban) reported offering MCV2 as part of routine immunisation services at their respective HF. However, the frequency at which MCV was offered varied; most HWs (76.4% overall; 86.1% rural and 76.7% urban) reported that MCV was offered at every static vaccination session, and less than half (40.3%) stated that MCV was offered at every outreach vaccination session. Many HWs (72.2% overall; 75.0% rural and 69.4% urban) reported that a minimum of five children were needed at vaccination sessions to open a 10-dose MCV vial. A notable proportion of HWs (12.5%) experienced cancellation of MCV sessions at least once in the prior year due to MCV stock-out, and when faced with a cancelled session, half (51.4%) reported that MCV would be offered as part of special catch-up vaccination sessions, while 38.9% reported that MCV would be offered at the child’s next visit. In the case of stock-out of one or more vaccines, 83.3% of the HWs administered any available vaccines, 9.7% waited until all vaccines were available in the stock and 5.6% provided based on situations. Many HWs (72.2% overall; 77.8% rural and 66.7% urban) stated that their HF had a prioritisation process when measles vaccine stock was low; of those with a prioritisation strategy (n=52), 57.7% gave priority to children coming from remote areas, while 26.9% gave priority to infants (table 1).

Availability of reporting and recording tools updated for MCV2

The availability of reporting and recording tools updated to include MCV2 varied. Among interviewed HWs, <50% reported availability of updated tally sheets and immunisation registers, whereas about two-thirds reported updated vaccination cards, and three-fourths reported updated monthly reporting forms. A slightly higher proportion of HWs in rural areas reported availability of updated immunisation registry and monthly reporting forms, and a higher proportion of HWs in urban areas reported availability of updated tally sheet (figure 1).

Immunisation performance monitoring practice and supportive supervision

HWs were also interviewed about their interactions with the performance monitoring team (PMT). About one-third (29% overall; 25.0% rural and 33.3% urban) reported regular PMT meetings with HF staff regarding routine immunisations. Among those who held regular meetings with HF staff (n=21), the frequency was monthly (52.4% overall; 55.6% rural and 50.0% urban), weekly (33.3% overall; 22.2% rural and 41.7% urban) or as needed (14.3% overall; 22.2% rural and 8.3% urban). There was variation in the topics covered at meetings, including session schedules, patient-related matters, operational challenges, vaccine stock situation, number of children vaccinated, strategies to increase the vaccination coverage and decrease dropout rate, data recording and reporting. Face-to-face (66.7% overall; 72.2% rural and 61.1% urban) meetings and phone calls (56.9% overall; 63.9% rural and 50.0% urban) were the most frequently reported communication methods (table 2).

One-third (33.3% overall; 30.6% rural and 36.1% urban) of HWs responded that they had never met yet during 2021 with performance monitoring team members or EPI focal persons from a sub-national or higher level, while 14% had one and 25% had two or more meetings in the past year. Among HWs who reported at least one meeting with performance monitoring team members or EPI focal persons from higher levels, the agendas covered a variety of topics, including EPI data reporting, routine immunisation performance, immunisation sessions, operational challenges, defaulter tracking and stock management. Among the HWs, 62.5% reported at least one supportive supervision visit from the district level, 34.7% reported no supervisory visits and the remaining 2.8% did not know whether there were visits from the district level in the last 1 year period (table 2).

Routine immunisation service delivery at the district and zonal levels

District and zonal EPI focal persons (n=29) were interviewed about their capacity to provide routine immunisation services and MCV2 in their respective areas. Many (68%) reported a power supply problem for vaccine cold chain management and only 37% reported that vaccines were stored at recommended temperatures, between 2–8°C. Among the assessed EPI units (n=29), 42% had a vaccine stock monitoring ledger book and 80% of these were up to date with recent vaccine and vaccine supply movement activities. Overall, 17% of the EPI units experienced vaccine stock-outs in the year prior to the assessment, and 100% believed the stock-outs affected the delivery of MCV2 in the routine immunisation programme. Only 21% of EPI units had a plan to discuss routine immunisation performance and challenges with local leaders, while 66% of the EPI units had a micro-plan for routine immunisations, which included MCV2 indicators. Most (83%) reported the availability of a supervision schedule to support HFs, however only 46% of the EPI units were verified as having a written supervision schedule (table 3).

Discussion

We assessed HF and district/zonal level practices and capacity with delivering MCV in the context of the routine immunisation programme in Oromia, Ethiopia, shortly after MCV2 was introduced in the national immunisation schedule. Our findings showed that MCV is not always offered with other vaccines at each vaccination session due to the lack of adherence to the open vial policy, vaccine stock-outs and cold chain constraints.

Notably, the most common reason for not providing MCV was the perception that five or more children were needed to open the 10-dose measles vaccine vial. In accordance with Ethiopia’s EPI guidance, a vial should be opened even for one eligible child presenting to the HF. However, immunisation providers are concerned about the perception of reporting high vaccine wastage; therefore, in practice, most providers require at least five eligible children to be present before opening a 10-dose measles vaccine vial. Similar practices have been reported in Zambia28 and Nigeria,29 where at least five or six children were reportedly needed to open the measles vaccine vial, respectively. Moreover, reluctance to open measles vaccine vials was reported in several African settings when only a few children presented at vaccination sessions.30 In Ethiopia, waiting for at least five children to open a measles vaccine vial may be a result of the indicative 35% measles vaccine wastage used for vaccine forecasting and micro-planning in the country.31 All possible causes contributing to wastage, including the effect of multiple-dose vials, time after reconstitution, number of doses required, multidose vial open policy, vaccination sessions, cold chain and others were considered in indicative vaccine wastage rate. That means, if the measles vaccine is offered to every eligible child as they present for vaccination, the wastage rate could be around the estimated indicative wastage rate. Hence, HWs should provide MCV for each child as they arrive. However, in practice, HWs were waiting for at least five children to accumulate to keep the wastage rate at or below 35%, which reflects a misunderstanding of the concepts of the policy. To get sufficient children to open a measles vaccine vial, most health facilities have special measles vaccination days. MCV is offered less frequently at vaccination sessions. In our findings, only three in four (76%) HWs reported MCV was offered at every static vaccination session at HFs. This practice was reported in other African countries. For instance, MCV was offered in only 63%32 and 87%33 of static sessions in Nigeria and Malawi, respectively. Training HWs and switching the 10-dose MCV vial to a five-dose vial could reduce the fear of vaccine wastage from HWs and waiting time for caregivers at vaccination sessions in order to open the vial.

Our assessment also showed that stock-outs of one or more vaccines led to cancellation of vaccination sessions and potential missed opportunities for offering MCV. One in 10 HWs reported cancelling vaccination sessions when one vaccine was out of stock. Cancelling the entire routine immunisation session because of one vaccine stock out is a missed opportunity to provide any of the remaining vaccines. Hence, these practices could impact the coverage and utilisation of measles vaccine. Therefore, uptake of vaccination could improve if HWs were encouraged to provide any available vaccines to all eligible children using every contact point with the immunisation system and reschedule the missed dose at the earliest time possible. Moreover, effective communication with caregivers and good record-keeping are critical to ensure that all vaccines are available to every child. Consideration should be given to incorporating these concepts into national EPI policy documents, guidelines, strategies and training, as applicable.

Measles vaccine stock-outs were also reported at district and HF levels, which sometimes led to the cancellation of vaccination sessions. Such interruptions not only limit service availability but may also undermine caregivers’ trust in the health system. Consistent with this, our earlier findings showed that caregivers who reported being turned away due to cancelled vaccination sessions were significantly less likely to return for their child’s MCV1 vaccination.26 The frequency of stock-outs for the measles vaccine was similar in this study (17%) as in Tanzania (14%)32 and Malawi (22%).33 Stock-out of vaccines might be attributed to limitations in stock management,34 35 improper utilisation of the vaccine stock ledger book and lack of timely vaccine requests. If a child misses MCV2 due to vaccine stock-out, some facilities reported having special catch-up vaccination sessions, while others wait for the next contact to offer the MCV2 doses. Currently, there is no other vaccine contact in the second year of life. Therefore, ensuring a reliable stock of MCV is crucial to facilitate timely vaccination.

In Ethiopia, vaccines and supplies are delivered from the central level to sub-hubs and then directly to district or HFs through the Ethiopia Pharmaceutical Supply Service.36 Vaccine requisition and delivery practices are based on the Vaccine Requisition Form (VRF) at the administrative and HF levels, with a known ordering date and lead time. However, most HFs, especially in health posts, are not using the VRF to request the vaccine every month.34 Lack of regular stock balance monitoring, knowledge gaps in vaccine needs forecasting and lack of supportive supervision and feedback may be contributing factors to lack of submission of VRF and well-timed ordering every month. This needs further prioritised examination for focused action.

Our assessment revealed that only about 37% of district-level cold chain equipment is maintained within the recommended temperature range. This deviation is likely due to episodes of both freezing and overheating, which can compromise vaccine potency. Therefore, consistent and effective monitoring of the vaccine cold chain is essential to ensure vaccine quality and programme effectiveness.

This study also highlighted gaps in the availability of updated routine immunisation recording and reporting tools, including the child’s vaccination card, tally sheets, immunisation registration books and vaccine stock management ledger books. Only a small proportion of HWs reported availability of updated tools to reflect the addition of MCV2 doses since introduction. Another recent study in northern Ethiopia demonstrated a similar situation in which a significant proportion of the HFs had no immunisation recording tools.37 The proper vaccination recording tools are not only used to record and document the doses of vaccines administered, but also necessary for HWs to appoint the caregivers for the remaining subsequent doses and to follow-up on missing doses for which they are eligible. Furthermore, the child vaccination card is also a reminder card for caregivers to return to the HF for the remaining doses. Hence, the lack of updated immunisation recording tools likely impacts the service delivery and utilisation of MCV2 in the assessed areas.

The assessment also demonstrated the gaps in the performance monitoring team and district-level EPI focal persons in supporting routine immunisation delivery service at the HF level. Only 30% of respondents reported a meeting with performance monitoring team members, this suggests a lack of systematic monitoring targets, identifying barriers, assessing integration opportunities with other health services, management support, timely discussion on immunisation issues and designing contextualised corrective actions to rectify challenges affecting routine immunisation performance. Further, critical gaps in planning and executing supportive supervision at each level were highlighted. This demonstrates the gap in tracking progress against acceptable standards and catchment targets, identifying weaknesses such as areas with low coverage or high dropouts and taking appropriate action.

One limitation of this study includes the exclusion of conflict-affected areas, which may differ in immunisation delivery service and therefore limit the generalisability of the findings to the broader population. In addition, due to resource constraints, we were able to include only a relatively small number of health facilities in this assessment, which may reduce the generalisability of the results. However, the sampled facilities were selected from a wide range of contexts, stratified by rural and urban settings as well as by high and low measles vaccination performance, to capture a variety of contexts within the sample.

Conclusion

Two years following the introduction of MCV2 in Ethiopia’s EPI schedule, we found that MCV was not always offered in static and outreach vaccination sessions in Oromia and HWs were reluctant to open the MCV vial if fewer than five children were present. Additionally, the availability of immunisation tools updated for MCV2 varied and highlighted additional gaps. Collectively, these challenges could lead to missed opportunities for vaccination and compromise progress towards improving MCV coverage. Switching from the 10-dose measles vaccine vial to a five-dose vial; establishing a sustainable system to supply routine immunisation monitoring tools; improving vaccine stock management, improving HWs knowledge through essential pre-service curricula and comprehensive in-service training on vaccine vial open policy and conducting regular supportive supervision could be of substantial benefit for increasing MCV delivery and coverage.
