Delta-matroids and toric degenerations in OG ⁡ ( n , 2 ​ n + 1 ) \OG(n,2n+1)
Grace Chen, Carl Lian

Abstract
. We construct an explicit, embedded degeneration of the general torus orbit closure in the maximal orthogonal Grassmannian OG ⁡ ( n , 2 ​ n + 1 ) \OG(n,2n+1) into a union of Richardson varieties. In particular, we deduce a formula for the cohomology class of the torus orbit closure, as a sum of products of Schubert classes. The moment map images of the degenerate pieces are the base polytopes of their underlying delta-matroids, and give a polyhedral decomposition of the unit hypercube.



1.
Introduction

Let
w_{1},\ldots,w_{n}\in\mathbb{C}^{r}
be a collection of vectors. The associated
matroid

M
is the data of the
r
-element subsets of
S\subset[n]
for which
\{w_{q}:q\in S\}
is a basis for
\mathbb{C}^{r}
. Many interesting combinatorial invariants of the matroid may be encoded geometrically in the associated torus orbit closure
Z\subset\Gr(r,n)

[
Spe09
]
, defined as follows. Let
A
be the
r\times n
matrix whose columns are given by the
w_{j}
. Let
\Lambda\in\Gr(r,n)
be the row-span of
A
, assumed to be of dimension
r
. The
n
-dimensional torus
T=(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{n}
acts on matrices
A
by scaling columns. Then,
Z=\overline{T\cdot\Lambda}
is the closure of the orbit of
\Lambda
under the induced
T
-action on
\Gr(r,n)
.

It is therefore of combinatorial and geometric interest to determine the cohomology classes (more generally, K-theory classes) of torus orbit closures
Z\subset\Gr(r,n)
. For the torus orbit closures of a
general
linear subspace
\Lambda
(associated to the
uniform
matroid
M
), Berget-Fink proved via equivariant localization
[
BF17
]
that

(1)

[Z]=\sum_{\mu\subset(n-r-1)^{r-1}}\sigma_{\mu}\sigma_{\overline{\mu}}\in H^{2(n-r-1)(r-1)}(\Gr(r,n)).

Here,
\overline{\mu}
denotes the complement of the partition
\mu
in a rectangle with
n-r-1
columns and
r-1
rows.

The formula (
1
) also follows from an earlier result of Anderson-Tymoczko
[
AT10
]
of the same shape for general torus orbits (more generally, Hessenberg varieties) in the full flag variety
\Fl(n)
, obtained using a degeneracy locus formula of Fulton
[
Ful91
]
. Klyachko
[
Kly85
]
had previously found a different formula for the class
[Z]
, expressed additively in terms of the Schubert basis. A comparison of these formulas on
\Gr(r,n)
via Coskun’s geometric Littlewood Richardson rule
[
Cos09
]
is given in
[
Lia24c
]
. A combinatorial interpretation for Klyachko’s coefficients on
\Fl(n)
has recently been obtained by Nadeau-Tewari
[
NT23
]
.

The form of (
1
) suggests that one may be able to obtain a different proof by degeneration. Namely, the formula would follow from the existence of an embedded degeneration of
Z
into a union of generically transverse intersections of two Schubert varieties (Richardson varieties) of classes
\sigma_{\mu},\sigma_{\overline{\mu}}
. Indeed, such a degeneration was constructed explicitly in
[
Lia24b
]
in the full flag variety setting, also re-proving the formula of Anderson-Tymoczko. See also
[
Lia24a
]
for connections between these degenerations and curve-counting questions in enumerative geometry. Via the toric moment map, the combinatorial shadow of this degeneration is Harada-Horiguchi-Masuda-Park’s polyhedral decomposition of the permutohedron
[
HHMP19
]
.

The purpose of this paper is to extend this story to type B. The type B analog of a matroid is a
delta-matroid
. See
[
EFLS24
,
ELS25
,
Lar25
]
for recent work on the combinatorics and geometry of delta-matroids. Let
\mathbb{C}^{2n+1}
be an odd-dimensional complex vector space, equipped with a non-degenerate symmetric quadratic form, and let
\OG(n,2n+1)
be the orthogonal Grassmannian of (maximal) isotropic subspaces of dimension
n
. Let
\Lambda\in\OG(n,2n+1)
be a point, given by the row span of a
n\times(2n+1)
-matrix
A_{\Lambda}
. Then, the (realizable) delta-matroid associated to a point
\Lambda
is given by the data of the admissible (Definition
2.3.1
)
n
-element subsets of independent column vectors of
A_{\Lambda}
.

The vector space
\mathbb{C}^{2n+1}
, and thus the orthogonal Grassmannian
\OG(n,2n+1)
, admit a natural action of the
n
-dimensional torus
T
, and it is again of interest to determine the class of a general torus orbit
[Z]
in
H^{*}(\OG(n,2n+1))
.

1

In
[
ELS25
]
, a
K
-theory class
y(\mathcal{D})
on
\OG(n,2n+1)
is associated to a delta-matroid
\mathcal{D}
. If
\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{D}(\Lambda)
is the realizable delta-matroid associated to
\Lambda\in\OG(n,2n+1)
, then
y(\mathcal{D})
does
not
always agree with the class of the structure sheaf of the associated torus-orbit closure
[\mathcal{O}_{\overline{T\cdot\mathcal{D}(\Lambda)}}]
, see
[
ELS25
, Proposition 2.3]
, in contrast to the setting of ordinary matroids
[
Spe09
]
. However, these classes do agree when
\Lambda
is general, which is the main case of interest in this paper.
We prove:

Theorem 1.0.1
.

Let
\Lambda\in\OG(n,2n+1)
be a general point, and let
Z_{\Lambda}=\overline{T\cdot\Lambda}
be the closure of the orbit of
T\cong(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{n}
. Then,

[Z_{\Lambda}]=\sum_{I\subset[n-1]}\sigma_{I}\sigma_{I^{c}}\in H^{n(n-1)}(\OG(n,2n+1)).

Here,
I^{c}
denotes the complement of
I\subset[n-1]
. See §
2.1
for notation for Schubert classes and Schubert varieties. While other proofs of Theorem
1.0.1
along the lines of the earlier work in type A are presumably possible, our approach is by degeneration. We prove:

Theorem 1.0.2
.

There exists an explicit,
T
-invariant degeneration of
Z_{\Lambda}
into a union of Richardson varieties

\Sigma_{I,I^{c}}:=\Sigma^{F}_{I}\cap\Sigma^{F^{\prime}}_{I^{c}},

as subschemes of
\OG(n,2n+1)
, each appearing with multiplicity 1.

In particular, Theorem
1.0.1
also holds
T
-equivariantly. Our degeneration is constructed in §
4
. It is, in some sense, as simple as possible: it proceeds iteratively by sending entries of a matrix whose row span is
\Lambda
to 0, one at a time. The intermediate steps go through torus orbits of subspaces lying in Richardson varieties associated to
allowed pairs
(Definition
3.1.1
). While the approach is similar to that of
[
Lia24b
]
, in type B, one needs more care to ensure that the degeneration of matrix entries is compatible with the underlying quadratic form. In §
3
, we identify coordinates for the intermediate Richardson varieties that appear, which allow us to carry out the degeneration in explicit terms while controlling the interaction with the quadratic form.

While the explicit equations allow us to identify limit components of our degeneration, the inexistence of further components is less apparent. This is established via the moment map: we show in §
5
that the moment polytopes (base polytopes of the associated delta-matroids) of the Richardson varieties
\Sigma_{I,I^{c}}
cover that of the general torus orbit. Here, the picture is again as simple as possible: the base polytope of the uniform delta-matroid is the unit hypercube
[0,1]^{n}
, and the pieces are cut out by hyperplanes.

That the limit components each appear with multiplicity 1 is also deduced on the level of moment polytopes. Unlike in the case of ordinary Grassmannians, this is not immediate, see Remark
5.3.2
.

In the appendix, we record some well-known foundational results on
\OG(n,2n+1)
and delta-matroids. In particular, we provide a proof of the equivalence of two definitions of the base polytope of the matroid associated to
\Lambda\in\OG(n,2n+1)
in Theorem
A.2.1
.

In February 2025, we learned, from a talk of Melissa Sherman-Bennett at the IAS workshop “Combinatorics of Enumerative Geometry,” of the forthcoming work of Knutson–Sanchez–Sherman-Bennett
[
KSSB
]
on degenerations of torus orbits in flag varieties
G/B
of arbitrary Lie type. They construct, for every Coxeter element
w\in W
of the associated Weyl group, a degeneration of the general torus orbit closure into a union of Richardson varieties, and study the corresponding moment map images. In particular, they obtain families of different formulas for the torus orbit closures, which push forward to formulas on the partial flag varieties
G/P
.

In type B, it is highly plausible, but as far as we are aware, not obvious, that (at least) one of their degenerations on the full type B flag variety
\SO(2n+1)/B
parametrizing maximal flags of isotropic subspaces pushes forward to the one that we have constructed on
\OG(n,2n+1)
. Their degenerations are valid in much greater generality than ours, but as we understand, are constructed more abstractly. We have tried to emphasize in this work the explicit nature of our degeneration; it would be interesting to match it to one of theirs. We thank the authors of
[
KSSB
]
for communicating their results and encouraging us to complete this paper.

Conventions

•

If
S
is a subset, then its cardinality is denoted
|S|
.

•

[n]
denotes the set
\{1,\ldots,n\}
, and
\overline{[n]}
denotes the set
\{\bar{1},\ldots,\overline{n}\}
. We also write
\widetilde{[n]}:=[n]\cup\overline{[n]}
and
\widetilde{[n]}^{0}=\widetilde{[n]}\cup\{0\}
.

•

If
a,b
are positive integers, then we often write
[a,b]
to mean the set of
integers
in the interval
[a,b]
, and
\overline{[a,b]}
to denote the set
\{\overline{a},\overline{a+1},\ldots,\overline{b}\}
.

•

The symbol
\overline{\phantom{x}}
denotes the natural involution on the set
\widetilde{[n]}^{0}
, where
\overline{0}=0
, as well as the natural involution on the set of
subsets

S\subset\widetilde{[n]}^{0}
. For example, if
S=\{0,1,\overline{2}\}
, then
\overline{S}=\{0,\bar{1},2\}
.

•

We use angle brackets
\langle\cdot\rangle
to denote linear span of vectors.

•

The entry of a matrix
A
in row
p
and and column
q
is denoted
a^{p}_{q}
. The column set of
A
will typically be identified with
\widetilde{[n]}^{0}
, in which case
q
is an element of
\widetilde{[n]}^{0}
.

•

If
A
is a matrix with column set
\widetilde{[n]}^{0}
and
S\subset\widetilde{[n]}^{0}
is a subset, then
A_{S}
denotes the sub-matrix of
A
consisting of columns indexed by
S
.

•

If
X
is an irreducible quasi-projective scheme over
\mathbb{C}
, then a “general point” is a closed point lying in a (possibly unspecified) non-empty Zariski open subset. The term “generic point” is reserved for the unique point (not closed unless
X
is itself a point) which lies in every non-empty Zariski open subset of

. The same goes for the general and generic fibers of a morphism

.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Orthogonal Grassmannians

Let
n\geq 2
be an integer. Let
\mathbb{C}^{2n+1}
be a vector space, for which we fix a basis

e_{1},\ldots,e_{n},e_{0},e_{\overline{n}},\ldots,e_{\overline{1}}

Throughout, we refer to this basis as the
standard basis
of
\mathbb{C}^{2n+1}
. The
coordinates
of a vector
x\in\mathbb{C}^{2n+1}
always refer to the coordinates in the standard basis, and matrices whose row vectors are elements of
\mathbb{C}^{2n+1}
expressed in standard coordinates are understood to have columns indexed by the set
\widetilde{[n]}^{0}
.

For any subset
S\subset\widetilde{[n]}^{0}
, let
H_{S}\subset\mathbb{C}^{2n+1}
denote the span of the basis vectors
e_{j}
for
j
lying in the
complement
of
S
. That is,
H_{S}
is the subspace of vectors which, when expressed in the standard basis, are zero in the coordinates indexed by
S
. In particular, the codimension of
H_{S}
in
\mathbb{C}^{2n+1}
is
|S|
. We also write
H_{j}=H_{\{j\}}
.

We equip
\mathbb{C}^{2n+1}
with the non-degenerate symmetric form defined by

y\cdot z=2y_{0}z_{0}+(y_{1}z_{\bar{1}}+y_{\bar{1}}z_{1})+\cdots+(y_{n}z_{\overline{n}}+y_{\overline{n}}z_{n}),

where the
y_{q},z_{q}
,
q\in\widetilde{[n]}^{0}
are the coordinates of
y,z
, respectively. Let
\OG(n,2n+1)
be the maximal orthogonal Grassmannian of
n
-dimensional isotropic subspaces of
\mathbb{C}^{2n+1}
. The
\mathbb{C}
-scheme
\OG(n,2n+1)
is smooth, projective, and irreducible of dimension
\frac{n(n+1)}{2}
. The orthogonal Grassmannian is naturally included in the usual Grassmannian
\Gr(n,2n+1)
of
n
-planes in
\mathbb{C}^{2n+1}
, which in turn is embedded in the projective space
\mathbb{P}^{\binom{2n+1}{n}-1}
via the Plücker embedding:

\OG(n,2n+1)\hookrightarrow\Gr(n,2n+1)\hookrightarrow\mathbb{P}(\wedge^{n}(\mathbb{C}^{2n+1}))\cong\mathbb{P}^{\binom{2n+1}{n}-1}.

The
n
-dimensional torus
T=(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{n}
acts linearly on
\mathbb{C}^{2n+1}
by:

\displaystyle(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n})\cdot e_{q}

\displaystyle=t_{q}e_{q},

\displaystyle(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n})\cdot e_{\overline{q}}

\displaystyle=t_{q}^{-1}e_{\overline{q}}

for
j=1,\ldots,n
, and
(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n})\cdot e_{0}=e_{0}
. The action of
T
on
\mathbb{C}^{2n+1}
induces a generically free action of
T
on
\OG(n,2n+1)
.

Let
\Lambda\in\OG(n,2n+1)
be a point. In this paper, we will be primarily be interested in the torus orbit closures

Z_{\Lambda}:=\overline{T\cdot\Lambda}\subset\OG(n,2n+1).

and their classes
[Z_{\Lambda}]\in H^{*}(\OG(n,2n+1))
. If
\Lambda
is a general point, then
Z_{\Lambda}
is irreducible of dimension
n
.

2.2.
Schubert and Richardson varieties

We next recall the definitions of Schubert varieties in
\OG(n,2n+1)
. We essentially follow the indexing of
[
Cos14
]
. For
q=0,\ldots,n
, define the isotropic subspaces

\displaystyle F_{q}

\displaystyle=\langle e_{1},\ldots,e_{q}\rangle,

\displaystyle F^{\prime}_{q}

\displaystyle=\langle e_{\overline{1}},\ldots,e_{\overline{q}}\rangle.

of
\mathbb{C}^{2n+1}
. When
q=0
, the subspaces
F_{q},F^{\prime}_{q}
are both zero. Define the complete isotropic flags

\displaystyle F

\displaystyle=(0=F_{0}\subset F_{1}\subset\cdots\subset F_{n}\subset F_{n}^{\perp}\subset\cdots\subset F_{1}^{\perp}\subset F_{0}^{\perp}=\mathbb{C}^{2n+1}),

\displaystyle F^{\prime}

\displaystyle=(0=F^{\prime}_{0}\subset F^{\prime}_{1}\subset\cdots\subset F^{\prime}_{n}\subset(F^{\prime}_{n})^{\perp}\subset\cdots\subset(F^{\prime}_{1})^{\perp}\subset(F^{\prime}_{0})^{\perp}=\mathbb{C}^{2n+1}).

The flags
F,F^{\prime}
are transverse, in the sense that the intersection of any two of their constituent subspaces is transverse.

Let
I\subset[n]
be a subset. Let
\lambda_{1}>\cdots>\lambda_{s}
be the elements of
I
, which we may identify with the partition
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{s})
. Let
\mu_{s+1}>\ldots>\mu_{n}
be the non-negative integers obtained by removing the integers
n-\lambda_{s},\ldots,n-\lambda_{1}
from
n-1,\ldots,0
. For example, if
I=\{1,4,6\}\subset\{1,2,3,4,5,6,7\}
, then
(\mu_{4},\mu_{5},\mu_{6},\mu_{7})=(5,4,2,0)
.

Definition 2.2.1
.

Define the
Schubert variety

\Sigma_{I}^{F}\subset\OG(n,2n+1)
to be the closure of the subscheme
\Sigma_{I}^{F,\circ}\subset\OG(n,2n+1)
of isotropic subspaces
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{C}^{2n+1}
satisfying:

\displaystyle\dim(\Lambda\cap F_{n+1-\lambda_{h}})

\displaystyle=h,\text{ for }h=1,\ldots,s,

\displaystyle\dim(\Lambda\cap F^{\perp}_{\mu_{h}})

\displaystyle=h,\text{ for }h=s+1,\ldots,n.

The Schubert variety
\Sigma_{I}^{F}
is non-empty, reduced and irreducible of codimension

w(I):=\lambda_{1}+\cdots+\lambda_{s}

This may be seen, for example, by realizing
\Sigma_{I}^{F,\circ}
as a double coset in
\OG(n,2n+1)
. In particular,
\Sigma_{I}^{F}
defines a class
\sigma_{I}:=[\Sigma_{I}^{F}]\in H^{2w(I)}(\OG(n,2n+1))
. The classes of Schubert varieties freely generate
H^{*}(\OG(n,2n+1))

[
BGG73
,
Bor54
]
.

Similarly, if
I^{\prime}\subset[n]
is a subset, to which we associate the partitions
\lambda^{\prime},\mu^{\prime}
as above, then we may define the Schubert variety
\Sigma_{I^{\prime}}^{F^{\prime}}\subset\OG(n,2n+1)
, of class
\sigma_{I^{\prime}}=[\Sigma_{I^{\prime}}^{F^{\prime}}]\in H^{2w(I^{\prime})}(\OG(n,2n+1))
.

Definition 2.2.2
.

Let
I,I^{\prime}\subset[n]
be subsets. Define the
Richardson variety

\Sigma_{I,I^{\prime}}:=\Sigma_{I}^{F}\cap\Sigma_{I^{\prime}}^{F^{\prime}}\subset\OG(n,2n+1)
.

A standard argument using the Kleiman transversality theorem shows that
\Sigma_{I,I^{\prime}}
is reduced and irreducible of codimension
w(I)+w(I^{\prime})
, the expected, and that a general point of the intersection in fact lies in the open locus
\Sigma_{I}^{F,\circ}\cap\Sigma_{I^{\prime}}^{F^{\prime},\circ}
. In particular, we have

[\Sigma_{I,I^{\prime}}]=\sigma_{I}\sigma_{I^{\prime}}\in H^{2(w(I)+w(I^{\prime}))}(\OG(n,2n+1)).

Because our Richardson varieties will always be defined with respect to the flags
F,F^{\prime}
, we drop the flags from the notation.

2.3.
Delta-matroids and polytopes

In this section, we discuss the (realizable) delta-matroid
\mathcal{D}(\Lambda)
associated to a point
\Lambda\in\OG(n,2n+1)
, and various auxiliary objects. See
[
DH86
,
Bou87
,
CK88
,
BGW03
]
for foundations of the theory of delta-matroids and
[
EFLS24
,
ELS25
,
Lar25
]
for more recent work.

Definition 2.3.1
.

A subset
S\subset\widetilde{[n]}
is
admissible
if, for all
q\in[n]
, we have
\{q,\overline{q}\}\not\subset S
. An admissible subset
S
is
maximal
if
|S|=n
.

The collection of admissible subsets
S\subset\widetilde{[n]}
is denoted
\Adm_{n}
, and the collection of maximal admissible subsets is denoted
\mAdm_{n}
.

Definition 2.3.2
.

Let
\Lambda\in\OG(n,2n+1)
be an isotropic subspace, and let
S\subset\widetilde{[n]}
be an admissible subset. We define
\rk_{\Lambda}(S)\in[0,|S|]
by the rank of the linear map

\Lambda\hookrightarrow\mathbb{C}^{2n+1}\to\mathbb{C}^{2n+1}/H_{S},

where we recall from §
2.1
that
H_{S}
is the subspace of
\mathbb{C}^{2n+1}
spanned by the vectors
e_{q}
,
q\notin S
.

We also define

g_{\Lambda}(S):=\rk_{\Lambda}(S)-|S\cap\overline{[n]}|.

Concretely, if
A
is any
n\times(2n+1)
matrix whose rows form a basis of
\Lambda
, then
\rk_{\Lambda}(S)
is the rank of the submatrix
A_{S}
(see §
1.1
).

Definition 2.3.3
.

Let
\Lambda\in\OG(n,2n+1)
be an isotropic subspace. The
delta-matroid

\mathcal{D}(\Lambda)\subset\mAdm_{n}
associated to
\Lambda
is the collection of maximal admissible
S\subset\widetilde{[n]}
for which
\rk_{\Lambda}(S)=n
.

In fact, the data of
\mathcal{D}(\Lambda)
determines the value of
\rk_{\Lambda}(S)
, and therefore the value of
g_{\Lambda}(S)
, for
any
admissible subset
S
, not necessarily maximal, see Corollary
A.1.4
of the Appendix. We deal only with realizable delta-matroids in this paper, that is, those that arise as
\mathcal{D}(\Lambda)
for some
\Lambda\in\OG(n,2n+1)
; the data of
\Lambda
will always be present.

We now define the polytope
\mathcal{P}(\Lambda)\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
associated to
\Lambda
. Let
\mathbb{R}^{n}
be an
n
-dimensional real vector space, with standard basis
f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}
.

Definition 2.3.4
.

Given a point
x=x_{1}f_{1}+\cdots+x_{n}f_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and an admissible subset
S\in\Adm_{n}
, define

x(S):=\sum_{q=1}^{n}\epsilon_{q}x_{q}\in\mathbb{R}

where

\epsilon_{q}:=\begin{cases}1&\text{ if }q\in S\\
-1&\text{ if }\overline{q}\in S\\
0&\text{ otherwise}\end{cases}

Definition 2.3.5
.

We define
\mathcal{P}(\Lambda)\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
to be the convex polytope cut out by the inequalities

x(S)\leq g_{\Lambda}(S)

for all
S\in\Adm_{n}
.

Taking
S
to be each of the singleton sets
\{q\},\{\overline{q}\}
yields immediately that
\mathcal{P}(\Lambda)\subset[0,1]^{n}
. For a general point
\Lambda\in\OG(n,2n+1)
, the polytope
\mathcal{P}(\Lambda)
is the full hypercube
[0,1]^{n}
. The polytope
\mathcal{P}(\Lambda)
is the base polytope of the delta-matroid
\mathcal{D}(\Lambda)
, which is usually defined to be the convex hull of the
0-1
vectors

\chi(S^{\prime}):=\sum_{q\in[n]\cap S^{\prime}}f_{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

ranging over maximal admissible
S^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}(\Lambda)
. A proof of the equivalence of these definitions is given in §
A.2
of the appendix.

Recall from §
2.1
that
\Lambda\in\OG(n,2n+1)
gives rise to a torus orbit closure
Z_{\Lambda}\subset\OG(n,2n+1)
. The image of
Z_{\Lambda}
under the
moment map

\mu:Z_{\Lambda}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{P}(\wedge^{n}\mathbb{C}^{2n+1})\to\mathbb{R}^{n}

is equal to twice-dilated polytope

\widehat{\mathcal{P}(\Lambda)}:=2\mathcal{P}(\Lambda)-(1,\ldots,1),

see
[
EFLS24
, Proposition 6.2]
and
[
GS87
, §5.2, Proposition 1]
. The inclusion
Z_{\Lambda}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{P}(\wedge^{n}\mathbb{C}^{2n+1})
is the Plücker embedding, and the map
\mathbb{P}(\wedge^{n}\mathbb{C}^{2n+1})\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is defined by

\sum_{S}x_{S}e_{S}\mapsto\sum_{S}\frac{|x_{S}|^{2}}{\sum_{S}|x_{S}|^{2}}f_{S},

where

f_{S}:=\sum_{q\in S\cap[n]}f_{q}-\sum_{q\in\overline{S}\cap[n]}f_{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

and the sums are over
all
(not necessarily admissible) subsets
S\subset\widetilde{[n]}
of cardinality
n
. Moreover, the dimension of
\widehat{\mathcal{P}(\Lambda)}
(and thus
\mathcal{P}(\Lambda)
) as a polytope in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
equals the dimension of
Z_{\Lambda}
as a subvariety of
\OG(n,2n+1)

[
GS87
, §3.2, Theorem 1]
.

2.4.
Degenerations of torus orbits

Let
U\subset\Spec(\mathbb{C}[t])=\mathbb{A}^{1}_{\mathbb{C}}
be a Zariski open subset containing the point
t=0
. (One can just as well take
U
to be an analytic open disk, or the spectrum of
\mathbb{C}[t]_{(t)}
or
\mathbb{C}[[t]]
.)

We will be interested in degenerations of torus orbit closures in
\OG(n,2n+1)
, by which we mean proper flat families

\pi:\mathcal{Z}\subset\OG(n,2n+1)\times U\to U,

which are fiberwise
T
-invariant, and whose (geometric) fibers away from
t=0
are given by torus orbit closures
Z_{\Lambda_{t}}
. Equivalently,
\pi
is a 1-parameter family in the Chow quotient
\OG(n,2n+1)//T
in the sense of
[
KSZ91
]
. (More precisely,
\pi
is a 1-parameter family in
\mathbb{P}(\wedge^{n}\mathbb{C}^{2n+1})//T
lying in the image of
\OG(n,2n+1)
.)

As an algebraic cycle, the special fiber
\pi^{-1}(0)
is given by a positive linear combination of irreducible components

c_{1}Z_{1}+\cdots+c_{m}Z_{m}.

of the same dimension as
Z_{\Lambda_{t}}
. Each of the components
Z_{k}
is itself a torus orbit closure
Z_{\Lambda_{k}}
for some
\Lambda_{k}\in\OG(n,2n+1)
, and the polytopes
\mathcal{P}(\Lambda_{k})
form a polyhedral decomposition of
\mathcal{P}(\Lambda)

[
KSZ91
, Proposition 3.6]
, see also
[
Kap93
, Proposition 1.2.11]
for the case of ordinary Grassmannians.

Furthermore, the coefficients
c_{k}
may be computed in terms of the moment polytopes as follows, see
[
KSZ91
, (1.3)]
and the proof of
[
Kap93
, Proposition 1.2.15]
. Let
\Xi
(resp
\Xi_{k}
) be the lattice generated by differences of vertices of
\mathcal{P}
(resp.
\mathcal{P}_{k}
). Then,
c_{k}=[\Xi:\Xi_{k}]
.

Corollary 2.4.1
.

Let
\pi
be a degeneration of torus orbits as above, whose general fiber is the torus orbit closure
Z_{\Lambda_{t}}
. Suppose that
Z_{\Lambda_{1}},\ldots,Z_{\Lambda_{m^{\prime}}}
are distinct torus orbit closures contained in the special fiber
\pi^{-1}(0)
, each of the same dimension of
Z_{\Lambda_{t}}
. Suppose further that

\mathcal{P}(\Lambda_{t})=\bigcup_{k=1}^{m^{\prime}}\mathcal{P}(\Lambda_{i}).

Then, the irreducible components of the special fiber
\pi^{-1}(0)
are precisely
Z_{\Lambda_{1}},\ldots,Z_{\Lambda_{m^{\prime}}}
. In particular, we have

[Z_{\Lambda_{t}}]=\sum_{k=1}^{m^{\prime}}c_{k}[Z_{\Lambda_{k}}]\in H^{2\dim(\Lambda_{t})}(\OG(n,2n+1)).

Proof.

If
\dim(Z_{\Lambda_{i}})=\dim(Z_{\Lambda_{t}})
, then by flatness,
Z_{\Lambda_{i}}
must be an irreducible component of the special fiber. As discussed above, it must appear with multiplicity 1. If there were any other components of the special fiber, then their (dilated) moment map images would be non-empty sub-polytopes of
\mathcal{P}(\Lambda_{t})
of the same dimension, and along with the
\mathcal{P}(\Lambda_{i})
, would form a polyhedral decomposition of
\mathcal{P}(\Lambda_{t})
. However, this is impossible by the assumption on the union of the
\mathcal{P}(\Lambda_{i})
. The formula in cohomology follows, as the class remains constant in a flat family of algebraic cycles.
∎

3.
Charts for Richardson varieties

Our degeneration of the general torus orbit of in
\OG(n,2n+1)
passes through torus orbits of subspaces
\Lambda\in\Sigma_{I,I^{\prime}}
lying in successively more special Richardson varieties, associated to
allowed pairs

(I,I^{\prime})
. In order to make these degenerations explicit, we will need affine coordinates on the Richardson varieties
\Sigma_{I,I^{\prime}}
, given in the end of this section by Corollary
3.5.4
.

3.1.
Allowed pairs

Definition 3.1.1
.

Let
I,I^{\prime}\subset[n-1]
be subsets. We say that pair
(I,I^{\prime})
is an
allowed pair
if
I\cap I^{\prime}=\emptyset
, and in addition one of the following holds:

(i)

I\cup I^{\prime}=[n-1]
,

(ii)

(I,I)=(\emptyset,\emptyset)
,

(iii)

(I,I^{\prime})=(\emptyset,\{j\})
for some
j\in[n-2]
,

(iv)

for some
k\in[2,n-1]
, we have
k\in I^{\prime}
and
I\cup I^{\prime}=[k,n-1]
, or

(v)

for some
j,k\in[n-1]
with
1\leq j\leq k-2
, we have
j,k\in I^{\prime}
and
I\cup I^{\prime}=\{j\}\cup[k,n-1]
.

We say that the allowed pair
(I,I^{\prime})
is
saturated
if
I\cup I^{\prime}=[n-1]
(case (i)). It is clear in cases (iii)-(v) that the integers
j,k
are intrinsic to the pair of subsets
(I,I^{\prime})
: for example, in case (v),
j
is the smallest element of
I\cup I^{\prime}
, and
k
is the second-smallest, both lying necessarily in
I^{\prime}
.

For indexing purposes, it will be convenient to view (ii)-(iv) as degenerate cases of (v). Cases (iii) and (iv) can be taken to be the degenerate cases of (v) where
k=n
and
j=0
, respectively, and case (ii) can be taken to be the further degenerate case where
(j,k)=(0,n)
. In the degenerate cases,
I,I^{\prime}
are still taken to be subsets of
[1,n-1]
, so requirements that
0,n\in I^{\prime}
are ignored. For example, taking
k=n
in case (v) reads: for some
j
with
1\leq j\leq n-2
, we have
j,n\in I^{\prime}
and
I\cup I^{\prime}\in\{j\}\cup[n,n-1]=\{j\}
. We interpret this to mean that
j\in I^{\prime}
and
I\cup I^{\prime}=\{j\}
, which is precisely case (iii).

Definition 3.1.2
.

Let
(I,I^{\prime})
be an allowed pair. If
(I,I^{\prime})
is not saturated, then define the non-negative integers
j(I,I^{\prime}),k(I,I^{\prime})\leq n
to be the values of
j,k
above. If
(I,I^{\prime})
is saturated, then we define
j(I,I^{\prime})=0
and
k(I,I^{\prime})=1
.

We have
0\leq j(I,I^{\prime})\leq k(I,I^{\prime})-2
, unless
(I,I^{\prime})
is saturated. Note that if
(I,I^{\prime})
is saturated, it is no longer the case that
k(I,I^{\prime})=1
necessarily lies in
I^{\prime}
. We now organize the set of allowed pairs into a rooted binary tree
\mathcal{T}_{n}
, see Definition
3.1.4
.

Definition 3.1.3
.

Let
(I,I^{\prime})
be a non-saturated allowed pair, and write
j=j(I,I^{\prime})
and
k=k(I,I^{\prime})
. Define the subsets
I_{+},I^{\prime}_{+}\subset[n-1]
by

\displaystyle I_{+}

\displaystyle=[j+1,k-1]\cup I,

\displaystyle I^{\prime}_{+}

\displaystyle=(I^{\prime}\setminus\{j\})\cup\{j+1\}.

Define also

\displaystyle\ell((I,I^{\prime}))

\displaystyle=(I,I^{\prime}_{+}),

\displaystyle r((I,I^{\prime}))

\displaystyle=(I_{+},I^{\prime}).

By definition, the non-empty interval
[j+1,k-1]
is disjoint from
I
and
I^{\prime}
, so
I_{+}
strictly contains
I
and remains disjoint from
I^{\prime}
. If
j\geq 1
, then also by definition, we have that
j
is the minimum element of
I^{\prime}
and that
j+1
does not lie in
I^{\prime}
or
I
. Thus,
I^{\prime}_{+}
increments this minimum element by 1 and remains disjoint from
I
. If
j=0
, then by definition
1\notin I,I^{\prime}
, so
I^{\prime}_{+}
simply adds 1 to
I^{\prime}
. By construction,
\ell((I,I^{\prime}))
and
r((I,I^{\prime}))
are also allowed pairs.

Definition 3.1.4
.

Let
\mathcal{T}_{n}
be the rooted binary tree constructed iteratively as follows. The root vertex is labeled by the allowed pair
(\emptyset,\emptyset)
. Every vertex of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
labeled by a non-saturated allowed pair
(I,I^{\prime})
gives rise to left and right child vertices labeled by the allowed pairs
\ell((I,I^{\prime}))
and
r((I,I^{\prime}))
, respectively. The
2^{n-1}
leaves of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
are the saturated allowed pairs.

Example 3.1.5
.

The tree
\mathcal{T}_{4}
is shown below.
(\emptyset,\emptyset)
(\emptyset,\{1\})
(\{1,2,3\},\emptyset)
(\emptyset,\{2\})
(\{2,3\},\{1\})
(\emptyset,\{3\})
(\{3\},\{2\})
(\emptyset,\{1,3\})
(\{1,2\},\{3\})
(\{3\},\{1,2\})
(\{1,3\},\{2\})
(\emptyset,\{2,3\})
(\{2\},\{1,3\})
(\{1\},\{2,3\})
(\{\emptyset,\{1,2,3\})

Every allowed pair appears exactly once in
\mathcal{T}_{n}
; we identify the set of vertices of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
with the set of allowed pairs. Indeed, any allowed
(I,I^{\prime})
can be obtained in a unique way by starting with
(\emptyset,\emptyset)
and iteratively either incrementing the minimum element of
I^{\prime}
(or adding 1) or adding the “gap”
[j+1,k-1]
to
I
. This corresponds to the unique downward path through
\mathcal{T}_{n}
from the root vertex to the vertex associated to
(I,I^{\prime})
.

Example 3.1.6
.

Take
n=9
, and let
(I,I^{\prime})
be the saturated allowed pair
(\{1,4,5,6,8\},\{2,3,7\})
. The unique path from the root of
\mathcal{T}_{9}
to
(I,I^{\prime})
is:

\displaystyle(\emptyset,\emptyset)\to(\emptyset,\{1\})\to(\emptyset,\{2\})\to\cdots\to(\emptyset,\{7\})\to(\{8\},\{7\})

\displaystyle\to

\displaystyle(\{8\},\{1,7\})\to(\{8\},\{2,7\})\to(\{8\},\{3,7\})\to(\{4,5,6,8\},\{3,7\})

\displaystyle\to

\displaystyle(\{4,5,6,8\},\{1,3,7\})\to(\{4,5,6,8\},\{2,3,7\})\to(\{1,4,5,6,8\},\{2,3,7\}\}).

Roughly speaking, the elements of
I^{\prime}
must be set in order from largest to smallest by successively taking the left fork down
\mathcal{T}_{n}
, and the gaps between consecutive elements of
I^{\prime}
must be filled into
I
at the appropriate times by taking the right fork.

3.2.
Matrices for allowed pairs

Let
(I,I^{\prime})
be an allowed pair, and write
j=j(I,I^{\prime})
and
k=k(I,I^{\prime})
. In order to give local coordinates for
\Sigma_{I,I^{\prime}}
, we first construct an
n\times(2n+1)
matrix
M_{I,I^{\prime}}
, each of whose entries is either 0 or one of the symbols
*,+,1
. Eventually,
M_{I,I^{\prime}}
will represent a general point of
\Sigma_{I,I^{\prime}}
.

Write
I=\{\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{s}\}
,
I^{\prime}=\{\lambda^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,\lambda^{\prime}_{s^{\prime}}\}
, where
\lambda_{1}>\cdots>\lambda_{s}
and
\lambda^{\prime}_{1}>\cdots>\lambda^{\prime}_{s^{\prime}}
. We also write
\mu_{s+1}>\cdots>\mu_{n}=0
and
\mu^{\prime}_{s^{\prime}+1}>\cdots>\mu^{\prime}_{n}=0
as in §
2.2
. The columns of
M_{I,I^{\prime}}
will be indexed (in order) by the set
\{1,\ldots,n,0,\overline{n},\ldots,\bar{1}\}
, and the rows are indexed by the set
[n]
.

We now set the entries of
M_{I,I^{\prime}}
, beginning with those that are 0.

(1)

If
1\leq p\leq s^{\prime}
, then in the
p
-th row of
M_{I,I^{\prime}}
, place the symbol 0 in the leftmost
(n+\lambda^{\prime}_{p})
entries.

(2)

If
s^{\prime}+1\leq p\leq n
, then in the
p
-th row of
M_{I,I^{\prime}}
, place the symbol 0 in the leftmost
\mu^{\prime}_{p}
entries.

Dually:

(3)

If
n\geq p\geq n-s+1
, then in the
p
-th row of
M_{I,I^{\prime}}
, place the symbol 0 in the rightmost
(n+\lambda_{n+1-p})
entries.

(4)

If
n-s\geq p\geq 1
, then in the
p
-th row of
M_{I,I^{\prime}}
, place the symbol 0 in the rightmost
\mu_{n+1-p}
entries.

We then fill the remaining entries of
M_{I,I^{\prime}}
with the symbols
*,+,1
, as follows:

(5)

In the rightmost unfilled entry of each row of
M_{I,I^{\prime}}
, place the symbol 1.

(6)

In all remaining unfilled entries
a^{p}_{\overline{q}}
(where
\overline{q}\in\overline{[n]}
) and
a^{p}_{0}
, place the symbol
+
.

(7)

In all remaining unfilled entries
a^{p}_{q}
(where
q\in[n]
), place the symbol
*
.

.

For
n=8
,

\Sigma_{I,I^{\prime}}\subset\OG(n,2n+1)
, we replace the entries

and

in

with complex numbers, to obtain a new matrix

. (The name
M_{I,I^{\prime}}
is reserved for the matrix of symbols constructed above, whereas

will denote a matrix whose entries are complex numbers.) An entry
a^{p}_{q}
of the new matrix

is called a “
-

-\in\{0,1,+,*\}
, if the corresponding entry of
M_{I,I^{\prime}}
is
-
. When we say that an entry
a^{p}_{q}
of

is “non-zero,” we mean that
a^{p}_{q}\in\mathbb{C}-\{0\}
. In particular, this is stronger than saying that
a^{p}_{q}
is not a 0-entry.

Definition 3.2.2
.

Let
\mathcal{M}_{I,I^{\prime}}
be the parameter space of
n\times(2n+1)
matrices
A
obtained by replacing the symbols
+,*
in
M_{I,I^{\prime}}
with complex numbers, with the property that the rows of
A
are orthogonal.

Write
b(I,I^{\prime})
and
d(I,I^{\prime})
for the numbers of
*
- and
+
-entries of
M_{I,I^{\prime}}
, respectively. Then,
\mathcal{M}_{I,I^{\prime}}
is the closed affine subscheme

\mathcal{M}_{I,I^{\prime}}\subset\mathbb{C}^{b(I,I^{\prime})}\times\mathbb{C}^{d(I,I^{\prime})}

cut out by the equations corresponding to orthogonality of the rows of
A
.

Lemma 3.2.3
.

Let
A\in\mathcal{M}_{I,I^{\prime}}
be a matrix. Then, the rows of
A
are linearly independent.

Proof.

By steps (3) and (4) of the construction of
M_{I,I^{\prime}}
, the 1-entries of
A
appear, from bottom to top, in columns

(n+1)-\lambda_{1},\ldots,(n+1)-\lambda_{s},\overline{1+\mu_{s+1}},\ldots,\overline{1+\mu_{n}}=\overline{1},

which are all distinct. The entries to the right of the 1-entries are zero. The linear independence follows.
∎

In particular, we obtain a map
\ssp:\mathcal{M}_{I,I^{\prime}}\to\OG(n,2n+1)
given by taking row span. The positions of the 0-entries in

guarantee further that the image of

lies in the Richardson variety
\Sigma_{I,I^{\prime}}
. We will see that a general point of
\mathcal{M}_{I,I^{\prime}}
in fact maps to the dense open locus
\Sigma_{I}^{F,\circ}\cap\Sigma_{I^{\prime}}^{F^{\prime},\circ}
of
\Sigma_{I,I^{\prime}}
.

We obtain two maps

\textstyle{\mathcal{M}_{I,I^{\prime}}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\pr}
\scriptstyle{\ssp}
\textstyle{\mathbb{C}^{d(I,I^{\prime})}}
\textstyle{\Sigma_{I,I^{\prime}}}

where
\pr
remembers the

-entries of a matrix

. We will show the following:

(1)

(Proposition

)

is birational.

(2)

(Corollary

) On the locus of
\mathcal{M}_{I,I^{\prime}}
where

Therefore, the rational map
\phi_{I,I^{\prime}}:\mathbb{C}^{d(I,I^{\prime})}\dashrightarrow\Sigma_{I,I^{\prime}}

3.4.
The map
\pr

We now return to our task of describing charts for
\Sigma_{I,I^{\prime}}
. Recall that we have defined
\mathcal{M}_{I,I^{\prime}}
to be the affine variety of matrices of shape
M_{I,I^{\prime}}
, with orthogonal rows. There is a natural projection map

\pr:\mathcal{M}_{I,I^{\prime}}\to\mathbb{C}^{d(I,I^{\prime})}

remembering only the
+
-entries.

Proposition 3.4.1
.

The map
\pr
is birational. That is, there is a non-empty Zariski open subset
V_{I,I^{\prime}}\subset\mathbb{C}^{d(I,I^{\prime})}
over which
\pr
is an isomorphism.

We will prove Proposition
3.4.1
by showing that, for a general choice of
+
-entries of a matrix
A\in\mathcal{M}_{I,I^{\prime}}
, there is a unique choice of
*
-entries so that the rows of
A
are orthogonal. This amounts to solving a system of linear equations in the
*
-entries whose coefficients are the chosen
+
-entries, and the open subset
V_{I,I^{\prime}}
will be simply the locus where the determinants of the coefficient matrices do not vanish.

We will first prove Proposition
3.4.1
in two special cases (Propositions
3.4.2
and
3.4.4
), and then combine the arguments to prove the claim in general.

Proposition 3.4.2
.

Proposition
3.4.1
holds when
I=\emptyset
and either
I^{\prime}=\emptyset
or
I^{\prime}=\{j\}
, where
j\in[1,n-2]
.

Proof.

As in Definition
3.1.2
, write
j=j(I,I^{\prime})=0
if
I^{\prime}=\emptyset
, and otherwise let
j
be the unique element of
I^{\prime}
.

Write
v_{p}
for the row vector in the
p
-th row of
A
, and write
v_{p}^{*}
for the vector whose coordinates are the
*
-entries of
v_{p}
. We claim that the vectors
v_{p}^{*}
can be uniquely solved for by pairing with the appropriate rows above. More precisely:

(1)

for
p=2,\ldots,j
, in increasing order, we solve for the
(p-1)

*
-entries of
v_{p}
by considering the system of
p-1
equations

v_{2}\cdot v_{p},v_{3}\cdot v_{p},\ldots,v_{p}\cdot v_{p}=0.

(2)

for
p=j+1,\ldots,n
, in increasing order, we solve for the
p

*
-entries of
v_{p}
by considering the system of
p
equations

v_{1}\cdot v_{p},v_{2}\cdot v_{p},\ldots,v_{p}\cdot v_{p}=0.

Case (1) only applies when
j\geq 2
. In case (1), the system of equations takes the form

A_{p}\cdot v_{p}^{*}=\gamma_{p},

where
A_{p}
is the submatrix of
A
with row set
[2,p]
and column set
\overline{[n-p+2,n]}
, and
\gamma_{p}\in\mathbb{C}^{p-1}
is a vector depending on
A
and the
*
-entries solved for in the earlier rows. In case (2),
A_{p}
is the submatrix of
A
with row set
[1,p]
and column set
\overline{[n-p+1,n]}
, and
\gamma_{p}\in\mathbb{C}^{p}
is again a vector depending on
A
and the
*
-entries solved for in the earlier rows. The columns of
A_{p}
are taken in the same order as they appear in
A
, so that column
\overline{n}
appears at the far left. See Example
3.4.3
for a depicted example of the matrices
A_{p}
. The vector
v_{p}^{*}
is now taken to be a column vector, with entries appearing from top to bottom with the
right-most

*
-entry
a^{p}_{n}
on top.

We claim that, in both cases, the anti-diagonal of
A_{p}
contains no 0-entries. This will imply that the determinant of
A_{p}
, viewed as a function of the
+
-entries of
A
, is not identically zero. Therefore, as long as the
+
-entries are chosen such that
\det(A_{p})\neq 0
for all
p
, there will exist a unique solution
v_{p}^{*}
for each
p
. We take
V_{I,I^{\prime}}\subset(\mathbb{C}-\{0\})^{d(I,I^{\prime})}
to be the non-empty Zariski open subset defined by the non-vanishing of the
\det(A_{p})
.

By construction, any entry immediately to the southwest of a non-0-entry is also not a 0-entry. Therefore, to verify the claim, it suffices to check that the upper right entry of every
A_{p}
is not a 0-entry. In increasing order of
p
, these upper right entries are:

a^{2}_{\overline{n}},\ldots,a^{2}_{\overline{n-j+2}},a^{1}_{\overline{n-j}},\ldots,a^{1}_{\bar{1}}.

By construction, all of the above are
+
-entries, except
a^{1}_{\bar{1}}=1
, as needed.

Therefore, for a general choice of
+
-entries in
V_{I,I^{\prime}}
, the vectors
v_{p}^{*}
can be solved for uniquely, determining a unique matrix
A
. It suffices now to observe that the remaining dot products

v_{1}\cdot v_{1},\ldots,v_{1}\cdot v_{j}

are automatically zero, for
any
choice of
+
- and *-entries of
A
. Therefore, the matrix
A
in fact lies in
\mathcal{M}_{I,I^{\prime}}
.
∎

\overline{[(n+1)-\lambda_{n-p},\ldots,(n+1)-\lambda_{n-p+1}]}
. See Example

for a depicted example of the matrices

.

More precisely, the non-0-entries of

form a subset of the lattice path

, alternating between rightward and upward steps, starting with a rightward step from southwest corner, and ending in a rightward step to the northeast corner. The upward steps are those in the columns in the interval
\overline{[n+2-\lambda_{n-p},n-\lambda_{n-p+1}]}
. In particular, if
\lambda_{n-p}-\lambda_{n-p+1}=1
, then this interval is empty. Accordingly,
R_{p}
consists of a unique row, and no upward step occurs in columns
\overline{n+2-\lambda_{n-p}},\overline{n-\lambda_{n-p+1}}
. Each rightward step in
A_{p}
starts from a
+
-entry and ends on a 1-entry.

Then,
v_{p}
is orthogonal to every row of
A
if and only if
A_{p}v_{p}^{*}=0
. Note that this equation does not depend on the values of the
*
-entries outside of the
p
-th row of
A
. By the structure of
A_{p}
described above, there is a unique such vector
v_{p}^{*}
with
A_{p}v_{p}^{*}=0
for
any
choice of non-zero
+
entries of
A
, and all of its coordinates non-zero. Indeed, each
*
-entry in
v_{p}
is determined by the entry immediately to the right by pairing with the appropriate row in
R_{p}
.

Similarly, when
p=n-s
, one can solve uniquely for the entries of
v_{n-s}^{*}
, all non-zero, from the equations

v_{n-s}\cdot v_{n-s}=0,\ldots,v_{n-s}\cdot v_{(n+1)-s-\lambda_{s}}=0.

The vectors

uniquely determine the matrix
A
, and all
*
-entries are non-zero. Furthermore,
v_{p}
is automatically orthogonal to any row outside
R_{p}
, so all of the rows of

are orthogonal. This completes the proof.
∎

Proposition 3.5.2
.

Proof.

4.2.
The degeneration in general

We first make precise the needed generality hypotheses on

Definition 4.2.1
.

We say that
\Lambda\in\Sigma_{I,I^{\prime}}
is
nice
if the birational map

\phi_{I,I^{\prime}}:\mathbb{C}^{d(I,I^{\prime})}\dashrightarrow\Sigma_{I,I^{\prime}},

constructed in the previous section, is an isomorphism over a neighborhood of
\Lambda
.

The notion of niceness depends on the allowed pair
(I,I^{\prime})
. If
\Lambda
is nice, then it may be viewed equally well as a matrix
A\in\mathcal{M}_{I,I^{\prime}}
or a point
a\in\mathbb{C}^{d(I,I^{\prime})}
with non-zero coordinates, corresponding to the
+
-entries of
A
. We also say that
a\in\mathbb{C}^{d(I,I^{\prime})}
or
A\in\mathcal{M}_{I,I^{\prime}}
is nice if it corresponds to a nice
\Lambda\in\Sigma_{I,I^{\prime}}
. We will freely identify nice
a,A,\Lambda
, but reserve each of these letters to indicate the type of object in question: a vector in
\mathbb{C}^{d(I,I^{\prime})}
, a matrix, or a subspace.

Definition 4.2.2
.

We say that
\Lambda\in\Sigma_{I,I^{\prime}}
is
very nice
if it is nice and, for every maximal admissible subset
S\in\mAdm_{n}
, either
S\in\mathcal{D}(\Lambda)
or, it is the case that
S\notin\mathcal{D}(\Lambda_{\bullet})
for
every

\Lambda_{\bullet}\in\Sigma_{I,I^{\prime}}
.

Similarly, we say that
a\in\mathbb{C}^{d(I,I^{\prime})}
or
A\in\mathcal{M}_{I,I^{\prime}}
are very nice if they correspond to very nice
\Lambda
.

Recall that
\mathcal{D}(\Lambda)
is the set of maximal admissible
S
for which the
n\times n
submatrix
A_{S}
has full rank. For a fixed
S\in\mAdm_{n}
, the locus of (nice)
\Lambda\in\Sigma_{I,I^{\prime}}
for which
S\in\mathcal{D}(\Lambda)
is open, but possibly empty. The very nice locus is the intersection of the non-empty open loci, and in particular non-empty. Equivalently, a nice
\Lambda
is very nice if it has the largest possible (ordered by inclusion) polytope
\mathcal{P}(\Lambda)
among all points of
\Sigma_{I,I^{\prime}}
.

Lemma 4.2.3
.

Suppose that
(I,I^{\prime})
is saturated and that
\Lambda\in\Sigma_{I,I^{\prime}}
is nice. Then:

(a)

\Lambda
is very nice.

(b)

Z_{\Lambda}=\Sigma_{I,I^{\prime}}
. In particular,
\dim(Z_{\Lambda})=n
.

Proof.

We claim that
T
acts transitively on the nice locus of
\Sigma_{I,I^{\prime}}
. By Proposition
3.4.4
, a nice subspace in
\Lambda\in\Sigma_{I,I^{\prime}}
is given by the row span of a matrix
A
obtained by replacing the
+
-entries of
M_{I,I^{\prime}}
with
any
non-zero complex numbers, and solving uniquely for the
*
-entries. Thus, we may identify a nice point of
\Sigma_{I,I^{\prime}}
with the tuple

a=(a_{0},a_{\overline{n}},\ldots,a_{\bar{2}})\in(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{n}=(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{d(I,I^{\prime})}

of
+
-entries appearing in
A
.

For
q\in[2,n]
, the element
(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n})\in T
acts on the coordinate
a_{\overline{q}}
by
\frac{t_{r(q)}}{t_{q}}
, where
\overline{r(q)}\in\overline{[q-1]}
is the column containing the unique 1-entry of
A
appearing in the same row as
a_{\overline{q}}
. The same is true if
\overline{q}=0
, in which case we interpret the denominator
t_{q}
to be equal to 1. The corresponding weights
f_{r(q)}-f_{q}\in\mathfrak{t}^{*}
form an integral basis of the weight lattice of
T
. Therefore, after a change of variables, the action of
T
on the nice locus of
\Sigma_{I,I^{\prime}}
, identified with
(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{n}
, is the standard one, and in particular transitive.

Now, because the
T
-action preserves the polytope
\mathcal{P}(\Lambda)
, every nice
\Lambda\in\Lambda_{I,I^{\prime}}
has the same polytope. Therefore, we obtain (a). Furthermore, the nice locus of
\Sigma_{I,I^{\prime}}
is contained in
Z_{\Lambda}
, and because the nice locus is dense, we have
\Sigma_{I,I^{\prime}}\subset Z_{\Lambda}
. The opposite inclusion is obvious, so we have (b).
∎

In our degeneration, we will modify the entries of a matrix

Definition 4.2.6

Lemma 5.2.3
.

Let
I\subset[n-1]
and
\Lambda_{I}\in\Sigma_{I,I^{c}}
be as in Proposition
5.2.1
. Then,

\rk_{\Lambda_{I}}(S)=\rk_{\Lambda_{I}}(S\cap[n])+\rk_{\Lambda_{I}}(S\cap\overline{[n]})

for all
S\in\Adm_{n}
.

Proof.

Let
A=\ssp^{-1}(\Lambda_{I})\in\mathcal{M}_{I,I^{c}}
(see §
3.2
) be the unique matrix in
\mathcal{M}_{I,I^{c}}
whose row span is equal to
\Lambda_{I}
. Then, we regard
\rk_{\Lambda_{I}}(S)
as the dimension of the column span
\langle A_{S}\rangle
of the sub-matrix
A_{S}
comprised of columns indexed by
S
.

Let
h_{i}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
, for
i=1,\ldots,n
, be the column vector whose
i
-th coordinate is 1, and all of whose other coordinates are 0. The 0-th column of
A
is a non-zero multiple of
h_{p}
, where
p=|I^{c}|+1
. Furthermore, we have

\displaystyle\langle A_{S\cap[n]}\rangle

\displaystyle\subset\langle h_{p},\ldots,h_{n}\rangle,

\displaystyle\langle A_{S\cap\overline{[n]}}\rangle

\displaystyle\subset\langle h_{1},\ldots,h_{p}\rangle.

Therefore, we have

\rk_{\Lambda_{I}}(S)=\rk_{\Lambda_{I}}(S\cap[n])+\rk_{\Lambda_{I}}(S\cap\overline{[n]})

as long as
h_{p}
does not lie in both
\langle A_{S\cap[n]}\rangle
and
\langle A_{S\cap\overline{[n]}}\rangle
.

Suppose for sake of contradiction that
h_{p}
lies in the column spans of
A_{S\cap[n]}
and
A_{S\cap\overline{[n]}}
. We claim first that
S\cap([2,n]\cup\overline{[2,n]})
is precisely the set of special columns of
P_{\SW}
and
P_{\NE}
. Without loss of generality, suppose that column
n
is special, or equivalently that
1\in I
. This means that

\langle A_{[1,n-1]}\rangle\subset\langle h_{p+1},\ldots,h_{n}\rangle.

Then, S must include
n
in order for the column span
\langle A_{S\cap[n]}\rangle
to possibly contain
h_{p}
. On the other hand,
\overline{n}\notin S
, because
S
is admissible.

Next, consider the columns
n-1,\overline{n-1}
, one of which must be special. The column span
\langle A_{S\cap[n]}\rangle
contains the
n
-th column vector of
A
, which is a linear combination

\gamma_{p}h_{p}+\gamma_{p+1}h_{p+1},

where
both
coefficients
\gamma_{p},\gamma_{p+1}
are non-zero, by Proposition
3.4.4
. If
n-1
is special and
n-1\notin S
, then the remaining vectors spanning
\langle A_{S\cap[n]}\rangle
lie in

\langle h_{p+2},\ldots,h_{n}\rangle,

so
h_{p}
cannot lie in the span
\langle A_{S\cap[n]}\rangle
. Alternatively, if column
\overline{n-1}
is special, then arguing as before shows that we need
\overline{n-1}\in S
in order for
h_{p}\in\langle A_{S\cap\overline{[n]}}\rangle
. Iterating this argument gives the claim in general.

Finally, note that
S
can contain at most one of
1,\bar{1}
. Without loss of generality, suppose that
1\notin S
. Then, a basis of
\langle A_{S\cap[n]}\rangle
is given by the vectors in the special columns among
S\cap[n]
, each of which is a linear combination with non-zero coefficients of the vectors
h_{p^{\prime}},h_{p^{\prime}+1}
, where
p^{\prime}=p,p+1,\ldots,n-1
. This column span cannot contain
h_{p}
, so we have reached a contradiction.
∎

Corollary 5.2.4
.

Adopting the setup of Proposition
5.2.1
, we have

g_{\Lambda_{I}}(S)=g_{\Lambda_{I}}(S\cap[n])+g_{\Lambda_{I}}(S\cap\overline{[n]}).

In particular, because

we have reduced Proposition

to the case where

or

.

Proposition 5.2.5
.

Proposition
5.2.1
holds when

.

Proof.

Let
\rho_{1}<\cdots<\rho_{r}
be the indices such that

n+1-\lambda_{\rho_{1}},n+1-\lambda_{\rho_{2}},\ldots,n+1-\lambda_{\rho_{r}}\in\SC(x)

are the special columns of
M_{I,I^{c}}
in
[n]

not

S
. It will be convenient also to set

\rho_{0}=0,\lambda_{0}=n+1\text{ and }\rho_{r+1}=s+1,\lambda_{s+1}=0.

Then, for every integer
i\in\{0,\ldots,r\}
, define the set

\widetilde{S}_{i}=[(n+1-\lambda_{\rho_{i}})+1,(n+1-\lambda_{\rho_{i+1}})-1].

By definition, the

are disjoint. We also have

\widetilde{S}_{0}

above, we observe that the subsets

By construction,

contains all of the

Remark 5.3.2
.

In contrast to the case of ordinary Grassmannians
[

, Proposition 1.2.15]
, it is not true for arbitrary points

that the sublattice

equals the full lattice
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
. For example, if

for

, then

is the convex hull of the vectors

, and

has index 2 in

. This example also appears in
[

, but note that our column indexing is different.

Proof of Theorem
1.0.2
.

We first show that, for any non-saturated allowed pair
(J,J^{\prime})
, in the degeneration

constructed in §

A.1.
Geometry of isotropic subspaces

We adopt the notation of §
2.1
. Fix throughout an isotropic subspace
\Lambda\in\OG(n,2n+1)
.

Lemma A.1.1
.

Let
q\in[n-1]
be an integer. Suppose that

\dim(\Lambda\cap H_{\{1,\ldots,q,q+1,\overline{q+1}\}})\geq n-q.

Then, we have

\dim(\Lambda\cap H_{\{0,1,\ldots,q,q+1,\overline{q+1},\ldots,n,\overline{n}\}})\geq 1.

Proof.

There is nothing to check if
q=n-1
, so assume that
q\leq n-2
. Observe that

\displaystyle\dim(\Lambda\cap H_{\{1,\ldots,q,q+1,\overline{q+1},q+2,q+3,\ldots,n\}})

\displaystyle\geq 1,

\displaystyle\dim(\Lambda\cap H_{\{1,\ldots,q,q+1,\overline{q+1},\overline{q+2},q+3,\ldots,n\}})

\displaystyle\geq 1,

because
H_{\{q+2,q+3,\ldots,n\}}
and
H_{\{\overline{q+2},q+3,\ldots,n\}}
have codimension
n-q-1
in
\mathbb{C}^{2n+1}
.

We claim that

\dim(\Lambda\cap H_{\{1,\ldots,q,q+1,\overline{q+1},q+2,\overline{q+2},q+3,\ldots,n\}})\geq 1.

Indeed, choose non-zero vectors

\displaystyle v

\displaystyle\in\Lambda\cap H_{\{1,\ldots,q,q+1,\overline{q+1},q+2,q+3,\ldots,n\}},

\displaystyle w

\displaystyle\in\Lambda\cap H_{\{1,\ldots,q,q+1,\overline{q+1},\overline{q+2},q+3,\ldots,n\}}.

so that, in coordinates,

\displaystyle v

\displaystyle=a_{0}e_{0}+a_{\bar{1}}e_{\bar{1}}+\cdots+a_{\overline{q}}e_{\overline{q}}+a_{\overline{q+2}}e_{\overline{q+2}}+a_{\overline{q+3}}e_{\overline{q+3}}+\cdots+a_{\overline{n}}e_{\overline{n}},

\displaystyle w

\displaystyle=b_{0}e_{0}+b_{\bar{1}}e_{\bar{1}}+\cdots+b_{\overline{q}}e_{\overline{q}}+b_{q+2}e_{q+2}+b_{\overline{q+3}}e_{\overline{q+3}}+\cdots+b_{\overline{n}}e_{\overline{n}}.

Because
\Lambda
is isotropic, we have
v\cdot v=w\cdot w=0
, hence
a_{0}=b_{0}=0
. Moreover, we have
v\cdot w=0
, hence
a_{\overline{q+2}}b_{q+2}=0
. Thus, one of
v,w
lies in
H_{\{1,\ldots,q,q+1,\overline{q+1},q+2,\overline{q+2},q+3,\ldots,n\}}
, as needed.

Note also by the same argument (swapping the roles of
q+3
and
\overline{q+3}
) that we have
both
the inequalities

\displaystyle\dim(\Lambda\cap H_{\{1,\ldots,q,q+1,\overline{q+1},q+2,\overline{q+2},q+3,\ldots,n\}})

\displaystyle\geq 1,

\displaystyle\dim(\Lambda\cap H_{\{1,\ldots,q,q+1,\overline{q+1},q+2,\overline{q+2},\overline{q+3},\ldots,n\}})

\displaystyle\geq 1.

Now, we iterate the argument: we can deduce further that

\dim(\Lambda\cap H_{\{1,\ldots,q,q+1,\overline{q+1},q+2,\overline{q+2},q+3,\overline{q+3},\ldots,n\}})\geq 1,

and so forth, until arriving at

\dim(\Lambda\cap H_{\{1,\ldots,q,q+1,\overline{q+1},\ldots,n,\overline{n}\}})\geq 1.

Finally, observe that

\Lambda\cap H_{\{1,\ldots,q,q+1,\overline{q+1},\ldots,n,\overline{n}\}}=\Lambda\cap H_{\{0,1,\ldots,q,q+1,\overline{q+1},\ldots,n,\overline{n}\}},

because
\Lambda
is isotropic.
∎

Lemma A.1.2
.

If
\dim(\Lambda\cap H_{\{1,\ldots,q\}})=n-q
, then
\dim(\Lambda\cap H_{\{1,\ldots,q,q+1,\overline{q+1}\}})\leq n-q-1
.

Proof.

Assume for sake of contradiction
\dim(\Lambda\cap H_{\{1,\ldots,q,q+1,\overline{q+1}\}})=n-q
. By Lemma
A.1.1
, we may choose a non-zero vector
v\in\Lambda\cap H_{\{0,1,\ldots,q,q+1,\overline{q+1},\ldots,n,\overline{n}\}}
. Note on the one hand that

\Lambda\subset\Lambda^{\perp}\subset v^{\perp},

and on the other hand that

H_{\{1,\ldots,q\}}=H_{\{0,1,\ldots,q,q+1,\overline{q+1},\ldots,n,\overline{n}\}}^{\perp}\subset v^{\perp}.

The subspaces
\Lambda,H_{\{1,\ldots,q\}}\subset v^{\perp}
have dimensions
n,2n+1-q
, respectively, and
v^{\perp}
has dimension
2n
. Thus,

\dim(\Lambda\cap H_{\{1,\ldots,q\}})\geq(n+(2n+1-q))-2n=n-q+1,

which is a contradiction.
∎

Corollary A.1.3
.

Let
S\subset\widetilde{[n]}
be an admissible subset. Let
J\subset[n]
be such that
J\cup\overline{J}
is disjoint from
S
. Write
\ell=|J|
. Then, for some admissible subset
J_{+}\subset J\cup\overline{J}
of size
\ell
(the largest possible), we have

\rk_{\Lambda}(S\cup J_{+})=\rk_{\Lambda}(S)+\ell

If
A
is any matrix whose rows form a basis of
\Lambda
, then recall from Definition
2.3.2
that
\rk_{\Lambda}(S)
is the rank of the submatrix
A_{S}
consisting of columns of
A
indexed by
S
. Equivalently, we have
\rk_{\Lambda}(S)=n-\dim(\Lambda\cap H_{S})
. On the level of matrices, Corollary
A.1.3
says that, for each
j\in J
, one can make a choice of either
j
or
\overline{j}
(to comprise
J_{+}
) so that the corresponding
\ell
column vectors of
A
are independent from each other and from the column span of
A_{S}
.

Proof.

We may assume that
S=\{1,2,\ldots,q\}
by permuting coordinates. We may replace
A_{S}
with a submatrix consisting of independent columns, so reduce to the case where
\rk(A_{S})=|S|=q
. We may similarly assume that
J=\{q+1,\ldots,q+\ell\}
by permuting coordinates. Then, the claim follows from Lemma
A.1.2
by induction on
\ell
.
∎

Corollary A.1.4
.

For any
S\in\Adm_{n}
, we have

\rk_{\Lambda}(S)=\max_{S^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}(\Lambda)}|S^{\prime}\cap S|.

Proof.

As above, we interpret
\rk_{\Lambda}(S)
as the rank of the matrix
A_{S}
. For any
S^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}(\Lambda)
, we have

\rk(A_{S})\geq\rk(A_{S\cap S^{\prime}})=|S^{\prime}\cap S|,

so
\rk(A_{S})\geq\max_{S^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}(\Lambda)}|S^{\prime}\cap S|
.

Conversely, let
S_{-}\subset S
be a maximal subset of linearly independent columns of
A_{S}
, so that
\rk(A_{S})=\rk(A_{S_{-}})=|S_{-}|
. Replacing
S
with
S_{-}
and taking

J=[n]\backslash\left((S_{-}\cap[n])\cup\overline{(S_{-}\cap\overline{[n]})}\right)

in Corollary
A.1.3
, there exists
S^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}(\Lambda)
such that
S^{\prime}\supset S_{-}
, namely,
S^{\prime}=S_{-}\cup J_{+}
. In particular, we have

\rk(A_{S})=|S_{-}|\leq|S^{\prime}\cap S|\leq\max_{S^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}(\Lambda)}|S^{\prime}\cap S|,

as needed.
∎

In the next section, we will also need the following property of the function
g_{\Lambda}(S)
, also defined in Definition
2.3.2
.

Corollary A.1.5
.

The function
g_{\Lambda}(S):=\rk_{\Lambda}(S)-|S\cap\overline{[n]}|
is
bisubmodular
. That is, for any two
S_{1},S_{2}\in\Adm_{n}
, we have

g_{\Lambda}(S_{1})+g_{\Lambda}(S_{2})\geq g_{\Lambda}(S_{1}\cap S_{2})+g_{\Lambda}(S_{1}\sqcup S_{2}),

where
S_{1}\sqcup S_{2}:=\{a\in S_{1}\cup S_{2}:\overline{a}\notin S_{1}\cup S_{2}\}
.

Proof.

We have:

\displaystyle|(S_{1}\cap S_{2})\cap\overline{[n]}|+|(S_{1}\cup S_{2})\cap\overline{[n]}|

\displaystyle=|S_{1}\cap\overline{[n]}|+|S_{2}\cap\overline{[n]}|,

\displaystyle\rk_{\Lambda}(S_{1})+\rk_{\Lambda}(S_{2})

\displaystyle\geq\rk_{\Lambda}(S_{1}\cap S_{2})+\rk_{\Lambda}(S_{1}\cup S_{2}),

\displaystyle\rk_{\Lambda}(S_{1}\cup S_{2})-\rk_{\Lambda}(S_{1}\sqcup S_{2})

\displaystyle\geq|\{j:j,\overline{j}\in S_{1}\cup S_{2}\}|.

The first two lines are elementary, and the third follows from Corollary
A.1.3
. The claim follows from adding the three lines.
∎

A.2.
Matroid polytopes

We adopt the notation of §
2.3
. In this section, we prove:

Theorem A.2.1
.

Let
\Lambda\in\OG(n,2n+1)
be any point, and let
\mathcal{X}(\Lambda)
be the convex hull in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
of the vectors

\chi(S^{\prime}):=\sum_{q\in[n]\cap S^{\prime}}f_{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

raniging over all
S^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}(\Lambda)
. Then,
\mathcal{X}(\Lambda)=\mathcal{P}(\Lambda)
, as where
\mathcal{P}(\Lambda)
is defined in Definition
2.3.5
.

Theorem
A.2.1
and the proof we provide are likely well-known, but we were not able to find a self-contained reference. One inclusion, Proposition
A.2.3
, is easy.

Lemma A.2.2
.

Let
S,S^{\prime}\subset\widetilde{[n]}
be admissible subsets, and assume furthermore that
S^{\prime}\in\mAdm_{n}
is maximal. Write
x=\chi(S^{\prime})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then, we have

x(S)=|S\cap S^{\prime}|-|S\cap\overline{[n]}|,

where
x(S)
is defined in Definition
2.3.4
.

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle x(S)

\displaystyle=|S^{\prime}\cap[n]\cap S|-|S^{\prime}\cap[n]\cap\overline{S}|

\displaystyle=(|S^{\prime}\cap[n]\cap S|+|S\cap\overline{[n]}|)-(|S^{\prime}\cap[n]\cap\overline{S}|+|S\cap\overline{[n]}|)

\displaystyle=(|S^{\prime}\cap[n]\cap S|+|S|-|S\cap[n]|)-(|S^{\prime}\cap[n]\cap\overline{S}|+|S\cap\overline{[n]}|)

\displaystyle=(|S|-|\overline{S^{\prime}}\cap[n]\cap S|)-(|S^{\prime}\cap[n]\cap\overline{S}|+|S\cap\overline{[n]}|)

\displaystyle=|S|-(|\overline{S^{\prime}}\cap[n]\cap S|+|\overline{S^{\prime}}\cap\overline{[n]}\cap S|)-|S\cap\overline{[n]}|

\displaystyle=(|S|-|S\cap\overline{S^{\prime}}|)-|S\cap\overline{[n]}|

\displaystyle=|S\cap S^{\prime}|-|S\cap\overline{[n]}|.

The first line follows from unwinding the definition of
x(S)=(\chi(S^{\prime}))(S)
. The remaining calculation follows from elementary set-theoretic considerations, using that
S\in\Adm_{n}
and
S^{\prime}\in\mAdm_{n}
.
∎

Proposition A.2.3
.

We have
\mathcal{X}(\Lambda)\subset\mathcal{P}(\Lambda)
.

Proof.

Because both
\mathcal{P}(\Lambda)
is convex, it suffices to prove that, for all
S^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}(\Lambda)
, we have
\chi(S^{\prime})\in\mathcal{P}(\Lambda)
. Write
x=\chi(S^{\prime})
as in Lemma
A.2.2
, and let
S\subset\widetilde{[n]}
be any admissible subset. By Lemma
A.2.2
, we have

\displaystyle x(S)

\displaystyle=|S\cap S^{\prime}|-|S\cap\overline{[n]}|

\displaystyle\leq\rk_{\Lambda}(S)-|S\cap\overline{[n]}|

\displaystyle=g_{\Lambda}(S).

The inequality
|S\cap S^{\prime}|\leq\rk_{\Lambda}(S)
follows from the fact that
S^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}(\Lambda)
. Indeed, if
A
is a matrix whose rows form a basis for
\Lambda
, then the square submatrix
A_{S^{\prime}}
comprised of columns indexed by
S^{\prime}
has full rank, so the submatrix
A_{S\cap S^{\prime}}
of
A_{S}
is comprised of
|S^{\prime}\cap S|
independent columns. As
x(S)\leq g_{\Lambda}(S)
for all admissible
S
, the point
x=\chi(S^{\prime})
satisfies all of the defining inequalities of
\mathcal{P}(\Lambda)
. This proves the Proposition.
∎

To establish the opposite inclusion, we employ the following standard strategy (see e.g.
[
LRS11
, §4.1.2-4.1.3]
): let
x\in\mathcal{P}(\Lambda)
be a vertex, so that a non-empty subset of the inequalities
x(S)\leq g_{\Lambda}(S)
are in fact equalities. By finding many admissible
S
for which
x(S)=g_{\Lambda}(S)
(Lemma
A.2.5
), we will show that
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
, and deduce that
x=\chi(S^{\prime})
for some
S^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}(\Lambda)
.

Lemma A.2.4
.

Let
x\in\mathcal{P}(\Lambda)
be any point. Then, the collection of subsets

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{x}=\{S\in\Adm_{n}:x(S)=g_{\Lambda}(S)\}.

is closed under the operations
\cap,\sqcup
. That is, we have

\displaystyle S_{1},S_{2}\in\mathcal{F}_{x}\Rightarrow S_{1}\cap S_{2}\in\mathcal{F}_{x}\text{ and }S_{1}\sqcup S_{2}\in\mathcal{F}_{x}.

Proof.

Immediate from bi-submodularity of the function
g_{\Lambda}
, Corollary
A.1.5
, and the fact that

x(S_{1})+x(S_{2})=x(S_{1}\cap S_{2})+x(S_{1}\sqcup S_{2}).

∎

Lemma A.2.5
.

Let
x\in\mathcal{P}(\Lambda)
be a vertex, and define
\mathcal{F}_{x}
as in Lemma
A.2.4
. Then, there exists a chain of admissible subsets

S_{1}\subset S_{2}\cdots\subset S_{n}\subset\widetilde{[n]},

with
|S_{j}|=j
for each
j
, and
S_{j}\in\mathcal{F}_{x}
.

Proof.

Consider any maximal chain

\mathcal{C}=(S_{j_{1}}\subset S_{j_{2}}\cdots\subset S_{j_{m}}\subset\widetilde{[n]})

of non-empty admissible subsets lying in
\mathcal{F}_{x}
, by which we mean that no further admissible subset
S\in\mathcal{F}_{x}
can be inserted into
\mathcal{C}
. We choose the indices so that
|S_{j_{k}}|=j_{k}
.

Write
j=j_{1}
. We claim that
j=1
; suppose instead that
j>1
. Without loss of generality, we assume for convenience that
S_{j}=[j]
. We claim that any
S\in\mathcal{F}_{x}
must have

S\cap([j]\cup\overline{[j]})\in\{\emptyset,[j],\overline{[j]}\}.

Indeed, by Lemma
A.2.4
, we have
S\cap[j]=S\cap S_{j}\in\mathcal{F}_{x}
. Unless
S\cap[j]=\emptyset
or
S\cap[j]=[j]
, the proper subset
S\cap S_{j}\subset S_{j}
may be appended to
\mathcal{C}
, a contradiction. If
S\cap[j]=[j]
, then because
S
is admissible, in fact
S\cap([j]\cup\overline{[j]})=[j]
. If instead
S\cap[j]=\emptyset
, then
S\cap([j]\cup\overline{[j]})=S\cap\overline{[j]}
. In this case, we have

(S\sqcup S_{j})\cap S_{j}=[j]-(\overline{S\cap\overline{[j]}})\in\mathcal{F}_{x}.

We again obtain a contradiction unless
S\cap\overline{[j]}\in\{\emptyset,\overline{[j]}\}
, as needed.

Fix any vector
v=v_{1}f_{1}+\cdots+v_{j}f_{j}\subset\langle f_{1},\ldots,f_{j}\rangle\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
for which
v_{1}+\cdots+v_{j}=0
. We claim that there exists an
\varepsilon>0
such that
x+\varepsilon v\in\mathcal{P}(\Lambda)
. Indeed, taking
\varepsilon
sufficiently small preserves the (finitely many)
strict
inequalities
(x+\varepsilon v)(S)<g_{\Lambda}(S)
for any admissible
S\notin\mathcal{F}_{x}
. Furthermore, all
equalities

(x+\varepsilon v)(S)=g_{\Lambda}(S)
for
S\in\mathcal{F}_{x}
remain true for any
\varepsilon
, because
S\cap([j]\cup\overline{[j]})\in\{\emptyset,[j],\overline{[j]}\}
and
v_{1}+\cdots+v_{j}=0
together imply that
v(S)=0
.

When
j>1
, the existence of such an
\varepsilon
for any
v
contradicts the fact that
x\in\mathcal{P}(\Lambda)
is a vertex. Indeed, we deduce that
x
may be perturbed non-trivially inside
\mathcal{P}(\Lambda)
along any tangent direction in the linear span
\langle f_{1}-f_{2},\ldots,f_{1}-f_{j}\rangle
, which cannot hold for a vertex of
\mathcal{P}(\Lambda)
. Therefore, we have
j=j_{1}=1
.

Similar arguments show that
j_{2}-j_{1}=1
, that
j_{3}-j_{2}=1
, and so on, until
j_{m}=n
(so
m=n
). Therefore, the maximal chain
\mathcal{C}
must be of length
n
, and the proof is complete.
∎

Proof of Theorem
A.2.1
.

By Proposition
A.2.3
, it suffices to show that
\mathcal{P}(\Lambda)\subset\mathcal{X}(\Lambda)
. It suffices in turn to show that any vertex
x\in\mathcal{P}(\Lambda)
lies in
\mathcal{X}(\Lambda)
. Fix such a vertex
x
.

Choose a chain of subsets
S_{j}
as in Lemma
A.2.5
. In particular, we have
x(S_{j})=g_{\Lambda}(S_{j})
for
j=1,\ldots,n
. By considering the equations
x(S_{j})=g_{\Lambda}(S_{j})
in increasing order of
j
, we see that there is a
unique
point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
x(S_{j})=g_{\Lambda}(S_{j})
for all
j
, and that
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
. Therefore, the given vertex
x\in\mathcal{P}(\Lambda)
must equal
\chi(S)
for some maximal admissible set
S
.

In particular, we have
\chi(S)(S)\leq g_{\Lambda}(S)
. By Lemma
A.2.2
, this implies that
n=|S|\leq\rk_{\Lambda}(S)
. Thus,
S\in\mathcal{D}(\Lambda)
, so
x=\chi(S)\in\mathcal{X}(\Lambda)
.
∎
