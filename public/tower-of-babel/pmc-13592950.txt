Prognostic value of the HALP (hemoglobin, albumin, lymphocyte, and platelet) score in emergency versus elective inguinal hernia repair
Authors: Mehmet Zeki Öğüt, Onur Ağ, Nizamettin Kutluer, Bekir Saricik, Tamer Gündoğdu, Ayşe Azak Bozan, Ümit Karatepe, Mehmet Buğra Bozan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The hemoglobin, albumin, lymphocyte, and platelet (HALP) score is a novel prognostic biomarker that reflects a patient’s immune-nutritional status. This study aimed to evaluate the diagnostic and prognostic value of the HALP score in patients undergoing emergency versus elective inguinal hernia repair. This retrospective study analyzed data from 235 patients who underwent inguinal hernia surgery at a single center. The patients were categorized into emergency (n = 97) and elective (n = 138) groups, with the emergency group further divided into intestinal resection (n = 12) and non-resection (n = 85) subgroups. Preoperative HALP scores were significantly lower in the emergency group than in the elective group (P < .001). Subgroup analysis revealed that HALP scores were significantly lower among patients who required intestinal resection compared with those who did not require resection in the emergency group and those who underwent elective surgery (P = .004 and P < .001, respectively). Univariate analysis showed that the HALP score was associated with small bowel resection. Receiver operating characteristic curve analysis demonstrated that a HALP score cutoff value of < 2.31 yielded 75% sensitivity and 90.2% specificity for predicting intestinal resection in incarcerated inguinal hernia. The accessibility and cost-effectiveness of the HALP score support its use as an adjunctive tool for risk stratification in inguinal hernia management, particularly in resource-limited settings.

1. Introduction

Inguinal hernia repair is one of the most frequently performed surgical procedures worldwide.[1] Although most cases are treated electively, most patients present with acute complications, such as incarceration or strangulation, and require urgent surgical intervention. This can increase patient morbidity and mortality; therefore, a prompt treatment plan is necessary.[2,3]

Preoperative identification of inguinal hernia cases that may develop complications remains a significant clinical challenge, and reliable and easily accessible prognostic biomarkers are needed.[4] Additionally, systemic inflammatory responses and nutritional status are increasingly important determinants of surgical outcomes.[5,6]

The hemoglobin, albumin, lymphocyte, and platelet (HALP) score has been identified as a new index that combines these parameters to reflect the immune-nutritional status of a patient.[7] The HALP score was initially used as a prognostic marker for oncological diseases,[8–10] but was later shown to yield clinically significant results in patients with cardiovascular diseases and those admitted for intensive care.[11,12] It is a parameter related to anemia (hemoglobin), malnutrition (albumin), immune competence (lymphocyte count), and inflammatory activity (platelet count). The HALP score combines these 4 parameters into a single, cost-effective composite index.

Although the prognostic value of the HALP score has been established in various disease groups, its use in inguinal hernia cases, particularly in determining surgical approach, has not been adequately investigated. Inflammation, tissue ischemia, and catabolic stress, which are pathophysiological mechanisms of hernia-related complications, may be reflected in the components of the HALP score.[13] Therefore, the HALP score can be considered a clinical predictive tool for elective and emergency inguinal hernia repairs.

This study aimed to demonstrate the diagnostic and prognostic value of the HALP score among patients undergoing inguinal hernia repair. Additionally, this study examined the HALP scores of patients who underwent inguinal hernia repair under emergency or elective conditions, and evaluated possible correlations between this score and basic clinical parameters.

2. Materials and methods

This retrospective, single-center, observational study was conducted at the General Surgery Clinic of Elaziğ Fethi Sekin City Hospital, Turkey. Data from all consecutive patients who underwent emergency inguinal hernia repair between August 2018 and August 2024, as well as from control patients who underwent elective open (conventional) inguinal hernia repair, were analyzed using the hospital’s electronic medical record (EMR) system.

The study was approved by the Elaziğ Fethi Sekin City Hospital Non-Interventional Research Ethics Committee (Approval No 2025/14-36), in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Because this study used de-identified retrospective data, the ethics committee waived the requirement for informed consent.

2.1. Study population and group classification

A retrospective evaluation was conducted on all patients aged ≥ 18 years who underwent inguinal hernia surgery at the Department of General Surgery, Elaziğ Fethi Sekin City Hospital between August 2018 and August 2024. The patients were categorized into 2 groups according to the urgency of surgical intervention.

Group 1 (Emergency cases): This group included patients with clinical or radiological signs of incarceration or strangulation who underwent emergency inguinal hernia repair. The emergency cases were divided into 2 subgroups: intestinal resection and non-resection groups.

Group 2 (Elective cases): This group included patients who had planned elective inguinal hernia repair and did not have an emergency condition such as incarceration or strangulation.

2.3. Data collection

Patient demographic (age and sex), clinical (American Society of Anesthesiologists score, hernia side [right, left, or bilateral], hospitalization period [days], surgical approach [open or laparoscopic]), and laboratory data were obtained from the hospital’s EMR in its electronic database system. Surgical and anesthesia reports were reviewed to verify this information.

Information bias was reduced by obtaining all variables directly from the EMR and by using standardized automated analyzers for laboratory measurements.

2.4. Surgical findings (emergency cases only)

Incarceration or strangulation was present, regardless of whether bowel resection was required.

2.5. Radiological and preoperative evaluations

In cases where emergency inguinal hernia surgery was performed, preoperative ultrasonography and/or computed tomography imaging reports confirmed incarceration or strangulation and assessed bowel viability. Routine imaging was not performed in elective cases that were diagnosed clinically.

All patients who underwent elective surgery underwent standard preoperative evaluation and optimization in an anesthesia clinic before surgery. Thus, the elective group was prepared under planned conditions and represented physiologically stable patients.

2.6. Laboratory parameters

Hematological and biochemical parameters were tracked from blood samples taken during preoperative diagnosis or anesthesia preparation. These parameters included white blood cell counts (WBC), hematocrit, hemoglobin, platelet count, neutrophils, lymphocytes, monocytes, and albumin.

The HALP score for each patient was manually calculated using the following formula:

The HALP score: (Hemoglobin [g/L] × Albumin [g/L] × Lymphocytes [×109/L])/Platelets [×109/L].[14]

2.7. Statistical analysis

The study size was determined by including all consecutive eligible patients during the predefined study period; therefore, no a priori sample size calculation was performed.

Patients with missing preoperative laboratory results, incomplete operative records, or unavailable postoperative documentation were excluded from the analysis. Missing data were not imputed, and only complete cases were included in the final dataset. No additional sensitivity analyses were performed. The primary analyses already included all eligible patients, and excluding cases with missing data did not change the distribution of key variables.

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 26.0 (IBM Corp.). Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk and Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests.

For group comparisons, normally distributed variables were analyzed using the independent-samples t-test, whereas non-normally distributed variables were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical data were evaluated using the chi-square or Fisher exact test, as appropriate. To evaluate the subgroup analysis of the emergency versus elective cases, a one-way analysis of variance and Kruskal–Wallis test were performed to determine the differences in parametric results. Preoperative risk evaluation for perioperative intestinal resection was performed with univariate analyses.

For discriminative analysis, diagnostic performance of the HALP score in identifying emergency cases was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. Area under the curve (AUC), optimal cutoff value, sensitivity, and specificity were calculated. Because lower HALP values are associated with higher clinical risk, ROC analysis was performed using the inverse relationship between HALP and the outcome, with lower HALP values indicating higher risk. The analysis was interpreted accordingly. No mathematical transformation was applied; instead, the inverse clinical relationship between HALP and the outcome was explicitly defined.

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range), as appropriate. Categorical variables are presented as number (n) and percentage (%). A P-value of < .05 was accepted as statistically significant.

3. Results

During the study period, 131 patients were initially screened in the emergency group. After excluding those with missing laboratory results (n = 19), incomplete clinical documentation (n = 8), and patients not meeting the eligibility criteria (n = 7), a total of 97 patients were included. From the cohort of 2345 elective cases, 179 were randomly selected using R software, version 4.3.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing). Following the exclusion of cases with missing laboratory data (n = 24), incomplete records (n = 6), and ineligible patients (n = 11), 138 patients were retained. The final analytical dataset comprised 235 patients.

Of 235 patients who met the inclusion criteria, 97 (41.3%) and 138 (58.7%) underwent emergency and elective surgeries, respectively. Among the emergency cases, intestinal resection was performed in 12 (5.1%) patients, whereas 85 (36.2%) did not require additional surgical intervention. Of all the patients, 25 (10.6%) were female and 210 (89.4%) were male. There were statistically significant differences between the emergency and elective groups in terms of age, sex, rate of mesh application, type of anesthesia administered, anesthesia scores, and the need for intestinal resection (P < .05), while no significant difference was observed regarding hernia side (P = .131; Table 1).

4. Discussion

The most important finding of this study is the performance of the preoperative HALP score in distinguishing between urgent and complicated cases among patients with inguinal hernia, and its potential clinical value as an adjunctive biomarker that may assist clinical assessment and risk stratification. Patients with incarcerated or strangulated inguinal hernias that required urgent surgery had significantly lower HALP scores than those who underwent elective surgery for inguinal hernia. Patients who required bowel resection had lower HALP scores, an observation associated with more complicated clinical presentations. This pattern suggests that the HALP score may reflect a state of low nutritional and immunological reserve, which makes patients more likely to develop acute complications and worse outcomes. This study is one of the few to investigate the prognostic value of the HALP score in patients with inguinal hernia. The HALP score, previously demonstrated as a prognostic biomarker in many cancer types, was evaluated in this study for hernia surgeries, one of the most common procedures performed in general surgery, and its clinical applicability is discussed.

Our study is consistent with the literature supporting the prognostic value of the HALP score in surgical patients, and it provides new and original data, particularly for the management of inguinal hernia surgery. Aydin et al emphasized lower median HALP scores in patients with complicated abdominal hernias.[15] Although that study evaluated all abdominal hernias, our study focused only on patients with inguinal hernias, providing results specific to this patient group. A study demonstrating the relationship between HALP scores and the development of incisional hernias in patients with colorectal cancer demonstrated a relationship between HALP scores and abdominal wall hernias.[16] Our study demonstrates that HALP scores are not only present or absent but also gradually decrease with disease severity. The significant decrease in HALP scores from elective cases to those requiring resection suggests a potential association between decreasing HALP scores and increasing clinical severity.

The HALP score, which can be obtained from routine preoperative laboratory tests and requires no additional cost, is a practical biomarker that assesses patient physiological status before elective and emergency surgical procedures. In developing countries and in healthcare systems with limited resources, the low cost and easy accessibility of the HALP score are of great clinical importance. Benli et al emphasized that these features make HALP a practical adjunctive decision support parameter, especially in emergency clinical settings.[17] In centers with inadequate advanced diagnostic capabilities, the HALP score may contribute to clinical assessment.

A study involving > 13,000 patients with various cancers indicated that the HALP score serves as a robust prognostic indicator of cancer-specific survival.[18] A low HALP score was similarly associated with adverse clinical outcomes in malignancies of various organs, including the gastrointestinal, urological, and respiratory systems.[19–21] This suggests that the HALP score is an indicator of overall physiological status and overall health.

A retrospective study by Xia et al[22] identified a strong association between HALP and long-term survival in patients admitted to intensive care units. They emphasized that a low HALP score is an independent risk indicator. Our study on patients with hernia shows that the HALP score is not just useful for serious medical conditions such as cancer or critical illness; it may also be associated with sudden clinical deterioration and complicated surgical outcomes in benign inguinal hernias.

The NLR, PLR, and Prognostic Nutritional Index (PNI) are all used as biomarkers in patients with inguinal hernias.[23–25] However, the HALP score can provide a more complete picture by showing all 3 parameters at once: immune system activity, nutritional status, and anemia levels.[26] NLR and PLR focus on the ratios of different immune cell populations during systemic inflammation. They do not directly show nutritional status. PNI, which is based on albumin and lymphocyte counts, works by examining nutrition and the immune system. In addition to these 2 criteria found in PNI, the HALP score also includes hemoglobin, which reflects oxygen-carrying capacity and chronic disease burden, and platelets, which reflect inflammatory activity.[27] The structure formed by the combination of these parameters may provide advantages related to the effective use of the HALP score. In a direct comparative study of patients with colorectal cancer, Li et al[28] found that both low PNI and the HALP score were associated with poor survival; however, the HALP score demonstrated superior prognostic performance and a higher hazard ratio in a multivariate analysis.

The HALP score can serve as a laboratory-based measure of physiological reserve and frailty. Frailty refers to an individual’s heightened vulnerability to stressors due to malfunctions in various physiological systems.[29] Although specific frailty indices and sarcopenia measurements are considered the gold standard for assessing this condition, these methods generally require advanced clinical investigations.[30] In contrast, the HALP score is a useful alternative because it is based on routine laboratory tests that are easy to obtain. The strong link between low HALP scores and high American Society of Anesthesiologists classes shows that this biomarker is closely linked to physiological frailty and could be useful in clinical practice.[20] The HALP score is a practical parameter that assesses physiological reserve in elective and emergency inguinal hernia cases. Low HALP scores in older adults or those with other health problems may indicate more fragility, and they are at a higher risk of complications during surgery. This score can help identify high-risk patients without the need for more tests, especially in emergencies when quick decisions need to be made.

Lin et al demonstrated a significant association between low HALP scores and radiologically defined sarcopenia among patients with gastric cancer.[31] The advantage of the HALP score is that data reflecting this condition can be obtained through routine blood tests without further investigations. Therefore, the HALP score can provide an indirect but valuable physiological window into a catabolic state, the presence of anemia, and the level of systemic inflammation.

The HALP score may serve as an adjunctive parameter that may assist clinical assessment and risk stratification when distinguishing between emergency and elective repairs. In particular, in emergency departments, a low HALP score may help identify high-risk patients with inguinal hernia who require closer physiological monitoring and surgical evaluation. Given the lack of advanced radiology or laboratory facilities in all healthcare institutions, the HALP score offers an easily accessible assessment tool that can partially address this gap in low-resource settings. Furthermore, very low HALP values may indicate serious complications such as bowel resection or the need for intensive postoperative care; this may help inform patients and their families about surgical risks before surgery. However, given the limited number of intestinal resection events, findings specifically related to bowel resection should be interpreted cautiously and considered exploratory.

The clinical use of the HALP score has the potential to enhance the cost-effectiveness of healthcare delivery. In many health systems, strategies aimed at preventing complications are among the most effective approaches for reducing overall costs.[32] Emergency surgical interventions, particularly those requiring bowel resection and prolonged intensive care unit stays, result in substantially higher expenses than elective repairs. In this context, using the HALP score to identify high-risk elective patients early can be considered a cost-effective strategy that promotes more efficient resource allocation.[9,33]

Future studies should be conducted with larger, multicenter, and prospective designs to confirm our findings. This will minimize selection bias and increase the generalizability of the results to different patient populations. Evaluating the HALP score in conjunction with existing surgical risk scores and frailty indices may provide a more integrated approach to clinical decision-making. Furthermore, investigating spontaneous changes in the HALP score over time may contribute to a clearer understanding of the dynamic nature and prognostic value of this parameter. Studies examining the predictive value of the HALP score in specific patient subgroups, such as older individuals, patients with multiple comorbidities, and those undergoing laparoscopic surgery, may enhance the clinical utility of this indicator. Comprehensive cost-effective analyses are needed to demonstrate potential of the HALP score in reducing complication rates and healthcare costs in the context of elective and emergency inguinal hernia repairs.

4.1. Limitations

The single-center study design restricts the applicability of the findings to larger populations and diverse healthcare settings. The retrospective design of this study presents intrinsic biases, including absent data and the challenge of controlling for all confounding variables. Furthermore, the lack of essential acute-phase reactants, such as C-reactive protein, in the emergency group limited our ability to directly compare the systemic inflammatory responses between the cohorts. Laboratory data in emergency situations may have been gathered under fluctuating physiological conditions, potentially affecting the accuracy of the HALP score. Additionally, the absence of an external validation cohort, particularly for regression-based findings related to bowel resection, limits the generalizability of these results. Although the exclusion criteria were strictly followed, unnoticed nutritional deficiencies, low-grade chronic inflammation, or unrecorded comorbidities may have affected the HALP score values and clinical outcomes.

Due to the limited number of intestinal resection events (n = 12), the findings should be interpreted with extreme caution.

5. Conclusion

This study suggests the potential utility of the HALP score as a prognostic indicator in inguinal hernia repair. Lower HALP scores were observed in patients requiring emergency surgery than in those undergoing elective procedures, suggesting an association with disease acuity. The HALP score demonstrated acceptable discriminative ability for identifying patients at increased risk of intestinal resection in emergency inguinal hernia and may contribute to clinical risk stratification. The HALP score’s accessibility and cost-effectiveness may be useful in resource-limited environments. The observed association between the HALP score and longer hospital stays, as well as bowel resection in emergency cases, supports its potential role in adjunctive risk stratification, particularly in emergency settings. The HALP score may serve as an adjunctive biomarker for risk stratification in patients with inguinal hernia; however, it should not be interpreted as a standalone diagnostic or clinical decision-making tool. Integrating the HALP score into preoperative assessments may contribute to risk assessment for patients with inguinal hernia by providing a more accurate classification of surgical risks.
