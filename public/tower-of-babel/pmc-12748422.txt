Cuproptosis-related gene DLD expression correlates with the prognosis and tumor immune microenvironment in clear cell renal cell carcinoma
Authors: Cheng Qian, Hu He, Ganhua You
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Renal cell carcinoma (KIRC), particularly clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccKIRC), is a leading cause of midlife mortality in urological cancers, with incidence steadily increasing over recent decades [1]. KIRC is highly heterogeneous, with ccKIRC (KIRC) comprising 75%ཞ80% of cases [2]. A critical factor in ccKIRC pathogenesis is the inactivation of Von Hippel-Lindau (VHL), which disrupts key molecular pathways [3]. VHL, a tumor suppressor, regulates hypoxia-inducible factors (HIFs) by mediating their degradation, thereby maintaining cellular balance [4, 5]. In VHL-deficient KIRC, HIF-1αand HIF-2αaccumulate, translocating to the nucleus to promote tumor growth, metastasis, and angiogenesis [6, 7]. This dysregulation has led to the use of tyrosine kinase inhibitors, such as sunitinib and pazopanib, in KIRC therapy [8]. However, resistance to these treatments, driven by KIRC’s ability to evade apoptosis or autophagy, remains a major challenge [9]. Despite targeted therapies being standard, nearly all patients eventually experience disease progression. Recently, ferroptosis, a novel form of cell death, has gained attention as a potential therapeutic approach for KIRC [10]. The role of copper toxicity in KIRC, and whether its resistance is DLD-dependent or independent, remains unclear.

Copper toxicity, a recently identified mechanism of cell death, garnered attention after its 2022 publication in Science. The study demonstrated that excess copper binds to lipoylated tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle proteins, causing aberrant oligomerization and destabilizing iron-sulfur (Fe-S) cluster proteins. This disruption induces proteotoxic stress and triggers cell death [11]. Additionally, copper ions regulate PD-L1 expression, contributing to immune evasion in cancer [12].

Copper toxicity represents a potential therapeutic avenue for malignant tumors. While cancer cells are traditionally thought to prefer aerobic glycolysis (Warburg effect) over the TCA cycle to rapidly generate energy for tumor progression [13], recent evidence underscores the TCA cycle’s significance in cancer prognosis. For example, phosphorylation of PDHA activates the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex, enhancing the TCA cycle and enabling cancer cells to adapt to metastatic environments, thereby promoting metastasis [14]. Furthermore, elevated copper levels may influence the tumor microenvironment, with copper toxicity-related gene models showing reduced immune cell infiltration in high-risk patients [15]. These findings require further validation through experimental studies.

DLD (dihydrolipoic amide dehydrogenase) is a key component of the glycine cleavage system and an E3 subunit of the α-ketoacid dehydrogenase complexes [16]. Primarily mitochondrial, DLD is also found in the nucleus [17]. Mutations in DLD are linked to various diseases, including DLD deficiency, an autosomal recessive metabolic disorder [18]. This condition is characterized by lactic acidosis and neurological decline due to the central nervous system’s vulnerability to oxidative metabolism defects [19]. Additionally, DLD has been implicated in copper toxicity, regulating specific metabolic pathways [11]. However, its impact on KIRC biology and the tumor microenvironment remains unclear.

To address this knowledge gap, we analyzed DLD expression in KIRC using multiple databases. Our results showed DLD downregulated in KIRC, confirmed through both public and clinical data. We identified DLD as an independent prognostic marker for KIRC, correlating with clinicopathological features. Functional enrichment and GSEA analyses highlighted DLD’s involvement in immune and tumor-related pathways. Immune infiltration analysis further linked DLD to various immune cells and checkpoints. In conclusion, our study suggests DLD as a potential diagnostic and prognostic marker for KIRC.

Materials and methods

Patient datasets

We retrieved RNA-seq data in TPM format from UCSC XENA (https://xenabrowser.net/datapages/), processed by the Toil pipeline, including TCGA pan-cancer and corresponding normal tissue data from GTEx. Additionally, we downloaded GSE53757 (72 KIRC patient samples and 72 paired-adjacent non-tumor samples) and GSE40435 (101 KIRC patient samples and 101 paired-adjacent non-tumor samples) from the GEO database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). The data of the Society for Clinical Proteomics Tumor Analysis (CPTAC) were obtained from the UALCAN database, including 84 normal kidney tissues and 110 renal cancer tissues. Data analysis was conducted using R (version 4.2.1) with the following packages: ggplot2 (3.3.6), stats (4.2.1), and car (3.1-0).

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining

The Human Protein Atlas (HPA) database (http://www.proteinatlas.org/) provides comprehensive proteomic and transcriptomic data from various human samples, including cellular, tissue, and pathological atlases. It includes cell-specific localization information for 44 normal tissues, 20 cancer types, and immunohistochemistry data for proteins in both normal and tumor tissues.

Survival analysis

Data Acquisition: RNA-seq data for TCGA-KIRC (Kidney Clear Cell Carcinoma) were retrieved from the TCGA database (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov) in TPM format, alongside clinical data. Data Filtering: Samples were filtered to exclude normal tissues, those without clinical information, and duplicates. Data Processing: A log2 transformation (value + 1) was applied. Prognostic models, including Overall Survival, Disease-Specific Survival, and Progression-Free Interval, were analyzed using the survminer (v0.4.9) and ggplot2 (v3.3.6) packages [20].

Cox regression analysis

Data Acquisition: Risk factors associated with KIRC prognosis were identified using univariate and multivariate regression analyses, with forest plots for visualization.

Establishment and evaluation of ROC curves and receiver operating characteristic plots

This study identified independent clinicopathological prognostic factors from Cox regression analyses to develop a nomogram for predicting overall survival (OS) at 1, 3, and 5 years in KIRC patients. Nomogram accuracy was validated through calibration curves, with model performance confirmed by alignment with the reference line. Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves assessed the prognostic value of DLD expression, with an AUC > 0.6 indicating predictive significance.

Creation and assessment of line charts

Protein–protein interaction (PPI) data were retrieved from the STRING database (https://cn.string-db.org/), which integrates comprehensive interaction information. Interactions with a confidence score > 0.7 were deemed significant.

Functional enrichment analysis

To investigate the biological processes and pathways related to DLD, we analyzed 15 genes positively and 15 genes negatively correlated with DLD in KIRC based on Spearman correlation coefficients. These 30 genes were visualized in a co-expression heatmap using ggplot. Gene Ontology (GO) analysis (covering biological process, cellular component, and molecular function) and KEGG pathway analysis were performed using DAVID, with results visualized through ggplot2. A corrected P value < 0.05 was considered significant.

Immune landscape analysis

I have added the description of TIMER in the method. The specific content is: In order to explore the infiltration pattern of immune cells in tumors and its relationship with gene expression, we utilized the Tumor immune Estimation Resource (TIMER) database(https://compbio.cn/timer2/) [21]. TIMER is a web-based platform developed by the Cistrome team, dedicated to analyzing the tumor immune microenvironment through high-throughput sequencing data, especially RNA-Seq data. Correlations between DLD and 24 immune cell types were calculated using the ssGSEA algorithm in the GSVA R package (1.46.0) [22], with immune markers from the Immunity article [23]. Differences in pDC scores between groups were evaluated using analysis of variance.

TISIDB database analysis

TISIDB is a comprehensive portal for exploring tumor-immune system interactions. To assess the immune relevance of DLD in cancer, we analyzed the correlation between DLD expression and immune checkpoint gene levels using the ‘Chemokine’ and ‘Immunomodulator’ modules. Additionally, chemokine and chemokine receptor expression in tumor-infiltrating immune cells (TIICs) was evaluated through the ‘Chemokine’ module.

qPCR

The following cell lines were employed in this study: (1) HK-2 cells (Product No.: CL-0109), human renal cortical proximal tubule epithelial cells, exhibiting epithelial-like morphology and adherent growth; (2) 786-O cells (Product No.: CL-0010), human renal clear cell adenocarcinoma cells, widely used in renal cell carcinoma (RCC) research to investigate tumor biology and therapeutic responses, also displaying epithelial-like morphology and adherent growth; (3) OS-RC-2 cells (Product No.: CL-0177) are renal cell carcinoma tumors exhibiting epithelial morphology and demonstrate tumorigenicity in nude mice. This cell line is commonly used to study the behavioral characteristics, drug responses, and potential therapeutic targets of renal cell carcinoma.The above cell lines were purchased from Wuhan Punos Biotechnology Co., Ltd. and validated by STR analysis.

Total RNA was extracted using the TRIzol® Plus RNA Purification Kit (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). First-strand cDNA synthesis was performed with SuperScript™ Ⅲ first-strand synthesis SuperMix qPCR (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Real-time quantitative PCR was conducted using the Power SYBR® Green PCR premix kit (Applied Biosystems, USA). The following primers were used: HumanGAPDH-Forward:5′-GGAGCGAGATCCCTCCAAAAT-3′;

HumanGAPDH-Reverse:5′-GGCTGTTGTCATACTTCTCATGG-3′;

HumanDLD-Forward:5′-CACCTTCGACCTCAACATCC-3′;

HumanDLD-Reverse: 5′-TGCTGTTGAGGGTCTTGATG-3′.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted in R (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). The Wilcoxon rank-sum test compared two independent samples, and univariate logistic regression assessed the relationship between DLD and other variables. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis was performed using the survival and survminer packages, while Spearman’s correlation evaluated associations between DLD, immune cells, and immune checkpoints. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Constructing protein interaction networks

This study explores how DLD proteins regulate metabolic reprogramming in KIRC through key nodes in the PPI network, constructed via STRING, which includes 10 high-scoring proteins such as AMT, BCKDHA, DLST, PDHA1, DBT, OGDH, DLAT, BCKDHB, PDHB, and PDHX. These proteins are central to mitochondrial energy metabolism, particularly the TCA cycle and branched-chain amino acid breakdown. Key hub nodes include: 1. DLAT, which partners with DLD to regulate pyruvate entry into the TCA cycle, influencing glucose metabolism; 2. OGDH, which collaborates with DLD to oxidize α-ketoglutaric acid and modulate DLD activity through metabolite accumulation; 3. The phosphorylation of PDHA1/PDHB, which correlates with DLD interaction strength, determines glucose metabolism pathways (aerobic respiration vs. glycolysis). Additionally, BCKDHA/BCKDHB suggests that DLD influences tumor proliferation via branched-chain amino acid metabolism, while PDHX stabilizes the complex. These hub nodes form a DLD-mediated metabolic regulatory network, broadening DLD’s role from enzyme activity to system-level functions: 1. The DLAT-PDHA1/PDHB axis regulates the Warburg effect; 2. The OGDH-DLST axis may mediate epigenetic modifications via metabolite accumulation. This study deepens our understanding of DLD’s involvement in KIRC progression, providing a theoretical basis for targeting metabolic networks in anti-tumor strategies [24–27] (Fig 8).

Discussion

A recent study revealed a new form of intracellular copper-induced cell death, termed ‘copper toxicity,’ which disrupts the TCA cycle by binding copper to lipoylated components, particularly the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex [30, 31]. DLD, involved in energy metabolism and the cell cycle [32–34], has been minimally explored in relation to tumours [35–38]. As a key regulator of copper toxicity, DLD may be a promising target for novel therapies. Its role in renal clear cell carcinoma remains understudied, prompting this analysis.

Dihydrolipoamide dehydrogenase (DLD) is a key component of the glycine cleavage system and an E3 subunit of the α-ketoacid dehydrogenase complexes [16, 39]. It is predominantly found in mitochondria, with a lesser presence in the nucleus [17]. Variants of DLD are associated with various diseases, and its deficiency, an autosomal recessive disorder, causes lactic acidosis and neurological deterioration due to disrupted oxidative metabolism [40, 41]. DLD has also been identified as a regulator of metabolic pathways in copper toxicity [42]. In this study, we assessed DLD expression and its prognostic value in cancer, revealing downregulation of DLD in KIRC at both the mRNA and immunohistochemical levels. Lower DLD expression correlated with advanced T-stages, M-stages, tumour stages (p < 0.05), higher grade classifications (p < 0.05), and poorer survival outcomes. In KIRC, reduced DLD expression was linked to worse overall survival (OS), disease-specific survival (DSS), and progression-free interval (PFI). These results suggest that DLD serves as a protective gene and a promising prognostic biomarker in KIRC.

The malignant transformation of KIRC leads to a metabolic phenotype that surpasses the traditional Warburg effect, evolving into a disease-specific state with extensive metabolic reprogramming. Studies [24, 25] show that KIRC cells exhibit altered glycolytic flux, including enhanced pentose phosphate pathway shunting [26], mitochondrial dysfunction (reduced oxidative phosphorylation efficiency [27]), and abnormal lipid metabolism (e.g., excessive fatty acid synthesis) [43], which together form a metabolic network suited to the tumor microenvironment. This reprogramming not only supports cell proliferation by providing nucleotide synthesis materials but also reduces ROS production, lowering apoptosis risk. Furthermore, mitochondrial energy depletion drives KIRC cells to rely on glycolysis, resulting in a “metabolic addiction” that contrasts with normal renal cells’ oxidative phosphorylation needs, highlighting metabolic pathways as potential therapeutic targets. Dihydrothiocarcinamide dehydrogenase (DLD), a key mitochondrial enzyme, regulates metabolic gene networks in KIRC, with abnormal expression contributing to metabolic reprogramming (Table 1). DLD affects metabolism through two main pathways: (1) Direct regulation of energy metabolism, where its role in converting pyruvate to acetyl-CoA is critical; decreased DLD expression impairs pyruvate entry into the TCA cycle, forcing reliance on glycolysis and enhancing the “glycolytic dominance” associated with mitochondrial dysfunction [26]; and (2) Acting as a hub in the transcriptional network, DLD may regulate fatty acid oxidation and amino acid metabolism by interacting with nuclear receptors (e.g., PPARγ) and transcription factors (e.g., HIF-1α). For example, DLD deletion can activate HIF-1α, promoting GLUT1 expression and creating a feedback loop that strengthens metabolic reprogramming [44]. Therefore, DLD serves as a “node molecule” in the metabolic network, both a product and a driver of metabolic reprogramming. KIRC stem cells, which express the copper transporter CTR2 [45], display metabolic characteristics that influence self-renewal and invasiveness. DLD regulates these properties through: (1) Metabolic phenotypic heterogeneity, where low DLD expression helps stem cells maintain low oxidative stress by promoting glycolysis and inhibiting oxidative phosphorylation, allowing survival in hypoxic conditions and resistance to mitochondrial-targeted therapies; and (2) Cross-talk between copper and energy metabolism, where CTR2-mediated copper uptake enhances mitochondrial complex IV activity. DLD disruption may uncouple copper metabolism from oxidative phosphorylation, leading to copper ion accumulation, ROS generation, and MAPK activation, contributing to drug resistance [45]. This “contradictory” regulation may drive therapeutic challenges. Given DLD’s central role in KIRC metabolism, it presents a promising target for intervention: (1) Single-drug targeting: Down-regulating DLD with small molecule inhibitors or siRNA could impair mitochondrial function, increase glycolytic dependence, and enhance sensitivity to glucose deprivation or glycolytic inhibitors like 2-DG [46]; (2) Combination therapy: Combining DLD inhibitors with VHL-targeted drugs (e.g., proteasome inhibitors) may reverse metabolic reprogramming by restoring HIF-1α degradation and reshaping metabolic gene expression [47]; and (3) Precision medicine: Considering DLD expression variations and KIRC subtypes (e.g., clear cell vs. papillary carcinoma) and baseline mitochondrial function will help predict treatment responses [48].

This study is the first to demonstrate a correlation between immune infiltration and DLD expression in KIRC tumors. The immune microenvironment plays a crucial role in tumor development by modulating molecular interactions that affect clinical outcomes [49]. Analysis using the TIMER database identified DLD as a key regulator of immune infiltration and the tumor microenvironment. We found a positive correlation between DLD expression and multiple immune cells, including eosinophils, neutrophils, Tcm, macrophages, iDC, mast cells, T helper cells, Tgd, pDC, cytotoxic cells, CD8 T cells, NK CD56bright cells, TReg, NK CD56dim cells, NK cells, Tem, aDC, T cells, and Th1 cells. Notably, DLD also correlated strongly with Tim-3, a marker of Treg cell exhaustion, which mediates the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment. In KIRC, Treg cell infiltration is associated with advanced pathological stages and poorer prognosis [50, 51]. The negative correlation between DLD and Treg cells suggests that higher DLD expression may enhance immune responses in KIRC.

KIRC features a highly immune-infiltrated tumor microenvironment (TME), enriched with CD8 + T cells and macrophages [52–54]. This is driven by KIRC-specific antigens, such as VHL mutation-related ones, and immune network modulation via metabolic reprogramming. The immune status of the TME directly impacts treatment efficacy: increased CD8 + T cell infiltration is associated with better response to PD-1 inhibitors, while a higher presence of myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) predicts poor prognosis [55, 56]. Key metabolic pathways in KIRC regulate both angiogenesis and immunity via: (1) Glycolytic-Angiogenesis Axis: Enhanced glycolysis leads to lactic acid buildup, activating the HIF-1α/VEGF pathway, promoting angiogenesis, and hindering immune cell infiltration while recruiting regulatory T cells (Tregs) through hypoxia [57, 58]; (2) Lipid Metabolism-Inflammatory Network: Overactive fatty acid synthesis produces unsaturated fatty acids, activating TLR4/NF-κB, which triggers pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g.IL-6, TNF-α), stimulates tumor-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), and impairs CD8 + T cell function [59, 60]; (3) Mitochondrial-Immune Checkpoint Linkage: Mitochondrial damage disrupts oxidative phosphorylation, releasing mtDNA, which activates the cGAS-STING pathway and potentially upregulates PD-L1, forming a “metabolic-immune checkpoint” network [61]. DLD is central to KIRC’s metabolism-immune interactions, with its absence leading to: (1) Metabolite-Mediated Immune Regulation: Pyruvate accumulation and lactic acid production lower TME pH, inhibiting CD8 + T cells. Fatty acid oxidation defects cause free fatty acid buildup, inducing macrophages to polarize to the immunosuppressive M2 phenotype [26, 43]; (2) Regulation of Immune Cell Recruitment and Function: DLD interacts with HIF-1α to promote chemokine expression in MDSCs, while its absence weakens dendritic cell antigen presentation via PPARγ activation [44]; (3) Mediating Intercellular Metabolic Crosstalk: Low DLD expression in KIRC stem cells supports Treg immunosuppression via lactic acid “reverse metabolism” and cooperates with CTR2 to generate ROS, activating the NLRP3 inflammasome and exacerbating inflammation [45, 62]. Given DLD’s crucial role, therapeutic strategies include: (1) Metabolism-Immune Dual Targeting: DLD inhibitors (e.g., dichloroacetic acid) combined with PD-1 antibodies can restore mitochondrial function, reduce lactic acid, and reverse T-cell exhaustion [46]; (2) Epigenetic-Metabolic Intervention: HDAC inhibitors (e.g., chidamide) increase DLD expression, remodeling metabolism, and, when paired with anti-angiogenic drugs (e.g., axitinib), improving immune infiltration [48]; (3) Precise Immunometabolic Typing: KIRC can be classified based on DLD expression and immune profile to guide personalized treatment, such as targeting “metabolic-immune active types” with DLD-focused therapies [61].

Chemokines and their receptors play a key role in immune cell migration [63–66]. Analysis using the TISIDB database revealed a negative correlation between DLD expression and CCL14, CXCL2, CCR10, and CXCR4 in KIRC, suggesting that high DLD expression may inhibit immune cell migration to the tumor microenvironment (TME). The active form of CCL14 binds to CCR1, CCR3, and CCR5, promoting immune cell chemotaxis [67, 68]. Additionally, CCR4 enhances macrophage phagocytosis [68] and regulates M1/M2 differentiation [69], while CX3CR1 controls leukocyte survival and NK cell activation [70]. These findings indicate that DLD modulates immune infiltration in KIRC.

A central focus of TME research is immune checkpoint inhibition, which enhances immune responses through inhibitors like programmed death ligand 1 (PD-L1) and programmed death receptor 1 (PD-1) [71]. Tumor cells express PD-L1, which binds PD-1, suppressing the immune response in PD-1-expressing cells. In renal clear cell carcinoma (KIRC), DLD expression negatively correlates with immunosuppressants ADORA2A and TGFB1, and immunoagonists TNFRSF4 and TNFRSF25. Checkpoint inhibitors, clinically validated in cancers such as colon and bladder cancer [71–73], effectively shrink tumors, induce durable responses, and have low toxicity, positioning them as a promising future cancer treatment strategy.

This study has several limitations. Firstly, it is based on data from an online database, necessitating clinical validation. Secondly, the bioinformatics analysis of DLD expression lacks experimental verification, warranting in vitro and in vivo investigation of its mechanisms. Finally, further research is needed to explore the biological effects of DLD on KIRC cells.

Conclusion

In summary, DLD is downregulated in advanced KIRC and may impact progression through key molecular pathways, with reduced expression associated with poor prognosis. Moreover, DLD expression correlates with immune cell infiltration, particularly pDCs, neutrophils, Tgd, mast cells, Tregs, ADCs, and cytotoxic cells. Future in vitro and in vivo studies are needed to further elucidate the role of DLD in KIRC.
