Real-World Outcomes After Switching to Faricimab in Patients with Chronic Diabetic Macular Edema at a Tertiary Eye Center in Singapore

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Diabetic macular edema (DME) is a leading cause of vision loss in patients with diabetes [1, 2], and intravitreal anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) therapy is now widely accepted as the first-line or “gold standard” treatment [3–5].

Faricimab is a bispecific antibody targeting both VEGF‑A and Ang‑2. In the international phase III YOSEMITE and RHINE trials, faricimab demonstrated non-inferiority to aflibercept in best‑corrected visual acuity at 1 year, enabled extension of dosing intervals to up to 16 weeks, achieved greater reduction in central macular thickness, and showed a comparable safety profile [6]. A subgroup analysis in Asian patients [7] showed similar results in vision, anatomy, and durability, supporting faricimab’s applicability in Asian populations.

However, real‑world evidence of faricimab in DME remains limited. Large multicenter studies such as FARETINA (USA) and FARWIDE (UK) have recently reported short-term outcomes [8, 9], but data from Asia remain scarce and limited to small single-center studies, with published reports to date primarily from India [10] and Japan [11–15]. Although Singapore has reported real-world outcomes in age-related macular degeneration (AMD) [16, 17], real‑world data on DME from Singapore have not yet been published. As treatment outcomes may differ across populations owing to ethnic and geographic socioeconomic disparities [4], validation with larger and more region-specific datasets is warranted to better assess its clinical applicability.

This study aims to retrospectively assess short-term visual and anatomical outcomes of switching to faricimab in Singaporean patients with DME to evaluate its effectiveness in real-world clinical practice.

Methods

Study Design

This retrospective observational study was conducted at a tertiary eye hospital in Singapore. Patient data were anonymized and extracted from electronic medical records. Approval from SingHealth Centralised Institutional Review Board was obtained (IRB no. 2017-2562), and informed consent was waived owing to the retrospective nature. The study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki.

Subjects

We included patients with diabetic macular edema (DME) managed at the Singapore National Eye Centre between 2022 and 2024 who switched to faricimab for suboptimal anatomical or visual response to prior therapy and, in a subset, for durability considerations, at the clinician’s discretion [18]. Patients received three or four consecutive intravitreal faricimab injections as a loading phase. The number of loading injections (three or four) and the injection interval were determined by the treating clinician. Primary analyses assessed pre to post changes in the overall cohort, followed by an exploratory subgroup comparison between the three-injection and four-injection groups. This study focused on the loading phase to compare short-term switch efficacy: standard care permitted three to four loading injections, whereas the YOSEMITE/RHINE trials used a fixed four-dose loading regimen. Eligible eyes were required to have available visual acuity measurements and optical coherence tomography (OCT) data at the time of switching and at the visit after the loading phase. Exclusion criteria included extended intervals or follow-up > 4 weeks beyond physician-specified schedule, missing post-injection visual acuity, or OCT images obscured by vitreous hemorrhage or significant lens opacity.

Study Measures

Visual acuity and retinal thickness were assessed at the time of the first faricimab injection (baseline) and at the first follow-up visit after completion of three or four consecutive loading injections. The interval between injections was determined by the treating physician on the basis of clinical judgment. Best available visual acuity (BAVA) was measured using a logMAR visual acuity system and converted to ETDRS letter scores for analysis. OCT images were obtained using the Spectralis (Heidelberg Engineering, Heidelberg, Germany). Central subfield thickness (CST) was automatically calculated using the device’s built-in segmentation software. All images were manually reviewed by the investigators, and segmentation errors were corrected when necessary.

Statistical Analysis

Paired t-tests were used to evaluate BAVA and CST changes before and after switching. Differences in injection intervals of prior therapy and faricimab interval were also assessed. Mann–Whitney U tests compared BAVA and CST changes, as well as non-normally distributed variables such as treatment interval and treatment duration, between the three- and four-injection groups, as normality was not assumed, and chi-squared tests assessed differences in ethnicity and prior treatment distribution. A subgroup analysis was performed according to the duration of prior anti-VEGF treatment before switching to faricimab. Eyes were classified into the short-duration group if the prior treatment duration was less than 1 year and into the long-duration group if the prior treatment duration was 1 year or longer. This 1-year cutoff was selected on the basis of a previous report suggesting that visual improvement and injection frequency may decline beyond 1 year of anti-VEGF treatment [19]. Analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was used to examine differences in final visual acuity (VA) between the two groups stratified by the treatment interval prior to switching, adjusting for baseline BAVA. Analyses were performed using R version 4.5.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) via RStudio version 2025.5.1.513 (RStudio, PBC, Boston, MA, USA).

Results

Subjects

A total of 157 eyes from 131 patients were included in the analysis. The mean age was 63.8 ± 9.2 years. Among the patients, 81 (51.6%) were male and 76 (48.4%) were female. Of the included eyes, 101 (from 85 patients) received three consecutive faricimab injections, while 56 eyes (from 47 patients) received four. There were no significant differences in baseline vision, CST, duration of prior treatment, age, sex, or ethnicity between the two groups. The mean interval between consecutive faricimab injections was 5.4 ± 1.7 weeks, and the follow-up period was 6.9 ± 4.3 weeks.

Prior to switching, the most common agent was ranibizumab (n = 130, 82.8%), followed by aflibercept (n = 18, 11.5%). Prior treatment duration had a median of 1.8 years (interquartile range [IQR]: 3.6 years). The final interval of previous treatment before switching had a median of 4.1 weeks (IQR: 2.4 weeks) and it was slightly longer in the three-injection group (median: 4.9 weeks, IQR: 3.1 weeks) compared with the four-injection group (median: 4.1 weeks, IQR: 2 weeks, p = 0.043) (Table 1). Visual Acuity Changes

Mean BAVA improved from 57.8 ± 16.5 letters at switch to 62.4 ± 15.0 letters after consecutive injections (mean gain 4.5 letters; p < 0.001). In the three‑injection subgroup, BAVA increased from 59.0 ± 15.6 to 63.2 ± 13.6, and in the four‑injection subgroup from 55.7 ± 17.8 to 60.8 ± 17.3 (both p < 0.001, Fig. 1, Table 2). There was no significant difference in visual gain between groups (p = 0.39). Further analysis revealed that eyes in the long-duration group (≥ 1 year prior to switching) achieved better final VA compared with those in the short-duration group (< 1 year) (64.3 ± 12.7 versus 57.6 ± 19.0 letters, p < 0.05). In addition, treatment intervals of previous therapy were significantly longer in the long-duration group than in the short-duration group (p = 0.001, Supplementary material). Separately, eyes with a treatment interval longer than 6 weeks prior to switching achieved a better final VA compared with those with shorter intervals. After adjustment for baseline VA, the final VA differed between groups (ANCOVA, p = 0.017). Adjusted means were 61.4 ± 1.0 letters in the short-interval group and 63.9 ± 1.3 letters in the long-interval group (Supplementary material).Fig. 1Changes in visual acuity following three or four consecutive faricimab injections. CST Changes

The mean central subfield thickness (CST) significantly improved from 431.4 ± 129.4 μm at the time of switching to faricimab to 334.2 ± 90.0 μm after consecutive injections, reflecting a mean reduction of 97.3 μm (p < 0.001). In the three-injection group, mean CST decreased from 424.8 ± 127.7 μm to 332.0 ± 97.8 μm, and in the four-injection group, from 443.3 ± 132.9 μm to 338.0 ± 74.7 μm (both p < 0.001, Fig. 2, Table 2).Fig. 2Changes in CST following three or four consecutive faricimab injections. Safety

Among the 157 eyes included in this study, one case of intraocular inflammation was observed after the first faricimab injection. This case was diagnosed as mild acute anterior uveitis, with no evidence of posterior uveitis or vasculitis. The inflammation resolved with topical corticosteroid treatment, and faricimab treatment was subsequently resumed without further complications. No cases of endophthalmitis were observed.

Discussion

In this study, patients with DME who had received long-term treatment but exhibited a suboptimal response were switched to faricimab and underwent consecutive injections. Our findings demonstrated significant short-term improvements in both visual acuity and CST, despite patients having shown a poor response on the basis of the clinician’s judgment to prior therapies at baseline. These results highlight the potential of faricimab as a treatment option even in eyes with longstanding disease and suboptimal response to prior therapies.

Prior single-center, real-world studies reporting on switch cases in patients with DME exist but have been limited by small sample sizes. Summarizing single-center reports, studies assessing short-term (< 6 months) outcomes [10, 11, 13, 14, 20–26] had a median sample size of 25 eyes (range, 7–69), whereas those evaluating ≥ 6-month outcomes [12, 15, 27–30] had a median of 29.5 eyes (range, 7–100), indicating that most analyses were based on small cohorts (Table 3). Many prior studies have shown OCT morphologic improvements without statistically significant visual gains [12, 13, 15, 20, 22, 24–26, 28, 29], suggesting a possible ceiling effect in treatment-experienced eyes. By contrast, several small case series have reported improvements in both vision and CST [10, 14, 21]. Nonetheless, their limited sample sizes precluded definitive conclusions. Notably, our study analyzed 157 eyes, making it the largest published single-center switch cohort to date to our best knowledge. The larger sample size may help overcome statistical variability and support the robustness of our findings. Summary of real-world faricimab switch cases in single-center studies

For studies with both short-term and long-term follow-up, only short-term results (e.g., early post-loading phase) are included here

PRN: pro re nata; N/A: not available; IVA: intravitreal afl ibercept; IVF: intravitreal faricimab; TAE: treat-and-extend

According to a recent meta-analysis on eyes switched to faricimab for DME, visual acuity (VA) improved by −0.05 logMAR (approximately + 2.5 ETDRS letters), and central subfield thickness (CST) decreased by about 103.5 µm within 3–12 months after switching [31]. In comparison, we observed a mean gain of 4.5 ETDRS letters and a reduction of 97.3 µm in CST after three or four injections, over a mean follow-up of 4.88 ± 1.75 months. These findings suggest that the VA improvements in our cohort may have been slightly greater than those previously reported.

When considering potential factors associated with outcomes after switching to faricimab, several possibilities should be taken into account, including whether a loading phase was administered, the treatment interval prior to switching, and overall treatment duration. In our study, injections were administered at intervals similar to a loading regimen. This suggests that employing a loading phase may confer additional visual benefits. With respect to the treatment interval prior to switching, the long-interval group achieved a modest but statistically significant better final VA, implying that preswitch treatment intervals could potentially influence prognosis, although larger studies are required to confirm this. In our subgroup analysis, even among eyes with chronic DME, with a median previous treatment duration of 21.6 months, eyes with a longer overall treatment duration also tended to achieve better outcomes compared with those with a shorter duration. A notable point is that these eyes were characterized by longer preswitch treatment intervals. In real-world settings, the number of anti-VEGF injections tends to be lower than that in clinical trials owing to factors such as economic burden and treatment fatigue [32, 33]. Consequently, our study population is likely distinct from those included in DRCR.net Protocol I [34] or T [35]. Real-world studies of anti-VEGF therapy have also shown that, beyond 1 year of treatment, both injection frequency and visual improvement tend to decline [19], suggesting that the long-duration group in our study may have represented such undertreated cases. This finding raises the possibility that patients who had been relatively undertreated over a longer period may have achieved greater improvements once switched to faricimab and initiated on an appropriate loading regimen. The median interval of previous treatments was slightly longer in the four-injection group than in the three-injection group, which may appear counterintuitive. However, in this real-world setting, the choice of a four-injection loading regimen may have reflected the treating physician’s concern regarding insufficient disease control under the previous treatment schedule, including eyes that had been treated at relatively longer intervals before switching. Interestingly, the short-term outcomes from FARETINA multicenter database study reported only a 0.7-letter gain at the fourth injection. Notably, approximately 60% of patients in that study did not receive a full loading phase, which may account for the modest visual gains observed and underscores the importance of an adequate initial loading strategy when switching to faricimab. Moreover, recently presented long-term results from the FARETINA study [36] demonstrated that, over 2 years of follow-up, patients in the switch group achieved a significant extension of treatment intervals while maintaining sustained CST improvements. The RHONE-X study, an open-label extension of the YOSEMITE and RHINE trials, recently reported that among patients switched from aflibercept 2 mg and who had four loading doses of faricimab, the proportion of eyes achieving a CST < 325 μm increased from 84% during aflibercept treatment to 95% after the switch to faricimab [37]. Taken together, these findings suggest that implementing a loading phase when switching to faricimab may be important to achieve meaningful visual improvements in the short term, while in the longer term, a more sustainable extended treatment interval and favorable anatomical outcome may ultimately be achieved.

The comparable short-term outcomes between the three- and four-injection loading groups may provide a practical real-world supplement to the four-injection loading regimen used in pivotal clinical trials. Although four initial monthly injections remain the evidence-based protocol, treatment regimens in clinical practice are often individualized according to disease activity, prior treatment response, treatment burden, and logistical factors. Our findings suggest that, in selected eyes switched from prior anti-VEGF therapy to faricimab, three loading injections may be sufficient to achieve short-term anatomical and functional improvements comparable to those observed after four loading injections. One possible explanation is that previously treated eyes may not require the same loading intensity as treatment-naïve eyes, particularly if residual disease activity is not severe or if some anti-VEGF responsiveness has been preserved. A reduced loading approach may therefore help decrease treatment burden in selected patients. However, because the number of loading injections was not randomly assigned and follow-up was relatively short, these results should be interpreted cautiously.

The observed improvements after switching to faricimab may reflect its ability to simultaneously inhibit VEGF-A and Ang-2, a dual mechanism unique to this bispecific antibody [38]. Ang-2 has been shown to disrupt endothelial junctions, promote pericyte detachment, increase vascular permeability, and induce inflammation by deactivating Tie2 signaling in diabetic retinopathy [39]. By simultaneously targeting VEGF-A and Ang-2, faricimab may offer more comprehensive vascular stabilization and disease modulation, extending potential benefits to treatment-resistant populations. These mechanistic differences could explain the observed anatomical and functional improvements in our cohort.

While our results are encouraging, definitive evaluation of real-world faricimab treatment regimens remains ongoing. Large-scale studies such as the SWAN trial (Japan) [40] are expected to clarify optimal dosing strategies and identify patient subgroups most likely to benefit. FARETINA (USA) and FARWIDE (UK) [9] are also conducting large-scale, multicenter studies to evaluate longer-term treatment outcomes, and their forthcoming results are awaited with interest.

This study has several strengths, including its relatively large sample size and focus on a real-world Asian population, which has been underrepresented in major phase III trials. However, limitations include its retrospective nature; the use of a real-world, clinically based dataset in which treatment regimens were not strictly standardized and varied among individuals, including the number of loading injections (three or four) and injection intervals, which were determined at the discretion of the treating physician rather than according to uniform objective criteria or a prespecified protocol; lack of a control group; and relatively short follow-up duration. In addition, the distribution between the three- and four-loading groups was unequal (approximately 2:1), which should be taken into account when interpreting the subgroup comparisons. Longer-term follow-up is necessary to determine whether these short-term benefits are sustained and translate into meaningful long-term visual preservation.

Conclusions

Switching to faricimab resulted in significant short-term visual and anatomical improvement in eyes with previously treated DME. These findings add to the growing body of evidence supporting the utility of faricimab in real-world settings and may inform clinical decision-making, particularly in patients with suboptimal response to prior anti-VEGF therapies.
