Socio-demographic, psychosocial, and suicidal behavior correlates of stigma among people with physical disabilities in Northwest Ethiopia
Authors: Shegaye Shumet, Endalamaw Salelew, Getachew Tesfaw Desalegn, Yohannes Mirkena, Dessie Abebaw Angaw, Tadele Amare Zeleke, Tilahun Kassew, Mesele Wondie
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPeople with physical disability may experience stigma in various ways, including social avoidance, negative attitudes and discrimination. Stigma among people with physical disability can vary based on study participants’ socio-demographic, behavioral and psychosocial factors. Therefore, identifying the socio-demographic, behavioral and psychosocial correlates of stigma is important because the findings of the study can help inform future interventions aimed at reducing stigma among individuals with a physical disability.ObjectiveThis study aimed to assess the psychosocial, suicidal behavior, and socio-demographic correlates of stigma among people with physical disabilities in the University of Gondar communities, northwest Ethiopia.MethodData collection was conducted among 269 participants who had a physical disability within University of Gondar communities in Northwest Ethiopia. The participants, who included students and employees at the University completed the stigma scale for chronic illness (SSCI-8), which was used to measure stigma. The participants also completed four other questionnaires, including the perceived social support, the Kessler − 10, and the WHODAS. We used independent sample t-tests and ANOVA to examine group differences in stigma, while Pearson correlation analysis identified the correlates of stigma with other continuous variables.ResultThe overall mean stigma score among people with a physical disability was 14.68 with 95% CI (13.98, 15.38). About 90.71% of people with physical disability had stigma. Stigma varied along socio-demographic variables, including marital status (single) with a mean (SD) score of 15.17 ± 5.64, educational level (diploma and above) with a mean (SD) score of 14.99 ± 5.89, and occupational status with a mean (SD) score of 15.54 ± 5.79 (p < 0.001). In addition, stigma varied among participants with suicidal ideation with a mean (SD) score of 16.93 ± 7.43 and attempt (18.74 ± 8.41), levels of psychological distress (severe) with a mean (SD) score of 20.88 ± 6.94 (p < 0.001), low perceived social support with a mean (SD) score of 18.60 ± 7.54 (p < 0.001), and moderate/severe level of functional impairment with a mean (SD) score of 21.76 ± 7.34 (p < 0.001). Moreover, stigma was positively correlated with psychological distress (r = 0.42, p < 0.001) and WHO Disability Assessment Schedule (r = 0.5, p < 0.001). Conversely, it was negatively correlated with perceived social support (r=-0.31, p < 0.001).ConclusionOur findings indicate that stigma is prevalent among people with physical disabilities, with variations observed based on marital status, occupation, and education level. Additionally, stigma showed significant correlations with functional impairment, psychological distress, perceived social support, and health related quality of life. Therefore, addressing stigma, promoting social support and providing appropriate interventions can help improve the psychological well-being and quality of life of individuals with physical disabilities.

Introduction

Stigma is a negative set of beliefs about people with specific characteristics, and people with disabilities have been stigmatized throughout history [1]. Numerous factors, such as inequality, social isolation, unfavorable opinions, and hate crimes, can contribute to disability stigma [2]. People with physical disabilities are often viewed as helpless and as an economic burden on society, causing them to be pitied and subjected to acts of assistance [1]. Additionally, physical impairments are sometimes believed to be the result of sinful acts committed in the past. Stigmatizing attitudes about disabilities can affect relationships between people with disabilities and their families, friends, and acquaintances. People with disabilities may themselves adopt negative beliefs about their disability and feel ashamed or embarrassed about it [3].

These negative beliefs related to disability could result in mental health problems, such as psychological distress [4, 5]. A study on physical disability and stigma found that variation of stigma experiences was correlated with variation in psychological distress [5]. People with physical disability might also experience perceived burdensomeness, economic difficulties and other social stressors that increase suicidal thought and behavior [6, 7], which indirectly prompt suicide risk.

Social support can play a crucial role in reducing the negative effect of stigma and promoting wellbeing among people with physical and sensory disabilities [8]. People with adequate social support could have increased self-esteem and reduce perceived discrimination which can improve subjective wellbeing [9]. However, if people with physical disability experience stigma they would have low social integration and participation which result in hopelessness and depression [10, 11]. Findings from earlier studies show that stigma is correlated with perceived social support and psychological distress among people with physical disability [12, 13]. However, the socio-demographic, psychosocial, and suicidal behavior correlates of stigma has not yet been known among people with physical disability in Ethiopia. Therefore, this study aimed to assess the psychosocial factors, suicidal behavior, and sociodemographic correlates of stigma among people with physical disabilities in northwest Ethiopia, which is an important initial step for the next stage of research that could address factors identified to be correlated with elevated stigma in the implementation of stigma reduction initiatives.

Methods and materials

Study design and period

An institution based cross-sectional study design was used among people with physical disability at University of Gondar between May and June 2021.

Study area

The University of Gondar is one of the oldest higher institutions and found in the historical town called Gondar. It was established in 1954 and starting its educational program with medical training. Now, the university has five campuses. Many people with physical disability were employed, while others were undergoing their education during our study period.

Study population

The study participants were all students who were studying in the University and employed staffs with physical disability.

Sample size determination and sampling procedure

The census sampling technique was used to recruit study participants. A total of 269 study participants took part in the interviews.

Measurement

Stigma was measured using the 8-item Stigma Scale for Chronic Illness (SSCI-8) tool. It is a reliable and valid instrument for assessing stigma in populations with chronic illnesses. The instrument assesses both enacted and internalized stigma [14]. It uses a five-point Likert scale, ranging from one (never) to five (always). The total score ranges from eight to forty, with a cutoff score greater than eight indicating the presence of stigma. It is a validated tool designed to measure perceived stigma in individuals with chronic illnesses. This instrument exhibits high internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.88 [15]. The instrument has not been previously validated or used with a similar study population in our setting. However, due to its strong psychometric properties, conciseness, applicability across different conditions, and cultural adaptability, we chose to use the tool. In our study, the reliability of the stigma measure was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha, which was 0.85.

Functional impairment among study participants was measured using the 12-item WHO Disability Assessment Schedule (WHODAS-2). A higher score on the WHODAS-2 indicates a greater degree of disability or functional impairment [16]. WHODAS 2.0 shows strong cultural adaptation and internal consistency, reflected in a Cronbach’s α of 0.88 [17]. It has been validated in diverse populations in Ethiopia, including individuals with mental disorders, musculoskeletal pain, and those affected by road traffic injuries [17, 18].

Psychological distress was assessed using the 10-item Kessler Psychological Distress Scale (K-10). The K-10 provides a global measure of psychological distress and helps identify individuals who may be experiencing mental health issues. The K-10 has been validated in Ethiopia, demonstrating good internal consistency with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.83 [19].

Perceived social support was measured using the 12-item Perceived Social Support Scale (PSSS). This widely used instrument assesses the availability and adequacy of support from family, friends, and significant others. The scale consists of 12 items that respondents rate based on their experiences with social support. Each item is scored, and the total score can range from 12 to 84, with higher scores indicating a greater perception of social support. The PSSS and its shorter versions have been validated in various populations in Ethiopia, including chronic patients, university students, and the general population [20, 21].

Suicidal ideation and attempt among people with disability was measured using the WHO Composite International Diagnostic Interview (CIDI) questionnaire [22]. Study participants were asked a question “After getting disabilities, have you ever seriously thought about committing or attempted suicide, respectively?” If respondents answer “yes” for the question, they would be considered to have suicidal ideation or attempt, respectively.

Data collection procedures

The questionnaire was designed in English, then translated into Amharic and back into English to ensure consistency. Data were collected using a face-to-face, interviewer-administered questionnaire. Of the 293 individuals with disabilities identified in the study area, 269 were interviewed. Fourteen individuals were excluded due to hearing problems (see Fig. 1). The questionnaire included sections on socio-demographic information, psychosocial factors, perceived social support, the WHO Disability Assessment Schedule 1 (WHODAS-2), and types of disabilities (visual, leg, and others). Data were collected using the Amharic version of the tool.

Data quality control

The pretest was conducted one week before the actual data collection, involving 5% of the estimated sample size. Training for data collectors and supervisors about data collection procedure, and maintaining privacy and confidentiality was given.

Data processing and analysis

After checking the questionnaires for completeness, data were entered into EPI-info version 7 and then transferred to STATA-14 for analysis. Descriptive statistics, including frequency and mean, were presented using tables. Independent sample t-tests and analysis of variance (ANOVA) were employed to identify variations of stigma across different variables. Pearson correlation analysis was used to determine the correlates of stigma with other continuous variables, with statistical significance set at a p-value of < 0.05.

Ethical consideration

Ethical approval

and clearance were obtained from the Ethical Review Board (IRB) of University of Gondar. Written informed consent was obtained from each study participants after explaining the purpose and benefit of the study. The participants were informed that they have the right to refuse participation in the study at any time and refusing to participate will not affect them with any means. The interview with study participants was conducted with strict privacy and confidentiality.

Result

Sociodemographic, Physical and psychosocial characteristics of the respondents

A total of 269 participants took part in the study. Three-fifths of the respondents were male. The mean age of the respondents was 26.67 years (SD ± 7.33). A large number, 218 (81%) of the study participants, were single, followed by those who were married (16%). The majority of the respondents, 236 (87.7%), were Orthodox Christians, and 232 (86.2%) had a degree or higher educational status. Regarding occupational status, 209 (77.7%) of the study subjects were students, and 159 (59.1%) of participants had a low monthly income. More than half (56.9%) of the participants came from rural settings. Of the total study subjects, 141 (52.4%) had visual impairment, followed by both legs physical disability (33.8%). A small number, 26 (9.7%), of the study participants had co-morbid physical illnesses, of which 10 individuals had neurological problems. Ninety-three (34.6%) of participants had mild to severe levels of psychological distress. Regarding behavioral factors, 43 (16%) of the study subjects reported ever having suicidal ideation, and 30 (11.2%) had planned to commit suicide. Of the participants who disclosed having ever contemplated suicide, roughly 19 (7.1%) had made a suicide attempt, with three of them having done so within the past month. Of all suicide attempts reported, 50% of the respondents used poisoning as a method to end their lives(Table 1).

Stigma among people with physical disability

The mean (SD) stigma score was 14.68 (± 5.83) with a 95% CI of 13.98 to 15.38. A large proportion (71.4%) of the study participants had higher levels of stigma (Fig. 3).

Psychosocial, behavioral and socio-demographic based profiling of stigma

The higher the mean score in each psychosocial, behavioral, and sociodemographic characteristic indicated that the study subjects had higher levels of stigma. Among sociodemographic factors, participants’ occupational, marital, and educational statuses showed variation in stigma. Participants who were single had the highest stigma, with a mean score of 15.17 (SD = ± 5.64), compared to those who were married or divorced/widowed. Individuals with a diploma or higher had higher levels of stigma, with a mean score of 14.99 (SD = ± 5.89), than those with primary or secondary education. Regarding occupational status, government employees had a higher mean score of stigma (15.54, SD = ± 5.79) than students (M = 11.70, SD = ± 4.96)(Table 2).

Of the total study participants, 43 (16.0%) had suicidal ideation. Individuals with suicidal ideation had higher levels of stigma, with a mean score of 16.93 (SD = ± 7.43), compared to people without suicidal ideation. Of the 43 individuals, 19 (7.1%) attempted suicide. People with suicidal behavior had a high mean stigma score of 18.74 (SD = ± 8.41). The mean score of psychological distress across study subjects was 18.05 (SD = ± 7.46). In this study, people with severe levels of psychological distress had the highest stigma, with a mean score of 20.88 (SD = ± 6.94), followed by mild (mean = 16.48, SD = ± 4.89) and moderate (mean = 16.43, SD = ± 5.45) levels of psychological distress. The mean (SD) score of perceived social support among study participants was 57.26 (± 14.09). People with low social support had the highest mean (SD) stigma score of 18.60 (± 7.54) (Table 2).

Variation of stigma was observed across levels of functioning (F (2,266) = 23.64, P < 0.001). The highest mean (SD) score was 21.76 (± 7.34) among people with moderate/severe disability, followed by those with mild disability (17.18 ± 5.78)(Table 2).

Correlation analysis

Pearson correlation coefficients were used to examine the association between stigma, disability, perceived social support, and psychological distress. Stigma was positively correlated with psychological distress (r = 0.42, p < 0.001) and scores on the WHO Disability Assessment Schedule (r = 0.5, p < 0.001), indicating that participants with higher stigma experienced more psychological distress and functional impairment. Conversely, stigma was negatively correlated with perceived social support (r = -0.31, p < 0.001), suggesting that participants with lower stigma had more perceived social support(Table 3).

Discussion

Stigma among people with physical disabilities is a complex phenomenon influenced by individual characteristics, social dynamics, cultural beliefs, psychological factors, and environmental conditions. Addressing these correlates through targeted interventions is crucial for reducing stigma and improving quality of life for this population. However, identifying and implementing strategies to reduce stigma in low-income countries, such as Ethiopia, has not received adequate attention. Therefore, our study aimed to identify some correlates of stigma, which might help in stigma reduction initiatives among people with physical disabilities.

In our study, about 90.71% of people with physical disabilities experienced stigma. This high prevalence might be related to the development of negative beliefs about their condition, leading to feelings of shame and embarrassment [23]. Stigmatizing attitudes towards disabilities can have a significant influence on relationships, coping strategies [13], and mental health conditions [24], contributing to higher levels of stigma. Our finding is notably higher compared to studies conducted in other countries, such as Niger [25] and South Africa [26]. The degree of stigma experienced by the study participants varied based on their sociodemographic characteristics. Individuals with a diploma or higher education had higher stigma scores than those with primary or secondary education, possibly due to lower levels of awareness and resources about disability. Government employees also reported higher stigma compared to students, potentially due to workplace dynamics and societal perceptions. Job performance expectations and hierarchical structures in government roles might contribute to this increased stigma. Regarding study participants’ marital status, single participants experienced the highest levels of stigma, likely influenced by societal norms and expectations surrounding marriage. Marriage is often seen as a status symbol, leading to judgment and pressure to find a partner. Single individuals may face stereotypes and biases, with the stigma impacting their chances of finding a spouse, particularly for those with disabilities [27]. Girls experience more adverse effects than boys in this regard [28]. Additionally, single individuals with severe functional impairments are less likely to marry than those with mild impairments, indicating potential stigma or discrimination based on functional ability [29]. Our correlation analysis supports this hypothesis, showing that participants with severe functional impairments experience higher levels of stigma.

Our study findings also revealed that stigma varied based on psychosocial factors. People with high levels of psychological distress experienced higher levels of stigma compared to those with no psychological distress. This may be linked to the presence of mental health-related stigma in addition to disability-related stigma. The study showed that stigma experienced by people with disabilities can significantly impact their psychological wellbeing and vice versa [30]. Our correlation analysis also revealed that there was a positive correlation between stigma and psychological distress (r = 0.42, p < 0.001) in this population. Individuals who reported higher levels of psychological distress also experienced significant levels of stigma. This could be associated with the interplay between internalized stigma, social isolation, coping mechanisms, perceived discrimination, societal attitudes, and lack of support services [31, 32], all of which contribute to the higher stigma mean scores observed among people with physical disabilities experiencing psychological distress. The other possible reason might be related with the relationship between personal and vicarious stigma experiences among people with physical disabilities and psychological distress. Both personal and vicarious stigma experiences significantly contribute to psychological distress among people with physical disabilities. Internalized stigma negatively affects self-esteem and quality of life, while vicarious stigma can exacerbate feelings of distress and isolation [29, 31]. However, our study did not determine the causality between psychological distress and stigma.

Additionally, we found that individuals with low perceived social support had higher levels of stigma. This might be associated with the interplay of isolation, vulnerability to negative perceptions, coping challenges, and psychological distress, all of which contribute to higher stigma mean scores in this population [33]. The high level of stigma among people with low social support might also be related to the presence of a high number of single study participants, as single adults’ happiness and well-being are significantly influenced by the role of social relationships. Our correlation analysis supports this idea, showing that people with lower perceptions of social support had higher levels of stigma. Therefore, addressing social support systems and fostering inclusive communities can help mitigate these effects and reduce stigma. Reports [34] have shown that single adults experience lower perceptions of social support availability and greater negative life events and discrimination, leading to poorer psychological well-being. Improving self-esteem and reducing perceived discrimination can help address these issues [35, 36].

The results of our study found that people with suicidal ideation or attempts have higher mean scores of stigma. This high mean score of stigma among physically disabled individuals with suicidal ideation and attempts might be related to the influence of societal attitudes, personal experiences of stigma, and the complexities associated with their disabilities. A previous study [37] indicated that individuals with physical disabilities who experience suicidal ideation or attempts often report higher levels of stigma, which can significantly impact their mental health and overall well-being. People with physical disabilities are at a higher risk of experiencing depression and suicidal ideation [38]. Disability-related factors such as stigma, social isolation, and negative future outlook can exacerbate mental health issues, leading to increased suicidal ideation and attempts [37, 39]. Therefore, addressing these issues through education, accessible mental health care, and community support is essential for improving mental health outcomes in this vulnerable population.

Furthermore, we assessed the participants’ levels of disability. Our study findings indicate that people with severe functional impairments had higher mean scores of stigma. Participants with severe functional impairments may not receive the necessary social support they need due to societal attitudes and misconceptions surrounding disabilities. Research indicates that individuals with greater functional limitations often experience higher levels of perceived stigma, which can negatively impact their mental health and quality of life [11]. This finding is also supported by our correlation analysis, which shows a positive correlation between stigma and levels of disability. Therefore, addressing stigma through education, advocacy, and support systems is crucial for improving the well-being of people with physical disabilities.

Limitations

The cross-sectional nature of our study limits our ability to assess causal relationships between stigma and psychological distress. Potential limitations of our study include social desirability and recall bias. Because the data collection method was face-to-face interviews, study participants might not have provided accurate responses about co-morbid stigmatized medical conditions. Additionally, study subjects may have had difficulty in recalling the frequency of suicidal attempts during the assessment of suicidal risk. Since our sampling is a census, it provides comprehensive data for the specific population being studied. However, this does not automatically ensure high external validity. Just because a census study provides accurate and complete data for one population does not mean that the findings are applicable to other populations or settings.

Implication and future direction

The findings of our study have significant implications for both research and practice. Firstly, the finding pertaining to the factors associated with stigma among people with physical disabilities will inform future research, which can utilize more robust longitudinal designs to establish directionality and make causal connections between the associations established from this study. Additionally, experimental studies can implement targeted stigma reduction interventions for individuals with specific risk factors identified by our present study. Furthermore, our findings suggest that health practitioners should consider implementing stigma reduction initiatives within programs that provide services for individuals with physical disabilities.

Conclusion

Our findings indicate that stigma is prevalent among people with physical disabilities, with variations observed based on marital status, occupation, and education level. Additionally, stigma showed significant correlations with functional impairment, psychological distress, perceived social support, and health-related quality of life. Therefore, addressing stigma, promoting social support and providing appropriate interventions can help improve the psychological well-being and quality of life of individuals with physical disabilities.
