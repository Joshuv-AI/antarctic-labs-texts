An Otolaryngology‐Focused Risk Stratification for Postoperative Dysphagia in Pediatric Cardiac Surgery
Authors: Matthew Zhang, Basir Mansoor, Marlaine Frelier, Cynthia Wang, Yann‐Fuu Kou, Stephen R Chorney, Romaine Johnson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesTo develop and validate a risk stratification model for postoperative dysphagia in pediatric cardiac surgery patients, focusing on factors relevant to otolaryngologic assessment and implications for Otolaryngology management.MethodsAnalysis of 174 pediatric patients undergoing cardiac surgery at a tertiary care center between January 1, 2022 and December 31, 2023. Logistic regression was used to develop a risk score based on preoperative and intraoperative factors. Patients were categorized into low, moderate, and high‐risk groups for dysphagia requiring Otolaryngology consultation. Secondary outcomes included impaired vocal fold mobility.ResultsOf 174 patients, postoperative dysphagia necessitating Otolaryngology evaluation was identified in 102 (58.6%) children. The final predictive model included age at surgery (OR, 0.80; 95% CI, 0.72–0.90; p < 0.001), cardiopulmonary bypass duration (OR, 1.73; 95% CI, 1.23–2.45; p = 0.002), and high‐risk complex procedures (e.g., Norwood) (OR, 6.49; 95% CI, 2.70–15.61; p < 0.001). Model discrimination was good (AUC 0.82). Predicted risk categories showed distinct dysphagia rates among the cohort: low‐risk (24.7% of the cohort, with 30.2% experiencing dysphagia), moderate‐risk (31.6%, with 38.2% experiencing dysphagia), and high‐risk (43.7%, with 89.5% experiencing dysphagia). Among high‐risk patients, 70.6% exhibited impaired vocal fold mobility at long‐term follow‐up.ConclusionThis Otolaryngology‐focused risk stratification model identifies pediatric cardiac surgery patients at high risk for postoperative dysphagia and associated impaired vocal fold mobility. Early identification of this population can aid consultation and therapeutic interventions like injection laryngoplasty.Level of EvidenceLevel 3.Keywords: impaired vocal fold mobility, pediatric cardiac surgery, postoperative dysphagia, risk stratification model

Introduction

Congenital heart defects (CHD) affect 9 per 1000 live births globally, amounting to approximately 1.3 million newborns each year [1, 2]. Pediatric cardiac surgery is a critical intervention for managing these defects, with one‐third of CHD cases requiring operative treatment [3]. These surgeries of the heart and great vessels carry a significant risk for injury to the recurrent laryngeal nerve (RLN) due to its anatomic proximity [4, 5]. The RLN's role in innervating the intrinsic laryngeal muscles and providing sensory innervation to the larynx below the vocal cords means that any nerve compromise can result in significant functional deficits.

Vocal fold mobility impairment from RLN injury commonly manifests as dysphagia (swallowing difficulties), affecting an estimated 64% of infants following congenital heart surgery [6]. Compromised laryngeal function can also lead to a cascade of related complications, including weakened airway protection during feeding, difficulty respirating, and voice disorders [7, 8]. The impact of dysphagia extends beyond immediate clinical implications, as it has been associated with a 45% increase in total hospital expenses from cardiac operations [9].

While studies have established the risk of dysphagia following cardiac surgery, there remains a gap in our understanding of how we can quantify and assess the impact of various factors [10]. Furthermore, there is limited guidance on approaches based on individual risk profiles. Early identification and intervention are crucial, as they may lead to reduced risk of morbidity, recurrent hospital admissions, and prolonged enteral feeding [11]. Our study seeks to examine key preoperative and intraoperative factors associated with postoperative dysphagia and vocal fold mobility impairment. We hypothesize that by developing a risk stratification model for pediatric patients undergoing cardiac surgery, we can create more targeted strategies and inform specific interventions, such as injection laryngoplasty, thereby reducing dysphagia‐related morbidity.

Materials and Methods

We performed a retrospective cohort study of 174 pediatric patients who underwent cardiac surgery between January 1, 2022 and December 31, 2023, at Children's Health, Dallas Campus, Dallas, TX, USA. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) at UT Southwestern (IRB number: STU‐2018‐0284) and was exempt from consent due to its retrospective nature. Cardiac procedures were grouped into categories: High‐risk complex procedures (Norwood, aortic arch repair/reconstruction, truncus arteriosus repair), single ventricle palliation (Fontan procedure), septal defect repairs (atrial septal defect [ASD] repair, ventricular septal defect [VSD] repair, patent foramen ovale [PFO] closure), outflow tract procedures (Tetralogy of Fallot repair [ToF]), less complex procedures (patent ductus arteriosus [PDA] ligation, vascular ring repair), and reoperations (reoperative sternotomy). Congenital heart conditions of each patient were collected, including ToF, PFO, ASD, VSD, PDA, transposition of great arteries (TGA), hypoplastic left heart syndrome (HLHS), coarctation of the aorta, truncus arteriosus, and/or vascular ring.

Preoperative variables included age at surgery, sex, BMI, ethnicity, race, type of insurance, previous cardiac surgeries, presence of comorbidities (neurological disorders, genetic syndromes, gastroesophageal reflux disease [GERD]), baseline dysphagia status, and preoperative vocal fold mobility when available. Intraoperative variables included duration of cardiac surgery, presence of complications, use of cardiopulmonary bypass, and duration of bypass. Postoperative variables included total hospital stay and feeding status at discharge.

Dysphagia was defined based on any mention of difficulty with feeding or swallowing in notes, or through findings from swallow studies. Additionally, the method of dysphagia assessment was recorded (clinical assessment vs. instrumental swallow study). Preoperative dysphagia status was available for 165 patients (94.8%), and preoperative vocal fold mobility was assessed in 11 patients (6.3%). A sensitivity analysis was performed restricting analysis to the 27 patients (15.5%) with instrumentally confirmed postoperative dysphagia. Patients' per os (PO) status at discharge (regular full diet, partial oral diet, tube feeding, unknown) was recorded as a postoperative variable to aid with dysphagia assessment. PO status at 1‐year follow‐up for patients with partial oral diet or tube feeding was collected if available.

Secondary postoperative outcomes include vocal fold status (normal, impaired, unknown), which were documented based on findings from flexible laryngoscopies. Follow‐up flexible laryngoscopy results were collected for patients with impaired vocal fold mobility if available. Preoperative vocal fold status was not well documented in most patients in our cohort, so it was not included in our analysis.

Logistic regression was performed to develop a risk stratification model for dysphagia using postoperative dysphagia as the outcome variable. The model used a backward selection process. All variables were entered into the model and sequentially deleted if the p value was ≥ 0.05. Patients were then classified as low risk (< 30% predicted probability), moderate risk (30%–60%), and high risk (> 60%) for dysphagia based on the model's prediction. That is, if the model predicted the patient was at low risk, then they were classified as low risk. We validated the model using k‐fold cross‐validation to ensure reliability and generalizability.

All statistical analyses were performed using Stata Statistical Software. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

The majority of patients identified as White or Caucasian (70.7%) and as non‐Hispanic (62.5%). For insurance, most had either private (44.8%) or Medicaid (50.0%). The most common comorbidities present include genetic syndromes (i.e., DiGeorge syndrome) in 14.9% and GERD in 14.9% of patients. There were 57 patients (32.8%) who had previous cardiac surgeries.

Table 1 further provides a comparison between the patients with postoperative dysphagia and those without dysphagia after their cardiac procedure. Postoperative dysphagia was significantly associated with younger age of surgery (p < 0.001), lower BMI (p < 0.001), increased duration of bypass (p < 0.001), and White or Caucasian for race (p = 0.046). Patients with postoperative dysphagia had an increased duration of stay in the hospital (p < 0.001). A greater proportion of patients with postoperative dysphagia presented with a genetic syndrome as a comorbidity compared to patients without postoperative dysphagia (p = 0.045).

Preoperative dysphagia was present in 42 patients (24.1%) and absent in 123 patients (70.7%). Among patients with preoperative dysphagia, 38 (90.5%) continued to have feeding difficulties postoperatively, while only 4 (9.5%) showed improvement. Among the 123 patients without baseline dysphagia, 57 (46.3%) developed new postoperative feeding difficulties. Only 27 patients (15.5%) with postoperative feeding difficulties had instrumental confirmation via videofluoroscopic swallow study (VFSS, n = 26) or fiberoptic endoscopic evaluation of swallowing (FEES, n = 1).

Table 2 presents laryngoscopy findings as well as diet status at both discharge and follow‐up. To assess for postoperative dysphagia, PO status at discharge was collected and classified based on a regular full diet (101, 58.0%), partial oral diet (29, 16.7%), tube feeding only (41, 23.6%), or unknown (3, 1.7%). PO status at 1‐year follow‐up was recorded for patients who were on partial oral diet or tube feeding PO status at discharge, with 20.8% on partial oral diet, and 24.5% on tube feeding. From flexible laryngoscopy findings, 14.4% of patients presented with postoperative impaired vocal fold mobility. The presence of postoperative dysphagia was associated with abnormal flexible laryngoscopy findings (p < 0.001). Of the 102 patients with postoperative dysphagia, 24.5% had documented impaired vocal fold mobility. For patients who had postoperative impaired vocal fold mobility, 80% had a repeat flexible laryngoscopy performed between 2 and 14 months of cardiac surgery, with 65% exhibiting persistent impaired vocal fold mobility.

These factors presented in Tables 1, 2, 3 were used to develop the risk model, presented in Figure 1 and Tables 4, 5. Figure 1 illustrates the distribution of key risk factors across the three risk categories, showing the clear separation in age at surgery and bypass duration that drives the model's predictions. Three key predictors were identified: age at surgery, cardiopulmonary bypass duration, and procedure complexity. In the final risk model, dysphagia rates between the categories were 30.2% for low risk, 38.2% for moderate risk, and 89.5% for high risk. The model featured good discrimination (AUC = 0.82) and calibration (Hosmer–Lemeshow p = 0.49). The risk model yields increased postoperative dysphagia rates with increased BMI (p < 0.001), increased bypass duration (p < 0.001), and younger age at surgery (p < 0.001). Children in the high‐risk group had a higher proportion of genetic syndromes compared to the other groups (18, 23.7%; 6, 10.9%; 2, 4.7%, from high, moderate, to low risk; p = 0.012). The model associated decreased rates of postoperative dysphagia in patients who had previous open cardiac surgeries (p < 0.001). Patients in the high‐risk category were associated with increased rates of partial or full tube feeding upon discharge (p < 0.001). Of those with available 1‐year follow‐up data, 50% remained on enteral feeding, compared to 33.3% in the moderate risk group and 25% in the low‐risk group. Discussion

This study serves to evaluate various risk factors that are associated with postoperative dysphagia after pediatric cardiac surgery in 172 children. The risk model effectively stratified patients into low, moderate, and high‐risk groups, with dysphagia rates of 30.2%, 38.2%, and 89.5%, respectively. The high‐risk group showed significantly increased dependence on enteral feeding at discharge (69.8%), with 50% remaining dependent on tube feeding at 1‐year follow‐up. In comparison, only 23.6% in the moderate risk category and 9.3% in the low‐risk category required enteral feeds at discharge. Furthermore, the high‐risk category had a significantly higher percentage of impaired vocal fold mobility postoperatively (26.3%), compared to 3.6% in the moderate risk and 7% in the low‐risk groups (p < 0.001).

Impaired vocal fold mobility is a recognized complication after cardiac surgery, due to the proximity of the RLN to the aortic arch and can be a cause of new postoperative feeding difficulties [4, 5, 11]. Our findings support this, as the high‐risk category is associated with complex cardiac operations such as the Norwood procedure, involving operating around the aortic arch. Some patients may recover spontaneously from impaired vocal fold mobility, which may explain why in our cohort patients with previous cardiac surgeries were associated with decreased postoperative dysphagia rates, as they may have developed potential compensatory mechanisms [12]. However, 6‐month recovery rates vary widely in the literature, from 9% to 35% [7, 12, 13]. Of the high‐risk category who presented with impaired vocal fold mobility after cardiac surgery, 70.6% had persistent impaired vocal fold mobility at follow‐up. Prolonged vocal fold mobility impairment may lead to increased hospital readmissions due to poor weight gain from feeding difficulties if not addressed, as well as increased aspiration risk [11, 14]. Given the associations between vocal fold mobility impairment and feeding difficulties, interventions to help with glottic closure can improve dysphagia in this population and may be beneficial [15, 16]. Our model could therefore guide early identification with targeted laryngoscopy and early management with injection laryngoplasty to mitigate adverse effects from vocal fold mobility impairment in high‐risk children.

While dysphagia after cardiac procedures has been frequently observed, the risk factors that increase this postsurgery complication, especially in pediatric patients, are not as well studied [6, 17]. Adverse effects of persistent dysphagia, including aspiration, pneumonia, need for tracheostomy, and increased hospital stays, highlight the importance of early identification and intervention [9, 18]. Although there have been studies that have developed risk stratification models for dysphagia after cardiac surgery, the featured models were focused on adult patients. The SSG‐OD model by Zhou et al., predicted dysphagia in critically ill patients, with their most important factors being sedative drug use duration, enteral feeding duration, and previous history of stroke [19]. While the duration of surgery was also a significant factor in our model, enteral feeding duration was a postoperative complication rather than a preoperative predictive factor in our study. History of stroke or other neurological co‐morbidities was not a significant factor in our model, likely due to the younger population.

Another model, the RODICS score developed by Grimm et al., shared more similarities with our model [20]. Lower BMI and more complex cardiac procedures were also associated with higher rates of postoperative dysphagia in their model [20]. However, their complex cardiac procedures focused on ventricular device placements or heart transplants, while our complex cardiac procedures focused on operations to correct congenital defects due to the different patient populations [20]. Furthermore, older age (median 61 years) in their model was associated with a higher rate of postoperative dysphagia, compared to our study, which associated younger age with dysphagia [20]. Their model also incorporated chronic lung and cerebrovascular disease as significant factors, which are more relevant in an older adult population [20]. In particular, their model yielded an AUC of 0.75, compared to our AUC of 0.82; this difference most likely reflects the more focused nature of our study on pediatric patients rather than a broader adult population [20]. These differences emphasize the need for a pediatric‐specific risk model to identify and manage younger patients at a higher risk of postoperative dysphagia.

This study has several critical limitations. First, preoperative vocal fold mobility was assessed in only 6.3% of patients, preventing analysis of baseline vocal fold function as a confounding variable. Second, our findings reveal that 90.5% of patients with preoperative dysphagia had persistent postoperative feeding difficulties, meaning a subset of our “postoperative dysphagia” cases represents persistent rather than incident dysfunction. Third, only 15.5% of patients with postoperative feeding difficulties had instrumental swallow studies, limiting our ability to distinguish true medical dysphagia from other feeding difficulties. Fourth, our procedure classification was institution‐specific rather than validated using established risk stratification systems. Finally, the retrospective design and single‐center nature limit generalizability.

The strengths of this study include a focused risk stratification model tailored specifically to pediatric cardiac surgery patients, addressing an important gap in clinical practice. The model incorporates clearly defined preoperative and intraoperative variables, robust statistical methodology including cross‐validation, and clinically relevant otolaryngologic outcomes. The large, well‐characterized patient cohort from a major tertiary care center bolsters the study's internal validity. Although generalizability may be limited by the single‐center, retrospective design, the use of widely performed cardiac procedures and routinely collected clinical variables enhances potential applicability to other pediatric cardiac surgery institutions.

Conclusion

This risk stratification model highlights key predictors of postoperative dysphagia in pediatric patients, differing from prior adult‐focused models by emphasizing younger age, congenital defect surgeries, and bypass duration as major risk factors. These findings reinforce the need for pediatric‐specific screening to identify high‐risk patients for postoperative dysphagia, who are more likely to present with persistent tube feeding and vocal fold mobility impairment. Consequently, close monitoring and early intervention should be considered in high‐risk patients to mitigate long‐term adverse outcomes. Future prospective studies could validate and refine this tool across diverse pediatric cardiac centers.
