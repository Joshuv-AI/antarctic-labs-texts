Niven numbers are an asymptotic basis of order 3
Kate Thomas

Abstract
. A base- g g Niven number is a natural number divisible by the sum of its base- g g digits. We show that, for any g ⩾ 3 g\geqslant 3 , all sufficiently large natural numbers can be written as the sum of three base- g g Niven numbers. We also give an asymptotic formula for the number of representations of a sufficiently large integer as the sum of three integers with fixed, close to average, digit sums.



1.
Introduction

A central question in additive number theory is to establish whether a given set of integers
\mathcal{S}
is an
asymptotic basis
for the integers, that is, to determine whether there exists a natural number
k
such that any sufficiently large integer can be written as the sum of
k
elements of
\mathcal{S}
. Here,
k
denotes the
order
of the basis.

Famously, Lagrange’s theorem gives that the squares are a basis of order 4, and Waring’s problem, solved by Hilbert, shows that
k
th
powers are also an additive basis. Some interesting variants of Waring’s problem consider
k
th
powers of integers which have restrictions on their digits in some base. For example, Pfeiffer and Thuswaldner
pfeiffer2007waring
show that the
k
th
powers of integers with certain congruence conditions on their sums of digits in different bases is an asymptotic basis. More recently, Green
green2025waring
established that, given any two digits which are coprime, the integers whose base-
g
expansions consists of only these digits satisfy Waring’s problem. Further references for additive bases coming from sets of integers with digit restrictions are given in the introduction of
sanna2021additive
.

A
base-
g
Niven number
is a natural number that is divisible by its base-
g
sum of digits. Such integers are also referred to as
Harshad numbers
. It is shown in
de2003counting
, and independently in
mauduit2005distribution
, that the number of base-
g
Niven numbers less than
x
is asymptotically
\eta_{g}x/\log x
for some constant
\eta_{g}>0
.

It is conjectured that the set of base-
g
Niven numbers is an asymptotic basis of order 2. In
sanna2021additive
Sanna established, conditionally upon a certain generalisation of the Riemann Hypothesis, that the set of base-
g
Niven numbers is an asymptotic basis with order growing linearly in
g
. Our result is the following unconditional statement.

Theorem 1.1
.

For any
g\geqslant 3
, the set of base-
g
Niven numbers is an asymptotic basis of order 3.

We first count, via the circle method, the number of representations of a sufficiently large integer
M
as the sum of three integers with a near-average digit sum. By showing that a proportion of such representations have each summand being a Niven number, we provide a lower bound for the number of representations of
M
as the sum of three Niven numbers. To state these results more precisely, we need the following notation.

Let
s_{g}(n)
denote the base-
g
digit sum of
n
. Let
K\geqslant 1
be a sufficiently large integer, and let
M\in(g^{K-1},g^{K}]
. For
k\in\mathbf{N}
, let

S_{g}(k)\coloneq\{n<g^{K}:s_{g}(n)=k\}\textrm{ and }\mathcal{N}_{g}(k)\coloneq\{n\in S(k):k\mid n\}.

Thus
\mathcal{N}_{g}(k)
is the set of Niven numbers in
S_{g}(k)
, that is, Niven numbers of a certain size with fixed digit sum. Let

\mu_{K}\coloneq\frac{(g-1)K}{2},

which is the average digit sum of
n<g^{K}
. For a fixed choice of
k_{1},k_{2},k_{3}\in\mathbf{N}
, let
S_{i}\coloneq S_{g}(k_{i})
for
i=1,2,3
, and let
\mathcal{N}_{i}\coloneq\mathcal{N}_{g}(k_{i})
for
i=1,2,3
. Suppose that
k_{1},k_{2},k_{3}\in\mathbf{N}
are such that

(1)

|k_{i}-\mu_{K}|\leqslant C_{g}\textrm{ and }k_{1}+k_{2}+k_{3}\equiv M(\operatorname{mod}\,g-1),

where
C_{g}:=g(g-1)\prod_{p\leqslant 10g^{2}}p
, and the product is over primes. The specific choice of constant here is unimportant, and any sufficiently large value would do, but this value is large enough to ensure that
k_{i}
with further desired properties exist. Let
r_{S_{1}+S_{2}+S_{3}}(M)
be the number of representations of
M=s_{1}+s_{2}+s_{3}
, for
s_{i}\in S_{i}
. Our main result is the following theorem, from which we can later deduce the corresponding result for
\mathcal{N}_{i}\subset S_{i}
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
g,K
and
M
be integers such that
g\geqslant 3
,
K
is sufficiently large in terms of
g
, and
M\in(g^{K-1},g^{K}]
. Suppose that
k_{1},k_{2},k_{3}
satisfy
Equation

1
. Then

r_{S_{1}+S_{2}+S_{3}}(M)=\frac{(g-1)M^{2}}{2(2\pi\sigma^{2}K)^{3/2}}(1+O_{g}((\log K)^{4}K^{-1/4})),

where
\sigma^{2}=(g^{2}-1)/12
.

Let
r_{\mathcal{N}_{1}+\mathcal{N}_{2}+\mathcal{N}_{3}}(M)
be the number of representations of
M=n_{1}+n_{2}+n_{3}
, where
n_{i}\in\mathcal{N}_{i}
for
i=1,2,3
. In order to relate the quantity
r_{S_{1}+S_{2}+S_{3}}(M)
to
r_{\mathcal{N}_{1}+\mathcal{N}_{2}+\mathcal{N}_{3}}(M)
, we require some further conditions on the choice of
k_{1},k_{2},k_{3}
. Throughout,
(a,b)
denotes the greatest common divisor of
a
and
b
. Suppose that
k_{1},k_{2},k_{3}\in\mathbf{N}
also satisfy, in addition to
Equation

1
,

(2)

\displaystyle(k_{i},k_{j})=1\textrm{ and }(k_{i},g)=1\textrm{ for }i,j=1,2,3\textrm{ and }i\neq j.

Let

c_{g}(k_{1},k_{2},k_{3})\coloneq\frac{4}{(g-1)^{2}}\prod_{i=1}^{3}(g-1,k_{i}).

Then we have the following theorem, from which
Theorem

1.1
is a corollary after showing that such a choice of
k_{1},k_{2}
and
k_{3}
exist.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
g,K
and
M
be integers such that
g\geqslant 3
,
K
is sufficiently large in terms of
g
, and
M\in(g^{K-1},g^{K}]
. Suppose that
k_{1},k_{2},k_{3}
fulfil
Equation

1
and
Equation

2
, then

r_{\mathcal{N}_{1}+\mathcal{N}_{2}+\mathcal{N}_{3}}(M)=\frac{(g-1)^{2}c_{g}(k_{1},k_{2},k_{3})}{4k_{1}k_{2}k_{3}}r_{S_{1}+S_{2}+S_{3}}(M)+O_{g}(M^{2}K^{-29/6}).

In particular,

r_{\mathcal{N}_{1}+\mathcal{N}_{2}+\mathcal{N}_{3}}(M)=c_{g}(k_{1},k_{2},k_{3})\frac{M^{2}}{(2\pi\sigma^{2})^{3/2}K^{9/2}}+O_{g}(M^{2}(\log K)^{4}K^{-19/4}),

where
\sigma^{2}=(g^{2}-1)/12
.

Our methods can be adapted to show the analogous versions of
Theorem

1.1
,
Theorem

1.2
and
Theorem

1.3
for base 2, however there is one main technical difference. For readability, we do not give the details here, but comment on the necessary changes in
Section

5
.

1.1.
Notation

Throughout, we consider
g
to be fixed. Let
\|x\|_{\mathbf{R}/\mathbf{Z}}
denote the distance of a real number
x
to the nearest integer. We write
e(x)
for
\exp(2\pi ix)
, and
e_{g-1}(x)
for
e(x/(g-1))
. We require the quantity
\ell
, defined to be

(3)

\ell\coloneqq\lceil 384g^{3}\log K\rceil.

Throughout,
\sigma=\sqrt{(g^{2}-1)/12}.

2.
An overview of the proof

This section outlines the key steps to showing
Theorem

1.1
. The main work is to establish
Theorem

1.2
, and then to show that a sufficient number of these representations are of integers which are actually Niven numbers.

The primary tool for showing
Theorem

1.2
is the circle method. Notably in our application there are few major arcs; to show the base-
g
result, we take
g-1
major arcs, these are short intervals around the rationals
j/(g-1)
for
j=0,\ldots,g-2
. The need to consider rationals of this form stems from the relation
s_{g}(n)\equiv n(\operatorname{mod}\,g-1)
, which is the only congruence obstruction to
Theorem

1.2
. The contribution from these major arcs is handled in
Section

4
, giving the main term in
Theorem

1.2
. We establish a uniform pointwise bound for the minor arcs; existing results cover a subset of the minor arcs, but are not sufficiently strong at certain minor arc points to give
Theorem

1.2
. This bound is proved in
Section

5
, and relies on a local limit theorem, the necessary consequences of which are given in
Section

3
.

In
Section

6
, we show
Theorem

1.3
by linking the number of representations of an integer as the sum of three integers with near-average digit sum, to representations where each summand is also a Niven number.

Finally a choice of
k_{1},k_{2}
and
k_{3}
which will allow us to deduce
Theorem

1.1
from
Theorem

1.3
is given in
Section

6.2
.

2.1.
Counting the number of representations of an integer in
S_{1}+S_{2}+S_{3}

The main congruence obstruction to finding solutions to
M=s_{1}+s_{2}+s_{3}
comes from the following fact. For all
n\in\mathbf{Z}
and any base
g
,
g\geqslant 2
,

(4)

s_{g}(n)\equiv n(\operatorname{mod}\,g-1)

Thus in order for
M=s_{1}+s_{2}+s_{3}
to have solutions with
s_{i}\in S_{i}
, we must have

(5)

k_{1}+k_{2}+k_{3}\equiv M(\operatorname{mod}\,g-1).

Let
\mu_{K}\coloneq(g-1)K/2
, then we also require that

(6)

|k_{i}-\mu_{K}|\leqslant C_{g}.

Note that
\mu_{K}
is the average base-
g
digit sum for
n<g^{K}
. By restricting to target digit sums
k_{i}
that are close to the average value, we ensure that the sets
|S_{i}|
are large. It is shown in
(
mauduit2005distribution
, Lemma 3)
that
|S_{g}(k)|
is unimodal in
k
, with maximum size when
k=\left\lfloor\mu_{K}\right\rfloor
. We require an asymptotic bound for the sizes of the sets
S_{i}
. The results of Mauduit and Sárközy
(
mauduit1997arithmetic
, Theorem 1)
, and Fouvry and Mauduit
(
fouvry2005entiers
, Theorem 1.1)
bound the size of
|S_{g}(k)|
, including for more general ranges of
k
relative to
\mu_{K}
, however the error terms stated for these results are too large for our purposes; we need something that is
o_{g}(g^{K}K^{-1})
. As we only require a bound for
|S_{i}|
when
k_{i}
satisfies
Equation

6
, we are able to use the local limit theorem stated in
Section

3
to get the following bound in this range.

Corollary 2.1
.

For
k_{i}
satisfying
Equation

6
,

|S_{i}|=\frac{g^{K}}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}K}}+O_{g}(g^{K}K^{-3/2}),

where
\sigma^{2}=(g^{2}-1)/12.

Now we outline the proof of
Theorem

1.2
. Let
f_{i}(\theta)
denote the Fourier transform
\widehat{\mathbf{1}_{S_{i}}}(\theta)
, for
i=1,2,3
, so

f_{i}(\theta)\coloneq\sum_{n\in S_{i}}e(n\theta).

Then by orthogonality,

(7)

r_{S_{1}+S_{2}+S_{3}}(M)=\int_{\mathbf{R}/\mathbf{Z}}f_{1}(\theta)f_{2}(\theta)f_{3}(\theta)e(-M\theta)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\theta.

We take the following simple major arcs:

\mathfrak{M}\coloneq\bigcup_{j=0}^{g-2}\Big[\frac{j}{g-1}-\varepsilon,\frac{j}{g-1}+\varepsilon\Big],

where
\varepsilon\coloneq K^{3/4}g^{-K}/(g-1)
throughout. Let the minor arcs be the remaining points,
\mathfrak{m}\coloneq(\mathbf{R}/\mathbf{Z})\setminus\mathfrak{M}
. Intuition for these major arcs can be provided by the results of
de2003counting
;
mauduit2005distribution
;
mauduit1997arithmetic
. These works show that the sets
S_{i}
are well-distributed across the possible residue classes for a given modulus
m
, with the quality of relevant error terms depending on the size of
m
, and whether it is coprime to
g
and
g-1
. The restriction to certain residue classes comes from
Equation

4
, but this is the only congruence restriction. As such, we expect cancellation in
\sum_{n\in S_{i}}e(n\theta)
unless
\theta
is very close to a multiple of
1/(g-1)
. At these points, there cannot be cancellation due to the following relationship, which holds for any
\theta\in\mathbf{R}
and
x\in\mathbf{Z}
,

(8)

f_{i}(\theta+x/(g-1))=e_{g-1}(k_{i}x)f_{i}(\theta).

As such, on intervals around multiples of
1/(g-1)
,
|f_{i}(\theta)|
behaves identically to around
\theta=0
, hence these intervals are included in our major arcs. The relation
Equation

8
follows immediately from
Equation

4
, as

f_{i}\Big(\theta+\frac{x}{g-1}\Big)=\sum_{n\in S_{i}}e\Big(n\Big(\theta+\frac{x}{g-1}\Big)\Big)=\sum_{n\in S_{i}}e(n\theta)e_{g-1}(s_{g}(n)x)=e_{g-1}(xk_{i})f_{i}(\theta).

In
Section

4
we evaluate the contribution to
Equation

7
from the major arcs, as stated in the following proposition.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
K,M\geqslant 1
be integers such that
K
is sufficiently large and
M\in(g^{K-1},g^{K}]
. Then

\int_{\mathfrak{M}}\prod_{i=1}^{3}f_{i}(\theta)e(-M\theta)=\frac{(g-1)M^{2}}{2(2\pi\sigma^{2}K)^{3/2}}+O_{g}(M^{2}(\log K)^{4}K^{-7/4}),

where
\sigma^{2}=(g^{2}-1)/12
.

We show in
Section

5
that
f_{i}(\theta)
is sufficiently small at
\theta\in\mathfrak{m}
so that the contribution to
Equation

7
from the minor arcs is subsumed into the error term of
Theorem

1.2
. Here is a precise statement.

Proposition 2.3
.

For all
\theta\in\mathfrak{m}
,

f_{i}(\theta)\ll_{g}g^{K}K^{-5/4}.

Via Parseval’s identity and the bound for
|S_{i}|
given by
2.1
, this is sufficient to prove that the minor arcs contribute only to the error term in
Theorem

1.2
. In this work, we use a bound on
|f_{i}(\theta)|
due to Fouvry and Mauduit,
fouvry2005entiers
, however we remark that one can find other bounds on exponential sums over sets of integers with fixed digit sums in
de2003counting
;
mauduit2017digits
;
shparlinski2024weyl
.

We apply the aforementioned result of Fouvry and Mauduit
fouvry2005entiers
to bound
f_{i}(\theta)
whenever
\theta
is such that
(g-1)\theta
has many non-zero digits in its
centred
base-
g
expansion. We define the notion of the centred base-
g
expansion in
Section

2.4
; this expansion shifts the range of digits to be centred around zero. This leaves the task of bounding
f_{i}(\theta)
for
\theta
bounded away from translates of
1/(g-1)
, and with the specific form

\theta=\frac{1}{g-1}\Big(\frac{\varepsilon_{m_{1}}}{g^{m_{1}}}+\ldots+\frac{\varepsilon_{m_{t}}}{g^{m_{t}}}+\eta\Big),

where
m_{1}<\ldots<m_{t}\leqslant K
,
t\leqslant\ell
,
\varepsilon_{m_{i}}\in(-\tfrac{g}{2},\tfrac{g}{2}]\cap\mathbf{Z}
and
|\eta|<g^{K}
. We use the fact that
t\leqslant\ell
to approximate the value of
e(n\theta)
for
n\in S_{i}
. It turns out that very little information about
n
is actually needed for this task. Obviously the digits of
n
must sum to
k_{i}
, as
n\in S_{i}
. We show that besides this, we also need to know the value of a very small number of digits of
n
, and the residue class modulo
g-1
of the sum of a fixed subset of digits.

By modelling the digits of
n
as independent copies of a uniform random variable, we replace the condition that
n\in S_{i}
by the probability that the random variables modelling the digits sum to
k_{i}
. We can estimate this term using the local limit theorem. This application of the local limit theorem uses that the target digit sums,
k_{i}
, are within a constant of the actual average digit sum
\mu_{K}=(g-1)K/2
, so that the bounds produced for
f_{1}(\theta),f_{2}(\theta)
and
f_{3}(\theta)
are identical.

Given
2.2
and
2.3
, we can deduce
Theorem

1.2
.

Proof of
Theorem

1.2
.

From
Equation

7
,

(9)

r_{S_{1}+S_{2}+S_{3}}(M)=\int_{\mathbf{R}/\mathbf{Z}}\prod_{i=1}^{3}f_{i}(\theta)e(-M\theta)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\theta.

2.2
gives that the contribution to the above integral from the major arcs provides the main term in
Theorem

1.2
. We show that the contribution from the minor arcs
\mathfrak{m}
is
O_{g}(g^{2K}K^{-7/4})
, and thus these points only contribute to the error term in
Theorem

1.2
. We have

\Big|\int_{\mathfrak{m}}\prod_{i=1}^{3}f_{i}(\theta)e(-M\theta)\Big|\leqslant\sup_{\theta\in\mathfrak{m}}|f_{1}(\theta)|\int_{\mathbf{R}/\mathbf{Z}}|f_{2}(\theta)||f_{3}(\theta)|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\theta.

By Cauchy-Schwarz and Parseval, this is

\leqslant|S_{2}|^{1/2}|S_{3}|^{1/2}\sup_{\theta\in\mathfrak{m}}|f_{1}(\theta)|.

2.1
gives
|S_{i}|\ll_{g}g^{K}K^{-1/2}
for
i=1,2
, and combining this with
2.3
gives

\Big|\int_{\mathfrak{m}}\prod_{i=1}^{3}f_{i}(\theta)e(-M\theta)\Big|\ll_{g}g^{2K}K^{-7/4}.\qed

2.2.
Restricting to Niven numbers with fixed digit sums

To deduce
Theorem

1.3
from
Theorem

1.2
, we show that for
“good”
choices of
k_{1},k_{2},k_{3}
, the number of representations of
M
as
s_{1}+s_{2}+s_{3}
with
k_{i}\mid s_{i}
for each
i
is roughly a
(k_{1}k_{2}k_{3})^{-1}
proportion of the total number of representations. Note that the conditions for the
k_{i}
stated in the introduction give such a choice.

It is shown by De Koninck, Doyon and Kátai in
de2003counting
and independently, Mauduit, Pomerance and Sárközy in
(
mauduit2005distribution
, Theorem C)
, that

(10)

\mathcal{N}_{i}\sim\frac{(k_{i},g-1)}{k_{i}}S_{i}.

The
(k_{i},g-1)
term arises from the congruence relation between
n
and
s_{g}(n)
given in
Equation

4
. We show that indeed the expected proportion of representations are of sums of three Niven numbers, by showing that for appropriate
k_{1},k_{2}
and
k_{3}
,

r_{\mathcal{N}_{1}+\mathcal{N}_{2}+\mathcal{N}_{3}}(M)=r_{S_{1}+S_{2}+S_{3}}(M)\prod_{i=1}^{3}\frac{(k_{i},g-1)}{k_{i}}(1+o_{g}(1)).

To show this, we exploit the fact that
Theorem

1.2
counts the number of ways to write
M
as the sum of three integers with
fixed
near-average digit sums. Let
g_{i}(\theta)
be the Fourier transform of
\mathcal{N}_{i}
,

g_{i}(\theta)\coloneq\sum_{n\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}e(n\theta).

By considering fixed target digit sums
k_{i}
, we gain in that we can relate
g_{i}(\theta)
to
f_{i}(\theta)
, detecting the condition that
k_{i}\mid n
by orthogonality. This reduces the problem to one of understanding the Fourier transform
f_{i}(\theta)
along translates of frequencies by multiples of
1/k_{i}
.

We show in
Section

5
that a strong bound for
f_{i}(\theta)
is available whenever
(g-1)\theta
has many digits in its centred base-
g
expansion. As such, a
“good”
choice of
k_{1},k_{2},k_{3}
requires that their reciprocals, and certain multiples thereof, have many non-zero digits in base
g
. This ensures that
g_{1}(\theta)
,
g_{2}(\theta)
and
g_{3}(\theta)
are only simultaneously large for
\theta\in\mathfrak{M}
, at which point we can use the results of
Section

5
to conclude the proof of
Theorem

1.3
.

2.3.
Probabilistic model for digits

Throughout, we switch to a probabilistic model, viewing the digits of
n
as random variables to model the condition that
s_{g}(n)=k_{i}
by a local limit theorem. To be precise, let us state some notation. Let
Y
be a random variable uniformly taking values in
\{0,\ldots,g-1\}-(g-1)/2
. Throughout,
\sigma^{2}
will denote the variance of
Y
,
\sigma^{2}=(g^{2}-1)/12
. The translation by
-(g-1)/2
is to ensure that
Y
is mean-zero; we will account for this shift where appropriate, and thus note that when
g
is even,
Y
is not integer valued. Let
X_{0},\ldots,X_{K-1}
be independent and identically distributed copies of
Y
, and let

X\coloneq\sum_{j=0}^{K-1}X_{j}g^{j}.

Then
X+(g^{K}-1)/2
is a uniform random integer supported on
\{0,\ldots,g^{K}-1\}
, with
j
th
digit
X_{j}+(g-1)/2
. This follows by the uniqueness of base-
g
expansions, as

X+\frac{g^{K}-1}{2}=\sum_{j=0}^{K-1}\Big(X_{j}+\frac{g-1}{2}\Big)g^{j}\textrm{ and }X_{j}+\frac{g-1}{2}\in\{0,\ldots,\frac{g-1}{2}\}.

Moreover, this means that
s_{g}(X+(g^{K}-1)/2)=\sum_{j=0}^{K-1}X_{j}+\mu_{K}.
For convenience, we define the following mean-zero digit sum function for
X
. For
X=\sum_{j=0}^{K-1}X_{j}g^{j}
, let
s(X)\coloneq\sum_{j=0}^{K-1}X_{j}
. Equivalently,
s(X)=s_{g}(X+(g^{K}-1)/2)-\mu_{K}
. Let

(11)

\xi_{i}\coloneq k_{i}-\mu_{K}.

Now we may replace the sum over
n\in S_{i}
by an average over
X
,

(12)

f_{i}(\theta)=g^{K}e\Big(\frac{g^{K}-1}{2}\theta\Big)\mathbb{E}_{X}e(X\theta)\mathbf{1}_{s(X)=\xi_{i}}.

Recasting
f_{i}(\theta)
as an expectation is not formally needed, but this interpretation as an average is convenient for subsequent sections.

2.4.
Centred base-
g
expansion

We also require the notion of a
centred
base-
g
expansion of a real number, as used by Green in
green2025waring
, where more detail regarding such expansions can be found. The centred base-
g
expansion is closely linked to the regular base-
g
expansion, but shifts the range of permissible digits. Let

\mathcal{R}_{g}\coloneq\{-\tfrac{g-1}{2},\ldots,\tfrac{g-1}{2}\}\textrm{ for odd }g\textrm{ and }\mathcal{R}_{g}\coloneq\{-\tfrac{g-2}{2},\ldots,\tfrac{g}{2}\}\textrm{ for even }g,

and let

(13)

I_{g}\coloneq(-1/2,1/2]\textrm{ for }g\textrm{ odd, and }I_{g}\coloneq\Big(-\frac{g-2}{2(g-1)},\frac{g}{2(g-1)}\Big]\textrm{ for }g\textrm{ even.}

Then for
\alpha\in I_{g}
, if

(14)

\alpha=\sum_{i\geqslant 1}\alpha_{i}g^{-i},\textrm{ with }\alpha_{i}\in\mathcal{R}_{g}\textrm{ for all }i,

we call this the centred base-
g
expansion of
\alpha
. Note that
I_{g}
is the interval for which the centred base-
g
expansion of any element has no integer part, as opposed to
[0,1)
for the regular base-
g
expansion. Let
\mathcal{R}_{g}^{+}=\max\mathcal{R}_{g}
and
\mathcal{R}_{g}^{-}=\min\mathcal{R}_{g}
.
As in the regular expansion, the centred expansion of a real number is unique, except when it ends in an infinite sequence of digits all equal to
\mathcal{R}^{+}
, or all
\mathcal{R}^{-}
. In this case, we would choose the latter representation. The reason for using this alternate notion of expansion is to use the following function, as defined in
green2025waring
.

Definition 2.4
.

Let
w_{K}(\alpha)
be the function counting the number of non-zero digits within the first
K
digits of the centred base-
g
expansion of
\alpha
, after the radix point. For
\alpha

sum to

From

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{Y_{0},\ldots,Y_{g-2}}

\displaystyle e_{g-1}(Y_{1}+\ldots+(g-2)Y_{g-2})\mathbf{1}_{\sum_{j=0}^{g-2}s(Y_{j})=\nu}

.

More precisely, we will partition

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{X_{\mathcal{D}}}e(X_{\mathcal{D}}\theta)\mathbb{E}_{X_{\mathcal{E}}}e(X_{\mathcal{E}}\theta)\mathbf{1}_{s(X_{\mathcal{D}})+s(X_{\mathcal{E}})=\xi}

5.2.

In this section, we prove

Let us restate

.

Lemma

5.7
.

For
\theta\in\tfrac{1}{g-1}I_{g}
suppose that
(g-1)\theta
has centred base-
g
expansion
\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\varepsilon_{i}g^{-i}
. Suppose further that there is an index
m
,
1\leqslant m\leqslant K
, such that
\varepsilon_{m}\neq 0
, and
\varepsilon_{m+1}=\ldots=\varepsilon_{m+T}=0
, for some integer
T\geqslant 0
. Let

be such that
\sum_{1\leqslant j\leqslant m}\varepsilon_{j}\equiv r(\operatorname{mod}\,g-1)
. Then

\mathbb{E}_{Y}e\Big(g^{m-1}Y\Big(\theta-\frac{r}{g-1}\Big)\Big)\ll g^{-T},

where
Y
is a random variable uniformly taking values in
\{0,\ldots,g-1\}-(g-1)/2

Proof.

As
\varepsilon_{m+1}=\ldots=\varepsilon_{m+T}=0
we have

(71)

\theta=\frac{a}{(g-1)g^{m}}+t,

where
a\coloneq\sum_{1\leqslant j\leqslant m}\varepsilon_{j}g^{m-j}
and
t\coloneq\sum_{j\geqslant m+T+1}\varepsilon_{j}g^{-j}
. Note that
a\in\mathbf{Z}
and
a\equiv r(\operatorname{mod}\,g-1)
. We also have
|t|<g^{-m-T}
. Thus

\displaystyle\theta-\frac{r}{g-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{a-g^{m}x}{(g-1)g^{m}}+t=\frac{a^{\prime}}{g^{m}}+t,

where
a^{\prime}\in\mathbf{Z}
. Crucially,
a^{\prime}\not\equiv 0(\operatorname{mod}\,g)
To see this, note that
a^{\prime}\equiv a\equiv\varepsilon_{m}(\operatorname{mod}\,g)
, and by assumption,
\varepsilon_{m}\neq 0
. Using this expression for
\theta
, we have

(72)

e\Big(g^{m-1}Y\Big(\theta-\frac{r}{g-1}\Big)\Big)=e\Big(Y\frac{a^{\prime}}{g}\Big)e(Ytg^{m-1})=e\Big(Y\frac{a^{\prime}}{g}\Big)+O(g^{-T}).

As
Y
uniformly takes values in
\{0,\ldots,g-1\}
and
a^{\prime}\not\equiv 0(\operatorname{mod}\,g)
, averaging
Equation

72
over
Y
gives the bound stated in the lemma, as
\mathbb{E}_{Y}e(Ya^{\prime}/g)=0
.
∎

We now prove
Lemma

5.8
, which we also restate for convenience.

Lemma

5.8
.

For
\theta\in\tfrac{1}{g-1}I_{g}
suppose that
(g-1)\theta
has centred base-
g
expansion
\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\varepsilon_{i}g^{-i}
. Let
T
be an integer such that
0\leqslant T\leqslant K
, and suppose that
\varepsilon_{m-T}=\ldots=\varepsilon_{m-1}=0
for some index
m
,
T\leqslant m\leqslant K
. Let

be such that
\sum_{1\leqslant j\leqslant m}\varepsilon_{j}\not\equiv r(\operatorname{mod}\,g-1)

\mathbb{E}_{Y_{m-T},\ldots,Y_{m-1}}e\Big(\sum_{j=m-T}^{m-1}Y_{j}g^{j}\Big(\theta-\frac{r}{g-1}\Big)\Big)\ll_{g}g^{-T},

where
Y_{m-T},\ldots,Y_{m-1}
are i.i.d. uniform random variables taking values in
\{0,\ldots,g-1\}-(g-1)/2
.

Proof.

The assumptions on the digits of
(g-1)\theta
ensure that
\theta
has the following form:

\theta=\frac{a}{(g-1)g^{m-T-1}}+t,

where
a\not\equiv r(\operatorname{mod}\,g-1)
, and
|t|<g^{-m}
. To obtain this, take
a=\sum_{1\leqslant j\leqslant m-T-1}\varepsilon_{j}g^{j}
, and
t=\sum_{j\geqslant m}\varepsilon_{j}g^{-j}
. Then we have

(73)

\theta-\frac{r}{g-1}=\frac{a^{\prime}}{(g-1)g^{m-T-1}}+t,

where
a^{\prime}=a-g^{m-T-1}r
. All we will require about
a^{\prime}
is that
a^{\prime}\not\equiv 0(\operatorname{mod}\,g-1)
, which follows from the fact that
a^{\prime}\equiv a-r(\operatorname{mod}\,g-1)
.
Let
Y\coloneq\sum_{j=m-T}^{m-1}Y_{j}g^{j}
; this uniformly takes values in
g^{m-T}\{0,\ldots,g^{T}-1\}-g^{m-T}(g^{T}-1)/2
.
Let

\gamma\coloneq e\Big(-g^{m-T}\frac{g^{T}-1}{2}\Big(\theta-\frac{r}{g-1}\Big)\Big).

As
Y
is uniformly distributed, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{Y}e\Big(Y\Big(\theta-\frac{r}{g-1}\Big)\Big)

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma}{g^{T}}\sum_{b=0}^{g^{T}-1}e\Big(g^{m-T}b\Big(\theta-\frac{r}{g-1}\Big)\Big)

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma}{g^{T}}\sum_{b=0}^{g^{T}-1}e\Big(g^{m-T}b\Big(\frac{a^{\prime}}{(g-1)g^{m-T-1}}+t\Big)\Big)

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma}{g^{T}}\sum_{b=0}^{g^{T}-1}e\Big(\frac{ga^{\prime}b}{g-1}+tg^{m-T}b\Big)

(74)

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma}{g^{T}}\frac{e\big(\frac{g^{T+1}a^{\prime}}{g-1}+g^{m}t\big)-1}{e\big(\frac{ga^{\prime}}{g-1}+g^{m-T}t\big)-1}.

As

, we have that
|e(ga^{\prime}/(g-1))-1|\geqslant|1-e(1/(g-1))|\eqqcolon\delta_{g}.
Therefore, for

sufficiently large,

\Big|e\Big(\frac{ga^{\prime}}{g-1}+g^{m-T}t\Big)-1\Big|=\Big|e\Big(\frac{ga^{\prime}}{g-1}\Big)(1+O(g^{-T}))-1\Big|\geqslant\delta_{g}/2.

Thus from
Equation

74
,

\Big|\mathbb{E}_{Y}e\Big(Y\Big(\theta-\frac{r}{g-1}\Big)\Big)\Big|\leqslant\frac{2}{g^{T}|e\big(\frac{ga^{\prime}}{g-1}+g^{m-T}t\big)-1|}\leqslant\frac{4}{\delta_{g}g^{T}}.

∎

Appendix A
Proof of
Theorem

5.1

The direct statement of the exponential sum bound,
Theorem

5.1
, does not appear as a named theorem in
fouvry2005entiers
. Instead, it appears in the proof of their result
(
fouvry2005entiers
, Theorem 1.2
)
. For completeness, we sketch the proof of
Theorem

5.1
here, following the necessary parts of the proof of
(
fouvry2005entiers
, Theorem 1.2)
. Let us restate the result.

Theorem

5.1
.

For
\theta\in\mathbf{R}/\mathbf{Z}
,

|f_{i}(\theta)|\leqslant g^{K}\exp\Big(-\frac{1}{2g}\sum_{i=0}^{K-1}\|g^{i}(g-1)\theta\|_{\mathbf{R}/\mathbf{Z}}^{2}\Big).

Proof.

By using orthogonality to detect the condition that
s_{g}(n)=k_{i}
for
n\in S_{i}
, we may rewrite
f_{i}(\theta)
as follows

f_{i}(\theta)=\sum_{n\in S_{i}}e(n\theta)=\int_{0}^{1}e(-xk_{i})\sum_{n<g^{K}}e(n\theta)e(s(n)x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x.

To bound
|f_{i}(\theta)|
, it suffices to bound

(93)

|f_{i}(\theta)|\leqslant\Big|\int_{0}^{1}\sum_{n<g^{K}}e(n\theta+s(n)x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\Big|.

Note that from now on, the specific target digit sum does not appear in the proof; the only relevance of the exact value of
k_{i}
, in so far as this bound is concerned, is the strength of the bound relative to the trivial bound. To control the right hand side of
Equation

93
, we use the recursive structure of the sum of digits function to isolate the contribution coming from each of the
K
digits of
n<g^{K}
. We have

\sum_{n<g^{K}}e(n\theta+s(n)x)=\prod_{\nu=0}^{K-1}\sum_{j=0}^{g-1}e(jg^{\nu}\theta+jx),

and thus

(94)

|f_{i}(\theta)|\leqslant\int_{0}^{1}\prod_{\nu=0}^{K-1}\Big|\sum_{j=0}^{g-1}e(jg^{\nu}\theta+jx)\Big|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=\int_{0}^{1}\prod_{\nu=0}^{K-1}|U(g^{\nu}\theta+x)|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

where, following the notation of
fouvry2005entiers
,
U(\alpha)\coloneq\sum_{j=0}^{g-1}e(j\alpha)
. The result
(
fouvry2005entiers
, Lemma 3.3)
gives that for any real
t,t_{0}
,

(95)

|U(t)U(t+t_{0})|\leqslant g^{2}\exp\big(-\frac{1}{g}\|t_{0}\|^{2}\big).

We will apply
Equation

95
with
t=g^{\nu}\theta+x
, and
t_{0}=(g-1)g^{\nu}\theta
for
\nu=0,\ldots,K-2
, so that
t+t_{0}=g^{\nu+1}\theta+x
. Crucially, applying
Equation

95
removes the dependency on the variable
x
. We have that

\displaystyle\prod_{\nu=0}^{K-1}|U(g^{\nu}\theta+x)|^{2}

\displaystyle=|U(\theta+x)U(g^{K-1}\theta+x)|\prod_{\nu=0}^{K-2}|U(g^{\nu}\theta+x)U(g^{\nu+1}\theta+x)|

(96)

\displaystyle\leqslant g^{2(K-2)}|U(\theta+x)U(g^{K-1}\theta+x)|\exp\Big(-\frac{1}{g}\sum_{\nu=0}^{K-2}\|(g-1)g^{\nu}\theta\|_{\mathbf{R}/\mathbf{Z}}^{2}\Big).

We obtain

\displaystyle\prod_{\nu=0}^{K-1}|U(g^{\nu}\theta+x)|

\displaystyle\leqslant g^{K-2}|U(\theta+x)U(g^{K-1}\theta+x)|^{1/2}\exp\Big(-\frac{1}{2g}\sum_{\nu=0}^{K-2}\|(g-1)g^{\nu}\theta\|_{\mathbf{R}/\mathbf{Z}}^{2}\Big)

(97)

\displaystyle\leqslant g^{K}\exp\Big(-\frac{1}{2g}\sum_{\nu=0}^{K-1}\|(g-1)g^{\nu}\theta\|_{\mathbf{R}/\mathbf{Z}}^{2}\Big).

The first inequality follows from taking the square root of the expression obtained in
Equation

96
. The final equality comes from the fact that
|U(\alpha)|\leqslant g
for any
\alpha
, and that
g\exp(-\|g^{K-1}(g-1)\theta\|_{\mathbf{R}/\mathbf{Z}}^{2}/2g)\geqslant ge^{-1/8g}\geqslant 1
for
g\geqslant 2
. As this expression no longer depends on
x
, combining
Equation

94
and
Equation

97
gives the bound stated in the theorem.
∎

Appendix B
Local limit theorem

In
Section

3
and
Section

5
we use the following local limit theorem statement,
3.1
. Recall that
X_{0},\ldots,X_{K-1}
are i.i.d. copies of
Y
, which uniformly takes values in
\{0,\ldots,g-1\}-(g-1)/2
.

3.1

(First part)
.

Let
\nu
be an integer. Then

\mathbb{P}(X_{0}+\ldots+X_{K}=\nu)=\frac{e^{-x^{2}/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}K}}+O_{g}(K^{-3/2}),

where
x=\nu/\sqrt{\sigma^{2}K}
, and
\sigma^{2}=(g^{2}-1)/12
is the variance of
Y
.

This can be deduced immediately from far more general local limit theorems, such as Theorem 13 of
(
petrov1972independent
, Ch. VII)
. The theorems in
(
petrov1972independent
, Ch. VII)
allow one to obtain explicit expressions for arbitrarily many lower order terms and allow for much more general i.i.d. random variables. In order to show
3.1
from one such more general theorem, it is necessary to calculate the cumulants of the random variable
Y
to obtain the error term we require. As we are only concerned with a very specific family of uniform distributions, and don’t require lower order terms, we can prove
3.1
directly in this special case. We do so here, following the proofs of the more general theorems given in
petrov1972independent
.

We start by analysing the characteristic function of
Y
. Let
\varphi_{Y}(z)
denote the characteristic function of
Y
,

\displaystyle\varphi_{Y}(z)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{Y}e^{izY}

(98)

\displaystyle=1+\frac{2}{g}\sum_{j=1}^{(g-1)/2}\cos(jz)\textrm{ for odd }g\textrm{, and }\frac{2}{g}\sum_{j=0}^{(g-2)/2}\cos\Big(\frac{2j+1}{2}z\Big)\textrm{ for even }g.

Around
z=0
,
\varphi_{Y}(z)
has the following expansion.

Lemma B.1
.

There exists
\varepsilon_{g}>0
such that for
|z|\leqslant\varepsilon_{g}
, we have
\varphi_{Y}(z)\geqslant 1/2
and

\varphi_{Y}(z)=1-\frac{\sigma^{2}z^{2}}{2}+O_{g}(z^{4}).

Proof.

Let
\varepsilon_{g}>0
be sufficiently small such that

\cos(jz)\geqslant 1/2\textrm{ for }j\in[1,g/2].

For odd
g
, using
Equation

98
we obtain:

\displaystyle\varphi_{Y}(Y)=\frac{1}{g}\Big(1+2\sum_{j=1}\cos(jz)\Big)\geqslant\frac{1}{g}\Big(1+\frac{g-1}{2}\Big)\geqslant\frac{1}{2},

and for even
g
,

\varphi_{Y}(z)=\frac{2}{g}\sum_{j=0}^{(g-2)/2}\cos\Big(\frac{2j+1}{2}z\Big)\geqslant\frac{1}{2},

thus proving the first part of the claim. Moreover,
\varepsilon_{g}
only depends on
g
, and so uniformly in
z
, depending only on
g
, we can expand
\cos(jz)
in this range

\cos(jz)=1-\frac{j^{2}z^{2}}{2}+O_{g}(z^{4}),\textrm{ for }j\in[1,g/2].

Plugging this expansion into
Equation

98
and using that
\sigma^{2}=(g^{2}-1)/12
, we see that for odd
g
,

\displaystyle\varphi_{Y}(z)=\frac{1}{g}+\frac{2}{g}\sum_{j=1}^{(g-1)/2}\cos(jz)

\displaystyle=1-\frac{z^{2}}{g}\sum_{j=1}^{(g-1)/2}j^{2}+O_{g}(z^{4})=1-\frac{\sigma^{2}z^{2}}{2}+O_{g}(z^{4}),

and for even
g
,

\displaystyle\varphi_{Y}(z)=\frac{2}{g}\sum_{j=0}^{(g-2)/2}\cos\Big(\frac{2j+1}{2}z\Big)

\displaystyle=1-\frac{z^{2}}{g}\sum_{j=0}^{(g-2)/2}\big(\frac{2j+1}{2}\Big)^{2}+O_{g}(z^{4})

\displaystyle=1-\frac{\sigma^{2}z^{2}}{2}+O_{g}(z^{4}).\qed

We may now prove
3.1
.

Proof of
3.1
.

By orthogonality,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X_{1}+\ldots+X_{K}=\nu)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Big(\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}e^{-it\nu}e^{it(X_{1}+\ldots+X_{K})}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\Big)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}e^{-it\nu}\varphi_{Y}(t)^{K}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t

(99)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi\sigma K^{1/2}}\int_{-\pi\sigma K^{1/2}}^{\pi\sigma K^{1/2}}e^{-itx}\varphi_{Y}\Big(\frac{t}{\sigma K^{1/2}}\Big)^{K}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t,

where
x=\nu/\sigma K^{1/2}
. To establish
3.1
then, it suffices to show that

(100)

\displaystyle\int_{-\pi\sigma K^{1/2}}^{\pi\sigma K^{1/2}}e^{-itx}\varphi_{Y}\Big(\frac{t}{\sigma K^{1/2}}\Big)^{K}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t=\sqrt{2\pi}e^{-x^{2}/2}+O_{g}(K^{-1}).

We approximate the characteristic function
\varphi_{Y}(t/\sigma K^{1/2})
close to 0 to obtain the main term in
3.1
, and bound the remaining contribution by
K^{-3/2}
. Let
\varepsilon_{g}
be as in
Lemma

B.1
. We show that for
|t|<K^{1/4}\varepsilon_{g}
,

(101)

\Big|\varphi_{Y}\Big(\frac{t}{\sigma K^{1/2}}\Big)^{K}-e^{-t^{2}/2}\Big|\ll_{g}e^{-t^{2}/2}t^{4}K^{-1}.

To show
Equation

101
, we expand
\log\varphi_{Y}(z)
in terms of
z
. For
|z|\leqslant\varepsilon_{g}
, we have
\varphi_{Y}(z)\geqslant 1/2
and in particular,
\varphi_{Y}(z)
is positive in this range so
\log\varphi_{Y}(z)
is defined. For
|z|\leqslant\varepsilon_{g}
, let
w=1-\varphi_{Y}(z)
. We have that
|w|\leqslant 1/2
and thus

\displaystyle\log\varphi_{Y}(z)

\displaystyle=\log(1-w)=-w+O_{g}(w^{2}).

From
Lemma

B.1
and the definition of
w
, we have

(102)

\log\varphi_{Y}(z)=-\frac{\sigma^{2}z^{2}}{2}+O_{g}(z^{4})

for
|z|\leqslant\varepsilon_{g}
. We apply
Equation

102
with
z=t/\sigma K^{1/2}
in the range
|t|\leqslant K^{1/4}\varepsilon_{g}
. Note that
\sigma^{-2}K^{-1/4}\leqslant 1
for all
g\geqslant 2
and
K\geqslant 1
, so in this range,
|t/\sigma K^{1/2}|\leqslant\varepsilon_{g}
.

Hence we have

\log\varphi_{Y}\Big(\frac{t}{\sigma K^{1/2}}\Big)^{K}=-\frac{t^{2}}{2}+O_{g}(t^{4}K^{-1})

which gives

\varphi_{Y}\Big(\frac{t}{\sigma K^{1/2}}\Big)^{K}=e^{-t^{2}/2}\exp\big(O_{g}(t^{-4}K^{-1})\big).

Using that
e(O_{g}(t^{-4}K^{-1}))=1+O_{g}(t^{-4}K^{-1})
, as
|t|\leqslant K^{1/4}\varepsilon_{g}
, gives the bound stated in
Equation

101
. We can now estimate
Equation

99
by evaluating the following integrals:

\displaystyle I_{1}\coloneq\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{itx}e^{-t^{2}/2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t,\hskip 5.0ptI_{2}\coloneq\int_{|t|>K^{1/4}\varepsilon_{g}}e^{itx}e^{-t^{2}/2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle I_{3}\coloneq\int_{|t|<K^{1/4}\varepsilon_{g}}\Big|\varphi_{Y}\Big(\frac{t}{\sigma K^{1/2}}\Big)^{K}-e^{-t^{2}/2}\Big|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t,\hskip 5.0ptI_{4}=\int_{K^{1/4}\varepsilon_{g}\leqslant|t|\leqslant\sigma K^{1/2}}\Big|\varphi_{Y}\Big(\frac{t}{\sigma K^{1/2}}\Big)\Big|^{K}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t.

Thus

\int_{-\pi\sigma K^{1/2}}^{\pi\sigma K^{1/2}}e^{-itx}\varphi_{Y}\Big(\frac{t}{\sigma K^{1/2}}\Big)^{K}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t=I_{1}+O_{g}(I_{2}+I_{3}+I_{4}).

The integral
I_{1}
is

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{itx}e^{-t^{2}/2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t=\sqrt{2\pi}e^{-x^{2}/2}.

Thus in order to show
Equation

100
, it remains to bound the other integral terms. We have

I_{2}\leqslant\int_{|t|>K^{1/4}\varepsilon_{g}}e^{-t^{2}/2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\ll_{g}K^{-10}\textrm{ and }I_{3}\ll\frac{1}{K}\int_{|t|<K^{1/4}\varepsilon_{g}}e^{-t^{2}/2}t^{4}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\ll_{g}\frac{1}{K},

using
Equation

101
for
I_{3}
. Finally we turn to
I_{4}
. For
t\in[K^{1/4}\varepsilon_{g},\sigma K^{1/2}]
, we have that
|\varphi_{Y}(t/\sigma K^{1/2})|\leqslant\varphi_{Y}(\varepsilon_{g}\sigma K^{1/4})
. To see that
\varphi_{Y}(\varepsilon_{g}\sigma K^{1/4})>0
, we use
Lemma

B.1
to expand the characteristic function

\varphi_{Y}(\varepsilon_{g}\sigma K^{1/4})=1-\frac{\varepsilon_{g}^{2}}{2K^{1/2}}+O_{g}(K^{-1})\leqslant 1-\frac{\varepsilon_{g}^{2}}{4K^{1/2}}

for
K
sufficiently large in terms of
g
. Thus

I_{4}\leqslant\int_{|t|\in[K^{1/4}\varepsilon_{g},\sigma K^{1/2}]}\Big(1-\frac{\varepsilon_{g}}{4\sigma^{2}K^{1/2}}\Big)^{K}\leqslant\sigma K^{1/2}\Big(1-\frac{\varepsilon_{g}}{4\sigma^{2}K^{1/2}}\Big)^{K}\ll_{g}K^{-10}.\qed
