Analysis of birefringence modulation performance in RM-embedded CO-polymer waveguides
Authors: Tianyue Wang, Guanghao Huang, Fumin Liu, Xue Zhang, Hui Kong, Shuzhi Sam Ge, Keum-Shik Hong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryUncontrolled birefringence causes measurement errors and signal distortion in high-precision optical systems, yet existing modulation methods suffer from complex fabrication or poor stability. Polymer waveguides serve as low-cost, flexible alternatives to traditional lithium niobate platforms. This work constructs an interferometer with reactive mesogen-embedded polymer waveguide arms to characterize polarization-dependent phase tuning performance via thermal excitation. Measured data reveal high phase modulation efficiencies for two orthogonal light polarizations, ultra-low heating power to generate full polarization phase retardation, and strong polarization tuning capability between modes. The results validate the excellent low-power, high-efficiency birefringence tuning capacity of the composite polymer waveguide architecture, offering a simple, compact route for advanced integrated photonic signal processing and precision interferometry.

Introduction

Birefringence is an optical property of materials characterized by distinct dielectric constants and refractive indices for two orthogonal polarization components of light, resulting in differences in propagation speed and phase variation.1 Moreover, the birefringence exhibits strong wavelength-dependent behavior and decreases sharply with increasing wavelength in the visible region before gradually saturating in the near-infrared region.2 As a crucial optical property, birefringence plays a vital role in diverse fields such as display technology,3 biomedical imaging,4 optical communications,5,6,7,8 and material stress analysis.9,10 Specifically, it finds applications in hologram generation, laser-based 3D displays,11,12 virtual reality (VR) displays,13 wavelength tuning of optical filters,14 as well as mode selection and beam shaping in lasers.15,16,17,18

Birefringence modulation is of pivotal significance in modern optical systems and precision measurement fields, as the adverse effects of high birefringence have become a key bottleneck restricting the performance improvement of various optical devices. While high birefringence exhibits unique advantages in specific scenarios, its detrimental impacts in many high-precision applications highlight the urgency of effective birefringence modulation. Specifically, in precision interferometry, unregulated high birefringence can introduce extra phase shifts, thereby significantly degrading the accuracy of measurement results.19,20,21 In imaging systems that demand high uniformity and consistency, it may lead to image distortion or unwanted coloration, undermining the quality of optical imaging,22 and in optical signal processing technologies such as optical phase modulation and holography, it can cause signal distortion, affecting the reliability of signal transmission and processing.23,24

Although existing techniques including laser direct writing,25 optical waveguide structural design,26,27,28,29 crystal alignment methods,30,31 and material blending32 can partially alleviate these adverse effects, they are plagued by complex processes and stringent fault tolerance requirements, making them difficult to apply in fields that require high precision in birefringence-induced phase retardation. Consequently, the development of a birefringence modulator with a simple structure and easy operation is imperative and indispensable, which further underscores that birefringence modulation is core and essential to advancing high-precision optical technologies.

The critical role of birefringence modulation is further reflected in the limitations of current modulation methods and the pursuit of higher performance. Most traditional birefringence modulation approaches rely on manual adjustment of fiber polarization controllers (PCs),16,17,18 which are inherently limited by low precision, poor repeatability, long adjustment time, low stability, and cumbersome operation—shortcomings that severely hinder their application in high-demand scenarios. To address these deficiencies and achieve more accurate, rapid, and stable birefringence modulation, lithium niobate optical waveguides have been developed and demonstrated as a promising solution.33,34,35 As a core material for birefringence modulation, lithium niobate possesses remarkable advantages such as excellent electro-optical modulation performance, strong compatibility with a broad range of wavelength bands, high modulation precision, and superior stability. These characteristics make it well-suited for meeting the high requirements of advanced optical systems, further confirming that high-performance birefringence modulation is not only necessary but also crucial for promoting the innovation and development of optical technologies in various fields.

As another key material for birefringence modulation, polymers have emerged as a vital alternative to lithium niobate, boasting unique characteristics that make them irreplaceable in advancing birefringence modulation technology. Specifically, polymers enable the fine-tuning of birefringent properties through the precise control of molecular chain structures via chemical synthesis, which provides a flexible and controllable way to tailor their optical performance according to specific modulation requirements.36 They also possess high thermo-optic coefficients (TOCs), a key feature that allows for the effective modulation of their refractive index. This advantage has been fully demonstrated in integrated photonic circuits for optical computing, verifying their practical value in advanced optical systems.37 Additionally, the mechanical properties of polymers can be flexibly tailored by incorporating various fillers, and they support facile fabrication into diverse geometries, as verified in our prior material-property studies.38,39,40 Such favorable processing characteristics make polymers well suited to satisfy the diverse structural demands of photonic devices such as the birefringence modulator. Compared with lithium niobate, polymers offer distinct advantages in cost-effectiveness, miniaturization potential, simplified fabrication processes, and structural flexibility, which make them well-suited for fabricating specialized structures and high-performance birefringence modulators.41,42 As a crucial supplementary material for birefringence modulation, polymers further expand the application boundaries of modulation technology and provide a more diverse solution for the development of high-performance optical modulation systems.

The Mach-Zehnder interferometer (MZI), as a classic optical interference structure, has been widely utilized in phase modulation, optical sensing, and integrated photonics fields, owing to its superior interference performance and structural flexibility. For instance, MZI structures have been successfully employed to achieve high-contrast logic gate operations43 and micro/nano optical fiber sensing,44 MZIs exhibit high sensitivity in refractive index and temperature detection. These studies collectively demonstrate that MZI structures hold significant application value in phase modulation and optical signal processing, laying a solid foundation for their application in birefringence modulation research.

In the previous work, a passive polarization beam splitter was designed based on the total internal reflection phenomenon at the interface between RM and CO-polymer material, which statically separates TE- and TM-polarized light as a single-function passive photonic device.38 In contrast, this study focuses on achieving active birefringence modulation via the thermo-optic effect. Specifically, a CO-series polymer (CO-polymer) waveguide MZI structure is proposed, with reactive mesogen (RM) optical waveguides embedded in both its upper and lower arms, aiming to analyze the birefringence modulation performance of RM-embedded CO-polymer waveguides. By applying heating power to the micro-heater mounted on the arms of the MZI, interference signals corresponding to the transverse electric (TE) and transverse magnetic (TM) modes were acquired at the output terminal. By comparing the phase variation characteristics of these two interference signals, the phase retardation introduced by the applied heating power was quantified. Notably, the proposed structure with RM waveguides embedded in CO-series polymer waveguides can realize π-phase retardation between TE and TM modes under extremely low heating power, demonstrating its great potential for high-efficiency, low-power birefringence modulation.

Operating principle and device design

To analyze the birefringence modulation performance of RM-embedded CO-polymer waveguides, a CO-polymer waveguide MZI structure is proposed, with RM waveguides embedded in both its upper and lower arms, as shown in Figure 1.Figure 1Schematic of the MZI copolymer waveguide, where RM waveguides are embedded in both the upper and lower arms to characterize birefringence modulation performance

Schematic of the MZI copolymer waveguide, where RM waveguides are embedded in both the upper and lower arms to characterize birefringence modulation performance

The core and cladding of the proposed MZI are composed of two CO-polymers, namely CO-154 and CO-152 (purchased from Chemoptics Co., Ltd., South Korea). At a wavelength of 1550 nm, the refractive indices of CO-154 and CO-152 are 1.54 and 1.52, respectively, with a birefringence of 0.001 for both. The tapered optical waveguide embedded in the CO-polymer waveguide is fabricated using RM. The RM material adopted in this work is RMS03-013C from Merck. Measured at 589 nm and room temperature (25°C), its ordinary and extraordinary refractive indices are no = 1.543 and ne = 1.680, respectively, yielding a large birefringence of 0.137. RM is a kind of polymerizable liquid crystal monomer that can form ordered anisotropic structures via rubbing alignment and permanently lock its liquid-crystal orientation after UV curing to fabricate solid birefringent optical waveguides.45 Previous studies have shown that the refractive index of RM is 1.536 for the TM mode, while its refractive index for the TE mode is estimated to be 1.673.38

For TE mode input, light is evenly split by the Y-branch into the upper and lower CO-polymer waveguides of the MZI, after which it couples into the straight RM waveguides through the tapered RM structure. In contrast, for TM mode input, since the refractive index of the CO-polymer core material (1.54) is higher than that of RM, light is primarily confined within the CO-polymer waveguides. For the TE mode, however, the refractive index of the RM material is significantly higher than that of CO-154, which easily induces multimode propagation and thus reduces coupling efficiency. To mitigate such coupling loss, a tapered RM waveguide structure was adopted, as illustrated in Figure 2A. This structure enables the large-sized guided mode in the CO-polymer waveguide to efficiently couple to the small-sized mode of the RM waveguide. For the TE mode, the coupling loss between the CO-polymer waveguide and the RM waveguide was calculated using the three-dimensional (3D) beam propagation method (BPM), with the results presented in Figure 2B. The CO-polymer waveguide has a width of 6 μm, a thickness of 4 μm, and a lateral core thickness of 2 μm, while the RM waveguide has a width of 4 μm and a thickness of 1.2 μm. The calculated results show that the tapered RM waveguide with a taper end width below 0.5 μm and a length exceeding 600 μm achieves a coupling loss lower than 0.5 dB.Figure 2Illustration of the mode coupling characteristics between CO-polymer and RM waveguides(A) Mode coupling region between the guided modes of the CO-polymer and RM waveguides.(B) Calculation of coupling loss for TE mode using the 3D BPM.

Device fabrication

The proposed device is fabricated via planar lightwave circuit (PLC) technology. Starting with a bare silicon wafer substrate, a CO-152 lower cladding film is sequentially deposited through spin-coating followed by UV curing. A polyimide layer is then overlaid and mechanically rubbed to induce directional anisotropic nanogrooves. Afterward, RM material is spin-coated and cured, then lithographically patterned and etched via oxygen plasma to define channel waveguides and tapered tip structures. Subsequently, the CO-154 core layer is deposited and dry-etched to a target ridge thickness, fully embedding the RM-structured waveguide inside the core film. The CO-152 upper cladding layer is then spin-coated and cured to complete full encapsulation of the waveguide stack. A bilayer of chromium and gold (Cr/Au) is next deposited using thermal evaporation, after which wet-etching patterns are integrated into micro-heaters. Characterization of the fabricated device

To measure the propagation losses of the RM and CO-polymer waveguides, straight waveguide structures of RM and CO-polymer were fabricated, as shown in Figures 4A and 4B, respectively. Consistent with the refractive index characteristics discussed earlier, for the TE mode, the refractive index of the RM material is higher than that of CO-154, enabling effective light confinement within the RM waveguide. In contrast, for the TM mode, the refractive index of the RM material is lower than that of CO-154, resulting in poor light confinement in the RM waveguide. Notably, both TE and TM modes are well confined in the CO-polymer waveguide structure due to its stable refractive index distribution. Therefore, TE-polarized light was selected as the input source to compare the propagation losses of the straight RM and CO-polymer waveguides under the same confinement condition. The propagation losses of the RM and CO-polymer waveguides were measured using the cutback method, yielding values of 3.35 dB/cm and 1.1 dB/cm at 1550 nm, respectively. It is evident that the propagation loss of the RM waveguide is significantly higher than that of the CO-polymer waveguide. The primary loss mechanisms in the RM waveguide are attributed to material absorption and the polyimide rubbing process. After rubbing, slight indentations are formed on the polyimide surface, which act as scattering centers and induce additional light scattering loss.Figure 4Cross-sectional structures and propagation loss characteristics of straight waveguides(A) The cross-view of straight RM waveguide.(B) The cross-view of a straight CO-polymer waveguide.(C) Propagation loss of straight RM and CO-polymer waveguides.

However, mode mismatch arises when the single-mode light from the CO-polymer waveguide couples into the RM waveguide, which supports higher-order modes. Imperfect mode conversion during coupling therefore excites distinct higher-order mode spots in the output field. To evaluate the birefringence modulation performance, a triangular voltage waveform with a 0.3 s period generated by the function generator was applied to the micro-heater on one arm of the MZI. A triangular voltage generates a linear temperature variation, which further induces a linear phase shift in the MZI arm through the thermo-optic effect. This enables direct observation of complete interference cycles and verification of the thermo-optic tuning linearity. The corresponding output interference signals for TE and TM modes are therefore acquired, as shown in Figures 6B and 6C, respectively. By analyzing the phase difference of these interference signals, we can characterize how the phase modulation difference between TE and TM modes varies with thermal power. Furthermore, from the modulation efficiency, we can clearly observe that the TE mode exhibits significantly higher efficiency than the TM mode. This not only confirms that TE and TM polarizations follow different optical paths in the device, but also demonstrates that they experience different TOCs in the waveguide materials.

The phase changes of the TE and TM modes as a function of heating power were calculated based on the output interference signals, with the results presented in Figure 7A. By analyzing the interference signals dependent on thermal power, the phase changes corresponding to different heating powers can be extracted. Using this phase change data, the phase modulation efficiency of the device was further calculated. Comparing the phase responses of TE and TM modes under identical heating power reveals a higher phase modulation efficiency for TE. This demonstrates that the TE mode possesses a greater TOC than the TM mode. Specifically, phase modulation efficiencies of 0.32 rad/mW and 0.21 rad/mW were achieved for the TE and TM modes, respectively; the corresponding thermal powers required to induce a π-phase shift were 9.77 mW and 14.8 mW, respectively. As the heating power increases, the phase retardation between the two modes gradually increases, yielding a phase retardation efficiency of 0.1 rad/mW and a relatively low power consumption of 30.7 mW for achieving π-phase retardation. To determine the TO coefficient of the birefringent polymer waveguide, the effective refractive index change corresponding to the modulated phase difference was also calculated as a function of temperature, as shown in Figure 7B. For the achievement of π-phase retardation, the micro-heater induces a temperature change of 72.1°C, with TO coefficients of −1.07 × 10−5/°C and −7.15 × 10−6/°C obtained for the TE and TM polarizations, respectively. Overall, a high birefringence modulation efficiency of 3.58 × 10−6/°C was achieved in the RM-embedded CO-polymer waveguides, further verifying the superior performance of the proposed structure for high-efficiency, low-power birefringence modulation.Figure 7Calculated phase and effective index variations under thermal modulation(A) The calculated phase changes of TE and TM modes as a function of heating power.(B) The calculated effective index change as a function of temperature.

Discussion

The experimental characterization of the MZI built with RM-incorporated CO-polymer waveguides clearly demonstrates the superiority of this composite waveguide architecture for thermally controlled birefringence modulation. Compared with traditional birefringence modulation techniques limited by complicated fabrication, poor stability, or low tuning precision, the proposed polymer platform delivers distinct merits including simple processing, structural flexibility, and low cost. Quantified phase modulation metrics reveal high thermal phase tuning efficiencies for both TE and TM polarizations, alongside an ultralow power demand to produce full π phase retardation between the two orthogonal modes.

Limitations of the study

Although the RM-embedded CO-polymer waveguide interferometer achieves favorable low-power birefringence modulation performance, this polymer-based photonic platform still has several inherent limitations compared with mainstream inorganic integrated photonics materials. First, mesogen-embedded CO-polymer waveguides suffer from relatively high optical propagation loss, which restricts their use in long-distance on-chip optical signal transmission. Second, polymer materials possess weaker optical confinement capability than silicon, so the footprint of mesogen-embedded CO-polymer waveguide devices is notably larger than silicon-based counterparts, hindering ultra-dense integration of photonic chips. Moreover, the polymer matrix exhibits poor thermal conductivity. Slow heat transfer inside the waveguide lengthens the material response time and limits the overall modulation speed. Such drawbacks make the proposed device less competitive for ultra-high-speed optical communication and high-density integrated photonic circuits, which will be the focus of our follow-up optimization work.

Method details

Device design and simulation

The proposed MZI device consists of an input Y-branch, two symmetric modulation arms, an output Y-branch, RM waveguide structures embedded in the two modulation arms, and micro-heaters on both arms. The waveguide adopts a ridge structure, with the core layer made of CO-154 and the cladding layer made of CO-152. The RM waveguide embedded within the CO-polymer waveguide core layer adopts a channel waveguide structure with a tapered transition design.

The taper length and taper end width are optimized using the three-dimensional beam propagation method (3D BPM). The simulations are performed using the BeamPROP module of the RSoft Photonics Suite. One arm of the MZI structure is selected as the simulation object, and a 3D CAD model is built based on the above structural parameters. The launch field is generated by calculating the fundamental TE-polarized eigenmode of the CO-polymer ridge waveguide at a wavelength of 1550 nm using the mode solver in BeamPROP (Compute Mode). The pathway monitor is used to monitor the zero-order TE mode. The transverse grid sizes are Δ(=Δ = = 0.05 μ = , and the longitudinal grid size is Δ = 0.2 μ = . The boundary condition is set to the Simple Transparent Boundary Condition (Simple TBC). Simulation results show that when the taper length is 800 μm and the taper end width is 0.1 μa, the coupling loss for the TE mode is below 0.5 dB. These optimized parameters are adopted for device fabrication in this work.

Device fabrication

The proposed integrated optical device, designed for analyzing the birefringence modulation performance of RM-embedded CO-polymer waveguides, was fabricated using planar lightwave circuit (PLC) technology. The entire fabrication process is schematically illustrated in Figure 3, with detailed steps as follows: First, a 7 μm-thick lower cladding layer of CO-152 was formed on a silicon wafer by spin-coating at 1000 rpm, followed by ultraviolet (UV) curing, as shown in Figure 3A. Next, a 0.2 μm-thick polyimide layer was spin-coated at 4500 rpm onto the lower cladding, cured at 250°C for 30 min, and then rubbed with a velvet roller to achieve alignment, as depicted in Figure 3B. Subsequently, an RM solution was spin-coated at 2000 rpm onto the rubbed polyimide surface and UV-cured to form a 1.2 μm-thick RM film. After depositing a photoresist layer on the aligned RM film, a tapered RM waveguide pattern was defined through photolithography and oxygen plasma etching processes with ICP and RF power of 100 and 50 W, as shown in Figure 3C. The ultra-narrow taper tip is not directly patterned by UV lithography. Instead, it is naturally formed by controlled lateral undercut etching during wet etching process after conventional UV photolithography. This etching-induced tapering effect gradually shrinks the waveguide end into a sharp sub-100 nm tip, which breaks the apparent diffraction limit of traditional UV lithography and enables sharp tip fabrication without electron beam lithography (EBL). A 4 μm-thick CO-154 layer was then coated at 2800 rpm over the tapered RM waveguide pattern, serving as the core layer for the TM mode; this CO-154 layer was subsequently etched to a depth of 2.1 μm via a second photolithography step combined with oxygen plasma etching, as shown in Figure 3D. Thereafter, a CO-152 cladding layer was spin-coated at 2000 rpm and cured to encapsulate the core and RM waveguide structures, as presented in Figure 3E. Finally, a chromium-gold (Cr–Au) bilayer film was deposited, and a 20 μm-wide, 1000 μm-long micro-heater was formed using a third photolithography process and wet etching, thereby completing the entire device fabrication, as shown in Figure 3F. For the fabricated MZI, each arm is 5504 μm long, and the coupling region/taper is 800 μm in length.

Quantification and statistical analysis

This work employs a CO-polymer MZI interferometric structure with RM waveguides embedded in both arms, and applies heating power to only one interference arm to achieve thermo-optic modulation. The incident light excites TE-polarized and TM-polarized modes, respectively, to propagate within the waveguide. After passing through the heated arm, both polarization modes undergo thermally induced phase shifts. The phase in the unheated reference arm remains constant. The optical fields from the two arms interfere, and by extracting the interference output curves under different heating powers, the total phase changes for the TE mode Δ∅TE, and for the TM mode Δ∅TM, are obtained separately. The formula for the thermally induced phase change of a single polarization mode is:(Equation 1)Δ∅=2πλ·Δneff(P)·L,where λ is the incident light wavelength, L is the length of the waveguide in the heated region, Δneff(P) is the effective refractive index change of the waveguide induced by the heating power P, and Δ∅ is the accumulated phase shift for the corresponding polarization mode. The heating power P and the waveguide temperature rise ΔT are linearly related:(Equation 2)ΔT=α·P,where α is the heating-power-to-temperature conversion coefficient, defined as following:49(Equation 3)α=tL∗w∗k∗(1+0.88∗tw),where t is the thickness of the polymer film, L and w are the length and width of the heater, and k is the thermal conductivity of the polymer material.

Differentiating the phase formula yields the slope of the phase change with respect to the heating power:(Equation 4)dΔ∅dP=2πLλ·dneffdT·dTdP,rearranging gives the respective thermo-optic coefficients for TE and TM:(Equation 5)TOCTE=dneff,TEdT,(Equation 6)TOCTM=dneff,TMdT,by linearly fitting the Δ∅TE-P and Δ∅TM-P curves to obtain the slope dΔ∅/dP, and substituting them into the above equations, the thermo-optic coefficients for the TE and TM modes can be determined separately.

At the same heating power, the difference in phase shifts between the TE and TM modes is the phase retardation Δϴ(P), which reflects the thermally induced birefringence phase difference:(Equation 7)Δϴ(P)=Δ∅TE(P)−Δ∅TM(P)
