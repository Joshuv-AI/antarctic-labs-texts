Effect of cerium oxide nanocatalyst on performance emissions and noise of diesel biodiesel blends in a variable compression ratio engine
Authors: M. Anish, Mohd Majid, T. M. Thamizh Thentral, Arun Raaza, Aravind Kumar, J. R. Deepak, Mohammad Kanan, T. Sathish, J. Jayaprabakar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
A recent study investigates the impact of adding biodiesel to petrol-diesel fuel blends and the use of catalysts on engine performance and emissions. This work focuses on cerium oxide nanoparticles incorporated into B10 and B20 fuel blends, where B10 consists of 90% diesel and 10% biodiesel derived from waste frying oil. The effects of these nanoparticles at concentrations of 20, 50, and 80 ppm were evaluated using a single-cylinder Variable Compression Ratio (VCR) engine. Tests were conducted at engine speeds of 1,500, 2,000, and 2,500 rpm to measure torque, power, brake thermal efficiency (BTE), specific fuel consumption (SFC), exhaust gas temperature (EGT), and emissions of CO, CO₂, unburned hydrocarbons (UHC), and NOx. The results revealed improvements of 6.43%, 6.46%, 12.78%, and 6.93% in torque, power, BTE, and EGT, respectively, compared to pure diesel. Fuel economy increased by 12.20%. Emissions of CO and UHC decreased by 1.84% and 18.33%, respectively, while NOx emissions increased by 48.43%. The study also identified a strong correlation between CO, NOx, and oxygen levels, with an inverse relationship observed between CO and NOx emissions.

Introduction

The energy demand has surged due to rapid industrial expansion and population growth. This escalating demand for fossil fuels is depleting these resources at an accelerated rate. Despite their dwindling availability, the combustion of fossil fuels continues to have a significant impact on the environment, primarily through a sharp increase in greenhouse gas emissions. Additionally, this scenario has increased gasoline prices and intensified the quest for alternative energy sources1. Primary energy carriers include conventional fuels, including oil, nuclear energy, natural gas, and coal. Moreover, wind, sun, and hydropower are other primary energy sources classified as renewable energy sources. Biofuels include a diverse range of alternatives derived from biomass. The options include vegetable oil, bio-hydrogen, biodiesel, bio-alcohol, and natural energy. Among them, biodiesel is particularly advantageous for use in transportation and combustion engines. It may be used either as a direct substitute or as a constituent of a fuel blend to replace fossil fuels in motor vehicle engines2,3. Recent research suggests that feedstocks derived from microalgae have the potential to be a viable alternative to fossil fuels, which are being rapidly depleted. Both ponds, which serve as wastewater treatment facilities and freshwater habitats, are conducive to the growth of algae. Fatty acid methyl esters are produced by transesterifying various oil feedstocks, including waste cooking oil, animal fats, vegetable oils, and microalgal lipids, using acid and alcohol catalysts. The combustion efficiency of these esters can be enhanced by incorporating nano-additives, such as titanium dioxide (TiO2).

As a consequence of these oxides, the calorific value of fuel blends is enhanced, leading to more efficient fuel combustion. Nevertheless, when biodiesel is used in diesel engines, its decreased volatility and increased viscosity might lead to challenges in achieving proper mixture formation, spray penetration, and atomization. These challenges may pose significant issues specifically for diesel engines4,5.

As a result of these issues, there is an increase in gasoline consumption, smoke emissions, and engine deposits. Blending, transesterification, oil heating, air preheating, micro-emulsification, and nano-additives are among the methods employed to address the challenges associated with using biodiesel in diesel engines. Recent advancements have shown that nano-additives hold promise as a viable solution. Many studies have focused on using metal-based nano-additives to address the limitations associated with biodiesel6. Nanoparticles often provide substantial benefits compared to particles on the scale of micrometres in numerous applications. A significant advantage is the increased reactive surface area, which improves combustion efficiency and reduces ignition delays7. Nano additives based on metal oxides have demonstrated improvements in performance, reduced emissions, enhanced combustion characteristics, and a decrease in total emissions. Nanoparticles improve the thermal and physical properties of biofuels and accelerate oxidation reactions8,9.

Multiple research investigations have explored the use of nanoparticles in biodiesel to enhance engine efficiency and reduce emissions. For example, a study investigated the characteristics of palm biodiesel and its effects on engine performance by analysing TiO₂ nanoparticles. Adding TiO₂ to biodiesel resulted in a 2.43% point improvement in brake thermal efficiency compared to pure diesel. The use of nanoparticles as oxidation catalysts enhanced the efficiency of combustion, resulting in reduced emissions of CO, hydrocarbons (HC), NOx, and smoke10,11. An investigation was conducted on diesel engines that utilised a combination of castor methyl ester (CME) biodiesel and diesel fuel, along with nanoparticle dispersions. The results showed that adding 25 parts per million (ppm) of nanoparticles to a 20% CME blend led to an enhancement in brake thermal efficiency (BTE) and a reduction in brake-specific fuel consumption (BSFC). This combination also resulted in decreases in hydrocarbon (HC) and carbon monoxide (CO) emissions12.

Furthermore, a study investigating the use of alumina nanoparticles in biodiesel from candle nut and soap nut showed improvements in brake thermal efficiency (BTE), reductions in brake-specific fuel consumption (BSFC), and decreased emissions13. A further investigation, combining cerium oxide nanoparticles and maize oil biodiesel, revealed a reduction in carbon monoxide (CO) and unburned hydrocarbons (UBHC), but with a slight increase in nitrogen oxide (NOx) emissions14. Additionally, research was conducted to investigate the acoustic emissions of a compression ignition (CI) engine and the effect of frequency on magnetic biodiesel-diesel mixtures15. A study on the use of marginatum biodiesel-diesel blends found significant performance enhancements and reduced exhaust emissions16. With the imminent threat of fuel shortages, the transition from depleting oil supplies to sustainable fuels such as biodiesel presents encouraging progress. Further research investigation examined the impact of carbon nanotube nanoparticles on biodiesel blends and found a significant decrease in detrimental exhaust emissions, namely NOx17. Several studies that used biodiesel-diesel mixes with exhaust gas recirculation systems have successfully reduced emissions, ranging from 25% to 75%18. Biodiesel, a fuel with comparable combustion characteristics to petroleum diesel but with reduced emissions, is produced from several sources, including soybean, rapeseed, palm oil, and neem oil. Research in industrialised nations is increasingly prioritising renewable energy and innovative technology. From an economic standpoint, biofuels are becoming an increasingly viable alternative to fossil fuels19,20.

Researchers have incorporated nanoparticles into diesel and biodiesel blends to enhance engine performance and reduce hazardous emissions. For instance, researchers conducted a study where ZnO nanoparticles were added to biodiesel and ethanol blends in a diesel engine to enhance its performance21. Aluminium nanoparticles, serving as promising energy transporters, can increase the initial combustion temperature, possibly reducing ignition delay and improving combustion efficiency22. Incorporating ceria nanoparticles into diesel emulsion fuel resulted in substantial decreases in particulate matter, carbon dioxide, and unburned hydrocarbons23. Using alumina and silica nanoparticles in diesel-butanol blends enhanced the performance of compression ignition (CI) engines and decreased emissions of soot particles24. Introducing aluminium nanoparticles into diesel fuel at varying levels significantly reduced fuel consumption, NOx emissions, and particulate matter emissions25. A separate investigation included alumina and cupric oxide nanoparticles in diesel fuel, improving combustion characteristics26. Using nano-additives has demonstrated the ability to enhance the thermophysical characteristics of fuels by leveraging their large surface-to-volume ratio. Additionally, these additives serve as oxygen buffers, effectively reducing NOx emissions27.

Metal-based nano-additives have led to a notable decrease in fuel consumption and emissions. This is achieved by generating hydroxyl radicals, which increase the soot oxidation process and thus lower the oxidation temperature28. Kumar et al. investigated the effects of metal and metal oxides on diesel/biodiesel nanoparticles (with sizes ranging from 10 to 20 nm). They observed enhancements in flash point, volatility, and viscosity29. Jaikumar et al. conducted a study on the impact of adding copper chloride (CuCl2) and cobalt chloride (CoCl2) nanoparticles to diesel-based mixtures at concentrations of 25/50 parts per million (ppm) in a single-cylinder compression ignition (CI) engine3. Mofijur et al. utilised Al2O3 and Ce-ZnO to enhance the efficiency and reduce exhaust emissions from an engine fueled by biodiesel. This resulted in a 0.1% enhancement in the quality of nano-fuels, a decrease in kinematic viscosity, and an increase in cetane number30. Syed et al. studied the incorporation of titanium dioxide and Magnesium Oxide (MgO) nanoparticles into regular diesel fuel. They observed enhancements in density, fire point, viscosity, and calorific value31. Sanjeevaraoet al.32. Analysed the efficiency and pollution levels of a compression ignition (CI) engine powered by cottonseed-based biodiesel. They used Al2O3 nanoparticles at concentrations ranging from 25 to 100 parts per million (ppm) in biodiesel emulsion fuels. Compared to diesel, the B20ANP100ppm blend exhibits superior performance and reduced emissions. India has lately started producing and using biodiesel. However, the general use of higher biodiesel blends with diesel fuel remains restricted. Many Asian nations, particularly India, have adopted a long-term policy of blending small amounts of biodiesel with diesel fuel to reduce emissions. India is making progress in manufacturing nanocomponents and seeks comprehensive research to assess the effects of gasoline inclusions on engine performance, specifically to tackle pollution problems in large urban areas. Prior studies have investigated the use of titanium oxide, iron/strontium, copper, graphene, and other substances to augment combustion and enhance the reactivity of engine emissions. This research aims to examine the use of alumina nanoparticles in diesel-biodiesel mixtures to improve combustion efficiency. The objective is to evaluate the impact of cerium oxide (CeO2) nanoparticles on the efficiency and emissions of diesel engines. Existing research has not adequately explored the use of cerium nanoparticles in diesel-biodiesel fuel blends (B10 and B20), underscoring the need for further investigation into their performance and emission properties.

Materials and methods

Experimental set-up

Experiments were carried out using an eddy-current dynamometer on a single-cylinder, four-stroke, water-cooled VCR diesel engine (Kirloskar TV-1) at constant speeds of 1500, 2000, and 2500 revolutions per minute (rpm) under load conditions of 25%, 50%, 75%, and 100%. Each test had a compression ratio of 17.5:1, an injection pressure of 205 bar, and an injection timing of 23 degrees below the center of gravity. An orifice meter was used in combination with a U-tube manometer to measure intake air flow, and gravimetric techniques were used to calculate fuel flow rate. Each experiment was repeated three times for analytical purposes, with the mean results utilized. Table 1 provides a detailed description of the experimental setup, including the engine specifications and the equipment used in this work. The ambient temperature was measured at 220 degrees Celsius, while the atmospheric pressure was recorded at 878 millibars36. The engine was operational under these circumstances. An eddy current dynamometer was used to ascertain the load and torque experienced by the engine. Based on the data in Tables 1 and 2, an exhaust gas analysis was conducted using a portable AVL petrol/gas toxicity tester. Table 2 contains further details on the accuracy of the measurements and the associated degree of uncertainty. Each test fuel was assessed three times to achieve optimal accuracy, with the mean results used for future computations. Several variables, including validation procedures, observational methods, equipment calibration, working conditions, and environmental exposures, may influence experimental accuracy. These factors contribute to possible sources of error and uncertainty. To ensure the precision of measured and calculated values, it is crucial to consistently evaluate the uncertainty of the variables using an analytical method37. In Table 3, uncertainties were calculated for various measured variables, including both fixed and random errors that may have occurred during equipment production. This study performed experiments at three distinct engine speeds: 1,500, 2000, and 2500 revolutions per minute (rpm). Each speed was evaluated under full load conditions, with three repetitions conducted for each speed to ensure the reliability and precision of the results. A comprehensive set of parameters was recorded during each test, including torque, power output, fuel consumption, specific fuel consumption (SFC), exhaust gas temperature (EGT), ambient temperature, ambient pressure, and environmental humidity. All collected data were subsequently transferred to a computer for detailed analysis. Before initiating the data collection, the engine was run for 10 min to clear any residual fuel from previous blends in the fuel lines. This step was essential to ensure that leftover fuel did not influence the results. Once the warm-up period was completed, data collection for each fuel mixture began. This procedure was rigorously followed for all fuel blends to maintain consistency and accuracy. Each test was conducted under strict supervision to avoid errors. Following the initial round of tests, any discrepancies were identified, and additional tests were performed to verify the accuracy of the results. Additionally, failure analyses were conducted on specific test series to investigate and address any issues encountered during the experiments thoroughly. Fuel preparation

Biodiesel was produced from waste cooking oil through transesterification, in accordance with ASTM D6751 specifications. Two primary blends were prepared: B10 (90% conventional diesel and 10% biodiesel by volume) and B20 (80% diesel and 20% biodiesel). To enhance fuel properties, cerium oxide (CeO₂) nanoparticles (beta phase, 99% pure, 10 nm in diameter) were added to the blends at three different concentrations: 20, 50, and 80 ppm by weight. Cerium oxide nanoparticles (CeO₂, β-phase, 10 ± 2 nm, 99% purity, Sigma-Aldrich) were weighed using an analytical microbalance with an accuracy of ± 0.1 mg. To ensure preliminary homogeneity, 100 mL of biodiesel was pre-dispersed with each nanoparticle dose (20, 50, and 80 ppm w/w) using a magnetic stirrer set at 1000 rpm for 30 min. The pre-dispersion was then subjected to ultrasonication with a 7 mm diameter titanium probe for 15 min at 40 °C to prevent agglomeration, using a UP200St model operating at 26 kHz and 200 W. The resulting stable dispersion was subsequently mixed with petro-diesel to prepare B10 and B20 blends, while continuously stirring for an additional 20 min.The morphology and size distribution of the CeO₂ nanoparticles were analyzed using scanning electron microscopy (JEOL JSM-7610 F), while X-ray diffraction (Cu-Kα, λ = 1.5406 Å) confirmed the crystallinity and phase purity of the nanoparticles. The stability of the fuel-nanoparticle dispersions was evaluated after 72 h through visual inspection and UV-Vis spectrophotometry (Shimadzu UV-2600, λ = 340 nm), showing no signs of sedimentation and indicating high nanoparticle stability within the blends. The blends were labelled based on their composition—for example, B10CE20 indicates B10 with 20 ppm CeO₂, while B20CE80 refers to B20 with 80 ppm CeO2. An ultrasonic disperser was used to ensure even distribution of nanoparticles. Finally, key fuel properties—including density, viscosity, heating value, and cetane index—were evaluated according to ASTM standards to assess quality. Table 4 presents the detailed measurements of various parameters for petro-diesel and biodiesel fuels, as evaluated according to ASTM standards. The cetane index of the fuel blends varied significantly, with pure diesel having a cetane index of 49 and biodiesel reaching a maximum of 58. This image showcases the high-resolution surface details captured by the SEM, including features such as surface texture, morphology, and structural characteristics. The X-ray diffraction (XRD) technique was employed to analyse the structural properties of the Cerium oxide nanoparticles. XRD measures the scattering of X-rays by the crystalline structures of the nanoparticles, allowing for the determination of their crystal structure, phase composition, crystallite size, and preferred orientation. This technique provides valuable insights into the material’s structural characteristics, which are essential for understanding how these nanoparticles will interact with fuel blends. The research involved creating various fuel blends, including B10 (comprising 10% biodiesel and 90% diesel) and B20 (comprising 20% biodiesel and 80% diesel). Cerium nanoparticles were introduced into these blends at concentrations of 20, 50, and 80 parts per million (ppm). The nanoparticles were incorporated into the fuel blends using an ultrasonic device for 15 min to ensure thorough mixing and achieve a homogeneous emulsion. This ultrasonic mixing process facilitated the even distribution of nanoparticles throughout the fuel mixture. Nine different fuel blends were prepared for experimental analysis: B0 (pure diesel), B10, B20, B10CE20, B10CE50, B10CE80, B20CE20, B20CE50, and B20CE80. These blends underwent comprehensive testing to determine key fuel properties, including dynamic and kinematic viscosity, density, higher heating value (HHV), lower heating value (LHV), and cetane index. These properties were measured immediately following the preparation of each fuel blend to ensure accuracy and consistency. Additionally, the stability of each fuel blend was evaluated over a 72-hour period after preparation. To assess the stability of cerium oxide nanoparticles in the biodiesel-diesel blends, both visual and analytical methods were used. Over a period of 72 h, the blends were periodically checked for signs of particle settling or agglomeration. Alongside these observations, UV-Vis spectrophotometry was employed to track absorbance changes, providing a quantitative measure of nanoparticle dispersion stability. This dual approach ensured a thorough evaluation, confirming that the nanoparticles remained well-dispersed with minimal sedimentation during testing, as per ASTM D7467 guidelines. Evaluating the safety and performance of these fuel blends in diverse applications depends on factors such as density, viscosity, flash point, and other key characteristics. The measurements must be precise, reliable, and consistent to facilitate accurate comparisons and assessments of different fuel types. The ASTM standards are crucial in ensuring these measurement requirements are fulfilled. This stability assessment was essential for understanding how the addition of Cerium nanoparticles might affect the long-term usability and performance of fuel blends. The results of these tests provide valuable insights into the effects of nanoparticle addition on the associated combustion features of such fuel mixtures.

Results and discussion

The research initially focuses on key performance parameters, including torque, power output, brake thermal efficiency (BTE), specific fuel consumption (SFC), and exhaust gas temperature. These metrics are measured at engine speeds of 1500, 2000, and 2500 rpm. These characteristics are crucial for comprehending the engine’s operating efficiency and performance under various circumstances. The measurements provide valuable information on the engine’s performance regarding power output, fuel efficiency, and heat regulation. After analysing the performance, the conversation’s focus turns to the emission evaluation. The emissions data include carbon monoxide/ dioxide measurements, unburnt hydrocarbons, and nitrogen oxides. These emissions are significant indicators of both the environmental effect and the engine’s combustion efficiency.

Engine torque, power, and BTE

The image illustrates the effect of adding cerium nanoparticles to biofuels on engine torque. It reveals that the maximum torque is achieved at an engine speed of 1500 revolutions per minute (rpm) for all fuel types. Torque decreases by 6.56% at 2000 rpm and 19.12% at 2500 rpm, even with fully open valves. This reduction is likely due to insufficient cylinder filling during the intake stroke at higher speeds—the shorter duration for air intake and pressure buildup results in lower combustion pressure. Consequently, the increased resistance from the engine’s moving parts results in a decrease in torque compared to the expected levels. At an engine speed of 2500 revolutions per minute (rpm), the fuel blends B10CE80 and B20CE80 demonstrate the highest torque values, whereas the blends B10 and B20 produce the lowest torque. The reduced torque observed with the B10 and B20 blends is attributed to the lower heating value of biodiesel than that of conventional diesel.

In contrast, introducing cerium nanoparticles into the fuel blends enhances the output torque. This improvement is due to the increased energy released during the combustion process, as the presence of nanoparticles leads to more efficient combustion, resulting in enhanced combustion efficiency and a higher average pressure within the combustion chamber. As a result, the force exerted on the piston is more significant, leading to an increase in both the force and torque produced by the engine. Kalaimurugan et al.40 Their investigation demonstrated a clear relationship between the dosage of nanoparticles and the viscosity and volatility of the material. Additionally, their research revealed that increasing the dosage of nanoparticles improves brake thermal efficiency (BTE). Figure 5 illustrates the potential impact of increasing the concentration of cerium nanoparticles in B10 and B20 fuel blends on the engine’s power output. The outcome is a rise in average pressure, increasing the force and torque applied to the piston (Fig. 5-a). Utilising cerium nanoparticles significantly increases power generation when the engine operates at speeds ranging from 1500 to 2500 revolutions per minute (rpm). Increasing the engine speed from 1500 to 2000 rpm results in a 20.23% improvement in power, while increasing the speed from 1500 to 2500 rpm leads to a 25.89% increase in power (Fig. 5b). This indicates a substantial rise in power generation due to the nanoparticles’ ability to enhance combustion efficiency. Therefore, to improve the accuracy of evaluating engine performance and fuel quality, it is advisable to use engine speeds of either 1500 or 2000 rpm. These lower speeds provide a more reliable and consistent basis for assessing the impact of different fuel blends and nanoparticle additives on engine power and efficiency.

The trend observed in engine power is consistent with that of torque for all fuel blends. Generally, a higher concentration of cerium nanoparticles in the fuel blends results in a more significant increase in braking power. This improvement can be attributed to enhanced combustion efficiency and more effective conversion of fuel energy into mechanical work. Nano-additives such as Al₂O₃ and CuO41 have been reported to improve brake thermal efficiency (BTE) by about 3–5%. In contrast, blends containing CeO₂ nanoparticles generally show a smaller increase of only 1–2%. This limited improvement can be attributed to the relatively low nanoparticle concentration (≤ 80 ppm) and the higher viscosity of biodiesel derived from waste cooking oil. Both factors tend to hinder effective spray atomization at higher engine speeds. The nanoparticle concentration was intentionally kept low to avoid agglomeration and ensure long-term blend stability. Mahgoub et al.42 also reported similar modest improvements with CeO₂-doped blends in direct injection (DI) engines, indicating that the type of feedstock and the uniformity of nanoparticle dispersion play important roles in determining the catalytic activity. These findings are consistent with the results of the present investigation. The additional energy generated within the cylinder is the primary factor driving this increase in power. This energy boost is achieved by increasing the surface area-to-volume ratio of the nanoparticles, enhancing the heat transfer coefficient. The presence of nanoparticles acts as a catalyst, reducing both the ignition delay and the combustion timings. This results in tremendous cylinder pressure and a rapid heat release rate.

Similarly, Gad et al.43 conducted research on the addition of alumina nanoparticles to diesel fuel, focusing on the characteristics of diesel combustion spray and flame dynamics. His study concluded that alumina nanoparticles could enhance the fuel combustion process and improve the penetration of the injected fuel into compressed air. Statistical analysis revealed that the average power for all fuel combinations is 4.50 kW at an engine speed of 1500 revolutions per minute (rpm). This average power increases to 5.25 kW at 2000 and 2500 rpm speeds and rises to 5.87 kW at 2500 rpm. Notably, there is a significant increase in power from 2000 to 2500 rpm. The rotational speed and torque characteristics influence the engine’s power output. A decrease in power at 2500 rpm is due to a sudden drop in torque. Increasing the engine speed beyond this point does not improve power production; instead, it leads to higher fuel consumption. The brake thermal efficiency (BTE) values for each fuel blend are depicted in Fig. 5c. B10 and B20 fuel blends exhibit the lowest BTE values.

In contrast, the maximum BTE is achieved using fuel blends B10CE80, B10CE50, and B20CE80. Adding cerium to B10 and B20 mixtures significantly improves brake thermal efficiency. This enhancement is attributed to the reduction in viscosity, improved fuel atomization, and increased oxygen concentration in the fuel blend. These factors collectively contribute to a higher BTE compared to other fuel options.

B10CE80, B20CE80, and B10CE50 exhibit the lowest SFC at engine speeds of 1500 and 2000 revolutions per minute (rpm), whereas B10 and B20 blends show the highest SFC. This is due to a calculated heating estimate for biodiesel, which requires using more fuel to produce the same amount of power. Increasing biodiesel proportion in diesel blends results in higher fuel density and lower heating value44. The highest power generation is associated with the fuel blends B10CE80 and B20CE80, which also have the lowest SFC. Generally, adding cerium to biodiesel enhances power output and combustion efficiency while reducing SFC. Specifically, SFC increases by 9.56% when the engine speed rises from 1500 to 2000 rpm and by 31.3% when the Engine speed increases from 2000 to 2500 rpm. Compared to pure diesel, the inclusion of cerium in diesel fuel results in an average reduction in SFC of 9.78% across all fuel mixes. For B10CE80, B20CE80, and B10CE50, the cutbacks in SFC are approximately 14.56%, 14.56%, and 13.88%, respectively, compared to diesel fuel.

Huang et al.45 explored various ferrous picrate-based homogeneous combustion catalyst doses to assess their impact on diesel engine performance and specific fuel consumption (SFC). Their study revealed that incorporating the catalyst resulted in a noticeable reduction in SFC and an enhancement of brake thermal efficiency (BTE). This indicates that the catalyst improves the engine’s overall efficiency by optimising fuel combustion. Similarly, Sun et al.46 evaluated the performance, emission, and combustion characteristics of a diesel engine using ferric chloride (FeCl3) as a fuel-borne catalyst (FBC) with waste cooking palm oil-based biodiesel. Their findings showed that the addition of FBC to biodiesel resulted in a reduction in SFC and an increase in BTE. The presence of FBC in the biodiesel blend led to higher cylinder gas pressure, a faster rate of heat release, and a shorter ignition delay time. These improvements are indicative of more efficient fuel combustion and better engine performance. Compared to these studies, the lower SFC observed with the B20CE80 blend can be attributed to its higher density and cetane index. The increase in cetane index and decrease in viscosity contribute to more efficient fuel combustion and better engine performance. The higher density of B20CE80 improves the fuel’s energy content, reducing specific fuel consumption. Thus, the improved characteristics of B20CE80 reflect a more effective utilisation of fuel, which enhances the overall engine efficiency compared to other fuel blends.

The engine’s performance is influenced by variations in density, kinematic/ dynamic viscosity, cetane index, and dissolved oxygen found in each fuel mixture. As the amount of cerium increases in the B10CE80 and B20CE80 mixtures, the kinematic and dynamic viscosities decrease. This differs from the scenario observed in the B10 and B20 fuel mixes, where cerium was absent. The presence of cerium nanoparticles in the intermolecular gaps of the fuel facilitates particle sliding, leading to a reduction in viscosity. Therefore, it is presumed that such combinations will exhibit the high-rated torque and the lowest specific fuel consumption (SFC) coefficient.

The analysis of these emissions offers valuable insights into how various operational conditions and fuel blends affect engine performance and environmental impact.

NOx emission

Figure 9a illustrates the relationship between engine speed and nitrogen oxide (NO) concentration for each fuel mixture. The data indicate that the gasoline blends B10CE80, B20CE20, B20CE50, and B20CE80 exhibit high levels of nitrogen oxides. Comparing Fig. 9a with Fig. 7-a reveals a negative correlation between carbon monoxide (CO) and nitrogen oxides (NO) across all three engine speeds, with the weakest correlation observed at 1500 rpm. The catalyst cerium facilitates the oxidative reaction of carbon monoxide, reducing nitric oxide’s state by absorbing oxygen. Among the blends tested, the combination of B20 and CE80 proved to be the most effective at reducing carbon monoxide levels. The optimal conditions for this reaction are influenced by the temperature of the exhaust gases, which may also affect the results. A direct relationship exists between NO concentration and the trend in exhaust gas temperature (EGT), highlighting the impact of rising EGT on NO production. Generally, an inverse relationship between carbon monoxide and nitrogen oxides can be inferred: as CO levels increase, NO levels tend to decrease, and vice versa. Thus, gasoline blends with the lowest CO and NO levels, such as B20CE20, B10CE50, and B10CE80, also show the lowest oxygen concentrations. Previous studies have shown that inadequate oxygen levels in the combustion chamber lead to incomplete fuel combustion, resulting in increased carbon monoxide emissions47. This incomplete combustion can be attributed to insufficient oxygen, which prevents the complete conversion of carbon into carbon dioxide. Additionally, areas with high concentrations of fuel, inefficient burning, and poor mixing contribute to higher CO emissions48.

The 48.43% rise in NOx emissions from cerium oxide nanoparticle-doped biodiesel blends poses a serious environmental challenge. This increase stems from biodiesel’s inherent properties higher oxygen content and elevated combustion temperatures—both of which encourage NOx production. Fortunately, several mitigation strategies exist. Exhaust Gas Recirculation (EGR) can help lower peak combustion temperatures by reintroducing exhaust gases into the intake, thereby suppressing NOx formation. After-treatment solutions, such as Selective Catalytic Reduction (SCR) and Lean NOx Traps (LNT), are also effective, breaking down NOx into harmless nitrogen and water. Earlier research49 indicates that pairing nanoparticle-enhanced biodiesel with EGR or SCR systems can optimise engine performance while maintaining control over NOx emissions.

UHC emission

When the cycle operates at 1,500 revolutions per minute (rpm), it consistently produces the lowest unburned hydrocarbon (UHC) emissions. However, as the engine speed increases to around 2000 rpm, closer to its maximum rated speed and power output, UHC emissions peak significantly, as illustrated in Fig. 9b; this increase in UHC emissions is likely linked to a substantial rise in exhaust gas temperature (EGT). The relationship between nitrogen oxide (NO) emissions and UHC emissions shows an inverse correlation. Specifically, fuels like B20, B10CE20, and B20CE80 exhibit the lowest UHC emissions, whereas fuels with 0% and 10% biodiesel content produce the highest UHC emissions. Research conducted by Dinesha et al.35 explored the effects of adding cerium oxide nanoparticles to biofuels and found that these nanoparticles significantly reduce UHC emissions.

Additionally, incorporating 80 parts per million of cerium oxide into biodiesel resulted in a notable reduction of approximately 30% in nitrogen oxide emissions. Specific fuel blends, whether high or low in fuel content, can contribute to increased UHC emissions. Low injection pressure is a primary cause of elevated UHC emissions, leading to poor mixture formation. Other contributing factors include ineffective atomization of the fuel, improper fuel penetration, and leakage from the sac volume and nozzle apertures. A mixture may become excessively lean for several reasons, such as small nozzle openings, high injection pressures, or prolonged ignition delays. Inadequate air-fuel ratios, incomplete combustion, deposition on the combustion chamber walls, and temperature reductions during the expansion phase all play significant roles in the formation of UHCs. Cold quench layers, such as wall films and cracks, often contain the highest concentrations of UHCs. Using cerium nanoparticles enhances the fuel injection process, improves fuel atomization by reducing viscosity, increases the surface area-to-volume ratio of the nanoparticles, and enhances the heat transfer rate. These improvements contribute to more efficient combustion and reduced UHC emissions.

Balance between performance and emissions

In this research, fuel composition affects engine performance and emissions, including nitrogen oxides, sulfur oxides, hydrocarbons, and carbon dioxide. We specifically investigate the impact of combining biodiesel with a catalyst in diesel fuel to assess the efficiency of diesel engines and their emission levels. Previous studies have suggested that biodiesel may increase specific exhaust emissions, such as smoke and carbon monoxide, while potentially reducing engine performance. Kukana et al.50 examined the effects of biodiesel (B100), waste cooking blend and diesel (B25), and pure diesel (B50) on engine performance and emissions. Their results indicated that the specific fuel consumption (SFC) patterns of B25 and B50 were comparable to those of diesel fuel. However, when running on a B100, increasing engine speed produces the opposite effect. Their study revealed a significant correlation between engine speed and unburned hydrocarbons (UHC) emissions. Although UHC emissions were not significantly influenced by engine speed, carbon monoxide (CO) emissions showed a clear and strong relationship with engine load. Additionally, research has shown that biodiesel leads to higher nitrogen oxide (NOx) emissions. Jin et al.51 found that adding manganese to diesel fuel reduced CO emissions by 14.3% and increased the fuel’s cetane number by 4.3%.

Shariff et al.52 investigated ferric chloride (FeCl3) as a catalyst in their study. Their results showed that, when compared to diesel, ferric chloride led to a marginal increase in CO2 emissions and a decrease in NO emissions. Compared to pure biodiesel, ferric chloride resulted in reductions of 52.6% in carbon monoxide (CO) emissions, 26.6% in total hydrocarbons, and 6.9% in smoke. The optimal fuel composition can be determined by evaluating these factors. Cost analysis

In the experiment, only the nanomaterial was obtained. Except for a few compounds, all raw materials are sourced from nature, resulting in minimal costs. The proposed application uses milligrams of nanoparticles for biodiesel transesterification, specifically for engine testing. Considering the numerous advantages of using biofuel, the cost of producing nanoparticles is reasonable. Given the potential applications of biofuel, its manufacturing cost remains low. However, this research lacks an assessment of the economic feasibility of biodiesel. The cerium additives used in this study cost approximately 4200 rupees per kilogram in India. The financial viability of incorporating nanoparticles into fuel is evident from the reduced cost of diesel fuel and the relatively minor proportion of fuel cost represented by nanoparticles. Additional benefits, such as reduced emissions, should also be taken into account. While toxicity depends on dosage and exposure duration, current risk assessments indicate that low-ppm fuel additives likely contribute only minor increases to ambient ceria levels. However, proper precautions should still be taken during additive handling and maintenance operations to protect workers. To minimise potential risks, we suggest three key measures: First, restrict the use of CeO₂ additives to engines with well-maintained diesel particulate filters (DPFs) and catalytic after-treatment systems. Second, implement workplace controls, including adequate ventilation, personal protective equipment (PPE), and exposure monitoring during fuel handling and filter maintenance. Third, treat DPF ash and washwater as potentially metal-contaminated waste until site-specific disposal testing confirms safe handling methods54. Before widespread adoption, future field studies should incorporate air quality monitoring, worker exposure assessments, and DPF ash characterisation to verify minimal environmental and health impacts.

The experimental results demonstrated that the optimal B20CE80 blend (20% biodiesel with 80 ppm CeO₂ nanoparticles) reduced specific fuel consumption by 14.56% compared to conventional diesel. For a medium-duty engine consuming approximately 20 L (17 kg) of fuel daily, this translates to daily savings of 2.91 L of fuel. At the current diesel price of ₹90 per litre, this amounts to a daily fuel cost savings of ₹ 262. The blend requires only 1.36 g of CeO₂ additive (at an 80 ppm concentration), costing just ₹5.71 per day, based on the market price of ₹ 4,200 per kilogram of nanoparticles. This results in impressive net daily savings of ₹256, yielding an exceptional benefit-to-cost ratio of 46:1. When projected annually, these savings accumulate to approximately ₹93,440 per engine, excluding additional benefits such as reduced maintenance costs or extended engine lifespan, making the B20CE80 blend both economically and environmentally advantageous.

Conclusion

This study examines the performance of various fuels in internal combustion engines (ICEs), assessing the impact of alumina-biodiesel-diesel blends on engine characteristics and emissions. Specifically, the research examines the performance and emissions of a single-cylinder variable compression ratio engine when cerium oxide (CeO2) nanoparticles are combined with B10 and B20 biodiesel-diesel fuel mixtures. Cerium nanoparticles were added to the fuel mixtures to assess the impact at concentrations of 20, 50, and 80 parts per million. Under full-load conditions, the engine was tested at three distinct speeds: 1500, 2000, and 2500 rpm. The results demonstrated that B20CE80 fuel exhibited notable differences compared to pure diesel fuel in several performance metrics: torque, power, specific fuel consumption (SFC), brake thermal efficiency (BTE), and exhaust gas temperature (EGT) were affected by 5.23%, 5.12%, 10.98%, 14.88%, and 5.99%, respectively. The experimental data confirmed these differences.

Additionally, the study observed a reduction in carbon monoxide (CO) and unburned hydrocarbons (UHC) by 2.94% and 20.56%, respectively. However, nitrogen oxide (NO) emissions increased by 48.43%. Previous research has shown that biodiesel fuel blends containing nanoparticles can reduce specific emissions by enhancing oxygen availability in the mixture, thereby facilitating more complete combustion of the diesel fuel. B20CE80 offers a balanced combination of improved performance and reduced emissions from both a cost and nanoparticle pricing perspective. Overall, the research suggests that the inclusion of catalysts in fuel blends generally leads to increased power output, elevated CO2 and NOx emissions, and a simultaneous reduction in CO and UHC emissions.

Comprehensive of the current study

Cerium oxide nanoparticles in B10 and B20 waste cooking oil biodiesel fuel blends are investigated in a VCR CI engine. Torque, power, brake thermal efficiency (BTE), and exhaust gas temperature (EGT) all improved by 6.43%, 6.46%, 12.78%, and 6.93%, respectively, when compared to pure diesel. Fuel economy increased by 12.20%. CO and UHC emissions reduced by 1.84% and 18.33%, respectively. However, NOx emissions increased by 48.43%. The study also discovered a strong link between CO, NO, and oxygen levels. Previous studies have shown that biodiesel fuel blends incorporating nanoparticles can reduce particulate emissions by increasing oxygen availability in the mixture, thereby allowing for more complete combustion of the diesel fuel. This study found that nano-additive-added biodiesel blends provide a balanced combination of increased performance and lower emissions, both in terms of cost and performance. Overall, the addition of catalysts to fuel blends increases power production while increasing CO2 and NOx emissions and decreasing CO and UHC emissions. Future works may employ these nanoparticles as an addition to boost performance and minimise NOx emissions.
