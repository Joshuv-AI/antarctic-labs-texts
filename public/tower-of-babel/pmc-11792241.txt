Occupational Therapists’ Perspectives on Community Implementation of Interventions Unfamiliar to Older Adults
Authors: Hui-Fen Mao, Athena Yi-Jung Tsai, Hsin-Chun Yeh, Ling-Hui Chang, Tonya J Roberts
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectivesThere is an increasing push for broad implementation of evidence-based interventions (EBIs) targeting older adults. Successful delivery of EBIs in communities requires therapists to adhere to protocols and to be responsive to contextual factors. This study explored the real-world implementation of an EBI, the Multiple-component Cognitive Intervention using Simulated Everyday Tasks (MCI-SET) in Taiwan from therapists’ perspectives on fidelity, challenges, and strategies for program delivery.Research Design and MethodsWe used a convergent mixed-methods design. Quantitative data were collected from the 13-item Perceived Fidelity Questionnaire (PFQ), assessing the perceived importance, proficiency, and adherence to the essential skills of MCI-SET. Qualitative data were collected via 2 World Café discussions on implementation challenges and strategies, which were recorded, transcribed verbatim, and analyzed using thematic analysis. Quantitative and qualitative findings were then compared and integrated to enrich the interpretation.Results47 occupational therapists completed the PFQ. They rated community-oriented skills as more important, demonstrated greater proficiency, and achieved higher adherence. Qualitative analysis revealed that therapists need to increase and sustain participation by strategically introducing the program, adapting the intervention protocol to make it engaging, relevant to everyday issues, and tailored to local needs, and building rapport. Both quantitative and qualitative findings highlighted the importance of community engagement skills and the adaptability of intervention to implementation and sustainability in communities.Discussion and ImplicationsReal-world scale-up of EBIs requires tailored adaptations responsive to community contexts, especially when initially introducing an intervention unfamiliar to the community. This emphasizes the need for a dynamic fidelity concept.Keywords: Cognitive intervention, Community engagement, Fidelity, Occupational therapy

Background and Objectives

Evidence-based community programs are essential for enhancing health and for preventing or delaying disability among older adults. To effect impactful changes at the population level, governments worldwide have integrated the adoption of evidence-based interventions (EBIs) within communities into public health initiatives for healthy aging (Arai et al., 2012; Mulati et al., 2022). However, large-scale rollouts of these programs may encounter contextual challenges related to local policies and regulations, community adoption, and/or adherence to the intervention protocol (Barnfield et al., 2020; Clemson et al., 2021). Translating EBIs into communities while maintaining implementation fidelity is challenging. It is therefore important to recognize the complexity of community adoption and implementation of EBIs and to systematically address the challenges.

Implementation science is “the scientific study of the use of strategies to adopt and integrate evidence-based health interventions into routine practice” (Bauer et al., 2015, p. 1). Principles of implementation science improve various domains and processes of community-based practice, including the development and effectiveness testing of a program, fidelity, scale-up and scale-out of EBIs, community responses, perspectives of interventionists, implementation facilitators and barriers, and cultural factors influencing program adoption and implementation (Bobitt & Jarrott, 2023).

The PRISM (the Practical, Robust Implementation, and Sustainability Model) framework examines multiple elements that affect the adoption, implementation, reach, effectiveness, and maintenance of health programs (Feldstein & Glasgow, 2008). These elements include the organizational perspectives of the intervention (e.g., organization readiness, administrators, and clinicians), implementation and sustainability infrastructures, and the external environment. The contextual elements are conceptualized as belonging to two domains: the external and internal domains (Feldstein & Glasgow, 2008). The external domain includes sociocultural–structural factors such as policy, regulations, cost, and reimbursement, which can facilitate large-scale implementation. The internal domain encompasses implementation and sustainability infrastructure such as community factors, physical environment, and partnerships (Feldstein & Glasgow, 2008).

Originally, the PRISM framework’s organizational perspective referred to the institution responsible for program delivery, encompassing varying personnel levels such as staff, managers, and leaders within a hierarchical structure (Feldstein & Glasgow, 2008). However, community health promotion often involves stakeholders from various entities without formal organizational ties or hierarchy. For example, the Taiwan Long-Term Care 2.0 Disability Prevention and Delay Initiative, which is the setting of this paper, aims to scale up evidence-based community health promotion nationally. This Initiative supports the programs targeting psychological support, cognitive enhancement, and physical capacities, to name a few. To achieve the objectives of the initiative, collaboration is required among governmental officials, contracted agencies for program development and quality monitoring, community center staff managing operations, and independent interventionists contracted for program delivery. Occupational therapists typically work as independent contractors, playing a key role in delivering evidence-based cognitive interventions—programs that are relatively unfamiliar to many communities and older adults.

Engagement with the community is crucial but is often “a missing link” in implementation research (Goodman et al., 2017). During community implementation, stakeholders’ perspectives and interests vary, and power dynamics may change with context and regulations. These factors significantly influence therapists’ experiences, especially when delivering an EBI that is unfamiliar to the community. Given these complexities, an analytical approach examining the perspectives of the organization, therapists, and clients separately can contribute to the knowledge and practical strategies for EBI implementation (Figure 1). The dynamics between interventionists and communities have been minimally explored. Similarly, few studies have used the PRISM framework to explore the perspective of independent interventionists navigating the complexity of working with multiple partners to deliver interventions.

Community health programs are often considered “complex interventions,” characterized by multiple components and targeted behaviors, multiple groups, or settings (Skivington et al., 2021). Thus, the strategies used by interventionists to enhance program fidelity are critical to the success of community implementation. Fidelity refers to adherence to the original plan during delivery (Kwan et al., 2019). Most studies on fidelity aim to confirm adherence to protocols for subsequent randomized controlled studies or implementation trials. Research teams often use fidelity checklists to observe adherence to key elements in typically tightly controlled environments (Mountain et al., 2017; Sprange et al., 2021). However, in the context of large-scale implementation, third-party observations require significant resources and do not offer insights into interventionists’ experiences. Understanding these experiences can improve fidelity, training, refinement of protocols, and dissemination planning (Bird et al., 2020; Breitenstein, et al., 2010; Toglia et al., 2020).

With the population aging, substantial resources from governments and organizations have been allocated to develop and implement large-scale community-based EBIs to either prevent disability or slow age-related functional decline (WHO, 2021). It is therefore imperative to examine the factors contributing to increased implementation fidelity. This study aimed to (a) explore therapists’ perspectives on the key fidelity elements of a complex intervention during community implementation, (b) identify the challenges that may interfere with fidelity and corresponding strategies, and (c) understand how their experiences may be situated within both external and internal contexts of disseminating nation-wide health promotion programs in Taiwan.

Research Design and Methods

Study Design

We employed a prospective convergent parallel mixed-method study design, collecting quantitative and qualitative data simultaneously (Edmonds & Kennedy, 2017). The quantitative approach used an anonymous Likert questionnaire to assess therapists’ perceptions of key fidelity elements in terms of importance, proficiency, and adherence. The qualitative approach used World Café–style roundtable discussion to examine the factors affecting implementation and fidelity (Fouché & Light, 2011; The World Café Community Foundation, 2019). The discussions were recorded and transcribed verbatim. Quantitative and qualitative data were analyzed separately. Integration of qualitative and quantitative findings was then conducted to compare and elucidate the quantitative results. National Cheng Kung University Governance Framework for Human Research Ethics approved the study (#110-683).

Setting: The External Contexts of MCI-SET Implementation

This study examined therapists’ perspectives on implementing the evidence-based Multiple-component Cognitive Intervention using Simulated Everyday Tasks (MCI-SET) program. MCI-SET is a complex intervention (Skivington et al., 2021), consisting of multiple components that aim to improve basic cognitive skills, cognitive functional performance, and activities of daily living. The 12-week program features 2-hr weekly sessions of cognitive training that involve repeated practice of challenging cognitive tasks and cognitive rehabilitation activities that emphasize applying cognitive strategies in daily life (Chang et al., 2021; Mao et al., 2021). The program protocol includes 24 standardized weekly activity plans, with an emphasis on tailoring to the cognitive skills of older adults at different levels of functioning.

Community implementation of MCI-SET is supported by Taiwan’s Ministry of Health and Welfare as part of the “Prevention and Delay of Disability Progression Initiative.” In 2017, the first year of the Initiative, over 200 program protocols were submitted and reviewed, of which 78, including MCI-SET, were approved and listed on a governmental database for centers to choose from (Taiwan Occupational Therapy Association, 2017, Meeting Presentation). Delivering MCI-SET requires qualification training, a 2-day workshop encompassing lectures and practical sessions, and periodic recertifications mandated by the government. MCI-SET is delivered by occupational therapists (for more information on the training and intervention protocol, refer to Mao et al., 2021).

Data Collection and Analysis

Participants

Data were collected during the two recertification workshops in 2019, one in southern Taiwan and one in northern Taiwan. The recertification workshop included an anonymous pre-workshop questionnaire that encouraged reflection on implementation, a 90-min lecture on recent advances in cognitive interventions, and a 90-min World Café discussion for sharing MCI-SET implementation experience.

Quantitative data collection via the Perceived Fidelity Questionnaire

The Perceived Fidelity Questionnaire (PFQ) was developed by the research team and included 13 key elements, namely, the skills that therapists need to implement MCI-SET in the community (Yeh et al., 2020). The elements were identified through literature reviews and research meetings. Face validity was examined at an expert meeting involving two occupational therapy faculty members and five occupational therapists, all experienced with MCI-SET, cognitive intervention, and community-based practice. The internal consistency of the PFQ was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha, yielding values of 0.88 for perceived importance, 0.89 for proficiency, and 0.87 for adherence, indicating high internal consistency and homogeneity of the items.

The elements were divided into two domains: 10 intervention-oriented elements embodying the unique core principles of MCI-SET that distinguish it from other cognitive interventions, and 3 community-oriented elements about implementation strategies that facilitate collaboration with the community.

Using a 5-point Likert scale, each element was scored on (1) Importance (how essential the element was to the intervention), rated from “very unimportant” to “very important,” (2) Proficiency (the therapist’s ability to execute the program), rated from “unable to execute” to “achieved proficiency,” and (3) Adherence (how consistently the element was executed according to the protocol), rated from “never” to “always.” Higher scores represented perceived stronger importance, better proficiency, or more rigorous adherence.

Qualitative data collection via World Café discussion

The World Café roundtable method fosters an informal and intimate setting that encourages constructive, participatory conversations on complicated issues, involving extensive participant engagement to explore and validate themes (Schiele et al., 2022; The World Café Community Foundation, 2019). Participants sat at tables seating seven to nine persons and rotated among them. Each table had a topic for discussion, with subsequent groups building on the previous discussions. Topics discussed included (a) contexts of program delivery, (b) adaptations made to intervention protocols during community implementation, (c) successful experiences of delivering the intervention, and (d) challenges and corresponding strategies. Therapists rotated among the World Café tables, contributing to all topics. Discussions at each table and summaries by table leaders were recorded and transcribed verbatim.

Data Analysis

Quantitative analysis: means and standard deviations (SD) were calculated for numerical variables and frequencies for categorical variables. Pearson’s correlation coefficient was used to observe relations among importance, proficiency, and adherence. A multiple linear regression model determined the association between importance and proficiency with adherence. The dependent variable (Y) is the adherence score (calculated as the total score divided by the number of items in the adherence domain), with importance and proficiency scores as independent variables, adjusting for participants’ gender, age, educational level, working areas, and years of experience in community-based practice. Analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Version 25 (SPSS Statistics V25, IBM Corporation, Somers, NY), with p-value of <.05, two-tailed as statistically significant.

Qualitative analysis

Transcripts from the World Café discussions were thematically analyzed using Atlas.ti 9.0. Open coding was first conducted to dissect data into meaning units and to examine differences and similarities. Thematic analysis was then conducted, involving going back and forth between the data, looking for patterns and connections that could help relate and combine initial codes into broader themes. To bolster the trustworthiness of the analysis, we employed peer debriefing through research meetings (Creswell & Poth, 2017).

Integration of mixed-method data

The integration began with a comparison of qualitative and quantitative findings, focusing on identifying the consistency and inconsistency between the two. Following this, the focus shifted to how each finding complements the other, such as whether qualitative findings can provide a deeper understanding of why certain questionnaire items received the highest or lowest scores. The process involved creating a convergence coding matrix for the targeted items and relevant quotes (Supplementary Table 1: Excerpt of the matrix). Lastly, the findings were integrated thematically to weave together the qualitative and quantitative results (Fetters, et al., 2013).

Results

Participants

Fifty-six occupational therapists participated in the study. Fifty-five completed the PFG anonymously (96.55% response rate). To ensure relevant and informed responses, only responses from therapists who had delivered at least one MCI-SET session (n = 47) were included in the quantitative data analysis. The average age was 33.1 ± 5.1 years, 62.5% were female, and 47% had 1–3 years of group-based community intervention experience (Table 1). All therapists participated in the World Café discussion.

Importance

Most key elements were considered important (x̅ = 4.08 ± 0.60). The mean score for all community-oriented element items (x̅ = 4.20 ± 0.58) was higher than that of intervention-oriented elements (x̅ = 4.04 ± 0.61). The highest scoring elements were “communicates well with community staff and participants (Item 13),” “recruits and maintains the participation of older adults through activity design, group dynamics, charisma, etc. (Item 12),” (both community domain) and “analyzes and adapts the activities according to cognitive goals (Item 7)” (intervention domain).

Proficiency and adherence

Both proficiency (x̅ = 3.56 ± 0.90) and adherence (x̅ = 3.62 ± 0.64) closely approached the theoretical midpoint of a 5-point scale, indicating that therapists viewed themselves as being moderate to mostly proficient in executing key elements of the intervention, and reported adhering to these elements between “half of the time” and “usually” during delivery.

The items with the highest score, indicating the highest perceived confidence, were “recruits and maintains the participation of older adults through activity design, group dynamics, charisma, etc. (Item 2),” “communicates well with community staff and participants (Item 13),” (both community domain), “analyzes and adapts the activities according to cognitive goals (Item 7),” and “sets clear cognitive goals to the activities (Item 4)” (both intervention domain). Conversely, the lowest scores, representing the most challenging aspects perceived by therapists in terms of proficiency and adherence, pertain to the intervention domain: “skills for guiding the older adults to raise self-awareness of cognitive function (Item 3),” “leads group discussions about how to use cognitive strategies in daily life (Item 10),” and “redesigns daily-life activities to develop cognitive intervention activities (Item 9).”

Qualitative Analysis: Threats to Implementation Fidelity and Relevant Strategies

Four themes emerged detailing the challenges and strategies related to implementation fidelity.

Theme 1: Strategic introduction and consistent clarification when introducing a complex intervention unfamiliar to the community

Our data showed challenges in attracting and retaining older participants in cognitive interventions like MCI-SET at community centers. To strategically attract participants, community staff often vaguely described MCI-SET, whereas families promoted the programs to older adults by emphasizing social interaction alone. Consequently, many participants were either unaware of or were even misinformed about MCI-SET’s cognitive focus, posing a significant challenge for the therapists to facilitate consistent engagement.

Family members tell their elders it’s just like a place where they can hang out and make friends. …They don’t think they’ll need to get involved with any activity, especially nothing that makes them think hard or that challenges their brains. They just wanna pop in and see what’s going on.

...Persons with dementia didn’t get why they were there and what they were supposed to do….and those who were frail were thinking, ‘I do not have any cognition problems, so why should I bother with this program?’

Therapists shared strategies to help participants better understand MCI-SET’s purposes and benefits, including emphasizing these points at the start of every session.

We usually chat with center staff before starting, and explain to participants what these cognitive classes are all about. At the start of every session, we remind them again why we’re here and what the intervention is. That way, they get the difference between ‘I could really get better in this class’ and ‘Am I just here for ten sessions to sing some songs?’ This can really change their motivation and how much they get involved later on.

Both qualitative and quantitative results showed that, when implementing an intervention in the community that participants are unfamiliar with, it is important for both center staff and therapists to introduce and explain the program strategically. Unsurprisingly, from therapists’ perspectives, the key element of “communicates well with community staff and participants (Item 13)” is essential to delivering the program.

Theme 2: Adapting the intervention protocol to align with participants, local preferences, and challenges

Therapists often have to adapt the interventions, planned or on-the-spot, to meet the often unpredictable needs of participants and communities, which could be either frustrating or rewarding. For example, center staff and participants typically favor physical exercise over less familiar cognitive interventions. Therapists are frequently asked to integrate or amplify physical exercise components within MCI-SET to boost attendance and maintain engagement.

So, a regular issue is that these center staff think it’s all about physical exercise. No matter how much you try to discuss with them, some are set on doing exercises. The staff in charge equate exercise with health because they see immediate effects, like breaking into a sweat or feeling muscle fatigue.

Therapists include physical exercise as a bridge to introduce the participants to cognition intervention and enjoy being agents who instigate appreciation of cognition. As one therapist noted, “Initially, everyone just wanted physical exercises, but after going through MCI-SET, they realized how cognition plays a part in their everyday lives.”

On-the-spot adaptations are also driven by various factors, including internal contextual factors such as the absence of a dedicated room for MCI-SET, distractions from other activities, fluctuating group sizes, group dynamics, and external contextual factors such as reimbursement rules for program expenses and changes in participant criteria. For instance, although the MCI-SET protocol recommends group sizes of 10–15 persons, the reimbursement rules encourage maximal enrollment, leading to groups beginning with 10 but often expanding to 20 or more. This growth indicates therapists’ skills in maintaining interest and participation, aligning with their self-reported proficiency in the PFG results.

Additionally, the requirement to be sensitive and adaptive to local needs can potentially explain the PFG results regarding the high importance of two key elements, “analyzes and adapts the activities according to cognitive goals” (Item 7), and “recruits and maintains the participation of older adults through activity design, group dynamics, charisma, etc.” (Item 12). Both elements need a tailored approach that meets the cognitive levels of individual participants and the particular needs of the community. PFG results also showed that many therapists felt proficient and that their implementation adhered to these two elements. Although challenging, when they succeeded in doing so, the rewards were evident. As one therapist recounted:

In the early weeks, things weren’t clicking … However, things changed once we integrated (participants’) personal experiences into the activities. I remember one older gentleman, a former tailor, who generally seemed disinterested. Then, we did a simulated sewing activity. He was entirely engaged and noticeably uplifted. Witnessing his enthusiasm and sense of achievement was profoundly gratifying for me … His family also appreciated our personalized approach. They even considered doing similar activities at home.

Theme 3: Overcoming engagement barriers by making the intervention explicitly relevant to everyday issues

One key intervention-specific element, “leads group discussions about how to use cognitive strategies in daily life” (Item 10), received the lowest score in perceived proficiency and adherence and also emerged as a significant challenge to fidelity, as per the transcripts.

Therapists described several reasons for this challenge. They noted participants’ reluctance to engage in discussions, with comments like, “the participants did not like to talk (in a group) or to think deeply, ” “they were more used to remaining passive listeners rather than active discussants, ” and “they want the therapists just to tell them what to do.” The diversity in participants’ cognitive, functional, and communication skills added challenges to these discussions.

Therapists shared strategies that facilitated the discussion. They found that starting with topics the participants were familiar with, such as potential everyday cognitive challenges, was helpful in making the intervention relevant to their own experiences.

You kick off straight from daily life. You ask something like, ‘Say you forgot your keys or mixed up the dates?’ You start with these common memory mix-ups that everyone can relate to. Then we guide them to connect these with the cognitive activities we just did.

Despite efforts to make the discussions relevant, they were frequently cut short.

When we get to the part of the class where we need to chat about cognitive strategies, the participants often don’t want to share in a group. The discussion time keeps getting shorter, from 15 minutes in initial sessions to only 2 or 3 minutes by the end. Since the participants aren’t engaged, we limit the time spent on it.

Theme 4: Facilitating the implementation and sustainability of the program by making it interesting and building rapport

The analysis revealed that keeping the program engaging, building trust, and forging strong relationships with community staff were key to the smooth implementation and sustainability of the program. Some strategies discussed included “getting to know the participants personally,” “helping out at the center beyond scheduled hours,” “using casual chats to highlight the benefits of cognitive activities,” and “discussing the specifics of the cognitive intervention like timing, participant numbers, and volunteer roles with both center staff and participants.”

You’ve got to get to know the older people, chat with center staff, and be willing to go the extra mile with your help. That stuff’s really important. And after each session, let them know how the activities are going, and give them the lowdown on each session, especially the results.

The therapists employed communication and relationship-building skills to foster acceptance and understanding of cognitive intervention and, in turn, to ensure that their expertise in cognitive intervention is acknowledged and valued. They believed that such acknowledgment contributes to the program’s sustainability.

Always communicate with the center staff, don’t just leave right after the session; even when the activities went really well, the participants had a blast, and we’re feeling great about the program, if we don’t take time to talk about what was accomplished, how things were set up, they won’t be asking for us next time around.

Because MCI-SET is a time-limited, voluntary program, the therapists felt pressured to “make the program appealing” to ensure continued participation; otherwise, “the center staff said, ‘you cannot keep the participants here, I will not ask for you next time around.’” Therefore, the therapists stressed the importance of dedicating time during and outside the session to communicate what the program was about, the purposes of the program design, and how to observe the progress and results.

Discussion and Implications

This paper examines the perceived fidelity, challenges, and strategies of delivering a complex cognitive intervention in real-world settings from the perspective of therapists. The quantitative survey shows that therapists emphasize community-rapport-building skills more than intervention-specific skills for implementation. Qualitative analysis revealed that the program participants’ lack of familiarity with the intervention and consequent low engagement were major challenges in its delivery. A tailored approach, sometimes spontaneous and on-site, was necessary to adapt the intervention for specific communities or participants. Both qualitative and quantitative findings underscore the importance of community engagement skills to maintain fidelity and program sustainability. Community engagement skills, such as effective communication, the strategic introduction of the intervention, and the design of engaging activities, were key to this process.

Our findings, aligned with the PRISM framework, illustrate the contextual influences on implementation and the mutual influences between internal and external contextual factors (Figure 1) (Feldstein & Glasgow, 2008). External sociocultural contextual factors include the long-standing promotion of physical exercise by the government, reimbursement rules, and prevailing societal views associating old age with relaxation, and reduced cognitive exertion and community engagement. Internal community contextual factors include unfamiliarity with group-based discussions, pressure to sustain participation, lack of a dedicated space to conduct the program, and fluctuating group sizes. These results highlight the challenges faced in implementation due to both internal and external factors.

Before 2017, physical exercise programs in Taiwan were strongly supported by governmental bureaus. This support made the public familiar with and receptive to physical exercise. Similarly, the government has promoted dementia awareness and prevention in recent years and the appreciation for social and cognitive-stimulating activities has grown. Yet, most older adults remain unfamiliar with the benefits and specifics of cognitive-enhancing programs for old age. Considering the cultural and policy backdrop, it was not surprising that our data showed challenges in attracting and retaining older participants in cognitive interventions like MCI-SET at community centers.

Consequently, when introducing cognitive interventions such as MCI-SET, although well-established in the literature but unfamiliar to the community, they received less priority and community interest, leading to tepid motivation to participate. These insights underscore the need for balanced governmental support for diverse health initiatives. Therapists felt burdened when championing an intervention sidelined by governmental focus. Additionally, traditional Taiwanese values, favoring home-based safe and low-demand leisure activities for older adults (Chiu & Fu, 2014), added challenges to promoting mentally stimulating, community-based programs like MCI-SET. Therapists also noted that participants’ educational levels influenced their engagement, with those with lower education often resisting cognitively demanding tasks.

The emphasis on community-rapport-building likely arises from challenges in introducing an unfamiliar intervention and the need to adapt to local needs. This approach aligned with Edwards et al.’s (2000) “community readiness,” describing a community’s progression in adopting new ideas. At the initial stages of program implementation, characterized by limited awareness, interventionists must first establish connections, that is, “find a door in.” Adaptations to the protocols, such as incorporating physical exercise and reducing discussion time, represent strategic approaches to effectively engage the participants.

Fidelity measures typically focus on intervention domains, the core elements of the intervention. Community engagement, crucial for interventions in communities, is often overlooked and seldom included in fidelity measures, even in interventions intended for community settings (Goodman et al., 2017; Hand et al., 2018). Consequently, its importance is often disregarded or marginalized during both training and implementation. Hence, we suggest integrating community engagement into fidelity measures to position community-oriented elements at the forefront of interventions. Interventionists may need additional support and training in community engagement. This could include providing program manuals and training on effective strategies for community engagement, and adapting interventions to meet local needs while maintaining fidelity to the core intervention-specific components. Training strategies can also include case studies, presentations of implementation progress, and field observations.

A notable observation from the survey results is that therapists attributed less importance to intervention-specific elements relative to community-oriented elements. We believe this result does not imply that intervention-specific key elements are unimportant. Rather, it suggests a dynamic state of implementation fidelity that needs to consider the stages of program implementation, as well as community and participant characteristics. For instance, launching an EBI that is unfamiliar to the community, as illustrated in this study, propels interventionists to prioritize community-rapport-building. As community acceptance grows, interventionists may then be able to focus more on executing intervention-specific key elements without compromising the program to appease participants. Such compromise, like allocating more time to physical exercise and reducing discussion time, could explain the moderate perceived fidelity and adherence to intervention-specific elements. Thus, fidelity assessments and their components may not be a static, one-time-fits-all list, but should reflect the dynamic nature of implementations within local communities.

Examining the fidelity of large-scale community-based programs is crucial to implementation. Fidelity checklists, or video recordings, and on-site observation by independent raters have been used to assess fidelity in controlled trials (Beit Yosef et al., 2019; Skidmore et al., 2014; Toglia et al., 2020; Walton et al., 2019). Nonetheless, these strategies can be challenging to broad community implementation due to several issues, such as difficulty in obtaining consent, limited resources, and potential observation effects on interventionist and participant performance (Sprange, 2021). This study used a fidelity questionnaire to explore therapists’ perceived fidelity, which was more feasible and cost-effective. However, additional studies are needed to examine the correlation between perceived and observed fidelity.

The regression analysis indicated that perceived proficiency is a significant predictor of adherence. This finding aligned with prior research which found a positive correlation between observed competence and adherence to key elements (Walton et al., 2019). This study extends current fidelity research, which often uses third-party observations to examine adherence, and points out the value of considering therapists’ self-reported proficiency as a metric for assessing adherence. Thus, enhancing their perceived proficiency can be instrumental in maintaining fidelity. Several strategies can bolster their confidence in intervention delivery, such as regular peer discussions about implementation experiences, challenges, and adherence strategies (Mao et al., 2021), continuing education courses, and structured case discussions.

This study has several limitations. First, this research is based on self-reported perceived fidelity, which may not align entirely with observed fidelity measures and may be influenced by social desirability bias. Additionally, this study was conducted within a specific social context–the implementation of MCI-SET in Taiwan. The unique attributes of the implementation contexts and the MCI-SET intervention may limit the generalizability of our findings to other interventions and contexts that are markedly different. Despite these limitations, this study also explored the feasibility of using a perceived fidelity checklist to provide information for future interventionist training and implementation of community-based cognitive intervention.

Conclusion

This study of MCI-SET implementation in Taiwan found that introducing an EBI that was unfamiliar to the community necessitates a customized strategy by therapists. To effectively “find a door in” and improve the program’s sustainability, interventionists require a tailored approach that includes building rapport and adapting the intervention protocol to align with the community’s expectations. Consequently, it makes sense that therapists consider intervention key elements that facilitate community engagement as being more important than intervention-specific elements.

We argue for a broader view of fidelity as a dynamic construct, reflecting adaptations to community-specific contexts and various stages of program development. Implementation fidelity should be viewed as a process rather than as a static, predefined, one-time assessment of adherence to intervention protocols. It requires constant monitoring and adaptation to ensure that interventions remain sustainable and effective in real-world settings.
