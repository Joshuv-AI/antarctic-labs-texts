Examining tobacco consumption: prevalence and associated factors among Cambodians aged 15–49 from the 2021–2022 Demographic and Health Survey
Authors: Myo Zin Oo, Soe Sandi Tint, Amaraporn Rerkasem, Por Leakhena, Nutchar Wiwatkunupakarn, Chaisiri Angkurawaranon, Kittipan Rerkasem
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTobacco consumption is a significant preventable cause of death worldwide. This study aimed to assess the prevalence and associated factors of tobacco consumption among Cambodian individuals aged 15–49, utilising data from the 2021–2022 Cambodia Demographic and Health Survey (CDHS).DesignCross-sectional study based on secondary analysis of the 2021–2022 CDHS.SettingNationwide household survey conducted across urban and rural areas of Cambodia.ParticipantsA total of 28 321 respondents aged 15–49 years were included in the analysis.Outcome measuresTobacco consumption categorised as no use, smoking tobacco, smokeless tobacco and dual use. Descriptive statistics, χ2 tests and multinomial logistic regression were used to assess associations between background characteristics and tobacco consumption, with ‘no consumption’ as the reference category. Statistical significance was set at p<0.05.ResultsAmong the 28 321 respondents (68.8% female), 91.8% were non-users of tobacco (reference group), while 6.9% reported smoking (predominantly males; adjusted relative risk ratios (ARRR)=39.29, 95% CI 29.70 to 51.96, p<0.001). Additionally, 0.2% used smokeless tobacco and 1.1% were dual users. Higher risks of smoking were observed among those aged 40–49 (ARRR=5.17, 95% CI 3.58 to 7.47, p<0.001) and among alcohol consumers (ARRR=1.85, 95% CI 1.57 to 2.18, p<0.001).ConclusionsWhile Cambodia has made notable progress in reducing tobacco consumption, the persistent challenges highlighted by the prevalence of smoking, particularly among specific demographics, indicate the need for targeted public health interventions.

Introduction

Tobacco use is a leading preventable cause of death worldwide, leading to more than 7 million deaths annually, including about 1.6 million non-smokers exposed to secondhand smoke.1 The majority of the world’s 1.3 billion tobacco users (approximately 80%) reside in low-income and middle-income countries.1 Tobacco is typically categorised into two main types: smoked tobacco, which involves inhaling smoke, and smokeless tobacco (SLT), which is consumed through chewing or snuffing. Cigarettes are the most commonly smoked tobacco product,1 with 32.6% of men and 6.5% of women reporting smoking in 2020, resulting in 1.18 billion regular users.2 Cigarette smoking is the primary risk factor for lung cancer,3 accounting for approximately 85% of all cases.4 Tobacco smoke contains over 7000 chemicals, many of which are toxic, with at least 70 recognised as carcinogenic and linked to various cancers.3 Furthermore, more than 360 million people globally consume SLT, particularly in South and Southeast Asia,5 6 and it carries health risks similar to those of smoked tobacco.7

Nicotine, the key psychoactive component of tobacco, compels ongoing use despite its risks.8 Many individuals attempting to quit often turn to electronic cigarettes or SLT, a practice known as ‘dual use’; however, this approach does not lead to better health outcomes.9 The associated factors of tobacco consumption are multifaceted, influenced by a combination of individual, social, economic and cultural factors, including age,10 11 gender,10 11 education,11 12 residence,11 occupation11 12 and alcohol consumption.11 13

Cambodia, a Southeast Asian nation with lower middle-income status, faces ongoing public health challenges from tobacco use, which accounts for around 15 000 deaths annually and imposes a significant economic burden,14 despite declining smoking rates.15 Tobacco smoking is a significant modifiable risk factor for cardiovascular disease (CVD),16 which is a leading cause of vascular conditions such as heart disease, stroke and peripheral artery disease, contributing to one in four CVD-related deaths.17 In Cambodia, noncommunicable diseases (NCDs) account for 64% of deaths, with a 23% of chance of dying between ages 30 and 70 from one of the four main NCDs, and the affordability of tobacco products remains a significant challenge for tobacco control efforts.18

However, the country has made notable progress in tobacco control through comprehensive measures, including the implementation of the Law on Tobacco Control in 2015 and the establishment of 135 cessation and counselling services in the country.14 To build on these advancements and effectively address the persistent challenges related to tobacco consumption, it is crucial to understand the current prevalence and factors associated with consumption behaviours. Thus, this study aimed to assess the prevalence and associated factors of tobacco consumption among Cambodian individuals aged 15–49, utilising data from the 2021–2022 Cambodia Demographic and Health Survey (CDHS).

Methods

Study design

Our study utilised a cross-sectional design based on the secondary data from the 2021–2022 CDHS.

Participants and data source

This section explains the participants and data source from the original dataset, which is the 2021–2022 CDHS. 19 The CDHS targeted women aged 15–49 years and men aged 15–49 years who were usual residents or who spent the night before the survey in the selected households. For our analysis, we included all CDHS respondents aged 15–49 years with available information on tobacco use and relevant sociodemographic variables. There were no missing data for these variables, and no additional exclusion criteria were applied.

Sample size and sampling

Based on the official Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS) methodology, the 2021–2022 CDHS19 employed a two-stage stratified cluster sampling design to estimate key indicators at the national level as well as for urban and rural settings across all 25 provinces of Cambodia. In the first stage, 709 enumeration areas (EAs) were selected with probability proportional to size from a national sampling frame based on the 2019 General Population Census, which listed all 36 852 EAs (village or village segments). Each province was stratified into urban and rural areas, yielding 49 sampling strata. In the second stage, a systematic sample of 30 households was drawn from each selected EA after a household listing operation, resulting in 21 270 households (7230 urban and 14 040 rural). According to the DHS protocol, no household replacements were permitted. Trained interviewers conducted face-to-face interviews with all eligible household members aged 15–49. If a household was vacant or unavailable at the time of the initial visit, interviewers made follow-up visits at different times and on different days. Households that remained unreachable after these attempts were classified as non-responses. Consequently, the reported response rates (99% for households, 98% for women and 97% for men) exclude uncontacted households, ensuring that the rates accurately represent those reached during data collection. All women aged 15–49 years in the selected households were eligible for the women’s survey, while all men aged 15–49 years were interviewed in half of these households.

Based on the CDHS documentation,19 there were on average 1.30 eligible women per urban household and 1.06 per rural household, and 1.13 eligible men per urban household and 0.93 per rural household. Thus, most households contributed one eligible respondent, while some included two or more individuals aged 15–49 years. From the interviewed households, a total of 19 496 women (98% response rate) and 8825 men (97% response rate), all aged 15–49, were surveyed, contributing to a total of 28 321 respondents analysed in both the 2021–2022 CDHS19 and our study. Sampling weights were calculated to adjust for non-proportional allocation and non-response, ensuring national representativeness across all strata.

Training and data collection process

Training for the 2021–2022 CDHS19 included preparation in questionnaire administration and the computer-assisted personal interviewing system, along with mock interviews and field practice. Data collection was carried out by 25 field teams, each supported and monitored by the National Institute of Statistics (NIS), the Ministry of Health and ICF International (ICF), ensuring data quality and integrity. Data collection began on 15 September 2021, and was completed on 15 February 2022.

Measurements

The original 2021–2022 CDHS19 utilised four survey instruments: (1) Household Questionnaire, (2) Woman’s Questionnaire, (3) Man’s Questionnaire and (4) Biomarker Questionnaire. The analysis in our study considered two key components of the questionnaire: (1) background characteristics (age, gender, education, marital status, residence, occupation, wealth quintile, exposure to mass media, internet usage in the last 12 months and alcohol consumption) as independent variables and (2) tobacco consumption as the dependent variable.

Outcome variable

The outcome variable in our study was tobacco consumption, which was derived from four questions in the 2021–2022 CDHS.19 These questions asked respondents about: (a) current cigarette smoking (‘every day’, ‘some days’ or ‘not at all’); (b) other types of tobacco currently smoked or used (kreteks, pipes, cigars, cheroots/cigarillos, water pipe, snuff by mouth, snuff by nose, chewing tobacco, betel quid with tobacco and other products); (c) whether they currently smoked tobacco every day or some days; and (d) whether they currently used SLT every day or some days.

Because question (b) includes both smoked and smokeless products, we first created two binary indicators reflecting any smoked-tobacco use and any smokeless-tobacco use. Smoked-tobacco use was coded as 1 if the respondent (1) reported smoking cigarettes ‘every day’ or ‘some days’ in question (a) or (2) reported smoking tobacco ‘every day’ or ‘some days’ in question (c); or (3) selected any smoked product in question (b) (kreteks, pipes, cigars, cheroots/cigarillos or water pipe). Smokeless-tobacco use was coded as 1 if the respondent selected any smokeless product in question (b) (snuff by mouth or by nose, chewing tobacco, betel quid with tobacco or other smokeless forms) and/or reported smokeless-tobacco use ‘every day’ or ‘some days’ in question (d). Respondents not meeting these criteria were coded 0 for the corresponding indicator. The two indicators were then combined to generate a four-category tobacco-use outcome:

No tobacco use: smoked=0 and smokeless=0.

No tobacco use: smoked=0 and smokeless=0.

Smoking tobacco: smoked=1 and smokeless=0.

Smoking tobacco: smoked=1 and smokeless=0.

SLT: smoked=0 and smokeless=1.

SLT: smoked=0 and smokeless=1.

Dual use: smoked=1 and smokeless=1.

Dual use: smoked=1 and smokeless=1.

This approach ensures that dual users are defined as individuals who use at least one smoked and one SLT product concurrently, consistent with global tobacco-surveillance practices and reducing the risk of misclassification. Retaining four categories enables separate estimation of smoking, smokeless and dual-use patterns, providing greater analytical specificity than a binary or collapsed classification. Including a separate dual use category also allowed examination of an understudied subgroup with distinct behavioural patterns and potential risks, preserving important heterogeneity that would otherwise be obscured if categories were collapsed.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was conducted using STATA V.15. Descriptive statistics, including frequency and percentage, were utilised to interpret background characteristics and tobacco consumption. For inferential statistics, the χ2 test was first applied to assess the bivariate associations between the background characteristics and tobacco consumption. Variables with p<0.25 in the χ2 test were considered eligible for inclusion in the regression analysis.

Subsequently, both bivariable (unadjusted) and multivariable (adjusted) multinomial logistic regression analyses were performed to examine how independent variables were associated with tobacco consumption, which included four outcomes (no consumption, smoking tobacco, SLT and dual use), with ‘no consumption’ as the reference category. Adjusted relative risk ratios (ARRR) with 95% CIs were reported in the main text, while unadjusted relative risk ratios are presented in online supplemental material to facilitate comparison between crude and adjusted associations. All independent variables were entered simultaneously in the multivariable model based on their theoretical relevance and evidence from the literature, to control for potential confounding and minimise omitted variable bias. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIFs) calculated from an equivalent binary logistic model, as VIFs are not directly available for multinomial regression. All predictors had VIFs <5 (mean VIF=1.86), indicating low multicollinearity. Model fit was evaluated using a likelihood ratio test comparing the full model with the null model, which showed significantly better fit (LR χ²(81)=6843.74, p<0.001). All observations (N=28 321) were included, with no missing data on the outcome or predictor variables.

To account for the complex two-stage stratified cluster sampling design of the CDHS, sampling weights were applied using the ‘svyset’ command in STATA, which specifies the survey’s stratification, clustering and sampling weights before analysis. This procedure adjusts for unequal probabilities of selection and the clustered nature of the data, ensuring that both descriptive and regression analyses yield nationally representative and unbiased estimates. Following Global Tobacco Surveillance System and Global Adult Tobacco Survey recommendations, we also examined unweighted cell counts and observed that some SLT use and dual use subgroups contained fewer than 25 observations. These sparse cells were retained but interpreted with caution due to potential instability in the corresponding estimates. The weighting procedure followed the DHS programme’s standardised methodology,19 which applies normalised weights based on sample selection probabilities and response rates to ensure accurate population-level representation. Wealth quintiles were based on the standard DHS-constructed wealth index, derived using principal component analysis of household assets and household characteristics and categorised into five groups (lowest, second, middle, fourth and highest) following the DHS methodology. P values less than 0.05 were considered as indicating statistical significance.

Ethical considerations

For the 2021–2022 CDHS,19 the protocols and questions employed in standard DHS surveys underwent thorough review and approval by the ICF Institutional Review Board. Additionally, the Cambodia National Ethics Committee for Health Research granted ethical approval on 10 May 2021, with reference number 083 NECHR. We obtained permission from the DHS Program to use the dataset for our study, with approval granted on 27 November 2023. Furthermore, on December 8, 2023, the Research Ethics Committee at the Faculty of Medicine, Chiang Mai University, Thailand, exempted our secondary data analysis study from ethical review, covering both application and approval procedures (COE: 0598/2023). The original protocol for our study included both alcohol and tobacco use and their associated factors. However, the final analysis focused only on tobacco use. The study title and proposal were subsequently updated in the DHS Program system in line with its project information editing requirements.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and/or the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting or dissemination plans of this research.

Results

In our study, the outcomes were analysed based on data extracted from a total of 28 321 Cambodian people aged 15–49, obtained from the nationally representative 2021–2022 CDHS.

Factors associated with tobacco consumption

Table 4 exhibits the factors associated with tobacco consumption. All results were compared with the ‘no consumption’ category as the reference group. Respondents aged 40–49 were about five times more likely to smoke tobacco (ARRR=5.17, 95% CI 3.58 to 7.47, p<0.001). Male respondents were substantially more likely to smoke tobacco (ARRR=39.29, 95% CI 29.70 to 51.96, p<0.001) but less likely to consume SLT (ARRR=0.29, 95% CI 0.11 to 0.78, p=0.014) than females. Urban residents were about 46% less likely to consume both smoking and SLT (ARRR=0.54, 95% CI 0.34 to 0.85, p=0.008) than rural residents. Those who used the internet almost every day in the last 12 months were also less likely to smoke tobacco (ARRR=0.84, 95% CI 0.73 to 0.96, p=0.012) and to engage in dual use (ARRR=0.51, 95% CI 0.37 to 0.69, p<0.001). Alcohol consumption remained a strong correlate of tobacco use: respondents who consumed alcohol were more likely to smoke tobacco (ARRR=1.85, 95% CI 1.57 to 2.18, p<0.001), to consume SLT (ARRR=2.03, 95% CI 1.21 to 3.41, p=0.008), and to engage in dual use (ARRR=2.48, 95% CI 1.75 to 3.52, p<0.001). Other sociodemographic variables, including marital status, occupation and exposure to mass media, did not show statistically significant associations with tobacco use after adjustment.

Discussion

Our study found that 91.8% of respondents reported no tobacco consumption, consistent with the 2021 National Adults Tobacco Survey (NATS),15 which indicated a decrease in cigarette smoking in Cambodia from 16.6% in 2014 to 13.04% in 2021. This decline reflects effective tobacco control measures. The higher proportion of females in our sample (68.8%) may contribute to lower consumption rates; the NATS reported a 21.4% reduction in male smoking and a slight decrease among females from 2.4% to 2.05%.15 This aligns with the 2019 Cambodia Population Census,20 where females constituted approximately 51.3% of the population. Differences in smoking rates between the DHS (ages 15–49) and NATS (ages 18 and older) may stem from variations in sampling methods and regional factors. Additionally, cultural norms in Southeast Asian countries, including Vietnam,21 Thailand,22 Myanmar23 and Cambodia,24 may discourage female smoking, potentially influence survey participation and the disclosure of smoking status.

Despite the high percentage of non-consumers, the 6.9% prevalence of smoking in our study remains a public health concern, particularly given the 2021 NATS report,15 which highlights that many smokers in Cambodia are unmotivated to quit. While our analysis does not evaluate cessation interventions, it is important to situate these findings within the broader tobacco-control literature, where a range of evidence-based interventions have been shown to support quitting in other settings. These include pharmacological treatments25 such as nicotine replacement therapy, bupropion, varenicline, cytisine and antidepressants as well as non-pharmacological approaches26 such as cognitive behavioural therapy, health education and counselling. These examples are provided as established global strategies rather than recommendations derived from our data. Although SLT use was markedly low (0.2%), the presence of dual use (1.1%) highlights the complexity of tobacco behaviours in Cambodia. Dual use is associated with higher health risks and more difficult cessation.27 While our study cannot assess these mechanisms, the pattern observed reinforces the importance of understanding how multiple-product use develops and persists. Future studies in Cambodia could explore the motivations for dual use and assess which cessation approaches may be most applicable to this group.

Regarding the associated factors of tobacco consumption, our analysis reveals that individuals aged 40–49 are significantly more likely to smoke (ARRR=5.17, 95% CI 3.58 to 7.47, p<0.001), consistent with trends indicating that prolonged tobacco exposure and established habits in older age groups contribute to higher smoking rates. This raises concerns about nicotine dependence among those over 40.28 29 However, this pattern likely reflects initiation of tobacco use at younger ages and its continuation into adulthood, emphasising the importance of preventive measures aimed at adolescents and young adults to curb future smoking prevalence. While our study focuses on ages 15–49, the absence of data for individuals over 49 limits our understanding of their smoking behaviours. Therefore, future public health efforts may benefit from considering interventions tailored to older adults, including tobacco cessation and broader health promotion strategies. Additionally, higher educational attainment was linked to lower tobacco consumption and reduced smoking risk ratios in our study, supported by findings from a nationally representative study in Japan,12 which highlights education’s role in increasing smoking awareness.

Our study reveals a notable difference in tobacco use between genders, with male respondents more likely to smoke (ARRR=39.29, 95% CI 29.70 to 51.96, p<0.001) and less likely to use SLT (ARRR=0.29, 95% CI 0.11 to 0.78, p=0.014). This aligns with patterns where men typically have higher smoking rates.30 The lower likelihood of SLT use among men may reflect perceptions that smokeless products offer less immediate nicotine satisfaction and that smoking is more socially acceptable among Cambodian males. Men who smoke are significantly influenced by peer pressure,31 which leads to increased tobacco consumption and lower quit rates. A qualitative study similarly reported that external pressures are particularly prominent among men.32 These contextual factors suggest that gender norms and social influences may be important considerations for understanding tobacco use patterns in Cambodia, although intervention strategies were not evaluated in this study.

Those residing in urban areas were less likely to consume both smoking and SLT (ARRR=0.54, 95% CI 0.34 to 0.85, p=0.008), suggesting that urban environments provide better access to health information, education and cessation support as well as the implementation of control and regulatory efforts.11 This finding aligns with previous studies11 33 that demonstrate how urbanisation can facilitate healthier behaviours through improved access to services and community programmes. Given higher tobacco use in rural areas, enhanced outreach to rural communities may be relevant for future tobacco control efforts; however, such policy approaches were not evaluated in this study. Smoking decreased progressively with increasing wealth status. Respondents from higher wealth quintiles were less likely to smoke compared with those in the lowest quintile (ARRR=0.68, 0.57, 0.46, and 0.34 for the second through highest groups, all p<0.001). Although this association was consistent overall, tobacco use remained relatively common among middle-income groups, possibly reflecting affordability, social acceptance and limited access to cessation information compared with wealthier populations.

Furthermore, our study found that 67.6% of respondents used the internet in the past year, with frequent users (almost daily) being less likely to smoke tobacco (ARRR=0.84, 95% CI 0.73 to 0.96, p=0.012) and to engage in dual use (ARRR=0.51, 95% CI 0.37 to 0.69, p<0.001). This association is observational and does not imply causality. However, given the growing internet penetration in Cambodia, 57% in 2022 according to the World Bank,34 online platforms may serve as a useful channel for disseminating tobacco-related health information. While such digital interventions were not evaluated in this study, their potential relevance to tobacco control in Cambodia warrants further exploration. Although the CDHS19 does not include specific questions on exposure to tobacco-related or antitobacco messages, indicators such as internet use and mass media exposure were included as proxies for access to health information and communication channels that may influence awareness and behavioural outcomes.

Respondents who consumed alcohol were more likely to smoke tobacco (ARRR=1.85, 95% CI 1.57 to 2.18, p<0.001) and to engage in dual use (ARRR=2.48, 95% CI 1.75 to 3.52, p<0.001). While this association is well-documented in the literature,13 our study adds valuable local data that also align with a previous study in Cambodia from 2013, 35 highlighting how tobacco and alcohol behaviours are often interlinked. While our analysis did not evaluate interventions, this co-use of alcohol and tobacco suggests that future tobacco control efforts in Cambodia may benefit from considering the broader behavioural context in which tobacco and alcohol use occur.

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, self-reported data may lead to response biases, as participants might underreport or misclassify their tobacco consumption, and there was no biochemical verification of smoking status. Although the outcome categories were carefully reconstructed using all relevant CDHS items, some degree of misclassification may still persist due to the structure of the tobacco-use questions. Second, the sample was limited to individuals who were at home at the time of the interview, which may have biased the results towards people who are not formally employed or have poorer health, as they are more likely to be available during survey visits. Third, the focus on individuals aged 15–49 excludes older adults, which may overlook important patterns relevant to understanding tobacco consumption in Cambodia. Additionally, while the analysis includes associated factors such as age, gender and other individual characteristics, unmeasured influences like mental health and social factors may also impact tobacco use behaviours. Fourth, the very small number of smokeless-tobacco users and dual users (<25 unweighted cases in some subgroups) may contribute to wide CIs and reduced stability of some multinomial regression estimates. Fifth, although the approach of selecting variables based on bivariate p<0.25 is aligned with common DHS analytic practice, other model-building strategies may also be appropriate. The cross-sectional design of our study limits causal inference. Furthermore, the 2021–2022 CDHS lacks information on electronic cigarettes and other emerging nicotine products, which restricts the ability to assess newer patterns of tobacco use. These limitations highlight the need for further research that includes a broader age range, considers additional factors influencing tobacco use and incorporates data on emerging nicotine products across diverse populations.

Strengths of the study

This study is the first to extensively examine various tobacco consumption patterns, including smoking, SLT use and dual use, in Cambodia using the latest 2021–2022 CDHS data. Our large sample size and nationally representative data provide robust estimates for policy planning, making this study a cost-effective alternative to conducting a new primary data collection. The inclusion of both smoking and SLT use offers a complete picture of tobacco consumption patterns, enhancing understanding of current trends. These findings will support evidence-based public health interventions and serve as a valuable resource for health professionals and policymakers, potentially influencing global discussions on public health initiatives related to tobacco consumption.

Recommendations for public health interventions

While the present study does not evaluate interventions, the observed associations may offer contextual insights that could inform future tobacco control efforts in Cambodia. Several potential strategic directions may be considered for exploration in future research and programme development. The higher prevalence of smoking among adults aged 40–49 suggests that age-specific cessation support could be relevant for future tobacco control initiatives, while preventive education targeting adolescents and young adults may help reduce early initiation. Gender-specific approaches may also be valuable, given the markedly different patterns of tobacco use between men and women, and the social and cultural influences affecting male smoking behaviours. The strong association between alcohol consumption and smoking suggests that integrated screening or counselling approaches may warrant consideration, although such models were not assessed in this study and should be pilot-tested for feasibility. Rural populations, who exhibited higher tobacco use, may benefit from strengthened outreach mechanisms, such as health centres and Village Health Support Groups, through enhanced training and community-based health communication, though these approaches require further investigation. While increasing internet penetration in Cambodia, digital platforms may represent a useful avenue for disseminating tobacco-related health information. Although such interventions were not evaluated in our study, their potential relevance to tobacco control merits future exploration. Finally, continued strengthening of national tobacco control policies, particularly those aimed at reducing tobacco access and promoting cessation support, may contribute to sustained progress in reducing tobacco-related harm. These recommendations are intended as contextual considerations rather than evidence-based recommendations from this study, and future pilot programmes or implementation research will be necessary to evaluate their feasibility and impact.

Conclusion

Our study reveals important patterns of tobacco consumption among Cambodian individuals aged 15–49, indicating that most respondents abstain from tobacco use while identifying notable differences by age, gender and other individual characteristics. These findings reinforce the value of ongoing tobacco control efforts in Cambodia and provide context for understanding which population groups may be more likely to use tobacco products. While the study did not evaluate interventions, the demographic patterns observed, such as higher smoking prevalence among men, older adults and those who consume alcohol, may help inform future planning of tobacco control initiatives. Further research, including evaluation studies and intervention trials, will be essential to determine which strategies are most effective in reducing tobacco use and addressing the unique needs of specific subgroups.
