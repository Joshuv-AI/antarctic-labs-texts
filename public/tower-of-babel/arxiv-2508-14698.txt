Fourier decay and absolute continuity for typical homogeneous self-similar measures in ℝ d {\mathbb{R}}^{d} for d ≥ 3 d\geq 3 Thanks: Supported in part by the Israel Science Foundation grant #1647/23.
Boris Solomyak

Abstract
. We consider iterated function systems (IFS) in ℝ d {\mathbb{R}}^{d} for d ≥ 3 d\geq 3 of the form { f j ( x ) = λ 𝒪 x + a j } j = 0 m \{f_{j}(x)=\lambda{\mathcal{O}}x+a_{j}\}_{j=0}^{m} , with a 0 = 0 a_{0}=0 and m ≥ 1 m\geq 1 . Here λ ∈ ( 0 , 1 ) \lambda\in(0,1) is the contraction ratio and 𝒪 {\mathcal{O}} is an orthogonal matrix. Given a positive probability vector 𝒑 \boldsymbol{p} , there is a unique invariant (stationary) measure for the IFS, called (in this case) a homogeneous self-similar measure, which we denote μ ⁡ ( λ ​ 𝒪 , 𝒟 , 𝒑 ) \mu(\lambda{\mathcal{O}},{\mathcal{D}},\boldsymbol{p}) , where 𝒟 = { a 0 , … , a m } {\mathcal{D}}=\{a_{0},\ldots,a_{m}\} is the set of “vector digits”. We obtain two results on Fourier decay for such measures. First we show that if 𝒟 {\mathcal{D}} spans ℝ d {\mathbb{R}}^{d} , then for every fixed 𝒪 {\mathcal{O}} and 𝒑 \boldsymbol{p} the measure μ ⁡ ( λ ​ 𝒪 , 𝒟 , 𝒑 ) \mu(\lambda{\mathcal{O}},{\mathcal{D}},\boldsymbol{p}) has power Fourier decay (equivalently, positive Fourier dimension) for all but a zero-Hausdorff dimension set of λ \lambda . In our second result we do not impose any restrictions on 𝒟 {\mathcal{D}} , other than the necessary one of affine irreducibility, and obtain power Fourier decay for almost all homogeneous self-similar measures; however, only for even d ≥ 4 d\geq 4 . Combined with recent work of Corso and Shmerkin [ 9 ] , these results imply absolute continuity for almost all self-similar measures under the same assumptions, in the super-critical parameter region.



1.
Introduction and statement of results

For a finite positive Borel measure
\mu
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, consider the Fourier transform

\widehat{\mu}(\xi)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}e^{-2\pi i\langle\xi,x\rangle}\,d\mu(x).

The measure is called
Rajchman
if
\widehat{\mu}(\xi)
tends to zero at infinity. By the Riemann-Lebesgue Lemma, every absolutely continuous measure
is Rajchman, but absolute continuity is not necessary. It is a delicate and much studied question to decide which singular measures are Rajchman.
Assuming they are, it is important to obtain specific quantitative bounds for the rate of decay at infinity.
Let
{\mathcal{M}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
be the set of finite positive measures in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}

For
\gamma>0
consider

{\mathscr{D}}_{d}(\gamma)=\bigl\{\nu\in{\mathcal{M}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}):\ \left|\widehat{\nu}(\xi)\right|=O_{\nu}(|\xi|^{-\gamma}),\ \ |\xi|\to\infty\bigr\},

and denote
{\mathscr{D}}_{d}=\bigcup_{\gamma>0}{\mathscr{D}}_{d}(\gamma)
. A measure
\nu
is said to have
power Fourier decay
if
\nu\in{\mathscr{D}}_{d}
.
This is equivalent to having a positive
Fourier dimension
, see
[
26
]
.
Further, let

{\mathscr{D}}_{d,\log}=\bigl\{\nu\in{\mathcal{M}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}):\ \exists\,\alpha>0,\ \left|\widehat{\nu}(\xi)\right|=O_{\nu}\bigl(\bigl|\log|\xi|\bigr|^{-\alpha}\bigr),\ \ |\xi|\to\infty\bigr\}.

Such measures are said to have
polylogarithmic Fourier decay
. Of course, other decay rates may occur as well.

There is on-going very intensive research of the Fourier decay phenomena and its applications, for
a broad class of measures which may be termed “fractal”. We are not going to give a comprehensive literature review: a “sample” of recent papers, which includes two surveys, is
[
2
,
3
,
31
,
4
,
5
,
23
]
; many more references can be found there.
However, much of the recent progress is for measures coming from non-linear dynamics, in particular, for stationary (invariant) measure of non-linear iterated function systems (IFS). Here we focus exclusively on the linear case.

A measure
\mu
is called
self-affine
if it is the invariant measure for a self-affine IFS
\{f_{j}\}_{j=0}^{m}
, with
m\geq 1
, where
f_{j}(x)=A_{j}x+a_{j}
, the matrices
A_{j}:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
are invertible linear contractions (in some norm) and
a_{j}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
are “digit” vectors.
This means that for some probability vector
\boldsymbol{p}=(p_{j})_{j\leq m}
holds

(1.1)

\mu=\sum_{j=0}^{m}p_{j}(\mu\circ f_{j}^{-1}).

The IFS is
self-similar
if all
A_{j}
are contracting similitudes, that is,
A_{j}=\lambda_{j}{\mathcal{O}}_{j}
for some
\lambda_{j}\in(0,1)
and orthogonal matrices
{\mathcal{O}}_{j}
.
The IFS and the corresponding invariant measure are called
homogeneous self-affine
(respectively,
homogeneous self-similar
)
if
A_{j}=A
for all
j\leq m
, and
A
is a general linear contracting map (respectively, a contracting similitude). Our results are on self-similar homogeneous IFS, and we
review the relevant history with some detail; however, we do not intend to duplicate the excellent survey by Sahlsten
[
31
]
, which is much more exhaustive.
Our main motivation for showing the power Fourier decay is to study
absolute continuity
of self-similar measures, but
it has many other applications which we do not discuss here, see, e.g.,
[
31
]
.

1.1.
Historical background

We start with the most basic case:
d=1
,
m=2
, a homogeneous self-similar measure. Up to an affine-linear conjugacy, we obtain a 1-parameter family of IFS
\{\lambda x,\lambda x+1\}
. The resulting self-similar measures are known as
Bernoulli convolutions
; they were
extensively studied since the 1930’s. Denote by
\nu_{\lambda}^{p}
the Bernoulli convolution, corresponding to the probabilities
(p,1-p)
. In the “classical case”

p=1/2
we omit the superscript
p
.
Erdős
[
12
]
proved that
\nu_{\lambda}
is not Rajchman when
\theta=1/\lambda
is a
PV (or Pisot)
number, that is,
an algebraic integer greater than one, all of whose Galois conjugates lie inside the unit circle. Salem
[
32
]
showed that if

1/\lambda
is not a Pisot number, then
\widehat{\nu}_{\lambda}
does vanish at infinity. What about decay rates?
Erdős
[
13
]
proved that for any compact interval
J\subset(0,1)
there exists
\alpha=\alpha(J)>0
such that

\nu_{\lambda}\in{\mathscr{D}}_{1}(\alpha)
for a.e.
\lambda\in J
. In a short but illuminating expository note, Kahane
[
19
]
indicated that Erdős’ argument actually gives that
\nu_{\lambda}\in{\mathscr{D}}_{1}
for all
\lambda\in(0,1)
in the complement of an exceptional set of zero Hausdorff dimension. (This technique became known as the
Erdős-Kahane argument
, which turned out to be useful in other contexts as well.)
All the above results hold for any

p\in(0,1)
; moreover,
the Erdős-Kahane argument works for any family of homogeneous IFS on the line, with the contraction rate as a parameter. As it often happens with results of “almost sure” kind, few specific
\lambda
are known, all algebraic, for which
\nu_{\lambda}
has power Fourier decay.
Dai, Feng, and Wang
[
11
]
proved this for the classical Bernoulli convolution
\nu_{\lambda}
, when
\theta=\lambda^{-1}
is a
Garsia number
, i.e.,
an algebraic integer, all of whose conjugates have modulus greater than 1, and the constant term of its minimal polynomial is
\pm 2
.
Recently, Streck
[
41
]
generalized this to a larger class of homogeneous IFS, still with a very special
algebraic contraction ratio.

What about weaker decay rates for explicit
\lambda
? Kershner
[
20
]
(see also
[
10
]
) proved that
\nu_{\lambda}\in{\mathscr{D}}_{1,\log}
for a rational
contraction rate
\lambda=p/q
, with
1<p<q
(note that for
\lambda=1/q
, with
q\geq 3
, the measure
\nu_{\lambda}
is non-Rajchman; in fact, these are the
simplest Pisot Bernoulli convolutions). In a joint work with Bufetov
[
8
, Corollary A.3]
it was shown that if
\lambda^{-1}
is an
algebraic integer which has at least one conjugate outside the unit circle, then
\nu_{\lambda}\in{\mathscr{D}}_{1,\log}
as well; this was extended by Gao and Ma
[
14
]
to an arbitrary set of digits.

The case of a
Salem number

\theta=\lambda^{-1}
is very interesting, but remains mysterious. This is, by definition, an algebraic integer which has all of its conjugates satisfying
|\theta_{j}|\leq 1
, but not a Pisot number.
It is known that
\nu_{\lambda}\notin{\mathscr{D}}_{1}
in the Salem case, see

[
19
]
(and
[
28
, Section 5]
for more details).
Curiously, it is not known whether the property
\nu_{\lambda}\in{\mathscr{D}}_{1}(\alpha)
is
topologically generic (of second category) in
(0,1)
. In fact, if it is, then the Salem numbers do not accumulate to one (which would confirm a
well-known conjecture). This follows from the fact that the set
\{\lambda\in(0,1):\ \nu_{\lambda}\notin{\mathscr{D}}_{1}\}
is
G_{\delta}
, see
[
28
, Lemma 5.4]
, and
the implication
\nu_{\lambda}\notin{\mathscr{D}}_{1}\implies\ \nu_{\lambda^{2}}\notin{\mathscr{D}}_{1}
.

Note that all the delicate arithmetic phenomena, having to do with the contraction ratio
\lambda
, disappear if we introduce dependence on a translation parameter. For instance, for the family of IFS
\{\lambda x,1+\lambda x,a+\lambda x\}
, with any fixed
\lambda\in(0,1)
and any probability vector

\boldsymbol{p}>0
, the corresponding self-similar measure belongs to
{\mathscr{D}}_{1}
for all
a\in{\mathbb{R}}\setminus E
, where
\dim_{\rm H}(E)=0
; see

[
35
, Proposition 3.1]
.

Next we briefly discuss absolute continuity of Bernoulli convolutions and more general homogeneous self-similar measures on
{\mathbb{R}}
. Using the convolution structure of
\nu_{\lambda}
and his “a.e.
\lambda
” power Fourier decay result, Erdős
[
13
]
deduced that
\nu_{\lambda}

is absolutely continuous, even of arbitrarily high smoothness, fixed
in advance, for Lebesgue-a.e.
\lambda
in a sufficiently small neighborhood of 1. A different,
transversality method
, was used to confirm
absolute continuity of
\nu_{\lambda}
for Lebesgue-a.e.
\lambda\in(1/2,1)
in
[
37
]
(see also
[
29
]
), and to estimate the Hausdorff dimension of the exceptional set
[
27
]
. More recently, following breakthrough results of Hochman
[
15
]
on the dimension of
self-similar measures, Shmerkin
[
33
]

showed that this exceptional set has Hausdorff dimension zero, invoking the classical Erdős-Kahane statement on power Fourier decay and a
beautiful “convolution lemma,” which we also use in this paper:

Lemma 1.1

(Shmerkin
[
33
]
)
.

Suppose that
\mu
and
\nu
are finite measures in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, such that
\dim_{\rm H}(\mu)=d
and
\nu\in{\mathscr{D}}_{d}
. Then
\mu*\nu
is absolutely continuous with
respect to
{\mathcal{L}}^{d}
.

Many of the above results, especially those for “typical” parameters, extend to arbitrary homogeneous self-similar measures on
{\mathbb{R}}
, with various kinds
of parameter dependence, see
[
33
,
35
]
.
Until a few years ago,
the only explicit parameters
\lambda
, for which
\nu_{\lambda}
is known to be absolutely continuous, were the reciprocals of Garsia numbers. More recently,
in a continuation of the line of research pioneered by Hochman
[
15
]
, but with many further delicate and technical innovations,
absolutely continuity was established
for a much larger class of examples,
all algebraic and satisfying certain arithmetic conditions, due to Varjú
[
42
]
and Kittle
[
21
]
.

In higher dimensions issues of irreducibility arise. Following
[
16
]
, we say that the IFS is
affinely irreducible
if the attractor is not contained in a proper affine subspace of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. It is easy to see that this is a necessary condition for the self-affine measure to be Rajchman, so this will always be our assumption. The IFS is called
linearly irreducible
if the linear maps
A_{j}
do not have a common proper invariant subspace.

We next consider self-similar homogeneous IFS in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
. Affine irreducibility means that the attractor is not contained in a line.
There are 2 cases:

(1)

Linearly reducible case.

If
A_{j}=A=\lambda\cdot I
, for
\lambda\in(-1,1)
, then power Fourier decay, for all but a zero Hausdorff dimension set of exceptions,
easily follows by the classical 1-dimensional argument. However, to deduce “typical”
absolute continuity via Shmerkin’s Lemma
1.1
is problematic, except in some very
special symmetric cases, because Hochman’s higher-dimensional results

[
16
]
require linear irreducibility. These issues will be clarified below for self-similar homogeneous IFS in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
for any
d\geq 2
.

(2)

Linearly irreducible case.

View
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\cong{\mathbb{C}}
as the complex plane, and let
Az=\lambda z
, where
\lambda
is a non-real complex number, with
|\lambda|<1
. In this setting, power Fourier
decay was obtained for all
\lambda
outside a zero-Hausdorff dimensional set of exceptional
parameters in a joint work with Shmerkin
[
34
]
. Moreover, Hochman’s
[
16
, Theorem 1.5]
applies here, so we obtained absolute
continuity of the self-similar measure for a.e. parameter in the super-critical region, more precisely, outside of a (different)
zero-Hausdorff dimensional set of parameters, see
[
34
, Theorem B]
.

The next step is to consider homogeneous self-similar IFS in dimensions
\geq 3
, and this is the topic of the current paper. One of the difficulties is that such
a system is
never
linearly irreducible.

We only mention the case of
non-homogeneous self-similar IFS
in passing, without going into details.
For questions on Fourier decay of non-homogeneous self-similar measures on
{\mathbb{R}}
, the reader is referred to

[
1
,
6
,
24
,
38
,
43
]
and references therein. Absolute continuity of such measures, for almost all relevant parameters, was established in
[
30
]
, and this was
extended to the planar case in a joint work with Śpiewak
[
40
]
. Lindenstrauss and Varjú
[
25
]
proved that in dimension
d\geq 3
, if the rotation parts of the similarities generate random walk with a spectral gap, then the self-similar measure is absolutely continuous, assuming that all the
contraction rations are sufficiently close to one. In the survey
[
31
]
Sahlsten showed how the methods of
[
25
]

can be used to obtain power Fourier decay in their setting (e.g., for algebraic rotation parts of the similarities in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,
d\geq 3
, when the spectral gap hypothesis holds) for any common contraction ratio
0<\lambda<1
, see
[
31
, Theorem 4.3]
.
Recently a significant
advance was achieved by Kittle and Kogler
[
22
]
, including
first explicit examples of absolutely continuous non-homogeneous self-similar measures in dimensions 1 and 2, and an extension of
[
25
]
.

1.2.
Statement of new results

Denote the digit set by
{\mathcal{D}}:=\{a_{0},\ldots,a_{m}\}
.
By a conjugation with a translation, we can always assume that
a_{0}=0\in{\mathcal{D}}
. In this case
affine irreducibility is equivalent to the digit set
{\mathcal{D}}
being a
cyclic family
for
A
, that is,
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
being the smallest
A
-invariant subspace containing
{\mathcal{D}}
.
Denote the homogeneous self-similar measure, determined by the contraction similarity map
A
, digit set
{\mathcal{D}}
, and the
probability vector
\boldsymbol{p}
, by
\mu(A,{\mathcal{D}},\boldsymbol{p})
. We will write
\boldsymbol{p}>0
if all
p_{j}>0
.
The special feature of a homogeneous self-similar (and a self-affine) measure is that it can be expressed as an infinite convolution product

(1.2)

\mu(A,{\mathcal{D}},\boldsymbol{p})=\Bigl(\Asterisk\prod\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}\Bigr)\sum_{j=0}^{m}p_{j}\delta_{A^{n}a_{j}}.

For every
\boldsymbol{p}>0
it is supported on the attractor (self-similar set)

K_{A,{\mathcal{D}}}:=\Bigl\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}:\ x=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}A^{n}b_{n},\ b_{n}\in{\mathcal{D}}\Bigr\}.

Theorem 1.2
.

For
d\geq 1
, let
A=\vartheta^{-1}{\mathcal{O}}
, where
\vartheta>1
and
{\mathcal{O}}\in O(d,{\mathbb{R}})
. Let
{\mathcal{D}}=\{0,a_{1},\ldots,a_{m}\}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
be any set of digits in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, such that
{\rm Span}({\mathcal{D}})={\mathbb{R}}^{d}
(so that
m\geq d
). Let
\boldsymbol{p}
be a positive probability vector.
Then the corresponding self-similar measure
\mu(\vartheta^{-1}{\mathcal{O}},{\mathcal{D}},\boldsymbol{p})
belongs to
{\mathscr{D}}_{d}
for all
\vartheta>1
outside of a set of Hausdorff dimension zero.

The proof is based on a variant of the Erdős-Kahane technique.

Next we state our second result on Fourier decay. The natural measure on the set of
similitudes
A=\vartheta^{-1}\cdot{\mathcal{O}}
, with
\vartheta>1
and

{\mathcal{O}}\in O(d,{\mathbb{R}})
is
is
{\mathcal{L}}^{1}|_{(0,1)}\times m_{d}
, where
m_{d}
is the Haar measure on
O(d,{\mathbb{R}})
.
Every orthogonal matrix
is diagonalizable, with eigenvalues of modulus 1. Having a real eigenvalue
\pm 1
is a degenerate case for
d
even; for a.e. orthogonal matrix
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2s}
all the eigenvalues are non-real and distinct. If
d=2s+1
, then
m_{d}
-a.e. orthogonal matrix in
O(d,{\mathbb{R}})
has one real eigenvalue
\pm 1

and
s
non-real distinct eigenvalues. Denote
{\mathbb{T}}^{+}:=\{e^{2\pi i\alpha}:\ \alpha\in(0,\frac{1}{2})\}
. We will use the notation

[N]=\{1,\ldots,N\}
for
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
.

Theorem 1.3
.

For almost every contraction similitude
A=\vartheta^{-1}{\mathcal{O}}
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
for an even
d
, for every cyclic set of digits
{\mathcal{D}}
and a probability vector
\boldsymbol{p}>0
, the
homogeneous self-similar measure
\mu=\mu(A,{\mathcal{D}},\boldsymbol{p})
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
has power Fourier decay, i.e.,
\mu\in{\mathscr{D}}_{d}
.

More precisely, for
d=2s
, with
s\geq 1
, let
{\mathcal{O}}
be an orthogonal matrix with distinct non-real eigenvalues

e^{\pm 2\pi i\alpha_{j}}
. Using a linear conjugacy and the identification
{\mathbb{R}}^{2s}\cong{\mathbb{C}}^{s}
, we can assume
without loss of generality, that
{\mathcal{O}}
is a diagonal
complex
s\times s
matrix

{\mathcal{O}}={\rm Diag}[e^{-2\pi i\alpha_{1}},\ldots,e^{-2\pi i\alpha_{s}}],\ \ \alpha_{j}\in(0,1/2).

Suppose that the digit set
{\mathcal{D}}=\{0,a_{1},\ldots,a_{m}\}
, with
a_{j}=(a_{j}^{(1)},\ldots,a_{j}^{(s)})\in{\mathbb{C}}^{s}
, is a cyclic set for
A

(equivalently, for every
\ell\in[s]
there exists
a_{j}

with
a_{j}^{(\ell)}\neq 0
). Fix
\vartheta>1
and
\boldsymbol{p}>0
, and
let
\ell\in[s]
. Then for every list
\{\alpha_{j}\}_{j\in[s]\setminus\ell}
of distinct numbers in
(0,1/2)
, there exists a set

{\mathcal{E}}_{\ell}\subset\vartheta\cdot{\mathbb{T}}^{+}
of
zero Hausdorff dimension, such that for all
\alpha_{\ell}
, with
e^{-2\pi i\alpha_{\ell}}\notin\vartheta^{-1}\cdot{\mathcal{E}}_{\ell}
, the Fourier transform
\widehat{\mu}(\vartheta^{-1}{\mathcal{O}},{\mathcal{D}},\boldsymbol{p})

has uniform power decay in the direction of vectors
\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2s}
satisfying

|\xi_{2\ell-1}+i\xi_{2\ell}|=\max_{j\leq s}|\xi_{2j-1}+i\xi_{2j}|.

Remark 1.4
.

(a) The first claim in the theorem above, about almost every contraction similitude
A=\vartheta^{-1}{\mathcal{O}}
, follows from the more precise statement by Fubini’s Theorem, with the exceptional set of parameters being
\bigcup_{\ell\in[s]}\bigl\{\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in(0,\frac{1}{2})^{\ell}:\ e^{-2\pi i\alpha_{\ell}}\in\vartheta^{-1}\cdot{\mathcal{E}}_{\ell}\bigr\}
.

(b)
The restriction to only even dimensions
d
is an artefact of the proof; we do not think it is essential, but we were not able to remove it.
It can be explained, roughly, as follows:

The argument proceeds by fixing all the eigenvalues, except one, say,
\ell
’s eigenvalue, and running a variant of the Erdős-Kahane scheme to estimate the Hausdorff dimension of the set of “bad” values of the undetermined eigenvalue for the Fourier decay. The problem arises
for direction vectors with a small
\ell
-component, with the bounds becoming non-uniform. Thus we are forced to exclude all
eigenvalues in turn.
Suppose
d=2s+1
.
Almost every similitude in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2s+1}
has
2s
non-real distinct eigenvalues and one real eigenvalue.
Excluding the real eigenvalue does not work, since the other eigenvalues determine it up to sign. All that we can get
for a typical homogeneous self-similar measure in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2s+1}
, with a single real eigenvalue,
is uniform power Fourier decay outside of an arbitrary circular cone around the real eigen-direction, fixed in advance. This is shown
by a minor modification of the proof of Theorem
1.3
; we leave the details to the reader.

1.3.
Application: fat Sierpiński tetrahedron

Consider the following IFS in
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
:

\{\lambda x,\ \lambda x+{\mathbf{e}}_{1},\ \lambda x+{\mathbf{e}}_{2},\ \lambda x+{\mathbf{e}}_{3}\}.

We call its attractor, denoted

S^{(3)}_{\lambda}
,
for
\lambda\in(\frac{1}{2},1)
, the “fat Sierpiński tetrahedron,” by analogy with the “fat Sierpiński gasket,” studied by many authors;
see, e.g.,
[
36
,
7
,
17
,
18
]
.

Corollary 1.5
.

For all
\lambda\in(4^{-1/3},1)
,
outside of a set of zero Hausdorff dimension, the set
S_{\lambda}
has positive 3-dimensional Lebesgue measure.

Proof sketch.

We use the method of Shmerkin
[
33
]
to show absolute continuity of the uniform self-similar measure on

S^{(3)}_{\lambda}
for
\lambda\in(4^{-1/3},1)\setminus{\mathcal{E}}
, where
\dim_{\rm H}({\mathcal{E}})=0
. We can replace the set of digits
\{0,{\mathbf{e}}_{1},{\mathbf{e}}_{2},{\mathbf{e}}_{3}\}
by the set of vertices of a regular tetrahedron
{\mathcal{T}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
, which has a larger group of symmetry, via a linear conjugacy, which preserves all the properties
of the self-similar measure.
Denote by
\mu_{\lambda}
the corresponding uniform self-similar measure in
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
.
We can write

(1.3)

\mu_{\lambda}=\mu_{\lambda^{k}}*\widetilde{\mu}_{\lambda}^{(k)},

where
\widetilde{\mu}_{\lambda}^{(k)}
is obtained by skipping every
k
-th term in the random base-
\lambda
series representation.
It suffices to obtain the absolute continuity result
for the intervals
\bigl(4^{-\frac{k-1}{3k}},1\bigr)
for all
k
. We have

\mu_{\lambda^{k}}\in{\mathscr{D}}_{3}
for all

\lambda\in(0,1)\setminus{\mathcal{E}}_{k}^{(1)}
, with
\dim_{\rm H}({\mathcal{E}}_{k}^{(1)})=0
, by Theorem
1.2
.

For the measure
\widetilde{\mu}_{\lambda}^{(k)}
we would like to use Hochman’s results
[
16
]
on the dimension of self-similar measures
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.
Note that

\widetilde{\mu}_{\lambda}^{(k)}
is a self-similar measure of similarity dimension
\frac{\log(4^{k-1})}{\log(1/\lambda^{k})}>3
in this parameter interval.
We cannot use
[
16
, Corollary 1.6]
directly, since the homogeneous IFS in
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
is not linearly irreducible.
However, in view of the symmetry, since
\widetilde{\mu}_{\lambda}^{(k)}
has uniform weights,
we obtain the same self-similar measure if the functions mapping
the big tetrahedron
{\mathcal{T}}
onto the
4^{k-1}
small ones, are pre-composed with a rotation taking
{\mathcal{T}}
into itself. This rotation may be
different for each smaller tetrahedron. This allows us to avoid a common proper invariant subspace for the linear parts of the IFS maps;
for instance, by using rotations about lines perpendicular to the facets of
{\mathcal{T}}
. A standard argument (see
[
16
, Section 6.6]
) yields that there exists a set
{\mathcal{E}}_{k}^{(2)}
of zero Hausdorff (even packing) dimension, such that for all

\lambda\in\bigl(4^{-\frac{k-1}{3k}},1\bigr)\setminus{\mathcal{E}}_{k}^{(2)}
the resulting IFS satisfies the exponential separation condition, hence

\dim_{H}(\widetilde{\mu}_{\lambda}^{(k)})=3
for all
\lambda\in\bigl(4^{-\frac{k-1}{3k}},1\bigr)\setminus{\mathcal{E}}_{k}^{(2)}
by
Hochman’s
[
16
, Corollary 1.6]
.
Finally, using (the higher-dimensional version of)

obtain absolute continuity for all

∎

The “fat tetrahedron” construction and the Lebesgue measure claim
can be analogously extended to an arbitrary dimension
d\geq 4
; we used
d=3
for
simplicity.

Recall Hochman’s exponential separation (ES) condition, used above, in the special case of self-similar IFS.

Definition 1.6

(M. Hochman
[
15
,
16
]
)
.

A homogeneous IFS
f_{j}(x)=Ax+a_{j},\ a_{j}\in{\mathcal{D}}
, is said to satisfy the ES condition if there exist a sequence
N_{j}\to\infty
and
{\varepsilon}>0
,
such that for any two words
v\neq w\in{\mathcal{D}}^{N_{j}+1}
, holds

\Bigl\|\sum_{n=0}^{N_{j}}A^{n}(v_{n}-w_{n})\Bigr\|\geq{\varepsilon}^{N_{j}}.

Equivalently, for any positive probability vector
\boldsymbol{p}
, the atoms of discrete measures approximating the self-similar measure
\mu(A,{\mathcal{D}},\boldsymbol{p})
:

\mu_{N_{j}}(A,{\mathcal{D}},\boldsymbol{p}):=\Bigl(\Asterisk\prod\limits_{n=0}^{N_{j}}\Bigr)\sum_{j=0}^{m}p_{j}\delta_{A^{n}a_{j}},

are
{\varepsilon}^{N_{j}}
-separated.

1.4.
Application: absolute continuity via
L^{q}
dimension

This application is based on a recent paper of Corso and Shmerkin
[
9
]
. In order to state their result, we need to
recall the notion of
L^{q}
dimension for a probability measure
\nu
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. It is given by

\dim_{q}(\nu)=\frac{\tau_{q}(\nu)}{q-1},\ \ q>1,\ \ \mbox{where}\ \ \tau_{q}(\nu)=\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{-\log\sum_{Q\in D_{n}}\nu(Q)^{q}}{n},

and
D_{n}
is the partition of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
into
2^{-n}
-mesh cubes. The “self-similar”
L^{q}
dimension of a homogeneous self-similar
measure
\mu
, corresponding to a weighted ISF with a contraction ratio
\vartheta^{-1}
and probability vector
\boldsymbol{p}=(p_{j})
, provides a
natural upper bound for the
L^{q}
-dimension of
\mu
:

(1.4)

\dim_{q}(\mu)\leq\dim_{\rm s}(\mu,q):=\frac{-\log\sum_{j}p_{j}^{q}}{(q-1)\log\vartheta}.

Now we can state
[
9
, Theorem 1.11]
, which is slightly reformulated for our purposes.

Theorem 1.7

(E. Corso and P. Shmerkin, 2024 preprint)
.

Let
d=2s+r
, with
r\in\{0,1\}
. Consider a homogeneous self-similar IFS in
{\mathbb{C}}^{s}\oplus{\mathbb{R}}^{r}\cong{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
of the following form.
Let
\vartheta>1
and
\alpha_{k}\in[0,1)
,
1\leq k\leq s
, be
such that
\alpha_{k}\notin{\mathbb{Q}}
and
\alpha_{i}-\alpha_{k}\notin{\mathbb{Q}}
for
i\neq k
(in particular,
\alpha_{i}\neq\alpha_{k}
). The IFS is

\Phi=\{f_{j}\}_{j=0}^{m}
where
f_{j}({\mathbf{x}})=\vartheta^{-1}{\mathcal{O}}({\mathbf{x}})+a_{j}
, with

{\mathcal{O}}={\rm Diag}[e^{-2\pi i\alpha_{1}},\ldots,e^{-2\pi i\alpha_{s}}]\ \ \mbox{and}\ \ a_{j}=(a_{j}^{(1)},\ldots,a_{j}^{(s)})\in{\mathbb{C}}^{s},\ \ \mbox{if}\ d=2s,

or

{\mathcal{O}}={\rm Diag}[e^{-2\pi i\alpha_{1}},\ldots,e^{-2\pi i\alpha_{s}},\pm 1]\ \ \mbox{and}\ \ a_{j}=(a_{j}^{(1)},\ldots,a_{j}^{(s)},a_{j}^{(d)})\in{\mathbb{C}}^{s}\oplus{\mathbb{R}},\ \ \mbox{if}\ d=2s+1.

Main Assumption:
All the coordinate IFS of the form

\{f_{j}^{(\ell)}\}_{j=0}^{m}
, where

f_{j}^{(\ell)}(z)=\vartheta^{-1}e^{-2\pi i\alpha_{\ell}}z+a_{j}^{(\ell)},\ \ \ell=1,\ldots,s,\ \ \mbox{on}\ \ {\mathbb{C}},

and

f_{j}^{(d)}(x)=\pm\vartheta^{-1}x+a_{j}^{(d)},\ \ \mbox{if}\ d=2s+1,\ \ \mbox{on}\ \ {\mathbb{R}},

fulfil the exponential separation condition.

Then, for any probability vector
\boldsymbol{p}>0
, the associated
self-similar measure satisfies

\dim_{q}(\mu)=\min\{d,\dim_{\rm s}(\mu,q)\}.

Remark 1.8
.

(a) The results of Corso and Shmerkin
[
9
]
are much more general; they work in the framework of “pleasant models,”
see
[

, Section 1]

(b) The Main Assumption
in the last theorem is a special case of the “projected exponential separation” condition, see
[

, Definition 1.10]

(c) Corso and Shmerkin
[
9
]
obtain analogous results on

dimensions of projected measures to arbitrary subspaces.

2.
Preliminary steps of the proof of Fourier decay

By the definition of the self-similar measure,

\widehat{\mu}(\xi)=\sum_{j=0}^{m}p_{j}\int e^{-2\pi i\langle\xi,Ax+a_{j}\rangle}\,d\mu(x)=\Bigl(\sum_{j=0}^{m}p_{j}e^{-2\pi i\langle\xi,a_{j}\rangle}\Bigr)\widehat{\mu}(A^{t}\xi),

where
A^{t}
is the matrix transpose of
A
. Iterating we obtain

(2.1)

\widehat{\mu}(\xi)=\prod_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{m}p_{j}e^{-2\pi i\langle(A^{t})^{n}\xi,a_{j}\rangle}\right)=\prod_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{m}p_{j}e^{-2\pi i\langle\xi,A^{n}a_{j}\rangle}\right),

where the infinite product converges, since
\|A^{n}\|\to 0
exponentially fast.
Let
\mu=\mu(\vartheta^{-1}{\mathcal{O}},{\mathcal{D}},\boldsymbol{p})
.
For
\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, with
\|\xi\|_{\infty}\geq 1
, consider
\eta(\xi)=(A^{t})^{N(\xi)}\xi
, where
N(\xi)\geq 0
is maximal, such that
\|\eta(\xi)\|_{\infty}\geq 1
.
Then
\|\eta(\xi)\|_{\infty}\in[1,\vartheta)
and (
2.1
) implies for
\eta=\eta(\xi,A)
:

(2.2)

\widehat{\mu}(\xi)=\widehat{\mu}(\eta)\cdot\prod_{n=1}^{N(\xi)}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{m}p_{j}e^{-2\pi i\langle\eta,\,A^{-n}a_{j}\rangle}\right)

The following is an elementary inequality; for the proof, see e.g.,
[
39
, Lemma 2.1]
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
{\bf p}=(p_{0},\ldots,p_{m})>0
be a probability vector and
\alpha_{0}=0,\ \alpha_{j}\in{\mathbb{R}}
,
j=1,\ldots,m
. Denote
{\varepsilon}=\min_{j}p_{j}
and write

{\|x\|}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}}}={\rm dist}(x,{\mathbb{Z}})
. Then
for any
k\leq m
,

(2.3)

\left|\sum_{j=0}^{m}p_{j}e^{-2\pi i\alpha_{j}}\right|\leq 1-2\pi{\varepsilon}{\|\alpha_{k}\|}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}}}^{2}.

Thus, using that
|\widehat{\mu}(\eta)|\leq 1
, we obtain

(2.4)

|\widehat{\mu}(\xi)|\leq\prod_{n=1}^{N(\xi)}\left(1-2\pi{\varepsilon}\max_{1\leq j\leq m}\Bigl\|\langle\eta,A^{-n}a_{j}\rangle\Bigr\|_{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}}}^{2}\right)=:\Psi(A,{\mathcal{D}},{\varepsilon},\xi).

We summarize the above discussion in the following

Lemma 2.2

(Reduction)
.

In order to show that
\mu(A,{\mathcal{D}},\boldsymbol{p})\in{\mathscr{D}}_{d}
, with
\boldsymbol{p}>0
, it suffices to verify that there exists
\gamma>0
, such that

\Psi(A,{\mathcal{D}}^{\prime},{\varepsilon},\xi)=O(|\xi|^{-\gamma}),\ \ |\xi|\to\infty,

for any
{\mathcal{D}}^{\prime}\subseteq{\mathcal{D}}
with
0\in{\mathcal{D}}^{\prime}
and
{\varepsilon}=\min_{j}p_{j}
.

3.
Proof of Theorem
1.2

Recall that now
{\mathcal{D}}=\{0,a_{1},\ldots,a_{m}\}
is a spanning set for
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. By Lemma
2.2
, we can assume that
m=d

and obtain from (

):

(3.1)

|\widehat{\mu}(\xi)|\leq=\prod_{n=1}^{N(\xi)}\left(1-2\pi{\varepsilon}\max_{1\leq j\leq d}\Bigl\|\langle a_{j},\vartheta^{n}({\mathcal{O}}^{t})^{-n}\eta\rangle\Bigr\|_{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}}}^{2}\right).

As already mentioned above, the proof uses a variant of the Erdős-Kahane method and has some common features with the proofs in

[
35
,
34
]
and other applications of this technique.

We start by defining
the exceptional set in Theorem
1.2
. For
\delta,\rho>0
and
1<B_{1}<B_{2}
we first define the “bad set” at scale
N
:

(3.2)

E_{N}(\delta,\rho,B_{1},B_{2})=\left\{\vartheta\in[B_{1},B_{2}]:\!\!\!\max_{\eta:\ 1\leq\|\eta\|_{\infty}\leq B_{2}}\frac{1}{N}\Bigl|\Bigl\{n\in[N]:\!\!\max_{1\leq j\leq d}\Bigl\|\langle a_{j},\vartheta^{n}({\mathcal{O}}^{t})^{-n}\eta\rangle\Bigr\|_{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}}}\!\!\!<\rho\Bigr\}\Bigr|>1-\delta\right\}.

The exceptional set is given by

{\mathcal{E}}(\delta,\rho,B_{1},B_{2})=\bigcap_{N_{0}=1}^{\infty}\bigcup_{N=N_{0}}^{\infty}E_{N}(\delta,\rho,B_{1},B_{2}).

Theorem
1.2
is immediate from the next two propositions. We assume that the orthogonal matrix
{\mathcal{O}}
and a spanning digit set

{\mathcal{D}}=\{0,a_{1},\ldots,a_{d}\}
are fixed. All the constants below depend on them.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
1<B_{1}<B_{2}
.
For any
\delta>0
, and
\rho\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
, we have
\mu(\vartheta^{-1}{\mathcal{O}},{\mathcal{D}},\boldsymbol{p})\in{\mathscr{D}}_{d}(\gamma)
for some
\gamma>0
,
whenever
\vartheta\not\in{\mathcal{E}}(\delta,\rho,B_{1},B_{2})
.
The power
\gamma
depends only on
\delta,\rho,B_{2}
, and
{\varepsilon}=\min_{j}p_{j}
.

Proposition 3.2
.

For any
\beta>0
and
1<B_{1}<B_{2}
there
exist
\delta>0
and
\rho\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
such that
\dim_{\rm H}({\mathcal{E}}(\delta,\rho,B_{1},B_{2}))\leq\beta
.

The proof of Proposition
3.2
is based on the following

Proposition 3.3
.

For any
1<B_{1}<B_{2}
there exist positive constants
\rho=\rho(B_{1},B_{2})
,
C_{0}=C_{0}(B_{1},B_{2})
, and
n_{1}=n_{1}(B_{1},B_{2})\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
such that, for any

and
\delta\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
, the

E_{N}(\delta,\rho,B_{1},B_{2})
can be covered
by
\exp\bigl(C_{0}\cdot\delta\log(1/\delta)N\bigr)
disks of diameter
B_{1}^{-N}
.

We first complete the proof of Proposition
3.2
, assuming the last proposition. The symbol
{\mathcal{H}}^{\beta}
denotes the

\beta
-dimensional Hausdorff measure.
By Proposition
3.3
, for
N_{0}\geq n_{1}
,

{\mathcal{H}}^{\beta}\left(\bigcup_{N=N_{0}}^{\infty}E_{N}(\delta,\rho,B_{1},B_{2})\right)\leq\sum_{N=N_{0}}^{\infty}\exp(C_{0}\cdot\delta\log(1/\delta)N)\cdot B_{1}^{-\beta N}\to 0,\ \ N_{0}\to\infty,

. ∎

and

.

.

Suppose that
\vartheta\notin{\mathcal{E}}(\delta,\rho,B_{1},B_{2})

for all

.

and

Lemma 3.4
.

There exists
n_{1}=n_{1}(B_{1},B_{2})\in{\mathbb{N}}

(3.5)

\left|\vartheta-\frac{\|\vec{L}_{n+1}\|}{\|\vec{L}_{n}\|}\right|\leq C_{1}\cdot\vartheta^{-n}\max\{\|\vec{{\varepsilon}}_{n}\|,\|\vec{{\varepsilon}}_{n+1}\|\}\leq C_{1}\cdot B_{1}^{-n}\max\{\|\vec{{\varepsilon}}_{n}\|,\|\vec{{\varepsilon}}_{n+1}\|)\},\ \ n\geq n_{1},

, and

\vartheta-\frac{\|\vec{L}_{n+1}\|}{\|\vec{L}_{n}\|}=\frac{\bigl(\|\vec{L}_{n}\|-\|\vartheta^{n}\eta\|\bigr)\cdot\|\vartheta^{n+1}\eta\|}{\|\vartheta^{n}\eta\|\cdot\|\vec{L}_{n}\|}-\frac{\|\vec{L}_{n+1}\|-\|\vartheta^{n+1}\eta\|}{\|\vec{L}_{n}\|}

3.8

Next we show (

R_{0}>0
, let

V_{R_{0}}=\bigl\{{\mathbf{x}}=(x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{4}:\ \exists\,w_{0}\in{\mathbb{C}},\ |w_{0}|\geq R_{0},\ \exists\,\theta\in{\mathcal{A}}_{\vartheta,b_{1}},\ x_{j}={\rm Re}(\theta^{j}w_{0}),\ 0\leq j\leq 3\bigr\}.

Denote by
{\mathcal{N}}_{\varepsilon}(V)
the open
{\varepsilon}
-neighborhood of
V
in the

\ell^{\infty}
metric.

Lemma 4.6

(variant of
[
35
, Lemma 2.2]
)
.

Given
r=r(\vartheta,b_{1})>0
,
there exist
R_{0}>0
and
C_{1}>0
depending only on
\vartheta
and
b_{1}
, such
that
there exist continuously differentiable functions

F:\,{\mathcal{N}}_{r}(V_{R_{0}})\to\{\theta\in{\mathbb{C}}:\ |\theta|>1,\ {\rm Im}(\theta)>0\}\ \ \mbox{and}\ \ G:\,{\mathcal{N}}_{r}(V_{R_{0}})\to{\mathbb{R}},

with
\theta=F({\mathbf{x}})
and
y_{3}=G({\mathbf{x}})
being the unique solutions to the system of equations

(4.17)

{\rm Re}(\theta^{j-3}(x_{3}+iy_{3}))=x_{j},\ \ \ 0\leq j\leq 2,

given an arbitrary
{\mathbf{x}}=(x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})\in V_{R_{0}}
.
Moreover,

\left|\frac{\partial G}{\partial x_{j}}\right|\leq C_{1},\ \ \ 0\leq j\leq 3,\ \ \ \mbox{on}\ \ {\mathcal{N}}_{r}(V_{R_{0}}).

The statement of
[
35
, Lemma 2.2]
is with
r=1
; however, the same proof works for any
r>0
depending on
\vartheta
and
b_{1}
; we
let

r=(1/2)(1+\vartheta)^{d-2}.

Observe that in (
4.15
) we have
\theta=\theta_{d-1}\in{\mathcal{A}}_{\vartheta,b_{1}}
by the assumption that
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in H
. Furthermore, by (
4.15
),

(4.18)

|w_{0}|\geq 2b_{2}^{d-2}\vartheta^{n}\geq R_{0}\ \ \ \mbox{for}\ n\geq n_{1}=n_{1}(H,\beta),

where we used that
|\tau_{d-1}|=\|\tau\|_{\infty}\geq 1
.
Thus,

{\mathbf{x}}=\left(\widetilde{A}_{n}^{(d-2)},\ldots,\widetilde{A}_{n+3}^{(d-2)}\right)\in V_{R_{0}}\ \ \ \ \mbox{for}\ n\geq n_{1},

in view of (
4.13
) and (
4.18
). Now we can apply Lemma
4.6
with our data from (
4.15
) for
n\geq n_{1}
.
Denote

\widetilde{Y}_{n}:={\rm Im}\left(2\tau_{d-1}\prod_{k=1}^{d-2}(\theta_{d-1}-\theta_{k})\theta_{d-1}^{n}\right),\ \ n\in{\mathbb{N}}.

Applying Lemma
4.6
, we obtain

\widetilde{Y}_{n+3}=G\left(\widetilde{A}_{n}^{(d-2)},\ldots,\widetilde{A}_{n+3}^{(d-2)}\right),\ \ n\geq n_{1},

so that

(4.19)

\widetilde{A}_{n+3}^{(d-2)}+i\widetilde{Y}_{n+3}=2\tau_{d-1}\prod_{k=1}^{d-2}(\theta_{d-1}-\theta_{k})\theta_{d-1}^{n+3},\ \ n\geq n_{1}.

Next we would like to apply the function
G
to
perturbed data. By (
4.14
) we have for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
:

(4.20)

\left|A_{n}^{(d-2)}-\widetilde{A}_{n}^{(d-2)}\right|<(1+\vartheta)^{d-2}\max\{|{\varepsilon}_{n}|,\ldots,|{\varepsilon}_{n+d-2}|\}\leq(1/2)(1+\vartheta)^{d-2}=r,

hence

{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}=\left(A_{n}^{(d-2)},\ldots,A_{n+3}^{(d-2)}\right)
satisfies
\|{\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}\|_{\infty}<r
, and so
{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{N}}_{r}(V_{R_{0}})
for
n\geq n_{1}
.
Thus,

Y_{n+3}:=G\left(A_{n}^{(d-2)},\ldots,A_{n+3}^{(d-2)}\right),\ \ n\geq n_{1},

is well-defined.
Writing
Y_{n+3}-\widetilde{Y}_{n+3}
as a sum of path integrals of the partial derivatives of
G
, we obtain from Lemma
4.6
and (
4.20
) that

(4.21)

|Y_{n+3}-\widetilde{Y}_{n+3}|\leq 4C_{1}(1+\vartheta)^{d-2}\max\{|{\varepsilon}_{n}|,\ldots,|{\varepsilon}_{n+d+1}|\},\ \ n\geq n_{1}

(the factor 4 comes from the estimate
\|{\mathbf{x}}\|_{1}\leq\|{\mathbf{x}}\|_{\infty}
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{4}
).

4.4.
Approximation

By (
4.19
),

(4.22)

\displaystyle\theta_{d-1}=\frac{\widetilde{A}_{n+4}^{(d-2)}+i\widetilde{Y}_{n+4}}{\widetilde{A}_{n+3}^{(d-2)}+i\widetilde{Y}_{n+3}}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\frac{A_{n+4}^{(d-2)}+iY_{n+4}}{A_{n+3}^{(d-2)}+iY_{n+3}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{A_{n+4}^{(d-2)}+iG\bigr(A_{n+1}^{(d-2)},\ldots,A_{n+4}^{(d-2)}\bigr)}{A_{n+3}^{(d-2)}+iG\bigl(A_{n}^{(d-2)},\ldots,A_{n+3}^{(d-2)}\bigr)}

\displaystyle=:

\displaystyle\Phi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}}(K_{n},\ldots,K_{n+d+2}),

for some function
\Phi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}}
of
(d+3)
variables, depending on the parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}
that have been fixed, in view of (
4.11
). Thus, given
K_{n},\ldots,K_{n+d+2}
, we are
able to get a good approximation of
\theta_{d-1}
. More precisely, we have

Lemma 4.7
.

There exist
C_{2}=C_{2}(H)
and
n_{2}=n_{2}(H)\geq n_{1}(H)
, such that

(4.23)

\left|\theta_{d-1}-\Phi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}}(K_{n},\ldots,K_{n+d+2})\right|\leq C_{2}\cdot\vartheta^{-n}\max\{|{\varepsilon}_{n}|,\ldots,|{\varepsilon}_{n+d+2}|\},\ \ \ n\geq n_{2}.

Proof.

Denote

B_{n}:=A_{n}^{(d-2)}+iY_{n}\ \ \ \mbox{and}\ \ \ \widetilde{B}_{n}:=\widetilde{A}_{n}^{(d-2)}+i\widetilde{Y}_{n}.

Then (
4.22
) implies

(4.24)

\bigl|\theta_{d-1}-\Phi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}}(K_{n},\ldots,K_{n+d+2})\bigr|=\left|\theta_{d-1}-\frac{B_{n+4}}{B_{n+3}}\right|=\left|\frac{\widetilde{B}_{n+4}}{\widetilde{B}_{n+3}}-\frac{B_{n+4}}{B_{n+3}}\right|.

Observe that

(4.25)

|B_{n+3}-\widetilde{B}_{n+3}|\leq(1+4C_{1})\cdot(1+\vartheta)^{d-2}\max\{|{\varepsilon}_{n}|,\ldots,|{\varepsilon}_{n+d+1}|\},\ \ n\geq n_{1},

by (
4.20
) and (
4.21
), whereas

(4.26)

2b_{2}^{d-2}\vartheta^{n+3}\leq|\widetilde{B}_{n+3}|=\bigl|\widetilde{A}_{n+3}^{(d-2)}+i\widetilde{Y}_{n+3}\bigr|\leq 2\cdot(2\vartheta)^{d-1}\vartheta^{n+3},\ \ n\geq n_{1},

in view of (
4.19
). Now the desired estimate follows easily.
∎

Analogously, given
K_{n},\ldots,K_{n+d+2}
, we can make a good “prediction” of
K_{n+d+3}
. Indeed,

\theta_{d-1}=\frac{\widetilde{A}_{n+5}^{(d-2)}+i\widetilde{Y}_{n+4}}{\widetilde{A}_{n+4}^{(d-2)}+i\widetilde{Y}_{n+4}}=\frac{\widetilde{A}_{n+4}^{(d-2)}+i\widetilde{Y}_{n+4}}{\widetilde{A}_{n+3}^{(d-2)}+i\widetilde{Y}_{n+3}}

yields

\displaystyle\widetilde{A}_{n+5}^{(d-2)}={\rm Re}\left[\frac{(\widetilde{A}_{n+4}^{(d-2)}+i\widetilde{Y}_{n+4})^{2}}{\widetilde{A}_{n+3}^{(d-2)}+i\widetilde{Y}_{n+3}}\right],

hence

(4.27)

\displaystyle A_{n+5}^{(d-2)}\approx{\rm Re}\left[\frac{(A_{n+4}^{(d-2)}+iY_{n+4})^{2}}{A_{n+3}^{(d-2)}+iY_{n+3}}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\rm Re}\left[\frac{\Bigl(A_{n+4}^{(d-2)}+iG\bigl(A_{n+1}^{(d-2)},\ldots,A_{n+4}^{(d-2)}\bigr)\Bigr)^{2}}{A_{n+3}^{(d-2)}+iG\bigl(A_{n}^{(d-2)},\ldots,A_{n+3}^{(d-2)}\bigr)}\right],

\displaystyle=:

\displaystyle\Psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}}(K_{n},\ldots,K_{n+d+2}),

for another function,
\Psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}}
, of
(d+3)
variables, depending on the parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}
. In view of (
4.12
), this yields

(4.28)

\displaystyle K_{n+d+3}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle A_{n+5}^{(d-2)}-\sum_{k=1}^{d-2}(-1)^{k}\sigma_{k}(\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{j})K_{n+(d+3)-k}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\Psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}}(K_{n},\ldots,K_{n+d+2})-\sum_{k=1}^{d-2}(-1)^{k}\sigma_{k}(\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{j})K_{n+(d+3)-k}

\displaystyle=:

\displaystyle\Xi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}}(K_{n},\ldots,K_{n+d+2}),

for yet another function,
\Xi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}}
, of
(d+3)
variables, depending on the parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}
.
The next lemma makes this approximation precise.

Lemma 4.8
.

There exist
C_{3}=C_{3}(H)
and
n_{3}=n_{3}(H)\geq n_{2}(H)
, such that

|K_{n+d+3}-\Xi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}}(K_{n},\ldots,K_{n+d+2})|\leq C_{3}\cdot\max\{|{\varepsilon}_{n}|,\ldots,|{\varepsilon}_{n+d+3}|\},\ \ \ n\geq n_{3}.

Proof.

We have by (
4.27
):

\displaystyle|K_{n+d+3}-\Xi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}}(K_{n},\ldots,K_{n+d+2})|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|A_{n+5}^{(d-2)}-{\rm Re}\bigl(B_{n+4}^{2}/B_{n+3}\bigr)\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left|B_{n+5}-\frac{B_{n+4}^{2}}{B_{n+3}}\right|=|B_{n+4}|\cdot\left|\frac{B_{n+5}}{B_{n+4}}-\frac{B_{n+4}}{B_{n+3}}\right|,

and the desired estimate follows from (
4.24
), (
4.23
), and (
4.26
).
∎

4.5.
Conclusion of the proof

Let

(4.29)

\rho:=(2C_{3})^{-1}\ \ \ \mbox{and}\ \ \ M:=2C_{3}+1.

Lemma
4.8
immediately implies the following

Lemma 4.9
.

Consider an arbitrary
\theta_{d-1}\in{\mathbb{C}}
, such that
(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime},\theta_{d-1},\overline{\theta}_{d-1})\in H
, and
\tau=(\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{d})\in{\mathbb{C}}^{2s}
,
such that
\tau_{2j}=\overline{\tau}_{2j-1}
for

j\in[s]
, with
1\leq|\tau_{d-1}|=\|\tau\|_{\infty}\leq\vartheta
. Define the corresponding sequences
K_{n},{\varepsilon}_{n}
by (
4.9
).
Then the following hold, independent of
\theta_{d-1}
:

(i)

for any
n\geq n_{3}(H)
, such that
\max\{|{\varepsilon}_{n}|,\ldots,|{\varepsilon}_{n+d+3}|\}<\rho
,
the number
K_{n+d+3}
is uniquely determined by
(K_{j})_{j=n}^{n+d+2}
.

(ii)

for any
n\geq n_{3}(H)
there are at most
M
choices of
K_{n+d+3}
given
(K_{j})_{j=n}^{n+d+2}
.

Now we can finish the proof of Proposition
4.5
. Suppose that
N\geq n_{3}(H)
.
Further, fix
\theta_{d-1}\in E_{H,N}(\delta,\rho)
, with
\rho
from (
4.29
) and
\delta>0
arbitrary. By definition, there exists

\tau=(\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{d})\in{\mathbb{C}}^{d}
, such that
\tau_{2j}=\overline{\tau}_{2j-1}\in{\mathbb{C}}
for
j\in[s]
, with
1\leq|\tau_{d-1}|=\|\tau\|_{\infty}\leq\vartheta
, satisfying

\Bigl|\Bigl\{n\in[N]:\ \Big\|\sum_{j=1}^{d}\tau_{j}\theta_{j}^{n}\Bigr\|<\rho\Bigr\}\Bigr|\geq(1-\delta)N.

Since
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in H
, there are
O_{H}(1)
choices for the initial part of the sequence
K_{1},\ldots,K_{n_{3}}
. The set
J:=\{n\in[N]:\ |{\varepsilon}_{n}|\geq\rho\}
has cardinality at most

\lfloor\delta N\rfloor
, by construction. In view of Lemma
4.9
, given
J
, there are at most
O_{H}(M^{\delta(d+4)N})
choices for the sequence
K_{1},\ldots,K_{N+d+2}
. By (
4.23
), the minimal number of disks of radius
O_{H}(\vartheta^{-N})
needed to cover
E_{H,N}(\delta,\rho)
, is at most

O_{H}(M^{\delta(d+4)N})\cdot{N\choose\lfloor\delta N\rfloor}=\exp(O_{H}(\delta\log(1/\delta)N)),

as desired. This completes the proof of Proposition
4.5
, and hence also of Proposition
4.4
, Theorem
4.2
, and
Theorem
1.3
.
∎
