Unveiling a putative class 3 L-asparaginase in Glutamicibacter sp.: from soil isolation to in silico characterization
Authors: Mahdis Mofidi, Gholamhossein Ebrahimipour, Maryam Azimzadeh Irani, Ali Etemadi, Mohammad Yaghoubi-Avini
Published: 2026

Background

L-asparaginase (EC 3.5.1.1) is a crucial therapeutic enzyme primarily used as a chemotherapeutic agent for patients with acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) [1]. Many leukemic cells are unable to synthesize L-asparagine due to a defect in the gene encoding L-asparagine synthetase, making them dependent on plasma L-asparagine. L-asparaginase hydrolyzes asparagine to aspartic acid and ammonia, reducing plasma asparagine levels and prolonging asparagine deprivation in cancer cells. It alters signaling pathways, decreases protein synthesis, and initiates apoptosis [2, 3].

Based on sequence homology, biochemical properties, and structural studies, L-asparaginases can be classified into three distinct types: bacterial type (class 1), plant type (class 2), and class 3, which are similar to the Rhizobium etli enzyme [4]. Each class shows distinct architectures. For example, class 1 asparaginases are usually homotetramers (dimers of two intimate dimers). In contrast, recently published structures of the ReAV and ReAIV enzymes of class 3 are homodimers composed of two subunits, each with an extended active site consisting of two tandem Ser-Lys residues, all of which are connected to a zinc center through a network of H-bonds [4, 5].

Owing to their high affinity for L-asparagine and broad substrate specificity, type II of class 1 asparaginases derived from Escherichia coli (EcAII) and Erwinia chrysanthemi (ErAII) are potent antitumor agents that are used to treat childhood leukemia [6]. Although these enzymes have been successfully used in treatment, they are associated with many side effects [7]. These effects may involve immunologic responses such as hypersensitivity, pancreatitis, hepatitis, or even systemic effects such as coagulation disorders and impairment of brain function in the form of neurotoxicity [8]. Additionally, prolonged use may trigger the production of neutralizing antibodies, reducing their efficacy [9]. Consequently, further research is essential to identify alternative microbial enzymes with greater therapeutic potential and fewer adverse effects.

Class 3 asparaginases have garnered significant interest as potential targets for new drug development, owing to their promising therapeutic potential and lower immunogenicity [10]. One approach to achieve this goal is to use enzyme engineering to convert alternative L-asparaginase types into high-affinity enzymes [11]. This approach needs detailed structural studies of proteins to elucidate their molecular structures, which can be used to improve their medicinal properties [12]. Another approach is to explore habitats such as the deep sea, extreme niches, and soil, which contain large populations of novel species with unique enzymes that may exhibit superior characteristics compared with well-known therapeutic L-asparaginases [13].

In this study, we identified an asparaginase enzyme (GlAVI) from a soil bacterium, and phylogenetic analysis indicated that it is most likely a homolog of Class 3 asparaginases. We also reported the three‑dimensional structural modeling, substrate interaction analysis, immunogenicity assessment, and molecular dynamics simulations. Systematic investigations of asparaginases are of pharmaceutical importance because enzymes with lower immunogenicity can serve as potential drug candidates for the treatment of certain types of leukemia, especially acute lymphoblastic leukemia in children.

Materials and methods

Sample collection, bacterial isolation, and qualitative screening for L-asparaginase production

Soil samples were collected from a depth of 4 cm in agricultural areas around Tehran, Iran. One gram of each sample was transferred to 50 mL of modified M9 medium [14] with 1% (w/v) asparagine and incubated at 25 °C at 100 rpm. After 24 h, 0.5 ml of the culture medium was transferred to a new sterile culture medium of the same composition, and this process was repeated three times. Then, 100 µl was spread onto modified M9 agar using the spread plate technique. Finally, morphologically distinct colonies were selected for further analysis [15].

The strains producing L-asparaginase were screened based on their ability to form a pink zone around colonies on modified M9 agar containing 0.009% phenol red as a pH indicator. The diameter of the pink zone is related to the amount of enzymes. L-glutaminase activity was assessed by replacing asparagine with glutamine as the nitrogen source [16].

L-asparaginase activity assay

L-asparaginase activity was determined using direct ammonia derivatization by Nessler’s reagent. For this purpose, 500 µL of a 10 mM L-asparagine solution in 50 mM Tris buffer at pH 7 and 450 µL of the same buffer were added to 50 µL of culture supernatant, and the mixture was incubated at 37 °C. The enzymatic reaction was halted by adding 500 µL of 5% (w/v) trichloroacetic acid, followed by centrifugation at 11,500 rpm for 10 min to remove precipitated proteins. One mL of distilled water and 200 µL of the derivatization reagent were added to 1 mL of the supernatant. After 10 min of incubation at room temperature, the absorbance was measured at 490 nm. All measurements were made in triplicate for the enzyme assays and the calibration curve.

Identification of bacteria via 16S rRNA gene, morphological characteristics, and biochemical methods

The best-producing strain was identified by 16S rDNA sequencing with the universal primers 28 F and 1492R. The 16S rDNA sequence was blasted against the type strain of other bacteria using the EzBiocloud server [17]. The phylogenetic relationship with other species was inferred by the Maximum-likelihood method and 500 bootstrap iterations with the help of the MEGA 11 software package [18]. For more accurate classification, biochemical tests were conducted in accordance with Bergey’s Manual of Systematic Bacteriology [19]. The sequence was deposited in GenBank under accession number PP033237.

Asparaginase gene sequencing

To determine the sequence of the asparaginase gene, asparaginase sequences from the nearest Glutamicibacter species, along with 300 nt upstream and 200 nt downstream, were retrieved from Genbank and aligned by MEGA 11. Specific primers were designed using Oligo7 software for the conserved regions flanking the asparaginase gene. PCR was conducted to amplify the enzyme-encoding gene using the forward primer 5’-CCGCCAGTCCGAAACCGTGAA-3’ and the reverse primer 5’-CGCTCGCAGGCTTGGTCGATG-3’. After sequencing with the same primers, the complete asparaginase gene was translated into an amino acid sequence via the open reading frame (ORF) Finder online server [20]. It can be retrieved from GenBank under accession number PP035160.

Phylogenetic tree construction and evaluation of identity and similarity percentages

Twenty-eight protein sequences from class 1, class 3, and the isolated strain L-asparaginase were used to construct a phylogenetic tree. The MEGA 11 software package was used to perform sequence alignment via the MUSCLE algorithm. A phylogenetic tree was inferred using maximum likelihood with the Le Gascuel model and 1000 bootstrap replicates. The identity and similarity percentages were subsequently calculated by the SIAS server [21].

Enzyme 3D structure prediction

Because the enzyme is known to require a divalent cation for structural integrity and catalysis, a zinc ion (Zn²⁺) was specified as a bound ligand. Zinc was defined using the standard residue name “ZN” with the default bonded parameters provided by AlphaFold 3’s small molecule database. The server was run in full-structure prediction mode without template forcing, allowing simultaneous folding of the protein and placement of the zinc ion. Five models were generated by AlphaFold 3, and the top-ranked model (by predicted template modeling score, pTM) was selected. Model quality was assessed using the predicted local distance difference test (pLDDT) — where values > 90 indicate high confidence — and the predicted alignment error (PAE) matrix [22].

Structural comparison and RMSD analysis

The crystal structures of the R. etli class 3 asparaginases (PDB IDs: 7OS5 and 8OSW) were obtained from the protein database. The monomeric form of these structures, along with the enzyme used in this study, was utilized to facilitate further investigations. PyMOL software aligns these structures and subsequently calculates the root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) to assess the structural differences between the monomeric forms of the enzyme [23].

Molecular docking studies

The RCSB Protein Data Bank provided structures of L-asparagine and L-glutamine, and Open Babel software added hydrogen atoms [24]. Ligand conformers were generated using the Frog2 web tool [25]. Rosetta macromolecular modeling software [26]was used to perform molecular docking of L-asparagine and L-glutamine with class 3 asparaginases (this study, types IV and V). It generated 10,000 output structures in which the zinc ion was fixed, and the ligand was treated as flexible. The docked structures with the highest binding energies were selected and analyzed in PyMOL to visualize interactions between the ligands and proteins [23].

Electrostatic potential surface

The PDB2PQR server [27] was utilized to prepare biomolecular structures for electrostatic calculations. This server automatically performs several common tasks, including adding a limited number of missing heavy atoms to structures, estimating titration states, and protonating biomolecules in a manner consistent with optimal hydrogen bonding. Additionally, the server assigns charge and radius parameters from a set of force fields. The surface charge of the selected enzyme was subsequently determined using the APBS plugin of PyMOL software [28].

Molecular dynamics simulation

GROMACS version 2020 was used to conduct molecular dynamics simulations [29]. The protein structure was obtained from a PDB file and prepared for simulation using the AMBER99SB-ILDN force field [30]. Ligand parameters were generated via ACPYPE [31], with the ligand geometry optimized and hydrogen atoms added via Open Babel [24].

The system was solvated in a cubic box (1.5 nm buffer) filled with TIP3P water molecules, and ions were added to neutralize the system via the Genion tool [29].

Energy minimization was performed using the steepest-descent algorithm with a maximum force tolerance of 1000 kJ·mol⁻¹·nm⁻¹. The system was then equilibrated in two phases. First, an NVT (constant number, volume, temperature) equilibration of 100 ps was performed using the Berendsen thermostat at 310 K (time constant 0.1 ps). Convergence was confirmed by monitoring temperature drift: the average temperature over the final 20 ps was 309.8 ± 0.3 K. Second, an NPT (constant number, pressure, temperature) equilibration of another 100 ps was run at 1 bar using the Parrinello–Rahman barostat (time constant 2.0 ps, compressibility 4.5 × 10⁻⁵ bar⁻¹). Production molecular dynamics simulations were performed for 200 ns with a 2 fs time step. Periodic boundary conditions were applied, and the Verlet cutoff scheme was used for nonbonded interactions. The Particle Mesh Ewald (PME) method was employed to handle long-range electrostatics, with a grid spacing of 0.16 nm.

GPU acceleration was enabled, and PME calculations were performed on the GPU using GROMACS mdrun options. The simulations were performed on 20 cores using the -nb and -pme GPU flags to ensure efficient parallelization. The final trajectories and energy data, including the RMSD, RMSF, and GROMACS analysis tools, were extracted for further analysis.

Evaluation of antigenic potential, toxicity, and allergenicity

The drug should be non-allergenic and nontoxic to prevent adverse reactions upon administration. Allergenicity was assessed using the AlgPred 2 server [32], which employs a comprehensive approach (SVMc + IgE epitope + ARPs BLAST + MAST) to identify allergenic regions. Another critical parameter, toxicity, was evaluated using the ToxinPred2 server [33]. The antigenicity of these proteins was predicted using the VaxiJen server [34], with a threshold set to the default value of 0.4. Tumors were selected as the target organisms for analysis.

B-cell epitope mapping

The linear B-cell epitopes of the GlAVI protein were predicted by the ABCPred server [35] using a threshold of 0.8 and a 16-mer sequence length. The AlgPred2 [32] and VaxiJen [34] servers were used to analyze the allergenicity and antigenicity of each epitope, respectively. PyMOL software was used to visualize the epitope maps [23].

T-cell epitope prediction and epitope density analysis

The MHC-II Binding Predictions server on the Immune Epitope Database (IEDB) platform was utilized to predict T-cell epitopes [36] using the server-provided consensus method for enhanced accuracy. The analysis was performed on eight reference alleles with high global frequencies: HLA-DRB101:01, HLA-DRB103:01, HLA-DRB104:01, HLA-DRB107:01, HLA-DRB108:01, HLA-DRB111:01, HLA-DRB113:01, and HLA-DRB115:01 [37]. Epitope prediction was performed using a percentile rank threshold of ≤ 10. The epitope density (fi) was calculated using the relative frequency fi = ni/N, where ni represents the number of predicted epitopes within the defined threshold range, and N is the total number of predicted epitopes (including those within and beyond the threshold) [38].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using Python with the pandas [39], scipy [40], seaborn [41], and matplotlib [42]. Nonparametric tests were applied due to potential nonnormality in the data. The Mann‒Whitney U test was used for pairwise comparisons, and the Kruskal‒Wallis test was used for multiple group comparisons. The results, including test statistics and p-values, were considered significant at p < 0.05. Data were summarized using medians and interquartile ranges and visualized with boxplots and strip plots to display epitope density distributions across groups.

Results

Isolation and screening of L-asparaginase-producing bacteria

Six distinct strains with distinct morphological characteristics were isolated from agricultural soil on M9 medium supplemented with asparagine. After inoculation on the phenol red-containing medium, three isolates with the largest diameter of the pink area were indicated for the asparaginase activity evaluation, and strain SBU-MF4, which presented the lowest glutaminase activity and the highest L-asparaginase production, was selected.

Characterization and identification of strain SBU-MF4

16S rDNA sequencing, followed by biochemical tests, was subsequently performed to identify the strain (Table S1). The sequence showed the highest homology, 99.13%, to Glutamicibacter uratoxydans, as analyzed using the EzBiocloud server.

Based on its physiological characteristics and 16S rDNA sequence, this bacterium belongs to the genus Glutamicibacter and is designated as Glutamicibacter sp. strain SBU-MF4. The phylogenetic tree of this strain with its nearest type strains is shown in Supplementary Fig. 1.

Enzyme activity

Enzyme activity was assessed via the Nesslerization method. The asparaginase and glutaminase activities were measured at 15 U/ml and 1.5 U/ml, respectively. At 6-hour intervals, the peak enzyme production coincided with the logarithmic phase of bacterial growth, occurring at 36 h post-inoculation. Additionally, enzyme activity declined sharply at the onset of the stationary phase (Fig. 1).

Phylogenetic tree of different classes of L-asparaginase enzymes

A phylogenetic tree was constructed by aligning the L-asparaginase gene sequence obtained in this study with 27 L-asparaginase sequences from classes 1 and 3, particularly L-asparaginase types I, II, ReAIV, and ReAV.

The phylogenetic tree (Fig. 2) revealed three clusters: class 1 type I L-asparaginases, class 1 type II, and class 3, as mediated by strong bootstrap values. Type I asparaginases are grouped into closely related branches, which are distinct from the central cluster of type II enzymes. The type II enzymes were divided into two clusters: one consisting of type II L-asparaginases from gram-positive bacteria and the other from gram-negative bacteria. The therapeutic asparaginases from E. coli and E. chrysanthemi are in the Type II cluster. The distance between these enzymes suggests the possibility of differing immunogenicity. Interestingly, Bacillus subtilis L-asparaginase was closely related to the therapeutic E. chrysanthemi enzyme, suggesting a horizontal gene transfer event.

The cluster corresponding to class 3 asparaginases was separated from the cluster corresponding to class 1. It is further divided into two clusters, with a bootstrap value of 100: the L-asparaginases from Glutamicibacter and R. etli. This distinction indicates that the Glutamicibacter enzymes represent a new type of L-asparaginase, designated as GlAVI. This enzyme shares only 18.18% sequence identity with the asparaginase of E. coli and 16.91% identity with E. chrysanthemi. It shares 39.39% and 31.06% identity with ReAIV and ReAV, respectively.

Comparison of the secondary structures of GlAVI and R. etli enzymes

GlAVI has 315 residues, 20 residues, and 52 residues shorter than ReAIV and ReAV. The structure of GlAVI consists of 10 beta sheets and 15 alpha helices, whereas the structures of ReAV and ReAIV include 11 beta sheets and 16 alpha helices. The alignment revealed that the residues forming the two catalytic Ser-Lys tandems [43] in ReAV and ReAIV are also preserved in the GlAVI sequence. The residues that form the metal coordination site are also conserved in the GlAVI sequence (Figs. 3 and 4). The protein sequence similarity between GlAVI and ReAIV is 32.07%, whereas the similarity with ReAV is 31.31%.

Structural prediction and structural comparison

AlphaFold 3 predicted the three-dimensional structure of GlAVI, yielding five protein models. The generated graph and indicators demonstrated that the predicted structures are highly reliable.

The predicted structure of GlAVI comprises 16 alpha helices and 10 beta sheets (Supplementary Fig. 2). This structure was compared with the structures of ReAV (7OS5) and ReAIV (8OSW). The root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) between GlAVI and ReAV is 0.991 Å, whereas the RMSD with ReAIV is 1.08 Å (Fig. 5).

Protein‒ligand docking and active site investigations

Molecular docking of asparagine and glutamine in the predicted GlAVI structure and in the structures of other class 3 asparaginases (ReAIV and ReAV) was performed using Rosetta software. The binding affinities of both ligands and their optimal binding modes were evaluated for all three structures and are reported in Supplementary Table 2. The results showed that GlAVI binds almost equally to asparagine and glutamine, while ReAIV and ReAV bind more favorably to glutamine (Table S2). All the generated docking poses for GlAVI, ReAIV, and ReAV were analyzed via PyMOL.

The prediction of GlAVI’s active site, which involves asparagine and zinc (Fig. 6), includes four amino acid residues: Ser54, Lys57, Ser82, and Lys250. All four residues are also highly conserved between ReAIV and ReAV (Table S3).

The protein surface within the active-site pocket exhibits regions of strong positive charge. This region’s surrounding arginine and lysine residues are well-suited for attracting negatively charged moieties, such as the substrate’s carboxylate group, into the active site, which is consistent with other reported L-asparaginases (Supplementary Fig. 3).

The global dynamics of GlAVI-Asn and GlAVI-Gln

Molecular dynamics simulations were performed for the GlAVI-Asn and GlAVI-Gln complexes. The system’s stability was evaluated by calculating the RMSD. The RMSD plot indicated that GlAVI-Asn was more stable than GlAVI-Gln, as reflected by their RMSD ranges and fluctuations. The average RMSD values for GlAVI-Asn and GlAVI-Gln were 0.33 and 0.44 nm, respectively. In the case of GlAVI-Asn, a relatively stable plateau was reached after 0.05 ns, suggesting that GlAVI-Asn achieved a stable conformation with minimal structural deviations during the simulation. In contrast, the RMSD plots for GlAVI-Gln showed more fluctuations, with a noticeable sudden jump after 0.75 ns, indicating the separation of glutamine from GlAVI (Fig. 7A).

To investigate the flexibility and local fluctuations of GlAVI-Asn and GlAVI-Gln, Root Mean Square Fluctuation (RMSF) analysis was performed. Most residues in GlAVI-Asn and GlAVI-Gln displayed relatively low fluctuations in their RMSF profiles. The average RMSF values for GlAVI-Asn and GlAVI-Gln were 0.12 and 0.14 nm, respectively (Fig. 7B).

Prediction of immunogenicity, allergenicity, and toxicity

The immunogenicity of asparaginase was evaluated through computational analysis of the enzymes EcAII, ErAII, ReAIV, ReAV, and GlAVI. The results indicated that all the enzymes were nontoxic. However, only EcAII and ErAII were predicted to be allergenic, whereas all enzymes were identified as antigens, except for ReAIV (Supplementary Table 4).

For further evaluation, we employed the relative frequency measure, as it has been previously used to determine the density of immunogenic epitopes in various proteins and has been correlated with their immunogenicity levels [44, 45].

In assessing the immunogenicity of asparaginases, we found that ErAII exhibited significantly higher predicted immunogenicity than the other enzymes evaluated. In contrast, no significant differences were observed among the remaining enzymes. Interestingly, when the relative frequencies of these asparaginases were compared, ErAII showed the highest predicted immunogenicity, whereas GlAVI showed the lowest (Fig. 8).

Discussion

In the present study, soil-derived bacteria were screened for L-asparaginase production, leading to the identification of Glutamicibacter sp. SBU-MF4 as the most promising isolate, based on its high L-asparaginase activity and comparatively low glutaminase activity. The selection of isolates with reduced glutaminase activity is particularly relevant for therapeutic applications, as excessive glutaminase activity has been associated with increased toxicity and adverse effects in clinical use. However, the contribution of glutaminase activity to antileukemic efficacy remains controversial. While some studies suggest that glutaminase activity may enhance cytotoxicity against leukemic cells, others report that L-asparaginases with minimal glutaminase activity can retain therapeutic effectiveness [46]. Therefore, the low glutaminase activity observed in SBU-MF4 may represent a potential advantage, although functional validation in leukemic cell models is required. On the other hand, in contrast to a previous opinion, a recent study on the therapeutic effects of L-asparaginases demonstrates that all enzymes from various classes exhibit dose- and time-dependent antileukemic activity via antiproliferative or antiapoptotic mechanisms, regardless of their affinity for the substrate, indicating ReAIV as a promising alternative to EcAII [47].

From a phylogenetic perspective, Glutamicibacter belongs to the family Micrococcaceae within the order Actinomycetales. Bacteria of this genus are found in soil, water, and air and have been isolated from various habitats, including different types of soil and cheese. The Glutamicibacter group of bacteria has significant applications in the production of antibiotics and enzymes, and they are widely recognized for their biological importance in the control, protection, and treatment of chronic human diseases through the production of bioactive compounds that exhibit antitumor, antituberculosis [48], antihelminth, antidiabetic [49], and antifungal [50] properties. For example, the intracellular extract of G. mysorens has demonstrated significant anticancer activity in prostate cancer cell lines [51]. However, this is the first report on L-asparaginase production and its structural investigations in Glutamicibacter species.

Phylogenetic and molecular analyses identified the enzyme, designated GlAVI, as a member of class 3 L-asparaginases. The phylogenetic tree revealed that GlAVI forms a distinct branch within the class 3 cluster. Moreover, GlAVI showed relatively low sequence identity compared to other class 3 asparaginases, at a level comparable to the average identities of ReAIV and ReAV with other L-asparaginases [43]. Also, structural alignment of the predicted three-dimensional structure of GlAVI with known class 3 enzymes, including ReAIV and ReAV, revealed low RMSD values, indicating a high degree of structural similarity despite modest sequence identity. Consistent with previously characterized class 3 asparaginases, GlAVI displayed a strong positive charge at the active site and conserved catalytic motifs, including the characteristic S–K dyad, suggesting preservation of the catalytic mechanism [4, 43]. These findings support the classification of GlAVI within the class 3 structural framework. However, no genes like GlAVI were found in the genomes of E. coli, R. etli, and other lineages outside Micrococcacea, indicating the absence of recent horizontal gene transfer from these sources [4, 52]. Therefore, GlAVI represents a putative novel type within class 3 L-asparaginases. However, further comparative genomic and biochemical analyses would be necessary to confirm its taxonomic and functional distinctness.

Docking analyses indicated that, despite overall similarity to ReAIV and ReAV, the GlAVI enzyme exhibits a stronger binding affinity for its substrate than either of them. Although GlAVI exhibits the same binding affinity toward L-asparagine and L-glutamine, molecular dynamics simulations further showed that the GlAVI–asparagine complex remained structurally stable, whereas the GlAVI–glutamine complex displayed higher fluctuations and partial substrate dissociation [53]. These computational observations are consistent with the experimental enzymatic data, which demonstrated substantially lower glutaminase activity than asparaginase activity. Nevertheless, it is important to note that docking scores and simulation stability do not directly equate to catalytic efficiency.

In addition to structural and functional analyses, immunoinformatic predictions suggest that GlAVI, along with R. etli asparaginases, has a lower density of T-cell epitopes than the currently used therapeutic enzymes EcAII and ErAII [54]. Furthermore, allergenicity prediction tools indicated no significant allergenic potential. Although EcAII and ErAII are highly effective in ALL treatment, their efficacy is limited by allergic reactions to L-asparaginase and adverse effects occurring in 30 to 70% of patients [46, 55]. In particular, ErAII, an alternative for patients who showed hypersensitivity to EcAII treatment, exhibited greater immunogenicity [44]. Therefore, reduced predicted immunogenicity may be a desirable characteristic of GlAVI, as hypersensitivity reactions are a major limitation of clinically used L-asparaginases. However, these findings are based solely on in silico prediction models and should be interpreted cautiously. Experimental validation through immunological assays and in vivo studies will be necessary to confirm these properties.

Conclusion

This study identified and characterized GlAVI, an L-asparaginase from Glutamicibacter sp. SBU-MF4, using a combination of experimental screening and computational analyses. The results indicate that GlAVI belongs to class 3 L-asparaginases and exhibits favorable predicted structural features, along with low glutaminase activity, a characteristic that may be advantageous for therapeutic applications. Phylogenetic and structural analyses further suggest that this enzyme represents a potentially divergent member of class 3 L-asparaginases found in Glutamicibacter species. Although these findings highlight the potential of GlAVI as a candidate enzyme, its therapeutic relevance remains to be confirmed. Further biochemical characterization, antileukemic activity assays, and in vivo studies will be necessary to determine whether GlAVI could serve as a viable alternative to currently used L-asparaginase preparations. In addition, our results emphasize the value of exploring Actinomycetota as an alternative microbial source for discovering novel L-asparaginases beyond the traditionally used Gram-negative bacteria.
