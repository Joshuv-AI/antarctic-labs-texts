Creating an Empirical Dermatology Dataset Through Crowdsourcing With Web Search Advertisements
Authors: Abbi Ward, Jimmy Li, Julie Wang, Sriram Lakshminarasimhan, Ashley Carrick, Bilson Campana, Jay Hartford, Pradeep K. Sreenivasaiah, Tiya Tiyasirisokchai, Sunny Virmani, Renee Wong, Yossi Matias, Greg S. Corrado, Dale R. Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
This survey study assesses the utility of crowdsourcing via web search advertisements to create a representative dermatology image dataset.

Introduction

Health datasets1,2,3 intended for use in research and medical education are traditionally obtained through prospective clinical studies, or retrospectively from health care facility electronic health record systems. However, a patient’s health care journey begins much before their first encounter with the health care system,4 and datasets derived from clinical sources are unlikely to capture these early disease presentation stages. Datasets of clinical origin also often reflect underlying health care access disparities across race and ethnicity,5 geography,6 socioeconomic status,7 and other dimensions.8

Dermatology is an area with evident need for representative images for research, artificial intelligence (AI) tool development, and medical education. Access to dermatologists9 is lacking in many community, rural, and global settings, and primary care physicians manage 70% of skin disease encounters.10 Dermatology lends itself well to creating a crowdsourced dataset where recruitment is directly centered on the individual experiencing a health condition, rather than on the subset of individuals presenting to the health system. Although self-taken dermatology images are of lower quality than clinical images, smartphone cameras allow patients to take multiple images and are useful in capturing early-stage or intermittent conditions.11

Most existing dermatology datasets are focused on malignant skin neoplasms,8,12 do not include information on race and ethnicity,13 and underrepresent darker skin tones.5,8 Dataset availability drives AI tool development,13 and biases in the underlying data can lead to fairness or equity gaps in the AI tools.14 There have been efforts to remedy the lack of representative dermatology datasets for both clinical users14,15 and everyday internet users,16 yet major unmet needs remain. Few dermatology datasets reflect the breadth and diversity of disease including common, short-duration, infectious, allergic, or inflammatory conditions. Dataset creation remains resource intensive and out of the reach of most researchers and educators.

Web symptom search is a key component of the modern health care journey, with 80% of US internet users17 or 55.8% of US adults18 reporting using the internet to look up health or medical information. Internet search could be an entry point to reach individuals early in their information- and care-seeking journey. Web search, social media, and other online advertising methods have been used for clinical study recruitment.19,20,21 Researchers have used search history to study infectious disease spread,22,23 detect foodborne illness hubs,24 forecast suicide rates,25 and predict malignant neoplasm onset.26,27 Search advertisements (ads) have a broad reach yet can be selectively displayed to individuals seeking specific health information.

In this work, we used search ads to create a dermatology image dataset—the Skin Condition Image Network (SCIN) dataset—composed of consented voluntary contributions from internet users. After deidentification, labeling, and curation, we will release SCIN as a resource for health education, research, and tool development.

The study aimed to address the following questions: (1) Is crowdsourcing with search ads effective? (2) How well does the crowdsourced dataset represent the population? (3) How do datasets generated with this method differ from those derived from clinical sources? (4) To what extent are self-taken images interpretable by dermatologists?

Methods

This survey study was approved by the Advarra institutional review board. The SCIN dataset is an open access collection of self-taken dermatology images contributed by individuals in the US aged 18 or older who went through an online informed consent process.

Search Ads

Starting with a list of 100 skin, hair, and nail conditions (eTable 1 in Supplement 1), we used the Google Search Ads Keyword Planner tool28 to generate and select a list of 3000 skin-related search keywords. The keyword list included common keywords like rash pictures or hives on skin, which were relevant to more than 1 dermatological condition, as well as more condition-specific terms like mole with irregular border or plaque psoriasis on feet. From March to November 2023, we then displayed text ads inviting contributions to the SCIN open access dataset to adults in the US searching for these keywords on a mobile device (to facilitate photo taking). There was no demographic or additional geographic selection or exclusion. Ads had less than 10% impression share for each keyword, meaning that less than 10% of individuals who searched for a keyword would see a SCIN ad.

SCIN Web Application

Individuals who clicked on the ad reached the landing page of a web application (eFigure 1 in Supplement 1) explaining the crowdsourcing initiative, the motivation for the open access dataset, and eligibility. Participation was limited to individuals older than 18 years in the US, and no compensation was provided to participants. Those who clicked “get started” went through the online informed consent process and contributed 1 to 3 images of their skin condition. The application requested 1 close-up photograph, 1 photograph from an angle, and 1 photograph showing the entire affected body part. Images could be uploaded from an existing photo library or taken live. Contributors were given the option to self-report their demographic information (ie, age, sex, gender, and race and ethnicity), and provide information related to their skin condition, including self-reported Fitzpatrick Skin Type (sFST) based on tanning and burning propensity (eTable 2 and eFigure 1 in Supplement 1).

Contributed Data Processing

After upload, a third-party team of trained personnel deidentified each image by redacting any areas that could identify individuals, such as facial areas, tattoos, or landmarks in the image. Deidentified images went through a further quality check. We then filtered the dataset by deleting the following categories: (1) spam or images not showing a skin condition (manual), (2) duplicates (algorithmic, using computed image features), (3) image safety for images that could show children (algorithmic, using a child presence score29), (4) image safety for images that could contain pornographic content (algorithmic, using a combination of pornographic and medical image scores30 to filter out potentially unsafe images while retaining medically relevant ones), and (5) images where no skin condition was visible after redaction (manual).

Subsequently, each submission (images with demographic and symptom information) was evaluated by 1 to 3 dermatologists out of a pool of 10 US board-certified dermatologists (mean [range] years of experience, 14.8 [5.0-35.0] years). The first 750 submissions with self-reported health and demographic information were reviewed by 3 dermatologists, and all remaining submissions were reviewed by 1 dermatologist. Dermatologists provided a differential diagnosis and a confidence score from 1 to 5 (with 1 being low and 5 being high) for each condition label in the differential diagnosis, using a list of 419 conditions created using Systematized Nomenclature of Medicine Clinical Terms (SNOMED CT) condition categories and a labeling interface, as previously described.31 Dermatologists also estimated FST (eFST) based on the images. The differential diagnoses for each submission were mapped into a list of condition labels and weights using a previously described aggregation procedure31 (eMethods in Supplement 1). Submissions that were not diagnosable were included in the SCIN dataset and labeled as such. Each submission was also labeled with estimated Monk Skin Tone32 (eMST; a 10-shade scale) by 2 trained, nonclinical annotators from separate geographical locations (US and India). The weighted differential diagnosis of the skin condition, median eFST, and median eMST labels were released with the dataset.

Measures to Protect Contributor Privacy

Through the informed consent and image upload process, contributors were warned about the risk of potential reidentification. Contributors were advised not to upload images containing identifying features. They were also advised not to upload the same images on social media or other sites.

In addition to the manual deidentification procedure described previously, we passed the images through a reverse web search to exclude contributions with near-identical copies publicly available. We scrubbed image (exchangeable image file format) metadata, and redacted image areas containing text through a partially automated optical character recognition process.

Contributor age was bucketed in 10-year intervals. Where there were fewer than 10 individuals for any combination of potentially identifying variables, we masked these variables by converting them to unknown or bucketed them into a larger group. The SCIN data use license prohibits users of the open access data from attempting to reidentify individual contributors to the SCIN dataset.

Data Analysis and Reporting

Ads

We obtained ad impressions, click-through rates, and submission rates, with age and gender breakdown, from the advertiser-facing Google Search ads dashboard. We reported these as monthly means.

Dataset

Image quality scores were computed using standard photograph sharpness and exposure quality metrics designed for regular images.33 For analysis, we use the image sharpness and exposure quality thresholds in eTable 3 in Supplement 1. When analyzing dermatologist confidence against image quality, we used the mean score across all images in the submission, and the top dermatologist confidence across their differential.

The analysis of age, sex, and race and ethnicity vs US population means from the US Census excluded cases where the contributor chose to prefer not to answer. Race and ethnicity categories in our survey included American Indian or Alaska Native; American Indian or Alaska Native alone; Asian; Asian alone; Black or African American; Black or African American alone; Hispanic, Latino, or Spanish Origin; Hispanic, Latino, or Spanish Origin alone; Middle Eastern or North African; Middle Eastern or North African alone; Native Hawaiian or Pacific Islander; Native Hawaiian or Pacific Islander alone; White; White alone; 2 or more races; and another race or ethnicity not listed. Respondents were able to choose more than one response to the survey question about race and ethnicity. Alone indicates that a respondent only chose a single option. Categories without alone includes all those who chose that option, including those who picked additional options. The US Census did not have the Middle Eastern or North African and another race or ethnicity category, and the US Census asked about Hispanic, Latino, or Spanish Origin differently than in our analysis; the US census defines Hispanic as a type ethnic origin distinct from race, and individuals who select Hispanic also must select a race category. In our study, we allowed individuals to choose Hispanic alone, without specifying any other race or ethnicity.

When choosing public datasets for comparison of disease distribution, we included those where information about diagnosis or diagnostic category was available with numbers for each diagnosis or category. When choosing public datasets for comparison of FST and eFST, we included datasets that had not been enriched for population groups, geographies, or skin types. Where FST was reported even for a fraction of the dataset,8 we included those numbers as representative of the entire dataset.

Statistical Analysis

To compare ad view, click-through, and submission rates, we used the 2-sample t test for gender and analysis of variance for age groups. Spearman rank order correlation was used for all correlation analyses, and the χ2 test was used to analyze differences between SCIN contributor demographics and the US census. P < .05 was considered statistically significant. Data analysis occurred from January to February 2024. We used SciPy version 1.14 for Python (Python Software Foundation) for statistical analysis.34

Results

Outcomes of Web Searches as a Method for Crowdsourcing Consented Images of Dermatological Conditions

Of the 656 674 individuals to whom an ad for the SCIN dataset was displayed, 90 445 (13.7%) clicked on it; of those who clicked on the ad, 5749 (6.4%) consented to providing images and completed the submission process (Figure 1A), resulting in a total of 5033 contributors to the filtered dataset. Of the 5033 contributors, 2596 (51.6%) reported information on sex (1732 female [66.7%] and 864 male [33.3%]), 2556 (50.8%) reported information on age (1329 of 2556 contributors aged <40 years [52.0%]), and 2648 (52.6%) reported information on race and ethnicity (331 Black or African American [12.5%]; 320 Hispanic, Latino, or Spanish Origin [12.1%]; 1957 White [73.9%]) (Table 1). The SCIN dataset received a median (IQR) of 22 (14-30) submissions per day, with a total of 5749 submissions over 251 days. The volume of contributions did not vary substantially over time.

Based on data from the web search ads tool, significantly more female than male individuals viewed a SCIN ad (Figure 1B and eTable 4 in Supplement 1). Female individuals were also significantly more likely to click through to the application landing page (Figure 1C and eTable 4 in Supplement 1). Having clicked on the ad, female and male individuals were similarly likely to make a submission to the dataset (Figure 1D). Monthly ad views were similar across age groups (Figure 1E), but the likelihood of clicking on the ad increased significantly with age group (Figure 1F and eTable 4 in Supplement 1). Having reached the application landing page, younger individuals were more likely to complete a contribution (Figure 1G and eTable 4 in Supplement 1).

Quality of Photographs and Dermatologist Ability to Label With a Differential Diagnosis

Of the 5749 submissions, 5631 (97.9%) contained genuine images of skin conditions, with 2523 of 2835 (89.0%) being of short duration (2170 of 4019 submissions [54.0%] submitted within less than 7 days of condition onset). There were 118 submissions that were spam or did not contain images of a skin condition and were excluded. Further filtering was performed only to exclude duplicates across submissions (267 submissions) and for image safety reasons (331 submissions), resulting in a dataset of 10 408 images from the 5033 contributions, which are publicly available.35

Of the 10 408 images in the dataset, most were well-exposed (7764 images [74.6%]) and sharp (8951 images [86.0%]) (Figure 2A and B); and 365 of 10 408 images (3.5%) were under- or overexposed (Figure 2B). The right skew of the sharpness distribution was largely caused by redaction outlines, background objects, or other image artifacts (eFigure 2 in Supplement 1). Dermatologist confidence was not correlated with image sharpness (Spearman R = 0.0054) or absolute image exposure quality (Spearman R = 0.0007) (Figure 2C). Of 5033 contributions, 3060 (60.8%) could be assigned a differential diagnosis. Dermatologists’ ability to provide a differential diagnosis—or image diagnosability—varied from 93.3% (614 of 658 contributions) for images with 10 self-reported variables viewed by 3 dermatologists, to 58.9% (452 of 767 contributions) for images that were viewed by a single dermatologist and had no self-reported demographic or symptom information. Similarly, dermatologists’ confidence in their differential diagnosis was correlated with the number of self-reported demographic and skin-condition–related variables provided (Spearman R = 0.1537; P < .001) (eFigure 3 in Supplement 1).

Representation of Sex, Age, and Race and Ethnicity

Contributors submitted 1 to 3 images (mean [SD], 2.1 [0.9] images) of their skin concern. Between 49.7% (2648 of 5033 individuals) and 81.2% (4088 of 5033 individuals) reported symptom information (eTable 5 in Supplement 1). Contributors were most likely to report the affected body part and texture of the condition, and least likely to report sFST.

Among contributors who reported demographic information, female individuals had significantly higher representation (Table 1) in the dataset compared with the US population census estimates.36 Younger individuals also had higher representation compared with the census (Table 1). Of 2614 contributors, 852 (32.6%) reported having a racial or ethnic identity other than White. The self-reported racial and ethnic distribution was difficult to compare directly with US population estimates36 due to differences in the way that the question was asked (see Methods) and the number of categories. However, individuals identifying as American Indian or Alaska Native, Middle Eastern or North African, another race or ethnicity not listed, or 2 or more races had higher representation compared with the US census population means, and Asian, Hispanic, and African-American groups had lower representation (Table 1).

Range of Skin Conditions Across FSTs in the SCIN Dataset

Based on retrospective dermatologist labeling, the submitted images largely represent common, recent-onset conditions of inflammatory and infectious origin (Figure 3A). Of 4019 contributions with self-reported duration, 780 (19.4%) represented concerns arising within the past day, 2170 (54.0%) within the past week, and 3135 (78.0%) within the past month (eTable 6 in Supplement 1). Among the 3060 contributions to the SCIN dataset where a differential diagnosis could be estimated, 159 (5.2%) were neoplasms, while 2622 (87.0%) were eruptions, cutaneous infections, or contact dermatitis (Figure 3A and eTable 7 in Supplement 1). This composition contrasts with existing datasets3,14,37,38,39,40,41,42 from clinical sources, which largely focus on benign and malignant neoplasms (Figure 3A and eTable 8 in Supplement 1).

Crowdsourcing images of skin conditions from US internet search users, without selectively enriching population groups, resulted in a dataset that was broadly distributed across FSTs (both eFST and sFST) compared with existing datasets that are similarly unenriched for skin types, geography, or population subgroups (Figure 3B and eTable 9 in Supplement 1). sFST differed from eFST for many images (eFigure 4 in Supplement 1). The eMST labels in the dataset (Figure 3C and eTable 10 in Supplement 1) provide a more nuanced representation of darker skin tones than eFST, showing that a US-origin dataset unenriched for skin tone or type has less than 1.5% of individuals (59 of 5008 individuals) with eMST 8, 9, or 10. eMST rating varied with rater geography, resulting in 2 different eMST distributions for the same set of images (Figure 3C).

Discussion

In this survey study, we assessed the feasibility of using search ads as a crowdsourcing method to create a representative, diverse, and high-quality consented dermatology image dataset. In contrast with most existing dermatology datasets that are designed for malignant neoplasm detection,14 the SCIN dataset largely contains common, short-duration, allergic, inflammatory, and infectious conditions. This dataset distribution, along with the range of body parts, skin types, and demographics represented, is a key strength of the crowdsourcing method.

The higher representation of younger individuals and female individuals in the dataset is likely explained through a combination of who has dermatological concerns, who searches for dermatology conditions using mobile devices, who clicks on ads, and who is likely to volunteer to upload images for an open access dataset. The SCIN dataset includes self-reported race and ethnicity in 52.6% of contributions, more often than in many dermatology datasets.8 While the self-reported racial and ethnic categorization we used was not directly comparable with US census methods, the rate of racial and ethnic minority group participation is encouraging.

We provide retrospectively obtained dermatologist skin condition labels for the dataset. Unlike malignant neoplasms, which require biopsy confirmation,43 many common conditions of the kind seen in the SCIN dataset lend themselves well to image-based clinical labeling.44,45 Although dermatologists had high confidence in their labels for many submissions, these should not be considered equivalent to a clinical diagnosis. The lack of correlation of image quality with dermatologist labeling confidence could be due to our use of standard image quality metrics rather than medically appropriate image quality,46 or because labeling dermatologists had access to more context via self-reported information.

The choice between skin phototype and skin tone depends on use case, and these labels in the SCIN dataset should be interpreted with care. FST evaluation, whether through self-assessment47 or retrospective eFST labeling from photographs,15 has known limitations. The MST scale was originally devised for use as color comparison cards held directly against the skin,32 and eMST can vary based on the labeler geographic origin (Figure 3C and eFigure 4 in Supplement 1). Despite the limitations inherent in retrospective skin type and tone labeling, the SCIN dataset allows a comparison of sFST, eFST, and eMST for dermatology images (eFigure 4 in Supplement 1) and facilitates further dermatology research into skin tone and skin type (Table 2).

Unenriched for demographic group or skin tone, the crowdsourced SCIN dataset is broadly distributed across FST, but has relatively few contributors with MST greater than 8. This finding indicates that representing the full MST spectrum requires more global contributors or enriched sampling.

The use of search ads for crowdsourcing could potentially be applied to other kinds of health data that internet users can be expected to have on hand, especially for use cases where the data can be self-labeled48 or where ground truth can be obtained retrospectively (Table 2). Scaling the crowdsourcing approach responsibly will require attention to consent procedures and data-specific privacy preservation strategies.

Limitations

The SCIN dataset reflects some underlying limitations of the crowdsourcing method. It contains few images of rare conditions, and chronic conditions may be underrepresented as a result of relying on web search as an entry point for the effort.49 The use of web search ads and the reliance on self-taken images using mobile phones influences the demographic composition of the dataset, albeit in a different manner than health-system–based dataset creation methods. Limited access to technology, health literacy, and language proficiency may exclude certain groups or cause them to be underrepresented. There are known limitations in photography technology for darker skin tones,50 and factors such as age could lead to lower quality images11 in specific groups. Because self-reported demographic information was optional and unverifiable, any comparisons made against the US population distribution are limited by potential selective self-reporting or incorrect reporting.

Although our study requested 3 photographs, these self-taken images are unlikely to provide sufficient detail for a definitive diagnosis of conditions like malignant neoplasms and may lack other relevant clinical context. Alternative strategies are therefore required for use cases where diagnostic ground truth is essential. For these reasons, crowdsourced datasets complement, rather than replace, clinically sourced datasets.

Conclusions

The findings of this this survey study suggest that it is possible to create a diverse, empirical health dataset without demographic targeting or selective sampling and that crowdsourcing is an effective and accessible alternative to existing health-system–centric dataset-creation methods. The SCIN dataset and the use of web search as an entry point open up new possibilities for research, medical education, and AI tool development.
