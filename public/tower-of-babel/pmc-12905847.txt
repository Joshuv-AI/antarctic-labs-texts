Serum N-glycomics for non-invasive detection of significant liver pathology across clinical phases of treatment-naïve chronic hepatitis B
Authors: Xinjie Li, Meijie Shi, Hao Yu, Jinbao Huang, Shanshan Ji, Bo Han, Yan Zhong, Pengyuan Kang, Yuqi Zhang, Cuiying Chen, Chao Zhang, Lei Xu, Xiaoling Chi, Tao Shen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEarly identification of significant liver pathology is crucial for timely antiviral intervention in individuals with chronic hepatitis B (CHB) infection. Current non-invasive methods show limited accuracy in detecting occult liver damage, particularly in those with normal ALT. Serum N-glycan profiles were determined using DNA sequencer-assisted fluorophore-assisted carbohydrate electrophoresis. Relative to mild pathology (G and S < 2), P1 increased by 27% in significant pathology (G and/or S ≥ 2), reaching 58.7%/48.7% in G4/S4 stages (vs. G0/S0). Background

Chronic hepatitis B (CHB) infection remains a major global health burden, affecting nearly 300 million people worldwide and serving as a primary driver of end-stage liver diseases including cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) [1, 2]. The natural history of CHB is characterized by progressive liver damage, and for treatment-naïve individuals with CHB infection regardless of disease phase, early and accurate identification of occult liver pathological damage followed by effective intervention is crucial for halting or slowing disease progression and improving long-term prognosis. China’s latest CHB guidelines have recommended that regardless of ALT levels, patients with significant liver pathology (i.e., moderate to severe inflammation and/or fibrosis) should be prioritized for antiviral therapy [3]. The recently released 2025 EASL Clinical Practice Guidelines and 2024 WHO guidelines also emphasize significant liver fibrosis as a core indication for initiating antiviral therapy [4, 5], as these individuals represent the key population for treatment benefit. This policy shift underscores the clinical urgency of accurately identifying occult significant liver pathological damage, particularly in “grey zone” patients with normal or mildly elevated ALT.

While liver biopsy is recognized as the histological “gold standard” for assessing liver inflammation and fibrosis, its inherent limitations, including invasiveness, high cost, risk of complications, sampling error, and inter-observer variability in pathological interpretation, severely restrict its widespread application in routine clinical practice [6, 7]. More importantly, the single-timepoint assessment nature of liver biopsy makes it difficult to serve as a repeatable tool to meet the clinical needs for long-term dynamic monitoring of individuals with CHB infection, and it is even less suitable for large-scale population screening [8]. Currently widely used non-invasive assessment methods, including serological markers (such as APRI, FIB-4) and transient elastography (LSM), have reduced the need for liver biopsy to some extent, particularly in excluding advanced fibrosis, but show limited accuracy in identifying early to intermediate inflammation/fibrosis [9, 10]. Systematic reviews and meta-analyses show that APRI score > 0.5 for diagnosing significant fibrosis has a sensitivity of only 72.9% and specificity of 64.7%, while FibroScan’s corresponding metrics are 75.1% and 79.3%, respectively [11]. These data indicate that existing non-invasive methods remain inadequate in identifying occult significant liver pathology, creating an urgent need for more accurate and convenient novel biomarkers.

Protein glycosylation is one of the most important post-translational modifications and is considered a highly promising source of disease diagnostic and prognostic biomarkers [12]. As the liver serves as the synthesis center for the vast majority of circulating glycoproteins, during chronic liver disease progression, persistent hepatocellular damage, inflammatory responses, and fibrosis progression systematically remodel glycosyltransferase activity, thereby altering serum N-glycan structural patterns, including changes in branching complexity, fucosylation, and galactosylation levels [12, 13]. Therefore, serum N-glycomic profiles can serve as a sensitive, systemic “window” reflecting the overall health status of the liver in real-time. Previous studies have confirmed characteristic serum N-glycan profile changes in patients with cirrhosis and HCC [14–16]. The GlycoFibroTest developed by Callewaert et al. can distinguish compensated cirrhosis from non-cirrhotic chronic liver disease with 79% sensitivity and 86% specificity [17]. GlycoCirrhoTest achieves a hazard ratio of 10.29 in predicting HCC risk in patients with cirrhosis, significantly outperforming AFP’s predictive value [18]. Recent studies have also found that in metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), the A2F bisect N-glycan of IgA can diagnose advanced fibrosis with high accuracy, providing further evidence for the application value of glycomic markers across different liver disease etiologies [19]. However, existing research has primarily focused on the diagnosis of cirrhosis or HCC. Studies evaluating the value of serum N-glycomics in diagnosing early significant liver pathological damage in treatment-naïve CHB across different clinical phases remain scarce, despite the critical importance of this clinical decision point.

Based on this background, this study aims to systematically analyze a cohort of treatment-naïve individuals with CHB confirmed by liver biopsy, delineating serum N-glycan profile characteristics and change patterns across different clinical phases and liver pathologies, elucidating the intrinsic associations between N-glycan features and liver histological inflammation grades and fibrosis stages, and on this basis, evaluating the comprehensive diagnostic performance of glycomic markers for early significant liver pathological damage in treatment-naïve individuals with CHB infection, thereby providing scientific evidence for developing novel non-invasive diagnostic tools and risk stratification strategies.

Methods

Study population and design

This multicenter cross-sectional study initially enrolled 658 treatment-naïve chronic hepatitis B virus (HBV) patients at the Guangdong Provincial Hospital of Traditional Chinese Medicine, West China Airport Hospital of Sichuan University, and the People’s Hospital of Chizhou between 2013 and 2023. Inclusion criteria comprised: (1) HBsAg positivity for over six months, (2) no prior antiviral therapy, and (3) availability of valid liver biopsy data. Treatment-naïve status was confirmed for all enrolled patients via telephone interview or written documentation. Exclusion criteria included: (1) concurrent liver diseases, (2) co-infection with other viruses (HAV, HCV, HDV, HIV), (3) malignant tumors, (4) immunosuppressive conditions, (5) incomplete essential clinical information, and (6) minor or pregnant individuals.

Patients were classified according to the 2025 European Association for the Study of the Liver (EASL) guidelines based on HBeAg status, HBV DNA levels, and ALT levels into four clinical phases [4]: HBeAg(+) infection phase, n = 137 (HBeAg positive, HBV DNA > 10^7 IU/mL, ALT ≤ 40 U/L); HBeAg(+) hepatitis phase, n = 144 (HBeAg positive, HBV DNA > 20,000 IU/mL, ALT > 40 U/L); HBeAg(-) infection phase, n = 253 (HBeAg negative, HBV DNA < 2,000 IU/mL, ALT ≤ 40 U/L); and HBeAg(-) hepatitis phase, n = 92 (HBeAg negative, HBV DNA > 2,000 IU/mL, ALT > 40 U/L). Thirty-two patients in the indeterminate phase were excluded from the analysis. The detailed recruitment flowchart is shown in Fig. 1A.

Clinical and laboratory assessments

Demographic data including age, gender, and body mass index (BMI) were recorded using standardized forms. Venous blood samples were collected after overnight fasting for biochemical and virological analyses. Liver function tests included alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), total bilirubin (TBIL), direct bilirubin (DBIL), indirect bilirubin (IBIL), albumin (ALB), globulin (GLB), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), and prothrombin time (PT). Complete blood count was performed to determine platelet count (PLT). For missing data (< 5% of variables), multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE) was performed, generating five imputed datasets with pooled estimates used for subsequent analyses.

Non-invasive fibrosis indices were calculated including the AST-to-platelet ratio index (APRI = AST/upper limit of normal × 100/PLT count) and fibrosis-4 index (FIB-4 = age × AST/(PLT × √ALT)). Liver stiffness measurement (LSM) was performed using transient elastography (FibroScan, Echosens, Paris, France) with ≥ 10 valid measurements and success rate > 60%.

Virological assessments

Hepatitis B virus (HBV)-related biomarkers, including hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg), hepatitis B surface antibody (HBsAb), hepatitis B e antigen (HBeAg), hepatitis B e antibody (HBeAb), and hepatitis B core antibody (HBcAb), were assessed using electrochemiluminescence immunoassay (ECLIA) on a Cobas e 801 analyzer (Roche Diagnostics, Basel, Switzerland). HBV DNA levels were quantified using the Cobas 6800 system (Roche Diagnostics). The most recent clinical variables and laboratory results obtained prior to liver biopsy were included. All assays included appropriate quality controls and were performed by certified technicians blinded to clinical outcomes.

Liver histopathological evaluation

Percutaneous liver biopsies were performed using 16-gauge needles with specimens ≥ 15 mm in length containing ≥ 6 portal tracts. Histopathological assessment was conducted by experienced hepatopathologists blinded to clinical data according to the Scheuer scoring system. Inflammation activity was graded as G0 (no activity), G1 (minimal activity), G2 (mild activity), G3 (moderate activity), or G4 (severe activity). Fibrosis was staged as S0 (no fibrosis), S1 (portal fibrosis without septa), S2 (portal fibrosis with few septa), S3 (numerous septa without cirrhosis), or S4 (cirrhosis). Significant liver pathological damage was defined as inflammation grade ≥ G2 and/or fibrosis stage ≥ S2 according to Chinese chronic hepatitis B guidelines [3].

Serum N-glycan assessment

Serum samples were stored at -80 °C until analysis. Serum N-glycan profiling was performed using DNA sequencer-assisted fluorophore-assisted carbohydrate electrophoresis (DSA-FACE) on the GlyFace platform with an N-glycomics analysis kit (Jiangsu Sysdiagno Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) according to manufacturer’s protocol [16]. 5 µl serum underwent enzymatic release of N-glycans from serum glycoproteins, fluorescent labeling, and desialylation procedures. The processed samples were analyzed for N-glycan profiles using an ABI3500dx genetic analyzer (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA). Each sample generated 12 distinct N-glycan peaks representing major serum glycoforms, which were detected and quantified. Data processing was performed using GeneMapper software version 4.1. The relative abundance of each peak was calculated as the ratio of individual peak height to the sum of all 12 peak heights, expressed as a percentage. N-glycan peaks (P0-P11) were categorized based on structural features including galactosylation status, fucosylation patterns, and branching complexity. Structural feature abundances were determined by summing the relative abundances of peaks sharing common glycosidic motifs.

Prediction model development and validation

Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed on glycomic and clinical variables to identify independent predictors of significant liver pathological damage. Prior to modeling, variance inflation factor analysis confirmed acceptable multicollinearity (VIF < 5 for variables). Three adjustment models were constructed: unadjusted, adjusted for demographic variables (gender, age, BMI), and adjusted for demographic and virological variables (HBsAg, HBV DNA), namely the glycomics combined model. Results were expressed as odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI).

The cohort was randomly divided into training (70%, n = 436) and validation (30%, n = 190) sets using stratified random sampling based on clinical phases, pathology severity (G and/or S ≥ 2 vs. <2), and gender to ensure proportional representation. Baseline characteristics showed no significant differences between sets (all p > 0.05) (Supplementary Table 2). The comprehensive performance of six prediction models for significant liver pathological damage was evaluated: (1) glycomics combined model with ALT and AST, (2) glycomics combined model, (3) clinical variable model selected through multivariate logistic regression, (4) liver stiffness measurement, (5) APRI score, and (6) FIB-4 index. Model performance was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, with area under the curve (AUC) as the primary metric. Model comparisons were performed using DeLong test. Optimal cutoff points were determined by maximizing Youden’s index. Performance metrics including sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), and accuracy were calculated with 95% CIs estimated by 2000 bootstrap resamples. Confusion matrices visualized classification performance. Variable importance was assessed using forest plots with ORs and 95% CIs. Statistical significance was determined using Wald test.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as median (interquartile range) and compared using the Mann-Whitney U test for two groups or Kruskal-Wallis test with Dunn’s post-hoc test for multiple groups. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages and analyzed using chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test as appropriate. Correlations between variables were assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient. For analyses involving multiple comparisons, the Benjamini-Hochberg method was applied for false discovery rate (FDR) correction to control type I error. All statistical analyses and graphical visualizations were performed using R (version 4.3.2). Key packages included: mice (multiple imputation), pROC (ROC analysis), caret (model training), boot (bootstrap CI), ggplot2, ComplexHeatmap, and corrplot for visualization. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses.

Results

Patient characteristics of different clinical phases

A total of 626 treatment-naïve chronic HBV patients were included and classified into four clinical phases: HBeAg(+) infection (n = 137), HBeAg(+) hepatitis (n = 144), HBeAg(-) infection (n = 253), and HBeAg(-) hepatitis (n = 92) (Fig. 1A).

Representative N-glycan profiles demonstrated distinct patterns between healthy controls and patients with different degrees of liver pathological damage (Fig. 1B). The Sankey diagram illustrated the mapping relationships between specific N-glycan peaks and their structural characteristic categories (Fig. 1C). Twelve N-glycan peaks (P0-P11) were identified with characteristic structural features including different combinations of galactose, N-acetylglucosamine, mannose, and fucose residues (Fig. 1D).

The median age was 37 (31–45) years with 58.6% males. Significant differences in demographic and laboratory parameters were observed across clinical phases (Fig. 2). Hepatitis phases showed significantly elevated liver function markers (ALP, LDH, TBA, ALT, AST, GGT) and fibrosis indices (LSM, APRI) compared to infection phases (all p < 0.0001). HBeAg(-) hepatitis patients additionally demonstrated decreased PLT and ALB, elevated GLB, TBIL, DBIL, PT, and FIB-4 index, indicating impaired hepatic synthetic function (Fig. 2).

Liver histopathological characteristics and N-glycan abundance patterns

Histopathological analysis revealed 66.0% of patients had significant inflammation (G ≥ 2) and 58.9% had significant fibrosis (S ≥ 2) (Fig. 3A). No inflammation (G0) and no fibrosis (S0) were most common in HBeAg(-) infection phase (6.7% and 5.1%), while severe inflammation (G4) and cirrhosis (S4) were most prevalent in HBeAg(+) hepatitis phase (12.5% and 13.9%) (Fig. 3A).

N-glycan abundance analysis showed P5 peak had the highest relative abundance, followed by P6, with distinct patterns observed across clinical phases, gender, disease severity, inflammation grades, and fibrosis stages (Fig. 3B). Standardized heatmaps demonstrated consistent alterations of multiple glycan peaks associated with liver pathological progression (Fig. 3C). Compared to their respective infection phases, P1 peak abundance increased by 19.6% and 36% in HBeAg(+) and HBeAg(-) hepatitis phases, respectively, while P11 peak increased by 82.4% and 73.4%. Conversely, P0 peak decreased by 20.3% and 27.6% in hepatitis phases, and P10 peak decreased by 21.6% and 20.3%. Notably, in patients with significant liver pathology (G and/or S ≥ 2) compared to those with mild pathology (G and S < 2), P1 increased by an average of 27%, with specific increases of 58.7% and 48.7% in G4 and S4 stages relative to G0 and S0 baselines, respectively. Similarly, P11 increased by 44.8% in patients with G and/or S ≥ 2 compared to mild pathology, and showed increases of 45.7% and 30.8% in G4 and S4 stages relative to G0 and S0, respectively. (Fig. 3C).

N-glycan abundance differences across liver pathological severity and clinical phases

In treatment-naïve chronic HBV patients, those with significant liver pathology (G and/or S ≥ 2) compared to mild pathology showed significantly decreased N-glycan peaks P0, P5, P8, and P10 (all p < 0.0001), while P1, P3, P6, P7, and P11 were significantly elevated. Similar patterns were validated in clinical phase comparisons, with both hepatitis phases showing comparable N-glycan alterations compared to their respective infection phases. Additionally, some N-glycan peaks exhibited phase-specific changes, such as P2 and P4 being significantly decreased only in HBeAg(+) hepatitis phase, while significant elevations of P6 and P8 were observed only in HBeAg(-) hepatitis phase (Fig. 4A).

Structural characteristic analysis revealed patients with G and/or S ≥ 2 had increased agalacto, mono-galactosylated, bisecting, fucosylated, and core-fucosylated glycans, while digalacto, non-fucosylated, and triantennary glycans were decreased, indicating altered glycosyltransferase activities during disease progression (Supplementary Fig. 1). Correlation analysis between glycomics and clinical parameters further demonstrated that peaks P0, P5, P8, and P10 exhibited negative correlations with liver function indicators such as ALT, AST, GGT and liver damage severity markers including G/S stage, LSM, APRI, while P1, P3, P6, P7, and P11 showed significant positive correlations, indicating that N-glycan profile changes were closely associated with hepatitis disease phases and pathological severity (Fig. 4B). Subgroup analysis revealed complex correlation patterns within N-glycan peaks and between N-glycan peaks and clinical variables, maintained in patients with significant liver damage but attenuated in those with mild pathology (Supplementary Figs. 2–3).

N-glycan profile characteristics in clinical phases with normal ALT

In CHB patients with normal ALT, N-glycan profiles maintained characteristic changes. In patients with G and/or S ≥ 2, peaks P1, P2, P3, and P7 showed significant elevations, while P8 and P10 peaks exhibited significant decreases. Particularly, P1 peak increased by 48.1% and 40.6% in G4 and S4 stages relative to G0 and S0 baselines, respectively, in the normal ALT population. Remarkably, N-glycan profile changes in patients with G and/or S ≥ 2 compared to mild pathology appeared more sensitive in the HBeAg(+) infection phase, manifesting as significant increases in P1, P2, P3, P4, P7 peaks and decreases in P5, P8, P10 peaks. (Fig. 5A). Most notably, P1 peak increased by 80.2% and 65.8% in G4 and S4 stages relative to G0 and S0 baselines, respectively, followed by P2 peak with increases of 54.1% and 45.2% (Fig. 5B). For HBeAg(-) infection phase, besides P1, P6 peak also showed specific decreases (Fig. 5A).

Structural characteristic and correlation analyses further confirmed that in normal ALT patients, HBeAg(+) patients with G and/or S ≥ 2 exhibited richer N-glycan profile changes closely correlated with liver function and damage indicators, highly consistent with previous analyses of all treatment-naïve populations, while HBeAg(-) patients showed only elevated agalacto glycans (Supplementary Figs. 2, 4). These comprehensive N-glycan profile changes indicated potential value for identifying significant liver pathology in CHB patients with normal ALT levels.

Multivariate logistic regression analysis of glycomics markers for diagnosing significant liver pathology

Through univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses, potential glycomics markers independently associated with significant liver pathology were identified (Table 1, Supplementary Table 1, Supplementary Figs. 5–6). In the glycomics combined model (Model 3) adjusted for demographics, HBsAg levels, and HBV DNA, N-glycan peaks P0, P1, P2, P7, and P11 all demonstrated independent associations with predicting significant liver pathology. Forest plot analysis revealed P11 as the strongest risk factor (OR = 3.84, 95%CI: 1.74–8.45, p = 0.0008), followed by P1 (OR = 2.04, 95%CI: 1.57–2.64, p < 0.0001) and P7 (OR = 1.75, 95%CI: 1.31–2.34, p = 0.0002). P2 served as a protective factor against significant liver pathology (OR = 0.07, 95%CI: 0.02–0.26, p < 0.0001), followed by P0 (OR = 0.30, 95%CI: 0.12–0.79, p = 0.0140). Integration with clinically relevant variables further enhanced the clinical predictive value for early non-invasive risk stratification (Table 1).

Performance of glycomics-based predictive models

Based on the established glycomics combined model, we further evaluated the performance of this model compared with other non-invasive diagnostic models in predicting significant liver pathological damage in both training and validation sets (Fig. 6, Supplementary Table 2). ROC analysis demonstrated that the glycomics combined model (AUC = 0.876, 95% CI: 0.844–0.908) significantly outperformed the clinical model (AUC = 0.818, 95% CI: 0.779–0.857), LSM (AUC = 0.817, 95% CI: 0.775–0.858), APRI (AUC = 0.830, 95% CI: 0.792–0.867), and FIB-4 (AUC = 0.672, 95% CI: 0.621–0.723) in the training set (all p < 0.001). The optimized model with ALT and AST achieved an AUC of 0.917 (95% CI: 0.891–0.942), further improving performance compared to the glycomics combined model (p = 0.0004). Consistent performance was demonstrated in the validation set, with AUCs of 0.891 (0.845–0.937) and 0.921 (0.884–0.958) for the glycomics combined and optimized models, respectively (Fig. 6A-B, Supplementary Table 5).

Variable importance analysis showed that in both the glycomics combined model and the optimized model with ALT and AST, P2 peak demonstrated the highest predictive value, followed by P11 peak, both exceeding the contribution of traditional indicator PT (Fig. 6C). Confusion matrices displayed the actual classification performance of different predictive models in diagnosing significant liver pathological damage in both training and validation sets (Fig. 6D). At optimal cutoffs, the glycomics combined model achieved sensitivity of 78.7% (95% CI: 74.0-83.3%) and specificity of 83.2% (77.5–89.0%) in the training set, with comparable performance in the validation set (78.0%, 70.4–85.2%; 88.9%, 80.4–96.1%). The optimized model demonstrated enhanced specificity of 94.6% (90.8–97.9%) and 98.4% (94.9–100.0%) with maintained sensitivity, achieving overall accuracies of 84.2% (80.7–87.4%) and 84.2% (78.9–89.5%) in both sets, demonstrating superior comprehensive performance compared to traditional diagnostic methods (Fig. 6E, Supplementary Table 3).

Subgroup analysis revealed that glycomics models demonstrated superior performance in detecting early-to-moderate liver damage (G/S ≥ 1–2) compared to LSM and other indicators, which only performed well in G4/S4 diagnosis, indicating that glycomics-related indicators support earlier diagnosis and intervention (Supplementary Table 4). Additionally, glycomics-based models maintained diagnostic value in chronic HBV patients with normal ALT, particularly in the HBeAg(+) infection phase, suggesting their application value in detecting occult but significant liver pathology (Supplementary Table 5).

Discussion

Through systematic analysis of 626 treatment-naïve individuals with CHB infection confirmed by liver biopsy, this study examined the relationship between serum N-glycomic profiles and disease phases as well as liver pathological damage. The glycomics-based diagnostic model demonstrated improved performance compared to several traditional non-invasive indicators, with maintained applicability in individuals with normal ALT who may harbor occult significant pathology. These findings support the potential utility of serum N‑glycomic markers as non‑invasive adjuncts to existing assessments for liver pathology; they may help risk‑stratify patients and reduce unnecessary biopsies if validated prospectively.

The specific N-glycan structural changes observed across different CHB disease phases and degrees of liver pathological damage provide insights into underlying biological mechanisms of liver injury. In patients with significant liver pathology, peaks P1 (NGA2F), P3 (NG1A2F), P6 (NA2F), P7 (NA2FB), and P11 (NA4Fb) were significantly elevated while peaks P0 (NGA2), P5 (NA2), P8 (NA3), and P10 (NA4) were significantly decreased. These changes correspond to increased abundance of agalactosylated, monogalactosylated, fucosylated, core-fucosylated, and branched glycans, accompanied by decreased digalactosylated and non-fucosylated glycans, correlating with pathological progression severity, which is consistent with previous research findings [20, 21]. These coordinated glycosylation changes integrate information reflecting both inflammatory/fibrotic processes and hepatocellular functional status, potentially providing more comprehensive assessment than individual liver enzyme markers.

N-glycan peaks P11 (NA4Fb), P1 (NGA2F), and P2 (NGA2FB) emerged as independent predictors for significant liver tissue damage in multivariate analysis. Based on peak annotation, P11 is consistent with core‑fucosylated, bisecting tetraantennary structures with reduced galactosylation, predominantly carried by acute-phase proteins and immunoglobulins. Its strong predictive power as a risk factor (OR = 3.84) likely reflects converging pathogenic mechanisms. First, core fucosylation catalyzed by α-1,6-fucosyltransferase (FUT8) is upregulated during liver fibrosis, with FUT8 expression correlating with hepatic stellate cell activation and TGF-β1 signaling [22, 23]. Second, bisecting N-glycan structures reflect altered N-acetylglucosaminyltransferase III (GnT-III, MGAT3) activity, which is induced by inflammatory cytokines and oxidative stress [24]. Third, reduced galactosylation indicates impaired β-1,4-galactosyltransferase (B4GALT) activity, a sensitive indicator of hepatocyte synthetic dysfunction [25]. Concurrently, the stepwise decline of digalactosylated glycans (such as P5) in patients with severe liver histopathology further reflects this impaired synthetic function [25]. Conversely, P2 represents high-mannose or hybrid-type structures with limited antenna processing. Its protective association (OR = 0.07) likely reflects preserved hepatocyte function, as these structures are preferentially expressed on serum proteins (transferrin, haptoglobin) under physiological conditions [26], with their dramatic decrease in advanced stages serving as an early indicator of disturbed glycosylation homeostasis.

The glycomics combined model demonstrated superior performance compared to traditional non-invasive indicators in diagnosing significant liver pathological damage, with diagnostic efficacy further enhanced when combined with conventional liver function markers. This performance may stem from the unique biological basis of glycomic markers: unlike transaminases that reflect acute hepatocellular damage, N-glycan modifications reflect chronic alterations in hepatic post-translational modification systems, potentially capturing signals from both inflammatory activity and fibrosis accumulation. Importantly, serum N-glycans have relatively long half-lives and appear less susceptible to transient physiological fluctuations, temporary inflammation, or short-term drug effects, which may provide more stable markers for disease monitoring [27, 28]. This characteristic is particularly crucial for individuals with normal ALT in the HBeAg(+) infection phase (“immune tolerant”) or HBeAg(-) infection phase (“inactive”), as these individuals may harbor occult liver damage that conventional biochemical markers often overlook [29, 30]. The clinical value lies not primarily in absolute AUC improvement, but in addressing critical unmet needs. Our results indicate that while LSM showed established utility in advanced liver injury stages (G4/S4), glycomic indicators demonstrated higher diagnostic performance for early liver injury (G/S stages 1–2). The glycomics model also maintained diagnostic capability in CHB populations with normal ALT, particularly in HBeAg(+) infection phase where the glycomics combined model achieved AUC of 0.876–0.924 compared to LSM’s AUC of 0.816, enabling identification of patients with occult significant pathology that conventional biomarkers may miss. Thus, glycomics markers should complement rather than replace existing tools, offering particular value in risk stratification of biochemically normal patients with potential underlying damage.

These findings align with evolving treatment paradigms. The 2025 EASL guidelines emphasize the “treat the liver, not the ALT” approach, focusing on liver pathology rather than biochemical indicators for treatment decisions [4]. The WHO 2024 global elimination strategy positions “functional cure” as a core objective, emphasizing early identification and treatment of patients with significant pathology [5]. The performance characteristics of glycomics-based diagnostic models may support these treatment approaches by facilitating earlier identification of candidates for antiviral therapy.

The observed HBeAg status-dependent glycomic differences likely reflect multifactorial interplay. HBeAg(+) infection represents immune tolerance with minimal inflammation despite high replication [30], while HBeAg(-) hepatitis involves active immune-mediated injury with fluctuating viremia [31], potentially influencing glycosylation patterns through distinct inflammatory profiles. HBeAg(-) patients often harbor precore/core promoter mutations influencing viral-host interactions [32]. Additionally, disease stage confounding exists: HBeAg(-) infection had the highest proportion of minimal pathology (G0/S0: 6.7%/5.1%) while HBeAg(+) hepatitis had the highest proportion of severe pathology (G4/S4: 12.5%/13.9%). HBeAg(-) patients are also typically older with longer disease duration, which may contribute to cumulative hepatocyte dysfunction [33]. The P6-specific decrease observed in HBeAg(-) hepatitis phase may reflect these combined effects rather than a single mechanism. Future matched studies controlling for age, viral load, and pathology severity would help dissect the relative contributions of these factors.

The technical aspects of glycomic detection support potential clinical implementation. The DNA sequencer-based serum N-glycan analysis platform requires minimal serum volume and offers standardization, reproducibility, and high throughput capabilities, potentially reducing reliance on liver biopsy [34]. Serum N-glycans demonstrate good stability (> 10 years at -80 °C, > 2 years at -20 °C, > 1 week at 4 °C) and resistance to degradation with minimal changes after three freeze-thaw cycles [27]. DSA-FACE technology shows robust performance with inter/intra-assay CV < 10%, with successful multi-center standardization demonstrated [34]. This high-throughput platform enables simultaneous analysis of 188 samples per run. However, several implementation obstacles remain: some hospitals in less-developed regions may lack the necessary capillary electrophoresis instrumentation, and integration with existing clinical algorithms needs development. While the technical platform is mature, systematic validation, standardization, and health economics studies are needed before widespread implementation.

In clinical practice, the glycomics model could serve as a risk stratification tool when combined with existing serological or imaging indicators. For patients classified as “low risk,” regular monitoring strategies may be adopted with reduced need for invasive examinations. For “high risk” patients, particularly those with normal ALT, the model may provide additional evidence to support liver biopsy decisions and treatment planning. From a resource utilization perspective, implementation of glycomic markers may reduce unnecessary invasive procedures while facilitating identification of patients requiring treatment, potentially offering benefits in resource-limited settings.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, as a cross-sectional study, this analysis examined correlations between glycans and pathology without assessing dynamic changes during disease progression or treatment. Evidence from hepatitis C and metabolic liver disease suggests glycomic profiles can normalize with successful intervention [12, 19]. Longitudinal studies should prioritize monitoring P11, P2, P1, and P5 through serial sampling with paired biopsies or elastography to determine temporal relationships with histological improvement and treatment outcomes. Such studies are currently planned by our group. Second, strict exclusion criteria ensured homogeneous CHB cohorts but may limit generalizability, as concurrent conditions (MASLD in ~ 30% of CHB patients [35], HIV/HBV co-infection in 10–15% [36]) are common in practice. Future validation in mixed populations with potentially adjusted algorithms will be necessary, though establishing “pure” CHB signatures provides essential reference patterns. Third, the study population primarily originated from a single geographical region (predominantly East Asian with genotypes B/C), where ethnic variation, genetic backgrounds, and HBV genotypes may influence glycosylation patterns. Future multi-center studies with genotype stratification across diverse geographic regions will be necessary to establish broader applicability. Additionally, potential selection bias may exist due to inherent limitations of liver biopsy, including sampling variability and inter-observer differences.

Conclusion

In summary, this study examined the relationship between serum N-glycomic characteristics and liver pathological damage in treatment-naïve individuals with CHB across different clinical phases. The glycomics-based diagnostic model for significant liver pathological damage demonstrated potential for clinical application, suggesting an alternative approach to tissue biopsy for non-invasive risk assessment and treatment decision-making in CHB infection.
