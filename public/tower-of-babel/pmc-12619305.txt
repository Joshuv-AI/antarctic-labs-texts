Anterior arch form-specific analysis of alveolar bone thickness and tooth inclination: associations and clinical relevance in skeletal class II malocclusion
Authors: Yifan Zhang, Jiashuo Wang, Yijia Yin, Peilin Li, Chaoran Xue, Xianrui Yang, Xianglong Han, Shufang Du, Peiqi Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTo evaluate the associations among alveolar bone thickness, labio‑palatal tooth inclination, anterior dental arch form, and vertical skeletal pattern in skeletal Class II malocclusion, and to derive anterior arch form–indicated risk predisposition for orthodontic tooth movement.MethodsCone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) scans of 100 skeletal Class II malocclusion patients were grouped into square, ovoid, and tapered arch forms, and were further divided into three vertical skeletal pattern groups. Alveolar bone thickness at three levels (3 mm, 6 mm apical from cementoenamel junction (CEJ), and root apex) on labial and palatal sides of maxillary central incisors, lateral incisors, and canines, as well as labio-palatal inclinations of these teeth, were measured and compared. Two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to compare the thickness of alveolar bone among nine dental arch form and vertical skeletal pattern groups. Pearson’s correlation test assessed the relationships between bone thickness and tooth inclination.ResultsAcross eighteen measurement sites, two‑way ANOVA identified an arch‑form main effect at eight sites and a vertical‑pattern main effect at seven sites (P < 0.05). The square group exhibited thicker bone than tapered group on the palatal side of central incisors, as well as the labial side of lateral incisors and canines (P < 0.05), while the tapered group had the thickest bone at labial root apex of central incisors (2.33 mm, P < 0.05). Significant differences in thickness were observed among three vertical skeletal pattern groups at 6 mm apical from CEJ and root apex sites of incisors and canines (P < 0.05). Square group had the smallest incisor inclination (103.09° for central incisors and 104.05° for lateral incisors, P < 0.05), and square group had smaller canine inclination (98.80°) than ovoid group (104.58°, P < 0.05). Significant correlations between bone thickness and tooth inclination were observed in three arch form groups (P < 0.05).ConclusionsAnterior dental arch form associates with alveolar bone thickness and tooth inclination, suggesting its potential to be a rapid, chair‑side preliminary indicator of tooth movement limits in skeletal Class II malocclusion.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-025-07121-9.

Background

The labio-palatal inclination and position of maxillary anterior teeth are critical in determining facial aesthetics and oral function [1, 2]. These teeth influence not only the harmony of facial proportions but also occlusion, masticatory efficiency, and speech clarity [3, 4]. In skeletal Class II malocclusion, characterized by a prognathic maxilla, retrognathic mandible, or a combination of both [5], these influences become particularly pronounced. Orthodontic camouflage treatment typically requires substantial retraction of maxillary incisors, while orthodontic-orthognathic combined therapy may involve expansion of maxillary canines to correct transverse deficiency during decompensation [6, 7].

Noteworthily, the success of orthodontic tooth movement is restricted by alveolar bone thickness [8]. Excessive tooth movement beyond the alveolar bone’s anatomical limits can lead to bone fenestration, dehiscence, and root resorption [9]. These complications compromise not only the long-term stability of orthodontic treatment but also periodontal health and tooth vitality [10]. Therefore, understanding the interplay between alveolar bone thickness and other relevant factors is essential for optimizing treatment planning.

Prior studies have shown a connection between alveolar bone thickness and dental arch morphology, particularly in the anterior region [11]. In Angle Class I malocclusion cases, square dental arches have been reported to have significantly thicker alveolar bone than tapered and ovoid ones [11, 12], suggesting the potential of dental arch form to serve as a predictor of alveolar bone boundaries, thereby aiding in the determination of safe limits for tooth movement. However, in skeletal Class II malocclusion, where massive tooth movement is common, this relationship remains unclear. Besides, the vertical skeletal pattern has also been identified as an influencing factor of alveolar bone thickness [13]. The independent and joint associations of dental arch form and vertical skeletal pattern with alveolar bone thickness still needs investigation in this population. Given these gaps, the present study focuses on the anterior dental arch form and examines its interaction with vertical skeletal pattern in predicting alveolar bone thickness.

Recent studies have also correlated alveolar bone thickness and tooth inclination. For example, in maxillary central incisors, labial bone thickness is positively correlated with incisor inclination, whereas palatal bone thickness is negatively correlated [13]. Conversely, in upper canines, palatal bone thickness shows a significant positive correlation with canine inclination [14]. These findings indicate that the correlation between inclination and bone thickness varies by tooth type and bone site. However, further exploration is required to clarify whether these correlations vary across different dental arch forms in skeletal Class II malocclusion.

The objectives of this study were to (1) evaluate the independent and joint associations of anterior dental arch form and vertical skeletal pattern with alveolar bone thickness in skeletal Class II malocclusion, (2) evaluate the association between anterior dental arch form and labio-palatal inclination of maxillary incisors and canines in skeletal Class II malocclusion, and (3) explore the correlation between labio-palatal inclination and alveolar bone thickness across different anterior dental arch forms. By elucidating these relationships, this study seeks to determine whether anterior dental arch form can serve as a preliminary indicator of alveolar bone thickness and tooth inclination, aiming to guide initial treatment considerations in skeletal Class II malocclusion.

Methods

This project was approved by the Institutional Review Board of West China Hospital of Stomatology, Sichuan University (approval number WCHSIRB-D-2024-472). In this study, subjects were randomly selected from CBCT images taken for diagnosis before orthodontic treatment between 2023 and 2024 in the Department of Orthodontics, West China Hospital of Stomatology. A retrospective analysis of cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) data was conducted, and workflow of the research process is shown in Fig. 1.

CBCT collection

All samples were obtained from the CBCT scans (J. Morita MFG Corp, Kyoto, Japan) taken with scan time 17.5 s, FOV 140 × 100 mm. The scans were preserved in digital imaging and communications in medicine (DICOM) format, with slice thickness of 0.25 mm.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

100 CBCT images were chosen based on the following criteria. Inclusion criteria: (1) Aged between 18 and 40 years, complete permanent dentition with no congenitally missing or supernumerary teeth (excluding the third molars); (2) Crowding less than 4 mm [13], calculated as the arch required space (the sum of individual mesiodistal widths of the second premolar to the second premolar on bilateral sides) minus the arch available space (the arch length of the dentition from the anterior teeth to the proximal contact of bilateral first molars) [15]; (3) Normally erupted maxillary anterior teeth, with no radiolucency at root apices, no significant root resorption or curvature; (4) Skeletal Class II malocclusion (ANB ≥ 4.7°, based on Chinese cephalometric norms [16]). Exclusion criteria: (1) History of orthodontic treatment or craniofacial surgery; (2) History of periodontal disease, observable alveolar bone absorption; (3) History of diseases or medication use that could affect bone metabolism, such as metabolic bone diseases, rheumatic diseases, hyperthyroidism, bone tumors, etc.

Anterior arch form dimension measurement and grouping

CBCT data in stereolithography (STL) format were imported into Geomagic Studio 2014 (Geomagic, Morrisville, NY, USA) for anterior arch form dimension measurement. The most labial point of proximal contact between the two maxillary central incisors and facial axis (FA) points of bilateral maxillary canines were marked. For each subject, intercanine width and intercanine depth were measured and their ratio (intercanine width/depth) was calculated to define the anterior arch form (Table 1, Additional file 1-Fig. S1). K-means clustering was applied to these ratios to classify the subjects into three arch form groups. For reporting, data-derived cut-points were used: square (>4.70), ovoid (3.70–4.70), and tapered (< 3.70) [17–21]. Based on SN-MP values of Chinese cephalometric norms, subjects in each arch form group were further divided into three subgroups for three vertical skeletal patterns: low-angle (< 27.3°), normal-angle (27.3°–37.7°), high-angle (>37.7°) 16.

Measurement methods of alveolar bone thickness and tooth inclination

The DICOM files of CBCTs were imported into Mimics 19.0 (Materialise, Leuven, Belgium) for subsequent measurements. Prior to measurement, each CBCT volume was re‑oriented with respect to three standard craniofacial reference planes: (1) Frankfort horizontal (FH) plane: defined by the bilateral Porions and the midpoint between the left and right Orbitales. (2) Midsagittal plane: constructed perpendicular to the FH plane and passing through the crista galli neck and the Opisthion. (3) Coronal plane: set perpendicular to both the Frankfort and midsagittal planes and intersecting the Opisthion [22]. Axial-guided navigation was utilized to choose observation planes (Fig. 2) [23]. The coronal reference line on the axial multiplanar reconstruction was aligned with the tooth’s maximum labio‑palatal width to define the axial cursor. By adjusting the axial cursor, which was lined up with the long axis of the tooth, on the coronal or sagittal multiplanar reconstructions, we could get an optimal visualization of the alveolar bone from the chosen perspective. The sagittal view was designated as measurement plane. On this plane, we focused on the maximum labio-palatal width of the selected tooth and marked reference lines on it[24, 25].The alveolar bone thickness and tooth inclination were measured respectively (Table 1; Fig. 3). For measurement of inclination, the palatal plane was defined as the plane perpendicular to the midsagittal plane and passing through the line connecting ANS and PNS of the maxilla, and the angle between the long axis of tooth and the palatal plane was measured as the tooth inclination.

Statistical analysis

All measurements were made by one examiner under consistent conditions. To assess data reliability, 20 CBCTs were randomly picked from the sample for remeasurement after two weeks. The inter-session repeatability was assessed through intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). The average values of the corresponding teeth on bilateral sides of maxillary dental arch were used for subsequent analysis.

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0 (IBM, NY, USA). The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test and Levene test were used for examining normality probability distribution and homogeneity of variance, respectively. A two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for primary group comparisons of alveolar bone thickness across the nine groups generated by the combination of dental‑arch form (3 levels) and vertical skeletal pattern (3 levels). Only when a site showed a significant main effect with no significant interaction did we perform a follow-up one-way ANOVA for that factor, followed by Tukey’s honestly significant difference (HSD) test for pairwise comparisons. An additional one-way ANOVA was conducted to compare tooth inclination differences among three arch-form groups. Pearson correlation test was used to analyze the correlation between tooth inclination and alveolar bone thickness.As a secondary, explanatory analysis, multiple linear regressions were fitted at each site to estimate the independent contribution of anterior arch form, vertical skeletal pattern, and tooth inclination (independent variables) to alveolar bone thickness (dependent variable) while adjusting for the others. All statistical tests were two‑tailed, with the significance level set at α = 0.05.

The sample size was determined using G*power 3.1.9.3 (University of Dusseldorf, Germany). A medium effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.40), conservatively selected to ensure an adequately powered sample size calculation [13, 16], a power of 0.85, and a two-sided significance level of 0.05 indicated that at least 90 participants were needed for a two‑way ANOVA with nine groups. Thus, a sample of 100 patients was included in this study.

Results

The results of normal distribution test indicated that all data were normally distributed, and thus parametric tests were applied, and means ± standard deviations (SDs) were used for description. High reliability of measurements was indicated by the ICC value of 0.884–0.952.

Patient demographic information

100 samples were divided into three arch form groups and subgroups for three vertical skeletal patterns: 33 in square group (9 low-angle, 13 normal-angle, 11 high-angle), 34 in ovoid group (9 low-angle, 13 normal-angle, 12 high-angle) and 33 in tapered group (9 low-angle, 9 normal-angle, 15 high-angle). The general information and arch-form-related measurements for each group are presented in Table 2. No statistically significant difference was found in gender, age, SNA angle, SNB angle, ANB angle or SN-MP angle among different arch form groups (P > 0.05).

The independent and interaction effects of anterior dental arch form and vertical skeletal pattern on alveolar bone thickness

Across 18 measurement sites, two‑way ANOVA identified an arch‑form main effect at eight sites and a vertical‑pattern main effect at seven sites (P < 0.05). An arch‑form × skeletal‑pattern interaction reached significance only at the root‑apex level (La) of the lateral incisors (P < 0.05) (Tables 3 and 4). Significant main effects of dental arch form were observed on labial bone thickness at five out of nine sites (P < 0.05), while vertical skeletal pattern exerted significant main effects on palatal thickness at six out of nine sites (P < 0.05).

Discussion

Orthodontic tooth movement is restricted by the anatomical boundaries of the alveolar bone. This study, for the first time, evaluated the association between anterior dental arch form and alveolar bone thickness in skeletal Class II malocclusion. Considering the varying tooth movement demands of different treatment strategies in skeletal Class II cases, not only maxillary incisors but also canines were analyzed. The findings suggest that anterior dental arch form may serve as a predictor of alveolar bone thickness and the anatomical limits of sagittal incisor movement and transverse canine expansion, thereby providing a reference framework for orthodontic treatment planning and risk assessment.

Clinical importance and classification of dental arch form

Consideration of the dental arch form is essential in orthodontic treatment planning to ensure long-term occlusal stability [18]. Previous studies have classified dental arch forms using commercial arch forms, such as OrthoForm (3 M Unitek, Monrovia, Calif) [26] and Natural Arch Form (American Orthodontics, Sheboygan, Wis) [27]. However, these classifications are based on clinical experience instead of measurement data. More recent approaches employ geometric or mathematical curve fitting to classify dental arch form with complex software [28, 29], which is impractical for clinical use. Given that this study mainly focused on the anterior segment of dental arch, the intercanine width/depth ratio could serve as an objective indicator for identifying dental arch form [17–21]. This study offers a clinically applicable classification method for Class II malocclusion.

Side- and site-specific associations of anterior dental arch form and vertical skeletal pattern with alveolar bone thickness

It has been verified that a correlation existed between dental arch form and vertical skeletal pattern [30]. However, the anterior-arch‑form × skeletal‑pattern interaction on alveolar bone thickness reached significance only at La of the lateral incisor, indicating largely independent contributions. As shown in results, both anterior arch form and vertical skeletal pattern exhibited side- and site-specific associations with alveolar bone thickness. Anterior arch form was associated with alveolar bone thickness on the palatal side of central incisors, as well as the labial side of lateral incisors and canines, whereas vertical skeletal pattern showed a greater relation with palatal thickness, especially at P6 and Pa sites.

Specifically, our data confirm that high‑angle skeletal patterns are associated with significantly thinner palatal alveolar bone, whereas low‑angle patterns display correspondingly greater palatal thickness, consistent with previous findings [16, 31, 32]. In particular, palatal bone at the mid‑root and apical levels was especially thin in the high‑angle group, a finding that may predispose these patients to post‑orthodontic complications when extensive maxillary‑incisor retraction is attempted. On the labial side, however, minimal significant differences in alveolar bone thickness were found among subjects with different vertical skeletal patterns, in line with Dalaie’s findings [13], suggesting that palatal constraints, rather than labial, should drive risk assessment in this subgroup.

Overall, the influence of anterior arch form and vertical skeletal pattern on alveolar bone thickness was side- and site‑dependent rather than uniform across all anterior positions, consistent with anatomical heterogeneity and conservative multiple‑comparison control. These findings support targeted risk assessment in treatment planning, emphasizing that arch form and vertical skeletal pattern can serve as key predictors of anatomical boundaries at specific sites in skeletal Class II cases. Notably, individual variability should also be taken into account in clinical practice, preferably with CBCT analysis.

Anterior arch form-related variations in alveolar bone thickness

In skeletal Class II subjects, square arches displayed greater mean alveolar bone thickness than tapered arches at the majority of measurement sites, consistent with previous findings [11, 33]. One proposed explanation is functional: patients with square dental arch may have stronger orofacial functions and greater masticatory muscle force, which could result in larger alveolar bone thickness, whereas tapered arches, often associated with lower muscle force, show reduced thickness [12]. An exception emerged at the labial apical level of the maxillary anterior teeth, where tapered arches exhibited the greatest thickness (up to 2.33 mm). This local increase is likely to be attributed to the proclined anterior teeth in the tapered group, consistent with findings that labial apical bone thickness increases with incisor proclination, while retroclined incisors are associated with thinner bone [34].

Correlation between alveolar bone thickness and tooth inclination across different anterior dental arch forms

The inclination of maxillary anterior teeth is crucial for orthodontic diagnosis and treatment. In this study, positive correlations were observed between the inclination and labial alveolar bone thickness at the apical level of maxillary central incisors, indicating that greater inclination corresponds to increased labial apical bone thickness. Conversely, negative correlations were mainly found on the palatal aspect. These findings align with prior reports [13, 34]. Interestingly, fewer correlation sites were found in the tapered group than the square group. Given that the dentoalveolar compensatory mechanism likely account for the correlation between tooth inclination and alveolar bone thickness [14], the reduced correlations in tapered arches may indicate that excess labial-inclination of incisors exceed the natural compensatory capacity of alveolar bone. The variability of the relationship between bone thickness and tooth inclination across different arch forms and measurement sites suggests that, in clinical practice, arch form and tooth inclination should be considered comprehensively to predict the anatomical limits of alveolar bone at specific sites and to plan orthodontic tooth movement appropriately.

The multiple regression models explained a notable proportion of variance across sites, and the incremental contribution of arch-form variables, based on Cohen’s 𝑓² benchmarks, ranged from mild to moderate (𝑓² = 0.02–0.15) at 11 sites, and moderate to strong (𝑓² = 0.15–0.35) at 2 sites. Notably, in the present study, after stratifying by tooth inclination ranges, square arches still showed thicker bone than tapered arches, further suggesting that the relationship between arch form and bone thickness remains relatively independent, even when inclination is considered as a confounding factor. These results revealed that arch form can be a preliminary predictor of alveolar bone thickness, and in clinical decision-making, both arch form and tooth inclination should be considered together to optimize treatment plans and enhance the safety of the treatment.

Clinical relevance: risk indication and strategy optimization

Drawing from the results, we propose anterior dental arch form as a practical predictor of alveolar bone anatomical boundaries during orthodontic planning. Since well‑defined thickness patterns emerged mainly in square and tapered arches, ovoid arches are advised to undergo personalized CBCT analysis, whereas CBCT indications for square and tapered arches can be tailored to the treatment biomechanics. Detailed recommendations are summarized below.

In skeletal Class II cases with square dental arches, despite generally greater bone volume, the labial root apex of maxillary central incisors was relatively thin (1.73 mm), indicating the need for CBCT examination before incisor retraction in camouflage treatment. Given the smallest incisor inclination in square arches, bodily movement rather than tipping movement is advisable to minimize risks such as dehiscence or compromised aesthetics [35]. Meanwhile, canines in square arches exhibited upright inclination and great labial alveolar bone thickness at cervical and middle thirds of the roots. Therefore, maxillary expansion and distal movement of canines after extraction are relatively safe in square arches.

In tapered dental arches, our results revealed low palatal bone thickness of central incisors at cervical (1.01 mm) and middle (2.26 mm) thirds of the roots. Previous studies confirmed that 5.1-mm incisor retraction would cause 1.09-mm palatal alveolar bone resorption [36]. This indicates iatrogenic damage risks and highlights the need for CBCT examination in incisor retraction cases of tapered arches. If necessary, periodontally accelerated osteogenic orthodontics (PAOO) may be considered to increase bone volume and reduce relapse risk [37]. Additionally, it has been verified that subjects with greater tooth inclination, especially when treated with maxillary incisor retraction, tends to experience significant resorption of the labial alveolar bone due to compressive forces [38, 39]. These biomechanical insights emphasize the importance of carefully controlling orthodontic forces, especially in tapered arches, where tooth inclination tends to be greater. Regarding the canines, labial alveolar bone at cervical and middle thirds of the roots is the thinnest among three arch form groups. As labial cervical bone thickness tends to decrease and dehiscence and fenestration rates increase after maxillary expansion [40], transverse expansion for tapered arches should be cautiously executed under CBCT guidance.

With the growing availability of limited‑field‑of‑view (FOV) CBCT, this modality could be adopted as a routine diagnostic adjunct in orthodontics, providing precise alveolar‑bone‑thickness measurements at substantially lower radiation doses than full‑volume scans. While anterior arch form offers a rapid, chair‑side estimate of the bony envelope, integrating limited‑FOV CBCT into the diagnostic workflow further enhances clinical decision‑making and enables finer risk stratification.

Limitations and considerations for future research

This study employed a retrospective, single-center design, which may limit sample diversity. While this does not affect the overall conclusions, Prospective longitudinal studies that track treatment outcomes and relapse rates would strengthen the clinical relevance of the findings.

To be noted, this study focused solely on skeletal Class II malocclusion due to its frequent requirement for substantial anterior tooth movement. Although age-related changes in alveolar bone have been reported in Class III cases, this relationship remains unclear in Class II subjects [41]. To minimize age-related bias, we included only periodontally healthy individuals aged 18–40, with even distribution across arch form groups. Moreover, although males generally exhibit thicker alveolar bone than females [42], the square arch group,which was paradoxically the thickest in our cohort, contained the highest proportion of female participants, indirectly suggesting that sex was not a major confounder. Nevertheless, future work should stratify analyses by sex to confirm or refute gender‑specific effects.

Additionally, this study was conducted based on a Chinese population, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other ethnic groups with different anatomical characteristics. Multicenter studies involving diverse ethnic groups are advisable.

Moreover, vertical skeletal pattern has been reported to be associated with dental arch form [30]. In sight of this potential association, in this study, an even distribution of vertical patterns across the three arch form groups was ensured and the interaction effects of dental arch form and vertical skeletal pattern in relation to alveolar bone thickness were assessed.
