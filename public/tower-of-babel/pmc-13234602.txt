Deletion 11q, monosomy 13, and deletion 13q in noncomplex karyotype acute myeloid leukemia
Authors: Adelaide J Kwon, Miguel D Cantu, Guilin Tang, Sa A Wang, Sharon K Germans, Derek Loneman, Prasad Koduru, Madhu Ouseph, Daniel A Arber, Robert P Hasserjian, Olga K Weinberg
Published: 2026

TO THE EDITOR:

Several major changes from the revised fourth edition of the World Health Organization (WHO) classification of hematolymphoid tumors to the International Consensus Classification (ICC) and the fifth edition of the WHO classification (WHO5) in 2022 involve updates to the diagnostic criteria for myelodysplastic syndromes (MDS)/neoplasms and myelodysplasia-related (MR) acute myeloid leukemia (AML).1, 2, 3 Both the ICC and WHO5 identify cytogenetic abnormalities (CA) and somatic mutations (SM) that are diagnostic of MR AML.2,3 Provided there are at least 20% blasts in blood or bone marrow (10% in ICC, which includes MDS/AML) and after excluding AML with defined recurrent genetic abnormalities (RGA), the presence of even 1 of the MR genetic abnormalities is sufficient to confer this diagnosis regardless of morphology.2,3 Although most of these abnormalities are common between the 2 classifications, some differences exist, including deletion 11q [del(11q)], monosomy 13 [-13], and deletion 13q [del(13q)] (collectively Chromosome 11 and 13 [Chr 11&13]). In patients with noncomplex karyotype AML with only 1 or 2 isolated defining SM or CA, these differences can have diagnostic and prognostic implications, as MR AML is associated with poorer outcomes.

The most recent changes in the ICC and WHO5 in 2022 to the diagnostic criteria for MDS and MR AML reflect an improved understanding of the underlying genetic drivers of myeloid diseases, increasingly shifting away from morphology and toward molecular and cytogenetic features. The ICC and WHO5 individually identify CA and SM as diagnostic of MR AML, with some differences between the classifications.2,3 The SM shared between the ICC and WHO5 include ASXL1, BCOR, EZH2, SF3B1, SRSF2, STAG2, U2AF1, and ZRSR2; RUNX1 is the only SM unique to the ICC.2,3 In contrast, there are several discordant CA between the 2 classification systems; Chr 11&13 are unique to the WHO5 and are not included in the ICC.2,3 Other CA, including trisomy 8 (+8), are present only in ICC.2,3 The CA shared between the ICC and WHO5 include complex karyotype (≥3 abnormalities), deletion 5q, monosomy 7 or deletion 7q, deletion 12p, deletion 17p, isochromosome 17q, and idic(X)(q13).2,3

Historically, Chr 11&13 were included among the initial MR CA in the fourth edition of the WHO classification of hematolymphoid tumors, as they were known to be associated with MDS, with del(11q) found in 1% to 2% of the cases and del(13q)/-13 in 2% to 4% of cases.4,5 In MDS, ∼70% of the cases with del(13q)/-13 and 50% of the cases with del(11q) are associated with a complex karyotype.4 Although some studies have postulated leukemogenic mechanisms, such as cryptic KMT2A rearrangements in del(11q) cases6 or deletion of 1 copy of RB1 in del(13q)/-13 cases,7 there are limited data to suggest that Chr 11&13 independently or directly impact the expression of disease-specific MR features. In AML without a history of MDS, complex karyotype, or other MR-defining genetic abnormalities, it remains unclear whether the presence of Chr 11&13 alone leads to clinicopathologic features and an aggressive disease course consistent with MR AML.

We have previously investigated the prognostic and clinicopathologic significance of +8 and determined that +8 alone did not confer the poorer prognosis typically associated with MR AML, suggesting that +8 alone may not contribute to MR-specific features.8 In this study, we sought to determine the significance of Chr 11&13 in AML lacking other MR CA, particularly their association with SM and patient outcomes.

We retrospectively identified de novo AML cases with Chr 11&13 as the sole abnormality from the University of Texas Southwestern Medical Center, Massachusetts General Hospital, Weill Cornell Medical Center, Dana-Farber/Brigham and Women’s Cancer Center, and MD Anderson Cancer Center between 2008 and 2024. Cases with complex karyotype (≥3 CA) and other MR CA and AML with RGA were excluded. To assess ICC-defined MDS/AML cases, we included cases with 10% to 19% blasts. We also identified +8 cases using the same criteria. A control group of AML cases without MR CA, complex karyotype, or RGA was identified from the same institutions. We then searched >1500 cases in public AML databases, including those from The Cancer Genome Atlas Program, Oregon Health & Science University, Washington University in St Louis, and Therapeutically Applicable Research to Generate Effective Treatments, for additional Chr 11&13 and +8 cases.9, 10, 11 We also reviewed genetic sequencing data and sorted cases into AML with and without SM (including RUNX1). Outcomes and overall survival (OS), defined as months from initial diagnosis to death or last follow-up, were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 10.6.1 in patients treated with intensive chemotherapy (induction or similar rigorous regimens) or hypomethylating agents.

To investigate the association between Chr 11&13 and SM, the Fisher exact test was used. The association between Chr 11&13 and clinicopathologic findings at initial diagnosis (white blood cell count, hemoglobin, platelets, and blast percentage) was analyzed using the unpaired t test. Finally, OS was analyzed using the Kaplan-Meier method coupled with the log-rank (Mantel-Cox) test.

The 345 identified AML and 3 MDS/AML cases had a median age of 65 years (range, 18-100) with a male-to-female ratio of 1.6. Of these, 198 cases (56.9%) had at least 1 SM (range, 1-5), and 59 cases had +8 (17.0%). There were only 11 cases (3.2%; 10 AML and 1 MDS/AML) identified with Chr 11&13, among which 9 had del(11q) (2.6% of total and 82% of the Chr 11&13 cases), and the remaining 2 had del(13q)/-13 (0.6% of total and 18% of the Chr 11&13 cases). Only 3 Chr 11&13 cases were identified among >1500 AML cases searched from public databases (<0.01% of all AML cases). Of the 11 cases, 4 (36%) had SM, 5 (45%) did not, and 2 (18%) had no available genetic data. In addition, 9 of the 11 cases had sole Chr 11&13 CA, whereas the remaining 2 had 1 other CA but not a complex karyotype. Genetic mutations are shown in Figure 1. No association was found between Chr 11&13 and SM (P = .52) or clinical parameters, including white blood cell count (P = .34), hemoglobin (P = .63), platelets (P = .40), or blast percentage (P = .30; supplemental Table 1). Among patients treated with intensive chemotherapy or hypomethylating agents (n = 328), OS was significantly shorter in patients with SM than in those without SM (P = .003) but not significantly different between patients with and without Chr 11&13, both when including (P = .25) and excluding (P = .74) +8 cases (Figure 2). Stratifying cases into patients with and without Chr 11&13 and with co-occurring SM also revealed no significant difference in OS, both when including (P = .37) and excluding (P = .38) +8 cases. Finally, stratifying cases into patients with and without Chr 11&13 and without co-occurring SM also revealed no significant difference in OS, both when including (P = .36) and excluding (P = .15) +8 cases. Limiting analysis to only patients who received induction chemotherapy (n = 261) showed similar results.

We found that the incidence of Chr 11&13 is very low in our database of selected AML cases (2.6% for del(11q) and 0.6% for del(13q)/-13), and these cases are similarly extremely rare within public databases of all AML cases (<0.01%). Furthermore, the presence or absence of Chr 11&13 does not appear to significantly affect OS. In contrast, a significantly shorter OS was seen in patients with SM. These results are similar to our previous findings with +8.8 The overall extremely low incidence of isolated Chr 11&13 alterations in AML and their lack of significant impact on outcomes, coupled with data showing no independent influence on MR-associated clinicopathologic disease characteristics, raises questions about whether the presence of Chr 11&13 aberrations alone should be considered sufficient to confer a diagnosis of MR AML.
