The rGO@AuNPs modified label-free electrochemical immunosensor to sensitive detection of CP-BNYVV protein of Rhizomania disease agent in sugar beet
Authors: Marziye Karimzade, Hashem Kazemzadeh-Beneh, Negar Heidari, Mehrasa Rahimi Boroumand, Parviz Norouzi, Mohammad Reza Safarnejad, Masoud Shams-Bakhsh
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
For the first time, a novel simple label-free electrochemical immunosensor was fabricated for sensitive detection of the coat protein of beet necrotic yellow vein virus (CP-BNYVV) as the causal agent of Rhizomania disease in sugar beet. To boost the amplification of the electrochemical signal, gold nanoparticles-reduced graphene oxide (AuNPs-rGO) nanocomposite was employed to modify the glassy carbon electrode. Anti-BNYVV polyclonal was immobilized onto a modified electrode by applying a thiol linker via a self-assembly monolayer (SAM) and activating the functionalized surface using (3-aminopropyl triethoxysilane) and glutaraldehyde. The determination step relied on the forming of an immunocomplex between the antigen and oriented antibody, resulting in a decrease in current in the [Fe (CN)6]3−/4− redox reaction. The response value exhibited direct proportionality to the concentrations of CP-BNYVV. Scanning electron microscopy, energy dispersive x-ray, cyclic voltammetry, and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy techniques collectively provided a comprehensive understanding of the structural, morphological, and electrochemical features during the modification steps. Under optimized experimental conditions, the fast Fourier transform square wave voltammetry responds to the logarithm of CP-BNYVV concentrations in a wide linear range from 0.5 to 50000 pg/mL and the limit of detection is calculated to be 150 fg/mL, implying the admirable sensitivity. Selectivity assay exhibited no cross-reactivity with other proteins from interfering virus samples. Satisfactory reproducibility and stability were achieved with a relative standard deviation of 3.1% and a stable value of 90% after 25 days, respectively. More importantly, the high performance of the immunosensor resulted in the direct detection of CP-BNYVV in spiked and infected plant samples, which affords a sensing platform with huge potential application for the early detection of BNYVV virus in field conditions.Graphical Abstract

Introduction

Rhizomania is a fungal-vectored virus disease affecting sugar beet (Beta vulgaris L.), caused by a soil-borne virus known as beet necrotic yellow vein virus (BNYVV) and transmitted by the plasmodiophorid Polymyxa betae [1]. The widespread epidemiology and rapid spread of the BNYVV virus in major sugar beet-growing regions worldwide have resulted in significant challenges and irreparable damage to the sugar beet production industry. This has led to reduced beet yield, decreased sugar content, and substantial economic losses in the absence of effective management strategies [2]. To mitigate these financial losses, the development of sensitive early detection systems is essential. These systems can facilitate suitable control strategies and disease eradication at the initial stages of infection.

Since the discovery of the BNYVV virus, numerous attempts have been made to identify and detect it. To date, various molecular techniques based on polymerase chain reaction (PCR) [3–5], remote sensing detection [6], the different types of enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) [7–9] and monoclonal and polyclonal antibodies based on coat protein (CP) antigen of BNYVV virus [10–13] have been applied to identify or detect the BNYVV virus in leaf and roots samples of infected sugar beet. Although molecular-based PCR techniques have been frequently utilized for the detection of BNYVV in infected sugar beet, they have main limitations such as the requirement for prior sequence information to construct primers, amplification of non-target PCR products due to non-specific primer binding to other sequences and susceptibility to errors by DNA polymerase due to potential mutations in the produced fragment, labor intensiveness, expense, time consumption, and the need for advanced equipment and skilled technicians, all of which reduce their sensitivity [8].

Although certain detection techniques such as antibody and serological-based assays boast high sensitivity, their drawbacks have hindered their widespread adoption for diagnosis. Limitations such as the accessibility of antibodies for target viruses, the requirement for large sample volumes, cross-reactivity issues, high costs associated with antibody development, antibody instability, and the time-consuming nature of completing ELISAs are significant factors diminishing the popularity of these techniques in virus diagnosis in plants [14–17]. Considering the mentioned limitation, there is a growing demand for new detection systems capable of providing accurate, selective, specific, and cost-effective detection or identification of causative pathogenic agents.In light of these challenges, the introduction of reliable and reproducible tools for BNYVV detection in sugar beet would have significant implications for early diagnosis and the implementation of practical strategies for timely disease management.

In the last decade, biosensing of disease-causing pathogens and generating of immunosensor-based polyclonal antibodies have emerged as alternative techniques for detecting bacterial or viral plant pathogens. These methods have demonstrated high efficiency compared to conventional techniques [18–20]. Although immunosensors offer high specificity, their reliance on labeling steps has contributed to the complexity of analytical methods and time consumption, thus reducing their popularity in the detection of plant pathogens [21]. Various transduction methods, including electrochemical, surface plasmon resonance, piezoelectric, and cantilever technologies, have been utilized to fabricate label-free immunosensors [22, 23]. Recently, label-free electrochemical immunosensors have shown high performance due to their simplicity, high sensitivity, accuracy, simple label-free analysis, portability for in-situ application, cost-effectiveness, repeatability, and rapid measurement of unlabeled proteins [19–21, 24].

The layer’s configuration and modification of surface electrode, antibody immobilization, and formation of a stable immunocomplex via antibody-antigen affinity are the most important basis of the analytical immunosensors, and they could powerfully affect the performance of the immunosensor [16, 25, 26]. Recent prominent progress in nanoscience and the intelligent use of nanomaterial to modify the electrode surface led to obviously increased performances of the electrochemical biosensor through boosting signal amplifications, enhancing sensitivity and selectivity for disease diagnosis and for detecting target molecules in various biological applications [27–29]. Among the candidate nanomaterials, gold nanoparticles (AuNPs), quantum dots, carbon nanostructures, and polymers nanostructures are widely applied in biosensor fabrication because of their suitable electrical properties, high bioreceptor capacity at a reduced volume, good biocompatibility and immobilization property [28]. Therefore, the nanoparticles (especially AuNPs) may play a catalyst role in the electron transfer between the analyte and the electrode surface in nanomaterial-based biosensors [30]. Besides this, reduced graphene oxide (rGO) itself plays a significant role in accelerating the speed of electron transfer due to considerable properties including high specific surface area, appropriate electrical, and thermal conductivity properties needed for electrochemical biosensors [31]. Overall, the prevalent studies on the use of AuNPs and rGO in the fabrication of the electrochemical biosensor exhibited that they play critical functions in the generation of a synergic manner between catalytic activity, conductivity, high carrier mobility, transparent, biocompatibility to boost the signal transduction, accelerating the electron transfer between proteins and electrode surfaces, enhancing the sensor stability, sensitivity, and selectivity in the existence of the common interferences [32–35]. Recently, AuNPs and rGO have been involved in biosensor designs of a label-free electrochemical immunosensor to detect the bacterial causal agent of citrus canker diagnosis in citrus trees [16], potato late blight pathogen (Phytophthora infestans) in potato [37], false smut (Ustilaginoidea virens) in rice [35], groundnut bud necrosis orthotospovirus [38], Agrobacterium tumefaciens in Rosa hybrida L. [39] and citrus tristeza virus in citrus [40]. Hence, integrating or anchoring nanomaterials (AuNPs) onto rGO sheets-based electrochemical biosensors has appeared as a topic of much current study to avoid aggregation, preserve the surface area and pore volume, protect them from interference of the surrounding environment and also improve the notable preferment of electrochemical properties [41, 42].

In this study, a label-free immunosensor was developed for the direct detection of Rhizomania, through utilizing a novel electrochemical method. In this method, the immobilization of polyclonal antibody was carried out on the modified AuNPs-rGO/GCE through the self-assembly of mercaptan compounds activated by (3-aminopropyl)triethoxysilane and glutaraldehyde. The process of detection entails monitoring alterations in the Fast Fourier Transform Square Wave Voltammetry (FFTSWV) in the presence of [Fe(CN)6]3−/4− as a redox probe. Additionally, the electrode surface was characterized using cyclic voltammetry (CV), electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) and Field Emission Scanning Electron Microscopy (FE-SEM).

Materials and methods

Experimental material and equipment

The purified anti-BNYVV and CP-BNYVV protein were generated by Karimzade et al. [13] and refolded via the protocol described by Kazemzadeh et al. [43, 44]. The potassium chloride (KCl), potassium ferricyanide (K (Fe [CN]6)3−/4−), bovine albumin serum (BSA), 11-Mercaptoundecanoic acid (MUA), 3-Mercaptopropinic acid (MPA), (3-aminopropyl) triethoxysilane (APTES), glutaraldehyde (Glu), chloroauric acid (HAuCl4.3H2O) and graphene oxide were provided from Merck company. 0.1 M Phosphate buffer saline (PBS) solution (consisted 10 mM Na2HPO4, 10 mM KH2PO4, and 250 mM NaCl, pH 7.4) was provided using distilled water as electrode buffer.

PalmSens potentiostat (Palm Instrument BV, the Netherlands) was used for electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) and cyclic voltammetry (CV) as tools for measuring the electrochemical features during the modification steps. The quantification experiments were done using a home-designed potentiostat made in the Center of Excellence at university of Tehran. The software was also written in C + + space using dolphin toolkits [20]. In addition, the morphological and structural characterizations of the bare, rGO@Au nanocomposite electrodes were investigated using a JEOL JSM-7610F Plus Field-Emission Scanning Electron Microscope (FE-SEM, Tokyo, Japan). Also, energy dispersive X-ray (EDX) and map analysis were used to validate the deposited structures on the electrode surface. All electrochemical measurements were conducted using a conventional three-electrode system consisting of a glassy carbon electrode (GCE), 2 mm in diameter, as the working electrode, an Ag/AgCl as the reference electrode, and a platinum wire 3 mm in diameter as the counter electrode.

Solution preparation

To obtain the recommended concentration of antibody solution (1 mg/mL), the lyophilized powder of anti-BNYVV polyclonal antibody (1.65 mg/mL) was dissolved in 10 mL of PBS and then was diluted with PBS (1:16.5 v/v). Later, 10 μL of the freshly prepared anti-BNYVV solution was used to immobilize the step of immunosensor fabrication. To obtain different concentrations of antigen solution from 0 pg/mL to 50,000 pg/mL, the lyophilized powder of recombinant CP protein (2.23 mg/mL) was liquefied by PBS and then diluted with PBS at the recommended ratio (v/v). For electrochemical measurements, the probe solution was prepared by adding 250 mM KCl and 5 mM [Fe (CN)6]3−/4− redox couple in 0.1 M PBS solution (pH 7.4). Subsequently, CV, EIS, and Fast Fourier Transform Square Wave Voltammetry (FFT-SWV) measurements were conducted in probe solution.

Preparation of AuNPs@GO nanocomposite

To prepare a homogenous suspension of GO/HAuCl4.3H2O (2:1 w/w) for preparing the source of nanocomposite solution, 20 mg of GO was first liquified in 5 mL of deionized water and ultra-sonicate action was performed for 1 h. After this time, the GO solution was mixed into a mixture of 2 mg/ml HAuCl.3H2O solution. The final mixture was then ultra-sonicated for 1 h. In the first step, the cleaned GCE was immersed in the solution and cycled in the potential range of −1.2 to 0.7 V vs. Ag/AgCl with a scan rate of 0.05 V/s for 20 cycles. Through this process, GO was reduced to reduced graphene oxide sheets (rGO), and Au NPs were formed and placed within rGO sheets. After this time, the voltammograms were recorded as the nanocomposite-modified GCE response [45].

Preparation of the immunosensor

The process of the immunosensor fabrication is revealed in Fig. 1. In the first step, the GCE polishing was mechanically performed using 1.0 and 0.03 alumina powder on a clean soft micro-cloth polishing pad, respectively. After that, the electrode was utterly washed with deionized water. The polished GCE was immersed into the container containing ethanol and then was ultra-sonicated for 20 min to omit the remained particles and attain a thoroughly clean surface. Next, the electrode was more electrochemically cleaned by placing it in a freshly prepared 0.5 M H2SO4 solution and cycled in the potential range of −0.3 to 1.3 V with a scan rate of 50 mV.s−1 for 25 scans. Lastly, to have the signal for the clean surface, the electrode response was recorded as a bare signal in the probe solution.Fig. 1Schematic of the steps involved in the fabrication of elechtrochemical immunosensor for the diagnosis CP-BNYVV of Rhizomania disease agent in sugar beet

Schematic of the steps involved in the fabrication of elechtrochemical immunosensor for the diagnosis CP-BNYVV of Rhizomania disease agent in sugar beet

After cleaning the GCE surface, the nanocomposite was modified as stated in Sect. "Preparation of AuNPs@GO nanocomposite" . The nanocomposite-modified GCE was then immersed in a 7:3 (v/v) water/ethanol solution containing 50 mM MPA and 25 mM MUA for 1 h. At that point, the modified electrode (MUA-MPA/rGO@AuNPs/GCE) was rinsed with ethanol and double-distilled water to eliminate any possible non-specific unattached molecule, respectively. Subsequently, to generate a self-assembled monolayer of APTES (APTES/MUA-MPA/rGO@AuNPs/GCE), the chronoamperometric technique was used. In this approach, the modified electrode was immersed in a homogenized solution (5 mL of ethanol, 250 μL of hydrochloric acid 10 M, 5 mL of 0.01 g potassium chloride, 5 ml of deionized water) under potential −1.8 V and time 220 s [46]. Before anti-BNYVV immobilization, the amino terminals of APTES were activated by dropping 10 µL of the glutaraldehyde solution (0.1% w/w) onto the modified electrode surface for 3 h, followed by washing with PBS (pH 7.4). Subsequently, the 10 μL of anti-BNYVV solution was cast on a modified electrode surface and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h to immobilize the antibody. To eliminate and block any nonspecific active sites, anti-BNYVV/Glu/APTES/MUA-MPA/rGO@Au NPs/GCE was rinsed with buffer and incubated with 10 µL of 10% BSA solution for 30 min at 37 °C, respectively. After each layering step, the electrode response was documented in the probe solution under the same potential range and sweep rate. The finalized prepared immunosensor was rinsed with washing PBS buffer and kept at 4 °C to use in the next analysis.

Plant protein extraction for real sample analyze

The infected and healthy sugar beet samples were collected from the Sugar Beet Seed Institute of Iran, Karaj, Alborz province, Iran. The incidence of BNYVV virus in samples was previously distinguished by using PCR and indirect-ELISA assays [13]. The yellow coloration of leaves and the beard-like appearance of the lateral roots were used for sap extraction. For this purpose, 0.25 g of leave or root tissues were primarily pestled by liquid nitrogen in mortar followed by suspension in 4 mL of extraction buffer (5 mM EDTA, 5 mM β-mercaptoethanol, or 2% (v/v) polyvinylpyrrolidone, in 1xPBS pH 7.5). To end, the sap sample was centrifuged at 4000 g for 5 min and the supernatant was kept at −20 °C for real sample analysis.

Morphological characterization of Au@rGO coated GCE electrode

For a more detailed examination, after each deposition step, surface morphology analysis using the FE-SEM technique was employed as the subsequent tool to characterize the arranged immunosensor surface. In Fig. 2b, a typical thin fold-like nanosheet microstructure is evident, displaying a spongy mass combined with jagged edges, indicative of the successful formation of rGO. The scraggy surface of rGO provides abundant sites for the successive deposition of gold nanoparticles (AuNPs) and plays a crucial role in enhancing the biosensor's sensitivity by reducing the detection limit [47]. Following the deposition of AuNPs on the rGO-modified electrode, the Au@rGO nanocomposite was observed in a spherical shape with homogeneous dispersion, indicating the successful anchoring of AuNPs (Fig. 2c). The anchored AuNPs are arranged in an orderly manner among the carbon sheets of the rGO, exhibiting a diameter ranging from approximately 68 to 200 nm. The well-dispersed AuNPs signify a strong interaction between AuNPs and the rGO, which can boost the number of immobilized biomolecules (target antibody or protein), amplifying the signals and accelerating the overall conductivity potential compared to the bare GCE electrode. In summary, the increased surface area due to the high dispersibility of AuNPs on rGO nanosheets directly promotes the reactivity of the modified GCE, facilitates antibody attachment and ultimately enhances the sensing performance of the biosensor [48].

Electrochemical characterization of the immunosensor

To acquire more comprehensive information, CV and EIS techniques were employed to assess the electrode surface characterization at each step of modification in a standard probe solution containing 250 mM KCl and 5 mM [Fe(CN)6]3−/4− in PBS at pH 7.5.

CV measurements

CV is one of the effective methods for monitoring the interface characterization of the sensor surface. Figure 4a depicts the obtained cyclic voltammogram of the modified electrodes at different stages of immunosensor fabrication in the potential range of −500 to 800 mV at a scan rate of 100 mV/s.Fig. 4a Cyclic voltammograms in PBS solution pH 7.5, containing 5 mM [Fe(CN)6]3−/4−, 250 mM KCl, and (b) Nyquist plots for (curve a) glassy bare carbon electrode, (curve b) AuNPs@rGO/GCE, (curve c) MUA-MPA/AuNPs@rGO/GCE, (curve d) APTES-Glu/MUA-MPA/AuNPs@rGO/GCE, (curve e) anti-BNYVV/APTES-Glu/MUA-MPA/AuNPs@rGO/GCE, (curve f) BSA/anti-BNYVV/APTES-Glu/MUA-MPA/AuNPs@rGO/GCE, (curve g) CP-BNYVV/BSA/anti-BNYVV/APTES-Glu/MUA-MPA/AuNPs@rGO/GCE (curve h) electrodes

a Cyclic voltammograms in PBS solution pH 7.5, containing 5 mM [Fe(CN)6]3−/4−, 250 mM KCl, and (b) Nyquist plots for (curve a) glassy bare carbon electrode, (curve b) AuNPs@rGO/GCE, (curve c) MUA-MPA/AuNPs@rGO/GCE, (curve d) APTES-Glu/MUA-MPA/AuNPs@rGO/GCE, (curve e) anti-BNYVV/APTES-Glu/MUA-MPA/AuNPs@rGO/GCE, (curve f) BSA/anti-BNYVV/APTES-Glu/MUA-MPA/AuNPs@rGO/GCE, (curve g) CP-BNYVV/BSA/anti-BNYVV/APTES-Glu/MUA-MPA/AuNPs@rGO/GCE (curve h) electrodes

As expected, the CV of bare GCE electrode in [Fe(CN)6]3−/4− revealed a reversible redox behavior (curve a). A significant amplification in the peak current of the bare electrode was observed when Au@rGO nanocomposites were deposited (curve b). It was found that the nanocomposite-modified electrode displayed a higher reduction and oxidation peak at a potential of nearly 0.2 V compared to the bare GCE (curve a). The significant amplification in the CV peak current of the bare electrode upon the deposition of Au@rGO nanocomposites is attributed to the larger conductive surface area. This expanded surface area is advantageous for immobilizing a greater amount of the bio-receptor. Furthermore, the electron transfer facilitates for the [Fe(CN)6]3−/4− pair under this condition which is associated with higher electrochemical response of the system. The above result has been proved in previous works by Du et al. (2019) and Althagafi et al. (2019) [49, 50], which stated that the modification of the electrode with GO led to improved electrical conductivity of the bare electrode.

When the modified GCE was incubated with MUA-MPA, there was a remarkable decrease in the peak current due to an increased electron transfer resistance (curve c). This observation indicates the successful loading of the proposed MUA-MPA on the electrode surface. In contrast, a significant increase in the peak current occurred by deposition of APTES, and faster electronic transfer was achieved (curve d). The amino ends of APTES initiated an electrostatic interaction with the redox probe, directing it toward the working electrode. Consequently, this interaction led to a remarkable increase in peak currents. This re-amplified in the peak current was higher than bare GCE while was less than Au@rGO/GCE (curve b). After modification with Glu, a prominent shift in CV peak current decreased due to increased electron transfer resistance, where the electrochemical response was less than bare GCE (curve e). Then, the peak current was gradually decreased when anti-BNYVV was immobilized on the modified electrode (curve f). In the subsequent step, the antibody underwent a reaction with glutaraldehyde, leading to crosslinking between glutaraldehyde and the antibody. This interaction induced a notable reduction in the peak current of the [Fe(CN)6]3−/4− redox probe, primarily attributable to the substantial insulating properties of the antibody at the electrode surface, resulting in the conspicuous disappearance of both cathodic and anodic peak currents, (curves e and f). This outcome aligns with expectations, considering the insulating characteristics of proteins that impede the diffusion of charge transfer to the electrode surface. This infers that the antibody was covalently bound by Glu to the surface working electrode, previously modified with APTES. The covalent binding of the antibody to the surface hindered the charge transfer against the electrode surface. Subsequently, a further significant drop in the peak current was observed by blocking the modified GCE with BSA solution to deactivate the amino ends of APTES activated with Glu (curve g). Using BSA yielded a more operative diffusion barrier than the preceding layer for the redox probe [17, 20]. Accordingly, the peak currents remained notably absent. Ultimately, upon casting the CP-BNYYVV antigen on the modified electrode, there was a subsequent reduction in CV peak current (curve h). This decline can be attributed to the formation of CP-BNYYVV-anti-BNYVV immunocomplexes on the electrode surface, suggesting the development of a mass transfer barrier that restricts the transfer of electrons to the GCE surface. The results from CV calculations showed that each step of the fabrication of the immunosensor is successful and can be used to detect the BNYVV virus.

EIS measurements

To further characterize and verify the stepwise assembly of the immunosensor, Nyquist plots of the EIS were recorded in the frequency range of 0.1 Hz to 1 MHz with a DC potential of 200 mV and a step potential of 10 mV. As presented in Fig. 4b, curves a-h show the Nyquist plots for the various modification steps established on the electrode surface. The linear portion at low frequencies and the semicircle at high frequencies formed two portions of Nyquist plots, associated with the diffusion-limited process and the electron-transfer-limited process, respectively. Therefore, measuring the alteration in the charge transfer resistance value (Rct) in the EIS curves can be additional confirmation for the CV data. As illustrated, the Rct of the unmodified GCE electrode revealed a significantly larger semicircle. Following the modification of the GCE with an AuNPs@rGO nanocomposite, the Rct value for the modified GCE notably decreased (curves a and b). These data suggest that the incorporation of AuNPs and RGO onto the surface of the GCE provides a surface area with larger electronic conductivity properties, which probably plays a helper role in the further immobilization of the content of grafted bio-receptor [51, 52].

Rct value increased dramatically after the MUA-MPA was layered on modified GCE (curve c), this escalation could be attributed to the development of the SAM layer on the electrode surface and the presence of a repulsive interaction among negative probe ions ([Fe(CN)6]3−/4−) and COO − polyanion. The formation of the SAM layer on the electrode surface is accompanied by a substantial increase in Rct value. Likewise, the addition of APTES (curve d), and accordingly the surface activation due to the SAM layer, indicates the value of Rct decrease, similar to when the AuNPs@rGO nanocomposite was deposited on the GCE electrode (curve b). In other words, semicircle of Rct value is formed for curves b and d, but it is so small that it cannot be distinguished at high and low frequencies. Indeed, the improvement of the diffusion of [Fe(CN)6]3−/4− ions and the reduction of the negative charge of the electrode surface can be a possible reason for these variations in the values of Rct and boosting of the peak current (Fig. 4a, curve d). Correspondingly, the drastic reduction in the Rct value due high electron charge transfer was confirmed by CV measurement for AuNPs@rGO nanocomposite and APTES on modified GCE electrodes, as shown in Fig. 4a (curves b and d). Furthermore, the hydroxyl end groups were engaged in interaction with APTES, which possesses primary amine groups on its surface. This facilitated the covalent immobilization of antibodies. The self-assembly of APTES on the electrode surface resulted in a high reduction in Rct against the electrode surface. This decline in impedance, likely stemming from the introduction of amino ends in the outer layer, accelerated the diffusion of the Fe [CN6] 4−/3 redox probe to the surface, (curve d). Following the immobilization of Glu, the anti-BNYVV antibody was covalently binded to the activated surface of the electrode, leading to a notable increase in the Rct value. This elevation is attributed to the substantial hindrance imposed by the antibody on the diffusion of [Fe(CN)6]3−/4− to the electrode surface, (curves e and f). This result confirms that the formation and actual binding among anti-BNYVV polyclonal antibody and the modified electrode surface occurred positively. In the final step, unbound aldehyde ends bound the free BSA molecules. The Rct value of curve g increased slightly after incubation with BSA (1%) for unactivated sites blocking the immunosensor surface, which implying BSA will not influence the sensitivity of the system. As a result, the appropriate dielectric characteristic of BSA contributed to a significantly greater augmentation in Rct. Ultimately, upon the binding of the CP-BNYVV protein to anti-BNYVV antibody sites, a notable increase in Rct was observed (curve h). This phenomenon can be ascribed to the insulating properties of proteins and geometric limitations. These factors likely restricted the accessibility of probe ions to the surface, consequently leading to a reduction in the charge transfer value toward the electrode surface. Overall, EIS results are in agreement with those of CVs and approve that excellent electron conductivity on the AuNPs@rGO nanocomposite sitting on GCE is indeed strengthened by the SAM layering APTES and Glu, which can offer a sensitive interface to accelerate the electron transfer.

Optimization of effective parameters on immunosensor efficiency

To get the optimal electrochemical response and to promote the performance of the immunosensor, it is imperative to explore the dependency of ΔI on experimental conditions. The paramount significance parameters in this context are the pH level, the parameters associated with the applied square wave (SW) waveform, specifically frequency, and pulse amplitude, and the incubation time of the CP-BNYVV antigen. The immunosensor signal response (ΔI) was calculated by the equation of ΔI (µA) = I0–I, where I0 is the FFT-SWV peak current in the absence of the CP-BNYVV and I is the FFT-SWV peak current for different concentrations of CP-BNYVV. The anti-BNYVV concentration was fixed at 1 × 10–8 g/mL and ΔI was recorded at a concentration of 50 pg/mL of CP-BNYVV CP-BNYVV antigen (Fig. 5). The FFT-SWV response, an approach that leads to a highly sensitive techniques with the broad application was recorded [51]. As expected, for the measurement of CP-BNYVV, the FFT-SWV technique demonstrated greater sensitivity compared to other conventional methods (Data not shown). The frequency and amplitude are substantial SWV parameters in the FFT-SWV technique, which strongly affects the sensitivity of the immunosensor. However, in this direction, the frequency (Hz) and amplitude (V) were systematically optimized. In this pursuit, the immunosensor response (toward 50 pg/mL of CP-BNYVV antigen) was recorded at various frequencies (5, 10, 15, 20, and 25 Hz), with concurrent variations in amplitude values (0.01, 0.05, 0.1, 0.2, and 0.5 V). As depicted in Fig. 5a, the electrode response exhibited an increase with the SW frequency up to 180 Hz and an amplitude of 20 mV, after which it declined. A plausible explanation for obtaining a smaller response at other frequency values may be attributed to factors such as solution resistance and limitations in the rates of processes occurring at the electrode surface. Therefore, the FFT-SWV technique at a frequency of 180 Hz and amplitude of 20 mV was applied in the following investigation to optimize the pH value and incubation time of CP-BNYVV.

It has been evidenced that high acidity and basicity conditions can affect the stability and reproducibility of immunosensors by denaturing immobilized antibodies and the structure of bounded proteins [48]. The pH value of an operating medium is another vital parameter that a key role in examining the performance of immunosensors. The impact of pH on the electrode response was investigated utilizing CP-BNYVV solutions at a concentration of 5 pg/mL, prepared in PBS at pH values of 6.5, 7, 7.5, 8, and 8.5. These solutions were individually incubated on the prepared immunosensor surface for 45 min. The subsequent response was recorded using the FFT-SWV technique. As anticipated, the result clearly showed that the peak current significantly hinges on pH value. The lowest peak current (the best repones of immunosensor) was attained at the pH of 7.5, while lower and higher pH values (< 7.5 and > 7.5) increased in the FFT-SWV peak current (Fig. 5b). The results affirmed that the highest current was observed for the CP-BNYVV spiked in PBS with a pH of 7.5, which provides a suitable condition for activity of mass-immobilized anti-BNYVV to further binding with CP-BNYVV protein. Regarding the FFT-SWV current peak at various pH values, PBS with pH = 7.5 was monitored as the optimum pH value in the next experiment.

The incubation time of antigen plays a fundamental impact on the sensitivity and of performance the target immunosensor, given that achieving the maximum interaction between antibodies and antigens requires an appropriate timeframe [47, 54]. To investigate this, six immunosensors were prepared and exposed to a CP-BNYVV antigen concentration of 5 pg/mL, with incubation periods of 15, 30, 45, 60, 75, and 90 min. Subsequently, the electrochemical responses of these immunosensors were documented using the FFT-SWV method (at a frequency of 180 Hz and amplitude of 20 mV), as depicted in Fig. 4c. The graph illustrates an enhancement in immunosensor response with increasing incubation time up to 50 min, after which it stabilizes. These results indicate that after 50 min, the adsorption of the analyte has reached a saturated level at the surface, attributed to the saturation of the binding sites of antibodies by antigens during the immunocomplex reaction between the anti-BNYVV antibody and CP-BNYVV antigen [54]. Therefore, the optimal incubation time for subsequent determinations was identified as 50 min (Fig. 5c).

FFT-SWV response and calibration curve

To validate the detection functionality of the proposed immunosensor, CP-BNYVV protein in various concentrations was adopted for FFT-SWV measurement under chosen optimum experimental conditions.

In Fig. 6a, the FFT-SWV voltammograms of the immunosensor showcase distinctive peaks at various CP-BNYVV concentrations under optimized immunoassay conditions. The FFT-SWV peak currents demonstrate a decrease with rising CP-BNYVV concentration in the incubation solution, indicating the inhibitory effect of the formed immunocomplex on the electron transfer of probe ions toward the electrode surface [18, 19]. Moreover, to obtain the calibration curve, the plots of all the differentiated signals ΔI (µA) vs. CP-BNYVV concentration (Fig. 6b) and vs. logarithm of CP-BNYVV concentration (Fig. 6b, insert figure) were designed. A linear correlation is evident between immunosensor responses (ΔI) and the logarithm of CP-BNYVV concentration within the range of 0.5 to 50,000 pg/mL, resulting in a correlation coefficient of 0.9963. According to the ref [53], the limit of detection (LOD) can be assessed by the calculation of LOD = 3 s/S, where S is the slope of the calibration curve and s is the standard deviation (SD) of the response. The Relative Standard Deviation (RSD) values remained below 5.1%, and the detection limit was estimated at 150 fg/mL with a three-times signal-to-noise ratio (S/N ratio). This heightened sensitivity is ascribed to the advantageous conductivity and electron transfer capabilities of the AuNPs@rGO nanocomposite for anti-BNYVV immobilization. Conclusions

The current study established a simple, sensitive, and reliable label-free electrochemical immunosensor based on CP-BNYVV coat protein, which was introduced as a high diagnosis system for the detection of BNYVV virus in sugar beet according to the FFT-SWV method. The nanocomposite-modified glassy carbon electrode incorporation of AuNPs@rGO combined with the FFT-SWV showed high sensitivity measurement. Based on the obtained data, using the stated nanocomposite as of one the very porous and highly conductive structures provides an ideal expanded bed for immobilizing anti-BNYVV antibodies, thereby promoting electrode sensitivity in a minor zone. This mode of action in the electrode sensitive by nanocomposite can be a good advantage for helping to miniaturize the sensing system. Additionally, considering the above advantages along with the cost-effective and simple practice of preparation, the developed detection system based on an electrochemical immunosensor has extensive sensitivity and satisfactory performance for the detection of CP-BNYVV protein in a relatively complex biological matrix. So, the offered sensing system eliminates the prerequisite for a complex procedure, such as RT-PCR and ELISA. In summary, the purposed immunosensor offers a novel promising platform for the highly sensitive detection of Rhizomona disease under field conditions. To guarantee food security and decline crop loss, the diagnosis of plant pathogens in the early stages of infection functions a fundamental role in agriculture industry. Recently, technological advancements in plant science have proceeded in plant diseases diagnostics from the molecular technique to electrochemical sensing platform of pathogens [18–20]. In this regard, sensing platform system based on electrochemical immunosensor have been progressively utilized for plant disease restraining. According to our survey, this is the first report establishing electrochemical immunosensor based FFT-SWV method for detecting BNYVV virus as causal agent of the Rhizomania disease in sugar beet under field conditions. Utilizing electrochemical immunosensor in the field of plant pathology, coupled with other new scientific topics such as machine learning and mathematical modeling, can be a more integral part of plant biology research which enables plant researchers to systematize the system level-approaches with experimentally testable predictions in the modern agriculture of the future.
