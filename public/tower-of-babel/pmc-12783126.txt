Network pharmacology and molecular dynamics simulation elucidate the potential mechanism of Batatasin-III in Bletilla striata against ulcerative colitis
Authors: Yao Tong, Ruichao Chen, Huqing Ling, Yi Shen, Huichuan Tian, Li Zeng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Batatasin-III, a phenanthrene compound isolated from Bletilla striata, has demonstrated potential anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory effects, yet its precise molecular mechanism against ulcerative colitis (UC) remains largely unexplored. This study integrates network pharmacology, molecular docking, ADMET profiling, and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations to systematically elucidate the multitarget therapeutic potential of Batatasin-III in UC treatment. Batatasin-III-related targets were retrieved from SwissTargetPrediction, while UC-associated genes were collected from GeneCards and OMIM databases. A total of 101 intersecting genes were identified and subjected to PPI network construction using STRING and topological analysis in Cytoscape. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses revealed significant involvement in key biological processes and pathways such as MAPK signaling, PI3K-Akt signaling, protein phosphorylation, and cytokine-mediated inflammation. Molecular docking showed strong binding affinities between Batatasin-III and core targets ALB (− 8.4 kcal/mol), MAPK3 (− 8.2 kcal/mol), ESR1 (− 7.7 kcal/mol), and HSP90AA1 (− 5.7 kcal/mol). ADMET evaluation via ADMETlab 3.0 predicted favorable drug-likeness, bioavailability, and low toxicity for Batatasin-III. Subsequent 100-ns MD simulations demonstrated high conformational stability (RMSD < 3.7 Å), sustained hydrogen bonding, and compact binding dynamics, particularly in ESR1-Batatasin-III and MAPK3-Batatasin-III complexes. MM/PBSA binding free energy analysis supported strong binding thermodynamics, with ALB-Batatasin-III exhibiting the most favorable ΔG_bind (− 29.65 kcal/mol). Residue energy decomposition highlighted critical contributions from TYR411, MET125, HIS524, and ASN171, among others. To validate these computational predictions, in vitro assays were conducted. A CCK-8 assay confirmed Batatasin-III was non-cytotoxic to RAW 264.7 macrophages. In an LPS-stimulated model, Batatasin-III significantly and dose-dependently inhibited the mRNA expression of key pro-inflammatory mediators, including TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β, and NOS2. Overall, Batatasin-III may exert therapeutic effects against UC through multitarget modulation of inflammation, kinase regulation, and epithelial repair, primarily via the MAPK and PI3K-Akt pathways. This study provides a validated mechanistic foundation for Batatasin-III as a potential bioactive compound for UC intervention and supports further in vivo validation.

Introduction

Bletilla striata is a traditional medicinal plant with a longstanding history of use in East Asian medicine. Among its numerous bioactive components, Batatasin-III stands out as a significant phenanthrene derivative, known for its multifaceted pharmacological properties1,2. Notably, Batatasin-III is one of the major constituents of Bletilla striata, representing a substantial proportion of its phenanthrene content. The primary bioactivities of Batatasin-III encompass anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and immunoregulatory effects, rendering it a pivotal mediator of the plant’s therapeutic potential3,4. Recent studies have spotlighted Batatasin-III for its pronounced effects against inflammation and mucosal injury, pointing to new therapeutic prospects in UC management5–8.

UC, a chronic and relapsing inflammatory disorder of the colon, has risen sharply in global prevalence, posing a daunting challenge to public health systems9. According to recent epidemiological surveys, the incidence of UC continues to escalate, with millions projected to be affected worldwide by 203010. While UC is rarely fatal in itself, its clinical sequelae-including colorectal cancer, hemorrhage, and toxic megacolon-can be devastating11,12. Persistent colonic inflammation leads to epithelial barrier breakdown, ulceration, and immune dysregulation, creating a vicious cycle of tissue injury and disease progression13. The urgent need for safer, more effective interventions has never been more pronounced.

Natural products, favored for their safety profiles and complex multi-target actions, have come to the forefront of UC research in recent years. Batatasin-III, in particular, has emerged as a promising candidate. Mechanistic studies reveal that Batatasin-III can restore epithelial integrity and modulate key inflammatory cascades. For example, cross-sectional studies have indicated that Bletilla striata extracts may alleviate intestinal mucosal damage and reduce relapse rates in patients with inflammatory bowel disease14. Moreover, Batatasin-III has demonstrated potent inhibition of pro-inflammatory mediators such as TNF-α and IL-6 and attenuation of oxidative stress in both cellular and animal models of colitis15,16. Experimental models have further confirmed that Batatasin-III supplementation reduces disease activity index and histological scores in DSS-induced colitis, suggesting direct amelioration of mucosal pathology17. The compound appears to act by rebalancing immune cell populations, suppressing the NF-κB and MAPK pathways, and mitigating oxidative injury18,19.

Despite this encouraging evidence, the precise molecular mechanisms and direct targets of Batatasin-III in the context of UC remain incompletely defined. To bridge this knowledge gap, we turn to cutting-edge computational tools—network pharmacology, molecular docking, and MD simulation—to unravel the compound’s complex bioactivity spectrum20. Network pharmacology offers a system-wide lens, integrating omics data and network topology to decode multi-target effects of botanical compounds21. Molecular docking predicts the spatial and energetic interactions between Batatasin-III and protein targets, identifying key binding affinities and modes. MD simulation, meanwhile, refines these predictions in silico, delivering dynamic, atomic-level insight into the stability and specificity of these interactions.

In this study, we systematically investigate the anti-colitic mechanisms of Batatasin-III, identify its putative molecular targets, and elucidate its mode of action by integrating network pharmacology, molecular docking, and MD simulation. This work is poised to lay a theoretical foundation for the further biomedical application of Batatasin-III in the prevention and treatment of UC.

Materials and methods

Collection of catechin targets

The isomeric SMILES numbers for Batatasin-III and other principal components of Bletilla striata were retrieved from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). Subsequently, the SMILES strings (COC1 = CC(= CC(= C1)O)CCC2 = CC(= CC = C2)O) were imported into both the SwissTargetPrediction (http://www.swisstargetprediction.ch/) and SEA Search Server (https://sea.bkslab.org/) platforms to predict potential targets for Batatasin-III and related compounds22,23. The candidate targets identified by these two independent algorithms were integrated, and overlapping results were consolidated to generate a refined list of predicted protein targets for further analysis. This comprehensive approach ensured coverage and reliability in target prediction, leveraging the unique strengths of each computational tool24. Finally, the compound–target interaction network was constructed and visualized using Cytoscape 3.9.2, providing a holistic map of the molecular interactions underlying the pharmacological activities of Batatasin-III.

Screening of UC-related targets

UC–associated targets were systematically identified from two authoritative databases: GeneCards (https://www.genecards.org/) and OMIM (https://omim.org/), utilizing the keyword “ulcerative colitis” for all searches25,26. For GeneCards, the keyword was entered into the search bar, and the resulting list of disease-relevant genes was exported in Excel format using the “Export” function. The primary fields extracted for further analysis included “Gene Symbol,” “Description,” and “Category.” These columns formed the basis of an initial screening to filter and prioritize the most relevant gene entries. OMIM, recognized as a comprehensive resource for human genes and genetic phenotypes, was queried in parallel by entering the disease term and navigating to the “Gene Map” section. The search results were subsequently downloaded as an Excel file, capturing phenotype–genotype associations and literature-based evidence. All gene targets related to UC from both databases were then consolidated into a single dataset, with duplicate entries removed to ensure non-redundant coverage. The curated collection of UC–related targets served as the foundation for subsequent network construction and analysis, enabling robust disease target mapping in this study.

Acquisition of intersection targets

The intersection of drug and disease targets were obtained by using Venny(https://bioinfogp.cnb.csic.es/tools/venny/) software, and the crossover targets were considered as potential targets for Batatasin-III in the treatment of UC.

Construction and analysis of the protein–protein interaction (PPI) network

The compound–gene interaction files (“network” and “type” files) were first prepared and imported into Cytoscape 3.9.2 to construct the “drug–target” network, enabling comprehensive topological analysis of network properties27. By dynamically adjusting node shape, color, transparency, and size according to degree value—the number of direct connections per gene—the visualization captures both the complexity and centrality within the network. For PPI analysis, the intersecting genes were uploaded to the STRING platform (https://string-db.org/). The species parameter was set as “Homo sapiens,” the minimum interaction confidence score was stringently adjusted to 0.900, and isolated nodes were concealed to ensure network clarity and analytic robustness28. The resulting high-confidence interaction network was exported as a TSV file and seamlessly imported into Cytoscape 3.9.2 for further exploration. Utilizing the “Network Analyzer” tool (Cytoscape → Tools → Network Analyzer → Analyze Network), a rich array of topological parameters was extracted-including degree, betweenness, and closeness. Network styles were generated using statistical parameters: node size and color gradations were mapped to degree value, accentuating the prominence of core targets, while edge thickness corresponded to the combined interaction score, visually encoding the strength of protein–protein associations. The top 10 hub targets were ultimately identified by ranking degree values, providing a strategic focus for downstream analyses. This multi-step, data-driven workflow leverages both STRING’s high-confidence interactome and Cytoscape’s advanced visualization capabilities, enabling clear identification of the pivotal proteins driving network connectivity in UC.

GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses

To further investigate the biological functions of the key target genes, GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were performed using the “clusterProfiler” package, with a p-value threshold set at p < 0.0529–31. GO functional analysis predicted gene functions across three categories: biological process (BP), cellular component (CC), and molecular function (MF), while KEGG pathway enrichment analysis identified key pathways associated with the anti-hypertensive targets of catechins. Both GO and KEGG analysis results were visualized using an online tool (https://cloud.metware.cn/).

ADMET analysis and toxicity-bioactivity prediction

ADMET is a crucial concept in drug research, representing the absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion, and toxicity of drugs. These characteristics are essential for assessing the safety, efficacy, and pharmacokinetic features of drugs. This study used the ADMETIab 3.0 database to evaluate the bioavailability32 of Batatasin-III. When assessing the bioavailability of the drug, we followed the five rules proposed by Lipinski33,34. The following assessments were conducted for Batatasin-III: (1) number of hydrogen bond donors (nHD): no more than 5; (2) number of hydrogen bond acceptors (nHA): no more than 10; (3) lipophilicity coefficient (LogP): no more than 5; (4) molecular weight (MW): typically no more than 500; (5) number of rotatable bonds (nRot): no more than 10.

Molecular docking

The 2D structures of small molecule ligands are obtained from the PubChem database (http://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). These 2D structures are then imported into Chem Office software to create their 3D structures, which are saved as mol2 files. Next, the RCSB PDB database (http://www.rcsb.org/) is used to select high-resolution crystal structures of protein targets as molecular pairs for receptor studies. The proteins are then processed using PyMOL software to remove water and phosphate groups, and the resulting files are saved as PDB files. Molecular docking is performed using AutoDock Vina1.5.6 software to explore the interactions between the protein and ligand. Autodock is used to process the structures of both the protein and small molecules, performing hydrogen addition and dehydratization on the protein, and hydrogen addition and torsion force determination on the small molecule ligands. The docking box coordinates are then determined, and the docking results are compared based on their scores to identify the optimal conformation of the molecular simulation. pymol and Discovery Studio 2019 software are used to visualize the interactions between the compounds and key residues, including 2D and 3D analysis diagrams. Generally, a binding energy of less than-5.0 kcal/mol indicates good binding activity, while a value less than-7.0 kcal/mol indicates strong binding activity. A lower binding energy suggests stronger binding activity, higher affinity, and a more stable conformation.

Molecular dynamics simulation

All MD simulations were performed using GROMACS 2022 software. The protein parameters were described by the CHARMM36 all-atom force field35, and the ligand topology and parameters were generated using the CGenFF server. Each protein–ligand complex was placed in the center of a cubic box and solvated with TIP3P water molecules, ensuring a minimum distance of 1.0 nm between the protein and the box edges. Counterions (Na⁺ or Cl⁻) were added to neutralize the system’s total charge. The final solvated systems contained approximately 60,000–75,000 atoms. The systems were first subjected to energy minimization using the steepest descent algorithm for 5000 steps to remove steric clashes. Following minimization, a two-phase equilibration was performed. First, a 200-ps NVT (constant Number of particles, Volume, and Temperature) ensemble equilibration was conducted at 310 K to stabilize the system temperature, using the V-rescale thermostat. Second, a 1-ns NPT (constant Number of particles, Pressure, and Temperature) ensemble equilibration was performed at 310 K and 1 bar to stabilize the pressure and density, using the Parrinello-Rahman barostat. Position restraints were applied to the protein backbone and ligand during equilibration. Finally, a 100-ns production MD simulation was run for each system under the NPT ensemble at 310 K and 1 bar without any restraints. The leap-frog algorithm was used with an integration time step of 2 fs. All bonds involving hydrogen atoms were constrained using the LINCS algorithm. Long-range electrostatic interactions were calculated using the Particle Mesh Ewald (PME) method with a cutoff of 1.2 nm. Trajectory frames were saved every 10 ps for subsequent analysis.

The root mean square deviation (RMSD), root mean square fluctuation (RMSF), radius of gyration (Rg), solvent-accessible surface area (SASA), and hydrogen bonds (HBonds) were analyzed using standard GROMACS tools. The binding free energy (ΔG_bind) was calculated from the final 50 ns of the trajectory using the MM/PBSA method implemented in the gmx_MMPBSA package.

Vitro experimental validation

Cell culture and treatment

The murine macrophage cell line RAW 264.7 was purchased from American Type Culture Collection. Cells were cultured in high-glucose Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin, maintained in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2.

Cell viability assay

The cytotoxicity of Batatasin-III was assessed using a Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay. Cells were seeded into 96 well plates at a density of 2 × 103 cells/well. After 24 h of incubation, the medium was replaced with fresh medium containing various concentrations of Batatasin-III (0.1 μg/mL, 1 μg/mL, 10 μg/mL). A negative control (NG) group was also included. Following incubation for 24 h, 10 μL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well, and the plates were incubated for 2 h. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. Cell viability was expressed as a percentage relative to the NG group.

All data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism (Version: 8.0, https://www.graphpad.com). In contrast, Batatasin-III stays comfortably within conventional physicochemical limits, reinforcing its promise as a lead compound with strong oral bioavailability potential.

Molecular docking validation

The molecular docking simulations demonstrated strong and specific interactions between Batatasin-III and four core UC–related targets: ALB, ESR1, HSP90AA1, and MAPK3 (Table 4). All docking calculations were based on minimized binding energy, with values lower than − 8 kcal/mol, indicating highly favorable binding conformations and spontaneous affinity between ligand and receptor proteins. According to commonly accepted molecular docking thresholds, binding free energies below − 7 kcal/mol typically represent strong and stable ligand–target interactions, thus reinforcing the credibility of these binding events in a physiological context40. To further broaden the scope of our investigation, four additional UC-related targets (MMP9, ERBB2, HSP90AB1, and MAPK14) were also analyzed. The result revealed an exceptionally strong binding affinity for HSP90AB1 (− 9.4 kcal/mol), but indicated weak or negligible interactions for MMP9 (− 1.7 kcal/mol), ERBB2 (− 3.8 kcal/mol), and MAPK14 (− 0.6 kcal/mol). Notably, the strong binding energy observed for HSP90AB1 is highly consistent with that of its isoform, our primary target HSP90AA1 (− 9.5 kcal/mol). This finding serves as a critical cross-validation, reinforcing the importance of the HSP90 family as a key target class for Batatasin-III. Therefore, based on a comprehensive assessment of all eight targets, the four original candidates (ALB, ESR1, HSP90AA1, and MAPK3), which consistently demonstrated the most robust binding potential, were selected for subsequent, more computationally intensive molecular dynamics simulations. As shown in Fig. 10, for these four selected complexes, Batatasin-III was consistently embedded deep within the respective binding pockets of the target proteins. In vitro validation of the anti-inflammatory effects of Batatasin-III

To assess the safety of Batatasin-III before functional assays, its cytotoxicity was measured in RAW 264.7 cells via the CCK-8 assay. As shown in Fig. 13A, treatment with various concentrations of Batatasin-III (0.1 μg/mL, 1 μg/mL, 10 μg/mL) did not result in any significant difference (ns) in cell viability compared to the NG control group. Batatasin-III inhibits LPS-induced mRNA expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines and NOS2

To validate the anti-inflammatory potential of Batatasin-III predicted by our in silico analysis, we examined its effect on the mRNA expression of key pro-inflammatory mediators (TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β) and NOS2 in LPS-stimulated RAW 264.7 cells.

TNF-α: Compared to the NG group (Fig. 13B), LPS stimulation significantly upregulated the mRNA expression of TNF-α(approx. sevenfold). However, pretreatment with Batatasin-III (1, 5, and 10 μg/mL) significantly and dose-dependently inhibited this LPS-induced expression.

IL-6: As shown in Fig. 13C, the IL-6 mRNA level in the LPS group was significantly elevated (approx. 2.8-fold) compared to the NG group. Batatasin-III pretreatment (1, 5, and 10 μg/mL) also significantly reversed this change in a dose-dependent manner.

IL-1β: LPS treatment led to a sharp increase (over tenfold) in IL-1β mRNA (Fig. 13D) expression, which was very low in the NG group. All tested concentrations of Batatasin-III (1, 5, and 10 μg/mL) significantly suppressed this upregulation, with the 10 μg/mL dose showing the strongest inhibition.

NOS2 (iNOS): NOS2 is a critical enzyme in the inflammatory response. LPS stimulation markedly induced NOS2 mRNA expression. Batatasin-III pretreatment (1, 5, and 10 μg/mL) significantly reduced its expression level (Fig. 13E), demonstrating a clear dose-dependent inhibition.

Discussion

UC, a chronic inflammatory bowel disease characterized by persistent mucosal inflammation and epithelial injury, has become an increasingly prevalent global health challenge41,42. In this study, a network pharmacology-based approach, coupled with computational pharmacology and molecular simulation, was adopted to explore the multi-target, multi-pathway, and multi-dimensional mechanisms by which Batatasin-III, a phenanthrene compound derived from Bletilla striata, may exert anti-UC effects.

A drug-disease-target network was first constructed, revealing that Batatasin-III shares 101 intersecting targets with UC, including inflammation-related genes such as MMP9, HSP90AA1, MAPK3, and ESR1. These targets formed the foundation for further analysis. The PPI network topology highlighted core nodes with high degree values-hubs that potentially regulate inflammatory signaling, immune responses, and epithelial repair. Targets such as MAPK3 and HSP90AA1 are particularly noteworthy due to their established roles in regulating mucosal inflammation, stress responses, and intestinal barrier integrity43–45.

GO and KEGG enrichment analyses shed light on the biological landscape influenced by Batatasin-III. In GO terms, target genes were enriched in MAP kinase activity regulation, serine phosphorylation, and protein kinase B signaling, all of which are key modulators of inflammatory cascades and epithelial regeneration. CC enrichment pointed to involvement in vesicle lumen, membrane raft, and protein kinase complex-indicating dynamic intracellular transport and membrane signaling processes. MF categories highlighted Batatasin-III’s potential to interact with kinase binding domains, including serine/threonine and tyrosine kinase activity sites.

KEGG pathway mapping further suggests that Batatasin-III may modulate key signaling axes including the PI3K-Akt, MAPK, and Rap1 pathways-all deeply implicated in UC progression, especially through their control over cell proliferation, apoptosis, angiogenesis, and cytokine production46–48. Additional enrichment in endocrine resistance and adherens junctions suggests that Batatasin-III may also modulate epithelial barrier repair and hormone-mediated inflammation-a mechanism echoed in recent studies of UC therapeutics49.

To validate binding specificity, molecular docking simulations demonstrated that Batatasin-III exhibited strong affinities (binding energies < − 8 kcal/mol) with ALB, ESR1, HSP90AA1, and MAPK3, forming stable ligand–receptor complexes via van der Waals forces, hydrophobic contacts, and multiple HBonds. For example, ILE214 and ASN171 formed crucial HBonds within the ATP-binding pockets of HSP90AA1 and MAPK3, stabilizing Batatasin-III within their catalytic cores-highlighting a potential mechanism of enzymatic inhibition or signaling suppression. Further MD simulations were conducted to assess the dynamic behavior and thermodynamic stability of these complexes. RMSD and Rg values indicated that Batatasin-III formed structurally stable complexes, especially with ESR1 and MAPK3, with fluctuations below 3 Å. Solvent-accessible surface area (SASA) and hydrogen bond analysis further supported stable conformational binding. Crucially, MM/PBSA free energy calculations confirmed strong binding affinities, with the ALB-Batatasin-III complex exhibiting a ΔG_bind of − 29.65 kcal/mol-the lowest among the four complexes, suggesting enhanced binding enthalpy and potential bioavailability at the systemic level50.

Residue energy decomposition analysis revealed that residues such as TYR411, PHE488, and ILE457 in ALB, and HIS524 and LEU346 in ESR1, made dominant contributions to ligand binding. These interactions mirror known pharmacophores involved in UC pathogenesis and support the hypothesis that Batatasin-III may target these proteins to disrupt pathological signaling or enhance mucosal healing.

While these comprehensive computational analyses provide a strong theoretical framework for the anti-UC mechanism of Batatasin-III, in silico predictions require biological validation to confirm their relevance. To address this, we conducted a series of in vitro experiments. First, a CCK-8 assay confirmed that Batatasin-III did not exhibit significant cytotoxicity on RAW 264.7 macrophages at concentrations up to 10 μg/mL, establishing its safety profile for functional assays. Crucially, we then investigated its anti-inflammatory effects in an LPS-stimulated macrophage model, which mimics the inflammatory activation central to UC pathogenesis. Our qPCR results demonstrated that Batatasin-III significantly and dose-dependently inhibited the mRNA expression of key pro-inflammatory mediators, including TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β, and NOS2. This experimental evidence directly validates our network pharmacology predictions. These cytokines (TNF, IL-6, IL-1β) are central nodes in the inflammatory networks we identified, and the suppression of NOS2 aligns with the modulation of inflammatory pathways such as the MAPK and PI3K-Akt signaling axes, which are known to regulate iNOS expression.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study successfully integrates comprehensive network pharmacology, molecular simulation, and in vitro experimental validation to elucidate the mechanism of Batatasin-III against UC. We computationally identified Batatasin-III as a promising multi-target agent, predicting its stable binding to key proteins like MAPK3 and HSP90AA1 and its modulation of critical inflammatory pathways. These hypotheses were then confirmed by our in vitro findings, which demonstrated that Batatasin-III potently suppresses the mRNA expression of TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β, and NOS2 in LPS-stimulated macrophages without exhibiting cytotoxicity.

This convergence of computational prediction and biological evidence provides a robust foundation for Batatasin-III’s therapeutic potential. It suggests that Batatasin-III, a natural phenanthrene derivative, may be a promising lead candidate for future UC therapeutics. While these in vitro results are encouraging, further in vivo studies using established colitis models are warranted to fully confirm its therapeutic efficacy and mechanism of action within a complex biological system.
