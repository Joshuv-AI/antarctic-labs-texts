Abelian surfaces in Hesse form and explicit isogeny formulas
Thomas Decru , Sabrina Kunzweiler

Abstract
. We develop a new method for the computation of ( 3 , 3 ) (3,3) -isogenies between principally polarized abelian surfaces. The idea is to work with models in ℙ 8 \mathbb{P}^{8} induced by a symmetric level- 3 3 theta structure. In this setting, the action of three-torsion points is linear, and the isogeny formulas can be described in a simple way as the composition of easy-to-evaluate maps. In the description of these formulas, the relation with the Burkhardt quartic threefold plays an important role. Furthermore, we discuss generalizations of the idea to higher dimensions as well as different isogeny degrees.



1.
Introduction

For explicit computations with elliptic curves, one often works with a plane projective model. Concretely, given an elliptic curve
E
with distinguished point
\mathcal{O}
, an embedding into the projective plane is defined by the choice of a basis for
L(3\mathcal{O})
. A typical choice is
\{1,x,y\}
where
x
has a pole of order two and
y
a pole of order three at
\mathcal{O}
. In this setting, the image of
E
under the embedding

\displaystyle E\hookrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{2},\quad P

\displaystyle\mapsto(x(P):y(P):1),~~P\neq\mathcal{O},

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}

\displaystyle\mapsto(0:1:0),

is defined by a Weierstraß equation.
In the following, we are interested in a different choice of coordinates for
L(3\mathcal{O})
.

From now on, assume that we are working over a field with characteristic
p\neq 3
.
We know that the
3
-torsion points of
E
act by linear transformation on the coordinates of a point. Denoting
E[3]=\langle P,Q\rangle
, we choose a basis so that the corresponding embedding
\iota:E\to\mathbb{P}^{2}
satisfies

\iota(R)+\iota(P)=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0\\
0&0&1\\
1&0&0\end{pmatrix}\cdot\iota(R),\quad\iota(R)+\iota(Q)=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&\omega&0\\
0&0&\omega^{2}\end{pmatrix}\cdot\iota(R),

for any point
R\in E
. Here
\omega=e_{3}(Q,P)
denotes the Weil pairing of
P
and
Q
; in particular
\omega
is a primitive third root of unity. The image of
E
in
\mathbb{P}^{2}
is in
Hesse form
. That is there exists a projective parameter
d=(d_{0}:d_{1})
with
d_{0}(d_{0}^{3}-d_{1}^{3})\neq 0
so that
\iota(E)=\mathcal{H}_{d}
with

(1)

\mathcal{H}_{d}:d_{1}(X^{3}+Y^{3}+Z^{3})=3d_{0}XYZ.

More formally, this embedding is induced by a symmetric theta structure of level
3
, and in particular, the coordinate functions
X,Y,Z
correspond to level-
3
theta functions,
[
28
, Section 5, Case (b)]
. Note that this model is defined over the base field if
E
has full rational
3
-torsion. In the literature, the neutral element is usually chosen to be
0_{\mathcal{H}_{d}}=(0:-1:1)
. In this case, the
3
-torsion basis generators from above are of the form

(2)

P=(-1:1:0),\quad Q=(0:-\omega^{2}:1)\quad\in\quad\mathcal{H}_{d}.

In this work, our focus is on principally polarized (p.p.) abelian surfaces. Throughout the paper, we work over a field
k
with characteristic
p\neq 2,3
which contains a primitive third root of unity
\omega
. The construction above generalizes to higher dimension.
In particular, for a p.p. abelian surface with full rational
3
-torsion, we can consider the embedding

\iota:A\hookrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{8}

induced by a symmetric theta structure of level
3
. As in the elliptic curve case, the action of
3
-torsion points in this model is normalized. Explicit equations for the image
\mathcal{A}_{d,h}:=\iota(A)
in
\mathbb{P}^{8}
are known. More precisely,
\mathcal{A}_{d,h}
is described as the zero locus of
8
quadrics and
4
cubic equations,
[
19
]
. In our notation
d=(d_{0}:\dots:d_{4})\in\mathbb{P}^{4}
is a coefficient vector defining the cubic equations, and
h=(h_{0}:\dots:h_{4})\in\mathbb{P}^{4}
defines the quadrics (see Theorem
3.1
for a precise description). In analogy with the elliptic curve setting, we say that
\mathcal{A}_{d,h}
is a principally polarized abelian surface in
Hesse form
.

1.1.
Contributions

The main result of our paper is a new method for the computation of
(3,3)
-isogenies between p.p. abelian surfaces in Hesse form. Here, the prefix
(3,3)
indicates that the kernel of the isogeny is a maximal
3
-isotropic group. The isogeny evaluation will be decomposed into four simple operations (see Notation
6.1
for more formal definitions):

–

S_{K}
: a symplectic transformation depending on the input kernel
K
,

–

\odot^{3}
: coordinate-wise cubing,

–

M_{3,3}
: a discrete Fourier transform (which is a symplectic transformation as well),

–

C_{\lambda}
: coordinate-wise (symmetric) scaling by the vector
\lambda\in\mathbb{P}^{4}
.

Using this notation, our results can be summarized as follows.

Main Theorem

(informal)
.

Let
\mathcal{A}_{d,h}
be an abelian surface in Hesse form. On input a maximal isotropic group
K=\langle Q_{1},Q_{2}\rangle\subset\mathcal{A}_{d,h}[3]
and
9
-torsion points
R_{1},R_{2}
satisfying
3\cdot R_{1}=Q_{1}
,
3\cdot R_{2}=Q_{2}
, we can determine a symplectic transformation
S_{K}
, and a vector
\lambda\in\mathbb{P}^{4}

so that

\phi=(C_{\lambda}\circ M_{3,3}\circ\odot^{3}\circ S_{K}):\mathcal{A}_{d,h}\to\mathcal{A}_{d^{\prime},h^{\prime}},

describes a
(3,3)
-isogeny with kernel
K
, between p.p. abelian surfaces in Hesse form.

Ignoring the cost of additions and scalar multiplications by a third root of unity
\omega
, the maps
S_{K}
and
M_{3,3}
are essentially free. The cost of an isogeny evaluation is then given by
9
cubings (application of
\odot^{3}
), and
9
multiplications (application of
C_{\lambda}
) over the field of definition.

We remark that this method allows us to evaluate multiplication by
3
on
\mathcal{A}_{d,h}
as the composition of a
(3,3)
-isogeny with its dual (provided that we are given two auxiliary
9
-torsion points as in the theorem).
Furthermore, we highlight that a priori the method works for general p.p. abelian surfaces in Hesse form. In particular, so-called gluing, splitting and diagonal isogenies are included. However, the concrete computation of the coefficient vector
\lambda
has to be adapted when working with certain edge cases.

On a technical side, our method is based on classical results about theta structures and the moduli space of abelian surfaces of level
3
. An important ingredient is the explicit description of
\mathcal{A}_{d,h}
by Gunji
[
19
]
which is based on the description of quadratic relations of level-
3
theta functions by Coble
[
7
]
, and cubic relations by Birkenhake and Lange
[
25
]
. Furthermore, the explicit relation of the model
\mathcal{A}_{d,h}
with the Burkhardt quartic and its dual play an important role. For this viewpoint, we rely on an analysis of the moduli space by Freitag and Manni
[
15
]
,
[
16
]
, Hunt’s exposition on the geometry of the Burkhardt quartic
[
21
]
, and further results by Bruin and Nasserden on the arithmetic of the Burkhardt quartic
[
5
]
.

Our article is accompanied by a SageMath
[
35
]
package implementing the described methods and different functionality concerning the arithmetic of abelian surfaces in Hesse form:

https://github.com/sabrinakunzweiler/level-3-arithmetic

1.2.
Comparison with isogeny formulas in the literature

Our method is inspired by
(2,2)
-isogeny formulas on Kummer surfaces using the level-
2
theta structure,
[
17
,
32
,
11
]
. In that setting, addition by
2
-torsion points acts by linear transformation on the coordinates of a point. This has led to remarkably simple isogeny formulas. Similar to our results, a
(2,2)
-isogeny is decomposed into four simple operations: a symplectic transformation, coordinate-wise squaring, a Hadamard transform, and coordinate-wise scaling. Note that the Hadamard transform is a discrete Fourier transform (with respect to a primitive square root of unity); and clearly cubing is the natural analogue of squaring. In that sense our method to compute
(3,3)
-isogenies can be seen as the natural analogue.

We note that there exist general purpose algorithms to compute
(\ell,\ell)
-isogenies in the level-
2
(or level-
4
) theta model developed in a series of work by Cosset, Lubicz and Robert
[
26
,
9
,
27
]
, see also
[
37
]
for concrete optimizations for different values of
\ell
. While these algorithms are also based on the theory of theta functions, they are different in nature. In particular, working with even level, the translation by
3
-torsion points is not given by linear transformations, hence this symmetry cannot be used to derive isogeny formulas.

Previous specialized algorithms to compute
(3,3)
-isogenies are based on different ideas. Explicit formulas to compute
(3,3)
-isogenies between Jacobians of genus-
2
curves were first given by Bruin, Flynn and Testa
[
4
]
in the context of
(3,3)
-descent. Some algebraic simplifications of the formulas were later provided in
[
13
]
which made them suitable in a cryptanalytic context. An advantage of the approach by Bruin, Flynn and Testa is that they only require the kernel of the
(3,3)
-isogeny to be rational, while our approach requires full rational
3
-torsion. However, the evaluation of the formulas is considerably more expensive than in our new framework.
The most efficient known method to compute
(3,3)
-isogenies is an algorithm by Corte-Real Santos, Costello and Smith
[
8
]
working on so-called
fast
Kummer surfaces, and requiring full rational
2
-torsion. In their optimized formulas, an isogeny evaluation costs
4
squarings and
26
multiplications while in our approach, a
(3,3)
-isogeny is evaluated with
9
squarings and
18
multiplications. This makes our new method (slighly) faster. But it should be mentioned that in turn, the tripling formula on the Kummer surface is more efficient.

The mentioned methods for the computation of
(3,3)
-isogenies all work on (different models of) the Kummer surface. In contrast to this, in our approach, we directly work on the abelian surface itself. Moreover, our method is not restricted to working with irreducible p.p. abelian surfaces. But the description of p.p. abelian surfaces in Hesse form,
\mathcal{A}_{d,h}
, naturally includes products of elliptic curves.

1.3.
Applications and perspectives

Explicit isogeny computations are important for algorithmic number theory. For instance,
(3,3)
-isogenies have been used in descent
[
4
]
or in the construction of Jacobians of genus-
2
curves with points of large order
[
20
]
. Moreover,
(3,3)
-isogenies have recently found applications in post-quantum cryptography
[
6
]
. Our method is particularly well-suited for the computation of
(3^{n},3^{n})
-isogenies for some large integer
n
. Decomposing the computation as a chain of
(3,3)
-isogenies, the auxiliary
9
-torsion points are naturally available at each step. For instance, our method could be applied in isogeny-based cryptography, more precisely in the two-dimensional variants of the signature scheme SQIsign
[
14
,
2
,
30
]
, one of which is in the running to become standardized by NIST
[
34
]
. One of the bottleneck computations in this scheme is the evaluation of a
(2^{n},2^{n})
-isogeny which could (after some parameter changes) be replaced by a
(3^{n},3^{n})
-isogeny.

Apart from these concrete applications, our results more generally show that the
\mathbb{P}^{8}
-model of a p.p. abelian surface is well-suited for explicit computations. Despite the similarities with
\mathbb{P}^{2}
-models for elliptic curves, we are not aware of many computational applications of the former in the literature.

An interesting question is whether our method generalizes to arbitrary dimension. We briefly elaborate on this question in the last section of the paper, but leave a concrete analysis of specific dimensions as future work.

(2,2)

1,2,3

In the preparation of this article, we verified that the computation of

with kernel

\phi=(C_{\lambda}\circ M\circ\odot^{3}\circ S_{K})(P),

where similar as before

denotes coordinate-wise (symmetric) scaling,

is discrete Fourier transform,

. Given that

Another future direction of research is the translation of our formulas to the Kummer surface of
\mathcal{A}_{d,h}
. On the one hand, under the projection
\mathcal{A}_{d,h}\to\mathcal{A}_{d,h}/\langle\pm 1\rangle
some of the symmetry concerning the action of
3
-torsion points gets lost. On the other hand there are fewer coordinates to work with. It would be interesting to see if the resulting formulas for the computation of
(3,3)
-isogenies, and in particular tripling, become more efficient.

2.
The Burkhardt quartic

The
Burkhardt quartic
is a hypersurface in
\mathbb{P}^{4}
defined by the quartic equation

(3)

\mathcal{B}:F(x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4})=x_{0}(x_{0}^{3}+x_{1}^{3}+x_{2}^{3}+x_{3}^{3}+x_{4}^{3})+3x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}x_{4}=0\subset\mathbb{P}^{4}.

The Burkhardt quartic is birationally equivalent to the moduli space of principally polarized abelian surfaces with a full level-
3
structure over
\mathbb{Z}[1/3,\omega]
,
[
36
]
. In particular, to any nonsingular point
h=(h_{0}:\dots:h_{4})\in\mathcal{B}
, one can associate an irreducible p.p. abelian surface with marked level-
3
structure. An explicit construction is provided in
[
5
]
.

2.1.
Automorphism group

The automorphism group of the Burkhardt quartic is isomorphic to the projective symplectic group
\text{PSp}_{4}(\mathbb{F}_{3})
. We use the representation
\text{Aut}(\mathcal{B})=\langle M,S_{0},S_{1},S_{2}\rangle
with

(4)

\begin{aligned} M=&~\begin{pmatrix}1&2&2&2&2\\
1&-1&2&-1&-1\\
1&2&-1&-1&-1\\
1&-1&-1&-1&2\\
1&-1&-1&2&-1\end{pmatrix},\end{aligned}\quad\begin{aligned} S_{0}=&~\mathop{diag}(1,\omega,1,\omega,\omega)\\
S_{1}=&~\mathop{diag}(1,1,1,\omega^{2},\omega)\\
S_{2}=&~\mathop{diag}(1,1,\omega,\omega,\omega).\end{aligned}

from
[
15
, Proposition 1]
. Here,
\mathop{diag}(\alpha_{0},\dots,\alpha_{4})
denotes the diagonal matrix with entries
\alpha_{0},\dots,\alpha_{4}
on the diagonal. The action on points of the quartic is given by the right action on the coordinate vector; we simply denote
h\cdot g
for
h\in\mathcal{B}
and
g\in\langle M,S_{0},S_{1},S_{2}\rangle
.

Remark 2.1
.

The automorphisms defined by
S_{0},S_{1},S_{2}
act by scaling the coordinates of a point on the Burkhardt quartic by third roots of unity in a compatible way. We note that

\langle S_{0},S_{1},S_{2}\rangle=\{\mathop{diag}(1,\omega^{a},\omega^{b},\omega^{c},\omega^{2(a+b+c)})\mid a,b,c\in\{0,1,2\}\}.

Furthermore, we will see later that the automorphism defined by
M
is related to a discrete Fourier transform (Definition
3.11
).

In our applications, we will be interested in reducible abelian surfaces, as well. For this purpose, we first recall a result about the singular points of
\mathcal{B}
.

Lemma 2.2
.

The singular locus of the Burkhardt quartic,
\mathcal{B}^{\text{sing}}
, consists of
45
singular points. Explicitly, the singularities
h=(h_{0}:h_{1}:h_{2}:h_{3}:h_{4})\in\mathcal{B}
are given by

(a)\quad h=\sigma(0:0:0:-\omega^{i}:1),\quad\text{with }i\in\{0,1,2\}

and
\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{4}\subset\mathcal{S}_{5}
a permutation fixing
h_{0}=0
,

(b)\quad h=(1:-\omega^{i}:-\omega^{j}:-\omega^{k}:-\omega^{-i-j-k})\quad\text{with }i,j,k\in\{0,1,2\}.

Moreover,
\text{Aut}(\mathcal{B})
acts transitively on
\mathcal{B}^{\text{sing}}
.

Proof.

A case distinction between
h_{0}=0
and
h_{0}\neq 0
yields the explicit description of the singularities of
\mathcal{B}
. There are
18
singularities of type
(a)
, and
27
of type
(b)
.

The transitivity of the action can be verified explicitly. Concretely, for any singular point
h\in\mathcal{B}
, we computed an element
g\in\text{Aut}(\mathcal{B})
with
h\cdot g=(0:0:0:-1:1)
; see
[
23
]
.
∎

2.2.
Burkhardt dual

The Burkhardt quartic is only birationally equivalent to the moduli space of principally polarized abelian surfaces with level-
3
structure. The Satake compactification of the moduli space is given by the normalization of the
dual of the Burkhardt quartic
,
[
15
]
. The latter will allow us to describe reducible abelian surfaces.

Definition 2.3
.

The
Burkhardt dual

\tilde{\mathcal{B}}
is the dual of the Burkhardt quartic
\mathcal{B}
, i.e, it is a hypersurface in
\mathbb{P}^{4}
defined by
\tilde{F}
, where
\tilde{F}
is the dual of the polynomial
F
defining
{\mathcal{B}}
.

The polynomial
\tilde{F}
is homogeneous of degree
18
. An explicit description is provided in
[
15
, Proposition 3]
. We further recall that by the definition of the dual,
\tilde{F}
is the minimal polynomial satisfying

\tilde{F}(\nabla F)\cong 0\pmod{F},

where
\nabla
denotes the gradient operator. In particular,
\nabla
defines an embedding
\nabla:\mathcal{B}\setminus\mathcal{B}^{\text{sing}}\to\tilde{\mathcal{B}}
. Explicitly,

(5)

\nabla:(x_{0}:\dots:x_{4})\mapsto\begin{pmatrix}4x_{0}^{3}+x_{1}^{3}+x_{2}^{3}+x_{3}^{3}+x_{4}^{3}\\
3(x_{0}x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}x_{3}x_{4})\\
3(x_{0}x_{2}^{2}+x_{1}x_{3}x_{4})\\
3(x_{0}x_{3}^{2}+x_{1}x_{2}x_{4})\\
3(x_{0}x_{4}^{2}+x_{1}x_{2}x_{3})\end{pmatrix}.

The action of
\text{Aut}(\mathcal{B})
extends to an action on the dual, where the elements
g\in\text{Aut}(\mathcal{B})
act by multiplication on the left with
g^{-1}
; we denote
g^{-1}\cdot d
for
d\in\tilde{\mathcal{B}}
. Note that, up to multiplication by a scalar,
M\equiv M^{-1}
for the matrix
M
defined above.

2.3.
Hessian of the Burkhardt quartic

Another variety that appears when studying the moduli space of abelian surfaces with level-
3
structure is the Hessian of the Burkhardt quartic.

Definition 2.4
.

The
Hessian of the Burkhardt quartic

H(\mathcal{B})
is the projective hypersurface in
\mathbb{P}^{4}
defined by

H(\mathcal{B}):H(F):=\frac{1}{486}\det\left(\frac{\partial F}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}\right)=0,

where
F
is the polynomial defining
\mathcal{B}
.

Note that the scaling ensures that
H(F)
is integral and has content
1
(see for example
[
5
, Section 3]
). One can check that
H(F)
is homogenous of degree
10
.

3.
Principally polarized abelian surfaces in Hesse form

Let
A
be a principally polarized abelian surface. Consider a line bundle
L
inducing the principal polarization. Then
L^{\otimes 3}
is very ample, and defines an embedding
\iota:A\hookrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{8}
. For any
3
-torsion point
T\in A[3]
, the translation map
P\mapsto P+T
acts by linear transformation on the coordinates of
\iota(A)
.
Here, we will consider a specific embedding
\Theta:A\to\mathbb{P}^{8}
induced by a symmetric theta structure of level
3
. In this setting, the action by
3
-torsion points is
normalized
.

We first recall an explicit description of the model
\Theta(A)\subset\mathbb{P}^{8}
. Then explain the relation of this model with points on the Burkhardt quartic and its dual in Subsection
3.2
, discuss symplectic transformations in Subsection
3.3
, and analyze the reducible setting in more detail in Subsection
3.4
. The action of three-torsion points is discussed in Section
4
.

Theorem 3.1

(Theorem 8.3 in
[
19
]
)
.

Let
A
be a principally polarized abelian surface with a level-
3
structure. Then there exist coefficient vectors
d=(d_{0}:\dots:d_{4}),h=(h_{0}:\dots:h_{4})\in\mathbb{P}^{4}
, and an embedding
\iota:A\to\mathbb{P}^{8}
with

\iota(A)=V(F_{1},F_{2},F_{3},F_{4},G_{0},\dots,G_{8}),

where
the
F_{i}
are the cubic polynomials

\displaystyle F_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d_{1}\sum_{j=0}^{8}X_{j}^{3}-3d_{0}(X_{0}X_{1}X_{2}+X_{3}X_{4}X_{5}+X_{6}X_{7}X_{8}),

\displaystyle F_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d_{2}\sum_{j=0}^{8}X_{j}^{3}-3d_{0}(X_{0}X_{3}X_{6}+X_{1}X_{4}X_{7}+X_{2}X_{5}X_{8}),

\displaystyle F_{3}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d_{3}\sum_{j=0}^{8}X_{j}^{3}-3d_{0}(X_{0}X_{4}X_{8}+X_{1}X_{5}X_{6}+X_{2}X_{3}X_{7}),

\displaystyle F_{4}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d_{4}\sum_{j=0}^{8}X_{j}^{3}-3d_{0}(X_{0}X_{5}X_{7}+X_{1}X_{3}X_{8}+X_{2}X_{4}X_{6}),

and the
G_{i}
are the quadratic polynomials

\displaystyle G_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle h_{0}x_{0}^{2}+h_{1}x_{1}x_{2}+h_{2}x_{3}x_{6}+h_{4}x_{5}x_{7}+h_{3}x_{4}x_{8},

\displaystyle G_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle h_{0}x_{1}^{2}+h_{1}x_{0}x_{2}+h_{2}x_{4}x_{7}+h_{3}x_{5}x_{6}+h_{4}x_{3}x_{8},

\displaystyle G_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle h_{0}x_{2}^{2}+h_{1}x_{0}x_{1}+h_{2}x_{5}x_{8}+h_{3}x_{3}x_{7}+h_{4}x_{4}x_{6},

\displaystyle G_{3}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle h_{0}x_{3}^{2}+h_{1}x_{4}x_{5}+h_{2}x_{0}x_{6}+h_{3}x_{2}x_{7}+h_{4}x_{1}x_{8},

\displaystyle G_{4}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle h_{0}x_{4}^{2}+h_{1}x_{3}x_{5}+h_{2}x_{1}x_{7}+h_{3}x_{0}x_{8}+h_{4}x_{2}x_{6},

3.3.
Symplectic transformations

The coefficient vectors
(d,h)
of a p.p. abelian surface in Hesse form
\mathcal{A}_{d,h}
are not isomorphism invariants. Coefficient vectors defining isomorphic p.p. abelian surfaces are related by symplectic transformations of the underlying theta structure. These correspond to automorphisms of the Burkhardt quartic.
Here, we explain how the automorphisms of the Burkhardt quartic act on
\mathcal{A}_{d,h}
.

Notation 3.9
.

For
\lambda=(\lambda_{0}:\dots:\lambda_{4})\in\mathbb{P}^{4}
, we denote by
\star
the coordinate-wise scaling

\lambda\star x=(\lambda_{0}x_{0}:\dots:\lambda_{4}x_{4})\quad\text{for }x\in\mathbb{P}^{4},

and when all
\lambda_{i}\neq 0
, we write

\lambda^{-1}=(\lambda_{0}^{-1}:\dots:\lambda_{4}^{-1})

for the coordinate-wise inverse.

We further extend the scaling map to
x\in\mathcal{A}_{d,h}\subset\mathbb{P}^{8}
:

C_{\lambda}(x)=(\lambda_{0}x_{0}:\lambda_{1}x_{1}:\lambda_{1}x_{2}:\lambda_{2}x_{3}:\lambda_{3}x_{4}:\lambda_{4}x_{5}:\lambda_{2}x_{6}:\lambda_{4}x_{7}:\lambda_{3}x_{8}).

The scaling is symmetric in the sense that it commutes with multiplication by
[-1]
.

Note that the image
C_{\lambda}(\mathcal{A}_{d,h})
is not necessarily in Hesse form. In the next proposition, we provide values
\lambda\in\mathbb{P}^{4}
for which this is the case.

Proposition 3.10
.

Let
\mathcal{A}_{d,h}\subset\mathbb{P}^{8}
be a p.p. abelian surface in Hesse form, and consider the scaling vectors

a_{0}=(1:\omega:1:\omega:\omega),\quad a_{1}=(1:1:1:\omega^{2}:\omega),\quad a_{2}=(1:1:\omega:\omega:\omega).

Then for any
\lambda\in\{t_{0},t_{1},t_{2}\}
, the image
C_{\lambda}(\mathcal{A}_{d,h})
is again in Hesse form, more precisely

C_{\lambda}(\mathcal{A}_{d,h})=\mathcal{A}_{\lambda^{-1}\star d,\lambda\star h}.

Proof.

This can be verified by an explicit computation, using the description of
\mathcal{A}_{d,h}
provided in Theorem
3.1
.
∎

We note that the transformations described in Proposition
3.10
are (necessarily) automorphisms of the Burkhardt quartic. The action of
C_{\lambda_{i}}
on
h
corresponds to the element
S_{i}
from Eq.
4
and as discussed in Subsection
2.2
, this extends to an action on the dual, i.e. the action on
d
.

The automorphism described by the matrix
M
(cf. Eq.
4
) can be interpreted as a discrete Fourier transform on
\mathcal{A}_{d,h}
.

Definition 3.11
.

Let

\displaystyle M_{3,3}=\begin{pmatrix}M_{3}&M_{3}&M_{3}\\
M_{3}&\omega M_{3}&\omega^{2}M_{3}\\
M_{3}&\omega^{2}M_{3}&\omega M_{3}\end{pmatrix},\quad\text{with }M_{3}=\begin{pmatrix}1&1&1\\

is defined as the left action of

on the coordinate vector of a point

M_{3,3}:(x_{0}:\dots:x_{8})\mapsto{M_{3,3}}\cdot(x_{0}:\dots:x_{8}).

.

Let
\mathcal{A}_{d,h}
be a p.p. abelian surface in Hesse form. Then the discrete Fourier transform defines an isomorphism

with

This can be verified by an explicit computation, using the description of

provided in Theorem

. ∎

Similarly as in Proposition

(resp.

from Eq.

The quadratic relations are induced by the Segre embedding. More precisely, the image of

These relations yield the

with coefficient vector

The cubic relations are induced by the equations for

\mathcal{H}_{(d_{0}^{\prime}:d_{1}^{\prime})}

\displaystyle x_{0}^{3}\quad+\quad\dots\quad+\quad x_{8}^{3}\quad=

\displaystyle~(x^{3}+y^{3}+z^{3})\cdot(x^{\prime 3}+y^{\prime 3}+z^{\prime 3}),

\displaystyle x_{0}x_{1}x_{2}+x_{3}x_{4}x_{5}+x_{6}x_{7}x_{8}=

\displaystyle x_{0}x_{3}x_{6}+x_{1}x_{4}x_{7}+x_{2}x_{5}x_{8}=

\displaystyle~(x^{\prime 3}+y^{\prime 3}+z^{\prime 3})\cdot(xyz),

\displaystyle x_{0}x_{4}x_{8}+x_{1}x_{5}x_{6}+x_{2}x_{3}x_{7}=

\displaystyle~3\cdot(xyz)\cdot(x^{\prime}y^{\prime}z^{\prime}),

\displaystyle x_{0}x_{5}x_{7}+x_{1}x_{3}x_{8}+x_{2}x_{4}x_{6}=

\displaystyle~3\cdot(xyz)\cdot(x^{\prime}y^{\prime}z^{\prime}).

reduces to the defining equations of the elliptic curves.

Finally, the neutral element is computed as
\iota_{S}\left((0:-1:1),(0:-1:1)\right)

If a reducible abelian surface comes from the Segre embedding of two elliptic curves in Hesse form, then we say that it is in product form. More precisely:

Definition 3.14

h=(0:0:0:-1:1)

Note that in general, for a reducible abelian surface, the quadratic equations in Theorem

Let

Note that Corollary

3

3

5.

This section is a brief interlude describing twists of abelian surfaces in Hesse form. These will appear in the proof of our main theorem in Section

. While we do not explore the construction in full detail, we remark that this can be viewed as the two-dimensional analogue of elliptic curves in

introduced in
[

]

Definition 5.1

the

by

Note that twisting a surface in Hesse form does not affect the action of the

-torsion subgroup

Lemma 5.2
.

Let

be a p.p. abelian surface which is the twist of some p.p. abelian surface

in Hesse form, i.e. there exists

contains a primitive root of unity

-torsion points

, and their action on points in

is given by

with

as in Eq.

.
Moreover multiplication by

is given by

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces~P=(x_{0}:x_{1}:x_{2}:x_{3}:x_{4}:x_{5}:x_{6}:x_{7}:x_{8})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces-P=(x_{0}:x_{2}:x_{1}:x_{6}:x_{8}:x_{7}:x_{3}:x_{5}:x_{4})}

.

Consider the canonical basis

of

. The map

is given by scaling the coordinates of a point. The same is true for the action of the

and

The translation of the multiplication by

∎

-rational element

Our main result states that a

-torsion points lying above the kernel.

Let
\mathcal{A}_{d,h}
be a p.p. abelian surface in Hesse form, and denote

(3,3)
-isogeny with kernel

Proof.

Throughout the proof, we use the following notation for the intermediate abelian surfaces and maps appearing in the computation of

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{A}_{d,h}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A_{1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A_{2}}

K_{2}^{\prime}=\langle Q_{1}^{\prime},Q_{2}^{\prime}\rangle
, where
K_{1}^{\prime}+K_{2}^{\prime}=\mathcal{A}_{d^{\prime},h^{\prime}}[3]
is the canonical decomposition of the
3
-torsion on the codomain. This means that the formulas from Theorem
6.4
could also be applied to compute the dual isogeny. The only subtlety is the computation of the scaling vector. A direct application of the theorem would require fixing additional
9
-torsion points
S_{1},S_{2}
lying above
P_{1},P_{2}
as well. In the following we show that this is not necessary.

Lemma 6.5
.

Let
\phi:\mathcal{A}_{d,h}\to\mathcal{A}_{d^{\prime},h^{\prime}}
be the
(3,3)
-isogeny from Theorem
6.4
. Then the dual isogeny
\hat{\phi}:\mathcal{A}_{d^{\prime},h^{\prime}}\to\mathcal{A}_{d,h}
is given by

\hat{\phi}:P\mapsto C_{\lambda^{\prime}}\circ M_{3,3}\circ\odot^{3}(P)

with
\lambda^{\prime}=(t_{0}/a_{0}:t_{1}/a_{1}:t_{2}/a_{2}:t_{3}/a_{3}:t_{4}/a_{4})
, where

(a_{0}:a_{1}:a_{1}:a_{2}:a_{3}:a_{4}:a_{2}:a_{4}:a_{3})=M_{3,3}\circ\odot^{3}\circ C_{\lambda}\circ M_{3,3}\circ\odot^{3}(0_{\mathcal{A}_{d,h}}).

Proof.

First note that
\phi(P_{1},P_{2})=\langle Q_{1}^{\prime},Q_{2}^{\prime}\rangle
, hence this is the kernel of the dual isogeny
\hat{\phi}
. It follows from Theorem
6.4
that there exists a vector
\lambda^{\prime}\in\mathbb{P}^{4}
so that
\hat{\phi}
is given by
\hat{\phi}:P\mapsto C_{\lambda^{\prime}}\circ M_{3,3}\circ\odot^{3}(P)
. To compute
\lambda^{\prime}
, it suffices to evaluate the multiplication by
3
map at the neutral element
0_{\mathcal{A}_{d,h}}
. This provides us with the condition

0_{\mathcal{A}_{d,h}}=C_{\lambda^{\prime}}\circ M_{3,3}\circ\odot^{3}\circ C_{\lambda}\circ M_{3,3}\circ\odot^{3}(0_{\mathcal{A}_{d,h}})

from which we deduce the coordinates of
\lambda^{\prime}
.
∎

6.2.
Isogenies with arbitrary kernel

In Theorem
6.4
, we provide an explicit formula to compute
(3,3)
-isogenies with kernel given by
K_{2}=\langle Q_{1},Q_{2}\rangle
, where
(P_{1},P_{2},Q_{1},Q_{2})
is the canonical
3
-torsion basis. This formula can be used to compute isogenies with arbitrary kernel by precomposing it with a suitable symplectic transformation (see Subsection
3.3
). Here, we make this precise for kernels of the form
\langle P_{1}+aQ_{1}+bQ_{2},P_{2}+bQ_{1}+cQ_{2}\rangle
which appear naturally in our application.

Lemma 6.6
.

Let
\mathcal{A}_{d,h}
be a p.p. abelian surface in Hesse form, and
(P_{1},P_{2},Q_{1},Q_{2})
the canonical basis of
\mathcal{A}_{d,h}[3]
. Consider the maximal isotropic subgroup
K=\langle P_{1}+aQ_{1}+bQ_{2},P_{2}+bQ_{1}+cQ_{2}\rangle\subset\mathcal{A}_{d,h}[3]
. Then the transformation

C_{\lambda(a,b,c)}:\mathcal{A}_{d,h}\to\mathcal{A}_{d^{\prime},h^{\prime}},

with

\lambda=(1:\omega^{2a}:\omega^{2c}:\omega^{2a+b+2c}:\omega^{2a+2b+2c})

is a symplectic transformation, and it holds that

C_{\lambda}(K)=K_{1}^{\prime},

where
K_{1}^{\prime}+K_{2}^{\prime}=\mathcal{A}_{d^{\prime},h^{\prime}}[3]
is the canonical decomposition.

Proof.

First note that
C_{\lambda}=C_{a_{0}}^{-a}\circ C_{a_{1}}^{-b}\circ C_{a_{2}}^{-c}
with
a_{0},a_{1},a_{2}
as in Proposition
3.10
, hence
C_{\lambda}
defines a symplectic transformation. Furthermore, it follows from the same proposition that the transformation
C_{\lambda}
is represented by the matrix

\begin{pmatrix}1&0&-a&-b\\
0&1&-b&-c\\
0&0&1&0\\
0&0&0&1\end{pmatrix}

with respect to the canonical bases of
\mathcal{A}_{d,h}
and
\mathcal{A}_{d^{\prime},h^{\prime}}
, respectively.
∎

Note that the theorem requires as input a kernel of the form
K_{2}
, whereas the transformation in Lemma
6.6
constructs a kernel of the form
K_{1}
. This can be achieved by simply applying a discrete Fourier transform and multiplying by
[-1]
(cf. Lemma
4.4
). The overall procedure is summarized in the next corollary.

Corollary 6.7
.

Let
\mathcal{A}_{d,h}
be a p.p. abelian surface in Hesse form, and
(P_{1},P_{2},Q_{1},Q_{2})
the canonical basis of
\mathcal{A}_{d,h}[3]
. Given a maximal isotropic subgroup
K=\langle P_{1}+aQ_{1}+bQ_{2},P_{2}+bQ_{1}+cQ_{2}\rangle\subset\mathcal{A}_{d,h}[3]
, and
9
-torsion points
R_{1},R_{2}
satisfying
3\cdot R_{1}=P_{1}+aQ_{1}+bQ_{2}
and
3\cdot R_{2}=P_{2}+bQ_{1}+cQ_{2}
. Further let
C_{\lambda}
be as in Lemma
6.6
. Then the
(3,3)
-isogeny with kernel
K
can be computed using the formulas from Theorem
6.4
with input
S_{K}(\mathcal{A}_{d,h})
and
S_{K}(R_{1}),S_{K}(R_{2})
, where
S_{K}=[-1]\circ M_{3,3}\circ C_{\lambda}
.

6.3.
Non-generic isogenies

In Theorem
6.4
, it is assumed that the domain of the isogeny
\mathcal{A}_{d,h}
is not exceptional (cf. Definition
6.3
). This property was essential to derive the concrete scalars for the map
C_{\lambda}
.

Here, we provide an alternative method to derive the scalars for the map
C_{\lambda}
which also works in the exceptional case unless the codomain admits extra automorphisms of order
3
. Apart from the
9
-torsion points
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
, these formulas further require the coordinates of
R_{1}+R_{2}
and
R_{1}-R_{2}
as input.

Lemma 6.8
.

Let
\mathcal{A}_{d,h}
be an exceptional p.p. abelian surface. Let
\tilde{t}=(\tilde{t}_{0}:\dots:\tilde{t}_{4})
as in Definition
6.3
. Then there exist at least two indices
i\in\{0,\dots,4\}
with
\tilde{t}_{i}\neq 0
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{A}_{d,h}\to\mathcal{A}_{d^{\prime},h^{\prime}}
be an isogeny as in Lemma
6.2
, and denote by
t^{\prime}=(t_{0}:\dots:t_{4}^{\prime})
, the coordinates defining the neutral element of the codomain. Then
t_{i}^{\prime}=\lambda_{i}\cdot\tilde{t}_{i}
for each
i\in\{0,\dots,4\}
, where
\lambda=(\lambda_{0}:\dots:\lambda_{4})
is the scaling vector defining
C_{\lambda}
. The statement now follows from Corollary
3.7
which states that there are at least two indices
i\neq j\in\{0,\dots,4\}
so that
t_{i}^{\prime},t_{j}^{\prime}
are nonzero.
∎

Proposition 6.9
.

Let
\mathcal{A}_{d,h}
be a p.p. abelian surface in Hesse form. Denote
(P_{1},P_{2},Q_{1},Q_{2})
for the canonical
3
-torsion basis, and let

\langle R_{1},R_{2}\rangle\subset\mathcal{A}_{d,h}[9]
be a maximal isotropic subgroup with
3\cdot R_{1}=Q_{1}
,
3\cdot R_{2}=Q_{2}
and
R_{3}=R_{1}+R_{2}
,
R_{4}=R_{1}-R_{2}
.

Further, let
\tilde{t}=(\tilde{t}_{0}:\dots:\tilde{t}_{4})
as in Definition
6.3
. If there are two indices
i\neq j>0
with
\tilde{t}_{i},\tilde{t}_{j}\neq 0
, then there exists a unique
\lambda\in\mathbb{P}^{4}
so that

\phi:\mathcal{A}_{d,h}\to\mathcal{A}_{d^{\prime},h^{\prime}},\quad P\mapsto C_{\lambda}\circ M\circ\odot^{3}(P)

defines a
(3,3)
-isogeny with kernel
K_{2}=\langle Q_{1},Q_{2}\rangle
, and the canonical symplectic basis for
\mathcal{A}_{d^{\prime},h^{\prime}}[3]
is given by
(P_{1}^{\prime},P_{2}^{\prime},Q_{1}^{\prime},Q_{2}^{\prime})
, where
P_{1}^{\prime}=\phi(R_{1}),\,P_{2}^{\prime}=\phi(R_{2})
and
Q_{1}^{\prime}=[-1]\phi(P_{1}),\,Q_{2}^{\prime}=[-1]\phi(P_{2})
.

Moreover, the coordinates of
\lambda
can be read off from the
9
-torsion points
R_{1},R_{2},R_{3},R_{4}
.

Proof.

We use the same notation as in the proof of Theorem
6.4
. That is we decompose the isogeny
A\xrightarrow{\phi_{2}}A_{2}\xrightarrow{C_{\lambda}}A_{d^{\prime},h^{\prime}}
for some
\lambda\in\mathbb{P}^{4}
. Let
(P_{1}^{\prime},P_{2}^{\prime},Q_{1}^{\prime},Q_{2}^{\prime})
be the canonical symplectic basis of
\mathcal{A}_{d^{\prime},h^{\prime}}
. With the same arguments as in the proof of the theorem, we may assume that
\phi(R_{1})=P_{1}^{\prime}
and
\phi(R_{2})=P_{2}^{\prime}
, hence
\phi(R_{3})=P_{1}^{\prime}+P_{2}^{\prime}=:P_{3}^{\prime}
and
\phi(R_{4})=P_{1}^{\prime}-P_{2}^{\prime}=:P_{4}^{\prime}
.

We denote

\phi_{2}(R_{i})=(x_{i,0}:\dots:x_{i,8})\quad\text{for }i=1,2,3,4.

The condition
C_{\lambda}(\phi_{2}(R_{i}))=P_{i}^{\prime}
for
i=1,2,3,4
provides us with four equalities.

\displaystyle\left(\lambda_{0}x_{1,0}:\lambda_{1}x_{1,1}:\dots:\lambda_{3}x_{1,8}\right)=

\displaystyle~(t_{1}^{\prime}:t_{1}^{\prime}:t_{0}^{\prime}:t_{3}^{\prime}:t_{4}^{\prime}:t_{2}^{\prime}:t_{4}^{\prime}:t_{3}^{\prime}:t_{2}^{\prime}),

\displaystyle\left(\lambda_{0}x_{2,0}:\lambda_{1}x_{2,1}:\dots:\lambda_{3}x_{2,8}\right)=

\displaystyle~(t_{2}^{\prime}:t_{3}^{\prime}:t_{4}^{\prime}:t_{2}^{\prime}:t_{4}^{\prime}:t_{3}^{\prime}:t_{0}^{\prime}:t_{1}^{\prime}:t_{1}^{\prime}),

\displaystyle\left(\lambda_{0}x_{3,0}:\lambda_{1}x_{3,1}:\dots:\lambda_{3}x_{3,8}\right)=

\displaystyle~(t_{3}^{\prime}:t_{4}^{\prime}:t_{2}^{\prime}:t_{4}^{\prime}:t_{3}^{\prime}:t_{2}^{\prime}:t_{1}^{\prime}:t_{1}^{\prime}:t_{0}^{\prime}),

\displaystyle\left(\lambda_{0}x_{4,0}:\lambda_{1}x_{4,1}:\dots:\lambda_{3}x_{4,8}\right)=

\displaystyle~(t_{4}^{\prime}:t_{3}^{\prime}:t_{2}^{\prime}:t_{1}^{\prime}:t_{1}^{\prime}:t_{0}^{\prime}:t_{3}^{\prime}:t_{4}^{\prime}:t_{2}^{\prime}).

For each
i
, the condition
\tilde{t_{i}}\neq 0
is equivalent to
t_{i}^{\prime}\neq 0
(cf. the proof of Lemma
6.8
). Now if
t_{i}^{\prime}\neq 0
for some
i>0
, then we can deduce one relation on the coordinates of
\lambda
from each of the above equalities.

Explicitly, if
t_{1}^{\prime}\neq 0
, then

(\lambda_{0}:\lambda_{1})=(x_{1,1}:x_{1,0}),~~(\lambda_{4}:\lambda_{3})=(x_{2,8}:x_{2,7}),~~(\lambda_{2}:\lambda_{4})=(x_{3,7}:x_{3,6}),~~(\lambda_{2}:\lambda_{3})=(x_{4,4}:x_{4,3}).

If
t_{2}^{\prime}\neq 0
, then

(\lambda_{4}:\lambda_{3})=(x_{1,8}:x_{1,5}),~~(\lambda_{0}:\lambda_{2})=(x_{2,3}:x_{2,0}),~~(\lambda_{1}:\lambda_{4})=(x_{3,5}:x_{3,2}),~~(\lambda_{1}:\lambda_{3})=(x_{4,8}:x_{4,2}).

If
t_{3}^{\prime}\neq 0
, then

(\lambda_{2}:\lambda_{4})=(x_{1,7}:x_{1,3}),~~(\lambda_{1}:\lambda_{4})=(x_{2,5}:x_{2,1}),~~(\lambda_{0}:\lambda_{3})=(x_{3,4}:x_{3,0}),~~(\lambda_{1}:\lambda_{2})=(x_{4,6}:x_{4,1}).

If
t_{4}^{\prime}\neq 0
, then

(\lambda_{3}:\lambda_{2})=(x_{1,6}:x_{1,4}),~~(\lambda_{1}:\lambda_{3})=(x_{2,4}:x_{2,2}),~~(\lambda_{1}:\lambda_{2})=(x_{3,3}:x_{3,1}),~~(\lambda_{0}:\lambda_{4})=(x_{4,7}:x_{4,0}).

Note that in all of the above cases, there is redundancy in the derived relations. In particular, using only one set of relations obtained for some
t_{i}^{\prime}\neq 0
is not enough to recover the coordinate vector
\lambda\in\mathbb{P}^{4}
. However, two sets of relations combined for some
t_{i}^{\prime},t_{j}^{\prime}\neq 0
with
i\neq j>0
is enough to recover
\lambda
.
∎

Remark 6.10
.

The only case that is missing in Proposition
6.9
is the case when
\tilde{t}
has exactly two non-zero indices, one of which is
i=0
. Indeed, in this case the statement of the proposition does not hold. The codomain admits a special automorphism of order
3
, and as a result the scaling
C_{\lambda}
is not uniquely defined by the images of the
9
-torsion points.

To see this, let
0_{\mathcal{A}_{d^{\prime},h^{\prime}}}=(t_{0}^{\prime}:\dots:t_{3}^{\prime})
be the neutral element of the codomain. For simplicity assume
t_{0}^{\prime},t_{1}^{\prime}\neq 0
and
t_{2}^{\prime}=t_{3}^{\prime}=t_{4}^{\prime}=0
. The extra automorphism is similar for the remaining cases.

We note that
t_{0}^{\prime 3}\neq-2t_{1}^{\prime 3},t_{1}^{\prime 3}
, otherwise one can check that
\mathcal{A}_{d^{\prime},h^{\prime}}
has a singularity at
0_{\mathcal{A}_{d^{\prime},h^{\prime}}}
. The conditions on
t_{i}^{\prime}
then imply

d^{\prime}=(t_{0}^{\prime 3}+2t_{1}^{\prime 3}:3t_{0}^{\prime}t_{1}^{\prime 2}:0:0:0),\quad h^{\prime}=(0:0:h_{2}^{\prime}:h_{3}^{\prime}:h_{4}^{\prime})

for some
h_{2}^{\prime},h_{3}^{\prime},h_{4}^{\prime}
. Now consider the map
C_{\lambda}
with
\lambda=(1:1:\omega:\omega:\omega)
(cf. Proposition
3.10
). This defines an automorphism of order
3
of
\mathcal{A}_{d,h}
.

7.
Example

To illustrate the main theorem and its applications, we provide a detailed example. In this example, we compute a
(9,9)
-isogeny between a product of elliptic curves. The isogeny computation is decomposed into a
(3,3)
-gluing and a
(3,3)
-splitting. The example illustrates the most important subtleties that can occur in the computation of
(3,3)
-isogenies. The example does not contain a generic
(3,3)
-isogeny computation, but we assure the reader that this is the most straightforward case. More details, and further examples can be found in our GitHub repository
[
23
]
.

7.1.
Setting

We work over the finite field
\mathbb{F}_{p^{2}}
with
p=269
. This field contains a primitive third root of unity
\omega\in\mathbb{F}_{p^{2}}
. Let
E_{1}:y^{2}=x^{3}+1
and
E_{2}:y^{2}=x^{3}+66x+134
over
\mathbb{F}_{p^{2}}\setminus\mathbb{F}_{p}
. The two curves are connected by a
5
-isogeny
\phi:E_{1}\to E_{2}
with kernel
\ker(\phi)=\langle(8,128)\rangle
. And in particular, they fit into the following commutative diagram:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces E_{1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces E_{2}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces E_{1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces E_{2}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\phi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces[2]}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces[2]}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\phi}

By Kani’s lemma
[
22
]
, this diagram induces a
(9,9)
-isogeny
\Phi:E_{1}\times E_{2}\to E_{1}\times E_{2}
with kernel

\ker(\Phi)=\{([2]P,\phi(P))\mid P\in E_{1}[9]\}.

This is the isogeny we wish to compute. Recall that our algorithm for the computation of
(3,3)
-isogenies requires certain
9
-torsion points as auxiliary input. Hence, we first fix a basis

E_{1}[27]=\left\langle P,Q\right\rangle\quad\text{with }P=(252,71),\,Q=(7\omega,13\omega+141),

and fix the following notation

P_{1}=[2]\cdot P,\;Q_{1}=[2]\cdot Q\in E_{1}[27],\quad P_{2}=\phi(Q),\;Q_{2}=\phi(P)\in E_{2}[27].

In particular, in this notation,
\ker(\Phi)=[3]\cdot\langle(P_{1},Q_{2}),(Q_{1},P_{2})\rangle
.

7.2.
Transformation to Hesse form

To apply our algorithm, it is necessary to translate the input to elliptic curves in Hesse form. This transformation is linear, and it is determined by specifying the image of the
3
-torsion elements. Concretely, the transformations are given by

f_{1}:E_{1}\xrightarrow{\begin{pmatrix}1&0&231\omega\\
1&19\omega&19\omega\\
1&250\omega&19\omega\end{pmatrix}}\mathcal{H}_{(-2:1)},\quad f_{2}:E_{2}\xrightarrow{\begin{pmatrix}1&0&179\\
-9\omega^{2}&9-9\omega&49\omega^{2}\\
-9\omega^{2}&-9-9\omega&49\omega^{2}\end{pmatrix}}\mathcal{H}_{(-18\omega^{2}:1)}.

These transformations are chosen so that

\displaystyle f_{1}([9]P_{1})=(-1:1:0),\;f_{1}([9]Q_{1})=(0:-\omega^{2}:1),

\displaystyle f_{2}([9]P_{2})=(-1:1:0),\;f_{2}([9]Q_{2})=(0:-\omega^{2}:1).

In our implementation in SageMath, an elliptic curve in Hesse form is constructed by calling

H1 = EllipticCurveHessianForm(E1, basis=[9*P1,9*Q1])
,
where
E1
is the elliptic curve in Weierstraß form, and
P1
,
Q1
are the points
P_{1},Q_{1}
defined above. The matrix describing the corresponding transformation can be accessed as
H1._trafo
. Similarly, the second elliptic curve in Hesse form is constructed by
H2 = EllipticCurveHessianForm(E2, basis=[9*P2,9*Q2])
.

As described in Subsection
3.4
, the image of the Segre embedding of two elliptic curves in Hesse form provides us with an abelian surface in Hesse form. Applying Lemma
3.13
we find

\iota_{S}\left(\mathcal{H}_{(18\omega^{2}:1)}\times\mathcal{H}_{(-2:1)}\right)=\mathcal{A}_{d,h},\quad\text{with }d=(-36\omega^{2}:18\omega^{2}:-2:1:1),\;h=(0:0:0:-1:1).

In the implementation, the surface
\mathcal{A}_{d,h}
is constructed as
A0 = AbelianSurfaceHessianForm([H2,H1])
.

7.3.
General strategy

With the above information as input, the isogeny
\Phi
can now be computed using the function
compute_isogeny_chain
. The intermediate computations performed by this function can be summarized as follows:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{H}_{(18\omega^{2}:1)}\times\mathcal{H}_{(-2:1)}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{A}_{d,h}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{A}_{d^{\prime},h^{\prime}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{A}_{d^{\prime\prime},h^{\prime\prime}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{A}_{\tilde{d},\tilde{h}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{A}_{\tilde{d}^{\prime},\tilde{h}^{\prime}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{A}_{\tilde{d}^{\prime\prime},\tilde{h}^{\prime\prime}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{H}_{(-2:1)}\times\mathcal{H}_{(18\omega^{2}:1)}.}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\iota_{S}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces S_{K}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces S_{K}{{}^{\prime}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces S_{K^{\prime\prime}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Phi_{1}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Phi_{2}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\iota_{S}^{-1}}

The maps
S_{K},S_{K}^{\prime},S_{K}^{\prime\prime}
are symplectic transformations and
\Phi_{1},\Phi_{2}
are
(3,3)
-isogenies computed using the formulas from Theorem
6.4
. Below, we provide more details on the computations. We note that for a general isogeny chain computation in the framework, all intermediate symplectic transformations are of the form
[-1]\circ M_{3,3}
(this is the case for
S_{K^{\prime}}
here), whereas in the first and last step, the symplectic transformation depends on the setup (this is the case for
S_{K}
and
S_{K^{\prime\prime}}
here).

7.4.
Gluing isogeny

As per Corollary
4.3
, the canonical basis of
\mathcal{A}_{d,h}[3]
is given by

\left(P_{1}^{0},P_{2}^{0},Q_{1}^{0},Q_{2}^{0}\right)=\left([9]\iota_{S}(0,P_{1}),\;[9]\iota_{S}(P_{2},0),\;[9]\iota_{S}(0,Q_{1}),\;[9]\iota_{S}(Q_{2},0)\right),

and
\ker(\Phi)[3]=\langle Q_{1}^{0}+P_{2}^{0},Q_{2}^{0}+P_{1}^{0}\rangle
. In order to apply Theorem
6.4
, we need to apply a symplectic transformation that transforms
K:=\ker(\Phi)[3]
to a group of the form
K_{2}
. As described in Corollary
6.7
, this is achieved by the transformation

S_{K}:=[-1]\circ M_{3,3}\circ\mathcal{C}_{\lambda},\quad\text{with }\lambda=(1:1:1:\omega:\omega^{2}).

Note that the value for
\lambda
is deduced from Lemma
6.6
. Explicitly, the coefficients defining the codomain surface of
S_{K}:\mathcal{A}_{d,h}\to\mathcal{A}_{\tilde{d},\tilde{h}}
are given by

\tilde{d}=(263:54\omega+51:3:57\omega+57:51\omega+54),\;\tilde{h}=(-\omega:\omega:\omega:\omega^{2}:1).

With the notation of Eq.
6
, the neutral element is defined by the vector
t=(-2\omega:\omega:\omega:\omega^{2}:1)
, and one can check that
\mathcal{A}_{\tilde{d},\tilde{h}}
is not exceptional.
Now we can apply the main theorem to compute the
(3,3)
-isogeny

\Phi_{1}:\mathcal{A}_{\tilde{d},\tilde{h}}\to\mathcal{A}_{d^{\prime},h^{\prime}},\quad P\mapsto C_{\lambda^{\prime}}\circ M\circ\odot^{3}(P)

with

\lambda^{\prime}=(88:170:181\omega+5:88\omega+93:1),

and

d^{\prime}=(107:144:169\omega+200:100\omega+31:1),\quad h^{\prime}=(104:76:203\omega+167:66\omega+233:1).

One can check that
h^{\prime}
is a
nonsingular
point on the Burkhardt quartic. This means that
\mathcal{A}_{d^{\prime},h^{\prime}}
is irreducible, and the
(3,3)
-isogeny
\Phi_{1}
is indeed a gluing.

7.5.
Splitting isogeny

By Theorem
6.4
, we have that
K^{\prime}:=\Phi_{1}(S_{K}(\ker(\Phi))[3]=K_{1}^{\prime}
, where
\mathcal{A}_{d^{\prime},h^{\prime}}=K_{1}^{\prime}+K_{2}^{\prime}
is the symplectic decomposition of the
3
-torsion. This is transformed into a kernel of the form
K_{2}
by applying a discrete Fourier transform (Lemma
4.4
). Moreover, a multiplication by
[-1]
is necessary to transform the generators into the correct form. Denote
S_{K^{\prime}}=[-1]\circ M_{3,3}
and
\mathcal{A}_{\tilde{d}^{\prime},\tilde{h}^{\prime}}
for the codomain of
S_{K^{\prime}}
. Then, one may again apply Theorem
6.4
to compute the splitting isogeny as

\Phi_{2}:\mathcal{A}_{\tilde{d}^{\prime},\tilde{h}^{\prime}}\to\mathcal{A}_{d^{\prime\prime},h^{\prime\prime}},\quad P\mapsto C_{\lambda^{\prime}}\circ M\circ\odot^{3}(P),

where

\lambda^{\prime\prime}=(144\omega:250\omega:126\omega+260:143\omega+134:1)

and

d^{\prime\prime}=(99\omega+99:85\omega+85:100\omega+184:169\omega+84:1),\quad h^{\prime\prime}=(-\omega:\omega:\omega:\omega^{2}:1).

As expected,
h^{\prime\prime}
is a singular point of the Burkhardt quartic, hence
\Phi_{2}
is indeed a
(3,3)
-splitting. In order to recover the two elliptic curves, one needs to apply another symplectic transformation to obtain a model equipped with the product polarization (Definition
3.14
). In our implementation, this transformation is computed using
product_structure_transformation
. This yields

S_{K^{\prime\prime}}:\mathcal{A}_{d^{\prime\prime},h^{\prime\prime}}\to\mathcal{A}_{\tilde{d}^{\prime\prime},\tilde{h}^{\prime\prime}},\quad P\mapsto C_{(1:1:1:\omega:\omega^{2})}\circ M_{3,3}\circ C_{(1:1:1:\omega:\omega^{2})}(P)

with

\tilde{d}^{\prime\prime}=(-36\omega^{2}:-2:18\omega^{2}:1:1),\quad\tilde{h}^{\prime\prime}=(0:0:0:-1:1).

And in particular,

\mathcal{A}_{\tilde{d}^{\prime\prime},\tilde{h}^{\prime\prime}}=\iota_{S}\left(\mathcal{H}_{(-2:1)}\times\mathcal{H}_{(18\omega^{2}:1)}\right).

Note that the codomain of the isogeny
\Phi
was already clear from the construction in this example.

8.
Generalization of the approach

The main ingredient for our new isogeny formula is Lemma
6.2
which states that for some appropriate scaling vector
\lambda
, the map

\phi:\mathcal{A}_{d,h}\to\mathcal{A}_{d^{\prime},h^{\prime}},\quad P\mapsto C_{\lambda}\circ M\circ\odot^{3}(P)

defines a
(3,3)
-isogeny with kernel
K_{2}
. It is only natural to ask, whether this approach can be generalized to higher dimensional abelian varieties defined by a symmetric theta structure of level
3
, and whether it further generalizes to different levels.

8.1.
Going up in dimension

To answer the first question, let
A
be a p.p. abelian variety of dimension
g
. Consider a line bundle
L
which determines the principal polarization of
A
. Then
L^{\otimes 3}
is very ample by Lefschetz’s Theorem; it defines an embedding
A\hookrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{3^{g}-1}
and one may choose an embedding so that the action of the three-torsion group is normalized. In analogy with our definition in dimension
2
, we say that
A
is in
Hesse form
. Such an embedding is induced by a symmetric theta structure and the coordinate functions correspond to level-
3
theta functions. In the following, we explain how the steps in the proof of Lemma
6.2
generalize to arbitrary dimension.

Similar as in Definition
4.2
, one can define a canonical symplectic basis

(P_{1},\dots,P_{g},Q_{1},\dots,Q_{g})

for
A[3]
so that the action of the points
P_{i}
is given by permutations of order
3
, and the action of
Q_{i}
is given by scalings with a third root of unity (cf.
[
28
]
). It follows that also in this general setting, the map defined by coordinate-wise cubing defines an isogeny
\odot^{3}:A\to A_{1}
with kernel
K_{2}=\langle Q_{1},\dots,Q_{g}\rangle
.

The discrete Fourier transform with respect to a third root of unity
\omega
may be defined inductively:

M_{3^{g}}=\begin{pmatrix}M_{3^{g-1}}&M_{3^{g-1}}&M_{3^{g-1}}\\
M_{3^{g-1}}&\omega M_{{3^{g-1}}}&\omega^{2}M_{3^{g-1}}\\
M_{3^{g-1}}&\omega^{2}M_{3^{g-1}}&\omega M_{3^{g-1}}\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{for }g>1.

Up to reordering the generators of the basis
(P_{1},\dots,P_{g},Q_{1},\dots,Q_{g})
, one can then check that
M_{3^{g}}\cdot P_{i}=-Q_{i}
and
M_{3^{g}}\cdot Q_{i}=P_{i}
(as in Lemma
4.4
). In particular, we see that on the codomain
A_{2}
of

\phi_{2}=(M_{3^{g}}\circ\odot^{3}):A\to A_{2},

the action of the points
\phi_{2}(P_{i})
is in canonical form. In the language of Section
5
, one could say that
A_{2}
is in twisted Hesse form. In particular, there exists a scaling
C_{\lambda}
with
\lambda\in\mathbb{P}^{(3^{g}-1)/2}
to transform
A_{2}
into Hesse form.

We expect that apart from certain edge cases, it will also be possible to determine the scaling vector
\lambda
from a given maximal 9-isotropic subgroup
\langle R_{1},\dots,R_{g}\rangle
above the kernel of the isogeny. However, we did not work out the general formulas in detail.

Remark 8.1
.

In the preparation of this manuscript, we further made this idea explicit in the case of elliptic curves. Isogeny formulas for elliptic curves in Hesse form are well known, and we verified that our new approach yields the same result. Interestingly, this interpretation of
3
-isogenies led to the discovery of some new tripling formulas on the Kummer line of elliptic curves in
twisted
Hesse form. We presented these results at a cryptography conference, and the article is currently in submission to the proceedings of this conference
[
12
]
.

8.2.
Different levels
\ell

We point out that our ideas could also lead to new isogeny formulas for different levels
\ell
. Indeed, as explained in the introduction, the inspiration for our work comes from the level-
2
theta setting, where particularly efficient
2
-isogeny formulas are known. In this case, the isogeny formulas follow from the classical duplication formulas of theta functions, and hold in arbitrary dimension
g
.

Remark 8.2
.

To formulate a generalization of Lemma
6.2
to arbitrary level
\ell>2
, we introduce the following notation:

–

\odot^{\ell}
: coordinate-wise exponentiation with exponent
\ell
, i.e.
\odot^{\ell}(x)=(x_{0}^{\ell}:\dots:x_{3^{g}-1}^{\ell})
.

–

M_{\ell^{g}}
: The discrete Fourier transform with respect to a primitive
\ell
-th root of unity and dimension
g
.

–

C_{\lambda}
with
\lambda\in\mathbb{P}^{{(\ell^{g}-1)}/2}
: coordinate-wise scaling, symmetric with respect to multiplication by
[-1]
.

Now let
A
be a p.p. abelian variety, and
\ell>2
a prime. Further, let

\Theta_{\ell}:A\to\mathbb{P}^{\ell^{g}-1}

be the embedding induced by a symmetric theta structure of level
\ell
.
We claim that there exists a scaling vector
\lambda\in\mathbb{P}^{{(\ell^{g}-1)}/2}
so that the map

\phi=(C_{\lambda}\circ M_{\ell^{g}}\circ\odot^{\ell}):\Theta_{\ell}(A)\to{A}^{\prime}

defines an isogeny with maximal
\ell
-isotropic kernel; and the codomain
{A}^{\prime}
is induced by a symmetric theta structure
\Theta_{\ell}^{\prime}
of level
\ell
as well. We describe the idea briefly.

First, one fixes a canonical symplectic basis
(P_{1},\dots,P_{g},Q_{1},\dots,Q_{g})
for
A[\ell]
. Similar as in the setting of abelian varieties in Hesse form, addition by a point
P_{i}
acts by certain permutations of order
\ell
; and addition by a point
Q_{i}
acts by scaling with
\ell
-th roots of unity. In particular, the map
\odot^{\ell}
defines an isogeny with kernel
K_{2}=\langle Q_{1},\dots,Q_{g}\rangle
. And as in the proof of Lemma
6.2
, there must exist a linear transformation so that the codomain is in the correct form. To describe this transformation explicitly, the key is to observe that the images of the points
P_{1},\dots,P_{g}
under
\phi_{2}:=M_{\ell^{g}}\circ\odot^{\ell}
act by scalings with
\ell
-th roots of unity on the points of the codomain of
\phi_{2}
.

Remark 8.3
.

Note that the number of coordinates to represent a point on an abelian variety grows with the level
\ell
. It would be interesting to see if the above method could still lead to more efficient isogeny formulas. Even in the case of elliptic curves, this could lead to new insights on isogenies. The next easiest case to consider is an elliptic curve defined as the intersection of two quadrics in
\mathbb{P}^{3}
. With a suitable choice of coordinates, this corresponds to a level-
4
theta structure (cf.
[
28
, §5, Case (c)]
). This model should therefore be suitable for the computation of
4
-isogenies.
