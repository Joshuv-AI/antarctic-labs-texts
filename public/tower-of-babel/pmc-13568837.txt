Hierarchical Collagen Nanofiber Gels Recapitulate a Physiologically Relevant Tumor Stromal Microenvironment
Authors: Naoko Sasaki, Tomoyuki Suezawa, Shiro Kitano, Ryohei Katayama, Michiya Matsusaki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCancer tissue forms cancer stroma with a high elastic modulus due to increased type I collagen (Col I) production from fibroblasts and cross‐linking of Col I fibers. Stiffened cancer stroma acts on cancer cells as a mechanical stress and promotes cancer progression. This highlights the need for culture scaffolds that recapitulate the structural, mechanical, and biological characteristics of the tumor stromal microenvironment. In this study, we attempted to create a scaffold material that mimics the fibrous structure and elastic modulus of in vivo cancer tissue using Col I. By gelation with collagen microfibers (CMFs) and collagen nanofibers (CNFs) at various concentrations, we were able to produce collagen gels with elastic moduli ranging from 1 to 18 kPa and 2 to 80 kPa, respectively. When cultured on CNF gels with a similar elasticity to in vivo colorectal cancer tissue, JC‐011 exhibited proliferation comparable to that on Matrigel. Furthermore, JC‐011 cells cultured on CNF gels exhibited cancer‐related protein expression patterns highly similar to those observed on Matrigel. Together, these findings demonstrate that hierarchical CNF gels recapitulate a physiologically relevant tumor stromal microenvironment and provide a promising collagen‐based platform for investigating cancer cell–matrix interactions.

Introduction

Although anticancer drugs are widely used in cancer treatment, personalized medicine—where drugs are selected according to the type and stage of cancer in each patient—is attracting increasing attention. To achieve this, it is necessary to culture patient‐derived cancer cells and perform drug sensitivity tests. However, cancer cells used in these tests are usually cultured on rigid polystyrene dishes, which may alter their properties. In fact, it has been reported that cancer cells show reduced proliferation and changes in their characteristics when cultured in two‐dimensional (2D) environments that differ from in vivo conditions in structure, composition, and mechanical properties [1]. Therefore, it is important to reproduce the tumor structure and mechanical environment found in vivo, and there is a need to develop scaffold materials that mimic the biological environment surrounding tumors.

Type I collagen (Col I) is the most abundant protein in the extracellular matrix (ECM) and plays important roles in scaffolds for cell adhesion, mechanical support of tissues and organs, and interactions with other ECM and growth factors [2]. In vivo, Col I forms stiff, aligned fibers that provide structural support for cells in stromal tissues such as the skin dermis [3]. In cancer tissues, a collagen‐rich stroma is formed due to increased collagen production by fibroblasts and enhanced cross‐linking of Col I fibers [4]. As a result, cancer tissues become mechanically stiffer, with elastic moduli ranging from tens to hundreds of kilopascals. For example, while the elastic modulus of normal mammary tissue is 800 Pa, breast cancer tissue shows an increase in modulus of 14–281 kPa with stage progression [5]. Although numerous studies have attempted to recapitulate tumor stromal tissues with a wide range of elastic moduli using collagen type I (Col I), accurately reproducing the mechanical properties of native tissues remains challenging. For example, several studies have cultured breast cancer cells in Col I gels to mimic interstitial structures; however, the reported Col I concentrations (0.1%–0.34 wt%) correspond to elastic moduli in the range of 0.17–1.2 kPa, which are several orders of magnitude lower than those of in vivo tumor tissues [6]. Consequently, various chemical modification and cross‐linking strategies have been explored to increase the stiffness of collagen matrices and better mimic the mechanical properties of tumor stroma.

Although numerous approaches have been reported—including physiological collagen crosslinking via glycation (as observed in fibrosis) [7], as well as nonphysiological chemical crosslinking methods such as glutaraldehyde [8], carbodiimide [9], genipin [10], and methacrylation (GelMA) [11], and physical modifications such as interpenetrating polymer networks (IPNs) [12, 13], and double network polymers (DNPs) [14, 15, 16]. Although collagen‐based systems have been widely used to model tumor stromal tissues, it remains challenging to recapitulate the mechanical properties of native tumor stroma using collagen alone without introducing nonphysiological modifications. Consequently, there is still a lack of physiologically relevant collagen‐based scaffold platforms that can reproduce the wide range of mechanical properties of tumor stromal tissues while preserving the native biochemical composition of the extracellular matrix. Addressing this unresolved limitation constitutes the central focus of the present study.

To overcome this limitation, we investigate hierarchical collagen structures (Figure 1a). We have previously reported a collagen microfiber (CMF) solution with a fiber diameter of approximately 5 µm, comparable to native collagen fibers in vivo. Furthermore, when CMFs are dispersed in phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS) at 4°C, a collagen nanofiber (CNF) solution with a fiber diameter of approximately 100 nm is obtained (Figure 1b) [17]. This hierarchical reduction in fiber diameter from CMFs to CNFs enables the preparation of high‐concentration collagen gels (up to 4.0 wt%), thereby providing a physiologically relevant collagen‐based platform capable of reproducing a broad range of elastic moduli comparable to those of tumor stromal tissues while avoiding nonphysiological modifications (Figure 1c). Here, we propose this hierarchical CNF platform as a physiologically relevant collagen scaffold that addresses this unmet need by enabling the fabrication of collagen matrices with tunable mechanical properties while preserving the native extracellular matrix composition.

Results and Discussion

Morphological Observation of CMFs and CNFs

Col I has a hierarchical fiber structure. The amino acid sequence Gly‐X‐Y is the basic unit, and three left‐handed polypeptide chains with different amino acid sequences, two α1 and one α2, each with a molecular weight of 100,000, are assembled to form a right‐handed triple‐helix molecule with a molecular weight of 300,000, a length of 300 nm, and a diameter of 1.5 nm (Figure 1a) [19]. Furthermore, in vivo, triple‐helix molecules regularly aggregate to form collagen protofibrils with a diameter of 100 nm and collagen fibrils with a diameter of 10 µm [20]. Collagen fibrils in which the fibrils are clustered together in this manner are considered to be relatively rigid chains. Therefore, if we can decrease the fiber diameter and improve the flexibility of the fibers, we expect to increase the entanglement between the fibers and improve the elastic modulus of the collagen gel.

To compare morphological properties of CMFs and CNFs, they were prepared and observed using Phase contrast (Ph) microscopy and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (Figures 2a,b). The presence of CNFs could not be confirmed in the Ph images, and CMFs could not be observed as whole fibers in the TEM images. Next, one hundred CMFs and CNFs in each microscopic image were randomly selected and the mean values of fiber length and fiber diameter were calculated from TEM images. The fiber length of CMF was 220 ± 72 µm and the fiber diameter was 5.0 ± 2.8 µm (Figure 2a). The fiber diameter of CNF was 80 ± 38 nm (Figure 2b).

Structural Analysis of CMFs and CNFs

For structural comparison of CMFs and CNFs, Col I has a molecular weight of 100,000 and is a triple‐helical protein consisting of two types of alpha chains with different amino acid sequences, two alpha 1 chains and one alpha 2 chain [19]. In the circular dichroism (CD) spectrum, a positive peak due to the triple‐helix structure characteristic of Col I is detected at a wavelength of around 222 nm [21]. However, gelatin, a thermally denatured form of collagen with three chains dissociated into single chains, does not show a positive peak because the triple‐helix structure has collapsed. In our study, a positive peak at around 220 nm was detected in collagen, CMFs, and CNFs, whereas it disappeared in gelatin, indicating that the triple‐helical structure was maintained even after the defibrillation process (Figure 2c).

Molecular weight analysis by sodium dodecyl sulfate poly acrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS‐PAGE) showed that for collagen (Col I), CMF, and CNF, two bands derived from α1 and α2 chains were detected at around 120 kDa, a band of β chains, which are dimers of α chains, at around 210 kDa, and a band of γ chains, which are trimers, at positions above the measurable range, respectively (Figure 2d). These results confirmed that the molecular weight was maintained even after the defibrillation process.

Next, infrared (IR) was used to measure the spectral changes associated with collagen disaggregation. Collagen, CMFs, and CNFs showed a broad peak near 3,400 cm−1 derived from N─H stretching vibration, a peak near 1,637 cm−1 derived from C═O stretching vibration, a peak near 1,536 cm−1 derived from N─H deformation vibration, and a peak near 1,231 cm−1 derived from C─N stretching vibration in the IR spectrum (Figure 2e).

These results indicate that amide bonds and their secondary structures were maintained in CMFs and CNFs after the defibrillation treatment.

Rheological Properties of CMF and CNF Solutions and Gels

To evaluate the physical properties of solutions and gels, CMF and CNF solutions were observed to be gelatinized by incubation at 37°C for 3 h (Figure 3a). The 1.0 wt% CMF and CNF solutions showed a storage modulus G' that was lower than the loss modulus G“, indicating that they had sol‐like rheological properties (Figure 3b,c). In addition, the 1.0 wt% CMF and CNF gels showed a storage modulus G' that was higher than the loss modulus G”, indicating a change to gel‐like rheological properties (Figure 3d,e). These results reveal that both 1.0 wt% CMF and CNF solutions generate a sol‐gel transition when incubated at 37°C. Compared to the 1.0 wt% CNF solution, the 2.0 and 3.0 wt% CNF solutions were cloudy and had reduced flowability (Figure 3f). The rheological properties of these solutions were also investigated: the 2.0 wt% CNF solution exhibited a sol‐gel transition similar to that of the 1.0 wt% CNF solution. However, the 3.0 wt% CNF solution already had gel‐like rheological properties at 4°C, as the storage modulus G' was higher than the loss modulus G″ (Figure 3g). These findings suggested that the CMF and CNF solutions exhibit gel‐like rheological properties when incubated at 37°C, whereas the 3.0 wt% CNF solution already has gel‐like rheological properties at 4°C.

Comparison of Elastic Modulus of CMF and CNF Gels

The elastic moduli of the 0.2–4.0 wt% CMF gels are shown in Figure 3h. The elastic modulus increased in a collagen concentration‐dependent manner for the 0.2–2.0 wt% CMF gels, and modulus changes from 1 to 18 kPa could be derived. However, the elastic modulus decreased for the 3.0% and 4.0 wt% CMF gels. Gelation of the collagen solution is thought to be attributed to an increase in hydrophobic physical cross‐linking due to heating (37°C) [5]. However, the physical crowding of CMFs caused by the high concentration makes it difficult for collagen fibers to form hydrophobic entanglements with each other in the gels, which may have resulted in a decrease in the elastic modulus of the gels.

Next, the elastic moduli of 0.2–4.0 wt% CNF gels were measured (Figure 3h). The CNF gels were able to derive a 50‐fold change in the elastic modulus from 1.5 to 80 kPa as the collagen concentration was increased. The reason for the reduced elastic modulus in the 4.0 wt% CNF gel, as well as in the 3.0 wt% CMF gel, is likely due to the physical crowding of the collagen fibers.

Collagen gels with a wide range of elastic modulus from 1 to 80 kPa could be produced by varying the collagen fiber diameter and concentration (Figure 3h). Even with the same collagen concentration, gels with different elastic moduli can be produced by varying the fiber diameter. Moreover, even with the same elastic modulus, gels with different collagen concentrations can be produced. These features are expected to be applied to cancer cell culture.

Evaluation of Hydrophobic Environment of CMF and CNF Gels and Observation of Fiber Entanglement

Comparison of the elastic moduli of CMF and CNF gels at the same concentrations confirmed that the CNF gels had a significantly higher elastic modulus at each concentration (Figure 3h). As mentioned above, hydrophobic interactions between fibers are assumed to strongly influence the gelation of collagen solutions, and these differences in elastic moduli are thought to be due to the hydrophobic environment formed in the gels. Therefore, we evaluated the hydrophobic environment in each gel using ANS‐Na, which is widely used as a hydrophobic fluorescent probe.

ANS‐Na is known as a charge‐transfer fluorophore [22] and is used as a fluorescent probe molecule to evaluate various physical properties because its fluorescent properties change dramatically depending on the surrounding environment. In particular, it has been reported that the fluorescent properties of ANS‐Na change markedly in response to changes in polarity [23, 24, 25, 26]. This fluorescence property is thought to be due to the two different excited states and the intramolecular charge transfer between them (Figure S1) [27]. Under hydrophobic conditions, as in the ground state, the molecule is nonplanar (NP) (A in Figure S1), and absorption of excitation light occurs at the naphthalene ring moiety. This NP excited state emits strong short‐wavelength fluorescence. On the other hand, under hydrophilic conditions, charge transfer from the phenyl moiety to the naphthalene moiety occurs with intramolecular rotation of the phenylamino group from the NP state, leading to the energetically more stable charge transfer (CT) state (B in Figure S1). The CT state emits weaker fluorescence at longer wavelengths because of the ease of charge release into the surrounding polar solvent and the radiative‐free transition to the ground state. Because of these characteristics, the CT state is used to evaluate the properties of hydrogels driven by hydrophobic interactions [28].

Compared to the PBS solution, each CNF gel had higher fluorescence intensity, and the intensity of the fluorescence peak increased with increasing concentration (Figure S2). The fluorescence peak shifted from 530 to 510 nm, suggesting that the fluorescence was emitted in a hydrophobic environment in the gel. This confirmed the usefulness of ANS‐Na for evaluating the hydrophobic environment in this collagen gel, and we then compared the hydrophobic environment in CMF and CNF gels.

After making a 1.0 wt% CMF solution, it was mixed with 40 µM ANS‐Na solution and gelatinized for measurement (Figure 3i). 0.5 wt% CMF gel also showed an increase in fluorescence intensity and a shorter wavelength shift of the fluorescence peak from 530 to 510 nm, as shown in Figure 3i. However, the intensity was below that of the CNF gel at the same concentration, suggesting that a more hydrophobic environment is formed in the CNF gel. It is assumed that the formation of such a hydrophobic environment affects the elastic modulus of the gel. Next, the hydrophobic domains and entanglement between fibers were observed using confocal laser scanning microscopy (Figure 3j) [29].

As shown in Figure 3j, CLSM images of CMF gels with a fiber diameter of 5 µm revealed hydrophobic domains of 42 ± 14 µm throughout, while CNF gels with a fiber diameter of 80 nm showed uniform sub‐micro hydrophobic domains on the surface. Furthermore, SEM images showed that rigid CMFs had difficulty in entangling, whereas CNFs with a fiber diameter nearly 60 times smaller and increased fiber flexibility showed dense entanglement with fiber domains of 200 ± 120 nm. This uniform and more abundant formation of sub‐micro hydrophobic physical cross‐links in the gel is thought to be responsible for the significantly higher elastic modulus of the CNF gel compared to the CMF gel.

Evaluation of Cell Behavior Using CMF and CNF Gels

For evaluation of cancer cell behavior, Matrigel, which was used as a positive control, is a hydrogel product extracted from mouse sarcoma and contains Col IV, the main component of basement membrane, as well as laminin and growth factors, and has been used as an alternative to animal experiments in many studies. However, its application is limited because it is derived from mice, making its transplantation and medical application in humans impossible. In studies using Matrigel for cancer cell culture, cell morphology correlated with the native metastatic potential of specific cancer cell types, in contrast to that on polystyrene culture dishes, suggesting that it may be useful as a scaffold material that can maintain cancer cell properties.

The nuclear stained images of JC‐011 cells on Matrigel and the cell density on each gel calculated from the images are shown in Figure 4a. From these results, no changes in cell density as a function of scaffold elastic modulus, fiber diameter, or collagen concentration were observed. On the other hand, significantly higher cell densities comparable to Matrigel were observed on the 1.0 wt% CNF gel with an elastic modulus of 40 kPa [30], which is close to that of living colorectal cancer tissue. This suggests that the growth rate of JC‐011 cells may be enhanced on a scaffold with an elastic modulus similar to that of stage‐advanced in vivo colorectal cancer tissue.

The 3D images of cell colonies obtained by staining of nuclei and actin fibers are shown in Figure 4b. On 0.5 wt% CMF gels with an elastic modulus of 2 kPa, flat cell colonies with flat cell growth were observed, while on CNF gels and Matrigel with a similar elastic modulus, round cell colonies with 3D growth were observed. These different cell morphologies on scaffolds with similar elastic moduli suggest that the fiber diameter of the scaffold affects cell behavior. Similar to cell density, colony volume was found to be significantly greater on Matrigel and 1.0 wt% CNF gels, while there were no significant differences in collagen concentration‐dependent changes. In addition, there was a trend toward higher colony volume on CNF gels between scaffolds with similar elastic moduli, although it was not significantly different (Figure 4c).

Cellular Characterization in CMF and CNF Gels

To evaluate differences in JC‐011 cell properties according to fiber diameter in 0.5 wt% CMF and CNF gels, we used flow cytometry to quantitatively analyze the expression of five cancer‐associated proteins that have been widely studied as cancer cell markers: epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), leucine‐rich repeat‐containing G protein‐coupled receptor 5 (Lgr5), CD44 variant 9 (CD44v9), epithelial cell adhesion molecule (EpCAM), and E‐cadherin (Figure S3). The expression of each cancer marker protein was represented as a histogram, and the ratio of the mean values of the peaks (mean value of red peaks/mean value of blue peaks) was used as the relative expression level. In addition, each value at 0.5 wt% was compared when the relative expression in Matrigel was set to 1 (Figure 4d) because we focused on simply comparison the effect of elastic modulus of CMF and CNF at the same concentration for the cancer marker expression. Although the collagen fiber morphologies of CMF and CNF differ under these conditions and may affect cell function, it is technically challenging to isolate and vary a single parameter of the collagen fibers for direct comparison. Therefore, we compared their cellular responses under conditions with the same elastic modulus. Compared to CMF gels, the expression levels of cancer stem cell markers CD44v9 and Lgr5 were higher on Matrigel and CNF gels, whereas the expression level of E‐cadherin, a cell adhesion molecule reported to be downregulated by malignant transformation (epithelial mesenchymal transition) [31], was lower (Figure 4d). However, no significant differences were detected in EGFR [32], which is involved in promoting cancer cell‐specific abnormal growth, and EpCAM [33], which has been studied as a poor prognostic marker in many cancer tissues. The JC‐011 cells cultured on Matrigel and CNF gel showed similar cancer cell marker expression trends, suggesting that the CNF gel can grow cells that show similar cancer‐associated protein expression properties as when using Matrigel.

Next, RNA sequencing was performed to compare and analyze cellular properties on each scaffold. Cluster analysis showed that the CNF gels and Matrigel fell into the same cluster, whereas the CMF gels formed a separate cluster from them (Figure 5). The results in Figure 5a show that the CNF gel and Matrigel have similar overall gene expression trends, whereas the CMF gel has a different expression trend than the other two. The results of principal component analysis (PCA) are shown in Figure 5b. There were clear differences in gene expression between cells cultured on the three types of gels: CMF gel, CNF gel, and Matrigel. The distribution and number of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) are shown in Figure 5c where two of each gel are compared. The number of DEGs in the CNF gel versus Matrigel was 168. On the other hand, the number of DEGs in the CMF gel versus Matrigel was 623, and the number of DEGs in the CNF gel versus CMF gel was 364. The number of DEGs between the CMF gel versus the CNF gel or the Matrigel was 2–4 times higher than the number of DEGs in the Matrigel versus the CNF gel, suggesting that JC‐011 cells cultured on the CMF gel exhibit different gene expressions. For detailed analysis Matrigel versus CMF, oxygen and stress related gene expressions have been upregulated such as response to organic substances, cellular response to stress, response to decreased oxygen levels, response to oxygen levels, and response to hypoxia. On the other hand, chemokine, cytokine, and immune related gene expressions have been upregulated such as chemokine activity, cytokine activity, signaling receptor binding, immune response, signaling receptor binding, and cellular response to cytokine stimulus in Matrigel versus CNF. In the case of CNF versus CMF, gene expressions such as response to stress, response to organic substances, and cellular response to stress which are the same as Matrigel versus CMF have been upregulated. However, chemokine, cytokine, and immune related gene expressions such as chemokine activity, cytokine activity, and signaling receptor binding, were also upregulated. These results suggested that gene expression of cancer cells on Matrigel were mostly similar to those of CNF. However, CNF and CMF also showed partly similar gene expressions. In Figure 5d, the pathways that were significantly different between each of the two gels and the number of genes associated with those pathways are shown. In addition, we evaluated the expression levels of several genes whose expression has been reported to increase in correlation with cancer grade and found that expression was increased in JC‐011 cells cultured on Matrigel and CNF gels compared to those on CMF gels (Figure 5e). These findings suggest that JC‐011 cells cultured on Matrigel and CNF gels may have increased stemness and malignancy compared to those on CMF gels.

Evaluation of Adsorption Amount of Adhesion Proteins in Each Gel and Comparison of Their Properties

To evaluate the adsorption of adhesion proteins in each gel, the amount of FN, LN, and Col IV adsorbed on the gels were analyzed and shown in Figure S4a–c. The amount of FN adsorbed on the Matrigel and CNF gels was 0.25 µg, nearly twice as much as on the CMF gels. Furthermore, the adsorption of LM on Matrigel was the highest at 48 ng, followed by 33 ng on the CNF gels and 15 ng on the CMF gels, a difference of more than twofold. On the other hand, there was no significant difference in the amount of Col IV adsorbed. These results suggest that protein adsorption is associated with differences in fiber diameter and the hydrophobic microenvironment of the gel surface. Because these physicochemical properties are closely interrelated, the relative contribution of each factor cannot be distinguished in the present study.

Although the present study primarily focused on the elastic modulus of collagen gels, cancer cells are known to respond not only to matrix stiffness but also to other viscoelastic properties, including stress relaxation and viscoelastic energy dissipation. Because the present CNF gels are formed through physical crosslinking and exhibit concentration‐dependent nanofiber entanglement, differences in stress relaxation behavior may also contribute to the observed cellular responses. Therefore, stress relaxation should also be considered as a potentially important mechanical cue when interpreting the biological responses observed in the present study. Future studies quantitatively evaluating stress relaxation and its relationship to cancer cell behavior will further improve our understanding of the mechanisms underlying the biological performance of these collagen scaffolds.

SEM images of each gel surface are shown in Figure S4d. Some pores and irregularities were observed on the CMF gel surface, while in CNF and Matrigel, the gel surface was found to be uniform and smooth. Since it has been reported that cell adhesion is enhanced on scaffold material surfaces with fewer irregularities, it is possible that the differences in surface structure in CMF gels, CNF gels, and Matrigel affect cell behavior. Fiber, pore, and domain sizes for each gel are summarized in Figure S4e. The fiber diameters were 5.0 µm for CMFs, 80 nm for CNFs, and 0.8 µm for Matrigel. Furthermore, the pore size was 10 µm for CMF gels, 0.6 µm for CNF gels, and 2.6 µm for Matrigel, and the domain size was 42, 0.2, and 1.5 µm, respectively. This confirms that Matrigel has intermediate size characteristics between CMF and CNF gels in fiber, pore, and domain size. It has been reported that gel size properties such as fiber diameter and pore size directly affect cell behavior [12, 13, 14, 15, 16], which may be attributed to adhesion between the gel and cells. Integrins, which are responsible for adhesion between Col I and cells, are transmembrane proteins and have been reported to be the starting point of various signal transductions through binding to collagen [34]. Furthermore, increased collagen adhesion has been reported to enhance integrin expression, leading to activation of signaling pathways such as Snail and Slug, suppression of E‐cadherin expression, and promotion of epithelial‐mesenchymal transition [35]. Although the present findings, including the observed differences in cell morphology, protein adsorption, and gene expression profiles, are consistent with a possible role of integrin‐mediated adhesion, direct experimental verification of this mechanism remains beyond the scope of the present study. Therefore, the differences in cell behavior observed among CMF gels, CNF gels, and Matrigel may be explained, at least in part, by differences in gel–cell adhesion mediated by integrins. Future studies investigating integrin expression and their interactions with each gel will help clarify the molecular mechanisms underlying the observed cellular responses.

Interestingly, although the CNF gels exhibited higher elastic moduli than Matrigel, they induced remarkably similar cellular morphology, expression of cancer‐associated markers, and global gene expression profiles. These findings suggest that cancer cell behavior is governed not only by bulk matrix stiffness but also by multiple physicochemical properties of the extracellular matrix. In addition to the nanoscale collagen architecture, the differential adsorption of extracellular matrix proteins and the resulting integrin‐mediated cell–matrix interactions are likely to cooperatively regulate cellular responses. Therefore, the Matrigel‐like biological responses observed on CNF gels may arise from the combined effects of these physicochemical cues rather than from elastic modulus alone. This interpretation is also consistent with the growing recognition that matrix architecture and ligand presentation, in addition to bulk stiffness, play central roles in regulating cell behavior within 3D microenvironments.

Conclusion

CMFs and CNFs were prepared by defibrillating collagen fibers. We reported that the CMF and CNF gels could achieve elastic modulus changes of 1 to 18 kPa and 2 to 80 kPa, respectively, by gelation of these solutions at 37°C and by varying the concentration. Thus, collagen gels with an elastic modulus of 80 kPa, comparable to that of in vivo cancer tissue, were produced for the first time by physical cross‐linking alone. When patient‐derived colorectal cancer cells were cultured on CMF and CNF gels, different cell behaviors were observed, with cell morphology and expression characteristics of cancer‐related proteins and genes on CNF gels similar to those on Matrigel. These findings suggest that the present CNF platform represents a promising physiologically relevant collagen‐based scaffold for investigating cancer cell–matrix interactions by reproducing several key biological characteristics observed with Matrigel. Although additional validation will be required for broader biological applications, the present results demonstrate the potential of hierarchical CNFs as a next‐generation collagen‐based tumor stromal model for cancer research.

Experimental Section

Preparation of CMF and CNF Solutions and Fabrication of CMF and CNF Gels

Fifty mg of pepsin‐treated porcine skin‐derived Col I sponge fragments (kindly provided by Nippon Ham Co., Ltd.) were placed in a 15 mL centrifuge tube (Corning, 430791, NY, USA) and homogenized in 5 mL of PBS (Sigma‐Aldrich, D5652‐10L, St. Louis, USA) at speed 6 (3,000 rpm) for 6 min using a homogenizer (Violamo, VH‐10 homogenizer, Japan) equipped with a shaft generator (Violamo, S10N‐8G with a diameter of 8 mm and length of 115 mm, Japan). The obtained suspension was centrifuged (KUBOTA, 5922, Osaka, Japan) for 3 min at 10,000 rpm and 25°C to remove air bubbles, and then resuspended by pipetting to prepare a 1.0 wt% CMF solution.

A 1.0 wt% CNF solution was prepared by allowing the CMF solution to stand and dissolve at 4°C for 1 day. Other concentrations were prepared by adjusting the amount of collagen added.

CMF and CNF gels were prepared by adding 300 µl of CMF and CNF solutions adjusted to various concentrations to 24‐well inserts (Corning, 3470) and then incubating at 37°C for 3 h.

Evaluation of Elastic Modulus

Compression tests were performed using a compression tester (EZ‐Test, SHIMADZU, Kyoto, Japan) at room temperature immediately after the CMF and CNF solutions were gelatinized. The elastic modulus was calculated using the following formula, where E is the elastic modulus (kPa), H is the height of the gel (mm), C is the contact area of the test fixture with the gel (mm2), and S is the slope of the stress‐strain curve (mN/mm). E=H/C×S

The slope of the graph was selected to follow the rising edge of the stress‐strain curve at strains below 5% [36]. The gel height was measured using a digital caliper (Digital Caliper CD‐15CP, Mitutoyo, Japan). The contact area of the jig was calculated by integrating the strain values from the stress‐strain curve.

Quantitative Evaluation of Hydrophobic Environment in CMF and CNF Gels

Seven hundred and fifty µl of 0.2, 0.5, and 1.0 wt% CNF and 1.0 wt% CMF solutions were placed in 1.5 mL Eppendorf tubes (Eppendorf, 3810X, Hamburg, Germany), respectively, and 750 µl of 40 µM sodium 8‐anilino‐1‐naphthalenesulfonate (ANS‐Na) (TCI, 1145‐19‐8, Tokyo, Japan) solution was added and mixed by pipetting. To a fluorescence cell (GL Science, FM20‐UV‐5, Tokyo, Japan), 1.4 mL of the mixed solution was added, incubated at 37°C for 3 h to gelatinize, and then measured at an excitation wavelength of 380 nm and emission wavelengths from 400 to 600 nm using a fluorescence spectrometer (Spectro Fluorometer FP‐8500, JASCO). A 20 µM ANS‐Na solution was used as a control. Because collagen molecules exhibit weak autofluorescence at an excitation wavelength of 380 nm, measurements were also performed on CNF gels at final concentrations of 0.1, 0.25, and 0.5 wt%, and only ANS‐Na‐derived fluorescence was evaluated by subtracting the value of autofluorescence.

Observation of Hydrophobic Domains in CMF and CNF Gels

For 0.5 wt% CMF and CNF gels containing 1.0 mM ANS‐Na, 500 µl of 1.0 wt% CMF or CNF solution and 500 µl of 2 mM ANS‐Na solution were mixed by pipetting in a 1.5 mL Eppendorf tube (Eppendorf, 3810X). Then, 100 µl of the mixture was dropped onto a cover glass (MATSUNAMI, C218181, Tokyo, Japan) and incubated for 3 h at 37°C. The gel surfaces were observed using a confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM) (Olympus, FV‐3000, Tokyo, Japan) with 405 nm excitation light.

Gel Culture of Cancer Cells

One hundred µl of 0.5–2.0 wt% CMF, 0.2–1.0 wt% CNF solution and Matrigel were added to 24‐well plate inserts (Corning, 3470) and incubated in 24‐well plates (IWAKI, 3820‐024, Shizuoka, Japan) at 37°C for 3 h to prepare CMF, CNF gels in each concentration and Matrigel. Matrigel was used as a positive control. JC‐011 cells were seeded at 1.0 × 104 cells on each gel and transferred the next day to 6‐well plates (IWAKI, 3810‐006) and cultured with an adapter for 2 days (total 3 days). The medium was changed on the second day of culture.

Evaluation of Adsorption Amount of Adhesion Protein in Each Gel

Rhodamine‐labeled fibronectin (FN) (Cytoskeleton, FNR‐01A, Denver, USA), rhodamine‐labeled laminin (LM) (Cytoskeleton, LMN‐01), and fluorescein‐labeled type IV collagen (Col IV) (ANASPEC, AS‐85112, California, USA) were diluted in PBS to prepare 20 µg/ml FN, LM and Col IV solutions. To adsorb FN, LM, and Col IV on the gels, 100 µl of FN, LM and Col IV solutions were added to 100 µl 0.5% CMF and CNF gels and Matrigel prepared in 24‐well inserts and incubated at 37°C for 30 min. Subsequently, the gels were washed in 2 mL of PBS solution for 30 min. To dissolve the gels, CMF and CNF gels were treated with 0.1 wt% collagenase (Sigma‐Aldrich, C0130, St. Louis, USA) and Matrigel with 100 µl of 0.1 wt% trypsin (WAKO, 203–09893) for 1 h at 37°C. The fluorescence intensity of these solutions was measured using a fluorescence spectrometer (Spectro Fluorometer FP‐8500, JASCO) at an excitation wavelength of 535 nm and emission wavelength of 550–650 nm for rhodamine and 490 nm and 500–550 nm for fluorescein, and analyzed quantitatively using a standard curve.

Statistical Analysis

All data were expressed as mean values with their corresponding standard deviations. Statistical comparisons between groups were performed using two‐tailed Student's t‐tests. p values were considered significantly different when p < 0.05. “n. s.” means no significant difference.
