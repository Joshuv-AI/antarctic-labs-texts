Computability of dimension groups
Maria Sabitova

Abstract
. We investigate the computability of the isomorphism set Iso ⁡ ( G A , G B ) \operatorname{Iso}(G_{A},G_{B}) between G A G_{A} and G B G_{B} , where G A G_{A} is a subgroup of ℚ n {\mathbb{Q}}^{n} generated by columns of integer powers of a non-singular n × n n\times n -matrix A A with integer entries. Assuming that the characteristic polynomial of A A is irreducible – and under an additional condition when n n is not prime – we prove that Iso ⁡ ( G A , G B ) \operatorname{Iso}(G_{A},G_{B}) is computable; that is, there exists an algorithm that determines the structure in finitely many steps. We also present illustrative examples.



1.
Introduction

It is well known that subgroups of
{\mathbb{Q}}
have a complete classification
[
B37
]
. However, the analogous problem for subgroups of

{\mathbb{Q}}^{n}
– equivalently, torsion-free abelian groups of rank at most
n
– remains open (even in light of
[
F73
]
). As shown in
[
Th06
]
, the classification problem becomes increasingly complex as
n
grows. In our previous works

[
S22
]
,
[
S24
]
,
[
MS25
]
,
[
S25
]
, we focus on a special class of subgroups of
{\mathbb{Q}}^{n}
: those defined by integer matrices. These groups arise naturally in the study of toroidal solenoids and
{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
-odometers.

Given a non-singular matrix
A\in\operatorname{M}_{n}({\mathbb{Z}})
, the associated toroidal solenoid is an
n
-dimensional topological abelian group. Such solenoids were first introduced by M. C. McCord in 1965
[
M65
]
. The earliest examples, corresponding to
n=1
and
A=2
, were studied by L. Vietoris in 1927
[
V27
]
and later extended by van Dantzig in 1930 to arbitrary
A\in{\mathbb{Z}}

[
D30
]
. The same class of subgroups also arises as the first cohomology groups of constant base
{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
-odometers
[
GPS19
]
, and they appear in the context of symbolic dynamics and dimension group theory via subshifts of finite type
[
BS24
]
.

More precisely, for a non-singular matrix
A\in\operatorname{M}_{n}({\mathbb{Z}})
, we define the subgroup
G_{A}
of
{\mathbb{Q}}^{n}
generated by columns of powers
A^{i}
,
i\in{\mathbb{Z}}
.
In this paper, we investigate computability questions related to these subgroups
G_{A}
. In
[
S22
]
and
[
S24
]
, we addressed the classification problem for these groups: given two non-singular matrices
A,B\in\operatorname{M}_{n}({\mathbb{Z}})
, we determined when the associated groups
G_{A}
and
G_{B}
are isomorphic (as abstract groups). The case
n=2
was treated in
[
S22
]
, while the general case was handled in
[
S24
]
. Applications to
{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
-odometers were explored in
[
MS25
]
.
In
[
S25
]
, we studied the endomorphism ring
\operatorname{End}(G_{A})
for arbitrary
n
and provided a general criterion for when a rational matrix
T\in\operatorname{M}_{n}({\mathbb{Q}})
defines an endomorphism of
G_{A}
. When the characteristic polynomial of
A
is irreducible – and under an additional assumption if
n
is not prime – we showed that every such endomorphism commutes with
A
, the ring

\operatorname{End}(G_{A})
is commutative and a finitely generated module over the ring
{\mathbb{Z}}[t,t^{-1}]
, and the automorphism group
\operatorname{Aut}(G_{A})
is a finitely generated abelian group.
Building on these theoretical results, the present paper aims to make the methods more practical by developing an algorithmic framework for computing examples. Although various examples were computed in our earlier works, here we adopt a more systematic computational approach. In particular, when the characteristic polynomial of
A
is irreducible, so that the structure of
G_{A}
is especially rigid, we prove that the isomorphism set between
G_{A}
and
G_{B}
is computable. That is, there exists an algorithm that determines the structure in finitely many steps. All components involved in the proofs are themselves computable and are implemented in mathematical software such as
[
SageMath
]
. We use
[
SageMath
]
together with the database
[
LMFDB
]
to test our proofs on both two- and higher-dimensional examples. At this stage, we do not address the computational complexity or efficiency of these procedures.

Our work is inspired by that of K. H. Kim and F. W. Roush
[
KR89
]
, who investigated the decidability of epimorphisms between dimension groups. In their setting,
G_{A}
appears as a special case of a dimension group with
A
non-singular (whereas in
[
KR89
]
, singular matrices are also allowed). Notably, epimorphisms between such groups are more restricted than general homomorphisms: if there exists an epimorphism from
G_{A}
to
G_{B}
, then
A
and
B
must be conjugate in
\operatorname{GL}_{n}({\mathbb{Q}})
. This implication does not hold for arbitrary group homomorphisms. For example, there exist isomorphic groups
G_{A}\cong G_{B}
for which matrices
A

and
B
have different eigenvalues and hence are not conjugated by a matrix in
\operatorname{GL}_{n}({\mathbb{Q}})

[
S22
, Example 7.6]
.
In particular, the results of
[
KR89
]
apply to the endomorphism ring
\operatorname{End}(G_{A})
and the automorphism group

\operatorname{Aut}(G_{A})
under additional assumptions; we present new algorithms and examples for computing each.
We also take inspiration from
[
CP24
]
, where the authors investigate computability of the linear representation group of constant-base

{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
-odometer systems.

0&0&1\end{pmatrix},\quad J=\begin{pmatrix}2&0&0\\
0&3&1\\
0&0&3\end{pmatrix}.

Then

is generated as a left and right

-module by

X=X(a,Y)=\begin{pmatrix}a&0&0\\
0&d&e\\

Hence,
\operatorname{End}(G_{A})=\{MXM^{-1}\}
, where

and

, and

\operatorname{Aut}(G_{A})=\{MXM^{-1}\}
, where

with

and

Y\in\operatorname{GL}_{2}({\mathbb{Z}}[\frac{1}{3}])
.

By Lemma
3.1
, we may assume for the remainder of the section that

and
{\mathcal{P}}^{\prime}(A)\neq\emptyset
in order to compute
\operatorname{End}(G_{A})
and
\operatorname{Aut}(G_{A})
. For a prime

, we denote by

the multiplicity of zero in the reduction of characteristic polynomial

of

modulo

. In particular,

if and only if

and

if and only if
0<t_{p}<n
.

3.1.
Irreducible characteristic polynomial

In this work, we concentrate on the most explicit case, when

n\geq 2
is arbitrary, the characteristic polynmial
h_{A}
of
A
is irreducible, and there exists a prime
p\in{\mathbb{N}}

such that
n
,
t_{p}
are coprime, denoted by

(n,t_{p})=1
. It is known from our previous work
[
S24
]
that under these assumptions, the structure of
G_{A}
is especially rigid.
That allows us to make calculations especially concrete.

Theorem 3.3

(
c.f.
,
[
KR89
]
)
.

Let
A\in\operatorname{M}_{n}({\mathbb{Z}})
be non-singular. Assume that characteristic polynomial
h_{A}\in{\mathbb{Z}}[t]
of
A
is irreducible and there exists a prime
p\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that
(n,t_{p})=1
.
Then
\operatorname{End}(G_{A})
,
\operatorname{Aut}(G_{A})
are computable.

Proof.

Let
A\in\operatorname{M}_{n}({\mathbb{Z}})
be given.
We first find the characteristic polynomial of
A
and check whether it is irreducible, which is known to be a computable condition (see
e.g.
,
[
GG13
, Algorithm 16.22]
). We then compute
t_{p}
for all
p\in{\mathcal{P}}
and verify the second condition

(n,t_{p})=1
. All of these steps are computable.
Let
\overline{{\mathbb{Q}}}
denote a fixed algebraic closure of
{\mathbb{Q}}
and let
\lambda\in\overline{{\mathbb{Q}}}
be an eigenvalue of
A
,
K={\mathbb{Q}}(\lambda)
, and let
{\bf u}\in K^{n}
be an eigenvector of
A
corresponding to
\lambda
.
Multiplying
{\bf u}
by an appropriate integer, without loss of generality, we can assume

{\bf u}\in{\mathbb{Z}}[\lambda]^{n}\subseteq({\mathcal{O}}_{K})^{n}
, where
{\mathcal{O}}_{K}
is the ring of integers of
K
.
Since
h_{A}
is irreducible, the absolute Galois group

\operatorname{Gal}(\overline{{\mathbb{Q}}}/{\mathbb{Q}})
acts transitively on the set of all eigenvalues of
A
,
i.e.
, there exist

\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{n}\in\operatorname{Gal}(\overline{{\mathbb{Q}}}/{\mathbb{Q}})
,
\sigma_{1}=\operatorname{id}
, such that
A=M\Lambda M^{-1}
, where

(3.4)

\Lambda=\operatorname{diag}\left(\begin{matrix}\sigma_{1}(\lambda)&\ldots&\sigma_{n}(\lambda)\end{matrix}\right),\quad M=\left(\begin{matrix}\sigma_{1}({\bf u})&\ldots&\sigma_{n}({\bf u}\end{matrix})\right)

with each
\sigma_{i}({\bf u})
written as a column,
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
. By
[
S25
, Proposition 4.1]
, for any

T\in\operatorname{End}(G_{A})
there exists
x\in{\mathcal{O}}_{K,\lambda}
such that

(3.5)

T=T(x)=MXM^{-1},\quad X=\operatorname{diag}\left(\begin{matrix}\sigma_{1}(x)&\ldots&\sigma_{n}(x)\end{matrix}\right).

Here,
x\in{\mathcal{O}}_{K,\lambda}
if and only if
x\in K
and there exists
\lambda^{i}
,

i\in{\mathbb{N}}\cup\{0\}
, such that
\lambda^{i}x\in{\mathcal{O}}_{K}
. Note that
{\mathbb{Z}}[A,A^{-1}]\subseteq\operatorname{End}(G_{A})
and hence
\operatorname{End}(G_{A})
is a
{\mathbb{Z}}[A,A^{-1}]
-module.
Note that
{\mathbb{Z}}[\lambda^{\pm 1}]={\mathbb{Z}}[\lambda^{-1}]
, since
\lambda
is an algebraic integer.
Thus,
\operatorname{End}(G_{A})
is a
{\mathbb{Z}}[\lambda^{-1}]
-module, where for
T\in\operatorname{End}(G_{A})
, the action of
\lambda
(respectively, of
A
) is
given by
\lambda\cdot T(x)=T(\lambda x)
(respectively,
A\cdot T=AT
). Note that

A
and
T
commute.
By
[
S25
, Corollary 4.3]
,
\operatorname{End}(G_{A})
is a finitely-generated
{\mathbb{Z}}[\lambda^{-1}]
-module and we will describe how to compute its finitely many generators as such. Let
T\in\operatorname{End}(G_{A})
be arbitrary, so that
T=T(x)
by (
3.5
). Since we are looking for generators of
\operatorname{End}(G_{A})
as a

{\mathbb{Z}}[\lambda^{-1}]
-module, without loss of generality, we can assume that
x
itself is an algebraic integer,
i.e.
,
x\in{\mathcal{O}}_{K}
.
Moreover, since
T(G_{A})\subseteq G_{A}
, there exists
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that
A^{k}T\in\operatorname{M}_{n}({\mathbb{Z}})
. Thus, without loss of generality, we can assume that
T\in\operatorname{M}_{n}({\mathbb{Z}})
. Thus, it is enough to find all
x\in{\mathcal{O}}_{K}
such that
T(x)\in\operatorname{M}_{n}({\mathbb{Z}})
.
Clearly, if
x\in{\mathbb{Z}}[\lambda]
, then
T(x)\in{\mathbb{Z}}[A]\subset\operatorname{M}_{n}({\mathbb{Z}})
.
Therefore, one only needs to consider a finite set

{\mathcal{G}}\subset{\mathcal{O}}_{K}
of representatives of the quotient
{\mathcal{O}}_{K}/{\mathbb{Z}}[\lambda]
,
i.e.
, whether

T(x)\in\operatorname{M}_{n}({\mathbb{Z}})
for each

x\in{\mathcal{G}}
. Thus, all generators of
\operatorname{End}(G_{A})
as a
{\mathbb{Z}}[\lambda^{-1}]
-module can be found in finitely many steps, provided that each step in the proof is computable.
Indeed, algorithms exist to compute an integral basis of
{\mathcal{O}}_{K}
, starting from
1,\lambda,\ldots,\lambda^{n-1}
,
as well as the index
l=[{\mathcal{O}}_{K}:{\mathbb{Z}}[\lambda]]
of
{\mathbb{Z}}[\lambda]
in
{\mathcal{O}}_{K}
and set
{\mathcal{G}}
(
e.g.
, Algorithm
A_{6}

[
G80
]
). Thus, each
x\in{\mathcal{G}}
can be written as
x=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}x_{i}\lambda^{i}
,
x_{0},\ldots,x_{n-1}\in{\mathbb{Q}}
, and

T(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}x_{i}A^{i}
.
This completes a proof that
\operatorname{End}(G_{A})
is computable.

We now make a few observations that will be used later in the proof.
For
x\in{\mathcal{O}}_{K}
and
l=[{\mathcal{O}}_{K}:{\mathbb{Z}}[\lambda]]
, by (
3.4
) and (
3.5
), we have that
l\cdot T(x)\in{\mathbb{Z}}[A]
.
Furthermore, the discriminant of the lattice in
{\mathcal{O}}_{K}
generated by coordinates of
{\bf u}\in({\mathcal{O}}_{K})^{n}
gives
m=(\det M)^{2}\in{\mathbb{Z}}
.
Thus,
m\cdot T(x)\in\operatorname{M}_{n}({\mathbb{Z}})
. Note that
A
and
T
commute, so that
T(G_{A})\subseteq G_{A}
if and only if there exists
j\in{\mathbb{N}}\cup\{0\}
such that
A^{j}T\in\operatorname{M}_{n}({\mathbb{Z}})
if and only if for each prime
p\in{\mathbb{N}}
there exists
j=j(p)\in{\mathbb{N}}\cup\{0\}
such that
A^{j}T\in\operatorname{M}_{n}({\mathbb{Z}}_{(p)})
, where

{\mathbb{Z}}_{(p)}=\left.\left\{\frac{q}{r}\in{\mathbb{Q}}\,\,\right|\,q,r\in{\mathbb{Z}},\,\,r\neq 0,\,\,(p,r)=1\right\},

a subring of
{\mathbb{Q}}
. Putting it all together, for
T=T(x)
with
x\in{\mathcal{O}}_{K}
, we have that
T\in\operatorname{End}(G_{A})
if and only if for each prime

p\in{\mathbb{N}}
dividing both
l
and
m
(
i.e.
,
p
divides the greatest common divisor
(l,m)
of
l
and
m
) there
exists

such that
A^{j}T\in\operatorname{M}_{n}({\mathbb{Z}}_{(p)})
. It is enough to check

bounded by the order of the image of the set

\{\lambda^{i}\,|\,i\in{\mathbb{N}}\cup\{0\}\}
in the finite ring
{\mathcal{O}}_{K}/(p^{g_{p}}{\mathcal{O}}_{K})
,
g_{p}={\min\{\operatorname{val}_{p}l,\operatorname{val}_{p}m\}}
. In particular, if

l
and
m
are coprime, then
\operatorname{End}(G_{A})\cong{\mathcal{O}}_{K,\lambda}
via
T(x)\mapsto x
.

We now show computability of
\operatorname{Aut}(G_{A})
. By
[
S25
, Corollary 4.2]
,
\operatorname{Aut}(G_{A})
is a finitely-generated abelian group.
Denote

{\mathcal{O}}^{\times}_{K,\lambda}=\{x\in K-\{0\}\,|\,\operatorname{val}_{{\mathfrak{p}}}(x)=0\text{ for any prime ideal }{\mathfrak{p}}\text{ of }{\mathcal{O}}_{K}\text{ not dividing }\lambda\}.

In other words,
x\in K-\{0\}
belongs to
{\mathcal{O}}^{\times}_{K,\lambda}
if and only if there are only prime ideals dividing
\lambda

in the prime decomposition of ideal
(x)=x{\mathcal{O}}_{K}
. By
[
S25
, Proposition 4.1]
, if
T(x)\in\operatorname{Aut}(G_{A})
, then
x\in{\mathcal{O}}^{\times}_{K,\lambda}
. The converse does not hold in general. The group
{\mathcal{O}}^{\times}_{K,\lambda}
is an example of a group of
S
-units in a number field
K
, where
S
is a finite set of prime ideals of
{\mathcal{O}}_{K}
. It is well-known that those groups are finitely generated and there are algorithms to determine generating sets. The subgroup

Y=\{x\in{\mathcal{O}}^{\times}_{K,\lambda}\,|\,T(x)\in\operatorname{Aut}(G_{A})\}\cong\operatorname{Aut}(G_{A})

is finitely generated, too. Assuming the group of units
{\mathcal{O}}_{K}^{\times}
of ring
{\mathcal{O}}_{K}
is known, here is one way to find generators of
{\mathcal{O}}^{\times}_{K,\lambda}
.
Clearly,
\lambda\in Y
, since

T(\lambda)=A
. Let

T(x)\in\operatorname{Aut}(G_{A})
,
x\in{\mathcal{O}}_{K,\lambda}^{\times}
, so that there exists a power of
\lambda
such that

\lambda^{j}x\in{\mathcal{O}}_{K}
,
j\in{\mathbb{N}}\cup\{0\}
. Without loss of generality, we can assume that
x\in{\mathcal{O}}_{K}\cap{\mathcal{O}}^{\times}_{K,\lambda}
.
Then there is

i\in{\mathbb{N}}\cup\{0\}
and
y\in{\mathcal{O}}_{K}-\{0\}
such that
\frac{\lambda^{i}}{x}=y
, equivalently,
xy=\lambda^{i}
. We now decompose
the ideal of
{\mathcal{O}}_{K}
generated by
\lambda
into (distinct) prime ideals:
(\lambda)=\lambda{\mathcal{O}}_{K}={\mathfrak{p}}_{1}^{i_{1}}\cdots{\mathfrak{p}}_{k}^{i_{k}}
, where

i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
{\mathfrak{p}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathfrak{p}}_{k}
are distinct prime ideals of
{\mathcal{O}}_{K}
. Let
l_{i}=|{\mathfrak{p}}_{i}|
,
l_{i}\in{\mathbb{N}}
, be the order of
{\mathfrak{p}}_{i}
in the class group of
K
,
i.e.
,
{\mathfrak{p}}_{i}^{l_{i}}=(z_{i})
is principal,
z_{i}\in{\mathcal{O}}_{K}
,
i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
.
From the uniqueness of prime decompositions and
xy=\lambda^{i}
, we have that
(x)={\mathfrak{p}}_{1}^{j_{1}}\cdots{\mathfrak{p}}_{k}^{j_{k}}
,

j_{1},\ldots,j_{k}\in{\mathbb{N}}\cup\{0\}
. Thus, the product
{\mathfrak{p}}_{1}^{j_{1}}\cdots{\mathfrak{p}}_{k}^{j_{k}}
is principal.
It is enough to find all principal products of this form among finitely many choices when

0\leq j_{i}<l_{i}
,
i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
. For each such principal product we fix a generator. We denote the (finite) set of such generators by
{\mathcal{G}}\subset{\mathcal{O}}_{K}
. Thus,

x
can be written as a product

(3.6)

x=ugz_{1}^{a_{1}}\cdots z_{k}^{a_{k}},\quad a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}\in{\mathbb{N}}\cup\{0\},\quad u\in{\mathcal{O}}_{K}^{\times},\quad g\in{\mathcal{G}}.

It is well-known that
{\mathcal{O}}_{K}^{\times}
is finitely generated and there are algorithms to find its finite generating sets (see
e.g.
,
[
G80
, Algorithm
A_{8}
]
).

We now assume that a finite generating set
\{f_{1},\ldots,f_{r}\}
of
{\mathcal{O}}_{K,\lambda}^{\times}
is known.
For each

f_{i}
, there exist
s_{i},t_{i}\in{\mathbb{N}}\cup\{0\}
such that
\lambda^{s_{i}}f_{i},\lambda^{t_{i}}f_{i}^{-1}\in{\mathcal{O}}_{K}
,
i\in\{1,\ldots,r\}
.
For any finite subset
I\subseteq\{1,\ldots,r\}
and its complement
I^{c}
in
\{1,\ldots,r\}
, let

x=\prod_{i\in I}f_{i}^{a_{i}}\times\prod_{i\in I^{c}}(f_{i}^{-1})^{a_{i}},\quad a_{1},\ldots,a_{r}\in{\mathbb{N}}\cup\{0\},

an arbitrary element of
{\mathcal{O}}_{K,\lambda}^{\times}
.
Then

\displaystyle\lambda^{\alpha}x

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{i\in I}(\lambda^{s_{i}}f_{i})^{a_{i}}\times\prod_{i\in I^{c}}(\lambda^{t_{i}}f_{i}^{-1})^{a_{i}}\in{\mathcal{O}}_{K},\quad\alpha=\sum_{i\in I}s_{i}a_{i}+\sum_{i\in I^{c}}t_{i}a_{i},

\displaystyle\lambda^{\beta}x^{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{i\in I}(\lambda^{t_{i}}f_{i}^{-1})^{a_{i}}\times\prod_{i\in I^{c}}(\lambda^{s_{i}}f_{i})^{a_{i}}\in{\mathcal{O}}_{K},\quad\beta=\sum_{i\in I}t_{i}a_{i}+\sum_{i\in I^{c}}s_{i}a_{i}.

Denote
N=\prod_{p\,|(l,m)}p^{g_{p}}
, where
g_{p}={\min\{\operatorname{val}_{p}l,\operatorname{val}_{p}m\}}
as above. It suffices to check whether
T(\lambda^{\alpha}x)\in\operatorname{End}(G_{A})
(equivalently,

T(x)\in\operatorname{End}(G_{A})
) for finitely many values of
\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{r}\}
. Indeed, it is enough to consider each

a_{i}
,
i\in I
(resp.,
i\in I^{c}
), bounded by the order of the image of the set
\{(\lambda^{s_{i}}f_{i})^{b}\}_{b\in{\mathbb{N}}\cup\{0\}}
(resp.,
\{(\lambda^{t_{i}}f_{i}^{-1})^{b}\}
) in the finite ring
{\mathcal{O}}_{K}/N{\mathcal{O}}_{K}
. (As mentioned above, we do not address the effectiveness of the algorithm in this paper. However, to simplify computations – similarly to the case of
\operatorname{End}(G_{A})
discussed earlier – one could bound the degrees
a_{i}
’s by the corresponding orders in the quotient rings
{\mathcal{O}}_{K}/(p^{g_{p}}{\mathcal{O}}_{K})
, where
p
runs over the primes dividing
(l,m)
.) The results are
congruences for powers
a_{1},\ldots,a_{r}
. We repeat with
x^{-1}
,
i.e.
, whether

T(\lambda^{\beta}x^{-1})\in\operatorname{End}(G_{A})
(equivalently,

T(x^{-1})\in\operatorname{End}(G_{A})
) for finitely many values of
\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{r}\}
, where each

a_{i}
,
i\in I
(resp.,
i\in I^{c}
), is bounded by the order of the image of the set

\{(\lambda^{t_{i}}f_{i}^{-1})^{b}\}_{b\in{\mathbb{N}}\cup\{0\}}
(resp.,
\{(\lambda^{s_{i}}f_{i})^{b}\}
) in
{\mathcal{O}}_{K}/N{\mathcal{O}}_{K}
. Note that if some
f_{i}
is a unit in
{\mathcal{O}}_{K}
,
i.e.
,

f_{i}\in{\mathcal{O}}_{K}^{\times}
, then
s_{i}=t_{i}=0
and the image of
\{1,f_{i}^{\pm 1},f_{i}^{\pm 2},\ldots\}
in

is a (finite) group.
Thus, to check whether
T(\lambda^{\alpha}x),T(\lambda^{\beta}x)\in\operatorname{End}(G_{A})
, it is enough to assume that

a_{i}
is bounded by the order of
f_{i}
in
{\mathcal{O}}_{K}/N{\mathcal{O}}_{K}
.
The final step involves solving, for each power

,

, a system of at most two congruences when working over the ring

, and potentially more when considering the rings
{\mathcal{O}}_{K}/(p^{g_{p}}{\mathcal{O}}_{K})
. Solving a system of congruences is computable,
either by applying the Chinese Remainder Theorem when the moduli are coprime, or by using more general methods such as the extended Euclidean algorithm and verifying compatibility when the moduli are not coprime.

We used the following steps above, all of which are computable. In particular, decomposing the ideal
(\lambda)
into prime ideals (see
e.g.
,
[

]
), deciding if a product of prime ideals is principal and if yes, producing a generator (see

,
[

(see
e.g.
,
[
G80
, Algorithm
A_{12}
]
).

Remark 3.4
.

It follows from the above that under the conditions that the characteristic polynomial of
A
is irreducible and there exists a prime
p\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that
(n,t_{p})=1
, as a
{\mathbb{Z}}[A,A^{-1}]
-module,

is generated by elements of the centralizer of

, which gives another way to compute
\operatorname{End}(G_{A})

(equivalently,
the unit group of

The algorithms mentioned in the proof of Theorem

are implemented in
[

]
, and we use it together with
[
LMFDB
]
to produce examples. We demonstrate with two examples (

-dimensional and

-dimensional). One can find
2
-dimensional examples in
[

]
.

.

Let

-59&15&1\\
\end{matrix}\right),\quad B=\left(\begin{matrix}0&1&0\\

is a prime, and

, so that the hypotheses of Theorem

hold.
For a root
\lambda
of

,
it is known that

and
[{\mathcal{O}}_{K}:{\mathbb{Z}}[\lambda]]=2

]
. Thus, we can take

.

4.
Computability of
\operatorname{Isom}(G_{A},G_{B})

In this section, we assume that the characteristic polynomial
h_{A}\in{\mathbb{Z}}[t]
of
A\in\operatorname{M}_{n}({\mathbb{Z}})
is irreducible and there exists a prime
p\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that

are coprime
(denoted by
(n,t_{p})=1
), where

is the multiplicity of zero in the reduction of

{h}_{A}
modulo

. Let
B\in\operatorname{M}_{n}({\mathbb{Z}})
be non-singular. We show that there is an algorithm to find all (group) isomorphisms between
G_{A}
and
G_{B}
or to show that
G_{A}
,
G_{B}
are not isomorphic. This demonstrates that given the assumptions stated above, the question of whether
G_{A}
and
G_{B}
are isomorphic is decidable and the set of all isomorphisms between
G_{A}
and
G_{B}
is computable. By decidable, we mean that there exists an algorithm that terminates in finitely many steps. We do not, however, make any claims about the efficiency or practicality of this algorithm.

Remark 4.1
.

If

, then

if and only if

. Moreover, in that case,

and

. It follows from
[

, Lemma 3.6, Lemma 3.11]
that if

and

, then
G_{A}\cong G_{B}
if and only if
\det A
,

Remark 4.3
.

Let
A,B\in\operatorname{M}_{n}({\mathbb{Z}})
be non-singular,
{\mathcal{P}}^{\prime}(A)\neq\emptyset
. Assume in addition that
A
,
B
are conjugate in
\operatorname{GL}_{n}({\mathbb{Q}})
,
i.e.
, there exists
P\in\operatorname{GL}_{n}({\mathbb{Q}})
such that
B=PAP^{-1}
.

If
n=2
, the characteristic polynomial of
A
is irreducible or

A
has eigenvalues
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\in{\mathbb{Z}}
such that
\operatorname{rad}\lambda_{i}
does not divide
\operatorname{rad}\lambda_{j}
,
i,j\in\{1,2\}
,
i\neq j
(cases
(a), (b) in Section
3.3
above), then for any isomorphism
T
from
G_{A}
to
G_{B}
there exists
l\in{\mathbb{N}}\cup\{0\}
such that

B^{l}T\in\operatorname{M}_{2}({\mathbb{Z}})
, and the transpose
(B^{l}T)^{t}
of
B^{l}T
is an epimorphism of dimension groups from
\mathscr{D}_{A^{t}}
to
\mathscr{D}_{B^{t}}
as defined in
[
KR89
]
. Conversely, the transpose of any epimorphism from

to
\mathscr{D}_{B^{t}}
is an isomorphism from
G_{A}
to
G_{B}
(by
[
S22
, Lemma 6.5]
). Analogously, the same result holds
for an arbitrary
n
such that there exists
t_{p}=t_{p}(A)
satisfying
(n,t_{p})=1
and
h_{A}
is irreducible.

In the following examples, we illustrate how the procedure outlined in the preceding proof can be applied, using the same notation introduced there. Number field computations were performed using
[

]
and
[

]
.

h_{A}=x^{3}-6x^{2}+11x-7
,
h_{B}=x^{3}-2x^{2}-9x-7
, respectively. Both
h_{A}
,
h_{B}
are irreducible over
{\mathbb{Q}}
,
{\mathcal{P}}(A)={\mathcal{P}}(B)={\mathcal{P}}^{\prime}(A)={\mathcal{P}}^{\prime}(B)=\{7\}
, and
t_{7}(A)=t_{7}(B)=1
. Thus, Theorem
4.2
applies.
One can check that there exist a root
\lambda
of
h_{A}
and a root
\mu
of
h_{B}
such that
K={\mathbb{Q}}(\lambda)={\mathbb{Q}}(\mu)
, and

\lambda
,
\mu
have the same prime ideal divisors in
{\mathcal{O}}_{K}
. Let

{\bf u}(\lambda)=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda^{2}-6\lambda+11\\[2.0pt]
\lambda-6\\[2.0pt]
1\end{pmatrix},\quad{\bf v}(\mu)=\begin{pmatrix}\mu^{2}-2\mu-9\\[2.0pt]
\mu-2\\[2.0pt]
1\end{pmatrix}

be eigenvectors of
A
,
B
corresponding to eigenvalues
\lambda
,
\mu
, respectively. Then, in the notation of Theorem
4.2
,
{\mathcal{O}}_{K}={\mathbb{Z}}[\lambda]
,
NM^{-1}\in\operatorname{M}_{n}({\mathbb{Z}})
,
\det NM^{-1}=5
,
t=l_{1}=1
,
l_{2}=5
,

,
{\mathcal{O}}_{K}^{\times}
is generated by
-1
and

, the ideal
(\lambda)
is prime and

, where

{\mathfrak{p}}_{1}=(\alpha)
,

,

,

and

. Note that

and some of the entries of

4.1.
2
-dimensional case

In this section, we give a complete solution to the decidability problem of whether two groups
G_{A}
and
G_{B}

are isomorphic, and to the computability of

\operatorname{Isom}(G_{A},G_{B})
, in the two-dimensional case.

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
A,B\in\operatorname{M}_{2}({\mathbb{Z}})
be non-singular. The existence of an isomorphism between
G_{A}
and
G_{B}
is decidable and the set of all
isomorphisms between
G_{A}
and
G_{B}
is computable.

Proof.

By Remark
4.1
, we assume that

A\not\in\operatorname{GL}_{n}({\mathbb{Z}})
and
{\mathcal{P}}^{\prime}(A)\neq\emptyset
for the rest of the proof.
As in Section
3.3
,
if
n=2
, then there are three cases.
In the case when the characteristic polynomial
h_{A}\in{\mathbb{Z}}[x]
of
A
is irreducible (equivalently,
A
has no rational eigenvalues), the result follows from Theorem
4.2
.

Assume the case when
h_{A}
is reducible (equivalently,
A
has eigenvalues
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\in{\mathbb{Z}}
) and every prime dividing one eigenvalue divides the other,
e.g.
,

\operatorname{rad}(\lambda_{2})\,|\operatorname{rad}(\lambda_{1})
. By
[
S22
, Proposition 5.1]
, we have that
G_{A}\cong G_{B}
if and only if

B
has eigenvalues
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}\in{\mathbb{Z}}
such that
\operatorname{rad}\mu_{i}=\operatorname{rad}\lambda_{i}
,
i=1,2
. The condition is computable, hence
whether
G_{A}\cong G_{B}
is decidable. We now discuss how to find all isomorphisms from
G_{A}
to
G_{B}
. By
[
S22
, Remark 4.2, Proposition 5.1]
, there exist
S,P\in\operatorname{GL}_{2}({\mathbb{Z}})
such that

A=SM\left(\begin{matrix}\lambda_{1}&0\\
0&\lambda_{2}\end{matrix}\right)M^{-1}S^{-1},\,\,M=\left(\begin{matrix}1&u\\
0&v\end{matrix}\right),

B=PN\left(\begin{matrix}\mu_{1}&0\\
0&\mu_{2}\end{matrix}\right)N^{-1}P^{-1},\,\,N=\left(\begin{matrix}1&x\\
0&y\end{matrix}\right),

where
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},u,v\in{\mathbb{Z}}
,
v\,|\,(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2})
,
(u,v)=1
,

\mu_{1},\mu_{2},x,y\in{\mathbb{Z}}
,
y\,|\,(\mu_{1}-\mu_{2})
,

(x,y)=1
,
\operatorname{rad}\lambda_{i}=\operatorname{rad}\mu_{i}
,
i=1,2
,

(4.11)

T=T(\nu_{i})=PN\left(\begin{matrix}\nu_{1}&\nu_{2}\\
0&\nu_{3}\end{matrix}\right)M^{-1}S^{-1}\in\operatorname{GL}_{2}({\mathcal{R}}),\quad\nu_{1},\nu_{3}\in{\mathbb{Q}}-\{0\},\,\nu_{2}\in{\mathbb{Q}}.

(Note that the above decompositions of
A,B
are computable by Remark
3.8
.)
Since
B^{i}T\in\operatorname{M}_{n}({\mathbb{Z}})
for some
i\in{\mathbb{N}}\cup\{0\}
, without loss of generality, we can assume that
T
itself is integer. Moreover, there exists
j\in{\mathbb{N}}\cup\{0\}
such that

A^{j}T^{-1}\in\operatorname{M}_{n}({\mathbb{Z}})
. These imply that
\nu_{1}\in{\mathbb{Z}}
has the same prime divisors as
\lambda_{1}
,
\nu_{3}=kv/y
,

k\in{\mathbb{Z}}
,
k
has the same prime divisors as
\lambda_{2}
, and

(4.12)

-\frac{u}{v}\nu_{1}+\frac{x}{y}k+\frac{\nu_{2}}{v}\in{\mathbb{Z}}.

Furthermore, any
T
as above is an isomorphism from
G_{A}
to
G_{B}
.
Let

(4.13)

\displaystyle\nu_{1}=\prod_{\text{prime }p\,|\lambda_{1}}p^{r_{p}},\quad k=\prod_{\text{prime }p\,|\lambda_{2}}p^{s_{p}},\quad\forall r_{p},s_{p}\in{\mathbb{N}}\cup\{0\},

\displaystyle\nu_{3}=\frac{kv}{y},\quad\nu_{2}=mv-x\nu_{3}+u\nu_{1},\quad m\in{\mathbb{Z}},

by (
4.12
). Then
T\in\operatorname{Isom}(G_{A},G_{B})
if and only if
T=B^{j}T(\nu_{i})
, where
j\in{\mathbb{Z}}
,

T(\nu_{i})
is given by (
4.11
), and
\nu_{1},\nu_{2},\nu_{3}
are given by (
4.13
).

Assume the case when
h_{A}
is reducible,
\operatorname{rad}(\lambda_{1})
does not divide
\operatorname{rad}(\lambda_{2})
, and
\operatorname{rad}(\lambda_{2})
does not divide
\operatorname{rad}(\lambda_{1})
. The result follows from
[
S22
, Proposition 6.9]
, where we addressed the existence of at least one isomorphism from
G_{A}
to
G_{B}
. Now, we are interested in finding all of them. By
[
S22
, Proposition 6.9]
,

T\in\operatorname{Isom}(G_{A},G_{B})
if and only if
T=B^{j}T(\nu_{i})
, where
j\in{\mathbb{Z}}
,

T(\nu_{i})
is given by (
4.11
), and
\nu_{1}
,
\nu_{3}
are given by (
4.13
),
\nu_{2}=0
, and (
4.12
) holds,
i.e.
,

(4.14)

-\frac{u}{v}\nu_{1}+\frac{x}{y}k\in{\mathbb{Z}}.

Note that (

,
\{s_{q}\}_{q}
. Solving the system is computable and therefore whether
