Special issue of Medicinal Chemistry Research in honor of Dr. Kenneth A. Jacobson
Authors: Carola Gallo-Rodriguez, Juan Bautista Rodriguez, Pnina Fishman
Published: 2026

This Special Issue of Medicinal Chemistry Research is dedicated to Dr. Kenneth A. Jacobson in recognition of his over 40 years of pioneering research at the National Institute of Diabetes and Digestive and Kidney Diseases (NIDDK) at the National Institutes of Health (NIH) located in Bethesda, Maryland, USA. Dr. Jacobson is recognized for his transformative contributions to medicinal chemistry in deeply comprehending purinergic receptor chemistry and translational drug discovery.

Dr. Kenneth Jacobson received a B.A. from Reed College in 1975, followed by an M.S. in 1978 and a Ph.D. under Prof. Murray Goodman’s supervision in 1981 from the University of California, San Diego. He received a Bantrell Fellowship at the Weizmann Institute of Science, Department of Organic Chemistry to carry out postdoctoral studies for further strengthening his multidisciplinary academic career. He was appointed at the National Institutes of Health in 1983, afterward becoming a tenured Research Chemist in 1988, Section Chief since 1993, Senior Investigator since 2003, and Lab Chief (2008–2018). Currently Dr. Jacobson serves as the John W. Daly Distinguished Scientist and Chief of the Molecular Recognition Section in the Laboratory of Bioorganic Chemistry, NIDDK at the NIH.

Dr. Kenneth A. Jacobson has dedicated his research and training in deeply comprehending purinergic receptor chemistry and translational drug discovery. Widely recognized for his intellectual guidance, Dr. Jacobson has pioneered the design, synthesis, and pharmacological characterization of both adenosine and P2 receptor ligands [1]. In this context, Dr. Jacobson’s lab has developed numerous small-molecule agonists and antagonists with significant translational potential [2, 3]. He has made enormous contributions comprise the first molecular model of adenosine receptors in 1992 and first publication on modeling the role of the second extracellular loop of G protein-coupled receptors in small molecule recognition [4, 5]. In 1985, he introduced a conceptual approach to receptor ligand tools called “functionalized congeners” that has guided much of his subsequent research [6]. He introduced many of the first selective and high affinity agonists/antagonists, including fluorescent ligands and single affinity labels, for adenosine, P2Y, and P2X receptors and developed the first selective, competitive antagonists for A1, A2B, A3, P2Y1, and P2Y13 receptors and positive allosteric modulators for A3 and P2X1 receptors [1, 7]. Jacobson’s lab developed more than 50 compounds that are commercially available for research studies. Five biopharma startups were founded based on the therapeutic potential of compounds from the Jacobson lab. Two selective A3 adenosine agonists developed by Dr. Jacobson are in Phase II or III clinical trials for liver cancer, MASH, psoriasis, and other inflammatory diseases [2]. A third A3 agonist was licensed for pharma development for pain treatment [8]. Recently, a mixed A3/A1 agonist entered clinical trials for concussion and traumatic brain injury, fulfilling his therapeutic concept for neuroprotection from the 1990’s.3 Dr. Jacobson together with Ray Stevens from University of Southern California succeeded in resolving the first X-ray crystal structures of P2Y and agonist-bound A2A receptors.

Dr. Jacobson is a Fellow of the American Chemical Society and served as Chair of the Medicinal Chemistry Division. He mentored nearly 75 postdoctoral fellows throughout his career and authored more than 900 scientific publications (h-index of 126). He is inventor on more than 60 patents on new receptor ligands and analytical methods, filed in the United States and other countries, he was highlighted as a Featured NIDDK Innovator from the Office of Technology Transfer and Development.

He has received a substantial number of awards including the Medicinal Chemistry Hall of Fame (2009), Philip S. Portoghese Medicinal Chemistry Award (2012), Smissman Award (2017), and the E. B. Hershberg Award (2023) all of them from the American Chemical Society [1, 6]. Dr. Jacobson was the first scientist granted Tu Youyou Award (2017) sponsored by MDPI. Other awards embrace the Sato Memorial International Award (2009, Pharmaceutical Society of Japan), ASPET Goodman and Gilman Award in Drug Receptor Pharmacology (2014, American Society for Pharmacology and Experimental Therapeutics), and World’s Most Influential Scientific Minds, Pharmacology and Toxicology (2016, Thomson Reuters).

Overall, Dr. Jacobson’s career exemplifies the power of public-sector science to yield innovative therapeutics from first-principles chemistry. Many of his trainees have established their own productive, independent research labs. His lab’s work underscores how sustained basic research, combined with a translational vision, can guide compounds toward the clinic and ultimately help patients.
