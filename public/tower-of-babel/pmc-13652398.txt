Splenic hematoma following ERCP for obstructive jaundice requiring splenectomy: a case report and literature review
Authors: Branislav Oluic, Dragan Vasin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction and importance:Splenic injury following endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) is an exceptionally rare but potentially life-threatening complication.Case presentation:We report the case of a female patient admitted with obstructive jaundice and suspected periampullary malignancy associated with markedly elevated CA 19-9 levels. Following ERCP, abdominal computed tomography demonstrated a large subcapsular splenic hematoma. The patient underwent splenectomy, cholecystectomy, hepaticojejunostomy, and Roux-en-Y reconstruction. Histopathological examination confirmed traumatic splenic injury without evidence of malignancy. The postoperative course was complicated by pancreatic fistula, which was successfully managed conservatively.Clinical discussion:ERCP-related splenic injury remains a rare complication with nonspecific clinical manifestations that may delay diagnosis. A review of the literature demonstrates substantial variability in clinical presentation and management, ranging from conservative treatment to splenectomy.Conclusion:This case highlights the diagnostic challenge of ERCP-related splenic hematoma in patients undergoing evaluation for suspected pancreatic head malignancy. Early recognition, prompt cross-sectional imaging, and individualized multidisciplinary management are essential for achieving favorable outcomes. In selected patients, modification of the operative strategy according to intraoperative findings may be lifesaving.

Introduction

Endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) is an established therapeutic procedure for the management of biliary and pancreatic disorders. Although generally safe, it remains associated with complications, including pancreatitis, hemorrhage, cholangitis, and perforation, with an overall complication rate of approximately 5–10%[1,2].

Splenic injury following ERCP is an exceptionally rare but potentially life-threatening complication. Reported manifestations include splenic laceration, subcapsular hematoma, and splenic rupture[3,4]. Because clinical presentation is often nonspecific, diagnosis may be delayed, increasing the risk of morbidity and mortality. Since the first report by Trondsen et al in 1989, only a limited number of cases have been described in the literature[5].

The proposed mechanism involves excessive traction on the gastrosplenic ligaments during endoscope manipulation, particularly during technically demanding procedures. Additional predisposing factors include chronic pancreatitis, peripancreatic fibrosis, adhesions from previous abdominal surgery, and prolonged procedure duration[6,7].

We present a case of an ERCP-related subcapsular splenic hematoma requiring splenectomy in a patient with obstructive jaundice and suspected periampullary malignancy, together with a brief review of the current literature.

This case report is reported in accordance with the SCARE 2025 guidelines for surgical case reports[8].

Case presentation

A 67-year-old female with a history of cardiac arrhythmia treated with a permanent pacemaker was admitted to our tertiary hepatopancreatobiliary center because of progressive obstructive jaundice. Two days before admission, she had undergone ERCP at another hospital for distal common bile duct obstruction. A CT performed at the referring institution demonstrated a hypovascular pancreatic head mass causing distal biliary obstruction, raising strong suspicion of pancreatic head malignancy. According to the referring physicians, endoscopic biliary decompression was unsuccessful because of severe mechanical obstruction, and no biliary stent was successfully placed. Because persistent obstructive jaundice required further management beyond the technical capabilities of the referring institution, the patient was transferred to our center. The original ERCP report and serial laboratory results were unavailable; only CT images accompanied the referral. At presentation, she was hemodynamically stable but exhibited progressive jaundice and general malaise. She had no previous abdominal surgery or chronic pancreatitis.

Given the patient’s history of a recent ERCP performed for biliary obstruction, the splenic lesion was interpreted as an ERCP-related splenic injury.

At our institution, endoscopic biliary drainage with stent placement is the preferred first-line approach when preoperative biliary decompression is indicated before major pancreatic surgery. In the present case, however, the previous endoscopic attempt had failed to achieve adequate biliary decompression because of severe mechanical obstruction, and the patient remained markedly jaundiced. Considering the failed endoscopic drainage and the need to optimize the patient’s condition before major surgery, PTBD was selected as an alternative method to achieve effective biliary decompression.

Following completion of the diagnostic evaluation – which demonstrated a hypovascular pancreatic head mass along with markedly elevated CA 19-9 levels, raising strong suspicion of pancreatic head malignancy – and after biliary decompression with PTBD, the patient underwent surgical exploration with the intention of addressing the splenic lesion and the suspected pancreatic head malignancy.

Because the large subcapsular splenic hematoma had been demonstrated preoperatively, operative management of the splenic lesion was included in the surgical plan. Intraoperative assessment confirmed a large organizing subcapsular splenic hematoma with extensive capsular involvement (Fig. 3), and splenectomy was performed because of the considerable risk of secondary rupture. During the operation, significant hemodynamic instability of undetermined etiology developed. Given the increased operative risk, continuation with radical pancreatoduodenectomy was considered unsafe, and the procedure was therefore abandoned. Instead, splenectomy was performed because of the large organizing subcapsular hematoma and the considerable risk of secondary splenic rupture. Cholecystectomy, hepaticojejunostomy, and Roux-en-Y reconstruction were subsequently performed to achieve definitive biliary drainage. An intraoperative incisional biopsy of the pancreatic head lesion was performed, together with regional lymph-node sampling for histopathological evaluation, while radical pancreatic surgery was deferred until recovery from the acute episode.

Histopathological examination of the incisional pancreatic biopsy demonstrated chronic fibrous inflammatory changes without evidence of tumor tissue. Seven group 6 lymph nodes (0/7), one lymph node from the hepatoduodenal ligament (0/1), and one mesenteric lymph node (0/1) showed no evidence of malignancy. The sampled common bile duct wall showed minimal inflammatory changes without tumor infiltration. Histopathological examination of the spleen confirmed a mechanical capsular defect associated with a large subcapsular hematoma. Despite the absence of histological confirmation of malignancy, the combination of the hypovascular pancreatic head mass, distal biliary obstruction, obstructive jaundice, and markedly elevated serum CA 19-9 levels supported a strong clinical suspicion of pancreatic head malignancy.

Postoperatively, the patient was managed in the surgical intensive care unit. Although bilirubin levels gradually declined, postoperative laboratory analysis demonstrated persistent cholestatic liver enzyme elevation associated with a marked inflammatory response and leukocytosis. Postoperative laboratory findings are summarized in Supplemental Digital Content Table S2, available at: https://links.lww.com/IJSCR/A148.

The postoperative course was complicated by a large intra-abdominal fluid collection measuring 50 × 25 mm, as demonstrated on postoperative CT imaging. Ultrasound-guided percutaneous drainage was performed, yielding fluid with an amylase concentration of 31 647 U/L, thereby confirming the diagnosis of postoperative pancreatic fistula. Conservative management with continued percutaneous drainage resulted in progressive reduction of the collection and clinical improvement, allowing discharge in a stable condition.

During the approximately 2-month postoperative follow-up, the patient demonstrated progressive clinical recovery without recurrence of the intra-abdominal collection or documented late complications related to splenectomy or hepaticojejunostomy. Following recovery from the index operation, further diagnostic evaluation and potential surgical treatment of the pancreatic head lesion were proposed. However, because of the substantial physical burden and prolonged recovery following the previous major operation and its postoperative complications, the patient declined further diagnostic evaluation and another potential major surgical procedure. She subsequently did not return for further follow-up at our institution.

Discussion

Splenic injury after ERCP is a rare but potentially life-threatening complication that may be underrecognized because its symptoms often mimic more common post-procedural conditions[3,4]. According to Kourdakis et al, only 34 cases have been reported since the first description by Trondsen et al in 1989[3,5].

Clinical presentation is highly variable and may be delayed for several days after ERCP. Abdominal pain, anemia, tachycardia, hypotension, and left pleural effusion should raise suspicion of splenic injury[3–5,7].

Available evidence is limited to case reports and small case series, demonstrating considerable variability in clinical presentation and management. Representative published cases are summarized in Supplemental Digital Content Table S3, available at: https://links.lww.com/IJSCR/A149.

Review of the available literature demonstrates that splenectomy remains the predominant treatment modality in hemodynamically unstable patients or in cases involving extensive splenic injury. In contrast, conservative management and splenic artery embolization may be successfully applied in selected stable patients with lower-grade lesions[7,9–11]. Our patient shared several characteristics with surgically managed cases, particularly the presence of a large organizing hematoma and the need for definitive operative treatment.

Compared with previously published reports[1–7,9–15], our patient represents a particularly complex clinical scenario. In addition to ERCP-related splenic injury, she presented with obstructive jaundice caused by a pancreatic head mass that was highly suspicious for malignancy, requiring preoperative biliary decompression and planned radical pancreatic surgery. Intraoperative hemodynamic instability necessitated modification of the operative strategy, while postoperative pancreatic fistula further complicated the clinical course. The successful multidisciplinary management of these consecutive challenges distinguishes the present report from most previously published cases describing isolated splenic injury after ERCP. Furthermore, this case highlights the importance of individualized intraoperative decision-making when unexpected complications occur in patients undergoing surgery for suspected pancreatic head malignancy.

Unlike traumatic splenic rupture, ERCP-related splenic injury is usually caused by indirect mechanical forces rather than direct impact. Published reports suggest that excessive gastric looping and torsional stress during difficult cannulation may generate sufficient traction to disrupt splenic capsular integrity[3,15]. The “long position” of the endoscope with bowing within the stomach has repeatedly been implicated as the principal mechanism of injury. Furthermore, therapeutic interventions such as sphincterotomy and biliary or pancreatic stenting may increase the degree of endoscopic traction and manipulation[10]. Conditions associated with reduced splenic mobility, including chronic pancreatitis, prior abdominal surgery, adhesions, altered anatomy, and fibrosis, have also been proposed as major predisposing factors[3,9,10].

Although the original ERCP procedural report was unavailable because the procedure had been performed at another institution, the operative findings and the absence of chronic pancreatitis or previous abdominal surgery suggest that excessive traction transmitted through the gastrosplenic ligament during duodenoscope manipulation represents the most plausible mechanism of splenic injury in the present patient.

Our case was further complicated by suspected malignant biliary obstruction that required multidisciplinary management. Although endoscopic biliary stenting is our preferred approach when preoperative biliary decompression is clinically indicated, the need for drainage should be individualized according to the severity of the biliary obstruction and the patient’s clinical condition; percutaneous drainage remains an appropriate alternative when endoscopic decompression is unsuccessful or not feasible.

Another clinically significant aspect was the postoperative pancreatic fistula that developed following the splenectomy. Because of the close anatomical relationship between the splenic hilum and the pancreatic tail, pancreatic injury remains a recognized complication during splenic hilar dissection[16]. The diagnosis of postoperative pancreatic fistula was supported by a postoperative intra-abdominal fluid collection measuring 50 × 25 mm and an amylase concentration of 31 647 U/L in the drained fluid. However, no definite macroscopic pancreatic injury was documented intraoperatively in our patient, and therefore the exact mechanism responsible for the fistula remains uncertain. Intraoperative incisional biopsy of the pancreatic lesion and manipulation of the pancreatic tail during splenic hilar dissection represent possible mechanisms; however, neither can be established as the cause in the present case.

The patient was successfully managed conservatively using image-guided percutaneous drainage. Periodic catheter irrigation with sterile saline was performed to maintain drain patency and facilitate evacuation of the collection. This adjunctive measure reflected our institutional practice and should not be interpreted as a universally recommended treatment strategy.

The strength of this report lies in the detailed description of a rare ERCP-related splenic injury requiring splenectomy, further complicated by postoperative pancreatic fistula. However, this report describes a single patient, and conclusions regarding optimal management should be interpreted with caution. Larger studies are needed to better define diagnostic and therapeutic strategies for this uncommon complication.

Although the pathology differs substantially from ERCP-related splenic injury, the report by Andargie et al[17] similarly illustrates that successful management of uncommon abdominal surgical emergencies depends on early diagnosis, timely operative intervention, and multidisciplinary decision-making.

A limitation of the present report is that detailed procedural information regarding ERCP, including procedure duration, patient positioning, technical difficulty, and endoscope manipulation, was unavailable because the intervention had been performed at another institution.

Conclusion

ERCP-related splenic injury should be considered in patients who develop abdominal pain, anemia, hypotension, or unexplained clinical deterioration following ERCP. This case demonstrates that successful management requires early diagnosis, individualized surgical decision-making, and close multidisciplinary collaboration. Furthermore, unexpected intraoperative deterioration may necessitate modification of the planned operative strategy to maximize patient safety. Early diagnosis, individualized treatment selection, and multidisciplinary management are essential for achieving favorable outcomes. Splenectomy represents one effective treatment option for hemodynamically unstable patients or those with extensive splenic injury.
