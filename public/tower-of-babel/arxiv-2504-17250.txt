A bi-Lipschitz invariant for analytic function germs
Nguyen Xuan Viet Nhan

Abstract
. In this paper, we introduce a new bi-Lipschitz invariant for analytic function germs in two variables, enhancing the Henry-Parusinski invariant.



1991 Mathematics Subject Classification
14H15, 32S05, 32S15

1.
Introduction

Let
f,g:(\mathbb{C}^{2},0)\to(\mathbb{C},0)
be analytic germs. We call
f
and
g

bi-Lipschitz equivalent
if there exists a germ of a bi-Lipschitz homeomorphism
h:(\mathbb{C}^{2},0)\to(\mathbb{C}^{2},0)
such that
f=g\circ h
. This paper focuses on finding bi-Lipschitz invariants for Lipschitz equivalence, a central problem in Lipschitz geometry of singularities. Many significant results have been established in this area; see, for example,
[
8
]
,
[
3
]
,
[
4
]
,
[
1
]
,
[
7
]
, and
[
6
]
. Among these, the invariant introduced by Henry and Parusiński
[
3
,
4
]
is regarded as the most refined, as it demonstrates that bi-Lipschitz equivalence allows moduli. A typical example is the family of function germs defined by

f_{t}(x,y)=x^{3}-3t^{2}xy^{4}+y^{6}.

Using Henry-Parusiński’s invariant, one can deduce that for distinct values
t
and
t^{\prime}
(with
t
close to
t^{\prime}
) the function germs
f_{t}
and
f_{t^{\prime}}
belong to different bi-Lipschitz equivalence classes. This invariant is a powerful tool, however, as shown in
[
7
]
, it is not sufficient to fully characterize the bi-Lipschitz type.

In
[
2
]
, Fernandes and Ruas studied the following family of germs:

g_{t}(x,y)=x^{3}-3t^{2}xy^{10}+y^{12}.

They proved that Henry-Parusiński’s invariant is constant while this family is not strongly bi-Lipschitz equivalent, meaning there is no Lipschitz vector field that trivializes
g_{t}
. An interesting question is whether this family is bi-Lipschitz trivial.

In this paper, we introduce an improved version of Henry–Parusiński’s invariant that can effectively distinguish the bi-Lipschitz equivalence classes of
g_{t}
(see Example
3.1
). Our approach builds upon the recent work of Păunescu and Tibăr
[
7
]
on gradient canyons under bi-Lipschitz equivalence (see also
[
5
]
). This invariant also uncovers germs with Lipschitz modality
\geq 2
(Example 3.2), providing a significant step toward a comprehensive classification of Lipschitz unimodal germs.

Notation and Conventions:
We use
|\cdot|
to denote the Euclidean norm. To compare the asymptotic behavior of functions
\varphi
and
\psi
near 0, we employ the standard notations
\varphi=o(\psi)
and
\varphi=O(\psi)
. The notation
\varphi\sim\psi
means
\varphi=O(\psi)
and
\psi=O(\varphi)
. We define
\mathbb{C}^{*}=\mathbb{C}\setminus{0}
. For a function of the form
a_{1}y^{h_{1}}+\dots+a_{k}y^{h_{k}}+o(y^{h_{k}})
, where the
h_{i}
are rational, we often write
a_{1}y^{h_{1}}+\dots+a_{k}y^{h_{k}}+\text{h.o.t.}
(higher-order terms).

2.
Main result

2.1.
Păunescu–Tibăr’s results

In this section, we recall some results due to Păunescu and Tibăr in
[
7
]
, which will be used in the next section.

By an analytic arc we mean a convergent power series of the form

x=\alpha(y)=c_{1}y^{n_{1}/N}+c_{2}y^{n_{2}/N}+\cdots,c_{i}\in\mathbb{C}

where
N\leq n_{1}<n_{2}<\ldots
are positive integers having no common divisor. There are
N
conjugates of
\alpha
that are of the following forms:

\alpha_{conj}^{(j)}=\sum c_{i}\theta^{jn_{i}}y^{n_{i}/N},\hskip 14.22636pt

where
\theta
is an
N
-th root of the unity,
j=0,\ldots,N-1
.

We call
n_{1}/N
the order of
\alpha
, denoted by
{\rm ord}_{y}(\alpha)=n_{1}/N
.

We denote by
\alpha_{*}
the image of the map
t\mapsto(\alpha(t^{N}),t^{N})
. Note that
\alpha_{*}=\alpha_{conj\>*}^{(j)}
for every
0\leq j\leq N-1
.

Given two analytic arcs
\alpha
and
\beta
, the contact order between
\alpha
and
\beta
, and between
\alpha_{*}
and
\beta_{*}
are defined by

•

{\rm ord}_{0}(\alpha,\beta)={\rm ord}_{y}(\alpha(y)-\beta(y))

•

{\rm ord}_{0}(\alpha_{*},\beta_{*})=\max{\rm ord}_{y}(\tilde{\alpha}(y)-\tilde{\beta}(y))

where the maximum is taken over all conjugates
\tilde{\alpha}
of
\alpha
and
\tilde{\beta}
of
\beta
.

It is obvious that
{\rm ord}_{0}(\alpha,\beta)\leq{\rm ord}_{0}(\alpha_{*},\beta_{*})
.

Let
f:(\mathbb{C}^{2},0)\to(\mathbb{C},0)
be a germ of analytic function with Taylor expansion

f(x,y)=H_{k}(x,y)+H_{k+1}(x,y)+\ldots

where
H_{i}
is a homogeneous polynomial of degree
i
. We assume that
f
is
a regular
in
x
, meaning
H_{k}(1,0)\neq 0
. We also assume that
f
does not have multiple root.

A
polar arc
of
f
is an analytic arc
x=\alpha(y)
such that
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}(\alpha(y),y)=0
. A conjugate of
\alpha
is considered as
a distinct polar arc from
\alpha
if
\theta\neq 1
.

For a polar arc
\alpha
, the gradient degree
d_{{\rm gr}}(\alpha)
is defined as follows:

•

If
f(\alpha(y),y)\not\equiv 0
then
d_{{\rm gr}}(\alpha)
is the smallest number
q
such that

{\rm ord}_{y}(\|{\rm grad}f(\alpha(y),y)\|)={\rm ord}_{y}(\|{\rm grad}f(\alpha(y)+cy^{q},y)\|),

for a generic
c\in\mathbb{C}
.

•

If
f(\alpha(y),y)\equiv 0
, we set
d_{{\rm gr}}(\alpha)=+\infty
.

It is straightforward to verify that the gradient degree is invariant under conjugation, i.e.,
d_{{\rm gr}}(\alpha)=d_{{\rm gr}}(\alpha_{conj}^{(j)})
for all
j=0,\ldots,N-1
.

In
[
7
, Section 3.2]
a method was introduced to compute
d_{{\rm gr}}(\alpha)
using the Newton polygon.

Example 2.1
.

Let
f_{t}(x,y)=x^{3}-3t^{2}xy^{10}+y^{12}
.

Then

\frac{\partial f_{t}}{\partial x}=3x^{2}-3t^{2}y^{10}

\frac{\partial f_{t}}{\partial y}=-30t^{2}xy^{9}+12y^{11}

There are two polar arcs
x=\alpha(y)=ty^{5}
and
x=\beta(y)=-ty^{5}
. From definition, it is easy to check that

d_{{\rm gr}}(\alpha)=d_{{\rm gr}}(\beta)=11/2.

Let
\alpha
be a polar arc of
f
. The
gradient canyon
of
\alpha_{*}
is the set

\mathcal{GC}(\alpha_{*})=\{\beta_{*}:\beta(y)=\alpha(y)+cy^{d_{{\rm gr}}(\alpha)}+{\rm h.o.t},\hskip 8.5359ptc\in\mathbb{C}\},

and
d_{{\rm gr}}(\alpha_{*}):=d_{{\rm gr}}(\alpha)
is called
the degree of the canyon
.

We say that
\alpha
is tangential if
\alpha_{*}
is tangent to the singular locus
\Sigma_{f}
of the tangent cone of the zero locus of
f
, which occurs if and only if
d_{{\rm gr}}(\alpha)>1
.
Note that

\Sigma_{f}=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{C}^{2}:\frac{\partial H_{k}}{\partial x}=\frac{\partial H_{k}}{\partial y}=0\}.

Moreover, if
d_{{\rm gr}}(\alpha)>1
then
\mathcal{GC}(\alpha_{*})
is
minimal
, meaning that for any polar arc
\gamma
, if
\mathcal{GC}(\gamma_{*})\subset\mathcal{GC}(\alpha_{*})
then
\mathcal{GC}(\gamma_{*})=\mathcal{GC}(\alpha_{*})
(see
[
7
, Theorem 2.4]
).

For an analytic arc
\gamma_{*}
, and positive real numbers
e\geq 1
,
\rho
, and
\epsilon\geq 0
, we define

{\rm Horn}^{(e)}(\gamma_{*},\rho,\epsilon)=\bigcup_{\beta}\{\beta_{*}\cap B(0,\epsilon)|\beta(y)=J^{e}(\gamma)(y)+cy^{e},|c|\leq\rho\}

where
J^{e}(\gamma)
is the truncation of
\gamma
up to power
e
.

Set

\mathcal{D}^{(e)}_{\gamma_{*},\rho}(\lambda,\epsilon)=\{f=\lambda\}\cap{\rm Horn}^{(e)}(\gamma_{*},\rho,\epsilon),

for
0<\lambda\ll\epsilon
.

Theorem 2.2

(
[
7
, Section 5.2]
)
.

Let
f,g:(\mathbb{C}^{2},0)\rightarrow(\mathbb{C},0)
be analytic function germs that are mini-regular in
x
and have no multiple roots. Suppose that there exists a bi-Lipschitz homeomorphic germ
\varphi:(\mathbb{C}^{2},0)\rightarrow(\mathbb{C}^{2},0)
such that
f=g\circ\varphi
. Let
\alpha
be a polar arc of
f
with
d=d_{{\rm gr}}(\alpha)>1
. Then, there exists a polar arc
\alpha^{\prime}
of
g
satisfying the following conditions:

(1)

d_{{\rm gr}}(\alpha^{\prime})=d_{{\rm gr}}(\alpha)=d

(2)

Moreover, given
\epsilon
sufficiently small and
\rho>0
, there are
0<\rho_{1}<\rho_{2}
such that

\mathcal{D}^{(d)}_{\alpha^{\prime}_{*},\rho_{1}}(\lambda,\epsilon)\subset\varphi\left(\mathcal{D}^{(d)}_{\alpha_{*},\rho}(\lambda,\epsilon)\right)\subset\mathcal{D}^{(d)}_{\alpha^{\prime}_{*},\rho_{2}}(\lambda,\epsilon)

for all
0<\lambda\ll\epsilon
.

2.2.
New invariant

Let us begin by explaining how we arrive at the new invariant. The formal statement will be presented at the end of the section.

Let
f,g:(\mathbb{C}^{2},0)\to(\mathbb{C},0)
be given as in Theorem
2.2
. Let

\alpha
and
\beta
polar arcs of
f
satisfying

(1)

{\rm ord}_{0}(\alpha,\beta)>1

(2)

\mathcal{GC}(\alpha_{*})\neq\mathcal{GC}(\beta_{*})

The condition (1) implies that
\alpha
and
\beta
are tangential, so there is a line
L\subset\Sigma_{f}
such that
\alpha_{*}
and
\beta_{*}
are both tangent to
L
.

We write

(2.1)

f(\alpha(y),y)=a_{0}y^{h_{0}}+a_{1}y^{h_{1}}+\dots+O(y^{h_{0}+d-1})

and

(2.2)

f(\beta(y),y)=b_{0}y^{l_{0}}+b_{1}y^{l_{1}}+\dots+O(y^{l_{0}+d^{\prime}-1})

where
a_{0},b_{0}\neq 0
,
d=d_{{\rm gr}}(\alpha)
and
d^{\prime}=d_{{\rm gr}}(\beta)
.

Remark 2.3
.

If
f(\alpha(y),y)=ay^{h}+{\rm h.o.t}
, then for any
\gamma=\alpha(y)+ty^{m}+{\rm h.o.t}
with
m>{\rm ord}_{y}(\alpha)
, we have

f(\gamma(y),y)=ay^{h}+\dots+u(t)y^{h+m-1}+{\rm h.o.t},

where
u(t)
is the first term that depends on
t
. The expansion of
f(\gamma(y),y)
can be written as

f(\gamma(y),y)=ay^{h}+\dots+O(y^{h+m-1}).

Note the latter expression is independent of perturbations of
\alpha
of order
m
.

It follows from Theorem
2.2
that there are polar arcs
\alpha^{\prime}
and
\beta^{\prime}
of
g
such that

(i)

d_{{\rm gr}}(\alpha^{\prime})=d_{{\rm gr}}(\alpha)
,
d_{{\rm gr}}(\beta^{\prime})=d_{{\rm gr}}(\beta)

(ii)

{\rm ord}_{0}(\alpha_{*},\beta_{*})={\rm ord}(\alpha^{\prime}_{*},\beta_{*}^{\prime})

(iii)

\varphi(\alpha_{*})\subset{\rm Horn}^{(d)}(\alpha^{\prime}_{*},\rho,\epsilon)
and
\varphi(\beta_{*})\subset{\rm Horn}^{(d^{\prime})}(\alpha^{\prime}_{*},\rho,\epsilon)
as germs at the origin for some
\rho>0
and
\epsilon>0
.

We may assume that

(2.3)

g(\alpha^{\prime}(y),y)=a^{\prime}_{0}y^{h^{\prime}_{0}}+a^{\prime}_{1}y^{h^{\prime}_{1}}+\ldots+O(y^{h^{\prime}_{0}+d-1})

and

(2.4)

g(\beta^{\prime}(y),y)=b^{\prime}_{0}y^{l^{\prime}_{0}}+b^{\prime}_{1}y^{l^{\prime}_{1}}+\ldots+O(y^{l^{\prime}_{0}+d^{\prime}-1})

where
a^{\prime}_{0},b^{\prime}_{0}\neq 0
.

Consider a sequence
(y_{n})\subset\mathbb{C}
with
y_{n}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.
From (iii), passing a subsequence if necessary, we can write

(2.5)

\varphi(\alpha(y_{n}),y_{n})=(\alpha^{\prime}(Y_{n})+O(Y_{n}^{d}),Y_{n})

and

(2.6)

\varphi(\beta(y_{n}),y_{n})=(\beta^{\prime}(\tilde{Y}_{n})+O(\tilde{Y}_{n}^{d^{\prime}}),\tilde{Y}_{n})

where
(Y_{n})
and
(\tilde{Y}_{n})
are sequences in a local coordinate.
From Remark
2.3
, it yields that

(2.7)

(g\circ\varphi)(\alpha(y_{n}),y_{n})=a^{\prime}_{0}Y_{n}^{h^{\prime}_{0}}+a^{\prime}_{1}Y_{n}^{h^{\prime}_{1}}+\dots+O(Y_{n}^{h^{\prime}_{0}+d-1})

and

(2.8)

(g\circ\varphi)(\beta(y_{n}),y_{n})=b^{\prime}_{0}\tilde{Y}_{n}^{l^{\prime}_{0}}+b^{\prime}_{1}\tilde{Y}_{n}^{l^{\prime}_{1}}+\dots+O(\tilde{Y}_{n}^{l^{\prime}_{0}+d^{\prime}-1}).

Observe that in a neighbourhood of the origin
|y_{n}|\sim|(\alpha(y_{n}),y_{n})|\sim|\varphi((\alpha(y_{n}),y_{n})|\sim|Y_{n}|
and

|y_{n}|\sim|(\beta(y_{n}),y_{n})|\sim|\varphi((\beta(y_{n}),y_{n})|\sim|\tilde{Y}_{n}|
.
Therefore,

|y_{n}|\sim|Y_{n}|\sim|\tilde{Y}_{n}|.

By
f=g\circ\varphi
, we have

(2.9)

\begin{cases}a_{0}y_{n}^{h_{0}}+a_{1}y_{n}^{h_{1}}+\ldots+O(y_{n}^{h_{0}+d-1})=a^{\prime}_{0}Y_{n}^{h^{\prime}_{0}}+a^{\prime}_{1}Y_{n}^{h^{\prime}_{1}}+\dots+O(Y_{n}^{h^{\prime}_{0}+d-1}),\\
b_{0}y_{n}^{l_{0}}+b_{1}y_{n}^{h^{\prime}_{1}}+\ldots+O(y_{n}^{l_{0}+d^{\prime}-1})=b^{\prime}_{0}Y_{2}^{l^{\prime}_{0}}+a^{\prime}_{1}\tilde{Y}_{n}^{l^{\prime}_{1}}+\dots+O(\tilde{Y}_{n}^{l^{\prime}_{0}+d^{\prime}-1})\end{cases}

Since
|y_{n}|\sim|Y_{n}|\sim|\tilde{Y}_{n}|
, it yields that
h_{0}=h^{\prime}_{0}
and
l_{0}=l^{\prime}_{0}
. We rewrite (
2.9
) as follows:

(2.10)

\begin{cases}a_{0}y_{n}^{h_{0}}+a_{1}y_{n}^{h_{1}}+\ldots+O(y_{n}^{h_{0}+d-1})=a^{\prime}_{0}Y_{n}^{h_{0}}+a^{\prime}_{1}Y_{n}^{h^{\prime}_{1}}+\dots+O(Y_{n}^{h_{0}+d-1}),\\
b_{0}y_{n}^{l_{0}}+b_{1}y_{n}^{h^{\prime}_{1}}+\ldots+O(y_{n}^{l_{0}+d^{\prime}-1})=b^{\prime}_{0}\tilde{Y}_{n}^{l_{0}}+a^{\prime}_{1}\tilde{Y}_{n}^{l^{\prime}_{1}}+\dots+O(\tilde{Y}_{n}^{l_{0}+d^{\prime}-1})\end{cases}

This implies that

(2.11)

\begin{cases}\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(\frac{y_{n}}{Y_{n}}\right)^{h_{0}}=\frac{a^{\prime}_{0}}{a_{0}}\\
\lim_{y\to 0}\left(\frac{y}{\tilde{Y}}\right)^{l_{0}}=\frac{b^{\prime}_{0}}{b_{0}}.\end{cases}

Since
\varphi
is bi-Lipschitz,

(2.12)

|\varphi(\alpha(y),y)-\varphi(\beta(y),y))|\sim|(\alpha(y),y)-(\beta(y),y)|=|\alpha(y)-\beta(y)|\sim|y|^{{\rm ord}_{0}(\alpha,\beta)}.

Note that
{\rm ord}_{0}(\alpha,\beta)>1
, yielding that

|\varphi(\alpha(y_{n}),y_{n})-\varphi(\beta(y_{n}),y_{n}))|\sim|y_{n}|^{{\rm ord}_{0}(\alpha,\beta)}=o(y_{n}).

It follows from (
2.5
) and (
2.6
),
|Y_{n}-\tilde{Y}_{n}|=o(y_{n})=o(Y_{n})
. Alternatively, we write

(2.13)

\tilde{Y}_{n}=Y_{n}+o(Y_{n}).

Passing a subsequence, we may assume that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{y_{n}}{Y_{n}}
and
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{y_{n}}{\tilde{Y}_{n}}
exist. Then, by (
2.13
) there must be an
c\in\mathbb{C}
such that

c=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{y_{n}}{Y_{n}}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{y_{n}}{\tilde{Y}_{n}}
. Hence, from (
2.11
) we have that

(2.14)

c^{h_{0}}=\frac{a_{0}^{\prime}}{a_{0}}\text{ and }c^{l_{0}}=\frac{b_{0}^{\prime}}{b_{0}}.

Indeed,
c
is the constant appearing in the Henry-Parusiński invariant
[
3
]
. It is solely determined by the tangent line
L
and is independent of the specific choices of
\alpha,\alpha^{\prime},\beta,\beta^{\prime}
.

Let us now consider the case that
h_{0}=l_{0}
. Then,

\frac{a^{\prime}_{0}}{a_{0}}=\frac{b^{\prime}_{0}}{b_{0}}.

Set
\delta={\rm ord}(\alpha,\beta)
. Note that
\delta>1
and
\delta\leq{\rm ord}(\alpha_{*},\beta_{*})
. As in (
2.12
),

|\varphi(\alpha(y),y)-\varphi(\beta(y),y)|\sim|y|^{\delta}.

By definition,
|Y_{n}-\tilde{Y}_{n}|\leq|\varphi(\alpha(y_{n}),y_{n})-\varphi(\beta(y_{n}),y_{n})|\sim|y_{n}|^{\delta}
. Thus,

|Y_{n}-\tilde{Y}_{n}|=O(y_{n}^{\delta})=O(Y_{n}^{\delta}).

We then write

\tilde{Y}_{n}=Y_{n}+O(Y_{n}^{\delta}).

Since
\mathcal{GC}(\alpha_{*})\neq\mathcal{GC}(\beta_{*})
, so
\delta<\min\{d,d^{\prime}\}
. It follows that

(2.15)

b^{\prime}_{0}\tilde{Y}_{n}^{l_{0}}+a^{\prime}_{1}\tilde{Y}_{n}^{l^{\prime}_{1}}+\dots+O(\tilde{Y}_{n}^{l_{0}+d^{\prime}-1})=b^{\prime}_{0}Y_{n}^{h_{0}}+a^{\prime}_{1}Y_{n}^{l^{\prime}_{1}}+\dots+O(Y_{n}^{h_{0}+\delta-1})

Combining with
2.10
, we obtain

(2.16)

\begin{cases}a_{0}y_{n}^{h_{0}}+a_{1}y_{n}^{h_{1}}+\ldots+O(y_{n}^{h_{0}+\delta-1})=a^{\prime}_{0}Y_{n}^{h_{0}}+a^{\prime}_{1}Y_{n}^{h^{\prime}_{1}}+\dots+O(Y_{n}^{h_{0}+\delta-1}),\\
b_{0}y_{n}^{h_{0}}+b_{1}y_{n}^{h^{\prime}_{1}}+\ldots+O(y_{n}^{h_{0}+\delta-1})=b^{\prime}_{0}Y_{n}^{h_{0}}+b^{\prime}_{1}Y_{n}^{l^{\prime}_{1}}+\dots+O(Y_{n}^{h_{0}+\delta-1})\end{cases}

Equivalently,

(2.17)

\begin{cases}y_{n}^{h_{0}}+\frac{a_{1}}{a_{0}}y_{n}^{h_{1}}+\ldots+O(y_{n}^{h_{0}+\delta-1})=\frac{a^{\prime}_{0}}{a_{0}}Y_{n}^{h_{0}}+\frac{a^{\prime}_{1}}{a_{0}}Y_{n}^{h^{\prime}_{1}}+\dots+O(Y_{n}^{h_{0}+\delta-1}),\\
y_{n}^{h_{0}}+\frac{b_{1}}{b_{0}}y_{n}^{h^{\prime}_{1}}+\ldots+O(y_{n}^{h_{0}+\delta-1})=\frac{b^{\prime}_{0}}{b_{0}}Y_{n}^{h_{0}}+\frac{b^{\prime}_{1}}{b_{0}}Y_{n}^{l^{\prime}_{1}}+\dots+O(Y_{n}^{h_{0}+\delta-1})\end{cases}

By
\frac{a^{\prime}_{0}}{a_{0}}=\frac{b^{\prime}_{0}}{b_{0}}
, we have

(2.18)

\begin{cases}y_{n}^{h_{0}}+\frac{a_{1}}{a_{0}}y_{n}^{h_{1}}+\ldots+O(y_{n}^{h_{0}+\delta-1})=\frac{a^{\prime}_{0}}{a_{0}}Y_{n}^{h_{0}}+\frac{a^{\prime}_{1}}{a_{0}}Y_{n}^{h^{\prime}_{1}}+\dots+O(Y_{n}^{h_{0}+\delta-1}),\\
y_{n}^{h_{0}}+\frac{b_{1}}{b_{0}}y_{n}^{h^{\prime}_{1}}+\ldots+O(y_{n}^{h_{0}+\delta-1})=\frac{a^{\prime}_{0}}{a_{0}}Y_{n}^{h_{0}}+\frac{b^{\prime}_{1}}{b_{0}}Y_{n}^{l^{\prime}_{1}}+\dots+O(Y_{n}^{h_{0}+\delta-1})\end{cases}

Now, define
\tilde{h}
as follows:

\tilde{h}=\begin{cases}\min\left\{h_{i}\mid\frac{a_{i}}{a_{0}}\neq\frac{b_{i}}{b_{0}}\right\},&\text{if such an index }i\text{ exists};\\
h_{0}+\delta-1,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Similarly, define
\tilde{h}^{\prime}
as

\tilde{h}^{\prime}=\begin{cases}\min\left\{h^{\prime}_{i}\mid\frac{a^{\prime}_{i}}{a_{0}}\neq\frac{b^{\prime}_{i}}{b_{0}}\right\},&\text{if such an index }i\text{ exists};\\
h_{0}+\delta-1,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Lemma 2.4
.

\tilde{h}=\tilde{h}^{\prime}
.

Proof.

If
\tilde{h}<h_{0}+\delta-1
and
\tilde{h}^{\prime}=h_{0}+\delta-1
, we can rewrite equations in
2.18
as the following forms

(2.19)

A+\tilde{a}y_{n}^{\tilde{h}}+{\rm h.o.t}=\frac{a^{\prime}_{0}}{a_{0}}B+O(Y_{n}^{h_{0}+\delta-1})

and

(2.20)

A+\tilde{b}y_{n}^{\tilde{h}}+{\rm h.o.t}=\frac{a^{\prime}_{0}}{a_{0}}B+O(Y_{n}^{h_{0}+\delta-1})

where
\tilde{a}\neq\tilde{b}
.
Subtracting side by side (
2.20
) by (
2.19
) we obtain

(2.21)

(\tilde{a}-\tilde{b})y_{n}^{\tilde{h}}+{\rm h.o.t}=O(Y_{n}^{h_{0}+\delta-1})

This is impossible since
|y_{n}|\sim|Y_{n}|
and
\tilde{h}<h_{0}+\delta-1
. Therefore, we can assume that both
\tilde{h}
and
\tilde{h}^{\prime}
are strictly less than
h_{0}+\delta-1
. Then, equations in
2.18
can be written as:

(2.22)

A+\tilde{a}y_{n}^{\tilde{h}}+{\rm h.o.t}=\frac{a^{\prime}_{0}}{a_{0}}(B+\tilde{a}^{\prime}Y_{n}^{\tilde{h}^{\prime}})+{\rm h.o.t}

and

(2.23)

A+\tilde{b}y_{n}^{\tilde{h}}+{\rm h.o.t}=\frac{a^{\prime}_{0}}{a_{0}}(B+\tilde{b}^{\prime}Y_{n}^{\tilde{h}^{\prime}})+{\rm h.o.t}

where
\tilde{a}\neq\tilde{b}
and
\tilde{a}^{\prime}\neq\tilde{b}^{\prime}
.
Substracting side-by-side the above equations we get

(2.24)

(\tilde{a}-\tilde{b})y_{n}^{\tilde{h}}+{\rm h.o.t}=\frac{a^{\prime}_{0}}{a_{0}}(\tilde{a}^{\prime}-\tilde{b}^{\prime})Y_{n}^{\tilde{h}^{\prime}}+{\rm h.o.t}

Since
|y_{n}|\sim|Y_{n}|
, it follows that
\tilde{h}=\tilde{h}^{\prime}
. The lemma is proved.
∎

Now, suppose that
\tilde{h}<h_{0}+\delta-1
. By Lemma
2.4
, we have
\tilde{h}^{\prime}=\tilde{h}
. Then, Equation (
2.24
) becomes:

(2.25)

(\tilde{a}-\tilde{b})y_{n}^{\tilde{h}}+{\rm h.o.t}=\frac{a^{\prime}_{0}}{a_{0}}(\tilde{a}^{\prime}-\tilde{b}^{\prime})Y_{n}^{\tilde{h}}+{\rm h.o.t}

It follows that

(2.26)

\frac{\tilde{a}^{\prime}-\tilde{b}^{\prime}}{\tilde{a}-\tilde{b}}=\frac{a_{0}}{a^{\prime}_{0}}\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(\frac{y_{n}}{Y_{n}}\right)^{\tilde{h}}=c^{\tilde{h}-h_{0}}

where
c=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{y_{n}}{Y_{n}}
as in (
2.14
).

We associate the pair of polar arcs
(\alpha,\beta)
with the following numerical values:

(2.27)

m(\alpha,\beta)=\tilde{h}

and

(2.28)

\nu(\alpha,\beta)=\begin{cases}\tilde{a}-\tilde{b},&\text{if }\tilde{h}<h_{0}+\delta-1,\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Here,
\tilde{a}
and
\tilde{b}
are the coefficients of
y^{\tilde{h}}
in the expansions of
f(\alpha(y),y)/a_{0}
and
f(\beta(y),y)/b_{0}
respectively (see the left-hand sides of (
2.18
)).

Note that
m(\alpha,\beta)=m(\beta,\alpha)
and
\nu(\alpha,\beta)=-\nu(\beta,\alpha)
. These functions define a new bi-Lipschitz invariant, summarized as follows.

Let
f:(\mathbb{C}^{2},0)\to(\mathbb{C},0)
be an analytic function germ that is mini-regular in
x
and has no multiple root. For a polar arc
\gamma:x=\gamma(y)
of
f
, we associate to
\gamma
to numbers
a_{0}(\gamma)
and
h_{0}(\gamma)
given by the expansion

f(\alpha(y),y)=a_{0}(\gamma)y^{h_{0}(\gamma)}+{\rm h.o.t}

where
a_{0}(\gamma)\neq 0
.

For a line
L\subset\Sigma_{f}
, we denote by
\Gamma(L)
the set of all polar arc whose image tangent to
L
and by
\Lambda(L)
the set of all pairs
(\alpha,\beta)
of polar arcs of
f
satisfying the following conditions:

(1)

\alpha_{*}
and
\beta_{*}
are tangent to
L
at the origin

(2)

\mathcal{GC}(\alpha_{*})\neq\mathcal{GC}(\beta_{*})

(3)

h_{0}(\alpha)=h_{0}(\beta)

(4)

m(\alpha,\beta)<h_{0}(\alpha)+{\rm ord}_{0}(\alpha,\beta)-1

where
m(\alpha,\beta)
is defined as in (
2.27
).
Set

\Delta(L)=\{a_{0}(\gamma)y^{h_{0}(\gamma)};(h_{0}(\alpha),\nu(\alpha,\beta)y^{m(\alpha,\beta)}):\gamma\in\Gamma(L),(\alpha,\beta)\in\Lambda(L)\}/\mathbb{C}^{*}

where
\nu(\alpha,\beta)
is defined as in (
2.28
) and the action of
\mathbb{C}^{*}
is given by:

\displaystyle\{a_{01}y^{h_{01}},\ldots,a_{0k}y^{h_{0k}};(l_{01},\nu_{1}y^{m_{1}}),\ldots,(l_{0n},\nu_{n}y^{m_{n}})\}\sim

\displaystyle\{a_{01}c^{h_{01}}y^{h_{01}},\ldots,a_{0k}c^{h_{0k}}y^{h_{0k}};(l_{01},\nu_{1}c^{m_{1}-l_{01}}y^{m_{1}}),\ldots,(l_{0n},\nu_{n}c^{m_{n}-l_{0n}}y^{m_{n}})\}.

We define
\mathbf{\rm Inv}^{2}(f)
as the set of all
\Delta(L)
for
L\in\Sigma_{f}
. From (
2.14
) and (
2.26
) we obtain

Theorem 2.5
.

Let
f,g:(\mathbb{C}^{2},0)\to(\mathbb{C},0)
be analytic germs that are mini-regular in
x
and have no multiple roots. If
f
and
g
are bi-Lipschitz equivalent then
\mathbf{\rm Inv}^{2}(f)=\mathbf{\rm Inv}^{2}(g)
.

3.
Examples

Consider an analytic family of germs
f_{t}(x,y):(\mathbb{C}^{2},0)\to(\mathbb{C},0)
,
t\in U
, where
U
is an open set in
\mathbb{C}^{k}
. We say that
f_{t_{1}}
and
f_{t_{2}}
are not
bi-Lipschitz equivalent for
t_{1},t_{2}
generic
if there exists a dense subset
U^{\prime}
of
U
such that for every
t_{1},t_{2}
in
U^{\prime}
and
t_{1}\neq t_{2}
,
f_{t_{1}}
is not bi-Lipschitz equivalent to
f_{t_{2}}
.

Example 3.1
.

Consider the family of function germs
f(x,y)=f_{t}(x,y)=x^{3}-3t^{2}xy^{10}+y^{12}
. Then,
f_{t_{1}}
and
f_{t_{2}}
are not bi-Lipschitz equivalent for
t_{1},t_{2}
generic.

Proof.

Consider
f_{t}
with
t\neq 0
. We have

\frac{\partial f_{t}}{\partial x}(x,y)=3x^{2}-3t^{2}y^{10}

and

\frac{\partial f_{t}}{\partial y}=-30t^{2}xy^{9}+12y^{11}.

There are two polar arcs
\alpha:x=ty^{5}
,
\beta:x=-ty^{5}
.
And,

f_{t}(\alpha(y),y)=y^{12}-2t^{3}y^{15}

and

f_{t}(\beta(y),y)=y^{12}+2t^{3}y^{15}.

It is easy to check that
d_{{\rm gr}}(\alpha)=d_{{\rm gr}}(\beta)=11/2
,
{\rm ord}_{0}(\alpha,\beta)=5<11/2
. This implies that
\alpha_{*}
and
\beta_{*}
are in different gradient canyons of
f_{t}
.
In addition,
h_{0}(\alpha)=h_{0}(\beta)=12
and
m(\alpha,\beta)=15<h_{0}(\alpha)+{\rm ord}_{0}(\alpha,\beta)-1=16
;
\nu(\alpha,\beta)=-4t^{3}
and
\nu(\beta,\alpha)=4t^{3}
.
Then,

\mathbf{\rm Inv}^{2}(f_{t})=\{y^{12},y^{12};(12,-4t^{3}y^{15}),(12,4t^{3}y^{15})\}/\mathbb{C}^{*}.

It follows that if
f_{t_{1}}
and
f_{t_{2}}
are bi-Lipschitz equivalent then there is a constant
c\in\mathbb{C}
such that

\begin{cases}c^{12}=1\\
c^{3}\in\left\{\frac{t_{2}^{3}}{t_{1}^{3}},-\frac{t_{2}^{3}}{t_{1}^{3}}\right\}\end{cases}

For
t_{1},t_{2}
generic, this is obviously impossible.
∎

Example 3.2
.

Let
f(x,y)=f_{b,c}(x,y)=x^{3}+bx^{2}y^{3}+y^{9}+cxy^{7}
where
b,c\in\mathbb{C}
are parameters. Then,
f_{b_{1},c_{1}}
are not bi-Lipschitz equivalent to
f_{b_{2},c_{2}}
for
(b_{1},c_{1})
and
(b_{2},c_{2})
generic. Consequently,
f_{b,c}
has Lipschitz modality at least
2
.

Proof.

We have

\partial f/\partial x=3x^{2}+2bxy^{3}+cy^{7},

\partial f/\partial y=3bx^{2}y^{2}+9y^{8}+7cxy^{6}.

Consider the set of all
(b,c)\in\mathbb{C}^{2}
satisfying
b\neq 0
and
\frac{4}{27}b^{3}+1\neq 0
and
c\neq 0
. Using Puiseux’s method we find that there are two polar arcs:

\alpha:x=-\frac{2b}{3}y^{3}+\frac{c}{2b}y^{4}+o(y^{4})\text{ and }\beta:x=-\frac{c}{2b}y^{4}+o(y^{4})

Computation shows

f(\alpha(y),y)=\left(\frac{4}{27}b^{3}+1\right)y^{9}-\frac{2bc}{3}y^{10}+O(y^{11})

f(\beta(y),y)=y^{9}+O(y^{11}),

h_{0}(\alpha)=h_{0}(\beta)=9,

d_{{\rm gr}}(\alpha)=d_{{\rm gr}}(\beta)=5,

{\rm ord}_{0}(\alpha,\beta)=3,

m(\alpha,\beta)=10<h_{0}(\alpha)+{\rm ord}_{0}(\alpha,\beta)-1=11,

\nu(\alpha,\beta)=\frac{-2bc}{3(\frac{4}{27}b^{3}+1)}=\frac{-18bc}{4b^{3}+27}.

Then, we have

\mathbf{\rm Inv}^{2}(f_{b,c})=\{(\frac{4}{27}b^{3}+1)y^{9},y^{9};(9,\frac{-18bc}{4b^{3}+27}y^{10}),(9,\frac{18bc}{4b^{3}+27})y^{10}\}/\mathbb{C}^{*}.

This implies that if
f_{b_{1},c_{1}}
and
f_{b_{2},c_{2}}
are bi-Lipschitz equivalent then there is a constant
u\in\mathbb{C}^{*}
such that

u^{9}\in\bigg\{\frac{\frac{4}{27}b_{2}^{3}+1}{\frac{4}{27}b_{1}^{3}+1},\frac{1}{\frac{4}{27}b_{1}^{3}+1}\bigg\}

and

u\in\bigg\{\frac{b_{2}c_{2}(4b_{1}^{3}+27)}{b_{1}c_{1}(4b_{2}^{3}+27)},\frac{-b_{2}c_{2}(4b_{1}^{3}+27)}{b_{1}c_{1}(4b_{2}^{3}+27)}\bigg\}.

This is obviously impossible for
(b_{1},c_{1})
and
(b_{2},c_{2})
generic. This ends the proof.
∎
