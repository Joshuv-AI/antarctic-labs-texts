The effect of ethyl methanesulfonate (EMS) and environmental factors on soybean traits
Authors: Khaled Ramadan, Souhail Nader, Loubna Mokrani, Ghrood Al Aswd, Samir Abou-Isba, Abdulkarim Dakah
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEthyl methanesulfonate (EMS) mutagenesis is a well-established tool used to induce genetic variability in crops such as soybean (Glycine max) for improvement of agronomic traits. This study evaluates the impact of EMS and environmental factors on morphological, physiological, productive, and biochemical traits across to evaluate the improvement of their traits, which are essential for breeding programs.MethodsSoybean seeds were treated with three concentrations of EMS (0.1%, 0.2%, and 0.3% v/v) for its mutagenesis. After the first generation (M1) was obtained, seeds of M1 were grown to M2 seeds obtained in three environmentally distinct stations differing in altitude, temperature, humidity, and soil characteristics. Traits evaluated were germination rate, plant height, branch number, flowering period, pod and seed yield, seed biochemical contents (protein, oil, phenolics), and seed protein profiles through SDS-PAGE. Statistical analysis involving ANOVA (one-way and two-way) and correlation tests were conducted for genotype traits with considering of variables (EMS and environmental factors).ResultsSeed treatment with EMS (0.1%) induced many plant traits by increasing their measures, and in some cases (0.2)% gave better results. In the second generation, environmental factors affected the studied traits. However, the values differed among plants treated with EMS, such as the increase in germination rate and protein content, and the decrease in the number of leaves, and oil content, in addition to an increase in plant height and total phenolic content in most treatments. The gel electrophoresis results showed the effect of environmental factors (especially temperature), on the other hand main protein fractions typically found in soybean seeds (conglycinin subunits and glycinin subunits) was appeared.ConclusionThe combined effect of EMS mutagenesis and environmental factors is responsible for genetic improvement. Low EMS concentrations favorably affect productivity and seed quality traits, these improvements are heritable, and are further modulated by environment. The study highlights the importance of evaluating for stability of traits across multiple environments, forming the basis for soybean variety breeding, and considering the changes in their characteristics, especially those which promote their economic, nutritional, and medicinal values.

Introduction

Chemical and physical mutagens are typically used at low concentrations (doses) in plant breeding programs, to obtain new varieties with high yields and desirable traits such as early maturity, reduced plant height, and disease resistance [1], in addition to increased oil and protein content [2]. However, it is important to control the stability of mutations across multiple generations and different environmental conditions. EMS (Ethyl methanesulfonate) is an alkylating agent that affects DNA [3] and has a strong impact on the chemical composition of seeds, including soybeans, allowing the attainment of desirable traits [4]. EMS has also been used to obtain high-yield varieties of soybeans [5]. Plant selection aims to choose the best genetic patterns based on the desired traits in the plant according to its practical uses, as the desired traits in crops differ from those desired in ornamental plants and differ from the traits desired in medicinal plants [6]. Genotypes are usually evaluated (in breeding programs) under different environmental conditions; the different responses of the genotypes being assessed in various environmental conditions are called Genotype × Environment Interaction (GEI). This relationship is significant because it provides information about the performance of the genotypes and their stability in different environments [7]. Therefore, the stability of genotypes can be defined when living organisms exhibit the same performance or behavior (or higher productivity) under different environmental conditions. On the other hand, it is important to study as many mutational traits as possible to determine their suitability for different environments, which is referred to as mutational variance [8]. It is generally believed that the harmful effects of mutations on germination rate, growth, and plant productivity increase in stressful environments, but some research suggests that these effects can be mitigated [9]. The productivity of a genotype can be classified based on the environment into three categories: Unconditionally harmful mutations across alternative environments, Conditionally neutral mutations, and Conditionally beneficial mutations [10]. It is clear that environments not only affect the mutation rate but also the mix of mutations that arise under different forms of stress. Consequently, genetic change in different environments is biased due to the availability of a different mix of mutations, which may in turn affect the available adaptive pathways. Furthermore, changes in genetic diversity and mutation rates are not known across a wide range of natural environments, where microbes and other organisms vary or change [11].

The impact of environmental factors on gene expression and phenotype increases, due to climate changes [12]. Environmental conditions (climate, soil, and viability) directly affect plants, particularly their germination, growth, and chemical composition. Many conditions, such as temperature, humidity, light, and the chemical components of the soil, as well as biological components, influence the morphological and physiological characteristics of plants. These environmental factors may limit plants growth in certain environments, making these environments suitable only for certain types of plants that are adapted to survive in these conditions. It is difficult for plants growing in harsh environments (high temperatures, extreme cold, and areas of high humidity or extreme dryness) to simultaneously grow in another environment because the differences in environmental factors contribute to the creation of a different environment, which directly affects plant biodiversity [13].

The importance of plants and their advantages in environmental studies and mutations has been clarified by Whittle & Johnston [14], ; as it is difficult to study the impact of environmental factors on the mutation rate in animals and bacteria, in addition to the fact that data in this field is scarce or nonexistent. On the other hand, the significance of plants makes them strong model systems for studying the effect of the environment (natural environmental factors) on the rate of spontaneous mutations, which would enhance knowledge about how and why genetic mutations arise. On the other hand, plant research has significantly contributed to understand many genetic processes. In addition to other features including low cost, availability, lack of ethical concerns regarding treatment, and often short generation times, as well as their benefits for studying environmental factors and cell lifespan, plants -unlike most living organisms- are capable of transmitting mutations that arise in somatic cells to successive generations, because the body in plants is constantly exposed to localized growth conditions. Since plants are non-moving living organisms, they cannot escape their local conditions, and thus they are forced to endure their growth conditions. This is useful in revealing the impact of environmental factors on the rate of genetic mutations. Consequently, their environmental conditions are usually consistent in the long term.

Soybean (Glycine max (L.) Merr.) is one of the ancient crops cultivated by humans, originally native to Southeast Asia. It is a dicotyledonous plant belonging to the Fabaceae family, with global soybean production reaching 399 million tons [12, 15]. It is characterized by a rapid life cycle of about 3–4 months and self-pollination, along with a large number of measurable morphological, physiological, chemical, and molecular traits, making it ideal for mutation studies [16]. Soybean seeds have nutritional importance [17] due to their chemical composition, which contains high levels of proteins rich in essential amino acids [18], and high levels of oil rich in unsaturated fatty acids [19, 20], and carbohydrates [21]. Additionally, it contains both macronutrients and micronutrients with many other components, including vitamins, as well as bioactive compounds like phenols [22]. Soybeans prefer sandy loam soil and clay loam soil, according to RDA [23], . Suitable soil for soybean cultivation must be rich in organic matter with adequate amounts of phosphorus, potassium, and lime. Neutral soil is suitable, saline soil is not suitable for soybean cultivation, and excessive soil moisture severely hinders soybean germination and early growth, as the movement of oxygen to the seeds and plant roots is limited. Soybeans can grow in a wide range of climatic conditions; however, the ideal temperature for their growth is around 30 °C. Soybeans tolerate high temperatures better than other crops, but temperatures above 40 °C negatively impact the rate of node formation, growth and the onset of flowering, with heat stress from 40 °C to 46 °C leading to pod drop. On the other hand, temperatures below 24 °C typically delay flowering by 2 or 3 days for every 0.5 °C decrease. Environmental factors (temperature and rainfall) affect the chemical properties of soybeans, particularly oil and protein [24].

Plants are living chemical factories for a vast array of secondary metabolites, which form the basis for many food and pharmaceutical industries. The biosynthesis of organic compounds begins with fundamental pathways, such as glucose catabolism, and then diversifies depending on the type of cell and growth stage (period, season, year) and environmental influences, taking into account the specificity of the organ or tissue. Therefore, the adaptation of plant morphology, anatomy, and physiological characteristics to changes in biotic and abiotic environments can affect the accumulation of secondary metabolites, as a result of the influence of environmental factors on the expression of the genes involved in the biosynthesis of secondary metabolites [25]. Secondary metabolites play a vital role for plants in interacting with their environment and adapting to both biotic (living) and abiotic (non-living) stressors [26]. The influence of temporary environmental changes, semi-permanent climate patterns, and biotic interactions collectively shapes the characteristics and behaviors of plants by affecting their secondary chemical compositions [27].

Many mutagenesis methods have been applied to soybean seeds, and various traits (morphological, physiological, chemical, and productive) have been studied to confirm the occurrence of mutations [28–30]. The increasing global demand for food (in terms of quality and quantity) has led to the development of high-yielding plant varieties, particularly through the use of various breeding methods, including chemical mutagenesis with EMS. On the other hand, mutations must be stable in subsequent generations, as well as under different environmental conditions. Therefore, this research aims to study the effect of environmental factors (climate and soil) on the traits of the second generation of EMS-mutated soybean plants to determine the stability of the studied traits.

Materials and methods

Plant material

Soybean seeds of SB-337 variety were obtained from Crop Department at the General Commission for Scientific Agricultural Research (Damascus, Syria). They are characterized by an average yield of 3200 kg/hectare and a maturation period of 120 days.

Obtention of the first generation of mutated soybean (M1)

Seed mutagenesis of soybeans was conducted according to the method of Zhou et al. [4] with some modifications. Seeds were soaked in water to be activated, by placing 335 seeds in a 1000 ml bottle, and adding 400 ml of distilled water. A total of 12 bottles were prepared, and placed on a shaker (JSR, JSOS 500) at 150 rpm for 4 h at 25 °C. Seeds were then washed with water (to remove impurities).

Three concentrations of EMS (Sigma) were prepared with distilled water (0.1, 0.2, and 0.3)% v/v (LD50 = 0.71% according to our previous study with same conditions) and then added to the bottles (1 ml per seed), with three bottles for each concentration (approximately 1000 seeds), in addition to 3 control bottles (only distilled water). The bottles were placed on the shaker at 150 rpm for 4 h at at 25 °C. Seeds were then washed with tap water ten times.

EMS solution was neutralized by adding 4% sodium hydroxide for 24 h at 25 °C [31].

Seeds were planted at the “1 May” station of the General Commission for Scientific Agricultural Research (33°29’57.8"N, 36°28’51.1"E, Alt 650 m asl), during the period from June to the end of October 2022. Two days before planting, the soil was irrigated. Each concentration had three replicates, each one included 5 rows of 7 m long and 50 cm between rows, seed depth of 3–5 cm, and 5–10 cm spacing between seeds. Irrigation was done once a week in the early stages and then once every two weeks. A complete fertilizer (Fertium) was added to the soil along with iron (N: P:K: Fe, 20:20:20:5). An insecticide (Dolan) was sprayed, and weeds were physically removed. The plant were allowed to self-fertilize and individual of M1 were obtaind.

After plant maturation, pods were collected and placed in cloth bags (raw), which were then stored in a dry and ventilated place away from moisture. After pods drying, seeds of the first generation M1 were collected, then seeds of each concentration were accumulated in the same bag, and for further testing later on.

Obtention of the second generation (M2) of mutated soybean

The first generation seeds of each treatment were treated in the same way during the first phase but without EMS. Seeds were activated by soaking them in distilled water for 4 h on a shaker, followed by soaking them in distilled water (without EMS) for another 4 h on the shaker.

The first generation seeds M1 (to obtain the second generation M2) were simultaneously planted at three stations belonging to the General Commission for Agricultural Scientific Research (Syria), from June until the end of October 2023, at “1 May” Station (33°29’57.8"N, 36°28’51.1"E, Alt 650 m asl), “Al-Taybah” Station (33°18’30.4"N, 36°12’36.5"E, Alt 730 m asl), and “Serghaya” Station (33°47’23.0"N, 36°08’37.7"E, Alt 1450 m asl), located in diverse environments. The agricultural conditions for obtaining the second generation were the same as those for the first generation, in terms of planting method, irrigation rate, fertilizer, pesticide, and agricultural services. The plant of M1 were allowed to self-fertilize and individual of M2 were obtaind. After pods drying, seeds of the second generation M2 were collected, then seeds of each concentration were accumulated in the same bag, and for further testing later on. This was done at every station separately.

Traits studied in M1 and M2 generations

Physiological and morphological traits: germination percentage (%) after three weeks of planting, plant height (cm), and number of branches per plant, number of leaves per plant, measured three months after planting.

Productivity traits: number of flowering plants after two months of planting, number of pods per plant, number of seeds per plant, number of seeds per pod, weight of 100 seeds (g), measured four months after planting (maturity stage).

Chemical traits of seeds: oil content by Soxhlet extraction with n-Hexane [32], protein content (which extracted by alkaline method with KOH) and in water extract by coomassie blue method [33, 34], , the yield of water extract (obtained by maceration method), soluble carbohydrates by phenol sulfuric acid method [35], total phenolic content by folin ciocalteu method, total flavonoid content by aluminum chloride method [36], antioxidant activity using DPPH assay [37], pigment content (chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b, carotenoids: carotene and zeaxanthin) by spectrophotometric method [38], qualitative detection: tannins, alkaloids, coumarins, glycosides, saponins, steroids, and triterpenes using several reagents [39, 40].

Gel Electrophoresis: Soybean proteins were separated on SDS PAGE (sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis) at 25 °C using electrophoresis apparatus (Electrophoresis, Cleaver, UK), with a separation gel concentration of 12% and a stacking gel concentration of 5% [41].

Statistical analysis

True experimental design was conducted in this study, EMS concentrations was the variable for first generation study, on the other hand, environmental factors and EMS concentrations were the variables for the second generation study. SPSS V.22 software was used to study the significant differences between the average values of morphological, physiological, productivity, and chemical characteristics at a significance level of p-value < 0.05, applying One-Way ANOVA with group separation using Duncan’s analysis for the first generation values, and applying Two-Way ANOVA for significant differences between the averages of all stations for each concentration of EMS regarding the morphological, physiological, productivity, and chemical characteristics for the second generation, in addition to the interaction between stations and concentrations. Scheffe’s analysis was conducted for group separation, and the experiments were carried out with an average of three replicates. The results were presented as mean ± standard deviation, and the trait of the number of flowering plants was not subjected to ANOVA due to relying on the total number of flowering individuals. Pearson correlation coefficient tests were conducted to determine the correlation between protein bands resulting from electrophoresis in terms of the presence or absence and bands intensity among the plants (control and mutant) at the three stations.

Results and discussion

Climatic data and soil analysis

The importance of studying environmental factors in plant cultivation areas lies in their direct influence on the morphological, physiological, and chemical characteristics of plants, which determines their practical uses.

Table 1 shows a relative convergence in values between 1 May station and Al-Taybah station, which is attributed to the proximity of the two locations compared to Serghaya station, characterized by the lowest temperature average and the highest relative humidity average during cultivation when compared to the other stations. On the other hand, wind speed was higher at 1 May station, which may be due to the differences in the topography around the 1 May station compared to Al-Taybah and Serghaya areas. As for rainfall average, it may have the least impact, as most soybean cultivation periods in Syria occur during the non-rainy months, and irrigation is completely needed.

Soil structure is an indicator of its quality due to its sensitivity to management practices [42], according to particle size, sand/silt/clay ratios could be determined in soil texture, which is one of the main indicators of quality and productivity, as it affects the adhesive and cohesive properties between soil particles, and thus the dynamics of water, nutrient cycling, and ion exchange [43].

Soil analysis results (Table 2) showed that the soil at the three stations is characterized by good nutrients content. 1 May station soil enriched with organic matter at 1.2%, total nitrogen at 0.06%, available phosphorus at 25.8 mg/kg, and calcium carbonate at about 52%. On the other hand, it had the lowest available potassium at 312 mg/kg. The soil at Al-Taybah station was higher than that at Serghaya station in calcium carbonate, available phosphorus, and available potassium. The differences in soil analysis results may be attributed to several factors: soil type, type of crops grown before planting soybeans, and fertilizers added in previous agricultural cycles. The variation in these values affects electrical conductivity and pH, with the highest electrical conductivity in 1 May soil (1.33 ms/cm) and the lowest value in Al-Taybah soil (0.64 ms/cm), noting that there is a relationship between electrical conductivity and salinity (S = E.C x 0.64) [44].

pH values were similar between 1 May soil and Serghaya soil, both considered as weakly alkaline, while Al-Taybah soil had the lowest pH, which is moderate. The high pH and low calcium carbonate content in Serghaya station soil may be due to the presence of other compounds - not determined - that raised the pH value compared to the other stations soils. Additionally, analysis results did not show a clear relationship between electrical conductivity and pH values.

1 May station soil was characterized by a balanced ratio of sand and clay, while Al-Taybah station soil had a higher clay content of 39% compared to a sand content (28%). Serghaya station soil had the highest clay content (49%), however, silt content was the lowest (21%) compared to the other stations. Studying the soil texture is important, especially for soybeans, due to the relative difficulty of growing soybeans in sandy and clayey soils [45].

It is essential to study the mineral content in the soil, as it affects the values of electrical conductivity and pH [46]. In addition, the increase in soil salinity leads to a higher concentration of soil solution compared to the concentration of plant sap in the roots; under these conditions, plant roots become unable to easily absorb water and nutrients from the soil. Therefore, the soil components must be balanced and suitable for the cultivated plant [47]. Moreover, adding iron to the soil is important because it plays a role in the composition of the nitrogenase enzyme, which is necessary for biological nitrogen fixation [48].

Irrigation timings and quantities significantly impact the chemical composition of soybean seeds, which in turn affects their nutritional value [49]. Thus, the soil in 1 May station may offer the best balance in physical texture, as its granular composition is ideal. Clay is vital for storing organic matter and essential minerals, while sand promotes root respiration and facilitates water movement within the soil. On the other hand, the soil in Serghaya may be unsuitable for the soybean cultivation due to its high clay content, which hinders the plant’s ability to utilize organic matter, in addition to the low silt content necessary for increasing water retention in the soil [50]. Overall, soil analysis results indicate that the agricultural stations possess characteristics conducive to soybean cultivation [51, 52].

Morphological, physiological, and productivity characteristics of the first and the second generations of soybean plants

The results of soybean cultivation show that the rural areas of Damascus can be considered suitable for soybean cultivation, noting that its cultivation in Syria is concentrated in Al Hasakah, Homs, Hama, Raqqa, and Alghab [53].

The results in Table 3 showed that the average values of branches number per plant, the number of leaves per plant, and the number of flowering plants increased with the increase in the concentration of EMS to 0.3% compared to the control. Conversely, the average values of germination rate, plant height, number of seeds per plant, number of seeds per pod, and weight of 100 seeds decreased as the EMS concentration increased to 0.3% compared to the control. The average number of pods per plant increased with the increase of EMS to 0.2% and then decreased at the concentration of 0.3% compared to the control.

Our results were consistent with some studies conducted on soybeans, in terms of low concentrations of EMS causing an increase in the germination rate [29], their results also indicated that the germination rate of the second generation was higher than those of the first generation for both control plant and the treated plant with EMS, with germination rates of about 88% in the first generation and 93% in the second generation. For plants treated with 0.1% EMS, the germination rate was 69% in the first generation and 95% in the second generation, this is consistent with the results of the second generation, except for some values at Serghaya station, and did not align with the results of the treatment at a concentration of 0.1%, where the germination rate of the second generation was lower than that of the first generation. In other studies, the germination rate decreased by about 20% at a concentration of 30 mM (0.32%) [54], and dropped to 50% at a concentration of 25 mM (0.27%) [55]. Regarding plant height during the first few weeks, our results did not align with the findings of Koshika et al. [56] which indicated that the low concentrations of 10 mM (0.1%) caused an increase in plant elongation, followed by a decrease in plant height as the concentration increased to 60 mM (0.64%). The results obtained by Yuan et al. [5] showed an increase in plant height when treated with EMS at a concentration of 0.6%, from 37 cm in the control to 41.5 cm in the mutant, which may be attributed to the different response of the studied variety.

Germination rate results and plant height between the first and the second generations were consistent with the findings of Da Silva et al. [57] which showed that the germination rate in the second generation increased compared to the first generation, while the plant height in the second generation was lower when compared to the first generation.

Flowering speed increased at concentrations of 0.1% and 0.2%, which aligns with Gopinath & Pavadai [30], results, where the increase continued up to a concentration of 0.3%, and then the flowering speed decreased with increasing EMS concentration. As for the number of pods, seeds, and leaves per plant, the low concentrations showed a decrease in both the number of pods and seeds, while the number of pods and seeds in the plant increased at relatively high concentrations, which is consistent with the previous reference. Regarding the number of leaves, the results indicated that low concentrations increased the number of leaves, which then decreased at high concentrations. The research results did not align with Sagel et al. [58] findings, as there was no correlation between the number of pods and EMS concentration, and the results for the number of pods per plant, did not match with the same reference; the number of pods increased at low concentrations and then decreased with the increasing concentration. The results for the weight of 100 seeds were consistent with the findings of Yuan et al. [5] which showed a decrease in weight from 14.7 g in the control to 14.4 g in the mutant with EMS, and were also consistent with Sharma et al. [19] results, who indicated that the weight ranged between 8.7 g and 11.1 g, and was relatively consistent with the results obtained by Chuwa et al. [59] who reported weight of 9 g. In contrast, they did not align with the results of Agyenim-Boateng et al. [60] which indicated that the weight of 100 seeds was between 15.5 g and 26.4 g, this difference is due to several factors, especially the cultivated variety. The results for the number of pods per did not match the findings of Sobko et al. [61] who indicated that it ranged between 20.4 and 30.0 pods/plant, while the results for the number of seeds per pod were consistent with the same research, as they were 2.2 seeds/pod, the previous reference also indicated that an increase in the number of lateral branches corresponds to a simultaneous increase in the number of pods per plant, but this relationship did not appear clearly in the results of the first and second generations.

Yuan et al. [5] results showed a positive correlation between the number of seeds and seed weight, and this relationship was consistent with first generation results, but did not clearly appear second generation results. It also showed a positive correlation between the number of pods and seed weight [5], which did not align with the results of the first and the second generations, except for Al-Taybah station. A weak correlation was observed between seed weight and plant height, which was consistent with the first generation results, but did not align with the second generation value.

The low germination rate at Serghaya station may be attributed to the lower temperature compared to 1 May and Al-Taybah stations. Additionally Sobko et al. [18] and Alsajri et al. [62] indicated that the increase in temperature to a certain extent may positively affect soybean productivity, which aligns with the higher number of pods and seeds per plant at 1 May station -especially the control- where temperature was the highest. The increase in the number of pods and seeds may be due to the larger leaf area -as a productivity trait- which increases with higher temperatures [63].

RDA [23], noted that an increase in the altitude of plant cultivation location prolongs the number of days required for flowering. However, the increase in plant height, appear to have more branches and pods [64], as evidenced by the results from Serghaya station. It is worth mentioning that plant height affects the number and location of pods, and taller crops may be easier to harvest for seeds, considering that tall plants may bend before the harvest period [65]. Additionally, the same reference highlighted the influence of organic matter in the soil on seed yield, which aligns with the results from the 1 May station especially the control. It was observed that clay in the soil increases plant height, consistent with findings from the Serghaya station. It was also clarified that increased phosphorus in the soil enhances plant height; however, this did not align with the results from the Serghaya station, where plants were taller with lower phosphorus levels in the soil.

The resutls in Table 4 showed that, many plants from the Al-Taybah station can be selected, especially those treated with EMS compound at a concentration of 0.2% or 0.3%, and from the Serghaya station, especially those treated with EMS compound at a concentration of 0.1%, by continuously cultivating individuals from these plants to obtain genetic lines with productive traits.

Statistical analysis showed significant differences between the concentrations in the first generation. It was observed that the plant height for the concentration 0.2% does not differ from 0.1% to 0.3%, and that the number of seeds per pod for the concentration 0.1% does not differ from the control and the concentration 0.2% (Table 3). Regarding the second generation, significant differences were found between stations for each concentration, with three groups for each station (Table 4).

The morphological characteristics are among the most important factors affecting productivity; like plant height and the number of branches which influence the number of seeds [66]. This was noted in the first generation but not clearly detected in the second generation, which may be due to the interaction with other factors, such as climatic factors, soil components, in addition to the varying behavior of plants treated with EMS across different stations. Table 4 shows the variation in plant characteristics based on treatment with EMS from one standpoint and the differences between stations from another standpoint. This aligns with the results obtained by Shrestha et al. [67] who studied several morphological, physiological, and productivity characteristics of 25 soybean varieties in three different environments, finding that environmental effects accounted for 36.55% of the variance, genotypes for 34.36%, while the interaction between the genotypes and the environment accounted for 29.07% of the total variance.

The chemical characteristics of first and second generations of soybean plants

The concentrations (or doses) of physical and chemical mutagens used in the experiment must be studied for each plant species or variety in order to determine the effect of these mutagens, whether the effect is positive or negative. EMS is considered to be the best in comparison to other chemical and physical mutagens, when used at low concentrations, especially for modifying the chemical composition of soybeans to increase protein and oil content [2].

It is noteworthy that even in traditional soybean breeding programs, spontaneous mutations may occur, but at a low rate. Obtaining a genetic line (origin) with several desirable genetic traits is challenging; for example, protein content may increase, but this could be accompanied by a decrease in one type of carbohydrate. However, the use of mutagens (especially EMS) increases the abundance of genetic variations that reflect on soybean traits. Thus, the development of new improved soybean varieties can be achieved through a combination of natural and induced genetic variations (by mutagens) [4]. It is important to mention that drying conditions for seeds should be standardized, because longer drying times increase protein content while decreasing oil content [68].

The oil yield in seeds treated with EMS reached a concentration of 0.2%, amounting to 21.3% when compared to the control, which was about 20.1% (Table 5). In contrast, the yield of proteins in the seeds decreased to 30.2% with the increase of EMS to 0.3% in comparison to the control, which was about 35%. However, the protein values in the aqueous extract did not differ between the control and the plants treated with EMS. Regarding the yield of the aqueous extract, which contains carbohydrates, phenols, flavonoids, and pigments, values increased with EMS concentration up to 0.2% and then decreased as EMS reached 0.3% in comparison to the control.

The results showed a direct relationship between the increase in total phenolic and flavonoid content and the increase in antioxidant activity. On the other hand, there was an inverse relationship between protein yield and oil yield.

The effect of EMS on protein concentration did not align with Pavadai et al. [2] findings, which showed that protein increases with increasing EMS concentrations and that protein concentration decreased in the second generation when compared to the first generation. In contrast, results for oil yield were consistent with the same reference, indicating that increased EMS concentration leads to an increase in oil yield; however, they did not align with the second generation results, where oil yield increased in the second-generation when compared to the first generation according to the same reference.

There is a positive correlation between carbohydrate and oil yield, and a negative correlation between both and protein yield [80], which is consistent with the results of the first generation (Table 5) and relatively consistent with the results of the second generation (Table 8).

Typically, protein increase in soybean seeds comes at the expense of oil and carbohydrates [81], which is consistent with the results of both the first and second generations. Additionally, the same reference indicated that amino acid concentration varies depending on the environment, which aligns with protein SDS-PAGE results, showing bands in one station and their absence in another (Fig. 1). The pathways converting carbohydrates to oil are significantly affected by chemical mutagens during the mutagenesis process, as induced mutations can cause rapid and widespread disruptions in key genes within the carbon cycle, and thus the correlation between carbohydrates and oil can become weak or nonexistent [4].

The results of the EMS effect on oil yield were consistent with those obtained by Zhou et al. [4] as it increased from 16.8% to 18.2%, but did not match the results for protein yield, which increased from 35.1% to 36.7%. The results of the EMS effect on oil yield were consistent with those indicated by Yuan et al. [5] as it increased from 19.3% to 23.3%, but did not match protein yield results, which increased from 35.4% to 41.4%.

Generally, low concentrations of EMS induce an increase in mutation rates that are positive in terms of plant productivity, increasing the concentration leads to damage related to plant productivity in addition to a decrease in germination rate. The reasons for the differences with reference studies in some criteria on the one hand, and between the reference studies on the other hand, are due to several factors, including differences in soybean varieties, activation time, EMS treatment time, EMS concentrations, the amount of EMS solution per seed, plant sensitivity to EMS due to differences in seed size, seed storage conditions and moisture content, in addition to differences in cultivation conditions (as environmental conditions affect genetic expression).

One of the advantages of modifying plant genetics using mutagens is that it can improve plants, especially from an economic perspective. Additionally, it modifies the plant’s genetic material without introducing new genes as is done in genetic engineering, which may ease the restrictions on the use and spread of mutated plants compared to genetically modified plants [4].

The impact of climate on chemical properties

It is essential to standardize the planting time of soybeans in experiments, as there is a relationship between the planting date and crop productivity [82]. Yao et al. [83] indicated a decrease in oil yield in the second generation compared to the first generation, for soybeans grown in two environmentally different regions. This corresponds with the oil yield results for the first generation (Table 5) and the second generation (Table 8).

Lower temperatures caused a decrease in protein content [41], which relatively aligns with the decreased protein content at Serghaya station -considered the lowest in temperature- compared to the protein content at 1 May and Al-Taybah stations. In contrast, High temperatures or thermal stress reduce the proportion of some proteins while increasing others [84], as (Fig. 1) shown. Protein content increases when temperatures are around 29 °C, and oil content increases when the temperature is around 26 °C [85], This aligns with the increased protein content at 1 May and Al-Taybah stations, compared to the increased oil -especially in the control- at Serghaya station, where the temperature did not exceed 25.7 °C. High temperatures during seed growth are associated with a decrease in oil content [73], which corresponds with controls at 1 May and Al-Taybah stations; both regions are characterized by relatively high temperatures compared to Serghaya area, which was accompanied by a decrease in oil proportion at those two stations. It is worth mentioning that the effect of heat may vary due to the influence of other environmental factors such as water stress or nitrogen availability during seed formation.

In contrast, High temperatures during seed filling increase oil content [86], which did not align with the results of the second generation, as station 1 May had the highest temperature but the lowest oil yield compared to the control and the mutant.

The soluble carbohydrate content in soybean seeds decreases with the rising temperature due to its effect on enzymes converting different types of carbohydrates to each other (starch synthase, ADP-glucose pyrophosphorylase, starch phosphorylase, and soluble starch synthase), or at lower temperatures, carbohydrates are not utilized for oil and protein synthesis due to reduced enzymatic activities [80]. This is consistent with the carbohydrate content results, as the highest value was in the control grown at Serghaya station, which had the lowest temperature. The results at the Serghaya station may show that increased rainfall reduces soybean crop yield [45, 82], but the main effect is that the planted variety is a summer variety, while the variety planted in the reference is grown during the rainy months.

Environmental factors have a crucial role in the biosynthesis and changes in organic materials in plants [25]. There are more than 2,140,000 secondary metabolites, divided into different groups consisting of 29,000 terpenoids, 12,000 alkaloids, 8,000 phenols, steroids, flavonoids, tannins, and others [87].

Low and high temperature ranges may negatively affect plant growth and productivity, as they lead to a decrease or increase in the regulation of responsive genes and impact the growth and development of plants. However, the biosynthesis of secondary metabolites is often associated with elevated temperatures in plants [25, 26, 87, 88], which aligns with the results of secondary metabolites (especially phenols, flavonoids, and alkaloids) at 1 May and Al-Taybah stations, compared to Serghaya station, characterized by a relatively low temperature (Table 1).

A strong correlation was observed between flavonoid content and elevation of the growth site, and this difference is attributed to the variation in solar radiation levels, especially ultraviolet rays [26, 89]. This was not consistent with the results obtained at Serghaya station, which is the highest in elevation but lowest in flavonoid content; this may be due to the lower temperatures there compared to 1 May and Al-Taybah stations (Table 1).

Soil effect on chemical properties

The results of protein content among the second-generation cultivation stations were not consistent with the findings of Urdă et al. [90] which indicated that increasing fertilization rates enhances protein yields. This disparity can be explained by the variations in the three stations’ soil compositions with respect to nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium levels (Table 2). While Nitrogen is essential for crop growth and is a key component of crop proteins, DNA, and nucleoproteins, potassium promotes root growth and enhances the activation of internal plant enzymes, thereby boosting photosynthesis in plants [91]. Phosphorus plays a role in biological processes such as photosynthesis, respiration, energy generation, and the synthesis of nucleic acids [92].

Some studies indicated that soybean production is positively related to the presence of phosphorus and potassium in the soil, while other studies proved that this relationship does not exist. In this case, other factors may influence productivity, such as phosphorus, sulphur, zinc, copper, magnesium, and calcium, in addition to pH levels [82]. It is known that potassium content is one of the major factors determining the chemical and physical productivity of soybeans. Certainly, the interactions between soil, plants, and climate vary regionally (depending on location), leading to productivity being more responsive to various soil variables based on the site [82].

Because the concentration of hydrogen ions in the soil has a negative correlation with soybean production and quality, it is crucial to investigate soil pH. The results of pH levels among agricultural soils are relatively close. Conversely, soybean productivity is positively impacted by the soil’s organic matter, potassium, nitrogen, and cation exchange capacity (which has positively correlated with electrical conductivity). On the other hand, soybean productivity is negatively impacted by the levels of boron, mercury, magnesium oxide, and cadmium [91].

Anthony et al. [86] indicated that soybean production is positively correlated with phosphorus in the soil and negatively correlated with pH and organic matter. However, the oil concentration in soybeans is significantly affected by phosphorus levels in the soil, as the relationship between them is negative. The relationship between potassium and protein was negative, while it was positive with oil. The correlation between pH and protein was negative, while it was positive with oil.

Considering that electrical conductivity reflects salinity, Ghassemi-Golezani et al. [93] indicated that increased salinity caused a decrease in oil and protein yield. This was consistent with the oil results at the 1 May station, which had the lowest oil yield and the highest electrical conductivity, but did not align with the protein results, as it was not the lowest. The decrease in oil with increased salinity is attributed to the effect on synthetic enzymes, while the decrease in protein is due to reduced nitrogen (nitrate) absorption from the soil [94]. The high nitrogen content at the 1 May station may be the reason why the protein there is unaffected (decreased) (Table 2).

Phosphorus increases protein [51, 95], which is in line with the findings in Table 8; protein concentrations at 1 May and Al-Taybah stations are higher than those of Serghaya station, noting that the phosphorus content in the soil is higher in the soils of the two stations when compared to Serghaya station soil (Table 2).

The increase in potassium increases oil [96], which aligns with the results in Table 2 that Al-Taybah station had the highest potassium content in the soil and the highest oil yield (as values for the control and the mutant) when compared to Serghaya soil, which had lower potassium and oil yield. 1 May soil was the lowest in potassium, which corresponded with the lowest oil yield. The same reference’s findings indicated that higher soil nitrogen levels resulted in higher protein levels in the seeds, which agrees with the results in Table 2 where the 1 May soil was the highest in nitrogen and the highest in protein (Table 8).

The differences in irrigation water quantity affect productivity and protein yield, as well as the differences in mineral elements within the plant [97], so the water treatments in the agricultural stations were equal (time and quantity of irrigation); however, the difference in soil composition -especially the amount of clay- causes an increase in water retention when compared to soils characterized by high sand content. On the other hand, excess moisture leads to a decrease in oxygen concentration in the soil, resulting in lower crop productivity. Meanwhile, lower soil moisture is associated with lower yield [98].

Clay soil is the most suitable for soybean production; however, an increase in clay content beyond the necessary (normal) limits leads to a decrease in soybean yield because it hinders the penetration of plant roots, while sandy soil makes it difficult to cultivate soybeans [45], thus, the soil at Serghaya station is more appropriate, but climatic factors, especially low temperatures, may have a greater impact on soybean growth. Climatic factors (temperature, precipitation, and radiation) have a greater impact on crop productivity than soil factors [82].

Phenols and flavonoids content increases under severe stress conditions caused by drought. Even if regular irrigation was applied at the stations, differences in soil characteristics may affect their ability to retain water [25, 26].

An increase in phosphorus raises alkaloids [87], which is consistent with the results of plants grown at 1 May and Al-Taybah stations, when compared to Serghaya station, which is characterized by the lowest concentration of phosphorus. Phenol results did not align with those of the same reference, which indicated that an increase in nitrogen raises phenolic content. In on our results, the soil at Al-Taybah station had the lowest nitrogen content but the highest phenolic content.

The addition of NPK fertilizer increases secondary metabolites (phenols, alkaloids, and terpenes) [26, 27, 89], but it is difficult to determine this in the results due to the irregularity of the soils regarding the increase of those elements.

An increase in organic matter in the soil raises secondary metabolites [89], but this did not align with the results from the 1 May station, which had the highest organic matter but was not the highest in secondary metabolites; conversely, Al-Taybah station had the lowest organic matter but was not the lowest in secondary metabolites.

The optimal pH level for the monocots growth is 5.0-6.5, whereas the optimal pH level for the growth of dicots is 6.0-7.5. The relationship between soil pH and secondary metabolites (phenols, tannins, flavonoids, and saponins) is often inverse [99], this relationship may not be clearly evident in the results due to the similarity of the soil pH levels among the stations.

The relationship between salinity and secondary metabolites is often positive [25, 26]. However, this was not consistent with 1 May station results, which had the highest salinity (calculated by means of the electrical conductivity value [44]), while it did not have the highest values for most of the secondary metabolites when compared to Al-Taybah station, which had lower electrical conductivity. This may be due to the higher sand content in 1 May station soil, as Chaouqi et al. [89] which indicated that secondary metabolites decrease when plants are grown in sandy soils, possibly due to the lack of resistance to root growth in such soils.

The effect of environmental factors on secondary metabolites

The lack of correlation between environmental factors and secondary metabolites in some cases may be due to unmeasured factors such as ultraviolet radiation, which increases secondary metabolites, and the effects of photoperiod and light intensity on the synthesis and storage of organic materials. Additionally, soil microbial content and soil ions may also play a role [25, 26]. On the other hand, the concentration of CO2, ozone, and radiation (spectrum colors), as well as the plants in the previous crop cycle or the plants growing during cultivation, and the impact of insects, particularly herbivores can also have an effect [87, 100].

While internal biochemical adaptability is more difficult to detect, environmental stress adaptation is a broad environmental behavior that is easy to assess, such as morphological adaptations. It is noteworthy that individual environmental stress can selectively increase the content of many secondary metabolites in plants, and individual factors generally interact with other factors; for example, high irradiation is often accompanied by high temperatures and water deficiency. Therefore, the synergistic effects of multiple environmental factors on the chemical synthesis, especially secondary metabolites in plants, should be studied and understood [26].

The interactions between Genotype x Year, Genotype x Location, and Genotype x Year x Location significantly affect on carbohydrates, proteins, and oil content [80]. This was shown by the results of the first generation among them (with considering the mutant as a new genotype), and the results of the first generation with the second generation grown at the same station (to indicate the year factor), and the results of the second generation grown at different stations (to indicate the interaction of the location factor when comparing the controls, and the interaction of the location factor and genotype when studying the results of the mutant at the three stations).

Soil type and climatic year determine the characteristic behavior of varieties. Therefore, soybeans that are planted in each area should be studied, and data from an area cannot be assumed to be suitable for soybean cultivation in another one. If the soil is not suitable in one of the factors, it can be managed individually. The quality of soybeans is affected by soil and climate interactions that operate on a regional basis, so researchers and farmers must understand quantitatively the relationship between soil characteristics, agricultural management, soybean yield, and seed quality in order to improve soybean production [81, 86]. It is worth noting that these factors should be studied during the flowering days and the onset of fruit (or seed) formation, as protein accumulation in developing seeds begins 10–12 days after flowering, while oil accumulation starts 15–20 days after flowering, then rapid composition is occurring 20–40 days after flowering, and the accumulation of these two components continues until 70 days after flowering. Therefore, changing environmental conditions, such as soil moisture stress during the pod-filling period, may have a significant impact on soybean seed formation [22].

Bands obtained after separation were consistent with references, which examined the total proteins of soybeans [41, 101]. They indicated the presence of conglycinin subunits (α′, α, β) with apparent molecular weights of (53, 72, 76) kDa, respectively, and the presence of glycinin subunits with molecular weights of (37–44) kDa and (17–22) kDa. Other references indicated that the molecular weights of the peptide conglycinin subunits (α′, α, β) range from (45–95) kDa, with the alkaline subunit of glycinin at 37 kDa and the acidic subunit of glycinin at 20 kDa [102–104].

The response of plants to multiple abiotic stresses is unique and cannot be directly predicted by studying a single stress applied individually. The optimal environmental conditions for plant growth are extremely complex, because the gene expression or activity of the encoded proteins involved in secondary metabolic pathways is modified at different growth stages or in the presence of different stresses. Selection for positive genetic traits -especially using low concentrations of EMS- can be conducted directly within the area where the plant is to be cultivated, by mutating with various concentrations, then selecting the most suitable ones according to the cultivation area (by studying plant traits). Subsequently, the plant should be grown in multiple environmental areas to ensure trait stability.

Additionally, when studies indicate that increased temperature or longer photoperiods increase the number of pods or seeds, it must be verified that the studied variety was cultivated in its optimal environmental factors, beyond which, the effect becomes negative [62]. This could be explained by the decrease in the number of flowers or their inability to reach the fruiting stage, meaning that the plant remains at the vegetative growth phase (formation of branches and leaves) instead of starting the reproductive growth phase (formation of flowers and then pods containing seeds).

General meteorological models indicated a potential increase in the average global temperature from 1.4 °C to 5.8 °C in the year 2100. Such temperature increases are expected to have negative effects on the vegetative growth, reproductive processes, and yield of important seed-producing crops, including soybeans. This suggests that soybean production may completely fail if temperatures rise to very high levels in the future [64]. Therefore, new varieties with desirable traits should be ameliorated either through traditional methods or mutagenesis. In addition, improving agricultural practices and reducing pollution sources should be considered. More studies are needed in this field, especially the impact of temperatures at their maximum and minimum levels, as well as the effects of different types of solar radiation.

Future works should incorporate TILLING or sequencing to verify EMS-induced point mutations, multi-season trials to confirm stability, and integration of trait-environment correlations.

Conclusions

The combination of EMS (ethyl methanesulfonate) mutagenesis and evaluation under various environmental conditions allowed for the identification of soybean genotypes with improved traits agronomically and biochemically through two generations. Low EMS concentrations 0.1–0.2% favorably affect productivity and seed quality traits, these improvements are heritable, and are further modulated by environment. The study highlights the importance of evaluating for stability of traits across multiple environments, forming the basis for soybean variety breeding, and considering the changes in their characteristics, especially those which promote their economic, nutritional, and medicinal values. The integration of morpho-physiological and biochemical investigation, supported with protein electrophoresis, confirms the suitability of induced mutations for developing soybean cultivars with improved nutritional and economic traits. Further multi-location and multi-generation trials will be very crucial in enhancing the selection of these robust mutant lines for varying climatic conditions, thus enhancing both the productivity and quality of the crop.
