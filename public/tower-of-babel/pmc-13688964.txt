Headache impact and quality of life following endometriosis surgery in women: a single-center observational study
Authors: Filiz Değirmenci, Hamza Yildiz, Hakan Aytan, Mustafa Agah Teki̇ndal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEndometriosis and migraine are common chronic conditions in women of reproductive age that impair quality of life and frequently co-occur as comorbid conditions. Evidence regarding the effects of treatments targeting one of these conditions on the course of the other remains limited. In particular, the association between endometriosis surgery and headache burden and quality of life has not been sufficiently investigated. The aim of this study was to examine changes in headache burden and quality of life following endometriosis surgery in women with and without migraine.MethodsThis single-center observational study included 88 women who had undergone endometriosis surgery between 3 months and 5 years prior to enrollment. Participants completed the Headache Impact Test–6 and the 12-Item Short Form Health Survey by retrospectively recalling their preoperative status, as prospective preoperative questionnaire records were not available, and reporting their current postoperative status. Changes over time, group differences, and time-by-group interactions were analyzed using mixed-design analysis of variance. Additional sensitivity analyses stratified by follow-up duration were performed to explore the potential influence of postoperative interval on change scores.ResultsIn both women with and without migraine, headache burden scores were lower and physical and mental quality of life component scores were higher at the postoperative assessment than in the retrospectively recalled preoperative period (p < 0.001). These differences were more pronounced among women with migraine.ConclusionWomen reported lower headache burden and better quality of life at the postoperative assessment than in the retrospectively recalled preoperative period in both groups, with greater changes observed among women with migraine. Given the retrospective assessment of preoperative status, prospective studies are needed to confirm these findings, optimize multidisciplinary management of endometriosis and migraine and to identify patient subgroups most likely to benefit from surgical intervention.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12905-026-04720-8.Keywords: Endometriosis, Migraine, Headache burden, Quality of life, Endometriosis surgery

Background

Endometriosis is a chronic gynecological disorder affecting approximately 10% of women of reproductive age and is commonly associated with pelvic pain, infertility, and impaired quality of life [1–7]. The diagnostic process is often prolonged due to the lack of disease-specific biomarkers and limitations in noninvasive diagnostic methods, and definitive diagnosis frequently requires surgical evaluation [6, 8]. Beyond its clinical burden, endometriosis is associated with substantial psychosocial and economic consequences for affected women and healthcare systems [5, 7].

Migraine is another highly prevalent chronic condition among women of reproductive age and represents one of the leading causes of disability worldwide [9, 10]. In addition to recurrent headache attacks, migraine negatively affects physical, emotional, social, and occupational functioning, contributing substantially to reduced quality of life and healthcare burden [11–13].

Although endometriosis and migraine affect different organ systems, growing evidence suggests a strong comorbid relationship between these conditions [14–20]. Recent evidence indicates that women with endometriosis have an approximately fivefold higher risk of migraine than women without endometriosis [21]. Furthermore, more than half of women with endometriosis may experience migraine, particularly hormonally related migraine subtypes [22]. The coexistence of these disorders may therefore impose an additive burden on quality of life and daily functioning [23].

The management of endometriosis–migraine comorbidity remains challenging. Hormonal therapies commonly used for endometriosis may influence migraine symptoms, while the potential impact of endometriosis surgery on headache outcomes remains poorly understood [24, 25]. Although laparoscopic surgery is an important therapeutic option for selected patients with endometriosis, evidence regarding its association with headache burden and quality of life remains limited. Merki-Feld et al. reported a reduction in migraine frequency among women with endometriosis but emphasized that the specific contribution of endometriosis surgery remains unclear and requires further investigation [26].

Given the high prevalence of endometriosis–migraine comorbidity and the limited evidence regarding postoperative headache outcomes, evaluating headache burden and quality of life after endometriosis surgery may contribute to a better understanding of the interaction between these conditions and support multidisciplinary patient management. Therefore, the aim of this study was to examine changes in headache burden and quality of life following endometriosis surgery in women with and without migraine.

Methods

Study design

This study employed a single-center retrospective observational design. The study relied on retrospectively recalled preoperative assessments and a single postoperative evaluation rather than prospectively collected longitudinal measurements. Within this framework, preoperative and postoperative outcomes were compared within the same individuals based on patient recall, with the aim of examining differences between women with and without migraine who had undergone endometriosis surgery. Preoperative outcomes were assessed retrospectively based on participants’ recall, whereas postoperative outcomes were evaluated according to their current clinical status during the same interview session. Preoperative HIT-6 and SF-12 scores were not available from prospective clinical records and were therefore obtained retrospectively through participant recall during the postoperative interview. These retrospectively recalled preoperative measurements were considered the baseline assessments for the purposes of the study. All data were collected at a single postoperative time point. Participants were asked to respond to the questionnaires and scales by recalling their preoperative experiences and by reporting their current postoperative condition. The study was conducted without any intervention to routine clinical care. The study is reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) Statement.

Setting and participants

Participants were women of reproductive age who had undergone endometriosis surgery between 3 months and 5 years prior and who presented for follow-up at the Department of Obstetrics and Gynecology of a tertiary care hospital between February 3 and July 3, 2025. In all participants, the indication for surgery was based on the combination of persistent cystic lesions measuring ≥ 5 cm despite at least three months of regular follow-up, chronic pelvic pain, and ultrasonographic findings consistent with endometriosis. Women presenting for routine outpatient follow-up during the study period were assessed for eligibility during their clinical visit, and those meeting the eligibility criteria were consecutively invited to participate. Because recruitment was conducted as part of routine outpatient clinical practice, a prospective screening log was not maintained. Therefore, the total numbers of women assessed for eligibility, excluded, or declining participation could not be reconstructed retrospectively. The lower limit of 3 months was selected to allow recovery from the immediate postoperative period and to align with commonly used migraine assessment intervals requiring a minimum three-month symptom history. Sample size was calculated using G*Power version 3.1.9.4 to evaluate the interaction between time (preoperative vs. postoperative) and group (with vs. without migraine) for headache impact and quality-of-life outcomes [27]. The power analysis was conducted assuming 80% power, a medium effect size (Cohen’s f = 0.25) [28], sphericity of 1, and a 5% type I error rate. The minimum required sample size was estimated as 17 participants per group (total n = 34). The final sample comprised 88 women, including 44 with migraine and 44 without migraine. Migraine diagnoses were verified using hospital medical records.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria for both groups were: (i) age 19–50 years; (ii) history of endometriosis surgery between 3 months and 5 years prior; (iii) being of reproductive age; (iv) adequate cognitive ability to understand the questionnaires; (v) absence of chronic neurological disorders (e.g., epilepsy, multiple sclerosis). For the migraine group, an additional criterion was the absence of chronic neurological disorders other than migraine.

Exclusion criteria for both groups included: (i) natural or surgical menopause; (ii) pregnancy or breastfeeding; (iii) diagnosed psychiatric disorder; (iv) recent history of head or neck trauma; and (v) refusal to provide written informed consent.

Data collection procedure

Data were collected through face-to-face interviews with women attending routine follow-up visits at the outpatient clinic. Prior to data collection, eligible participants were informed about the study aims and procedures, and written informed consent was obtained. Participants were asked to complete the questionnaires and scales first by recalling their preoperative period and then by reporting their current postoperative status. Because prospectively collected preoperative HIT-6 and SF-12 scores were not available, participants were asked to retrospectively recall their preoperative headache burden and health-related quality of life. Prospective assessment remains the preferred method for establishing baseline patient-reported outcomes. When prospective baseline data cannot be obtained, retrospective patient recall may be used in observational postoperative outcome studies to approximate preoperative status, provided that the findings are interpreted with appropriate caution. A previous study suggests that retrospective assessment may provide a reasonable group-level estimate of preoperative health status in some clinical contexts, although it should not be regarded as an exact historical measure of baseline status [29]. However, agreement with prospectively collected scores varies across instruments, recall intervals, clinical conditions, and patient characteristics [30]. Retrospectively recalled scores may also systematically portray preoperative health as worse than it was, thereby exaggerating the apparent magnitude of postoperative change [31]. Accordingly, retrospectively recalled HIT-6 and SF-12 scores were considered estimates of perceived preoperative status rather than exact historical measurements and were interpreted with explicit consideration of potential recall bias. Reproductive health variables, including dysmenorrhea, dyspareunia, menstrual irregularity, infertility, and menstruation-related headache, were obtained through participant self-report during the interview and reflected recalled preoperative status and current status at the time of assessment. Infertility was defined as self-reported failure to achieve pregnancy after at least 12 months of regular unprotected intercourse. Migraine diagnosis was confirmed through responses in the Sociodemographic Form and verified using hospital records. Surgical treatment consisted of laparoscopic endometrioma cystectomy. When minimal endometriotic implants were observed during surgery, coagulation by electrocautery was performed according to routine clinical practice. More detailed baseline endometriosis and operative variables, including disease stage, lesion characteristics beyond size and persistence, lesion location, laterality, and completeness of excision, were not systematically available in the hospital records. Postoperative clinical information, including hormonal therapy, migraine-specific treatment modifications, and analgesic use, was also not systematically available and therefore could not be included in the analyses. Each interview lasted approximately 25 min.

Outcome measures

Data were collected using a structured questionnaire comprising three components: the Sociodemographic and Clinical Characteristics Form, the Headache Impact Test–6 (HIT-6), and the 12-Item Short Form Health Survey (SF-12).

Sociodemographic and clinical characteristics form

Developed by the researchers based on the literature [5, 7, 8, 14, 15, 17, 22], this form included questions on sociodemographic variables (e.g., age, education level), anthropometric measures (height and weight for body mass index calculation), and reproductive health characteristics, including the presence and severity of dysmenorrhea and dyspareunia before and after surgery, the presence of menstruation-related headache in the last three months before surgery and in the three months preceding the current assessment, and whether a physician had diagnosed migraine.

Headache Impact Test (HIT-6)

The HIT-6 was used to assess the impact of headache on daily functioning and quality of life. The scale consists of six items covering pain severity, role functioning, social functioning, vitality, emotional distress, and cognitive functioning. Gandek et al. (2003) demonstrated that the instrument has good comprehensibility and cross-cultural validity across 27 languages [32]. The Turkish validity and reliability study was conducted by Yalınay Dikmen et al. (2021) [33]. Total scores range from 36 to 78, with higher scores indicating greater headache-related impairment. Scores are interpreted as ≤ 49 (little or no impact), 50–55 (moderate impact), 56–59 (substantial impact), and ≥ 60 (severe impact). Cronbach’s α values were 0.753 for the first assessment and 0.864 for the second assessment [33].

Short Form-12 Health Survey (SF-12)

The SF-12 was used to assess general health-related quality of life during the preceding four weeks. Developed by Ware et al. (1995, 1996) as a shorter version of the SF-36 [34, 35], the SF-12 consists of 12 items and yields Physical Component Summary (PCS) and Mental Component Summary (MCS) scores, each ranging from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating better health. The Turkish validity and reliability study was conducted by Soylu and Kütük (2022). Cronbach’s α values were 0.742 for PCS and 0.759 for MCS [36].

Variables

The primary outcomes were changes in HIT-6, PCS, and MCS scores between the recalled preoperative period and the postoperative assessment. Preoperative and postoperative HIT-6, PCS, and MCS scores were defined as dependent variables. Independent variables included migraine status, sociodemographic characteristics, body mass index, and reproductive health variables. Clinical variables such as disease stage, postoperative hormonal therapy, migraine-specific treatment changes, and analgesic use were not included in the analytical model because these data were not systematically available for all participants. Migraine status was the primary grouping variable for between-group comparisons.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 25 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation, and categorical variables as frequency and percentage. Normality and homogeneity of variance assumptions were assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk and Levene tests. Between-group comparisons were conducted using the independent samples t-test when parametric assumptions were met and the Mann–Whitney U test otherwise. Comparisons among three or more groups were performed using one-way ANOVA with Tukey HSD post-hoc tests when assumptions were met, or the Kruskal–Wallis test with Bonferroni–Dunn post-hoc tests when assumptions were violated. Within-group comparisons were conducted using paired t-tests or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, as appropriate.

Assumptions for repeated-measures analyses were evaluated using Mauchly’s test of sphericity and Box’s M test for equality of covariance matrices. Mixed-design (group × assessment period) repeated-measures ANOVA was applied to the primary outcomes (HIT-6, PCS, and MCS scores), examining differences between women with and without migraine across the retrospectively recalled preoperative and current postoperative assessments. Although the preoperative assessment was obtained retrospectively through participant recall rather than prospectively, repeated-measures analysis was considered appropriate because each participant contributed two correlated assessments (retrospectively recalled preoperative and current postoperative), allowing evaluation of within-participant differences while simultaneously comparing women with and without migraine. Greenhouse–Geisser [37] or Huynh–Feldt [38] corrections were used when necessary. Multiple comparisons were adjusted using Bonferroni correction. Categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Changes in paired HIT-6 impact categories between the recalled preoperative and postoperative assessments were evaluated using the McNemar–Bowker test. Monte Carlo simulation was applied when expected cell frequencies were below 20%. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Effect sizes were reported as eta squared (η²) and interpreted as small (≈ 0.01), moderate (≈ 0.06), and large (≥ 0.14). Given the observational design and the retrospective assessment of preoperative outcomes, the repeated-measures analyses were intended to compare correlated assessments within individuals and explore associations rather than establish causal relationships. Potential confounding variables were not included as covariates in the primary analyses. Owing to the exploratory nature of the study and the limited sample size, no covariates were entered into the primary analytical model to minimize the risk of overfitting. To explore the potential influence of postoperative follow-up duration and to partially address possible recall bias, additional sensitivity analyses were performed by stratifying participants according to time since surgery (≤ 24 months, 25–48 months, and ≥ 49 months). Differences in change scores (ΔHIT-6, ΔPCS, and ΔMCS) across follow-up intervals were examined using one-way ANOVA. No missing data were identified for the primary outcome variables. Therefore, no imputation procedure was performed, and all 88 participants were included in the primary analyses.

Ethical considerations

The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki [39]. Ethical approval was obtained from the Mersin University Clinical Research Ethics Committee (approval date: January 8, 2025; approval number: 2025/034). Institutional permission was also obtained from Mersin University Hospital prior to data collection. All participants provided written informed consent, and confidentiality and anonymity of participant data were strictly maintained.

Results

A total of 88 eligible women who provided written informed consent were included in the analyses (44 with migraine and 44 without migraine). Because a prospective screening log was not maintained during routine outpatient recruitment, the total numbers of women assessed for eligibility, excluded, or declining participation were unavailable. The groups were similar with respect to age, body mass index, educational level, and marital status (all p > 0.05). Employment status differed significantly between groups, with a higher proportion of full-time employment among women with migraine (84.1% vs. 54.5%, p = 0.003). For PCS scores, a significant group effect was found for migraine status (F = 31.15, p < 0.001, η² = 0.26), along with significantly higher scores at the postoperative assessment (F = 182.16, p < 0.001, η² = 0.67). The estimated mean improvement from the recalled preoperative period to the postoperative assessment was 6.63 points (95% CI: 5.65–7.61). The group × time interaction was also significant (F = 20.23, p < 0.001, η² = 0.19).

Similarly, MCS scores differed significantly between groups (F = 62.00, p < 0.001, η² = 0.41) and showed significantly higher scores at the postoperative assessment (F = 304.24, p < 0.001, η² = 0.78). The estimated mean improvement from the recalled preoperative period to the postoperative assessment was 10.92 points (95% CI: 9.67–12.16). A significant interaction was also observed, indicating that the magnitude of improvement differed by migraine status (F = 83.28, p < 0.001, η² = 0.49).

HIT-6 scores also differed significantly between groups (F = 359.40, p < 0.001, η² = 0.80) and were lower at the postoperative assessment than in the recalled preoperative period (F = 147.70, p < 0.001, η² = 0.63). The estimated mean reduction in HIT-6 score was 4.00 points (95% CI: 3.35–4.65). The significant group × time interaction demonstrated differential change between groups (F = 6.52, p = 0.012, η² = 0.07).

Discussion

This study identified statistically significant differences between the recalled preoperative period and the postoperative assessment in headache burden and quality of life, with larger changes observed among women with migraine. The significant group, time, and group × time interaction effects across the primary outcome measures indicate that the magnitude of change differed by migraine status; however, given the retrospective assessment of preoperative outcomes, these findings should be interpreted cautiously and do not establish causality.

Although categorical analysis of HIT-6 impact levels could not be statistically tested in the migraine group due to all participants being classified in the “severe impact” category at the recalled preoperative assessment, postoperative distributions clearly shifted toward lower impact categories. In the non-migraine group, a statistically significant reduction in headache impact categories was observed. The concurrent differences observed in headache burden and reproductive health symptoms suggest that improvements in gynecologic symptoms and headache burden may occur simultaneously during the postoperative period.

The findings of this study are consistent with existing literature demonstrating a strong comorbid relationship between endometriosis and migraine [15–22]. In a systematic review, Gómez-Dabó et al. (2025) reported that women with endometriosis have an approximately fivefold increased risk of developing migraine [21]. Pasquini et al. (2023) similarly found that 53.4% of women with endometriosis were diagnosed with migraine, with a substantial proportion classified as menstrually related migraine [22]. These findings align with the present study, in which nearly all women in the migraine group reported menstruation-related headache and exhibited a very high headache burden preoperatively.

Merki-Feld et al. (2024) reported that endometriosis–migraine comorbidity is particularly associated with migraine with aura and observed a significant reduction in migraine attack frequency over time; however, they emphasized the need to clarify whether this improvement is attributable to endometriosis surgery [26]. In this context, preliminary evidence suggests an association between surgical excision of endometriotic lesions and lower migraine frequency, indicating that surgery may be related to migraine outcomes [40]. The present findings extend this limited evidence base by demonstrating that endometriosis surgery is associated with clinically meaningful improvements in headache burden as well as in both the physical and mental components of quality of life. The larger effect sizes observed among women with migraine further suggest that the association between surgery and reduced headache-related disability may be more pronounced in this subgroup.

The observed postoperative decline in menstruation-related headache and reproductive health symptoms, including dysmenorrhea, dyspareunia, and menstrual irregularity, suggests that improvements in gynecologic symptoms and headache burden may occur simultaneously during the postoperative period. Previous studies have suggested that hormonal and inflammatory mechanisms may contribute to the coexistence of endometriosis and migraine [41–48]. Recent reviews have further emphasized the potential role of inflammatory pathways in endometriosis pathophysiology, including cytokine signaling, oxidative stress, and immune dysregulation [49]. In addition, lifestyle and dietary factors may influence symptom burden and quality of life in women with endometriosis, although the available evidence remains limited and heterogeneous [50]. However, these pathways were not evaluated in the present study and therefore no mechanistic conclusions can be drawn from the current findings. Consistent with previous literature, Smirnoff and Moskatel (2025) reported that women with chronic migraine exhibit significantly higher rates of pelvic pain syndromes and dyspareunia than women without migraine, further supporting the close relationship between migraine and gynecologic pain disorders [51].

An additional important finding of this study was the significant improvement in headache impact observed in women without migraine following endometriosis surgery. Preoperatively, most women in this group were classified as having moderate or substantial headache impact, whereas postoperative assessments showed a shift toward lower impact categories. These findings suggest that improvements in headache burden were observed not only among women with migraine but also among women without a formal migraine diagnosis. However, because headache subtypes were not classified using neurological evaluation and/or prospective headache diaries, headache symptoms reported within the menstrual window were interpreted as menstruation-related headache rather than as pure menstrual migraine or menstrually related migraine according to the International Classification of Headache Disorders, 3rd edition (ICHD-3) [52].

Study limitations

This study has several limitations. First, preoperative HIT-6 and SF-12 outcomes were assessed retrospectively based on patient recall, which may have introduced recall bias. The accuracy of recalled patient-reported outcomes may vary according to the instrument used, the length of the recall interval, the clinical context, and individual patient characteristics. In particular, retrospective assessments may not reproduce exact historical symptom burden or health-related quality of life and may portray preoperative status as worse than it was. Consequently, retrospectively recalled HIT-6 scores may have been higher and PCS and MCS scores lower than would have been obtained through prospective assessment, potentially inflating the apparent magnitude of postoperative improvement. However, the precise magnitude of this potential bias could not be quantified in the present study because prospectively collected baseline assessments were unavailable. Prospectively collected baseline questionnaires and headache diaries would therefore provide more reliable temporal and individual-level measurements.

Second, although migraine diagnoses were verified through hospital records, the diagnostic process was not confirmed using a structured neurological evaluation based on the International Classification of Headache Disorders, 3rd edition (ICHD-3), or through prospective headache diaries. This limitation restricted the precise classification of headache subtypes, particularly menstrual migraine. Consequently, headaches reported during the menstrual window were interpreted as menstruation-related headache rather than as specific ICHD-3 diagnostic entities.

Third, this was a single-center study with a relatively limited sample size, which may restrict the generalizability of the findings. Furthermore, because detailed participant screening and recruitment data were not prospectively recorded during routine outpatient recruitment, the representativeness of the study sample and the potential for selection bias could not be fully evaluated. If women who attended routine postoperative follow-up differed systematically from those who did not attend follow-up, the observed postoperative outcomes may not fully reflect the experiences of the broader population of women undergoing endometriosis surgery. In addition, because hormonal profiles, inflammatory markers, and biological mediators were not measured, the underlying mechanisms responsible for the observed postoperative clinical changes could not be directly evaluated. In addition, more detailed baseline endometriosis and operative variables, including disease stage, lesion characteristics beyond size and persistence, lesion location, laterality, and completeness of excision, were not systematically available in the hospital records and therefore could not be evaluated as potential confounding variables. Postoperative clinical information, including hormonal therapy, migraine-specific treatment changes, and analgesic use, was also not systematically available; these factors may have influenced headache and quality-of-life outcomes and should be considered as potential sources of residual confounding.

Finally, postoperative assessments were conducted at a single time point, while the time since surgery varied between 3 months and 5 years, which may have introduced heterogeneity in postoperative outcomes. To partially address this issue, additional sensitivity analyses stratified by time since surgery were performed and showed a broadly similar pattern of changes across follow-up intervals. Nevertheless, residual heterogeneity related to postoperative duration cannot be completely excluded.

Because preoperative outcomes were assessed retrospectively, recall bias may have contributed to an overestimation of baseline symptom burden and, consequently, the magnitude of postoperative change. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than establishing a causal relationship. In addition, the observed differences may also reflect concomitant medical treatment, changes in migraine management, natural symptom variability over time, and regression to the mean, in addition to any potential association with surgical intervention.

Clinical implications

The findings of this study suggest that endometriosis surgery may be associated with lower headache-related functional impairment and better quality of life in the postoperative period. These findings support the potential value of incorporating routine headache screening into endometriosis care and, conversely, systematically evaluating gynecologic symptoms in headache clinics. In addition, nurses specializing in women’s health and neurology play a pivotal role in the care process through symptom monitoring, education on headache and menstrual diaries, promotion of treatment adherence, and patient counseling. Accordingly, increasing awareness of endometriosis–migraine comorbidity among nurses working in women’s health and neurology may contribute to improving the quality of care delivered to women. Multidisciplinary care models involving gynecology and neurology professionals may further contribute to improved clinical outcomes and strengthened patient-centered management.
