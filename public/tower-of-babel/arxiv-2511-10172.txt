Introduction

Propellant sloshing is a well-known, but not completely mastered phenomenon in space vehicles. It is particularly critical in both microgravity environments—such as interplanetary spacecraft requiring high pointing stability—and high-g conditions, as encountered during launch, re-entry, and landing. In both cases, sloshing can significantly affect vehicle performance and stability, and must often be explicitly considered in the design of the guidance, navigation, and control (GNC) subsystem.

For stability analysis and control design, the most common approach to modeling sloshing is through an equivalent mechanical representation
[
1
]
, where the moving propellant is treated as a mechanical system interacting with the rigid (or flexible) spacecraft. Pendulum-based models and mass-spring-damper systems are widely used by control analysts to assess sloshing-induced perturbations on vehicles subjected to persistent non-gravitational acceleration along one of their body axes.

In this work, we present a rigorous mathematical formulation of pendulum dynamics, starting from a single spherical pendulum attached to a rigid spacecraft. We derive the nonlinear equations of motion for this 8-degree-of-freedom multi-body system, and then extend the formulation to include multiple pendulums, representing multiple sloshing modes within a tank and/or multiple tanks on the same vehicle. Furthermore, we derive the corresponding linearized equations of motion, explicitly accounting for a nominal longitudinal force acting on the vehicle – consistent with the high-g sloshing regime – expressed in either the inertial or body frame. Finally, we demonstrate the mathematical equivalence between the pendulum and mass-spring-damper models and validate the proposed models through time-domain simulation and frequency-domain analysis.

3
Linearized pendulum model

In the previous section we have derived the nonlinear equations of motion of the system using the absolute position of the rigid body’s center of mass
{\bm{r}}^{I}_{OB}
as a state. When it comes to the design, analysis, and implementation of a controller for such a system, working with
{\bm{r}}^{I}_{OB}
might not be the best choice. More specifically, in a concrete implementation, the on-board software will likely estimate the global MCI of the system neglecting sloshing, i.e., assuming that all propellant mass is static, and will try to control its position (and velocity) as needed by applying on it the appropriate control torques and forces. Therefore, when deriving a linearized model of the system, it is worth relying on this "nominal" global center of mass to describe the system, rather than using directly
B
. With this approach, the noinal center of mass, which we indicate with
\bar{G}
, will have constant coordinates in the rigid body fixed reference frame.

The mass-spring-damper model is an alternative mechanical model that can be used to represent sloshing dynamics. Model validation

5.1
Nonlinear model

The validation of the nonlinear model was carried out by injecting open-loop force and torque commands into the plant and comparing the time response of the Simscape model with the model derived in this work. The input time profiles and the corresponding responses are shown in Figure
3
, along with the difference between the Simscape and our model responses in green (scaled by a factor of 1000). The comparison indicates an excellent match, and the residual numerical discrepancies are attributed to the different numerical propagators used for simulation: while Simscape employs its own fixed-step RK5 algorithm, our analytical model was implemented in Julia using
DifferentialEquations.jl
’s
DP5()
fixed-step propagator. Both models used the same timestep of 1 ms. The maximum temporal error is less than 0.002% of the maximum absolute value of the time response for all position and attitude states.

Figure 3:
Time response of the nonlinear models to open-loop inputs.

5.2
Linear model

Validation of the linearized models was carried out by comparing the Bode plots of the Simscape linearized plant with those of the analytical model. The comparison was performed for input forces expressed in the inertial frame (Figure
4
) and in the body frame (Figure
5
). The vertical translational dynamics, being a decoupled double integrator, is not included in the plots. The results show an almost perfect match in both cases, except for a minor discrepancy in the low-frequency range when considering forces in the inertial frame. This discrepancy is attributed to the limited numerical accuracy of Simscape’s plant linearization, as it cannot be explained by any physical behavior of the system.

6
Conclusion

This work developed both nonlinear and linearized equivalent mechanical models for propellant sloshing under high-g conditions. It also demonstrated the equivalence between the linearized pendulum model and the linearized mass-spring-damper model. Validation was performed by comparing time and frequency responses with corresponding MATLAB Simscape models, confirming the correctness of the derived models.
