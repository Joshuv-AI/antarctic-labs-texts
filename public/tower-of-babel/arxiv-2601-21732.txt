Neural Wasserstein Two-Sample Tests
Xiaoyu Hu

Abstract
The two-sample homogeneity testing problem is fundamental in statistics and becomes particularly challenging in high dimensions, where classical tests can suffer substantial power loss. We develop a learning-assisted procedure based on the projection 1 1 -Wasserstein distance, which we call the neural Wasserstein test . The method is motivated by the observation that there often exists a low-dimensional projection under which the two high-dimensional distributions differ. In practice, we learn the projection directions via manifold optimization and a witness function using deep neural networks. To adapt to unknown projection dimensions and sparsity levels, we aggregate a collection of candidate statistics through a max-type construction, avoiding explicit tuning while potentially improving power. We establish the validity and consistency of the proposed test and prove a Berry–Esseen type bound for the Gaussian approximation. In particular, under the null hypothesis, the aggregated statistic converges to the absolute maximum of a standard Gaussian vector, yielding an asymptotically pivotal (distribution-free) calibration that bypasses resampling. Simulation studies and a real-data example demonstrate the strong finite-sample performance of the proposed method.



Keywords:
deep neural network, max statistic, high dimensions, sample splitting, test of homogeneity, Wasserstein distance

Introduction

The problem of the two-sample homogeneity hypothesis test is of fundamental importance in statistics and practice

;

;

Moreover, this test plays a crucial role in transfer learning, where it is often used to identify the nature of distributional shifts and to guide the selection of appropriate learning strategies

With the increasing prevalence of high-dimensional data, there is an increasing demand for methods tailored to such data. Our primary focus is on devising a valid and powerful test suitable for scenarios with diverging dimensions.

For the two-sample homogeneity test, one popular approach is based on kernel-based statistics
(
56
)
, including maximum mean discrepancy (MMD)
(
22
)
and energy distance
(
60
)
.
Due to their simplicity and wide applicability, these kernel-based methods, especially MMD, have attracted much attention in both methodological and theoretical developments
(
21
;
23
;
59
;
31
)
.
To improve statistical power,
39
proposed a method involving the learning of a kernel function parameterized by deep neural nets on the training data, followed by the implementation of an MMD test using the learned kernel function and permutation on the test data.
Additionally, to avoid permutations,
34
;
57
utilized sample splitting to learn a one-dimensional function related to the kernel and construct a test statistic with a standard Gaussian limiting null distribution.
These approaches are not particularly developed for high-dimensional contexts, which may result in limited power in high dimensions; see Section
5
.
In high-dimensional settings,
74
investigated the performance of kernel-based tests with permutation.
18
;
71
introduced studentized MMD and studied the asymptotic properties as the dimension and the sample size diverge. Their theoretical results highlight intrinsic limitations of kernel-based methods in power under high-dimensional regimes.
Moreover,
52
demonstrated that the power of MMD can decay rapidly as the dimension increases under fair alternatives, a phenomenon that we also observe for other comparative methods in Section
5
.

In contrast to kernel-based statistics, the Wasserstein distance has emerged as a powerful tool for measuring the discrepancies between distributions, thanks to its ability to preserve the underlying geometry of the distribution space, which has achieved success in many tasks, such as generative modeling
(
4
)
. However, the curse of dimensionality hinders its application in statistical estimation and hypothesis testing problems. For example,
28
attempted to employ the Wasserstein distance in the two-sample test, but the convergence rate therein deteriorates rapidly with increasing dimensionality. To mitigate the curse of dimensionality, several variants of the Wasserstein distance are proposed, including the smoothed Wasserstein distance
(
19
)
, sliced or max-sliced Wasserstein distance and projection Wasserstein distance
(
51
;
12
;
37
)
. In the case of low and fixed dimensions, there are works studying distributional limits of these variants and their applications in the two-sample test
(
46
;
20
;
54
;
69
)
.
However, these results do not extend to high-dimensional regimes.
The exploration of Wasserstein-based methods for high-dimensional two-sample testing remains relatively limited, apart from a small number of recent studies.

68
adopted the projected Wasserstein distance as a test statistic and employed a permutation test in practice. In parallel to our development,
26
proposed a test based on the max-sliced Wasserstein distance combined with bootstrapping.
However, these approaches can suffer from relatively low power due to the use of a single projection dimension and substantial computational overhead arising from resampling.

Our contributions

We develop a new learning-assisted and Wasserstein-based two-sample testing framework for high-dimensional data, with the following main contributions.

•

Projection-Wasserstein test statistic with deep neural network approximation.
Building on the projection 1-Wasserstein distance, we propose a two-sample test that learns (i) discriminative projection directions on the Stiefel manifold and (ii) an approximate 1-Lipschitz witness function using deep neural networks.

•

Tuning-free adaptivity via max-type aggregation.
To adapt to unknown projection dimensions and sparsity levels, we aggregate statistics over a candidate set of hyperparameters using a max-type statistic, avoiding delicate tuning while potentially improving power. Theoretical power analysis under local alternatives are provided for the proposed test with and without sparsity regularizations.

•

Pivotal limiting null and nonasymptotic guarantees.
We establish a Berry–Esseen type bound for Gaussian approximation and show that the aggregated test has a pivotal limiting null distribution, regardless of the ambient dimension and underlying distributions. This pivotal structure yields substantial computational savings compared to resampling-based methods such as permutation and bootstrap.

Specifically, the proposed test leverages the Kantorovich–Rubinstein duality to express the projection 1-Wasserstein distance
between the distributions of
X,Y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
as

\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F},U\in\mathcal{S}_{d,k}}\{\mathbb{E}f(U^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}X)-\mathbb{E}f(U^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}Y)\},

(1)

where
k
is the projection dimension,
\mathcal{F}=\{f:\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\mathbb{R}\mid f\text{ is 1-Lipschitz, }f(0)=0\}
and
\mathcal{S}_{d,k}=\{U\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times k}:U^{{\scriptscriptstyle\top}}U=I_{k}\}
. In contrast with existing works
(
68
, e.g.,)
utilizing a similar distance but bypassing estimation of the optimal
f

our strategy is to estimate a discriminative function
f
and projection directions
U
that achieve the supremum of (
1

our theoretical and numeric studies demonstrate the strength of this strategy. Further distinctions between our approach and the method proposed by

are discussed in Remark

.

Practically, to decouple the dependence caused by estimation, we employ data splitting to divide the available dataset into two disjoint subsets. The discriminative function and directions are trained on one subset, and the test statistic is constructed using the other subset. The training phase involves estimating the projection directions
U
and the 1-Lipschitz function
f
. However, optimizing both
f
and
U
jointly turns out to be too complex to achieve satisfactory numerical performance. To address this issue, we propose a practically effective two-step algorithm in which the optimization procedures for
U
and
f
are separated.
For

, we consider both unregularized estimation and regularized estimation under
\ell_{1}
and
\ell_{0}
sparsity constraints to achieve adaptivity to unknown sparsity levels. The non-smoothness and the manifold constraint motivate us to adopt the proximal gradient method for manifold optimization to obtain the discriminative
U

(
9
)
.
For
f
, motivated by the excellent approximation power of deep neural networks, we adopt deep neural networks (where the number of layers grows with the sample size) to learn the discriminative function.

The proposed test features several advantages. First, unlike data-driven calibration methods such as bootstrapping and permutation
(
74
;
68
)
, the proposed pivotal test statistic enables a scalable computation. Second, in contrast with the studentized MMD, of which the limiting null distributions depend on both diverging dimension and sample size
(see
18
;
71
, for detailed discussions)
, our result holds irrespective of the dimensionality, underlying population or training algorithms; see Figures S.1 and S.2 for illustration. In practice, it could be challenging to determine whether the dimensionality is sufficiently large for applying the asymptotic null distribution of the studentized MMD test.
Third, the proposed approach adapts to unknown projection dimensions and sparsity levels through appropriate aggregation, thereby eliminating the intricate step of selecting the projection dimension and the sparsity parameter. It has the potential to effectively capture the disparities between distributions via integrating information across multiple projection directions and regularization parameters in the proposed max-type statistic. The numerical results in Figure S.6 showcase the benefits of utilizing additional projections in some challenging scenarios.

2
A distribution-free test statistic

2.1
Projection Wasserstein distance and two-sample tests

Let
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be the set of Borel probability measures on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with the finite first moment.
According to Definition 6.1 of
65
, the 1-Wasserstein distance (hereafter, Wasserstein distance) between two probability measures
\mu
and
\nu
in
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is

W(\mu,\nu)=\inf_{\pi\in\Gamma(\mu,\nu)}\int\|x-y\|_{2}d\pi(x,y),

where
\Gamma(\mu,\nu)
represents the set of joint distributions on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
with marginals
\mu
and
\nu
.

The Wasserstein distance, also known as the optimal transport distance, characterizes the minimal cost required to transport one distribution to another. It preserves the geometry of the space of distributions and finds wide applications in statistics and machine learning. Despite advantages of the Wasserstein distance, its empirical estimation suffers from the curse of dimensionality
(
13
;
16
;
36
)
.
One popular strategy to alleviate this issue is through projection
(
37
;
47
, e.g.,)
.

For
\mathscr{X},\mathscr{Y}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
T:\mathscr{X}\to\mathscr{Y}
and
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{X})
, let
T_{\#}\mu
denote the push-forward of
\mu
by
T
, i.e.,
T_{\#}\mu(A)=\mu(T^{-1}(A))
for any Borel set
A
in
\mathscr{Y}
. Also, each element
U\in\mathcal{S}_{d,k}
is viewed as a linear function
U(x)=U^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}x
for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The
k
-dimensional projection Wasserstein distance
(
49
;
47
)
between
\mu
and
\nu
is

PW_{k}(\mu,\nu)=\sup_{U\in\mathcal{S}_{d,k}}W(U_{\#}\mu,U_{\#}\nu)=\sup_{U\in\mathcal{S}_{d,k}}\inf_{\pi\in\Gamma(\mu,\nu)}\int\|U^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}(x-y)\|_{2}d\pi(x,y).

(2)

The following Lemma
1
, due to
49
, asserts that
PW_{k}
is a proper distance over
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

Lemma 1

(Paty and Cuturi, 2019)
.

The
PW_{k}
is a distance over
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Also, for
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, there exists a
U_{0}\in\mathcal{S}_{d,k}
such that

PW_{k}(\mu,\nu)=W(U_{0\#}\mu,U_{0\#}\nu).

From Lemma
1
, we conclude that
PW_{k}(\mu,\nu)
vanishes if and only if
\mu=\nu
. Moreover, the supremum in (
2
) can be achieved, and we call
U_{0}
an optimal projection.

The projection Wasserstein distance
PW_{k}(\mu,\nu)
is the same as the Wasserstein distance
W(\mu,\nu)
if the two distributions
\mu
and
\nu
satisfy the spiked transport model with the subspace dimension
k
, see Proposition 3 in
47
.

Given two independent random samples in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
X_{1},\dots X_{n_{x}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i.i.d.}}{{\sim}}\mu
and
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n_{y}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i.i.d.}}{{\sim}}\nu
, the goal is to test the hypothesis

H_{0}:\mu=\nu\qquad\text{versus}\qquad H_{1}:\mu\neq\nu,

where
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
are two underlying distributions. In light of Lemma
1
, the null hypothesis is equivalent to
PW_{k}(\mu,\nu)=0
, based on which one can devise a test by using the sample version of
PW_{k}(\mu,\nu)

(
68
, e.g.,)
. One difficulty of such test is to accurately estimate the p-value or critical value.

2.2
Overview of the proposed new two-sample test

Instead of directly adopting the distance of (
2
) and its sample version, we first utilize Lemma
1
and the Kantorovich-Rubinstein theorem
(
64
)
to observe

PW_{k}(\mu,\nu)=W(U_{0\#}\mu,U_{0\#}\nu)=\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\{\mathbb{E}f(U_{0}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\top}}X)-\mathbb{E}f(U_{0}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\top}}Y)\},

(3)

where
X\sim\mu,Y\sim\nu
,
\mathcal{F}=\{f:\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\mathbb{R}\mid f\text{ is 1-Lipschitz, }f(0)=0\}
and
U_{0}
is the optimal projection in Lemma
1
.
If
PW_{k}(\mu,\nu)\neq 0
,
then
\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\{\mathbb{E}f(U_{0}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\top}}X)-\mathbb{E}f(U_{0}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\top}}Y)\}\neq 0
and consequently there exists a function
f_{0}\in\mathcal{F}
such that
\mathbb{E}g_{0}(X)-\mathbb{E}g_{0}(Y)\neq 0
for
g_{0}=f_{0}\circ U_{0}
. Conceptually, we would choose
f_{0}
such that
\mathbb{E}g_{0}(X)-\mathbb{E}g_{0}(Y)
is as close to
PW_{k}(\mu,\nu)
as possible, as discussed in Appendix
B
. It is intuitive to construct a test statistic based on the sample version of
\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\{\mathbb{E}f(U_{0}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\top}}X)-\mathbb{E}f(U_{0}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\top}}Y)\}
and estimates of
U_{0}
. Moreover, the representation in (
3
) motivates an effective two-step procedure, where
U_{0}
is estimated first, followed by the estimation of
f_{0}
. The full estimation procedure is detailed in Section
2.3
, and the resulting test statistic is presented in Section
2.4
. It turns out that, estimating the null distribution of this test statistic is much more tractable, making it easier to compute the p-value or critical value.

There are two difficulties in finding an estimate
\hat{f}
of the oracle discriminative function
f_{0}
and an estimate
\hat{U}
of the direction
U_{0}
from observed data. First, the Lipschitz class is too large to ensure the estimation accuracy.
Second, it is hard to construct a pivotal test statistic due to the complex correlation among the quantities
\hat{f}(\hat{U}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}X_{i})
and
\hat{f}(\hat{U}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}Y_{j})
for
i=1,\dots,n_{x}
and
j=1,\dots,n_{y}
.
To tackle these issues, we resort to a suitably chosen function class for estimation and utilize the sample splitting technique to decouple the dependence. Below we elaborate these ideas.

For a suitable function class, we consider estimating
f_{0}
using neural networks with rectified linear unit (ReLU) activation functions, which have successful applications in various fields and have gained increasing attention in statistics
(
55
;
73
;
15
;
72
)
. Specifically, consider the network architecture
(D,\mathbf{p})
consisting of
D\in\mathbb{N}
hidden layers and a width vector
\mathbf{p}=(p_{0},\dots,p_{D+1})\in\mathbb{N}^{D+2}
. Let
A_{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{\ell+1}\times p_{\ell}}
and
b_{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{\ell+1}}
be a weight matrix and a bias vector, respectively, for the
\ell
-th layer. Let
\sigma(x)=\max(x,0)
be the ReLU activation function. A neural network with the network architecture
(D,\mathbf{p})
is any function of the following form

\phi:\mathbb{R}^{p_{0}}\to\mathbb{R}^{p_{D+1}},\quad\phi(x)=\mathcal{L}_{D}\circ\sigma\circ\mathcal{L}_{D-1}\circ\sigma\cdots\sigma\circ\mathcal{L}_{1}\circ\sigma\circ\mathcal{L}_{0}(x),

(4)

where
\mathcal{L}_{\ell}(x)=A_{\ell}x+b_{\ell}
for
\ell=0,\dots D.
The width
L
is defined as the maximum width of the hidden layers, i.e.,
L=\max\{p_{1},\cdots,p_{D}\}
.
In this paper, we take the following functional class

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}(D,\mathbf{p},s,B):=\bigg\{\phi\text{ of the form \eqref{eq:func-nn}}:

\displaystyle\max_{\ell=0,\dots,D}\|A_{\ell}\|_{\infty}\vee\|b_{\ell}\|_{\infty}\leq 1,

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=0}^{D}\|A_{\ell}\|_{0}+\|b_{\ell}\|_{0}\leq s,\|\phi\|_{\infty}\leq B\bigg\}.

To overcome the complex correlation among the estimators, we employ sample splitting to decouple the dependence. Specifically, we randomly partition the indices
1,\ldots,n_{x}
into disjoint
\tilde{\mathcal{I}}_{X}
and
\mathcal{I}_{X}
such that
\{1,\ldots,n_{x}\}=\tilde{\mathcal{I}}_{X}\cup\mathcal{I}_{X}
, and partition the indices
1,\ldots,n_{y}
into
\tilde{\mathcal{I}}_{Y}
and
\mathcal{I}_{Y}
such that
\{1,\ldots,n_{y}\}=\tilde{\mathcal{I}}_{Y}\cup\mathcal{I}_{Y}
. With these partitions, we estimate
f_{0}
and
U_{0}
by using the observations
\mathcal{D}_{fit}=\mathcal{X}_{fit}\cup\mathcal{Y}_{fit}
, where
\mathcal{X}_{fit}=\{X_{i},i\in\tilde{\mathcal{I}}_{X}\}
and
\mathcal{Y}_{fit}=\{Y_{j},j\in\tilde{\mathcal{I}}_{Y}\}
, and then construct a test statistic based on the observations
\mathcal{D}_{test}=\{X_{i},i\in\mathcal{I}_{X}\}\cup\{Y_{j},j\in\mathcal{I}_{Y}\}
. This sample-splitting strategy ensures that the estimators
\hat{f}
and
\hat{U}
, obtained from the training subset, are independent of the testing subset used to construct the test statistic. Consequently, conditional on
\hat{f}
and
\hat{U}
, the key quantity
\hat{S}_{n}
(to be defined in (
9
)) becomes a weighted average of independent random variables. This decoupling of the complex dependence between the estimators and the test statistic is crucial for establishing the validity of our test and enables a rigorous power analysis.

We are now ready to present the main idea of the proposed approach. By using the fitting data
\mathcal{D}_{fit}
, we estimate
U_{0}
and employ deep neural networks in
\mathcal{N}(D,\mathbf{p},s,B)
to estimate
f_{0}
; details are provided in Section
2.3
. Once we obtain the estimator
\hat{g}=\hat{f}\circ\hat{U}
, we construct the test statistic on the rest of the data
\mathcal{D}_{test}
, which is detailed in Section
2.4
.

2.3
An effective algorithm for computing
(\hat{f},\hat{U})

The representation in (
3
) motivates us to estimate
U_{0}
and
f_{0}
separately in two steps. In the first step, by utilizing the primal formulation in (
2
), we estimate
U_{0}
without involving the function space of
f_{0}
.
After obtaining
\hat{U}
, in the second step we train the discriminative function by fitting a deep neural network on the projected data
\{\hat{U}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}X:X\in\mathcal{X}_{fit}\}
and
\{\hat{U}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}Y:Y\in\mathcal{Y}_{fit}\}
. Below we outline these two steps.

Step 1.
Let
\tilde{n}_{x}=|\tilde{\mathcal{I}}_{X}|
and
\tilde{n}_{y}=|\tilde{\mathcal{I}}_{Y}|
, and define

\mu_{n}=\frac{1}{\tilde{n}_{x}}\sum_{i\in\tilde{\mathcal{I}}_{X}}\delta_{X_{i}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\nu_{n}=\frac{1}{\tilde{n}_{y}}\sum_{j\in\tilde{\mathcal{I}}_{Y}}\delta_{Y_{j}},

(5)

where
\delta_{x}
denotes the Dirac delta function at
x
.
We first describe a generic framework for estimating
U_{0}\in\mathcal{U}(\varrho)
for subset
U(\varrho)
, where
U(\varrho)
is a subset of
\mathcal{S}_{d,k}
and determined by a regularization parameter
\varrho
(e.g., sparsity level). This leads to the following estimator

\displaystyle\hat{U}\in\mathop{\arg\max}\limits_{U\in\mathcal{U}(\varrho)}\bigg\{\min\limits_{\pi\in\Gamma(\mu_{n},\nu_{n})}\sum_{i\in\tilde{\mathcal{I}}_{X}}\sum_{j\in\tilde{\mathcal{I}}_{Y}}\pi_{ij}\|U^{{\scriptscriptstyle\top}}(X_{i}-Y_{j})\|_{2}\bigg\},

(6)

where
and

\Gamma(\mu_{n},\nu_{n})=\left\{\pi\in\mathbb{R}^{\tilde{n}_{x}\times\tilde{n}_{y}}:\sum_{i\in\tilde{\mathcal{I}}_{X}}\pi_{ij}=\frac{1}{\tilde{n}_{y}}\text{ and }\sum_{j\in\tilde{\mathcal{I}}_{Y}}\pi_{ij}=\frac{1}{\tilde{n}_{x}}\right\}.

While a natural choice of
\mathcal{U}(\varrho)
is
\mathcal{S}_{d,k}
, additional structural constraints can be incorporated to help achieve adaptivity and favorable performance in high dimensions. Specific choices of the constraint set
\mathcal{U}(\varrho)
depending on some regularization parameter
\varrho
and the corresponding estimation algorithms are developed later.

Step 2.
After obtaining an estimator
\hat{U}
in the first step, we use deep neural networks to train the discriminative function on
\mathcal{D}_{fit}
. Specifically, we find the estimate

\hat{f}\in\mathop{\arg\max}_{f\in\mathcal{N}(D,\mathbf{p},s,B)}\left\{\frac{1}{\tilde{n}_{x}}\sum_{i\in\tilde{\mathcal{I}}_{X}}f(\hat{U}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\top}}X_{i})-\frac{1}{\tilde{n}_{y}}\sum_{j\in\tilde{\mathcal{I}}_{Y}}f(\hat{U}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\top}}Y_{j})\right\}.

(7)

For the network parameters
D
,
\mathbf{p}
,
s
and
B
, the theoretical analysis in Theorem
1
suggests the choices of these parameters do not have much influence on the size of the proposed test. In constrast, the power analysis demonstrates that these neural network parameters do affect the test power, and practical guidelines for their selection are provided later.

Remark 1
.

Throughout this paper,
f_{0}
represents the conceptual oracle discriminative function, while
f
denotes a generic function from a specified function class. We emphasize that the oracle discriminative function
f_{0}
belongs to the class of 1-Lipschitz functions
\mathcal{F}=\{f:\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\mathbb{R}\mid f\text{ is 1-Lipschitz, }f(0)=0\}
. We do not assume that
f_{0}
itself lies within the class of neural networks. Neural networks are employed solely to approximate this class and obtain the estimator
\hat{f}
.

Remark 2
.

Instead of the proposed two-step procedure, one might consider estimating
\hat{f}
and
\hat{U}
simultaneously by solving

(\hat{f},\hat{U})\in\underset{f\in\mathcal{N}(D,\mathbf{p},s,B),U\in\mathcal{U}(\varrho)}{\arg\max}\left\{\frac{1}{|\tilde{\mathcal{I}}_{X}|}\sum_{i\in\tilde{\mathcal{I}}_{X}}f(U^{{\scriptscriptstyle\top}}X_{i})-\frac{1}{|\tilde{\mathcal{I}}_{Y}|}\sum_{j\in\tilde{\mathcal{I}}_{Y}}f(U^{{\scriptscriptstyle\top}}Y_{j})\right\}.

(8)

However, in practice, optimizing (
8
) poses significant challenges. For instance, we find that directly optimizing both
f
and
U
using an alternating algorithm often fails to achieve satisfactory power.

2.4
The test statistic via aggregating multiple projection dimensions and regularization parameters

After obtaining
\hat{U}
and
\hat{f}
, based on the rest of the data
\mathcal{D}_{test}
, one may construct the following test statistic

\left(\frac{\bar{n}_{x}\bar{n}_{y}}{\bar{n}_{x}+\bar{n}_{y}}\right)^{1/2}\frac{S_{n}(\hat{g})}{\hat{\sigma}},

where
\bar{n}_{x}=|\mathcal{I}_{X}|
and
\bar{n}_{y}=|\mathcal{I}_{Y}|
are respectively the number of observations of

and

in the test data
\mathcal{D}_{test}
, and

\displaystyle S_{n}(\hat{g})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\bar{n}_{x}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{X}}\hat{g}(X_{i})-\frac{1}{\bar{n}_{y}}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}_{Y}}\hat{g}(Y_{j}),

(9)

\displaystyle\hat{\sigma}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\bar{n}_{y}}{\bar{n}_{x}+\bar{n}_{y}}\frac{1}{\bar{n}_{x}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{X}}\big(\hat{g}(X_{i})-\mathbb{E}_{\bar{n}_{x}}(\hat{g}(X))\big)^{2}+

\displaystyle\qquad\frac{\bar{n}_{x}}{\bar{n}_{x}+\bar{n}_{y}}\frac{1}{\bar{n}_{y}}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}_{Y}}\big(\hat{g}(Y_{j})-\mathbb{E}_{\bar{n}_{y}}(\hat{g}(Y))\big)^{2};

in the above,
\mathbb{E}_{\bar{n}_{x}}\hat{g}(X)=\bar{n}_{x}^{-1}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{X}}\hat{g}(X_{i})
and
\mathbb{E}_{\bar{n}_{y}}\hat{g}(Y)=\bar{n}_{y}^{-1}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}_{Y}}\hat{g}(Y_{j})
.

A crucial issue is the choice of the projection dimension
k
and the regularization parameter
\varrho
for estimating the optimal projection. While these parameters do not affect the size, as evidenced in Theorem
1
, they influence the power performance.
To get around this delicate issue, we propose to aggregate strength from multiple values of
(k,\varrho)
via a max-type test statistic, as follows.
Given a candidate set of hyperparameters
\mathcal{C}=\{(k_{j},\varrho_{j}),j=1,\dots,m\}
, let
\hat{S}_{n}=(S_{n}(\hat{g}_{1}),\dots,S_{n}(\hat{g}_{m}))^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}
, where
\hat{g}_{j}
is the corresponding estimator using the hyperparameter pair
(k_{j},\varrho_{j})
for
j=1,\dots,m
. Instead of selecting a value of
j
, we aggregate the test statistics corresponding to
\mathcal{C}
into the following final max-type test statistic,

T_{n}=\max_{j=1,\dots,m}\bigg(\frac{\bar{n}_{x}\bar{n}_{y}}{\bar{n}_{x}+\bar{n}_{y}}\bigg)^{1/2}|e_{j}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\top}}\hat{\Sigma}^{-1/2}\hat{S}_{n}|,

(10)

where
n=n_{x}+n_{y}
is the total sample size,
e_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
has 1 in the
j
th coordinate and 0 otherwise, and
\hat{\Sigma}
is the sample estimate of

\Sigma=\mathrm{var}\{(\bar{n}_{x}\bar{n}_{y}/(\bar{n}_{x}+\bar{n}_{y}))^{1/2}\hat{S}_{n}\mid\mathcal{D}_{fit}\}.

(11)

Given a significance level
\alpha\in(0,1)
, we reject the null hypothesis if
T_{n}>q_{1-\alpha}
, where
q_{1-\alpha}
is the
1-\alpha
quantile of
\max_{j=1,\dots,m}|Z_{j}|
with
Z\sim N(0,I_{m})
, as demonstrated in Theorem
1
.

Remark 3
.

Although Equation (
3
) may suggest a one-sided test, we find it beneficial to employ a two-sided test when aggregating multiple test statistics obtained from several candidate parameters. One reason is that the standardization by
\hat{\Sigma}^{-1/2}
rotates the vector
\hat{S}_{n}
, which may alter the signs of the entries of
\hat{S}_{n}
. Adopting a one-sided test in this case leads to potential loss of power and also adds difficulties to our theoretical analysis.

Remark 4
.

To ensure
\hat{\Sigma}^{-1}
is numerically stable, we require the smallest eigenvalue of
\hat{\Sigma}
to be no smaller than some constant
\kappa>0
.
This can be achieved by selecting a suitable set
\mathcal{C}
of candidate hyperparameters. For example, in practice, if we find that
\hat{\Sigma}
is near singular, then we can adopt the following backward elimination procedure to reduce the size of
\mathcal{C}
. Let
\hat{\Sigma}_{-j}
be the sample covariance after excluding
(k_{j},\rho_{j})
. Then we find
j^{\prime}=\arg\max_{j}\Lambda_{\min}(\hat{\Sigma}_{-j})
and then remove
(k_{j^{\prime}},\rho_{j^{\prime}})
from
\mathcal{C}
. Continue this process recursively until
\hat{\Sigma}
is well conditioned. Our numeric experiments show that this procedure practically works well.

Remark 5
.

In practice, a small set
\mathcal{C}
of candidate hyperparameters and small projection dimensions
k
are often sufficient, i.e., with a bounded
m
and a bounded
k_{\circ}=\max\{k:(k,\varrho)\in\mathcal{C}\}
. Note that
a larger value of
k
does not necessarily lead to higher power. This is because the estimation variability of
\hat{f}
increases with
k
, which leads to greater variability in the test statistic and negatively impacts the power.

Suppose that Assumptions

Remark 6
.

The sub-Gaussian assumption on

In addition, the power analysis can be extended to accommodate the Lipschitz-constrained neural network via modifying the proofs; we do not pursue this direction in order to maintain a focused presentation and to numerically utilize existing computational packages designed for ordinary ReLU networks.

From Theorem

Remark 10
.

If the corresponding optimal projection direction satisfies
\|U_{0}\|_{1}\leq\tau_{\star}
, then
PW_{k_{\star}}(\mu,\nu)=PW_{k_{\star},\tau_{\star}}^{(1)}(\mu,\nu)
.
Note that the proposed test does not require the true projection

to be sparse. Rather, the key requirement is the existence of a suitable function class (e.g.,

) that is sufficiently rich to distinguish the null hypothesis from the alternative.

As indicated by (

In addition, Theorem

suggests that power can be further improved by incorporating multiple projection dimensions

still needs to be specified), but also potentially increases the power via aggregating the strength from multiple projection dimensions and regularization parameters.
In contrast to existing methods that require both candidate set selection and parameter tuning, our approach requires only the former, substantially reducing methodological and computational complexity.

Remark 11
.

The proposed test is consistent as long as there exists a parameter pair
(k_{\star},\tau_{\star})
in the candidate set that satisfies the signal condition, highlighting its adaptive nature. This feature distinguishes our method from approaches that rely on pre-specified parameters or data-driven tuning. Finally, numerical results in the Supplementary Material demonstrate that the proposed max-type test statistic achieves a significant power gain over tuning-based alternatives.

Remark 12
.

Our testing strategy differs significantly from the permutation test in

The use of deep neural networks to compute the maximum has the potential to amplify the signal. To see this, from the proof of Theorem
3
, with probability tending to 1,

\max_{j=1,\dots,m}|S_{n}(\hat{g}_{j}^{(1)})|\geq\sup_{U\in\mathcal{S}_{d,k,\tau}^{(1)}}W(U_{\#}\mu,U_{\#}\nu)-cR_{n}^{(1)}(k,\tau,d),

for any
(k,\tau)\in\mathcal{C}
and some constant
c>0
. Using deep neural networks enables better approximation of the maximizer in (
7
), resulting in a larger
\max_{j=1,\dots,m}|S_{n}(\hat{g}_{j}^{(1)})|
and thus achieving higher power.
Second, we establish the limiting null distribution of the proposed test statistic and adopt its quantile to determine the test threshold. Third, the proposed approach is adaptive to potentially sparse structures in projection directions within high-dimensional contexts by incorporating a flexible sparsity parameter
\tau
. Fourth, our approach bypasses the need to select the projection dimension
k
and the sparsity parameter
\tau
via a max-type test statistic. Notably, the max-type test statistic yields superior power performance compared to the method depending on parameter tuning or a single projection dimension, as discussed in the Supplementary Material.

3.3
Computational algorithm

The optimization problem (
13
) involves projection onto the intersection of the Stiefel manifold
\mathcal{S}_{d,k}
and the
\ell_{1}
ball, which is computationally nontrivial. To address this challenge, we instead consider the following penalized optimization problem,

\displaystyle\max\limits_{U\in\mathcal{S}_{d,k}}\min\limits_{\pi\in\Gamma(\mu_{n},\nu_{n})}\sum_{i\in\tilde{\mathcal{I}}_{X}}\sum_{j\in\tilde{\mathcal{I}}_{Y}}\pi_{ij}\|U^{{\scriptscriptstyle\top}}(X_{i}-Y_{j})\|_{2}-\rho\|U\|_{1},

(17)

where
\rho\geq 0
.
A key observation is that the estimator
\hat{U}_{\rho}
obtained from (
17
) serves as the optimal solution to the constrained problem (
13
) with
\tau=\|\hat{U}_{\rho}\|_{1}
. This property allows the selection of the constraint parameter
\tau
to be transformed into the choice of an appropriate penalty parameter
\rho
in (
17
).
Therefore, in practice, for a given collection of the candidate parameter pairs
(k_{j},\rho_{j}),j=1,\dots,m
, we compute the corresponding estimates
\hat{g}_{j}^{(1)}
and evaluate the final test statistic in an analogous manner.

In the above, the Stiefel manifold
\mathcal{S}_{d,k}
constraint and the nonsmooth penalty function add to the complexity of the problem (
17
). Existing algorithms
(
37
;
27
)
are primarily designed for computing the entropic regularized projection Wasserstein distance, and the nonsmooth penalty renders these algorithms less applicable for our task. In Appendix
A
, we provide an alternating optimization algorithm for (
17
), which leads to satisfactory numeric performances.

Power enhancement with
\ell_{0}
regularization

Let the constraint set for the projection in (
6
) be
\mathcal{S}_{d,k,\varpi}^{(0)}=\{U\in\mathcal{S}_{d,k}:\|U\|_{0}\leq\varpi\}
where
k\leq\varpi\leq kd
.

4.1
Estimation and the test statistic

Under
\ell_{0}
regularization, the optimization problem in (
6
) becomes

\displaystyle\hat{U}_{k,\varpi}^{(0)}\in\mathop{\arg\max}\limits_{U\in\mathcal{S}_{d,k,\varpi}^{(0)}}\bigg\{\min\limits_{\pi\in\Gamma(\mu_{n},\nu_{n})}\sum_{i\in\tilde{\mathcal{I}}_{X}}\sum_{j\in\tilde{\mathcal{I}}_{Y}}\pi_{ij}\|U^{{\scriptscriptstyle\top}}(X_{i}-Y_{j})\|_{2}\bigg\}.

(18)

Given
\hat{U}_{k,\varpi}^{(0)}
, we then use the form in (
7
) from Step 2 to obtain the estimator
\hat{f}_{k,\varpi}^{(0)}
, with the network parameters suggested in Thereom
4
.

Given a candidate set of hyperparameters
\mathcal{C}^{(0)}=\{(k_{j},\varpi_{j}),j=1,\dots,m\}
, let
\hat{S}_{n}^{(0)}=(S_{n}(\hat{g}_{1}^{(0)}),\dots,S_{n}(\hat{g}_{m}^{(0)}))^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}
, where
\hat{g}_{j}^{(0)}
is the corresponding estimator using the hyperparameter pair
(k_{j},\varpi_{j})
for
j=1,\dots,m
.
The corresponding max-type test statistic is given by

T_{{n}}^{(0)}=\max_{j=1,\dots,m}\bigg(\frac{\bar{n}_{x}\bar{n}_{y}}{\bar{n}_{x}+\bar{n}_{y}}\bigg)^{1/2}|e_{j}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\top}}(\hat{\Sigma}^{(0)})^{-1/2}\hat{S}_{{n}}^{(0)}|,

(19)

where
\hat{\Sigma}^{(0)}
is the sample estimate of

\Sigma^{(0)}=\mathrm{var}\{(\bar{n}_{x}\bar{n}_{y}/(\bar{n}_{x}+\bar{n}_{y}))^{1/2}\hat{S}_{{n}}^{(0)}\mid\mathcal{D}_{fit}\}.

(20)

Given a significance level
\alpha\in(0,1)
, we reject the null hypothesis if
T_{{n}}^{(0)}>q_{1-\alpha}
, where
q_{1-\alpha}
is the
1-\alpha
quantile of
\max_{j=1,\dots,m}|Z_{j}|
with
Z\sim N(0,I_{m})
.

Corollary 2
.

Suppose that Assumption
1
holds and
\Sigma^{(0)}
satisfies Assumption
2
. Under the null hypothesis, we have
T_{n}^{(0)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}\max_{j=1,\dots,m}|Z_{j}|
, as
n\to\infty
, where
Z\sim N(0,I_{m})
. Moreover, with the network structures specified in Theorem
3
, we have

\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\bigg|\mathbb{P}(T_{{n}}^{(0)}\leq t)-\mathbb{P}\bigg(\max_{j=1,\dots,m}|Z_{j}|\leq t\bigg)\bigg|\leq c\frac{k_{\circ}^{3/2}\eta^{3}(\varpi_{\circ}\log(dn))^{3/2}+k_{\circ}\eta^{2}\varpi_{\circ}\log(dn)\log(n)}{n^{1/2}}

for some positive constant
c>0
, where
k_{\circ}=\max\{k:(k,\varpi)\in\mathcal{C}^{(0)}\}
and
\varpi_{\circ}=\max\{\varpi:(k,\varpi)\in\mathcal{C}^{(0)}\}
.

The weak convergence of the proposed test statistic under
H_{0}
guarantees asymptotic control of the type I error at level
\alpha
.
Additionally, Corollary
2
establishes the Berry–Essen type bound for the Gaussian approximation of the test statistic under
\ell_{0}
regularization, with the convergence rate of order
n^{-1/2}\log(dn)\{(\log(dn))^{1/2}+\log(n)\}
if
\varpi_{\circ}\lesssim 1
.

4.2
Power analysis

We study the power performance under
\ell_{0}
regularization.
Define

R_{n}^{(0)}(k,\varpi,d)=(\varpi k)^{\frac{1}{2}}(\log(dn))^{\frac{3}{2}}\big\{n^{-\frac{1}{k+2}}+n^{-\frac{1}{2}}(k\varpi)^{\frac{1}{2}}+n^{-\frac{2}{k+2}}\log(n)+n^{-1}\log(n)k\varpi\big\}.

Theorem 4
.

Suppose Assumptions
1
and
3
hold, and
\Sigma^{(0)}
satisfies Assumption
2
. For any
(k,\varpi)\in\mathcal{C}
, take the corresponding ReLU neural network to be

\mathcal{N}(D,(k,2k,6(k+1)N,\dots,6(k+1)N,1),s,4(k\varpi)^{1/2}\theta_{n})
,
where
D\asymp\log n
,
s\asymp ND
,
N\geq 2^{k}\vee(4(k\varpi)^{1/2}\theta_{n}+1)e^{k}
and

N\asymp n^{\frac{k}{2+k}}(\log n)^{-\frac{3k}{2+k}}.

If there exists a
(k_{\star},\varpi_{\star})\in\mathcal{C}^{(0)}
such that

PW_{k_{\star},\varpi_{\star}}^{(0)}(\mu,\nu)=\sup_{U\in\mathcal{S}_{d,k_{\star},\varpi_{\star}}^{(0)}}W(U_{\#}\mu,U_{\#}\nu)\geq c_{a}R_{n}^{(0)}(k_{\star},\varpi_{\star},d)

(21)

for some sufficiently large constant
c_{a}>0
, then we have
\mathbb{P}(T_{n}^{(0)}>q_{1-\alpha})\to 1
as
n\to\infty
.

Compared to the signal conditions in Theorem
3
, the condition in (
21
) is considerably weaker provided that
\varpi_{\star}\lesssim 1
, reflecting the reduced complexity induced by the
\ell_{0}
sparsity structure.
Moreover, Theorem
4
suggests that incorporating multiple projection dimensions and sparsity parameters can improve power.

Remark 13
.

If the corresponding optimal projection direction satisfies
\|U_{0}\|_{0}\leq\varpi_{\star}
, then
PW_{k_{\star}}(\mu,\nu)=PW_{k_{\star},\varpi_{\star}}^{(0)}(\mu,\nu)
.
Again, the proposed test does not require the true projection
U_{0}
to be sparse. Rather, the essential requirement is the existence of a suitably rich function class capable of distinguishing the null hypothesis from the alternative.

4.3
Computational algorithm

Solving the optimization problem (
18
) is computationally challenging due to the nonconvex nature of the
\ell_{0}
regularization. To address this difficulty, we adopt a sequential
\ell_{1}
approximation strategy to find the solution within
\mathcal{S}_{d,k,\varpi}^{(0)}
. Specifically, we consider a sequence of
\ell_{1}
sparisity parameters
\rho_{j},j=1,\dots,L
, and for each
\rho_{j}
solve the following optimization problem

\displaystyle\hat{U}_{\rho_{j}}\in\mathop{\arg\max}\limits_{U\in\mathcal{S}_{d,k}}\bigg\{\min\limits_{\pi\in\Gamma(\mu_{n},\nu_{n})}\sum_{i\in\tilde{\mathcal{I}}_{X}}\sum_{j\in\tilde{\mathcal{I}}_{Y}}\pi_{ij}\|U^{{\scriptscriptstyle\top}}(X_{i}-Y_{j})\|_{2}-\rho_{j}\|U\|_{1}\bigg\}.

(22)

For each resulting candidate, we retain the
\varpi
largest entries in absolute value and then apply a QR decomposition to obtain an orthonormal projection. Since the QR step may densify the projection, we repeat this pruning procedure several times. Among these candidates, we select the projection that attains the largest empirical projection Wasserstein distance while satisfying the constraint, and take it as the final estimator
\hat{U}_{k,\varpi}^{(0)}
. Numerical results demonstrate the satisfactory performance of the proposed algorithm.

7
Conclusion

In this paper, we employ deep neural networks and the projection Wasserstein distance to develop a powerful test for high-dimensional two-sample testing problems. By aggregating strength from multiple values of hyperparameters via a max-type test statistic, the proposed method not only avoids tuning crucial hyperparameters but also potentially increases the power. The test statistic is shown to asymptotically follow the distribution of the absolute maximum of a standard normal random vector, regardless of the estimation algorithms and the dimensionality. Such a pivotal statistic yields substantial computational efficiency when compared to data calibration methods such as permutation and bootstrapping. Despite the reduction in sample sizes due to data splitting, the proposed approach still attains excellent numerical performance, attributed to the utilization of multiple informative directions and advanced algorithms for learning discriminative functions via training deep neural networks.

While the sample splitting technique is handy for valid and effective inference in high-dimensional models
(
53
;
7
)
, it is unclear whether such a loss of sample size is unavoidable.
Investigating this question is beyond the scope of this paper and left for future investigation.

Appendix A
Optimization algorithm

In this section, we provide detailed procedures for solving (
17
).
Let
F(U;\pi)=l(U;\pi)+h(U)
with
l(U;\pi)=-\sum_{i\in\tilde{\mathcal{I}}_{X}}\sum_{j\in\tilde{\mathcal{I}}_{Y}}\pi_{ij}\|U^{{\scriptscriptstyle\top}}(X_{i}-Y_{j})\|_{2}
and
h(U)=\rho\|U\|_{1}
.
The optimal transport (OT) subproblem about
\pi
is

\max_{\pi\in\Gamma(\mu_{\tilde{n}_{x}},\nu_{\tilde{n}_{y}})}l(U;\pi),

(A.1)

which is solved by the linear programming, and the subproblem about
U
is solved by the proximal gradient method for nonsmooth optimization over the Stiefel manifold (ManPG)
(
9
)
.
Specifically, the ManPG first computes a descent direction
V
by solving the following problem

\begin{split}\min_{V}&<\nabla l(U_{t};\pi_{t+1}),V>+\frac{1}{2\gamma}\|V\|_{F}^{2}+h(U_{t}+V)\\
s.t.~~&V^{{\scriptscriptstyle\top}}U_{t}+U_{t}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\top}}V=0,\end{split}

(A.2)

where
U_{t}
is obtained in the
t
-th iteration,
\nabla l(U_{t};\pi_{t+1})
is the gradient of
l(\cdot;\pi_{t+1})
, and
\gamma>0
is a step size.
Then, a retraction is performed to update
U
on the Stiefel manifold. Algorithm
1
outlines this process.

Retraction operation is an important concept in manifold optimization; see
1
for more details.
There are many common retractions for the Stiefel manifold, including exponential mapping, the polar decomposition and the Cayley transformation.
For example, the exponential mapping
(
14
)
is given by,

\mathrm{Retr}_{U}(rV)=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}U&Q\end{array}\right]\mathrm{exp}\left(r\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}U^{{\scriptscriptstyle\top}}V&-R^{{\scriptscriptstyle\top}}\\
R&0\end{array}\right]\right)\left[\begin{array}[]{c}I_{k}\\
0\end{array}\right],

where
QR=(I_{d}-UU^{{\scriptscriptstyle\top}})V
is the unique QR factorization.

It remains to solve the subproblem (
A.2
).
Based on the Lagrangian function and the KKT system, we have

\mathcal{A}_{t}(V(\Lambda))=0,

(A.3)

and
V(\Lambda)=\mathrm{prox}_{\gamma h}(B(\Lambda))-U_{t}
,

Appendix B
Remarks on
f_{0}

In Section
2
, we introduced the oracle discriminative function
f_{0}
. While this function does not directly enter our test procedure—it is introduced primarily to convey the intuition behind our method—we discuss below how
f_{0}
can be conceptually chosen.

In Section
2.6
, we have showed that, for the purpose of empirical approximation of
PW_{k}(\mu,\nu)
, it suffices to restrict attention to a smaller function class
\mathcal{F}_{n}=\{f\in\mathcal{F}:f(x)=f(\tilde{x}),\text{ where }\tilde{x}=P_{[-d^{1/2}\theta_{n},d^{1/2}\theta_{n}]^{k}}(x)\}
that grows with
n
.
Given the compactness of
\mathcal{F}_{n}
, the supremum

f_{0}\in\underset{f\in\mathcal{F}_{n}}{\arg\max}\left\{\mathbb{E}f(U_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}X)-\mathbb{E}f(U_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}Y)\right\}

(B.4)

admits a solution. While this
f_{0}
technically depends on
n
, it is deterministic (non-random) and serves as the conceptual target that the empirical discriminative function
\hat{f}
approximates.
Note that the function
f_{0}
in the above belongs to the 1-Lipschitz function class
\mathcal{F}
since
\mathcal{F}_{n}\subset\mathcal{F}
.

We also note that the function
f_{0}
may not be unique. However, this does not affect the validity of our test or the theoretical guarantees. The key objective is to approximate the quantity
PW_{k}(\mu,\nu)
, rather than to recover a specific function
f_{0}
. In this sense, the essential step is the use of a neural network function class
\mathcal{N}
(which grows with
n
) to approximate the class
\mathcal{F}_{n}
(although
PW_{k}(\mu,\nu)
depends on
\mathcal{F}
, but
\mathcal{F}_{n}
suffices here, as discussed in Section
2.6
), and thereby approximate the quantity

\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}_{n}}\left\{\mathbb{E}f(U_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}X)-\mathbb{E}f(U_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}Y)\right\}

by

\sup_{f\in\mathcal{N}}\left\{\mathbb{E}f(U_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}X)-\mathbb{E}f(U_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}Y)\right\}.

Thus, it is ultimately the expressive power of
\mathcal{N}
that matters, rather than the approximation of a specific function
f_{0}
within
\mathcal{F}_{0}
or
\mathcal{F}
. In this regard, Theorem
2
shows that the key statistic
S_{n}(\hat{f},\hat{U})
, constructed using the empirical function
\hat{f}
which is based on the neural network, provides an accurate approximation of
PW_{k}(\mu,\nu)
.
