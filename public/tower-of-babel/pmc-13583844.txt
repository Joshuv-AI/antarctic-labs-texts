Lay health-worker delivered and technology-based interventions for sexual and reproductive health among adolescents and young adults in low- and middle-income countries: a scoping review
Authors: Matthias Kern, Christine Neumann, Benjamin Bosompim, Sujata Sujata, Samuel Mayeden, Fabiola V Moshi, Dorit Ann, Joy Mauti, Adi Lukas Kurniawan, Nolwazi Cynthia Dlamini, Sarah Nabukeera, Sphindile Machanyangwa, Dagmawit Tewahido, Sachin Shinde, Till Bärnighausen, Mary Mwanyika-Sando, Emmanuel Anongeba Anaba, Justine Bukenya, Michael Laxy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAdolescents and young adults (AYAs) in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) are at high risk of harmful sexual and reproductive health (SRH) practices. Preventive and educational interventions by lay health workers or through technological means are a cost-effective and scalable solution. Unfortunately, to date, there is too little synthesised evidence on available lay health-worker delivered or technology-based interventions, their target populations and content, their implementation and effect on different SRH outcomes as well as facilitators and barriers to intervention effectiveness.ObjectivesTo help fill this knowledge gap and to identify where further research is needed, we conducted a scoping review of technology-based or lay health-worker delivered preventive and educational SRH interventions targeting AYAs in LMICs.DesignScoping review.Data sourcesWe searched MEDLINE (through PubMed) and Embase for eligible studies published from 1 January 2000 until 19 January 2026.EligibilityEligible were studies assessing the effect of preventive and educational lay health-worker delivered or technology-based interventions on any SRH outcomes among AYAs (aged 10–24 years) in LMICs published in English in peer-reviewed journals.Data extractionData extraction covers study details, methods, intervention strategies, outcomes and findings.ResultsThe scoping review includes 101 studies based on 95 research projects evaluating 94 unique interventions. Studies were conducted in 40 different countries and most (n=65) were published since 2020. Included interventions range from single-session interventions lasting less than half an hour in total to multiyear whole-of-community interventions. Most (n=54) employ technological means such as websites or social media pages (n=18), smartphone apps (n=6), text-messaging (n=15), videos (n=11) or mass-media campaigns (n=4) for intervention delivery, while the remaining (n=47) typically centre around regular face-to-face educational sessions and are delivered by peers (n=23), adult community members (n=15) or a combination of both (n=9). Included studies investigate intervention effects on a wide range of SRH outcomes, with those pertaining to SRH knowledge or attitudes getting most attention. Most studies report significant improvements for most assessed outcomes. This is particularly true for SRH knowledge, while results are slightly more mixed when it comes to some SRH behavioural or physiological outcomes. No noticeable differences in intervention effectiveness were observed between lay health-worker delivered and technology-based interventions. Identified facilitators of intervention effectiveness include the role of community outreach, as practised by some of the lay health-worker delivered interventions, in overcoming culturally grounded resistance to SRH education of AYAs. Important gaps in the literature include a scarcity of interventions tailored to male or out-of-school AYAs and a lack of attention to the extent to which improvements in SRH knowledge and attitudes translate to improvements in behavioural and physiological outcomes.ConclusionWe identified a diverse variety of lay health-worker delivered and technology-based SRH interventions showing positive effects on many different SRH outcomes among AYAs in LMICs, testifying to the great potential of those approaches. However, more research is needed focusing on so-far understudied groups and outcomes.Trial registration detailsThis scoping review adheres to a protocol previously published in this journal and that is registered on the Open Science Framework website (http://osf.io/vna2z).

Introduction

Due to their rapid physiological, psychological and social development, adolescents and young adults (AYAs) often struggle with making healthy sexual and reproductive health (SRH) choices, frequently engaging in risky sexual behaviours, such as unprotected sex, multiple sexual partners and early onset of sexual activity.1–3 These behaviours increase the risk of sexual violence, unintended pregnancies and sexually transmitted infections (STIs).4 In line with this, early pregnancies and STIs are among the most significant health threats during this life phase.5–9 In many low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), these challenges are even more substantial, due to pronounced gender inequality, cultural practices such as child marriage,2 limited access to and acceptability of modern contraceptives, and an overall lack of sexual and reproductive health literacy. Consequently, compared with high-income countries (HICs), AYAs in LMICs have more than twice the birth rate,10 are much less likely to use SRH services, and experience substantially higher incidence rates of HIV and other STIs.9

Preventive and educational SRH interventions can help tackle these issues. As AYAs already possess the cognitive capacities and social autonomy to critically evaluate and modify their behaviours while, at the same time, still being malleable in their SRH behaviours,11 adolescence and young adulthood constitute an ideal window of opportunity for such interventions.12 Covering topics such as understanding consent, fostering healthy relationships, safe sex practices and STI and pregnancy prevention, preventive and educational SRH interventions equip AYAs with the knowledge, skills and sense of self-efficacy necessary to make informed decisions about sexual health.13–16

A remaining obstacle to implementing these interventions in LMICs, however, lies in the frequent shortage of professional healthcare providers.17 Accordingly, to be scalable, SRH interventions in LMICs need to rely on other routes of delivery. Two such routes that have emerged as promising in LMICs are lay health-worker delivered and technology-based interventions. Interventions delivered by lay health-workers, such as community health workers, frontline health workers, volunteers or peers are widely implemented in LMICs and have been shown to be effective.12 Technology-based interventions, often referred to as mHealth or eHealth interventions, use technological tools such as text messaging, websites, (social) media campaigns, smartphone apps, didactic games and chatbots,18 19 are increasingly feasible in LMICs,20 21 and have, due to their cost-effectiveness, gained considerable popularity in these settings.22

While lay health-worker delivered and technology-based interventions hold promise for improving SRH among AYAs in LMICs, critical knowledge gaps remain regarding their effectiveness and sustainability. For lay health-worker delivered interventions, evidence is lacking on how variations in content, delivery settings and training, and supervision of delivery agents affect intervention success.23 Additionally, many lay health-worker delivered interventions are school-based, often excluding vulnerable groups such as migrants or early school-leavers, leaving limited knowledge on how to effectively reach out-of-school AYAs in community settings. Similarly, most evidence on technology-based interventions comes from HICs,22 making it difficult to assess their feasibility, accessibility and cultural appropriateness in LMICs.18

To address these knowledge gaps, this work aims to comprehensively synthesise the literature on lay health-worker delivered and technology-based preventive and/or educational SRH interventions for AYAs in LMICs. It aims to synthesise information on the targeted populations, intervention content and implementation, study details, investigated outcomes and findings, explore implementation facilitators and barriers, and finally identify research gaps.

Methods

This scoping review follows the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) checklist.24 The review protocol has been published elsewhere,1 with registration on Open Science Framework (http://osf.io/vna2z).

Eligibility

We used the Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome (PICO) model to guide our search strategy. We focused on studies assessing preventive and educational lay health-worker delivered and technology-based interventions, as these seem particularly well-suited for low-resource settings, and to studies conducted in LMICs to ensure that findings are applicable to those settings that are home to a large proportion of AYAs globally25 and where many SRH issues faced by AYAs are particularly pronounced. While this was not prespecified as an exclusion criterion during the study planning phase and in the review protocol,1 we decided not to include teacher-delivered school-based interventions that were integrated within the school’s SRH curriculum. This decision was made to maintain a feasible number of included studies while prioritising the substantial evidence gaps related to interventions delivered by other lay health-workers and approaches reaching out-of-school AYAs. However, we included studies conducted in school-settings if the interventions were technology-based or delivered by other lay health-workers, such as peers.

We did not restrict our search to studies employing a particular design or investigating particular SRH outcomes to obtain a wide-ranging overview of available interventions.

In line with this, we applied the following inclusion and exclusion criteria.

Data sources and search

We searched MEDLINE (through PubMed) and Embase for eligible studies published from 1 January 2000 until 19 January 2026. MEDLINE and Embase are two of the most widely used and comprehensive sources for biomedical and public health literature. Both have a good coverage of international journals in medicine, nursing, public health and related disciplines and complement each other very well leading to a very good recall when used together.26 Additionally, we examined references and bibliographies of identified studies and reviews to ensure that no relevant publications were missed. We only considered published articles written in English and did not conduct a search of grey literature.

First, a broad search strategy was performed in PubMed. We confirmed the sensitivity of the search strategy by identifying various sentinel articles. The PubMed strategy was adapted to suit Embase. The detailed search strategy for both databases is provided in online supplemental file 1. We exported the final search results into Covidence (Veritas Health Innovation, Melbourne, Australia), where duplicates were removed.

Selection of studies

First, for each search result, title and abstract were screened independently by two reviewers drawn from a larger pool of reviewers. The reviewers discussed any differences of opinion, and when necessary, consulted a third reviewer to resolve discrepancies. After exclusion of irrelevant studies, all remaining articles were screened in their entirety in the same manner. A study flow diagram detailing the specific reasons for exclusion was maintained following the PRISMA-ScR guidelines.24

Data extraction

We developed an extraction form and tested it on five randomly selected studies. Subsequently, two reviewers independently extracted data from studies included in the review using the extraction form (see online supplemental file 2).

We extracted information about publication details, study methods and design, the intervention itself including the intervention design process, target population, delivery agents and their training and supervision, setting, format, content, duration and frequency of the intervention, the outcomes investigated and findings including effectiveness findings, and facilitators and barriers to intervention delivery.1

Synthesis of evidence

All included studies are systematically synthesised in the text and in a table. This synthesis describes, in the text and flow diagram, the number of articles screened, assessed for eligibility and included in the review, along with reasons for exclusion at each stage. Studies are grouped based on interventions assessed, outcomes investigated and delivery agents. Barriers and facilitators to delivering the interventions are discussed. Based on a comprehensive analysis of all identified studies, we identify research gaps and areas for future research. Additionally, we discuss the limitations of the review process and provide an interpretation of the results with respect to the objectives of the review, as well as possible implications or next steps. We follow the PRISMA-ScR checklist and guidelines to ensure a robust and replicable process.24

Patient and public involvement

Patients and/or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting, or dissemination plans of this research.

As seen in figure 2, included studies were conducted in 40 different countries, most commonly in Eastern Africa (n=30), followed by Western Africa (n=17) and South-Eastern Asia (n=13). Most studies were conducted in only one country, four studies were conducted in two countries, that is, Ghana and Nigeria,27 Brazil and Kenya,28 South Africa and Tanzania29 and Indonesia and the Democratic Republic of Congo,30 and one study was conducted in three countries, namely Brazil, Nepal and Senegal.31

Target population

Most evaluated interventions targeted both sexes (n=59), while a substantial number of studies focused specifically on females (n=38) and only a very small number targeted males exclusively (n=4). 33 studies evaluated interventions targeting adolescents from early to middle adolescence, followed by 30 studies targeting middle to late adolescence and 14 studies targeting adolescents of all age groups. Only a minority of studies (n=24) targeted only a single age group, with middle adolescence gaining most attention (n=18).

Examining the investigated outcomes in figure 3, we see that, expectedly, the only included interventions seeking to improve menstrual hygiene practices32–34 were conducted exclusively among females. The same goes for the only study assessing intervention effects on abortion.35 Interventions aiming to improve gender equitable relationships tended to be rather gender-specific in their content and thus tended to target either exclusively males or exclusively females. Indeed, all four studies36–39 exclusively targeting males primarily aimed at reducing gender-based violence and generally improving attitudes and behaviour towards women and girls.

The majority of studies (n=57) targeted both in-school and out-of-school adolescents. A sizeable number of studies targeted only in-school adolescents (n=43). Even more surprising than the large number of studies targeting only in-school adolescents is the dearth of studies evaluating interventions exclusively targeting out-of-school adolescents. Only one study40 exclusively targeted this high-risk demographic.

Six studies specifically targeted unmarried adolescents.32 36 39 41–43 Two of them specifically targeted unmarried male adolescents, and two specifically targeted unmarried adolescent girls and young women (AGYW). Five studies only included married adolescents,44–47 two of which were aimed at married couples46 47 and three at married AGYW.44 45 48 Three studies were only conducted among adolescents exerting some kind of risk behaviour.40 49 50 Other studies only targeted AYAs living with parents,51 refugees,52 53 or young mothers.54

Interventions

The Study Description table (see online supplemental file 3) includes a brief description of the main component of each intervention evaluated in an included study. The overwhelming majority of included interventions were only evaluated by one included study. Three papers based on three separate research projects assessed the effects of exposure to the Nigerian55 56 and South African57 versions respectively of the SRH-themed television drama series MTV Shuga. Another three papers, based on two separate research projects, dealt with the Vhutshilo intervention.58–60 The GlobalConsent37 38 intervention, the CyberSenga61 62 intervention inspired by the Senga63 intervention, the CyberRwanda64 65 and SKILLZ66 67 interventions, as well as the text messaging intervention investigated by Rokicki and colleagues68 69 were each evaluated by two separate papers, based on the same trial. Three separate papers by McCarthy and colleagues, based on separate trials in Tajikistan,70 Bolivia35 and Palestine,71 respectively, dealt with three distinct but related text-messaging interventions to increase contraceptive use.

Technology-based interventions

With 54, the majority of the 101 included studies were technology-based. 18 of those studies employ educational websites/social media pages, which participants could browse on their own42 61 62 72–77 or were guided through by teachers.43 78 79 Some of them consisted of predefined curricula that participants had to complete,43 61 62 75 79 80 while on others, participants could choose the content they were interested in.64 65 73–75 77 Many of the websites used videos,42 72 74 77 79 80 cartoons73 77 79 and other illustrations72 73 in addition to text-based content, or provided self-tests42 and quizzes73 78 80 to more effectively convey SRH information. In addition, many websites offered individual anonymous SRH counselling via bulletin boards, contact forms or email.74 75 78

Another 15 studies evaluated text-messaging interventions. While some of those interventions were unidirectional,35 50 68 70 71 others were bidirectional, allowing participants to respond to text messages. In the latter case, this often took the form of quizzes where participants were sent a multiple-choice question, then responded with their answer and finally received feedback on their answer.69 81 Other text-messaging interventions allowed participants to reflect on intervention content collectively via group chats.54 82 In all but one intervention, in which a single message promoting pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) and STI testing was sent twice over a period of 1 year,50 text messages were sent at least weekly, with most of them sending messages either daily (n=5) or at least multiple times per week (n=2). A number of interventions tailored the content of the messages to the recipient based on, for instance, marital status, age and gender.35 70 71 83

11 studies used videos as an intervention. The videos covered topics ranging from broad SRH knowledge56 84 over HIV prevention,85 STIs86 and consequences of premarital sex87 to gender-based violence,37 38 sexting88 and cervical cancer.89 The format of the videos ranged from serial drama shows featuring young actors, like in the previously mentioned MTV Shuga Naija56 and GlobalConsent37 38 interventions, over animated videos88 90 to slide-show educational videos86 and recorded lectures.85 91 The majority of video-based interventions consisted of a single session,85–89 92 with the remaining lasting between 556 and 884 sessions. The majority of videos were approximately 20–30 min long,37 38 56 86 88 89 with the remaining lasting between less than 15 min87 92 and 2 hours.85 Many videos were shown in a face-to-face setting and integrated with group discussions,56 86 question-and-answer sessions89 90 or other intervention components.84 87 Among the virtually administered video-based interventions, some were integrated with interactive components37 38 91 or offered the opportunity to discuss the video content with experts via instant messaging.88

Four studies evaluated the effects of mass media campaigns on HIV prevention outcomes,31 57 abstinence41 and attitudes towards sexual violence,55 respectively. The campaigns in question were the previously mentioned MTV Shuga Naija and MTV Shuga Down-South 2 TV series, which were aired in Nigeria from 2018 to 202055 and South Africa in 2020,57 respectively, the MTV staying alive campaign, which consisted, among other components, of a documentary, public service announcements, a website and live concerts conducted globally in 2002,31 and the national multimedia campaign Zip-Up! consisting of television and radio spots, billboards, leaflets and rallies initiated in Nigeria in 2004.41

Another six studies evaluated smartphone applications. Winskell and colleagues93 and Raj and colleagues32 evaluated smartphone games in which participants could play different characters as they were confronted with relatable real-life challenges94 or met inspiring characters.32 Macharia and colleagues evaluated the effect of a smartphone application providing on-demand SRH information to adolescents.95 Turah and colleagues51 and Andriani and colleagues96 assessed the effectiveness of smartphone applications conveying information about premarital sex and postponement of marriage, respectively, using frequently asked questions,96 images and animations.51

Non-technology-based interventions

Among the non-technology-based interventions (N=47), a small minority centred around on-demand63 97 or regular52 98 face-to-face individual counselling sessions. This was, for instance, the case for the Senga intervention, where community-dwelling women (adults above the age of 35 and AYAs between 18 and 20) served as points of contact for AYAs with all questions relating to SRH.63

The remaining overwhelming majority of such interventions were built around regular group face-to-face educational sessions. In these sessions, lessons delivered by a wide array of delivery agents were frequently reinforced with the help of more interactive components such as group-discussions,36 44 48 59 99–102 role-play,36 99 100 103–106 sports39 66 67 107 or educational board games.108

Next to those group educational sessions with AYAs, many of these interventions also included additional home visits,46 47 102 109 (ad-hoc) individual sessions,110 111 visits to local clinics,102 112 care-taker sessions,30 53 103 113 husband sessions44–46 and community sessions.30 44 46 99 Separate caretaker sessions often focused on improving caretaker–child SRH-related communication. They aimed to do so by teaching caretakers how to build trusting relationships and communicate empathetically with the AYAs in their care and sensitising caretakers to the struggles AYAs are confronted with.53 103 Husband sessions were often meant to complement interventions among married AGYW, by teaching husbands about gender equitable relationships and sensitising them towards gender-based violence.44–46 Community sessions typically took the form of community dialogue sessions,44 46 99 sometimes involving participants’ testimonials30 or films.99 They were typically employed to foster community acceptance44 and a sense of ownership of the intervention which in turn was often credited with increasing effectiveness of the intervention.63

Two interventions include financial support to participants as an additional intervention component.99 111 Many lay health-worker delivered interventions also included take-home educational materials, such as leaflets,114 115 comic strips,116 journals117 or booklets,48 113 or gift items such as t-shirts,115 calendars,116 or certificates.118

Delivery agent

Delivery agents could be broadly classified into three categories: Peers, so AYAs of a similar age to the intervention participants, adult community members, and a combination of both.

Most papers evaluated interventions delivered by peers (n=23). Peer educators were typically slightly older than the study participants and were recruited from study participants’ schools,104 113 116 119 universities97 109 or communities27 58 66 67 101 120 for instance, by teachers104 or on-site supervisors from partner organisations.58 Selection criteria included communication skills,97 104 acceptability to the target audience,97 121 SRH knowledge97 109 113 and social activity109 and skills,119 which, in some cases, were assessed via interviews97 or tests.104 109 113 Most studies described providing training to peer educators, but only a few went into detail about duration, setting and content of the training. Those that did, described training lasting between 4121 and 20 days104 and covering both SRH content as well as didactic lessons. Similarly, only a few studies described in detail how and by whom peer educators were guided, monitored, and supervised.

15 included interventions were delivered by adult community members. These included individuals recruited from the local communities, variously referred to as mentors,45 46 53 112 facilitators,59 108 community volunteers,44 118 community health workers99 or social mobilisers.102 Only a few papers described recruitment, training and supervision of adult community member delivery agents in detail. Daniel and colleagues described recruiting them through job advertisements in local newspapers, at educational institutions and elsewhere.47 Most other studies seem to have taken a more informal approach to recruitment of adult delivery agents. Some studies described recruiting them among leaders of local clubs48 103 or via suggestions from intervention participants.63 A small number of papers indicated selection criteria for adult delivery agents such as permanent residence in the intervention area,63 being within a certain age range,44 literacy in the local language and active engagement in the community,118 support for desired SRH behaviours44 118 and being married.44 Most described delivery agents receiving some kind of training, typically as part of a face-to-face workshop lasting anywhere between half a day99 to half a week103 and one and a half115 to 2 weeks,98 118 with most lasting around 1 week.44 63 102 Only a few papers described guidance and supervision of adult community member delivery agents, sometimes indicating that there was supervision by research staff99 or that delivery agents were equipped with manuals for guidance.59

Nine interventions were delivered by a combination of peers and adult community members. In those studies, adult community members often provided guidance, supervision and monitoring for peer educators103 106 115 and were often tasked with additional functions such as stakeholder analysis103 or community sensitisation.111 In some studies, adult community members and peers delivered different components of the intervention.111 In others, there were multiple experimental groups to assess differences in intervention effectiveness based on the delivery agents.33

Intervention duration and frequency

As seen in figure 4, interventions lasted between less than 1 day to more than 6 years with the largest number of interventions (n=39) lasting between 1 month and half a year. The longest-lasting interventions were typically whole-of-community interventions, often aiming to improve attitudes, for instance, towards early marriage,47 99 or quasi-experimental evaluations of existing public health campaigns.55 117 All but one52 of the interventions consisting of a single session, prominently featured videos,42 72 85–89 121 either as a standalone intervention or integrated into a web-based intervention42 72 or a peer education session.121 Interventions delivered daily were typically on the shorter side, with durations between less than a week and less than half a year. The only exception being a text-messaging intervention aiming to improve PrEP adherence.49 Only a few interventions were delivered on a monthly or less than monthly basis (n=5). Most commonly, interventions were delivered on a weekly basis (n=21). Intervention setting

Among the non-technology-based interventions (n=47), the largest number (n=27) was delivered, either fully (n=18) or partly (n=9), in a community setting. Eight of those were delivered in community centres, and the remaining 19 in various other community settings, such as in traditional huts,53 in one of the participants’ houses or under a tree45 or on the street.47 A number of those interventions also included home visits (n=6), for instance, for interventions targeting married AGYW to reach their husbands,44 46 or intervention components that are delivered in school (n=2). 18 studies evaluated interventions completely (n=14) or partly (n=4) delivered in an educational setting such as a school (n=17) or a university (n=1). Those studies almost exclusively evaluated peer-based interventions delivered by students of the respective institutions to their fellow students. Other settings included the homes of the adult delivery agents,100 church,103 and a juvenile detention centre.121

Evaluation design

The Study Description table (see online supplemental file 3) details the evaluation design employed by included studies. Of the 101 included studies a majority of 74 studies used preintervention and postintervention measurements with an intervention and a control group. Group allocation was either non-randomised (n=29), cluster-randomised (n=22) or individually randomised (n=23). 15 studies assessed intervention effectiveness based on a single-group pre–post comparison. 12 studies assessed intervention effects based on post-intervention assessment alone, either by comparing values among exposed and non-exposed study participants (n=11) or via qualitative post-intervention in-depth interviews among participants (n=1).

Intervention outcomes and effects

Figure 5 details the investigated outcomes and the corresponding findings by papers. To provide an overview, we classified outcomes into 16 categories. The most assessed outcomes were those pertaining to SRH knowledge (n=64), followed by SRH attitudes (n=49) and condom use (n=26). The least assessed outcomes were abortion (n=1), followed by STIs (n=2) and menstrual hygiene practices (n=3). Most studies reported significant improvements for most of the investigated outcomes. All studies assessing gender equitable relationships, SRH skills, SRH-related communication or menstrual hygiene practices, and 94% of studies assessing SRH knowledge reported significant improvements in all investigated outcomes falling into these categories. On the flip side, no study reported significant improvements for STIs or abortions. Negative intervention effects were primarily reported for SRH attitudes, and even there, only by a small fraction of studies.28 74 99 Among the more commonly assessed groups of outcomes, non-significant findings were most common for abstinence (5/13) and sexual risk behaviour (5/15). Interestingly, a number of interventions which showed positive effects on SRH knowledge and/or attitudes did not show any effects on SRH behaviours such as sexual activity,74 SRH service utilisation82 or sexual risk behaviour75 90 or physiological outcomes such as pregnancy65 69 or STIs.63 There are no noticeable differences in the effectiveness of interventions based on target population, delivery agent, duration, setting or any of the other examined factors.

Included studies mentioned relevant barriers to intervention implementation and effectiveness. The most frequently mentioned among them was culturally grounded resistance to SRH education,29 30 35 39 41 44 46 47 49 54 55 61 83 84 88 95 105 107 108 110 111 123 124 particularly for AGYW, coming from the community at large, parents or spouses.29 46 For technology-based interventions technical issues, particularly connectivity in more rural areas71 81 125 sometimes constituted another obstacle. Regarding facilitators, a number of studies assessing both technology-based54 55 78 94 126 and lay health-worker delivered interventions29 97 attributed the effectiveness of their intervention in part to the inclusion of interactive components which were hypothesised to facilitate learning processes. Among studies assessing lay health-worker delivered interventions many also credited the success of their intervention in part to various community outreach efforts creating community support for the intervention.33 36 45 63 97 99

Discussion

This review synthesises information from 101 peer-reviewed publications using data from 95 different research projects evaluating 94 different lay health-worker delivered or technology-based interventions aiming to improve SRH among AYAs in LMICs. Most papers were published in the year 2020 or later, indicating increased interest in the topic.

The interventions evaluated by papers included in this review were conducted in 40 different countries, with most of them (n=64) being implemented in African countries, primarily in Eastern and Western Africa. The large number of studies conducted in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) is a promising finding. The region’s exacerbated struggles with issues such as teen pregnancy and STIs,127 along with its pronounced shortage of professional healthcare providers,17 renders lay health-worker delivered and technology-based SRH interventions particularly vital for these countries. The many studies identified in this review that were conducted there provide a solid evidence base for future intervention efforts in the region. However, the relative scarcity of studies conducted in LMICs, for instance, in Central (n=1) and Western Asia (n=4) and Central(n=2) and South America (n=4) hints towards a gap in the literature. Given the importance of cultural appropriateness of intervention content and implementation, many lessons learnt from studies conducted in the SSA context might not be immediately applicable to these regions.

Most interventions evaluated by included studies targeted a relatively broad age range, with most studies including AYAs during middle adolescence (n=95) and a large number additionally including AYAs during early adolescence (n=33) or late adolescence (n=30) or both (n=14). As SRH challenges are highly age-specific, and AYAs’ SRH educational and didactic needs evolve substantially from early adolescence to early adulthood, the content of interventions targeting such a broad age range might not be relevant or helpful for all participants, and the fact that almost none of those studies reported age-specific intervention effects carries the risk of obscuring this. More studies evaluating interventions specifically tailored to AYAs of a narrower age bracket would constitute a valuable addition to the literature.

Most interventions targeted both female and male AYAs. What is striking is that, while a sizeable minority of studies (n=38) covering a broad range of different SRH outcomes targeted female AYAs exclusively, only four papers, two of which were based on the same trial, exclusively targeted male AYAs and all of these interventions primarily aimed at reducing gender-based violence and generally improving attitudes and behaviour towards women and girls.36–39 Since adolescent boys and young men might face SRH challenges and social pressures that are different from those experienced by AGYW, and might have different didactic and educational needs, further studies evaluating interventions specifically targeting this demographic and addressing various SRH outcomes are warranted.

Similarly, while most studies targeted both in-school and out-of-school AYAs (n=57), and a sizeable minority (n=43) exclusively targeted in-school AYAs, only one intervention exclusively targeted out-of-school AYAs.40 Given that out-of-school AYAs’ often socially and economically compromised living circumstances make them vulnerable to sexual exploitation and unhealthy SRH practices40 and that these AYAs might have didactic needs that are considerably different from those of their in-school counterparts, a lot might be gained by developing more interventions specifically targeting this demographic. Our review reveals multiple alleys through which out-of-school AYAs can be identified, reached and recruited, including via household registers,114 116 123 126 community stakeholders,72 112 118 social media,71 73 128 existing youth groups,36 39 73 103 door-to-door/street outreach approaches40 45 48 95 107 110 or existing support infrastructure (non-governmental organisations, community health facilities, etc).29 49 59 71 80 83 100 111

Testifying to their increasing feasibility in LMICs, the majority of included studies (n=54) evaluated technology-based interventions. As demonstrated and emphasised by many of the included studies, these approaches possess certain advantages, which might facilitate intervention effectiveness. Approaches like SRH educational websites, text-messaging interventions and smartphone applications often allow for more privacy when engaging with potentially sensitive SRH educational content.72 74 95 Furthermore, they are often easy to use95 and can be more flexible, by allowing participants, for instance, on an educational website,64 65 73–75 77 90 to focus on the content that is most relevant to them or by tailoring content to different target audiences, as seen in some of the text-messaging interventions.35 70 71 83 A potential drawback to these approaches mentioned by some of the studies lies in technical issues, particularly connectivity in more rural areas.71 81 125

Lay health-worker delivered interventions (n=47) consisted primarily of regular group face-to-face educational sessions. They were delivered either by peers (n=23), adult community members (n=15) or a combination of both (n=9). While peer delivery agents were frequently recruited from their respective educational institutions,97 104 109 116 recruitment of adult delivery agents was often less straightforward and typically not described in much detail. Even more regrettably, only a few papers thoroughly explained how delivery agents were trained and supervised. Many studies indicated that delivery agents did receive training, sometimes indicating the duration of the training but they rarely elaborated on the content. Similarly, while they sometimes indicated that there was supervision and by whom, they seldom explained exactly what this looked like. Since training and supervision are crucial for intervention fidelity and to inform implementation, the lack of comprehensive documentation of these aspects of the respective interventions in much of the literature constitutes a major shortcoming.

Another crucial omission in much of the literature is that many studies neglected to explicate the theory of change underlying the evaluated interventions. This lack of an (explicit) theoretical underpinning makes it difficult to draw inferences about underlying mechanisms, which renders the obtained knowledge less transferable, making it more difficult to develop new or refine existing interventions based on accumulated knowledge.

Included studies investigated numerous outcomes, most commonly those pertaining to SRH knowledge and SRH attitudes. Most studies reported significant improvements for most of the reported outcomes. This could be indicative of publication bias, and while beyond the aims of this scoping review, future research conducting a formal risk of bias assessment would be highly valuable.

There were, however, some interesting exceptions. For instance, none of the few studies investigating outcomes pertaining to STIs or abortion found significant improvements. Equally interesting, many of the interventions that did lead to improvements in SRH knowledge or attitudes did not show any effects on more downstream SRH behavioural (ie, sexual activity, sexual risk behaviour) or physiological (ie, pregnancy, STIs) outcomes. This might be indicative of a genuine lack of effectiveness of the interventions in question for such outcomes or largely an artefact of the chosen study designs. It is reasonable to assume that effects on behavioural and physiological outcomes might be weaker and/or take longer to materialise. Since these outcomes, in many of those studies, were only considered secondary outcomes, those studies might have been insufficiently powered and conducted on too short a time frame to detect possible effects on those outcomes. In line with this, many papers attributed non-significant effects on behavioural and physiological outcomes to the last follow-up being too close to the baseline assessment for potential improvements in those outcomes to surface65 69 74 75 or a lack of statistical power to detect the presumably relatively small changes.69 75 82 Since improving SRH behaviours and SRH-related physiological health should be a number one priority, more research is needed employing study designs that are capable not just of detecting the effects of preventive and educational SRH interventions on adolescents’ SRH knowledge and attitudes, but also of investigating the extent to which improvements in those outcomes translate to improvements in those more downstream outcomes.

There were no noticeable differences in the effectiveness of interventions based on target population, delivery agent, duration, setting or any of the other examined factors. In particular, we did not find relevant differences in intervention effectiveness between lay health-worker delivered and technology-based interventions.

Included studies mentioned both facilitators and barriers to intervention effectiveness. Despite included interventions often going to great lengths to culturally tailor intervention content,29 33 57 62 71 76 88 102 108 118 125 one very commonly mentioned barrier to intervention implementation and effectiveness was culturally grounded resistance to SRH education,29 30 35 39 41 44 46 47 49 54 55 61 83 84 88 95 105 107 108 110 111 123 124 particularly for AGYW, coming from the community at large, parents or spouses.29 46 One possible remedy for this resistance, credited by many included studies with increasing intervention effectiveness,33 36 45 63 97 99 was community and stakeholder engagement and participation. To achieve this, a number of lay health-worker delivered interventions incorporated community sessions typically taking the form of community dialogue sessions44 46 99 and sometimes involving participants’ testimonials30 or films99 as a way to foster community acceptance44 and a sense of community ownership of the intervention. Other interventions sought to increase support for the intervention from particular stakeholders, for instance, via separate sessions for caretakers30 53 103 or spouses.44–46

Another facilitator frequently mentioned by both studies evaluating technology-based interventions54 55 78 94 126 and those evaluating lay health-worker delivered interventions29 97 was the integration of interactive components into intervention content. For technology-based interventions these often took the form of bulletin boards,74 77 self-tests42 or quizzes73 77 78 80 on SRH educational websites, or of group chats,54 91 text messages with multiple-choice questions69 81 or smartphone games.32 94 129 For lay health-worker delivered interventions, they often took the form of group-discussions,36 44 48 59 99–102 106 119 role-play,36 99 100 103–106 130 sports,39 66 67 107 or educational board games.108

To the best of our knowledge, this review represents the first attempt to synthesise evidence on lay health-worker delivered and technology-based preventive and educational SRH interventions among AYAs in LMICs. Owing to our comprehensive search strategy we could identify a large number of publications evaluating a wide range of interventions addressing different SRH issues, delivered by various delivery agents in various settings to different target audiences. Nonetheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, only English-language databases and search engines were included, which may have excluded relevant studies published in other languages. Similarly, we did not consider grey literature, which means some relevant studies might have been overlooked. Third, the heterogeneity of interventions and measured outcomes rendered it difficult to compare effectiveness between interventions. Instead, in this scoping review, we opted to collate and map existing evidence and highlight areas where knowledge remains limited.

Conclusion

This scoping review identified a large number of lay health-worker delivered or technology-based preventive or educational SRH interventions targeting AYAs in LMICs. Results show the great potential these interventions have for improving SRH among this high-risk population, provide details on their implementation, highlight facilitators and barriers to intervention effectiveness and uncover gaps in the literature. Relevant take-away messages include the large diversity of available interventions, an increasing trend towards technology-based interventions in LMICs, the importance of incorporating interactive elements into intervention content and the existence of substantial culturally grounded resistance to SRH education in many settings and the role of community outreach and stakeholder involvement in mitigating this.

There are also a number of gaps in the literature we could identify. Only a few studies provided sufficient detail on the recruitment, training and supervision of delivery agents. More information on these aspects of the respective interventions would be crucial to guide further intervention efforts. Similarly, only a few studies elaborate on the theoretical underpinnings of the evaluated intervention, limiting the extent to which their findings can be informative for future research.

Moreover, we found that a large share of included studies target AYAs of a relatively large age range. To do justice to AYAs age-specific SRH challenges and educational needs, more studies evaluating interventions focusing on narrower age brackets would be needed. In a similar vein, we could identify a dearth of studies evaluating interventions specifically targeting adolescent boys and young men. Given that the SRH challenges they are confronted with might differ from those faced by AGYW, more such studies would be needed.

The same can be said for studies evaluating interventions specifically targeting out-of-school AYAs. Only a single study in our review specifically targets this high-risk demographic, leaving numerous questions unanswered about how to most effectively reach this population, and how to most effectively tailor SRH educational content to their SRH educational and didactic needs. Finally, not enough is known about the extent to which improvements in SRH knowledge or attitudes translate to improvements in SRH behaviours and SRH related physiological outcomes. More studies are needed that not only assess these outcomes, but that are also designed in a way that is suitable to detect potentially weaker and time-delayed intervention effects on these outcomes.
