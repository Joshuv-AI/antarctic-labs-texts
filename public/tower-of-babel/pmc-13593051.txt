Effect of esketamine on postoperative pulmonary complications after radical surgery for lung cancer
Authors: Jiangyuan Huang, Jingjing Wang, Xiaoxia Gu, Ping Pang, Liangqing Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Thoracoscopic radical resection for lung cancer is widely performed, but postoperative pulmonary complications (PPCs) remain one of the most common and severe perioperative adverse events, which prolong hospital stay and increase postoperative morbidity. Esketamine exerts anti-inflammatory and lung-protective properties, while its effect on reducing PPCs after thoracoscopic lung cancer surgery still requires further clinical verification. This study aimed to investigate whether perioperative application of esketamine could reduce the incidence of PPCs in patients undergoing thoracoscopic radical resection for lung cancer.Methods:A total of 80 adult patients scheduled for thoracoscopic radical lung cancer surgery were enrolled. The primary outcome was the overall incidence of PPCs on postoperative day 1. Secondary outcomes included the incidence of PPCs on postoperative days 3 and 7, PPC severity score, postoperative nausea and vomiting, postoperative hospital stay, indwelling time of the thoracic drainage tube, 24-hour postoperative resting visual analog pain score, unplanned intensive care unit admission, and postoperative mortality.Results:The incidence of PPCs on postoperative day 1 was significantly lower in the esketamine group than in the control group (37.5% vs 72.5%, P = .002). No significant between-group differences were observed in the incidence of PPCs on postoperative days 3 and 7 or in any of the other secondary outcomes.Conclusion:Perioperative esketamine administration can effectively decrease the early postoperative (postoperative day 1) incidence of PPCs in patients undergoing thoracoscopic radical lung cancer surgery, with no obvious impact on other secondary clinical outcomes.

1. Introduction

Lung cancer remains the leading cause of cancer-related mortality globally. In China, it accounts for approximately 30% of all cancer deaths, and the mortality burden is projected to rise by nearly 40% by 2030.[1] Thoracoscopic radical resection has become the primary surgical option for eligible lung cancer patients. However, these patients frequently present with impaired preoperative pulmonary function, and thoracic procedures carry a high risk of postoperative pulmonary complications (PPCs).[2] Previous international studies have reported that the incidence of PPCs reaches up to 40.4% after major abdominal surgery, while the overall incidence ranges from 2.0% to 5.6% in general surgical populations.[3,4] The development of PPCs is closely associated with adverse postoperative recovery outcomes.[5,6] Notably, 14% to 30% of patients suffering from PPCs die within 30 days after major surgery, whereas the 30-day mortality rate remains only 0.2% to 0.3% in patients without PPCs.[7] Given their high morbidity and mortality, PPCs have drawn increasing clinical attention, and strategies to reduce PPCs have become a critical research focus in perioperative medicine.

Esketamine is the S-enantiomer of racemic ketamine, with approximately twice the anesthetic potency. Its affinity for N-methyl-D-aspartate receptors is nearly double that of ketamine; accordingly, only half the dose is required to achieve an equivalent anesthetic depth. Accumulating clinical evidence has demonstrated that esketamine is associated with a shorter recovery time, superior postoperative analgesia, faster restoration of cognitive function, and a lower incidence of psychiatric side effects compared with racemic ketamine.[8] Furthermore, esketamine can reduce the minimum alveolar concentration of sevoflurane during one-lung ventilation (OLV) and preserve hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction.[9,10] These pharmacological and clinical properties render esketamine valuable for perioperative management, especially in cardiothoracic procedures with a high susceptibility to PPCs.

A growing number of clinical studies have verified the favorable efficacy and safety of esketamine anesthesia in thoracoscopic surgery, laparoscopic cholecystectomy, and breast lumpectomy.[11,12] Its application ensures smooth and rapid emergence, stable perioperative respiratory and hemodynamic status, and a favorable adverse reaction profile. Previous research by Liang et al indicated that esketamine could downregulate pro-inflammatory mediators such as tumor necrosis factor-α, interleukin-1, interleukin-6 (IL-6), and interleukin-8,[13,14] inhibit inducible nitric oxide synthase activity and neutrophil adhesion molecule expression, and elevate anti-inflammatory cytokine levels, thereby exerting multi-target anti-inflammatory effects. Nevertheless, there remains a lack of systematic clinical investigations focusing on the impact of esketamine on PPCs in patients undergoing pulmonary lobectomy and radical lung cancer resection. The clinical value and lung-protective potential of esketamine in thoracic anesthesia therefore warrant further exploration.

Accumulating basic research has revealed that low-dose esketamine, whether given as a single intravenous bolus (0.1–0.5 mg/kg) or continuous infusion (0.1–0.2 mg/kg/h), substantially suppresses the expression of neutrophil surface adhesion molecules. This intervention further reduces pulmonary neutrophil sequestration and attenuates inflammatory lesions in the lung.

This study aimed to investigate the effect of perioperative esketamine administration on PPCs in patients undergoing thoracoscopic radical lung cancer surgery. We hypothesized that esketamine exerts a lung-protective effect by suppressing the release of pulmonary inflammatory cytokines, attenuating intrapulmonary inflammatory aggregation, and consequently reducing the incidence of early PPCs.

2. Methods

2.1. Study design and approval

This single-center, prospective, randomized, double-blind clinical trial was registered at https://clinicals.gov (ChiCTR2300074459, registration date: August 8, 2023) and approved by the Institutional Review Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Guangdong Medical University (PJKT2023-072; approval date: August 20, 2023). Written informed consent was obtained from all subjects before entering the clinical trial. The study was designed according to the Declaration of Helsinki and was reported in accordance with the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials.

2.2. Study population

The study population was screened for potential participants through the surgical plan system before surgery. All patients entering the group had to meet the inclusion criteria without meeting any of the exclusion criteria.

Inclusion criteria: 18 to 65 years old; patients with lung cancer who underwent radical surgery with selective thoracoscopic lobectomy; and American Society of Anesthesiologists grade II to III. Patients diagnosed with early lung cancer were excluded from American Society of Anesthesiologists Class I per standard anesthesia classification, regardless of the absence of comorbidities.

Exclusion criteria: People who are known or suspected to be allergic or contraindicated to various components of research drugs or other opioids; brain injury, intracranial injury, or patients with existing intracranial hypertension; pulmonary hypertension (average pulmonary pressure >25 mm Hg); chronic pain and long-term use of painkillers, psychiatric drugs (including opioids, NSAIDs, sedatives, and antidepressants), alcohol abuse and addiction; heart failure (New York Heart Association grade >2), severe arrhythmia, acute myocardial infarction in the past 6 months, patients who have not received regular antihypertensive treatment or have poor blood pressure control (SBP ≥160 mm Hg and/or DBP ≥100 mm Hg) or (SBP ≤90 mm Hg and/or DBP ≤60 mm Hg); a history of neurological abnormalities, such as patients with epileptic seizures; or a history of previous thoracic surgery.

2.3. Randomization and blinding

The blinding method of this study was implemented as follows: the grouping situation was placed in an opaque envelope and numbered in order. Patients, anesthesiologists, surgeons, nurses, and data collectors did not know the grouping situation. Full-time staff recorded the patients assigned to each group according to the random number table. Other researchers were not aware of this. The research coordinator was the only one who clearly knew which group the patients were assigned to. The only person who had the computer-generated random number table was the statistician, who did not participate in data management and statistical analysis. The dose of physiological saline and the use of esketamine were recorded on the anesthesia record sheet by full-time staff to ensure the implementation of the blinding method.

2.4. Study procedures

2.4.1. Anesthesia and perioperative care

Standard preoperative fasting and nil per os protocols were implemented for all enrolled patients, and no preoperative medications were prescribed. This measure aimed to rule out the confounding influences of preoperative sedatives and anticholinergic drugs on airway secretion production, respiratory drive, and the diagnosis of PPCs. Following entry into the operating room, supplemental oxygen was provided alongside psychological intervention to mitigate preoperative anxiety. After entering the operating room, an 18G upper limb venous channel was established, a static drop of compound sodium chloride solution was maintained, pulse oxygen saturation, electrocardiogram, body temperature, and noninvasive sleeve pressure measurement were routinely monitored, and the forehead grease was wiped with gauze dipped in alcohol. After drying, the electrodes were placed on the forehead, the EEG dual-frequency index (bispectral index) was connected, the depth of anesthesia was continuously monitored, a moderate mask was used to gently cover the patient’s nose, and the patient was instructed to breathe deeply (the mask delivered oxygen at 5 L/min). General anesthesia induction: the appropriate double-cavity bronchial catheter was selected (according to the measured value of the inner diameter of the trachea, ≥17 mm, selected Fr39; ≥15 mm, selected Fr37; ≥13 mm, selected Fr35; ≥11 mm, selected Fr30; 9–11 mm, selected Fr28), and a fiber bronchoscope was used for double-cavity tube positioning.

A uniform surgical protocol was implemented for all participants. All video-assisted thoracoscopic pulmonary lobectomies were performed by 3 dedicated senior thoracic surgeons. No differences were observed between the 2 groups with respect to lymph node dissection coverage, intraoperative manipulations, and tissue processing. Such standardization guaranteed consistent surgical quality and mitigated bias attributable to procedure-related complications.

2.4.2. Mechanical ventilation settings

All participants adopted the pressure control mode guaranteed by capacity. The ventilation parameters were set as follows: tidal volume, 8 mL/kg for double pulmonary ventilation; a tidal volume of 6 mL/kg was adopted for OLV, representing the maximum value within the 4 to 6 mL/kg range advocated by guidelines for lung-protective ventilation in thoracic procedures. Ventilator settings were fully standardized according to body weight across all participants. The application of 5 to 8 cm H2O positive end-expiratory pressure together with lung recruitment maneuvers helped attenuate the risk of ventilator-associated lung injury; 0.6 to 1.0 for double pulmonary ventilation, 0.4 to 0.5 for single pulmonary ventilation; positive end-expiratory pressure, 5 to 8 cm H2O; inhalation ratio, 1:2; platform pressure, <25 cm H2O; and breathing frequency adjustment to maintain the target PETCO2 at 35 to 45 mm Hg. The recruitment maneuver strategy included giving a positive airway pressure of 25 cm H2O for 15 to 20 seconds immediately after intubation, giving a positive airway pressure of 25 cm H2O for 15 to 20 seconds when restoring double pulmonary ventilation, and giving a continuous airway positive pressure of 20 cm H2O for 15 to 20 seconds at the beginning of OLV. Intraoperative maintenance fluid consisted of compound sodium chloride solution infused at a rate of 4 to 6 mL/(kg·h). Colloid solutions were supplemented exclusively in the presence of hypovolemia. During circulatory regulation, mean arterial pressure was maintained between 70 and 100 mm Hg, and heart rate was controlled within 50 to 100 beats per minute. Ephedrine or phenylephrine was administered to correct hypotension, and short-acting β-adrenergic receptor blockers were used for hypertension or tachycardia. All vasoactive medications were given in discrete low doses in response to hemodynamic fluctuations; continuous infusion of long-acting vasoactive agents was avoided throughout the procedure.

If hypoxemia occurred during the operation (defined as blood oxygen saturation [SpO2] <92% or PaO2 <60 mm Hg, with no evidence of hemodynamic damage, airway problems, or tracheal displacement), FiO2 was gradually increased, and 5 cm H2O airway positive pressure was continuously increased in nonventilated lungs. The criteria for defining hypoxemia and corresponding therapeutic measures outlined herein are the mainstream clinical strategies for hypoxemia associated with thoracic OLV, which are in line with anesthesia guidelines at home and abroad.

2.4.3. Anesthesia regimen and study intervention

The identical drug regimen used in both study groups included: 0.05 mg/kg midazolam, 0.3 to 0.5 µg/kg sufentanil, 0.2 mg/kg cis-atracurium, 2 mg/kg propofol for induction, 0.15 mg/kg·h cis-atracurium, 3.5 mg/kg·h propofol, and 6 µg/kg·h remifentanil for intraoperative maintenance. The difference between the 2 study groups during intraoperative maintenance was as follows: In the esketamine group, 0.25 mg/kg of esketamine was administered intravenously during the induction phase, and 0.125 mg/kg/h of esketamine was continuously infused during the intraoperative maintenance phase. The saline group received an intravenous injection of saline solution equivalent in volume to that of the esketamine group during anesthesia induction and a continuous infusion of saline solution equivalent in volume to that of the esketamine group throughout the surgery. Both groups underwent ultrasound-guided lower radial artery puncture catheterization and ultrasound-guided inferior jugular vein puncture catheterization. Both groups were given 0.33% ropivacaine 30 mL chest 5, chest 7 paravertebral nerve block before skin cutting. After intubation, auscultation and fiber bronchoscopy were used to determine the position of the catheter, and then surgery was performed. After the operation, patient-controlled intravenous analgesia was given for analgesia. The patient-controlled intravenous analgesia scheme was: 2 µg/kg of sufentanil + 1 µg/kg of dexmedetomidine. During the study, the use of saline and esketamine was charted on medical records in a blinded manner.

The current study employed a low-dose regimen of esketamine: 0.25 mg/kg for anesthesia induction, followed by continuous intraoperative infusion at a rate of 0.125 mg/kg per hour. The dosing strategy was established with reference to existing literature on anesthesia for thoracic procedures as well as preliminary experiments conducted at our center and was well within the clinically acceptable low-dose safety range. Given that high-dose esketamine is capable of impairing immune function, high-dose regimens were excluded from this research.

2.5. Outcomes

The primary outcome was the occurrence of pulmonary complications on the first day after surgery (including pneumonia, respiratory failure, pleural effusion, pneumothorax, atelectasis, and bronchospasm, according to the Assess Respiratory Risk in Surgical Patients in Catalonia (ARISCAT); the method is shown in Table S1, Supplemental Digital Content 1[15]). Pneumothorax was recorded as a complication only if it occurred on the ventilated surgical lung side. Secondary outcomes included the incidence of PPCs on postoperative days 3 and 7, the severity score of PPCs (see Table 3), postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV), postoperative hospitalization days, drainage tube indwelling time, postoperative 24-hour resting pain score, unplanned admission to the intensive care unit (ICU), and postoperative death. The severity score of PPCs ranges from level 0 to 5 (the method is shown in Table S2, Supplemental Digital Content 2[16]), where level 0 indicates that there are no symptoms or signs of PPCs, levels 1 to 4 indicate that the form of complications gradually worsens, and level 5 indicates death before discharge. We recorded data related to baseline characteristics, intraoperative surgery and anesthesia, and patient group. The necessary postoperative examination items included chest X-ray examination, blood routine examination, and blood gas analysis. The primary and secondary outcomes were evaluated by well-trained investigators.

Diagnosis of postoperative pneumonia was established according to the ARISCAT clinical scoring criteria, integrating clinical symptoms, thoracic imaging findings, and complete blood count results. Microbiological culture was excluded from the early diagnostic workup. Early postoperative pulmonary inflammatory lesions primarily stem from preoperative latent inflammation, surgical trauma, OLV-mediated inflammatory injury, and atelectasis-related inflammatory exudates, which are pathologically different from late-developing hospital-acquired bacterial pneumonia.

2.6. Statistical analysis

Descriptive analysis was used to describe the baseline characteristics of the 2 groups of patients, and descriptive statistics were used for continuous variables (mean [standard deviation] or median [interquartile range]). Frequencies and percentages were used for categorical variables. For comparisons of the primary outcome and the other 2-category variables, the Pearson χ2 test was used. Fisher precision test was used only when the expected count in at least 25% of cells was <5. The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was used to assess normality. Continuous variables were analyzed using the t test or the Mann–Whitney U test for normally or non-normally distributed data, respectively. We also analyzed the characteristics of each subgroup, including gender, age, smoking history, forced expiratory volume in 1 second/forced vital capacity, ARISCAT risk level (Table S3, Supplemental Digital Content 3[17]), OLV duration, etc. According to the results of our pre-experiment, using PASS (Power Analysis and Sample Size) 16.0 software (Limited Liability Company), we used a power analysis based on the primary outcome to calculate the sample size (N). We calculated that when the test level was 0.025, the power was 80%, and P1 (experimental group) = 0.54 and P2 (control group) = 0.86, each group needed 35 people to demonstrate a statistical difference between the 2 groups. Considering that 10% of the data might be lost, we finally planned to collect data from 80 patients.

4. Discussion

Among the patients who underwent elective radical surgery for lung cancer, we found that the incidence of pulmonary complications on the first day after surgery in the esketamine group was lower than that in the control group. Under the conditions of our study, we found that there was no significant difference between the 2 groups in the incidence of PPCs on postoperative days 3 and 7 or the severity of PPCs, and the incidences of PONV, postoperative hospitalization days, drainage tube indwelling time, postoperative 24-hour resting pain score, unplanned postoperative admission to the ICU, and postoperative death did not differ significantly between the 2 groups. We believe that the results of this study are of guiding significance for evaluating the treatment of PPCs from the perspective of accelerated recovery surgery.

PPC is a complex syndrome that may be affected by many factors. The occurrence of PPCs often affects the prognosis of patients and slows down the recovery process of patients. Therefore, it is important to reduce PPCs in patients. The ARISCAT scoring standard is the most commonly used scoring scale to evaluate PPCs in patients, which has been proved to be effective and reliable.[18] In this study, the ARISCAT scoring criteria were used to evaluate the occurrence of PPCs in the 2 groups of patients. This study found that the overall incidence of pulmonary complications on the first day after surgery in the esketamine group was 37.5%, while that in the control group was 72.5%, P = .002 < .05, indicating that esketamine could effectively reduce the occurrence of postoperative lung complications in patients undergoing radical lung cancer surgery. However, in terms of the incidence of individual PPCs, except for the significant difference between the 2 groups in postoperative pneumonia (30.0% vs 52.5%, P = .041), there were no significant differences in the incidence of individual PPCs (respiratory failure, 2.5% vs 7.5%; pleural effusion, 10.0% vs 15.0%, P = .735; pneumothorax, 2.5% vs 2.5%, P = 1.000; atelectasis and bronchospasm, P = not applicable). We suspected that this difference could be attributable to the following reasons: first, we included more patients with ARISCAT scores ≥26, and second, the patients we included had a low lung function index, forced expiratory volume in 1 second/forced vital capacity, which might lead to a higher incidence of postoperative lung complications in our study as a whole, and the difference was not obvious. Therefore, the benefits of esketamine on PPCs in patients with radical lung cancer need to be verified in future prospective clinical trials. Besides, on postoperative days 3 and 7, there was no significant difference in the incidence of PPCs between the 2 groups, which may be attributed to pharmacological interventions by clinicians.

The incidence of clinically diagnosed pneumonia was relatively high on postoperative day 1 in this study. The main reasons are as follows: most enrolled patients had subclinical airway inflammation preoperatively. Combined with stress-induced pulmonary inflammation triggered by video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery and OLV, as well as early postoperative atelectasis, these findings were classified as pneumonia according to the ARISCAT clinical assessment system. Such early inflammatory changes did not result from new-onset postoperative bacterial infection. Given the long turnaround time for microbiological culture and antibiotic efficacy evaluation, these tests were not adopted as early observational indicators.

At present, the exact molecular mechanism by which esketamine alleviates PPCs remains to be further elucidated. Accumulated evidence has demonstrated that esketamine can suppress the activation of inflammatory cells induced by oxygen free radicals and downregulate the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as interleukins and tumor necrosis factor, thereby attenuating perioperative inflammatory responses. Perioperative opioid administration is known to exert immunosuppressive effects; however, esketamine can partially counteract opioid-related immunosuppression by reducing perioperative opioid consumption, inhibiting the overexpression of inflammatory mediators, and exerting protective anti-inflammatory effects.[19] Furthermore, esketamine acts as an N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor antagonist, binds to μ-opioid receptors,[20] elevates central serotonin and norepinephrine concentrations,[21] and blocks the activation of opioid-induced injury signaling pathways. It also alleviates opioid tolerance and postoperative hyperalgesia.[22] By inhibiting the transmission of noxious stimuli and reducing central nervous system sensitization, esketamine can lower postoperative stress responses, peripheral leukocyte counts, and C-reactive protein levels.

Both ketamine and esketamine exhibit prominent dose-dependent effects on systemic inflammatory status and immune function. Low-dose esketamine can attenuate the production of pro-inflammatory mediators such as tumor necrosis factor-α, IL-6, and interleukin-8, conferring anti-inflammatory properties and immune preservation. Nevertheless, supra-therapeutic high doses are capable of inhibiting natural killer cell activity and triggering immunosuppression. The clinical low-dose strategy utilized herein not only elicits pulmonary protective and anti-inflammatory actions but also successfully circumvents the immunosuppressive hazards caused by high-dose exposure.

The esketamine regimen adopted herein lies in the therapeutic window for neutrophil modulation. It blunts excessive neutrophil activation and attenuates pro-inflammatory cytokine production, which ultimately reduces the incidence of pulmonary complications following lung cancer resection.

Although our findings are partially inconsistent with those of previous studies, the present trial is one of the first investigations specifically exploring the impact of intraoperative esketamine administration on PPCs in patients undergoing thoracoscopic lung cancer resection. Our results indicated that esketamine effectively reduced the risk of early postoperative respiratory failure, which may be attributed to its ability to relax bronchial smooth muscle and improve lung compliance.[23] Additionally, the opioid-sparing properties of esketamine minimize residual opioid effects, preserve carbon dioxide chemosensitivity,[24] facilitate the recovery of spontaneous breathing after surgery, shorten thoracic drainage indwelling time, and accelerate overall anesthetic and functional recovery. In this study, the dosage regimen of esketamine was 0.25 mg/kg for anesthesia induction followed by continuous infusion at 0.125 mg/kg/h for intraoperative maintenance.[25] This regimen yielded both immediate and sustained clinical benefits for patients undergoing lung cancer surgery, including stable hemodynamics, reduced opioid consumption, fewer opioid-related adverse events, satisfactory postoperative analgesia, and decreased reliance on rescue analgesics, all of which are consistent with the core objectives of enhanced recovery after surgery.

Moreover, our findings further confirmed the clinical feasibility and safety of low-dose perioperative esketamine. The significant reduction in early PPC incidence in the esketamine group (P = .002) was consistent with recent clinical evidence reported by Wang et al.[26] A recent meta-analysis also demonstrated that perioperative intravenous esketamine could reduce intraoperative opioid requirements and lower PPC risk in patients undergoing pulmonary lobectomy. In addition, esketamine exhibits a markedly lower incidence of psychotomimetic side effects compared with racemic ketamine. By modulating opioid receptor activity, it effectively reduces opioid-related adverse reactions and delays the development of opioid tolerance.[27–29] Collectively, low-dose intravenous esketamine confers protective benefits against PPCs in patients undergoing radical lung cancer resection. Furthermore, esketamine preserves spontaneous breathing and airway reflexes during anesthesia, enhances sympathetic tone, reduces the risk of intraoperative hypotension and cardiac depression,[30–33] and exerts stable protective effects on both respiratory and circulatory homeostasis.

Currently, clinical studies regarding esketamine mainly concentrate on its postoperative analgesic efficacy, antidepressant effects, and other common perioperative applications. Literature focusing on the association between esketamine and PPCs in thoracic surgical patients remains scarce, and no directly relevant clinical trials were retrieved across major academic databases. Accordingly, our findings provide valuable preliminary clinical evidence for perioperative esketamine use in thoracoscopic lung cancer surgery.

Nevertheless, this study has several notable limitations. First, it was a single-center trial with a relatively small sample size, which may limit the generalizability of our conclusions. Multicenter collaborative studies with larger cohorts are warranted in future investigations. Second, statistical results based on the conventional P < .05 threshold may not guarantee robust replicability across independent cohorts. Third, this trial did not detect perioperative inflammatory cytokine levels, including IL-6 and interleukin-10, which are closely associated with ventilator-induced lung injury, tracheobronchial mucosal trauma, and surgical stress responses. Such laboratory indicators should be supplemented in subsequent research. Fourth, several intraoperative variables, including intraoperative urine output and blood loss, were not fully balanced or analyzed between groups, which should be further optimized and standardized in follow-up studies.

In summary, this randomized, placebo-controlled, double-blind clinical trial was conducted to assess the efficacy of perioperative intravenous esketamine infusion in reducing the incidence of PPCs in patients undergoing thoracoscopic radical lung cancer surgery. If our study hypothesis is validated, perioperative esketamine administration could serve as a promising therapeutic strategy within the enhanced recovery after surgery protocol, effectively lowering the risk of PPCs and promoting postoperative rehabilitation in this patient population.
