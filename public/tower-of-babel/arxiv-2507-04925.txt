Critical exponent of ternary words with few distinct palindromes
L​’ubomíra Dvořáková a, Lucas Mol b, Pascal Ochem c

Abstract
We study infinite ternary words that contain few distinct palindromes. In particular, we classify such words according to their critical exponents.



1
Introduction

We consider the trade-off between the number of distinct palindromes and the critical exponent in infinite words.
For brevity, every mention of a number of palindromes will refer to a number of distinct palindromes, including the empty word.
This trade-off has been recently investigated for binary words

9
]

Removing the constraint on the alphabet size does not give anything new:

•

•

Preliminaries

An
alphabet

\mathcal{A}
is a finite set and its elements are called
letters
.
A
word

u
over
\mathcal{A}
of
length

n
is a finite string
u=u_{0}u_{1}\cdots u_{n-1}
, where
u_{j}\in\mathcal{A}
for all
j\in\{0,1,\dots,n-1\}
.
If
{\mathcal{A}}=\{\tt 0,\tt 1,\dots,\tt d-1\}
, the length of
u
is denoted
|u|
and
|u|_{\tt f}
denotes the number of occurrences of the factor
{\tt f}\in\mathcal{A}^{+}
in the word
u
. The
Parikh vector

\vec{u}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
is the vector defined as
{\vec{u}}=(|u|_{\tt 0},|u|_{\tt 1},\dots,|u|_{\tt d-1})^{T}
.
The set of all finite words over
\mathcal{A}
is denoted
{\mathcal{A}}^{*}
. The set
{\mathcal{A}}^{*}
equipped with concatenation as the operation forms a monoid with the
empty word

\varepsilon
as the neutral element. If
w=ux
for some
u,x\in{\mathcal{A}}^{*}
, then
wx^{-1}=u
.
We will also consider the set
{\mathcal{A}}^{\omega}
of infinite words (that is, right-infinite words) and the set
{}^{\omega}\hskip-4.26773pt{\mathcal{A}}^{\omega}
of bi-infinite words.
A word
v
is an
e
-
power
of a word
u
if
v
is a prefix of the infinite periodic word
uuu\cdots=u^{\omega}
and
e=|v|/|u|
.
We write
v=u^{e}
. We also call
u^{e}
a repetition with period
u
and exponent
e
.
For instance, the Czech word
kavka
(a kind of bird – jackdaw) can be written in this formalism as
(kav)^{5/3}
.
A word is
\alpha^{+}
-free
(resp.
\alpha
-free
) if it
contains no repetition with exponent
\beta
such that
\beta>\alpha
(resp.
\beta\geqslant\alpha
).

The
critical exponent

E({\bf u})
of an infinite word
{\bf u}
is defined as

E({\bf u})=\sup\left\{e\in\mathbb{Q}:\ v^{e}\text{ is a factor of }{\bf u}\text{ for a non-empty word }v\right\}.

If each factor of
{\bf u}
has infinitely many occurrences in
{\bf u}
, then
{\bf u}
is
recurrent
.
Moreover, if for each factor the distances between its consecutive occurrences are bounded, then
{\bf u}
is
uniformly recurrent
.
The
reversal
of the word
w=w_{0}w_{1}\cdots w_{n-1}
, where the
w_{i}
are letters, is the word
w^{R}=w_{n-1}\cdots w_{1}w_{0}
.
A word
w
is a
palindrome
if
w=w^{R}
.

Consider a factor
w
of a recurrent infinite word
{\bf u}=u_{0}u_{1}u_{2}\cdots
. Let
j<\ell
be two consecutive occurrences of
w
in
\bf u
. Then the word
u_{j}u_{j+1}\cdots u_{\ell-1}
is a
return word
to
w
in
\bf u
.

Let
\mathcal{L}({\bf u})
denote the set of factors of
\bf u
.
Given a word
w\in\mathcal{L}({\bf u})
, we define the sets of left extensions, right extensions and bi-extensions of
w
in
{\bf u}
over an alphabet
\mathcal{A}
respectively as

L_{{\bf u}}(w)=\{{\tt i}\in{\mathcal{A}}:{\tt i}w\in\mathcal{L}({\bf u})\},\qquad R_{{\bf u}}(w)=\{{\tt j}\in{\mathcal{A}}:w{\tt j}\in\mathcal{L}({\bf u})\}

and

B_{{\bf u}}(w)=\{({\tt i},{\tt j})\in{\mathcal{A}}\times{\mathcal{A}}:{\tt i}w{\tt j}\in\mathcal{L}({\bf u})\}.

If
\#L_{{\bf u}}(w)>1
, then
w
is called
left special (LS)
. If
\#R_{{\bf u}}(w)>1
, then
w
is called
right special (RS)
. If
w
is both LS and RS, then it is called
bispecial (BS)
.
We define
b(w)=\#B_{{\bf u}}(w)-\#L_{{\bf u}}(w)-\#R_{{\bf u}}(w)+1
and we distinguish
ordinary BS factors
with
b(w)=0
,
weak BS factors
with
b(w)<0
and
strong BS factors
with
b(w)>0
.

A factor
w
of an infinite word
\bf u
is
left special
if it has at least two left extensions, i.e.,
{\tt i}w,{\tt j}w
are factors of
\bf u
for at least two distinct letters
{\tt i,j}\in\mathcal{A}
. A
right special
factor is defined analogously. A factor is called
bispecial
if it is both left and right special.

A
morphism
is a map
\psi:{\mathcal{A}}^{*}\to{\mathcal{B}}^{*}
such that
\psi(uv)=\psi(u)\psi(v)
for all words
u,v\in{\mathcal{A}}^{*}
.
The morphism
\psi
is
non-erasing
if
\psi(\tt i)\not=\varepsilon
for each
{\tt i}\in{\mathcal{A}}
.
Morphisms can be naturally extended to infinite words by setting

\psi(u_{0}u_{1}u_{2}\cdots)=\psi(u_{0})\psi(u_{1})\psi(u_{2})\cdots\,
.
A
fixed point
of a morphism
\psi:{\mathcal{A}}^{*}\to{\mathcal{A}}^{*}
is an infinite word
\bf u
such that
\psi(\bf u)=\bf u
.
We associate to a morphism
\psi:{\mathcal{A}}^{*}\to{\mathcal{A}}^{*}
the
(incidence) matrix

M_{\psi}
defined for each
k,j\in\{0,1,\dots,d-1\}
as
[M_{\psi}]_{kj}=|\psi(\tt j)|_{\tt k}
.

If there exists
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that
M_{\psi}^{N}
has positive entries, then
\psi
is a
primitive
morphism.
By definition, we have for each
u\in{\mathcal{A}}^{*}
the following relation for the Parikh vectors
\vec{\psi(u)}=M_{\psi}\vec{u}
.

Let
{\bf u}
be an infinite word over an alphabet
\mathcal{A}
and let
\psi:{\mathcal{A}}^{*}\to{\mathcal{B}}^{*}
be a morphism.
Consider a factor
w
of
\psi({\bf u})
. We say that
(w_{1},w_{2})
is a
synchronization point
of
w
if
w=w_{1}w_{2}
and for all
p,s\in\mathcal{L}(\psi({\bf u}))
and
v\in\mathcal{L}({\bf u})
such that
\psi(v)=pws
there exists a factorization
v=v_{1}v_{2}
of
v
with
\psi(v_{1})=pw_{1}
and
\psi(v_{2})=w_{2}s
. We denote the synchronization point by
w_{1}\bullet w_{2}
.

Given a factorial language
L
and an integer
\ell
, let
L^{\ell}
denote the words of length
\ell
in
L
.
The
Rauzy graph
of
L
of order
\ell
is the directed graph
whose vertices are the words of
L^{\ell-1}
, the arcs are the words of
L^{\ell}
, and the arc
corresponding to the word
w
goes from the vertex corresponding to the prefix of
w
of length
\ell-1

to the vertex corresponding to the suffix of
w
of length
\ell-1
.

An
overpal
is a word of the form
axax^{R}a
, where
x^{R}
is
the reversal of the (possibly empty) word
x
and
a
is a single letter.
Overpals were introduced by Rajasekaran, Rampersad, and Shallit
[
1
]
.

A
pattern

p
is a non-empty finite word over the alphabet

\Delta=\left\{A,B,C,\dots\right\}
of capital letters called
variables
.
An
occurrence
of
p
in a word
w
is a non-erasing morphism
h:\Delta^{*}\to\Sigma^{*}

such that
h(p)
is a factor of
w
.
A
letter pattern
is a non-empty finite word over the alphabet
\left\{a,b,c,\ldots\right\}
of lowercase variables
such that every variable stands for one letter from the alphabet
\left\{\texttt{0},\texttt{1},\texttt{2},\ldots\right\}

and different variables denote different letters.
For example, the occurrences of the letter pattern
abaca
over the ternary alphabet are

01020
,
02010
,
10121
,
12101
,
20212
,
21202
.
Letter patterns were introduced by Elena Petrova
[
16
]
.

General result

Theorem 1
.

There exists an infinite ternary
\beta^{+}
-free word containing only
p
palindromes
for the following pairs
(p,\beta)
. Moreover, this list of pairs is optimal.

(a)

(5,\tfrac{10}{3})

(b)

(6,2)

(c)

(16,\tfrac{41}{22})

(d)

(18,\tfrac{7}{4})

Proof.

The optimality is obtained by backtracking. For example, to obtain the step between
items

(c)
and
(d)
, we show that
there exists no infinite ternary
\tfrac{41}{22}
-free word containing at most
17
palindromes.
The proof of the positive results is split into cases depending on the factor complexity of the considered words:

Theorem

3
for the exponential cases,
Theorem

5
for the first polynomial case, and
Theorem

8
for the second polynomial case.
In any case, to check that the proposed word does not contain more than the claimed number
of palindromes, we only need to check the factors up to length
k+2
,
where
k
is the length of the longest allowed palindrome.
∎

We need some terminology and a lemma from

A morphism

Lemma 2

[

Lemma 4
.

The word
t^{\omega}(\texttt{0})
is obtained from the Thue-Morse word
(i.e., the fixed point of
\texttt{0}\mapsto\texttt{01}
;
\texttt{1}\mapsto\texttt{10}

If we erase every occurrence of

from

,
we get the fixed point of

is inserted correctly. The word

Theorem 5
.

The word
t^{\omega}(\texttt{0})
contains 6 palindromes and is
2^{+}
-free.
Every bi-infinite ternary
\tfrac{9}{4}
-free word containing at most 6 palindromes has the same factor set as one
of the six words obtained from
t^{\omega}(\texttt{0})
by letter permutation.

Proof.

Matthieu Rosenfeld has written a program that implements the algorithm of
Julien Cassaigne
[
4
]
to test whether a morphic word
w
avoids a pattern
P
, with
w
and
P
as input.
We use it to show that
t^{\omega}(\texttt{0})
avoids the pattern
ABABA
. Moreover,
t^{\omega}(\texttt{0})
contains neither
000
,

111
, nor
22
. So,
t^{\omega}(\texttt{0})
is
2^{+}
-free.
Alternatively, the reader can convince herself that the insertion of the letter
2

in the middle of every factor
10
in the Thue-Morse word does not create an overlap.

Suppose now that
\mathbf{w}
is a ternary
\tfrac{9}{4}
-free word containing at most
6
palindromes.
Note that
\mathbf{w}
must contain the four palindromes
\varepsilon
,
0
,
1
, and
2
.
Further, we see that
\mathbf{w}
must contain two palindromes from the set
\{\texttt{00},\texttt{11},\texttt{22}\}
, because a backtracking search shows that there are only finitely many ternary
\tfrac{9}{4}
-free words containing at most six palindromes and containing at most one palindrome from
\{\texttt{00},\texttt{11},\texttt{22}\}
.
By permuting the letters if necessary, we assume that
\mathbf{w}
contains the palindromes
00
and
11
.

So
\mathbf{w}
contains the six palindromes
\varepsilon
,
0
,
1
,
2
,
00
, and
11
, and hence no
22
and no palindrome of the form
aba
or
abba
, where
a
and
b
are letters. Thus, it is not hard to see that every return word to
\tt{2}
in
\mathbf{w}
belongs to
R_{1}\cup R_{2}
, where

R_{1}=\{\texttt{201},\texttt{2001},\texttt{2011},\texttt{20011}\}

and

R_{2}=\{\texttt{210},\texttt{2110},\texttt{2100},\texttt{21100}\}.

Further, we see that
\mathbf{w}
does not contain factors from both
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
(otherwise it would contain either
\tt{020}
or
\tt{121}
). By permuting
0
and
1
if necessary, we may assume that
\mathbf{w}
contains no factors from
R_{2}
. It follows that
\mathbf{w}
is obtained from a binary word
\mathbf{v}
by inserting the letter
2
in the middle of every factor
10
. (Note that
\mathbf{v}
is obtained from
\mathbf{w}
by deleting every
2
.)

We claim that
\mathbf{v}
is
\tfrac{7}{3}
-free. Suppose otherwise that
