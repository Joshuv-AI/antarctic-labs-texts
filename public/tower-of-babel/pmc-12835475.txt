Selection of reference genes and proteins for expression studies in pediatric gliomas
Authors: Sonia Nava-Salazar, Oscar A. Pérez-González, Noemí Baranda-Ávila, Alfonso Marhx-Bracho, Carlos Díaz-Ávalos, Leticia Granados-Rojas, Bryan V. Phillips-Farfán, Mario Perezpeña-Diazconti, Gabriel Gutiérrez-Ospina, America Vanoye-Carlo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
A critical aspect of gene and protein expression analysis is the selection of stable reference genes and proteins. These references are essential for normalizing data and enabling accurate comparisons between samples. In today’s context, molecular typing is essential to enhance diagnostic precision and inform therapeutic decisions, further emphasizing the need for reliable reference molecules. In this study, we analyzed the expression levels of six reference gene candidates and seven proteins in pediatric glioma samples. Gliomas are the most common solid tumors found in the central nervous system of children and are typically classified into low-grade gliomas, optic pathway gliomas, and high-grade gliomas. We evaluated the expression levels of the six target genes using RefFinder web-based software. Our findings indicate that YWHAZ and GAPDH are the most reliable pairs of reference genes, while β-Actin and 14-3-3ζ serve as the most dependable reference proteins. Interestingly, the expression of putative housekeeping proteins in low-grade gliomas closely resembles that in high-grade gliomas but differs from the expression observed in optic pathway gliomas. Furthermore, we found no correlation between RNA amounts and protein levels, suggesting variations in how protein expression is regulated. Overall, the results show that the expression of reference genes in pediatric gliomas differs from what has been reported for adult gliomas. This research provides valuable insights into the molecular characteristics of pediatric gliomas and may have important implications for diagnosis and treatment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-025-04119-1.

Introduction

Central nervous system tumors are the most common solid tumors in children and a leading cause of morbidity and mortality related to childhood cancer [1], with gliomas being the predominant tumors. While there is a higher proportion of low-grade gliomas (LGG), high-grade gliomas (HGG) are aggressive tumors with a poor prognosis in children [18, 28].

The classification of central nervous system tumors has evolved significantly over the past decade. The latest system distinguishes between adult and pediatric tumors, marking a major advancement in how we understand glioma biology and integrate that knowledge into their categorization. However, in clinical practice, the most commonly used classifications are based on the distinction between low-grade and high-grade tumors.

A better understanding of glioma biology could allow for improved treatments to limit their long-term sequela and associated mortality [5, 14]. In cancer research, analyzing gene and protein expression is essential for comprehending tumor biology and identifying potential therapeutic targets. One critical aspect is the selection of stable reference genes and proteins to normalize data and enable accurate comparisons between samples. For gliomas, in the last two decades, only four studies with patient samples have been reported [8, 15, 25, 29]. However, no study has explored reference genes and proteins for pediatric glioma tumors; in fact of the four mentioned studies, just one included pediatric glioma samples [8]. Specialized research focused on pediatric gliomas is necessary since knowledge obtained from adult gliomas cannot be directly applied in the pediatric field [9, 13, 21].

In this work, we investigate gene and protein expression stability in pediatric glioma tumors. The expression of seven candidate reference genes [8, 12, 15, 25] was assessed in 24 pediatric glioma tumors, including low-grade and high-grade tumors. Using the stability of these reference genes, we aimed to provide insights into their potential reliability for future gene and protein expression studies in pediatric gliomas. The findings from this study will improve the accuracy and reliability of gene and protein expression analysis in pediatric gliomas.

Materials and methods

Patients. Twenty-four glioma samples were obtained from pediatric patients at the Neurosurgery department of the Instituto Nacional de Pediatría in México City between July 2018 and February 2022 as part of the standard care provided for treating children with gliomas (Table S1). The Institutional Review Board approved the study (INP-032/2018). Written informed consent was obtained from patients or legal guardians before sample collection [11]. Only tumoral samples with more than 80% of tumoral cell density were included. Tumoral cell density was established by the pathology service of INP by H&E staining (Figure S1). Tumors were classified as low-grade or high-grade tumors mainly based on Ki-67 expression and p53. Low-grade gliomas showed less than 5% of Ki-67 and no p53 expression, while high-grade gliomas showed more than 25% of Ki-67 reactivity and p53 presence.

Tumoral tissue preservation. For RNA analysis, tissues were collected in labeled sterile tubes with RNAlater (Qiagen, Valencia, CA, USA) and preserved in liquid nitrogen. Samples for protein analysis were immersed in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) supplemented with glucose and antibiotics. Excess blood vessels were mechanically removed, and the tissues were washed with PBS-glucose to remove residual blood. Samples were stored in liquid nitrogen until analysis [6].

RNA extraction and purification. Sample lysis was performed using the TissueLyser® protocol (Qiagen, Valencia, CA). Thirty milligrams of the tissue were lysed and homogenized using steel beads, lysis buffer, and the rotor at 20 to 30 Hz for 3 min. Total RNA was isolated through affinity columns using a commercial RNA extraction and purification kit (RNeasy® Mini Kit, Qiagen, Valencia, CA). The purity and concentration were analyzed with agarose gel electrophoresis and the Nanodrop ND-1000 ultra-spectrophotometry equipment (NanoDrop) [23].

cDNA synthesis and gene expression analysis. cDNA was obtained by reverse transcription PCR using the High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription® Kit (Applied Biosystems) according to their protocol, with random primers. The obtained cDNA was evaluated to determine its purity and concentration in a Nanodrop ND-1000 (NanoDrop).

The expression of six reference genes was analyzed: Tubulin beta 3 class III (TUBB3), Peptidylprolyl isomerase A (PPIA), tyrosine 3-monooxygenase/tryptophan 5-monooxygenase activation protein zeta (YWHAZ), Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH), Eukaryotic 18 S rRNA (18 S), and β-Actin (ACTN) (Table S2). Quantitative PCR (qPCR) was conducted in a final volume of 20 µL, utilizing 1X TaqMan probes (Table S3) and 1X TaqMan Universal PCR Master Mix (Applied Biosystems). The amplification was performed using the StepOnePlus™ Real-Time PCR System under the following conditions: 2 min at 50 °C, followed by 10 min at 95 °C, and then 40 cycles of 15 s at 95 °C and 1 min at 60 °C. After each run, a melting curve analysis was performed to confirm the specificity of the amplification. Samples were analyzed in triplicate, and gene expression levels were determined by the cycle threshold (Ct) values.

Protein extraction and quantification. Tumoral tissues were homogenized in a lysis buffer (8 M Urea, 20 mM HEPES, 1 mM EDTA at pH 8.0). The samples were sonicated at 20 W for three cycles, each consisting of 10 s at 4 °C, using a Sonic Vibra Cell Model VC50 (Sonics and Materials Inc. Danbury, USA). The homogenates were centrifuged at 16,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was stored at -80 °C. The Bradford method was employed to quantify the total protein content. The assay was performed in microplates, and the absorbance was measured at 595 nm. A standard curve, using bovine serum albumin, was constructed to determine the protein concentration [3].

SDS-PAGE and western blot analysis. Electrophoresis was performed using acrylamide/bisacrylamide mixtures of 12% and 15% to separate the protein mixture. Protein samples ranging from 5 to 30 µg were prepared with Laemmli buffer and 0.1 M dithiothreitol and boiled. Electrophoresis was carried out on ice at 150 V for 2–3 h in a running buffer (192 mM glycine and 25 mM Tris). Proteins were transferred from the gel to polyvinylidene difluoride membranes using a wet transfer system at 90 V for 1.5 h on ice with transfer buffer (20% methanol, 192 mM glycine, and 25 mM Tris). Blocking was performed using 5% non-fat dry milk in PBS for 30 min. Membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies specific to the target proteins in PBS containing 0.1% Tween-20 (Table S4). The membrane was incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP) conjugated secondary antibodies (ab6728, Abcam; sc-2357, Santa Cruz Biotechnology) for 1 h at room temperature. Proteins were detected using a chemiluminescence detection system (WBKLS0500, Millipore). Images were captured using a ChemiDoc Imaging system and densitometric analysis was performed using Image Lab software (V.6.0.1). The analyzed proteins were Cyclophilin A (CyPA), Ribosomal protein L13A, 14-3-3ζ, GAPDH, β-Actin, ɑ-Tubulin and β-Tubulin (Table S2).

Statistical Analysis. Gene and protein expression were analyzed using the RefFinder web tool [32]. RefFinder, integrates data from the computational programs ΔCt [26], NormFinder [2], GeNorm [30], and BestKeeper [20]. NormFinder assesses gene expression stability by considering intra-group and inter-group variations through a linear mixed model, accounting for systematic differences between experimental groups. geNorm operates on the principle that two ideal reference genes should maintain a constant expression ratio across all samples; it quantifies gene stability by calculating the average pairwise variation between candidate genes. The BestKeeper method utilizes raw cycle threshold (Cq) data to determine standard deviation (SD), coefficient of variation (CV), and correlation with a geometric mean index called the BestKeeper Index based on the Ct values of stable genes. The Comparative ΔCt method measures the consistency of cycle threshold (Ct) differences between two stable reference genes across all samples, assessing expression constancy without needing a reference index or group-based modeling.

The correlation between mRNA and protein levels was assessed using a Pearson correlation coefficient matrix, which utilized CT values for mRNA and the logarithm of optical density for protein expression. This correlation was examined for the five pairs of gene-protein relationships evaluated in the study. Differences in protein abundance were assessed through a linear model, treating proteins as categorical factors.

Results

Expression levels of reference genes

The cycle threshold (Ct) values for the candidate reference genes across the twenty-four pediatric glioma samples varied from 10 to 36 cycles (Fig. 1A). Notably, the 18 S gene had the highest expression level, with the lowest Ct value of 15.10 ± 4.44. In contrast, TUBB3 exhibited the lowest expression level, reflected by a higher Ct value of 30.17 ± 1.25. Although 18 S showed high expression, it also displayed considerable variability among the samples, with a Ct range from 10.87 to 28.11. On the other hand, TUBB3 demonstrated consistent expression with minimal variability, exhibiting a Ct range of 27.93 to 32.30.

Glioma samples were classified as LGG, optic pathway gliomas (OPG), and HGG. Expression levels of the six genes were analyzed by a group using the same tools. Further classification of glioma samples resulted in variable outcomes. Expression of the YWHAZ gene was the most stable and reliable reference gene for LGG tumors by a consensus between the ΔCt method (2.55), NormFinder (0.575), and geNorm tools (1.043). The GAPDH gene was also ranked as stable using the ΔCt method and the geNorm (1.043) tool. In contrast, Bestkeeper showed TUBB3 as a reference gene with the lowest value (1.19); however, it was superior to 1 (Table 1).

For OPG, the ΔCt method, NormFinder, and BestKeeper tools consistently identified the YWHAZ gene as the optimal candidate for a reference gene with indexes of 1.09, 0.578, and 0.43 respectively. However, the geNorm analysis suggested that GAPDH and ACTN were the most suitable reference genes for this specific type of glioma tumor, both with an index of 0.785 (Table 1).

Candidate reference genes were the most variable in the HGG group. As for the other groups, YWHAZ had the best score using the ΔCt method and the NormFinder tool (2.74 and 0.405). GeNorm indicated that ACTN and 18 S were better candidates but with a value greater than allowed (1.616). Bestkeeper results in the HGG group suggested that the candidate gene with the lowest value was TUBB3 (1.06), but the value was above 1 (Table 1).

To address variability in the results, the RefFinder tool [32] was utilized. RefFinder is a web-based tool that evaluates potential reference genes, integrating the four currently available computational programs (geNorm, NormFinder, BestKeeper, and the comparative ΔCt method) (Table 2). According to RefFinder, YWHAZ emerged as the top choice for reference genes in glioma gene expression studies across all samples (1.57) and the three classified groups: LGG (1.32), OPG (1.32), and HGG (1.68). GAPDH ranked second for all gliomas (2.00) and LGG (2.00), while ACTN and 18 S were top choices for OPG (1.68) and HGG (2.99), respectively (Table 2).

Expression levels of reference proteins

The results showed that CyPA and β-Actin were the most easily detectable proteins, both of which were present in all glioma samples when tested with just five micrograms of total protein. In contrast, the detection of ɑ-Tubulin and β-Tubulin proved to be more challenging.

Expression levels of the seven proteins were evaluated in all glioma samples, which were classified by malignancy grade and location, including low-grade glioma (LGG), optic pathway glioma (OPG), and high-grade glioma (HGG) (Fig. 1B). To assess protein stability, the NormFinder algorithm was used, which calculates the overall variation within a dataset as well as the variance within groups [8].

Stability values obtained through NormFinder [2] are presented in Table 3. The results indicate the expression stability of different proteins ranked from best to worst as follows: β-Actin (0.175), 14-3-3ζ (0.353), β-Tubulin (0.522), GAPDH (0.567), ɑ-Tubulin (0.670), Ribosomal protein L13A (0.629), and CyPA 0.809), based on the analysis of all pediatric glioma samples. However, the stability values varied when analyzing each group of gliomas separately. In the case of low-grade gliomas (LGG), β-Actin was the most stable protein, with a stability value of 0.117, while Ribosomal protein L13A (0.893) was the least stable. For optic pathway gliomas (OPG), 14-3-3ζ (0.075) emerged as the most stable protein, whereas GAPDH (0.718) was identified as the least stable. In high-grade gliomas (HGG), β-Actin (0.128) again proved to be the most stable protein, while CyPA (0.918) showed the lowest stability score. Across all groups, β-Actin and 14-3-3ζ consistently demonstrated greater stability compared to the other proteins.

We conducted linear models to investigate the differences in protein levels based on tumor type. Our analysis treated tumor type and protein as fixed factors, including an interaction term to assess how the combination of these factors influences the results. Significant interactions were specifically noted for the OPG group, involving the proteins α-Tubulin, CyPA, and GAPDH (Fig. 1C). In OPG tumors, the mean values for α-Tubulin and GAPDH were the lowest, while the mean value for CyPA was the highest.

Discussion

Gene and protein expression analysis by RT-qPCR and western blotting is integral to the field of cancer biology, and it is essential to employ internal controls, genes, and proteins with high stability for data normalization. Tumors often exhibit a high degree of expression variability compared to normal tissues due to genetic damage, genetic alterations, and cellular heterogeneity, many commonly used reference molecules, which show instability in tumoral samples, raising concerns about the reliability of experimental findings [17]. In response, numerous studies have identified the most stable genes and proteins in various types of cancer [24]. These efforts are crucial for improving cancer research and ensuring accurate results.

Gliomas are characterized by significant heterogeneity, high proliferation rates, and invasive behavior. While there have been efforts to identify reference molecules in glioma tumors, most studies have primarily concentrated on adult gliomas, particularly glioblastoma multiforme [8, 10, 15, 25]. In contrast, pediatric gliomas, which account for about 50% of brain tumors in children [22], have received comparatively less attention.

In our study, we utilized general classifications based on tumor grading. Grades 1 and 2 were categorized as low-grade tumors, while grades 3 and 4 were classified as high-grade tumors. Tumors designated as “optic” correspond to grades 1 and 2 and are located in the optic pathway. This subgroup was treated as a separate category due to its unique clinical management implications, including lower surgical resectability. Consequently, optic tumors often receive more intensive treatment compared to other low histopathological grade tumors, which are typically easier to resect.

We aimed to address this gap by investigating the expression of six potential reference genes in 24 pediatric glioma samples and the expression of seven potential reference proteins in 22 samples. Candidate genes and their protein counterparts were selected based on the most commonly used molecules for normalizing real-time PCR and Western blotting data in glioma biology studies. This selection was made alongside the genes identified in studies focused on discovering reference genes, as mentioned earlier. To determine the stability of potential reference genes, we employed different algorithms. However, the results of each of the four programs exhibited slight variations. To mitigate any inherent biases associated with these algorithms, we also employed the RefFinder tool for a comprehensive analysis.

RefFinder, which integrates data from computational programs like ΔCt, NormFinder, GeNorm, and BestKeeper, was used in order to combine the statistical frameworks of the different algorithms in a single output and reduce the bias and the dependence of the conclusions on the assumptions of the unique method used. The result from RefFinder, a “consensus ranking”, based on the geometric mean of the ranking occupied by each gene with each of the four algorithms used in the analysis, identified the YWHAZ gene as the most stable reference gene in all glioma samples in this study. It encodes the protein 14-3-3ζ, which is highly conserved across various species and is widely expressed in the brain and other tissues. This gene and its associated protein play essential roles in cellular processes such as proliferation, migration, and differentiation [7, 31].

The GAPDH gene also showed highly stable expression across all glioma samples, which is significant given its widespread use as a housekeeping gene in expression studies [10, 24]. In pediatric glioma tumors, it ranked as the second most stable reference gene, indicating a common stability across these samples. The GAPDH gene is traditionally known for codifying a glycolytic enzyme. However, it has been associated with various other functions, including tRNA export, DNA repair, cytoskeletal organization, and antiapoptotic processes, among others [4], and increased GAPDH protein expression has been reported in gliomas [19]. Following these data, our study demonstrated that the GAPDH protein does not maintain a stable expression between pediatric glioma samples or within different malignant grade groups, so it could not be considered a reference protein.

When analyzing the abundance of putative reference genes in pediatric glioma samples, the 18 S gene emerged as the most highly expressed, followed by YWHAZ, ACTN, PPIA, TUBB, and GAPDH. This differs from findings in adult glioma samples reported previously [15], where the most highly expressed gene was PPI, 18 S, ACTB, YWHAZ, and GAPDH. In the case of adult gliomas, GAPDH, ACTN, and 18 S were reported as stable genes, while YWHAZ and PPIA exhibited variable expression [8, 10].

These differences in gene expression between adult and pediatric gliomas may be attributed to intrinsic distinctions between these age groups. These findings underscore the importance of dedicated studies for pediatric gliomas, as the choice of reference genes may significantly impact research outcomes in this specific context.

The analysis of gene stability expression according to malignant grade and location reported that YWHAZ and GAPDH have the most stable expression. For OPG, YWHAZ, ACTN, and GAPDH showed stable expression. On the other hand, for HGG, the most stable gene was YWHAZ, followed by 18 S.

The variations in reference gene stability among different grades of pediatric glioma tumors suggest the need for careful consideration when selecting reference genes for specific subgroups. The choice of stable reference genes may depend on tumor grade and location. In addition, it can also be influenced by the analytical method employed. It is important to tailor the selection of reference genes to the sample characteristics to ensure accurate and reliable results.

Reference proteins for western blotting abundance studies were also evaluated in pediatric glioma samples. Candidate proteins were chosen according to the reference genes reported for gliomas and those analyzed in this study. The stability of candidate reference proteins was evaluated using the Normfinder algorithm.

When analyzing all glioma samples, the best reference protein was β-Actin followed by 14-3-3ζ, with a smaller variation between samples. General linear models corroborated results. The best candidate protein for use as a normalization reference, obtained when analyzing LGG and HGG, was β-Actin. For OPG samples, the best option for protein normalization was 14-3-3ζ.

General linear models detected two general patterns for protein abundances. ɑ-Tubulin, β-Tubulin, β-Actin, GAPDH, 14-3-3ζ and CyPA levels were very similar for HGG and LGG samples. On the other hand, OPG showed another behavior for protein abundance; ɑ-Tubulin, GAPDH, and CyPA showed dissimilar levels. Ribosomal protein L13A was differentially expressed in all three groups of glioma samples.

The unexpected differences in protein abundance patterns between pediatric OPG and other LGG are significant findings and raise intriguing questions about the molecular diversity of these tumors. OPG and LGG are typically classified as low-grade gliomas and often as pilocytic astrocytomas. The distinct protein abundance patterns observed suggest that there may be multiple subtypes within these categories. An effect of the brain region on tumor biology is also highlighted since optic pathway tumors showed a unique protein abundance pattern likely influenced by their location. This region has specific physiological and functional properties that could lead to variations in protein expression compared to other brain regions [16].

The absence of a correlation between mRNA and protein abundances suggests that post-transcriptional regulation plays a role in protein expression in glioma samples. This could involve translation efficiency, protein degradation, and post-translational modifications. Cancer cells, including glioma cells, often have complex and dysregulated molecular mechanisms [27]. The low number of samples analyzed in our study could also contribute to the absence of a correlation. The biological and molecular heterogeneity of glioma samples, even within the same tumor grade, makes it challenging to detect consistent patterns with a small sample size.

The present study has some limitations: a low number of samples, which makes it difficult to analyze protein and gene expression using a more detailed tumor classification; and the fact that we explore the expression of genes and proteins that have already been reported for adult gliomas, rather than searching for new targets using big data technologies.

Despite these challenges, our findings remain relevant, as the distinction between low-grade and high-grade tumors is generally consistent across different classifications. In clinical practice, specific characteristics can aid in accurately classifying tumors, paving the way for a more in-depth exploration of pediatric glioma biology.

This study pointed out the need for a comprehensive understanding of the molecular processes that govern protein expression in gliomas. Targeted therapies may need to consider the post-transcriptional and translational regulation of specific proteins. Future studies with larger sample sizes and a focus on the mechanisms of post-transcriptional regulation in gliomas may provide more insight into the factors that regulate mRNA and protein abundance.

In this study, some of the genes and their related proteins were evaluated as potential references for pediatric gliomas. The study is a good first approximation since there may be better candidates. However, more investigation is required to identify the optimal reference genes and proteins for different types of pediatric gliomas.
