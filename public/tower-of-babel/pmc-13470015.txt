How ecocentric leadership drives environmental performance through green culture and work engagement in Egyptian hospitality and tourism
Authors: Mohammed Aldoghan, Hanan Eid Badwy, Nadia A Abdelmegeed Abdelwahed, Bassam Samir Al-Romeedy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigates the impact of ecocentric leadership on environmental performance in the Egyptian hospitality and tourism sector. It explores the mediating roles of green organizational learning, as well as the sequential mediation of digital green culture and green work engagement, grounded in the Resource-Based View (RBV) and Social Exchange Theory (SET). Data were collected from 404 middle-level managers working in five-star hotels and category-A travel agencies in Greater Cairo using a structured questionnaire. A non-probability convenience sampling method was employed. Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) was used to test the hypothesized relationships. Findings confirm that ecocentric leadership significantly enhances environmental performance, both directly and indirectly through digital green culture and green work engagement, indicating a clear sequential mediation pathway. Moreover, green organizational learning partially mediates the relationship between ecocentric leadership and digital green culture, and digital green culture positively affects green work engagement, while green work engagement positively influences environmental performance. Hospitality and tourism organizations should invest in ecocentric leadership development and promote a culture of green learning and engagement to improve environmental outcomes. This study provides an integrated model linking leadership, learning, culture, and engagement to sustainability performance based on RBV and SET, offering practical and theoretical contributions within an emerging market context like Egypt.Keywords: Ecocentric leadership, Environmental performance, Digital green culture, Green work engagement, Green organizational learning, Tourism and hospitality industry, EgyptSubject terms: Sustainability, Environmental impact

Introduction

The tourism and hospitality industry has recently undergone a profound transformation driven by rising environmental concerns, stakeholder expectations, and global sustainability agendas1. As a result, sustainability has shifted from a peripheral concern to a strategic priority, requiring organizations to rethink traditional management approaches and adopt more environmentally responsible practices2. Within this transformation, the hospitality sector in particular has emerged as a critical arena for sustainability implementation due to its intensive use of energy, water, and natural resources, as well as its direct interaction with environmentally sensitive destinations1. This shift has brought growing attention to ecocentric leadership, a leadership paradigm grounded in ecological ethics, systems thinking, and long-term environmental responsibility3. Unlike traditional managerial approaches focused on short-term or anthropocentric objectives, ecocentric leadership emphasizes the interconnectedness of organizational practices with ecological systems, making it particularly relevant to tourism and hospitality enterprises whose operations depend heavily on natural environments and cultural assets4,5. By embodying values such as ecological integrity and intergenerational equity, ecocentric leaders promote a sustainability-oriented culture that motivates employees to adopt environmentally responsible behaviors6.

Building on this perspective, recent research suggests that the effectiveness of ecocentric leadership is not only reflected in direct outcomes but also in its ability to activate internal organizational mechanisms that translate sustainability values into practice7. Ecocentric leadership is recognized for its influence on several organizational dimensions that directly shape environmental performance8. One key dimension is the development of a digital green culture, where sustainability principles are embedded into digital tools, data systems, and operational platforms, enhancing efficiency, transparency, and low-impact practices (Bahgat et al. 2021). In the hospitality context, digital technologies such as smart energy management systems, environmental monitoring platforms, and data-driven sustainability dashboards play a crucial role in optimizing resource use and reducing environmental impact, thereby justifying the integration of digitalization within green cultural practices9. Another dimension is green organizational learning, which encourages continuous environmental awareness, reflective practices, and knowledge sharing among employees7. Together, these dimensions translate leadership values into improved ecological outcomes, including reduced emissions, enhanced waste management, and sustainable resource use10,11.

Moreover, these organizational mechanisms do not operate in isolation but interact dynamically to reinforce sustainability outcomes. The interaction between green organizational learning and digital green culture is mutually reinforcing. As organizations expand learning initiatives and environmental education, employees become more capable of applying digital solutions that support sustainability goals, thereby strengthening the digital green culture embedded in both strategic and operational processes11,12. Furthermore, both digital green culture and green work engagement—defined by enthusiasm, dedication, and active involvement in sustainable work—have demonstrated direct positive effects on environmental performance. Engaged employees are more likely to collaborate on green innovations and uphold environmentally responsible practices, amplifying the organization’s environmental outcomes13,14.

From this standpoint, the influence of ecocentric leadership extends beyond direct effects to encompass indirect and process-based pathways7. Ecocentric leadership exerts its strongest influence through these mediating mechanisms. By shaping digital green culture and enhancing green work engagement, ecocentric leaders indirectly foster substantial improvements in environmental performance8. When employees internalize ecological values and actively participate in sustainability-driven digital transformation and green initiatives, leadership vision becomes embedded in organizational routines and behavioral norms7. Thus, ecocentric leadership contributes to an integrated organizational ecosystem where environmental performance is both a strategic priority and a shared cultural value (Bahgat et al. 2021).

Importantly, examining these relationships within the Egyptian context offers additional relevance and practical significance. Egypt represents a unique case characterized by high energy consumption in hospitality operations due to climatic conditions, particularly the extensive reliance on cooling systems in hotels and resorts15. At the same time, the country possesses significant potential for renewable energy—especially solar energy—which can be leveraged to support sustainable tourism development16. Moreover, Egypt’s tourism industry is closely tied to environmentally sensitive destinations such as coastal areas and heritage sites, increasing the urgency of adopting sustainable practices17. Despite these contextual characteristics, limited empirical research has examined how leadership-driven sustainability mechanisms operate within the Egyptian hospitality sector.

Although significant progress has been made, the existing literature remains fragmented and lacks a coherent explanation of how these mechanisms operate in an integrated manner, particularly within the tourism and hospitality sectors. Furthermore, several critical gaps persist, limiting a comprehensive understanding of how ecocentric leadership drives sustainability outcomes in these contexts. While interest in sustainability has expanded, empirical research on ecocentric leadership within this sector remains limited, as most existing studies tend to focus on broader leadership models and overlook the distinctive ecological orientation that characterizes ecocentric leadership4,18. Moreover, little is known about the mechanisms through which ecocentric leadership shapes environmental performance, particularly the mediating roles of digital green culture and green work engagement, which are essential for translating leadership vision into operational outcomes. In addition, prior research has often examined these constructs in isolation rather than as part of an integrated system of relationships. The dynamic interplay between green organizational learning and digital green culture is also underexplored, leaving gaps in understanding how these interdependent constructs jointly contribute to sustainability performance19,20. Accordingly, there is a need for a comprehensive framework that captures both the direct and indirect pathways through which ecocentric leadership drives environmental performance within context-specific settings such as Egypt.

Addressing these research gaps, the present study examines the effect of ecocentric leadership on digital green culture, green organizational learning, and environmental performance in tourism and hospitality businesses. It also investigates the impact of green organizational learning on digital green culture, the effects of digital green culture and green work engagement on environmental performance, and the mediating roles of both green organizational learning and green work engagement in shaping the sequential influence of ecocentric leadership on environmental outcomes. By doing so, the study moves beyond fragmented analyses and provides an integrated examination of the underlying mechanisms linking leadership to sustainability outcomes. Collectively, these objectives provide a comprehensive lens for understanding the pathways through which leadership fosters sustainability-driven transformation.

This study contributes to theory by presenting an empirically grounded model that integrates ecocentric leadership with digital green culture, green organizational learning, and green work engagement. Methodologically, it advances the field by applying a multi-level, mediation-based approach that captures the complex processes linking leadership to environmental performance. Practically, the findings offer actionable insights for industry leaders and policymakers seeking to cultivate sustainability-oriented cultures and enhance environmental performance within the tourism and hospitality sector, particularly in emerging economies with context-specific environmental and operational challenges such as Egypt.

Literature review and hypotheses development

Resource-based view (RBV) and social exchange theory (SET)

The connection between the RBV and SET offers a valuable theoretical lens through which to interpret the findings and conceptual model of the study. The RBV asserts that organizations gain sustainable competitive advantage by developing and leveraging unique, valuable, and inimitable internal resources and capabilities21. Within the context of this study, digital green culture, green organizational learning, and green work engagement are framed as strategic intangible assets fostered by ecocentric leadership, as illustrated in Fig. 1. These elements when embedded deeply into the organizational fabric enhance environmental performance and long-term adaptability in an increasingly sustainability-driven tourism and hospitality market22–24.

Proposed model.

Ecocentric leadership, as examined in the study, acts as a catalyst for building these internal capabilities7. It nurtures green organizational learning by encouraging continuous knowledge sharing, environmental awareness, and sustainability-focused innovation. This learning, in turn, feeds into and reinforces a digital green culture, where technological systems and workplace practices reflect and support environmental values25,26. Through the lens of the RBV, such a culture becomes a distinctive organizational capability difficult to replicate and strategically valuable particularly when combined with engaged employees who are committed to ecological goals27.

Further, SET complements the RBV by explaining the psychological and behavioral mechanisms that underpin green work engagement28,29. When employees perceive their leaders as authentically committed to environmental ethics and observe organizational investments in green learning and digital tools, they develop a sense of trust, fairness, and reciprocal obligation. This exchange encourages deeper involvement in sustainability initiatives, leading to higher levels of green work engagement (Bahgat et al. 2021). Employees become intrinsically motivated to contribute to environmental goals, not only because of formal expectations but because of a shared ethical commitment cultivated by leadership. This engagement, alongside digital green culture, becomes a key pathway through which ecocentric leadership indirectly enhances environmental performance10,30.

Together, RBV and SET provide a cohesive explanation of the sequential impact of ecocentric leadership. The RBV contextualizes the development of green capabilities as strategic resources31, while SET highlights the relational and motivational dynamics that enable those capabilities to be activated through employee behavior32. In essence, ecocentric leadership drives environmental performance not only through structural and cultural transformation but also by shaping positive leader–employee relationships that result in meaningful, engaged participation in sustainability efforts—thereby reinforcing both theoretical perspectives in a mutually supportive way7,8.

The effect of ecocentric leadership on digital green culture

Ecocentric leadership is a leadership approach rooted in ecological awareness, ethical accountability toward the environment, and systems thinking. Leaders who adopt this orientation prioritize ecological well-being alongside organizational objectives, embedding sustainability into both strategic planning and operational decision-making4,33. In parallel, digital green culture represents the collective organizational norms, values, and practices that integrate environmental sustainability into digital technologies and processes. It reflects the extent to which digital systems and behaviors are aligned with environmental priorities, such as energy efficiency, digital dematerialization, responsible data usage, and eco-conscious innovation10. Ecocentric leadership significantly contributes to shaping and advancing digital green culture. By consistently modeling environmentally responsible behaviors and articulating a clear ecological vision, such leaders influence how digital technologies are selected, implemented, and utilized10,34. Rather than focusing solely on technological efficiency, ecocentric leaders guide digital transformation toward sustainability, emphasizing the adoption of green IT infrastructure, the digital tracking of environmental metrics, and the reduction of paper-based processes. Through their leadership, a cultural shift emerges where digital tools are deliberately used to minimize ecological impact and reinforce sustainability across the organization35,36. The influence of ecocentric leadership on digital green culture is well supported by established theoretical perspectives. According to the RBV, digital green culture can be seen as a distinctive, valuable, and difficult-to-imitate organizational resource that enhances long-term competitiveness37. Ecocentric leaders are instrumental in developing this resource by integrating ecological principles into digital strategies, thereby transforming digital capabilities into enduring strategic assets4. From the lens of SET, when employees observe authentic environmental commitment from leadership, they develop a sense of trust and shared values. This psychological contract fosters reciprocal behavior, motivating employees to actively support and enrich the organization’s digital sustainability practices38,39. Ultimately, ecocentric leadership not only initiates the formation of digital green culture but also ensures its ongoing integration and resilience as a central pillar of environmental performance4. Accordingly, the following hypothesis is proposed:

H1:

Ecocentric leadership positively impacts digital green culture

The effect of ecocentric leadership on green organizational learning

Green organizational learning refers to the process by which organizations acquire, disseminate, and apply knowledge specifically aimed at improving their environmental performance. It involves fostering a culture of continuous learning around sustainability practices, ecological innovation, and adaptive responses to environmental challenges24,40. Ecocentric leadership plays a foundational role in advancing green organizational learning by fostering a climate that encourages environmental inquiry, critical thinking, and shared knowledge8. Leaders with an ecological orientation actively promote a learning culture where sustainability is not treated as a one-time initiative but as an ongoing development capability. They encourage staff to challenge unsustainable practices, explore eco-innovations, and apply lessons from both internal experiences and external best practices to improve environmental outcomes28. From the perspective of the RBV, such learning becomes a core organizational capability that enhances long-term adaptability and ecological competitiveness41. Ecocentric leaders enable this by prioritizing the development of environmental knowledge assets, investing in green training programs, and institutionalizing learning processes that align with ecological goals42. Simultaneously, SET highlights how employees, when exposed to consistent environmental commitment from their leaders, feel a sense of mutual obligation to contribute to green knowledge creation and application. This reciprocal trust encourages deeper engagement in sustainability learning, making green organizational learning both a reflection of leadership values and a mechanism for collective environmental progress4,25,43. Hence, the following hypothesis is suggested:

H2:

Ecocentric leadership positively impacts green organizational learning

The effect of green organizational learning on digital green culture

Green organizational learning plays a vital role in shaping and reinforcing digital green culture by equipping employees with the knowledge, skills, and mindset necessary to integrate environmental values into digital practices44. When sustainability-focused learning becomes embedded within the organization—through training programs, cross-departmental knowledge exchange, and reflective practices—it creates the cognitive and behavioral foundation needed to apply ecological principles to digital systems and technologies (Bahgat et al. 2021). As employees gain a deeper understanding of environmental issues and sustainable solutions, they become more capable of designing, utilizing, and maintaining digital tools in ways that align with the organization’s green objectives45. Under the RBV, this learning process builds internal capabilities that are both strategic and hard to replicate. By fostering continuous environmental learning, organizations enhance their ability to develop a digital culture that reflects sustainability across all digital touchpoints—from IT infrastructure to customer-facing platforms25,46. Additionally, SET suggests that as employees feel supported and empowered through meaningful learning experiences, they are more likely to reciprocate by embedding those values into their digital work. This sense of mutual commitment encourages ownership and proactive engagement in shaping a digital culture where sustainability is not just an add-on but a core element of the organization’s identity43,47. Accordingly, the following hypothesis is proposed:

H3:

Green organizational learning positively impacts digital green culture

The mediating role of green organizational learning in the relationship between ecocentric leadership and digital green culture

The relationship between ecocentric leadership and digital green culture is not simply direct—it is significantly shaped and amplified through the mediating role of green organizational learning. This learning process acts as the internal engine that operationalizes the values and vision of ecocentric leaders, ensuring that sustainability is not only promoted at the strategic level but embedded into the technical and cultural dimensions of digital transformation48,49. In organizations where ecological leadership is present but green learning mechanisms are weak or absent, the digital culture may remain misaligned with sustainability goals, highlighting the essential function of learning as a connector between leadership intention and cultural change4. From a theoretical standpoint, the RBV clarifies this mechanism by positioning green organizational learning as a strategic capability that converts leadership direction into tangible, competitive resources49. Ecocentric leaders provide the motivation and strategic clarity, but it is through structured environmental learning that employees develop the skills and knowledge necessary to integrate sustainability into digital tools, workflows, and innovation efforts. This learning transforms intangible leadership values into organizational routines that shape and sustain a digital green culture over time34, (Bahgat et al. 2021). At the same time, SET adds a relational dimension to this process. It suggests that when leaders invest in employees’ development—through sustainability-focused training, support, and involvement—employees perceive this as a form of trust and respect. In response, they are more likely to internalize environmental values and apply them within their digital work. This reciprocal exchange reinforces not only the uptake of green practices but also the cultural normalization of sustainability across the digital domain50,51. Thus, green organizational learning serves as the crucial mediating mechanism that bridges ethical leadership and technological culture, grounded both in the strategic development of capabilities (RBV) and in the psychological dynamics of trust and reciprocity (SET)48,52,53. So, the following hypothesis is assumed:

H4:

Green organizational learning positively mediates the relationship between ecocentric leadership and digital green culture

The effect of ecocentric leadership on environmental performance

Environmental performance refers to the extent to which an organization achieves its ecological objectives by reducing environmental harm and promoting sustainable resource use. It encompasses measurable improvements in areas such as energy efficiency, waste management, emissions reduction, and ecosystem preservation—serving as a critical indicator of an organization’s commitment to sustainability28. Ecocentric leadership directly contributes to elevating environmental performance by embedding ecological responsibility into the fabric of organizational strategy and execution8. Leaders who operate from an ecocentric mindset establish environmental goals as core performance metrics, not peripheral concerns54. They influence organizational systems, resource allocation, and employee behavior in ways that prioritize long-term ecological well-being. This leadership approach mobilizes cross-functional efforts, ensures accountability in environmental practices, and fosters a culture where sustainability becomes a shared performance expectation33,34. Such leadership cultivates strategic capabilities—like innovation in green operations, cross-level alignment around sustainability, and the institutionalization of ecological practices—that are difficult for competitors to replicate in the light of the RBV. These internal strengths translate into consistent and superior environmental outcomes55,56. Additionally, SET provides insight into how employees respond to ecocentric leadership: perceiving their leader’s commitment as genuine and values-driven, employees are more likely to reciprocate by aligning their actions with environmental goals4. This reciprocal trust and shared purpose lead to deeper engagement with sustainability initiatives, ultimately reinforcing and sustaining high levels of environmental performance across the organization57 (Bahgat et al. 2021). Therefore, the following hypothesis is formulated:

H5:

Ecocentric leadership positively impacts environmental performance

The effect of digital green culture on green work engagement

Green work engagement refers to an employee’s psychological and emotional involvement in environmentally responsible tasks, characterized by enthusiasm, dedication, and absorption in sustainability-related work activities. It reflects a proactive and positive orientation toward contributing to the organization’s environmental goals, going beyond compliance to demonstrate personal commitment and initiative in green practices14,58. Digital green culture plays a significant role in fostering and sustaining green work engagement by shaping the organizational environment in which employees interact with sustainability through digital systems10. When digital technologies are aligned with ecological values—such as through energy-efficient platforms, virtual collaboration tools that reduce travel, or data systems that track environmental performance—employees are more likely to perceive their work as meaningful and consistent with broader environmental objectives. This alignment between digital infrastructure and sustainability values reinforces employees’ sense of purpose and strengthens their motivation to contribute actively to ecological initiatives45,59. A strong digital green culture becomes a valuable internal asset that enhances organizational performance by facilitating employee engagement in green activities. It creates an enabling environment that supports innovation, autonomy, and environmental accountability through the lens of the RBV12,60. Simultaneously, SET helps explain how a values-aligned digital culture fosters reciprocal relationships: when employees feel supported by systems that reflect shared environmental ethics, they respond with increased commitment and discretionary effort in sustainability-related tasks4. Thus, digital green culture not only provides the technological means for sustainability but also cultivates the psychological conditions necessary for deep and sustained green work engagement60. So, the following hypothesis is demonstrated:

H6:

Digital green culture positively impacts green work engagement

The effect of green work engagement on environmental performance

Green work engagement plays a vital role in enhancing environmental performance61. When employees are genuinely engaged in sustainability-related work, they show greater enthusiasm, initiative, and persistence in adopting and promoting eco-friendly behaviors. These behaviors may include minimizing resource use, contributing to environmental innovations, participating in green initiatives, and helping the organization meet its ecological targets14,62. This form of engagement contributes to environmental performance by encouraging consistent, voluntary efforts that go beyond formal job requirements63. Engaged employees not only implement green practices themselves but also influence their teams and departments, fostering a collective commitment to sustainability64. Over time, these individual and group actions help embed environmental responsibility into the organizational culture, leading to more sustainable outcomes65. Green work engagement represents a valuable internal capability that enhances organizational performance according to RBV66. An engaged workforce—driven by sustainability values—acts as a source of competitive advantage by consistently contributing to ecological goals67. In parallel, SET explains that when organizations support and recognize employees’ green efforts, a sense of mutual obligation is created. This trust encourages employees to invest more effort into environmental initiatives, thereby reinforcing and sustaining high levels of environmental performance68,69. Accordingly, the following hypothesis is suggested:

H7:

Green work engagement positively impacts environmental performance

The subsequential impact of ecocentric leadership

The influence of ecocentric leadership on environmental performance often unfolds through a series of interconnected organizational mechanisms, particularly digital green culture and green work engagement. Rather than exerting impact in a direct, linear fashion, ecocentric leadership initiates a cascading effect—first shaping the organization’s digital environment and cultural values, which in turn foster higher levels of employee engagement in sustainability-related tasks. These intermediate outcomes ultimately translate into tangible improvements in environmental performance7,70–72. By embedding ecological values into digital systems and workflows, ecocentric leaders cultivate a digital green culture that supports environmentally responsible practices through technology. This cultural environment not only aligns digital tools with sustainability objectives but also signals a clear organizational commitment to ecological integrity10, (Bahgat et al. 2021). As a result, employees are more likely to view their work through an environmental lens, leading to deeper psychological engagement in green tasks4. The RBV highlights how digital green culture and green work engagement—when nurtured by ecocentric leadership—function as valuable, rare, and inimitable organizational resources that drive superior environmental outcomes10,73. Simultaneously, SET explains how leadership behavior that consistently supports sustainability generates trust and reciprocity39. Employees, in turn, respond with commitment and proactive involvement in ecological efforts28. Together, these two mediators form the core pathways through which ecocentric leadership contributes to lasting and systemic improvements in environmental performance. Therefore, the following hypothesis is postulated:

H8:

Digital green culture and green work engagement sequentially mediate the positive relationship between ecocentric leadership and environmental performance.

Methodology

Sample and data collection

The research focuses on middle-level management of five-star hotels and category-A travel agencies in Egypt, as these organizations are actively implementing green initiatives to enhance sustainability74, (Khairy et al. 2025). Middle-line management is also crucial in strategy formulation, product concept development, and filtering ideas from frontline employees to top management. Furthermore, middle managers, who operate between the organizational core and the executive apex, play a critical role within the hierarchy. They range from first-line to senior managerial positions, typically positioned just below top leadership in both large and small organizations75,76.

According to the Egyptian Ministry of Tourism and Antiquities, 1666 category-A travel agencies and 30 five-star hotels were registered in the Greater Cairo region in 2022. A non-probability sampling approach was adopted, whereby participants were selected based on their relevance and alignment with the study’s target population. Data were collected from volunteers in Greater Cairo using convenience sampling due to budget constraints and the wide geographical distribution of tourism establishments. This non-probability sampling technique is commonly used in hospitality and tourism studies where access to sampling frame is difficult77. It allows researchers to gather relevant data quickly and cost-effectively from readily available participants78. The questionnaire was completed after obtaining the participants’ oral consent to take part in the study. A total of 404 usable questionnaires were gathered from 50 category-A travel agencies and 20 five-star hotels, with 136 responses (33.7%) from travel agencies and 268 responses (66.3%) from hotels.

All procedures in this study adhered to ethical guidelines and regulations governing research involving human participants. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of Helsinki, confirming compliance with ethical standards related to confidentiality, informed consent, and voluntary participation. Before data collection began, participants were informed about the study’s purpose, the nature of their involvement, and their right to withdraw at any time without consequences. Oral informed consent was obtained from each participant prior to questionnaire distribution in accordance with local cultural and institutional norms. No personally identifiable information was collected, ensuring anonymity and confidentiality throughout the research process. Given that the study posed minimal risk and involved voluntary, non-invasive survey responses from adult professionals, the ethics committee approved the use of oral consent.

Questionnaire design and measurement

A quantitative research method was employed in this study through the use of a systematically structured survey instrument for data collection. The survey consisted of two sections: the first section gathered demographic information such as gender, age, and years of professional experience, while the second section measured the four study variables using a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree).

Ecocentric leadership was assessed using a six-item scale adapted from Biswas et al.7. Green organizational learning was measured using an eight-item scale adapted from Özgül and Zehir49. Digital green culture was measured through a six-item scale developed by Alabdali et al.10. Green work engagement was assessed using a five-item scale adapted from Aboramadan58. Finally, environmental performance was measured using a four-item scale adapted from Iqbal et al.79. The full items are in the attached link https://shorturl.at/zvTOY

Data analysis

WarpPLS software version 7.0 was used in this study to evaluate the proposed model through the PLS-SEM approach, as it is considered one of the most suitable tools for analyzing complex research frameworks80. The PLS-SEM analysis was also conducted to assess the variations in path coefficients among the variables under investigation.

Respondents under the age of 30 represent 19.6%, while individuals aged 40 to 49 account for 13.1%, and a mere 3.5% are 50 years or older, indicating a limited presence of older managers, likely attributable to career advancement to higher organizational tiers or prevailing retirement trends.

Collectively, these findings furnish compelling empirical support for the adequacy of the measurement model and warrant the transition to the structural model analysis.

Direct, mediation, and sequential mediation effects

The structural model examines the direct, indirect, and sequential relationships among Ecocentric Leadership (EL), Green Organizational Learning (GOL), Digital Green Culture (DGC), Green Work Engagement (GWE), and Environmental Performance (EP). As shown in Fig. 2, the results provide empirical support for the majority of the proposed hypotheses, with significant path coefficients, explanatory power (R2), and bootstrapped confidence intervals (95% BCLB and BCUB).

Final model of the study.

Regarding the direct effects, Ecocentric Leadership has a significant positive influence on Digital Green Culture (β = 0.24, p < 0.01), indicating that environmentally oriented leadership fosters a digital environment supportive of sustainability practices (H1). EL also exerts a strong positive effect on Green Organizational Learning (β = 0.76, p < 0.01), supporting H2 and suggesting that ecocentric leaders actively promote organizational learning processes oriented toward environmental values. In addition, Green Organizational Learning significantly enhances Digital Green Culture (β = 0.49, p < 0.01), supporting H3 and confirming that learning-oriented organizations are more capable of embedding sustainability into digital practices.

With respect to mediation, Green Organizational Learning partially mediates the relationship between Ecocentric Leadership and Digital Green Culture (H4), as evidenced by both the significant direct effect and the indirect pathway through GOL. Furthermore, Ecocentric Leadership has a significant direct effect on Environmental Performance (β = 0.24, p < 0.01), supporting H5 and indicating that leadership contributes directly to sustainability outcomes beyond its indirect pathways.

Additionally, Digital Green Culture has a strong positive effect on Green Work Engagement (β = 0.81, p < 0.01), supporting H6 and demonstrating that a digitally enabled green environment enhances employees’ sustainability-related engagement. Green Work Engagement, in turn, significantly influences Environmental Performance (β = 0.40, p < 0.01), supporting H7 and highlighting the role of employee engagement in achieving environmental outcomes.

Finally, the results confirm the sequential mediation effect proposed in H8. Ecocentric Leadership influences Environmental Performance indirectly through a chain mechanism involving Digital Green Culture and Green Work Engagement. This is supported by statistically significant paths and bootstrapped confidence intervals, confirming the robustness of the serial mediation model. The R2 values further demonstrate the explanatory power of the model, with 57% of variance explained in Green Organizational Learning, 47% in Digital Green Culture, 66% in Green Work Engagement, and 35% in Environmental Performance. Overall, the findings illustrate that Ecocentric Leadership drives environmental performance through a multi-level process involving organizational learning, digital culture, and employee engagement.

Discussion

This research examines the impact of ecocentric leadership on environmental performance in the Egyptian hospitality industry, with green organizational learning, digital green culture, and green work engagement serving as mediating variables. Guided by Social Exchange Theory (SET) and the Resource-Based View (RBV), the study clarifies how internal organizational capabilities and social exchange dynamics influence environmental outcomes. The following discussion addresses each of the eight proposed hypotheses.

The findings show that ecocentric leadership has a significant effect on digital green culture (H1). In line with the RBV, digital green culture emerges as a valuable and non-imitable organizational resource that strengthens the environmental competitiveness of the firm. Ecocentric leaders guide the use of digital technologies according to sustainability principles, thereby fostering environmentally responsible digital practices. These results align with studies by Miao and Nduneseokwu4 and Chen and Sriphon39, which indicate that leaders who prioritize ecological values and support digital innovation cultivate trust and shared responsibility among employees, encouraging them to internalize and uphold digital green norms. By emphasizing sustainable digital initiatives, such as energy-efficient technologies and paperless systems, ecocentric leaders help establish a durable, environmentally supportive organizational culture. This is particularly relevant within the hospitality sector, where digital transformation is increasingly essential.

The results confirm the positive influence of ecocentric leadership on green organizational learning (H2). This finding is consistent with Nieto et al.42 and Hasan et al.8, who show that ecocentric leaders foster inquiry, critical reflection, and collaborative learning around sustainability issues. In line with SET, employees interpret such leadership behaviors as a demonstration of shared values and mutual commitment, which encourages their participation in learning activities aligned with environmental goals. In the hospitality industry, where rapid responsiveness to sustainability expectations is essential, ecocentric leaders play a key role in embedding green learning within organizational culture.

The findings also affirm that green organizational learning is a major driver of digital green culture (H3), supporting the work of Baquero44 and Alkhozaim et al.25. Through the lens of the RBV, organizational learning develops a strong internal knowledge base that enables the adoption of environmentally responsible digital practices, positioning learning as a valuable cultural resource. Ongoing training initiatives provide employees with the awareness and expertise needed to integrate green values into digital systems and everyday work behaviors. From the SET perspective, when employees perceive recognition and support for their learning efforts, they reciprocate by actively engaging in digital innovation and strengthening green cultural norms. This relationship is particularly relevant in the hospitality industry, where digital transformation and sustainability performance increasingly intersect.

The mediation analysis further indicates that green organizational learning is a central mechanism through which ecocentric leadership influences digital green culture (H4). Leadership alone may be insufficient to shift cultural norms unless supported by learning processes that reinforce and disseminate ecological values. Consistent with SET, leaders who prioritize learning signal a commitment to employee development, motivating employees to adopt eco-friendly digital practices. Within the hospitality sector, this mediating pathway highlights how ecocentric leadership drives cultural transformation by cultivating learning-driven environments that support sustainable digital behaviors.

The results confirm that ecocentric leadership positively influences environmental performance (H5), supporting the findings of Kim et al.57 and Bahgat et al. (2021). According to SET, employees tend to reciprocate the ecological values demonstrated by their leaders by adopting environmentally responsible behaviors. Leaders who emphasize environmental responsibility help establish a workplace culture grounded in shared sustainability goals. In the hospitality sector, where day-to-day operations generate substantial environmental impact, environmentally conscious leadership translates directly into more sustainable operational practices.

The findings also empirically validate the relationship proposed in H6, showing that a strong digital green culture enhances green work engagement. This aligns with the research of Li and Lin60. From the perspective of SET, when employees perceive that organizational norms actively support environmental behavior, they are more likely to reciprocate through sustained engagement in green practices. In the hospitality industry, where employees directly interact with guests and operational processes, their level of engagement becomes a key driver of sustainability performance.

The results further show that green work engagement has a positive effect on environmental performance (H7). According to the RBV, engaged employees represent a valuable and unique organizational resource that contributes to environmental efficiency and innovation66. Their proactive behaviors often result in cost savings and improved organizational reputation. SET also explains that employees who feel valued and aligned with their organization’s environmental aspirations tend to go beyond formal job requirements by initiating or supporting activities such as recycling, waste reduction, and energy conservation. In the hospitality sector, where operational activities significantly affect environmental outcomes, engaged employees play a central role in implementing and maintaining sustainable practices.

H8 examined the full sequential pathway and was supported. The findings confirm that ecocentric leadership fosters a digital green culture, which subsequently strengthens green work engagement, ultimately leading to improved environmental performance. This aligns with the RBV, as leadership, culture, and engagement collectively represent a system of interconnected resources that competitors would find difficult to imitate. From the perspective of SET, this sequential pathway reflects a chain of reciprocal influences: leaders instill environmental values, employees internalize these values through cultural norms, and subsequently engage in purposeful behavior. In the hospitality sector, this step-by-step process illustrates how top-down leadership can diffuse throughout the organization to generate tangible environmental outcomes.

Theoretical implications

The findings of this study contribute significantly to the theoretical development of both the RBV and SET, particularly within the context of sustainability in tourism and hospitality businesses. While the RBV traditionally emphasizes tangible and intangible assets that generate competitive advantage2, this research extends the theory by showing that ecocentric leadership acts as a strategic catalyst for building valuable internal capabilities—specifically digital green culture, green organizational learning, and green work engagement82. These capabilities function as embedded organizational resources that are rare, difficult to imitate, and environmentally consequential83. By linking ecocentric leadership to environmental performance through these mediating constructs, the study reinforces the RBV’s emphasis on internal capability development and demonstrates how sustainability-oriented leadership influences outcomes by shaping cultural and behavioral dimensions.

The study also enriches SET by applying it to leadership processes grounded in environmental values. The findings illustrate how ecocentric leaders, through consistent ecological commitment and value-driven behavior, cultivate trust and a psychological contract with employees. This relational exchange motivates employees to reciprocate through greater engagement in green practices and stronger alignment with organizational environmental objectives69. Moreover, the inclusion of digital green culture as a contextual factor broadens the application of SET by showing that employees respond not only to leadership behavior but also to the sustainability-oriented systems and practices embedded within the organization84. The perceived integrity of these systems further reinforces reciprocal commitment.

In addition, the mediating role of green organizational learning between ecocentric leadership and digital green culture expands both theoretical frameworks by introducing a process-based mechanism that links leadership to cultural transformation. The RBV is strengthened by positioning organizational learning as a core capability that enables the integration of ecological principles into digital domains, rather than viewing it as a peripheral support function. SET is deepened by demonstrating that learning-oriented environments signal investment in employees’ development, which elicits reciprocal behavior and enhances their commitment to sustainability2.

Finally, the sequential pathway from ecocentric leadership to environmental performance—mediated by digital green culture and green work engagement—provides a multi-level framework that reinforces both theoretical perspectives. It demonstrates that performance outcomes emerge not from isolated leadership actions but through interconnected organizational processes involving culture, learning, and employee engagement85. In doing so, the study offers a comprehensive and sector-specific contribution that connects leadership, strategy, and sustainability through the dual lens of RBV and SET, and aligns these theoretical insights with the operational realities of the tourism and hospitality industry.

Practical implications

This study offers important implications for hospitality managers, policymakers, and sustainability practitioners in developing economies such as Egypt. Drawing on the empirical findings, particularly the significant direct, mediating, and sequential relationships identified in the model, the results demonstrate that ecocentric leadership enhances environmental performance both directly and indirectly through digital green culture and green work engagement. More importantly, the validated sequential pathway linking leadership, digital green culture, employee engagement, and environmental performance highlights an integrated mechanism through which sustainability outcomes can be achieved.

First, hospitality firms should institutionalize ecocentric leadership, given its strong influence on green organizational learning and its direct and indirect effects on both digital green culture and environmental performance. This can be achieved by providing regular training on digital literacy, environmental ethics, and sustainability-driven decision-making. Embedding ecological values into leadership KPIs, performance appraisals, and leadership development programs can help reshape the normative basis of managerial behavior. Since the findings confirm that leadership drives both learning and culture formation, organizations should position leaders as central agents of sustainability transformation rather than symbolic supporters. In addition, promoting a strong digital green culture requires investment in eco-friendly digital systems such as e-auditing tools, smart energy monitoring technologies, and digital sustainability dashboards, while reinforcing environmental norms through communication campaigns and reward mechanisms. This is particularly critical given the strong effect of digital green culture on green work engagement, which emerged as the most influential relationship in the model. Establishing cross-functional green teams on digital platforms—for example, Trello or Slack—can also support peer learning and the sharing of green best practices.

Second, enhancing green work engagement necessitates aligning HRM practices with environmental goals, as the findings indicate that green work engagement plays a key role in improving environmental performance. This may include implementing green performance incentives, integrating sustainability expectations into job descriptions, and offering targeted eco-skills development programs. Employee engagement can also be strengthened through initiatives such as gamified environmental challenges or partnerships with local environmental NGOs, which build emotional commitment and active participation in sustainability initiatives. These practices are essential, as the findings confirm that employee engagement represents the direct behavioral pathway through which environmental performance is realized.

Third, green organizational learning should be systematically embedded into corporate learning structures, given its significant role in shaping digital green culture and mediating the relationship between ecocentric leadership and organizational culture. Organizations can achieve this by creating knowledge-sharing workshops, developing green knowledge repositories, encouraging experimentation with sustainable practices, and utilizing digital systems to monitor and assess ecological learning outcomes. Linking learning results to performance evaluations and innovation processes can further reinforce the adoption of sustainability-oriented behaviors. This highlights that learning mechanisms act as a critical transmission channel through which leadership influences organizational culture.

Finally, public–private partnerships and national tourism authorities can accelerate digital green transformation by offering subsidies for sustainability-oriented digital tools, promoting ecolabel certification, and incorporating sustainability criteria into licensing requirements for hotels and travel agencies. Such policy-level interventions are particularly important in light of the study’s evidence that sustainability outcomes emerge from an interconnected system of leadership, learning, culture, and employee engagement, rather than isolated initiatives. These interventions can create an enabling environment that supports organizational transitions toward sustainability.

Limitations and future research

Despite the valuable contributions of this study to sustainable leadership and organizational behavior in the tourism and hospitality sectors, several limitations should be acknowledged, each offering important opportunities for future research. First, the study’s sample consisted exclusively of employees from travel agencies and hotels in Egypt. While this provides meaningful insights within a developing-economy context, the results may reflect cultural, regulatory, or economic characteristics unique to the Egyptian tourism industry. To strengthen external validity, future research should replicate the model in different countries or regions with varying environmental policies, digital infrastructures, and sustainability cultures. Cross-national comparative studies would yield a deeper understanding of how ecocentric leadership operates across diverse contexts.

A second limitation concerns the treatment of ecocentric leadership as a single, unified construct. This approach does not account for potential sub-dimensions that may differentially influence organizational learning, digital green culture, or environmental performance. Future research could explore the multidimensional structure of ecocentric leadership by developing detailed typologies or leadership profiles that clarify which specific attributes are most impactful in advancing sustainability outcomes. Moreover, while the study focuses on internal organizational mechanisms, it does not consider the influence of external stakeholders—such as environmentally conscious customers, governmental sustainability incentives, or international tour operators—on leadership effectiveness. In Egypt’s tourism sector, such external pressures may either reinforce or hinder sustainable practices. Future studies could incorporate perspectives from stakeholder theory or institutional theory to examine how these external drivers interact with ecocentric leadership, potentially moderating or mediating its impact on environmental performance.

Additionally, the study does not account for potential moderating variables that could shape the relationships under investigation. Factors such as organizational size, digital maturity, regulatory pressures, or national cultural characteristics may influence how leadership practices are translated into learning, cultural change, and performance outcomes. Future research could employ moderated mediation models to explore how these contextual variables strengthen or weaken the pathways identified in this study, providing a more comprehensive understanding of sustainability governance in hospitality organizations.
