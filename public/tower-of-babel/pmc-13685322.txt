Objective assessment of surgical performance in otolaryngology residency
Authors: Antoine E Melkane, Nadim Khoueir, Simon Rassi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeA surgeon's technical competence is a key predictor of surgical outcomes, yet residents' performance is often assessed by direct observation without structured, predefined criteria, which can be biased and unreliable. This study aimed to implement structured assessment tools across multiple procedures in an ENT residency, assess their feasibility, and evaluate their impact on resident performance.MethodsTwo procedures per residency year were selected. Seven validated tools were adopted from the literature and an eighth was newly developed; each comprised a Task-Specific Checklist (TSC) and a Global Rating Scale (GRS). Residents were assessed over two rounds, each by three physicians plus a self-evaluation. The primary outcomes were implementation of the eight grids and the feasibility of multi-evaluator, multi-procedure assessment in routine practice. The secondary outcome was impact on performance measured as the change in TSC and GRS scores between rounds and the faculty–self agreement.ResultsAll eight grids were implemented and digitized; assessment was feasible for six of eight procedures, as endoscopic sinus surgery and pediatric bronchoscopy yielded too few eligible cases. No significant between-round differences were found for individual procedures, though non-significant improvement trends were observed. Pooled self-assessments improved significantly over time. Self- and faculty assessments did not differ significantly, though limited statistical power precludes firm conclusions. Junior residents improved most, whereas senior residents showed a ceiling effect.ConclusionThis pilot study demonstrates the feasibility of structured, multi-evaluator surgical assessment in an ENT residency. Using multiple evaluators limits rater bias and will enable inter-rater reliability testing, not feasible at this stage. These preliminary results support a larger, multi-center study incorporating reliability and validity testing before broader implementation.Keywords: Medical education, Assessment tool, Evaluation grid, Surgical skills, Competency-based learning

Introduction

The technical competence of a surgeon is undoubtedly one of the most important predictors of a good surgical outcome. To avoid surgical errors, it is essential to measure surgical competence and use this process to provide the necessary feedback for corrective actions. Various learning and assessment methods have been used to measure and improve surgical skills. Traditional tools used in most ENT departments for more than twenty years, such as basic-sciences and in-training examinations which accurately assess clinical and therapeutic knowledge, as well as the semiannual evaluations conducted by faculty members providing mostly subjective feedback across several aspects of residency training, have not proven to be valid or reliable instruments for assessing surgical competence. A landmark analysis of bariatric surgeons in 2013 demonstrated that peer-rated technical skill was directly associated with lower rates of postoperative complications and mortality, providing objective evidence that intraoperative competence translates into measurable patient outcomes [1].

Competency-based education has become a widely adopted concept in the surgical field [2–4]. However, very few standardized assessment tools are available for the objective evaluation of surgical skills, particularly in Otolaryngology–Head and Neck Surgery [5]. Current surgical assessment relies on retrospective review of performance through simple direct observation, without predefined criteria or scoring systems, a process that is often unreliable and biased [6, 7].

Over the past two decades, surgical training has progressively shifted from a time-based apprenticeship model toward competency-based medical education (CBME), in which progression is anchored to the demonstrated acquisition of defined competencies rather than to time spent in training. This paradigm requires assessment tools that are objective, reproducible, and capable of generating actionable formative feedback. In otolaryngology, several complementary approaches have been explored to operationalize CBME. Simulation-based education, in particular, has gained prominence: structured otolaryngology boot camps for junior trainees have been shown to improve trainee confidence, immediate knowledge, and technical skill acquisition across a range of core procedures [8]. Such initiatives, however, predominantly assess skills in simulated or pre-clinical settings rather than during live operative performance. A persistent gap therefore remains in the objective, longitudinal assessment of residents’ intraoperative technical performance across the full breadth of procedures encountered during training, and in the practical implementation of such assessment within the day-to-day workflow of a residency program. The present work was conceived to address this gap.

Background

The concept of Objective Structured Assessment of Technical Skills (OSATS) was developed by the Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education (ACGME) in the 1990s to ensure an objective, competency-based method of surgical assessment [9–12]. Since its introduction, OSATS has been widely implemented in medical education. Assessment tools for a large variety of surgical procedures, whose feasibility, reliability, and validity have been well established in accredited residency programs particularly in the United States and Canada, are now available in the literature [13–16].

In ENT, validated assessment grids exist for myringotomy, tonsillectomy, septoplasty, pediatric bronchoscopy, and neck dissection [17–21]. An assessment tool for endoscopic sinus surgery and another for mastoidectomy have demonstrated feasibility, reliability, and validity in cadaver dissection programs, and experts deemed them appropriate for transfer to residency programs [22, 23]. The long-term goal of these tools is to support residents, maximize training opportunities especially when limited, and target specific tasks requiring performance improvement. This assessment approach aims to accelerate performance advancement and foster consolidation of newly acquired skills.

The objective of this work was to incorporate assessment tools for several surgical procedures into our ENT residency program, determine the feasibility of such an assessment across all residency levels by involving all departmental faculty members, and measure its impact on residents’ individual surgical performance in the operating room. While several assessment tools have already been validated in the literature, no model incorporating multiple standardized tools within a single residency program, with an in-depth evaluation of its impact on the academic curriculum, has yet been reported.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study was designed as a pilot feasibility study aimed at evaluating the implementation of structured surgical assessment tools within our ENT residency program. Given that our program recruits only one to two residents per postgraduate year, all residents enrolled during the 2023–2024 academic year, which corresponds to the period covered by our project, were invited to participate. Consequently, no formal a priori sample size calculation was performed. The sample size was determined by the total number of eligible residents available during the study period (n = 4), which is consistent with the exploratory nature of pilot studies intended to assess feasibility and generate preliminary data for larger future investigations.

Inclusion criteria consisted of enrollment in the ENT residency program at Hotel-Dieu de France University Hospital during the study period and participation in the selected surgical procedures undergoing evaluation. Residents from all postgraduate years (PGY2–PGY5) were eligible. Exclusion criteria included refusal to participate, inability to complete both rounds of evaluation, or incomplete assessment data. No residents met any exclusion criterion, and all eligible residents were included in the study.

Learning objectives are clearly defined for each year of training, including specific objectives related to surgical training in the operating room. Certain procedures must be covered and mastered within a given year, while others are introduced during a year and completed or reinforced the following year.

We chose to focus on two procedures per residency year, assigning each a corresponding evaluation grid, for a total of eight grids. The procedures were distributed as follows; myringotomy/tympanostomy tube placement and tonsillectomy for 2nd-year residents (PGY2), septoplasty and tracheostomy for 3rd-year residents (PGY3), mastoidectomy and functional endoscopic sinus surgery for 4th-year residents (PGY4), pediatric bronchoscopy and neck dissection for 5th-year residents (PGY5). The allocation of procedures to specific training years was not arbitrary but followed the progressive structure of our residency curriculum and the principle of increasing technical and decision-making complexity across the years. Procedures introduced earliest (myringotomy/tympanostomy tube placement and tonsillectomy) are high-frequency, foundational procedures that PGY2 residents are expected to master first and that establish core microsurgical and dissection skills. Mid-residency procedures (septoplasty, tracheostomy for PGY3; mastoidectomy and functional endoscopic sinus surgery for PGY4) demand greater anatomical knowledge, instrument handling, and management of intraoperative risk. The procedures assigned to senior residents (pediatric bronchoscopy and neck dissection for PGY5) are the most complex, requiring advanced judgment, multidisciplinary coordination, and the integration of skills acquired in earlier years. Each procedure was therefore matched to the training year in which our curriculum expects it to be learned and consolidated, ensuring that residents were assessed on surgeries appropriate to their stage of training.

Each resident underwent two evaluations at a three-month interval, and encompassing two different surgical procedures. Each evaluation was conducted by three independent evaluators who were all present in the operating room during the procedure, completing their evaluation grids simultaneously and individually, in addition to the resident’s self-evaluation. Evaluators were exclusively physicians affiliated with the ENT department of Hotel-Dieu de France University Hospital. Written informed consent was obtained from all the residents participating in this pilot study.

Outcome measures

Consistent with the three-part aim of the study, the primary outcomes were (i) the successful implementation of the eight structured assessment grids across all residency years, and (ii) the feasibility of conducting the assessment in routine clinical practice, operationalized as the ability to assemble three independent evaluators per case, to capture eligible cases for each procedure within the study period, and to transition the grids to a digital platform. The secondary outcome was the impact of the assessment on resident operative performance, measured as the change in mean TSC and GRS scores between the two evaluation rounds for each procedure, the change in pooled self-assessment scores over time, and the agreement between faculty and self-assessment at each round. Comparisons between junior and senior residents, and the exploration of ceiling effects, were pre-specified as exploratory analyses given the small sample size.

Selection of preexisting assessment tools

We identified in the literature validated and standardized evaluation grids for seven surgical procedures: myringotomy, tonsillectomy, septoplasty, mastoidectomy, endoscopic sinus surgery, neck dissection, and pediatric bronchoscopy [17–23]. The first six grids were adopted as published, and the last one was subjected to minor modifications to be adapted to our in-house protocol.

All grids were consistent with the OSATS model and followed the same structure, with a Task-Specific Checklist (TSC) dedicated to the technical steps specific to each procedure (Tables 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 and 7), and a Global Rating Scale (GRS) identical across the seven procedures, allowing global assessment of resident’s performance (Table 9). With the exception of Table 1 (myringotomy/tympanostomy tube insertion), whose items were scored as binary (yes/no) as in the originally published instrument, all checklist items of Tables 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8 and 9 were scored on a 5-point Likert scale, where 3 represented the minimum acceptable level of competence, 4 indicated competence, and 5 corresponded to expert-level performance.

Development of a new tracheostomy assessment tool (Table 8)

We developed a new evaluation grid for tracheostomy, following the same model as the existing evaluation grids and consistent with the OSATS format, including both TSC and GRS components. Creation and validation were carried out in collaboration with the entire ENT team: one pediatric otolaryngologist, one otologist, two rhinologists, one laryngologist, and one head and neck surgeon, two of whom are experts in medical education. The expert panel reviewed the content of both parts of the grid and ensured appropriate terminology. Final validation was obtained through a simplified two-round modified Delphi technique, establishing consensus among members. More specifically, the development process began with an initial drafting phase, in which an inventory of the key technical steps of tracheostomy was compiled from the surgical literature and from existing OSATS-based instruments, and arranged into a sequential checklist mirroring the structure of the seven published grids. In the first Delphi round, each expert independently rated the relevance and clarity of every proposed item and suggested additions, deletions, or rewording. Items reaching predefined consensus were retained, those rejected were removed, and contested items were revised. In the second round, the revised grid was recirculated for final rating, after which full consensus was achieved and the grid was finalized. This approach ensured inclusion of multiple surgical competencies -appropriate knowledge, judgment, and technical skills- within the tool. Our objective, similar to that of the authors of the seven other grids, was to generate evidence for a reliable tool capable of tracking residents’ surgical performance throughout their academic training, while remaining simple and easy for clinical faculty to implement.

All six experts were members of the same academic ENT department in which the study was conducted. This single-centre composition was a deliberate, practical choice for this pilot phase: it ensured that the panel was directly familiar with the institutional surgical protocol, the operating-room workflow, and the training structure into which the grid would be immediately implemented, thereby maximizing content relevance and feasibility of in-house deployment. We acknowledge that this approach limits the external validity of the new grid, and broader multi-center expert input is planned during the larger validation study to strengthen its generalizability.

Evaluation procedure

Before using this new instrument, a training session was held for faculty members to familiarize them with the different grids and standardize evaluation procedures. A first round of evaluations was launched in September 2023, using printed forms. Three grids were completed per procedure, with two procedures per resident, resulting in 24 completed grids for the four residents. Each evaluation included two attending physicians and the chief resident for the sake of availability. When the chief resident was being evaluated, a third attending was invited to the operating room. Residents were informed in advance of each scheduled evaluation and reviewed the corresponding grid beforehand. After each procedure, the resident completed a self-evaluation, resulting in eight self-evaluations for the first round.

A second round of evaluations was conducted in December 2023 to track changes in scores after a three-month interval. After the first round, the grids were uploaded to the institution server using the LimeSurvey platform, enabling digital completion for the second round and facilitating score calculations and statistical data collection.

Statistical analysis

Of the eight TSC grids, Table 1 (myringotomy/tympanostomy tube insertion) consisted of 30 binary items (yes/no), resulting in a maximum TSC score of 30. Tables 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 and 7 included between 8 and 24 surgical steps, each scored on a five-point Likert scale with three explicit descriptors (1: unable to perform, 3: performs with minimal prompting, 5: performs easily with good flow). Table 8 (tracheostomy), newly developed for this study, consisted of 14 items scored on the same five-point Likert scale, with a maximum TSC score of 70. Maximum TSC scores across grids ranged from 30 to 120, with higher scores reflecting better performance (Tables 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7 and 8).

The GRS grid was identical for all eight procedures, consisting of 12 criteria scored on the same five-point Likert scale, with a maximum score of 60 (Table 9). At the end of this grid, two subjective binary questions were added; one on formative feedback and one on independent performance capability, along with an open comment box. Since these came after the GRS total score, they were not included in the statistical analysis or further elaborated in the pilot study.

We carried out a prospective analytical study comparing two means from paired samples (first vs. second round). Because each sample size was < 30, we tested for normality of score differences. As differences were not normally distributed, a non-parametric Wilcoxon test was performed using the SPSS v.25 software. For faculty evaluations, each procedure was analyzed individually. For residents’ self-evaluations, scores for all residents were pooled into a single TSC and GRS score for the first round (TSC1, GRS1) and the second round (TSC2, GRS2). We also compared mean scores between faculty evaluations and residents’ self-evaluations. Since sample size was < 30 (n = 24), normality was tested for all four scores (TSC1, TSC2, GRS1, GRS2), which were normally distributed, allowing the use of an independent-samples Student t-test. Since GRS scores for all eight procedures shared the same denominator of 60, they were directly comparable. TSC scores had denominators ranging from 30 to 120 depending on the procedure; for uniformity and to allow cross-procedure comparisons, all TSC scores were standardized to a denominator of 100. For each procedure, mean TSC and GRS scores were calculated across the three independent evaluators per round, and these means served as the basis for all statistical comparisons. Inter-rater reliability (IRR) was not formally assessed in this pilot study, given the variable composition of evaluator panels across sessions, which precluded a consistent within-panel agreement analysis. IRR assessment is planned for the larger-scale study.

Results

Implementation of the assessment grids

All eight assessment grids were successfully established and deployed across the four training years (PGY2–PGY5), with two procedures assigned to each year. Seven grids were adapted from previously validated, published instruments, while the eighth (tracheostomy) was newly developed and validated through a two-round modified Delphi process. Following the first round of evaluations, all grids were transitioned to a digital format on the LimeSurvey platform for the second round, enabling electronic completion and automated score collection. For each surgical procedure, two sets of scores were collected, corresponding to the two evaluations performed at different time points. For each set, we obtained three TSC scores and three GRS scores from the three independent evaluators, in addition to the resident’s self-evaluation (TSC and GRS).

Feasibility of assessment

It is important to note that during the entire pilot study period, we were able to recruit only one case of pediatric bronchoscopy for foreign body inhalation; only a single evaluation was therefore obtained. As for endoscopic sinus surgery, although performed regularly in our department, only complicated cases or revision surgeries were encountered during the study period, none of which were deemed appropriate for a PGY4 resident, therefore no evaluations were possible despite extending the inclusion period until the end of February. Consequently, these two procedures were excluded from the statistical analysis.

Impact on resident performance

The different scores from the two rounds of evaluations for each of the eight surgical procedures are presented in Table 10. Two complementary comparisons were then performed: Table 11 presents the within-resident comparison of scores between the first and second evaluation rounds, whereas Table 12 presents the between-group comparison of faculty evaluations versus residents’ self-evaluations. We did not find any significant difference between the TSC and GRS scores of the first and second evaluations. This result was somewhat expected given the small sample size of this pilot study. However, non-significant trends toward improvement were observed for all procedures in both score categories, except for tracheostomy. When pooling self-evaluation scores, a significant improvement was noted in both TSC and GRS between the first and second rounds (p = 0.03 and p = 0.04 respectively) (Table 11). When comparing scores between faculty evaluations and residents’ self-evaluations, no significant differences were found for TSC1, GRS1, TSC2, or GRS2 (Table 12). It should be noted, however, that the absence of a statistically significant difference is not equivalent to demonstrated agreement between self- and faculty assessments; given the limited sample size, the study was underpowered to draw firm conclusions regarding calibration accuracy.

Discussion

Principal findings

In this pilot study, we successfully implemented eight OSATS-based assessment grids spanning all residency years and subsequently digitized them for routine use. Assessment proved feasible for six of the eight procedures, with endoscopic sinus surgery and pediatric bronchoscopy limited by eligible caseload. Regarding impact, faculty-rated scores showed non-significant upward trends for most procedures, pooled self-assessment improved significantly over time, and no significant difference was found between self- and faculty assessment, while junior residents improved most and senior residents showed a ceiling effect.

Comparison with existing evidence

When revisiting Miller’s Pyramid model, it becomes clear that the initial levels; knows, knows how, and shows how, are already well assessed in our academic system through written and oral examinations. However, the top of the pyramid; does, or the actual task performance, remains poorly evaluated. It is at this level that we sought to intervene by integrating evaluation grids for surgical competencies.

This approach of defining learning objectives and matching them to assessment tools has been used for decades in aviation, where performance is scored step by step and gaps addressed through individualized training. The surgical field has since adopted it, producing numerous assessment instruments.

In our pilot study, implementing eight surgical evaluation grids posed a major logistical challenge, making feasibility somewhat difficult. Indeed, each evaluation required assembling three surgeons in the operating room for 1 to 2 h. This insistence on multiple evaluators reflects the conclusions of Williams et al., who demonstrated that increasing the number of observations per resident significantly improves evaluation reliability [24]. To address logistical constraints, we mutually agreed that the third evaluator would always be the chief resident. When the chief resident was being evaluated, extra efforts were made to recruit a third attending surgeon.

The evaluation grids selected for this study had, for the most part, been validated and published in the literature (Tables 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 and 7) [17–23]. Only one grid had to be created, fully aligned with the others and validated by our department through a simplified two-round modified Delphi technique to ensure consensus (Table 8). The TSC part of each grid focuses on the technical steps specific to each surgical procedure, while the GRS allows a global performance assessment with an emphasis on the competencies defined by the CANMEDS framework: medical expertise, communication, collaboration, scholarship, professionalism, and leadership. A central goal was to assess whether this grid-based method could distinguish novice from expert operators and, above all, track the progression of a resident’s performance over training.

Although our statistical analysis did not reveal significant differences in TSC and GRS scores between the first and second evaluation rounds, which was expected due to the small sample size, we still observed non-significant improvement trends for all procedures except tracheostomy (Table 11). We attribute this exception to the fact that the first tracheostomy was performed on a patient with an untreated neck, while the second was performed on a previously irradiated patient with a pharyngeal primary tumor, making the dissection considerably more difficult. We wish to emphasize that these trends should not be over-interpreted. None of the between-round differences in faculty scores reached statistical significance, septoplasty was the only procedure showing a clearly visible upward shift, and tracheostomy scores in fact declined for the reasons noted above. The data therefore do not demonstrate a robust, generalized improvement in operative performance, and the present study should be read as evidence of feasibility rather than of measurable skill gain. This pattern also illustrates the confounding effect of case complexity on raw score comparisons, reinforcing the need for complexity adjustment in future, adequately powered analyses.

On the other hand, the significant improvement observed in residents’ self-evaluations after a three-months-interval highlights the fact that they felt more confident performing the same procedure over time (Table 11). When comparing residents’ self-evaluations to faculty evaluations, no significant differences were found across all four scores (Table 12). While this finding may be consistent with developing metacognitive awareness — the ability to accurately self-monitor one’s own surgical performance — caution is warranted in interpreting this result. A non-significant difference does not constitute evidence of agreement or calibration accuracy; it may simply reflect insufficient statistical power given the small sample size. Formal evaluation of self-assessment accuracy will be necessary in larger studies to evaluate whether residents demonstrate genuine metacognitive calibration.

We observed a notable discrepancy in scores, especially among junior residents, between studies from North American departments and the present study in our department. In reality, training in our department is more concentrated in the second half of residency, meaning that senior residents (PGY4-PGY5) often continue to perform procedures usually assigned to junior residents (PGY2-PGY3), due to lower surgical volumes compared to abroad. However, scores converged among senior residents. Importantly, this discrepancy did not hinder the study, as its purpose was formative rather than summative. Additionally, the study allowed us to better focus on the early residency years and address long-standing gaps in surgical training. Of note, surgical training in our program is conducted predominantly at a single institution, with residents not routinely rotating to external units. It is therefore plausible that residents acquire the bulk of the operative experience available at this site before reaching the senior level, which may partly explain the apparent performance plateau observed in senior residents. Extending the assessment to a different institution would be a valuable exercise to test this hypothesis, although it would present logistical challenges.

A consistent finding in referenced studies is that substantial improvement is noted in junior residents, whereas a ceiling effect is observed in senior residents [17]. It is therefore possible that several TSC or GRS items did not show statistical differences between residents and attending surgeons because of this ceiling effect. We observed a similar tendency in our small cohort, though without statistically significant results.

Including three evaluators per assessment had several implications. First, it may help reduce the halo effect or horn effect typically observed when a single evaluator, familiar with the resident, forms biased opinions influenced by non-medical factors (attire, glasses, hairstyle, background, etc.) [18]. However, it may also trigger the Hawthorne effect, where subjects aware of being evaluated show increased motivation [17]. Finally, having multiple evaluators enables calculation of IRR, once the larger-scale study is launched. It quantifies the agreement among evaluators observing the same performance and is a key indicator of grid reliability; in the referenced studies it was consistently demonstrated, supporting the validity of these tools [18, 20–23]. We acknowledge, nonetheless, that the absence of IRR data is an important limitation of the present pilot study rather than a minor omission: without it, observed within- and between-resident score differences cannot be confidently attributed to true changes in technical competence rather than to differences between raters. We emphasize that, although our overall cohort was small, IRR is fundamentally an item-level property and is therefore not inherently constrained by the number of residents assessed; the GRS, being identical across all procedures, would in principle provide a substantial pool of items for such analysis. In our specific protocol, formal computation was nevertheless precluded by the inconsistent composition of the three-evaluator panels across sessions. In the planned larger study, evaluator panels will be kept consistent wherever possible, and IRR of both the shared GRS items and the procedure-specific TSC items will be reported, since validity and reliability -rather than feasibility alone- are the properties that ultimately need to be established.

Our evaluation tool was administered immediately after surgery to minimize recall bias and allow the supervising surgeon and evaluators to provide structured real-time feedback to residents.

Future directions

Digitizing the evaluation grids during the second round undoubtedly facilitated the assessment process, score calculation, and statistical data collection. It also supports a broader plan to integrate the grids into routine training, with regular (biannual or quarterly) evaluations scheduled per residency year and delivered to faculty through direct links. The most resource-intensive aspect of the method was the need to assemble three evaluators in the operating room for each assessment, which proved time-consuming and would represent the principal barrier to scaling the model to larger, higher-volume services such as orthopaedic or general surgery. Several strategies could mitigate this burden, including reducing the number of evaluators once IRR has been established, integrating assessments into routine operative supervision rather than scheduling dedicated sessions, and leveraging technology. In particular, the use of intraoperative video recording combined with artificial-intelligence-assisted motion and step analysis could, in the future, partially or wholly substitute for the physical presence of multiple evaluators, enabling asynchronous, blinded, and potentially more objective scoring. Such automation would substantially improve scalability while simultaneously addressing the non-blinded nature of the current approach.

We initially decided that the evaluation would be purely formative, but in the future, once the process becomes standardized, it could become summative and part of the requirements for obtaining specialty certification.

Limitations and strengths

Despite the potential benefits of this evaluation method, one major limitation is its non-blinded nature, which may lead to evaluator bias when faculty members assess residents they know. To mitigate this observer bias, regular faculty training sessions should be organized to ensure proper use of the instrument. For future research, incorporating blinded video-based evaluations would also help eliminate this type of faculty-related bias.

Another important limitation is the small sample size, which reflects the pilot nature of the study and the limited number of residents enrolled in a single training program. Consequently, the study was not powered to detect small differences in performance scores, and the findings should be interpreted primarily as evidence of feasibility rather than effectiveness. A further limitation is the potential for confounding between resident identity, procedure difficulty, and assessment modality (paper vs. digital). Because each resident was assigned to two specific procedures corresponding to their training year, and only one resident was evaluated per PGY level, it is not possible to disentangle whether observed score differences reflect individual progression, inherent procedural difficulty, or an effect of the digital platform introduced in the second round. This confounding is inherent to the single-center, single-cohort design and will be addressed through randomization and larger sample sizes in future work. Additionally, the single-center design limits the generalizability of these findings to other residency programs or healthcare systems with different training structures, patient volumes, or faculty compositions. The development of the tracheostomy grid (Table 8) involved faculty exclusively from one institution, which may limit the external validity of that instrument; broader, multi-center expert input will be sought in future validation efforts. Finally, IRR was not formally assessed in this pilot study, representing a methodological gap that must be addressed in the planned larger-scale study to confirm the validity and reproducibility of these assessment instruments.

Notwithstanding these limitations, the study has several strengths. To our knowledge, it is among the first to implement a coordinated set of eight OSATS-based instruments covering every residency year within a single program, rather than validating a single tool in isolation. The use of three independent evaluators per case reduced individual rater bias and halo effects, immediate post-procedure scoring minimized recall bias, and the standardization and subsequent digitization of the grids provide a practical, reproducible template that other programs could readily adopt. The consecutive, inclusive recruitment of all eligible residents, complete follow-up with no attrition, and the transparent acknowledgment of confounders further strengthen the internal coherence of this feasibility work.

Conclusion

The principal contribution of this work is to show that a coordinated set of eight OSATS-based instruments, spanning all residency years, can be deployed within a single training program and digitized for routine use, despite the logistical demands of multi-evaluator assessment. We were able to demonstrate the feasibility of this project in our ENT department and are now ready to move toward full implementation of this operative evaluation method. This initiative could also, in a second phase, be extended to other surgical departments.

Finally, we were able to show a trend toward improved performance based on the physicians’ evaluations, and a significant improvement when relying on the residents’ self-evaluations. These observations should, however, be interpreted with caution: given the small sample size and the absence of IRR testing, the present findings establish feasibility and acceptability rather than effectiveness or validity. The practical implications of this work are nonetheless concrete. For educators, it provides a ready-to-use, digitized model for embedding structured, competency-based operative assessment across all residency years, with the potential to deliver targeted, real-time formative feedback and to identify specific technical gaps early in training. For programs, it offers a template that could, once reliability is confirmed, evolve from a formative tool into a summative component of certification. The necessary next steps are a larger, multi-center, longitudinal study with formal inter-rater and self-assessment reliability analyses, reporting of confidence intervals, adjustment for case complexity and supervision level, and exploration of blinded, video- and technology-assisted scoring to reduce evaluator burden and observer bias. Such work would move this approach from demonstrated feasibility toward validated, scalable, and ultimately summative competency-based surgical assessment.
