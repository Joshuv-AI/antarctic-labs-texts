Weak Partial Representations
Felipe Castro, Glauber Quadros, Thaísa Tamusiunas

Abstract
. We introduce the notion of partial representation of a weak Hopf algebra. We present the universal algebra H p ​ a ​ r w {H_{{}_{par}}^{w}} , which factorizes these partial representations by algebra morphisms. Also, it is shown that H p ​ a ​ r w {H_{{}_{par}}^{w}} is isomorphic to a partial smash product, that it has the structure of a Hopf algebroid and also that it can be endowed with a quantum inverse semigroup structure. Moreover, it is shown that the algebra objects in the module category over H p ​ a ​ r w {H_{{}_{par}}^{w}} correspond to symmetrical partial module algebras.

1.
Introduction

Dokuchaev, Exel and Piccione analyzed the behavior of partial representations of groups in
[
12
]
. Among others results, they established a correspondence between partial representations of the group
G
and representations of the groupoid algebra
K_{par}G
, which is defined over a groupoid
\Gamma(G)
constructed from the group
G
. This can be viewed as a partial-to-global result, as it describes a partial representation in terms of a global representation. An important aspect about the algebra
K_{par}G
is that it is generated by an indutive groupoid, which is a type of ordered groupoid. However, this specific groupoid has a significant characteristic: it is associated with an inverse semigroup via the classical Ehresmann-Schein-Nambooripad Theorem (ESN Theorem)
[
11
, Theorem 4.1.8]
. Furthermore, by the definition of the functors in the ESN Theorem, the image

\Gamma(G)
is more than just an inverse semigroup; it is an inverse monoid.

These results naturally raise the question: does the same apply to structures that extend groups? One possible way to extend groups is through groupoids, where not all elements are composable with each other and there are multiple identity elements instead of a single one. Indeed, a groupoid is a small category in which all morphisms are invertible. A theory for partial representations of groupoids was developed in
[
9
]
and
[
10
]
, showing that each partial representation of a groupoid corresponds to a unique global representation of a groupoid algebra. The algebra associated with this correspondence is generated by the Birget-Rhodes expansion of the groupoid
[
10
, Theorem 3.3]
, which is a type of ordered groupoid
[
8
, Proposition 3.1]
. More specifically, it is a locally complete inductive groupoid
[
9
, Remark 2.10]
, which has the additional characteristic of being associated with an inverse category, via a generalization of the ESN Theorem
[
7
, Theorem 2.17]
. When the groupoid is, in particular, a group, this inverse category coincides with the inverse monoid that appears in the group case.

Another possible generalization of groups is via a Hopf algebra, which can be interpreted as a generalization of groups by linearity. In
[
2
]
, Alves, Batista and Vercruysse studied partial representations for Hopf algebras. Given a Hopf algebra
H
, they introduced a universal algebra
H_{par}
, which has the universal property of factorizing a partial representation of
H
by an algebra morphism. Also, among other properties, they showed its intrinsic relation with a partial action of
H
. The algebra
H_{par}
presented in
[
2
]
was shown to be a Hopf algebroid, and it establishes a one-to-one correspondence between partial representations of Hopf algebras and representations of the constructed Hopf algebroid. This result also generalizes the correspondence for groups presented in
[
12
]
.

The reason for citing the ESN Theorem is that in
[
1
]
, a significant step was made in the direction to understand how this theorem could work in a Hopf context. In this reference, it was introduced the concept of quantum inverse semigroups, which is an algebraic structure that generalizes inverse semigroups by linearity. Examples of quantum inverse semigroups include Hopf algebras, weak Hopf algebras, inverse semigroup algebras and inverse semigroupoid algebras (in particular, inverse monoid algebras and inverse category algebras). Also, it was proved that the constructed Hopf algebroid
H_{par}
of
[
2
]
admits a quantum inverse semigroup, provided that the Hopf algebra is cocommutative.

There is yet another generalization of groups, which is the focus of our work: weak Hopf algebras. Partial representations of weak Hopf algebras unify the theories of partial representations for both groupoids and Hopf algebras, as each weak Hopf algebra
H
can be viewed as a generalization of a groupoid (by linearity) as well as of a Hopf algebra. Regarding weak Hopf algebras, a similar construction for partial representations is also possible. It was already expected, and to the theory be consistent, our initial hypothesis was that it should exists a universal algebra
H_{par}^{w}
with the universal property of factorizing a partial representation of
H
by an algebra morphism and this algebra should also possess a Hopf algebroid structure in the weak case. This hypothesis holds true, as we will demonstrate throughout the paper. Naturally, another question arises: is it possible to endow the universal algebra
{H_{{}_{par}}^{w}}
with a quantum inverse semigroup structure? The answer is yes, provided that the weak Hopf algebra is cocommutative, and this result is shown in
7.2
.

In summary, in a Hopf context, partial representations of Hopf algebras are equivalent to representations of a constructed Hopf algebroid
[
2
, Theorem 4.2 and Theorem 4.10]
; and in the weak case, partial representations of weak Hopf algebras are equivalent to representations of another constructed Hopf algebroid. Both types of Hopf algebroids (standard and weak), under the assumption of cocommutativity of
H
, admit a quantum inverse semigroup structure. So far, the theory remains well-behaved.

Therefore, our main purpose in this paper is to introduce the concept of partial representation of a weak Hopf algebra and some of its consequences. This notion unifies the partial representations of Hopf algebras discussed in
[
2
]
and
[
1
]
, as well as the partial representations of groupoids presented in
[
9
]
and
[
10
]
. The paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we introduce some preliminary results and terminology. In Section 3, we define partial representations of a weak Hopf algebra in an algebra, provide examples, and discuss their properties. In Section 4, we present the universal algebra
{H_{{}_{par}}^{w}}
, which factorizes any partial representation of a weak Hopf algebra by an unique algebra morphism. We also show that
{H_{{}_{par}}^{w}}
is isomorphic to a partial smash product. In Section 5, we prove that
{H_{{}_{par}}^{w}}
has a structure of a Hopf algebroid. In Section 6, we define partial modules over weak Hopf algebras, demonstrate that the category of partial modules is isomorphic to the category of partial representations, and show that a symmetric partial module algebra corresponds to an algebra object in the module category over the associated universal Hopf algebroid. Finally, in Section 7, we prove that
{H_{{}_{par}}^{w}}
admits a quantum inverse semigroup structure.

For readers who are less familiar with weak Hopf algebras, we refer them to
[
5
]
and
[
6
]
for additional information.

Throughout, unless otherwise stated, all algebras are modules over a field
\Bbbk
and unadorned
\otimes
means
\otimes_{\Bbbk}
. Rings and algebras are associative and unital. For all the paper we will adopt the Sweedler notation (summation understood).

3.
Partial Representation Theory

We start this section by introducing the notion of partial representation of a weak Hopf algebra in an algebra.

Definition 3.1
.

A partial representation of a weak Hopf algebra
H
in an algebra
A
, or simply a weak partial representation, is a
\Bbbk
-linear map
\pi:H\to A
such that the following statements hold:

(PR1)

\pi(1_{H})=1_{A}
;

(PR2)

\pi(h)\pi(k_{1})\pi(S(k_{2}))=\pi(hk_{1})\pi(S(k_{2}))
;

(PR3)

\pi(h)\pi(S(k_{1}))\pi(k_{2})=\pi(hS(k_{1}))\pi(k_{2})
;

(PR4)

\pi(h_{1})\pi(S(h_{2}))\pi(k)=\pi(h_{1})\pi(S(h_{2})k)
;

(PR5)

\pi(S(h_{1}))\pi(h_{2})\pi(k)=\pi(S(h_{1}))\pi(h_{2}k)
;

(PR6)

\pi(h)=\pi(h_{1})\pi(S(h_{2}))\pi(h_{3})
.

Observe that the definition of partial representation of Hopf algebras does not require the condition (
PR6
), because it is a consequence from the others, as it can be seen in
[
2
]
*Proposition 3.3. In the weak case we have following equivalences.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
\pi:H\to A
a linear map and suppose that it satisfies the conditions (
PR1
-
PR5
). Then the following assertions are equivalent:

(1)

\pi(h)=\pi(h_{1})\pi(S(h_{2}))\pi(h_{3})
;

(2)

\pi(1_{1})\pi(S(1_{2}))=1_{A}
;

(3)

\pi(S(1_{1}))\pi(1_{2})=1_{A}
;

(4)

\pi(S(h))=\pi(S(h_{1}))\pi(h_{2})\pi(S(h_{3}))
.

Moreover, if
H
has invertible antipode, then we have 3 more conditions to add to the above ones:

(5)

\pi(h)=\pi(h_{3})\pi(S^{-1}(h_{2}))\pi(h_{1})
;

(6)

\pi(S^{-1}(1_{2}))\pi(1_{1})=1_{A}
;

(7)

\pi(1_{2})\pi(S^{-1}(1_{1}))=1_{A}
.

Proof.

Since
\pi
satisfies (
PR1
-
PR5
),

\displaystyle\pi(h_{1})\pi(S(h_{2}))\pi(h_{3})

\displaystyle=\pi(h_{1})\pi(S(h_{2})h_{3})

\displaystyle=\pi(h1_{1})\pi(S(1_{2}))

\displaystyle=\pi(h)\pi(1_{1})\pi(S(1_{2}))

and

\displaystyle\pi(h_{1})\pi(S(h_{2}))\pi(h_{3})

\displaystyle=\pi(h_{1}S(h_{2}))\pi(h_{3})

\displaystyle=\pi(S(1_{1}))\pi(1_{2}h)

\displaystyle=\pi{(S(1_{1}))}\pi(1_{2})\pi(h).

Moreover, we also have

\displaystyle\pi(S(h_{1}))\pi(h_{2})\pi(S(h_{3}))

\displaystyle=\pi(S(h_{1}))\pi(h_{2}S(h_{3}))

\displaystyle=\pi(S(1_{1}h))\pi(1_{2})

\displaystyle=\pi(S(h)S(1_{1}))\pi(1_{2})

\displaystyle=\pi(S(h))\pi(S(1_{1}))\pi(1_{2}).

and

\displaystyle\pi(S(h_{1}))\pi(h_{2})\pi(S(h_{3}))

\displaystyle=\pi(S(h_{1})h_{2})\pi(S(h_{3}))

\displaystyle=\pi(1_{1})\pi(S(h1_{2}))

\displaystyle=\pi(1_{1})\pi(S(1_{2})S(h))

\displaystyle=\pi(1_{1})\pi(S(1_{2}))\pi(S(h)).

Applying (

∎

.

Let
\pi:H\to A

\pi(h_{1})\pi(S(h_{2}))=\pi(h_{1}S(h_{2}))=\pi(\varepsilon_{t}(h)),~\forall h\in H

(2)

\pi(S(h_{1}))\pi(h_{2})=\pi(S(h_{1})h_{2})=\pi(\varepsilon_{s}(h)),~\forall h\in H

Suppose that (

Supposing now that (

there is a unique algebra morphism

is an algebra morphism such that

, where

Thus we have that

is the unique linear map such that

\displaystyle=\hat{\pi}([x^{1}]\cdots[x^{n}])

\displaystyle=\hat{\pi}(P(x^{1})\cdots P(x^{n}))

\displaystyle=\hat{\pi}\circ P(x^{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes x^{n})

\displaystyle=\pi^{\prime}(x^{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes x^{n})

\displaystyle=\pi^{\prime}(x^{1})\cdots\pi^{\prime}(x^{n})

\displaystyle=\pi(x^{1})\cdots\pi(x^{n}).

Since

, it follows that
\hat{\pi}
is the unique algebra morphism such that

Conversely, suppose that there exists

6.
Partial modules

This section aims to introduce the notion of a partial module over a weak Hopf algebra, and its relation with the structures developed in this paper. We define partial modules over weak Hopf algebras based on the partial representations of the weak Hopf algebra over the endomorphisms algebra, inspired by the classical connection between modules and representations. As expected, the category of partial modules over a weak Hopf algebra is isomorphic to the category of its partial representations. Moreover, we will see that a symmetric partial module algebra over a weak Hopf algebra corresponds to an algebra object in the module category over the associated universal Hopf algebroid.

Given
M
a
\Bbbk
-vector space, consider the algebra
\End_{\Bbbk}(M)
associated with
M
. In order to introduce the notion of partial
H
-module, we will use the notion of partial representation of
H
over
\End_{\Bbbk}(M)
, and the classical connection between action and representation.

Let
\bullet\colon H\otimes M\longrightarrow M
be a linear transformation such that the induced map

\displaystyle\pi_{\bullet}\colon H

\displaystyle\longrightarrow

\displaystyle{}\End_{\Bbbk}(M)

\displaystyle h

\displaystyle\longmapsto

\displaystyle{}\pi_{\bullet}(h)\colon M

\displaystyle\longrightarrow M

\displaystyle m

\displaystyle\longmapsto\pi_{\bullet}(h)(m)=h\bullet m

is a partial representation. In this context, the notion of a partial module arises as follows.

Definition 6.1
.

Let
H
be a weak Hopf algebra. A
partial
H
-module
is a vector space

with a linear map
\bullet\colon H\otimes M\longrightarrow M
(called

(PM1)

(PM5)

\displaystyle=([h_{1}]\triangleright 1_{B})([h_{2}k]\triangleright b)

\displaystyle=(h_{1}\bullet 1_{B})(h_{2}k\bullet b)

(4)

\displaystyle(h_{1}k\bullet b)(h_{2}\bullet 1_{B})

\displaystyle=([h_{1}k]\triangleright b)([h_{2}]\triangleright 1_{B})

\displaystyle=([h_{1}]\triangleright([k]\triangleright b))E_{h_{2}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(E_{h_{2}})\triangleright([h_{1}]\triangleright([k]\triangleright b))

\displaystyle=[h_{3}][S^{-1}(h_{2})][h_{1}]\triangleright([k]\triangleright b)

\displaystyle=[S(S^{-1}(h_{3}))][S^{-1}(h_{2})][S(S^{-1}(h_{1}))]\triangleright([k]\triangleright b)

\displaystyle=[S(S^{-1}(h)_{1})][S^{-1}(h)_{2}][S(S^{-1}(h)_{3})]\triangleright([k]\triangleright b)

\displaystyle=[S(S^{-1}(h))]\triangleright([k]\triangleright b)

\displaystyle=[h]\triangleright([k]\triangleright b)

\displaystyle=h\bullet(k\bullet b)

Therefore,

is a symmetrical partial
H
-module algebra.

Then, we have the functor

F\colon\mathbf{Alg}\left({}_{{H_{{}_{par}}^{w}}}\!\!\!\mathbf{Mod}\right)\to{{}_{H}}\mathbf{sym\text{-}parMod}

given by

on morphisms.

Reciprocally, let

{}_{H}\mathbf{parMod}

{}_{{H_{{}_{par}}^{w}}}\!\!\!\mathbf{Mod}
with action

.

Firstly, we need to endow

with an structure of
{A_{{}_{par}}^{w}}
-bimodule, as follows:

\displaystyle E_{h}b:\mskip-6.0mu

\displaystyle=h_{1}\bullet(S(h_{2})\bullet b)

\displaystyle=(h_{1}\bullet 1_{B})(h_{2}S(h_{3})\bullet b)

\displaystyle=(1_{1}h\bullet 1_{B})(1_{2}\bullet b)

\displaystyle=(1_{1}h\bullet 1_{B})(1_{2}\bullet 1_{B})(1_{3}\bullet b)

\displaystyle=(1_{1}\bullet((h\bullet 1_{B})1_{B}))(1_{2}\bullet b)

\displaystyle=1\bullet((h\bullet 1_{B})1_{B}b)

\displaystyle=(h\bullet 1_{B})b

and

\displaystyle bE_{h}:\mskip-6.0mu

\displaystyle=h_{2}\bullet(S^{-1}(h_{1})\bullet b)

\displaystyle=(h_{2}S^{-1}(h_{1})\bullet b)(h_{3}\bullet 1_{B})

\displaystyle=(S^{-1}(h_{1}S(h_{2}))\bullet b)(h_{3}\bullet 1_{B})

\displaystyle=b(h\bullet 1_{B})

\displaystyle E_{h}(bb^{\prime})=(h\bullet 1_{B})(bb^{\prime})=((h\bullet 1_{B})b)b^{\prime}=(E_{h}b)b^{\prime},

\displaystyle(bb^{\prime})E_{h}=(bb^{\prime})(h\bullet 1_{B})=b((h\bullet 1_{B})b^{\prime})=b(E_{h}b^{\prime}),

\displaystyle b(E_{h}b^{\prime})=b((h\bullet 1_{B})b^{\prime})=(b(h\bullet 1_{B}))b^{\prime}=(bE_{h})b^{\prime}

\displaystyle[h]\triangleright(bb^{\prime})=h\bullet(bb^{\prime})=(h_{1}\bullet b)(h_{2}\bullet b^{\prime})=([h_{1}]\triangleright b)([h_{2}]\triangleright b^{\prime}),

so the multiplication

in

.

As

, for any

Then, we have the functor

7.
Quantum inverse semigroups

In
[
1
]
, a new Hopf structure was introduced, called
quantum inverse semigroup
. The primary goal was to extend the concept of inverse semigroups into the Hopf context, analogous to how Hopf algebras generalize groups. In that work, the authors proved that when
H
is a cocommutative Hopf algebra, the partial Hopf algebra
H_{par}
has a quantum inverse semigroup structure. When we are dealing with weak Hopf algebras, it also happens. So, in this section, we shall prove that
{H_{{}_{par}}^{w}}
is a quantum inverse semigroup. From now on, assume that
\Bbbk
is a field of characteristic 0 (to maintain consistency with the definition of QISG given in
[
1
]
).

Definition 7.1
.

[
1
, Definition 3.1]

A quantum inverse semigroup (QISG) is a triple
(H,\Delta,\mathcal{S})
in which:

(QISG1)

H
is a (not necessarily unital)
\Bbbk
-ring.

(QISG2)

\Delta:H\longrightarrow H\otimes H
is multiplicative and coassociative.

(QISG3)

\mathcal{S}:H\longrightarrow H
is a
\Bbbk
-linear map, called
pseudo-antipode
, satisfying

(i)
\mathcal{S}(hk)=\mathcal{S}(k)\mathcal{S}(h)
, for all
h,k\in H
.

(ii)
I*\mathcal{S}*I=I
and
\mathcal{S}*I*\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{S}
in the convolution algebra
\operatorname{End}_{\Bbbk}(H)
.

(QISG4)

The sub
\Bbbk
-rings generated by the images of
I*\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{S}*I
mutually commute, that is, for every
h,k\in H
,

h_{1}\mathcal{S}\left(h_{2}\right)\mathcal{S}\left(k_{1}\right)k_{2}=\mathcal{S}\left(k_{1}\right)k_{2}h_{1}\mathcal{S}\left(h_{2}\right)

A QISG is unital if
H
is a unital
\Bbbk
-algebra and
\mathcal{S}\left(1_{H}\right)=1_{H}
. A QISG is co-unital if
H
is a
\Bbbk
-coalgebra and
\varepsilon_{H}\circ\mathcal{S}=\varepsilon_{H}
.

For readers interested in learning more about this structure, we refer them to
[
1
]
, where properties and several examples are presented.

Theorem 7.2
.

Let
H
be a cocommutative weak Hopf algebra over
\Bbbk
. Then, the Hopf algebroid
{H_{{}_{par}}^{w}}
has the structure of a unital QISG.

Proof.

The proof follows similar steps as in
[
1
, Theorem 3.12]
with the appropriate adaptations to the weak case. For the reader’s convenience, at each step of the proof, we will highlight where the properties of the weak case appear. For this, consider the following linear map:

\delta\colon H\longrightarrow\delta(H)\subseteq{H_{{}_{par}}^{w}}\otimes{H_{{}_{par}}^{w}},\quad h\longmapsto\left[h_{1}\right]\otimes\left[h_{2}\right].

We shall demonstrate that the map
\delta
is a partial representation of
H
in
\delta(H)

3.4

PR6

Through this proof, we use (*) to denote when the cocommutative property is used.

For
h,k\in H

\displaystyle\delta(h)\delta\left(k_{1}\right)\delta\left(S\left(k_{2}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\left[h_{1}\right]\left[k_{1}\right]\left[S\left(k_{4}\right)\right]\otimes\left[h_{2}\right]\left[k_{2}\right]\left[S\left(k_{3}\right)\right]

\displaystyle{=}\left[h_{1}\right]\left[k_{1}\right]\left[S\left(k_{2}\right)\right]\otimes\left[h_{2}\right]\left[k_{3}\right]\left[S\left(k_{4}\right)\right]

\displaystyle{=}\left[h_{1}k_{1}\right]\left[S\left(k_{2}\right)\right]\otimes\left[h_{2}k_{3}\right]\left[S\left(k_{4}\right)\right]

\displaystyle{=}\left[h_{1}k_{1}\right]\left[S\left(k_{4}\right)\right]\otimes\left[h_{2}k_{2}\right]\left[S\left(k_{3}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\delta\left(hk_{1}\right)\delta\left(S\left(k_{2}\right)\right),

which proves (
PR2
). Also,

\displaystyle\delta(h_{1})\delta\left(S(h_{2})\right)\delta\left(h_{3}\right)

\displaystyle=\left[h_{1}\right]\left[S(h_{4})\right]\left[h_{5}\right]\otimes\left[h_{2}\right]\left[S(h_{3})\right]\left[h_{6}\right]

\displaystyle{=}\left[h_{1}\right]\left[S(h_{2})\right]\left[h_{3}\right]\otimes\left[h_{4}\right]\left[S(h_{5})\right]\left[h_{6}\right]

\displaystyle{=}\left[h_{1}\right]\otimes\left[h_{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\delta\left(h\right),

{H_{{}_{par}}^{w}}\otimes{H_{{}_{par}}^{w}}

In order to define the pseudo-antipode, consider the linear map

\widetilde{S}:H\longrightarrow({H_{{}_{par}}^{w}})^{\mathrm{op}},\quad h\longmapsto[S(h)]

We shall prove that the map

\displaystyle\tilde{S}(h)\cdot_{\text{op }}\tilde{S}\left(k_{1}\right)\cdot_{\text{op }}\tilde{S}\left(S\left(k_{2}\right)\right)

({H_{{}_{par}}^{w}})^{\mathrm{op}}

\displaystyle I*S*I\left(\left[h^{1}\right]\ldots\left[h^{n+1}\right]\right)=

\displaystyle S*I*S\left(\left[h^{1}\right]\ldots\left[h^{n}\right]\right)

\displaystyle=\left[S\left(h_{1}^{n}\right)\right]\ldots\left[S\left(h_{1}^{1}\right)\right]\left[h_{2}^{1}\right]\ldots\left[h_{2}^{n}\right]\left[S\left(h_{3}^{n}\right)\right]\ldots\left[S\left(h_{3}^{1}\right)\right]
