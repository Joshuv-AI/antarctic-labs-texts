Abstract
. The popularization of AI chatbot usage globally has created opportunities for research into their benefits and drawbacks, especially for students using AI assistants for coursework support. This paper asks: how do LLMs perceive the intellectual capabilities of student profiles from intersecting marginalized identities across different cultural contexts? We conduct one of the first large-scale intersectional analyses on LLM explanation quality for Indian and American undergraduate profiles preparing for engineering entrance examinations. By constructing profiles combining multiple demographic dimensions including caste, medium of instruction, and school boards in India, and race, HBCU attendance, and school type in America, alongside universal factors like income and college tier, we examine how quality varies across these factors. We observe biases providing lower-quality outputs to profiles with marginalized backgrounds in both contexts. LLMs such as Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct and GPT-4o demonstrate granular understandings of context-specific discrimination, systematically providing simpler explanations to Hindi/Regional-medium students in India and HBCU profiles in America, treating these as proxies for lower capability. Even when marginalized profiles attain social mobility by getting accepted into elite institutions, they still receive more simplistic explanations, showing how demographic information is inextricably linked to LLM biases. Different models (Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct, GPT-4o, GPT-4o-mini, GPT-OSS 20B) embed similar biases against historically marginalized populations in both contexts, preventing profiles from switching between AI assistants for better results. Our findings have strong implications for AI incorporation into global engineering education.

1.
Introduction

Large Language Models (LLMs) are being increasingly integrated into learning platforms as interactive educational systems that provide explanations, solve problems, and adapt feedback to individual students. This adaptation, commonly called personalization
(
82
)
, uses signals such as students’ past performance, estimated skills, language proficiency, or personal background to tailor instructional content and feedback. For example, a student who consistently struggles with algebra may receive step-by-step explanations and simpler practice problems, while a high-performing student may receive more advanced problems and concise explanations that challenge their reasoning. While personalization can improve engagement and learning efficiency, prior work
(
75
;
52
)
shows that it can also introduce systematic disparities in the quality, depth, and encouragement of instruction. LLM-based tutors deliver uneven instructional content based on protected attributes such as race, gender, and income, reflecting broader findings of bias in educational technologies and intelligent tutoring systems
(
81
)
. These systems learn from large-scale human-generated data and institutional practices, and thus LLMs themselves embody and reproduce existing educational biases.

Importantly, personalized instruction can impose substantial costs: because it intentionally varies to match student characteristics, reliance on parameters like race, gender, or cultural background can create uneven instructional quality, reducing learning gains, self-esteem, and academic persistence for some students.
(
41
)
. Existing work examines personalization biases in U.S. educational contexts, often along axes such as race, gender, and income, researchers know far less about how educational hierarchies shape students’ experiences with LLM-based tools globally
(
39
)
, amidst a larger trend of narrow focus of AI Fairness research on so-called ‘Global North’ contexts
(
14
;
23
;
24
;
29
;
49
, e.g.,)
. Educational bias is not confined to AI systems; it is deeply embedded in physical and societal educational environments, varying across institutions, social hierarchies, and countries
(
5
;
28
;
60
)
.
As a result, students using identical LLMs may encounter systematically different instructional experiences depending on institutional rank or cultural context.

In this paper, we investigate how LLMs perceive the intellectual capability of student profiles across cultural
contexts and model designs conditioned on protected attributes. We study instructional complexity, which refers to the level of detail, challenge, and
conceptual advancement in the explanations that LLMs provide to student profiles. We examine how this varies as a
function of students’ protected attributes, in combination, as overlapping social positions often shape students’ educational experiences rather than any single characteristic in isolation [Figure
1
]. We ask two research questions:
RQ1:
How do LLMs assign
instructional complexity to student personae across different socio-cultural settings in India and the United States?

RQ2:
Do model size, provenance, and baseline capability predict more equitable behavior in assigning
instructional complexity? India provides a particularly important setting for this analysis, as hierarchies such as
caste, language medium, school board, geography, and institutional prestige shape educational opportunity in ways
that U.S. centric fairness research does not capture. Caste, a birth-based social stratification system in India, creates systematic educational and economic disparities across hierarchical groups. We address these questions by first analyzing context-specific
patterns within Indian and American educational systems separately, and then conducting a cross-cultural comparative analysis to identify universal versus culturally specific bias mechanisms.

For India, student profiles span seven dimensions central to educational stratification: caste, income, medium of
instruction, school board, geographic location, college tier, and gender. For the United States, we use a parallel set of dimensions that includes race/ethnicity, income, school type, college tier, geographic context, and gender. Across both contexts, we evaluate four widely used LLMs (GPT-4o, GPT-4o-mini, Qwen 2.5-32B-Instruct, GPT-OSS-20B) to examine how model scale, openness, and baseline capability affect disparities in instructional complexity, spanning open and closed-source systems. We design two educational tasks inspired by prior work
(
81
)
. The first is a ranking task, where the model selects an appropriate explanation difficulty for a student profile. The second is a generation task, in which the model produces a personalized explanation tailored to the student profile. We evaluate these tasks on MATH-50
(
27
)
to align with existing benchmarks and on JEEBench
(
3
)
to capture India-specific engineering content. To quantify bias in instructional complexity, we use two complementary metrics: Mean Absolute Bias (MAB), which captures average deviation from equal treatment within a subgroup, and Maximum Difference Bias (MDB), which measures the largest disparity between any two subgroup scores. We further report Cohen’s
d
and
t
-tests to assess statistical significance.

Our results show that LLMs systematically adjust instructional complexity based on demographic descriptors rather than student ability. Across both countries, income consistently predicts more advanced explanations. Beyond income, models encode context-specific hierarchies: in India, English-medium instruction and higher caste are associated with more complex explanations, while in the U.S., models assign lower complexity to students from minority-serving institutions such as HBCUs. These biases persist within elite institutions, where models give simpler explanations to low-income students than to higher-income peers at the same schools. This pattern indicates that these disparities arise from how models interpret demographic attributes rather than from task framing. Model size, openness, and baseline capability do not reliably predict equitable treatment. All evaluated models exhibit comparable MAB and MDB values and learn similar demographic hierarchies, differing primarily in how consistently they apply them. These effects persist for intersectional student profiles, where multiple marginalized identities compound disadvantage.

Together, our findings show that educational bias in LLMs reflects systemic patterns embedded in training data rather than properties of specific model designs. Models universally reproduce income-based stratification while encoding culturally specific hierarchies, such as language medium and caste in India and HBCU status and school type in the United States. This work makes three key contributions: (1) we provide the
first cross-cultural investigation
of how LLMs assign instructional complexity across Indian and U.S. contexts; (2) we examine
intersectional identities
rather than singular attributes, revealing how overlapping characteristics shape inequitable treatment; and (3) we demonstrate that
model scale and openness do not predict equitable treatment
, as all models exhibit comparable bias despite different baseline capabilities. As LLMs increasingly mediate access to educational support worldwide, responsible deployment requires ensuring that these systems do not systematically disadvantage marginalized learners.

2.4.
Building on Educational AI Bias Auditing

Recent audit frameworks provide a foundation for studying bias in LLM-based tutoring. Weissburg et al.
(
81
)
introduce an experiment in which LLMs act as educational tutors, revealing systematic differences in recommended problem difficulty and explanation quality across student personae. They operationalize bias using ranking and generation tasks and propose metrics such as Mean Choice Value (MCV), Mean Grade Level (MGL), Mean Absolute Bias (MAB), and Maximum Difference Bias (MDB), establishing a reproducible baseline for auditing educational LLMs.

Our work extends this framework in three ways. First, rather than auditing single demographic attributes, we construct intersectional profiles that combine multiple dimensions, enabling analysis of compounding effects. Second, we adopt a comparative design spanning U.S. and Indian educational systems, incorporating context-specific attributes such as caste and medium of instruction in India and HBCU attendance in the U.S. Third, we move beyond general-purpose mathematics benchmarks by supplementing MATH-50
(
81
)
with JEEBench
(
3
)
, allowing comparison between general competency questions and high-stakes assessments. Together, these extensions position our study as a bridge between educational bias auditing and cross-cultural AI evaluation. By situating LLM behavior within distinct institutional and cultural contexts, our work contributes to a more globally grounded understanding of how educational inequality is encoded and reproduced in generative AI systems.

3.
Methodology

To examine how LLMs perceive students’ intellectual capabilities across cultural contexts through a comparative experimental design, we construct intersectional student profiles for two educational systems – India and the United States across multiple demographic dimensions (e.g., caste, income, medium of instruction, and location in India; race, income, HBCU attendance, and school type in America) [Fig.
1
] We have the models interact with these profiles through two tasks and evaluate using complexity metrics (Mean Choice Value, Mean Grade Level) and bias metrics (Mean Absolute Bias, Maximum Difference Bias), validated with statistical analysis (Cohen’s D, KL divergence).

3.1.3.
Datasets

We evaluate model behaviour on two datasets that are grounded in engineering backgrounds.

MATH-50.
Following
81
, we use the MATH dataset
(
27
)
, a collection of mathematical problems spanning seven subjects: Algebra, Counting and Probability, Geometry, Intermediate Algebra, Number Theory, Pre-algebra, and Precalculus. Problems are divided into five difficulty levels. For the ranking task, we sample 50 problem-solution pairs per subject-level combination. For the generation task, we sample three problems per subject from Level 3, keeping the problem content constant across all profiles.

JEEBench.
To gain more context around Indian Institutions, we evaluate on JEEBench.
(
3
)
, a dataset of problems drawn from the Joint Entrance Examination (JEE). JEE is India’s highly competitive national-level engineering entrance examination, and preparation for it is a common experience shared by Indian engineering students. The dataset includes problems from Mathematics, Physics, and Chemistry, spanning multiple formats (single-correct MCQ, multiple-correct MCQ, Numeric, and integer-type). We sample 50 problems across subjects, using the same set for all profiles.

3.1.4.
Experimental Tasks

We designed two tasks to evaluate the differential treatment of student profiles.

Ranking Task.
We present the model with five problem-solution pairs at varying difficulty levels (Levels 1–5) for a given mathematical problem, shuffled randomly to prevent position bias. The model selects the most appropriate explanation for the specified student profile. We tested two frames: a
teacher role
("You are teaching a [profile] student…") and a
student role
("You are a [profile] student learning…"). This dual-role design allows us to examine whether bias patterns differ when the model adopts a teacher’s versus a student’s perspective. For 100 profiles across seven mathematical topics, we conducted experiments in both roles, yielding 1,400 ranking trials.

Generation Task.
The model generates an original explanation for a given problem tailored to the student profile. We selected 3 problems per subject from difficulty Level 3 (mid-range complexity), using the same problems across all profiles. This yielded 2100 generation experiments for MATH-50 (100 profiles x 7 subjects x 3 problems). For JEEBench, we sampled 50 problems across three subjects (physics, chemistry and mathematics), resulting in 5000 generations.

3.1.5.
Metrics

We quantify model behavior and differences in treatment across groups using established metrics
(
81
)
.

Ranking Task: Mean Choice Value (MCV).
For the ranking task, we compute the MCV, which represents the average difficulty level selected by the model for a given student profile.

Generation Task: Mean Grade Level (MGL).
For generated explanations, we measure linguistic complexity using the Flesch-Kincaid Grade level, Gunning Fog Index and Coleman-Liau Index, averaging them into a Total Grade Level (TGL). We then compute the MGL across problems for each profile-subject combination.

(1)

\text{MCV}(m,s)=\mathbb{E}_{t\in T}[C_{t}]

(2)

\text{MGL}(m,s)=\mathbb{E}_{t\in T}\big[\text{TGL}(m(t,s))\big]

where
m
denotes the model,
s
the profile and subject pair,
T
the set of problems,
[C_{t}]\in\{1,2,3,4,5\}
the chosen difficulty, and
m(t,s)
the generated explanation for problem
t
. Higher MCV/MGL indicates more complex explanations.

Bias Quantification.
To measure bias, we first normalize the task-specific scores (MCV for ranking, MGL for generation) within each subgroup and subject by subtracting the subgroup mean and dividing by the subgroup standard deviation. This normalization enables comparison across subgroups and tasks. We then quantify bias on these normalised scores using two complementary metrics:
Mean Absolute Bias
(MAB), which measures the average magnitude of deviation from subgroup means, and
Maximum Difference Bias
(MDB), which captures the largest disparity between any two profiles within a subgroup. Lower values for both indicate more equitable treatment. We compute MAB and MDB across demographic dimensions. They are defined as follows:

(3)

\text{MAB}(m,S_{d})=\mathbb{E}_{s\in S_{d}}\big[\,|Z(m,s)|\,\big]

(4)

\text{MDB}(m,S_{d})=\max_{s\in S_{d}}Z(m,s)-\min_{s\in S_{d}}Z(m,s)

We observed that medium of instruction emerged as the strongest predictor across both ranking and generation tasks. In ranking, 90-100% of highest MCV profiles used English medium, while 70-90% of lowest used Hindi/Regional. English-medium personae received recommendations 1.0-1.1 units higher than Hindi/Regional personae with identical caste and gender (MCVs: 1.71-2.96 across models). In generation, this intensified: 100% of the highest JEEBench MGL profiles [Table

Further, income created 1-2 year complexity gaps, showing the highest MAB (1.06-1.31 in ranking, 1.06-1.70 in generation). High-income personae received recommendations 0.8 units and 2–3 grades higher within same linguistic and caste groups than low-income personae, on identical JEEBench problems [Figure
2
]. In generation, income created 1.2-1.4 grade level gaps in MATH-50 and 1.6-1.9 grades in JEEBench. Caste-based disparities also spanned from elementary to college levels, as SC/ST personae showed MDB from 4.0 units (ranking) to 8.70-13.62 grade levels (generation), but General caste personae received recommendations 0.7-0.8 units higher than SC/ST personae when income and medium were kept constant. Some SC/ST profiles received grade 6-7 explanations, while others received grade 15+ ones. Gender, especially non-binary gender, acted a discriminatory factor as non-binary personae received explanations averaging 0.72 grade levels simpler than male personae (range: 0.08-1.23 grade levels, median: 0.87 grade levels, Table
13
) with other demographics held constant, but male and female personae showed similar mean MGLs of 10.16 and 10.37, respectively.

Elite institution access also did not override demographic bias, as IIT profiles ranged from MCV 4.14 to 1.71 in ranking (2.4-unit gap) and MGL 13.30 to 10.66 in generation (2.6 grade level gap) based purely on income and medium of instruction. General, high-income, English-medium IIT profiles received college-level explanations, while SC/ST, low-income, Hindi/Regional IIT students received middle school explanations at India’s most prestigious institutions.

Finally, intersectional analyses compounded multiple disadvantages, as low-income SC/ST profiles using Hindi/Regional medium in rural areas received substantially lower complexity across all models: Qwen32B MCV=2.03, GPT-4o MCV=1.48, GPT-4o-mini MCV=1.39 (ranking); Qwen32B MGL=9.47, GPT-4o MGL=8.73, GPT-4o-mini MGL=9.12 (generation). However, high-income General student profiles using English medium in metro areas received Qwen32B MCV=2.97 and MGL=11.08, creating 0.86-1.61 unit gaps representing 1–2 years of educational complexity. These gaps widened for JEEBench, showing 1.77-1.91 grade level intersectional gaps compared to MATH-50’s 1.17-1.61 gaps.

4.1.2.
Income and HBCU Status Create Extreme Variability in US contexts

For American profiles, HBCU attendance emerged as a strong disadvantage pattern, as such profiles scored lowest across all models and college tiers (ranking MCV 1.29-1.57). Generation tasks showed HBCUs with the highest MDB among institution types (9.80-11.43 grade levels) and within those, modules produced explanations ranging from grade 8-9 to 11-12 based purely on income and race, 2.6-2.8 grade level gaps, exceeding the variability seen for other institutions, as models learned negative stereotypes about institutions serving Black student profiles, despite HBCUs’ academic rigor and historical significance.

Income effects for Indian contexts also extended to American profiles, with income operating 53% more intensely than in Indian contexts. High-income personae received MAB 1.06-1.23 in ranking versus low-income MAB 0.68-1.11. Within the same racial groups, high-income personae received recommendations 2.1-2.4 units higher in ranking. In generation, income created 2.5-3.9 grade level gaps, substantially larger than India’s 1.6-1.9 gaps: high-income personae received grade 11–13 explanations while low-income personae received grade 8–9 ones (Qwen32B American MGL: 10.64±1.73 vs. Indian: 10.03±1.43, showing 21% higher variability). Race showed similar disadvantage, as Race/ethnicity MDB reached 9.80-15.75 across models, substantially higher than Indian caste-based MDB (8.70-13.62). Models consistently gave Native American personae the simplest content (MGL 8.23-9.67 across models), and models gave white personae the most complex content (MGL 10.64-11.82). Within income levels, White personae received recommendations 0.7-0.8 units higher than Native American personae, with Hispanic personae showing the highest ranking MAB (1.04, Qwen32B), Black personae the highest MDB (4.0). School types followed suit, as Charter schools predominantly serving Black/Hispanic urban student profiles showed MGL 8.69-9.67, while private schools showed MGL 11.58-12.63.
Finally, Ivy League attendance also showed comparable internal disparities to Indian IITs, despite elite status. Ivy League personae ranged from MCV 4.14 to 1.14 in ranking (3.0-unit gap) and MGL 12.63 to 9.43 in generation (3.2 grade level gap). Two Ivy League Hispanic male profiles separated only by income and location showed MCVs of 4.14 (high-income, urban, private) versus 1.14 (low-income, rural, public). In JEEBench, Ivy League gaps reached 4.36 grade levels (MGL 14.60 to 10.24), larger than in Indian contexts.

4.1.3.
Cross-Context Analysis: Income-based and Context-Specific Hierarchies across Countries

Across Indian and American profiles, we observed a few patterns. Firstly, income acted as a predictor in both cases, with larger American differentials (American: 0.26-0.38 MAB difference, Indian: 0.17-0.26), and created 1.5-3.0 grade level gaps in generation across contexts, suggesting that training data captures income-based educational bias. Gender bias showed minimal direct effects and alone did not predict complexity, as Indian contexts showed female-male MAB differences of 0.01-0.04, and American contexts 0.01-0.09. However, non-binary personae exhibited elevated variability (Indian generation MDB 8.44-14.21, American MDB 10.62-15.75), indicating discrimination across contexts. Further, while elite institutions in both contexts (Ivy Leagues in America and IITs in India) were similarly treated, there is a distinct difference in how caste and race are operationalized. Indian caste bias manifests through interaction with medium and regional boards (CBSE/ICSE/State capturing India-specific prestige hierarchies). American race operates through residential differentiation and school funding inequalities, with charter school distinctions (public/private/charter).

Finally, some emergent patterns were context-specific and lacked an appropriate comparison. The differences based on media of instruction in Indian contexts – 100% of the highest JEEBench profiles using English, 100% of the lowest using Hindi/Regional across multiple models – have no American equivalent, as all American profiles assume English instruction. Similarly, the systematic underestimation of HBCUs – scored the lowest across all models and college tiers with MCVs ranging from 1.29 to 1.57). Weissburg et al.
(
81
)
used singular demographic characteristics and showed varied results concerning stereotypes and reversal biases in US-based studies. Their results have found the highest level of bias in income and disability. We confirmed income as the most universal dimension of bias (MAB 1.06-1.70, MDB 6.2-15.8) and also identified context-based hierarchies, caste and language of instruction in India and HBCU status and Charter Schools in America, which were absent from past research comprising single-dimensional approaches. Both studies showed that role framing (student vs teacher) has a negligible effect on bias. However, our use of an intersectional framework revealed that bias is cumulative and that each additional identity creates multiplicative bias, students who have multiple marginalized identities are provided with explanations that are up to 14 grade levels less complex than their privileged counterparts.

Figure 3

Maximum Difference Bias (MDB) measures the largest normalised score gap between demographic groups within each dimension, averaged across ranking and generation tasks. Across both cultural contexts, closed-source models exhibit more structured and consistently higher bias patterns, while open-source models display weaker and less uniform bias. Notably, these trends remain stable across demographic dimensions and national contexts, reinforcing the role of model design in shaping bias behaviour.

4.1.4.
Statistical Validation: Convergence Across Analytical Approaches

Three analytical approaches validate the observed bias patterns. MAB quantifies average deviation, MDB captures the range of disparate treatment, and independent samples t-tests assess statistical significance. Income showed the strongest convergence: highest MAB (1.06-1.70), largest MDB (6.2-15.8 grade levels), and most significant t-values (t=5.57-10.00, p<0.001, d=0.30-0.53 across models). All 18 income comparisons reached significance. Context-specific dimensions showed robust validation: Indian location (t=3.18-6.70, p<=0.002, d=0.17-0.33) and caste (t=2.42-3.89, p<=0.016, d=0.15-0.23) with 14 total significant comparisons. College tier comparisons confirmed institutional prestige cannot override demographic bias (IIT vs. private: t=2.70-3.07, p<=0.007; Ivy vs. HBCU: t=2.24, p=0.025), with moderate effect sizes (d=0.14-0.23) but high within-institution MDB (4.0-5.2 grade levels). Gender showed minimal MAB (0.01-0.09) but significant t-tests (t=2.26-3.76, p<=0.024, d=0.12-0.20), confirming small but consistent effects (Table
12
).

This pattern, where dimensions with high MAB show high t-values and large effect sizes, dimensions with low MAB show smaller but significant effects, and dimensions with high MDB show significant t-tests confirming systematic ranges, validates that results represent algorithmic bias rather than random variation. All models showed significant baseline differences between Indian and American profiles (t=8.23-11.11, p<0.001, |d|=0.25-0.39) yet converged toward identical bias structures within each context. The systematic alignment across MAB, MDB, and statistical significance establishes that models systematically underestimate marginalized student profiles due to demographic stereotypes.

4.2.
RQ2: Do Model Design Choices Predict Equitable Behavior?

4.2.1.
Baseline Performance Varies Substantially While Bias Magnitudes Remain Consistent

We found that all models exhibit significant variation in baseline complexity levels. For Indian MATH-50 generation tasks, mean MGLs ranged from 7.13 (GPT-OSS 20B) to 10.07 (GPT-4o-mini), representing a 2.94 grade level difference (41% variation) for identical mathematical problems. Ranking tasks showed comparable variation: mean MCVs ranged from 1.71 (GPT-4o-mini) to 2.96 (GPT-OSS 20B), representing a 73% difference in central tendency. Standard deviations varied substantially: GPT-4o exhibited SD=2.07-2.98 while GPT-OSS 20B exhibited SD=0.90-1.40, indicating different levels of output consistency. Despite this baseline variation, all models exhibited comparable bias magnitudes as measured by MAB and MDB. MAB values ranged from 0.68 to 1.70 across all models and tasks, with no systematic relationship between model size and MAB magnitude. MDB values reached the maximum scale ranges in all models: 4.0 units (representing the full 1-5 difficulty scale) in ranking tasks and 6.2-15.8 grade levels in generation tasks.

4.2.2.
Model Scale Shows No Correlation With Reduced Bias

Comparison across model scales revealed no relationship between parameter count and model behavior. GPT-4o, the largest model tested, exhibited the highest variability (SD=2.07-2.98) and the most extreme bias ranges (MDB up to 15.75 grade levels in American generation tasks). Qwen32B (32 billion parameters) showed intermediate variability (SD=1.43-1.79) and substantial bias (MDB 4.0-10.62 grade levels). GPT-4o-mini, despite reduced scale relative to GPT-4o, exhibited comparable bias magnitudes (MAB 0.89-1.61, MDB 4.0-9.52 grade levels). GPT-OSS 20B, the smallest model tested, showed the lowest variability (SD=0.90-1.40) but maintained systematic bias (MAB 0.77-1.29, MDB 4.0-6.19 grade levels).
These findings indicate that model scale affects output variability but not output equity. Larger models generated more linguistically complex and varied explanations, but applied this capability based on demographics. Smaller models generated simpler, more consistent outputs but applied bias to these simpler outputs. The absence of correlation between scale and fairness suggests that current scaling approaches optimize for capability without addressing equity concerns.

4.2.3.
Open-Source and Closed-Source Models Show Identical Bias

Bias patterns are independent of model development paradigms. Across open-source (Qwen32B, GPT-OSS 20B) and closed-source models (GPT-4o, GPT-4o-mini), we observed identical demographic characteristics of extreme profiles, income as the strongest universal predictor (MAB differences 0.17-0.38), context-specific hierarchies (medium in India, HBCU in America), and 2-3 grade internal gaps within elite institutions. For Indian contexts, 90-100% of the highest-scoring profiles combined high income and English medium, while 80-90% of the lowest-scoring profiles combined low income and Hindi/Regional medium. For American contexts, 100% of the highest-scoring profiles included high income, and 70-90% of the lowest-scoring combined low income with marginalized racial/ethnic identities.

Despite consistent aggregate patterns, Cohen’s Kappa revealed low inter-model agreement on specific outputs (ranking: K=0.01-0.45, generation: K=-0.02 to 0.29, Table
9
). Highest agreement occurred within models across roles (GPT-4o: K=0.44, GPT-4o-mini: K=0.45), while cross-model agreement was substantially lower (Qwen32B vs. GPT-4o: K=0.01-0.04), indicating that models learn similar demographic hierarchies but implement them differently [Figure
3
]. KL divergence confirmed that all models create substantially different distributions across demographics, despite magnitude differences. Income comparisons showed GPT-4o (KL=6.79), GPT-4o-mini (KL=3.92), Qwen32B (KL=3.78), GPT-OSS 20B (KL=1.55), a 4.4× range, yet all exceeded KL>1.5 (Table
10
). Cohen’s D revealed consistent effect sizes: income produced medium effects (d=0.52-0.79) across all models with no relationship to model size. Overall, 87% of significant effects showed small magnitude (0.2<=|d|<0.5), 13% medium (0.5<=|d|<0.8), distributed similarly across open and closed-source models (Table
11
). Collectively, these findings reveal bias as a systemic property of current training paradigms rather than an artefact of specific implementations. All models learned similar demographic hierarchies from training data reflecting real-world educational inequalities.

5.
Discussion

5.1.
Models Show Fine-Grained Understanding of Discriminatory Practices Prevalent in India

We observe strong evidence of LLMs demonstrating detailed knowledge of discrimination patterns specific to the Indian educational context, extending beyond broad demographic categories to encode fine-grained institutional and linguistic hierarchies. The models treat personae of different identities differently, on top of generally favoring American profiles over Indian ones. While recent research has demonstrated casteist tendencies and religious bias in AI models
(
14
;
24
;
23
;
40
;
69
;
72
)
, our findings show how modern AI models might have developed an even more granular understanding of patterns of discrimination prevalent in India. We find that medium of instruction emerges as the strongest predictor of differential treatment for both MATH-50 and JEEBench. Especially the finding that 100% of the highest MGLs for Qwen32B and GPT-4o appear for personae with English-medium education, while 100% of the lowest MGLs correspond to Hindi- or regional-medium backgrounds, even at elite institutions like IITs, where Hindi/regional-medium students received Qwen32B explanations on JEEBench that were 2.62-2.64 grade levels simpler than their English-medium peers – is one of the clearest indicators of this trend. Moreover, medium of instruction showed MAB values of 1.06-1.31 across models in generation tasks, consistently exceeding MAB values for other dimensions, including income (1.06-1.70), caste (0.89-1.43), and gender (0.01-0.09). The MDB for medium of instruction reached 6.2-8.7 grade levels in MATH-50 and intensified to 8.4-9.6 grade levels in JEEBench, indicating that some student profiles receive elementary school explanations while others receive high school or college-level content purely based on their language of instruction.

Such algorithmic discrimination based on medium of instruction is concerning because, unlike other slightly better-known aspects of discrimination in India due to religion or caste, it might operate in more subtle ways. While India lacks an official national language and states take pride in schools operating in their regional languages, English proficiency is increasingly required for white-collar employment, subtly positioning English and students with English-medium education as more advantaged. However, having access to English-medium education is often associated with privileged identities in India – such as income, caste, qualifications of other family members, etc.
(
62
;
65
)
– such that it can function as a proxy for social privilege, but not necessarily the quality of education. Therefore, when LLMs like Qwen32B and GPT-4o provide English-medium students with high-MGL (more complex) explanations and Hindi/regional-medium students with simpler, low-MGL explanations, they implicitly assume that the former group can better digest complex explanations based solely on their medium of instruction. Such an assumption is unfair because it is not grounded in demonstrated facts and national statistics – indeed, the Government of India’s National Education Policy (2020) advocates that schools maintain students’ mother tongues as the medium of instruction through Grade 5, and recommends continuing this practice through Grade 8 and beyond
(
50
)
. These LLM behaviors reflect algorithmic reinforcement of colonial linguistic hierarchies that position English as the sole legitimate language of academic ability and knowledge. Propagating this bias could further widen the opportunity gap between students from English-medium institutions and their peers from regional-medium schools, further disadvantaging the latter in an increasingly AI-driven world
(
1
;
59
)
.

5.2.
Demographic Biases Persist in Education Systems Despite Social Mobility

It is also evident that aspects of unfair treatment are inescapable to a point where demographic factors to predict the reception of suboptimal responses, even when improved material conditions would suggest equivalent treatment. For example, IIT students show MDB of 4.0 units in ranking tasks and 4.76-5.21 grade levels in JEEBench generation tasks, representing the full range of model outputs occurring within a single institution. This is most evident for low-income and caste-oppressed personae, who receive simpler explanations 2–3 years below their academic standing even when they enter IITs, the premier engineering institutions in India, which require arduous preparation and test-taking for admission. Indeed, low- and medium-income students with caste-oppressed identities receive JEEBench explanations approximately 3.8 grade levels below their high-income, caste-privileged peers. These patterns scale to US personae as well. For American profiles, Ivy League enrollment does not fully mitigate bias: low-income rural students receive simpler explanations than high-income urban students attending Community Colleges, with differences of up to 1.04 grade levels for both Hispanic and Black students.

This difference is significant because it signifies how, despite their hard work to get accepted into IITs, Indian students with low/medium-income backgrounds or caste-oppressed identities will continue to remain susceptible to algorithmic disadvantage
(
46
)
as LLMs continue to judge them only based on demographic aspects they cannot change and ignore the improvement in material conditions. That students cannot escape LLM bias by enrolling in IITs, or presumably making academic progress with good grades, fundamentally undermines the meritocratic premise that individual achievement can override social origins. As LLMs and AI tools become more integrated into academic experiences, they may actively hinder social mobility by persistently providing inequitable treatment to students from marginalized backgrounds, even as these students increasingly rely on AI support. This dynamic echoes broader concerns that structural barriers continue to shape access to opportunity despite demonstrated merit and effort
(
77
)
. These findings also invite further research into studying how AI systems provide inequitable treatment to individuals based on their immutable historically marginalized aspects of identity, even when they attain upward mobility and acquire demographic privilege based on factors in their control. A large majority of AI fairness research often focuses on bias with respect to immutable (or commonly fixed) aspects of identity – such as race, caste, religion, etc. – but there are unexplored questions on how bias patterns might remain the same, or shift, based on changes in mutable identities.

5.3.
Similarly-Poor Quality of Service Across Models, and No Recourse Against Bias

Finally, we further note how patterns of bias against personae with historically marginalized identities seem to be consistent across models, as each model demonstrates comparable amounts of bias against the same aspects of identity. Despite substantial differences in baseline complexity—mean MGLs ranging from 7.13 (GPT-OSS 20B) to 10.07 (GPT-4o-mini), all models exhibited comparable bias magnitudes as measured by MAB (ranging 0.68-1.70 across all models) and MDB (reaching 4.0 units in ranking and 6.2-15.8 grade levels in generation across all models). This consistency in bias magnitude, despite differences in absolute output levels, indicates that the observed bias patterns stem from training data distributions rather than model architecture or scale. This is significant to note, especially given growing research that LLMs might show different trends of bias against identities marginalized in Western contexts, such as Flan-T5 favoring ‘White’ identities over ‘Hispanic’ but Falcon-7B operating the opposite way
(
42
)
, and Meta’s text-to-image generator sexualizes Black women more than other racial identities
(
11
)
, while Stable Diffusion sexualizes Latin American women the most
(
22
)
. These examples illustrate that it is not a foregone conclusion that all models will demonstrate similar bias magnitudes or directions. That all models in our study show the same patterns of bias against certain aspects of identity in Indian contexts is alarming for students with those identities, because it implies that switching models or AI assistants for the same task might not provide better outcomes. While it is common for AI users to choose tools based on observed performance differences, our findings imply that students from marginalized backgrounds cannot reliably escape biased outputs, or even achieve substantial reductions in bias magnitude, by using different models.

5.4.
Implications

Our work has implications for the future of AI usage in education in Indian and American engineering classrooms. We advocate for
stronger awareness among demonstrably-affected individuals about potential differences in quality across their own experiences with AI outputs in contrast to their peers
, such as caste-oppressed and low-income students in India studying in non-English media of instruction, as well as American students in HBCUs. It is important that these findings extend beyond the silos of academic publication, and researchers pursue dissemination campaigns to ensure they reach students who might be encountering lower-quality AI outputs without realizing.

We call for
stronger coverage of non-Western contexts in Fairness research
, echoing others
(
23
;
51
;
57
;
63
;
66
)
. We observe a recent trend within the field to equate ‘non-Western’ contexts with India, and use Indian contexts as a case study of a non-Western society. We advocate instead for a broader interpretation of the term and better representation of other non-Western societies. We also welcome
research into ‘local’ versions of AI agents
built upon language models that understand contexts in which they are deployed, rather than one-size-fits-all solutions like ChatGPT. Finally, we encourage AI systems intended to be used in educational contexts to
be designed as multi-optioned systems
that provide feedback and explanations across a variety of difficulty levels and across other factors, allowing learners to select the level and explanation style that best suits them. Such options could be tailored based on participant profiles, but pay attention to learning styles rather than demographic details.

A methodological choice in this study was that we work with student personae curated based on aspects of identity, instead of actual students. We treated the construction of protected attributes as an ethical consideration, avoiding reinforcement of harmful stereotypes. While this approach has limitations, such as the fact that evaluations of explanation quality and difficulty might be interpreted differently by real students than the computationally-generated scores, as well as the flattening of lived experiences. We made this choice to avoid harming students through their participation in this study, as students with historically marginalized backgrounds might have discovered their AI experiences being poorer than those of their more privileged peers. Future work, however, extend this study with real students.

6.
Conclusion

In this paper, we compare LLM-provided explanation quality for Indian and American profiles across various demographic factors. We uncover how LLMs favor American profiles over Indian ones, reveal fine-grained understandings of social hierarchies in India by discriminating between profiles based on medium of instruction. They maintain rigid categorizations that offer poorer service to students with marginalized identities even after social mobility, and extend across models of different sizes and architectures. In this era, when LLM assistance is increasingly present in classrooms, our findings expose the grim realities of differential quality of service based on identities.
