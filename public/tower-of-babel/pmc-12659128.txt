Background

Breast cancer remains the most common cancer in women and the second leading cause of cancer-related mortality globally [1]. Nearly 20,000 deaths were estimated in Brazil for 2023–2025, with poor prognosis mainly associated with metastasis. There is an urgent need for new anticancer therapies to improve patients’ outcomes and quality of life [2, 3].

Natural compounds with anticancer activity have gained attention as potential therapeutic agents [4–7]. Among them, β-lapachone, a semi-synthetic naphthoquinone from the inner bark of Tabebuia avellanedae, exhibits antimicrobial, analgesic, anti-inflammatory and antitumor effects [8, 9]. Its antineoplastic activity has been reported in a variety of tumors, including lung [10], colon [11], gastric [12], prostate [13] and breast cancers [14].

β-lap suppresses tumor progression by inducing cell cycle arrest, promoting cell death, and modulating EMT [15–17]. Recent evidence suggests that β-lap can restore E-cadherin expression and downregulate mesenchymal markers such as vimentin, Snail, and Twist, thereby interfering with EMT and reducing the metastatic potential of breast and cervical cancer cells in vitro [17, 18].

To improve the physiological relevance of drug screening, 3D cell culture systems have emerged as an intermediate platform between bidimensional (2D) cultures and in vivo models, better recapitulating tumor morphology, physiology, and responsiveness, particularly in breast cancer [19–22]. Based on our previous experience generating scaffold-free cardiac spheroids [23, 24], we developed a 3D model employing the MCF-7 cell line, which was validated by assessing spheroid responses to doxorubicin [25]. While β-lap has been evaluated in some 3D models, such as oral squamous cell carcinoma [26] and hepatocellular carcinoma [27], only one study explored its effects in 3D breast cancer, showing inhibition of mammosphere formation in cancer stem cells [28].

Given the limited research on β-lap in 3D breast cancer models, this study aimed to evaluate its cytotoxic and antimetastatic effects on mammary tumor spheroids (MTS). Our findings revealed that β-lap induced cell death, reduced viability and suppressed aspects of metastasis.

Methods

Cell line and culture conditions

Human breast cancer cell line MCF-7 (ATCC® HTB-22™) was provided by the National Cancer Institute (Brazil). Cells were dissociated from monolayers with trypsin/EDTA in HBSS without calcium and magnesium and harvested from the cell culture flasks for spheroids production. The isolated cells were seeded in 96-well U-plates pre-coated with 1% agarose at a density of 6,250 cells/mL. Spheroids were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS, 1,000 U/mL penicillin, and 50 µg/mL streptomycin, at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere.

Patent

The composition and method for synthesizing the molecule β-lapachone are described in a patent application currently under analysis by the Brazilian National Institute of Industrial Property (BR 10 2016 029628-5). The patent application was made through a partnership between the Oswaldo Cruz Institute (IOC)/Oswaldo Cruz Foundation (Fiocruz), Petrobras/Leopoldo Américo Miguez de Mello Research, Development, and Innovation (CENPES), and the State University of Northern Fluminense Darcy Ribeiro (UENF).

Spheroid treatment and migration assay

After 5 days of culture, a time in which the spheroids are already established, they were treated with increasing concentrations of β-lapachone (0.3, 0.6, 1.2, 2.5, 5.0, and 10.0 mg/L) for 72 h, diluted from a stock concentration of 8720 mg/L in culture medium. The concentration of DMSO in the medium remained below 0.05%. For the migration assay, spheroids were transferred to 24-well plates either directly onto uncoated plastic surfaces or onto gelatin-pre-coated coverslips (used for immunofluorescence analysis). In both setups, no extracellular matrix was added, allowing cells to spontaneously migrate from the spheroid body onto the surface. Immediately after transfer, spheroids were treated with sub-cytotoxic concentrations of β-lap (0.3, 0.6, 1.2 mg/L), and untreated spheroids were used as controls. Spheroid growth and cell migration were monitored daily under a phase-contrast microscope (Nikon Eclipse TS100, Minato-ku, TKY). The images were acquired with the BELView program, version 7.1. The quantification of spheroid diameter or cell dispersion from spheroids were made by measuring the diameter over the course of the study, using the software Image J (Bethesda, MD).

Cell death analysis

The cell death was evaluated using flow cytometry. Briefly, after isolation of spheroid cells with 0.25% trypsin/EDTA solution, the cells were resuspended in RPMI 1640, washed twice with PBS (4º C) and incubated with 20 µL of 7-amino-actinomycin D (7-AAD - BD Via-Probe™) (BD Bioscience, San Jose, CA), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Positive control cells were heat-treated at 60 °C for 10 min. The samples were assessed with a FACSCalibur flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA), and data analysis was performed using Summit v6.1 software (Beckman Coulter, Miami, FL).

Viability analysis

Spheroids with 5 days of culture were treated with different concentrations of β-lapachone (0.3, 0.6, 1.2, 2.5, 5.0, and 10.0 mg/L) or DMSO at the same final concentration used in the treatments (< 0.05%) for 72 h. After treatment, CellTiter-Glo® 3D reagent (Promega, Madison, WI) was added in a 1:1 ratio to the medium, using a pool of ten spheroids per condition. Spheroids exposed to 100 °C for 10 min were used as negative controls. The plate was shaken for 5 min and incubated for 25 min at room temperature, protected from light. Luminescence was measured using a GloMax® microplate luminometer (Promega, Madison, WI). Viability values were normalized to the untreated control and expressed as percentages. Three independent experiments were performed, each in triplicate.

Fluorescence and confocal microscopy

After 48 h of treatment, migrating spheroids on a gelatin-pre-coated coverslip were fixed and permeabilized with ethanol 100% (Merck, Darmstadt, HE). Non-specific antibody binding was blocked using PBS containing 4% BSA, followed by overnight incubation at 4 °C with the primary antibodies: anti-E-cadherin (rabbit monoclonal, Sigma-Aldrich, SAB5500022, 1:100) and anti-vimentin (mouse monoclonal, Sigma-Aldrich, V5255, 1:200). Then, cells were washed with PBS containing 1% BSA and incubated with the appropriate secondary antibodies (Alexa Fluor® 488 or 594) for 1 h at 37 °C. DNA was stained with DAPI for 10 min at 20 °C. Migrating spheroids were mounted with ProLong™ Gold Antifade Mountant and analyzed using an inverted laser scanning 710 confocal microscope (Zeiss, Oberkochen, BW).

Western blotting

Spheroids were harvested from the 96-U-well plastic plate, washed twice with cold PBS and cells were lysed in RIPA buffer supplemented with 10% protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche, Indianapolis, IN) and 10% phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Sigma Aldrich, St. Louis, MO). Lysates were sonicated and protein concentration was quantified using the BCA protein assay kit (Pierce, Rockford, IL). Ten µg of protein was loaded onto 10% SDS-PAGE gels. After electrophoresis, proteins were transferred to nitrocellulose membranes (Bio-rad, Hercules, CA) and blocked with 5% skim milk in TBST for 30 min. Membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies: anti-E-cadherin (Sigma-Aldrich, SAB5500022, 1:2000) and anti-vimentin (mouse monoclonal, Sigma-Aldrich, V5255, 1:3000), followed by incubation with appropriate HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature (25 °C). As an endogenous control, a mouse monoclonal anti-GAPDH antibody was used (Fitzgerald, 10R-G109a, 1:80000). Membranes were washed, incubated with ECL chemiluminescent substrate, and exposed to X-ray film. Densitometry analysis was performed using Image Studio Lite 4.0 software. Relative expression of the target proteins was determined by the ratio between values of intensity of its band by the values of the GAPDH band. The values for untreated cultures were used to normalize the relative expression levels of treated samples at the same time point.

Statistical evaluation

Statistical evaluations were done using GraphPad Prism software 5.0, applying one-way ANOVA, two-way ANOVA with Bonferroni post hoc or the Mann–Whitney test. Data were presented as mean ± SD from a minimum of 3 experiments, with 5 spheroids analyzed for each experimental condition. For Western Blotting analysis, 60 spheroids were pooled for each condition. Statistical significance was determined when p < 0.05.

Results

β-lapachone is cytotoxic to spheroids

MCF-7 spheroids were treated with distinct concentrations of β-lap (0.3, 0.6, 1.2, 2.5, 5.0, and 10.0 mg/L). Brightfield imaging revealed that β-lap induced notable morphological alterations after 24 h of treatment with the three highest concentrations (2.5, 5.0, and 10.0 mg/L) (Fig. 1A), and also after 72 h of exposure to 1.2 mg/L (Fig. 1B). Treated spheroids exhibited loss of compactness, surface irregularities, and darkening, suggesting structural disruption and cytotoxicity (Fig. 1A and B). Quantitative analysis confirmed a reduction in spheroid diameter with 1.2 mg/L of β-lap compared to the untreated control group (Fig. 1B).

To assess whether the observed reduction in spheroid size at 72 h with 1.2 mg/L of β-lap could be associated with cytotoxicity, cell death was quantified using 7-AAD staining by flow cytometry. 7-AAD detects loss of membrane integrity, allowing identification of non-viable cells. At this concentration, approximately 98% of the cells were non-viable, representing an increase of about 35% in cell death compared to the control (Fig. 2A and B).

To complement these results, we performed a time-course CellTiter-Glo® 3D assay to evaluate β-lap cytotoxicity at 24, 48, and 72 h. At 1.2 mg/L, a 25% reduction in cell viability was observed starting at 48 h. At 2.5 mg/L, viability decreased by 35% at 24 h and reached 45% from 48 h onward. Viability dropped by more than 50% at 5.0 and 10.0 mg/L across all time points. Furthermore, the vehicle control (DMSO < 0.05%) showed viability comparable to untreated spheroids, indicating that the solvent did not induce cytotoxicity, whereas the positive control (spheroids exposed to 100 °C for 10 min) had an average viability of 7% (Fig. 2C). These findings confirm that β-lap induces cytotoxicity in breast tumor spheroids.

β-lapachone suppresses cell migration and metastatic potential in MTS

We further investigated whether β-lap would affect the migratory behavior of tumor cells. We performed a migration assay using our scaffold-free 3D spheroid model, previously shown to reproduce in vitro features of metastasis, such as collective migration and EMT process [25].

Based on early morphological findings (Fig. 1), later confirmed by viability data (Fig. 2C), we selected the three lowest concentrations of β-lap (0.3, 0.6, and 1.2 mg/L) to minimize the interference from cytotoxicity and focus specifically on its effect on migration. Spheroids were seeded in a 24-well plate and treated for 72 h. Interestingly, all concentrations of β-lap, except 0.3 mg/L at 24 h, reduced cell spreading from the MTS body compared to the untreated control. Conversely, the untreated control spheroid exhibited progressive migration over time (Fig. 3A - E). Furthermore, a reduction in the number of clustered cells emanating from the MTS in the treated condition was also observed, suggesting inhibition of collective migration (Fig. 3F and H). These data demonstrated that β-lap effectively suppressed cell migration in this 3D model.

β-lap has previously been reported to modulate EMT-related proteins such as E-cadherin (E-cad) and vimentin (VIM) in cancer cells [17, 18]. To investigate the association between cell migration and the EMT in our 3D spheroid model, we analyzed the expression of these markers specifically in cells migrating out from MCF-7 spheroids, excluding cells inside the spheroid core.

We observed that cells adjacent to the MTS body exhibited strong E-cad labeling, whereas peripheral migrating cells exhibited reduced E-cad and increased VIM levels (Fig. 4A and B). Peripheral migrating cells displayed a mesenchymal-like phenotype (Fig. 4B). Next, we assessed whether β-lap could modulate EMT during migration. Western blotting results revealed that β-lap treatment led to an increase in E-cad expression by 18.4% (p = 0.0190) and a reduction in VIM expression by 34.3% (p = 0.0381), compared to the untreated condition (Fig. 4C - D). These findings were supported by immunofluorescence analysis, in which we observed increased E-cad staining and decreased VIM labeling in migrating spheroids during β-lap treatment (Fig. 4E - H). Together, these data demonstrate that β-lap not only impairs cell migration but also suppresses the EMT process in breast tumor spheroids.

Taken together, these results suggest that β-lap inhibited breast tumor spheroid progression, either promoting cytotoxicity or suppressing aspects of metastasis, as evidenced by reduced collective migration and inhibition of the EMT process.

Discussion

Despite major advances in cancer therapy, breast cancer remains a common malignancy with a high mortality index, mostly because of tumor recurrence and metastasis [29–31]. Thus, identifying novel therapeutic strategies to control tumor growth and metastasis is imminent [32–34].

For centuries, the inner bark of the lapacho trees (Tabebuia avellanedae) has been used in traditional phytomedicine to treat several conditions, including infections, fever, and neoplasms. The major bioactive component of this tree is the natural naphthoquinone β-lapachone [8, 9]. Owing to its broad pharmacological activities, including antitumor effects described in multiple solid tumors [10, 15, 18, 35–37], β-lap has been intensively studied as a promising anticancer compound. Interestingly, β-lapachone (ARQ501) in combination with Gemcitabine is in clinical phase II for metastatic pancreatic cancer [38], and its analog ARQ761 has entered early-phase clinical testing in solid tumors [39].

Although β-lap’s anticancer effects have been well studied in 2D models, investigations using 3D systems remain limited. 3D systems better replicate cell–cell and cell–matrix interactions, hypoxic gradients, and drug resistance, thus providing more predictive data on tumor behavior and therapy response [40–42]. These properties make them a well-suited platform for predictive drug screening and mechanistic evaluation [43, 44]. Recent advances using 3D breast cancer models have reinforced their translational relevance, especially for studying invasion, EMT, and drug resistance [22, 45, 46], yet reports on β-lap in such systems are rare. This highlights the novelty and translational relevance of our findings.

The cytotoxic activity of β-lap is well established, though its mechanisms remain under investigation [47, 48]. Two major pathways have been proposed: (i) inhibition of topoisomerase I/II [49, 50], and (ii) NQO1-dependent reduction leading to redox cycling, excessive ROS generation, PARP1 hyperactivation, and NAD⁺/ATP depletion, culminating in apoptotic or necrotic cell death depending on context [51–54]. The strong expression of NQO1 in breast and other solid tumors may explain the selective activity of β-lap against tumor versus non-tumor tissues. Previous studies also demonstrated β-lap’s ability to inhibit tumor growth, promote cell cycle arrest, and impair breast cancer stemness by reducing mammosphere formation [26, 28, 55].

In our 3D spheroid model, β-lap-induced cytotoxicity was confirmed by cell death and viability assays. Notably, high basal levels of necrosis in untreated spheroids, as expected in hypoxic 3D cultures [25], were further increased by β-lap, confirming its cytotoxic action beyond the intrinsic stress of the spheroids. The underlying mechanisms involved in this process are currently being investigated by our group in other studies.

Metastasis remains the major cause of cancer-related mortality, with EMT representing a critical early step in this process [56–59]. To assess the impact of β-lap on this process, we performed a spheroid migration assay using subtoxic concentrations of β-lap. This approach allowed us to distinguish between cytotoxicity and genuine inhibition of migration. In our migration assays, β-lap suppressed collective cell migration and modulated EMT markers, upregulating E-cadherin and reducing vimentin. In parallel, multiple studies have reported that β-lap regulates EMT by increasing E-cadherin while decreasing vimentin and transcription factors such as Snail and Twist [11, 17, 18, 60], supporting our observations. Importantly, ROS-mediated mechanisms may also contribute to the observed modulation of EMT, given the role of oxidative stress in this process [61]. These mechanisms remain to be elucidated and will be further investigated in future studies.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we demonstrated, for the very first time, the antitumor and antimetastatic effect of β-lap on breast tumor spheroids. This natural compound induced cell death, reduced viability, and suppressed metastatic behavior by reducing collective migration and modulating the EMT process. Taken together, our findings support β-lapachone as a promising anticancer natural compound under active investigation for potential use in breast cancer treatment.
