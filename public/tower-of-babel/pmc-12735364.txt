Undergraduate Competency-Based Medical Curriculum in Community Medicine: An Ongoing Journey
Authors: Hemangini K. Shah, Frederick S. Vaz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The specialty of Community Medicine plays a pivotal role in training of the Indian Medical Graduate. It provides them a deep exposure to the health and healthcare in the community where most of the healthcare is needed and are equipped with skills to communicate efficiently with the patient, family, and community. Reform in medical education in the past few years through the adoption and implementation of Curriculum-Based Medical Education (CBME) has further transformed the specialty of Community Medicine. Introduction of Family Adoption Programme, where the specialty of Community Medicine plays a pivotal role, has brought to the fore opportunities and fresh challenges. We have put forth the evolution of the curriculum in Community Medicine from the Graduate Medical Education Regulation 1997 to the current CBME guidelines 2024 attempting to provide the readers, especially the faculty in Community Medicine, easy tracing of the changes in the curriculum over the past few years and serve as a ready reckoner amid the conundrum of multiple guidelines.

BACKGROUND

As we time-travel along the by lanes of undergraduate training in Community Medicine, we stumble upon GMER 1997, which stated the goal for undergraduate training in Community Medicine as to prepare them to function as community and first-level physicians in accordance with the institutional goals.

The objectives for training the undergraduates in Community Medicine, though brief, were laid down, as per Knowledge, Skills, and Integration domains. On the hindsight, the curriculum could have been more well defined by incorporating assessment methods. Further on, each university developed their own version of the curriculum in Community Medicine with the focus on acquisition of knowledge instead of competency and assessment focused on testing this acquired knowledge.

A total of 382 teaching hours were allocated to Community Medicine, which included 12 weeks of clinical postings of 3 hours each. Teaching hours were fixed at 60 hours in first MBBS, 200 hours in second MBBS (56 hours classroom and 8 weeks of clinical posting of 3 hours each), and 122 hours in Third MBBS Part I (50 hours classroom and 4 weeks of clinical posting of 3 hours each). Theory lectures, demonstrations, seminars, and so on in addition to clinics were noted to have been listed as the teaching-learning methods.

The curriculum largely remained unchanged over the next 2 decades, and then, on the 4th of November 2019, in exercise of the powers conferred by Section 33 of the Indian Medical Council Act, 1956 (102 of 1956), the Board of Governors in the supersession of Medical Council of India effected a major amendment to the “Regulations on Graduate Medical Education, 1997,”[1] rechristening it “Regulations on Graduate Medical Education (Amendment), 2019”,[2] the GMER 2019.

This brought about a major, dynamic, and radical change in medical education in India. Up till then, medical education and the curriculum seldom underwent any change, resulting in the medical graduates being trained on a syllabus using largely didactic methods in an otherwise skill-based profession. From 1997 till 2019, for around 22 years, medical education remained static with no major reform, while the world over medical education needed to be kept up with the changing times.

The GMER 2019, for the first time, introduced the concept of Competency-Based Medical Education (CBME). The document explicitly stated that “Competency-based learning would include designing and implementing medical education curriculum that focuses on the desired and observable ability in real-life situations”; in essence, medical education entirely focused on outcomes, wherein additionally, assessment would drive learning. The GMER 2019 envisaged training a “Indian Medical Graduate” who would encompass the roles of a clinician, leader, communicator, lifelong learner, and above all a professional who is committed to excellence, is ethical, and is responsive and accountable to patients, community, and the profession.

The CBME concept was largely implemented in medical colleges across India, albeit with initial resistance and scepticism, maybe because every state had their own local challenges. MCI/NMC supplemented this switchover through several modules covering various newer aspects of the CBME-based curriculum and a detailed three-volume competency-based curriculum. Novel elements included Foundation course, Early Clinical Exposure, AETCOM, and Electives.

As the country was putting together CBME and was braced for major change, COVID-19 pandemic Stuck, dampening rollout of CBME curriculum, affecting timelines and the change envisaged under new curriculum. NMC subsequently introduced modifications to meet this challenge, which included the Pandemic module and modified timelines for batches 2021[3] and 2022.[4] While the purpose was limited to speciality of community medicine, it seems to be moving beyond to cover all changes post CBME. Keeping up the march toward the change, a newer element, Family Adoption Programme (FAP), was introduced for the 2021 batch in an attempt to provide early community exposure.[3] Subsequently, a revised CBME 2023[5] and GMER 2023[6] was introduced, with minor changes to the GMER 2019.

In September 2024, NMC notified the CBME 2024,[7] wherein there has been further metamorphosis in almost all aspects. This included new competency tables in three volumes, with new competencies added, some revised and some modified. Teaching hours were also modified, and further changes have been effected in most of the areas.

The specialty of Community Medicine plays an important role in training of an Indian Medical Graduate. Training in Community Medicine gives the students deep exposure to the health and healthcare in the community. In addition, students learn communication skills at family and community levels. Reform in medical education in the past few years through the adoption and implementation of CBME has also transformed the specialty of Community Medicine. The introduction of the novel concept of FAP, where the specialty of Community Medicine plays a pivotal role, has brought to the fore opportunities and fresh challenges.

This paper traces the evolution of the curriculum in Community Medicine from the GMER 1997 to the current CBME guidelines 2024. We have attempted to provide the readers a comparative description of all the changes in the curriculum over the past few years. In the conundrum of multiple guidelines for different batches of students, this paper serves as a ready reckoner for the faculty in Community Medicine.

THE GMER 2019 (ACTUALLY A MAJOR AMENDMENT TO GMER 1997)

The GMER 2019, for the first time in medical education in India, introduced the concept of Competency-Based Medical Education, thus transforming medical education in India, an attempt to bring it at par with contemporary medical education practiced in the rest of the world.

Under the GMER 2019, the MBBS course started with a foundation course. Community Medicine was allocated 8 hours for a field visit under the Community Orientation Module. GMER 2019 introduced newer teaching learning methods (Large group Teaching, LGT; Small Group teaching, SGT; Self-Directed Learning, SDL; DOAP; etc.) as well as gave more importance on teaching students in small groups by capping didactic lectures at one-third of teaching hours.

Community Medicine was allotted 52 hours of teaching in the first year (LGT 20 hours, SGT 27 hours, SDL 5 hours). Sixty hours of teaching was allocated in the second professional year (LGT 20 hours, SGT 30 hours, SDL 10 hours). In addition, there is clinical posting of 4 weeks (5 days a week, 3 hours each day). The teaching schedule for the Phase III Part I was 105 hours, comprising 40 hours of LGT, 60 hours of SGT, and 5 hours of SDL. This was supplemented by 6 weeks clinical posting (6 days a week, 3 hours each day). In addition, Community Medicine had a role to play in AETCOM and Electives.

GMER 2019, for the first time, introduced a competency-based syllabus through competency tables for all broad specialities. Community Medicine competencies were published in volume II under Medicine and Allied subjects. The syllabus was divided into 20 topics and 107 competencies. For each competency, the teaching–learning method, assessment method, or horizontal or vertical integration was suggested. Each competency was also assigned a domain (Knowledge-K, Skill-S, Attitude-A, and Communication-C) and level to be achieved by the student (Knows-K, Knows How-KH, Shows How-SH, Performs independently-P). A special feature was the rollout of specially developed modules on various aspects of the New CBME curriculum. The modules included: Foundation module, Assessment module, electives module, AETCOM module, Logbook guidelines, Alignment, and Integration Module.

MODIFICATIONS FOR THE 2021 AND 2022 BATCHES

Minor changes in the teaching hours were made for the 2021 and the 2022 batches to account for the disruption due to the COVID-19 pandemic. The noteworthy change was the introduction of the FAP from the 2021 batch. The FAP was designed as an institutional program for the student coordinated by the Community medicine department. The clinical posting duration was reduced from 10 weeks to 8 weeks for the 2022 batch. A new Module called the Pandemic module was introduced. Community Medicine had an important role in imparting this module to the students; in addition, an online learning module was also introduced to guide online learning during the COVID-19 Pandemic.

CBME 2023 AND GMER 2023

The GMER 2023 reiterated making medical education more learner-centric (rather than pedagogic), patient-centric, gender-sensitive, outcome-oriented, and environment-appropriate. It focused on an outcome-driven curriculum which conforms to global trends. Emphasis is made on the alignment and integration of subjects both horizontally and vertically while respecting the strengths and necessity of subject-based instruction and assessment.

GMER 2023 also focused on the importance of ethical values, responsiveness to the needs of the patient, and acquisition of communication skills by providing dedicated curriculum time in the form of a longitudinal program based on Attitude, Ethics, and Communication (AETCOM) competencies. Emphasis was also placed on collaborative and interdisciplinary team work, professionalism, altruism, and respect in professional relationships with due sensitivity to differences in thought, social and economic position, and gender.

The CBME guidelines 2023 were more explicit in outlining the broad competencies in Community Medicine. The curriculum objectives were also described in detail under the domains of Knowledge, Skills, and Integration. As far as integration was concerned, the CBME 2023 guidelines mandate the departments of Community Medicine to adopt an integrated approach toward other clinical disciplines, public health services, NGOs, environmental sciences, social sciences, management, hospital administration, research, and so on to impart training to enable the graduate to work at all levels of health care.

A major change in teaching hours was also effected by CBME 2023. The teaching hours in Phase I were reduced to 40 Hours, but it was bolstered with FAP for 27 hours. The Phase II teaching hours were drastically reduced to 25 hours with 30 hours of FAP, while major bulk of teaching in Community Medicine was moved to Phase III, Part I with a total of 145 hours of teaching and 31 hours of FAP. The clinical postings were capped at 8 weeks (4 weeks in Phase II and 4 Weeks in Phase III, Part I). The guidelines also explicitly allocated specific AETCOM competencies to each department, Community Medicine being allotted AETCOM competency 3.5 [Table 1].

CBME 2024

On completion of 5 years since the implementation of the GMER 2019, NMC conducted an exercise to have a relook at all aspects of the various components in the existing regulations and guidelines and adapt them to the changing demography, socioeconomic context, perceptions, values, advancements in medical education, and expectations of stakeholders. Emerging health care issues, particularly in the context of emerging diseases, impact of advances in science and technology, and shorter distances on diseases and their management also needed consideration in the curriculum and syllabus. The subject goals for Community Medicine have been streamlined in these guidelines.

Again, a major change has been made to the teaching hours in Community Medicine. For Phase I, 40 hours of teaching have been allotted with 24 hours of FAP. In the second phase, 35 hours of teaching and 24 hours of FAP were allotted. Again, similar to CBME 2023, major teaching–learning in Community Medicine will occur in Phase III, Part I at 150 hours and 36 hours of FAP. The community orientation module in foundation course has been reduced from 8 to 5 hours.

The competencies have undergone a major change, the first revision since they were introduced in GMER 2019. The number of topics remains unchanged at 20, but the competencies have drastically increased from 107 to 136. The maximum number of competencies is in the topic: Nutrition (22 competencies from earlier 8 competencies). The other topics which now have additional competencies include biostatistics, epidemiology, reproductive, maternal and child health, occupational health, geriatric services, disaster management, hospital waste management, health planning and management, health care of community, international health, and essential medicines [Table 2]. There is more clarity in the teaching–learning methods with inclusion of FAP and clinical posting. Another important feature is the introduction of certifiable skills. Ten competencies have certifiable skills. These include topics of sociology, health education, nutrition, biostatistics, and epidemiology. However, horizontal and vertical integration of Community Medicine with other specialities has not been described in the CBME 2024 guidelines.

DISCUSSION

The evolution of the curriculum of Community Medicine, especially with introduction of Small Group Teaching with a large proportion of hours devoted to it, is a welcome move toward better understanding of concepts but needs to be backed with adequate infrastructure in terms of small rooms and adequate faculty strength to engage these smaller batches. Further, for quality implementation, the faculty at all levels need to be abreast about the changing scenarios and be trained in the newer concepts of CBME.

Overall, a move toward shift from theory to a deeper understanding and practical application of the subject by the students is envisaged. This can be monitored and ensured by the multiple opportunities provided for assessment with special emphasis on certifiable skills.

The component of Family Adoption Program has created a new opportunity of community engagement for the students. As an early community exposure, the students would be oriented to the holistic aspects of health and disease and comprehend the interplay of various sociocultural factors.

CONCLUSION

The CBME with reference to Community Medicine is well spelt out, and for its implementation in letter and spirit, training of faculty, improvement of infrastructure, provision of adequate resources, ownership of all stake holders, and so on will play a significant role.
