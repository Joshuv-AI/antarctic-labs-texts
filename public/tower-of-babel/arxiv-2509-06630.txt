Positivity bounds from thermal field theory entropy
Xin-Yi Liu, Yongjun Xu

Abstract
We present an approach to deriving positivity bounds on effective field theories by analyzing the thermodynamic behavior of thermal quantum field systems. Focusing on scalar theories with higher-dimensional operators, we compute the finite-temperature entropy using thermal field theory techniques. We argue that consistency with fundamental thermodynamic principles—specifically, the expectation that entropy increases with the introduction of new degrees of freedom—imposes nontrivial constraints on Wilson coefficients. In particular, we show that the coefficient of the leading dimension-8 operator must be strictly positive. This thermodynamic perspective offers an alternative to traditional S-matrix-based derivations of positivity bounds and provides a complementary perspective into the interplay between entropy, unitarity, and causality in quantum field theory.

II
Basic idea

Consider a system in thermal equilibrium characterized by two widely separated mass scales: a heavy mass
M
and a light mass
m
. We examine the system at an intermediate temperature T satisfying
m\ll T\ll M
, where the heavy modes are Boltzmann suppressed and can be integrated out, while the light modes remain thermally active. In this regime, the full thermal entropy of the system can be approximated using an effective field theory that includes only the light degrees of freedom:

S_{m+M}\simeq S_{m\text{-EFT}}.

(1)

Naively, one expects that introducing new heavy degrees of freedom should only increase the thermal entropy, as more microscopic configurations become accessible. This intuition naturally leads to the inequality:

S_{m+M}>S_{m-\text{only}}.

(2)

At the classical level, it is natural to expect that the entropy of the full system
m+M
should exceed that of the light sector
m
alone. Motivated by this
inequality, one might be tempted to compute the thermal entropy within the
effective field theory and compare it with that of the free field theory,
anticipating that such a comparison would yield a corresponding positivity condition.

However, this classical intuition does not always hold in quantum theory,
as the situation becomes more subtle when quantum entanglement is involved.
For example, consider a combined system
A+B
prepared in a pure quantum state, so that the total entropy vanishes,
S_{A+B}=0
. If we only observe subsystem
A
by tracing out
B
, the reduced state of
A
generally becomes mixed.

In this case, although the total entropy is zero, the entropy of the observed
subsystem can still be nonzero due to quantum entanglement:

S_{A+B}=0\quad\text{but}\quad S_{A}>0.

(3)

Therefore, in addition to the thermal entropy of
m
and
M
, we must try to include the entanglement entropy between them. That is,

S_{m}=S_{m,\text{thermal}}+S_{m\rightarrow M,\text{entangled}},

(4)

and

S_{M}=S_{M,\text{thermal}}+S_{M\rightarrow m,\text{entangled}}.

(5)

This entanglement entropy is generally ultraviolet divergent and depends on the
choice of regulator, so it does not capture the thermal dynamical information
we aim to extract. We will encounter this divergence again when performing thermal
calculations. For our purposes, the only property we need to emphasize is that
the entanglement entropy between two subsystems is symmetric:

S_{m\rightarrow M,\text{entangled}}=S_{M\rightarrow m,\text{entangled}}.

(6)

We now invoke the Araki-Lieb inequality
Araki and Lieb 1970
;
Witten 2020
to
derive a bound on the total entropy:

S_{M+m}\,\geq\,\big|S_{m}-S_{M}\big|.

(7)

Equality is achieved only when the global state is pure or when one of the
subsystems is trivial (i.e., has zero entropy).

Combining Eqs. (
1
,
4
-
7
), at temperature
T
satisfying
m\ll T\ll M
, we finally obtain:

S_{m,\text{EFT}}>|S_{m,\text{thermal}}-S_{M,\text{thermal}}|.

(8)

At temperatures much lower than the mass of a heavy particle
(T\ll M)
, the high-energy degrees of freedom are difficult to excite. As a result, the thermal entropy is exponentially suppressed:
S_{M,thermal}\propto T^{\frac{3}{2}}e^{-\frac{M}{T}}
, This contribution can be approximated as zero. Therefore, the inequality becomes:

S_{m,\text{EFT}}>S_{m,\text{thermal}}.

(9)

This inequality fits our physical intuition (
2
), but is now rigorously justified after accounting for the contribution from entanglement entropy.

III
Calculation of Thermal entropy

We begin by considering a simple scalar field theory that possesses a shift symmetry, which physically corresponds to taking the massless limit
(m\to 0)
. Such a field often emerges as a Goldstone boson in effective field theories, representing the low-energy fluctuations around spontaneously broken continuous symmetries. Throughout this section, we adopt natural units with
k_{B}=\hbar=1
.

The low-energy effective Lagrangian for such a scalar field
\phi
up to dimension 8 operator is generally given by:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{M}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\partial\phi\right)^{2}+\frac{c}{M^{4}}\left(\partial\phi\right)^{4}+\cdots.

(10)

where
c
is a dimensionless coupling constant, and
M
denotes a high-energy cutoff scale in the effective theory. The first term corresponds to the standard kinetic energy of a free scalar field, while the second term represents the leading-order self-interactions of the field.

To incorporate finite-temperature effects, we consider the system in thermal
equilibrium with a heat bath at temperature
T
, which allows us to compute the
statistical properties of the scalar field within a consistent quantum field
theoretical framework.

From a theoretical perspective, to prepare a thermal equilibrium state at
temperature
T=\tfrac{1}{\beta}
, we can employ the Euclidean path integral
formalism on a compact manifold with temporal circumference
\beta
.
Concretely, this approach involves the following steps
Laine and Vuorinen 2016
:

•

Imaginary Time (Wick Rotation): We replace real time
t
by imaginary (Euclidean) time
\tau
through the transformation:

\displaystyle\tau\equiv it.

(11)

This step transforms the Minkowski spacetime into a Euclidean spacetime, making the field theory resemble a classical statistical system.

•

Euclidean Lagrangian: Under Wick rotation, the Lagrangian transforms into its Euclidean counterpart. The Euclidean Lagrangian
L_{E}
is related to the original Minkowski Lagrangian by:

\displaystyle L_{E}(\tau,x)\equiv-\mathcal{L}_{M}(t,x),

(12)

•

Periodic Boundary Conditions and Temperature: At finite temperature, the Euclidean time direction becomes compactified with period
\beta
, where

\displaystyle\tau\sim\tau+\beta,\text{ where }\beta\equiv\frac{1}{T}.

(13)

This periodicity reflects the thermal equilibrium condition. And this emerges naturally from the trace over states in the definition of the partition function:

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}(T)=\text{Tr}(e^{-\beta H}).

(14)

Following these steps, the Euclidean action for the scalar theory becomes:

\displaystyle S_{E}

\displaystyle=S_{0}+S_{I}

\displaystyle=\int^{\beta}_{0}d\tau\int_{\textbf{x}}\left[\frac{1}{2}\left(\partial\phi\right)^{2}-\frac{c}{M^{4}}\left(\partial\phi\right)^{4}+\cdots.\right]

(15)

where we clearly separate the free action
S_{0}
(quadratic in
\phi
) from the interaction terms
S_{I}
(higher-order terms in
\phi
). Note that in the zero-temperature limit
(\beta\to\infty)
, the periodicity condition disappears, and we naturally recover the standard Euclidean quantum field theory defined on infinite spacetime.

A crucial motivation for studying this theory at finite temperature is understanding how thermal entropy, a fundamental quantity describing the disorder and microscopic states of the system, constrains the properties of the theory. In particular, as we shall see from detailed calculations, consistency conditions related to the entropy demand the positivity of certain coupling constants. In this model, we will see that this implies:

\displaystyle c>0.

(16)

Thus, we can find that thermal field theory not only provides a rich physical framework to study quantum systems at finite temperature but also imposes powerful theoretical constraints on allowed interactions.

To quantify these ideas precisely, we construct the thermal partition function:

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}(T)=C\int\mathcal{D}\phi e^{-S_{E}}=C\int\mathcal{D}\phi e^{\left(-S_{0}-S_{I}\right)}.

(17)

Given that the coupling
\frac{c}{M^{4}}

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}(T)

\displaystyle=C\int\mathcal{D}\phi e^{-S_{0}}\left[1-S_{\mathrm{I}}+\frac{1}{2}S_{\mathrm{I}}^{2}-\frac{1}{6}S_{\mathrm{I}}^{3}+\cdots\right]

IV
the connection to causality and unitarity

Previous work on positivity bounds has primarily focused on analyzing
2\rightarrow 2
scattering amplitudes to extract constraints from fundamental principles such as causality and unitarity. Causality requires that the scattering amplitude be analytic in the upper half of the complex energy plane, while unitarity implies the optical theorem, which states that the imaginary part of the forward scattering amplitude must be non-negative. By combining these principles within the framework of dispersion relations, one can derive positivity conditions on low-energy parameters of the EFT, such as Wilson coefficients. These coefficients appear in the derivative expansion of the EFT Lagrangian, and positivity bounds require certain combinations of them to be strictly positive.

In our context, unitarity is reflected in the preservation of the trace of the density matrix under unitary time evolution. That is, the total probability of all physical outcomes must remain normalized:
\frac{d}{dt}\mathrm{Tr}(\rho)=0.
This property underlies the validity of entropy-related inequalities such as the Araki-Lieb inequality.

Causality, while more subtle, is encoded in the
i\epsilon
prescription of QFT, which ensures microcausality—the requirement that fields commute (for bosons) or anticommute (for fermions) at spacelike separation. Specifically, for a scalar field:

[\phi(x),\phi(y)]=0\quad\text{for}\quad(x-y)^{2}>0.

This condition guarantees that events separated by spacelike intervals cannot influence each other, consistent with special relativity.
This microcausality condition arises from the analytic structure of the Feynman propagator:

G_{F}(x-y)=\int\frac{d^{4}p}{(2\pi)^{4}}\frac{ie^{-ip\cdot(x-y)}}{p^{2}-m^{2}+i\epsilon},

where the
i\epsilon
shifts the poles away from the real axis. The field commutator can be written as the difference between the Feynman and Dyson (time-reversed) propagators:

[\phi(x),\phi(y)]=G_{F}(x-y)-G_{F}(y-x),

and this vanishes for spacelike separations due to the symmetry of contour deformation in the complex
p^{0}
plane, enforced by the
i\epsilon
prescription.

The
i\epsilon
prescription can also be understood as a regularization of the path integral. One may equivalently insert a term
-i\epsilon\phi^{2}(x)
into the action to ensure convergence for large field amplitudes:

S\rightarrow S-i\epsilon\int d^{4}x\,\phi^{2}(x)
.
When performing a Wick rotation to Euclidean space, the prescription dictates that the time variable be rotated anticlockwise in the complex plane:
\tau\rightarrow it
,
which ensures that poles in the complex energy plane are avoided appropriately. As a result, both causality and unitarity are preserved in thermal field theory, and they underlie the entropy-based positivity bounds derived in this work.

V
Conclusion and discussion

In this work, we computed the thermal entropy of a scalar field theory with a shift symmetry and demonstrated that the requirement of entropy positivity imposes a nontrivial constraint on the sign of higher-dimensional operators in the low-energy effective field theory. In particular, we showed that the coefficient of the leading dimension-8 operator must be strictly positive.

Our derivation was based entirely on thermodynamic consistency, rather than relying on scattering amplitudes or dispersion relations. This offers a complementary perspective on EFT constraints, highlighting the role of entropy and the thermal partition function in encoding information about underlying physical principles such as unitarity and causality.

The thermal entropy approach developed here can be generalized in several directions. For example, it would be natural to extend our analysis to include fermionic or gauge degrees of freedom, or to explore the implications of gravitational interactions. In particular, effective theories involving gravity often pose challenges to traditional positivity bounds due to the presence of massless poles and the absence of an S-matrix in asymptotically de Sitter or AdS spacetimes. Since our approach does not rely on the flat-space S-matrix, it may offer a path forward in these contexts.

After posting v1 of this manuscript, we became aware of a closely related preprint by Fernández-Sarmiento, Penco, and Rosen
Fernández-Sarmiento et al. 2025
through correspondence from Riccardo Penco. Their analysis formulates an entropy-positivity condition in terms of a negative thermal shift of the grand potential and tests it across several systems, together with a discussion of regimes where the bound may not apply. Our approach starts from a different argument: by beginning with the Araki-Lieb inequality and an explicit leading-order thermal QFT computation, we demonstrate this condition in a Lorentz-invariant scalar EFT and obtain the positive sign of the leading dimension-8 operator. We also outline the assumptions of our derivation and its regime of validity within this setup.
