Curvature measures and the sub-Riemannian Gauss-Bonnet theorem
Davide Barilari, Eugenio Bellini, Andrea Pinamonti

Abstract
. We adopt a measure-theoretic perspective on the Riemannian approximation scheme proving a sub-Riemannian Gauss-Bonnet theorem for surfaces in 3D contact manifolds. We show that the zero-order term in the limit is a singular measure supported on isolated characteristic points. In particular, this provides a unified interpretation of previous results obtained in [ BTV17 , GHVM25 ] . Moreover we give natural geometric conditions under which our result holds, namely when the surface admits characteristic points of finite order of degeneracy. This notion, which we introduce, extends the concept of mildly degenerate characteristic points of [ Ros23 ] for the Heisenberg group. As a byproduct, we prove that the mean curvature around an isolated characteristic point of finite order of degeneracy is locally integrable. In particular, this positively answers a question by [ DGD12 ] for analytic surfaces in every analytic 3D contact manifold.



1.
Introduction

Embedded surfaces play a fundamental role in the theory of contact structures. Since the seminal works of Giroux
[
Gir91
,
Gir00
]
, they have become central tools in the classification of contact structures, marking a turning point in the study of 3-dimensional contact topology. From a different perspective, works such as
[
Bla10
,
EKM12
]
have explored the interplay between contact and Riemannian geometry, introducing analytical techniques that have deepened our understanding of the geometry of contact manifolds.

The study of surfaces in contact 3-manifolds has also begun to attract attention from the point of view of sub-Riemannian geometry. Contributions such as
[
BBC22
,
BB23
,
BBCH21
]
have addressed the existence and properties of induced metric structures and the corresponding stochastic geometric evolutions, while other works have explored the tightness of distributions in terms of sub-Riemannian curvature invariants
[
ABBR24
]
.

Within the sub-Riemannian framework, one of the main challenges is the establishment of a canonical notion of Gaussian curvature and of a Gauss-Bonnet type theorem.
Recent contributions which are close in spirit with our investigation were achieved in
[
BTV17
,
GHVM25
,
DV16
,
Vel23
]
(cf. also
[
ABS08
]
for the almost-Riemannian setting). In particular, the results in
[
BTV17
,
GHVM25
]
rely on the so-called
Riemannian approximation scheme
, i.e., a family of Riemannian metrics
g^{\varepsilon}
converging to the sub-Riemannian one. This is a powerful tool to transport results from classical Riemannian geometry into the sub-Riemannian context.

Our goal in this paper is to continue the above-mentioned investigation by developing a
measure-theoretic viewpoint
on the Gauss–Bonnet theorem for surfaces embedded in
3
-dimensional contact sub-Riemannian manifolds, thereby providing a unified perspective on the previous results.

The main novelty of our approach is that, instead of focusing on the limit of the Gaussian curvature functions
K^{\varepsilon}
associated with the Riemannian approximation scheme
g^{\varepsilon}
, we directly look at the limit of the Gaussian
curvature measures

K^{\varepsilon}\sigma^{\varepsilon}
, where
\sigma^{\varepsilon}
is the corresponding surface area form. In doing so, we are able to characterize both an absolutely continuous and a singular part of this limit, the singular part being concentrated on characteristic points. The notion of a curvature measure, as employed here, is inspired by the seminal work of Federer
[
Fed59
]
.

1.1.
Characteristic vector field and its singularities

In order to state our results, we review some preliminaries on contact sub-Riemannian manifolds. More details are given in Sections
2.1
,
2.2
.

A three-dimensional contact sub-Riemannian manifold is a triple
(M,\mathcal{D},g)
, where
M
is a smooth three-dimensional manifold,
\mathcal{D}
is a
contact distribution
and
g
a
sub-Riemannian metric
, i.e., a smooth metric on
\mathcal{D}
. Given an oriented surface
S
embedded in
M
, the intersection
TS\cap\mathcal{D}
defines a singular field of directions, whose singularities are the points
p\in S
where
T_{p}S=\mathcal{D}_{p}
, called characteristic points. The characteristic set
\Sigma(S)
is the set of characteristic points of
S
.

The sub-Riemannian metric together with the orientation of
S
induce a choice of a unique
characteristic vector field
, which is a field
X\in\mathfrak{X}(S)
satisfying

(2)

\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\{X_{p}\}=\begin{cases}T_{p}S\cap\mathcal{D}_{p},&p\notin\Sigma(S),\\
0,\qquad\qquad\quad&p\in\Sigma(S).\end{cases}

We recall that given a smooth manifold
S
and a smooth vector field
X\in\mathfrak{X}(S)
vanishing at
q\in S
, the differential of
X
at
q
is the linear map
D_{q}X:T_{q}S\to T_{q}S
defined by
D_{q}X(Y)=[Y,X]_{q}
.

We say that a characteristic point is
degenerate
if
\det(D_{q}X)=0
, and
non-degenerate
otherwise.

Remark 1.1
.

One can prove that, since
\mathcal{D}
a contact distribution, we have that
\mathrm{div}(X)|_{q}\neq 0
. The latter condition is independent of the measure used to compute the divergence. Being
\mathrm{trace}(D_{q}X)=\mathrm{div}(X)|_{q}
, at degenerate characteristic points one necessarily has
\dim\ker(D_{q}X)=1
.

For more details we refer to Section
2.2
(cf. in particular Remark
2.2
).

1.2.
The Riemannian approximation

It is well-known
[
ABB20
]
that the sub-Riemannian metric
g
induces a canonical choice of a vector field
f_{0}\in\mathfrak{X}(M)
called the Reeb vector field, which is globally transverse to the distribution
\mathcal{D}
(see
Section

2.1
for a more detailed definition). We consider the family of Riemannian metrics
g^{\varepsilon}
approximating
g
for which

(3)

g^{\varepsilon}|_{\mathcal{D}}=g,\qquad g^{\varepsilon}(\mathcal{D},f_{0})=0,\qquad g^{\varepsilon}(f_{0},f_{0})=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}.

Given a compact orientable surface
S
embedded in
M
, we denote with
K^{\varepsilon}
and
\sigma^{\varepsilon}
the Gaussian curvature and area form of the Riemannian surface
(S,g^{\varepsilon}|_{S})
, respectively. Our approach to achieve a Gauss-Bonnet theorem consists in studying the family of signed Borel measures
K^{\varepsilon}\sigma^{\varepsilon}
as
\varepsilon\to 0
. Unfortunately, the latter sequence does not converge as
\varepsilon\to 0
. However, the rescaled sequence

(4)

\sqrt{\varepsilon}\,K^{\varepsilon}\sigma^{\varepsilon}

strongly converges in
C^{1}(S)^{*}
, as
\varepsilon\to 0
, to a signed Borel measure, which we denote
\mu_{-1}
, with vanishing total mass. This limit is regular away from the characteristic set, while near each isolated characteristic point it concentrates masses in a way dictated by the local behaviour of the characteristic foliation. Nonetheless, the existence of the limit measure
\mu_{-1}
allows us to identify and remove the divergent term from
K^{\varepsilon}\sigma^{\varepsilon}
: in our main result we prove that the difference

(5)

K^{\varepsilon}\sigma^{\varepsilon}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\mu_{-1}

strongly converges in
C^{1}(S)^{*}
to a sum of Dirac deltas supported at the characteristic points, each weighted by the
topological index
of the characteristic vector field.

Our main result can be stated under the assumption that the characteristic points are
of finite order of degeneracy
. This means that either the characteristic points are non-degenerate, or, if degenerate, their order of degeneracy is finite along a (suitably defined) curve tangent to the kernel of the differential. The notion introduced here generalizes the concept of mildly degenerate characteristic points, as defined in
[
Ros23
]
for the Heisenberg group. We refer to Section
1.3
after the statement for the formal definition

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
(M,\mathcal{D},g)
be a contact sub-Riemannian manifold and
S
be an embedded, compact and orientable surface, with characteristic vector field
X
. Denote
K^{\varepsilon}
and
\sigma^{\varepsilon}
the Gaussian curvature and area form induced on
S
by the metric
g^{\varepsilon}
, respectively.
Assume that the characteristic set consists of points which are of finite order of degeneracy. Then the following limit of signed Borel measures exists in the strong
C^{1}(S)^{*}
sense

(6)

\mu_{-1}:=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\sqrt{\varepsilon}K^{\varepsilon}\sigma^{\varepsilon}.

Furthermore the characteristic set is finite
\Sigma(S)=\{q_{1},\dots,q_{\ell}\}\subset S
and the following limit exists in the strong
C^{1}(S)^{*}
sense

(7)

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\left(K^{\varepsilon}\sigma^{\varepsilon}-\frac{\mu_{-1}}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\right)=2\pi\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\mathrm{ind}(q_{i})\delta_{q_{i}},

where
\delta_{q_{i}}
denotes a Dirac delta centred at
q_{i}\in S
and
\mathrm{ind}(q_{i})
denotes the index of
X
at
q_{i}
.

Remark 1.3
.

We stress that characteristic points of finite order of degeneracy are automatically isolated (cf. Theorem
1.10
), therefore, by compactness of
S
,
\Sigma(S)
is finite. For non-compact surfaces
S
, characteristic points with finite order degeneracy form a discrete set, hence one can obtain the validity of the statement of Theorem
1.2
by considering the convergence in
C^{1}_{c}(S)^{*}
, i.e., with test functions that have compact support. We stress that in this case (
7
) in particular implies that for
\varphi\in C^{1}_{c}(S)
one has

(8)

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{S}\varphi\left(K^{\varepsilon}\sigma^{\varepsilon}-\frac{\mu_{-1}}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\right)=2\pi\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\varphi(q_{i})\mathrm{ind}(q_{i})\delta_{q_{i}},

the sum in the right hand side being finite since
\varphi
has compact support.

Remark 1.4
.

The measure
\mu_{-1}
can be computed explicitly as in
Remark

6.1
. Notice that the Riemannian Gauss-Bonnet theorem implies that for every
\varepsilon>0
one has

\sqrt{\varepsilon}\int_{S}K^{\varepsilon}\sigma^{\varepsilon}=2\pi\sqrt{\varepsilon}\chi(S)

where
\chi(S)
is the Euler characteristic of
S
. Hence taking the limit when
\varepsilon\to 0
one gets by (
6
) the following consequence, thus recovering the statements of
[
BTV17
]
(cf. also
[
Vel23
]
and
[
GHVM25
]
)

(9)

\int_{S}\mu_{-1}=0.

1.3.
Characteristic points of finite order

The notion of characteristic point of finite order of degeneracy, which we discuss here, extends the concept of mildly degenerate characteristic point introduced in
[
Ros23
]
for the specific case of the Heisenberg group. This extension is not immediate, since in the Heisenberg case the definition relies in an essential way on the existence of a Lie group structure. In the general setting different techniques are required.

Our approach not only permits the extension of the notion to arbitrary spaces, but also yields finer structural results. Indeed, we show that points of finite order of degeneracy can only occur isolated, and we give sharp estimates on the norm of the characteristic vector field in their vicinity. Finally, we compute the index of the characteristic vector field at such singularity in terms of invariants.

We start by defining horizontal kernel extensions at degenerate characteristic points, i.e., characteristic points
q
where
\det(D_{q}X)=0
.

Definition 1.5
.

Let
S
be a surface embedded in a 3D contact sub-Riemannian manifold, with characteristic vector field
X
. Let
q\in S
be a degenerate characteristic point. A
horizontal kernel extension
at
q
is an arc-length parametrized horizontal smooth curve
\gamma:(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\to S
satisfying

(10)

\gamma(0)=q,\qquad\dot{\gamma}(0)\in\ker(D_{q}X).

Recall by Remark
1.1
that the characteristic vector field
X
at degenerate characteristic points satisfies
\det(D_{q}X)=0
and
\dim\ker(D_{q}X)=1
. Hence by a direct application of the center manifold theorem one can prove the following result (a more detailed proof is given in Section
3.1
).

Lemma 1.6
.

Let
q\in S
be a degenerate characteristic point, i.e.,
\det(D_{q}X)=0
. Then a horizontal kernel extension
\gamma:(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\to S
always exists.

In general, a horizontal kernel extension is not unique. The definition of order of degeneracy of a characteristic point is based on the following observation.

Proposition 1.7
.

Let
q\in S
be a degenerate characteristic point, i.e.,
\det(D_{q}X)=0
. Then

(i)

the order of vanishing

Given a function
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
such that
f(0)=0
, the order of vanishing of
f
at
0

k:=\inf\left\{i\in\mathbb{N}:f^{(i)}(0)\neq 0\right\}
.

t=0
of the following smooth function

(11)

\lambda_{\gamma}:(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\to\mathbb{R},\qquad\lambda_{\gamma}(t)=g(X,\dot{\gamma}(t)),

does not depend on the choice of the horizontal kernel extension
\gamma
,

(ii)

if
\lambda_{\gamma}
has finite order of vanishing
k\in\mathbb{N}
at
t=0
, with
k
odd, then the following quantity

(12)

\Lambda^{(k)}(q):=\left.\frac{d^{k}}{dt^{k}}\lambda_{\gamma}(t)\right|_{t=0},

does not depend on the choice of the horizontal kernel extension.

Definition 1.8
.

We define the
order of degeneracy
of the characteristic point
q
, denoted
\mathrm{ord}(q)
, as the order of vanishing at
t=0
of the function
\lambda_{\gamma}
in (
11
) for some horizontal kernel extension
\gamma
.

Remark 1.9
.

We highlight here that if the order of degeneracy of
q
is an odd integer
k\in\mathbb{N}
, the quantity
\Lambda^{(k)}(q)
defined in (
12
) is well-defined, while if the order of degeneracy of
q
is an even integer, the quantity
\Lambda^{(k)}(q)
is well-defined only up to a sign (cf. Section
3
).

We stress that for a degenerate characteristic point
q
one necessarily has
\mathrm{ord}(q)\geq 2
, cf. Lemma
3.1
. By abuse of notation, we set
\mathrm{ord}(q)=1
for non-degenerate characteristic points
q
. We say that a characteristic point has finite order of degeneracy if
\mathrm{ord}(q)<+\infty
.

Characteristic points of finite order of degeneracy satisfy the following properties.

Theorem 1.10
.

Let
S
be an oriented surface embedded in a 3D contact sub-Riemannian manifold, with characteristic vector field
X
. Let
q\in S
be a characteristic point of order of degeneracy
k\geq 2
, then:

(i)

q
is an isolated characteristic point,

(ii)

there exist local coordinates
(x,y)
near
q
and a positive constant
C>0
such that

(13)

q=(0,0),\qquad C^{-1}\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2k}}\leq|X|\leq C\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2k}},

(iii)

the index of
q
satisfies

(14)

\mathrm{ind}(q)=\begin{cases}\mathrm{sign}\left(\mathrm{div}_{q}(X)\Lambda^{(k)}(q)\right),&\text{if}\,\,k\,\,\text{odd},\\
0,\qquad&\text{if}\,\,k\,\,\text{even}.\end{cases}

We stress that characteristic points with finite order of degeneracy are isolated. In the analytic case, the converse also holds: isolated characteristic points have finite order of degeneracy, cf.
7.4
.

Remark 1.11
.

We stress that in Theorem
1.10
one has
k\geq 2
. Actually one can include the case of non-degenerate characteristic points, i.e., when
k=1
. Indeed claims (i) and (ii) hold thanks to
\det(D_{q}X)\neq 0
, while in (iii) one should suitably reinterpret the quantity
\Lambda^{(k)}(q)
for
k=1
as follows

(15)

\Lambda^{(1)}(q)=\frac{\det(D_{q}X)}{\mathrm{div}_{q}(X)}=\frac{\det(D_{q}X)}{\mathrm{trace}(D_{q}X)}.

All the quantities
\Lambda^{(k)}(q)
for all
k\geq 1
can be defined and computed in a unified way through an auxiliary affine connection as follows.

Proposition 1.12
.

Let
q\in S
be a characteristic point of order
k\geq 2
. Let
\nabla
be the Levi-Civita connection on
TS
associated with
g^{1}|_{S}
. There exists a neighbourhood
U\subset S
of
q
such that the following linear endomorphism is (fibrewise) diagonalizable

(16)

\nabla X:TS|_{U}\to TS|_{U},\qquad Y\mapsto\nabla_{Y}X.

In particular
\nabla X
admits an eigenvector field
v_{0}\in\mathfrak{X}(U)
satisfying
v_{0}|_{q}\in\ker D_{q}X
and
|v_{0}|_{q}=1
. Then

(17)

\Lambda^{(k)}(q)=\left.v_{0}^{(k-1)}\left(\frac{\det(\nabla X)}{\mathrm{trace}(\nabla X)}\right)\right|_{q}.

Furthermore,
k
is the smallest natural number such that the right-hand side of (
17
) is non zero.

Notice that for
k=1
the vector
v_{0}
is not defined but (
17
) makes sense and coincides with (
15
).

As it will be clear from the proof (see Section
7.1
), the previous result is actually independent on the choice of the affine connection
\nabla
used to compute (
17
).

1.4.
Consequences: integrability

A direct consequence of item
(ii)
of
Theorem

1.10
, being the function
(x^{2}+y^{2k})^{-1/2}
integrable near the origin in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
for
k\geq 1
, is the following.

Corollary 1.13
.

Let
S
be a surface embedded in a 3D contact sub-Riemannian manifold with a smooth measure
\sigma
. Let
X
be the characteristic vector field. Assume all characteristic points have finite order of degeneracy, then

(18)

\frac{1}{|X|}\in L^{1}_{loc}(S,\sigma).

This theorem extends
[
Ros23
, Theorem 4.5]
, whose proof exploits the isometries of the Heisenberg group. Our approach relies on the normal form of
4.1
.

Corollary

1.13
is related with integrability properties of the sub-Riemannian mean curvature
\mathcal{H}
of
S
, which can be defined as the limit of mean curvatures of the Riemannian approximations, see
[
BB24
]
. The estimate
|\mathcal{H}|\leq C|X|^{-1}
follows from a technical but standard observation, cf.
[
Ros23
, Section 2.1]
and
[
DGN12
, Prop. 3.5]
. In the analytic case, we prove the following.

Corollary 1.14
.

Let
S
be an analytic surface embedded in an analytic 3D contact sub-Riemannian manifold with a smooth measure
\sigma
.
Let
\mathcal{H}
be the sub-Riemannian mean curvature. If every characteristic point is isolated then

(19)

\mathcal{H}\in L^{1}_{loc}(S,\sigma).

The previous result extends
[
Ros23
, Theorem 4.7]
concerning a conjecture in
[
DGN12
]
.

1.5.
Consequences: Euler characteristic

Combining Poincaré-Hopf theorem with
(iii)
of
Theorem

1.10
we can compute the Euler characteristic of a surface in terms of the invariants
\Lambda^{(k)}(q)
.

Corollary 1.15
.

Let
S
be a compact oriented surface embedded in a 3D contact sub-Riemannian manifold. Assume that every characteristic point has finite order of degeneracy. Then the characteristic set is finite
\Sigma(S)=\{q_{1},\dots,q_{\ell}\}
, and

(20)

\chi(S)=\sum_{k_{i}\,\mathrm{odd}}\mathrm{sign}\left(\mathrm{div}_{q_{i}}(X)\Lambda^{(k_{i})}(q_{i})\right),

where
k_{i}=\mathrm{ord}(q_{i})
, and
\Lambda^{(k_{i})}(q_{i})
are the invariants defined in (
12
).

Remark 1.16
.

We stress that if all characteristic points are non-degenerate, i.e.,
k_{i}=1
for all
i=1,\ldots,\ell
, then, thanks to (
15
), formula (
20
) reduces to the classical identity

(21)

\chi(S)=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\mathrm{sign}\left(\det(D_{q_{i}}X)\right).

Going back to our main Theorem
1.2
, the atomic measure appearing in (
7
) is obtained as limit of the Gaussian curvature measures
K^{\varepsilon}\sigma^{\varepsilon}
of the Riemannian surfaces
(S,g^{\varepsilon}|_{S})
. It is therefore natural to compare their masses with the pointwise limit of the Gaussian curvature
K^{\varepsilon}
at characteristic points.

However, it is known that for
\varepsilon\to 0
the Gaussian curvature
K^{\varepsilon}
at characteristic points diverges, and it converges only after an appropriate rescaling. More precisely, let
q\in S
be a characteristic point, it was shown in
[
BBC22
]
that

(22)

\widehat{K}(q):=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\varepsilon^{2}K^{\varepsilon}(q)=-1+\frac{\det(D_{q}X)}{\mathrm{trace}(D_{q}X)^{2}}.

One can compare such a limit with our results by extending the function
\widehat{K}
near characteristic points through the following definition

(23)

\widehat{K}(q)=-1+\frac{\det(\nabla X|_{q})}{\mathrm{trace}^{2}(\nabla X|_{q})},

where
\nabla
is the Levi-Civita connection of the metric
g^{1}|_{S}
. In particular, restating Corollary
1.15
, one can compute the Euler characteristic in terms of this curvature invariant
\widehat{K}
and its derivatives.

Proposition 1.17
.

Let
S
be an oriented surface embedded in a 3D contact sub-Riemannian manifold with characteristic vector field
X
. Let
q\in S
be a characteristic point of finite odd order of degeneracy
k\in\mathbb{N}
, and let
v_{0}
is the eigenvector field defined in
1.12
. Then

(24)

\mathrm{div}_{q}(X)\Lambda^{(k)}(q)=v_{0}^{(k-1)}\left(\widehat{K}+1\right)\Big|_{q},

where
\widehat{K}
is the function defined in (
23
). If
S
is compact and all characteristic points are of finite order of degeneracy, then

(25)

\chi(S)=\sum_{k_{i}\,\,\text{odd}}\mathrm{sign}\left(v_{0}^{(k_{i}-1)}\left(\widehat{K}+1\right)\Big|_{q_{i}}\right),

where
\{k_{1},\dots,k_{\ell}\}
are the orders of the characteristic points
\{q_{1},\dots,q_{\ell}\}
.

Again, we stress that if all characteristic points are non-degenerate
k_{i}=1
for all
i
and the formula (
25
) reduces to (
21
) since
\mathrm{sign}(\widehat{K}+1)=\mathrm{sign}(\det D_{q}X)
.

1.6.
An example: the horizontal plane in the Heisenberg group

It is interesting to illustrate the discrete contribution to the limiting measure in Theorem
1.2
for the explicit example of the plane
\{z=0\}
in the three-dimensional Heisenberg group.

Let
(\mathbb{R}^{3},\mathcal{D},g)
be the Heisenberg group: the contact distribution
\mathcal{D}
is expressed as the kernel of a differential form
\omega
which, in cylindrical coordinates
x=r\cos\theta,y=r\sin\theta,z
, reads

(26)

\omega=dz+\frac{r^{2}}{2}d\theta.

The extended metrics (
3
), whose restrictions to
\mathcal{D}
coincide with the sub-Riemannian metric
g
, have the expression

(27)

g^{\varepsilon}=dr^{2}+r^{2}d\theta^{2}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\omega\otimes\omega.

Let
S=\{(x,y,z)\mid z=0\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Substituting the expression of
\omega
into the one of
g^{\varepsilon}
and imposing
dz=0
we obtain

(28)

g^{\varepsilon}|_{S}=dr^{2}+f_{\varepsilon}(r)^{2}d\theta^{2},\qquad\text{where}\qquad f_{\varepsilon}(r)=r\sqrt{1+\frac{r^{2}}{4\varepsilon}}.

A standard computation shows that

(29)

K^{\varepsilon}\sigma^{\varepsilon}=-f^{\prime\prime}_{\varepsilon}(r)drd\theta=-\frac{r\left(r^{2}+6\varepsilon\right)}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}\left(r^{2}+4\varepsilon\right)^{3/2}}drd\theta.

One can then explicitly compute the limit

(30)

\mu_{-1}:=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\sqrt{\varepsilon}K^{\varepsilon}\sigma^{\varepsilon}=-drd\theta.

In this specific example, the statement of Theorem
1.2
can be proved by a direct computation and shows that the discrete part of the measure is a delta in the origin, which is characteristic.

Lemma 1.18
.

For every
\varphi\in C^{1}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, the following equality holds

(31)

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\varphi\left(K^{\varepsilon}\sigma^{\varepsilon}-\frac{\mu_{-1}}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\right)=2\pi\varphi(0),

where the measures
K^{\varepsilon}\sigma^{\varepsilon}
and
\mu_{-1}
are defined as in (
29
) and (
30
)

The proof is an explicit computation and is contained in Appendix
B
.

2.
Surfaces in 3D contact sub-Riemannian manifolds

We recall some preliminaries on contact sub-Riemannian manifolds and fix our notation. We refer to
[
Gei08
]
and
[
ABB20
]
for introductions to contact and sub-Riemannian geometry, respectively.

2.1.
Contact sub-Riemannian manifolds

A three-dimensional contact sub-Riemannian manifold is a triple
(M,\mathcal{D},g)
, where
M
is a smooth three-dimensional manifold,
\mathcal{D}
is a
contact distribution
and
g
a
sub-Riemannian metric
, i.e., a smooth metric on
\mathcal{D}
. We recall that a plane field
\mathcal{D}
is a contact distribution if locally
\mathcal{D}=\ker\omega
for some one-form
\omega
satisfying
d\omega|_{\mathcal{D}}
is non-degenerate. In dimension three this is equivalent to requiring
\mathcal{D}+[\mathcal{D},\mathcal{D}]=TM
.

A sub-Riemannian contact manifold is called co-orientable if the one-form satisfying
\mathcal{D}=\ker\omega
can be chosen globally. In this case the three-form
\omega\wedge d\omega
is a global volume form and
M
is also orientable. For simplicity in what follows we always assume the contact structure to be co-oriented.

The Reeb vector field associated with a contact form
\omega
is the vector field
f_{0}\in\mathfrak{X}(M)
uniquely determined by the following two equations

(32)

\omega(f_{0})=1,\qquad d\omega(f_{0},\cdot)=0.

The contact form
\omega
is called normalized with respect to the sub-Riemannian metric if it satisfies

(33)

d\omega(f_{1},f_{2})=1,

for every positively oriented orthonormal frame for
\mathcal{D}
(i.e.,
d\omega|_{\mathcal{D}}
is the area form defined by the metric
g
).
Without further notice, in the rest of the paper we always assume
\omega
to be normalized.

Given a contact sub-Riemannian manifold one can define the length of an absolutely continuous curve
\gamma:[0,1]\to M
which is
horizontal
(i.e., almost everywhere tangent to
\mathcal{D}
) as

(34)

\mathrm{length}(\gamma)=\int_{0}^{1}\|\dot{\gamma}(t)\|_{g}dt,

and the sub-Riemannian distance
d_{SR}:M\times M\to\mathbb{R}
as follows

(35)

d_{SR}(q_{0},q_{1})=\inf\{\mathrm{length}(\gamma)\mid\omega(\dot{\gamma})=0,\,\gamma(0)=q_{0},\,\gamma(1)=q_{1}\},\qquad\forall\,q_{0},q_{1}\in M.

where the infimum is over all horizontal absolutely continuous curve joining the two points.
It is well-known that
d_{SR}
is actually a distance function since
\mathcal{D}
is bracket-generating, see
[
ABB20
, Ch. 3]
.

Any sub-Riemannian space can be seen as a Gromov-Hausdorff limit of Riemannian spaces. In the contact sub-Riemannian case there exists a special family of approximating Riemannian metrics.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
(M,\mathcal{D},g)
be a contact sub-Riemannnian manifold and let
f_{0}
be the associated Reeb vector field. For any
\varepsilon>0
we define the Riemannian metric
g^{\varepsilon}
as the unique one satisfying

(36)

g^{\varepsilon}|_{\mathcal{D}}=g,\qquad g^{\varepsilon}(\mathcal{D},f_{0})=0,\qquad g^{\varepsilon}(f_{0},f_{0})=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}.

Furthermore, if
S\subset M
is an embedded oriented surface, we denote by
\sigma^{\varepsilon}
the area form of the oriented Riemannian surface
(S,g^{\varepsilon}|_{S})
, i.e., the differential two-form satisfying

(37)

\sigma^{\varepsilon}(v^{\varepsilon}_{1},v^{\varepsilon}_{2})=1.

for every positively oriented orthonormal frame
v^{\varepsilon}_{1},v^{\varepsilon}_{2}
of
S
.

2.2.
Surfaces in three-dimensional contact sub-Riemannian manifolds

Let
S
be a surface embedded in a co-oriented three-dimensional contact manifold
(M,\mathcal{D})
. The intersection
TS\cap\mathcal{D}
defines a field of directions, whose singularities are the points
p\in S
where
T_{p}S=\mathcal{D}_{p}
, and are called characteristic points. The characteristic set
\Sigma(S)
is the set of characteristic points. We recall that
\Sigma(S)
is contained in a one-dimensional submanifold of
S
, hence has measure zero
[
BBC22
]
.

Let
\omega\in\Omega^{1}(M)
be a contact form. If the surface is oriented by some area form
\sigma\in\Omega^{2}(S)
, then we can define the characteristic vector field as the unique vector field
X\in\mathfrak{X}(S)
satisfying

(38)

\iota_{X}\sigma=\omega|_{S}.

The characteristic vector field vanishes at characteristic points:

(39)

\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\{X_{p}\}=\begin{cases}T_{p}S\cap\mathcal{D}_{p},\quad&p\notin\Sigma(S),\\
0,&p\in\Sigma(S).\end{cases}

For a given surface, there exist several different characteristic vector fields. In fact, they constitute a conformal class: rescaling the area form
\sigma
by a positive function results in a rescaling of
X
by the reciprocal of the same function.

Remark 2.2
.

Any characteristic vector field has non zero divergence at characteristic points. Recall that, the divergence of
X
with respect to the area form
\sigma
is defined by (
L_{X}
denoting the Lie derivative)

(40)

\mathrm{div}(X)\sigma=L_{X}\sigma=d\iota_{X}\sigma.

Therefore, taking the differential of both sides of (
38
) and using
L_{X}=d\circ\iota_{X}+\iota_{X}\circ d
we obtain

(41)

\mathrm{div}(X)\sigma=d\omega|_{S}.

Now, if
q\in S
is a characteristic point, then

T_{q}S=\mathcal{D}_{q}
.
By the contact condition, the restriction of
d\omega
to
\mathcal{D}
is non degenerate, therefore (
41
) implies that
\mathrm{div}(X)
does not vanish at
q
.

In the case of a surface embedded in a contact sub-Riemannian manifold we can fix a canonical representative characteristic vector fields by fixing the area form.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
(M,\mathcal{D},g)
be a contact sub-Riemannian manifold and let
S\subset M
be an embedded oriented surface. Let
\sigma^{1}
be the area form defined in (
37
) for
\varepsilon=1
. We define the sub-Riemannian characteristic vector field
X\in\mathfrak{X}(S)
as

(42)

\iota_{X}\sigma^{1}=\omega|_{S},

where
\omega
denotes the contact form normalized as in (
33
).

The divergence of the sub-Riemannian characteristic vector field and its norm are related.

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
(M,\mathcal{D},g)
be a contact sub-Riemannian manifold and
S
an embedded oriented surface.
Let
\sigma^{1}
be the area form defined in (
37
) for
\varepsilon=1
.
Then the divergence of the sub-Riemannian characteristic vector field
X
, computed with respect to
\sigma^{1}
, satisfies on
S

(43)

\mathrm{div}(X)^{2}+|X|^{2}=1.

We stress that in (
43
) the norm
|\cdot|
is the sub-Riemannian norm on
\mathcal{D}
.

Proof.

Let us first work on
S\setminus\Sigma(S)
and set
f_{1}=X/|X|
the normalized characteristic vector field, let
f_{2}
be the oriented horizontal normal and let
f_{0}
be the Reeb field associated with the normalized contact form
\omega
. Notice that, being
g^{1}|_{\mathcal{D}}
equal to
g
, then
f_{1}
has norm
1
for
(S,g^{1}|_{S})
.
Let
J:TS\to TS
be the orthogonal map defined by

(44)

\sigma^{1}(v,Jw)=g^{1}(v,w),\qquad v,w\in TS.

We define an orthonormal frame for
g^{1}|_{S\setminus\Sigma(S)}
as

(45)

e_{1}=f_{1},\qquad e_{2}=Je_{1}.

Then there exists a function
a:S\to[-1,1]
such that,

(46)

e_{1}=f_{1},\qquad e_{2}=af_{2}+\sqrt{1-a^{2}}f_{0}.

Taking the differential of the 1-forms in both sides of (
42
) and evaluating the result at
e_{1},e_{2}
we get

(47)

\mathrm{div}(X)=d\omega(e_{1},e_{2})=d\omega(f_{1},af_{2}+\sqrt{1-a^{2}}f_{0})=ad\omega(f_{1},f_{2})=a,

where, in the third equality we have used that
d\omega(f_{0},\cdot)=0
from (
32
), and in the last one the normalization condition (
33
).
Moreover, using again (
44
), we can compute

(48)

\displaystyle|X|^{2}

\displaystyle=g^{1}(X,X)=\sigma^{1}(X,JX)=\iota_{X}\sigma^{1}(JX)=\omega(JX)

\displaystyle=|X|\omega(af_{2}+\sqrt{1-a^{2}}f_{0})=|X|\sqrt{1-a^{2}},

where the fourth equality follows from (
42
), and the fifth from (
46
) recalling that
e_{1}=f_{1}=X/|X|
. This implies
|X|=\sqrt{1-a^{2}}
and, being

a=\mathrm{div}(X)
thanks to (
47
), identity (
43
) is proved on
S\setminus\Sigma(S)
. By continuity, the equality is proved on
S
.
∎

We recall the definition of differential of a vector field at a singular point.

Definition 2.5
.

Let
S
be a smooth manifold and
X\in\mathfrak{X}(S)
a smooth vector field vanishing at
q\in S
. The differential of
X
at
q
is the map

(49)

D_{q}X:T_{q}S\to T_{q}S,\qquad D_{q}X(Y)=[Y,X]_{q}.

Notice that the definition of
D_{q}X(Y)
requires a choice of a vector field extension of
Y
, near
q
. However, since
X_{q}=0
, the vector
[Y,X]_{q}
only depends on
Y_{q}
.

Remark 2.6
.

Let
\nabla
be the Levi-Civita connection on
TS
for
g^{1}|_{S}
, and
q
a characteristic point, then

\nabla_{Y}X|_{q}=\nabla_{X}Y|_{q}+[Y,X]|_{q}=(D_{q}X)Y,\qquad\forall\,Y\in T_{q}M,

where we used that
\nabla_{X}Y|_{q}=0
since
X|_{q}=0
. At a characteristic point
q
we have the equality

(50)

\mathrm{div}_{q}(X)=\mathrm{trace}(D_{q}X)=\mathrm{trace}(\nabla X|_{q}).

By
Remark

2.2
we deduce that
\mathrm{trace}(D_{q}X)\neq 0
and that
\rank D_{q}X\geq 1
at characteristic points.

3.
Order of degeneracy of characteristic points. Proof of Proposition
1.7

Throughout this subsection
S
denote an oriented surface embedded in a contact sub-Riemannian manifold
(M,\mathcal{D},g)
with sub-Riemannian characteristic vector field
X
.

We start by proving the existence of a horizontal kernel extension
\gamma:(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\to S
.

Proof of Lemma
1.6
.

According to
Remark

2.2
, it holds
\mathrm{div}_{q}(X)=\mathrm{trace}(D_{q}X)\neq 0
. Therefore, the characteristic polynomial of the endomorphism
D_{q}X:T_{q}S\to T_{q}S
reads

(51)

P(\lambda)=\lambda(\lambda-\mathrm{div}_{q}(X)).

Applying the classical center manifold theorem (one see for example
[
Per01
, Thm. 1, Sec. 2.12]
) we deduce the existence of a smooth embedded submanifold
\mathcal{C}
which is invariant under the flow of
X
and such that
T_{q}\mathcal{C}=\ker D_{q}X
. Let
\gamma:(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\to S
be a smooth regular parametrization of
\mathcal{C}
. Since
\mathcal{C}
is inavriant under the flow of
X
, we deduce that
\gamma
is horizontal. Reparametrizing
\gamma
by arc-length we find a horizontal kernel extension.
∎

Lemma 3.1
.

Given a horizontal kernel extension
\gamma:(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\to S
, the following smooth function

(52)

\lambda_{\gamma}:(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\to\mathbb{R},\qquad\lambda_{\gamma}(t)=g(X,\dot{\gamma}(t))|_{\gamma(t)},

satisfies
\lambda_{\gamma}(0)=\dot{\lambda}_{\gamma}(0)=0
, i.e., has order of vanishing at zero
\geq 2
.

Proof.

Since at characteristic points
X
vanishes, then
\lambda_{\gamma}(0)=0
. Denoting
\nabla
the Levi-Civita connection of
g^{1}|_{S}
, we compute

\frac{d}{dt}g(X,\dot{\gamma})|_{\gamma(t)}=g(\nabla_{\dot{\gamma}(t)}X,\dot{\gamma}(t))+g(X,\nabla_{\dot{\gamma}(t)}\dot{\gamma}(t))

The second term at
t=0
is zero since
X
vanishes at characteristic points. The first one vanishes as well: consider any vector field
Y
extension of
\dot{\gamma}(t)
and compute (at
t=0
)

(53)

\nabla_{\dot{\gamma}(t)}X|_{q}=\nabla_{Y}X|_{q}=(D_{q}X)Y=0,

where we have used
Remark

2.6
and the fact that
Y|_{q}\in\ker D_{q}X
by construction.
∎

3.1.
Well-posedness of the order. Proof of Proposition
1.7

We now move to the proof of the fact that the order of degeneracy is well-defined.

Proof of Proposition
1.7
.

Let
q\in S
be a degenerate characteristic point, i.e., such that
\det(D_{q}X)=0
. Recall that a horizontal kernel extension at
q
is an arc-length parametrized horizontal smooth curve
\gamma:(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\to S
satisfying

\gamma(0)=q
and
\dot{\gamma}(0)\in\ker(D_{q}X)
.

(ii). We now prove that the order of vanishing of the following smooth function

(54)

\lambda_{\gamma}:(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\to\mathbb{R},\qquad\lambda_{\gamma}(t)=g(X,\dot{\gamma}(t))|_{\gamma(t)},

at
t=0
does not depend on the choice of the horizontal kernel extension
\gamma
. Notice that, once proved this statement, then Lemma
3.1
implies that
\mathrm{ord}(q)\geq 2
.

Let
g^{1}
be the Riemannian metric described in
2.1
. For the sake of simplicity in this proof we denote
g^{1}|_{S}
by
g
and the corresponding area form
\sigma^{1}
by
\sigma
. Let
\gamma:(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\to S
be a kernel extension, and let

be a vector field which extends
\dot{\gamma}
. Let
N
denote the oriented orthonormal complement in

with respect to

. Then, from (
42

to the surface

can be computed as

(55)

\omega|_{S}(\cdot)=\sigma(X,\cdot)=\det\begin{pmatrix}g(T,X)&g(T,\cdot)\\

Let
\mathcal{C}=\gamma(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)
. We claim that

(56)

\mathcal{C}=\{p\in S\mid\omega(T)|_{p}=0\}=\{p\in S\mid g(N,X)|_{p}=0\}.

We observe that the function
g(N,X)
is a submersion near
q
. First observe that being
X|_{q}=0
and
[T,X]|_{q}=D_{q}X(\dot{\gamma}(0))=0
one has
\nabla_{T}X|_{q}=0
. For the divergence at the point

, one has

\mathrm{div}_{q}(X)=\mathrm{trace}(\nabla X|_{q})=g(\nabla_{T}X,T)|_{q}+g(\nabla_{N}X,N)|_{q}=g(\nabla_{N}X,N)|_{q},

where we used
Remark

2.6
. Thus, differentiating
g(N,X)
in the direction of
N
and evaluating the result at

(57)

\displaystyle N(g(N,X))|_{q}=g(\nabla_{N}N,X)|_{q}+g(N,\nabla_{N}X)|_{q}=\mathrm{div}_{q}(X),

where
g(\nabla_{N}N,X)|_{q}=0
since
X|_{q}=0
. According to
Remark

2.2
,
\mathrm{div}_{q}(X)\neq 0
. Therefore, the regular value theorem implies that the vanishing set of
\omega(T)
is a smooth curve near
q
. On the other hand, since

and
\dot{\gamma}
is horizontal, the vanishing set of
\omega(T)
contains
\mathcal{C}
; proving (
56
).

Let now
{\gamma}^{\prime}:(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\to S
be another horizontal kernel extension and let
{T}^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{X}(S)
be a vector field extension of its velocity field

. Since

(58)

\dot{\gamma}^{\prime}={T}^{\prime}_{q}=\pm T_{q}=\pm\dot{\gamma}(0).

Let us define the functions

as follows

(59)

\lambda_{\gamma}(t)=g(\dot{\gamma}(t),X)|_{\gamma(t)},\qquad{\lambda}^{\prime}_{\gamma}(t)=g(\dot{\gamma}^{\prime}(t),X)|_{\gamma^{\prime}(t)}.

Denoting with
{\mathcal{C}}^{\prime}=\gamma^{\prime}(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)
, the assertion that the two functions have the same order at

is equivalent to prove the following

(60)

\mathrm{order}(g({T}^{\prime},X)|_{{\mathcal{C}}^{\prime}},q)=\mathrm{order}(g(T,X)|_{\mathcal{C}},q).

where we denote by
\mathrm{order}(f|_{{\mathcal{C}}},q)
the order of vanishing of

at

\mathcal{C}
(cf.
Appendix

A
).

We prove now equality (
60

\mathcal{C}^{\prime}

(61)

{\mathcal{C}}^{\prime}=\{p\in S\mid\omega(T^{\prime})|_{p}=0\}.

Denote
\mathrm{order}(\mathcal{C}\cap{\mathcal{C}}^{\prime},q)

\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
at

(see

A.4

(62)

\displaystyle\mathrm{order}(\mathcal{C}\cap{\mathcal{C}}^{\prime},q)+1

\displaystyle=\mathrm{order}\left(\omega(T^{\prime})|_{\mathcal{C}},q\right)=\mathrm{order}\left(g(T,X)g(N,T^{\prime})|_{\mathcal{C}},q\right)

\displaystyle>\mathrm{order}\left(g(T,X)|_{\mathcal{C}},q\right),

where in the second equality we have used the expression (

\omega|_{S}
and
g(N,X)|_{\mathcal{C}}\equiv 0
, thanks to (
56

g(N,{T^{\prime}})|_{q}=0
.

By construction
\omega(T^{\prime})|_{{\mathcal{C}}^{\prime}}\equiv 0
, hence by (

(63)

g(N,X)g(T,T^{\prime})|_{{\mathcal{C}}^{\prime}}=\left.{g(T,X)g(N,T^{\prime})}\right|_{{\mathcal{C}}^{\prime}}.

Decomposing

and using that

\displaystyle g(T^{\prime},X)|_{{\mathcal{C}}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\left.\left(g(T,X)g({T^{\prime}},T)+g(N,X)g(T^{\prime},N)\right)\right|_{{\mathcal{C}}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=g(T,X)g({T^{\prime}},T)\left.\left(1+\frac{g(T^{\prime},N)^{2}}{g(T,T^{\prime})^{2}}\right)\right|_{{\mathcal{C}}^{\prime}}

where in the last equality we used (

is multiplied by two functions that are non vanishing at

(65)

\mathrm{order}(g(T^{\prime},X)|_{{\mathcal{C}}^{\prime}},q)=\mathrm{order}(g(T,X)|_{{\mathcal{C}}^{\prime}},q)=\mathrm{order}(g(T,X)|_{\mathcal{C}},q),

where the last equality combines (

A.2

(iii). Finally, let us show that if

(68)

\displaystyle\left.\frac{d^{k}}{dt^{k}}\right|_{t=0}g(T^{\prime},X)|_{{\mathcal{C}}^{\prime}}

4.
Normal forms and coordinate characterization. Proof of Theorem
1.10

We provide a local coordinates characterization of the finite order condition.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
S
be a surface embedded in a
3D
contact sub-Riemannian manifold with characteristic vector field
X
.
Let
q\in S
be a characteristic point with order of degeneracy
\mathrm{ord}(q)=k\geq 2
. Then there exists coordinates
(x,y)
on
S
near
q
such that

(71)

q=(0,0),\qquad X=x(\nu+a_{1}(x,y))\partial_{x}+\left(\frac{\mu}{k!}y^{k}+xa_{0}(x,y)+b_{0}(y)\right)\partial_{y},

where
\nu=\mathrm{div}(X)|_{q}
,
\mu=\Lambda^{(k)}(q)
is defined in (
66
), and
a_{0},a_{1},b_{0}
are smooth functions satisfying

(72)

b_{0}(y)=O(y^{k+1}),\qquad a_{i}(x,y)=O\left(\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}\right),\qquad i=0,1.

Moreover, in these coordinates, the curve
y\mapsto(0,y)
is an horizontal kernel extension.

Proof.

Let
q
be a characteristic point such that
\det(D_{q}X)=0
. By
Remark

2.6
,
\nu:=\mathrm{div}(X)|_{q}=\mathrm{trace}(D_{q}X)\neq 0
. Thus, the characteristic polynomial of
D_{q}X:T_{q}S\to T_{q}S
reads

(73)

P(\lambda)=\lambda(\lambda-\nu),

and
D_{q}X
is diagonalizable at
q
. In particular, there exists a smooth regular curve
\sigma:(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\to S
such that

(74)

\sigma(0)=q,\qquad D_{q}X(\dot{\sigma}(0))=\nu\dot{\sigma}(0).

Let
\gamma
be a horizontal kernel extension at
q
and
T\in\mathfrak{X}(S)
be a vector field extending
\dot{\gamma}
. Since
\dot{\gamma}(0)
and
\dot{\sigma}(0)
are linearly independent, for
\varepsilon>0
small enough, the following map defines coordinates near
q
:

(75)

\varphi:(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)^{2}\to S,\qquad\varphi(x,y)=e^{yT}\circ\sigma(x).

In such coordinates
q=(0,0)
and moreover

(76)

\dot{\sigma}(0)=\partial_{x},\qquad\gamma(t)=(0,t)\qquad\forall\,t\in(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon).

Hence there exists
a,b:(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
smooth functions such that

(77)

X=(\nu x+a(x,y))\partial_{x}+b(x,y)\partial_{y}.

which, thanks to the assumptions on
D_{q}X
in (
73
), (
74
), (
76
), also satisfy

(78)

a(0,0)=b(0,0)=0,\qquad\nabla a(0,0)=\nabla b(0,0)=0.

Moreover, for any
t\in(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)
the curve
\gamma(t)=(0,t)
should be an integral curve of
X
, hence
a(0,y)=0
. Writing

(79)

a(x,y)=a(0,y)+x\int_{0}^{1}\partial_{x}a(tx,y)dt,

it follows that there exists a smooth function
a_{1}(x,y)
such that

a(x,y)=xa_{1}(x,y)
.
Since
a(0,0)=0
and
\nabla a(0,0)=0
, we deduce that
a_{1}(0,0)=0
, thus, by differentiability of
a_{1}
the function
a_{1}
satisfies (
72
). Moreover, since
a(0,y)=0
the function
\lambda_{\gamma}
can be computed as

(80)

\lambda_{\gamma}(t)=g(X|_{(0,t)},\dot{\gamma}(t))=b(0,t).

Combining (
78
) and the fact that we assume
\mathrm{ord}(q)=k
, then the function
b
satisfies

(81)

b(0,0)=0,\qquad\partial_{x}b(0,0)=0,\qquad\partial_{y}^{j}b(0,0)=\begin{cases}0,\quad 0\leq j<k,\\
\mu,\quad j=k.\end{cases}

Repeating the argument above, we can write for the function

5.
Levi-Civita connection forms and Riemannian
\varepsilon
-frames

In this section, we review the definition and fundamental properties of the Levi-Civita connection 1-forms, recalling their relation to the indices of isolated zeroes of vector fields.

5.1.
Levi-Civita connection forms

We recall that given a smooth map
F:\mathbb{S}^{1}\to\mathbb{S}^{1}
written as follows
F(e^{i\theta})=e^{if(\theta)}
, where
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is smooth, then the degree of
F
, denoted
\mathrm{deg}(F)
, is the following integer

(94)

\mathrm{deg}(F)=\frac{1}{2\pi}(f(2\pi)-f(0))=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}f^{\prime}(\tau)d\tau.

In what follows, a smooth ball
B
in
S
denotes an open set whose closure is diffeomorphic to a closed Euclidean ball in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

Definition 5.1
.

Let
(S,g)
be a smooth Riemannian surface, let
X\in\mathrm{Vec}(S)
a smooth vector field and let
q\in S
be an isolated zero of
X
. Let
B
be smooth ball such that
q
is the unique zero of
X|_{B}
. Consider the following map

(95)

F:\partial B\simeq\mathbb{S}^{1}\to\mathbb{S}^{1},\qquad\varphi(p)=\frac{X}{|X|}\bigg|_{p}.

The index of the critical point
q
is defined as the degree of the map
F

(96)

\mathrm{ind}(q)=\deg(F).

We now define Levi-Civita connection form associated with a smooth vector field (playing the role of the characteristic vector field) and list some of its classical properties.

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
(S,g)
be an oriented Riemannian surface and let
X
be a smooth vector field vanishing on a set
Z\subset S
. On
S\setminus Z
we define the orthonormal frame

(97)

e_{1}:=\frac{X}{|X|},\qquad e_{2}:=\frac{JX}{|X|},

where
J:TS\to TS
is the complex structure of the Riemannian surface
(S,g)
. The Levi-Civita connection form associated with the the frame
e_{1},e_{2}
is the following differential 1-form
\eta\in\Omega^{1}(S\setminus Z)

(98)

\eta(v)=g(\nabla_{v}e_{1},e_{2})=\frac{1}{|X|^{2}}g(\nabla_{v}X,JX),\qquad\forall\,v\in T\left(S\setminus Z\right).

The Levi-Civita connection form satisfies the following properties:

(i)

Let
\theta_{1},\theta_{2}
be the coframe dual to
e_{1},e_{2}
, i.e.
\theta_{i}(e_{j})=\delta_{ij}
. Then

(99)

\eta=-c_{1}\theta_{1}-c_{2}\theta_{2},

where
c_{1},c_{2}
are the structural functions satisfying
d\theta_{i}=c_{i}\theta_{1}\wedge\theta_{2}
, for
i=1,2
.

(ii)

Let
K
denote the Gaussian curvature and
\sigma
the area form of
(S,g)
, then on
S\setminus Z

(100)

d\eta=K\sigma.

(iii)

Assume
q
is an isolated zero of
X
and let
\{C_{\delta}\}_{\delta>0}
be a family of smooth embedded circles in
S
enclosing
q
and converging uniformly to it. Then

(101)

\lim_{\delta\to 0}\int_{C_{\delta}}\eta=2\pi\,\mathrm{ind}(q).

Proof.

(i),(ii)
. These are standard properties of the Levi-Civita form. See for instance
[
Pet16
, Ex. 3.4.28]
or
[
ABB20
, Sec. 4.4.1]
for details. For completeness we prove
(i)
.

The forms
\theta_{1},\theta_{2}
form a basis of 1-forms on
S\setminus Z
, therefore

\eta=\eta(e_{1})\theta_{1}+\eta(e_{2})\theta_{2}.

We can then compute the coefficient

(102)

\displaystyle\eta(e_{1})

\displaystyle=g(\nabla_{e_{1}}e_{1},e_{2})=-g(e_{1},\nabla_{e_{1}}e_{2})=-g(e_{1},\nabla_{e_{2}}e_{1}+[e_{1},e_{2}])=-c_{1}.

Where we used the fact that
\nabla
is metric and torsion free, and
g(e_{i},e_{j})
is constant. Repeating analogous computations one obtains
\eta(e_{2})=-c_{2}
.

(iii)
. If
\delta>0
is small enough we may assume that
q
is the only zero of
X|_{B_{\delta}}
, where
B_{\delta}
is the connected component of
S\setminus C_{\delta}
containing
q
, so that
C_{\delta}=\partial B_{\delta}
. We would like to express in coordinates

(103)

F:\partial B_{\delta}\to\mathbb{S}^{1},\qquad F(p)=\frac{X}{|X|}\bigg|_{p}=e_{1}|_{p}.

Fix a regular parametrization

\gamma:[0,2\pi]\to C_{\delta}
of
\partial B_{\delta}=C_{\delta}
.
Choosing
\delta>0
small enough, consider an oriented smooth orthonormal frame
e_{1}^{\prime},e_{2}^{\prime}
on
B_{\delta}
and a function
\phi:[0,2\pi]\to\mathbb{R}
such that

(104)

F(\gamma(t))=\cos\phi(t)e_{1}^{\prime}+\sin\phi(t)e_{2}^{\prime},\qquad\forall\,t\in[0,2\pi].

Moreover, by (
94
), one has

(105)

\phi(2\pi)-\phi(0)=2\pi\deg(F)=2\pi\,\mathrm{ind}(q).

Consider the Levi-Civita connection form
\eta^{\prime}
associated with the frame
e_{1}^{\prime},e_{2}^{\prime}
, namely

(106)

\eta^{\prime}(v)=g(\nabla_{v}e_{1}^{\prime},e_{2}^{\prime}),\qquad\forall\,v\in TB_{\delta}.

Note that due to (
103
) and (
97
), along
\gamma(t)
, one has

e_{1}^{\prime}=\cos\phi(t)\,e_{1}-\sin\phi(t)\,e_{2},\qquad e_{2}^{\prime}=\sin\phi(t)\,e_{1}+\cos\phi(t)\,e_{2}

We stress that
e_{1}^{\prime},e_{2}^{\prime}
is smooth on
S
while
e_{1},e_{2}
is singular on
Z
. For any
t\in[0,2\pi]
we can compute

(107)

\displaystyle\eta^{\prime}(\dot{\gamma}(t))

\displaystyle=g(\nabla_{\dot{\gamma}(t)}e_{1}^{\prime},e_{2}^{\prime})=g(\nabla_{\dot{\gamma}(t)}(\cos\phi(t)\,e_{1}-\sin\phi(t)\,e_{2}),\sin\phi(t)\,e_{1}+\cos\phi(t)\,e_{2})

\displaystyle=-\dot{\phi}(t)+g(\nabla_{v}e_{1},e_{2})=-\dot{\phi}(t)+\eta(\dot{\gamma}(t)).

Notice that, since
\eta^{\prime}
is smooth on
B_{\delta}
, applying Stokes theorem we find by absolute continuity of the integral

(108)

\lim_{\delta\to 0}\int_{\partial B_{\delta}}\eta^{\prime}=\lim_{\delta\to 0}\int_{B_{\delta}}d\eta^{\prime}=0.

We next combine (
108
) together with (
107
) to compute the limit

(109)

\displaystyle\lim_{\delta\to 0}\int_{\partial B_{\delta}}\eta

\displaystyle=\lim_{\delta\to 0}\int_{\partial B_{\delta}}\eta-\eta^{\prime}=\lim_{\delta\to 0}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\left(\eta(\dot{\gamma}(t))-\eta^{\prime}(\dot{\gamma}(t))\right)dt

\displaystyle=\lim_{\delta\to 0}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\dot{\phi}(t)dt=\phi(2\pi)-\phi(0)=2\pi\,\mathrm{ind}(q).\hfill\qed

5.3.
Estimates on Levi-Civita forms

Given an oriented Riemannian surface
(S,g)
and a differential form
\eta\in\Omega^{1}(S)
we define the norm of
\eta
at the point

as

(123)

\|\eta\|(q)=\sup_{v\in T_{q}S,\,v\neq 0}\frac{|\eta_{q}(v)|}{|v|},

where
|v|=\sqrt{g(v,v)}
. In particular, the norm of a differential 1-form is a function

\|\eta\|:S\to\mathbb{R}
.

Observe that for
\alpha,\beta\in\Omega^{1}(S)
, and
\sigma
is the area form of
(S,g)
, the following inequality holds

(124)

\left|\int_{S}\alpha\wedge\beta\,\right|\leq\int_{S}\|\alpha\|\|\beta\|\sigma.

We now compute the Levi-Civita connection form
\eta^{\varepsilon}
associated with a Riemannian
\varepsilon
-frame of
5.3

Lemma 5.6

Let
\eta^{\varepsilon}
be the Levi-Civita connection 1-form associated with the Riemannian
\varepsilon
-frame. Then

(125)

\eta^{\varepsilon}=-\frac{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}{b_{\varepsilon}}c_{1}\theta_{1}-c_{2}\frac{b_{\varepsilon}}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\theta_{2}-\frac{e_{1}(b_{\varepsilon})}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\theta_{2},

where

satisfies

,

, and

is defined in (

).

Proof.

We compute the differentials of the coframe
\theta_{1}^{\varepsilon},\theta_{2}^{\varepsilon}
in (

(126)

d\theta_{1}^{\varepsilon}=c_{1}\theta_{1}\wedge\theta_{2}=\frac{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}{b_{\varepsilon}}c_{1}\theta_{1}^{\varepsilon}\wedge\theta_{2}^{\varepsilon},

Analogously for

(127)

(ii)

Lemma 5.8
.

Let
q\in S
be a characteristic point of finite order
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
. Then there exists a family of smooth curves
\{C_{\delta}\}_{\delta>0}
, enclosing the point

and converging uniformly to it, such that

\lim_{\delta\to 0}\mathrm{length}\left(C_{\delta}\right)=0,\qquad\limsup_{\delta\to 0}\int_{C_{\delta}}|\eta^{\varepsilon}|<+\infty,

where
\eta^{\varepsilon}
is the Levi-Civita connection form described

.

Proof.

According to
4.1
, there exist coordinates
(x,y)
near
q
and
\mu,\nu\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
such that

(155)

q=(0,0),\qquad X=x(\nu+a_{1}(x,y))\partial_{x}+\left(\mu\frac{y^{k}}{k!}+xa_{0}(x,y)+b_{0}(y)\right)\partial_{y},

We set
C_{\delta}=\{(x,y)\in S\mid x^{2}+y^{2}=\delta^{2}\}
. Let
B_{\delta}=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\mid x^{2}+y^{2}<\delta^{2}\}
. We may assume that the coordinates
(x,y)
are defined on an open set containing the closure of
B_{1}
. Let
e_{1}^{\prime},e_{2}^{\prime}
be a smooth oriented orthonormal frame for the metric

(in this proof

is fixed hence we omit it in the notation) defined on the whole
B_{1}
, and write

(156)

X=|X|(\cos\phi\,e_{1}^{\prime}+\sin\phi\,e_{2}^{\prime})=:{V_{1}^{\prime}e_{1}^{\prime}+V_{2}^{\prime}e_{2}^{\prime}}

for some smooth function
\phi:B_{\delta}\setminus\{q\}\to\mathbb{R}
. We parametrize
C_{\delta}
by

as follows

(157)

\gamma(t)=(\delta\cos(t),\delta\sin(t)),\qquad\forall\,t\in[0,2\pi].

For any function

we denote the derivative along
\gamma
with
\dot{f}
, in particular

(158)

\dot{f}=\frac{d}{dt}f(\gamma(t))=(x\partial_{y}f-y\partial_{x}f)|_{\gamma(t)}.

Repeating verbatim the computations (
107
), which was needed in the proof of

, we find

(159)

\eta^{\varepsilon}(\dot{\gamma})-g(\nabla_{\dot{\gamma}}e_{1}^{\prime},e_{2}^{\prime})=\dot{\phi}.

Since
{V_{1}^{\prime}}=|X|\cos\phi

{V_{2}^{\prime}}=|X|\sin\phi
, we have
\left({V_{1}^{\prime}}\right)^{2}+\left({V_{1}^{\prime}}\right)^{2}=|X|^{2}

(160)

\dot{\phi}=\frac{d}{dt}\left(\arctan{\left(\frac{{V_{2}^{\prime}}}{{V_{1}^{\prime}}}\right)}\bigg|_{\gamma(t)}\right)=\frac{{V_{1}^{\prime}}{\dot{V}_{2}^{\prime}}-{V_{2}^{\prime}}{\dot{V}_{1}^{\prime}}}{\left({V_{1}^{\prime}}\right)^{2}+\left({V_{2}^{\prime}}\right)^{2}}=\frac{1}{|X|^{2}}{V^{\prime}}\wedge{\dot{V}^{\prime}}

where we identify

as a vector in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and, given

, we set

. Combining (
160
) with (
159
) yields

\eta^{\varepsilon}(\dot{\gamma})-g(\nabla_{\dot{\gamma}}e_{1}^{\prime},e_{2}^{\prime})=\frac{1}{|X|^{2}}{V^{\prime}}\wedge{\dot{V}^{\prime}}

We now introduce the following notation

V_{1}=\nu x+xa_{1}(x,y),\qquad V_{2}=\mu\frac{y^{k}}{k!}+xa_{0}(x,y)+b_{0}(y),\qquad{V=(V_{1},V_{2})},

so that

. Since both

are smooth basis for

, there exists a smooth map

, and a constant

Since

, by adapting the constant

. Therefore, substituting the expression of

in terms of

\displaystyle|\eta^{\varepsilon}(\dot{\gamma})|

\displaystyle\leq C+\frac{1}{|X|^{2}}\left|AV\wedge\frac{d}{dt}\left(AV\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq C+\frac{1}{|X|^{2}}\left|AV\wedge\left(\dot{A}V+A\dot{V}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq C+C^{2}+\frac{C}{|X|^{2}}\left|V\wedge\dot{V}\right|.

By definition of
\gamma

\displaystyle\dot{V}_{1}=-\nu y-ya_{1}(x,y)+xh_{1}(x,y),\qquad\dot{V}_{2}=-ya_{0}(x,y)+xh_{0}(x,y),

\displaystyle\left|V\wedge\dot{V}\right|

\displaystyle=\left(x\begin{pmatrix}\nu+a_{1}\\

\frac{1}{|X|^{2}}\left|V\wedge\dot{V}\right|\leq|\ell_{1}|+\frac{1}{2}|\ell_{2}|+|\ell_{3}|\frac{|y|^{k+1}}{x^{2}+y^{2k}}\leq 2C+C\frac{|y|^{k+1}}{x^{2}+y^{2k}}.

Substituting the latter inequality in (

Therefore, we have the following estimate

\int_{C_{\delta}}|\eta^{\varepsilon}|=\int_{0}^{2\pi}|\eta^{\varepsilon}(\dot{\gamma})|dt\leq 2\pi C+C\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{\delta^{k+1}|\sin(t)|^{k+1}}{\delta^{2}\cos(t)^{2}+\delta^{2k}\sin(t)^{2k}}dt.

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\frac{\delta^{k+1}\sin(t)^{k+1}}{\delta^{2}\cos(t)^{2}+\delta^{2k}\sin(t)^{2k}}dt

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\frac{\delta^{k+1}\left(\sin(t)^{k+1}+k\cos(t)^{2}\sin(t)^{k-1}\right)}{\delta^{2}\cos(t)^{2}+\delta^{2k}\sin(t)^{2k}}dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\frac{d}{dt}\left(\arctan\left(\frac{\delta^{k}\sin(t)^{k}}{\delta\cos(t)}\right)\right)dt=\frac{\pi}{2}.\qed

Remark 5.9
.

There exists a smooth vector field

6.

Let
(S,g)

Its dual space,

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\|\mu_{\varepsilon}-\mu_{0}\|_{C^{1}(S)^{*}}=0.

Appendix A
Some technical lemmas

A.1.
Orders of contact

We recall the definition of order of vanishing of a function and order of contact of two curves, which will be needed in what follows.

Let
S
be a smooth surface and
\mathcal{C}\subset S
be an embedded curve. Let
q\in\mathcal{C}
and let
f:S\to\mathbb{R}
a smooth function. Let
\gamma:\mathbb{R}\to\mathcal{C}
be a smooth regular parametrization such that
\gamma(0)=q
, then the order of
f
at
q
, along
\mathcal{C}
, is defined as

(233)

\mathrm{order}(f|_{\mathcal{C}},q)=\inf\left\{i\in\mathbb{N}\,:\,\left.\frac{d^{i}}{dt^{i}}f(\gamma(t))\right|_{t=0}\neq 0\right\}.

It is elementary to see that the order of a function at a point along a curve does not depend on the parametrization since for
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
a smooth function having order of vanishing
k\in\mathbb{N}
at the origin, and
\varphi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
a diffeomorphism such that
\varphi(0)=0
, then

(234)

\left.\frac{d^{k}}{dt^{k}}f(\varphi(t))\right|_{t=0}=\left(\varphi^{\prime}(0)\right)^{k}f^{(k)}(0).

Let us define the order of contact of two embedded curves.

Definition A.1
.

Let
S
be a smooth surface and
\mathcal{C}_{1},\mathcal{C}_{2}\subset S
be two embedded curves. Then
\mathcal{C}_{1},\mathcal{C}_{2}
are said to have contact of order
k\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{+\infty\}
at
q\in\mathcal{C}_{1}\cap\mathcal{C}_{2}
, and we write

(235)

\mathrm{order}(\mathcal{C}_{1}\cap\mathcal{C}_{2},q)=k,

if
k\in\mathbb{N}
is the largest integer such that there exist smooth regular parametrizations
\gamma_{i}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathcal{C}_{i}
, for
i=1,2
, such that

(236)

\gamma_{1}(0)=\gamma_{2}(0)=q,\qquad\frac{d^{j}\gamma_{1}}{dt^{j}}(0)=\frac{d^{j}\gamma_{2}}{dt^{j}}(0),\qquad\forall\,\,0\leq j\leq k.

If the order of contact of the intersection of two curves is large enough, one can use equivalently any of the curve to compute the order of vanishing of a function.

Lemma A.2
.

Let
S
be a smooth surface,
f:S\to\mathbb{R}
be a smooth function. Let
\mathcal{C}_{1},\mathcal{C}_{2}\subset S
be smooth embedded curves and assume that

(237)

\mathrm{order}(\mathcal{C}_{1}\cap\mathcal{C}_{2},q)\geq\mathrm{order}(f|_{\mathcal{C}_{1}},q).

Then

(238)

\mathrm{order}(f|_{\mathcal{C}_{1}},q)=\mathrm{order}(f|_{\mathcal{C}_{2}},q).

Proof.

Let
m=\mathrm{order}(\mathcal{C}_{1}\cap\mathcal{C}_{2},q)
and
k=\mathrm{order}(f|_{\mathcal{C}},q)
. By definition of order of contact of curves there exist smooth regular parametrizations
\gamma_{i}:\mathbb{R}\to S
,
i=1,2
, such that for every smooth
f:S\to\mathbb{R}

(239)

\left.\frac{d^{i}}{dt^{i}}f(\gamma_{1}(t))\right|_{t=0}=\left.\frac{d^{i}}{dt^{i}}f(\gamma_{2}(t))\right|_{t=0},\qquad\forall\,0\leq i\leq m.

Since by hypothesis
m\geq k
, we can conclude that
\mathrm{order}(f|_{\mathcal{C}_{1}},q)=\mathrm{order}(f|_{\mathcal{C}_{2}},q)
.

∎

Remark A.3
.

Notice that
if
k=\mathrm{order}(f|_{\mathcal{C}_{1}},q)=\mathrm{order}(f|_{\mathcal{C}_{2}},q)
and
\gamma_{i}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathcal{C}_{i}
,
i=1,2
, are smooth parametrizations satisfying
\gamma_{1}(0)=\gamma_{2}(0)=q
and
\dot{\gamma}_{1}(0)=a\dot{\gamma}_{2}(0)

(240)

\left.\frac{d^{k}}{dt^{k}}f(\gamma_{1}(t))\right|_{t=0}=a^{k}\left.\frac{d^{k}}{dt^{k}}f(\gamma_{2}(t))\right|_{t=0}.

If a curve is a regular level set, we can then relate the two notions just introduced as follows.

Lemma A.4
.

Let
S
be a smooth surface, and
\mathcal{C}_{1},\mathcal{C}_{2}\subset S
be smooth embedded curves. Assume that
u_{2}:S\to\mathbb{R}
is a submersion such that
\mathcal{C}_{2}=u_{2}^{-1}(0)
. Then

(241)

\mathrm{order}(\mathcal{C}_{1}\cap\mathcal{C}_{2},q)=\mathrm{order}(u_{2}|_{\mathcal{C}_{1}},q)-1.

Proof.

There exist local coordinates
(x,y)
near
q

q=(0,0)
and
u_{2}(x,y)=y

Let
\gamma_{1}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathcal{C}_{1}

Since

Let

\mathrm{order}(y|_{\mathcal{C}_{1}},(0,0))=\mathrm{order}(y\circ\gamma_{1},0)=\mathrm{order}(y_{1},0)=1=\mathrm{order}(\mathcal{C}_{1}\cap\mathcal{C}_{2},(0,0))+1.

If
n\geq 1
, then necessarily
\dot{x}_{1}(0)\neq 0
, therefore up to reparametrization we may assume
x_{1}(t)=t
, or equivalently

\gamma_{1}(t)=(t,y_{1}(t))
.
Since
\mathcal{C}_{2}=\{(x,0)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\mid x\in\mathbb{R}\}
we deduce that

(243)

n=\mathrm{order}(y_{1},0)-1=\mathrm{order}(y\circ\gamma_{1},0)-1=\mathrm{order}(y|_{\mathcal{C}_{1}},(0,0))-1.\qed

We also list here another useful property of the order of vanishing.

Lemma A.5
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
be a smooth function and
X,Y\in\mathfrak{X}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
be vector fields, linearly independent at the origin. Let
\mathcal{C}_{1},\mathcal{C}_{2}
be the orbits of the flows of
X
and
X+fY
through the origin, where
f\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, then

(244)

\mathrm{order}(\mathcal{C}_{1}\cap\mathcal{C}_{2},(0,0))=\mathrm{order}(f|_{\mathcal{C}_{1}},(0,0)).

Proof.

If
f(0,0)\neq 0
, since
X,Y
are linearly independent at the origin one has

\mathrm{order}(\mathcal{C}_{1}\cap\mathcal{C}_{2},(0,0))=0=\mathrm{order}(f|_{\mathcal{C}_{1}},(0,0)).

Assume now that
f(0,0)=0
. Since
X,Y
are linearly independent at the origin, there exist coordinates
(x,y)
such that

(245)

X=\partial_{x},\qquad X+fY=\partial_{x}+f(a\partial_{x}+b\partial_{y}),

where
a,b
are some smooth functions and
b(0,0)\neq 0
.
Notice that in these coordinates

\mathcal{C}_{1}=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\,:\,y=0\}.

From
Lemma

A.4
we can infer that

(246)

\displaystyle\mathrm{order}(\mathcal{C}_{1}\cap\mathcal{C}_{2},(0,0))

\displaystyle=\mathrm{order}(y|_{\mathcal{C}_{2}},(0,0))-1=\inf\{k\in\mathbb{N}\,:\,(\partial_{x}+fY)^{(k)}(y)|_{(0,0)}\neq 0\}-1

\displaystyle=\inf\{k\in\mathbb{N}\,:\,(\partial_{x}+fY)^{(k-1)}(\partial_{x}+fY)(y)|_{(0,0)}\neq 0\}-1

\displaystyle=\inf\{k\in\mathbb{N}\,:\,(\partial_{x}+fY)^{(k-1)}(fb)|_{(0,0)}\neq 0\}-1.

By induction one can show that, for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
, there exist smooth functions
a_{0},\dots,a_{k-1}
such that

(247)

(\partial_{x}+fY)^{k}(fb)=b\partial^{k}_{x}f+\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}a_{j}\partial_{x}^{j}f.

Since
b(0,0)\neq 0
, we see that the smallest
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
(\partial_{x}+fY)^{k}(fb)|_{(0,0)}\neq 0
coincides with the smallest
k
for which
\partial_{x}^{k}f(0,0)\neq 0
. In other words

(248)

\displaystyle\inf\{k\in\mathbb{N}\,:\,(\partial_{x}+fY)^{(k-1)}(fb)|_{(0,0)}\neq 0\}

\displaystyle=\inf\{k\in\mathbb{N}\,:\,(\partial_{x})^{(k-1)}(f)|_{(0,0)}\neq 0\}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{order}(f|_{\mathcal{C}_{1}},q)+1.

The proof can be concluded combining the latter equality with (
246
).
∎

A.2.
The degree of a class of singularities

Lemma A.6
.

Let
\nu,\mu\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
and let
k\in\mathbb{N}
and consider the vector field in
\mathbb{R}^{2}

(249)

X=\nu x\partial_{x}+\frac{\mu}{k!}y^{k}\partial_{y}.

Then, the index of the origin can be computed as

(250)

\mathrm{ind}(0,0)=\begin{cases}0,\qquad&k\,\,\text{even},\\
\mathrm{sign}(\nu\mu),\qquad&k\,\,\text{odd}.\end{cases}

Proof.

The index of the origin for
X
coincides with the degree of the map

(251)

\varphi:\mathbb{S}^{1}\to\mathbb{S}^{1},\qquad\varphi(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)=\frac{(\nu\cos\theta,\frac{\mu}{k!}\sin^{k}\theta)}{|(\nu\cos\theta,\frac{\mu}{k!}\sin^{k}\theta)|}.

For
k
even,
\varphi
is not surjective, hence its image is contractible and its degree is zero.
Now assume
k
odd. For
s\in[0,1]
consider the vector field

(252)

X_{s}=\nu x\partial x+\frac{\mu}{k!}\left(sy+(1-s)y^{k}\right)\partial_{y},

and note that the only vanishing point of
X_{s}
is the origin since
k
odd. Therefore the map

(253)

F:[0,1]\times\mathbb{S}^{1}\to\mathbb{S}^{1},\qquad F(s,(x,y))=\left.\frac{X_{s}}{|X_{s}|}\right|_{(x,y)}

is a smooth homotopy. Moreover,
X_{0}=X
, thus the degree at the origin of
X
equals the degree at the origin of
X_{1}=\nu x\partial_{x}+\frac{\mu}{k!}y\partial_{y}
. The latter field has a non degenerate singularity at the origin, therefore

(254)

\mathrm{ind}(0,0)=\mathrm{sign}\det(D_{(0,0)}X_{1})=\mathrm{sign}\left(\det\begin{pmatrix}\nu&0\\
0&\frac{\mu}{k!}\end{pmatrix}\right)=\mathrm{sign}(\nu\mu).

∎

Appendix B
The horizontal plane in the Heisenberg group

In this section, we explicitly compute the sequence
\{K^{\varepsilon}\sigma^{\varepsilon}\}\subset C^{1}(S)^{*}
for the horizontal plane in the Heisenberg group. The following lemma will be used throughout the section.

Lemma B.1
.

Let
\{\psi_{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon>0}\subset L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
be a sequence of non-negative functions. If, for every
\rho>0

(255)

(i)\,\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{B_{\rho}(0)}\psi_{\varepsilon}(x)dx=2\pi,\qquad(ii)\,\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{\rho}(0)}\psi_{\varepsilon}(x)dx=0,

then
\psi_{\varepsilon}\to 2\pi\delta_{0}
weakly in
C^{1}_{c}(S)^{*}
, i.e., for any
\varphi\in C^{1}_{c}(S)
it holds

(256)

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\varphi(x)\psi_{\varepsilon}(x)dx=2\pi\varphi(0).

Proof.

Let
\varphi\in C^{1}_{c}(\mathbb{R})
. Combining
(i)
and
(ii)
one finds

(257)

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\varphi(x)\psi_{\varepsilon}(x)dx-2\pi\varphi(0)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\varphi(x)\psi_{\varepsilon}(x)dx-2\pi\varphi(0)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\varphi(x)\psi_{\varepsilon}(x)dx-\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\varphi(0)\psi_{\varepsilon}(x)dx\right|

\displaystyle=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(\varphi(x)-\varphi(0))\psi_{\varepsilon}(x)dx\right|.

Therefore, for every
\rho>0
we have

(258)

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\varphi(x)\psi_{\varepsilon}(x)dx-2\pi\varphi(0)\right|

\displaystyle=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(\varphi(x)-\varphi(0))\psi_{\varepsilon}(x)dx\right|\leq\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\varphi(x)-\varphi(0)|\psi_{\varepsilon}(x)dx

\displaystyle=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{B_{\rho}(0)}|\varphi(x)-\varphi(0)|\psi_{\varepsilon}(x)dx+\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{\rho}(0)}|\varphi(x)-\varphi(0)|\psi_{\varepsilon}(x)dx

\displaystyle\leq 2\pi\|\varphi-\varphi(0)\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{\rho}(0))}.

Since
\rho>0
is arbitrary and
\varphi
is smooth, taking the limit of
\rho\to 0
of both sides of the latter inequality yields the result.
∎

B.1.
The plane
z=0
in the Heisenberg group

Let
(\mathbb{R}^{3},\mathcal{D},g)
be the Heisenberg group: the contact distribution
\mathcal{D}
is expressed as the kernel of the normalized contact form
\omega
which, in cylindrical coordinates
x=r\cos\theta,y=r\sin\theta,z
, reads

(259)

\omega=dz+\frac{r^{2}}{2}d\theta.

The extended metrics (
3
) have the expression

(260)

g^{\varepsilon}=dr^{2}+r^{2}d\theta^{2}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\omega\otimes\omega.

Let
S=\{(x,y,z)\mid z=0\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Substituting the expression of
\omega
into the one of
g^{\varepsilon}
and imposing
dz=0
we obtain

(261)

g^{\varepsilon}|_{S}=dr^{2}+f_{\varepsilon}(r)^{2}d\theta^{2},\qquad\text{where}\qquad f_{\varepsilon}(r)=r\sqrt{1+\frac{r^{2}}{4\varepsilon}}.

A standard computation shows that

(262)

K^{\varepsilon}\sigma^{\varepsilon}=-f^{\prime\prime}_{\varepsilon}(r)drd\theta=-\frac{r\left(r^{2}+6\varepsilon\right)}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}\left(r^{2}+4\varepsilon\right)^{3/2}}drd\theta.

The form
d\alpha
in the statement of
Theorem

1.2
can be computed as

(263)

d\alpha=-\left(\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\sqrt{\varepsilon}\frac{r\left(r^{2}+6\varepsilon\right)}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}\left(r^{2}+4\varepsilon\right)^{3/2}}\right)drd\theta=-drd\theta.

Lemma B.2
.

For every
\varphi\in C^{1}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, the following equality holds

(264)

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\varphi\left(K^{\varepsilon}\sigma^{\varepsilon}-\frac{\mu_{-1}}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\right)=2\pi\varphi(0).

Proof.

Based on the computations above, we can write

(265)

K^{\varepsilon}\sigma^{\varepsilon}-\frac{d\alpha}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}=\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}-\frac{r\left(r^{2}+6\varepsilon\right)}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}\left(r^{2}+4\varepsilon\right)^{3/2}}\right)drd\theta=\left(\frac{1}{r\sqrt{\varepsilon}}-\frac{\left(r^{2}+6\varepsilon\right)}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}\left(r^{2}+4\varepsilon\right)^{3/2}}\right)dxdy.

It is sufficient to show that the function

(266)

\psi_{\varepsilon}=\frac{1}{r\sqrt{\varepsilon}}-\frac{\left(r^{2}+6\varepsilon\right)}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}\left(r^{2}+4\varepsilon\right)^{3/2}},

satisfies the hypothesis of
Lemma

B.1
. Notice that
\psi_{\varepsilon}>0
. It remains to show
(i)
and
(ii)
.

(i)
Let
\rho>0
, then

(267)

\displaystyle\int_{B_{\rho}(0)}\psi_{\varepsilon}(x,y)dxdy

\displaystyle=2\pi\int_{0}^{\rho}\left(-f_{\varepsilon}^{\prime\prime}(r)+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\right)dr=2\pi\left.\left(-f^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}(r)+\frac{r}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\right)\right|_{0}^{\rho}

\displaystyle=2\pi\left(-\frac{2\varepsilon+\rho^{2}}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}\sqrt{4\varepsilon+\rho^{2}}}+\frac{\rho}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\right)+2\pi,

where we have used the explict expression of
f^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}

(268)

\displaystyle f^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}(r)=\frac{2\varepsilon+r^{2}}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}\sqrt{4\varepsilon+r^{2}}}.

Taking the limit of
\varepsilon\to 0
of both sides in (
267
) gives

(269)

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{B_{\rho}(0)}\psi_{\varepsilon}(x,y)dxdy

\displaystyle=2\pi+2\pi\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{\rho\sqrt{4\varepsilon+\rho^{2}}-\rho^{2}-2\varepsilon}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}\sqrt{4\varepsilon+\rho^{2}}}

\displaystyle=2\pi+2\pi\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{\rho^{2}\sqrt{1+\frac{4\varepsilon}{\rho^{2}}}-\rho^{2}-2\varepsilon}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}\sqrt{4\varepsilon+\rho^{2}}}

\displaystyle=2\pi+2\pi\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{\rho^{2}+2\varepsilon+o(\varepsilon)-\rho^{2}-2\varepsilon}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}\sqrt{4\varepsilon+\rho^{2}}}=2\pi,

which is condition
(i)
of
Lemma

B.1
.

(ii)
Fix
\rho>0
, then, for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{\rho}(0)
we have

(270)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus B_{\rho}\rho(0)}|\psi_{\varepsilon}(x,y)|dxdy=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus B_{\rho}(0)}\psi_{\varepsilon}(x,y)dxdy

\displaystyle=2\pi\int_{\rho}^{+\infty}\left(-f_{\varepsilon}^{\prime\prime}(r)+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\right)dr=\frac{2\varepsilon+\delta^{2}}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}\sqrt{4\varepsilon+\rho^{2}}}-\frac{\rho}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}.

Repeating the computation in the proof of point
(i)
and taking the limit as
\varepsilon\to 0
yields point
(ii)
.
∎
