The effects of different post-activation potentiation strategies on the performance of elite female track cyclists in position 1 of team sprint
Authors: Tianhe Li, Yapu Liang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The study aimed to optimise post-activation potentiation (PAP) strategies for Rider 1 in elite team sprints to improve performance over 250 m (opening lap), with a focus on female cyclists. Eight national-level track cyclists participated in this study, undergoing four sets of activation strategies: control (CON), dynamic high inertia (DYN, 4 × 4 pedal strokes), isometric contraction (ISO, 4 × 4 s, 4 angles), and back squat activation (BSQ, 4 × 4 rep, 80%1RM). The tests were divided into pre-activation and post-activation phases, including measurements at 4 min, 8 min, and 12 min after activation. The tests included a 250 m time trial (TT) and segment timing, with measurements of peak torque, peak power, average power, and cadence. The mean cadence, torque, and power for the first 62.5 m of pedal revolutions were collected. Paired-sample t-tests were used to assess activation differences. Multiple group comparisons were conducted using analysis of variance (ANOVA). The Bonferroni correction was used to control Type I errors. For significant activation strategies, linear or non-linear regression was applied to extrapolate the torque-cadence and power-cadence profiles, and the parameter differences were examined to investigate profile changes. Cohen’s d and Cohen’s f were used as effect sizes. After DYN activation, the 250 m TT significantly improved (p = 0.018), primarily through a reduction in the 62.5 m time (p = 0.006) and an increase in peak torque (p = 0.018). After 12 min of ISO activation, the 250 m TT showed a large effect but did not reach the significance level under Bonferroni correction (p = 0.135, d = 0.860), with a notable reduction in the 62.5 m time (p = 0.003). PAP can be strategically employed to enhance the performance of elite female Rider 1 in team sprints.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-75464-4.

Introduction

PAP is a mechanism that enhances muscle performance after a high-intensity stimulus1. The mechanisms of PAP can be attributed to both myogenic and neurogenic factors. The classical theory attributes the PAP mechanism primarily to the increased activity of myosin light chain phosphorylation, which subsequently enhances the Ca2+ binding sensitivity with actin’s myofilaments2–4. Additionally, PAP increases synaptic transmission between Ia afferent terminals (nerve endings that originate from muscle spindles) and α motoneurons (alpha motor neurons; neurons that innervate skeletal muscle fibres) of the homonymous muscle and increases the recruitment of higher-order motor units4–6.

Multiple factors influence the performance-enhancing effect of PAP. One key factor is neuromuscular fatigue, which can reduce muscle contraction strength and cell excitability, leading to observable decreases in strength, power, and velocity3. The net effect on the performance of a subsequent explosive activity is determined by the balance between PAP and fatigue3,7. The potentiation-fatigue relationship emphasizes the importance of selecting the appropriate rest time after activation3,8. Meta-analysis studies indicate that activation activities with an intensity of at least 80% of 1RM and a rest interval of 3–12 min will have a positive impact on subsequent explosive activities9,10.

Considering the characteristics of different sports and the application of PAP strategies in competition or training, the types of activation methods are highly varied. For example, studies on taekwondo athletes, rowers, and track cyclists explored activation methods similar to those used in their specific sports disciplines11–13.

For elite athletes, the demands of training and competition are often greater than for regular athletes, requiring highly customised training programs14. The benefits of utilising PAP in elite competitions may potentially maximise elite athletes’ performance. Research indicates that individuals with resistance training experience and greater strength exhibit significantly greater PAP effects compared to beginners8.

Competition in elite track cycling is characterised by a high level of intensity, particularly in the team sprint event. The Team Sprint is a high-intensity cycling event contested over three laps of a 250 m velodrome, covering a total distance of 750 m. The race features a unique format where three riders start simultaneously from a stationary position. Rider 1 (the “Starter”) leads from a start gate for the first lap. After completing Lap 1, Rider 1 exits, and Rider 2 takes the lead for Lap 2. Following lap 2, Rider 2 exits, leaving Rider 3 to complete the final lap solo. The race ends when Rider 3 crosses the finish line.

Recent research by Kordi et al. (2024) has underscored the critical importance of the opening lap in determining overall success15. Their analysis revealed that teams achieving the fastest first lap have a significantly higher probability of securing a medal. This finding emphasizes the strategic value of a powerful start in the Team Sprint, highlighting the need for teams to optimise their performance in the crucial opening phase of the race. Although the first lap lasts 17–18 s for men and 18–19 s for women, performance in this phase is primarily determined by neuromuscular capabilities, such as maximum strength and power, rather than glycolytic energy supply15–17. Specifically, peak torque and peak power output normalised to system mass have been shown to be strong predictors of 15 m, 65 m, and 125 m split times15. These findings suggest that the initial acceleration phase has a disproportionate influence on overall opening lap performance and, consequently, the team’s chances of success. The maximal muscle power output during cycling (primarily contributed by the quadriceps and gluteus maximus) is constrained by the force–velocity properties of muscle contraction, activation-relaxation kinetics, and neuromuscular coordination across the continuum of possible movement frequencies18.

In the studies by Tomaras et al. (2011) and Zhang et al. (2024), an intermittent sprint warm-up and a PAP warm-up were applied, respectively, with both observing an increase in power output after rest19,20. Furthermore, Munro et al. (2017) explored different strategies of a high inertia PAP protocol in track cycling, revealing the feasibility of applying PAP activation strategies to enhance average power13.

Research on PAP in cycling, particularly in specific team sprint positions, is still in its early stages. While PAP has proven effective in other sports, its application in cycling, especially under actual competition conditions, remains underexplored. Most studies focus on ergometer-based tests and male athletes, with limited research on female cyclists, leaving gaps in understanding their response to PAP.

This study aims to optimise PAP strategies for Rider 1 in elite team sprints to improve performance over 250 m (opening lap), with a focus on female cyclists. By evaluating these strategies under real competition conditions, we seek to provide practical guidance and validate the effectiveness of PAP for elite female cyclists.

Methodology

Participants

Eight elite female track cyclists from the Henan Province track cycling sprint team and the Chinese National Cycling Team were selected as participants (mean ± standard deviation (M ± SD): 23.3 ± 1.3 years; n = 8, height: 1.66 ± 0.02 m; weight: 61.96 ± 1.44 kg). The participants had an average of 7.6 ± 0.7 years of experience in sprint track cycling training and strength conditioning. The participants’ best performance in the 250 m lap of the team sprint over the past year was 18.94 ± 0.65 s. The 1RM test prior to the experiment revealed an average back squat 1RM of 135.63 ± 12.37 kg. All participants had engaged in and mastered specialised training for position 1 in the team sprint over the past four years. The selection criteria for participants included the following: a National Level 1 or higher sports ranking and optimal physical health with no major illnesses. All participants voluntarily agreed to participate in the experiment and signed an "Informed Consent Form" that outlined the specifics of the study and its protocols before the commencement of the experiment. The participants were required to complete a "Basic Information Questionnaire" that collected data on their name, age, place of birth, medical history, family history, length of training, sports ranking, and sports experience, among other information. They agreed publication of identifying information in an online open-access publication.

The participants were directed to follow a regular diet for the 24 h preceding the test and to refrain from eating for two hours before the test. During the experiments, only water consumption was allowed. Before the start of the experiment, the staff pre-adjusted the gear ratio (i = 3.64) and ensured they matched the SRM testing equipment. Participants were required to ride ‘LOOK’ track bikes equipped with closed chainrings. They wore competitive cycling clothing and clip-in shoes and were also required to wear sock covers during the ride.

Ethical approval

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Sports Science Experiment Ethics Committee of Beijing Sport University (2023270H).

Study design

This study employed a controlled experiment to investigate the effects of different activation methods on lead cyclists’ performance. Before the experiment, the order of activation for the participants was randomly assigned. Following the allocation sequence, each participant completed one type of activation test per week over four weeks. All participants experienced the same individualised training load during the test period. After a two-day rest and adjustment period, they commenced a seven-day recovery training program, which included both specialised training and physical conditioning. The training regimen included alternating content, load intensity, and load volume to facilitate physical recovery and stabilise competitive performance levels. To account for circadian rhythms, every test was arranged in the early morning between 9:00 and 10:00. The experiment was conducted in the laboratory and training field at the Track Cycling Center in Panyu District, Guangzhou.

Warm up

All participants completed a standardised warm-up consisting of uniform duration and content. Each group’s time was measured individually to control timing. The warm-up consisted of 15 min of low-intensity cycling at a speed of 15 km/h, completing 15 laps. After this, they accelerated for 1 lap at a speed of 25 km/h. A 4-min active recovery period followed the warm-up, allowing athletes to leave the track and gradually decelerate while unlocking their pedals and cycling shoes. Subsequently, with the assistance of the researchers, the wheels were changed to a five-spoke wheel in the front and a disc wheel in the rear, with the entire process completed within 5 min.

Test protocol

The athletes performed three activation protocols and one control protocol, following a randomised but complete test sequence. Each protocol included four tests: before activation (baseline level Pre), 4 min after activation (P4), 8 min after activation (P8), and 12 min after activation (P12). Each test was separated by a 48-h interval, serving as a washout period. According to team sprint rules, the 250 m TT used a standing start, with starts initiated from the two starting lines on either side of the track following a 30-s countdown. Participants’ bicycles and shoes were secured with the assistance of staff members, and participants chose their preferred leg position for the start. Upon the countdown, participants sprinted 250 m, with the first 20 m marked as a no-crossing zone (timing lines are set separately at 62.5 m and 125 m to record the time individually). The time was recorded by the FinishLynx timing system, which was connected to a computer.

The SRM crank power meters (SRM ORIGIN-TRACK CARBON) were pre-installed on the participants’ track bikes and calibrated. To ensure accurate sampling points, the power meter was connected to a mobile device (Pad) and a computer. Peak torque, peak power output, and average power output were directly measured using a crank-based power meter and displayed via the SRMX software on the mobile device. The script files used to synchronise the start and end of the dynamometer and timing system have been pre-installed. Video recordings and Dartfish (a video analysis software for technique analysis) were synchronised to capture the start and sprint times, serving as an additional safeguard for the sampling intervals and timing21.

The researchers recorded participants’ performance in the 250 m TT (time taken for a sprint from a standing start over 250 m, with additional segment timings at the 62.5 m and 125 m marks), peak torque, peak power and average power. After completing the test, athletes conducted self-relaxation and organisation exercises, followed by static stretching, which lasted approximately 20 min. This cool down included slow, relaxed cycling and stretching of the quadriceps, hamstrings, and gluteus maximus, as well as foam rolling.

During the experimental testing period, participants followed the same training program, maintaining a moderate load and low intensity and volume. Exercise type, intensity, and duration were arranged in an alternating manner to ensure that participants’ performance remained stable without significant fluctuations, thereby maintaining their competitive state.

Activation protocol

Control protocol

The Control Group (CON) refrained from engaging in activation interventions, instead adopting an approach of active recovery. This involved a leisurely cycling session lasting 12 min, with the speed kept below 10 km/h, thus ensuring the session’s duration was broadly consistent with the timeframes employed for the other activation interventions.

Dynamic PAP protocol

The Dynamic High Inertia Load Group (DYN) simulated the load experienced during acceleration using a modified indoor cycling trainer. It features a unique flywheel weighing 40 kg, with a breadth of 8 cm, a diameter of 70 cm, and a gear ratio of 6:1. The flywheel’s rim is fitted with rubber rollers and tension springs. During activation, participants were required to perform out-of-saddle pedalling in a standing start position using their dominant leg. They completed 4 full pedal strokes per leg, repeated for 4 sets with a 2-min rest interval between sets.

Isometric PAP protocol

In the Specific Isometric Contraction Group (ISO), the pedal and flywheel were immobilised using a hydraulic jack and steel metal rod to minimise rotation. Participants selected their dominant leg and perform out-of-saddle pedalling with maximum force for 4 s, maintaining peak effort13,22. This was repeated for 4 sets with a 2-min rest period between sets. Four different crank positions were tested: 45°, 60°, 90°, and 135°each for 4 s.

The selection of 45°, 60°, 90°, and 135° as the ISO PAP activation angles is based on a comprehensive consideration of optimising muscle coordination and energy transfer, torque–angle relationship, joint mechanics and muscle activation, muscle fatigue accumulation, and biomechanical efficiency13,23–27.

From 30° to 150°, within the propulsive phase (downstroke), this range includes the optimal activation angles and torque generation for the knee extensors (VAS), knee flexors (HAM), and hip extensors (GMAX)28. This strategy is also based on previous research on isometric contractions in cycling and has been appropriately refined13,27.

Back Squat PAP protocol

The Back Squat Group (BSQ) performs barbell squats with an 80% 1-repetition maximum (1RM) load. The squats were controlled, with participants completing 4 repetitions, repeated for 4 sets with a 2-min rest interval between sets. Prior to this study,, a 1RM barbell squat test was conducted according to the NSCA 1RM protocol during physical training sessions to determine the load corresponding to 80% of each athlete’s 1RM29. The squat activation strategy was chosen based on previous research showing that loads of ≥ 80% 1RM effectively induce PAP9,10. The volume was adjusted to match the stress levels of the other activation strategies.

The study employed a 2-min rest interval between sets. Research on PAP strategies in rowing and track cycling has demonstrated the effectiveness of a 2-min rest interval between sets12,13. This design also considers the overall smoothness and efficiency of the experiment.

Where T is the average pedal torque and PR is the pedalling rate (cadence). The constant a < 0 specifies the decline in average pedal force with increasing cadence, while b represents the individual’s maximum average pedal torque Tmax.

Based on these functions, parameters such as the current maximum torque (Tmax = b), maximum cadence (PRmax =—b/a), maximum power output (Pmax = -b2/4a), and the related optimal cadence (PRopt = -b/2a) can be derived. To distinguish between peak torque (Tpeak) and maximum torque (Tmax), the following definitions are applied: peak torque refers to the highest measured torque during a TT; maximum torque refers to the y-axis intersection of the T-c profile.

The analysis of torque-cadence relationships and power-cadence relationships (field tests) differs from the direct measurement methods mentioned earlier. It is based on the study by Dunst et al. (2021) and Gardner et al. (2007)17,30. The mean torque (Nm) and mean power (W) at the pedals, averaged per revolution, as well as the corresponding mean pedal rate (PR or cadence) in rpm, were derived. The data from the first 8 cycles after a standing start were collected as the configuration profile. However, it is worth noting that applying this method in the experiment investigating the effect of PAP activation on the 250 m TT may have some limitations31.

Statistical analysis

The recorded data were organised in Excel, with participant personal information replaced by ID numbers to ensure blinding during data analysis. SPSS 27.0 was used for statistical analysis. The data are presented as the M ± SD for all variables. Normality and equality of variances were evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk and Levene’s tests, respectively. The least squares method was employed to fit linear and quadratic regression models for the torque-cadence and power-cadence relationships, respectively.

To assess the differences before activation among the four groups (CON, DYN, ISO, and BSQ), analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used, with ηp2 and Cohen’s f as effect sizes. To evaluate the post-activation differences between different PAP protocols (CON, DYN, ISO, and BSQ), including the 250 m TT and segment performance, paired-sample t-tests were employed, calculating Cohen’s d and confidence intervals as effect sizes. To evaluate the differences in post-activation performance between experimental and control groups among the trials, including peak torque, peak power output, and average power output, paired-sample t-tests were used, calculating Cohen’s d and confidence intervals as effect sizes. Bonferroni correction was used for multiple comparisons between different experimental groups and the control group.

For the groups that showed significant improvement after the activation intervention, an analysis of the torque-cadence and power-cadence relationship profiles was conducted, including a t-test of the profile parameters. If more than two groups were compared, a one-way ANOVA was applied17,30,31.

The significance level was set at p < 0.05. Cohen’s d greater than 0.8 was considered large, between 0.8 and 0.5 was categorised as medium, between 0.5 and 0.2 was considered small, and less than 0.2 was deemed insignificant. Cohen’s f greater than 0.40 was considered large, between 0.40 and 0.25 was categorised as medium, between 0.25 and 0.10 was considered small, and less than 0.10 was deemed insignificant32–34.

Results

Before activation, there were no significant differences among the four groups in the 0–62.5 m segment timing (p > 0.05, ηp2 = 0.006, Cohen’s f = 0.075, insignificant), 62.5-125 m segment timing (p > 0.05, ηp2 = 0.001, Cohen’s f = 0.028, insignificant), and 125-250 m segment timing (p > 0.05, ηp2 = 0.002, Cohen’s f = 0.050, insignificant). Additionally, before activation, there were no significant differences among the four groups in the 250 m TT (p > 0.05, ηp2 = 0.001, Cohen’s f = 0.025, insignificant), peak torque (p > 0.05, ηp2 = 0.080, Cohen’s f = 0.294, medium), peak power output (p > 0.05, ηp2 = 0.001, Cohen’s f = 0.025, insignificant), and average power output (p > 0.05, ηp2 = 0.003, Cohen’s f = 0.054, insignificant).

Discussion

The principal aim of this study was to investigate the effects of three different activation strategies on performance in the first lap of the team sprint event for elite female track cyclists. We examined changes in split times, peak power output, average power output, peak torque and torque-cadence and power-cadence profiles at various time points post-activation. Our results reveal significant performance improvements, with variations in the affected parameters, extent, and timing of the greatest effect depending on the specific strategy. Participants exhibited an improvement in cycling performance 4 min after performing the DYN strategy. In contrast, performance enhancements were observed 12 min following the execution of the ISO strategy.

Our findings align with those of previous research. As reported by Hara et al. (2020), the DYN strategy led to significant improvements in rowing performance metrics, such as distance and mean power (Pmean), among experienced female rowers, demonstrating a stronger effect than the ISO strategy12. Similarly, Munro et al. (2016) observed enhancements in Pmean and alterations in the power-cadence profile in cycling performance four minutes post-DYN and sixteen minutes post-ISO strategy13. However, discrepancies exist between these findings and our study regarding the optimal timing and effectiveness of PAP strategies. These differences may be due to variations in experimental materials, activation protocols, data processing, and statistical methodologies used across studies.

In accordance with Gardner (2007) and Dunst et al. (2021, 2022, 2023) regression analysis of the torque, cadence and power data obtained during the initial acceleration phase revealed linear T-c and quadratic P–c functions with high quality, indicating them as valid16,17,30,31,35.

Considering the observed alterations in their characteristic profile parameters, the DYN strategy resulted in a notable enhancement in maximal torque (Tmax), thereby facilitating an increase in initial acceleration due to the elevated torque generation in accordance with Newton’s second law (F = ma)36. This increase is likely to have contributed to the higher Ppeak achieved during acceleration, which is crucial for achieving top speed and improving total TT performance.

Following ISO activation, a reduction in the steepness of the T-c slope and a rightward shift in PRopt were observed. This shift, reflecting an increase in fatigue-free PRopt, likely reduced the discrepancy between actual and optimal cadence, which has been demonstrated to decrease with fatigue16. This, in turn, has been shown to enhance mean power output and overall TT performance16,35. Our findings indicate that the change above resulted in a significant increase in average power and was associated with a substantial reduction in the 250 m TT duration.

The DYN strategy involves explosive, high-inertia movements. This approach activates the Type II (fast-twitch) muscle fibres in the lower limbs and promotes myosin regulatory light chain phosphorylation37. This phosphorylation enhances the binding affinity between myosin and actin, leading to faster and stronger, which facilitates sarcomere sliding and increases overall muscle force output38. This process further explains the observed increase in Tmax, as the enhanced contractile properties allow for greater torque generation during the initial phase of the sprint.

While theoretically, the increased activation of Type II fibres could contribute to an improvement in PRmax, the DYN focus on generating Tmax may not optimally enhance the neuromuscular efficiency required to sustain peak cadence. It is important to note that PRmax is influenced not only by muscle strength but also by factors such as neuromuscular coordination, the muscle length-tension relationship, and muscular endurance during movement39. Therefore, while force output may be increased, this does not necessarily translate into an increase in PRmax.

However, if there is an extended rest period after activation exceeding 4 min, calcium ion saturation in the muscle fibres may occur. This saturation can diminish the efficacy of the myosin regulatory light chain phosphorylation process, as the elevated levels of calcium ions may overload the sarcoplasmic reticulum and impair further calcium release40. This would reduce muscle contraction efficiency, weakening the potentiation effect and possibly decreasing the muscle’s ability to generate force effectively in subsequent explosive efforts41. Evidence from studies indicates that Ca2⁺ saturation limits the influence of phosphorylation on peak isometric force production, and this phenomenon could explain the reduced performance after prolonged rest intervals42.

The ISO strategy involves performing maximal voluntary contractions (MVC) at key angles that optimise skeletal muscle activation efficiency. This strategy activates central mechanisms, such as improved motor unit recruitment and increased neural excitability. MVC improves motor unit recruitment by engaging a greater number of muscle fibres, which enhances neuromuscular efficiency and force output, particularly in high-intensity efforts43. This recruitment leads to more effective coordination between muscle fibres, improving overall performance.

The increased excitability of the nervous system following MVC results in a higher firing frequency of motor neurons, meaning that electrical impulses are sent to the muscles at a faster rate44. This leads to quicker neuromuscular responses and stronger force production45. Building on previous research and our findings, it can be inferred that these adaptations lead to rapid force generation and improved coordination, especially at higher cadences46. Furthermore, isometric contractions cause changes in the physical properties of the muscles, particularly in the tendons, which may become stiffer47. Studies have also noted that PAP can result in changes in pennation angle, which can influence the transmission of force3,48. These enhanced the efficiency of force transmission during subsequent high-speed dynamic movements and led to an increase in PRmax.

The BSQ strategy theoretically has the potential to activate the strength and explosiveness of the quadriceps and gluteal muscles in the lower limbs49,50. However, no significant overall effects were observed in this study, at least for elite female athletes. This could be attributed to the strategy involving alternating concentric and eccentric contractions of the leg muscles, which does not match the movement pattern of a cycling sprint, as well as unnecessary fatigue accumulation in the lower back and individual differences among the athletes51.

In the PAP potentiation-fatigue dynamic balance theory, although fatigue gradually diminishes over time, the decay of the enhancement effect caused by peripheral mechanisms may occur more rapidly3. This further explains why the DYN strategy is only effective within 4 min post-activation. Unlike the DYN strategy, the ISO strategy produces longer-lasting effects, with significant improvements observed up to 12 min post-activation. This can be attributed to the potentiation-fatigue dynamic balance, where the MVC-induced potentiation results in longer-lasting changes in central mechanisms and the mechanical properties of skeletal muscles and tendons3.

Limitations of the study

The study was limited to a small sample size of eight elite female track cyclists. This restricts the generalizability of the findings to a broader population of cyclists, including male cyclists and cyclists at different performance levels. The study focused exclusively on three specific activation strategies (dynamic high inertia, isometric contraction, and back squat activation). The effects of other potentially beneficial activation methods were not explored. The study assessed performance improvements only in the immediate timeframe following activation strategies. The long-term impacts of these activation methods on athlete performance and training adaptation were not examined. While efforts were made to control external variables, the influence of factors such as environmental conditions, individual cyclists’ daily physical conditions, and psychological factors on performance was not fully accounted for. The criteria for creating valid maximal torque (T-c) and power (P–c) profiles, as outlined by Dunst et al. (2021), may not have been fully met in this study due to the inclusion of grid points from the first 7 s of the TT31. This methodological decision could result in an overestimation of true maximal torque while potentially underestimating maximal cadence. However, given that this approach was applied consistently across all trials to derive the profiles associated with each TT, it allows for a valid assessment of differences between these profiles.

Conclusion

The use of high-inertia activation methods, such as dynamic high-inertia and isometric activation, can enhance performance in the first lap (250 m TT) of the female track cycling team sprint, particularly by improving split times over either the first or last 62.5 m. These improvements are associated with changes in maximal torque following dynamic high-inertia activation or maximal cadence after isometric activation, leading to better acceleration (improved Tmax/kg) and higher mean power output, likely due to a reduced gap between actual and optimal cadence. The results also indicate that the timing of PAP strategies is crucial for enhancing performance and varies depending on the strategy: dynamic high-inertia activation peaked at 4 min post-activation, while isometric activation showed significant effects 12 min post-activation. Contrary to previous assumptions, the back squat does not equally improve field track cycling standing start performance, at least for females. In practice, it is important to consider the feasibility of activation methods and the scheduling of warm-up times before competition.
