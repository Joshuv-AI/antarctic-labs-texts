Association between clock genes polygenic risk score for depression and obesity in a population with high burden of obesity
Authors: Hadil Adnan Abdulkader, Maha Al-Asmakh, Zumin Shi, Mashael Al-Shafai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCircadian rhythms disruption causes clustering of metabolic disorders and depression. The comorbidity of depression and obesity is common and could be attributable to shared genetic background. We aimed to examine the association between clock genes polygenic risk score (PRS) for depression and obesity among Qatari adults. The interaction between PRS and lifestyle factors was also examined.MethodThis cross-sectional study was conducted among 6,000 Qatari adults who participated in the Qatar Biobank Study. A polygenic risk scores (PRS) was constructed based on 185 single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) within 18 clock genes. Dietary patterns were constructed using factor analysis based on food frequency intake. The association between exposure variables including clock genes PRS, lifestyle factors, and obesity was assessed by multivariable logistic regression models.ResultsThe mean age of the participants was 40.2 (13.0). The prevalence of obesity was 42.9%. A positive association between clock genes PRS and obesity was found. After adjusting for sociodemographic and lifestyle factors, across quartiles of PRS, the odds ratio (95%CI) for obesity were 1.00, 1.24, 1.19, 1.26 (95%CI 1.08–1.39), respectively. There was a borderline significant interaction between PRS and a mixed dietary pattern in relation to obesity (p for interaction 0.072). The association between PRS and obesity was only observed among those with a high intake of mixed dietary pattern. No statistically significant interactions between PRS with smoking, physical activity and other dietary patterns were found. However, the association between PRS and obesity was only observed among non-smokers, those with moderate physical activity, a low intake of sweet/fast food pattern, and high intake of modern breakfast dietary pattern.ConclusionClock genes PRS is positively associated with obesity and interacts with dietary pattern.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12986-025-01030-y.

Background

Obesity has increased recently forming a serious public health crisis, with at least 2.8 million people dying every year due to obesity [1]. Although this rise is caused by multifactorial factors, changes in lifestyle and diet have been linked to the increase in obesity [2]. Obesity is a major risk factor for metabolic diseases such as cardiovascular diseases, and type 2 diabetes mellitus [3]. Several studies showed that the disruption of circadian rhythm is related to negative health consequences, including obesity and cardiovascular disease [4].

Circadian rhythms regulate fundamental biological processes and are maintained by a transcriptional auto-regulatory feedback loop including core clock genes [5]. There has been rising interest in the circadian system’s role in metabolic phenotypes like obesity. Several studies found an association between disruption of circadian rhythm and obesity [6–10]. Circadian disruption could have a broad effect on glucose metabolism and fluid balance and induce inflammation, thrombosis, and endothelial dysfunction [62]. Shift work is also a well-known cause of chronic circadian disruption because of dysregulation in the sleep-wake cycle, fasting-feeding cycle, and light exposure at night-time. Besides, shift work has been significantly linked with increased triglyceride levels, decreased HDL cholesterol levels, obesity, and type 2 diabetes (14, 149, 150, 151, 152). Furthermore, social jetlag, a behavior triggered by misalignment between the central and peripheral clocks leading to chronic circadian dysregulation, has been linked with inflammation, obesity, metabolic dysfunction and depression (15, 153). A study that used wild-type mice to induce chronic jetlag leading to circadian disruption showed the development of leptin resistance which is a hallmark of human obesity and could be associated with the development of circadian disruption-induced obesity and metabolic syndrome [11]. Indeed, Zimmet et al. proposed a recent concept of “circadian syndrome” highlighting the significance of circadian rhythms in the pathogenesis of several metabolic disorders and depression [47].

Current evidence has revealed a link between certain genetic polymorphisms in core clock genes and increased obesity risk among several populations [3, 12, 13]. For instance, some polymorphisms in the CLOCK gene including rs3749474, rs4580704, and rs1801260 are found to be linked with body mass index, energy consumption, and obesity [14, 15]. Indeed, it has been demonstrated that individuals who carry these polymorphisms are characterized as eating more energy and fat, sleeping less,, and being obese [16]. However, Monteleone et al. observed that rs1801260 (T3111C) SNP of CLOCK gene was not associated with obesity while it affected obese participants to have higher body mass index (BMI) [17]. Polymorphisms in PER3 clock gene were found to be related to extreme obesity in the Brazilian population [3]. Additionally, an association between REV-ERBα gene polymorphism and obesity was observed in Spanish obese male participants [18]. In contrast, polymorphism in the CRY1 gene was negatively linked with central obesity risk among Chinese adults [12].

While most studies provide data on the association of clock gene polymorphisms with obesity risk, they have primarily focused on Western populations and their findings may not generalize to populations with diverse genetic backgrounds. Therefore, further research is needed to better understand the association between clock gene variants with obesity risk in populations like Qatari that are disproportionately impacted by obesity. The population of Qatar is recognized by a very small size with a high percentage of consanguinity [19] and about 70% of the adults are overweight or obese [20]. Applying polygenic architecture profiling by calculating PRS, which refers to the cumulative risk given by genetic variants across the genome, could improve disease prediction [21]. Also, PRSs have revealed potential clinical value in risk factor identification [22], population-level risk stratification [23, 24], and helping in risk-reducing behaviors [25].

To our knowledge, the interaction between clock genes and lifestyle factors in relation to obesity is not well-studied among the Qatari adult population. Therefore, this cross-sectional study aims to assess the association between clock gene PRS for depression, lifestyle factors, and obesity using data from Qatar BioBank Study (QBB) as well as to test the interaction between clock genes and lifestyle factors with obesity. As obesity and depression share a common etiology, circadian disruption, we hypothesized that clock gene PRS for depression is positively associated with obesity.

Method

Study population

A cross-sectional study was conducted using data from adult participants (18 years and above) from the Qatar Biobank (QBB) study. QBB is a population-based survey with more than 14,000 participants. The recruitment process and data collection by QBB have been thoroughly described in the previous report [28]. The current analysis used all the available information on sociodemographic characteristics, lifestyle factors including dietary intake, and BMI, and clock gene polymorphisms. Data about sociodemographic characteristics and lifestyle factors were collected using a questionnaire while the weight and height of participants were measured by research nurses at QBB using a Seca stadiometer. All the participants who had genetic data available in QBB were included in the study (N = 6000).

The research protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of QBB (E-2021-QF-QBB-RES-ACC-00061–0178) and the Institutional Review Board of Qatar University (QU-IRB 1978-E/23).

Genetic data

Whole Genome Sequencing (WGS) was performed as part of the Qatar Genome Program using the Illumina HiSeq TenX platform resulting in raw sequencing reads. Using the bcl2fastq software from Illumina, a paired FASTQ format was conducted from raw sequencing reads. Then, FASTQ files were aligned against the reference genome sequence (GRCh37) through the bwakit program (v.0.7.11). Variant calling was conducted using GATK 3.4 and then individuals vcf’s were going through a joint calling step to transform into multi-sample VCF files for all participants. Therefore, the genomic data was received in a VCF file containing selected 185 SNPs in 18 clock genes that were chosen based on existing literature [26]. Supplementary Table 1 presents the list of the 185 SNPs selected for PRS.

Outcome variable: obesity

A BMI of 25–29.9 kg/m2 was considered overweight while obesity was described as a BMI of ≥ 30 kg/m2 [27].

Exposure variable: PRS

Clock gene polygenic risk analysis was performed based on the existing literature [26, 30]. Initially, the selected datasets for the genome-wide association studies (GWAS) discovery samples were the Psychiatric Genomics Consortium (PGC), second major depression disorder dataset (MDD2) and the 2018 bipolar disorder dataset (BIP2018). The MDD2 dataset contained 75,607 cases and 344,901 controls while the BIP2018 dataset contained 20,352 cases and 31,258 controls of the European ancestry population. The data of these datasets are available online on the PGC website.

The selection of the European ancestry population is based on a significant correlation between heritability and allele frequency between Europeans and the Qatar Genome Program (QGP) [31]. The target phenotype of the study is obesity which is different from the phenotypic trait in discovery samples because of the lack of sufficient available GWASs for traits related to obesity. However, the examined phenotypic trait could be different from the phenotype in the discovery sample allowing cross-phenotype analyses [30].

After finding the discovery samples, PRS was calculated for each participant in the target sample as the sum of the associated alleles weighted by their effect sizes in the discovery samples, measured as the logarithm of the odds ratio (OR). The SNPs frequency with alleles C/G or A/T was individually reviewed to avoid any possible strand ambiguity.

Covariates

The assessment of dietary intake as part of lifestyle factors was performed through a self-administered computerized food frequency questionnaire (FFQ). This questionnaire included the dietary habits, alterations, and consumption frequency of drinks and food throughout the past year [28]. The FFQ included 102 food items and was adapted from the European Prospective Investigation into Cancer and Nutrition (EPIC) research, which is under validation in Qatar [29]. Based on similarities in nutritional profiles or cooking techniques, the food items were divided into 38 food groups for the analysis. To create dietary patterns, the frequency consumption (times/week) of the 38 food categories was utilized as the input variable using factor analysis. The number of dietary patterns was determined depending on the following criteria: a scree plot (Supplementary Fig. 1), an eigenvalue greater than 1, and the food patterns to be interpretable according to the food culture of Qatar. Accordingly, three dietary patterns were found: (1) sweets/fast food; (2) mixed dietary pattern; and (3) modern breakfast. The 3 variables explained 32.9% of the variation in food intake (Supplementary Table 2).

We consider self-reported age, gender, education level, smoking, leisure time physical activity as covariates. Leisure time physical activity level was measured by the international physical activity questionnaire and categorized as tertiles based on total metabolic equivalent of task (MET).

Statistical analysis

Descriptive data were presented as the mean (SD) (95%) or as a percentage. For categorical variables, Chi-square tests were performed to compare the differences while ANOVA was used to compare the differences in continuous variables between groups. Multivariable logistic regression models were performed to examine the association between exposure variables (e.g., clock gene PRS) and obesity. A set of models were used: model 1 adjusted for age and sex; and model 2 further adjusted for education, smoking, and physical activity.

Multiplicative interactions (moderation effects) between clock gene RPS and lifestyle factors were tested by adding the product terms of the factors in the logistic regression model. Sensitivity analyses were also conducted by subgroup analyses of sex, education, smoking status, and physical activity. In subgroup analyses, dietary patterns were dichotomized as low or high intake based on the median intake. P for interaction was derived using the contrast command in STATA. To test the linear trend, the quartiles of PRS were modeled as a continuous variable in multivariable logistic regression. To assess the potential non-linear association between PRS and obesity, we used the restricted cubic spline method in logistic regression. Three knots were put at 10th, 50th, and 90th of PRS. The result was visually presented.

To examine the association between sociodemographic and lifestyle factors with obesity, a multivariable logistic regression model was used by including age, sex, education, smoking status, and physical activity. Effect sizes were presented as odds ratios (95%CI). Statistical significance was considered when P < 0.05. All the data analyses were performed by using the STATA 18 software.

Results

Sample characteristics

Using QBB data, 6,000 participants (3,368 females) were included in this study. The demographic characteristics of the sample categorized by quartiles of PRS are summarized in Table 1. Participants were 40.2 (SD = 13.0) years old on average, and 51.5% (n = 3,081) had low education levels. The majority were non-smokers (n = 4,111, 68.5%). The total leisure-time physical activity was low (median 2.5 (inter quartile range 0–21) MET hours/week). The mean BMI and waist circumference was 29.6 kg/m2 (SD = 6.1) and 89.2 cm (SD = 14.2), respectively. Based on BMI, 34.8%, and 42.9% of the participants were overweight and obese. Across quartiles of PRS, there was no significant difference in age, gender, and education. The prevalence of smoking and physical activity differed across PRS quartiles. The prevalence of obesity was the lowest in the first quartile of PRS (39.9%).

The clock gene PRS was normally distributed (Supplementary Fig. 2).

A statistically significant association was found between PRS and obesity before and after adjusting for potential confounding variables such as age and gender (Model 1. Further adjusting for education, smoking, and physical activity (Model 2) did not change the association. Subgroup analyses were conducted to examine the connection between PRS quartiles and lifestyle factors concerning obesity (Table 3). There was a marginal significant interaction between PRS and mixed dietary pattern (p for interaction 0.072). PRS was positively associated with obesity among those with a high intake of mixed dietary pattern. No such association was found among those with a low intake of mixed dietary pattern. Although there was no significant multiplicative interaction between PRS and sociodemographic and other lifestyle factors in relation to obesity, the association between PRS and obesity were only significant in some subgroups. The positive association between quartiles of PRS and obesity was only found in females, those with a high education level, non-smokers, those with a low intake of sweets/fast food, high intake of modern breakfast. Comparing extreme quartiles of PRS, the risk of obesity in non-smokers was 1.4 times greater. Among those with a low consumption of sweets/fast food, high PRS had a 1.4 times greater likelihood of having obesity. However, among those with high consumption of modern breakfast, PRS had a strong association with obesity. In RCS analysis, the association between PRS and obesity was linear (Supplement Fig. 2).

Association between sociodemographic factors and obesity

Further analysis of obesity in association with PRS quartiles and sociodemographic factors is presented in Table 4. A positive association was observed between age and obesity, with each 1-year increment associated with a 4% increment in risk of obesity. Gender was the strongest factor that influenced the risk of obesity, women had a 51% increased likelihood of having obesity than men.

Participants with high levels of education had a 40% decreased risk of obesity when compared to those with low education levels. Similarly, smoker had a decreased prevalence of obesity as compared with non-smokers. Physical activity was inversely associated with obesity. Compared with tertile 1 of physical activity, tertile 3 had a 19% decreased risk of obesity.

Discussion

The prevalence of obesity in Qatar is among the greatest in the world [32]. To our knowledge, the current study is the first to analyze the association between the PRS with obesity within-the-clock genes and lifestyle factors among adults in Qatar. The main finding of the study was a significant positive association between clock gene PRS and obesity which was independent of age, sex, education, smoking, and physical activity. There was a borderline significant interaction between PRS and mixed dietary pattern with high loadings of both healthy and unhealthy food items. Overall, the level of physical activity was low. High physical activity was inversely associated with obesity.

Comparisons with other studies

Our findings on the positive association between clock gene PRS and obesity was in line with the current knowledge. Several studies observed significant associations of polymorphisms in circadian genes with obesity [3, 13, 14, 17, 18, 34, 35]. For example, gene polymorphisms of PER3 were found to be positively linked with obesity [3]. The study of Riestra et al. on the interaction of 23 CLOCK gene SNPs and BMI in an African American population found that the CLOCK variants rs3792603 and rs6820823 were nominally connected with reduced BMI while the variant rs11726609 was nominally linked with raised BMI [36]. However, most previous studies have considered genetic variants of a single clock gene in relation to obesity. In our study, we covered 185 SNPs in 18 clock genes [26]. A PRS for obesity was generated by 12 SNPs in 6 clock genes indicating a significant association with BMI within the Spain cohort study [37]. Another PRS for obesity was created by common genetic variants for BMI demonstrating an interaction between obesity PRS and obesity lifestyle risk index [38]. A recent study by Huls et al. also revealed an interaction between high PRS for obesity, and demographic and lifestyle factors among obese European children aged 2–16 years [33].

Potential mechanisms

Studies reported the concomitance of obesity and depression. A meta-analysis demonstrated a reciprocal link between depression and obesity, suggesting depression as a predictive of developing obesity [39]. A shared etiology of depression and obesity was indicated in a twin study with a genetic correlation of 0.16 [40]. Bahrami et al. study found that 57% of the shared major depression SNPs had a positive association with BMI [41]. This suggests that both obesity and depression have a shared genetic background in disease mechanisms.

Circadian rhythm acts as a shared etiology for both through various interconnected pathways. The circadian machinery is involved in sleep regulation including sleep duration, timing, and quality through the core clock genes [42]. Dashti et al. study found a significant association between higher PRS for sleep duration and the risk of developing obesity and depression highlighting the potential of using sleep duration PRS for identifying the risk of certain health conditions [43]. CLOCK genetic variants were also found to be associated with a reduction in sleep, energy intake, and the development of obesity [44]. Cortisol is a stress hormone following a circadian pattern and is elevated when experiencing stress; dysregulation of endocrine secretion for cortisol can lead to increased insulin levels and buildup of abdominal fat [45]. Leptin and insulin, hormones regulating energy metabolism following a 24-hour rhythm [46], affect mood [21]. Insulin and leptin resistance and metabolic inflammatory signals have been linked to depression. These signals are positively associated with obesity [47].

The circadian clock aligns with individuals’ behavior [46]. A genome-wide association study observed positive correlations between breakfast skipping and obesity, body fat, and depressive symptoms [48]. Late-eating individuals were also found to be 2.1 times more likely to be obese than early-eating with significantly higher values of inflammatory markers like IL6 and CRP. These findings could be explained from a circadian view as late-eaters suffered modifications in the daily body temperature pattern with a 26-minute phase delay and reduced amplitude and in the cortisol pattern with reduced amplitude [49].

Gene-nutrition interaction

In the study we found a borderline significant interaction between PRS and mixed dietary pattern. The dietary intake factor was assessed through 3 dietary patterns including sweets/fast food (high intake of fast food, soft drinks, and desserts), mixed dietary pattern (high intake of chicken/meat/fish, fruits, and vegetables), and modern breakfast (high intake of Yoghurt, milk, bread). These dietary patterns are comparable to published studies from this region that used FFQ-based dietary intakes [50–52]. A study conducted in the Jordanian population reported 3 dietary patterns which were Mediterranean (like modern breakfast), high protein (like mixed dietary), and fast food (like sweets/fast food) patterns [51].

A limited nubmer of studies have examined the relationship between dietary patterns and clock genes. A recent study examined the association between the genetic variant of CRY1 (rs2287161) and unhealthy dietary patterns demonstrating no significant association between rs2287161 and unhealthy dietary patterns on BMI and body fat mass [53]. In the current study, the association between PRS and obesity was significant among those with a low intake of sweets/fast food. While, a strong significant association found between high consumption of mixed dietary patterns and modern breakfast with PRS quartile in regards to obesity. Ding et al. demonstrated that higher diet quality attenuated genetic susceptibility to obesity [54]. A further study showed that individuals who carried the rs2287161 variant of CRY1 had lower BMI when they had the highest adherence to healthy dietary patterns, which included a high intake of vegetables, diaries, fruits, meat, and eggs [53]. Additionally, another study examined whether a combined score representing a healthy diet improved the interaction of genetic variants related to obesity via genetic risk score based on 32 BMI- and 14 waist-hip ratio-associated SNPs. Diet score is calculated based on the consumption of fish, fruits, vegetables, whole grains, and nuts/seeds as favorable food and red/processed meats, sweets, sugar-sweetened beverages, and fried potatoes as unfavorable food. Their findings proposed that the interaction between genetic susceptibility and obesity traits was greater among participants with higher scores for a healthy diet [55]. Overall, the findings of the current study may act as preliminary data on the interaction between dietary intake and clock gene polymorphisms regarding obesity among the Qatari population. Further studies are needed to confirm the result of this study.

Association between sociodemographic and lifestyle factors and obesity

In the present study, a strong association between gender and obesity risk was found as females had a 1.5-fold risk increase compared to men indicating that women are at higher risk of being obese. Also, women with high PRS were positively associated with obesity. This can be explained by the difference in energy metabolism, where females typically have a higher percentage of body fat compared to males beginning with puberty until menopause [56]. Indeed, men have greater degrees of visceral fat, liver fat, and upper extremity mass than women when adjusted for BMI and age [57]. Generally, women have greater rates of obesity than men [58]. Hence, sex-specific countermeasures should be considered for obesity prevention implications.

The current study showed a significant association between education level and obesity as participants with high education levels had a decreased risk of obesity by 40% compared to low levels of education. However, among participants with high education levels, high PRS demonstrated 29% increased obesity risk. Conversely, the Huls et al. study exhibited that participants from families with a low parental education level were at a greater risk of obesity among those with increased genetic susceptibility compared to children from families with a high parental education level [33]. Barcellos et al. showed that education diminished the gap in unhealthy body size among those with a genetic risk of obesity from 20 to 6% [59]. This could be explained by the increased awareness about obesity among educated people [60]. However, in our study high education seems unable to mitigate the risk of obesity in high PRS.

Smokers tend to have lower BMI compared to non-smokers due to the effect of smoking on body fat distribution [61]. In our study, no significant association was found between smoker and ex-smoker participants and obesity PRS quartiles while non-smoker participants had a 1.4 times greater risk of obesity. Thus, smoker and ex-smoker participants had a decreased risk of obesity as compared with non-smokers. Al-Qahtani reported also no significant association between obesity and smoking among adults in Saudi Arabia, and obesity risk diminished non-significantly from non-smokers to smokers [63].

The significant rise in obesity occurrence over the past few decades has been mostly caused by alterations in lifestyle, such as increasing calorie intake and decreased physical activity [64–66]. Our study observed that all individuals with high PRS in the three subgroups of Leisure-time physical activity had an increased risk of obesity, particularly in the second tertile which had 1.6 times greater obesity risk. Nevertheless, the interaction analysis of obesity with physical activity showed a significant decrease in obesity risk by 19% among individuals in the third tertile. The possible explanation of these findings could be derived from the observation that scheduled physical activity tends to regulate circadian rhythms and the expression of the clock gene [67].

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths including a large sample size (N = ~ 6000), the Generalizability of the findings to the Qatar population as a whole since the data used were collected from a random sample of the Qatari population, and the used Food Frequency Questionnaire (FFQ) includes 102 different food items representing the frequently consumed food in Qatar. Also, participants in the QBB survey provided a variety of demographic, health, and dietary data. A further important strength of this study is the application of PRS facilitating the evaluation of the cumulative effect of all genetic variants, which individually exhibited a small impact on the studied outcomes. However, there are some limitations of this study. The nature of this study is cross-sectional; thus, it cannot be used to establish the causality. Insufficient GWAS for examined traits are available to be used as discovery samples. Despite this, the studied phenotypic trait may vary from the trait in the discovery sample allowing cross-phenotype analyses [30]. Additionally, the self-reporting lifestyle activities may lead to inevitable measurement errors and the FFQ specifies only the frequency not the portion sizes of the food. Moreover, related individuals were not excluded, nor was kinship or relatedness accounted for in the statistical models. Given the high levels of consanguinity within the Qatari population, this limitation could result in inflated association signals or an elevated risk of false positives, thereby introducing potential bias into the study’s findings.

Conclusion

In conclusion, clock gene PRS was positively associated with obesity among Qatari population, especially among those with a high intake of mixed dietary pattern. Obesity risk in genetically predisposed individuals may vary with sociodemographic and lifestyle factors, supporting targeted interventions focused on modifiable lifestyle factors. Further research should explore these genetic mechanisms, identify biomarkers, and promote awareness of circadian health and lifestyle management in obesity prevention, particularly for high-risk groups such as women.
