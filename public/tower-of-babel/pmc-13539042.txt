Equipping Health Systems to Meet Older Adults’ Care Needs: A Novel Age-Friendly Patient and Family Advisory Council
Authors: Corrie Harris-Chunga, Timothy W Farrell, Mari Ransco
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAs the older adult population continues to grow, health care systems must adapt their delivery models to meet this demographic’s specific needs. University of Utah Health is addressing this challenge by integrating the Institute for Healthcare Improvement’s 4Ms framework across its care continuum. This framework emphasizes What Matters, Medication, Mentation, and Mobility to support age-friendly care delivery for older adults. To support this integration, the Division of Geriatrics and the Patient Experience Department partnered in 2022 to launch a novel Age-Friendly Patient and Family Advisory Council (PFAC) comprised of older adults. During its inaugural year, council members shared personal care experiences framed around the 4Ms to help co-design organizational improvement initiatives. Analysis of the Age-Friendly PFAC sessions revealed three primary themes: the need for stronger patient-provider communication, a demand for individualized care strategies, and a recognition of the emotional complexities involved in the care experience. This research brief highlights the insights gathered from the sessions and outlines the development and implementation of the Age-Friendly PFAC, providing a scalable model for health systems looking to build their own councils.Keywords: age-friendly care, geriatrics, patient engagement, patient experience, patient family advisory counsel

Introduction

The United States Census Bureau estimates the population above age 65 will nearly double in the next 30 years, from 55.8 million in 2020 to 83.7 million in 2050. 1 This demographic shift will challenge health systems to consistently deliver evidence-based geriatrics care. To meet this challenge, The John A. Hartford Foundation, Institute for Healthcare Improvement (IHI), American Hospital Association, and Catholic Health Association of the United States created the Age-Friendly Health Systems movement. 2 Age-Friendly Health Systems aim to “follow an essential set of evidence-based practices, cause no harm, and align with What Matters to older adults, their families, and caregivers.” Many health care institutions have received the Committed to Care Excellence designation, the highest Age-Friendly Health Systems designation offered by IHI. 3 Research shows that patients are more likely to seek additional care at a health system when they receive care that aligns with the Age-Friendly Health Systems model. 4 As part of this commitment to age-friendly care, Geriatrics Division leadership partnered with a Patient Experience Department to create an Age-Friendly Patient and Family Advisory Council (PFAC), comprised of older adults.

Systems utilize PFACs to gather insights on organizational initiatives, allowing patients, families, and caregivers to co-design care that ultimately improves the patient experience. 5 In the first year of the program the Age-Friendly PFAC met four times, with each session dedicated to one of the four elements (“4Ms”) of Age-Friendly Health Systems. As defined by IHI, the 4Ms include What Matters, Medication, Mentation, and Mobility. 2 (See Figure 1) Each session’s content was designed to identify patient priorities and determine areas for improvement in care delivery. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first PFAC comprised of older adults and focused on age-friendly care described in the literature.

The 4Ms Framework of an Age-Friendly Health System from the Institute of Healthcare Improvement. Includes definitions of Age-Friendly Health Systems, What Matters, Medication, Mentation, and Mobility

This brief provides a template for health systems looking to establish their own age-friendly care-centered PFAC. These councils offer a direct mechanism for integrating patient perspectives into care design, and scaling the creation of these councils ensures that future care equips health systems to meet the unique priorities of older adults.

Method

To create this Age-Friendly PFAC, the Patient Experience Department sampled the population of patients who had attended an appointment at a geriatrics clinic between July and December 2022 and sent them an emailed invitation to apply via the online survey vendor Qualtrics (Provo, Utah). This invitation included meeting details and payment information (each member would be paid $100 for their participation per session). Of 69 applicants, 22 underwent a telephone interview, and 18 attended an onboarding orientation. During orientation, all interested patients were accepted and signed a confidentiality agreement. This ensured they felt protected when sharing personal information during sessions. The council was made up of 11 women and 7 men, of whom 17 are White and 1 is Asian, with an average age of 73.

Before each Age-Friendly PFAC session, participants received materials outlining one of the 4Ms, its scope, and discussion prompts via the online event invitation platform Greenvelope (Seattle, Washington). Sessions were conducted through the virtual meeting platform Zoom (San Jose, California) and facilitated by two members of the Patient Experience Department, including this brief’s first author. Facilitators guided participants in sharing lived experiences and recommendations for improving care related to the specified M. The sessions had an average attendance of 10 participants.

Following the Age-Friendly PFAC sessions, meeting audio recordings were transcribed using Microsoft Stream’s (Redmond, Washington) transcription feature. Transcripts were manually de-identified using the Safe Harbor Method, which required removal of all Protected Health Information. 6 An inductive approach to qualitative content analysis 7 was applied to the transcripts. Recurring words and phrases were coded and organized into key concepts, and frequently occurring key concepts grouped into categories. Codes and categories were uploaded to the online AI-assistance tool Delve (New York City, NY) for post-hoc analysis. The de-identification and coding processes were completed by one of the facilitators of the PFAC session and author of this brief, with support from the co-facilitator. Institutional Review Board (IRB) review was not required for this brief, as the IRB deemed this project a quality improvement activity.

Communication With Providers

Barriers to effective communication, and facilitators of positive interactions, were key concepts identified within this category. Unsuccessful communication stemmed from perceived inattention to patients’ priorities, inadequate education on diagnoses or treatment plans, and feeling unheard, leading to feelings of exclusion from care decisions. Conversely, effective communication occurred when interactions were less structured, and when participants felt each of their concerns were addressed. Participants also recognized their role in these conversations, with one saying: “Don’t assume that the providers know what you’re going through … be sure and share it.” These themes were explored through all sessions, and were especially highlighted in this What Matters meeting discussion question: “How would you like your provider to ask you about your priorities, values, goals, and preferences about your health care?”

Personalized Care

Session analysis revealed that providers aligning care with individual patient values, goals, and priorities, and aiding in coordinating that care between other providers, as key concepts in this category. One participant stated: “The provider hasn’t helped me understand my priorities … it’s never been focused or coordinated to any extent.” Participants described personalized care as a marker of quality, noting that instances where providers acknowledged individual circumstances were highly meaningful. These findings primarily came from questions asked at the following meetings: “When thinking about (mobility, mentation, medication) what matters most to you and why?”

Emotional Considerations

Emotions experienced by participants were consistently shared through the PFAC sessions. Key concepts encompassed both distressing and positive experiences related to health care and aging. Participants reported feelings of frustration with the health care system. Sadness and grief often accompanied end-of-life circumstances. Anxiety regarding the aging process and its accompanying health challenges was common, with a pronounced fear of potential cognitive decline. One participant shared: “I recognize that there is some grief in my own life, thinking about the future and … potentially having cognitive issues.” These emotions were highlighted during the Mentation meeting, when asked: “In a transition of care, what support would you need?”

Alongside these challenges, participants articulated positive emotions that reflected resilience and compassion. They expressed empathy for others facing similar struggles, including those experiencing loneliness or barriers to care. Participants shared appreciation for the positive experiences of care they received.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this Age-Friendly PFAC represents the first initiative of its kind. Research indicates that 54% of hospitals in the United States have implemented a PFAC. 5 While condition-specific PFACs often include older adults, 8 a PFAC dedicated to age-friendly care made up of older adults offers a novel way to integrate this patient population’s perspectives into organizational improvements.

Analysis of the PFAC sessions identified key concepts that contain actionable feedback within the categories of improving communication with providers, personalizing care, and managing emotional considerations. These categories reflect aspects of care that can be enhanced by providers and health care systems. Engaging with the priorities of this rising population of patients directly improves the care these patients experience and ultimately improves the patient experience. While these priorities align with younger adults, age-friendly care especially benefits older adults with multi-complexities like chronic conditions and polypharmacy.

National Applications

The categories identified reflect conversations in the national age-friendly care movement. For example, a large national convenient care system seeking IHI recognition implemented a survey tool that asks older adult patients questions about the efforts made to both listen to what matters most to them about their health issues, and to help them understand those issues. 9 The questions align with recommendations from the Age-Friendly PFAC and outline a practical approach for integrating the 4Ms framework into routine care.

Communication With Providers

Concerns regarding the depth of communication with providers were frequently noted, often attributed to time constraints imposed by clinic schedules. Respondents recommended that providers engage in discussions about what matters most to older adults across all areas of care, not limiting these conversations to geriatrics appointments. When informational and relational needs are unmet, achieving patient-centered care becomes challenging. Active listening, eye contact, and respect, are vital for positive experiences. Systemic communication training for providers and staff could improve care quality and overall experience.

Personalized Care

Patient-centered approaches in care planning and implementation are essential for fostering trust and engagement. Incorporating strategies that elicit and honor patient values, preferences, and goals can strengthen the effectiveness of age-friendly care models. Recommendations include creating opportunities to improve patients’ ability to use electronic health portals, via instructional videos or in-person training classes. Participants also suggest encouraging older adults to designate a proxy with portal access. Research supports organizational initiatives to increase portal use can improve use of shared functionality among registered care partners. 10 Accounting for individual preferences may increase confidence in care decisions, understanding of diagnoses, and adherence to treatment plans.

Emotional Considerations

Participants emphasized the importance of addressing the emotional dimensions of care as part of their patient experience. In addition to communicating effectively and personalizing care, integrating elements of compassion into these interactions is seen as beneficial. In crafting a toolkit for conducting conversations around what matters most to patients, IHI materials recommend including prompts as simple as “What makes you happy?” and “What do you worry about?” to involve both positive and distressing emotions around care. 9 Providers initiating these conversations, and sharing system resources for grief support and caregiver wellbeing, can ensure patients feel supported while navigating the health care system.

Applied Recommendations

Feedback from the initial four sessions, particularly around medication management and personalized care, led to tangible improvements. One notable initiative involves collaboration between the outpatient geriatrics pharmacist and inpatient pharmacists to enhance medication safety for older adults. Additionally, the geriatrics pharmacist is conducting educational outreach to update primary care teams on revised treatment guidelines for older adults with diabetes. Insights shared by Age-Friendly Care PFAC participants were instrumental in shaping these strategies.

Limitations

This brief is subject to various limitations that warrant acknowledgment. First, this Age-Friendly PFAC model was implemented at a single site, with a council primarily made up of White older adults, constraining the generalizability of findings to other health systems and limit the representativeness of older adults across the state. Furthermore, discussions were conducted solely in English, excluding perspectives from non-English-speaking populations. Finally, recruitment was conducted via email, and all sessions were held virtually, restricting participation to those comfortable online. Despite these limitations, the methodology was rigorously designed to pilot this approach within the IHI framework, 3 with the goal of informing future strategies for patient engagement.

Conclusion

Incorporating the patient voice when designing care is essential for delivering positive and effective experiences for the growing older adult population. The Age-Friendly Health Systems model provides a structured approach that ensure effective care is delivered to every older adult at every encounter. Establishing an Age-Friendly PFAC offers a direct mechanism for integrating patient perspectives into care design and improving the quality of 4Ms implementation. Implementing changes based on PFAC recommendations fosters trust and can significantly improve the patient experience. Participants described their involvement in the Age-Friendly PFAC as fulfilling, and seeing their recommendations applied motivates them to continue improving care for older adults.

Future Directions

Future research should explore the scalability and longitudinal impact of Age-Friendly PFACs across diverse health care settings. The 4Ms framework provides a proactive, person-centered approach to care that aims to enhance patient and provider experiences. Evaluating its influence on provider fulfillment could inform strategies to reduce burnout, improve care, and support broader IHI framework adoption. Further work should also assess the experiences of older adults, their caregivers, and providers when implementing the 4Ms model compared with traditional disease-focused care. Because patients consistently express a desire to prioritize their goals, structured methods for eliciting “What Matters,” such as Patient Priorities Care, 11 should be integrated into clinical workflows. Additionally, future research should incorporate defined improvement initiatives into PFAC activities to ensure meaningful integration of patient perspectives into system-level decision-making.
