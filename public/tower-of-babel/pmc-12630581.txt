Tooth loss, dental prosthesis use, and cause-specific mortality in older Japanese adults: a 7-year cohort study
Authors: Faiz Abdurrahman, Taro Kusama, Yudai Tamada, Sakura Kiuchi, Masashige Saito, Toshiyuki Ojima, Jun Aida, Katsunori Kondo, Ken Osaka, Kenji Takeuchi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Poor oral health, particularly tooth loss, is associated with increased risk of all-cause mortality. However, the impact of dental prosthesis use on these associations remains unclear. This study evaluated the relationship between the number of remaining teeth, dental prosthesis use, and mortality from specific causes using seven-year follow-up data from the Japan Gerontological Evaluation Study (JAGES). All-cause mortality and ten cause-specific mortality outcomes were assessed. The number of remaining teeth and use of dental prostheses were the main exposures. Cox proportional hazard models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs). Among 43,774 participants (mean age 73.7 years [SD 5.9]; 53.2% women), 5,707 (13.0%) died during follow-up, corresponding to 20.7 deaths per 1,000 person-years. Participants with 0–9 or 10–19 teeth without dental prostheses exhibited higher cause-specific mortality rates than those with ≥ 20 teeth. Specifically, those with 0–9 teeth without dental prostheses had a significantly elevated risk of mortality from cancer, cardiovascular disease, respiratory disease, and injury (HRs ranging from 1.31 to 1.91; all p < 0.05). These associations were attenuated among dental prostheses users. Tooth loss was associated with increased risk of mortality from multiple specific causes; however, dental prostheses may mitigate this risk.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-24357-1.

Introduction

Oral diseases rank among the most prevalent global conditions. Approximately 29.4% of the population (2.3 billion people) have untreated caries in permanent teeth, and 9.8% (0.8 billion people) have severe periodontitis1. Untreated caries and periodontal diseases can lead to tooth loss2, which is a prevalent health condition in later life due to its irreversible nature and cumulative impact over time2. Consequently, tooth loss represents a critical health issue among older adults worldwide.

Tooth loss has been consistently linked with increased risk of chronic disease and subsequent mortality3. Existing literature indicates associations with all-cause mortality4, as well as elevated risks of mortality from cardiovascular disease5,6, respiratory disease5, and lung cancer7. Plausible pathways include impaired mastication and a poorer diet leading to malnutrition8, chronic oral inflammation that increases the systemic inflammatory burden9,10, and broader frailty-related effects11. Clarifying cause-specific mortality pattern is critical for identifying high-risk groups and guiding targeted prevention.

Dental prosthesis use can restore occlusal support and improve masticatory function, thereby potentially mitigating adverse health outcomes associated with tooth loss. Prior studies suggest that dental prosthesis use may lower the risk of malnutrition12 and reduce the risk of cognitive decline among older adults with tooth loss13. However, a comprehensive analysis of the associations between tooth loss, dental prosthesis use, and various cause-specific mortality outcomes has not been conducted.

This study examined the associations of the number of remaining teeth, dental prosthesis use, and cause-specific mortality from various diseases and disorders. We hypothesized that the influence of tooth loss and the use of dental prostheses on cause-specific mortality would vary by cause of death, reflecting the potential involvement of different mechanisms across diseases.

Methods

Study design and participants

This cohort study analyzed seven-year follow-up data from the Japan Gerontological Evaluation Study (JAGES), an ongoing longitudinal study established in 2010 to investigate health-related factors among adults aged ≥ 65 years in Japan14. The details of the surveys in JAGES is described elsewhere14. Baseline information was collected via a self-administered, mailed questionnaire during the 2010 JAGES wave (August 2010–December 2011); the response rate was 64.7%. Cause-of-death data, obtained from databases maintained by the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare of Japan, were linked through December 2017 using sex, date of birth, and date of death as key variables15. Of the 46,144 participants from nine municipalities, 6,383 died during follow-up; cause of death was ascertained for 6,312 decedents (98.9%). Participants with invalid weight or height measurements (n = 340) and those dependent in activities of daily living (ADL; basic routine tasks such as transferring, bathing, toileting, eating, and dressing) at baseline (n = 2,030) were excluded, resulting in 43,774 participants included in the analysis (Fig. 1).

Outcome variables

All-cause and cause-specific mortality were assessed as outcomes. Cause-specific mortality categories were based on a previous study5 and included deaths from cancer (International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision [ICD-10] codes C00–D48), cardiovascular disease (ICD-10 codes I00–I99), and respiratory disease (ICD-10 codes J00–J99). To comprehensively assess the effects of tooth loss across various causes of death, additional causes were included: infectious and parasitic disease (ICD-10 codes A00–B99); endocrine, nutritional, and metabolic disease (ICD-10 codes E00–E90); mental and behavioral disorder (ICD-10 codes F00–F99); nervous disease (ICD-10 codes G00–G99); digestive disease (ICD-10 code K00-K93); genitourinary disease (ICD-10 code N00–N99); and injury, poisoning, and external causes (ICD-10 code S00–T98).

Explanatory variables

The number of remaining teeth and dental status (a composite variable combining the number of remaining teeth and dental prosthesis use) were used as explanatory variables. The number of remaining teeth was assessed with the question: “How is the condition of your teeth?” with response options: “I have more than 20 teeth,” “I have 10–19 teeth,” “I have 1–9 teeth,” and “I have 0 teeth.” Responses were categorized into three groups: “≥20 teeth,” “10–19 teeth,” and “0–9 teeth”16. The self-reported number of teeth has been validated as broadly consistent with clinical examinations17.

Dental prosthesis use was assessed with the question: “Do you wear dentures or bridges (non-removable dentures)?” with response options: “No,” “Yes, in the upper jaw,” “Yes, in the lower jaw,” and “Yes, in both jaws.” Responses were dichotomized into two groups: non-use of dental prostheses (those who answered “No”) and use of dental prostheses (all the other responses)18. Dental status was categorized into five groups by combining the number of remaining teeth and dental prosthesis use: “≥20 teeth,” “10–19 teeth with dental prostheses,” “10–19 teeth without dental prostheses,” “0–9 teeth with dental prostheses,” and “0–9 teeth without dental prostheses”19.

Covariates

Based on previous literature5,23,24, the following potential confounders were included: sex (male/female), age (65–69, 70–74, 75–79, 80–84, or ≥ 85 years), equivalent income (< 2.00, 2.00–3.99, or ≥ 4.00 million JPY), education (≤ 9, 10–12, or ≥ 13 years), comorbidities (0, 1, or ≥ 2), smoking status (never, former, or current), alcohol consumption (never, former, or current), marital status (with, or without spouse), walking time (< 30, 30–59, or ≥ 60 min/day), and body mass index (< 18.5, 18.5–29.9, or ≥ 30.0). Equivalent income was calculated by dividing household income by the square root of the number of household members19. Comorbidities were defined as the number of self-reported medical conditions, including cancer, heart disease, stroke, hypertension, diabetes, obesity, hyperlipidemia, osteoporosis, joint disease, injury/fracture, respiratory disease, gastrointestinal disease, liver disease, mental disorders, swallowing difficulties, vision and hearing impairment, elimination problems, sleep problems, undiagnosed illness, and other conditions.

Statistical analysis

Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate cause-specific hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the associations between the number of remaining teeth, dental status, and all-cause and cause-specific mortality. Multiple imputation (MI) was used to handle missing data, reduce selection bias, and improve statistical efficiency25. Twenty imputed datasets were generated using multivariate imputation by chained equations (MICE) under a missing-at-random (MAR) assumption. The imputation model included all analysis variables (exposure and covariates) with variable-appropriate conditional models (logistic for binary variables; ordered logistic for ordinal variables). Each dataset was analyzed separately, and the estimates were subsequently combined based on Rubin’s rules26. As a subgroup analysis, we compared dental prosthesis use versus non-use within each stratum of the number of remaining teeth (≥ 20, 10–19, 0–9) using the same covariate set as the primary Cox models. Additionally, complete-case analyses were conducted as sensitivity analyses. Statistical significance level was set at α = 0.05, and two-sided p-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using Stata/MP 18.0 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, Texas, USA). The STROBE checklist was followed.

Ethical issue

This study was conducted under a collaborative research agreement with the associated municipalities. Ethical approval (no. 2493) was obtained from the Ethics Board of Chiba University. JAGES participants were informed that participation in the study was voluntary, and that completing and returning the questionnaire by mail constituted consent. An anonymized dataset was created. All procedures adhered to relevant guidelines and regulations, including the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

Participants with fewer teeth—especially those not using dental prostheses—were older and more likely to have lower income and educational attainment, higher comorbidity burden, and shorter daily walking time. Mortality rates by number of remaining teeth and dental status

During a median follow-up of 2,485 days (interquartile range: 2,218–2,697 days; maximum: 2,707 days), 5,707 deaths (13.0%) were recorded. The all-cause mortality rate was 20.7 per 1,000 person-years. Participants with 0–9 or 10–19 teeth exhibited higher all-cause and cause-specific mortality rates than those with ≥ 20 teeth (Supplementary Table S4). Mortality rates by dental status are shown in Table 2. Among participants with 0–9 or 10–19 teeth, those not using dental prostheses had higher all-cause mortality rates than those using dental prostheses. Across causes of death, mortality rates were generally highest among in the 0–9 teeth without dental prostheses group, with exceptions for digestive diseases and mental and behavioral disorders (Table 2).

Association of the number of remaining teeth with all-cause and cause-specific mortality

Cox proportional hazards models with the number of remaining teeth as the explanatory variable are summarized in Fig. 2 (detailed results are shown in Supplementary Table S5). After adjusting for all the covariates, participants with 0–9 teeth (HR = 1.39 [95% CI 1.30–1.49]) and 10–19 teeth (HR = 1.18 [95% CI 1.09–1.28]) had a higher risk of all-cause mortality compared with those who had ≥ 20 teeth. Additionally, participants with 0–9 teeth had a higher risk of mortality from cancer (HR = 1.31 [95% CI 1.18–1.45]), cardiovascular disease (HR = 1.37 [95% CI 1.18–1.59]), respiratory disease (HR = 1.65 [95% CI 1.35–2.02]), digestive diseases (HR = 2.01 [95% CI 1.30–3.08]), and injury and external causes (HR = 1.62 [95% CI 1.18–2.24]). Participants with 10–19 teeth had a higher risk of mortality from cancer (HR = 1.12 [95% CI 1.00–1.26]), cardiovascular disease (HR = 1.20 [95% CI 1.02–1.42]), and respiratory disease (HR = 1.35 [95% CI 1.08–1.70]).

Association of dental status with all-cause and cause-specific mortality

Cox proportional hazards models using dental status as the explanatory variable are shown in Table 3. Participants with 0–9 teeth (HR = 1.42 [95% CI 1.30–1.56]) or 10–19 teeth (HR = 1.23 [95% CI 1.10–1.37]) who did not use dental prostheses had a higher risk of all-cause mortality than those with ≥ 20 teeth. For cause-specific mortality, compared with ≥ 20 teeth, those with 0–9 teeth who used dental prostheses had a higher risk of mortality due to cancer (HR = 1.31 [95% CI = 1.14–1.52]), cardiovascular disease (HR = 1.35 [95% CI = 1.11–1.64]), respiratory disease (HR = 1.72 [95% CI = 1.34–2.21]), and injury, poisoning, and external causes (HR = 1.91 [95% CI = 1.27–2.88]). Similarly, participants with 10–19 teeth who did not use dental prostheses had a higher risk of mortality due to cancer (HR = 1.19 [95% CI = 1.01–1.39]) and respiratory disease (HR = 1.47 [95% CI = 1.09–1.98]) than those with ≥ 20 teeth. Complete-case analyses yielded point estimates that were directionally consistent with the MI results; several associations were attenuated and not statistically significant (Supplementary Table S6 and S7). Supplementary Table S8 presents subgroup analyses comparing dental prosthesis use and non-use within tooth-count strata. Although 95% CIs of estimates included null association with no consistent pattern of point estimates direction across causes among those with ≥ 20 and 10–19 teeth, among those with 0–9 teeth, most HRs were < 1 across several causes but were not statistically significant; digestive diseases were an exception, showing an elevated risk.

Discussion

This study examined the associations between the number of remaining teeth and ten types of cause-specific mortality outcomes, and assessed whether the use of dental prostheses modifies these associations. An inverse dose-response relationship was observed: fewer teeth were associated with higher risks of all-cause mortality and several specific causes, with effect sizes varying by cause. Among participants with fewer number of remaining teeth, the use of dental prostheses was generally associated with a lower mortality risk than non-use, suggesting that prosthetic rehabilitation may mitigate part of the excess risk.

Compared with ≥ 20 teeth, this study found that having 0–9 or 10–19 teeth was associated with higher risks of all-cause mortality and mortality from cancer, cardiovascular disease, and respiratory disease after multivariable adjustment. These findings align with a prior meta-analysis linking tooth loss to all-cause mortality4, and with several cohort studies reporting associations between fewer remaining teeth and mortality from cancer7,27,28, cardiovascular disease5,23,28, and respiratory disease5,28. Our results extend the prior findings by showing elevated risks for digestive disease and injury/external-cause deaths among individuals with 0–9 teeth. However, some studies reported no statistically significant associations for cancer or cardiovascular mortality29,30, or an association limited to oro-digestive cancer mortality31. Differences in study design, case definitions, covariate adjustment, and statistical power likely contribute to heterogeneity in the previous findings.

Several pathways may underlie these associations. Tooth loss impairs masticatory function; reduced chewing ability can lead to avoidance of hard, nutrient-dense foods (such as fruits, vegetables, and meats), resulting in poorer dietary quality8. This may reduce intake of essential micronutrients, which can impair immune function, decrease antioxidant capacity, and hinder cellular repair, thereby increasing vulnerability to cancer, cardiovascular disease, and respiratory infections32,33. Low-grade systemic inflammation associated with periodontal disease9 is also a plausible pathway. However, periodontal status and inflammatory biomarkers were not measured in this cohort; therefore, we refrain from mechanistic inference. In addition, tooth loss may elevate the risk of injury-related mortality via contributions to frailty and cognitive impairment, both established risk factors for falls and injuries11,34.

We observed that among individuals with 0–9 or 10–19 teeth, those using dental prostheses had lower risks of all-cause and several cause-specific deaths—including respiratory, infectious/parasitic, endocrine/metabolic, nervous-system, and genitourinary disease, and injury/external causes—than those not using dental prostheses. These findings are consistent with a prior study suggesting lower mortality among dental prosthesis users23. These associations may be partly explained by the restoration of oral function and occlusal support, which improves masticatory performance and swallowing35 and can facilitate better food selection and nutritional status36. In addition, dental prosthesis use has been associated with a lower risk of cognitive decline13, which could indirectly reduce the risk of injury-related mortality. Further research is warranted to elucidate the mechanisms through which dental prosthesis use is associated with cause-specific mortality.

This study emphasizes the significance of oral health, particularly in maintaining teeth and occlusal support throughout the life course, in relation to mortality patterns among older adults. Population-level interventions that address the broader determinants of oral health are needed to reduce the burden of tooth loss and improve access to prosthetic rehabilitation37. However, socioeconomic disparities in the utilization of dental prosthesis remain a significant concern. Expanding dental coverage under universal health coverage (UHC) may help address these disparities38. Policies aimed at reducing financial and access-related barriers to dental prosthesis use are essential to promote equitable oral healthcare globally.

This study has limitations. First, tooth count and dental prosthesis use were self-reported, which may have led to exposure misclassification; however, self-reported tooth counts have shown reasonable validity17, suggesting limited bias. Second, the baseline response rate (64.7%) may introduce selection bias; nonetheless, a previous study indicated that such bias typically affects prevalence more than associations39. Third, despite extensive adjustment, residual confounding due to unmeasured variables cannot be entirely ruled out. Lastly, the questionnaire did not collect information on the proximate causes of tooth loss or inflammatory biomarkers; therefore, we were unable to distinguish inflammation-related pathways from mastication/nutrition mechanisms. Despite the limitations, the study possesses notable strengths, including the use of nationwide prospective cohort data and a large sample size (n = 43,774), which enhance the robustness of the findings.

In conclusion, fewer remaining teeth were associated with higher risks of mortality from cancer, cardiovascular disease, respiratory disease, digestive disease, injury, poisoning, and other external causes in older adults. Additionally, dental prosthesis use was associated with a lower mortality risk among those with tooth loss, with variation by cause of death. Reducing the lifetime burden of tooth loss and improving equitable access to prosthetic rehabilitation may help improve health outcomes in aging populations.
