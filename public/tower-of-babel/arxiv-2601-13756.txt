A turnpike property in an eigenvalue optimization problem
Adam Kaminer, Thomas Kriecherbauer, Lars Grüne, Michael Margaliot

Abstract
We consider a constrained eigenvalue optimization problem that arises in an important nonlinear dynamical model for mRNA translation in the cell. We prove that the ordered list of optimal parameters admits a turnpike property, namely, it includes three parts with the first and third part relatively short, and the values in the middle part are all approximately equal. Turnpike properties have attracted considerable attention in econometrics and optimal control theory, but to the best of our knowledge this is the first rigorous proof of such a structure in an eigenvalue optimization problem.



I
Introduction

Eigenvalue optimization problems are important in many scientific fields
(see, e.g.
[
22
,
4
]
and the references therein) and have found applications in
matrix theory, mechanical vibrations,
control theory, and stability analysis.
Here, we consider an
eigenvalue optimization problem arising in an important nonlinear ODE model from systems biology. Our main result is a proof that the problem admits a
turnpike property: the ordered list of optimal parameters is made of three pieces: the first and last piece are short, and the middle piece is long and the optimal parameters there are approximately equal.

Turnpike properties received considerable attention in the context of optimal control, see
[
8
]
and the references therein, with roots in mathematical economy
[
26
,
5
]
. The turnpike property describes the phenomenon that optimal trajectories stay near an optimal equilibrium—the so-called turnpike equilibrium—most of the time. There are many different ways to formalize this phenomenon mathematically; we will give one of the most commonly used variants in Eq. (
30
) below, in order to compare it to the turnpike property we establish in the eigenvalue problem.

In optimal control, there are two main techniques to establish that the turnpike property holds: One uses a system-theoretic property called strict dissipativity, which provides an abstract energy inequality that prevents optimal solutions to stay away from the turnpike equilibrium for a long time
[
9
]
. The second technique uses that the turnpike equilibrium is part of an equilibrium (in an extended state space), which is hyperbolic for the dynamical system resulting from the necessary optimality conditions
[
25
,
31
]
. In both cases, the turnpike equilibrium is an optimal equilibrium that is determined by solving a static optimization problem.

To the best of our knowledge, our result in this paper is the first rigorous proof of a turnpike property in an eigenvalue optimization problem. For this problem, none of the techniques from optimal control applies directly. However, our approach in this paper bears some similarity with the second approach, as we also make use of the fact that an
auxiliary dynamical system admits an
hyperbolic equilibrium point.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. The next section describes the eigenvalue optimization problem that we consider, and motivates it using two applications to dynamical systems.
Section
III

states the main results, and the proof is given in Section
IV
. Section
V
details several numerical examples that demonstrate the various quantities defined in the proofs.
The final section concludes and describes possible topics for further research.

II
Eigenvalue optimization problem

Consider the
(n+2)\times(n+2)
symmetric and tri-diagonal matrix

B(\lambda):=\begin{bmatrix}0&\lambda_{0}^{-1/2}&0&0&0&\dots&0&0&0\\
\lambda_{0}^{-1/2}&0&\lambda_{1}^{-1/2}&0&0&\dots&0&0&0\\
0&\lambda_{1}^{-1/2}&0&\lambda_{2}^{-1/2}&0&\dots&0&0&0\\
&&\vdots\\
0&0&0&0&0&\dots&\lambda_{n-1}^{-1/2}&0&\lambda_{n}^{-1/2}\\
0&0&0&0&0&\dots&0&\lambda_{n}^{-1/2}&0\\
\end{bmatrix},

(1)

with
\lambda_{i}>0
for
i=0,\dots,n
.

Since
B(\lambda)
is symmetric, all its eigenvalues are real. Since it is also componentwise
nonnegative and irreducible, its maximal eigenvalue (or Perron root), denoted
\sigma(B(\lambda))
, is
simple and positive
[
12
, Chapter 8]
. We consider the following eigenvalue optimization problem.

Problem 1
.

Find
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{>0}
that minimizes
\sigma(B(\lambda))
subject to the constraint

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n+1}\sum_{i=0}^{n}\lambda_{i}\leq 1.

(2)

This problem admits a unique solution (see below), that we denote by
\bar{\lambda}=\begin{bmatrix}\bar{\lambda}_{0}&\dots&\bar{\lambda}_{n}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}
. The minimal Perron root is
\bar{\sigma}:=\sigma(B(\bar{\lambda}))
.

Remark 2
.

[Existence and uniqueness of the optimal solution]
The induced
L_{2}
matrix norm of a matrix
A
is
\|A\|_{2}=\sqrt{\sigma(A^{\top}A)}
, and if
A
is symmetric this gives
\|A\|_{2}=\sigma(A)
. Let
e^{i}
,
i=1,\dots,n+2
, be the standard basis in
\mathbb{R}^{n+2}
. Then

(e^{i})^{\top}B^{\top}(\lambda)B(\lambda)e^{i}=\begin{cases}\lambda_{0}^{-1},&\text{ if }i=1,\\
\lambda_{i-1}^{-1}+\lambda_{i-2}^{-1},&\text{ if }i=2,\dots,n+1,\\
\lambda_{n}^{-1}&\text{ if }i=n+2.\end{cases}

Thus, if some
\lambda_{i}\to 0
then

\sigma(B(\lambda))=\|B(\lambda)\|_{2}\to\infty
. This implies that there exists
\eta>0
such that the constraint in Problem
1
can be replaced by

\lambda_{i}\geq\eta\text{ for all }i,\text{ and }\frac{1}{n+1}\sum_{i=0}^{n}\lambda_{i}\leq 1.

This constraint defines a compact subset in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, and since
\sigma(B(\lambda))
is a continuous function of the
\lambda_{i}
s, we conclude that Problem
1
admits an optimal solution
\bar{\lambda}=\bar{\lambda}(n)
.
Furthermore,
since the Perron root is a monotonically non-decreasing function of the matrix entries
[
12
, Chapter 8]
,
\bar{\lambda}
satisfies
\frac{1}{n+1}\sum_{i=0}^{n}\bar{\lambda}_{i}=1
, see also Remark
4
below.

An induced
matrix norm is convex in the matrix entries. Using this it can be shown that Problem
1
is strictly convex on
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{>0}

[
23
]
,
and thus the optimal solution
\bar{\lambda}
is unique.

Remark 4
.

We use in (

)

\sum_{i=0}^{n}\lambda_{i}\leq n+1
rather than the more general upper bound
\sum_{i=0}^{n}\lambda_{i}\leq(n+1)c

and thus
\sigma(B(c\lambda))=c^{-1/2}\sigma(B(\lambda))
.

.

Note that
\lambda=1_{n+1}
satisfies the constraints in Problem

. For this value we get that
B(1_{n+1})
is an
(n+2)\times(n+2)

2\cos(\frac{k\pi}{n+3}),\quad k=1,2,...,n+2,

\sigma(B(1_{n+1}))=2\cos(\frac{\pi}{n+3}).

(3)

However, we will show in Lemma

.

Remark 6
.

Letting
\omega_{i}:=\lambda_{i}^{-1/2}
,
i=0,\dots,n
, we can cast Problem
1

as follows: minimize the Perron root
\sigma

of the
(n+2)\times(n+2)

symmetric matrix

A(\omega):=\begin{bmatrix}0&\omega_{0}&0&0&0&\dots&0&0&0\\
\omega_{0}&0&\omega_{1}&0&0&\dots&0&0&0\\
0&\omega_{1}&0&\omega_{2}&0&\dots&0&0&0\\
&&\vdots\\
0&0&0&0&0&\dots&\omega_{n-1}&0&\omega_{n}\\
0&0&0&0&0&\dots&0&\omega_{n}&0\\
\end{bmatrix}

subject to:

\displaystyle\omega_{i}>0\text{ for all }i,\text{ and }\frac{1}{n+1}\sum_{i=0}^{n}\omega_{i}^{-2}\leq 1.

(4)

We can write
A(\omega)
as a linear
function of the
\omega_{i}
s:
A(\omega)=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\omega_{i}E_{i}
, where
E_{i}
is the
(n+2)\times(n+2)
matrix with all entries zero, except for entries
(i+1,i+2)
and
(i+2,i+1)
, that are one.
However, now the constraint (
4
)
is nonlinear in the
\omega_{i}
s.

Our main result (Theorem

admits a turnpike property.

II-A

Maximizing protein production rate in the ribosome flow model

The ribosome flow model (RFM) is a phenomenological model for the uni-directional flow of particles
along a 1D chain of
n
sites.
It can be obtained as the dynamic mean field approximation of a fundamental model from statistical mechanics called the totally asymmetric simple exclusion principle
[
2
]
.

The RFM includes
n+1
positive parameters
\lambda_{0},\dots,\lambda_{n}
, where
\lambda_{i}
is the transition rate from site
i
to site
i+1
, and
n
state-variables
x_{1}(t),\dots,x_{n}(t)
,
with
x_{i}(t)
describing
the density of particles in site
i
. This is normalized such that
x_{i}(t)\in[0,1]
for all
t
, where
x_{i}(t)=0
[
x_{i}(t)=1
] corresponds to site
i
being completely empty [full].
The state space is thus the
n
-dimensional unit cube
[0,1]^{n}
.

The RFM equations are:

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{1}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{0}(1-x_{1})-\lambda_{1}x_{1}(1-x_{2}),

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{2}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{1}x_{1}(1-x_{2})-\lambda_{2}x_{2}(1-x_{3}),

(5)

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{n}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{n-1}x_{n-1}(1-x_{n})-\lambda_{n}x_{n}.

Note that defining
x_{0}(t)\equiv 1
and
x_{n+1}(t)\equiv 0
, this can be written as

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{i}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{i-1}x_{i-1}(1-x_{i})-\lambda_{i}x_{i}(1-x_{i+1}),\quad i=1,\dots,n.

(6)

This equation states that the change in the density in site
i
is the flow from site
i-1
to site
i
given by
\lambda_{i-1}x_{i-1}(1-x_{i})
minus the flow from site
i
to site
i+1
given by
\lambda_{i}x_{i}(1-x_{i+1})
.
Note that the last flow depends on three factors: the transition rate
\lambda_{i}
,
the density
x_{i}
in site
i
, and the “free space”
(1-x_{i+1})
in site
i+1
.
In particular, as the density in a site increases the flow into this site decreases, and this allows to model and analyze the formation of traffic jams of particles using the RFM. Traffic jams of ribosomes along the mRNA molecule have been implicated with various diseases (see e.g.
[
30
]
).

The RFM and its variants have been extensively used to model and analyze the flow of ribosomes along the mRNA molecule during translation which is a fundamental process in gene expression
(see, e.g.,
[
29
,
23
,
24
,
34
,
28
,
32
,
17
,
14
,
13
,
20
,
7
]
).
Networks of interconnected RFMs have been used to model large-scale translation in the cell and the competition for shared resources
[
10
,
27
,
21
,
15
,
16
]
.

Ref.
[
19
]
showed that the RFM admits a unique equilibrium
e=e(\lambda_{0},\dots,\lambda_{n})
, with
e\in(0,1)^{n}
, and that for any initial condition
x(0)\in[0,1]^{n}
the solution of the RFM converges to
e
(see also
[
18
]
).
By (
II-A
), the equilibrium satisfies

\displaystyle\lambda_{0}(1-e_{1})

\displaystyle=\lambda_{1}e_{1}(1-e_{2})

\displaystyle=\lambda_{2}e_{2}(1-e_{3})

(7)

\displaystyle=\lambda_{n-2}e_{n-2}(1-e_{n-1})

\displaystyle=\lambda_{n-1}e_{n-1}(1-e_{n})

\displaystyle=\lambda_{n}e_{n},

that is, at the equilibrium the flows into and out of each site are equal. In particular, the steady state flow out of the last site is

R(\lambda):=\lambda_{n}e_{n},

(8)

and this corresponds to the steady state protein production rate, which is an important biological quantity. Note that (
II-A
) and the fact that
e\in(0,1)^{n}
implies that

R(\lambda)<\min\{\lambda_{0},\dots,\lambda_{n}\}.

(9)

II-B

Minimizing the maximal characteristic frequency in a linear chain

As another application of Problem
1
,
consider an ordered 1D chain of
N
masses
m_{1},\dots,m_{N}
, with a spring
with elasticity
K_{j}
connecting masses
m_{j}
and
m_{j+1}
. Let
x_{j}(t)
denote the location of mass
m_{j}
at time
t
. Then

m_{j}\ddot{x}_{j}=K_{j}(x_{j+1}-x_{j}){+}K_{j-1}(x_{j-1}-x_{j}),\quad j=1,\dots,N,

(19)

with
K_{0}:=0
and
K_{N}:=0
. A series of coordinate transformations
[
6
]
yields the equivalent model

\dot{u}=Su

where
u:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}^{2N-1}
is the state vector, and
S

is the tri-diagonal skew-symmetric matrix

S:=\begin{bmatrix}0&p_{1}^{1/2}&0&\dots&0&0&0\\
-p_{1}^{1/2}&0&p_{2}^{1/2}&\dots&0&0&0\\
&&&\vdots\\
0&0&0&\dots&-p_{2(N-1)-1}^{1/2}&0&p_{2(N-1)}^{1/2}\\
0&0&0&\dots&0&-p^{1/2}_{2(N-1)}&0\end{bmatrix},

with
p_{2j-1}:=\frac{K_{j}}{m_{j}}
and
p_{2j}:=\frac{K_{j}}{m_{j+1}}
. The eigenvalues of
S
are the purely imaginary values
\sqrt{-1}\omega_{i}
, with

-\omega_{N-1}<\dots<-\omega_{1}<\omega_{0}<\omega_{1}<\dots<\omega_{N-1},

and
\omega_{0}=0
. These are the characteristic frequencies of the linear chain.
The maximal frequency
\omega_{N-1}
is often associated to the strain and
tear in such mechanical systems.
Ref.
[
33
]

showed that the problem of minimizing
\omega_{N-1}
, that is, minimizing the maximal characteristic frequency of the chain under the constraint

\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\frac{m_{i}+m_{i+1}}{K_{i}}\leq N-1,

(20)

coincides with Problem
1
.

Remark 11
.

The constraint in (
20
) can be explained as follows. Every couple of two consecutive masses
m_{i},m_{i+1}
along the chain is replaced by a “weighted equivalent mass”
\frac{m_{i}+m_{i+1}}{K_{i}}
, where
K_{i}
is the elasticity of the spring that connects the two masses.
The constraint upper bounds the sum of all these equivalent masses. For example, when
N=2
,
Eq. (
19
)
becomes

\displaystyle m_{1}\ddot{x}_{1}

\displaystyle=K_{1}(x_{2}-x_{1}),

\displaystyle m_{2}\ddot{x}_{2}

\displaystyle=K_{1}(x_{1}-x_{2}),

and letting
\delta x:=x_{2}-x_{1}
gives

\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}{\delta x}=-K_{1}\left(m_{1}^{-1}+m_{2}^{-1}\right)\delta x,

so
\omega_{1}=\sqrt{K_{1}\left(m_{1}^{-1}+m_{2}^{-1}\right)}

and the optimization problem here is to minimize
\omega_{1}
subject to the constraint
\frac{m_{1}+m_{2}}{K_{1}}\leq 1
.

Ref.
[
33
]
proved several useful properties of the optimal solution of Problem
1
. First, the optimal rates are symmetric:

\bar{\lambda}_{i}=\bar{\lambda}_{n-i},\quad i=0,\dots,n,

(21)

and strictly increasing up to the middle of the chain, that is,

\bar{\lambda}_{0}<\bar{\lambda}_{1}<\dots<\bar{\lambda}_{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}.

(22)

Note that these equations imply that
\lambda_{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}
is always the maximal rate, and if
n
is odd then
\lambda_{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}=\lambda_{\lfloor n/2\rfloor+1}
.

Ref.
[
33
]
also proved that the corresponding steady state densities in the RFM satisfy

\bar{e}_{i}=1-\bar{e}_{n+1-i},\quad i=1,\dots,n,

(23)

and

\frac{\bar{e}_{i}}{1-\bar{e}_{i}}=\frac{\bar{\lambda}_{i}}{\bar{\lambda}_{i-1}},\quad i=1,\dots,n.

(24)

Note that this implies in particular that if
n
is odd, that is,
n=2k+1
, then
\bar{e}_{k+1}=1/2
.

III
Main results

We can now state our main results. All the proofs are given in Section
IV

Theorem 12
.

Fix
n\in\mathbb{N}
with
n>1
. Then the optimal parameters
\bar{\lambda}=\bar{\lambda}(n)
and the minimal Perron root
\bar{\sigma}=\bar{\sigma}(n)
satisfy:

2\sqrt{1-\frac{4\ln(2)}{n+1}}<\bar{\sigma}<2\sqrt{1-\frac{1}{n+1}},

(25)

and

\frac{4}{\bar{\sigma}^{2}}\left(1-\frac{\ln(2)}{2^{i}}\right)<\bar{\lambda}_{i}<\frac{4}{\bar{\sigma}^{2}},\text{ for all }i\in\{0,1,\dots,\lfloor n/2\rfloor-1\},

(26)

if

Furthermore, if

We now describe several implications of these results.

First,
in using the RFM to model mRMA translation, the value of
n
can be several hundreds. It is thus
important
to know the optimal protein production rate for large values
on
n
. Eq. (
25

\lim_{n\to\infty}\bar{\sigma}(n)=2.

Since
\bar{R}(n)=(\bar{\sigma}(n))^{-2}
, this implies that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\bar{R}(n)=1/4
.
As shown in
Example

for
n=100
we already have a value close to this limit, namely,
\bar{\sigma}(100)=1.9892
, and
the optimal steady-state production rate is
\bar{R}(100)=(\bar{\sigma})^{-2}=0.2527
.

Second, note that combining (
21
) and (
29
) yields a kind of turnpike property. The optimal
\bar{\lambda}_{i}
s converge exponentially fast
to the value
4/\bar{\sigma}^{2}
, and in particular the optimal values in the bulk are all approximately equal to
4/\bar{\sigma}^{2}
. However, this is not exactly the form of the turnpike property known in optimal control. Translated to the notation used here, this property demands the following, cf.
[
8
, Eq. (11)]
:

\begin{array}[]{l}\mbox{There exists a scalar }\lambda^{\star}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}\mbox{ such that
the following property holds for any }n\in\mathbb{N}.\mbox{ For any }\varepsilon>0\\
\mbox{there is an integer }N(\varepsilon)\in\mathbb{N}\mbox{ such that }|\bar{\lambda}_{i}-\lambda^{*}|<\varepsilon\mbox{ for all }i=0,\ldots,n,\mbox{ except for at most }N(\varepsilon)\\
\mbox{indices.}\end{array}

(30)

While Theorem
12
implies that
\bar{\lambda}_{i}
is close to
4/\bar{\sigma}^{2}
most of the time in this sense, this value depends on
n
. In contrast, (
30
) requires that
\lambda^{*}
is independent of
n
. We can, however, resolve this problem by using the fact that

converges to
\lambda^{*}:=1
as

.
Hence, given
\varepsilon>0
we can pick

so large that

1<4/\bar{\sigma}^{2}<1+\varepsilon

holds for all
n\geq N(\varepsilon)
and
\frac{1}{2^{N(\varepsilon)/2}}<\varepsilon
(choosing
N(\varepsilon)
even). This implies that the desired inequality
|\bar{\lambda}_{i}-\lambda^{*}|<\varepsilon
indeed holds
for all
i=\frac{N(\varepsilon)}{2},\ldots,n-\frac{N(\varepsilon)}{2}
, i.e., for all but at most
N(\varepsilon)
indices.

Third,
Fig.
1
shows that the maximal value of the
\bar{\lambda}_{i}
s is larger than one for the case
n=100
.
Eq. (
25
) gives
\frac{4}{\bar{\sigma}^{2}}>1+\frac{1}{n}
, and combining this (
27
)
and (
28
)
implies that there exists

such that

Thus, as
n
increases the maximal optimal rate cannot converge to
\lambda^{*}=1
at an exponential rate. In particular, the turnpike property (

) cannot be of exponential form (cf.
[
8
, eq. (18)]
).

IV-C

Hyperbolic map formulation

Define a parameter-dependent mapping
F_{s}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
by

F\left(\begin{bmatrix}u\\
v\end{bmatrix}\right):=\begin{bmatrix}su^{2}-v\\
u\end{bmatrix}.

Then (
44

\begin{bmatrix}\bar{a}_{i+1}\\
\bar{a}_{i}\end{bmatrix}=F_{\bar{r}}\left(\begin{bmatrix}\bar{a}_{i}\\
\bar{a}_{i-1}\end{bmatrix}\right),

(51)

so

\begin{bmatrix}\bar{a}_{i+1}\\
\bar{a}_{i}\end{bmatrix}

is an orbit of
F_{\bar{r}}
emanating from
\begin{bmatrix}\bar{a}_{1}\\
\bar{a}_{0}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}1\\
0\end{bmatrix}
,
and ending at
\begin{bmatrix}\bar{a}_{n+3}\\
\bar{a}_{n+2}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\

.

We now analyze the asymptotic behavior of (

The map

F_{s}
admits two fixed points for any

. The first is

\begin{bmatrix}0\\
0\end{bmatrix}
, which is not relevant in our context
because if the
\bar{a}_{i}
s
converge to zero then the
\bar{\lambda}_{i}
s converge to zero and this contradicts
the argument in Remark
2

. The second fixed point of
F_{s}
is

\begin{bmatrix}2/s\\
2/s\end{bmatrix}.

(52)

The Jacobian of
F
at this fixed point is
\begin{bmatrix}4&-1\\
1&0\end{bmatrix}
,
and this has eigenvalues

\alpha:=2-\sqrt{3},\quad\alpha^{-1}=2+\sqrt{3}.

and corresponding
eigenvectors

\begin{bmatrix}1\\
4-\alpha\end{bmatrix},\quad\begin{bmatrix}1\\
4-\alpha^{-1}\end{bmatrix}.

Note that

0<\alpha<1<\alpha^{-1}
. Thus, (
52

is a hyperbolic fixed point of
F
. Note that although
F_{s}
depends on
s
, the Jacobian at the fixed point and its eigenvalues do not depend on
s
.

Rate of convergence to the equilibrium

Let

\bar{q}(n):=\frac{2}{\bar{r}(n)}=\frac{2}{\bar{\sigma}^{2/3}\bar{\lambda}_{0}^{1/3}}.

(53)

Remark 19
.

The analysis of the map
F_{s}
suggests that the orbit
\begin{bmatrix}\bar{a}_{i+1}\\
\bar{a}_{i}\end{bmatrix}_{i}
of
F_{\bar{r}}
emanating from
\begin{bmatrix}\bar{a}_{1}\\
\bar{a}_{0}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}1\\
0\end{bmatrix}
approaches the hyperbolic fixed point
\begin{bmatrix}\bar{q}\\
\bar{q}\end{bmatrix}
at an exponential rate for
0\leq i\leq\lfloor(n+3)/2\rfloor
moving close to the stable manifold of the fixed point. For
i>\lfloor(n+3)/2\rfloor
the iterates then move away from the fixed point staying close to the unstable manifold. In fact, one may use the theory of hyperbolic fixed points to prove a result similar to Theorem
12
. As a first result, one obtains that for any
\beta>\alpha=2-\sqrt{3}
there exists a constant
C_{\beta,\bar{r}}>0
such that

\bar{q}>\bar{a}_{i}>\bar{q}-C_{\beta,\bar{r}}\beta^{i}\quad\mbox{ for }\quad 0\leq i\leq\lfloor(n+3)/2\rfloor.

(55)

The core of the proof of the lower bound in (
55
) is the central linearization result in the Hartman-Grobman theory, see e.g.
[
11
, Chapter IX]
. It states that in a suitable neighborhood of a hyperbolic fixed point the dynamics is topologically equivalent to the the dynamics generated by the linear map given by the Jacobian. In our situation this means that there exists a homeomorphism
\Psi_{s}
from a neighborhood
U_{s}
of the fixed point onto a neighborhood
V_{s}
of the origin such that for
x\in U_{s}
we have

F_{s}(x)=\Psi_{s}^{-1}\Big(\begin{bmatrix}4&-1\\
1&0\end{bmatrix}\Psi_{s}(x)\Big).

Although the dynamics generated by the linear map can easily be analyzed, the map
\Psi_{s}
poses some challenges. It cannot be expected to be differentiable or Lipschitz continuous and one has to work with
\gamma
-Hölder norms where
0<\gamma<1
can be chosen arbitrarily. However, the choice of
\gamma
has an effect on the size of the neighborhoods
U_{s}
and
V_{s}
. This leads then to
\beta=(2-\sqrt{3})^{\gamma}
and a constant
C_{\beta,\bar{r}}
in (
55
) that is hard to evaluate but surely becomes huge if
\beta
is chosen close to
2-\sqrt{3}
.

In view of the discussion above it is perhaps remarkable that the identities presented in this paper allow for an elementary proof of

\bar{q}>\bar{a}_{i}>\bar{q}-C_{\bar{r}}\beta^{i}\quad\mbox{ for }\quad 0\leq i\leq\lfloor(n+3)/2\rfloor,

with
\beta=1/2
and
C_{\bar{r}}=2\bar{q}\ln{\bar{q}}<3/2
. This inequality together with relation (
IV-B
) are the basis for our proof of Theorem
12
.

) for this index as well, and this completes the proof.
∎

If follows from (
54
) that
0=\bar{a}_{0}=\bar{a}_{n+3}<\bar{q}
and that
1=\bar{a}_{1}=\bar{a}_{n+2}<\bar{q}
.
The next result shows that in fact
all the
\bar{a}_{i}
s are upper bounded by
\bar{q}
.

Lemma 21
.

Fix
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Then

\bar{a}_{i}<\bar{q},\quad i=0,\dots,n+3.

(61)

Proof.

Assume the claim is false. As
\bar{a}_{0}=0
and
\bar{a}_{1}=1
then there exists an index
\ell\in\{2,\dots,\lfloor n/2\rfloor+1\}
such that

\bar{a}_{\ell}\geq\bar{q}\text{ and }\bar{a}_{\ell-1}<\bar{q}.

(62)

The following argument only uses
\bar{a}_{\ell}\geq\bar{q}
and
\bar{a}_{\ell-1}<\bar{a}_{\ell}
:

\displaystyle\bar{a}_{\ell+1}

\displaystyle=\bar{r}\bar{a}_{\ell}^{2}-\bar{a}_{\ell-1}

\displaystyle\geq\bar{r}\bar{q}\bar{a}_{\ell}-\bar{a}_{\ell-1}

\displaystyle=2\bar{a}_{\ell}-\bar{a}_{\ell-1}

\displaystyle>\bar{a}_{\ell}

\displaystyle\geq\bar{q}.

Thus, we have
a_{\ell+1}\geq\bar{q}
and
\bar{a}_{\ell}<\bar{a}_{\ell+1}
which allows to repeat the argument with
\ell
being replaced by
\ell+1
.
Continuing in this way, we arrive at
\bar{a}_{n+3}\geq\bar{q}
and this is a contradiction, as
\bar{a}_{n+3}=0
.
∎

Observe that we can combine (
58
) and (
60
) in the proof of Lemma
20
to get

q\bar{a}_{i-1}\leq\bar{a}_{i}^{2},\quad i=2,\dots,\lfloor(n+3)/2\rfloor.

Using in addition Lemma
21
we learn that

\bar{q}\bar{a}_{i-1}\leq\bar{a}_{i}^{2}<\bar{q}\bar{a}_{i},\quad i=2,\dots,\lfloor(n+3)/2\rfloor,

leading to an improvement of Lemma
18
.

Remark 22
.

It follows from
the above that

\bar{a}_{0}<\bar{a}_{1}<\dots<\bar{a}_{\lfloor(n+3)/2\rfloor}<\bar{q}.

Also, the fact that
\bar{a}_{2}<\bar{q}
yields
\bar{r}<2/\bar{r}
, so
\bar{r}<\sqrt{2}
, and
\bar{q}=2/\bar{r}>\sqrt{2}
.

Using the results above we can derive bounds on the optimal
\bar{\lambda}_{i}
s. We begin with an upper bound.

Lemma 23
.

We have

\bar{\lambda}_{i}<\frac{4}{\bar{\sigma}^{2}},\quad i=0,1,\dots,n.

Proof.

Combining (
IV-B
) and (
61
) gives

\bar{\lambda}_{i}<(\bar{\lambda}_{0}/\bar{\sigma})^{2/3}\bar{q}^{2}
, and using (
53
) completes the proof.
∎

The next result provides a lower bound on the
\bar{\lambda}_{i}
s.

Lemma 24
.

We have

\displaystyle\bar{\lambda}_{i}>\frac{4}{\bar{\sigma}^{2}}(\bar{q})^{\frac{-3}{2^{i+1}}},\quad i=0,1,\dots,\lfloor n/2\rfloor-1.

(63)

Furthermore,
if
n
is even then

\bar{\lambda}_{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}>\frac{4}{\bar{\sigma}^{2}}(\bar{q})^{\frac{-4}{2^{(n/2)+1}}},

(64)

and if
n>1
is odd then

\bar{\lambda}_{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}>\frac{4}{\bar{\sigma}^{2}}(\bar{q})^{\frac{-3}{2^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor+1}}}.

Proof.

We consider three cases.

Case 1. Suppose that
i=0
. Eq. (
53
)
gives
\bar{\lambda}_{0}=\frac{8}{\bar{\sigma}^{2}(\bar{q})^{3}}
. Write this as

\bar{\lambda}_{0}=\frac{4}{\bar{\sigma}^{2}}(\bar{q})^{-3/2}\cdot\frac{2}{(\bar{q})^{3/2}}.

By (
54
),

\frac{2}{(\bar{q})^{3/2}}>1
for
n>1
, so
\bar{\lambda}_{0}>\frac{4}{\bar{\sigma}^{2}}(\bar{q})^{-3/2}
.

Case 2. Suppose that
i\in\{1,\dots,\lfloor n/2\rfloor-1\}
. Then the bound follows from combining (
IV-B
),
Lemma
20
, and (
53
).

Case 3. Suppose that
i=\lfloor n/2\rfloor
.
If
n
is even then (
45
) gives
\bar{a}_{i+1}=\bar{a}_{i+2}
, so (
IV-B
)
gives

\displaystyle\bar{\lambda}_{i}

\displaystyle=(\bar{\lambda}_{0}/\bar{\sigma})^{2/3}\bar{a}_{i+1}^{2},

and using the bound in Lemma
20

proves (
64
). If
n>1
is odd then the proof follows from the last
statement in Lemma
20
.
∎

We can now prove the main result.
Fix
i\in\{0,1,\dots,\lfloor n/2\rfloor-1\}
. Then (
63
) gives

\displaystyle\bar{\lambda}_{i}>\frac{4}{\bar{\sigma}^{2}}e^{\frac{-3\ln(\bar{q})}{2^{i+1}}}

Using the bound
e^{-x}>1-x
for any
x>0
yields

\bar{\lambda}_{i}>\frac{4}{\bar{\sigma}^{2}}\left(1-\frac{3\ln(\bar{q})}{2^{i+1}}\right).

(65)

By (
54
), we have
\ln(\bar{q})<\frac{2}{3}\ln(2)
, and this proves the inequality on the left-hand side of (
26
).
The second inequality follows from Lemma
23
.
Eqs. (
27
)
and (
28
) follow similarly from Lemma
24
.

We now prove (
25
). Assume first that
n>1
is odd. Then

\displaystyle n+1

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\bar{\lambda}_{i}

\displaystyle=2\sum_{i=0}^{(n-1)/2}\bar{\lambda}_{i}

\displaystyle>2\frac{4}{\bar{\sigma}^{2}}\sum_{i=0}^{(n-1)/2}\left(1-\frac{3\ln(\bar{q})}{2^{i+1}}\right)

\displaystyle>\frac{4}{\bar{\sigma}^{2}}(n+1-6\ln(\bar{q})),

where we used (
65
). Now assume that
n
is even. Then

\displaystyle n+1

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\bar{\lambda}_{i}

\displaystyle=2\sum_{i=0}^{(n/2)-1}\bar{\lambda}_{i}+\bar{\lambda}_{n/2}

\displaystyle>\frac{4}{\bar{\sigma}^{2}}\left(2\sum_{i=0}^{(n/2)-1}\left(1-\frac{3\ln(\bar{q})}{2^{i+1}}\right)+(1-\frac{4\ln(\bar{q})}{2^{(n/2)+1}})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{\bar{\sigma}^{2}}\left(n+{1}-6\ln(\bar{q})(1-\frac{1}{2^{n/2}})-2\ln(\bar{q})\frac{1}{2^{n/2}}\right)

\displaystyle>\frac{4}{\bar{\sigma}^{2}}(n+1-6\ln(\bar{q})).

Thus, in both cases we have

\frac{\bar{\sigma}^{2}}{4}>1-\frac{6\ln(\bar{q})}{n+1},

(66)

54

25

13
.

To prove (
29

\frac{4}{\bar{\sigma}^{2}}-\bar{\lambda}_{i}<\frac{4}{\bar{\sigma}^{2}}\frac{4}{3}\frac{\ln(2)}{2^{i}}

Using (

\displaystyle\frac{4}{\bar{\sigma}^{2}}

\displaystyle<\frac{n+1}{n+1-6\ln(\bar{q})}

\frac{4}{\bar{\sigma}^{2}}-\bar{\lambda}_{i}<\frac{4}{3}\frac{n+1}{n+1-4\ln(2)}\frac{\ln(2)}{2^{i}}.

VI
Discussion

We consider a nonlinear eigenvalue optimization problem that arises in an important nonlinear ODE model from systems biology called the ribosome flow model. The main result is that the optimal solution satisfies a turnpike property.
To the best of our knowledge, this is the first proof of such a structure in an eigenvalue optimization problem.
Although we consider a specific problem, our analysis is based on studying the general recursion (
44
), and it may be of interest to prove a turnpike structure in other recursions.

There is a simple intuitive explanation for the turnpike property. To see this, consider modifying the
(n+2)\times(n+2)
matrix
B

in (
1
) to the
(n+2)\times(n+2)

matrix

C:=\begin{bmatrix}0&\lambda_{0}^{-1/2}&0&0&0&\dots&0&0&\lambda_{n+1}^{-1/2}\\
\lambda_{0}^{-1/2}&0&\lambda_{1}^{-1/2}&0&0&\dots&0&0&0\\
0&\lambda_{1}^{-1/2}&0&\lambda_{2}^{-1/2}&0&\dots&0&0&0\\
&&\vdots\\
0&0&0&0&0&\dots&\lambda_{n-1}^{-1/2}&0&\lambda_{n}^{-1/2}\\
\lambda_{n+1}^{-1/2}&0&0&0&0&\dots&0&\lambda_{n}^{-1/2}&0\end{bmatrix},

(69)

that is, we added
\lambda_{n+1}^{-1/2}
at entries
(1,n+2)
and
(n+2,1)
. Note that
C
has a kind of cyclic structure.
Pose the
problem:
\min\sigma(C)
under the constraints
\lambda_{i}\geq 0
and
\sum_{i=0}^{n+1}\lambda_{i}\leq n+2
. By symmetry,
the solution is
\lambda_{i}=1
for all
i
. Thus, we may interpret the turnpike property for the problem with the matrix
B
as follows. The zero entries at
(1,n+2)
and
(n+2,1)
in
B
lead to a transient behavior of the optimal
\bar{\lambda}_{i}
s
before and after the bulk. In the bulk, they are close to
1
just like in the optimal solution with the matrix
C
. This interpretation is in line with the turnpike property in some optimal control problems, where the initial and final condition
constraints enforce a transient behavior of the optimal control values before and after the turnpike. The problem of minimizing
\sigma(C)
then plays the role of the static optimization problem used for determining the turnpike equilibrium in optimal control.

Further
topics for research include the following. First, as noted above, turnpike properties in optimal control is an important and widely studied topic. As noted in Ref.
[
31
]
: “As it is well known, the turnpike property is actually due to a general hyperbolicity
phenomenon. Roughly speaking, in the neighborhood of a saddle point, any trajectory of a given
hyperbolic dynamical system, which is constrained to remain in this neighborhood in large
time, will spend most of the time near the saddle point. This very simple observation is at the
heart of the turnpike results.” We have made this statement explicit in the context of our turnpike result in Remark
19
.
In this respect, it may be of interest to try and formulate the optimization problem studied here as an optimal control problem. One possible approach is to use Remark
4
to fix
R>0
and re-write (
II-A
) and
(
8
) as follows.
Find a positive control sequence
u(0),\dots,u(n)

that steers the discrete-time
control system

x(k+1)=1-\frac{R}{u(k)x(k)}

from
x(0)=1
to
x(n+1)=0

while minimizing
\sum_{i=0}^{n}u(i)
.
