On the Strong Convexity of PnP Regularization using Linear Denoisers
Arghya Sinha, Kunal N. Chaudhury

Abstract
In the Plug-and-Play (PnP) method, a denoiser is used as a regularizer within classical proximal algorithms for image reconstruction. It is known that a broad class of linear denoisers can be expressed as the proximal operator of a convex regularizer. Consequently, the associated PnP algorithm can be linked to a convex optimization problem 𝒫 \mathcal{P} . For such a linear denoiser, we prove that 𝒫 \mathcal{P} exhibits strong convexity for linear inverse problems. Specifically, we show that the strong convexity of 𝒫 \mathcal{P} can be used to certify objective and iterative convergence of any PnP algorithm derived from classical proximal methods.

2
Background

Any PnP algorithm has two basic components: the loss function and the denoiser. We will work with linear inverse problems, where the task is to recover an unknown image
\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
from linear measurements
\boldsymbol{b}=\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\xi}+\boldsymbol{w}
,
where
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
is the forward operator,
\boldsymbol{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
is the observed image, and
\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
is white Gaussian noise. The standard loss function in this case is

\ell_{\mathbf{A}}(\boldsymbol{x})=\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{b}\|_{2}^{2}.

(2)

This model includes inpainting, deblurring, and superresolution. The forward operator
\mathbf{A}
is a sampling operator in inpainting, a blur (convolution) in deblurring, and composition of blur and subsampling in superresolution
[
23
]
. On the other hand, we will work with linear denoisers such as NLM
[
24
]
, symmetric NLM
[
1
]
, LARK
[
25
]
, GLIDE
[
26
]
, and GMM
[
13
]
. We refer the reader to
[
16
,
17
]
and
[
1
, Sec. IV]
for more information on symmetric and kernel denoisers.

In PnP algorithms, regularization is performed by plugging a denoiser into a proximal algorithm such as ISTA, FISTA, HQS, or ADMM
[
1
,
12
,
20
,
6
]
. For example, starting with an initial guess
\boldsymbol{x}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the update in PnP-ISTA involves a gradient step on the loss followed by a denoising (regularization) step:

\boldsymbol{x}_{k+1}=\mathbf{W}\big(\boldsymbol{x}_{k}-\gamma\nabla\ell_{\mathbf{A}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{k})\big),

(3)

where
\gamma
is the step size and
\mathbf{W}
is the denoiser.

It was observed in
[
1
]
that for symmetric denoisers such as DSG-NLM
[
1
]
and GMM
[
13
]
,
\mathbf{W}
can be expressed as the proximal operator of a convex function
[
22
]
.

Definition 2.1
.

Consider an Euclidean space
(\mathbb{E},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)
and let
\varphi\in\Gamma_{0}(\mathbb{E})
. The proximal operator of
\varphi
is defined as

(\forall\,\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{E})\quad\mathrm{prox}_{\varphi,\|\cdot\|}(\boldsymbol{x})=\underset{\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{E}}{\arg\min}\ \frac{1}{2}\|\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{x}\|^{2}+\varphi(\boldsymbol{z}).

(4)

It was shown in
[
1
]
that there exists
\varphi_{\mathbf{W}}\in\Gamma_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
such that
\mathbf{W}=\mathrm{prox}_{\varphi_{\mathbf{W}},\|\cdot\|_{2}}
. An exact formula for
\varphi_{\mathbf{W}}
was later reported in
[
13
]
. However, it will be convenient for us to work with the following reformulation.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{S}^{n}
and
\sigma(\mathbf{W})\subset[0,1]
. Define
\varphi_{\mathbf{W}}\in\Gamma_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
to be

\varphi_{\mathbf{W}}(\boldsymbol{x})=\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}\mathbf{W}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{W})\boldsymbol{x}+\iota_{\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{W})}(\boldsymbol{x}).

(5)

Then
\mathbf{W}=\mathrm{prox}_{\varphi_{\mathbf{W}},\|\cdot\|_{2}}
.

A proof of Proposition
2.1
is provided in the Appendix for completeness. In other words, if
\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{S}^{n}
and
\sigma(\mathbf{W})\subset[0,1]
, we can view PnP-ISTA as the standard ISTA algorithm applied to problem (
22
), where the regularizer
\varphi=\varphi_{\mathbf{W}}
is given by (
5
). This applies not only to ISTA but to any PnP algorithm based on proximal methods, such as FISTA, ADMM, and HQS.

On the other hand, kernel denoisers such as NLM are not symmetric and hence cannot be expressed as a proximal operator. This comes as a consequence of a classical result due to Moreau
[
27
]
. Customized to linear operators, the result is as follows.

Theorem 2.1
.

Consider the Euclidean space
(\mathbb{R}^{n},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{2})
with inner product
\langle\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\rangle_{2}=\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}\!\boldsymbol{y}
. A linear operator
\mathbf{W}
on
(\mathbb{R}^{n},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{2})
is a proximal operator if and only if
\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{S}^{n}
and
\sigma(\mathbf{W})\subset[0,1]
.

Motivated by this result, it was observed in
[
20
]
that if
\mathbf{W}
is a nonsymmetric kernel denoiser, we must work with an inner product different from the standard inner product (dot product) on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. More precisely, consider a kernel denoiser of the form
\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{D}^{-1}\,\mathbf{K}
, where the kernel matrix
\mathbf{K}\in\mathbb{S}^{n}_{+}
is nonnegative with positive diagonal elements, and
\mathbf{D}=\diag(\mathbf{K}\boldsymbol{e})
is the normalizing matrix
[
16
]
. It was observed in
[
20
]
that
\mathbf{W}
is a proximal operator of some
\varphi\in\Gamma_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
(also see Proposition
3.1
) in
(\mathbb{R}^{n},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathbf{D}})
, where

\langle\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\rangle_{\mathbf{D}}:=\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}\mathbf{D}\boldsymbol{y}.

(6)

Accordingly, the gradient in (
3
) should be changed to

\nabla_{\mathbf{D}}\ell_{\mathbf{A}}(\boldsymbol{x})=\mathbf{D}^{-1}\nabla\ell_{\mathbf{A}}(\boldsymbol{x}).

(7)

The point is that the gradient depends on the choice of the inner product, and (
7
) is the gradient with respect to the inner product (
6
). The other updates remain unchanged. Following
[
20
]
, we will refer to these as scaled-PnP-ISTA. Similarly, by replacing the gradient with (
7
), we get scaled versions of FISTA, ADMM, and HQS. In Sec.
3
, we establish the objective and iterate convergence of these algorithms, which was left as an open problem in
[
28
]
.

3
Strong Convexity of PnP

We begin by defining strong convexity in the general setting of an Euclidean space
[
22
]
.

Definition 3.1
.

A function
f\in\Gamma_{0}(\mathbb{E})
is said to be strongly convex on the Euclidean space
(\mathbb{E},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)
if there exists
\mu>0
such that, for all
\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{E}
and
\theta\in[0,1]
,

\displaystyle f(\theta\boldsymbol{x}+(1-\theta)\boldsymbol{y})\leqslant\theta f(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle+(1-\theta)f(\boldsymbol{y})

\displaystyle-\frac{\mu}{2}\theta(1-\theta)\|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}\|^{2}.

(8)

The largest
\mu
in (
8
) is called the strong convexity index of
f
. It follows from (
8
) that
f
is
\mu
-strongly convex if and only if
\boldsymbol{x}\mapsto f(\boldsymbol{x})-(\mu/2)\|\boldsymbol{x}\|^{2}\in\Gamma_{0}(\mathbb{E})
. Based on this observation, we can conclude that the nonempty level sets of
f
are closed and bounded. Consequently, we obtain the following important result (see
[
22
]
for a proof).

Theorem 3.1
.

Suppose
f\in\Gamma_{0}(\mathbb{E})
is strongly convex on
(\mathbb{E},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)
. Then there exists an unique
\boldsymbol{x}^{*}\in\mathbb{E}
such that
\boldsymbol{x}^{*}=\arg\min_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{E}}\,f(\boldsymbol{x})
.

3.1
Symmetric denoiser

We first establish strong convexity of (
22
) for
\varphi=\varphi_{\mathbf{W}}
, where
\mathbf{W}
is a symmetric denoiser. We make a general observation in this regard.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{S}^{n}_{+}
,
\boldsymbol{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},d\in\mathbb{R},
and
\mathbb{V}
be a subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Consider the function
f\in\Gamma_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
given by

f(\boldsymbol{x})=\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}\!\mathbf{Q}\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{c}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}+d+\iota_{\mathbb{V}}(\boldsymbol{x}).

(9)

Then
f
is strongly convex on
(\mathbb{R}^{n},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{2})
if and only if
\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{Q})\cap\mathbb{V}=\{\mathbf{0}\}
. Moreover, the strong convexity index of
f
is

\mu=\inf\,\left\{\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}\boldsymbol{v}:\ \boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{V}\cap\mathcal{S}_{n}\right\}.

(10)

Proof.

Suppose
f
is strongly convex (with index
\mu>0
) and there exists
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{Q})\cap\mathbb{V},\,\boldsymbol{v}\neq\mathbf{0}
. Letting
\theta=1/2,\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{v},
and
\boldsymbol{y}=\mathbf{0}
in (
8
), we arrive at the impossible result:

\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{c}^{\top}\!\boldsymbol{v}\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{c}^{\top}\!\boldsymbol{v}-\frac{1}{8}\mu\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{2}^{2}

Thus, we must have
\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{Q})\cap\mathbb{V}=\{\mathbf{0}\}
if
f
is strongly convex.

Conversely, assume that
\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{Q})\cap\mathbb{V}=\{\mathbf{0}\}
. We assert that
\mu>0
. Indeed, if
\mu=0
, we would have
\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}\!\mathbf{Q}\boldsymbol{v}=0
for some
\boldsymbol{v}_{0}\in\mathbb{V}\cap\mathcal{S}_{n}
as the set
\left\{\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}\boldsymbol{v}:\ \boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{V}\cap\mathcal{S}_{n}\right\}
is closed. Moreover, as
\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{S}^{n}_{+}
, we would have
\boldsymbol{v}_{0}\in\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{Q})
, producing a nonzero
\boldsymbol{v}_{0}\in\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{Q})\cap\mathbb{V}
, contrary to our assumption.
To verify (
8
), note that it suffices to work with
\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{V}
, the effective domain of
f
. Now, for all
\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{V}
and
\theta\in[0,1]
, a simple calculation gives us

\displaystyle\theta f(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle+(1-\theta)f(\boldsymbol{y})-f(\theta\boldsymbol{x}+(1-\theta)\boldsymbol{y})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\theta(1-\theta)(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y})^{\top}\!\mathbf{Q}(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}).

Since
\mathbb{V}
is a subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{V}
. Hence, we have from (
10
) that
(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y})^{\top}\!\mathbf{Q}(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y})\geqslant\mu\|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}\|_{2}^{2}
. This completes the verification of (
8
). It is also evident from the above analysis that the strong convexity index of
f
is
\mu
.
∎

We are now ready to establish strong convexity of (
22
) when the loss function is (
2
) and the regularizer is (
5
).

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
and
\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{S}^{n}
, where
\sigma(\mathbf{W})\subset[0,1]
. Consider the function
f\in\Gamma_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
given by

f(\boldsymbol{x})=\ell_{\mathbf{A}}(\boldsymbol{x})+\lambda\,\varphi_{\mathbf{W}}(\boldsymbol{x})

(11)

where
\ell_{\mathbf{A}}
and
\varphi_{\mathbf{W}}
are given by (
2
) and (
5
). Then
f
is strongly convex on
(\mathbb{R}^{n},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{2})
for any
\lambda>0
if and only if
\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})\cap\mathrm{fix}\,(\mathbf{W})=\{\mathbf{0}\}
. Moreover, if

\mathbf{Q}:=\mathbf{A}^{\top}\!\mathbf{A}+\lambda\mathbf{W}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{W}),

(12)

then the strong convexity index of
f
is

\mu=\inf\,\left\{\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}\!\mathbf{Q}\boldsymbol{v}:\ \boldsymbol{v}\in\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{W})\cap\mathcal{S}_{n}\right\}.

Proof.

To use Lemma
3.1
, we write (
11
) as in (
9
), where
\mathbf{Q}
is given by (
12
),
\boldsymbol{c}=\boldsymbol{0},d=0
, and
\mathbb{V}=\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{W})
. The desired result would follow from Lemma
3.1
if we can show that
\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{Q})\cap\mathbb{V}=\{\mathbf{0}\}
.

Now, since
\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{S}^{n}
and
\sigma(\mathbf{W})\subset[0,1]
, we have
\mathbf{W}^{\dagger}\in\mathbb{S}^{n}
and
\sigma(\mathbf{W}^{\dagger})\subset[0,1]
, so that
\mathbf{W}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{W})\in\mathbb{S}^{n}_{+}
. In particular, this means
\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{S}^{n}_{+}
, and

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{Q})=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})\cap\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{W}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{W})).

(13)

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{Q})\cap\mathbb{V}=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})\cap\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{W}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{W}))\cap\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{W}).

We claim that
\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{W}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{W}))\cap\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{W})=\mathrm{fix}\,(\mathbf{W})
. Indeed, if
\mathbf{W}\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{x}
, then trivially
\mathbf{W}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{W})\boldsymbol{x}=\mathbf{0}
and
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{W})
. In the other direction, if
\mathbf{W}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{W})\boldsymbol{x}=\mathbf{0}
and
\boldsymbol{x}=\mathbf{W}\boldsymbol{z}
for some
\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we would have
\mathbf{W}^{\dagger}\mathbf{W}\boldsymbol{z}=\mathbf{W}^{\dagger}\mathbf{W}^{2}\boldsymbol{z}=\mathbf{W}\boldsymbol{z}=\boldsymbol{x}
. Multiplying this by
\mathbf{W}
, we get
\boldsymbol{x}=\mathbf{W}\boldsymbol{x}
, establishing our claim. Combining this with (
13
), we have
\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{Q})\cap\mathbb{V}=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})\cap\mathrm{fix}\,(\mathbf{W})=\{\mathbf{0}\}
, establishing our claim.
∎

We apply Thm.
3.2
to inpainting, deblurring, and superresolution. We assume that at least one pixel is sampled for inpainting, and the blur is nonnegative (and nonzero) for deblurring and superresolution; these conditions are typically met in practice.

Corollary 3.1
.

Let
\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{S}^{n}_{+}
be stochastic and irreducible, and let
\mathbf{A}
be the forward model corresponding to inpainting, deblurring, or superresolution. Then optimization (
22
) with
\ell=\ell_{\mathbf{A}}
and
\varphi=\varphi_{\mathbf{W}}
is strongly convex on
(\mathbb{R}^{n},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{2})
.

Proof.

Since
\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{S}^{n}_{+}
and
\mathbf{W}\boldsymbol{e}=\boldsymbol{e}
(
\boldsymbol{e}
is the all-ones vector), we have
\sigma(\mathbf{W})\subset[0,1]
. Thus,
\mathbf{W}
satisfies the hypothesis in Thm.
3.2
. On the other hand, we can conclude from
\mathbf{W}\boldsymbol{e}=\boldsymbol{e}
that
\mathrm{span}\{\boldsymbol{e}\}\subset\mathrm{fix}\,(\mathbf{W})
. In fact, since
\mathbf{W}
is irreducible, we can conclude from the Perron-Frobenius theorem
[
29
]
that
\mathrm{fix}\,(\mathbf{W})=\mathrm{span}\{\boldsymbol{e}\}
. Consequently, to show that
\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})\cap\mathrm{fix}\,(\mathbf{W})=\{\mathbf{0}\}
, it suffices to check
\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{e}\neq\mathbf{0}
. It is clear that
\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{e}\neq\mathbf{0}
for inpainting and deblurring. Moreover,
\mathbf{A}
is a lowpass blur followed by downsampling in superresolution, so
\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{e}\neq\mathbf{0}
. Thus,
\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})\cap\mathrm{fix}\,(\mathbf{W})=\{\mathbf{0}\}
for inpainting, deblurring, and superresolution. It follows from Thm.
3.2
that (
22
) with
\varphi=\varphi_{\mathbf{W}}
is strongly convex on
(\mathbb{R}^{n},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{2})
.
∎

3.2
Kernel denoiser

We next extend Thm.
3.2
to kernel denoisers of the form
\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{D}^{-1}\mathbf{K}
. As noted earlier,
\mathbf{W}
is generally nonsymmetric, and it follows from Thm.
2.1
that
\mathbf{W}
is not a proximal operator on
(\mathbb{R}^{n},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{2})
. However, we can write

\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{D}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{W}_{s}\mathbf{D}^{\frac{1}{2}},\quad\mathbf{W}_{s}:=\mathbf{D}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{K}\mathbf{D}^{-\frac{1}{2}}.

(14)

That is,
\mathbf{W}
is similar to
\mathbf{W}_{s}\in\mathbb{S}^{n}
. Hence,
\sigma(\mathbf{W}_{s})=\sigma(\mathbf{W})\subset[0,1]
. We know from Proposition
2.1
that
\mathbf{W}_{s}
is the proximal operator of
{\varphi_{\mathbf{W}_{s}}}
given by (
5
). We use this to construct the regularizer associated with
\mathbf{W}
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
\mathbf{W}
be a kernel denoiser. Define
\mathbf{W}_{s}
as in (
14
) and the corresponding regularizer
\varphi_{\mathbf{W}_{s}}
in (
5
). Define
\phi_{\mathbf{W}}\in\Gamma_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
to be

\phi_{\mathbf{W}}(\boldsymbol{x})=\varphi_{\mathbf{W}_{s}}(\mathbf{D}^{\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{x}).

(15)

Then
\mathbf{W}=\mathrm{prox}_{\phi_{\mathbf{W}},\|\cdot\|_{\mathbf{D}}}
, where
\|\cdot\|_{\mathbf{D}}
is induced by (
6
). In other words,
\mathbf{W}
is a proximal operator on
(\mathbb{R}^{n},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathbf{D}})
.

Proof.

Fix
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Following (
15
) and Definition
2.1
, we need to verify that
\mathbf{W}\boldsymbol{x}
is the minimizer of
h\in\Gamma_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
given by

\displaystyle h(\boldsymbol{z})=\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{D}^{\frac{1}{2}}(\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{x})\|_{2}^{2}+\varphi_{\mathbf{W}_{s}}(\mathbf{D}^{\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{z}),

where we have used the fact that
\|\cdot\|_{\mathbf{D}}=\|\mathbf{D}^{\frac{1}{2}}\cdot\|_{2}
. Performing the substitution
\boldsymbol{y}=\mathbf{D}^{\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{z}
, we get

\underset{\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}{\arg\min}\ h(\boldsymbol{z})=\mathbf{D}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\Big(\underset{\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}{\arg\min}\ g(\boldsymbol{y})\Big),

(16)

where

g(\boldsymbol{y}):=h(\mathbf{D}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{y})=\frac{1}{2}\|\boldsymbol{y}-\mathbf{D}^{\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{x}\|_{2}^{2}+\varphi_{\mathbf{W}_{s}}(\boldsymbol{y}).

However, from Proposition
2.1
and Definition
2.1
, we have

\underset{\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}{\arg\min}\ g(\boldsymbol{y})=\mathrm{prox}_{\phi_{\mathbf{W}},\|\cdot\|_{2}}(\mathbf{D}^{\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{x})=\mathbf{W}_{s}(\mathbf{D}^{\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{x}).

The desired result follows from (
14
) and (
16
).
∎

Having identified the regularizer associated with a kernel denoiser, we next analyze when the optimization problem is strongly convex on
(\mathbb{R}^{n},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathbf{D}})
.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
and
\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be a kernel denoiser. For
\lambda>0
, let
f\in\Gamma_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
be

f(\boldsymbol{x})=\ell_{\mathbf{A}}(\boldsymbol{x})+\lambda\,\phi_{\mathbf{W}}(\boldsymbol{x}).

(17)

Then
f
is strongly convex on
(\mathbb{R}^{n},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathbf{D}})
if and only if

\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})\cap\mathrm{fix}\,(\mathbf{W})=\{\mathbf{0}\}
.

Proof.

We can deduce this from Thm.
3.2
. Indeed, note that (
17
) is strongly convex on
(\mathbb{R}^{n},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathbf{D}})
if and only if
g(\boldsymbol{x}):=f(\mathbf{D}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{x})
is strongly convex on
(\mathbb{R}^{n},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{2})
. Now, it follows from (
2
) and (
15
) that

\displaystyle g(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=\ell_{\mathbf{A}}(\mathbf{D}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{x})+\lambda\,\phi_{\mathbf{W}}(\mathbf{D}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=\ell_{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{D}^{-1/2}}(\boldsymbol{x})+\lambda\,\varphi_{\mathbf{W}_{s}}(\boldsymbol{x}).

We are now in the setting of Thm.
3.2
. In particular,
g
is strongly convex on
(\mathbb{R}^{n},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{2})
if and only if

\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{D}^{-\frac{1}{2}})\cap\mathrm{fix}\,(\mathbf{W}_{s})=\{\mathbf{0}\}.

(18)

However,
\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{D}^{-\frac{1}{2}})=\mathbf{D}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})
. Moreover, we have from (
14
) that
\mathrm{fix}\,(\mathbf{W}_{s})=\mathbf{D}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{fix}\,(\mathbf{W})
.
Consequently,

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{D}^{-\frac{1}{2}})\cap\mathrm{fix}\,(\mathbf{W}_{s})=\mathbf{D}^{\frac{1}{2}}\big(\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})\cap\mathrm{fix}\,(\mathbf{W})\big).

Since
\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})\cap\mathrm{fix}\,(\mathbf{W})=\{\mathbf{0}\}
by assumption, this verifies (
18
) and completes the proof.
∎

Similar to Corollary
3.1
, we can deduce the following result from Thm.
3.3
.

Corollary 3.2
.

Let
\mathbf{W}
be a kernel denoiser and
\mathbf{A}
correspond to inpainting, deblurring, or superresolution. Then (
22
) with
\ell=\ell_{\mathbf{A}}
and
\varphi=\phi_{\mathbf{W}}
is strongly convex on
(\mathbb{R}^{n},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathbf{D}})
.

4
Convergence Analysis

We now explain how Corollaries
3.1
and
3.2
can be used to establish convergence of any PnP algorithm derived from a proximal method. We have seen that the updates in (
3
) correspond to running ISTA in
(\mathbb{R}^{n},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{2})
for symmetric denoisers. On the other hand, if we replace the gradient in (
3
) with (
7
), then PnP-ISTA corresponds to running classical ISTA in
(\mathbb{R}^{n},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathbf{D}})
for kernel denoisers. Henceforth, we will not distinguish between symmetric and kernel denoisers, assuming that the gradient is calculated as explained above. The following is the main result of this note.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
\mathbf{W}
be a linear denoiser as in Corollaries
3.1
and
3.2
, and let
\mathbf{A}
correspond to inpainting, deblurring, or superresolution. Then any PnP algorithm, with
\mathbf{W}
as regularizer, corresponds to solving a convex optimization problem of the form (
22
). Moreover, we can guarantee objective and iterate convergence of the PnP iterates
\{\boldsymbol{x}_{k}\}
, i.e.,
\ell(\boldsymbol{x}_{k})+\lambda\varphi(\boldsymbol{x}_{k})\to\nu
and
\boldsymbol{x}_{k}\to\boldsymbol{x}^{*}
, where
\nu
and
\boldsymbol{x}^{*}
are the minimum value and the (unique) minimizer of (
22
).

Thm.
4.1
can be deduced from Corollaries
3.1
and
3.2
and standard objective convergence results for proximal algorithms. The proof is deferred to the Appendix.

We note that in our prior work
[
28
,
30
]
, iterate convergence was established for PnP-ISTA and PnP-ADMM, but the focus was on symmetric denoisers. On the other hand, Thm.
4.1
provides a unified analysis for symmetric and kernel denoisers, extending the results in
[
28
]
. Moreover, the convergence guarantee in Thm.
4.1
covers any PnP algorithm in principle, not just PnP-ISTA and PnP-ADMM. We remark that the condition
\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})\cap\mathrm{fix}\,(\mathbf{W})=\{\mathbf{0}\}
in Corollaries
3.1
and
3.2
was used in
[
28
]
to deduce linear convergence of the iterates of PnP-ISTA and PnP-ADMM.

6
Conclusion

We showed that PnP regularization of linear inverse problems using kernel denoisers amounts to solving a strongly convex optimization problem. Consequently, we established objective and iterative convergence for any PnP algorithm applied to linear inverse problems, thereby providing a solid theoretical foundation for these methods. In particular, the current analysis addresses two unresolved issues: the iterate convergence of PnP-FISTA and of scaled PnP algorithms using nonsymmetric denoisers.

7.1
Proof of Proposition 2.1

We have to show that, for any
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\mathbf{W}\boldsymbol{x}
is a minimizer of the function
h=q+\iota_{\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{W})}
, where

q(\boldsymbol{z})=\frac{1}{2}\|\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{x}\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{z}^{\top}\mathbf{W}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{W})\boldsymbol{z}.

Since
\sigma(\mathbf{W})\subset[0,1]
, the matrix
\mathbf{W}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{W})
is positive semidefinite, so that
q
, and hence
h
, is convex. Consequently,
\mathbf{W}\boldsymbol{x}
is a minimizer
h
if and ony if
\mathbf{0}\in\partial h(\mathbf{W}\boldsymbol{x})
, where
\partial h
is the subdifferential of
h
.
To compute
\partial h
, we apply the sum rule for the subdifferential
[
31
, Thm. 23.8]
. This requires us to check that

\mathrm{ri}(\mathrm{dom}\ q)\,\cap\,\mathrm{ri}\left(\mathrm{dom}\ \iota_{\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{W})}\right)\neq\emptyset,

(19)

where
\mathrm{ri}
and
\mathrm{dom}
stand for the relative interior and the domain of a set
[
31
]
. However,
\mathrm{dom}\ q=\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and

\mathrm{ri}\left(\mathrm{dom}\ \iota_{\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{W})}\right)=\mathrm{ri}(\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{W}))=\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{W}),

so that (
19
) holds trivially. Applying the sum rule to
h=q+\iota_{\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{W})}
, we have, for all
\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

\displaystyle\partial h(\boldsymbol{z})

\displaystyle=\nabla q(\boldsymbol{z})+\partial\iota_{\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{W})}(\boldsymbol{z})

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{x}+\mathbf{W}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{W})\boldsymbol{z}+\partial\iota_{\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{W})}(\boldsymbol{z}).

(20)

We know that
\partial\iota_{\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{W})}(\boldsymbol{z})
is the normal cone
[
22
, Example 3.5]
of
\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{W})
for all
\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{W})
. In particular, since
\mathbf{W}
is symmetric, the normal cone of
\mathcal{R}({\mathbf{W}})
is its orthogonal complement
\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{W})
.
Moreover, since
\mathbf{W}^{\dagger}\mathbf{W}^{2}=\mathbf{W}\mathbf{W}^{\dagger}\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{W}

[
29
]
, we have from (
20
) that

\partial h(\mathbf{W}\boldsymbol{x})=-(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{W}^{\dagger}\mathbf{W})\boldsymbol{x}+\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{W}),

(21)

Applying
\mathbf{W}^{\dagger}\mathbf{W}^{2}=\mathbf{W}
once more, we get
\mathbf{W}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{W}^{\dagger}\mathbf{W})\boldsymbol{x}=\mathbf{0}
. Thus,
\boldsymbol{0}\in\partial h(\mathbf{W}\boldsymbol{x})
, which completes the proof.

7.2
Proof of Theorem 4.1

For a linear denoiser, we know that the PnP algorithm corresponds to solving the optimization problem

\nu=\min_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\,f(\boldsymbol{x}),\quad f(\boldsymbol{x}):=\ell_{\mathbf{A}}(\boldsymbol{x})+\lambda\,\varphi(\boldsymbol{x}).

(22)

using a proximal (or scaled proximal) algorithm. It follows from Corollaries 3.1 and 3.2 that when
\mathbf{A}
corresponds to inpainting, deblurring, or superresolution,
f
is strongly convex on
(\mathbb{R}^{n},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)
for some
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
. Thus, we can conclude from Thm. 3.1 that
\nu
is finite, and there exists a unique minimizer
\boldsymbol{x}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
f(\boldsymbol{x}^{*})=\nu
.

Objective convergence, i.e.,
f(\boldsymbol{x}_{k})\to\nu
, where
\{\boldsymbol{x}_{k}\}
are the iterates of the PnP algorithm, follows from standard results on the (objective) convergence of proximal algorithms; e.g., see
[
22
,
32
]
. These results implicitly assume that (
22
) has a minimizer, which is guaranteed for our problem.

To prove iterate convergence, it suffices to show that the (subsequential) limit points of the sequence
\{\boldsymbol{x}_{k}\}
are identical. Recall from
[
33
]
that
\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is called a subsequential limit of the sequence
\{\boldsymbol{x}_{k}\}
if there exists a subsequence
\{\boldsymbol{x}_{k_{i}}\}
of
\{\boldsymbol{x}_{k}\}
that converges to
\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}
. It is evident that
\{\boldsymbol{x}_{k}\}
converges to some
\boldsymbol{x}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
if and only if all its subsequences are convergent and their limits equal
\boldsymbol{x}^{*}
.

We begin with the observation that the PnP iterates
\{\boldsymbol{x}_{k}\}
are bounded. Indeed, since
f
is strongly convex, it is coercive
[
22
]
, i.e.,
f(\boldsymbol{x})\to\infty
as
\|\boldsymbol{x}\|\to\infty
, where
\|\cdot\|
is the norm induced by
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
. Thus, if
\{\boldsymbol{x}_{k}\}
were not bounded, we would end up contradicting the fact that
\{f(\boldsymbol{x}_{k})\}
is convergent.

As
\{\boldsymbol{x}_{k}\}
is bounded, we know from the Bolzano-Weierstrass theorem that it has at least one subsequential limit
[
33
]
. In fact, we claim that
\{\boldsymbol{x}_{k}\}
has exactly one such limit point, namely, the minimizer
\boldsymbol{x}^{*}
of (
22
). Indeed, suppose
\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}
is a subsequential limit of
\{\boldsymbol{x}_{k}\}
. Then there exists a subsequence
\{\boldsymbol{x}_{k_{i}}\}
such that
\boldsymbol{x}_{k_{i}}\to\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}
. However, since
f
is lower semicontinuous, we have

\nu\leqslant f(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}})\leqslant\lim_{k\to\infty}\,f(\boldsymbol{x}_{k_{i}})=\lim_{k\to\infty}\,f(\boldsymbol{x}_{k})=\nu,

that is,
\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}
is a minimizer of (
22
). Finally, since
f
has exactly one minimizer, it must be that
\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}=\boldsymbol{x}^{*}
. This holds for any subsequential limit
\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}
, whereby we it follows that
\{\boldsymbol{x}_{k}\}
converges to
\boldsymbol{x}^{*}
.

7.3
Computation of
\mu

We discuss how the strong convexity index
\mu
is computed in the experiments in Sec. V. Determining
\mu
exactly is not easy as it requires us to solve an optimization over a nonconvex unit sphere
\mathbb{V}\cap\,\mathcal{S}_{n}
(see Thm. 3.2). Nonetheless, we can establish a lower bound for
\mu
by expanding the domain to
\mathcal{S}_{n}
. The lower bound in question (which turns out to be positive as reported in Sec. V) is simply the smallest eigenvalue
\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{Q})
, where

\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{A}^{\top}\!\mathbf{A}+\lambda\,\mathbf{W}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{W}).

The computational challenge here is that neither the forward model
\mathbf{A}
nor the denoiser
\mathbf{W}
cannot be stored and manipulated as matrices; we have to work with
\mathbf{A},\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{W}
and
\mathbf{W}^{\dagger}
as operators (black boxes). This is where we can utilize the power method
[
34
]
, which involves repeatedly applying
\mathbf{Q}
to determine its dominant eigenvalue
d(\mathbf{Q})
, i.e., the eigenvalue with the largest absolute value. We apply the power method twice to find
\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{Q})
. The first application is to identify the dominant eigenvalue
d(\mathbf{Q})
, which in our case is
d(\mathbf{Q})=\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{Q})\geqslant 0
since
\mathbf{Q}
is positive semidefinite. We next apply the power method on the shifted operator
\mathbf{Q}_{s}=\mathbf{Q}-d(\mathbf{Q})\mathbf{I}
to get
d(\mathbf{Q}_{s})
. We can verify that
d(\mathbf{Q}_{s})=\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{Q})-d(\mathbf{Q})
, which gives us

\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{Q})=d(\mathbf{Q}_{s})+d(\mathbf{Q}).

In addition to the operators
\mathbf{A}
,
\mathbf{A}^{\top}
, and
\mathbf{W}
, which can all be applied efficiently, we also require the pseudoinverse
\mathbf{W}^{\dagger}
to compute
\mathbf{Q}
. This can be achieved through multiple applications of
\mathbf{W}
.
