Multifunctional TPU-HAp/HA-TiO2 membranes for prosthesis osteointegration and prevention of in situ infection
Authors: Eleonora Bianchi, Marco Ruggeri, Barbara Vigani, Carmela Tommasino, Elisabetta Maffioli, Pietro Grisoli, Cinzia Boselli, Antonia Icaro Cornaglia, Silvia Rossi, Giuseppina Sandri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Bone defects and implant-associated infections remain major challenges in orthopedic reconstruction, requiring multifunctional biomaterials able to promote osteointegration while preventing bacterial colonization. This work aimed to develop an electrospun nanofibrous membrane based on thermoplastic polyurethane (TPU) and hydroxyapatite (HAp), coated with hyaluronic acid (HA) and functionalized with titanium dioxide (TiO₂) nanoparticles as lining of orthopedic implants. The developed membranes were characterized in terms of morphology, surface properties, mechanical performance, degradation, antibacterial activity, and biological response. The HA-TiO₂-coated membrane showed increased surface wettability and roughness compared with uncoated TPU-based membranes, while maintaining fibrous morphology. After 6 weeks in phosphate-buffered solution, uncoated membranes showed approximately 6% mass loss, whereas HA-coated membranes showed approximately 50% mass loss during the first week, consistent with partial coating removal. The TiO₂-containing membrane reduced bacterial viability after 24 h, corresponding to approximately 1.31 and 1.05 log₁₀ reductions for Escherichia coli and Staphylococcus aureus, respectively. Both human adipose-derived stem cells and SAOS-2 cells adhered to and proliferated on the HA-TiO₂-functionalized membranes, with extracellular matrix sialoprotein deposition observed after 21 days. Subcutaneous implantation in rats for 18 days showed no evident inflammatory or foreign-body response. Overall, the results support the potential of the TPU-HAp/HA-TiO₂ membrane as a multifunctional biomaterial for lining orthopedic implant interfaces, although further bone-specific and infection-relevant in vivo studies are required to establish its osteointegration and infection-prevention efficacy.

Introduction

Orthopedic prostheses are surgically implanted as devices designed to replace, support, or restore the function of missing or damaged joints and limbs, primarily targeting the hip, knee, and shoulder to alleviate pain and improve mobility. Advanced materials, such as titanium alloys and polyethylene, are the gold standard (Xu et al., 2024).

The surgical procedure considers a metal implant inserted into the intramedullary canal of the bone (femur, tibia, or humerus). The healing time is long due to the poor osteoconductive and osteoinductive properties and several months are generally required and this makes prone to mechanical complications such as implant loosening or bone fractures (Qu et al., 2019; Anjum et al., 2022). Consequently, there is a growing demand for bioactive systems as prosthesis lining capable of promoting osteointegration. Another key concern in orthopedic implantology is represented by colonization and infections. In fact, surgical site infection (SSI) is one of the most serious and prevalent postoperative complications associated with orthopedic prosthesis implant (Arciola et al., 2018; Metsemakers et al., 2018). These infections pose significant clinical and socioeconomic challenges, often resulting in delayed healing, prolonged antibiotic therapy, extended hospitalization, and, in severe cases, amputation of the affected limb (Hayashi et al., 2022). The development of an infection at the surgical site severely compromises the success of interventions and adversely affects patient outcomes. In fact, the incidence of SSI following orthopedic procedures remains one of the most challenging complications associated with implant surgery due to its clinical and socioeconomic impact. Depending on the surgical procedure, patient population, and healthcare setting, the incidence of SSI varies considerably, with pooled global estimates around 11% for general surgery, and substantial variability across surgical specialties and countries (Liu et al., 2024; Gillespie et al., 2021). Such high infection rates continue to represent a major challenge in orthopedic and trauma surgery, particularly when biomaterials are employed (Ene et al., 2021; Henkelmann et al., 2017; Preethi and Bellare, 2021; Afewerki et al., 2020). Although biomaterial-based implants play a crucial role in providing structural support and guiding osteointegration, their implantation introduces surfaces that serve as potential sites for microbial adhesion and development. Bacterial colonization of an implant conceivably creates the conditions for biofilm formation and in the biofilm, the bacteria possess strong resistance to antibiotics, antimicrobial chemotherapeutics and host immune responses, creating a persistent infection difficult to eradicate. In many cases, this needs the surgical removal of the infected implant, further complicating patient management and increasing treatment costs (Hayashi et al., 2022; Sharma et al., 2023). To address this challenge, modern strategies in biomaterials research have focused on developing implants that combine osteogenic and antimicrobial properties. Ideally, the surface of osteoconductive implant should be locally functionalized with a minimal effective concentration of antibacterial agents. This localized modification prevents bacterial colonization while preserving the intrinsic osteoconductivity and biocompatibility of the implant surface, ensuring that this remains favorable for osteoblast adhesion, proliferation, and differentiation (Bianchi et al., 2022; Shineh et al., 2024). The balance between infection prevention and osteointegration is essential to improve clinical outcomes and reducing SSI incidence in orthopedic applications. Accordingly, the design of a multifunctional coating that promote tissue integration while preventing bacterial adhesion represents a critical frontier in bone tissue engineering and regenerative medicine. In fact an ideal implant should not only provide a three-dimensional (3D) structure for cell homing, proliferation, and differentiation but also prevent bacterial colonization during the early stages of the implant (Dubey et al., 2023).

Synthetic polymers such as thermoplastic polyurethane (TPU) are widely used in biomedical applications due to their tunable elasticity, durability, and biocompatibility. In particular, TPU-based materials have been successfully employed in vascular grafts, wound dressings, and orthopedic applications, demonstrating favorable mechanical behavior that can mimic native soft and hard tissues (Bianchi et al., 2025a). However, polymeric materials alone generally lack bioactivity and osteoconductivity, and inorganic fillers or surface modifications to support osteointegration are crucial. At this purpose, hydroxyapatite (HAp), a calcium phosphate ceramic that closely resembles the mineral phase of natural bone, has been extensively used to enhance the osteoconductivity of polymeric materials (Bianchi et al., 2022). HAp doping into TPU matrices should significantly improve bioactivity, mechanical performance, and cell adhesion, providing a biomimetic environment for osteointegration. In fact, the release of calcium and phosphate ions from HAp promotes osteoblast differentiation and extracellular matrix (ECM) mineralization, facilitating new bone tissue formation (Ramesh et al., 2018).

Beyond bulk composition, surface characteristics also play a pivotal role in determining the biological and antibacterial performance of biomaterials. Surface coatings can enhance cell adhesion, modulate protein adsorption, and control bacterial interactions. Hyaluronic acid (HA), a naturally occurring glycosaminoglycan found in the ECM, is particularly attractive for surface functionalization due to its excellent biocompatibility, hydrophilicity, and ability to promote cell migration and proliferation. Moreover, HA coatings have been shown to improve initial cell anchorage and reduce inflammatory responses, thereby facilitating tissue integration (Ye et al., 2025).

Given these premises, the present study aimed at the design and the development of a membrane as multifunctional lining of prosthesis to favour osteointegration and to prevent microbial colonization, with an ultimate goal of minimizing infection risk and surgery reoccurrence. In particular, its fibrous architecture is intended to provide a biomimetic interface capable of supporting cell adhesion and improve tissue healing. At this purpose, a nanocomposite nanofibrous membrane based on TPU doped with HAp and coated with HA functionalized with titanium dioxide (TiO₂) nanoparticles (NPs) was manufactured. TiO₂ NPs were selected as unspecific antimicrobial agent to prevent bacterial colonization. In fact, they are characterized by chemical stability, biocompatibility, and antimicrobial effects, due to the development of reactive oxygen species (ROS) capable of damaging bacterial membranes and interfering with metabolic processes avoiding antimicrobial resistance. Moreover, TiO₂ NPs have been shown to support osteoblast proliferation and differentiation, making them highly suitable for bone tissue engineering (Zhang et al., 2021; Chen et al., 2024).

The rationale for combining TPU, HAp, HA, and TiO₂ was based on the complementary functions expected from each component in order to simultaneously satisfy all requirements of an orthopedic implant. TPU provides a flexible, mechanically resistant polymeric framework and is suitable for electrospinning. However, TPU alone possesses limited intrinsic osteoconductivity. HAp was therefore loaded into the TPU fibers to introduce a bone-mineral-like inorganic phase and provide an osteoconductive component. Conversely, because HAp is embedded within the polymer matrix, its contribution to the immediate surface properties may be limited. HA was consequently applied as a surface coating to increase hydrophilicity and provide a biomimetic ECM-associated surface. Finally, TiO₂ NPs doped HA coating provided an additional antibacterial function at the material–environment interface. This layered approach was intended to support the complementary functions, while limiting the amount of TiO₂ required within the bulk membrane.

Therefore, the hypothesis underlying this study was that the integration of HAp within the TPU nanofibrous membrane, combined with a HA-TiO₂ surface coating, could provide a multifunctional platform capable of promoting osteointegration while limiting bacterial adhesion and colonization.

Materials and methods

Materials

The following polymers were used: medical-grade TPU (Pathway, Lubrizol Advanced Materials, New Milford, USA); HA (based on β −1,3-linked N-acetylglucosamine and β-(1,4)-d-glucuronic acid) with a low molecular weight of 212 kDa (Bioiberica, Barcellona, Spain). The inorganics used were: HAp, nanopowder, < 200 nm particle size, ≥97% synthetic (Sigma-Aldrich, USA); TiO2 nanopowder, <100 nm particle size (BET), 99.5% trace metals basis (Sigma-Aldrich, USA). The solvent used was glacial acetic acid (Carlo Erba Reagents, Val-de-Reuil Cedex, France).

TPU (12% w/w) was dissolved in water:acetic acid (1:9 ratio) at room temperature under magnetic stirring overnight. At TPU complete solubilization, 1% w/w HAp NPs was added to the blend.

To obtain the active coating, 5% w/w HA was prepared in water and stirred until complete solubilization. HA solution was then doped with 0.2% w/w of TiO2 NPs, based on a preliminary in vitro evaluation of TiO2 cytotoxicity (Supplementary Information: Fig. S1).

Development of the electrospun fibers

Nanofibrous membranes were developed by means of an electrospinning apparatus (STKIT-40, Linari Engineering, Pisa, Italy) equipped with a volumetric pump (Razel R99-E) and a high-voltage power supply (Razel R99-E 40 kV). A static and flat collector was used to obtain randomly collected and porous micrometric fibers. The electrospinning parameters were set as follows: needle tip-collector distance 15 cm, flow rate 0.758 mL/h, voltage 22 kV. The relative humidity and temperature ranged between 30 and 35%, and 25–27 °C, respectively. The obtained fibers were insoluble in water.

HA coating

Electrospun fibers were cut in pieces (4 × 4 cm) and immersed into the HA (T3) or HA-TiO2 (T4) solu for 1 h. After that, the obtained systems were moved to a clean Petri dish and dried in a laboratory drying oven at 45 °C overnight.

Systems' chemico-physical characterization

The membrane morphology was assessed with a scanning electron microscope (SEM) (Phenom Pure G6, AlfaTest srl, Italy) after 15 nm gold sputtering (Luxor Au sputter coater, Luxor Tech, Belgium). The dimensions were evaluated by an image analysis software (ImageJ, ICY, Institut Pasteur, Paris, France). For this purpose, to ensure that the measured fibers were randomly chosen and representative of the whole system, three different images were used, and 30 analyses each were performed, with a final total of 90 analyses. Moreover, the doping with HAp and the presence of TiO2 NPs into the final multifunctional formulation (T4) were assessed by SEM-EDX (Tescan, Mira3XMU, Brno, Czech Republic), recording an EDX spectrum.

The membrane topography and profilometry was determined by means of Atomic Force Microscopy (AFM, Tosca 200 Anton Paar, Austria) in tapping mode (cantilever APARROW-NCR, Silicon SPM-Sensor, Al-coating; thickness: 4.6 μm, Length: 160 μm, width: 45 μm, resonance frequency: 285 kHz, force constant: 42 N/m; Anton Paar, Austria).

The wettability of the membrane was tested by contact angle measurements (DMe-211 Plus; FAMAS software, Kyowa, Osaka, Japan) by recording a droplet shape (0.4 μL of PBS) through the CCD camera 100, 500, and 1000 ms after the droplet touched the nanofibrous membrane surface.

The apparent zeta potential (ζ) of the systems was determined by streaming potential measurement, performed with SurPASS™ 3 (Anton Paar GmbH, Italy) equipped with a cylindrical cell. The membrane were cut in pieces (10 × 10 mm) and mounted in the sample holder of the cylindrical cell. The measurement was carried out using 0.1 mol/L KCl aqueous solution as streaming solvent, in 2–10 pH range (Bondi et al., 2025).

Mechanical properties evaluation

The mechanical properties were evaluated by means of a dynamometer (TA-XT plus, Stable Microsystems, Italy) equipped with a 5.0 kg load cell. The systems were cut in pieces (2 × 1 cm) and loaded between two tensile grips (A/TG probe; final distance: 50.0 mm). The upper grip was moved at a constant speed of 5.0 mm/s until the system's rupture. The mechanical properties were evaluated both in dry and hydrated state. The maximum force at break was recorded, and the elongation (%) and Young's modulus were calculated (Bianchi et al., 2025b).

Systems in vitro degradation

To provide an initial assessment of the stability of the different membrane formulations under quasi-physiological conditions, each system was weighed (around 10 mg) and placed in 2 mL of PBS (phosphate saline buffer pH 7.4, Sigma- Aldrich, Milan, Italy) at 37 °C. Samples were removed after 1, 2, 4, and 6 weeks from the solution, washed in distilled water, dried in an oven at 50 °C until a constant weight was reached and reweighed. The weight loss (%) was calculated as the ratio between the weight after degradation and the initial weight (Bianchi et al., 2025b). At every time interval (1, 2, 4, and 6 weeks), the membrane morphology was evaluated using SEM and SEM-EDX, as previously described in paragraph 2.4, in order to evaluate the morphological changes during the degradation process. This experimental design was intended as a first-stage evaluation of membrane stability under standardized quasi-physiological conditions. More complex degradation environments, including acidic conditions and enzymatic media, were beyond the scope of the present study and will require further investigation.

In vitro evaluation

Proliferation and cell viability were carried out using human osteogenic sarcoma cells (SAOS-2, Sigma-Aldrich, Milano, Ital; 1st–5th passages), and human adipose stem cells (hASC, ZenBio, Durham, NC, USA; 1st–3rd passages) as cells models. hASC were chosen in order to evaluate their capability to differentiate into bone-like cells and produce ECM components when cultured onto the membrane environments. SAOS-2 cells were cultured using McCoy growth medium (McCoy's 5 A Medium Modified, with l-Glutamine and sodium bicarbonate, Sigma-Aldrich, Milan, Italy) supplemented with 200 IU/mL penicillin/0.2 mg/mL streptomycin (pen/strep/ampho100x, Euroclone, Pero (MI), Italy) and 10% v/v FBS (fetal bovine serum, Euroclone, Pero (MI), Italy). hASC were cultured using basal-α-MEM medium (α-MEM, ZenBio, Durham, NS, USA), supplemented with 10% v/v FBS, 0.22% w/v bicarbonate, and 200 IU/mL penicillin/0.2 mg/mL streptomycin. Both cell types were grown in an incubator (CO2 Incubator, PBI International, Milano, Italy) at 37 °C, with 5% CO2 and 95% relative humidity.

Cell proliferation assay

The membrane were cut (5 mm diameters, 0.3 mm thickness), sterilized by UV radiation for 30 min, and placed in a 96-well plate to perfectly cover the bottom. SAOS-2 or hASC were seeded onto the membrane at a density of 2 × 104 cells/well and re-incubated. SAOS-2 and hASC grown in standard conditions (growth medium, GM) were considered as positive control, while Triton 10% v/v was considered as negative control. After 7, 14, and 21 days of contact with the membrane, the medium was removed, and 10% v/v of Alamar blue (AlamarBlue HS cell viability reagent, Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher, Monza, Italy) was diluted with the cell medium and added into the wells. After 3 h of incubation in dark at 37 °C, the Alamar blue was collected and transferred in a new plate. Each well was then refilled with fresh culture medium and left in culture. The fluorescence intensity (FI) was recorded using a microplate reader (FLUOstar® Omega, BMG LABTECH, Aylesbury, UK) with λex =530 nm and λem =590 nm. The fluorescence intensity (FI) was directly related to cell viability.

Cell morphology

The cell morphology after 21 days of contact with the membrane was investigated using CLSM after nuclei and cytoskeleton staining. Cells grown onto the systems were fixed using a 3% (v/v) glutaraldehyde solution in PBS. ECM was stained using bone sialoprotein antibody (Thermofisher, Monza, Italy; 100 μL/sample at 1 μg/mL in PBS) to immuno-label sialoprotein (24 h contact time at 4 °C). The primary antibody was stained with ATTO 488 goat anti rabbit IgG (Sigma Aldrich, Milano, Italy), as secondary antibody (green). Cellular cytoskeleton was stained with TRICT-phalloidin (red, Sigma-Aldrich, Milano, Italy; 50 μL/sample at 50 μg/mL in PBS) for 40 min, in the dark. Cell nuclei were stained with Hoechst 33258 (blue, Sigma-Aldrich, Milano, Italy; 50 μL/sample at 1:10,000 in PBS) for 10 min in the dark. Samples were imaged using a Confocal Laser Scanning Microscope (CLSM, Leica TCS SP8, Leica Microsystems, Buccinasco (MI), Italy) with (a) λex = 346 nm and λem = 460 nm for Hoechst 33258; (b) λex = 540 nm and λem = 565 nm for TRICT phalloidin, and (c) λex = 501 nm and λem = 523 nm for ATTO 488 goat anti rabbit IgG. The acquired images were processed with a software (Leica Microsystem, Buccinasco (MI), Italy). Cells were also imaged using SEM, as previously described in paragraph 2.4.

Antibacterial activity evaluation

T4 membrane antibacterial activity was tested by measuring the microbicidal effect (ME). In particular, Escherichia coli ATCC 10356 and Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 6538 were used as representative strain of Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacteria, respectively. The bacteria were grown overnight at 37 °C in Tryptone Soya Broth (Oxoid, Basingstoke, Hampshire, UK). Bacterial cultures were centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 20 min to separate cells from the broth and then suspended in sterile water. The optical density of the microbial suspensions was adjusted to A = 0.3 (wavelength: 650 nm), which corresponds to a number of cells of: 1 × 107–1 × 108 CFU/mL. To measure the ME of T4, microbial suspensions (10 μL) were placed on glass slides (26 × 76 mm) and covered with the systems (20 × 20 mm). This glass–system was maintained in a humid environment (with 1 mL of PBS in a Falcon test-tube) at room temperature for 5 and 24 h. The same setup was used with the undoped membrane (T3) as a control. After the two contact times, a volume of 9 mL of sterile water was added in each test-tube, gently shaking to obtain the detachment of the system from the slides. Microbial suspensions were then placed in Tryptone Soya Agar (Oxoid; Basingstoke, Hampshire, UK) and bacterial colonies were enumerated after incubation at 37 °C for 24 h (Taglietti et al., 2019). The ME value was calculated for each contact time according to the following equation (Fraise et al., 2004):ME=logNc−logNdwhere Nc is the number of CFU of the control constituted by the microbial suspension alone, and Nd is the number of CFU of the microbial suspension in presence of the systems with TiO2 nanoparticles.

The microbiological endpoint was specifically designed to quantify the reduction in viable bacteria induced by the membrane, in order to provide a direct and immediately interpretable measure of the tested material, independently of the absolute bacterial load of the corresponding control.

The antibacterial activity was also evaluated by SEM analysis, to explore the initial bacterial adhesion and progressive spreading and colonization onto the exposed membrane. Specifically, the samples were gently washed with PBS and fixed with 3% v/v glutaraldehyde solution. Subsequently, samples were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (50%, 75%, and 100% ethanol), dried, and analyzed by SEM. The analysis was performed onto T3 and T4 systems at different time intervals of exposure with the bacteria (5 and 24 h).

In vivo evaluation of systems safety

The animal experiments were compliant with the standard international ethical guidelines (European Communities Council Directive 2010/63/EU). The protocol was approved by the Local Institutional Ethics Committee of the University of Pavia for the use of animals and by Istituto Superiore di Sanità (ISS) (authorization no. 217/2023-PR). Six male Wistar rats (weight: 200–250 g; Envigo RMS S.r.l., Italy) were used for the study. Animals were divided into two experimental groups (n = 3 per treatment) receiving T3 or T4 membranes. Rats were anesthetized with equitensine (3 mL/kg), and the dorsal region was shaved to remove all hair. Subsequently, T3 and T4 membranes (5 mm diameter) were subcutaneously implanted according to the procedure previously described by Bianchi et al. (Bianchi et al., 2023). The subcutaneous implant experiment was intentionally designed as a preliminary local safety and biocompatibility assessment. In particular, the model was used to investigate the local tissue response to the T3 and T4 membranes, before considering their evaluation in more complex orthopedic models. Eighteen days after the treatment, animals were sacrificed according to the approved protocol, and full-thickness biopsies containing the implanted membranes and surrounding tissue were collected for histological evaluation. Biopsies from intact skin and saline-treated sites were collected as controls. No adverse events or abnormal clinical signs were observed during the experimental period.

Histological analysis

As previously described by Bianchi et al. (Bianchi et al., 2023), tissue samples were immediately fixed in neutral buffered formalin (4% v/v) and embedded in paraffin. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and picrosirius red (PSR) staining were performed and the sections were then observed using a light microscope (Carl Zeiss Axiophot) and imaged (Nikon DS-Fi2).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using Astatsa statistical calculator. Data are reported as mean value ± standard deviation (s.d.). The number of independent experimental replicates (n) is indicated in the corresponding figure legends. Technical measurements obtained from the same experimental unit were averaged before statistical analysis and were not considered independent biological replicates. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Scheffé post-hoc testing was performed for comparisons. A p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Fig. 2b shows the ζ of the membranes in 2–10 pH range. This analysis was performed to investigate the effect of HA and HA-TiO₂ coatings on the surface charge properties of the systems. The measurements were performed using KCl concentration at 0.1 mM higher than the generally preferred of 0.01 mM to maximize the surface charge density and allow samples discrimination, despite of the reduction of the electrical double layer, since TPU is not characterized by a net charge density (Chen et al., 2023). HA is a weak polyelectrolyte with carboxylate groups (–COOH) on its glucuronic acid units. These groups have a pKa around ∼3–4, meaning that they start to deprotonate and become negatively charged above pH ≈ 3–4. For this reason, at neutral and higher pH, most carboxyl groups are deprotonated, and HA results strongly negatively charged (Zane et al., 2016). Furthermore, although HAp possesses negatively charged phosphate (PO₄3−) and hydroxyl (OH−) groups that can contribute to the surface charge, the similar ζ potential values observed for T1 and T2 indicate that this contribution is limited, likely because HAp particles are embedded within the TPU matrix and are not fully exposed at the fibers surface. Conversely, the surface coatings applied in T3 and T4 have a more pronounced effect on the ζ potential, as HA and TiO₂ nanoparticles are directly exposed at the surface and can actively contribute to the interfacial charge properties. In fact, T3 and T4 show significantly lower zeta potential values at basic pH, since both HA and TiO2 NPs are characterized by a strongly negatively charged surface at higher pH. This last finding is aligned with the literature that clearly show how TiO2 is characterized by a marked decrease of zeta potential increasing the pH with a IEP ranging from 5 to 6 (Casey-Power et al., 2022).

Mechanical properties evaluation

As regards the mechanical properties (Fig. 3), the presence of HAp and HA-TiO2 coating leads to an increase in the fibrous membrane's maximum force at break (Fmax) and Young's Modulus (YM). This Fmax improvement is probably due to TiO2 high surface area and to the strong hydrogen bonds formed between the HA hydroxyl groups (-OH) and the NPs. In fact, these lead to an enhancement in interfacial adhesion, consequently increasing the stress transfer efficiency within the polymeric network and reducing the polymer chains slippage under mechanical loading (Naeini et al., 2025). Nevertheless, this reinforcing effect was maintained under hydrated conditions, indicating that the inorganic phase and the surface coating could contribute to preserve the mechanical integrity of the membranes. Similarly, the presence of TiO2 leads to an elongation % reduction due to the restriction of the polymeric chains' mobility, additionally resulting in greater crystallinity, stiffness and strength (El-Taweel, 2024; Bendaoued et al., 2022). On the other hand, T3 and T4 elongation % significantly increases after hydration, while T1 and T2 are characterized by constant values. This could be due to the partial loss of the NPs after the hydration of the coating, allowing a greater polymeric chains' mobility. Moreover, the Fmax and YM increase highlights a uniform TiO2 distribution onto the surface. In fact, it has been shown that the NPs agglomeration leads to mechanical properties reduction (Johari et al., 2020). This is in agreement with finding that proved the change in fiber diameters and in structural stiffness of thermoplastic polyurethane fibers doped with HAp and the enhancement of osteoconduction membrane (Mi et al., 2014). Membranes in vitro degradation

Fig. 4a shows the weight loss of the membranes during incubation in PBS at 37 °C, selected to provide an initial assessment of the systems stability under quasi-physiological conditions. It is noticeable that the coated membrane, T3 and T4, are characterized by a strong weight loss of about 50% during the first week and then they reach a plateau, while T1 and T2 are stable and characterized by a low weight loss of about 6% in 6 weeks. The pronounced initial weight loss observed for T3 and T4 is consistent with the presence of the HA-based surface coating and suggests a substantial contribution of the coating to the early mass loss of the systems. The relatively stable mass of T1 and T2 over the investigated period indicates greater stability of the uncoated TPU-HAp matrices under the present experimental conditions (Zhang et al., 2021; Chen et al., 2024). In fact, all the membranes maintain the fibers integrity and dimensions in time (Fig. 4b). Moreover, SEM-EDX (Fig. 4c) confirmed the persistence of Ti, Ca, and P in T4 throughout the degradation period. These findings indicate that the inorganic components were retained within the membrane during the investigated period (Johari et al., 2020). In vitro evaluation

Fig. 5 reports the hASC and SAOS-2 cells adhesion and proliferation capability onto the membranes. As regards hASC, their proliferation results superimposable to the one of the control of cells grown in standard conditions (GM) when cultured onto T2, T3 and T4 membranes. In fact, no statistically significant differences are noticeable (Fig. 5a). On the other hand, an enhancement of SAOS-2 proliferation occurs when HA and TiO2 are present. This enhanced response may reflect the combined influence of surface wettability, topography, interfacial chemistry, and mechanical properties. Moreover, HA is a major component of the native ECM and is known to regulate osteoblast adhesion and proliferation through interactions with cell surface receptors such as CD44 and RHAMM, activating intracellular signalling pathways that promote cell growth and cytoskeletal organization (Cowman and Turley, 2023). Furthermore, HA enhances wettability and nutrient diffusion, supporting cell spreading and metabolic activity. In parallel, TiO₂ NPs introduce nanoscale surface roughness and increase surface energy, which enhances integrin-mediated cell adhesion. Antibacterial activity evaluation

Metal oxide nanoparticles are widely used as key elements for the control of bacterial growth, leading to survival and biofilm formation inhibition. This is due to the fact that bacteria could develop a resistance against the most used antibiotics and chemotherapeutics, representing a huge threat to human health. More importantly, there is a great risk that surgical procedures could expose the patients to multidrug-resistant bacteria. However, inorganics, especially when in nanosize and μg/mL concentrations, are generally effective to prevent bacterial colonization on the surfaces and, consequently, infections (El-Taweel, 2024; Liu et al., 2009). Table 2 reports the ME (log10 reduction in bacterial count) evaluated for T3 and T4 membranes at 5 and 24 h of contact with Escherichia coli and Staphylococcus aureus, used as representative strains of Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacteria. T4 possesses a slight ME against both E. coli and S. aureus, since the membranes decrease the bioburden after 24 h of contact with the bacteria. Moreover, the SEM analysis of the membranes in contact with the E. coli (Fig. 6a) and S. aureus (Fig. 6b) shows clusters of bacteria onto T3 membranes after 5 h, while T4 is clear, with no sign of bacterial adhesion after 24 h. These results are in line with the ME evaluation, confirming that the nanofibrous membrane doped with TiO2 could represent an effective tool to reduce bacterial recovery and apparent adhesion, and colonization on the surface. The antibacterial effect observed may be consistent with mechanisms previously proposed for TiO₂ nanoparticles, including the capability to penetrate the bacterial cell membrane, especially of Gram-negative, which possess a thinner and easily crossable peptidoglycans-based membrane (Bianchi et al., 2022; Kim et al., 2019; Erdem et al., 2015). In litera TiO2 NPs coatings led to a reduction of E. coli, P. aeruginosa and S. aureus survival and biofilm disruption in dark assays (Kiwi et al., 2014; Wang et al., 2016). These effects have been attributed in previous studies to multiple non-photocatalytic mechanisms of action identified for TiO₂. In particular, previous studies have demonstrated membrane disruption, increased membrane permeability, ionic imbalance, metabolic impairment and structural cell damage by combining microscopy, ionic flux measurements, proteomic/transcriptomic analyses and electrochemical approaches (Erdem et al., 2015; Sohm et al., 2015; Zhukova, 2015). The reported main route in literature seems related to membrane disruption and ionic leakage. In vivo evaluation of systems safety

Although TiO₂ is widely investigated for antibacterial and biomedical applications, its incorporation into a biomaterial also requires consideration of the potential consequences of NPS exposure. In fact, previous studies reported that titanium particles were detected in tissues surrounding implants, and experimental studies have associated titanium-derived particulate materials with cellular and inflammatory responses and, in some settings, changes in the local microbial environment (Kotsakis and Ganesan, 2025). These effects are strongly dependent on particle size, concentration, surface characteristics, aggregation state, exposure duration, and biological environment (Kotsakis and Ganesan, 2025; Yao et al., 2017; Asa'ad et al., 2022). Therefore, the incorporation of TiO₂ into a biomaterial needs to be accompanied by evaluation of its local tissue tolerance. In the present study, the preliminary in vivo experiment was designed to assess local tissue response to the complete membrane. The in vivo safety of T3 and T4 was investigated in a murine subcutaneous implant model, taking biopsies of intact skin (Fig. 7a) and of the area corresponding to the implant (Fig. 7b) as control. After 18 days of treatment, completely regenerated tissue is visible in every sample. More importantly, the implants area is hardly recognizable from the adjacent skin, showing orderly arranged collagen fibers with no inflammation, leukocyte recruitment or foreign body response. Conclusions

In this study, a multifunctional electrospun membrane based on TPU and HAp, coated with HA and functionalized with TiO₂ NPs, was successfully developed as a potential solution for lining implant to induce osteointegration in infection-prone environments. The loading of HAp into the TPU matrix preserved the fibrous architecture while enhancing osteoconductive features, whereas the HA-TiO₂ coating significantly improved surface wettability and roughness, creating a favorable microenvironment for cell adhesion. The homogeneous distribution of TiO₂ NPs on the membrane surface contributed not only to enhanced mechanical performance, but also to effective antibacterial activity against Escherichia coli and Staphylococcus aureus, reducing bacterial adhesion and survival without compromising cytocompatibility. The weight loss test performed for 6 weeks suggested a slow degradation rate that should guarantee structural support during the osteointegration. Furthermore, in vitro biological evaluations demonstrated that the HA-TiO₂-functionalized membranes supported the adhesion, spreading, and proliferation of both human adipose-derived stem cells and osteoblast-like cells, promoting ECM production. Finally, in vivo subcutaneous implantat provided preliminary evidence of local biocompatibility and membranes' safety, showing no signs of inflammation or foreign body reaction.

Although further investigations in more complex in vivo models and against a broader range of clinically relevant bacterial strains will be required to further elucidate the long-term regenerative and anti-infective potential of these membranes, the present results support the initial hypothesis that the combination of HAp doping within the TPU matrix and a bioactive HA-TiO₂ surface coating can generate a multifunctional membrane capable of promoting osteointegration-related responses while reducing bacterial colonization.
