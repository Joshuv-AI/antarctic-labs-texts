( H , H 2 ) (H,H^{2}) -smoothing effect of Navier-Stokes equations with additive white noise on two-dimensional torus


Abstract
. This paper is devoted to the regularity of Navier-Stokes (NS) equations with additive white noise on two-dimensional torus 𝕋 2 \mathbb{T}^{2} . Under the conditions that the external force f ⁡ ( x ) f(x) belongs to the phase space H H and the noise intensity function h ⁡ ( x ) h(x) satisfies ‖ ∇ h ‖ L ∞ ≤ π ​ ν ​ λ 1 \|\nabla h\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq\sqrt{\pi}\nu\lambda_{1} , where ν \nu is the kinematic viscosity of the fluid and λ 1 \lambda_{1} is the first eigenvalue of the Stokes operator, it was proved that the random NS equations possess a tempered ( H , H 2 ) (H,H^{2}) -random attractor whose (box-counting) fractal dimension in H 2 H^{2} is finite. This was achieved by establishing, first, an H 2 H^{2} bounded absorbing set and, second, an ( H , H 2 ) (H,H^{2}) -smoothing effect of the system which lifts the compactness and finite-dimensionality of the attractor in H H to that in H 2 H^{2} . Since the force f f belongs only to H H , the H 2 H^{2} -regularity of solutions as well as the H 2 H^{2} -bounded absorbing set was constructed by an indirect approach of estimating the H 2 H^{2} -distance between the solution of the random NS equations and that of the corresponding deterministic equations.

Introduction

In this paper we study the asymptotic dynamics of the following stochastic two-dimensional Navier-Stokes (NS) equations on
\mathbb{T}^{2}=[0,L]^{2}

(1.1)

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} &{\rm{d}}u_{h}+\left(-\nu\Delta u_{h}+(u_{h}\cdot\nabla)u_{h}+\nabla p\right){\rm{d}}t=f(x)\,{\rm{d}}t+h(x)\,{\rm{d}}W,\\
&\nabla\cdot u_{h}=0,\\
&u_{h}(0)=u_{h,0},\end{aligned}\right.

endowed with periodic boundary conditions, where

When

(1.2)

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} &\frac{{\rm{d}}u}{{\rm{d}}t}-\nu\Delta u+(u\cdot\nabla)u+\nabla p=f(x),\\
&\nabla\cdot u=0,\\
&u(0)=u_{0}.\end{aligned}\right.

Main results. Main techniques

The main results of the paper are now introduced.
To begin with, following Crauel & Flandoli
[
10
]
we reformulated the stochastic NS equation
(
1.1
)
via a scalar Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process as the following random equation

(1.3)

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm{d}}v}{{\rm{d}}t}+\nu Av+B\big(v(t)+hz(\theta_{t}\omega)\big)=f(x)-\nu Ahz(\theta_{t}\omega)+hz(\theta_{t}\omega),

where
A=-P\Delta

B(u)=P((u\cdot\nabla)u)
with
P:\mathbb{L}_{per}^{2}\to H
the Helmholtz-Leray orthogonal projection, and

is a tempered random variable. This random equation is known as a conjugate system to
(
1.1
)
and generates a random dynamical system (RDS)
\phi
in
H
, see
Subsection 3.3
for more detail.
The intensity function of the noise
h(x)
is supposed to satisfy the following assumption.

Assumption 1
.

Let
h\in H^{3}
, and

\|\nabla h\|_{L^{\infty}}<{\sqrt{\pi}}\nu\lambda_{1},

where
\lambda_{1}=4\pi^{2}/L^{2}
is the first eigenvalue of the Stokes operator
A
.

In
Theorem 4.4
we constructed an
H^{2}
-random absorbing set as the following.

Main Theorem 1

(
H^{2}
random absorbing set)
.

Let
f\in H
and
1
hold. Then the RDS
\phi
has a random absorbing set
\mathfrak{B}_{H^{2}}
given as a random
H^{2}
neighborhood of the global attractor
\mathcal{A}_{0}
of the deterministic equation
(
1.2
)
:

\displaystyle\mathfrak{B}_{H^{2}}(\omega)=\left\{v\in H^{2}:\,{\rm dist}_{H^{2}}(v,\mathcal{A}_{0})\leq\sqrt{\rho(\omega)}\,\right\},\hskip 8.50012pt\omega\in\Omega,

where
\rho(\cdot)
is a tempered random variable.
As a consequence, the random attractor
\mathcal{A}
of
\phi
is a bounded and tempered random set in
H^{2}
.

This result generalizes
1.1
to random cases, while a major technical difficulty arises here that the standard techniques used for the deterministic NS equations in obtaining the
H^{2}
regularity do not apply to the random equation
(
1.3
)
. More precisely,
1.1
was achieved by estimating the time-derivative
u_{t}
of solutions, see
[
31
]
. This, however, cannot be done for random equation
(
1.3
)
since the Winner process is not derivable in time. On the other hand, a more direct method of estimating the
H^{2}
-norm of
v
by multiplying both sides of equation
(
1.3
)
by
A^{2}v
is not applicable either since
f
belongs only to
H
.

In order to overcome this difficulty we employed a comparison approach, adapted from an idea of Sun
[
35
]
used in a study of the regularity of deterministic wave equations. We
estimated the difference
w:=v-u
between the solutions of the random and the deterministic NS equations instead of estimating the solution
v
itself, where
u
is taken as a solution corresponding to an initial value lying in the global attractor
\mathcal{A}_{0}
.
By proving that
w
is asymptotically bounded in
H^{2}
(see
4.3
) it follows the desired
H^{2}
random absorbing set of
v
since
\cup_{t\geq 0}u(t)\subset\mathcal{A}_{0}
has been bounded in
H^{2}
(see
1.1
).

After the
H^{2}
regularity (
H^{2}
boundedness) of the random attractor
\mathcal{A}
has been established we were interested in its compactness in
H^{2}
. In
Theorem 6.1
we proved that the random attractor
\mathcal{A}

is not only compact but also finite-dimensional in
H^{2}
, and the attraction happens in
H^{2}
as well. More precisely,

Main Theorem 2

(
(H,H^{2})
-random attractor)
.

Let
f\in H
and
1
hold. Then the random attractor
\mathcal{A}
of the random NS equation is an
(H,H^{2})
-random attractor of finite fractal dimension in
H^{2}
.

This theorem follows from standard bi-spatial random attractor theory
[
14
]
, where a key step in proving an attractor to be
(H,H^{2})
is to prove the
(H,H^{2})
-asymptotic compactness of the system. Nevertheless, instead of the required
(H,H^{2})
-asymptotic compactness of the system, we here proved a more technical
(H,H^{2})
-smoothing property. This smoothing property is essentially a local
(H,H^{2})
-Lipschitz continuity in initial values, and has been shown powerful in estimating the fractal dimension of attractors and constructing exponential attractors, see for instance Cholewa et al
.

[
7
]
, most recently Carvalho et al
.

[
6
]
and references therein. In addition, once the finite-dimensionality of the attractor in
H
has been known (as in our case), from the
(H,H^{2})
-smoothing property it would follow immediately the
finite-dimensionality in
H^{2}
.
Therefore, in
Theorem 5.6
we derived an
(H,H^{2})
-smoothing property as stated below.

Main Theorem 3

(
(H,H^{2})
-smoothing effect)
.

Let
f\in H
and
1
hold.
Then for any tempered set
\mathfrak{D}
in
H
there
exist random variables
T_{{\mathfrak{D}}}(\cdot)
and
L_{\mathfrak{D}}(\cdot)
such that any two solutions
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
of random NS equations
(
1.3
)
corresponding to initial values
v_{1,0},

v_{2,0}
in
\mathfrak{D}\left(\theta_{-T_{\mathfrak{D}}(\omega)}\omega\right)
, respectively, satisfy

\displaystyle\left\|v_{1}\!\left(T_{\mathfrak{D}}(\omega),\theta_{-T_{\mathfrak{D}}(\omega)}\omega,v_{1,0}\right)-v_{2}\!\left(T_{\mathfrak{D}}(\omega),\theta_{-T_{\mathfrak{D}}(\omega)}\omega,v_{2,0}\right)\right\|^{2}_{H^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq L_{\mathfrak{D}}(\omega)\|v_{1,0}-v_{2,0}\|^{2}_{H},\hskip 8.50012pt\omega\in\Omega.

Note that the proof of this theorem is technical and the periodic boundary condition greatly facilitated the analysis. We believe that the main idea of this paper applies to smooth bounded domains or some unbounded domains under proper boundary conditions, while more technical calculations and analysis would be expected.
Since multiplicative noises would lead to a special structure of the noise being coupled, our idea of the comparison approach seems not applicable for multiplicative noises. Hence, the
H^{2}
random absorbing sets as well as the
H^{2}
random attractor of NS equations with multiplicative noises remain open.

.

Preliminaries:
(X,Y)
-random attractors and their fractal dimension

In this section we introduce the basic theory of
(X,Y)
-random attractors and their fractal dimension needed in this paper. The readers are referred to
[
1
,
10
,
14
,
22
]
and references therein.

Let
(X,\|\cdot\|_{X})
be a Banach space and
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
be a probability space with a group
\{\theta_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
of measure-preserving self-transformations on
\Omega
.
Denote by
\mathcal{B}(\cdot)
the Borel
\sigma
-algebra of a metric space, and let

\mathbb{R}^{+}:=[0,\infty)
.

Definition 2.1
.

A mapping
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\Omega\times X\rightarrow X
is called a
random dynamical system (RDS)
in
X
, if

(i)

\phi
is
(\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{+})\times\mathcal{F}\times\mathcal{B}(X),\mathcal{B}(X))
-measurable;

(ii)

for every
\omega\in\Omega
,
\phi(0,\omega,\cdot)
is the identity on
X
;

(iii)

\phi
satisfies the following cocycle property

\displaystyle\phi(t+s,\omega,x)=\phi(t,\theta_{s}\omega,\phi(s,\omega,x)),\hskip 8.50012pt\forall t,s\in\mathbb{R}^{+},~\omega\in\Omega,~x\in X;

(iv)

the mapping
x\mapsto\phi(t,\omega,x)
is continuous.

A set-valued mapping
\mathfrak{D}
:
\Omega\mapsto 2^{X}\setminus\emptyset
,
\omega\mapsto\mathfrak{D}(\omega)
, is called a
random set
in
X
if it is
measurable
in the sense that the mapping
\omega\to{\rm dist}_{X}(x,\mathfrak{D}(\omega))
is
(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}))
-measurable for each
x\in X
. If each its image
\mathfrak{D}(\omega)
is closed (or bounded, compact, etc.) in
X
, then
\mathfrak{D}
is called a closed (or bounded, compact, etc.) random set in
X
.

A random variable
\zeta:\Omega\mapsto X
is called
tempered
if

\lim_{t\to\infty}e^{-\varepsilon t}|\zeta(\theta_{-t}\omega)|=0,\hskip 8.50012pt\varepsilon>0.

Note that if
\zeta
is a tempered random variable, then so is
|\zeta|^{k}
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
A random set
\mathfrak{D}
in
X
is called
tempered
if there exists a tempered random variable
\zeta
such that
\|\mathfrak{D}(\omega)\|_{X}:=\sup_{x\in\mathfrak{D}(\omega)}\|x\|\leq\zeta(\omega)
for all
\omega\in\Omega
.

Denote by
\mathcal{D}_{X}
the collection of all the tempered random sets in
X
.

Definition 2.2
.

A random set
\mathfrak{B}
in
X
is called a
(random) absorbing set
of an RDS
\phi
if it pullback absorbs every tempered random set in
X
, i.e., if for any
\mathfrak{D}\in\mathcal{D}_{X}
there exists a random variable
T_{\mathfrak{D}}(\cdot)
such that

\displaystyle\phi(t,\theta_{-t}\omega,\mathfrak{D}(\theta_{-t}\omega))\subset\mathfrak{B}(\omega),\hskip 8.50012ptt\geq T_{\mathfrak{D}}(\omega),\ \omega\in\Omega.

Definition 2.3
.

A random set
\mathcal{A}
is called the
random attractor
of an RDS
\phi
in
X
if

(i)

\mathcal{A}
is a tempered and compact random set in
X
;

(ii)

\mathcal{A}
is invariant under
\phi
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(\theta_{t}\omega)=\phi(t,\omega,\mathcal{A}(\omega)),\hskip 8.50012ptt\geq 0,~\omega\in\Omega;

(iii)

\mathcal{A}
pullback attracts every tempered set in
X
, i.e., for any
\mathfrak{D}\in\mathcal{D}_{X}
,

\lim\limits_{t\rightarrow\infty}{\rm dist}_{X}\big(\phi(t,\theta_{-t}\omega,\mathfrak{D}(\theta_{-t}\omega)),\,\mathcal{A}(\omega)\big)=0,\hskip 8.50012pt\omega\in\Omega,

where

{\rm dist}_{X}(\cdot,\cdot)
denotes the Hausdorff semi-metric between subsets of
X
, i.e.,

{\rm dist}_{X}(A,B)=\sup_{a\in A}\inf_{b\in B}\|a-b\|_{X},\hskip 8.50012pt\forall A,B\subset X.

Let
Y
be a Banach space with continuous embedding
Y\hookrightarrow X
.

Definition 2.4
.

A random set
\mathcal{A}
is called the
(X,Y)
-random attractor
of an RDS
\phi
in
X
, if

(i)

\mathcal{A}
is a tempered and compact random set in
Y
;

(ii)

\mathcal{A}
is invariant under
\phi
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(\theta_{t}\omega)=\phi(t,\omega,\mathcal{A}(\omega)),\hskip 8.50012ptt\geq 0,~\omega\in\Omega;

(iii)

\mathcal{A}
pullback attracts every tempered set in
X
in the metric of
Y
, i.e., for any
\mathfrak{D}\in\mathcal{D}_{X}
,

\lim\limits_{t\rightarrow\infty}{\rm dist}_{Y}\big(\phi(t,\theta_{-t}\omega,\mathfrak{D}(\theta_{-t}\omega)),\,\mathcal{A}(\omega)\big)=0,\hskip 8.50012pt\omega\in\Omega.

Note that in addition to the temperedness, compactness and pullback attraction in
Y
, the measurability in
Y
of an
(X,Y)
-random attractor was also required by definition.

The following criterion is an adaption of
[
14
, Theorem 19]
.

Lemma 2.5
.

(
[
14
, Theorem 19]
)
Let
\phi
be an RDS. Suppose that

(i)

\phi
has a random absorbing set
\mathfrak{B}
which is a tempered and closed random set in
Y
;

(ii)

\phi
is
(X,Y)
-asymptotically compact, that is, for any
\mathfrak{D}\in\mathcal{D}_{X}
,
x_{n}\in\mathfrak{D}(\theta_{-t_{n}}\omega)

and
t_{n}\to\infty
, the sequence

\{\phi(t_{n},\theta_{-t_{n}}\omega,x_{n})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
has a convergent subsequence in
Y
.

Then
\phi
has a unique
(X,Y)
-random attractor
\mathcal{A}
given by

\mathcal{A}(\omega)=\bigcap_{s\geq 0}\overline{\bigcup_{t\geq s}\phi(t,\theta_{-t}\omega,\mathfrak{B}(\theta_{-t}\omega))}^{Y},\hskip 8.50012pt\omega\in\Omega.

Suppose that
E
is a precompact set in
X
, and let
N(E,\varepsilon)
denote the minimum number of balls of radius
\varepsilon
in
X
required to cover
E
. Then the
(box-counting) fractal dimension of
E
is defined as

d_{f}^{X}(E)=\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{\log N(E,\varepsilon)}{-\log\varepsilon},

where the superscript “
\ {}^{X}
” indicates the space to which the fractal dimension is referred.

The following lemma is a random adaption of a well-known property of fractal dimension under Hölder continuous projections, see, e.g., Robinson
[
32
]
.

Lemma 2.6
.

(
[
15
, Lemma 5]
)
Let
\phi
be an RDS in
X
with a random attractor
\mathcal{A}
of finite fractal dimension. If
\phi
has a tempered random absorbing set
\mathfrak{B}
which is bounded in
Y
and there are random variables
T_{\omega}
and
L(\omega)
such that the following
(X,Y)
-smoothing property on
\mathfrak{B}

\displaystyle\|\phi(T_{\omega},\theta_{-T_{\omega}}\omega,x_{1})-\phi(T_{\omega},\theta_{-T_{\omega}}\omega,x_{2})\|_{Y}\leq L(\omega)\|x_{1}-x_{2}\|_{X}^{\delta},\hskip 8.50012ptx_{1},x_{2}\in\mathfrak{B}(\theta_{-T_{\omega}}\omega),

holds for some
\delta>0
for all
\omega\in\Omega
,
then
\mathcal{A}
has finite fractal dimension in
Y
:

d_{f}^{Y}\big(\mathcal{A}(\omega)\big)\leq\frac{1}{\delta}\,d_{f}^{X}\big(\mathcal{A}(\theta_{-T_{\omega}}\omega)\big),\hskip 8.50012pt\omega\in\Omega.

Notet that in the case of
\{\theta_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
being ergodic and the RDS
\phi
being Lipschitz in initial values, the fractal dimension of the random attractor is
\mathbb{P}
-a.s
.
constant, see Langa & Robinson
[
22
]
.

3.
The Navier-Stokes equations and the attractors in
H

3.1.
Functional spaces

In order to study the periodic boundary conditions
we first
introduce Sobolev spaces of periodic functions. Such functions can be represented as Fourier series, which makes their analysis significantly more straightforward than for the spaces on bounded domains. The following settings are standard, the readers are referred to, e.g.,
[
31
,
36
,
37
]
. etc.

Denote by
(C_{per}^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))^{2}
the space of two-component infinitely differentiable functions that are
L
-periodic in each direction, and by
\mathbb{L}_{per}^{2}
,
\mathbb{H}_{per}^{1}
and
\mathbb{H}_{per}^{2}
the completion of
(C_{per}^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))^{2}
with respect to the
(L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))^{2}
,
(H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))^{2}
and
(H^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))^{2}
norm, respectively.

The phase space
(H,\|\cdot\|)
is a subspace of
\mathbb{L}_{per}^{2}
of functions with zero mean and free divergence, i.e.,

\displaystyle H=\left\{u\in\mathbb{L}_{per}^{2}:\,\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}u\ {\rm{d}}x=0,\ {\rm div}\,u=0\right\},

endowed with the norm of
\mathbb{L}_{per}^{2}
, i.e.,

\|\cdot\|=\|\cdot\|_{\mathbb{L}_{per}^{2}}
. For
p>2
, we write
(L^{p}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))^{2}
simply as
L^{p}
and its norm as
\|\cdot\|_{L^{p}}
.

Note that any
u\in\mathbb{L}_{per}^{2}
has an expression
u=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\hat{u}_{j}e^{{\rm i}j\cdot x}
, where
{\rm i}=\sqrt{-1}
and
\hat{u}_{j}
are Fourier coefficients. Thus,
\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}u\,{\rm{d}}x=0
is equivalent to
\hat{u}_{0}=0
. Therefore, any
u\in H
is in form

u=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\setminus\{0\}}\hat{u}_{j}e^{{\rm i}j\cdot x}.

Let
P:\mathbb{L}_{per}^{2}\rightarrow H
be the Helmholtz-Leray orthogonal projection operator. In this periodic space, we define the stokes operator
Au=-P\Delta u=-\Delta u
for all
u\in D(A)
. In this bounded domain, the projector
P
does not commute with derivatives. Then the operator
A^{-1}
is a self-adjoint positive-definite compact operator from
H
to
H
.

For
s>0
, the Sobolev space
H^{s}:=D(A^{s/2})
is defined in a standard way by

H^{s}=\left\{u\in H:\|u\|^{2}_{H^{s}}=\sum\limits_{j\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\backslash\{0\}}|j|^{2s}|\hat{u}_{j}|^{2}<\infty\right\},

endowed with the norm
\|\cdot\|_{H^{s}}=\|A^{s/2}\cdot\|
.

For
u,v\in H^{1}
, we define the bilinear form

B(u,v)=P((u\cdot\nabla)v),

and, in particular,
B(u):=B(u,u)
.

The following Poincaré’s inequality is standard, see, e.g.,
[
31
, lemma 5.40]
.

Lemma 3.1

(Poincaré’s inequality)
.

If
u\in H^{1}
, then

(3.1)

\|u\|\leq\left(\frac{L}{2\pi}\right)\|u\|_{H^{1}}.

Thus, with

\lambda_{1}:=\frac{4\pi^{2}}{L^{2}},

we have
\|u\|_{H^{1}}^{2}\geq\lambda_{1}\|u\|^{2}
. For later purpose we denote by
\lambda:=\alpha\nu\lambda_{1}/4
.

We let the noise intensity
h
satisfy
1
, i.e.,
h\in H^{3}
and

(3.2)

\displaystyle\|\nabla h\|_{L^{\infty}}<{\sqrt{\pi}}\nu\lambda_{1}.

(In particular,
\|\nabla h\|_{L^{\infty}}<\sqrt{\pi}\nu
for the scale
L=2\pi
).
Clearly, the tolerance of the noise intensity increases as the kinematic viscosity
\nu
increases.

3.2.
Global attractor of the deterministic Navier-Stokes equation

We now recall the global attractor of the deterministic NS equations
(
1.2
)
, i.e. for
h(x)\equiv 0
, which will be crucial for our analysis later. Under projection
P
, the equation is rewritten as

(3.3)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u+\nu Au+B(u)=f,\hskip 8.50012ptu(0)=u_{0}.

This equation generates an autonomous dynamical system
S
in phase space
H
with a global attractor
\mathcal{A}_{0}
. In addition, the following regularity result of the attractor
\mathcal{A}_{0}
seems optimal for
f\in H
in the literature.

Lemma 3.2
.

(
[
31
, Proposition 12.4]
)
For
f\in H
the deterministic NS equations
(
3.3
)
generates a semigroup
S
which possesses a global attractor
\mathcal{A}_{0}
in
H
and has an
absorbing set which is bounded in
H^{2}
. In particular, the global attractor
\mathcal{A}_{0}
is bounded in
H^{2}
.

We will prove that the global attractor
\mathcal{A}
is not only bounded in
H^{2}
, but also has finite fractal dimension (and thus compact) in
H^{2}
.

Remark 3.3
.

As
f
belongs only to
H
, one cannot obtain an energy equation of
\|Au\|
directly and thus
3.2
was proved by estimating the time-derivatives
\partial_{t}u
of solutions rather than estimating the solutions
u
themselves. However, this method does not apply to the random NS equation
(
3.5
)
since the Wiener process is not derivable in time. Hence, in
Section 4
we will employ a comparison approach to obtain
H^{2}
random absorbing sets for
(
3.5
)
.

3.3.
Generation of an RDS

We now transform the stochastic Navier-Stokes equation
(
1.1
)
to a random NS equation which generates an RDS
\phi
on
H
.
Following Crauel, Defussche & Flandoli
[
9
]
, let

z(\theta_{t}\omega)=-\int_{-\infty}^{0}e^{\tau}(\theta_{t}\omega)(\tau)\,{\rm{d}}\tau,\hskip 8.50012pt\forall t\in\mathbb{R},\ \omega\in\Omega.

Then
z(\theta_{t}\omega)
is the one-dimensional Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process which solves the equation

{\rm{d}}z(\theta_{t}\omega)+z(\theta_{t}\omega)\,{\rm{d}}t={\rm{d}}\omega.

Moreover, there exists a
\theta_{t}
-invariant subset
\tilde{\Omega}\subset\Omega
of full measure such that
z(\theta_{t}\omega)
is continuous in
t
for every
\omega\in\tilde{\Omega}
and the random variable
|z(\cdot)|
is tempered, namely, for each
\varepsilon>0
it holds

\lim_{t\to\infty}e^{-\varepsilon t}|z(\theta_{-t}\omega)|=0,\hskip 8.50012pt\forall\omega\in\Omega.

In fact, it satisfies

\lim_{t\to\pm\infty}\frac{|z(\theta_{t}\omega)|}{|t|}=0,\hskip 8.50012pt\lim_{t\to\pm\infty}\frac{1}{t}\int^{t}_{0}z(\theta_{s}\omega)\,{\rm{d}}s=0.

Moreover, by the ergodicity of the parametric system, see, e.g.,
[
1
]
or
[
40
]
, we have

(3.4)

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{t\rightarrow\pm\infty}\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}|z(\theta_{s}\omega)|^{m}\,{\rm{d}}s=\mathbb{E}\big(|z(\theta_{t}\omega)|^{m}\big)=\frac{\Gamma(\frac{1+m}{2})}{\sqrt{\pi}},\hskip 8.50012pt\omega\in\tilde{\Omega},

for all
m\geq 1
, where
\Gamma
is the Gamma function. Hereafter, we will not distinguish
\tilde{\Omega}
and
\Omega
.

We introduce the following transformation

u_{h}(t)=v(t)+hz(\theta_{t}\omega),\hskip 8.50012ptt>0,\ \omega\in\Omega.

Under projection
P
, the system
(
1.1
)
of
u_{h}
is then transformed to the abstract evolution equations

(3.5)

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} &\frac{{\rm{d}}v}{{\rm{d}}t}+\nu Av+B\big(v(t)+hz(\theta_{t}\omega)\big)=f(x)-\nu Ahz(\theta_{t}\omega)+hz(\theta_{t}\omega),\\
&v(0)=u_{h}(0)-hz(\omega),\end{aligned}\right.

of
v
in
H
.

By virtue of the standard Galerkin method, we have the well-posedness of weak solutions of
(
3.5
)
as stated below.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
f\in H
and
1
hold. Then for each
v(0)\in H
and
\omega\in\Omega
, there exists a unique weak solution

\displaystyle v(t)\in C_{loc}([0,\infty);H)\cap L^{2}_{loc}(0,\infty;H^{1})

satisfying
(
3.5
)
in distribution sense with
v|_{t=0}=v(0)
. In addition, this solution is continuous in initial values in
H
.

Therefore, by

\phi(t,\omega,v_{0})=v(t,\omega,v_{0}),\hskip 8.50012pt\forall t\geq 0,\,\omega\in\Omega,\,v_{0}\in H,

where
v
is the solution of
(
3.5
)
,
we associated an RDS
\phi
in
H
to
(
3.5
)
.

3.4.
The random attractor in
H

We now construct the random attractor
\mathcal{A}
in
H
for the RDS
\phi
. Though the construction here is essentially standard, the detail will be given since we are under a new condition
(
3.2
)
in
1
and, in addition, the bounds obtained here will be crucial later in constructing
H^{2}
random absorbing sets and the local
(H,H^{2})
-Lipschitz continuity of
\phi
.

We first make some uniform estimates on solutions of
(
3.5
)
corresponding to initial values
v(0)
in
H
, with careful use of the condition
(
3.2
)
in
1
. Throughout this paper, we let
C
denote a positive constant whose value may vary from line to line.

We will frequently make use of the following two fundamental lemmas, see, e.g.,
[
36
,
37
]
.

Lemma 3.5

(Gronwall’s lemma)
.

Let
x(t)
be a function from
\mathbb{R}
to
\mathbb{R}^{+}
such that

\dot{x}+a(t)x\leq b(t).

Then for all
t\geq s
,

x(t)\leq e^{-\int^{t}_{s}a(\tau)\,{\rm{d}}\tau}x(s)+\int_{s}^{t}e^{-\int^{t}_{\eta}a(\tau)\,{\rm{d}}\tau}b(\eta)\,{\rm{d}}\eta.

Lemma 3.6

(Gagliardo-Nirenberg inequality)
.

If
u\in L^{q}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
,
D^{m}u\in L^{r}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
,
1\leq q,r\leq\infty
, then there exists a constant
C
such that

\|D^{j}u\|_{L^{p}}\leq C\|D^{m}u\|_{L^{r}}^{a}\|u\|^{1-a}_{L^{q}},

where

\frac{1}{p}-\frac{j}{2}=a\left(\frac{1}{r}-\frac{m}{2}\right)+\frac{1-a}{q},\hskip 8.50012pt1\leq p\leq\infty,~0\leq j\leq m,~\frac{j}{m}\leq a\leq 1,

and

C
depends only on
\{m,\,j,\,a,\,q,\,r\}
.

Lemma 3.7

(
H
bound)
.

Let
f\in H
and
1
hold.
Then there are random variables
T(\omega)
and
\zeta_{1}(\omega)
, where
\zeta_{1}
is tempered, such that any
solution
v
of the system
(
3.5
)
corresponding to initial values
v(0)\in H
satisfies

\displaystyle\|v(t,\theta_{-t}\omega,v(0))\|^{2}\leq e^{-\lambda t}\|v(0)\|^{2}+\zeta_{1}(\omega),\hskip 8.50012ptt\geq T_{1}(\omega).

Proof.

Note that since, by
(
3.2
)
,
{\|\nabla h\|_{L^{\infty}}}/{\sqrt{\pi}}<\nu\lambda_{1}
, there exist
\alpha\in(0,1]
and
\beta>0
such that

(3.6)

\displaystyle\frac{\|\nabla h\|_{L^{\infty}}}{\sqrt{\pi}}=(1-\alpha)\nu\lambda_{1},

(3.7)

\displaystyle\frac{\|\nabla h\|_{L^{\infty}}}{\sqrt{\pi}}\left(1+\beta\right)=\nu\lambda_{1}-\frac{1}{2}\alpha\nu\lambda_{1}.

Then multiplying the equation
(
3.5
)
by
v
and integrating over
\mathbb{T}^{2}
, by integration by parts we have

\frac{1}{2}\frac{{\rm{d}}}{{\rm{d}}t}\|v\|^{2}+\nu\|A^{\frac{1}{2}}v\|^{2}=-(B(v+hz(\theta_{t}\omega)),v)+\big(f-\nu Ahz(\theta_{t}\omega)+hz(\theta_{t}\omega),v\big),

so by Poincaré’s inequality
(
3.1
)
,

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm{d}}}{{\rm{d}}t}\|v\|^{2}+\left(2-\frac{\alpha}{2}\right)\nu\lambda_{1}\|v\|^{2}+\frac{\alpha\nu}{2}\|A^{\frac{1}{2}}v\|^{2}

\displaystyle\hskip 8.50012pt\leq\frac{{\rm{d}}}{{\rm{d}}t}\|v\|^{2}+2\nu\|A^{\frac{1}{2}}v\|^{2}

(3.8)

\displaystyle\hskip 8.50012pt\leq 2\big|\big(B(v+hz(\theta_{t}\omega)),v\big)\big|+2\big|\big(f-\nu Ahz(\theta_{t}\omega)+hz(\theta_{t}\omega),v\big)\big|.

Applying Hölder’s, Young’s and Poincaré’s inequalities, we obtain

\displaystyle 2\big|\big(B(v+hz(\theta_{t}\omega)),v\big)\big|

\displaystyle\leq 2\big|\big(B(v,hz(\theta_{t}\omega)),v\big)\big|+2\big|\big(B(hz(\theta_{t}\omega),hz(\theta_{t}\omega)),v\big)\big|

\displaystyle\leq 2|z(\theta_{t}\omega)|\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}|v|^{2}|\nabla h|\ {\rm{d}}x+2|z(\theta_{t}\omega)|^{2}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}|v||h||\nabla h|\ {\rm{d}}x

\displaystyle\leq 2|z(\theta_{t}\omega)|\|\nabla h\|_{L^{\infty}}\|v\|^{2}+2|z(\theta_{t}\omega)|^{2}\|\nabla h\|_{L^{\infty}}\|v\|\|h\|

\displaystyle\leq 2|z(\theta_{t}\omega)|\|\nabla h\|_{L^{\infty}}\|v\|^{2}+\frac{\alpha\nu\lambda_{1}}{8}\|v\|^{2}+C|z(\theta_{t}\omega)|^{4}\|\nabla h\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}\|h\|^{2},

and

\displaystyle 2\big|\big(f-\nu Ahz(\theta_{t}\omega)+hz(\theta_{t}\omega),v\big)\big|\leq\frac{\alpha\nu\lambda_{1}}{8}\|v\|^{2}+C\|f\|^{2}+C|z(\theta_{t}\omega)|^{2}\left(\|Ah\|^{2}+\|h\|^{2}\right).

Substituting these inequalities into
(
3.8
)
yields

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm{d}}}{{\rm{d}}t}\|v\|^{2}+\left(2-\frac{3\alpha}{4}\right)\nu\lambda_{1}\|v\|^{2}+\frac{\alpha\nu}{2}\|A^{\frac{1}{2}}v\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2|z(\theta_{t}\omega)|\|\nabla h\|_{L^{\infty}}\|v\|^{2}+C|z(\theta_{t}\omega)|^{4}\|\nabla h\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}\|h\|^{2}

\displaystyle+C\|f\|^{2}+C|z(\theta_{t}\omega)|^{2}\left(\|h\|^{2}+\|Ah\|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2|z(\theta_{t}\omega)|\|\nabla h\|_{L^{\infty}}\|v\|^{2}+C\left(1+|z(\theta_{t}\omega)|^{4}\right).

Therefore,

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm{d}}}{{\rm{d}}t}\|v\|^{2}+\left[\left(2-\frac{3\alpha}{4}\right)\nu\lambda_{1}-2\|\nabla h\|_{L^{\infty}}|z(\theta_{t}\omega)|\right]\|v\|^{2}+\frac{\alpha\nu}{2}\|A^{\frac{1}{2}}v\|^{2}\leq C\left(1+|z(\theta_{t}\omega)|^{4}\right).

Applying Gronwall’s lemma, we obtain

\displaystyle\|v(t)\|^{2}+\frac{\alpha\nu}{2}\int_{0}^{t}e^{\left(2-\frac{3\alpha}{4}\right)\nu\lambda_{1}(s-t)+2\|\nabla h\|_{L^{\infty}}\int_{s}^{t}|z(\theta_{\tau}\omega)|\,{\rm{d}}\tau}\|A^{\frac{1}{2}}v(s)\|^{2}\,{\rm{d}}s

\displaystyle\hskip 8.50012pt\leq e^{-\left(2-\frac{3\alpha}{4}\right)\nu\lambda_{1}t+2\|\nabla h\|_{L^{\infty}}\int_{0}^{t}|z(\theta_{\tau}\omega)|\,{\rm{d}}\tau}\|v(0)\|^{2}

\displaystyle\hskip 17.00024pt+C\int_{0}^{t}e^{\left(2-\frac{3\alpha}{4}\right)\nu\lambda_{1}(s-t)+2\|\nabla h\|_{L^{\infty}}\int_{s}^{t}|z(\theta_{\tau}\omega)|\,{\rm{d}}\tau}\left(1+|z(\theta_{s}\omega)|^{4}\right){\rm{d}}s,

and then

\displaystyle\|v(t)\|^{2}+\frac{\alpha\nu}{2}\int_{0}^{t}e^{\left(2-\frac{3\alpha}{4}\right)\nu\lambda_{1}(s-t)}\|A^{\frac{1}{2}}v(s)\|^{2}\,{\rm{d}}s

\displaystyle\hskip 8.50012pt\leq e^{-\left(2-\frac{3\alpha}{4}\right)\nu\lambda_{1}t+2\|\nabla h\|_{L^{\infty}}\int_{0}^{t}|z(\theta_{\tau}\omega)|\,{\rm{d}}\tau}\|v(0)\|^{2}

(3.9)

\displaystyle\hskip 17.00024pt+C\int_{0}^{t}e^{\left(2-\frac{3\alpha}{4}\right)\nu\lambda_{1}(s-t)+2\|\nabla h\|_{L^{\infty}}\int_{s}^{t}|z(\theta_{\tau}\omega)|\,{\rm{d}}\tau}\left(1+|z(\theta_{s}\omega)|^{4}\right){\rm{d}}s.

Note that by the ergodicity
(
3.4
)
for
m=1
, we have

\lim\limits_{t\rightarrow\pm\infty}\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}|z(\theta_{s}\omega)|\,{\rm{d}}s=\mathbb{E}(|z(\theta_{t}\omega)|)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}},\hskip 8.50012pt\omega\in\Omega,

so there exists a random variable
T_{1}(\omega)\geq 1
such that, for all
t\geq T_{1}(\omega)
,

\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}|z(\theta_{s}\omega)|\,{\rm{d}}s\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}+\frac{\beta}{\sqrt{\pi}},

and then

\displaystyle 2\|\nabla h\|_{L^{\infty}}\int_{0}^{t}|z(\theta_{s}\omega)|\,{\rm{d}}s

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\|\nabla h\|_{L^{\infty}}}{\sqrt{\pi}}\left(1+\beta\right)t

(3.10)

\displaystyle=\left(2\nu\lambda_{1}-\alpha\nu\lambda_{1}\right)t\hskip 8.50012pt\text{(by {\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\eqref{c2}})}.

Hence, by
(
3.9
)
and
(
3.10
)
,

\displaystyle\|v(t)\|^{2}+\frac{\alpha\nu}{2}\int_{0}^{t}e^{\left(2-\frac{3\alpha}{4}\right)\nu\lambda_{1}(s-t)}\|A^{\frac{1}{2}}v(s)\|^{2}\,{\rm{d}}s

\displaystyle\hskip 8.50012pt\leq e^{-\frac{\alpha}{4}\nu\lambda_{1}t}\|v(0)\|^{2}+C\int_{0}^{t}e^{\left(2-\frac{3\alpha}{4}\right)\nu\lambda_{1}(s-t)+2\|\nabla h\|_{L^{\infty}}\int_{s}^{t}|z(\theta_{\tau}\omega)|\,{\rm{d}}\tau}\left(1+|z(\theta_{s}\omega)|^{4}\right){\rm{d}}s.

With
\lambda:=\alpha\nu\lambda_{1}/4,

this estimate is then rewritten as

\displaystyle\|v(t)\|^{2}+\frac{\alpha\nu}{2}\int_{0}^{t}e^{\left(\frac{8}{\alpha}-3\right)\lambda(s-t)}\|A^{\frac{1}{2}}v(s)\|^{2}\,{\rm{d}}s

\displaystyle\hskip 8.50012pt\leq e^{-\lambda t}\|v(0)\|^{2}+C\int_{0}^{t}e^{\left(\frac{8}{\alpha}-3\right)\lambda(s-t)+2\|\nabla h\|_{L^{\infty}}\int_{s}^{t}|z(\theta_{\tau}\omega)|\,{\rm{d}}\tau}\left(1+|z(\theta_{s}\omega)|^{4}\right){\rm{d}}s.

Replacing
\omega
with
\theta_{-t}\omega
yields

\displaystyle\|v(t,\theta_{-t}\omega,v(0))\|^{2}+\frac{\alpha\nu}{2}\int_{0}^{t}e^{\left(\frac{8}{\alpha}-3\right)\lambda(s-t)}\|A^{\frac{1}{2}}v(s,\theta_{-t}\omega,v(0))\|^{2}\,{\rm{d}}s

\displaystyle\hskip 8.50012pt\leq e^{-\lambda t}\|v(0)\|^{2}+C\int^{0}_{-t}e^{\left(\frac{8}{\alpha}-3\right)\lambda s+2\|\nabla h\|_{L^{\infty}}\int_{s}^{0}|z(\theta_{\tau}\omega)|\,{\rm{d}}\tau}\left(1+|z(\theta_{s}\omega)|^{4}\right){\rm{d}}s.

Define a random variable by

\displaystyle\zeta_{1}(\omega):=C\int_{-\infty}^{0}e^{\left(\frac{8}{\alpha}-3\right)\lambda s+2\|\nabla h\|_{L^{\infty}}\int_{s}^{0}|z(\theta_{\tau}\omega)|\,{\rm{d}}\tau}\left(1+|z(\theta_{s}\omega)|^{4}\right){\rm{d}}s,\hskip 8.50012pt\omega\in\Omega.

Then
\zeta_{1}(\cdot)
is a tempered random variable such that
\zeta_{1}(\omega)\geq 1
and

\displaystyle\|v(t,\theta_{-t}\omega,v(0))\|^{2}+\frac{\alpha\nu}{2}\int_{0}^{t}e^{\left(\frac{8}{\alpha}-3\right)\lambda(s-t)}\|A^{\frac{1}{2}}v(s,\theta_{-t}\omega,v(0))\|^{2}\,{\rm{d}}s

(3.11)

\displaystyle\hskip 8.50012pt\leq e^{-\lambda t}\|v(0)\|^{2}+\zeta_{1}(\omega),\hskip 8.50012ptt\geq T_{1}(\omega).

The lemma follows.
∎

Lemma 3.8

(
H^{1}
bound)
.

Let
f\in H
and
1
hold.
Then for any bounded set
B
in
H
there is a random variable
T_{B}(\omega)>T_{1}(\omega)
such that, for all
t\geq T_{B}(\omega)
,

\sup_{v(0)\in B}\left(\big\|A^{\frac{1}{2}}v(t,\theta_{-t}\omega,v(0))\big\|^{2}+\int_{t-\frac{1}{2}}^{t}\|Av(s,\theta_{-t}\omega,v(0))\|^{2}\ {\rm{d}}s\right)\leq\zeta_{2}(\omega),

where
\zeta_{2}(\omega)
is a tempered random variable given by
(
3.16
)
such that
\zeta_{2}(\omega)>\zeta_{1}(\omega)\geq 1
,
\omega\in\Omega
.

Proof.

Multiplying
(
3.5
)
by
Av
and integrating over
\mathbb{T}^{2}
, by integration by parts we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{{\rm{d}}}{{\rm{d}}t}\|A^{\frac{1}{2}}v\|^{2}+\nu\|Av\|^{2}

\displaystyle=-\big(B(v+hz(\theta_{t}\omega),v+hz(\theta_{t}\omega)),\,Av\big)

(3.12)

\displaystyle\hskip 8.50012pt\ +\big(f-\nu Ahz(\theta_{t}\omega)+hz(\theta_{t}\omega),Av\big).

Applying the Hölder inequality, the Gagliardo-Nirenberg inequality, Poincaré’s inequality and the Young inequality, we obtain

\displaystyle\big|\big(B(v+hz(\theta_{t}\omega),v+hz(\theta_{t}\omega)),\,Av\big)\big|=\big|\big(B(v+hz(\theta_{t}\omega),v+hz(\theta_{t}\omega)),Ahz(\theta_{t}\omega)\big)\big|

\displaystyle\hskip 8.50012pt\leq\|Ahz(\theta_{t}\omega)\|\|v+hz(\theta_{t}\omega)\|_{L^{4}}\|A^{\frac{1}{2}}(v+hz(\theta_{t}\omega))\|_{L^{4}}

\displaystyle\hskip 8.50012pt\leq C\|Ahz(\theta_{t}\omega)\|\|v+hz(\theta_{t}\omega)\|^{\frac{1}{2}}\|A^{\frac{1}{2}}(v+hz(\theta_{t}\omega))\|\|A(v+hz(\theta_{t}\omega))\|^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\hskip 8.50012pt\leq C\|Ahz(\theta_{t}\omega)\|\|A^{\frac{1}{2}}(v+hz(\theta_{t}\omega))\|^{\frac{3}{2}}\|A(v+hz(\theta_{t}\omega))\|^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\hskip 8.50012pt\leq C\|Ahz(\theta_{t}\omega)\|\left(\|A^{\frac{1}{2}}v\|^{\frac{3}{2}}+\|A^{\frac{1}{2}}hz(\theta_{t}\omega)\|^{\frac{3}{2}}\right)\left(\|Av\|^{\frac{1}{2}}+\|Ahz(\theta_{t}\omega)\|^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)

(3.13)

\displaystyle\hskip 8.50012pt\leq\frac{\nu}{4}\|Av\|^{2}+C\left(1+\|Ah\|^{2}|z(\theta_{t}\omega)|^{2}\right)\|A^{\frac{1}{2}}v\|^{2}+C\left(1+|z(\theta_{t}\omega)|^{6}\right),

and

(3.14)

\displaystyle\big(f-\nu Ahz(\theta_{t}\omega)+hz(\theta_{t}\omega),Av\big)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\nu}{4}\|Av\|^{2}+C\|f\|^{2}+C|z(\theta_{t}\omega)|^{2}.

Substituting
(
3.4
)
and
(
3.14
)
into
(
3.4
)
we have

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm{d}}}{{\rm{d}}t}\|A^{\frac{1}{2}}v\|^{2}+\nu\|Av\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\left(1+\|Ah\|^{2}|z(\theta_{t}\omega)|^{2}\right)\|A^{\frac{1}{2}}v\|^{2}+C\left(1+|z(\theta_{t}\omega)|^{6}+\|f\|^{2}\right)

(3.15)

\displaystyle\leq C\left(1+|z(\theta_{t}\omega)|^{2}\right)\|A^{\frac{1}{2}}v\|^{2}+C\left(1+|z(\theta_{t}\omega)|^{6}\right).

Applying Gronwall’s lemma on
(\eta,t)
with
\eta\in(t-1,t)
,
t>1
, we have

\displaystyle\|A^{\frac{1}{2}}v(t)\|^{2}+\nu\int_{\eta}^{t}e^{\int_{s}^{t}C(1+|z(\theta_{\tau}\omega)|^{2})\,{\rm{d}}\tau}\|Av(s)\|^{2}\,{\rm{d}}s

\displaystyle\leq e^{\int_{\eta}^{t}C(1+|z(\theta_{\tau}\omega)|^{2})\,{\rm{d}}\tau}\|A^{\frac{1}{2}}v(\eta)\|^{2}+C\int_{\eta}^{t}e^{\int_{s}^{t}C(1+|z(\theta_{\tau}\omega)|^{2})\,{\rm{d}}\tau}\left(1+|z(\theta_{s}\omega)|^{6}\right){\rm{d}}s,

and then integrating over
\eta\in(t-1,t-\frac{1}{2})
