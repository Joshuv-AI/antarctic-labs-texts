The impact of dexmedetomidine on postoperative delirium in obstructive sleep apnea patients (DPOD-OSA): study protocol for a prospective randomized controlled trial
Authors: Yan Li, Ran Zhang, Na Li, Xi-ying Wang, Li Zheng, Mao-wei Xing, Yi Zou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRecent studies suggest that patients at intermediate-high risk of obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) are at greater risk of developing postoperative delirium (POD). Background

Postoperative delirium (POD) is a common complication associated with increased mortality, prolonged length of stay (LOS), substantial healthcare costs, and decreased quality of life [1]. Several studies identified obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) as a substantial risk factor for POD [2–4]. Recently, OSA is considered to precipitate POD in surgical populations [5]. OSA, which should be viewed as a low-grade chronic inflammatory disease, is a sleep disorder characterized by repeated upper airway obstruction resulting in loss of a normal sleep architecture, intermittent oxygen desaturations and increased nocturnal endogenous stress [6, 7]. Previous studies have demonstrated intravenous dexmedetomidine (DEX) administration was associated with a reduced incidence of POD [8, 9]. DEX is conducive to POD reduction due to its role on property of anti-inflammatory, sleep promotion and inhibiting oxidative stress (which is the main pathophysiology of OSA) [10–12]. DEX is expected to reduce the incidence of POD in patients with OSA theoretically due to its properties of anti-inflammatory, sleep promotion and inhibiting oxidative stress. However, data are minimal to focus on investigating the role of DEX playing in POD in patients at intermediate-high risk of OSA. Therefore, we design this randomized controlled trial (RCT) to investigate the impact of DEX on POD in OSA patients and try to explore an optimal dosage for preventing POD among patients with OSA.

Trail design

This is a single-center, prospective, randomized, controlled, single-blind (patient — blinded, implementers/investigators — not blinded, observers — blinded) trial. This study will be conducted at the Department of Anaesthesiology, Beijing Friendship Hospital, Capital Medical University. It has been approved by the Ethics Committee of Beijing Friendship Hospital, Capital Medical University, Beijing, China (Ethics Committee number is 2025-P2-274-02) and has been registered with Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (Chictr) (registration no. ChiCTR2500108088). Patients who achieve the inclusion criteria will sign the informed consent.

Participants

Based on preoperative assessment of the STOP-Bang questionnaire (SBQ) scores, patients are classified into the two categories: intermediate-high risk of OSA (STOP-Bang 3–8), and low risk of OSA (STOP-Bang 0–2). Patients who are considered as intermediate-high risk for OSA will be suggested to take a portable monitoring device for the diagnosis of OSA. AHI values > 5/h are considered OSA.

Inclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria for participation include patients who are: (1) 18 years and older; (2) scheduled for elective laparoscopic surgery and the estimated surgery duration is between 2 and 5 h; (3) the American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) Physical Statuses I – III; (4) the ability to understand and complete the research questionnaire; (5) volunteer to participate in this research and sign the informed consent; (6) no history of neurocognitive dysfunction; (7) completion of a preoperative evaluation (the preoperative evaluations include medical history, >2 risk factors being positive using the SBQ, Mini-mental State Examination (MMSE), Athens Insomnia Scale (AIS); 8) AHI values > 5/h.

Exclusion criteria

Exclusion criteria for participation include patients who are: (1) schizophrenia or active psychosis; (2) history of drug or alcohol dependence or abuse in the past 3 months; (3) dementia or obvious nervous system diseases (such as stroke, epilepsy, intracranial tumor, Parkinson’s disease, etc.); (4) heart rate < 50 beats/min, sick sinus syndrome or severe atrioventricular block; (5) a previous diagnosis of dementia or an MMSE score less than 24 were excluded. MMSE scores are adjusted for age and education level [13]; (6) neurosurgery planned.

Randomization and blinding

The patient allocation is placed in opaque-sealed, serially numbered envelopes corresponding with the randomization schedule created by a research analyst who is not involved in the study. Based on computer-generated random numbers, all recruited OSA patients are randomized with a 1:1:1 allocation into three groups: the high dose of DEX treatment group (Group HD), the low dose of DEX treatment group (Group LD) and the control group (Group C). The researcher (A) who is responsible for screening participants and obtaining informed consent is blinded to the group allocation. Patients are blinded to the group allocation. However, it is not possible to provide a “placebo” anesthesia since anesthesiologists need to adjust the timing and dose of dexmedetomidine. Therefore, anesthesiologist (B) who implements anesthesia is unblinded to the assignment of interventions. The researchers (C) who assess the outcomes (including the assessment of POD) are blinded. Statisticians (D) performing data analysis remain blinded. Patients are blinded to the anesthetic agent; however, anesthesiologists and nurses are not because they need to adjust the timing and dose of dexmedetomidine. Emergency unblinding is permitted only for participant safety and requires authorization by the principal investigator; all unblinding events will be documented.

Interventions

Patients in Group HD will receive a loading dose of 0.5 μg/kg IV infusion over 10 min followed by a continuous infusion of 0.2 to 0.7 μg/kg per hour from induction of anesthesia to the end of surgery. Participants in Group LD will receive a continuous infusion of 0.2 to 0.7 μg/kg per hour to about 30 min before the end of operation. Group C will not get DEX during the perioperative period.

To reduce bias, anesthesia and peri-operative clinical treatment are performed in a standardized manner in three groups. The anesthesia is induced with midazolam (0.02–0.04 mg/kg), etomidate (0.2–0.6 mg/kg), sufentanil (0.3–0.6 μg/kg), rocuronium (0.6–1 mg/kg)/cisatracurium (0.15–0.2 mg/kg) and maintained with propofol and remifentanil in all groups. Inhalation anesthetics are not applied during surgeries. We adjust propofol concentration and remifentanil concentration according to mean arterial blood pressure ± 20% and heart rate ± 20% and maintain bispectral index between 40 and 60. Patient-controlled analgesia pump containing sufentanil (100 μg) and flurbiprofen axetil (100 mg) in a total volume of 100 ml of saline is set to deliver a basal infusion of 2 ml/h and bolus doses of 2 ml, with a 15-min lockout period.

Data collection

Data collection of predisposing factors and precipitation factors associated with POD

Data regarding relevant demographic characteristics, coexisting diseases, Charlson Comorbidity Index, preoperative MMSE, laboratory data (hemoglobin, creatinine, GFR, sodium and albumin level), long-term medications, type of surgery (digestive system, urinary system), administered analgesics and sedatives are collected for each patient, duration of anesthesia, blood transfusion, intensive care unit admission. Body Mass Index (BMI) is calculated using weight and height data from preoperative admission evaluation.

The severity of insomnia of patients is assessed with AIS scores before surgery and consecutively 7 days after surgery. It is an eight-item questionnaire that evaluates sleep onset, night and early-morning waking, sleep time, sleep quality, frequency and duration of complaints, distress caused by the experience of insomnia, and interference with daily functioning [14].

Data after the procedure

The control group is not treated with DEX. POD is assessed using the 3-min diagnostic Confusion Assessment Method (3D-CAM) or the Confusion Assessment Method for Intensive Care Unit (CAM-ICU) for 7 consecutive days or until discharge. We use the Richmond Agitation and Sedation Scale (RASS) to assess the level of sedation and drowsiness. The primary outcome will be the occurrence of POD. The secondary outcomes include pain at rest and provoked pain during postoperative day 1–7, length of stay (LOS) and postoperative complications within 30 days.

Study endpoints

Primary outcome

The primary outcome is the incidence of POD. The first step of the assessment of POD is to determine the level of arousal with RASS. Patients in deep sedation or patients who are unarousable to voice cannot be further assessed for POD. The initial assessment for POD is performed when patients are sufficiently aroused (RASS > −4) [15] on the day of surgery [16, 17]. Patients in the intensive Care Unit are evaluated using the CAM-ICU and patients on the ward are assessed with the 3D-CAM, respectively. These assessments are conducted twice daily with a minimum interval of 6 h. All recruited subjects are evaluated for 7 consecutive days or until discharge if the hospital stay is <7 days, by trained research observers. The incidence and duration of POD are both recorded.

Secondary outcomes

The secondary outcomes are as follows: ① Pain at rest and provoked pain during postoperative day 1–7 are assessed using the numeric rating scales from 0 to 10. For analysis, the highest numeric rating scales score recorded is incorporated as our value of interest. Incidence and intensity of postoperative pain are recorded by a small group of expertly trained researchers. ② LOS. The LOS is defined as the number of days between surgery and the day of discharge. ③ Any postoperative complications within 30 days are recorded by telephone follow-up (i.e., hypoxia, reintubation, myocardial infarction/ischemia, renal insufficiency, wound infection, pneumonia, sepsis, deep vein thrombosis). The data collection process will commence with the acquisition of baseline data and continue until the conclusion of the follow-up period (Table 1).

Data monitoring

Recording of basic characteristics, intraoperative medication doses, vital signs, postoperative AIS scores, and other observations that need to be obtained in the ward will be done by the anesthesiologist (C), who is unaware of the subgroups. All raw data are recorded in the case report form (CRF), and missing data are recorded as such. The CRFs are available at Chinese Clinical Trial Registry.

The data monitoring committee (DMC) consists of three anesthesiologists, a statistician, and a scientific researcher. The functions of the DMC are trial protocol development and improvement, case grouping, data analysis and management, and results proofreading. When approximately 50% of participants have completed outcome assessments, the DMC will conduct a blinded interim data review focused on safety events, protocol adherence, data completeness, and recruitment progress. Electronic data will be placed into a password-protected email that will not be shared with the public until the results are released, together with the original data and results, which will be delivered to DMC. To aid in the retention and thorough follow-up of participants, all original records (CRFs, informed consent forms, and related documents) will be preserved and retained for ten years before being destroyed following hospital regulations.

Sample size calculations

The primary endpoint is the incidence of POD. According to previous research, 24% of patients with OSA are expected to experience POD and 9.9% of the treated OSA patients are expected to develop POD [3, 18]. With an 80% power at a 2-sided significance level of P = 0.05, the sample size is 110 for each group. Considering 10% of possible dropouts and the ratio between each group by 1:1:1, we will enroll 123 patients in each group, and a total of 369 cases is required.

Statistical analysis

Continuous demographic variables exhibiting a normal distribution are compared using t-tests, whereas those with a non-normal distribution are analyzed via Wilcoxon rank-sum tests. The difference between two medians and 95% CIs are calculated using the Hodges–Lehmann estimators. Categorical variables are compared with Pearson chi-square tests. Differences between groups are expressed as relative risk and 95% confidential interval (CI). Multiple logistic regression is used to determine the association of DEX application in patients at intermediate-high risk of OSA with the incidence of POD, subsyndromal delirium, intensive Care Unit admission, and non-delirium complications within 30 days. The incidence of POD in the subgroups of patients stratified according to age (<65 years or ≥65 years) is also compared as exploratory analyses. The overall discriminatory ability of the binomial logistic regression model is assessed using Area Under the Curve. Multivariable linear regression is adopted to analyze the influencing factors of maximum postoperative pain at rest and maximum provoked pain after surgery, LOS and hospitalization costs. The effect of DEX application on POD and their potential interactions is further assessed with stratified logistic regression models adjusting for covariates. Covariates evaluated included age, sex, BMI, educational level, history of smoke and alcohol abuse, ASA classification, SBQ scores, maximum postoperative pain at rest and provoked pain, preoperative hemoglobin and albumin concentration, Charlson Comorbidity Index, MMSE, duration of surgery, total volume and baseline medical diagnoses. All data will be collected by a statistician. The pattern of missing data will be evaluated before data analyzing. The primary analysis will use multiple imputation for data assumed to be missing at random. All tests are two-sided, a P-value of <0.05 is considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses are performed with SPSS 26.0 software (IBM SPSS, Inc., Chicago, IL).

Study dropouts

Participation can be stopped if: ① Unexpected reasons leading to the inability to administrate dexmedetomidine (such as new-onset atrioventricular block or arrhythmia during operation, inability to infuse dexmedetomidine as indicated according to randomization), ② withdrawal of consent, ③ conversion of laparoscopic to open surgery, ④ loss of follow-up or refusal of postoperative evaluation, ⑤ re-exploration for postoperative complications such as bleeding, anastomotic fistula, intestinal obstruction, etc. The reasons and circumstances for dropouts will be documented in the CRF.

Safety evaluation

In the process of clinical research, researchers should write the adverse events record form truthfully and in detail, including the clinical features, occurrence time, severity, duration, related treatment, and outcomes of adverse events. When an adverse event occurs, the observing clinician must decide whether to withdraw the participants from the observation or not according to the condition. When the anesthesiologist recorded the adverse event, it would be reported to the relevant department, and a follow-up process will be carried out according to the regulations of the hospital.

Discussion

Given patients enrolled in different studies are accompanied with variable risk factors of POD, the incidence of POD in patients undergoing major surgeries varies greatly ranging from 5 to 15% to as high as 73% in elderly cardiac surgery patients [19, 20]. Increasing evidence links OSA with adverse postoperative complications including neurological, respiratory, cardiac, and infective complications. Recent data indicate that OSA may exert adverse impact on POD [4, 21]. OSA is a sleep disturbance, characterized [3] by repeated episodes of upper airway collapse, leads to disrupted sleep, intermittent hypoxia, daytime sleepiness, and increased arousal threshold from sleep [22]. There is compelling biological plausibility to support an association between OSA and POD. Chronic OSA is likely to induce hypoxia, systemic inflammation, hypercoagulability, and disrupted sleep architecture [23], which are likely the potential precipitating and/or predisposing factors of POD [24]. Therefore, patients with OSA are found strongly associated with POD in various surgical populations [2–4].

OSA is often either underdiagnosed preoperatively or not considered clinically important enough to warrant intervention. 60% of surgical patients with moderate to severe OSA are not recognized or diagnosed preoperatively [25, 26]. Given the substantial underdiagnosis of OSA, the association between OSA and POD may be even stronger than suggested. The high prevalence of undiagnosed OSA requires a reliable, efficient, and easily used screening tool. The SBQ (sensitivity 84–100% and specificity of 37–56%) is a “concise and easy-to-use screening tool for OSA” [27–29]. The SBQ includes 8 items (snoring, daily tiredness or fatigue, observed sleep apnea, high blood pressure, BMI > 35 kg/m2, age > 50 years, neck circumference > 40 cm). Although laboratory polysomnography is the golden-standard test used in the diagnosis of OSA, portable monitoring devices may be efficient, cost-effective alternatives to full-night polysomnography for surgical patients [30].

Preoperative OSA can be considered a “modifiable” preoperative risk factor that depends on the underlying pathophysiological mechanism in the individual patient. A thorough comprehension of the pathophysiology of sleep and its relationship to cognitive decline is crucial for exploring the potential to avert a potentially reversible cause of POD. Currently, no guidelines exist for the perioperative management of surgical patients with OSA and the prevention of postoperative delirium. It is widely recognized that systemic inflammatory mediators, significantly elevated by surgical stress, may contribute to the pathogenesis of POD. The primary treatments for OSA are adenotonsillectomy in children and continuous positive airway pressure therapy in adults [31]. While these interventions effectively resolve the sleep-disordered breathing associated with OSA, they do not consistently mitigate the risk of POD. Although perioperative DEX use seems to benefit surgical patients, its role in preventing POD among patients with OSA remains unclear.

Intraoperative and postoperative DEX administration is reported to significantly reduce the risk of POD. However, the optimal intraoperative dose of DEX remains controversial. Moreover, robust clinical evidence from well-controlled trials regarding the optimal administration timing and dosing schedule of DEX has yet to be established. Therefore, we hypothesize that different DEX dosage would modulate the concentration of stress hormones and the systemic inflammatory response to surgical trauma. The fluctuations in stress hormone and inflammatory mediator levels triggered by surgery are hypothesized to influence the incidence and duration of postoperative delirium. The results of this methodologically rigorous trial are expected to yield not only clinical evidence of DEX’s efficacy in preventing POD among patients with OSA but also to recommend an optimal dosage.

In general, this clinical trial aims at investigating the impact of DEX on OSA patients, exploring the association between severity of AHI and incidence of POD, analyzing the potential impact of different types of sleep apnea (central sleep apnea vs. obstructive sleep apnea) on POD, and determining the optimal intraoperative dosage of DEX for preventing POD.
