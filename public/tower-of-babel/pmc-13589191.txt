Community Incidence of Influenza Virus Infections During Postpandemic Seasons in China, 2022–2025: A Serial Prospective Cohort Study
Authors: Hongjun Zhang, Jun Guo, Shuhui Guan, Xiaoqing Xu, Ming Pan, Jiaxin Chen, Qi Yan, Libo Dong, Weijuan Huang, Hejiang Wei, Yanhui Cheng, Guoqing Chen, Shilin Xu, Shiqian Dai, Youhan Xia, Bo Liu, Fei Wang, Meiyu Chen, Dayan Wang, Jianfang Zhou, Jun Liu, Tim K. Tsang, Jun Han, Cuiling Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundFollowing unprecedented disruption of influenza circulation during COVID‐19, concerns arose about postpandemic immunity debt. Community influenza epidemiological and burden data are scarce in mainland China. We estimated influenza incidence in Yancheng (2022–2025) and evaluated protection conferred by preexisting antibodies.MethodsWe recruited 5‐ to 59‐year‐old participants each November from 2022 to 2024, with follow‐up through May–June the next year. Blood specimens were collected at enrollment and follow‐up. Weekly home visits were conducted to obtain nasopharyngeal swabs and record symptomatic episodes. Logistic regression was used to quantify protective effects of hemagglutination inhibition (HI) antibodies.ResultsAcross the three cohorts, influenza seroincidence rate was 34.5%–43.5% in unvaccinated children and 30.3%–33.0% in unvaccinated adults. For the 2023–2024 and 2024–2025 cohorts, PCR‐confirmed influenza incidence ranged from 7.2% to 9.0% in children and 3.1%–10.2% in adults. The 50% protective preseason HI titers against PCR‐confirmed A(H1N1)pdm09 infection and symptomatic illness were 1:28 and 1:30 using the conventional definition of uninfected participants, compared with 1:16 and 1:17 under the stringent definition requiring neither positive PCR results nor antibody seroconversion during follow‐up. Higher preseason HI titers predicted protection against serologically confirmed A(H1N1)pdm09 and A(H3N2), with 72% and 86% protection at a titer of 1:20 for each subtype, respectively.ConclusionsWe observed substantial influenza incidence in the 5–59 age group across the 2022–2025 winter seasons. A HI titer of 1:20 conferred 50% protection against PCR‐confirmed symptomatic A(H1N1)pdm09 illness using the stringent definition. These findings provide critical evidence to strengthen postpandemic surveillance.

Introduction

Pandemic and seasonal influenza viruses impose a substantial global burden on morbidity and mortality [1, 2]. In developed countries, epidemiological features and disease burden associated with influenza have been increasingly understood [3, 4]. China is a large country spanning diverse climatic zones, resulting in varied seasonal influenza patterns [5, 6]. However, comprehensive data on influenza epidemiology and its public health impact remain limited in mainland China.

A hospital‐based influenza‐like illness (ILI) sentinel surveillance system has been established in mainland China since 2001, with continuous expansion of the surveillance network [5]. Influenza surveillance provides real‐time data for policy decisions, focusing on healthcare‐seeking cases. However, estimating influenza‐associated outpatient disease burden using surveillance data remains challenging due to undefined catchment populations around sentinel hospitals and their potential underrepresentation [7]. Moreover, individuals' anxiety and control measures related to pandemic and epidemics can alter patient healthcare‐seeking behavior and clinician decision‐making, leading to surveillance bias [8].

Since the 1920s, some community‐based cohort studies have been conducted to elucidate disease burden and transmission dynamics of influenza [9, 10]. There are limited data on influenza disease burden at the community level in mainland China. Only a few cross‐sectional serological surveys have been conducted to assess seroprevalence against A(H1N1)pdm09 during the 2009 influenza pandemic [11]. Our team previously conducted a series of community‐based serological cohort studies to estimate incidence of influenza infections in Shanxi province and Ningbo City during the 2018–2019 and 2019–2020 seasons [7, 12].

The COVID‐19 pandemic fundamentally disrupted global influenza circulation patterns, with near‐complete suppression of influenza activity during 2020–2021 due to nonpharmaceutical interventions [13, 14]. As restrictions were lifted, concerns emerged about potential “immunity debt” effects, where reduced exposure during the pandemic could lead to increased susceptibility and altered transmission dynamics in postpandemic seasons [15].

Here, we conducted a comprehensive community‐level cohort study in Yancheng City, during the critical postpandemic period of 2022–2025. We enrolled people aged 5–59 years to estimate the incidence of influenza virus infections and symptomatic illness using virological and serological testing in the wintertime influenza seasons and to determine the protection conferred by various preexisting antibodies against influenza virus in this altered epidemiological landscape.

Methods

Recruitment and Follow‐Up of Participants

The study employed a 3‐year consecutive cohort design, recruiting participants in five townships of Yandu District, Yancheng City, with populations ranging from 40,000 to 97,000, each comprising 10–35 neighborhood communities/villages. Recruitment was conducted in phases: two townships in Year 1, one township in Year 2, and two townships in Year 3. From 2022 to 2024, separate cohorts of participants were recruited annually in November (preseason/baseline survey), and each cohort was followed for approximately 6 months through the winter influenza season until May or June of the following year (postseason survey).

Households with at least one child of 5–17 years were recruited by local general practitioners working in community health centers or village clinics. Eligibility for enrollment required that at least two household members aged 5–59 years consented to complete the 6‐month study procedures. After providing written informed consent/assent during the preseason survey, participants completed a face‐to‐face questionnaire administered by trained staff, followed by blood collection. The questionnaire collected data on demographics, health status, and influenza vaccination history.

Following the preseason survey, staff from Yancheng Center for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) or general practitioners contacted participating households weekly via telephone calls or WeChat messages. When an acute respiratory illness (ARI) was reported by the participants, healthcare workers conducted a home visit within 1–2 days of illness reporting. Acute illness was defined by one or more of the following symptoms: fever (feverishness, chills, or elevated temperature ≥ 37.8°C), cough, sore throat, runny nose, headache, myalgia, or phlegm. One to two additional home visits were conducted every 2–3 days during the illness episode.

During the home visits, healthcare workers collected nasopharyngeal swabs from all enrolled participants regardless of symptoms and completed a questionnaire to identify symptoms and illness onset date. Additionally, healthcare workers documented daily symptoms for each subsequent day during the illness to complete a daily symptom diary. In the 2022–2023 cohort study, ARI surveillance began with a delay and ran from mid‐February 2022 to mid‐May 2023.

During the postseason survey, participants provided blood samples and completed a brief questionnaire to obtain information about influenza vaccination during the preceding winter season.

The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the National Institute for Viral Disease Control and Prevention, Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention.

Laboratory Test

Each nasopharyngeal swab was tested by real‐time reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT‐PCR) at the influenza laboratory of Yancheng CDC, which is a member of the National Influenza Surveillance Laboratory Network. Laboratory technicians followed the testing procedures according to the Chinese National Influenza Surveillance Technical Guidelines.

Paired sera were tested in parallel by hemagglutination inhibition (HI) assay for seasonal influenza A(H1N1) pdm09, A(H3N2), and influenza B/Victoria lineages. Influenza vaccine strains or locally isolated circulating strains were used for serological testing according to weekly reports released by the Chinese National Influenza Center (CNIC) and internal data from CNIC. The H3N2 strains, A/JSFN/318/2022, A/Thailand/8/2022, and A/Croatia/10136RV/2023, were used to test sera from the three consecutive annual cohorts. The A(H1N1)pdm09 strain A/Victoria/2570/2019 was used for the 2022–2023 cohort and A/Victoria/4897/2022 for the 2023–2024 and 2024–2025 cohorts. B/Austria/1359417/2021(Victoria) was used for three cohorts. A/JSFN/318/2022 (H3N2) and A/Thailand/8/2022 (H3N2) were inoculated and passaged in MDCK cell; the other viruses used for serological testing were passaged in embryonated chicken eggs. Influenza B/Victoria viruses were treated with ether prior to use to increase sensitivity for the HI assay.

The sera for the HI assay were treated with receptor‐destroying enzyme (RDE) at 37°C overnight to remove nonspecific inhibitors, and residual RDE was destroyed by heat inactivation at 56°C for 30 min. Standardized turkey or guinea pig red blood cells (RBC) were added to the sera to remove nonspecific agglutinins, and the adsorbed sera without disturbing the packed RBCs were transferred.

In recent decades, circulating A(H3N2) viruses have altered their preference for receptor binding and gained the ability of neuraminidase (NA) to agglutinate RBCs prior to host entry [16]. So our study applied the recommended HAI assay protocols that 20‐nM oseltamivir carboxylate be added to inhibit the NA‐dependent agglutination caused by modern H3N2 viruses when using guinea pig RBCs [17].

Sera were tested in serial doubling dilutions from an initial dilution of 1:10 to an endpoint dilution of 1:1280 by HI assay using standard methods. Antibody titers < 1:10 were imputed as 1:5, and antibody titers ≥ 1:1280 were imputed as 1:1280.

Statistical Analysis

HI antibody titers of paired sera were compared to determine serologic evidence of infections of influenza viruses, indicated by a fourfold or greater increase in antibody titers between preseason and postseason sera. Overall serological incidence was defined as the proportion of participants with serological evidence of infections against ≥ 1 influenza strain, while subtype‐specific serological incidence was defined as the proportion of participants with evidence against each specific strain. Participants with evidence of infection against multiple strains were counted once in the overall incidence but contributed to each relevant subtype‐specific incidence.

An episode of PCR‐confirmed infection with seasonal influenza A(H1N1)pdm09, A(H3N2), or B/Victoria was defined as a positive result of one or more nasopharyngeal swabs collected within two consecutive weeks by RT‐PCR testing. An episode of PCR‐confirmed illness with influenza virus was defined as a PCR‐confirmed influenza infection within 2 weeks since onset of ARI.

We used a Poisson regression model to estimate the incidence rates of influenza infections detected by serology and PCR‐confirmed infections/illness due to influenza in children aged 5–17 years and adults aged 18–59 years, respectively. To account for potential underestimation in seroincidence rate, we used local surveillance data to model weekly influenza activity proxy, incorporating a 14‐day delay for antibody response. Weekly influenza activity proxy was calculated as the product of the following: (1) the proportion of outpatients with ILI and (2) the proportion of ILI cases testing positive for influenza (by type/subtype). The Poisson regression with subtype‐specific surveillance offsets during follow‐up was used to estimate the adjusted incidence rates [18].

To evaluate the protection of homologous HI antibodies, we analyzed the association between preseason antibody levels and influenza infections/illness. We used logistic regression to estimate the association, adjusted for age and the year in which the participants were enrolled. The protection rate at different antibody titers was calculated as the odd ratio (OR) reduction compared with the OR at an HI titer < 1:10. Two types of infection outcomes were defined: participants with PCR‐confirmed infections/illness due to influenza virus and participants with serologically confirmed influenza virus infections. We applied two distinct definitions for uninfected participants. The conventional definition refers to individuals who had no PCR‐confirmed influenza infection during the study period. The stringent definition requires individuals to have neither a positive PCR test nor antibody seroconversion [19].

Categorical variables between groups were compared using the chi‐squared test; Fisher's exact test was adopted when cell counts were sparse. For nonnormally distributed continuous data across multiple groups, the Kruskal–Wallis rank‐sum test was used for overall difference analysis. When the result was significant, Dunn's test was used for multiple comparisons. Statistical analyses were performed using R version 3.3.1 and the packages plotrix (3.7), descr (1.1.3), and Dunn's test (1.3.5).

Results

Characteristics of Participants

In the 2022–2023, 2023–2024, and 2024–2025 cohort, 1131, 383, and 839 participants were enrolled, respectively. A total of 1012 (89%), 352 (95%), and 774 (92%) provided paired sera during the preseason and postseason survey for each cohort, respectively. Between 43% and 45% of participants in the three cohorts were children of 5–17 years old (Table 1).

Notably, the winter epidemics in 2023–2024 and 2024–2025 began earlier, starting in September–October, a significant shift compared to previous years.

Incidence of PCR‐Confirmed Influenza Infections and Illness

Overall, we identified 118 PCR‐confirmed influenza infections during the study period across the three cohorts, including 63 influenza A(H1N1)pdm09, 52 A(H3N2), and 5 B/Victoria. Of those infections, 93 (79%) developed ARI symptoms. The temporal distribution of PCR‐confirmed infections by subtype identified during the study period was similar to the patterns of local influenza virus activity during the same time periods (Figure 1B).

In the 2023–2024 and 2024–2025 cohort, the overall incidence of PCR‐confirmed infections ranged from 7.2% to 9.0% in children (5–17 years) and from 3.1% to 10.2% in adults (18–59 years). In the 2022–2023 cohort, the overall incidence of PCR‐confirmed infections was 5.8% in children and 2.8% in adults during the approximately 3‐month ARI surveillance period from late November 2022 to mid‐May 2023, with significantly higher rates of A(H3N2) infection observed in children relative to adults. The same age‐related pattern was observed for PCR‐confirmed A(H1N1)pdm09 infections in the 2024–2025 cohort (Table 2).

Protection of HI Antibody

Our study showed that the higher preseason HI antibody titers against A(H1N1)pdm09 in participants aged 5–59 years old were associated with a reduced risk of PCR‐confirmed A(H1N1)pdm09 infection and illness (p < 0.05) (Table S2). When we used the conventional definition of uninfected participants as those who did not have PCR‐confirmed A(H1N1)pdm09 infections, preseason HI titers of 1:28 (95% CI, 1:14–1:527) and 1:30 (95% CI, 1:14–1:4265) against A(H1N1)pdm09 were associated with 50% protection against PCR‐confirmed A(H1N1)pdm09 infection and symptomatic illness, respectively. A preseason HI titer of 1:40 against A(H1N1)pdm09 was associated with 57% (95% CI, 27%–75%) and 55% (95% CI, 19%–75%) protection against PCR‐confirmed A(H1N1)pdm09 infection and symptomatic illness, respectively (Figure 2A,B).

The correlation between preseason HI antibody titers and PCR‐confirmed A(H3N2) infection and symptomatic illness did not reach statistical significance but approached the conventional threshold (p = 0.05) when the stringent definition of uninfected participants was used (p = 0.06 for both comparisons) (Table S3).

Furthermore, we found that higher preseason HI antibody titers were associated with reduced risk of A(H1N1)pdm09 and A(H3N2) infections detected by serology among participants aged 5–59 years (Tables S2 and S3). The 50% protection rate was achieved at a HI titer of 1:11 against A(H1N1)pdm09 or a HI titer of 1:8 against A(H3N2); these thresholds were consistent regardless of whether the conventional or stringent definitions of uninfected participants were applied. Meanwhile, HI titers of 1:20 and 1:40 against A(H1N1)pdm09 and A(H3N2) were associated with 72%–85% protection against A(H1N1)pdm09 and 86%–94% protection against A(H3N2) infection detected by serology, respectively (Figures 2C,D and 3C,D). Additionally, preseason HI antibody titers against A(H1N1)pdm09 and A(H3N2) were associated with a reduced risk of homologous infections detected by serology among both unvaccinated children and adults under the conventional definition of uninfected participants. When the stringent definition of uninfected participants was adopted, the association between preseason HI antibody titers and serologically confirmed A(H3N2) infection was significant in unvaccinated children, while the same correlation merely bordered on the α = 0.05 significance threshold in unvaccinated adults (p = 0.055) (Figures S1 and S2).

Discussion

The study represents the first comprehensive assessment of community‐level influenza incidence across three consecutive post‐COVID influenza seasons in mainland China, providing critical insights into influenza burden following the unprecedented disruption of the COVID‐19 pandemic. Our findings reveal substantially elevated influenza burden during specific seasons of the 2022–2025 period, with altered epidemic timing, suggesting that the postpandemic epidemiological landscape reflects significant changes rather than a simple return to pre‐2020 transmission patterns. The high incidence across three consecutive seasons, combined with temporal shifts in epidemic timing, indicates fundamental alterations in influenza transmission and burden consistent with postpandemic immunity gap effects.

Our postpandemic findings reveal elevated seroincidence across the study period: 43.5% and 31.5% in children and adults, respectively, during 2022–2023, 38.5% and 30.3%, respectively, during 2023–2024, and 34.5% and 33.0%, respectively, during 2024–2025. The findings provide compelling evidence for postpandemic immunity gap effects and altered transmission dynamics, compared with previous Chinese cohort studies. Xu et al. conducted a serological cohort study in northern China during the 2018–2019 influenza season, reporting seroincidence rates of 31% in children and 21% in adults [12]. Importantly, for the 2023–2024 and 2024–2025 cohort, serum collection was initiated approximately 1–2 months after the start of the winter epidemics, whereas in the 2022–2023 cohort, it coincided with the overlapping period of summer and winter epidemics. Coupled with the 14‐day delay required for postinfection antibody titer elevation, the cumulative incidence of influenza infections estimated based on a fourfold antibody titer rise may have been substantially underestimated. Using local surveillance data to adjust the potential underestimation, we found that the seroincidence could be underestimated by 7.9% in children and 2.1% in adults during the 2022–2023 cohort and by 9.9% and 10.7%, respectively, during the 2024–2025 cohort. Consequently, the overall seroincidence for all influenza subtypes and adults may exceed 50% in children and 33% in adults during the 2022–2023 cohort and 43% in children and adults during the 2024–2025 cohort. However, our estimates may be conservative for the whole prolonged or early‐onset winter influenza, making the observed elevations even more significant evidence of immunity gap effects. From March 2020 to November 2021, influenza activity in mainland China remained at historically low levels. After that, a small wave of influenza B occurred from December 2021 to March 2022. Crucially, there was an over 2‐year circulation gap for both A(H3N2) and A(H1N1)pdm09 subtypes. As COVID‐19 control measures were gradually lifted, waning population immunity and accumulation of susceptible individuals led to the observed resurgence of influenza activity with consistently elevated or comparable incidence rates despite potential underestimation.

During the study period of the 2022–2023 and 2023–2024 cohort, which coincided with A(H3N2) circulation, seroincidence of A(H3N2) infection was significantly higher in children than in adults across both cohorts. In contrast, no significant age‐related differences in seroincidence of A(H1N1)pdm09 infection were observed during the two waves of A(H1N1)pdm09 epidemics. This differential age pattern may be attributed to the above 2‐year circulation gap in influenza virus transmission and the antigenic drift observed in circulating A(H3N2) strains during this period compared to previous circulating strains. Typically, more pronounced differences in incidence between children and adults were observed when pandemic strains or strains with major antigenic drifts emerged and became prevalent [12, 20, 21]. New HA antigenic variants emerge every 2–5 years for A(H3N2) viruses due to their relatively rapid evolution [22]. However, age‐specific differences in incidence rate of serologically confirmed infection were inconsistent with patterns of PCR‐confirmed infection, possibly due to a narrow optimal window for nasopharyngeal swab collection, variation in uneven motivation among follow‐up personnel, varying participant compliance, and asymptomatic infection proportions across influenza subtypes (Tables S4 and S5). The temporal patterns observed during our study period provide additional evidence of postpandemic epidemiological disruption. The earlier onset of winter epidemics and sustained off‐season circulation patterns observed during our study period aligned with global reports of disrupted influenza seasonality following COVID‐19 restrictions [13, 14]. These temporal shifts suggest that immunity gap effects extended beyond elevated incidence rates to fundamental alterations in viral transmission dynamics and seasonal patterns. Despite the complex and variable influenza patterns observed in Yancheng from 2022 to 2025, some key characteristics of influenza seasonality remained consistent with prior findings: A(H3N2) virus displayed wintertime and summertime epidemics and the summer peak was dominated by A(H3N2), while A(H1N1)pdm09 primarily peaked in winter in spite of low‐level activity in the summer of 2024 [6, 23].

When using the conventional definition of uninfected participants, the HI titers of 1:28 and 1:30 associated with 50% protection against PCR‐confirmed A(H1N1)pdm09 infection, and symptomatic illness were consistent with prior experimental challenge studies and community‐based observational studies that indicated 50% protection at HI titers of approximately 1:20 to 1:40 [24, 25]. When our study employed a more stringent criterion for defining uninfected controls, the 50% protective HI titers derived from the stringent definition were 1:16 and 1:17, respectively, and these values were considerably lower than the corresponding HI titer thresholds identified under the conventional definition.

Unlike previous cohort studies [25, 26, 27], which relied solely on virological testing (PCR) to define infections, our approach incorporated both PCR and serological testing for comprehensive infection classification. Participants were considered infected if they had either PCR‐positive results or serological evidence of infection (seroconversion in paired sera) and uninfected if both tests were negative. This dual‐testing approach enhances diagnostic accuracy, significantly reducing the likelihood of misclassifying individuals with mild or asymptomatic influenza infections as uninfected controls.

For serologically confirmed A(H1N1)pdm09 infection, the titers of 1:11 were associated with 50% protection, with HI titers of 1:40 achieving 85% protection. These findings suggest that higher antibody titer levels are required to effectively reduce viral shedding and symptomatic illness, compared to preventing serologically confirmed infections, with HI titers of 1:40 being sufficient to prevent most asymptomatic A(H1N1)pdm09 infections. Given the low influenza vaccine coverage (1%–3%), preexisting HI antibodies were mainly from natural infection. The consistency between our findings and protective thresholds observed in vaccine studies suggests that HI antibodies, regardless of source (natural infection or vaccination), may confer similar protection at comparable HI antibody levels.

Our study identified an association between HI antibody levels and serologically confirmed A(H3N2) infection, but no protective effect against PCR‐confirmed A(H3N2) infection was observed, which was inconsistent with the previous study [28]. This discrepancy may be attributed to the limited number of PCR‐confirmed A(H3N2) cases, which reduced the statistical power to detect potential correlations. Indeed, the association between preseason HI antibody levels and PCR‐confirmed A(H3N2) infections did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.06) but approached the conventional threshold under the stringent definition of uninfected participants. In future studies, we will expand the sample size to evaluate the HI antibody protective level against PCR‐confirmed A(H3N2) infection. Crucially, it is essential to assess protection of the HI antibody level under the current HI assay protocol that incorporates 20‐nM oseltamivir carboxylate.

Furthermore, our study simultaneously demonstrated that there was correlation between HI antibody titers and protective immunity against both serologically confirmed A(H1N1)pdm09 and A(H3N2) infections in children and adults, with comparable protective titers observed across age groups.

There are several limitations in our study. First, although the midlatitude region where Yancheng is located experiences semi‐annual influenza cycle with peaks in January–February and June–August [29], cohort initiation was delayed by 1–2 months relative to early winter epidemic, and the overlapping summer and autumn–winter peaks in 2022–2023 complicated seasonal pattern estimation, potentially leading to underestimation of peak winter incidence. Second, delayed acute respiratory infection surveillance for the 2022–2023 cohort limited comprehensive comparison between PCR‐confirmed and serological infection incidence for that season. Third, our study sample may lack representativeness because we only recruited households containing at least one child. Furthermore, restricting participants to the 5–59 age group limits our assessment of disease burden in children under 5 years and seniors aged 60 and above. Fourth, variations in participant availability due to school and work commitments may have affected the frequency and timeliness of follow‐ups and sample collection. Fifth, the potential effects of household clustering were not accounted for when evaluating the association between antibody levels and protection. Finally, our stringent serological case definition required both fourfold, or greater rise in antibody titers in paired sera may have resulted in an underestimation of the true incidence of infections detected by serology [30].

In conclusion, our study revealed a substantial incidence of influenza virus infections among population 5–59 years of age in Yancheng, during the first three postpandemic wintertime seasons, with evidence supporting immunity gap effects most pronounced in the immediate postrestriction period. We demonstrated that HAI titers of 1:20 provided approximately 50% protection against PCR‐confirmed influenza A(H1N1)pdm09 infection and symptomatic illness, while reducing serologically confirmed infection by over 70%. These findings provide essential evidence for postpandemic influenza epidemiology and inform enhanced surveillance and vaccination strategies for populations with altered immunity profiles.
