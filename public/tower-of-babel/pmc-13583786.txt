Benefits of childhood vaccines in ecological studies focusing on Germany: a scoping review protocol
Authors: Tristan Nolting, David D Martin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionGeneral statements that vaccinations are among the most important preventive measures in medicine are common in public health guidance. While the efficacy of individual vaccines is well established through randomised controlled trials and observational studies, ecological studies at the population level can provide complementary insights into the relative historical contributions of different childhood vaccinations to reductions in morbidity and mortality. To date, there has been a lack of systematic reviews of such comparative studies, especially with a focus on Germany. The aim of this scoping review is to map the state of research on ecological studies that compare the relative contribution of different childhood vaccinations to reducing morbidity and mortality, with a focus on reported data from Germany in an international comparison. A more precise scientific and public communication about vaccinations strengthens confidence in evidence-based vaccination recommendations.Methods and analysisThis scoping review follows the Joanna Briggs Institute methodology for scoping reviews. Reporting will be in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR). A comprehensive search will be conducted in MEDLINE via PubMed, Embase via Elsevier, Web of Science, Scopus via Elsevier and Google Scholar, supplemented by grey literature sources. Studies will be selected and data charted by at least two independent reviewers using the Population/Concept/Context (PCC) framework. Results will be synthesised descriptively and presented thematically.Ethics and disseminationNo ethical approval is required as this is a secondary analysis of published data. The findings will be disseminated through publication in a peer-reviewed journal and as part of the first author’s doctoral thesis. The protocol is registered on the Open Science Framework (https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/4FH3X).

Introduction

Vaccinations are highly regarded worldwide due to their effectiveness against infectious diseases. The German Robert Koch Institute (RKI) writes on its website: ‘Vaccinations are among the most important and effective preventive measures available in medicine’ (translation by the author).1 The European Commission’s website contains the following statement: ‘Vaccinations are the most important tool for disease prevention and one of the most cost-effective measures in public health’.2 The US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) make a similar statement: ‘Vaccinations are one of the greatest achievements of science and public health. They have contributed to dramatic declines in vaccine-preventable diseases (diseases that vaccines can help prevent or lessen)’.3

In numerous studies dealing with vaccinations, this statement that vaccinations are considered one of the most important measures in medicine due to their effectiveness in reducing morbidity and mortality is presented as a premise or principle at the beginning of the text.4–8 This tenor is also found in German journalistic articles that focus on the topic of vaccinations.9–13

General statements such as ‘vaccinations are one of the most important measures in medicine’ are often made in science, politics and the media without always making it clear whether the principle of vaccination itself or the benefits of all types of different vaccines are meant. This ambiguity has the potential to cause misunderstandings, as sweeping statements about vaccinations could give the impression that all vaccines make a comparable contribution to reducing morbidity and mortality—something that can only be clarified through comparative analyses.

While the efficacy of many individual vaccines is well established through randomised controlled trials (RCTs) and observational studies, the question arises if there are sufficient comparative studies on the different contributions of vaccinations to reducing morbidity and mortality—especially in Germany.

If ecological studies report substantial differences in the population-level impact of different vaccination programmes, this information may contribute to a more differentiated understanding of historical changes in morbidity and mortality. How can this be addressed?

There are several ways in medical science to measure the benefits of vaccinations. In addition to interventional and observational studies, it is possible to evaluate reported data at the population level, often referred to as an ecological or aggregate study. Even though these studies have some limitations (for example, due to the sometimes low validity of reported data or ecological fallacy), they nevertheless serve to a significant extent to generate new hypotheses.

An influential example of the hypothesis-generating potential of ecological studies is The Role of Medicine by the epidemiologist Thomas McKeown. Using historical population-level data from England and Wales, McKeown argued that much of the decline in infectious disease mortality preceded the introduction of many medical interventions and was largely attributable to improvements in living conditions rather than medical care such as vaccines alone.14 Although aspects of his interpretation have since been challenged and refined, the resulting scientific debate illustrates how ecological analyses can generate important epidemiological hypotheses and stimulate further research.15

Ecological studies of population-level notification data sit at one end of the well-known trade-off between internal and external validity: they offer high external validity and are particularly powerful for generating new comparative hypotheses, while having inherent limitations in internal validity. However, the question arises as to how often ecological studies have been conducted to date on the comparative benefits of vaccinations in reducing morbidity and mortality, particularly with regard to reported data from Germany. Ecological studies are defined here as aggregated analyses of population data (eg, reported data) that have examined the influence of vaccinations on morbidity and mortality descriptively or analytically (eg, using interrupted time series analysis).

While there are numerous and very comprehensive historical works on individual vaccinations or specific periods in the history of vaccination, for example, on smallpox vaccination16 or polio vaccination, there is still no comprehensive monograph that systematically compares the relative contribution of the most important vaccinations throughout history from a medical-epidemiological perspective. In Germany, for example, there is only one comprehensive specialist publication on the history of vaccination in the 19th and 20th centuries, but it focuses primarily on political, social and legal aspects.17

An initial search query on PubMed.gov (as of 12 January 2025) using the search terms ‘scoping review’, ‘ecological study’ and ‘vaccine’ yielded 16 hits, but no scoping review was found that searched for ecological studies on the comparative benefits of vaccinations. To our knowledge, no scoping review has systematically mapped comparative ecological studies assessing the relative population-level impact of different childhood vaccination programmes.

Furthermore, it is likely that few comparative ecological studies on the benefits of vaccinations have been conducted to date. A scoping review is, therefore, appropriate to map the current state of this probably limited and heterogeneous body of research, rather than conducting a quantitative evidence synthesis. The exploratory nature of this review focuses on describing what has been published and identifying methodological patterns and research gaps, rather than synthesising effect estimates.

The scoping review is to be conducted as part of a dissertation at Witten/Herdecke University, which also includes conducting an ecological study on the comparative benefits of vaccinations with a focus on Germany.

The aim of the dissertation is not to question or confirm the fundamental benefits of vaccinations, but rather to enable more precise scientific and public communication by taking a differentiated view of their respective contributions. Experiences during the COVID-19 pandemic highlighted the importance of precise and transparent communication about vaccine benefits and limitations, as communication practices may influence public trust.18 19 A precise presentation can strengthen confidence in vaccinology and vaccine recommendations in the long term by making the strengths and limitations of individual vaccinations evidence-based and transparent.20–22

A focus on studies comparing at least two childhood vaccinations is central to the primary aim of this review, which is to map the relative epidemiological contributions of different vaccines. While single-vaccine ecological studies provide valuable information, they do not permit direct comparison of impact across vaccines. By requiring comparative designs, we aim to identify studies that can inform more nuanced public health communication about the differential benefits of individual vaccines. This scoping review will map the breadth and heterogeneity of comparative ecological studies and thereby identify research gaps, particularly for the German context.

Two research questions arise from the above:

Primary: What ecological studies exist that compare the relative benefits of different vaccines in reducing morbidity and mortality, with a focus on reported data from Germany?

Methods and analysis

This scoping review will be reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) to ensure a transparent and reproducible representation of the state of research.23 The protocol development is based on the recommendations of the Joanna Briggs Institute Manual for Evidence Synthesis (2020) and the guidance of the National Institutes of Health.24

The final scoping review manuscript will use the PRISMA-ScR as a reporting guideline to ensure consistency in reporting. The review is registered in advance as a protocol on OSF to promote transparency and document methodological changes.

Inclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria are based on the Population/Concept/Context (PCC framework) and are derived directly from the research questions to define the scope of the review. They will be refined iteratively based on preliminary searches.

Population: population-level (aggregated) data on infectious diseases and vaccine uptake/coverage. Comorbidity data will only be extracted if reported at the population level and solely to describe how included studies addressed potential confounding, rather than to infer individual-level associations.

The eligibility criteria are used for screening. Title/abstract and full-text screening are carried out in two phases:

Pilot process: several rounds (at least 2) with 20–50 records per round; records are selected randomly; participants: all reviewers; cut-off: ≥80% agreement (via Covidence); discrepancies are discussed in team meetings and criteria are adjusted.

The following variables will be extracted from included studies: author(s), year of publication, country/region (whether Germany is included), study design (eg, time-series, repeated cross-sectional), vaccinations compared, infectious diseases/outcomes examined, data sources (eg, notification data, vaccination coverage), analytical methods, main findings regarding relative contributions, reported limitations and any adjustment for confounding factors.

Synthesis and presentation of results

The results will be synthesised descriptively to capture the breadth and heterogeneity of existing comparative ecological studies. Data will be cleaned by the lead author and analysed using Microsoft Excel. A PRISMA-ScR flow diagram will be produced. Results will be presented thematically (eg, by country, vaccinations compared, study design and methodological approaches).

The review will assess whether there is sufficient methodological consistency across included studies (in terms of study design, outcome definitions, measures of vaccination and analytical approaches) to allow for future systematic reviews or quantitative evidence synthesis. If sufficient methodological consistency is identified, the feasibility of future systematic reviews or quantitative evidence synthesis will be discussed. Conversely, should no eligible studies be identified, this finding will be reported as evidence of an important research gap.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were not involved in the design, conduct or reporting of this scoping review protocol, as it is based on secondary published data without direct participant involvement.

Ethics and dissemination

Ethical approval is not required for this scoping review as it involves secondary analysis of already published data. The findings will be disseminated through publication in a peer-reviewed open-access journal and as part of the first author’s doctoral thesis at Witten/Herdecke University. The protocol is registered on the Open Science Framework (https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/4FH3X) to promote transparency.
