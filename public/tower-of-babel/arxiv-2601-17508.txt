Double-Cover-Based Analysis of the Bethe Permanent of Block-Structured Positive Matrices
Binghong Wu, Pascal O. Vontobel

Abstract
We consider the permanent of a square matrix with non-negative entries. A tractable approximation is given by the so-called Bethe permanent that can be efficiently computed by running the sum-product algorithm on a suitable factor graph. While the ratio of the permanent of a matrix to its Bethe permanent is, in the worst case, upper and lower bounded by expressions that are exponentially far apart in the matrix size, in practice it is observed for many ensembles of matrices of interest that this ratio is strongly concentrated around some value that depends only on the matrix size. In this paper, for an ensemble of block-structured matrices where entries in a block take the same value, we numerically study the ratio of the permanent of a matrix to its Bethe permanent. It is observed that also for this ensemble the ratio is strongly concentrated around some value depending only on a few key parameters of the ensemble. We use graph-cover-based approaches to explain the reasons for this behavior and to quantify the observed value.

II
Background:

NFGs, Double Covers, and the Cycle Index

II-A

Normal factor graphs and partition sums

A factor graph
\mathsf{N}
can be used the depict a multivariate function that is the product of (simpler) multivariate functions. The former function is called the global function and the latter functions are called local functions. The corresponding factor graph consists of function nodes and variable nodes, where for each local function one draws a function node, for every variable one draws a variable node, and for every variable appearing as an argument of a local function one draws an edge between the corresponding variable and function nodes. A valid configuration is an assignment of values to the variable nodes such that the global function takes on a nonzero
value.

The partition sum
Z(\mathsf{N})
is defined to be the sum of the global function over all possible variable assignments, or, equivalently, the sum of the global function over all valid configurations. With suitable local functions, partition sums encode many combinatorial quantities of interest.

In the present paper, we use normal factor graphs (NFGs) in the sense of
[
2
,
3
]
, where the factorization is formulated such that all variable nodes have degree two or one, and because of this the variable nodes are then usually omitted in drawings, i.e., there are no variable nodes in drawings, and variables are simply associated with edges or half-edges.

Our analysis builds upon the double-cover NFG framework for the permanent
[
5
,
15
,
16
,
14
,
17
]
. We adopt the formulation from these papers, restating here only the key definitions and propositions required for our derivation. (We refer to Appendix
A

III
Positive Block-Constant Model

We now specialize to positive block-constant matrices with fixed row and column partitions and set up the multivariate coefficient-extraction framework used in the subsequent analysis.

Assumption 1
.

Let
n
and
m
be positive integers. Fix positive integer vectors
\bm{k},\bm{\ell}\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}^{m}
such that
\sum_{i\in[m]}k_{i}\!=\!\sum_{j\in[m]}\ell_{j}\!=\!n
and fix the matrix
\mathbf{B}\triangleq(b_{i,j})\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{m\times m}

\mathbf{A}\triangleq\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{B},\bm{k},\bm{\ell})\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{n\times n}

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}\triangleq\begin{pmatrix}b_{1,1}\mathbf{1}_{k_{1}\times\ell_{1}}&\cdots&b_{1,m}\mathbf{1}_{k_{1}\times\ell_{m}}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
b_{m,1}\mathbf{1}_{k_{m}\times\ell_{1}}&\cdots&b_{m,m}\mathbf{1}_{k_{m}\times\ell_{m}}\end{pmatrix},

(19)

where

To capture the combinatorics of closed walks on
\hat{\mathsf{N}}(\mathbf{A})

\bm{t}=(t_{1},\dots,t_{m})
and
\bm{u}=(u_{1},\dots,u_{m})

Definition 2
.

The fundamental matrix governing the walk transitions is the

\displaystyle\mathbf{W}

\displaystyle\triangleq\mathbf{B}^{\mathsf{T}}\cdot\operatorname{diag}(\bm{t})\cdot\mathbf{B}\cdot\operatorname{diag}(\bm{u}).

The total weight of length-

\displaystyle\mathbf{S}

\displaystyle\triangleq\operatorname{diag}(\bm{u})^{1/2}\cdot\mathbf{W}\cdot\operatorname{diag}(\bm{u})^{-1/2}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{diag}(\bm{u})^{1/2}\cdot\mathbf{B}^{\mathsf{T}}\cdot\operatorname{diag}(\bm{t})\cdot\mathbf{B}\cdot\operatorname{diag}(\bm{u})^{1/2}.

Conclusion and Outlook

The results in this paper significant extend the classes of matrices for which the ratio

To understand the difference between

In the specific NFG

In contrast, similar to the construction of

The weighted transition matrix

Proof of Proposition

This section provides the derivation of the asymptotic partition sums using analytic combinatorics in several variables (ACSV)
[
18
]
.

First, recall the relevant setup and definitions. The generating functions for the partition sums, derived in Lemma

\displaystyle C^{\mathrm{G}}(\bm{z})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\det(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{S}(\bm{z}))},

(
24

\displaystyle C^{\mathrm{B}}(\bm{z})

\displaystyle=\frac{\exp\bigl(\tfrac{1}{2}\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{S}(\bm{z}))\bigr)}{\sqrt{\det(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{S}(\bm{z}))}}.

(
25

Here,
\bm{z}\triangleq(\bm{t},\bm{u})
denotes the collection of
2m
indeterminates.

As established in Lemma

given by

\displaystyle Z_{n}^{\mathrm{G}}(\bm{k},\bm{\ell})=[\bm{z}^{\bm{r}}]\,C^{\mathrm{G}}(\bm{z}),

(38)

\displaystyle Z_{n}^{\mathrm{B}}(\bm{k},\bm{\ell})=[\bm{z}^{\bm{r}}]\,C^{\mathrm{B}}(\bm{z}).

(39)

To treat both cases within a unified framework, we adopt the standard ACSV form
F(\bm{z})=P(\bm{z})Q(\bm{z})^{-\alpha}

\mathbf{S}(\bm{z})

\lambda_{1}(\bm{z})\geq\lambda_{2}(\bm{z})\geq\dots\geq\lambda_{m}(\bm{z})

When
\bm{z}

\lambda_{1}(\bm{z})

\displaystyle Q(\bm{z})\triangleq 1-\lambda_{1}(\bm{z}).

(40)

We effectively isolate this singularity by decomposing the determinant as

\displaystyle\det(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{S}(\bm{z}))=Q(\bm{z})\prod_{i=2}^{m}(1-\lambda_{i}(\bm{z})).

(41)

The remaining factors constitute the analytic part

. Specifically, we identify:

•

Gibbs case (
\alpha\!=\!1
): The analytic part is

\displaystyle P_{\mathrm{G}}(\bm{z})\triangleq\prod_{i=2}^{m}\bigl(1-\lambda_{i}(\bm{z})\bigr)^{-1}.

(42)

•

Bethe case (
\alpha\!=\!1/2

\displaystyle P_{\mathrm{B}}(\bm{z})\triangleq\mathrm{e}^{\lambda_{1}(\bm{z})/2}\prod_{i=2}^{m}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\lambda_{i}(\bm{z})/2}}{\bigl(1-\lambda_{i}(\bm{z})\bigr)^{1/2}}.

(43)

With the functions

,
Q

For
\bm{z}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{2m}

\mathbf{S}(\bm{z})

\nabla Q(\bm{z})=-\nabla\lambda_{1}(\bm{z})\neq\bm{0}

[
18

is smooth at any point in

where

Since the generating functions have non-negative coefficients, the multivariate Vivanti–Pringsheim theorem
[

implies that the boundary of the domain of convergence meets the positive real orthant at a minimal point

are determined by the minimal smooth critical point

\displaystyle\begin{cases}Q(\bm{w})=0,&\text{(singularity condition)}\\
\bm{r}=-\eta\nabla_{\log}Q(\bm{w}),\quad\eta>0.&\text{(criticality condition)}\end{cases}

(44)

Here,

\nabla_{\log}Q(\bm{w})=\bigl(w_{i}\partial_{z_{i}}Q(\bm{w})\bigr)_{i=1}^{2m}
.

Proof Sketch

We briefly sketch the geometric intuition behind decomposing the multivariate integral into tangential and normal components before proceeding to the technical details.

In logarithmic coordinates
\bm{\zeta}\!=\!\log\bm{z}
, the multivariate Cauchy integral for the coefficient
[\bm{z}^{\bm{r}}]F(\bm{z})
localizes around the critical point
\bm{w}
. The calculation splits into two orthogonal components:

•

Tangential Integration (Gaussian): Integration over the
(d_{\mathrm{eff}}\!-\!1)
directions tangent to the surface

yields a Gaussian integral. This contributes the geometric prefactor involving the determinant of the Hessian
\mathcal{H}
of the phase function restricted to the manifold.

•

Normal Integration (Hankel): Integration over the direction normal to the surface is governed by the singularity
Q^{-\alpha}
. By introducing the local normal coordinate
q\triangleq-\tilde{Q}(\bm{\zeta})
and deforming the path to the canonical Hankel contour
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{H}}
, this component is identified with the reciprocal Gamma function via

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{H}}}(-q)^{-\alpha}\mathrm{e}^{-\eta\,q}\,\mathrm{d}q\;=\;\frac{\eta^{\alpha-1}}{\Gamma(\alpha)}.

(45)

A key step in our proof is establishing that the Lagrange scaling parameter satisfies
\eta\!=\!n
. This follows directly from Euler’s homogeneous function theorem, given that the Perron–Frobenius eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}(\bm{z})
(and hence
Q
) is homogeneous of degree
1
.

Finally, evaluating the analytic part

at the critical point and combining it with the Gaussian and Hankel factors yields the asymptotic formulas in Proposition
6
.

This concludes the proof sketch. We now proceed with the detailed derivation.

C-B

Asymptotic Factorization via ACSV

Having established the standard form
F(\bm{z})=P(\bm{z})Q(\bm{z})^{-\alpha}
and the smoothness of the singular variety at the dominant critical point
\bm{w}
, we now evaluate the coefficient extraction integral.

Under Assumption
1
, the partitioning
\bm{r}\triangleq(\bm{k},\bm{\ell})\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{2m}
for admissible configurations are constrained by the global balance condition
\sum_{i=1}^{m}k_{i}\!=\!\sum_{j=1}^{m}\ell_{j}
. This implies that the support of the coefficient array lies on a codimension-one hyperplane defined by
\bm{c}\cdot\bm{r}\!=\!0
with
\bm{c}\!=\!(\bm{1}_{m},-\bm{1}_{m})
. Consequently, the problem possesses one redundant degree of freedom, reducing the effective dimension to

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{eff}}\triangleq 2m-1.

(46)

We now formulate the integral representation for the coefficient
a_{\bm{r}}
in this reduced space. To eliminate the redundancy, we parameterize the problem using
d_{\mathrm{eff}}
independent variables (e.g., by eliminating
\ell_{m}\!=\!\sum_{i=1}^{m}k_{i}\!-\!\sum_{j=1}^{m-1}\ell_{j}
and the corresponding indeterminate
u_{m}
). In the following, we let
\bm{z}
and
\bm{r}
denote these effective coordinates and partitioning, respectively. Under this convention,
a_{\bm{r}}
is given by the multivariate Cauchy integral

\displaystyle a_{\bm{r}}=\frac{1}{(2\pi i)^{d_{\mathrm{eff}}}}\oint_{\mathcal{C}}F(\bm{z})\,\bm{z}^{-\bm{r}-\bm{1}}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{z}.

(47)

Introducing logarithmic coordinates
\bm{z}=\exp(\bm{\zeta})
and setting
\tilde{P}(\bm{\zeta})\triangleq P(\mathrm{e}^{\bm{\zeta}})
and
\tilde{Q}(\bm{\zeta})\triangleq Q(\mathrm{e}^{\bm{\zeta}})
, the integral transforms into the exponential form

\displaystyle a_{\bm{r}}=\frac{1}{(2\pi i)^{d_{\mathrm{eff}}}}\int_{\mathcal{C}}\exp(-\bm{r}\cdot\bm{\zeta})\,\tilde{P}(\bm{\zeta})\,\tilde{Q}(\bm{\zeta})^{-\alpha}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\zeta}.

(48)

Let
\bm{\zeta}^{*}\triangleq\log\bm{w}
, where
\bm{w}
satisfies
Q(\bm{w})=0
. The gradients in
\bm{\zeta}
-space and in
\log
-space agree

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{\zeta}}\tilde{Q}(\bm{\zeta}_{*})=\nabla_{\log}Q(\bm{w}).

Set
\bm{v}\triangleq\nabla_{\log}Q(\bm{w})
and choose an orthonormal basis
\bm{v}^{(2)},\dots,\bm{v}^{(d_{\mathrm{eff}})}
for the tangent space at
\bm{\zeta}^{*}
with
\bm{v}^{(j)}\perp\bm{v}
. We use the local chart

\displaystyle\bm{\zeta}=\bm{\zeta}^{*}+u\,\bm{v}+\sum_{j=2}^{d_{\mathrm{eff}}}y_{j}\,\bm{v}^{(j)},

(49)

where

is the (unnormalized) normal coordinate and
\bm{y}\triangleq(y_{2},\dots,y_{d_{\mathrm{eff}}})^{\mathsf{T}}
are tangential coordinates. The Jacobian matrix is
\mathbf{J}=[\bm{v},\bm{v}^{(2)},\dots,\bm{v}^{(d_{\mathrm{eff}})}]
, hence
|\det(\mathbf{J})|=\|\bm{v}\|_{2}=\|\nabla_{\log}Q(\bm{w})\|_{2}
, and

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\bm{\zeta}=\|\nabla_{\log}Q(\bm{w})\|_{2}\,\mathrm{d}u\wedge\mathrm{d}y_{2}\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathrm{d}y_{d_{\mathrm{eff}}}.

Expanding
\tilde{Q}
around
\bm{\zeta}^{*}
in these coordinates gives

\displaystyle\tilde{Q}(\bm{\zeta})

\displaystyle=\tilde{Q}(\bm{\zeta}^{*})+\nabla\tilde{Q}(\bm{\zeta}^{*})\cdot(\bm{\zeta}-\bm{\zeta}^{*})+O(\|\bm{\zeta}-\bm{\zeta}^{*}\|^{2})

\displaystyle=u\,\|\bm{v}\|_{2}^{2}+O(\|\bm{\zeta}-\bm{\zeta}^{*}\|^{2})

\displaystyle=u\,\|\nabla_{\log}Q(\bm{w})\|_{2}^{2}+O(\|\bm{\zeta}-\bm{\zeta}^{*}\|^{2}).

(50)

Therefore, to first order,

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\tilde{Q}(\bm{\zeta})

\displaystyle\approx\|\nabla_{\log}Q(\bm{w})\|_{2}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}u,

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{\|\nabla_{\log}Q(\bm{w})\|_{2}^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}\tilde{Q}.

Substituting into the volume form yields the separated wedge form

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\bm{\zeta}\approx\frac{1}{\|\nabla_{\log}Q(\bm{w})\|_{2}}\,\mathrm{d}\tilde{Q}\wedge\mathrm{d}\bm{y}.

Here, we identify the tangential volume element
\mathrm{d}\bm{y}
(defined via the orthonormal basis) with the oriented holomorphic
(d\!-\!1)
area form
\mathrm{d}A
for
\log\mathcal{V}
, consistent with the convention in the residue formula in
[
18
, Theorem 9.45]
. This leads to the relation

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\bm{\zeta}\approx\frac{1}{\|\nabla_{\log}Q(\bm{w})\|_{2}}\,\mathrm{d}\tilde{Q}\wedge\mathrm{d}A.

Hence, near the critical point
\bm{\zeta}^{*}
, the coefficient integral is approximated by

\displaystyle a_{\bm{r}}=\frac{1}{(2\pi i)^{d_{\mathrm{eff}}}}\int_{\mathcal{C}}\frac{\exp(-\bm{r}\cdot\bm{\zeta})\tilde{P}(\bm{\zeta})\tilde{Q}(\bm{\zeta})^{-\alpha}}{\|\nabla_{\log}Q(\bm{w})\|_{2}}\mathrm{d}\tilde{Q}(\bm{\zeta})\wedge\mathrm{d}A.

(51)

We now localize the Cauchy integral near the dominant critical point
\bm{\zeta}^{*}=\log\bm{w}
. Following the stratification analysis setup (e.g.,
[
18
, Theorem 9.45]
), the original integration cycle is deformed (without crossing singularities) into a local tube aligned with the geometry of the singular manifold
\mathcal{V}=\{\bm{z}:Q(\bm{z})=0\}
.

To derive the geometric and singular contributions, we expand the exponential kernel
-\bm{r}\cdot\bm{\zeta}
using the local coordinate decomposition (
49
). Recall that
\bm{y}=(y_{2},\dots,y_{d_{\mathrm{eff}}})^{\mathsf{T}}
denotes the tangential coordinates and
q\triangleq\tilde{Q}(\bm{\zeta})
parameterizes the normal direction. Applying the saddle-point alignment
\bm{r}\!=\!-\eta\nabla_{\log}Q(\bm{w})
, we find that the phase function decouples into a linear normal component and a quadratic tangential component as

\displaystyle-\bm{r}\cdot\bm{\zeta}

\displaystyle\approx-\bm{r}\cdot\bm{\zeta}^{*}-\bm{r}\cdot(\bm{\zeta}\!-\!\bm{\zeta}^{*})

\displaystyle\approx-\bm{r}\cdot\bm{\zeta}^{*}+\eta\,q-\frac{1}{2}\|\bm{r}\|_{2}\,\bm{y}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathcal{H}\,\bm{y},

where
\mathcal{H}
represents the Hessian matrix governing the curvature of the singular manifold
\mathcal{V}
restricted to the tangent space. This separation in the exponent implies that the integral factorizes into two orthogonal components:

•

Tangential Component (Geometry)
: Along the directions of
\bm{y}
(where
q=0
), the linear variation vanishes because
\bm{r}
is a normal vector. The dominant contribution arises from the quadratic curvature term, leading to a standard Gaussian integral
[
18

given by

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{eff}}-1}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\|\bm{r}\|_{2}\,\bm{y}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathcal{H}\,\bm{y}\right)\mathrm{d}\bm{y}=\sqrt{\frac{(2\pi)^{d_{\mathrm{eff}}-1}}{\det(\|\bm{r}\|_{2}\mathcal{H})}}.

Regarding the global prefactor
(2\pi i)^{-d_{\mathrm{eff}}}
from the Cauchy integral, we refer to the standard saddle-point analysis in
[
18
, Theorem 9.45]
. The deformation of the integration cycle to the path of steepest descent naturally handles the imaginary units, effectively canceling the factor
i^{-(d_{\mathrm{eff}}-1)}
associated with the tangential dimensions. Combining the resulting real prefactor
(2\pi)^{-(d_{\mathrm{eff}}-1)}
with the Gaussian integral above, the net contribution from the tangential geometry is

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{(2\pi\|\bm{r}\|_{2})^{d_{\mathrm{eff}}-1}\det(\mathcal{H})}}.

•

Normal Component (Singularity)
: To provide a unified treatment for both algebraic singularities (
\alpha\notin\mathbb{Z}
) and polar singularities (
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
), we adopt the Hankel contour framework adapted from the singularity analysis of generating functions (see, e.g., the transfer theorem in
[
19
, Theorem VI.3]
). This approach treats the singularity generically as a branch point.

To align with the standard Hankel contour definition (where the path wraps around the positive real axis), we define the normal coordinate with a sign flip as

\displaystyle q\triangleq-\tilde{Q}(\bm{\zeta})=\lambda_{1}(\mathrm{e}^{\bm{\zeta}})\!-\!1.

(52)

With this definition, the singular factor becomes
(-q)^{-\alpha}
. Applying the saddle-point alignment, the expansion of the exponential kernel
-\bm{r}\cdot\bm{\zeta}
yields a decaying term
-\eta\,q
(since
\eta>0
).

The justification for retaining the constant prefactor
\|\nabla_{\log}Q(\bm{w})\|_{2}^{-1}
in (
51

\bm{\zeta}^{*}
where the variations in the smooth prefactors are negligible. We then extend the path to the full Hankel contour
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{H}}
, which starts and ends at
+\infty
and winds counter-clockwise around the origin.

This extension is justified because the contribution from the added tails is exponentially small due to the decay of
\mathrm{e}^{-\eta\,q}
. Furthermore, in the specific case of poles (
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}
), the contributions from the two parallel lines of the contour along the branch cut exactly cancel, naturally recovering the standard residue result.

The normal integral thus matches the canonical representation of the reciprocal Gamma function
[

given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{norm}}=-\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{H}}}(-q)^{-\alpha}\mathrm{e}^{-\eta\,q}\,\mathrm{d}q.

By substituting

, we obtain the final result

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{norm}}=\eta^{\alpha-1}\left(-\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{H}}}(-u)^{-\alpha}\mathrm{e}^{-u}\,\mathrm{d}u\right)=\frac{\eta^{\alpha-1}}{\Gamma(\alpha)}.

Combining the constant amplitude
\bm{w}^{-\bm{r}}P(\bm{w})

, we obtain the final asymptotic formula

\displaystyle a_{\bm{r}}\sim\frac{\bm{w}^{-\bm{r}}P(\bm{w})}{\sqrt{(2\pi\|\bm{r}\|_{2})^{d_{\mathrm{eff}}-1}\det(\mathcal{H})}}\cdot\frac{1}{\|\nabla_{\log}Q(\bm{w})\|_{2}}\cdot\frac{\eta^{\alpha-1}}{\Gamma(\alpha)}.

(53)

Remark 9
.

We verify the correctness of the gradient factor by checking invariance under the rescaling
Q\mapsto Q_{c}\triangleq c\,Q
for any constant

.
Since the generating function scales as
F_{c}(\bm{z})=P(\bm{z})(cQ(\bm{z}))^{-\alpha}=c^{-\alpha}F(\bm{z})
, the asymptotic coefficient must satisfy the exact scaling law

\displaystyle a_{\bm{r}}^{(c)}=c^{-\alpha}\,a_{\bm{r}}.

(54)

We now examine how the components of the derived asymptotic formula transform:

•

The gradient norm scales linearly as
\|\nabla_{\log}Q_{c}(\bm{w})\|_{2}=c\,\|\nabla_{\log}Q(\bm{w})\|_{2}
.

•

The Lagrange multiplier
\eta
, determined by
\bm{r}=-\eta_{c}\nabla_{\log}Q_{c}(\bm{w})
, scales inversely such that
\eta_{c}=c^{-1}\eta
.

•

Consequently, the normal Hankel integral contribution scales as

\displaystyle\frac{\eta_{c}^{\alpha-1}}{\Gamma(\alpha)}=c^{-(\alpha-1)}\frac{\eta^{\alpha-1}}{\Gamma(\alpha)}.

To recover the required total scaling
c^{-\alpha}
in (

), the remaining geometric prefactor must contribute exactly a factor of

.
Since the gradient norm scales as
c^{1}
, it must appear with
power one
in the denominator

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\|\nabla_{\log}Q_{c}(\bm{w})\|_{2}}=c^{-1}\frac{1}{\|\nabla_{\log}Q(\bm{w})\|_{2}}.

In contrast, a squared denominator would scale as
c^{-2}
, leading to an incorrect total scaling of
c^{-(\alpha+1)}
.
This confirms that the formula in the first edition
[
20
, Theorem 9.5.4]
(power one) is dimensionally consistent, whereas the expression in the second edition
[
18
, Theorem 9.45]
(squared norm) is incorrect.

It remains to identify the Lagrange multiplier
\eta
in the critical-point equations. Although the integration is carried out in effective coordinates, the algebraic characterization of the critical point is most naturally derived using the full symmetry of the transition matrix in the ambient space. From the saddle-point alignment

\displaystyle\bm{r}=-\eta\,\nabla_{\log}Q(\bm{w}),\qquad Q(\bm{z})=1-\lambda_{1}(\bm{z}),

Since
\lambda_{1}(\bm{w})\!=\!1

\mathcal{V}

\mathrm{d}\lambda_{1}\!=\!\lambda_{1}\mathrm{d}\log\lambda_{1}\!=\!\mathrm{d}\log\lambda_{1}
. We thus equivalently have

\displaystyle\bm{r}=\eta\,\nabla_{\log}\lambda_{1}(\bm{w}),\qquad\nabla_{\log}\lambda_{1}(\bm{w})=\left.\frac{\partial\log\lambda_{1}}{\partial\log\bm{z}}\right|_{\bm{w}}.

This alignment holds componentwise as

\displaystyle k_{i}

\displaystyle=\eta\,\left.\frac{\partial\log\lambda_{1}(\bm{t},\bm{u})}{\partial\log t_{i}}\right|_{(\bm{t},\bm{u})=\bm{w}},\qquad i=1,\ldots,m,

(55)

\displaystyle\ell_{j}

\displaystyle=\eta\,\left.\frac{\partial\log\lambda_{1}(\bm{t},\bm{u})}{\partial\log u_{j}}\right|_{(\bm{t},\bm{u})=\bm{w}},\qquad j=1,\ldots,m.

(56)

Invoking the partitioning constraints from Assumption

, summing (

) over

and (

) over

\displaystyle n=\sum_{i\in[m]}k_{i}

\displaystyle=\eta\sum_{i\in[m]}\left.\frac{\partial\log\lambda_{1}}{\partial\log t_{i}}\right|_{\bm{w}},

(57)

\displaystyle n=\sum_{j\in[m]}\ell_{j}

\displaystyle=\eta\sum_{j\in[m]}\left.\frac{\partial\log\lambda_{1}}{\partial\log u_{j}}\right|_{\bm{w}}.

(58)

To evaluate the sums of logarithmic derivatives, note that the state-transition matrix
\mathbf{S}(\bm{t},\bm{u})
is constructed with entries proportional to the product

. Consequently,
\mathbf{S}
is homogeneous of degree

in
\bm{t}
(fixing
\bm{u}
) and also of degree

in
\bm{u}
(fixing
\bm{t}
). Explicitly, for any

,

\displaystyle\mathbf{S}(c\,\bm{t},\bm{u})=c\,\mathbf{S}(\bm{t},\bm{u}),\qquad\mathbf{S}(\bm{t},c\,\bm{u})=c\,\mathbf{S}(\bm{t},\bm{u}),

hence the Perron–Frobenius eigenvalue scales linearly, i.e.,

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}(c\,\bm{t},\bm{u})=c\,\lambda_{1}(\bm{t},\bm{u}),\qquad\lambda_{1}(\bm{t},c\,\bm{u})=c\,\lambda_{1}(\bm{t},\bm{u}).

Therefore
\lambda_{1}
is a degree-

homogeneous function in
\bm{t}
(with
\bm{u}
fixed) and likewise in
\bm{u}
(with
\bm{t}
fixed). By Euler’s theorem for homogeneous functions,

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in[m]}\left.\frac{\partial\log\lambda_{1}}{\partial\log t_{i}}\right|_{\bm{w}}

\displaystyle=1,

(59)

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in[m]}\left.\frac{\partial\log\lambda_{1}}{\partial\log u_{j}}\right|_{\bm{w}}

\displaystyle=1.

(60)

Applying the Euler identity (

) and (

) to (

) and (

\displaystyle\eta=n.

(61)

This confirms that the global balance constraints align with the spectral scaling properties.

With

\displaystyle a_{\bm{r}}\sim\frac{\bm{w}^{-\bm{r}}P(\bm{w})}{\sqrt{(2\pi\|\bm{r}\|_{2})^{d_{\mathrm{eff}}-1}\det(\mathcal{H})}}\cdot\frac{1}{\|\nabla_{\log}Q(\bm{w})\|_{2}}\cdot\frac{n^{\alpha-1}}{\Gamma(\alpha)}.

Evaluating the analytic parts

at the critical point (where

Appendix D
Deriving
(\bm{t^{*}}\!,\bm{u^{*}}\!)
from Sinkhorn’s Theorem

It turns out that the ACSV saddle-point parameters
(\bm{t^{*}}\!,\bm{u^{*}}\!)
can be obtained with the help of Sinkhorn’s theorem. We restrict attention to the two-type block-constant setting
m=2
, where
\mathbf{B}=(b_{i,j})\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{2\times 2}
and the expanded matrix
\mathbf{A}
consists of four constant blocks of sizes
k_{i}\times\ell_{j}
(
i,j\in\{1,2\}
), with every entry in block
(i,j)
equal to
b_{i,j}
. Note, however, that the results in this appendix can be straightforwardly generalized to any positive integer
m
.

We begin by recalling the classical Sinkhorn theorem
[
21
]
, which characterizes the diagonal scaling of strictly positive matrices.

Lemma 10
.

For any strictly positive square matrix
\mathbf{A}
, there exists a unique doubly stochastic matrix
\mathbf{U}
expressible in the form

\displaystyle\mathbf{U}=\mathbf{D}_{1}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{D}_{2},

(65)

where
\mathbf{D}_{1}
and
\mathbf{D}_{2}
are diagonal matrices with strictly positive diagonal entries. While
\mathbf{U}
is unique, the scaling matrices
\mathbf{D}_{1}
and
\mathbf{D}_{2}
are unique up to a common scalar factor.

Since
\mathbf{U}
is doubly stochastic, its row and column sums must equal
1
. Combined with Eq. (
65
), this implies constraints on the diagonal entries of
\mathbf{D}_{1}
and
\mathbf{D}_{2}
. Leveraging the block-constant structure of
\mathbf{A}
described in Assumption
1
, we can explicitly characterize these constraints.

Lemma 11
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be the block-constant matrix defined above. There exist positive scalars
\overrightarrow{V}_{\mkern-6.0mu1},\overrightarrow{V}_{\mkern-6.0mu2},\overleftarrow{V}_{\mkern-6.0mu1},\overleftarrow{V}_{\mkern-6.0mu2}
satisfying the coupled system

\displaystyle\overrightarrow{V}_{\mkern-6.0mu1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\ell_{1}b_{1,1}\overleftarrow{V}_{\mkern-6.0mu1}\!+\!\ell_{2}b_{1,2}\overleftarrow{V}_{\mkern-6.0mu2}},

(66)

\displaystyle\overrightarrow{V}_{\mkern-6.0mu2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\ell_{1}b_{2,1}\overleftarrow{V}_{\mkern-6.0mu1}\!+\!\ell_{2}b_{2,2}\overleftarrow{V}_{\mkern-6.0mu2}},

(67)

\displaystyle\overleftarrow{V}_{\mkern-6.0mu1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k_{1}b_{1,1}\overrightarrow{V}_{\mkern-6.0mu1}\!+\!k_{2}b_{2,1}\overrightarrow{V}_{\mkern-6.0mu2}},

(68)

\displaystyle\overleftarrow{V}_{\mkern-6.0mu2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k_{1}b_{1,2}\overrightarrow{V}_{\mkern-6.0mu1}\!+\!k_{2}b_{2,2}\overrightarrow{V}_{\mkern-6.0mu2}},

(69)

such that the diagonal scaling matrices in (

\displaystyle\mathbf{D}_{1}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{diag}(\underbrace{\overrightarrow{V}_{\mkern-6.0mu1},\dots,\overrightarrow{V}_{\mkern-6.0mu1}}_{k_{1}},\underbrace{\overrightarrow{V}_{\mkern-6.0mu2},\dots,\overrightarrow{V}_{\mkern-6.0mu2}}_{k_{2}}),

(70)

\displaystyle\mathbf{D}_{2}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{diag}(\underbrace{\overleftarrow{V}_{\mkern-6.0mu1},\dots,\overleftarrow{V}_{\mkern-6.0mu1}}_{\ell_{1}},\underbrace{\overleftarrow{V}_{\mkern-6.0mu2},\dots,\overleftarrow{V}_{\mkern-6.0mu2}}_{\ell_{2}}).

(71)

Proof.

The block-constant structure of
\mathbf{A}
implies that all rows of type
i
are identical, as are all columns of type
j
. By the uniqueness of the Sinkhorn scaling, the associated diagonal factors in
\mathbf{D}_{1}
and
\mathbf{D}_{2}
must be uniform within each type. Substituting these uniform values into the row-sum constraint
\sum_{j}u_{1,j}=1
yields

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\overrightarrow{V}_{\mkern-6.0mu1}A_{1,j}(\mathbf{D}_{2})_{jj}

\displaystyle=\overrightarrow{V}_{\mkern-6.0mu1}\left(\sum_{j\in\text{ Type }1}b_{1,1}\overleftarrow{V}_{\mkern-6.0mu1}+\sum_{j\in\text{ Type }2}b_{1,2}\overleftarrow{V}_{\mkern-6.0mu2}\right)

\displaystyle=\overrightarrow{V}_{\mkern-6.0mu1}(\ell_{1}b_{1,1}\overleftarrow{V}_{\mkern-6.0mu1}+\ell_{2}b_{1,2}\overleftarrow{V}_{\mkern-6.0mu2}).

Rearranging this equation recovers (
66
). The remaining equations (
67
)–(
69
) follow symmetrically from the constraints for row type 2 and both column types.
∎

Theorem

Theorem 12

Let
m=2
and
\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{2\times 2}
. Fix
\bm{r}=(k_{1},k_{2},\ell_{1},\ell_{2})

\overrightarrow{V}_{\mkern-6.0mu1},\overrightarrow{V}_{\mkern-6.0mu2},\overleftarrow{V}_{\mkern-6.0mu1},\overleftarrow{V}_{\mkern-6.0mu2}

\displaystyle t_{1}^{*}=k_{1}\overrightarrow{V}_{\mkern-6.0mu1}^{2},\quad t_{2}^{*}=k_{2}\overrightarrow{V}_{\mkern-6.0mu2}^{2},\quad u_{1}^{*}=\ell_{1}\overleftarrow{V}_{\mkern-6.0mu1}^{2},\quad u_{2}^{*}=\ell_{2}\overleftarrow{V}_{\mkern-6.0mu2}^{2}.

(72)

The choice

•

Manifold constraint:
The condition

corresponds to the unit spectral radius requirement for the weighted transition matrix

in Definition

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}(\bm{t^{*}}\!,\bm{u^{*}}\!)=1.

(73)

•

Gradient alignment:
The condition
\bm{r}=-\eta\nabla_{\log}Q(\bm{w})
is satisfied componentwise, meaning the scaled logarithmic derivatives of the dominant eigenvalue match the respective block dimensions. That is, with
\eta=n
, for all

,

\displaystyle n\left.\frac{\partial\log\lambda_{1}}{\partial\log t_{i}}\right|_{\bm{w}}=k_{i},\qquad n\left.\frac{\partial\log\lambda_{1}}{\partial\log u_{j}}\right|_{\bm{w}}=\ell_{j}.

(74)

Proof.

We first define the scaling vectors

\displaystyle\bm{\overrightarrow{V}}\triangleq\begin{pmatrix}\overrightarrow{V}_{\mkern-6.0mu1}\\
\overrightarrow{V}_{\mkern-6.0mu2}\end{pmatrix},\qquad\bm{\overleftarrow{V}}\triangleq\begin{pmatrix}\overleftarrow{V}_{\mkern-6.0mu1}\\
\overleftarrow{V}_{\mkern-6.0mu2}\end{pmatrix}.

The Sinkhorn system (
66
)–(
69
) can be rewritten in matrix form as

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{\overrightarrow{V}_{\mkern-6.0mu1}}\\[2.0pt]
\frac{1}{\overrightarrow{V}_{\mkern-6.0mu2}}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{B}\begin{pmatrix}\ell_{1}&0\\
0&\ell_{2}\end{pmatrix}\bm{\overleftarrow{V}},

(75)

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{\overleftarrow{V}_{\mkern-6.0mu1}}\\[2.0pt]
\frac{1}{\overleftarrow{V}_{\mkern-6.0mu2}}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{B}^{\mathsf{T}}\begin{pmatrix}k_{1}&0\\
0&k_{2}\end{pmatrix}\bm{\overrightarrow{V}}.

(76)

To facilitate the analysis, we introduce the auxiliary matrix

, given as

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}(t_{1},t_{2},u_{1},u_{2})

\displaystyle\triangleq\begin{pmatrix}\ell_{1}^{-1}u_{1}&0\\
0&\ell_{2}^{-1}u_{2}\end{pmatrix}\mathbf{B}^{\mathsf{T}}\begin{pmatrix}k_{1}&0\\
0&k_{2}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle\qquad\cdot\begin{pmatrix}k_{1}^{-1}t_{1}&0\\
0&k_{2}^{-1}t_{2}\end{pmatrix}\mathbf{B}\begin{pmatrix}\ell_{1}&0\\
0&\ell_{2}\end{pmatrix}.

(77)

Next, we claim that
\bm{\phi}\triangleq\bm{\overleftarrow{V}}
is the right Perron eigenvector of
\mathbf{M}(\bm{t^{*}}\!,\bm{u^{*}}\!)
with the eigenvalue
1
. Substituting the constructed parameters into the definition of
\mathbf{M}
yields

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}\bm{\overleftarrow{V}}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\ell_{1}^{-1}u_{1}^{*}&0\\
0&\ell_{2}^{-1}u_{2}^{*}\end{pmatrix}\mathbf{B}^{\mathsf{T}}\begin{pmatrix}k_{1}&0\\
0&k_{2}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle\qquad\cdot\begin{pmatrix}k_{1}^{-1}t_{1}^{*}&0\\
0&k_{2}^{-1}t_{2}^{*}\end{pmatrix}\mathbf{B}\begin{pmatrix}\ell_{1}&0\\
0&\ell_{2}\end{pmatrix}\bm{\overleftarrow{V}}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{(a)}}}{{=}}\begin{pmatrix}\ell_{1}^{-1}u_{1}^{*}&0\\
0&\ell_{2}^{-1}u_{2}^{*}\end{pmatrix}\mathbf{B}^{\mathsf{T}}\begin{pmatrix}k_{1}&0\\
0&k_{2}\end{pmatrix}\bm{\overrightarrow{V}}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{(b)}}}{{=}}\bm{\overleftarrow{V}},

where step
\mathrm{(a)}
follows from applying (
75
) to the right-most matrix-vector product, and step
\mathrm{(b)}
follows from applying (
76
) to the remaining expression. Thus,
\mathbf{M}\bm{\overleftarrow{V}}=\bm{\overleftarrow{V}}
, establishing that
1
is an eigenvalue of
\mathbf{M}(\bm{t^{*}}\!,\bm{u^{*}}\!)
. To identify this eigenvalue as the dominant one, we invoke the Perron–Frobenius theorem. Since
\mathbf{M}
is strictly positive, the Perron–Frobenius theorem implies that the spectral radius of
\mathbf{M}
is the unique eigenvalue admitting a strictly positive eigenvector, and the associated positive eigenvector is unique up to scaling. Because
\bm{\overleftarrow{V}}>0
and
\mathbf{M}\bm{\overleftarrow{V}}=\bm{\overleftarrow{V}}
, we must have
\lambda_{1}(\bm{t^{*}}\!,\bm{u^{*}}\!)=1
.

To analyze the gradient alignment constraints in (
55
)–(
56
), we need to compute the logarithmic derivatives of the dominant eigenvalue at
\bm{w}=(\bm{t^{*}}\!,\bm{u^{*}}\!)
. Define the row vector
\bm{\psi}^{\mathsf{T}}\triangleq(\ell_{1}/\overleftarrow{V}_{\mkern-6.0mu1},\ell_{2}/\overleftarrow{V}_{\mkern-6.0mu2})
. Its normalization with respect to
\bm{\phi}
is

\displaystyle\bm{\psi}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\phi}=\begin{pmatrix}\ell_{1}/\overleftarrow{V}_{\mkern-6.0mu1}&\ell_{2}/\overleftarrow{V}_{\mkern-6.0mu2}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\overleftarrow{V}_{\mkern-6.0mu1}\\
\overleftarrow{V}_{\mkern-6.0mu2}\end{pmatrix}=\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}=n.

A similar direct calculation using (
75
)–(
76
) verifies that
\bm{\psi}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{M}=\bm{\psi}^{\mathsf{T}}
. Thus,
\bm{\psi}^{\mathsf{T}}
is the left Perron eigenvector of
\mathbf{M}
with the eigenvalue
1
.

We seek to evaluate the sensitivity of the eigenvalue with respect to the parameter
\log t_{1}
. To this end, we define the first-order perturbation terms as

\displaystyle\tilde{\lambda}_{1}\triangleq\frac{\partial\lambda_{1}}{\partial\log t_{1}},\qquad\tilde{\mathbf{M}}\triangleq\frac{\partial\mathbf{M}}{\partial\log t_{1}}.

(78)

Although the ACSV gradient alignment condition requires the logarithmic derivative
\partial\log\lambda_{1}/\partial\log t_{1}
, the manifold constraint
\lambda_{1}(\bm{t^{*}}\!,\bm{u^{*}}\!)=1
ensures that this coincides with the standard derivative
\tilde{\lambda}_{1}
at the solution
(\bm{t^{*}}\!,\bm{u^{*}}\!)
. We determine this value using standard eigenvalue perturbation theory (see, e.g.,
[
22
]
).

Consider a small perturbation
\varepsilon
in the parameter
\log t_{1}
. This induces perturbations in the matrix
\mathbf{M}\to\mathbf{M}\!+\!\varepsilon\tilde{\mathbf{M}}\!+\!O(\varepsilon^{2})
, the eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}\to\lambda_{1}\!+\!\varepsilon\tilde{\lambda}_{1}\!+\!O(\varepsilon^{2})
, and the right Perron eigenvector
\bm{\phi}\to\bm{\phi}+\varepsilon\tilde{\bm{\phi}}+O(\varepsilon^{2})
. The eigenvalue equation for the perturbed system is

\displaystyle\bigl(\mathbf{M}+\varepsilon\tilde{\mathbf{M}}+O(\varepsilon^{2})\bigr)\bigl(\bm{\phi}+\varepsilon\tilde{\bm{\phi}}+O(\varepsilon^{2})\bigr)

\displaystyle=\bigl(\lambda_{1}+\varepsilon\tilde{\lambda}_{1}+O(\varepsilon^{2})\bigr)\bigl(\bm{\phi}+\varepsilon\tilde{\bm{\phi}}+O(\varepsilon^{2})\bigr).

Expanding both sides and retaining only terms linear in
\varepsilon
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}\bm{\phi}+\varepsilon(\mathbf{M}\tilde{\bm{\phi}}\!+\!\tilde{\mathbf{M}}\bm{\phi})\approx\lambda_{1}\bm{\phi}+\varepsilon(\lambda_{1}\tilde{\bm{\phi}}\!+\!\tilde{\lambda}_{1}\bm{\phi}).

Since
\mathbf{M}\bm{\phi}=\lambda_{1}\bm{\phi}
, the zero-order terms cancel. Equating the coefficients of
\varepsilon
yields

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}\tilde{\bm{\phi}}+\tilde{\mathbf{M}}\bm{\phi}=\lambda_{1}\tilde{\bm{\phi}}+\tilde{\lambda}_{1}\bm{\phi}.

(79)

To isolate
\tilde{\lambda}_{1}
, we left-multiply the equation by the left Perron eigenvector
\bm{\psi}^{\mathsf{T}}
, yielding

\displaystyle\bm{\psi}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{M}\tilde{\bm{\phi}}+\bm{\psi}^{\mathsf{T}}\tilde{\mathbf{M}}\bm{\phi}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{1}\bm{\psi}^{\mathsf{T}}\tilde{\bm{\phi}}+\tilde{\lambda}_{1}\bm{\psi}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\phi}.

Using
\bm{\psi}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{M}=\lambda_{1}\bm{\psi}^{\mathsf{T}}
, the term
\lambda_{1}\bm{\psi}^{\mathsf{T}}\tilde{\bm{\phi}}
cancels on both sides. Solving for
\tilde{\lambda}_{1}
, we arrive at

\displaystyle\tilde{\lambda}_{1}=\frac{\bm{\psi}^{\mathsf{T}}\tilde{\mathbf{M}}\bm{\phi}}{\bm{\psi}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\phi}}.

(80)

Using the definition of
\tilde{\mathbf{M}}
and the parameters, the numerator of the RHS of (
80
) becomes

\displaystyle\bm{\psi}^{\mathsf{T}}\tilde{\mathbf{M}}\bm{\phi}

\displaystyle=\bm{\psi}^{\mathsf{T}}\begin{pmatrix}\ell_{1}^{-1}u_{1}^{*}&0\\
0&\ell_{2}^{-1}u_{2}^{*}\end{pmatrix}\mathbf{B}^{\mathsf{T}}\begin{pmatrix}k_{1}&0\\
0&k_{2}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle\quad\cdot\begin{pmatrix}k_{1}^{-1}t_{1}^{*}&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}\mathbf{B}\begin{pmatrix}\ell_{1}&0\\
0&\ell_{2}\end{pmatrix}\bm{\overleftarrow{V}}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{(a)}}}{{=}}k_{1}\overrightarrow{V}_{\mkern-6.0mu1}\begin{pmatrix}\ell_{1}/\overleftarrow{V}_{\mkern-6.0mu1}&\ell_{2}/\overleftarrow{V}_{\mkern-6.0mu2}\end{pmatrix}\mathbf{B}^{\mathsf{T}}\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{(b)}}}{{=}}k_{1},

where step
\mathrm{(a)}
uses the relation derived in (
75
) that the right-most product yields
(\overrightarrow{V}_{\mkern-6.0mu1},0)^{\mathsf{T}}
, and step
\mathrm{(b)}
simplifies the scalar product using the first component of (
76
). Consequently, we obtain

\displaystyle\tilde{\lambda}_{1}\triangleq\frac{\bm{\psi}^{\mathsf{T}}\tilde{\mathbf{M}}\bm{\phi}}{\bm{\psi}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\phi}}=\frac{k_{1}}{n}.

(81)

This calculation yields
k_{1}/n
in the
\log t_{1}
direction, confirming that the parameters derived from the Sinkhorn solution satisfy the componentwise ACSV constraint
k_{1}=\eta\frac{\partial\log\lambda_{1}}{\partial\log t_{1}}
with the scaling factor
\eta=n
. The corresponding conditions for
t_{2},u_{1},u_{2}
follow immediately due to the symmetry of the setup.
∎

Finally, we highlight a connection between the system of equations in Lemma
11
and the scaled Sinkhorn permanent
\perm_{\mathrm{scS}}(\mathbf{A})
. Since the parameters
\overrightarrow{V}_{\mkern-6.0mui}
and
\overleftarrow{V}_{\mkern-6.0muj}
are precisely the scaling factors in Sinkhorn’s matrix-scaling iterations, the ACSV critical point
(\bm{t^{*}}\!,\bm{u^{*}}\!)
is intrinsically linked to the value of
\perm_{\mathrm{scS}}(\mathbf{A})
.

We start by introducing the Sinkhorn permanent and its scaled variant. Fundamentally, these quantities are rooted in a variational framework. We refer the reader to
[
8
]
and
[
9
]
for the explicit expressions of the Sinkhorn permanent
\perm_{\mathrm{S}}(\mathbf{A})
and the scaled Sinkhorn permanent
\perm_{\mathrm{scS}}(\mathbf{A})
as the minima of the unscaled and scaled Sinkhorn free energy minimization problems, respectively. Following the notation in
[
23
]
, the scaled Sinkhorn permanent is related to the (unscaled) Sinkhorn permanent by the scaling factor
\mathrm{e}^{-n}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\perm_{\mathrm{scS}}(\mathbf{A})\triangleq\mathrm{e}^{-n}\cdot\perm_{\mathrm{S}}(\mathbf{A}).

(82)

Rearranging terms in (
65
) yields the decomposition
\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{D}_{1}^{-1}\mathbf{U}\mathbf{D}_{2}^{-1}
. Based on this factorization, the Sinkhorn approximation is defined as the product of the permanents of these inverse scaling matrices, i.e.,
\perm_{\mathrm{S}}(\mathbf{A})\triangleq\perm(\mathbf{D}_{1}^{-1})\cdot\perm(\mathbf{D}_{2}^{-1})
. Since the exact permanent factorizes as
\perm(\mathbf{A})=\perm_{\mathrm{S}}(\mathbf{A})\perm(\mathbf{U})
, the accuracy of this approximation depends entirely on the permanent of the doubly stochastic matrix
\mathbf{U}
. Utilizing the established bounds
n!/n^{n}\leq\perm(\mathbf{U})\leq 1

[
24
,
25
]
, the range for the ratio of the permanent of
\mathbf{A}
to its scaled Sinkhorn approximation is

\displaystyle\mathrm{e}^{n}\cdot\frac{n!}{n^{n}}\;\leq\;\frac{\perm(\mathbf{A})}{\perm_{\mathrm{scS}}(\mathbf{A})}\;\leq\;\mathrm{e}^{n},

(83)

where the lower bound scales as
\mathrm{e}^{n}n!/n^{n}\approx\sqrt{2\pi n}
by Stirling’s formula.

With these results, we can rewrite the exact asymptotic expressions for

and

in Proposition

.

Proposition 13
.

Let
m
be a positive integer. As
n\to\infty
, we can rewrite
Z_{n}^{\mathrm{G}}(\bm{k},\bm{\ell})
and
Z_{n}^{\mathrm{B}}(\bm{k},\bm{\ell})
in Proposition

as

\displaystyle Z_{n}^{\mathrm{G}}

\displaystyle\!\sim\!\frac{n\cdot\bm{k}^{-\bm{k}}\bm{\ell}^{-\bm{\ell}}\!\cdot\!\mathrm{e}^{2n}\!\cdot\!\bigl(\perm_{\mathrm{scS}}(\mathbf{A})\bigr)^{2}}{\|\bm{r}\|_{2}(2\pi\|\bm{r}\|_{2})^{m-1}\sqrt{\det(\mathcal{H})}}\cdot\prod_{i=2}^{m}\frac{1}{1\!-\!\rho_{i}},

(84)

\displaystyle Z_{n}^{\mathrm{B}}

\displaystyle\!\sim\!\frac{n\cdot\bm{k}^{-\bm{k}}\bm{\ell}^{-\bm{\ell}}\!\cdot\!\mathrm{e}^{2n}\!\cdot\!\bigl(\perm_{\mathrm{scS}}(\mathbf{A})\bigr)^{2}}{\|\bm{r}\|_{2}(2\pi\|\bm{r}\|_{2})^{m-1}\sqrt{\det(\mathcal{H})}}\cdot\sqrt{\prod_{i=2}^{m}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\rho_{i}}}{1\!-\!\rho_{i}}}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{\mathrm{e}}{\pi n}}.

(85)

Here,
\|\bm{r}\|_{2}=\sqrt{\sum_{i\in[m]}k_{i}^{2}+\sum_{j\in[m]}\ell_{j}^{2}}
,
\det(\mathcal{H})
denotes the determinant of the Hessian of the constraint function restricted to the tangent space, and
\rho_{i}=\lambda_{i}/\lambda_{1}
are the spectral ratios.

Proof.

We evaluate the components of the general ACSV expansion directly. First, substituting the Sinkhorn parametrizations
t_{i}^{*}=k_{i}\overrightarrow{V}_{\mkern-6.0mui}^{2}
and
u_{j}^{*}=\ell_{j}\overleftarrow{V}_{\mkern-6.0muj}^{2}
given in (
72
) into the saddle-point contribution
\bm{w}^{-\bm{r}}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\bm{w}^{-\bm{r}}

\displaystyle=\bm{k}^{-\bm{k}}\bm{\ell}^{-\bm{\ell}}\Biggl(\prod_{i\in[m]}\overrightarrow{V}_{\mkern-6.0mui}^{-k_{i}}\prod_{j\in[m]}\overleftarrow{V}_{\mkern-6.0muj}^{-\ell_{j}}\Biggr)^{2}

\displaystyle=\bm{k}^{-\bm{k}}\bm{\ell}^{-\bm{\ell}}\bigl(\perm_{\mathrm{S}}(\mathbf{A})\bigr)^{2}

\displaystyle=\bm{k}^{-\bm{k}}\bm{\ell}^{-\bm{\ell}}\cdot\mathrm{e}^{2n}\bigl(\perm_{\mathrm{scS}}(\mathbf{A})\bigr)^{2}.

(86)

Next, the gradient alignment conditions (
55
) and (
56
) imply
\nabla_{\log}Q(\bm{w})=-\bm{r}/n
. Consequently, the gradient norm simplifies to
\|\nabla_{\log}Q(\bm{w})\|_{2}=\|\bm{r}\|_{2}/n
.

For the spectral components, the matrix
\mathbf{M}
shares the same non-zero eigenvalues
\{\lambda_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
with
\mathbf{W}
in Definition
2
and
\mathbf{S}
in Definition
3
. At the critical point, the dominant eigenvalue is
\lambda_{1}\!=\!1
. We define the spectral ratios as
\rho_{i}\triangleq\lambda_{i}/\lambda_{1}
(where
\rho_{i}=\lambda_{i}
since
\lambda_{1}=1
) for
i=2,\dots,m
. The resulting spectral factors are then given by
\prod_{i=2}^{m}(1\!-\!\rho_{i})^{-1}
for
Z_{n}^{\mathrm{G}}
and
\sqrt{\prod_{i=2}^{m}\mathrm{e}^{\rho_{i}}(1\!-\!\rho_{i})^{-1}}
for
Z_{n}^{\mathrm{B}}
.

Finally, the effective dimension of the integral is
d_{\mathrm{eff}}\!=\!2m\!-\!1
. Combining the geometric factor
\sqrt{(2\pi\|\bm{r}\|_{2})^{2m-2}\det(\mathcal{H})}
with the gradient norm contribution
\|\bm{r}\|_{2}/n
leads to the effective global prefactor

\displaystyle\frac{n}{\|\bm{r}\|_{2}(2\pi\|\bm{r}\|_{2})^{m-1}\sqrt{\det(\mathcal{H})}}.

Multiplying this prefactor by the main term and the respective spectral factors establishes the asymptotic formulas.
∎

With the explicit asymptotic forms for the coefficients

\displaystyle\frac{\bigl(\perm(\mathbf{A})\bigr)^{2}}{\bigl(\perm_{\mathrm{scS}}(\mathbf{A})\bigr)^{2}}
