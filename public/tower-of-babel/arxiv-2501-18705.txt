Hyperbolicity, topology, and combinatorics of fine curve graphs and variants
Roberta Shapiro

Abstract
. Given a surface, the fine k k -curve graph of the surface is a graph whose vertices are simple closed essential curves and whose edges connect curves that intersect in at most k k points. We note that the fine k k -curve graph is hyperbolic for all k k and, for k ≥ 2 , k\geq 2, show that it contains as induced subgraphs all countable graphs. We also show that the direct limit of this family of graphs, which we call the finitary curve graph, has diameter 2, has a contractible flag complex, contains every countable graph as an induced subgraph, and has as its automorphism group the homeomorphism group of the surface. Finally, we explore some finite graphs that are not induced subgraphs of fine curve graphs.



1.
Introduction

Let
S
be an orientable surface, sometimes denoted
S_{g,b}
(if
S
has genus
g
and
b
boundary components) or just
S_{g}
(if
S
is closed and compact). The
fine
k
-curve graph of
S
, denoted
{\mathcal{C}^{\dagger}_{k}}(S),
is the graph whose vertices are embedded simple, closed, essential curves. Two vertices
u
and
v
are connected by an edge if
|u\cap v|\leq k.
When
k=0
, we call the resulting graph the
fine curve graph
, denoted
{\mathcal{C}^{\dagger}}(S)
.

We would be amiss to not include the observation that
{\mathcal{C}^{\dagger}_{k}}(S)
is hyperbolic.

Observation/Theorem 1.1
.

Let
S_{g}
be a closed, orientable surface with
g\geq 2.
Then,
{\mathcal{C}^{\dagger}_{k}}(S_{g})
is hyperbolic.

Theorem
1.1
is known for the fine curve graph due to work of Bowden–Hensel–Webb
[
7
]
and the proof reduces to this fact via quasi-isometries.

We now define the
finitary curve graph of
S
,
denoted
{\mathcal{C}^{\dagger}_{<\infty}}(S),
to be

{\mathcal{C}^{\dagger}_{<\infty}}(S):=\varinjlim_{k}{\mathcal{C}^{\dagger}_{k}}(S).

The vertices of
{\mathcal{C}^{\dagger}_{<\infty}}(S)
are again embedded essential simple closed curves in
S,
but now edges connect curves that intersect at finitely many points.

Our second theorem computes the diameter of
{\mathcal{C}^{\dagger}_{<\infty}}(S)
in the path metric.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
S=S_{g,b}
be a compact, orientable surface with
g\geq 1
or
b\geq 4.
Then,
\diam({\mathcal{C}^{\dagger}_{<\infty}}(S))=2
.

So
{\mathcal{C}^{\dagger}_{<\infty}}(S_{g})
is quasi-isometric to a point. Define the
flag complex
of
{\mathcal{C}^{\dagger}_{<\infty}}(S_{g})
to be the simplicial complex where vertices
v_{0},\ldots,v_{k}
span a
k
-cell if
|v_{i}\cap v_{j}|<\infty
for all
i\neq j.
We may then ask whether the flag complex
{\mathcal{C}^{\dagger}_{<\infty}}(S_{g})
is homotopic to a point. This question is answered in the affirmative in the following theorem.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
S_{g,b}
be an orientable surface with
g\geq 1
or
b\geq 4.
Then the flag complex of
{\mathcal{C}^{\dagger}_{<\infty}}(S_{g,b})
is contractible.

We also study more combinatorial properties of the fine curve graph and finitary curve graph. In the following theorems, we show that any finite graph can be embedded as an induced subgraph of the fine curve graph of a surface of high enough genus or the fine
k
-curve graph of any surface that contains an essential embedded annulus.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
G
be a finite graph on
n
vertices. Then
G
is isomorphic to an induced subgraph of
{\mathcal{C}^{\dagger}}(S_{g})
with
g\geq{n\choose 2}-n+1
, so
g=O(n^{2}).

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
G
be a graph on countably many vertices and
S_{g,b}
be an orientable surface with
g\geq 1
or
b\geq 4.
Then
G
is isomorphic to an induced subgraph of
{\mathcal{C}^{\dagger}_{<\infty}}(S_{g,b})
and
\mathcal{C}_{k}^{\dagger}(S_{g,b})
for
k\geq 2
.

We have a corollary that relates to the Erdős-Rényi graph, which is the unique (up to isomorphism) random graph on countably many vertices.

Corollary 1.6
.

Let
ERG
be the Erdős-Rényi graph. Then
ERG
is isomorphic to an induced subgraph of
{\mathcal{C}^{\dagger}_{<\infty}}(S_{g,b})
and
\mathcal{C}_{k}^{\dagger}(S_{g,b})
with
k\geq 2.

Define the
curve graph
of a surface
\Sigma
to be the graph whose vertices are isotopy classes of essential simple closed curves and whose edges connect pairs of isotopy classes that admit disjoint representatives. Bering–Gaster prove that the curve graph of a surface
\Sigma
has
ERG
as an induced subgraph if and only if
\Sigma
has infinite genus
[
3
]
. One would hope that the fine curve graph would grant us more flexibility in this regard, but alas, the proof of Bering–Gaster holds.

However, we have a new method of constructing finite graphs that, given a surface of genus
g
with
b
boundary components, there is a graph with
O(g+b)
vertices does not appear as an induced subgraph. We call such graphs
inadmissible
.

Theorem 1.7

(Construction of inadmissible graphs)
.

Let
\Sigma=S_{g,b}
. Then there exists a graph
G_{\Sigma}
that does not appear as an induced subgraph of
{\mathcal{C}^{\dagger}}(\Sigma).

Finally, we find the automorphism group of the finitary curve graph in the following theorem.

Theorem 1.8
.

Let
S_{g}
be a closed oriented surface with
g\geq 1.
Then the natural map

\Phi:\Homeo(S_{g})\to\Aut{\mathcal{C}^{\dagger}_{<\infty}}(S_{g})

is an isomorphism.

Along the way, we prove an incredibly useful result that is the hammer behind most of the above theorems: given a finite collection of arcs and curves in a surface, there exists an arc or curve in any isotopy class and arbitrarily close to any representative such that it intersects all curves or arcs in the collection finitely many times each and at crossing intersections (see Section
2
for definitions). This is the content of Lemma
4.1
and Corollary
4.2
.

A brief history of curve graphs.
The
curve graph
of a surface
S
is the graph whose vertices are isotopy classes of essential simple closed curves and whose edges connect vertices that admit disjoint representatives. The curve graph is a classical tool used to study the
mapping class group
of
S,
which is the group of orientation-preserving homeomorphisms modulo isotopy. In a seminal paper, Masur–Minksy show that the curve graph is Gromov hyperbolic
[
10
]
. The
k
-curve graph, which has the same vertices as the curve graph while edges connect curves that minimally intersect at most
k
times, is remarked to be hyperbolic by Agrawal–Aougab–Chandran–Loving–Oakley–Shapiro–Xiao
[
1
]
.

An object of recent study is the
fine curve graph
, which is defined to be the fine
k
-curve graph with
k=0
and is notated
{\mathcal{C}^{\dagger}}(S).
It was originally introduced by Bowden–Hensel–Webb in the case that vertices are smooth curves to prove that
\Diffeo_{0}(S_{g})
admits unbounded quasimorphisms (for
g\geq 1
), and, as a corollary, is not uniformly perfect
[
7
]
. Fine curve graphs with edges not corresponding to disjointness were introduced in Le Roux–Wolff
[
11
]
and Booth–Minahan–Shapiro
[
6
]
; both papers study automorphism groups of such graphs. Moreover, Booth studies the automorphism group of the fine curve graph whose vertices are
C^{\infty}
curves and edges correspond to disjointness
[
4
]
and also studies homeomorphisms that preserve the set of continuously differentiable curves
[
5
]
.

A note on the tameness of curves.
Any curve in a surface is tame: at each point, there is a neighborhood of the point where the curve is homeomorphic to one that is locally flat
[
9
, Theorem A1]
. In other words, given a curve
c,
there is a neighborhood of each point
x\in c
that is hoemeomorphic to the open unit disk such that the image of
c
under that homeomorphism is the
x
-axis. This further implies that any two nonseparating curves can be taken to each other by a homeomorphism of the surface.

Despite curves themselves being tame, any two curves can have incredibly complicated intersection patterns. For example, two curves can intersect in at isolated points, in intervals, or in Cantor sets (just to name a few). Moreover, given parameterized unit square
I\times I
in
S
, there is a way to construct a curve in
S
such that every arc
\{t\}\times I
intersects this curve infinitely many times. As such, we will mainly use the fact that the intersection set of two curves is compact.

2.
Hyperbolicity of fine
k
-curve graphs

The goal of this section is to prove Theorem
1.1
. We will do this by induction on
k
and show that the fine
k
-curve graph is quasi-isometric to the fine
(k-1)
-curve graph. The base case of the fine
1
-curve graph and the fine curve graph is addressed in Proposition
2.1
. The inductive step is proven in Proposition
2.2
.

We first introduce some nomenclature for points of
u\cap v
that are not accumulation points for the set
u\cap v
. Two curves
u
and
v
are
touching
at a point
c\in u\cap v
if, in an arbitrarily small neighborhood
N
of
c,

u
and
v
can be isotoped to be disjoint. Otherwise,
u
and
v
are
crossing
at
c.
Examples of such intersections can be found on the right and left, respectively, of Figure
1
.

Figure 1
.
Left: a crossing intersection. Right: a touching intersection.

We now introduce several graph notations. If
\{v_{i}\}_{I}
are vertices of a graph
G
, we denote a (possibly infinite) path in
G
by
(\ldots,v_{j-1},v_{j},v_{j+1},\ldots).
We endow all graphs with the path metric, wherein the distance between two vertices is the length of a shortest path between them. We further parameterize all edges to have unit speed and length one. When considering quasi-isometries, we can work solely with vertices, as every point on an edge is distance at most
\frac{1}{2}
from a vertex.

Proposition 2.1

(Base Case)
.

Let
S_{g}
be a closed, orientable surface with
g\geq 2.
Then,
{\mathcal{C}^{\dagger}}(S_{g})
is quasi-isometric to
{\mathcal{C}^{\dagger}_{1}}(S_{g})
.

Proof.

Let
\iota:{\mathcal{C}^{\dagger}}(S_{g})\to{\mathcal{C}^{\dagger}_{1}}(S_{g})
be the natural inclusion map. Let
\df(\cdot,\cdot)
and
\dof(\cdot,\cdot)
denote distances in
{\mathcal{C}^{\dagger}}(S_{g})
and
{\mathcal{C}^{\dagger}_{1}}(S_{g}),
respectively.

We will show that
\iota
is a quasi-isometry with respect to
\df
and
\dof.
We will abuse notation and denote
\iota(u)
by
u
. Quasi-surjectivity is achieved since
\iota
is bijective on the vertices and every point on an edge is distance at most
\frac{1}{2}
from any vertex. Thus it is enough to check the distance conditions required by quasi-isometry by considering vertices.

First, for any two vertices
u
and
v,
we have that
\dof(u,v)\leq\df(u,v),
as the image of any path in
{\mathcal{C}^{\dagger}}(S_{g})
is a path of the same length in
{\mathcal{C}^{\dagger}_{1}}(S_{g}).
We now claim that
\df(u,v)\leq 2\dof(u,v)
.

Let
u
and
v
be vertices such that
\dof(u,v)=1.
If
u
and
v
are disjoint, we are done. If
u
and
v
are touching, isotope
u
off of itself and away from
v
to make
u^{\prime}.
We then have that
(u,u^{\prime},v)
is a path in
{\mathcal{C}^{\dagger}}(S_{g}),
so
\df(u,v)=2\leq 2\dof(u,v).
If
u
and
v
are crossing, then up to homeomorphism,
u
and
v
are the curves pictured in the left of Figure
2
. There is therefore a curve
w
in the subsurface not filled by
u
and
v.
An example of such a curve can be seen on the right of Figure
2
.

Figure 2
.
Any pair of crossing curves in
{\mathcal{C}^{\dagger}_{1}}(S_{g})
is, up to homeomorphism of
S_{g}
, equivalent to the two blue curves on the left. We may find a curve disjoint from both (such as the red curve on the right) outside the torus they fill.

We therefore conclude that, for all vertices
u
and
v
, we have
\frac{1}{2}\df(u,v)\leq\dof(\iota(u),\iota(v))\leq 2\df(u,v)
.
∎

With that in mind, we will now prove the inductive step.

Proposition 2.2

(Inductive Case)
.

Let
S_{g}
be a closed, orientable surface with
g\geq 2.
Then
{\mathcal{C}^{\dagger}_{k}}(S_{g})
is quasi-isometric to
\mathcal{C}^{\dagger}_{k+1}(S_{g})
.

Proof.

Let
\iota:{\mathcal{C}^{\dagger}_{k}}(S_{g})\to\mathcal{C}^{\dagger}_{k+1}(S_{g})
be the natural inclusion map. Let
\dkf(\cdot,\cdot)
and
\dkof(\cdot,\cdot)
denote distances in
{\mathcal{C}^{\dagger}_{k}}(S_{g})
and
\mathcal{C}^{\dagger}_{k+1}(S_{g})
respectively.

We will show that
\iota
is a quasi-isometry with respect to
\dkf
and
\dkof.
We will abuse notation and denote
\iota(u)
by
u
. Quasi-surjectivity follows as in the base case. It is enough to check the quasi-isometric embedding condition on vertices.

First, for any two vertices
u
and
v,
we have that
\dkof(u,v)\leq\dkf(u,v),
as the image of any path in
{\mathcal{C}^{\dagger}_{k}}(S_{g})
is a path in
\mathcal{C}^{\dagger}_{k+1}(S_{g}).
It remains to show that for any vertices
u
and
v
,
\dkf(u,v)\leq 3\cdot\dkof(u,v).

Suppose
u_{i}
and
u_{i+1}
are consecutive vertices along a geodesic path in
\mathcal{C}^{\dagger}_{k+1}(S_{g})
that intersect
k+1
times. We claim that
d_{k}(u_{i},u_{i+1})\leq 3.
We prove this via casework.

Case 1: Touching intersection.
Suppose
u_{i}
and
u_{i+1}
have a touching intersection. Isotope
u_{i}
in a neighborhood of this intersection to create
u^{\prime}_{i}.
We then have that
|u^{\prime}_{i}\cap u_{i+1}|=k,
so
u^{\prime}_{i}
and
u_{i+1}
are adjacent in
{\mathcal{C}^{\dagger}_{k}}(S_{g}).
It remains to find a length 2 path between
u_{i}
and
u^{\prime}_{i}.

Consider a regular neighborhood of
u_{i}\cup u^{\prime}_{i}.
Take
u^{\prime\prime}_{i}
to be a boundary component of this neighborhood that is essential in
S_{g}.
Thus,
u^{\prime\prime}_{i}
is disjoint from
u_{i}
and
u^{\prime}_{i}
and is therefore adjacent to both.

We conclude that
(u_{i},u^{\prime\prime}_{i},u^{\prime}_{i},u_{i+1})
is a path in
{\mathcal{C}^{\dagger}_{k}}(S_{g}).

Case 2: Bigons.
The proof of this case is similar to that of Case 1. The only difference is that we obtain
u^{\prime}_{i}
by isotoping
u_{i}
to remove an innermost bigon with
u_{i+1}
such that the isotopy is done in a neighborhood of the bigon. We therefore have that
|u^{\prime}_{i}\cap u_{i+1}|=k-1,
so
u^{\prime}_{i}
is adjacent to
u_{i+1}
in
{\mathcal{C}^{\dagger}_{k}}(S_{g}).
We obtain
u^{\prime\prime}_{i}
analogously to Case 1.

Case 3: Essential intersections.
We may now assume that
u_{i}
and
u_{i+1}
are in minimal position, as otherwise we could apply our work from cases 1 or 2. Thus, may construct a path of length two between
u_{i}
and
u_{i+1}
in
\mathcal{C}_{k}^{\dagger}(S_{g})
using conventional surgery techniques, such as those used for finding paths in the curve graph
\mathcal{C}(S)
(see for example Masur–Minsky
[
10
, Lemma 2.1]
).
∎

We are now ready to prove Theorem
1.1
.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

We proceed by induction. The fine curve graph is Gromov hyperbolic by Remark 3.2 and Theorem 3.8 of Bowden–Hensel–Webb
[
7
]
. By Proposition
2.1
, the fine curve graph is quasi-isometric to the fine 1-curve graph, so we have that the fine 1-curve graph is hyperbolic.

Suppose now that the
k
-curve graph is hyperbolic. By Proposition
2.2
, the
(k+1)
-curve graph is quasi-isometric to the fine
k
-curve graph and is therefore hyperbolic, as desired.
∎

3.
Diameter of finitary curve graphs

In this section, we prove that the finitary curve graph has diameter 2, which is the content of Theorem
1.2
. This implies that the finitary curve graph is quasi-isometric to a point (and therefore trivially hyperbolic). Along the way, we prove Proposition
3.4
about the existence of a curve (or arc) that intersects a collection of other curves (or arcs) finitely many times each.

Curves crossing an annulus.
Let
\nu
be an embedded annulus in
S_{g}
bounded by two disjoint homotopic curves
v
and
v^{\prime}
. A curve
u

crosses
\nu
at
u_{i}
if there exists a closed connected subset
u_{i}
of
u
such that
u\cap\nu
is a single connected component and
|u_{i}\cap v|=|u_{i}\cap v^{\prime}|=1.
Suppose
u
is the image of a map
f_{u}:[0,1]\to S_{g}
that is an embedding on
(0,1)
and
f(0)=f(1)
. If
u_{i}\cap\nu
is the image of
[t_{1},t_{2}]
, we call
f(t_{1})
the
starting point
and
f(t_{2})
the
endpoint
. Otherwise, if
u_{i}\cap\nu
is the image of
[0,t_{1}]\cup[t_{2},1]
, then
t_{2}
is the starting point and
t_{1}
is the endpoint.

Furthermore,
u

crosses

\nu

n

times
if there exist
n
(but not
n+1
) closed connected subsets
u_{i}
of
u
, pairwise disjoint except potentially at their boundaries, such that
u
crosses
\nu
at
u_{i}
for each
i.
Examples of crossings can be seen in Figure
3
. We note that, a priori,
n
can be infinity. The following lemma rules out this possibility.

Figure 3
.
We have an example of a curve
u
that crosses the annulus bounded by green curves
v
and
v^{\prime}
five times and forms two loops. The crossing strands are purple while the loops are burgundy.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\nu
be an embedded annulus in
S_{g}
bounded by two homotopic curves
v
and
v^{\prime}
. Then, an embedded curve
u
crosses
\nu
finitely many times.

Proof.

Suppose
u
crosses
\nu
infinitely many times. By compactness, there must be a sequence
\{p_{n}\}
of points in
u\cap v^{\prime}
that converges to some
p\in u\cap v^{\prime}
. There exists a subsequence
\{p_{n_{k}}\}
such that
p_{n_{1}},p_{n_{2}},\ldots,p_{n_{k}},\ldots,p
appear sequentially along both
u
and
v^{\prime}.
Fix a parametrization
f_{u}
of
u
such that
f_{u}^{-1}(p)=\{0,1\}
. We may again take a subsequence such that, up to reparametrization, each
p_{i}
is the endpoint of some
u_{i}.

Abusing notation, we will call this subsequence
\{p_{n}\}.
Let
q_{i}
be the starting point of the
u_{i}
for which
p_{i}
is an ending point. Thus, we have that
q_{1},p_{1},q_{2},p_{2},\ldots
appear sequentially along
u.
Define
t_{i}=f_{u}^{-1}(p_{i})
,
t=f_{u}^{-1}(p),
and
s_{i}=f_{u}^{-1}(q_{i}).
Up to taking a subsequence, we then have that
s_{1}<t_{1}<s_{2}<t_{2}<\cdots<s_{i}<t_{i}<\cdots
is a bounded strictly increasing real sequence that has a subsequence that converges to
t.
We then have that the entire sequence approaches
t
, so by continuity,
q_{i}=f_{u}(s_{i})\to p.
However,
\{q_{i}\}\subset v
, and there is a neighborhood of
v^{\prime}
disjoint from
v
, making this convergence a contradiction.

We conclude that
u
cannot cross
v
infinitely many times.
∎

A
loop of
u
in
\nu
is a connected, closed subset of
u
both of whose boundary points are in one of
v
or
v^{\prime}
and whose interior is disjoint from both
v
and
v^{\prime}.
In other words, a loop of
u
is a portion of
u
that bounds a bigon with
v
or
v^{\prime}.
Examples of loops can be seen in Figure
3
.

We now prove the following lemma, a direct consequence of Lemma
3.1
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\nu
be an embedded annulus in
S_{g}
bounded by two homotopic curves
v
and
v^{\prime}
and let
u
be a curve in
S_{g}.
Then, finitely many loops of
u
intersect any curve
v^{\prime\prime}
contained in the interior of
\nu.

Proof.

We first consider loops that intersect
v.
Applying Lemma
3.1
to the annulus bounded by
v
and
v^{\prime\prime},
we obtain that finitely many loops cross this annulus. Therefore, finitely many loops that intersect
v
also intersect
v^{\prime\prime}.

We repeat the above procedure with loops that intersect
v^{\prime}
, thus considering all loops of
u
in
\nu
that intersect
v^{\prime\prime}.

∎

We note that Lemmas
3.1
and
3.2
also work if
\nu
is a strip (a disk parameterized by
I\times I
where
I\times\{0\},I\times\{1\}\subset\partial S
and
v=\{0\}\times I
and

) and

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.
The key point of the proof of Theorem
1.2
is contained in Lemma
3.3
. Let
a
be a curve in
S
and
\alpha\subset a
a closed, connected subarc with nonempty interior. Define a

of
\alpha
to be a disk

such that the interior of
\alpha
is contained in the interior of

,
\alpha\cap\partial B=\partial\alpha,
and

One way to construct such a B is to take a union of 1)
\bigcup U_{x}
of simply connected neighborhoods

for

such that

is locally flat in
U_{x}
and

and 2) all disks bounded by
\overline{\bigcup U_{x}}.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
S
be any orientable surface. Let
\nu
be an embedded annulus in
S
(closed as a subset of
S
) and
\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{n}
a finite collection of curves and/or arcs in
S
such that
\cup\gamma_{i}\cap\nu
is a collection of arcs in
\nu
disjoint from each other except potentially: 1) in
\partial\nu
or 2) the
\gamma_{i}
may coincide for the entirety of a connected component of
\nu\cap\bigcup\gamma_{i}
. Then, there is a curve
w\subset\nu
such that
|w\cap\bigcup\gamma_{i}|<\infty.
In particular, we can choose
w
such that all intersections of
w
with each
\gamma_{i}
are crossing.

The same can be done with
\nu
a strip (disk) parameterized by
I\times I
where
I\times\{0\},I\times\{1\}\subset\partial S
and
v=\{0\}\times I
and
v^{\prime}=\{1\}\times I.

We note that if
\partial\nu
(if
\nu
is an annulus) or
\{0\}\times I
(if
\nu
is a strip) is essential in
S,
then the constructed
w
is essential in
S.

Proof.

Let
v
and
v^{\prime}
be the boundary curves of
\nu
if
\nu
is an annulus (or
v=\{0\}\times I
and
v^{\prime}=\{1\}\times I
if
\nu
is a strip). If either of
v
or
v^{\prime}
satisfy the conditions on
w,
we are done. Suppose neither is a suitable
w.
For the remainder of the proof, we will use the language of curves an annuli, but an identical argument works for strips and arcs.

For simplicity of notation, let
u=\bigcup\gamma_{i}.
Since all
\gamma_{i}
are disjoint or coincide in the interior of
\nu,
we may continue to use the terms “strand” and “loop” of
u
in
\nu,
which will apply to the relevant
\gamma_{i}
.

Let
v^{\prime\prime}
be a curve in the interior of
\nu
and orient
v,\ v^{\prime},
and
v^{\prime\prime}
in a compatible way with some pre-set orientation of
\nu.

We hope to construct a curve

4.

\mathbf{{\mathcal{C}^{\dagger}_{<\infty}}(S_{g})}

In this section, we will show that the flag complex induced by

In particular, let
\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{n}

\gamma
adjacent each
\gamma_{i}
, so the sphere is in the link of a vertex.

We define an

We will now define the set of “messy” intersections between a finite set of curves and arcs. Given curves or arcs
\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{n}
in a surface

we define
\mathcal{E}(\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{k})\subset S_{g,b}
in the following way. (We will abuse notation by treating the

both as injective maps from

or
I
into
\Sigma
and as the images of such maps; the use should be clear from context.)

Let
E=\{p\in S|p\in\gamma_{i}\cap\gamma_{j}\text{ for some }i,j\};
these are all the points of intersection between the arcs and curves. However, we only want the potentially messy intersections, not the intersections where two curves or arcs coincide for an open interval. Let
P_{i}=\partial\{\gamma_{i}^{-1}(p)|p\in E\}\subset I;
we note that
P_{i}
is empty if
\gamma_{i}
is disjoint from all other
\gamma_{j}
. Moreover,
P_{i}
is totally disconnected since it is the boundary of a subset of the interval. To get these points back on the surface, let

We then have the following result about any such

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\Gamma=\{\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{n}\}
be a collection simple closed curves and/or arcs in
\Sigma,
an orientable and potentially infinite-type surface. Then there is a curve
\gamma

Moreover, all intersections between
\gamma

Proof.

The goal is to construct a curve disjoint from the “messy” intersections between the existing curves and arcs. We claim that
\mathcal{E}
is compact (and therefore closed) and totally disconnected.

First,
\mathcal{E}
is a finite union of compact sets and is therefore itself compact. Second, suppose

is a connected component of

with more than one point. Then at least one of
\gamma_{i}(P_{i})
is not nowhere dense in

Let

be a connected open subset of

(via the subspace topology) in which

is dense. Note that

must have more than one point. Since

This contradicts the fact that

Since

is compact and totally disconnected, we have that

is the hyperbolic plane, which is simply connected, so

must be connected. We further have that the images of

comprise a collection of

coincide at any arc. Abusing notation, we call this collection of arcs

Let

be a curve in

By Lemma
3.4

\gamma.

∎

1.3
.

Let
\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{n}

By Proposition

Let

[

Let
\Phi:\Homeo(S_{g})\to\Aut{\mathcal{C}^{\dagger}_{<\infty}}(S_{g})

\Psi_{2}\circ\Psi_{1}=\Phi^{-1}.

Let

\displaystyle\Psi_{2}\circ\Psi_{1}\circ\Phi(\varphi)

\displaystyle=\Psi_{2}\circ\Psi_{1}(f_{\varphi}),\textrm{ where }f_{\varphi}\textrm{ permutes vertices as prescribed by }\varphi

\displaystyle=\Psi_{2}(\overline{f_{\varphi}})

\displaystyle=\Psi_{2}(\Psi_{2}^{-1}(\varphi))

\displaystyle=\varphi.

Conversely, let

\displaystyle\Phi\circ\Psi_{2}\circ\Psi_{1}(f)

\displaystyle=\Phi\circ\Psi_{2}(\overline{f})

\displaystyle=\Phi(\varphi_{\overline{f}}),\textrm{ where }\varphi_{\overline{f}}\textrm{ permutes curves as prescribed by }\overline{f}

\displaystyle=\Phi(\varphi_{f}),\textrm{ since }f\textrm{ and }\overline{f}\textrm{ permute vertices in the same way}
