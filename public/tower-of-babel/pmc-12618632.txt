The chain mediating roles of teacher−student relationships and perceived parental pressure between academic activities and self-efficacy in college students
Authors: Hui Wang, Zhiyi Li, Mengyao Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
While diverse academic activities are acknowledged to confer benefits on cognitive development of college students, their association with self-efficacy remains inconclusive in existing literature. This study investigates the relationship between academic activities and self-efficacy among Chinese undergraduates, with a focus on the mediating roles of teacher-student relationships and perceived parental pressure. Drawing on a sample of 1222 undergraduate students from a comprehensive university in China, we employed multiple linear regression to analyze the direct effects. Results showed that both academic activities and teacher-student relationships had positive effects on self-efficacy, while perceived parental pressure exerted a negative influence. The structural equation model and bootstrapped analyses (with 1000 resamples) revealed a sequential mediation pathway: Academic activities enhanced teacher-student relationships, which in turn reduced perceived parental pressure, ultimately improving self-efficacy. These findings underscore the cultural embeddedness of self-efficacy within the context of Chinese higher education. The study provides empirical evidence for educational policymakers to promote structured academic activities and foster supportive teacher-student relationships as effective strategies for enhancing undergraduates’ self-efficacy, while addressing the potential detrimental effects of excessive parental pressure.

Introduction

Self-efficacy serves as a protective resource for university students1,2, which not only contributes to overcoming life challenges but also preserves students’ agentic capacities3–5. Research has also found that self-efficacy could reshape health behaviors, enhance resilience, and promote confidence in complex tasks6,7, highlighting it as a crucial element in well-being. Furthermore, individuals with low self-efficacy tend to shirk responsibilities and exhibit reduced problem-solving abilities in workplace contexts8, ultimately reducing job satisfaction9,10. The positive roles of self-efficacy become particularly pronounced in the Chinese cultural, because university students face compounded pressures from parental expectations, exam-oriented education, and an increasingly competitive job market11.

There are various factors affecting students’ self-efficacy, such as psychological components, educational contexts, and social environments12,13. It is worth noting that extracurricular activities, serving as a dynamic integration platform, could harmonize these multifaceted predictors. Additionally, extracurricular activities are more cost-effective and acceptable to university students compared to professional training or psychological counseling. Therefore, exploring the relationships between extracurricular activities and self-efficacy has become a hot topic14.

Although prior studies have made some constructs regarding the impact of extracurricular activities on self-efficacy, several shortcomings persist. Firstly, there is incompleteness in related research. Extracurricular activities among university students can be categorized into non-academic (or social) and academic activities. Most existing studies predominantly focus on social activities rather than academic activities15,16. Secondly, there is a dearth of holistic discussions on the mechanism that integrates social and psychological pathways. Lastly, the cultural specificity of the Chinese context has been underexplored in extant studies. The collectivist ethos exerts a potent yet often unspoken group-level pressure on young adults. These stresses provide a critical perspective for examining the connection between academic engagement and self-efficacy among Chinese university students.

This study aims to elucidate the relationship between academic activities and university students’ self-efficacy, and reveal the mediating mechanisms based on social network characteristics. Specifically, this paper explores how academic activities influence self-efficacy through teacher-student relationships and perceived parental pressure. The manuscript is structured as follows. We first introduce the background and questions of this study. Secondly, the research hypothesis and the theoretical model are proposed through literature review and synthesis. The third section briefly describes the data and methods in the study. Subsequently, the results of the statistic model are interpreted. The conclusion section summarizes the paper, then we point out the limitations and suggest directions for future studies in the last part.

Literature review and theoretical model

Academic activities and self-efficacy

The scholars have not yet reached a consensus on the influence of academic activities on self-efficacy. Social cognitive theory posits that reinforcing cognition ability and adjusting self-evaluation through social interactions are the foundations for building self-efficacy17. Based on this, relevant research argues that academic activities (such as scientific research, competitions, and academic conferences) can enhance self-efficacy through mastery experiences and vicarious experiences18–20. Some empirical studies support the positive views21. Additionally, research on the social benefits of academic activities emphasizes the motivational effect of peer interactions22.

However, some research holds opposing viewpoints based on the negative experience. The learned helplessness theory suggests that repeated academic failures or negative feedback can lead students to develop a helplessness belief, thereby diminishing their self-efficacy23. If students attribute their academic failures to internal factors rather than external factors, their self-efficacy will be significantly weakened24.Excessive academic pressure can result in a decrease in self-efficacy, particularly in performance-oriented environments25. Similar conclusions have been drawn from studies on Chinese students26. Moreover, low-achieving students might experience self-doubt due to social comparisons in highly competitive environments, leading to a decrease in their sense of self-efficacy27,28.

While the extant literature presents divergent perspectives on the relationship between academic activities and self-efficacy, this study supports the positive association. This is grounded in two informed considerations. On the one hand, academic engagement is inherently open and multifaceted. A setback in one instance does not preclude success in the future. Moreover, the intellectual cultivation, cognitive growth, and spirit of collaboration experienced through academic activities endure as psychological assets. On the other hand, studies reporting detrimental outcomes predominantly emerge under socially extreme conditions, such as hyper-competitive environments or institutional pressures. This suggests that the observed negative correlation between academic activities and self-efficacy likely originates from deteriorating social contexts rather than inherent properties of scholarly activities. Therefore, the following hypothesis is proposed:

Hypothesis 1

Academic activities are positively associated with self-efficacy among university students.

Mediating role of teacher−student relationships

Academic activities create structured opportunities for sustained interaction between students and teachers, facilitating mutual learning and behavioral modeling. Through long-term cooperation, students gradually develop cognitive trust and emotional trust in teachers29. Furthermore, teacher guidance provides students with a sense of security for exploratory learning. Meanwhile, the experience of overcoming academic challenges triggers emotional resonance between teachers and students, strengthens emotional attachment, and subsequently enhances the quality of teacher-student relationships30.

Teacher-student relationships epitomize hierarchical social networks in a collectivist cultural framework. The Confucian adage “Once a teacher, forever a father” encapsulates both respect for pedagogical authority and inherent relational inequality31. However, extended academic collaboration functions as a relational equalizer. As mutual familiarity and interpersonal intimacy accumulate through scholarly co-creation, the psychosocial discomfort stemming from vertical hierarchy gradually diminishes. Consequently, the perceived power distance between students and teachers narrows, which enhances the satisfaction of students32.

Improved teacher-student relationships might lead to a marked increase in students’ self-efficacy. Frequent communication between teachers and students could help students to increase their knowledge and cognitive abilities. In terms of social support, positive teacher-student relationships and the consequent instrumental support can provide students with enough security and confidence in the face of setbacks33. Additionally, according to Maslow’s theory of needs, gaining social approval is a core objective in the pursuit of value34. In collectivist cultures, acknowledgment from teachers tends to generate spillover effects, which reinforces students’ self-efficacy. Based on the above discussion, this study proposes the following hypothesis 2.

Hypothesis 2

Academic activities enhance teacher-student relationships, thus improving self-efficacy among university students.

Mediating role of perceived parental pressure

In addition to direct effects, teacher-student relationships might indirectly affect students’ self-efficacy by alleviating the stress of parental expectations. The association between teacher-student relationships and parental pressure can be analyzed through role substitution and the development of coping strategies. College students are going through a critical transitional phase between familial dependence and societal autonomy35. This developmental stage generates a heightened demand for experienced guidance. However, many parents exhibit limitations in providing appropriate support, while simultaneously maintaining high expectations concerning their children’s educational attainment and professional success36. Parental expectations can exert tremendous pressure on young adults. In this circumstance, quality teacher-student relationships provide an effective outlet for alleviating familial pressures37.

Parental pressure might lead to a decline in self-efficacy. From the perspective of goal achievement, excessive parental expectations may surpass students’ capabilities, causing prolonged failure to meet these expectations. This situation produces self-doubt and diminishes self-efficacy as students internalize their inability to achieve imposed standards38. In the view of social comparison, exposure to parental pressure compels students into constant social comparisons with peers. When others outperform them, individuals experience eroded confidence39. Self-efficacy mainly depends on fulfilling needs for autonomy40,41. When parental expectations override student agency, students perceive specific academic goals as externally imposed, thus reducing their motivation. Additionally, persistent anxiety about disappointing parents or fearing failure consumes cognitive resources and undermines emotional well-being42. Therefore, mitigating these parental pressures could potentially restore students’ self-efficacy.

Hypothesis 3

Teacher-student relationships could alleviate perceived parental pressure, thus influencing self-efficacy.

Moderating effect of teacher−student relationships

The incentive effect of teacher-student relationships on self-efficacy may exhibit heterogeneity due to the frequency of academic activities. According to the substitution effect of social capital43, individuals with limited access to one type of social capital (e.g., peer networks) may rely more on alternative sources (e.g., teacher relationships) to sustain social support. Therefore, students who frequently participate in academic activities can accumulate more social capital and peer support, which buffer their dependence on teacher support.

In addition to this substitution effect, the inherent differences between teacher-student relationships and peer interactions may also contribute to the declining significance of teacher-student bonds. Compared to peer interactions, which typically occur in a relaxed setting, teacher-student exchanges often take place in a formal and structured environment. As a result, university students may derive greater benefits from their peers in such an informal and comfortable atmosphere. As a study in a Chinese high school found, peer interactions have a greater impact on student performance than teacher-student interactions among the different types of personal relationships44.

As discussed earlier, teachers primarily contribute to students’ development through knowledge dissemination, support provision, and social recognition. As the relative importance of teacher-student relationships diminishes in the high academic participation group, the influence of teachers’ vicarious experiences and verbal persuasion on self-efficacy may also decline. Accordingly, we propose the following Hypothesis 4.

Hypothesis 4

The association between teacher-student relationships and self-efficacy is stronger among the low academic participation group than the high academic participation group.

Theoretic model

This study aims to examine whether academic activities (individual behavior) affect university students’ self-efficacy (psychological outcomes) through teacher-student relationships and perceived parental pressure (social network characteristics). Both social cognitive theory and stress process theory posit that teacher-student relationships, and the consequent stress relief might mediate or moderate the association between academic activities and self-efficacy. Methods

Sample

The research data was derived from a survey conducted among undergraduates at a university in Shaanxi Province in December 2023, with ethical approval granted by the Ethics Committee of the School of Public Administration at Xi’an University of Architecture and Technology (XAUAT20231120). All participants provided written informed consent prior to their inclusion in the study. This institution was selected based on the richness and accessibility of its data resources. The university’s comprehensive academic disciplines, well-established specialization system, and diverse student demographics ensured sample representativeness. Additionally, we maintained close collaboration with the university staff, which facilitated survey implementation.

A stratified sampling method was employed in two stages: First, stratified sampling was conducted at a 10% sampling rate across academic departments and classes. And simple random sampling used to select specific classes. Subsequently, under the guidance of class instructors, all students from the selected classes completed the questionnaire. Among the 1,296 students surveyed, 1,222 participants completed the questionnaire, yielding a response rate of 94.3%. After excluding cases with missing values in key variables, the final valid sample size for this study comprised 1,206 respondents, accounting for one-twelfth of the college’s total undergraduate population. The sample consisted of 41% male students, 26% urban residents, and 50% STEM majors, with a distribution of 72% lowerclassmen (first- and second-year students) and 28% upperclassmen (third-year to fifth-year students). These demographic characteristics mirrored the overall composition of the university population, thereby affirming the scientific rigor of the sampling methodology and the robust representativeness of the sample.

Measurements

Dependent variables: self-efficacy

Self-efficacy was measured using the General Self-Efficacy Scale (GSES), a widely validated instrument in psychological research45. This scale comprises 10 items designed to assess participants’ belief in their capacity to manage some prospective situations. Responses were recorded on a 4-point Likert scale (1 = Not at all true to 4 = Exactly true). Total scores were calculated by summing all items, yielding a range from 10 to 40. The internal consistency reliability of this scale in the current study was satisfactory (Cronbach’s α = 0.958).

Independent variables: academic activities

Academic activities were assessed using questions related to academic engagement from Chinese College Student Experience Questionnaire (CCESQ) and National Undergraduate Education Quality Survey (Student Version, NUEQS)46,47. Academic activities referred to off-campus academic exchanges, collaborative research with faculty, competition participation, and journal article submissions. Responses were recorded on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = Never to 5 = Always). The score was created by summing all items (Cronbach’s α = 0.925), resulting in a range from 5 to 25. Higher scores indicated a greater frequency of academic participation behaviors.

Mediator variables: social network characteristics

Teacher-student relationships were assessed through a self-report scale adapted from CCESQ48. It examines respondents’ perceived quality of interactions with various teachers, including instructional teachers, student affairs staff, administrative teachers, and support staff. Participants rated each relationship dimension on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = Poor to 5 = Excellent). A composite teacher-student relationship index was created by summing all item scores (Cronbach’s α = 0.966). Higher scores indicated more positive relationships.

Perceived parental pressure was measured using a single-item question adapted from the Parental Expectations subscale of the College Stress Inventory49. Prior research has demonstrated that single-item measures are appropriate for assessing well-defined, narrow constructs50,51. The question asks respondents about the pressure they experience from their parents’ educational expectations. Participants rated this perception on a 5-point scale (1 = No pressure to 5 = Extreme pressure). This variable captured the intensity of perceived pressure from parental expectations, with higher scores indicating greater subjective pressure.

Control variables: socio-demographic indicators

Drawing on previous research52–54, this study included the following control variables: age (measured by years), gender (1 = male, 0 = female), ethnicity (1 = Han, 0 = ethnic minority), household registration (1 = urban, 0 = rural), political affiliation (1 = China Communist Party member, 0 = non-CCP), religious affiliation (1 = religious, 0 = non-religious). High school type (1 = key high school, 0 = other), major (1 = STEM disciplines, 0 = non-STEM disciplines), grade (1–5 scale, with 5 representing senior year), academic performance (1–5 scale, with higher values indicating better class ranking), household income (1–10 scale, with 10 indicating highest income).

Analytical strategy

First, descriptive analysis, kernel density estimation, and correlation analysis of core variables were utilized to grasp the respondents’ demographic profiles and assess the sample’s representativeness. Subsequently, this study employed stepwise multiple linear regression and robustness checks to examine the main effects. Building on this, generalized structural equation modeling was applied to test the mediating pathways. To further verify the statistical significance of these indirect effects, a bootstrap method with 1,000 resampling iterations was implemented. Using interaction terms, the Suest test, and the Chow test, we examined moderation effects in the final analysis.

Ethics approval.

This study has been approved by the Ethics Committee of the School of Public Administration at Xi’an University of Architecture and Technology (XAUAT20231120). The participants agreed to participate in the study through the informed consent process. The procedure has been reviewed by the Ethics Committee of the School of Public Administration at Xi’an University of Architecture and Technology and certified that the study was performed in full compliance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Model 2 incorporated academic activities as an extension of Model 1. The adjusted R² increased by nearly 15% compared to Model 1, indicating that academic activities constituted a key predictor of college students’ self-efficacy. These results further revealed a positive effect of academic activities on self-efficacy (β = 0.456, p < 0.001). Model 3 introduced the teacher-student relationships variable, demonstrating a positive correlation between teacher-student relationships and self-efficacy (β = 0.575, p < 0.001). Notably, the coefficient for academic activities in Model 3 decreased by 33% relative to Model 2, suggesting that student-teacher relationships partially mediated the influence of academic activities on self-efficacy.

Model 4 added the perceived parental pressure variable. Results indicated a negative correlation between perceived parental pressure and self-efficacy (β = −0.637, p < 0.001). A comparison between Model 4 and Model 3 showed that the coefficient for academic activities decreased from 0.305 to 0.289, while the coefficient for student-teacher relationships slightly declined from 0.575 to 0.570. The inclusion of perceived parental pressure as a predictor attenuated the direct explanatory power of both academic activities and teacher-student relationships on self-efficacy.

To mitigate potential endogeneity issues arising from cross-sectional data, we employed propensity score matching (PSM) and instrumental variable (IV) methods to test the robustness of the relationship between academic activities and self-efficacy (see Table 3). For the PSM analysis, we classified respondents with academic activities scores above 6 as treated. For IV estimation, we used two instruments, namely interest class before college (1 = yes, 0 = no) and parental supervision before college (1–5 scale, higher values indicate more frequent supervision). The results confirm that the positive effect of academic activities on self-efficacy is statistically robust.

Table 4 presents the bootstrap-based estimates of indirect effects. We assessed the significance of indirect pathways by examining whether the confidence intervals included zero. Tests of moderation effects

To examine the potential moderating effects, we employed a multi-method analytical approach incorporating interaction analysis, Suest tests for coefficient equality, and Chow tests for structural stability, with detailed results presented in Table 5. The empirical results indicated that this interaction did not reach statistical significance (t = 1.42, p = 0.157). Suest test statistics failed to reject the null hypothesis (χ² = 0.01, p = 0.905). Chow test results showed no evidence of structural breaks (F = 0.03, p = 0.855). This suggested that while both academic activities and teacher-student relationships independently contributed to self-efficacy, their combined effect did not operate synergistically in this model.

Discussion and conclusion

The purpose of this study was to examine whether the effects of academic activities on university students’ self-efficacy were mediated by teacher-student relationships and perceived parental pressure. The first hypothesis that academic activities are positively associated with self-efficacy among university students was supported. This result aligns with the previous studies that students who actively engage in academic activities had higher levels of self-efficacy55–57. It has shown that academic activities ought to be encouraged and promoted in higher education. However, it should be acknowledged that the difficulties and pressures inherent in academic activities objectively exist and cannot be overlooked. Especially in recent years, frequent news reports on depression, dropout, and even suicide incidents also demonstrate the adverse influence of high-pressure academic environments, the uncertainty associated with academic results, and their potentially low expected returns58–60. Therefore, our research findings only suggested that the positive incentives from academic activities may outweigh their negative effects.

The second hypothesis that academic activities enhance teacher-student relationships, thus improving self-efficacy among university students was supported. The positive connection between academic activities and teacher-student relationships is consistent with previous research61,62. Through continuous collaboration and interaction, the social distance between teachers and students is effectively narrowed, and their intimacy and trust are enhanced63. Teachers can provide both instrumental assistance and emotional support for their students, thereby improving self-efficacy. For Chinese university students, student-teacher interactions may demonstrate a more pronounced effect. Social approval from teachers has a stronger impact on students’ self-identity and self-efficacy64,65. Additionally, teacher-student relationships may act as a rehearsal for students’ interactions with other social ties in the future, such as superiors or leaders in labor fields. Some studies have confirmed that students with better teacher-student relationships show comparative advantages in workplace communication and have better job performance66,67.

The third hypothesis that teacher-student relationships indirectly influence self-efficacy by mitigating perceived parental pressure was supported. Compared to university students with poor teacher-student relationships, those experiencing positive relationships demonstrated lower perceptions of parental pressure, aligning with findings from prior research68,69. This finding showed distinctive value because parents and teachers constitute primary stressors for university students70,71. Through academic activities, we observed the tension dynamics and coupling mechanisms between school and family systems. This offers novel insights for subsequent explorations of university students’ development from a social network perspective.

Our fourth hypothesis that the association between teacher-student relationships and self-efficacy is stronger among low academic engagement group than high engagement group, was not supported. A potential explanation for this insignificant interaction effect may be the universal importance of teacher-student relationships for Chinese university students. Due to the resource advantage and the inherent authority of instructors, most students rely on teachers for valuable information, academic support, and career guidance72. Another contributing factor could be Confucian culture’s emphasis on reverence for pedagogical roles, which positive teacher-student relationships might reinforce undergraduate students’ motivations regardless of their participation levels. Moreover, academic activities and teacher-student relationships exert relatively independent direct effects on self-efficacy. Academic activities primarily enhance self-efficacy by enabling students to acquire knowledge and skills through practical experience, whereas teacher-student relationships operate more through emotional support and role modeling. These two factors function through distinct pathways rather than generating additional effects via intertwining mechanisms.

Beyond theoretical interpretation, measurement biases may also contribute to the non-significant moderation effect. First, there exists a range restriction issue. Although academic activity scores range from 5 to 25, the mean is 12 (SD = 5) with a concentrated distribution, potentially lacking enough variability. Second, temporal dynamics remain uncaptured. Student participation in academic activities typically exhibits temporal patterns (e.g., concentration during specific months), during which interactions between academic activities and teacher-student relationships might emerge. However, our single-timepoint measurement cannot detect such features.

Overall, the findings reveal that engagement in academic activities improves teacher-student relationships among undergraduates, which subsequently buffers parental pressure and enhances self-efficacy. These results carry important theoretical and practical implications. In individualistic cultures, self-efficacy tends to rely more on personal achievements and autonomy support73, where parental pressure is typically perceived as detrimental to both autonomy and self-efficacy74. In contrast, our study reveals parental pressure is transformed through improved teacher-student relationships, ultimately serving as an indirect channel for enhancing students’ self-efficacy. By examining the mechanism linking academic activities and self-efficacy through the lens of authority relationships, our findings contribute to diversifying both stress-coping theory and self-efficacy theory. To enhance academic engagement and self-efficacy among college students, universities should establish mentorship programs, integrate academic metrics into student development frameworks, and offer psychological support. Additionally, personalized plans should be tailored to student needs, which prioritizes research opportunities for practice-oriented learners and teacher-student interaction for those requiring emotional support.

Limitations and future studies

There are several limitations in this study. First, we cannot entirely rule out the possibility that students with higher self-efficacy may be more inclined to participate in academic activities due to the cross-sectional nature of our data. Although we have implemented multiple robust approaches including instrumental variable estimation and propensity score matching, this endogeneity issue necessitates more rigorous statistical inference in future research. Second, our sample primarily focused on undergraduate students and did not include graduate students. Given that graduate students engage in academic activities more frequently, this sampling limitation might result in a less comprehensive understanding of the connotations of academic activities.

Future research should address these limitations through both theoretical refinement and methodological advancement. Theoretically, although this study emphasizes teacher-student interactions in academic activities, it neglects the influence of peer social networks. Subsequent studies should incorporate student-student relationships into the analytical framework.

As for the endogeneity issue, improvements are needed in three aspects. Frist, the incorporation of more theoretically grounded instrumental variables (e.g., distance to academic hubs) could better satisfy exclusion restrictions. Second, longitudinal designs with cross-lagged panel models would help establish temporal precedence. Third, natural experiments comparing self-efficacy changes between research-intensive and control groups may provide stronger evidence.

Additionally, we recommend expanding sample coverage to include graduate students. In cases where this proves impractical, statistical techniques such as survey weighting adjustment or simulation-based sensitivity analysis could be employed.
