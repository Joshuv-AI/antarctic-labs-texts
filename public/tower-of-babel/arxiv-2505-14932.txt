FOL-Pretrain: A complexity annotated corpus of first-order logic
Isabelle Lee, Sarah Liaw, Dani Yogatama

Abstract
Transformer-based large language models (LLMs) have demonstrated remarkable reasoning capabilities such as coding and solving mathematical problems to commonsense inference. While these tasks vary in complexity, they all require models to integrate and compute over structured information. Despite recent efforts to reverse-engineer LLM behavior through controlled experiments, our understanding of how these models internalize and execute complex algorithms remains limited. Progress has largely been confined to small-scale studies or shallow tasks such as basic arithmetic and grammatical pattern matching. One barrier to deeper understanding is the nature of pretraining data—vast, heterogeneous, and often poorly annotated, making it difficult to isolate mechanisms of reasoning. To bridge this gap, we introduce a large-scale, fully open, complexity-annotated dataset of first-order logic reasoning traces, designed to probe and analyze algorithmic reasoning in LLMs. The dataset consists of 3.5 billion tokens, including 8.8 million LLM-augmented, human-annotated examples and 7.5 million synthetically generated examples. Each synthetic example is verifiably correct, produced by a custom automated theorem solver, and accompanied by metadata tracing its algorithmic provenance. We aim to provide a scalable, interpretable artifact for studying how LLMs learn and generalize symbolic reasoning processes, paving the way for more transparent and targeted investigations into the algorithmic capabilities of modern models.



1
Introduction

Large Language Models (LLMs) have shown impressive performance across a broad spectrum of tasks, ranging from natural language inference and commonsense reasoning to more formal domains like mathematical problem solving and algorithmic reasoning.
While such capabilities suggest that LLMs may engage in some form of reasoning, what this entails remains unclear—especially given that the concept of “reasoning” itself is poorly defined and contested in the literature
(
Bender and Koller 2020
)
.
The mechanisms underlying these abilities are not well understood, particularly the processes by which models acquire them during training.
This lack of undertanding poses challenges for both interpretability and control.
Recent studies highlight systematic limitations and error patterns in LLM reasoning behaviors
Tyen et al. 2024
;
Min et al. 2022
, casting doubt on the validity of existing benchmarks for assessing genuine reasoning abilities.
These challenges motivate a deeper inquiry into what reasoning means in the context of LLMs, and how it should be assessed.

Recent advances in reasoning-augmented language models—such as OpenAI’s O1
(
OpenAI 2024
)
, DeepSeek’s R1
(
DeepSeek-AI et al. 2025
)
, and the Qwen reasoning model
(
Bai et al. 2023
)
—have intensified interest in structured problem-solving within LLMs.
These models have shown strong performance on complex tasks, including AIME mathematical challenges
(
ValsAI 2025
)
and coding benchmarks
(
Chen et al. 2021
;
Jimenez et al. 2024
)
, often leveraging large-scale reinforcement learning and external verifiers.
A key strategy has been the use of long training sequences that include chains-of-thought (CoT) or reasoning traces
(
Wei et al. 2023
)
.
In addition to many advances occur during post-training, emerging research explores how reasoning mechanisms might be integrated into pretraining itself
(
Geiping et al. 2025
;
Tack et al. 2025
;
Ruan et al. 2025
)
.
Rather than viewing reasoning as a byproduct of scale alone, these approaches suggest it may be possible to guide and structure its development, enabling more systematic investigation into the mechanisms by which reasoning is acquired, and how it might be deliberately induced—if we better understood the relationship between reasoning and model training.

1.1
Background on interpretability and Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning

To better understand reasoning in LLMs, a plethora of recent work have turned to mechanistic explanations.

Lindsey et al. 2025
analyze model circuitry to trace internal reasoning patterns, though their findings are limited to carefully controlled settings.

Elhage et al. 2022
, and
Wang et al. 2022
identify components like induction heads and algorithmic circuits, offering insights into how models support multi-step reasoning.
However, other findings suggest that CoT traces may align poorly with actual inference dynamics.
While CoT prompting has improved performance on complex reasoning tasks
(
Wei et al. 2023
;
Kojima et al. 2023
)
, several studies demonstrate that its explanations can be unfaithful to the model’s true computational process
(
Turpin et al. 2023
;
Wei Jie et al. 2024
)
. Techniques such as self-consistency sampling
(
Wang et al. 2023
)
, contrastive CoT
(
Chia et al. 2023
)
, and faithful reasoning frameworks
(
Lyu et al. 2023
)
attempt to bridge this gap. Nonetheless, the central challenge remains: how to reliably relate surface-level reasoning traces to the internal mechanisms that actually drive model behavior.
Compounding this difficulty is the inherent ambiguity of natural language—what constitutes a “correct” or “faithful” explanation is often subjective and context-dependent.
As a result, widely agreed-upon standards for evaluating the correctness or validity of natural language reasoning remain elusive.

1.2
FOL and formal reasoning presents an opportunity

Formal reasoning offers a valuable opportunity to study the reasoning processes of language models. Unlike natural language reasoning—which is often ambiguous and difficult to verify—formal logic provides a structure that is both interpretable and, in many cases, verifiable. At the same time, purely symbolic tasks like solving math equations may be too constrained and lack the linguistic richness of natural language. First-order logic (FOL), in contrast, strikes a compelling balance: it can represent natural language-like structures while remaining algorithmically grounded. In particular, FOL is semi-decidable—there exists an algorithm to confirm when a formula is valid (i.e., a theorem), but no general procedure can confirm invalidity in all cases
(
Boolos et al. 2007
)
. This property makes it a tractable yet nontrivial domain for exploring reasoning behavior in models.

Building on this insight, we release a comprehensive, pretraining-scale dataset of first-order logic expressions. This dataset enables us to study not only how models learn to represent and manipulate logical reasoning during training, but also how they generate and follow reasoning traces at inference time. Importantly, first-order logic expressions naturally include structured reasoning steps that resemble chains of thought, while also maintaining a surface similarity to natural language syntax.

Our contributions

We summarize the key contributions of this work as follows:

1.

We release a fully open-access dataset of first-order logic expressions, with complete transparency on the generation process, including both human-curated and algorithmically verified components.

2.

Our dataset is unique in its scale—designed for pretraining—and is accompanied by meticulous annotations that trace the algorithmic provenance and structure of each example.

3.

We provide step-by-step reasoning traces for many examples, aligning with the growing interest in chain-of-thought (CoT) style supervision that has proven effective in enhancing reasoning in large language models.

2.2
Dataset generation

We constructed the dataset through a hybrid approach combining human-curated rules, automated symbolic rule generation, and LLM prompting. The detailed breakdown of the inference rules as well as human curated rules are shown in §
C
, §
E
, §
D
, and §

. Our process began with the collection of existing benchmarks for logical reasoning, which provided both direct training material and a basis for generating generalized logical proofs via LLMs.

To expand the diversity of logical forms, we developed templates representing canonical inference rules in first-order logic, including De Morgan’s laws, tautologies, and implications. These templates were instantiated with meaningful predicates using LLMs. For example, the general form

\neg(\alpha\lor\beta)\rightarrow\neg\alpha\land\neg\beta

(2)

was instantiated as

\neg(\text{Sunny}(x)\lor\text{Breezy}(x))\rightarrow\neg\text{Sunny}(x)\land\neg\text{Breezy}(x),

(3)

where
\text{Sunny}(x)
and
\text{Breezy}(x)
are predicates describing environmental properties. Such instantiations were generated by prompting GPT-4o with the abstract logical form and a set of domain-relevant predicate candidates.
Additionally, we used symbolic computation tools such as SymPy to synthesize first-order logic expressions, ensuring both syntactic correctness and logical validity. This multi-stage generation process enabled the creation of a high-quality, diverse dataset designed to evaluate and train models on robust logical reasoning tasks.

Finally, we generate chains of reasoning traces by sequentially linking the simplification steps. This process is akin to Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning, where each intermediate reasoning step builds upon the previous one, forming a structured sequence that leads to the final conclusion. However, unlike traditional reasoning traces, our approach ensures that each step is programmatically verified to be correct, leveraging SymPy’s symbolic computation capabilities. 4
Experiments

We conduct two studies: token-level evaluation and representation-level probing to measure how a 125M-parameter GPT-2 variant internalizes FOL. We train nanoGPT with 125M parameters,
Karpathy 2022
’s implementation of GPT-2-small, with 12 layers and 12 heads per layer. We pretrain from scratch on our FOL-Pretrain corpus. We used an embedding size of 768 and block size of 1024 tokens and a micro-batch size of 12, with gradient accumulation steps set to 40 to simulate a larger effective batch size. No dropout was applied during pre-training, and the AdamW optimizer is used with a learning rate of
1\times 10^{-4}
, weight decay of 0.1, and gradient clipping at 1.0. Learning rate included a warm-up phase over 400 iterations, with a decaying schedule until a minimum learning rate of
1\times 10^{-5}
, over a total of 20,000 iterations. We trained on 2 NVIDIA RTX A6000 for approximately 40 hours.

Representational evidence of syntactic awareness benchmark

Large-scale language models are often evaluated on downstream correctness, but end-task accuracy does not reveal whether an LLM’s hidden states encode the well-formedness of a formula. We therefore recast our original

Implications

These two experiments jointly show how a compact, transparent, perturbable FOL corpus is a scalable artifact for dissecting the symbolic competencies of LLMs. Embedding-level RSA exposes latent syntactic knowledge, even in small checkpoints, without any auxiliary classifier, while token-level accuracy shows how an LLM can internalize the mapping from raw FOL syntax to its semantic outcome under a generate-next-token objective. Thus, future work includes developing scaling laws for logic or extending the representational benchmark—once RSA confirms that validity is encoded geometrically, causal intervention tools can be used to localize where in the network that information first appears.

5
Conclusion

We present a dataset of verifiably correct first-order logic expressions as structured reasoning traces, generated through a combination of symbolic formula construction and rule-based simplification using SymPy. Unlike prior datasets that rely on heuristic or human-authored reasoning steps, our approach ensures that each transformation is programmatically validated for correctness, enabling fine-grained supervision for logical reasoning tasks. We analyze the distribution of rule complexities and reasoning trajectories, demonstrating a diverse and interpretable range of examples suitable for pretraining or evaluation. To establish an initial performance benchmark, we evaluate on both
True
/
False
classification and a syntactic-validity probe. In the latter, a GPT-2 small model pretrained on our corpus achieves an RSA correlation over twice that of a BERT baseline. Beyond these initial experiments, FOL-Pretrain offers a reproducible artifact for the community as the accompanying metadata enables grouping by complexity or rule type, and its programmatic generation ensures extensibility to richer logic or new transformation schemes. Finally, this dataset also provides a reliable foundation for benchmarking models on tasks that require multi-step, interpretable, and verifiable reasoning.

6
Limitations and Future Work

While this work provides a transparent, large-scale dataset for investigating algorithmic reasoning in language models, it has several limitations. First, the focus is on first-order logic, which, while expressive, does not capture the full range of reasoning phenomena found in naturalistic tasks. Second, although the dataset includes both human-annotated and synthetic data, the distributional alignment with real-world model pretraining corpora remains limited. Third, we do not perform comprehensive downstream evaluations of models trained on this dataset, which we leave for future work. Future work includes pretraining neural theorem provers (e.g.,
(
Yang et al. 2023
)
), on FOL-Pretrain and fine-tuning code LLMs (e.g.,
(
Jimenez et al. 2024
)
,
(
OpenAI et al. 2024
)
) thereby supplying premise selection priors, generating tactic suggestions, and improving logical consistency.

Broader impact

On the positive side, this dataset may support the development of more interpretable and controllable reasoning systems, serving as a tooling ecosystem for formal reasoning at scale. Such advances could benefit education, formal verification, and safety-critical AI applications. However, any improvements in symbolic reasoning capabilities also carry risks, such as enabling more persuasive misinformation generation or automating complex forms of manipulation. As with any pretraining-scale resource, ethical use depends heavily on how downstream applications are developed and deployed. We encourage responsible use and open evaluation of models trained on this dataset.

If two conditional statements are true, given a true

Constructive Dilemma (CD)

\begin{array}[]{c}((p\rightarrow q)\land(r\rightarrow s)),\\

If two conditional statements are true and at least one

\begin{array}[]{c}((p\rightarrow q)\land(r\rightarrow s)),\\

Guidelines:

The authors should reflect on how these assumptions might be violated in practice and what the implications would be.

The authors should reflect on the scope of the claims made, e.g., if the approach was only tested on a few datasets or with a few runs. In general, empirical results often depend on implicit assumptions, which should be articulated.

•

If the contribution is a dataset and/or model, the authors should describe the steps taken to make their results reproducible or verifiable.

•

Depending on the contribution, reproducibility can be accomplished in various ways. For example, if the contribution is a novel architecture, describing the architecture fully might suffice, or if the contribution is a specific model and empirical evaluation, it may be necessary to either make it possible for others to replicate the model with the same dataset, or provide access to the model. In general. releasing code and data is often one good way to accomplish this, but reproducibility can also be provided via detailed instructions for how to replicate the results, access to a hosted model (e.g., in the case of a large language model), releasing of a model checkpoint, or other means that are appropriate to the research performed.

•

While NeurIPS does not require releasing code, the conference does require all submissions to provide some reasonable avenue for reproducibility, which may depend on the nature of the contribution. For example

(a)

If the contribution is primarily a new algorithm, the paper should make it clear how to reproduce that algorithm.

(b)

If the contribution is primarily a new model architecture, the paper should describe the architecture clearly and fully.

(c)

If the contribution is a new model (e.g., a large language model), then there should either be a way to access this model for reproducing the results or a way to reproduce the model (e.g., with an open-source dataset or instructions for how to construct the dataset).

(d)

We recognize that reproducibility may be tricky in some cases, in which case authors are welcome to describe the particular way they provide for reproducibility. In the case of closed-source models, it may be that access to the model is limited in some way (e.g., to registered users), but it should be possible for other researchers to have some path to reproducing or verifying the results.

Experiment statistical significance

Answer:
[Yes]

Justification: The results are accompanied by error bars, confidence intervals, or statistical significance tests, at least for the experiments that support the main claims of the paper.

Guidelines:

•

The answer NA means that the paper does not include experiments.

•

The authors should answer "Yes" if the results are accompanied by error bars, confidence intervals, or statistical significance tests, at least for the experiments that support the main claims of the paper.

•

The factors of variability that the error bars are capturing should be clearly stated (for example, train/test split, initialization, random drawing of some parameter, or overall run with given experimental conditions).

•

The method for calculating the error bars should be explained (closed form formula, call to a library function, bootstrap, etc.)

•

The assumptions made should be given (e.g., Normally distributed errors).

•

It should be clear whether the error bar is the standard deviation or the standard error of the mean.

•

It is OK to report 1-sigma error bars, but one should state it. The authors should preferably report a 2-sigma error bar than state that they have a 96% CI, if the hypothesis of Normality of errors is not verified.

•

For asymmetric distributions, the authors should be careful not to show in tables or figures symmetric error bars that would yield results that are out of range (e.g. negative error rates).

•

If error bars are reported in tables or plots, The authors should explain in the text how they were calculated and reference the corresponding figures or tables in the text.
