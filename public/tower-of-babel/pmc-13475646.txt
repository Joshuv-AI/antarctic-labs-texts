Gut protist diversity in Eurasian beavers (Castor fiber) revealed by metabarcoding
Authors: Ekaterina Kandaurova, Jiří Novák, Jan Náhlovský, Aneta Perglerová, Joshua Benjamin Jewell, Aleš Vorel, Pavel Munclinger, Pavla Tůmová, Vladimír Hampl
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGut protists are an important part of the microbial community that is often overlooked or limited to certain “flagship” species, mainly parasites. This also applies to Eurasian beavers (Castor fiber), which act as aquatic engineers in riparian habitats and have been identified as potential reservoirs of the well-known intestinal protist parasite – Giardia intestinalis. In this study, we focus on exploring protistan diversity in Eurasian beavers from freshly collected stool samples using molecular methods, including targeted barcoding of specific parasitic groups (Giardia, Cryptosporidium and Microsporidia) and general metabarcoding of the V4 region of 18S rRNA on the Illumina MiSeq platform. Two individuals were tested positive for Cryptosporidium spp. using the targeted barcoding. The metabarcoding approach revealed that the gut community was predominantly composed of gut commensals, Blastocystis, and trichomonads, found in every individual. More detailed investigation of Blastocystis confirmed the potential existence of a specific beaver subtype. Colpodellids and parasitic Eimeria spp. were only occasionally present. Geographic location of the host, unlike sex, has a significant impact on gut microbiota composition, though more research is needed due to limited sampling. Finally, we tested a new primer set for amplifying the V4 region of 18S rRNA, which shows promise for improving the detection of Metamonada protists in the gut samples.Keywords: Amplicon sequencing, 18S-V4, Giardia intestinalis, Cryptosporidium spp., Microsporidia, Blastocystis, Intestinal protists

Introduction

The Eurasian beaver (Castor fiber) is a semi-aquatic herbivore and the largest rodent in Europe, highly adapted to life in riparian environments, where it plays an important ecological role as an ecosystem engineer by its unique ability to alter landscapes by falling trees and constructing dams and lodges. It is closely related to the North American beaver (Castor canadensis), but it differs in having more elongated skulls with triangular nasal openings and, genetically, in a different number of chromosomes (Eurasian = 48 pairs, North American 40 pairs). Historically, the Eurasian beaver was widespread across Europe and parts of Asia, but by the early 20th century, its populations had been decimated by overhunting for fur and castoreum (Müller-Schwarze, 2011; Rosell and Campbell-Palmer, 2022). Following decades of protection and coordinated reintroduction efforts, C. fiber has made a remarkable recovery and is now considered one of Europe's most notable conservation successes. Continental estimates suggest that the total number of beavers reached approximately 1,5 million individuals by 2020 (Halley et al., 2021). The beaver population in the Czech Republic has been restored through natural re-colonisation with immigration from Austria, Bavaria, Saxony, and Poland, and targeted reintroduction since 1990 (Munclinger et al., 2022).

Beavers are obligate herbivores with seasonal feeding strategies. During winter months, woody plants comprise approximately 70% of their diet, while in summer herbaceous vegetation constitutes up to 90% (Mikulka et al., 2022). An important aspect of the beaver's ecology is its habit of defecating directly into water. This practice, unusual among large terrestrial mammals, creates a direct pathway for the gut microbiota and potential pathogens to enter aquatic ecosystems. This behaviour may influence the composition of microbial communities in aquatic ecosystems and contribute to the transmission of waterborne zoonotic diseases. The North American beaver is considered to be a potential reservoir host of Giardia intestinalis assemblages A and B infective for humans, although the direct connection with human infections has not been proven (Heyworth, 2016; Prystajecky et al., 2015; Sulaiman et al., 2003), in contrast to a direct zoonotic transmission from chinchillas (Tůmová et al., 2018). In the faecal samples of Eurasian beavers, the cysts of Giardia (Bystrianska et al., 2021; Turillazzi et al., 2024), Blastocystis (Šmigová et al., 2025), and oocysts of Cryptosporidium (Bystrianska et al., 2021; Goodman et al., 2017; Šmigová et al., 2025) were detected by microscopy. Additionally, DNA of Cryptosporidium proliferans, of a new subtype (ST) of Blastocystis sp., and of two species of microsporidia (Enterocytozoon bieneusi (genotype D) and Encephalitozoon intestinalis) was detected by PCR (Šmigová et al., 2025). These findings suggest that the Eurasian beavers may serve as a reservoir for some of these parasites. Eimeria sprehnii, originally described in the North American beaver (Ernst et al., 1970), has also been found in Eurasian beavers' samples in Poland, sometimes causing severe infections (Demiaszkiewicz et al., 2014; Drózdz et al., 2004). Finally, the cited studies also reported several trematode, cestode, and nematode species in the faecal samples of these rodents.

Amplicon-based metabarcoding serves as a cost-effective screening tool applicable to intestinal content and faecal samples (Stensvold, 2024). A study by Pratama et al. (2019) used Illumina MiSeq sequencing of the V3 and V4 variable regions of the 16S rRNA gene to profile bacterial communities throughout the gastrointestinal tract of three specimens of wild Eurasian beavers. The intestine was dominated by members of the phyla Firmicutes (41.2%) and Actinobacteria (23.6%), followed by Proteobacteria, Verrucomicrobia, Fusobacteria, and Bacteroidetes. This contrasts with the microbiome composition of Mongolian beavers (Chu et al., 2025), North American beavers and other herbivores (Gruninger et al., 2016; Wong et al., 2016, 2017), which is dominated by the phyla Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes. Firmicute families Ruminococcaceae and Lachnospiraceae, occurring primarily in the caecum and colon, are likely involved in the degradation of lignocellulose. In contrast, the eukaryotic component of the gut microbiome, often referred to as an eukaryome, has not been metabarcoded in either beaver species. This is despite the fact that protists are important members of intestinal ecosystems, acting not only as parasites but also as commensals and mutualists, often involved in the digestion of a cellulose-rich diet (Brune and Dietrich, 2012; Newbold et al., 2015) and shaping the prokaryotic component of the microbiome. Yet some of these prominent protist groups might be overlooked and remain undetected (Mann et al., 2019; Owens et al., 2024).

In this study, we performed amplicon-based metabarcoding using the V4 region of the eukaryotic 18S rRNA, together with group-specific adjustments of this marker to improve the detection, from freshly collected stool samples of two populations of European beaver in the Czech Republic. This non-invasive approach provides a first look into the eukaryome of the large intestine and its variability among individuals, sexes, and populations. Additionally, the presence of Giardia, Cryptosporidium and Microsporidia was assessed using taxon-specific barcoding.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

All samples collected in the study were approved by regional nature protection authorities of the Czech Republic (acts. no.: Šumava SZ NPS 03665/2022/6, and Ústecký Kraj KUUK/072360/2025 and KUUK/151075/2020) and followed the handling protocol for trapped animals approved by the Ministry of Environment (no. MZP/2022/630/1956).

Samples

Faecal samples were collected in two different geographical areas, Western (N = 35) and Northern (N = 5) Czechia. The Western Czech population originates from reintroduction events conducted in Bavaria from 1966 to 1991, using beavers from various sources (Šimůnková and Vorel, 2015) thus exhibiting an admixed genetic signal from relict populations in France, Norway and Eastern Europe (Frosch et al., 2014; Munclinger et al., 2022). The Northern Czech population comprises beavers from the expanding relict Elbe population, with a significant depletion of genetic variability (Munclinger et al., 2022). The majority of samples (37) were freshly collected from live-captured animals (the trapping procedures are described in Horníček et al. (2021) and Korbelová et al. (2016)) as part of field monitoring and telemetry research conducted by the Czech University of Life Sciences in Prague in the years 2022 and 2023. An additional three samples were obtained from road-killed animals. Faeces from live-trapped beavers were obtained by gently palpating the rectum, and faeces of road-killed beavers were extirpated from the rectum. Samples were put into a 1.5 ml tube with 96% ethanol and stored in a freezer (−20°C). The information on the samples is provided in Supplementary Table S1.

DNA isolation

The DNeasy PowerSoil Pro Kit (Qiagen, Germany) was used for DNA extraction. Because the samples were previously stored in ethanol, the faecal material was first dried at 37°C for 10 min. About 250 mg of dried faecal material was transferred into PowerBead Pro tubes with beads and lysis buffer (CD1). Mechanical disruption was performed using a FastPrep-24™ 5G bead-beating grinder and lysis system, applying the “faeces” program twice to ensure thorough cell lysis. After lysis, the manufacturer's protocol was followed, and DNA was eluted in a final volume of 40 μL with two sequential 20 μL aliquots of elution buffer (C6) to maximise recovery. The concentration of the extracted DNA was measured with a Quantus™ Fluorometer (Promega, WI, United States) (Supplementary Table S1). Samples had been then diluted to a concentration of 1 ng/μL. Additionally, pools for male and female individuals were prepared, as well as a total pool of all individuals.

Marker selection, evaluation and enrichment

As a primary marker, the amplicon of the V4 region by the combination of TAReuk454FWD1 and TAReukREV3 primers (Stoeck et al., 2010) was used, hereafter TarEuk. To improve the detection of elusive groups, four new primer pairs were designed to target V4 regions of Archamoebae (set Arch), Metamonada (sets M1 and M2), and Microsporidia (set Micro) (Supplementary Table S2). For the primer design, we used alignments of targeted protist groups and focused on the protist groups that are expected to be present in the intestine, neglecting the detection sensitivity of non-intestinal protists. These primer sets were tested in silico using the PR2 PrimerEvaluation tool and the TestPrime v1.0 function with the SILVA database. Primers were later modified to include overhang adapters at their 5′ ends, enabling the attachment of NEBNext Illumina index primers (New England Biolabs, MA, United States). TarEuk primers were ultimately prepared in 4 modifications, each including 0-3 N’ phasing (Wu et al., 2015) between the adapter and primer sequence.

Amplicon libraries and sequencing

To avoid over-amplification and to decrease the PCR bias, the optimal number of cycles for each sample was determined by qPCR. The qPCR reaction mix contained 10 μl of 2x Q5 polymerase master mix (New England Biolabs, MA, United States), 1 μl of each forward and reverse primer mix (0.1 μM final concentration), 2 μl of Green-Fluorescent DNA Stain (1 μM final concentration) (Jena Biosciences, Germany), 5 μl of nuclease-free water, and 1 μl (1 ng) of genomic DNA. The thermal protocol consisted of the following steps: 98°C for 30 s, followed by 35 cycles at 98°C for 10 s, 53°C for 30 s, and 72°C for 20 s, followed by the final extension at 72°C for 2 min. The optimal number of amplification cycles was estimated visually by recording the start of the exponential phase of the qPCR curve (Supplementary Table S1), and then this number was used in the primary PCR.

To prepare amplicon libraries for Illumina sequencing, a two-step PCR-based approach was employed (Bourlat et al., 2016). The reaction mix for the primary PCR contained 10 μl of 2x Q5 polymerase master mix, 1 μl of each forward and reverse primer mix (0.1 μM final concentration), 7 μl of nuclease-free water, and 1 μl (1 ng) of genomic DNA. The thermal protocol remained the same as for qPCR, but with the sample-specific number of cycles.

The products of the primary PCR were used as the template in the secondary PCR, in which the primers complementary to overhangs were used, and unique dual-index (UDI) sequences and Illumina adapters were attached to the amplicons (New England Biolabs, MA, United States). The PCR reaction mix contained 10 μl of 2x Q5 polymerase master mix, 2 μl of UDI primers, 6 μl of nuclease-free water, and 2 μl of products from the primary PCR. The thermal protocol remained mostly the same as for qPCR, but the annealing temperature was set to 65°C, and the number of cycles was 10.

The amplicon libraries were purified using SPRI magnetic beads (Beckman Coulter, IN, United States) following the manufacturer's protocol. The DNA concentration of each library was then measured using a Quantus™ Fluorometer (Promega, WI, United States). The libraries were pooled, and their fragment size distribution within these pools was assessed using the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent, CA, United States) using the High Sensitivity DNA Assay. The final pool included 48 libraries (40 individual sample libraries prepared by general eukaryotic primers, eight libraries from male and female pools prepared by group-specific primers), and it was sequenced on the Illumina MiSeq platform with the MiSeq Reagent Kit v2, employing a 2×250 bp paired-end configuration at the Biocev Core Facility (OMICS-Genomics Biocev, Vestec, Czech Republic).

Amplicon data processing

Sequencing read quality was assessed using FastQC (v0.11.8) (Andrews, 2010). Raw reads were then trimmed using cutadapt (Martin, 2011) to remove primer sequences and filtered for quality using fastp (Chen et al., 2018) with a minimum Phred threshold of 20. Trimmed sequences for individuals were processed separately from the group-specific pools, but all samples were processed in the R environment version 4.3.1 (R Core Team, 2026) within RStudio IDE (Allaire, 2012). The Amplicon Sequence Variants (ASVs) were inferred using the DADA2 pipeline (dada2 package) (Callahan et al., 2016). Reads were filtered under the following parameters: truncQ = 2; truncLen = c(229,226); maxEE = c(2,2), minLen = 175. ASVs were then inferred using the pool = “pseudo” option and then merged with minOverlap = 20. Chimaeras were removed using the removeBimeraDenovo function implemented in the package with default parameters. Taxonomic classification of ASVs was performed by using an RDP naïve Bayesian classifier implemented as the assignTaxonomy function in the DADA2 package (Callahan et al., 2016). The classifier was trained on the PR2 (version 5.0.0) (Guillou et al., 2013) reference database formatted for DADA2. Additionally, to compare the effectiveness of primers in detecting Metamonada species, Metamonada reads from all primer sets were clustered using CD-HIT-EST (Li and Godzik, 2006) with the parameter c = 1 (100% identity). This step was done to group identical sequences, which differed in length due to amplification by different primer pair.

Phylogenetic placement

To verify the affiliation of ASVs, phylogenetic placement was performed by mapping sequence variants onto reference trees. ASVs classified with a bootstrap support value > 70 at the Subdivision level were placed on the reference trees of respective groups: Apicomplexa + Chrompodellida, Bigyra (Blastocystis), Ciliophora and Parabasalia. The remaining ASVs were placed on a global eukaryotic reference tree. The global reference alignment and tree were adopted from a previous study (Novák et al., 2024). The group-specific alignments and trees were constructed de novo. The curated 18S database of Blastocystis subtypes (Cabodevilla and Caro, 2024; Caro et al., 2024) enriched by the partial Blastocystis sequence from a beaver host from Slovakia (Šmigová et al., 2025) and the full-length sequences PZ726876 and PZ726877 generated in this study were used for Blastocystis placement. For the remaining three groups, the reference sequences were selected from the PR2 database. These were subsequently aligned by MAFFT v.7.516 (Yamada et al., 2016), trimmed by TrimAl (Capella-Gutiérrez et al., 2009), and maximum-likelihood phylogenetic trees were inferred using IQ-TREE v2.8.2 (Minh et al., 2020) with -m MFP option (Kalyaanamoorthy et al., 2017) and 1000 ultrafast bootstraps (Hoang et al., 2018). Then, ASVs were added into the reference alignments using MAFFT with the --addfragments strategy. Resulting alignments were trimmed with TrimAl (--gt 0.01) to remove poorly aligned positions, and the Evolutionary Placement Algorithm (EPA) option of RAxML v.8.2.12 (Stamatakis, 2014) was performed with the GTRCATI model. Phylogenetic placements with confidence scores were focused using the GAPPA (Czech et al., 2020) tool by accumulating likelihood-weight ratios (LWRs). ASVs with a cumulative LWR ≥ 0.80 were retained for downstream interpretation. This filtering criterion was uniformly applied across both global and group-specific placements to eliminate uncertain or ambiguous assignments. Trees were visualised in iTOL (Letunic and Bork, 2024).

Microbiome components delineation

To simplify the biological interpretation of the dataset, ASVs were categorised by presumed biological origin. This categorisation was performed based on the taxonomy and the ecological context. The categories were as follows: 1) Prokaryota – ASVs classified as Bacteria or Archaea, regardless of bootstrap support; 2) Metazoa – ASVs with bootstrap values > 70 classified under the Metazoa subdivision; 3) Food – ASVs with bootstrap values > 70 classified under the Embryophyceae class; 4) Fungi – ASVs with bootstrap values > 70 belonging to the Fungi subdivision; 5) Intestinal protists – ASVs with bootstrap values > 70 classified under one of six selected eukaryotic subdivisions, Apicomplexa (excluding Gregarinomorphea), Bigyra, Ciliophora, Fornicata, Parabasalia, and chrompodellids; 6) Low-confidence – Eukaryotic ASVs with bootstrap values ≤ 70 at the subdivision level; 7) contamination – ASVs with bootstrap values > 70 assigned to groups other than those mentioned above. The last category was primarily composed of three ecological types: (i) gregarines (Gregarinomorphea), which are parasites of invertebrates such as insects; (ii) Chlorophyta environmental algae, likely introduced through food, soil, or water; and (iii) various free-living protists such as cercozoans (e. g., Sandonidae, Glissomonadida), commonly found in terrestrial and aquatic environments. After the phylogenetic placement, all ASVs classified as Ciliophora were transferred to the “contamination” category, as they all clustered with free-living Ciliophora species. Further, the global placement of most “low-confidence” ASVs did not resolve their affiliations, as they were placed close to the root. In the case of “low-confidence” ASVs that were placed near terminal nodes, their assignment was further validated by BLAST against the NCBI nucleotide NR database. This approach helped to recognise one ASV (TarEuk_seq670) as a member of Colpodellida and was therefore reclassified to the “intestinal protist” category.

Statistical analysis

To investigate the alpha diversity between localities and sex, samples were first sorted, and all samples containing fewer than 1000 reads were removed, leaving 29 samples. Then the samples were rarified to the minimum number of reads (n = 1038). To assess the normality of the data, the Shapiro-Wilk test was applied. Species richness and evenness were then calculated, and the differences between datasets defined by sex or geographical area were tested using a t-test or a Wilcoxon test (non-parametric equivalent). For the multivariate analysis, the data were Hellinger-transformed, and compositional differences between samples were visualised using non-metric multidimensional scaling analysis (NMDS) based on the Bray-Curtis dissimilarity. For the significance testing, we used PERMANOVA with 999 permutations. All analyses and visualisations were conducted using R v.4.3.1 (R Core Team, 2026) with phyloseq (McMurdie and Holmes, 2013), ggplot2 (Wickham, 2016), eulerr (Micallef and Rodgers, 2014), vegan (Oksanen et al., 2024), microbiome (Lahti and Shetty, 2017) and rstatix (Kassambara, 2023) packages.

Barcoding of specific pathogens

To determine the presence of Giardia in beaver faecal samples, a combination of nested PCR on two markers was used - beta-giardin (bg) and triosephosphate isomerase (tpi), designed to amplify A-F Giardia assemblages (Lecová et al., 2018, 2020). Positive controls comprised DNA isolated from trophozoite cultures of WBc6 (assemblage A) and isolate 49 (assemblage B) (Lecová et al., 2018). The PCR reaction mix was done according to Perglerová et al. (2026) as follows: PCR mix contained 2.5 μl of 10x Taq polymerase buffer, 1.25 μl DMSO, 2.0 μl of 25 mM MgCl2, 0.5 μl of 10 mM (each) dNTP Mix, 0.625 μl of 10 mM of each forward and reverse primer, 0.1 μl of Taq polymerase (5 U/μl) (New England Biolabs, MA, United States), 15.4 μl of nuclease-free water, and 2 μl of template DNA. The thermal protocol consisted of the following steps: 94°C for 5 min, followed by 35 cycles at 94°C for 30 s, annealing temperature for 30 s, and 72°C for 60 s, followed by the final extension at 72°C for 10 min. The following annealing temperatures were used: 65°C and 55°C for bg in the first PCR and second PCR, and 50°C for tpi in both PCRs. Samples with potential Giardia positive signals were also evaluated by qPCR. The qPCR reaction mix contained 12.5 μl of 2x SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix (Thermo Scientific, MA, United States), 1 μl of 10 mM of each forward and reverse primer, 8.5 μl of nuclease-free water, and 2 μl of template DNA, following the thermal protocol: 95°C for 5 min, followed by 40 cycles at 95°C for 15 s, and 60°C for 30 s for annealing/extension.

To detect Microsporidia, a segment of ribosomal DNA was amplified using microsporidia-specific primers (Vossbrinck et al., 1993). Genomic DNA isolated from Encephalitozoon cuniculi cultures was used as a positive control (1 ng in reaction). The PCR reaction mix contained 7.5 μl of EmeraldAmp MAX HS PCR Master Mix (Takara Bio, Japan), 1 μl of 10 mM of each forward and reverse primer, 4.5 μl of nuclease-free water, and 1 μl of template DNA. The thermal protocol consisted of the following steps: 98°C for 3 min, followed by 35 cycles at 98°C for 10 s, 55°C for 30 s, and 72°C for 60 s, followed by the final extension at 72°C for 5 min.

For barcoding of Cryptosporidium spp., a small part of the 18S rDNA was amplified using Cryptosporidium-specific primers (Kuzehkanan et al., 2011). Positive controls were performed with genomic DNA isolated from cultured Cryptosporidium spp. (1 ng in reaction). The PCR reaction mix contained 7.5 μl of EmeraldAmp MAX HS PCR Master Mix (Takara Bio, Japan), 1 μl of 10 mM of each forward and reverse primer, 4.5 μl of nuclease-free water, and 1 μl of template DNA. The thermal protocol consisted of the following steps: 98°C for 3 min, followed by 35 cycles at 98°C for 10 s, 60°C for 30 s, and 72°C for 60 s, followed by the final extension at 72°C for 5 min. The results of all group-specific PCRs were visualised with gel electrophoresis (1.5% gel). The presence of Cryptosporidium was verified by Sanger sequencing of the suspect bands.

Additionally, to facilitate assigning the Blastocystis sp. ASVs, the full-length of the 18S rDNA was amplified similarly to Hernández et al. (2021). The first PCR reaction contained 10 μl of 2x Q5 polymerase master mix (New England Biolabs, MA, United States), 1 μl of 10 mM of each forward (RD5) and reverse (Blasto18SR) primer, 7 μl of nuclease-free water, and 1 μl of genomic DNA pool from all individuals (6 ng/μL). The thermal protocol consisted of the following steps: 98°C for 30 s, followed by 32 cycles at 98°C for 10 s, 62°C for 30 s, and 72°C for 2 min, followed by the final extension at 72°C for 2 min. The PCR products were subcloned and transformed into E. coli. Colony PCR using nested Blastocystis-specific primers (RD5 and BhRDr) was used to select clones originating from Blastocystis. The reaction mix contained 7.5 μl of EmeraldAmp MAX HS PCR Master Mix (Takara Bio, Japan), 1 μl of 10 mM of each primer, 4.5 μl of nuclease-free water, and 1 μl of bacterial DNA. The thermal protocol consisted of the following steps: 98°C for 3 min, followed by 35 cycles at 98°C for 10 s, 60°C for 30 s, and 72°C for 60 s, followed by the final extension at 72°C for 5 min. Inserts from positive colonies were sequenced in full length.

Results

The overall eukaryome diversity in the stool samples of 40 Eurasian beavers was characterised by using general eukaryotic (TarEuk) primers. The quality filtering yielded 1,583,499 reads (∼40,000 per sample) and 1,246 unique ASVs (Supplementary Table S3). The ASVs were categorised based on taxonomy and ecological context into seven categories (Supplementary Table S4) using criteria described in Methods. The affiliation of ASVs from the category “intestinal protists” was validated through phylogenetic placements on the reference trees of the respective groups (Figs. S1–S4), and ASVs from the category “low-confidence” were inspected by placement in the global reference tree (Fig. S5). The phylogenetic inspection helped correct assignments of some ASVs as described in Methods. The final proportions of reads belonging to ASV categories for the entire dataset are shown in Fig. 1A. Only the 171 ASVs retrieved by the TarEuk primer set in the category of intestinal protists (Supplementary Table S4) were used in the subsequent analyses.

Only reads in the “Intestinal protists” category were used in the analyses shown in B-F. B) Taxonomic breakdown of intestinal protist reads across individual beaver samples. The P-value indicates the significance of the area factor as determined by PERMANOVA with 999 permutations. However, since only 17.3% of the variation in protist communities is explained by area, this factor has a moderate-to-strong effect.

The relative abundance of taxonomic groups of intestinal protists for individual samples is shown in Fig. 1B. The vast majority is represented by Blastocystis and parabasalids, which are present in every sample; however, their ratios vary. Blastocystis fraction is composed of 12 ASVs, which were all placed in a beaver clade in the reference tree (Supplementary Fig. S2). This clade consisted of Blastocystis 18S rDNA sequences obtained from beaver hosts by Šmigová et al. (2025) and two full-length sequences of Blastocystis 18S rDNA retrieved from the DNA pool of all our samples in this study (NCBI accession numbers PZ726876 and PZ726877). The diversity of parabasalids was higher, comprising 147 ASVs, and interestingly, the V4 region of these contained long insertions that were not observed in reference sequences from the GenBank database. The presence of these insertions was confirmed by Sanger sequencing of a few subcloned and randomly selected V4 amplicons. All ASVs of parabasalids were phylogenetically placed into the Trichomonadidae clade (Supplementary Fig. S4), suggesting that the parabasalid diversity is represented exclusively by small trichomonads. The remaining two protist groups, colpodellids and Eimeria, were rare, occurring in only 10 and 4 samples, respectively. A heatmap of the read abundances of the 30 most frequent ASVs of intestinal protists across samples is shown in Fig. 1C.

The number of detected protist ASVs (species richness), as well as their abundance evenness (Shannon Diversity index), were compared between geographical areas and sexes (Fig. 1D and E). The sex of the host had an insignificant influence on the microbiota composition (0.091 and 0.059 for the species richness and Shannon index, respectively), although a slight increase in the number of observed ASVs and evenness was detected for the male samples. On the other hand, the effect of population was highly significant (0.001 and 0.003 for species richness and Shannon index, respectively), with samples from the Northern population containing fewer ASVs and a less even representation. The multivariate analysis (Fig. 1F) showed a clear distinction between the samples from different populations. Although samples of 3 juveniles from the Western population belonging to the same family (20, 21 and 22) were compositionally much closer to the Northern population. Finally, PERMANOVA confirmed that geography plays a key role in the microbiome differentiation (R2 = 0.173, p-value = 0.001) in Eurasian beavers. Yet these results may be ultimately biased due to the small sample size and an imbalance in the distribution of populations within the dataset (5 vs 35).

Because the 18S rRNA gene sequence of some intestinal protists is divergent and may not be amplified by general eukaryotic primers, we have designed and tested primer pairs tailored to amplify the V4 region of Archamoebae, Metamonada, and Microsporidia (Arch, M1, M2, and Micro). Amplicon sequencing of pools of male and female samples yielded, after final processing but without categorisation, 39,200; 27,373; 114,476; and 100,093 reads and 39; 82; 154; and 652 ASVs for Arch, M1, M2, and Micro primer sets, respectively (Supplementary Table S2). The proportions of reads assigned to high taxonomic ranks for each group-specific primer pair are shown in Fig. 2A and compared to the general TarEuk primer set. The sequencing of amplicons generated by group-specific primers revealed altogether 126 ASVs of intestinal protists, of which 124 belonged to the previously detected higher taxa, Blastocystis, Parabasalia, Colpodellida, and Eimeria. No amplicons from Archamoebae and Microsporidia were detected. To quantify the yield of novel Metamonada ASVs, all Metamonada ASVs were clustered using CD-HIT-EST, and the presence of each of the 80 unique ASVs across the dataset from different primer sets was visualised in an Euler diagram (Fig. 2B). The new primer sets recovered 35 Metamonada ASVs not detected by the general TarEuk primer set, and most of these novel ASVs were recovered by the primer pair M2. These represent Parabasalia (33 ASVs) and the genus Enteromonas (Fornicata, 2 ASVs recovered by Micro primer set), the only non-parabasalian metamonad detected in the study. No new genera of parabasalids were detected by group-specific primers.

Comparison of primer set performance in amplifying intestinal protists in pooled beaver stool samples. Although targeting Microsporidia, the Micro primer set was the only pair which detected the genus Enteromonas (Fornicata, Metamonada).

As neither of the primers used for the metabarcoding amplified Giardia spp., Microsporidia, or Cryptosporidium spp., the presence of these parasites was also assessed by specific PCRs. In the case of Giardia, the nested PCR targeted two genes: beta-giardin (bg) and triosephosphate isomerase (tpi) (Lecová et al., 2018). Initial screening across all 40 samples revealed no products in most samples (Supplementary Figs. S6 and S7), however, six samples (6, 10, 11, 16, 22, and 36) showed amplification of weak smears or bands within the expected size range. These ambiguous patterns were considered potentially indicative of low-level amplification due to PCR inhibition. To minimise the effect of PCR inhibition, these samples were amplified again after 10-fold and 40-fold dilutions of the template DNA, still yielding no product. In parallel, these samples were tested by qPCR but yielded no positive results. Based on these results, all samples are considered negative for Giardia. For microsporidia, the screening across all 40 samples (Supplementary Fig. S8) showed only a single signal larger than expected, which was not confirmed as a Microsporidia by Sanger sequencing. Based on these results, all samples are considered negative for Microsporidia. The barcoding for Cryptosporidium spp. Showed a positive signal in three samples (1, 37, and 39) and a faint signal in eight samples (5, 6, 9, 11, 33, 34, 35, and 36; Supplementary Fig. S9). Sanger sequencing of the three suspect positives confirmed 2 samples (1 and 39) as positive for Cryptosporidium spp. (NCBI accession numbers PZ726878 and PZ726879).

Discussion

In this study, we focused on mapping the diversity of intestinal protists in Eurasian beavers from freshly collected stool samples. This represents a popular non-invasive approach suitable for this purpose, yet it is important to keep in mind that faecal samples represent a distinct niche containing only a subset of gut microbes (Tang et al., 2020) and are prone to contamination from the external environment. Using our enhanced V4 metabarcoding protocol, we were able to retrieve altogether (i.e., using all primer pairs) 297 unique sequences of protists from five protist groups: Blastocystis, parabasalids, colpodellids, Enteromonas, and Eimeria. The overall diversity is smaller than that found in caviomorphs (Khadem et al., 2024; Kim et al., 2022; Nie, 1950; Zikmundová et al., 2024) and murine (Khadem et al., 2024; Kim et al., 2022) rodents previously. This may reflect the biological reality but may also be affected by methodology, such as marker choice (some studies used the V9 variable region), primer choice, or, most importantly, the fact that the referenced studies extracted DNA directly from intestinal contents. To some extent, the reported diversity may also be affected by the handling and interpretation of the data. Aware of the possibility of contamination (introduced either by mastication of trees with insect larvae or ingesting water with cysts), we conservatively excluded from our results groups whose presence in the mammal intestine is highly unlikely, such as gregarines or free-living ciliates, some of which were reported in the referenced studies. A serious problem affecting all studies of metabarcoding intestinal protists is amplification of the host, food and fungal DNA (Fig. 1A). The number of reads belonging to these other categories was so eminent that the protist taxa were simply sidelined. In our preliminary tests, we tested the use of PNA blocking primer (Mann et al., 2019) with negative results (not shown in the study).

The detection of Blastocystis in all 40 sampled beavers in this study indicates a 100% prevalence of this protist across both populations. This is in sharp contrast with previous meta-studies (Farzam et al., 2025; Hunter-Barnett and Viney, 2024), which reported Blastocystis from rodents, but not specifically from Castoridae. Also Šmigová et al. (2025) reported only a single positive beaver out of 21 inspected. The phylogeny of newly acquired full-length rDNA sequences confirmed the existence of a specific beaver subclade – a potential new subtype, to which all Illumina ASVs clustered. The intraspecific variability of this subtype is demonstrated by the relatively high number of 12 ASVs.

Parabasalid ASVs were also present in all samples, and they exhibited extensive insertions within the alignment of the 18S rRNA V4 region compared to sequences in GenBank. These are unlikely to be technical artefacts, since similar patterns were also observed via Sanger sequencing. Sequencing of 18S rRNA from beaver parabasalids isolated into culture may help to resolve this issue. Importantly, all ASVs assigned to Parabasalia were confidently placed within the Trichomonadidae lineage (Cepicka et al., 2010). Some ASVs were deeply nested within the genus Tetratrichomonas, a taxon characterised by a high species richness and a wide host range (Cepicka et al., 2006).

Members of the genus Eimeria are obligate intracellular parasites that inhabit the intestinal tracts of a wide range of vertebrates, including rodents. In our dataset, three Eimeria ASVs were exclusively detected in samples originating from the Western region, and they were placed within a clade comprising a broad phylogenetic diversity of Eimeria species, many of which are associated with rodent hosts (Levine and Ivens, 1988). The sequence of Eimeria sprehni, previously detected and described in beavers in Poland (Drózdz et al., 2004), is not available, and so the detected ASVs may belong to this species.

Flagellates of the order Colpodellida occupy a transitional phylogenetic position between free-living microbial predators and the obligate intracellular parasites of the phylum Apicomplexa. Although traditionally considered free-living, the DNA of Colpodella has been detected in host-associated environments in many recent studies. Reports have described the presence of Colpodella in a variety of ecological and clinical contexts, including ticks, herbivore faeces, and even human patients (Hasapis et al., 2025; Soliman et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2022; Yuan et al., 2012). Overall, Colpodellida sequences were detected in 25% of the sampled beavers from both populations, indicating a notable prevalence. While the clinical relevance of these lineages remains uncertain and contamination cannot be entirely ruled out, their recurrent detection in host-associated samples underscores the importance of targeted molecular surveys across wild and domestic mammals.

While analysing host effects on the diversity of microbiota, we found that the composition of the protist community is significantly affected by the population from which the host originates, which seems to reflect the diversity of hosts. While the population in Western Czechia was founded by the expansion of a genetically diverse admixed population in Bavaria, the North Czechia population originated from the Elbe River relict population and shows diminished genetic variability (Munclinger et al., 2022). The difference likely resulted from the absence of some protist taxa, such as Eimeria, in the samples from Northern Czechia. Although this may reflect differences in protists, it may also be the result of insufficient sampling of this population, from which only 5 samples were available. Additional sampling may resolve this question. In a more balanced comparison between sexes, the significance of the distinction was low, although a higher diversity of protists was observed in the male stool.

The use of primer pairs tailored to amplify groups with divergent 18S rRNA sequences (Metamonada, Archamoebae, and Microsporidia) did not recover any microsporidian and archamoeban ASVs. The primers may be inefficient (positive controls were not used), but these protists may also be truly absent from the samples, as microsporidia were not detected even by the specific PCR. On the other hand, primers designed for Metamonada did recover several novel Metamonada ASVs and, importantly, exhibited higher specificity for the targeted groups and a lower tendency to amplify host and plant DNA (Fig. 2A). The performance of the two pairs targeting Metamonada cannot be easily compared, as we recovered very different numbers of reads - 27,373 vs. 114,476; however, the fraction of novel ASVs was higher for M2 (22/59), making it a promising set for the enhancement of amplification of metamonads in combination with the general eukaryotic primer set. Ultimately, we admit that this approach of using a multiplex of general and group-specific primers can be outperformed by using a single extraordinarily efficient marker (Owens et al., 2024). On top of that, using the long-read sequencing method could also shed new light on gut microbiome diagnostics (Stensvold, 2024).

Finally, neither amplicon sequencing nor specific PCR detected any Giardia or Microsporidia in any of the samples. These results suggest that Giardia and Microsporidia were absent or occurred at very low prevalence in the sampled populations. On the other hand, targeted barcoding confirmed 2 samples (1 and 39) positive for Cryptosporidium. These pathogens are occasionally detected in wild rodents, lagomorphs and cervids in Central Europe (Cervero-Aragó et al., 2021; Hughes, 2024; Jäckel et al., 2024; Perec-Matysiak et al., 2019), showing that these wild animals, including beavers, may serve as potential zoonotic reservoirs.

In conclusion, this study presents a comprehensive analysis of protistan diversity in the stool of the European beaver. The work focuses on detecting protists by combining targeted barcoding with general metabarcoding methods. Moreover, it introduces an enhancement to the general metabarcoding protocol, in which new primer pairs, potentially usable in a single multiplex, play a key role by being tailored to detect even the hard-to-detect protist groups, such as members of Metamonada.
