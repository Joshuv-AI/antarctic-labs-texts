Seismic evidence for lower-mantle heat supply beneath the Cordilleran slab window in western Canada
Authors: Quan Zhang, Pascal Audet, Cong Ji, Clément Estève
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDivergent plate boundaries drive magmatism and plate spreading, yet their role in whole-mantle convection remains unclear, as most systems are submarine. When spreading centres subduct, slab windows open pathways for asthenospheric and potentially deeper mantle upwelling. Here we use 20 years of teleseismic P-to-S receiver functions from 429 stations to map the 410 and 660 km discontinuities beneath western Canada. Common-conversion-point stacking reveals coherent double depressions across a slab window formed by the subducted Resurrection/Farallon Ridge system, in contrast to the adjacent craton. Mineral physics and seismic tomography constraints indicate mantle transition zone temperature anomalies of 100-300 K that follow the slab-window geometry. Monte Carlo estimates of constrained whole-mantle geotherms further suggest a warmer-than-average lower mantle. Together, these observations suggest that the fossil slab window provides a conduit for lower-mantle heat into the upper mantle and lithosphere, contributing to long-term uplift of the Canadian Cordillera.Subject terms: Seismology, Tectonics

Introduction

The Canadian Cordillera, forming the mountainous western margin of North America from the U.S. border to the Yukon and Northwest Territories, is an accretionary orogen built by long-lived subduction, terrane accretion, and subsequent intracontinental deformation1–5. This broad orogenic belt has a high mean elevation and marks the transition from the thick, cold North American craton in the continental interior to a thin, tectonically active lithosphere along the Pacific margin6–12. Its geological history, involving multiple episodes of plate reorganization including the subduction of several oceanic plates, ridge-trench interactions, and the opening and closure of marginal basins, has produced a complex surface record of thrusting, strike-slip faulting, crustal extension, and widespread Cenozoic magmatism13–20. Plate reconstructions and geochemical data show that spreading centres within the Resurrection/Farallon ridge system were progressively subducted beneath western North America, generating slab windows, reorganizing mantle flow, and driving intraplate magmatism4,17,21.

Several lines of evidence from geochemical, geological, and geophysical data and models across the Canadian Cordillera indicate an anomalously hot and buoyant upper mantle that supports the high mean elevation22–26. However, the depth and origin of this heat source remain unresolved. Models explaining these characteristics involve some combination of small–scale convection in a former back–arc setting6,27,28, slab window tectonism17,21, and lithospheric removal processes8,29. However, these models are typically framed within an upper–mantle context and do not uniquely constrain whether the forces that maintain Cordilleran elevation and fuel its magmatism are rooted shallowly in the upper mantle or are the surface expression of deeper mantle dynamics30.

The mantle transition zone (MTZ), situated between ~410 and 660 km depth (d410 and d660, respectively), is a mineralogical boundary layer that regulates mass and heat transfer between the upper and lower mantle and provides a useful window into this problem31. The depths of the 410 km and 660 km seismic discontinuities, which are primarily controlled by pressure-temperature-composition-dependent phase transitions in the olivine and garnet systems, integrate thermal and compositional anomalies over broad regions of the mantle32,33. Systematic deviations of these boundaries from their reference depths, as well as variations in MTZ thickness, have been used to infer elevated temperatures and plume-like upwellings feeding hotspots such as Iceland and the East African Rift34–37. Despite mounting evidence for an anomalously hot mantle beneath western North America, the MTZ beneath the Canadian Cordillera remains unconstrained, and its potential to discriminate between shallow and deep sources of Cordilleran heat has not been exploited.

Here we use dense teleseismic P–wave receiver function data across western Canada to image the topography of the 410 and 660 km discontinuities and the thickness of the MTZ beneath the Canadian Cordillera and adjacent craton. By jointly interpreting MTZ boundary depths and seismic velocity anomalies from multiple tomographic models, we place quantitative bounds on mantle temperatures from the base of the lithosphere into the lower mantle. Our results indicate that the observed MTZ structure is consistent with a Cordilleran heat source originating from deep, possibly lower–mantle, upwellings coincident with the slab window system. Our study suggests that slab windows can facilitate communication between deep–mantle heat sources and the base of the continental lithosphere, with implications for how plate-boundary reorganizations couple to whole–mantle convection.

Results and discussion

Mantle transition zone topography in western Canada

We use seismic data from 429 stations spanning 13 networks covering the Canadian Cordilleran landmass from the coast to the continental interior and craton (Fig. 1a) to image the MTZ discontinuities (Methods). The seismic data are processed to obtain teleseismic P-wave receiver functions, which are source-normalized waveforms that are sensitive to impedance contrasts in the subsurface and are commonly used to map the d410 and d66038–41. The receiver functions are processed for Common Conversion Point stacking in a 3D grid, where the individual raypaths are corrected using a three-dimensional seismic velocity model42; we pick the d410 and d660 discontinuities from the 3D grid43–45. We then interpolate the d410 and d660 data to produce maps of the MTZ topography, shown in Fig. 1b, c.

The d410 and d660 maps display strong spatial consistency and show a transition from downward-to-upward boundary deflections observed to coincide with the Tintina Fault-Rocky Mountain Trench (TF-RMT) (Fig. 1). The area with maximum d410 downward deflection (approximately +16 km) is localized within the red ellipse in the western Cordillera. The maximum d660 anomaly of approximately +12 km is also found in the same area, but displays lower variability than that of d410. Upward deflections of d410 and d660 are consistently observed on the craton side (Fig. 1b, c). The MTZ thickness (Fig. 1d) shows a slight thinning (by ~−9 km from the 250-km reference) within the red ellipse and its northeast extension in the Mackenzie Mountains at the border between Yukon and the Northwest Territories. We also observe a thickened MTZ beneath Alberta by ~+9 km.

In Fig. 2, we examine the relation between the MTZ topography model and upper mantle structure by comparing the recomposed, stacked RFs (from the 3D CCP grid) with an independent, global seismic velocity model (DETOX-P0146—Supporting Information) along several profiles (displayed in Fig. 2a–d). The profile locations are shown on maps of seismic velocity anomalies at depths of 400 km and 650 km for comparison (Fig. 2e, f). At 400 km depth, the seismic velocity model shows a pronounced lateral contrast between the relatively slow mantle beneath the Cordillera and the faster mantle beneath the Craton (Fig. 2c). This contrast is broadly consistent with the regional dichotomy observed in both d410 and d660 variations (Fig. 1b, c), but the velocity anomaly is not used to infer discontinuity depths. Profile A-A’ (Fig. 2a) follows the strike of the Canadian Cordillera from northwest to southeast, showing a consistent deepening of the d410 and d660. The effect of the subducting Juan de Fuca plate beneath Vancouver Island7 can be observed towards Point A’ on the profile A-A’ (Fig. 2a) as high-velocity anomalies at depths of ~400 km and a shallowing of the d410 and d660. The stacked receiver functions and the resulting interface picks along profiles (B-B’), (C-C’), and (D-D’) in Fig. 2b–d show an eastward transition from positive to negative d410 and d660 anomalies in those profiles (Fig. 2b–d), which occurs within a similar distance range of the change from negative to positive seismic velocity anomalies at 400 km depth (Fig. 2e). This transition approximately coincides with the TF-RMT, which is commonly interpreted as the eastern limit of Neogene and younger basaltic magmatism in the upper mantle5, indicating an abrupt change in tectonics and upper mantle structure across d410 down into the MTZ. At 650 km (Fig. 2f), however, the seismic velocity anomalies are consistently negative and do not show a correlation with the d660 or surface tectonics, despite modest MTZ thickness variations (Fig. 1d).

Anomalously hot mantle beneath the western Canadian Cordillera

The d410 and d660 discontinuities are typically attributed to mineral phase transitions within the olivine system. Variations from those predicted depths are commonly interpreted to primarily reflect mantle temperature variations over a broad region47. Specifically, the d410 is generated by the transition from olivine to wadsleyite, whereas the d660 arises from the transformation of ringwoodite to bridgmanite (perovskite) and ferropericlase48,49. These phase changes are governed by Clapeyron slopes, which describe how the equilibrium pressure of a phase transition varies with temperature. In a relatively hot mantle regime, the positive Clapeyron slope of the olivine-wadsleyite transition results in a deepening of the d410, while the negative Clapeyron slope associated with the ringwoodite-bridgmanite transition leads to a shallower d66050 and associated thinning of the MTZ. Although the mantle consists of 40–60% olivine, it is also significantly influenced by the presence of garnet and pyroxenes36. In regions where the mantle temperature exceeds the global average by 200–300 K, the dominant phase transition involves the transformation of majorite garnet to bridgmanite at around 660 km depth51–53. This transition, like the olivine-wadsleyite transition at d410, exhibits a positive Clapeyron slope, causing both the d410 and d660 discontinuities to deflect downward in anomalously hot regions54.

In the western Canadian Cordillera, both d410 and d660 deepen by approximately 10 and 8 km, respectively, relative to their reference depth beneath the backarc and coastal regions (Fig. 1b, c). Such double-depression of the d410 and d660 is inconsistent with the predictions for the ringwoodite-bridgmanite transition. We cannot rule out that the positive correlation between the d410 and d660 reflects an incomplete correction of 3D variations in seismic velocity in this region55. However, careful consideration of this effect in independent studies has yielded similar double-depressed MTZ boundaries in the same region55 and in other regions, including the western United States56, Iceland36, and the East African Rift35 (Fig. 3a, b). Those results were interpreted as evidence of an additional post-garnet transition54, which has a positive Clapeyron slope at the lower MTZ boundary. We therefore interpret our results as evidence of an anomalously hot thermal regime across the MTZ below the western Canadian Cordillera, where the d660 marks the post-garnet transition.

To further investigate this, we estimate temperature anomalies in westernmost Canada (red ellipse in Fig. 1) using the d410 and d660 data and 12 independent seismic velocity models (Methods) (Fig. 4). For both MTZ boundaries, we estimate the temperature anomalies by using the range of published Clapeyron slopes for the corresponding depths of the discontinuities (Fig. 4a, b). Temperature anomalies calculated for 12 global seismic velocity models are shown in Fig. 4e; those estimated for the preferred model (DETOX-P01, see Supplementary Information) are shown by the orange histograms and KDEs in Fig. 4c, d. In this region, the average temperature anomaly at 650 km depth from DETOX-P01 is +127 K, slightly lower than the depth-based estimate for d660 (+138 to +221 K). This discrepancy likely reflects both compositional effects that are unaccounted for in the temperature calculation, and the limited resolution of deep tomography models57. Experimental studies indicate that the garnet-dominated 660 km phase transition can occur at temperatures 200–300 K higher than the global average51–53, implying that temperature estimates based solely on velocity anomalies may systematically underestimate deep-mantle thermal perturbations. Nevertheless, all estimates from these independent seismic observables indicate an anomalously hot thermal regime in the western Canadian Cordillera.

Lower-mantle heat supply beneath the Cordilleran slab window

Multiple geophysical data and models show evidence of a sub-crustal heat source supporting the high mean elevation of the Canadian Cordillera6,17,21,27,29. The origin of the heat source, however, remains debated, and interpretations range from small-scale convection in a former back-arc6 to slab window tectonism58 underneath the North Cordilleran Volcanic Province and lithospheric delamination8,29. Our results show evidence of a deep heat source beneath the western Canadian Cordillera, which cannot be attributed to small-scale convection or lithospheric delamination processes. The slab window hypothesis is also not typically linked with deep mantle processes, although a recent seismic velocity model of Patagonia in South America suggests a thermal upwelling above 400 km depth beneath the Patagonian slab window59. Here, the strong spatial coincidence of the depressed MTZ and volcanism in the Northern Cordilleran Slab Window (NCSW) suggests a genetic link, where upwellings associated with this fossil divergent boundary interact with, and possibly traverse, the transition zone (Fig. 5). In particular, the pronounced depression of the d410 beneath the northern margin of the NCSW (Fig. 1b, c) has previously been explained by advective upwelling of anomalously hot mantle around the edges of the subducting slab55, consistent with the observed P-wave velocity structure in Fig. 242,55. Along the southern margin of the NCSW, we resolve up to ~8 km of MTZ thinning, suggesting that the strongest upwelling is localized near the Juan de Fuca slab edge. Furthermore, similar MTZ anomalies have been reported above a slab window in southern California56, indicating that such signatures are not unique to western Canada.

Independent geochemical and volcanological data support the idea that slab windows can tap unusually deep mantle sources. In the southern US Cordillera, Madsen17 reconstructs the history of the Resurrection and Eshamy plates and shows that magmas of the northern Cordilleran volcanic province erupted above a large slab window inboard of the trench. They emphasize that the most Mg-rich, alkalic basalts have trace-element characteristics consistent with an ocean-island basalt source, and that basalts on the Queen Charlotte Islands record subslab mantle coming into contact with North American lithosphere through this window17. In contrast, the Anahim hotspot has been interpreted as the result of small-scale mantle advection at the edge of the slab window, without requiring a deep, lower-mantle upwelling7, and may therefore exhibit a mixture of plume-like and upper-mantle characteristics. Helium-isotope data show that relatively high 3He/4He groundwaters and geothermal fluids occur in tectonically active regions of the western Canadian Cordillera along the continental margin60, indicating a mantle contribution to the volatile budget. However, direct geochemical constraints on the depth and geometry of the upwelling beneath the slab window and the Anahim hotspot remain sparse, highlighting the need for further local seismic and geochemical investigations.

To examine a potential deep-seated heat source beneath the NCSW, we extend the temperature estimates to the whole mantle using the DETOX-P01 model46 and the same empirical Vp-temperature relationship described above. In this region, DETOX-P01 predicts negative temperature anomalies between ~1000 and 1500 km depth, indicating the presence of relatively cold material (Fig. B2 in the Supplementary Information). Although the mean temperature anomaly within this depth range is modest (generally < 50 K), the lateral variations are large, reaching amplitudes of up to ~220 K. In the 1000-km slice of DETOX-P01, we observe a continuous high-velocity structure extending from the Pacific margin into the subsurface of northwestern Canada, consistent with previous interpretations of a deep, detached segment of the subducted Resurrection plate e.g.18,61. The transition from the hot MTZ beneath the NCSW to this colder lower-mantle anomaly is very sharp (over only ~3∘ in latitude-longitude at 1000 km depth), suggesting that the slab locally perturbs but does not completely block broader upwelling from greater depths. This deep-rooted hot upwelling has been detected in the global tomographic model SEMUCB-WM162.

More speculatively, we estimate possible lower-mantle temperature anomalies by constructing a local geotherm from the surface to the core-mantle boundary. We combine the temperature estimates from the d410 and d660 depth anomalies, observational constraints on lithospheric geotherms in western Canada27, and widely used reference mantle geotherms63 in Fig. B3 in the Supplementary Information. Using a Monte Carlo approach, we generate an ensemble of one-dimensional geotherms in which the lithosphere, upper mantle, lower mantle and D″ are treated as separate layers, while allowing for uncertainties in potential temperature, thermal conductivity, and phase-boundary Clapeyron slopes, including the thermal effect of D″64. Although this parameterization does not fully capture the three-dimensional and non-adiabatic nature of mantle convection, it provides a first-order estimate of the plausible thermal range.

Our results show that, for the “cooler” end-member geotherms consistent with the MTZ constraints, the 90% confidence interval of temperatures in the lower mantle beneath the Cordillera still lies above the reference model, with average lower-mantle temperatures ~150–200 K higher than the global reference. This quantitative estimate supports our qualitative inference that the mass and/or heat responsible for the elevated topography of the Cordillera relative to the adjacent craton may be linked to deep-mantle dynamics62. In other words, the long-wavelength topography, the complex surface tectonics, and the deep thermal structure beneath western North America appear to be different manifestations of a single, integrated deep-seated upwelling system, as shown in the schematic Fig. 5.

This study proposes a direct link between slab-window tectonism in a former backarc setting linked with a deep-seated heat source, possibly in the lower mantle. However, a causal mechanism cannot be established with these data alone. Further testing of this model will require integrating the effect of subducting ridges in whole mantle convection models, and mapping the MTZ in other regions where a slab window exists, e.g., Mexico, Costa Rica, Patagonia, Antarctica[,65.

Methods

Data

We select seismic waveform from 429 stations (shown in Fig. 1a, spanning 13 networks including 1E, 2K, 7C, CN, EO, PO, PQ, RV, TD, XL, XY, Y5, and NY66–78, using publicly available data provided by NSF SAGE. The dataset covers events with magnitudes in the range of 5.5 to 8 that occurred between 2003 and 2020, and with an epicentral distance range of 30 to 90 degrees, ensuring the generation of P-to-S converted waves at the d410 and d660. We also ensure that each selected event was detected by at least 10 stations. In addition, only the seismic recordings with SNR (signal-to-noise ratio) ≥2 are selected. 469 events are selected by this criterion, ensuring adequate spatial coverage and high-quality seismic data across a wide geographical region.

Receiver functions

We employ receiver functions to image the MTZ discontinuities and determine their depth and MTZ thickness. The raw recordings are windowed between -30 s and 120 s relative to the P-wave arrival time predicted by the 1-D seismic velocity model PREM79. We then rotate the seismograms from the vertical-north-east (ZNE) to the vertical-radial-transverse (ZRT) coordinate system. We calculate receiver functions by deconvolving the vertical component from the radial component using a time-domain deconvolution method80 with 100 iterations, and a Butterworth filter with corner frequencies of 0.02–0.25 Hz to calculate 42,746 high-quality receiver functions. Receiver functions are then back-projected onto their theoretical ray paths, and amplitudes are averaged into a 3-D grid to perform common conversion point (CCP) stacking.

To highlight the weak P-to-S converted phases (i.e., Pds) from the MTZ discontinuities, we calculate the ray paths by refining the 1-D reference model-AK13581 using the only regional P-wave seismic velocity model covering the depth range from the surface to the core-mantle boundary available for this region (CAP22; Boyce42). This correction reduces the impact of anomalous velocities along the ray path on the estimated depths of the discontinuities. We use an empirical relation82 to convert the dVp-to-dVs. The configuration of the 3-D grid is constructed using 90-km circular bins with a 60-km lateral spacing and a 1-km radial (depth) spacing covering depths from 0 to 800 km, considering the Fresnel zone radius as λz≈60–80 km, and the vertical resolution as λ/4 ≈ 2 km.

To ensure statistical robustness, we perform a 50-time bootstrap resampling on the CCP amplitudes, calculating the mean, standard deviation, and number of piercing points within each bin, achieving a confidence level of 80% (see the example in Fig. B4 in the Supplementary Information). Within each bin, we search for local maxima in the mean CCP amplitude in the range of ±20 km of both discontinuities, generally covering the estimated depth range: 381–427 km for d410 and 620–676 km for d66083. Only bins with root mean square (RMS) less than 0.025 at depths 300–800 km, and data points at the search local maxima with a standard deviation lower than 0.05 and with greater than 30 piercing points, are retained to ensure robustness. Piercing point density maps and uncertainty maps are provided in Fig. B5 in the Supplementary Information. We then employ Ordinary Kriging interpolation, which accounts for the spatial covariance between grid points84, to map the topography of these interfaces from the scattered points. This yields a reasonable model for the fluctuations in the depths of d410 and d660, from which we calculate the thickness of the MTZ.
