Effects of postpartum hand expression on milk volume and breastfeeding outcomes: a systematic review and meta-analysis protocol
Authors: Weina Zhou, Liying Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractIntroductionHand expression is a manual method of milk expression that requires no equipment and is commonly used in the early postpartum period, particularly when direct breastfeeding is not possible or not effective. Randomised trials have reported mixed findings across different maternal–infant contexts. This review aims to synthesise randomised evidence on the effects of postpartum hand expression on expressed milk volume and breastfeeding outcomes.Methods and analysisWe will include individually randomised parallel-group controlled trials involving human postpartum women expressing breast milk in the early postpartum period. The intervention is postpartum hand expression; comparators will include electric or manual pump expression and usual care without structured hand-expression training. We will search PubMed, Embase, CINAHL, PsycINFO, Web of Science and CENTRAL from inception, without language or date restrictions and also search OpenGrey, ClinicalTrials.gov and WHO ICTRP, plus reference lists. Two reviewers will independently screen studies, extract data and assess risk of bias using the Risk of Bias tool for randomised trials, version 2. Eligible outcomes include expressed milk volume, breastfeeding outcomes, supplementation, time to onset of copious milk secretion and maternal outcomes. Where appropriate, we will conduct pairwise meta-analyses, calculating risk ratios for dichotomous outcomes and mean differences or standardised mean differences for continuous outcomes with 95% CIs. Heterogeneity will be assessed using χ² and I², with prespecified subgroup and sensitivity analyses. Certainty of evidence will be assessed using Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE).Ethics and disseminationThis study will be based on previous research findings, so that ethics approval is not required. Data searching commences in June 2026 and expects to complete in January, 2027. The findings will be disseminated through peer-reviewed journals and academic conferences.PROSPERO registration numberCRD420261327070.Keywords: Clinical Protocols, Health Services, Infant, Meta-Analysis

Introduction

Breastfeeding and human milk feeding remain foundational public health strategies, with well-established benefits for infants and mothers across the life course.1–3 However, achieving recommended breastfeeding practices is increasingly difficult in many settings, and early introduction of formula and early cessation remain common.3 Global and national policies continue to recommend initiation within the first hour after birth and exclusive breastfeeding for the first 6 months, supported by professional organisations such as WHO/UNICEF and the American Academy of Paediatrics.4–6

A frequent and clinically salient barrier to sustained exclusive breastfeeding is maternal concern about ‘not having enough milk’, particularly during the first 2 weeks postpartum when lactation is being established.7–9 Systematic evidence indicates that perceived insufficient milk supply is common and strongly associated with supplementation and breastfeeding discontinuation.10 In parallel, timely secretory activation is a critical physiological milestone; delayed onset is associated with lower milk production and shorter breastfeeding duration, and has identifiable risk factors at the maternal and care-process levels.11–13 These observations underpin the clinical emphasis on protecting early lactation through prompt, effective milk removal and targeted support.

In many real-world scenarios, direct breastfeeding in the early postpartum period is not possible or not effective, including after caesarean birth, maternal–infant separation, infant prematurity, neonatal illness and neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) admission.14–17 In such contexts, expressing mother’s own milk becomes a practical necessity to establish supply and to provide human milk to the infant. The clinical rationale is particularly compelling for preterm and low-birth-weight infants, for whom mother’s own milk is associated with better outcomes than formula feeding in evidence syntheses, including reduced necrotising enterocolitis risk.18–20 Consequently, maternity and neonatal guidance documents routinely emphasise early initiation of lactation support and teaching mothers how to express milk effectively.

Hand expression is a low-cost, immediately available technique that can be taught at the bedside and used with or without equipment.21 It is often combined with breast massage and may be more feasible than pump use in resource-limited settings or during the earliest hours postpartum.22 Importantly, a growing body of randomised and comparative studies has evaluated hand expression relative to pump-based expression across diverse populations and time windows. For example, among mothers of healthy term newborns with early feeding difficulties, early hand expression was associated with higher breastfeeding rates at 2 months compared with electric pumping in a randomised trial.23 In contrast, among mothers of very low birth-weight infants, early exclusive hand expression during the first postpartum week produced lower cumulative milk volumes than exclusive electric pumping in a repeated-measures randomised trial.24 More recent trials in mothers of preterm infants have compared manual techniques with manual pump methods during the first postnatal week, while other studies have examined expression method effects on milk volume and composition.25–28 These trials suggest that effects may differ by clinical context, timing and intensity of expression and comparator technology—creating uncertainty for bedside recommendations.

Although evidence syntheses have addressed milk expression methods, important limitations remain. The most influential Cochrane review on methods of milk expression (last updated in 2016) evaluated a broad range of expression approaches and outcomes (including acceptability, safety, contamination and cost) rather than focusing specifically on postpartum hand expression and clinically prioritised outcomes such as early expressed milk volume and subsequent breastfeeding status.21 Moreover, several relevant studies have been published after 2016, including trials in preterm populations and additional work on early lactation processes and expression practices, which are not captured in older syntheses.21 Recent qualitative and clinical reviews further highlight the burden, variability and implementation challenges of milk expression during mother–infant separation, reinforcing the need for an updated, clinically focused synthesis.29–31 Therefore, an up-to-date systematic review and meta-analysis is warranted to quantify the effects of postpartum hand expression on milk volume and breastfeeding outcomes, and to explore potential sources of heterogeneity that can inform context-specific recommendations.

Materials and methods

Protocol registration and reporting

The protocol has been prepared in line with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses protocols (PRISMA-P) 2015 guidance, and a completed PRISMA-P checklist is provided in online supplemental file S1.32 Any substantive changes made after registration (eg, eligibility criteria, outcomes or planned analyses) will be recorded with the date and rationale in the PROSPERO entry and will also be clearly described in the final systematic review and meta-analysis report.

Search strategy and information source

The search strategy will be developed by our research team. Comprehensive searches to identify all relevant randomised controlled trials (RCTs) will be conducted in the following electronic databases from inception: PubMed, Embase, CINAHL, PsycINFO, Web of Science and the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL). No restrictions on publication date or language will be applied.

Search terms will be formulated according to the participants, intervention and outcomes, and will combine controlled vocabulary (eg, MeSH/Emtree terms) with free-text terms. Boolean operators (AND/OR) will be used, together with database-specific techniques (eg, truncation, phrase marks and wildcards). To focus on RCT evidence, the strategy will incorporate RCT-related terms and, where appropriate, validated trial filters. The full search strategies for all databases are provided in online supplemental file S2. An information specialist/librarian will be invited to peer review the search strategies and revise them where needed.

To minimise publication bias, we will search grey literature via OpenGrey and conference proceedings. Trial registries, including ClinicalTrials.gov and the WHO International Clinical Trials Registry Platform, will also be searched to identify ongoing or unpublished trials. Reference lists and bibliographies of included studies and relevant reviews will be screened, and key journals and conference abstracts will be hand searched. If necessary, we will contact experts in the field to identify additional eligible trials.

Eligibility criteria

Population

We will include postpartum women who are expressing breast milk in the early postpartum period. Eligible participants may be primiparous or multiparous, may have had a vaginal birth or caesarean birth, and may be cared for in postnatal wards, settings involving mother–infant separation or community care after discharge. Infants may be term or preterm and may be well or hospitalised. We will exclude studies that focus only on antenatal milk expression and studies that do not include postpartum participants.

Intervention

The intervention of interest is postpartum hand expression of breast milk. Hand expression refers to manual milk expression using the hands, which may be taught at the bedside and delivered as a structured technique or programme. We will include interventions started in the early postpartum period and delivered either in hospital or after discharge. Studies will be eligible when hand expression is the primary intervention or when its effect can be clearly distinguished and data can be extracted for the hand-expression component.

Comparison

Eligible comparators include breast pump expression, which may use an electric pump or a manual pump, and usual care without structured hand-expression training. We will also include studies comparing hand expression with alternative expression strategies, provided the comparator is clearly described. Studies are eligible when cointerventions such as routine breastfeeding counselling are delivered similarly across groups, so that the contrast primarily reflects the expression method.

Outcomes

Studies must report at least one eligible outcome. Milk volume outcomes include expressed milk volume measured in millilitres, reported as volume per expression session, daily volume or cumulative volume over a defined period in the early postpartum phase. Breastfeeding outcomes include exclusive breastfeeding and any breastfeeding at clinically relevant time points such as discharge and follow-up, as well as measures of breastfeeding initiation. Additional outcomes include time to onset of copious milk secretion, use of formula or donor milk supplementation during the early postpartum period and maternal outcomes such as pain, engorgement, mastitis, adverse events, acceptability and satisfaction.

Study design

We will include individually randomised parallel-group controlled trials. We will exclude quasirandomised studies, non-randomised studies, observational designs, qualitative studies, case series, protocols and conference abstracts that do not provide sufficient data for analysis.

Study selection

All records identified through the database searches and other sources will be imported into a reference management software (EndNote X9), where duplicate entries will be removed. After deduplication, the titles and abstracts of all identified studies will be screened independently by two reviewers (WZ and LZ) against the predefined eligibility criteria, and clearly irrelevant studies will be excluded. Full texts of potentially eligible studies will then be obtained and assessed independently by the same two reviewers for final inclusion. Any disagreements will be resolved through discussion and consensus; if consensus cannot be reached, a third senior reviewer will be consulted to adjudicate. The overall selection process, including the numbers of records identified, screened, excluded and included, will be documented using a PRISMA 2020 flow diagram (online supplemental figure S1).33

Data extraction

Data extraction will be conducted independently by two reviewers, WZ and LZ, for all included studies. Disagreements will be resolved through discussion, and a third reviewer will arbitrate when consensus cannot be reached. A standardised data extraction form will be developed with reference to the Cochrane Handbook and piloted before full extraction. We will extract study information including author, year, country and setting, design, sample size, trial registration, funding and key methodological features such as randomisation, allocation concealment and blinding. Participant data will include maternal age, parity, previous breastfeeding experience, mode of birth, gestational age, infant status and care setting, antenatal breastfeeding or hand-expression training, eligibility criteria and other relevant baseline characteristics. Intervention and comparator details will cover the hand expression technique and training, timing of initiation postpartum, duration and frequency, total exposure, setting and provider, cointerventions and pump type or usual care components for comparators. Outcome data will include expressed milk volume and breastfeeding outcomes with definitions and time points, effect estimates with measures of variance and information on adherence and attrition. When data are missing or unclear, we will contact corresponding authors for clarification.

Risk of bias assessment

The methodological quality of the included studies will be assessed independently by two reviewers (WZ and LZ). Any disagreements will be resolved through discussion, and if necessary, adjudicated by a third senior reviewer. We will evaluate risk of bias using the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool for randomised trials, version 2 (RoB 2).34 The following domains will be assessed: bias arising from the randomisation process, bias due to deviations from intended interventions, bias due to missing outcome data, bias in measurement of the outcome and bias in selection of the reported result. Each domain will be judged as ‘low risk of bias’, ‘some concerns’ or ‘high risk of bias’, and an overall risk-of-bias judgement will be generated for each included study.

Certainty of evidence assessment

We will assess the certainty of evidence using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) approach.34 Certainty will be judged across five domains: risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision and publication bias. For this outcome, the overall body of evidence will be rated as high, moderate, low or very low certainty, following current GRADE guidance.34 Two reviewers (WZ and LZ) will independently rate the certainty of evidence; any discrepancies will be resolved through discussion, and if needed, a third senior reviewer will adjudicate.

Data synthesis and analysis

We will synthesise evidence using pairwise meta-analysis where studies are sufficiently comparable in participants, the hand expression and comparator regimens, and outcome definitions and measurement time points. If quantitative pooling is not appropriate, we will provide a structured narrative synthesis and report it in line with the Synthesis Without Meta-analysis approach.35

For dichotomous outcomes such as exclusive breastfeeding, any breastfeeding and use of formula or donor milk supplementation, we will calculate risk ratios with 95% CIs. For meta-analysis, the natural logarithm of the risk ratio and its SE will be used, and pooled estimates will be back-transformed to risk ratios for presentation.36 For continuous outcomes such as expressed milk volume, we will calculate mean differences when outcomes are reported in the same unit and comparable time windows, and standardised mean differences when measurement approaches differ, with 95% CIs. For pooling, study-specific mean differences or standardised mean differences and their standard errors will be used; where necessary, SEs will be derived from SD, CIs, p values or other available statistics.37 A two-sided p value below 0.05 will be considered statistically significant. Where needed, milk volume will be harmonised to millilitres and analysed within clinically meaningful postpartum windows, prioritising the early postpartum period and prespecified follow-up time points.

We will undertake meta-analysis only when heterogeneity is acceptable clinically and methodologically. Statistical heterogeneity will be assessed using the χ2 test and the I statistic, where I below 25% indicates no heterogeneity, 25%–49% indicates low heterogeneity, 50%–74% indicates moderate heterogeneity and 75% or above indicates high heterogeneity.38 When quantitative pooling is appropriate, we will use a random-effects model as the primary meta-analytic approach. Statistical heterogeneity will be assessed using the I2 statistic and χ2 test, and explored through predefined subgroup analyses where sufficient data are available. Sensitivity analyses will be conducted to examine the robustness of the pooled estimates. If heterogeneity is substantial and cannot be adequately addressed, we will refrain from presenting a pooled estimate and instead provide a narrative synthesis.39 We will conduct prespecified subgroup analyses, where data permit, to explore heterogeneity. Subgroups will include infant status and care context (term healthy dyads vs preterm or hospitalised infants and mother–infant separation), timing of initiation of hand expression, comparator type (electric pumping, manual pumping and usual care), intervention mode (hand expression alone or combined with pumping), parity, previous breastfeeding experience and antenatal breastfeeding or hand-expression training. If subgroup analysis is not feasible for these maternal factors, they will be addressed in the narrative synthesis.

Sensitivity analyses will evaluate robustness by excluding studies at high risk of bias or with some concerns, and by assessing the influence of individual studies on pooled estimates. If at least 10 studies contribute to an outcome, we will assess potential publication bias and small-study effects using funnel plot inspection and Egger’s test. The certainty of evidence for key outcomes will be assessed using the GRADE approach across risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, publication bias and summarised in a summary of findings table.40

Discussion

Study aims and anticipated contribution

This systematic review and meta-analysis will quantify the effects of postpartum hand expression on expressed milk volume and breastfeeding outcomes, and examine whether effects vary by clinical context and implementation features. Effective milk removal is particularly important when early direct breastfeeding is not feasible or effective, such as after caesarean birth or during mother–infant separation.3 For preterm and low-birth-weight infants, mother’s own milk is associated with better outcomes than formula feeding in evidence syntheses, highlighting the clinical importance of optimising early lactation support.18 Although a Cochrane review assessed methods of milk expression, it was last updated in 2016 and did not provide a focused, up-to-date synthesis of postpartum hand expression on clinically prioritised outcomes.21 Since then, additional randomised trials, including studies in preterm populations, have been published.25 41–45 Together, this review is expected to provide more actionable evidence to inform bedside teaching, postnatal care pathways and future research.

Methodological strengths

Methodologically, this review is designed to maximise transparency, reproducibility and clinical usefulness. The protocol is registered prospectively and will be reported in line with established guidance for systematic review protocols and completed reviews. Study selection and data extraction will be conducted independently by two reviewers to minimise errors and subjective judgement. Risk of bias in included randomised trials will be appraised using the revised Cochrane tool, and the certainty of evidence for key outcomes will be graded and presented in a summary of findings table.46 Where meta-analysis is not appropriate, we will adopt structured narrative synthesis methods to ensure clear and consistent reporting. Quantitative synthesis will use standard meta-analytic approaches, with heterogeneity assessed using recognised statistics and, where relevant, accounted for using random-effects models.

Implications for practice

This review is expected to inform practical, context-specific lactation support in the early postpartum period. Hand expression can be taught at the bedside and integrated into routine postnatal care as an immediately available option, particularly when early direct breastfeeding is not feasible or effective, or when pump access is limited.23 For mother–infant separation and NICU-related care, the findings may help refine protocols on when to prioritise hand expression alone, when to transition to pumping, and when to use a combined approach, while aligning with broader guidance on supporting breastfeeding during hospitalisation and separation. If benefits are confirmed for early expressed milk volume and downstream breastfeeding outcomes, hospitals may strengthen staff training, standardise teaching materials, and incorporate structured follow-up to support adherence and timely troubleshooting.47

Limitations and future research

Studies are likely to vary in population and care context, including term vs preterm infants and degrees of mother–infant separation, which may introduce substantial heterogeneity.23 We will address this using clinically meaningful time windows, random-effects models when appropriate, and prespecified subgroup and sensitivity analyses. Intervention reporting may be inconsistent, particularly regarding technique, timing, frequency, training and cointerventions, which can limit interpretability and replication. Future trials should use clearer intervention descriptions, follow established reporting guidance and standardise outcome definitions and measurement time points for milk volume and breastfeeding outcomes. In addition, future research should prioritise adequately powered randomised trials in high-need settings such as NICU-related separation, and should include implementation outcomes such as acceptability, burden and resource use alongside clinical outcomes.
