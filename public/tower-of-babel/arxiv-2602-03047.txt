Equilibrium measures for higher dimensional rotationally symmetric Riesz gases
Sung-Soo Byun, Peter J. Forrester, Satya N. Majumdar, Gregory Schehr

Abstract
. We study equilibrium measures for Riesz gases in dimension d d with pairwise interaction kernel | x − y | − s |x-y|^{-s} , subject to radially symmetric external fields. We characterise broad classes of confining potentials for which the equilibrium measure is supported on the unit ball and admits an explicit density. Our main contribution is a converse construction: starting from a prescribed radially symmetric equilibrium density given as a power series in the squared radius, we determine the associated external potential and establish the corresponding Euler–Lagrange variational conditions. A key ingredient in the proof is an identity between two F 2 3 {}_{3}F_{2} hypergeometric functions evaluated at unit argument, which is of independent interest. As applications, we identify the external potentials corresponding to equilibrium densities proportional to ( 1 − | x | 2 ) α (1-|x|^{2})^{\alpha} , α > − 1 \alpha>-1 , and show that these potentials can be expressed in terms of Gauss hypergeometric functions F 1 2 {}_{2}F_{1} , reducing to polynomials for special values of α \alpha . We also determine the equilibrium measure associated with purely power-type external potentials—often referred to as Freud or Mittag–Leffler potentials in the context of log gases—for which the equilibrium density admits an explicit F 1 2 {}_{2}F_{1} representation. Furthermore, we apply our framework to a Coulomb gas in dimension d + 1 d+1 confined by a harmonic potential to the half-space. We derive a necessary condition under which the equilibrium measure is fully supported on the boundary hyperplane of dimension d d , with the induced density corresponding to that of a Riesz gas with exponent s = d − 1 s=d-1 .

Introduction

Long-range repulsive interactions play a central role in many-body systems and have therefore attracted a wide interest across both physics
[
16
]
and mathematics
[
71
,
61
,
72
,
15
]
.
When combined with an external confining field, such interactions typically give rise, in the thermodynamic limit, to equilibrium configurations with compact support, nontrivial density profiles, and microscopic structures that depend on the form of the interactions. These features appear in a broad range of physical contexts, including classical plasmas and electrolytes
[
9
,
4
,
24
,
51
]
, trapped ions and cold atomic gases
[
13
]
, as well as rotating fermionic systems and effective descriptions of quantum Hall states
[
20
,
28
,
67
,
58
,
73
]
.

A unifying theoretical framework for these systems is provided by
Riesz gases

[
70
]
, which describe
N
identical particles in dimension
d
interacting through a repulsive pairwise potential of the form
|x_{i}-x_{j}|^{-s}
, with
x_{i}
’s denoting the positions of the particles. The exponent
s
governs the effective interaction range.
By varying
s
, Riesz gases interpolate between Coulomb systems (
s=d-2
), logarithmic interactions (
s\to 0
) which play a central role in random matrix theory
[
66
,
39
,
14
]
, and more general power-law interactions. The interplay among the interaction exponent
s
, the spatial dimension
d
, and the confining potential determines both the shape of the equilibrium measure and the structure of its support, thereby offering a flexible framework to investigate how dimensionality and interaction range influence collective behaviour. In particular, these systems exhibit qualitatively distinct regimes depending on whether the interactions are genuinely long-ranged (
s<d
) or effectively short-ranged (
s>d
).

While many general properties of Riesz gases–such as the existence of equilibrium measures, large-deviation principles, and qualitative features of minimisers–are now well understood
[
61
,
72
]
, explicit descriptions of equilibrium densities remain scarce. In one dimension, interesting progress has been made recently, in particular in the physics literature: for the harmonic potential
[
3
]
, and in the presence of an additional hard wall
[
35
,
53
,
54
,
32
,
33
]
, the equilibrium measure can be computed explicitly, often in connection with the statistics of the rightmost particle; see also
[
59
]
in the context of linear and counting statistics. In higher dimensions, however, and for general confining potentials, such explicit results are largely unavailable, leaving many questions about equilibrium structures open. Even for radially symmetric potentials, which is the setting we focus on in this paper, closed-form expressions are typically available only in special situations, such as harmonic confinement or for specific interaction exponents.
Notable examples include the Coulomb gas
s=d-2
, as well as more exotic
“iterated Coulomb gases” with
s=d-4

[
19
]
; see also
[
18
]
for
s=d-3
.
In this work, we study rotationally invariant Riesz gases in arbitrary dimension under radially symmetric external potentials and identify broad
classes of equilibrium densities that admit explicit expressions.

The Riesz gas studied in this paper is defined as follows. For positive integers
N
and
d
, let
X_{N}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N}
be a point configuration.
We consider the associated
N
–particle Hamiltonian

(1.1)

\mathcal{H}_{N}(X_{N}):=\sum_{1\leq i\neq j\leq N}g(x_{i}-x_{j})+N\sum_{i=1}^{N}V(x_{i}),

where
V:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to(-\infty,+\infty)
is a confining potential and

(1.2)

g(x):=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{s}|x|^{-s},&s\neq 0,\\
-\log|x|,&s=0,\end{cases}\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Without loss of generality, we often normalise the confining potential by
V(0)=0.

In particular, the choice
s=d-2
yields the
d
–dimensional Coulomb gas. Note that the case
s=0
, corresponding to log gases
[
39
]
, can be formally recovered from the general Riesz gas by taking the limit since

(1.3)

-\log|x|=\lim_{s\to 0}s^{-1}(|x|^{-s}-1).

A special feature of the Coulomb potential is that it satisfies the
d
-dimensional Poisson equation

(1.4)

\Delta g(x)|_{s=d-2}=-c_{d}\delta(x),\qquad c_{d}=2\pi^{d/2}/\Gamma(d/2).

Here
\Delta
is the Laplacian,
\delta(x)
is the
d
-dimensional Dirac delta, and
c_{d}
is the surface area of the unit ball in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
We consider the canonical Gibbs measure at inverse temperature
\beta>0
, given by

(1.5)

d\mathbb{P}_{N,\beta}(X_{N})=\frac{1}{Z_{N,\beta}}\exp\Big(-\frac{\beta}{2}N^{-\frac{s}{d}}\mathcal{H}_{N}(X_{N})\Big)dX_{N}.

Here we adopt a scaling under which the resulting equilibrium measure is supported on a compact set. Other conventions are also commonly used in the literature, in which the support grows with
N
; see e.g.
[
3
]
.

By definition, the equilibrium measure
\mu_{V}
is the unique minimiser of the energy

(1.6)

I_{V}[\sigma]:=\iint_{(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{2}}g(x-y)\,d\sigma(x)\,d\sigma(y)+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}V(x)\,d\sigma(x),

subject to the constraint that the total charge
Q=\int\sigma(x)\,dx
is conserved.
Furthermore, according to Frostman’s theorem
[
44
]
(see also
[
36
, Theorem 2]
), for
0<s<d
,
\mu_{V}
is characterised by the Euler–Lagrange condition

(1.7)

2\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(x-y)\,d\mu_{V}(y)+V(x)\begin{cases}=c&\textup{if }x\in\textup{supp}(\mu_{V}),\\
\geq c&\textup{if }x\not\in\textup{supp}(\mu_{V})\;.\end{cases}

Here the constant
c
is called the (modified) Robin’s constant.

In this work, we aim to find a class of potentials
V
and its associated equilibrium measure supported on the unit ball
\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:|x|\leq 1\}
for which both admit an explicit expression.
Our starting point is to prescribe the equilibrium measure and then determine the corresponding potential. Specifically, upon a minor abuse of notation we write

d\mu(x)=\mu(x)\,dx
, where

(1.8)

\mu(x)={d\over c_{d}}\bigg(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}|x|^{2k}\bigg)\cdot\mathbbm{1}_{\{|x|\leq 1\}}\,.

Here,
c_{d}
is given as in (
1.4
).
Assuming that
\mu
defines a valid probability density, we then ask:

What is the confining potential
V(x)
for which
\mu

arises as the corresponding equilibrium measure?

We aim to contribute to this question for a broad class of external potentials.

Before addressing the general Riesz gas case, we first focus on the Coulomb interaction. In the Coulomb case
s=d-2
, the equilibrium problem admits a particularly transparent formulation.
The external potential is characterised by the Poisson equation
(
1.4
) with
g(x)|_{s=d-2}
replaced by
{1\over 2}V(x)|_{s=d-2}
and
the Dirac delta replaced by
-\mu(x)
as given by (
1.8
). This reads

(1.9)

\displaystyle\Delta\Big(V(x)|_{s=d-2}\Big)=2d\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}|x|^{2n}.

Recalling that the radial part of the
d
-dimensional Laplacian is

{1\over r^{d-1}}{d\over dr}r^{d-1}{d\over dr}
with
(r=|x|),

a power series ansatz for
V(x)
together with our choice of normalisation
V(0)=0
shows that

(1.10)

\displaystyle V(x)|_{s=d-2}=\frac{d}{2}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{a_{n}}{(n+\frac{d}{2})(n+1)}|x|^{2n+2}.

From the perspective of equilibrium measures, the equality part of the
Euler–Lagrange condition (
1.7
) is already satisfied by the above computation.
The simplest way to ensure that the inequality part of (
1.7
) is also satisfied is to choose the external potential
V(x)=+\infty
for
|x|>1
.
In this case, the potential (
1.10
) with a hard-wall constraint outside

|x|>1
indeed gives rise to (
1.8
) as an equilibrium measure; see
[
29
]
.
A natural question, which also arises in applications (see e.g.
[
71
]
), is whether defining the external potential
V(x)
for all

x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
via (
1.10
) preserves the validity of the
Euler–Lagrange conditions.
For a radially symmetric density in the Coulomb case, with
Q=\int_{|x|<1}\mu(x)\,dx
the mass of the measure, it is a celebrated result of Newton that for the outside region
|x|>1
,
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(x-y)\,d\mu_{V}(y)=Qg(x)
. Using this identity, one finds that the inequality part of the Euler–Lagrange condition (
1.7
) requires that, for
|x|>1
,

(1.11)

\displaystyle 2Qg(x)+V(x)|_{s=d-2}\geq c=2\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(y)\,d\mu_{V}(y).

When the Coulomb condition
s=d-2
is removed, the preceding arguments for
deriving
V(x)
on
|x|\leq 1
and for verifying the Euler–Lagrange conditions
after extending
V(x)
to all of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
are no longer applicable.
This leads to substantial additional difficulties in the study of general Riesz gases and partly explains why relatively few explicit examples are known; see
[
19
, Section 2]
for several known cases and related
conjectures.

In this setting, the computation of the external potential requires evaluating a genuinely
d
-dimensional integral.
However, as we shall see, the assumption of radial symmetry reduces this problem to a one-dimensional integral, albeit with a seemingly complicated integrand.
Moreover, in order to ensure the inequality part of the Euler–Lagrange
conditions, one is naturally led to the problem of generalising (
1.11
).

Our main results, which will be presented in detail in the following section, can be summarised as follows.

•

In Theorem
2.1
, we formulate the general framework.
We show how to construct an external potential
V
for which the equality part of the Euler–Lagrange condition holds, and we reformulate the inequality part of the Euler–Lagrange condition as an explicit inequality involving the coefficients defining the equilibrium measure.
This general result is discussed in Subsection
2.1
.
We provide two explicit examples for which the required inequalities can be verified. See Table
1
below for a summary.

•

The first example, presented in Theorem
2.4
, concerns external potentials expressed in terms of Gauss hypergeometric functions
{}_{2}F_{1}
.
In this case, the corresponding equilibrium density is proportional to

(1-|x|^{2})^{\alpha}
with
\alpha>-1
. This example is treated in Subsection
2.2
.

•

The second example, presented in Theorem
2.6
, considers purely power-type external potentials of the form
|x|^{2p}
, where
p
is a nonnegative integer.
In this setting, the density of the equilibrium measure admits an explicit representation in terms of a
{}_{2}F_{1}
function.
This case is discussed in Subsection
2.3
.

•

Finally, in Theorem
2.7
, we study the Coulomb case with a half-space constraint.
By analysing the points at which the Euler–Lagrange inequality is violated, we derive a necessary condition under which the ensemble is fully confined to the boundary hyperplane.
This result is presented in Subsection
2.4
.

At a technical level, the most challenging part of our analysis arises in the soft wall setting, where one must establish the inequality part of the Euler–Lagrange condition (
1.7
).
In contrast, the classical result of Riesz
[
70
]
(see Corollary
2.3
below) for the equilibrium measure inside a unit ball in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with constant external potential makes a hard wall assumption that the potential is infinite outside of the ball. This makes the inequality part of the Euler-Lagrange condition redundant.

2.1.
Formulation for general case

To handle the general setting, we first introduce a sequence of real numbers
\{a_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
subject to the following conditions.

Definition 1
.

For
d\geq 1
, let
\{a_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
be a sequence of real numbers satisfying

(2.1)

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{a_{n}}{2n+d}=\frac{1}{d},

and assume that, for every
0\leq x\leq 1
, the series

(2.2)

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}x^{2n}\qquad\textup{and}\qquad\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{s}{2}+n)\Gamma(\frac{s-d}{2}+n+1)}{\Gamma(\frac{d}{2}+n)\,n!}\bigg(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{a_{k}}{2n+s-2k-d}\bigg)x^{2n}

are well defined (i.e. convergent), and that the first is non-negative.
Under these conditions, we say that the sequence
\{a_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
is
admissible
.

Since
\mu(x)=\mu(|x|)
in (
1.8
) for all
d\geq 1
, we have

(2.3)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mu(x)\,dx=\frac{2\pi^{d/2}}{\Gamma(\frac{d}{2})}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mu(r)r^{d-1}\,dr;

note that on the LHS the notation
dx
denotes the

d
-dimensional Lebesgue measure, in keeping with the domain of integration.
Thus the condition (
2.1
) ensures that
\mu
is, if non-negative, a probability measure, by integrating to unity.

We formulate a class of rotationally symmetric potentials
V
together with their associated equilibrium measure. Recall that the (rising) Pochhammer symbol is given by
(x)_{n}=\Gamma(x+n)/\Gamma(x)
.

Theorem 2.1

(
Radially symmetric equilibrium measure
)
.

Let
d\geq 1
and
s\in[d-2,d)
.
For an admissible sequence
\{a_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
|x|\leq 1
, define

(2.4)

V(x):=\frac{2d}{s}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(\frac{s}{2})_{n}(\frac{s-d}{2}+1)_{n}}{(\frac{d}{2})_{n}\,n!}\bigg(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{a_{k}}{2n+s-2k-d}\bigg)|x|^{2n}.

For
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, let
\mu(x)
be given by (
1.8
).
Then we have the following.

(i)

(Euler-Lagrange equality)
For any
|x|<1
, we have

(2.5)

\frac{2}{s}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{d\mu(y)}{|x-y|^{s}}+V(x)={2d\over s}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}{a_{k}\over 2k+d-s}.

Consequently,
\mu
is the equilibrium measure associated with the potential

(2.6)

V_{\rm h}(x):=\begin{cases}V(x)&\textup{if }|x|\leq 1,\\
+\infty&\textup{if }|x|>1.\end{cases}

(ii)

(Euler-Lagrange inequality)
Suppose furthermore that
V
is well defined for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Then
\mu
is the equilibrium measure for
V
if and only if the inequality

(2.7)

\displaystyle\frac{2d}{s}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\frac{s}{2})_{n}(\frac{s-d}{2}+1)_{n}}{(\frac{d}{2})_{n}\,n!}\bigg(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{a_{k}}{2k+d+2n}\bigg)\,\frac{1}{|x|^{2n+s}}+V(x)\geq{2d\over s}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}{a_{k}\over 2k+d-s}

holds for every
|x|\geq 1
.

In the definition of (
2.6
), the hard wall is placed at the “natural” boundary of the support of the equilibrium measure.
Such a regime is commonly referred to as “the soft edge meeting the hard edge”; see e.g.
[
27
,
6
,
7
,
31
,
29
,
30
]
.

Note that by (
1.6
) and (
1.7
), we have

(2.8)

\displaystyle I_{V}[\mu_{V}]=\frac{c}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}V(x)\,d\mu_{V}(x).

As an immediate consequence of (
2.8
), in the two situations described in Theorem
2.1
(i) and (ii), the associated energy admits an explicit expression:

(2.9)

\displaystyle I_{V}[\mu]=I_{V_{\rm h}}[\mu]=\frac{d}{s}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{a_{k}}{2k+d-s}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{|x|<1}V(x)\,d\mu(x).

Remark 1

(Coulomb gases
s=d-2
)
.

Note that as
s\to d-2
, we have

\displaystyle V(x)\sim\frac{d}{\Gamma(\frac{s-d+2}{2})}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(n+\frac{d}{2})\,(n+1)}\bigg(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{a_{k}}{2n+2+s-2k-d}\delta_{k,n}\bigg)|x|^{2n+2}.

Noting that
\Gamma(\frac{s-d+2}{2})(2n+2+s-2k-d)\delta_{k,n}\to 2
in this limit, it follows that
V(x)|_{s=d-2}
is given by (
1.10
).
Indeed, since

\Delta\Big(|x|^{2n+2}\Big)=2(n+1)(2n+d)|x|^{2n},

where
\Delta
is the Laplacian, we have the Poisson equation (
1.10
).
Also, the terms in (
2.7
) in this limit can be identified with the corresponding terms in (
1.11
) with
Q=1
.

Remark 2

(Connection to the short-range regime
s\to d
)
.

We now take the formal limit
s\to d
, which gives a connection to the short range regime.
In the limit
s\to d
, we have

\displaystyle V(x)\sim 2\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\bigg(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{a_{k}}{2n+s-2k-d}\delta_{k,n}\bigg)|x|^{2n}\sim-\frac{2}{d-s}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_{n}|x|^{2n}=\frac{2}{d-s}\bigg(a_{0}-\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}|x|^{2n}\bigg).

We write

(2.10)

\displaystyle\widetilde{V}(x)=\lim_{s\to d}\frac{d-s}{2}V(x).

Then it follows that

(2.11)

\displaystyle\mu(x)|_{s=d}=a_{0}-\widetilde{V}(x).

This is consistent with the explicit formula for the equilibrium measure in the short-range case. More precisely, it follows from
[
3
,
48
]
that, in the short-range regime, the equilibrium measure is given by a Thomas–Fermi type density of the form
(c-\widetilde{V}(x))^{d/s}
.
We note that, by convention, in the short-range regime
s>d
, the prefactor
N
in front of the potential
V
in (
1.1
) is replaced by
N^{s/d}
. In the critical case
s=d
, however, the definition of the prefactor requires an additional
\log N
correction; see
[
49
,
50
,
57
]
as well as Appendix C of the arXiv version of
[
3
]
. Such logarithmic corrections are consistent with the need for the renormalisation appearing in (
2.10
).

2.2.
Power type equilibrium measure

As a first prominent example, we consider a class of polynomial potentials whose associated equilibrium measure is of power type. To proceed, let us introduce the corresponding sequence
\{a_{k}\}
.
We first recall that the hypergeometric function
{}_{2}F_{1}
is defined by the Gauss series

(2.13)

{}_{2}F_{1}(a,b;c;z):=\frac{\Gamma(c)}{\Gamma(a)\Gamma(b)}\sum_{s=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma(a+s)\Gamma(b+s)}{\Gamma(c+s)s!}z^{s},\qquad(|z|<1)

and by analytic continuation elsewhere. Note that if
a=-m
with
m=0,1,2,\dots
, the hypergeometric function becomes the polynomial

(2.14)

{}_{2}F_{1}(-m,b;c;z):=\frac{\Gamma(c)}{\Gamma(b)}\sum_{s=0}^{m}(-1)^{s}\frac{\Gamma(b+s)}{\Gamma(c+s)}\binom{m}{s}z^{s}.

Lemma 2.2

(
Admissible sequence for the power type equilibrium measure
)
.

Let
d\geq 1
and
s\in[d-2,d)
. Define

(2.15)

a_{k}=\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+1+\frac{d}{2})}{\Gamma(\alpha+1)\Gamma(\frac{d}{2}+1)}\,\frac{\Gamma(-\alpha+k)}{\Gamma(-\alpha)k!},\qquad\alpha>-1.

(We will see in (
2.18
) that this gives rise to a well defined non-negative density.)
Then the potential (
2.4
) associated with the corresponding density (
1.8
) is given by

(2.16)

V(x)=-\frac{d}{s}\,\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+1+\frac{d}{2})}{\Gamma(\frac{d}{2}+1)}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{d-s}{2})}{\Gamma(\alpha+1+\frac{d-s}{2})}\Big({}_{2}F_{1}(\tfrac{s}{2},\tfrac{s-d}{2}-\alpha;\tfrac{d}{2};|x|^{2})-1\Big).

Furthermore, if
\alpha=\frac{s-d}{2}+n
with
n=0,1,2,\dots
, the potential
V
in (
2.16
) becomes a polynomial

(2.17)

\displaystyle\begin{split}V(x)=\frac{\Gamma(\frac{s}{2}+n+1)\Gamma(\frac{d-s}{2})}{\Gamma(\frac{s}{2}+1)}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{(-1)^{k+1}}{k!(n-k)!}\frac{\Gamma(k+\frac{s}{2})}{\Gamma(k+\frac{d}{2})}|x|^{2k}.\end{split}

As an immediate consequence of Theorem
2.1
(i) and Lemma
2.2
, we have the following corollary.

Corollary 2.3

(
Power type equilibrium measure with hard edges
)
.

Let
d\geq 1
and
s\in[d-2,d)
, and assume
\alpha>-1
.
Define
V_{\mathrm{h}}
as in (
2.6
), where
V
is given by (
2.16
). Then the equilibrium measure associated with
V_{\rm h}
is given by

(2.18)

d\mu(x)=\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+1+\frac{d}{2})}{\Gamma(\alpha+1)\Gamma(\frac{d}{2}+1)}\,(1-|x|^{2})^{\alpha}\cdot\mathbbm{1}_{\{|x|\leq 1\}}\,\frac{\Gamma(\frac{d}{2}+1)}{\pi^{d/2}}\,dx.

Proof.

We see that (
2.18
) follows from (
1.8
), (
2.15
) and the binomial expansion

(2.19)

(1-z)^{\alpha}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma(-\alpha+k)}{\Gamma(-\alpha)k!}z^{k},\qquad(|z|<1).

Then the corollary immediately follows from Theorem
2.1
(i).
∎

Remark 4
.

We discuss some important special cases of Corollary

.

1. (Box potential)
For the special case when
\alpha=\frac{s-d}{2}
, the potential in Corollary
2.3
reduces to the so-called
box potential
, namely
V_{\rm h}
vanishes inside the unit ball and equals
+\infty
outside. For this box potential, the associated equilibrium measure is given by

(2.20)

d\mu_{\rm box}(x)=\frac{\Gamma(\frac{s}{2}+1)}{\Gamma(\frac{s-d}{2}+1)\Gamma(\frac{d}{2}+1)}\,(1-|x|^{2})^{\frac{s-d}{2}}\cdot\mathbbm{1}_{\{|x|\leq 1\}}\,\frac{\Gamma(\frac{d}{2}+1)}{\pi^{d/2}}\,dx,

as read off from (
2.18
).
This result goes back to Riesz
[
70
]
; see also

[
18
, Theorem 1.1]
and
[
11
]
.
We also mention that for
-2<s\leq d-2
, the equilibrium measure for the box potential is known to become singular and concentrates uniformly on the boundary of the unit ball. For
s=d-1
this result can be deduced by projecting from a uniformly charged surface of a
d+1
-dimensional ball with respect to the Coulomb potential in dimension
d+1
; see
[
15
, Proposition 4.2]
.

2. (Quadratic potential)
From (
2.17
), for
n=1
the potential
V
is quadratic, while (
2.18
) gives that the density is proportional to
(1-|x|^{2})^{1+(s-d)/2}
. It is of interest to compare this to the density profile for
d
-dimensional spinless fermions in a harmonic trap, in the scaled large
N
limit. For the latter, one has the Thomas–Fermi density which is proportional to
(1-|x|^{2})^{d/2}
, where
|x|
is measured in units of the maximum shell label; see e.g.
[
17
]
. For
s\in[d-2,d)
as required by Lemma
2.2
and Corollary
2.3
, the exponents of the densities only coincide for
d=1
,
s=0
. In fact then there is an equivalence at finite
N
between the squared ground state wave function of the Fermi gas and the Boltzmann factor of the log-gas system with coupling
\beta=2
; see e.g.
[
34
]
.

Note that if we consider the potential
V
in (
2.16
) with soft edges, instead of
V_{\mathrm{h}}
as in Corollary
2.3
, not every choice of
\alpha>-1
yields a valid potential. In particular, the inequality (
2.7
) fails for arbitrary
\alpha>-1
. For instance, in (
2.17
), when
n
is even the leading term of the polynomial is negative, which is not admissible for defining the model, in the sense that the second Euler-Lagrange condition cannot be satisfied. On the other hand, when
n
is odd the potential is well defined and the associated equilibrium measure can be constructed. We formalise this in the following theorem.

Theorem 2.4

(
Power type equilibrium measure with soft edges
)
.

Let
d\geq 1
and
s\in[d-2,d)
. For
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, let

(2.21)

\displaystyle\begin{split}V(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{2m+1}b_{k}|x|^{2k},\qquad b_{k}=\frac{\Gamma(\frac{d-s}{2})\Gamma(2m+2+\frac{s}{2})(-1)^{k+1}}{\Gamma(k+\frac{d}{2})k!(2m+1-k)!}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{s}{2}+k)}{\Gamma(\frac{s}{2}+1)}.\end{split}

Then the associated equilibrium measure
\mu
is given by (
2.18
), where

(2.22)

\alpha=\frac{s-d}{2}+2m+1.

Furthermore, the associated energy
I_{V}[\mu]
is given by

(2.23)

\displaystyle\begin{split}I_{V}[\mu]&=\frac{\Gamma(\frac{d-s}{2})\Gamma(2m+2+\frac{s}{2})}{\Gamma(\frac{d}{2})}\bigg(\frac{1}{(2m)!}\frac{1}{s}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{2m+1}\frac{(-1)^{k+1}}{k!(2m+1-k)!}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{s}{2}+k)\Gamma(\frac{s}{2}+2m+2)}{\Gamma(\frac{s}{2}+1)\Gamma(\frac{s}{2}+k+2m+2)}\bigg).\end{split}

The first few polynomials in (
2.21
) are given as follows.

•

For
m=0
,

(2.24)

V(x)|_{m=0}=\frac{\Gamma(\frac{d-s}{2})\Gamma(2+\frac{s}{2})}{\Gamma(\frac{d}{2}+1)}|x|^{2}.

•

For
m=1
,

(2.25)

\displaystyle\begin{split}V(x)|_{m=1}&=\frac{\Gamma(\frac{d-s}{2})\Gamma(4+\frac{s}{2})}{\Gamma(\frac{d}{2}+1)}\frac{(s+2)(s+4)}{6(d+2)(d+4)}\Big(|x|^{6}-\frac{3(d+4)}{s+4}|x|^{4}+\frac{3(d+2)(d+4)}{(s+2)(s+4)}|x|^{2}\Big).\end{split}

Remark 5

For the potential

where
d\geq 1
and
|y|<1
.
This identity was also presented in

, Lemma 2.4]
, based on the theory of fractional differential operators
[

, Corollary 4]
. For the case
d=1
; see
[

, Eq.(2.3)]
and
[
3
, Eq.(9)]
. See also
[

]
and the references therein for further examples of such identities.

Remark 6

(Expression in terms of the Jacobi polynomial)
.

Recall the hypergeometric expression of the Jacobi polynomial
[
68
, Eq.(15.9.1)]

(2.27)

P_{n}^{(a,b)}(x)=\frac{\Gamma(n+a+1)}{\Gamma(a+1)\,n!}{}_{2}F_{1}(-n,n+a+b+1;a+1;\tfrac{1-x}{2}).

By using this, one can observe that the potential
V
in (
2.17
) can be expressed as

(2.28)

V(x)=-\frac{2}{s}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{d-s}{2})\Gamma(2m+2+\frac{s}{2})}{\Gamma(2m+1+\frac{d}{2})}\bigg(P_{2m+1}^{(a,b)}(1-2|x|^{2})-\frac{\Gamma(2m+1+\frac{d}{2})}{(2m+1)!\Gamma(\frac{d}{2})}\bigg),

where

(2.29)

a=\frac{d}{2}-1,\qquad b=-2m-1+\frac{s-d}{2}.

Remark 7

(Consistent check with
[
3
]
for the one dimensional Riesz gas)
.

For the case
d=1
and
m=0
, the purely quadratic potential (
2.24
) takes the form

(2.30)

\displaystyle\begin{split}V(x)=\frac{2\,\Gamma(\frac{1-s}{2})\Gamma(2+\frac{s}{2})}{\sqrt{\pi}}\,x^{2}.\end{split}

The associated limiting measure is then given by (
2.18
), supported on
[-1,1]
, with exponent
\alpha=\tfrac{s+1}{2}
.

We now compare this with the results of
[
3
]
, where the one-dimensional Riesz gas with Hamiltonian

(2.31)

\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{N}x_{j}^{2}+\frac{J\textup{sgn}(s)}{2}\sum_{j\not=k}\frac{1}{|x_{j}-x_{k}|^{s}}=\frac{J\textup{sgn}(s)s}{2}\bigg(\frac{1}{J\textup{sgn}(s)s}\sum_{j=1}^{N}x_{j}^{2}+\frac{1}{s}\sum_{j\not=k}\frac{1}{|x_{j}-x_{k}|^{s}}\bigg)

was studied. Let
B(x,y)
denote the gamma function evaluation of the Euler beta integral. In the large-
N
limit, the limiting density was shown to be

(2.32)

\ell_{s}^{-1}F_{s}(y/\ell_{s}),\qquad F_{s}(z)=\frac{1}{B(\gamma_{s}+1,\gamma_{s}+1)}\Big(\frac{1}{4}-z^{2}\Big)^{\gamma_{s}}.

Here, for
-2<s<1
,

(2.33)

\gamma_{s}=\frac{s+1}{2},\qquad\ell_{s}=\Big(\frac{J|s|\pi(s+1)}{\sin(\frac{\pi}{2}(s+1))B(\frac{s+3}{2},\frac{s+3}{2})}\Big)^{\frac{1}{s+2}}.

Note that
\gamma_{s}
coincides with
\alpha=\tfrac{s+1}{2}
.
Furthermore, to enforce
\ell_{s}=2
so that the limiting support is
[-1,1]
, we choose

(2.34)

J=\frac{\sin(\frac{\pi}{2}(s+1))B(\frac{s+3}{2},\frac{s+3}{2})}{|s|\pi(s+1)}2^{s+2}.

Then since

\displaystyle\frac{1}{J\textup{sgn}(s)s}=\frac{\pi}{\sin(\frac{s+1}{2}\pi)}\frac{|s|(s+1)}{B(\frac{s+3}{2},\frac{s+3}{2})2^{s+2}\textup{sgn}(s)s}=\frac{\pi}{\sin(\frac{1-s}{2}\pi)}\frac{2\,\Gamma(2+\frac{s}{2})}{\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma(\frac{1+s}{2})},

one can see that the resulting quadratic potential is consistent with (
2.30
).

2.3.
Power type external potential

As a second prominent example, we consider the purely power type external potential. As before, we begin with introducing the associated sequence.

Lemma 2.5

(
Admissible sequence for the power type external potential
)
.

Let
d\geq 1
and
s\in[d-2,d)
. For
p\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
, let

(2.35)

a_{k}=-\frac{\sin(\pi\frac{d-s}{2})}{\pi}\frac{\Gamma(1+\frac{s}{2})}{\Gamma(\frac{d}{2})}\frac{2p+s}{d}\,\frac{\Gamma(k+\frac{d-s}{2})}{(k+\frac{d-s}{2}-p)k!}.

(We will see in (
2.37
) that this gives rise to a well defined non-negative density.)
Then the potential (
2.4
) associated with this sequence is given by

(2.36)

V(x)=\frac{\Gamma(\frac{d-s}{2})\Gamma(\frac{s}{2}+p)}{\Gamma(\frac{d}{2}+p)}\,\frac{2p+s}{2p}|x|^{2p}.

The purely power type potential (
2.36
) has been extensively investigated for log gases. For
d=1
, it is commonly referred to as the
Freud potential
(see e.g.
[
26
]
), while for
d=2
it is known as the
Mittag–Leffler potential
(see e.g.
[
21
,
23
,
5
,
12
]
).
However, for general Riesz gases, the purely power-type potential has not been extensively investigated, and we formulate its equilibrium measure in the following theorem.

Theorem 2.6

(
Power type external potential with soft edges
)
.

Let
d\geq 1
and
s\in[d-2,d)
. Let
p\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
. Then the equilibrium measure
\mu
associated with the purely power type potential
V
in (
2.36
) is given by

(2.37)

d\mu(x)=\frac{\Gamma(1+\tfrac{s}{2})}{\Gamma(1+\frac{s-d}{2})\Gamma(\frac{d}{2}+1)}\frac{2p+s}{2p-d+s}\,{}_{2}F_{1}(\tfrac{d-s}{2},\tfrac{d-s}{2}-p;\tfrac{d-s}{2}+1-p;|x|^{2})\cdot\mathbbm{1}_{\{|x|\leq 1\}}\,\frac{\Gamma(\frac{d}{2}+1)}{\pi^{d/2}}\,dx.

Furthermore, we have

(2.38)

I_{V}[\mu]=\frac{\Gamma(\tfrac{d-s}{2})\Gamma(\frac{s}{2})}{\Gamma(\frac{d}{2})}\,\frac{(2p+s)^{2}}{2p(4p+s)}.

See Figure
1
for the graphs of
\mu(|x|)
.

Remark 8

(Purely quadratic case)
.

We consider the case
p=1
. Then (
2.36
) is given by

(2.39)

V(x)|_{p=1}=\frac{\Gamma(\frac{d-s}{2})\Gamma(\frac{s}{2}+2)}{\Gamma(\frac{d}{2}+1)}\,|x|^{2}.

This coincides with the special case (
2.24
) of the previous example. Note that in the one-dimensional case
d=1
, this purely quadratic potential has been extensively studied in
[
3
]
, where all regimes with
s>-2
have been analysed, including both the long-range
(s<1)
and short-range
(s>1)
interaction regimes.
We also note that for the log gas case, the energy (
2.38
) becomes

(2.40)

\displaystyle\lim_{s\to 0}\Big(I_{V}[\mu]|_{p=1}-\frac{1}{s}\Big)=\begin{cases}\frac{3}{4}+\log 2&\textup{if }d=1,\\
\frac{3}{4}&\textup{if }d=2.\end{cases}

This corresponds to the weighted energy of the semi-circle and circular law, respectively.

Although Theorem
2.6
is, in principle, valid for
s\geq d-2
, the explicit formula for the equilibrium measure in (
2.37
) remains well defined for
p=1
even in the extended regime
s=d-3
.
In this case one finds

(2.41)

d\mu(x)=\frac{\Gamma(\frac{1+d}{2})}{\pi^{(1+d)/2}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}\,dx\;,

which coincides exactly with the result obtained in
[
18
]
. If one sets
s=d-3
and consider the case
p>1
we observe the following phenomenon. For instance if one sets
p=2
, the formula in (
2.37
), with
s=d-3
, yields

(2.42)

d\mu(x)=\frac{1}{\pi^{(1+d)/2}}\frac{d+1}{2}\Gamma\Big(\frac{d-1}{2}\Big)\frac{2x^{2}-1}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}\,dx\;.

Clearly, this density is nonnegative only for
|x|\in[\sqrt{2}/2,1]
.
Although the expression in (
2.42
) is likely not the correct equilibrium profile—since, in particular, it is not properly normalised over
|x|\in[\sqrt{2}/2,1]
—its qualitative behaviour is reminiscent of the observation made in
[
19
, Section 2.1.3]
. There, numerical simulations in the case
s=d-3
and
p>1
suggest that the equilibrium measure is not supported on the entire unit ball, but rather concentrates on a spherical shell.

Remark 9

(Freud potential for the one-dimensional log gas
d=1,s=0
)
.

For
d=1
and
s=0
, the potential (

(2.43)

V(x)|_{d=1,s=0}=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}\,\Gamma(p)}{\Gamma(p+\frac{1}{2})}x^{2p}.

Then the associated equilibrium measure is supported on

with density

(2.44)

\frac{2p}{\pi}|x|^{2p-1}\int_{1}^{1/|x|}\frac{u^{2p-1}}{\sqrt{u^{2}-1}}\,du;

[

and
[

For an application to random matrix theory, specifically to products of random matrices, we refer the reader to
[

.
Here, compared to
[

This density can be expressed in terms of the incomplete beta function as

(2.45)

-\frac{p}{\pi}|x|^{2p-1}B_{x^{2}}(\tfrac{1}{2}-p,\tfrac{1}{2})=\frac{1}{\pi}\frac{2p}{2p-1}{}_{2}F_{1}(\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{1}{2}-p;\tfrac{3}{2}-p;x^{2}),

where we have used
[

See also

2.4.
A constrained half plane equilibrium problem in the Coulomb case

In this subsection, we present another application of Theorem
2.1
for Coulomb gases with a half-space constraint.

We begin by discussing the two-dimensional case.
Let
x=(x_{0},x_{1})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
. For the two-dimensional Coulomb potential
-\log|x|
, consider the problem of finding the equilibrium measure when the external potential is given by

(2.47)

V({x})=\begin{cases}|{x}|^{2},&\textup{if }x_{0}>a,\\
+\infty,&\textup{otherwise}.\end{cases}

In words, there is a hard wall to the left of
x_{0}=a
, while to the right there is a confining harmonic potential. For
a=-\infty
the former has no effect, and we know from
(
1.8
) and (
1.10
) with
a_{0}=1
,
a_{1}=a_{2}=\cdots=0
and
d=2
that the equilibrium measure is the constant density
{1\over\pi}
supported on the unit disk. Moreover, the fact that the case
a=-\infty
corresponds to soft wall boundary conditions tells us that this remains the equilibrium measure for all
a<-1
. In
[
8
]
it was established that for
a\in(-1,\sqrt{2})
that the equilibrium measure decomposes as a singular component supported on
x_{0}=a
and an absolutely continuous component supported in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Moreover, for
a\geq\sqrt{2}
, the equilibrium density is fully supported on
x_{0}=a

with the semi-circle functional form

(2.48)

\sigma(x)=\delta(x_{0}-a){\sqrt{2-x_{1}^{2}}\over\pi}\mathbbm{1}_{\{|x_{1}|\leq\sqrt{2}\}}.

We now consider the
(d+1)
-dimensional Riesz gas with parameter
s
, specialised to the Coulomb case so that

s=d-1
.
Let
\hat{x}=(x_{0},x_{1},\dots,x_{d})
, and with
a\in\mathbb{R}
consider the potential

(2.49)

V(\hat{x})=\begin{cases}|\hat{x}|^{2},&\textup{if }x_{0}>a,\\
+\infty,&\textup{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Note that if
a=-\infty
, thus there is no constraint, then the equilibrium measure is uniform on the unit sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
,

(2.50)

d\mu_{V}(\hat{x})|_{a=-\infty}=\frac{\Gamma(\frac{d+3}{2})}{\pi^{(d+1)/2}}\,\mathbbm{1}_{\{|\hat{x}|\leq 1\}}\,d\hat{x};

this follows from the case
a_{0}=1
,
a_{1}=a_{2}=\cdots=0
of (
1.8
) and (
1.10
).

For
d=0
and
d=1
, an interesting critical phenomenon was obtained in
[
35
,
8
]
. As described in the case
d=1
in Section
2.4
, this is characterised by a critical value
a_{\mathrm{cri}}=a_{\mathrm{cri}}(d)
separating two of three possible regimes. We revisit those here.

(i)

No effective hard wall
:
a<-1
. In this regime, the hard-wall constraint has no effective influence; consequently, the equilibrium measure coincides with that in (
2.50
) for the unconstrained case.

(ii)

Partially effective hard wall
:
a\in(-1,a_{\mathrm{cri}})
. The equilibrium measure consists of an absolutely continuous component supported in
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
, together with a singular component supported on the codimension-one set
\{a\}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

(iii)

Fully effective hard wall
:
a>a_{\mathrm{cri}}
. The equilibrium measure becomes completely confined to
\{a\}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

To be more precise the following was shown.

•

In one-dimension
d+1=1
, it was shown in
[
35
]
that the threshold is at
a_{\rm cri}(0)=1
, and for
a>1
, the measure is purely Dirac measure

(2.51)

d\mu_{V}(x)=\delta_{a}(dx_{0}).

•

In two-dimension
d+1=2
, it was shown in
[
8
]
that the threshold is at
a_{\rm cri}(1)=\sqrt{2}
. For
a>\sqrt{2}
, the measure is purely one-dimensional support
with corresponding density (
2.48
). (See also Remark
17
for an extension to the elliptic potential interpolating between the one- and two-dimensional regimes.)

Such a phenomenon is a special feature of the Coulomb case. For the general Riesz gas, it might be that no fully effective hard wall arises. For instance, the log gas in one-dimension, the resulting measure is absolutely continuous with respect to the one-dimensional measure. Many works have addressed this situation; as an incomplete listing see
[
32
,
33
,
62
,
10
,
52
,
69
,
65
,
43
,
64
]
.
One should note for the Coulomb case, the effect of the half plane constraint is not that of a balayage measure
[
2
,
1
,
22
]
(see also the recent review
[
15
, Section 5]
), where all the displaced charge density due to the barrier constraint accumulates on the boundary.
The latter would hold,
for example, if the condition
x_{0}>a
in (
2.49
) was changed to
|\hat{x}|>a
.

We can establish a partial analogue for higher dimensions.

Theorem 2.7

(
Coulomb gases constrained on the half space
)
.

Let
d=0,1,2,\dots
and define

(2.52)

a_{\rm cri}\equiv a_{\rm cri}(d):=(d+1)\Big(\frac{\Gamma(\frac{d}{2}+1)}{\sqrt{\pi}\,\Gamma(\frac{d+3}{2})}\Big)^{\frac{d}{d+1}}.

Then we have the following.

(i)

The equilibrium measure is supported in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
only if
a\geq a_{\rm cri}
.

(ii)

Suppose that the equilibrium measure is supported in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Introduce the notation
\hat{x}=(x_{0},x)\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
with
x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then the equilibrium measure is given by

(2.53)

d\mu_{V}(\hat{x})=\delta_{a}(dx_{0})\,d\mu_{W}(x),

where
\hat{x}=(x_{0},x)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and

(2.54)

d\mu_{W}(x)=\frac{2R}{\pi}\,(1-|x/R|^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}\cdot\mathbbm{1}_{\{|x|\leq R\}}\,\frac{\Gamma(\frac{d}{2}+1)}{\pi^{d/2}}\,dx,\qquad R=\Big(\frac{\sqrt{\pi}\,\Gamma(\frac{d+3}{2})}{\Gamma(\frac{d}{2}+1)}\Big)^{\frac{1}{d+1}}.

Furthermore, for any
\beta>0
, we have

(2.55)

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{\log\mathbb{P}[x_{0}\geq a]}{N^{\frac{d+3}{d+1}}}=-\frac{\beta}{2}C(a;d),

where

(2.56)

C(a;d)=a^{2}+\Big(\frac{\sqrt{\pi}\,\Gamma(\frac{d+3}{2})}{\Gamma(\frac{d}{2}+1)}\Big)^{\frac{2}{d+1}}\frac{d(d+1)}{(d-1)(d+3)}-\frac{(d+1)^{2}}{(d-1)(d+3)}.

The values of
C(a,d)
for the first few values of
d
are

(2.57)

\displaystyle C(a;0)=a^{2}+\frac{1}{3},\qquad C(a;1)=a^{2}+\frac{\log 2}{2},\qquad C(a;2)=a^{2}+\frac{5}{9}\Big(\frac{(6\pi)^{3/2}}{6}-1\Big).

Note that in (
2.56
), there is a removable singularity at
d=1
, since
\lim_{d\to 1}C(a,d)=C(a,1)
exists. Also, it is easy to check that
C(a;d)>a^{2}
.
The leading asymptotic behaviour of
\mathbb{P}[x_{0}>a]
in (
2.55
) is similar in spirit to many works using the so-called Coulomb gas method; see e.g.
[
63
]
for a result of this type relating to the case
d=1
and
a=0
, and the review
[
41
]
for an historical introduction and development.

As a conjecture, we propose the following, as is known from

[
8
,
35
]
for
d=0,1
.

Conjecture 2.8
.

The equilibrium measure is supported in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
if and only if
a\geq a_{\rm cri}
.

At present, we are not aware of a rigorous proof of this statement in full
generality. Nevertheless, in Appendix
A
we establish
Conjecture
2.8
in the special case
d=3
.

Remark 11
.

By Lemma
4.1
, the conjecture reduces to proving that, for
a\geq a_{\mathrm{cri}}
,
F(t,x)\geq F(0,0)
for any
t\geq 0
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

3.
Proofs of Theorems
2.1
,
2.4
, and

This section is devoted to the proofs of Theorems
2.1
,
2.4
, and
2.6
. Each proof is presented in a separate subsection below.

3.1.
Proof of Theorem
2.1

In this subsection, we prove Theorem
2.1
. It is sufficient to consider the case
d-2<s\leq d
, since the Coulomb gas case
s=d-2
follows from consideration of the Poisson equation, as previously discussed.

For the probability measure
\mu
given by (
1.8
), let
f
be the radial density, i.e.

(3.1)

f(r)=d\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}r^{2k+d-1}.

Notice here that by (
2.1
), we have
\int_{0}^{1}f(r)\,dr=1
.

The overall strategy for the proof of Theorem
2.1
is summarised as follows.

•

In Lemma
3.1
, using rotation invariance, we rewrite the potential

\int|x-y|^{-s}\,d\mu(x)

originally given as a
d
-dimensional integral, in terms of a one-dimensional real integral involving the radial density (
3.1
). Here, the key input is the Funk-Hecke formula (
3.4
). As a consequence, the expression contains a certain hypergeometric function; see (
3.2
).

•

In Lemma
3.2
, by applying the Gauss series expansion of the hypergeometric function (
2.13
) together with the quadratic transformation (
3.10
), we express the integral from the previous step as a series involving the moments of the radial density. Up to this step, we exclude the case
d=1
and assume
d\geq 2
.

•

In Proposition
3.4
, we evaluate this series using the expansion (
3.1
). A crucial step here is an algebraic identity (Lemma
3.3
) which allows us to significantly simplify the resulting expression. This proposition also applies when
d=1
, in which case the result follows directly without the previous reduction steps. Consequently, for general
d\geq 1
and
s\in(d-2,d)
, we can explicitly rewrite the Euler–Lagrange equations in the form stated in Theorem
2.1
.

We begin with the first step.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
d\geq 2
and
s\in(d-2,d)
. For the probability measure
\mu
in (
1.8
), let
f
be the associated radial density (
3.1
).
Let
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then we have

(3.2)

\displaystyle\int\frac{d\mu(x)}{|x-y|^{s}}=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{(|y|+r)^{s}}\,{}_{2}F_{1}\Big(\frac{s}{2},\frac{d-1}{2}\,;\,d-1\,;\,\frac{4r|y|}{(|y|+r)^{2}}\Big)\,f(r)\,dr.

Consequently, the Euler-Lagrange variational conditions (
1.7
) are given by

(3.3)

\frac{2}{s}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{(|y|+r)^{s}}\,{}_{2}F_{1}\Big(\frac{s}{2},\frac{d-1}{2}\,;\,d-1\,;\,\frac{4r|y|}{(|y|+r)^{2}}\Big)\,f(r)\,dr+V(y)\begin{cases}=c&\textup{if }|y|\leq 1,\\
\geq c&\textup{if }|y|>1.\end{cases}

Proof.

First, we recall the Funk-Hecke formula; see e.g.
[
18
, Appendix A.2]
. For
d\geq 2
, let
\sigma_{S_{1}}
be the uniform probability measure on the unit centered sphere
S_{1}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:|x|=1\}.
Then for
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
|z|=1
, we have

(3.4)

\int_{S_{1}}g(z\cdot x)\,d\sigma_{S_{1}}(x)=\frac{\Gamma(\frac{d}{2})}{\sqrt{\pi}\,\Gamma(\frac{d-1}{2})}\int_{-1}^{1}g(t)(1-t^{2})^{\frac{d-3}{2}}\,dt.

By applying this, we have

(3.5)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\int\frac{d\mu(x)}{|x-y|^{s}}&=\frac{\Gamma(\frac{d}{2}+1)}{\pi^{d/2}}\frac{1}{d}\int_{0}^{1}\bigg(\int_{S_{1}}\Big(|y|^{2}+r^{2}-2|y|r\frac{y}{|y|}\cdot u\Big)^{-\frac{s}{2}}\,du\bigg)f(r)\,dr\\
&=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{d}{2})}{\Gamma(\frac{d-1}{2})}\int_{0}^{1}\bigg(\int_{-1}^{1}(1-t^{2})^{\frac{d-3}{2}}\Big(|y|^{2}+r^{2}-2|y|rt\Big)^{-\frac{s}{2}}\,dt\bigg)f(r)\,dr.\end{split}

Here, we have used
|S_{1}|=2\pi^{d/2}/\Gamma(d/2)
.
Note that

\displaystyle\quad\int_{-1}^{1}(1-t^{2})^{\frac{d-3}{2}}\Big(|y|^{2}+r^{2}-2|y|rt\Big)^{-\frac{s}{2}}\,dt=\int_{0}^{\pi}\sin^{d-2}(\theta)\Big(|y|^{2}+r^{2}-2|y|r\cos(\theta)\Big)^{-\frac{s}{2}}\,d\theta

\displaystyle=2^{d-2}\int_{0}^{\pi}\sin^{d-2}(\theta/2)\cos^{d-2}(\theta/2)\Big((|y|+r)^{2}-4|y|r\cos^{2}(\theta/2)\Big)^{-\frac{s}{2}}\,d\theta

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{d-1}}{(|y|+r)^{s}}\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\sin^{d-2}(\theta)\cos^{d-2}(\theta)\Big(1-\frac{4|y|r}{(|y|+r)^{2}}\cos^{2}(\theta)\Big)^{-\frac{s}{2}}\,d\theta.

Since

\frac{4|y|r}{(|y|+r)^{2}}<1,\qquad\textup{for }r\in(0,1),

it follows from the binomial expansion (
2.19
) that

\displaystyle\quad\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\sin^{d-2}(\theta)\cos^{d-2}(\theta)\Big(1-x\cos^{2}(\theta)\Big)^{-\frac{s}{2}}\,d\theta=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{s}{2}+k)}{\Gamma(\frac{s}{2})k!}x^{k}\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\sin^{d-2}(\theta)\cos^{2k+d-2}(\theta)\,d\theta

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{s}{2}+k)}{\Gamma(\frac{s}{2})k!}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{d-1}{2})\Gamma(k+\frac{d-1}{2})}{\Gamma(k+d-1)}x^{k}=\frac{\Gamma(\frac{d-1}{2})^{2}}{2\Gamma(d-1)}{}_{2}F_{1}\Big(\frac{d-1}{2},\frac{s}{2};d-1;x\Big).

Here, we have used

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\sin^{n}(\theta)\cos^{m}(\theta)\,d\theta=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{n+1}{2})\Gamma(\frac{m+1}{2})}{\Gamma(\frac{m+n}{2}+1)},\qquad(n,m>-1).

Then we obtain

(3.6)

\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}(1-t^{2})^{\frac{d-3}{2}}\Big(|y|^{2}+r^{2}-2|y|rt\Big)^{-\frac{s}{2}}\,dt=\frac{2^{d-2}}{(|y|+r)^{s}}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{d-1}{2})^{2}}{\Gamma(d-1)}{}_{2}F_{1}\Big(\frac{d-1}{2},\frac{s}{2};d-1;\frac{4|y|r}{(|y|+r)^{2}}\Big).

Combining (
3.5
) and (
3.6
), the lemma follows.
∎

Remark 13
.

For the special case
d=2,s=1
, the hypergeometric functions can be expressed
in terms of the complete elliptic integral

Next, we evaluate the integral in (

Lemma 3.2
.

For
|y|>1
, we have

(3.8)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\quad\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{(|y|+r)^{s}}\,{}_{2}F_{1}\Big(\frac{s}{2},\frac{d-1}{2}\,;\,d-1\,;\,\frac{4r|y|}{(|y|+r)^{2}}\Big)\,f(r)\,dr\\
&=\frac{\Gamma(\frac{d}{2})}{\Gamma(\frac{s}{2})\Gamma(\frac{s-d+2}{2})}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{s}{2}+n)\Gamma(\frac{s-d+2}{2}+n)}{\Gamma(\frac{d}{2}+n)\,n!}\int_{0}^{1}r^{2n}f(r)\,dr\,\frac{1}{|y|^{2n+s}}.\end{split}

For
|y|<1
, we have

(3.9)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\quad\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{(|y|+r)^{s}}\,{}_{2}F_{1}\Big(\frac{s}{2},\frac{d-1}{2}\,;\,d-1\,;\,\frac{4r|y|}{(|y|+r)^{2}}\Big)\,f(r)\,dr\\
&=\frac{\Gamma(\frac{d}{2})}{\Gamma(\frac{s}{2})\Gamma(\frac{s-d+2}{2})}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{s}{2}+n)\Gamma(\frac{s-d+2}{2}+n)}{\Gamma(\frac{d}{2}+n)\,n!}\int_{0}^{|y|}r^{2n}f(r)\,dr\,\frac{1}{|y|^{2n+s}}\\
&\quad+\frac{\Gamma(\frac{d}{2})}{\Gamma(\frac{s}{2})\Gamma(\frac{s-d+2}{2})}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{s}{2}+n)\Gamma(\frac{s-d+2}{2}+n)}{\Gamma(\frac{d}{2}+n)\,n!}\int_{|y|}^{1}\frac{1}{r^{2n+s}}f(r)\,dr\,|y|^{2n}.\end{split}

Proof.

For simplicity we write
w=|y|.

By the change of variables
r\mapsto wt
, we have

\displaystyle\quad\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{(w+r)^{s}}\,{}_{2}F_{1}\Big(\frac{s}{2},\frac{d-1}{2}\,;\,d-1\,;\,\frac{4rw}{(w+r)^{2}}\Big)\,f(r)\,dr

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{w^{s-1}}\int_{0}^{1/w}\frac{1}{(1+t)^{s}}\,{}_{2}F_{1}\Big(\frac{s}{2},\frac{d-1}{2}\,;\,d-1\,;\,\frac{4t}{(1+t)^{2}}\Big)\,f(wt)\,dt.

We use the quadratic transform: for
0\leq z\leq 1
,

(3.10)

{}_{2}F_{1}\Big(a,b,2b,\frac{4z}{(1+z)^{2}}\Big)=(1+z)^{2a}{}_{2}F_{1}\Big(a,a+\frac{1}{2}-b,b+\frac{1}{2},z^{2}\Big);

see
[
38
, Eq.2.11(5)]
.

By applying (
3.10
), for
w>1
, we have

\displaystyle\quad\frac{1}{w^{s-1}}\int_{0}^{1/w}\frac{1}{(1+t)^{s}}\,{}_{2}F_{1}\Big(\frac{s}{2},\frac{d-1}{2}\,;\,d-1\,;\,\frac{4t}{(1+t)^{2}}\Big)\,f(wt)\,dt

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{w^{s-1}}\int_{0}^{1/w}{}_{2}F_{1}\Big(\frac{s}{2},\frac{s-d+2}{2}\,;\,\frac{d}{2}\,;\,t^{2}\Big)\,f(wt)\,dt=\frac{1}{w^{s}}\int_{0}^{1}{}_{2}F_{1}\Big(\frac{s}{2},\frac{s-d+2}{2}\,;\,\frac{d}{2}\,;\,\frac{r^{2}}{w^{2}}\Big)\,f(r)\,dr

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(\frac{d}{2})}{\Gamma(\frac{s}{2})\Gamma(\frac{s-d+2}{2})}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{s}{2}+n)\Gamma(\frac{s-d+2}{2}+n)}{\Gamma(\frac{d}{2}+n)\,n!}\int_{0}^{1}r^{2n}f(r)\,dr\,\frac{1}{w^{2n+s}}.

Here, we have used the definition (
2.13
).

In contrast to the case
w>1
, for
w<1
the application of the quadratic transformation (
3.10
) requires splitting the integral:

\displaystyle\quad\frac{1}{w^{s-1}}\int_{0}^{1/w}\frac{1}{(1+t)^{s}}\,{}_{2}F_{1}\Big(\frac{s}{2},\frac{d-1}{2}\,;\,d-1\,;\,\frac{4t}{(1+t)^{2}}\Big)\,f(wt)\,dt=\textup{I}+\textup{II},

where

I

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{w^{s-1}}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{(1+t)^{s}}\,{}_{2}F_{1}\Big(\frac{s}{2},\frac{d-1}{2}\,;\,d-1\,;\,\frac{4t}{(1+t)^{2}}\Big)\,f(wt)\,dt,

II

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{w^{s-1}}\int_{1}^{1/w}\frac{1}{(1+t)^{s}}\,{}_{2}F_{1}\Big(\frac{s}{2},\frac{d-1}{2}\,;\,d-1\,;\,\frac{4t}{(1+t)^{2}}\Big)\,f(wt)\,dt.

The first term can be handled as before, which leads to

\displaystyle\textup{I}=\frac{\Gamma(\frac{d}{2})}{\Gamma(\frac{s}{2})\Gamma(\frac{s-d+2}{2})}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{s}{2}+n)\Gamma(\frac{s-d+2}{2}+n)}{\Gamma(\frac{d}{2}+n)\,n!}\int_{0}^{w}r^{2n}f(r)\,dr\,\frac{1}{w^{2n+s}}.

For the second term, by first applying the change of variable
t\mapsto 1/u
and then (
3.10
), we have

II

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{w^{s-1}}\int_{w}^{1}\frac{u^{s-2}}{(1+u)^{s}}\,{}_{2}F_{1}\Big(\frac{s}{2},\frac{d-1}{2}\,;\,d-1\,;\,\frac{4u}{(1+u)^{2}}\Big)\,f(w/u)\,du

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{w^{s-1}}\int_{w}^{1}u^{s-2}{}_{2}F_{1}\Big(\frac{s}{2},\frac{s}{2}\,;\,1\,;\,u^{2}\Big)f(w/u)\,du

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{w^{s-1}}\int_{1}^{1/w}\frac{1}{t^{s}}{}_{2}F_{1}\Big(\frac{s}{2},\frac{s-d+2}{2}\,;\,\frac{d}{2}\,;\,\frac{1}{t^{2}}\Big)f(wt)\,dt=\int_{w}^{1}\frac{1}{r^{s}}{}_{2}F_{1}\Big(\frac{s}{2},\frac{s-d+2}{2}\,;\,\frac{d}{2}\,;\,\frac{w^{2}}{r^{2}}\Big)f(r)\,dr.

Thus it follows that

\displaystyle\textup{II}=\frac{\Gamma(\frac{d}{2})}{\Gamma(\frac{s}{2})\Gamma(\frac{s-d+2}{2})}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{s}{2}+n)\Gamma(\frac{s-d+2}{2}+n)}{\Gamma(\frac{d}{2}+n)\,n!}\int_{w}^{1}\frac{1}{r^{2n+s}}f(r)\,dr\,w^{2n}.

Combining all of the above, we obtain the desired result.

Remark 14
.

In the limit
s\to d-2
,
d>2
, the RHS of (
3.8
) simplifies to
|y|^{-s}
, which is thus the evaluation of the LHS of (
3.2
) for
|y|>1
. This is consistent with Newton’s theorem as noted above (

).

Next, we present an algebraic identity.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
d\geq 1
and
s\in(d-2,d)
. Then for any
k=0,1,\dots,

(3.11)

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{s}{2}+n)\Gamma(\frac{s-d}{2}+n+1)}{\Gamma(\frac{d}{2}+n)\,n!}\Big(\frac{1}{2n+2k+d}+\frac{1}{2n-2k+s-d}\Big)=0.

Proof.

For
k=0,1,\dots,
we define

(3.12)

\displaystyle F(k)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{s}{2}+n)\Gamma(\frac{s-d}{2}+n+1)}{\Gamma(\frac{d}{2}+n)\,n!}\frac{1}{n+k+\frac{d}{2}},

(3.13)

\displaystyle G(k)

\displaystyle:=-\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{s}{2}+n)\Gamma(\frac{s-d}{2}+n+1)}{\Gamma(\frac{d}{2}+n)\,n!}\frac{1}{n+\frac{s-d}{2}-k}.

We claim that
F(k)=G(k)
.

Note first that for
k=0,

\displaystyle F(0)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{s}{2}+n)\Gamma(\frac{s-d+2}{2}+n)}{\Gamma(\frac{d}{2}+n+1)\,n!}=\frac{\Gamma(1+\frac{s-d}{2})\Gamma(\frac{s}{2})}{\Gamma(1+\frac{d}{2})}{}_{2}F_{1}(\tfrac{s}{2},1+\tfrac{s-d}{2};1+\tfrac{d}{2};1)=\frac{\Gamma(\tfrac{s}{2})\Gamma(1+\tfrac{s-d}{2})\Gamma(d-s)}{\Gamma(d-\frac{s}{2})\Gamma(1-\frac{s-d}{2})},

where we have used the fact that for
c-a-b>0
,

(3.14)

{}_{2}F_{1}(a,b;c;1)=\frac{\Gamma(c)\Gamma(c-a-b)}{\Gamma(c-a)\Gamma(c-b)};

see e.g.
[
68
, Eq.(15.4.20)]
.
Similarly, we have

\displaystyle G(0)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{s}{2}+n)\Gamma(\frac{s-d}{2}+n)}{\Gamma(\frac{d}{2}+n)\,n!}=-\frac{\Gamma(\frac{s}{2})\Gamma(\frac{s-d}{2})}{\Gamma(\frac{d}{2})}{}_{2}F_{1}(\tfrac{s}{2},\tfrac{s-d}{2};\tfrac{d}{2};1)=-\frac{\Gamma(\tfrac{s}{2})\Gamma(\tfrac{s-d}{2})\Gamma(d-s)}{\Gamma(\frac{d-s}{2})\Gamma(d-\frac{s}{2})}.

Then by the reflection identity of the gamma function, we have
F(0)=G(0).

Next, we consider the case
k\geq 1
. We proceed by induction and assume that
F(\ell)=G(\ell)
holds for
\ell=0,1,\dots,k-1
.
Note that

(3.15)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{1}{k!}\sum_{\ell=0}^{k}(-1)^{\ell}\binom{k}{\ell}\frac{1}{x+\ell}&=\frac{1}{k!}\int_{0}^{1}t^{x-1}\bigg(\sum_{\ell=0}^{k}(-1)^{\ell}\binom{k}{\ell}t^{\ell}\bigg)\,dt=\frac{1}{k!}\int_{0}^{1}t^{x-1}(1-t)^{k}=\frac{\Gamma(x)}{\Gamma(x+k+1)}.\end{split}

Using this, note that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{k!}\sum_{\ell=0}^{k}(-1)^{\ell}\binom{k}{\ell}F(\ell)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{s}{2}+n)\Gamma(\frac{s-d+2}{2}+n)}{\Gamma(\frac{d}{2}+n)\,n!}\bigg(\frac{1}{k!}\sum_{\ell=0}^{k}(-1)^{\ell}\binom{k}{\ell}\frac{1}{n+\ell+\frac{d}{2}}\bigg)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{s}{2}+n)\Gamma(\frac{s-d+2}{2}+n)}{\Gamma(\frac{d}{2}+n+k+1)\,n!}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(1+\frac{s-d}{2})\Gamma(\frac{s}{2})}{\Gamma(1+k+\frac{d}{2})}{}_{2}F_{1}(\tfrac{s}{2},1+\tfrac{s-d}{2};1+\tfrac{d}{2}+k;1)=\frac{\Gamma(\tfrac{s}{2})\Gamma(1+\tfrac{s-d}{2})\Gamma(k+d-s)}{\Gamma(k+d-\frac{s}{2})\Gamma(k+1-\frac{s-d}{2})}.

Similarly, it follows that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{k!}\sum_{\ell=0}^{k}(-1)^{\ell}\binom{k}{\ell}G(\ell)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{s}{2}+n)\Gamma(\frac{s-d}{2}+n+1)}{\Gamma(\frac{d}{2}+n)\,n!}\bigg(\frac{1}{k!}\sum_{\ell=0}^{k}(-1)^{\ell}\binom{k}{\ell}\frac{1}{n+\frac{s-d}{2}-\ell}\bigg)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{k}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{s}{2}+n)\Gamma(\frac{s-d}{2}+n-k)}{\Gamma(\frac{d}{2}+n)\,n!}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{k}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{s}{2})\Gamma(\frac{s-d}{2}-k)}{\Gamma(\frac{d}{2})}{}_{2}F_{1}(\tfrac{s}{2},\tfrac{s-d}{2}-k;\tfrac{d}{2};1)=(-1)^{k}\frac{\Gamma(\tfrac{s}{2})\Gamma(\tfrac{s-d}{2}-k)\Gamma(k+d-s)}{\Gamma(\frac{d-s}{2})\Gamma(k+d-\frac{s}{2})}.

Therefore, we can see that

(3.16)

\sum_{\ell=0}^{k}(-1)^{\ell}\binom{k}{\ell}F(\ell)=\sum_{\ell=0}^{k}(-1)^{\ell}\binom{k}{\ell}G(\ell).

Since
F(\ell)=G(\ell)
for all
\ell=0,1,\dots,k-1
due the induction hypothesis, we conclude that
F(k)=G(k)
, which completes the proof.
∎

Furthermore, the Robin constant
c
in (

) is given by

(3.20)

c=\frac{2d}{s}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{a_{k}}{2k+d-s}.

Proof.

By assumption, the radial density is given by (
3.1
). Then the case
|y|>1
follows immediately from Lemmas
3.1
and
3.2
.

Next, we consider the case

Note that by (

\displaystyle\quad\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{s}{2}+n)\Gamma(\frac{s-d+2}{2}+n)}{\Gamma(\frac{d}{2}+n)\,n!}\int_{0}^{|y|}r^{2n}f(r)\,dr\,\frac{1}{|y|^{2n+s}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{s}{2}+n)\Gamma(\frac{s-d+2}{2}+n)}{\Gamma(\frac{d}{2}+n)\,n!}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}d\,a_{k}\int_{0}^{|y|}r^{2n+2k+d-1}\,dr\,\frac{1}{|y|^{2n+s}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{s}{2}+n)\Gamma(\frac{s-d+2}{2}+n)}{\Gamma(\frac{d}{2}+n)\,n!}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{d\,a_{k}}{2n+2k+d}|y|^{2k+d-s}.

Also we have

\displaystyle\quad\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{s}{2}+n)\Gamma(\frac{s-d+2}{2}+n)}{\Gamma(\frac{d}{2}+n)\,n!}\int_{|y|}^{1}\frac{1}{r^{2n+s}}f(r)\,dr\,|y|^{2n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{s}{2}+n)\Gamma(\frac{s-d+2}{2}+n)}{\Gamma(\frac{d}{2}+n)\,n!}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}d\,a_{k}\int_{|y|}^{1}r^{2k+d-1-2n-s}\,dr\,|y|^{2n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{s}{2}+n)\Gamma(\frac{s-d+2}{2}+n)}{\Gamma(\frac{d}{2}+n)\,n!}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{d\,a_{k}}{2k+d-2n-s}(|y|^{2n}-|y|^{2k+d-s}).

Combining these, we obtain

\displaystyle\quad\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{s}{2}+n)\Gamma(\frac{s-d+2}{2}+n)}{\Gamma(\frac{d}{2}+n)\,n!}\int_{0}^{|y|}r^{2n}f(r)\,dr\,\frac{1}{|y|^{2n+s}}+\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{s}{2}+n)\Gamma(\frac{s-d+2}{2}+n)}{\Gamma(\frac{d}{2}+n)\,n!}\int_{|y|}^{1}\frac{1}{r^{2n+s}}f(r)\,dr\,|y|^{2n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{s}{2}+n)\Gamma(\frac{s-d+2}{2}+n)}{\Gamma(\frac{d}{2}+n)\,n!}\bigg(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{d\,a_{k}}{2k+d-2n-s}\bigg)|y|^{2n}

\displaystyle\quad+d\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}\bigg(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{s}{2}+n)\Gamma(\frac{s-d+2}{2}+n)}{\Gamma(\frac{d}{2}+n)\,n!}\Big(\frac{1}{2n+2k+d}+\frac{1}{2n+s-2k-d}\Big)\bigg)|y|^{2k+d-s}.

Here, the second term vanishes due to Lemma

3.1
and
3.2

We need a special treatment for the case

\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{d\mu(x)}{|x-y|^{s}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}|y|^{-s}\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}x^{2k}}{(1-x/y)^{s}}\,dx=\frac{1}{2}|y|^{-s}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{y^{n}}\frac{\Gamma(s+n)}{\Gamma(s)\,n!}\int_{-1}^{1}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}x^{2k+n}\,dx

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}|y|^{-s}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{y^{2n}}\frac{\Gamma(s+2n)}{\Gamma(s)\,(2n)!}\int_{-1}^{1}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}x^{2(k+n)}\,dx=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma(s+2n)}{\Gamma(s)\,(2n)!}\bigg(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{a_{k}}{2k+2n+1}\bigg)\frac{1}{|y|^{2n+s}}.

For
|y|<1

\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{d\mu(x)}{|x-y|^{s}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}|y|^{-s}\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}x^{2k}}{|1-x/y|^{s}}\,dx

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}|y|^{-s}\int_{(-|y|,|y|)}\frac{\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}x^{2k}}{|1-x/y|^{s}}\,dx+\frac{1}{2}|y|^{-s}\int_{(-1,1)\setminus(-|y|,|y|)}\frac{\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}x^{2k}}{|1-x/y|^{s}}\,dx.

The remaining computations follow in the same manner as above.

The evaluation of Robin’s constant follows by setting
y=0
in (
3.19
).

Next we show the following inequality.

Lemma 3.6

Let

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\quad\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{s}{2}+n)\Gamma(\frac{s-d}{2}+n+1)}{\Gamma(\frac{d}{2}+n)\,n!}\bigg(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{a_{k}}{2k+d-2n-s}\bigg)x^{2n}\\
&=\frac{\Gamma(\frac{s}{2})\Gamma(\frac{s-d}{2}+1)}{\Gamma(\frac{d}{2})}\bigg(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{a_{k}}{2k+d-s}\bigg)+\frac{\Gamma(\frac{s}{2}+1)\Gamma(\frac{s-d}{2}+1)}{2\,\Gamma(\frac{d}{2}+1)}V(x)\\
&=\frac{\pi}{\sin(\pi\tfrac{d-s}{2})}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{s}{2})}{\Gamma(\frac{d}{2})}\frac{\Gamma(1+\frac{s}{2})}{\Gamma(\frac{d}{2})}\,\frac{2p+s}{2p}-\frac{\pi}{\sin(\pi\tfrac{d-s}{2})}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{s}{2}+1)}{\Gamma(\frac{d}{2}+1)}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{s}{2}+p)}{\Gamma(\frac{d}{2}+p)}\,\frac{d(2p+s)}{4p}|x|^{2p}.\end{split}

Combining the above, one can see that the inequality (
2.7
) is equivalent to that in Lemma
3.6
.

Note that by (
3.31
), we have

(3.37)

\frac{d}{s}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{a_{k}}{2k+d-s}=\frac{\Gamma(\tfrac{d-s}{2})\Gamma(\frac{s}{2})}{\Gamma(\frac{d}{2})}\,\frac{2p+s}{4p}.

Note that by (
2.13
),

\displaystyle\quad\int_{0}^{1}{}_{2}F_{1}(\tfrac{d-s}{2},\tfrac{d-s}{2}-p;\tfrac{d-s}{2}+1-p;r^{2})r^{2p+d-1}\,dr

\displaystyle=\frac{(d-s-2p)}{\Gamma(\tfrac{d-s}{2})}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\Gamma(\tfrac{d-s}{2}+k)}{(d-s-2p+2k)\,k!}r^{2k+2p+d-1}\,dr=\frac{2p-d+s}{2(4p+s)}\frac{\Gamma(1+\tfrac{s-d}{2})\Gamma(p+\frac{d}{2})}{\Gamma(p+\frac{s}{2}+1)}.

Then by (
2.36
) and (
2.37
), we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}V(x)\,d\mu(x)=\frac{s(2p+s)}{4p(4p+s)}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{d-s}{2})\Gamma(\tfrac{s}{2})}{\Gamma(\frac{d}{2})}.

Therefore by (
2.8
), we obtain (
2.38
).
∎

4.1.
The two-dimensional case revisited

It is instructive to recall the derivation for the case
d=1
from
[
8
]
, where Conjecture
2.8
is a theorem, and one can make use of several well-known computations from logarithmic potential theory.

Note that the variational condition is given by

(4.1)

2\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\log\frac{1}{|x-y|}d\mu_{V}(y)+|x|^{2}\begin{cases}=c&\textup{if }x_{0}\geq a=\sqrt{2},\,|x_{1}|<\sqrt{2},\\
\geq c&\textup{otherwise}.\end{cases}

For
t,x\in\mathbb{R}
, let

(4.2)

\displaystyle\begin{split}G(t,x)&:=2\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\log\frac{1}{|(a+t,x)-\widehat{y}|}d\mu_{V}(\hat{y})+(a+t)^{2}+x^{2}\\
&=2\int_{|y|<\sqrt{2}}\log\frac{1}{|(a+t,x)-(a,y)|}\frac{\sqrt{2-y^{2}}}{\pi}\,dy+(a+t)^{2}+x^{2}.\end{split}

Hence
G(t,x)
denotes the potential as specified by the LHS of (
4.1
), with
t
the horizontal coordinate with value zero at the position of the plane
x_{0}=a
, and
x
the vertical coordinate
corresponding to
x_{1}
.

It is well known that
[
71
]

(4.3)

\displaystyle 2\int_{\mathbb{R}}\log\frac{1}{|u-v|}\frac{\sqrt{2-v^{2}}}{\pi}\mathbbm{1}_{\{|v|<\sqrt{2}\}}\,dv+u^{2}-1-\log 2\begin{cases}=0,&|u|\leq\sqrt{2},\\
\geq 0&|u|>\sqrt{2},\end{cases}\qquad u\in\mathbb{R}.

Therefore if
t=0
in (
4.2
) we have

\displaystyle G(0,x)\begin{cases}=1+\log 2+a^{2}&\textup{if }|x|\leq\sqrt{2},\\
\geq 1+\log 2+a^{2}&\textup{if }|x|\geq\sqrt{2}.\end{cases}

To complete the verification of the variational condition,
it now suffices to show that for any
t\geq 0
and
x\in\mathbb{R}
,

(4.4)

G(t,x)\geq G(0,0)=1+\log 2+a^{2}.

In preparation for verifying (
4.4
), using complex variables we write

(4.5)

\displaystyle 2\int_{|y|<\sqrt{2}}\log\frac{1}{|(a+t,x)-(a,y)|}\frac{\sqrt{2-y^{2}}}{\pi}\,dy=2\int_{|y|<\sqrt{2}}\log\frac{1}{|t+i(x-y)|}\frac{\sqrt{2-y^{2}}}{\pi}\,dy.

Then

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}G(t,x)

\displaystyle=2(a+t)-2\int_{\mathbb{R}}\operatorname{Re}\Big[\frac{1}{t+i(x-y)}\Big]\frac{\sqrt{2-y^{2}}}{\pi}\cdot\mathbbm{1}_{\{|y|<\sqrt{2}\}}\,dy

\displaystyle=2(a+t)-2\int_{\mathbb{R}}\operatorname{Im}\Big[\frac{1}{u-z}\Big]\frac{\sqrt{2-u^{2}}}{\pi}\cdot\mathbbm{1}_{\{|u|<\sqrt{2}\}}\,du,

where
z=-x+it
.
Another well-known integral identity related to the semicircle law is its Stieltjes transform

(4.6)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{1}{u-z}\frac{\sqrt{2-u^{2}}}{\pi}\mathbbm{1}_{\{|u|<\sqrt{2}\}}\,du=-z+\sqrt{z^{2}-2},\qquad z\in\mathbb{C}_{+}.

Using this in the previous equation shows

(4.7)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}G(t,x)

\displaystyle=2(a+t)-2\operatorname{Im}\Big[x-it+\sqrt{(x-it)^{2}-2}\Big]=2(a+t)+2\Big(t-\operatorname{Im}\sqrt{(x-it)^{2}-2}\Big).

Observe that this is precisely where the threshold
a>\sqrt{2}
arises.
Namely, for the measure to be the correct equilibrium measure, one requires

\frac{d}{dt}G(t,x)\Big|_{t=0}=2a-2\operatorname{Im}\sqrt{x^{2}-2}

to be positive. In particular, by taking
x^{2}
as small as possible and thus
x=0
, this condition yields the requirement
a>\sqrt{2}=a_{\rm cri}
, which moreover establishes statement (i) of
Theorem
2.7
.

We will make use of (
4.7
) to verify (
4.4
), which
we do in two steps.

•

(Step 1). We first show that for any
t\geq 0
,
G(t,0)\geq G(0,0)
. This follows from the fact that for
a>\sqrt{2}=a_{\rm cri}
,

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}G(t,0)=2(a+t)+2\Big(t-\sqrt{t^{2}+2}\Big)\geq 0.

•

(Step 2). Next, we show that for any fixed
t>0
,
G(t,x)\geq G(t,0)
. This follows from a similar computation above, which gives

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dx}G(t,x)

\displaystyle=2x-2\int_{\mathbb{R}}\operatorname{Re}\Big[\frac{i}{t+i(x-y)}\Big]\frac{\sqrt{2-y^{2}}}{\pi}\cdot\mathbbm{1}_{\{|y|<\sqrt{2}\}}\,dy

\displaystyle=2x+2\int_{\mathbb{R}}\operatorname{Re}\Big[\frac{1}{y-(x+it)}\Big]\frac{\sqrt{2-u^{2}}}{\pi}\cdot\mathbbm{1}_{\{|y|<\sqrt{2}\}}\,du

\displaystyle=2x+2\operatorname{Re}\Big(-(x+it)+\sqrt{(x+it)^{2}-2}\Big)=2\operatorname{Re}\sqrt{(x-it)^{2}-2}\geq 0.

Here, we have used

\displaystyle\operatorname{Re}\Big[\frac{i}{t+i(x-y)}\Big]=\operatorname{Re}\Big[\frac{1}{y-(x+it)}\Big]=\frac{y-x}{t^{2}+(x-y)^{2}},

as well as (
4.6
) in going from the first to the third
equality. The final inequality has made use of a general property of the square root function with branch cut on the negative real axis.

Combining these steps, it follows that for any
t\geq 0
and
x\in\mathbb{R}
,
G(t,x)\geq G(0,0)
, which completes the proof.

, we have

(A.1)

\displaystyle{}_{2}F_{1}(a,a;2a;z)=-\frac{\Gamma(2a)}{\Gamma(a)^{4}}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma(a+k)^{2}}{(k!)^{2}}(1-z)^{k}\Big(\log(1-z)+2\psi(k+a)-2\psi(k+1)\Big),

where
\psi(z)=\Gamma^{\prime}(z)/\Gamma(z)
is the digamma function. In particular for
a=1
, we have

(A.2)

\displaystyle{}_{2}F_{1}(1,1;2;z)=-\frac{\log(1-z)}{z}.

By using this, the function
G(x)
in (
2.58
) for
d=3
is simplified as

(A.3)

\displaystyle\begin{split}G(x)&=-\frac{3}{2\pi x}\int_{0}^{1}r\sqrt{1-r^{2}}\log\Big(\frac{(r-x)^{2}+t^{2}}{(r+x)^{2}+t^{2}}\Big)\,dr+x^{2}\\
&=-\frac{3}{\pi x}\operatorname{Re}\int_{0}^{1}r\sqrt{1-r^{2}}\log\Big(\frac{r-x-it}{r+x-it}\Big)\,dr+x^{2}\\
&=-\frac{3}{\pi x}\operatorname{Re}\int_{-1}^{1}r\sqrt{1-r^{2}}\log(r-x-it)\,dr+x^{2}.\end{split}

Note that by using the Stieltjes transform (
4.6
) of the semi-circle law, we have

\displaystyle(xG(x))^{\prime}

\displaystyle=3x^{2}+\frac{3}{\pi}\operatorname{Re}\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{r}{r-x-it}\sqrt{1-r^{2}}\,dr=3x^{2}+\frac{3}{\pi}\operatorname{Re}\int_{-1}^{1}\Big(1+\frac{x+it}{r-x-it}\Big)\sqrt{1-r^{2}}\,dr

\displaystyle=3x^{2}+\frac{3}{2}+3\,\operatorname{Re}\bigg[(x+it)\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{1}{r-x-it}\frac{\sqrt{1-r^{2}}}{\pi}\,dr\bigg]

\displaystyle=3x^{2}+\frac{3}{2}+3\,\operatorname{Re}\Big[-z^{2}+z\sqrt{z^{2}-1}\Big]=3t^{2}+\frac{3}{2}+3\operatorname{Re}\Big[z\sqrt{z^{2}-1}\Big],

where
z=x+it
.
Therefore it follows that

(A.4)

\displaystyle G(x)=3t^{2}+\frac{3}{2}+\frac{1}{x}\operatorname{Re}\Big[(z^{2}-1)^{\frac{3}{2}}\Big].

Let
w=\sqrt{z^{2}-1}=u+iv
. Then we have
u^{2}-v^{2}=x^{2}-t^{2}-1
and
uv=tx
.
Thus we have

\displaystyle x^{2}G^{\prime}(x)=3x\operatorname{Re}(zw)-\operatorname{Re}w^{3}=3x^{2}u-3xtv-u^{3}+3uv^{2}=u(3x^{2}-u^{2}).

Since
u>0
, it suffices to show that
3x^{2}-u^{2}>0
.
By using
(u^{2}+v^{2})^{2}=(x^{2}-t^{2}-1)^{2}+4t^{2}x^{2}
, we have

u^{2}=\frac{\sqrt{(x^{2}-t^{2}-1)^{2}+4t^{2}x^{2}}+x^{2}-t^{2}-1}{2}.

Then the desired inequality
3x^{2}-u^{2}>0
is equivalent to

5x^{2}+t^{2}+1>\sqrt{(x^{2}-t^{2}-1)^{2}+4t^{2}x^{2}}.

This inequality immediately follows from

(5x^{2}+t^{2}+1)^{2}-\Big((x^{2}-t^{2}-1)^{2}+4t^{2}x^{2}\Big)=4x^{2}(3+2t^{2}+6x^{2})>0.

Therefore, we obtain that
G^{\prime}(x)\geq 0
.

Remark 18
.

The function
G(x)
in (
A.4
) admits an explicit representation in terms of
x
and
t
:

(A.5)

G(x)=3t^{2}+\frac{3}{2}-\frac{1}{x}\Big((t^{2}-x^{2}+1)^{2}+4t^{2}x^{2}\Big)^{\frac{3}{4}}\sin\bigg(\frac{\pi}{4}+\frac{3}{2}\arctan\Big(\frac{t^{2}-x^{2}+1}{2tx}\Big)\bigg).

From this expression, one can also directly verify that
G^{\prime}(x)\geq 0.

Remark 19
.

The approach used in this appendix for
d=3
may potentially be extended
to odd dimensions
d
, since, by (
A.1
), the
hypergeometric function
{}_{2}F_{1}
appearing in (
2.58
) again
reduces to a combination of logarithm and rational functions. However,
carrying out such computations in a systematic manner remains challenging,
and we leave this for future work.
