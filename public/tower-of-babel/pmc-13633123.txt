Neuroligin-3–CSPG4 interaction maintains oligodendrocyte precursor cell progenitor state and promotes glioma proliferation through mechanotransduction
Authors: Yoon Seok Kim, Shawn M Gillespie, Anna C Geraghty, Belgin Yalçın, Alexis English Ivec, Aerin Yang, Rebecca Mancusi, Jared Hysinger, James Reed, Richard Drexler, Michael Quezada, Karen Malacon, Pamelyn Woo, Youkyeong Gloria Byun, Christopher Mount, Mable Lam, Yuan Pan, J Bradley Zuchero, Jacqueline Trotter, Michelle Monje
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGlioma pathophysiology is robustly regulated by interactions with neurons. Key to these interactions is the role of neuroligin-3 (NLGN3), a synaptic adhesion molecule shed in response to neuronal activity that functions as a paracrine factor crucial for glioma growth. Here we elucidate the mechanistic pathway whereby shed NLGN3 interacts with glioma and their normal glial counterparts. NLGN3 binds to chondroitin sulfate proteoglycan 4 (CSPG4, also known as NG2) on both glioma and healthy oligodendrocyte precursor cells (OPCs), facilitating CSPG4 shedding by ADAM10. NLGN3–CSPG4 interactions alter membrane tension, thereby activating mechanotransducers, primarily PIEZO1, leading to membrane depolarization and subsequent ADAM10-mediated CSPG4 shedding. The NLGN3–CSPG4–PIEZO1 pathway maintains OPCs in an undifferentiated, stem-like state and promotes glioma proliferation, underscoring its dual roles in healthy and malignant contexts.Subject terms: Glial biology, Cancer in the nervous system, Oligodendrocyte

Main

Gliomas are a devastating group of brain cancers. High-grade gliomas such as glioblastoma (GBM) and H3K27M-altered diffuse midline gliomas (DMGs, DMG originating in the brainstem is also called diffuse intrinsic pontine glioma (DIPG)) are the leading cause of primary brain tumor-related death in both adults and children, while low-grade gliomas cause substantial neurological morbidity. Many forms of glioma arise from oligodendrocyte precursor cells (OPCs), and OPC-like cancer cells comprise an important cellular subpopulation within gliomas such as GBM and DMG (for review, please see ref. 1). Concordantly, many mechanistic parallels exist between healthy OPCs and malignant gliomas. A key similarity is the proliferation-promoting effect of neuronal activity. Glutamatergic neuronal activity robustly promotes the proliferation of both healthy OPCs2 and glioma cells3. In OPCs, this activity-regulated proliferation results in the generation of new mature oligodendrocytes and adaptive changes in myelination that tune neural circuit function and contribute to brain functions like attention, learning and memory2,4–6. Gliomas hijack these mechanisms of glial plasticity to promote growth3,7–11 and invasion9–11.

Neuron–glioma interactions are mediated by activity-regulated paracrine growth factors3,7,8 and by neuron-to-glioma synapses9,10. One important mechanism underlying activity-regulated glioma growth is the activity-regulated shedding of the synaptic protein neuroligin-3 (NLGN3)3,7. OPCs are a major cellular source of shed NLGN3 (ref. 7). For context, it is important to note that OPCs receive extensive synaptic inputs from neurons12–14, although the function of neuron-to-OPC synapses is still incompletely understood. NLGN3 shedding occurs through neuronal activity regulation of ADAM10 protease, which cleaves NLGN3 to release the large N-terminal ectodomain into the extracellular microenvironment. Shed NLGN3 promotes the proliferation of both high-grade3,7,15 and low-grade8 gliomas through stimulation of proliferation signaling pathways PI3K-mTOR3,7, RAS and SRC7. Genetic knockout (KO) of Nlgn3 gene from the cells in the brain microenvironment or pharmacological blockade of NLGN3 shedding through ADAM10 inhibition markedly slows glioma growth and progression in preclinical models of high-grade7 and low-grade gliomas8. The stark dependency of gliomas on NLGN3 signaling can be understood in part by observations that NLGN3 exposure increases glioma gene expression related to multiple neuron–glioma interaction mechanisms; notably, synapse-related gene expression7 and concordantly neuron-to-glioma synapses are reduced in the absence of NLGN3 (ref. 16). Taken together, these observations highlight the therapeutic potential of disrupting NLGN3 signaling in glioma cells. However, a number of pressing questions remain that include mechanisms by which NLGN3 interacts with and signals to the glioma cell, whether similar interactions occur on healthy OPCs, and what role NLGN3 shedding may have in healthy oligodendroglial biology. Here we show that shed NLGN3 engages a shared cell-surface interacting partner on glioma cells and healthy OPCs to activate a mechanotransductive signaling pathway. This interaction increases membrane tension, activates mechanosensitive ion channels, chiefly PIEZO1, and promotes ADAM10-dependent ectodomain shedding. In glioma cells, this signaling mechanism drives proliferation, whereas in healthy OPCs it maintains an undifferentiated progenitor state. These results reveal a shared mechanism through which neuron–glial interactions regulate both malignant glioma growth and normal OPC biology.

Results

Unbiased biochemical screening for NLGN3 binding partners

To screen for putative interaction partners of shed NLGN3, we performed affinity capture chromatography using an immobilized NLGN3 (Extended Data Fig. 1a) column16,17 and solubilized membranes from patient-derived DIPG/DMG and adult GBM cultures (Fig. 1a,b). Mass spectrometry (Methods) of column eluates identified several putative NLGN3-interacting partners from both cell cultures, prominently including chondroitin sulfate proteoglycan 4 (CSPG4; Fig. 1b). CSPG4 contains two laminin-neurexin-sex-hormone binding globulin domains (Fig. 1c)18–20, similar to those in neurexins (mediating well-characterized interaction with postsynaptic neuroligins21,22), highlighting CSPG4 as a likely interaction partner. CSPG4 (NG2) is a canonical marker of OPCs (which were previously referred to as ‘NG2 cells’) and is also highly expressed in a variety of gliomas, particularly in the OPC-like cellular populations23,24. Of note, the canonical NLGN3 binding partners in neurons—neurexins25—were not found to be expressed in these patient-derived glioma cell models. NRXN gene expression was similarly absent or low in the patient-derived models, and variable but generally low in primary patient samples of DMG and GBM (Extended Data Fig. 1d,e).

We then tested whether NLGN3 binds chondroitin sulfate (CS), the glycan substrate covalently linked to CSPG4 (Fig. 1c). Using fluorescence size-exclusion chromatography (FSEC; Extended Data Fig. 1b) with recombinant NLGN3 ectodomain, we found that both CS and the established NLGN-interacting partner heparin26 showed a notable binding-induced left shift in FSEC (Extended Data Fig. 1c). Although the CS-induced shift was smaller, CS binding was robustly dose dependent (Extended Data Fig. 1f,g). Together, these biochemical data support CSPG4 as a candidate NLGN3 interactor.

Because both CSPG4 ectodomains and NLGN3 are shed in response to neuronal activity27, we asked whether they are detectable in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) of human glioma patients. Western blotting of CSF from patients with DIPG/DMG or leukemia control patients detected both NLGN3 and CSPG4 in two of four glioma patients, but in none of the leukemia patients (Fig. 1d,e)7. These findings support CSPG4 as a candidate interacting partner for shed NLGN3, while leaving unresolved how these proteins interact and whether this interaction is linked to activity-dependent shedding3,7,8,28.

NLGN3 exposure augments ADAM10-mediated CSPG4 proteolysis

Previous studies showed that neuronal activity induces ADAM10-dependent shedding of both NLGN3 and CSPG4 ectodomains7,27. Here we found that NLGN3 shedding precedes CSPG4 shedding after neuronal activity, suggesting that shed NLGN3 may subsequently promote CSPG4 cleavage in glioma cells and OPCs. To test this, we treated glioma cultures with recombinant NLGN3 ectodomain and analyzed conditioned medium for shed CSPG4 by western blotting (Fig. 2a).

We found that glioma cells constitutively shed CSPG4 (Fig. 2b), a process inhibited by the ADAM10 inhibitor GI254023X (5 µM, 15 min; Fig. 2b). Notably, NLGN3 treatment significantly increased CSPG4 shedding (Fig. 2b). The absence of CSPG4 C-terminal signal in conditioned medium confirmed ectodomain-specific shedding, while chondroitinase ABC treatment revealed variable glycosylation of shed CSPG4 (Fig. 2b).

We found that NLGN3-induced CSPG4 shedding occurs across a spectrum of glioma subtypes, including human IDH1 wild-type (WT) GBM (Fig. 2c) and a mouse model of NF1-associated optic glioma8 (Fig. 2d). Furthermore, we confirmed that this is not exclusive to malignant cells, as primary OPCs readily shed CSPG4 ectodomains in response to NLGN3 (Fig. 2e). Consistent with previous findings27, NLGN3-induced shedding of CSPG4 was inhibited by pretreating OPCs with an ADAM10 inhibitor. These results collectively demonstrate that NLGN3 exposure is sufficient to enhance ADAM10-dependent shedding of CSPG4 ectodomains from OPCs and glioma cells (Fig. 2f).

Membrane depolarization disinhibits ADAM10 and increases CSPG4 shedding

We then examined how NLGN3 augments ADAM10-dependent CSPG4 shedding. ADAM10-mediated shedding of proteins, like NLGN3 and CSPG4, is regulated in part by an auto-inhibitory c-loop mechanism that obstructs the active site29; an inhibition relieved by membrane depolarization30. We therefore conducted a CSPG4 shedding assay in which cells were exposed to various depolarizing stimuli (Fig. 3)27. First, we visualized depolarization by GCaMP6s imaging in glioma cells9,10,15; cells were treated with either vehicle or 200 nM NLGN3, followed by wide-field calcium imaging over 20 min (2-min baseline, 18-min post-treatment; Fig. 3a). While spontaneous activity was rare in controls, NLGN3-treated cells showed markedly increased calcium transients (1.7% versus 21.9%; Fig. 3b), consistent with reports implicating NLGN3 and membrane depolarization in glioma progression3,7,9,10. To further verify the role of membrane depolarization, we treated glioma cells with the voltage-gated K+-channel blocker, 4-aminopyridine (4-AP; 2.5 mM)31–33, which elicited prominent calcium responses (with 98.9% of cells responding), comparable to a previously study33 (Fig. 3c,d) and increased CSPG4 shedding (Fig. 3e,f, left), without changing glioma cell death (Extended Data Fig. 2a); CSPG4 shedding was markedly attenuated by cotreatment with an ADAM10 inhibitor (Fig. 3f, right), indicating that depolarization-induced CSPG4 shedding is ADAM10 dependent.

We then investigated whether ADAM10-mediated shedding of CSPG4 is contingent upon the duration of depolarization; to address this question, we used optogenetics—a method developed for precise control of membrane potential using light stimuli34. We achieved this by expressing the light-gated cation channel channelrhodopsin-2 (ChR2) in glioma cells and OPCs and by illuminating blue light (470 nm, 1 mW mm−2, 0–60 s, every 15 min, 1 h) to induce controlled depolarization (Fig. 3g and Extended Data Fig. 2b, top). We observed a robust increase in CSPG4 shedding with optogenetic depolarization of glioma cells (Fig. 3h; *P < 0.05). Notably, the magnitude of shedding was contingent upon the stimulation duration and consequent magnitude of membrane depolarization, with longer light stimulation duration resulting in larger membrane depolarization amplitude (Fig. 3i,j and Extended Data Fig. 2b, bottom; *P < 0.05), indicating that depolarization directly enhanced ADAM10-mediated CSPG4 shedding, without any changes in cell death (Extended Data Fig. 2c). These results directly support the notion that membrane depolarization enhances ADAM10-mediated CSPG4 shedding.

Intriguingly, CSPG4 ectodomains have also been reported to be shed in response to actin depolymerizing agents18,28,35. Because disruption of actin microfilaments has been shown to induce membrane depolarization36,37, we hypothesized that actin depolymerization may promote CSPG4 shedding through depolarization-mediated ADAM10 activation. To test this, we treated glioma cells with cytochalasin D (2 µM, 18 min), an actin depolymerase and performed whole-field calcium imaging (Fig. 3k, left). As expected, cytochalasin D treatment caused marked depolymerization of actin filaments (Extended Data Fig. 2d) and triggered robust calcium transients (20.8% responding cells; Fig.3k, right), comparable to NLGN3 and 4-AP treatments (Fig. 3l and Extended Data Fig. 2e), consistent with prior findings36,37.

To directly assess the effect of actin depolymerization on CSPG4 shedding, we treated glioma cells with cytochalasin D (2 µM, 15 min) and subsequently analyzed conditioned medium for CSPG4 ectodomains (Fig. 3m, left). We observed a significant increase in CSPG4 shedding, which was completely blocked by ADAM10 inhibition (Fig. 3m, right), reinforcing the conclusion that actin depolymerization promotes ADAM10-mediated shedding through depolarization.

Collectively, our results demonstrate that depolarization enhances ADAM10-mediated shedding of NLGN3 (Fig. 3f,h,j,m,n). These findings also suggest that NLGN3 exposure results in a membrane depolarization that enhances ADAM10-mediated CSPG4 shedding (Fig. 3o). We therefore wondered if the membrane-bound actuators responsible for translating NLGN3 exposure into enhanced ADAM10 activity in glioma cells and OPCs were cation-conducting channels sensitive to disruptions in the actin cytoskeleton.

NLGN3 disinhibits ADAM10 via mechanotransduction

Actin depolymerization, which mimics NLGN3 treatment in inducing membrane depolarization, ADAM10 activation and CSPG4 cleavage (Fig. 3), is also known to increase shear stress and membrane tension38–40. To explore whether NLGN3 itself alters tension, we assessed changes in membrane tension using Flipper-TR (Spirochrome), a widely used method to measure mechanical changes in live cells (Fig. 4a)41,42. Remarkably, we observed a significant increase in membrane tension in glioma cells after treatment with NLGN3 (Fig. 4a,b, left). Notably, this increase was dependent on the presence of CSPG4, as we did not observe changes in tension in CSPG4 KO cells (Fig. 4a,b, right). Given these results, we formulated a model in which the force of NLGN3 interacting with CSPG4 causes increased membrane tension that is sensed by mechanotransducers, depolarization-mediated disinhibition of ADAM10 and ultimately shedding of CSPG4 (refs. 30,38–40,43,44).

To validate our model, we endeavored to identify actuators for sensing and responding to the NLGN3–CSPG4 interaction and the increase in membrane tension. Increased shear stress and membrane tension are known to activate mechanotransducers such as PIEZO and transient receptor potential channels that are associated with the cytoskeleton38–40,43,44. Thus, we interrogated multiple single-cell RNA-seq (scRNA-seq) datasets from pediatric DMG and adult IDH1 WT GBMs to curate a list of mechanotransducers expressed in OPC-like cells also expressing CSPG4 (Fig. 4c and Extended Data Fig. 3a), finding several including PIEZO1, PIEZO2 and several transient receptor potential channels (Extended Data Fig. 3b)45,46. The expression level of the mechanotransducer PIEZO1 was the highest (Extended Data Fig. 3b), and immunostaining (Fig. 4d) and western blot (WB; Extended Data Fig. 3c) analyses indicated high levels of PIEZO1 protein in glioma cells. In addition, several studies have linked PIEZO1 to cancer progression and poor patient prognosis, including in brain tumors, where its activity promotes proliferation and invasion47–49.

To assess whether mechanotransduction is necessary for NLGN3-induced ADAM10 activity, we performed CSPG4 shedding assays using the mechanosensitive channel blockers gadolinium (Gd3+) and GsMTx4 (Extended Data Fig. 3d)50. Remarkably, blockade of channel conduction proved to be as effective as the ADAM10 inhibitor in preventing NLGN3-induced shedding of CSPG4 (Fig. 4e and Extended Data Fig. 3e,f). In contrast, treatment of cells with the PIEZO1 agonist Yoda1 resulted in enhanced shedding (Fig. 4e and Extended Data Fig. 3e,f). Consistent with other assays (Fig. 2b and Extended Data Fig. 3g,h), only the N-terminal ectodomain was released into the conditioned medium (Extended Data Fig. 3i).

Using CRISPR–Cas9, we generated PIEZO1 KO glioma cells and observed a marked reduction in NLGN3-induced CSPG4 shedding (Fig. 4f,g), confirming the necessity of PIEZO1. Wide-field calcium imaging confirmed that calcium transients induced by both NLGN3 and cytochalasin D were significantly diminished by GsMTx4, indicating that these responses are mediated by mechanotransducer activity (Fig. 4h–l and Extended Data Fig. 4a). Previous studies47,51 demonstrated that osmotic stress can preserve cellular morphology and membrane functionality. We detected large inward currents after stimulation with hypertonic solution. Notably, these responses were significantly decreased in PIEZO1 KO, confirming that the current mainly arises from PIEZO1 (Fig. 4n,o). We then used cell-attached recordings under stepwise negative pressure to investigate mechanosensitive currents50–52. Initially, we observed that a sequence of stimulations resulted in significant inward currents (Fig. 4p). These currents were markedly attenuated by the presence of GsMTx4 (7.9 ± 1.9 versus 2.8 ± 0.23 pA, at −40 mmHg) and augmented by the PIEZO1 agonist Yoda1 (ref. 51; 7.9 ± 1.9 versus 23.6 ± 6.0 pA, at −40 mmHg), confirming that these currents are elicited by the activation of the mechanosensitive channels, primarily PIEZO1 (Fig. 4q,r). Furthermore, we observed a notable increase in mechanosensitive currents with NLGN3 (Fig. 4r; 7.9 ± 1.9 versus 17.1 ± 5.0 pA, at −40 mmHg), similar to Yoda1. This finding is consistent with earlier observations (Fig. 4a,b) and indicates that mechanotransduction is directly modulated by NLGN3. To further validate, we conducted cell-attached recordings in CSPG4 KO glioma cells. Surprisingly, we observed minimal currents after a series of negative pressures with or without NLGN3 (Extended Data Fig. 4c,d).

Together, these findings support a model in which NLGN3–CSPG4 engagement increases membrane tension, activating mechanotransducers that drive depolarization, ADAM10 activation and ectodomain shedding (Fig. 4s). While PIEZO1 likely serves as a major transducer in this pathway, the efficacy of broad-spectrum inhibitors and the partial nature of the KO phenotype suggest that other mechanosensitive channels may also contribute52.

Functional consequences in NLGN3–mechanotransduction-mediated pathways

To explore downstream signaling cascades, we examined the PI3K–mTOR pathway3,7. In glioma cells treated with NLGN3 or Yoda1, we observed robust Ser473 phosphorylation of AKT (Fig. 5a)7,48,49. In contrast, NLGN3-induced pAKT was markedly reduced, although not completely abrogated, as the Yoda1-induced phosphorylation, in GsMTx4 treated cells (Fig. 5a) and PIEZO1 KO cells (Fig. 5b,c). These results suggest that NLGN3-induced activation of the PI3K–mTOR pathway is mediated primarily through PIEZO1, while the observed residual signals suggest that other mechanotransducers may also act in concert with PIEZO1, reiterating a broader role for mechanotransduction as a central pathway for translating NLGN3 signaling into proliferation and growth in glioma3,7,8,15.

To understand the consequences of PIEZO1 depletion, we performed bulk RNA-seq of a patient-derived GBM model with or without PIEZO1. Differential gene expression analysis revealed that, upon PIEZO1 KO (Fig. 5g), GBM cells dramatically increase expression of several stress/apoptosis-related genes including CASP8, UBB and GSN. We then assessed transcriptomic changes through bulk RNA-seq of OPCs undergoing differentiation in the presence or absence of NLGN3 and modulators of CSPG4 shedding. We determined that OPCs treated with NLGN3 clustered apart from cells exposed to vehicle or pretreated with inhibitors of CSPG4 shedding before NLGN3 exposure (Fig. 6i). Furthermore, our differential gene expression analysis comparing NLGN3-treated OPCs and vehicle-treated OPCs clearly demonstrates an enrichment of genes associated with stemness and synaptic communication (Pdgfra, Cacng4 and Kcnip3) and a suppression of genes related to differentiation (Mbp, Plp, Enpp6; Fig. 6j). Of note, we found that NLGN3 treatment of OPCs resulted in upregulation of several genes that also increased in expression when human glioma cells were treated with NLGN3 in a previous study7, demonstrating the functional similarities of Nlgn3 in OPCs and glioma cells (Fig. 6k). Finally, using RT-qPCR analyses in OPCs, we verified that NLGN3 treatment prevented the expression of myelin-specific transcripts Plp and Mbp and maintained the expression of the differentiation inhibitor Sox11 relative to OPCs exposed only to vehicle control after growth factor withdrawal (Extended Data Fig. 7d)59.

Previous in vivo genetic mouse modeling experiments demonstrated that NLGN3 is chiefly shed from OPCs, indicating that OPC-derived NLGN3 shedding may have a role in oligodendroglial biology. PIEZO1 is expressed in OPCs and expression remains stable across OPCs of different ages (Extended Data Fig. 7h). The in vitro studies described above implicate NLGN3 signaling in maintenance of a self-renewing, undifferentiated state in OPCs, raising the possibility that NLGN3 shed from OPCs may contribute to OPC stemness and population maintenance. We therefore tested the effects of Nlgn3 loss on OPC population homeostasis using a conditional KO mouse model, PDGFRa::CreERT;Nlgn3fl/fl, where Nlgn3 is specifically knocked out from OPCs upon tamoxifen administration (Extended Data Fig. 7e). Remarkably, acute deletion of NLGN3 led to an approximately 30% reduction in the number of OPCs in the corpus callosum (Extended Data Fig. 7f,g and Fig. 6l). Taken together, these findings indicate that NLGN3–CSPG4–PIEZO1 signaling contributes to OPC population maintenance by promoting maintenance of OPCs in a progenitor state.

Discussion

In this study, we uncover a mechanotransduction-based mechanism linking activity-regulated NLGN3 signaling to both glioma proliferation and OPC state regulation. We identify CSPG4 as a prominent NLGN3-interacting partner on glioma cells and healthy OPCs and show that NLGN3–CSPG4 interaction increases membrane tension, activates mechanosensitive ion channels, chiefly PIEZO1, and promotes ADAM10-dependent CSPG4 ectodomain shedding. Through this pathway, NLGN3 supports glioma cell proliferation while maintaining healthy OPCs in an undifferentiated progenitor state. These findings establish the NLGN3–CSPG4–PIEZO1 pathway as a shared mechanism between malignant and normal glial biology and suggest that gliomas exploit activity-regulated mechanisms of OPC state control to promote tumor growth.

Using unbiased biochemical screening, we identified CSPG4 as a prominent NLGN3-interacting partner. CSPG4 was prioritized based on its reproducible enrichment in affinity chromatography–mass spectrometry experiments, strong expression in both OPCs and glioma cells, and established roles in progenitor maintenance and oncogenic signaling23,24,59,60. Building on these findings, we uncovered a mechanism whereby NLGN3 interacts with CSPG4, thus altering membrane tension and activating the mechanotransducers, primarily PIEZO1, leading to membrane depolarization and depolarization-induced activity of the ADAM10, a sheddase that processes multiple substrates including CSPG4 ectodomain (but not C-terminal CSPG4) and NLGN3 (refs. 7,29,39).

Beyond its implications for glioma biology, these findings shed new light on roles for NLGN3 in the regulation of OPC states, elucidating a newly appreciated role for activity-regulated maintenance of homeostatic OPC population dynamics through the NLGN3–CSPG4–mechanotransduction pathway. NLGN3–CSPG4–mechanotransduction signaling thus joins a growing list of mechanistic parallels between neuron–glial and neuron–glioma interactions, supporting a stem-like, undifferentiated state in both healthy and malignant glial cells. In healthy OPCs, this stem-like state appears to prevent differentiation rather than directly driving proliferation, suggesting that NLGN3 may contribute to OPC maintenance through inhibition of differentiation rather than expansion of the population through proliferation. This interpretation is supported by our observation that OPC numbers decline after NLGN3 deletion in vivo, despite no significant effect on OPC proliferation in vitro, indicating that NLGN3 signaling may preserve OPC homeostasis by sustaining a pool of undifferentiated cells rather than inducing active cell division.

Our findings also suggest that activation of PIEZO1 by NLGN3–CSPG4 signaling selectively prevents OPC differentiation without significantly affecting proliferation, whereas it was reported that PIEZO1 activation in aged OPCs inhibits both proliferation and differentiation56. These differing outcomes may reflect the influence of developmental context, as neonatal and aged OPCs likely interpret mechanical cues differently—consistent with age-related transcriptional shifts reported in ref. 61. In addition, variations in the mode of PIEZO1 activation may contribute to the observed differences. Mechanical stress is identified in ref. 56 from increased extracellular matrix stiffness as the activating stimulus, while in our study, PIEZO1 is engaged through protein-mediated interactions between NLGN3 and CSPG4(refs.3,7,56). Mechanotransducers like PIEZO1 can respond to a variety of distinct modes of activation38,43,51, which may influence channel gating, localization and downstream signaling in context-specific ways. Moreover, while our data support a central role for mechanotransduction, primarily through PIEZO1, in mediating the effects of NLGN3, residual pAKT signaling after PIEZO1 KO and mechanosensitive channel inhibition suggests that additional, nonmechanosensitive pathways may also contribute.

While our findings provide a mechanistic framework linking NLGN3, CSPG4 and mechanotransduction signaling, several limitations of the current study should be noted. Although our in vivo data indicate that PIEZO1 KO attenuates the NLGN3/CSPG4-dependent glioma phenotype, the precise mechanisms by which PIEZO1 integrates NLGN3-mediated and CSPG4-mediated signaling in vivo remain to be elucidated. Furthermore, while our data indicate that NLGN3 binding to CSPG4 enhances membrane tension and mechanosensitive channel activity, the absence of mechanotransduction in CSPG4-deficient cells suggests that CSPG4 is also required for baseline mechanosensitivity and membrane organization. This implies that CSPG4 not only serves as an interacting partner for NLGN3 but also may contribute to the structural context necessary for mechanotransduction through its linkage to the actin–membrane network, as the complete loss of response in CSPG4 KO cells may reflect a disruption of mechanical coupling rather than a selective loss of NLGN3 responsiveness. Consistent with our findings, previous studies have shown that CSPG4 promotes glioma proliferation, invasion and tumor growth in vivo, and that its loss or inhibition suppresses malignancy62,63, further supporting our data that the NLGN3–CSPG4 signaling pathway may contribute to glioma progression through both mechanotransductive and proliferative mechanisms. Together, these findings highlight the complexity of NLGN3–CSPG4–mechanotransduction across developmental stages and activation contexts, and underscore the importance of considering both biochemical and biomechanical inputs when studying mechanotransduction in glioma and OPC biology.

While our findings define a NLGN3–CSPG4–mechanotransduction pathway critical for glioma proliferation and OPC state maintenance, they also raise important considerations for therapeutic targeting. The substantial reduction in OPC numbers after NLGN3 deletion underscores that this signaling pathway may have a role in sustaining the OPC pool in the healthy brain. Given the molecular parallels between glioma cells and OPCs, therapeutic interventions aimed at disrupting this pathway could pose risks to healthy glial function, including myelination12,14,58,59,64. Although our study did not assess the long-term functional consequences of the observed OPC loss, these findings emphasize the need for further study and targeted delivery strategies or transient inhibition approaches to minimize potential toxicities.

In summary, the findings presented here underscore a critical role for an NLGN3–CSPG4–mechanotranduction pathway in the interplay between neuronal activity and glioma progression, advancing understanding of a key mechanistic pathway by which gliomas exploit neuronal signals for growth. The identification of mechanotransduction as an important mediator in this process suggests new therapeutic opportunities, highlighting the potential for targeting mechanosensitive pathways in glioma and possibly in other cancers.

Methods

Mice and housing conditions

All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with protocols approved by the Stanford University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee and performed in accordance with institutional guidelines. Animals were housed according to the standard guidelines with unlimited access to water and food, under a 12-h light/12-h dark cycle. For brain tumor xenograft experiments, the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee has a limit on indications of morbidity (as opposed to tumor volume). Under no circumstances did any of the experiments exceed the limits indicated and mice were immediately killed if they showed signs of neurological morbidity or if they lost 15% or more of their initial body weight.

Recombinant NLGN3 purification and NLGN3 capture assay

DNA encoding the signal peptide and ectodomain of human NLGN3 (Sino Biological, HG11160-UT) was cloned into pD649 with C-terminal AviTag and 6xHis tags. Plasmids were transfected into Expi293 cells (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A14635), induced 18–24 h post-transfection and media were collected after 72–96 h, filtered through a 0.22-micron Steriflip, and purified using HisPur Cobalt Resin (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 89964). Protein was washed with 100 mM NaCl, 20 mM HEPES, pH 7.5, 20 mM imidazole, eluted with 100 mM NaCl, 20 mM HEPES, pH 7.5, 250 mM imidazole, further purified by gel filtration in 100 mM NaCl, 20 mM HEPES, pH 7.5, and concentrated to 3–6 mg ml−1. For NLGN3 affinity capture, 30 million SU-DIPG13pons or MGG8 neurosphere cells were pelleted without dissociation (200g, 3 min, at room temperature), washed in calcium-free and magnesium-free HBSS, snap frozen in dry ice/ethanol and stored at −80 °C. Pellets were thawed on ice, resuspended in 5 ml lysis buffer with protease inhibitors (10 mM HEPES, pH 7.5, 10 mM MgCl2, 20 mM KCl, 1× Halt protease inhibitor cocktail; Thermo Fisher Scientific, 78429), and centrifuged at 20,000g for 20 min at 4 °C. Pellets were resuspended in 1 ml lysis buffer, homogenized with 20 strokes using a 2-ml glass Dounce, diluted to 4 ml and spun at 20,000g for 20 min at 4 °C; this was repeated thrice. Pellets were then washed in 2.5-ml salt wash buffer (lysis buffer + 150 mM NaCl) until no protein was detected in the cleared supernatant by Bradford assay. Purified membrane fractions were solubilized in 1% L-MNG, 150 mM NaCl, 20 mM HEPES, 10% glycerol and 1× Halt protease inhibitor cocktail. NLGN3 ectodomains were biotinylated using the BirA biotin–protein ligase standard reaction kit (Avidity, EC 6.3.4.15), purified and concentrated using a 30-kDa Amicon filter (MilliporeSigma, UFC503096), and immobilized on streptavidin resin (Pierce ProFound Biotinylated-Protein Interaction Pull-Down Kit; Thermo Fisher Scientific, 21115). Solubilized membrane proteins were incubated with NLGN3 resin overnight at 4 °C with rotation, washed thrice with supplied acetate buffer plus 150 mM NaCl and eluted in LDS sample buffer + β-mercaptoethanol (BME) at 42 °F for 15 min. Proteins were resolved on a 3–8% Tris-acetate gel at 120 V for 15 min, and upper gel slices were excised for mass spectrometry. Protein abundance was normalized to actin, and the enrichment index was calculated as the ratio of each protein detected with NLGN3 capture to the same protein detected without NLGN3 capture.

Wide-field GCaMP6s Ca2+ imaging of glioma cells

To generate a stable GCaMP6s-expressing glioma cell line, cells were lentivirally transduced with a virus encoding pLV-Ef1a-GCaMP6s-p2A-nls-tdTomato10,15. GCaMP6s-expressing glioma cells were enriched by sorting transduced cells based on the tdTomato fluorescence. For calcium imaging, cells were plated on poly-l-lysine/laminin-coated glass-bottom 24-well plates at least 24 h before imaging. On the day of imaging, cells were washed twice with 1 ml Tyrode’s solution (150 mM NaCl, 10 mM HEPES-NaOH, pH 7.3–7.4, 10 mM glucose, 4 mM KCl, 2.5 mM CaCl2, 1.5 mM MgCl2; 320–330 mOsm), incubated for 15 min at 37 °C with 5% CO2 and washed once more immediately before imaging. Drug treatments were prepared as 100× stocks and added directly to wells at 1× final concentration (10 µl per well). Final compound concentrations matched those used in the shedding assay, with calcium-free and magnesium-free HBSS as vehicle. Wide-field calcium imaging was performed on a confocal microscope using GCaMP6/GFP filters. Cells were localized at 10% laser power to minimize photobleaching, and time-lapse imaging was acquired at 50% laser power. After baseline acquisition, imaging was paused, 10 µl drug stock was added and gently mixed with a 200-µl pipette tip and imaging was resumed. Cells were classified as active if ΔF/F0 exceeded 20% at any poststimulation time point, and the active fraction was calculated as the proportion of total cells meeting this criterion. Experiments were performed in triplicate under matched culture time, temperature and imaging conditions.

Apoptosis assay by annexin V/DAPI costaining

Apoptosis was assessed using annexin V–Alexa Fluor 568 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and DAPI. Cells were collected, washed twice with cold Ca2+/Mg2+-free HBSS and resuspended in 1× binding buffer (10 mM HEPES, 140 mM NaCl, 2.5 mM CaCl2, pH 7.5). Cells were plated in 96-well V-bottom or U-bottom plates at 100 µl per well, stained with 5 µl annexin V–Alexa Fluor 568 and 0.5 µl DAPI (5 µg ml−1 final) and incubated for 15 min at room temperature in the dark. After adding 400 µl 1× binding buffer, samples were analyzed by flow cytometry within 1 h. annexin V+/DAPI− cells were classified as early apoptotic, annexin V+/DAPI+ as late apoptotic and annexin V−/DAPI− as viable.

F-actin staining after cytochalasin D treatment

F-actin polymerization was assessed using an F-actin visualization kit (Cytoskeleton, BK005) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Glioma cells were cultured on PDL-coated glass coverslips and treated with HBSS vehicle or 2 µM cytochalasin D for 15 min at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Cells were washed, fixed in 1× fixative solution for 10 min at room temperature, permeabilized for 5 min and stained with 100 nM rhodamine phalloidin for 30 min in the dark. Coverslips were washed thrice, mounted with the supplied mounting medium, cured for 30 min in the dark and imaged by confocal microscopy using rhodamine-compatible settings (excitation = 535 nm, emission = 585 nm).

Phospho-AKT flow cytometry analysis

Cells were dissociated and treated as described for the shedding assay. After incubation, media were removed, and cells were detached with 100 µl TrypLE (Gibco) for 5 min at 37 °C. Cells were transferred to 96-well U-bottom plates containing 15 µl freshly opened 16% paraformaldehyde (PFA) and fixed for 10 min at room temperature on a shaker, protected from light. Cells were centrifuged at 1,600g for 3 min, resuspended in 200 µl ice-cold 100% methanol, mixed and incubated overnight at −20 °C. After fixation, cells were centrifuged at 1,600g for 4 min and washed twice with 200 µl wash buffer (1% BSA, 0.05% sodium azide in PBS or commercial FACS/MACS buffer). Live cell membrane tension measurement

Primary cultured cells were plated on PDL-coated or poly-L-lysine-coated coverslips and stained with 1 µM Flipper-TR (Spirochrome) in imaging solution (150 mM NaCl, 10 mM HEPES, pH 7.3, 10 mM glucose, 2 mM CaCl2, 2 mM MgCl2, 4 mM KCl) for 30 min at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Electrophysiology

For whole-cell recording experiments, primary cultured cells were recorded using the methods described previously66. Cells were recorded in extracellular medium containing 150 mM NaCl, 4 mM KCl, 2 mM CaCl2, 2 mM MgCl2, 10 mM HEPES, pH 7.3–7.4, and 10 mM glucose, adjusted to 335–345 mOsm. Borosilicate patch pipettes (Harvard Apparatus, 5–6 MΩ) were filled with intracellular medium containing 140 mM potassium gluconate, 10 mM EGTA, 2 mM MgCl2 and 10 mM HEPES, pH 7.2. Signals were acquired using a MultiClamp 700B amplifier and Digidata 1400 digitizer (Molecular Devices), with cells visualized on a Leica DM LFSA microscope. Whole-cell voltage-clamp recordings were performed at −70 mV. For optogenetic experiments, 460 nm light at 1.0 mW mm−2 was delivered for 1 s, and photocurrents were measured. For mechanosensitive current recordings, cells were stimulated with isotonic extracellular medium or hypertonic medium containing 300 mM sucrose to evoke osmotic stretch-induced currents. Cell-attached recordings were performed as previously described. The external solution used to zero membrane potential contained 140 mM KCl, 1 mM MgCl2, 10 mM glucose and 10 mM HEPES, pH 7.3, with KOH. Patch pipettes (3–6 MΩ) contained 130 mM NaCl, 5 mM KCl, 1 mM CaCl2, 1 mM MgCl2, 10 mM TEA-Cl and 10 mM HEPES, pH 7.3, with NaOH, with 30 µM Yoda1, 10 µM GsMTx4 or 500 nM NLGN3 added where indicated. Membrane patches were stimulated with 500-ms or 1-s negative pressure pulses using a Clampex-controlled HSPC-1 pressure clamp device (ALA Scientific), with pressure steps from 0 to −60 mmHg in −10 mmHg increments applied every 30 s. All recordings were performed at room temperature and analyzed using pClamp10 (Molecular Devices). Mechanically activated currents were quantified from peak currents measured from pressure-step onset to 1.0 s to capture late or secondary currents.

Statistics and reproducibility

No statistical methods were used to predetermine sample sizes. Sample sizes were chosen based on those commonly used in comparable studies and were equal to or larger than those reported in such studies. All experiments were performed at least thrice independently to obtain representative images and graphs. No data were excluded from the analyses. Where applicable, experiments were randomized, and investigators were blinded to allocation during experiments and outcome assessment, as described in the Methods. For calcium imaging, we performed experiments in three independent biological replicates, defined as experiments conducted on different dates with independently isolated, treated and imaged cells. Individual cell measurements within each experiment were considered technical replicates nested within each biological replicate. For western blot experiments in Fig. 3, the NLGN3 control in Fig. 3f,m is from the same run (first run, n = 4, all six conditions). Fig. 3m adds an independent second run (n = 4) for CytoD-related conditions (n = 8 total). For each condition, cell-level responses were averaged to generate one replicate-level summary value for each biological replicate. Independent calcium imaging datasets are presented separately in Extended Data Fig. 2e, corresponding to Fig. 3, and Extended Data Fig. 4a, corresponding to Fig. 4. Where applicable, statistical analyses were performed at the biological replicate level using replicate-level means. Power analyses were conducted using a two-sided two-sample t-test framework, with α = 0.05, pooled inter-replicate variance, Cohen’s d effect sizes and standard normal approximations to estimate the number of biological replicates required. For Fig. 3, statistical testing was performed directly using n = 3 independent biological replicates per condition, and replicate-level statistics are reported herein. For Fig. 4, power analyses indicated that substantially larger numbers of biological replicates would be required for some comparisons. Therefore, replicate-level statistical testing was not emphasized, and the results were interpreted in the context of limited statistical power.
