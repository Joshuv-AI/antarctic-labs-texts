Retrobulbar haemorrhage post sub-Tenons block: a case report
Authors: Alessandro M Costa, Sharanjeet Sidhu, Thomas G Hardy
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRetrobulbar hemorrhage is a rare, sight-threatening complication from trauma, orbital surgery, and, rarely, regional orbital anesthetic injections. This may lead to orbital compartment syndrome with vascular and neural compromise and subsequent irreversible vision loss if not addressed promptly. Retrobulbar hemorrhage following sub-Tenon’s anesthetic is very rare, typically occurring in patients with an increased risk of bleeding from various causes.Case presentationA 75-year-old Asian male with no known bleeding diathesis developed an acute retrobulbar hemorrhage immediately following a routine sub-Tenon’s block performed for a planned encysted bleb needling. The patient experienced sudden onset of severe ocular pain with tense eyelids, loss of extraocular movements, and ocular hypertension. Emergency lateral canthotomy and cantholysis resulted in prompt resolution of orbital compartment syndrome.ConclusionThis case highlights a rare occurrence of retrobulbar hemorrhage following a routine sub-Tenon’s block in a patient without predisposing risk factors. Prompt recognition and timely intervention were crucial in preserving the patient’s vision. The case underscores the importance of vigilance and awareness of retrobulbar hemorrhage as a potential complication of sub-Tenon’s block.Keywords: Retrobulbar hemorrhage, Sub-Tenons block, Canthotomy, Cantholysis

Introduction

Retrobulbar hemorrhage (RBH) is a rare and potentially rapidly progressive condition that poses a significant threat to vision. RBH is primarily a clinical diagnosis with hallmark features including severe pain, nausea, vomiting, periorbital bruising, reduced ocular motility, proptosis, vision loss, relative afferent pupillary defect, elevated intraocular pressure (IOP), and subconjunctival hemorrhage [1].

Untreated, RBH can result in orbital compartment syndrome, characterized by optic nerve compression, venous tamponade, and central retinal artery occlusion. These complications can lead to optic nerve ischemia and irreversible vision loss [1, 2]. The gold-standard treatment is prompt surgical decompression via lateral canthotomy and cantholysis. To optimize visual outcomes, the intervention is recommended within 120 min of symptom onset [3]. Pharmacological adjuncts, including ocular hypotensives (that is, acetazolamide, mannitol, and topical therapies), reversal of antithrombotic therapy (that is, andexanet and idarucizimab) or bleeding diatheses (that is, tranexamic acid and vitamin K), and intravenous corticosteroids are often required. [5]

RBH may result from trauma, orbital or eyelid surgery, and, in rare instances, spontaneous hemorrhage [4] or regional orbital anesthetic techniques [1]. With advancements in regional orbital blocks, sub-Tenon’s block (STB) has emerged as the preferred method for orbital anesthesia and akinesia due to its enhanced safety profile [6]. The STB involves administering local anesthetic using a blunt cannula into the sub-Tenon’s space, a potential space between Tenon’s capsule and the sclera [6]. The local anesthetic tracks posteriorly to the intraconal space providing analgesia via blockade of the short ciliary nerve and, after diffusion along the extraocular muscle paths, akinesia from blockade of the motor nerves [6]. This technique significantly reduces the risk of complications compared with the sharp-needle methods of peribulbar and retrobulbar blocks with estimated RBH incidence of 0.4%. [7, 8]

There are few publications describing RBH post STB [9–12]. This case report contributes to the existing literature by detailing an instance of RBH in a patient with no underlying risk factors following a routine STB. With prompt recognition and timely intervention, the patient’s visual outcome was preserved. This case underscores the need for continued vigilance among clinicians performing regional orbital anesthesia to minimize the risk of RBH and optimize patient safety.

Case presentation

A 75-year-old man was scheduled for an elective needling of an encysted right trabeculectomy bleb.

The patient’s ocular history included primary open angle glaucoma, for which he had undergone a right selective laser trabeculoplasty 6 months prior. This was followed by a right trabeculectomy with mitomycin C 2 months earlier and four subsequent bleb needlings with adjunctive 5-fluorouracil. Additional ophthalmic history included bilateral cataract surgery and bilateral pan-retinal photocoagulation for nonproliferative diabetic retinopathy. His medical history included type 2 diabetes, hypertension, dyslipidemia, and asthma. Current medications included atorvastatin, enalapril, fluticasone/vilanterol, mirtazapine, semaglutide, insulin, and salbutamol. He was not on any antiplatelet or anticoagulant therapy.

Preoperative visual acuity was 6/30 right and 6/9 left. His intraocular pressures were 31 mmHg in the right and 27 mmHg in the left. His blood pressure was between 130 and 140 mmHg systolic with blood sugar levels of < 7 mmol/L.

Given the patient’s history of a prior uncomplicated STB in the same eye under similar blood pressure conditions, a decision was made by the surgeon and anesthetist to proceed with another STB to facilitate the bleb needling. Under topical anesthesia and sedation with fentanyl and propofol, an inferonasal limbal conjunctival incision and blunt dissection was performed using Westcott scissors. A curved 19G, 25 mm blunt metal sub-Tenon’s cannula was advanced into the sub-Tenon’s space, through which 3.5 mL of 2% lignocaine was administered via the inferonasal approach.

Upon speculum removal, the patient complained of right eye pain and was found to have tense eyelids requiring manual manipulation to open, intermittent pulse related nonperfusion of the central retinal artery on ophthalmoscopy, globally reduced eye movements, and an intraocular pressure of 46 mmHg. The planned bleb needling was abandoned.

A clinical diagnosis of retrobulbar haemorrhage (RBH) was made, and an emergency lateral canthotomy and upper and lower lateral cantholysis were performed within 30 min of the STB. The procedure provided immediate symptomatic relief with an of IOP decrease to 10 mmHg. Ocular motility remained limited; however, due to the insidious onset of the RBH following the STB, it was not possible to assess for any immediate improvement in motility.

The patient was admitted overnight for observation and the following morning his right sided visual acuity was 6/75 and intraocular pressure of 8 mmHg with no relative afferent pupillary defect, full range of eye movements and an inferonasal subconjunctival hemorrhage. The patient was discharged with topical chloramphenicol.

At 1 week follow up visual acuity was 6/19 and IOP 8 mmHg. He was reviewed in the oculoplastics clinic a month later with a healed canthotomy and cantholysis wound. Eight months later, his visual acuity was 6/12 right and 6/9.6 left with intraocular pressures of 17 mmHg in the right and 26 mmHg in the left, with a plan for right eye observation and left incisional glaucoma surgery.

Discussion

RBH is a rare but serious complication following a STB. Our review identified four previously reported cases of RBH associated with STB; however, these cases all involved patients with identifiable risk factors such as anticoagulation or antiplatelet use, increased axial length, or administration of a higher-than-recommended volume of local anesthetic into the sub-Tenon’s space [9–12]. By contrast, the case we describe occurred in a patient without these established risk factors other than a mildly elevated systolic blood pressure, highlighting the need to consider RBH as a potential complication even in low-risk individuals.

Several patient-related factors have been implicated in RBH development. Advanced age has been associated with reduced vascular elasticity, increasing vessel fragility and susceptibility to hemorrhage [13]. While coagulopathies and the use of anticoagulant or antiplatelet therapy have traditionally been considered risk factors, one study found no significant increase in RBH incidence among patients on these medications [14].

Technical aspects of the procedure can also influence RBH risk. The use of flexible, blunt-tipped sub-Tenon’s specific cannulas is generally preferred due to their lower risk of vascular trauma compared with rigid cannulas [15]. Additionally, shorter cannulas may further reduce the likelihood of complications by limiting inadvertent penetration into deeper orbital structures [16].

The volume and rate of anesthetic administration may also contribute to RBH risk. Current guidelines recommend administering a maximum of 3–4 mL of local anesthetic at a rate of 1 mL per 3 s to prevent sudden increases in orbital pressure, which could disrupt fragile orbital microvasculature [17].

Injection site is another factor with an infero-nasal quadrant being the preferred site for STB due to its relatively low vascular density [18]. By contrast, an infero-temporal approach carries a higher risk of vascular injury, particularly due to the proximity of vortex veins to the sclera and venous tortuosity in this quadrant [19].

This case underscores the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion for RBH, even in patients without apparent risk factors. Given the potential for sight-threatening complications, clinicians performing STB should exercise meticulous technique, ensure careful patient selection, and be prepared to recognize and manage RBH promptly.

Conclusion

Retrobulbar hemorrhage is an uncommon but potentially sight-threatening complication of sub-Tenon’s blocks. Although its incidence is low, particularly with the use of specific, flexible cannulas and controlled injection techniques, vigilance remains crucial. Preoperative risk assessment, safe procedural practices, and prompt management of complications are essential to achieving favorable outcomes. Continued refinement of techniques and further research into risk mitigation strategies will help minimize this rare-but-serious complication.
