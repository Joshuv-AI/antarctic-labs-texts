Markedly Delayed Onset ICANS in the Setting of CAR T-Cell-Mediated Aseptic Meningoencephalitis: Two Cases
Authors: Andy Y Wang, Catherine Suen, Iyas Daghlas, Anupama Kumar, David Oh, Douglas Pet, Vanja Douglas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveImmune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS) typically manifests within the first week following chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T-cell therapy and has rarely been described to develop beyond 1 month after treatment. This report describes the occurrence of markedly delayed ICANS in the setting of non-infectious meningoencephalitis.MethodsWe report two cases of markedly delayed ICANS with cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) pleocytosis comprised primarily of CAR T-cells.ResultsTwo patients, aged 54 and 67 years, developed ICANS at 71 and 46 days, respectively, following administration of ciltacabtagene autoleucel (cilta-cel), a B-cell maturation antigen (BCMA)-directed CAR T-cell therapy. CSF analysis demonstrated elevated total nucleated cell (TNC) counts of 1055 and 605 cells/μL, respectively, with marked lymphocytic predominance (94% and 90%), of which 90.4% and 95.4% were CAR T-cells. Both patients improved with corticosteroids and immunotherapy.DiscussionThe identification of CAR T-cells in CSF analysis proved critical for clinical management, enabling early discontinuation of empiric meningeal antimicrobial therapy and implementation of targeted immunosuppressive treatment. This report highlights a rare presentation of markedly delayed ICANS associated with robust infiltration of CAR T-cells into the CSF, expanding our understanding of the temporal spectrum and pathophysiology of ICANS.Keywords: ICANS, CAR T-cell, neurotoxicity, cerebrospinal fluid analysis, case reports

Introduction

Immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS) is common after chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T-cell therapy, typically occurring 2-7 days following infusion. 1 Rarely, ICANS may have a delayed onset in the third or fourth week post-infusion. 2 Variable clinical presentations for delayed-onset ICANS have been described including cognitive changes, cranial neuropathies, parkinsonism, and Guillan-Barré syndrome.3-5 Only a few reports describe ICANS occurring beyond this time window, and the clinical risk is poorly understood.6-8 Here, we describe two patients who presented with markedly delayed neurotoxicity found to have cerebrospinal fluid pleocytosis comprised primarily of CAR T-cells.

Methods

Data Availability and Patient Consents

This case series is exempt from formal ethics review board approval by the University of California, San Francisco, and was prepared in accordance with CARE guidelines. Patients were consented via written informed consent-to-disclose forms and as part of research protocols (IRB #10-00545 and #21-34280) to detect CAR T-cells in excess CSF by flow cytometry. Further identifying data are not publicly available to protect patient confidentiality.

Case Series

Patient 1

A 54-year-old man with IgA lambda multiple myeloma refractory to conventional treatments underwent CAR T-cell therapy directed against B-cell maturation antigen (BCMA) with ciltacabtagene autoleucel (cilta-cel). On the day of CAR T-cell infusion, he developed grade 1 cytokine release syndrome (CRS) and was started on tocilizumab. CRS progressed to grade 2 on day 8, and he was administered intravenous (IV) dexamethasone 10 mg every 12 h for 3 days. No symptoms concerning for neurotoxicity were noted. 71 days post-infusion, the patient developed a left-sided throbbing pain, which improved with acetaminophen. The next day, he suddenly developed word-finding difficulties and mild upper extremity postural tremors (Figure 1). Concern for ICANS prompted treatment with oral dexamethasone 10 mg twice a day, which led to improvement over a matter of hours after starting steroids and complete return to baseline by the day after.

Neurological examination 1 day after starting steroids was normal except for mild bilateral proximal leg weakness. CSF was notable for 1055 x106/L total nucleated cells (TNCs) with lymphocytic predominance (94%), protein of 207 mg/dL, and glucose of 78 mg/dL. Meningeal-dosed IV ceftriaxone, vancomycin, ampicillin, and acyclovir were administered. MRI brain with and without contrast demonstrated stable post-radiation changes in the left orbit from a prior plasmacytoma, and video electroencephalogram (EEG) showed no epileptiform activity. CSF flow cytometry revealed 90.4% CAR T-cells. Further CSF studies including bacterial culture, herpes simplex virus polymerase chain reaction (HSV PCR), and varicella zoster virus (VZV) PCR, VZV IgM, and VZV IgG were unremarkable. Metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) of CSF and serum samples detected human herpesvirus 7 (HHV-7), with a serum copy number of 342 copies/mL, considered clinically noncontributory. Given clinical stability and a negative infectious workup, antibiotics were discontinued after 5 days. The patient was treated with IVIG 1 g/kg daily × 2 doses and oral dexamethasone 10 mg twice a day and discharged after 14 days on a 5-week steroid taper. CSF was reassessed on post-infusion days 79, 84, and 126, and showed 58 TNCs (98% lymphocytes; 92.9% CAR T-cells), 17 TNCs (97% lymphocytes; 92.8% CAR T-cells), and 13 TNCs (97% lymphocytes, CAR T-cell % not tested), respectively.

Patient 2

A 67-year-old man with IgA lambda multiple myeloma refractory to conventional treatments underwent treatment with cilta-cel. 7 days post-infusion, he experienced grade 1 CRS and was started on tocilizumab. This progressed to grade 2 CRS the following day, for which he received IV dexamethasone 10 mg every 12 h for three doses, escalated to 20 mg every 6 h for 1 day, decreased to 20 mg twice a day for 1 day, 10 mg twice a day for 1 day, and then 10 mg on the last day. No symptoms concerning for neurotoxicity were noted. 46 days post-infusion, he began to experience progressive brain fog, confusion, headaches, fatigue, and gastrointestinal upset (Figure 1). He presented to the hematology clinic three days later and was admitted to the hospital for evaluation.

Neurological exam at admission was unremarkable. CSF showed 605 TNCs (x106/L) with lymphocytic predominance (90%), glucose of 52 mg/dL, and protein of 288 mg/dL. CSF labs were negative for bacterial Gram stain and culture, HSV PCR, and VZV PCR, VZV IgM, and VZV IgG. CSF mNGS detected HHV-7, with a serum copy number of 900 copies/mL, which was favored to be clinically noncontributory. The patient was continued on his home oral dexamethasone (8 mg daily), prophylactic acyclovir, and atovaquone. Vancomycin and meropenem were administered and then discontinued 1 day later, when CSF flow cytometry revealed 95.4% CAR T-cells. MRI of the brain showed no acute intracranial abnormalities, and EEG showed no epileptiform activity. On day 52 post-infusion, the patient experienced acute-onset confusion, right-sided facial droop, and receptive aphasia. CT/CTA head and neck was without hemorrhage or large vessel occlusion, and the EEG was without seizures. He received additional oral dexamethasone 4 mg that day, with symptoms improving by the end of the day and completely resolving by the next morning. The patient was treated with 1 dose of IV cyclophosphamide 2,000 mg/m2 and IVIG 2 g/kg over 4 days and was discharged after 7 days in the hospital. At the time of this report, no additional CSF has been sampled.

Discussion

In this case series, we describe two patients presenting with markedly delayed ICANS (71 and 46 days post-infusion), associated with CSF pleocytosis consisting mainly of CAR T-cells. Both patients presented with mild clinical symptoms and had negative infectious workup, unremarkable neuroimaging, and absence of epileptiform activity on EEG. Meningeal-dosed antibiotics were discontinued early in both cases, given suspicion that symptoms and CSF findings were driven by CAR T-cell activity in the CSF rather than meningoencephalitis due to an infectious pathogen.

To our knowledge, only a few reports of markedly delayed ICANS have been previously published (Table 1). Jung et al described a 50-year-old patient with diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) treated with axicabtagene ciloleucel who developed new neurological symptoms 8 months post-infusion. CAR T-cells were identified in the CSF, and the infectious workup was unremarkable. The patient ultimately passed away with a brain autopsy suggestive of severe CAR T-cell–triggered autoimmune encephalitis. 7 Others have reported recurrent ICANS, with episodes spanning into the markedly delayed timeframe. Badar et al described a 76-year-old patient with DLBCL who received axicabtagene ciloleucel CAR T-cell therapy and experienced grade 3 CRS and recurrent ICANS on days 6, 33, and 172 post-infusion; 6 however, CSF data were not reported. De Philippis et al described a 44-year-old patient with mantle cell lymphoma who received brexucabtagene autoleucel and developed grade 2 CRS and recurrent ICANS on days 11, 30, and 58 post-infusion. 8 CSF analysis demonstrated 141 TNCs on day 30 (5.4% CAR T-cells) and 156 on day 58 (93.6% CAR T-cells).

ICANS has been attributed to endothelial activation and increased permeability of the blood-brain barrier, resulting in cytokine-mediated neuroinflammation. 9 Disruption of the blood-brain barrier may result in trafficking of CAR T-cells to the central nervous system via mural cells that express CD19.10,11 Some investigators have postulated that the quantity of CAR T-cells in the CSF does not correlate with ICANS severity. 9 Whether CAR T-cells in the CSF are bystanders or active contributors to neurotoxicity remains unclear, and more studies are needed to elucidate the mechanistic pathways underlying ICANS and the heterogeneity in timing of presentation.

It is also notable that both patients were found to have HHV-7 serum positivity, a common cause of childhood febrile illness with known lifelong latency and an uncertain role in neurologic disease even among immunocompromised patients, though often reported in case studies involving transplant patients. 12 HHV-7 has been detected in the brain tissue of patients not suspected of a viral infection at the time of death, and even when present in CSF, its pathogenic role is unclear. 13 However, given that HHV-7 is a CD4 + T-lymphotropic virus, whether it interacts with a CAR-T-cell population warrants further investigation.

The necessity of CSF analysis in evaluating ICANS remains debated. Lumbar punctures are often challenging in patients undergoing CAR T-cell therapy, given frequent thrombocytopenia, concern about cerebral edema, and agitation. One cohort study questioned the utility of lumbar puncture, finding that CSF analysis did not lead to any relevant therapeutic modification even in severe ICANS. 14 Others have stressed the importance of CSF sampling, especially in cases with atypical timing or recurrence, noting that ICANS can mask or co-occur with infection or leptomeningeal lymphoma.15,16 Our cases further underscore the utility of CSF analysis in delayed ICANS, which, given its rarity, must be distinguished from the broad spectrum of CNS infectious and inflammatory processes for which this population is at risk.

Conclusion

Two patients presented with markedly delayed ICANS associated with CAR T-cell-mediated aseptic meningoencephalitis. These cases, along with a review of prior literature, contribute to the still-nascent understanding of the clinical, pathologic, and temporal spectrum of ICANS. Further research is needed to understand the heterogeneous phenomena of ICANS and the pathophysiologic significance and clinical implications of CAR T-cells in the CSF.
