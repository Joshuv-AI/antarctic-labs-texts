MRSA-dominant colonization and synergistic pathogenicity with Pseudomonas aeruginosa in a polymicrobial bone infection model
Authors: Hang Zhou, Wanchang Liu, Xinliang Peng, Maohui Li, Zhongyuan He, Hang Liu, Xingyu Yang, Zhaoyang Guo, Wentao Zhang, Wei Fu, Hanchao Liang, Lei Shi, Rui Deng, Yaohua Chen, Ruixiang Zhang, Yongjun Dang, Chao Xie, Yunying Wang, Zhongliang Deng, Lei Chu, Youliang Ren

ABSTRACT
ObjectivePolymicrobial bone infections (PBI) remain difficult to treat due to multi-drug resistance and poorly defined microbial interactions. Co-infection with methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) and Pseudomonas aeruginosa (PA) represents a clinically prevalent subtype of PBI. Using a rat PBI model, this study aimed to elucidate the spatiotemporal dynamics of MRSA-PA co-infection and its impact on host responses, bacterial persistence, and antimicrobial susceptibility.MethodsTitanium screws were pre-contaminated with fluorescently labeled MRSA (mCherry) or PA (green fluorescent protein, GFP) and implanted 5 mm apart into the femoral mid-diaphysis of Sprague–Dawley rats. Animals were assigned to five groups: sterile, MRSA, PA, MRSA + PA, and PA + MRSA. Longitudinal evaluation included hematology, serum biochemistry (CRP, PCT, TNF-α, and IL-6), micro-CT imaging, and hard-tissue sectioning. Colony-forming unit (CFU) counts and histological analyses (H&E, Gram, and DAPI staining) were performed to evaluate bacterial burden, spatiotemporal colonization and biofilm formation. Local inflammation and osteoclast activation were further assessed by TNF-α immunohistochemistry and tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) staining. Antimicrobial susceptibility was evaluated by automated MIC testing and complementary disk diffusion assays. Transcriptomic and whole-genome sequencing analyses were performed to characterize transcriptional adaptations and virulence- and resistance-associated gene profiles.ResultsCo-infection induced a synergistic inflammatory response, with a 6.8-fold increase in neutrophil counts relative to baseline, and caused severe osteolysis, with an approximately 80% reduction in bone–implant contact relative to the sterile group. At postoperative day 7, the co-infection groups showed increased local TNF-α-positive and TRAP-positive areas, supporting an association between the amplified inflammatory response and early osteoclast activation. Spatial analyses revealed asymmetric colonization dynamics: MRSA rapidly disseminated to and colonized PA-contaminated sites by postoperative day 7, whereas reciprocal PA dissemination into MRSA-dominant sites was not detected until day 28. Both pathogens concurrently infiltrated the osteocyte lacuno-canalicular network, uncovering a previously unrecognized reservoir for polymicrobial persistence within bone. Multi-omics profiling revealed susceptibility shifts and extensive adaptive reprogramming in PA.ConclusionMRSA acts as a dominant ecological driver in PBI that amplifies bone destruction and facilitates polymicrobial persistence, emphasizing the need for multi-species therapeutic strategies.The translational potential of this articleThis study establishes a reproducible rat model of polymicrobial bone infection and reveals dynamic MRSA–PA interactions, pathogen-specific ex vivo susceptibility shifts, and polymicrobial occupation of the osteocyte lacuno-canalicular network. These findings highlight the limitations of single-pathogen-based treatment strategies and support the development of multispecies-directed antimicrobial, immunomodulatory, and biofilm-targeting approaches. The model provides a clinically relevant platform for mechanistic studies and preclinical evaluation of diagnostics, therapeutics, drug-delivery systems, and orthopaedic implants.

Introduction

Post-traumatic bone infection (PTBI) remains one of the most challenging complications in orthopedic trauma, characterized by prolonged treatment courses, frequent relapse, and suboptimal cure rates [[1], [2], [3]]. Despite aggressive surgical debridement combined with systemic antibiotic therapy representing the current standard of care, recurrence of infection after initial eradication remains high at 6.8%–16.67% [[4], [5], [6], [7]]. Moreover, polymicrobial bone infection (PBI) accounts for up to 37.8% of PTBI cases, and is consistently associated with higher rates of multidrug resistance, greater need for repeat surgical interventions, and increased risk of treatment failure compared with monomicrobial disease [3,[8], [9], [10]]. However, despite well-established animal models for implant-associated, post-traumatic, or hematogenous osteomyelitis, no standardized in vivo model for PBI currently exists, thereby hindering mechanistic studies and preclinical therapeutic evaluation [[11], [12], [13]]. Therefore, establishing a reliable animal model that closely recapitulates clinical PBI is critical for elucidating its pathogenesis and advancing treatment development.

Epidemiological evidence indicates that co-infection with Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) and Pseudomonas aeruginosa (P. aeruginosa, PA) represents the most prevalent subtype of polymicrobial bone infection, accounting for approximately 16.7% of reported cases [1,3]. Notably, the detection rate of methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA) in PBI is nearly 3.7-fold higher than that observed in monomicrobial infections, underscoring its strong association with disease complexity and treatment failure [14]. Accumulating experimental and clinical data suggest that co-colonization of these two species can synergistically enhance pathogenicity, aggravate tissue destruction, and alter antibiotic susceptibility as well as resistance to host immune clearance [[15], [16], [17]]. These interspecies interactions are thought to involve metabolic cooperation, quorum-sensing interference, and biofilm-mediated protection [[18], [19], [20], [21]]. However, most existing studies have relied on in vitro co-culture systems or soft-tissue infection models, which fail to recapitulate the unique anatomical, biomechanical, and immunological features of the bone microenvironment. Therefore, a clinically relevant polymicrobial bone infection model based on MRSA–PA co-infection is essential to elucidate interspecies interactions, immune evasion, antibiotic tolerance, and cooperative pathogenic mechanisms in PBI.

Direct injection of bacterial suspensions into the medullary cavity or bone defects is commonly used to establish experimental osteomyelitis models; however, its applicability to polymicrobial infection is limited [11,12,22]. In MRSA–PA co-infection, complex interspecies interactions may distort early colonization events when mixed bacterial suspensions are injected simultaneously, resulting in biased or unstable infection outcomes [23]. Notably, PA has been shown to suppress S. aureus growth and virulence in co-culture, potentially reducing effective S. aureus colonization in bone despite equivalent inoculation doses [24,25]. For example, PaEVs from PA can inhibit the initial growth of S. aureus by suppressing the pyruvate fermentation pathway [26]. Thus, a key methodological challenge is to introduce precise and reproducible quantities of two bacterial species while preserving their native phenotypes and enabling stable co-infection. Based on previous experience, implantation of titanium pins or screws pre-contaminated with a single bacterial strain provides a robust and reproducible platform for modeling implant-associated osteomyelitis [[27], [28], [29], [30]]. This approach offers several advantages, including controlled bacterial loading, prevention of inoculum leakage, and the ability to implant screws contaminated with different strains at defined locations or time points, thereby minimizing early interspecies interference and facilitating spatiotemporal analysis of colonization dynamics within the bone microenvironment.

In this study, we established a rat PBI model by implanting titanium screws pre-contaminated with fluorescently labeled MRSA (mCherry) or PA (GFP) at spatially separated sites in the femur. Systemic and local host responses to co-infection were comprehensively evaluated using hematological and biochemical analyses, micro-CT imaging, and histopathological assessment. TNF-α immunohistochemistry and tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase staining were further performed to examine the association between local inflammation and osteoclast activation. In parallel, colony-forming unit (CFU) quantification combined with fluorescence-based localization was employed to define the spatiotemporal colonization dynamics of MRSA and PA in vivo. Antimicrobial susceptibility was evaluated using automated MIC testing and complementary disk diffusion assays, while transcriptomic and whole-genome sequencing analyses were performed to characterize transcriptional adaptation and virulence- and resistance-associated gene profiles. Our findings reveal temporally asymmetric MRSA–PA colonization, amplified inflammation, exacerbated osteolysis, and pathogen-specific susceptibility shifts in a polymicrobial bone infection model. Collectively, this study uncovers previously unrecognized interspecies interactions during PBI, and establishes a robust in vivo framework for mechanistic investigation of refractory PBI and preclinical evaluation of therapeutic strategies.

Materials and methods

Bacterial culture and fluorescent labeling

Plasmids encoding the mCherry fluorescent protein together with a chloramphenicol resistance marker were introduced into methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA, ATCC BAA 1717) to generate a stable MRSA mCherry strain exhibiting red fluorescence with a peak emission wavelength of 610 nm. Similarly, plasmids encoding the green fluorescent protein together with a streptomycin resistance marker were introduced into P. aeruginosa (PA, ATCC 15692) to generate a stable PA GFP strain exhibiting green fluorescence with a peak emission wavelength of 509 nm. MRSA mCherry and PA GFP strains were cultured in Luria Bertani (LB) broth supplemented with chloramphenicol at a final concentration of 10 μg per milliliter or streptomycin at a final concentration of 25 μg per milliliter, respectively. Fluorescent signal stability and expression efficiency were verified using fluorescence microscopy prior to subsequent experiments.

Preparation of bacteria contaminated titanium screws

Titanium screws contaminated with bacteria were prepared according to previously established protocols with minor modifications [[27], [28], [29], [30]]. Briefly, sterile titanium screws (5 mm in length, 1.5 mm in diameter) were immersed in logarithmic phase MRSA mCherry bacterial suspension at a concentration of 1 × 106 CFU/mL. Screws were incubated at 37°C for 16 h to allow bacterial adhesion. Following incubation, the screws were removed from the suspension and air dried under sterile conditions for 30 min. Titanium screws contaminated with PA GFP were prepared using the same procedure.

The bacterial load on the screw surfaces was quantified as described in previous reports [[27], [28], [29]]. In brief, individual screws were placed into 1 mL sterile phosphate buffered saline (PBS), sonicated at 40 kHz for 15 min to detach adherent bacteria, and subsequently vortexed for 5 min. The resulting bacterial suspensions were serially diluted tenfold and plated onto LB agar plates for colony enumeration.

Establishment of the rat femoral PBI model

All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University (Approval No: IACUC-SAHCQMU-2024-00020) and were conducted in accordance with institutional and national guidelines for animal welfare. Sixty 12-week-old male Sprague Dawley rats were adapted to a standard micro-isolation environment one week before surgery.

Rats were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital sodium (40 mg/kg). Following hair removal, skin disinfection, and sterile draping, a longitudinal incision approximately 2 cm in length was made along the lateral aspect of the left thigh to expose the femoral diaphysis. Under continuous saline irrigation to prevent thermal injury, two bone tunnels were created through the anterior and posterior cortical bone using a 1.2 mm diameter drill bit, with an inter tunnel distance of 5 mm. Titanium screws contaminated with MRSA-mCherry and or PA-GFP were then implanted into the prepared bone tunnels. The 5-mm inter-screw distance allowed the two bacterial species to be initially separated while still permitting subsequent in vivo migration and interspecies interaction within the rat femur [[27], [28], [29],31]. Based on the bacterial contamination of the implanted screws, five groups were established: the sterile control group with sterile screws, the PA group receiving PA-GFP-contaminated screws only, the MRSA group receiving MRSA-mCherry-contaminated screws only, the MRSA + PA group in which the proximal screw was contaminated with MRSA-mCherry and the distal screw with PA-GFP, and the PA + MRSA group in which the proximal screw was contaminated with PA-GFP and the distal screw with MRSA-mCherry. Each group consisted of twelve rats.

Hematological and serological analysis of experimental rats

Peripheral blood samples were collected from the subclavian vein of rats at 7 days before surgery and on postoperative days 1, 3, 7, 14, 21, and 28. Leukocytes, neutrophil, and monocyte counts were determined using an automated veterinary hematology analyzer (BC-2800vet). Whole blood samples were centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 5 min, after which serum was carefully collected for subsequent analyses. Serum concentrations of C reactive protein (CRP; Multi Sciences, EK394), procalcitonin (PCT; mlbio, ml107018), interleukin 6 (IL-6; Multi Sciences, EK306), and tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α; Multi Sciences, EK382) were quantified using commercially available enzyme linked immunosorbent assay kits according to the manufacturers’ instructions.

Hard-tissue section staining and micro-CT analysis

On postoperative days 7 and 28, three rats from each experimental group were randomly selected and euthanized by overdose administration of pentobarbital sodium. Femoral specimens were harvested and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 48 h. Micro-CT (SCANCO Medical AG, Switzerland) was used to scan the femoral samples containing screws, and the bone volume fraction surrounding the screw surfaces was quantitatively analyzed using the manufacturer-provided software.

For hard-tissue histological analysis, femoral samples with retained screws were processed using gradient ethanol dehydration, followed by resin infiltration and light induced polymerization embedding as previously reported [29]. Three parallel samples were prepared for each group. After embedding, specimens were adhered to glass slides, and targeted regions within the embedded blocks were sectioned using a precision microtome. Sections were polished to a final thickness of approximately 10 μm and stained with methylene blue and basic fuchsin. The bone implant contact area percentage was quantified using ImageJ software and further analyzed with GraphPad Prism.

Quantification of bacteria on screw surfaces

Bacterial colonization of implanted titanium screws was quantified according to previously established protocols [[27], [28], [29]]. Briefly, screws were carefully retrieved from the femora and immediately placed into centrifuge tubes containing 1 mL sterile PBS. The screws were sonicated at 40 kHz for 15 min and subsequently vortexed for 5 min to detach adherent bacteria from the screw surfaces. The sonicates were plated onto mannitol salt agar plates for selective isolation of MRSA and cetrimide agar plates for selective isolation of PA [32]. Bacterial colonies were counted after incubation at 37°C for 24 h.

Bone tissue decalcification and histological staining

Harvested femoral samples were decalcified using ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid solution, followed by graded dehydration and paraffin embedding. Sagittal sections of the femur containing the largest cross-sectional area of the screw tunnel were cut at a thickness of 5 μm for hematoxylin and eosin staining and Gram staining. Tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) staining was performed using a commercial TRAP staining kit (Servicebio, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. For TNF-α immunohistochemistry, sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, subjected to antigen retrieval and endogenous peroxidase blocking, and subsequently incubated with a primary antibody against TNF-α (Servicebio, GB11188-100). After incubation with the secondary antibody (Servicebio, GB23303), immunoreactivity was visualized using 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB). The TRAP-positive area normalized to the total tissue area and the percentage of TNF-α-positive area were quantified using ImageJ software.

To visualize bacterial colonization at the screw implantation site, additional sections were stained with 4′,6 diamidino 2 phenylindole (DAPI) and examined using fluorescence microscopy. Cell nuclei exhibited blue fluorescence, whereas MRSA-mCherry and PA-GFP displayed red and green fluorescence signals, respectively.

Antibiotic susceptibility testing

Colonies of MRSA or PA were selected and resuspended in 4 mL sterile saline in a test tube. The bacterial suspension was gently mixed and adjusted to a turbidity of 0.5 McFarland standards using a spectrophotometer. Each standardized bacterial suspension was loaded into the antibiotic susceptibility testing rack, with an empty McFarland control tube placed adjacent to each sample tube for calibration. For MRSA isolates, VITEK AST GP639 cards were used, whereas VITEK AST GN335 cards were applied for PA isolates. The cards were inserted into the VITEK automated microbial susceptibility analyzer (bioMérieux, France) and incubated for 16 h prior to automated analysis. Antibiotic susceptibility profiles were interpreted according to the 2025 Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute Global Standard.

To further verify the antimicrobial susceptibility phenotypes, a disk diffusion assay was performed using the recovered bacterial isolates. Bacterial suspensions were adjusted to a turbidity of 0.5 McFarland standard and evenly inoculated onto Mueller–Hinton agar plates. Daptomycin disks (Liofilchem, Italy) and amikacin disks (Oxoid, UK) were placed on the agar surface, followed by incubation at 37°C for 18 h. Antimicrobial susceptibility was evaluated by measuring the diameters of the inhibition zones (XMVision KBII V6.31, China).

Transcriptome sequencing analysis

Total RNA from PA strains was extracted using TRIzol reagent. The RNA concentration and purity were quantified using Nanodrop 2000, with acceptable OD260/280 ratios ranging from 1.8 to 2.2. Only RNA samples with a total yield of at least 2 μg and a concentration of not less than 100 ng/μL were included in downstream analyses. rRNA was removed using the Ribo-Zero rRNA Removal Kit (Illumina), and the remaining RNA was fragmented into approximately 200 base pair segments. cDNA synthesis was performed using reverse transcriptase, and the obtained cDNA was then amplified by PCR and purified, followed by sequencing on the Illumina platform. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were analyzed using the DESeq2 R package, with a significance threshold of a corrected P-value < 0.05 and |log2FC| > 0.585.

Genomic sequencing analysis

Genomic DNA from isolated MRSA and PA strains was extracted using the Bacterial Genomic DNA Extraction Kit (Shanghai Shenggong, China). DNA quality was evaluated by agarose gel electrophoresis and micro volume spectrophotometry, and only samples with concentrations greater than 50 ng/μL and OD260/280 ratios between 1.8 and 2.0 were selected for sequencing. Genomic DNA was mechanically fragmented, followed by end repair, adapter ligation, and polymerase chain reaction amplification to construct Illumina sequencing libraries. After quality control and quantification, paired end sequencing was performed on the Illumina platform. Genome assembly and annotation were conducted, and the resulting sequences were aligned against virulence factor gene (http://www.mgc.ac.cn/VFs/) and antibiotic resistance gene (https://card.mcmaster.ca/) databases to identify pathogenicity and resistance associated determinants.

Statistical analysis

CFU data for the bacterial-contaminated screws were quantified, and statistical significance was evaluated using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Hematological and serological data from the experimental rats were analyzed using two-way ANOVA. Statistical differences in bone volume fraction around screws and BIC percentages were assessed by one-way ANOVA. The incidence of femoral fractures observed at 28 days after model establishment were quantified as percentages, and intergroup differences were assessed by Fisher's Exact Test. TNF-α-positive and TRAP-positive areas were analyzed using one-way ANOVA. For MIC testing, MIC values from the single bacterial infection groups were used as reference standards, and MIC values from all experimental groups were normalized accordingly before intergroup comparisons conducted using two-way ANOVA. Differences in the distribution of antibiotic resistance genes and virulence associated genes identified by genomic sequencing were expressed as percentages and analyzed using Fisher's Exact Test. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (10.4.1).

Results

Establishment of a rat femoral model of PBI

Based on previously established protocol, we created a rat PBI model in which MRSA and PA co-colonize the implant site using bacteria-contaminated titanium screws [[27], [28], [29], [30]]. Briefly, sterile titanium screws were incubated with bacterial suspensions (1 × 106 CFU/mL MRSA-mCherry or PA-GFP) for 16 h, resulting in approximately 1 × 106 CFU adherent to each screw (Figs. S1 and S2). Two contaminated screws were then implanted 5 mm apart into the mid-diaphysis of the rat femur (Fig. 1A and B). The MRSA + PA and PA + MRSA groups were included to control for potential positional effects within the femur, as bacteria in the proximal site may migrate more readily toward distal regions due to gravity. Co-infection with MRSA and PA triggers an exaggerated and prolonged systemic inflammatory response in the PBI model

Hematological parameters serve as essential indicators of the host's systemic inflammatory status during infection [33,34]. As shown in Fig. 2 and Fig. S3, co-infection induced a stronger and more sustained systemic inflammatory response than monomicrobial infection. On postoperative day 7, total leukocyte counts in MRSA + PA group increased by 157.7% relative to baseline, corresponding to 1.6-fold and 1.3-fold increases compared with the PA and MRSA groups, respectively (Fig. 2B and Fig. S3B). Monocyte counts demonstrated distinct temporal patterns across groups. MRSA group exhibited an early increase on postoperative day (POD) 3, whereas both co-infection groups (MRSA + PA and PA + MRSA) demonstrated marked elevations on POD14 and POD21, indicating a prolonged pro-inflammatory state (Fig. 2C). In the MRSA + PA group, neutrophil counts increased by 681.6% from baseline on POD7, exceeding those observed in the PA and MRSA groups by 2.3-fold and 2.0-fold, respectively. Analysis of serum inflammatory mediators further supported the exaggerated inflammatory response induced by co-infection of MRSA and PA. ELISA analysis revealed significantly elevated serum levels of procalcitonin (PCT), C-reactive protein (CRP), and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) in both co-infection groups compared with the sterile group (Fig. 2E–G and Fig. S3). Notably, PCT levels were significantly increased as early as POD1 in the MRSA + PA group, preceding detectable changes in most hematological parameters. This early elevation suggests that PCT may represent a sensitive indicator of early PBI. In contrast, a significant increase in CRP was not observed until postoperative day 21, consistent with a delayed systemic inflammatory response during infection progression (Fig. 2F). TNF-α levels remained persistently elevated in the MRSA + PA group and reached statistical significance even at POD28, indicating sustained inflammatory activation in PBI (Fig. 2G). However, interleukin-6 (IL-6) levels did not differ significantly among groups at any time point (Fig. 2H), in line with previous reports describing variable or nonspecific serum interleukin 6 responses in chronic or implant associated infections [35,36].

Collectively, the pronounced and prolonged increases in hematological parameters and serum inflammatory mediators indicate that co-infection with MRSA and PA amplifies both the intensity and duration of the systemic inflammatory response in PBI.

Co-infection with MRSA and PA induces severe bone destruction and implant destabilization in the PBI model

Radiographic evaluation revealed a pathogen-dependent gradient of bone destruction in the PBI model (Fig. 3). Micro-CT imaging on POD28 confirmed pronounced osteolysis in the MRSA and co-infection groups, whereas the PA group displayed only limited peri-implant resorption. Moreover, pathological fractures and overt screw loosening were observed in the co-infection groups, indicating severe structural compromise of the femur (Fig. 3A and B). Quantitative assessment further supported these observations (Fig. 3C and D). Peri-implant bone volume fraction (BV/TV) in the MRSA and co-infection groups was significantly reduced compared with the sterile group. Methylene blue–basic fuchsin staining of femoral sections provided complementary evidence of aggravated bone pathology in co-infected animals. Compared with monomicrobial infection, femoral sections from MRSA + PA and PA + MRSA groups displayed more extensive osteolytic bone loss, prominent inflammatory cell infiltration, and pronounced reactive bone formation surrounding the implant site (Fig. 3E). At POD7, the bone–implant contact (BIC) ratio in the MRSA group and MRSA + PA group was 4.06 ± 1.26% and 4.81 ± 2.37%, respectively, substantially lower than the value measured in the sterile group (12.49 ± 3.04%) (Fig. 3F and G). By POD28, BIC in the sterile group increased to 32.29 ± 7.00%, reflecting progressive osseointegration, whereas the MRSA + PA (6.74 ± 3.76%) and PA + MRSA (4.90 ± 6.04%) groups showed minimal improvement and remained significantly lower than the sterile group (Fig. 3H and I).

To further investigate whether the amplified inflammatory response was associated with enhanced osteoclast activation, TNF-α immunohistochemistry and TRAP staining were performed on femoral sections collected at POD7 and POD28 (Fig. 4 and Fig. S4). At POD7, both co-infection groups exhibited markedly increased TNF-α-positive areas compared with the sterile group, with an overall increasing trend relative to the monomicrobial groups (Fig. S4A). This increase was accompanied by a parallel elevation in the TRAP-positive area, indicating enhanced osteoclast activation during the early stage of co-infection (Fig. 4A). At POD28, TNF-α expression remained elevated in infected bone tissue, whereas the TRAP-positive area no longer differed significantly among groups (Fig S4B and Fig S4). Unidirectional migration of MRSA toward the PA-contaminated site by postoperative day 7

After ultrasonication and vortex processing, bacteria were selectively recovered from explanted screws using MSA for MRSA and CA for PA, enabling species specific quantification (Fig. 5B–F). MRSA was detected on 100% (6/6) of PA-contaminated screws, whereas no PA colonies were recovered from MRSA-contaminated screws. Histological analyses further supported this unidirectional behavior (Fig. 5G and Fig. S5). H&E staining demonstrated dense inflammatory cell infiltration and focal sequestrum formation around bacteria-contaminated screw tracts. In the MRSA group, Gram staining revealed abundant positive cocci at the same anatomical regions, consistent with heavy MRSA colonization. Notably, Gram-positive bacteria were also observed at PA-contaminated sites in the MRSA + PA (distal defect) and PA + MRSA (proximal defect) groups. Fluorescent imaging provided additional histological evidence of MRSA invasion into PA-colonized regions. Co-colonization by MRSA (mCherry, red) and PA (GFP, green) was observed at PA-contaminated implantation sites, whereas MRSA-contaminated sites exhibited only red fluorescence, indicating the exclusive presence of MRSA. These spatial patterns paralleled CFU quantification and provide convergent evidence that MRSA establishes infection at both its original implantation site and the adjacent PA-colonized site by POD7.

Taken together, these results demonstrate a unidirectional migratory behavior in which MRSA actively invades PA-colonized environments, thereby promoting the initial establishment of mixed-species biofilms within bone.

Co-infection reshapes antibiotic susceptibility and drives transcriptional adaptation in PA

To investigate whether mixed-species infection modulates bacterial antimicrobial responsiveness, the minimum inhibitory concentrations (MIC) assays were performed on MRSA and PA isolates recovered from each experimental group. The heatmaps revealed that co-infection induced distinct and pathogen-specific shifts in susceptibility profiles (Fig. 8A and B). For MRSA, isolates from the MRSA + PA group exhibited a significant reduction in the MIC of daptomycin, reflected by a 0.71-fold change relative to the MRSA mono-infection group (Fig. 8A). This increase in susceptibility suggests that exposure to the polymicrobial microenvironment may weaken MRSA's tolerance to membrane-targeting antibiotics. Additionally, modest decreases in MIC values were observed for linezolid and vancomycin, although these changes did not reach statistical significance. For PA, co-infection with MRSA resulted in a significant 1.25-fold increase in the MIC of amikacin (Fig. 8B). By comparison, modest reductions in MIC were observed for ciprofloxacin and polymyxin E. Disk diffusion assays showed that MRSA isolates recovered from the MRSA + PA group exhibited significantly larger daptomycin inhibition zones, whereas PA isolates exhibited significantly smaller amikacin inhibition zones than their corresponding monomicrobial controls (Fig. S8). Given the pronounced antimicrobial responsiveness changes in PA, we next performed transcriptomic profiling to investigate the underlying regulatory mechanisms. Compared with isolates recovered from mono-infection, PA isolates obtained from the co-infection microenvironment exhibited extensive transcriptional remodeling, with 101 significantly differentially expressed genes, including 80 upregulated and 21 downregulated transcripts (Fig. 8C). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis revealed significant enrichment of pathways related to phenazine biosynthesis, antibiotic biosynthesis, and antibiotic metabolism, indicating coordinated metabolic adaptation in response to co-colonization with MRSA (Fig. 8D). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis further identified perturbations in ribosomal pathways and ATP binding cassette transporter systems, suggesting altered translational activity and modified transport of nutrients, metabolites, and antimicrobial compounds (Fig. 8E). Consistent with these observations, Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) demonstrated positive enrichment of ribosome related pathways, ABC transporter activity, and transmembrane antiporter systems, accompanied by marked suppression of fatty acid biosynthesis pathways (Fig. 8F–I). This transcriptional profile reflects a shift toward enhanced protein synthesis and transport capacity coupled with reduced lipid biosynthetic investment, consistent with metabolic reprogramming under competitive and inflammatory pressures within the PBI microenvironment.

Collectively, these findings indicate that co-infection induces coordinated phenotypic and transcriptional adaptation in PA, which may contribute to its altered antibiotic susceptibility, persistence, and adaptive advantages during chronic polymicrobial bone infection.

Discussion

PBI accounts for over 35% of fracture-related osteomyelitis cases, positioning it as one of the most formidable challenges in modern orthopedics [3,[41], [42], [43], [44]]. Its clinical management is frequently thwarted by complex microbial ecology, frequent involvement of multidrug-resistant pathogens, and limited effectiveness of current antimicrobial strategies [3,[41], [42], [43], [44]]. Despite its clinical relevance, progress in understanding the pathogenesis of PBI has been hindered by the lack of animal models that accurately replicate multispecies colonization within bone. In this study, we addressed this gap by establishing a robust rat model of MRSA and PA co-infection using bacteria-contaminated titanium screws, creating a reproducible system that recapitulates the spatial distribution of bacteria, persistence characteristics, and pathogenic synergy characteristic of human PBI.

Using this model, we demonstrate that polymicrobial infection elicits a markedly exacerbated inflammatory response and bone destruction compared with monomicrobial infection. At postoperative day 7, neutrophil counts were significantly elevated in the MRSA + PA group relative to either single species infection, indicating amplified systemic inflammation. Similar additive or synergistic inflammatory responses have been reported in cystic fibrosis patients co-infected with S. aureus and PA [45,46]. This was accompanied by increased local TNF-α expression and TRAP-positive area, suggesting an association between the amplified inflammatory response and early osteoclast activation. Consistent with these systemic and local changes, micro-CT and histological analyses revealed more extensive cortical erosion, impaired osseointegration, and a substantially increased incidence of pathological fractures in co-infected animals. These observations closely mirror clinical reports in which polymicrobial osteomyelitis is associated with accelerated bone destruction, mechanical instability, and higher infection recurrence rates [8,47]. Therefore, this PBI model provides a robust and physiologically relevant platform for elucidating host–pathogen and pathogen–pathogen interactions in infected bone.

Whether S. aureus and PA interact antagonistically or cooperatively within complex physiological environments remains controversial [16,32,[48], [49], [50], [51]]. Although previous studies have shown that PA can markedly inhibit S. aureus growth in vitro through the secretion of HQNO, pyocyanin, and LasA, such inhibitory effects appear substantially attenuated in vivo [19,52,53]. Consistent with this observation, our PBI model demonstrated that MRSA exhibited strong interspecies invasive capacity. By postoperative day 7, MRSA had migrated toward and successfully colonized PA-contaminated screw surfaces. This directional dissemination may be promoted by the hitchhiking motility of MRSA via interaction between wall teichoic acid of MRSA and lipopolysaccharide (LPS) of PA [54]. Moreover, PA has been reported to develop loose biofilm structures by reducing matrix cross-linking, thereby facilitating S. aureus infiltration and multispecies biofilm formation [19]. By day 28, both species achieved long-term persistence within bone tissue through the formation of mixed-species biofilms. Notably, within the mixed-species biofilms, PA occupied the central regions of the three-dimensional structure, whereas MRSA predominantly resided at the periphery. This spatial organization resembles patterns reported in previous studies in which S. aureus assumed a dominant structuring role during mixed-species biofilm formation [20,55]. This structured arrangement likely facilitates cooperative protection from antibiotics and immune clearance, enabling both pathogens to sustain long-term colonization [18,56]. Collectively, these findings indicate that MRSA and PA can overcome their in vitro antagonism and establish stable co-colonization in the PBI model through cooperative biofilm formation, ultimately enabling long-term survival within bone tissue.

Antibiotic susceptibility of both S. aureus and PA was significantly altered under co-infection conditions. Specifically, MRSA isolates from the mixed-infection group exhibited increased susceptibility to daptomycin, whereas PA showed reduced susceptibility to amikacin. Consistently, 2-Heptyl-4-hydroxyquinoline N-oxide (HQNO) secreted by PA is known to enhance the membrane fluidity of S. aureus, which in turn improves the efficacy of membrane-targeting antibiotics and fluoroquinolones [52,57,58]. Conversely, S. aureus has been reported to enhance aminoglycoside tolerance in PA, including tobramycin [52,59]. However, contrasting evidence also exists, as Orazi et al. indicated that HQNO, pyoverdine, and pyochelin secreted by PA induce a shift toward fermentative metabolism in S. aureus, thereby reducing its growth and susceptibility to antibiotics [60]. Our findings strengthen the notion that interspecies interactions modulate antibiotic responses in complex ways, posing substantial challenges for antimicrobial therapy of PBI. Whether prolonged coexistence within mixed-species biofilms further promotes horizontal transfer of resistance determinants and the emergence of high-risk multidrug-resistant pathogens remains a critical avenue for future investigation [61].

At the molecular level, transcriptomic analyses revealed extensive regulatory adaptation in PA during co-infection. Enrichment of ribosomal and ABC transporter pathways suggests enhanced translational capacity and increased efflux potential, providing a plausible explanation for the elevated amikacin MIC [62,63]. In parallel, fatty acid biosynthesis pathways were markedly downregulated, a metabolic shift known to alter membrane composition and permeability [64,65]. This remodeling likely facilitates increased susceptibility to membrane-targeting antibiotics such as polymyxin E and enhances intracellular accumulation of fluoroquinolones. These findings indicate that altered antimicrobial responsiveness during co-infection is driven by transcriptional reprogramming.

Whole-genome sequencing revealed that polymicrobial MRSA–PA co-infection does not substantially reshape the core virulence or antimicrobial resistance gene repertoires of either pathogen. Notably, the reduced prevalence of lukD in MRSA isolates recovered from the co-infection model suggests selective modulation of virulence rather than broad genomic remodeling. Leukocidins are major mediators of immune cell lysis and acute inflammation, and their diminished representation may reflect an adaptive shift toward persistence-oriented strategies in the presence of PA. Consistent with previous reports, interspecies interactions within polymicrobial biofilms can suppress aggressive toxin production in S. aureus, favoring phenotypes that enhance long-term survival under competitive conditions [66]. Together, these findings support the concept that pathogenic synergy in MRSA–PA co-infection arises primarily from functional and regulatory interactions rather than mutational events.

A notable finding in this study is the simultaneous colonization of two distinct bacterial species within the OLCN. In the MRSA + PA co-infection group, both pathogens were detected within this submicron-scale bone microarchitecture, representing, to our knowledge, the first in vivo evidence of polymicrobial occupation of the OLCN. Previous studies have shown that S. aureus can undergo asymmetric cell division to generate deformed daughter cells that are passively forced into canaliculi, evading immune clearance and enabling long-term survival [[67], [68], [69]]. Moreover, studies simulating submicron-space invasion have shown that S. aureus proliferation within nanoscale pores is not dependent on agr quorum-sensing activation, but rather on cell wall-regulated genes such as PBP4 [69]. Our findings extend this concept by demonstrating that MRSA and PA can co-colonize the OLCN, highlighting a novel niche for bacterial persistence in bone. However, the exact interaction dynamics and specific regulatory mechanisms that govern OLCN invasion during multispecies co-infection remain to be fully elucidated.

Despite establishing a reproducible rat model of polymicrobial bone infection and delineating the spatiotemporal colonization patterns of MRSA and PA, several limitations remain. First, this study focused on the clinically prevalent combination of S. aureus and PA, whereas other relevant polymicrobial combinations, such as S. aureus and E. coli, were not investigated. Nevertheless, the current femoral PBI model provides a flexible platform for studying diverse bacterial combinations and evaluating antimicrobial materials and local drug-delivery strategies. Second, prophylactic antibiotics were not administered before implantation of the contaminated screws. Although this design enabled characterization of intrinsic MRSA–PA colonization and interspecies interactions without antimicrobial interference, it does not fully reproduce standard perioperative practice. In addition, the antimicrobial susceptibility findings were obtained ex vivo; therefore, subsequent studies should determine whether these susceptibility shifts translate into differential treatment responses in vivo using clinically optimized antimicrobial regimens. Finally, the molecular and environmental determinants of mixed-species biofilm formation remain incompletely understood. Further investigation of interspecies signaling, biofilm organization, and host immune responses may provide deeper insight into the mechanisms underlying polymicrobial persistence in bone.

Conclusion

In this study, we established a rat model of MRSA-PA polymicrobial bone infection using spatially separated bacteria-contaminated titanium screws. The model revealed temporally asymmetric interspecies dissemination, persistent mixed-species colonization, amplified systemic and local inflammation, and aggravated bone destruction. MRSA-PA co-infection was also associated with pathogen-specific susceptibility shifts and extensive transcriptional adaptation. Together, these findings advance mechanistic understanding of MRSA-PA co-infection in bone and provide a physiologically relevant platform for the development and evaluation of therapeutic strategies against polymicrobial osteomyelitis.
