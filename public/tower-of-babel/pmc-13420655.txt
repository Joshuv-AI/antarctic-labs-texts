Left Ventricular Metastasis from Hepatocellular Carcinoma Presenting With Systemic Embolization and Stroke

History of presentation

A 71-year-old man with a history of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) status post right hepatectomy presented with progressive dizziness accompanied by gait instability, diplopia, vertigo, and tongue numbness. He denied chest pain, dyspnea, palpitations, or syncope. On arrival, his blood pressure was 183/105 mm Hg, heart rate 96 beats/min, respiratory rate 18 breaths/min, temperature 36.5 °C, and oxygen saturation 99% on room air. Neurologic examination revealed left-sided tongue numbness, dysdiadochokinesia, left lower extremity ataxia, and diplopia. The multifocal neurologic findings raised concern for an acute embolic cerebrovascular event and prompted urgent neuroimaging.

Past medical history

His medical history was notable for hepatitis C virus infection diagnosed 20 years earlier, which had been eradicated. Four years before presentation, he was diagnosed with HCC and underwent Y90 radioembolization followed by right hepatectomy. Additional comorbidities included hypertension, coronary artery disease, and peripheral arterial disease. He was a former smoker with a 35–pack-year history and denied alcohol use.

Differential diagnosis

Given his focal neurologic deficits, primary neurologic etiologies included acute ischemic stroke due to large-artery atherosclerosis in the setting of multiple vascular risk factors, hypertensive emergency, and intracranial metastatic disease.

Cardioembolic and malignancy-associated mechanisms were also considered, including left ventricular (LV) thrombus, nonbacterial thrombotic endocarditis, malignancy-associated hypercoagulability, paradoxical embolism through a patent foramen ovale, and intracardiac tumor.

Investigations

Magnetic resonance imaging of the brain with and without intravenous contrast demonstrated multiple small acute infarcts involving the bilateral cerebral hemispheres, left cerebellum, and right pons, consistent with a systemic embolic distribution. An enhancing subcentimeter lesion in the left frontal lobe with surrounding vasogenic edema was also identified, suspicious for metastatic disease. Computed tomography (CT) angiography of the head and neck with intravenous contrast did not demonstrate a proximal large-vessel occlusion but revealed severe atherosclerotic narrowing of the proximal right internal carotid artery.

A 12-lead electrocardiogram demonstrated normal sinus rhythm with T-wave abnormalities. High-sensitivity troponin T was mildly elevated at 231 ng/L with minimal interval change on repeat measurement (255 ng/L), without clinical or electrocardiographic evidence of acute coronary syndrome.

Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated a 2.97 × 1.79-cm hyperechoic mass within the inferoapical LV cavity with partial independent mobility, initially favored to represent thrombus (Figure 1, Video 1). The LV was normal in size with preserved systolic function (biplane ejection fraction 63%) and no regional wall motion abnormalities. A noncompressible hyperechoic density was also visualized within the inferior vena cava measuring 0.8 × 2.3 cm, raising concern for tumor thrombus. No hemodynamically significant valvular disease was identified. No intracardiac shunt was reported, although agitated shunt study was not performed. In the absence of infarction-related wall motion abnormalities and given his oncologic history, cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) imaging was obtained for further tissue characterization.

CMR with gadolinium contrast demonstrated a 29-mm intracavitary mass attached to the apical lateral wall of the LV (Figure 2, Video 2). The lesion demonstrated contrast enhancement, consistent with vascularized neoplasm rather than avascular thrombus. LV systolic function was mildly reduced on CMR (ejection fraction 46%).

Contrast-enhanced CT of the chest demonstrated a necrotic right hilar mass with obstruction of the adjacent right middle lobe bronchus, consistent with metastatic disease; imaging of the abdomen and pelvis further revealed multiple intra-abdominal lesions involving the retroperitoneum, spleen, kidneys, and a soft tissue mass within the right psoas muscle. CT-guided percutaneous biopsy of the right psoas lesion confirmed metastatic HCC.

Management

Anticoagulation was initiated when LV thrombus was suspected. After CMR imaging findings suggested metastatic tumor rather than thrombus, management was redirected toward systemic oncologic therapy. The patient was referred to hematology-oncology and initiated on combination immunotherapy with nivolumab and ipilimumab. Radiation therapy was planned for intracranial and thoracic lesions.

Outcome and follow-up

Following initiation of systemic therapy and radiation, the patient remained neurologically stable without recurrent embolic events. He continues multidisciplinary follow-up with oncology, radiation-oncology, cardiology, and neurology.

Discussion

Cardiac metastases are uncommon in the general population but occur more frequently than primary cardiac tumors1,2 (Figure 3). In HCC, cardiac involvement has been reported in fewer than 6% of cases at autopsy.3 Among malignancies, lung cancer remains the most frequent source of cardiac metastasis, followed by breast cancer, hematologic malignancies, and melanoma.1 HCC represents a less common etiology of secondary cardiac tumors.

Cardiac spread in HCC most often occurs through direct venous extension along the inferior vena cava, with the right atrium being the predominant site of involvement. Metastatic deposits typically affect the epicardium or myocardium and only rarely involve the cardiac cavities.4 Intracardiac tumor thrombus extending via the vena cava into the right heart is a recognized phenomenon in HCC and other malignancies,4 which explains why most reported cases describe right-sided cardiac involvement.5,6

Left-sided cardiac metastasis is distinctly rare. Proposed mechanisms include hematogenous dissemination via pulmonary metastases with retrograde extension through the pulmonary veins, septal invasion, or paradoxical tumor embolization through a patent foramen ovale.7 Although left atrial cases have been reported,8 LV metastasis remains exceptionally uncommon. Only isolated reports describe LV involvement. In prior cases, LV involvement has typically occurred in the context of advanced systemic disease and often manifests as myocardial wall infiltration with or without extension into the ventricular cavity rather than a discrete intracavitary mass.7,9 One report described infiltrative LV involvement in a patient with hepatitis C–related cirrhosis, and another documented myocardial infiltration in a young, noncirrhotic patient who underwent surgical resection but succumbed to widespread metastatic disease within months, underscoring that even aggressive local management does not alter the trajectory of disseminated HCC.7,9 In both instances, cardiac involvement reflected advanced systemic tumor burden and portended a poor prognosis.

In contrast, our case is notable for a prominent intracavitary LV mass presenting with embolic stroke, representing a distinct clinical phenotype. Although CMR did not demonstrate a definite tumor thrombus, the multifocal infarct pattern was most consistent with an embolic process attributable to the intracardiac metastatic lesion, whether through tumor surface fragmentation, transient thrombus formation, or malignancy-associated hypercoagulability.

This case highlights an important diagnostic pitfall. Intracavitary LV masses are most commonly attributed to thrombus, particularly in patients with systemic embolization. However, in patients with known malignancy, especially HCC, metastatic disease should remain an important consideration. Failure to recognize tumor involvement may lead to inappropriate anticoagulation while delaying oncologic therapy.

Although CMR imaging later demonstrated mildly reduced LV systolic function (ejection fraction 46%), the patient did not present with clinical features of heart failure. Instead, the presentation was dominated by embolic phenomena, which, together with the intracavitary location of the mass, further complicated the initial diagnostic assessment. Concomitant inferior vena cava involvement also contributed to the complex clinical picture. Compared with previously reported LV cases,8,9 this presentation emphasizes the potential for metastatic HCC to mimic thrombus and to manifest with systemic embolic events rather than overt heart failure symptoms.

Unlike prior reported cases in which LV involvement was predominantly infiltrative and identifiable on transthoracic echocardiography alone,7,9 the discrete intracavitary morphology in this case raised concern for thrombus and necessitated further characterization. Contrast-enhanced CMR, with its superior tissue characterization, was instrumental in distinguishing avascular thrombus from vascularized neoplasm and ultimately guided the transition from anticoagulation to systemic oncologic therapy.

Cardiac involvement in HCC reflects advanced malignancy and carries a poor prognosis, with reported median survival of approximately 102 days after diagnosis of cardiac metastasis.1 Surgical resection is generally reserved for select cases complicated by heart failure or pulmonary embolism, given limited life expectancy and the potential risks associated with cardiopulmonary bypass in disseminated disease. Overall, cardiac metastasis signifies aggressive tumor biology and adverse outcomes.10

Conclusions

This case highlights an uncommon presentation of HCC with LV metastasis manifesting as systemic embolization and stroke. In patients with known malignancy and an intracardiac mass, multimodality imaging, particularly CMR, is critical to differentiate tumor from thrombus and guide appropriate management.
