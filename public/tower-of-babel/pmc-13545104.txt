Trend of Myopia Incidence Among Adolescents (2018–2025) in Senior High School: An 8-Year Retrospective Study in Multi-Regions of China, With Projections to 2030
Authors: Siyuan Chen, Lu Yao, Yuchai Wan, Chen Zhao, Qi Qi, Xuefeng Wang, Yan Tang, Ding Ding, Zhuo Chen, Heyao Liu, Zhikang Zou, Yong Liu, Linsong Qi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo investigate myopia incidence over the past 8 years in senior high schools in China and predict its 5-year trend.MethodsThis study was a multiregional 8-year retrospective study. A total of 5440 male students aged 17–19 years (mean 18.3 ± 0.5 years) were included nationwide from September 2018 to September 2025. Each student was tested for visual acuity and cycloplegic refraction and followed up each year for 3 years. Historical data on refraction and myopia prevalence from 2018 to 2025 were analysed based on a Gaussian Process Regression model to predict the myopia incidence over the next 5 years (2026–2030).ResultsFrom 2018 to 2025, myopia incidence in the 5440 students over the past 8 years was 24.51%. Incidence reached a peak of 32.87% in 2020, with a low of 13.20% in 2024. In the −0.25 to +0.50 D baseline refraction subgroup, the 3-year myopia incidence peaked at 31.6%. The incidence in the first grade of senior high school was 1.36% (74/5440), rising to 4.49% (236/5253) and 19.74% (969/4908) in the second and third grades, respectively. Predictions show myopia incidence dropping from 19.23% in 2026 to 16.06% by 2030.ConclusionThe incidence rate of myopia among students in senior high school fluctuated slightly between 2018 and 2025, but is projected to decline over the next 5 years. Students with spherical equivalent refractions of <+0.50 D require attention regarding myopia prevention.

Introduction

Myopia has emerged as an urgent global public health challenge, characterised by its escalating prevalence and the potential to cause substantial visual impairment [1]. Projections indicate that by the year 2050, more than half of the global population will be affected by myopia [2]. The epidemiological distribution of myopia exhibits notable variability across populations with diverse ethnic and environmental backgrounds [3]. Many studies [4, 5] have demonstrated that the prevalence of myopia in Chinese children is considerably higher than that observed in European countries, exhibiting a discernible trend of earlier age of onset. In 2022, data from the Chinese Health Commission [6] revealed that the overall myopia rate among Chinese children and adolescents was 53.6%, with rates of 14.5% amongst children aged 6 years old and 36, 71.6 and 81% for primary school, junior high school and senior high school students, respectively. These figures unequivocally position China as the global epicentre of myopia, with the largest population of myopic individuals worldwide. Consequently, uncovering the underlying patterns of myopia progression, forecasting its developmental trajectory and implementing preventive and control measures through early screening and vigilant monitoring are crucial research avenues in the realm of public health.

The Chinese Ministry of Education, in collaboration with the Air Force, has initiated an experimental programme known as the Teenager Aviation School [7, 8]. This programme recruits non-myopic ninth-grade male graduates (between 14 and 16 years of age) from junior high schools across various regions of China into ordinary local public senior high schools that offer comparable educational standards, contingent upon their successful completion of physical examinations and academic assessments. Currently, there are 16 Teenager Aviation Schools located in 15 cities throughout China. Throughout their schooling, students are provided with accommodation and meals, and a daily regimen of outdoor physical training is incorporated into their schedule. In all other respects, the academic workload and pressure is on par with those of grade-matched peers in different classes within the same school. Students from the 16 Teenager Aviation Schools share a high degree of similarity in aspects such as living environment, diet and accommodation, educational standards, academic curricula and medical care provisions. Moreover, the baseline ‘hyperopia reserve’ upon enrolment can also reflect the effectiveness of myopia prevention and control measures in China in recent years.

Artificial intelligence (AI) has been integrated effectively and successfully into various medical disciplines [9–11]. For example, Tan [12] used AI to detect 99% of choroidal neovascularisation cases in myopic eyes. Tang [13] employed AI to monitor axial growth in myopic children who were wearing orthokeratology lenses, without requiring them to discontinue the use of these lenses. AI can also be utilised to predict the development of high myopia in school-aged children during their adolescence [14]. Lin [15] formulated a sophisticated algorithm with the capability to predict the onset of myopia and high myopia up to 8 years in advance, incorporating pivotal variables such as age, current myopia status and the annual rate of myopia progression. Moreover, an AI-powered predictive model was employed to estimate the anticipated prevalence of myopia among children and adolescents in China by the year 2050, utilising a comprehensive dataset encompassing nearly two decades of observational data [16]. Despite the recognised gravity of myopia as a global public health issue, there remains a dearth of scholarly investigations specifically targeting the incidence of myopia among senior high school students, as well as the predictive modelling of myopia onset trends within this group.

This study conducted a retrospective analysis of 5440 male students from 16 Teenager Aviation Schools across 15 cities nationwide, including data from the 3-year senior high schools between 2018 and 2025, summarising the incidence of myopia amongst students during their high school years. This study was also performed to forecast myopia onset trends over the next 5 years through a machine learning-based predictive model. The findings will enable early detection of myopia risks and facilitate proactive interventions to mitigate its progression among senior high school students.

Methods

Study Design and Subjects

This study was a multiregional 8-year retrospective study. Male students (N = 5440, 5440 eyes) aged 17–19 years (mean 8.3 ± 0.5 years) were included from Teenager Aviation Schools nationwide from September 2018 to September 2025. Visual acuity and cycloplegic refraction was measured in each student by trained ophthalmic professionals (optometrists or ophthalmologists) each year for 3 years. The study followed the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval for the study (2023-162-S01, dated July 13, 2023) was obtained from the Air Force Medical Centre. Written informed consent was obtained from the students and their parents or legal guardians.

Inclusion criteria [8] included: (1) spherical equivalent refraction (SER, calculated as spherical power + 1/2 cylinder power) between −0.25 dioptre (D) and +2.00 D in each eye and (2) uncorrected visual acuity better than 6/6 in each eye. Exclusion criteria comprised: (1) a history of ocular surgery, trauma or disease that affected vision; (2) receiving myopia prevention and control treatment, such as atropine eye drops, orthokeratology lenses or corneal refractive surgery. Data from the right eyes were included for statistical analysis.

Ocular Examination

All students underwent a comprehensive ocular examination, including fundoscopy, slit-lamp examination, cycloplegic refraction and distance (5 m) visual acuity (using a Landolt C chart). Before refraction, bilateral cycloplegia was performed using tropicamide-phenylephrine ophthalmic solution (compound tropicamide 0.5%/phenylephrine 0.5%) (Mydrin-P, Santen), four times at 5 min intervals. Pupil dilation was determined after the instillation of the fourth drop. Retinoscopy (Professional Combi, Keeler, keelerglobal.com/) was then performed by two experienced optometrists. The mean SER measured by the two optometrists separately was calculated for analysis [17]. SER ≤ −0.50 D indicated myopia. Incident myopia was defined as the absence of myopia at baseline with myopia development during the 3-year follow-up period.

In order to predict the incidence of myopia up to the year 2030, historical data on refraction and myopia incidence over the period from 2018 to 2025 were analysed based on a Gaussian Process Regression (GPR) model. The 3-year SER change, baseline SER, final SER and myopia incidence over 3 years were designated as the dependent variables of the model. The mean values of the predictions and 95% confidence intervals based on the standard deviation were obtained by predicting the refraction and myopia incidence in the next 5 years through GPR. Additionally, a trend analysis of myopia incidence across the baseline SER subgroups was conducted; subgroups were >−0.25 D to ≤+0.50 D, >+0.50 D to ≤+1.00 D and >+1.00 D.

Statistical Analysis

GPR was performed using Python 3.9 version (Python Software Foundation, python.org/). Other statistical analyses were performed using Prism 10.1.2 software (GraphPad Software, graphpad-prism.cn/). The Kolmogorov‒Smirnov test and the normal distribution histogram method were used to assess the normality of the distributions of the continuous variables. All measurement data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Comparisons between groups were made using analysis of variance (one-way ANOVA). The Bonferroni method was conducted for pairwise comparisons [18]. The significance level was set at p < 0.05.

Discussion

This study investigated the incidence of myopia amongst students enrolled in Teenager Aviation Schools across China, covering cohorts enrolling from 2018 to 2025. It examined the baseline SER, final SER and the SER changes observed over the course of 3 years in senior high school. Additionally, it compared the overall incidence of myopia among teenagers, the incidence from 2018 to 2025 and examined the occurrence of myopia in each academic year. Most students had a baseline SER within −0.25 to ≤+0.50 D, especially within −0.25 to 0 D, the subgroup that showed the highest incidence of myopia. In contrast, the smallest subgroup had a baseline SER > +1.00 D and showed the lowest myopia incidence. Furthermore, there were slight fluctuations in both SER changes and the incidence of myopia across academic grades. However, based on the predictive results, the incidence of myopia is projected to exhibit a gradual downward trend over the next 5 years.

For the students who entered in 2018 and 2019, in the early stages of the school’s establishment, this represented a period when the concepts of myopia prevention and control were being developed amongst students, parents and teachers. The nascent stage, marked by a lack of prior experience, resulted in an escalating incidence of myopia within these initial cohorts. The students entering in 2020 exhibited the highest prevalence of myopia and the largest change in SER, likely associated with the COVID-19 pandemic. Wang [19] observed that home isolation during this period was closely associated with the development of myopia. Myopia is a multifactorial disease, and its development is influenced by risk factors such as prolonged close work and insufficient light exposure [20]. Indeed, a significant correlation was demonstrated between the reduction in outdoor time during this period and the increased incidence of myopia among school-age children and adolescents [21]. During the pandemic, students experienced remote home-learning, transitioning to online courses from January to June 2020. This shift precipitated a marked increase in indoor activity time and digital screen time, concurrent with a decrease or complete cessation of outdoor activities [22]. Additional factors, such as an imbalanced diet and insufficient sleep during this home-based learning period, may have collectively accelerated the progression of myopia [23]. In a previous survey of nine provinces in China, myopia incidence increased by 11.7% during COVID-19 [24], which was broadly consistent with the results of the present study. Although the students entering in 2021 were also impacted by the pandemic, the schools gradually reinstated residential measures. During this period, the students, along with their parents and teachers, explored methods for preventing and controlling myopia amidst the epidemic. As a favourable outcome, the incidence of myopia decreased when compared with the 2020 cohort. Among students entering in 2024, there was a notable rise in the proportion having an SER > +1.00 D, which has led to a more pronounced reduction in the incidence of myopia within that cohort.

The International Myopia Institute [25] defined ‘pre-myopia’ as an SER > −0.50 D and ≤ +0.75 D in children. However, previous research has demonstrated that during the 3-year senior high school period, the incidence of myopia remained relatively high for adolescents with SER < +1.00 D [24, 26]. Therefore, this study has extended the application of ‘pre-myopia’ to encompass senior high school adolescents. Baseline SER has also been identified as having an independent influence on myopia, and a lower baseline SER was associated with increased risk of myopia incidence [27], which is consistent with the results of the present study. Specifically, the incidence of myopia in the group with an SER ranging from −0.25 to ≤+0.50 D was significantly higher than in the other two groups. This further underscores the importance of monitoring refractive changes continuously in pre-myopic adolescents. Furthermore, for non-myopic senior high school students, despite the general consensus that axial length tends to stabilise during this period [24, 28], the third year of senior high school has emerged as the period with the highest risk for myopia incidence. This may be linked to the intensive near-work students undertake prior to the college entrance examination. Educational pressure stands out as one of the key environmental factors influencing the onset and progression of myopia. Near work is believed to be associated with myopia onset and development, with this correlation becoming more pronounced under conditions of prolonged near-work time and shorter working distances [29]. This aligns with the findings of the current study [30], which observed a higher incidence of myopia among students in their third year of high school.

The predictions indicated a downward trend in myopia incidence rate over the ensuing 5-year period. This decline may be attributed, in part, to a slight increase in baseline SER of students entering their first year of senior high school [31]. Additionally, the increased awareness and prioritisation of myopia prevention and control efforts across the country, driven by societal, educational and familial stakeholders, may also play a role in contributing to this observed trend. Furthermore, this study found that students with SER > −0.25 D and ≤+0.50 D still exhibited a relatively high incidence of myopia over the subsequent 5 years, indicating that this group is in the ‘pre-myopia’ stage and should represent a key target population for myopia prevention and control efforts [32].

The study’s strength lies in its sample of senior high school students across China, a demographic that has received less attention in previous research. Nevertheless, the limitations of this study were as follows: Firstly, only male subjects were included. However, this excludes the confounding factor of gender, which may allow a more specific description of the distribution and refractive errors among Chinese non-myopic senior high school students. The scope of research subjects should be expanded in the future to include both genders. Second, the subjects included in the study were under semi-closed management, which is stricter than for ordinary high school students in terms of myopia prevention and control management. It also means that these results may not fully reflect the incidence patterns in more typical school settings, although the learning pressure experienced by the study subjects, such as the intensity of near-work time, was comparable to that of other high school students. Notably, students under semi-closed management demonstrated a lower loss-to-follow-up rate. Adherence is a critical determinant of efficacy for any myopia control strategy [33]. Thirdly, important confounders such as time spent outdoors, doing near-work or viewing screens, as well as parental myopia, socioeconomic status and educational intensity were not measured. These aspects should be addressed in future research.

In summary, the myopia incidence rate among students fluctuated from 2018 to 2025. Over the next 5 years, the incidence of myopia among students in Teenager Aviation Schools is expected to decline, with a slight increase in the hyperopia reserve levels and a slowdown in myopic shift. However, students with SER < +0.50 D still constitute the primary target population, and Grade 12 represents the critical phase for myopia prevention during the senior high school period.
