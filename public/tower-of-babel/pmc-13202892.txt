Investigation of the satisfaction levels of patients visiting the emergency department
Authors: Zeynep Abbasoğlu, Ahmet Butun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionEmergency Departments (EDs) constitute a critical component of healthcare systems by providing rapid and lifesaving care for urgent conditions. Beyond clinical effectiveness, patient satisfaction has emerged as a key indicator of service quality and healthcare system performance. In ED settings, satisfaction is influenced by multiple factors, including communication quality, perceived waiting times, staff attitudes, and environmental conditions. Given the increasing patient volume and workload in EDs, particularly in Turkey, systematically evaluating patient satisfaction is essential for identifying areas for improvement and strengthening patient-centred care. This study aimed to determine the satisfaction levels of patients visiting the ED and to examine the factors associated with satisfaction across demographic characteristics and ED experiences.MethodsThis descriptive cross-sectional study was conducted in the ED of a tertiary hospital with a 700-bed capacity located in southeastern Turkey. The study sample consisted of 559 patients who visited the ED between September 1, 2024 and December 30, 2024. Data were collected using a socio-demographic and ED experience questionnaire, and the Brief Emergency Department Patient Satisfaction Scale, which comprises 18 items and five sub-dimensions. Descriptive statistics, independent samples t-tests, and one-way ANOVA were performed using SPSS 22.0.ResultsThe overall mean ED patient satisfaction score was 60.92 ± 15.62, indicating a moderate-to-high level of satisfaction. Among sub-dimensions, the highest satisfaction scores were observed for nurse satisfaction and doctor satisfaction, while satisfaction with the physical environment and reception staff was at a moderate level. Satisfaction scores tended to decrease with higher education levels and with increased frequency of ED visits. Patients visiting the ED during daytime hours and those perceiving their condition as very urgent reported higher satisfaction levels. Interactions with both nurses and doctors were strongly associated with higher overall satisfaction.ConclusionThe results indicate that patient satisfaction in the ED is shaped by a complex interaction of demographic factors, healthcare utilisation patterns, and care-related experiences. Interactions with nurses and doctors play a central role in determining overall satisfaction, highlighting the importance of team-based and patient-centred care. Introduction

Emergency Departments (ED) are one of the most critical components of the healthcare system, standing out as units that respond to urgent health problems, accidents, and life-threatening situations. The primary purpose of the ED is to increase patients’ chances of survival and minimize potential complications by providing rapid and effective healthcare services [1]. However, the quality of services provided in the ED is not limited to medical interventions alone, but is also directly related to meeting patients’ expectations and ensuring overall satisfaction [2]. Moreover, EDs are among the most critical units of the healthcare system, as patients’ experiences directly affect trust in healthcare services and the overall efficiency of the health system [3].

The quality of services provided in the ED should be evaluated not only by medical success but also by the level of patient-perceived satisfaction. Patient satisfaction is an independent and meaningful indicator in measuring the quality of healthcare services and plays a critical role in determining the effectiveness of service delivery [4]. Especially in EDs, communication quality, the information process, and waiting times are among the key parameters that directly affect patient satisfaction [5]. The effectiveness of information sharing may have a stronger impact than waiting times; insufficient information may increases the perception of dissatisfaction [4]. Moreover, differences have been observed between patients’ perceived waiting time and actual waiting time, and it has been determined that satisfaction perception is more closely related to perceived time [6]. Patient satisfaction in the ED is also related to the attitude and level of empathy of the staff providing the service; the staff’s attitude and level of empathy can be decisive [7]. A multicentre study conducted by Wang and colleagues (2024) revealed that demographic characteristics, the quality of care provided, and the components of humanised care are associated with patient satisfaction in the ED [8]. Patient satisfaction is significantly affected by the quality of healthcare perceived by patients, and this effect plays a mediating role in the trust that patients have in the healthcare system [2].

Patient satisfaction is a critical indicator in the evaluation of healthcare services and is related not only to medical outcomes but also to patients’ perceptions of the service process and the extent to which their expectations are met [9]. High level of workload, the increased number of patients, and time pressure are among the significant factors affecting patient satisfaction in the ED [10]. The increasing patient volume in the ED in Turkey and around the world puts significant pressure on the efficiency and effectiveness of service delivery, leading to serious debates in healthcare systems [11, 12]. Patient volume and limited resources in the ED can negatively affect the effectiveness of the services provided and patient satisfaction [13]. Long waiting times, lack of communication with healthcare staff, and insufficient staff numbers can result in patients’ dissatisfaction [14]. The existing literature shows that socio-demographic characteristics also have a significant impact on patient satisfaction [3]. In particular, factors such as gender, age, and availability of social security directly influence patients’ expectation levels and perceptions of satisfaction [1].

Patient satisfaction is a multidimensional concept that not only reflects how patients evaluate the healthcare they receive but also provides a general perception of the quality of the services offered [15]. Measuring this concept in the ED is more complex compared to other clinical units due to factors such as high workload, time pressure, and communication issues [16]. The data regarding patients satisfaction could provide healthcare providers with an opportunity to improve clinical practices and strengthen a patient-centered care approach [17]. Additionally, identifying factors influencing patient satisfaction could guides strategic improvements in weak areas while reinforcing the strengths of healthcare services [18]. High patient satisfaction leads to better adherence to treatment processes by patients, which has been observed to both reduce healthcare costs and increase overall clinical success [19]. Regularly measuring satisfaction in the ED is a critical both for continuously monitoring service quality and for ensuring the sustainability of healthcare systems [9]. Despite the growing body of literature on ED patient satisfaction, there remains a need for context-specific studies that examine how demographic characteristics, healthcare utilization behaviours, and ED experience-related factors interact to influence satisfaction. In particular, there is limited evidence from Turkey that simultaneously considers these multiple dimensions. Therefore, this study aimed to determine the satisfaction levels of patients visiting the ED and to examine how patient satisfaction varies according to socio-demographic characteristics and ED–related experiences.

Methods

Study design

This study is a descriptive cross-sectional study.

Settings, participants, and sampling

The setting of the study was ED at a tertiary hospital with a 700-bed capacity and located in the southeast of Turkey. Participants were patients who visited the ED at a tertiary hospital. The minimum sample size was determined as 385 using the ‘Sample Size Calculator’ website, with a 50% incidence rate, 5% margin of error, and 95% confidence interval. In this study, a total of 559 patients presenting to the ED were participated. A convenience sampling was used to recruit participants.

Data collection

Data were collected between September 1, 2024 and December 30, 2024. Data collection was conducted by the researcher (Z.A.), who was present in the ED during designated data collection periods. During these periods, the researcher approached eligible patients individually, provided brief information about the study, and asked whether they would be willing to participate. The questionnaire was administered to those who agreed to participate voluntarily. Completion of the questionnaire took approximately 10 min. The data were collected by using questionnaire technique with a data collection form consisting of 2 parts.

Data collections forms

Socio-demographic and emergency department experience and evaluation information form

This form was developed by the researchers (Z.A. and A.B) (Suplementary file). The form consists of 15 questions including participants’ age, gender, education level, availability of social security (refers to whether individuals are covered by the national Social Security Institution or equivalent public health insurance schemes, which provide access to publicly funded healthcare services), the time period they visited the ED, how they arrived to the ED, the condition that led them to go to the ED, whether they took any action for their health problem before going to the ED, how urgent they thought their health situation was, whether they visited their General Practitioner (GP) regarding their complaint before visiting the ED, whether they plan to visit the GP in the future for the same problem, how many times they have visited the ED in the past year, the profession of the healthcare staff they received service from, and their satisfaction with the ED regarding the doctor, nurse, other staff, and overall satisfaction.

Brief Emergency Department Patient Satisfaction Scale (BEPSS)

The Brief Emergency Department Patient Satisfaction Scale was developed by Mohammed Atari and Maryam Atari in 2015 to assess the satisfaction of patients visiting the ED [3]. The Turkish validity and reliability study of this scale was conducted in 2022 by Konateke and Yılmaz [1]. The scale is prepared in a four-point Likert format and consists of a total of 18 items and five sub-dimensions.

The sub-dimensions of the scale are as follows:

Doctor satisfaction: Measures patients’ satisfaction with their interactions with doctors, the level of medical information provided, and the approach of doctors.

Doctor satisfaction: Measures patients’ satisfaction with their interactions with doctors, the level of medical information provided, and the approach of doctors.

Nurse satisfaction: Includes items assessing nurses’ attentiveness, communication, and quality of care.

Nurse satisfaction: Includes items assessing nurses’ attentiveness, communication, and quality of care.

Waiting time satisfaction: Determines patients’ perceptions of waiting times in the ED and their satisfaction with how this process is managed.

Waiting time satisfaction: Determines patients’ perceptions of waiting times in the ED and their satisfaction with how this process is managed.

Physical environment satisfaction: Includes patients’ opinions on the hygiene, comfort, and overall environment of the ED.

Physical environment satisfaction: Includes patients’ opinions on the hygiene, comfort, and overall environment of the ED.

Overall satisfaction: Evaluates the overall satisfaction with ED experiences.

Overall satisfaction: Evaluates the overall satisfaction with ED experiences.

The statements on the scale were rated on a four-point Likert scale ranging from “1 = completely disagree” to “4 = completely agree.” Sub-dimension scores were calculated as continuous variables, with higher scores indicating higher levels of patient satisfaction. In the present study, all statistical analyses were conducted using these continuous scores rather than categorical classifications.

The total Cronbach’s Alpha value of the emergency service patient satisfaction scale was 0.940, indicating that the scale has high internal consistency. The alpha values for the sub-dimensions were as follows: doctor satisfaction 0.923, nurse satisfaction 0.880, waiting time satisfaction 0.885, physical environment satisfaction 0.891, and overall satisfaction 0.915. The alpha values of all sub-dimensions were above 0.80, indicating that the scale is reliable.

Data analysis

The data obtained in the study were analysed using the SPSS 22.0 statistical program. Frequency and percentage analyses were used to determine the descriptive characteristics of the participants, while mean and standard deviation statistics were used to examine the scale. Skewness and kurtosis values were examined to determine whether the research variables showed a normal distribution. In the relevant literature, results regarding the skewness and kurtosis values of variables are considered normally distributed if they are between + 1.5 and − 1.5 [20] or between + 2.0 and − 2.0 [21]. It was determined that the variables showed a normal distribution (Table 1). Parametric methods were used in the analysis of the data.

In terms of arrival mode, no statistically significant differences were found for total satisfaction, nurse satisfaction, reception staff satisfaction, doctor satisfaction, or overall satisfaction (all p > 0.05). Only physical environment satisfaction differed significantly according to arrival mode (p = 0.015), with participants arriving on foot reporting higher scores than those arriving by public transportation.

Undertaking treatment before coming to the ED was associated only with physical environment satisfaction (p = 0.045). No significant differences were found for total satisfaction or other sub-dimensions (all p > 0.05). In addition, self-perceived urgency of health condition was significantly associated with nurse satisfaction (p = 0.003) and physical environment satisfaction (p = 0.002). No statistically significant differences were observed for total satisfaction, reception staff satisfaction, doctor satisfaction, or overall satisfaction (all p > 0.05).

Visiting a GP before coming to the ED was associated only with physical environment satisfaction (p = 0.049). No significant differences were found for total satisfaction or other sub-dimensions. Similarly, willingness to visit a GP in the future was associated only with physical environment satisfaction (p = 0.004). Moreover, the number of ED visits in the past year was significantly associated with nurse satisfaction (p = 0.001), reception staff satisfaction (p = 0.002), and physical environment satisfaction (p = 0.017). However, no statistically significant differences were found for total satisfaction, doctor satisfaction, or overall satisfaction.

Participants’ satisfaction scores according to their experiences with ED staff are presented in Table 7. Significant differences were observed across all satisfaction dimensions according to participants’ reported satisfaction with overall ED services, doctors, nurses, and other staff (all p < 0.001). In all cases, participants who reported being satisfied had significantly higher total satisfaction and sub-dimension scores than those who were unsure or dissatisfied.

Regarding the type of healthcare staff providing care, statistically significant differences were found for total ED patient satisfaction (p = 0.001), nurse satisfaction (p < 0.001), reception staff satisfaction (p = 0.001), physical environment satisfaction (p = 0.001), doctor satisfaction (p = 0.022), and overall satisfaction (p = 0.009). Participants who received care from both nurses and doctors generally reported higher satisfaction scores than those who received care only from doctors.

Discussion

The study results have shown that many different factors such as individuals’ demographic characteristics, availability of social security, how they arrive at the ED, and the quality of the service received, affect participants’ satisfaction. In the present study, 69.1% of participants (n = 386) reported receiving care from both nurses and doctors (Table 7), suggesting that patient experiences in the ED were commonly shaped by interactions with more than one professional group. This finding highlights the importance of coordinated, team-based care and effective communication among healthcare professionals in improving patient satisfaction. A study conducted by Tanniru & Khuntia [22] demonstrated that nursing care and communication quality affect patient satisfaction. Communication quality, empathy, attentiveness, respect, and the ability to provide clear information may all contribute to patients’ overall evaluation of care. Existing evidence indicates that positive provider–patient communication is associated not only with greater patient satisfaction but also with improved treatment adherence, trust in healthcare professionals, and patient safety. Empathetic communication and patient-centred interactions may reduce anxiety, improve patients’ understanding of treatment recommendations, and strengthen their willingness to follow medical advice [23, 24]. In addition, nursing practices and triage processes in the ED affect patient satisfaction [25]. In line with the results of this current study, the existing literature highlighted that strengthening intra-team communication improves the patient experience and stated that interdisciplinary collaboration plays a key role in increasing satisfaction [26].

According to Tables 7 and 80.7% of participants were satisfied with doctors, 79.8% were satisfied with nurses, 75.7% were satisfied with other staff, and 77.3% reported overall satisfaction with ED services. These percentages correspond to the proportion of participants classified in the “satisfied” category for each dimension. The close alignment between satisfaction with healthcare staff and overall satisfaction suggests that the quality of patient–provider interaction may play an important role in shaping the patient experience. The existing literature emphasized that there is a strong connection between nurses’ workload and patient satisfaction, noting that a high workload weakens communication quality and continuity of care, thus lowering satisfaction levels [27]. These results support the high levels of satisfaction observed in this study and indicate that investments in these areas significantly improve the patient experience [28]. Similarly, patient satisfaction increased significantly when nurses employed empathetic approaches and provided explanatory communication in their interactions with patients [29]. This result demonstrates that healthcare staff shape the patient experience not only through their technical skills but also through their communicative qualities.

The findings presented in Table 7 indicate that participants who were satisfied with nurses had significantly higher overall ED patient satisfaction scores than those who were unsure or dissatisfied with nursing care (p < 0.001). Participants who reported satisfaction with nurses had a mean total satisfaction score of 64.53 ± 13.45, compared with 49.95 ± 14.05 among those who were unsure and 37.70 ± 16.17 among those who were dissatisfied. This finding suggests that satisfaction with nursing care is closely associated with patients’ overall evaluation of their ED experience. In line with the results of this study, the existing literature reports that increased workload and burnout among ED nurses have negative effects on patient satisfaction and perceived quality of care [30]. Therefore, regulations aimed at improving the working conditions of nurses in the ED are an important strategy for increasing patient satisfaction. Similarly, another study noted that patients were generally satisfied with nursing services; this satisfaction was particularly influenced by elements such as nurses’ attention to patient privacy, efforts to relieve pain, timely administration of medications, and easy accessibility to nurses [31].

The education level of individuals participating in the study affects how they perceive and evaluate healthcare services. In this study, education level was significantly associated with nurse satisfaction, reception staff satisfaction, and overall satisfaction, whereas no statistically significant difference was observed for total ED patient satisfaction scores. Participants with a bachelor’s degree or higher generally reported lower scores in these sub-dimensions compared with those with lower educational levels. Similarly, the existing literature showed that patients with lower education levels were more likely to report patient satisfaction compared to those with higher education [32]. This can be explained by the fact that individuals with lower education levels have limited knowledge and expectations regarding the healthcare system [9]. Additionally, the existing literature also indicates that individuals with lower education levels tend to have higher trust in healthcare staff and avoid critically evaluating negative aspects [33]. Individuals with a higher level of education tend to focus more on the technical aspects of the service and are able to evaluate the difficulties they encounter more critically [18]. In addition, since individuals with higher education levels have greater awareness of the healthcare system, their evaluations of every aspect of the service can be more detailed and demanding [34]. Consequently, the decrease in satisfaction scores with increasing education levels can be interpreted as being associated with higher expectations.

This study showed that more frequent ED visits were associated with lower nurse satisfaction, reception staff satisfaction, and physical environment satisfaction scores, although no statistically significant difference was observed for total ED patient satisfaction. Individuals who visit the ED more frequently may develop more critical expectations regarding communication, waiting processes, and environmental conditions based on their previous experiences. Also, this could be a result of negative experiences with healthcare services in the past or that chronic health issues, which require ongoing care, could negatively impact their satisfaction expectations. In line with the results of this study, the existing literature showed that patients who frequently visited the ED had significantly lower satisfaction levels, which were associated with long waiting times and inadequate information processes [35]. Additionally, another study reported that problems in communication with healthcare staff negatively affected the satisfaction levels of frequent visitors [14]. These results highlight the importance of developing special improvement strategies in healthcare services aimed at frequent ED users.

Of participants,76.7% were rated their health condition as “urgent” and “very urgent” in total. This result reflects the often-highlighted discrepancy in the literature between “perceived urgency” and professional clinical assessment. A large portion of patients identify their condition as “urgent,” but this assessment does not always align with the clinical reality [36]. In a multicentre study conducted in Germany by Schäfer and colleagues (2021), it was observed that the level of urgency perceived by patients varied according to their attachment to the GP and the severity of somatic symptoms; however, health literacy did not have a significant effect on this perception [37].

In addition, participants who stated that they would return to the ED if they experienced similar symptoms in the future reported significantly higher physical environment satisfaction scores than those who responded “no” or “maybe.” This finding suggests that previous experiences in the ED, particularly perceptions of the physical environment, may influence future preferences for seeking emergency care.

In this study, only 25.9% of participants reported visiting a GP before attending the ED. This finding may indicate limited use of primary healthcare services prior to ED attendance. Previous research conducted in Turkey has similarly shown that many patients prefer to use the ED directly rather than consulting primary care services first [38]. In a study conducted by Butun [38], more than half of the participants (61.8%) reported that they did not visit their GP before visiting the ED. In the same study, 10.8% of the participants reported that they had not visited a GP at all in the past year [38]. Furthermore, the limited operating hours and appointment procedures of family health centres lead patients to visit the ED as a faster and more easily accessible option [38].

Implication for practice and recommendations

This study demonstrates that evaluating patient satisfaction solely on the basis of clinical outcomes is insufficient and highlights the necessity of a multidimensional approach that incorporates demographic characteristics, and healthcare utilization experiences. The results indicate that patient satisfaction in the ED is strongly shaped by interactions with nurses and doctors, underscoring the pivotal role of communication skills in determining the quality of healthcare services. Furthermore, the findings suggest that policies and practices aimed at increasing the effective use of primary healthcare services may help reduce unnecessary reliance on emergency care services. In this context, addressing demographic differences, and strengthening service processes and information mechanisms are needed for enhancing patient satisfaction.

From a practical perspective, increasing health literacy at the population level and strengthening awareness of what constitutes an emergency condition emerge as key priorities. Transparent and comprehensible information about the triage process can reduce dissatisfaction related to waiting times and foster more effective communication between patients and healthcare staff. In addition, strengthening the primary care system, particularly GP practices in terms of infrastructure, accessibility, and quality of care may encourage individuals to use primary care as their first point of contact. For patients with frequent ED visits, the development of individualized health management strategies and integrated support models may improve satisfaction and reduce reliance on emergency services. Supporting multidisciplinary teamwork in the ED and expanding continuous in-service training for healthcare staff, particularly in communication skills and patient-centred care, are also recommended. Additionally, providing clear and accessible information tailored to patients with different educational backgrounds may contribute to more equitable healthcare experiences.

Strengths and limitations

One of the strengths of this study is the sample size and heterogeneity of its dataset. Data were collected from 559 participants with diverse sociodemographic characteristics. This diversity enhances the reliability of the results and provides a more robust basis for interpreting the results.

Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. The study was conducted in a single hospital within a specific time frame, which may limit the generalizability of the results to the ED across Turkey. In addition, the inclusion of only patients who were able to communicate may have excluded critically ill individuals, potentially leading to the underrepresentation of certain patient profiles. The use of self-reported questionnaires may also have introduced response bias, and the dynamic nature of patient satisfaction, varying over time, events, and personal experiences, should be considered when interpreting the results.

Conclusion

This study indicate that patient satisfaction in the ED is not only limited to individuals’ immediate care experiences but also is shaped through a complex interaction of service organization, communication quality, interpersonal care, and broader individual factors such as demographic characteristics, and perceived health. Overall satisfaction levels were found to be moderate to high, with interactions with nurses and doctors emerging as key determinants of patient satisfaction. Higher satisfaction among individuals who received care from both nurses and doctors highlights the positive contribution of team-based care to the patient experience, underscoring the critical role of nurses not only in clinical treatment but also in information provision and psychosocial support.

Another notable result of this study is the low rate of visits to GP, indicating underutilization of primary healthcare services and contributing to unnecessary overcrowding in the ED. Lower environmental satisfaction among individuals who did not consult a GP suggests a potential association between trust in primary care services and perceptions of satisfaction within emergency settings. Conversely, higher environmental satisfaction reported by individuals who intend to visit a GP in the future demonstrates that healthcare experiences play a significant role in shaping patients’ subsequent healthcare preferences. Collectively, these results emphasise the importance of optimizing ED workflows, strengthening effective communication, and implementing strategies that encourage appropriate use of primary care services to enhance patient satisfaction and reinforce trust in the healthcare system.
