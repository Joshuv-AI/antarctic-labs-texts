Deep-learning-based artificial intelligence approaches for grading and progression prediction of clear cell renal cell carcinoma
Authors: Jing Zhou, BoJin Su, Chang Zhao, QunXiong Huang, Na Cheng, JinMing Di, JianXiong Gu, Hailing Liu, Qiong Liang, DaYang Hui, ZiJin Weng, LuYing Tang, RiHong Yang, LanQing Han, JianNing Chen, Yong Ren, ChunKui Shao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryThe grading classification of clear cell renal cell carcinoma (CCRCC) is a crucial prognostic factor. However, manual observation can lead to subjective and inconsistent diagnoses. In this study, we aimed to develop a deep-learning-based grading system for CCRCC using whole-slide image (WSI) and to predict disease progression. A total of 305 CCRCC cases were used to develop the renal cell carcinoma artificial intelligence diagnosis (RCCAID) model for grading (188 training, 47 verification, and 70 testing cases) and the renal cell carcinoma artificial intelligence prognostic (RCCAIP) progression prediction model (288 cases). Progression was defined as the presence of distant and/or lymph node metastasis. Xception, ResNet50, and Inception V3 were employed. Model performance was assessed via confusion matrix, receiver operator characteristic curve (ROC) curve, and classification map. RCCAID achieved area under the curve (AUC) values of 0.987 (internal), 0.996 (in-house), and 0.967 (external). The quadratic weighted Cohen’s kappa was 0.864, comparable to senior pathologists (0.871). RCCAIP achieved an AUC of 0.94 for progression prediction. Higher grades correlated with worse prognosis. RCCAID can accurately grade CCRCC in whole-slide images, and RCCAIP is effective in progression prediction, even for low-grade tumors.Subject areas: Health sciences

Introduction

Clear cell renal cell carcinoma (CCRCC) is the most common subtype of renal cell carcinoma (RCC), accounting for approximately 70% of all renal tumors.1 Tumor grade has widely been accepted as a strong prognostic factor for CCRCC.2 The widely accepted grading system is the World Health Organization/International Society of Urological Pathology (WHO/ISUP) grading criteria. This classification defines grade 1–3 based on the prominence of the nucleolus under a specific objective lens, while grade 4 is based on the presence of cellular pleomorphism, including atypical tumor giant cells, and/or sarcomatoid/rhabdoid differentiation.3 Validation studies have shown that the WHO/ISUP grading system correlates with the outcome of CCRCC.4,5,6 However, there is a lack of consensus among pathologists regarding the interpretation of certain features, such as the distinction between grade 1 and grade 2 tumors, pleomorphic cells, and tumor giant cells. These differences in opinion often result in discrepancies and inconsistencies in diagnosis. Furthermore, tumor heterogeneity often leads to the presence of different grades of tumor cells within the same tumor area. Usually, CCRCC does not show a uniform grade throughout the entire tumor; the WHO/ISUP grading is based on the single high-power field that has the highest grade within the sampled component of the tumor.7 This approach requires pathologists to dedicate more time to comprehensively observe all tumor cells under the microscope. Despite their best efforts, there is still a possibility that focal high-grade areas may be missed due to the limitations and inherent subjectivity of the human eye. How to avoid the diagnosis bias between pathologists and reduce the workload of pathologists are problems needed to be solved.

Given that the tumor grade is closely associated with patient outcomes, with the highest grade having the greatest impact, it is imperative to find ways to prevent the omission of focal high-grade areas, posing a challenging issue for pathologists. With the rapid development of image scanning and visualization techniques, whole-slide image (WSI) is becoming a powerful pathological diagnostic method. It also opens the gate for artificial intelligence (AI) to improve pathologist’s diagnostic efficiency and consistency. Among AI technologies, deep learning (DL) algorithms have shown great potential in pathological image analysis.8 Target segmentation and automatic quantification can be achieved by extracting the coordinates, colors, and gray scales of image pixels through DL. More importantly, microscopic information and sub-cellular features that cannot be distinguished by human eyes can also be extracted, avoiding different observers’ diagnosis bias. A plenty of studies had made significant progress in AI-assisted pathological diagnosis in breast cancer,9 gastric cancer,10 prostate cancer,11 and intestinal cancer,12 which focused on benign and malignant diagnosis, disease grading, staining analysis, and early tumor screening. DL can also help quantify of the proportion of eosinophils in inflammatory cells in nasal polyps, providing accurate morphological basis for clinical treatment.13 DL model can enhance the diagnosis rate of early hepatocellular carcinoma and risk stratification of hepatocellular nodular lesion patients for its advantages in patch-level recognition, even for scarce biopsy specimens.14 With the aid of AI technology, we believe we can achieve high efficiency and accuracy grading interpretation and not miss any focal area of high grade of CCRCC.

In this study, we developed a DL CCRCC grading model which can automatically distinguish and quantify different grades. What is more, we constructed a prognostic model based on the automated grading system. Our specific aims were, firstly, establishing an AI diagnosis model that can provide the proportion of each grade; secondly, developing a prognosis model that can predict the progression status of patients with CCRCC. With these two DL models, pathologists can make a more efficient and accurate grading diagnosis, further providing prognostic information for clinical surgeons and CCRCC patients.

Results

Study participants

In this study, we collected a total of 305 formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded pathological slides from 305 CCRCC patients’ pathological H&E-stained slides from the Third Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University (SYSU) between June 2003 and September 2019, which included 235 cases from Headquarter, 52 cases from LingNan Hospital, and 18 cases from YueDong Hospital. In addition, we collected 63 CCRCC cases from the Sixth Affiliated Hospital of SYSU from 2019 to 2025. Each hospital has independent tissue processing and staining procedures. For the development of the DL diagnostic model, we randomly selected 188 cases of the scanned slides from the Headquarter cohort for the initial tagging stage. The remaining 47 cases of the Headquarter cohort were reserved for the verification stage. In the final inspection stage, we evaluated the model’s performance using a separate cohort of 70 cases, which included 52 cases from LingNan Hospital and 18 cases from YueDong Hospital. Additionally, 63 CCRCC cases from the Sixth Affiliated Hospital of SYSU, spanning from 2019 to 2025, were selected as an independent external dataset for model validation.

To construct the prognosis model, we initially screened 148 cases out of the 305 cases with complete clinical information. However, due to the limited number of cases, we further selected 140 cases of H&E-stained WSIs downloaded from the Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) portal (project TCGA-KIRP) that had corresponding follow-up data. For model construction, we randomly selected 80% of these cases, while the remaining 20% were reserved for effectiveness evaluation. Performance evaluation of DL grading diagnostic model

The effectiveness of each model was evaluated in both the internal and external validation set. The area under the curve (AUC) values in the internal validation dataset for Resnet50, InceptionV3, and Xception models at 128 × 128 pixels were 0.969, 0.962, and 0.970, respectively. When the image was compressed to 256 × 256 pixels, the AUC values for Resnet50, InceptionV3, and Xception models were slightly higher (0.988, 0.988, and 0.987, respectively, Figures 1A and 1B). In the external dataset, the AUC values for Resnet50, InceptionV3, and Xception models at 128 × 128 pixels were 0.981, 0.977, and 0.976, respectively. At 256 × 256 pixels, the AUC values for Resnet50, InceptionV3, and Xception models were also slightly higher (0.994, 0.983, and 0.996, respectively, Figures 1C and 1D). The best DL grading diagnostic model was Xception at 256 × 256 pixels, achieving the highest AUC value of 0.996 in the external validation set, along with the best accuracy (ACC), sensitivity (SENS), and F1 score. All three models showed higher AUC values when the patches were compressed into 256 × 256 pixels compared to 128 × 128 pixels, indicating that if the graphics processing unit meets the requirement, the highest patch model should be chosen.

RCCAID serves as an effective grading classification map

With the implementation of RCCAID, we can automatically identify and differentiate tumor regions within WSIs. Moreover, the model allows for the accurate quantification of the proportion of each grade present in every WSI. This valuable information is visually depicted through a classification map, as illustrated in Figures 4A and 4B. In certain cases, such as the one shown in Figure 4B, the majority of the samples were classified as grade 3. However, a small percentage (4.7%) of grade 4 samples were mistakenly classified within the grade 3 area. These misclassifications could potentially be overlooked by less attentive observers. The annotated heatmaps (Figures 4A and 4B) demonstrate the overlap between the pathologist’s markings and the model’s inference. The pathologist’s annotations (represented in color on the left) correspond to specific tumor grades, and on the right side, the model’s predictions are visualized.

Comparison of RCCAID model’s nuclear-level diagnostic performance with pathologists

To further validate the RCCAID model’s diagnostic performance at the nuclear level, we conducted a side-by-side, real-time comparison between the AI and pathologists. The labels for the training and validation sets were established through consensus among three senior pathologists, which served as the “gold standard.”

Our model demonstrated high AUC (0.987 and 0.996) and ACC in the validation sets, reflecting strong alignment with human expert consensus. To provide the requested quantitative evidence, we calculated the Cohen’s kappa between the model’s predictions and the expert-labeled ground truths, achieving a value of 0.864, indicating “almost perfect agreement” according to Landis and Koch criteria. The quadratic weighted Cohen’s kappa coefficients for the senior pathologist (SP1), junior pathologist (JP2), and the RCCAID model, relative to the gold standard, were all above 0.8, indicating excellent consistency. SP1 showed the highest consistency with the gold standard, with a coefficient of 0.8707. The model’s consistency with the gold standard was slightly lower at 0.8643. JP2, while still within the excellent consistency range, had the lowest consistency, with a coefficient of 0.8354 (see Figure S1; Table S2).

Performance evaluation of the renal cell carcinoma artificial intelligence prognostic model

The effectiveness of renal cell carcinoma artificial intelligence prognostic (RCCAIP) was evaluated, and the results demonstrated an AUC value of 0.94, an ACC value of 0.90, a SENS value of 0.89, and an F1 score of 0.89 (Figures 5A and 5B). These findings suggest that RCCAIP is a highly sensitive and specific model for accurately identifying the progression status of patients with CCRCC. It is worth noting that some of our data were sourced from the TCGA dataset, which has different whole-slide image staining and scanning parameters compared to those used in our hospital. The exact details of these parameters are not available to us, and it is possible that these differences could have influenced the results. However, despite these potential inconsistencies, we still achieved a strong AUC value, demonstrating that our model is adaptable and can be applied across different hospitals with varying whole-slide image staining and scanning parameters.

Clinical characteristics and grading analysis of progression-free and progression groups

Clinical data of progression-free and progress groups showed an onset age range of 20–88 years (median, 54 years). Most patients in the progression group were >54 years old, while no obvious age tendency was noted in the progression-free group. CCRCC was more common in males, with a male-to-female ratio of 3.2:1 in our cohort. To identify factors associated with tumor progression, clinicopathological characteristics and grade-related proportion distributions were compared between the two groups (statistical methods: chi-squared test/Fisher’s exact test for p values; normal approximation for 95% confidence intervals [CIs] of mean progression rate differences; two-tailed tests, p < 0.05 as significant). As shown in Table 1, age (p = 0.0002), T stage (p < 0.0001), N stage (p = 0.0003), M stage (p < 0.0001), and tumor-node-metastasis (TNM) stage (p < 0.0001) were significantly associated with tumor progression, whereas sex was not (p = 0.872). Specifically, the progression rate was higher in patients aged >54 years (36.25%) than in those ≤54 years (19.53%, 95% CI: 0.042–0.187), T3-T4 stage (87.50%) than T1-T2 stage (12.05%, 95% CI: 0.453–0.687), N1-N3 stage (91.67%) than N0 stage (21.33%, 95% CI: 0.152–0.548), M1/Mx stage (36.67%/41.54%) than M0 stage (18.37%, 95% CI: 0.179–0.521), and TNM III/IV stage (100%) than I/II stage (0%/3.85%, 95% CI: 0.846–0.954). No significant difference in progression rate was observed between males (29.23%) and females (27.96%, 95% CI: −0.067–0.098).

Regarding WHO/ISUP grade, significant differences in proportion distribution and mean progression rates were found between the two groups across all four grades (Table 2). Grade 1 showed a marginal association with tumor progression (p = 0.050), with mean progression rates of 10% (progression group) and 16% (progression-free group, 95% CI: −0.118–0.002). Grade 2 (p < 0.0001) was associated with lower progression risk, as its mean progression rate was lower in the progression group (42%) than in the progression-free group (65%, 95% CI: −0.312 to −0.148). In contrast, grade 3 (p < 0.0001) and grade 4 (p < 0.0001) correlated with increased progression risk, with higher mean progression rates in the progression group (33% and 15%, respectively) than in the progression-free group (15% and 4.1%, respectively; 95% CI: 0.128–0.232 for grade 3; 0.071–0.147 for grade 4). Boxplots of raw grade distribution data showed that the progression-free group had a narrower, lower grade distribution (predominantly grade 2), while the progression group exhibited a wieder, more advanced-grade distribution (Figures 5C and 5D). Grade 2 accounted for the largest proportion in both groups (42% in progression group vs. 65% in progression-free group). The progression group had higher proportions of grade 3 (33%) and grade 4 (15%) than the progression-free group (15% and 4.1%, respectively).

Grade distribution of the progression and progression-free groups

Our data confirmed an association between higher WHO/ISUP grades and worse CCRCC progression. However, grading distribution analysis (Figures 5C and 5D; Table 2) indicated that progression status is not solely determined by high grades. Notably, even grade 1/2 cases may have poor outcomes, while advanced-grade cases may have favorable outcomes, suggesting other contributing factors to CCRCC progression. Nevertheless, our RCCAIP model shows potential for progression status prediction.

Survival analysis between the progress and progress-free groups

We compared the prognosis data from three different hospitals, with complete overall survival (OS), progression-free survival (PFS), and disease-free survival (DFS) data, including 22 samples in the progression group and 88 samples in the progression-free group. All units are in “days”, and no data were missing. The sample size of the progress group was smaller, which is consistent with clinical observations that progressive cases are relatively rare during follow-up. The Shapiro-Wilk test was performed to assess the distribution. The median OS, PFS, and DFS in the progression group were 65.5, 54.5, and 54.5 days, respectively, which were higher than the median values in the progression-free group (all 43.0 days), as shown in Tables S3 and S4. However, the standard deviations of the progression group (46.8–48.3) were much larger than those in the progression-free group (20.8), indicating greater individual survival variation in the progression group due to factors such as metastasis, treatment, and comorbidities (see Table S5).

Kaplan-Meier survival curves (Figures 6A–6C) clearly show that the survival rates of the progression group are significantly worse than those of the progression-free group. The 6-month survival rates for OS/PFS/DFS in the progression group were 40.9%, 27.3%, and 27.3%, respectively. The curve trend reflects the rapid decrease in survival in the progression group, indicating a higher incidence of endpoint events (14 cases). The “longer survival time” observed in the progression group represents the survival of patients post-progression, but clinical outcomes still show significantly worse prognosis than the progression-free group. Subgroup analysis of the progression group (n = 22) based on recurrence time, metastasis type, and outcome cause (see Table S4) revealed that patients with multiple organ metastasis had similar median OS (65.5 days) to those with single-organ metastasis (66.0 days). Furthermore, 59.1% of the patients died due to renal cancer progression, and comorbidities worsened prognosis. The median OS of patients treated with advanced strategies (e.g., targeted therapy) between 2018 and 2019 was significantly longer (108.0 days) compared to earlier periods.

Discussion

In this study, we developed two DL models for CCRCC: RCCAID for grading diagnosis based on the WHO/ISUP system and RCCAIP for progression prediction. RCCAID automatically differentiates CCRCC grades and quantifies the proportion of each grade component, achieving a high ACC of 0.996, indicating its potential as an auxiliary tool for pathologists. Pathologically, grade 1 tumors (with barely visible nucleoli) are rare and often contain scattered cells with small nucleoli, which may explain the lower classification ACC of grade 1 in external validation. In contrast, grade 4 tumors (with distinct nuclear pleomorphism) showed the highest classification precision, attributed to their prominent morphological features that facilitate model recognition. RCCAIP exhibited favorable diagnostic performance (AUC = 0.94), demonstrating potential in estimating CCRCC progression status. The use of Shapley additive explanations (SHAP) values confirmed that the model depends on biologically relevant features, particularly high-grade tumor components, to generate its prognostic scores, consistent with established pathological grading standards.

CCRCC grading is prone to inter-pathologist subjectivity due to variations in training and experience, compounded by tumor heterogeneity with mixed grades across regions. The WHO/ISUP system requires identifying the highest grade in any high-power field, a challenging task that risks missing focal high-grade areas. RCCAID addresses these limitations by providing consistent grading criteria and mapping the distribution of each grade across whole-slide images, aiding in the detection of missed focal lesions. Comparative analysis confirmed RCCAID’s reliability: it achieved AUC values of 0.987 and 0.996 in validation sets, with a Cohen’s kappa coefficient of 0.864 (almost perfect agreement per Landis and Koch criteria) with expert consensus. Its quadratic weighted Cohen’s kappa (0.8643) was slightly lower than that of senior pathologists (0.8707) but higher than junior pathologists (0.8354), highlighting utility in settings with limited senior pathologist resources.

By integrating DL into daily pathology diagnostic routines, the workload of pathologists can be reduced, especially in situations where specialists are not readily available. In our research, we collected pathological sections from four different hospitals, each with its own tissue processing and staining procedures. Our AI model was able to perform well across these different processing conditions, indicating its potential for widespread application in various hospitals.

While there have been several studies utilizing computer images or DL to assist in CCRCC grading classification, our work stands out due to its strength and innovation. One notable aspect is that our study was based on the widely accepted WHO/ISUP grading system, which is considered more reliable than the Fuhrman grading system used in previous studies.15,16,17 These previous studies had limited sample sizes and achieved lower AUC values, with only 0.84 reported.15 Although a weakly supervised DL model was used, the study based on the Fuhrman grading system17 had some issues, such as difficult interpretation and poor repeatability. Additionally, the classification implied that each of the three parameters would increase in a parallel manner with increasing grade. Mounting evidence suggests that the Fuhrman grading system is inadequate for many cases of RCCs and does not reflect the correlation between tumor grade and prognosis.18 Some studies have explored the application of AI models in the prognosis of cancers, such as lung cancer, colorectal cancer, and renal cancer.19,20,21,22,23 There were a few studies focusing on the DL-based survival prediction model of CCRCC.19,20 Wessels et al. employed convolutional neural networks (CNNs) to predict 5-year OS in CCRCC.19 Marostica et al. trained CNNs to differentiate between low- and high-risk stage RCC patients.24 Another recent study investigated a multimodal recurrence scoring system for predicting the outcome of CCRCC, incorporating a WSI-based score, six SNP-based scores, and the Leibovich score, which required obtaining genomic signatures.25 Chen et al. developed and validated a computational prognostic model for CCRCC using a risk score, demonstrating survival prediction when combined with clinicopathologic factors.26 In contrast, our preoperative prognostic model integrates TNM stage, nuclear grade, and clinical characteristics, and survival analyses (from three hospitals and TCGA data) consistently confirmed poorer prognosis in the progression group—despite longer median OS/PFS/DFS reflecting post-progression survival—with rapid survival decline, larger individual variations (driven by metastasis, treatment, and comorbidities), and higher endpoint event rates (30.0% vs. 2.2% in TCGA data). Kaplan-Meier curves and log rank test further confirmed the inferior survival outcomes of the progression group. Additionally, our study suggests that computational image analysis techniques may have the potential to extract clinically relevant prognostic information from routine histological H&E images, which aligns with previous research that supports the presence of valuable prognostic cues within basic H&E slides. Moreover, our study highlights the prospective utility of computational image analysis techniques for extracting and leveraging such prognostic information.

Performance evaluation of the RCCAIP model showed favorable diagnostic performance, with an AUC of 0.94, ACC of 0.90, SENS of 0.89, and F1 score of 0.89. These metrics imply that the RCCAIP model may have reasonable SENS and specificity for identifying the progression status of CCRCC patients, which could provide a basis for its potential clinical utility pending further validation. Notably, a subset of the study data was derived from the TCGA dataset, which differs from in-hospital data in terms of whole-slide image staining and scanning parameters. Although detailed parameter information was unavailable and potential inconsistencies might introduce bias, the model still achieved a high AUC value. This finding indicates the preliminary adaptability of the RCCAIP model, suggesting it may maintain relatively stable performance across different medical institutions with heterogeneous whole-slide image processing protocols. Such cross-platform compatibility could be beneficial for improving the model’s clinical generalizability, though this requires verification in larger, independent cohorts.

Several studies have provided support for the use of DL in conjunction with whole-slide images to predict cancer outcomes that may not be easily identifiable by human eyes and can be reproduced.21,27 The WHO/ISUP grading system is commonly used to assess the prognosis of CCRCC. It suggests that higher grades (3 and 4) are associated with worse outcomes.28 However, our study data revealed that while higher grades generally correlated with worse progress, there were cases where patients with grade 2 tumors experienced lymph node and distant metastasis. RCCAID can learn image features in a way that surpasses human perception. It can make predictions by detecting morphological characteristics that may not be observable to the human eyes. With the assistance of RCCAID, which provides a comprehensive distribution of every grade within the panorama, we can review areas that may have been missed and make accurate grading diagnoses. This technology enables us to identify subtle features and patterns that may have been overlooked by human observers, allowing for a more precise and reliable assessment. This suggests that the WHO/ISUP grading system may not be flawless, and our AI model could potentially provide additional insights to complement it.

Limitations of the study

This study developed and validated two promising AI models for grading and progression prediction in CCRCC, though several limitations need to be addressed in future research. Despite using multi-institutional data, the small sample size may limit the models’ generalizability. Larger, more diverse cohorts (e.g., from various geographical regions, ethnic backgrounds, and clinical settings) are needed for external validation to ensure robustness across different populations. The current model does not integrate genomic or transcriptomic data, which could improve prognostic ACC and enable more personalized risk assessments. The real-time clinical applicability of the models remains untested, as practical factors such as clinical workflow and variations in pathologist expertise may affect their utility. Real-world testing is essential to determine their integration into routine practice without burdening healthcare providers. Tumor necrosis was excluded due to its low incidence in the dataset, but including it in future versions could enhance prognostic performance, given its role in the ISUP grading system. Although the RCCAIP model demonstrated high prognostic ACC, there was no ablation study to isolate the impact of imaging features beyond TNM staging. The “incremental value” of our model lies in its ability to convert categorical manual grading into continuous, objective quantification, capturing spatial heterogeneity that is missing from standard clinical variables. Our feature importance analysis (see Figure 5) identified specific grade proportions (e.g., grade 4 as a risk factor) as primary predictors. This granular imaging information contributes to the model’s high performance (AUC 0.98), surpassing the resolution of traditional TNM staging. A formal statistical ablation comparing standalone clinical versus imaging models would strengthen the work. However, due to the retrospective nature of our clinical database, separating clinical and pathological data for independent model training is challenging. Future research should explore multi-modal models to assess the value of imaging features in prognostic evaluation. Finally, while data augmentation reduced cross-center variations, the absence of a standardized stain normalization protocol (e.g., Macenko method) remains a limitation. Future models should integrate such protocols to improve stability and reproducibility across diverse settings.

Nevertheless, our study successfully developed two AI models that assist pathologists in accurate CCRCC grading and progression prediction. These models hold promise for improving diagnostic efficiency, reducing workload, and complementing existing clinical tools to optimize CCRCC management.

Method details

Pathological data composition

In this study, we used two cohorts from different sources for (1) developing the DL diagnostic model, and (2) performing the patient survival prediction model. We collected a total of 305 formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded pathological slides from 305 CCRCC patients’ pathological hematoxylin-eosin-stained slides from the Third Affiliated Hospital of SYSU between June 2003 to September 2019, which included 235 cases from Headquarter, 52 cases from LingNan Hospital, and 18 cases from YueDong Hospital. In addition, we collected 63 CCRCC cases from the Sixth Affiliated Hospital of SYSU from 2019 to 2025. Each hospital has independent tissue processing and staining. All slides were scanned at 40× magnification (0.25 μm/pixel) through an automatic digital slide scanner (Panoramic 250 FLASH, 3DHISTECH Ltd., Budapest, Hungary).

For the development of the DL diagnostic model, we randomly selected 80% (188 cases) of the scanned slides from the Headquarter cohort for the initial tagging stage. The remaining 20% (47 cases) of the Headquarter cohort was reserved for the verification stage. In the final inspection stage, we evaluated the model’s performance using a separate cohort of 70 cases, which included 52 cases from LingNan Hospital and 18 cases from YueDong Hospital. Additionally, 63 CCRCC cases from the Sixth Affiliated Hospital of SYSU, spanning from 2019 to 2025, were selected as an independent external dataset for model validation.

To construct the prognosis model, we initially screened 148 cases out of the 305 cases with complete clinical information. However, due to the limited number of cases, we further selected 140 cases of H&E stained WSIs downloaded from the Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) portal (project TCGA-KIRP) that had corresponding follow-up data. For model construction, we randomly selected 80% of these cases, while the remaining 20% were reserved for effectiveness evaluation.

Ethics approval for the use of these slides in our study was obtained from the Institute Research Ethics Committee of the Third Affiliated Hospital of SYSU. None of the patients received any systematic or loco-regional therapy prior to surgery. Clinicopathological data were obtained from the medical records archives. The clinical data for the prognosis model are presented in Table 1.

Pixel-level annotation

The annotation process of the WSIs involved three steps: the initial tagging stage, the verification stage, and the final inspection stage. There is specialist pathologists, each with more than 15 years of experience, utilized the ASAP 1.9 software (Radboud University Medical Center, Netherlands) to identify and outline the regions of interest (ROIs) in WSIs. The pathologists excluded areas such as blank spaces, hemorrhage, and necrosis. Additionally, they annotated the grade of each region according to the WHO/ISUP classification guideline.3 The annotated results obtained from the computational analysis were thoroughly reviewed and revised by pathologists. In cases where there were conflicting opinions among the pathologists, a senior specialist with more than 20 years of diagnostic experience was consulted to make the final decision. Patches within the ROIs were automatically extracted at a resolution of 1024 × 1024 pixels and a magnification of 40× using Openslide (version 1.1, University of Pittsburgh, Pittsburgh, PA, USA). All patches were resized to 256 × 256 pixels and 128 × 128 pixels, respectively, to accommodate server storage and GPU computing capacity limitations. After review by the pathologists, a total of 128,652 effective training patches and 18,403 effective test patches for the verification and inspection stages were obtained.

To prevent data leakage, the dataset partitioning was performed strictly at the patient level. All patches derived from a single patient were allocated exclusively to either the training, verification, or testing set, ensuring that no patient-specific information was shared across different sets.

Diagnostic model

Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) have been proven to be effective in extracting features from medical pathological images and have shown good performance in tissue classification, cancer detection, and patient prognosis prediction.29 In this study, we utilized three classic and efficient CNN models (Xception, ResNet50, and Inception V3) to predict the 4 tumor nuclear grades (Grade 1, 2, 3, and 4) based on patches of size 256 × 256 pixels and 128 × 128 pixels generated from the annotation process. All models were implemented in Keras (version 2.2) using the Adam optimizer. The models were initialized with parameters pretrained on the ImageNet dataset, and the original last layer of each model was replaced with a layer having four outputs and using Softmax as the activation function. We designated the best model as the Renal Cell Carcinoma Artificial Intelligence Diagnosis (RCCAID) model.

Specific operational details (see Table S1):

Model training: We used the Adam optimizer with an initial learning rate of 1e−4. The model was trained for 100 epochs with a batch size of 32. An early stopping criterion based on validation loss (patience = 10 epochs) was applied to prevent overfitting.

Data augmentation: To improve model generalization, we implemented several data augmentation strategies, including random horizontal and vertical flips, random rotations (90°, 180°, 270°), and adjustments in brightness/contrast.

Preprocessing & Patch sampling: Patches were extracted at 40× magnification (1024 × 1024 pixels) and resized to 256 × 256 and 128 × 128 pixels. The sampling stride was set to 1024 pixels, meaning no overlap between the patches.

Stain normalization: Given the multi-center nature of our data, we applied extensive color augmentation to achieve stain invariance, minimizing the impact of staining variations between hospitals.

Panoramic diagnostic map and quantitative analysis

The accurate location coordinates of the visual fields in the digital pathology of patients were obtained through the mapping process MASK and XML files. Each output of the model corresponded to a certain kind of prediction probability for each visual field. The four tumor grades were represented by different colors, ranging from cool to warm, to indicate the increasing malignancy of the pathology. By using the coordinates of the visual field, the panorama of the patient’s pathology could be reassembled, showing the areas with potential malignant risk and their distribution density in the digital pathology panorama. The warmer the color, the higher the tumor grade.

Patient prognosis prediction model

Based on the RCCAID models described earlier, we determined the proportion of the four CCRCC nuclear grades in each WSI. To obtain accurate ground truth, we selected patients with distinct outcomes (either progress or progress-free) as the research cohort. A total of 288 cases with complete clinical data, including TNM stage and overall survival, were collected. The TNM staging system is widely used as a clinical prognostic tool, as it can predict clinical relapse, overall survival, and cause-specific survival.30,31 After thoroughly analyzing the clinical data, we categorized the 288 cases into two distinct groups: the progression group and the progression-free group. This categorization was based not only on the TNM stage but also on the patients’ overall outcomes. This approach was necessary because studies have shown that patients with the same tumor stage and grade may have diverse survival outcomes.32,33 The progression group included 25 cases from our hospitals and 58 cases from TCGA, all of which exhibited clinical features such as distant metastasis and/or lymph node metastasis. The progression-free group included 123 cases from our hospitals and 82 cases from TCGA, characterized by disease-free status and the absence of distant metastasis or lymph node involvement. To classify prognosis, we established a three-layer fully connected network, with the final layer consisting of two outputs: progress-free and progress. We named this model the Renal Cell Carcinoma Artificial Intelligence Prognostic Model (RCCAIP). Graphical abstract illustrates the workflow for developing the CCRCC deep learning grading diagnostic and prognostic models.

The RCCAIP model is a three-layer fully connected network that takes the quantified proportions of Grade 1, Grade 2, Grade 3, and Grade 4 (derived from the RCCAID patch classification across the entire WSI) as input features. To enhance transparency, we performed a feature importance analysis on the trained RCCAIP network. This analysis was conducted by examining the weights of the first fully connected layer, which directly links the input features to the network. The key findings, presented in the revised Results and Discussion sections, are as follows:

Dominant Feature: The proportion of Grade 4 patches demonstrated the highest positive weight and the strongest correlation with disease progression, confirming its role as the dominant prognostic factor.

Protective Feature: In contrast, the proportion of Grade 1 patches exhibited the highest negative weight, indicating a protective or low-risk association, consistent with clinical knowledge.

We also implemented the SHAP framework to visualize feature contributions and enhance the transparency of the RCCAIP model. We generated an SHAP beeswarm plot (see Figure S2A) to illustrate the overall impact of histological grade proportions across the evaluation cohort. The plot clearly shows that Grade 4 and Grade 3 patch proportions are the most influential features. Higher values of these features (represented by red dots) are associated with significantly higher SHAP values, confirming their roles as primary risk factors. To demonstrate the decision-making process for individual patients, we provided an SHAP waterfall plot for a representative high-risk case (see Figure S2B). This plot quantifies how each specific feature pushes the model’s prediction from the base value (0.374) to the final output (0.933). For instance, the high proportions of Grade 4 (50%) and Grade 3 (45%) in this patient contributed +0.26 and +0.17 to the final risk score, respectively.

These analyses provide a granular, biologically plausible explanation for the model’s predictions, ensuring that the RCCAIP system operates with high transparency and clinical reliability.

Quantification and statistical analysis

The study conducted various statistical analyses to evaluate the performance of the two models. For the diagnostic model RCCAID, we evaluated its performance using several metrics including the AUC to assess the model’s ability. Additionally, we considered metrics such as ACC, SENS, and F1-score, which is the harmonic mean of precision and recall. A higher F1 score indicates a better balance between precision and recall. The AUC ranges from 0.5 to 1, where 1 represents a perfect classifier performance and 0.5 represents a random performance. To compare the diagnostic performances of different models, we utilized the AUC values. The optimal model was selected based on both the F1 score and the AUC value to evaluate its diagnostic performance. We used the macro average to calculate the multi-classification AUC. Furthermore, we provided pathologists with a panoramic diagnostic map that offers a comprehensive understanding of the distribution and proportion of the four grades within the WSI. For the prognostic model RCCAIP, we assessed its performance using metrics such as AUC, ACC, SENS, and F1-score to evaluate its prognostic capabilities.

We compared the clinicopathological features between the progress and progress-free groups, including age, sex, tumor stage, node stage, metastasis stage, and TNM stage. Additionally, we compared the data of the progress and progress-free groups across the four grades (Grade 1–4) by different proportion intervals, calculating the p-values for overall group differences and the 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the mean progression rate difference. The chi-square test was used for P-value calculation, with Fisher’s exact test applied for small-sized samples; the normal approximation method was adopted for 95% CI calculation. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and a difference was defined as statistically significant when the P-value was <0.05 or the false discovery rate (FDR) was <0.05. When comparing OS, PFS and DFS between the progress and progress-free groups, the non-parametric Mann-Whitney U test was used for intergroup comparison. To intuitively display the survival differences between the two groups, Kaplan-Meier survival curves for OS, PFS and DFS, and the log rank test was used to verify the statistical significance of the survival differences.
