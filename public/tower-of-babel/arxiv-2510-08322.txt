Boundary Representations, Geometry of Matrix Ranges, and C ∗ -Envelopes of Finite-Dimensional Operator Systems
Douglas Farenick, Chi-Kwong Li, and Sushil Singla

Abstract
. An analysis of the boundary representations and C ∗ -envelopes of some finite-dimensional operator systems ℛ {\mathcal{R}} is undertaken by considering relationships between operator-theoretic properties of a d d -tuple 𝔵 = ( x 1 , … , x d ) \mathfrak{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{d}) of elements in a unital C ∗ -algebra 𝒜 {\mathcal{A}} and operator-system properties of the linear span 𝒪 𝔵 \mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{x}} of { 1 𝒜 , x 1 , x 1 ∗ , … , x d , x d ∗ } \{1_{\mathcal{A}},x_{1},x_{1}^{*},\dots,x_{d},x_{d}^{*}\} . This approach lends itself well to the study of certain phenomena in single- and several-variable operator theory, such as the Smith-Ward property. The matrix range of a d d -tuple of elements 𝔵 \mathfrak{x} is matrix-affinely homeomorphic to the matrix state space of the operator system determined by 𝔵 \mathfrak{x} , and many of our methods connect the geometry of these compact matrix convex sets to operator system properties, including various forms of nuclearity and lifting properties.

1.
Introduction

The theory of operator systems, introduced by Arveson in
[
3
]
and later treated formally by
Choi and Effros in
[
11
]
, has advanced substantially over the last two decades, with the proof of the existence of the
noncommutative Choquet boundary representing one of the theory’s pinnacle achievements. This existence of the noncommutative Choquet
boundary, established first by Arveson
[
5
]
for separable operator systems and then completed later by Davidson and Kennedy

[
17
]
for arbitrary operator systems, provides a new avenue towards
determining the minimal C
∗
-cover, which is also known as the C
∗
-envelope
[
32
]
,
of any given operator system. Our purpose in this paper is to better understand boundary representations and minimal C
∗
-covers
for certain finite-dimensional operator systems, using results and techniques involving
noncommutative states and noncommutative convexity. In particular, the (joint) numerical and matrix ranges of a
d
-tuple of operators
have a prominent role in our work.

Formally, an operator system
{\mathcal{R}}
is a complex involutive vector space equipped with a partial ordering on all matrix spaces over
{\mathcal{R}}

and having a distinguished element
e_{\mathcal{R}}
that serves as an Archimedean order unit for the matrix order. However, by the Choi-Effros theorem

[
11
]
, such operator systems
{\mathcal{R}}
may be represented faithfully, in a variety of ways,
as unital
*
-closed subspaces of
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
, the algebra of
bounded linear operators acting on a complex Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
; therefore, informally, operator systems are linear subspaces
{\mathcal{R}}

of unital C
∗
-algebras
{\mathcal{A}}
such that
1\in{\mathcal{R}}
, where
1
denotes the multiplicative identity of

{\mathcal{A}}
, and
x^{*}\in{\mathcal{R}}
, for every
x\in{\mathcal{R}}
. Because operator systems carry a matrix order structure, but are
merely linear spaces rather than algebras, they play a role in a wide variety of settings, ranging from quantum information
theory to noncommutative geometry. Very recently, through the work of Davidson and Kennedy
[
16
]
,
the category
{\sf OpSys}
, whose objects are operator systems and morphisms are unital completely positive linear maps,
has been shown to be dual to the category
{\sf NCConv}
, whose objects are noncommutative convex sets and morphisms
are continuous noncommutative affine functions. This categorical duality will be useful in some of our analysis in this paper.

Suppose that
\mathfrak{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})
is a
d
-tuple of elements
in
{\mathcal{A}}
, and define an operator subsystem
\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{x}}\subseteq{\mathcal{A}}

by

(1.1)

\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{x}}=\mbox{\rm Span}\{1,x_{1},x_{1}^{*},\dots,x_{d},x_{d}^{*}\}.

As each finite-dimensional operator system
{\mathcal{R}}
has the form
\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{x}}
, for some
d\in\mathbb{N}

and
x_{1},\dots,x_{d}\in{\mathcal{R}}
, it is natural to consider them in this basis-dependent way (although, in principle, we do not assume
the elements
1,x_{1},x_{1}^{*},\dots,x_{d},x_{d}^{*}
to be linearly independent).

A single element
x
in a unital C
∗
-algebra
{\mathcal{A}}
determines various operator systems of interest, each of which
has properties that are, to some extent, governed by
x
.
The first of such operator systems is in fact the smallest of all operator subsystems of
{\mathcal{A}}
that contain
x\in{\mathcal{A}}
: namely,

(1.2)

\mathcal{O}_{x}=\mbox{Span}\{1,x,x^{*}\}.

In considering
d
-tuples determined by the consecutive integral powers of
x
, we denote the resulting operator systems by

\mathcal{O}_{x}^{[d]}
, where

(1.3)

\mathcal{O}_{x}^{[d]}=\mbox{Span}\{1,x,x^{*},x^{2},(x^{2})^{*},\dots,x^{d},(x^{d})^{*}\}.

This paper begins with a section defining the objects under study and establishing notation. Our study begins with
a basic, yet illustrative case: a
d
-tuple of commuting normal elements. This basic case will be employed in subsequent
sections. Motivated by the fact that a normal operator acting on a separable Hilbert space is unitarily equivalent or approximately
unitarily equivalent to a diagonal operator (in some given orthonormal basis), we show that every operator system of the form

\mathcal{O}_{x}
may be represented as
\mathcal{O}_{T}
, for some block-diagonal operator
T
with extremal, spectral
matrix blocks. We follow this
with a determination of the operator system features of
\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{x}}
when the joint numerical range of the

d
-tuple
\mathfrak{x}
of selfadjoint elements is a simplex in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We also study a variety of nuclearity and lifting properties for operator systems
determined by a
d
-tuple
\mathfrak{x}
, and apply these ideas to the study of essential matrix ranges and the
Smith-Ward preservation property.

For a unital C

-algebra
{\mathcal{A}}

\mathcal{A}^{d}

-copies of
{\mathcal{A}}
,

d
-copies of

.

Boundary Representations, Noncommutative States, and Noncommutative Convexity

The monographs by Blecher and Le Merdy
[
7
]
,
Effros and Ruan
[
20
]
, and Paulsen
[
49
]
provide the foundations of operator systems and algebras; the monograph

2.1.
Equivalence and canonical equivalence

In the operator system category
{\sf OpSys}
, where the objects are operator systems and the morphisms are
unital completely positive (ucp) linear maps, we shall write

{\mathcal{R}}\cong{\mathcal{T}},

for operator systems
{\mathcal{R}}
and
{\mathcal{T}}
, if they are isomorphic in
{\sf OpSys}
. Thus,
{\mathcal{R}}\cong{\mathcal{T}}
if and only if there is a
linear bijection
\phi:{\mathcal{R}}\rightarrow{\mathcal{T}}
for which both
\phi
and
\phi^{-1}
are ucp maps; we call such maps
unital
complete linear order isomorphisms. Because our maps are unit preserving, we may rephrase the order property in terms of an
isometric property:

{\mathcal{R}}\cong{\mathcal{T}}
if and only if there exists a unital
complete surjective linear isometry
\phi:{\mathcal{R}}\rightarrow{\mathcal{T}}
. If
{\mathcal{A}}
and
\mathcal{B}
are unital C
∗
-algebras
such that
{\mathcal{A}}\cong\mathcal{B}
in the category
{\sf OpSys}
, then the Kadison-Schwarz inequality shows that

{\mathcal{A}}
and
\mathcal{B}
are also isomorphic as unital C
∗
-algebras.

Definition 2.1
.

If
\mathfrak{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})
and
\mathfrak{y}=(y_{1},\dots,y_{d})
are
d
-tuples of elements from unital C
∗
-algebras
{\mathcal{A}}
and

\mathcal{B}
, respectively, then
\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{x}}
and
\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{y}}
are
canonically equivalent

if there exists a
unital complete order isomorphism
\phi:\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{x}}\rightarrow\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{y}}
such that

\phi(x_{k})=y_{k}
, for every
k=1,\dots,d
.

Canonical equivalence of operator systems
\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{x}}
and
\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{y}}
shall be denoted by

\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{x}}\simeq\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{y}}

to distinguish this equivalence from the more general equivalence
\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{x}}\cong\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{y}}
.

2.2.
Boundary representations

Definition 2.2
.

A
C
∗
-cover
of an operator system
{\mathcal{R}}
is a pair,
({\mathcal{A}},\kappa)
, consisting of a unital C
∗
-algebra
{\mathcal{A}}

and a unital complete order embedding
\kappa:{\mathcal{R}}\rightarrow{\mathcal{A}}
such that
{\mathcal{A}}={\rm C}^{*}\left(\kappa({\mathcal{R}})\right)
.

In principle, an operator system
{\mathcal{R}}
has many many different C
∗
-covers, thereby allowing
{\mathcal{R}}
to generate many different
C
∗
-algebras. There is, among all C
∗
-covers of
{\mathcal{R}}
, a maximal one
[
40
]
and a minimal
one
[
32
]
, both distinguished by certain universal properties. The minimal C
∗
-cover of
{\mathcal{R}}
is called
the
C
∗
-envelope
of
{\mathcal{R}}
, and is denoted by
{\rm C}_{\rm min}^{*}({\mathcal{R}})
. These minimal C
∗
-covers are one of the main objects of study in this paper.

Theorem 2.3

(Hamana)
.

If
{\mathcal{R}}
is an operator system, then
there exists a unital C
∗
-algebra
{\rm C}_{\rm min}^{*}({\mathcal{R}})
and unital complete order embedding
\iota_{\rm e}:{\mathcal{R}}\rightarrow{\rm C}_{\rm min}^{*}({\mathcal{R}})

with the following universal property: for any C
∗
-cover
({\mathcal{A}},\kappa)
of
{\mathcal{R}}
,
there is a unital surjective
*
-homomorphism
\pi:{\mathcal{A}}\rightarrow{\rm C}_{\rm min}^{*}({\mathcal{R}})
such that
\iota_{\rm e}(x)=\pi\left(\kappa(x)\right)
, for every
x\in{\mathcal{R}}
.

Moreover, if
(\mathcal{B},\iota_{\mathcal{B}})
is any other C
∗
-cover of
{\mathcal{R}}
that has
the same universal property that
{\rm C}_{\rm min}^{*}({\mathcal{R}})
has, then there is a unital
*
-isomorphism

\varrho:\mathcal{B}\rightarrow{\rm C}_{\rm min}^{*}({\mathcal{R}})
such that
\iota_{\rm e}=\varrho\circ\iota_{\mathcal{B}}
.

Formally, the C
∗
-envelope of an operator system
{\mathcal{R}}

is a pair consisting of a C
∗
-algebra and a unital completely order emebdding into this algebra. However, the second part of Theorem
2.3
asserts the
uniqueness of the C
∗
-envelope up to an isomorphism that fixes
{\mathcal{R}}
, and so we most often refer only to the C
∗
-algebra
{\rm C}_{\rm min}^{*}({\mathcal{R}})
and assume
implicitly the existence of an embedding
\iota_{\rm e}:{\mathcal{R}}\rightarrow{\rm C}_{\rm min}^{*}({\mathcal{R}})
satisfying the universal properties of Theorem
2.3
.

The noncommutative Choquet boundary theorem (Theorem
2.5
below), established by of Arveson
[
5
]
for separable operator systems,
and by Davidson and Kennedy
[
17
]
for arbitrary operator systems,
asserts that the C
∗
-envelope of an operator system
{\mathcal{R}}
may be determined from the boundary representations of
{\mathcal{R}}
, independently
of how
{\mathcal{R}}
is represented as an operator subsystem of a unital C
∗
-algebra.

Definition 2.4
.

If
{\mathcal{R}}\subseteq{\mathcal{A}}
is an operator system in a unital C
∗
-algebra
{\mathcal{A}}
such that
{\mathcal{A}}={\rm C}^{*}({\mathcal{R}})
, then a
representation (that is, a unital
∗
-homomorphism)
\pi:{\mathcal{A}}\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{\pi})
of

{\mathcal{A}}
on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{\pi}
is a
boundary representation of
{\mathcal{R}}
if

(1)

\pi
is an irreducible representation of
{\mathcal{A}}
, and

(2)

the ucp map
\pi_{|{\mathcal{R}}}:{\mathcal{R}}\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{\pi})
has a unique ucp extension (namely,
\pi
) to
{\mathcal{A}}
.

Theorem 2.5

(Arveson-Davidson-Kennedy)
.

If
{\mathcal{R}}
is an operator system generating a unital C
∗
-algebra
{\mathcal{A}}
, then, for every

q\in\mathbb{N}
and
X\in\mathcal{M}_{q}({\mathcal{R}})
,

\|X\|=\max\left\{\|\pi^{[q]}(X)\|\,|\,\pi\mbox{ is a boundary representation of }{\mathcal{R}}\right\},

where
\pi^{[q]}:\mathcal{M}_{q}({\mathcal{A}})\rightarrow\mathcal{M}_{q}\left(\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{\pi})\right)

is the homomorphism

[a_{ij}]_{i,j=1}^{q}\mapsto\left[\pi(a_{ij})\right]_{i,j=1}^{q}.

Moreover, if
\mathcal{J}_{\mathcal{R}}
is the ideal of
{\mathcal{A}}

consisting of elements
a\in{\mathcal{A}}
for which
\pi(a)=0
, for every
boundary representation
\pi:{\mathcal{A}}\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{\pi})
of
{\mathcal{R}}
, then

{\rm C}_{\rm min}^{*}({\mathcal{R}})={\mathcal{A}}/\mathcal{J}_{\mathcal{R}}.

2.3.
Noncommutative states and convexity

The theory of noncommutative convex sets, developed by Davidson and Kennedy
[
16
]
, is an extension of
matrix convexity to a setting where infinite matrices of higher cardinalities are incorporated. Because our interest is with finite-dimensional operator
systems, the relevant cardinalities are the finite cardinalities
n
together with
\aleph_{0}
. Therefore, we restrict the discussion of noncommutative
state spaces and noncommutative convexity below to this case alone.

A
noncommutative state
on a finite-dimensional operator system
{\mathcal{R}}

is a ucp map on
{\mathcal{R}}
with values in
\mathcal{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
, for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
, or a ucp map on
{\mathcal{R}}

with values in
\mathbb{B}=\mathcal{B}(\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N}))
.
Maps of the former type are called
matrix states
on
{\mathcal{R}}
, and for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

S_{n}({\mathcal{R}})
denotes the set of all matrix states on

{\mathcal{R}}
with values in
\mathcal{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
. The set of all maps of this latter type is denoted by
S_{\aleph_{0}}({\mathcal{R}})
.

The
noncommutative state space
of a finite-dimensional operator system
{\mathcal{R}}
is the graded set
S_{\rm nc}({\mathcal{R}})
given by

S_{\rm nc}({\mathcal{R}})=\bigsqcup_{n\leq\aleph_{0}}S_{n}({\mathcal{R}}).

If we disregard
S_{\aleph_{0}}({\mathcal{R}})
, then the graded set

S_{\rm mtrx}({\mathcal{R}})=\bigsqcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}S_{n}({\mathcal{R}})

is called the
matrix state space
of
{\mathcal{R}}
. The matrix state spaces of infinite-dimensional
operator systems
{\mathcal{R}}
are defined in exactly the
same way, but the noncommutative state space of
{\mathcal{R}}
could involve cardinalities beyond
\aleph_{0}
;
see
[
14
,
16
]
for further details.

Definition 2.6
.

A graded set
K_{\rm mtrx}=\displaystyle\bigsqcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}K_{n}
is
matrix convex
in
a topological vector space
\mathcal{V}
if:

(1)

K_{n}\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{n}(\mathcal{V})
, for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
;

(2)

v_{1}\oplus v_{2}\in K_{n_{1}+n_{2}}
, for all
n_{1},n_{2}\in\mathbb{N}
and
v_{j}\in K_{n_{j}}
; and

(3)

\beta^{*}v\beta\in K_{n}
, for every
v\in\mathcal{M}_{m}(\mathbb{C})
and linear isometry
\beta:\mathbb{C}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{m}
.

Moreover, if each
K_{n}
is closed, then
K_{\rm mtrx}
is said to be
closed
; and if each
K_{n}
is
compact, then
K_{\rm mtrx}
is said to be
compact
.

The axioms indicate that each of the levels
K_{n}
of a matrix convex set
K_{\rm mtrx}
are convex,
and that there are interactions between the various levels in the
grading. In particular, matrix convex sets
K_{\rm mtrx}
are closed under finite sums of the form

\sum_{j}\gamma_{j}^{*}\nu_{j}\gamma_{j}
, where

\nu_{j}\in K_{n_{j}}
,
\gamma_{j}:\mathbb{C}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{n_{j}}
is a linear map, and
\sum_{j}\gamma_{j}^{*}\gamma_{j}=1_{n}\in\mathcal{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
. Such
sums are called
matrix convex combinations
of the elements
\nu_{j}\in K
.

The matrix state space of an arbitrary operator system
{\mathcal{R}}
is a matrix convex set;
when each
S_{n}({\mathcal{R}})
is equipped with the bounded-weak topology,
the matrix state space
S_{\rm mtrx}({\mathcal{R}})
is compact.

Definition 2.7
.

A
matrix extreme point

\nu
of a matrix convex set
K_{\rm mtrx}=\displaystyle\bigsqcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}K_{n}

in a topological vector space
\mathcal{V}
is
an element
\nu\in K
for
which the only expressions of
\nu
as a matrix convex combination
\sum_{j}\gamma_{j}^{*}\nu_{j}\gamma_{j}
of elements
\nu_{j}\in K_{n_{j}}
,
such
that each

\gamma_{j}
is surjective, are those matrix convex combinations in which each
n_{j}=n
and there are unitary matrices
u_{j}\in\mathcal{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})

such that
\nu_{j}=u_{j}^{*}\nu u_{j}
.

The (non-graded) set of (classical) extreme points of a convex set
K
is denoted by
\partial_{\rm ext}\,K
, while
the graded set of matrix extreme points of a matrix convex set
K_{\rm mtrx}
is denoted by
\partial_{\rm m-ext}\,K
. The Krěin-Milman theorem of
Webster and Winkler
[
56
]
asserts, for compact matrix convex sets
K_{\rm mtrx}
, that

K_{\rm mtrx}=\overline{\mbox{m-conv}\left(\partial_{\rm m-ext}\,K_{\rm mtrx}\right)},

where
\mbox{m-conv}\,\mathcal{G}
denotes the set of all matrix convex combinations of elements of a graded set

\mathcal{G}
.

If
\nu\in K_{n}
is a matrix extreme point of a matrix convex set
K_{\rm mtrx}
,
then
\nu\in\partial_{\rm ext}K_{n}
; the converse, however, is generally not true.

A noncommutative convex set in a separable operator space
\mathcal{V}
is achieved by adding an additional set,

K_{\aleph_{0}}
to the grading of a matrix convex set, where
K_{\aleph_{0}}

is a set consisting of (countably) infinite matrices over the base space
\mathcal{V}
, where
\mathcal{V}

is assumed to be a weak
∗
-closed subspace of

\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
, for some Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
.

Definition 2.8
.

A
noncommutative convex set

K_{\rm nc}
in a separable operator space
\mathcal{V}

is a graded set

K_{\rm nc}=\bigsqcup_{n\leq\aleph_{0}}K_{n},

where
K_{n}\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{n}(V)
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and
K_{\aleph_{0}}
is a
set of infinite matrices over
\mathcal{V}
with uniformly bounded
finite submatrices. Furthermore,
if
\mathcal{I}
is a finite or countably infinite set, and if
n
and
\{n_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
are cardinalities no larger than
\aleph_{0}
,
then for every family
\{a_{n,n_{i}}\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
of isometries
a_{n,n_{i}}:\ell^{2}(n_{i})\rightarrow\ell^{2}(n)

such that
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}a_{n,n_{i}}a_{n,n_{i}}^{*}=1_{n}
, and every bounded collection
\{\nu_{n_{i}}\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
of

\nu_{n_{i}}\in K_{n_{i}}
, we have
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}a_{n,n_{i}}\nu_{n_{i}}a_{n,n_{i}}^{*}\in K_{n}
, where the convergence of the sums,
when
\mathcal{I}
is infinite, is with respect to the strong operator topology.

The graded set
K_{\rm nc}
described above
is not the most general noncommutative convex set; as with the noncommutative state spaces,
one can consider additional sets
K_{\kappa}
in the grading, using cardinals
\kappa
beyond
\aleph_{0}
,
if the situation warrants it. However,
in the present paper, there is no need to go beyond
\aleph_{0}
.

Our primary example of a matrix convex set is
S_{\rm nc}({\mathcal{R}})
, the noncommutative state space of
a finite-dimensional operator system
{\mathcal{R}}
. Another example is
\mathbb{C}_{\rm nc}
,
which is the graded set of bounded linear operators acting on separable Hilbert space.

Because of the grading, noncommutative convex sets are completely determined
by the sets indexed by finite cardinalities
[
16
, Proposition 2.2.10]
; hence,
K_{\rm nc}=L_{\rm nc}

if and only if
K_{\rm mtrx}=L_{\rm mtrx}
.
We shall require this fact later in this paper, and it allows us to focus on matrix state spaces and
matrix convexity, even though the machinery of noncommutative convexity
theory is required for the categorical duality between
{\sf OpSys}
and

{\sf NCConv}
.

By
A_{\rm nc}(K_{\rm nc})
, we denote the space of all continuous noncommutative
affine functions
K_{\rm nc}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}_{\rm nc}
, and
this space carries the structure of an operator system
[
16
, Section 2.5]
.
In particular, with the noncommutative convex set
S_{\rm nc}({\mathcal{R}})
, where
{\mathcal{R}}
is an operator system of finite dimension,
the categorical duality results of Webster-Winker
[
56
]
and Davidson-Kennedy
[
16
]
assert

A_{\rm nc}\left(S_{\rm nc}({\mathcal{R}})\right)\cong{\mathcal{R}}

in the category
{\sf OpSys}
.

In matrix convexity and noncommutative convexity, there are a variety of different notions of extreme point. Our choice of using matrix extreme points in this
paper is due to the connection with pure matrix states.

2.4.
Noncommutative numerical ranges

Definition 2.9
.

If
\mathfrak{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})
is a
d
-tuple of elements from a unital C
∗
-algebra
{\mathcal{A}}
, then the

noncommutative (joint) numerical range
of
\mathfrak{x}
is the graded set

W_{\rm nc}(\mathfrak{x})=\bigsqcup_{n\leq\aleph_{0}}W_{n}(\mathfrak{x}),

where, for
n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\aleph_{0}\}
,

W_{n}(\mathfrak{x})=\left\{\left(\phi(x_{1}),\phi(x_{2}),\dots,\phi(x_{d})\right)\,|\,\phi\in S_{n}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{x}})\right\}.

If we disregard the set
W_{\aleph_{0}}(\mathfrak{x})
, then the graded set

W_{\rm mtrx}(\mathfrak{x})=\bigsqcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}W_{n}(\mathfrak{x}),

is called the
(joint) matrix range
of
\mathfrak{x}
.

Because the noncommutative state space
S_{\rm nc}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{x}})

is a noncommutative convex set, so is the noncommutative numerical range
W_{\rm nc}(\mathfrak{x})
.
Moreover, the matrix range

W_{\rm mtrx}(\mathfrak{x})
is
compact with respect to the norm topology. The first set in the grading,
W_{1}(\mathfrak{x})
, is the joint numerical range of the

d
-tuple
\mathfrak{x}
.

The matrix range
W_{\rm mtrx}(\mathfrak{x})

determines the operator system
\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{x}}
uniquely up to canonical isomorphism in the category
{\sf OpSys}

[
4
,
15
]
:

3.
Boundary Representations of Commuting Normal Operators

It is instructive to consider the case of
d

normal elements. While the results
of this section overlap with similar work of Passer
[

,
our approach is somewhat different.

If
\mathfrak{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})
is a
d
-tuple of
commuting normal elements, then
{\rm C}^{*}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{x}})
is abelian. Therefore,
all irreducible representations of
{\rm C}^{*}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{x}})
occur on spaces of dimension 1;
thus, every boundary representation of
{\rm C}^{*}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{x}})
has values in
\mathbb{C}
.

Definition 3.1
.

The
degree-
d

associated with a compact set

is the operator subsystem
\mathcal{F}_{X}^{[d]}
of
C(X)
given by

\mathcal{F}_{X}^{[d]}=\mbox{\rm Span}\{1,z,\overline{z},z^{2},\overline{z}^{2},\dots,z^{d},\overline{z}^{d}\}\subseteq C(X).

Suppose
x\in\mathfrak{A}
is normal and let
X=\sigma(x)
, the spectrum of
x
.
Because the Gelfand transform is completely isometric, we immediately obtain the canonical equivalences

\mathcal{F}_{X}^{[d]}\simeq\mathcal{O}_{x}^{[d]},

for every
d\in\mathbb{N}
.
Hence,

{\rm C}_{\rm min}^{*}(\mathcal{O}_{x}^{[d]})\simeq{\rm C}_{\rm min}^{*}(\mathcal{F}_{X}^{[d]})\cong C(\partial_{C}\mathcal{F}_{X}^{[d]}),

where
\partial_{C}\mathcal{F}_{X}^{[d]}
denotes the classical Choquet boundary of the function system
\mathcal{F}_{X}^{[d]}
.

In what follows, we shall use basic operator theory to determine
\partial_{C}\mathcal{F}_{X}^{[d]}
, starting with
an extension below of
[
2
, Proposition 1.6]
.

Theorem 3.2
.

If
\mathfrak{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})
is a
d
-tuple of
commuting normal elements, then
a character
\rho:{\rm C}^{*}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{x}})\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
is a boundary representation
of
\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{x}}
if and only if

\left(\rho(x_{1}),\rho(x_{2}),\dots,\rho(x_{d})\right)\in\partial_{\rm ext}W_{1}(\mathfrak{x}).

Proof.

Assume a character

\rho:{\rm C}^{*}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{x}})\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
is a boundary representation for
\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{x}}
.
To show

\left(\rho(x_{1}),\rho(x_{2}),\dots,\rho(x_{d})\right)\in\partial_{\rm ext}W_{1}(\mathfrak{x})
, it is equivalent to prove that
\rho
is a pure state on
\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{x}}
, by
Theorem
2.11
. The pureness of
\rho
is
an immediate consequence of
[
30
, Proposition 2.12]
.

Suppose now
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{d})\in\partial_{\rm ext}W_{1}(\mathfrak{x})
. By Theorem
2.11
, there exists a pure
state
\phi
on
\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{x}}
such that
\lambda_{k}=\phi(x_{k})
, for
k=1,\dots,d
.
Let
\Phi
be a ucp extension of
\phi
to

{\rm C}^{*}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{x}})
and consider a GNS decomposition
of
\Phi
: a representation
\pi:{\rm C}^{*}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{x}})\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{\pi})

on some Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{\pi}
and a unit vector
\xi\in\mathcal{H}_{\pi}
such that
\Phi(y)=\langle\pi(y)\xi,\xi\rangle
, for every

y\in{\rm C}^{*}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{x}})
.
Because
\lambda\in W\left(\pi(x_{1}),\dots,\pi(x_{d})\right)\subseteq W(\mathfrak{x})
, the element
\lambda
is an extreme point
of
W\left(\pi(x_{1}),\dots,\pi(x_{d})\right)
. Moreover, the element
\lambda
is
in the spatial numerical range of the
d
-tuple
(\pi(x_{1}),\dots,\pi(x_{d}))
,
which implies the unit vector
\xi
is an eigenvector for each
\pi(x_{k})
, with corresponding eigenvalue
\lambda_{k}

[
35
]
.
Because
\pi(x_{k})
is normal,
\xi
is an eigenvector for
\pi(x_{k}^{*})
as well. Hence,
\Phi
is multiplicative on

{\rm C}^{*}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{x}})
.
As
\Phi
agrees with
\phi
on the generating set,
\phi
has a unique ucp extension to
{\rm C}^{*}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{x}})
, implying

\phi
is a boundary representation.

\hfill\square

The C
∗
-envelope of
\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{x}}
is determined by its boundary representations, but whether

\partial_{\rm ext}W_{1}(\mathfrak{x})
is topologically closed
depends on the geometry of the
W_{1}(\mathfrak{x})
in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
.
However, in the case of

d=1
, we can take advantage of a property that holds in the complex plane, but can fail in higher dimensions:
if
K\subseteq\mathbb{C}
is compact and convex, then

\partial_{\rm ext}\,K

is topologically closed
[
51
, Theorem 4.4]
. In particular,
\partial_{\rm ext}W_{1}(x)
is a closed subset of
\mathbb{C}
,
for every
x
, normal or otherwise.

Theorem 3.3
.

If
\mathfrak{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})
is a
d
-tuple of
commuting normal elements, then

{\rm C}_{\rm min}^{*}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{x}})\cong C\left(\overline{\partial_{\rm ext}W_{1}(\mathfrak{x}})\right).

Proof.

Denote the abelian C
∗
-algebra
{\rm C}^{*}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{x}})
by
{\mathcal{A}}
, which may be considered as a
unital C
∗
-algebra its enveloping von Neumann algebra
{\mathcal{A}}^{**}
. Thus, we may view

\mathfrak{x}
as a
d
-tuple
\mathfrak{n}
of commuting normal operator
N_{1},\dots,N_{d}

acting on some complex Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
.

By the spectral theorem for commuting normal operators, there exists a spectral measure
e
on the Borel sets of the joint
spectrum
\sigma(\mathfrak{n})\subset\mathbb{C}^{d}
such that

f(\mathfrak{n})=\int_{\sigma(\mathfrak{n})}f\,de,

for every continuous function
f:\sigma(\mathfrak{n})\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
.
Let
\mathcal{E}
be the closure of
\partial_{\rm ext}W_{1}(\mathfrak{n})
, which is a subset of
\sigma(\mathfrak{n})
,
and let
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{E}}
be the range of the projection
e(\mathcal{E})
. The subspace
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{E}}
is invariant under
each
N_{k}
and
N_{k}^{*}
,
and so
N_{k}
decomposes as

N_{k}=N_{k}{}_{\mathcal{E}}\oplus N_{k}{}_{0}
, where
N_{k}{}_{\mathcal{E}}
is the restriction of
N_{k}
to
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{E}}
and

N_{k}{}_{0}
is the restriction of
N_{k}

to
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{E}}^{\bot}
. Thus,
\mathfrak{n}=\mathfrak{n}_{\mathcal{E}}\oplus\mathfrak{n}_{0}
, and

f(\mathfrak{n}_{\mathcal{E}})=\int_{\mathcal{E}}f\,de,

for every continuous function
f:\mathcal{E}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
.

Consider the linear map
\phi:\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{n}}\rightarrow\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{n}_{\mathcal{E}}}
given by

\phi\left(\alpha 1_{\mathcal{H}}+\sum_{k=1}^{d}\beta_{k}N_{k}+\sum_{k=1}^{d}\gamma_{k}N_{k}^{*}\right)=\alpha 1_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{E}}}+\sum_{k=1}^{d}\beta_{k}N_{k}{}_{\mathcal{E}}+\sum_{k=1}^{d}\gamma_{k}N_{k}{}_{\mathcal{E}}.

Because
\mathfrak{n}=\mathfrak{n}_{\mathcal{E}}\oplus\mathfrak{n}_{0}
, the linear
map
\phi
is a unital complete contraction.

To show
\phi
is a complete isometry, select
p\in\mathbb{N}
and
a matrix
X\in\mathcal{M}_{p}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{n}})
. Note that
X
can be expressed as

X=A\otimes 1_{\mathcal{H}}+\sum_{k=1}^{d}B_{k}\otimes N_{k}+\sum_{k=1}^{d}C_{k}\otimes N_{k}^{*},

for some complex matrices
A,B_{k},C_{k}\in\mathcal{M}_{p}(\mathbb{C})
. Applying the
p
-th ampliation
\phi^{[p]}
of
\phi
to
X
, we obtain

\phi^{[p]}(X)=A\otimes 1_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{E}}}+\sum_{k=1}^{d}B_{k}\otimes N_{k}{}_{\mathcal{E}}+\sum_{k=1}^{d}C_{k}\otimes N_{k}{}_{\mathcal{E}}^{*}.

By the noncommutative Choquet Theorem
[
5
,
17
]
,
the norm of
X
is determined by some boundary character
\rho:{\rm C}^{*}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{x}})\rightarrow\mathbb{C}

of
\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{x}}
:

(3.1)

\|X\|=\|A+\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{d}\rho(N_{k})B_{k}+\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{d}\overline{\rho(N_{k})}C_{k}\|.

However, in setting

\varrho=(\rho(N_{1}),\rho(N_{2}),\dots,\rho(N_{d}))
, we deduce from Theorem
3.2
that
\varrho\in\mathcal{E}
, and so

(3.2)

\|A+\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{d}\rho(N_{k})B_{k}+\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{d}\overline{\rho(N_{k})}C_{k}\|\leq\|\phi^{[p]}X\|.

Together, (
3.1
) and (
3.2
) combine to show
\phi
is a complete isometry.

Because
{\rm C}^{*}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{n}_{\mathcal{E}}})
is commutative
and the joint spectrum of
\mathfrak{n}_{\mathcal{E}}
is
\mathcal{E}
, Theorem
3.2
implies the Šilov boundary ideal in

{\rm C}^{*}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{n}_{\mathcal{E}}})

is trivial and, thus,

Hence,

.

\hfill\square

.

Corollary 3.6
.

4.
Spectrally-Extreme Operator
d
-Tuples

The main result of this section is a generalisation of our work in the previous section for commuting normal operators,
where extremal points of spectrum played a significant role. By the classical Weyl-von Neumann-Berg theorem

[
13
, Chapter II]
, a
d
-tuple
of commuting normal operators is approximately unitarily equivalent to a
d
-tuple of (commuting) diagonal operators, and such operators can
be viewed as being direct sums of
1\times 1
matrices.
The generalisation presented in this section
entails replacing extremal elements of the numerical range by matrix-extreme points of the matrix range, and using a notion of matrix-valued
(joint) spectrum in place of the classical joint spectrum of commuting normal operators.

Definition 4.1
.

If
\mathfrak{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})
is a
d
-tuple of elements from a unital C
∗
-algebra
{\mathcal{A}}
, then the

noncommutative reducing spectrum
of
\mathfrak{x}
is the graded set

R_{\rm nc}(\mathfrak{x})=\bigsqcup_{n\leq\aleph_{0}}R_{n}(\mathfrak{x}),

where, for
n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\aleph_{0}\}
,
R_{n}(\mathfrak{x})
consists of all
d
-tuples

\left(\pi(x_{1}),\dots,\pi(x_{d})\right)

determined by an irreducible representation of
{\rm C}^{*}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{x}})
on the Hilbert space
\mathbb{C}^{n}
, if
n\in\mathbb{N}
, or on the
Hilbert space
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N})
, if
n=\aleph_{0}
.
If we disregard the set
R_{\aleph_{0}}(\mathfrak{x})
, then the graded set

R_{\rm mtrx}(\mathfrak{x})=\bigsqcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}R_{n}(\mathfrak{x}),

is called the
reducing matricial spectrum
of
\mathfrak{x}
.

The definition of
R_{\rm mtrx}(\mathfrak{x})
and
R_{\rm nc}(\mathfrak{x})
above depart from analogous definitions in

[
8
,
31
]
in that here we require
\pi
to be an irreducible representation. We should also note that
for certain
\mathfrak{x}
, the graded set
R_{\rm mtrx}(\mathfrak{x})
is empty.

Definition 4.2
.

A
d
-tuple
\mathfrak{x}\in{\mathcal{A}}^{d}
is

convexoid
if
R_{\rm mtrx}(\mathfrak{x})\supseteq\overline{\partial_{\rm m-ext}W_{\rm mtrx}(\mathfrak{x})}
.

Recall that an operator
T
acting on a separable Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}

is
block diagonal
if there is a finite or countably infinite family of finite-dimensional Hilbert spaces
\mathcal{H}_{k}

such
\mathcal{H}=\bigoplus_{k}\mathcal{H}_{k}
and
T=\bigoplus_{k}T_{k}
, where is each
T_{k}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{k})
and

\sup_{k}\|T_{k}\|<\infty.

A similar notion applies to operator systems.

Definition 4.3
.

(
[
18
]
) A separable operator system
{\mathcal{R}}\subseteq\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})

is
block diagonal
if there exists a
countable family
\{\pi_{j}\}_{j\in\Lambda}
of finite-dimensional irreducible representations
\phi_{j}:{\rm C}^{*}({\mathcal{R}})\rightarrow\mathcal{M}_{n_{j}}(\mathbb{C})

and a unitary operator
u:\mathcal{H}\rightarrow\displaystyle\bigoplus_{j\in\Lambda}\mathbb{C}^{n_{j}}
such that

uxu^{*}=\bigoplus_{j\in\Lambda}\pi_{j}(x)
, for every
x\in{\mathcal{R}}
.

The following theorem, which is the main result of this section, shows the study of
d
-dimensional operator systems in the category

OpSys
may be reduced
the study of block-diagonal operator systems.

Theorem 4.4
.

If
\mathfrak{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})
is a
d
-tuple of elements in a unital C
∗
-algebra
{\mathcal{A}}
, then
there exists a separable Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
and a block diagonal operator system
\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{T}}

determined by a
d
-tuple
\mathfrak{T}

of block diagonal operators
T_{k}=\displaystyle\bigoplus_{j}T_{kj}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})

such that

(1)

\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{x}}\simeq\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{T}}
,

(2)

(T_{k1},\dots,T_{kd})
is a matrix extreme point of
W_{\rm mtrx}(\mathfrak{x})=W_{\rm mtrx}(\mathfrak{T})
, for every
k
, and

(3)

(T_{k1},\dots,T_{kd})
and
(T_{\ell 1},\dots,T_{\ell d})
are not unitarily equivalent, if
\ell\not=k
.

Proof.

The compact matrix convex set
W_{\rm mtrx}(\mathfrak{x})
is the closure of the matrix convex hull of
\partial_{\rm m-ext}W_{\rm mtrx}(\mathfrak{x})
, and the set
\partial_{\rm m-ext}W_{\rm mtrx}(\mathfrak{x})
has the property that

\mathfrak{a}\in\partial_{\rm m-ext}W(\mathfrak{x})
,
for some
\mathfrak{a}\in\mathcal{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})^{d}
, if and only if
u^{*}\mathfrak{a}u\in\partial_{\rm m-ext}W_{\rm mtrx}(\mathfrak{x})
, for every unitary
u
.
Let
\tilde{\partial}_{\rm m-ext}W_{\rm mtrx}(\mathfrak{x})
be a graded set containing
exactly one element from each unitary orbit of each matrix extreme point of
W(\mathfrak{x})
.

Select a countable graded set
\Lambda
of
\tilde{\partial}_{\rm m-ext}W_{\rm mtrx}(\mathfrak{x})
such that the closures of
\Lambda
and

\tilde{\partial}_{\rm m-ext}W_{\rm mtrx}(\mathfrak{x})
coincide. Hence, the closure of the matrix convex hull of
\Lambda
is
W_{\rm mtrx}(\mathfrak{x})
.
By using the correspondence between matrix extreme points and pure matrix states (Theorem
2.11
),
there is a countable subset
\Omega\subseteq\partial_{\rm m-ext}\,S\left(\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{x}}\right)

such that
\{(\phi(x_{1}),\dots,\phi(x_{d}))\,|\,\phi\in\Omega\}=\Lambda
. For each
\phi\in\Omega
, denote the co-domain of
\phi
by

\mathcal{M}_{n_{\phi}}(\mathbb{C})
, and construct the Hilbert space

\mathcal{H}=\bigoplus_{\phi\in\Omega}\mathbb{C}^{n_{\phi}}.

The linear map

\Phi:\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{x}}\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
in which

\Phi(x)=\bigoplus_{\phi\in\Omega}\phi(x)

is ucp, and let
T_{k}=\Phi(x_{k})
and
T_{k\phi}=\phi(x_{k})
, for each
\phi\in\Omega
.
To see that
\{\phi(x_{1}),\dots,\phi(x_{d})\}
is an irreducible set in
\mathcal{M}_{n_{\phi}}(\mathbb{C})
, let

\rho:{\rm C}^{*}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{x}})\rightarrow\mathcal{H}_{\rho}
be the minimal Stinespring representation of
\phi
; that is,

\phi=v^{*}\rho v
for some linear isometry
v:\mathbb{C}^{n_{\phi}}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}_{\rho}
. Since
\phi
is pure, the commutant of

\rho(\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{x}})
is one-dimensional. Hence, by the Commutant Lifting Theorem
[
3
, Corollary 1.3.2]
,
the commutant of
\phi(\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{x}})
in
\mathcal{M}_{n_{\phi}}
is one-dimensional,
implying
\{\phi(x_{1}),\dots\phi(x_{d}))\}
is irreducible in
\mathcal{M}_{n_{\phi}}(\mathbb{C})
.
Furthermore, because no two distinct elements of
\Lambda
are unitarily equivalent, the
d
-tuples

(T_{1\phi},\dots,T_{d\phi})
and
(T_{1\psi},\dots,T_{d\psi})
are not unitarily equivalent,
if
\phi
and
\psi
are distinct elements of
\Omega
.

The linear map
\Phi
is ucp; thus,
W_{\rm mtrx}(\mathfrak{T})\subseteq W_{\rm mtrx}(\mathfrak{x})
. Conversely, for every
\phi\in\Omega
,

(\phi(x_{1}),\dots\phi(x_{d}))\in W_{\rm mtrx}(\mathfrak{T})
, and so

\mbox{m-conv}\,\Lambda\subseteq W_{\rm mtrx}(\mathfrak{T})
. Therefore,

W_{\rm mtrx}(\mathfrak{x})=\overline{\mbox{m-conv}(\partial_{\rm m-ext}\,W_{\rm mtrx}(\mathfrak{x}))}=\overline{\mbox{m-conv}\,\Lambda}\subseteq W_{\rm mtrx}(\mathfrak{T}).

Hence,
W_{\rm mtrx}(\mathfrak{x})=W_{\rm mtrx}(\mathfrak{T})
, which proves (1).

\hfill\square

Corollary 4.5
.

For every
\mathfrak{x}\in{\mathcal{A}}^{d}
, there exists a convexoid operator
d
-tuple
\mathfrak{T}
such that

\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{x}}\simeq\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{T}}
.

Proof.

The elements
(T_{k1},\dots,T_{kd})
in Theorem
4.4
are both matrix extreme points
and elements of the reducing matricial spectrum. Furthermore, the graded set of all
(U^{*}T_{k1}U,\dots,U^{*}T_{kd}U)
,
where
U
is a unitary matrix of appropriate size, is dense in
\overline{\partial_{\rm m-ext}W_{\rm mtrx}(\mathfrak{T})}

and contained in the closed graded set
R_{\rm mtrx}(\mathfrak{T})
.

\hfill\square

The proof of Theorem
4.4
suggests there is a dependence on the choice of
dense subset of
\tilde{\partial}_{\rm m-ext}W_{\rm mtrx}(\mathfrak{x})

to produce the block-diagonal
d

\mathfrak{T}
. However, it is readily verified that

Theorem 4.6
.

If the block diagonal
d
-tuple
\mathfrak{T}=\displaystyle\bigoplus_{k\in\Omega}\mathfrak{T}_{k}
in Theorem
4.4

above has the property that all of the irreducible representations of
{\rm C}^{*}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{T}})

then there is a subset
\Omega^{\prime}\subseteq\Omega
such that, for the operator
d
-tuple

{\mathfrak{T}}^{\prime}=\displaystyle\bigoplus_{j\in\Omega^{\prime}}\mathfrak{T}_{j}
, we have

\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{T}^{\prime}}\simeq\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{T}}
, and

{\rm C}^{*}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{T}^{\prime}})={\rm C}_{\rm min}^{*}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{T}^{\prime}})={\rm C}_{\rm min}^{*}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{T}})

Proof.

By hypothesis, every boundary representation for
\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{T}}
occurs on a Hilbert space of finite dimension.
Therefore, if

\pi:{\rm C}^{*}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{T}})\rightarrow\mathcal{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
is a boundary representation for
\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{T}}
,
then
\pi_{|\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{T}}}
is pure
and
\pi(\mathfrak{T})\in\partial_{\rm m-ext}W_{\rm mtrx}(\mathfrak{T})
. By the

\Omega
,
\pi(\mathfrak{T})
is in the closure of the unitary orbit of some direct summand
\mathfrak{T}_{k}
of
\mathfrak{T}
.
Hence, the irreducible representation
\pi_{k}:{\rm C}^{*}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{T}})\rightarrow\mathcal{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
that sends

to
\mathfrak{T}_{k}

has the unique extension property

55
, Theorem 4.8]
. Since

\pi_{k}
is a boundary

\Omega^{\prime}\subseteq\Omega

is a boundary

. As every boundary representation of

5.
Simplexes, Automatic Complete Positivity, and Nuclearity

A
(classical) simplex
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a compact convex set
K
such that,
if one selects
\lambda\in\partial_{\rm ext}K
, then the set

\{\mu-\lambda\,|\,\mu\in\partial_{\rm ext}K,\,\mu\not=\lambda\}

is a set of linearly independent elements over
\mathbb{R}
.

Thus, if
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a simplex, then necessarily
\partial_{\rm ext}K
is a finite (and, hence, closed) set.

When moving to compact convex sets in spaces other than
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, a more robust
definition of simplex exists using the Choquet ordering, and this leads to a notion of simplex in noncommutative convexity.

Definition 5.1
.

[
39
]

A compact noncommutative convex set
K_{\rm nc}
over is
a
(Choquet) simplex
, if every element of
K_{\rm nc}

has a unique representing measure that is maximal in the Choquet order.

Let us return to the situation where

K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a compact, convex set.
Define two matrix convex sets,
K^{\rm min}
and
K^{\rm min}
, as follows.

Definition 5.2
.

If
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a compact convex set, then

(1)

K_{n}^{\rm max}
consists of all
d
-tuples
\mathfrak{a}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{d})

of selfadjoint
n\times n
matrices for which
W_{1}(\mathfrak{a})\subseteq K
.

(2)

K_{n}^{\rm min}
consists of all
d
-tuples
\mathfrak{a}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{d})

of selfadjoint
n\times n
matrices for which there exist positive

h_{1},\dots,h_{m}\in M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
and
\lambda_{j}=(\lambda_{j1},\dots,\lambda_{jd})\in K
, for
j=1,\dots,m
,
such that

1_{n}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}h_{j}\,\mbox{ and }\,a_{\ell}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\lambda_{j\ell}h_{j},\,\mbox{ for each }\ell=1,\dots,d.

The graded sets
K^{\rm min}=\displaystyle\bigsqcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}K_{n}^{\rm min}
and

K^{\rm max}=\displaystyle\bigsqcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}K_{n}^{\rm max}
are called the
minimal
and
maximal

matrix convex sets containing
K
.

It is clear that
K^{\rm min}\subseteq K^{\rm max}
. Therefore, define

\theta(K)
by

\theta(K)=\inf\{\alpha>0\,|\,K^{\rm max}\subseteq\alpha\cdot K^{\rm min}\},

or by
\theta(K)=\infty
, if no
\alpha>0
exists for which
K^{\rm max}\subseteq\alpha\cdot K^{\rm min}
.

Definition 5.3
.

An operator system
{\mathcal{R}}
is

(1)

nuclear
, if
{\mathcal{R}}\otimes_{\rm min}{\mathcal{T}}={\mathcal{R}}\otimes_{\rm max}{\mathcal{T}}
, for every operator system
{\mathcal{T}}
, and

(2)

an
OMAX system
, if every positive linear map
\phi:{\mathcal{R}}\rightarrow{\mathcal{T}}
, for every operator system
{\mathcal{T}}
,
is completely positive.

The minimal and maximal tensor product structures
\otimes_{\rm min}
and
\otimes_{\rm max}
, respectively,
on the algebraic tensor product space
{\mathcal{R}}\otimes{\mathcal{T}}
are analysed in detail in

[
38
]
.

Theorem 5.4
.

Assume
\mathfrak{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})
is a
d
-tuple of selfadjopint elements
in
{\mathcal{A}}
.
If
\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{x}}
is a nuclear operator system, then

\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{x}}
is an OMAX operator system,
the numerical range
W_{1}(\mathfrak{x})
of
\mathfrak{x}
is a simplex in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and

\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{x}}\cong{\rm C}_{\rm min}^{*}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{x}})\cong C\left({\partial_{\rm ext}W_{1}(\mathfrak{x})}\right).

Conversely, if
\mathfrak{x}
is such that
W_{1}(\mathfrak{x})
is a simplex in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, then
\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{x}}

is a nuclear operator system.

Proof.

If
\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{x}}
is a nuclear operator system, then its noncommutative state space
S_{\rm nc}\left(\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{x}}\right)

is a noncommutative simplex
[
39
, Corollary 5.2]
. Therefore, the state space

S_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{x}})
of
\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{x}}
is a simplex
[
39
, Proposition 4.4]
,
which implies the numerical range
W_{1}(\mathfrak{x})
is a simplex in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
The scaling constant
\theta(K)
for a simplex
K
is
\theta(K)=1

[
48
, Theorem 4.1]
, and so, by
[
48
, Proposition 2.1(2)]
,
every positive linear map
\phi:\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{x}}\rightarrow B(H)

is a ucp map. That is,
\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{x}}
is an OMAX operator system.

The finite-dimensionality of
\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{x}}
implies it is canonically unitally order isomorphic to its bidual,
which is an injective operator system
[
33
]
. Thus,
\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{x}}
is unitally completely order isomorphic
to a finite-dimensional C
∗
-algebra
\mathcal{B}
such that
\mathcal{B}
is an OMAX operator system. Because full matrix
algebras admit positive linear maps that are not completely positive,
\mathcal{B}
cannot have any direct summands that are nonabelian.
Hence,
\mathcal{B}
is abelian, which leads us to note

\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{x}}
is unitally completely order isomorphic a finite-dimensional abelian C
∗
-algebra (namely,
\mathcal{B}
).

To prove
C\left({\partial_{\rm ext}W_{1}(\mathfrak{x})}\right)\cong{\rm C}_{\rm min}^{*}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{x}})
,
let
b_{k}\in\mathcal{B}

be given by
b_{k}=\phi(x_{k})
, for
k=1,\dots,d
,
where
\phi:\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{x}}\rightarrow\mathcal{B}
is a unital complete order isomorphism. Hence,

\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{x}}\simeq\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{b}},

where
\mathfrak{b}=(b_{1},\dots,b_{d})
.

The elements
b_{1},\dots,b_{d}
are commuting selfadjoint operators with numerical range
W_{1}(\mathfrak{b})
;
thus, by Theorem
3.3
,

{\rm C}_{\rm min}^{*}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{b}})\cong C\left({\partial_{\rm ext}W_{1}(\mathfrak{b})}\right).

Hence,
{\rm C}_{\rm min}^{*}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{x}})\cong C\left({\partial_{\rm ext}W_{1}(\mathfrak{x})}\right)
.

It remains to prove that the operator system
\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{x}}
satisfies

\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{x}}\cong C\left({\partial_{\rm ext}W_{1}(\mathfrak{x})}\right)
.
This is an immediate consequence of the fact that
\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{b}}=\mathcal{B}
, which is a unital C
∗
-algebra; hence, the
C
∗
-envelope of
\mathcal{B}
is
\mathcal{B}
itself.

Conversely, let
K=W_{1}(\mathfrak{x})
, which we assume to be a simplex in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Thus,

K^{\rm max}=K^{\rm min}
as matrix convex sets
[
6
,
48
]
.
Therefore, there is a unique compact
noncommutative convex set
K_{\rm nc}
, for which
K
is the level
1
set in the grading, and
K_{\rm nc}
is a noncommutative simplex

[
39
, Proposition 4.4, Theorem 5.5]
. Hence, the operator
system
A_{\rm nc}(K_{\rm nc})
of continuous noncommutative affine functions on
K_{\rm nc}
has the property that the bidual

A_{\rm nc}(K_{\rm nc})^{dd}
is a von Neumann algebra.

The categorical duality between
{\sf OpSys}
and
{\sf NCConv}

implies the operator system
A_{\rm nc}(K_{\rm nc})
is unitally complete order isomorphic to an operator system
{\mathcal{R}}
whose
noncommutative state space is
K_{\rm nc}

[
16
]
. Because the matrix state space of
\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{x}}

is matrix affinely homeomorphic to
K^{\rm max}=K^{\rm min}
, we deduce that
\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{x}}^{dd}
is unitally completely order
isomorphic to a finite-dimensional
von Neumann algebra, which is a nuclear object in the category
{\sf OpSys}

[
38
, Corollary 6.8]
.
Because
\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{x}}\simeq\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{x}}^{dd}
,
we deduce that
\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{x}}
itself is nuclear operator system.

\hfill\square

6.
Nuclearity in Tensor Products with C
∗
-Algebras

The previous section demonstrates that the nuclearity of an operator system
\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{x}}
is a rather special property,
closely connected to the geometry of the joint numerical range of
\mathfrak{x}
.
There are, however, weaker notions of nuclearity in the
category
{\sf OpSys}
that occur through the
consideration of tensor products of operator systems with unital C
∗
-algebras.

6.1.
C
∗
-nuclear operator systems

Definition 6.1
.

An operator system
{\mathcal{R}}
is
C
∗
-nuclear
if

{\mathcal{R}}\otimes_{\rm min}{\mathcal{A}}={\mathcal{R}}\otimes_{\rm max}{\mathcal{A}}
, for every unital C
∗
-algebra
{\mathcal{A}}
.

If
x^{*}=x
, then
\mathcal{O}_{x}
is
2
-dimensional and, hence, C
∗
-nuclear
[
36
, Proposition 6.13]
. In the cases where
x

is nonselfadjoint, it is not known whether every operator system
\mathcal{O}_{x}
is C
∗
-nuclear.

We begin with the following known result
[
36
,
37
]
; see also

[
25
, Section 8]
or
[
26
, Proposition 6.12]
.

Theorem 6.2
.

The following
3
-dimensional operator systems are C
∗
-nuclear:

(1)

\mathcal{O}_{u}
, where
u\in{\mathcal{A}}
is unitary with spectrum
\sigma(u)=S^{1}
;

(2)

\mathcal{O}_{x}
, where
x=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}0&0\\
1&0\end{array}\right]\in\mathcal{M}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
.

The following two results are consequences of Theorem
6.2
.

Theorem 6.3
.

If
x\in{\mathcal{A}}
is such that
\|x\|\leq 1
and
S^{1}\subseteq\sigma(x)
, then

\mathcal{O}_{x}
is C
∗
-nuclear and
{\rm C}^{*}_{e}(\mathcal{O}_{x})=C(S^{1})
.

Proof.

By
[
24
, Proposition 5.2]
, all pure matrix states
\rho
on
\mathcal{O}_{x}
are one-dimensional with
\phi(x)\in S^{1}
. Conversely, every

\zeta\in S^{1}
is an extreme point of the numerical range
W_{1}(x)
, and therefore
\zeta=\rho(x)
, for some character
\rho:{\rm C}^{*}(x)\rightarrow\mathbb{C}

[
3
, Theorem 3.1.2]
. Hence, the matrix range
W_{\rm mtrx}(x)
of
x
is
the closure of the matrix convex hull of
S^{1}
. Let
u\in\mathbb{B}
be a unitary operator with spectrum
S^{1}
; hence,
matrix range
W_{\rm mtrx}(u)
is the
closure of the matrix convex hull of
S^{1}
. Thus,
W(x)=W(u)
, which implies
\mathcal{O}_{x}\simeq\mathcal{O}_{u}
, and so the
C
∗
-envelopes of
\mathcal{O}_{x}
and
\mathcal{O}_{u}
coincide. From Theorem
3.8
,
we have
{\rm C}^{*}_{e}(\mathcal{O}_{u})=C^{*}(u)\simeq C(S^{1})
. The C
∗
-nuclearity of
\mathcal{O}_{x}
follows from that of
\mathcal{O}_{u}

(Theorem
6.2
).

\hfill\square

Theorem 6.4
.

If
x\in\mathcal{M}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
, then
\mathcal{O}_{x}
is C
∗
-nuclear.

Proof.

As every
1
- or
2
-dimensional operator system is C
∗
-nuclear, we assume

\mathcal{O}_{x}
is
3
-dimensional.

Because every matrix is unitarily similar to a matrix with constant diagonal entries
[
45
]
,
there exists a unitary
u\in\mathcal{M}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
such that
u^{*}xu=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\alpha&\beta\\
\gamma&\alpha\end{array}\right]
. Because
\mathcal{O}_{u^{*}xu}=\mathcal{O}_{y}
for the
matrix
y=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}0&0\\
1&0\end{array}\right]
, we have

\mathcal{O}_{x}\simeq\mathcal{O}_{y}
. Hence, by Theorem
6.2
,
\mathcal{O}_{y}
is C
∗
-nuclear.

\hfill\square

Definition 6.5
.

An element
x\in{\mathcal{A}}
is
quadratic
if there exists
f\in\mathbb{C}[t]
of degree
2
such that
f(x)=0
.

By making use of matrix ranges, Theorem
6.4
extends to general quadratic elements, as stated in Theorem
6.7
below. To prove this,
the following result will be useful.

Lemma 6.6
.

Let
{\mathcal{R}}
be an operator subsystem of a unital C
∗
-algebra
{\mathcal{A}}
, and let

\iota_{\rm e}:{\mathcal{R}}\rightarrow{\rm C}_{\rm min}^{*}({\mathcal{R}})
be the canonical embedding of
{\mathcal{R}}
into its C
∗
-envelope

{\rm C}_{\rm min}^{*}({\mathcal{R}})
. If
x\in{\mathcal{R}}
and
\lambda\in\sigma\left(\iota_{\rm e}(x)\right)
, then
\lambda\in\sigma(x)
.

Proof.

Let
y=\iota_{\rm e}(x)
.
The complex number
\lambda
belongs to the left spectrum or right spectrum of
y
. Without loss of generality, assume

\lambda
is in the left spectrum of
y
. Hence, there exists a state
\psi
on
{\rm C}_{\rm min}^{*}({\mathcal{R}})
such that
\psi(y)=\lambda
and

\psi(y^{*}y)=\psi(y^{*})\psi(y)
. Now consider a state
\phi
on
{\rm C}^{*}(x)
that extends the state
\psi\circ\iota_{\rm e}

on
{\mathcal{R}}
, and note that
\phi(x)=\lambda
and

\phi(x^{*}x)=\phi(x^{*})\phi(x)
. Hence,
\lambda
is in the left spectrum of
x
.

\hfill\square

Theorem 6.7
.

Assume
x\in{\mathcal{A}}
is a quadratic element.

(1)

If
x
is normal, then
{\rm C}_{\rm min}^{*}(\mathcal{O}_{x})=\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{C}
.

(2)

If
x
is non-normal, then
{\rm C}_{\rm min}^{*}(\mathcal{O}_{x})=\mathcal{M}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
.

In both cases,
\mathcal{O}_{x}
is C
∗
-nuclear.

Proof.

By the polynomial spectral mapping theorem, the spectrum of a quadratic element
x
has at most two points, which correspond to the roots
of the degree-2 annihilating polynomial
f\in\mathbb{C}[t]
of
x
.

If
x
is normal, then there must be two distinct spectral points, for otherwise

x
would be linear rather than quadratic. By Theorem
5.4
,
\mathcal{O}_{x}
is nuclear (and, hence, C
∗
-nuclear)
and
{\rm C}_{\rm min}^{*}(\mathcal{O}_{x})=\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{C}
.

Therefore, assume now that
x
is non-normal.
By
[
24
, Proposition 5.9]
, the pure matrix states
\phi
on
\mathcal{O}_{x}
are either

(a)

one-dimensional, with
\phi(x)
an extreme point of
W_{1}(x)
, or

(b)

two-dimensional, with
W_{1}(\phi(x))=W_{1}(x)
.

If every pure matrix state were one-dimensional, then the matrix range of
x
would be the closure of the matrix convex hull of the topological boundary

\partial W_{1}(x)
of the numerical range
W_{1}(x)
of
x
, and would therefore coincide the with matrix range of a normal operator
N

with spectrum
\partial W_{1}(x)
. Hence,
\mathcal{O}_{x}\simeq\mathcal{O}_{N}
. Because
{\rm C}_{\rm min}^{*}(\mathcal{O}_{N})
is a quotient of
{\rm C}^{*}(N)
, the element

\iota_{\rm e}^{\mathcal{O}_{x}}(x)=\iota_{\rm e}^{\mathcal{O}_{N}}(N)
is normal in
{\rm C}_{\rm min}^{*}(\mathcal{O}_{N})
. By Lemma
6.6
, the spectral points
of
\iota_{\rm e}^{\mathcal{O}_{x}}(x)
are spectral points of
x
; thus, the normal element
\iota_{\rm e}^{\mathcal{O}_{N}}(N)
has one or two points of spectrum,
implying its numerical range,
W_{1}(N)=W_{1}(x)
, is a point or a line segment, which contradicts the non-normality of
x
.
Hence,
\mathcal{O}_{x}
admits at least one pure matrix state with values in
\mathcal{M}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
.

Assume
\phi:\mathcal{O}_{x}\rightarrow\mathcal{M}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
is a pure matrix states, and let
a=\phi(x)
. As indicated in (b) above, the numerical range

W_{1}(a)
of
a
coincides with that of
x
. Because the numerical range of
2\times 2
matrices is a complete invariant under unitary similarity,
every other pure matrix state
\psi
on
\mathcal{O}_{x}
with values in
\mathcal{M}_{2}(\mathbb{C})

has the form
\psi=w^{*}\phi w
, for some unitary
w\in\mathcal{M}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
. Hence,

\partial_{\rm m-ext}W_{\rm mtrx}(x)\subseteq W_{\rm mtrx}(a)\subseteq W_{\rm mtrx}(x).

In taking the closure of the matrix convex hull of the lefthand side above, we obtain
W_{\rm mtrx}(x)=W_{\rm mtrx}(a)
,
whence
\mathcal{O}_{x}\simeq\mathcal{O}_{a}
.
By Theorem
6.4
,
\mathcal{O}_{a}
is C
∗
-nuclear and has C
∗
-envelope
\mathcal{M}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
. Hence, the same is true of

\mathcal{O}_{x}
.

\hfill\square

The second assertion of Theorem
6.7
does not have an extension to cubic elements. For example, if
x_{0}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
is invertible,
then
{\rm C}^{*}(x)\cong\mathcal{M}_{2}(\mathbb{C})={\rm C}_{\rm min}^{*}(\mathcal{O}_{x})
, where

x=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}0&x_{0}\\
0&0\end{array}\right].

However, if

x=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}0&1&x_{0}\\
0&0&1\\
0&0&0\end{array}\right],

then
{\rm C}^{*}(x)\cong\mathcal{M}_{3}(\mathbb{C})\otimes{\rm C}^{*}(x_{0})

[
3
]
. Thus, if
{\rm C}^{*}(x_{0})
is simple, then so is
{\rm C}^{*}(x)
,
which yields
{\rm C}_{\rm min}^{*}(\mathcal{O}_{x})={\rm C}^{*}(x)
, which will not generally coincide with
\mathcal{M}_{3}(\mathbb{C})
.

The operator systems in Theorems
6.3
and
6.7
are closely tied to circles and ellipses, respectively,
through their numerical ranges. One may also consider a square, which arises not from a
4\times 4
normal matrix, but from the
numerical range of
x=u+iv
, where
u
and
v
are free symmetries.

Theorem 6.8
.

If
\mathfrak{u}=(u_{1},u_{2})
, where

u_{1},u_{2}\in\mathbb{B}_{\rm sa}
are free symmetries, then
\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{u}}
is a C
∗
-nuclear and OMAX operator system.

Proof.

The operator subsystem
NC(2)=\mbox{\rm Span}\{1,u_{1},u_{2}\}
, known as the noncommutative
square
[
27
]
, coincides with
\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{u}}
and has C
∗
-envelope

{\rm C}^{*}(\mathbb{Z}_{2})*{\rm C}^{*}(\mathbb{Z}_{2})

[
27
, Corollary 6.6]
. Because
NC(2)
is C
∗
-nuclear

[
27
, Theorem 7.3]
, so is
\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{u}}
. The operator system dual
NC(2)^{d}
of
NC(2)

is an operator subsystem of the C
∗
-algebra

\mathbb{C}^{4}

[
27
, Proposition 6.11]
, which is abelian.
Thus, all positive linear maps into
NC(2)^{d}
are completely positive,
which implies all positive linear maps with domain
NC(2)
are completely positive.
Hence,
\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{u}}
is an OMAX operator system.

\hfill\square

Because
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
are free symmetries, the joint matrix range
W_{\rm mtrx}(\mathfrak{u})
consists of all pairs of selfadjoint matrices
(h,k)
for
which
\|h\|\leq 1
and
\|k\|\leq 1
. In particular,
W_{1}(\mathfrak{u})
is the square
[-1,1]\times[-1,1]\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}=\mathbb{C}
.

It should also be noted that
\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{u}}
, where
\mathfrak{u}
is a
d
-tuple of free symmetries, for
d\geq 3
,
is not C
∗
-nuclear

[
28
, Theorem 4.3]
. However,
\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{u}}
is WEP-nuclear, as defined below.

6.2.
WEP-nuclear operator systems

Definition 6.9
.

A unital C
∗
-algebra has the weak expectation property (WEP) if, for every unital representation
\pi:{\mathcal{A}}\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{\pi})
,
there exists a ucp map
\phi:\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{\pi})\rightarrow\pi({\mathcal{A}})^{{}^{\prime\prime}}
such that
\phi\circ\pi=\pi
.

The weak expectation property may be viewed as a weaker form of nuclearity in the category of C
∗
-algebras. Interestingly,
one may use operator system tensor products to characterise the weak expectation property for unital C
∗
-algebras, as is done
in
[
28
]
.

Definition 6.10
.

An operator system
{\mathcal{R}}
is
WEP-nuclear
if
{\mathcal{R}}\otimes_{\rm min}{\mathcal{A}}={\mathcal{R}}\otimes_{\rm max}{\mathcal{A}}
, for every unital C
∗
-algebra

{\mathcal{A}}
with the weak expectation property.

Of course, every nuclear or C
∗
-nuclear operator system is WEP-nuclear. Some operator systems that are not C
∗
-nuclear, but are WEP-nuclear,
are given by free symmetries
[
29
, Theorem 6.3]
and
free unitaries
[
29
, Theorem 6.3]
.

Theorem 6.11
.

If
\mathfrak{w}
is
d
-tuple of free unitaries and
\mathfrak{u}
is a
d
-tuple of free symmetries,
then
\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{w}}
and
\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{u}}
are WEP-nuclear operator systems.

The class of unital C
∗
-algebras with the weak expectation property includes
all nuclear C
∗
-algebras and injective von Neumann algebras. Because
\mathbb{B}
is an injective von Neumann algebra,
a WEP-nuclear operator system
{\mathcal{R}}
admits only one
operator system structure on the algebraic tensor product
{\mathcal{R}}\otimes\mathbb{B}
. If such happens for an arbitrary operator system
{\mathcal{R}}
,
we call that operator system
\mathbb{B}
-nuclear.

6.3.
\mathbb{B}
-nuclear operator systems and the lifting property

Definition 6.12
.

An operator system
{\mathcal{R}}
is
\mathbb{B}
-nuclear
if
{\mathcal{R}}\otimes_{\rm min}\mathbb{B}={\mathcal{R}}\otimes_{\rm max}\mathbb{B}
.

An important result of Kavruk (see Theorem
6.15
below)
shows that
\mathbb{B}
-nuclearity is equivalent to the lifting property, if one restricts to operator systems of finite dimension.

Definition 6.13
.

An operator system
\mathcal{S}
has the
lifting property
if, for every unital C
∗
-algebra
\mathcal{A}
and ideal

\mathcal{J}
of
\mathcal{A}
, and
every ucp map
\phi:\mathcal{S}\rightarrow\mathcal{A}/\mathcal{J}
,
there exists a ucp map
\tilde{\phi}:\mathcal{S}\rightarrow\mathcal{A}
, called a
lift
of
\phi
to
{\mathcal{A}}
, such that
\phi=\pi_{\mathcal{J}}\circ\tilde{\phi}
, where

\pi_{\mathcal{J}}
denotes the canonical quotient homomorphism
\mathcal{A}\rightarrow\mathcal{A}/\mathcal{J}
.

We shall use the following elementary fact frequently, and so it is stated formally below.

Lemma 6.14
.

If an operator system
\mathcal{S}
has the lifting property, then so does every operator system
\mathcal{R}
which is unitally completely order
isomorphic to
\mathcal{S}
.

Proof.

By hypothesis, there exists a ucp bijection
\psi:\mathcal{S}\rightarrow\mathcal{R}

such that
\psi^{-1}
is completely positive. Let
\mathcal{J}
be an ideal of a unital C
∗
-algebra
\mathcal{A}
, and suppose

\phi:\mathcal{R}\rightarrow\mathcal{A}/\mathcal{J}
is a ucp map. Then

\psi\circ\phi:\mathcal{S}\rightarrow\mathcal{A}/\mathcal{J}
is a ucp map. By hypothesis,
\psi\circ\phi
has a ucp lift
\beta
to
\mathcal{A}
;
thus,
\pi_{\mathcal{J}}\left(\beta(s)\right)=\phi\circ\psi(s)
, for every
s\in\mathcal{S}
.
Define
\tilde{\phi}:\mathcal{R}\rightarrow\mathcal{A}
by
\tilde{\phi}=\beta\circ\psi^{-1}
. The map
\tilde{\phi}
is ucp, and if
r\in\mathcal{R}
, then

\pi_{\mathcal{J}}\left(\tilde{\phi}(r)\right)=\pi_{\mathcal{J}}\left(\beta(\psi^{-1}(r))\right)=\phi\circ\psi\left(\psi^{-1}(r)\right)=\psi(r),

which shows
\tilde{\phi}
is a lift of
\phi
.

\hfill\square

A sufficient condition, and a necessary and sufficient condition, for the lifting property are known.

Theorem 6.15

(Kavruk)
.

Suppose that
{\mathcal{R}}
is a finite-dimensional operator system.

(1)

If
{\mathcal{R}}
is an OMAX operator system, then
{\mathcal{R}}
has the lifting property
[
36
, Lemma 9.10]
.

(2)

{\mathcal{R}}\otimes_{\rm min}\mathbb{B}={\mathcal{R}}\otimes_{\rm max}\mathbb{B}
if and only if
{\mathcal{R}}
has the lifting property
[
36
, Theorem 4.3]
.

Corollary 6.16
.

Every finite-dimensional C
∗
-nuclear operator system has the lifting property.

Corollary 6.17
.

Every finite-dimensional WEP-nuclear operator system has the lifting property. In particular, if

\mathfrak{w}
is
d
-tuple of free unitaries and
\mathfrak{u}
is a
d
-tuple of free symmetries,
then the operator systems
\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{w}}
and
\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{u}}
have the lifting property.

Proof.

Because
\mathbb{B}
is an injective von Neumann algebra,
\mathbb{B}

has the weak expectation property; hence, every WEP-nuclear operator system has the lifting property.
In particular,
\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{w}}
and
\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{u}}
have the lifting property, by Theorem
6.11
.

\hfill\square

7.
The Smith-Ward Property

Recall the canonical quotient homomorphism
\mathbb{B}\rightarrow\mathbb{B}/\mathbb{K}

is denoted by
\pi_{\mathbb{K}}
and that, for notational simplicity, if
T\in\mathbb{B}
, then
\dot{T}
denotes
\pi_{\mathbb{K}}(T)
.

Definition 7.1
.

A
d
-tuple
\mathfrak{A}=(A_{1},\dots,A_{d})
of selfadjoint operators
A_{j}\in\mathbb{B}_{\rm sa}

has the
Smith-Ward property
if the inclusion map
\iota:\mathcal{O}_{\dot{\mathfrak{A}}}\rightarrow\mathbb{B}/\mathbb{K}

has a completely positive lifting to
\mathbb{B}
.

In the case of
d=2
, we sometimes replace the selfadjoint pair
(A_{1},A_{2})
with the (nonselfadjoint) operator
T=A_{1}+iA_{2}
; in so doing, we have

\mathcal{O}_{(A_{1},A_{2})}=\mathcal{O}_{T}
and
\mathcal{O}_{(\dot{A}_{1},\dot{A}_{2})}=\mathcal{O}_{\dot{T}}
.

Observe that Smith-Ward property for a
d
-tuple
\mathfrak{A}
of selfadjoint operators requires the corresponding operator subsystem

\mathcal{O}_{\dot{\mathfrak{A}}}
of the Calkin algebra
\mathbb{B}/\mathbb{K}
to have a property reminiscent of the lifting property; the difference here
is that only the inclusion map
\iota:\mathcal{O}_{\dot{\mathfrak{A}}}\rightarrow\mathbb{B}/\mathbb{K}
need have a lifting to
\mathbb{B}
, rather than every
ucp map
\psi:\mathcal{O}_{\dot{\mathfrak{A}}}\rightarrow\mathbb{B}/\mathbb{K}
.

In the case
d=1
, all operator systems
\mathcal{O}_{\dot{\mathfrak{A}}}
have the Smith-Ward property; whereas, for all
d\geq 4
, Paulsen has shown
in
[
50
, Theorem 3.3]
that there are

d
-tuples
\mathfrak{A}
for which
\mathcal{O}_{\dot{\mathfrak{A}}}
does not have the Smith-Ward property.
The Smith-Ward Problem concerns the cases
d=2
and
d=3
.

Smith-Ward Problem:
Does every pair or triple of selfadjoint operators in
\mathbb{B}
have the Smith-Ward property?

The Smith-Ward Problem originates in the work of Smith and Ward
[
53
,
54
]
, and it is usually formulated
in terms of essential matrix ranges.

7.1.
Essential matrix ranges

A noncommutative state
\phi
on a unital C
∗
-subalgebra
{\mathcal{A}}\subseteq\mathbb{B}
is said to be
essential
if

\phi(K)=0
, for every compact operator
K\in{\mathcal{A}}
.

Definition 7.2
.

[
8
]
Assume
\mathfrak{A}=(A_{1},\dots,A_{d})
is a
d
-tuple of selfadjoint
operators
A_{j}\in\mathbb{B}_{\rm sa}
.

(1)

The
essential matrix range
of
\mathfrak{A}
is the graded set

W_{\rm ess-mtrx}(\mathfrak{A})=\bigsqcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}W_{{\rm ess},n}(\mathfrak{A}),

where
W_{{\rm ess},n}(\mathfrak{A})
is the set of all
d
-tuples of
n\times n
matrices
\mathfrak{a}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{d})

in which each
a_{j}
is given by
a_{j}=\phi(A_{j})
, for some essential matrix state
\phi
on
{\rm C}^{*}({\mathfrak{A}})
.

(2)

The
essential noncommutative numerical range
of
\mathfrak{A}
is the graded set

W_{\rm ess-nc}(\mathfrak{A})=\bigsqcup_{n\leq\aleph_{0}}W_{{\rm ess},n}(\mathfrak{A}),

where
W_{{\rm ess},\aleph_{0}}(\mathfrak{A})
is the set of all
d
-tuples of operators
\mathfrak{a}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{d})

in which each
a_{j}
is given by
a_{j}=\phi(A_{j})
, for some essential noncommutative state
\phi:{\rm C}^{*}({\mathfrak{A}})\rightarrow\mathbb{B}
.

Alternative descriptions of essential noncommutative and matrix ranges are given by the next result.

Theorem 7.3
.

If
\mathfrak{A}\in\mathbb{B}_{\rm sa}^{d}
is a selfadjoint operator
d
-tuple, then

W_{\rm ess-nc}(\mathfrak{A})=W_{\rm nc}(\dot{\mathfrak{A}})

and

W_{\rm ess-mtrx}(\mathfrak{A})=W_{\rm mtrx}(\dot{\mathfrak{A}})=\bigcap_{\mathfrak{K}\in\mathbb{K}_{\rm sa}^{d}}W_{\rm mtrx}(\mathfrak{A}+\mathfrak{K}).

Proof.

If
\psi
is a noncommutative state on
\mathcal{O}_{\dot{\mathfrak{A}}}
, then
\psi

induces an essential noncommutative state
\phi

on
\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{A}}
via
\phi=\psi\circ\pi_{\mathbb{K}}
. This proves that
W_{\rm nc}(\dot{\mathfrak{A}})\subseteq W_{\rm ess-nc}(\mathfrak{A})
.

Conversely, if
\phi
is an essential noncommutative state on
\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{A}}
, then define a function

\psi
on
\mathcal{O}_{\dot{\mathfrak{A}}}
by
\psi(\dot{X})=\phi(X)
, for every
\dot{X}\in\mathcal{O}_{\dot{\mathfrak{A}}}
.
This is a well-defined function, since if
\dot{X}_{1}=\dot{X}_{2}
, then
X_{1}=X_{2}+K
, for some compact
K
,
which implies
\phi(X_{1})=\phi(X_{2})
. Thus,
\psi
is a ucp map, which proves

W_{\rm ess-nc}(\mathfrak{A})\subseteq W_{\rm mtrx}(\dot{\mathfrak{A}})
.

The equality of
W_{\rm ess-mtrx}(\mathfrak{A})

and
\displaystyle\bigcap_{\mathfrak{K}\in\mathbb{K}_{\rm sa}^{d}}W_{\rm mtrx}(\mathfrak{A}+\mathfrak{K})

is established in
[
43
]
.

\hfill\square

The connection between matrix ranges and the Smith-Ward is described by the following basic result, noted in
[
50
]
for selfadjoint pairs.

Proposition 7.4
.

If
\mathfrak{A}=(A_{1},\dots,A_{d})
is
d
-tuple of
A_{j}\in\mathbb{B}_{\rm sa}
, then
the
operator system
\mathcal{O}_{\dot{\mathfrak{A}}}
has the Smith-Ward property
if and only if there exists a
d
-tuple
\mathfrak{K}
of selfadjoint compact operators
such that
W_{\rm mtrix}(\dot{\mathfrak{A}})=W_{\rm mtrx}(\mathfrak{A}+\mathfrak{K})
.

Proof.

Assume the inclusion map
\iota:\mathcal{O}_{\dot{\mathfrak{A}}}\rightarrow\mathbb{B}/\mathbb{K}

has a completely positive lifting
\tilde{\iota}
to
\mathbb{B}
.
For each
k
, the operator
\tilde{\iota}(\dot{T}_{k})\in\mathbb{B}
satisfies
\pi_{\mathbb{K}}\left(\tilde{\iota}(\dot{A}_{k})\right)=\dot{A}_{k}
. Thus,

A_{k}-\tilde{\iota}(\dot{A}_{k})\in\ker\pi_{\mathbb{K}}=\mathbb{K}
, which implies
\tilde{\iota}(\dot{A}_{k})=A_{k}+K_{k}
, for some compact operator
K_{k}
.
Let
\mathfrak{K}=(K_{1},\dots,K_{d})\in\mathbb{K}^{d}
. The equation
\tilde{\iota}(\dot{A}_{k})=A_{k}+K_{k}
implies each
K_{k}

is selfadjoint and

W_{\rm mtrx}(\mathfrak{A}+\mathfrak{K})\subseteq W_{\rm mtrx}(\dot{\mathfrak{A}})
.

Conversely,
assume there exists a
d
-tuple
\mathfrak{K}
of selfadjoint compact operators
such that
W_{\rm mtrix}(\dot{\mathfrak{A}})=W_{\rm mtrx}(\mathfrak{A}+\mathfrak{K})
. Hence, there is a unital complete order isomorphism

\phi:\mathcal{O}_{\dot{\mathfrak{A}}}\rightarrow\mathcal{O}_{{\mathfrak{A}+\mathfrak{K}}}
implementing the isomorphism

\mathcal{O}_{\dot{\mathfrak{A}}}\simeq\mathcal{O}_{{\mathfrak{A}+\mathfrak{K}}}
and such that

\phi
is a lift of the inclusion map
\iota:\mathcal{O}_{\dot{\mathfrak{A}}}\rightarrow\mathbb{B}/\mathbb{K}
.

\hfill\square

We now show the lifting of the identity map in the Calkin algebra is invariant under unital complete order isomorphism.

Proposition 7.5
.

If
\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{S}\in\mathbb{B}_{\rm sa}^{d}
are
d
-tuples of selfadjoint operators such that

\mathcal{O}_{\dot{\mathfrak{A}}}\simeq\mathcal{O}_{\dot{\mathfrak{S}}}
, then
\mathfrak{A}
has the Smith-Ward property if and only if

\mathfrak{S}
has the Smith-Ward property.

Proof.

Assume
\mathfrak{A}
has the Smith-Ward property and let
\phi:\mathcal{O}_{\dot{\mathfrak{S}}}\rightarrow\mathcal{O}_{\dot{\mathfrak{A}}}
be a unital complete order isomorphism. By hypothesis, there is a ucp lift

\psi:\mathcal{O}_{\dot{\mathfrak{A}}}\rightarrow\mathbb{B}
such that
q_{\mathbb{K}}\circ\psi(\dot{A}_{j})=\dot{A}_{j}
, for each
j
.
As shown in the proof of Proposition
7.4
, there are compact operators
K_{j}
such that
\psi(\dot{A}_{j})=A_{j}+K_{j}
.
Hence,

W_{\rm mtrx}(\dot{\mathfrak{S}})=W_{\rm mtrx}(\dot{\mathfrak{A}})=W_{\rm mtrx}(\mathfrak{A}+\mathfrak{K}).

By Theorem
7.3
,
W_{\rm mtrx}(\dot{\mathfrak{S}})\subseteq W_{\rm mtrx}(\mathfrak{S}+\mathfrak{K})
. Hence, the
inclusion

W_{\rm mtrx}(\mathfrak{A}+\mathfrak{K})=W_{\rm mtrx}(\dot{\mathfrak{S}})\subseteq W_{\rm mtrx}(\mathfrak{S}+\mathfrak{K})

implies there exists a ucp
\delta:\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{A}+\mathfrak{K}}\rightarrow\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{S}+\mathfrak{K}}

such that
\delta(A_{j}+K_{j})=S_{j}+K_{j}
, for each
j
. Thus, the ucp map
\delta\circ\psi\circ\phi
is a ucp lift of the identity map

\iota:\mathcal{O}_{\dot{\mathfrak{S}}}\rightarrow\mathbb{B}/\mathbb{K}
.

Interchanging the roles of
\mathfrak{A}
and
\mathfrak{S}
completes the proof.

\hfill\square

Although the Smith-Ward Problem remains open, and some tuples of operators do not have the Smith-Ward property,
all
d
-tuples of selfadjoint operators enjoy the following local Smith-Ward property.

Theorem 7.6

(Local Smith-Ward Property)
.

(
[
43
]
)

If
\mathfrak{A}=(A_{1},\dots,A_{d})
is a fixed
d
-tuple of selfadjoint operators,
and if
q\in\mathbb{N}
, then there is a
d
-tuple

\mathfrak{K}
of compact selfadjoint
operators such that

(7.1)

W_{{\rm ess},n}(\mathfrak{A})=W_{n}(\mathfrak{A}+\mathfrak{K}),\,\mbox{ for all }1\leq n\leq q.

As indicated in
[
52
]
, Theorem
7.6
above has the following stronger form:
for every
\mathfrak{A}\in\mathbb{B}_{\rm sa}^{d}
and

q\in\mathbb{N}
, there is a linear map
\tau_{q}:\mbox{Span}\{A_{1},\dots,A_{d}\}\rightarrow\mathbb{K}
such that, if
\mathfrak{K}_{q}=(\tau_{q}(A_{1}),\dots,\tau_{q}(A_{d}))
,
then
W_{{\rm ess},n}(\mathfrak{A})=W_{n}(\mathfrak{A}+\mathfrak{K}_{q})
for each
n\leq q
.

In passing from pairs of selfadjoint operators
(A_{1},A_{2})
to a single operator
T=A_{1}+iA_{2}
, the Smith-Ward Problem takes on the following form.

Smith-Ward Problem for Single Operators:
for every
T\in\mathbb{B}
,
is
\dot{T}\in\mathbb{B}/\mathbb{K}
determined, as an object in
the category
OpSys
, by a compact perturbation of
T
?

In the formulation above, “as an object in
OpSys
” means as a
3
-dimensional operator system, up to unital complete order isomorphism.

7.2.
The Smith-Ward property for commuting selfadjoint operators

In this section we show that all
d
-tuples of commuting selfadjoint operators have the Smith-Ward property.

Definition 7.7
.

The
joint essential spectrum
of a
d
-tuple
\mathfrak{A}
of commuting selfadjoint operators
A_{1},\dots,A_{k}

is the set
\sigma_{\rm ess}(\mathfrak{A})
of all
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
for which
there exists an essential state
\rho
on
\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{A}}

such that
\rho(A_{k})=\lambda_{k}
, for each
k
, and
\rho
is mutliplicative on
{\rm C}^{*}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{A}})
.

Note that the joint spectrum of a
d
-tuple
\mathfrak{A}
of commuting selfadjoint operators is defined by using states

\phi
on
\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{A}}
that are multiplicative on
{\rm C}^{*}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{A}})
, and so

\sigma_{\rm ess}(\mathfrak{A})\subseteq\sigma(\mathfrak{A}).

Evidently,

\sigma_{\rm ess}(\mathfrak{A})\subseteq\sigma_{\rm ess}(A_{1})\times\cdots\times\sigma_{\rm ess}(A_{d}),

and an element
\lambda\in\sigma(\mathfrak{A})
is an eigenvalue of finite mutliplicity if

0\,<\,\sum_{j=1}^{d}\ker(A_{j}-\lambda_{j}1)\,<\,\infty.

A more precise relationship between the spectrum and essential spectrum is given by the following result of Cho

[
9
]
.

Theorem 7.8
.

If
\mathfrak{A}
is a
d
-tuple of commuting selfadjoint operators, then

\sigma_{\rm ess}(\mathfrak{A})
consists of all
\lambda\in\sigma(\mathfrak{A})
for which
\lambda
is not an isolated eigenvalue of
finite multiplicity.

With this result, we can now prove:

Theorem 7.9
.

Every
d
-tuple
\mathfrak{A}\in\mathbb{B}_{\rm sa}^{d}

of commuting selfadjoint operators has the Smith-Ward property.

Proof.

Because
{\rm C}^{*}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{A}})
is an abelian C
∗
-algebra whose maximal ideal space is homeomorphic to

\sigma(\mathfrak{A})
,
there is a unital isomorphism

\pi:{\rm C}^{*}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{A}})\rightarrow C\left(\sigma(\mathfrak{A})\right)
. Moreover, because

\sigma(\mathfrak{A})
is a compact metric space, the inverse map
\pi^{-1}
is
approximately unitarily equivalent, modulo
\mathbb{K}
,
to a diagonal representation
\rho:C\left(\sigma(\mathfrak{A})\right)\rightarrow\mathbb{B}
, by the Weyl-von Neumann-Berg theorem

[
13
, Corollary II.4.5]
.
Hence, if
D_{j}=\rho\circ\pi(A_{j})
, for each
j=1,\dots,d
, and if
\mathfrak{D}=(D_{1},\dots,D_{d})\in\mathbb{B}^{d}
, then

\mathcal{O}_{{\mathfrak{D}}}\simeq\mathcal{O}_{{\mathfrak{A}}}

and
\mathcal{O}_{\dot{\mathfrak{D}}}\simeq\mathcal{O}_{\dot{\mathfrak{A}}}
. Therefore, by Proposition
7.5
, we
may assume, without loss of generality, that

A_{1},\dots,A_{d}
are commuting diagonal operators
A_{\ell}=\mbox{Diag}(a_{\ell 1},a_{\ell 2}\dots)
.

The joint eigenvalues
\mathfrak{a}_{j}=(a_{1j},\dots,a_{dj})
of
\mathfrak{A}
are dense in the spectrum
\sigma(\mathfrak{A})
, and, by
Theorem
7.8
, the joint eigenvalues
\mathfrak{a}_{j}
that are excluded from the joint essential spectrum

\sigma_{\rm ess}(\mathfrak{A})

are those that
are topologically isolated in
\sigma(\mathfrak{A})
and have finite multiplicity.

Let
\sigma_{\rm p}(\mathfrak{A})
denote the joint point spectrum (that is, the set of diagonal
d
-tuples
\mathfrak{a}_{j}
)
of
\mathfrak{A}
.
To prove
\mathfrak{A}
has the Smith-Ward property, it is sufficient to prove, by the proof of
[
50
, Theorem 3.13]
,
that
\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\mbox{\rm dist}\left(\mathfrak{a}_{k},\sigma_{\rm ess}(\mathfrak{A})\right)=0
.

If
\sigma_{\rm p}(\mathfrak{A})
is a finite set, then
\mathfrak{a}_{k}\in\sigma_{\rm ess}(\mathfrak{A})
whenever
\mathfrak{a}_{k}
is an eigenvalue of
infinite multiplicity; if
\mathfrak{a}_{k}\not\in\sigma_{\rm ess}(\mathfrak{A})
, then

\mathfrak{a}_{k}
occurs on the diagonal only finitely many times. Hence, the condition

\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\mbox{\rm dist}\left(\mathfrak{a}_{k},\sigma_{\rm ess}(\mathfrak{A})\right)=0

is readily satisfied.

Assume
\sigma_{\rm p}(\mathfrak{A})
is an infinite set, and
let
\varepsilon>0
.
Because
\overline{\sigma_{\rm p}(\mathfrak{A})}=\sigma(\mathfrak{A})
is compact,
there exist finitely many points
\mathfrak{b}_{1},\dots,\mathfrak{b}_{n}\in\overline{\sigma_{\rm p}(\mathfrak{A})}
such that

\overline{\sigma_{\rm p}(\mathfrak{A})}\subseteq\cup_{i=1}^{n}B[\mathfrak{b}_{i},\varepsilon/3]
, where

B[\mathfrak{b}_{i},\varepsilon/3]
denotes the closed ball of radius
\varepsilon/3
around
\mathfrak{b}_{i}
.
For a given
i
, either
\sigma(\mathfrak{A})\cap B[\mathfrak{b}_{i},\varepsilon/3]
is a finite set or an infinite set. Because the spectrum is an
infinite set,
\sigma(\mathfrak{A})\cap B[\mathfrak{b}_{i},\varepsilon/3]
is an infinite set for at least one
i
, and there are only finitely
many
i^{\prime}
for which
\sigma(\mathfrak{A})\cap B[\mathfrak{b}_{i^{\prime}},\varepsilon/3]
is a finite set.
By the Bolzano-Weistrass theorem,
whenever
\sigma(\mathfrak{A})\cap B[\mathfrak{b}_{i},\varepsilon/3]
is infinite set, it contains a limit point
\mathfrak{c}_{i}
, which is necessarily
a non-isolated point of
\sigma(\mathfrak{A})
, implying
\mathfrak{c}_{i}\in\sigma_{\rm ess}(\mathfrak{A})
.
Hence,
exist
\mathfrak{c}_{1},\dots,\mathfrak{c}_{k}\in\sigma_{\rm ess}(\mathfrak{A})
such that,
except for perhaps a number of elements of
\sigma_{\rm p}(\mathfrak{A})
,
each element of
\sigma_{\rm p}(\mathfrak{A})
is contained in
\varepsilon
-ball around
\mathfrak{c}_{i}
.
In other words,
\mbox{\rm dist}\left(\mathfrak{a}_{k},\sigma_{\rm ess}(\mathfrak{A})\right)\leq\varepsilon
, for all but finitely many

\mathfrak{a}_{k}
. Because
\varepsilon>0
is arbitrary,
we obtain
\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\mbox{\rm dist}\left(\mathfrak{a}_{k},\sigma_{\rm ess}(\mathfrak{A})\right)=0
.

\hfill\square

7.3.
Single operators with the Smith-Ward property

Some further examples of selfadjoint pairs
(A_{1},A_{2})
with the Smith-Ward property
are considered below, but in the form of a single operator,
T=A_{1}+iA_{2}
.

7.3.1.
The Smith-Ward property for contractions
T
with
\sigma(T)\supseteq S^{1}

Theorem 7.10
.

Every
T\in\mathbb{B}
with
\|T\|\leq 1
and
\sigma(T)\supseteq S^{1}

has the Smith-Ward property.

Proof.

If
{\rm C}^{*}(\mathcal{O}_{T})\cap\mathbb{K}=\{0\}
, then every noncommutative state on
{\rm C}^{*}(\mathcal{O}_{T})
is essential, which implies

W_{\rm mtrx}(\dot{T})=W_{\rm mtrx}(T)
. Therefore, by Theorem
6.3
,
\mathcal{O}_{\dot{T}}
is C
∗
-nuclear.
Hence, by Theorem
6.15
,

\mathcal{O}_{\dot{T}}
has the lifting property

[
36
, Theorem 4.3]
and, consequently, that
T
has the Smith-Ward property.

Assume, therefore, that
{\rm C}^{*}(\mathcal{O}_{T})\cap\mathbb{K}\not=\{0\}
. Hence,
{\rm C}^{*}(\mathcal{O}_{T})
admits
essential states. Note that, by the Kadison-Schwarz inequality, any state
\phi
on
{\rm C}^{*}(\mathcal{O}_{T})
that sends
T
to a point
\lambda
on the unit circle
is a character on
{\rm C}^{*}(\mathcal{O}_{T})
; in particular, every essential state on
{\rm C}^{*}(\mathcal{O}_{T})
that maps
T
to the unit circle
is an essential character on
{\rm C}^{*}(\mathcal{O}_{T})
, resulting in a point of the essential spectrum
\sigma(\dot{T})
of
T
.

Select
\lambda\in S^{1}
such that
\lambda
is not an eigenvalue of
T
.
We aim to show
\lambda=\omega(T)
, for some essential state
\omega
on
{\rm C}^{*}(\mathcal{O}_{T})
. As noted earlier,

\lambda=\phi(T)
for some character
\phi
on
{\rm C}^{*}(\mathcal{O}_{T})
. Because every state on
{\rm C}^{*}(\mathcal{O}_{T})

extends to a state on
\mathbb{B}
, there are states
\omega_{\rm ess}
and
\omega_{\rm nor}
on
\mathbb{B}
, and
t\in[0,1]
, for which

\phi(X)=t\omega_{\rm ess}(X)+(1-t)\omega_{\rm nor}(X)
for every
X\in{\rm C}^{*}(\mathcal{O}_{T})
,
where
\omega_{\rm ess}(\mathbb{K})=\{0\}
and
\omega_{\rm nor}
on
\mathbb{B}
is given
by
\omega_{\rm nor}(Y)=\operatorname{Tr}(Y\rho)
, for some
fixed density operator
\rho\in\mathbb{B}
.

We now show that
\phi=\omega_{\rm ess}{}_{|{\rm C}^{*}(\mathcal{O}_{T})}
. First we note that
\phi
is not the restriction of

\omega_{\rm nor}
to
{\rm C}^{*}(\mathcal{O}_{T})
. Suppose, on the contrary, that
\phi=\omega_{\rm nor}{}_{|{\rm C}^{*}(\mathcal{O}_{T})}
. Thus,

\lambda=\omega_{\rm nor}(T)=\operatorname{Tr}(\rho T)
.
Because
\rho
is a density operator,
\operatorname{Tr}(\rho T)=\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{j}\langle T\xi_{j},\xi_{j}\rangle
, where each
\xi_{j}
is a unit eigenvector of
\rho
corresponding to the eigenvalue
\alpha_{j}
, and
\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{j}=1
. Hence,
\lambda
is in the convex hull of the spatial numerical range of
T
;
however, the spatial (that is, classical) numerical range of
T
is already convex,
by the Toeplitz-Hausdorff theorem. Thus,
\lambda=\langle T\xi,\xi\rangle
, for some unit vector
\xi
. Using the case of equality in the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we obtain

T\xi=\lambda\xi
. However, this contradicts the assumption that
\lambda
is not an eigenvalue of
T
. Therefore, it cannot be that
\phi
is
the restriction of

\omega_{\rm nor}
to
{\rm C}^{*}(\mathcal{O}_{T})
.

By the argument above, the value of
t\in[0,1]
cannot be
t=0
.
If
0<t<1
, then
\lambda=\omega_{\rm ess}(T)=\omega_{\rm nor}(T)
, because
\phi(T)=\lambda
is an extreme point of
W_{1}(T)
. However, the previous paragraph shows

\lambda=\omega_{\rm nor}(T)
leads to a contradiction; hence,
t\not\in(0,1)
.

The only remaining possibility is that
t=1
, in which case
\phi=\omega_{\rm ess}{}_{|{\rm C}^{*}(\mathcal{O}_{T})}
.
Applying the Kadison-Schwarz inequality argument, we have that
\omega_{\rm ess}
is a character on
{\rm C}^{*}(\mathcal{O}_{T})
. Since

\omega_{\rm ess}
is an essential state on
{\rm C}^{*}(\mathcal{O}_{T})
, we deduce
\lambda\in\sigma(\dot{T})
.

If
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\in S^{1}
are distinct eigenvalues of
T
, with corresponding eigenvectors
\eta_{1}
and
\eta_{2}
, then
T^{*}\eta_{j}=\overline{\lambda}_{j}\eta_{j}
and

\lambda_{1}\overline{\lambda}_{2}\langle\eta_{1},\eta_{2}\rangle=\langle T\eta_{1},T\eta_{2}\rangle=\langle T^{*}T\eta_{1},\eta_{2}\rangle=\langle\eta_{1},\eta_{2}\rangle.

Because
\lambda_{1}\overline{\lambda}_{2}\not=1
, it must be that
\langle\eta_{1},\eta_{2}\rangle=0
. Hence, by the separability of
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N})
,
T
has at most
countably many eigenvalues on
S^{1}
.

The arguments above show that, except for at most countably many points in
S^{1}
,
all
\lambda\in S^{1}
are elements of the essential spectrum of
T
. Since
\sigma(\dot{T})
is closed,
it follows that
\sigma(\dot{T})\supseteq S^{1}
. Hence,
\dot{T}
satisfies
\|\dot{T}\|\leq 1
and
\sigma(\dot{T})\supseteq S^{1}
, which implies

\mathcal{O}_{\dot{T}}
is C
∗
-nuclear. As we demonstrated earlier, this implies
\mathcal{O}_{\dot{T}}
has the lifting property,
and so
T
has the Smith-Ward property.

\hfill\square

7.3.2.
The Smith-Ward property for quadratic operators

Theorem 7.11
.

Every quadratic operator
T\in\mathbb{B}
has the Smith-Ward property.

Proof.

If
T\in\mathbb{B}
is quadratic, then
\dot{T}
also satisfies a polynomial equation of degree
1
or
2
.
In both cases, the resulting operator system
\mathcal{O}_{\dot{T}}
is C
∗
-nuclear, by Theorem
6.7
.
Therefore, by Theorem
6.15
,

\mathcal{O}_{\dot{T}}
has the lifting property
and, hence, that
T
has the Smith-Ward property.

\hfill\square

7.4.
Essential matrix ranges with Smith-Ward geometry

The results of this section show how the “shape” of the essential matrix range can be used to deduce the Smith-Ward property for
certain tuples.

Definition 7.12
.

A compact matrix convex set
K_{\rm mtrx}
over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
has
Smith-Ward geometry
if, for every

d
-tuple
\mathfrak{A}
of selfadjoint operators,

W_{\rm ess-mtrx}(\mathfrak{A})=K_{\rm mtrx}
implies

\mathfrak{A}
has the Smith-Ward property.

Proposition 7.13
.

If
K\subseteq R^{d}
is a simplex, then the noncommutative simplex
K^{\rm min}
has
Smith-Ward geometry.

Proof.

By Theorem
5.4
, if
\mathfrak{A}
is a
d
-tuple
\mathfrak{A}
of selfadjoint operators such that

W_{\rm ess-mtrx}(\mathfrak{A})=K^{\rm min}
, then the operator system
\mathcal{O}_{\dot{\mathfrak{A}}}
is nuclear. Therefore,

\mathcal{O}_{\dot{\mathfrak{A}}}
has the lifting property and, hence, the Smith-Ward property.

\hfill\square

Proposition 7.14
.

The matrix range of
a
d
-tuple
\mathfrak{a}
of selfadjoint
operators for which
\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{a}}
is an OMAX
operator system has Smith-Ward geometry.

Proof.

We are to prove that, if
\mathfrak{A}\in\mathbb{B}_{\rm sa}^{d}
is such that
W_{\rm ess-mtrx}(\mathfrak{A})=W_{\rm mtrx}(\mathfrak{a})
, then

\mathcal{O}_{\dot{\mathfrak{A}}}
has the lifting property.

The hypothesis
\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{a}}
is an OMAX system implies

\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{a}}
has the lifting
property, by Theorem
6.15
, whereas the hypothesis
W_{\rm ess-mtrix}(\mathfrak{A})=W_{\rm mtrx}(\mathfrak{a})

implies

\hfill\square

8.
Matrix Cubes and Discs

The square
\displaystyle\square
and disc
\overline{\mathbb{D}}

are the compact convex subsets of
\mathbb{C}
given by

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\displaystyle\square&=&[-1,1]\times[-1,1]\subset\mathbb{C},\\
\overline{\mathbb{D}}&=&\{\zeta\in\mathbb{C}\,|\,|\zeta|\leq 1\}.\end{array}

Henceforth, assume
u
and
v
are free symmetries, and
w
is a unitary element such that
w^{4}=1
and
\sigma(w)
coincides with the
fourth roots of unity. The elements
u+iv
and
(1+i)w
each have numerical range equal to the square
\displaystyle\square
.
Thus,

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\displaystyle\square{}^{\rm min}&=&W_{\rm mtrx}\left(w+iw\right),\mbox{ and}\\
\displaystyle\square{}^{\rm max}&=&W_{\rm mtrx}\left(u+iv\right).\end{array}

By
[
48
, p. 3233]
, we have the following sharp scaled inclusion,

(8.1)

\displaystyle\square{}^{\rm max}\subseteq\sqrt{2}\,\displaystyle\square{}^{\rm min},

which leads to the following scaled-OMAX result.

Proposition 8.1
.

The operator system
\mathcal{O}_{w+iw}
is not an OMAX operator system; however, if

\psi:\mathcal{O}_{w+iw}\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
is a unital positive linear map, then the unital linear map

\phi:\mathcal{O}_{w+iw}\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
in which
\phi(w+iw)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\psi(w+iw)
and

\phi(w^{*}-iw^{*})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\psi(w^{*}-iw^{*})
is completely positive.

Proof.

The fact that the
\mathcal{O}_{w+iw}
is not an OMAX operator system is established in

[
44
]
, while the complete positivity of
\phi

follows from
[
48
, Proposition 2.1]
using the scaling constant
\sqrt{2}

arising in (
8.1
) above.

\hfill\square

The minimal and maximal matrix convex sets determined by the closed unit
\overline{\mathbb{D}}
are the matrix ranges of the
bilateral shift operator
b
acting on
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})
and the nilpotent operator
s=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}0&2\\
0&0\end{array}\right]

acting on
\mathbb{C}^{2}

[
1
]
,
[
27
, Proposition 5.3]
.
That is,

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\overline{\mathbb{D}}{}^{\rm min}&=&W_{\rm mtrx}\left(b\right),\mbox{ and}\\
\overline{\mathbb{D}}{}^{\rm max}&=&W_{\rm mtrx}\left(s\right).\end{array}

By the well-known inequalities involving numerical radius and norm, we have the following sharp scaled inclusion:

(8.2)

\overline{\mathbb{D}}{}^{\rm max}\subseteq 2\,\displaystyle\overline{\mathbb{D}}{}^{\rm min}.

An interesting relationship holds
between the matrix squares and the matrix discs
[
12
, Theorem 2.5]
:

Theorem 8.2

(Choi-Li)
.

The following statements are equivalent for a matrix
y\in\mathcal{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
:

(1)

y\in\displaystyle\square{}^{\rm min}
;

(2)

\frac{1}{1+i}\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}0&y^{*}+y\\
i(y^{*}-y)&0\end{array}\right]\in\overline{\mathbb{D}}{}^{\rm max}
.

We now make note of the fact
that these matrix squares and matrix discs have Smith-Ward geometry.

Proposition 8.3
.

The extremal matrix convex sets
\displaystyle\square{}^{\rm min}
,
\displaystyle\square{}^{\rm max}
,
\overline{\mathbb{D}}^{\rm min}
, and

\overline{\mathbb{D}}^{\rm max}
have Smith-Ward geometry.

Proof.

Recall that we are to prove that if
T\in\mathbb{B}
has essential matrix range equal to any one of

\displaystyle\square{}^{\rm min}
,
\displaystyle\square{}^{\rm max}
,
\overline{\mathbb{D}}^{\rm min}
, and

\overline{\mathbb{D}}^{\rm max}
, then the identity map
\iota:\mathcal{O}_{\dot{T}}\rightarrow\mathbb{B}/\mathbb{K}
has
a completely positive lifting to
\mathbb{B}
.
Because the lifting property is
invariant under unital complete order isomorphism, if the essential matrix range of
T\in\mathbb{B}

coincides with one of
\mathcal{O}_{u+iv}
,
\mathcal{O}_{b}
, or
\mathcal{O}_{s}
, then
\mathcal{O}_{\dot{T}}
will have the lifting property,
as we proved earlier that each of
\mathcal{O}_{u+iv}
,
\mathcal{O}_{b}
, and
\mathcal{O}_{s}
has the lifting property.

With the normal element
w+iw
, let

x=\displaystyle\bigoplus_{1}^{\infty}(w+iw)\in\mathbb{B}
and note that every eigenvalue of
x
is an isolated eigenvalue
of infinite multiplicity. Thus,
W_{\rm mtrx}(\dot{x})=W_{\rm mtrx}(x)=W_{\rm mtrx}(w+iw)
. Because normal operators have the Smith-Ward property,
so does any operator
T
for which
W_{\rm mtrx}(\dot{T})=W_{\rm mtrx}(\dot{x})
, by Proposition
7.5
.

\hfill\square
