Assessment of some hematological and inflammatory parameters among patients with active pulmonary tuberculosis in Ibb governorate, Yemen
Authors: Bashir A Al-Ofairi, Majed A Al-Jahrani, Abeer Esmail, Ahmed M Abdulkareem, Marwan K Saeed
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundActive pulmonary tuberculosis (PTB) is a highly prevalent chronic infectious disease and one of the most worldwide health problems, especially in our country “Yemen”. This study aimed to evaluate certain hematological and inflammatory parameters in patients with active pulmonary tuberculosis (PTB) before and after treatment in Ibb governorate, Yemen.Subjects and MethodsThe study collected 100 blood and serum samples from active PTB patients, diagnosed using Ziehl-Neelsen staining for acid-fast bacilli, culture on Löwenstein-Jensen medium, tuberculin skin test and chest radiography, supervised by a specialist physician. These samples were analyzed for various hematological and inflammatory parameters before initiating standard anti-TB treatment and again at two, four and six months post-treatment.ResultsThe findings indicated changes in hematological parameters: Hemoglobin (Hb) levels were increased from 11.00 ± 1.41 g/dl before treatment to 11.95 ± 1.32, 13.08 ± 1.04, and 14.21 ± 0.96 after two, four, and six months of treatment, respectively. The total white blood cell count (Total WBCs) before treatment was 11,172 ± 2656.53, which decreased to 8903 ± 2026.31, 6650 ± 1336.47 and 5229 ± 591.40/mm3 after two, four and six months of treatment. Additionally, inflammatory parameters were showed a significant decrease: erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) was dropped from 95.43 ± 21.98 mm/hr before treatment to 10.53 ± 6.65 mm/hr after 6 months and C-reactive protein (CRP) levels were decreased from 41.04 ± 12.08 mg/L before treatment to 0.24 ± 1.18 mg/L after 6 months. Moreover, the study demonstrated that hematological parameters (Hb increased, Total WBCs decreased) and inflammatory parameters were normalized in PTB patients after anti-TB treatment in Ibb governorate, Yemen.ConclusionThese parameters gradually normalized after treatment with anti-TB drugs, suggesting their potential use in the diagnosis and monitoring of treatment efficacy in active PTB patients.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-12261-y.Keywords: Mycobacterium tuberculosis, Active pulmonary tuberculosis, Hemoglobin, Total white blood cell, C-reactive protein, Erythrocyte sedimentation rate

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB), caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis (MTB), is an airborne transmitted disease that principally affects the respiratory system, it remains an important infectious cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide, with significant public health implications, mainly in low- and middle-income countries, where resources for early diagnosis, treatment and prevention are constrained. In 2022, about 10.6 million individuals were infected, with 7.5 million cases detected globally and around 1.30 million fatalities [1–4].

In 2017 and 2018 the TB cases in Yemen according to study conducted by [5] was 9935 and 8791 respectively.

TB is a highly predominant chronic infectious disease. MTB is an aerobic intracellular binding bacterium (bacillus), preferentially colonizing oxygen-rich tissues like the lungs. Robert Koch, a German researcher, was the first to isolate MTB, marking a crucial step in understanding this devastating global epidemic [6–9].

Pulmonary TB (PTB), a chronic granulomatous disease, is the most widespread clinical manifestation of TB. Despite the discovery of anti-TB antibiotics over six decades ago, PTB remains a public health challenge, especially in developing nations [10, 11].

MTB exhibits the ability to persist in a clinically dormant state within humans. While 95% of infected individuals develop latent TB, the disease remains a significant threat, ranking as the fourth leading cause of death in Yemen. The Ministry of Health in Yemen prioritizes TB control, recognizing it as a major infectious disease. Factors contributing to the high incidence of PTB in Yemen include poor health services, illiteracy, rapid population growth, low income levels, and the ongoing conflict with Saudi Arabia and the United Arab Emirates [12, 13].

Sputum smear microscopy is the primary diagnostic method used to identify TB in developing countries. It relies on microscopic examination of sputum samples for the presence of MTB. In developed countries with advanced laboratory capabilities, TB diagnosis is also achieved through culture methods, which is considered the current gold standard, or rapid molecular tests. Effective chemotherapy for TB has been available since the 1950s. However, the prognosis for untreated TB remains serious, particularly for patients who lack access to appropriate treatment due to misdiagnosis [14–16].

The clinical route of chemotherapy-treated PTB requires prolonged treatment regimens, typically lasting from 6 to 12 months. While standard protocols emphasize monitoring infection activity and response to therapy, evaluating these aspects can be challenging. The effectiveness of sputum examination is often limited by the low sensitivity of microscopy and the impact of poly-chemotherapy on MTB growth in culture. Despite these limitations, microscopic examination of acid-fast stained (AFB) sputum smears remains a crucial diagnostic tool for initiating PTB therapy and implementing respiratory isolation measures [17–19].

Hematological abnormalities have been recognized in patients with MTB infections for nearly a century. These abnormalities can range from subtle to severe, including anemia and leukocytosis. Anemia, characterized by a decrease in Hb levels, is commonly reported in PTB patients. This phenomenon is attributed to cytokine secretion by activated macrophages in response to MTB bacilli, which can lead to decreased erythropoietin production, reduced responsiveness of erythroid precursors, impaired iron mobilization from macrophages, and ultimately, a disruption in the transfer of iron to developing erythrocytes [11, 12, 20–24].

An increase in white blood cells (WBCs) count is commonly observed during active PTB infection. This elevation is primarily driven by increased neutrophil leukocytes and macrophages, reflecting the body’s immune defense mechanisms aimed at combating the invading bacterial population [12, 25].

ESR is a well-established laboratory method for detecting inflammation, frequently employed in the assessment PTB and monitoring response to clinical therapy. Inflammation triggers the release of proteins, including cytokines, into the bloodstream. These cytokines increase erythrocyte stickiness, leading to erythrocyte aggregation and an accelerated sedimentation rate, resulting in elevated ESR values [26–29].

ESR values are significantly elevated in patients with active PTB, approximately 15 times higher than in healthy individuals, demonstrating a strong statistical significance. ESR is routinely incorporated into the assessment of active PTB, and elevated ESR levels are consistent with the expected inflammatory response in these patients. Following anti-TB antibiotic therapy, ESR values show a significant decrease, indicating a positive response to treatment and improved prognosis. C-reactive protein (CRP), an acute phase marker, is also closely associated with active PTB. CRP serves as a sensitive and specific indicator of acute inflammation, with elevated levels particularly observed in bacterial infections such as active PTB. The liver synthesizes CRP in response to various promoters, with its production significantly enhanced by tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) and interleukin 6 (IL-6) [30–35].

Numerous studies have demonstrated CRP as a sensitive indicator of PTB activity. A return to normal CRP levels after initial elevation suggests a positive therapeutic response. Studies have revealed significantly higher CRP levels in acid-fast smear-positive PTB patients compared to smear-negative patients [36–40].

Materials and methods

The aims of study

The present prospective cohort study was designed to: (1) Assess some hematological parameters (Hb and Total WBCs counts) in active PTB before and after treatment in Ibb Governorate, Yemen. (2) Detect the inflammatory parameters (CRP and ESR) before and after treatment of these patients. (3) Explore the relationship between hematological& inflammatory parameters with the most potential risk factors (AFB severity, comorbidity of diabetes mellitus and sex).

Study design, subjects and population

The present prospective cohort study was conducted on (100) active PTB patients, consisting of 60 males and 40 females. The study was conducted at various hospitals, Pulmonary Tuberculosis center and medical laboratories in Ibb Governorate, Yemen from December 2019 to March 2021. Flow chart that represent the present study was showed in (Fig. 1).

Flow chart of the present study

Testing of patients for active PTB

The diagnosis of active PTB was confirmed through Ziehl-Neelsen (ZN) staining for acid-fast microscopy, tuberculin skin test (TST), and culture on Löwenstein-Jensen (LJ) medium, as well as chest radiography [41] under the supervision of a specialist physician.

ZN staining for acid fast microscopy

All AFB smears were prepared, fixed and stained using ZN stain (carbolfuchsin as the primary stain, acid-alcohol as a decolorizing agent, and a methylene blue counterstain). AFB grading criteria was as followed: 1–9 AFB/100 fields was 1+, 1–9 AFB/10 fields was 2+, 1–9 AFB/fields was 3+ and > 9 AFB/fields was 4+ [42].

Anti-TB treatment regimen

PTB patients were given standard TB drugs for 2 months (Isoniazid, Rifampicin, Ethambutol and Pyrazinamide), 4 months and 6 months (Isoniazid and Rifampicin). Daily dosage of anti-TB drugs (dose range) in mg/kg was as follows: Isoniazid 4–6 , Rifampicin 8–12, Pyrazinamide 20–30 , Ethambutol 15–25 [43].

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The present study was included active PTB patients of any age and both sex, who were diagnosed as having active PTB (diagnosed by ZN staining for acid fast microscopy, culture on LJ medium, TST and chest radiography and who were given standard anti-TB treatment as two (Isoniazid, Rifampicin, Ethambutol and Pyrazinamide), four and six months (Isoniazid and Rifampicin).

All the following patients were excluded: malignancy (ovarian, endometrium, cervix, lung, breast, liver, gastric, pancreatic, colorectal malignancies), benign conditions (such as menstruation, pregnancy, cirrhosis, pancreatitis, renal and hepatic insufficiencies, uterine fibrinoids, benign ovarian tumors, postmenopausal period, pelvic inflammatory disease, pelvic irradiation, peritonitis and pleuritis.

Data, sample collection and laboratory diagnosis of patients with active PTB

The data was collected from each participants by structured and accurate questionnaire. Blood samples were collected from active PTB patients at the Center of Tuberculosis in Ibb governorate - Yemen. Five (ml) of venous blood were collected using strict hygiene and biosafety precautions. Two (ml) were transferred into plain tubes, where serum samples were separated after clotting and centrifugation. These serum samples were stored in sterile eppendorf tubes at −70 °C until further analysis for CRP testing using the latex agglutination “Semi quantitative” technique. Two (ml) were transferred into tubes containing trisodium citrate anticoagulant for ESR testing within two hours. Lastly, one (ml) was transferred into tubes with EDTA-anticoagulant for Hb and total WBCs counting tests, which were also performed within two hours.

Hemoglobin (Hb) test values were measured using the Rayto 9200 biochemistry analyzer (Rayto-Semi-Auto-Chemistry-Analyzer-Rt-9200-Biochemical-Analysis-Machine, China), with normal ranges of 13–18 g/dl for men and 12–15 g/dl for women. Total white blood cells (total WBCs) count values were determined using an improved neubauer ruled counting chamber (haemocytometer), with normal ranges of 4000–10000 cells/mm3. Erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) values were measured using the Westergren tube technique, with normal ranges of up to 15 mm/hr for men and up to 20 mm/hr for women [44, 45]. C-reactive protein (CRP) values were assessed using a slide agglutination test for qualitative and semi-quantitative detection, with normal ranges below 6 mg/L [46].

Sample size calculation and loosed cases clarification

The sample size was determined for within-subject (paired) comparisons of hematological and inflammatory markers. Assuming α = 0.05, power = 80%, a traditional standardized effect size d = 0.30, and a within-subject correlation of ρ = 0.50, the required number of complete cases is ≈88. Allowing for ≈10% loss to follow-up, we aimed to recruit 100 participants. With 100 enrolled, the study has adequate power (≥80%) to detect small-to-moderate changes in the estimated hematological and inflammatory markers. During the follow up, some participants were lost for several reasons, including death (3), transfer (2), or inability to continue contribution due to personal situations (5). Such cases were excluded from the analysis, and only those who completed all follow up were included in the final evaluation. Although every effort was made to maintain participant follow up.

Statistical analysis

Data after collection was organized, coded, entered and analyzed using SPSS software, version 21, including chi-square X2 test, independent T test, one-way ANOVA test, Tukey Post Hoc HSD tests and paired sample t-test for pre- and post-treatment comparisons as well as Spearman correlation analysis. A significance level of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Furthermore, 66 patients were smokers, 34 were non-smokers, 13 were co-morbid diabetes mellitus and 87 were non-diabetics. Statistically significant associations were observed between AFB severity and sex, diabetes and age groups. Discussion

The demographic characteristics of active PTB patients in Ibb governorate, Yemen

TB is an infectious airborne disease caused by MTB, posing a significant public health challenge across all age groups [47, 48]. Initially, the present study focused on the demographic profile of one hundred patients who tested sputum-positive for acid-fast bacilli (AFB), as outlined in (Table 1).

The present study was revealed a higher prevalence of male cases (60%) compared to female cases (40%), potentially linked to greater social activity and increased exposure to male-specific risk factors for active pulmonary TB, such as smoking, close contact, overcrowding, and diabetes. This result was in agreement with other studies in general populations [11, 49–51].

Furthermore, the results of the present study were indicated a higher incidence of active PTB in rural patients (71%) compared to urban patients (29%). This disparity may be attributed to limited access to healthcare facilities, reduced availability of diagnostic services in rural areas, higher rates of smoking, poverty, communal living arrangements and lower levels of education among the rural population. Similar studies [47] and [52] were reported comparable trends, with rural areas exhibiting higher rates of active PTB infections.

Moreover, the results were showed that the majority of smear AFB-positive infections were classified as + 3 (40%), followed by + 4 (39%), with the lowest proportion at + 1 (5%). Previous studies [53] have demonstrated varying distributions of smear AFB positivity levels, with some reporting higher rates of + 3 infections (75%), followed by + 1 (17.3%) and +2 (7.7%), while others [54] have shown different patterns with predominant +3 (80%) or + 2 (20%) classifications. Other studies [55] showed that higher smear AFB positivity infection were +1(49.1%), followed by + 2 (25.4%), +3 (20.3%) and +4 (5.3%). Other study by [56] showed that higher smear AFB positivity infection were +2 (35.29%), followed by + 1 (27.45%), and +3 (21.57%). These findings were highlighted the variability in smear AFB positivity levels across different studies and populations.

The findings were also indicated a higher prevalence of active PTB infection in smokers (66%) compared to non-smokers (34%) among patients. This association may be linked to the detrimental effects of smoking on the structural and immunological defenses of the organism, including respiratory epithelium rupture, changes in pathogen adherence, impaired mucociliary clearance, reduced macrophage phagocytic activity, and CD4+ T lymphopenia, which collectively increase susceptibility to PTB [6, 57]. This study was aligned with previous studies [58–61] reporting similar proportions of smoker patients with PTB, i.e. 60.2%, 72.2%, 68.9% and 54.22% respectively.

Patients with diabetes exhibit compromised cell-mediated immunity, micronutrient deficiencies, pulmonary microangiopathy and renal insufficiency, predisposing them to PTB [62, 63]. Comparable studies by other researchers [49, 50, 54, 55] have reported varying percentages of diabetic patients with active PTB infection (13.5%, 11.5%, 2%, 15%) respectively, highlighting the increased vulnerability of this population.

In the present study, the mean age of patients with active PTB was 33.28 years, with the highest infection rate observed in the 21–35 age group (n=46) and the lowest was in individuals over 50 years old (n=16). These results are consistent with previous studies [11, 14, 64, 65], which also reported similar age distributions among PTB patients. Study by [66] was found the highest infection rate was among 16–34 years old (46.4%) and the lowest among those under 16(7.6%). Similarly [67], reported the highest infection rates in the 25–34 year (37.5%) and 35–44 year (25%) age groups, with the lowest rate among 16–24 year-olds (7.5%). This age- related infection pattern is further confirmed by [47], which identified the 18–27 year (47%) and 28–37 year (25.7%) age groups as experiencing the highest infection rates. Finally [49], was found the highest infection rates among 25–34 year-olds (31.6%) and 36–45 year-olds (28.3%), with the lowest rates were observed in those aged 0–14 (0.2%) and those ≥65 years (4.1%).

Efficacy of anti-TB drugs regarding to weight among active PTB patients

The findings of the current study were indicated a decrease in weight among patients with PTB before treatment, with a mean weight of 46.57 kg as shown in (Fig. 2). This finding was consistent with previous study [68] which identified weight loss as a prominent feature of TB. Additionally, the results were aligned with other studies [39, 69, 70] that have reported similar findings. A study by [70] found that the mean weight of patients with active PTB infection was 41.7 kg, attributing weight loss to cytokine effects such as TNF-α, IL-1, IL-6 and interferon-γ (IFN-γ) [71, 72]. Malnutrition is commonly observed in PTB patients, particularly in underweight individuals due to dietary imbalances [73].

Furthermore, the findings were revealed a significant increase in the mean weight of patients with active PTB infection during the second, fourth and sixth months after treatment, as demonstrated in (Fig. 2). Previous studies have also reported an increase in body weight during anti-TB treatment [70, 74]. Another study showed that the mean body weight before treatment was 41.7 kg, 43.13 kg after 2 months of treatment, and 47.13 kg after 4 months of treatment [70]. Study by [68] was demonstrated a significant weight change during treatment, with 31.9% of patients gaining at least 5% body weight after 2 months of treatment and 62.4% by the end of treatment. Moreover, a study reported mean body weights of 45.9 kg at diagnosis, 48.9 kg after two months and 51.1 kg at the end of six months of treatment respectively [75]. Successful TB treatment is associated with weight recovery and nutritional improvement.

Efficacy of anti-TB drugs regarding to hb levels and total WBCs among active PTB patients before & after treatments

In the present study, the mean of Hb level among individuals with active PTB infection before treatment was lower at 11 g/dl, which rose to 11.95 g/dl after 2 months of treatment, further increasing to 13.08 g/dl after 4 months and reaching 14.21 g/dl after 6 months of treatment as demonstrated in (Fig. 3). These findings are in accordance with prior research studies [11, 14, 23, 25, 76, 77]. Other previous studies by [23, 78–81] have indicated mean Hb levels (g/dl) among patients with active PTB infection before treatment ranging from 9.65 to 11.60. Anemia is a widely recognized as a common TB-associated morbidity [82, 83], defined by Hb < 13 g/dl for men or Hb < 12 g/dl for women [84–86]. The decline in Hb may be attributed to the cytokine TNF-α released by activated macrophages in response to TB bacilli, leading to reduced erythropoietin production and hindrance in reticuloendothelial of iron transport within developing erythrocytes [87–89]. While various pathogeneses have been proposed for PTB-related anemia, most studies point towards inflammatory mediators inhibiting erythropoiesis [90–93]. Nutritional deficiencies [94] and malabsorption syndromes [95] can exacerbate anemia severity. Notably, patients with PTB-associated anemia often exhibit bone marrow iron deficiency and similar red blood cell distribution width patterns as seen in iron-deficiency anemia [96–99]. Inflammatory processes commonly lead to decreased hemoglobin concentrations through cytokine-mediated effects on erythropoietin production, erythroid precursor responsiveness, and iron mobilization from macrophages [100, 101]. An excess of pro-inflammatory cytokines like IL-6, TNF-α, and IFN-γ can contribute to anemia by reducing erythropoietin production, impairing bone marrow response to erythropoietin, and altering iron metabolism, thereby hindering erythropoiesis [92, 102].

Furthermore, in the present study, the average total WBCs count among patients with active PTB infection before treatment was higher and after 2,4,6 months of treatment was decreased as depicted in (Fig. 4). Similar results have been reported by other studies [14, 25, 39, 76, 103], contrasting with findings from previous studies [21, 104]. Additional studies [67, 78, 79] have reported varying mean total WBCs counts (/mm3) among individuals with active PTB infection before treatment. In another study [11], the majority of patients (80%) was exhibited white blood cell counts within the normal range, with hyperleukocytosis was observed in 11% and leukopenia in only 9% of cases. The increase in white blood cells during active PTB infection is attributed to elevated levels of polymorphonuclear leukocytes and macrophages as part of the body’s immune response to combat the invading bacterial population [12, 105].

Concerning to Hb, previous studies consistent with the findings of this study have also documented an increase in Hb levels after anti-TB treatment [69, 106]. For instance, a study by [82] reported mean Hb levels of 11.8, 12.6 and 13.4 g/dl before treatment, after 2 months, and after 6 months of treatment, respectively. Another study by [107] revealed median Hb levels of 10.6, 10.6, 11.1 and 11.7 g/dl before treatment, after 2, 4 and 8 weeks of treatment, respectively. Notably, anemia in TB is predominantly characterized as inflammatory anemia or anemia of inflammation. Interestingly, this type of anemia often resolves with TB treatment due to improvements in iron retention, erythropoietin response, nutritional event, and malabsorption as inflammation and bacterial burden decrease with anti-TB medication [82, 108, 109]. This phenomenon may explain the observed tendency towards resolution of anemia with marginal statistical significance in the current study. The increase in Hb and hematocrit levels serves as indicators of a favorable response to treatment in patients with active PTB [105, 110–113].

A separate study by [52] reported a mean total of WBCs count of 9120/mm3 before treatment and 6660/mm3 after treatment in patients with active PTB infection.

In a separate study by [82], it was reported that the mean total of Total.WBCs among patients with PTB infection before treatment and after 2 months of treatment was 6900 and 4800 per cubic millimeter, respectively.

The efficacy of anti-TB drugs according some inflammatory markers levels among active PTB

The findings of this study were indicated that the mean of ESR among patients with active PTB infection before treatment was higher as shown in (Fig. 5). These findings were consistent with previous studies [14, 20, 21, 27, 39, 76, 114, 115]. Other studies by [79, 116] reported mean ESR values of 76.70 and 115.17 mm/hr among patients with active PTB infection before treatment, respectively. Inflammation in the body often leads to the release of proteins (IL-1, IL-2, INF-γ, and TNF-α) into the bloodstream, which can cause red blood cells to become stickier. The sticky cells are more likely to overlap together and sink faster in the tube, leading to higher ESR levels [27, 117].

Due to the activation of the immune system in TB patients, there is an overproduction of cytokines such as IL-1, IL-2, INF-γ, and TNF-α. These cytokines stimulate the liver to produce acute-phase proteins like CRP [117, 118]. The findings revealed that the mean of CRP level among patients with active PTB infection before treatment was elevated at 41.04 mg/L. These results align with previous studies [11, 66, 119, 120]. Other studies by [79, 80] demonstrated mean CRP values of 103.88 and 86.15 mg/L among patients with active PTB infection before treatment, respectively. Additionally, a study by [120] indicated that CRP had high sensitivity (93%, 95% CI: 85–97) and moderate specificity (62%, 95% CI: 42–79) for detecting active pulmonary TB. CRP levels increase in response to IL-6 [120, 121].

The results of the study demonstrated that the mean of ESR among patients with active PTB infection after 2, 4, and 6 months of treatment showed ESR levels of 69.18 mm/hr, 35.91 mm/hr and 10.53 mm/hr respectively. Additionally, the findings indicated that the mean of CRP level among patients with active PTB infection after 2, 4 and 6 months of treatment was 20.22, 5.82 and 0.24 respectively as shown in (Fig. 5). These results are in line with previous studies [69, 104, 122, 123] which have shown elevated CRP serum levels and ESR in PTB patients (72–87% and 75–87%), with reductions to levels similar to control groups following anti-TB treatment. Other studies have also reported decreases in ESR levels post anti-TB treatment [27, 105, 123]. Furthermore, a study by [124] revealed that ESR levels in patients before initiating anti-TB treatment were 58.65 mm/hr, decreasing to 17.0 mm/hr after completing treatment. Another study demonstrated a decrease in CRP levels post anti-TB treatment [39, 106, 114, 125–127].

Additionally, a study by [128] indicated that CRP levels among patients who had started treatment were 66.55 mg/L, reducing to 23.87 mg/L after completing treatment, with patients who had almost completed the treatment having a mean CRP level of 4.78 ± 4.34 mg/L. Similarly, a study by [129] showed a decrease in CRP levels from 55.32 mg/L before initiating anti-tubercular treatment to 5.39 mg/L after completing treatment. Another study by [107] reported median CRP levels among patients with active PTB infection before treatment and after 2, 4, and 8 weeks of treatment as 56.2 mg/L, 13.0 mg/L, 14.0 mg/L, and 8.6 mg/L respectively. Moreover, a study by [124] demonstrated that CRP levels in patients before initiating anti-TB treatment were 16.56 mg/L, decreasing to 2.29 mg/L after completing treatment. IL-6, the principal cytokine responsible for inducing CRP synthesis, decreases in concentration in the serum of patients treated for PTB [69], leading to reduced inflammatory conditions in lung tissue and subsequently decreased levels of CRP and ESR.

Association between some hematological and inflammatory parameters with AFB severity, comorbidity and sex

The study was revealed a decrease in mean Hb levels (g/dl) with an increase in smear AFB positivity, with values of 12.72, 12.08, 11.38 and 9.94 for +1, +2, +3 and +4 respectively, showing statistical significance (p < 0.05) as depicted in (Table 2). Another study reported Hb levels of 9.5 for +1, 9.1 for +2 and 9.4 for +3 [130]. Subgroup analysis demonstrated a progressive increase in Hb levels as the number of bacilli in the smear rose. Statistical analysis using one-way ANOVA was confirmed these differences to be significant (p < 0.05). Further evaluation using the Tukey method indicated significant differences between various subgroups, highlighting a progressive decrease in Hb levels with increasing smear positivity (p < 0.05).

Moreover, in the current study, the mean total of total WBCs levels (/mm3) were significantly elevated with increasing smear AFB positivity, with values of 7820, 8343.75, 10580, and 13369.23 for +1, +2, +3 and +4 respectively (p < 0.05) as illustrated in (Table 2). Subgroup analysis revealed a progressive increase in total WBCs levels as the number of bacilli in the smear increased. Statistical analysis using one-way ANOVA was confirmed these differences to be significant (p < 0.05). Subsequent application of the Tukey method indicated significant differences among all groups, suggesting a progressive rise in total WBCs count with increasing smear positivity (p < 0.05).

The present study was demonstrated a significant increase in the mean levels of ESR rate in (mm/hr) with higher smear positivity for AFB. The ESR levels were 56.00, 66.94, 93.88 and 113.77 for +1, +2, +3 and +4 smear positivity, respectively, showing statistical significance (p < 0.05). Previous studies by [52, 131] also supported these findings. Another study cited as reference [132] found that the median ESR levels increased with higher smear AFB positivity, with values of 23, 38.8, and 48.1 for +1, +2 and +3 smear positivity, respectively. Subgroup analysis confirmed a progressive increase in ESR levels as the number of bacilli in the smear increased, with statistically significant differences identified through one-way ANOVA analysis (p < 0.05) and Tukey method.

Elevated ESR levels are considered a marker of disease activity in PTB, as observed in this study. The rise in ESR levels is indicative of disease severity and serves as a prognostic indicator, particularly in patients with increased sputum positivity. Previous studies have also reported elevated ESR levels in TB patients. Additionally, the current study was revealed a significant increase in the mean levels of CRP in (mg/L) with higher smear AFB positivity, showing values of 16.80, 26.25, 43.20 and 48.00 for +1, +2, +3 and +4 smear positivity, respectively (p < 0.05). Reference [132] also supported these findings by showing an increase in median CRP levels with higher smear AFB positivity; 22.9, 40.8 and 55.4 for +1, +2, and +3 respectively. Similarly, reference [130] indicated an increase in mean CRP levels with increased smear AFB positivity; 13.1, 20.4 and 37.5 for +1, +2 and +3 respectively. Another study highlighted that CRP levels were highest in the Smear3+ group (60.00) compared to Smear2+ (35.83) and Smear1+ (5) patients [38].

Other studies have indicated that the elevation of CRP in PTB is closely linked to disease severity and increased lung tissue damage due to higher AFB grading [39, 133, 134]. Subgroup analysis was revealed a progressive increase in CRP levels with higher smear AFB positivity levels (+1, +2, +3, +4). This rise in CRP levels can be attributed to the escalating bacterial load triggering an inflammatory response. In diabetic individuals, the combination of infection susceptibility, oxidative stress, and increased tissue inflammation may contribute to a poorer outcome in active PTB. In the current study, diabetic patients with active PTB infection exhibited lower mean Hb levels in (g/dl) compared to non-diabetic patients, with values of 10.03 for diabetic patients and 10.98 for non-diabetic patients, showing statistical significance (p < 0.05). Additionally, the mean of total WBCs in (/mm3) was significantly higher in diabetic patients with active PTB infection compared to non-diabetic patients. The findings also revealed that the mean of ESR levels in (mm/hr) were significantly higher in diabetic patients with active PTB infection compared to non-diabetic patients (p < 0.05) as shown in (Table 2). Furthermore, the mean of CRP levels in (mg/L) were significantly elevated in diabetic patients with active PTB infection compared to non-diabetic patients (p < 0.05) as presented in (Table 2).

In this study, the average Hb levels in (g/dl) were found to be lower in female patients with active PTB infection compared to male patients, with values of 10.10 for females and 11.59 for males, showing statistical significance (p < 0.05). These findings contradict a study by [108]. Since iron is an important growth factor for MTB, the retention of iron in the reticuloendothelial system is considered one of the host defense mechanisms, and numerous therapeutic trials have been conducted [135]. The impact of iron retention may be more exaggerated in women with TB because women are more likely than men to be iron deficient [136]. This may explain why female sex is a risk factor for anemia. In addition, the study revealed that the average of total WBCs (/mm3) was significantly higher in female patients with active PTB infection compared to male patients, with values of 12,347.50 for females and 10,388.33 for males, showing statistical significance (p < 0.05). Additionally, the study found that the average of ESR levels in (mm/hr) and CRP levels in (mg/L) were higher in female patients with active PTB infection compared to male patients, with values of 107.53 for ESR in females and 11.59 for males, and 44.70 for CRP in females and 38.60 for males, showing statistical significance (p < 0.05). According to these results, females may be more likely to be infected by severe active PTB compared to males.

Correlation of hematological and inflammatory parameters with AFB severity

This study indicated significant positive correlation (p < 0.05) between age and AFB severity. Hb was negatively correlated with AFB severity (r = −0.661, p < 0.001) similar results by [137] showed significant negative correlation between hemoglobin levels and TB severity (r = −0.58, p < 0.0001) and other previous studies [138, 139]. Whereas Total WBCs count showed positive correlation with AFB severity. Positive correlation was also detected among inflammatory parameters (ESR levels and CRP titer) and AFB severity (p < 0.05) as illustrated in (Table 3).

Limitations

Some It is essential to address important limitations of the findings. First: due to the period limit and the economic condition in Yemen, the present study is carried out on (100) patients. Second: numerous complications in finding the samples, testing it and following the patient during six months for monitoring efficacy of treatment. These obstacles include war, siege and COVID-19. In addition, it is hoped that, the study obtained the requirements for diagnosis MTB infection based on gene Xpert to detect active pulmonary infection with TB. However, the situation of our country and the critical stage of the world at that time impaired us from obtaining it. In addition, lack of control group, no alternative markers studied overreliance on expensive tests in this study. Accordingly, the AFB staining, chest findings by X-ray, culture and/or TST are used. Furthermore, other limitations includes; the study was conducted at a single center. Absence of a healthy control group. Use of a semi-quantitative method CRP. Potential confounding factors such as nutritional status.

Conclusions

The findings of this study suggest that several factors for example; high grade of AFB severity, age between 21 and 35 years, male gender, rural residence, and diabetes may be related to higher risk of active PTB. In addition, hematological parameters (e.g., Hb and Total WBCs counts) decreased, whereas inflammatory markers (CRP and ESR) showed significant elevation before treatment in active PTB patients. These parameters showed gradual improvement following anti-TB therapy. Although these results indicate that hematological and inflammatory markers could have potential value in supporting the diagnosis and monitoring of treatment response in active PTB patients, further large-scale studies are recommended to confirm these findings.

Recommendations

Future studies include suitable control group of healthy persons to allow for more accurate comparison of hematological and inflammatory alterations associated with PTB. Conducting multicenter studies with larger and more diverse populations although that in Governorate there is only one center for TB. Furthermore, additional biomarkers such as cytokines could be used. It is also recommended to include follow-up assessments, principally among drug-resistant cases.
