An expanded cortical map of von Economo neurons in the human medial prefrontal cortex
Authors: Daniela Ortiz-Muñoz, Carlos Arturo González-Acosta, Oscar Alonso Plaza-Patiño, Efraín Buriticá-Ramírez, Lina Vanessa Becerra-Hernández
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractVon Economo neurons (VENs) are large bipolar projection cells, confined to the anterior cingulate and frontoinsular cortices, yet their broader cortical distribution remains unresolved. Previously, we reported these neurons in the dorsomedial portion of cortical area 9 and polar-medial area 10. Here, we describe the presence, density, and morphological features of VENs across seven cytoarchitectonic areas of the human medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) (25, 32p, 32s, 10m, 14r, 11m, and 10r). Using immunohistochemistry and quantitative morphometry in postmortem tissue, we identified VENs and established consistent distribution patterns, with VENs significantly more abundant in dysgranular (32p, 32s, 25) than granular areas (10m, 10r, 11m, 14r), showing higher densities and larger somata preferentially located on cortical crests and in the right hemisphere. On average, VENs were 55% larger than neighboring pyramidal neurons, and their density covaried with soma size, revealing a regional efficiency gradient. These findings extend the known VEN map beyond previously described regions, indicating that their organization reflects cytoarchitectonic specialization and hemispheric asymmetry. By demonstrating that VENs populate a broader mPFC network implicated in interoception, motivation, and social cognition, these results support a proposed role for VENs as integrative nodes within prefrontal limbic circuits, with potential relevance for neuropsychiatric disorders.

Introduction

Von Economo neurons (VENs) are characterized by a large, spindle-shaped soma and by two distinctive, thick, and sparsely branched dendritic processes that emerge from the apical and basal poles of the cell body (Nimchinsky et al. 1999; Allman et al. 2002). These processes are oriented perpendicularly toward the pial surface (apical dendrite) and toward the subcortical white matter (basal dendrite) (Allman et al. 2002; Fajardo et al. 2008). Their distribution has been previously described in the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) and the frontoinsular cortex (FI) (Nimchinsky et al. 1999; Allman et al. 2011).

The human medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) constitutes one of the three principal subdivisions of the prefrontal cortex, together with the dorsolateral and orbitofrontal regions. It has been primarily associated with motivation, emotional control (Fuster 2008; Xu et al. 2019), and the temporal regulation of action, enabling decisions about when to act based on internal and external signals (Hunt 2021). Comparative studies in rodents, nonhuman primates, and humans have revealed a progressive specialization of medial prefrontal circuits in social processing, correlated with the increasing complexity of the social repertoire across species (Gangopadhyay et al. 2021). This region exhibits marked cytoarchitectonic heterogeneity, including dysgranular and granular areas (Öngür et al. 2003), reflecting both its phylogenetic derivation and its functional connectivity.

Our research group has previously reported the presence of VENs in the mPFC, specifically in the dorsomedial portion of cortical area 9 and in the polar portion of cortical area 10 (Fajardo et al. 2008; González-Acosta et al. 2018). This prefrontal cortical surface displays a highly interconnected internal organization, with reciprocal projections among subregions such as the ACC and the FI cortex. Such architecture suggests that the presence of VENs may not be restricted to the areas described by Nimchinsky et al. (1999) and Allman et al. (2011). Moreover, its connectivity pattern has been proposed to favor the dynamic integration of emotional, cognitive, and contextual information (Haber et al. 2022). The mPFC participates in the integration of interoceptive (Lieberman et al. 2019) and exteroceptive signals, enabling functions such as perspective taking (Isoda 2021), social categorization, social decision-making, and emotional regulation in social contexts, processes encompassed under the concept of social cognition (Grossmann, 2013; Bzdok et al. 2013).

The aim of the present study was to identify and quantify VENs in seven previously unexplored areas of the mPFC (the septal area or cortical area 25, and areas 32p, 32 s, 10m, 11m, 14r, and 10r) according to the nomenclature of Öngür et al. (2003), analyzing their distribution in relation to the cytoarchitectonic organization of each region. Our findings confirm the presence of VENs across nearly the entire medial surface of the human medial prefrontal cortex, revealing density and proportion patterns that may reflect differentiated functional demands as well as diverse connectivity profiles within this established frontal limbic network.

Methods

Tissue samples

Brain tissue samples were obtained from 6 adult donors aged 27 to 35 years, provided by the Instituto Nacional de Medicina Legal y Ciencias Forenses (INMLCF) of Valle del Cauca (Table 1). Donors with a history of immediate hospitalization prior to death, or with neurological or psychiatric disorders, or any central nervous system pathology listed as a cause of death or identified postmortem, were excluded. From each fresh brain, tissue blocks of approximately 1 cm3 were bilaterally dissected from mPFC areas 25, 32p, 32s, 10m, 11m, 14r, and 10r, according to the topographical criteria defined by Öngür et al. (2003). From each hemisphere, one tissue block of approximately 1 cm3 per cytoarchitectonic area was dissected, yielding an average of 14 blocks per subject (7 areas × 2 hemispheres). Sampling was centered on each target area, with a margin left between adjacent sampling sites to avoid overlapping between regions. Area boundaries were established following the topographical and morphological criteria of Öngür et al. (2003): area 25 was sampled from the subgenual cortex overlying the septal area; areas 32p and 32s from the paracingulate gyrus; and areas 10m, 14r, 11m, and 10r from the medial surface of the gyrus rectus and the adjacent ventromedial prefrontal surface. The anatomical location of each sampled region is illustrated in (Fig. 1). Samples were rinsed for 5 min in 0.9% saline solution and subsequently immersion-fixed in 2% paraformaldehyde containing lysine and sodium periodate, adjusted to pH 7.4, and maintained at 4 °C for 7 to 10 d. Several tissue blocks were excluded due to insufficient immunoreactivity, resulting in absent or uninterpretable NeuN labeling. Specifically, the following samples were excluded: left area 32s of subject CEC-CON006; left area 14r of subjects CEC-CON006 and CEC-CON012. These exclusions did not affect the overall conclusions, as data from the remaining hemispheres and subjects were available for all areas. All procedures were approved by the Human Research Ethics Committees of the Faculty of Health at Universidad del Valle (027-021) and the INMLCF (PPY-2022-003) and were conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Histological procedure and cell quantification

Histological processing and analytical procedures were performed according to protocols previously established by our group and described in González-Acosta et al. (2018). Coronal sections (50 μm) immunolabeled with anti-NeuN (clone A60, MAB377, EDM Millipore-Sigma Aldrich, Merck; 1:2500) were analyzed by bright-field light microscopy using an AxioCam HRc camera coupled to a ZEISS Scope A1 microscope. A total of 2,517 photomicrographs were acquired, covering sections from both hemispheres and all regions of interest (Table S1).

Samples included both crests and walls, depending on the anatomical site of extraction. Cortical architecture was reconstructed for each section to estimate total cortical thickness and layer V thickness, stratified by region, hemisphere, and subject (Table S2). Cytoarchitectonic verification of the regions of interest was performed directly on NeuN-immunolabeled sections using low-magnification objectives (4× and 10×), without the use of adjacent Nissl-stained sections. Anti-NeuN (Fox-3) selectively labels mature postmitotic neurons and produces a differential staining pattern particularly useful for VEN identification: VEN somata exhibit characteristically weaker cytoplasmic labeling compared to neighboring pyramidal neurons in sublayer Vb, while their ovoid nuclei and proximal processes remain clearly delineated. This staining contrast, combined with the distinctive spindle-shaped morphology and bipolar orientation of VENs, allowed reliable distinction from other bipolar neurons within the same layer. This approach is consistent with protocols previously established by our group (Fajardo et al. 2008; González-Acosta et al. 2018). VEN identification was conducted using 10× and 40× objectives within the full extent of layer Vb, following previously established morphological criteria. Two observers with extensive prior experience in similar studies independently validated cell classification (Buriticá-Ramírez and Pimienta-Jimenez 2007; González-Acosta et al. 2018).

In sections in which VENs were identified, VEN-to-pyramidal neuron ratios were calculated by counting both populations within defined grids placed over layer Vb. Grid dimensions were adjusted to the specific thickness of layer Vb in each cortical region and sector: in walls, grid height was adapted to the laminar thickness of layer Vb; in crests, standardized grids of 300 × 300 μm were used. In addition, the somatic area of all identified VENs and of at least 5 adjacent pyramidal neurons per VEN was measured. Measurements were performed on calibrated images acquired with a 40× objective, by manually delineating the soma using ImageJ (NIH, USA). Only neurons displaying intact morphology and without overlapping were included. In total, 1,003 VENs and approximately 5,000 pyramidal neurons were measured across all areas, hemispheres, and subjects. All analyses were performed under double-blind conditions to minimize bias and ensure data reliability.

We also conducted an exploratory assessment of the local spatial arrangement of VENs. For each analyzed field, we recorded the presence or absence of VEN “clusters,” operationally defined as small groups of two to three or more VEN somata located in proximity and clearly separated from other VENs within the same field. This assessment was based on visual inspection of high-resolution images and did not include formal spatial analyses; it should, therefore, be considered a qualitative, observer-dependent measure.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using R software (RStudio version 4.4.2; R Core Team). Descriptive statistics included calculations of means, medians, standard deviations, and coefficients of variation for each variable. Data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk and Kruskal–Wallis tests. Given that most datasets did not meet the assumption of normality, nonparametric tests were employed for inferential comparisons.

Differences in the VENs/Pyramidal neuron ratio between crests and walls, as well as between hemispheres, were evaluated using the Mann–Whitney U test. Comparisons of soma size between neuronal types (VENs vs. pyramidal neurons) and across cortical sectors were analyzed using one-way ANOVA or, where applicable, two-way ANOVA. For multiple comparisons, Dunn’s post hoc test with Bonferroni correction was applied. To examine potential interactions among cortical regions, gyrus sector (crests vs. wall), and hemisphere, generalized linear mixed models (GLMMs) were fitted. Inter-rater reliability for neuronal identification and morphometric measurements was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05 for all comparisons. When relevant, effect size estimates and estimated marginal means (Emmeans) were also computed to enhance interpretability of the results.

Results

In the present study, we sought to explore cortical areas on the medial surface of the human prefrontal cortex in which the presence of VENs has not previously been examined and/or reported, namely: the septal area (cortical area 25), ventromedial regions (14r and 11m), portions 10m and 10r of area 10, and portions 32p and 32s of area 32 according to Öngür et al. (2003). Tables and graphs presenting the results are organized following a caudo-rostral sequence within the mPFC, starting from the anterior cingulate region: 25, 32p, 32s, 10m, 14r, 11m, 10r.

Density of Von Economo neurons across seven regions of the human medial prefrontal cortex: hemispheric and gyral portion differences

In sections in which VENs were identified, the highest cellular density was found in area 32p (4.70 VENs/mm2, SD = 8.94), with no statistically significant differences compared with areas 32s and 25 (P = 1.00). In contrast, area 11m (1.38 VENs/mm2, SD = 4.37) exhibited one of the lowest VEN densities in the mPFC (P < 0.0001), with no statistically significant differences compared with areas 10m, 10r, and 14r (P = 1.00). Accordingly, two clusters of cortical areas can be distinguished based on VEN density: 11m, 10m, 10r, and 14r as low-density regions, and 25, 32s, and 32p as high-density regions (Fig. 2A). VENs were in both crests and walls but were observed more frequently in the crests across all cortical areas examined (Fig. 2B, Table 2). This difference was statistically significant in all cortical areas, including 10m (P = 0.0058), 11m (P = 0.0012), and 32p (P < 0.00001), except for area 25.

In addition, hemispheric differences were observed (P = 0.0179), with higher VEN density in the right hemisphere compared with the left. A rightward bias in VEN density was evident across all cortical areas studied, except for area 25, in which density was higher in the left hemisphere. However, a statistically significant hemispheric difference was detected only in area 14r, where VEN density was higher in the right hemisphere (P < 0.005) (Fig. 2C).

Von Economo neuron-to-pyramidal neuron ratio (mean, standard deviation) in sublayer Vb: hemispheric and gyral portion differences

The proportion of VENs relative to pyramidal neurons in layer Vb displayed a heterogeneous distribution across mPFC areas. The highest VEN to pyramidal ratios were observed in regions 32p (0.91, 1.48) and 32s (1.03, 1.94), followed by 25 (1.20, 2.99), consistent with the areas that also exhibited the highest VENs/mm2 values. In contrast, more anterior and ventromedial areas such as 10r (0.25, 0.61), 10m (0.29, 0.82), and 11m (0.24, 0.77) showed markedly lower ratios (Fig. 2E).

In several areas, the VEN to pyramidal ratio was significantly higher in crests than in walls. For instance, in area 11m, the ratio was higher in crests compared with walls (P = 0.0012), and in 32p, a significant increase in the ratio was observed in crests relative to walls (P < 0.00001). Similarly, the VEN to pyramidal ratio was significantly higher (P < 0.0001) in right-hemisphere areas such as 32p and 32s compared with their left hemisphere counterparts and with more rostral or ventromedial areas (Table 2).

Across the full dataset (2,517 fields analyzed), VEN clusters were very rare. In most fields, VENs appeared as isolated neurons rather than forming clusters. We identified 23 clusters composed of 2 to 3 VENs each (Fig. 3), corresponding to approximately 0.91% of all fields. These groups are scattered across several of the cortical areas examined and in different sectors of the gyri, without hemispheric predominance. Of the 23 VENs clusters identified by visual inspection, the majority were found in areas 32p (12 clusters) and 25 (8 clusters).

Somatic size of Von Economo neurons in the human medial prefrontal cortex

VENs observed in the cortical areas analyzed in the present study exhibited their characteristic morphology, with elongated spindle-shaped somata and a perpendicular orientation relative to the pial surface. These neurons displayed a single apical and a single basal process, both aligned along the vertical axis, which clearly distinguished them from surrounding pyramidal neurons. Immunostaining with the anti-NeuN antibody showed weak cytoplasmic labeling contrasted with a more intense nuclear signal, facilitating identification and allowing clear delineation of their centrally located, ovoid nuclei (Fig. 3). This combination of shape, orientation, and staining pattern was consistent with previous descriptions of VENs in humans and other higher primates.

With respect to neuronal soma size (somatic area in μm2), VENs were consistently larger than pyramidal neurons within the same cortical layer across all cortical sectors evaluated. In area 32p, the mean somatic area of VENs was 148.3 μm2 (SD: 35.2), compared with 95.6 μm2 (SD: 25.4) in pyramidal neurons. In 32s, VENs exhibited a somatic area of 152.7 μm2 (SD: 38.1) and pyramidal neurons 97.2 μm2 (SD: 23.9). In area 25, VENs reached 145.6 μm2 (SD: 30.8), versus 92.4 μm2 (SD: 21.7) in pyramidal neurons. In more rostral areas such as 10r, somatic size was 131.1 μm2 (SD: 28.3) for VENs and 91.8 μm2 (SD: 22.5) for pyramidal neurons; in 10m, 128.4 μm2 (SD: 26.9) and 90.2 μm2 (SD: 20.3), respectively; in 11m, 130.7 μm2 (SD: 31.4) and 93.0 μm2 (SD: 24.6); and finally, in 14r, 127.5 μm2 (SD: 29.6) for VENs compared with 89.7 μm2 (SD: 19.8) for pyramidal neurons (Fig. 2D and E).

Overall, VENs were approximately 1.55 times (55%) larger than pyramidal neurons within the same layer Vb, a difference that was statistically significant (P < 0.001). Analysis of VEN somatic size in relation to gyral topography revealed that neurons located in crests were significantly larger than those in walls. The mean somatic area in crests was 145.9 μm2 (SD: 31.7), whereas in walls it was 122.3 μm2 (SD: 26.4), corresponding to a 1.19-fold (19.3%) larger size in crests (P = 0.010). With respect to laterality, a significant hemispheric effect on VEN somatic size was identified. Neurons in the right hemisphere exhibited a mean somatic area of 141.2 μm2 (SD: 30.8), whereas in the left hemisphere it was 126.2 μm2 (SD: 27.9), corresponding to a 1.12-fold (11.9%) larger size in the right hemisphere (model estimate: +15.02 μm2, P = 0.011) (Fig. 2E).

Discussion

The present work allowed us to demonstrate the presence of von Economo neurons across the remaining medial surface of the human prefrontal cortex, specifically within cortical area 25 and regions 32p, 32s, 10m, 14r, 11m, and 10r, according to the cytoarchitectonic map of Öngür et al. (2003). The exploration of new regions in which these neurons might be located has been driven both by interest in understanding their functional significance and by connectivity studies in nonhuman primates describing links between the anterior insular cortex and the ACC with the mPFC (Cabeen et al. 2020), areas in which VENs have been previously reported. Our findings extend and refine the map of VEN distribution within the human prefrontal cortex, where these neurons have been proposed to represent a subcortical projection cell type based on their localization within layer Vb of areas that are strongly connected with diencephalic, brainstem, and spinal cord structures, as well as on their morphology, somatic size, and transcriptomic evidence showing similarities to pyramidal neurons of the same cortical layer (Hodge et al. 2020).

The functional significance of VENs remains an open question that cannot be resolved based on anatomical localization alone. As noted by Raghanti et al. (2015), the widespread phylogenetic distribution of VENs across species of varying encephalization and social complexity indicates that the selective forces underlying their emergence have not been fully identified, and caution is warranted when inferring function from location. Nevertheless, several convergent lines of evidence, beyond anatomical localization, support the notion that VENs in the human mPFC may participate in integrative functions linking interoception, affect, and social cognition. First, Transcriptomic profiling has shown that VENs share molecular signatures with extratelencephalic projection neurons (Hodge et al. 2020), a finding further supported by Pineda et al. (2024), who identified a population of layer V VAT1L-expressing neurons in the human prefrontal cortex sharing molecular identity with VENs and showing selective depletion in frontotemporal lobar degeneration (FTLD).

Second, their biochemical profile, including expression of neuromedin B, ATF3, and IL4Rα, has been associated with interoceptive and autonomic functions in the frontoinsular and anterior cingulate cortices (Allman et al. 2011). Third, the cytoarchitectonic organization of the areas where VENs are most abundant in our sample, agranular and dysgranular regions proximal to the ACC, is directly relevant. According to the Structural Model (García-Cabezas et al. 2019), areas of simpler laminar elaboration, such as the agranular and dysgranular cortices that include areas 25, 32p, and 32s, establish preferential feedback connections with subcortical structures through infragranular layers V and VI, the precise laminar location of VENs. This architectural feature predicts a connectivity profile consistent with the functional domains, visceromotor control, interoceptive integration, and autonomic regulation, previously attributed to these areas. Finally, in humans, VENs appear restricted to limbic and paralimbic sectors of the frontal, insular, and temporal cortices, a species-specific pattern that, as proposed by González-Acosta et al. (2022), suggests a functional association with the limbic structures in which they are embedded. These converging morphological, molecular, architectonic, and clinical lines of evidence support a proposed, though not yet directly demonstrated, role for VENs as integrative projection neurons within prefrontal limbic circuits.

The mPFC, described as a region with a mosaic-like cytoarchitectonic organization, encompasses areas ranging from nearly agranular to dysgranular and granular, each with distinct connectivity profiles. Our results are consistent with this heterogeneity: dysgranular areas (32p, 32s, and 25) exhibited the highest neuronal densities and the greatest VEN to pyramidal ratios, particularly in crests (32p, 32s) and in walls (25). This pattern aligns with previous reports linking dysgranular zones to interoceptive and affective integration, functions that have been attributed to VENs. In contrast, granular areas that are more rostral and dorsal relative to the ACC (14r, 11m, 10r, 10m) showed substantially lower VEN densities and VEN to pyramidal ratios, indicating that VEN distribution is not uniform and may reflect functional specializations of individual subregions (Fig. 2F).

The literature has reported very low proportions of VENs relative to the total neuronal population of layer V, including cortical area 10p (0.0008) (González-Acosta et al. 2018), cortical area 9 (0.005), and cortical area 24 (0.03) (Fajardo et al. 2008). In our study, the VEN to pyramidal ratio ranged from 0.24 to 1.20 across the 7 cortical areas analyzed. This difference may be explained by methodological factors: previous studies assessed the entirety of layer V, whereas we quantified exclusively within sublayer Vb, where VENs are concentrated. Nevertheless, the comparative pattern was preserved: dysgranular areas (the more caudal regions) exhibited higher ratios, whereas granular areas (more rostral, dorsal, and ventral) showed lower ratios. More distal sectors from the ACC, such as 10p, and ventral sectors such as 11m displayed the lowest values, with statistically significant differences across cortical areas and between gyral sectors.

These findings support two non-mutually exclusive hypotheses. The first relates to mechanical factors associated with cortical gyrification, particularly in regions adjacent to the cingulate gyrus, which may modulate the concentration of VENs in crest and walls. The second proposes a greater abundance of VENs in agranular/dysgranular areas, consistent with their divergent connectivity toward subcortical structures (González-Acosta et al. 2022). Both hypotheses are elaborated in detail in the following sections, supporting a proposed role for VENs as rapid projection neurons within motivational, autonomic, and affective circuits.

With respect to somatic size, VENs exhibited significantly larger cell bodies than pyramidal neurons within the same layer (P < 0.001), in agreement with reports describing up to fivefold larger somata (Nimchinsky et al. 1999; Raghanti et al. 2015; Evrard 2018). This effect was evaluated using a linear mixed-effects model with fixed effects of cell type, cortical area, hemisphere, and gyral sector (crest/wall), and a random effect for the subject. Agranular/dysgranular sectors within or adjacent to the ACC, which showed higher VEN densities, also exhibited larger somata, whereas granular areas distal to the ACC displayed smaller sizes. This indicates that cell type and regional cytoarchitecture are the primary determinants of VEN somatic size. Gyral sector (crest vs. wall) showed a modest effect (P = 0.01), and hemispheric laterality did not emerge as a relevant factor in this model (P = 0.247).

However, we observed a subtle lateralization trend, with significant hemispheric differences (P = 0.0179) and slightly higher values in the right hemisphere, consistent with literature describing limbic lateralization toward the right hemisphere for socioemotional and predominantly sympathetic autonomic functions (Ahern and Schwartz 1985; Allen et al. 2021). Nonetheless, the crest/wall effect was more pronounced than the hemispheric effect, indicating that local folding geometry exerts a stronger influence on the relative presence of VENs.

Regarding cortical topography, González-Acosta et al. (2018) reported higher density and greater somatic volume in crests compared with walls, a finding aligned with the mechanical hypothesis of cortical folding. In our study, this trend was replicated in areas 32p and 32s; however, in 25 and 14r, the inverse pattern predominated. In addition, we observed significant gyral sector differences in 10m (p.adj = 0.0058), 11 m (p.adj = 0.00004), and 14r (p.adj = 0.0292). This indicates that the relationship between crest/wall location and VEN presence depends on the region-specific cytoarchitectonic organization. For most areas (32p, 32s, 10m, 14r, 11m, and 10r), tissue sections contained both crests and walls, allowing topographic comparisons between sectors. Area 25 was an exception: due to its deep subgenual location and curved geometry, the majority of successfully obtained tissue blocks corresponded predominantly to walls, with very few crest samples available. This limits the interpretation of crests-versus-walls topographic effects specifically for area 25. The absence of VENs in the deep portions of sulci further supports the influence of cortical folding (Butti et al. 2014) and differential proliferative (Kolk and Rakic 2022) dynamics on neuronal morphology.

One intrinsic variable that may contribute to VEN morphology is the tension generated during growth toward crests (Hilgetag and Barbas 2006). This phenomenon, described as axonal tension, is considered relevant to gyral formation (Striedter et al. 2015) and could account for the elongated somatic profile of these neurons. On this basis, some authors have suggested that VENs may not represent an independent lineage, but rather an elongation of preexisting neurons, possibly pyramidal cells. In partial support of this hypothesis, Hodge et al. (2020) reported genetic similarities between VENs and projection neurons targeting subcortical structures. However, this mechanical model has important limitations, as it does not account for the presence of VENs in walls or in lissencephalic brains; therefore, the question of a specialized lineage remains open.

Functional analysis of the cortical areas containing VENs further complements this interpretation, given the previously reported presence of VENs in the ACC, FI, dorsomedial cortical area 9, and polar cortical area 10 (Hakeem et al. 2009; Allman et al. 2010; Butti et al. 2014; Cauda et al. 2014), and the present report of their occurrence in additional cortical regions of the human prefrontal cortex (25, 32p, 32s, 10m, 14r, 11m, 10r) that are integrated into functions such as interoception, emotional monitoring, fear processing, social cognition, and motivation (Hiser and Koenigs 2018). Dysgranular cingulate areas (32p, 32s) are associated with internal state monitoring, autonomic regulation, and conflict evaluation; cortical area 25 with basic emotions and visceromotor control; and more rostral and ventromedial areas (10, 14r, 11m, 10r) with social cognition, prospection, self-referential processing, and contextual decision-making. The localization of VENs within these sectors suggests an expanded role, not limited to autonomic modulation, but extending to efficient intracortical transmission within higher-order behavioral regulatory circuits.

These functional domains overlap with disorders in which alterations in VEN density or morphology have been reported, including schizophrenia (Brüne et al. 2010), frontotemporal dementia, for which the evidence of VEN vulnerability is particularly robust, with selective loss reported across multiple independent studies (Seeley et al. 2006; Seeley et al. 2007; Kim et al. 2012; Santillo et al. 2013; Gittings et al. 2023; Pineda et al. 2024), Alzheimer’s disease (Kim et al. 2012; Santillo and Englund 2014), and autism spectrum disorder, although findings in the latter are heterogeneous: while some studies report reductions in VEN density or near-total absence of these neurons (Uppal et al. 2014), and others describe increases in the VEN-to-pyramidal ratio or atypical morphological changes (Santos et al. 2011; Simms et al. 2009), Kennedy et al. (2007) found no significant reduction in frontoinsular VEN number in autism, suggesting that VEN pathology in this condition may depend on the cortical region examined, the age of the sample, or methodological differences across studies. All are characterized by structural or functional abnormalities of the mPFC, together with deficits in social cognition, emotional regulation, or interoception, supporting the notion that VENs constitute vulnerable nodes within these circuits.

A secondary and exploratory observation in our material was the occasional presence of small clusters of 2 and 3 VENs. In total, we identified 23 such groupings, detected solely by visual inspection and without formal spatial analytical methods due to their rarity. While some clusters were observed in areas contributing a larger number of sampled fields, such as 32p, others appeared in regions with fewer sampled fields but high VEN density, such as area 25, suggesting that these groupings may reflect local variations in neuronal packing rather than a genuine spatial organizational pattern.

The vast majority of VENs identified in our sample series appeared as isolated neurons intermingled with pyramidal cells, consistent with the view that VENs constitute a sparse and widely distributed population. This result agrees with previous work from our group in the medial portion of area 10p (González-Acosta et al. 2018), where no defined clusters were observed. Nevertheless, other studies in cortical area 24 have reported local aggregations of VENs in cluster-like arrangements (Jacot-Descombes et al. 2020). The discrepancy between these observations and our data may reflect regional differences within the medial prefrontal system, variations in density or laminar thickness, or the absence of uniform quantitative criteria to define a “cluster.” To determine whether VENs exhibit any consistent deviation from spatial randomness, future studies will need to apply quantitative point-pattern analyses to full reconstructions of the neuronal field.

Future studies should extend the molecular characterization of VENs in these mPFC regions, building on established markers such as VAT1L, CHST8, LYPD1, and SULF2 (Yang et al. 2018), GABRQ (Dijkstra et al. 2016), and transcriptomic evidence of their selective vulnerability to TDP-43 pathology in ALS and FTD (Gittings et al. 2023; Pineda et al. 2024).

Methodological considerations

The acquisition of cortical area 25 tissue posed challenges related to its subgenual location. The acquisition of cortical area 25 tissue posed challenges related to its subgenual location, as the cutting plane intersected adjacent structures in several initial blocks, resulting in absent or uninterpretable cellular content. After adjusting the sampling plane, adequate tissue was obtained from four subjects for the right hemisphere and two subjects for the left hemisphere. Complete details of tissue availability by subject ID are provided in (Table S2). This limitation constrains the interpretation of topographic and hemispheric effects in this region. In addition, the ventral and deeply curved geometry of cortical area 25 resulted in most available blocks corresponding to walls, with very few crests. This limitation did not affect the identification or quantification of VENs, but it does constrain the interpretation of topographic effects (crest vs. wall) in this region compared with the other areas studied.

Another limitation was the small number of postmortem subjects who met our strict inclusion criteria and did not fulfill exclusion criteria. Although brain tissue was collected from individuals with postmortem intervals between 10 and 15 h, several samples exhibited histological irregularities (altered or reduced immunoreactivity) that led us to exclude them from the study. The age of the individuals whose brain tissue was analyzed was restricted to a narrow range of young adults (27 to 35 years at the time of death), precluding the organization of age groups or the examination of variations in VEN abundance across different stages of the lifespan between 18 and 65 years.

Among the most difficult methodological aspects to address in our samples was the inclusion of individuals of both sexes. Ultimately, only one female sample could be included. Meaningful comparisons between one female and five male samples are not feasible. This limitation has been consistent across our previous studies: in 2008, Fajardo et al. (2008) were unable to include any female samples, and in González-Acosta et al. (2018) were only able to include a single female sample. Thus, across 3 studies of VENs using fresh human brain tissue, of 19 postmortem subjects sampled, only 2 females have been successfully included.

Conclusion

Taking together, our findings demonstrate that the distribution of VENs in the human mPFC is not homogeneous, but instead follows clearly defined cytoarchitectonic, topographic, and hemispheric gradients. Agranular/dysgranular regions proximal to the ACC exhibit higher densities and larger somatic sizes than granular regions distal to the ACC. In several areas, crests contain more VENs than walls, and a rightward hemispheric bias in VEN density was observed, reaching statistical significance in area 14. This pattern suggests that VEN organization reflects a balance between density and somatic size, modulated both by mechanical factors associated with tension generated during cortical gyrification and by factors related to subregional connectivity and functional lateralization.

We propose that the interaction between cortical mechanics and functional architecture, particularly limbic and visceromotor connectivity in dysgranular mPFC and socioaffective integration in ventromedial PFC, co-determines the localization and morphology of these neurons. Advancing our understanding of the true role of VENs in the human brain will require integrative approaches combining lineage-specific markers, structural and functional connectomics (including tract tracing and tractography), single-cell molecular profiling, and clinical correlates, with particular emphasis on dysgranular subregions. Only through such multiscale integration will it be possible to elucidate their function, their vulnerability in neuropsychiatric disorders, and their contribution to the circuits linking interoception, affect, and social cognition.
