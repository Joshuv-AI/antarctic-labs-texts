Global research priorities for back pain, neck pain and osteoarthritis: a scoping review
Authors: James J Young, Julie Rønne Pedersen, Lauren K King, Michael Zywiel, Anthony V Perruccio, Vinod Chandran, Y Raja Rampersaud, Gillian Hawker, Andrew M Briggs, Jan Hartvigsen, Bart Koes
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThe primary aim of this scoping review was to synthesise published research priorities for back pain, neck pain and osteoarthritis. The secondary aim was to compare these research priorities to the priority areas identified in the Global Strategy to Improve Musculoskeletal Health.DesignScoping review conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis extension for Scoping Reviews.Data sourcesLiterature searches were performed in MEDLINE, EMBASE and the James Lind Alliance Priority Setting Partnership database from inception to March 2025.Eligibility criteriaPeer-reviewed studies reporting the development of a prioritised ranking of research topics related to back pain, neck pain or osteoarthritis were included.Data synthesisIncluded research priority sets were described. All research priorities for back pain, neck pain and osteoarthritis were presented. Research priorities were categorised according to priority areas identified in the Global Strategy to Improve Musculoskeletal Health.Results15 studies from 3721 citations and three from the James Lind Alliance were included. Eight priority sets related to back and/or neck pain identified 155 research priorities. 15 priority sets related to osteoarthritis identified 206 research priorities. Most priority sets (69.6%) included patients in the development process, but only 17.4% included policymakers. Just three priority sets included participants from outside Europe, North America or Australia.Most priorities were in the clinical and basic science research priority area (73.1%) followed by the health policy and systems research priority area (14.1%), health economics research priority area (4.7%), public health research priority area (4.4%), epidemiological and population health research priority area (1.9%) and the other priority area (1.7%).ConclusionsThe majority of published back pain, neck pain and osteoarthritis research priorities fall within clinical and basic science and were developed in high income countries. Given the known high population burden of musculoskeletal conditions, a broader approach to research beyond clinical and basic science is likely required to achieve population-level health benefits. Future initiatives should include all relevant members of the global musculoskeletal health community, including from low- and middle-income countries, to help ensure the development of broad-based research priorities that address all research domains required to improve back and neck pain and osteoarthritis health globally.

Background

Musculoskeletal conditions account for a leading share of the global disability burden.1 2 Back pain, neck pain and osteoarthritis are among the most prevalent musculoskeletal conditions. These have significant pain and disability-related individual and population-level impact,3–5 and consequently lead to major healthcare expenses and overall societal burden and cost.6 Because the burden of these three conditions continues to increase, a coordinated global response is needed to confront the challenge that must include a comprehensive research agenda.7 To meet the challenge, the Global Strategy to Improve Musculoskeletal Health was created.7 8

The Global Strategy to Improve Musculoskeletal Health (released in 2021) identified five research priority areas (table 1) needed to develop a learning health system.7 Although several research priorities for back pain, neck pain and osteoarthritis have been put forward by a number of different groups over the years,9 it is unclear to what extent current research priorities align with the recently identified global research priority areas. An overview and synthesis of published priorities is required to inform an overall global research agenda for the most prevalent and high-burden conditions. Bourne et al9 previously reviewed research priorities for musculoskeletal conditions up to 2017 and found seven research priority sets for osteoarthritis, four for back pain and none for neck pain. As several research prioritisation studies have been published since the Bourne et al9 study, a new systematic examination is warranted. Further, while the Bourne et al9 study categorised the research priorities by thematic area, these were broad categories that do not directly align with the Global Strategy to Improve Musculoskeletal Health priority areas7 and include priorities for all musculoskeletal conditions, like rheumatoid arthritis, osteoporosis and systemic lupus erythematosus.

Therefore, a comprehensive summary of the research priorities put forth within the back pain, neck pain and osteoarthritis research ecosystems is needed as a foundational step in the development of an aligned global musculoskeletal health research agenda. To engage stronger collaborative networks and shared research infrastructure, understanding the extent to which all priority areas are covered (or are under-represented) and identifying potential synergies in the research for these most prevalent and burdensome conditions is key. A comprehensive review with a view towards improving coordination of research and cooperation among the scientific community, including patient, clinician and policymaker partners, and to inform the necessary resourcing and funding allocations to meet these goals is needed.10 11

The primary aim of this scoping review was to identify and synthesise all published research priorities for back pain, neck pain and osteoarthritis. The secondary aim was to map these priorities to research priority areas identified in the recently developed Global Strategy to Improve Musculoskeletal Health.7

Methods

We conducted a scoping review of published research priorities for back pain, neck pain and osteoarthritis, enabling us to summarise what is known about this topic and identify gaps in the existing scientific literature.12 This review was conducted according to best practice guidance for development of scoping review protocols13 and reported according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis extension for Scoping Reviews statement.14 Given the scoping design, no critical appraisal of study quality, evidence certainty or risk of bias was conducted.14 15

Eligibility criteria

Studies were included if they met the following eligibility criteria: (1) contained recommendations for key research priorities, domains and/or objectives (ie, a research priority set); (2) used a formal prioritisation method (eg, survey ranking, Delphi consensus); (3) targeted one or more of the following conditions: back pain, neck pain or osteoarthritis; (4) published in peer-reviewed literature, and (5) if updated regularly, was the most current version. We excluded studies targeting pathological causes of back or neck pain (eg, infection, neoplasm, inflammatory diseases) or postsurgical or trauma-related pain, as well as editorials, commentaries, narrative reviews and studies with priorities related to consensus statements or the development of clinical guidelines and priorities specifically aimed at research methods.

Search strategy

The literature search was performed on 01 March 2025 in MEDLINE (Ovid) and EMBASE (Ovid) with no publication date or language restrictions. The search strategy was developed in consultation with a health sciences librarian, with search terms informed by previous Cochrane reviews on back pain,16 neck pain17 and osteoarthritis,18 and on a previous scoping review of research priorities for musculoskeletal disorders.9 The search was developed in MEDLINE and adapted to EMBASE (online supplemental additional file 1). We also searched the James Lind Alliance Priority Setting Partnership database, Cochrane Priority Setting Methods Group and Cochrane Musculoskeletal Review Group for relevant publications. Forward citation tracking of included studies was performed in Web of Science and reference lists were screened for additional relevant studies.

Study selection

All records identified in the search strategy were uploaded to the Covidence systematic review software (Veritas Health Innovation, Melbourne, Australia) where duplicates were automatically removed. All remaining records were screened using a two-phase screening process by two independent reviewers (JJY and JRP). In phase one, the reviewers screened titles and abstracts according to the study eligibility criteria. In phase two, full-text versions of the remaining potentially relevant studies were screened and reasons for exclusion were recorded. Disagreements in both phases were resolved through discussion.

Data extraction

The following information was extracted from all included studies by two independent reviewers (JJY and JRP) using a standardised extraction form developed and piloted in Covidence: first author, publication year, target condition(s) and specific research domain/topic, target audience and setting, number of priorities reported, development and prioritisation method, participants involved in development (professional role, country/region), source of funding declared, final research priorities identified, endorsement by professional society, implementation and/or evaluation timeline. Differences in data extraction between the reviewers were resolved through discussion.

Evidence synthesis

Studies with multiple research priority sets (eg, one set of research priorities developed by clinicians and one set developed by patients) were analysed separately. For the primary aim, the top five research priorities from each included priority set were summarised for back and neck pain and for osteoarthritis separately. Priority sets for back and neck pain were considered as a single category since some priority sets targeted both conditions.

For the secondary aim, individual research priorities were categorised according to the five research priority areas identified in the Global Strategy to Improve Musculoskeletal Health.7 An ‘Other’ category was added for any priorities that did not fall within these five defined research priority areas. Two independent reviewers (JJY and JRP) categorised each research priority based on the priority areas (and explanatory text) presented in table 1. Differences in categorisation between reviewers were resolved through discussion. The counts and proportions of research priorities (all research priorities) falling within each priority area were calculated overall and for back and neck pain and osteoarthritis separately.

Results

Study selection

The scholarly database search (online supplemental additional file 1) identified 3907 citations with three additional citations identified in the James Lind Alliance Priority Setting Partnership database. After removal of duplicates, titles and abstracts of the remaining 3449 records were screened and 117 articles were deemed relevant for full-text screening. Absolute agreement between reviewers was 97.2% with a Kappa statistic of 0.44. After full-text screening, 18 studies met the eligibility criteria for this review; 6 for back and/or neck pain19–24 and 12 for osteoarthritis.25–36 Absolute agreement for full-text screening was 89.7% with a Kappa statistic of 0.63. Back and neck pain research priorities

The six included studies19–24 identified eight priority sets related to back pain and/or neck pain: five for back pain and one each for back and neck pain, neck pain and degenerative cervical myelopathy (online supplemental additional file 2). Three of the priority sets were limited to research in primary care. Four priority sets were developed by patients alone, one by clinicians alone, two by clinicians and researchers, and one by patients, clinicians and researchers. No priority sets included involvement of policymakers. Four priority sets involved participants from the USA only, one from Australia only and three with international participants. Sources of funding were declared for all included priority sets. No priority sets included an implementation plan or timeline for evaluation.

The eight back and neck pain priority sets identified 155 unique research priorities (online supplemental additional file 3). The top five research priorities from each set are presented in online supplemental additional file 3.

Osteoarthritis research priorities

The 12 included studies25–36 identified 15 priority sets related to osteoarthritis: 8 for general/unspecified joint osteoarthritis, 4 for knee and hip osteoarthritis, 2 for knee osteoarthritis and 1 for foot and ankle osteoarthritis (online supplemental additional file 2). Eight priority sets were limited to treatment research, including surgical and non-surgical treatment, and two priority sets were limited to osteoarthritis management programme implementation research. Three priority sets were developed by patients alone, two by clinicians and researchers, six by patients, clinicians and researchers, two by clinicians, researchers and policymakers and two by patients, clinicians, researchers and policymakers. Six priority sets included participants from the UK only, two from Australia only, one from Canada only, one from Denmark only, one from the USA only and four with international participants. Sources of funding were declared for all included priority sets. Two priority sets included a 3-year to 5-year implementation timeline and one priority set included a 5-year implementation timeline.

The 15 osteoarthritis priority sets identified 206 unique research priorities (online supplemental additional file 3). The top five research priorities from each priority set are presented in online supplemental additional file 3.

Categorisation into global research priority areas

Categorisation by global research priority area for all research priorities is also presented in online supplemental additional file 2 and in figure 2. Most priorities were categorised in the clinical and basic science research priority area (overall 73.1%: back/neck pain 61.3%; osteoarthritis 82.0%) followed by the health policy and systems research priority area (overall 14.1%: back/neck pain 20.0%; osteoarthritis 9.7%), health economics research priority area (overall 4.7%: back/neck pain 4.5%; osteoarthritis 4.9%), public health research priority area (overall 4.4%: back/neck pain 7.1%; osteoarthritis 2.4%), epidemiological and population health research priority area (overall 1.9%: back/neck pain 3.9%; osteoarthritis 0.5%) and the other priority area (overall 1.7%: back/neck pain 3.2%; osteoarthritis 0.5%).

Discussion

This scoping review identified 23 published research priority sets that included 361 unique research priorities for back pain, neck pain and osteoarthritis. Most research priorities were focused on clinical and basic science research, with far fewer targeting health policy and systems research, epidemiological and population health research, public health research or health economics research. This may reflect preferences of clinicians and patients, who comprised the majority of priority setting participants, and overall lack of involvement of other relevant partners in the prioritisation exercises, especially those with roles in policymaking, population-based research and those representing low- and middle-income countries. Only three research priority sets included an implementation timeline or evaluation plan.

There was considerable overlap in the research priorities identified for back pain, neck pain and osteoarthritis, most of which fell within the clinical and basic science area, where the foci were diagnosis and treatment modalities and service models. This finding supports the growing recognition that these musculoskeletal conditions are more similar than different.37–41 Future initiatives could consider developing shared research priorities for these three conditions and indeed musculoskeletal health in general, as it is likely there is significant overlap in other research priority domains like public health messaging and health system redesign. However, consideration should be given to the lack of osteoarthritis research priorities targeted beyond the knee and hip joint. Specific priorities for hand osteoarthritis were not identified and only one priority set for foot and ankle osteoarthritis was included. Likewise, while a priority set targeted at degenerative cervical myelopathy was included, there were no research priorities aimed at lumbar spinal stenosis; both of which represent a spinal osteoarthritis process.40–43 Likewise, lumbar disc herniation(s) is another specific low back pain condition44 that was not represented in our findings that could potentially be included in coordinated research priority development processes.

Despite their considerable population-level burden, the vast majority of the research priorities for back pain, neck pain and osteoarthritis reflected a clinical and basic science global research priority, while only 11% of published research priorities identified were categorised into the health economics research, public health research and epidemiological and population health research priority areas combined. This mirrors the earlier finding of Bourne et al’s9 review of musculoskeletal research priorities and is likely a reflection of the types of participants included in the prioritisation exercises. The overwhelming involvement of clinicians and researchers in the development process likely explains the limited or lack of research priorities beyond the clinical and basic sciences. Furthermore, while patient partners were included in the development process in 70% of the priority sets, policymakers were only included in 17% (all in osteoarthritis research priority sets). Therefore, it is perhaps not surprising that other global research priority areas, covering domains that are likely of greater import to government, public health and health systems professionals, were not well-represented. As partners best positioned to activate funding mechanisms and understand broader needs to improve health systems, the inclusion of more policymaker and cross-sectoral partners (eg, civil society, delivery organisations, third party funders) in the development of research priorities would likely increase the number of resultant research priorities targeted to these priority areas and better reflect the need to improve the evidence-to-policy pipeline. If greater efforts are not made to include system-level partners and administrators, the research community runs the risk of ignoring key research domains needed to improve global musculoskeletal health.

It is important to recognise that the Global Strategy to Improve Musculoskeletal Health used a different lens in its approach to priority setting as compared with existing priority setting studies. The broad perspective of the global strategy aimed at system improvement for all musculoskeletal conditions, including pain, injury and trauma. This contrasts with the likely more specific purposes, even if not explicitly stated, that drove priority settings in the back pain, neck pain and osteoarthritis specific studies. For example, many of the included priority sets were aimed at some type of treatment or care area (eg, primary care research, surgical treatment), and therefore were oriented to a greater degree towards research priorities falling within the clinical and basic science and health policy and systems research categorisations. It is important that priority setting initiatives explicitly define the scope and position in the health system for which they are targeted. This would help to identify where gaps in research efforts exist.

The burden of musculoskeletal disorders and specifically back pain, neck pain and osteoarthritis, is growing fastest in low- and middle-income countries due to population growth and increasing lifespan.3–5 Yet, a lack of participation by partners from low- and middle-income settings in the included studies was noted. Only three of the identified priority sets included participants from Asia, two from Oceania (excluding Australia) and one each from South America and Latin America/Caribbean in the prioritisation exercises. There remains an urgent need for the development of research priorities specific to these settings, where building capacity to deliver care for these musculoskeletal conditions is a priority.7 45–47 At minimum, perspectives from all global regions are essential for informing the back pain, neck pain and osteoarthritis research agenda for the future.

Limitations

The main limitation of this scoping review is that we did not analyse and combine specific research priorities to develop an overall summarised research agenda (eg, which priorities were most common or which priorities are applicable across conditions). However, the purpose, via a scoping review design, was to provide an overview of published priorities put forth by the research community. For this reason, only published research agendas in scientific journals that used a formal prioritisation exercise were included. Other research agendas and priorities have been published, for example, in the development of treatment guidelines. While guidelines often include a section on future research priorities, they typically do not employ any formalised prioritisation process and simply reflect the discussions and opinions of the guideline development group. In addition, we presented research priorities as described in the publications; priorities with multiple aims (eg, James Lind Alliance Degenerative Cervical Myelopathy, 2020),25 were not deconstructed.

We did not include research priority sets developed by interest groups (eg, a national physiotherapy association) that were not published in scientific journals. While these research priorities have relevance and may have been developed with a formal prioritisation process, there is considerable difficulty in systematically searching for and retrieving these documents globally. Likewise, we did not include published research agendas by some interest groups that include specific research priorities related to back pain, neck pain or osteoarthritis, but overall were aimed at research by that group (eg, chiropractic research,48 as many of the included research priorities were not applicable to this review.

We did not perform any critical appraisal or risk of bias assessments since the purpose of this study was to map what the research community has published rather than make judgements on the quality of the recommendations. Variability in prioritisation methods used across the included studies were noted and different approaches may be associated with different degrees of bias. Finally, we did not perform any analyses aimed at the progress in any of the proposed research areas. Future studies may benefit from a formal analysis of research output related to specific research priorities or priority areas.

Conclusions

To overcome the scale of the global burden associated with back pain, neck pain and osteoarthritis, a whole system approach and response is required, yet the majority of identified research priorities were narrowly focused on clinical and basic sciences. Widening the research lens is key to achieving broad-ranging population health benefits. Future initiatives should include all relevant members of the global musculoskeletal health community, including patients, clinicians, individual-based and population-based researchers, policymakers, professional bodies and other cross-sectoral partners, from low-income, middle-income and high-income countries, to help ensure the development of research priorities that span and address all research domains required to improve back and neck pain and osteoarthritis health globally.
