Informing life-course immunization and pandemic preparedness: a COVID-19 vaccine evaluation and HPV introduction experience in Sierra Leone
Authors: Desmond Maada Kangbai, Ronald R Mutebi, Angela K Shen, Oliver Eleeza, AbdulRaheem Yakubu, Haurace Nyandemoh, Joyce M Kallon, Aminata Nunie, Eric Nzirakaindi Ikoona, Mame Toure, Susan Michaels-Strasser
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSierra Leone launched its COVID-19 vaccination in March 2021 and achieved 73.2% full coverage among individuals aged 12 and older by December 2022. In October 2022, the country introduced the human papillomavirus (HPV) vaccine for 10-year-old girls, leveraging infrastructure and lessons from the COVID-19 vaccine deployment. We conducted a COVID-19 vaccine post-introduction evaluation (cPIE) to document best practices, assess impact on routine immunization, and identify key levers that supported HPV vaccine rollout, and recommendations for future pandemic preparedness.MethodsWe adapted the World Health Organization COVID-19 Post-Introduction Evaluation tool and conducted 84 structured interviews with national, district, and health facility staff in six districts, complemented by a desk review. Qualitative data were analysed thematically.ResultsBy October 2023, COVID-19 full vaccination coverage was estimated at 86.7% among people aged ≥ 12 years, and first-dose coverage exceeded 100% based on programme denominators, likely reflecting uncertainty in population projections. Over 84% of doses were administered via surge campaigns and periodic intensification of routine immunization. Key lessons included: (1) delivering adult vaccination services closer to clients (e.g., schools, workplace, and homes); (2) re-tooling existing health infrastructure and systems; (3) engaging community leaders to build demand across the life-course; (4) strengthening electronic data systems for vaccination tracking; (5) integrating vaccines within one delivery platform; and (6) promoting operational innovations for rapid response. HPV vaccine uptake exceeded expectations, with 119% of the targeted girls vaccinated and 68% of doses administered in schools, supported by COVID-19 vaccination platforms, including cold chain, digital registries, and community mobilization networks. However, surge delivery approaches strained workforce capacity and were reported to disrupt routine immunization in some areas.ConclusionSierra Leone’s experience highlights the feasibility of using emergency vaccination platforms to accelerate life-course vaccination and new vaccine introductions in low-resource settings. Sustained preparedness will require earlier integration guidance, strengthened coordination, financing, and improved data systems to support adult and adolescent immunization alongside routine services.Keywords: COVID-19 vaccines, HPV vaccine introduction, Life-course immunization, New vaccine introduction, Pandemic preparedness

Introduction

Low-income countries have extensive experience conducting vaccination campaigns, both as part of routine immunization and in response to epidemic response [1, 2]. Sierra Leone, a West African nation significantly affected by the 2014–2016 Ebola outbreak, previously rolled out Ebola vaccines to target adult populations [3, 4]. As COVID-19 vaccines were rapidly being developed, the Government of Sierra Leone promptly began preparing to vaccinate priority populations, particularly adults, as a central strategy to contain the spread of the pandemic, minimize mortality and morbidity, and aid economic recovery [5].

In March 2021, Sierra Leone was one of the first ten countries to receive COVID-19 vaccines through COVID-19 Vaccines Global Access (COVAX), a global initiative aimed at ensuring equitable access to vaccines for all countries [6]. Guided by the World Health Organization (WHO)-recommended risk stratification framework [7], vaccination was rolled out in four phases: Phase 1 targeted healthcare workers (HCWs) and individuals aged 70 and above; Phase 2 focused on adults 40–69; Phase 3 covered those aged 18–39; and Phase 4 included adolescents 12 years and older (Fig. 1). Initially, vaccines were administered to Phases 1 and 2 groups. By January 2022, with an improved global vaccine supply, eligibility was expanded to all individuals aged 12 years and above. Targets for each district and population category were allocated using population projections from the 2021 Mid-Term Population and Housing Census, whose estimates were lower than those from Worldometer projections [8, 9].

Despite persistent health system constraints such as limited infrastructure, workforce shortage [10], global vaccine supply constraints [11], inadequate operational funding [12], and demand-side barriers, including barriers to vaccine access and hesitancy [13], Sierra Leone achieved 73.2% full COVID-19 vaccination coverage among individuals 12 years and older by December 2022, surpassing WHO’s 70% coverage target. This success was driven by the implementation of a nationally developed COVID-19 National Deployment and Vaccination Plan (NDVP), a playbook for guiding in-country stakeholders in reaching target populations with COVID-19 vaccines. The central strategy was anchored in surge campaigns that offered swift and rapid means of reaching a large proportion of people.

In October 2022, Sierra Leone introduced the human papillomavirus (HPV) vaccines to 10-year-old girls, aiming to reduce the burden of cervical cancer, the second most common cancer among women in the country [14]. Schools served as outreach sites for HPV vaccination, building on a successful 2014 school-based pilot and the infrastructure developed during the COVID-19 vaccine rollout [15]. By December 2022, as global COVID-19 vaccine uptake plateaued and WHO coverage targets were met, both COVID-19 and HPV vaccines were incorporated into the country’s routine immunization schedule [16].

In 2023, the focus shifted towards routine childhood immunization catch-up efforts through monthly Periodic Intensification of Routine Immunization (PIRI) campaigns. The campaigns also included other primary healthcare services, including deworming and Vitamin A supplementation. As recommended by the WHO for all new vaccine introductions [17], a post-introduction evaluation (PIE) was conducted to document best practices and identify areas for improvement. This paper presents best practices for COVID-19 vaccine deployment, its impact on routine vaccines, key levers for HPV vaccine introduction, and recommendations for future pandemic preparedness.

Methods

Study design

We conducted a COVID-19 vaccine PIE (cPIE) in Sierra Leone from October 2–6, 2023, using an adapted WHO cPIE protocol [17]. The evaluation collected data at national, regional, district, and health facility levels and included a desk review of relevant documents, such as vaccination coverage data.

Study setting and sampling of study sites and participants

Sierra Leone introduced COVID-19 vaccination for people aged 12 years and older. We purposively selected one district from each of the six regions to capture variation in geography, immunization performance, COVID-19 burden, and population distribution. Within each district, we selected four health facilities, except in the district with the highest reported COVID-19 caseload, where five health facilities were included (Fig. 2).

Respondents were purposively sampled based on roles in COVID-19 vaccine rollout and included national EPI leadership, district health management teams, health facility in-charges, and HCWs. In total, 84 interviews were completed: one EPI program leadership, six with district health teams, 26 with health facility in-charges, and 51 HCWs (Table 1).

Data collection tools

We adapted WHO cPIE tools to the national context, structured around nine focus areas: regulatory preparedness, planning and coordination; service delivery; financing; supply chain and waste management; human resource; vaccine demand and safety, monitoring and evaluation; and integration of COVID-19 vaccination with routine immunisation, including HPV vaccination.

Questionnaires were digitised using Kobo Toolbox with skip logic and validation checks [18]. Tools were reviewed for face and content validity, pilot tested and revised before data collection. Data collectors were trained using standard operating procedures (SOPs) to ensure consistency. support consistent and accurate data collection. Desk review and field data collection

Prior to fieldwork, we conducted a desk review of published and unpublished programme materials (Table 3) to summarise the COVID-19 vaccine deployment context and refine field data collection. Five multidisciplinary teams conducted concurrent fieldwork over five days. Each team included an external evaluator (CDC, WHO, or UNICEF), a Ministry of Health representative, and an immunization partner from ICAP or any other organization.

Vaccine usage and wastage

In addition to interviews, teams observed national, district, and facility storage conditions, including cold-chain equipment functionality, injection supplies, power supply, and temperature monitoring systems (Table 3). Vaccine stock records and immunization data were also reviewed. Observations and qualitative insights were recorded in designated sections of the survey tools, according to SOPs.

Data management and storage

Data was collected on encrypted tablets in real time. One team member led the interviews and note taking while, while another entered responses into the electronic tool. After each interview, electronic entries and notes were reconciled. Hard-copy notes were destroyed after verification. Data was encrypted and accessible only to the evaluation team.

Data analysis

Quantitative data were summarised descriptively using Microsoft Excel (version 22H2). Qualitative data were thematically analysed using inductive coding, guided by NDVP domains [19]. Findings from interviews and the desk review were triangulated and synthesised as: (I) themes by NDVP components; (ii) examples of real-time application of COVID-19 platforms and lessons that informed HPV introduction (iii), and recommendations for future readiness. Discrepancies in thematic classification were resolved through team discussions and consultations with subject-matter experts.

Results

Regulatory preparedness

Sierra Leone leveraged prior experience with expedited regulatory processes used during Ebola vaccine imports, clinical trials and pilot use to fast-track emergency use authorization of five COVID-19 vaccines. Between February 2021 and October 2023, the country received 9,836,730 COVID-19 vaccine doses, predominantly Johnson & Johnson (43.2%), followed by Pfizer-BioNTech (32.8%), AstraZeneca (14.4%), Sinopharm (7.5%), and Sinovac (7.5%) (Table 4). The average time from submission of required regulatory documentation (i.e., regulatory dossier, import license, and indemnity agreement) decreased from 42 days, in March 2020, to 11 days by the end of 2021. Due to the short shelf life of early vaccines donated to the country, the Pharmacy Board of Sierra Leone advised that donations should have at least six-month remaining shelf life.

Planning and coordination

A national COVID-19 Vaccination Technical Working Group (TWG) led the NDVP development and implementation, with parallel district TWGs supporting subnational level implementation. National sub-TWGs provided daily technical guidance across NDVP domains. The country’s external independent advisory body on vaccines, The National Immunization Technical Advisory Group of Experts in Sierra Leone (NITAG SL) provided recommendations on eligibility, for vaccination, introduction of additional vaccine products, including heterogenous schedules, booster dosing, and vaccination in pregnancy. All recommendations, except for the vaccination of pregnant individuals, were adopted by the Government of Sierra Leone. The Intra-agency Coordination Committee (ICC), chaired by the Minister of Health, enabled the endorsement of key decisions. Vaccine mandates, including use of vaccination cards to access public spaces were reported, although enforcement varied.

Financing

COVID-19 vaccines were supplied through donations, through COVAX and bilateral arrangements. Respondents noted inadequate early operational funding, especially at district level. As donor funding increased, surge campaigns expanded. In January 2023, COVID-19 vaccination was integrated into routine immunisation to reduce operational costs; however, maintaining coverage among adults continued to rely on periodic intensified delivery approaches. Respondents reported uncertainty regarding long-term financing for sustained COVID-19 vaccination.

Service delivery strategies and coverage

Vaccination was delivered through static health facilities, outreach posts, and mobile clinics, using three approaches: routine delivery, surge campaigns, and PIRIs. Routine delivery relied on established health facilities and pre-existing outreach posts operating seven days a week. Surge campaigns and PIRIs expanded access by adding new outreach posts, deploying mobile clinics, and intensified mobilisation, typically implemented for one week per month.

Sixteen surge campaigns were implemented. Teams typically included two vaccinators, one recorder, and one social mobilizer, with districts deciding allocation across delivery modes. Surge campaigns commenced in Freetown where 65% (7,765) of COVID-19 cases occurred. The number of surge teams increased from 299 during the initial three campaigns to 1,385 in the last 13 surge campaigns between 2021 and 2022 (Fig. 3). Following COVID-19 vaccine integration into routine immunization in 2023, surge campaigns were replaced by PIRIs, and mobile teams that mostly consisted of workplaces were reduced or discontinued in some districts.

By 31 October 2023, 98% of vaccine doses received had been administered (Table 4). Estimated full vaccination coverage was 86.7% among people aged ≥ 12 years. First-dose coverage exceeded 100% when using programme denominators, which likely reflects uncertainty in population projections used for target estimates (Table 4).

Supply chain and waste management

A national cold-chain readiness assessment conducted in December 2020 identified gaps in capacity for 2–8 °C and − 20 °C cold-chain equipment (CCE) storage. Ultra-low temperature equipment (-70 °C), previously procured for Ebola vaccination was found to be adequate at national-level. At service delivery level, 44% (515 out of 1509) of health facilities lacked CCEs; subsequent procurement reduced this gap to 9.5% (143) nationally. Survey findings corroborated this, with 8% (2/ 26) of evaluated health facilities lacking functional CCEs.

Waste disposal predominantly involved burning and burying (77%) while only six health facilities had functional incinerators. Most health facilities (76%) did not monitor open vaccine wastage; however, desk review data indicated a closed vial wastage rate of 1.5% during the implementation period (Table 4).

Human resources management and training

Most facilities (21/26) reported adequate training for vaccinators. Nonetheless, 13 out of 26 health facilities recruited additional volunteer staff, including nurses, pharmacists, and community health workers (CHWs). High attrition rates among volunteers were reported in 18 out of 26 health facilities, attributed to delayed or unpaid stipends. Staffing constraints sometimes reduced teams from four to three members. Respondents frequently described fatigue and burnout due to prolonged campaign activities.

Vaccine acceptance, risk communication, and demand

No HCWs reported outright refusal of the vaccine; however, 88% (23/26) of facility in-charges reported vaccine related rumors contributing to hesitancy. Common rumors related to side effects (73%), concerns about counterfeit vaccines (57%), and beliefs that vaccination was unnecessary (46%).

The Ministry of Health adapted a rumor, misinformation, and concerns monitoring log from the Ebola response. Between April 2021 and October 2022, 15,000 entries were recorded, mostly related to COVID-19 and Ebola. COVID-19 messages included concerns about vaccine safety and side effects and doubts about the existence of COVID-19. Community-led Action (CLA) was implemented in 8 of 16 districts, with CHWs supporting community mobilisation. Risk communication used multiple channels, including RapidPro Short Message Services (SMS) reminders for second and booster doses, with a maximum of four messages spaced one week apart, beginning two weeks prior to the vaccination due date.

Vaccine safety

Respondents from four districts reported sufficient training on identification and managing adverse events following immunization (AEFIs). Most health facilities reported confidence in monitoring (88%; 23/26) and managing AEFIs (73%; 19/26). Non-reporting was commonly attributed to perceived absence of events (83.3%; 15/26), and less frequently to form availability constraints (5.6%; 1/26). The District Health Information Software 2 (DHIS2) COVID-19 AEFI tracker was used in 1,340 health facilities. From March 2021 to March 2022, 123 AEFI cases were reported among 1,743,563 vaccinated individuals (1 per 100,000); Six cases were classified as serious, including three deaths determined to be unrelated to vaccination. The majority were female (61%), with common symptoms including headache, fever, dizziness, weakness, and body pain.

Monitoring and evaluation

Three platforms were used: DHIS2 for case-based data, and Google Drive and WhatsApp for aggregate data reporting. Case-based reporting lagged behind aggregate reporting, with a backlog of two million records reported in late 2022. Districts implemented migration strategies, including incentives for timely reporting and daily performance targets. Some HCWs indicated that they delivered COVID-19 vaccines only during surge and PIRI campaigns, suggesting incomplete understanding of integration into primary healthcare services.

As of October 31, 2023, 98% of all vaccines received (9.6 million doses) had been administered. Full vaccination coverage reached 86.7% of the eligible targeted population, with 88.1% of those receiving at least one dose considered fully vaccinated. However, only 27.2% of fully vaccinated individuals received booster doses (Table 4). Most (84%) vaccine doses were delivered through surge and PIRI campaigns.

Interaction between COVID-19 vaccination and routine immunisation

Routine childhood immunisation declined at the onset of the pandemic (April - June 2020) and during periods of COVID-19 surge (late 2021 and early 2022), as shown in Fig. 4 for BCG, Pentavalent, and Measles-Rubella vaccine coverages. In 2022, measles outbreaks were reported in 36% (69/190) of chiefdoms, prompting reactive campaigns and PIRI activities, with that improved coverage in 2023. Respondents differed on perceived effects of COVID-19 vaccination on routine services: some cited strengthened cold chain and staff skills, while others highlighted increased workload.

Interaction between COVID-19 vaccination and HPV vaccination introduction

HPV vaccination for 10-year-old girls was introduced in October 2022, primarily through schools, leveraging infrastructure established for COVID-19 vaccination. A case-based electronic HPV vaccination registry, adapted from the COVID-19 vaccination DHIS2 registry was developed to track doses, with plans to expand case-based registries to other routine vaccines by 2026. COVID-19 vaccination risk communication structures supported the HPV rollout through multilingual materials and stakeholder engagement.

During school-based sessions, girls received HPV vaccination and eligible COVID-19 doses, while boys and school staff were offered COVID-19 vaccination, maximizing the opportunity to vaccinate all students during the on-site visit (Fig. 5). Coverage for HPV vaccinations exceeded targets, with 119% of the estimated cohort vaccinated. Most (68%) vaccinations occurred through school-based clinics. Some districts reported increased COVID-19 vaccine uptake during HPV rollout; however, respondents also noted that this may reflect concurrent mobilisation and shared delivery platforms rather than a direct causal effect. To a less extent, two health facilities reported school absenteeism linked to parental vaccine hesitancy during the immunization days.

Key recommendations on future preparedness

Respondents emphasized the need for accelerated regulatory pathways for new products, coordinated decision making, sustainable financing, strengthened cold chain and waste management, workforce support, tailored risk communication for adult/adolescent vaccination, and improved safety and data systems (Table 5).

Discussion

Sierra Leone’s COVID-19 vaccine rollout provides insights on deploying vaccines during epidemics and on adapting emergency platforms to support life-course immunization. The evaluation documented strategies used to achieve high coverage, described operational challenges associated with intensified delivery, and identified lessons that were subsequently applied to HPV vaccine introduction. Furthermore, we propose strategies to inform the creation of emergency preparedness plans for vaccine deployment in anticipation of future epidemics affecting the general population.

Although Sierra Leone’s early COVID-19 seroprevalence estimates suggested limited population-level spread by March 2021 [20, 21], the country implemented COVID-19 vaccination rapidly [22]. Vaccination delivery relied heavily on intensified approaches: over 84% of doses were administered through surge campaigns and PIRIs. Respondents consistently described the value of “bringing vaccination closer to people” through mobile, outreach, workplace, and school-based approaches, delivery models that can be particularly important in settings with dispersed rural populations and where adult demand for vaccination may be variable [23]. However, intensified delivery is resource-intensive. Health facilities reported workforce fatigue, challenges sustaining volunteer incentives, and reduced team composition in some settings. These findings support using surge approaches selectively, including addressing outbreaks, coverage gaps, or hard-to-reach groups, while strengthening routine systems to sustain delivery over time.

A key implementation lesson concerns timing of integration guidance. Respondents reported that delayed operational guidance for integrating COVID-19 vaccination into routine immunization, issued after two years from the grant of the first Emergency Use Listing [24], contributed to continued reliance on campaign-style delivery and uncertainty among HCWs about COVID-19 vaccination as part of primary healthcare. Earlier integration planning, alongside revised tools, reporting pathways, and supportive supervision, may reduce fragmentation and improve sustainability, especially for adult and adolescent vaccines. This context likely explains the low booster dose uptake observed (27.2%), as reduced surge mobilisation, changing risk perception, and uncertainty around routine delivery diminished demand for additional COVID-19 doses.

The evaluation also demonstrated how emergency investments can be repurposed for new vaccine introductions. Sierra Leone’s HPV vaccine rollout leveraged COVID-19 platforms, including cold chain improvements, digital registries, and community mobilisation structures. The use of schools as a delivery platform enabled rapid access to the target cohort and supported high reported coverage. While this approach aligns with global best practices for adolescent vaccination, it requires strong intersectoral coordination, microplanning aligned with academic calendars, and adequate human-resource capacity. The Sierra Leone experience highlights schools as a high-impact but resource-intensive platform that should be strategically embedded within national immunisation planning. Importantly, reports of increased COVID-19 uptake during HPV rollout should be interpreted cautiously: the evaluation suggests an association that may reflect concurrent mobilisation and shared delivery platforms rather than a direct causal effect. Future studies that include community and recipient perspectives could clarify how integrated delivery influences acceptance and service use.

The findings further underscore that effective life-course vaccination depends on tailored demand-generation strategies, resilient workforce structures, and sustainable financing. Adapting COVID-19 risk-communication approaches to engage parents, teachers, community leaders, and adolescents was central to HPV vaccine acceptance, emphasising that adolescent vaccination requires engagement models distinct from those used for infant immunisation. At the same time, prolonged intensified delivery contributed to health-worker fatigue and reliance on volunteer staff, reinforcing workforce resilience as a limiting factor for integrated vaccine delivery. Uncertainty around long-term financing for COVID-19 and HPV vaccination further highlights the need to establish co-financing mechanisms and embed adolescent vaccines within national immunisation budgets.

Consistent with global reports [25, 26], Sierra Leone experienced declines in routine childhood immunisation at the onset of the pandemic and during periods of COVID-19 surge, with a subsequent rise in measles outbreaks reported in 2022. The linkage between intensified emergency response and routine service disruption underscores the need for preparedness plans that explicitly protect essential services, maintain surveillance for vaccine-preventable diseases, and integrate catch-up strategies early during crisis response. A systems-thinking approach, one that considers routine immunisation, outbreak response, workforce management, and supply chain resilience together, is essential for reducing secondary harms during emergencies.

The strength of this evaluation is the real-time assessment of how COVID-19 vaccine delivery lessons were applied to HPV vaccine introduction. However, our evaluation has limitations. First, coverage estimates exceeded 100% for some indicators, likely reflecting uncertainty in population projections used for denominators rather than true uptake beyond the population. Second, the evaluation did not include vaccine recipients, parents, teachers, or community members, limiting demand-side interpretation, particularly relevant for adolescent HPV vaccination. Third, fieldwork occurred during a period when COVID-19 emergency operations had been scaled down, reducing opportunities to observe high-intensity vaccination sessions. Nevertheless, interviews with HCWs, a priority population for vaccination, provided valuable implementation perspectives, including views from HCWs as both vaccinators and a priority group for vaccination.

Implications for policy and practice

Findings point to several priorities: (i) design adult and adolescent vaccination strategies that extend beyond facilities through outreach, workplace, and school-based delivery; (ii) invest in trusted community messengers and sustained risk communication to address misinformation; (iii) strengthen data systems and digital registries for case-based tracking and timely decision-making; (iv) protect routine services during emergency vaccination by planning workforce surge capacity and catch-up activities; and (v) establish sustainable financing mechanisms to support life-course immunisation and reduce dependence on short-term donor funding.

Conclusions

Sierra Leone’s experience shows that high COVID-19 vaccination coverage can be achieved in a in resource-limited settings through strong coordination, community engagement, and the strategic use of existing systems. However, heavy reliance on surge delivery can strain workforce and disrupt routine services, highlighting the need for more sustainable delivery models and earlier integration planning. The successful introduction of the HPV vaccine during the pandemic illustrates how emergency platforms can be repurposed to expand life-course immunization. Strengthened coordination, financing, and data systems, paired with community-centred communication, will be central to future vaccine introductions and pandemic preparedness in similar settings.
