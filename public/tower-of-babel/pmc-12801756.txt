Residency-trained nurse’ knowledge and attitude towards palliative care in a specific region of China: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Wanting Xia, Xiuhua Yang, Shan Huang, Ting Li, Xiarong Wang, Wang Feng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AimThe purpose of this study was to insight into the perceptions, beliefs, and perceptions of Residency-trained nurse and to identify their attitudes and requirements for palliative care.MethodsA descriptive, cross-sectional, questionnaire-based survey were conducted. From January to May 2025, 367 residency-trained nurse were recruited from five tertiary hospitals in Chongqing Municipality, China. Participants completed a demographic form, 20-item the Palliative Care Quiz for Nursing (PCQN), 29-item the Frommelt Attitudes Towards Care of the Dying (FATCOD) and 32-item Death Attitude Profile-Revised (DAP-R) questionnaire.Independent t-tests and univariate analysis were employed to investigate the relationships among the three main variables, and multiple regression analysis was used to identify the factors influencing the knowledge acquisition of palliative care among resident nurses.ResultsResidency-trained nurse’ palliative care knowledge level was low (8.77±2.97; range = 0–20) and their attitude toward palliative care was moderate low (75.32±8.29, range: 30–120). Knowledge was significantly correlated with attitude (r = -0.321, p ≤0 .001). The linear regression model with four factors (The reasons for choosing the nursing profession、Do the people in your family openly discuss topics related to death、FATCOD、DAP) explained 15.8% of the variance in total knowledge related to palliative care(F = 3.426, p＜0.001).ConclusionsThe attitudes of resident nurses towards death and caring for terminally ill patients are positive, but their overall level of knowledge about palliative care is relatively low. This might be due to the insufficient palliative care education in the nursing plan. In the nursing residency training program in Chongqing City, China, palliative care education should be incorporated into the nursing curriculum, and nursing educators should particularly emphasize the teaching of social psychology and spiritual care, and strengthen the students' psychological handling ability in dealing with the challenges during the patients' terminal stage.

Introduction

As the global population continues to age [1], along with changes in the disease spectrum and the shrinking family size, the diagnosis rate of non-communicable diseases has been increasing year by year [2]. In particular, patients with malignant cancers, cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases, etc. have poor treatment outcomes, high mortality risks, and poor quality of life [3]. Patients and their families not only have to endure physical pain but also face huge economic burdens [4]. Globally, the demand for high-quality palliative care has increased sharply. The World Health Organization defines palliative care as “an approach that improves the quality of life of patients and their families facing the problems associated with life-threatening illness, through the prevention and relief of suffering by means of early identification and assessment and treatment of pain and other problems, physical, psychosocial and spiritual” [5]. palliative care not only alleviates the physical pain of patients, but also provides comprehensive care that respects the dignity of patients, takes into account their wishes, and offers psychological and social support. It has become an indispensable part of the medical treatment for patients with life-limiting diseases. As the professional who has the most contact with terminally ill patients within the medical team, nurses are the main assessors of patient information, the main implementers of palliative care practices, the coordinators of the multidisciplinary team for palliative care, and play a very important role in the full-cycle care of terminally ill patients and their families [6]. Although the global demand for hospice care is huge, in middle- and low-income countries such as those in Asia, Africa, and South America, the accessibility and quality of palliative services still have a significant gap compared to developed countries like Europe, Australia, and the United States [7].

As the country with the fastest aging rate [8] and the largest elderly population in the world [9], China is confronted with unprecedented challenges in elderly care and end-of-life care. It is projected that by 2065, China will become the oldest among the world’s 20 most populous countries [10].It is projected that by 2065, China will be the oldest among the world’s 20 most populous countries. However, due to the traditional Chinese cultural taboo on discussing death, the incomplete education system for palliative care, and the uneven distribution of medical resources, the development of palliative care in China is still in its infancy [11, 12]. Therefore, the demand for palliative care services has become particularly urgent [13]. According to data from the National Bureau of Statistics of China, approximately 7.5 million people die in China each year. Among them, around 6 million people (80%) require palliative care services. However, data shows that only 0.7% (146 out of 22,000) of hospitals on the Chinese mainland provide hospice care [14]. At present, the palliative care services provided can only meet the needs of approximately 0.3% of patients in the terminal stage of life [15]. As a bridge connecting different levels of medical institutions, facilitating cross-professional collaboration, and enabling communication between patients and their families [6], nurses are the professional healthcare workers who have the most frequent contact with patients and their families. Their knowledge level of palliative care and attitude towards hospice care directly determine the quality of care for patients in the terminal stage [16].

At present, most relevant studies focus on in-service clinical nurses [17–19] and nursing students [20–22], while there is a significant lack of attention to “standardized training nurses” who are in the crucial period of professional ability shaping. Nurse standardized training is a new policy implemented by the National Health Commission of China in 2015. That is, after completing their studies in medical colleges, nursing students continue to receive systematic and standardized training [23]. The standardized training base for nurses in Chongqing City, China, is established by various hospitals in accordance with the requirements of the document indicators. They submit applications to the Chongqing Health Commission to join the standardized training base for nurses. The training base undergoes an annual assessment by the health committee each year. The assessment covers aspects such as the teaching ability of the teachers and the theoretical and practical skills of the trainees, in order to ensure the quality of the training provided by the base. The Chongqing Health Commission uniformly issues recruitment announcements and relevant requirements every year, targeting all undergraduate and junior college students with a background in nursing. The training period for undergraduate students is one year, and for junior college students, it is two years. The training program combines clinical theory with practical skills, aiming to help new nurses quickly adapt to clinical work and complete the transformation from intern nurses to qualified nurses [24]. As the new force and future pillar of the nursing team in China, the palliative care knowledge reserve and humanistic care concept of this group will directly affect the overall level of end-of-life care services in China in the next few decades. However, when providing palliative care for terminally ill patients, standardized training nurses not only face challenges in terms of knowledge and skills, but also endure unique psychological pressure [25]. They need to frequently confront the patients’ pain and death, which not only may lead to emotional exhaustion and psychological burden [26], but also their personal attitudes and perceptions towards death may profoundly affect the quality of care and the communication effectiveness with patients and their families. Therefore, paying attention to and supporting them during this psychological adaptation period is of vital importance for cultivating future nursing pillars with sound humanistic care capabilities.

In developed countries, palliative care education has been integrated into the core curriculum and postgraduate education system for nurses. For example, countries such as the United States and the United Kingdom have not only established a specialized discipline of palliative nursing but also established a complete certification and training system to ensure that nurses have the ability to provide high-quality end-of-life care [27–29]. Research shows that systematic palliative care training can significantly improve nurses’ knowledge level and self-efficacy, and enhance the quality of care they provide to terminally ill patients [30]. Compared with international advanced practices, the development of hospice care in China is still in its infancy. Hospice care education in medical colleges and universities across the country is not widespread, and there is a lack of a complete hospice care education system [11]. The section on palliative care is usually arranged as a chapter or a section in professional courses, with about 2–4 class hours, which is far from meeting the needs of clinical practice and society [31]. In addition, most current studies mainly focus on frontline practicing nurses in clinical settings, investigating their knowledge level of palliative care and attitudes towards death. The results show that the overall knowledge level of palliative care among Chinese nurses is relatively low. Moreover, influenced by the traditional cultural taboo on death, some nurses may have negative or avoidant attitudes towards hospice care.

As the new force and future pillar of the nursing team, nurses undergoing standardized training are in a crucial shaping period for the formation of their professional values, knowledge system, and clinical practice abilities. The level of their preparedness will directly affect the overall quality of palliative care services in China in the future. In China, the standardized training for nurses is initiated by the National Health Commission. This system is highly consistent with the internationally-recognized “transition period practice program” in terms of core concepts. For instance, it is similar to the “nurse residency program” in the United States [32, 33] and the “pre-registration training” in the United Kingdom [34, 35], all of which aim to alleviate the “transformation shock” of new nurses through structured clinical rotations, mentorship, and theoretical training, enhancing their clinical competence and professional identity, and ultimately ensuring patient safety [36]. However, the key difference lies in its implementation context and mandatory nature: The training in China is a mandatory system uniformly implemented nationwide under government leadership, with its specific content and standards being refined and regulated by the health commissions of each province under the national framework; while many similar projects in Europe and the United States (such as the NRPs in the United States) are usually initiated more independently by medical institutions or professional organizations [32]. Therefore, the “standardized trained nurses” in this study can be understood as a more universal research object in the global nursing field - “nurses in the transition period” - a specific manifestation of the specific medical context in China. Therefore, this study adopted a cross-sectional survey method to systematically evaluate the understanding of palliative care and the attitude towards hospice care among nurses undergoing standardized training, providing empirical evidence for improving the standardized training curriculum for nurses and formulating targeted training strategies. This will further enhance the capacity of palliative care services in China and improve the quality of care for patients in the terminal stage of life.

Methods

Study design

This was a descriptive, cross-sectional study carried out among mainland Chinese residency-trained nurse.

Setting and participants

This study was conducted in a tertiary hospital in Chongqing municipality, China. The hospital successfully applied to become a standardized training base for nurses in 2021, and began to recruit trainee nurses for training. From January to May in 2025, a stratified sampling method was used to select 5 nursing standardization training bases in Chongqing as the research subjects. Inclusion criteria were (1) obtained the registered nurse practice qualification certificate, (2) completed the standardized training at the nursing training base in Chongqing or during the training period, (3) could correctly understand the questionnaire content and had no reading difficulties, (4) volunteered to participate in this study. Exclusion criteria were absent from work due to personal leave, sick leave, or maternity leave.

Refer to the calculation method of the sample size for multi-factor research, which is 5–10 times the number of independent variables. This study includes a total of 30 independent variables. The calculated required sample size should be 150–300. Considering 10% of invalid questionnaires, the final determined sample size is 334.

Study instrument

General information questionnaire

The questionnaire was designed by the researchers themselves, and mainly included information such as the department, age, gender, educational background, place of origin, monthly family income. In addition, respondents were asked to indicate whether they avoided discussing death, whether death was openly discussed at home, whether there were any serious illnesses or deaths of relatives in the past three years, the situation of accompanying a loved one during their final days, whether they were familiar with hospice care knowledge, the channels through which they learned about hospice care-related knowledge, whether they had participated in hospice care training, the duration of the hospice care training courses they attended within the past two years, and whether they were willing to participate in hospice care volunteer services.

Palliative Care Quiz for Nursing (PCQN)

PCQN was developed by Ross, a professor from the Ottawa College of Nursing, in 1996 [37]. Zou Min [38] translated and localized this scale and introduced it into China. The PQCN assesses three aspects of palliative care: philosophy and principles of palliative care (n = 4), management of pam and other symptoms (n = 13), and psychosocial aspects of care (n = 3). All the items are multiple-choice questions. The answer options include “True”, “False”, and “I don’t know”. The correct answer for odd-numbered questions is “False”, while for even-numbered questions it is “True”. In accordance with the standard scoring procedure for this instrument, the “I don’t know” responses were treated as incorrect and scored 0 points. One point was awarded for each correct answer, and 0 points for incorrect or “I don’t know” answers. The scores for each dimension were summed to obtain the total score, ranging from 0 to 20, with a higher score indicating better knowledge mastery. The test-retest reliability of this questionnaire is 0.782, and the Cronbach’s α coefficient was 0.758.

Frommelt Attitudes Toward Care Of the Dying scale form B (FATCOD-B)

FATCOD was developed by Frommelt in 1989 and was divided into two parts: A and B. Part B can be used to assess the attitudes towards hospice care among medical workers and medical students. Dr. Wang Liping [39]translated the scale into Chinese in 2016 and further revised it. It is applicable for evaluating the attitudes of medical workers towards hospice care. The original scale consists of 30 items. Through factor analysis, the Chinese version reviser found that item 10 was in a separate factor, so it was deleted, leaving 29 options. This scale consists of 6 dimensions, namely attitudes towards the interests of patients receiving palliative care, attitudes towards caring for terminally ill patients, attitudes towards the necessity of providing support to family members, attitudes towards the fear and anxiety experienced by caregivers when dealing with terminally ill patients, attitudes towards communication with terminally ill patients, and attitudes towards caring for the family members of patients. A Likert 5-point scoring method was used, where 1 to 5 represent “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree”. Items 3, 5, 9, 10 (Original 11 items), 12 (Original 13 items), 13 (Original 14 items),, 14 (Original 15 items),,, 16 (Original 17 items),, 18 (Original 19items),,, 27 (Original 28 items),,, and 28 (Original 29 items),, are reverse-scored items. Prior to data analysis, scores for these reverse-scored items were transformed (1 = 5, 2 = 4, 3 = 3, 4 = 2, 5 = 1). After transformation, scores for all items were summed to yield a total score, with higher scores indicating a more positive attitude toward caring for dying patients. The Cronbach’s α coefficient of the Chinese version of FATCOD-B scale is 0.796, the test-retest reliability is 0.959, and the content validity was 0.92.

Death Attitude Profile-Revised (DAP-R)

The Death Attitude Profile was developed by Wong et al. in 1994 and is widely used to assess respondents’ attitudes towards death. The validity and reliability of the Chinese version was assessed by Tang Lu et al. [40]. The Death Attitude Scale consists of 32 items, which are divided into five dimensions: Fear of Death, Death Avoidance, Natural Acceptance, Escape Acceptance, and Approach Acceptance. Each item has five options: “Strongly Disagree”, “Disagree”, “No Opinion”, “Agree”, and “Strongly Agree”. A 5-point scoring method is adopted, and all are positively scored. The death attitude tendency of nursing students is obtained by comparing the average scores of each dimension. The higher the score, the higher the degree of agreement with that dimension. Studies have shown that the test-retest reliabilities of this scale in the Chinese population are 0.86, 0.88, 0.65, 0.97, and 0.84 respectively. It has good content validity and structural validity and can measure death attitudes well.

Data collection

Before conducting the survey, researchers need to receive training on the survey content, filling methods, instructions, precautions, etc. After obtaining the consent of the teaching staff at the training base, they will select qualified trainee nurses who meet the inclusion and exclusion criteria to participate in the survey. During the training, uniform and standard instructions are used, and explanations are provided for items that the trainees do not understand. To ensure the response rate and quality of the responses, the researchers first promise the trainees strict confidentiality of the research data. After obtaining the consent of the trainees and signing the informed consent form, an online one-on-one survey was conducted using Questionnaire Star. The questionnaire survey is expected to take 10 to 15 min and ensure that the trainees could continue working or rest. During the filling process, any doubts raised by the trainees are promptly resolved. Additionally, the electronic questionnaire was configured to require a response to all items before submission, thus preventing any missing data. At the same time, the researchers closely monitor the emotional status of the trainees in the survey and provide comfort and support in a timely manner. If necessary, they refer the trainees to a professional psychologist for handling. Throughout the research process, the principles of respect, non-harm, benefit, and informed consent are always followed.

Data analysis

The collected data were statistically analyzed using IBM SPSS 27.0 software. Categorical variables were statistically described using frequency and percentage; continuous variables (knowledge and attitude) were compiled as mean values and standard deviation (SD). Independent t-tests and one-way ANOVA were used, and Pearson correlation was employed to examine the relationships among the three main variables. Finally, multiple regression analysis was employed to identify the factors influencing the knowledge acquisition of palliative care among resident nurses. The variables included in the regression model are defined and coded in Table 1. Categorical independent variables were included in the regression model using dummy coding. Specifically, the multicategorical variable “reasons for choosing the nursing profession” was transformed into three dummy variables, with “like the nursing profession” set as the reference category. The overall model fit, significance, and the regression coefficients with their significance for all independent variables were reported.

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 367 valid questionnaires were collected in this study. Three hundred and nineteen participants (86.9%) were women; mean age 22.85 ± 1.806; three hundred and thirty-one participants (90.2%) obtained a master’s degree. Among them, 121 participants (33%) stated that they had experienced serious illness or the death of a close family member in the past three years; 50 participants indicated that they would avoid discussing topics related to death, 66 participants said that death-related topics were never openly discussed in their families, 331 participants (90.2%) had taken courses on hospice care during their school years, 104 participants (28.3%) had never seen a terminally ill patient; 59 participants (16.1%) were very familiar with the knowledge related to palliative care. Discussion

This study investigated the knowledge and attitudes of nurses participating in the standardized training program regarding palliative care, in order to determine which factors influence their mastery of knowledge related to palliative care. The results show that the trainee nurses have a positive attitude towards death and caring for the terminally ill, but their overall level of knowledge regarding palliative care is relatively low.

This study found that the overall level of knowledge about palliative care among the resident nurses in Chongqing, China was relatively low (PCQN = 8.77), which is consistent with the results of a quantitative meta-analysis conducted by Wang et al. [20] on 26 items regarding the knowledge and attitudes of nursing students towards end-of-life care. Analyzing the reasons, it may be related to the fact that China currently lacks a relatively mature training system for palliative care. Most of the trainee nurses are fresh graduates of nursing schools, and the sources of their knowledge on palliative care mainly come from educational institutions. However, there are also relatively few institutions in China that offer courses related to palliative care [41]. This educational deficiency leads to the lack of experts in the field of palliative care, and trainee nurses lack qualified teachers and role models [42]. Therefore, trainee nurses have very few opportunities to receive training from medical institutions. From the perspective of patient safety and care quality, the lack of such knowledge reserves may pose significant potential risks to terminally ill patients and their families. Firstly, in terms of symptom management, a lack of knowledge regarding pain assessment, the use of opioid drugs, etc., may result in patients’ pain and other distressing symptoms not being effectively alleviated, thus causing them to endure avoidable physical and mental pain during the final stage of life. Secondly, the low scores in the fields of psychological, social and spiritual care indicate that trainee nurses may have difficulty in identifying and responding to the anxiety, depression and grief of patients and their families, and also be unable to provide effective communication and spiritual support. Therefore, it is very necessary to strengthen the training of transitional nurses on knowledge related to palliative care.

On the other hand, it might also be related to the relatively specialized nature of palliative care knowledge. During the standardized training of the resident nurses in chongqing city, they undergo a 3-month rotation in various departments. Therefore, they may only have the opportunity to come into contact with and learn about palliative care-related knowledge when rotating in departments such as palliative care wards, pain wards, and oncology wards. Similarly, although the resident nurses have obtained the qualification certificate of a practicing nurse, they are still in the status of trainees when they first enter the department for training. The resident nurses need to receive training from the department’s specialized nurses. Studies have confirmed that nurses working in oncology departments or cancer centers show higher levels of palliative care knowledge than those working in general wards or intensive care units (ICU) [43, 44], and the knowledge level of nurses caring for non-cancer patients is lower [45]. Therefore, it might also be related to the teaching level of the instructors in non-palliative care wards.

The results of this study also show that among the various dimensions of the palliative care knowledge of the trainee nurses, the scores for pain and symptom management were the highest. This might be related to the fact that the trainee nurses have accumulated more experience in basic patient care and various symptom management during their daily training in various departments. The dimension with the lowest score was the psychological, social and spiritual support dimension, which was consistent with the studies conducted among nurses in Iran and Spain. Although the framework of the nursing curriculum has shifted from a medical focus to a process-oriented model, the emphasis on teaching social psychological and spiritual care is relatively low. Moreover, Chinese clinical nurses usually teach nursing students in the classroom and during practice, and they have a heavy workload. They usually focus on the physical health of patients, but do not pay sufficient attention to the social psychological and spiritual care of patients. Therefore, during the teaching period, the trainee nurses’ acquisition of social psychological and spiritual care knowledge is limited.

In this study, the majority of the trainee nurses held a positive attitude towards end-of-life care. The total average score of FATCOD-B was 75.32, which falls within the low to moderate range. Compared with previous studies that used FATCOD to survey nursing students, this score was slightly lower than that of Palestinian fourth-year nursing students [46] (FATCOD-B = 96.96); it was also much lower than the survey results of Zhou et al. [47] on Chinese junior nursing undergraduates (FATCOD-B = 101.34), Kirkpatrick et al.‘s [48] survey of American nursing students ((FATCOD-B = 122.95), and Henoch et al.‘s [49] survey of Swedish nursing students. Research shows that educational and cultural background influence the attitudes of participants towards caring for patients at the end of life [50]. Although the reason why American and Swedish students may have higher scores in caring for terminally ill patients might be that both countries have a long history of research and policies in palliative care. Therefore, their nursing students can receive relatively adequate education in palliative care. However, the FATCOD-B score in this study was lower than the survey results of Chinese undergraduate students. This might be due to the fact that the proportion of nursing students with a background in a specific discipline in the training program was 90.2% in this study. For registered nurses who have not yet accumulated rich experience in end-of-life care, their attitudes towards death and the terminally ill may mainly stem from the training in hospice care courses during their undergraduate nursing training [51].

In this study, nurse trainees predominantly maintained an accepting and positive attitude toward death, with a total mean score of 98.08 on the Death Attitude Profile-Revised (DAP-R), which is consistent with the findings reported by Xie et al. [52] in a survey of 2119 nursing students in mainland China. The majority of trainees viewed death as a natural part of life. Multiple surveys involving nursing students in China have likewise confirmed that their attitude toward end-of-life care constitutes a positive developmental process [53–55]. In fact, compared with nursing students, registered nurses typically exhibit lower levels of fear of death and avoidance, as well as higher levels of acceptance. A study on oncology nurses has shown that their most dominant attitude towards death is “positive attitude toward death”, and this attitude is associated with a higher level of hospice care knowledge [56]. More importantly, research has indicated that this positive attitude towards death can be strengthened through emotional regulation training, offering practical insights for targeted interventions among younger nurses [57, 58]. However, the rate of formal training in this area remains relatively low. Future nursing education should begin by identifying the specific attitudes exhibited by students—such as fear of death, neutral acceptance, or avoidant acceptance—and implement targeted educational initiatives along with simulated end-of-life care training. Such approaches can help refine students’ attitudes and perspectives, thereby better preparing a new generation of practitioners for the field of palliative care.

In this study, it was found that there was a relatively low negative correlation between the knowledge of trainee nurses regarding palliative care and their attitudes towards palliative care and death.The significant negative correlation between knowledge and attitude suggests that a lack of understanding may contribute to more negative or apprehensive feelings toward palliative care, potentially creating a barrier to its effective implementation.This might be related to cultural differences. In Chinese culture, it is taboo to talk about death-related topics. For instance, Chinese people often avoid saying the number “four” because its pronunciation is similar to that of death. Trainee nurses might feel greater psychological and physical pressure when caring for patients at the end of life, and thus tend to be less willing to do so compared to others.

In this study, the reasons for choosing to study nursing, family members openly discussing death-related topics, attitudes towards palliative care, and attitudes towards death were found to be factors influencing the knowledge level of palliative care among trainee nurses. Firstly, the reasons for choosing to study the nursing major can be explained from the perspective of motivation theory. According to the Self-Determination Theory, intrinsic motivation (such as professional identity) prompts nurses to view end-of-life care as a means to achieve personal values and the concept of care, thereby fostering a more positive and resilient attitude. On the other hand, individuals influenced by extrinsic motivation (such as family arrangements) are more likely to experience emotional detachment or avoidance when facing the emotional challenges of end-of-life care. This lack of internal drive makes it difficult for them to fully recognize and engage in the practice of humanistic care. Secondly, the openness of the family environment reduces the taboo and mystery of death-related topics, reducing the anxiety about death (Death Anxiety) of individuals. Family discussions are equivalent to an informal “death education”, providing nurses with a framework for thinking about life, death, and grief in advance. When they come into contact with professional palliative care knowledge, this knowledge is more likely to be assimilated and absorbed rather than rejected due to emotional resistance. Finally, the fear of death is the greatest psychological obstacle in practicing palliative care. Nurses who can accept death can better maintain emotional stability, shift their attention from their own anxiety to the needs of patients, and thus be able to more clearly assess the symptoms of patients and listen to their psychological needs. This process itself is a process of deepening knowledge understanding and application. Critically, enhancing the palliative care competency of residency-trained nurses will directly translate into higher-quality care outcomes for patients and their families [59]. With solid knowledge of pain and symptom management, nurses are better equipped to promptly identify, accurately assess, and effectively alleviate patient discomfort, thereby significantly enhancing physical comfort and minimizing suffering [60, 61]. Furthermore, a positive care attitude enables nurses to communicate more proactively and compassionately with patients about their concerns and wishes, while also providing timely psychological support and bereavement care for family members grappling with anxiety and grief [62, 63]. This comprehensive care, grounded in both knowledge and empathy, collectively leads to a care experience where patients can live their final days with greater dignity and peace, and family members experience reduced psychological distress and report higher satisfaction with the care provided [6].

Apart from this, this study also has two interesting findings. One of them is that there is a weak negative correlation between palliative care knowledge (PCQN) and attitude (FATCOD-B). We consider that this might be related to the following scenarios: First, As residency-trained nurses acquire more knowledge about end-of-life suffering, symptom complexity, and the dying process, they may become more acutely aware of the profound emotional challenges involved in palliative care and their own limitations in addressing them. This nascent awareness might temporarily induce higher levels of anxiety, a sense of powerlessness, or emotional exhaustion, which could be reflected as more cautious or less positive responses on the attitude scale; Second, Higher knowledge may foster a more critical and realistic perspective among nurses. They might move beyond uncritically endorsing the “idealized” care scenarios presented in the attitude scale and become more adept at recognizing the real-world barriers to such care (e.g., inadequate resources, lack of systemic support). This heightened awareness could realistically temper their attitude scores.;Third, Within the Chinese cultural context that often avoids discussions about death, systematic palliative care education might initially act by breaking this taboo, thereby activating and intensifying underlying death-related conflicts and anxieties. Consequently, the initial acquisition of knowledge might not alleviate but rather exacerbate psychological discomfort in this specific setting, leading to a temporary negative impact on attitudes.

Another critical finding of this study is the limited explanatory power of the regression models (R²=15.8%). This clearly indicates that approximately 84.2% of the variance is driven by factors not measured in this study. This does not mean the identified factors are unimportant, but rather highlights the complexity of the factors shaping nurses’ palliative care competency. This “unexplained variance” likely stems from deeper, less easily quantified elements, such as: deeply held personal philosophies toward death and individual values; the organizational culture and workplace climate regarding palliative care support within healthcare institutions; the absence of positive role models or mentors in clinical practice; and intrinsic traits like emotional intelligence, empathy, and habits of self-reflection among nurses. This finding strongly suggests that future research should move beyond conventional demographic and training variables to explore these underlying, potentially more decisive psychosocial and organizational factors.

In conclusion, the findings of this study—inadequate knowledge, moderately positive attitudes, and the significant influence of clinical experience among residency-trained nurses—collectively point to a more universal core question: Why do nurses in this professional transition period tend to have such knowledge and attitudes toward palliative care? The answer lies in the inherent characteristics of this career phase. Firstly, the theory-practice gap is a universal challenge for new graduates; the limited palliative care curriculum in pre-licensure education often fails to adequately prepare them for the complex and emotionally intense end-of-life scenarios encountered in clinical practice [36]. Secondly, “transition shock” compels novice nurses to prioritize adapting to the overwhelming demands of general clinical work, often at the expense of complex, emotionally demanding competencies like palliative care [64, 65]. Finally, the lack of a supportive motivational ecosystem—such as the absence of role models and organizational cultures that discourage open discussion about death—further impedes the translation of potential positive attitudes into consolidated knowledge and conviction. Therefore, the findings of this study transcend the specific Chinese context and illuminate a common blind spot in the global nursing community’s effort to support new nurses’ successful transition into well-rounded clinical experts. This highlights an urgent need: it is recommended that the systematic palliative care support and education be incorporated into the onboarding training programs for all newly graduated nurses.

Conclusion

This study reveals that the standardized training nurses in Chongqing city, China have significant deficiencies in palliative care knowledge, and their attitude towards providing such care services is only moderately positive. This highlights a key flaw in the current standardized training courses for nursing. It is urgent to incorporate comprehensive, standardized and mandatory palliative care education into the training plan. Such measures should not merely focus on knowledge dissemination, but should specifically target and positively influence potential attitudes, promote open discussions about death, and stimulate the intrinsic motivation of nurses. By doing so, educators and hospital administrators can better prepare this generation of new nurses to provide compassionate, competent and comprehensive end-of-life care services in an aging society.
