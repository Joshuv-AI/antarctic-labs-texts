Transportation cost and Contraction coefficient for channels on von Neumann algebras
Roy Araiza, Marius Junge, Peixue Wu

Abstract
. We present a noncommutative optimal transport framework for quantum channels acting on von Neumann algebras. Our central object is the Lipschitz cost measure, a transportation-inspired quantity that evaluates the minimal cost required to move between quantum states via a given channel. Accompanying this is the Lipschitz contraction coefficient, which captures how much the channel contracts the Wasserstein-type distance between states. We establish foundational properties of these quantities, including continuity, dual formulations, and behavior under composition and tensorization. Applications include recovery of several mathematical quantities including expected group word length and Carnot-Carathéodory distance, via transportation cost. Moreover, we show that if the Lipschitz contraction coefficient is strictly less than one, one can get entropy contraction and mixing time estimates for certain classes of non-symmetric channels.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
From operator Lipschitz geometry to optimal transport theory

First introduced by Connes
[
10
]
, spectral triples serve as a method to encode distance on noncommutative spaces. Indeed, a spectral triple
(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{H},D)
, consists of a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, an involutive algebra
\mathcal{A}
of operators on
\mathcal{H}
, and a self-adjoint Dirac operator
D
on
\mathcal{H}
such that

\displaystyle|||X|||_{L}:=\|[D,X]\|_{op}<\infty,

for all
X\in\mathcal{A}
.
Subsequently, Rieffel’s semimal work
[
33
,
34
]
axiomatized Lipschitz semi-norms on algebras and order unit spaces:

(0)

|||\,\cdot\,|||_{L}:\mathcal{A}\longrightarrow[0,\infty)
;

(1)

|||\textbf{1}|||_{L}=0
;

(2)

|||x|||_{L}=|||x^{*}|||_{L},\ x\in\mathcal{A}
.

Such a seminorm
generates
a metric on the normal state space
S(\mathcal{A})
via the dual formula

(1.1)

W_{L}(\rho,\sigma)=\sup\left\{|\rho(x)-\sigma(x)|:x=x^{*}\in\mathcal{A},\ |||x|||_{L}\leq 1\right\},\quad\rho,\sigma\in S(\mathcal{A}).

In the classical setting, Monge
[
28
]
formulated the optimal transport problem as finding a mapping
T:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{Y}
, where
\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}
are finite sets, such that
T
rearranges an initial distribution
\mu
to a target distribution
\nu
while minimizing the total transportation cost:

\inf_{T}\sum_{x\in X}c(x,T(x))\,d\mu(x),

where
c(x,y)
is the cost of moving a unit mass from
x
to
y
. However, this formulation requires the transport map
T
to be measurable and mass-preserving, which makes the problem non-linear and, in some cases, ill-posed. To address this issue, Kantorovich
[
26
]
introduced the concept of transport plans
\gamma
, which are modelled as probability measures on
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
with marginals
\mu
and
\nu
, allowing for mass to be split and distributed among multiple destinations. Then the reformulated problem becomes

W_{1,c}(\mu,\nu)=\inf_{\gamma\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X},y\in\mathcal{Y}}c(x,y)\gamma(x,y),

where
\Pi(\mu,\nu)
is the set of all transport plans (couplings) between
\mu
and
\nu
. Replacing the cost function
c
by a distance
d
on
\mathcal{X}
, we have the celebrated Kantorovitch duality formulation
[
26
]
:

(1.2)

W_{1,d}(\mu,\nu)=\sup\left\{\left|\int_{\mathcal{X}}f(x)d\mu(x)-\int_{\mathcal{X}}f(x)d\nu(x)\right|:|f(x)-f(y)|\leq d(x,y),\ \forall x,y\in\mathcal{X}\right\},

Note the striking resemblance between Equation (
1.1
) and Equation (
1.2
): the operator Lipschitz framework
contains
Kantorovich’s duality formulation in the commutative cases. Our approach exploits three distinct advantages formulating transport in terms of Lipschitz seminorms:

(1)

Direct noncommutative generality:
states on arbitrary von Neumann algebras are treated on equal footing with classical probability measures.

(2)

Operator-algebraic tools:
commutators, derivations and completely bounded norms replace geometric couplings, enabling powerful functional-analytic estimates.

(3)

Seamless classical limit:
setting the von Neumann algebra as
L_{\infty}(\mathcal{X})
and a suitable seminorm immediately recovers Wasserstein distances without additional work.

These features make the Lipschitz approach an ideal starting point for studying channels, and their contraction coefficients and mixing phenomena—topics that are central to this paper.

1.2.
Transportation cost and contraction coefficient of channels

In this work, inspired by (
1.1
), we provide a systematic method to quantify the
cost
of quantum transport plans, modeled as quantum channels, and to analyze the
contraction coefficient
of channels under this distance, referred to as the
Lipschitz constant
.

Let
\mathcal{N}
be a von Neumann algebra, and
|||\cdot|||_{L}
be a Lipschitz seminorm defined on a weak-
*
dense involutive algebra
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{N}
(see Section
3.1
for more general definition allowing operator space structure).

For a quantum channel
\Phi:\mathcal{N}\to\mathcal{N}
, which is assumed to be normal, unital and completely positive, we define two measures:

•

The
transportation cost
(or
Lipschitz Cost
):

(1.3)

\mathrm{Cost}_{L}(\Phi):=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x=x^{*}\\
|||x|||_{L}\leq 1\end{subarray}}\|\Phi(x)-x\|_{op},

where
\|\cdot\|_{op}
denotes the operator norm.

•

The
contraction coefficient
(or
Lipschitz constant
):

(1.4)

\mathrm{Lip}_{L}(\Phi):=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x=x^{*}\\
|||x|||_{L}\leq 1\end{subarray}}\|\Phi(x)\|_{L}.

To streamline the presentation, we only consider channels
\Phi
such that
\Phi(\mathcal{A})\subseteq\mathcal{A}
. Recall that every von Neumann algebra
\mathcal{N}
has a unique predual
\mathcal{N}_{*}

[
36
]
, which is the Banach space of all normal (weak-
*
continuous) linear functionals on
\mathcal{N}
. We denote by
S(\mathcal{N})
the normal state space of
\mathcal{N}
, i.e., unital positive elements in
\mathcal{N}_{*}
. Via standard duality argument, for the predual channel
\Phi_{*}:\mathcal{N}_{*}\to\mathcal{N}_{*}
, we have
\mathrm{Cost}_{L}(\Phi)=\sup_{\rho\in S(\mathcal{N})}W_{L}\big(\rho,\Phi_{*}(\rho)\big)
, which serves as a natural measure of cost, reflecting the minimal resources required to implement the quantum transformation
\Phi
. This idea was first explored in
[
11
,
27
]
and later in
[
3
]
as a means to quantify the cost of quantum operations.

In contrast,
\mathrm{Lip}_{L}(\Phi)
quantifies the maximal “amplification factor” of the Lipschitz seminorm under the channel
\Phi
. A large Lipschitz constant indicates that the channel is capable of generating or amplifying correlations, while a small Lipschitz constant suggests that the channel tends to mix the system, thereby promoting convergence to equilibrium.

1.3.
Overview of the main results

1.3.1.
Fundamental properties

A first natural question is whether (
1.4
) can be represented as the contraction coefficient under the Wasserstein metric, which motivates its name. In the classical (commutative) setting these two quantities coincide by Kantorovich duality. Whether equality still holds for general von Neumann algebras remained open, although one inequality is already known
[
20
, Proposition 4.4]
.

As a first step toward closing this gap, we show that equality
does
hold whenever the underlying semi-norm is Lipschitz, that is, obeys unit degeneracy and self-adjoint invariance:

Proposition 1.1
.

Let
\Phi\!:\mathcal{N}\!\to\!\mathcal{N}
be a normal, unital, completely positive map and let
\Phi_{*}:\mathcal{N}_{*}\!\to\!\mathcal{N}_{*}
denote its predual. For any Lipschitz semi-norm with domain
\mathcal{A}
such that
\Phi(\mathcal{A})\subseteq\mathcal{A}
, we have

\sup_{x=x^{*}\in\mathcal{A}}\frac{|||\Phi(x)|||_{L}}{|||x|||_{L}}\;=\;\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\rho,\sigma\in S(\mathcal{N})\\
\rho\neq\sigma\end{subarray}}\frac{W_{L}\bigl(\Phi_{*}(\rho),\Phi_{*}(\sigma)\bigr)}{W_{L}(\rho,\sigma)}.

Motivated by
[
39
]
, we next consider
matrix
Lipschitz structures. A
matrix Lipschitz semi-norm
is a family
\{|||\!\cdot\!|||_{L^{(n)}}\}_{n\geq 1}
with

|||\!\cdot\!|||_{L^{(n)}}:\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathcal{A})\to[0,\infty)
satisfying

(1)

Degeneracy on the unit
:
|||\textbf{1}|||_{L^{(n)}}=0.

(2)

Self-adjoint invariance (Lipschitz isometric)
:
|||x|||_{L^{(n)}}=|||x^{*}|||_{L^{(n)}}\quad\forall\,x\in\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathcal{A})
.

(3)

|||x\oplus y|||_{L^{(n+m)}}=\max\{|||x|||_{L^{(n)}},|||y|||_{L^{(m)}}\},\quad x\in\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathcal{A}),y\in\mathbb{M}_{m}(\mathcal{A}),n,m\geq 1
.

(4)

|||axb|||_{L^{(m)}}\leq\|a\|_{op}\cdot|||x|||_{L^{(n)}}\cdot\|b\|_{op},\quad\quad x\in\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathcal{A}),a\in\mathbb{M}_{m\times n},b\in\mathbb{M}_{n\times m}
.

Writing
|||\!\cdot\!|||_{L}:=|||\!\cdot\!|||_{L^{(1)}}
, we set

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cost}_{L}^{cb}(\Phi):=\sup_{n\geq 1}\;\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x=x^{*}\in\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathcal{A})\\
|||x|||_{L^{(n)}}\leq 1\end{subarray}}\bigl\|\Phi^{(n)}(x)-x\bigr\|_{op},

\displaystyle\mathrm{Lip}_{L}^{cb}(\Phi):=\sup_{n\geq 1}\;\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x=x^{*}\in\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathcal{A})\\
|||x|||_{L^{(n)}}\leq 1\end{subarray}}|||\Phi^{(n)}(x)|||_{L^{(n)}}.

and

1.3.2.
Applications

We now illustrate the versatility of our framework through several concrete applications. We begin by showing that, in the purely classical setting, the transportation cost of a conditional expectation coincides with the familiar
expected word length
in a finite group.

Word length via conditional expectations (see Section
4.1
)
:

Let
G
be a finite group equipped with a finite symmetric generating set
S
. The
word length
of
g\in G
(with respect to
S
) is the minimal number of generators or their inverses required to express
g
, that is

(1.5)

\ell_{S}(g)=\min\{n\in\mathbb{N}:g=s_{1}s_{2}\cdots s_{n},\;s_{i}\in S\}.

Endow
G
with the normalized Haar measure
\mu
, so that
\mu(\{g\})=|G|^{-1}
for all
g\in G
. Consider

\mathcal{N}\;=\;L_{\infty}(G,\mu)\;=\;\bigl\{f:G\to\mathbb{C}\,\big|\,f\text{ bounded}\bigr\},\quad\mathcal{H}\;=\;L_{2}(G,\mu),

with inner product
\langle f_{1},f_{2}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\;=\;\int_{G}f_{1}(g)\,\overline{f_{2}(g)}\,d\mu(g)
. We define a conditional expectation

(1.6)

E_{\mathrm{fix}}(f)=\left(\int_{G}f(g)\,d\mu(g)\right)\textbf{1}_{G},\quad f\in\mathcal{N}.

With this in hand, we can now characterize the expected group word length via the Lipschitz cost measure:

Theorem 1.2

(c.f. Theorem
4.2
)
.

There exists a Lipschitz semi-norm
|||\cdot|||_{L}
on
\mathcal{N}
such that

\mathrm{Cost}_{L}(E_{\mathrm{fix}})=\int_{G}\ell_{S}(g)\,d\mu(g).

Carnot-Carathéodory distance for locally compact Lie groups (see Section
4.2
):

Let
G
now be a locally compact Lie group with Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
and Haar measure
\mu
. For a unitary representation
\pi:G\longrightarrow U(\mathcal{K})
, we denote the induced representation of the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
on the same Hilbert space
d\pi:\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{u}(\mathcal{K})
, where
\mathfrak{u}(\mathcal{K})
denotes the skew-adjoint operators on
\mathcal{K}
.

For a fixed subspace
\mathfrak{h}\subseteq\mathfrak{g}
, we obtain a non-commutative Lipschitz semi-norm on
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{K})
as follows: let
X_{1},\dots,X_{m}
be an orthonormal basis of
\mathfrak{h}
. For

\mathcal{A}\;:=\;\bigl\{x\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{K})\bigm|[d\pi(X_{j}),x]\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{K})\text{ for all }j\bigr\},

define the Lipschitz semi-norm:

|||x|||_{L}:=\Bigl\|\sum_{j=1}^{m}[d\pi(X_{j}),x]^{*}[d\pi(X_{j}),x]\Bigr\|^{1/2}_{op},\qquad x\in\mathcal{A}.

Theorem 1.3

(c.f. Theorem
4.6
)
.

For every unitary representation
\pi
and
g\in G
,

\mathrm{Cost}_{L}\bigl(\text{ad}_{\pi(g)}\bigr)\;\leq\;d_{\mathfrak{h}}(g,e),\ \text{ad}_{\pi(g)}(x):=\pi(g)^{*}x\,\pi(g).

where
d_{\mathfrak{h}}
is the sub-Riemannian (Carnot–Carathéodory) distance on
G
associated with
\mathfrak{h}
defined in (
4.4
).

Entropy contraction and mixing times (see Section
5
)
:

We next use the Lipschitz constant formalism to bound entropy contraction coefficients and derive mixing-time estimates for (not necessarily symmetric) quantum channels on
\mathcal{N}=\mathbb{M}_{d}
. Since we are in finite dimension, there exists a trace and we adopt the usual quantum convention, i.e.,
\Phi
is trace preserving and completely positive. Assume
\Phi
has a unique fixed state
\sigma
with full rank, define the entropy contraction coefficient

(1.7)

\eta^{D}(\Phi,\sigma):=\sup_{\rho\in S(\mathcal{N})}\frac{D(\Phi(\rho)\|\sigma)}{D(\rho\|\sigma)}\in[0,1].

Motivated by
[
8
]
, a general upper bound on the entropy contraction coefficient is given as follows:

Theorem 1.4

(c.f. Theorem
5.6
)
.

Assume additionally
\Phi
is a strictly positive channel, then for any Lipschitz semi-norm
|||\cdot|||_{L}
,

\eta^{D}(\Phi,\sigma)\leq\mathrm{Lip}_{L}(\Phi^{*})\max\left\{\mathrm{Lip}_{L}(\Phi^{\mathrm{*,BKM}}),\mathrm{LogLip}(\Phi,\sigma)\right\},

where
\Phi^{\mathrm{*,BKM}}:=\Gamma_{\sigma}^{-1}\circ\Phi\circ\Gamma_{\sigma},\quad\Gamma_{\sigma}(x):=\int_{0}^{1}\sigma^{s}x\,\sigma^{1-s}ds
and the logarithmic Lipschitz constant is defined by

\displaystyle\mathrm{LogLip}_{L}(\Phi,\sigma)\;:=\;\sup_{\rho\in S(\mathcal{N}),\,\rho>0}\frac{|||\,\log\Phi(\rho)-\log\sigma\,|||_{L}}{|||\,\log\rho-\log\sigma\,|||_{L}}.

Note that in the commutative case, the Lipschitz constant of
\Phi^{\mathrm{*,BKM}}
and logarithmic Lipschitz constant for
\Phi
can be upper bounded by
1
if the channel satisfies non-negative sectional curvature condition
[
8
]
. However, since in the noncommutative setting there is no coupling notion, more technical issues arise.

Nevertheless, using bounded mean oscillation (BMO) techniques developed in
[
9
]
, we can bound
\mathrm{LogLip}(\Phi,\sigma)
for general unital channels, yielding the following mixing-time estimate:

Theorem 1.5

(c.f. Theorem
5.13
)
.

Let
\Phi
be unital with unique fixed point
\mathbf{1}/d
. There exists a universal constant
c_{\mathrm{abs}}>0
such that, for any commutator semi-norm
|||\cdot|||_{L}
with
\mathrm{Lip}_{L}(\Phi)<1
and
\mathrm{Lip}_{L}(\Phi^{*})\leq 1
,

t_{\mathrm{mix}}\!\bigl(\varepsilon,\Phi\bigr)\;\leq\;c_{\mathrm{abs}}\,\frac{\log(1/\varepsilon)+\log d}{-\log\bigl(\mathrm{Lip}_{L}(\Phi)\bigr)\;-\;\log\!\bigl(\mathrm{Lip}_{L}(\Phi^{*})\bigr)}.

Finally, we relate the two quantitative measures introduced above and obtain a cost induced mixing time bound that underpins the lower-bound results of
[
13
]
for simulating open quantum systems. Recall that for a quantum channel
\Phi
with fixed point algebra
\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{fix}}
and a conditional expectation
E_{\mathrm{fix}}
onto
\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{fix}}
, define the cost induced mixing time

\displaystyle t_{\mathrm{mix}}^{L}(\varepsilon,\Phi^{*})=\inf\{n\geq 1:\mathrm{Cost}_{L}^{cb}((\Phi^{*})^{n})\geq(1-\varepsilon)\mathrm{Cost}_{L}^{cb}(E_{\mathrm{fix}})\}.

The following bound for cost induced mixing time connects the Lipschitz constant and transportation cost:

Proposition 1.6

(c.f. Proposition
5.15
)
.

Suppose
\Phi^{*}\circ E_{\mathrm{fix}}=E_{\mathrm{fix}}\circ\Phi^{*}=E_{\mathrm{fix}}
and
\mathrm{Lip}_{L}^{\mathrm{cb}}(\Phi)<1
. Then, for every
\varepsilon>0
,

t_{\mathrm{mix}}^{L}\!\bigl(\varepsilon,\Phi^{*}\bigr)\;\leq\;\frac{\log(1/\varepsilon)}{-\log\bigl(\mathrm{Lip}_{L}^{\mathrm{cb}}(\Phi^{*})\bigr)}.

1.4.
Relation to other works

A first step toward transportation cost for channels was taken in
[
11
,
27
]
, where the quantum Wasserstein–1 distance was defined in
n
-qubit system. Yet, as noted in
[
3
]
, the resulting metric is upper bounded by
n
; it therefore fails to resolve genuinely non–local behaviour in large systems. Our framework overcomes this limitation by allowing
arbitrary
Lipschitz semi-norms: by selecting suitable commutator norms one can tailor the cost to the physical locality structure of interest.

The notion of a Lipschitz constant for quantum channels was introduced in
[
20
]
, and several ad-hoc estimates were obtained later
[
35
]
. The present paper advances the subject in two directions:

(1)

We give a unified, operator-algebraic treatment of
both
transportation cost and Lipschitz constant for arbitrary von Neumann algebras, clarifying their functorial and tensorial properties.

(2)

We provide the first systematic analysis of entropy contraction for non-symmetric primitive channels and translate these bounds into mixing-time estimates.

One interesting direction for future research is to study the transportation cost and contraction coefficient of channels via the coupling approach to noncommutative optimal transport theory, which is currently an active area of investigation, see for example
[
6
,
15
,
1
,
7
,
12
,
4
,
2
]
.

Organization of the paper.

Section
2
recalls the basic operator-algebraic tools used throughout.
Section
3
establishes the fundamental properties of transportation cost and Lipschitz constant in full generality.
Section
4
illustrates the cost formalism by recovering several familiar quantitative invariants.
Finally, Section
5
applies our Lipschitz estimates to entropy contraction coefficients and mixing-time bounds.

2.
Preliminary

Basics on von Neumann algebras
.— General references for von Neumann algebras are
[
36
,
14
]
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a Hilbert space, and let
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
denote the algebra of bounded linear operators on
\mathcal{H}
. A subset
\mathcal{N}\subseteq\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
is called a
von Neumann algebra
if it is a unital
*
-subalgebra that is closed in the weak operator topology (or equivalently, in the strong operator topology). By the double commutant theorem, this is equivalent to
\mathcal{N}=\mathcal{N}^{\prime\prime}
, where
\mathcal{N}^{\prime}:=\{x\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}):[a,x]=ax-xa=0,\ \forall a\in\mathcal{N}\}
denotes the commutant of
\mathcal{N}
in
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
.

The weak operator topology (also known as weak
∗
-topology) on
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
is the topology of pointwise convergence on
\mathcal{H}
; that is, a net
(x_{\alpha})\subseteq\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
converges to
x\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
in the weak operator topology if and only if

\langle\xi,x_{\alpha}\eta\rangle\to\langle\xi,x\eta\rangle\quad\text{for all }\xi,\eta\in\mathcal{H}.

For any operator
x\in\mathcal{N}
, the
operator norm
is defined by

\|x\|_{\mathcal{N}}=\|x\|_{op}=\sup_{\xi\in\mathcal{H},\|\xi\|=1}\|x\xi\|=\sqrt{\sup\sigma(x^{*}x)},

where
\sigma(x^{*}x)
denotes the spectrum of
x^{*}x
. We denote the sets of self-adjoint and positive elements in
\mathcal{N}
as

\mathcal{N}_{s.a.}=\{x\in\mathcal{N}:x=x^{*}\},\quad\mathcal{N}_{+}=\{x^{*}x:x\in\mathcal{N}\}.

We denote by
\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{N}}
the identity operator in
\mathcal{N}
.

Every von Neumann algebra
\mathcal{N}
has a
predual

\mathcal{N}_{*}
, which is the Banach space of all weak
∗
-continuous linear functionals on
\mathcal{N}
. A linear functional
\phi:\mathcal{N}\to\mathbb{C}
is called a
state
if it is positive (i.e.,
\phi(x^{*}x)\geq 0
for all
x\in\mathcal{N}
) and normalized (i.e.,
\phi(\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{N}})=1
). We denote by
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{N})
the set of normal states on
\mathcal{N}
(i.e., weak
∗
-continuous states). A state
\phi
is called
faithful
if
\phi(x^{*}x)=0
implies
x=0
. If
\mathcal{H}
is separable, then
\mathcal{N}
admits at least one faithful normal state.

A state
\tau
is called a
tracial state
if it additionally satisfies the
trace property
:

\tau(xy)=\tau(yx),\quad\forall x,y\in\mathcal{N}.

Not every von Neumann algebra admits a tracial state. In particular, type III von Neumann algebras (ever-present in quantum field theory) do not admit any tracial states. For type III von Neumann algebras, every normal positive linear functional
\phi
has a non-trivial modular group
\{\sigma_{t}^{\phi}\}
obtained via Tomita–Takesaki theory
[
36
]
. This modular group cannot be trivialized, and thus there is no cyclic invariance under the trace property because the modular automorphism group disrupts this cyclicity.

When we require the trace property, we assume that
\mathcal{N}
is a
semifinite von Neumann algebra
. That is,
\mathcal{N}
admits a faithful normal semifinite trace
\tau:\mathcal{N}_{+}\to[0,\infty]
. More precisely,
\tau
satisfies:

(i)

Faithfulness: If
\tau(x^{*}x)=0
, then
x=0
.

(ii)

Normality: For any bounded increasing net
\{x_{\alpha}\}\subset\mathcal{N}_{+}
,

\tau\left(\sup_{\alpha}x_{\alpha}\right)=\sup_{\alpha}\tau(x_{\alpha}).

(iii)

Semifiniteness: For any nonzero
a\in\mathcal{N}_{+}
, there exists a nonzero
e\in\mathcal{N}_{+}
with
e\leq a
such that
\tau(e)<\infty
.

For example,
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
, where
\mathcal{H}
is separable and infinite-dimensional, is a semifinite von Neumann algebra when equipped with the usual (unbounded) trace
\operatorname{Tr}
. A von Neumann algebra
\mathcal{N}
is called
finite
if the family formed of the finite, i.e.,
\tau(\textbf{1}_{\mathcal{N}})<\infty
, normal traces separates points of
\mathcal{N}
. If
\mathcal{N}
admits a single faithful normal finite trace, then it is finite. Though, in general a finite von Neumann algebra may fail to have a faithful finite trace. All finite-dimensional von Neumann algebras are examples of finite von Neumann algebras.

In the prescence of a trace
\tau
, one may naturally consider the corresponding
noncommutative
L_{p}
spaces
. Define
\mathcal{N}_{0}=\bigcup_{\tau(e)<\infty}e\mathcal{N}e
, where the union is over all projections
e\in\mathcal{N}
with
\tau(e)<\infty
. For
1\leq p<\infty
, the noncommutative
L^{p}
space
L_{p}(\mathcal{N},\tau)
is defined as the completion of
\mathcal{N}_{0}
with respect to the norm

\|a\|_{L_{p}(\mathcal{N},\tau)}=\left(\tau(|a|^{p})\right)^{1/p}.

We will often use the shorthand notation
\|\cdot\|_{p}
when no confusion arises. We identify
L_{\infty}(\mathcal{N}):=\mathcal{N}
, and the predual space
\mathcal{N}_{*}\cong L_{1}(\mathcal{N},\tau)
via the duality

(2.1)

a\in L_{1}(\mathcal{N})\longleftrightarrow\phi_{a}\in\mathcal{N}_{*},\quad\phi_{a}(x)=\tau(ax).

This identification allows us to treat elements of
L_{1}(\mathcal{N})
as normal linear functionals on
\mathcal{N}
.

Channels.
—A channel
\Phi:\mathcal{N}\to\mathcal{N}
is a normal, unital, and completely positive linear map on a von Neumann algebra
\mathcal{N}
. Let
\mathfrak{C}(\mathcal{N})
denote the set of all channels on
\mathcal{N}
. Here, completely positive means for any matrix algebra
\mathbb{M}_{n}
, the linear operator
id_{\mathbb{M}_{n}}\otimes\Phi
is positive. Here
id
denotes a identity channel in this paper.

We adopt the
Heisenberg picture
, where channels act on observables within the algebra
\mathcal{N}
. This contrasts with the more common
Schrödinger picture
in quantum information theory, where channels act on states.

The predual map
\Phi_{*}:\mathcal{N}_{*}\to\mathcal{N}_{*}
, representing the Schrödinger picture, is defined via the duality between
\mathcal{N}
and its predual
\mathcal{N}_{*}
. Specifically, for all
\rho\in\mathcal{N}_{*}
and
x\in\mathcal{N}
, we have

(2.2)

\Phi_{*}(\rho)(x)=\rho(\Phi(x)).

This relation ensures that the action of
\Phi_{*}
on states corresponds to the action of
\Phi
on observables. In fact, when
\mathcal{N}
is semifinite and equipped with a faithful normal semifinite trace
\tau
, we can relate
\Phi
and
\Phi_{*}
via (
2.1
):

(2.3)

\tau\big(\rho\,\Phi(x)\big)=\tau\big(\Phi_{*}(\rho)\,x\big).

By setting
x=\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{N}}
, it follows that
\Phi_{*}
is
trace-preserving
:

(2.4)

\tau\big(\Phi_{*}(\rho)\big)=\tau(\rho).

Thus, the predual map
\Phi_{*}
preserves the trace, aligning with the definition of a quantum channel in the Schrödinger picture.

Note that every completely positive map between von Neumann algebras is automatically a complete contraction:

(2.5)

\|\Phi\|_{cb}\leq 1,\quad\|\Phi\|_{cb}:=\sup\{\|(id_{\mathbb{M}_{n}}\otimes\Phi)(X)\|_{op}:\|X\|_{op}\leq 1,\ X\in\mathbb{M}_{n}\otimes\mathcal{N},n\geq 1\}.

In the case that
\Phi:\mathcal{N}\to\mathcal{N}
is unital completely positive, then

\|\Phi:\mathcal{N}\to\mathcal{N}\|_{cb}=\|\Phi:\mathcal{N}\to\mathcal{N}\|_{op}=\|\Phi(\textbf{1}_{\mathcal{N}})\|=1.

Conditional expectations
.— If
\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{fix}}\subseteq\mathcal{N}
is a von Neumann subalgebra, then a conditional expectation
E_{\mathrm{fix}}:\mathcal{N}\to\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{fix}}
is a channel such that

(2.6)

E_{\mathrm{fix}}(n_{1}xn_{2})=n_{1}E_{\mathrm{fix}}(x)n_{2},\quad\forall x\in\mathcal{N},\ n_{i}\in\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{fix}}.

The index
\mathcal{I}(E_{\mathrm{fix}})
of a conditional expectation
E_{\mathrm{fix}}
is defined by

(2.7)

\mathcal{I}(E_{\mathrm{fix}})=\inf\{c>0:\ x^{*}x\leq cE_{\mathrm{fix}}(x^{*}x),\ \forall x\in\mathcal{N}\},

and we denote
\mathcal{I}^{cb}(E_{\mathrm{fix}})=\sup_{n\geq 1}\mathcal{I}(id_{\mathbb{M}_{n}}\otimes E_{\mathrm{fix}})
.

We illustrate this notion in finite dimension. Recall that any finite dimensional von Neumann algebra is isomorphic to

(2.8)

\bigoplus_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{i})\otimes\mathbb{C}\cdot\textbf{1}_{\mathcal{K}_{i}},\mathcal{H}=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{H}_{i}\otimes\mathcal{K}_{i},\quad d_{\mathcal{H}_{i}}=\text{dim}\mathcal{H}_{i},\ d_{\mathcal{K}_{i}}=\text{dim}\mathcal{K}_{i}

Assume
\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{fix}}
has the form (
2.8
) and
\mathcal{N}=\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
. Denote
P_{i}
as the projection of
\mathcal{H}
onto
\mathcal{H}_{i}\otimes\mathcal{K}_{i}
, then there exists a family of states
\tau_{i}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{K}_{i})
such that

(2.9)

\displaystyle E_{\mathrm{fix}}(X)=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{n}\tr_{\mathcal{K}_{i}}\big(P_{i}XP_{i}(1_{\mathcal{H}_{i}}\otimes\tau_{i})\big)\otimes 1_{\mathcal{K}_{i}},\quad E_{\mathrm{fix}*}(\rho)=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{n}\tr_{\mathcal{K}_{i}}\big(P_{i}\rho P_{i}\big)\otimes\tau_{i},

where
\tr_{\mathcal{K}_{i}}
is the partial trace with respect to
\mathcal{K}_{i}
. It is also easy to see that a state
\sigma
is invariant for
E_{\mathrm{fix}}
, i.e.,
E_{\mathrm{fix}*}(\sigma)=\sigma
if and only if
\sigma=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}\sigma_{i}\otimes\tau_{i},
for some states
\sigma_{i}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H}_{i})
and probability distribution
\{p_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
. The conditional expectation is completely determined if an invariant state is specified, and we denote it as
E_{\mathrm{fix},\sigma}
. We drop the index
\sigma
if it is clear from the context. Note that the invariant state is not unique and the flexibility comes from different choices of
\sigma_{i}
and the probability distribution
\{p_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
. The explicit calculation of the index of tracial conditional expectation is given in
[
17
]
:

(2.10)

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(E_{\mathrm{fix},\tr})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\min\{d_{\mathcal{H}_{i}},d_{\mathcal{K}_{i}}\}d_{\mathcal{K}_{i}},\quad\mathcal{I}^{cb}(E_{\mathrm{fix},\tr})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{\mathcal{K}_{i}}^{2}.

3.
Basic properties of transportation cost and Lipschitz constant of channels

Let
\mathcal{N}
be a von Neumann algebra, and
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{N}
is a weak-
*
dense subalgebra. A
matrix Lipschitz semi-norm
introduced in
[
39
]
is a family of seminorms
|||\cdot|||_{L^{(n)}}:\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathcal{A})\to[0,\infty),\ n\geq 1
such that

(1)

Degeneracy on the Unit
:
|||\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathcal{A})}|||_{L^{(n)}}=0.

(2)

Self-Adjoint Invariance (Lipschitz isometric)
:
|||x|||_{L^{(n)}}=|||x^{*}|||_{L^{(n)}}\quad\forall\,x\in\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathcal{A})
.

(3)

|||x\oplus y|||_{L^{(n+m)}}=\max\{|||x|||_{L^{(n)}},|||y|||_{L^{(m)}}\},\quad x\in\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathcal{A}),y\in\mathbb{M}_{m}(\mathcal{A}),n,m\geq 1
.

(4)

|||axb|||_{L^{(m)}}\leq\|a\|_{op}\cdot|||x|||_{L^{(n)}}\cdot\|b\|_{op},\quad\quad x\in\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathcal{A}),a\in\mathbb{M}_{m\times n},b\in\mathbb{M}_{n\times m}
.

Here
\|\cdot\|_{op}
denotes the operator norm. We denote
|||\cdot|||_{L}=|||\cdot|||_{L^{(1)}}
.

For a quantum channel
\Phi:\mathcal{N}\to\mathcal{N}
, which is assumed to be unital, normal, and completely positive, denote

(3.1)

id_{\mathbb{M}_{n}}\otimes\Phi=\Phi^{(n)}

as the
n
-th amplification of
\Phi
. We define two quantities for channels. As mentioned in the introduction, we only consider channels
\Phi(\mathcal{A})\subseteq\mathcal{A}
.

Definition 3.1
.

The transportation cost (or Lipschitz Cost) of a quantum channel
\Phi
and its complete transportation cost are defined by

(3.2)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cost}_{L}(\Phi):=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x=x^{*}\\
|||x|||_{L}\leq 1\end{subarray}}\|\Phi(x)-x\|_{op},

(3.3)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cost}_{L}^{cb}(\Phi):=\sup_{n\geq 1}\sup\left\{\|\Phi^{(n)}(x)-x\|_{op}:\ x=x^{*}\in\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathcal{A}),|||x|||_{L^{(n)}}\leq 1\right\}.

The Lipschitz constant of a quantum channel
\Phi
and its complete Lipschitz constant are defined by

(3.4)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Lip}_{L}(\Phi):=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x=x^{*}\\
|||x|||_{L}\leq 1\end{subarray}}\|\Phi(x)\|_{L},

(3.5)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Lip}^{cb}_{L}(\Phi):=\sup_{n\geq 1}\sup\left\{\|\Phi^{(n)}(x)\|_{L^{(n)}}:\ x=x^{*}\in\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathcal{A}),|||x|||_{L^{(n)}}\leq 1\right\}.

When the semi-norm is defined on the full von Neumann algebra, i.e.,
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{N}
, we are in the usual quantum setting. Since
\Phi(\mathcal{A})\subseteq\mathcal{A}
, without loss of generality we can actually assume the semi-norm is defined on
\mathcal{N}
, via standard approximation procedure. Recall that every von Neumann algebra
\mathcal{N}
possesses a unique predual

\mathcal{N}_{*}

[
36
]
, the Banach space of all normal
(weak
∗
‑continuous) linear functionals on
\mathcal{N}
. Denote by
S(\mathcal{N})
its (normal) state space.
To discuss the dual semi-norm, we make use of the identification

(3.6)

\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathcal{N}_{*})=\operatorname{CB}(\mathcal{N},\,\mathbb{M}_{n})=\{f:\mathcal{N}\to\mathbb{M}_{n}:f\text{\ completely\ bounded}\}.

We can actually define the dual seminorm on
\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathcal{A}^{*})=\mathrm{CB}(\mathcal{A}^{**},\mathbb{M}_{n})
in which
\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathcal{N}_{*})
embeds into. In particular, the operator space dual structure on
\mathcal{N}_{*}
is inherited via the inclusion
\mathcal{N}_{*}\subseteq\mathcal{A}^{*}
.

One may define the seminorm
|||\cdot|||_{L^{(n)}}^{*}
for
f\in\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathcal{N}_{*})
as follows:

(3.7)

|||f|||_{L^{(n)}}^{*}:=\sup\left\{\left\|\langle\!\!\!\langle f,x\rangle\!\!\!\rangle\right\|_{op}:x\in\mathbb{M}_{m}(\mathcal{N}),\,|||x|||_{L^{(m)}}\leq 1,\ m\in\mathbb{N}\right\}.

Via Smith lemma
[
32
]
, the supremum over
m
can be choose as
m\leq n
. Here
\langle\!\!\!\langle f,x\rangle\!\!\!\rangle
denotes the matrix pairing

(3.8)

\langle\!\!\!\langle f,x\rangle\!\!\!\rangle:=\left[f_{ij}(x_{{kl}})\right]\in\mathbb{M}_{mn},\ f=[f_{ij}]\in\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathcal{N}_{*}),\ x=[x_{kl}]\in\mathbb{M}_{m}(\mathcal{N}).

It is well known (see, e.g.,
[
33
]
) that if
|||\cdot|||_{L^{(n)}}
is self-adjoint invariant, then the supremum in
(
3.7
) can be restricted to self‑adjoint elements:
for every
f=f^{*}\in\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathcal{N}_{*})
,

(3.9)

|||f|||_{L^{(n)}}^{*}:=\sup\left\{\left\|\langle\!\!\!\langle f,x\rangle\!\!\!\rangle\right\|_{op}:x=x^{*}\in\mathbb{M}_{m}(\mathcal{N}),\,|||x|||_{L^{(m)}}\leq 1,\ m\in\mathbb{N}\right\}.

Using a standard off-diagonal trick in operator space theory, the supremum over
m
can be given by
m\leq 2n
. Accordingly, one can defined an induced metric on the space
\operatorname{CP}(\mathcal{N},\,\mathbb{M}_{n})=\{\rho:\mathcal{N}\to\mathbb{M}_{n}:\rho\ \text{completely\ positive}\}
:

(3.10)

W_{L^{(n)}}(\rho,\sigma)=|||\rho-\sigma|||_{L^{(n)}}^{*},\ \rho,\sigma\in\operatorname{CP}(\mathcal{N},\,\mathbb{M}_{n}).

Let us call
W_{L}
the
Wasserstein L-metric
. In
[
39
]
, the author further restricts to unital, completely positive maps. However, as we will see below, to establish a duality relation, it is important to stay within completely positive maps since the unital property is too restrictive.

It is straightforward to show that

(3.11)

\mathrm{Cost}_{L}(\Phi)=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x=x^{*}\\
|||x|||_{L}\leq 1\end{subarray}}\|\Phi(x)-x\|_{op}=\sup_{\rho\in S(\mathcal{N})}W_{L}\big(\rho,\Phi_{*}(\rho)\big),

In fact, note that
x=x^{*}
,
\|\Phi(x)-x\|_{op}=\sup_{\rho\in S(\mathcal{N})}\rho(\Phi(x)-x)
. Taking the supremum and exchanging the order

\displaystyle\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x=x^{*}\\
|||x|||_{L}\leq 1\end{subarray}}\|\Phi(x)-x\|_{op}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x=x^{*}\\
|||x|||_{L}\leq 1\end{subarray}}\sup_{\rho\in S(\mathcal{N})}\rho(\Phi(x)-x)

\displaystyle=\sup_{\rho\in S(\mathcal{N})}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x=x^{*}\\
|||x|||_{L}\leq 1\end{subarray}}(\Phi_{*}(\rho)-\rho)(x)

\displaystyle=\sup_{\rho\in S(\mathcal{N})}\|\Phi_{*}(\rho)-\rho\|_{L}^{*}.

Remark 3.2
.

Given such a matrix Lipschitz seminorm
\{L^{(n)}\}_{n\geq 1}
, if one wishes to realize
\mathrm{Cost}^{cb}_{L}(\Phi)
as a Wasserstein L-metric, then one must consider alternate pairing for
\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathcal{N})
. In particular, one must consider the matricial state space

S(\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathcal{N})):=\{F:\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathcal{N})\to\mathbb{C}:F\,\,\text{unital completely positive}\}

rather than
\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathcal{N}_{*})
, and use the natural scalar pairing given as

\langle\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathcal{N}),\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathcal{N})_{*}\rangle,\quad\langle x,F\rangle=F(x).

A slightly more involved duality argument shows that under the assumptions of degeneracy on the unit and self-adjoint invariance, the (complete) Lipschitz constant coincides with the (complete) contraction coefficient with respect to the Wasserstein L-metric defined in (
3.10
):

Proposition 3.3
.

Under the assumptions (
1
)– (
4
) and
\Phi(\mathcal{A})\subseteq\mathcal{A}
, we have

(3.12)

\mathrm{Lip}_{L}(\Phi)=\sup_{\rho\neq\sigma\in S(\mathcal{N})}\frac{W_{L}(\Phi_{*}(\rho),\Phi_{*}(\sigma))}{W_{L}(\rho,\sigma)},\quad\mathrm{Lip}_{L}^{cb}(\Phi)=\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\rho,\sigma\in\mathrm{CP}(\mathcal{N},\mathbb{M}_{n})\\
\rho(\mathrm{\textbf{1}})=\sigma(\mathrm{\textbf{1}})\end{subarray}}\frac{W_{L^{(n)}}(\Phi^{(n)}_{*}(\rho),\Phi^{(n)}_{*}(\sigma))}{W_{L^{(n)}}(\rho,\sigma)}.

Proof.

We present the proof for
\mathrm{Lip}_{L}(\Phi)
; the case of
\mathrm{Lip}^{cb}_{L}(\Phi)
follows analogously, requiring only standard operator-space refinements. Moreover, since
\Phi(\mathcal{A})\subseteq\mathcal{A}
, we can assume
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{N}
to streamline the proof.
Since

|||\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{N}}|||_{L}=0,

the triangle inequality implies that for any
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
and any
x\in\mathcal{N}
, we have

|||x+\lambda\,\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{N}}|||_{L}=|||x|||_{L}.

Thus,
|||\cdot|||_{L}
descends to a well-defined seminorm on the quotient
\mathcal{N}/(\mathbb{C}\,\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{N}})
. In particular, for any
x\in\mathcal{N}
,

|||x|||_{L}=\sup\Big\{\,|f(x)|:f\in\mathcal{N}_{*},\ f(\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{N}})=0,\ |||f|||_{L}^{*}\leq 1\,\Big\}.

Because
\Phi
is unital, an analogous representation holds for
\Phi(x)
:

(3.13)

|||\Phi(x)|||_{L}=\sup\Big\{\,|f(\Phi(x))|:f\in\mathcal{N}_{*},\ f(\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{N}})=0,\ |||f|||_{L}^{*}\leq 1\,\Big\}.

We now claim that if
x=x^{*}
, then in (
3.13
) one may restrict the supremum to self-adjoint functionals. To see this, let
\varepsilon>0
and choose
f_{0}\in\mathcal{N}_{*}
with
f_{0}(\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{N}})=0
and
|||f_{0}|||_{L}^{*}\leq 1
such that

|f_{0}(\Phi(x))|\geq|||\Phi(x)|||_{L}-\varepsilon.

By multiplying
f_{0}
by a suitable phase factor
e^{i\theta}
, we may assume without loss of generality that
f_{0}(\Phi(x))\in\mathbb{R}
. Then, setting

f:=\frac{f_{0}+f_{0}^{*}}{2},

we obtain a self-adjoint functional satisfying
f(\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{N}})=0
,
|||f|||_{L}^{*}\leq 1
, and

f(\Phi(x))=f_{0}(\Phi(x)).

Thus, (
3.13
) may be rewritten for self-adjoint
x
as

|||\Phi(x)|||_{L}=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}f=f^{*}\in\mathcal{N}_{*}\\
f(\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{N}})=0,\;|||f|||_{L}^{*}\leq 1\end{subarray}}|f(\Phi(x))|.

Taking the supremum over all self-adjoint
x
with
|||x|||_{L}\leq 1
, we have

\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x=x^{*}\\
|||x|||_{L}\leq 1\end{subarray}}|||\Phi(x)|||_{L}=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x=x^{*}\\
|||x|||_{L}\leq 1\end{subarray}}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}f=f^{*}\in\mathcal{N}_{*}\\
f(\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{N}})=0,\;|||f|||_{L}^{*}\leq 1\end{subarray}}|f(\Phi(x))|.

Exchanging the order of supremum yields

\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x=x^{*}\\
|||x|||_{L}\leq 1\end{subarray}}|||\Phi(x)|||_{L}=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}f=f^{*}\in\mathcal{N}_{*}\\
f(\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{N}})=0,\;|||f|||_{L}^{*}\leq 1\end{subarray}}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x=x^{*}\\
|||x|||_{L}\leq 1\end{subarray}}|f(\Phi(x))|=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}f=f^{*}\in\mathcal{N}_{*}\\
f(\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{N}})=0,\;|||f|||_{L}^{*}\leq 1\end{subarray}}|||\Phi_{*}(f)|||_{L}^{*},

where for the last equality above, we used (
3.9
) under the assumption (
2
).

Finally, note that every self-adjoint functional
f\in\mathcal{N}_{*}
with
f(\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{N}})=0
can be expressed (up to scaling) as a difference of states; that is, there exist
\rho,\sigma\in S(\mathcal{N})
and
c\in\mathbb{R}
such that
f=c(\rho-\sigma)
.

Hence, one may identify

\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}f=f^{*}\in\mathcal{N}_{*}\\
f(\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{N}})=0,\;|||f|||_{L}^{*}\leq 1\end{subarray}}|||\Phi_{*}(f)|||_{L}^{*}=\sup_{\rho,\sigma\in S(\mathcal{N}),\;|||\rho-\sigma|||_{L}^{*}\leq 1}|||\Phi_{*}(\rho-\sigma)|||_{L}^{*}.

By the definition of the Wasserstein distance
W_{L}
in (
3.10
), the last expression is equal to

\sup_{\rho\neq\sigma\in S(\mathcal{N})}\frac{W_{L}\big(\Phi_{*}(\rho),\Phi_{*}(\sigma)\big)}{W_{L}(\rho,\sigma)}.

This completes the proof of (
3.12
).
∎

3.1.
Basic properties of transportation cost

Our principle goal is to quantify the cost of implementing certain quantum processes modeled as channels on von Neumann algebras. Mathematically, our goal is to find an appropriate cost functional
C
defined on channels

C:\mathfrak{C}(\mathcal{N})\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0},

such that it satisfies the following axioms:

(1)

(
No cost
)
C(id)=0
.

(2)

(
Subadditivity under concatenation
)
C(\Phi\circ\Psi)\leq C(\Phi)+C(\Psi)
.

(3)

(
Convexity
) For any probability distribution
\{p_{i}\}_{i\in I}
and any channels
\{\Phi_{i}\}_{i\in I}
, we have

(3.14)

C(\sum_{i\in I}p_{i}\Phi_{i})\leq\sum_{i\in I}p_{i}C(\Phi_{i}).

(4)

(
Universal upper bound
)
C(\Phi)
is upper bounded by a universal constant depending on the underlying dimension.

(5)

(
Tensor additivity
) For finitely many channels
\{\Phi_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq m}
, we have

(3.15)

C(\bigotimes_{i=1}^{m}\Phi_{i})=\sum_{i=1}^{m}C(\Phi_{i}).

We now verify that
\mathrm{Cost}^{cb}_{L}(\Phi)
satisfies the above axioms
(1)-(3)
. Under some additional assumptions,
(4),(5)
also hold. We begin by showing that
\mathrm{Cost}_{L}(\Phi),\mathrm{Cost}^{cb}_{L}(\Phi)
defined in Definition
3.1
satisfy the first three properties:

Proposition 3.4
.

The (complete) Lipschitz cost satisfies

(1)

(No cost)
\mathrm{Cost}_{L}(id)=\mathrm{Cost}^{cb}_{L}(id)=0
.

(2)

(Subadditivity under concatenation) For two channels
\Phi,\Psi:\mathcal{N}\to\mathcal{N}
, we have

(3.16)

\mathrm{Cost}_{L}(\Phi\circ\Psi)\leq\mathrm{Cost}_{L}(\Phi)+\mathrm{Cost}_{L}(\Psi),\quad\mathrm{Cost}^{cb}_{L}(\Phi\circ\Psi)\leq\mathrm{Cost}^{cb}_{L}(\Phi)+\mathrm{Cost}^{cb}_{L}(\Psi).

(3)

(Convexity) For any
p_{1},p_{2}\in[0,1]
with
p_{1}+p_{2}=1
, we have

(3.17)

\mathrm{Cost}_{L}(p_{1}\Phi+p_{2}\Psi)\leq p_{1}\mathrm{Cost}_{L}(\Phi)+p_{2}\mathrm{Cost}_{L}(\Psi).

Proof.

Property (
1
) follows from the definition. To show (
2
), for any
n\geq 1
, for any
x=x^{*}\in\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathcal{A}),|||x|||_{L^{(n)}}\leq 1
,

\displaystyle\|(\Phi\circ\Psi)^{(n)}(x)-x\|_{op}

\displaystyle=\|(\Phi\circ\Psi)^{(n)}(x)-\Phi^{(n)}(x)+\Phi^{(n)}(x)-x\|_{op}

\displaystyle\leq\|\Phi^{(n)}\circ(\Psi^{(n)}(x)-x)\|_{op}+\|\Phi^{(n)}(x)-x\|_{op}

\displaystyle\leq\|\Psi^{(n)}(x)-x\|_{op}+\|\Phi^{(n)}(x)-x\|_{op},

where we use the triangle inequality and
\|\Phi^{(n)}\|_{op}=1
for channels. Taking the supremum over
x
and
n\geq 1
, we conclude the proof of (
2
). Property (
3
) follows from the triangle inequality.
∎

To show the remaining two properties, we require additional assumptions. Suppose
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})

1

1

Note that in some cases,
\mathcal{S}
can be a set of unbounded operators(for example differential operators) we postpone this discussion for future work.
is a finite set of operators and we call
\mathcal{S}
the
resource set
. In this paper, we assume
\mathcal{S}
satisfies the following properties:

Assumption 3.5
.

The commutant of
\mathcal{S}
, denoted as
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
, is a von Neumann subalgebra. Moreover, there exists a normal conditional expectation
E_{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}\cap\mathcal{N}}:\mathcal{N}\to\mathcal{S}^{\prime}\cap\mathcal{N}

We recall the existence of such a conditional expectation is equivalent to
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}\cap\mathcal{N}
is invariant under the modular automorphism
group of some faithful normal state on
\mathcal{N}

[

. Note that the conditional expectation is not unique but it is fixed throughout this paper.
We consider the two families of matrix
Lipschitz seminorms (
\ell_{\infty}
and
\ell_{2}

(3.18)

|||x|||_{\mathcal{S}^{(n)}}:=\sup_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\left\|[\mathbb{I}_{n}\otimes s,x]\right\|_{op},\quad|||x|||_{\mathcal{S}^{(n)},2}:=\left\|\left(\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}[\mathbb{I}_{n}\otimes s,x]^{*}[\mathbb{I}_{n}\otimes s,x]\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\right\|_{op}

for

\mathrm{Cost}^{(cb)}_{\mathcal{S}}(\Phi),\quad\mathrm{Cost}^{(cb)}_{\mathcal{S},2}(\Phi),

Lemma 3.6

Suppose

First, it is easy to see that

\|\Phi(E_{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}\cap\mathcal{N}}(x))-E_{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}\cap\mathcal{N}}(x)\|_{op}=c>0

Therefore, in this paper we restrict attention to channels that fix

\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{fix}}:=\mathcal{S}^{\prime}\cap\mathcal{N},\quad E_{\mathrm{fix}}:=E_{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}\cap\mathcal{N}}.

Now we can present the

Proposition 3.7

(Universal upper bound)
.

For a channel
\Phi

(3.21)

\mathrm{Cost}_{\mathcal{S}}(\Phi)\leq\mathrm{Cost}_{\mathcal{S}}(E_{\mathrm{fix}})\,\|\Phi-id\|_{op},\quad\mathrm{Cost}_{\mathcal{S}}^{cb}(\Phi)\leq\mathrm{Cost}^{cb}_{\mathcal{S}}(E_{\mathrm{fix}})\,\|\Phi-id\|_{cb}.

Proof.

For any

\displaystyle\|\Phi^{(n)}(x)-x\|_{op}

\displaystyle=\|\Phi^{(n)}(x)-E_{\mathrm{fix}}^{(n)}(x)+E_{\mathrm{fix}}^{(n)}(x)-x\|_{op}

\displaystyle=\|\Phi^{(n)}(x)-\Phi^{(n)}\circ E_{\mathrm{fix}}^{(n)}(x)+E_{\mathrm{fix}}^{(n)}(x)-x\|_{op}

\displaystyle=\|(\Phi^{(n)}-id)\circ(E_{\mathrm{fix}}^{(n)}-id)(x)\|_{op}

\displaystyle\leq\|(\Phi^{(n)}-id)\|_{op}\|(E_{\mathrm{fix}}^{(n)}-id)(x)\|_{op}

Taking the supremum over
x
and
n\geq 1
, we conclude the proof.
∎

Definition 3.8
.

The constant
\mathrm{Cost}^{cb}_{\mathcal{S}}(E_{\mathrm{fix}})=:\kappa(\mathcal{S})
is called the expected length of
\mathcal{S}
.

To establish
tensor additivity
, we first specify how to define Lipschitz cost of the tensor product channel
\Phi_{1}\otimes\Phi_{2}
, where
\Phi_{i}:\mathcal{N}_{i}\to\mathcal{N}_{i},\ i=1,2
, and
\mathcal{N}_{i}\subseteq\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{i})
. Given
\mathcal{S}_{1}\subset\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{1}),\mathcal{S}_{2}\subset\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{2})
, define the resource set for the composite system by

(3.22)

\mathcal{S}_{1}\vee\mathcal{S}_{2}:=(\mathcal{S}_{1}\otimes\textbf{1}_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{2})})\cup(\textbf{1}_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{1})}\otimes\mathcal{S}_{2})=\{s_{1}\otimes\textbf{1}_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{2})},\textbf{1}_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{1})}\otimes s_{2}:s_{1}\in\mathcal{S}_{1},s_{2}\in\mathcal{S}_{2}\}.

Here
\textbf{1}_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})}
is the identity operator acting on
\mathcal{H}
. In general, for
d
-composite system, suppose we have resources for each single system
\mathcal{S}_{i}\subset\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{i}),1\leq i\leq d
, the resource set for the composite system is given by

(3.23)

\widetilde{\mathcal{S}_{j}}:=\{\widetilde{X_{j}}\big|X\in\mathcal{S}_{j}\},\quad\vee_{j=1}^{d}\mathcal{S}_{j}:=\bigcup_{j=1}^{d}\widetilde{\mathcal{S}_{j}}=\{\widetilde{X_{j}}\big|X\in\mathcal{S}_{j},1\leq j\leq d\},

where

\widetilde{X_{j}}=\textbf{1}_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{1})}\otimes\cdots\otimes\textbf{1}_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{j-1})}\otimes\underbrace{X}_{\text{j-th\ position}}\otimes\,\textbf{1}_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{j+1})}\otimes\cdots\otimes\textbf{1}_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{d})}.

As done in (
3.18
), we may define the matrix Lipschitz semi-norm on
\bigotimes_{i=1}^{d}\mathcal{N}_{i}
, which is the weak-
*
closure of the algebraic tensor product. For each channel
\Phi_{j}
, a natural induced channel
\widetilde{\Phi_{j}}
on
\bigotimes_{i=1}^{d}\mathcal{N}_{i}
is defined by

(3.24)

\widetilde{\Phi_{j}}=id_{\mathcal{N}_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes id_{\mathcal{N}_{j-1}}\otimes\underbrace{\Phi_{j}}_{j-th\ position}\otimes\,id_{\mathcal{N}_{j+1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes id_{\mathcal{N}_{d}}

We are able to confirm tensor additivity axiom for composite-system Lipschitz cost:

Proposition 3.9

(Tensor additivity)
.

Suppose
\Phi_{j}:\mathcal{N}_{j}\to\mathcal{N}_{j}
are quantum channels and
\mathcal{S}_{j}\subset\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{j})
are the resource sets for each single system for any
1\leq j\leq d
, then

(3.25)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cost}_{\vee_{j=1}^{d}\mathcal{S}_{j}}^{cb}(\Phi_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\Phi_{d})\leq\sum_{j=1}^{d}\mathrm{Cost}_{\widetilde{\mathcal{S}_{j}}}^{cb}(\widetilde{\Phi_{j}}).

Moreover, if

(3.26)

\mathrm{Cost}_{\widetilde{\mathcal{S}_{j}}}^{cb}(\widetilde{\Phi_{j}})=\mathrm{Cost}_{\mathcal{S}_{j}}^{cb}(\Phi_{j}),

we have tensor additivity.

Proof.

We only need to show the case when
d=2
because the general case follows from induction.

(i) Subadditivity.
For any
x\in\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathcal{N}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{N}_{2})
and
n\geq 1
, using the decomposition

(3.27)

\Phi_{1}\otimes\Phi_{2}-id=(\Phi_{1}\otimes id)(id\otimes\Phi_{2}-id)+(\Phi_{1}\otimes id-id)

and
\Phi_{1}\otimes id_{\mathcal{N}_{2}}
is unital completely positive thus
\|\Phi_{1}\otimes id_{\mathcal{N}_{2}}\|_{op}=1
, we have

\displaystyle\|(\Phi_{1}\otimes\Phi_{2})^{(n)}(x)-x\|_{op}\leq\|(id_{\mathcal{N}_{1}}\otimes\Phi_{2})^{(n)}(x)-x\|_{op}+\|(\Phi_{1}\otimes id_{\mathcal{N}_{2}})^{(n)}(x)-x\|_{op},

Therefore, note that
|||x|||_{\widetilde{\mathcal{S}_{i}}^{(n)}}\leq|||x|||_{(\mathcal{S}_{1}\vee\mathcal{S}_{2})^{(n)}},\ i=1,2
, taking the supremum over
n\geq 1
, we get

(3.28)

\mathrm{Cost}_{\mathcal{S}_{1}\vee\mathcal{S}_{2}}^{cb}(\Phi_{1}\otimes\Phi_{2})\leq\mathrm{Cost}_{\widetilde{\mathcal{S}_{1}}}^{cb}(\widetilde{\Phi_{1}})+\mathrm{Cost}_{\widetilde{\mathcal{S}_{2}}}^{cb}(\widetilde{\Phi_{2}}).

(ii) Superadditivity.
By definition and the assumption that
\mathrm{Cost}_{\widetilde{\mathcal{S}_{i}}}^{cb}(\widetilde{\Phi_{i}})=\mathrm{Cost}_{\mathcal{S}_{i}}^{cb}(\Phi_{i})
, for any
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, there exist
n_{1},n_{2}\in\mathbb{N}
, and
f_{i}=f_{i}^{*}\in\mathbb{M}_{n_{i}}(\mathcal{N}_{i})
,
i=1,2
, such that
|||f_{i}|||_{\mathcal{S}_{i}^{(n_{i})}}\leq 1
, and

(3.29)

(1-\varepsilon)\mathrm{Cost}_{\mathcal{S}_{i}}^{cb}(\Phi_{i})\leq\|\Phi^{(n_{i})}(f_{i})-f_{i}\|_{op}.

Since
\Phi^{(n_{i})}(f_{i})-f_{i}
is self-adjoint, there exist states
\rho_{i}\in S(\mathbb{M}_{n_{i}}(\mathcal{N}_{i}))
with
\rho_{i}(\mathbb{I}_{n_{i}}\otimes\textbf{1}_{\mathcal{N}_{i}})=1
,

(3.30)

\displaystyle\rho_{i}\left(\Phi^{(n_{i})}(f_{i})-f_{i}\right)=\|\Phi^{(n_{i})}(f_{i})-f_{i}\|_{op}.

Now we define

(3.31)

F=f_{1}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{n_{2}}\otimes\textbf{1}_{\mathcal{N}_{2}}+\mathbb{I}_{n_{1}}\otimes\textbf{1}_{\mathcal{N}_{1}}\otimes f_{2}\in\mathbb{M}_{n_{1}}(\mathcal{N}_{1})\otimes\mathbb{M}_{n_{2}}(\mathcal{N}_{2}),

we have

\displaystyle|||F|||_{(\mathcal{S}_{1}\vee\mathcal{S}_{2})^{(n_{1}n_{2})}}

\displaystyle=\max\{\sup_{s_{1}\in\mathcal{S}_{1}}\|[\mathbb{I}_{n_{1}}\otimes s_{1}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{n_{2}}\otimes\textbf{1}_{\mathcal{N}_{2}},F]\|_{op},\sup_{s_{2}\in\mathcal{S}_{2}}\|[\mathbb{I}_{n_{1}}\otimes\textbf{1}_{\mathcal{N}_{1}}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{n_{2}}\otimes s_{2},F]\|_{op}\}

\displaystyle=\max\{|||f_{1}|||_{\mathcal{S}_{1}^{(n_{1})}},|||f_{2}|||_{\mathcal{S}_{2}^{(n_{2})}}\}\leq 1.

Finally,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cost}_{\mathcal{S}_{1}\vee\mathcal{S}_{2}}^{cb}(\Phi_{1}\otimes\Phi_{2})\geq\|(\Phi^{(n_{1})}\otimes\Phi^{(n_{2})})(F)-F\|_{op}

\displaystyle\geq\bigg|(\rho_{1}\otimes\rho_{2})\bigg(\big(\Phi^{(n_{1})}(f_{1})-f_{1}\big)\otimes\mathbb{I}_{n_{2}}\otimes\textbf{1}_{\mathcal{N}_{2}}+\mathbb{I}_{n_{1}}\otimes\textbf{1}_{\mathcal{N}_{1}}\otimes\big(\Phi^{(n_{2})}(f_{2})-f_{2}\big)\bigg)\bigg|

\displaystyle=\|\Phi^{(n_{1})}(f_{1})-f_{1}\|_{op}+\|\Phi^{(n_{2})}(f_{2})-f_{2}\|_{op}\geq(1-\varepsilon)(\mathrm{Cost}_{S_{1}}^{cb}(\Phi_{1})+\mathrm{Cost}^{cb}_{S_{2}}(\Phi_{2})).

For the last equality, we used (
3.30
) and for the last inequality, we used (
3.29
). Since
\varepsilon
is arbitrary, we have
\mathrm{Cost}_{\mathcal{S}_{1}\vee\mathcal{S}_{2}}^{cb}(\Phi_{1}\otimes\Phi_{2})\geq\mathrm{Cost}_{S_{1}}^{cb}(\Phi_{1})+\mathrm{Cost}^{cb}_{S_{2}}(\Phi_{2})
which concludes the proof of superadditivity.
∎

3.2.
Basic properties of Lipschitz constant

The
Lipschitz constant
of a channel quantifies how strongly the
channel can
expand
or
contract
a prescribed Lipschitz seminorm. It should have the following physical meaning:

•

Contraction
,
\mathrm{Lip}(\Phi)<1
:

\Phi
dissipates fine structures encoded by the Lipschitz semi-norm; the dynamics
is mixing.

•

Isometry
,
\mathrm{Lip}(\Phi)=1
:

\Phi
acts noiselessly with respect to the Lipschitz semi-norm.

•

Amplification/scrambling
,
\mathrm{Lip}(\Phi)>1
:
\Phi
enlarges the spread of some Lipschitz observables, indicating information‑scrambling.

Mathematically, our goal is to find an appropriate functional
\mathrm{Lip}
defined on channels

\mathrm{Lip}:\mathfrak{C}(\mathcal{N})\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0},

such that it satisfies the following axioms:

(1)

(
Noiseless
)
\mathrm{Lip}(id)=1
.

(2)

(
Submultipilicative under concatenation
)
\mathrm{Lip}(\Phi\circ\Psi)\leq\mathrm{Lip}(\Phi)\mathrm{Lip}(\Psi)
.

(3)

(
Convexity
) For any probability distribution
\{p_{i}\}_{i\in I}
and any channels
\{\Phi_{i}\}_{i\in I}
, we have

(3.33)

\mathrm{Lip}(\sum_{i\in I}p_{i}\Phi_{i})\leq\sum_{i\in I}p_{i}\mathrm{Lip}(\Phi_{i}).

(4)

(
Universal upper bound
)
\mathrm{Lip}(\Phi)
is upper bounded by a universal constant depending on the underlying dimension.

(5)

(
Tensor maximality
) For finitely many channels
\{\Phi_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq m}
, we have

(3.34)

\mathrm{Lip}(\bigotimes_{i=1}^{m}\Phi_{i})=\max_{1\leq i\leq m}\mathrm{Lip}(\Phi_{i}).

First we show that for a matrix Lipschitz semi-norm, the (complete) Lipschitz constant defined in (
3.4
) and (
3.5
) in Definition
3.1
satisfies the first three axioms.

Proposition 3.11
.

For a quantum channel
\Phi
,
\mathrm{Lip}_{L}(\Phi)
and
\mathrm{Lip}^{cb}_{L}(\Phi)
satisfy:

(1)

(Noiseless)
\mathrm{Lip}(id)=1
.

(2)

(Submultipilicative under concatenation)
\mathrm{Lip}(\Phi\circ\Psi)\leq\mathrm{Lip}(\Phi)\mathrm{Lip}(\Psi)
.

(3)

(Convexity) For any probability distribution
\{p_{i}\}_{i\in I}
and any channels
\{\Phi_{i}\}_{i\in I}
, we have

(3.35)

\mathrm{Lip}(\sum_{i\in I}p_{i}\Phi_{i})\leq\sum_{i\in I}p_{i}\mathrm{Lip}(\Phi_{i}).

Proof.

Property
(1)
follows from the definition. For property
(2)
, for any
n\geq 1
and
x\in\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathcal{N})
, we have

(3.36)

|||\Phi^{(n)}\circ\Psi^{(n)}(x)|||_{L^{(n)}}\leq\mathrm{Lip}_{L^{(n)}}(\Phi^{(n)})|||\Psi^{(n)}(x)|||_{L^{(n)}}\leq\mathrm{Lip}_{L^{(n)}}(\Phi^{(n)})\mathrm{Lip}_{L^{(n)}}(\Psi^{(n)})|||x|||_{L^{(n)}}.

Taking the supremum over all
x
and
n\geq 1
, we conclude the proof. For property
(3)
, it follows from the definition and triangle inequality for semi-norm.
∎

For the
universal upper bound
, we need the same assumption as in Section
3.1
. Using the matrix Lipschitz semi-norm given in (
3.18
), we have

Proposition 3.12

(Universal upper bound)
.

Under the Assumption
3.5
, for any channel
\Phi
with
\Phi\circ E_{\mathrm{fix}}=E_{\mathrm{fix}}
where
E_{\mathrm{fix}}
is defined in (
3.20
), we have

(3.37)

\mathrm{Lip}_{\mathcal{S}}(\Phi)\leq 2\sup_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\|s\|_{op}\mathrm{Cost}_{\mathcal{S}}(E_{\mathrm{fix}}),\quad\mathrm{Lip}^{cb}_{\mathcal{S}}(\Phi)\leq 2\sup_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\|s\|_{op}\mathrm{Cost}^{cb}_{\mathcal{S}}(E_{\mathrm{fix}})

Proof.

For any
n\geq 1
and
x\in\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathcal{N})
, by definition of
\mathrm{Cost}_{\mathcal{S}}
defined in Definition
3.1
, we have

(3.38)

\|x-E_{\mathrm{fix}}^{(n)}(x)\|_{op}\leq\mathrm{Cost}_{\mathcal{S}^{(n)}}(E_{\mathrm{fix}})\,|||x|||_{\mathcal{S}^{(n)}}

By definition of
\mathrm{Lip}_{\mathcal{S}^{(n)}}
, we have

\displaystyle|||\Phi^{(n)}(x)|||_{\mathcal{S}^{(n)}}

\displaystyle=\sup_{s\in S}\Bigl\|\Bigl[\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{M}_{n}}\otimes s,\,\Phi^{(n)}(x)(x)\Bigr]\Bigr\|_{\rm op}

\displaystyle=\sup_{s\in S}\Bigl\|\Bigl[\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{M}_{n}}\otimes s,\,\Phi^{(n)}(x)-E_{\mathrm{fix}}^{(n)}(x)\Bigr]\Bigr\|_{\rm op}

\displaystyle\leq 2\,\sup_{s\in S}\|s\|_{\rm op}\,\|\Phi^{(n)}(x)-E_{\mathrm{fix}}^{(n)}(x)\|_{\rm op}

\displaystyle\leq 2\,\sup_{s\in S}\|s\|_{\rm op}\,\|x-E_{\mathrm{fix}}^{(n)}(x)\|_{op}

\displaystyle\leq 2\,\sup_{s\in S}\|s\|_{\rm op}\,\mathrm{Cost}_{\mathcal{S}^{(n)}}(E_{\mathrm{fix}})\,|||x|||_{\mathcal{S}^{(n)}}.

In the second equality, we use the assumption that
E_{\mathrm{fix}}^{(n)}(x)
commutes with
\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{M}_{n}}\otimes\mathcal{S}
, since
E_{\mathrm{fix}}:\mathcal{N}\to\mathcal{N}\cap\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
. The first inequality uses the definition of commutator and triangle inequality, and
\|s\|_{op}=\|\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{M}_{n}}\otimes s\|_{op}
The second inequality uses the contractivity of
\Phi^{(n)}
under the operator norm and
\Phi^{(n)}\circ E_{\mathrm{fix}}^{(n)}=E_{\mathrm{fix}}^{(n)}
. Taking the supremum over
x
and
n\geq 1
, we conclude the proof.
∎

Using the same definition of matrix Lipschitz semi-norm on tensor product of von Neumann algebras in Section
3.1
, we can prove the
tensor maximality
:

Proposition 3.13

(Tensor maximality)
.

Suppose
\Phi_{j}:\mathcal{N}_{j}\to\mathcal{N}_{j}
are quantum channels and
\mathcal{S}_{j}\subset\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{j})
are the resource sets for each single system for any
1\leq j\leq d
, then

(3.39)

\mathrm{Lip}^{cb}_{\vee_{j=1}^{d}\mathcal{S}_{j}}\left(\bigotimes_{j=1}^{d}\Phi_{j}\right)\leq\max_{1\leq i\leq d}\,\mathrm{Lip}_{\widetilde{\mathcal{S}_{j}}}^{cb}(\widetilde{\Phi_{j}}),

where
\bigvee_{j=1}^{d}\mathcal{S}_{j},\ \widetilde{\mathcal{S}_{j}}
are defined in (
3.23
) and
\widetilde{\Phi_{j}}
is defined in (
3.24
). Moreover, if
\mathrm{Lip}_{\widetilde{\mathcal{S}_{j}}}^{cb}(\widetilde{\Phi_{j}})=\mathrm{Lip}_{\mathcal{S}_{j}}^{cb}(\Phi_{j})
, we have equality in (
3.39
).

To prove the above result, we need a compatibility lemma:

Lemma 3.14
.

Suppose
\Phi_{j}:\mathcal{N}_{j}\to\mathcal{N}_{j}
are quantum channels and
\mathcal{S}_{j}\subset\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{j})
are the resource sets for each single system for
j=1,2
. Then for any
n\geq 1
and
s_{1}\in\mathcal{S}_{1},s_{2}\in\mathcal{S}_{2}
and
x\in\mathbb{M}_{n}\otimes\mathcal{N}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{N}_{2}
,

(3.40)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Phi_{1}}^{(n)}\left((\mathbb{I}_{n}\otimes\textbf{1}_{\mathcal{N}_{1}}\otimes s_{2})x\right)=(\mathbb{I}_{n}\otimes\textbf{1}_{\mathcal{N}_{1}}\otimes s_{2})\cdot\widetilde{\Phi_{1}}^{(n)}\left(x\right),

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Phi_{2}}^{(n)}\left((\mathbb{I}_{n}\otimes s_{1}\otimes\textbf{1}_{\mathcal{N}_{2}})x\right)=(\mathbb{I}_{n}\otimes s_{1}\otimes\textbf{1}_{\mathcal{N}_{2}})\cdot\widetilde{\Phi_{2}}^{(n)}\left(x\right).

Proof.

Via the well-known infinite-dimensional Stinespring’s dilation theorem for completely positive maps, see
[
32
, Theorem 4.1]
, there exist Hilbert spaces
\mathcal{K}_{i}
, isometries
V_{i}:\mathcal{H}_{i}\to\mathcal{K}_{i}
(due to unitality of
\Phi_{i}
), unital
*
-homomorphisms
\pi_{i}:\mathcal{N}_{i}\to\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{K}_{i})
, such that

(3.41)

\Phi_{i}(X_{i})=V_{i}^{*}\pi(X_{i})V_{i},\quad X_{i}\in\mathcal{N}_{i}.

Then we have

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Phi_{1}}^{(n)}\left((\mathbb{I}_{n}\otimes\textbf{1}_{\mathcal{N}_{1}}\otimes s_{2})x\right)

\displaystyle=(id_{\mathbb{M}_{n}}\otimes\Phi_{1}\otimes id_{\mathcal{N}_{2}})\left((\mathbb{I}_{n}\otimes\textbf{1}_{\mathcal{N}_{1}}\otimes s_{2})x\right)

\displaystyle=(\mathbb{I}_{n}\otimes V_{1}^{*}\otimes\textbf{1}_{\mathcal{N}_{2}})\cdot(id_{\mathbb{M}_{n}}\otimes\pi_{1}\otimes id_{\mathcal{N}_{2}})\left((\mathbb{I}_{n}\otimes\textbf{1}_{\mathcal{N}_{1}}\otimes s_{2})x\right)\cdot(\mathbb{I}_{n}\otimes V_{1}\otimes\textbf{1}_{\mathcal{N}_{2}})

\displaystyle=(\mathbb{I}_{n}\otimes V_{1}^{*}\otimes\textbf{1}_{\mathcal{N}_{2}})\cdot(\mathbb{I}_{n}\otimes\pi_{1}(\textbf{1}_{\mathcal{N}_{1}})\otimes s_{2})\cdot\widetilde{\pi_{1}}^{(n)}(x)\cdot(\mathbb{I}_{n}\otimes V_{1}\otimes\textbf{1}_{\mathcal{N}_{2}})

\displaystyle=(\mathbb{I}_{n}\otimes V_{1}^{*}\otimes s_{2})\cdot\widetilde{\pi_{1}}^{(n)}(x)\cdot(\mathbb{I}_{n}\otimes V_{1}\otimes\textbf{1}_{\mathcal{N}_{2}})

\displaystyle=(\mathbb{I}_{n}\otimes\textbf{1}_{\mathcal{N}_{1}}\otimes s_{2})\cdot(\mathbb{I}_{n}\otimes V_{1}^{*}\otimes\textbf{1}_{\mathcal{N}_{2}})\cdot\widetilde{\pi_{1}}^{(n)}(x)\cdot(\mathbb{I}_{n}\otimes V_{1}\otimes\textbf{1}_{\mathcal{N}_{2}})

\displaystyle=(\mathbb{I}_{n}\otimes\textbf{1}_{\mathcal{N}_{1}}\otimes s_{2})\cdot\widetilde{\Phi_{1}}^{(n)}(x).

For the first equality, we use the definition of
\widetilde{\Phi_{1}}^{(n)}
. For the second equality, we use (
3.41
). For the third equality, we use the fact that
\pi_{1}
is a
*
-homomorphism. For the fourth equality, we use the fact that
\pi_{1}
is unital. The remaining equalities follow from the definition. The same argument holds for
\Phi_{2}
and we conclude the proof.
∎

Now we are ready to show the tensor maximality:

Proof of Proposition
3.13
.

By induction, we only need to prove

.

3.23

\displaystyle=\max\left\{|||\widetilde{\Phi_{2}}^{(n)}\circ\widetilde{\Phi_{1}}^{(n)}(x)|||_{\widetilde{\mathcal{S}_{1}}^{(n)}},|||\widetilde{\Phi_{1}}^{(n)}\circ\widetilde{\Phi_{2}}^{(n)}(x)|||_{\widetilde{\mathcal{S}_{2}}^{(n)}}\right\},

where the last equality follows from the definition of
\widetilde{\Phi_{j}}^{(n)}
defined in (

For the third equality, we use Lemma
3.14
.
Using the same argument, we can show that

\widetilde{x_{1}}=x_{1}\otimes\textbf{1}_{\mathcal{N}_{2}}\in\mathbb{M}_{n_{1}}(\mathcal{N}_{1})\otimes\mathcal{N}_{2},\quad\widetilde{x_{2}}=\textbf{1}_{\mathcal{N}_{1}}\otimes x_{2}\in\mathcal{N}_{1}\otimes\mathbb{M}_{n_{2}}(\mathcal{N}_{2}).

\displaystyle|||x_{j}|||_{\mathcal{S}_{j}^{(n_{j})}}=|||\widetilde{x_{j}}|||_{\widetilde{\mathcal{S}_{j}}^{(n_{j})}}=|||\widetilde{x_{j}}|||_{(\mathcal{S}_{1}\vee\mathcal{S}_{2})^{(n_{j})}}.

). Taking the supremum over all

\displaystyle\mathrm{Lip}^{cb}_{\mathcal{S}_{1}\vee\mathcal{S}_{2}}(\Phi_{1}\otimes\Phi_{2})\geq\max_{j=1,2}\mathrm{Lip}_{\mathcal{S}_{j}}^{cb}(\Phi_{j})

We conclude the proof by noting that

.
∎

3.3.
Continuity and mixing property

In the remaining section, we discuss more useful properties of transportation cost and Lipschitz constant of channels. We take the special commutator semi-norm defined in (
3.18
): for any
n\geq 1
,
|||x|||_{\mathcal{S}^{(n)}}:=\sup_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\left\|[\mathbb{I}_{n}\otimes s,x]\right\|_{op}
with Assumption
3.5
.

4.
Applications of transportation cost

4.1.
Expected group word length

As a first application, we illustrate how our notion of cost, derived from the chosen set of generators, relates directly to the classical concept of word length in a finite group. Let
G
be a finite group equipped with a finite generating set
S
. The
word length
of an element
g\in G
with respect to
S
is defined as the minimum number of generators (or their inverses) required to express
g
. More precisely:

\ell_{S}(g)=\min\{n\in\mathbb{N}:g=s_{1}s_{2}\cdots s_{n},\;s_{i}\in S\cup S^{-1}\}.

Without loss of generality, we assume the generating set
S\subseteq G
is symmetric (i.e.
S=S^{-1}
). This function
\ell_{S}:G\to\mathbb{N}
has several well-known properties:

•

\ell_{S}(e)=0
for the identity element
e\in G
(by convention).

•

\ell_{S}(g^{-1})=\ell_{S}(g)
for all
g\in G
.

•

\ell_{S}(gh)\leq\ell_{S}(g)+\ell_{S}(h)
for all
g,h\in G
(triangle inequality).

Note that the function
\ell_{S}
depends both on the choice of generating set
S
and the group structure of
G
.

We now reformulate these concepts in the von Neumann algebra setting. Consider the normalized Haar measure
\mu
on the finite group
G
, i.e. the uniform distribution, so that
\mu(\{g\})=|G|^{-1}
for every
g\in G
. We define

(4.1)

\mathcal{N}=L_{\infty}(G,\mu):=\{f:G\to\mathbb{C}\mid f\text{ bounded}\},\quad\mathcal{H}=L_{2}(G,\mu),

with the inner product

\langle f_{1},f_{2}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=\sum_{g\in G}f_{1}(g)\overline{f_{2}(g)}\mu(g).

In this setting,
\mathcal{N}
is a commutative von Neumann algebra. We obtain a representation

\pi:\mathcal{N}\to\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}),\quad f\mapsto\pi(f),

where
(\pi(f)\widetilde{f})(g)=f(g)\widetilde{f}(g)
for every
\widetilde{f}\in\mathcal{H}
and
g\in G
. Thus
\pi(f)
acts as a pointwise multiplication operator on
L_{2}(G,\mu)
.

To measure cost in this operator framework, we use the left-regular representation:

\lambda:G\to\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}),\quad(\lambda_{g}\widetilde{f})(h)=\widetilde{f}(g^{-1}h),\quad\widetilde{f}\in\mathcal{H},\ h\in G.

Choosing the set of resources as

\mathcal{S}:=\{\lambda_{s}:s\in S\}\subseteq\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}),

we introduce a Lipschitz semi-norm on
\mathcal{N}
by

(4.2)

|||f|||_{\mathcal{S}}:=\sup_{s\in S}\|[\lambda_{s},\pi(f)]\|_{op}.

This setting allows us to translate combinatorial aspects of the group (e.g. the word length and related metrics) into operator-theoretic language. In the next lemma, we will see how to calculate these operator norms and the corresponding Lipschitz semi-norm in the case of finite groups.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
G
be a finite group with the normalized counting measure
\mu
, and let
\mathcal{H}=L_{2}(G,\mu)
. Suppose
f\in\mathcal{N}=L_{\infty}(G,\mu)
, where
(\pi,\mathcal{H})
is the standard representation defined by
\pi(f)(\widetilde{f})=f\widetilde{f}
for
\widetilde{f}\in\mathcal{H}
. Then:

(1)

We have

\|\pi(f)\|_{op}=\|f\|_{\infty}=\sup_{g\in G}|f(g)|.

(2)

For any symmetric generating set
S\subseteq G
(i.e.
S=S^{-1}
), we have

|||f|||_{\mathcal{S}}=\sup_{s\in S}\sup_{g\in G}|f(sg)-f(g)|.

Proof.

(1) Since
\pi(f)
acts by pointwise multiplication, for any
\widetilde{f}\in\mathcal{H}
with
\|\widetilde{f}\|_{\mathcal{H}}=1
we have

\|\pi(f)(\widetilde{f})\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}=\int_{G}|f(g)\widetilde{f}(g)|^{2}\,d\mu(g)\leq\|f\|_{\infty}^{2}\int_{G}|\widetilde{f}(g)|^{2}\,d\mu(g)=\|f\|_{\infty}^{2}.

This shows
\|\pi(f)\|_{op}\leq\|f\|_{\infty}
.

To prove the reverse inequality, let
g_{0}\in G
be such that
|f(g_{0})|=\|f\|_{\infty}
. Consider the function
\widetilde{f}:=\sqrt{|G|}\cdot 1_{\{g_{0}\}}
, where
1_{\{g_{0}\}}
is the indicator function of the singleton set
\{g_{0}\}
. Since
\mu(\{g_{0}\})=1/|G|
, we have

\|\widetilde{f}\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}=\int_{G}|\widetilde{f}(g)|^{2}d\mu(g)=|\widetilde{f}(g_{0})|^{2}\mu(\{g_{0}\})=|G|\cdot 1\cdot\frac{1}{|G|}=1.

Now,

\|\pi(f)(\widetilde{f})\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}=\int_{G}|f(g)\widetilde{f}(g)|^{2}d\mu(g)=|f(g_{0})|^{2}|\widetilde{f}(g_{0})|^{2}\mu(\{g_{0}\})=\|f\|_{\infty}^{2}.

Hence
\|\pi(f)\|_{op}\geq\|f\|_{\infty}
. Combining both inequalities gives
\|\pi(f)\|_{op}=\|f\|_{\infty}
.

(2) Consider the commutator
[\lambda_{s},\pi(f)]
for
s\in S
. Here
\lambda_{s}
is the left-regular representation acting by
(\lambda_{s}\widetilde{f})(g)=\widetilde{f}(s^{-1}g)
. We compute:

\displaystyle[\lambda_{s},\pi(f)](\widetilde{f})(g)

\displaystyle=\lambda_{s}(\pi(f)\widetilde{f})(g)-\pi(f)(\lambda_{s}\widetilde{f})(g)

\displaystyle=(f\widetilde{f})(s^{-1}g)-(f\cdot\lambda_{s}\widetilde{f})(g)

\displaystyle=f(s^{-1}g)\widetilde{f}(s^{-1}g)-f(g)\widetilde{f}(s^{-1}g).

By the left-invariance of the counting measure (and substituting
h=s^{-1}g
), we obtain

\|[\lambda_{s},\pi(f)]\|_{op}=\sup_{\|\widetilde{f}\|_{\mathcal{H}}=1}\|[\lambda_{s},\pi(f)]\widetilde{f}\|_{\mathcal{H}}.

Note that for any
\|\widetilde{f}\|_{\mathcal{H}}=1
, we have

\displaystyle\|[\lambda_{s},\pi(f)]\widetilde{f}\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{G}|f(s^{-1}g)\widetilde{f}(s^{-1}g)-f(g)\widetilde{f}(s^{-1}g)|^{2}\,d\mu(g)

\displaystyle=\int_{G}|f(h)-f(sh)|^{2}|\widetilde{f}(h)|^{2}\,d\mu(h),

where we used the substitution
h=s^{-1}g
. Since
\|\widetilde{f}\|_{\mathcal{H}}=1
, choosing
\widetilde{f}
to be supported on the point
h_{0}
where
|f(h_{0})-f(sh_{0})|
attains its maximum shows

\|[\lambda_{s},\pi(f)]\|_{op}\geq\sup_{h\in G}|f(h)-f(sh)|.

On the other hand, for any
\widetilde{f}
,

\displaystyle\|[\lambda_{s},\pi(f)]\widetilde{f}\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{h\in G}|f(h)-f(sh)|^{2}\int_{G}|\widetilde{f}(h)|^{2}d\mu(h)=\sup_{h\in G}|f(h)-f(sh)|^{2},

thus

\|[\lambda_{s},\pi(f)]\|_{op}=\sup_{h\in G}|f(h)-f(sh)|.

Since
|||f|||_{\mathcal{S}}
is defined (in this context) as

|||f|||_{\mathcal{S}}=\sup_{s\in S}\|[\lambda_{s},\pi(f)]\|_{op},

we conclude that

|||f|||_{\mathcal{S}}=\sup_{s\in S}\sup_{g\in G}|f(sg)-f(g)|.

This completes the proof.
∎

From the definition (
4.2
) and Lemma
4.1
, it is immediate that
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
consists precisely of the constant functions on
G
. Indeed, if
f\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
, then for every
s\in S
, we have
[\lambda_{s},\pi(f)]=0
, which implies
f(sg)=f(g)
for all
g\in G
. Since
S
generates
G
, it follows that
f
is constant. Consequently, the conditional expectation
E_{\mathrm{fix}}:\mathcal{N}\to\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
is given by

(4.3)

E_{\mathrm{fix}}(f)=\left(\int_{G}f(g)\,d\mu(g)\right)1_{G}.

With this in hand, we can now characterize the expected group word length via the Lipschitz cost measure.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
\mathcal{N}
,
\mathcal{H}
, and
\mathcal{S}
be as in the finite group setup (
4.1
), with the Lipschitz semi-norm (
4.2
). Then we have

\mathrm{Cost}_{\mathcal{S}}(E_{\mathrm{fix}})=\int_{G}\ell_{S}(g)\,d\mu(g).

Proof.

Lower bound:
Denote
\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{s.a.}}
as the set of real-valued functions. We need to show that

\sup_{f\in\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{s.a.}},|||f|||_{\mathcal{S}}\leq 1}\|\pi(E_{\mathrm{fix}}(f)-f)\|_{op}\geq\int_{G}\ell_{S}(g)\,d\mu(g).

Consider the function

f_{\mathcal{S}}:=\ell_{S}-\left(\int_{G}\ell_{S}(g)\,d\mu(g)\right)1_{G}.

Note that
f_{\mathcal{S}}
is real-valued and hence self-adjoint. By construction,
E_{\mathrm{fix}}(f_{\mathcal{S}})=0
. We now estimate its Lipschitz semi-norm:

|||f_{\mathcal{S}}|||_{\mathcal{S}}=\sup_{s\in S}\sup_{g\in G}|f_{\mathcal{S}}(sg)-f_{\mathcal{S}}(g)|=\sup_{s\in S}\sup_{g\in G}|\ell_{S}(sg)-\ell_{S}(g)|.

Since adding or removing a single generator changes the word length by at most
1
, we have

|\ell_{S}(sg)-\ell_{S}(g)|\leq 1.

Thus
|||f_{\mathcal{S}}|||_{\mathcal{S}}\leq 1
.

Using Lemma
4.1
, we then have

\|\pi(E_{\mathrm{fix}}(f_{\mathcal{S}})-f_{\mathcal{S}})\|_{op}=\|\pi(-f_{\mathcal{S}})\|_{op}=\|f_{\mathcal{S}}\|_{\infty}=\sup_{g\in G}|f_{\mathcal{S}}(g)|.

Since
f_{\mathcal{S}}(e)=\ell_{S}(e)-\int_{G}\ell_{S}(g)\,d\mu(g)=0-\int_{G}\ell_{S}(g)\,d\mu(g)
, we see that

\|f_{\mathcal{S}}\|_{\infty}\geq|f_{\mathcal{S}}(e)|=\int_{G}\ell_{S}(g)\,d\mu(g).

This establishes the desired lower bound.

Upper bound:
Now we show

\sup_{f\in\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{s.a.}},|||f|||_{\mathcal{S}}\leq 1}\|\pi(E_{\mathrm{fix}}(f)-f)\|_{op}\leq\int_{G}\ell_{S}(g)\,d\mu(g).

Take any
f\in\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{s.a.}}
with
|||f|||_{\mathcal{S}}\leq 1
. Using the definition of
E_{\mathrm{fix}}
and the invariance of
\mu
, we have:

\displaystyle\|\pi(E_{\mathrm{fix}}(f)-f)\|_{op}

\displaystyle=\sup_{g\in G}\left|f(g)-\int_{G}f(h)\,d\mu(h)\right|

\displaystyle=\sup_{g\in G}\left|f(g)-\int_{G}f(hg)\,d\mu(h)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{g\in G}\int_{G}|f(g)-f(hg)|\,d\mu(h).

For a fixed
h\in G
, write
h=s_{1}s_{2}\cdots s_{\ell_{S}(h)}
with
s_{i}\in S
. A telescoping sum argument yields

|f(g)-f(hg)|\leq\sum_{k=1}^{\ell_{S}(h)}|f(u_{k})-f(s_{k}u_{k})|,

where
u_{k}:=s_{k+1}\cdots s_{\ell_{S}(h)}g
. Since
|||f|||_{\mathcal{S}}\leq 1
, we have

|f(u_{k})-f(s_{k}u_{k})|\leq\sup_{u\in G}|f(u)-f(s_{k}u)|\leq|||f|||_{\mathcal{S}}\leq 1.

Thus

|f(g)-f(hg)|\leq\ell_{S}(h).

Substituting this back, we get

\|\pi(E_{\mathrm{fix}}(f)-f)\|_{op}\leq\int_{G}\ell_{S}(h)\,d\mu(h).

Since this holds for all
f
with
|||f|||_{\mathcal{S}}\leq 1
, we conclude

\sup_{f\in\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{s.a.}},|||f|||_{\mathcal{S}}\leq 1}\|E_{\mathrm{fix}}(f)-f\|_{op}\leq\int_{G}\ell_{S}(g)\,d\mu(g).

Combining the lower and upper bounds completes the proof.
∎

4.2.
Carnot–Carathéodory distance

The geometric approach to characterize the cost of a unitary operator by its distance to the identity operator is due to Nielsen
[

,
30
,
31
]
. The key idea in defining the geometric quantum cost measure is to regard the infinitesmial generators of the unitary operators as resources, or in other words, elements in the Lie algebra. It has a connection to sub-Riemannian geometry, as we review below.

Suppose
G
is a locally compact Lie group with Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
and Haar measure
\mu
. Let
\mathfrak{h}\subseteq\mathfrak{g}
be a linear subspace of the Lie algebra. Suppose
S=\{X_{1},\dots,X_{m}\}
is a linearly independent set spanning
\mathfrak{h}
, i.e.
\mathrm{span}\{X_{1},\dots,X_{m}\}=\mathfrak{h}
. We endow
\mathfrak{h}
with the inner product making
\{X_{1},\dots,X_{m}\}
an orthonormal set; for any
h\in\mathfrak{h}
written as
h=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\alpha_{j}X_{j}
, we define

\|h\|_{S,\mathfrak{h}}:=\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{m}|\alpha_{j}|^{2}}.

This choice of norm
\|\cdot\|_{S,\mathfrak{h}}
on
\mathfrak{h}
induces a so-called
Carnot-Carathéodory (CC) distance
on the group
G
. To define this distance, we identify each tangent space
T_{g}G
with
\mathfrak{g}
via left translation: for
g\in G
, let
L_{g}:G\to G
be the left-translation map
L_{g}(h)=gh
. The differential
d(L_{g})_{h}
of the left-translation map
L_{g}
at a point
h\in G
is defined as the tangent map from
T_{h}G
to
T_{gh}G
. More precisely, if
\gamma:(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\to G
is a smooth curve with
\gamma(0)=h
and
\gamma^{\prime}(0)=X\in T_{h}G
, then

d(L_{g})_{h}(X):=\frac{d}{dt}\bigg|_{t=0}L_{g}(\gamma(t))=\frac{d}{dt}\bigg|_{t=0}(g\gamma(t))\in T_{gh}G.

In particular, when
h=e
(the identity of
G
), we have a natural identification
T_{e}G\cong\mathfrak{g}
and we denote
d(L_{g})_{e}
as
d(L_{g})
. Consider a one-parameter subgroup
\gamma(t)=\exp(tX)
with
\gamma(0)=e
and
\gamma^{\prime}(0)=X\in\mathfrak{g}
. Then

d(L_{g})(X)=\frac{d}{dt}\bigg|_{t=0}(g\exp(tX))\in T_{g}G.

A piecewise smooth curve
\gamma:[0,1]\to G
is said to be
horizontal w.r.t.
\mathfrak{h}\subseteq\mathfrak{g}
, if there exists a measurable function
X:[0,1]\to\mathfrak{h}
such that for almost all
t\in[0,1]
,

\gamma^{\prime}(t)=d(L_{\gamma(t)})(X(t)).

In other words, at each point
\gamma(t)
, the velocity
\gamma^{\prime}(t)
comes from the subspace
\mathfrak{h}
of
\mathfrak{g}
.

Given two points
g,h\in G
, we define the following distance as

(4.4)

d_{\mathfrak{h}}(g,h):=\inf\left\{\int_{0}^{1}\|X(t)\|_{S,\mathfrak{h}}\,dt:\gamma^{\prime}(t)=d(L_{\gamma(t)})(X(t)),\ \gamma(0)=g,\ \gamma(1)=h\right\}.

This definition endows
G
with a length metric that measures the shortest “cost” of traveling from
g
to
h
by curves whose directions are restricted to the subspace
\mathfrak{h}\subseteq\mathfrak{g}
. Throughout this paper, we will assume
\mathfrak{h}
is a Hörmander system. In this case,
d_{\mathfrak{h}}
defines a non-degenerate metric inducing a sub-Riemannian geometry on
G
. The resulting metric is known as the
Carnot-Carathéodory distance
, satisfying several well-known properties
[
22
]
:

•

d_{\mathfrak{h}}(g,g)=0
for any
g\in G
.

•

d_{\mathfrak{h}}(g,h)=d_{\mathfrak{h}}(h,g)
for all
g,h\in G
.

•

d_{\mathfrak{h}}(g_{1},g_{2})\leq d_{\mathfrak{h}}(g_{1},g_{3})+d_{\mathfrak{h}}(g_{2},g_{3}),\ \forall g_{1},g_{2},g_{3}\in G
(triangle inequality).

•

d_{\mathfrak{h}}(hg_{1},hg_{2})=d_{\mathfrak{h}}(g_{1},g_{2}),\ \forall g_{1},g_{2},h\in G
(left-invariance).

Example 4.3
.

(Geometric cost
[
29
,
30
,
31
]
)
Suppose
G=SU(2^{n})
and
\mathfrak{h}
is taken to be the full Lie algebra
\mathfrak{su}(2^{n})
. The orthonormal set is given by

(4.5)

\{\sigma,p_{\sigma^{\prime}}\sigma^{\prime}:\sigma\in\Sigma,\sigma^{\prime}\in\Sigma^{\prime}\},

where
p_{\sigma^{\prime}}>0
is a weight constant,
\Sigma
is the set of Pauli operators with weight less than or equal to
2
,
\Sigma^{\prime}
is the set of Pauli operators with weight greater than
2
. For any
U\in SU(2^{n})
, the geometric cost is defined to be
d_{\mathfrak{h}}(g,e)
. See
[
3
]
for a discussion of the relation between Lipschitz cost and geometric cost in this specific setting.

Now we show that by choosing the Lipschitz norm accordingly, we can represent Carnot-Carathéodory distance
d_{\mathfrak{h}}(g,e)
as the Lipschitz cost of a channel, similar to Section
4.1
.

To illustrate the idea, we discuss the commutative case first. Suppose
\mathcal{N}=L_{\infty}(G,\mu)
. For any
g\in G
define
R_{g}:\mathcal{N}\to\mathcal{N}
by

(4.6)

R_{g}(f)(h)=f(hg),\ f\in\mathcal{N},h\in G.

The Lipschitz semi-norm on
\mathcal{N}
is given by

(4.7)

\|f\|_{\mathrm{Lip},\mathfrak{h}}:=\sup_{g\neq h}\frac{|f(g)-f(h)|}{d_{\mathfrak{h}}(g,h)},

and we denote
\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{Lip}}:=\{f\in\mathcal{N}:\|f\|_{\mathrm{Lip},\mathfrak{h}}<\infty\}
. Then
d_{\mathfrak{h}}(g,e)
can be represented as the Lipchitz cost of
R_{g}
:

Proposition 4.4
.

The Lipschitz cost of
R_{g}
with respect to the Lipschitz semi-norm (
4.7
) is equal to
d_{\mathfrak{h}}(g,e)
:

(4.8)

\mathrm{Cost}_{\mathfrak{h}}(R_{g}):=\sup_{f\in\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{Lip}},\|f\|_{\mathrm{Lip},\mathfrak{h}}\leq 1}\|R_{g}(f)-f\|_{\infty}=d_{\mathfrak{h}}(g,e).

Proof.

For the upper bound, for any
f\in\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{Lip}}
, we have

\displaystyle\|R_{g}(f)-f\|_{\infty}=\sup_{h}|f(hg)-f(h)|\leq\|f\|_{\mathrm{Lip},\mathfrak{h}}\sup_{h}d_{\mathfrak{h}}(hg,h)=\|f\|_{\mathrm{Lip},\mathfrak{h}}d_{\mathfrak{h}}(g,e),

where the last equality used the left-invariance property of
d_{\mathfrak{h}}
. For the lower bound, we choose a function
f_{0}(g):=d_{\mathfrak{h}}(g,e)
which has Lipschitz semi-norm less than 1 and we have

\displaystyle\|(R_{g}-id)(f_{0})\|_{\infty}\geq|R_{g}(f_{0})(e)-f_{0}(e)|=d_{\mathfrak{h}}(g,e)\geq\|f_{0}\|_{\mathrm{Lip},\mathfrak{h}}d_{\mathfrak{h}}(g,e).

∎

To bring the problem to a noncommutative setting, suppose
\pi:G\to U(\mathcal{K})
is a strongly continuous unitary representation on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{K}
. By differentiation, this induces a representation of the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
on the same Hilbert space:

d\pi:\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{u}(\mathcal{K}),

where
\mathfrak{u}(\mathcal{K})
denotes the skew-adjoint operators on
\mathcal{K}
. This Lie algebra representation is consistent with the group representation in the sense that for every
X\in\mathfrak{g}
,

\pi(\exp(X))=\exp(d\pi(X)).

For any representation

\displaystyle\pi:G\to U(\mathcal{K}),

we can define a Lipschitz semi-norm on
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{K})
corresponding to the representation and
\mathfrak{h}\subseteq\mathfrak{g}
.

Definition 4.5
.

Let
\mathfrak{h}\subseteq\mathfrak{g}
be a subspace of the Lie-algebra and
X_{1},\cdots,X_{m}
be an orthonormal set such that
span\{X_{1},\cdots,X_{m}\}=\mathfrak{h}
. The Lipschitz semi-norm corresponding to the representation
\pi:G\to U(\mathcal{K})
is given by

(4.9)

\|x\|_{d\pi(\mathfrak{h})}:=\|\big(\sum_{j=1}^{m}[d\pi(X_{j}),x]^{*}[d\pi(X_{j}),x]\big)^{1/2}\|_{op},

for any
x\in\text{Dom}_{\mathfrak{h}}:=\{y\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{K}):[d\pi(X_{j}),y]\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{K}),\ \forall 1\leq j\leq m\}
.

For any channel
\Phi:\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{K})\to\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{K})
, we can define the Lipschitz cost

(4.10)

\mathrm{Cost}{\mathfrak{h},\pi}(\Phi):=\sup_{x\in\text{Dom}_{\mathfrak{h}},\|x\|_{d\pi(\mathfrak{h})}\leq 1}\|\Phi(x)-x\|_{op}.

A simple observation is the Lipschitz cost
\mathrm{Cost}_{\mathfrak{h},\pi}(\text{ad}_{\pi(g)})
provides a lower bound for
d_{\mathfrak{h}}(g,e)
for any representation
\pi
, where
\text{ad}_{\pi(g)}:\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{K})\to\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{K})
is defined as
\text{ad}_{\pi(g)}(x)=\pi(g)x\pi(g)^{*}
.

Theorem 4.6
.

For any
g\in G
, we have

(4.11)

\mathrm{Cost}_{\mathfrak{h},\pi}(\text{ad}_{\pi(g)})\leq d_{\mathfrak{h}}(g,e).

Proof.

Let
\gamma(t)
be a piecewise differentiable horizontal path such that

\gamma(0)=e,\gamma(1)=g,\quad\gamma^{\prime}(t)=d(L_{\gamma(t)})(X(t))

for some
X:[0,1]\to\mathfrak{h}
and it achieves the infimum in
d_{\mathfrak{h}}(g,e)
. Consider for any
x\in\text{Dom}_{\mathfrak{h}}
,

(4.12)

x(t):=\pi(\gamma(t))^{*}x\pi(\gamma(t))\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{K}).

Using the formula connecting
\pi
and
d\pi
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\pi(\gamma(t))=\pi(\gamma(t))\,d\pi(X(t)).

Hence the derivative of
x(t)
is given by

(4.13)

\displaystyle x^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=\big(\frac{d}{dt}\pi(\gamma(t))\big)x\pi(\gamma(t))+\pi(\gamma(t))^{*}x\big(\frac{d}{dt}\pi(\gamma(t))^{*}\big)

\displaystyle=\pi(\gamma(t))d\pi(X(t))\,x\,\pi(\gamma(t))^{*}+\pi(\gamma(t))\,x\,d\pi(X(t))^{*}\,\pi(\gamma(t))^{*}

\displaystyle=\pi(\gamma(t))\,[d\pi(X(t)),x]\,\pi(\gamma(t))^{*},

where we used the fact that
d\pi(X(t))
is a skew-adjoint operator. Then we have

(4.14)

\displaystyle\|x-\text{ad}_{\pi(g)}(x)\|_{op}=\|x(1)-x(0)\|_{op}=\|\int_{0}^{1}x^{\prime}(t)dt\|_{op}\leq\int_{0}^{1}\|[d\pi(X(t)),x]\|_{op}dt.

Since
X(t)\in\mathfrak{h}\subseteq\mathfrak{g}
, we can represent
X(t)
as linear combination of
X_{1},\cdots,X_{m}
, i.e.,

(4.15)

X(t)=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\alpha_{j}(t)X_{j}\,.

Then by Hölder’s inequality, we have

(4.16)

\displaystyle\|[d\pi(X(t)),x]\|_{op}

\displaystyle=\|\sum_{j=1}^{m}\alpha_{j}(t)[d\pi(X_{j}),x]\|_{op}

\displaystyle\leq(\sum_{j=1}^{m}|\alpha_{j}(t)|^{2})^{1/2}\|(\sum_{j=1}^{m}|[d\pi(X_{j}),x]|^{2})^{1/2}\|_{op}

\displaystyle=\|X(t)\|_{S,\mathfrak{h}}\|x\|_{d\pi(\mathfrak{h})}.

By (
4.14
), we have

(4.17)

\|x-\text{ad}_{\pi(g)}(x)\|_{op}\leq\int_{0}^{1}\|X(t)\|_{S,\mathfrak{h}}dt\|x\|_{d\pi(\mathfrak{h})}=d_{\mathfrak{h}}(g,e)\|x\|_{d\pi(\mathfrak{h})},

which concludes the proof.
∎

Remark 4.7
.

Readers who are familiar with sub-Riemannian geometry can show that the left regular representation saturates the inequality. The argument follows the same argument as the finite group, using the Lipschitz function given by the distance function to the unit. A technical difficulty is to extend the notion of
\ell_{2}
-type Lipschitz semi-norms to non-smooth functions. It can be done using the smoothing property of Laplace-Beltrami operator. For compact groups, we can simply restrict the discussion to functions given by matrix coefficients from finite dimensional representations.

5.
Applications of Lipschitz constant

In this section, we discuss some applications to quantum information theory. We assume the von Neumann algebra is the full matrix algebra
\mathcal{N}=\mathbb{M}_{d}
. In this case,
S(\mathcal{N})
is the set of density operators. Since we have a trace in this setting, we adopt the convention of a quantum channel being trace preserving and completely positive in this section. We adopt ketbra notation:
\ket{\psi}
stands for a column vector and
\bra{\psi}
for a row vector.

For a quantum channel
\Phi
, we denote the Lipschitz constant of
\Phi
for some Lipschitz semi-norm
|||\cdot|||_{L}
as
\mathrm{Lip}_{L}(\Phi^{*})=\sup_{X=X^{\dagger}}\frac{|||\Phi^{*}(X)|||_{L}}{|||X|||_{L}}
.

5.1.
Entropy contraction from Lipschitz contraction

The first application is to use Lipschitz constant to bound the entropy contraction coefficient for quantum channels. Suppose
\Phi
is a trace preserving completely positive map and
\sigma\in S(\mathcal{N})
is a fixed density operator, define

(5.1)

\eta^{\mathbb{D}}(\Phi,\sigma):=\sup_{\rho\in S(\mathcal{N})}\frac{\mathbb{D}(\Phi(\rho)\|\sigma)}{\mathbb{D}(\rho\|\sigma)}.

Here
\mathbb{D}(\cdot\|\cdot)
is a divergence measure. We further assume that
\sigma
is faithful and it is the unique fixed point state
\Phi(\sigma)=\sigma
. Our goal is to upper bound
\eta^{\mathbb{D}}(\Phi,\sigma)
via the Lipschitz constant of
\Phi
and get a sufficient condition for
\eta^{\mathbb{D}}(\Phi,\sigma)<1
.

In this paper, we only consider the case when the divergence measure is the relative entropy and we leave the other divergence measures for future research. First recall the definition

(5.2)

D(\rho\|\sigma)=\tr\bigl[\rho(\log\rho-\log\sigma)\bigr],\quad\mathrm{supp}(\rho)\subseteq\mathrm{supp}(\sigma).

We denote
\eta^{D}(\Phi,\sigma)
as the contraction coefficient defined in (
5.1
) for Umegaki relative entropy. The elementary derivative formula is given by

Lemma 5.1

(Derivative of relative entropy)
.

Let
\sigma
be a full–rank density operator
and
\rho(t)
is a family of density operators which are smooth with respect to
t\in\mathbb{R}
. Then we have

(5.3)

\left.\frac{d}{dt}\,D\bigl(\rho_{t}\|\sigma\bigr)\right|_{t=0}=\left\langle\rho^{\prime}(0),\log\rho(0)-\log\sigma\right\rangle_{\mathrm{HS}}.

Proof.

A well-known functional calculus for logarithmic function produces

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\log\rho(t)=\int_{0}^{\infty}(\rho(t)+\lambda\textbf{1})^{-1}\rho^{\prime}(t)(\rho(t)+\lambda\textbf{1})^{-1}dt.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\tr( \rho(0) \frac{d}{dt} \log\rho(t)\bigg|_{t = 0})=\tr(\int_0^\infty\rho(0)(\rho(0) + \lambda\textbf{1})^{-1} \rho'(0) (\rho(0) + \lambda\textbf{1})^{-1} d\lambda)=\tr(\rho'(0))=0.

As a result, by chain rule, we have

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}D\bigl(\rho_{t}\|\sigma\bigr)\bigg|_{t=0}=\frac{d}{dt}\tr(\rho(t)\log\rho(t) - \rho(t)\log\sigma)\bigg|_{t=0}=\left\langle\rho^{\prime}(0),\log\rho(0)-\log\sigma\right\rangle_{\mathrm{HS}}.

∎

For the supremum of
\eta^{D}(\Phi,\sigma)
, one has two cases:

(1)

The suprema is achieved at a state
\rho\neq\sigma
.

(2)

The suprema is achieved by a sequence of states
\rho_{n}
and any convergent subsequence necessarily converges to
\sigma
.

5.1.1.
The suprema is achieved at a state
\rho\neq\sigma

First we show that the optimizer must be a full-rank state if the channel
\Phi
is strictly positive, i.e.,
\Phi
maps any state to a full-rank state.

Lemma 5.2
.

Suppose the supremum of
\eta^{D}(\Phi,\sigma)
is achieved at state
\rho\neq\sigma
:

\eta:=\eta^{D}(\Phi,\sigma)=\frac{D(\Phi(\rho)\|\sigma)}{D(\rho\|\sigma)},\quad\rho\neq\sigma.

If
\Phi
is strictly positive, then
\rho
must be full-rank and

(5.4)

\displaystyle\Phi^{*}(\log\Phi(\rho)-\log\sigma)=\eta(\log\rho-\log\sigma).

Proof.

First, we assume that
\rho
is not full rank. Let
P_{+}
denote the projection onto the support of
\rho
and set
P_{-}:=\textbf{1}-P_{+}
. For any state
\widetilde{\rho}
such that
\mathrm{supp}(\widetilde{\rho})\subseteq P_{-}(\mathcal{H})
, define

(5.5)

\rho_{t}=(1-t)\rho+t\widetilde{\rho},\quad f(t):=\frac{D(\Phi(\rho_{t})\|\sigma)}{D(\rho_{t}\|\sigma)},\quad t\geq 0.

Since
\eta^{D}(\Phi,\sigma)
is achieved at
\rho
,
\max_{t\in[0,1]}f(t)=f(0)
. Therefore, the right derivative at
0
exists and it is a non-negative finite number

\displaystyle f^{\prime}(0+)=\frac{\left\langle\Phi(\widetilde{\rho}-\rho),\log\Phi(\rho)-\log\sigma\right\rangle_{\mathrm{HS}}-\eta\,\left\langle\widetilde{\rho}-\rho,\log\rho-\log\sigma\right\rangle_{\mathrm{HS}}}{D(\rho\|\sigma)}\in(-\infty,0].

Simplifying the above numerator using
D(\Phi(\rho)\|\sigma)=\eta D(\rho\|\sigma)
, we have

\displaystyle\left\langle\Phi(\widetilde{\rho}-\rho),\log\Phi(\rho)-\log\sigma\right\rangle_{\mathrm{HS}}-\eta\,\left\langle\widetilde{\rho}-\rho,\log\rho-\log\sigma\right\rangle_{\mathrm{HS}}

\displaystyle=\left\langle\Phi(\widetilde{\rho}),\log\Phi(\rho)-\log\sigma\right\rangle_{\mathrm{HS}}-\eta\,\left\langle\widetilde{\rho},\log\rho-\log\sigma\right\rangle_{\mathrm{HS}}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\left\langle\widetilde{\rho},\Phi^{*}(\log\Phi(\rho))\right\rangle_{\mathrm{HS}}}_{\mathrm{possibly\ unbounded}}-\underbrace{\eta\left\langle\widetilde{\rho},\log\rho\right\rangle_{\mathrm{HS}}}_{\mathrm{possibly\ unbounded}}-\underbrace{\left\langle\widetilde{\rho},\Phi^{*}(\log\sigma)-\eta\log\sigma\right\rangle_{\mathrm{HS}}}_{\mathrm{bounded}}.

For any pure state
\widetilde{\rho}=\outerproduct{v}{v}\in P_{-}(\mathcal{H})
, we have

\displaystyle\left\langle\widetilde{\rho},\log\rho\right\rangle_{\mathrm{HS}}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\bra{v}\log(\rho+ \varepsilon\textbf{1})\ket{v}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\bra{v}\log(P_+ \rho P_+ + \varepsilon P_+ + \varepsilon P_-)\ket{v}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\log\varepsilon\bra{v}P_{-}\ket{v}=-\infty.

Since
\sigma
has full rank,
\left\langle\widetilde{\rho},\Phi^{*}(\log\sigma)-\eta\log\sigma\right\rangle_{\mathrm{HS}}\in\mathbb{R}
. Moreover, using
\ f^{\prime}(0+)\in\mathbb{R}
, we must have

(5.6)

\left\langle\widetilde{\rho},\Phi^{*}(\log\Phi(\rho))\right\rangle_{\mathrm{HS}}=\bra{v}\Phi^{*}\big(\log\Phi(\rho)\big)\ket{v}=-\infty.

However, since
\Phi(\rho)
is full-rank,
\log\Phi(\rho)
is a bounded operator implying that
\bra{v}\Phi^{*}\big(\log\Phi(\rho)\big)\ket{v}<\infty
which is a contradiction. Thus
\rho
must have full-rank.

To prove (
5.4
), for any traceless
X=X^{\dagger}
consider
\rho_{t}=\rho+tX
and define
f(t)
as in (
5.5
). Since
\rho
is full-rank, there exists a small
\varepsilon>0
such that
\rho(t)\geq 0
for
t\in(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)
. Under the assumption
\rho
achieves the maximum of
\eta^{D}(\Phi,\sigma)
, we know that
f(t)
achieves the maximum at
0
, implying
f^{\prime}(0)=0
. Therefore, via Lemma
5.1
, we have

(5.7)

f^{\prime}(0)=\frac{\left\langle\Phi(X),\log\Phi(\rho)-\log\sigma\right\rangle_{\mathrm{HS}}-\eta\,\left\langle X,\log\rho-\log\sigma\right\rangle_{\mathrm{HS}}}{D(\rho\|\sigma)},

which implies that for any traceless
X=X^{\dagger}
,

\displaystyle\left\langle X,\Phi^{*}(\log\Phi(\rho)-\log\sigma)\right\rangle_{\mathrm{HS}}=\eta\left\langle X,\log\rho-\log\sigma\right\rangle_{\mathrm{HS}}.

Since
X
is arbitrary traceless Hermitian operator, we have

(5.8)

\Phi^{*}(\log\Phi(\rho)-\log\sigma)=\eta(\log\rho-\log\sigma)+\lambda\textbf{1}.

Note that
D(\Phi(\rho)\|\sigma)=\eta D(\rho\|\sigma)
, by taking
\tr(\rho\, \cdot)
on both sides, we have
\lambda=0
, concluding the result.
∎

In the case when
\Phi
is not strictly positive, we only have a support relation for the optimizer.

Proposition 5.3
.

Suppose the supremum of
\eta^{D}(\Phi,\sigma)
is achieved at state
\rho\neq\sigma
:

\eta:=\eta^{D}(\Phi,\sigma)=\frac{D(\Phi(\rho)\|\sigma)}{D(\rho\|\sigma)},\quad\rho\neq\sigma.

Then we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{supp}(\rho)^{\perp}=\mathrm{Ker}(\rho)=\Phi^{*}(\mathrm{Ker}(\Phi(\rho))).

Proof.

Let
P_{+}
denote the projection onto the support of
\rho
and set
P_{-}:=\textbf{1}-P_{+}
. Repeating the argument in Lemma
5.2
on the reduced algebra
P_{+}\,\mathcal{N}\,P_{+}
gives

(5.9)

P_{+}\Phi^{*}(\log\Phi(\rho)-\log\sigma)P_{+}=\eta P_{+}(\log\rho-\log\sigma)P_{+}.

As in Lemma
5.2
, for any state
\widetilde{\rho}
such that
\mathrm{supp}(\widetilde{\rho})\subseteq P_{-}(\mathcal{H})
, define
\rho_{t}=(1-t)\rho+t\widetilde{\rho},\quad t\geq 0
. Since
\eta^{D}(\Phi,\sigma)
is achieved at
\rho
,
\max_{t\in[0,1]}f(t)=f(0)
, where
f(t):=\frac{D(\Phi(\rho_{t})\|\sigma)}{D(\rho_{t}\|\sigma)}
. Therefore, the right derivative at
0
exists and it is a non-negative finite number

\displaystyle f^{\prime}(0+)=\frac{\left\langle\Phi(\widetilde{\rho}-\rho),\log\Phi(\rho)-\log\sigma\right\rangle_{\mathrm{HS}}-\eta\,\left\langle\widetilde{\rho}-\rho,\log\rho-\log\sigma\right\rangle_{\mathrm{HS}}}{D(\rho\|\sigma)}\in(-\infty,0].

Simplifying the above numerator using
D(\Phi(\rho)\|\sigma)=\eta D(\rho\|\sigma)
, we have

\displaystyle\left\langle\Phi(\widetilde{\rho}-\rho),\log\Phi(\rho)-\log\sigma\right\rangle_{\mathrm{HS}}-\eta\,\left\langle\widetilde{\rho}-\rho,\log\rho-\log\sigma\right\rangle_{\mathrm{HS}}

\displaystyle=\left\langle\Phi(\widetilde{\rho}),\log\Phi(\rho)-\log\sigma\right\rangle_{\mathrm{HS}}-\eta\,\left\langle\widetilde{\rho},\log\rho-\log\sigma\right\rangle_{\mathrm{HS}}

\displaystyle=\left\langle\widetilde{\rho},\Phi^{*}(\log\Phi(\rho))\right\rangle_{\mathrm{HS}}-\eta\left\langle\widetilde{\rho},\log\rho\right\rangle_{\mathrm{HS}}-\left\langle\widetilde{\rho},\Phi^{*}(\log\sigma)-\eta\log\sigma\right\rangle_{\mathrm{HS}}

For any pure state
\widetilde{\rho}=\outerproduct{v}{v}\in P_{-}(\mathcal{H})
, we have

\left\langle\widetilde{\rho},\log\rho\right\rangle_{\mathrm{HS}}=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\bra{v}\log(\rho+ \varepsilon\textbf{1})\ket{v}=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\log\varepsilon=-\infty.

Since
\sigma
has full rank,
\left\langle\widetilde{\rho},\Phi^{*}(\log\sigma)-\eta\log\sigma\right\rangle_{\mathrm{HS}}\in\mathbb{R}
. Moreover, using
\ f^{\prime}(0+)\in\mathbb{R}
, we must have

(5.10)

\left\langle\widetilde{\rho},\Phi^{*}(\log\Phi(\rho))\right\rangle_{\mathrm{HS}}=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\bra{v}\Phi^{*}\bigg(\log\big( \Phi(\rho) + \varepsilon\textbf{1}\big)\bigg)\ket{v}=-\infty.

Denote the spectral decomposition of
\Phi(\rho)
as

\displaystyle\Phi(\rho)=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\lambda_{j}\outerproduct{\psi_j}{\psi_j}=\sum_{j:\lambda_{j}>0}\lambda_{j}\outerproduct{\psi_j}{\psi_j}+\sum_{j:\lambda_{j}=0}0\cdot\outerproduct{\psi_j}{\psi_j}.

Via (
5.10
), we have

\displaystyle-\infty

\displaystyle=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\bra{v}\Phi^{*}\bigg(\log\big( \Phi(\rho) + \varepsilon\textbf{1}\big)\bigg)\ket{v}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\bra{v}\Phi^{*}\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{d}\log\big( \lambda_j+\varepsilon\big)\outerproduct{\psi_j}{\psi_j}\bigg)\ket{v}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\log\big( \lambda_j+\varepsilon\big)\bra{v}\Phi^{*}(\outerproduct{\psi_j}{\psi_j})\ket{v}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\sum_{j:\lambda_{j}>0}\log\big( \lambda_j+\varepsilon\big)\bra{v}\Phi^{*}(\outerproduct{\psi_j}{\psi_j})\ket{v}+\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}(\log\varepsilon)\sum_{j:\lambda_{j}=0}\bra{v}\Phi^{*}(\outerproduct{\psi_j}{\psi_j})\ket{v}

Note that
\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\sum_{j:\lambda_{j}>0}\log\big( \lambda_j+\varepsilon\big)\bra{v}\Phi^{*}(\outerproduct{\psi_j}{\psi_j})\ket{v}
is finite for any
\ket{v}
, we must have

\displaystyle\sum_{j:\lambda_{j}=0}\bra{v}\Phi^{*}(\outerproduct{\psi_j}{\psi_j})\ket{v}\neq 0,\quad\forall\ket{v}\in P_{-}(\mathcal{H}).

Since the pure state
\ket{v}\in P_{-}(\mathcal{H})
is arbitrary, we have

\displaystyle P_{-}(\mathcal{H})\subseteq\mathrm{supp}\left(\Phi^{*}\big(\sum_{j:\lambda_{j}=0}\outerproduct{\psi_j}{\psi_j}\big)\right).

Using a similar limit argument as above and (
5.9
), we have

\displaystyle P_{+}\Phi^{*}(\log\Phi(\rho)-\log\sigma)P_{+}=\eta P_{+}(\log\rho-\log\sigma)P_{+}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j:\lambda_{j}>0}\log\lambda_{j}\cdot P_{+}\Phi^{*}(\outerproduct{\psi_j}{\psi_j})P_{+}+\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}(\log\varepsilon)\cdot P_{+}\Phi^{*}\big(\sum_{j:\lambda_{j}=0}\outerproduct{\psi_j}{\psi_j}\big)P_{+}-P_{+}\Phi^{*}(\log\sigma)P_{+}.

Note that
\eta P_{+}(\log\rho-\log\sigma)P_{+}
,
\sum_{j:\lambda_{j}>0}\log\lambda_{j}\cdot P_{+}\Phi^{*}(\outerproduct{\psi_j}{\psi_j})P_{+}
, and
P_{+}\Phi^{*}(\log\sigma)P_{+}
are finite operators, we must have

\displaystyle P_{+}\Phi^{*}\big(\sum_{j:\lambda_{j}=0}\outerproduct{\psi_j}{\psi_j}\big)P_{+}=0,

implying that

\displaystyle P_{-}(\mathcal{H})=\mathrm{supp}\left(\Phi^{*}\big(\sum_{j:\lambda_{j}=0}\outerproduct{\psi_j}{\psi_j}\big)\right),

which concludes the result.
∎

Remark 5.4
.

An immediate corollary of Proposition
5.3
shows that (
5.4
) holds on the diagonals, i.e.,

\displaystyle P_{+}\Phi^{*}(\log\Phi(\rho)-\log\sigma)P_{+}=\eta\,P_{+}(\log\rho-\log\sigma)P_{+},

\displaystyle P_{-}\Phi^{*}(\log\Phi(\rho)-\log\sigma)P_{-}=\eta\,P_{-}(\log\rho-\log\sigma)P_{-},

which recovers the classical case, see
[
8
, Lemma 2]
. However, it is not clear whether the off-diagonal terms are equal.

5.1.2.
The suprema is achieved by a sequence of states
\rho_{n}
and any convergent subsequence necessarily converges to
\sigma
.

In this case, we have

\displaystyle\eta^{D}(\Phi,\sigma)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{D(\Phi(\rho_{n})\|\sigma)}{D(\rho_{n}\|\sigma)}.

Without loss of generality, we assume that
\rho_{n}\to\sigma
. Then we have a bounded sequence of traceless Hermitian operators
X_{n}
such that

(5.11)

\rho_{n}=\sigma+\varepsilon_{n}X_{n},\quad\varepsilon_{n}\to 0.

Using the well-known second order asymptotics of relative entropy, see for example (
[
19
]
or
[
5
, Section 4.1]
), we have

(5.12)

\displaystyle D(\rho_{n}\|\sigma)

\displaystyle=D(\sigma+\varepsilon_{n}X_{n}\|\sigma)=\varepsilon_{n}^{2}\tr\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}X_{n}(\sigma+r\textbf{1})^{-1}X_{n}(\sigma+r\textbf{1})^{-1}dr\right)+o(\varepsilon_{n}^{2}).

Denote the Bogoliubov-Kubo-Mori(BKM) inner product as

(5.13)

\left\langle X,Y\right\rangle_{\mathrm{BKM},\sigma}:=\left\langle X,\Gamma_{\sigma}(Y)\right\rangle_{\mathrm{HS}},\quad\Gamma_{\sigma}(Y):=\int_{0}^{1}\sigma^{s}Y\sigma^{1-s}ds.

Using the scalar integrals

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}a^{s}b^{1-s}ds=\frac{a-b}{\log a-\log b},\quad\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(a+r)(b+r)}dr=\frac{\log a-\log b}{a-b},

and functional calculus for left and right multiplication operator, we have
\Gamma_{\sigma}^{-1}(X)=\int_{0}^{\infty}(\sigma+r\textbf{1})^{-1}X_{n}(\sigma+r\textbf{1})^{-1}dr
.

Then define
h_{n}=\Gamma_{\sigma}^{-1}(X_{n})
, one has

\displaystyle\eta^{D}(\Phi,\sigma)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{D(\Phi(\rho_{n})\|\sigma)}{D(\rho_{n}\|\sigma)}

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\left\langle\Phi(X_{n}),\Gamma_{\sigma}^{-1}\circ\Phi(X_{n})\right\rangle_{\mathrm{HS}}}{\left\langle X_{n},\Gamma_{\sigma}^{-1}(X_{n})\right\rangle_{\mathrm{HS}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\left\langle h_{n},\Phi^{*}\circ\Gamma_{\sigma}^{-1}\circ\Phi\circ\Gamma_{\sigma}(h_{n})\right\rangle_{\mathrm{BKM},\sigma}}{\left\langle h_{n},h_{n}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{BKM},\sigma}}

Denote the BKM dual as

(5.14)

\Phi^{*,\mathrm{BKM}}:=\Gamma_{\sigma}^{-1}\circ\Phi\circ\Gamma_{\sigma}.

Then it is routine to check that under the assumption that
\Phi
has a unique full-rank fixed state
\sigma
,
\Phi^{*}\circ\Phi^{*,\mathrm{BKM}}
is:

•

\mathrm{BKM}
-symmetric, i.e.,
\left\langle\Phi^{*}\circ\Phi^{*,\mathrm{BKM}}(X),Y\right\rangle_{\mathrm{BKM},\sigma}=\left\langle X,\Phi^{*}\circ\Phi^{*,\mathrm{BKM}}(Y)\right\rangle_{\mathrm{BKM},\sigma}
.

•

positive, i.e., for any operator
X
,
\left\langle\Phi^{*}\circ\Phi^{*,\mathrm{BKM}}(X),X\right\rangle_{\mathrm{BKM},\sigma}\geq 0
.

•

ergodic, i.e., the fixed point space is given by
\{c\textbf{1}:c\in\mathbb{C}\}
.

In the quantum literature, ergodic is also called primitive for the channel. Therefore,
\Phi^{*}\circ\Phi^{*,\mathrm{BKM}}
can be diagonalized in the space
L^{2}(\mathcal{N},\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle_{\mathrm{BKM},\sigma})
, i.e., there exist a basis
\{f_{j}\}_{j=1}^{d^{2}}
for
L^{2}(\mathcal{N},\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle_{\mathrm{BKM},\sigma})
and
\{\lambda_{j}\}_{j=1}^{d^{2}}
with

\displaystyle 1=\lambda_{1}>\lambda_{2}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{d^{2}}\geq 0,

such that

\displaystyle\Phi^{*}\circ\Phi^{*,\mathrm{BKM}}(f_{j})=\lambda_{j}f_{j}.

For
h_{n}=\Gamma_{\sigma}^{-1}(X_{n})
, there exists
c_{j}
such that
h_{n}=\sum_{j}c_{j}f_{j}
and

(5.15)

\left\langle\Phi^{*}\circ\Phi^{*,\mathrm{BKM}}(h_{n}),h_{n}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{BKM},\sigma}=\sum_{j=1}^{d^{2}}c_{j}\lambda_{j}\left\langle f_{j},h_{n}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{BKM},\sigma}\quad\left\langle h_{n},h_{n}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{BKM},\sigma}=\sum_{j=1}^{d^{2}}c_{j}\left\langle f_{j},h_{n}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{BKM},\sigma}.

Note that
f_{1}=\textbf{1}
, we have
\left\langle f_{1},h_{n}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{BKM},\sigma}=\tr(X_n)=0
, therefore

\displaystyle\frac{\left\langle\Phi^{*}\circ\Phi^{*,\mathrm{BKM}}(h_{n}),h_{n}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{BKM},\sigma}}{\left\langle h_{n},h_{n}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{BKM},\sigma}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{d^{2}}c_{j}\lambda_{j}\left\langle f_{j},h_{n}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{BKM},\sigma}}{\sum_{j=1}^{d^{2}}c_{j}\left\langle f_{j},h_{n}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{BKM},\sigma}}=\frac{\sum_{j=2}^{d^{2}}c_{j}\lambda_{j}\left\langle f_{j},h_{n}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{BKM},\sigma}}{\sum_{j=2}^{d^{2}}c_{j}\left\langle f_{j},h_{n}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{BKM},\sigma}}

\displaystyle\leq\lambda_{2}\frac{\sum_{j=2}^{d^{2}}c_{j}\left\langle f_{j},h_{n}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{BKM},\sigma}}{\sum_{j=2}^{d^{2}}c_{j}\left\langle f_{j},h_{n}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{BKM},\sigma}}=\lambda_{2}.

Using the fact that
\Phi^{*}\circ\Phi^{*,\mathrm{BKM}}(f_{2})=\lambda_{2}f_{2}
, for a Lipschitz semi-norm
|||\cdot|||_{L}
, we have

(5.16)

\mathrm{Lip}_{L}(\Phi^{*}\circ\Phi^{*,\mathrm{BKM}})\geq\frac{|||\Phi^{*}\circ\Phi^{*,\mathrm{BKM}}(f_{2})|||_{L}}{|||f_{2}|||_{L}}=\lambda_{2}.

Note that the last part of the above argument obviously goes through in infinite dimension. In summary, we show that

Lemma 5.5
.

If
\eta^{D}(\Phi,\sigma)
is achieved by a sequence of states
\rho_{n}
and any convergent subsequence necessarily converges to
\sigma
, then we have

(5.17)

\eta^{D}(\Phi,\sigma)\leq\mathrm{Lip}_{L}(\Phi^{*}\circ\Phi^{*,\mathrm{BKM}}).

Combine the two cases, we have the following theorem

Theorem 5.6
.

Suppose
\Phi
is strictly positive, the entropy contraction coefficient satisifes

(5.18)

\eta^{D}(\Phi,\sigma)\leq\max\{\mathrm{Lip}_{L}(\Phi^{*}\circ\Phi^{*,\mathrm{BKM}}),\mathrm{Lip}_{L}(\Phi^{*})\sup_{\rho\in S(\mathcal{N}),\rho>0}\frac{|||\log\Phi(\rho)-\log\sigma|||_{L}}{|||\log\rho-\log\sigma|||_{L}}\}

for any Lipschitz semi-norm
|||\cdot|||_{L}
.

Before we proceed, we have the following remark:

Remark 5.7
.

When the channel is not stricly positive, one can choose
\Phi_{\varepsilon}:=(1-\varepsilon)\Phi+\varepsilon\mathcal{R}_{\sigma}
, where
\mathcal{R}_{\sigma}(X):=\tr(X)\sigma
is the replacer channel. Then
\Phi_{\varepsilon}
is strictly positive and the only extra technicality is

\displaystyle\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{|||\log\left((1-\varepsilon)\Phi(\rho_{\varepsilon})+\varepsilon\sigma\right)-\log\sigma|||_{L}}{|||\log\rho_{\varepsilon}-\log\sigma|||_{L}}.

When the channel is unital,
\sigma
is maximally mixed state, thus by unit degeneracy property of
|||\cdot|||_{L}
, we have

(5.19)

\mathrm{Log}\mathrm{Lip}_{L}(\Phi,\textbf{1}/d)=\sup_{\rho\in S(\mathcal{N}),\rho>0}\frac{|||\log\Phi(\rho)|||_{L}}{|||\log\rho|||_{L}}.

The technical part is how to deal with
\log\Phi(\rho)
.

5.1.3.
Concrete estimates via BMO norms

Using Theorem
5.6
, we need to get estimates for
\mathrm{Lip}_{L}(\Phi^{*})
, which was extensively discussed in
[
20
]
. Thus, we focus on the term

(5.20)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Log}\mathrm{Lip}_{L}(\Phi,\sigma):=\sup_{\rho\in S(\mathcal{N}),\rho>0}\frac{|||\log\Phi(\rho)-\log\sigma|||_{L}}{|||\log\rho-\log\sigma|||_{L}}.

When the channel is unital, that is,
\sigma=\textbf{1}/d
, we have
\mathrm{Log}\mathrm{Lip}_{L}(\Phi,\textbf{1}/d)=\frac{|||\log\Phi(\rho)|||_{L}}{|||\log\rho|||_{L}}
.

The main technical tool we use is the bounded mean oscillation (BMO) seminorm. Given a symmetric quantum Markov semigroup
T_{t}:\mathcal{N}\to\mathcal{N}
, for any
X\in\mathcal{N}
with
\lim_{t\to\infty}T_{t}(X)=0
, define

(5.21)

\|X\|_{\mathrm{BMO},T_{t}}:=\sup_{t\geq 0}\max\{\|T_{t}(X^{*}X)-T_{t}(X^{*})T_{t}(X)\|_{op}^{1/2},\|T_{t}(XX^{*})-T_{t}(X)T_{t}(X^{*})\|_{op}^{1/2}\}.

Note that the symmetric quantum Markov semigroup on
\mathcal{N}
inherits a Markov dilation from the standard Markov dilation of the heat semigroup on
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2};\mathcal{N})
. Then one has the following well-established relation between BMO semi-norm and
normalized
Schatten
p
-norms, see
[
25
]
for discrete time version and
[
24
, Theorem 0.2]
for the continuous time semigroup version:

(5.22)

\|X\|_{p}\lesssim p\|X\|_{\mathrm{BMO},T_{t}},\quad\forall X:\lim_{t\to\infty}T_{t}(X)=0.

In the following, we denote
a\lesssim b
if there exists a universal constant
c_{abs}>0
such that
a\leq c_{abs}\cdot b
.

The Lipschitz estimate established in
[
9
]
is useful:

Lemma 5.8
.

For any self-adjoint
A\in\mathcal{N}
and
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is a Lipschitz function on the spectrum of
A
, there is a symmetric quantum Markov semigroup
T_{t}:\mathcal{N}\to\mathcal{N}
and a universal constant
c>0
such that

(5.23)

\|[f(A),x]\|_{\mathrm{BMO},T_{t}}\leq c\|f\big|_{\mathrm{spec}(A)}\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}\|[A,x]\|_{\infty},\ \forall x\in\mathcal{N}.

The above estimate provides a universal upper bound on the log Lipschitz constant defined in (
5.20
), when the channel is unital:

Proposition 5.9
.

For any commutator Lipschitz semi-norm and unital, strictly positive channel
\Phi
, we have an absolute constant
c_{abs}>0
such that

(5.24)

\mathrm{Log}\mathrm{Lip}_{L}(\Phi,\textbf{1}/d)\leq c_{abs}\mathrm{Lip}_{L}(\Phi)\frac{\log^{2}d}{\lambda_{\min}(\Phi)},

where
\lambda_{\min}(\Phi)
is the minimal eigenvalue of
\Phi(\rho)
over any state
\rho
.

Proof.

Using the comparison between the operator and normalized Schatten norm
\|\cdot\|_{op}\leq d^{1/p}\|\cdot\|_{p}
, the comparison between the normalized Schatten norm and BMO semi-norms (
5.22
) and Lemma
5.8
, we have

\displaystyle\|[\log\Phi(\rho),x]\|_{op}

\displaystyle\leq d^{1/p}\|[\log\Phi(\rho),x]\|_{p}

\displaystyle\lesssim d^{1/p}p\|[\log\Phi(\rho),x]\|_{\mathrm{BMO},T_{t}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{d^{1/p}p}{\lambda_{\min}(\Phi)}\|[\Phi(\rho),x]\|_{op}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{d^{1/p}p}{\lambda_{\min}(\Phi)}\mathrm{Lip}_{\{x\}}(\Phi)\|[\rho,x]\|_{op}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{d^{2/p}p}{\lambda_{\min}(\Phi)}\mathrm{Lip}_{\{x\}}(\Phi)\|[\rho,x]\|_{p}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{d^{2/p}p^{2}}{\lambda_{\min}(\Phi)}\mathrm{Lip}_{\{x\}}(\Phi)\|[\rho,x]\|_{\mathrm{BMO},T_{t}}

\displaystyle=\frac{d^{2/p}p^{2}}{\lambda_{\min}(\Phi)}\mathrm{Lip}_{\{x\}}(\Phi)\|[\exp(\log\rho),x]\|_{\mathrm{BMO},T_{t}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{d^{2/p}p^{2}}{\lambda_{\min}(\Phi)}\mathrm{Lip}_{\{x\}}(\Phi)\|[\log\rho,x]\|_{op}.

Finally, we choose
p=\log d
to conclude the proof.
∎

Here we remark that the upper estimate is not sharp but works for general channels. We will discuss its implications on mixing time estimate in Section
5.2
.

5.1.4.
An example

In this subsection, we show that for some nice channels,
\mathrm{LogLip}(\Phi,\textbf{1}/d)
can be explicitly calculated and it implies sharp entropy contraction coefficients. We consider the depolarizing channel

(5.25)

\Phi_{p,d}(\rho)=(1-p)\rho+\tfrac{p}{d}\textbf{1},\quad p\in[0,1],\ d\geq 2.

Note that
\Phi_{p,d}
is symmetric, i.e.,
\Phi_{p,d}=\Phi_{p,d}^{*}
. For any Lipschitz seminorm
|||\cdot|||_{L}
, via unit degeneracy property, one has

(5.26)

|||\Phi_{p,d}(X)|||_{L}=(1-p)|||X|||_{L},\quad\forall X.

Therefore, we have
\mathrm{Lip}_{L}(\Phi_{p,d})=1-p
. We aim to get an explicit upper bound on the entropy contraction coefficient
\eta^{D}(\Phi_{p,d},\textbf{1}/d)
, here
\textbf{1}/d
is the unique fixed point of
\Phi_{p,d}
. First recall that if
\eta^{D}(\Phi_{p,d},\textbf{1}/d)
is achieved by a sequence of states
\rho_{n}
and any convergent subsequence necessarily converges to
\textbf{1}/d
, via Lemma
5.5
, we have

(5.27)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{D(\Phi_{p,d}(\rho_{n})\|\textbf{1}/d)}{D(\rho_{n}\|\textbf{1}/d)}=\eta^{D}(\Phi_{p,d},\textbf{1}/d)\leq\mathrm{Lip}_{L}(\Phi_{p,d})^{2}=(1-p)^{2}.

If
\eta^{D}(\Phi_{p,d},\textbf{1}/d)
is achieved by a state
\rho\neq\textbf{1}/d
, via Lemma
5.2
,
\rho
has full rank and satisfy

(5.28)

\Phi_{p,d}(\log\Phi_{p,d}(\rho)-\log\textbf{1}/d)=\eta(\log\rho-\log\textbf{1}/d).

Then for any Lipschitz semi-norm
|||\cdot|||_{L}
, we have

\displaystyle|||\Phi_{p,d}\left(\log\Phi_{p,d}(\rho)-\log\textbf{1}/d\right)|||_{L}=\eta|||\log\rho-\log\textbf{1}/d|||_{L}.

Via (
5.26
) and unit degeneracy property for
|||\cdot|||_{L}
, we have

(5.29)

\displaystyle(1-p)|||\log\Phi_{p,d}(\rho)|||_{L}=|||\Phi_{p,d}\left(\log\Phi_{p,d}(\rho)\right)|||_{L}=\eta|||\log\rho|||_{L}.

To get an explicit upper bound on
\eta
, we consider the qubit case, i.e.,
d=2
. A concrete estimate is given as follows:

Lemma 5.10
.

For any
p\in[0,1]
, one has

(5.30)

|||\log\Phi_{p,2}(\rho)|||_{S}\leq(1-p)|||\log\rho|||_{S}

for the full-rank density
\rho
that achieves
\eta^{D}(\Phi_{p,d},\textbf{1}/2)
.

Proof.

For the full-rank density operator
\rho
, since
d=2
, the spectral decomposition is given by
\rho=\lambda\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}+(1-\lambda)(\textbf{1}-\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi})
. Then by functional calculus, we have

\displaystyle\log\rho=\log\frac{\lambda}{1-\lambda}\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}+\log(1-\lambda)\cdot\textbf{1}.

Similarly, using the definition of depolarizing channel, we have

\displaystyle\log(\Phi_{p,d}(\rho))=\log\left(\frac{(1-p)\lambda+p/2}{(1-p)(1-\lambda)+p/2}\right)\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}+\log( (1-p)(1-\lambda) + p/2)\textbf{1}.

Taking the Lipschitz semi-norm, we have

\displaystyle|||\log(\Phi_{p,d}(\rho))|||_{L}=\left|\log\left(\frac{(1-p)\lambda+p/2}{(1-p)(1-\lambda)+p/2}\right)\right||||\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}|||_{L},

\displaystyle|||\log\rho|||_{L}=\left|\log\frac{\lambda}{1-\lambda}\right||||\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}|||_{L}.

Denote
x=\lambda-1/2\in(-1/2,1/2)
and

(5.31)

f_{p}(x):=\left|\frac{\log(1+2(1-p)x)-\log(1-2(1-p)x)}{\log(1+2x)-\log(1-2x)}\right|

Via elementary calculus, we see that
f_{p}(x)
is an even function and it is decreasing on
(0,1/2)
. Therefore,

(5.32)

\sup_{x\in(-1/2,1/2)}f_{p}(x)=\lim_{x\to 0}f_{p}(x)=1-p.

This implies that

\displaystyle|||\log(\Phi_{p,d}(\rho))|||_{L}=f_{p}(x)|||\log\rho|||_{L}\leq(1-p)|||\log\rho|||_{L},

which concludes the proof.
∎

Using (
5.27
), (
5.29
) and the above lemma, we have

Proposition 5.11
.

\eta^{D}(\Phi_{p,2},\textbf{1}/2)\leq(1-p)^{2}
.

Remark 5.12
.

In the qubit setting, using the Pauli representation of a Hermitian operator
X=v_{0}\textbf{1}+v_{1}\sigma_{x}+v_{2}\sigma_{y}+v_{3}\sigma_{z}
, and choosing
\mathcal{S}
as the set of Pauli operators, we have

(5.33)

|||X|||_{\mathcal{S}}=2\max\left\{\sqrt{v_{1}^{2}+v_{2}^{2}},\sqrt{v_{2}^{2}+v_{3}^{2}},\sqrt{v_{1}^{2}+v_{3}^{2}}\right\}.

Via similar calculations as in
[
5
]
, one can get explicit upper bound for Pauli-type channels, with the Lipschitz semi-norm given by commutator of Pauli operators. It recovers the sharp entropy contraction coefficients established in
[
23
]
.

5.2.
Mixing time estimate from Lipschitz contraction

5.2.1.
Mixing time estimates from entropy contraction

In this subsection, we use the estimates in the previous subsection to get explicit mixing time estimates for general ergodic unital channels, defined by

(5.34)

t_{\mathrm{mix}}(\varepsilon,\Phi):=\inf\{n\geq 1:\|\Phi^{n}(\rho)-\textbf{1}/d\|_{1}\leq\varepsilon,\quad\forall\rho\in S(\mathcal{N})\}.

An elementary inequality we will frequently use is the following Pinsker inequality and its reverse version, see
[
37
]
for a summary:

(5.35)

\frac{1}{2}\|\rho-\sigma\|^{2}_{1}\leq D(\rho\|\sigma)\leq\frac{\lambda_{\max}(\rho)}{\lambda_{\min}(\sigma)}\|\rho-\sigma\|_{1}.

Since
\Phi
is unital,
\sigma=\textbf{1}/d
and we have
\Phi^{*,\mathrm{BKM}}=\Phi
. Applying Theorem
5.6
, we have

Theorem 5.13
.

Suppose
\Phi
is unital with a unique fixed point
\textbf{1}/d
. Then there exists a universal constant
c_{abs}>0
such that for any commutator semi-norm
|||\cdot|||_{L}
with
\mathrm{Lip}_{L}(\Phi)<1,\ \mathrm{Lip}_{L}(\Phi^{*})\leq 1
,

(5.36)

t_{\mathrm{mix}}(\varepsilon,\Phi)\leq c_{abs}\frac{\log(1/\varepsilon)+\log d}{-\log(\Lip_L(\Phi))-\log( \Lip_L(\Phi^*))}.

Proof.

Denote the “lazy” version of
\Phi
as

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Phi}=\frac{1}{2}\Phi+\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{R}_{\textbf{1}/d},\quad\mathcal{R}_{\textbf{1}/d}(X):=\tr(X)\textbf{1}/d.

Then
\widetilde{\Phi}
is strictly positive and for any
N\geq 1
and
\rho\in S(\mathcal{N})
,

\displaystyle\|\widetilde{\Phi}^{N}(\rho)-\textbf{1}/d\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{2D(\widetilde{\Phi}^{N}(\rho)\|\textbf{1}/d)}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{2\mathrm{Lip}_{L}\big((\widetilde{\Phi}^{*})^{N}\big)}\sqrt{\max\left\{\mathrm{Lip}_{L}(\widetilde{\Phi}^{N}),\mathrm{Log}\mathrm{Lip}_{L}(\widetilde{\Phi}^{N},\textbf{1}/d)\right\}}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{2\mathrm{Lip}_{L}\big((\widetilde{\Phi}^{*})^{N}\big)\mathrm{Lip}_{L}(\widetilde{\Phi}^{N})}\sqrt{c_{abs}\frac{d\log^{2}d}{1-\frac{1}{2^{N}}}},

where the first inequality follows from Pinsker inequality (
5.35
), the second inequality follows from Theorem
5.6
applied to
\widetilde{\Phi}^{N}
, and the last inequality follows from Proposition
5.9
with the fact
\lambda_{\min}(\widetilde{\Phi}^{N})\geq(1-\frac{1}{2^{N}})\frac{1}{d}
. Note that

\displaystyle\mathrm{Lip}_{L}(\mathcal{R}_{\textbf{1}/d})=\mathrm{Lip}_{L}(\mathcal{R}^{*}_{\textbf{1}/d})=0,

and

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Phi}^{N}=\frac{1}{2^{N}}\Phi^{N}+(1-\frac{1}{2^{N}})\mathcal{R}_{\textbf{1}/d}.

Via convexity of Lipschitz constant, one has

\displaystyle\mathrm{Lip}_{L}\big((\widetilde{\Phi}^{*})^{N}\big)=\mathrm{Lip}_{L}\big(\frac{1}{2^{N}}(\Phi^{*})^{N}+(1-\frac{1}{2^{N}})\mathcal{R}_{\textbf{1}/d}\big)\leq\frac{1}{2^{N}}\mathrm{Lip}_{L}(\Phi^{*})^{N},

and similarly
\mathrm{Lip}_{L}\big(\widetilde{\Phi}^{N}\big)\leq\frac{1}{2^{N}}\mathrm{Lip}(\Phi)^{N}
. Therefore, we get

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2^{N}}\|\Phi^{N}(\rho)-\textbf{1}/d\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\|\widetilde{\Phi}^{N}(\rho)-\textbf{1}/d\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{2\mathrm{Lip}_{L}\big((\widetilde{\Phi}^{*})^{N}\big)\mathrm{Lip}_{L}(\widetilde{\Phi}^{N})}\sqrt{c_{abs}\frac{d\log^{2}d}{1-\frac{1}{2^{N}}}}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{2\frac{1}{2^{N}}\mathrm{Lip}_{L}(\Phi^{*})^{N}\frac{1}{2^{N}}\mathrm{Lip}_{L}(\Phi)^{N}}\sqrt{c_{abs}\frac{d\log^{2}d}{1-\frac{1}{2^{N}}}},

which produces

\displaystyle\|\Phi^{N}(\rho)-\textbf{1}/d\|_{1}\lesssim\sqrt{d}\log d\left(\sqrt{\mathrm{Lip}_{L}(\Phi)\mathrm{Lip}_{L}(\Phi^{*})}\right)^{N}.

Setting the right hand side to be less than
\varepsilon
, we get an upper bound on the mixing time

(5.37)

t_{\mathrm{mix}}(\varepsilon,\Phi)\leq c_{abs}\frac{\log(1/\varepsilon)+\log d}{-\log(\Lip_L(\Phi))-\log( \Lip_L(\Phi^*))}.

∎

We remark that using
\chi^{2}
-divergence,
[
21
]
also got mixing time estimates recently.

5.2.2.
Estimates of cost induced mixing time

We now derive an upper bound on the cost induced mixing time from the Lipschitz constant. The following lemma, which connects transportation cost with the Lipschitz constant of a given quantum channel
\Phi
, enabling us to derive an upper bound on the cost induced mixing time defined by

(5.38)

t_{\mathrm{mix}}^{L}(\varepsilon,\Phi^{*})=\inf\{n\geq 1:\mathrm{Cost}_{L}^{cb}((\Phi^{*})^{n})\geq(1-\varepsilon)\mathrm{Cost}_{L}^{cb}(E_{\mathrm{fix}})\}.

Here we assume
E_{\mathrm{fix}}
is the conditional expectation onto the fixed point algebra:
\Phi^{*}\circ E_{\mathrm{fix}}=E_{\mathrm{fix}}
.

Lemma 5.14
.

Under the additional assumption that

\Phi^{*}\circ E_{\mathrm{fix}}=E_{\mathrm{fix}}\circ\Phi^{*}=E_{\mathrm{fix}},

we have

\mathrm{Cost}_{L}^{cb}(\Phi^{*})\geq\bigl(1-\mathrm{Lip}_{L}^{cb}(\Phi^{*})\bigr)\,\mathrm{Cost}_{L}^{cb}(E_{\mathrm{fix}}).

Proof.

Given any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
n\geq 1
and
X_{n}\in\mathbb{M}_{n}\otimes\mathcal{N}
, such that
|||X_{n}|||_{L^{(n)}}\leq 1
and

(5.39)

\|(id_{\mathbb{M}_{n}}\otimes E_{\mathrm{fix}})(X_{n})-X_{n}\|_{op}\geq\mathrm{Cost}_{L}^{cb}(E_{\mathrm{fix}})-\varepsilon.

Then

\displaystyle\|(id_{\mathbb{M}_{n}}\otimes\Phi^{*})(X_{n})-X_{n}\|_{op}

\displaystyle\geq\|(id_{\mathbb{M}_{n}}\otimes E_{\mathrm{fix}})(X_{n})-X_{n}\|_{op}-\|(id_{\mathbb{M}_{n}}\otimes\Phi^{*})(X_{n})-(id_{\mathbb{M}_{n}}\otimes E_{\mathrm{fix}})(X_{n})\|_{op}

\displaystyle=\|(id_{\mathbb{M}_{n}}\otimes E_{\mathrm{fix}})(X_{n})-X_{n}\|_{op}-\|(id_{\mathbb{M}_{n}}\otimes\Phi^{*})(X_{n})-(id_{\mathbb{M}_{n}}\otimes E_{\mathrm{fix}}\circ\Phi^{*})(X_{n})\|_{op}

\displaystyle\geq\mathrm{Cost}_{L}^{cb}(E_{\mathrm{fix}})-\varepsilon-\|(id_{\mathbb{M}_{n}}\otimes id_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})}-id_{\mathbb{M}_{n}}\otimes E_{\mathrm{fix}})\left((id_{\mathbb{M}_{n}}\otimes\Phi^{*})(X_{n})\right)\|_{op}

\displaystyle\geq\mathrm{Cost}_{L}^{cb}(E_{\mathrm{fix}})-\varepsilon-\mathrm{Cost}_{L}^{cb}(E_{\mathrm{fix}})|||(id_{\mathbb{M}_{n}}\otimes\Phi^{*})(X_{n})|||_{L^{(n)}}

\displaystyle\geq\mathrm{Cost}_{L}^{cb}(E_{\mathrm{fix}})-\varepsilon-\mathrm{Cost}_{L}^{cb}(E_{\mathrm{fix}})\mathrm{Lip}_{L}^{cb}(\Phi^{*})

\displaystyle=(1-\mathrm{Lip}_{S}^{cb}(\Phi))\mathrm{Cost}_{L}^{cb}(E_{\mathrm{fix}})-\varepsilon.

For the first inequality, we use the triangle inequality; for the first equality, we use the assumption
E_{\mathrm{fix}}\circ\Phi^{*}=E_{\mathrm{fix}}
; for the third inequality, we use the definition of
\mathrm{Cost}_{L}^{cb}(E_{\mathrm{fix}})
; for the fourth inequality, we use the definition of
\mathrm{Lip}_{L}^{cb}(\Phi^{*})
. Since
\varepsilon
is arbitrary, we conclude the proof of
\mathrm{Cost}_{L}^{cb}(\Phi^{*})\geq\bigl(1-\mathrm{Lip}_{L}^{cb}(\Phi)\bigr)\,\mathrm{Cost}_{L}^{cb}(E_{\mathrm{fix}})
.
∎

The following result shows that
\mathrm{Lip}_{L}^{cb}(\Phi)<1
implies an upper bound on
t_{\mathrm{mix}}^{L}(\varepsilon,\Phi^{*})
:

Proposition 5.15
.

Suppose
\Phi^{*}\circ E_{\mathrm{fix}}=E_{\mathrm{fix}}\circ\Phi^{*}=E_{\mathrm{fix}}
and
\mathrm{Lip}_{L}^{cb}(\Phi)<1
, then for any
\varepsilon>0
, we have

(5.40)

t_{\mathrm{mix}}^{L}(\varepsilon,\Phi^{*})\leq\frac{\log(1/\varepsilon)}{-\log(\Lip_L^{cb}(\Phi^*))}.

Proof.

Via Lemma
5.14
, for any
n\geq 1
, via submultiplicativity of Lipschitz constant,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cost}_{L}^{cb}((\Phi^{*})^{n})

\displaystyle\geq\bigl(1-\mathrm{Lip}_{L}^{cb}((\Phi^{*})^{n})\bigr)\,\mathrm{Cost}_{L}^{cb}(E_{\mathrm{fix}})

\displaystyle\geq\bigl(1-\mathrm{Lip}_{L}^{cb}(\Phi^{*})^{n}\bigr)\,\mathrm{Cost}_{L}^{cb}(E_{\mathrm{fix}}).

Recall the definition of
t_{\mathrm{mix}}^{L}(\varepsilon,\Phi^{*})
in (
5.38
), for any
\varepsilon>0
, if
n\geq\frac{\log\varepsilon}{\log(\Lip_L^{cb}(\Phi))}
, then we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cost}_{L}^{cb}(\Phi^{*}\circ\cdots\circ\Phi^{*})\geq(1-\varepsilon)\mathrm{Cost}_{L}^{cb}(E_{\mathrm{fix}}),

which concludes the proof of the upper bound.
∎

Remark 5.16
.

If
\Phi
admits only one fixed point state
\sigma
(called primitive), with the choice of
E_{\mathrm{fix}}(X)=\tr(\sigma X)\textbf{1}
, we always have
\Phi^{*}\circ E_{\mathrm{fix}}=E_{\mathrm{fix}}\circ\Phi^{*}=E_{\mathrm{fix}}
.
