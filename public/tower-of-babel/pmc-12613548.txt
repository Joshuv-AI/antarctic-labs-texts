The effectiveness of culturally-focused simulation-based education on empathy development among Saudi nursing students: a quasi-experimental study
Authors: Ashwaq A. Almutairi, Shorok Hamed Alahmedi, Abdulaziz M. Alodhailah
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEmpathy is a core nursing competency that enhances patient outcomes and satisfaction, yet evidence consistently documents empathy decline during nursing education. Background

Nursing is fundamentally a humanistic profession in which empathy is not only a desirable trait but a core clinical competency that directly influences patient outcomes, satisfaction, and adherence to care. Empathy enables nurses to accurately perceive patients’ perspectives, regulate their own emotional responses, and engage in supportive, culturally sensitive interactions. Yet, despite its recognized importance, a decline in empathy across the course of nursing education has been consistently documented, raising concerns about graduates’ preparedness for person-centred practice [1, 2].

Empathy is widely recognized as a multidimensional construct that encompasses cognitive, affective, and behavioural components. The cognitive component refers to the ability to understand another person’s perspective, thoughts, or feelings, often described as “perspective-taking.” The affective component involves the capacity to share or resonate with another person’s emotional experiences, allowing individuals to emotionally connect with others [3–6].

Globally, healthcare systems are facing unprecedented cultural diversification, driven by migration, globalization, and medical tourism. In Saudi Arabia, these shifts are particularly pronounced. The Vision 2030 reforms have accelerated international recruitment, resulting in hospitals serving patients and families from a wide range of linguistic, religious, and cultural backgrounds [7, 8]. This demographic transformation requires a nursing workforce capable of providing culturally congruent care that respects patients’ diverse health beliefs and practices. However, traditional didactic methods in Saudi nursing education have historically prioritized biomedical content and technical proficiency, leaving fewer structured opportunities for cultivating empathy or cultural competence [9].

Simulation-based education holds a promising pedagogical potential to this challenge. Unlike lectures or case studies, simulation engages learners cognitively, affectively, and behaviorally in safe, experiential settings that mirror clinical realities. By immersing students in patient perspectives and encouraging structured reflection, simulation aligns with experiential learning theory [10] and perspective-taking frameworks [11], both of which underscore the role of active engagement and reflection in shaping empathic capacities. Previous international studies have reported that simulation can enhance empathy, though findings remain mixed, potentially due to variability in intervention design and contextual adaptation [12].

Cultural tailoring of simulation may be a key determinant of its effectiveness. When scenarios reflect the cultural realities that students will encounter in practice, learners may experience greater emotional resonance and clinical relevance. Evidence from East Asia, North America, and Europe suggests that culturally adapted simulation interventions are not only acceptable to students but can also generate meaningful improvements in empathy and cultural sensitivity [13]. However, there remains a scarcity of research within Arab and Gulf contexts, where cultural and educational dynamics differ significantly.

This study addresses this gap by evaluating a culturally focused simulation-based educational intervention designed for Saudi nursing students. Grounded in the empathy continuum [12] and perspective-taking theory [3], the intervention aimed to enhance multidimensional empathy and foster readiness for culturally diverse practice. By applying validated empathy measures and assessing student satisfaction, this research contributes empirical evidence to inform the integration of cultural empathy simulations into nursing curricula in Saudi Arabia and comparable multicultural healthcare environments.

Methods

Study design

This study employed a quasi-experimental, single-group pre-test/post-test design to evaluate the effectiveness of a culturally focused simulation-based intervention on empathy among undergraduate nursing students. The choice of this design was guided by the educational context in which the research was embedded. While randomized controlled trials are considered the gold standard for determining causal relationships, the feasibility of implementing randomization was limited by curriculum scheduling constraints, the ethical obligation to provide all students equal access to the intervention, and logistical challenges of dividing intact academic cohorts. The single-group design enabled rigorous assessment of within-subject changes while maintaining ecological validity in an educational setting.

Setting

The study was conducted during the first semester of the 2022–2023 academic year at the College of Nursing, King Saud University (KSU), Riyadh, Saudi Arabia. Established in 1957, KSU is one of the oldest and most prestigious universities in the Kingdom, enrolling large numbers of nursing undergraduates annually. The simulation sessions were delivered in the university’s high-fidelity simulation center, which is equipped with immersive three-dimensional (3D) virtual reality technology and debriefing facilities that enable interactive and reflective learning.

Sample and sampling

Participants were first-year Bachelor of Nursing students enrolled in foundation-level nursing courses. The rationale for selecting first-year students was twofold: first, empathy skills are most malleable during early training, making this developmental stage ideal for intervention [14]; and second, early exposure to cultural simulation may provide a foundation for subsequent clinical practice. Although second- and third-year students possess more simulation and clinical exposure, our focus was on examining the impact of early curricular integration of empathy-focused interventions. Students were recruited using convenience sampling from intact cohorts scheduled for simulation sessions. Inclusion criteria included: (1) enrollment as a first-year nursing student at king Saud university; (2) proficiency in Arabic to ensure accurate completion of survey instruments; and (3) willingness to provide informed consent. Students absent from the simulation or with incomplete data (>20% missing responses) were excluded.

A priori sample size estimation was conducted using G*Power software (version 3.1). Based on an anticipated medium effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.3) derived from previous simulation-empathy studies [15], with an alpha of 0.05 and power of 0.80, the minimum required sample size was 128 participants. To account for attrition and missing data, we targeted recruitment of 250 students. Ultimately, 203 students completed both pre- and post-intervention surveys and were included in the final analysis.

Data collection tools

Comprehensive State Empathy Scale (CSES)

The CSES is a multidimensional measure of empathy originally developed by Davis (1980) and later adapted for healthcare contexts. It was selected for its strong psychometric properties and ability to capture both cognitive and affective aspects of empathy in situational contexts [16]. The tool aims to assess temporary changes in empathy levels in response to interventions such as simulation. It consists of 30 items grouped into six subscales: empathic concern, personal distress, shared affect, empathic imagination, helping motivation, and cognitive empathy, with each subscale containing five items. Items are scored on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). Higher scores indicate greater empathy on the specific subdimension. Validity evidence has been reported through confirmatory factor analysis, with Cronbach’s alpha coefficients consistently exceeding 0.85 in healthcare education studies. For the current research, the CSES was translated into Arabic following standardized forward–backward translation procedures, reviewed by an expert panel of bilingual nursing educators, and pilot tested with 20 nursing students for clarity and cultural appropriateness. Internal consistency reliability in our sample was excellent (Cronbach’s α = 0.966), and construct validity was confirmed through confirmatory factor analysis.

Satisfaction with Cultural Simulation Experience Scale (SCSES)

The SCSES was designed to evaluate nursing students’ satisfaction with culturally focused simulation activities. It was originally developed by Everson et al. (2015) in the context of cultural empathy simulation research. The tool aims to measure perceived value and acceptability of simulation-based education. The SCSES comprises 18 items across three domains: cultural competence (11 items), learning from debriefing (4 items), and reflection on nursing practice (3 items). Items are rated on a 7-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 7 = strongly agree), yielding a possible total score range of 18–126, with higher scores indicating greater satisfaction. Previous research has demonstrated excellent reliability (Cronbach’s α > 0.90) and construct validity in cross-cultural nursing student samples. In the present study, the SCSES was translated into Arabic using a forward–backward translation method, reviewed by a panel of cultural nursing education experts, and pretested with a small group of students to ensure face validity. Psychometric testing in our sample demonstrated high internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.976) and good factor structure consistency with the original instrument.

Intervention (simulation scenarios and facilitation)

The culturally focused simulation intervention comprised three phases: a structured pre-brief, immersive simulation scenarios, and a guided debrief. During the pre-brief, students were oriented to the simulation environment, expectations, and confidentiality agreements. The immersive component involved a series of 3D virtual reality scenarios depicting culturally diverse patient encounters commonly observed in Saudi hospitals. Examples included communication with a Filipino patient with limited Arabic proficiency, managing dietary restrictions for an Indian patient during religious fasting, and addressing end-of-life care preferences of a Sudanese family. Each scenario was designed to highlight cultural values, communication barriers, and ethical dilemmas that required empathic engagement and culturally congruent nursing responses.

The debrief followed immediately after each scenario, using open-ended prompts to encourage reflection on emotions, assumptions, and culturally sensitive care strategies. Debriefing was conducted in line with the PEARLS framework to ensure a structured and supportive environment for learning [17, 18].

Facilitators’ qualifications and training

The sessions were facilitated by three master’s-prepared nursing educators specializing in medical–surgical and community health nursing. All facilitators had a minimum of five years of experience in undergraduate nursing education and formal certification in simulation facilitation and debriefing from the Society for Simulation in Healthcare. In preparation for this study, they completed a focused training workshop on cultural competence in simulation, which covered scenario standardization, fidelity maintenance, and strategies for promoting empathic reflection during debriefing. This ensured consistency, high fidelity, and educational effectiveness across all simulation sessions.

Data collection procedure

Data were collected at two points: immediately before (T1) and immediately after (T2) the simulation intervention. At T1, students completed the Arabic version of the CSES to establish baseline empathy levels. Following the intervention, students completed both the CSES (T2) and the SCSES. All instruments were administered electronically using the Qualtrics platform on either university-provided computers or students’ personal devices. Each student was assigned a unique anonymous code to allow pre- and post-data matching while preserving confidentiality. Research assistants were present during administration to provide technical support and ensure standardized procedures. In our study, facilitators employed open-ended questions to encourage critical reflection, consistent with best practice debriefing models such as PEARLS.

Data analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 26. Descriptive statistics summarized demographic characteristics and scale scores. Prior to inferential analyses, assumptions of normality were tested using the Shapiro–Wilk test and inspection of skewness and kurtosis values, while sphericity in ANOVA was evaluated using Mauchly’s test. Where assumptions were violated, appropriate corrections (e.g., Greenhouse–Geisser) or non-parametric tests were applied. Pre–post differences in empathy subscales were examined using paired-samples t-tests, with effect sizes (Cohen’s d) calculated. A mixed-design ANOVA was used to examine potential moderating effects of gender and language background. Non-parametric tests (Mann–Whitney U and Chi-square) were used to analyze SCSES satisfaction data. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval

was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of King Saud University (No: KSU-HE-22-449, dated 06/09/2022). Participation was strictly voluntary. Students were informed of the study’s objectives, their right to decline or withdraw at any time without academic penalty, and the confidentiality of their responses. Written informed consent was obtained prior to data collection. All procedures adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and institutional ethical standards.

Results

Participant characteristics

Table 1 presents the demographic profile of the 203 nursing students who participated in the study. The cohort was predominantly female (64.5%), which is consistent with the gender distribution commonly observed in nursing education programs both in Saudi Arabia and internationally. This gender imbalance is important to note, as prior research has consistently demonstrated that female nursing students tend to report higher baseline empathy levels, which could influence both pre-intervention scores and the magnitude of change following simulation-based education. In terms of age, nearly half of the participants were between 22 and 24 years (48.8%), while 40.4% were younger than 22, and only 10.8% were older than 24. The mean age of the cohort was 20.6 years (SD = 1.3), reflecting a relatively homogeneous group of early-career students at the beginning of their nursing training. This homogeneity enhances the internal validity of the findings but may limit their generalizability to more experienced cohorts. Linguistically, the majority of students reported Arabic as the sole language spoken at home (69.4%), while nearly one-third (30.6%) spoke Arabic alongside at least one additional language. This bilingual or multilingual subgroup is noteworthy because language diversity has been associated with greater perspective-taking and intercultural sensitivity, factors that may influence empathy development. Collectively, these demographic characteristics illustrate that the sample was comprised mainly of young, female, and Arabic-speaking students, with a significant proportion having multilingual backgrounds—factors that provide a valuable context for interpreting both empathy outcomes and satisfaction with culturally focused simulation.

Reliability of measurement instruments

Table 2 presents the psychometric properties of the Comprehensive State Empathy Scale (CSES) and the Satisfaction with Cultural Simulation Experience Scale (SCSES) as applied in this study. Across all subscales, Cronbach’s α coefficients ranged between 0.85 and 0.94, reflecting excellent internal consistency and confirming that each subscale reliably measured its intended construct. These results are consistent with international studies reporting strong reliability of the CSES in healthcare education and reinforce its suitability for assessing multidimensional empathy in diverse cultural settings. Similarly, the SCSES demonstrated particularly high internal consistency, with α values exceeding 0.90 across all domains, indicating that students perceived the items as coherent reflections of their satisfaction with cultural simulation. Composite reliability (CR) values above 0.79 and average variance extracted (AVE) scores above 0.50 for all subscales provide further support for convergent validity, suggesting that the items within each factor converged to represent the underlying construct effectively. Taken together, these findings indicate that both instruments performed robustly in this Saudi nursing student sample, with psychometric indices surpassing recommended thresholds for educational and psychological research. This strengthens confidence in the accuracy and interpretability of the subsequent analyses based on these scales.

Effect of simulation on empathy (CSES)

Table 3 illustrates the pre- and post-intervention scores across the six subscales of the Comprehensive State Empathy Scale (CSES), highlighting consistent and statistically significant improvements in all domains of empathy following the culturally focused simulation. The most pronounced gains were observed in shared affect and distress, both of which reflect the affective dimension of empathy. Specifically, shared affect increased by nearly three points with a medium-to-large effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.66), while distress rose by approximately four points (d = 0.63). These findings suggest that the immersive simulation was particularly effective in strengthening students’ emotional resonance with patient experiences, an outcome that aligns with experiential learning theory and the empathy continuum framework, both of which emphasize emotional engagement as a precursor to empathic growth. Empathic concern, another affective dimension, also demonstrated a notable increase (d = 0.51), underscoring that students became more sensitive to and motivated by patients’ emotional states.

On the cognitive side, empathic imagination and cognitive empathy both showed significant improvements with effect sizes of 0.53 and 0.45, respectively. These findings indicate that the simulation not only fostered emotional responsiveness but also enhanced students’ ability to adopt patients’ perspectives and cognitively understand their situations, a skill crucial for culturally congruent care in diverse clinical environments. Interestingly, helping motivation showed the smallest, yet still statistically significant, change (d = 0.32), suggesting that behavioral intentions may be more resistant to short, single-session interventions and may require repeated or longitudinal exposure to consolidate.

Satisfaction with the simulation (SCSES)

Table 4 presents the descriptive statistics of students’ satisfaction with the culturally focused simulation experience, measured by the Satisfaction with SCSES. Across all domains, median scores were significantly higher than the scale midpoints (all p < 0.001), underscoring the high acceptability and perceived value of the intervention among participants. The highest ratings were observed in the “Learning from Debrief” domain (median = 47, IQR 35–56), highlighting the critical role of structured reflection in facilitating deeper learning and consolidating empathy-related insights. Similarly, strong satisfaction in “Cultural Competence” (median = 43, IQR 33–48) suggests that the simulation effectively addressed students’ needs to understand and integrate cultural perspectives into nursing care, reinforcing its relevance to the Saudi healthcare context. While “Reflecting on Practice” scored slightly lower (median = 17, IQR 12–21), the domain still demonstrated robust outcomes, indicating that students recognized the transferability of the simulation experience to their professional practice, albeit with some challenges in bridging the gap between simulated and clinical environments. Overall satisfaction with the simulation was notably high (median = 105, IQR 80–125), confirming that the intervention was well-received by the cohort and suggesting strong potential for curricular integration.

Discussion

This study examined the impact of a culturally focused simulation-based educational intervention on empathy development among first-year Saudi nursing students. Using a validated multidimensional empathy scale and a culturally tailored satisfaction tool, the findings demonstrated statistically significant improvements across all six empathy subdimensions. Students also reported high satisfaction with the intervention, particularly with opportunities for cultural competence development and structured debriefing. Together, these results suggest that immersive, culturally sensitive simulation is an effective pedagogical strategy for fostering empathy in nursing education.

The observed gains in empathy across cognitive, affective, and behavioral domains affirm the potential of simulation-based education as a powerful tool for cultivating empathy in health professions students [19]. Notably, empathic concern and shared affect showed the largest improvements, suggesting that the intervention successfully engaged students emotionally and enhanced their ability to resonate with patients’ experiences [20]. This finding aligns with Levett-Jones and Cant’s “empathy continuum” framework, which emphasizes affective engagement as a prerequisite for deeper cognitive and behavioral empathy [21]. It also supports perspective-taking theory, which posits that exposure to another person’s worldview stimulates emotional resonance and helps learners adopt others’ perspectives in meaningful ways [22].

Our findings parallel those of Shin et al. [23], who reported similar effect sizes when Korean nursing students engaged in cultural simulations with foreign patients. Likewise, Ozcan et al. [24] found that empathy increased when undergraduate nursing students participated in structured simulation-based communication training. By contrast, other studies have reported neutral or even negative effects of simulation on empathy [12, 25]. Such discrepancies may be explained by differences in intervention design, with quality of debriefing, cultural relevance of scenarios, and facilitator expertise serving as critical determinants of success. The robust outcomes in the present study likely reflect the integration of these essential elements, particularly the focus on structured reflection through debriefing.

The high satisfaction levels observed across SCSES domains underscore the importance of aligning simulation content with students’ cultural and clinical realities. Saudi Arabia’s healthcare system is uniquely multicultural, with patients representing diverse ethnic, linguistic, and religious backgrounds. By designing scenarios that reflected common patient encounters in Saudi hospitals—such as interactions with Filipino, Indian, or Sudanese patients—the intervention resonated with learners’ anticipated practice environments. This cultural tailoring likely enhanced authenticity, learner engagement, and perceived relevance, thereby amplifying empathy outcomes [26].

Our findings extend this literature by demonstrating that even short, 30-minute culturally tailored simulations can yield meaningful improvements in empathy, making such interventions feasible within crowded curricula [27, 28].

Interestingly, male students showed relatively greater gains in helping motivation, suggesting that simulation may be particularly effective in addressing empathy domains where deficits exist. Similarly, multilingual students exhibited higher levels of empathic imagination and cognitive empathy, possibly reflecting enhanced cognitive flexibility and perspective-taking skills associated with bilingualism [29–31]. These findings underscore the need to consider individual learner characteristics when designing empathy-focused educational strategies [19, 32].

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, outcomes were measured immediately after the intervention, which precludes conclusions about long-term retention of empathy gains. Longitudinal studies assessing whether improvements persist and translate into clinical behavior are necessary. Second, all measures relied on self-report scales, which are susceptible to response bias. Incorporating observational measures or patient-reported outcomes could provide a more comprehensive assessment of empathy. Third, the single-institution sample limits generalisability. Cultural, curricular, and institutional factors in other settings may influence intervention effectiveness. Replication in multiple Saudi and international nursing schools is warranted. Nevertheless, we recognize that the absence of a comparison or control group limits causal inference and generalizability, and this limitation is acknowledged in the discussion.

Implications for nursing education

Despite these limitations, the findings carry important implications for nursing education. The significant gains across all empathy subdimensions support the integration of culturally focused simulations into early nursing curricula. Given that the intervention required only 30 min yet produced meaningful improvements, it is feasible for widespread adoption. High satisfaction levels suggest strong student receptivity, which may facilitate educator buy-in and institutional support. To maximize impact, future implementations should ensure cultural relevance of scenarios, employ trained facilitators skilled in debriefing, and provide opportunities for repeated exposure across the curriculum.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that culturally focused simulation-based education is an effective and well-received strategy for enhancing empathy among Saudi nursing students. By combining immersive virtual experiences with structured reflection, the intervention strengthened both affective and cognitive dimensions of empathy. The results underscore the importance of cultural tailoring, early curricular integration, and high-quality debriefing in fostering empathy. While methodological limitations necessitate cautious interpretation, the findings contribute valuable evidence to the growing literature on empathy education and highlight pathways for preparing nursing graduates to provide compassionate, culturally competent care in diverse healthcare environments.
