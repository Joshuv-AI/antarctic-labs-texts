Nasal Streptococcus pyogenes Infection Exacerbates Psoriasiform Relapse in Mice Through TLR2-Dependent Dendritic Cell Metabolic Reprogramming and γδ T Cell Activation
Authors: Lijiaming Zhou, Zhengchun Wang, Jingxia Zhao, Tingting Di, YuJiao Meng, Haoyue Zhu, Yueyue Ma, Xiaohan Yu, Wenbo Liu, Yanqi Su, Linze Li, Ping Li, Yan Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNasal Streptococcus pyogenes (SP) infection triggers psoriasis relapse, with the systemic-to-cutaneous immune relay poorly understood. This study aims to elucidate role of dendritic cell (DC) metabolic reprogramming and trafficking in driving SP-induced psoriasis relapse.MethodsA murine psoriasis model was established using imiquimod (IMQ) and intranasal SP challenge, with disease severity evaluated via PASI scoring and histology, epidermal proliferation (Ki67) and T cell (CD3) via immunofluorescence, immunohistochemistry, and IL-23, IL-17A, and IL-1β levels via enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) in skin. Immune cell infiltration (DCs, γδ T cells) was analyzed by flow cytometry, RNA-seq identified affected genes and pathways, and real-time polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) /Western blot (WB) detected transcription and protein levels of target genes in SP lipoteichoic acid (LTA)-activated BMDCs in vitro. Oxygen consumption rate (OCR), extracellular acidification rate (ECAR), wound healing, Transwell migration, and co-culture assays assessed metabolic reprogramming and migration function of BMDCs in response to LTA in vitro. CD11c-Luc reporter mice were monitored via bioluminescence imaging (BLI) to dynamically track DC migration in vivo, and confocal immunofluorescence co-localization was used to visualize DC-γδT cell interactions in skin.ResultsNasal SP infection exacerbated psoriasis-like lesions by increasing PASI scores (10.3 ± 0.2 in Re-SP vs 8.2 ± 0.4 in Re, P < 0.01), epidermal thickness, pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-17A levels increased by approximately 2.1-fold in Re-SP compared to Re), and promoting DC maturation, γδT cell infiltration, and IL-17A production via TLR2/NF-κB/nitric oxide synthase 2(NOS2) signaling. RNA-seq and in vitro assays demonstrated SP induces DC aerobic glycolysis (as evidenced by significantly increased ECAR and suppressed OCR) and hyper-mobility through TLR2 activation, upregulating TLR2, NF-κB markers (p-IKBα, p-p65), NOS2, and myosin-related proteins (p-myosin phosphatase target subunit 1, MYPT1 and p-myosin light chain, MLC), with inhibited TLR2 reducing these effects. In vivo, SP triggered rapid DC activation and trafficking to skin, forming immunological synapse with γδT cells to induce IL-17A.ConclusionThese findings suggest that nasal SP infection may exacerbate psoriasis relapse through TLR2-dependent DC metabolic reprogramming and trafficking, which in turn promote γδ T cell IL-17A production. However, as these findings are derived from preclinical mouse models and in vitro experiments, validation in human psoriasis cohorts and chronic disease models is required before clinical translation can be considered.Keywords: psoriasis relapse, Streptococcus pyogenes, infection, dendritic cell migration, γδ T cells

Introduction

Psoriasis is a chronic systemic inflammatory dysregulation characterized by erythematous plaques and silvery scales, representing a significant global health burden.1 While primarily manifesting in the dermis, psoriasis is increasingly recognized as a multi-systemic disorder involving joints, the gastrointestinal tract, and cardiovascular health.2,3 Beyond physical symptoms, the systemic nature of the disease extends to neuropsychiatric comorbidities; the psychological stress and social stigma associated with visible lesions significantly elevate the risk of depression and anxiety in affected individuals.4

Therapies for psoriasis, including topical glucocorticoids, systemic immunosuppressants, and biologics, can achieve satisfactory short-term efficacy. Biologics targeting the interleukin (IL)-23/IL-17 immune pathway have revolutionized the therapeutic landscape for psoriasis.5 Although they offer remarkable efficacy, issues such as recurrence and complications arising from treatment are also challenging.6–8 Its unpredictable cycle of relapse is a major clinical challenge. A classic trigger for psoriasis relapse, particularly guttate psoriasis, is upper respiratory infection with Streptococcus pyogenes (abbreviated as SP, also known as Group A streptococcus, GAS).9 This long-observed clinical correlation suggests a sophisticated remote immune communication where a localized infection in the nasopharyngeal mucosa reinitiates a robust inflammatory cascade in the skin.

Paradoxically, while biologics like secukinumab effectively suppress skin inflammation, they may concurrently increase susceptibility to respiratory infections.10 This creates a difficult clinical dilemma: the most effective treatments may make patients more vulnerable to very infections that can worsen their psoriasis. A key unanswered question is how a remote infection reinitiates skin inflammation, especially in people taking immune-modifying drugs. Finding an answer is not only of academic interest but also a pressing clinical necessity to mitigate treatment-related risks and prevent relapse.

The leading theory for SP infection associated relapse is molecular mimicry.11 This theory, which suggests that streptococcal proteins accidentally activate skin-targeting T cells, is plausible. However, this adaptive immune mechanism does not fully account for the rapid onset of infection-triggered flares, which often occur within days of pharyngitis—a timeframe too short for classical adaptive T cell memory responses. This temporal discrepancy suggests that innate immune mechanisms, particularly DCs in sensing bacterial components via Toll-like receptors (TLRs), may play a more immediate role in initiating relapse.12 Through TLRs, particularly TLR2 which senses Gram-positive components, DCs initiate the production of IL-23 to drive the pathogenic IL-17 pathway within hours of activation without requiring prior antigen sensitization.13 While TLR2 is a key sensor, SP also expresses other pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs, eg, streptolysin O, M protein, CpG DNA) that may engage additional receptors.14,15 However, the bioenergetic requirements governing DC behavior after bacterial sensing remain enigmatic. Several studies propose that metabolic reprogramming—specifically the shift toward aerobic glycolysis—may serve as the metabolic “engine” fueling DC hyper-activation and migration.16,17

Following activation at mucosal sites, DCs undergo a maturation program that includes upregulation of chemokine receptors (particularly CCR7),18,19 enabling their egress from peripheral tissues and migration via afferent lymphatics to draining lymph nodes. From there, activated DCs can enter the circulation and home to distant peripheral tissues, including the skin, where they present antigens and secrete polarizing cytokines to instruct local immune responses. This trafficking pathway provides a biological basis for the proposed “nasal-skin axis”, whereby DCs activated in the nasopharyngeal mucosa can disseminate inflammatory signals to cutaneous sites.

Psoriasis is fundamentally a T-cell mediated disease. While Th17 cells are pivotal, skin-resident γδ T cells have emerged as rapid, potent sources of IL-17A during early-stage inflammation.20–22 Whether these γδ T cells require specific instructions from metabolically reprogrammed mucosal DCs to initiate a relapse is a critical missing link in current understanding. C57BL/6J mice (common psoriasis model), IMQ model recapitulates human plaque psoriasis; relevance to human guttate psoriasis triggered by SP.

Previous animal models have demonstrated that intranasal streptococcal infection can exacerbate IMQ-induced skin inflammation through the induction of skin tissue-resident memory T cells.23 However, these studies did not investigate the early innate events linking mucosal infection to cutaneous inflammation. Our study advances beyond these reports by identifying the specific role of TLR2-mediated DC metabolic reprogramming and trafficking as the initial trigger for the “nasal-skin” immune axis, providing a mechanistic framework for the rapid onset of infection-triggered flares that is not fully explained by adaptive T cell memory alone.

While the IMQ-induced psoriasis model in C57BL/6J mice recapitulates key features of human plaque psoriasis, it is important to acknowledge the limitations of translating findings from this acute model to human disease, which is typically chronic and relapsing. Furthermore, differences in immune system composition and skin architecture between mice and humans may influence the relative contributions of specific cell types. Nevertheless, this model remains valuable for dissecting early mechanistic events in infection-triggered exacerbation and for identifying pathways that may be amenable to therapeutic intervention.

In this study, we hypothesized that nasal SP infection triggers a TLR2-dependent metabolic reprogramming in mucosal DCs, specifically shifting toward aerobic glycolysis. This metabolic shift provides the metabolic energy and biosynthetic precursors required for their enhanced trafficking to the skin, where they subsequently prime γδ T cells to initiate psoriasis relapse. We aim to delineate the “nasal-skin” immune axis and identify novel therapeutic checkpoints—within TLR2 signaling or DC metabolic pathways—to mitigate infection-induced recurrence in psoriatic patients.

Materials and Methods

Animals and Ethical Statement

This study was conducted in strict accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines (https://arriveguidelines.org). Male C57BL/6J mice (SPF grade, 5‒6 weeks old and weighing 20 ± 2 g) were purchased from Beijing Weitonglihua Biotechnology Co, Ltd. (Beijing, China) [No. SCXK(Jing) 2021–0006], and housed in a level three microbial breeding room, which is temperature-controlled (22 ± 2°C) with a humidity of 50 ± 10% and a 12-hour light/dark cycle, with ad libitum access to a standard sterile diet and water. Male C57BL/6J mice were used exclusively in this study to avoid potential confounding effects of female hormonal cycles on immune responses, which could introduce variability in the IMQ-induced model.

All animal experiments were conducted in strict accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (NIH Publication No. 85–23, revised 1996) and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Beijing Institute of Traditional Chinese Medicine (Protocol Approval Number: BJTCM-M-2024-02-06). All efforts were made to minimize animal suffering.

Euthanasia was performed at experimental endpoints by cervical dislocation under deep isoflurane (RWD Life Science Co, Ltd, R510-22-10) anesthesia (induction 3%, maintenance 1.5% in oxygen for 2 min), a method consistent with the American Veterinary Medical Association (AVMA) Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals (2020 edition).

Following euthanasia, blood was collected via cardiac puncture, and serum was clotted at room temperature for 30 minutes, and then centrifuged at 3000 × g for 10 minutes at 4°C. The resulting serum was aliquoted and stored at −80°C until analysis. Full-thickness skin biopsies from the dorsal lesional area, spleens, and other specified tissues (eg, nasal-associated lymphoid tissues, draining lymph nodes) were harvested as described in the corresponding sections. Skin samples were divided for different downstream applications: portions were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for histology and immunofluorescence; portions were placed in RPMI-1640 medium on ice for flow cytometry; and remaining tissues were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C for protein and RNA extraction. All dissection instruments were cleaned between animals to prevent cross-contamination.

No specific humane endpoints were predefined; however, animals were monitored twice daily for signs of distress (eg, >20% weight loss, inability to reach food/water, moribund state). Any animal meeting these criteria would be euthanized immediately; none occurred in this study.

Bacterial Preparation and Viability Confirmation

Streptococcus pyogenes (strain BNCC370002) was commercially obtained from BeiNa Culture Collection (Beijing, China). Bacteria were cultured overnight in Todd-Hewitt broth at 37°C under 5% CO2. The bacterial suspension was washed twice with sterile PBS and adjusted to 1.2 × 108 CFU/10 μL based on OD600 measurements. Viability was confirmed by serial dilution plating on blood agar plates before each experiment, with viability >95% required for use.

Experimental Design and Psoriasiform Dermatitis Modeling

Mice were randomly assigned to experimental groups (n=8 per group/cage, total 40) using the RAND() function in Excel (Microsoft). Block randomization was applied to ensure balanced group sizes. Sample size was determined based on previous IMQ-induced psoriasis models,23,24 which demonstrated that this sample size provides >80% power to detect a 30% difference in PASI scores at α=0.05. A post-hoc power analysis confirmed that the observed effect sizes for our primary endpoints (PASI score, epidermal thickness, IL-17A levels) exceeded the minimum detectable effect size for the sample sizes used. No animals were lost during the study due to technical reasons (eg, failed infection, poor IMQ response, or sample loss). All animals that entered the study reached the experimental endpoint. The allocation and handling of animals followed a CONSORT-style flow: 40 mice were assessed for eligibility → all 40 were randomized → 0 were excluded → 40 completed the study and were analyzed.

Investigators responsible for clinical scoring and histological analysis were blinded to the treatment groups to minimize bias. Control (Ctrl) group: Received sham treatments, including phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) nasal instillation and two applications of Vaseline on the shaved back. Pure psoriasis model-initial flare (PM) group: Subjected to the initial induction of psoriasiform dermatitis (daily topical application of 62.5 mg of 5% imiquimod cream on the shaved backs of mice for 7 consecutive days) on days 40–46. Imiquimod cream was applied to a defined area of approximately 2 cm × 3 cm on the shaved dorsal skin. Relapse (Re) group: Underwent a standard protocol for psoriasis relapse induction (the usage and dosage were consistent with those in the PM group on days 40–46) after the resolution of the initial flare (consistent with those in the PM group on days 1–7). Resolution of the initial flare was defined as PASI score < 2 for 3 consecutive days (Day 7–9). Streptococcus pyogenes infection (SP): Underwent nasal instillation with a suspension of SP (1.2 × 108 CFU in 10 μL of PBS, 5 μL per nostril) on day 35 to simulate a localized upper respiratory infection,23,25 and the initial induction of psoriasiform dermatitis (consistent with that in the PM group) was performed. Relapse with S. pyogenes infection (Re-SP) group: Underwent the relapse protocol (consistent with that in Re group) and nasal instillation of S. pyogenes (consistent with that in the SP group), to model infection-triggered psoriasis relapse. The experimental timeline is detailed in Figure 1A. The results between the different groups need to be compared and analyzed with each other. Treatments were applied at the same time each day to minimize order effects.

Nasal Streptococcus pyogenes infection exacerbates psoriasiform dermatitis in an imiquimod-induced murine relapse model. (A) Schematic illustration of the experimental protocol. Five groups were established: Control (Ctrl), S. pyogenes nasal challenge (SP), imiquimod-induced primary model (PM), relapse model (Re), and relapse combined with SP infection (Re-SP). (B) Representative flow cytometry plots and statistical analysis of dendritic cell (DC) subsets in nasal-associated lymphoid tissue (NALT) following nasal SP challenge, confirming successful regional immune activation.

Exclusion criteria were pre-defined as follows: (1) failure to develop IMQ-induced skin lesions (PASI score < 2 at Day 46); (2) evidence of systemic illness (weight loss >20% or inability to reach food/water); (3) technical failure (eg, sample loss). No animals met these criteria during the study.

On day 40, 2 mice in each group were randomly selected for flow cytometry analysis of nasal lymphoid-related tissues (NALTs, which in mice have a function analogous to that of human tonsils).26 An increase in the proportions of total DCs, MHCII⁺CD86⁺ DCs, and MHCII⁺CD80⁺ DCs was observed, as shown in Figure 1B, indicating that infection was successful, after which psoriasis relapse induction (daily IMQ from day 40 to day 46) was performed.

In vivo Phenotypic Assessment

The severity of psoriasiform inflammation was assessed daily by two independent, blinded investigators using the Psoriasis Area and Severity Index (PASI). Erythema, scaling, and thickening were scored independently on a scale from 0 (none) to 4 (very severe). The cumulative score served as the total PASI.

Following euthanasia (as described in section 2.1), spleens were excised and weighed, and the spleen index was calculated as the ratio of the spleen weight (mg) to the body weight (g).

Histological and Immunohistochemical Analysis

Full-thickness skin biopsies from the lesional back area were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 5 um thickness. The sections were stained with H and E for general histopathological assessment. By using a Leica Aperio CS2 scanner (Leica, Germany), the epidermal thickness was measured from the basal layer to the stratum corneum in ten random, nonoverlapping high-power fields (HPFs, 200x) per sample using ImageJ software.

For CD3 staining, antigen retrieval was performed, and the sections were incubated with an anti-CD3 primary antibody (Abcam, ab135372, 1:200). Detection was performed using a horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibody and DAB chromogen. CD3+ cells were manually counted in five representative HPFs per section.

For Ki67 staining, the sections were incubated with an anti-Ki67 primary antibody (ab15580, Abcam; 1:500) followed by a fluorescently labeled secondary antibody (Abcam, ab150113, 1:500). The nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Ki67+ nuclei in the epidermal basal and suprabasal layers were quantified in five HPFs per section.

Flow Cytometry

Single-cell suspensions were prepared from the NALTs, draining lymph nodes (LNs), and skin. NALT and LN samples were minced through 70 μm mesh to obtain single-cell suspensions. The cells were subsequently washed and resuspended in cell staining buffer. To obtain skin cells, excised skin samples were cut into small pieces and digested with 2 mg/mL collagenase A (Roche, NO. 10103586001) in 2 mL of HBSS at 37°C for 2 hours in a thermoshaker (90 rpm). The skin samples were passed through 40 μm cell strainers (Novbio, NS040) to obtain single-cell suspensions.

For flow cytometry, fluorescence-minus-one (FMO) controls were used for each fluorophore to define positive gates. Single-stained compensation controls were included for each antibody to correct spectral overlap. Cell viability was assessed using Zombie NIR™ Fixable Viability Kit.

The prepared single-cell suspensions were counted by adjusting their cell densities to within 106 cells per 100 uL. Next, 1 uL of Fc blocking buffer (BD Biosciences, NO. 553142, 2.4G2) was added, and cells were then incubated for 10 min in dark. Afterward, 500 uL of Zombie NIR™ Fixable Viability Kit (BioLegend, NO. 423105, 1:1000) was added to cell suspensions to determine their viability. Cells were stained under light-protected conditions for 25 minutes, then washed with 2 mL washing buffer, centrifuged to remove the supernatant, and resuspended. Then, add cell surface staining antibody mixture to each tube, mix thoroughly, and incubate at room temperature away from light for 25 minutes. Cell surface staining antibody mixture was carried out using the following antibodies: Spark Plus UV395-CD45 (BioLegend, Clone 30-F11, 1:100), BV480-CD3 (BD Biosciences, Clone 17A2, 1:100), Super Bright 600- TCRγ/δ (Invitrogen, Clone GL3, 1:100), AF700-CD11b (BioLegend, Clone M1/70, 1:100), BB515-CD11c (BD Biosciences, Clone N418, 1:100), and PerCP.Cy 5.5-MHC II (BioLegend, Clone Clone M5/114.15.2, 1:100), BV421-CD86 (BioLegend, Clone GL1, 1:100), and PE-CY7-CD80 (BioLegend, Clone 16–10A1, 1:100). Add 2 mL of washing buffer to each tube, mix thoroughly, centrifuge, discard the supernatant, and resuspend cells. For intracellular IL-17A staining, cells were fixed, permeabilized (Cytofix/CytopermTM Fixation/Permeabilization Kit, BD Biosciences, 554714) for 10 min, and stained with an BV785-IL-17A antibody (BioLegend, Clone TC11-18H10.1, 1:100) for 30 min. After washing cells with washing buffer, resuspend cells and perform on-machine detection. A detailed account of all the antibodies employed in this study can be found in Supplementary Table-1.

The data were obtained utilizing an Aurora Spectral Flow Cytometer (Cytek) and subsequently analyzed with FlowJo software (v10.8.1). cDCs were identified as CD11c+ CD11b+ cells. Activated DC subsets were defined as MHC-IIhiCD80+ or MHC-IIhiCD86+ cells. γδ T cells were gated as CD3+ TCR γ/δ+ cells, and the frequency of IL-17A-producing cells was determined.

Cytokine Measurement

Skin tissue was homogenized in PBS with protease inhibitors. Levels of IL-17A (Elabscience, NO. E-MSEL-M0006), IL-23 (Elabscience, NO. E-MSEL-M0037), IL-1β (Elabscience, NO. E-MSEL-M0003) in tissue homogenates, and IL-6 (Elabscience, NO. E-MSEL-M0001), CXCL10 (Elabscience, NO. E-EL-M0021) in serum, were quantified using specific enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Absorbance was measured on a microplate reader.

In situ Immunofluorescence and Co-Localization Analysis

To directly visualize the spatial distribution and physical interaction between DCs and γδ T cells within the cutaneous microenvironment, multi-color immunofluorescence staining was performed on formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) skin sections (5 um). Following deparaffinization, rehydration, and heat-induced antigen retrieval, non-specific binding was blocked with 5% normal goat serum. Sections were then incubated overnight at 4°C with a cocktail of primary antibodies: rabbit anti-mouse CD11c (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Clone 14–0114-81, 1:100,) and armenian hamster anti-mouse CD3d (Abcam, ab213362, 1:200). After washing in PBS, sections were incubated in the dark for 1 h at 37°C with secondary antibodies: Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated goat anti- rabbit IgG (Abcam, ab307134, 1:500) and Alexa Fluor 647-conjugated goat against armenian hamster IgG (Abcam, ab173004, 1:500). Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Images were acquired using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Zeiss LSM 880). The accumulation of CD11c+ DCs and CD3d+ γδ T cells was quantified across 5 HPFs per section. Cellular conjugation (co-localization) was defined as distinct pairs where CD11c+ signals and CD3d+ signals exhibited extensive membrane-to-membrane contact, indicative of an immunological synapse-like interaction.

Transcriptomic Analysis

Total RNA was extracted from LNs (n=3 mice per group: Ctrl, Re, and Re-SP) using TRIzol reagent. RNA purity and quantification were evaluated using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, USA). RNA integrity (RIN) was assessed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA); only samples with RIN > 7.0 were used for library preparation. The libraries were subsequently constructed using a VAHTS Universal V10 RNA-seq Library Prep Kit (Premixed Version) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Transcriptome sequencing and analysis were conducted by OE Biotech Co, Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

Library preparation and 150 bp paired-end sequencing were performed on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Reads were aligned to the mouse reference genome using HISAT2. Read alignment rates exceeded 90% for all samples. Differential gene expression analysis between groups was performed using DESeq2 with Benjamini-Hochberg adjusted P < 0.05 and |log2(fold change)| > 1 as the thresholds for significantly differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses were conducted on the DEGs using the R packet ggradar.

Bone Marrow-Derived Dendritic Cell (BMDCs) Culture and in vitro Assays

Bone marrow cells were flushed from the femurs and tibias of C57BL/6J mice (n=3 per group, totally 9) and cultured for 7 days in RPMI-1640 medium (Salbrio, 11875) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, A3160901), 20 ng/mL recombinant mouse IL-4 (PeproTech, NO. 214–14), 20 ng/mL recombinant mouse granulocyte‒macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF; PeproTech, NO. 315–03), and 1% penicillin‒streptomycin (Gibco, 15140122). On day 7, nonadherent and loosely adherent cells were collected as BMDCs. Detailed methods for BMDCs culture and characterization are provided in Supplementary Methods. Purity (CD11c/CD45>90%) was confirmed by flow cytometry; a representative plot is shown in Supplementary Figure S1.

Stimulation: BMDCs were pretreated with 100 ng/mL purified S. pyogenes lipoteichoic acid (LTA; Merk, NO. L3140-5MG) for 6 hours as, with or without an incubation with the TLR2 inhibitor TL2-C29 (25 μM; InvivoGen, Cat.code: inh-c29). TL2-C29 is a specific small-molecule TLR2 inhibitor. Pilot experiments using CCK-8 assay confirmed that 25 μM TL2-C29 did not affect BMDC viability. Additionally, we kept the final DMSO concentration after diluting TL2-C29 below 0.1% to avoid any interference from solvent toxicity.

Group: Ctrl group: BMDCs without LTA and TL2-C29; LTA group: BMDCs with LTA, without TL2-C29; TL2-C29 group: BMDCs without LTA and TL2-C29. The results between the different groups need to be compared and analyzed with each other.

qPCR: Total RNA from BMDCs was reverse transcribed. Gene expression was analyzed using SYBR Green master mix on a QuantStudio system, with the results normalized to those of Gapdh. Relative expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. Primer sequences used for PCR were presented in Supplementary Table-2.

Western Blotting: BMDCs were lysed in RIPA buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA protein assay kit (Shanghai Yamay Biomedical Technology). Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS‒PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes. The membranes were blocked and subsequently incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies (diluted 1:1000) against the following targets: antibodies against phospho-NF-κB p65 (Ser536), total NF-κB p65, phospho-IκBα (Ser36), total IκBα, NOS2, TLR2 were from Abcam, phospho-MLC2 (Thr18/Ser19), total MLC2 were from PeproTech, and phospho- MYPT1 (Thr696), total MYPT1, tubulin, β-actin were from Immunoway. After the membranes were incubated with appropriate secondary antibodies, the protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) substrate and imaged with a chemiluminescence imaging system (Bio-Rad). Band intensity was quantified by densitometric analysis using ImageJ software, with tubulin/β-actin serving as the loading control.

Cellular Metabolic Assay: The cellular metabolic profiles of BMDCs were evaluated using the OCR and ECAR Cell Hypoxia Fluorescence Assay Kits (Elabscience, NO. E-BC-F070 and E-BC-F069), which use fluorescent probes sensitive to changes in oxygen and pH, respectively. BMDCs were seeded in black-walled, clear-bottom 96-well plates. Following the indicated treatments, the culture medium was replaced with the assay buffer supplied in the kits according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The fluorescent signals corresponding to the OCR (indicative of mitochondrial respiration) and ECAR (indicative of glycolytic flux) were measured kinetically using a multimode microplate reader (BioTek Synergy H1) at the excitation/emission wavelengths specified in the kit protocol. Basal metabolic rates were recorded, followed by real-time monitoring of ECAR/OCR changes.

Scratch Wound Healing Migration Assay: A uniform scratch was made in a confluent monolayer of BMDCs using a 200 μL pipette tip. The cells were subsequently washed with PBS and cultured with fresh medium containing LTA with or without TL2-C29. Scratch closure was monitored and quantified at 0 and 24 hours. The migration area was quantified by measuring the reduction in the scratch width (or area) using ImageJ software.

Primary γδ T Cell Isolation and Co-Culture Assays

Primary γδ T cells were isolated from the spleens of naïve C57BL/6J mice (from the batch as experiment 2.6) using a Mouse γδ T Cell Isolation Kit (Miltenyi Biotec, 130–092-125) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, splenocytes were subjected to negative selection via magnetic-activated cell sorting (MACS) to deplete nonγδ T cells. The purity of the isolated γδ T cell population (TCRγδ+/ CD3+>90%) was routinely verified by flow cytometry prior to coculture experiments.27

Transwell Assay: To assess the migratory capacity of activated DCs towards γδ T cells, Transwell assay was performed. Briefly, LTA-pretreated BMDCs (as described above) were resuspended in complete medium and seeded into the upper chamber of a 24-well Transwell insert with an 8.0 μm pore polycarbonate membrane (Corning). Freshly isolated γδ T cells were suspended in complete medium and placed in the lower chamber. The plate was incubated for 6 hours at 37°C. Following incubation, the medium in the upper chamber was carefully removed. Cells that had migrated through the membrane and adhered to the bottom side of the Transwell insert were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 minutes and stained with 0.1% crystal violet solution for 20 minutes. After extensive washing to remove excess dye, the stained cells on the underside of the membrane were imaged using an inverted light microscope. The number of migrated BMDCs was quantified by counting the stained cells in five randomly selected HPFs (200x magnification) per membrane, and the average count was calculated.

Direct Co-culture for Cytokine Production: BMDCs, prepared and characterized as described in the preceding section, were co-cultured with freshly isolated γδ T cells at a stimulator-to-responder ratio of 1:10 in complete RPMI-1640 medium for 24 hours in a 37°C, 5% CO2 incubator. Cell-free supernatants were collected and stored at −80°C until analysis. Concentrations of IL-17A, IL-23, IL-12p70, and nitrite (a stable metabolite of nitric oxide, measured via Griess reagent) were quantified using specific ELISA kits (Elabscience) following the manufacturers’ protocols.

In Situ Visualization of DC-γδ T Cell Conjugates in Skin Tissue: To directly visualize the physical interaction between DCs and γδ T cells within the psoriatic microenvironment, an immunofluorescence co-staining protocol was performed on formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded skin tissue sections from mice in the Ctrl, PM, Re, SP and Re-SP groups. Following antigen retrieval, tissue sections were blocked and simultaneously incubated with a combination of primary antibodies: a rat anti-mouse CD11c antibody (clone 14–0114-81, Thermo Fisher Scientific) to label dendritic cells and a rabbit anti-mouse CD3d antibody (clone ab213362, Abcam) to label γδT cells, overnight at 4°C. After washing, sections were incubated with appropriate secondary antibodies conjugated to distinct fluorophores (eg, Alexa Fluor 488 for CD11c and Alexa Fluor 594 for CD3d). Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Images were acquired using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Zeiss LSM 880). Conjugates were defined as distinct cellular pairs or clusters where CD11c+ and CD3d+ cells were in direct and extensive membrane-to-membrane contact.

In vivo Bioluminescence Imaging for Tracking DC Migration

To dynamically monitor the spatio-temporal migration of DCs in vivo following infection and during psoriasis relapse, CD11c-Luc reporter mice (C57BL/6Smoc-Itgaxem3(IRES-tdTomato-luci)Smoc) were utilized. These transgenic mice specifically express luciferase under the control of the CD11c (Itgax) promoter, allowing for non-invasive longitudinal tracking of CD11c⁺ DCs. The mice were purchased from Shanghai Model Organisms Center, Inc. [License: SCXK (Shanghai) 2024–0007; Cat. NO. NM-KI-190116].

Experimental groups (Ctrl, SP, Re, and Re-SP) were established as described in Section 2.2. Bioluminescence imaging (BLI) was performed at 24 h post-SP infection (Day 36), during the relapse induction phase (Day 42), and Day 45. Mice were intraperitoneally injected with D-luciferin potassium salt [150 mg/kg in sterile dPBS; (MedChemExpress, HY-12591B)]. After 10 min, mice were anesthetized with 2% isoflurane and placed in the dark chamber of the IVIS Spectrum in vivo Imaging System (PerkinElmer, USA). Images were acquired from both prone and supine positions to visualize DC accumulation in the skin and lymph nodes, respectively. Imaging parameters were set as follows: auto-exposure (typically 30–120 s), F/stop = 1, binning = medium, and Field of View (FOV) = D. Anesthesia was induced with 2% isoflurane and maintained with 1.5% isoflurane for approximately 10–15 min per imaging session. D-luciferin injection was administered 10 min prior to imaging. The bioluminescent signals were quantified as total photon flux (photons/sec/cm2/sr) within defined Regions of Interest (ROI) using Living Image software (v.4.4, PerkinElmer). Data were collected from at least three biological replicates per group.

Statistical Analysis

No a priori sample size calculation; sample size (n=6-8 per group) was determined based on previous IMQ-induced psoriasis models, which demonstrated that this sample size provides >80% power to detect a 30% difference in PASI scores at α=0.05. All quantitative data are presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Outliers were identified using Grubbs’ test (α=0.05) for each endpoint. No significant outliers were detected in the final datasets analyzed. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (v9.0). Data were tested for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. For normally distributed data, one-way or two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s or Sidak’s post-hoc test for multiple comparisons was performed. Non-parametric data were analyzed using the Kruskal–Wallis test followed by Dunn’s post-hoc test. For post-hoc comparisons following ANOVA, adjusted P-values using Tukey’s or Sidak’s method are reported. All adjusted P-values are reported alongside raw P-values in the figures and corresponding legends. All experiments were performed with at least three biological replicates. Results

Nasal S. pyogenes Challenge Intensified Cutaneous Psoriasiform Relapse and Systemic Pro-Inflammatory Environment

To investigate the impact of concurrent upper respiratory infection on psoriatic relapse, we established a murine model in which IMQ-induced relapse was combined with the nasal administration of S. pyogenes (Figure 1A). Mice that experienced both psoriasis relapse and nasal S. pyogenes infection were included in the Re-SP group. Mice that experienced only relapse (Re group) served as our main comparison group. PASI scores were significantly higher in Re-SP mice (10.3 ± 0.2) compared to Re group (8.2 ± 0.4), P < 0.001 (Figure 2A). Concomitant with the exacerbated clinical phenotype, histopathological analysis revealed pronounced acanthosis, dermal immune cell infiltrates and increased epidermal thickening in the Re-SP group (Figure 2B and C). The mean difference in epidermal thickness between Re-SP and Re groups was 15.7 μm (95% CI: 7.8–23.6 μm). Moreover, significant keratinocyte proliferation and T-cell infiltration were detected in the Re-SP group. We confirmed increased cellular activity by staining for Ki67 (a cell proliferation marker) and CD3 (a T-cell marker). More proliferating keratinocytes (Figure 2B–D) and more infiltrating T cells (Figure 2B–E) were detected in the skin of Re-SP mice than in the skin of Re or Ctrl mice. This finding indicates a state of heightened tissue and immune activation that characterizes infection-aggravated relapse.

This exacerbated skin pathology was accompanied by a heightened systemic immune response. A significant increase in the spleen index in the Re-SP group compared with that in all other groups was noticed (Figure 2F). Furthermore, the levels of key inflammatory molecules were elevated. In the skin, we detected increased amounts of IL-17A, IL-23, and IL-1. In the blood serum, the levels of IL-6 and CXCL10 were increased (Figure 2G). IL-17A levels were 248 ±96 pg/mL in Re-SP vs 118 ± 52 pg/mL in Re, P < 0.05. These results reveal that a concurrent nasal infection strongly worsens psoriatic relapse, intensifying both local skin inflammation and the systemic inflammatory milieu during psoriatic relapse.

Exacerbated Relapse Was Associated with the Expansion of Activated DCs and Producing IL-17A γδ T Cells in Skin and LNs

Given the central role of the IL-23/IL-17 axis in psoriasis, we applied flow cytometry to analyze immune cells in the skin and LNs. We aimed to identify the cellular drivers of the exacerbated disease seen in the Re-SP group. In both the skin and LNs, we detected a significant increase percentage of conventional dendritic cells (cDCs) in CD45⁺ cells. More importantly, we observed a robust expansion in a specific subset of activated DCs in total cDCs. These cells expressed high levels of MHCII and the costimulatory molecules CD80 or CD86 (Figure 3A and B). The increase was greatest in the Re-SP group compared to the Re and Ctrl groups. Parallel analysis of T cell populations showed that in the Re-SP group, the proportion of γδ T cells among CD3 cells and the proportion of IL-17A-producing γδ T cells among total γδ T cells were significantly and specifically increased. This increase happened in both the skin and the LNs (Figure 3A and C). These data reveal a coordinated expansion of two key cell types only in the infection-worsened relapse. The synchronized expansion of activated MHC-IIhi CD80/86+ cDCs and IL-17A-producing γδ T cells suggest a localized potentiation of the IL-23/IL-17 axis within the draining lymph nodes and lesional skin.

Exacerbated relapse is associated with the expansion of activated cDCs and IL-17A⁺ γδ T cells. (B and C) Comparison of the proportions of cDCs (CD11c⁺ CD11b⁺) among CD45⁺ cells, activated DC subsets (MHC-IIhi CD80/CD86⁺) among total cDCs, γδ T cells (TCRγ/δ⁺) among CD3⁺ cells, and pathogenic IL-17A⁺ γδ T cells among total γδ T cells across the groups. We observed more activated DCs, which are likely producers of IL-23. We also observed more IL-17A-producing γδ T cells. This synergistic expansion provides a direct cellular explanation for the elevated levels of IL-23 and IL-17A we measured. These findings strongly suggest that a reinforced interaction between these two cell types drives aggravated relapse.

Transcriptomic Profiling of LNs Revealed the Activation of the Immune Response, Inflammatory Response, and a Prominent “Cell Migration” Signature

We performed RNA sequencing on the lymph nodes to understand the molecular changes in our model. Our goal was to define the transcriptional programs that underlie exacerbated relapse. The analysis revealed a distinct molecular signature in the Re-SP group. Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed that the dual challenge (Relapse + SP) induced a unique transcriptional landscape distinct from either individual stimulus. (Figure 4A). We then identified the DEGs. We identified that many genes were altered in both the SP group and the Re-SP group compared with those in the Ctrl group. A Venn diagram illustrated the core set of genes whose expression changed in both comparisons (Figure 4B and C). This core set represented the shared molecular response to S. pyogenes. Pathway analysis provided functional insights. GO analysis of these core genes revealed enrichment for expected terms such as “inflammatory response” and “immune response”. We also observed significant enrichment for terms related to cell migration, such as “chemokine receptor activity” and “C-C chemokine binding” (Figure 4D). This core set was predominantly enriched in pathways governing leucocyte chemotaxis and cell-matrix adhesion, highlighting a robust “migratory program” triggered by SP infection. KEGG pathway analysis further supported this finding, revealing the activation of pathways related to “motor proteins”, “cytokine–cytokine receptor interaction” and “chemokine signaling pathway” (Figure 4E).

DEGs were defined as |log2(fold change)| > 1 and adjusted P < 0.05 (Benjamini-Hochberg). (C) Venn diagram displaying the overlap of DEGs between comparisons. A focused look at specific genes confirmed key findings. A heatmap shows the upregulation of expression of genes central to our hypothesis (Figure 4F). These include receptors such as Tlr2, signaling molecules such as Nf-κB and NOS2, and metabolic genes (HK2 and LDHA). Importantly, the expression of many chemokines (Ccl3, Cxcl10, etc.) and genes involved in cell adhesion and motility (Actb and Myh9) also increased. We also observed increased expression of markers for activated DCs (Itgax and Cd86). These transcriptomic data strongly suggest that increased cellular chemotaxis and migration is a key feature of the Re-SP model. These findings suggest that the migration of immune cells, particularly DCs, may be a critical link connecting nasal infection to worsening skin inflammation.

Streptococcal LTA Activated DCs via TLR2, Inducing a Proinflammatory, Glycolytic, and Hypermigratory Phenotype

Our in vivo data revealed that DC reprogramming and migration are key events. We hypothesized that these changes were a direct result of S. pyogenes activation. To test this hypothesis, we studied BMDCs in vitro. We stimulated BMDCs with a key bacterial component, S. pyogenes LTA. We first examined transcription level via qPCR. LTA treatment significantly increased the mRNA levels of activation markers like Tlr2, NOS2, P65, IKKα, and IKKβ (Figure 5A). We also observed a significant increase in the mRNA levels of glycolytic enzymes (HK2, PKM, and LDHA) and a regulator of motility (MYPT1). Critically, these increases were blocked when we pretreated the cells with a TLR2 inhibitor (TL2-C29).

Streptococcal LTA promotes TLR2-dependent metabolic reprogramming and hyper-migration in BMDCs. (A) qPCR analysis of pro-inflammatory, metabolic (HK2, LDHA), and motility-related (MYPT1) genes in BMDCs stimulated with LTA (100 ng/mL) ± TLR2 inhibitor (TL2-C29). (B) Real-time metabolic profiles showing OCR and ECAR. (C) Representative images and quantification of the scratch wound healing assay. Red dashed lines indicate the wound edge at the endpoint (24 h), marking the position to which cells have migrated. Data are mean ± SEM; error bars indicate standard error of the mean. Statistical significance was determined by one-way ANOVA. Data are representative of five independent experiments; *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, **** P < 0.0001 vs Ctrl; #P < 0.05, ##P < 0.01, ###P < 0.001, ####P < 0.0001 vs LTA.

Next, we assessed cellular metabolism. Using fluorescent probes, we measured two key rates: the ECAR and the OCR. LTA stimulation induced a pronounced metabolic shift toward aerobic glycolysis, characterized by elevated ECAR, which serves as the bioenergetic fuel for DC maturation and chemotaxis (Figure 5B). Treatment with a TLR2 inhibitor reduced this metabolic shift.

Finally, we tested a key functional outcome: cell migration. In a scratch wound healing assay, LTA-stimulated BMDCs showed a markedly increased ability to migrate and close the wound (Figure 5C). This increased migration was also dependent on TLR2 signaling, as it was blocked by the inhibitor.

We then confirmed these findings at the protein level. Western blot analysis revealed that LTA signaling through TLR2 activated the NF-κB pathway. LTA-TLR2 signaling orchestrated the canonical NF-κB activation, as evidenced by the phosphorylation of p65 and IκBα (Figure 6). The analysis also revealed increased levels of proteins linked to cell migration (p-MYPT1 and p-MLC). The TLR2 inhibitor TL2-C29 inhibited these changes.

These in vitro results provide mechanistic evidence. They demonstrated that LTA, which acts through TLR2, is sufficient to transform DCs. This transformation gives DCs a proinflammatory gene profile, reprograms their metabolism to favor glycolysis, and greatly increases their ability to move. These are precisely the traits suggested by our in vivo relapse model.

While our in vitro experiments using purified LTA provide mechanistic clarity regarding TLR2-dependent DC activation, we acknowledge that live SP infection involves multiple virulence factors (including superantigens, streptolysin O, and M protein) that may contribute to DC activation and γδ T cell responses in vivo. The relative contribution of LTA versus other SP components remains to be determined.

LTA-Activated DCs Drived IL-17A Production from γδ T Cells and Formed Stable Conjugates in vitro and in vivo

We next asked whether these reprogrammed DCs could engage and activate γδ T cells. We performed a direct coculture assay. We cultured LTA-treated BMDCs with isolated γδ T cells. Primary γδ T cell purity (>90%) was confirmed by flow cytometry prior to co-culture experiments (Figure 7A). LTA-activated DCs triggered substantial release of IL-17A from γδ T cells (Figure 7B). Untreated DCs did not have this effect. Activated DCs themselves also produced high levels of key signaling molecules. These included IL-12p70, IL-23, and NO (Figure 7B and C). All of these effects—IL-17A release and DC cytokine production—were dependent on TLR2 signaling. Crucially, we observed direct physical interaction. LTA-primed DCs formed intimate heterotypic cell conjugates with γδ T cells, reminiscent of the formation of an immunological synapse (Figure 7D). We also tested directional recruitment. Applying Transwell assay, we identified that γδ T cells exhibited potent chemo-attractive properties to activated DCs, suggesting a positive feedback loop (Figure 7E). Mirroring these in vitro findings, immunofluorescence staining of skin sections from Re-SP mice revealed a significantly higher frequency of CD11c+ DCs in direct membrane contact with CD3d+ γδT cells compared to skin from Re or SP mice (Figure 8A).

These findings demonstrate that LTA-activated DCs are both functionally and physically primed to engage with γδ T cells. They can form direct cell-to-cell contacts and stimulate massive IL-17A production, effectively creating a potent inflammatory unit.

In vivo Bioluminescence Imaging Tracks the Dynamic Trafficking of DCs from Lymph Nodes to Skin During SP-Related Relapse

To visually corroborate the migration of DCs from systemic lymphoid tissues to the cutaneous environment following S. pyogenes infection, we employed CD11c-Luc reporter mice for longitudinal in vivo bioluminescence imaging (BLI). At 24 hours post-infection (Day 36), the SP and Re-SP groups exhibited robust bioluminescent signals concentrated in the cervical, axillary, and inguinal lymph node regions (Figure 8B). Notably, diffused bioluminescent signals began to emerge in the dorsal skin of SP and Re-SP mice, corresponding precisely to the localized skin lesions and suggesting the initiation of DC trafficking from LNs to the skin.

By Day 42 (relapse induction phase), the Re-SP group displayed intense bioluminescent signals in the dorsal skin, which were significantly more potent than those observed in the SP and Re groups. Photon flux in the dorsal skin ROI was significantly higher in Re-SP mice (2.4 × 106 ± 0.6 × 106 p/s/cm2/sr) compared to SP group (1.7 × 106 ± 0.4 × 106 p/s/cm2/sr), P< 0.01 (Figure 8B). The signal intensity in the Re-SP group showed a progressive increase compared to Day 36, accompanied by persistent high signals in the draining LNs. These results demonstrate that the combination of SP infection and imiquimod-induced relapse triggers a massive, systemic activation of DCs and markedly enhances their recruitment to the skin compared to either challenge alone.

Collectively, these in vivo findings provide direct visual evidence that nasal SP infection orchestrates the systemic-to-cutaneous migration of DCs in mice, which may contribute to the exacerbation of psoriasis relapse in this preclinical model. However, the translational relevance of these findings to human disease remains to be established.

Discussion

Psoriasis is a complex skin disease influenced by both genetics and environment.28 The established epidemiological link between streptococcal pharyngitis and guttate psoriasis has long been associated with immune dysregulation.29–31 In the present study, we demonstrated that nasal SP infection significantly intensifies psoriatic relapse in mice through a previously unrecognized “nasal-skin” immune axis. Our data provide mechanistic evidence for TLR2-driven metabolic reprogramming and migration of DCs and their subsequent orchestration of the IL-23/IL-17A axis. These findings suggest a model for infection-triggered exacerbation that warrants further investigation in human disease. We also showed directly that BMDCs activated by bacterial component LTA, can stimulate γδ T cells to produce IL-17A. These findings align with those of modern models of psoriasis, in which IL-23 from certain immune cells drives IL-17 production.32,33

A pivotal finding of our study is the bioenergetic shift in DCs upon TLR2 ligation. The transition from oxidative phosphorylation to aerobic glycolysis—reminiscent of the Warburg effect—is not merely a metabolic byproduct but a functional requirement for DC maturation and hyper-mobility.34 This glycolytic switch provides the rapid ATP supply and biosynthetic precursors necessary for DC trafficking from the nasal mucosa to distant cutaneous sites.35

Furthermore, our findings are further substantiated by emerging clinical evidence. We detected an increase in activated (MHC-IIhiCD86+/CD80+) DCs in the skin and LNs in our infection relapse model. This finding mirrors the accumulation of mature DCs and HLA DR+ cells seen in human guttate psoriasis.36,37 The observed metabolic reprogramming and increased migratory capacity in LTA-activated BMDCs align with recent reports on metabolic dysregulation in human psoriatic lesions. Previous studies in human psoriatic lesions have suggested heightened glycolytic activity in DCs,38,39 though human validation was not performed in the present study. The congruence between our murine model and published human tissue profiling is suggestive but remains speculative until directly tested in patient samples. This congruence between our murine model and human tissue profiling strengthens the translational relevance of the TLR2-DC metabolism axis in psoriatic pathogenesis, particularly in the context of infection-triggered exacerbation.40

Moreover, our study provides new mechanistic details that go beyond confirming these links. First, we offer evidence for a specific alternative pathway. The leading theory for infection-triggered flares is “molecular mimicry”, where bacterial proteins accidentally activate skin-reactive T cells. Our work revealed that a different TLR2-dependent pathway is also involved. Our data indicate that TLR2 engagement by purified LTA can initiate the disease cascade in a susceptible host. Second, we connected this initial signal to a profound change inside the DCs. When stimulated by TLR2, DCs switched their metabolism to rely more on glycolysis. This metabolic reprogramming is not just a side effect, as it likely provides the energy and building blocks needed for DCs to produce more inflammatory signals and to migrate more effectively.16,41,42 These findings highlight the importance of immunometabolism in the study of psoriasis relapse. Third, we identified key cellular interactions. Beyond soluble mediators like IL-23, we identified that physical conjugation—potentially forming a non-canonical immunological synapse—between metabolically primed DCs and skin-resident γδ T cells are essential for the amplification of IL-17A production. This heterotypic cell-to-cell interaction underscores the complexity of the innate-adaptive interface during infection-triggered flares. This provides a more precise cellular story than simply referring to the broader Th17 response.43,44

Beyond the TLR2-LTA axis identified here, other components, including streptolysin O, M protein, and superantigens, may also contribute to psoriasis relapse. Streptococcal superantigens, including pyrogenic exotoxins and M protein, can polyclonally activate T cells independent of antigen specificity. Molecular mimicry between streptococcal antigens and skin proteins may sustain chronic T cell responses. While our in vitro experiments demonstrate that LTA alone is sufficient to trigger TLR2-dependent DC activation, it remains to be determined whether LTA is the dominant TLR2 ligand in vivo during live SP infection. Future studies using isogenic SP mutants deficient in specific virulence factors (eg, ΔltaS, Δslo, ΔspeA) would help clarify the relative contributions of each component to DC activation and psoriasis relapse. Additionally, the potential role of streptococcal superantigens in polyclonally activating γδ T cells independent of DC-TLR2 signaling warrants further investigation.

Additionally, SP infection may alter the nasopharyngeal microbiome, which could influence systemic immune tone. While our study focused on the LTA/TLR2 pathway, it is likely that multiple mechanisms operate in parallel during natural infection. Future studies should investigate the relative contribution of each pathway using isogenic SP mutants and comprehensive microbiological analyses.

In summary, our work confirms the importance of the IL-23/IL-17 axis but reframes how it is triggered. We propose a model in which an infection is sensed through the innate immune receptor TLR2. This sensing leads to metabolic rewiring of DCs. These altered DCs has high migratory ability and then directly activate resident γδ T cells in the skin. This model complements the mimicry hypothesis, suggesting that multiple ways for an infection to cause a psoriasis flare coexist.

The TLR2-dependent innate pathway identified here does not exclude the molecular mimicry hypothesis; rather, these two mechanisms may operate synergistically. TLR2-driven DC activation and migration could provide the initial inflammatory milieu that facilitates subsequent T cell cross-reactivity, while molecular mimicry may sustain chronic inflammation once established. The rapid onset of innate-driven flares may be followed by the more sustained adaptive responses driven by molecular mimicry, potentially explaining both the rapid and chronic phases of infection-triggered psoriasis relapse.

The mechanism we identified has practical implications for psoriasis management, particularly for patients on modern biologics. Patients taking IL-17/IL-23 inhibitors are still at risk for respiratory infections. Our research highlights new proactive strategies for their care. First, it underscores the need for active infection management. Vigilant screening and prompt treatment of S. pyogenes throat infections are critical.31 This should be viewed not only as treating an infection but also as preventing a potential psoriasis flare. Second, it reveals new potential drug targets to prevent these flares.28 From a translational perspective, our findings advocate for “flare-interception” strategies. Targeting early upstream events, such as inhibiting DC-specific glycolytic enzymes (eg, HK2) or blocking TLR2 signaling, could theoretically prevent exacerbations without inducing the systemic immunosuppression associated with long-term biologic use. This is particularly relevant for patients on IL-17/23 inhibitors who remain vulnerable to respiratory triggers.42 The goal of such treatments would be to neutralize the bacterial trigger without broadly suppressing the immune system. This approach could be used as a short-term preventive measure when a patient has a known respiratory infection. Third, it suggests a way to block key cellular interactions. We showed that activated DCs form direct connections with γδ T cells in the skin. Disrupting this cellular synapse is another potential intervention point. For example, drugs that block the adhesion molecules holding these cells together could be explored. In essence, this work suggests potential avenues for developing flare-interception strategies that warrant further investigation. Their goal would be to help patients maintain remission, even when they face challenge with an infection.

Our findings may apply beyond those concerning psoriasis. Other respiratory pathogens can worsen similar diseases. For example, pathogens such as Staphylococcus aureus or certain viruses also activate innate immune receptors. They might worsen other IL-17-driven conditions, such as axial spondyloarthritis or hidradenitis suppurativa.45 These diseases can be aggravated through similar mechanisms. This process might involve metabolic reprogramming of key immune cells. This connection could reveal shared target for new treatments.

This study also calls for an update to the theory of psoriasis relapse. We propose a new, step-by-step model. 1) Mucosal triggering: A nasal S. pyogenes infection occurs. Bacterial components engage the TLR2 receptor on DCs in the area or in circulation. 2) DC reprogramming: TLR2 signaling activates the cell and turns on proinflammatory genes via the NF-κB pathway. It also switches the cell’s metabolism to glycolysis. This gives the DC a hyperactive, mobile state. 3) Migration and signaling: These reprogrammed DCs travel and migrate to the draining lymph nodes and to the skin. There, they can present antigens and secrete the cytokine IL-23. 4) Effector cell activation: IL-23, along with other signals, activates specific skin-resident immune cells, such as γδ T cells. This activation drives them to produce large amounts of IL-17A. 5) Inflammation amplification: IL-17A acts on keratinocytes. This action causes epidermal hyperplasia.32 It also creates a chemokine gradient that draws more immune cells into the area. Within skin lesions, DCs and γδ T cells form stable physical connections. These conjugates help perpetuate the ongoing inflammation. This new model highlights two key concepts; it highlights the importance of innate immune cell metabolism and emphasizes the specific cellular partnership between DCs and γδ T cells as critical control points in the disease process. Despite these insights, several limitations warrant consideration. First, while the IMQ-induced model recapitulates key features of human psoriasis, its acute nature may differ from the chronic, recalcitrant cycles seen in patients. Our study timeframe was sufficient to observe acute relapse and early immune-cell migration events, but it may not be adequate to assess chronic relapse, or long-term immune memory. Second, the study lacked female mice, validation in TLR2-KO animals and TLR2-KO BMDCs, which limits the generalizability of the findings. Third, the use of purified LTA and a chemical inhibitor-based approach provide clarity but simplifies the complexity of a live SP infection, which involves a secretion of superantigens and toxins. Otherwise, while the RNA-seq data reveal a prominent “cell migration” signature, we did not directly track nasal mucosa-origin DCs using CFSE or other lineage-tracing approaches, nor did we demonstrate that blocking DC migration (eg, using CCR7 antagonists) alters relapse severity. Such experiments would strengthen the causal link between DC trafficking and disease exacerbation and represent important directions for future studies. Additionally, the causal role of glycolysis in DC migration was not directly tested using glycolytic inhibitors (eg, 2-DG) in this study. From a translational perspective, our findings suggest that targeting early upstream events—such as inhibiting DC-specific glycolytic enzymes (eg, HK2) or blocking TLR2 signaling—could theoretically prevent exacerbations without inducing systemic immunosuppression. However, this remains a hypothesis that requires testing in well-designed preclinical and clinical studies. Further studies using topical 2-DG, HK2 inhibitors, or TLR2 antagonists in the IMQ relapse model are needed to validate this approach before any clinical translation can be considered. Meanwhile, while we demonstrated DC-γδ T cell conjugate formation, we did not perform Transwell separation experiments with 0.4 μm pores to determine whether direct cell contact is strictly required versus soluble factors alone. This represents an important direction for future studies. Such experiments are needed to establish causality and will be addressed in future investigations. Nasal SP infection may also alter the local microbiome composition, which could serve as a confounding factor in our model. The potential impact of microbiome changes on DC activation and psoriasis relapse was not investigated in this study and represents an important avenue for future research. While our findings suggest a potential strategy, the feasibility and safety of targeting DC-specific glycolysis in humans requires extensive preclinical investigation. It is also crucial to design and conduct studies in human patient cohorts to validate our findings.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the study reveals a novel immune-metabolic mechanism where nasal S. pyogenes infection triggers psoriasis relapse via TLR2-dependent signaling, with streptococcal LTA recognition inducing aerobic glycolysis in mucosal DCs, promoting their maturation, trafficking to the skin, and driving γδ T cells IL-17A release. These findings suggest that the DC-metabolism-migration axis represents a potential therapeutic target for preventing infection-induced psoriasis recurrence. However, given the preclinical nature of this study, further validation using human samples, chronic disease models, and clinical cohorts is required to confirm the translational relevance of these findings. Future studies using live SP infection models, TLR2-KO mice, and DC-specific metabolic inhibitors will be essential to establish causality and explore therapeutic applications.
