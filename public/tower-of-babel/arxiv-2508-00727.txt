Abstract
. We prove that for a bifibration P P between small categories, the length of the cup product in the kernel of the induced morphism P ∗ {P^{*}} in the Baues-Wirsching cohomology with coefficients in any natural system is a lower bound for the homotopic sectional category (also called Švarc genus) of P P . Our results extend classical Švarc-type inequalities to the categorical setting and introduce a computationally efficient method via a reduced cochain complex for Baues–Wirsching cohomology.



Introduction

The homotopic sectional category (or Švarc genus) of a continuous map
f\colon X\to Y
is a well-known homotopy invariant which, in an informal sense, measures how far is
Y
from being dominated by
X
through
f
. Formally, the
homotopic sectional category
or
Švarc genus
of
f
is the least integer
n\geq 0
such that there is a cover
\{U_{0},\ldots,U_{n}\}
of
Y
by open subspaces that admit local homotopic sections. Specifically, for every
i\in\{0,\ldots,n\}
there is a continuous map
s_{i}\colon U_{i}\rightarrow X
that satisfies
f\circ s_{i}\simeq\iota_{i}
where
\iota_{i}\colon U_{i}\rightarrow Y
is the inclusion (see
[
19
]
). If we impose that the maps
s_{i}\colon U_{i}\rightarrow X
are true sections (not only up to homotopy), then we define the sectional category of the map
f
. Both notions are equivalent for fibrations. The homotopic sectional category of a map is not only significant in itself but also generalizes two other important notions: namely the LS-category (
[
8
]
) and the topological complexity (
[
9
]
).

The study of topological invariants of small and acyclic categories has recently gained importance in different areas of research such as Computational Topology, Geometry, Combinatorics and simplicial methods. This importance stems, among other factors, from the suitability of such structures for developing algebraic combinatorial models that reflect interactions between different objects offering complexity and flexibility that are not achievable by simplicial complexes or preorders (see, for example,
[
6
,
10
]
).

The LS-category and the topological
complexity were studied in the context of small categories in
[
16
]
and
[
13
]
. In this work, we undertake the task of defining and studying the notions of sectional category and homotopic sectional category (that we call the Švarc genus) in the context of small categories.

In the context of topological spaces, the length of the cup product of the kernel of the induced morphism
p^{*}\colon\mathrm{H}(B)\to\mathrm{H}(E)
in cohomology serves as a lower bound for the Švarc genus of a fibration
p\colon E\to B
(see
[
19
]
). This inequality provides excellent lower bounds for computing the LS-category and the topological complexity of spaces.

In the context of small categories, Baues and Wirsching introduced a cohomology theory with coefficients in a natural system
D
endowed with an endopairing (
[
3
,
5
]
). Our main result in this work is the following Švarc type inequality in the context of small categories for Baues-Wirsching cohomology: the length of the cup product in
\ker\mathrm{H}(P;D)
is a lower bound for the Švarc genus of
P
. Moreover, we develop a computational improvement for such cohomology by means of a
reduced
cochain complex which greatly simplifies and facilitates the computations.

The paper is organized in two sections. In Section
1
we further develop the notion of relative Baues-Wirsching cohomology
[
4
]
and introduce the novel notion of relative cup product in order to define cup length in this context. Furthermore, we introduce an improvement in the computation of Baues-Wirsching cohomology by introducing a
reduced
cochain complex. To improve readability and ensure the exposition is self-contained, we have included

some preliminaries on Baues-Wirsching cohomology from scratch
and a fully detailed example (Subsection
1.7
).

Section
2
deals with the following topic. First, we prove some properties of bifibrations and we highlight a remarkable family of bifibrations: coverings of small categories. Second, we introduce the original notions of sectional category and Švarc genus of a functor and prove that both notions coincide for bifibrations (mirroring what happens in the topological context). Finally, we prove the main result of this work: Theorem
2.18
. This result is a Svarc inequality for bifibrations which claims that the length of the cup product of the bifibration (a purely algebraic invariant) is a lower bound for its Svarc genus.

In this paper we will assume that all categories are small unless stated otherwise. Moreover if
\mathcal{E}
is a category, we will denote by
\mathcal{E}
its set of objects, by
\mathrm{Mor}(\mathcal{E})
the morphisms of
\mathcal{E}
and by
\mathcal{E}(e_{1},e_{2})
the set of arrows between the objects
e_{1},e_{2}\in\mathcal{E}
.

1.2.

Natural systems will be the coefficients of Baues-Wirching cohomology of small categories (see

Remark 1.4
.

Every morphism

D(\alpha,\beta)=D((\mathrm{id}_{D},\beta)\circ(\alpha,\mathrm{id}_{A}))=D(\mathrm{id}_{D},\beta)\circ D(\alpha,\mathrm{id}_{A}).

We will denote by
\alpha_{*}
and

D(\alpha,\mathrm{id}_{A})

and any natural system

in
\mathcal{D}

in

Proposition 1.7
.

be a natural system in

.

(2)

G^{*}\colon\mathrm{H}^{n}(\mathcal{E};D)\rightarrow\mathrm{H}^{n}(\mathcal{D};G^{*}D)
.

(3)

(G\circ F)^{*}\colon\mathrm{H}^{n}(\mathcal{E};D)\rightarrow\mathrm{H}^{n}(\mathcal{C};(G\circ F)^{*}D)

,

and

are the same as a consequence of functoriality. Then
(G\circ F)^{*}=F^{*}\circ G^{*}
.

First, we have

(G\circ F)^{*}D=D\circ\widehat{(G\circ F)}=D\circ\widehat{G}\circ\widehat{F}=G^{*}D\circ(\widehat{F})=F^{*}(G^{*}(D)).

Take any chain
(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n})
in

and any cochain

\displaystyle F^{*}(G^{*}(f))(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n}))

\displaystyle=G^{*}(f)(F(\lambda_{1})\circ\cdots\circ F(\lambda_{n}))

\displaystyle=f((G\circ F)(\lambda_{1}),\dots,(G\circ F)(\lambda_{n})).

So the morphisms in cohomology are also the same.
∎

1.3.
Relative Cohomology

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a small category,
D
a natural system on
\mathcal{C}
and let
\iota\colon\mathcal{U}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{C}
be a subcategory of
\mathcal{C}
. Recall that we have a natural system
\iota^{*}D
in
\mathcal{U}
by the composition
D\circ\hat{\iota}
. For each integer

the inclusion induces a morphism

\iota^{n*}\colon\mathcal{F}^{n}(\mathcal{C};D)\rightarrow\mathcal{F}^{n}(\mathcal{U},\iota^{*}D)
.

Definition 1.8
.

For each non-negative integer
n\geq 0
we define the group of cochains of
(\mathcal{C};D)
relative to
\mathcal{U}
, denoted by
\mathcal{F}^{n}(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{U};D)
, as the kernel of the morphism

.

Proposition 1.9
.

The groups
\mathcal{F}^{n}(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{U};D)
form a cochain subcomplex of
\mathcal{F}{\bullet}(\mathcal{C};D)
.

Proof.

We must only prove that if
f
is in
\mathcal{F}^{n-1}(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{U};D)
then
\delta(f)
is in
\mathcal{F}^{n}(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{U};D)
. Take any
n
-chain
(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})
in
\mathcal{U}
, then the
n-1
chains
(\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{n})
,
(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{i}\circ\lambda_{i+1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})
and
(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n-1})
are in

because
\mathcal{U}
is a subcategory, hence

vanish in them.

So

\displaystyle\delta(f)(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})=

\displaystyle\lambda_{1*}f(\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{n})

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}(-1)^{i}f(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{i}\circ\lambda_{i+1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})

\displaystyle+(-1)^{n}\lambda_{n}^{*}f(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n-1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda_{1*}(0)+(-1)^{n}\lambda_{n}^{*}(0)=0.\qed

1.4.
Cup product

Let
D,D^{\prime},D^{\prime\prime}
be three natural systems in the category
\mathcal{C}
. A
pairing

\mu\colon(D;D^{\prime})\rightarrow D^{\prime\prime}
is a natural way to associate each
2
-chain
(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
with a homomorphism

\mu_{(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})}\colon D(\lambda_{1})\otimes D(\lambda_{2})\rightarrow D(\lambda_{1}\circ\lambda_{2}).

We will denote by
x\cdot y
the element
\mu(x\otimes y)
. Moreover, the association is natural in the sense that it satisfies the following three identities (
[
3
, Def. 5.19]
):

(1)

(g^{*}x)\cdot y=x\cdot(g_{*}y)
,

(2)

f_{*}(x\cdot y)=(f_{*}x)\cdot y
,

(3)

h^{*}(x\cdot y)=x\cdot(h^{*}y)
.

Remark 1.11
.

If the three natural systems are the same system
D
the pairing
\mu\colon(D;D)\rightarrow D
is called an
endopairing
.

Consider a pairing
\mu\colon(D;D^{\prime})\rightarrow D^{\prime\prime}
. The
absolute cup product

\smile\colon\mathrm{H}^{n}(\mathcal{C};D)\otimes\mathrm{H}^{m}(\mathcal{C};D^{\prime})\rightarrow\mathrm{H}^{n+m}(\mathcal{C};D^{\prime\prime})

is defined by first defining the homomorphism on cochains that takes
f\otimes g
into
f\smile g
given by

(f\smile g)(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n},\lambda_{n+1},\dots,\lambda_{n+m})=f(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n})\cdot g(\lambda_{n+1},\dots,\lambda_{n+m})

and then defining a morphism in cohomology:

\{f\}\smile\{g\}=\{f\smile g\}
. This is well defined since we have the following lemma.

Proposition 1.12

(
[
3
, Lemma 5.20]
)
.

The cup product satisfies:

(2)

\delta(f\smile g)=(\delta f)\smile g+(-1)^{n}f\smile(\delta g)

where
f
is an
n
-cochain.

By an inductive argument, we can extend Identity (
2
).

Proposition 1.13
.

Let
f_{1},\dots,f_{n}
be cochains of degrees
m_{1},\dots,m_{n}
respectively, then:

(3)

\delta(f_{1}\smile\dots\smile f_{n})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(-1)^{N_{i}}f_{1}\smile\dots\smile(\delta f_{i})\smile\dots\smile f_{n}

where
N_{1}=0
and
N_{i+1}=\sum_{j=1}^{i}m_{J},1\leq i\leq n
.

Now we will prove that the cup product behaves well when we have a functor
F\colon\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathcal{D}
between two categories.

Proposition 1.14
.

Let
F\colon\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathcal{D}
be a functor between two small categories and let
D_{1},D_{2},D_{3}
be three natural systems in
\mathcal{D}
with a pairing
\mu:(D_{1},D_{2})\rightarrow D_{3}
. We have a pairing
F^{*}\mu:(F^{*}D_{1},F^{*}D_{2})\rightarrow F^{*}D_{3}
. Let

F_{i}^{*}\colon\mathrm{H}(\mathcal{D};D_{i})\rightarrow\mathrm{H}(\mathcal{C},F^{*}D_{i}),\quad i=1,2,3,

be the morphisms induced by
F
in cohomology.
We claim that

F_{3}^{*}(\{f\}\smile\{g\})=F_{1}^{*}(\{f\})\smile F_{2}^{*}(\{g\})

for the absolute cup product determined by
F^{*}\mu
.

Proof.

The existence of the pairing
F^{*}\mu
follows from the fact that if
(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})\in N_{2}(\mathcal{C})
is a
2
-chain then we have a morphism

F^{*}\mu_{(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})}:F^{*}D_{1}(\lambda_{1})\otimes F^{*}D_{2}(\lambda_{2})\rightarrow F^{*}D_{3}(\lambda_{1}\circ\lambda_{2})

given by

F^{*}\mu_{(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})}=\mu_{(F(\lambda_{1}),F(\lambda_{2})}.

Take any elements
f\in\mathcal{F}^{n}(\mathcal{D},D_{1})
and
g\in\mathcal{F}^{m}(\mathcal{D},D_{2})
, we have:

\displaystyle F_{3}^{*}(f\smile g)(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n+m})

\displaystyle=(f\smile g)(F(\lambda_{1}),\ldots,F(\lambda_{n+m}))

\displaystyle=f(F(\lambda_{1}),\ldots,F(\lambda_{n}))\cdot g(F(\lambda_{n+1}),\ldots,F(\lambda_{n+m}))

\displaystyle=F_{1}^{*}f(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})\cdot F^{*}_{2}g(\lambda_{n+1},\ldots,\lambda_{n+m}).\qed

1.5.
Relative cup product and cup-length

A family of subcategories
\{\mathcal{U}_{0},\ldots,\mathcal{U}_{n}\}
of the category
\mathcal{C}
is a
geometric cover
if for every chain in
\mathcal{C}
there is some
i\in\{0,\ldots,n\}
such that the chain belongs to
\mathcal{U}_{i}
.

Definition 1.15
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a category and let
\mathcal{U}
and
\mathcal{V}
be two subcategories. We define the subcategory
\mathcal{U}\cup\mathcal{V}
as the smallest subcategory of
\mathcal{C}
that contains all the objects and morphisms of both
\mathcal{U}
and
\mathcal{V}
, as well as all their compositions.

Proposition 1.16
.

Suppose that we have a geometric cover
\{\mathcal{U}_{0},\dots,\mathcal{U}_{n}\}
of
\mathcal{U}=\mathcal{U}_{0}\cup\ldots\cup\mathcal{U}_{n}
. Then we can define a cup product

\smile\colon\mathrm{H}^{p_{0}}(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{U}_{0};D)\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathrm{H}^{p_{n}}(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{U}_{n};D)\rightarrow\mathrm{H}^{p}(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{U};D)

where
p=p_{0}+\dots+p_{n}
.

Proof.

For
i\geq 1
we will denote by
P_{i}
the sum
p_{0}+\cdots+p_{i-1}
.

Since we have a geometric cover we have a morphism

(4)

\smile\colon\mathcal{F}^{p_{0}}(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{U}_{0};D)\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathcal{F}^{p_{n}}(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{U}_{n};D)\rightarrow\mathcal{F}^{p}(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{U};D)

because the cochain
f_{0}\smile\dots\smile f_{n}
vanishes in every chain in
\mathcal{U}
. Indeed, for any
p
-chain
(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{p})
we have that

(f_{0}\smile\dots\smile f_{n})(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{p})=f_{0}(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{p_{0}})\cdots f_{n}(\lambda_{P_{n}+1},\dots,\lambda_{p}).

But since the chain
(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{p})
is in
\mathcal{U}
and we have a geometric cover, the chain belongs to some
\mathcal{U}_{i}
. Hence the subchain
(\lambda_{P_{i}+1},\dots,\lambda_{P_{i+1}})
is in
\mathcal{U}_{i}
, so
f_{i}((\lambda_{P_{i}+1},\dots,\lambda_{P_{i+1}})=0
and the cup-product also vanish.

Moreover the morphism (
4
) is well defined in cohomology because Formula (
3
) ensures that, afeter a tedious computation, that if we have two sequences
f_{0},\dots,f_{n}
and
f^{\prime}_{0},\dots,f^{\prime}_{n}
such that
\{f_{i}\}=\{f^{\prime}_{i}\}
then

\{f_{0}\smile\dots\smile f_{n}\}=\{f^{\prime}_{0}\smile\dots\smile f^{\prime}_{n}\}.

In the case of two elements the proof goes as follows.
If we have four cochains
f,f^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}^{n}(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{U}_{0};D)
and
g,g^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}^{n}(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{U}_{1};D)
such that
\{f\}=\{f^{\prime}\}
and
\{g\}=\{g^{\prime}\}
, we will prove that
\{f\smile g\}=\{f^{\prime}\smile g^{\prime}\}
.

As we know
f=f^{\prime}+\delta h_{1}
and
g=g^{\prime}+\delta h_{2}
with
h_{1}\in\mathcal{F}^{n-1}(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{U}_{0};D)
and
h_{2}\in\mathcal{F}^{m-1}(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{U}_{1};D)
, by using the fact that
\delta
is a derivation (Formula (
2
)) we have

\displaystyle(f\smile g)(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n},\lambda_{n+1},\ldots\lambda_{n+m})

\displaystyle=f(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})\cdot g(\lambda_{n+1},\ldots,\lambda_{n+m})

\displaystyle=(f^{\prime}+\delta h_{1})(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})\cdot(g^{\prime}+\delta h_{2})(\lambda_{n+1},\ldots,\lambda_{n+m})

\displaystyle=f^{\prime}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})\cdot g^{\prime}(\lambda_{n+1},\ldots,\lambda_{n+m})

\displaystyle\quad+f^{\prime}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})\cdot\delta h_{2}(\lambda_{n+1},\ldots,\lambda_{n+m})

\displaystyle\quad+\delta h_{1}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})\cdot g^{\prime}(\lambda_{n+1},\ldots,\lambda_{n+m})

\displaystyle\quad+\delta h_{1}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})\cdot\delta h_{2}(\lambda_{n+1},\ldots,\lambda_{n+m})

\displaystyle=(f^{\prime}\smile g^{\prime}+f^{\prime}\smile\delta h_{2}+\delta h_{1}\smile g^{\prime}+\delta h_{1}\smile h_{2})(\lambda_{1},\ldots\lambda_{n+m}).

But we also have that

(1)

\delta(f\smile h_{2})=\delta f\smile h_{2}+(-1)^{n}f\smile\delta h_{2}=(-1)^{n}f\smile\delta h_{2}
, since
\delta f=0
;

(2)

\delta(h_{1}\smile g)=\delta h_{1}\smile g+(-1)^{n-1}h_{1}\smile\delta g=\delta h_{1}\smile g
, since
\delta g=0
;

(3)

\delta(h_{1}\smile\delta h_{2})=\delta h_{1}\smile\delta h_{2}+(-1)^{n-1}h_{1}\smile\delta\delta h_{2}=\delta h_{1}\smile\delta h_{2}
, since
\delta\delta=0
.

Therefore

f\smile g=f^{\prime}\smile g^{\prime}+\delta((-1)^{n}f\smile h_{2}+h_{1}\smile g+h_{1}\smile\delta h_{2}).\qed

1.6.
A computational improvement

We develop a computationally cheaper way to compute the Baues-Wirsching cohomology of a small category (Definition
1.6
). More precisely, we show how to obtain the cohomology from two different projective resolutions of the constant functor
\mathbb{Z}\colon\mathcal{F}\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\textbf{{Ab}}
(see Definition
1.19
and Corollary
1.32
). On the one hand, by applying a derived functor to the first resolution, we recover the cochain complex
(\mathcal{F}*(\mathcal{C};D),\delta)
defined by Baues and Wirsching. On the other hand, by applying the derived functor to the second resolution, we obtain a
reduced
cochain complex
\tilde{F}^{*}(\mathcal{C};D),\tilde{\delta})
satisfying
H^{*}(\tilde{\mathcal{F}}^{*}(\mathcal{C};D),\tilde{\delta})\cong H^{*}(\mathcal{F}^{*}(\mathcal{C};D),\delta)
. The reduced cochain complex is the subcomplex of the cochain cochain complex
F^{*}(\mathcal{C};D)
given by the cochains
f
such that
f(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n})=0
if
\lambda_{i}=1
(
\lambda_{i}
is an identity) for any
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
. An element
f\in F^{*}(\mathcal{C};D)
will be referred as a
non degenerate
cochain.

Recall that for a small category
\mathcal{C}
, the category of functors
[\mathcal{F}\mathcal{C},\textbf{{Ab}}]
is an abelian category with enough projectives and injectives
[
7
, Section 1.1]
. It is known that for every natural system
D\colon\mathcal{F}\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\textbf{{Ab}}
we have that
\varprojlim D\simeq\mathrm{Nat}(\mathbb{Z},D)\simeq\mathrm{H}^{0}(\mathcal{C},D)

[
7
, Section 1.2]
. Hence, we can define the right derived functor with an injective resolution:

0\rightarrow D\rightarrow I_{0}\rightarrow\dots\rightarrow I_{n}\rightarrow\dots

and compute the cohomology of the chain complex obtained by applying
\mathrm{Nat(\mathbb{Z},-)}.

Dually, we can begin with a projective resolution of
\mathbb{Z}
:

0\leftarrow\mathbb{Z}\leftarrow P_{0}\leftarrow\dots\leftarrow P_{n}\leftarrow\dots,

then we can apply
\mathrm{Nat}(-,D)
to obtain a cochain complex, and finally, we can compute its cohomology.

is

2.
Bifibrations, Švarc genus and sectional category

In this section, we recall the notion of a bifibration, state and prove some of its properties, and provide some relevant examples. After that, we introduce the notions of Švarc genus and sectional category of a functor, and we prove some of their properties and the main result of the paper.

2.1.
Homotopy in small categories

We begin by recalling the notion of homotopy in small categories as introduced by Lee (
[
11
,
12
]
) and by establishing some notation.

The
interval category
\mathbb{I}_{m}
of length
m\geq 0
consists of
m+1
objects with zigzag arrows,

0\longrightarrow 1\longleftarrow 2\longrightarrow\cdots\longrightarrow(\longleftarrow)m.

Given two small categories
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{D}
we denote its product by
\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{D}
. Let
F,G\colon\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{D}
be two functors between small categories. We say that
F
and
G
are
homotopic
, denoted by
F\simeq G
, if, for some
m\geq 0
, there exists a functor
H\colon\mathcal{C}\times\mathbb{I}_{m}\rightarrow\mathcal{D}
, called a homotopy (of length
m
), such that
H_{0}=F
and
H_{m}=G
.

.

2.5.
Coverings

In this subsection we prove that coverings of small categories are examples of bifibrations.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a small category and let
C
be an object of
\mathcal{C}
. We define the sets:

\mathcal{U}_{C}=\{f\in\mathrm{Mor\,}\mathcal{C}\,\colon\mathrm{Codom}(f)=C\}

and

\mathcal{F}_{C}=\{f\in\mathrm{Mor\,}\mathcal{C}\,\colon\mathrm{Dom}(f)=C\}.

In other words,
\mathcal{U}_{C}
is the set of morphisms that have
C
as their target and
\mathcal{F}_{C}
is the set of morphisms that have
C
as its source .

Any functor
P\colon\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{D}
between small categories induces maps
P_{\mathcal{U}_{C}}\colon\mathcal{U}_{C}\rightarrow\mathcal{U}_{P(C)}
and
P_{\mathcal{F}_{C}}\colon\mathcal{F}_{C}\rightarrow\mathcal{F}_{P(C)}
. Both maps are defined by the property that if we have a morphism
f
with domain or codomain
C
then the morphism
P(f)
has the object
P(C)
as its domain or codomain.

Definition 2.10
.

[
2
]

A functor
P\colon\mathcal{E}\rightarrow\mathcal{B}
is a

if its surjective on objects and for every object

the morphisms

and
P_{\mathcal{F}_{C}}
are bijective.

Proposition 2.11
.

Every covering is a bifibration.

Proof.

First let us see that

is a fibration. Let

be a morphism in

. If we fix a element

such that

, we can lift

using the fact that

is a bijection between

and

. We will denote that lift of

by

and the source of this map by
\widehat{C}
, so

. Now suppose that we have the following situation :

2.6.
Švarc genus and sectional category

The aim of this section is to introduce for the first time the notions of sectional category and Švarc genus of a functor; to show that they coincide for bifibrations; to prove Theorem
2.18
and to show it in an example.

Definition 2.13
.

Let
P\colon\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathcal{D}
be a functor. We define its
sectional category
, denoted by
\mathrm{sc}(P)
, as the least integer
n\geq 0
such that there is a geometric cover
\{\mathcal{U}_{0},\ldots,\mathcal{U}_{n}\}
of
\mathcal{C}
by subcategories that admit local sections, that is, such that for every
i\in\{0,\ldots,n\}
there is a functor
s_{i}\colon\mathcal{U}_{i}\rightarrow\mathcal{D}
which is a local right inverse of
F
, in the sense that
P\circ s_{i}=\iota_{i}
, where
\iota_{i}\colon\mathcal{U}_{i}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}
is the inclusion.

Example 2.14
.

In the case of the bifibration defined in Example
2.12
there is no global section. Otherwise we would have
P(C)=C_{i}
and
P(D)=D_{j}
; but if we have a pullback of
\alpha
we can not have a pullback for
\beta
and viceversa. Nonetheless there are local sections for the subcategories:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces C}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces D}
and
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces C}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces D.}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\alpha}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\beta}

So we can conclude that
\mathrm{sc}(P)=1
.

Definition 2.15
.

Let
F\colon\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathcal{D}
be a functor, we define its
homotopic sectional category
or
Švarc genus
, denoted by
\mathrm{Sg}(P)
, as the least integer
n\geq 0
such that there is a geometric cover
\{\mathcal{U}_{0},\ldots,\mathcal{U}_{n}\}
of
\mathcal{C}
by subcategories that admit local homotopic sections, i.e. such that for every
i\in\{0,\ldots,n\}
there is a functor
s_{i}\colon\mathcal{U}_{i}\rightarrow\mathcal{D}
that is a local right homotopic inverse of
F
, in the sense that
P\circ s_{i}\simeq\iota_{i}
where
\iota_{i}\colon\mathcal{U}_{i}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}
is the inclusion.

Proposition 2.16
.

For every bifibration
P\colon\mathcal{E}\rightarrow\mathcal{B}
we have that

\mathrm{Sg}(P)=\mathrm{sc}(P).

Proof.

We always have that
\mathrm{Sg}(P)\leq\mathrm{sc}(P)
since a local section is also a homotopic section. To prove the other inequality take any geometric cover
\{\mathcal{U}_{0},\ldots,\mathcal{U}_{n}\}
with local homotopic sections
s_{i}
. For each
i\in\{0,\ldots,n\}
we have a homotopy
H\colon\mathcal{U}_{i}\times\mathbb{I}_{m}\rightarrow\mathcal{B}
such that
H_{0}=P\circ s_{i},H_{m}=\iota_{i}
and the following diagram commutes:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces U_{i}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{E}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{U}_{i}\times\mathbb{I}_{m}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{B}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces s_{i}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces i_{0}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces P}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H}

Now by the right homotopy lifting property (Proposition
2.7
) there is a homotopy
\widetilde{H}\colon\mathcal{U}_{i}\times\mathbb{I}_{m}\rightarrow\mathcal{E}
such that
P\circ\widetilde{H}_{m}=H_{m}=\iota_{i}
, so
\widetilde{H}_{m}
is a local section of
P
.
∎

Example 2.17
.

The bifibration defined in Example
2.6

has no global covers since for every functor
S\colon\mathcal{B}\rightarrow\mathcal{E}
we have that
S(h)=\mathrm{id}_{P(\bullet)}
because

there are no other endomorphisms from
P(\bullet)
into itself. Hence we can not have a section because

P\circ S(h)=P(S(h))=P(\mathrm{id}_{P(\bullet)})=\mathrm{id}_{\bullet}\neq h.

In this case the only subcategories of
\mathcal{B}=\mathbb{Z}_{2}
are the trivial one and the total one, so if we want a geometric cover it must contain the total category. Therefore we can conclude that
\mathrm{sc}(P)=\infty
.

We now prove the main result of the paper.

Theorem 2.18

(Svarc inequality)
.

Let
P\colon\mathcal{E}\rightarrow\mathcal{B}
be a bifibration and let
D
be a natural system in
\mathcal{B}
with an endopairing. The length of the cup product in
\ker{P^{*}}
, denoted by
\mathrm{c.p.l}(\ker{P^{*}})
, is a lower bound for the Svarc genus of
P
:
\mathrm{c.p.l}(\ker{P^{*}})\leq\mathrm{Sg}(P)
.

Proof.

We assume that the Švarc genus is finite since otherwise the result is trivial. By Proposition
2.16
we know that
\mathrm{Sg}(P)=\mathrm{sc}(P)
so we can assume that we have local sections and not only local homotopic sections. Let
\{\mathcal{U}_{0},\ldots,\mathcal{U}_{n}\}
be a geometric cover of
\mathcal{B}
such that every
\mathcal{U}_{j}
has a local section
s_{j}
. Let
\{f_{0}\},\ldots,\{f_{n}\}
be cohomology classes in
\ker P^{*}\subset\mathrm{H}(\mathcal{B};D)
. We have
\iota_{j}=P\circ s_{j}
for each
j\in\{0,\ldots,n\}
and therefore
\iota_{j}^{*}=(P\circ s_{j})^{*}=s_{j}^{*}\circ P^{*}
; thus, every class
\{f_{j}\}
is in
\ker\iota_{j}^{*}
since it is in
\ker P^{*}
.

Using the exact sequence (
1
):

\mathrm{H}^{n}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{U}_{j};D)\xrightarrow{\gamma_{j}}\mathrm{H}^{n}(\mathcal{B};D)\xrightarrow{\iota_{j}^{*}}\mathrm{H}^{n}(\mathcal{U}_{j};D)

we have that every element
\{f_{j}\}
is of the form
\{f_{j}\}=\gamma_{j}(\{g_{j}\})
.

Now if we use the relative cup product defined in Proposition
1.16
and using the fact that we have a geometric cover, it follows that there is a morphism:

\smile\colon\mathrm{H}(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{U}_{0};D)\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathrm{H}(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{U}_{n};D)\rightarrow\mathrm{H}(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{U}_{1}\cup\cdots\cup\mathcal{U}_{n};D).

Moreover we have that

\mathrm{H}(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{U}_{1}\cup\cdots\cup\mathcal{U}_{n};D)=\mathrm{H}(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{C};D)=0.

This entails the wanted result using the Proposition
1.17
. Indeed, we know that

\{f_{0}\}\smile\ldots\smile\{f_{n}\}=\{f_{0}\smile\ldots\smile f_{n}\}=0.\qed

Now we will see two cases in which the inequality holds strictly and another in which it is an equality.
