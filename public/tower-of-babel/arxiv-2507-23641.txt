Polynomial Lattices for the BIKE Cryptosystem
Michael Schaller

Abstract
In this paper we introduce a rank 2 2 lattice over a polynomial ring arising from the public key of the BIKE cryptosystem [ 1 ] . The secret key is a sparse vector in this lattice. We study properties of this lattice and generalize the recovery of weak keys from [ 3 ] . In particular, we show that they implicitly solved a shortest vector problem in the lattice we constructed. Rather than finding only a shortest vector, we obtain a reduced basis of the lattice which makes it possible to check for more weak keys.



1
Introduction and Related Work

One of the round
4
candidates of the NIST competition for Post-Quantum cryptography was the BIKE cryptosystem
[
1
]
.
It has not been selected for standardization, but the related scheme HQC
[
16
]
will be standardized.

In this paper we construct a lattice over a polynomial ring to study the BIKE cryptosystem.
The lattice is constructed from the public key while the secret key is a sparse vector in this lattice.
We investigate the properties of this lattice, in particular the successive minima of the lattice.

Moreover, in our framework we can reinterpret the work from
[
3
]
in which a class of weak keys for BIKE has been exhibited.
We extend their ideas using the work of Mahler
[
14
]
and Lenstra
[
13
]
on lattices over polynomial rings and reformulate the results of
[
3
]
.
This enables us to give a more general perspective on the problem and to obtain more weak keys than in the original work in
[
3
]
.
It turns out that (without using this perspective) they find a shortest vector in the lattice.
Using the lattice we are able to obtain a reduced basis instead of just a shortest vector in the lattice which allows to test for more weak keys.

We would like to point out that the weak keys from
[
3
]
are not related to the weak keys that are studied in connection with decoding failure rates.

Our framework bears similarity to the function field decoding approach introduced in
[
7
]
.
While they work on different problems, in particular a search to decision reduction, several of the general ideas about the BIKE cryptosystem and more generally quasi-cyclic codes are implicit in their work.
Thinking about the Minkowski embedding for rings of integers of number fields and classical lattices in the real numbers makes the connection to their work more obvious.

There have also been other works that investigated lattices over non-archimedean fields in other settings.
Especially noteworthy are the works of Bernstein
[
6
]
, Cohn and Heninger
[
9
]
who used an analogy with Coppersmith’s theorem in order to reinterpret the Guruswami-Sudan list decoding algorithm.
In addition, there have been applications to the computations of Riemann-Roch spaces, for example in Bauch’s PhD thesis
[
4
]
.

The structure of the paper is as follows.
In Section
2
we briefly describe the BIKE cryptosystem and the weak key attack by the authors of
[
3
]
.
In Section
3
we deal with lattices over
\mathbb{Z}
and give some motivation for the use of polynomial lattices for BIKE later.
In Section
4
we introduce and prove results needed later about lattices in the non-archimedean case.
Section
5
contains the main part of the paper in which we introduce a lattice related to the BIKE cryptosystem and prove all the statements we need for the lattices in our treatment of the weak key attack on the BIKE cryptosystem.
We conclude with Section
6
.

2
The BIKE Cryptosystem and a Class of Weak Keys

First of all we describe the BIKE cryptosystem
[
1
]
.
Let us fix a prime
r
such that
2
generates
\mathbb{F}_{r}^{\times}
.
The secret key consists of two sparse (weight
v\sim\sqrt{r}
) elements
h_{1},h_{2}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}[x]/(x^{r}-1)
.
The public key is
\frac{h_{1}}{h_{2}}
.
The goal of a key recovery attack is to recover
h_{1}
and
h_{2}
from
h
.
Actually, we do not need the very same
h_{1}
and
h_{2}
.
Any two sparse elements
h_{1},h_{2}
satisfying
h=\frac{h_{1}}{h_{2}}
are good enough.

Next we sketch the idea of the attack from
[
3
]
.
Let us rewrite the equation for the public key as

h_{1}=hh_{2}\mod x^{r}-1.

This gives us the rational reconstruction problem which can be solved uniquely by the extended Euclidean algorithm as long as
\deg(h_{1})+\deg(h_{2})<r
.
The proof of uniqueness is in essence the same as the proof of Theorem 4.8 in
[
20
]
.
The authors of
[
3
]
also use group actions that come from the code being quasi-cyclic and from the squaring map on
\mathbb{F}_{2}[x]/(x^{r}-1)
to get more weak keys.
We will not describe it here, but only mention that one can do the same thing using the new approach from our paper.

More information about the rational reconstruction problem in the case of integers can be found in
[
20
]
.

In the next sections we will give another perspective on the problem which we deem more natural and which allows to change the condition that
\deg(h_{1})+\deg(h_{2})<r
.

3
Lattices and Thue’s Lemma

In this section we motivate the use of lattices in the later sections.
The results here give an indication of what will happen in Sections
4
and
5
for polynomial lattices.
Here we consider lattices as discrete subgroups of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
(in contrast to the next sections where we consider lattices over polynomial rings).
All the lattices are assumed to be full rank.

Let
\gamma\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
.
Now we introduce
\gamma
-ary lattices (usually called
q
-ary lattices where
\gamma
is replaced by
q
).
Let
k\leq n
and
A\in\mathbb{Z}/(\gamma)^{k\times n}
be a matrix.
We define

\Lambda_{\gamma}^{\perp}(A)=\{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}:Ax=0\mod\gamma\}.

We get the following well known lemma.

Lemma 1
.

Let
k\leq n
and
A\in\mathbb{Z}/(\gamma)^{k\times n}
be a matrix.
Then

\operatorname{covol}(\Lambda_{\gamma}^{\perp}(A))\leq\gamma^{k}.

Proof.

Let
\pi:\mathbb{Z}^{n}\to\mathbb{Z}/(\gamma)^{n}
be the natural projection.
Then

\Lambda_{\gamma}^{\perp}(A)=\pi^{-1}(\ker A).

Hence

\mathbb{Z}^{n}/\Lambda_{\gamma}^{\perp}(A)\cong(\mathbb{Z}/(\gamma)^{n})/(\ker A)\cong\operatorname{im}A\subseteq\mathbb{Z}/(\gamma)^{k}.

The last inclusion implies the claim.
∎

We first state Thue’s lemma which can be found for instance in
[
20
]
and then prove it using Minkowski’s Theorem.

Lemma 2
.

Let
\gamma,b,a^{\ast},t^{\ast}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
.
Assume
0<a^{\ast}<\gamma<a^{\ast}t^{\ast}
.
Then there exist
a,t\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
|a|<a^{\ast},0<|t|<t^{\ast}
satisfying

a\equiv bt\mod\gamma.

Proof.

Consider the following lattice:

L=\{(a,t):a-bt\equiv 0\mod\gamma\}.

We want to estimate the covolume of the lattice in order to apply Minkowski’s Theorem.
We have the trivial inclusions
\gamma\mathbb{Z}^{2}\subseteq L\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{2}
.
But more is true.

L=\{(a,t)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}:(1,-b)(a,t)^{T}\equiv 0\mod\gamma\}
is an
\gamma
-ary lattice.
Thus, using Lemma
1
, we get that

\operatorname{covol}(L)\leq\gamma.

Under the assumption that the volume of a convex, symmetric (around
0
) set
R
is larger than
4\gamma
, we get, using Minkowski’s Theorem, a nonzero lattice point in
R
.
The rectangle
R=\{(x_{1},x_{2}):|x_{1}|<a^{\ast},|x_{2}|<t^{\ast}\}
has volume
4a^{\ast}t^{\ast}>4\gamma
.
Hence there exists a nonzero lattice point in it.
It is easy to see that if
t=0
we also get
a=0
and the statement is proven.
∎

Note that we could also additionally assume
r\geq 0
since we will get a nonzero lattice point
x
and its inverse
-x
.
In
[
20
]
the author gives an efficient algorithm relying on the extended Euclidean algorithm to give an explicit solution for Lemma
2
.
Note that this amounts to finding a short vector in a rank
2
lattice.
So instead of the extended Euclidean algorithm we could run a lattice reduction algorithm like the Lagrange algorithm for rank
2
lattices.

4
Lattices over Polynomial Rings

In this section we explore the concept of a lattice over a polynomial ring
k[x]
where
k
is a field.
The study of this subject has been initiated by Mahler in
[
14
]
in which he treated an analogue of Minkowski’s geometry of numbers
[
18
]
for non-archimedean fields.
He also proved an analogue of Minkowski’s Theorem.
We will mainly be concerned with the paper by Lenstra
[
13
]
in which he presented a polynomial time algorithm for lattice reduction which ends up with a basis that has an orthogonality defect of
0
.
We will define lattices only over polynomial rings and not over general non-archimedean fields as done in
[
14
]
.
For the purposes of this paper it is not even necessary to go to the field of rational functions or to Laurent series.
We will also not go into the improvements of lattice reduction algorithms over polynomial rings like
[
19
,
11
]
since for our purposes Lenstra’s algorithm is sufficient.

We mainly recall the relevant results from
[
13
]
, state the analogue of Minkowski’s Theorem due to Mahler
[
14
]
and add a few results we could not find in the literature.

Definition 3
.

For
f\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[x]
we define
|f|=\deg f
for
f\neq 0
and
|0|=-\infty
.
We define the
norm
of
a=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[x]^{n}
as

|(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})|=\max\{|a_{1}|,\ldots,|a_{n}|\}.

Note that
|f|
for
f\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[x]
does not behave like a non-archimedean norm, but like the additive inverse of a discrete valuation or equivalently like the logarithm of a non-archimedean norm.

Definition 4
.

Let
b_{1},\ldots,b_{n}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[x]^{n}
be linearly independent over
\mathbb{F}_{q}[x]
.
Then the
lattice
spanned by
b_{1},\ldots,b_{n}
is defined to be

L=\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mu_{i}b_{i}:\mu_{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[x]\right\}.

The
determinant

\det(L)
of the lattice is defined as the determinant of the matrix
B
which has
b_{1},\ldots,b_{n}
as rows.
The
covolume

\operatorname{covol}(L)=|\det(L)|
of the lattice
L
is the degree of the determinant.
The
orthogonality defect
of the basis
b_{1},\ldots,b_{n}
of the lattice
L
is defined to be

\operatorname{OD}(b_{1},\ldots,b_{n})=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}|b_{i}|\right)-|\det(L)|.

We call a basis with orthogonality defect
0
a
reduced
basis.

Remark 5
.

Note that
OD(b_{1},\ldots,b_{n})\geq 0
.
Since the determinant of a lattice is unique up to elements in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\ast}
we will consider the determinant only up to units.
Moreover, since we work with the additive inverse of a valuation, we get after taking the orthogonality defect to an appropriate power (say
q^{\operatorname{OD}(b_{1},\ldots,b_{n})}
) the correct analogue of the usual definition of the orthogonality defect.

Next we define the analogue of an important classical notion for lattices.

Definition 6
.

The first
successive minimum
is defined to be the norm
|m_{1}|
of a shortest non-zero vector
m_{1}
in the lattice
L
.
Iteratively we define the
j
-th successive minimum for
2\leq j\leq n
as follows.
Take
j-1
linearly independent vectors
m_{1},\ldots,m_{j-1}\in L
that attain the corresponding successive minima.
The
j
-th
successive minimum
is the norm
|m_{j}|
of a shortest vector
m_{j}\in L
that is linearly independent of
m_{1},\ldots,m_{j-1}
.

Remark 7
.

The value of the
j
-th successive minimum does not depend on the choice of the previous vectors
m_{1},\ldots,m_{j-1}
.

Mahler defined a more general notion of successive minima with respect to more general convex bodies.
Studying these could be an interesting problem for further research.

Lenstra proved the following result about the relation of successive minima and orthogonality defect.

Theorem 8

(Lenstra)
.

Let
b_{1},\ldots,b_{n}
be a basis of the lattice
L
with
OD(b_{1},\ldots,b_{n})=0
, ordered in such a way that
|b_{1}|\leq|b_{2}|\leq\ldots\leq|b_{n}|
.
Then
|b_{j}|
is the
j
-th successive minimum and in particular
b_{1}
is a shortest vector of the lattice
L
.

We will not describe the algorithm from Lenstra’s paper, but we state the main result.
Actually, the algorithm is not very long and also simple to implement.

Theorem 9

(Lenstra)
.

Let
C>0
be such that
|b_{i}|\leq C
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
.
Then there exists an algorithm that takes
O(n^{3}C(\operatorname{OD}(b_{1},\ldots,b_{n})+1))
arithmetic operations to transform a basis
b_{1},\ldots,b_{n}
of
L
into a reduced basis for
L
.

In particular one can find a shortest vector in the given time.
Note that one gets the following corollary using
\operatorname{OD}(b_{1},\ldots,b_{n})\leq nC
.

Corollary 10

(Lenstra)
.

Let
C>0
be such that
|b_{i}|\leq C
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
.
Then there exists an algorithm that takes
O(n^{4}C^{2})
arithmetic operations to transform a basis
b_{1},\ldots,b_{n}
of
L
into a reduced basis for
L
.

We will prove the following two lemmata since we could not find the statements and the proofs in the literature.
However, they are not strictly necessary for the remainder of the paper.
It is a classical result that for lattices
L_{2}\subseteq L_{1}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
we have
[L_{1}:L_{2}]=\frac{\operatorname{covol}(L_{2})}{\operatorname{covol}(L_{1})}
.
We prove the following analogue.

Lemma 11
.

Let
L_{2}\subseteq L_{1}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}[x]^{n}
be two lattices.
Then

[L_{1}:L_{2}]=q^{\left|\frac{\det(L_{2})}{\det(L_{1})}\right|}=q^{\operatorname{covol}(L_{2})-\operatorname{covol}(L_{1})}.

Proof.

By the elementary divisor theorem we can choose a basis
b_{1},\ldots,b_{n}
of
L_{1}
and
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[x]^{\ast}
such that
\alpha_{1}b_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}b_{n}
is a basis of
L_{2}
.
Then by multilinearity of the determinant we get that

\det(L_{2})=\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}\right)\det(L_{1}).

Hence

\left|\frac{\det(L_{2})}{\det(L_{1})}\right|=\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\alpha_{i}|.

Furthermore,

[L_{1}:L_{2}]=\prod_{i=1}^{n}q^{|\alpha_{i}|}=q^{\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\alpha_{i}|}.

This finishes the proof.
∎

We define an analogue of
\gamma
-ary matrices in this setting.
Let
g\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[x]
.
Further assume
k\leq n
and
A\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[x]^{k\times n}
.
We define

\Lambda_{g}^{\perp}(A)=\{a\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[x]^{n}:Aa\equiv 0\mod g\}.

We have the following analogue to Lemma
1
.

Lemma 12
.

Let
k\leq n
and
A\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[x]^{k\times n}
.
Then

[\mathbb{F}_{q}[x]^{n}:\Lambda_{g}^{\perp}(A)]\leq q^{|g|k}.

Proof.

The proof is identical to the proof of Lemma
1
.
∎

Using Lemma
11
we can also rewrite the previous lemma as
\operatorname{covol}(\Lambda_{g}^{\perp}(A))\leq|g|k
.

Example 13
.

Let
A=(I_{k}|0_{n-k})
.
Then

\Lambda_{g}^{\perp}(A)=(g\mathbb{F}_{q}[x]^{k})\times\mathbb{F}_{q}[x]^{n-k}.

In this case, we get equality in Lemma
12
.
Namely,

\operatorname{covol}(\Lambda_{g}^{\perp}(A))=|g|k.

Finally we will state the theorem by Mahler which is the analogue of Minkowski’s Theorem in a slightly generalized form as in
[
2
]
.

Theorem 14

(Mahler)
.

Let
L
be a lattice and
|m_{j}|
be its
j
-th successive minimum. Then

\sum_{j=1}^{n}|m_{j}|=\operatorname{covol}(L).

Corollary 15

(Mahler)
.

Let
L
be a lattice.
Then for the first successive minimum
|m_{1}|
we have
|m_{1}|\leq\frac{\operatorname{covol}(L)}{n}
, i.e., there exists a non-zero lattice vector in
L
of norm at most
\frac{\operatorname{covol}(L)}{n}
.

5
BIKE from a Lattice Viewpoint

In the BIKE cryptosystem we have a similar problem as in Thue’s Lemma
2
from Section
3
, but in the polynomial setting.
We want to solve

h_{1}-hh_{2}\equiv 0\mod x^{r}-1,

for
h_{1}
and
h_{2}
which are sparse.
This problem has been studied as a problem of rational reconstruction (without the lattice viewpoint) in
[
3
]
.
Again we can build a lattice

L=\{(h_{1},h_{2}):h_{1}-hh_{2}\equiv 0\mod x^{r}-1\}.

Note that this lattice can be constructed solely from the public key.
We will propose a new algorithm to get weak keys in a similar way as in
[
3
]
by using lattice reduction and adding a possibility for using brute force.
In practice our algorithm improves only very slightly upon the algorithm proposed before, but it gives a more unified perspective on it, especially since lattice reduction for polynomial rings over finite field is computationally easy in general and not just for the
2
-dimensional case as for the integers.

We can even write down an explicit basis for our lattice as we show in the following lemma.

Lemma 16
.

Let
L=\{(h_{1},h_{2}):h_{1}-hh_{2}\equiv 0\mod x^{r}-1\}
.
Then the vectors

(x^{r}-1,0),(h,1)

form a basis of
L
.

Proof.

It is obvious that both vectors are in
L
.
To show that they form a basis take
(h_{1},h_{2})\in L
.
Then also

(h_{1}^{\prime},0)=(h_{1},h_{2})-h_{2}(h,1)\in L.

But then

h_{1}^{\prime}\equiv 0\mod x^{r}-1,

and consequently we can express
(h_{1},h_{2})
as a linear combination of
(x^{r}-1,0)
and
(h,1)
with coefficients in
\mathbb{F}_{q}[x]
.
∎

Note that a similar result also holds for the case of the integers from Section
3
.
It is now easy to compute the determinant of
L
.

Corollary 17
.

Let
L=\{(h_{1},h_{2}):h_{1}-hh_{2}\equiv 0\mod x^{r}-1\}
.
Then the determinant of
L
is
x^{r}-1
.

Proof.

The determinant of

\begin{pmatrix}x^{r}-1&0\\
h&1\end{pmatrix}

is
x^{r}-1
.
∎

From Corollary
17
we get the following result for rational reconstruction, similar to Thue’s Lemma
2
.

Corollary 18
.

Let
L=\{(h_{1},h_{2}):h_{1}-hh_{2}\equiv 0\mod x^{r}-1\}
.
There exists
a,b\in L
with
|a|+|b|=r
.
In particular there exists
a\in L
with
|a|\leq\frac{r-1}{2}
.

Proof.

We have
\operatorname{covol}(L)=r
from the previous corollary.
By applying Theorem
14
we get the vectors
a,b
as desired.
∎

We could have also proven the statement using Lemma
12
.
In fact, for
L
as above we can compute
a
and
b
using Lenstra’s algorithm.
We get the following result from Lenstra’s Corollary
10
.

Lemma 19
.

Let
L=\{(h_{1},h_{2}):h_{1}-hh_{2}\equiv 0\mod x^{r}-1\}
.
Then one can find the successive minima of
L
in time
O(r^{2})
.

Now, let
h_{1}
and
h_{2}
be the actual private keys.
In case that
|(h_{1},h_{2})|<\frac{r}{2}
, using Lemma
19
, we recover essentially the attack from
[
3
]
since the attack from
[
3
]
usually computes the shortest vector
a
from Corollary
18
.
In
[
3
]
the authors also analyzed the probability that the condition on the degrees is satisfied.

We can now slightly extend this attack using brute force.
It is worth mentioning that in the attack from
[
3
]
this is not possible since they only found a shortest vector, but no reduced basis.
As long as the degrees of the private key are not too far from the degrees of the reduced basis we can compute the reduced basis and then try to find the private key as described in Algorithm
1
.

Algorithm 1

Weak Keys via Lattice Reduction

Input:

•

Public Key
h
for BIKE

•

A bound
B
on the degree we use for brute force.

Step 1:

Compute a reduced basis
a=(a_{1},a_{2}),b=(b_{1},b_{2})
for the lattice
L=\{(h_{1},h_{2}):h_{1}-hh_{2}\equiv 0\mod x^{r}-1\}
using Lenstra’s algorithm.

Step 2:

for

\mu_{1},\mu_{2}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}[x]_{<B}

do

Compute
(h_{1}^{\prime},h_{2}^{\prime})=\mu_{1}(a_{1},a_{2})+\mu_{2}(b_{1},b_{2})
.

if

h_{1}^{\prime}
and
h_{2}^{\prime}
are sparse
then

return

(h_{1}^{\prime},h_{2}^{\prime})
.

end

if

end

for

However, there is a slight difference in the weak keys we get.
In
[
3
]
they have the condition
\deg(h_{1})+\deg(h_{2})<r
, whereas we have the condition
|(h_{1},h_{2})|=\max\{\deg(h_{1}),\deg(h_{2})\}<\frac{r}{2}+B
.
Some of the weak keys from
[
3
]
we will not recover with Algorithm
1
, and on the other hand some of our weak keys will not be recovered by the algorithm in
[
3
]
.
Combining both approaches enables us to check for more (however not many more) weak keys than in the original paper.
Using calculations similar to those in
[
3
]
we expect to gain at most a few bits upon the original attack for reasonable values on the bound
B
of the degree when using brute force.
One can also apply to our approach the same improvements as in
[
3
]
using group actions which we have not considered here.
In our case the analysis on the success probability follows along similar lines as in
[
3
]
.

One problem on which the hardness of BIKE rests is the following.

Problem 20
.

How hard is it to find short vectors in the lattice
L
where the ”norm” is defined by
|(h_{1},h_{2})|=\operatorname{wt}(h_{1})+\operatorname{wt}(h_{2})
.

However, this is not even a norm and the natural norm on the lattice is quite far from the norm in Problem
20
.
Are there better algorithms to tackle this problem?

6
Conclusion and Open Questions

In this work we recast the rational reconstruction problem for key recovery of the BIKE cryptosystem as a problem of lattices over polynomial rings.
Two questions that immediately arise are whether taking Mahler’s
[
14
]
more general definition of successive minima can give more weak keys and whether they can be computed efficiently.

One of the main open questions is whether there are better key recovery attacks that use a similar approach.
In particular, the lattice constructed in this paper is a rank
2
lattice and closely related to the Euclidean algorithm and continued fractions.
Is there a way to construct a higher rank lattice that makes it possible to get (many) more keys?

We would like to point out that there have been two problems that started out with rank
2
lattices (or extended Euclidean algorithm and continued fractions) and could be improved considerably using higher rank lattices.
One is Wiener’s classical attack
[
22
]
on small private keys using continued fractions and its improvements by Boneh and Durfee
[
8
]
relying on Coppersmiths attack
[
10
]
which uses the LLL algorithm.
Another one is the decoding of Reed-Solomon codes by Berlekamp and Massey
[
5
,
15
]
(reinterpreted as continued fractions by Mills
[
17
]
, Welch and Scholtz
[
21
]
) and the breakthrough of List Decoding by Guruswami and Sudan
[
12
]
(reinterpreted by Bernstein
[
6
]
, Cohn and Heninger
[
9
]
as an analogue of Coppersmith’s theorem).
Can something similar be done for the key recovery of the secret key of BIKE?

In addition, it might also be worth applying similar methods to HQC
[
16
]
.
