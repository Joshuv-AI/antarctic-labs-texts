CYP8B1 suppressed by Ras/ERK leads to cholesterol accumulation and bile acids reduction and facilitates hepatic tumorigenesis
Authors: Juan Li, Huiling Li, Lili Dong, Liang Yao, Xiaoying Dong, Yingyan Guo, Chunwen Pu, Aiguo Wang, Jun Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCholesterol (CHO)/bile acids (BAs) homeostasis is critically involved in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), yet the underlying mechanisms and the specific regulatory role of CYP8B1-a key enzyme maintaining this balance-remain poorly defined. This study seeks to clarify the roles of CYP8B1 and its regulated CHO/BA homeostasis in the initiation and progression of HCC driven by aberrant RAS/ERK signaling.MethodUtilizing TCGA data analysis, clinical sample examination (including protein/mRNA level assessments and serum measurements), in vitro cell line studies, an HCC animal model (with histopathological classification and inhibitor treatments), and dietary interventions (a high-CHO diet and Taurodeoxycholic acid [TDCA, a main secondary BA] treatment) to investigate the roles of Ras/ERK and CYP8B1 in CHO/BA homeostasis and HCC progression.ResultsClinical HCC data and cell lines frequently show ERK hyperactivation with concomitant CYP8B1 reduction and CHO accumulation. In Hras12V transgenic mice (Ras-Tg), CHO was accumulated in hepatic tumor tissues (T), peri-tumoral tissues (P), and serum compared with wild-type (WT) mice, and this accumulation predominantly resulted from the attenuation of BA biosynthesis induced by reduced CYP8B1 activity. Inhibition of ERK activity by AZD6244 (a p-ERK inhibitor) notably elevated CYP8B1 activity and BAs levels and reduced CHO level in Ras-Tg. Further investigation revealed that SREBP2 is critically involved in the ERK-mediated regulation of CYP8B1 and CHO/BA homeostasis. Intriguingly, although CYP8B1 activity were extremely down-regulated in T compared with P, CHO was significantly lower in T than in P, and the combination of the extreme up-regulation of apolipoprotein (APO) A1 and APOB in T with disordered expression of serum high-density lipoprotein (HDL) and low-density lipoprotein (LDL) might reflect active efflux of CHO from hepatoma cells. Notably, further elevation of the CHO level with a high-CHO diet perturbed multiple signaling pathways (mTOR, NF-κB, GSK3β/β-catenin, caspase 3, and BAX/BCL2) and suppressed hepatic tumor progression but not tumorigenesis. Intriguingly, feeding TDCA significantly inhibits hepatic tumorigenesis and development by inhibiting ERK and mTOR pathways.ConclusionCYP8B1 repressed by Ras/ERK plays crucial roles in CHO/BA homeostasis which facilitate hepatic tumor progression. These findings provide a novel theoretical foundation and therapeutic perspective for HCC treatment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40170-026-00439-0.Keywords: Ras/ERK, CYP8B1, Cholesterol, Bile acids, Hepatocellular carcinoma, Hepatocytes

Background

The symbol "#" is used to indicate that these two authors are co-first authors.

Cholesterol (CHO) is an essential lipid for the maintenance of cellular homeostasis. In addition to being a precursor for bile acids (BAs), steroid hormones, and vitamin D synthesis, CHO is an essential component of plasma membranes, is enriched in lipid rafts and plays a key role in intracellular signal transduction [1, 2]. Because the rapid proliferation of cells requires the availability of CHO for membrane synthesis and because cancer cells typically exhibit high CHO levels, the ability to accumulate cellular CHO through multiple pathways is considered to be consistent with carcinogenesis [3]. In line with this hypothesis, numerous preclinical studies have consistently suggested the involvement of CHO in cancer [4], and the impact of CHO on oncogenic events, including tumor development, cell migration and angiogenesis, has been recognized [3]. Moreover, The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) project provides support for a role of the CHO pathway in cancer progression [5]. However, the intracellular CHO homeostasis shows variations among different cancer types [6, 7], and contradictory epidemiological studies have questioned the roles of CHO in cancer [5]. Intriguingly, recent evidence suggests that the cholesterol metabolites can promote or suppress breast cancer [8, 9]. Therefore, CHO might play dual functions on carcinogenesis depending on the cancer type or developmental stage.

BAs are only synthesized and secreted in the liver, which is the main pathways for CHO catabolism [10]. As an amphiphilic molecule with strong detergent properties, BAs are always considered closely related to HCC. Clinical studies have shown that compared to the healthy control, the serum levels of BAs in patients with liver cirrhosis gradually increase as the condition worsens, while the serum levels of BAs in HCC patients are lower than those in patients with liver cirrhosis [11]. This reflects the accumulation of BAs in liver which may result from the blockage of BAs secretion due to the functional damages. And the toxic effects of excessive BAs on liver cells may further lead to liver fibrosis, cirrhosis, and even HCC [12]. However, BAs are also used to treat HCC related diseases [13]. Additionally, the opposite results of the same BAs have been showed in different experiments [14, 15]. These complex situations require further precise researches, such as using a special pathogenic induced HCC model.

HCC development is driven by interactions among genetic predisposition, environmental factors (such as metabolic syndrome, alcohol, aflatoxin B1, or aristocholic acid) and viruses (hepatitis B virus and hepatitis C virus) [16]. Independent of these risk factors, numerous studies have shown that hyperactivation of the Ras/ERK signal transduction pathways is frequently detected in human hepatocarcinogenesis and is associated with an aggressive tumor phenotype and poor prognosis [17]. Accumulating evidence has established that CYP8B1/Cyp8b1, a rate-limiting enzyme in bile acid biosynthesis and the major pathway for cholesterol excretion from hepatocytes, is subject to tight repression by ERK signaling under both physiological and pathological conditions [18, 19]. Under normal metabolic regulation, the intestinal FXR–FGF15/19–FGFR4/β-Klotho axis activates hepatic ERK and JNK, which cooperate with SHP to transcriptionally repress CYP8B1 [20]. In addition, CYP8B1 expression is modulated by multiple other regulatory networks, including PPARα-mediated induction, inflammatory cytokine-JNK signaling, FXR-MAFG-dependent repression, and a negative feedback loop whereby CYP8B1 itself inhibits Raf/MEK/ERK phosphorylation [21, 22]. Although ERK has been proposed to regulate CYP8B1 via metabolic reprogramming or by modulating the activity of nuclear receptors such as FXR and LXR [23], the direct oncogenic mechanism by which constitutively active Ras/ERK signaling represses CYP8B1 in HCC remains poorly defined. Moreover, whether and how Ras/ERK signaling disturbs CHO/BA homeostasis in vivo during hepatocarcinogenesis has not been systematically explored.

In the present study, TCGA database, clinical HCC patient samples and cell lines were first explored to investigate the association between ERK and CHO homeostasis. The functions of Ras/ERK in CHO/BA homeostasis in normal hepatocytes and hepatoma cells were then investigated using Ras-Tg mice with additional treatments with AZD6244 and fatostatin. The effects of TDCA and excess CHO on hepatic tumorigenesis and progression were also determined.

Materials and methods

Human participants

Human HCC tissues (n = 18) were obtained from patients undergoing surgical resection. These samples were collected with permission from both the patients and the Dalian Public Health Clinical Center. Clinical characteristics including age, gender, liver disease etiology (HBV, HCV, ALD, autoimmune liver disease), presence of cirrhosis and histologic tumor grade were documented in Supplementary Table 4.

Animal studies

The experimental and histopathological examination procedures for animal handling and tissue sampling were conducted in compliance with protocols approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Dalian Medical University. Male Ras-Tg and male wild-type (WT) mice, all of which had a C57BL/6J background, were bred and cohoused in the Laboratory Animal Center of Dalian Medical University. The animals were maintained in a pathogen-free environment under controlled conditions (12-hour light/dark cycle, 23℃). A standard chow diet (AIN93G) and water were provided available ad libitum before the experiments. After the mice were sacrificed, a samples of liver tissues from WT mice and of hepatic peri-tumoral liver tissues (P) and hepatic tumor tissues (T) from Ras-Tg mice were removed and immediately flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen. The remaining tissue portions were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin, embedded in paraffin, sectioned, stained with hematoxylin and eosin using standard methods and then subjected to histopathological examination.

Sample size determination and animal grouping

Age-matched C57BL/6 background Ras-Tg mice were used for hepatocellular carcinoma modeling, with wild-type littermates as controls. Mice were randomly assigned to groups using a random number generator to minimize selection bias. Sample sizes (n = 5–13 across independent experiments) were determined by a priori power analysis using G*Power 3.1 software, based on our laboratory’s pilot and historical data from the same Ras-Tg model (expected effect size Cohen’s d ≥ 1.2, intra-group coefficient of variation < 15%). The significance level was set at α = 0.05 with a target power ≥ 80% to detect biologically relevant differences. All animal procedures were performed in accordance with institutional animal care guidelines and the 3R principles.

Results

Hras/ERK and CYP8B1 is involved in CHO homeostasis in human HCC

Exploration of TCGA data revealed that, among the key enzymes related to CHO/BAs homeostasis, only CYP8B1 was significantly reduced in HCC comparing with normal liver tissues and its expression level is significantly and positively correlated with the overall survival rate of HCC patients (Fig. S1A). In addition, the expression of Hras and ERK was significantly increased and positively correlated with each other in HCC (Fig. S1A, B). Moreover, they are all negatively correlated with the expression of CYP8B1 and overall survival rate of HCC patients (Fig. S1A, B).

Clinically, elevated levels of p-ERK (8/18 (44.4%)) and reduced levels of CYP8B1 (14/18 (77.8%)) were observed in HCC compared with the respective adjacent non-tumor liver tissues (Fig. 1A). In addition, ERK hyperactivation was detected concomitant with reduced CYP8B1 in six (33.3%) of the 18 samples. The protein and mRNA expression for CYP8B1 were consistently reduced in seven (38.9%) of the 18 samples (Figs. 1A and S2). Further investigation showed that the serum CHO level in patients with liver cirrhosis increased significantly after the occurrence of tumor, and the serum CHO of patients with large liver cancer is significantly higher than that of patients with small liver cancer. Specially, the serum CHO content of patients with HCC is significantly reduced after surgery (Fig. 1B). In vitro study showed that the p-ERK levels were markedly elevated in all examined HCC cell lines except HepG2. Conversely, the CYP8B1/Cyp8b1 levels were significantly down-regulated (Figs. 1 C and S3A). Under the anti-ERK treatment, CYP8B1/Cyp8b1 expression and enzyme activity were consistently up-regulated in MHCC97H cells (Figs. 1D and S3B).

Ras-Tg mice are suitable model for HCC related mechanistic dissection

To ascertain the roles of the Ras/ERK pathway in CHO/BA homeostasis, Ras-Tg mice, which are considered an animal model of liver cancer, were examined. In this model, the Hras12V oncogene is initiated by the Albumin enhancer/promoter and specifically expressed in hepatocytes. Ras-Tg mice progressively develop hepatic dysplasia, nodules, adenoma, and carcinoma over time (Fig. 2A) [24]. According to the histopathological diagnostic criteria for mouse hepatic tumors described by Frith and Ward [25], we approximately classified the hepatic alterations into three stages: tumorigenesis (nodule, < 2 mm), initial development (adenoma, 2–5 mm), and malignant progression (carcinoma, > 5 mm). These stages clearly demonstrate the periodic pathological features of HCC. Notably, the hepatic alterations primarily involved nodules and adenoma in five-month-old (5 M) Ras-Tg mice, and these mice were thus characterized as being at the initial stages of tumorigenesis and development with few liver alterations. However, the alterations in eight-month-old (8 M) Ras-Tg mice primarily involved adenoma and carcinoma, which characterized these mice as being further in tumor development with multiple massive hepatic tumors (Fig. 2B). Accordingly, the liver-to-body weight ratio was remarkably increased in 8 M Ras-Tg mice compared with 8 M WT and 5 M Ras-Tg mice but slightly increased in 5 M Ras-Tg mice compared with 5 M WT mice (Fig. 2C). Intriguingly, the Hras12V mRNA levels were gradually and significantly increased from P to T and with age in liver tissues of Ras-Tg mice (Fig. 2D). Accordingly, concomitant with the expression of the Hras12V transgene, the protein levels of Hras12V and p-ERK were significantly elevated in Ras-Tg mice (Fig. 3B, C).

To validate ERK activation histologically, we performed p-ERK immunohistochemistry on liver sections from 8-month-old Ras-Tg mice, with paired H&E staining to define tumor boundaries (Fig. S4). H&E staining clearly distinguished tumor lesions from adjacent non-tumorous liver tissue. Corresponding p-ERK immunostaining exhibited significantly stronger signals in tumor areas than in adjacent non-tumor tissue, confirming elevated ERK phosphorylation within hepatic tumors. Furthermore, 7-day inhibition of the ERK pathway markedly reduced hepatic tumor burden, as reflected by decreased liver-to-body weight ratio and improved histological morphology on H&E staining (Fig. S5A, E). Consistent with effective pathway suppression, immunohistochemistry confirmed reduced p-ERK levels, while TUNEL staining showed increased apoptosis in treated tumors (Fig. S5A). Notably, this treatment did not affect Hras12V expression or tumor multiplicity (Fig. S5B–D).Collectively, these data support the validity of this Ras-Tg model for investigating Hras/ERK-driven mechanisms during hepatocarcinogenesis.

CHO is accumulated in Ras-Tg mice

The CHO level was significantly increased in both T and P from 5 M to 8 M Ras-Tg mice (Fig. 2E). Intriguingly, the CHO level was significantly lower in T than P from 8 M Ras-Tg mice (Fig. 2E). CHO staining of cryo-sectioned tissues further confirmed these findings (Fig. S6). Consistently, the CHO level was also significantly elevated in the serum of 5 M and 8 M Ras-Tg mice compared with WT mice (Fig. 2E), and the CHO levels were significantly higher in both P and serum from 8 M compared with 5 M Ras-Tg mice, which is consistent with the expression levels of the Hras12V oncogene and p-ERK (Figs. 2D, E).

CYP8B1 and BAs is reduced in Ras-Tg mice

The mRNA levels of Cyp8b1 were consistently and significantly down-regulated in both P and T from 8 M Ras-Tg mice compared with those from 8 M WT mice based on NGS data (Table S1), and these results were further validated by RT-qPCR (Fig. 3C). Consistently, liver tissues from 5 M Ras-Tg and WT mice presented the same phenotypes, and a western blotting assay confirmed these results at the protein level (Figs. 3B, C). The changes in the enzyme activities of CYP8B1 in 5 M and 8 M mice were consistent with their protein levels (Figs. 3B, C). As a result, the BA levels were decreased in samples of liver tissue, serum, and gallbladder from both 5 M and 8 M Ras-Tg mice compared with those from WT mice, with the exception that no change was detected in the serum of 8 M Ras-Tg mice (Fig. 3D) which might be due to the obstruction of bile ducts by multiple massive hepatic tumors. A detailed assay of the gallbladder bile composition showed a decreased level of primary BAs in 5 M Ras-Tg mice, which is consistent with the observed reductions in CYP8B1 enzyme activities (Fig. S7A). In 8 M Ras-Tg mice, the reduction of primary BAs was mainly due to cholic acid (CA) and its derived BA but not beta-muricholic acid (βMCA), which is consistent with the notable decrease in the enzyme activity of CYP8B1 (Figs. 3 A, C, and S7B).

Additionally, consistent with the NGS data, the RT-qPCR results showed that Fxr and Abcb11 were gradually and significantly decreased from the hepatic tissues from WT mice to P and T from Ras-Tg mice (Fig. S8A, Table S1). Thus, simultaneous repression of the FXR/ABCB11 pathway and down-regulation of CYP8B1/Cyp8b1 indicates that FXR is not involved in the Ras oncoprotein-induced suppression of CYP8B1/Cyp8b1. Intriguingly, the BAs level was lower in T than in P (Fig. 3D). Further investigation indicated that a decreased expression of Slc10a1 (a liver-specific sodium/BA cotransporter for BA absorption) and an increased expression of Abcc2 (a biliary transporter for BA efflux) in T might be associated with this effect (Fig. S8B).

ERK induces disorder of CHO/BA homeostasis in Ras-Tg mice

P-ERK inhibitor (AZD6244) treatment down-regulated the p-ERK levels in both P and T from Ras-Tg mice, and conversely, CYP8B1/Cyp8b1 was up-regulated in these tissues compared with the controls (Figs. 4 A, S9A). An enzyme activity assay showed elevated CYP8B1 activity in AZD6244-treated Ras-Tg mice, which was consistent with the increase in the levels of CA and its derived primary BA but not βMCA (Figs. 4 C and S9A). As a consequence, reduced CHO levels and elevated BA levels were detected in P, T, serum, and gallbladder from AZD6244-treated Ras-Tg (Figs. 4B.C).

SREBP2 mediates ERK signaling-dependent regulation of CYP8B1 and CHO/BA homeostasis

The cytoplasmic precursor and nuclear mature forms of SREBP2 were elevated in P and T from 8 M Ras-Tg mice compared with the WT mice (Fig. S10). AZD6244 treatment significantly down-regulated the nuclear SREBP2 level but not that of its cytoplasm precursor in P and T compared with the controls (Fig. 4A). Treatment with fatostatin (an inhibitor of SREBPs) reduced the nuclear SREBP2 level and resulted in a similar phenotype as that of the AZD6244-treated Ras-Tg mice, with the exception that the gallbladder bile composition (Figs. 4D, E, F, and S9B).

These results suggest that SREBP2 is critically involved in the ERK-mediated regulation of CYP8B1 and CHO/BA homeostasis.

Disorder of lipoproteins and apolipoproteins contribute to the relatively lower CHO levels in T

The CHO content was significantly elevated in both serum LDLs and HDLs from Ras-Tg mice compared the WT mice at 5 M and 8 M (Fig. 5A). Intriguingly, the predominantly HDL-associated protein APOA1 was significantly elevated in the serum from Ras-Tg mice relative to that of WT at 5 M and 8 M, but the predominantly LDL-associated protein APOB showed no changes in expression (Fig. 5B). However, APOA1 and APOB were significantly elevated in P and T at both the mRNA and protein levels in 5 M and 8 M Ras-Tg mice compared with the WT mice (Figs. 5 C, D). In particular, the observed changes in 8 M Ras-Tg carrying multiple hepatic tumors notably more extreme compared with those in 5 M Ras-Tg mice, and a similar finding was obtained for T compared with P from 8 M Ras-Tg mice (Figs. 5A, C, D).

Excess CHO inhibits hepatic tumor progression but not tumorigenesis in Ras-Tg mice

The high-CHO diet significantly increased the levels of CHO and BAs in P, and T of Ras-Tg compared with those in the controls (Figs. 6 A and S11). Intriguingly, although the elevated CHO level had no effect on the incidence of nodules (< 2 mm) or total hepatic lesions, it significantly increased the incidence of adenoma (2–5 mm) but decreased the incidence of carcinoma (> 5 mm) compared with that in the controls (Figs. 6B, C). Accordingly, the liver weight and liver-to-body weight ratio were significantly decreased in the high-CHO diet group (Fig. 6D). Moreover, these results were further validated by another set of experiments in which Ras-Tg mice were treated with only CHO via intragastric administration to exclude the possible effects of CA, which has been suggested to play a role in hepatic tumorigenesis and tumor development (Fig. S12). Further investigation revealed that the high-CHO diet had no effect on the levels of albumin, Hras12V, or p-ERK (Figs. 6E, F). Additional examination of signaling pathways in high-CHO diet treated mice showed that multiple pathways were perturbed: [1] p-mTOR and NF-kB were activated in P but showed no obvious change in T; [2] GSK3β/β-catenin and caspase-3 were activated in both P and T; [3] the BCL2 and BAX levels were increased in P but decreased in T from high-CHO diet-treated Ras-Tg mice compared with the controls (Fig. 6F).

The TDCA treatment significantly reduced the number of hepatic lesions and almost no > 5 mm lesions were detected comparing with the control group (Figs. 7 A, C). Additionally, the phosphorylation levels of MEK, ERK and mTOR were significantly reduced in the TDCA treated group (Fig. 7D). Discussion

The liver is the central organ controlling CHO/BAs homeostasis in the body. Although it has been proposed that ERK plays important roles in regulating BAs biosynthesis in normal hepatocytes, the underlying mechanisms related to disordered CHO/BA homeostasis in HCC remain unclear. Multiple studies have shown that the expression of CYP8B1, a key enzyme in BAs biosynthesis, is decreased in patients with HCC [26, 27]. CYP8B1 can be inhibited by tumor related signaling pathways such as ERK through transcription factors LXR and FXR [18]. In the present study, the reduced expression of CYP8B1 and its negative correlation to ERK expression and HCC prognosis were further confirmed based on TCGA database (Fig. S1) and clinical HCC samples (Fig. 1). By studying Ras-Tg mice, we found that activation of ERK tightly suppressed the expression of CYP8B1/Cyp8b1 and its enzyme activity in both 5 M and 8 M Ras-Tg mice, therefore reduces the BA levels and elevates the CHO levels in P, T, and serum (Figs. 2 and 3). Correspondingly, the changes in primary BAs in the gallbladder were focus on CA, TCA and their derivatives (Fig. S7). Inhibition of ERK pathway significantly recovered the expression of CYP8B1 and contents of BAs (Fig. 4). These evidences indicate that the ERK pathway contributes to down-regulate the expression of CYP8B1, which in turn results in the accumulation of CHO and the reduction of BAs.

The mechanistic link between ERK signaling and SREBP2 activation has been well documented [28], which strongly supports our proposed regulatory pathway. In non-transformed cells under growth factor stimulation, activated ERK has been shown to directly phosphorylates nuclear SREBP2 at Ser455, thereby inhibiting its SUMOylation at Lys464 and blocking the recruitment of the HDAC3-containing corepressor complex [29]. We therefore propose that in the context of constitutive Ras/ERK activation in HCC, a similar post-translational regulatory cascade hyperactivates SREBP2.This process relieves transcriptional repression and enhances the transcriptional activity of SREBP2 on genes related to cholesterol metabolism [29]. In our Ras-Tg mouse model, constitutive Ras/ERK activation hyperactivates SREBP2 through this post-translational regulatory cascade. Importantly, pharmacological inhibition of SREBP2 using fatostatin significantly restored CYP8B1 expression and reversed cholesterol and bile acid disorders (Fig. 4D-F), providing direct functional evidence that SREBP2 serves as a key downstream mediator of ERK-dependent CYP8B1 suppression. We therefore propose that hyperactivated SREBP2 competes with the HNF4ɑ/PGC-1ɑ complex to inhibit CYP8B1 transcription, establishing a direct molecular link from oncogenic Ras/ERK signaling to metabolic reprogramming in HCC.

While our genetic and pharmacological evidence supports a functional ERK-SREBP2-CYP8B1 axis driving cholesterol and bile acid remodeling, we acknowledge that direct Cyp8b1 gain- and loss-of-function studies are required to formally confirm whether its suppression alone is sufficient to recapitulate the full metabolic phenotype. Our clinical and mouse data demonstrate a reciprocal, stepwise dysregulation between p-ERK and CYP8B1 during hepatocarcinogenesis, and pharmacological rescue further supports a causal rather than merely correlative role. Nonetheless, definitive proof of sufficiency will require hepatocyte-specific Cyp8b1 genetic models in future investigations. Also, although SREBP2 appears to mediate ERK-dependent suppression of CYP8b1, it does not seem to bind the Cyp8b1 promoter directly, implying that the regulation involves indirect metabolic or transcriptional mechanisms. The precise molecular basis of this interplay will need to be clarified in follow-up work.

Notably, consistent with a previous report [18, 19], the predominant roles of ERK in suppressing BAs biosynthesis were observed in P. In contrast, the controlling effect of ERK on suppressing BAs biosynthesis is relatively weak in T. In humans, reductions in CYP8B1 expression are also observed in a portion of HCC cases without hyperactivated ERK signaling. These results indicate that multiple mechanisms in addition to ERK are involved in suppressing BAs biosynthesis in HCC. However, at least in Ras-Tg mice, the observed down-regulation of the FXR/ABCB11 expression excludes a role for FXR in repressing BAs biosynthesis in HCC with activated ERK.

CHO toxicity has been reported in multiple cell types [30], but there is little evidence showing CHO toxicity in tumor cells. Notably, although attenuated BAs synthesis was detected in T compared with P, the CHO levels were significantly lower in T than in P. Further investigation indicated that disorder of lipoproteins and apolipoproteins might yield this result by promoting the efflux or blocking the influx of CHO in T. Therefore, we speculated that excess CHO might exhibit toxicity toward hepatic tumor cells. This hypothesis was confirmed by feeding Ras-Tg mice with a high-CHO diet, which suppressed hepatic tumor progression but not tumorigenesis. Clinically, hypercholesterolemia has been suggest to be one of the paraneoplastic syndromes of HCC [31]. Consistently, our investigation found that the serum CHO was significantly increased after HCC genesis. Specially, the levels of serum CHO were positively correlated to the HCC sizes and HCC surgery significantly reduced the circulation CHO (Fig. 1). These data indicate that although CHO accumulation is essential for growth, CHO efflux and influx in hepatoma cells occur simultaneously and are tightly controlled by the regulation of specific lipoproteins and apolipoproteins to maintain CHO homeostasis and avoid excess-CHO-induced toxicity.

CHO is a critical component of membrane bilayers and is enriched in lipid rafts, supporting its key roles in intracellular signal transduction and mitochondrial functions [32]. However, the molecular mechanisms linking cholesterol to cancer biology remain incompletely understood. Of note, excess cholesterol exerts well-documented cytotoxicity through three interconnected mechanisms: ER stress triggered by impaired ER membrane integrity and prolonged unfolded protein response (UPR) signaling [33]; oxidative stress via reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation and lipid peroxidation [34]; and membrane disruption that rigidifies lipid bilayers and dysregulates survival-related signaling hubs such as mTOR [35]. Our investigation provides the first demonstration that multiple signaling pathways are markedly altered by excess cholesterol in the liver of Ras-Tg mice. Tumor cells actively upregulate cholesterol efflux pathways (APOA1 and APOB) to defend against free cholesterol overload, indicating inherent vulnerability to cholesterol-induced lipotoxicity. In tumor tissues (T), perturbation of apoptosis-associated pathways (GSK3β/β-catenin, caspase 3, BCL2/BAX) by excess cholesterol likely contributes to suppressed hepatic tumor progression. By contrast, in peritumoral tissues (P), excess cholesterol simultaneously disturbs proliferation-related (mTOR, NF-κB) and apoptosis-related pathways, which may counteract each other and exert minimal net effect on tumorigenesis. In our model, a high-cholesterol diet overwhelms adaptive efflux capacity, initiating a cascade of ER stress and oxidative stress that ultimately induces apoptosis and restricts tumor growth (Fig. 6F). These novel results provide a new perspective for further investigation of cholesterol functions in cancer biology.

Of note, our findings reveal a threshold-dependent dual role of cholesterol in hepatocarcinogenesis, resolving the apparent paradox between pathway-driven cholesterol accumulation and dietary cholesterol overload. Moderate cholesterol elevation in peritumoral tissue promotes oncogenic signaling and tumor progression, whereas excessive dietary cholesterol induces free cholesterol lipotoxicity, ER stress, and apoptosis to suppress the growth of established tumors. Importantly, these effects are mediated directly by cholesterol excess rather than secondary bile acid signaling or lipoprotein alterations, as supported by TDCA treatment and cholesterol efflux (APOA1/APOB) data. This biphasic, U-shaped model identifies cholesterol as a context-dependent regulator rather than a simply pro-tumorigenic factor.

Regarding BAs, its effects are complex and were considered to have dual effects of protection and pathogenesis on HCC [36]. On the one hand, BAs accumulation induced inflammation has toxic effects on hepatocytes and can promote the progress of HCC [37, 38]. On the other hand, HCC is rare in adult patients with cholestasis [39, 40]. In particular, it has been found that the BAs and their derivatives in HCC patients were down regulated comparing with cirrhosis patients [15]. Moreover, certain BAs, such as ursodeoxycholicacid (UDCA) and obenzcholic acid, have been shown to delay liver injury and HCC progression [13]. The anti-inflammatory, antioxidant and cytoprotective activities of UDCA may be helpful to improve the treatment of advanced liver disease, especially in combination with sorafenib and other drugs to improve the therapeutic effect of targeted drugs for HCC [41–43]. Besides, taurine-conjugated BAs exhibit less cytotoxic compared to unconjugated BAs and glycine-conjugated BAs [44]. TDCA, a taurine-conjugated BA which regulates immune function and inhibits various inflammatory responses, has been considered to have potential clinical application [44, 45].

BAs exert paradoxical effects during HCC progression, which can be reconciled by distinguishing cytotoxic BA accumulation from receptor-mediated signaling, as well as cell-specific responses between hepatocytes and tumor cells. In our Ras-driven HCC model, endogenous BA levels decline during tumor development, accompanied by downregulation of the tumor-suppressive receptor FXR. Impaired BA-FXR signaling releases intrinsic growth inhibition and fosters a pro-tumor microenvironment, independent of BA cytotoxicity. In contrast, exogenous TDCA—a low-cytotoxic taurine-conjugated BA—exerts tumor-suppressive effects mainly through activating TGR5 signaling rather than metabolic cytotoxicity. TDCA-induced TGR5 activation effectively inhibits oncogenic ERK and mTOR pathways, thereby blocking tumor growth. Collectively, this dual regulatory model resolves conflicting clinical and experimental observations: reduced physiological FXR signaling promotes tumor initiation, whereas targeted TGR5 activation by specific BA species restrains advanced tumor growth. These divergent outcomes arise from differential expression and functional coupling of FXR and TGR5 in premalignant and malignant hepatocytes, providing key insights for future studies on BA receptor functions across distinct stages of hepatocarcinogenesis.

While our current tissue-level analyses define the in vivo relevance of the Ras/ERK–SREBP2–CYP8B1 regulatory axis, they cannot fully distinguish tumor cell-autonomous mechanisms. Accumulated in vitro evidence has established a reciprocal crosstalk between cholesterol homeostasis and ERK signaling. For instance, Chen et al. demonstrated that dysregulated cholesterol synthesis can, in turn, activate the MEK/ERK pathway in HCC cells [46],underscoring the complex, bidirectional nature of this regulatory axis. In line with these cell-intrinsic regulations, our in vivo pharmacological interventions further verify the functional importance of this metabolic cascade. Further in vitro experiments, including cholesterol manipulation and detection of cell viability, apoptosis and ERK activity, will be conducted in subsequent research to precisely validate tumor cell-specific mechanisms.

Conclusion

This study presents several significant findings. First, we demonstrated that CHO/BA homeostasis-associated rate-limiting enzymes CYP8B1 are disordered in most human HCCs and that ERK hyperactivation is involved in at least a portion of human HCCs. By studying Ras-Tg mice, we further found that (1) Ras/ERK signaling represses CYP8B1, in part through SREBP2, thereby promoting cholesterol accumulation and reducing BA synthesis, and these effects play predominant roles in hepatocytes but partial roles in hepatoma cells, in which additional mechanisms enhance the phenomena; (2) excess CHO perturbs multiple signaling pathways and suppresses hepatic tumor development but has no effect on tumorigenesis, thus CHO toxicity is tightly controlled in hepatoma cells by active efflux of CHO; and (3) TDCA blocks hepatic tumorigenesis and development by inhibiting ERK and mTOR pathways in Ras-Tg. This study demonstrates that Ras/ERK signaling, potentially through SREBP2, modulates CYP8B1 expression and cholesterol/bile acid homeostasis to promote the initiation and progression of HCC, thus providing novel insights for clinical therapeutic strategies.
