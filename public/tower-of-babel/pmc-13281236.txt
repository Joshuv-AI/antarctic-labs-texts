Effects of different polishing and glazing systems on the physical properties of ceramics
Authors: Gonca Deste Gökay, Murat Şenol, Perihan Oyar, Ayhan Gürbüz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThe purpose of this study was to assess the surface roughness, color stability, and contact angle of lithium disilicate glass-ceramic (LD) and zirconia-reinforced lithium silicate (ZLS) ceramics following surface finishing and to compare these values using both manufacturer-specific polishing and glazing procedures and alternative methods designed for other types of ceramics.Materials and methodsLD and ZLS ceramics were used to fabricate 80 samples, which were allocated into eight groups (n = 10) according to manufacturer-specific polishing (VITA Suprinity Polishing Set Clinical and OptraGloss Ceramic Kit) and glazing protocols (VITA Akzent Plus and Ivocolor Glaze) and their interchanged applications. Measurements of surface roughness, contact angle, and color stability were made following the polishing and glazing processes. The results were evaluated using parametric statistical analysis.ResultsGlazing produced a statistically significant change with the LD ceramic, whereas polishing did not. In the ZLS group, there was a significant difference between the glazing methods, but there was no statistically significant difference between the polishing techniques. In both ceramics, the contact angle values of the glazed samples were greater than those of the polished samples.ConclusionSurface finishing procedures significantly affected both the color change of the ZLS ceramic and the surface roughness of the LD ceramics. The contact angle values of the glazed samples in both ceramics were approximately two to five times greater than those of the polished samples. It may be advisable for clinicians to use nonmanufacturer-specific techniques for polishing ceramic materials, especially in areas that do not affect their appearance, such as the back of the mouth.

Introduction

The accuracy, productivity, and uniformity of indirect dental restorative fabrication workflows have been greatly improved by developments in computer-aided design and computer-aided manufacturing (CAD/CAM) technology [1]. Monolithic ceramic blocks with a range of mechanical and aesthetic qualities have been created for restorations as a result of the growing use of CAD/CAM technology in dentistry. LD ceramics are unique in that they provide high strength and aesthetics. The flexural strength of these ceramics is increased by crystallization to the disilicate phase after being created in the metasilicate phase for CAD/CAM processing. Zirconia (0% by weight) can be added to the LD to reinforce it, combining the benefits of both materials [2, 3].

ZLS ceramics were first used in dentistry. They are made up of fine metasilicate and lithium orthophosphate crystals that are uniformly distributed throughout a glassy matrix and range in size from 0.5 to 1.0 μm [4]. Approximately half of the material’s microstructure is made up of the crystalline phase, which is directly linked to the material’s noticeable increase in optical characteristics over those of LD ceramics [5]. A finishing and polishing operation must be carried out before the final restoration is given to the patient because the CAD/CAM milling process usually results in a rough surface [6]. In clinical applications, a smooth surface is essential for restorative material color stability and long-term performance [7]. Manufacturers have created a variety of finishing and polishing procedures with several processes and specific parts to accomplish this goal [8]. Materials such as multistep polishing strips, polishing pastes with diamond filler, rubber polishing kits with varying grits, and surface-protective coatings such as glazes are frequently used in these procedures. Polishing, which is typically performed chairside without the use of lab equipment, is an abrasion-based method for improving and smoothing a glossy repair surface [9].

By sealing surface defects with a fire process in a porcelain furnace, glazing, on the other hand, produces a smooth, glossy finish and increases the mechanical endurance of ceramic restorations [10]. The most popular surface finishing technique before cementation is glazing in a porcelain furnace. However, according to recent studies, hand polishing can also result in a glossy, smooth surface that is on par with glazing [11]. For ceramic restorations, a variety of chairside polishing systems are already on the market; each is made in accordance with the manufacturer’s specifications and customized for a particular type of ceramic.

The lifetime and surface performance of these technologies vary [12]. Several studies have investigated how well different proprietary polishing processes work on different ceramic materials, and the findings show that certain systems perform better than others do [13]. However, the use of interchangeable polishing kits across various types of ceramics has not been thoroughly studied. For example, zirconia polishing techniques were shown to be quite successful even on feldspathic porcelain, according to Park et al. [12]. Similarly, Irusa et al. [14] reported that the zirconia polishing procedure produced the least amount of surface roughness on feldspathic and LD ceramics. However, the aforementioned study did not investigate whether glazing materials made by different manufacturers would produce comparable outcomes. Assessing the surface roughness, color stability, and contact angle of the LD and ZLS ceramics after glazing and polishing was the aim of this study. These values were then compared using the manufacturer-specific polishing and glazing systems as well as alternative systems made for different types of ceramics. Given the high expense of these goods, it may be less necessary for dentists to buy numerous specialist polishing or glazing materials if no appreciable differences across the systems were discovered. Efficiency may also be improved in clinical practice by reducing the variety of finishing materials [14].

The null hypothesis of the present study was that there would be no significant difference in the surface roughness, color stability, or contact angle of the LD and ZLS ceramics following the finishing procedures when either the manufacturer-specific or interchanged finishing systems were used.

Materials and methods

Specimen preparation

In this study, CAD/CAM standard blocks (7 × 12 mm) of LD ceramic (IPS e.max CAD HT A2; Ivoclar Vivadent, Schaan, Liechtenstein) and ZLS ceramic (Vita Suprinity HT A2; Vita Zahnfabrik, Bad Säckingen, Germany) were cut into 1.5-mm-thick samples. The compositions and structures of these materials are shown in Table 1. A total of 80 samples were sliced from the LD and ZLS CAD/CAM blocks using a low-speed diamond saw (Metkon Micracut 201; Metkon Instruments, Bursa, Turkey) under continuous water cooling (n = 40). To standardize the surface roughness, each sample was sequentially polished under water irrigation using silicon carbide abrasive papers of 600, 800, and 1200 grit, with each step applied for 30 s.

The specimens were finished to standardized dimensions of 1.5 mm × 7 mm × 12 mm using a two-step protocol with an electric high-speed handpiece operating at 60,000 rpm. First, each sample was ground using a fine-grit diamond bur under a constant load of 0.5 N for 60 s. A second finishing step was subsequently performed using an extrafine diamond bur under identical load and duration parameters [7]. Since variations in applied pressure have been demonstrated to have a substantial effect on surface roughness in dental ceramics, a constant axial pressure of approximately 0.5 N was maintained throughout the process using a standardized hand stabilization technique to reduce operator-related variability [15].

The manufacturer-recommended polishing and glazing protocols were as follows: for the ZLS (Vita Suprinity HT A2; Vita Zahnfabrik), polishing was performed using the VITA Suprinity Polishing Set Clinical and glazing with VITA Akzent Plus Powder/Fluid; for the LD ceramic (IPS e.max CAD HT A2; Ivoclar Vivadent), polishing was carried out using the OptraGloss Ceramic Kit and glazing with IPS Ivocolor Glaze Powder/Fluid. The experimental groups were designed to include both manufacturer-recommended polishing and glazing protocols and intentionally interchanged protocols between materials.

The ZLS (Group ZLS) and LD (Group LD) specimens were split into four subgroups at random on the basis of the completed procedure (n = 10) (Table 4): ZLS.1: VITA Suprinity Polishing Set Clinical (VS-P) (VITA Zahnfabrik); ZLS.2: VITA Zahnfabrik); ZLS.3: OptraGloss Ceramic Kit (OG-P) (Ivoclar Vivadent); ZLS.4: IPS Ivocolor Glaze Powder/Fluid (IPS-G) (Ivoclar Vivadent); LD.1: OptraGloss Ceramic Kit (Ivoclar Vivadent); LD.2: IPS Ivocolor Glowder/Fluid (Ivoclar Vivadent); LD.3: VITA Suprinity Polishing Set Clinical (VITA Zahnfabrik); and LD.4: VITA Akzent Plus Powder/Fluid (VITA Zahnfabrik).

Diamond-coated cups were used for the hand finishing processes performed on the subgroups (ZLS.1, LD.1, ZLS.3, and LD.3), and they were changed every two specimens to maintain abrasive uniformity. In accordance with the manufacturer’s recommendations, the treatments were carried out using a contra-angle laboratory handpiece (Kavo Ewl 4990; KaVo Dental Gmbh, Germany) under water cooling to avoid overheating and microstructural changes. To maintain uniformity, all the finishing and polishing procedures were performed by the same experienced operator, and the samples were ultrasonically cleaned in distilled water for 10 min to remove residual debris and surface contaminants, followed by air drying. To maintain abrasive uniformity, the diamond-coated cups were changed every two specimens. To avoid overheating and microstructural changes, the same skilled operator carried out each step under water cooling. The polishing procedure was further standardized and defined to increase reproducibility. In accordance with previously published polishing protocols for CAD/CAM glass ceramics [16, 17], the diamond-impregnated polishing tools utilized in this work conform to a sequential grit reduction scheme (coarse, medium, and fine phases).

A glazing material (VITA Akzent Plus, in powder–fluid form), prepared in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions, was applied to the samples and fired using a manufacturer-recommended VITA Vacumat 6000 furnace (VITA Zahnfabrik, Bad Säckingen, Germany) as part of the glaze-firing finishing procedure for the ZLS.2 and LD.4 subgroups. After a 4-minute drying phase at 400 °C, the firing cycle proceeded to a 5-minute heating phase at a rate of 80 °C/min until it reached 800 °C, where it was held for one minute without the use of a vacuum. Particular focus was placed on layer consistency and application uniformity for the glazing processes. Under carefully monitored handling circumstances, a fine sable brush with consistent strokes was used to apply the glazing paste. To avoid pooling and uneven thickness, the extra glaze material was carefully removed. The application sought to obtain a clinically indicated thin and uniform glaze layer in accordance with laboratory techniques described for LD ceramics, even though glaze thickness was not directly measured with a micrometer [18]. To guarantee uniformity in thermal exposure, the firing cycles closely adhered to the manufacturer-recommended crystallization and glazing plans.

A manufacturer-recommended Programat P300 furnace (Ivoclar, Schaan, Liechtenstein) was used to apply and burn IPS Ivocolor Glaze (powder-fluid form), which was made in accordance with the manufacturer’s specifications, for the LD.2 and ZLS.4 subgroups. After a 6-minute drying phase at 403 °C, the firing cycle was heated at a rate of 60 °C per minute until it reached a final temperature of 710 °C. For one minute, the temperature was maintained. The temperature range for vacuum was 450 °C to 709 °C.

Surface roughness

Following the polishing and finishing processes, a contact profilometer (Perthometer M2; Mahr Gmbh, Ingolstadt, Germany) was used to quantify the surface roughness. A cutoff value of 0.8 mm, a measuring length of 5 mm, and a stylus speed of 0.5 mm/s were used to analyze each sample (n = 10). A speed of 0.5 mm/s was used for the measurements. Three measurements were made for each sample: one in the center and one mm to the right and left. The final Ra value in micrometers (µm) was calculated by taking the mean of these measurements.

Color measurements

Color measurements of all the samples were performed before and after the adjustment and surface finishing procedures using a dental spectrophotometer (VITA Easyshade Advance; Vita Zahnfabrik) against a standardized gray background. Color coordinates were recorded in the CIELAB color space under controlled measurement conditions. The Commission Internationale de l’Éclairage (CIE) developed the CIELAB system, which uses three factors to quantify color: a* from green (− a*) to red (+ a*), b* from blue (− b*) to yellow (+ b*), and L* from 0 (black) to 100 (white).

As directed by the manufacturer, the spectrophotometer was calibrated in “Basic shade” mode prior to each measurement. Each sample was measured three times to reduce variability, and the analysis was based on the average value. To maintain uniformity, the same operator performed each color assessment. The variables ∆L′, ∆C′, and ∆H′ in this equation represent variations in hue, chroma, and brightness, respectively. With ΔE00 > 0.8 denoting the perceptibility limit and ΔE00 > 1.7 denoting the clinical unacceptability limit, the clinical thresholds were taken into consideration [20].

Contact angle measurement

The sessile drop method and a goniometer were used to evaluate contact angles in a controlled laboratory setting. The gadget was linked to a PC running specific contact angle evaluation software (DSA3, V1.0.3-08, Krüss). Throughout the measurements, the room temperature was maintained at 23 ± 1 °C. To maintain environmental uniformity, all the measurements were made during the same lab session even though the relative humidity was not actively controlled [21].

A calibrated microsyringe was used to deposit an 11 µL droplet of distilled water. Five seconds after the droplet was placed, the contact angle was measured. As suggested in surface energy evaluation studies of dental biomaterials, this short stability time reduces gravitational distortion and evaporation effects [22, 23]. To ensure measurement reliability, the droplet symmetry and baseline stability were visually verified using the goniometer’s real-time imaging system before angle calculation.

Statistical analysis

Finishability of the materials (Group LD vs. Group ZLS) and performance of the assigned finishing systems (Subgroups LD.1–4 and ZLS.1–4) in terms of surface roughness, contact angle, and color stability were compared using a two-way analysis of variance. (ANOVA) with post hoc Tukey’s HSD and dependent t tests for paired samples to assess the suggested null hypothesis. The normality of the data distribution was examined using the Kolmogorov‒Smirnov test. The equality of variances was assessed using Levene’s tests. All the statistical tests were conducted using SPSS 18.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) with a significance level of α = 0.05.

VS-P had the lowest Ra value, whereas IPS-G had the highest Ra value.

Color change

The first color measurement was taken before polishing or glazing was applied to the ceramic samples, and a second color measurement was taken after polishing or glazing was applied. The difference between the two measurements was calculated as ΔE. The CIELAB color coordinates (ΔL*, Δa*, Δb*) are listed in Table 6. The results and statistical analysis of the color change ΔE values of the ZLS and LD ceramic groups made using different surface finishing techniques are shown in Table 7. A statistically significant difference in ΔE values between the LD and ZLS ceramics was found to be caused by the IPS Ivocolor Glaze alone (p < 0.05). In the ZLS group, there was a significant difference between the glazing methods (IPS-G and VA-G) (p < 0.005), but there was no statistically significant difference between the polishing techniques (p > 0.05). Furthermore, there was no discernible change in the LD ceramic group, but there was a statistically significant difference between the polishing and glazing processes (p < 0.05). Compared with the glazing process, the polishing process resulted in lower ΔE values in both ceramic groups. The lowest ∆E value was obtained for the ZLS ceramic surface treated with OG-P, whereas the highest ∆E value was obtained for the ZLS ceramic surface treated with IPS-G. For both ceramics, the IPS-G process produced larger ∆E values than the other processes did. Applying the IPS-G treatment to ZLS ceramics resulted in a clinically noticeable color change. Discussion

This study examined the surface roughness, color stability, and contact angle of LD and ZLS ceramics following glazing and polishing. These values were then compared following surface finishing using glazing and polishing systems specific to the manufacturer as well as alternative systems designed for different types of ceramics. The polishing and glazing processes caused a statistically significant difference in the surface roughness of the LD ceramic and caused a statistically significant difference in color change in the ZLS ceramics; thus, the null hypothesis was partially accepted.

Vichi et al. [24] reported that the best methods for reducing the roughness of CAD-CAM silica-based glass ceramics were manual finishing and polishing for 60 s and glazing paste, which supported the results of the present study. In the present study, the Ra values of the ZLS ceramic were lower than those of the LD ceramic. This may be due to the different crystal sizes of these ceramics. ZLS has a mean crystal size of approximately 0.5 μm, whereas LD has a mean crystal size of 1.5 μm [25–27, 27]. The lower crystalline volume and finer microstructure of ZLS might account for its smoother surface than that of LD after polishing [22]. 28 Furthermore, the concentration of silicon dioxide differed between ZLS (56.0–64.0) and LD (57.0–80.0). Because the crystalline phase increases with increasing silicon dioxide concentration, the superior crystalline content of the LD may account for its reduced capacity to be smoothed after polishing [28]. Additionally, because zirconia more effectively polished the material, the increased zirconium dioxide content of ZLS may help explain its reduced superficial roughness following polishing [28]. Microstructural variations can account for the trend toward lower surface roughness values in ZLS relative to those in LD. LD has larger lithium disilicate crystals (~ 1.5 μm), whereas ZLS has smaller lithium silicate crystals (~ 0.5 μm) [29, 29] Larger crystals are more prone to partial pull-out or favor protrusion from the glassy matrix during polishing, which increases surface defects. On the other hand, ZLS’s finer and more uniformly dispersed crystalline phase minimizes microcrack formation and crystal dislodgment by lowering the concentration of interfacial stress during abrasion. Additionally, during surface finishing, zirconia reinforcement improves mechanical properties and helps to increase structural integrity [30]. The smoother postpolishing surface shown in the ZLS samples is probably supported by this enhanced resistance to microfracture development. In the present study, the polishing/glazing treatments did not significantly affect the Ra values of the different ceramics. Compared with polished ceramics, glazed ceramics were found to have higher surface roughness levels. This outcome is in line with the findings of earlier research [31, 32].

While Irusa et al. [14] evaluated various cross-polishing kits for zirconia and LD/ZLS, their investigations were primarily limited to surface roughness. They reported that the lowest surface rougness values were achieved with zirconia polishers; however, their protocol excluded the glazing stage. The present study significantly extends these findings by incorporating glazing into the experimental design and introducing critical parameters such as the contact angle and color stability. The present results indicate that the glazing groups exhibited perceptible color change (ΔE00 > 1.7), highlighting potential clinical risks regarding the aesthetic longevity of ZLS that were not addressed in the Ra-focused analysis by Irusa et al. [14]

The surface roughness of the LD ceramics but not that of the ZLS ceramics was significantly affected by the glazing treatment. This discrepancy can be explained by differences in the materials’ microstructural properties [25–27, 27]. With the exception of those glazed with IPS-G, the surface roughness of the ceramics was within the range that is considered clinically acceptable. The surface roughness values of the ceramics did not significantly differ as a result of the glazing or polishing procedures.

An optimal threshold surface roughness of 0.2 μm, at which time bacterial retention is facilitated and the incidence of biological problems increases, has been proposed by certain in vivo studies [33]. Additionally, the patient may experience discomfort if the surface roughness of the sensory fibers of the tongue is greater than 0.5 μm. However, depending on the patient’s age, location, and type of tooth, natural enamel roughness varies between 0.64 and 0.90 μm [34, 35]. With the exception of the samples where IPS-G was applied, which were significantly below the abrasive wearing threshold (1.5 μm), all of the Ra values measured in this investigation were reported to vary between 0.13 μm and 0.18 μm with regard to the clinical acceptability of the completed surfaces [36].

This study revealed that various ceramic materials can be effectively polished using nonmanufacturer-specific polishing techniques. Compared with the manufacturer-specific polishers, the other polishing systems did not exhibit appreciable variations in profilometry, despite the production of surfaces with varying surface roughness.

Glazing with IPS-G caused a statistically significant difference between the LD and ZLS ceramics. Glazing groups for both ceramics demonstrated clinically noticeable color changes. Accordingly, glazing may influence the optical properties of these materials and should be considered cautiously. Previous studies have demonstrated that surface finishing procedures, including glazing, may affect the optical properties and color stability of dental ceramics [37, 38]. These changes have been associated with modifications in surface texture, light reflection, and microstructural characteristics after glazing procedures. Therefore, the clinically noticeable color changes observed in the present study may be related to the alterations induced by the glazing process. Additional in vitro and clinical studies are required to confirm these findings. The degradation of the glaze layer over time under intraoral thermal cycling and pH fluctuations may alter the roughness and optical properties of the underlying ceramic structure. The high color change exhibited by ZLS with glazing materials indicates that this material requires a more meticulous approach in surface finishing protocols. Following the various surface finishing, there was no statistically significant change in the color of the LD ceramic. The microstructural and chemical differences between the ZLS and LD ceramics may be responsible for the clinically unsatisfactory color change observed in the glazed ZLS samples (ΔE > 1.7). Compared with LD (0–8 wt%), ZLS has a much greater zirconia concentration (8–12 wt%), and its crystalline phase is more finely distributed (~ 0.5 μm). Zirconia particles alter light-scattering behavior by increasing the refractive index heterogeneity within the glass-ceramic matrix. The relationships among the crystalline phase size, distribution, and surrounding glass matrix are crucial for the optical performance of glass-ceramics. Differences in the refractive index and thermal compatibility at the glaze–ceramic interface might increase diffuse light scattering and change light transmission when a glaze material designed mainly for LD ceramics is applied to ZLS. Any discrepancy in the optical characteristics of the substrate and glaze becomes crucial because glazing adds an extra layer of glass. While glazing on ZLS produced unacceptable ΔE values, cross-type polishing systems remained clinically acceptable because the polishing systems act by mechanical surface refinement without the addition of a secondary optical layer [29, 30]. Glaze firing parameters also affect optical behavior and surface topography. Surface roughness and light reflection in LD ceramics have been shown to be strongly affected by glaze thickness and burning procedures [18]. Compared with LDs, small changes in the thickness of the glazing layer may result in more pronounced optical effects because the ZLS has a finer and more uniform microstructure. Compared with the glazed groups, the polished groups showed less color change for both ceramics. Glazed surfaces exhibit higher surface roughness compared with mechanically polished surfaces due to glaze-related surface irregularities and post-firing heterogeneities whereas polishing procedures tend to produce smoother and more homogeneous surfaces [39, 40]. Surface topography influences the optical behavior of dental ceramics, as increased surface roughness enhances light scattering and affects translucency and perceived color [39]. In this context, the differences in color change observed between glazed and polished groups may be partially attributed to variations in surface roughness induced by the finishing procedures. However, this effect may also be influenced by ceramic composition, glazing protocol, and processing conditions, which may explain the variability reported in the literature. This study demonstrated that nonmanufacturer-specific techniques might be used to polish ceramic materials, particularly in areas where they do not affect appearance, such as the posterior regions of the mouth. The degradation of the glaze layer over time under intraoral thermal cycling and pH fluctuations may alter the roughness and optical properties of the underlying ceramic structure.

Compared with the glazed samples, the polished samples had lower contact angle values for both types of ceramics. The OG-P group had the lowest contact angle values (5.41 and 6.78, respectively). The contact angle values created in VS-P were approximately twice as large as those formed in OG-P in both ceramics. Following two distinct glaze applications, the contact angle values for both ceramics were similar. In both ceramics, the contact angle values of the glazed samples were approximately two to five times greater than those of the polished samples. Compared with polishing processes, glazing applications generally reduce the hydrophilicity and increase the contact angle.

From a clinical perspective, it should be noted that the fundamental advantage of manual polishing systems is their ability to complete in-office restoration in a single session while retaining performance comparable to or superior to that of glazing systems [41, 42]. Manual polishing allows clinicians to complete in-office restoration without the need for thermal treatment, which speeds up and streamlines the process overall. This is especially important given the growing popularity of monolithic restorations.

On the basis of the results of this study, it may be advisable for clinicians to use nonmanufacturer-specific techniques for polishing ceramic materials, especially in areas that do not affect appearance, such as the back of the mouth. The glazing method for bothceramics is not recommended for the use of anterior teeth because it causes clinically noticeable color changes (∆E00 > 1.7). The highest ∆E value of the ZLS ceramics glazed with IPS is due to the high positive a value (red) and the high negative b value (blue). Compared with polished ceramics, glazed ceramics exhibited higher color coordinates a and b and had negative color components (-a: green axis, -b: blue axis), contributing to higher ∆E values. Although minor shade adjustments before cementation may partially compensate for color deviations, such corrections may not fully restore optical harmony because of changes in translucency and light scattering behavior. Therefore, preventive material selection rather than postadjustment correction appears to be the most predictable clinical approach. On the basis of the present findings, interchanged polishing systems may be considered clinically acceptable in posterior functional regions, where minor color variations are less perceptible and surface roughness remains within biologically acceptable limits. However, in anterior aesthetic zones—where color stability, translucency, and gloss are critical—manufacturer-recommended glazing protocols may be preferable to minimize the risk of perceptible color shifts. Owing to variations in translucency and light scattering behavior, small shade modifications made prior to cementation may partially rectify color discrepancies but may not completely restore optical harmony. Consequently, the most reliable clinical strategy seems to be preventive material selection as opposed to postadjustment correction. In posterior functional zones, where surface roughness remains within medically acceptable bounds and slight color differences are less noticeable, interchanged polishing techniques may be deemed clinically appropriate on the basis of the current data. To reduce the possibility of noticeable color changes, manufacturer-recommended glazing procedures might be better in anterior aesthetic zones, where color stability, translucency, and gloss are crucial.

In addition to time efficiency, cost considerations are relevant in clinical decision-making. Chairside polishing kits are typically reusable for multiple restorations, whereas glaze materials require furnace equipment, firing cycles, and additional laboratory steps. When both systems demonstrate comparable surface performance, the use of interchanged polishing kits may reduce the inventory burden and overall material expenditure in clinical practice. Compared with glazing (lab-based, 1–2 h total cycle), polishing systems offer higher operating efficiency (chairside, 2–5 min per restoration) and lower recurring costs (~$20–50/kit for 20–50 uses). Cross-type kits (e.g., PLZ or Dialite ZR) are versatile for ZLS/LD but may cost 20–30% more upfront than manufacturer-specific kits (~$100–150 vs. $80–120) [43].

From an efficiency standpoint, the two-step manual polishing process took approximately 120 s for each sample, while the drying, heating, holding, and cooling cycles of the glaze-firing processes took approximately 15 to 20 min. As a result, the polishing processes were eight to ten times quicker than the glazing processes were. This time gap may have a major effect on clinical productivity and patient turnover in chairside CAD/CAM workflows. Clinical decision-making takes cost into account in addition to time efficiency. While glaze materials require furnace equipment, firing cycles, and additional laboratory operations, chairside polishing kits are usually reusable for several restorations. The use of interchangeable polishing kits may lessen the inventory load and total material expense in clinical practice when both techniques result in equal surface performance.

OptraGloss (OG-P) has emerged as the optimal finishing protocol for achieving successful resin cementation and effective plaque control because of its combination of low surface roughness and superior hydrophilicity compared with those of other treatments. Caution is advised regarding the application of IPS Ivocolor (IPS-G), as it exacerbates surface roughness and may compromise aesthetic outcomes in ZLS materials. The surface dynamics of ZLS and LD ceramics are differentially influenced by finishing agents, highlighting the necessity of material-specific selection over a standardized finishing protocol.

A limitation of this study is that surface roughness was measured using a contact profilometer at three points per sample, which may not fully represent the overall surface topography compared with full-field, noncontact methods. More research utilizing both contact and noncontact techniques to measure surface roughness and compare/combine the results can yield additional data and insights, enabling clinicians to be advised appropriately. Another limitation is that the use of two different types of ceramics as well as polishing and glazing materials is among the limitations of this study. Future studies are necessary to eliminate these limitations.

Conclusion

Both the surface roughness of the LD ceramics and the color change of the ZLS ceramics were significantly affected by the surface finishing technique.

In both ceramics, the contact angle values of the glazed samples were approximately two to five times greater than those of the polished samples. On the basis of the results of the present study, clinicians can advise the use of nonmanufacturer-specific techniques for polishing ceramic materials in the posterior regions of the mouth where aesthetics are not affected.
