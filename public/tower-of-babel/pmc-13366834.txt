Biochemical composition and demographic distribution of kidney stones: a hospital-based cross-sectional study in Eastern Nepal
Authors: Binod Kumar Lal Das, Bishal Khattri, Rikesh Jung Karkee, Santosh Kumar Yadav, Punam Jha, Madhab Lamsal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNephrolithiasis is a major urological condition globally, with an increasing prevalence linked to shifting dietary patterns and climatic changes. In Nepal, the growing number of kidney stone cases presents a substantial challenge to the healthcare system. Understanding the chemical nature of these stones and their relationship with demographic factors and systemic biochemical markers is essential for developing effective localized clinical management and prevention strategies.ObjectiveThis study aims to analyze the biochemical composition of kidney stones and evaluate their demographic distribution among patients at a tertiary care center in Eastern Nepal.MethodsA descriptive hospital-based cross-sectional study was conducted at the B.P. Koirala Institute of Health Sciences (BPKIHS), Dharan. The study included final 111 patients who underwent surgical intervention for urolithiasis. The stone composition was determined using standard qualitative inorganic chemical analysis techniques. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS version 11.5, employing Chi-square tests to assess associations.ResultsThe study population showed a male predominance, with males comprising 57.7% and females 42.3%, resulting in a male-to-female ratio of 1.36:1. The mean age of participants was 43.51 ± 13.72 years, with the highest prevalence in the 31–45 age group (48.6%). Chemical analysis revealed that calcium oxalate was the most prevalent stone type (59.5%), followed by calcium phosphate (12.6%), struvite (11.7%), and mixed stones (10.8%). Uric acid and cystine stones were least common (2.7% each). Urinary pH was significantly associated with stone composition (p = 0.01), with acidic pH favoring Calcium Oxalate and alkaline pH favoring Calcium Phosphate. Struvite stones exhibited the largest dimensions (median 20 mm).ConclusionCalcium oxalate is the main constituent of kidney stones in Eastern Nepal, with a higher occurrence in males and individuals aged 31–45 years, particularly from the Terai region. Urinary pH showed a significant association with stone composition. Struvite stones were associated with larger stone size. These findings highlight the importance of routine stone analysis and urinary pH evaluation for targeted prevention and personalized management of nephrolithiasis.

Introduction

Kidney stones, known as nephrolithiasis or urolithiasis, are solid mineral formations that develop within the renal calyces and pelvis when urine becomes overly saturated with specific minerals [1]. This excessive saturation prompts a complex process of crystal nucleation, growth, aggregation, and retention within the kidneys [2]. These stones exhibit a wide range of physical characteristics, varying from tiny sand-like grains to rare instances reaching the size of golf balls, with textures that can be smooth or jagged and colors ranging from yellow to brown [1, 3]. Globally, kidney stones are recognized as the third most common condition affecting the urinary tract, following urinary tract infections and prostate-related pathological disorders [4].

Epidemiological data indicate that urolithiasis is a widespread global issue, affecting approximately 12% of men and 5% of women at some point in their lifetimes. [5]. While the prevalence varies between countries, approximately 80% of stones worldwide are composed of calcium oxalate (CaOx) and calcium phosphate (CaP) [6]. Other types, such as uric acid, struvite, and cystine stones, occur less frequently, making up roughly 9%, 10%, and 1% of cases, respectively [6, 7]. The rising prevalence and high recurrence rates, which can reach 60% to 80% over a lifetime in Asian populations, impose a substantial economic and social burden on both developed and developing nations [8, 9].

The development of these stones is multifactorial, involving a combination of endogenous and exogenous lithogenic factors. [10]. These include age, gender, dietary patterns, fluid intake, and climate, as well as systemic comorbidities such as hypertension, diabetes, and obesity [11, 12]. Furthermore, factors such as urinary pH play a critical role in stone formation; for instance, acidic urine favors the formation of uric acid stones, while alkaline urine increases the likelihood of calcium phosphate and struvite stones.

Despite growing health concerns, there is a notable scarcity of localized research on the detailed chemical composition of stones and their associated demographic and clinical risk factors in Eastern Nepal. Understanding these regional variations is essential, as dietary and environmental factors contribute differently to stone formation across distinct geographical areas. This study aims to analyze the biochemical composition of kidney stones and determine their association with demographic and clinical characteristics in a hospital-based population in Eastern Nepal. By focusing on a defined geographical area, this study enables a more targeted exploration of potential variations in kidney stone patterns, influenced by demographic, cultural, and environmental factors. The findings are expected to contribute valuable insight for both clinical management and public health interventions aimed at reducing the burden of kidney stone disease in Nepal.

Methods

Study design and setting

This research was designed as a hospital-based cross-sectional study to investigate biochemical composition and demographic distribution of kidney stones. The study was conducted at the B.P. Koirala Institute of Health Sciences (BPKIHS), a tertiary care center in Dharan, Nepal. The research involved a collaborative effort between the Department of Biochemistry and the Department of Surgery, where clinical assessments and laboratory analyses were performed. The study duration spanned from September 2024 to July 2025.

Study population and sampling technique

The study population consisted of patients diagnosed with kidney stones who were successfully operated on in the Department of Surgery. Patients were recruited consecutively during the study period based on eligibility criteria. Only surgically retrieved stones were included for analysis; patients managed conservatively without intervention were not included, as stone samples were not available for chemical analysis. A convenience sampling approach was applied, and the final analysis included 111 participants who met the inclusion criteria as shown in Fig. 1.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria required participants to be adult patients (≥ 18 years) diagnosed with kidney stones, successfully operated on within the Surgery Department, and willing to provide written informed consent. Conversely, the exclusion criteria were limited to those patients who did not provide consent for participation.

Ethical considerations

The research protocol received formal approval from the Institutional Review Committee (IRC) of BPKIHS (Reference Number: IRC 62/081/082). The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles and regulations established by the Declaration of Helsinki. Before any data collection, participants were provided with a comprehensive Information Sheet explaining the study objectives and procedures. All subjects provided written informed consent; in the case of illiterate participants, consent was obtained from their legal guardians in the presence of a witness. Participants were assured of confidentiality and anonymity, with the right to withdraw from the study at any time without affecting their medical care.

Data collection and standardized questionnaire

A structured proforma/questionnaire was used to collect comprehensive data from each participant. This included age, gender, demographic location, stone localization, stone size, and history of chronic disease present.

Anthropometric and physical examination

A general physical examination was performed on all participants. Body Mass Index (BMI) was determined by dividing weight in kilograms by the square of height in meters. It was classified as underweight, normal, overweight, and obese. Blood pressure was measured using a standard aneroid-type sphygmomanometer after the subject had rested for at least 5 min in a supported seated position.

Qualitative chemical examination of calculi

Following surgical removal, stone fragments were rinsed with normal water and dried in an incubator. Large stones were cut longitudinally, while smaller ones were powdered for qualitative chemical analysis using the inorganic chemistry techniques as described in Practical Clinical Biochemistry by Varley (4th edition) [13]. Statistical analysis

Data were entered into Microsoft Excel 2021 and analyzed using SPSS version 11.5. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was used to determine the normality of data distribution. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages, while numerical data were summarized as mean ± SD or median. Chi-square tests were applied to assess associations between categorical data. A p-value of ≤ 0.05 was regarded as statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the study population

A total of 111 participants diagnosed with nephrolithiasis were included in this study. The group had more males than females, with 64 males (57.7%) and 47 females (42.3%). The mean age of the population was 43.51 ± 13.72 years, with the highest prevalence found in the 31–45 years age group (48.6%), followed by the 46–60 years age group (27%). Regarding geographic distribution, a majority of patients (75.7%) were from the Terai region, while 24.3% resided in hilly or mountainous areas.

Biochemical composition of kidney stones

Qualitative chemical analysis showed that calcium oxalate (CaOx) was the most frequently observed stone type, accounting for 59.5% (n = 66) of the samples. This was followed by Calcium Phosphate (CaP) at 12.6%, Struvite at 11.7%, and Mixed stones (Calcium Oxalate and Calcium Phosphate) at 10.8%. Uric Acid (UA) and Cystine stones were less common, each representing 2.7% of the total sample. Demographic and clinical distribution of stone types

The distribution of stone types varied by gender, although the overall association was not statistically significant (p = 0.587). CaOx stones were more frequent in males (38 cases) than in females (28 cases). Notably, Uric Acid stones were only observed in male patients, while Struvite stones were slightly more common in females (n = 7) than in males (n = 6).

Age-wise, the 31–45 age group carried the heaviest burden of CaOx stones (31 cases). In participants over 60 years of age, there was a noticeable increase in the proportion of Uric Acid stones. In terms of anatomical location, the kidney accounted for 60.4% of all stone types, with the ureter coming in second at 24.3%, the urethra at 9.9%, and the bladder at 5.4%.

A statistically significant association was found between urinary pH and stone composition (p = 0.01). Acidic urine (pH ≤ 5.5) was strongly associated with CaOx stones (36 cases), whereas neutral or alkaline urine (pH > 5.5) was associated with a higher prevalence of CaP stones (13 cases). Association with stone size

Analysis of stone dimensions showed that Struvite stones tended to be the largest, with a median size of approximately 20 mm and some exceeding 40 mm. CaOx stones also showed a wide range, with a median of 15 mm, while UA and Cystine stones were generally smaller and more uniform, clustering between 10 mm and 17 mm. Discussion

This hospital-based cross-sectional study conducted at the B.P. Koirala Institute of Health Sciences (BPKIHS), analyzed the biochemical composition and demographic distribution of kidney stones among 111 patients in Eastern Nepal. Understanding the regional variation in stone composition is vital because urolithiasis is a multifactorial condition influenced by geography, climate, and different metabolic factors.

The study population exhibited a male predominance (57.7% male vs. 42.3% female), consistent with global trends where males are more prone to nephrolithiasis. A study conducted by Joshi HN et al. at Kathmandu University reported a nearly similar distribution of male and female patients, with 57.95% being male and 42.05% female [14]. Numerous studies on urolithiasis have shown a higher prevalence in males, with reported male-to-female ratios ranging from 1.1:1 to 7.6:1 [15–17]. In our study, the ratio was found to be 1.36, which is consistent with these observations. The studies conducted by Shakhssalim N et al. and Gupta K et al. reported that testosterone and dihydrotestosterone may promote the development of calcium oxalate nephrolithiasis, whereas estrogen appears to have a protective effect against stone formation [18, 19]. However, since the role of testosterone in human metabolism is highly complex and cannot be fully clarified through a cross-sectional national study, further research is needed in the future [20].

The age group between 31 and 45 years old had the highest rate of stone formation (48.6%), indicating that working-age adults are the most vulnerable group in Eastern Nepal. This aligns with other regional data indicating that the primary burden of urolithiasis falls upon individuals in their most productive years. Similarly, a comparable study by Basukala S et al., at Kathmandu Military Hospital, reported 51% of patients fell within the same age group [21]. Geographically, the majority of patients (75.7%) were from the Terai region. The hot climate of the Terai, combined with potential fluid loss through perspiration, likely contributes to higher urinary concentration and supersaturation of minerals.

Regarding medical history, approximately one-fifth of the participants (20.7%) reported having a chronic disease, while one-third (33.3%) reported a family history of kidney stones. These factors are recognized as significant contributors to the risk of stone formation. In the cohort study of 7561 veterans with kidney stones in the united states showed that hyperparathyroidism increases the risk of kidney stone formation by enhancing calcium excretion, which results from increased bone resorption (known as resorptive hypercalciuria) [22]. A study done by Singh S et al. between November 2011 and April 2013 suggested that conditions such as diabetes mellitus, hypertension, urinary tract infections, gout, inflammatory bowel disease, chronic diarrhea, and malignancies like leukemia and lymphoma have been linked to the development of kidney stones [23]. Not surprisingly, kidney stone formation was more common among individuals with a family history of urolithiasis, which may be partly explained by shared dietary habits within the family, as certain foods are known to increase the risk of stone formation. A case control study done by Shirazi F et al. found a significant association between family history and a two-fold increase in urolithiasis risk [24].

More than half of the participants (52.3%) were classified as normal weight based on their body mass index (BMI), whereas a significant percentage were either overweight (33.3%) or obese (8.1%). Regarding body mass index (BMI), Taylor et al. reported a significant association between higher BMI and the development of urolithiasis, likely due to increased urinary excretion of calcium oxalate and uric acid. [25] In contrast, the present study observed that lower BMI was also linked to a higher risk of kidney stone formation, consistent with the findings of Trinchieri et al. and Bokhari et al [26].

In terms of chemical composition, Calcium Oxalate (CaOx) was identified as the most prevalent stone type, accounting for 59.5% of all analyzed calculi. This confirms that calcium-based stones remain the dominant variety in Nepal, mirroring international trends where they typically comprise 60–80% of all cases [27, 28]. The high prevalence of CaOx in this cohort is likely multifactorial and may be influenced by a combination of high dietary oxalate intake and low fluid volume, which increases the supersaturation of oxalate in the urine. While dietary factors may play a role, current evidence suggests that calcium oxalate nephrolithiasis is primarily influenced by overall dietary balance, hydration status, and metabolic factors rather than any single dietary component. In Nepal, dietary patterns rich in oxalate-containing foods, such as high tea consumption, combined with low fluid intake influenced by socioeconomic and geographical factors, may play a contributory but not exclusive role in stone formation [29].

After calcium oxalate stones, calcium phosphate and struvite stones emerged as the next most common types, representing 12.6% and 11.7% of the study population, respectively. The presence of struvite stones is particularly noteworthy as they are generally associated with urinary tract infections (UTIs) caused by urease-producing bacteria. While CaOx stones were more frequent in males, Struvite stones were slightly more common in females (n = 7 vs. n = 6), which correlates with the higher incidence of UTIs in the female population.

In our study, 60.4% of the stone localized in the kidney and 24.3% in the ureter. Interestingly, while urolithiasis affects all ages, our data showed a distinct age-associated shift in composition. In participants older than 60 years, the proportion of Uric Acid (UA) stones increased significantly. This trend likely reflects age-related changes in renal function and a decline in urinary pH, as urinary pH is the critical determinant for uric acid crystallization. This study highlights that age and urinary pH appear to be key factors influencing uric acid (UA) stone formation, which is similar to the study done by Zhang et al. in central China [30].

A major finding was the significant association between urinary pH and stone composition (p = 0.01). Acidic urine (pH ≤ 5.5) was highly associated with CaOx stone formation, whereas neutral or alkaline urine (pH > 5.5) favored Calcium Phosphate stones [31]. This highlights the importance of pH management as a preventive strategy; for instance, the use of urine-alkalinizing agents can mitigate the risk of uric acid and certain calcium stones.

Although data on dietary risk factors for nephrolithiasis in Nepal remain limited, evidence from international studies consistently highlights several key contributors, including low fluid intake, high consumption of sodium and animal protein, obesity, and insufficient intake of fruits and vegetables [32]. Studies from neighboring South Asian countries such as India and Pakistan, where dietary habits and climatic conditions are broadly similar to Nepal, have also demonstrated a associations between kidney stone formation and diets rich in salt, refined carbohydrates, and animal protein [33]. Our findings likely represent a risk pattern that aligns with global trends but is also influenced by the unique dietary practices of the Nepalese population.

Stone size was also found to be closely associated with biochemical composition. Struvite stones exhibited the largest dimensions, with a median size of 20 mm and some reaching over 40 mm. This confirms their tendency to form “staghorn calculi,” which can occupy the entire renal pelvis, so the finding aligns with the understanding that struvite stones are often associated with infections, which can lead to rapid growth and large sizes [34]. In contrast, UA and Cystine stones were generally smaller and more uniform in size, typically ranging between 10 mm and 17 mm.

These findings provide important descriptive insights into stone composition and demographic distribution in Eastern Nepal. While general measures such as adequate hydration and healthy lifestyle practices are widely recommended for kidney stone prevention, this study did not include detailed dietary or urinary biochemical assessments. Therefore, specific preventive strategies cannot be directly derived from these findings. Future studies incorporating metabolic evaluation and dietary analysis would further strengthen preventive recommendations in this population.

Conclusion

In conclusion, Calcium Oxalate is the most prevalent kidney stone type in Eastern Nepal, primarily affecting males and adults in the 31–45 age group. Geographical distribution shows a heavy burden in the Terai region. Analysis reveals that stone composition is significantly associated with urinary pH, while struvite stones tend to form the largest calculi. These findings provide valuable insight for the development of targeted prevention and treatment strategies for renal stone disease.
