Can ICD‐11 Personality Disorder Trait Dimensions Be Categorically Specified?
Authors: Gunnar Valdemar Grunert, Erik Simonsen, Mickey T. Kongerslev, Bo Bach
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe new ICD‐11 Personality Disorder (PD) classification introduces a global severity classification (i.e., mild, moderate, and severe) of personality dysfunction, which may be further accompanied by up to five trait domain specifiers delineating individual manifestations of personality disturbance (i.e., Negative affectivity, Detachment, Dissociality, Disinhibition, and Anankastia). Although these trait domains are conceptualized as dimensions, the ICD‐11 only allows clinicians to code them as being categorically present versus absent. It is therefore appropriate to examine the ability of clinicians' judgements in capturing variance in trait domains using dichotomous trait ratings. A total sample of 238 patients was characterized by their clinicians using both dimensional and dichotomous single‐item ratings for each trait domain. Subsequently, clinicians filled out the Informant Personality Inventory for ICD‐11 (IPiC) as a standardized criterion‐measure for trait domains. The dichotomous ratings were all moderately associated with their corresponding IPiC score, except for Negative Affectivity, which only showed a small but statistically significant association. The dimensional ratings were all moderately to strongly associated with their corresponding IPiC scores. However, only the dimensional single‐item ratings of Disinhibition and Dissociality were significantly superior to the dichotomous single‐item ratings in capturing the IPiC trait domain scores. Findings overall support the use of categorical assignment of trait domain specifiers in the ICD‐11 although dimensional formats improve the accuracy of clinicians' judgements.

Introduction

The International Classification of Diseases, 11th Edition (ICD‐11) presents a fundamentally new model for the conceptualization and assessment of personality disorders (PD) and related traits (WHO 2024). A diagnosis of ICD‐11 Personality Disorder (PD) is characterized by an enduring disturbance characterized by problems in functioning of aspects of the self (e.g., identity, self‐worth, accuracy of self‐view, self‐direction), and/or interpersonal dysfunction (e.g., ability to develop and maintain close and mutually satisfying relationships, ability to understand others' perspectives and to manage conflict in relationships). The disturbance has persisted over a period of at least 2 years, manifests across a range of situations, is associated with substantial distress or impairment, and cannot be better explained by other conditions or contextual factors (WHO 2024). In addition to the new content of the essential requirements, a severity determination of mild, moderate, or severe is now mandatory along with the possibility of coding up to five trait domain specifiers, including Negative Affectivity, Detachment, Dissociality, Disinhibition, and Anankastia (WHO 2024). These five trait domains are the focus of the present study.

It has been suggested that the trait domains may be useful for clinical management by supporting conceptualization and treatment planning according to the individual stylistic expression of personality disturbance (Bach and Presnall‐Shvorin 2020; Hempel et al. 2025; MacBeth and Dimaggio 2025; Natoli et al. 2024; Sauer‐Zavala et al. 2025; Simonsen et al. 2025). One example relevant for treatment planning and case conceptualization, disinhibition has been found to be uniquely associated with higher risk of dropout in PD treatment (De Salve et al. 2026; Rodriguez‐Seijas et al. 2020). Several instruments have been developed to assess the ICD‐11 trait domain specifiers, of which one of the most well‐established is the Personality inventory for ICD‐11 (PiCD; Oltmanns and Widiger 2018) self‐report form as well as its corresponding informant‐rating version, the Informant Personality inventory for ICD‐11 (IPiC; Oltmanns and Widiger 2021).

Like most trait‐models of personality and personality pathology, the ICD‐11 defines the five trait domain specifiers as dimensional constructs (Bach et al. 2017; Oltmanns and Widiger 2018, 2021; WHO 2024). However, as a diagnostic coding framework, the ICD‐11 only allows the trait domain specifiers to be coded categorically (i.e., present or not present). In other words, despite the empirical nature of the trait domains, users of the ICD‐11 classification are forced to code them as being either prominent or not prominent.

At least one study has examined the ability of clinicians to capture dimensional information in categorical ratings of diagnosis. Keeley et al. (2021) examined paraphilic disorders using experimental vignettes. They showed that clinicians were able to integrate dimensional indicators in a meaningful way when assigning categorical ICD‐11 diagnoses. However, the ability of such categorical coding to meaningfully capture features of the trait dimensions in ICD‐11 PD remains an uninvestigated question. The present study specifically sought to fill this gap.

Goal of This Study

To address the aforementioned gap in the research literature and for the purpose of ICD‐11 implementation, the present study aimed to assess whether ICD‐11's dichotomous coding of trait domains—in comparison to a single‐item dimensional rating—can adequately capture the variance in the dimensional trait structure as measured by a psychometrically validated clinician‐rating approach.

Methods

Procedure

A total sample of 238 patients was characterized by their clinicians using both dichotomous (0 or 1) and dimensional (0, 1, 2, 3, or 4) single‐item ratings for each trait domain. Subsequently, clinicians filled out the Informant Personality inventory for ICD‐11 (IPiC), which we employed as a standardized and structurally valid criterion measure for trait domains (Bach et al. 2020; Oltmanns and Widiger 2021). In order to carry out this procedure, we initially invited approximately 2500 mental health clinicians working in Danish Mental Health Services to contribute to this study, of whom 257 responded and 133 eventually provided complete data. They were specifically recruited from professionals working in Region Zealand Mental Health Services, a national Danish network of psychologists and psychiatrists working with personality disorders, and some master's‐level psychology students in clinical internships. All clinicians were asked to rate one or two of their patients whom they, as a requirement, must have seen for at least five sessions or at least 5 hours (e.g., clinical assessment or therapy sessions). The patients in question did not necessarily have to be diagnosed with a PD but could as well be characterized by the much more prevalent features of subthreshold personality difficulty (WHO 2024). Clinicians recorded their patients' age, gender, mental health diagnoses, and the estimated severity of personality disturbance according to the ICD‐11. Before answering the IPiC, clinicians were asked to specify the presence of trait domains in two different formats. First, guided by simple descriptions of the trait domains from the ICD‐11 guidelines, clinicians rated the trait domains as present versus absent. Secondly, the clinicians rated the trait domains on a 5‐point Likert scale (0 = not at all, 4 = very strong degree) (WHO 2024).

For more methodological details, we refer to the procedures presented in Bach et al. (2020) covering the collection of data for the present study too. All data collection was carried out in accordance with local ethical research standards, and the Danish Data Protection Agency approved the data collection.

Contributing Clinicians

A total of 133 mental health clinicians completed the administered survey. They recorded their professional backgrounds as well as years of experience working in mental health services. The group consisted of 96 (40.3%) psychologists, 46 (19.3%) medical doctors, 74 (31.1%) nurses and nurse assistants, and 22 (9.2%) other mental health professionals. A total of 74% of the clinician sample were female and their years of working experience in mental health care ranged from 1 to 40 years with a mean of 14.55 years (SD 11.69).

Measures

The Informant Personality Inventory for ICD‐11 (IPiC) was developed by Oltmanns and Widiger (2021) from the original self‐report Personality Inventory for ICD‐11 form (Oltmanns and Widiger 2018). The IPiC contains 60 items distributed evenly among the five ICD‐11 trait domains with 12 items per domain. All items are ranked on a 5‐point Likert scale from strongly disagree to strongly agree with statements. Results from the original validation study for the IPiC indicated good convergent, criterion, as well as structural validity (Oltmanns and Widiger 2021). The original publication describing this sample, by Bach et al. (2020) found that the hypothesized ICD‐11 compatible 4‐factor structure fitted the data well and reported high internal consistency (in the range of 0.88–0.91 α) for all trait domains, except for Negative Affectivity (0.63 α).

Statistical Approach

To assess whether clinicians could accurately assess their patients' ICD‐11 trait qualifiers using dichotomous and dimensional single‐item ratings, we examined domain‐congruent correlations between dichotomous/Likert domain ratings and IPiC domain scores. To examine suitability of the data for correlation analysis, we examined the skew and kurtosis of the IPiC scores, as these scores functioned as the criterion variables for all correlation analyses. Point‐biserial correlations were used to examine the correlation between dichotomous trait ratings and IPiC scores. Pearson correlations were used to examine the correlation between Likert trait ratings and IPiC scores. Steiger's test was used to test the significance of differences in the strength of correlations between the two rating formats of dichotomous and Likert ratings (Meng et al. 1992). To correct for multiple testing, we used Benjamini–Hochberg FDR to adjust p‐values (Benjamini and Hochberg 1995). We did this within each family of related tests. So, one FDR for the five point‐biserial tests, one for the five Pearson tests and one for the five Steiger tests. Lastly, as a secondary moderation analysis, we examined whether clinicians' years of working experience or clinicians' educational background moderated the correlations by use of linear interaction models and once again controlled for multiple testing. We operationalized educational background in two groups: One group having received specialized training in assessment and psychopathology (doctors and psychologists) and all others.

All analyses were conducted in R version 4.4.2, and an R Markdown file can be acquired upon contact with the first author.

Moderation Analysis

Neither years of working experience nor educational background, as indicators of having specialized training in assessment of psychopathology, moderated the associations after adjusting for multiple testing. For work experience, some unadjusted significant interactions emerged for Negative Affectivity (dimensional: β = −0.072, p = 0.017; dichotomous: β = −0.140, p = 0.024) and Anankastia (dichotomous: β = −0.216, p = 0.045), but none survived FDR correction (all adjusted p > 0.05). For education, one unadjusted significant interaction was observed for Anankastia (dichotomous: β = 5.340, p = 0.042), also non‐significant after FDR adjustment (adjusted p = 0.307).

Discussion

This study's primary aims were to assess the ability of clinicians to capture meaningful trait information when forced to use single‐item present/absent ratings and whether single‐item dimensional ratings improved their ability in doing this. The results indicated that dichotomous ratings, based only on the descriptions provided in the ICD‐11, were significantly associated with relevant IPiC scores, even after adjusting for multiple testing. Moreover, the associations were overall in the moderate range except for the clinically broad and transdiagnostic domain of Negative Affectivity (Widiger and Oltmanns 2017). Importantly, the results indicate that clinicians can capture a clinically meaningful proportion of variance in trait scores using dichotomous ratings as described in the ICD‐11 codes.

The correlation coefficient between the 5‐point Likert ratings and the IPiC scores was substantially and significantly higher for Disinhibition and Dissociality compared to the dichotomous rating format. The results might thereby indicate that a continuous rating format is more accurate compared to dichotomous ratings in capturing variance in trait domains. This finding is not surprising, as prior research on scale development and psychometrics suggests that psychometric properties tend to fare better on items with more than 2 response points (e.g., Lee and Paek 2014) and that psychopathology in a broad sense, and especially psychometrically speaking, is better conceptualized on a continuum rather than dichotomous categories (Haslam et al. 2020).

The finding that dimensional ratings performed superior to dichotomous ratings, for the trait domains of Disinhibition and Dissociality, aligns well with prior research. These specific trait domains largely characterize tendencies that are more observable and behaviorally anchored (e.g., Zimmermann et al. 2019). This may very well facilitate clinicians' ability to differentiate meaningful degrees of the trait domains, compared to more internally experienced trait domains. Further strengthening this claim, the self‐other agreement for dissociality and disinhibition has been reported to be higher compared to the other trait domains (Oltmanns and Widiger 2020). Negative Affectivity, in contrast, has shown lower self‐other agreement compared to other trait domains (Oltmanns and Widiger 2020) and seems to be better defined by internal experience and better captured by self‐reports (Bach et al. 2017; Zimmermann et al. 2019).

Based on the finding that at least for some trait domains, continuous rating formats seem to perform better, one tentative recommendation could be that clinicians might consider introducing continuous ratings of the trait domains in clinical practice that can complement the dichotomous rating processes. Alternatively, when possible, self‐reports with norm based benchmarks could complement the assessment process. Several self‐report instruments suitable for the ICD‐11 trait domains, such as the PiCD or the PID‐5BF+ have published representative norm‐based distributions in German (Weweck et al. 2021) and Danish contexts (Grunert and Bach 2026). However, one of the main goals of the ICD‐11 is to simplify the process of assessing and classifying features of mental disorders (Reed et al. 2019). The finding that the dichotomous ratings capture statistically significant proportions of trait variance strengthens the argument for using the dichotomous approach in contexts where resources or expertise might be sparse. A main driver of the work in the ICD‐11 has been to provide descriptions and guidelines that can be implemented in routine clinical practice without the need for extensive specialized training (Reed et al. 2019). Supporting this, we found that neither years of working experience nor educational background moderated the associations significantly. This indicates that the ICD‐11 trait domains can be rated accurately by both novice and expert clinicians from differing backgrounds.

Limitations and Future Directions

Even though the inclusion of a broad group of mental health patients probably increased the natural variance in trait domain scores, further studies should seek to examine whether our results can be replicated in pure personality disorder samples. Furthermore, it would be interesting to examine whether degree of personality dysfunction moderated clinicians' ability to capture variance in trait scores using dichotomous ratings. It might be that a person with a severe personality disorder compared to a mild personality disorder might be easier to rate dichotomously. Even though the IPiC has shown strong associations with the PiCD, a topic relevant for further study might be whether clinicians dichotomous ratings capture the same amount of variance in self‐reported trait scores. This is especially important since the ratings on the IPiC and the dichotomous trait ratings were done by the same clinician, increasing the risk of shared method variance inflating correlations between the different measurements. Importantly, our moderation analyses should be seen as tentative and exploratory and should be replicated in future studies with adequate power. Lastly, as described in the section on the procedure for our data collection, our recruiting process may have resulted in a significant sample bias due to the voluntary and open‐ended invitation strategy. We opted to invite through a variety of networks as described, ensuring a broad variation of clinicians and clinician backgrounds, however, it is realistic that clinicians more interested in the subject of personality pathology were more likely to participate in the study. This could have resulted in inflated correlation estimates. We therefore emphasize the need for replication in different samples and with different and complementary data collection procedures.
