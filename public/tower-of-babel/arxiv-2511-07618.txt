Nanorod Pair Complexes Manipulated via Magnetic Casimir Forces
S. Pal, L. M. Woods, C. Persson, I. Brevik, U. De Giovannini, M. Boström

Abstract
Controlling nanoscale interactions to suppress aggregation from short-range attractive forces is a key problem in nanoengineering. Here, we demonstrate a route to modulate Casmir-Lifshitz interactions between anisotropic nanoparticles with the magnetic fluids. By semi-classical quantum electrodynamics, we study ground state dispersion forces for cylindrical dielectric nanorods made of polystyrene (PS), and zinc oxide (ZnO) embedded in toluene-based host media with gold-coated magnetite nanoparticles and also predict magnetic contributions to the non-retarded excited state interaction. The variation in magnetic permeability enables tuning between repulsive and attractive interaction and a thermally unstable and measurable magnetic Casimir traps are predicted between a pair of ZnO–PS nanoparticles whose equilibrium position can be modulated over an order of magnitude with a small variation in the size of the magnetite nanoparticle. This provides an alternative magnetic Casimir-effect pathway to reversibly tune quantum electromagnetic forces at the nanoscale for assembly and enhancement of colloidal stability.

What if the very surrounding media in space itself could be engineered into a tool for nano-manipulation? For instance, can we take the background media as a magnetic fluid, in which electromagnetic fluctuations exert forces that can pull, push or most interestingly trap matters at the nanoscale level? Such fluctuation-induced interactions, which are referred to as van der Waals
Wang 1928
;
London 1930
(vdW) and Casimir-Lifshitz (CL)
Casimir 1948
;
Dzyaloshinskii et al. 1961
forces together, typically act to bring microscopic bodies into contact, leading to unwanted self-aggregation in nanofabrication and soft-matter systems
Parsegian and Ninham 1969
;
Dobson et al. 2006
;
Munday et al. 2009
;
Esteso et al. 2015
;
Woods et al. 2016
;
Zhao et al. 2019
;
Rodriguez-Lopez et al. 2020
;
Esteso et al. 2022
;
Spreng et al. 2022
;
Shelden et al. 2024
;
Lee et al. 2024
. An important practical issue in nano and micro-devices is to overcome close contact attractions that causes building blocks to cluster together and an ideal solution would be to engineer systems where the interaction turns repulsive at small distances
Shelden et al. 2024
.
Beyond simply mitigating stiction, these fluctuations could also be harnessed to produce stable and controllable interaction potentials.
Fundamental investigations of the influence on CL forces by a magnetic surrounding media have been largely restricted to simple geometries
Richmond and Ninham 1971
;
Klimchitskaya et al. 2019
;
Velichko et al. 2020
. Exploiting such a foundation, we have recently expanded the theoretical approach by including the contributions of transverse magnetic (TM) and transverse electric (TE) modes within a model described by the proximity force theorem
Derjaguin 1934
;
Blocki and Swiatecki 1981
in such a way that both energy contributions due to TM and TE-modes would partially act as candidates to enable quantum trapping between two quasi-planar surfaces
Pal et al. 2025
.

In this letter, we go beyond these simple geometries and study an experimentally feasible setup which comprises of pairs of cylindrical nanorods made of polystyrene (PS) and zinc oxide (ZnO). The use of the full cylindrical geometry permits a more realistic description of nanoscale behavior, albeit at the price of considerable added theoretical complexity. This stimulates further development of analytical and numerical studies of Lifshitz and vdW interaction between elongated nanoparticles
Richmond et al. 1972
;
Mitchell et al. 1973
;
Davies et al. 1973
;
Nyland and Brevik 1994
;
Pal et al. 2024
;
Wang et al. 2025
.
One of us previously derived a few asymptotic limits for this kind of a system
Nyland and Brevik 1994
. Here, we examine the
magnetic contributions
associated with a surrounding ferrofluid for the long-range and high-temperature limits of ground-state dispersion interactions between cylindrical nanoparticles. We also predict a new kind of magnetic contribution to the retarded excited-state interaction. In an illustrative example, we study the CL interaction between a ZnO nanorod and a PS nanorod suspended in a toluene and gold-coated magnetite nanoparticle (5-7%) fluid mixtures. In the case of two unequal nanorods, we find that subtle change of nanoparticle concentration into the ferrofluid can induce a transition from short-range repulsion to long-range attraction between them. It is the fluid’s magnetic permeability and its dependence upon the magnetite nanoparticle size that dictates both interaction strength and sign, in contrast to the fluid dielectric function which remains nearly constant. Such a device could enable controlled, magnetic-induced entropic CL trapping.
Early studies of CL interactions between magnetic fluida
Klimchitskaya et al. 2019
;
Velichko et al. 2020
were mainly concentrated on the observation of a repulsive force by changing the optical properties of the medium. By contrast, we have implemented here a way in which this zero-frequency, purely entropically driven Casimir trapping is realized exclusively by tuning the fluid’s magnetic permittivity, an effect not investigated earlier.

To lay foundations for such a framework, we develop, within the semi-classical quantum electrodynamics, the general theory for ground and excited state dispersion interactions for dielectric cylindrical nanorods embedded in a magnetic fluid. In the subsequent sections, we present analytical results for the excited state interaction between thin cylinders and the corresponding free energy, followed by numerical analysis of the ground-state forces. We briefly describe the essential optical and magnetic properties of the interacting nanorods and background fluid in the main text and at length in the Supplementary Information. Lastly we demonstrate here that the retardation substantially diminishes the overall interaction strength as well as amplifies the relative weight of magnetic response from the long-range, classical entropic contributions.

Here, we thus propose a novel experimentally feasible approach to manipulate nanorod pair complexes by suspending them in a magnetically diluted fluid, where the interplay between repulsive non-magnetic and attractive magnetic contributions to the CL force can be continuously tuned to form stable magnetic CL traps appearing as equilibria whose strength and range derive not from mechanical considerations but from spectral properties of quantum electromagnetic fluctuations.

Here we are also interested if a dispersion interaction in excited state could be realized between two equal cylindrical nanoparticles. In order to gain an understanding into this issue, it is instructive to start by considering the corresponding analogous situation of two identical atoms; one in its excited state while the other in its ground state. In such a scenario, the interaction can be described as a superposition of a symmetric and an

As a result, a strong resonant dispersion interaction will be maintained between the atoms until (I) the atoms move sufficiently far apart or (II) a photon is emitted, which reduces most of the coherence between them. We will in the current work explore a more versatile system consisting of nanorod pair complexes where the materials are described by the dielectric functions shown in Fig. (
2
). Here the intermediate magnetic fluid has a dielectric function in between those of ZnO and PS. This indicates that the non-retarded and retarded CL interaction between a ZnO and PS nanorod will be repulsive

In Fig. (
3
) we show that in the case of a pair of ZnO and PS nanorods in a magnetic fluid with toluene mixed with 5-7% magnetite it is possible to obtain trapping that is
entirely
controlled by the magnetic contribution to the CL force. When the magnetic permeability of the fluid is set to one (
\mu_{3}=1
), the system only experiences repulsion. This dependence of trapping on the magnetic permeability has not been possible to achieve in the past. Notably, when the diameter of the magnetite nanoparticles decreases with fixed volume fraction of magnetite, or a weak magnetic field is applied, leading to reduced magnetic permeability of the fluid
Zhang et al. 2024
, the trapping occurs at increasingly larger nanorod pair separations. Ultimately, in the absence of magnetic permeability effects, there is no trapping at all and the nanorod complex is split into two separate nanorods.

Replacing the magnetite nanoparticles with commercially available gold-coated magnetite nanoparticles leads to an additional mechanism to tune the trapping distance as can be seen in Fig. (
4
). With changes in the gold coating the dielectric function of the background media changes and the trapping distance can be shifted.

This framework suggests a new route for actively tuning the interacting forces between two unequal nanorods where a purely attractive potential can be driven into repulsive–attractive trapping potentials and even into regimes of complete repulsion. By a careful choice of the material parameters, one can tailor finite nanosystems that give rise to reversible adhesive behavior, stable tunable trapping and on-demand destabilization of clusters. These results demonstrate a general platform for nanocluster design in magnetic colloids where adjustable magnetic Casimir forces can be exploited for NEMS/MEMS applications.

Note that the proof-of-principle theory presented here assumes that trapping takes place at separations much larger than the diameter of the nanorod. In this situation, the trapping minima are very weak and can be affected by thermal noise [see Sec. (S.6) in the Supplementary Information]. Nevertheless, this restriction can be mitigated by applying the proximity force approximation (PFA), valid in the short-distance regime, where enhanced stability of the trapping potential is expected.

Going forward, the framework we have developed here indicates some exciting prospects for future extension of tunable dispersion-force control to biomolecular materials. In particular, these ideas could be extended to direct biopolymer complexation with artificial nanothreads via magnetic nanoparticles in biofluids with a suitable choice of materials. This has a great potential in a variety of future diagnostic and therapeutic nanomedicine research
Coene and Leliaert 2022
. It also provides a route to field-controlled assembly in hybrid soft–magnetic nanostructures and could also find utility for biosensing, targeted delivery, reconfigurable nanoarchitectures, and beyond.

S.2
Dispersion interaction for thin cylinders

Though the expression for the dispersion relation in Eq. (
S. 41
) defines the full solution of our problem, it is very cumbersome and of no practical advantage in its original form. For more useful and transparent solutions we consider the special case of “thin cylinder approximation” in the limit of
a\ll R
. There exists here a physical lower limit on the magnitude of
a
: it must be so large that classical fluid mechanics is applicable. Usually, a lower limit of about 100 nm should be more than sufficient. One must observe that in the limit of very thin cylindrical structures, e.g. DNA strings, one must use a quantum mechanical formalism.

To reduce the complexity of our solutions, we followed a method developed for finding the non-retarded interaction between cylinders
Mitchell et al. 1973
. The main contribution comes from the range of axial wave vectors,
k\lesssim 1/R
, which along with the thin cylinder approximation
a/R\ll 1
leads to a further approximation
|u_{\alpha}a|\ll 1,(\alpha=1,2,3)
(
|\xi a/c|\ll 1
). In this region, the Bessel functions are expanded to a leading order term for its small arguments
u_{\alpha}a
and
u_{\alpha}R
and the approximated matrix elements
M_{mn}
can be written as,

M_{mn}\simeq\Big(\frac{a}{R}\Big)^{m+n}\frac{(m+n-1)!}{m!(n-1)!}<\Big(\frac{a}{R}\Big)^{m+n}2^{m+n}

(S. 42)

This shows that with increasing
m
and
n
, these matrix elements decrease rapidly. On the other hand when
k>1/R
, using Matsubara frequencies, we find

u^{2}_{\alpha}(i\xi_{p})=-\Big(\frac{\xi^{2}_{p}}{c^{2}}\mu_{\alpha}(i\xi_{p})\varepsilon_{\alpha}(i\xi_{p})+k^{2}\Big)

(S. 43)

At this point, it is convenient to introduce a new variable
\nu_{\alpha}=e^{i\pi/2}u_{\alpha}
leading to a new relation for the modified Bessel function,

H_{n}(xe^{i\pi/2})=-\frac{2i}{\pi}\,e^{-in\pi/2}K_{n}(x),\quad-\pi<\mathrm{arg}(x)<\pi/2

(S. 44)

Hence the matrix elements
M_{mn}
can be connected with the modified Bessel function, using the new variable
\nu_{\alpha}
,

M_{mn}\propto H_{m+n}(u_{3}R)\propto K_{m+n}(\nu_{3}R)

(S. 45)

Therefore, in the region
kR\gg 1
i.e.,
|\nu_{3}R|\gg 1
, the matrix elements become

M_{mn}\simeq\sqrt{\frac{1}{\nu_{3}R}}\,e^{-\nu_{3}R}

(S. 46)

The above asymptotic expression, shows that in this limit the matrix elements are finite and they are independent of
m
and
n
.

Thus, we obtain finite contributions in the long and short separation limits. From Eqs. (
S. 42
) and (
S. 46
), it is very clear that the dominant effect can only be found in lower values of
m
and
n

In the case of two identical thin cylindrical nanoparticles, if a long-lived antisymmetric state exists it results in a long-ranged excited state interaction. In general, this would formally be given by

S.4
Preliminary considerations for the impact of magnetic fluid

In view of the complexity of the formalism, we first note some relative magnitudes of central quantities as part of the theory.
Consider first the dispersion relation: adopting a Lorentz form for the ferrofluid magnetic susceptibility
\chi_{ms}
with
a characteristic frequency
\omega_{0m}
, we have
Nyland and Brevik 1994

\mu(i\xi)=1+\frac{4\pi\chi_{ms}}{1+\xi^{2}/\omega_{0m}^{2}},

(S. 66)

with
\xi=-i\omega
being the imaginary frequency. Here
\omega_{0m}
is relatively low, typically
\omega_{0m}=3\times 10^{10}
rad/s, in contrast to the characteristic dielectric
\omega_{0e}
which is typically
\omega_{0e}=3\times 10^{16}
rad/s. Hence, only zero frequency contribution is influenced strongly by the magnetic effects.

At large separations, non-zero frequency contributions to the dispersion interaction are screened out by retardation. Thus, the magnetic part of the dispersion forces is an essential part of the long-range/high-temperature zero-frequency contribution. A characteristic property of both the non-retarded and zero-frequency entropic interaction between cylinders is its scaling
R^{-5}
dependence
Langbein 1972
;
Mitchell et al. 1973
;
Smith et al. 1973
;
Israelachvili 1973
;
Parsegian 2006
;
Misquitta et al. 2010
;
Ninham and Lo Nostro 2010
;
Ambrosetti et al. 2016
.
Another way of looking at the condition for nonretarded theory is to observe that the distance
R
between the cylinders must be much smaller
than the wavelength
\lambda_{0}=2\pi c/(n\omega_{0})
corresponding to the proper frequency
\omega_{0}
for the
material (
n
is the refractive index). In the electric case with
\omega_{0}=\omega_{0e}
one gets
\lambda_{0e}\sim 50~
nm, while in the
magnetic case with
\omega_{0}=\omega_{0m}
one gets
\lambda_{0m}
to lie in the centimeter region.
Thus in the magnetic case the nonretarded theory is often applicable, at least when the
condition
R\ll\lambda_{0}
is satisfied. Also, in the absence of magnetic fields, effects due to magnetic nonlinearities are usually negligible in Casimir
problems.
However, a very recent paper discussed how the Casimir force in specific
cases can be tuned by magnetic fields
Zhang et al. 2024
.
The details on how we modeled the magnetic permeability of the magnetic fluid are given in the ”Materials Modeling” section.
