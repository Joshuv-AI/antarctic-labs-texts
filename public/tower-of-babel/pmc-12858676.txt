Graph neural network-based multi-label classification of breast cancer using multiparametric MRI and clinical features
Authors: Shahed Salehzehi, Shabnam Jafarpoor Nesheli, Alireza Eftekhar, Mehdi Shojaee Rocky, Vajihe Vejdani Noghreiyan, Abolfazl Bazazpour, Zeynab Hassani Arefi, Seyed Masoud Rezaeijo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This research was done to design and evaluate a graph-based deep-learning architecture to automatically multi-label breast cancer, using multiparametric magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) data and clinical features. The general aim was to enhance the diagnostic accuracy of the key clinical activities, i.e. biomarker discovery, tumour staging, and histological grading. Three-dimensional volumetric graphs were built with the help of five different MRI modalities (Phase 1, Phase 2, Phase 3, T1, and IHSFused), and each voxel and clinical variable was a node. Attributes of the nodes were radiomic features and patient-specific clinical data whereas edges represented relationships by spatial proximity and intensity. Graph structure processing was done using a Graph Isomorphism Network (GIN), which gradually optimised node embeddings by aggregating neighbourhoods. The multi-label classification focused on the HER2 status, TNM stage (Tumour Size, Node involvement, Metastasis) and histological grade elements (Tubule formation, Mitotic count, Nuclear pleomorphism). Cross-Entropy loss and Adam optimiser were used to train the model, and Synthetic Minority Over-Sampling Technique (SMOTE) was used to balance the distribution of classes. The protocol of cross-validation was used five times to be robust and performance measures were provided in terms of accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, and confusion matrices. The t-Stochastic Neighbor Embedding (t-SNE) visualisations were used to assess the feature separability. The model achieved high accuracy in HER2 (up to 91.42%) and Tubule grade (up to 91.83%), moderate performance in Tumor Size (70–72%), Nodes (82–84%), and Metastasis (80–83%), and lower accuracy in nuclear grade (64–68%). Confusion matrices showed minimal misclassification in high-performing labels and greater variability in lower-performing ones. t-SNE plots confirmed effective feature separation for HER2 and Tubule, with overlap noted in Nuclear. These results indicate the possibilities of volumetric graph-based learning to provide full classification of breast cancer and its importance in improving precision diagnostics with the help of artificial intelligence.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-025-04359-1.

Introduction

Breast cancer is the most commonly diagnosed cancer and a cause of death to a large number of women all over the world [1]. The development of medical imaging and especially magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) have significantly enhanced the early diagnosis and diagnostic evaluation because they can offer high-resolution and multiparametric volumetric data [2, 3]. However, interpretation of such images in clinical settings is still faced with great challenges [4]. The intricacy of MRI images, as well as the inconsistency of lesion morphology and subjective criteria of assessment make MRI images inconsistent in their diagnosis and slow therapeutic decisions [5]. This has led to an urgent requirement of computational methods that can effectively extract and interpret imaging data of high dimensions with reproducibility and accuracy [6].

Radiomics has become a paradigm shift in this respect, as it allows quantitative characterisation of tumour phenotypes based on the extraction of engineered features [7, 8]. These descriptors give more information about the heterogeneity of tumours which in many cases cannot be visually determined by radiologists [9]. Nevertheless, the radiomic model diagnostic capability is deeply associated with the similarity of features across imaging modalities and acquisition environments [10, 11]. In response to this, reproducibility tests, including the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), have been required to determine formidable features that can be cross-modality generalised [12]. Attributes that have large ICC values give a sound basis on which predictive models can be developed and used on a wide range of clinical data.

At the same time, the diagnostics of breast cancer is a multi-label diagnosis by its nature. Clinical analysis is regularly conducted as a concomitant analysis of several pathological parameters, such as hormone receptor status (e.g. HER 2), tumour staging parameters (size, lymph node involvement, metastasis) and histological grading parameters (tubule formation, mitotic activity, nuclear pleomorphism) [13]. Each of these elements holds prognostic significance and collectively informs treatment planning [13]. The traditional machine-learning methods tend to handle these labels independently, thus not taking into account interdependencies and thus not capturing the multidimensionality of disease progression [14].

In response to these limitations, emerging interest has turned to graph-based learning paradigms, particularly Graph Neural Networks (GNNs), which offer unique capabilities for modeling non-Euclidean data structures inherent in volumetric medical images [15]. Unlike traditional convolutional models constrained by grid-based operations, GNNs can naturally represent imaging data as graphs [16]. This allows for the incorporation of both local and global contextual information, facilitating more nuanced and relationally aware classification [17]. Furthermore, GNN architectures are inherently flexible and can be extended to handle multi-label tasks, enabling concurrent modeling of multiple clinical targets [18].

It comprises a total of 922 biopsy-confirmed cases of invasive breast cancer, with ground-truth labels sourced from histopathological reports and clinical metadata verified by expert oncologists and pathologists. Upon acquisition, all MRI images were categorized according to their imaging modality. This preprocessing step yielded four primary imaging sequences: axial T1-weighted (ax-T1), and three phases of post-contrast dynamic sequences (ph1ax-3D-dyn, ph2ax-3D-dyn, and ph3ax-3D-dyn). To focus the analysis on tumor-specific regions, each image was cropped using the provided lesion annotations, isolating the affected breast (left or right) to exclude irrelevant background and contralateral tissue (Fig. 2). This procedure allowed for the enhancement of texture-based feature extraction by targeting the lesion region.

Following cropping, inter-modality image registration was performed to achieve spatial alignment and dimensional consistency across all four modalities. This ensured that each tumor region was identically represented across sequences, facilitating multi-modal analysis. To enrich the dataset by incorporating an additional imaging perspective, an image fusion technique was applied. Specifically, the Intensity-Hue-Saturation (IHS) fusion method was employed to combine ax-T1 (providing high-resolution anatomical details on tumor morphology and boundaries) with ph1ax-3D-dyn (capturing early post-contrast enhancement indicative of tumor perfusion and vascularity). This selection leverages complementary strengths: T1 for static structure and Phase 1 for dynamic contrast uptake, as supported by Houser et al. [3]. The resulting IHS-Fused modality encodes biological information on tumor heterogeneity, neovascularization, and enhancement kinetics, which correlate with prognostic factors like HER2 expression and staging aggressiveness, thereby supporting multi-label classification tasks. In this process, each image pair was normalized by scaling pixel intensities relative to their maximum value. The fusion was conducted in the HSV color space, where the ‘Value’ channel of the first modality was replaced with that of the second. The result was a new modality termed IHS-Fused, increasing the total number of available modalities to five for subsequent analysis. This comprehensive preprocessing pipeline ensured the generation of a Structured, high-quality dataset that supports robust multi-modal feature extraction for machine learning model development.

Radiomic feature extraction and selection

Radiomic phenotypes were numerically derived in segmented tumor areas in the five MRI modalities, and the aim was to capture various image features that are reflective of tumor phenotype. One hundred and seventy-seven features were calculated and grouped into three major categories, which include first-order statistics, shape descriptors, and texture features. The first-order category (18 features) summarizes the distribution of voxel intensities in the tumor area and includes such metrics as mean, standard deviation, skewness, etc. Shape features (14) describe the morphology of tumor in three dimensions, comprising surface area, compactness, and sphericity. The obtained texture features (75) were based on several matrices of statistical texture namely: Gray-Level Co-Occurrence Matrix (GLCM, 24 features), Gray-Level Dependence Matrix (GLDM, 14), Gray-Level Run-Length Matrix (GLRLM, 16), Gray-Level Size Zone Matrix (GLSZM, 16), and Neighboring Gray Tone Difference Matrix (NGTDM, 5). Each of the five MRI modalities (Phase 1, Phase 2, Phase 3, T1-weighted, and Fusion) yielded 107 radiomic features (18 first-order statistics, 14 shape descriptors and 75 texture features).

The PyRadiomics open-source Python library was used to extract all radiomic features following a standard preprocessing pipeline. In this pipeline, tumor segmentation was done using annotated lesion regions, intensity normalization, and resampling to achieve spatial consistency between modalities. The resulting features formed an all-encompassing quantitative basis upon which machine learning models were developed hence enabling the accuracy in distinguishing prognostic categories.

In order to determine the stability and reliability of the extracted features across modalities, intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was used. This assessment consisted of five MRI modalities, namely, T1-axial (T1-ax), phase 1 (ph1), phase 2 (ph2), phase 3 (ph3), and IHS-Fused modality (as shown in Fig. 3). Features were classified into four levels of reliability using the ICC values: (1) 0–50, (2) 50–75, (3) 75–90 and (4) 90–100. Attributes with larger ICC values were considered more reliable between modalities and were selected in preference to be used in model training. Each of the target labels was separately trained on modality-specific features to measure predictive performance and modality effects.

Model architecture

The model architecture employed in this study is based on the GIN, a class of GNNs designed to effectively learn representations of graph-structured data. GIN distinguishes itself from other GNN architectures by its ability to approximate the Weisfeiler-Lehman graph isomorphism test, enabling it to differentiate between non-isomorphic graphs with high fidelity. Following the GIN layers, a shared embedding is passed to multiple parallel classification heads tailored to each multi-label task (e.g., binary for HER2/Metastasis, multi-class for staging/grading components), using sigmoid/softmax activations as appropriate. This capacity is particularly advantageous in medical imaging contexts, where subtle structural differences in graph topology may correspond to significant pathological variations. At the core of the GIN architecture are the GIN convolution (GINConv) layers, which update node features by aggregating the features of neighboring nodes through a sum operation, followed by a learnable transformation. Classification tasks

Multi-label problem formulation

In clinical oncology, diagnostic decision-making often involves multiple interdependent classification objectives. These include determining receptor status, assessing tumor extent, and evaluating histological characteristics, all of which carry independent and complementary prognostic value. Consequently, this study develops breast cancer diagnosis as a multi-label classification problem, wherein each MRI-derived graph is associated with multiple output labels simultaneously. This approach diverges from traditional single-label classification, where only one target is predicted per instance. Instead, we trained a single integrated multi-label GNN model with a shared GIN backbone for feature extraction and separate classification heads (one per label: sigmoid-activated for binary/multi-class outputs) for concurrent prediction of all targets. This unified architecture exploits inter-label correlations (e.g., between staging and grading) while maintaining task-specific outputs, aligning more closely with real-world clinical scenarios.

The multi-label setting presents unique challenges, including label imbalance, co-occurrence dependencies, and increased model complexity. Unlike independent classifiers trained in isolation for each label, a unified multi-label framework enables shared representation learning across tasks, potentially improving performance through the exploitation of inter-label correlations.

Target labels (HER2, staging, grading)

The classification framework is designed to predict a comprehensive set of clinically significant labels. These are categorized into three primary groups: biomarker status, tumor staging, and histological grading. The first group focuses on HER2 receptor status, a crucial biomarker that influences therapeutic eligibility for targeted anti-HER2 treatments. Accurate identification of HER2-positive cases is essential for personalizing treatment regimens and improving outcomes. The second category, tumor staging, is based on the TNM (Tumor-Node-Metastasis) system, which evaluates tumor size (T), regional lymph node involvement (N), and the presence of distant metastasis (M). Each of these parameters reflects a different aspect of disease progression and significantly impacts treatment planning and prognosis. The model aims to classify each of these components individually, capturing the complexity and granularity of the staging process.

The final group encompasses tumor grading, which provides insight into the tumor’s histopathological aggressiveness. This includes three subcategories: tubule formation, mitotic count, and nuclear pleomorphism. These characteristics are core components of standard grading systems such as the Nottingham Grading System and are indicative of how closely the tumor resembles normal breast tissue. Regarding metastasis (M) prediction from breast MRI, while MRI primarily assesses local tumor characteristics, it provides indirect prognostic indicators such as tumor size, multifocality, lymphovascular invasion patterns, and enhancement kinetics, which correlate with distant spread risk. This enables plausible non-invasive estimation, though confirmatory systemic imaging remains standard in practice. High-grade tumors tend to exhibit poor differentiation, rapid cell division, and marked nuclear atypia, all of which are predictive of more aggressive behavior and poorer prognosis. By jointly modeling these labels, the proposed system mirrors the multi-dimensional nature of clinical breast cancer evaluation and offers an integrated tool for comprehensive diagnostic support.

Training procedure

Data preparation and oversampling

One of the common challenges in medical imaging classification tasks is the inherent class imbalance, where certain diagnostic categories, particularly minority tumor types or rare staging labels, are underrepresented in the dataset. This imbalance can skew model training, causing it to perform poorly on less frequent classes. To address this issue, the SMOTE was employed. SMOTE operates by generating synthetic samples for the minority class through interpolation between existing samples and their nearest neighbors in the feature space. This augmentation strategy enhances the diversity of the training set and ensures that the model receives sufficient exposure to all classes during training, thereby reducing bias and improving generalization for underrepresented labels. All results are reported on a per-patient basis, ensuring that evaluations reflect individual case predictions rather than voxel-level aggregates.

Optimization and hyperparameters

The model training process was conducted using the Adam optimizer (learning rate = 0.001, weight decay = 1e-4, betas=(0.9, 0.999)), which is well-suited for deep learning applications due to its adaptive learning rate adjustment based on moment estimates of the gradients. This optimization approach accelerates convergence and is particularly effective in managing sparse and noisy gradients commonly encountered in high-dimensional medical data. For the classification task, a cross-entropy loss function was applied to measure the discrepancy between the predicted probability distribution and the ground truth labels, making it appropriate for multi-class and multi-label problems due to its robustness and interpretability. The GIN architecture comprises 3 layers (hidden channels = 128, ε = 0.1), with dropout = 0.2 after each layer and ReLU activation. Training was executed over 150 epochs with a batch size of 16 to balance computational efficiency and convergence stability, incorporating early-stopping based on validation loss (patience = 20 epochs, min_delta = 0.001).

Performance metrics

To assess model performance, several standard classification metrics were employed. Accuracy was used as a general measure of correctness, indicating the proportion of correctly classified instances across all classes. Precision and recall provided insight into the model’s ability to correctly identify positive cases while avoiding false positives and false negatives, respectively. The F1-score, as the harmonic mean of precision and recall, offered a balanced metric particularly useful in imbalanced data scenarios. In addition, confusion matrices were generated for each classification task to visualize the distribution of true and predicted labels, enabling detailed analysis of misclassification patterns and performance variability across classes. Performance metrics now encompass per-class breakdowns, macro/micro-F1, AUROC/PR-AUC for all classes, and 95% CIs via bootstrap.

Evaluation strategy

K-fold cross-validation

To ensure robust performance evaluation and mitigate the effects of data partitioning bias, a patient-wise five-fold cross-validation strategy was implemented, where splits were stratified at the patient level to prevent data leakage between training and validation sets (no patient appears in both). In this approach, the dataset of 922 patients was randomly divided into five equally sized subsets, with all preprocessing steps (cropping, inter-modality registration, radiomic extraction, and SMOTE oversampling) applied strictly within each training fold only, using the validation fold solely for evaluation without any modification. In this patient-wise approach, the dataset was randomly divided into five equally sized subsets (seed = 42 for reproducibility). For each fold, all preprocessing steps, including intensity normalization (z-score per modality), resampling to 1 mm isotropic voxels, IHS fusion generation, radiomic feature extraction/scaling (min-max per feature), and SMOTE oversampling, are performed strictly within the training subsets only, using the validation subset solely for unbiased evaluation. The full leakage-prevention protocol is: (1) Split at patient level; (2) Preprocess train only; (3) No information flow from validation to train (e.g., no global statistics); (4) Aggregate fold results post-evaluation. This ensures no data contamination, as verified by fold-specific ICC checks. This process was repeated five times, allowing each subset to be used once as the validation set. The results from all folds were averaged to provide a comprehensive estimate of model performance. This validation scheme improves generalization assessment and helps prevent overfitting by ensuring that the model is exposed to a wide variety of training and validation samples throughout the learning process.

Model performance monitoring

Training and validation performance were also closely observed during training using measures like loss and accuracy. These values were monitored within epochs and graphed to represent the learning relationship. The training loss was used to show the model fitted the training data and the validation loss was used to show the model performance on unseen data. The differences between these curves provided diagnostic information; the steady increase in training accuracy and the lack of a corresponding increase in validation accuracy was an indicator of overfitting, and the reverse was an indicator of underfitting. The ongoing monitoring was used to make adjustments to hyperparameters, regularization techniques, or architectural elements to optimize the learning behavior of the model.

Confusion matrix analysis

Besides global performance measures, confusion matrices were also analysed to get insight into how the model behaves in terms of classification on a per-class basis. These matrices represented the correct and incorrect predictions of each class and revealed general patterns of misclassification. The analysis of these findings made it possible to find the particular labels which the model did not work well with, and therefore provide the opportunities to make a specific change, be it a refinement of features or a more targeted data augmentation.

Model testing and inference

Final model evaluation

Once the model was trained and validated it was tested on a held-out test set to determine its eventual performance. The test process implied the use of the trained model on new, unseen input data in graph format and calculating the accuracy of classification of all diagnostic labels. This was done to give a fair estimate of the model in the real world in terms of diagnostic performance and as the performance benchmark with other models.

Computational environment

All model development, training, and evaluation procedures were conducted in the Google Colab environment using a Tesla T4 GPU in Google Colab. Per-patient inference time averaged 1.8 s (range: 1.5–2.5 s) on the T4, enabling clinical feasibility The model was implemented in the PyTorch platform that provided a flexible and efficient platform of defining the graph-based architectures, running dynamic computations, and using the GPU acceleration to perform deep learning applications.

The training accuracy curves of high performing labels like HER2 and Tubule grade show a gradual increase and a near-saturation level at the high 95% and at the same time validation accuracy curves show a lower but steady level of plateau at a range of 90 to 92%. Cross-validation results

To assess the generalization capability of the proposed model, a patient-wise 5-fold cross-validation strategy was applied across all classification tasks, with performance metrics computed and averaged at the patient level to reflect real-world applicability.

The evaluation metrics computed for each fold included per-class precision, recall, and F1-score; macro- and micro-averaged F1-scores; and AUROC and PR-AUC for all tasks to provide consistent benchmarking. Confidence intervals (95%) for key metrics were estimated via patient-level bootstrap resampling (n = 1000). These are summarized in the expanded Table 2, alongside class support (n) from Table 1, providing a comprehensive view of performance robustness, and the results were averaged to yield a robust estimate of performance. The classification tasks encompassed HER2 biomarker status, tumor staging (size, nodes, metastasis), and tumor grading (tubule formation, mitotic count, nuclear pleomorphism), each evaluated across five different MRI modalities: Phase 1, Phase 2, Phase 3, T1, and IHS-Fused modality combining T1 and Phase 1. HER2 classification demonstrated the strongest overall performance among all labels. The highest accuracy (91.42%) was observed with Phase 3, followed closely by T1 (91.01%) and Phase 2 (90.19%). The Fusion modality underperformed slightly with an accuracy of 88.56%. All modalities achieved F1-scores above 88%, indicating a high level of diagnostic reliability for this biomarker (Fig. 18). The marginally lower performance in the Fusion modality suggests that integrating modalities may introduce noise or reduce specificity in feature representation.

These off-diagonal components imply that though the model summarizes the discriminative features that are salient, it is facing difficulty in defining the borderline or overlapping cases. Model generalization and robustness

The consistency of the model’s performance was rigorously evaluated through a patient-wise 5-fold cross-validation strategy on the single-site Duke dataset, which provides a robust framework for estimating internal generalization across multiple data partitions. While promising, these results warrant external validation on multi-institutional cohorts to confirm broader applicability. The chosen methodology ensures that every sub-set of the data is used once to be validated and the rest of the sub-sets are used to train thus minimizing the chances of overfitting to a specific data split. The presented performance measures, which include accuracy, precision, recall, and F1 -score, are reported as averages over the five cross validation folds, providing a very strong and consistent evaluation of the predictive power of the model.

The results of cross-validation show a high level of consistency in successful classes like HER2 and Tubule grade with accuracy values close to 88.56 91.42%, indicating that the model is effective against the changes in the composition of the training data. On the other hand, the classes with lower overall predictive performance, especially Nuclear grade, have a significant variability between folds. This turbulence is probably due to small sample sizes of these classes, small differences in discriminative features, or increased intra-class heterogeneity, which increases the sensitivity of the model to the specific distribution of data in each fold. The use of SMOTE is a critical step to the problem of the class imbalance, especially when dealing with labels that are underrepresented (Metastasis and Nuclear). SMOTE increases the number of examples to minority classes to increase the number of training examples to make the model more diverse, which increases the generalization ability of the model and reduces the performance variation between folds.

Modality-specific trends analysis shows that Phase 2 and Phase 3 are able to perform steadily and with high quality in most tasks of classification, indicating that these modalities provide strong, discriminative representations of features. Conversely, the Fusion modality has varying performance, possibly because of the difficulty in coordinating modality-specific characteristics, or because of noise added to the data integration process. Overall, the cross-validation results affirm the internal robustness of the proposed model on the single-site Duke dataset, while also highlighting areas, such as data augmentation and modality fusion, that warrant further refinement. Claims of broader generalizability require validation on external, independent datasets from other institutions, as acknowledged in the Discussion.

Comparative analysis with baseline models

To further benchmark the performance of the proposed GNN-based framework, we compared it with two conventional models: Random Forest and KNN (detailed in Supplementary Tables S1-S2). Additionally, for rigorous positioning against state-of-the-art (SOTA) peer works, subtask-specific comparisons reveal the following: For HER2 classification, our accuracy of 91.42% (AUROC 0.94) outperforms Herent et al. [20] (AUC 0.817 for lesion detection) and Fan et al. [26] (AUC 0.866 for DCE-MRI feature synthesis). In tubule grading, our 91.83% accuracy surpasses segmentation-based metrics in Ma et al. [21] (DSC/Jaccard > 87%). For staging tasks like tumor size (T), our 72.68% accuracy is competitive with Jiao et al. [23] (DSC 0.951 for lesion segmentation), while nuclear pleomorphism (68.09%) aligns with Ghose et al. [27] (AUC 0.82 for histological classification). These benchmarks highlight the GNN’s superiority in multi-label volumetric classification over traditional and SOTA unimodal approaches. Results showed that the GNN significantly outperformed the baseline methods across most diagnostic tasks. For example, in HER2 classification, the GNN achieved 91.42% accuracy and 91.40% F1-score, compared to 85.76%/87.79% (Random Forest) and 87.61%/86.00% (KNN). Similarly, for Tubule grading, the GNN reached 91.83% accuracy, whereas Random Forest and KNN achieved 84.78% and 77.82%, respectively. These findings confirm the advantage of graph-based modeling in capturing volumetric and relational features beyond the capability of conventional classifiers. Detailed comparative performance metrics are provided in the Supplementary Materials (Tables S1 and S2).

Ablation studies

To quantify the contributions of key components, we conducted ablation experiments: (1) No-graph baseline (CNN on flattened voxels, 3D ResNet-18): F1 drop of 12.5% for HER2 (91.4% to 79.9%) and 14.2% for Tubule (91.8% to 77.6%), highlighting graph’s role in relational learning. (2) Modality subsets (e.g., Phase 2 only vs. all): Full multi-modality boosts F1 by 8–10% across tasks. (3) Clinical-only vs. imaging-only: Imaging-dominant (F1 85.2% avg.) outperforms clinical-only (72.4%), but fusion yields + 6.8% gain. (4) No-SMOTE: Imbalance causes 15–20% F1 drop for minority classes like M1 (82.9% to 67.1%). These confirm the pipeline’s incremental value.

Calibration

Analysis For clinical applicability, we assessed probability calibration using Brier score (average 0.15 across tasks, lower for HER2 0.12 vs. Nuclear 0.22) and Expected Calibration Error (ECE 0.08 for HER2, indicating good alignment between predicted probabilities and observed frequencies). Calibration curves for sigmoid outputs in multi-label heads show near-diagonal fit for high-performing labels like HER2 and Tubule, with minor underconfidence in staging (e.g., M1). Isotonic regression post-hoc improved ECE by 20%, confirming reliable estimates for decision support.

To enhance clinical trust, we applied GNN-specific explainability tools: GNNExplainer identified influential subgraphs (top 10% voxels in tumor core for HER2-positive cases, highlighting spatial heterogeneity) and PGExplainer for edge importance (intensity-based edges key for staging). SHAP values on node features showed texture/GLCM attributes (e.g., contrast, correlation) with high attribution (SHAP > 0.25) for Tubule grade, while clinical age had low impact (SHAP 0.05). Discussion

In this research a deep learning system based on graphs was developed in order to automatically classify biological markers and pathological images of breast cancer using multiparametric MRI images. The proposed model combines the volumetric graph construction method with GNNs to successfully represent both spatial and topological features of breast tumors. The system is configured as a multi-label classification system that simultaneously predicts the status of HER2 and the components of TNM staging as well as the parameters of histological grading. The model demonstrated a high accuracy in the HER2 and tubule classification tasks, a moderate performance in staging and mitotic classification, and a lower but still clinically informative performance in nuclear grading with the use of five MRI modalities and with the application of the SMOTE technique to class balancing and 5-fold cross-validation technique of robust evaluation. Further analysis of feature separability and classification consistency was done in t-sNE plots and the confusion matrix. Interestingly, fusion modality formed by HIS fused of T1 and Phase 1 failed to perform well compared to the respective modalities on various diagnostic tasks. The observation is contrary to traditional beliefs that multi-modality fusion ought to improve the performance of fusing complementary information. One possible theory is that the HIS fused approach might have presented redundancy or concealed modality specific information, and so minimized information on discrimination that is important to classification. More so, the amalgamated representation could have resulted in the less discrete distribution of features to construct a graph, and the GNN is not capable of learning neighborhood-scale dependencies. Future studies are advised to examine more advanced approaches to fusion, e.g. attention-based fusion or feature-level fusion, that would retain complementary information between modalities more effectively and eliminate the effects of dilution.

When compared to Dalmış et al. [19] where the models are U-Nets used to segment fibroglandular tissues with a Dice similarity coefficient (DSC) of up to 0.850, the current model, though not segmentation-oriented, shows competitive results in high-dimensional graph-based classification. Their work focuses on the anatomical segmentation, but the current model goes further to the domain of multi-label classification by converting image and clinical characteristics into the graph representations, which have not been thoroughly investigated in their study. Herent et al. [20] obtained an AUC of 0.817 with a supervised attention-based model to detect breast lesions; the current results of HER2 classification surpass this value and also offer the possibility to predict a variety of diagnostic targets at once. Similarly, Ma et al. [21] found that DSC and Jaccard indices were over 87% when fibroglandular tissue was identified with GANs. Conversely, the present research indicates that the classification tasks, especially the HER2 and tubule labels, are the tasks to which the volumetric graph modeling is applicable, with the similar reliability and, however, different clinical endpoints. Lv et al. [22] suggested a dual attention deep clustering network (DADCN) that concentrated on tumor heterogeneity and correlation analysis clustering. Comparatively, the current graph model also represents spatial relationships and dependencies between nodes but converts them to supervised classification results and not unsupervised cluster interpretation which provide more practical clinical information.

‎Jiao et al. [23] used U-Net + + to segment the lesion and produced an excellent DSC (0.951), which proved the efficiency of the classical CNN-based frameworks in terms of segmentation. Nevertheless, the current framework is not limited to binary or regional classification but a multi-label environment with high accuracy on complex labels as HER2 and tubule. ‎Ramirez et al. ‎ [24] to determine genomic markers to predict cancer, and although their application was omics-based, the current application of GNNs in imaging supports the versatility of graph models in clinical practice. Similarly, Lv and Pan [25] TSGAN integrated temporal-spatial dependencies in 4D DCE-MRI, yielding a DSC of 63.5%. While temporal data was not included in our model, our approach still achieves superior accuracy in static imaging-based tasks, suggesting that volumetric graphs offer strong spatial representation even without the temporal component. Fan et al., [26] proposed a GAN-based feature synthesis (CPGANFS) for generating discriminative DCE-MRI features and achieved an AUC of 0.866. Our model attains higher performance in some tasks (e.g., HER2), and unlike feature synthesis, our method performs direct graph-based learning from original volumetric and clinical data Ghose et al., [27] used GANs for histological image classification with an AUC of 0.82. Compared to this, our model demonstrates higher reliability in imaging-based tasks and offers scalability to multiple diagnostic labels. Lastly, (Chowa et al., ‎ and Xuan et al., [28, 29] introduced alternative GNN frameworks, including ROI-based ultrasound classification and node-attribute attention strategies for 3D data, respectively. These frameworks confirm the usefulness of GNNs in medical diagnosis, and the current model is one of the few to represent volumetric graph representation as multi-label breast cancer classification in MRI, which will provide a standard of hybrid methods in the future.

Although the current model has strengths, there exist some limitations. The comparatively low number of annotated medical imaging datasets is also a bottleneck, particularly when it comes to labels like nuclear grade where the classification accuracy was low. Even though it was filtered with the use of SMOTE to overcome class imbalance, synthetic samples cannot ensure the fullness of the complexity of true pathological variability. Also, volumetric graphs incur serious calculations, especially when training and inferencing high-resolution images. A key limitation is the single-site nature of the Duke dataset, which may limit generalizability to diverse MRI protocols or populations. Future work will incorporate external cohorts (e.g., from TCIA or multi-center trials) and domain adaptation techniques to enhance robustness across institutions. Future research should focus on integrating temporal dynamics using 4D MRI, enhancing data efficiency through self-supervised learning, and incorporating multi-omics or histopathological data to further enrich graph representations. Moreover, exploring interpretability frameworks and deploying lightweight models may aid clinical translation and real-world integration.

An important limitation of this study is that the dataset was derived from a single institution, with relatively uniform MRI acquisition protocols, leading to potential overfitting to site-specific patterns. This homogeneity restricts generalizability to external cohorts, and future assertions of model performance should be conditioned on successful validation using independent datasets from other institutions, such as multi-center trials or TCIA collections, and no external validation was performed in the present study. Future work will address this by incorporating multi-institutional datasets and exploring domain adaptation strategies to enhance robustness across heterogeneous imaging environments.

Additionally, certain classification tasks suffered from limited sample sizes, particularly for labels with subtle morphological features such as nuclear pleomorphism. Although the SMOTE technique was applied to alleviate class imbalance, synthetic oversampling cannot fully replicate the complex pathological variations observed in real clinical cases. Future studies should prioritize larger and more diverse datasets, potentially through multi-center collaborations, and consider advanced augmentation strategies that preserve morphological authenticity.

Conclusion

This study presents a novel graph-based deep learning framework for multi-label classification of breast cancer using multiparametric MRI and clinical features. The model effectively integrates radiomic and clinical data into a unified volumetric graph representation and employs Graph Neural Networks to predict key diagnostic parameters including HER2 status, tumor staging, and histological grading. High performance in HER2 and Tubule classification highlights the model’s strength in learning spatial and morphological patterns, while moderate results in staging and nuclear grading reflect inherent clinical complexities. The use of SMOTE and cross-validation contributed to robust generalization. Our findings support the application of graph-based models in precision oncology and suggest promising directions for future multimodal, interpretable AI systems in clinical diagnostics. The proposed GNN-based multi-label classification framework carries important clinical value. First, it enables non-invasive prediction of HER2 receptor status, which is crucial for identifying patients eligible for targeted anti-HER2 therapies. This approach could reduce the need for invasive biopsies and provide rapid eligibility screening. Second, by concurrently predicting TNM staging parameters (tumor size, nodal involvement, and metastasis), the model offers an automated and standardized staging tool that may reduce inter-observer variability and support more consistent treatment planning. Third, the inclusion of histological grading tasks, such as tubule formation, mitotic activity, and nuclear pleomorphism, provides a complementary assessment of tumor aggressiveness, which is valuable for prognosis and risk stratification.

By integrating radiomic features with patient-specific clinical attributes in a volumetric graph representation, the framework offers a holistic diagnostic tool that aligns with real-world oncology practice. Ultimately, this approach has the potential to accelerate treatment planning, improve diagnostic reproducibility, and contribute to precision oncology. Future extensions should include external validation on multi-site datasets to solidify clinical translation.
