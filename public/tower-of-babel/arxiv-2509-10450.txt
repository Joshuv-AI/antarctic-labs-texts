A linear-time algorithm for Chow decomposition
Alexander Taveira Blomenhofer, Benjamin Lovitz

Abstract
. We propose a linear-time algorithm to compute low-rank Chow decompositions. Our algorithm can decompose concise symmetric 3 3 -tensors in n n variables of Chow rank n / 3 n/3 . The algorithm is pencil-based, hence it relies on generalized eigenvalue computations. We also develop sub-quadratic time algorithms for higher order Chow decompositions, and Chow decompositions of 3 3 -tensors into products of linear forms which do not lie on the generic orbit. In particular, we obtain a sub-quadratic-time algorithm for decomposing a symmetric 3-tensor into a linear combination of W-tensors.

Primary: 14Q15, 15A69, secondary: 14N07

1.
Introduction

Let
T\in(\mathbb{K}^{n})^{\otimes 3}
be a 3-tensor over
\mathbb{K}\in\{\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}\}
, and let
\mathcal{L}_{T}\subseteq(\mathbb{K}^{n})^{\otimes 2}
be the space of all matrices which arise from contraction of
T
by some vector
f\in\mathbb{\mathbb{K}}^{n}
along the first index.
We define the
contraction variety

X_{T}
to be the set of all vectors
f\in\mathbb{\mathbb{K}}^{n}
such that the contraction
T_{f}
of
T
along
f
has rank strictly smaller than a generic contraction. For instance, if general elements of
\mathcal{L}_{T}
have full rank, then we have

\displaystyle X_{T}=\{f\in\mathbb{\mathbb{K}}^{n}\mid\det(T_{f})=0\}.

In the low-rank setting, the contraction variety often carries information that allows one to compute the minimum rank decomposition of a tensor and certify its uniqueness. For instance, if
T\in\mathbb{K}^{n\times n\times n}
is a concise symmetric tensor of symmetric rank at most
n
, then its contraction variety is a union of
n
hyperplanes. Indeed, if
T
has a decomposition
T=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}^{\otimes 3}
, then by conciseness,
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}
form a basis of
\mathbb{K}^{n}
. We will later see that then

\displaystyle X_{T}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\langle a_{i}\rangle^{\perp}.

Here and throughout, we take the orthogonal complement with respect to the bilinear pairing
\langle a,b\rangle=a^{T}b
, which is an inner product for
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{R}
. Hence, the contractions
T\mapsto T_{f}
that make
T_{f}
singular are precisely those for which
f
is orthogonal to one of
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}
. This fact can be turned into a decomposition algorithm via matrix pencils. For symmetric rank, it is classically known how to compute the minimum rank decomposition under the given assumptions. In fact, algorithms for concise tensors of symmetric rank
n
have been discovered and rediscovered so many times that it is difficult to give correct attribution to a single reference. See the discussion under
related work
.

Surprisingly, we find that a very similar approach also works for tensors of low Chow rank.
Recall that the
Chow rank
is the minimum decomposition of a symmetric tensor
T
into terms of the form
a_{i1}a_{i2}a_{i3}
, where

a_{i1}a_{i2}a_{i3}:=\frac{1}{3!}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{3}}a_{i\sigma(1)}\otimes a_{i\sigma(2)}\otimes a_{i\sigma(3)}

denotes the symmetrization of the tensor product
a_{i1}\otimes a_{i2}\otimes a_{i3}
. If we view
T
as a homogeneous polynomial, then this decomposes
T
into a sum of products of linear forms.

We will see that the contraction variety of a concise symmetric order
3
tensor of Chow rank
n/3
is also a union of hyperplanes. Precisely, if the rank-1 factors are given by the vectors
\{a_{i1}a_{i2}a_{i3}\}_{i=1,\ldots,n/3}
as in (
1
), then
\{a_{ij}\}_{i=1,\dots,n/3,j=1,2,3}
forms a basis for
\mathbb{K}^{n}
, and we have

\displaystyle X_{T}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{r}\langle a_{i1}\rangle^{\perp}\cup\langle a_{i2}\rangle^{\perp}\cup\langle a_{i3}\rangle^{\perp}.

This fact will allow us to give a linear-time, pencil-based algorithm to compute Chow decompositions.

Organization and overview of results

Our main result is the following:

1.1 Theorem
.

Let
n=3r
, and let
T\in\mathbb{K}^{n\times n\times n}
be a concise symmetric 3-tensor of Chow rank
r
. Then the Chow rank decomposition of
T
is unique, and it can be recovered in time
\mathcal{O}(n^{3})
.

Note that the runtime is linear in the size
n^{3}
of the tensor. This result is presented in
Section

2
. The rank assumptions, together with conciseness, imply that each Chow rank 1 term lies on the generic orbit of the Chow variety, which is
\GL_{n}e_{1}e_{2}e_{3}
. In
Section

4
, we examine what can be said if one allows non-generic orbits, i.e., if the Chow rank 1 components may be chosen from any of the
4
orbits in the Chow variety (namely, the orbits of
e_{1}^{3},e_{1}e_{2}e_{3},e_{1}^{2}e_{2},
and
e_{1}e_{2}(e_{1}+e_{2})
). In particular, we obtain the following:

1.2 Theorem
.

Let
T=\sum_{i=1}^{r}a_{i1}a_{i2}a_{i3}
be a symmetric tensor for which the spaces
U_{i}=\langle a_{i1},a_{i2},a_{i3}\rangle
are in direct sum. Then a minimum Chow rank decomposition of
T
can be obtained in time
\mathcal{O}(n^{4})
.

The Chow rank decomposition is unique over
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{R}
, and has a controlled non-uniqueness over
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{C}
resulting from the fact that
e_{1}^{3}+e_{2}^{3}
has Chow rank 1. See
Section

4
for more details. In particular,
Theorem

1.2
gives an
\mathcal{O}(n^{4})
-time algorithm to decompose a symmetric tensor into W-tensors. These are Chow rank 1 tensors of the form
a_{i1}^{2}a_{i2}
.

In
Section

3
, we discuss a generalization to symmetric tensors
T
of odd order
2d+1
, that is,
T\in S^{2d+1}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
. We show that if
T
is general of Chow rank
r=\mathcal{O}(n^{d})
, then there is an efficient algorithm to compute the unique minimum Chow rank decomposition. Note that pencil-based methods admit a similar odd-order generalization for symmetric rank, which also allows to go up to rank
\mathcal{O}(n^{d})
. The
\mathcal{O}
-notation hides constants depending on
d
. We leave open the question of generalizing our construction to even order.

While our main focus is on Chow decompositions, we expect our methodology to be of independent interest. Our approach can in principle be extended to decomposition problems for which the contraction variety is an arrangement of planes of constant codimension.

Model of computation

Here and throughout, we work in the real (or complex) model of computation, and assume access to an oracle to diagonalize a
k\times k
diagonalizable matrix in time
\mathcal{O}(k^{3})
. This is a standard model of computation for many tensor decomposition algorithms already present in the literature, e.g.
[
9
,
13
,
12
]
.

Related work

As mentioned above, methods for low-rank tensor decomposition have been studied from an ample variety of angles. For symmetric tensor rank, algorithms exist based on either simultaneous diagonalization, tensor power iteration
[
1
]
, semidefinite programming
[
15
]
, the eigenvalue method
[
20
]
and, most importantly for this work, pencils
[
14
]
. The earliest algorithmic results of this flavour appear to be due to Sylvester
[
19
]
and Prony
[
2
]
in 1795. See
[
18
]
for a modern exposition of Prony’s work. A similar algorithm based on simultaneous matrix diagonalization was introduced by Harshman
[
8
]
and later by Leurgans, Ross, and Abel
[
14
]
(and then rediscovered several times thereafter). Matrix pencils have also been studied from the geometric viewpoint, e.g., via the variety of complete collineations
[
16
]
,
[
7
]
.

At the same time, comparatively little is known for the Chow variety. They are overall one of the least understood standard models of tensor decomposition (see
[
21
, p. 2]
). The identifiability of Chow decompositions has only recently been addressed (
[
21
]
,
[
3
]
). Prior work of Kayal and Saha
[
11
]
gave an
n^{c}
-time algorithm for Chow decomposition in the black-box model of computation for an uncontrolled constant
c
. By contrast, the exponents for our algorithms are tightly controlled, and we can handle similar rank constraints. For example, for order-3 tensors our algorithm can decompose tensors up to Chow rank
n/3
, while that of
[
11
]
handles Chow rank
n/9
. More recently,
[
6
]
(see also
[
5
]
) gave
\text{poly}(n)
-time algorithms for decomposing homogeneous polynomials into powers of low-degree polynomials in the black-box model.

Applications

Chow decompostions of cubic forms correspond to depth-three
\Sigma\Pi\Sigma
arithmetic circuits, and the Chow rank of a form determines the minimum fan-in of any such circuit that computes it. See e.g.
[
10
]
and the references therein.
Chow decompositions are also relevant in quantum information theory, as Chow rank-1 terms are symmetrizations of pure product states, and include many commonly used examples such as W-states and GHZ-states. In particular, our algorithm from
Section

4
can be used to decompose a given tripartite quantum state into a superposition of W-states.

2.
Chow decompositions for third order tensors

Let
\mathbb{K}\in\{\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}\}
. A Chow decomposition of a symmetric order-
3
tensor
T\in S^{3}(\mathbb{K}^{n})
has the form

(1)

\displaystyle T=\sum_{i=1}^{r}a_{i1}a_{i2}a_{i3}

where for vectors
v_{1},\dots,v_{d}\in\mathbb{K}^{d}
we let
v_{1}\cdots v_{d}:=\frac{1}{d!}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{d}}v_{\sigma(1)}\otimes\cdots\otimes v_{\sigma(d)}
. If
r
is minimal, then (
1
) is called a Chow-rank decomposition.

We consider the case where the set
\{a_{i1},a_{i2},a_{i3}\}_{i=1,\ldots,r}
of all vectors occurring in the decomposition is linearly independent. Note that this holds whenever
T
is a concise tensor of Chow rank
r=n/3
. In the following, we determine the contraction variety.

2.1 Proposition
.

Let
T
be a concise tensor with a Chow decomposition as above and where
\{a_{i1},a_{i2},a_{i3}\}_{i=1,\ldots,r}
is linearly independent. In particular,
n=3r
. Then,

\displaystyle X_{T}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{r}\langle a_{i1}\rangle^{\perp}\cup\langle a_{i2}\rangle^{\perp}\cup\langle a_{i3}\rangle^{\perp}.

Proof.

For
f\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
, we may write
T_{f}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}M_{if}
, where
M_{if}
is the contraction of
a_{i1}a_{i2}a_{i3}
along
f^{T}
. Note that the images of
M_{if}
are contained in
U_{i}=\langle a_{i1},a_{i2},a_{i3}\rangle
and the sum of the spaces
U_{i}
is direct. Therefore, it holds that
\im T_{f}=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{r}\im M_{if}
. Consequentially,
T_{f}
is rank-deficient if and only if at least one of the
M_{if}
has rank less than
3
.

(As an aside, note that for generic
f
, all
M_{if}
have rank
3
and
T_{f}
has rank
3r
, which equals
n
by conciseness).

Let us find the set of
f
for which
M_{if}
has rank less than
3
. For this purpose, we can assume without loss of generality that
a_{ij}=e_{j}
for
j=1,2,3
, and that
f=\alpha e_{1}+\beta e_{2}+\gamma e_{3}+g
for some
g\in\im(M_{if})^{\perp}
. Then
3M_{if}=\alpha e_{2}e_{3}+\beta e_{1}e_{3}+\gamma e_{1}e_{2}
, which has determinant
\frac{1}{4}\alpha\beta\gamma
. So
M_{if}
is rank deficient if and only if
f\in\langle a_{i1}\rangle^{\perp}\cup\langle a_{i2}\rangle^{\perp}\cup\langle a_{i3}\rangle^{\perp}
. This completes the proof.
∎

2.1
shows that the contraction variety of a concise tensor with a low-rank Chow decomposition is a union of codimension-1 subspaces. Therefore, we can proceed with a pencil-based algorithm and intersect
X_{T}
with a line, just like in the classical pencil-based algorithm for tensor rank.
By
2.1
, the generalized eigenvalue problem

\displaystyle\det(T_{f}-\lambda T_{g})=0

will have
3r
solutions, which we label
\lambda_{i1},\lambda_{i2},\lambda_{i3}
, where
i=1,\ldots,r
. Let the labeling be chosen such that
f-\lambda_{ik}g\in\langle a_{ik}\rangle^{\perp}
. As the solutions correspond to intersection points of the generic line
f-\lambda g
with
X_{T}
, the
3r
solutions will be pairwise distinct. The corresponding eigenvectors we label accordingly as
x_{i1},x_{i2},x_{i3}
. The eigenspaces are one-dimensional, as there are
3r
pairwise distinct eigenvalues.
Denote
h_{ik}:=f-\lambda_{ik}g\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
.

Note that
x_{ik}\perp U_{j}
for
j\neq i
. Indeed,
M_{jh_{ik}}x_{ik}=0
, and
M_{jh_{ik}}
is a symmetric matrix with
\im(M_{jh_{ik}})=U_{j}
.

2.2 Remark
.

Even when
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{R}
, we always consider
U_{i}
and
\im M_{if}
as complex subspaces. While the matrices
T_{f}
and
T_{g}
are symmetric, they may not be positive definite (for instance,
(e_{1}e_{2}e_{3})_{f}
has zeros on the diagonal). Therefore, the generalized eigenvalue problem can have complex solutions. In other words, for real
f,g\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the intersection points of the line
\lambda\mapsto f+\lambda g
with
X_{T}
may be complex.
Therefore, we will always perform algorithmic operations over
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{C}
, unless explicitly stated otherwise.

In the following Proposition, we show a criterion to match eigenvectors together, which belong to the same Chow rank 1 term.

2.3 Proposition
.

Let
\ell\in\mathbb{K}^{n}
be generic. Then
x_{ik}^{T}T_{\ell}x_{i^{\prime}k^{\prime}}\neq 0
if and only if
i=i^{\prime}
.

Proof.

If
i\neq i^{\prime}
then
x_{ik}^{T}T_{\ell}x_{i^{\prime}k^{\prime}}=0
, since
x_{ik}\perp U_{i^{\prime}}
for
i^{\prime}\neq i
. If
i=i^{\prime}
, then it suffices to exhibit a single choice for which
x_{ik^{\prime}}^{T}T_{\ell}x_{ik}\neq 0
, as this is an open condition on
\ell
. By symmetry, it suffices to prove that
x_{11}^{T}T_{\ell}x_{1k}\neq 0
for
k=1,2
. Choose
\ell
so that
\langle\ell,a_{ik}\rangle=\delta_{i=1,k=1}
. Then

\displaystyle x_{11}^{T}T_{\ell}x_{1k}=x_{11}^{T}\sum_{i}M_{i\ell}x_{1k}=x_{11}^{T}M_{1\ell}x_{1k}=\alpha_{k}\langle x_{11},a_{12}\rangle\langle x_{1k},a_{13}\rangle,

where
\alpha_{1}=\frac{1}{3}
and
\alpha_{2}=\frac{1}{3!}
. Suppose toward contradiction that one of the inner products on the righthand side is zero, e.g. the first. Note that

(2)

\displaystyle M_{h_{11}}=\frac{1}{3!}(a_{11}v^{T}+va_{11}^{T}),

where
v=\langle h_{11},a_{12}\rangle a_{13}+\langle h_{11},a_{13}\rangle a_{12}
. Since there are no repeated eigenvalues, the coefficients of
a_{13}
and
a_{12}
are both nonzero. Since
M_{h_{11}}x_{11}=0
, we have

(3)

\displaystyle x_{11}\in\langle a_{11}\rangle^{\perp}\cap\langle v\rangle^{\perp}.

But if it furthermore holds that
\langle x_{11},a_{12}\rangle=0
, then
x_{11}\perp U_{1}
, so
x_{11}=0
, a contradiction. This completes the proof.
∎

Thus, by examining the inner products
x_{i^{\prime}k^{\prime}}^{T}T_{\ell}x_{ik}
we can partition the eigenvectors into triples
\{x_{i1},x_{i2},x_{i3}\}
and obtain a basis
\{T_{f}x_{i1},T_{f}x_{i2},T_{f}x_{i3}\}
for
U_{i}
. To see that they form a basis, note that
T_{f}
is a full-rank matrix,
x_{i1},x_{i2},x_{i3}
are linearly independent, and
T_{f}x_{ik}\in U_{i}
for
k=1,2,3
.

We now claim that
\langle x_{ik}\rangle^{\perp}\cap\langle h_{ik}\rangle^{\perp}\cap U_{i}=\langle a_{ik}\rangle
. By symmetry it suffices to prove this for
i=k=1
. The containment
\supseteq
is easily verified from Proposition
2.1
and (
3
). For the containment
\subseteq
, it is evident from the formula (
2
) that

\displaystyle\langle x_{11}\rangle^{\perp}\cap U_{1}=\langle a_{11},v\rangle.

Since clearly
v\notin\langle h_{11}\rangle^{\perp}
, the claim is proven.

The above shows that we can obtain non-zero vectors
a_{ik}^{\prime}
in
\langle a_{ik}\rangle
. We know that
T=\sum_{i=1}^{r}c_{i}a_{i1}^{\prime}a_{i2}^{\prime}a_{i3}^{\prime}
for some scalars
c_{i}
, and it remains only to compute these
c_{i}
. To do this, we solve the system
T_{f}\;x_{i1}=c_{i}(a_{i_{1}}^{\prime}a_{i_{2}}^{\prime}a_{i_{3}}^{\prime})_{f}\;x_{i1}
for
c_{i}\in\mathbb{K}
. Here, we use again that
x_{i1}\perp U_{i^{\prime}}
for
i^{\prime}\neq i
. Finally, we output
\{c_{i}a_{i_{1}}^{\prime}a_{i_{2}}^{\prime}a_{i_{3}}^{\prime}:i=1,\dots,r\}
.

We have proved the following theorem.

2.4 Theorem
.

Suppose that
T=\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i1}a_{i2}a_{i3}
with
\{a_{ik}\}_{i\in[r],k=1,2,3}
linearly independent. Then the following algorithm produces the terms
a_{i1}a_{i2}a_{i3}
:

(1)

Sample generic
f,g,\ell\in\mathbb{K}^{n}
.

(2)

Find the
3r
solutions
\lambda_{ik}\in\mathbb{C},x_{ik}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
to the generalized eigenvalue problem
\det(T_{f}-\lambda T_{g})=0
, where
i=1,\ldots,r
and
k=1,2,3
.

(3)

Set
h_{ik}:=f-\lambda_{ik}g
.

(4)

For each vector
x_{ik}
, find the vectors
x_{i^{\prime}k^{\prime}}
for which
x_{i^{\prime}k^{\prime}}^{T}T_{\ell}x_{ik}\neq 0
. This partitions the vectors into triples
\{x_{i1},x_{i2},x_{i3}\}
.

(5)

Let
U_{i}=\langle T_{f}x_{i1},T_{f}x_{i2},T_{f}x_{i3}\rangle
.

(6)

Compute nonzero vectors

\displaystyle a_{ik}^{\prime}\in\langle x_{ik}\rangle^{\perp}\cap\langle h_{ik}\rangle^{\perp}\cap U_{i}.

for
k=1,2,3
.

(7)

Solve the system
T_{f}\;x_{i1}=c_{i}(a_{i1}^{\prime}a_{i2}^{\prime}a_{i3}^{\prime})_{f}\;x_{i1}
for
c_{i}\in\mathbb{K}
, and output
\{c_{i}a_{i1}^{\prime}a_{i2}^{\prime}a_{i3}^{\prime}:i=1,\dots,r\}
.

Runtime

The algorithm from
Theorem

2.4
has runtime
\mathcal{O}(n^{3})
. Computing
T_{f},T_{g},
and
T_{\ell}
takes time
\mathcal{O}(n^{3})
. Solving the generalized eigenvalue problem takes time
\mathcal{O}(n^{3})
. In Step (4), it takes time
\mathcal{O}(n^{3})
to compute the vectors
T_{\ell}x_{i^{\prime}k^{\prime}}
, and then time
\mathcal{O}(n)
to compute each of the
\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
inner products
x_{ik}^{T}T_{\ell}x_{i^{\prime}k^{\prime}}
, giving a total runtime of
\mathcal{O}(n^{3})
. Step (6) can be computed in time
\mathcal{O}(n^{3})
. In Step (7), for each
i
we need to compute
T_{f}\;x_{i1}
, compute
(a_{i1}^{\prime}a_{i2}^{\prime}a_{i3}^{\prime})_{f}\;x_{i1}
, and solve for
c_{i}
. Each of these steps takes time
\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
, so doing this for
i=1,\dots,n
takes time
\mathcal{O}(n^{3})
in total.

3.
Chow decompositions for higher order tensors

In this section, we develop an algorithm to compute Chow decompositions for tensors of odd order
2d+1
. In the case
d=1
, we recover exactly the algorithm from the previous section.

Let us start from a general tensor
T
of Chow rank
r
, say with minimum rank decomposition

\displaystyle T=\sum_{i=1}^{r}a_{i1}\cdot\cdots\cdot a_{i(2d+1)}

and
a_{ij}
, where
i=1,\ldots,r
,
j=1,\ldots,2d+1
in general position. After contracting by a vector
f\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
, we obtain a symmetric tensor of order
2d
, which may be reshaped into a matrix
T_{f}\colon S^{d}(\mathbb{C}^{n})\to S^{d}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
.
Clearly, we have that
T_{f}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}M_{if}
, where
M_{if}
denotes the matrix reshaping of the contraction of
a_{i1}\cdot\cdots\cdot a_{i(2d+1)}
by
f
.

First, we need to establish that the images of the matrices
M_{1f},\ldots,M_{rf}
will be in direct sum. Fortunately, this is guaranteed by a recent work, see
[
3
]
.

3.1 Lemma
.

For
b=(b_{1},\ldots,b_{2d+1})\in(\mathbb{C}^{n})^{2d+1}
, define
L(b)
as the subspace of
S^{d}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
spanned by all vectors

\displaystyle b_{i_{1}}\cdot\cdots\cdot b_{i_{d}}\in S^{d}(\mathbb{C}^{n}),

where
i_{1},\ldots,i_{d}
are
d
pairwise distinct indices chosen from
\{1,\ldots,2d+1\}
.
Then, a sum of
r
general spaces
L(b^{(1)})+\ldots+L(b^{(r)})
is direct, as long as

\displaystyle r\leq\dfrac{\binom{n+d-1}{d}}{\binom{2d+1}{d}}-\binom{2d+1}{d}.

In particular, the symmetric matrices
M_{f}(b^{(1)}),\dots,M_{f}(b^{(r)})\colon S^{d}(\mathbb{C}^{n})\to S^{d}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
, given by

\displaystyle M_{f}(b^{(q)})=\frac{d!^{2}}{2(2d+1)!}\sum_{I\dot{\cup}J\dot{\cup}\{k\}=[2d+1]}\langle f,b^{(q)}_{k}\rangle\cdot(b^{(q)}_{I})(b^{(q)}_{J})^{T}+\langle f,b^{(q)}_{k}\rangle\cdot(b^{(q)}_{J})(b^{(q)}_{I})^{T},

have images in direct sum. Here,
b^{(q)}_{I}:=b^{(q)}_{i_{1}}\cdots b^{(q)}_{i_{d}}
, and the sum runs over all partitions of
[2d+1]
into disjoint sets, where
I
and
J
both contain
d
elements.

Proof.

First, note that the image of the matrix
M_{f}(b)
is contained in
L(b)
for all
f
and all
b\in(\mathbb{C}^{n})^{2d+1}
. Next, note that the class of all spaces
L(b)
is
\GL_{n}
invariant, since
U^{\otimes d}L(b)=L(Ub_{1},\ldots,Ub_{2d+1})
for each matrix
U\in\GL_{n}
. Additionally,
S^{d}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
is an irreducible
\GL_{n}
-module, by Schur-Weyl duality.
This means that the conditions of
[
3
, Theorem 3.4]
are satisfied (if we take
T_{b}:=L(b)
in
[
3
, Theorem 3.4]
and
X
as the dense open subset of
(\mathbb{C}^{n})^{2d+1}
where
L(b)
has constant dimension).
We obtain that for general
b^{(1)},\ldots,b^{(r)}\in(\mathbb{C}^{n})^{2d+1}
, the spaces
L(b^{(1)}),\ldots,L(b^{(r)})
are in direct sum. In particular, the images of
M(b^{(1)}),\ldots,M(b^{(r)})
are in direct sum.
∎

3.2 Remark
.

In
Lemma

3.1
, the dimension of a space
L(b)
is upper bounded by
\binom{2d+1}{d}
for each
b\in(\mathbb{C}^{n})^{2d+1}
. Additionally, if
2d+1\leq n
, then we can easily see that this bound is an equality for generic
b
. Indeed, after a change of coordinates, it suffices to check this statement for the standard basis
b=e=(e_{1},\ldots,e_{n})
. Then, the expressions
b_{I}
correspond bijectively to the square-free monomials
x_{I}=\prod_{i\in I}x_{i}
in variables
x_{1},\ldots,x_{2d+1}
of degree
d
, which are of course linearly independent.
Using that, it is immediate to see that the image of
M_{f}(e)
equals
\langle e_{I}\mid I\subseteq[2d+1],|I|=d\rangle
for general
f
and thus the rank of
M_{f}(e)
is
\binom{2d+1}{d}
.

Let us now consider again the contraction variety.
From
Lemma

3.1
, we obtain that the rank of
T_{f}
drops below its generic rank, if and only if one of
M_{1f},\ldots,M_{rf}
has rank less than
\binom{2d+1}{d}
. In the next step, we will examine when the latter can happen. Our goal is to prove the following statement.

3.3 Theorem
.

Let
T=\sum_{i=1}^{r}a_{i1}\cdot\cdots\cdot a_{i(2d+1)}
be a generic symmetric tensor of Chow-rank
r
, where
r\leq\dfrac{\binom{n+d-1}{d}}{\binom{2d+1}{d}}-\binom{2d+1}{d}
and
2d+1\leq n
.
Then, its contraction variety is given by

\displaystyle X_{T}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{r}\langle a_{i1}\rangle^{\perp}\cup\ldots\cup\langle a_{i(2d+1)}\rangle^{\perp}.

Proof.

By
Lemma

3.1
and
Remark

3.2
, the matrices
M_{if}=M_{f}(a_{i1},\dots,a_{i(2d+1)})
have rank equal to
\binom{2d+1}{d}
, if one considers a contraction by a generic
f\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
. By
Lemma

3.1
, the rank of a generic contraction
T_{f}
therefore equals
r\binom{2d+1}{d}
under the given assumptions
2d+1\leq n
and
r\leq\dfrac{\binom{n+d-1}{d}}{\binom{2d+1}{d}}-\binom{2d+1}{d}
. In this regime, the contraction variety of a general Chow tensor of rank
r
is thus given by

\displaystyle X_{T}=\{f\in\mathbb{C}^{n}\mid\rk T_{f}<r\binom{2d+1}{d}\}

By
Lemma

3.1
, we have that the images of
M_{1f},\ldots,M_{rf}
are in direct sum. Therefore,

\displaystyle X_{T}=\{f\in\mathbb{C}^{n}\mid\exists i\colon\rk M_{if}<\binom{2d+1}{d}\}.

We will show in the following
Lemma

3.4
that the rank of
M_{if}
drops if and only if
f
is orthogonal to one of the vectors
a_{i1},\ldots,a_{i(2d+1)}
. Thus, we conclude with the desired result.
∎

3.4 Lemma
.

Let
b_{1},\ldots,b_{2d+1}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
be general and assume that
2d+1\leq n
. Consider the Chow rank 1 tensor
M(b):=b_{1}\cdots b_{2d+1}\in S^{2d+1}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
and for each
f\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
the matrices
M_{f}(b)
from
Lemma

3.1
. Then,

\displaystyle\rk M_{f}(b)<\binom{2d+1}{d}\Longleftrightarrow f\in\langle b_{1}\rangle^{\perp}\cup\ldots\cup\langle b_{2d+1}\rangle^{\perp}.

Proof.

By using the
\GL_{n}
action, we may w.l.o.g. assume that
b
consists of standard basis vectors, so that
b=e:=(e_{1},\ldots,e_{2d+1})
.

We begin by proving the direction “
\implies
.” Let
\alpha_{i}=\langle e_{i},f\rangle
. Clearly, acting with the torus
(\mathbb{C}^{\times})^{2d+1}\subseteq(\mathbb{C}^{\times})^{n}
on
e
cannot change the rank of
M_{f}(e)
. If we let
\beta:=(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{2d+1})
, then

\displaystyle M_{f}(\beta\cdot e)=\alpha_{1}\beta_{2}\cdots\beta_{2d+1}\frac{e}{e_{1}}+\ldots+{\beta_{1}\cdots\beta_{2d}\alpha_{2d+1}}\frac{e}{{e_{2d+1}}},

where
e/e_{i}:=e_{1}\cdots e_{\hat{i}}\cdots e_{2d+1}
is the Chow rank 1 tensor of order
2d
where the term
e_{i}
is omitted. Choosing
\beta_{i}=\frac{\alpha_{i}}{\sqrt[2d]{\alpha_{1}\cdots\alpha_{2d+1}}}
, we obtain
M_{1,\dots,1}(e)=\frac{e}{e_{1}}+\dots+\frac{e}{e_{2d+1}}
.
Hence,
M_{f}(e)
is rank deficient if and only if
M_{(1,\ldots,1)}(e)
is rank deficient. Since a general vector
f\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
will satisfy the open condition
\langle e_{i},f\rangle\neq 0
, we conclude that
\rk M_{f}(b)=\binom{2d+1}{d}
, if all
\langle b_{i},f\rangle
are nonzero. This shows the direction “
\implies
”.

Let us now show the direction “
\impliedby
”. Let
\Pi
be the matrix for which
\Pi_{L(e)}=I_{L(e)}
and
\Pi_{L(e)^{\perp}}=0
, where
L(e)^{\perp}
is the orthogonal complement to
L(b)
with respect to the bilinear pairing
\langle x,y\rangle=x^{T}y
(or the sesquilinear form; they are equivalent since
L(e)
has a real basis).
Note that
M_{\ell}(e)\Pi=M_{\ell}(e)
for any
\ell\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
. Let us view
M_{\ell}(e)\Pi
as a map on
L(e)
, which is nonsingular for generic
\ell\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
. Since the set of singular matrices has codimension 1, for generic
f,g\in\mathbb{K}^{n}
the (complex) subspace spanned by
M_{f}(e)\Pi,M_{g}(e)\Pi
must contain a matrix
M_{\ell}(e)\Pi
whose image is properly contained in
L(e)
, i.e., a matrix which is singular on
L(e)
. Hence,
M_{\ell}(e)
has subgeneric rank.

In particular, there exists some
\lambda
such that
f-\lambda g\in X_{M(b)}
. By the already shown direction “
\implies
”, we know that there exists some
i\in\{1,\ldots,2d+1\}
such that
f-\lambda g\in\langle b_{i}\rangle^{\perp}
. Fix such
i
. Note that
f-\lambda g\notin\langle b_{j}\rangle^{\perp}
for
j\neq i
, since a general line cannot intersect the codimension 2 subspace
\langle b_{i}\rangle^{\perp}\cap\langle b_{j}\rangle^{\perp}
. Furthermore, there can only be precisely one
\lambda
such that
f-\lambda g\in\langle b_{i}\rangle^{\perp}
, since the degree of a hyperplane is
1
. It is therefore justified that we denote this unique
\lambda
by
\lambda_{i}
.

We thus showed that the intersection
B=X_{M(b)}\cap\langle b_{i}\rangle^{\perp}\neq\{0\}
is nontrivial. However, the set
B
has to be of codimension
1
, since otherwise the general line
\lambda\mapsto f-\lambda g
could not intersect
B
. Since
\langle b_{i}\rangle^{\perp}
is irreducible, this shows that
\langle b_{i}\rangle^{\perp}\subseteq X_{M(b)}
. However, the matrix
M(b)
and thus also the contraction variety
X_{M(b)}
are invariant under permutations
b\mapsto(b_{\sigma(1)},\ldots,b_{\sigma(2d+1)})
, where
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{2d+1}
. Therefore, by symmetry, we conclude that
\langle b_{1}\rangle^{\perp}\cup\ldots\cup\langle b_{2d+1}\rangle^{\perp}\subseteq X_{M(b)}
. This shows “
\impliedby
”.
∎

3.5 Remark
.

From the proof of
Lemma

3.4
, we conclude that the general line
f-\lambda g
intersects
X_{M(b)}
precisely in the
2d+1
points
\lambda_{i}
of the line for which
f-\lambda_{i}g\in\langle b_{i}\rangle^{\perp}
. Since
\dim V=\binom{2d+1}{d}
, this means that by symmetry, each generalized eigenvalue has algebraic multiplicity
\binom{2d+1}{d}/(2d+1)
.

We will show in the following that the geometric multiplicity is equal to the algebraic multiplicity for points in the generic orbit. This is nontrivial and needs a specific combinatorial argument, see
3.6
. In fact, for non-generic orbits, it is not always true that the algebraic and geometric multiplicity agree. We will see a counterexample in
Section

4
: Already for
d=1
, on the orbit of the W-tensor
b_{1}^{2}b_{2}
, the algebraic multiplicity is
2
and the geometric multiplicity is
1
.

3.6 Proposition
.

Let
T=e_{1}\cdots e_{2d+1}
and let
\partial=e_{1}+\dots+e_{2d}
. Then
T_{\partial}
has rank
2\binom{2d}{d-1}
.

Note that this proposition shows that the kernel has dimension
\binom{2d+1}{d}-2\binom{2d}{d-1}=\binom{2d+1}{d}/(2d+1)
, proving that the geometric multiplicity is as expected.

The proof requires a well-known fact about Johnson graphs. For positive integers
k<n
, the
Johnson graph

J(n,k)
is the undirected graph with vertices given by subsets of
[n]
of size
k
, with two vertices adjacent if and only if the corresponding subsets intersect in exactly
k-1
elements. Let
A(n,k)
be the adjacency matrix of
J(n,k)
.

3.7 Lemma

(Theorem 9.1.2 in
[
4
]
)
.

The eigenvalues of
A(n,k)
are given by
\alpha_{n,k}(j):=(k-j)(n-k-j)-j
with multiplicity
\binom{n}{j}-\binom{n}{j-1}
for
j=0,\dots,\min\{k,n-k\}
.

Now we can prove the proposition.

Proof of Proposition
3.6
.

For a subset
S\subseteq[2d+1]
we use the shorthand
e_{S}:=\prod_{i\in S}e_{i}
. Also, we denote by
e_{1}\cdots e_{\hat{i}}\cdots e_{2d}
the product where index
i
is omitted. Note that

(4)

\displaystyle T_{\partial}

\displaystyle=e_{2d+1}(\sum_{i=1}^{2d}e_{1}\cdots e_{\hat{i}}\cdots e_{2d})

\displaystyle=\binom{2d}{d}^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{2d}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subseteq[2d+1]\setminus\{i\}\\
|S|=d\end{subarray}}e_{S}\otimes e_{[2d+1]\setminus(S\cup\{i\})}

\displaystyle=\binom{2d}{d}^{-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subseteq[2d+1]\\
|S|=d\end{subarray}}e_{S}\otimes\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\notin S\cup\{2d+1\}\end{subarray}}e_{[2d+1]\setminus(S\cup\{i\})}\right).

As mentioned in
Remark

3.2
, the tensors
e_{S}
are linearly independent, so the rank of
T_{\partial}
is equal to the dimension of the subspace

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}:=\bigg\langle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\notin S\cup\{2d+1\}\end{subarray}}e_{[2d+1]\setminus(S\cup\{i\})}:S\subseteq[2d+1],|S|=d\bigg\rangle.

Note that we can split by whether
S
contains
d+1
and we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}=\>

\displaystyle\bigg\langle\sum_{i\in Q}e_{Q\setminus\{i\}}:Q\subseteq[2d],|Q|=d+1\bigg\rangle

\displaystyle\oplus\>

\displaystyle\bigg\langle\sum_{i\in R}e_{(R\cup\{2d+1\})\setminus\{i\}}:R\subseteq[2d],|R|=d\bigg\rangle

We denote these two spaces by
\mathcal{Q}
and
\mathcal{R}
, respectively. Note that
\mathcal{Q}
and
\mathcal{R}
are indeed in direct sum, as they are supported on disjoint sets of monomials. To complete the proof, we will show that
\dim(\mathcal{Q})=\dim(\mathcal{R})=\binom{2d}{d-1}
. We count the dimensions of these two vector spaces separately.

3.8 Claim
.

\dim(\mathcal{Q})=\binom{2d}{d-1}
.

Let
V_{k}:=\langle e_{S}:S\subseteq[2d],|S|=k\rangle
for
k\in\{d,d+1\}
, and define a linear map
D:V_{d+1}\rightarrow V_{d}
by
D(e_{Q})=\sum_{i\in Q}e_{Q\setminus\{i\}}
. Similarly, define
U:V_{d}\rightarrow V_{d+1}
by
U(e_{T})=\sum_{i\notin T}e_{T\cup\{i\}}
. Note that
\mathcal{Q}=D(V_{d+1})
. Since
\dim(V_{d+1})=\binom{2d}{d-1}
, it suffices to prove that
D
is injective. We will do this by proving that
U\circ D:V_{d+1}\rightarrow V_{d+1}
is invertible. Note that

\displaystyle UD(e_{Q})=(d+1)e_{Q}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subseteq[2d]\\
|S|=d+1\\
|S\cap Q|=d\end{subarray}}e_{S},

so
UD=(d+1)I+A(2d,d+1)
. Recall that
A(2d,d+1)
denotes the adjacency matrix of the Johnson graph. By Lemma
3.7
, this map has eigenvalues

\displaystyle(d+1)+(d+1-j)(2d-(d+1)-j)-j=(d+1-j)(d-j)

for
j=0,\dots,d-1
. Since these are all nonzero,
UD
is invertible and the claim is proven.

3.9 Claim
.

\dim(\mathcal{R})=\binom{2d}{d-1}
.

To prove the claim, first note that
\mathcal{R}
is isomorphic to the space

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{\prime}:=\bigg\langle\sum_{i\in R}e_{R\setminus\{i\}}:R\subseteq[2d],|R|=d\bigg\rangle,

Define
D^{\prime}:V_{d}\rightarrow V_{d-1}
as
D^{\prime}(e_{R})=\sum_{i\in R}e_{R\setminus\{i\}}
and
U^{\prime}:V_{d-1}\rightarrow V_{d}
as
U^{\prime}(e_{S})=\sum_{i\notin S}e_{S\cup\{i\}}
. Arguing similarly to above, we have that
D^{\prime}U^{\prime}=(d+1)I+A(2d,d-1)
, which has eigenvalues
(d-j)(d+1-j)
for
j=0,\dots,d-1
. Hence,
D^{\prime}U^{\prime}
is invertible, so
U^{\prime}
is injective. Hence,
\dim(\mathcal{R}^{\prime})=\rk(D^{\prime})=\rk(U^{\prime}D^{\prime})
. Arguing similarly, we have
U^{\prime}D^{\prime}=dI+A(2d,d)
, which has eigenvalues
(d-j)(d-j+1)
for
j=0,\dots,d
, and the zero eigenvalue has multiplicity
\binom{2d}{d}-\binom{2d}{d-1}
. This proves that
\rk(U^{\prime}D^{\prime})=\binom{2d}{d-1}
, and completes the proof.
∎

Finally, we need a criterion to group eigenvectors together, which belong to the same Chow rank 1 term. We will use a criterion similar to Step (4) in the algorithm of
Theorem

2.4
, but this time it requires a bit more technical effort to prove. The following Proposition will lay the groundwork for the grouping criterion, by explicitly describing the eigenvectors.

3.10 Proposition
.

Let
T=e_{1}\cdots e_{2d+1}
, let
\partial=e_{1}+\dots+e_{2d}
and
\partial_{d+1}=e_{1}+\dots+e_{2d+1}
, let
y=\prod_{j=1}^{d}(e_{2j}-e_{2j-1})
, let
\mu=e_{1}+\dots+e_{2d-1}+e_{2d+1}
, and let
z=(e_{2d+1}-e_{2d})\prod_{j=1}^{d-1}(e_{2j}-e_{2j-1})
. Then

(5)

\displaystyle T_{\partial}y=0,

and similarly,

(6)

\displaystyle T_{\mu}z=0.

Furthermore,

(7)

\displaystyle z^{T}T_{\partial_{2d+1}}y\neq 0.

Proof.

Denote by
\sigma(R)
the number of odd elements in a set
R
. Note that

\displaystyle y=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}R\subseteq[2d],\>|R|=d\\
\forall j\in[d]\colon|R\cap\{2j-1,2j\}|=1\end{subarray}}(-1)^{\sigma(R)}e_{R}.

From here, it is easily calculated that for the contraction of
T
by
y
, we have

\displaystyle(I_{n}^{\otimes d+1}\otimes y^{T})T=\alpha\;e_{2d+1}y

for some non-zero scalar
\alpha\in\mathbb{K}^{\times}
. We first verify (
5
). Note that

\displaystyle T_{\partial}y=\alpha\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}R\subseteq[2d]\\
|R|=d\\
|R\cap\{2j-1,2j\}|=1\forall j\in[d]\end{subarray}}(-1)^{\sigma(R)}w_{R},

where

\displaystyle w_{R}=e_{2d+1}\sum_{i\in R}e_{R\setminus\{i\}}.

Let
S\subseteq[2d]
be a subset of size
d-1
. If
|S\cap\{2j,2j-1\}|=2
for some
j\in[d]
, then none of the
w_{R}
are supported on
S\cup\{2d+1\}
. Otherwise, the only possibility is that
|S\cap\{2j,2j-1\}|=1
for all but one index
j^{\prime}\in[d]
, and for this index
|S\cap\{2j^{\prime},2j^{\prime}-1\}|=0
. In this case, there are only two choices of
R
for which
w_{R}
is supported on
S\cup\{2d+1\}
, namely
R=S\cup\{2j^{\prime}\}
and
R=S\cup\{2j^{\prime}-1\}
. The coefficients of
w_{R}
for these two choices are additive inverses, hence
T_{\partial}y
is not supported on
S\cup\{2d+1\}
. This verifies (
5
). By symmetry, (
6
) also holds.

It remains to verify (
7
). Note that

\displaystyle T_{\partial_{2d+1}}y=\alpha y,

so

\displaystyle z^{T}T_{\partial_{2d+1}}y=\alpha\;z^{T}y=\alpha\;2^{d-1}(e_{2d+1}-e_{2d-1})^{T}(e_{2d}-e_{2d-1})=\alpha\;2^{d-1}\neq 0.

This completes the proof.
∎

Now for the algorithm. Let us write
U_{i}=\langle a_{i,J}:|J|=d\rangle
for the space spanned by the “monomials” of degree
d
in
a_{i}=(a_{i1},\ldots,a_{id})
.

3.11 Theorem
.

Suppose that
T=\sum_{i=1}^{r}a_{i1}\ldots a_{i(2d+1)}
with
\{a_{ik}\}_{i\in[r],k=1,2,3}
a set of points in general position. Denote
U_{i}
as described above. Then, the following algorithm produces the terms
a_{i1}\ldots a_{i(2d+1)}
in time
\mathcal{O}(n^{3d+1})
, supposed that
r\leq\dfrac{\binom{n+d-1}{d}}{\binom{2d+1}{d}}-\binom{2d+1}{d}
and
2d+1\leq n
.

(1)

Sample generic
f,g,\ell\in\mathbb{K}^{n}
and compute the matrices
T_{f},T_{g},T_{\ell}

(2)

By
Theorem

3.3
, the generalized eigenvalue problem
\det(T_{f}-\lambda T_{g})=0
has
(2d+1)r
solutions
\lambda_{ik}
, with eigenspaces
V_{ik}
of dimension
\binom{2d+1}{d}/(2d+1)
, by
3.6
. Represent each eigenspace by a matrix
A_{ik}
, whose columns form a basis for the eigenspace.

(3)

Set
h_{ik}:=f-\lambda_{ik}g
.

(4)

For each matrix
A_{ik}
, find the matrices
A_{i^{\prime}k^{\prime}}
for which
A_{i^{\prime}k^{\prime}}^{T}T_{\ell}A_{ik}\neq 0
. This partitions the matrices into sets
\{A_{i1},\ldots,A_{i(2d+1)}\}
.

(5)

Compute
\im T_{\ell}A_{i1}+\ldots+\im T_{\ell}A_{i(2d+1)}
, which is equal to
U_{i}
.

(6)

Having computed bases for the spaces
U_{i}
, construct matrices
\Pi_{i}
for which
\Pi_{i}|_{U_{i}}=I_{U_{i}}
and
\Pi_{i}|_{U_{j}}=0
for
j\neq i
.

(7)

Compute
a_{ik}^{\prime}=((h_{ik}^{T})^{\otimes d-1}\otimes I_{n})\Pi_{i}((h_{ik}^{T})^{\otimes d+1}\otimes I_{n}^{\otimes d})T
. Each
a_{ik}^{\prime}
will be a multiple of
a_{ik}
.

(8)

For each
i=1,\ldots,r
, pick a general element
x_{i1}\in V_{i1}
and solve the system
T_{\ell}\;x_{i1}=c_{i}(a_{i1}^{\prime}\cdots a_{i(2d+1)}^{\prime})_{\ell}\;x_{i1}
for
c_{i}\in\mathbb{K}
.

(9)

Output
\{c_{i}a_{i1}^{\prime}\cdots a_{i(2d+1)}^{\prime}:i=1,\dots,r\}
.

Proof.

Correctness:

Step (2): Covered by
Theorem

3.3
and
3.6
.

Step (4): It is clear that
A_{i^{\prime}k^{\prime}}^{T}T_{\ell}A_{ik}=0
if
i\neq i^{\prime}
, since the vectors in the image of
A_{ik}
are orthogonal to any vector that is not in
U_{i}
, and those in the image of
A_{i^{\prime}k^{\prime}}
are orthogonal to any vector not in
U_{i^{\prime}}
.
Therefore, it remains to show the converse direction, that is,
A_{ik^{\prime}}^{T}T_{\ell}A_{ik}\neq 0
. By
\mathfrak{S}_{2d+1}
symmetry, it suffices to show this for the case
k=d,k^{\prime}=d+1
. Also by
\GL_{n}
symmetry, we may assume that
(a_{i1},\ldots,a_{i(2d+1)})=(e_{1},\ldots,e_{2d+1})
.
But then, the question corresponds exactly to the setting of
3.10
: Indeed, the eigenspace
V_{i(d+1)}
corresponds to the kernel of the matrix
T_{\partial}
from
3.10
and the eigenspace
V_{id}
corresponds to the kernel of the matrix
T_{\mu}
from
3.10
. (Note that the
T
in
3.10
equals our
M_{i}
).
Now, (
7
) guarantees that there exist eigenvectors, i.e., elements
z\in\im A_{id}
and
y\in\im A_{i(d+1)}
such that
z^{T}T_{\ell}y\neq 0
for the contraction
\ell=e_{1}+\ldots+e_{2d+1}
. In particular,
z^{T}T_{\ell}y
will be nonzero for a generic contraction
\ell
. This shows that
A_{id}^{T}T_{\ell}A_{i(d+1)}\neq 0
.

Step (5): We need to show that
W_{i}:=\im T_{\ell}A_{i1}+\ldots+\im T_{\ell}A_{i(2d+1)}
is indeed equal to
U_{i}
. First, note that the spaces
\im A_{i1},\ldots,\im A_{i(2d+1)}
are eigenspaces of
T_{g}^{-1}T_{f}
to distinct eigenvalues, and must therefore be in direct sum. By
3.6
, the sum of these images has dimension
\binom{2d+1}{d}
. Since
\rk(T_{\ell})
is maximal, it follows that
W_{i}=T_{\ell}(\im A_{i1}+\ldots+\im A_{i(2d+1)})
also has dimension
\binom{2d+1}{d}
.
Furthermore,
\im T_{\ell}=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{r}\im M_{if}
and we have that
\im A_{ik}\subseteq\ker M_{i^{\prime}f}
for each
i^{\prime}\neq i
. Thus, we have
W_{i}\subseteq\im M_{if}\subseteq U_{i}
, and therefore
U_{i}=W_{i}
for dimension reasons.

Step (7): Clearly,
\Pi_{i}((h_{ik}^{T})^{\otimes d+1}\otimes I_{n}^{\otimes d})T=((h_{ik}^{T})^{\otimes d+1}\otimes I_{n}^{\otimes d})M_{i}
, since
\Pi_{i}
annihilates every vector in
U_{i^{\prime}}
, if
i^{\prime}\neq i
, and
\Pi_{i}
acts as the identity on
U_{i}
. Therefore,
a_{ik}^{\prime}=((h_{ik}^{T})^{\otimes 2d}\otimes I_{n})M_{i}
is the
2d
-fold contraction of the Chow rank-1 tensor
M_{i}=a_{i1}\cdots a_{i(2d+1)}
by
h_{ik}
. Recall that
h_{ik}
is orthogonal to
a_{ik}
. Therefore, contracting with
h_{ik}
annihilates every term in the symmetrized tensor product
a_{i1}\cdots a_{i(2d+1)}
where
a_{ik}
is on the left side. Consequentially, if we contract
2d
times by
h_{ik}
, then the only terms that do not get annihilated are those, where
a_{ik}
is on the rightmost side. Each of those clearly yields a multiple of
a_{ik}
. A short calculation shows that the multiple is nonzero, as it is proportional to
\prod_{k^{\prime}\neq k}\langle a_{ik^{\prime}},h\rangle
, and a general point
h_{ik}
in the contraction variety does not lie in the intersection of two irreducible components.

Step (8): Note that
Tx_{i1}=M_{i}x_{i1}
, since
x_{i1}
annihilates all
M_{i^{\prime}}
with
i^{\prime}\neq i
. Since
M_{i}
and
a_{i1}^{\prime}\cdots a_{i(2d+1)}^{\prime}
are nonzero multiples of each other, it suffices to show that they do not vanish after contraction with
x_{i1}
. But if
0=M_{i}x_{i1}=T_{x_{i1}}
, then
0=f^{T}T_{x_{i1}}=T_{f}x_{i1}
. This contradicts the fact that
T_{f}
is general and the eigenvectors
x_{i1}
lie in the image of a general
T_{f}
, hence not in the kernel.

Runtime:
We suppress constants of
d
in the
\mathcal{O}
-notation. The claim is that the total runtime is
\mathcal{O}(n^{3d+1})
, with the dominating part being Step (7). All other steps take time
\mathcal{O}(n^{3d})
or less.

Step (1): Computing the contractions and reshaping them into matrices
T_{f},T_{g},T_{\ell}
takes time
\mathcal{O}(n^{2d+1})
.

Step (2): A generalized eigenvalue problem on matrices of size
n^{d}
can be solved in time
\mathcal{O}(n^{3d})
.

Step (4): Computing the product
T_{\ell}A_{ik}
of an
\mathcal{O}(n^{d})\times\mathcal{O}(n^{d})
matrix with a matrix of size
\mathcal{O}(n^{d})\times\mathcal{O}(1)
takes time
\mathcal{O}(n^{2d})
. Doing this for all eigenvectors takes time
r\mathcal{O}(n^{2d})
. Subsequently, computing the products of
T_{\ell}A_{ik}
with all other
A_{i^{\prime}k^{\prime}}
takes time
\mathcal{O}(n^{d})
for each
i^{\prime}
and
k^{\prime}
, which results in a total time of
\mathcal{O}(r^{2}n^{d}+rn^{2d})
for Step (4). Using
r=\mathcal{O}(n^{d})
, this takes time
\mathcal{O}(n^{3d})
.

Step (5): The matrices
T_{\ell}A_{ik}
have already been computed in Step (4), so there is not much to do here. Joining their columns together yields a basis of
\im T_{\ell}A_{i1}+\ldots+\im T_{\ell}A_{i(2d+1)}
. This can be done in time
\mathcal{O}(r)\leq\mathcal{O}(n^{d})
.

Step (6): We already computed a basis
\mathcal{B}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{r}\mathcal{B}_{i}
of
U_{1}\oplus\ldots\oplus U_{r}
in Step (5), where each
\mathcal{B}_{i}
is a basis of
U_{i}
. The matrices
\Pi_{i}
can be interpolated from a dual basis
\mathcal{B}^{\ast}
to
\mathcal{B}
. The dual basis can be computed by matrix inversion, which on a vector space of dimension
\mathcal{O}(n^{d})
, which takes time
\mathcal{O}(n^{3d})
.

Step (7): Contracting
T
with a vector
h_{ik}
takes time
\mathcal{O}(n^{2d+1})
. All subsequent contractions are dominated in complexity by the first contraction. Thus we obtain the vector
v:=((h_{ik}^{T})^{\otimes d+1}\otimes I_{n}^{\otimes d})T
in time
\mathcal{O}(n^{2d+1})
. Multiplying the size
\mathcal{O}(n^{d})\times\mathcal{O}(n^{d})
matrix
\Pi_{i}
with the vector
v
takes time
\mathcal{O}(n^{2d})
and is thus also bounded by the complexity of the first contraction. All subsequent contractions of
\Pi_{i}v
are of negligible complexity. We need to repeat the procedure for each
i
and
k
, resulting in a complexity of
\mathcal{O}(rn^{2d+1})
. Using
r=\mathcal{O}(n^{d})
, we get a total complexity of
\mathcal{O}(n^{3d+1})
for Step (7).

Step (8): The vectors
T_{\ell}\;x_{i1}
are linear combinations of the columns of
T_{\ell}A_{i1}
, which were computed in Step (4). Therefore, there is not much additional effort to compute them. Contracting a Chow rank
1
matrix by
\ell
takes time
\mathcal{O}(n)
, if the rank decomposition is known. The matrix vector multiplication of
(a_{i1}^{\prime}\cdots a_{i(2d+1)}^{\prime})_{\ell}
with
x_{i1}
takes time
\mathcal{O}(n^{2d})
. Thus doing Step (8) for each
i=1,\ldots,r
, takes at most time
\mathcal{O}(rn^{2d})\leq\mathcal{O}(n^{3d})
.
∎

4.
Third order Chow decompositions into non-generic orbits

Let
T\in S^{3}(\mathbb{K}^{n})
be again a symmetric order-
3
tensor with a Chow decomposition

\displaystyle T=\sum_{i=1}^{r}a_{i1}a_{i2}a_{i3}

Write
U_{i}=\langle a_{i1},a_{i2},a_{i3}\rangle
. In this section, we consider the case where
T
is concise and the
U_{i}
are in direct sum, i.e.
\mathbb{K}^{n}=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{r}U_{i}
.
However, contrary to
Section

2
we no longer assume that the entire
\{a_{ik}\}_{i\in[r],k=1,2,3}
is linearly independent. Thus, linear dependencies are allowed among
a_{i1},a_{i2},a_{i3}
. We call a tensor
direct-sum concise
, if it is concise and it has a minimum Chow rank decomposition where the
U_{i}
are in direct sum.

If linear dependencies are allowed and
n\geq 3
, then each Chow-rank-1 term
a_{i1}a_{i2}a_{i3}
lies in one of
4
distinct
\GL_{n}
-orbits, which are described as follows.

(1)

The orbit
\GL_{n}e_{1}^{\otimes 3}
contains those Chow-rank-1 terms
abc
, where
\dim\langle a,b,c\rangle=1
. It equals the Veronese variety
\nu_{3}(\mathbb{K}^{n})
.

(2)

The generic orbit
\GL_{n}e_{1}e_{2}e_{3}
contains Chow-rank-1 terms
abc
, where all
a,b,c\in\mathbb{K}^{n}
are linearly independent. This is the unique orbit where
\dim\langle a,b,c\rangle=3
.

(3)

The orbit of the
W-tensor

\GL_{n}e_{1}^{2}e_{2}
contains those Chow-rank-1 terms
abc
, where either
a
and
b
, or
b
and
c
are collinear (but not both). Here,
\dim\langle a,b,c\rangle=2
. The orbit closure is the tangential variety to the Veronese variety
\nu_{3}(\mathbb{K}^{n})
.

(4)

The orbit
\GL_{n}e_{1}e_{2}(e_{1}+e_{2})
contains those Chow-rank-1 terms
abc
, where
\dim\langle a,b,c\rangle=2
but each pair of two vectors from
a,b,c
is linearly independent. Over
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{C}
, it equals the orbit of
e_{1}^{3}+e_{2}^{3}
, since the bivariate polynomial
x^{3}+y^{3}=(x+y)(x+\zeta y)(x+\zeta^{2}y)
factors into a product of three linearly dependent linear forms, where
\zeta\in\mathbb{C}
denotes a primitive third root of unity.

Note that the complex factorization in case (IV) implies that the real and the complex case need different treatments. Indeed, over
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{C}
, the closure of orbit (IV) contains the rank-2 secant variety
\sigma_{2}(\nu_{3}(\mathbb{C}^{n}))
of the Veronese variety. This means that already a sum of two general elements of orbit (IV) will not have a unique decomposition.

1

This is a special case of the fact that proper secant varieties can never be identifiable. Precisely, if
X
is any variety and
Y=\sigma_{2}(X)
, then
Y
is not
2

X
is
4
-identifiable). Cf. also the example in
[
17
, Remark 3.10]
.
One may look at the example
T=e_{1}^{3}+e_{2}^{3}+e_{3}^{3}+e_{4}^{3}
, which has Chow rank 2.

The Chow rank of
T
is at most
2
by construction, and at least 2 because the flattening rank is
4

The tensor
T
is non-identifiable, as it can be written both as
T=(e_{1}^{3}+e_{2}^{3})+(e_{3}^{3}+e_{4}^{3})
and as
T=(e_{1}^{3}+e_{3}^{3})+(e_{2}^{3}+e_{4}^{3})

\displaystyle T

\displaystyle=(e_{1}+e_{2})(e_{1}+\zeta e_{2})(e_{1}+{\zeta}^{2}e_{2})+(e_{3}+e_{4})(e_{3}+\zeta e_{4})(e_{3}+\zeta^{2}e_{4}),

In addition, note that over
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{C}
, each point in the type (IV) orbit can be written as a sum of two points in the type I orbit. However, this is not a problem when considering the minimum rank decomposition. Note that if there is an odd number of type (I) orbits, then an arbitrary type (I) orbit need not be grouped into a pair. We now formulate the different objectives for
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{C}
and
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{R}
.

4.1 Question

(Real)
.

Given a symmetric, direct-sum concise tensor
T\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n\times n}
of Chow rank
n
, find the unique minimum Chow rank decomposition.

4.2 Question

(Complex)
.

Given a symmetric, direct-sum concise tensor
T\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n\times n}
of Chow rank
n
, find a minimum Chow rank decomposition, and show uniqueness up to grouping pairs of type (I) orbits into type (IV) orbits.

Note that in the real case, it is possible to distinguish between a sum of two type (I) terms and a type (IV) term. A sum of two real type (I) terms will be of the form
a^{3}+b^{3}
with real
a,b\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, whereas a real type (IV) term can be written uniquely in the form
z^{3}+\bar{z}^{3}
. The pairing into complex conjugates will ensure uniqueness of the minimum rank decomposition. Let us quickly prove this fact.

4.3 Lemma
.

A real Chow rank one tensor of type (IV) can be written uniquely as
x^{3}+y^{3}
for vectors
x,y\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
. It furthermore holds that
x\notin\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and
y=\overline{x}
.

Proof.

Let
a,b,c\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
define a type IV term. That is,
a,b,c
are pairwise non-collinear and
c\in\langle a,b\rangle
. Then, after a real change of coordinates
\varphi\in\GL_{n}(\mathbb{R})
, we may assume that
a=e_{1},b=e_{2}
and
c=e_{1}+e_{2}
. Consider the identity

e_{1}e_{2}(e_{1}+e_{2})=\frac{1}{3(\zeta^{2}-\zeta)}\Big[(e_{1}-\zeta e_{2})^{\otimes 3}-(e_{1}-\zeta^{2}e_{2})^{\otimes 3}\Big],

where
\zeta\in\mathbb{C}
is a primitive third root of unity. This shows that
e_{1}e_{2}(e_{1}+e_{2})=z^{3}+\bar{z}^{3}
for
z=e_{1}-\zeta e_{2}
. Note that
\zeta^{2}=\bar{\zeta}
.
After a reversing the real linear change of coordinates,
abc=(\varphi^{-1}(z))^{\otimes 3}+(\overline{\varphi^{-1}(z)})^{\otimes 3}
is still of the desired form. By construction,
\varphi^{-1}(z)\notin\mathbb{R}^{n}
, because
abc
does not have Waring rank 1.
∎

Prony’s method and our main algorithm can be used to decompose symmetric tensors into class (I) and (II) orbits, respectively. We first present an algorithm for decomposing a tensor into class (III) orbits (W-tensors), and then describe how the three different algorithms for classes (I-III) can be combined into a single Chow decomposition algorithm.

Decomposition into W-tensors

Let
T=\sum_{i=1}^{r}a_{i1}^{2}a_{i2}
. In this subsection we give an
\mathcal{O}(n^{4})
-time algorithm for recovering these Chow rank 1 terms when the spaces
U_{i}:=\langle a_{i1},a_{i2}\rangle
are in direct sum. We first determine the contraction variety.

4.4 Proposition
.

It holds that
X_{T}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{r}\langle a_{i1}\rangle^{\perp}
.

Proof.

Let
M_{i}=a_{i1}^{2}a_{i2}
. For
f\in\mathbb{K}^{n}
, we may write
T_{f}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}M_{if}
, where
M_{if}
is the contraction of
a_{i1}^{2}a_{i2}
along
f^{T}
. Note that for generic
f
,
\im M_{if}=U_{i}
for all
i
. Note also that
\text{rank}(T_{f})=2r
for generic
f\in\mathbb{K}^{n}
, and the rank of
T_{f}
drops (i.e.,
f
lies in the contraction variety) if and only if
\text{rank}(M_{if})<2
for some
i
. For such
i
, we can assume without loss of generality that
a_{i1}=e_{1}
and
a_{i2}=e_{2}
. Let
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}
be the first two coordinates of
f
. Then

(8)

\displaystyle 3M_{if}=2\alpha_{1}e_{1}e_{2}+\alpha_{2}e_{1}e_{1},

which has rank
\leq 1
if and only if
\alpha_{1}=0
. This completes the proof.
∎

Hence, the contraction variety is a union of
r
distinct hyperplanes. Furthermore, we see that for generic
f
and
g
all of the generalized eigenvalues will have geometric multiplicity 1 (and algebraic multiplicity 2), as these correspond to intersection points of the generic line
f-\lambda g
with the contraction variety.

Our decomposition algorithm now proceeds as follows. First, we choose generic
f,g\in\mathbb{K}^{n}
, and solve the generalized eigenvalue problem

\displaystyle\det(T_{f}-\lambda T_{g})=0.

The solutions
\lambda
are precisely those for which the rank of
M_{i(f-\lambda g)}
is 1 for some
i
. We denote these eigenvalues by
\lambda_{i}
. All are distinct with geometric multiplicity 1.

We let
a_{i1}^{\prime}=T_{f}x_{i1}
, which is a nonzero scalar multiple of
a_{i1}
. Next, we let
b_{i}
be a vector that is orthogonal to
a_{j1}
for all
j\neq i
, and satisfies
\langle b_{i},a_{i1}\rangle=1
. Then
3((b_{i}^{T})^{\otimes 2}\otimes I)T=a_{i2}+2\langle b_{i},a_{i2}\rangle a_{i1}
. This gives a second basis element
v_{i}
for
U_{i}
.

Now, to recover the terms
a_{i1}^{2}a_{i2}
, we let
\Pi_{i}
be the matrix for which
\Pi_{i}|_{U_{i}}=I_{U_{i}}
and
\Pi_{i}|_{U_{j}}=0
for
j\neq i
. Then

\displaystyle\Pi_{i}T_{f}g

\displaystyle=(a_{i1}^{2}a_{i2})_{f}g=((a_{i1}^{\prime})^{2}(\alpha a_{i1}^{\prime}+\beta v_{i}))_{f}g

\displaystyle=\alpha\frac{1}{3}\langle f,a_{i1}^{\prime}\rangle\langle g,a_{i1}^{\prime}\rangle v_{i}

\displaystyle\quad+\beta\frac{1}{3}(\langle f,a_{i1}^{\prime}\rangle\langle g,a_{i1}^{\prime}\rangle v_{i}+(\langle f,a_{i1}^{\prime}\rangle\langle g,v_{i}\rangle+\langle f,v_{i}\rangle\langle g,a_{i1}^{\prime}\rangle)a_{i1}^{\prime})

This gives a linear system of equations for
\alpha
and
\beta
, which uniquely determines
\alpha
and
\beta
because they appear as the coefficients of linearly independent vectors. It takes
\mathcal{O}(n^{3})
time to compute all the matrices
\Pi_{i}
collectively, and
\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
time for each index to solve the linear system for
\alpha
and
\beta
.

We have proven the following theorem:

4.5 Theorem
.

Suppose that
T=\sum_{i=1}^{r}a_{i1}^{2}a_{i2}
with
U_{i}:=\langle a_{i1},a_{i2}\rangle
in direct sum. Then this is the unique minimal decomposition of
T
into W-tensors, and the following algorithm produces the terms
a_{i1}^{2}a_{i2}
:

(1)

Sample generic
f,g,\ell\in\mathbb{K}^{n}
.

(2)

Find the solutions
\lambda_{i},x_{i}
to
\det(T_{f}-\lambda T_{g})=0
(generalized eigenvalues and eigenvectors).

(3)

Let
a_{i1}^{\prime}=T_{f}x_{i1}
, and let
b_{i}
be a vector that is perpendicular to all
a_{j1}^{\prime}
for
j\neq i
, and satisfies
\langle b_{i},a_{i1}\rangle=1
. Compute
v_{i}=((b_{i}^{T})^{\otimes 2}\otimes I)T
and let
U_{i}=\langle a_{i1}^{\prime},v_{i}\rangle
.

(4)

Let
\Pi_{i}
be the matrix for which
\Pi_{i}|_{U_{i}}=I_{U_{i}}
and
\Pi_{i}|_{U_{j}}=0
for
j\neq i
.

(5)

Solve the linear system
\Pi_{i}T_{f}g=((a_{i1}^{\prime})^{2}(\alpha a_{i1}^{\prime}+\beta v_{i}))_{f}g
for
\alpha
and
\beta
. Let
a_{i2}^{\prime}=\alpha a_{i1}^{\prime}+\beta v_{i}
, so that
a_{i1}^{2}a_{i2}=(a_{i1}^{\prime})^{2}a_{i2}^{\prime}
.

(6)

Output
\{(a_{i1}^{\prime})^{2}a_{i2}^{\prime}:i=1,\dots,r\}
.

Runtime

This algorithm has runtime
\mathcal{O}(n^{4})
. Step (1) takes time
\mathcal{O}(n)
. Step (2) takes time
\mathcal{O}(n^{3})
. Step (3) takes time
\mathcal{O}(n^{3})
for each index
i
, hence
\mathcal{O}(n^{4})
total. Steps (4-6) take time
\mathcal{O}(n^{3})
.

Third order Chow decompositions into mixtures of orbits

Prony’s method and our main algorithm decompose symmetric tensors into type (I) and (II) orbits, respectively, and our algorithm in the previous subsection can decompose symmetric tensors into type (III) orbits. In this subsection we show how to combine these algorithms to answer Questions
4.1
(real case) and
4.2
(complex case).

Let
T=\sum_{i=1}^{r}a_{i1}a_{i2}a_{i3}
be a direct-sum-concise tensor. We will present an
\mathcal{O}(n^{4})
-time algorithm in both the real and complex settings.
We first determine the contraction variety by analyzing the four cases separately.

(1)

In this case,
U_{i}=\langle a_{i1}\rangle
and
\text{rank}(M_{if})
drops if and only if
f\in\langle a_{i1}\rangle^{\perp}
.

(2)

In this case,
a_{i1},a_{i2},a_{i3}
are linearly independent, and
\text{rank}(M_{if})
drops if and only if
f\in\langle a_{i1}\rangle^{\perp}\cup\langle a_{i2}\rangle^{\perp}\cup\langle a_{i3}\rangle^{\perp}
. This was proven in
Section

2
.

(3)

In this case, we have
\dim U_{i}=2
and, without loss of generality,
M_{i}=a_{i1}^{2}a_{i2}
, with
a_{i2}\notin\langle a_{i1}\rangle
. In this case,
\text{rank}(M_{if})<2
if and only if
f\in\langle a_{i1}\rangle^{\perp}
. This was proven in
Section

4
.

(4)

In this case, there exist complex linearly independent vectors
v
and
w
for which
M_{i}=v^{3}+w^{3}
. The rank drops if and only if
f\in\langle v\rangle^{\perp}\cup\langle w\rangle^{\perp}
.

So, by intersecting the contraction variety
X_{T}
with a general line, we obtain one eigenvector for each index in cases (I) and (III), three eigenvectors for each index in case (II), and two eigenvectors for each index in case (IV).
Our algorithm proceeds then in two steps:

(1)

Classify, which eigenvectors belong to types (II) and (III), and partition them according to the index
i
.

(2)

For the remaining eigenvectors:

(a)

For
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{R}
: Classify the remaining eigenvectors into types (I) and (IV) and partition them according to the index
i
.

(b)

For
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{C}
: Pair up the eigenvectors arbitrarily (giving rise to type (IV) terms in the decomposition), possibly with one leftover (giving rise to a type (I) term in the decomposition).

(3)

Recover a minimum Chow rank decomposition of
T
.

Step 1: Partition the eigenvectors along
i
for types (II) and (III)

For the first step, we can partition the eigenvectors corresponding to indices in type (II) using a similar trick as in Section
2
:

4.6 Proposition
.

Let
x_{ij},x_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}},x_{i^{\prime\prime}j^{\prime\prime}}
be distinct eigenvectors. Then the three contractions
x_{ij}^{T}T_{\ell}x_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}
,
x_{ij}^{T}T_{\ell}x_{i^{\prime\prime}j^{\prime\prime}}
,
x_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}^{T}T_{\ell}x_{i^{\prime\prime}j^{\prime\prime}}
are all nonzero if and only if
\dim(U_{i})=3
and
i=i^{\prime}=i^{\prime\prime}
.

Proof.

This is proven essentially identically to
2.3
.
∎

For the remaining eigenvectors, we can determine if they belong to case (III) as follows:

4.7 Proposition
.

Let
x_{ij}
be a generalized eigenvector of
(T_{f},T_{g})
, and let
\ell\in\mathbb{K}^{n}
be generic. Then
x_{ij}^{T}T_{\ell}x_{ij}=0
if and only if
i
belongs to case (III).

Proof.

First suppose that
i
belongs to case (III), i.e.
\dim(U_{i})=2
and
a_{i1},a_{i2},a_{i3}
are not pairwise linearly independent. In this case, there is a single generalized eigenvalue
\lambda_{i1}
with geometric multiplicity
1
. Without loss of generality we can assume
M_{i}=e_{1}^{2}e_{2}
. Note that
3!M_{ie_{2}}=e_{1}e_{1}^{T}
. Hence,
M_{if}e_{2}=\langle f,e_{1}\rangle e_{1}
and
M_{ig}e_{2}=\langle g,e_{1}\rangle e_{1}
are both non-zero scalar multiples of
e_{1}
. It follows that
e_{2}
is a generalized eigenvector of
(M_{if},M_{ig})
. Since
T_{x_{ij}}x_{ij}=(M_{i})_{e_{2}}e_{2}=0
, this direction is proven.

Conversely, suppose that
x_{ij}^{T}T_{\ell}x_{ij}=0
for generic
\ell
. Then
T_{x_{ij}}x_{ij}=0
. If
i
belongs to type (I), then
T_{x_{ij}}x_{ij}
is a nonzero scalar multiple of
a_{i1}
, hence nonzero. A similar argument applies for type (IV). The index
i
can also not have type (II) by Proposition
4.6
. Hence,
i
has type (III).
∎

Step 2: Partition the remaining eigenvectors

At this point, only type (I) and (IV) eigenvectors remain to be sorted. Over the real numbers, the eigenvectors
x_{i1},x_{i2}
in a type (IV) orbit will satisfy the property that
T_{x_{i1}}x_{i1}
is a scalar multiple of
\overline{T_{x_{i2}}x_{i2}}
. In this way, we pair up the type (IV) indices uniquely, and classify the remaining indices as type (I).

Over the complex numbers, due to the non-uniqueness described above, an arbitrary pairing of these eigenvectors will correspond to a sum of two type (I) orbits, i.e. a single type (IV) orbit. This arbitrary pairing leaves possibly one eigenvector leftover, which will correspond to a single type (I) orbit. By construction, the decomposition will be unique up to this choice.

Step 3: Recover a minimum Chow rank decomposition of
T
.

This step is almost as straightforward as running the algorithms for each type independently.

(1)

In this case, we can set
a_{i1}^{\prime}
to be any non-zero element of the one-dimensional space
U_{i}
, solve the system
T_{f}x_{i1}=c\langle f,a_{i1}^{\prime}\rangle\langle x_{i1},a_{i1}^{\prime}\rangle a_{i1}^{\prime}
for
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}
, and let
a_{i1}^{3}=\alpha(a_{i1}^{\prime})^{3}
. This takes time
\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
for each index
i
.

(2)

In this case we can recover the term
a_{i1}a_{i2}a_{i3}
identically as in Section
2
. This takes time
\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
for each index
i
.

(3)

In this case, we need a small modification of the W-tensor decomposition algorithm to recover the spaces
U_{i}
. For each index
i
of type (III), let
a_{i1}^{\prime}=T_{f}x_{i}
. Let
b_{i}
be a vector that is orthogonal to all spaces
U_{i}
from cases (I,II, IV), orthogonal to all vectors
a_{j1}^{\prime}
for
j\neq i
, and satisfies
\langle b_{i},a_{j1}^{\prime}\rangle=1
. Then
U_{i}=\langle a_{i1}^{\prime},((b_{i}^{T})^{\otimes 2}\otimes I)T\rangle
. From here, we can recover the components
a_{i1}^{2}a_{i2}
identically as in Section
4
. In this case, it takes
\mathcal{O}(n^{4})
time to construct the spaces
U_{i}
. It takes
\mathcal{O}(n^{3})
time to compute all the matrices
\Pi_{i}
collectively, and
\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
time for each index to solve the linear system for
\alpha
and
\beta
for each
i
, so
\mathcal{O}(n^{3})
time total. Hence, this step takes time
\mathcal{O}(n^{4})
time total.

(4)

In this case, for the two eigenvectors
x,y
we let
v^{\prime}=T_{x}x
, let
w^{\prime}=T_{y}y
, and solve the systems
T_{f}x=\alpha\langle f,v^{\prime}\rangle\langle x,v^{\prime}\rangle v^{\prime}
and
T_{f}y=\beta\langle f,w^{\prime}\rangle\langle x,w^{\prime}\rangle w^{\prime}
for
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{C}
. We then let
v:=\alpha^{1/3}v^{\prime}
and
w:=\beta^{1/3}w^{\prime}
, and determine vectors
a,b,c
for which
v^{3}+w^{3}=abc
. The vectors
a,b,c
are given by
a=v+w
,
b=v+\zeta w
and
c=v+\zeta^{2}w
, where
\zeta
is a primitive third root of unity.
In total, the procedure takes time
\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
for each pair of eigenvectors.

We have proven the following theorem. We omit the step-by-step version for brevity.

4.8 Theorem
.

Let
T=\sum_{i=1}^{r}a_{i1}a_{i2}a_{i3}
be a direct-sum concise symmetric tensor. Then there is an
\mathcal{O}(n^{4})
-time algorithm to recover a minimum Chow rank decomposition of
T
. This decomposition is unique if
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{R}
, and if
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{C}
it is unique up to a choice of grouping pairs of type (I) terms into type (IV) terms.
