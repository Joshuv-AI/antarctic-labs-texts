Self-Employment as a Signal: Career Concerns with Hidden Firm Performance Journal: Working Paper
Georgy Lukyanov, Konstantin Popov, Shubh Lashkery

Abstract
We analyze a dynamic labor market in which a worker with career concerns chooses each period between (i) self-employment that makes output publicly observable and (ii) employment at a firm that pays a flat wage but keeps individual performance hidden from outside observers. Output is binary and the worker is risk averse. The worker values future opportunities through a reputation for talent; firms may be benchmark (myopic) (ignoring the informational content of an application) or equilibrium (updating beliefs from the very act of applying). Three forces shape equilibrium: an insurance–information trade-off, selection by reputation, and inference from application decisions. We show that (i) an absorbing employment region exists in which low-reputation workers strictly prefer the firm’s insurance and optimally cease producing public information; (ii) sufficiently strong reputation triggers self-employment in order to generate public signals and preserve future outside options; and (iii) with equilibrium firms, application choices act as signals that shift hiring thresholds and wages even when in-firm performance remains opaque. Comparative statics deliver sharp, testable predictions for the prevalence of self-employment, the cyclicality of switching, and wage dynamics across markets with different degrees of performance transparency. The framework links classic career-concerns models to contemporary environments in which some tasks generate portable, public histories while firm tasks remain unobserved by the outside market (e.g., open-source contributions, freelancing platforms, or sales roles with standardized public metrics). Our results rationalize recent empirical findings on the value of public performance records and illuminate when opacity inside firms dampens or amplifies reputational incentives.

This setting captures a common choice in modern labor markets: whether to invest in a public track record that travels across jobs, or to accept an opaque wage inside a firm. The trade-off is first-order. Self-employment exposes the worker to risk but creates portable information that improves future outside options; hidden employment insures current consumption but slows market learning. The paper’s contribution is to characterize the dynamic interaction of this insurance–information trade-off with selection into public production and with firms’ inference from applications, delivering sharp comparative statics and testable implications for occupations with portable public histories.

The worker’s problem has a simple structure. Because current public production raises expected utility more for higher-ability workers and because the posted wage is type-independent in a period, the difference between choosing public production and hidden employment is strictly increasing in talent and strictly decreasing in the posted wage. Our first main result is an
absorbing hidden-employment region
. In the benchmark (myopic) case, the posted wage equals the public posterior mean. If a worker below the cutoff takes a hidden job, the public state does not change next period, so the same comparison repeats—hidden employment is absorbing (Proposition

Our second result establishes existence and uniqueness of the equilibrium cutoff when firms infer from applications. Equilibrium posting ties the wage to acceptance: the posted wage equals the truncated-posterior mean of the types who apply, and the cutoff solves the fixed point. Existence and uniqueness follow from the monotonicity in Lemma

Our third result compares equilibrium to the benchmark. Because equilibrium wages price the selected applicant pool, they are (weakly) below benchmark wages at the same public state. Consequently, the equilibrium acceptance cutoff is (weakly) lower (Proposition
5

The framework yields transparent empirical implications. Early in careers—when a single public outcome moves beliefs more—stronger workers should be more willing to generate public signals; later, absorbing hidden employment becomes more prevalent. Transitions into hidden employment should be more common after runs of low public realizations. When employers price the applicant pool, wage dispersion after observed successes and failures should be compressed relative to a myopic benchmark.

Our analysis speaks to facts from modern labor markets. Online platforms and open-source ecosystems provide portable, public records of performance; causal evidence shows that public signals substantially improve workers’ subsequent outcomes

. By allowing workers to choose between public and hidden production technologies, our framework offers a unified explanation for when we should observe self-employment as a reputational strategy and when we should see long spells of opaque employment with limited outside learning.

We connect classic career-concerns models

;
Dewatripont et al. 1999a
;
Dewatripont et al. 1999b
;

to environments with coexisting public and hidden performance. Relative to that literature, we (i) endogenize the

themselves become signals for equilibrium firms. These elements yield predictions for the incidence and timing of switching, wage dynamics across transparency regimes, and the response to changes in risk exposure or output variance.

Our starting point is the dynamic-career-concerns framework in which market beliefs about talent respond to observed performance
(
Holmström 1999
)
. The structure and publicity of information are central to incentives: better information can increase or decrease effort depending on how signals reach the market and how they are aggregated
(
Dewatripont et al. 1999a
;
Dewatripont et al. 1999b
)
. We also draw on
Gibbons and Murphy 1992
, who show that explicit incentive contracts should optimally account for the implicit incentives coming from career concerns. In our setting, the implicit incentives arise predominantly from the worker’s choice of
where
to produce information (publicly vs. opaquely), rather than from an explicit pay-for-performance scheme.

Our analysis is related to dynamic reputation models in which current actions affect future beliefs
(
Board and Meyer-ter-Vehn 2013
, e.g.,)
. We differ by making the
observability of outcomes
endogenous through the employment-mode decision. This generates selection on reputation: relatively strong types choose public production to separate, which amplifies the informativeness of public signals; weak types value insurance and hide. The result is an
absorbing employment region
in which workers optimally stop producing public information—a mechanism that slows social learning even in otherwise transparent markets.

A second thread studies the social value of early-career information and the difficulty firms face in appropriating its benefits.
Terviö 2009
shows how markets can under-invest in talent discovery when the returns to information about novice workers are largely external. Empirically, making performance public on online labor markets or via credible third parties causally improves workers’ subsequent outcomes
(
Pallais 2014
;
Stanton and Thomas 2016
;
Horton 2017
)
. Our theory provides conditions under which workers voluntarily seek such transparency by going solo, and conditions under which they rationally prefer opaque employment.

A large empirical literature documents how employers learn about worker productivity over time and the extent to which that learning is public or private
(
Altonji and Pierret 2001
)
. We contribute a distinct mechanism: when firm performance is not disclosed externally,
the act of applying
for such employment becomes informative to equilibrium employers. This echoes broader insights that markets learn not only from realized outcomes but also from
selection
and
participation
decisions. In equilibrium, wages and hiring thresholds internalize the informational content of applications even if post-hire outputs remain opaque.

Our applications to open-source contributions and freelancing platforms connect to research on reputation systems and public track records
(
Bar-Isaac and Tadelis 2008
)
. In such settings, outside audiences can readily observe some outputs, while many internal tasks remain hidden. The predictions of our model—about switching, absorbing hidden employment for weaker reputations, and wage dynamics across transparency regimes—organize a number of recent empirical facts and suggest new tests, such as how shocks to the visibility of output (e.g., the introduction of new public metrics) shift the incidence of self-employment and the slope of wage–reputation profiles across markets.

Across these strands, our contribution is to endogenize the generation of public information through an employment-mode choice and to analyze how this interacts with firm sophistication in inference. Doing so yields new comparative statics and testable predictions for the distribution of public track records, the clustering of opaque employment spells, and the mapping from reputation to wages when some production is deliberately hidden.

Transparency premia across occupations

Application-based inference

With equilibrium pricing, application choices are informative. Relative to naïve environments (e.g., where HR ignores selection), we predict (i) lower average wages for otherwise identical public states, (ii) fewer mid-type applicants, and (iii) stronger sorting on outside reputations.

Empirical probes

The model yields simple tests: (i) event studies of wage paths after public successes/failures for workers who remain outside versus those who enter opaque employment; (ii) cross-occupation comparisons of the share of opaque spells by public-reputation quantiles; (iii) within-platform evidence that mid-quantile workers are more likely to keep generating public signals when employers are more “equilibrium” (e.g., settings with better screening or more experienced recruiters).

8
Conclusion

This paper studies career concerns when some production is publicly observable and some is opaque. A risk-averse worker chooses each period between self-employment, which generates public signals about talent, and hidden firm employment, which pays a flat wage and produces no public information. Firms post wages either by benchmarking myopically on the public state or, in equilibrium, by inferring from applications and pricing the selected applicant pool. The worker’s problem exhibits single crossing, so behavior is governed by a cutoff policy in the public state.

Three forces organize the results. First, an
insurance–information trade-off
: hidden employment offers insurance but suppresses public learning and future outside options. Second,
selection
: relatively stronger types opt to produce public signals, making observed outcomes more informative. Third,
inference from applications
: equilibrium wage posting discounts for adverse selection in the applicant pool. Together these forces generate an
absorbing hidden-employment region
—a set of public beliefs for which workers optimally accept hidden employment forever—alongside switching back to public production when reputation is sufficiently strong.

We deliver a simple characterization under both posting regimes. In the benchmark (myopic) case, wages equal the public posterior mean and the absorbing region expands with risk aversion and with career length (as single outcomes move beliefs less). Under equilibrium posting, a fixed point links the acceptance cutoff to the truncated-posterior wage; existence and uniqueness follow from monotonicity of the truncated mean and single crossing in the worker’s problem. Pricing sophistication compresses wages relative to the benchmark at a given public state and tightens self-selection, shifting the cutoff down.

The framework yields transparent empirical implications. Early in careers, when public signals move beliefs more, workers are more willing to generate public information; later, absorbing hidden employment becomes more prevalent. After public successes (failures), the benchmark predicts wide wage dispersion, whereas equilibrium posting corrects for winner’s-curse effects through application-based inference, compressing the success–failure gap. Across occupations, environments with portable public histories (e.g., open portfolios, platform ratings, contest leaderboards) should feature stronger selection into public production by higher-ability workers and sharper wage responses to public outcomes.
20
20

20

Examples include code and data science (open repositories), academic research (working papers and preprints), design (public portfolios), and online freelancing (platform ratings).

We showed that the main insights are robust. Allowing the worker to learn about own type preserves the cutoff structure (in private beliefs) and the absorbing logic because hidden employment freezes the public state. Introducing partial public observability inside firms shrinks, but does not eliminate, the absorbing region unless internal signals become nearly as informative as public production. Letting firms offer simple
(w,p)
menus shifts the cutoff but does not overturn the central margin: the entry decision mediates the trade-off between insurance and reputation.

Two design lessons follow. First,
organizational transparency
is a lever: policies that selectively externalize performance (client-visible tasks, standardized public metrics) can substitute for self-employment as a reputational technology,
21
21

21

For instance, making some client KPIs or review summaries public, adopting external code review, or giving structured, verifiable titles that map to outside-market signals.
reducing the absorbing hidden-employment region and speeding market learning. Second,
marketplace curation
(platform labels, verified histories) changes the relative returns to public signals and therefore the equilibrium mix of public versus hidden production.

Naturally, the model is stylized. We abstract from multi-tasking, multi-dimensional ability, strategic project choice, and richer long-term contracts, and we treat firms as short-lived competitors rather than repeat principals. Extending the analysis to relational contracts, internal renegotiation with private performance signals, or general priors beyond the Beta family would sharpen the link to personnel and organizational economics. On the empirical side, the predictions invite structural or event-study tests on settings with observable public tracks and opaque firm spells (e.g., platform markets, open-source ecosystems, or occupations with client-visible KPIs).

By tying a classic career-concerns logic to environments with coexisting public and hidden performance, the paper provides a compact theory of when employment becomes absorbing, how application-based inference reshapes wages, and why the returns to public production are largest early in careers. We hope this framework proves useful for organizing empirical work on modern reputation systems and for guiding organizational choices about transparency and the design of early-career opportunities.
