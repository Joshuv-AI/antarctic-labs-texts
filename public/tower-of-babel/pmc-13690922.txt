Epidemiological study on bovine cystic echinococcosis in an endemic focus of Türkiye: ten-year trends, abattoir surveillance, and molecular characterization
Authors: Ridvan Kirman, Muzaffer Akyuz, Mohammed Mebarek Bia, Ibrahim Balkaya, Esin Guven, Hamza Avcioglu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCystic echinococcosis (CE) is a significant zoonotic infection caused by the larval stage of Echinococcus granulosus sensu lato, and it is a serious public health problem in Türkiye. The study aims to determine the current prevalence of bovine CE in Erzurum, to identify its molecular genotypes, and to analyze long-term trends in infection prevalence. A total of 1,241 slaughtered cattle were examined, and the CE prevalence was 24.09% (299/1,241). The lungs were the most frequently infected organ, with a rate of 93.31% (p < 0.05). The study revealed that the highest infection rates were observed in cattle over 5 years of age and in females (p < 0.05). The vast majority of infected cattle exhibited single cysts (73.24%) (p < 0.05), with cyst diameters ranging from 1 to 15 cm. Medium-sized cysts (2–5 cm) were predominant in 73.06% of cases (p < 0.05). Microscopic examinations revealed that the fertility rate of cysts was 9.25%. Cyst fertility significantly increased with cyst size (p < 0.05). A molecular analysis of 105 selected cyst samples targeting the 12 S rRNA and COI gene regions identified all isolates as E. granulosus sensu stricto. The epidemiological data from the last thirty years demonstrate a decrease in the prevalence rate of approximately 10% per decade in the region, with a decline from 46.4% to 24.09%. Consequently, in order to maintain the current downward trend in prevalence observed in CE endemic regions and to minimize public health risks, it is crucial to implement comprehensive control programs based on a ‘One Health’ approach and to continue periodic epidemiological studies to monitor the parasite’s status in hosts.Keywords: Cystic echinococcosis, E. granulosus s.l., Cattle, Erzurum, Molecular characterization, Türkiye

Introduction

Cystic echinococcosis (CE), caused by the larval stage of Echinococcus granulosus sensu lato (E. granulosus s.l.), is a significant zoonotic disease transmitted by carnivores (Eckert and Thompson 2017). The parasite’s life cycle requires two mammalian hosts: a carnivorous definitive host that harbors the adult tapeworm and an herbivorous intermediate host in which the metacestode (larval form) develops. Although the parasite causes infections in humans that are accompanied by severe clinical symptoms, humans generally serve as dead-end hosts for this parasite (Romig et al. 2017). Cystic echinococcosis heavily impacts global public health, particularly in regions with intensive livestock farming. In the context of livestock, the disease poses a variety of threats that impact both animal health and the livestock economy. The disease can lead to productivity losses in infected animals, such as slowed weight gain, reduced milk production, and decreased fertility. The most substantial and quantifiable consequence of this phenomenon is the direct financial loss resulting from the destruction of infected livers and lungs in slaughterhouses (Budke et al. 2006; Merino et al. 2017).

The disease has been detected in cattle on all continents except Antarctica, with its prevalence being particularly notable in regions such as South America, the Mediterranean Basin, Asia, and Africa (Deplazes et al. 2017). The prevalence of CE in cattle worldwide varies significantly, ranging from 0.1% to 80% (Cardona and Carmena 2013; Deplazes et al. 2017). Epidemiological studies conducted across the country have revealed that the infection rate in cattle varies between 3% and 46.4% (Arslan and Umur 1997; Düzlü et al. 2010). Due to its geographical location and traditional livestock farming practices, Türkiye is one of the countries most affected by the disease. In particular, in regions where pasture-based livestock farming is widespread, such as Eastern Anatolia, the prevalence of the disease is reported to be above the national average (Avcioglu et al. 2022).

Echinococcus granulosus s.l. is a species complex comprising cryptic species that differ in morphology, development, host specificity, and pathogenicity (Romig et al. 2017). Recent advancements in molecular biology have led to the classification of E. granulosus s.l. into five distinct species: E. granulosus sensu stricto (G1 and G3 genotypes), E. equinus (G4), E. ortleppi (G5), E. canadensis (G6, G7, G8, and G10), and E. felidis (Alvarez Rojas et al. 2014; Vuitton et al. 2020).

In endemic regions, periodic prevalence studies of the host population are essential for monitoring the disease, identifying the species and genotypes present, and developing region-specific control programmes (Alvarez Rojas et al. 2014; Craig et al. 2017). Literature data indicates that the highest number of human CE cases in the Türkiye is found in Erzurum province (Altintas 2003, 2008; Altintas et al. 2020; Kurt et al. 2020). Alongside these significant human cases, the prevalence of CE in local animal hosts is also remarkably high (Arslan and Umur 1997; Simsek et al. 2010; Avcioglu et al. 2021, 2022), marking the region as a major hotspot for CE in the country. Therefore, this study aims to determine the current prevalence and cyst characteristics of bovine CE in Erzurum and to identify the molecular genotypes of the isolates. Furthermore, we analyze epidemiological data from the last thirty years to identify long-term trends in infection prevalence within the region.

Materials and methods

Study area

This study was conducted between January 2017 and March 2018 in the Erzurum province (39° 52′ N, 41° 17′ E), Türkiye. Erzurum is the livestock and trade centre of the region, and livestock farming is one of the province’s main sources of income. Sampling was carried out through weekly visits to the province’s main abattoir. This facility was specifically selected as it possesses the highest slaughter capacity in the region and processes livestock from all districts of Erzurum, thereby providing a representative sampling of the regional animal population. Ethical approval for the study was granted by the Atatürk University Animal Research Local Ethics Committee (Approval no: 2015/27).

Sample collection, macroscopic and microscopic examination

A total of 1,241 cattle carcasses were systematically inspected for the presence of hydatid cysts. The examination involved a thorough visual inspection, manual palpation and systematic dissection of the lungs, liver, heart and spleen. Information such as the animals’ ear tag number, gender and infected organ, as well as the number of cysts, was recorded. Age determination in cattle was performed by querying ear tag numbers in the TURKVET system. After counting all the visible cysts, one representative cyst was collected from each infected organ of each animal. If only one organ was affected, one sample was provided; if both organs were affected, two separate samples were provided. These representative cysts were specifically selected from larger ones to ensure sufficient material was available for subsequent fertility and molecular analyses. These collected samples were then transported on ice to the laboratory for processing and molecular characterization. To investigate the effect of cyst size on fertility, size measurements were performed exclusively on solitary cysts, as these are more suitable for precise measurement. These cysts were classified according to size using a caliper, and categorized as small (< 2 cm), medium (2–5 cm) or large (> 5 cm) (Chihai et al. 2016). The determination of fertility was made by the examination of cyst fluid under a light microscope (10x and 40x magnification) for the presence of protoscolices. The viability of the protoscolices was confirmed by the 0.1% eosin exclusion test. Cysts containing viable protoscolices were classified as fertile, while those that were calcified, caseous, or lacked protoscolices were recorded as sterile. Protoscolices and germinal layers from cysts were washed three times in sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) and preserved in 70% ethanol at -20 °C for subsequent molecular analysis.

DNA isolation

Total genomic DNA (gDNA) was extracted from germinal layers or protoscolices using the G-spin Total DNA Extraction Kit (Intron, South Korea), following the manufacturer’s instructions with a modified proteinase K digestion step. In summary, approximately 50 mg of germinal layer were meticulously minced, and the digestion step was prolonged to 12 h at 56 °C with a doubled concentration of proteinase K to guarantee complete lysis. The concentration and purity of the isolated DNA were determined using a spectrophotometer (NanoDrop® One; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Madison, WI). Subsequently, the purified DNA samples were stored in a temperature of -20 °C, pending further Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR) analysis.

PCR amplification and sequencing

A 254-base pair (bp) fragment of the mitochondrial 12 S ribosomal RNA (rRNA) gene was amplified using E. granulosus s.s.-specific primers, Egss1F/R (Dinkel et al. 2004). The PCR reaction was carried out in a 50 µL volume, comprising 2.0 U of Taq DNA polymerase, 1x PCR buffer, 2 mM MgCl2, 0.2 mM dNTPs, and 20 µM of each primer. Samples that initially yielded negative results in the first PCR were subsequently subjected to COI-PCR using JB3/JB4.5 primers (Bowles et al. 1992) to obtain a 444 bp partial fragment. The COI-PCR reaction (50 µL) consisted of 1.25 U of Taq DNA polymerase, 1x PCR buffer, 2.5 mM MgCl2, 0.25 mM dNTPs, and 20 µM of each primer. Confirmed E. granulosus s.l. isolates from our laboratory were utilized as positive controls, while sterile distilled water was employed as a negative control in each run.

Amplification was performed in a Veriti gradient thermal cycler (Applied Biosystems, Carlsbad, CA). The 12 S rRNA PCR conditions comprised 40 cycles of denaturation (94 °C for 30 s), annealing (57 °C for 1 min), and extension (72 °C for 40 s). The COI-PCR conditions comprised an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 35 cycles of 94 °C for 50 s, 53 °C for 50 s, and 72 °C for 50 s, with a final extension at 72 °C for 10 min. The separation of the PCR products was conducted through gel electrophoresis, followed by staining with SYBR Safe (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA). The visualization of the products was facilitated under UV transillumination (Vilber Lourmat, Quantum ST4, France).

The purification of high-quality PCR products was accomplished through the utilization of the PureLink Quick Gel Extraction Kit (Invitrogen). Bidirectional sequencing was performed with an ABI PRISM 310 Genetic Analyzer (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA). The resulting chromatograms were subjected to a visual inspection, followed by a series of edits and alignments using BioEdit software. The assessment of sequence homology was conducted by comparing the data with reference sequences in the GenBank database using the BLAST algorithm. Representative nucleotide sequences obtained in this study were deposited in GenBank under the following accession numbers: MW421608–MW421619 for the 12 S rRNA gene, and MW421873-MW421875 and MW421884-MW421886 for the COI partial regions.

Statistical analyses

The occurrence data of hydatid cysts in cattle samples were subjected to the chi-square (χ²) test to determine associations with age, gender, cyst size, and organ localization at p < 0.05. Fisher’s exact test was used to evaluate the relationships between cyst size and fertility status, as well as between organ localization and fertility status, with a significance level set at p < 0.05. The proportions of all positive cases were presented within a 95% confidence interval. All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (v4.3.2) via the RStudio interface (R Core Team, 2025).

Results

Prevalence and distribution of CE

A total of 1,241 cattle were screened during the study period, with an overall prevalence of CE at 24.09% (299/1,241; 95% CI: 21.76–26.59%). The distribution of infection according to organ involvement, age, and gender is summarized in Table 1. Regarding organ involvement, the lungs were the most frequently affected organ with a rate of 93.31% (279/299; 95% CI: 89.7–95.76%, χ² = 485; p < 0.05). No hydatid cysts were identified in visceral organs other than the lungs and liver. The prevalence of CE significantly increased with age, reaching 45.58% (95% CI: 38.83–52.49%) in the >5 years age group (χ² = 67,66; p < 0.05). Furthermore, the occurrence of infection was significantly associated with gender, with a higher prevalence of 33.79% (95% CI: 30.38–37.37%) observed in females (χ² = 89,28; p < 0.05).

Cyst characteristics

Out of 1,241 inspected cattle, 299 were found to be infected with CE; however, due to operational constraints at the abattoir, sampling could only be performed on 279 of these infected animals. Including those with mixed (lungs and liver) involvement, one cyst was collected from each infected organ, resulting in a total of 292 cyst samples.

The number of cysts per infected animal ranged from one to eleven. The predominant proportion of infected cattle (73.24%, 219/299; 95% CI: 67.78–78.1%) harbored a solitary cyst, while 22.74% (68/299; 95% CI: 18.2–28%) harbored 2–4 cysts (χ2 = 248,51; p < 0.05). High-intensity infections were rare, with sporadic cases exhibiting five (two cattle), seven (two cattle), and eleven (one cattle) cysts, respectively (1.67%, 5/299; CI: 0.62–4.08%).

Out of the 292 collected cyst samples, size measurements were obtained for 219 solitary cysts. The diameters of these cysts ranged from 1 to 15 cm. Small cysts (< 2 cm) were observed in a relatively infrequent manner (10.96%, 24/219; CI: 7.29–16.05%), while the majority of cases (73.06%, 160/219; CI: 66.58–78.71%) exhibited a medium size (2–5 cm). Large cysts (> 5 cm) were rare (15.98%, 35/219; CI: 11.52–21.67%).

Microscopic examination revealed an overall cyst fertility rate of 9.25% (27/292; CI: 6.29–13.31%). When analyzed by organ, the fertility rate was 9.39% (26/277; CI: 6.34 - %13.6%) in pulmonary cysts and 20% (3/15; CI: 5.31–48.63%) in hepatic cysts (p > 0.05). Of the cysts, four were identified as caseous, and two were classified as calcified.

Regarding the effect of cyst size on fertility, none of the small cysts (0/24, 0%; 95%; CI: 0–13.8) were fertile. By contrast, fertility increased with cyst size: 8.12% for medium cysts (13/160; 95%; CI: 4.81–13.4), rising to 40% for large cysts (14/35; 95%; CI: 25.55–56.43) (p < 0.05).

Molecular characterization

Molecular analysis was performed on 105 of the 292 collected cyst samples. Due to budget constraints, molecular analysis was only performed on 105 of the 292 samples. A representative subset of 105 samples was therefore carefully selected based on proportional distribution of organ localization, cyst size and fertility status, to ensure unbiased representation. Of these, 98 (93.3%) isolates were successfully amplified using the E. granulosus s.s. specific 12 S rRNA PCR. The subsequent seven samples, which had initially returned negative results in the PCR, were successfully amplified using COI-specific primers. Collectively, both PCR assays confirmed that all evaluated isolates belonged to the E. granulosus s.s. To further characterize the isolates, all COI-PCR positive samples (n = 7) and selected 12 S-PCR positive isolates (n = 38) with high DNA concentrations and high purity were subjected to sequencing (n = 45 total).

Echinococcus granulosus s.s. Erzurum isolates with accession numbers MW421608-MW421619 (partial 12 S rRNA gene region) displayed 99.6–100% nucleotide identity with each other. A comparison of certain Erzurum isolates (MW421613, MW421615-MW421619) with reference sequences revealed 100% similarity to isolates from Iran (MH395770) and Iraq (LC757382). In contrast, other Erzurum isolates (MW421608-MW421612 and MW421614) showed 100% identity with isolates from Iran (MH395769), Türkiye (MG672196), Estonia (KY766883), Australia (NC044548), Spain (MG672137), China (OR730806), and Portugal (HG975345).

Echinococcus granulosus s.s. Erzurum isolates (MW421873-MW421875 and MW421884–MW421886, partial COI gene region) displayed 99.2%-100% nucleotide identity with each other. The Erzurum isolates (MW421873-MW421875) exhibited 100% identity with sequences from Palestine (KC109658) and Türkiye (MW421879). In contrast, the isolates designated as MW421884-MW421886 showed 100% identity with isolates from Iran (OP185238, OM663710, OR619410, MT073987, MW350099), Portugal (FN646368, HF947574), Türkiye (MG886837, MN732821, MN990735, HM598451), India (LC822528), Brazil (KC660075, KX527915), and Croatia (PV189156).

Discussion

Cystic echinococcosis poses serious public health and economic challenges in Türkiye, particularly in the Eastern Anatolia Region, where livestock farming is a primary source of income. Erzurum province has the highest reported number of human CE cases and is the main endemic focal point in Türkiye (Altintas 2003, 2008; Altıntaş et al. 2020; Kurt et al. 2020). However, despite the high number of human cases, data on the current prevalence in animal hosts is limited. Studies on CE prevalence in cattle in Erzurum are limited to data from Arslan and Umur (1997) and Simsek et al. (2010), which were published thirty and sixteen years ago, respectively. The time that has elapsed since these studies were conducted makes it necessary to re-evaluate the epidemiological situation in the region. This study therefore aimed to comprehensively examine the prevalence of CE in cattle in Erzurum, including organ distribution, cyst characterization, and genetic diversity.

The study determined the overall prevalence of CE in cattle in Erzurum was 24.09%. A review of surveillance data from slaughterhouses across Türkiye reveals that the prevalence of bovine CE ranges from 3% to 46.41% (Arslan and Umur 1997; Umur 2003 cık et al. 2004; Ulutaş Esatgil and Tüzer 2007; Köse and Sevimli Kırca 2008; Yılmaz et al. 2009; Demir and Mor 2010; Düzlü et al. 2010; Simsek et al. 2010; Oğuz and Değer 2013; Acıöz et al. 2008; Kara et al. 2019; Şahin et al. 2024). The observed variation between regions can be attributed to the influence of various geographical, agroecological, cultural and community awareness factors, as well as attitudes towards the dog population. The study period, sample size, animal gender, pasture use, and livestock systems have also been identified as additional contributing factors to these observed variations (Romig et al. 2011; Otero-Abad and Torgerson 2013). The rate documented in our study, 24.09%, aligns with the prevalence data reported from Türkiye. However, it is lower than that reported in a small number of studies (Arslan and Umur 1997; Umur 2003 cık et al. 2004; Köse and Sevimli 2008; Simsek et al. 2010; Oğuz and Değer 2013). It is also significantly higher than rates reported in the central and western parts of the country. This phenomenon can be attributed to conventional livestock farming practices and the substantial presence of stray dogs in rural areas of Erzurum. From a chronological standpoint, the prevalence in Erzurum, which was 46.41% 30 years ago (Arslan and Umur 1997), decreased to 33.9% 13 years later (Simsek et al. 2010), reaching 24.09% today. It is noteworthy that a decrease of approximately 10% is recorded every ten years. In a similar manner, Avcıoğlu et al. (2022) reported a 10% decrease in the prevalence of cystic echinococcosis in sheep every ten years over a 30-year period in the same study area. This finding lends further support to the downward trend that has been observed in cattle. In Türkiye, local municipalities have been responsible for collecting stray dogs, neutering, vaccinating, and treating them with praziquantel for over the past two decades (Avcioglu et al. 2021). Praziquantel applications have been shown to play a critical role in reducing the prevalence of CE in livestock. While a decline in infection rates has been documented among cattle in the region over the past several decades, prevalence remains substantial. One of the primary reasons for this phenomenon is believed to be the reintroduction of treated dogs into their natural habitats. This practice has been identified as a significant risk factor, contributing to the rapid reinfection of the dogs and the continuous contamination of pastures. Moreover, the ongoing presence of conventional risk factors that continue the parasite cycle remains a persistent concern in the region. The parasite’s life cycle is sustained by established habits, including pasture-based livestock farming, uncontrolled slaughtering practices (particularly the sacrificial festival period), and the feeding of infected organs to dogs. The 24.09% prevalence rate documented in this study substantiates the ongoing severity of the threat to human and animal health in the region. This threat is attributable to the inadequacy of parasite control strategies and the insufficient management of livestock practices.

Many studies have reported that the lungs are the most commonly affected organs in cattle (Negash et al. 2013; Moudgil et al. 2021; Aschenborn et al. 2022; Endale and Mathewos 2024; Lucas et al. 2024). In contrast, reports of predominant liver involvement are in the minority (Ady et al. 2012; Kumsa 2019; Arbab et al. 2025). A similar distribution has been observed in studies conducted throughout Türkiye. While the majority of studies (Arslan and Umur 1997; Umur 2003 cık et al. 2004; Ulutaş Esatgil and Tüzer 2007; Yılmaz et al. 2009; Simsek et al. 2010; Oğuz ve Değer 2013) report predominantly lung involvement, a small number of studies (Köse and Sevimli 2008; Düzlü et al. 2010; Macin et al. 2021) have noted a predominance of liver involvement. The lung involvement rate of 93.31% obtained in this study is consistent with the results of previous studies. However, the fact that only 1% of cases were isolated liver infections indicates a distinct pulmonary predominance and more pronounced organ specificity. The high incidence of cysts in the lungs is attributed to the organ’s spongy parenchyma, extensive capillary network, and dense reticuloendothelial cells, whereas the compact tissue of the liver is more resistant (Kebede et al. 2011). Oncospheres that traverse the intestinal mucosa reach the liver via the portal vein and subsequently the lungs via pulmonary circulation. In high-altitude regions such as Erzurum (approximately 2,000 m), physiological adaptations, including increased capillary volume and vasodilation due to low oxygen pressure, may facilitate oncosphere entrapment and subsequent cyst development within the lung parenchyma (Getaw et al. 2010; Lucas et al. 2024). We therefore hypothesise that these altitude-induced pulmonary changes could contribute to the higher incidence of lung involvement compared to liver involvement in cattle in the study area, although further research is needed to validate this.

The population-level dynamics of the CE are dependent upon the fertility rate of the cysts. This rate is associated with the probability of infection of the definitive host by the consumption of cyst-containing intermediate host tissues (Hüttner and Romig 2009; Banda et al. 2020). In cattle, the fertility of cysts is typically low, remaining below 20%. Cyst fertility is regarded as a pivotal epidemiological parameter in evaluating the sustainability of the parasite’s biological cycle and the degree of infection pressure in the affected region (Romig et al. 2017). Furthermore, a low cyst fertility rate may indicate poor adaptation of the parasite to the bovine host (Banda et al. 2020). A comprehensive review of the global literature indicates that fertility rates in cattle exhibit significant variations depending on geographical regions and the prevalence of dominant parasite strains. A substantial proportion of the global findings align with the overall average; for instance, low rates have been documented, including 1.6% in Peru (Lucas et al. 2024), 6.5% in Kenya (Ady et al. 2012), and between 4.3% and 15.9% in Ethiopia (Kumsa 2019; Negash et al. 2013), with an observed rate of 13% in Algeria (El Fegoun et al. 2020). Conversely, fertility rates that significantly exceeded the established literature average have been documented in Namibia (100%, all isolates being G5 strain/bovine strain) (Aschenborn et al. 2022) and in Ethiopia (38.8%, genotypes were not identified) (Endale and Mathewos 2024). The existing literature on the subject is scant, with most sources detailing the cyst fertility status in cattle in Türkiye. A review of the extant literature indicates that several researchers have reported low fertility rates. According to Ulutaş, Esatgil, and Tüzer (2007), the rate was 0.6%. Concurrently, Köse and Sevimli (2008) documented a rate of 5.42%. Conversely, Macin et al. (2021) reported a fertility rate of 90% (54/60) for cattle, which is significantly above the mean fertility rate documented in the extant literature; the cattle isolates in that study were identified as E. granulosus s.s. The fertility rate of 9.25% observed in this study is generally consistent with national and international literature data. Romig et al. (2017) reported that E. ortleppi (G5 strain) achieved high fertility rates in cattle. Echinococcus granulosus s.s. typically produces non-fertile cysts in cattle (Hüttner and Romig 2009; Banda et al. 2020). The majority of isolates identified from all hosts in Erzurum province to date have been reported as E. granulosus s.s. (Simsek et al. 2010; Kurt et al. 2020; Avcioglu et al. 2021, 2022). The low fertility rate observed in present study is associated with the limited capacity of this prevalent genotype in the region to develop fertile cysts in cattle. This finding provides further evidence in support of the hypothesis that the sheep-dog cycle plays a primary role in parasite transmission in the region.

Although cyst fertility is influenced by various factors, such as host species, age and the dominant genotype in the region, the impact of cyst size is of particular interest. Our findings reveal a direct correlation between cyst size and fertility rates: small cysts were completely sterile, whereas fertility increased to 13% in medium-sized cysts and 40% in large cysts. Biologically, this positive trend may reflect the chronological and functional maturity of the metacestode. Small cysts generally exhibit early, immature, or growth-limited stages and lack a fully differentiated germinal layer. Conversely, as cyst diameter increases, it is thought that cellular differentiation within the germinal membrane triggers active protoscolices production and potentially increases fertility (Stoore et al. 2018; Issa and Mero, 2023). This maturation process corroborates the findings of Torgerson et al. (2009), who recorded protoscolices accumulation as a function of cyst volume relative to host age. Similarly, Dalimi et al. (2002) reported variable fertility rates depending on cyst size, and Mathewos et al. (2022) demonstrated a positive correlation between the two parameters. From an epidemiological perspective, this size-dependent fertility suggests that larger cysts pose a significant risk to the continuation of the parasite’s life cycle. While small, sterile or degenerate cysts largely function as ecological dead ends (Chihai et al. 2016), large, anatomically mature cysts containing viable protoscolices pose a critical transmission risk if definitive hosts such as stray dogs gain access to infected offal due to inadequate slaughterhouse practices (Mathewos et al. 2022). In conclusion, investigating the relationship between cyst size and fertility in slaughterhouses provides useful information about local infection pressures, exposure duration and host-parasite adaptation. However, further research is needed to fully elucidate this issue.

In the present study, a solitary cyst was identified in the majority of infected cattle (73.97%), while 2–4 cysts were detected in 24.31% of the animals. Rare presence of multiple cysts in infected organs is consistent with literature data (Mehmood et al. 2020; Lucas et al. 2024). We suggest that the low cyst density observed in the present study can be attributed to the duration of cattle exposure to the parasite and the level of environmental contamination in the pastures. This variation in the number of cysts per organ is hypothesized to be attributable to factors such as the host’s immune response, the quantity of infective eggs to which the animal is exposed throughout its lifespan, and the non-homogeneous distribution of environmental contamination (Ibrahim 2010; Mehmood et al. 2020). Consequently, the cumulative effect of infection pressure in the region, combined with individual host susceptibility and management factors, has shaped the current cyst burden profile.

The increase in parasite prevalence with age in cattle is attributed to continuous exposure to infected eggs and the rate of parasite accumulation in the host (Budke et al. 2006). The findings of this study demonstrate a statistically significant increase in the prevalence of the infection from 14.47% in the < 2-year-old group to 45.58% in the > 5-year-old group (p < 0.05). This observation lends further support to the hypothesis that the infection in cattle in the region exhibits a cumulative nature and constitutes a lifetime risk. The prevalence of infection in female cattle (33.79%) was found to be statistically significantly higher than in males (10.33%) (p < 0.05). This result suggests a robust correlation with livestock management practices in Erzurum. Specifically, raising female dairy cows on pasture for breeding and milk production increases the risk of exposure to parasite eggs in the region. In contrast, male cattle raised for meat are typically slaughtered at a younger age and raised in intensive systems rather than on pastures, reducing the risk of infection.

The molecular characterization of Echinococcus species present in endemic regions is imperative for elucidating parasite transmission dynamics and implementing efficacious public health strategies. Echinococcus granulosus s.s., the most prevalent and clinically pathogenic member of the E. granulosus s.l. complex, is the primary causative agent in over 90% of human CE cases and over 95% of animal infections globally (Cardona and Carmena 2013; Alvarez Rojas et al. 2014; Thompson 2017). Current data from Türkiye also supports this hypothesis; E. granulosus s.s. is reported as the most frequently isolated species in human CE cases (Erdoğan et al. 2017; Orsten et al. 2018; Kurt et al. 2020). Genetic evidence has been presented in previous studies conducted throughout Türkiye for the presence of G1, G3, G4, G5, G6, and G7 genotypes in different animal hosts (Simsek et al. 2010; Cengiz and Gonenc 2020; Avcioglu et al. 2021). In the present study, E. granulosus s.s. was identified as the etiological agent in all sequenced bovine CE cases in the Erzurum region. This finding is in complete parallel with the dominant species reports in both national and international literature. Echinococcus ortleppi (G5, cattle strain) exhibits a low prevalence in cattle worldwide and demonstrates a geographically limited distribution. Although the presence of E. ortleppi in dogs in Erzurum has been previously documented (Avcioglu et al. 2021), the absence of the species in cattle in this study suggests that the G5 infection burden in the region remains negligible for cattle. In summary, the study’s findings indicate the preeminent role of E. granulosus s.s. in the region, underscoring the essential function of sheep and dogs in the parasite’s life cycle within the area. This genotypic dominance is also a key epidemiological factor explaining the high endemicity of human CE cases in Türkiye and the ongoing pressure on public health.

The findings of this study highlight the urgent need for a comprehensive One Health approach to effectively control and prevent CE in Turkey, especially in endemic regions. Although infection rates in animals have decreased, the disease remains prevalent. This reflects the multifaceted transmission cycle that is inextricably linked to animal health, human behavior and environmental factors. Improper slaughtering practices, the inadequate disposal of infected offal at slaughterhouses and the presence of stray dogs acting as definitive hosts continue to contaminate pastures. Therefore, breaking this transmission chain cannot be achieved through isolated veterinary interventions alone. It requires an integrated control strategy combining rigorous slaughterhouse surveillance, management of the stray dog population, public health education and collaborative data-sharing networks among veterinarians, medical doctors and environmental authorities.

A limitation of the study is that, due to the rapid pace of the slaughtering process at the abattoir, cyst samples could only be collected from 273 out of 299 CE-positive cattle; samples could not be collected from the remaining 26. Consequently, the present study was unable to ascertain the fertility, number, and size of cysts in the 26 cattle subjects. A further limitation is that, due to financial constraints, molecular analysis could only be performed on 105 of the 292 cyst samples collected.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the present study was conducted with the objective of ascertaining the current prevalence of bovine CE in Erzurum province, a region in Türkiye that has been identified as a significant endemic focus for the disease. The study revealed a positivity rate of 24.09%. This data is particularly noteworthy, as it indicates that approximately one in four cattle in the region is infected. Our epidemiological data reveal that the prevalence in the region has decreased from 46.4% to 24.09%, recording a decrease of approximately 10% every decade over the last thirty years. Whilst this linear downward trend is indicative of the partial success of the parasite control strategies that have been implemented in the region, the current prevalence rate indicates that the infection pressure remains high. Despite the low fertility rate detected in cattle (9.25%), it appears that cattle act as reservoirs in maintaining infection in the parasite’s life cycle in the region. The molecular identification of all examined isolates as E. granulosus s.s. once again emphasizes the pivotal role of sheep and dogs in the parasite’s life cycle. It is imperative to periodically monitor the disease status in hosts in endemic regions to evaluate the effectiveness of control measures. In order to maintain the downward trend in prevalence that has been observed in the study area every ten years and to minimize public health risks, it is essential to implement a more comprehensive control programme based on a ‘One Health’ approach.
