Polycationic nanopeptide-fused endolysins for the control of Mannheimia haemolytica
Authors: Sidra Moqaddes, Hechao Du, Jieting Lin, Jae Eun Hyun, Mawra Gohar, Alexei Savchenko, Kim Stanford, Brenda Ralston, Tim A. McAllister, Yan Dong Niu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBovine respiratory disease (BRD) remains a major economic and animal health concern in cattle production, exacerbated by rising antimicrobial resistance and limited vaccine efficacy. Endolysins, phage-derived peptidoglycan hydrolases, have emerged as promising alternatives to antibiotics, but their activity against gram-negative bacteria is hindered by the outer membrane (OM) barrier. In this study, we identified five distinct prophage-derived endolysins from Mannheimia haemolytica and successfully expressed two (185 and 587AP2) along with their engineered polycationic nanopeptide (PCNP) fusions (PCNP-185 and PCNP-587AP2). All four recombinant proteins demonstrated muralytic activity, with original endolysins exhibiting higher enzymatic efficiency. However, PCNP-fused variants showed superior antibacterial effects as compared to those without. Especially, PCNP-185 achieved the most consistent multi-log10 reductions of up to 4.5 in bacterial counts across M. haemolytica serotypes, particularly when combined with EDTA which increased permeability of the OM. These findings highlight the synergistic potential of engineered endolysins and EDTA in overcoming the gram-negative barrier. Additionally, selecting an appropriate buffer was found crucial to rule out the effect of buffers on antibacterial activity of endolysins. To our knowledge, this is the first report of engineering and evaluating prophage-derived endolysins against M. haemolytica, underscoring their promise as innovative therapeutics for BRD.Key points• Prophage endolysins are potent antibacterials with engineering potential.• Chelator synergy boosts endolysin efficacy; buffer choice is critical.• Endolysins hold strong promise as next-generation therapeutic agents against multi-drug-resistant M. haemolytica causing bovine respiratory disease. Introduction

Bovine respiratory disease (BRD), frequently associated with multidrug resistant (MDR) bacterial pathogens, is one of the major health concerns with significant impact on feedlot cattle industry in North America (Andrés-Lasheras et al. 2022; Gaudino et al. 2022; Peel 2020). Among the pathogens implicated in BRD, M. haemolytica is of major importance (Abi Younes et al. 2024; Valeris-Chacin et al. 2022; Credille 2020). Among 16 serotypes identified, A1 and A6 are most often recovered from the lungs of symptomatic cattle, whereas serotype A2 is considered a commensal organism and occasionally cause disease. (Hauglund et al. 2015). The primary approaches to control M. haemolytica are vaccination and the use of antibiotics such as phenicols, tetracyclines, and macrolides (O’Connor et al. 2019a, b; Credille 2022). However, vaccines often provide incomplete protection due to antigenic variation among strains and the complex, multifactorial nature of BRD (Richeson and Falkner 2020; Bowland and Shewen 2000). Furthermore, reliance on antibiotics is increasingly challenged by the rapid emergence of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) and growing regulatory and consumer pressure to minimize antibiotic use (Abi Younes et al. 2024; Credille 2020; Stanford et al. 2020). These limitations highlight an urgent need for alternative treatment strategies that can effectively target M. haemolytica while mitigating development of AMR.

Endolysins are enzymes naturally produced by bacteriophages (phages) at the end of lytic cycle to degrade peptidoglycan layer in bacterial cell wall and release from the host (Loessner 2005). They offer several advantages such as (a) unique mode of action–target a particular pathogen with minimal effect on commensal microbiota, (b) target peptidoglycan–development of resistance is less likely without compromising host fitness, and (c) synergistic effect–with other antimicrobial agents (Abdelrahman et al. 2021; Grishin et al 2020). Over the last decade, endolysins have been developed against gram-positive pathogens like Staphylococcal species (Indiani et al. 2019). WHO has recognized several phage-derived endolysins, e.g., exebacase (CF-301) as non-traditional antimicrobials and clinical development of these endolysins has progressed from preclinical studies to advanced human trials (Khan et al 2024; Anastasiou et al. 2019).

Despite their efficacy against gram-positive bacteria, their activity against gram-negative organisms is limited due to the presence of OM, a barrier to accessing the peptidoglycan layer (Loessner 2005). Unlike modular gram-positive endolysins possessing a cell wall–binding domain (CBD) and one or more enzymatically active domain(s) (EAD), gram-negative endolysins are usually globular with a single EAD and no CBD (Sisson et al. 2024a, b). The independent nature of these domains, however, allows for protein engineering by fusing with OM-destabilizing peptides to create engineered constructs with enhanced antibacterial activity (Briers et al. 2014; Wojciechowska 2025). Several engineered gram-negative endolysins, such as SPN1S and Lys68, have demonstrated improved efficacy particularly when combined with OM permeabilizers like EDTA (Chen et al. 2021; Park et al. 2014; Zeth et al. 2014) or polycationic nanopeptides (PCNPs) (Briers et al. 2014). Polycationic nanopeptides are short, positively charged peptides designed to interact with and disrupt the negatively charged cell wall of gram-negative bacteria where the OM blocks the entry of therapeutic enzymes (Carratala et al. 2023) . Briers et al. (2014) demonstrated that engineering endolysins with OM-permeabilizing peptides “Artilysins” enables effective penetration through gram-negative OM and confers potent bactericidal activity against multidrug-resistant pathogens including P. aeruginosa and Acinetobacter baumannii.

Previously, we reported that diverse prophages were inducible from M. haemolytica strains, suggesting that the endolysins encoded by these prophages are functional and capable of lysing the bacterial host (Niu et al. 2015; Hsu et al. 2013). Furthermore, whole-genome sequencing and bioinformatics analysis identified distinct endolysin genes (e.g., P2-like, λ-like, and Mu-like)from these lysogenic phages (Niu et al. 2015). Therefore, we hypothesized that with proper engineering, these prophage-derived endolysins may be effective for controlling M. haemolytica. This study aimed to engineer these novel endolysins with and without PCNP and assess their antimicrobial potential against M. haemolytica strains in the absence and presence of EDTA.

Materials and methods

Bacterial strains and growth conditions

All M. haemolytica isolates (Table 1) included in this study were kindly provided by Agriculture and Agri-food Canada (AAFC) and Alberta Agriculture and Irrigation. For routine growth, M. haemolytica isolates were streaked on tryptic soy agar (TSA; MilliporeSigma, Burlington, MA, USA) supplemented with 5% sheep blood (Oxoid Ltd., Thermo Fisher Scientific, Basingstoke, UK) and incubated for 18–22 h at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. For broth cultures, a single colony was inoculated into freshly prepared brain heart infusion broth (BHIB; MilliporeSigma). Overnight cultures were incubated for 20–24 h at 37 °C with shaking at 180 rpm with 5% CO2. To prepare log-phase cultures, the overnight culture was diluted 1:100 in fresh BHI broth and grown to an optical density of 0.5–0.6 at 600 nm (OD600nm). Escherichia coli DH5α was used for cloning procedures, and E. coli BL21(DE3) was used for protein expression. Both strains were cultured in lysogeny broth (LB; MilliporeSigma) or on LB agar at 37 °C. The pBAD vector (Széliová et al. 2016) carries the ampicillin resistance (bla) gene conferring ampicillin resistance. Therefore, when screening for recombinant plasmids, the medium was supplemented with ampicillin (Bio Basic Inc., Markham, ON, Canada) to a final concentration of 100 µg/mL. The antibiotic pressure ensured that only transformed cells survived and were retained during growth and protein expression (Shilling et al. 2022).

Comparison of endolysin sequence from M. haemolytica

Endolysin sequences of Mannheimia species were downloaded from National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) and annotated based on conserved domains (CDs). Sequence alignment and dendrogram construction were performed using Molecular Evolutionary Genetics Analysis (MEGA 7.0) (Kumar et al. 2016). The phylogeny was based on the neighbor-joining (NJ) method and analyzed using the bootstrap program with a repetition of 1000 times. Protein sequences of endolysins were aligned in Geneious Prime using Clustal Omega, and pairwise amino acid percent identity was calculated with the built-in Percent Identity Matrix tool (Alexyuk et al. 2024).

In silico analysis of PCNP

The physicochemical properties of the 9-amino-acid polycationic nanopeptide (PCNP) sequence were evaluated in silico to support the proposed mechanism of OM interaction. The theoretical isoelectric point (pI), grand average of hydropathicity (GRAVY), and instability index were calculated using the ExPASy ProtParam tool (Gasteiger et al. 2005). The net charge and Boman index were determined using the Antimicrobial Peptide Database (APD3) prediction module (Wang et al. 2016). To predict the three-dimensional conformation and spatial arrangement of the cationic residues, the PCNP amino acid sequence was submitted to the PEP-FOLD 3 de novo peptide structure prediction server (Lamiable et al. 2016) utilizing standard parameters. The resulting highest-probability 3D model was downloaded as a PDB file, and the spatial projection of the nitrogen-rich cationic side chains was visualized and rendered using UCSF ChimeraX version 1.6.1 (Pettersen et al. 2021).

All the endolysin DNA sequences were obtained by gene synthesis in IDT (Integrated DNA Technologies, San Diego, USA). The sequences were then amplified with Platinum™ PCR Super Mix High Fidelity polymerase (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and constructs were purified with the S.N.A.P.™ Gel Purification Kit (Invitrogen-Thermo Fisher Scientific).

The plasmid was extracted using Ez-10 Spin Column Plasmid DNA Minipreps Kit (BBI Life Sciences Corporation, Ontario, Canada). Endolysins 185 (an abbreviation for USDA-ARS-185) and 587AP2 led to fusion with PCNP (KRK KRK KRK; Briers et al. 2014) via linker (AGAGAGAGAGAGAS) to generate PCNP-185 and PCNP-587AP2, respectively. PCNP (KRK KRK KRK) is a synthetic positively charged peptide consisting of nine amino acids with a sequence of lysine-arginine-lysine (KRK) repeated thrice. These amino acids impart a cationic property to this PCNP, enabling it to bind with phosphate groups of negatively charged lipopolysaccharide (LPS). This binding results in charge neutralization and subsequent destabilization of LPS thus promoting the permeability of the gram-negative OM (Carratalá et al. 2023; Rodríguez-Rubio et al. 2016; Briers et al. 2014).

The structure of the fusion peptide (from the N-terminal to the C-terminal) sequentially comprises: PCNP, linker, and the original peptide.

Ligation-independent cloning (LIC) was used to insert the endolysin gene into the pBAD expression vector. The pBAD plasmid was first digested with the blunt-end restriction enzyme SspI (New England Biolabs, MA, USA) and dephosphorylated with calf intestinal alkaline phosphatase (CIAP) to prevent vector self-ligation. The linearized vector was then treated with dGTP and T4 DNA polymerase (Thermo Fisher Scientific) to generate LIC-compatible 5′ overhangs. In parallel, the endolysin PCR product was purified and treated with dCTP and T4 DNA polymerase to produce complementary 5′ overhangs. The treated vector and insert were mixed and incubated at 22 °C for 10 min to allow annealing of the LIC-generated overhangs. In LIC, T4 DNA polymerase is used in the presence of a single dNTP to generate long, complementary 5′ overhangs on both the insert and vector via its 3′ → 5′ exonuclease activity; these extended single-stranded regions anneal stably without the need for a ligase, and the remaining nicks will be repaired in E. coli DH5α after transformation (Stevenson et al. 2013).

The annealed LIC reaction mixture was transformed into E. coli DH5α competent cells and plated on LB agar supplemented with ampicillin (100 µg/mL) for the selection of recombinants. Colonies were screened by colony PCR to confirm insert presence, and plasmids from positive clones were extracted using the E.Z.N.A.® Plasmid Mini Kit (Omega Bio-Tek, Norcross, GA, USA). Sequence accuracy and correct reading frame were verified by Sanger sequencing (University of Calgary). Verified recombinant pBAD–endolysin plasmids were subsequently transformed into E. coli BL21(DE3) cells for protein expression. The primer pairs used in this study are shown in supplementary Table S1.

Recombinant protein expression and purification

Recombinant endolysin expression was performed in E. coli BL21(DE3) using L-arabinose (Fisher Scientific) induction. For small-scale expression, 250 mL of LB medium supplemented with ampicillin (100 µg/mL) was inoculated with an overnight culture of sequence-verified transformants and incubated at 37 °C with shaking at 225 rpm until mid-log phase (OD600nm = 0.5–0.6). Protein expression was induced by adding L-arabinose to a final concentration of 0.2%, followed by incubation at 37 °C and 180 rpm for 2–3 h (Terol et al 2021; Tiemann et al. 2018). Cells were harvested by centrifugation at 4200 × g for 20 min at 4 °C. The resulting pellet was resuspended in 10 mL sterile 1 × phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH = 7.4; Fisher Bioreagents) and centrifuged again under the same conditions.

The washed pellet was resuspended in 20 mL wash buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, 500 mM NaCl, 50 mM imidazole; pH 8.0; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Ontario, Canada) and lysed by sonication (10 s on/15 s off for 5 cycles or until lysate appeared clarified). Cell debris was removed by centrifugation at 6500 × g for 20 min, and the clarified supernatant was filtered through a 0.2-µm low-protein-binding Acrodisc filter (FroggaBio, Concord, ON, Canada). The filtrate was immediately added to 250-µL high-density cobalt agarose beads (GoldBio, St. Louis, MO, USA) pre-washed by centrifugation, one sterile water wash, and two washes with 5 mL wash buffer. Binding was performed by tumbling the mixture for 1 h at 4 °C using an end-to-end rotator. Beads were transferred to a 5-mL gravity flow column (Thermo Fisher Scientific), washed with 20 mL wash buffer (4 × 5 mL), and bound protein was eluted using elution buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, 500 mM NaCl, 500 mM imidazole, pH 8.0; Thermo Fisher Scientific). A 500-µL initial elution was collected, followed by five 300-µL fractions at 5-min intervals. Protein expression and purity were evaluated by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE; Hagiwara 2022) using an 8–16% precast polyacrylamide gel (GenScript, Piscataway, NJ, USA) and a pre-stained molecular weight ladder (10–250 kDa; Thermo Fisher Scientific).

For concentration and buffer exchange, pooled elution fractions were transferred to Amicon Ultra-15 centrifugal filter units (10 kDa MWCO; MilliporeSigma) and centrifuged at 4200 × g for 25 min at 4 °C. The retentate was washed twice with sterile 1 × PBS and recovered into sterile microcentrifuge tubes. Protein concentrations were determined using the Qubit® Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and quantified using a Qubit® 3.0 fluorometer. Purified protein was stored at −20 °C for later use.

Large-scale protein purification was performed using a 1-L culture following the same induction conditions. Cell lysates were loaded onto HisTrap™ HP nickel-affinity columns (Cytiva, Marlborough, MA, USA) connected to an ÄKTA pure™ chromatography system (Cytiva), and purification was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Fractions containing recombinant endolysin were pooled, concentrated, and dialyzed against Dulbecco’s PBS (DPBS; pH = 7.4; Thermo Fisher Scientific) using an Amicon Ultra-15 Centrifugal Filter with 10 kDa MWCO (MilliporeSigma). Peptidoglycan degradation assay

The ability of endolysins to degrade peptidoglycan was evaluated using a muralytic/peptidoglycan degradation assay as described previously (Zampara et al. 2020). To obtain OM permeabilized M. haemolytica, an overnight culture of M. haemolytica 535 was inoculated into fresh BHI broth and grown to mid-log phase (OD600nm = 0.7–0.8; ~ 7 × 108 CFU/mL). Cells were harvested by centrifugation at 4200 × g for 15 min at 4 °C and resuspended in 0.05 M chloroform-saturated Tris-HCl buffer (pH 7.7; Thermo Fisher Scientific). The suspension was incubated at 22 °C with shaking at 180 rpm for 45 min to permeabilize the OM. Following incubation, cells were centrifuged and washed twice with PBS and once with 0.08 M K2HPO4/KH2PO4 buffer (pH 7.3) and then resuspended in the same buffer to a final OD600nm of 1.0.

Endolysin preparations were serially diluted to final concentrations of 167, 133, 100, 67, 33, 25, 17, 13, 10, 6.7, 3.3, and 0 µg/mL. Lysozyme (MilliporeSigma) at equivalent concentrations served as a positive control. For each reaction, 30 µL of diluted enzyme was mixed with 270 µL of permeabilized cell suspension in triplicate wells of a microplate. Kinetic turbidity reduction was measured at 600 nm every 3 min for 3 h using a microplate spectrophotometer under the following conditions: 37 °C, no shaking.

Antibacterial activity assay

The antibacterial activity of endolysin against M. haemolytica was initially evaluated with HEPES buffer following a previously described protocol with minor modifications (Song et al. 2012). Strains MH535, MH330, and MH587 were grown to mid-exponential phase (OD600nm = 0.5–0.6; ~ 108 CFU/mL) and diluted 1:100 in 20 mM HEPES/NaOH buffer (4-hydroxyethylpiperazine ethane sulfonic acid; Fisher Scientific; pH=7.4) to obtain a final density of ~ 106 CFU/mL. One hundred microliters of the diluted bacterial suspension was mixed with 50 µL of endolysin (final concentration: 50 µg/mL) and 50 µL of 20 mM HEPES/NaOH. To evaluate the synergistic effect of EDTA with endolysins, HEPES/NaOH was replaced with 50 µL of 0.5 mM EDTA.

Following incubation at 37 °C for 60 min without shaking, samples were serially diluted (10–1 to 10–4), and 100 µL from each dilution was plated in duplicate onto TSA blood agar. Plates were incubated overnight at 37 °C in 5% CO2, and colonies were enumerated from plates with isolated colonies.

After buffer evaluation, the experiment was repeated by including an additional M. haemolytica strain from serotype 6 (3927A). In this assay, endolysins were used at an equimolar concentration (22 nmol) instead of a fixed mass (50 µg) to ensure comparable molar concentrations among the different enzymes in the reaction mixtures, as using 50 µg would result in different molar concentrations due to variations in molecular weight. Lysozyme (22 nmol) was also included for comparative analysis.

Evaluation of buffers for antibacterial activity assay

HEPES was found to have false positive effect on antibacterial activity assay results. Therefore, different buffers were tested to assess their buffering capacity and to select a buffer that have minimized effect on log₁₀ reduction following endolysin treatment. M. haemolytica was diluted in various buffers including HEPES (5 mM, 10 mM, and 20 mM; pH = 7.4), PBS (pH = 7.4), DPBS (pH = 7.4) and a homemade buffer (H-buffer; 140 mM NaCl, 5 mM KCl, 2.5 mM CaCl2, 1 mM MgSO4,25 mM NaHCO3, 1–2 mM Na2HPO; pH = 7.4) mimicking bovine extracellular conditions (Vulliemin et al. 1983) which was prepared on the basis of established mammalian balanced salt solutions and bovine plasma electrolyte composition. Antibacterial activity of the endolysins was evaluated in all buffers and based on results obtained (supplementary data 1, Fig. S1). DPBS was selected for antibacterial activities of endolysin in the next trial for having minimum effect on antibacterial activity after 1-h incubation time.

Data analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism v10.2 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Significant difference (p < 0.05) between the endolysin treatment groups was analyzed using one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s multiple comparisons test.

Results

Comparison of endolysin sequence from Mannheimia phages

A total of 68 Mannheimia endolysin sequences were found in NCBI. After removing duplicate sequences, 18 unique endolysin sequences remained for characteristic analysis (Table 2). Conserved domain (CD) results indicated that, in addition to the endolysins of several Mannheimia species isolated by USDA-ARS that belong to the carboxypeptidase-LdcB superfamily, the remaining Mannheimia endolysins belong to the lysozyme-like superfamily.

All these Mannheimia phage endolysins have only an EAD and possess no CBD. Based on the analysis of phylogenetic relationship and amino acid identity, the endolysins of Mannheimia phages were further classified into six genotypes (in different colors, supplementary Fig. S2. Multiple sequence alignment of five selected endolysins (535AP1, 535AP2, 587AP2, USDA-ARS-185, and PKL10) revealed significant structural diversity. While PKL10, 535AP1, and 535AP2 shared a highly conserved N-terminal architecture indicating a common catalytic backbone, 587AP2 and USDA-ARS-185 exhibited divergence or truncation in this region. Consistent with endolysin modularity, greater variability was observed across all sequences in the central and C-terminal regions, marked by substantial insertions and deletions that suggest divergence in host specificity or catalytic regulation. Pairwise analysis confirmed that amino acid identity of these endolysins were less than 70% with 535AP1 and 535AP2 being the most similar (69.8% identity), followed by PKL10 with intermediate similarity (~ 50% to both). In addition, 587AP2 shared only 33.3% identity with the 535 variants. USDA-ARS-185 was the most divergent, displaying limited homology to the 535 group (< 28%) and no detectable identity (0%) with 587AP2. This supports the evaluation of a functionally diverse endolysin panel against M. haemolytica. Endolysins derived from USDA-ARS-1296 and USDA-ARS-1388 were excluded from expression studies because they were predicted to belong to the carboxypeptidase family. These enzymes function by removing C-terminal D-alanyl residues from the peptidoglycan stem peptide, an action that causes minimal structural destabilization to the cell wall compared to other endolysin classes (Choi et a1. 2023).

In silico analysis of PCNP

To support the proposed mechanism of outer-membrane permeabilization, in silico physicochemical profiling of the PCNP was conducted using ExPASy ProtParam and APD3. The analysis revealed a highly cationic nature, with a net charge of + 9, a theoretical isoelectric point (pI) of 12.32, and a grand average of hydropathicity of −4.1. Structural projections indicate a dense clustering of positively charged residues (supplementary Fig. S3). These parameters heavily favor strong electrostatic interactions with the polyanionic phosphate groups of the M. haemolytica lipopolysaccharide (LPS) layer.

Discussion

This study investigates the potential of prophage-derived endolysins as a novel therapeutic strategy against M. haemolytica infections. By employing recombinant technology, four endolysins with muralytic and antibacterial activity were expressed and purified, demonstrating their efficacy in degrading peptidoglycan and reducing bacterial viability. Although endolysins have been studied in the treatment of various pathogenic bacteria (like Staphylococcus, Streptococcus, Pseudomonas,and E. coli), this study is the first report on the engineering and evaluation of antibacterial activity of prophage-derived endolysins as a therapeutic approach against M. haemolytica.

The challenge of the expression of recombinant endolysins—being heterogeneous proteins—is protein toxicity in the expression vector resulting in degradation of host and little or no expression of protein (Rosano and Ceccarelli 2014). Protein toxicity is a crucial feature as antibacterial activity is the primary function of phage-derived endolysins. Such toxic proteins can significantly impact the viability of host cells both during transformation and particularly during protein expression. The pBAD is ideal for expressing and optimizing solubility of toxic proteins in an E. coli host with an advantage of tight regulation with araBAD promoter (Terpe 2006). In the absence of arabinose, araBAD promoter exhibits minimal basal expression, reducing background noise and ensuring tight control over gene expression. This feature is advantageous when expressing toxic proteins and helps in endolysin expression in this study. Using the pBAD vector, two original endolysins (185 and 587AP2) and two fused endolysins (PCNP-185 and PCNP-587AP2) were successfully expressed.

The muralytic (peptidoglycan-degrading) activity of the endolysins, expressed in units/µg, demonstrated a characteristic kinetic profile. Chloroform treatment was first applied to permeabilize the OM of M. haemolytica (MH535), thereby allowing the endolysins direct access to the peptidoglycan layer (Khakhum et al. 2016; Briers et al. 2007). Upon enzyme addition, an immediate and linear decline in OD600nm was observed, indicating high catalytic efficiency when substrate availability was abundant. As the reaction progressed, the slope of OD reduction decreased, reflecting a shift toward conditions where enzyme activity became limited either by partial enzyme depletion or by insufficient substrate supply as accessible peptidoglycan became progressively exhausted (Terpe 2006).

Consistent with this kinetic pattern, our experimental results revealed robust muralytic activity for endolysin 185 and 587AP2. Murein hydrolases, including phage endolysins, degrade bacterial peptidoglycan into soluble fragments, and based on the type of bond cleaved, our endolysins appear capable of hydrolyzing the β−1,4-glycosidic linkage between N-acetylglucosamine (NAG) and N-acetylmuramic acid (NAM), consistent with a lysozyme-like catalytic mechanism. In contrast, the engineered variants PCNP-185 and PCNP-587AP2 exhibited reduced muralytic activity compared to their native forms. This decline may result from steric interference or altered domain flexibility caused by the fused PCNP moiety, which could hinder optimal folding or access of the enzymatic activity domain (EAD) to its substrate. Such effects may be mitigated in future designs by optimizing linker length or repositioning the peptide segment at the N- or C-terminus. Notably, these findings agree with the observations of Briers et al. (2014) which emphasize the selection of the variable terminal for fusion and keeping the conserved terminal available for catalytic activity. In the initial proof-of-concept phase, they constructed fourteen combinations by fusion of 7 endolysins with 7 different OM permeabilizers using either a single or double bond at the C-terminus or the N-terminus. Later, 31 combinations of two endolysins OBPgp279 and PVP-SE1gp146 with PCNP were constructed in a similar manner to find a suitable candidate for muralytic activity and antibacterial activity against P. aeruginosa, E. coli, S. Typhimurium, and A. baumannii. Overall, their study concluded that successful development of PCNP-fused endolysins requires systematic optimization of peptide choice, fusion orientation, and linker design, with the best configuration determined empirically for each endolysin scaffold.

It is noteworthy that the reduction in muralytic activity observed after PCNP fusion did not diminish the overall antibacterial activity of the engineered endolysins. A ≥ 3-log10 reduction in viable bacterial counts is equivalent to 99.9% killing and is the accepted benchmark for defining bactericidal activity in endolysin or antimicrobial activity/killing assays (CLSI M26-A. 1999). Both PCNP-185 and PCNP-587AP2 demonstrated markedly enhanced bactericidal activity compared to their native counterparts. Among all, PCNP-185 consistently produced strong antibacterial effects across all M. haemolytica strains representing three serotypes. This improvement likely results from the enhanced membrane-permeabilizing capability of the PCNP moiety, which facilitates more efficient translocation of the enzyme across the gram-negative OM, which is a substantial permeability barrier that restricts the movement of larger biomolecules and limits the access of many antibacterial agents (Maher and Hassan 2023). Overall, the fusion of endolysins with PCNP significantly improved their ability to traverse the outer membrane barrier, particularly when used synergistically with EDTA, leading to enhanced antibacterial activity relative to the native enzymes. This synergistic traversal is mechanically supported by our bioinformatic analysis, which confirms the PCNP possesses the requisite net positive charge (+ 9) and structural conformation to electrostatically neutralize and destabilize the negatively charged LPS, acting as a highly efficient membrane anchor for the endolysin.

Crucially, previous studies have shown that similar standalone polycationic peptides require significantly higher minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs)—typically in the micromolar (μM) range—to achieve cell death (Kaur et al. 2025). This is orders of magnitude higher than the low molarity utilized in our assays (22 nM). Therefore, we postulate that the PCNP in our construct acts strictly as an OM permeabilizer, facilitating access to the peptidoglycan layer, while the observed rapid bactericidal activity is fundamentally driven by the enzymatic cleavage of the endolysin. In addition, the reduced efficacy against strain MH587 raises the question of whether this tolerance is strain-specific or a broader characteristic of the primarily commensal serotype A2 (McCluskey et al. 1994). While the exact mechanism remains unknown, distinct capsular or OM structures in serotype A2 likely impede PCNP-mediated membrane traversal.

In line with previous studies, the specificity and potency of endolysins can be further modulated through protein engineering strategies (Pottie et al. 2024). Beyond PCNP, several transmembrane peptides including tracheal antimicrobial peptides (TAPs), cecropin A, and cell-penetrating peptides (CPPs) have been successfully employed to enhance endolysin delivery across bacterial envelopes (Islam et al. 2022). Engineered endolysins may also be fused to other lysins or protein domains to broaden their host range or improve catalytic efficiency. Studies have reported that engineered variants often exhibit greater lytic activity and wider microbial targets than their natural enzyme counterparts. For instance, Liu et al. (2023) identified three bacteriophage-derived lysins from Campylobacter and evaluated their antibacterial activity against E. coli. To overcome the gram-negative OM barrier, they engineered these lysins by fusing them with colicin translocation and receptor-binding domains, significantly enhancing their bactericidal efficacy, particularly for a TRBK-Cly02 fusion construct against both laboratory and multidrug-resistant E. coli strains. A notable example is the fusion of the P. aeruginosa bacteriocin pyocin S2 (PyS2) which mediates receptor binding and OM translocation with the GN4 lysin to generate the PyS2–GN4 lysocin, effectively delivering the lysin to the periplasm to cleave peptidoglycan (Heselpoth et al. 2019).

Fusion orientation also plays a role in determining the activity of engineered endolysins. Although previous reports indicate that N-terminus fusion often yields more active constructs than C-terminus fusion, several studies have successfully engineered active C-terminus fusions as well (Wang et al. 2018; Ma et al. 2017). Fusion of membrane-active peptides at the N-terminus of endolysins has been shown to enhance outer-membrane permeabilization and bactericidal activity against gram-negative pathogens (Briers et al. 2014). On the other hand, addition of functional domains (including receptor/translocation domains), particularly at the C-terminus, can improve binding and target interaction of engineered lysins (Rodríguez-Rubio et al. 2016). In our design, PCNP was fused to the N-terminus of the endolysins. Conversely, in E. coli phage endolysin Lysep3, 3–12 hydrophobic amino acids were sequentially added to the C-terminus to create hydrophobic-modified endolysins, which exhibited increased antibacterial potency in proportion to the number of added hydrophobic residues (Yan et al. 2019; Lv et al. 2015). These collective observations highlight the flexibility of endolysin engineering and reinforce that fusion domain choice and orientation can be tailored to optimize antibacterial activity.

Additionally, the enhanced antimicrobial activity observed in the presence of EDTA highlights the strong potential of combining endolysins with OM permeabilizers to overcome the intrinsic defenses of gram-negative bacteria. EDTA enhances the activity of gram-negative endolysins by chelating and displacing the stabilizing divalent cations, primarily Ca2⁺ and Mg2⁺, that cross link lipopolysaccharide molecules within the OM, thereby increasing membrane permeability (Briers et al. 2014). In our system, the PCNP alone was insufficient to fully disrupt the OM; rather, it appeared to prime or partially destabilize the membrane, enabling EDTA to complete the permeabilization process and allow the endolysin to reach its peptidoglycan target. The combination of endolysin with EDTA is however feasible only in topical drug formulations (Blasco et al. 2020). Its use for systemic treatment is limited because EDTA can interfere with blood coagulation, even at relatively low concentrations of 1.3 mmol/L (Briers et al. 2011; Triantaphyllopoulos et al. 1955). In our ongoing investigations, we are evaluating the replacement of EDTA with citric acid. Citric acid and citrate salts possess a well-established safety profile and are widely used in pharmaceutical, food, and medical applications (Stops et al. 2006). Citrate compounds are routinely employed in clinical settings, including as anticoagulants in blood collection systems and in various parenteral formulations, demonstrating their compatibility with systemic exposure under controlled conditions (Scaravilli et al. 2018). Recent studies have demonstrated that citric acid can synergize with phage endolysins by destabilizing the OM of gram-negative bacteria through metal ion chelation and pH-mediated permeability changes (Sisson et al. 2024a, b). These findings suggest that citric acid may serve as a mild permeabilizing agent in endolysin-based antimicrobial formulations and therefore warrants investigation as a potential alternative to EDTA in future therapeutic development.

HEPES initially used as a buffer in antibacterial activity is a widely used Good’s buffer, a class of zwitterionic buffering agents developed to maintain physiological pH with minimal biological interference (Good et al. 1966). However, increasing evidence suggests that certain Good’s buffers can influence microbial physiology in a species-dependent manner, and inhibitory responses have been reported under specific experimental conditions (Prakash et al. 2025). In particular, HEPES-containing media have been shown to generate reactive oxygen species such as hydrogen peroxide when exposed to light, potentially contributing to oxidative stress and reduced bacterial viability (Zigler et al. 1985). Given the sensitivity of gram-negative organisms to perturbations in OM stability and permeability, such buffer-associated stress may be especially detrimental to M. haemolytica (Nikaido 2003). Together, these observations suggest that HEPES toxicity may reflect buffer-specific physiological effects rather than acting solely as an inert pH stabilizer.

While 587AP2 originates from a known λ-like phage, the lineage of 185 remains uncharacterized. Therefore, although their structural divergence highlights the evolutionary diversity of M. haemolytica endolysins, correlating specific architectures directly to P2-like versus lambdaλ-like phage families requires further study of a wider phage library.

Beyond structural diversity and strong antibacterial activity, the practical implementation of endolysins in veterinary medicine is an important consideration for their future development. Endolysins have been proposed as alternative antimicrobials for livestock diseases such as bovine mastitis and other bacterial infections affecting dairy cattle, where they may serve as adjuncts or alternatives to conventional antibiotics (Abdelrahman et al. 2021; Zduńczyk and Janowski 2020). They are also being explored as natural antimicrobials for controlling bacterial pathogens in food safety, agriculture, and livestock production systems (Fernández et al. 2018). However, large-scale implementation in veterinary practice requires consideration of safety considerations, production cost, and manufacturing scalability. Recombinant endolysins can be produced using microbial expression systems such as bacteria (E. coli), yeast (Saccharomyces cerevisiae), plant, mammalian, or cell-free systems which provide relatively efficient and scalable platforms for protein production (Cremelie et al. 2024). Nevertheless, as with other protein-based therapeutics, economic feasibility will depend on optimizing fermentation yield, purification processes, and formulation strategies to ensure that production costs remain compatible with the economic constraints of livestock production systems.

In conclusion, the fusion of endolysins with PCNP significantly improved their ability to traverse the OM barrier, particularly when used synergistically with EDTA, leading to enhanced antibacterial activity relative to the native enzymes. These findings underscore the promise of endolysins as innovative therapeutic agents against M. haemolytica, especially in the context of escalating antimicrobial resistance. Despite these encouraging results, the translational application of endolysins remains limited by gaps in pharmacokinetic, immunological, and toxicity data. Therefore, additional studies, including in vitro cell culture models and in vivo animal experiments, will be essential to fully evaluate their therapeutic potential and advance them toward clinical use.
