A comparative evaluation of sufficient dimension reduction and traditional statistical methods for composite biomarker score construction in diagnostic classification
Authors: Hulya Ozen, Ertugrul Colak, Dogukan Ozen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCombining multiple biomarkers into a single diagnostic score can improve disease classification. However, traditional methods such as logistic regression and linear discriminant analysis depend on restrictive distributional assumptions, which can limit their effectiveness when dealing with complex or heterogeneous datasets. To address these limitations, more practical methodological alternatives are required.MethodsThis study investigates the utility of two sufficient dimension reduction (SDR) methods, Minimum Average Variance Estimation (MAVE) and the Outer Product of Gradients (OPG), for constructing composite biomarker scores in binary diagnostic classification. A comprehensive simulation study was conducted under four data-generating scenarios, varying in sample sizes, mean shifts, variance heterogeneity, normal and non-normal distributional forms. SDR based scores were constructed using likelihood ratio statistics under both the central subspace and central mean subspace frameworks. Classification performance was evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). Traditional methods, logistic regression and linear discriminant analysis, were included as benchmarks. To demonstrate practical utility, the methods were applied to the Breast Cancer Wisconsin (Diagnostic) dataset.ResultsIn simulations, SDR methods outperformed traditional approaches consistently in settings with variance heterogeneity and mixture structures, yielding higher AUC values. The performance of SDR methods was less robust where the data had strong skewness, though it remained comparable to that of traditional methods. In the real dataset, SDR methods achieved similar discriminative accuracy to traditional methods while offering more compact and interpretable summaries of biomarker contributions.ConclusionsSDR methods combined with likelihood-based scoring show potential for a relatively robust and interpretable framework in the context of this paper. They are particularly advantageous in settings with complex diagnostic structures, while maintaining competitiveness in well-structured data. These findings support the use of SDR methods as practical tools for combining biomarkers in precision medicine.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12874-025-02747-3.

Introduction

Biomarkers play a critical role in modern medicine by helping detect, monitor, and predict disease. Yet, relying on a single biomarker is often insufficient, especially for complex conditions where symptoms may be unclear or heterogeneous across patients. In recent years, combining multiple biomarkers into a single composite score has emerged as a powerful approach to improve diagnostic accuracy and tailor treatments to individual patients. This strategy is particularly valuable in precision medicine, aiming to capture biological variability and facilitate early disease detection, even before symptoms arise. By integrating information from several markers, composite models can provide better overall accuracy than any single biomarker alone [1–3]. Although composite biomarker models are widely adopted, optimal strategies for combining biomarkers under flexible distributional assumptions remain underexplored.

Traditional statistical methods for combining biomarkers, such as logistic regression and linear discriminant analysis (LDA), have been widely employed due to their simplicity and interpretability [4]. However, these approaches often assume linearity and normality, which may not hold true in complex biological datasets, potentially leading to suboptimal diagnostic performance [5, 6]. To address the limitations of traditional parametric models, a range of statistical methods have been developed to optimally combine multiple biomarkers into a single diagnostic score. The likelihood ratio (LR) approach [1] offers optimal discrimination when the joint distribution of biomarkers is known but relies on strong distributional assumptions that are often unrealistic in clinical data. To overcome these limitations [2], introduced model-free sufficient dimension reduction (SDR) methods such as Sliced Inverse Regression and Sliced Average Variance Estimation, which identify informative linear combinations of biomarkers without requiring full distributional knowledge. Other approaches, like the min–max method [7] and multivariate receiver operating characteristic models [8], aim to optimize diagnostic accuracy by combining a small set of biomarkers through data-driven cutoff selection.

In addition to these statistical methods, machine learning algorithms such as random forests, support vector machines, and artificial neural networks have gained popularity in biomarker studies [9, 10]. While these methods often achieve high predictive accuracy, their complexity limits interpretability, which is critical for clinical decision-making and real-world applicability. The resulting decision functions frequently do not correspond to easily interpretable or clinically actionable linear combinations of biomarkers.

In view of these challenges, there remains a need for methods that balance predictive performance, interpretability, and flexibility with respect to distributional assumptions, particularly in moderate to high-dimensional biomarker settings. In this study, we focus on SDR methods that offer a principled approach to reducing predictor dimensionality while retaining outcome-relevant information. Instead of modeling the full conditional distribution of the outcome, SDR methods seek a low-dimensional projection of predictors such that the response becomes conditionally independent of the original predictors given the projection [11, 12]. Among SDR methods, Minimum Average Variance Estimation (MAVE) [13] and the Outer Product of Gradient (OPG) have gained attention due to their nonparametric nature and ability to capture nonlinear relationships [13–15]. MAVE minimizes the average variance of the response conditional on projected predictors using local linear smoothing, whereas OPG estimates dimension reduction directions through gradients of the regression function. Both methods are well-suited for complex data structures and have shown promising results in simulation and empirical studies [16].

Despite their potential, the use of SDR methods for constructing composite biomarker scores has been very limited. To our knowledge, the only related study is that of Pfeiffer and Bura (2008), who employed linear SDR methods such as SIR and SAVE. However, more recent nonparametric and nonlinear SDR methods like MAVE and OPG have not yet been evaluated in this diagnostic context. This evaluation is particularly relevant because these methods are designed to handle complex data characteristics, such as heteroskedasticity, nonlinear dependencies and mixture distributions, that commonly arise in clinical biomarker analyses and may challenge the assumptions of traditional methods such as logistic regression and LDA. Furthermore, performance of these SDR methods compared to traditional methods such as logistic regression and LDA remains underexplored, particularly in the presence of heterogeneous or non-normal data distributions. Addressing this methodological gap, we conducted a comprehensive simulation study evaluating MAVE and OPG for combining multiple biomarkers into composite scores using the theoretically optimal likelihood ratio framework under diverse data generating scenarios. Additionally, their practical utility was assessed on a clinical breast cancer dataset.

Methods

In this study, we compare several statistical methods for constructing composite biomarker scores, focusing on two SDR methods alongside traditional approaches. To comprehensively evaluate their performance, we designed an extensive simulation study under various data-generating scenarios reflecting realistic biomarker relationships. Additionally, we applied these methods to a real-world clinical dataset to assess their practical utility in disease classification.

Sufficient dimension reduction methods in the study

Sufficient dimension reduction (SDR) methods aim to reduce the dimensionality of predictor variables \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{X}\in\:{\mathbb{R}}^{P}$$\end{document} while preserving their relationship with a response variable Y. The main idea is to identify a lower-dimensional subspace S, often characterized by a matrix \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:B\:\in\:\:{\mathbb{R}}^{pxd}$$\end{document} (where d≪p) such that, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\left.Y\perp\:X\:\right|{B}^{T}X$$\end{document}, meaning Y depends on X only through the linear combinations\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{B}^{T}X.$$\end{document} SDR methods do these transformations without loss of information [2].

Two major subspaces are commonly targeted in SDR. The central dimension reduction subspace (CS), denoted \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{S}_{\left.Y\right|X}$$\end{document}, is the minimal subspace satisfying this conditional independence and captures the full dependence between X and Y. In settings where the primary interest lies in the conditional mean \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:E\left(Y|X\right)$$\end{document}, the goal shifts toward estimating the central mean dimension reduction subspace (CMS). Different SDR methods vary in how they estimate these subspaces [11, 17]. These methods do not require strong parametric assumptions, such as linearity or constant variance, making them particularly appealing for high-dimensional biomarker studies where nonlinearities and complex interactions are common [18].

In this study, we employ two SDR methods, MAVE and OPG, to construct composite biomarker scores while reducing predictor dimensionality. MAVE, introduced by Xia et al. (2002), is a fully nonparametric technique that minimizes the average conditional variance of Y given projected predictors, using local linear smoothing. This adaptive procedure makes MAVE well-suited for capturing nonlinear and interaction effects among biomarkers. OPG estimates effective dimension reduction directions by aggregating outer products of the gradients of the regression function. Eigen-decomposition of the resulting matrix yields the principal directions that preserve the response-relevant information [13–15, 19].

To enhance performance in complex data structures, we adopt hybrid approaches proposed by [16], which combine MAVE and OPG with a double-kernel local linear smoothing framework. Double-kernel smoothing employs distinct kernel settings for the regression surface and its gradient, improving local adaptivity and accuracy in sufficient direction estimation. These enhancements aim to improve the estimation of both CS and CMS in the presence of nonlinear and high-dimensional data structures. In our analysis, both hybrid methods are implemented using the functions provided in the MAVE package in the R programming environment [20].

Combining biomarkers into composite scores

Following the application of SDR methods, the number of the estimated structural dimensions may be greater than one. In such cases, multiple projection directions are obtained, each capturing a distinct aspect of the relationship between the predictors and the response variable. To facilitate effective group separation and enable classification, it becomes necessary to integrate the information from these multiple projections into a single composite score. In this study, we adopt the approach proposed by Pfeiffer and Bura (2008) for constructing such a composite score.

For each combination of SDR method (MAVE or OPG) and target subspace (CS or CMS), the structural dimension was estimated independently, resulting in four separate models. In each case, 10-fold cross-validation was used to determine the optimal dimension \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\widehat{d}}_{cv}$$\end{document} that minimized prediction error. This likelihood-based approach provides an interpretable and probabilistically grounded way to consolidate information from multiple biomarker dimensions. It also facilitates a direct comparison of SDR based scores with scores derived from traditional methods such as logistic regression and LDA, which rely on fixed linear combinations of the original predictors.

Performance evaluation

In the simulation framework, the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) was employed as the metric to quantify classification performance. AUC reflects the probability that a randomly selected observation from the positive class is assigned a higher composite score than a randomly selected observation from the negative class, thus serving as a robust, threshold-independent measure of discrimination. For both SDR methods, MAVE and OPG, the optimal structural dimension \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\widehat{d}}_{cv}$$\end{document} was estimated via 10-fold cross-validation. These estimations were carried out under both the CS and the CMS frameworks. Composite scores were subsequently derived by forming linear combinations of the estimated sufficient predictors, and their discriminatory capacity was evaluated through corresponding AUC values. To assess performance under known structural conditions, AUC values were also calculated for the fixed case of d = 2, corresponding to the true dimensionality assumed during data generation. For comparative purposes, AUC values were likewise calculated for composite scores derived from traditional linear combination approaches, including logistic regression with main effects and LDA.

A Monte Carlo simulation framework was employed to ensure an unbiased and robust evaluation. In each of the 1000 independent simulation iterations, the generated dataset was randomly partitioned into a training set (80%) and a testing set (20%). In all simulation scenarios, data were split into training and test sets using stratified sampling to preserve class proportions. The training set was used to estimate the optimal structural dimension \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\widehat{d}}_{cv}$$\end{document} and derive the corresponding linear combinations of predictors. These estimated transformations were then applied to the test set to compute composite scores, and their discriminatory performance was evaluated using AUC values. This process was repeated across 1000 independent simulation runs to account for variability and ensure robust performance estimates. The simulation study was conducted separately for each of the four data generation scenarios considered. For each scenario, the distribution of AUC values was summarized by reporting both the mean and standard error across the 1000 replications, thereby providing a comprehensive evaluation of model stability and effectiveness.

All computations in this study were performed using the RStudio environment. The analyses utilized several R packages: the caret package [23] for model training and cross-validation; the MAVE package [20] for implementing SDR methods; and the pROC package [24] for calculating the AUC and evaluating classification performance. Simulation codes are given in the Supplementary Material.

Real data application

To evaluate the real-world applicability of the proposed methods, we used the Breast Cancer Wisconsin (Diagnostic) dataset, a widely studied dataset in the medical research community [25]. This dataset was collected to explore whether nuclear characteristics obtained from fine needle aspiration images of breast tissue could help distinguish between benign and malignant tumors. It comprises 569 samples, with 357 benign and 212 malignant cases. Each sample is described by 10 numerical features: mean values of radius, texture, perimeter, area, smoothness, compactness, concavity, concave points, symmetry, and fractal dimension. These features represent various morphological and textural properties of cell nuclei. Since the distributions of the biomarkers were approximately symmetric across groups, no transformation was applied prior to analysis.

To estimate unbiased AUC values for each method, we applied 10-fold cross-validation. In each fold, 57 observations were used (39 benign and 18 malignant). Importantly, for each fold, the optimal structural dimensions for the MAVE and OPG methods were also selected using a nested 10-fold CV. After obtaining the composite scores in each fold, AUC values were calculated on the test set, and their mean and standard deviation were reported across the 10 folds to summarize performance.

In the final step, we used the entire dataset to fit the models using a fixed structural dimension of d = 1 for the SDR methods. This allowed for an examination of the variable contributions (loadings or coefficients) in each method to understand how different features influenced classification and to compare the discriminative patterns across SDR, logistic regression, and LDA approaches.

In Scenario 3, the control group was generated from a multivariate normal distribution, whereas the case group followed a non-normal distribution. Both groups were designed to have equal expected means, centered at zero. To investigate the effects of variance heterogeneity under different sample sizes, the degree of heterogeneity between group variances was systematically increased. The results presented in Table 2 demonstrate that increasing variance heterogeneity led to a consistent improvement in AUC values for the SDR methods, reflecting improved discriminatory performance. It is noteworthy that the performance disparity between SDR methods and traditional linear combination techniques has increased in favor of the former. In contrast, traditional methods did not demonstrate any significant enhancement in classification accuracy under increasing heterogeneity. This improvement may appear counterintuitive given that group means were fixed. However, the increased variance heterogeneity introduces additional discriminative information in the covariance structure, which SDR methods particularly can detect through local adaptation. As a result, these methods achieve higher AUC values even in the absence of mean shifts between groups.

Real data set results

The performance metrics from the 10-fold cross-validation are summarized as follows. The logistic regression and LDA methods both achieved AUC values of 0.983, with standard deviations of 0.017 and 0.016, respectively. For MAVE, the composite score yielded AUCs of 0.938 (0.057) for CS and 0.959 (0.051) for CMS. OPG produced AUCs of 0.940 (0.144) for CS and 0.985 (0.014) for CMS. The relatively high standard deviation observed for the OPG-CS method may indicate instability in subspace estimation across cross-validation folds. This variability could arise from sensitivity to local structures or outliers in the data, especially when the estimated structural dimension is small and class separation is strong. These results demonstrate competitive performance from SDR methods, especially under the CMS framework. Notably, MAVE estimated dimensions as either 1 or 2 across folds, while OPG consistently selected dimension 1. The strong performance of traditional methods may be attributed to the low structural dimensionality and the relatively clear class separation observed in the dataset.

When models were fit on the full dataset with d = 1, we assessed the coefficients assigned to each biomarker. In logistic regression, the strongest positive contributors to malignancy were smoothness (β = 76.432), concave points (β = 66.821), and symmetry (β = 16.278). Conversely, radius (β = −2.049) and fractal dimension (β = −68.337) were negatively associated with malignancy. The LDA model showed a similar trend, with the largest coefficients for concave points (28.53) and smoothness (8.61), followed by symmetry (4.489) and concavity (3.592).

In the SDR models, both MAVE and OPG showed highly similar directions in the CS and CMS subspaces. Discussion

In this study, we explored a practical approach to combining high-dimensional biomarker data into composite diagnostic scores using SDR methods, specifically MAVE and OPG. These methods do not require strong assumptions about the underlying relationship between predictors and outcomes, making them well-suited for complex biomedical data. We paired each method with a likelihood ratio-based scoring strategy and compared their performance to more traditional classifiers, such as logistic regression and LDA. Our goal was to evaluate how well SDR methods can support disease classification, both in controlled simulation settings and with real clinical data, with the aim of assessing their utility in both theoretical and applied diagnostic contexts.

The simulation results demonstrated that SDR based composite scores consistently outperformed traditional methods in scenarios characterized by variance heterogeneity, mixture structures, and non-normal data conditions commonly observed in biomedical studies. This aligns with earlier findings that projections of high-dimensional data tend to approximate normality [21], thereby justifying the use of normal-based likelihood ratios on SDR derived subspaces [2]. However, our results also showed that when the normality assumption is strongly violated, as in Scenario 4 where biomarker distributions followed a log-normal pattern, the LR scoring performance declined. In such settings, the non-normality of the projections leads to less accurate LR estimation, which lowers AUC values and increases variability in the estimated structural dimension. Although SDR methods are generally adaptable to complex data structures, the LR scoring step relies on the approximate normality of the projections. This link between strong skewness and reduced model performance highlights the practical impact of violating the normality assumption. Therefore, applying suitable data transformations may be necessary to maintain reliable diagnostic performance in real-world applications. Beyond these distributional effects, the improved performance of SDR methods under increased variance heterogeneity may initially appear unexpected. However, this behavior reflects the adaptive nature of SDR, particularly MAVE, which estimates sufficient directions through local linear smoothing. As noted by Xia et al. (2002), this adaptivity allows the method to respond to local variance structures and capture discriminative information arising from heteroskedasticity. Consequently, as between group variance differences increase, SDR methods can identify more informative projection directions, enhancing class separation even when group means remain fixed.

Overall, these results indicate that SDR based composite scores are most advantageous when the data exhibit variance heterogeneity or mixture structures, as these are conditions under which traditional methods tend to lose discriminatory power. In contrast, when both groups deviate substantially from normality, particularly under strong skewness or heavy-tailed distributions, SDR based composite scores may underperform traditional methods such as logistic regression. Therefore, preliminary evaluation of key distributional features, including skewness, tail behavior, and variance heterogeneity, can help guide researchers in determining whether SDR based composite scores or traditional methods are more appropriate for a given dataset.

While logistic regression can perform well in ideal, normally distributed settings, as also noted by Pfeiffer and Bura (2008), its effectiveness declines when faced with complex nonlinear relationships or higher-order interactions, unless the model is significantly expanded. However, such expansions often require impractically large sample sizes. In contrast, SDR methods can naturally account for these complex structures without explicitly modeling interactions, offering a more practical and interpretable alternative [2]. Although this theoretical advantage was discussed, our simulation design did not directly include nonlinear or higher-order interaction structures. Future studies could explore how SDR methods perform under these more complex relationships to further evaluate their robustness in real-world biomarker applications.

Although our study did not specifically aim to compare the CS and CMS frameworks, we both included evaluating the overall robustness of SDR methods. The AUC results were consistently similar across all scenarios, indicating that both approaches effectively capture the key discriminative information. This suggests that, in classification focused settings where predictive performance is the priority, CS and CMS can be used interchangeably without compromising accuracy.

Applying our methods to the Breast Cancer Wisconsin dataset showed that, in well-structured and clearly separable data, traditional approaches perform similarly to SDR methods. However, SDR based composite scores achieve almost the same discriminative accuracy while providing simpler, lower dimensional summaries that are easier to interpret. Biomarkers with higher absolute loadings exert stronger influence on disease discrimination, offering clear interpretability comparable to that of regression coefficients. In our analysis, concave points and smoothness exhibited the largest loadings across both SDR methods and subspace frameworks, confirming their dominant role in differentiating malignant from benign cases. This study has several strengths. It unifies two nonparametric SDR methods under a likelihood-based diagnostic framework and evaluates their performance using both theoretical (DSM) and empirical data settings. Moreover, we utilize cross-validation to estimate structural dimensions, providing practical insights into real-data applications.

Limitations

While the findings are promising, this study has certain limitations. First, performance declines in the presence of strong skewness and noise (such as in log-normal), and diagnostic accuracy can be sensitive to misestimating the structural dimension. In particular, we observed that the estimated dimension (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\widehat{d}}_{cv})\:$$\end{document}became less stable under such conditions, suggesting that cross-validation based dimension selection may be affected by data heterogeneity or skewed data conditions. Second, the likelihood ratio–based composite scores depend on parametric assumptions regarding the normality of the projections, which may not hold in all cases. Third, although AUC was selected as the primary evaluation metric due to its interpretability and threshold-independence, other diagnostic measures such as sensitivity, specificity, and F1-score could offer complementary insights, particularly in imbalanced or threshold-sensitive clinical settings. Future studies may incorporate these alternative metrics to provide a more nuanced evaluation of diagnostic performance. Furthermore, this study focused only on binary classification. Future research could extend the framework to multiclass problems or longitudinal biomarker data. Finally, our simulations did not explicitly incorporate nonlinear or high-order interaction structures. Future work could extend the current framework to examine SDR performance under more complex dependency patterns among biomarkers.

Conclusion

The integration of SDR methods with a likelihood ratio–based scoring offers a practical and computationally efficient alternative to traditional methods for combining multiple biomarkers into a single composite score. These methods are particularly advantageous when the discriminative structure lies beyond simple mean shifts and linear separability. As high-dimensional biomarker data become increasingly prevalent in clinical decision-making, SDR based frameworks hold promises for improving diagnostic accuracy while maintaining interpretability and statistical robustness. Future integration of SDR based frameworks into clinical diagnostic pipelines may support more accurate and interpretable decision making in precision medicine.
