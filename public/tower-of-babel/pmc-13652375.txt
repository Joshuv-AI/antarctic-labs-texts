Frozen autograft–prosthesis composite reconstruction with total hip arthroplasty for proximal femoral osteosarcoma: a case report
Authors: Viet Nhuan Ngo, Phuoc Hung Do, Quang Vinh Pham, Hoang Phu Nguyen, Ba Vu Dong Phan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction and importance:Reconstruction after proximal femoral resection requires restoration of both a large osseous defect and stable hip joint function. Frozen autograft–prosthesis composite (FAPC) reconstruction using liquid nitrogen-treated autograft represents a viable biological option for this indication. We present a case of proximal femoral osteosarcoma managed with this technique.Case presentation:A 25-year-old man presented with a 3-month history of atraumatic right hip pain. Imaging and biopsy confirmed Enneking stage IIB high-grade osteosarcoma of the proximal femur. Wide resection resulted in an 18-cm defect, reconstructed using a free, liquid nitrogen-treated autograft combined with total hip arthroplasty (THA) and plate fixation. Nonunion at the distal junction was identified at 8 months and managed with supplementary iliac crest bone grafting at 10 months, achieving complete union at 26 months. At 43 months, there was no evidence of recurrence or metastasis, and the Musculoskeletal Tumor Society (MSTS) score was 90%.Clinical discussion:This case suggests that THA can be incorporated into the FAPC construct in a young, active patient. A trochanteric hook plate with cerclage cables was used to stabilize the construct, avoiding cortical screw fixation into the brittle, recycled bone while enabling reattachment of the abductor mechanism. Distal junction nonunion, a recognized complication of liquid nitrogen freezing, was successfully managed with secondary bone grafting.Conclusion:Combining liquid nitrogen-treated autograft with THA may be a feasible limb-salvage strategy for proximal femoral osteosarcoma, achieving satisfactory oncological and functional outcomes at medium-term follow-up. This approach may be particularly relevant in resource-limited settings.

Among available devitalization methods, liquid nitrogen treatment has been shown to preserve osteoinductive potential and biomechanical strength compared with other techniques[2,3]. Frozen autograft–prosthesis composite (FAPC) reconstruction combining liquid nitrogen-treated autograft with joint replacement has demonstrated favorable outcomes in proximal femoral tumors[4–6].

However, the role of total hip arthroplasty (THA) within the FAPC construct remains poorly defined. We report a case of FAPC reconstruction using a free liquid nitrogen-treated autograft combined with THA following wide resection of proximal femoral osteosarcoma. This case report has been prepared in line with the SCARE 2025 criteria[7].

Case presentation

A 25-year-old male presented with a 3-month history of progressive right hip pain without antecedent trauma. Physical examination revealed localized tenderness over the proximal femur and restricted hip range of motion. Plain radiographs demonstrated an aggressive lesion, and magnetic resonance imaging showed a 12-cm intramedullary tumor with extraosseous extension (Fig. 1). Incisional biopsy confirmed conventional high-grade osteosarcoma. Staging showed no distant metastasis, consistent with Enneking stage IIB disease. Figure 1.Preoperative imaging. Wide resection of the proximal femur was performed in August 2022 via a direct lateral approach, resulting in an 18-cm defect, which included surgical margins beyond the 12-cm tumor boundary. Tumor involvement precluded preservation of the greater trochanter. Key muscular attachments, including the gluteus medius, gluteus minimus, vastus lateralis, and iliopsoas, were tagged to facilitate later reconstruction.

The resected segment underwent gross preparation. Surrounding soft tissue was removed, residual tumor was debrided macroscopically, cortical perforations were created, and the femoral canal was reamed. The segment was immersed in liquid nitrogen for 20 minutes, thawed at room temperature for 15 minutes, and then thawed in distilled water for 10 minutes, according to the protocol of Tsuchiya et al[8]. No fracture of the recycled autograft occurred during liquid nitrogen treatment or femoral canal preparation.

Reconstruction was performed using a recycled autograft combined with uncemented total hip arthroplasty. A standard femoral stem and a press-fit acetabular cup were used. The construct was stabilized using a trochanteric hook plate with cerclage cables, and an ipsilateral iliac crest autograft was applied at the distal osteotomy junction (Fig. 2). Postoperative radiographs confirmed satisfactory implant positioning and stability (Fig. 3A). Due to limited access to chemotherapy agents during the COVID-19 pandemic, neoadjuvant chemotherapy was not administered. Six cycles of adjuvant chemotherapy with doxorubicin and cisplatin were initiated 4 weeks postoperatively, once access was established. At 8 months, radiological evaluation demonstrated nonunion at the distal osteotomy junction (Fig. 3B). The patient underwent a second surgical procedure at 10 months, and an autologous iliac crest bone graft was applied. Serial radiographs showed progressive consolidation, with complete union achieved at 26 months (Fig. 3C). Full weight-bearing was permitted from September 2024.

At the final follow-up at 43 months, the patient was ambulatory without assistive devices and had resumed recreational activities, including swimming and cycling. There was no evidence of local recurrence, implant loosening, autograft fracture, or distant metastasis. The Musculoskeletal Tumor Society (MSTS) score was 90% (27/30). Hip range of motion was satisfactory, and the Trendelenburg sign was negative.

Clinical discussion

Reconstruction after wide resection of proximal femoral osteosarcoma presents three challenges: restoring a large bone defect, reconstructing stable hip joint function, and reattaching key soft-tissue structures. In the present case, an 18-cm proximal femoral defect with hip joint involvement required a single-stage reconstructive solution.

Megaprosthesis is the standard option for proximal femoral reconstruction, providing immediate stability and early functional recovery[1]. However, its long-term durability in young and active patients is limited by aseptic loosening, periprosthetic fracture, and mechanical failure, frequently requiring revision[1].

Allograft–prosthesis composite (APC) offers a biological alternative, with the advantage of soft-tissue reattachment and restoration of bone stock[9]. This option was not feasible in our setting due to the absence of a bone bank. In addition, potential size and shape mismatch between donor allograft and host anatomy may compromise construct stability and increase technical complexity[9]. In this context, FAPC was considered the most appropriate option.

To our knowledge, the use of a standard non-long-stem THA not bypassing the distal osteotomy site has not been previously described in FAPC reconstruction; the novelty lies in pairing this prosthetic configuration with cerclage-based fixation using a trochanteric hook plate[4–6,10]. In the present case, THA was selected due to the patient’s young age and high functional demands. At 43 months of follow-up, there was no evidence of acetabular wear, cup migration, or mechanical failure, with an MSTS score of 90%. These observations suggest that THA may be a viable option within an FAPC construct in young patients.

Adequate fixation of the whole construct is essential for achieving bone graft union. However, the fixation strategy must account for the altered mechanical properties of the recycled autograft. In APC reconstruction, fixation is typically achieved using compression plates with cortical screws placed within the graft[11]. However, direct application of this technique to liquid nitrogen-treated autograft carries a substantial risk of iatrogenic fracture, as the recycled cortical bone becomes brittle and less tolerant to drilling and screw insertion. In FAPC series, a trochanteric hook plate with cerclage cables has therefore been preferred[4,5]. Cerclage-based fixation avoids cortical drilling, reducing this risk while maintaining adequate construct stability. In addition, the proximal eyelets of the trochanteric hook plate permit direct suture reattachment of the gluteus medius, gluteus minimus, and vastus lateralis tendons, thereby avoiding drill holes in the recycled cortex. Restoration of the abductor mechanism is a key determinant of hip stability and functional outcome after proximal femoral reconstruction.

Nonunion at the osteotomy junction was identified at 8 months postoperatively. Several factors likely contributed to the delayed union. First, a standard femoral stem was used and did not bypass the distal osteotomy site; a long-stem prosthesis was not available at our institution. This resulted in stress concentration at the junction and transfer of mechanical load primarily to the plate–cerclage construct without intramedullary support[4,5]. Second, six consecutive cycles of adjuvant chemotherapy were initiated 4 weeks postoperatively. Continuous chemotherapy administration during the early healing phase may have impaired graft–host integration, as systemic chemotherapy has been identified as a risk factor for biological reconstruction failure[12]. These factors should be considered potential confounders when interpreting the delayed union.

Nonunion was managed with supplementary iliac crest bone grafting at 10 months. Complete radiological union was achieved at 26 months. Nonunion at the graft-host junction has been reported in frozen autograft series, with rates varying according to devitalization technique and fixation method[8,13]. In resource-limited settings where long-stem prostheses are unavailable, close radiological surveillance and a low threshold for secondary bone grafting are recommended. These findings underscore the combined role of biological and mechanical factors in achieving union after FAPC reconstruction.

A key technical consideration was the sequence of graft preparation. Liquid nitrogen treatment increases bone brittleness and the risk of iatrogenic fracture during instrumentation. In this case, all preparatory steps, including soft tissue clearance, macroscopic tumor removal, cortical perforation, femoral canal reaming, and draping were completed prior to treatment with liquid nitrogen. This sequence is not routinely detailed in the literature, but may represent an important technical consideration when combining recycled autograft with arthroplasty reconstruction.

This report is limited by its single-case design, deviation from the standard neoadjuvant chemotherapy protocol, and use of implants dictated by resource availability rather than surgical preference, all of which limit generalizability.

Conclusion

This case suggests that an FAPC reconstruction combined with total hip arthroplasty may be a feasible limb-salvage option for proximal femoral osteosarcoma in young, active patients. It achieved satisfactory functional recovery (MSTS of 90%) and maintained oncological control at 43 months of follow-up. This approach may be particularly valuable in resource-limited settings where allografts and megaprostheses are not readily available. Careful attention to graft preparation, fixation strategy and implant selection is essential to optimize outcomes and minimize complications.
