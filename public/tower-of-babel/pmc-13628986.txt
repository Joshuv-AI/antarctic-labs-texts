EMBED: Ensemble MATCONT-Based Detection and Classification of Bifurcations in Dual Phosphorylation–Dephosphorylation Reaction Networks
Authors: Guturu L. Harika, Krishnamachari Sriram
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We present EMBED, an ensemble-based extension of MATCONT that addresses the persistent design–build–test–learn (DBTL) gap in synthetic biology from a mechanistic, dynamical systems perspective. While the DBTL gap is widely attributed to biological complexity, context dependence, and incomplete models, here we show that it also arises fundamentally from the geometry of high-dimensional parameter spaces, where desired dynamical regimes occupy narrow and fragile regions. Conventional use of MATCONT is limited to single-parameter or single-trajectory bifurcation analysis, restricting its ability to capture this global organization. To overcome this, we integrate MATCONT with large-scale parameter sampling and automated continuation, enabling high-throughput and reproducible ensemble bifurcation analysis. Using dual phosphorylation–dephosphorylation (PdP) systems as a model, we demonstrate that although saddle-node, pitchfork, and saddle-node–transcritical bifurcations are theoretically possible, they are confined to finely tuned parameter regions and are therefore difficult to realize experimentally. In contrast, robust nonsingular dynamics such as ultrasensitive and biphasic responses dominate under biologically relevant conditions. We further show that the choice of control parameter fundamentally constrains accessible dynamics, establishing a key design axis for biochemical systems. By quantifying the prevalence, robustness, and accessibility of dynamical regimes, EMBED enables the identification of parameter regions that are not only functionally desirable but also experimentally realizable. Thus, EMBED complements existing DBTL approaches by providing a global, structure-based framework that links kinetic parameters to dynamical behavior. In doing so, it transforms MATCONT from a local analysis tool into a scalable platform for global dynamical mapping and offers a principled strategy for the rational design of robust biological circuits across signaling and gene regulatory networks.

Introduction

Synthetic biology aims to engineer biological systems with predictable, robust, and programmable functionalities. However, rational design of gene and signaling networks remains challenging due to parameter uncertainty, environmental variability, and context dependence. A central difficulty is identifying kinetic parameter regimes that reliably produce desired behaviors. Even with fixed network topology, parameter variations can generate qualitatively distinct dynamics, including graded responses, ultrasensitivity, bistability, and oscillations. − Importantly, these qualitative behaviors are determined primarily by the underlying network architecture and kinetic parameter regimes rather than by the particular mathematical formulation used to describe the reaction kinetics. In many biochemical systems, reduced kinetic models obtained from mechanistic mass-action descriptions retain the same qualitative dynamical repertoire, including steady-state organization and bifurcation structure, while significantly reducing computational complexity. Such reduced formulations therefore provide an efficient and biologically meaningful framework for large-scale exploration of high-dimensional parameter spaces, motivating the modeling approach adopted in this work. This contributes to the persistent discrepancy between model predictions and experimental observationscommonly referred to as the DBTL gap.

Recent advances in synthetic biology have emphasized the design–build–test–learn (DBTL) cycle as a systematic framework for engineering biological systems. , In this paradigm, the design phase relies heavily on computational models to predict functional behaviors, which are then experimentally implemented and iteratively refined. However, a fundamental limitation of current DBTL implementations is that the design phase often focuses on identifying isolated parameter sets that yield desired behaviors, without quantifying the robustness or prevalence of these behaviors across the high-dimensional parameter space. As a result, designs that appear successful in silico frequently fail during the build and test phases due to parameter uncertainty, biological variability, and environmental fluctuations. − This limitation reflects an intrinsic gap in DBTL, where local model predictions do not translate into globally robust and experimentally realizable behaviors. In this work, we address this gap by introducing an ensemble-based bifurcation framework that systematically maps parameter space to dynamical regimes, enabling identification of behaviors that are not only theoretically feasible but also robust and experimentally accessible, as summarized in Figure .

Each sampled parameter set is then analyzed using a MATCONT-based workflow involving steady-state computation, numerical continuation, and detection of singularities such as limit points (LPs) and branch points (BPs). The resulting bifurcation structures are classified into distinct dynamical regimes, including saddle-node, pitchfork, and saddle-node–transcritical bifurcations, as well as nonsingular behaviors such as ultrasensitive and biphasic responses. This framework enables the mapping of the parameter space to functional outcomes, revealing that robust dynamical behaviors dominate, while bifurcation-driven regimes occur in narrowly constrained regions. The EMBED approach facilitates the design phase and iterative refinement of selecting the control parameter and performs dynamic targeted experimental exploration.

From a dynamical systems perspective, these behaviors are governed by bifurcation theory, which identifies parameter thresholds where qualitative changes occur. Bifurcations partition a high-dimensional parameter space into distinct regimes and underpin key synthetic biology functions such as switches, memory, and oscillators, typically associated with saddle-node (SN), transcritical (TC), pitchfork (PF), and Hopf bifurcations. Despite this framework, idealized bifurcations predicted by models are rarely observed in experiments. This discrepancy arises from parameter sloppiness, structural model approximations, and neglected effects such as stochasticity and hidden interactions. Importantly, bifurcation regimes often occupy narrow and finely tuned regions of parameter space, making them intrinsically fragile to perturbation. As a result, experimentally accessible systems are more likely to exhibit robust nonsingular behaviors, such as ultrasensitive and biphasic responses, rather than idealized bifurcation structures.

We propose three complementary explanations for this discrepancy. First, bifurcation regimes require finely tuned parameters and symmetry conditions that are difficult to maintain in biochemical environments. Second, intrinsic stochasticity blurs sharp bifurcation boundaries, smoothing transitions and favoring robust responses. Third, methodological limitations are critical: widely used tools such as MATCONT and XPPAUT are inherently local, rely on single-trajectory continuation, and do not enable systematic exploration of high-dimensional parameter spaces. − We attempted to implement an automated detection approach based on the method described in automatic detection of saddle-node–transcritical interaction by. In practice, this implementation was not robust for our model: in some cases, it indicated SNTC interaction, while in many other cases, it failed to detect one, the global organization, prevalence, and robustness of dynamical regimes remain unresolved.

Dual phosphorylation–dephosphorylation (PdP) cycles, central to MAPK signaling pathways, provide an ideal platform to study these issues. These systems exhibit rich nonlinear behaviorsincluding bistability, hysteresis, oscillations, ultrasensitivity, and biphasic responsesarising from enzymatic nonlinearities and regulatory interactions. − Experimental studies confirm bistability and oscillations in MAPK systems, establishing them as canonical models of nonlinear biochemical computation. Even without explicit feedback, PdP systems generate nonlinearities through enzyme saturation, sequestration, and competition. Classical studies have demonstrated SN, TC, and PF bifurcations in biochemical networks. −

The dual phosphorylation–dephosphorylation (PdP) cycle considered in this work has been extensively studied using both detailed mechanistic mass-action models and reduced Michaelis–Menten (MM) formulations derived through the quasi-steady-state approximation (QSSA). While the mechanistic model explicitly represents enzyme–substrate complexes, the reduced MM model preserves the same biochemical network topology and reproduces the same qualitative dynamical behaviors over corresponding parameter regimes, including ultrasensitivity, graded and biphasic responses, multistability, and codimension-1 bifurcations. Consequently, the reduced MM formulation has become the standard representation for analyzing PdP dynamics because it retains the essential nonlinear mechanisms while substantially reducing computational complexity. Since the objective of the present work is to systematically map the organization of dynamical regimes across parameter space rather than compare alternative kinetic formulations, we adopt the MM model throughout this study.

However, existing studies focus on isolated parameter regimes and do not resolve how multiple dynamic behaviors are organized across the full parameter space. In particular, it remains unclear whether bifurcation-driven behaviors are robust and prevalent or confined to narrow, fragile regions relative to dominant nonsingular dynamics. Addressing this requires a systematic exploration of high-dimensional parameter spaces, which is not feasible with conventional continuation tools. Existing frameworks such as MATCONT and XPPAUT require manual initialization and do not support ensemble-scale analysis, preventing comprehensive sampling, automated classification, and global mapping of dynamical regimes. ,,

In this work, we address these limitations by introducing EMBED, an ensemble MATCONT-based bifurcation framework that integrates large-scale parameter sampling with automated continuation. This approach enables systematic mapping of SN, TC, and PF bifurcation regimes alongside nonsingular dynamics across a high-dimensional parameter space. By moving beyond single-trajectory continuation, EMBED quantifies the prevalence, robustness, and accessibility of dynamical behaviors and provides a mechanistic explanation for the rarity of classical bifurcations in experiments. Although demonstrated using a canonical dual phosphorylation–dephosphorylation network, the ensemble continuation strategy is broadly applicable to mechanistic nonlinear ODE models for signaling and gene regulatory systems, provided the corresponding dynamical system can be formulated for numerical continuation offering a scalable strategy to bridge nonlinear dynamical theory with the experimentally realizable biological design.

The successive model reductions preserve the steady-state solutions and qualitative bifurcation structure of the original system while substantially improving analytical and computational tractability.

We comment here on the Markevich et al. model, and our proposed Michaelis–Menten-based PdP model. Both our models capture the distributive dual phosphorylation–dephosphorylation but differ in complexity and symmetry. Markevich et al. include ten reactions with multiple enzyme–substrate complexes and redistribution steps, especially in dephosphorylation via MKP3 (see Scheme 2, dephosphorylation steps in their paper), capturing biochemical realism through intermediate recycling and enzyme sequestration. In contrast, our model uses only eight reactions with symmetric, minimal MM stepseach phosphorylation and dephosphorylation step proceeds via one intermediate without feedback or rebinding. Despite its simplicity, we will show below that our model retains key nonlinearities and saturations to exhibit bistability and multiple bifurcations, revealing rich dynamics that can emerge from structurally minimal, symmetric, dual PdP cycles.

Reduction to the MM Kinetic Model Helps to Perform Experiments for Specific Dynamics

For biochemical regulatory modules such as phosphorylation–dephosphorylation (PdP) cycles, mathematical models can be formulated either using full mass-action kinetics or using Michaelis–Menten (MM) reduced kinetics. While the mass-action formulation provides the most mechanistically detailed description, the MM-based representation is often more practical and informative for biological interpretation and experimental design. In a full mass-action framework, each enzymatic reaction must explicitly account for enzyme–substrate binding and unbinding steps, catalytic conversion, and intermediate complex formation. As a result, a simple PdP cycle involving a kinase and a phosphatase typically requires several dynamical variables and a large number of kinetic parameters, including association rates, dissociation rates, catalytic rate constants, and total enzyme concentrations. This level of detail can significantly increase model dimensionality and parameter uncertainty, making it difficult to systematically analyze the dynamical behavior of the system or to relate model parameters directly to experimentally controllable quantities.

In contrast, the Michaelis–Menten formulation exploits the quasi-steady-state approximation for enzyme–substrate complexes and reduces the description of each enzymatic step to a small number of effective parameters, typically the maximum catalytic rate V max and the Michaelis constant K m . Consequently, the PdP module can be represented using a much smaller parameter set that directly captures the effective enzymatic activity and substrate affinity. For example, whereas the full mass-action representation of a kinase–phosphatase cycle may involve multiple rate constants for binding, dissociation, and catalysis by both enzymes, the MM formulation reduces these to a few composite parameters describing the effective phosphorylation and dephosphorylation fluxes. This reduction substantially simplifies the parameter space and makes it easier to perform systematic dynamical analyses such as steady-state mapping, sensitivity analysis, and bifurcation exploration.

From an experimental perspective, this reduction is particularly advantageous because the effective parameters in MM models are closely related to experimentally tunable variables. Parameters such as V max depend on enzyme concentration and catalytic efficiency, while K m reflects substrate affinity and can be modulated through mutations, competitive inhibitors, or substrate analogs. By using simulations of the reduced model, experimentalists can therefore identify which combinations of enzyme levels, substrate concentrations, or regulatory factors are most likely to shift the system into regimes of interest, such as ultrasensitive responses, biphasic dynamics, or potential switching behavior. In practice, this means that model-guided parameter sweeps can suggest targeted perturbationsfor example, increasing kinase abundance, reducing phosphatase activity, or modifying substrate availabilitythat are experimentally feasible. Thus, the MM-based representation serves as an effective bridge between theoretical modeling and experimental manipulation, enabling researchers to explore complex dynamical behaviors with a manageable number of biologically interpretable parameters and to guide the design of controlled perturbation experiments.

To further relate the present computational framework to experimentally characterized PdP systems, we note that the parameter ranges explored in this study are based on experimentally measured kinetic quantities reported for distributive dual-MAPK phosphorylation. Ferrell and Bhatt experimentally demonstrated that MAPK activation proceeds through a distributive dual-phosphorylation mechanism and reported Michaelis constants of approximately 300 nM and 46 nM for the first and second phosphorylation reactions, respectively, together with a phosphatase Michaelis constant of approximately 300 nM. The catalytic rate constants and enzyme concentration ranges were selected to be consistent with experimentally measured turnover rates and kinase/phosphatase concentrations reported by Witzel and Blüthgen. These experimentally determined values lie within or were used to define the sampling ranges adopted in the present ensemble analysis (Table ), indicating that the explored parameter space remains biologically plausible. We emphasize that these experimental studies validate the biochemical mechanism and physiologically relevant kinetic regime underlying the model rather than the bifurcation structures themselves, which are computational predictions arising from the systematic exploration of the experimentally admissible parameter space.

MATCONT–LHS Framework for Ensemble Bifurcation Analysis (EMBED)

Beyond identifying bifurcation points for individual parameter sets, MATCONT can be used through both a graphical user interface and a command-line interface (CLI). In the present MATCONT-LHS framework, we use the CLI to automate bifurcation analysis across ensembles of parameter sets, enabling the construction of ensemble bifurcation landscapes analogous to ensemble-based parameter estimation approaches widely used in systems biology. In the classical parameter estimation, large ensembles of parameter sets are generated to fit experimental time-series data or steady-state dose–response curves, yielding a family of models consistent with observations rather than a single best-fit solution. MATCONT–LHS extends this philosophy from trajectory fitting to dynamical-structure fitting. Latin Hypercube Sampling (LHS) is a statistical stratified sampling technique used to efficiently explore high-dimensional parameter spaces. Unlike simple random sampling, LHS divides each parameter range into equal-probability intervals and ensures that each interval is sampled exactly once. This guarantees more uniform space-filling coverage of the parameter space with significantly fewer samples. As a result, LHS reduces clustering and sampling bias, making it particularly suitable for nonlinear dynamical systems where bifurcation structures are sensitive to parameter variations. In systems biology, this becomes crucial because kinetic parameters are often uncertain, correlated, and experimentally heterogeneous. Therefore, LHS provides a computationally efficient and statistically robust way to generate diverse yet representative ensembles of parameter sets for bifurcation and uncertainty analysis.

Within this framework, parameter ensembles are generated not only to reproduce observed system outputs but also to realize specific qualitative dynamics, such as bistability, oscillations, or excitability. This ensemble-based approach reveals that the same biological network can support multiple bifurcation mechanisms across parameter space. For example, transitions traditionally attributed to a pitchfork bifurcation may, for different parameter ensembles, arise via saddle-node or saddle-node–transcritical bifurcations.

Importantly, this perspective allows one to generate parameter ensembles conditioned on bifurcation features, in direct analogy to generating parameter ensembles conditioned on experimental data. Instead of asking whether a given parameter set exhibits a bifurcation, the question is inverted: which regions of parameter space give rise to a desired bifurcation topology or dynamical phenotype? In this sense, bifurcation diagrams are treated as targets of inference, not merely diagnostic tools. By aggregating bifurcation information across the LHS ensemble, MATCONT–LHS constructs probabilistic maps of bifurcation types and stability regimes, effectively producing an ensemble bifurcation diagram. These diagrams capture both the diversity and prevalence of dynamical behaviors, uncovering previously hidden bifurcation regimes and revealing how qualitative system behavior reorganizes under parametric uncertainty. Overall, MATCONT–LHS establishes a conceptual parallel between parameter estimation and bifurcation discovery, positioning bifurcation analysis as a statistical, ensemble-driven process rather than a single-model exercise. This shift is particularly relevant for biological systems, where variability, uncertainty, and context dependence are intrinsic features rather than modeling artifacts.

The computational workflow underlying the MATCONT–LHS framework is organized into a sequence of modular steps, including parameter initialization, sampling bounds definitions, Latin Hypercube Sampling (LHS), steady-state computation, numerical continuation, branch extension, dynamical classification, and visualization of bifurcation structures. These steps are executed through the MATCONT command-line interface (CLI), allowing the bifurcation analysis to be automated across the sampled parameter ensemble. For each sampled parameter set, the ODE system is first integrated to steady state, followed by equilibrium continuation using MATCONT to identify bifurcation points such as limit points and branch points. When branch points are detected, additional continuation is performed to capture disconnected solution branches. The resulting bifurcation structures are then categorized and aggregated across the ensemble to construct statistical summaries and visual representations. The algorithm corresponding to this workflow is provided below. Full implementation details, including code blocks and parameter configurations, are provided in Section S4 of the Supporting Information SI-7.

Although the present study demonstrates EMBED using a Michaelis–Menten formulation of the dual phosphorylation–dephosphorylation cycle, the computational framework itself is not restricted to this kinetic representation. EMBED can be applied to any ODE-based biochemical model compatible with MATCONT, provided that the corresponding MATLAB function describing the governing equations is supplied to the CLI-based workflow. While the automated workflow remains unchanged, the resulting bifurcation landscapes and distributions of dynamical regimes are expected to depend on the underlying mathematical model and its associated nonlinearities.

For all parameter sets, numerical continuation was performed using a common set of MATCONT continuation settings with adaptive step-size control, automatic detection of limit points (LPs) and branch points (BPs), and branch switching at detected BP points. Parameter sets for which numerical continuation failed or terminated prematurely were automatically excluded from the ensemble statistics. The remaining parameter sets were classified according to their bifurcation topology, while the corresponding steady-state response curves were independently categorized as graded, ultrasensitive, or biphasic based on their qualitative dose–response characteristics. A complete description of the continuation settings, classification criteria, failure-handling procedure, and MATLAB implementation is provided in Section S4 of the Supporting Information S1.7.

Results and Discussions

Dynamics from a Single Kinetic Parameter Set

We first analyze the bifurcation structure of the dual PdP model using XPPAUT and MATCONT, which reliably detect limit points (LPs) and branch points (BPs) for a single, specific parameter set. However, BP points cannot be unambiguously classified as transcritical or pitchfork bifurcations using these tools alone. To resolve this ambiguity and rigorously characterize the nature of the bifurcations, we employ Sotomayor’s theorem. , Details of MATCONT, XPPAUT, and Sotomayor’s theorem are provided in Section (S2) of the Supporting Information SI-7.

In Section (S3) of the Supporting Information (SI-7), we apply the conditions of Sotomayor’s theorem to generic one-dimensional models of saddle-node–transcritical (SNTC) and saddle-node–pitchfork (SNPF) bifurcations to verify consistency. The conditions of the theorem are satisfied for the observed dynamics, as shown in supplementary Tables S3 and S4. The corresponding dynamical behaviors are illustrated in Supplementary Figure S1.

To perform numerical bifurcation analysis, we choose the parameters that is close to the Markevich et al. model. The parameters are provided in Supporting Information SI-7, Table S2. We fix all parameters and use to the total substrate ATOT as the bifurcation parameter. The codimension-1 bifurcation is shown in Figure A. We would like to comment here on the choice of bifurcation parameter which plays a critical role in shaping the qualitative dynamics of dual phosphorylation–dephosphorylation (PdP) systems. In our model, the total substrate concentration (ATOT) serves as the bifurcation parameter, while kinase and phosphatase levels remain fixed. This allows the system to preserve structural and mathematical symmetry, enabling the emergence of transcritical (TC), saddle-node (SN), and pitchfork (PF) bifurcations through parameter variations alone. As ATOT increases, enzyme saturation and substrate competition drive nonlinearities that shift the system from graded responses (via TC), to bistable switches (via SN), and, in symmetric cases, to PF bifurcations when two symmetric steady states lose stability simultaneously. In contrast, the Markevich model uses MAPKKTOT (the kinase) as the bifurcation parameter, holding total MAPK and phosphatase constant. This setup introduces structural asymmetry due to complex enzyme–substrate interactions and enzyme recycling, particularly within phosphatase-mediated dephosphorylation steps. As a result, the Markevich model may not exhibit TC or PF bifurcations but may generate bistability exclusively through SN bifurcations arising from enzyme sequestration and rebinding. Thus, our model’s bifurcation behavior stems from intrinsic mass-action and Michaelis–Menten nonlinearities, while the Markevich model relies on complex enzymatic redistribution mechanisms. The use of ATOT emphasizes substrate-driven ultrasensitivity and allows spontaneous symmetry breaking, while MAPKKTOT as a parameter reflects kinase-limited regimes and introduces indirect feedback-like behavior. Ultimately, the choice of bifurcation parameter governs the system’s symmetry, feedback structure, and nonlinearity; and therefore the types and conditions of bifurcations that can emerge. Bifurcation Analysis, Classification, and Model Reduction

Numerical Bifurcation Analysis

To characterize the nonlinear dynamics of the dual phosphorylation–dephosphorylation (PdP) system, we performed numerical continuation using a hierarchy of mathematically equivalent models derived from the same biochemical reaction network. The full mechanistic model is formulated using mass-action (MA) kinetics, in which every elementary enzyme–substrate binding, dissociation, and catalytic reaction is explicitly represented. Applying the quasi-steady-state approximation (QSSA) reduces the full model to a two-dimensional (2D) Michaelis–Menten formulation by eliminating the rapidly equilibrating enzyme–substrate complexes. In this reduced formulation, the elementary kinetic rate constants are incorporated into the corresponding Michaelis constants (for example, K m1 = (k –1 + k 2)/k 1) and the maximum rates V max1 = k 2 KTOT. A further reduction of the 2D model yields a one-dimensional (1D) formulation from which the phase-line diagrams are constructed, providing a simple graphical interpretation of the steady states, their stability, and bifurcation points (see Supporting Information SI-7, Section S1.2 for the reduction of the model from the full MA model).

Importantly, these three formulations represent the same biochemical mechanism and are linked through systematic model reduction, rather than independent assumptions. The kinetic parameter sets used to generate the various bifurcation behaviors in the full mass-action model are consistently mapped to the corresponding parameters in the reduced models through QSSA relationships. Consequently, every parameter regime investigated produces identical steady-state branches (but not identical but similar steady states), stability changes, and bifurcation structures in the full, 2D, and 1D formulations. This agreement demonstrates that the successive reductions preserve the essential nonlinear dynamics of the PdP system and neither introduce nor eliminate the bifurcation behavior.

Using these equivalent formulations, we systematically explored the parameter space by varying selected kinetic parameters while keeping the biochemical reaction topology and governing equations unchanged in the 2D-ODE model. Consequently, Figures , , , and do not represent different mathematical models but rather different dynamical regimes arising from distinct kinetic parameter sets within the same biochemical network. While numerical continuation using MATCONT and XPPAUT robustly detects singularities such as limit points (LPs) and branch points (BPs), these tools identify both transcritical and pitchfork bifurcations simply as branch points and therefore do not explicitly distinguish between these bifurcation types. Furthermore, coupled bifurcation interactions, such as the saddle-node–transcritical (SNTC) bifurcation, cannot be rigorously classified from numerical continuation alone. Consequently, although continuation accurately locates the bifurcation points and constructs the global bifurcation diagrams, a rigorous analytical framework is required to determine the precise mathematical nature of each bifurcation. This motivates the application of Sotomayor’s theorem in the following section.

Bifurcation Classification Using Sotomayor’s Theorem

To rigorously classify the bifurcation points identified numerically, we apply Sotomayor’s theorem, which provides necessary and sufficient conditions for codimension-1 bifurcations based on eigenvalue degeneracy and higher-order derivatives.

Before applying Sotomayor’s theorem to the biochemical PdP model, we first validated our application of the theorem using canonical one-dimensional normal forms whose bifurcation structures are analytically well established. Specifically, we considered the normal forms for saddle-node–transcritical (SNTC; ẋ=Sx±ax2±bx3 , where S, a, and b are parameters) and saddle-node–pitchfork (SNPF; ẋ=Sx±ax3±bx5 , where S, a, and b are parameters) bifurcations. These benchmark systems enabled us to gain confidence in the application of Sotomayor’s theorem before analyzing the considerably more complex biochemical PdP model. For these one-dimensional systems, we first performed the conventional linear stability analysis by explicitly determining the steady states and their stability. The bifurcation classifications obtained analytically were then independently verified using numerical methods implemented in Mathematica, confirming the expected bifurcation behavior and the corresponding nondegeneracy conditions prescribed by Sotomayor’s theorem. The complete derivations, linear stability analysis, and numerical verification are provided in the Supporting Information SI-7 (Sections S3.1 and S3.2).

Having established confidence in the methodology, we then applied the same analytical framework directly to the PdP reaction network. Unlike the canonical normal forms, where different governing equations describe different bifurcation types, the PdP system retains the same reaction topology and governing equations throughout the analysis. The distinct bifurcation behaviors (SN, TC, PF, and SNTC) arise solely from different kinetic parameter regimes. Owing to the complexity of the biochemical model, conventional analytical linear stability analysis is generally intractable. Therefore, the bifurcation classification based on Sotomayor’s theorem was verified numerically using Mathematica for representative kinetic parameter sets corresponding to each bifurcation type before interpreting the results.

The steady states used for the application of Sotomayor’s theorem were first computed independently in Mathematica for the corresponding kinetic parameter sets and bifurcation parameter values associated with each codimension-1 bifurcation. These equilibrium solutions were then verified against the steady states obtained from the numerical continuation results generated using both MATCONT and XPPAUT to ensure consistency across the three computational approaches. The validated steady states at the corresponding limit points (LPs) and branch points (BPs) were subsequently used to evaluate the nondegeneracy conditions prescribed by Sotomayor’s theorem. For completeness, the steady-state values obtained for all bifurcation cases, together with the corresponding verification, are provided in tabular form in the Supporting Information SI-7 (Table S3)

For the bistable regime (Figure ), the limit points satisfy the nondegeneracy conditions for saddle-node bifurcations (Table ), confirming that bistability arises from SN mechanisms. In the SNTC case (Figure ), the branch point satisfies the conditions for a transcritical (TC) bifurcation, while the corresponding limit point satisfies the saddle-node conditions (Table ), establishing the coexistence of SN and TC dynamics. In the pitchfork case (Figure ), the branch point satisfies higher-order degeneracy conditions, including vanishing first- and second-order terms and nonzero higher-order derivatives (Table ), distinguishing it from transcritical bifurcations. This highlights a key difference: while both PF and TC appear as branch points in numerical continuation, PF requires additional symmetry-induced degeneracy that cannot be inferred from numerical continuation tools alone.

Thus, Sotomayor’s theorem plays a critical role by providing a rigorous, parameter-based classification of bifurcations. It resolves ambiguities inherent in numerical detection, enables discrimination between closely related bifurcation types, and confirms higher-order interactions such as SNTC. Without this analytical validation, branch points identified numerically could be misclassified, leading to incorrect interpretation of system dynamics.

Finally, we emphasize that the global organization of dynamical regimes reported here refers to the complete bifurcation landscape of the PdP system within the adopted mechanistic framework. Our conclusions are not inferred solely from the reduced Michaelis–Menten formulation but are consistently supported by the full mass-action model, the reduced two-dimensional (2D) QSSA model, and the corresponding one-dimensional (1D) phase-line formulation, all of which reproduce identical bifurcation structures for the same kinetic parameter sets. Thus, the observed organization of monostable, bistable, symmetric, and asymmetric dynamical regimes is an intrinsic property of the underlying PdP reaction mechanism and is preserved across successive model reductions. While any mathematical model represents an approximation of biological reality, the agreement among these independent yet mathematically equivalent formulations provides strong evidence that the identified bifurcation landscape reflects the inherent nonlinear organization of the adopted biochemical mechanism rather than an artifact of model simplification.

Consistency across Model Reductions and Rate-Balance Analysis

To facilitate both analysis and visualization, we considered three mathematically equivalent representations of the PdP system: (i) the full mechanistic model, (ii) a reduced two-dimensional (2D) model obtained using the quasi-steady-state approximation (QSSA), and (iii) a one-dimensional (1D) model derived from the corresponding rate-balance equation.

Across all kinetic parameter regimes examined, the full model, the reduced 2D model, and the 1D rate-balance formulation produce identical steady-state branches, stability changes, and bifurcation structures, respectively. In particular, the SN, TC, PF, and SNTC bifurcation diagrams shown in Figures ,,,, respectively, are reproduced consistently by all three formulations. This agreement demonstrates that the model reductions preserve the essential nonlinear dynamics of the biochemical system while substantially simplifying the mathematical analysis.

The QSSA-based reduction decreases the dimensionality of the governing equations while retaining the fundamental biochemical mechanisms, including enzyme saturation, substrate competition, and conservation constraints. The further reduction to a one-dimensional rate-balance representation provides an intuitive geometric interpretation of nonlinear dynamics.

The rate-balance plots are constructed by plotting the net reaction rate, d[AP]dt , as a function of the substrate concentration [APP]. Steady states correspond to the zero crossings of the curve, while the local slope determines their stability. Single, double, and triple intersections correspond to mono-, bi-, and multistable regimes, respectively, whereas tangential intersections identify bifurcation points. Consequently, the rate-balance representation provides a simple graphical interpretation of how steady states are created, annihilated, and exchanged stability as kinetic parameters are varied.

The complete agreement among the full mechanistic model, the reduced 2D model, and the 1D rate-balance formulation confirms that the observed bifurcation behaviors are intrinsic properties of the underlying PdP mechanism rather than artifacts introduced by model reduction. The reduced formulations therefore preserve dynamic fidelity while substantially improving analytical tractability, enabling efficient exploration, visualization, and rigorous classification of the complete bifurcation landscape.

Codimension-2 Bifurcations: Small Region of Important Dynamics

The 2D model consists of 11 parameters. Among these parameters, changes in k 4 and K m5 give rise to different bifurcations such as saddle-node, transcritical, and pitchfork. The parameter k 4 is the kinetic rate constant for the release of kinase at the end of the second phosphorylation of the two-step dual phosphorylation. Meanwhile, K m5 is the Michaelis–Menten constant for the first dephosphorylation reaction of the two-step dual dephosphorylation. To show the ranges of k 4 and K m5 for each bifurcation, we simulate the two-parameter bifurcation of k 4 and K m5 against ATOT, respectively. While these two-parameter diagrams provide a structured view of how bifurcation regimes are organized locally, they do not capture how frequently these regimes occur across the full parameter space. In particular, the narrowness of the regions observed here raises an important question: do these bifurcation regimes remain equally restricted when explored across large parameter ensembles? We address this question in the subsequent section using the EMBED ensemble bifurcation framework, where we systematically examine whether large-scale parameter sampling leads to similar conclusions regarding the limited extent of these dynamical regimes.

Consequently, even minor variations in kinetic parameters arising from intrinsic noise, experimental uncertainty, or environmental fluctuations can shift the system away from the desired dynamical regimes. This suggests that although such parameter regimes can be identified computationally, they are difficult to achieve and maintain experimentally. Whether this intrinsic narrowness persists at the level of large-scale parameter exploration is investigated in the subsequent section by using ensemble bifurcation analysis.

Bridging the Design Gap in DBTL through Ensemble Bifurcation Analysis

The Design–Build–Test–Learn (DBTL) cycle provides a structured approach for engineering biological systems, where computational models guide experimental implementation and iterative refinement. , Despite its widespread adoption, a persistent challenge lies in the design phase, where models are typically used to identify specific parameter sets that produce desired behaviors such as bistability, oscillations, or ultrasensitivity. While such approaches demonstrate theoretical feasibility, they often fail to account for the global organization of the underlying parameter space. Consequently, designs based on single or narrowly tuned parameter sets are highly sensitive to perturbations and frequently fail during experimental realization.

This limitation is particularly critical in nonlinear biochemical systems, where distinct dynamical regimes are separated by bifurcations that occupy narrow and fragile regions of parameter space. Classical studies in signaling and gene regulatory networks have demonstrated bistability, oscillations, and switch-like behavior under carefully chosen parameter conditions. ,− However, these studies typically focus on specific parameter regimes and do not quantify how prevalent or robust these behaviors are across the full parameter space. As a result, rare but mathematically valid dynamical regimes may be overrepresented in modeling studies, while more robust and experimentally accessible behaviors remain underexplored. By systematically exploring the full parameter space, as in the EMBED framework, it becomes possible to identify experimentally feasible parameter sets that not only realize these rare dynamics but also uncover parameter regimes where such behaviors persist over wider regions of parameter space.

Several studies have highlighted the role of parameter sloppiness and uncertainty in limiting predictive accuracy in systems biology models. , In such systems, large variations in parameters can produce similar outputs, while small changes in critical directions can lead to qualitative shifts in the dynamics. This anisotropic structure of the parameter space implies that desirable behaviors such as bistability or symmetry-driven bifurcations may exist only in finely tuned regions that are difficult to achieve experimentally. Indeed, experimental studies of synthetic circuits often report deviations from predicted behaviors due to context dependence, stochasticity, and unmodeled interactions. ,

To address these limitations, ensemble-based approaches have been proposed to explore parameter uncertainty and robustness in biological systems. , However, these methods primarily focus on fitting experimental data or exploring steady-state variability and do not explicitly characterize the global organization of dynamical regimes across the kinetic parameter space of a given mathematical model. In the present work, “global organization” refers to the distribution and connectivity of dynamical regimes across the admissible kinetic parameter space of the selected mathematical model. It does not imply a complete characterization of the underlying biological system, whose behavior ultimately depends on the chosen modeling framework and associated assumptions. In contrast, the framework introduced here extends ensemble concepts to dynamical systems analysis by integrating Latin Hypercube Sampling (LHS) with numerical continuation, as summarized in Figure . This enables systematic exploration of high-dimensional parameter space of the selected mathematical model and classification of the resulting dynamical behaviors based on their underlying bifurcation structure.

Within the DBTL paradigm, this approach fundamentally enhances the design phase by shifting the focus from identifying single-parameter solutions to mapping the distribution, prevalence, and robustness of dynamical regimes. Instead of asking whether a system can exhibit a particular behavior, the framework quantifies how likely it is for that behavior to occur under realistic parameter variability. This distinction is crucial for experimental design as it prioritizes parameter regimes that are both functionally desirable and robust to perturbations.

By constructing ensemble bifurcation landscapes for the selected mathematical model, the proposed EMBED framework reveals that classical bifurcation-driven behaviors such as saddle-node, pitchfork, and saddle-node–transcritical bifurcations are confined to narrow regions of parameter space, whereas robust nonsingular dynamics such as ultrasensitive and biphasic responses dominate. This observation provides a mechanistic explanation for the frequent discrepancy between theoretical predictions and experimental observations in synthetic biology.

The parameter ranges explored in this study are consistent with those of experimentally characterized distributive phosphorylation systems. Ferrell and Bhatt experimentally demonstrated that MAPK activation proceeds through a distributive dual-phosphorylation mechanism and reported Michaelis constants of approximately 300 nM and 46 nM for the first and second phosphorylation reactions, respectively, together with a phosphatase Michaelis constant of approximately 300 nM. These experimentally determined values fall within the sampling ranges considered in the present ensemble analysis (Table ), indicating that the investigated parameter space encompasses biologically plausible kinetic regimes. Although the experimental study focused on ultrasensitive steady-state responses rather than bifurcation phenomena, it provides experimental support for the biochemical mechanisms and kinetic assumptions underlying the EMBED framework.

Mapping Dynamical Regimes and Structural Constraints via Ensemble Bifurcation Analysis

The 2D dual PdP model exhibits a diverse spectrum of dynamical behaviors arising from variations in kinetic parameters, spanning both singular (bifurcation-driven) and nonsingular responses. Using the EMBED–PdP framework, we systematically uncover and classify these behaviors across high-dimensional parameter space, revealing not only the accessible dynamics but also the structural constraints that govern their realization. The dominant codimension-1 bifurcations observed are saddle-node (SN) and pitchfork (PF), along with their coupled form, the saddle-node–transcritical (SNTC) interaction, while nonsingular behaviors include ultrasensitive, biphasic, and graded responses. Importantly, comparison between single-parameter bifurcation results and ensemble analysis reveals that while individual parameter sets suggest well-defined bifurcation structures, these represent only isolated realizations; ensemble analysis exposes how these structures persist, shift, or vanish under parameter variability, thereby identifying which kinetic parameters must be tightly constrained and which can be varied to achieve desired dynamics.

PF, SNTC, and DBTL Organizing Principle

A central organizing principle governing the dynamic landscape of the dual PdP system is the presence or absence of symmetry. Ensemble bifurcation analysis reveals that symmetry is the primary structural constraint controlling the accessibility of higher-order bifurcation phenomena. In particular, pitchfork (PF) bifurcations arise only when the phosphorylation and dephosphorylation modules possess complete kinetic symmetry, corresponding to identical enzyme abundances, Michaelis constants, and catalytic rate constants in the opposing reaction pathways. Under these conditions, the governing equations exhibit a Z2 symmetry, whereby interchanging the phosphorylation and dephosphorylation variables leaves the system invariant, producing the characteristic supercritical PF bifurcation shown in Figure I.

Most symmetry breaking perturbations do not preserve the PF bifurcation. Instead, perturbations of KTOT, K m7, k 6, k 4, and k 8 predominantly generate biphasic responses (Figure II), whereas perturbations of PTOT, K m3, and k 2 primarily produce ultrasensitive or graded dynamics (Figure III). These nonsingular responses occupy substantially larger regions of parameter space than the symmetric PF regime, demonstrating that exact symmetry is highly sensitive to kinetic perturbations and that smooth nonlinear responses constitute the generic operating mode of the PdP network.

The Michaelis constants K m1 and K m5 play a unique structural role in controlling symmetry breaking. Expanding the admissible variation of K m5 while maintaining the remaining symmetry conditions generates the coexistence of PF and SNTC bifurcations with LP < BP (Figure IV), whereas increasing the variation of K m1 produces PF and SNTC bifurcations with LP > BP (Figure V). These results demonstrate that substrate saturation governs the unfolding of the symmetric PF bifurcation into the asymmetric SNTC structures. From the perspective of singularity theory, the PF bifurcation exists only on the symmetry manifold, whereas symmetry breaking separates the degenerate bifurcation into interacting saddle-node (SN) and transcritical (TC) bifurcations. Consequently, the PF bifurcation represents a limiting symmetric case of the broader SNTC family rather than an independently robust dynamic regime.

From the Design–Build–Test–Learn (DBTL) perspective, Figure provides a quantitative design map identifying the kinetic parameters that govern structural topology and those that primarily tune response thresholds. In particular, KM1 and K m5 act as structural control parameters whose perturbation fundamentally reorganizes the steady-state manifold, whereas several other kinetic parameters mainly modulate quantitative operating characteristics while preserving the qualitative dynamics. Thus, EMBED extends conventional continuation analysis by identifying not only mathematically admissible bifurcations but also the parameter regions in which they remain experimentally accessible and robust under biological variability, providing practical design guidance for engineering biochemical networks.

SN Bifurcation and DBTL Perspective

Unlike the PF bifurcation, which requires strict global symmetry of the phosphorylation and dephosphorylation modules, saddle-node (SN) bifurcations arise under conditions of partial symmetry in which the nonlinear balance between phosphorylation and dephosphorylation fluxes generates multiple steady states. Geometrically, the SN bifurcation corresponds to the formation of a folded steady-state manifold in which a stable and an unstable equilibrium coalesce at a limit point (LP), producing the characteristic S-shaped bistability response. Although SN bifurcations are generally more accessible than PF bifurcations, the present ensemble analysis demonstrates that their occurrence remains strongly constrained by the underlying kinetic parameter space.

The ensemble analysis reveals that the bistable SN structure is considerably more fragile than that suggested by an individual continuation diagram. Perturbations of K m5, K m7, k 6, and k 8 predominantly collapse the folded steady-state manifold into biphasic responses (Figure II), whereas variations in PTOT, K m3, k 2, and k 4 primarily generate ultrasensitive or graded dynamics (Figure III). In both cases, the disappearance of the limit points eliminates bistability while preserving the underlying nonlinear response, indicating that ultrasensitive and biphasic behaviors represent the dominant operating modes, once the balance required for bistability is disrupted.

An interesting exception is observed when the admissible range of the total kinase concentration KTOT is expanded (Figure IV). In this case, both classical S-shaped SN bifurcations and biphasic responses coexist, demonstrating that enzyme abundance influences the accessibility of bistability without completely destroying the folded manifold. This distinction highlights the difference between structural robustness, which governs the existence of the bistable manifold, and threshold robustness, which determines the location of the switching thresholds while preserving their qualitative topology.

Mechanistically, these results identify substrate saturation as the primary driver of the bistability in dual PdP systems. The Michaelis constants K m1 and K m5 act as structural control parameters that determine the existence of the folded manifold, whereas several other kinetic parameters mainly regulate quantitative response characteristics, such as activation thresholds or response steepness, without altering the overall topology of the steady-state manifold.

From the Design–Build–Test–Learn (DBTL) perspective, Figure provides quantitative guidance for engineering robust biochemical switches. Rather than identifying isolated bistable parameter sets, EMBED distinguishes the kinetic parameters that must be tightly controlled to preserve bistability from those that primarily tune its operating characteristics. This landscape-based perspective enables rational prioritization of experimental design by focusing on parameter regions that maximize both the robustness and experimental accessibility of bistable behavior.

Although the S-shaped SN bifurcation shown in Figure represents the canonical realization of bistability in dual PdP systems, ensemble bifurcation analysis further reveals an alternative manifestation of the same underlying nonlinear equilibrium manifold, namely, the Z-shaped saddle-node (Z-SN) bifurcation (Figure ). Although the S-shaped and Z-shaped bifurcation diagrams differ in their visual appearance, both originate from the same fundamental mechanism: the formation of a folded steady-state manifold through the nonlinear balance between the phosphorylation and dephosphorylation fluxes. Their differences primarily reflect the geometric arrangement of the equilibrium branches and the ordering of the associated limit points rather than distinct biochemical mechanisms.

Despite their different geometries, both S-shaped and Z-shaped bifurcations require the formation of a folded steady-state manifold generated through balanced enzyme saturation in opposing phosphorylation and dephosphorylation pathways. Consequently, both depend critically on the Michaelis constants controlling substrate saturation and exhibit a similar sensitivity to perturbations of enzyme abundance and catalytic activity.

From the Design–Build–Test–Learn perspective, Figure complements the S-shaped analysis by demonstrating that different manifestations of bistability are governed by the same structural constraints. Rather than treating S-shaped and Z-shaped bifurcations as independent design objectives, EMBED identifies the common kinetic parameters responsible for both behaviors and quantifies their accessibility across the global parameter space. Together, Figures and show that the primary challenge is not establishing the mathematical existence of bistability but identifying experimentally accessible parameter regions capable of sustaining bistable behavior under realistic biological variability.

Overall Observations without Constraining the Parameters: Implications in Designing Experiments and DBTL Cycle

While Figures ,, elucidate the mechanisms underlying individual bifurcation phenomena, they do not address how frequently these behaviors occur across the complete kinetic parameter space. EMBED addresses this question by performing large-scale ensemble continuation over independently sampled parameter sets, thereby quantifying the prevalence of competing dynamical regimes.

The ensemble statistics in Figure reveal a clear and consistent trend. Across all sampling strategies, codimension-1 bifurcations occupy only a small fraction of the accessible parameter space, whereas nonsingular responses overwhelmingly dominate. Bridging Experimental Kinetics and Predicted Dynamical Regimes

One of the primary motivations of the proposed EMBED framework is to bridge the computational bifurcation analysis with experimentally realizable biochemical systems. While the previous sections characterized the global dynamic landscape of the PdP network, it is equally important to determine whether these predicted behaviors remain consistent with experimentally measured phosphorylation kinetics. To address this question, experimentally reported kinetic parameters were incorporated into the EMBED workflow and progressively expanded to investigate how experimentally accessible perturbations reorganized the underlying dynamical landscape.

Experimentally measured kinetic parameters for distributive MAPK phosphorylation were compiled from Ferrell and Bhatt and Witzel and Blüthgen. Ferrell and Bhatt reported Michaelis constants of approximately 300 nM for the first phosphorylation reaction, 46 nM for the second phosphorylation reaction, and approximately 300 nM for phosphatase-mediated dephosphorylation, while Witzel and Blüthgen provided experimentally grounded enzyme concentrations and catalytic rate constants for a mechanistic MAPK model. Together, these values define biologically realistic constraints for the reduced Michaelis–Menten model used in this study. All literature-derived parameters lie within the broader sampling ranges explored by EMBED (Table ).

While Figures ,, establish the mechanistic origins of the different bifurcation classes, Figure quantifies their prevalence across the global kinetic parameter space using large-scale ensemble sampling. The analysis reveals that the PdP network is intrinsically dominated by graded, ultrasensitive, and biphasic responses, whereas saddle-node, pitchfork, and saddle-node–transcritical (SNTC) bifurcations occupy only small regions of parameter space. Importantly, this statistical organization is consistently recovered under both constrained and broad parameter sampling, indicating that the rarity of higher-order bifurcations is an inherent property of the biochemical network rather than a consequence of the sampling strategy. Figure A shows the ensemble obtained using only experimentally reported kinetic parameters. Under these experimentally constrained conditions, all sampled parameter sets exhibit graded, ultrasensitive, or biphasic responses, with no saddle-node, pitchfork, or saddle-node–transcritical (SNTC) bifurcations. These observations agree well with the experimental studies of distributive MAPK phosphorylation, which reported robust ultrasensitive steady-state responses under physiologically relevant enzyme concentrations rather than bistable or higher-order bifurcation phenomena. ,

To investigate whether additional dynamic behaviors become accessible within experimentally plausible kinetic regimes, the sampling intervals were expanded while retaining the experimentally reported values within the sampled ranges. As shown in Figure B, ultrasensitive and biphasic responses continue to dominate the ensemble, while only a small number of S-shaped and Z-shaped saddle-node bifurcations emerge. The rare S-shaped and Z-shaped parameter sets identified during the wide-range exploration were subsequently used as seed regions for targeted local sampling. These targeted searches demonstrate that once a rare dynamical region is identified, EMBED can efficiently characterize the surrounding parameter landscape and identify neighboring parameter combinations that preserve desired qualitative dynamics.

Finally, Figure E investigates parameter regions supporting pitchfork and saddle-node–transcritical (SNTC) bifurcations. Symmetry-constrained sampling was performed by imposing KTOT = PTOT, K m3 = K m7, k 2 = k 6, and k 4 = k 8, while allowing K m1 and K m5 to vary independently. Collectively, Figure reveals a hierarchical organization of the PdP parameter space. Experimentally characterized parameter values occupy regions dominated by robust-graded, ultrasensitive, and biphasic responses. Broad exploration of biologically plausible parameter ranges uncovers rare bistable regimes, while targeted local sampling progressively enriches S-, Z-, SNTC-, and pitchfork bifurcations. These results demonstrate that higher-order bifurcations are not excluded by the underlying biochemical mechanism but occupy increasingly restricted regions of the admissible kinetic parameter space.

Importantly, the perturbed parameters investigated here correspond to experimentally controllable biochemical quantities rather than abstract mathematical variables. The Michaelis constant (KM) reflects the effective affinity between an enzyme and its substrate and can be modified experimentally through site-directed mutagenesis, engineering of docking interactions, competitive inhibitors, substrate analogs, scaffold proteins, or post-translational modifications that alter enzyme–substrate binding affinity. ,, Likewise, total kinase and phosphatase concentrations (KTOT and PTOT) can be systematically varied using inducible promoters, gene overexpression, CRISPR-mediated transcriptional regulation, RNA interference, or targeted protein degradation strategies. , Consequently, the parameter perturbations explored by EMBED correspond directly to experimentally accessible biochemical manipulations.

Taken together, Figure demonstrates how experimentally measured kinetic parameters provide biologically realistic starting points for systematic exploration of biochemical parameter space. Using experimentally validated kinetic values, EMBED progressively identifies broader admissible regions, isolates rare dynamical behaviors, and enriches parameter regimes supporting higher-order bifurcations. Rather than relying on exhaustive experimental trial-and-error, the framework identifies which experimentally controllable kinetic parameters should be perturbed, the direction of perturbation, and the approximate parameter ranges required to access specific dynamical regimes. EMBED helps identify experimentally relevant parameter regimes for designing phosphorylation–dephosphorylation systems within the Design–Build–Test–Learn (DBTL) paradigm.

Discussion and Conclusions

In this work, we developed EMBED, an ensemble bifurcation framework that transforms conventional MATCONT-based analysis into a scalable, high-throughput approach for mapping the global dynamical landscape of dual phosphorylation–dephosphorylation (PdP) systems. By integrating large-scale parameter sampling with automated continuation, EMBED overcomes the inherent limitations of single-parameter bifurcation analysis, which captures only a narrow slice of system behavior. In contrast, ensemble analysis reveals how bifurcation structures distribute across parameter uncertainty, showing that features appearing sharp and well defined in local analyses are often sparse and fragile in the full parameter space. Importantly, despite the high dimensionality and large ensemble sizes, the computational cost remains remarkably low, enabling rapid, on-the-fly exploration of parameter space without compromising the rigor of numerical continuation. Sotomayor’s theorem provides the theoretical basis for interpreting these results by classifying nonhyperbolic steady states and distinguishing transcritical (TC), pitchfork (PF), and saddle-node–transcritical (SNTC) bifurcations. Our analysis shows that many apparent PF-like behaviors arise from structurally sensitive interactions between SN and TC bifurcations and readily unfold under parameter perturbations. Thus, these bifurcations act as organizing centers rather than robust dynamical regimes.

A key finding is that bifurcation-driven behaviors in PdP systems occupy extremely narrow and finely tuned regions of parameter space. Two-parameter bifurcation analysis reveals thin regime boundaries, while ensemble analysis further demonstrates that these regions have low measure and are statistically rare. Consequently, small parameter variationsarising from biological variability or experimental uncertaintyreadily shift the system away from these regimes. In contrast, nonsingular dynamics such as ultrasensitive, biphasic, and graded responses dominate across biologically relevant conditions, highlighting their intrinsic robustness.

This provides a mechanistic explanation for the long-standing discrepancy between the model predictions and experimental observations. Although classical models predict SN, TC, and PF bifurcations, these behaviors are difficult to realize experimentally because of parameter sloppiness, structural model approximations, and the limited volume of bifurcation-supporting regions. EMBED quantitatively resolves this gap by linking theoretical possibilities to the experimentally accessible dynamics.

It is important to note that the dynamical landscape reported here is interpreted within the adopted mechanistic PdP framework rather than as a universal property of all phosphorylation–dephosphorylation systems. As discussed in Section , the reduced two-dimensional Michaelis–Menten formulation was deliberately chosen because its effective kinetic parameters, namely, V max and K M , are experimentally measurable and directly amenable to biochemical perturbation, whereas many elementary rate constants in the full mass-action formulation are difficult to estimate or manipulate experimentally. Importantly, we demonstrated that the reduced model reproduces the same bifurcation structures as the full mass-action model, thereby preserving the essential nonlinear dynamics while providing a more experimentally tractable parametrization. Consequently, the ensemble bifurcation landscapes obtained here identify experimentally feasible kinetic regimes within the adopted PdP model and provide quantitative guidance for realizing specific dynamical behaviors, while recognizing that the precise locations and extents of these regimes may vary across alternative mechanistic models, parameter priors, or biological systems.

Beyond the specific PdP application, the principal contribution of EMBED is methodological. Unlike conventional ensemble modeling and global sensitivity analysis, which primarily quantify parameter sensitivity, uncertainty propagation, or statistical variability in model outputs, and parameter-space mapping approaches that identify regions associated with particular responses, EMBED explicitly characterizes the underlying bifurcation structure across an ensemble of kinetic parameter sets. By integrating large-scale parameter sampling, automated numerical continuation, systematic model reduction, codimension-1 and codimension-2 bifurcation analyses, and rigorous analytical classification using Sotomayor’s theorem, the framework transforms classical bifurcation analysis from a local, single-parameter diagnostic tool into a global, ensemble-based methodology. This enables not only the identification of distinct dynamical regimes but also the quantification of their prevalence, accessibility, and robustness within the admissible kinetic parameter space. Consequently, EMBED provides a practical framework for identifying experimentally feasible kinetic regimes that realize desired dynamical behaviors, thereby establishing a direct link between nonlinear dynamical systems analysis and model-guided experimental design within the DBTL paradigm.

Recent advances in agentic artificial intelligence (AI) further highlight the potential of mechanistic frameworks such as EMBED for autonomous scientific discovery. Unlike conventional AI approaches that primarily perform prediction or optimization, agentic AI systems are designed to iteratively reason, formulate hypotheses, select informative computational or experimental actions, and continuously refine their decisions based on newly acquired evidence. In this context, the ensemble bifurcation landscapes generated by EMBED provide substantially richer information than individual continuation diagrams by quantifying the prevalence, robustness, and accessibility of competing dynamical regimes across the admissible parameter space. Such mechanistic landscape information could serve as an interpretable knowledge base for future autonomous Design–Build–Test–Learn (DBTL) workflows, enabling intelligent agents to prioritize experimentally accessible and robust operating regimes rather than searching blindly through high-dimensional parameter spaces. Although the present work focuses on the development of the EMBED framework itself, integrating ensemble bifurcation analysis with emerging agentic AI systems represents a promising direction toward more interpretable and autonomous model-guided biological design.

From a DBTL perspective, the EMBED framework bridges the gap between model-based design and experimental implementation by enabling global exploration of parameter space and identification of robust dynamical regimes. Rather than focusing on isolated parameter sets, the framework facilitates the selection of parameter regimes that are both functionally relevant and robust to variability. By revealing that bifurcation-driven behaviors are intrinsically rare and confined to narrow regions, while nonsingular dynamics dominate, this work provides a mechanistic explanation for the persistent discrepancy between computational predictions and experimental outcomes. Furthermore, the framework highlights how the choice of control parameterssuch as ATOT, KTOT, or PTOTconstrains accessible dynamics, establishing control parameter selection as a key design axis. The ability to perform rapid, ensemble-scale bifurcation analysis enables efficient in silico iteration of design–test cycles, reducing reliance on finely tuned parameter choices and accelerating the overall DBTL workflow. As such, EMBED offers a scalable and generalizable strategy for the rational design of biochemical networks across signaling and gene regulatory systems. Overall, EMBED establishes ensemble bifurcation analysis as a necessary extension of classical bifurcation theory, converting it from a local diagnostic tool into a global, design-oriented framework. While demonstrated for PdP systems, the approach is general and applicable to broader signaling and gene regulatory networks. Future work will incorporate intrinsic stochasticity, where noise is expected to further blur bifurcation boundaries and reinforce the dominance of robust dynamical regimes.
