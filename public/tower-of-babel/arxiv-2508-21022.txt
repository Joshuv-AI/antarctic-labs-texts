Fast Convergence Rates for Subsampled Natural Gradient Algorithms on Quadratic Model Problems
Gil Goldshlager, Jiang Hu, Lin Lin

Abstract
Subsampled natural gradient descent (SNGD) has shown impressive results for parametric optimization tasks in scientific machine learning, such as neural network wavefunctions and physics-informed neural networks, but it has lacked a theoretical explanation. We address this gap by analyzing the convergence of SNGD and its accelerated variant, SPRING, for idealized parametric optimization problems where the model is linear and the loss function is strongly convex and quadratic. In the special case of a least-squares loss, namely the standard linear least-squares problem, we prove that SNGD is equivalent to a regularized Kaczmarz method while SPRING is equivalent to an accelerated regularized Kaczmarz method. As a result, by leveraging existing analyses we obtain under mild conditions (i) the first fast convergence rate for SNGD, (ii) the first convergence guarantee for SPRING in any setting, and (iii) the first proof that SPRING can accelerate SNGD. In the case of a general strongly convex quadratic loss, we extend the analysis of the regularized Kaczmarz method to obtain a fast convergence rate for SNGD under stronger conditions, providing the first explanation for the effectiveness of SNGD outside of the least-squares setting. Overall, our results illustrate how tools from randomized linear algebra can shed new light on the interplay between subsampling and curvature-aware optimization strategies.

Introduction

Neural networks are gaining traction as an alternative to traditional numerical methods for solving scientific simulation problems. For example, in the quantum sciences,
neural network wavefunctions
(NNWs) have been used to represent quantum wavefunctions and find solutions to Schrödinger’s equation for model systems
(
Carleo & Troyer 2017
)
as well as molecules and materials
(
Pfau et al. 2020
;
Hermann et al. 2020
;
Li et al. 2022
)
.
In parallel, neural networks have been applied to solve other, non-quantum partial differential equations, with such approaches collectively referred to as
physics-informed neural networks

(
Raissi et al. 2019
, PINNs,)
.

Despite some initial successes, such scientific machine learning approaches face severe optimization challenges arising from ill-conditioned and non-convex loss landscapes
(
Bukov et al. 2021
;
Krishnapriyan et al. 2021
;
Rathore et al. 2024
)
. One promising approach to address these challenges is to use optimization methods based on natural gradient descent
(
Amari 1998
, NGD,)
, which takes advantage of the geometry of the function space to accelerate convergence. For example, NNWs are most commonly trained with the stochastic reconfiguration algorithm
(
Sorella 2001
)
, which is simply the quantum version of natural gradient descent
(
Pfau et al. 2020
)
. In the PINNs community, natural gradient methods are less common but have been shown to outperform more commonly used optimizers
(
Müller & Zeinhofer 2023
;
Dangel et al. 2024
)
.

Recently, both fields have seen the introduction of
subsampled
natural gradient algorithms that scale to millions of parameters without approximation
(
Chen & Heyl 2024
;
Guzmán-Cordero et al. 2025
)
. These methods take advantage of a low rank structure that arises when the network is trained using mini-batches whose size is smaller than the number of parameters. In fact, such an approach was proposed years earlier by
(
Ren & Goldfarb 2019
)
, but found limited success in traditional machine learning applications. We refer to the basic version of this method as subsampled natural gradient descent, or SNGD. Even more recently, a variant of SNGD has been proposed which can leverage momentum to accelerate the convergence at no extra cost
(
Goldshlager et al. 2024
)
. This method is known as subsampled projected-increment natural gradient descent, or SPRING for short.

Since their introduction, the SNGD and SPRING algorithms have been applied and extended by a variety of follow-up studies
(
Rende et al. 2024
;
Smith et al. 2024
;
Scherbela et al. 2025
;
Li et al. 2022
;
Gu et al. 2025
)
, but a theoretical understanding of these algorithms has been lacking. In fact, most previous analyses of natural gradient methods have avoided the subsampled regime by assuming that either the gradient, the Fisher matrix, or both are available deterministically
Amari 1998
;
Zhang et al. 2019
;
Martens 2020
;
Ren & Goldfarb 2019
;
Wu et al. 2024
. In the rare case that any work has considered a stochastic gradient
and
Fisher matrix, the analysis has either yielded a slow convergence rate or none all
Cai et al. 2019
;
Yang et al. 2020
.
As a result, the following question has remained unanswered:

How can we explain the fast convergence of subsampled natural gradient algorithms for scientific machine learning tasks, namely their superiority relative to stochastic gradient methods and the additional acceleration provided by SPRING?

We provide the first satisfying answers to this question by analyzing SNGD and SPRING for two idealized parametric optimization problems. The basis for both model problems is the general parametric optimization problem

\min_{\theta}\;\mathcal{L}(v_{\theta})

(1)

where
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
are the parameters to be optimized,
v_{\theta}\in\mathcal{H}
is a parameterized function in some Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, and
\mathcal{L}:\mathcal{H}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a loss function defined on the Hilbert space. This form is general enough to include both NNWs and PINNs as special cases.

To facilitate the analysis, we consider the scenario in which the model
v_{\theta}=J\theta
is linear and the loss
\mathcal{L}(v)
is a convex quadratic. We also assume for simplicity that the Hilbert space is
\mathcal{H}=\mathbb{R}^{m}
and the Jacobian
J
is of full column rank. These assumptions lead us to the following model problem:

Problem 1

(Linear least-quadratics (LLQ))
.

Minimize

L_{LQ}(\theta)=\mathcal{L}(J\theta),

(2)

where
J\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
and
\mathcal{L}(v)=\frac{1}{2}v^{\top}Hv+v^{\top}q+c
is a strongly convex quadratic function.

We call this problem
linear least-quadratics
since it is similar to linear least-squares except that the squared error function has been replaced by a general strongly convex quadratic function. LLQ serves as a natural model problem for natural gradient optimization and can also arise from making a Gauss-Newton approximation to the general parametric optimization problem
eq.

1
. We note that while LLQ is formally equivalent to minimizing a general quadratic function of
\theta
, its compositional structure
Q(J\theta)
is important since it enables us to define non-trivial natural gradient algorithms which can leverage the availability of
J
to accelerate convergence.

In the special case that
\mathcal{L}(v)=\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert v-b\right\rVert^{2}
is a squared error function, we recover the standard linear least squares, which we take as our second model problem:

Problem 2

(Linear least-squares (LLS))
.

Minimize

L_{LS}(\theta)=\mathcal{L}(J\theta)

(3)

where
J\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
and
\mathcal{L}(v)=\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert v-b\right\rVert^{2}
is a squared error function.

We use linear least-squares as a starting point to inform our analysis of SNGD and SPRING, and its simple structure enables us to prove stronger results than are possible for the more general case of LLQ. Linear least-squares is also of independent interest as a model problem since it arises as a Gauss-Newton approximation to the general nonlinear least-squares problem
\min_{\theta}{\scriptstyle\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert v_{\theta}-b\right\rVert^{2}}
, which encompasses many machine learning tasks including PINNs.

To further facilitate the analysis, we focus on instances of
1
and
2
which are
consistent
in that the optimal parameters
\theta^{*}
satisfy
v_{\theta}^{*}=\argmin_{v}\mathcal{L}(v)
. This consistency condition is particularly relevant for scientific machine learning since in scientific applications it is often the case that physically meaningful solutions can only be attained when the model
v_{\theta}
is powerful enough to nearly exactly minimize
\mathcal{L}
.
It is also similar to the concept of interpolation which has previously been used to study the convergence of both first- and second-order stochastic optimization methods
Ma et al. 2018
;
Meng et al. 2020
, and in the case of linear least-squares it simply means that the underlying linear system is consistent,
J\theta^{*}=b
.

We now state our contributions:

1.

For consistent linear least-squares, we prove fast convergence rates for both SNGD and SPRING under mild conditions; see
Theorem

1
. These results explain why subsampled natural gradient algorithms can outperform stochastic gradient descent. They also represent the first convergence proof for SPRING in any setting and the first rigorous demonstration that SPRING can accelerate SNGD, achieving up to a square-root improvement in the convergence rate. The rates follow from new equivalences between SNGD and a regularized Kaczmarz method
Goldshlager et al. 2025
and between SPRING and an accelerated regularized Kaczmarz method
(
Dereziński et al. 2025
, ARK,)
. The connection to ARK additionally provides the first principled explanation for the structure of SPRING.

2.

For consistent linear least-quadratics, we prove a fast convergence rate for SNGD under stronger conditions; see
Theorem

4
and
Corollary

5
. This result provides the first explanation for why subsampled natural gradient algorithms can be effective for problems without a least-squares structure, such as when training NNWs. The proof generalizes the analysis of the regularized Kaczmarz method to incorporate the effects of the function space hessian
H
. We supplement our analysis with numerical experiments demonstrating that SPRING can dramatically outperform SNGD for LLQ, making the analysis of SPRING for LLQ a tantalizing direction for future research; see
Figure

1
.

Overall, our work demonstrates the power of viewing subsampled natural gradient algorithms through the lens of randomized linear algebra, using this perspective to provide the first meaningful bounds on their convergence rates. Our approach may also have broader implications for the analysis of subsampled curvature-aware optimization strategies such as subsampled Newton or Gauss-Newton algorithms.

1.1
Related works

In the context of PINNs, one recent work has analyzed the convergence properties of NGD in the deterministic setting
Xu et al. 2024
and another has analyzed a damped Newton method
Rathore et al. 2024
. In the context of NNWs, two previous works have analyzed the convergence properties of stochastic gradient descent
Li et al. 2023
;
Abrahamsen et al. 2024
. Additionally, it has been proposed to apply acceleration techinques directly in the function space to further accelerate the convergence of SPRING
Li et al. 2025
, and it has also been proposed to use Gauss-Newton or Riemannian Newton methods
Webber & Lindsey 2022
;
Armegioiu et al. 2025
. In a more general machine learning context, one previous work analyzed a scheme that is equivalent to our SNGD
Cai et al. 2019
, but attained much weaker bounds on the convergence rate and only considered the least-squares setting. Finally, the previous works
Goldshlager et al. 2024
;
Goldshlager et al. 2025
have also made connections between subsampled natural gradient algorithms and randomized block Kaczmarz methods, but did not identify direct equivalences between the two and did not use these connections to obtain convergence guarantees for either SNGD or SPRING.

Subsampling has also been studied in the context of optimization methods other than natural gradient descent. For example, subsampled proximal point algorithms have been proposed and analyzed for solving stochastic optimization problems with objective functions of the least-squares type
Bertsekas 2011
;
Asi & Duchi 2019
;
Davis & Drusvyatskiy 2019
. Subsampled Newton methods have also been investigated in
Roosta-Khorasani & Mahoney 2016a
;
Roosta-Khorasani & Mahoney 2016b
;
Bottou et al. 2018
;
Bollapragada et al. 2019
, where two separate mini-batches are used to construct the gradient and Hessian approximations to ensure fast convergence.

2
Background

2.1
Neural network wavefunctions

Our first motivating application is the use of neural networks to find ground state wavefunctions for quantum systems
Carleo & Troyer 2017
. In this setting, the wavefunction is parameterized by
\Psi_{\theta}
and ground states are found by minimizing the variational Monte Carlo loss

L_{\rm VMC}(\theta)=\frac{\langle\Psi_{\theta},H\Psi_{\theta}\rangle}{\langle\Psi_{\theta},\Psi_{\theta}\rangle},

(4)

where
H
is the Hamiltonian for the system of interest. To apply natural gradient methods, the parametric model is defined to be the
normalized
wavefunction
v_{\theta}=\Psi_{\theta}/\left\lVert\Psi_{\theta}\right\rVert
and the corresponding function space loss is
\mathcal{L}_{\rm VMC}(v)=\langle v,Hv\rangle
. Clearly
\mathcal{L}_{\rm VMC}
does not exhibit a least-squares structure, making LLQ the appropriate model problem for training NNWs.

2.2
Physics-informed neural networks

Our second motivating application is the use of neural networks to solve boundary value problems

\{\mathcal{D}u=f\text{ on }\Omega
,
u=g\text{ on }\partial\Omega\}
where
\Omega
is a physical domain with boundary
\partial\Omega
and
\mathcal{D}
is a differential operator. PINNs proceed by parameterizing
u:=u_{\theta}
by a neural network and minimizing

L_{\rm PINN}(\theta)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}(\mathcal{D}u_{\theta}(s)-f(s))^{2}{\rm d}s+\frac{w}{2}\int_{\partial\Omega}(u_{\theta}(s)-g(s))^{2}{\rm d}s,

(5)

where
w
is a hyperparameter that controls the relative importance of the two loss terms.
There are multiple ways to split the PINN loss
eq.

5
to obtain a parametric optimization problem, but we focus on the split used by energy natural gradient descent
(
Müller & Zeinhofer 2023
, ENGD,)
since this is the setting in which SNGD and SPRING have been demonstrated to be effective
Guzmán-Cordero et al. 2025
.
This formulation can be summarized as
L_{\rm PINN}(\theta)=\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert v_{\theta}-b\right\rVert^{2}
where

v_{\theta}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{D}u_{\theta}\\
\sqrt{w}\cdot u_{\theta}\end{bmatrix},\quad b=\begin{bmatrix}f\\
\sqrt{w}\cdot g\end{bmatrix}.

(6)

This nonlinear least-squares form motivates the use of LLS as a model for PINNs.

2.3
Natural Gradient Descent

For the general parametric optimization problem
eq.

1
natural gradient descent is defined by the iteration

\theta_{t+1}=\theta_{t}-\eta(J^{\top}J+\lambda I)^{-1}\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}(v_{\theta_{t}}),

(7)

where
J=J_{\theta}v_{\theta_{t}}
is the
model Jacobian
,
\lambda
is a regularization parameter, and
\eta
is a step size. The matrix
J^{\top}J
is known as the Fisher information matrix and encodes the geometry of the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, potentially accelerating the convergence relative to gradient descent. Unfortunately, directly evaluating
eq.

7
has a cost of
\mathcal{O}(n^{3})
due to the matrix inversion, which is far too expensive for modern machine learning applications for which
n
can be in the millions.

2.4
Subsampled natural gradient algorithms

Using the chain rule, we can decompose
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}(v_{\theta_{t}})=J^{\top}r
where
r=\nabla_{v}\mathcal{L}(v_{\theta_{t}})\in\mathcal{H}
is the
function space gradient
, or equivalently the dual of the Fréchet derivative of
\mathcal{L}
evaluated at
v_{\theta_{t}}
. We can then write NGD in the form

\theta_{t+1}=\theta_{t}-\eta(J^{\top}J+\lambda I)^{-1}J^{\top}r:=\theta_{t}-\eta J^{+(\lambda)}r,

(8)

where in the last step we have defined a compressed notation for the regularized pseudoinverse of
J
.

In practice, the values of
J
and
r
are never available over the entire physical domain at once, but are rather sampled at a small number of points for each iteration. Supposing a mini-batch size of
k
and denoting the sampled points collectively by
S
, the quantities that we have available in practice are thus
J_{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times n}
and
r_{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
. Indeed, for both NNWs and PINNs these quantities are routinely computed for finite sample sets
S
by using automatic differentiation libraries to both apply differential operators and take derivatives with respect to the parameters. For the purposes of our analysis, we assume that
S
is always sampled uniformly without replacement from
\set{1,\ldots,m}
.

In the common case that
k\ll n
, significant computational savings can be had by applying the Woodbury matrix identity to
eq.

8
:

J_{S}^{+(\lambda)}=(J_{S}^{\top}J_{S}+\lambda I)^{-1}J_{S}^{\top}=J_{S}^{\top}(J_{S}J_{S}^{\top}+\lambda I)^{-1}.

(9)

Using the final expression the matrix to be inverted now has size
k\times k
and the bottleneck step becomes the formation of
J_{S}J_{S}^{\top}
which has a cost of
O(nk^{2})
: an enormous reduction relative to naïve NGD. The resulting method is known as subsampled natural gradient descent (SNGD) and has been proposed, for example by
(
Ren & Goldfarb 2019
;
Chen & Heyl 2024
)
.

To accelerate the convergence of SNGD,
(
Goldshlager et al. 2024
)
introduced the SPRING algorithm which is essentially a momentum method that is specially designed to be compatible with SNGD. In the SPRING algorithm, a momentum vector
\phi_{t}
is tracked alongside the parameter vector
\theta_{t}
and the two iterates are updated using the equations

\displaystyle\phi_{t+1}

\displaystyle=\mu\phi_{t}+J_{S}^{+(\lambda)}(r_{S}-\mu J_{S}\phi_{t}),

(10)

\displaystyle\theta_{t+1}

\displaystyle=\theta_{t}-\eta\phi_{t+1},

where
\mu\in(0,1)
is a momentum coefficient typically taken to be slightly less than
1
. The SPRING algorithm was originally motivated by intuitive connections to the randomized block Kaczmarz method, but no rigorous connection was established and no convergence guarantees have been provided until this point. To summarize, in
Algorithm

we present unified pseudocode for the SNGD and SPRING algorithms for general parametric optimization problems.

3
Analysis of SNGD and SPRING for Linear Least-Squares

The linear least-squares problem is defined by
v_{\theta}=J\theta
and
\mathcal{L}(v)=\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert v-b\right\rVert^{2}
, and it thus follows that the model Jacobian is
\nabla_{\theta}v_{\theta}=J
and the function space gradient is
\nabla_{v}\mathcal{L}(v_{\theta})=J\theta-b
. As a result, the SNGD algorithm takes the form

\theta_{t+1}=\theta_{t}-\eta J_{S}^{+(\lambda)}(J_{S}\theta_{t}-b_{S}).

(11)

Nearly all previous analyses of SNGD have viewed this iteration through lens of stochastic optimization theory, splitting the update direction as

J^{+(\lambda)}(J_{S}\theta_{t}-b_{S})=[(J_{S}J_{S}^{\top}+\lambda I)^{-1}][J_{S}^{\top}(J_{s}\theta-b_{s})]

(12)

and then attempting to analyze the properties of the stochastic preconditioner
(J_{S}J_{S}^{\top}+\lambda I)^{-1}
and the stochastic gradient
J_{S}^{\top}(J_{s}\theta-b_{s})
. This approach has the major limitation that for small samples sizes, little can be said about the inverse of the subsampled preconditioner. It also makes it difficult to handle the correlation between the two stochastic estimators, which has led most previous analyses to assume separate samples
S,S^{\prime}
for each piece.

We resolve these challenges by instead viewing SNGD as a
randomized block Kaczmarz
method
Needell & Tropp 2014
. Following previous analyses of such methods, we invoke the consistency assumption
J\theta^{*}=b
to rewrite the SNGD iteration
eq.

11
as

\theta_{t+1}=\theta_{t}-\eta(J_{S}^{+(\lambda)}J_{S})(\theta_{t}-\theta^{*}).

(13)

All the stochasticity is now bundled into the term
J_{S}^{+(\lambda)}J_{S}
which is a regularized projector onto the row space of
J_{S}
. Denoting this regularized projector by
P(S)
and subtracting
\theta^{*}
from both sides of
eq.

13
, we can write the SNGD iteration in the form

(\theta_{t+1}-\theta^{*})=(I-\eta P(S))(\theta_{t}-\theta^{*}).

(14)

It is now apparent that
rather than complicating things, the use of the same minibatch
\mathbf{S}
for both the stochastic gradient and the stochastic preconditioner is favorable and results in a consistent reduction of the solution error
. Indeed analyzing
P(S)
as a whole is much more tractable than analyzing the inverse of the stochastic preconditioner alone, and many previous works on randomized Kaczmarz and related methods have analyzed the properties of such projection matrices
Needell & Tropp 2014
;
Gower & Richtárik 2015
;
Dereziński & Rebrova 2024
;
Dereziński et al. 2025
.
Building on this insight we obtain the following results:

Theorem 1

(Equivalences and convergence rates for linear least-squares)
.

For linear least-squares, the SNGD algorithm with
\eta=1
is directly equivalent to the regularized Kaczmarz method of
Goldshlager et al. 2025
. Moreover, The SPRING algorithm is equivalent to the Nesterov-accelerated regularized Kaczmarz (ARK) method of
Dereziński et al. 2025
under an appropriate transformation of the iterates and hyperparameters.

As a result, for consistent problems
J\theta^{*}=b
, the SNGD algorithm with
\eta=1
satisfies

\mathbb{E}\,\left\lVert\theta_{t}-\theta^{*}\right\rVert^{2}\leq(1-\alpha)^{t}\left\lVert\theta_{0}-\theta^{*}\right\rVert^{2}

(15)

where
\overline{P}=\mathbb{E}_{S}\left[P(S)\right]
and
\alpha=\lambda_{\rm min}(\overline{P})
. Furthermore, for appropriate choices of
\eta
and
\mu
SPRING satisfies

\mathbb{E}\,\left\lVert\theta_{t}-\theta^{*}\right\rVert^{2}=\mathcal{O}\left((1-\sqrt{\alpha/\beta})^{t}\right)\left\lVert\theta_{0}-\theta^{*}\right\rVert^{2}

(16)

where
\beta=\lambda_{\rm max}\left(\mathbb{E}_{S}\left[(\overline{P}^{-1/2}P(S)\overline{P}^{-1/2})^{2}\right]\right)
.

As discussed in
(
Goldshlager et al. 2025
, Corollary 3.3)
, the convergence rate
(1-\alpha)^{t}
for SNGD can be much faster than the convergence rate of mini-batch stochastic gradient descent (SGD) for the same problem, which helps to explain the fast convergence of SNGD for PINNs
Guzmán-Cordero et al. 2025
. In particular, the rate of SGD with a mini-batch size of
k
can be at best
(1-k\kappa_{\mathrm{dem}}^{-2}(J))^{t}
where
\kappa_{\rm dem}(J)=\left\lVert J\right\rVert_{F}\left\lVert J^{+}\right\rVert_{2}
is the Demmel condition number of
J
. On the other hand, the parameter
\alpha
in SNGD can be significantly larger than
k\kappa_{\mathrm{dem}}^{-2}(J)
. For example, when the rows of
J
are generated from a Gaussian distribution and the eigenvalues values of
J^{\top}J
decay as
k^{-\beta}
, it holds in the limit
m\rightarrow\infty,\lambda\rightarrow 0
that
\alpha=\Omega(k^{\beta}\kappa_{\mathrm{dem}}^{-2}(J))
. The assumption of Gaussian data is idealized and can be relaxed, for example to handle sub-Gaussian distributions or other types of “incoherent” data
Dereziński & Rebrova 2024
. Furthermore, the assumption of singular value decay is realistic for PINNs
Wang et al. 2022
. The advantage of SNGD over SGD can even extend to inconsistent problems, especially when tail averaging is applied to the iterates as in
Epperly et al. 2024
;
Goldshlager et al. 2024
. In summary:

SNGD converges rapidly for linear least-squares due to its equivalence with the regularized Kaczmarz method.

Additionally, it can be easily verified using
\overline{P}^{-1}\preceq\frac{1}{\alpha}I
that
1\leq\beta\leq\frac{1}{\alpha}
, which implies that the convergence rate of SPRING in
Theorem

1
entails up to a square-root speed up relative to the convergence rate of SNGD. This helps to explain why SPRING can accelerate SNGD for PINNs and provides the first convergence proof for SPRING of any kind. It also explains the empirical observation that the benefits of SPRING are greater for small batch sizes
Guzmán-Cordero et al. 2025
, since for smaller batch sizes
\alpha
is smaller and thus the gap between the
(1-\alpha)^{t}
convergence rate of SNGD and the best-case
(1-\sqrt{\alpha})^{t}
convergence rate of SPRING is more significant. Going beyond the convergence rates, the equivalence between SPRING and ARK suggests that the previously ad hoc structure of the SPRING algorithm arises naturally from viewing it as an accelerated regularized Kaczmarz method. Conversely, using our equivalence proof to translate the ARK method into the form of SPRING yields a new way to formulate ARK which is more concise than previous formulations such as those of
Gower et al. 2018
;
Dereziński et al. 2025
. Lastly, the equivalence between SPRING and ARK suggests that the adaptive momentum technique from
(
Dereziński et al. 2025
)
may be applicable to SPRING as well, potentially paving a path towards a more black-box version of the SPRING optimizer. In summary:

SPRING outperforms SNGD for consistent linear least-squares due to its equivalence with the Nesterov-accelerated regularized Kaczmarz method.

4
Analysis of SNGD for Linear Least-Quadratics

We now turn our attention to the linear least-quadratics
1
which serves as a model for more general parametric optimization problems such as NNWs for which the loss does not exhibit a least-squares structure.
LLQ is defined by
v_{\theta}=J\theta
and
\mathcal{L}(v)=\frac{1}{2}v^{\top}Hv+v^{\top}q+c
and it thus follows that the model Jacobian is
\nabla_{\theta}v_{\theta}=J
and the function space gradient is
\nabla_{v}\mathcal{L}(v_{\theta})=HJ\theta+q
. As a result, the SNGD algorithm takes the form

\theta_{t+1}=\theta_{t}-\eta J_{S}^{+(\lambda)}(H_{S}J\theta_{t}+q_{S}).

(17)

We now show that just as in the case of linear least-squares, we can leverage an appropriate consistency assumption to translate this iteration into a more amenable form where the stochasticity is bundled into the regularized projector
P(S)
. We note that unlike the previous analysis which was drawn from the randomized block Kaczmarz literature, the following analysis is all new.

Our starting point is the observation that the SNGD iteration
eq.

17
is equivalent to a regularized Kaczmarz iteration for the least-squares subproblem
\min_{\theta}\left\lVert J\theta-(HJ\theta+q)\right\rVert^{2}
. To obtain the benefits of the regularized Kaczmarz method, it is thus natural to assume that this subproblem is consistent, namely that
HJ\theta+q\in{\rm range}(J)
. The intuitive meaning of this assumption is that the function space gradient always lies within the tangent space of the parametric model. In the setting of NNWs, this property could hold if the neural network is powerful enough to represent the entire Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, or more realistically if the wavefunction
\Psi_{\theta}
remains within a low-energy subspace that can be fully represented by the neural network. To ensure that this containment property holds in the case of LLQ we introduce the following strong consistency condition:

Assumption 2

(Strong consistency for linear least-quadratics)
.

An LLQ problem is
strongly
consistent if the following two conditions hold:

1.

The problem is consistent, namely
J\theta^{*}=\argmin_{v}\mathcal{L}(v)
.

2.

{\rm range}(HJ)\subseteq{\rm range}(J)
.

Here point (1) ensures that
\nabla_{v}\mathcal{L}(v_{\theta^{*}})=HJ\theta^{*}+b=0
, which implies
HJ\theta+q=HJ(\theta-\theta^{*})
.
Point (2) then ensures that
HJ(\theta-\theta^{*})\in{\rm range}(J)
, which confirms that
HJ\theta+q\in{\rm range}(J)
.
Note that for the special case of linear least-squares,
2
is equivalent to the standard consistency assumption since
H=I\implies{\rm range}(HJ)={\rm range}(J)
.

Under
2
since
HJ\theta+q\in{\rm range}(J)
it follows that
HJ\theta+q=J^{+}J(HJ\theta+q)
and so the SNGD direction can be rewritten as

\displaystyle J_{S}^{+(\lambda)}(H_{S}J\theta_{t}+q_{S})

\displaystyle=J_{S}^{+(\lambda)}(HJ\theta_{t}+q)_{S}

\displaystyle=J_{S}^{+(\lambda)}(JJ^{+}(HJ\theta_{t}+q))_{S}

\displaystyle=P(S)J^{+}(HJ\theta_{t}+q).

Plugging this into
eq.

17
, we can rewrite the entire SNGD iteration as

\theta_{t+1}=\theta_{t}-\eta P(S)J^{+}(HJ\theta_{t}+q).

(18)

As promised, we have now bundled all of the stochasticity into the regularized projector
P(S)
. The result is that
under an appropriate consistency condition, the SNGD update for LLQ arises from applying the regularized projector
\bm{P(S)}
to the deterministic, unregularized natural gradient direction
\bm{J^{+}(HJ\theta_{t}+q)}
. This insight is critical to our analysis of SNGD for LLQ and provides an intuitive explanation for why SNGD can enjoy some of the same benefits as NGD.

4.1
Conditions for the convergence of SNGD for LLQ

Even with
2
and the resulting appealing
eq.

18
, it is not clear whether the combination of subsampling and the anisotropic function-space hessian
H
can lead to convergent algorithms. In fact, it turns out that SNGD can still fail to converge for LLQ when
\lambda
is too small:

Proposition 3

(Conditions for the convergence of SNGD for linear least-quadratics)
.

Consider a fixed instance of
1
for which
2
holds and let
\overline{P}=\mathbb{E}_{S}\left[P(S)\right]
as in
Theorem

1
. Define
M=\overline{P}J^{+}HJ
and let
\xi(\lambda)
be the minimum real part of any eigenvalue of
M
, as a function of
\lambda
. Then:

1.

For fixed
\lambda
, if
\xi(\lambda)<0
then there exists an initial guess
\theta_{0}
such that SNGD is divergent for
every
choice of
\eta>0
.

2.

Conversely, there exists
\lambda_{0}
such that for any
\lambda>\lambda_{0}
,
\xi(\lambda)>0
and SNGD is convergent for a small enough choice of the step size
\eta
and any initial guess
\theta_{0}
.

The proof of
Proposition

3
can be found in
Appendix

B
and numerical examples manifesting the failure condition
\xi(\lambda)<0
can be found in
Appendix

C
.

These results provide a new explanation for the common observation that it is essential to choose
\lambda>0
when training NNWs, beyond maintaining the invertibility of
J_{S}^{\top}J_{S}
or reducing the variance of the iterates.

It is worth noting that without subsampling, the convergence of NGD for LLQ is less nuanced and confirms previous intuitions about NGD; see
Proposition

8
in
Appendix

B
for further discussion of this point.

4.2
Fast convergence of SNGD for LLQ

Proposition

3
poses a hurdle to obtaining an explicit convergence rate for SNGD for LLQ, since it is difficult to determine how large
\lambda
must be to ensure convergence and when
\lambda
is large, the convergence can be slowed substantially.
To facilitate the analysis we thus introduce the additional assumption that
\overline{P}
shares an eigenbasis with
J^{\top}J
.
This guarantees
\xi(\lambda)>0
since
M=\left(\overline{P}(J^{T}J+\lambda I)^{-1}\right)\left(J^{T}HJ\right)
becomes a product of two positive definite factors.
While this assumption is not expected to hold in practice, it is natural in that it holds for two special cases: (i) for arbitrary
J
it holds when sampling
S
from an appropriate determinantal point process, and (ii) when the rows of
J
arise from a Gaussian distribution and it holds asymptotically as
m\rightarrow\infty
; see
Proposition

9
in
Appendix

B
for details.
Concretely we obtain the following main result:

Theorem 4

(Convergence rate of SNGD for linear least-quadratics)
.

Suppose that
2
holds and additionally
\overline{P}
shares an eigenbasis with
J^{\top}J
. Define the positive definite matrix
\overline{Q}=(J^{\top}J)^{-1/2}\overline{P}(J^{\top}J)^{-1/2}
and let

\gamma=1/\lambda_{\rm max}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[(J^{\top}J)^{-1/2}P(S)\overline{Q}^{-1}P(S)(J^{\top}J)^{-1/2}\right]\right).

(19)

Then
\gamma\geq\kappa^{-1}(\overline{Q})
and with step size
\eta=\gamma/\lambda_{\rm max}(\tilde{H})
, the expected squared error of the SNGD iterates for
1
satisfies

\mathbb{E}\,\left\lVert\theta_{t}-\theta^{*}\right\rVert^{2}_{\overline{Q}^{-1}}\leq\left(1-\kappa^{-1}(\tilde{H})\cdot\alpha\cdot\gamma\right)^{t}\left\lVert\theta_{0}-\theta^{*}\right\rVert^{2}_{\overline{Q}^{-1}}.

(20)

This result represents the first convergence proof for SNGD outside of a least-squares setting.
Interestingly, relative to LLS the convergence rate of SNGD is slowed down by a factor of
\gamma
in addition to the expected factor of
\kappa^{-1}(\tilde{H})
. The reason for this extra slow-down is that even with the alignment assumption regarding
\overline{P}
and
J^{\top}J
, the expected step matrix
M
is still not symmetric. To address this issue we measure the solution error in the norm defined by the inverse of
\overline{Q}
, but this worsens the quadratic term in the error bound.
Nonetheless, the convergence rate of SNGD in
eq.

20
can be much faster than that of mini-batch SGD:

Corollary 5

(Convergence rate comparison for linear least-quadratics)
.

Suppose that the conditions of
Theorem

4
hold and additionally the rows of
J
are generated from a Gaussian distribution whose covariance matrix
\Sigma
exhibits polynomial spectral decay, i.e.,
\sigma_{i}(\Sigma)\leq i^{-\beta}\sigma_{1}(\Sigma)
for
\beta>1
, where
\sigma_{i}(\Sigma)
denotes the
i
-th largest singular value of
\Sigma
. Then in the limit of
m\rightarrow\infty
,
\lambda\rightarrow 0
and assuming
k\leq n/2
, it holds
\alpha=\Omega(k^{\beta}\kappa_{\rm dem}^{-2}(J)
and
\gamma=\Omega(\kappa^{2}(J))
. As a result, the SNGD algorithm satisfies

\mathbb{E}\,\left\lVert\theta_{t}-\theta^{*}\right\rVert_{\overline{Q}^{-1}}\leq\left(1-C\kappa^{-1}(\tilde{H})\cdot k^{\beta}\kappa_{\rm dem}^{-2}(J)\cdot\kappa^{-2}(J)\right)^{t}\left\lVert\theta_{0}-\theta^{*}\right\rVert^{2}_{\overline{Q}^{-1}}

(21)

for a universal constant
C>0
. In comparison, the best convergence rate we can attain for mini-batch SGD is
\mathbb{E}\,\left\lVert\theta_{t}-\theta^{*}\right\rVert^{2}\leq(1-\alpha_{\rm SGD})^{t}\left\lVert\theta_{0}-\theta^{*}\right\rVert^{2}
where

\alpha_{\rm SGD}\leq\tilde{C}\kappa(\tilde{H})\cdot k\kappa_{\mathrm{dem}}^{-2}\!\bigl((J^{\top}\tilde{H}J)^{1/2}\bigr)\cdot\kappa^{-1}(J^{\top}\tilde{H}J)

(22)

for
\tilde{C}=\frac{1}{2}\max_{i}\frac{\|(\tilde{H}J)_{i}\|}{\|\tilde{H}\|_{2}\|J_{i}\|}
.

The dependence of the SNGD convergence rate on
k
and
J
is as
k^{\beta}\kappa_{\rm dem}^{-2}(J)\kappa^{-2}(J),
whereas the dependence of the SGD convergence rate is at best
k\kappa_{\rm dem}^{-2}(J)\kappa^{-2}(J)
using
\kappa^{2}_{\rm dem}((J^{\top}\tilde{H}J)^{1/2})\leq\kappa_{\rm dem}^{2}(J)\kappa(\tilde{H})
and
\kappa(J^{\top}\tilde{H}J)\leq\kappa^{2}(J)\kappa(H)
. As such,
when
\bm{k}
and
\bm{\beta}
are even moderately large, SNGD can converge much more rapidly than SGD
. We note as before that the assumption of Gaussian data is idealized and can likely be relaxed, while rapid singular value decay is realistic for NNWs
Park & Kastoryano 2020
. It is also worth noting that as in the case of SNGD, the convergence rate of SGD for LLQ is slower than might be expected. For SGD, the reason for the slow-down lies in the fact that the asymmetric stochastic gradient estimator
J_{S}^{\top}H_{S}J
does not correspond to the gradient of any convex component function. Consequently, the existing analysis of
Needell et al. 2014
which would yield a
\kappa_{\rm dem}^{-2}(J)
dependence is not applicable here. In summary:

Under appropriate conditions, the analysis of the regularized Kaczmarz method can be generalized to prove that SNGD converges rapidly for linear least-quadratics.

The proofs of
Theorem

4
and
Corollary

5
can be found in
Appendix

B
.

5
Towards an Analysis of SPRING for Linear Least-Quadratics

The analysis of SPRING for linear least-quadratics is more challenging due to the interaction between the acceleration scheme and the function space hessian
H
. As such, we for now provide only preliminary explorations. We first note that SPRING can be analyzed easily in two extreme cases:

Proposition 6

(Convergence rate of SPRING for linear least-quadratics in extreme cases)
.

When
k=m
and
\lambda=0
, SPRING becomes equivalent to NGD and its convergence thus follows from
Proposition

8
. Furthermore, when
H=I
, LLQ reduces to LLS and the convergence of SPRING thus follows from
Theorem

1
. In both of these cases, it holds

\mathbb{E}\,\left\lVert\theta_{t}-\theta^{*}\right\rVert^{2}=\mathcal{O}\left(\left(1-\kappa^{-1}(\tilde{H})\sqrt{\alpha/\beta}\right)^{t}\right)\left\lVert\theta_{0}-\theta^{*}\right\rVert^{2}.

(23)

While this convergence rate is appealing, it seems unrealistic to hope that the factor of
\gamma
from
Theorem

4
will disappear when analyzing SPRING. We thus formulate the following conjecture:

Conjecture 7

(Convergence rate of SPRING for linear least-quadratics in general)
.

When
2
holds and additionally
\overline{P}
commutes with
J^{\top}J
, and with an appropriate choice of hyperparameters, SPRING for LLQ satisfies

\mathbb{E}\,\left\lVert\theta_{t}-\theta^{*}\right\rVert^{2}=\mathcal{O}\left((1-\kappa(\tilde{H})^{-1}\sqrt{\alpha/\beta}\cdot\gamma)\right)^{t}\left\lVert\theta_{0}-\theta^{*}\right\rVert^{2}.

(24)

We defer the resolution of this conjecture to future work. In the meantime, we numerically test SGD, SNGD and SPRING for two difficult instances of
1
. The results in
Figure

1
demonstrate that SPRING dramatically outperforms both SGD and SNGD for these problems, providing support for the spirit of our conjecture.
We provide further details on these two experiments and an additional ablation study regarding the batch size
k
in
Appendix

D
. In summary:

SPRING can dramatically outperform SNGD for linear least-quadratics, and future works should aim to provide a theoretical explanation for this phenomenon.

. All methods have their hyperparameters tuned independently for each problem.

Conclusions

In this work we have provided the first fast convergence rates for SNGD, leveraging ideas from randomized linear algebra to explain its effectiveness for both linear least-squares and the more general linear least-quadratics. We have also provided the first convergence analysis for SPRING, proving that it can accelerate SNGD for linear least-squares and explaining why its benefits are amplified for small batch sizes. We emphasize that viewing these algorithms as randomized block Kaczmarz methods has enabled us to obtain convergence guarantees for arbitrary batch sizes and has also explained for the first time why it is beneficial to estimate both the stochastic gradient and the stochastic preconditioner with a single batch of samples.

Looking forward, the analysis of SPRING for linear least-quadratics represents a clear and important direction for further research. It should also be very fruitful to study the convergence of other recently proposed algorithms for training NNWs, such as those of
Li et al. 2025
;
Armegioiu et al. 2025
;
Jiang et al. 2025
, in the setting of LLQ. Going beyond existing algorithms, we expect that the study of optimal row-access methods for LLQ can lead to the development of entirely new algorithms which could ultimately inspire new state-of-the-art optimizers for neural network wavefunctions and other parametric optimization problems. Other promising directions include (i) generalizing our results to inconsistent or non-quadratic parametric optimization problems, and (ii) extending our analytical framework to study the effects of subsampling on optimizers other than natural gradient descent.

Proof of

The equivalence between SNGD and regularized Kaczmarz is direct; compare
eq.

11

Goldshlager et al. 2025
.

For the case of SPRING and ARK, we first reproduce the ARK method as defined by
Dereziński et al. 2025
:

\displaystyle w_{t}

\displaystyle=J_{S}^{+(\lambda)}(J_{S}\tilde{\theta}_{t}-b_{S}),

(25)

\displaystyle\tilde{\phi}_{t+1}

\displaystyle=\mu(\tilde{\phi}_{t}-w_{t}),

\displaystyle\tilde{\theta}_{t+1}

\displaystyle=\tilde{\theta}_{t}-w_{t}+\tilde{\eta}\tilde{\phi}_{t+1}

We use the symbols
\tilde{\theta},\tilde{\phi},\tilde{\eta}
since these variables do not correspond directly to the variables
\theta,\phi,\eta
of SPRING. Indeed, we now show that the SPRING algorithm with initial guess
\theta_{0}
, initial momentum
\phi_{0}=0
, momentum coefficient
\mu
, and step size
\eta
is equivalent to the ARK algorithm
eq.

25
with initial guess
\tilde{\theta}_{0}=\theta_{0}
, initial momentum
\tilde{\phi}_{0}=0
, momentum coefficient
\mu
, and step size
\tilde{\eta}=1-\frac{1-\eta}{\mu}
in that the resulting iterates satisfy
\tilde{\phi}_{t}=-\mu\phi_{t}
,
\tilde{\theta}_{t}=\theta_{t}-\mu\phi_{t}
.

Proceeding by induction, as a base case we have
\tilde{\phi}_{0}=-\mu\phi_{0}=0
and
\tilde{\theta}_{0}=\theta_{0}-\mu\phi_{0}=\theta_{0}
. Now, suppose these identities hold for some fixed
t\geq 0
. Then calculate using
eq.

25
and
eq.

10

\displaystyle\tilde{\phi}_{t+1}

\displaystyle=\mu(\tilde{\phi}_{t}-J_{S}^{+(\lambda)}(J_{S}\tilde{\theta}_{t}-b_{s}))

\displaystyle=-\mu(\mu\phi_{t}+J_{S}^{+(\lambda)}(J_{S}(\theta_{t}-\mu\phi_{t})-b_{S}))

\displaystyle=-\mu(\mu\phi_{t}+J_{S}^{+(\lambda)}(J_{S}\theta_{t}-b_{S}-\mu J_{S}\phi_{t}))

\displaystyle=-\mu\phi_{t+1}.

Using the above, proceed to verify

\displaystyle\tilde{\theta}_{t+1}

\displaystyle=\tilde{\theta}_{t}-J_{S}^{+(\lambda)}(J_{S}\tilde{\theta}_{t}-b_{s})+\tilde{\eta}\tilde{\phi}_{t+1}

\displaystyle=\theta_{t}-\mu\phi_{t}-J_{S}^{+(\lambda)}(J_{S}\theta_{t}-b_{S}-\mu J_{S}\phi_{t})-\left(1-\frac{1-\eta}{\mu}\right)\mu\phi_{t+1}

\displaystyle=\theta_{t}-\phi_{t+1}-\mu\phi_{t+1}+(1-\eta)\phi_{t+1}

\displaystyle=(\theta_{t}-\eta\phi_{t+1})-\mu\phi_{t+1}

\displaystyle=\theta_{t+1}-\mu\phi_{t+1}.

This completes the inductive step.

The convergence rate for SNGD is similar to previous analyses of regularized Kaczmarz methods
Goldshlager et al. 2025
;
Dereziński et al. 2025

J\theta^{*}=b
the SNGD iteration for LLS is rewritten as

\theta_{t+1}-\theta^{*}=(I-P(S))(\theta_{t}-\theta^{*}).

(26)

\mathbb{E}\,\left\lVert\theta_{t+1}-\theta^{*}\right\rVert^{2}=(\theta_{t}-\theta^{*})^{\top}\mathbb{E}\left[(I-P(S))^{2}\right](\theta_{t}-\theta^{*})

(27)

The expectation can then be bounded using
P(S)^{2}\preceq P(S)
, yielding

\mathbb{E}\left[(I-P(S))^{2}\right]\preceq I-2\overline{P}+\overline{P}=I-\overline{P}.

(28)

The convergence rate of
(1-\alpha)^{t}
follows.

For SPRING, we invoke previous analyses of ARK to obtain the convergence bound. See for example equation (1) of

Dereziński et al. 2024
)

(
Gower et al. 2018
;
Richtárik & Takác 2020
)
for previous discussions.

Appendix B
Proofs for LLQ

Proof of
Proposition

3
.

Recall that under
2
the SNGD iteration can be written as

\theta_{t+1}=\theta_{t}-\eta P(S)J^{+}(HJ\theta_{t}+q).

(29)

Subtracting
\theta^{*}
from both sides and using
HJ\theta+q=HJ(\theta-\theta^{*})
it follows

(\theta_{t+1}-\theta^{*})=(I-\eta P(S)J^{+}HJ)(\theta_{t}-\theta^{*}).

(30)

The expected error thus evolves as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\theta_{t+1}-\theta^{*}\right]

\displaystyle=(I-\eta\overline{P}J^{+}HJ)(\theta_{t}-\theta^{*})

\displaystyle=(I-\eta M)(\theta_{t}-\theta^{*}),

and iterating this calculation implies

\mathbb{E}\left[\theta_{t}-\theta^{*}\right]=(I-\eta M)^{t}(\theta_{0}-\theta^{*}).

(31)

If
\xi(\lambda)>0
and
M
thus has an eigenvalue with negative real part, it follows that there exists an eigenvalue of
I-\eta M
that lies outside the unit circle in the complex plane for any choice of
\eta>0
, leading the expectation of the SNGD iterate to diverge when the initial guess contains a component of the corresponding eigenvector. This proves the first statement of the proposition.

Regarding the second statement, note first that

\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow\infty}\lambda\overline{P}=\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow\infty}\lambda\mathbb{E}_{S}\left[J_{S}^{\top}(J_{S}J_{S}^{\top}+\lambda I)^{-1}J_{S}\right]=\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{E}_{S}\left[J_{S}^{\top}(I+J_{S}J_{S}^{\top}/\lambda)^{-1}J_{S}\right]=\frac{k}{m}J^{\top}J.

(32)

It follows that

\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow\infty}\lambda M=\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow\infty}\lambda\overline{P}J^{+}HJ=\frac{k}{m}J^{\top}HJ\succeq 0.

(33)

Similarly

\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow\infty}\lambda(M^{\top}+M)=\frac{2k}{m}J^{\top}HJ\succeq 0,

(34)

and so there exists
\lambda_{0}
such that for
\lambda>\lambda_{0}
it holds
M^{\top}+M\succeq 0
.

Next, write the expected squared error of SNGD in terms of M:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\,\left\lVert\theta_{t+1}-\theta^{*}\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle=(\theta_{t}-\theta^{*})^{\top}(I-P(S)J^{+}HJ)^{\top}(I-P(S)J^{+}HJ)(\theta_{t}-\theta^{*})

\displaystyle=(\theta_{t}-\theta^{*})^{\top}\left(I-\eta(M^{\top}+M)+\eta^{2}B\right)(\theta_{t}-\theta^{*})

where
B=\mathbb{E}_{S}\left[(J^{+}HJ)^{\top}P(S)^{2}J^{+}HJ\right]
. For
\lambda>\lambda_{0}
we already have
M^{\top}+M\succeq 0
and thus for small enough
\eta
it holds
\left\lVert I-\eta(M^{\top}+M)+\eta^{2}B\right\rVert<1
, making SNGD convergent.
∎

Proposition 8

(Convergence of NGD)
.

Consider a consistent LLQ problem
J\theta^{*}=\argmin_{v}\mathcal{L}(v)
and let
\tilde{H}=(J^{\top}J)^{-1/2}J^{\top}HJ(J^{\top}J)^{-1/2}
be the projection of
H
onto the column space of
J

Then the NGD algorithm with step size
\eta=1/\lambda_{\max}(\tilde{H})

\left\lVert\theta_{t}-\theta^{*}\right\rVert_{J^{\top}J}\leq(1-\kappa^{-1}(\tilde{H}))^{t}\left\lVert\theta_{0}-\theta^{*}\right\rVert_{J^{\top}J}.

(35)

Compared with the gradient descent, the rate is improved from
1-\kappa^{-1}(J^{T}HJ)
to
1-\kappa^{-1}(\tilde{H})
, effectively removing the conditioning of
J
from the problem and confirming the standard intuition behind NGD. The proof of

8
can be found in
Appendix

B
.

Proof of
Proposition

8
.

Since
J\theta^{*}=\argmin_{v}\mathcal{L}(v)
it holds
HJ\theta+q=HJ(\theta-\theta^{*}).
Consider the evolution of the error in

\displaystyle\theta_{t+1}-\theta^{*}

\displaystyle=\theta_{t}-\eta J^{+}(HJ\theta_{t}+q)-\theta^{*}

\displaystyle=(\theta_{t}-\theta^{*})-\eta J^{+}HJ(\theta_{t}-\theta^{*})

Multiplying on the left by
(J^{\top}J)^{1/2}

(J^{\top}J)^{-1/2}(J^{\top}J)^{1/2}

\displaystyle(J^{\top}J)^{1/2}(\theta_{t+1}-\theta^{*})

Proposition 9

(Sufficient conditions for
Theorem

4
)
.

The condition that
\overline{P}
shares an eigenbasis with
J^{\top}J
holds in two special cases:

1.

For arbitrary
J

S

k\text{-}{\rm DPP}(JJ^{\top}+\lambda I)

\Pr[S]=\frac{\det(J_{S}J_{S}^{\top}+\lambda I)}{\sum_{|S^{\prime}|=k}\det(J_{S^{\prime}}J_{S^{\prime}}^{\top}+\lambda I)}.

(37)

When the rows of

Proof.

For the case of DPP sampling, the expectation matrix
\overline{P}
can be calculated explicitly and is known to share an eigenbasis with
J^{\top}J
; see for example eq. (E.7) of
Goldshlager et al. 2024

For the case of Gaussian data, it is known that in the limit
m\rightarrow\infty

becomes equivalent to a Gaussian sketch of the Cholesky factor
L
of the covariance matrix of the Gaussian distribution; see the proof of Theorem 3.2 in
Goldshlager et al. 2025

shares an eigenbasis with
L^{T}L
; this is for example a consequence of Lemma 4.1 of
Dereziński et al. 2024
. Finally, in the limit
m\rightarrow\infty
the covariance estimator
\frac{1}{m}J^{\top}J
converges to the covariance matrix
L^{\top}L
. Thus
\overline{P}
,
L^{\top}L
, and
J^{\top}J
all share an eigenbasis in the limit.
∎

Proof of
Theorem

4
.

First, the bound
\gamma\geq\kappa^{-1}(\overline{Q})
holds since

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[(J^{\top}J)^{-1/2}P(S)\overline{Q}^{-1}P(S)(J^{\top}J)^{-1/2}\right]

\displaystyle\preceq\frac{1}{\lambda_{\rm min}(\overline{Q})}\mathbb{E}\left[(J^{\top}J)^{-1/2}P(S)^{2}(J^{\top}J)^{-1/2}\right]

\displaystyle\preceq\frac{1}{\lambda_{\rm min}(\overline{Q})}\mathbb{E}\left[(J^{\top}J)^{-1/2}\overline{P}(J^{\top}J)^{-1/2}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\lambda_{\rm min}(\overline{Q})}\overline{Q}

\displaystyle\preceq\kappa(\overline{Q})I.

For the SNGD convergence bound, recall from the proof of
Proposition

3
that the SNGD update can be written as

\theta_{t+1}-\theta^{*}=(I-\eta P(S)J^{+}HJ)(\theta_{t}-\theta^{*}).

(38)

Define
Q=P(S)(J^{\top}J)^{-1}
so that this can be condensed to

\theta_{t+1}-\theta^{*}=(I-\eta QJ^{\top}HJ)(\theta_{t}-\theta^{*}).

(39)

Note that
\mathbb{E}\left[Q\right]:=\overline{Q}=\overline{P}(J^{\top}J)^{-1}=(J^{\top}J)^{-1/2}\overline{P}(J^{\top}J)^{-1/2}\succeq 0
. We exploit this property by taking the expectation of the squared
\overline{Q}^{-1}
norm of both sides, yielding

\mathbb{E}\,\left\lVert\theta_{t+1}-\theta^{*}\right\rVert^{2}_{\overline{Q}^{-1}}=(\theta_{t}-\theta^{*})^{\top}(\overline{Q}^{-1}-2\eta J^{\top}HJ+\eta^{2}J^{\top}HJ\mathbb{E}\left[Q^{\top}\overline{Q}^{-1}Q\right]J^{\top}HJ)(\theta_{t}-\theta^{*}).

(40)

The choice of the
\overline{Q}^{-1}
norm has ensured that every term in this expression is positive definite, which is the key to making the analysis go through. For the quadratic term note

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[Q^{\top}\overline{Q}^{-1}Q\right]

\displaystyle=(J^{\top}J)^{-1/2}\;\mathbb{E}\left[(J^{\top}J)^{-1/2}P(S)\overline{Q}^{-1}P(S)(J^{\top}J)^{-1/2}\right](J^{\top}J)^{-1/2}

\displaystyle\preceq(J^{\top}J)^{-1}/\gamma.

Thus when
\eta=\gamma/\lambda_{\rm max}(\tilde{H})
it holds

\displaystyle\eta^{2}J^{\top}HJ\mathbb{E}\left[Q^{\top}\overline{Q}^{-1}Q\right]J^{\top}HJ

\displaystyle\preceq\eta^{2}J^{\top}HJ(J^{\top}J)^{-1}J^{\top}HJ/\gamma

\displaystyle\preceq\eta^{2}J^{\top}H^{2}J/\gamma

\displaystyle\preceq\eta J^{\top}HJ.

Returning to the expected squared error and substituting this result yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\,\left\lVert\theta_{t+1}-\theta^{*}\right\rVert^{2}_{\overline{Q}^{-1}}

\displaystyle\leq(\theta_{t}-\theta^{*})^{\top}(\overline{Q}^{-1}-\eta J^{\top}HJ)(\theta_{t}-\theta^{*})

\displaystyle\leq(\theta_{t}-\theta^{*})^{\top}(\overline{Q}^{-1}-\kappa^{-1}(\tilde{H})\cdot(J^{\top}J)\cdot\gamma)(\theta_{t}-\theta^{*})

\displaystyle\leq(\theta_{t}-\theta^{*})^{\top}(\overline{Q}^{-1}-\kappa^{-1}(\tilde{H})\cdot\overline{P}\overline{Q}^{-1}\cdot\gamma)(\theta_{t}-\theta^{*})

\displaystyle=(1-\kappa^{-1}(\tilde{H})\cdot\alpha\cdot\gamma)\left\lVert\theta_{t}-\theta^{*}\right\rVert_{\overline{Q}^{-1}}.

The main result follows by iterating this bound.
∎

To prove
Corollary

5
, we need the following result regarding the ordering of the diagonal entries of the expected projector
\overline{P}
of a diagonal matrix:

Lemma 10
.

Let
D={\rm diag}(d_{1},\ldots,d_{n})
with
d_{1}\geq\ldots\geq d_{n}
. Let
\overline{P}=\mathbb{E}\left[(ZD)^{+}(ZD)\right]
where
Z
is a
k\times n
random Gaussian sketch matrix. Then
\overline{P}
is diagonal by
Proposition

9
. Furthermore, its diagonal entries
p_{1},\ldots,p_{n}
satisfy
p_{1}\geq\ldots\geq p_{n}
.

Proof of
Lemma

10
.

In general, the diagonal entry
p_{i}
takes the form

p_{i}=e_{i}^{\top}\mathbb{E}\left[DZ^{\top}(ZD^{2}Z^{\top})^{+}(ZD)\right]e_{i}=d_{i}^{2}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[z_{i}^{\top}(ZD^{2}Z^{\top})^{+}z_{i}\right]

(41)

where
z_{i}
is column
i
of
Z
. Now fix some
i<j
and consider
\tilde{D}=D-e_{i}e_{i}^{\top}(d_{i}-d_{j})
, so that the diagonal entries of
\tilde{D}
are the same as
D
except that its
i
th entry is equal to
d_{j}
instead of
d_{i}
. Similarly let
\tilde{P}=\mathbb{E}\left[(Z\tilde{D})^{+}(Z\tilde{D})\right]
. Note that
D\succeq\tilde{D}
and symmetry it holds that
\tilde{p}_{i}=\tilde{p}_{j}
. Furthermore, for any
\ell\neq i
it holds

\tilde{p}_{\ell}=\tilde{d}_{i}^{2}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[z_{i}^{\top}(Z\tilde{D}^{2}Z^{\top})^{+}z_{i}\right]\geq d_{i}^{2}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[z_{i}^{\top}(ZD^{2}Z^{\top})^{+}z_{i}\right]=p_{\ell}

(42)

using
\tilde{d}_{i}=d_{i}
and
D\succeq\tilde{D}
. Additionally, it is easy to verify that
\mathrm{Tr}(D)=\mathrm{Tr}(\tilde{D})=k
which implies
\tilde{p}_{i}\leq p_{i}
to keep the trace constant. Putting these results together we have

p_{j}\leq\tilde{p}_{j}=\tilde{p}_{i}\leq p_{i},

(43)

and since this holds for arbitrary
i<j
it follows
p_{1}\geq\ldots\geq p_{n}
.
∎

We can now prove the corollary:

Proof of
Corollary

5
.

We first prove the convergence rate for SNGD.
It has been shown in
(
Goldshlager et al. 2025
, Corollary 3.3)
that under the conditions of
Corollary

5
,
\alpha=\Omega(k^{\beta}\kappa^{-1}_{\rm dem}(J))
.
To bound
\gamma
, we first recall
\gamma\geq\kappa^{-1}(\overline{Q})
from
Theorem

4
and thus turn our attention to bounding
\kappa(\overline{Q})
. Under our assumption of Gaussian data, it is known that a uniform row sample from
J
becomes equivalent to a random Gaussian sketch of
L
where
\Sigma=L^{\top}L
is the Cholesky decomposition of the covariance matrix
(
Goldshlager et al. 2025
, proof of Theorem 3.2)
. Additionally, Lemma 4.1 of
Dereziński & Rebrova 2024
shows replacing
L
with the diagonal matrix of its singular values does not change the spectrum of
\overline{P}
. Using the commutation result of
Proposition

9
and
\overline{Q}=\overline{P}(J^{\top}J)^{-1}=\frac{1}{m}\overline{P}(L^{\top}L)^{-1}
it follows that replacing
L
with the diagonal matrix of its singular values does not change the spectrum of
\overline{Q}
either. As a result, it suffices to prove a bound on
\kappa(\overline{Q})
in the simplified setting of Gaussian sketches of a diagonal matrix
D
.

Assume without loss of generality that
D={\rm diag}(d_{1},\ldots,d_{n})
where
d_{1}\geq\ldots\geq d_{n}
. Furthermore, let
\overline{P}={\rm diag}(p_{1},\ldots,p_{n})
and recall by
Lemma

10
that
p_{1}\geq\ldots p_{n}
. It then holds that
\kappa(\overline{Q})=(p_{i}/d_{i}^{2})/(p_{j}/d_{j}^{2})
for some
i,j
.
Then if
i\leq j
we can bound
\kappa(\overline{Q})\leq p_{i}/p_{j}\leq\kappa(\overline{P})
, whereas if
i\geq j
we can bound
\kappa(\overline{Q})\leq d_{j}^{2}/d_{i}^{2}\leq\kappa^{2}(\overline{D})
. Translating back to the original problem, it follows that

\kappa(\overline{Q})\leq\max(\kappa(\overline{P}),\kappa^{2}(J))
.
In the Gaussian case with fast spectral decay,
\kappa^{2}(J)
is on the same order as
\kappa_{\rm dem}^{2}(J)
and
\kappa(\overline{P})\leq 1/\alpha\ll\kappa_{\rm dem}^{2}(J)
, so it follows
\kappa^{-1}(\overline{Q})=\Omega(\kappa^{-2}(J))
. Combining this with the earlier bound on
\alpha
we have
\alpha\cdot\gamma=\Omega(k^{\beta}\kappa_{\rm dem}^{-2}(J)\cdot\kappa^{-2}(J))
, and the rate of SNGD follows.

We next analyze the convergence rate of mini-batch SGD. First consider the standard SGD update with batch size
k=1
, given by

\theta_{t+1}=\theta_{t}-\frac{\eta}{w_{i}}J_{i}^{\top}(H_{i}J\theta_{t}+q_{i}),

(44)

where
J_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times n}
and
H_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
denote the
i
-th row of the matrices
J
and
H
, respectively. The scalar
w_{i}>0
is a non-negative weight, and
\eta>0
is the step size.

Following
Needell et al. 2014
, we set
w_{i}
proportional to the local Lipschitz constants, specifically

w_{i}=\frac{L_{i}}{\bar{L}},\qquad L_{i}:=\|J_{i}^{\top}H_{i}J\|_{2},\quad\bar{L}:=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}L_{i}.

With this choice, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{(w)}[\|\theta_{t+1}-\theta^{*}\|^{2}]=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{(w)}\!\left[\|\theta_{t}-\tfrac{\eta}{w_{i}}J_{i}^{\top}(H_{i}J\theta_{t}+q_{i})-\theta^{*}\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|\theta_{t}-\theta^{*}\|^{2}-2\mathbb{E}^{(w)}\!\left[\big\langle\theta_{t}-\theta^{*},\tfrac{\eta}{w_{i}}J_{i}^{\top}(H_{i}J\theta_{t}+q_{i})\big\rangle\right]

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}^{(w)}\!\left[\tfrac{\eta^{2}}{w_{i}^{2}}\|J_{i}^{\top}(H_{i}J\theta_{t}+q_{i})\|^{2}\right].

At this stage, the classical analysis
Needell et al. 2014
;
Garrigos & Gower 2023
relies on convexity, but here
J_{i}^{\top}H_{i}J
is asymmetric and does not correspond to the gradient of any convex component function. Using instead the estimator’s unbiasedness together with the local Lipschitz constant
L_{i}
(which defines
w_{i}
), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{(w)}[\|\theta_{t+1}-\theta^{*}\|^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq\|\theta_{t}-\theta^{*}\|^{2}-\tfrac{2\eta}{m}\!\left\langle\theta_{t}-\theta^{*},J^{\top}(HJ\theta_{t}+q)\right\rangle

\displaystyle+\Big\|\mathbb{E}^{(w)}\!\Big[\tfrac{2\eta^{2}}{w_{i}^{2}}(J_{i}^{\top}H_{i}J)^{\top}(J_{i}^{\top}H_{i}J)\Big]\Big\|_{2}\|\theta_{t}-\theta^{*}\|^{2}

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}^{(w)}\!\Big[\tfrac{2\eta^{2}}{w_{i}^{2}}\|J_{i}^{\top}(H_{i}J\theta^{*}+q_{i})\|^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle\leq\Big(1-\tfrac{2\eta\lambda_{\min}(J^{\top}HJ)}{m}-2\eta^{2}\hat{L}^{2}\Big)\|\theta_{t}-\theta^{*}\|^{2},

where
\hat{L}^{2}:=\left\|\mathbb{E}^{(w)}\!\Big[\tfrac{1}{w_{i}^{2}}(J_{i}^{\top}H_{i}J)^{\top}(J_{i}^{\top}H_{i}J)\Big]\right\|_{2}=\left\|\mathbb{E}\!\Big[\tfrac{1}{w_{i}}(J_{i}^{\top}H_{i}J)^{\top}(J_{i}^{\top}H_{i}J)\Big]\right\|_{2}
and we assume
HJ\theta^{*}+q=0
.
The optimal rate is then achieved at step size
\eta=\frac{\mu}{2m\hat{L}^{2}}
, and the SGD iteration in
eq.

44
satisfies

\mathbb{E}\,\|\theta_{t+1}-\theta^{*}\|^{2}\leq\left(1-\alpha_{\rm SGD}\right)\|\theta_{0}-\theta^{*}\|^{2}\text{ where }\alpha_{\rm SGD}=\frac{\lambda_{\min}^{2}(J^{\top}HJ)}{2m^{2}\hat{L}^{2}}.

(45)

To obtain an upper bound for
\alpha_{\rm SGD}
we must lower bound
\hat{L}^{2}
. To this end define
\tilde{C}=\frac{1}{2}\max_{i}\frac{\|(\tilde{H}J)_{i}\|}{\|\tilde{H}\|_{2}\|J_{i}\|}
. Then,

\displaystyle\hat{L}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|\mathbb{E}\left[\tfrac{1}{w_{i}}J^{\top}H_{i}^{\top}J_{i}J_{i}^{\top}H_{i}J\right]\right\|_{2}=\bar{L}\left\|J^{\top}\mathbb{E}\left[H_{i}^{\top}H_{i}\frac{\|J_{i}\|_{2}^{2}}{\|J_{i}^{\top}(HJ)_{i}\|_{2}}\right]J\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\geq\bar{L}\left\|J^{\top}\mathbb{E}\left[H_{i}^{\top}H_{i}\frac{\|J_{i}\|_{2}}{\|(\tilde{H}J)_{i}\|_{2}}\right]J\right\|_{2}\geq\tfrac{\bar{L}}{2\tilde{C}\|\tilde{H}\|_{2}}\|J^{\top}\mathbb{E}[H_{i}^{\top}H_{i}]J\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{\bar{L}}{2m\tilde{C}\|\tilde{H}\|_{2}}\|J^{\top}H^{2}J\|_{2}\geq\frac{\bar{L}}{2m\tilde{C}\kappa(\tilde{H})}\|J^{\top}HJ\|_{2},

where we use
2
to argue that
\tilde{H}J=HJ
in the first inequality and
\|J^{\top}H^{2}J\|_{2}\geq\lambda_{\min}(\tilde{H})\|J^{T}HJ\|_{2}
in the last inequality.
Substituting this bound into
eq.

45
, the convergence parameter is bounded by

\alpha_{\rm SGD}\leq\tilde{C}\kappa(\tilde{H})\cdot\frac{\lambda_{\min}(J^{\top}HJ)}{m\overline{L}}\cdot\kappa^{-1}(J^{\top}HJ).

When
H_{i}JJ_{i}^{\top}\geq 0
, we have
L_{i}=H_{i}JJ_{i}^{\top}=\mathrm{tr}(J_{i}^{\top}H_{i}J)
, which yields

\bar{L}=\tfrac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathrm{tr}(J_{i}^{\top}H_{i}J)=\tfrac{1}{m}\mathrm{tr}(J^{\top}HJ)=\tfrac{1}{m}\|(J^{\top}HJ)^{1/2}\|_{F}^{2}.

Thus,
\bar{L}\geq\tfrac{1}{m}\|(J^{\top}HJ)^{1/2}\|_{F}^{2}
and for
k=1
we obtain the final bound

\alpha_{\rm SGD}\leq\tilde{C}\kappa(\tilde{H})\cdot\kappa_{\mathrm{dem}}^{-2}\!\bigl((J^{\top}HJ)^{1/2}\bigr)\cdot\kappa^{-1}(J^{\top}HJ).

(46)

Lastly, for mini-batch SGD with batch size
k>1
it is shown in
Jain et al. 2018
that the convergence rate can scale at most linearly with
k
, which implies that in general

\alpha_{\rm SGD}\leq\tilde{C}\kappa(\tilde{H})\cdot k\kappa_{\mathrm{dem}}^{-2}\!\bigl((J^{\top}HJ)^{1/2}\bigr)\cdot\kappa^{-1}(J^{\top}HJ).

∎

Proof of
Proposition

6
.

First, when
k=m
and
\lambda=0
SPRING becomes equivalent to NGD since in this case
J_{S}^{+(\lambda)}J_{S}=J^{+}J=I
and thus the momentum update takes the form

\displaystyle\phi_{t+1}

\displaystyle=\mu\phi_{t}+J^{+}(HJ\theta_{t}+q-\mu J\phi_{t})

\displaystyle=\mu(I-J^{+}J)\phi_{t}+J^{+}(HJ\theta_{t}+q)

\displaystyle=J^{+}(HJ\theta_{t}+q).

It follows that in this case SPRING converges as

\left\lVert\theta_{t}-\theta^{*}\right\rVert_{J^{\top}J}\leq(1-\kappa^{-1}(\tilde{H}))^{t}\left\lVert\theta_{0}-\theta^{*}\right\rVert_{J^{\top}J}.

(47)

Second, when
H=I

1
reduces to
2
and thus in this case SPRING satisfies the convergence bound

\mathbb{E}\,\left\lVert\theta_{t}-\theta^{*}\right\rVert^{2}=\mathcal{O}\left((1-\sqrt{\alpha/\beta})^{t}\right)\left\lVert\theta_{0}-\theta^{*}\right\rVert^{2}.

(48)

Noting that when
k=m
we have
\alpha=\beta=1
and when
H=I
we have
\kappa^{-1}(\tilde{H})=1
we can unite these two rates by putting them both in the form required by the proposition, namely

\mathbb{E}\,\left\lVert\theta_{t}-\theta^{*}\right\rVert^{2}=\mathcal{O}\left((1-\kappa^{-1}(\tilde{H})\sqrt{\alpha/\beta})^{t}\right)\left\lVert\theta_{0}-\theta^{*}\right\rVert^{2}.

(49)

∎
