Hookworm infestation in the biliary tract: a case report
Authors: Saroj Kumar Gupta, Sangita Sharma, Rabin Hamal, Hari Prasad Kattel, Naresh Bahadur Khadka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHookworms are parasitic nematodes that primarily infect humans in impoverished communities. They attach to the intestines to feed on blood, which results in anemia and other health complications. While parasitic infections of the biliary tract can occur and lead to significant issues such as cholangitis, pancreatitis, and biliary obstruction, hookworms are not documented to be associated with these biliary infestations.Case presentationA 53-year-old female Tibeto-Burman ethnicity following Buddhism, with a significant smoking history and no alcohol consumption presented to Tribhuvan University Teaching Hospital with an 8-month history of progressively worsening abdominal pain, fever, decreased appetite, nausea, and vomiting. Physical examination revealed tenderness in the right upper quadrant and positive Murphy’s sign, while laboratory tests indicated elevated liver enzymes, high eosinophils count, prolonged prothrombin time, and signs of inflammation. Imaging studies revealed cholelithiasis and choledocholithiasis, along with the unusual finding of a live worm in the biliary tract during an endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP), which was later confirmed as a hookworm infestation. The patient was treated with a comprehensive antiparasitic regimen (Metronidazole 400 mg), along with pain management (Tramadol hydrochloride, and Acetaminophen) and gastrointestinal support (Cremaffin syrup). After a week, she reported significant improvement and was discharged, with subsequent follow-up showing resolution of symptoms along with marked improvement in liver function tests and normal eosinophil count.ConclusionThis instance highlights the fact that hookworms can cause obstructive illness even though they are rare in the biliary system. It emphasizes the importance of timely endoscopic examination and focused antiparasitic therapy, as well as the necessity of taking into account atypical parasitic causes when symptoms cannot be adequately explained by usual biliary disorders.Keywords: Cholelithiasis, Hepatobiliary, Hookworm, Nepal

Introduction

Hookworms are nematodes belonging to the Strongyloidea superfamily, specifically the Ancylostomatidae family. The two main genera that infect people are Ancylostoma and Necator. The oral features of these parasites make them unique; in particular, they have cutting plates or teeth lining their buccal capsules, which they employ to attach themselves to the host’s intestines and consume blood. Anemia and associated signs and symptoms such as abdominal pain, nausea, and anorexia in afflicted persons are caused by this feeding process, which is a major contributor to the pathogenicity of hookworm infections [1]. Hookworm infection is a widespread chronic condition predominantly impacting impoverished communities, primarily in Asia and sub-Saharan Africa. Transmission rates are highest in coastal areas with sandy soils conducive to the survival of infective larvae. Key contributing factors to the prevalence of human hookworm infections include poor socioeconomic status, inadequate sanitation, improper sewage disposal, unsanitary health practices, and consumption of unsafe raw food [2, 3]. Hookworm larvae in their third stage first induce developmental stoppage in the soil before entering the human host through the skin. After entry, they migrate to the lungs within 10 days, leading to symptoms such as cough and sore throat, resembling Löffler’s syndrome due to eosinophilia. Though hookworm pneumonitis is usually mild, it can persist for over a month until the larvae move to the gastrointestinal tract. A. duodenale larvae can also infect humans through oral ingestion, resulting in Wakana disease, which manifests as nausea, vomiting, pharyngeal irritation, cough, dyspnea, and hoarseness [2].

Hookworm infection is endemic in Nepal as part of the broader burden of soil-transmitted helminth (STH) infections. Community-based studies from different ecological regions of Nepal have documented variable but persistent prevalence of hookworms (Ancylostoma duodenale and Necator americanus), particularly in rural and peri-urban agricultural populations where poor sanitation, barefoot farming, and inadequate hygiene practices are common. Although national deworming programs have reduced intensity, hookworm transmission continues in several communities, indicating ongoing endemicity in the region [4].

Together with the roundworm Ascaris lumbricoides and the whipworm Trichuris trichiura, these two hookworms are frequently referred to as soil-transmitted helminths [5]. A. lumbricoides, Clonorchis sinensis, Fasciola species, Opisthorchis species, Metorchis conjunctus, and Dicrocoelium dendriticum are frequently responsible for biliary tract infestations.

In rare cases, parasites like Strongyloides stercoralis have also been seen to affect the biliary system. Biliary colic and obstructive jaundice can occur due to A. lumbricoides, which typically reside in the small intestine but can migrate to the bile duct through the duodenal papilla. This can lead to serious complications such as cholangitis and cholangiocarcinoma. The ability of these parasites to block the bile duct emphasizes the importance of effective detection and treatment of both transient and chronic infections in the biliary system. Notably, hookworm infection is not typically associated with bile duct infestations This case is unique because it documents a live hookworm causing biliary tract infestation with choledocholithiasis, identified incidentally during ERCP—an extremely rare and previously undocumented presentation from Nepal [6, 7].

The objective of this case report is to describe an extremely rare presentation of hookworm infestation that was detected during ERCP. Reporting this case is to increase clinical awareness that, while being traditionally intestinal, hookworm can appear as an uncommon cause of biliary obstruction, especially in endemic areas, and should be taken into consideration when making a differential diagnosis for unexplained biliary disease.

Case presentation

A 53-year-old female, Tibeto-Burman ethnicity following Buddhism from Kathmandu, was admitted to Tribhuvan University Teaching Hospital in the gastroenterology department (Annex-III); with an 8-month history of abdominal pain. The pain had an insidious onset and was gradually progressive in nature. It was predominantly located in the right upper quadrant and radiated to the back. Over the preceding one week, the pain increased in intensity and became colicky. Associated symptoms included decreased appetite, nausea, and vomiting. She also reported a 7-day history of fever accompanied by chills and rigors, as well as high-colored urine. She denied abdominal distension, jaundice, easy bruising, hematemesis, melena, shortness of breath, decreased urine output, altered sensorium, orthopnea, paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea, abnormal body movements, significant weight loss, or any noticeable lumps or swellings. She had a significant history of smoking (20 pack-years) and denied alcohol consumption.

On examination, the patient appeared well nourished, with no pallor or peripheral edema. Icterus was present, while clubbing, dehydration, and lymphadenopathy were absent. Vital signs revealed a pulse rate of 92 beats per minute, blood pressure of 140/80 mmHg, respiratory rate of 20 breaths per minute, and an axillary temperature of 98°F. Abdominal examination on inspection showed a non-distended abdomen with no visible scars or dilated veins. On palpation, the abdomen was soft with tenderness localized to the epigastric region and right upper quadrant, and Murphy’s sign was positive. On percussion, no abnormal findings were noted. On auscultation, bowel sounds were absent. Respiratory system examination revealed bilateral equal air entry with normal vesicular breath sounds, and cardiovascular examination demonstrated normal heart sounds (S1 and S2) with no added sounds or murmurs.

A contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CECT) of the abdomen revealed the presence of cholelithiasis and choledocholithiasis, along with right renal cortical cysts and minimal ascites. An abdominal ultrasound supported these findings, showing a common bile duct (CBD) measuring 11 mm with gallbladder sludge and multiple gallstones. In addition, upper gastrointestinal (UGI) endoscopy revealed antral gastritis.

The patient underwent an endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP), during which stones were removed (shown in Fig. 1), and stenting was performed. Notably, the procedure revealed the presence of a live worm extracted from the biliary papilla, raising concerns about a possible parasitic infection (shown in Fig. 2).

Initial laboratory investigations revealed a complete blood count (CBC) showing a hemoglobin level of 11.5 g/dL, a white blood cell count of 9100 cells/mm3 with slightly eosinophilic (14%), and a platelet count of 239,000 cells/mm3. Serological tests for hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg), hepatitis C virus (HCV), and human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) returned negative results. Renal function tests were within normal limits with urea 2.5 mmol/L, creatinine 58 umol/L, sodium 138 mEq/L, and potassium 3.6 mEql/L. Prothrombin time (PT) was prolonged at 16.1 s with an International Normalized Ratio (INR) of 1.21. Live function tests showed significant elevation total and direct bilirubin 69 umol/L and 43 umol/L and liver enzymes aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), and alkaline phosphatase (ALP) at 423 U/L, 414 U/L, and 556 U/L, respectively. Total protein 65 gm/L, and albumin 41 gm/L. Amylase and lipase levels were within normal ranges at 42 U/L and 15 U/L. Urinalysis and stool examination showed no significant abnormalities. The C-reactive protein (CRP) was elevated at 53 mg/L, indicating a significant inflammatory response.

The extracted worm was subsequently sent to the laboratory for further examination. A direct wet mount examination of the biliary fluid was done [8]. Analysis of the biliary fluid revealed the presence of both adult worms with their anterior mouths having teeth and at the posterior end males have bell-shaped bursal rays that hold females during copulation and the eggs that were colorless, oval-shaped and, having a very thin wall, followed by a clear ring around a dense cluster of cells in the center of the egg shown in Fig. 3, the characteristics of eggs confirming a diagnosis of hookworm infestation in the biliary tract.

The patient was managed with a comprehensive pharmacological approach targeting the parasitic infection and its associated symptoms. For antiparasitic treatment, Metronidazole 400 mg was prescribed orally three times daily for five days, alongside Ciprofloxacin 500 mg twice daily during the same period and a single bedtime dose of Albendazole 400 mg to enhance parasite elimination. To alleviate abdominal pain from biliary obstruction, Tramadol hydrochloride, and Acetaminophen were administered as needed over two days, while Cremaffin syrup (15 ml) was given at bedtime for 5 days to address gastrointestinal motility issues or constipation. In addition, Esomeprazole 40 mg was provided once daily for 10 days to protect the gastric mucosa and manage potential acid-related symptoms. This multifaceted approach aimed to effectively resolve the infectious and symptomatic aspects of the patient’s condition. One week after treatment, the patient was feeling well, and the patient was discharged. Follow-up was done 1 week after discharge when all the symptoms subsided. At the time of discharge, laboratory investigations showed improvement in most parameters. Hemoglobin was 12.5 g/dL, total leukocyte count 10,080/mm3 with normal eosinophils count (4%) and platelet count 317,000/mm3. Renal function tests were within acceptable limits with urea 2.6 mmol/L, creatinine 55 umol/L, sodium 138 mEq/L, and potassium 3.7 mEql/L. Liver function tests demonstrated marked improvement with total and direct bilirubin 12 umol/L and 3 umol/L, respectively, AST 31 IU/L, ALT 101 IU/L, alkaline phosphatase 67 IU/L, total protein 57 gm/L, and albumin 34 gm/L. We were unable to further confirm identification through scanning electron microscopy and molecular characterization due to the unavailability of resources, a limitation in our report.

Discussion

Hookworm infestation is a common parasitic infection in tropical and subtropical regions; however, involvement of the biliary tract is exceedingly rare. This case represents the first reported instance from Nepal of hookworm infestation affecting the biliary system, posing a significant diagnostic challenge due to its atypical location and nonspecific clinical manifestations. The rarity of biliary involvement, combined with overlapping features of common hepatobiliary disorders, contributed to delayed clinical suspicion and diagnostic complexity. The patient belonged to a lower middle–class farming community and reported poor hygienic practices, including regular barefoot exposure while working in agricultural fields—an established risk factor that facilitates skin penetration by infective larvae. Such socioeconomic and occupational factors not only increased susceptibility to infection but also delayed consideration of an unusual parasitic etiology. Typically, hookworm infection presents with gastrointestinal symptoms and anemia resulting from chronic blood loss; however, ectopic migration to the biliary tract can produce atypical presentations that further complicate diagnosis and management. The lifecycle of hookworms begins when eggs are excreted in human feces and hatch into rhabditiform larvae within 1–2 days under favorable environmental conditions such as warmth, moisture, and shade. Over the next 5–10 days, the larvae undergo two molts to become infective filariform larvae, which can survive in soil for up to 3–4 weeks. Upon contact with human skin, these larvae penetrate the epidermis, enter the bloodstream, migrate through the heart to the lungs, ascend the bronchial tree, and are subsequently swallowed into the gastrointestinal tract. They ultimately reach the jejunum, where they mature into adult worms—most commonly Ancylostoma duodenale or Necator americanus. Adult hookworms measure approximately 1 cm in length and attach to the intestinal mucosa, feeding on host blood and leading to anemia and nutritional deficiencies. Female worms may lay 10,000–20,000 eggs daily, and while most adult worms survive for 1–2 years, some may persist longer, resulting in variable disease severity depending on worm burden [8, 9]. In endemic areas, preventive chemotherapy is recommended for at-risk populations. Preventive strategies therefore emphasize improved sanitation, avoidance of soil exposure, health education, and screening of mothers with epidemiological risk factors during pregnancy to reduce transmission and disease burden. [10]

Parasites can invade the biliary tract either by penetrating the biliary mucosa or by establishing a permanent habitat, leading to significant health issues. These infections are particularly concerning in tropical regions, where they may cause biliary obstruction or severe complications such as cholangiocarcinoma. Common causative organisms include A. lumbricoides, C. sinensis, various Fasciola and Opisthorchis species, M. conjunctus, and D. dendriticum, with rare cases of S. stercoralis infestation also reported. This case from Nepal is exceptional, describing biliary obstruction caused by choledocholithiasis complicated by hookworm infestation—a rare helminth involvement in the bile duct. The uniqueness of this case lies in its unusual etiology, potential for serious complications, atypical clinical manifestations, and diagnostic challenge. Consequently, this report serves as an important reference for understanding the clinical features, diagnostic strategies, and therapeutic approaches for parasitic invasions of the biliary tract. [7]

This case is unique due to the rare presentation of hookworm infection in the biliary tract, a site typically affected by Ascaris. The patient’s risk factors and atypical clinical manifestations posed a diagnostic challenge, as biliary parasitic infections are often overlooked. While biliary ascariasis is more commonly diagnosed through physical examination, patient history, stool microscopy for eggs, or direct observation of adult worms—and can be supported by imaging when the parasite itself is not isolated—our case required a more comprehensive evaluation. Ultrasonography is generally effective in visualizing Ascaris in the pancreatic ducts, biliary tree, duodenum, and stomach; however, in this instance, a live hookworm was identified in the biliary tract via ERCP, with laboratory confirmation of eggs and adult worms. This underscores the importance of thorough diagnostic workup, although our limited facilities prevented further confirmation with scanning electron microscopy. Overall, this case highlights that parasitic infections can be easily missed and should be considered in differential diagnoses for similar clinical presentations. [10–12]

Anthelmintic therapy is required for all patients with biliary tract parasitic diseases, even those who are asymptomatic. The patient was managed with a comprehensive pharmacological regimen, including Metronidazole, Ciprofloxacin, and Albendazole for parasitic infection, along with analgesics and gastrointestinal motility agents for symptom relief, resulting in a full recovery within a week. Therefore, if a diagnosis is established accurately and promptly, the prognosis is favorable and the death rate is low. [12, 13]

Conclusion

This case highlights the complex interplay between gastrointestinal pathology and parasitic infections in a 53-year-old female patient presenting with significant abdominal pain, elevated liver enzymes, and biliary obstruction due to choledocholithiasis. The identification of a live hookworm in the biliary tract, alongside the confirmed presence of adult worms and ova in laboratory analysis, underscores the importance of considering even a rare parasitic infestation in differential diagnoses, especially in patients with unexplained abdominal symptoms and jaundice. Despite the patient’s history of smoking and absence of alcoholic liver disease, the investigations revealed a clear pathological process driven by both gallstones and a parasitic infection. This case emphasizes the necessity for comprehensive diagnostic evaluations in such presentations and the need for timely intervention to address both the obstructive and infectious components of the patient's condition. Further management and follow-up will be essential to prevent recurrence and effectively treat the underlying hookworm infestation, highlighting the importance of multidisciplinary approaches in gastrointestinal medicine.

Limitations

A key limitation of this report is the inability to further confirm species identification through advanced diagnostic methods such as scanning electron microscopy and molecular characterization. These techniques could not be performed due to the unavailability of required resources at our center. Consequently, parasite identification was based on conventional morphological features, which, while supportive, may lack the precision offered by advanced modalities.
