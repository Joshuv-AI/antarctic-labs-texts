Epidemiology and genomic characteristics of carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae in intensive care unit from 2021 to 2024
Authors: Zheng Ma, Li Mo, Chunhong Li, Jihua Hu, Wenyu Zheng, Shaohui Zeng, Bohai Yu, Shuo Jiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeThe primary aim of this research was to elucidate the clinical epidemiology and genetic traits of carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae (CRKP) infection and colonization within the intensive care unit (ICU) of a tertiary care teaching hospital in China, spanning the period from 2021 to 2024.MethodsAll CRKP isolates were obtained from patients in the ICU, and relevant clinical data pertaining to these patients were collected. The whole genome sequencing was conducted for all isolates, followed by downstream genomic analyses.ResultsThe study identified 29 non-duplicate CRKP strains, completely isolated from ICU, with sputum as the most common anal swab sample. Patients with factors including a prior ICU stay, use of a central venous catheter and antibiotic treatment were found to be associated with CRKP infection. Our collected CRKP isolates demonstrated multidrug resistance, with additional resistance to cephalosporins. Further virulence assays revealed that these isolates exhibited strong serum survival capabilities despite lacking the mucoid phenotype. Molecular epidemiology indicated that all strains harbored the blaKPC-2 gene and belonged to the KL64-O2v1 serotype. Although all isolates lacked the regulator of mucoid phenotype (rmpA) gene, they carried the colibactin (clb) and yersiniabactin (ybt) genes. These findings suggest a complex interplay between resistance and virulence factors that contribute to the pathogenicity of these CRKP strains.ConclusionThis study provided insights into the molecular epidemiology of CRKP within ICUs from 2021 to 2024, identifying potential factors associated with CRKP infections. Our findings underscore the critical importance of monitoring CRKP and implementing robust antimicrobial stewardship programs to limit the spread of CRKP infections.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-025-04497-0.

Introduction

Carbapenemase-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae (CRKP) represents a significant global public health challenge, characterized by high morbidity and mortality rates. Known for its pathogenic capabilities, K. pneumoniae is responsible for a variety of infections, including pneumonia, urinary tract infections, bacteremia, meningitis, and liver abscesses [1]. Additionally, this bacterium can persist on abiotic surfaces, such as medical devices, posing further infection risks. Within the human body, K. pneumoniae primarily colonizes mucosal surfaces like the oropharynx and gastrointestinal tract, with the potential to disseminate to other tissues, thereby elevating the risk of infection [2].

In recent years, clinical isolates of K. pneumoniae have displayed a wide array of resistance and virulence profiles. Previous studies provide substantial evidence of considerable genetic diversity within CRKP strains [3, 4]. Although various mechanisms contribute to carbapenem resistance in CRKP, the production of carbapenemase remains the most prevalent and clinically significant factor [5]. The resistance to carbapenem is primarily facilitated by the enzyme Klebsiella pneumoniae carbapenemase (KPC), which is encoded by a gene located on mobile genetic elements, thereby enabling the horizontal transmission of resistance genes across different strains [6]. In China, approximately 60% of CRKP isolates belong to sequence type 11 (ST11), with the KPC-2 variant of the carbapenemase gene (blaKPC-2) being the most prevalent [7]. Additionally, the incidence of hypervirulent CRKP (hvCRKP) strains, which carry virulence genes, has surged in China in recent years, and notably, certain hvCRKP strains present significant public health threats due to their high transmissibility, multidrug resistance, and enhanced virulence [8]. These characteristics can significantly complicate both the treatment and clinical management of CRKP infections.

Patients in intensive care units (ICUs) are at an elevated risk of acquiring CRKP, a vulnerability linked to several factors such as antibiotic exposure, frequent invasive procedures, and prolonged hospital stays [4, 9, 10]. Studies indicate that rectal colonization by CRKP is common among ICU patients [4, 11], with colonization by K. pneumoniae serving as a significant risk factor for subsequent infection [12]. Due to the challenges in diagnosing asymptomatic CRKP colonization [13], most CRKP strains are isolated from patients who present with symptoms. Consequently, individuals harboring asymptomatic infections may act as significant reservoirs for both infection and transmission [11, 14]. Implementing comprehensive surveillance of CRKP colonization and infection could provide valuable insights into epidemic trends, inform therapeutic decisions and public health strategies, and evaluate the effectiveness of various interventions [15].

In this study, whole-genome sequencing was employed to identify antimicrobial resistance and virulence genes, as well as to analyze the genomic features and conduct phylogenetic assessments of colonization and infection by CRKP in the intensive care unit of a tertiary teaching hospital in southern China. The insights gained from these data will enhance the understanding of CRKP epidemiology within ICUs and serve as a foundation for developing targeted therapeutic approaches, alongside prevention and control measures.

Materials and methods

Study design and sample collection

Samples and data were sourced from a prospective cohort study conducted from January 2021 to August 2024 in the ICU of a major tertiary teaching hospital in Guangdong, China. The study targeted adult patients newly admitted to the ICU. Exclusion criteria included patients with ICU stays of 48 h or less, or those with incomplete clinical or microbiological data. CRKP isolates were gathered from two primary sources: clinical specimens obtained during standard diagnostic procedures and anal swabs collected via a proactive screening initiative. This screening targeted adults (aged 18 and older) expected to have hospital stays longer than 48 h upon ICU admission. In cases where multiple CRKP isolates with identical susceptibility profiles were found in a single patient, only the initial isolate was included for genomic analysis. In this study, ICU-acquired CRKP was defined according to criteria adapted from the Diagnostic Criteria for Nosocomial Infection in China [16]. Specifically, ICU-acquired CRKP refers to a culture obtained from either a colonization or clinical specimen collected more than 48 h following ICU admission. In contrast, non-ICU-acquired CRKP is defined as a culture collected within the first 48 h of ICU admission. Clinical information was gathered, including sample collection date, sample type, admission department, demographic details, underlying conditions, and patient outcomes. In this study, colonization was defined as the detection of CRKP in patients without any signs or symptoms of infection [17]. Conversely, CRKP infection was defined by the presence of the microorganism from the blood cultures or other usually sterile body sites together with clinical and/or radiological signs of infection [18]. Isolates from various sources were identified using the Clin-TOF II system (Bioyong, Beijing, China) and tested for carbapenem resistance in accordance with the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI-M100, 34th edition) guidelines. Selected isolates were preserved at − 80 °C in the MicroBank (Biocaring, Guangdong, China) for subsequent analysis.

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing

The minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) was tested by VITEK® 2 Compact (BioMérieux, Marcy l'Etoile, Lyon, France) using CLSI guideline breakpoints [19]. Control strains utilized included E. coli ATCC 25922 and K. pneumoniae ATCC 700603. The antibiotics included imipenem, ertapenem, amikacin, cefepime, levofloxacin, piperacillin/tazobactam, ceftriaxone, cefoperazone/sulbactam, cotrimoxazole, cefuroxime, amoxicillin/clavulanic acid, cefoxitin, ceftazidime and meropenem. Carbapenem resistance was defined as resistance to one or more carbapenems [20].

String test

Klebsiella pneumoniae strains were cultured on Columbia 5% sheep blood agar (Detgerm, Guangzhou, China) and incubated at 37 °C overnight. The formation of viscous strings was observed by lightly touching the surface of individual colonies using a standard bacterial inoculation loop. Repeat this procedure three times to confirm the accuracy of the experiment. If a string ≥ 5 mm in length is formed when the colony is lifted, the result is considered positive [21].

Biofilm formation assay

This assay uses crystal violet staining to assess biofilm formation capacity [22], with each strain tested in triplicate. Bacterial colonies grown overnight in LB broth are inoculated into 5 mL of fresh LB broth and incubated at 37 °C with agitation at 120 rpm for 16–18 h. The bacterial suspension is then standardized to a 0.5 McFarland standard, and 10 µL is dispensed into each well of a 96-well plate, with negative controls set up concurrently. The plate is then sealed and incubated overnight at 37 °C. After incubation, the plate is retrieved, the bacterial suspension is aspirated, and the wells rinsed with PBS buffer. Formaldehyde fixation solution is added to each well and incubated for 15 min, followed by removal, rinsing with distilled water, and air-drying. Then, 1% crystal violet staining solution is added to each well for 15 min, followed by aspiration, washing with sterile distilled water until colorless, and air-drying. Finally, absolute ethanol is added to each well, gently agitated for 10 min, and absorbance at 570 nm is measured using a microplate reader Evolyzer 150 (Zürich, Switzerland). The K. pneumoniae strains with OD above 0.500 were classified as strong biofilm producers, those with OD between 0.100 and 0.500 as moderate, and those below 0.100 as weak.

Serum killing assay

Serum bactericidal activity was assessed following established protocols [23]. In brief, serum was isolated from healthy control participants and stored at −80 °C for preservation. A bacterial inoculum in the mid-logarithmic growth phase, containing 106 colony-forming units (CFU), was mixed with 75% pooled human serum to evaluate its bactericidal properties. The final mixture was incubated at 37 °C, with viable bacterial counts assessed at 0, 1, 2, and 3 h. The response to serum-mediated killing, as indicated by the viable counts, was evaluated and categorized into six distinct grades. The interpretation of results for six grades was followed by the previous protocols [24]. Each strain was tested a minimum of three times, and the mean results were calculated and expressed as a percentage of the initial inoculum. The responses, based on viable counts, were graded from 1 to 6. Strains were then categorized as follows: serum sensitive if they received a grade of 1 or 2, intermediately sensitive if graded 3 or 4, and serum resistant if they were assigned a grade of 5 or 6.

Genomic DNA extraction and sequencing data analysis

Bacterial genomic DNA (gDNA) extraction was performed using the TIANamp Bacteria Genomic DNA Kit (TIANGEN BIOTECH, Beijing, China). Whole-genome sequencing was conducted on a NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, United States), with library preparation facilitated by the QuarPrep EZ DNA Library Kit (Dynegene, Shanghai, China). The resulting short reads underwent quality control checks using FastQC. Subsequent de novo assembly was executed with SPAdes v3.15.3, employing k-mer sizes of 21, 33, 55, 77, 99, and 127 [25]. Quality assessments were then carried out using checkM v1.16 [26]. Kleborate v2.3.1 [27] was utilized to perform in silico analysis of the bacterial genomes for multilocus sequence typing (MLST), identification of antibiotic resistance genes, determination of capsule locus types, and detection of virulence factors. Beta-lactam resistance genes and key virulence factors of CRKP were further selected for heatmap comparison. Additionally, PlasmidFinder v2.1.6 [28] was employed to predict potential plasmid presences within the genomes. For each genome, plasmid replicon types were assigned using the formula based on the plasmid PubMLST scheme [29]. The K. pneumoniae strain HS11386 (accession number: CP003200.1) was chosen as the reference genome for our phylogenetic analysis due to its direct relevance to the isolates examined in our study. This strain is classified as ST11 and harbors the blaKPC-2 gene, having been initially isolated from a sputum specimen in China in 2011 [30]. We utilized Snippy v4.6.0 (https://github.com/tseemann/snippy) to map all K. pneumoniae isolates in our study to this reference genome, allowing for rapid haploid variant calling and alignment. Following this, recombination sites were identified and removed using Gubbins v2.4.1 [31] to ensure a more accurate phylogenetic inference. The clean core genome alignments resulting from these processes were then used to construct a maximum-likelihood phylogenetic tree using IQ-TREE v2.2.0.3 [32].

Statistics analysis and visualization

Statistical analyses for the study were conducted using SPSS 26.0 software (SPSS, Inc., Chicago, IL, USA), employing the Chi-squared test and Wilcoxon test as appropriate to evaluate the data. A significant level of 5% was used throughout these analyses. For the visualization and annotation of the phylogenetic trees, iTOL v6 was utilized [33].

Results

The median age of the patients was 60 years, and 59.8% of the participants were male. String test, biofilm formation and serum killing

A string test was conducted on 29 CRKP isolates, which revealed that only two strains exhibited positive string formation on blood agar plates, indicative of the hypermucoviscous phenotype. Of the 29 CRKP strains analyzed, the majority exhibited the ability to form biofilms, with variations in the extent of formation. Among these, 9 strains (9/29, 31.0%) exhibited weak biofilm formation, 10 strains (10/29, 34.5%) displayed moderate biofilm formation, and 10 strains (10/29, 34.5%) showed strong biofilm formation. Despite variations in biofilm formation capabilities among isolates, this trait contributes to the pathogenicity and resistance of K. pneumoniae. Serum resistance was assessed in a total of 29 strains. Of these, 27.2% (7/29) were sensitive to serum, 36.4% (11/29) exhibited moderate resistance, and 36.4% (11/29) demonstrated full serum resistance. Molecular epidemiology

A total of 29 CRKP isolates were collected, all of which belong to the KL64-O1/O2v1 capsular type (Fig. 3). Notably, all isolates were classified as ST11, except for one strain from patient P28, which was ST2240-like, differing by a single allele (Table 2). The CRKP isolates were obtained from four different patient sites: sputum, anal swab, urine, and blood. The highest prevalence was observed in anal swabs (13/29), followed by sputum samples (11/29). Each of the analyzed strains harbored between 8 to 13 resistance genes, encompassing those for beta-lactams, sulfonamides, trimethoprim, tetracyclines, quinolones, and aminoglycosides (Table S4). The analysis of carbapenemase genes indicated that all isolates possessed the blaKPC-2 gene, which often co-existed with blaCTX-M-65. In our analysis of virulence genes specific to K. pneumoniae, we concentrated on those related to siderophores (iro, iuc, and ybt), the colibactin toxin (clb), and the regulator of the mucoid phenotype (rmpA). Notably, all isolates were found to lack the rmpA gene (Table S4). However, the majority of isolates possessed the iuc cluster, which encodes aerobactin, with the exception of isolates from three patients. Discussion

In this study, WGS was employed to elucidate the genomic characteristics and phylogenetic relationships of CRKP colonization in ICU patients. The insights gained from this analysis contribute to a deeper understanding of the prevalence and genetic diversity of CRKP in regions experiencing epidemics. The emergence of carbapenemase-mediated resistance, particularly involving CRKP, represents a significant global public health challenge. Notably, K. pneumoniae was first identified as a producer of the blaKPC-2 carbapenemase in China in 2007. Since that time, reports of CRKP have proliferated, with incidence rates rapidly increasing across the country. ICUs serve as critical environments for the emergence and dissemination of antimicrobial resistance, highlighting the urgent need for effective monitoring and control strategies to mitigate the spread of these resistant strains. In this study, we conducted a comprehensive screening of CRKP colonization within a Chinese teaching hospital, identifying the ST11 KL64-O1/O2v1 strain as the primary source of ICU infections. Our findings further indicate that patients with CRKP infections were more likely to have had a prior ICU stay, utilized a central venous catheter, and undergone antibiotic treatment. These insights are crucial for identifying patients at high risk of CRKP infection and underscore the importance of genomic data in guiding antibiotic resistance surveillance and informing control strategies.

In terms of demographics, our study found no significant differences between patients with CRKP and CSKP infections. However, existing literature suggests that CRKP infections are more frequently observed in elderly patients, likely due to severe underlying conditions such as immunocompromised states [34, 35]. Regarding infection sites, both CRKP and CSKP predominantly affect the respiratory tract, consistent with findings from previous studies [36].Observational studies have also shown that treatments and prior antibiotic use are more common among CRKP-infected patients [37, 38]. Interventions such as prior chemotherapy and catheter use may compromise patient health, increasing susceptibility to infections. Additionally, the clinical use of antibiotics is a critical factor in triggering resistance, as highlighted by several studies that discuss the role of antibiotic misuse in the development of AMR [8]. Our research also identified a notably high mortality rate associated with CRKP infections, aligning with other studies that underscore the increased severity and mortality risk of CRKP in hospital settings [39, 40]. By providing insights into high-risk profiles, this study aims to support more informed decision-making in the initial therapy for CRKP.

The use of WGS in the surveillance and control of antimicrobial resistance has been a major development that now allows for early detection and tracking of transmission, as well as thorough characterization of pathogens and resistance mechanisms [41]. MLST data revealed that ST11 (97.3% carrying KL64/KL47) was the dominant type from the non-ICU-acquired group and the ICU-acquired group, which further increased the body of evidence suggesting that sister clades of ST11 carrying either the KL64 or KL47 capsular type were the predominant CRKP clones in China and that there are differences in prevalence between China and other countries [42]. In a previous 6-year national prevalence study, CRKP-ST11 has diversified into two major subclone including KL47-OL101 and KL64-O2v1 [43]. However, KL47-OL101 was replaced in a stepwise manner by KL64-O2v1 over time driven by recC mutation [44]. KL64-O2v1 strains expressing O2 antigens, are more likely to be associated with increased host susceptibility due to a scarcity of anti-O2 antibodies in human B cell repertoires, thereby enhancing their pathogenicity [45]. In our analysis, all strains belonged to KL64-O2v1, with no presence of KL47-OL101, indicating a successful clonal replacement within the hospital. Although the gradual replacement of KL47 by KL64 is likely driven by high recombination rates and the enhanced pathogenicity of the O2 antigen, K. pneumoniae serotype remains region-specific. A longitudinal retrospective study of CRKP in China demonstrated KL64 predominance in the eastern and central regions, while KL47 was more prevalent in the north and northeast [46]. Our hospital's findings, showing KL64 dominance, suggest that ICU CRKP cases are more likely to originate from endemic lineages in the eastern regions. Notably, the ST23 lineage has gained attention in China due to its high prevalence of virulence and rapidly increasing dissemination potential [47]. Epidemiological analyses from 2018 to 2023 have indicated that the prevalence of ST23 CRKP in China has exhibited a significant average annual growth rate, ranging from 30 to 59% [48]. However, in our study, the trend of ST23 was not observed within the hospital setting, as no ST23 isolates were identified. Instead, we isolated a ST2240-like strain, which differed by one allele. Currently, there are no reports of ST2240 CRKP, suggesting that it does not presently pose an epidemic risk. Our genomic investigation of the ST2240-like strain may offer valuable clinical insights for the control and management of CRKP infections. Our findings emphasize the importance of understanding K. pneumoniae lineage distribution and divergence, fostering enhanced surveillance and source tracing of CRKP outbreaks in ICUs.

In this study, all CRKP strains were found to harbor the blaKPC-2 gene, consistent with previous studies indicating a high prevalence of blaKPC-2 in CRKP among ICU and hospitalized patients in China [5, 49]. This prevalence is notably higher than the rates reported in South America, the USA, Australia, Lebanon, and Singapore [5]. In a multicenter cohort analysis in China, the prevalence of the blaNDM gene in CRKP was found to be relatively low [5], and none of our isolates contained the blaNDM gene. Additionally, our results indicate a high prevalence of virulent genes, including the ybt, clb, and iuc gene clusters. Previous studies in China [50, 51] have identified the most virulent CRKP strains as those carrying the aerobactin siderophore loci and yersiniabactin, typically associated with the mobile element ICEKp3. Although we did not perform long-read sequencing, the presence of virulence traits strongly suggests that ICEKp3 is present in all genomes examined, indicating a high risk and mobility of virulence factors. Although genomic evidence suggests potential hypervirulence in CRKP, the string test revealed that the majority of our isolates were not hypervirulent. However, they did exhibit multidrug resistance. The high virulence and antimicrobial resistance in K. pneumoniae can be attributed to various plasmids, such as IncHI1B [52] and IncFII [53], as identified in previous studies. The majority of isolates exhibited moderate biofilm formation and were able to survive in serum; the latter trait is consistent with the presence of siderophore gene clusters such as ybt and iuc, while the clb cluster may further contribute to overall virulence. Previous studies conducted in a hospital in North-West London [54] demonstrated that these three key virulence loci were prevalent, even among isolates carrying beta-lactamase genes, highlighting the potential for AMR and virulence determinants to jointly exacerbate patient morbidity. However, in our isolates, the majority were not hypermucoviscous, as indicated by negative string tests, which aligns with the absence of rmpA genes. Interestingly, our isolates demonstrated co-occurrence of blaKPC-2 and blaCTX-M-65, with the exception of two isolates. This observation aligns with a recent study [55] indicating the presence of IncFII/IncR-KPC-2 plasmids and other phage-like plasmids capable of co-carrying these two ARGs. Our findings underscore regional differences in the prevalence of antimicrobial resistance and virulence-associated genes, which may contribute to local clonal expansion.

Our study has several limitations. First, the primary limitations of our study include the fact that all clinical data were collected from a single hospital, which limits our ability to generalize the epidemiology of CRKP across a broader scope. Additionally, the lack of long-read sequencing prevents us from thoroughly tracing the blaKPC genetic background, which would be beneficial for identifying potential horizontal gene transfer and potential drivers for clonal transmission.

In summary, by integrating the epidemiological data with WGS analysis, we described distribution of CRKP strains in ICU over a 4-year period. The CRKP strains in our ICU are primarily of the ST11-KL64 type and carry multiple virulent genes. Given the high prevalence of virulence genes, there is an urgent need for more effective infection monitoring and transmission control strategies with WGS being a helpful tool that could aid in this goal. Additionally, the hospital infection control department and the ICU should establish strict prevention and control measures, strengthen bacterial resistance monitoring, and enhance pharmaceutical regulation to effectively curb the emergence and spread of CRKP.
