Application of microneedle patches in the alleviation of cardiac infarction in animal models: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Samaneh Narimani, Hanieh Salehipourmehr, Ali Baradar Khoshfetrat, Elham Dehghani, Hassan Amini, Zeinab Aliyari Serej, Emel Sokullu, Reza Rahbarghazi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMyocardial infarction (MI) is associated with high mortality rates in the clinical setting. Microneedle patches (MNP) are sophisticated platforms for delivering various cargoes into target tissues with high efficiency rates. This systematic review aimed to evaluate the potential regenerative outcomes of MNP application, with or without cargo, in animal models of experimentally induced MI.MethodsA comprehensive systematic search was performed using computerized databases, including Embase, PubMed, Scopus, WOS, and Google Scholar without language limitation, up to Dec 2025, to identify animal studies using MNP in various MI models. Two authors independently evaluated methodological quality according to the modified CAMARADES (Collaborative Approach to Meta-Analysis and Review of Animal Data from Experimental Studies) checklist. Data were extracted and reported by theme for both the control and experimental groups.ResultsOf A total of 458 studies, 21 papers preliminarily met the inclusion criteria for the systematic review, and subsequently, 18 studies were included in the meta-analysis. The overall effect size observed in the groups transplanting cargo containing MNP was compared to the matched control group. Data revealed enhanced ejection fraction (EF) (Standard difference in mean (SMD): 19.84; confidence interval (CI) 95%: 15.94–23.73), fractional shortening (FS) (SMD: 11.24; 95% CI: 8.91–13.56), angiogenesis (SMD: 14.32; CI 95%: 9.57–19.07), and reduced fibrosis (SMD: −11.12; 95% CI: −13.46, −8.78).ConclusionOur results indicated that MNP could be an alternative therapy to promote ischemic cardiac tissue healing, mainly by enhancing myocardial angiogenesis, and the reduction of fibrosis coincided with improved EF and FS. Introduction

Myocardial infarction (MI) is considered the leading cause of human mortality, with long-term socioeconomic outcomes [1]. The complete or narrowing of the coronary artery is the primary underlying mechanism of MI, with significant cardiomyocyte death, fibrosis, and hypertrophy [2]. Modalities such as percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG), thrombolysis, and pharmacotherapy are the most commonly used therapeutic regimens in patients with acute MI (AMI) [3]. Aberrant ventricular remodelling in AMI patients leads to cardiomyopathy and chronic heart failure (CHF) [4].

Although conventional therapies may alleviate MI symptoms, they are not able to completely regenerate the necrotic region and improve cardiac function [5]. These approaches face some potential limitations and pitfalls that need the development of novel therapeutics. Recurrent angina with the possibility of MI [6], neuropsychiatric consequences [7], postoperative arrhythmia and stroke, wound infection, etc. [8] can increase the rate of mortalities in the clinical setting after PCI and/or CABG.

Significant progress has been made in the development of de novo MI treatment strategies, including stem cell and factor therapies, genetic manipulation, at both basic and translational levels [9]. Emerging data have shown that these cells with stemness features and paracrine properties can directly trans-differentiate into cardiac cells or secrete various pro-angiogenesis factors, resulting in the reduction of post-ischemia changes [10]. Of note, the route of administration plays a key role in on-target delivery rate, retention time, and biological outcomes [11]. For instance, local delivery can decrease dosage and influence retention time. Despite being invasive and with potential risks, intramyocardial injection is preferred to other transplantation routes such as systemic and intracoronary routes [12, 13]. However, the advised intramyocardial injection dose is limited to small volumes ranging from 10 to 20 μL per injection site. Of course, it should not be forgotten that the injection of therapeutic substances directly into the myocardium is problematic due to the dynamic heart beating and mechanical pressure, which can increase the possibility of substance leakage [14]. Furthermore, iatrogenic inflammation and damage could be induced following direct intramyocardial injection and structural disruption [15]. Besides, most of the injected stem cells or other cell types die a few hours after transplantation due to mechanical stress, excessive amounts of free radicals [12], anoikis and inflammation, and lack of blood perfusion [16]. Thus, new alternative delivery approaches should be taken for MI candidates.

Tissue engineering techniques have been used to increase the therapeutic efficiency using various biomaterials, cells, and factors to provide temporary structural and functional support [17]. Hydrogels are commonly utilized because of in vivo-like similarities to the extracellular matrix (ECM) [18]. The application of amorphous hydrogels faces some drawbacks, such as inappropriate absorption features and poor mechanical properties [19, 20]. Therefore, attempts have been made to develop hydrogels with defined geometries and physicochemical features. Microneedle patches (MNP) are valid bioengineering platforms with numerous micron-sized arrays connected to a supporting patch for in situ and systemic delivery of cells and by-products, and compounds [21–23]. It was suggested that MNP regenerative purposes differ based on their loaded components, supporting substrates, geometry, and fabrication process [23]. In this regard, MNPs are generally classified in terms of releasing capacity into different categories as follows: solid, coated, dissolving, hydrogel, hollow, separable, and swellable MNPs [22]. The higher engraftment efficiency, controllable and relatively sustained release of therapeutics, make MNPs ideal for different therapeutic protocols [24, 25]. Compared to various administration routes, MNPs can be used for painless delivery of therapeutic agents by creating micro-channels on the skin or other internal organs [26, 27]. Recently, several studies have been conducted related to the application of MNPs in different MI animal models.

To the best of our knowledge, there are few experiments associated with meta-analysis of MNP application in different MI animal models. Most of the previously conducted studies have focused on the regenerative potential of separate MNP systems after ischemia induction in the myocardium. To yield minimum bias and logic interpretation of obtained data, and assess the angiogenic properties of MNPs in infarcted myocardium, the conduct of a systematic review and meta-analysis study seems mandatory. In the current systematic review and meta-analysis, the antifibrotic and pro-angiogenic properties of MNPs were monitored in an animal model of MI, focusing on cardiac parameters such as ejection fraction (EF) and fractional shortening (FS).

The current data can help us in the development of a de novo releasing platform for sustained and long-term release of therapeutics, stem cells, and stem cell-based factors with relatively minimal side effects and enhanced regenerative outcomes in MI individuals.

Methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis are presented conforming to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement (Supplementary Tables 1 and 2) [28]. The protocol used was registered on PROSPERO (CRD42025615993).

Eligibility criteria

The studies that evaluated MNP transplantation on animals with MI and met the following criteria were considered eligible: 1) study design: All peer-reviewed preclinical studies were candidates to be included. Any clinical study on humans, review, retrospective, and epidemiological studies were excluded. As well, the studies presented in seminars or conferences were excluded. 2) Model animals: Animals with MI in any gender or age were considered for inclusion criteria. The other human disease models, using animals or ischemia-induced organs, such as skin and arteries, were excluded. In vitro studies were also excluded. 3) Intervention: The studies in which MNP, with/without any cargos, was applied were included. Additionally, studies involving patch (without microneedles) or hydrogel transplantation were excluded. In addition to these, studies with no access to the full text were excluded. 4) Study reports: The outcome indicators were left ventricular EF, FS, fibrotic changes, and angiogenesis. In circumstances where the studies encompass more than one dataset for outcome analysis, they were separated by a numerical identifier. In the case of data from different time points, the selection was done based on the common time, although all data in the subgroup analysis based on time were included. In cases where there was an insufficient amount of data, such as sample size and measurement time, the outcome was excluded.

Information sources

We performed a comprehensive systematic search from the computerized databases, involving Embase, PubMed, Scopus, WOS, and Google Scholar up to Dec 2025, without language limitation, to find out preclinical studies transplanting MNP on MI animal models.

Search strategy

The search strategy included an appropriate term combination (Supplementary Table 3). We used a mix of subject headings and free words from the database establishment.

Study selection

After a thorough search, all collected studies were uploaded to EndNote 18 software, and duplicate citations were removed. Titles and abstracts were independently screened by two authors using the Rayyan web-based software [29]. Irrelevant studies and those that did not meet the inclusion criteria were excluded. The remaining studies were screened by full-text, and their eligibility was assessed.

Data extraction

Data were independently extracted from included studies by two authors using a data extraction table in a pre-designed Excel spreadsheet. Any arising disagreements between the reviewers are resolved through discussion or with a third author. Extracted data from the included articles comprised: (1) author, (2) year of publication, (3) study group; animal characteristics including (4) species, (5) sex, (6) age, and (7) weight, (8) MI induction method, (9) the time between injury induction and start of medication; the characteristics of MNP including (10) composition, (11) cargo, (12) initiator, (13) cross-linker, (14) mold, (15) solution compaction manner, and (16) rout of transplantation. The data collected for the nature of the presented outcome, outcome measurement time, animal number per group, mean outcome, and standard deviation (SD) or standard error of the mean (SEM). For graphically demonstrated data, the values extracted from graphs using AxioVision Rel. 4.8. In the case of missed or unclear data, the corresponding authors of the articles were contacted by e-mail. In studies with multiple experiments, each experiment was considered an independent data point. Additionally, data with outcome measurement times less than one week were excluded from the meta-analysis.

Risk of bias assessment

The methodological validity of articles was assessed by two independent investigators using the modified CAMARADES (Collaborative Approach to Meta-Analysis and Review of Animal Data from Experimental Studies) quality checklist [30]. Again, any disagreements were under review by a third reviewer.

Statistical analysis

The outcomes of selected data were analysed using RevMan 5.4.1. Data are presented as mean ± SD with a 95% confidence interval (CI). Statistical heterogeneity was analysed using the I2 value and chi-square test, following established methods [31]. In this study, p < 0.05 and I2 > 50% were considered indicative of substantial statistical heterogeneity. A fixed-effect model was applied when heterogeneity was low (I2 ≤ 50%), while a random-effects model was used when substantial heterogeneity was present (I2 > 50%) to account for variability among studies [32].

Publication bias

We assessed potential publication bias using visual inspection of funnel plots and formal statistical tests. Given the limited number of studies per outcome (n ≤ 12), we applied Egger’s linear regression test [33]. Begg and Mazumdar’s rank correlation test [34], and Duval and Tweedie’s trim-and-fill method [35] to evaluate funnel plot asymmetry and estimate adjusted effect sizes.

Results

Study selection

The systematic search yielded 846 studies, of which 388 were duplicates. A detailed screening of titles and abstracts was performed to identify potentially eligible studies, and those deemed irrelevant were excluded. Subsequently, based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria, the full texts of 24 articles were reviewed, and 3 articles [36–38] were excluded later. Finally, 21 full papers were retrieved for thorough examination, of which 18 studies were included in the meta-analysis (Fig. 1).

Flow diagram of the review process according to PRISMA

Risk of bias in studies

Here, we used a modified CAMARADES quality checklist to assess the selected experiments. In the included articles, all of the studies, not only published in peer-reviewed journals, but also were in compliance with animal welfare regulations. In 12 articles (57.14%), the animals were allocated randomly in each group. No conflict-of-interest statement was mentioned in all 21 studies. None of the articles induced the disease model blindly. Otherwise, outcomes in 4 studies (19%) were assessed blindly. The sample size calculation methodology was not declared in any studies (Fig. 2, Supplementary Table 4).

Risk of bias assessment of included studies based on the modified version of the CAMARADES quality checklist

Study characteristics

The detailed characteristics of each article are represented in Table 1. Here is a statistical description of some features: a) The year of publication: The included investigations were published from 2018 to 2026. b) Species: Thirteen studies (61.9%) used rats as MI models, of which twelve (92.31%) were Sprague−Dawley. Mice were used in six articles (28.57%), where the C57BL/6J and Kunming species were used in five and one articles (83.33% and 16.67%), respectively. On the other hand, two studies (9.52%) applied both rats and Yorkshire pigs as models. c) Sex: The used pigs were female, while male sex was the choice of seventeen studies (80.95%). Also, in one study (4.76%), both male and female animals were used. d) Age: 61.54% (N = 8) of the studies used just rats as the MI model, which were aged between 5 and 12 weeks. 15.38% (N = 2) of the mentioned studies described the rats’ age with the “adult” term. On the other side, 83.33% (N = 5) of mouse MI modelled studies were aged between 6 and 10 weeks. e) Weight: Eight investigations (38.1%) in which rats subjected to MI weighed in 188–300 grams. In the case of the mouse, two studies (9.52%) reported a weight of 20–25 grams. Additionally, the used pigs weighed 20–35 kilograms. f) MI induction manner: The majority of studies (N = 20; 95.24%) induced MI by left anterior descending coronary artery (LAD) ligation. g) The time between MI induction and MNP transplantation of the same number of articles (N = 20; 95.24%) varied from immediate to delayed administration (until 10 days). h) Crosslinker: In eight articles (38.1%), the light, including Ultraviolet (UV) (N = 7; 87.5%) and Fluorescent (blue) light (N = 1; 12.5%), was used as a cross-linker. Whereas, chemical type was applied in four studies (19.05%). i) The applied mold to fabricate the microneedles in twelve studies (57.14%) was made up of Polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS), and in two articles (9.52%) of silicone. j) To air removal and solution compaction in MNP cavities, vacuum system in eight (38.1%), centrifugation in eight (38.1%), too, and both vacuum system and centrifugation in one study (4.76%) were used.

Results of syntheses

In total, 18 studies were included in the meta-analysis, in which the effect of MNP compared to a control group, focusing on four key areas: EF, FS, Fibrosis, and angiogenesis, was evaluated. For EF, the meta-analysis included seventeen studies. The overall effect size (Standard difference in mean (SMD)) of empty MNP compared to the control was 12.32, with a 95% CI ranging from 6.89 to 17.75. This result was statistically significant (Z = 4.44, p < 0.00001), indicating a significant effect of empty MNP on EF compared to the control group. There was a significant heterogeneity among the studies, with an I-squared value of 92% (Fig. 3a). The effect of empty MNP on EF was further examined at different time points. Significant effects were observed at 2 weeks (effect size = 5.92, p = 0.007), 3 weeks (effect size = 8.28, p < 0.00001), and 4 weeks (effect size = 13.71, p = 0.00001), but not at 1 week (p = 0.72) (Fig. 4a). The meta-analysis of FS included fifteen studies. The overall effect size of empty MNP compared to the control was 8.29, with a 95% CI from 5.42 to 11.16. This effect was also statistically significant (Z = 5.66, p < 0.00001), demonstrating a significant effect of empty MNP on FS compared to the control group. Statistical heterogeneity was present among these studies, with an I-squared value of 84% (Fig. 3b). The effects of empty MNP on FS at different time points were: 1 week (effect size = 0.35, p = 0.77), 2 weeks (effect size = 3.52, p = 0.02), 3 weeks (effect size = 5.69, p < 00001), and 4 weeks (effect size = 8.79, p < 0.00001) (Fig. 4b). Also, the meta-analysis of fibrosis included fourteen studies: The effect size of empty MNP compared to the control was −7.48, with a 95% CI of −12.31 to −2.65. This effect was statistically significant (Z = 3.03, p = 0.002). Additionally, a significant heterogeneity (I2 = 98%) was represented (Fig. 3c). Both effects of 3 (effect size = −3.5, p < 00001) and 4 (effect size = −10.71, p = 0.001) weeks in the application of empty MNP were significant (Fig. 4c). Finally, the meta-analysis of angiogenesis included seven studies. The overall effect size of empty MNP compared to the control was 19.28, with a 95% CI from 9.45 to 29.11. This effect was also statistically significant (Z = 3.84, p = 0.0001). Similar to fibrosis, with I2 = 98%, there was a significant heterogeneity among studies (Fig. 3d). A statistically significant effect was observed in 4 weeks (effect size = 24.57, p = 0.0003), while the result of 1 week (effect size = 2.00, p = 0.28) was not (Fig. 4d). Another subgroup analysis was performed based on animal species. The effects of both rats and mice were significant in EF and FS (Fig. 5a,b). In the case of fibrosis, the overall effect size of −8.98 (−18.02, 0.05 CI, p = 0.05) for rats and −2.19 (−4.55, 0.17 CI, p = 0.07) for mice was presented (Fig. 5c). Only rats were in the evaluation of empty MNP by angiogenesis, and the effect size of them was significant (Z = 19.28, p = 0.0001) (Fig. 5d).

At the same time, the effect of the cell, cytokine, and MP groups was not significant (Fig. 6a). The majority of cargos, including NP, factor, EV, drug, combination therapy, NP + cargo, EV + cargo, and MP + cargo, had a significant impact on FS. While MP did not show the same effect (Fig. 6b). In the case of fibrosis, NP (Z = 4.00, p < 0.0001), factor (Z = −7.4, p < 0.00001), EV (Z = −13.81, p = 0.01), combination therapy (Z = −24.45, p < 0.00001), NP + cargo (Z = 5.33, p < 0.00001), and MP + cargo (Z = −7.68, p = 0.0008) represented a significant effect. The effect of the drug, EV + cargo, and MP was not favourable (Fig. 6c). On the other side, the effect size of cell (Z = 10, p < 0.00001), NP + cargo (Z = 4.09, p < 0.00001), NP (Z = 2.89, p = 0.004), and combination therapy (Z = 30.08, p < 0.0001) was significant in angiogenesis. In comparison, EV (Z = 10.6, p = 0.31) did not show a significant effect (Fig. 6d).

Assessment of publication bias

Formal statistical tests revealed significant funnel plot asymmetry across all assessed outcomes, though the nature and magnitude of asymmetry varied.

Publication bias assessment for EF

For EF, Egger’s regression test indicated substantial asymmetry (intercept = 3.93, 95% CI: 1.60 to 6.27, p = 0.0038). This was corroborated by Begg and Mazumdar’s rank correlation test (Kendall’s τ = 0.67, p = 0.0026). The Duval and Tweedie trim-and-fill method under the random-effects model estimated that five studies were missing on the left side of the funnel; after their imputation, the adjusted effect size decreased from 1.98 (95% CI: 1.07 to 2.90) to 0.92 (95% CI: 0.03 to 1.88) (Fig. 7a).

Publication bias assessment of studies by funnel plot analysis. EF (a), FS (b), fibrosis (c), and angiogenesis (d)

Publication bias assessment for FS

For FS, Egger’s test was also significant (intercept = 3.19, 95% CI: 1.22 to 5.15, p = 0.0051), as was Begg and Mazumdar’s test (τ = 0.67, p = 0.0040). The trim-and-fill analysis suggested four missing studies on the left side, with the adjusted random-effects estimate reducing from 2.07 (95% CI: 1.36 to 2.79) to 1.45 (95% CI: 0.66 to 2.24) (Fig. 7b).

Publication bias assessment for fibrosis

For the fibrosis outcome, evidence for asymmetry was mixed. Begg and Mazumdar’s test showed significant negative asymmetry (τ = −0.64, p = 0.026), but Egger’s regression did not reach statistical significance (intercept = −4.83, 95% CI: −10.38 to 0.72, p = 0.077). The trim-and-fill analysis did not impute any missing studies, and the observed effect size remained unchanged at −2.46 (95% CI: −4.35 to −0.58) (Fig. 7c).

Publication bias assessment for angiogenesis

For angiogenesis, strong evidence of asymmetry was detected. Egger’s regression intercept was 7.65 (95% CI: 0.60 to 14.70, p = 0.041), and Begg and Mazumdar’s test showed perfect positive correlation (τ = 1.00, p = 0.014). Most notably, the trim-and-fill analysis imputed two missing studies on the left side, with the adjusted random-effects estimate shifting dramatically from 2.82 (95% CI: −0.79 to 6.43) to −0.10 (95% CI: −3.54 to 3.34), effectively reducing the effect to near zero. (Fig. 7d). Collectively, these results indicate statistically detectable funnel plot asymmetry for all outcomes, with particularly strong evidence of publication bias in angiogenesis, where imputation of two missing studies rendered the pooled effect non-significant. The trim-and-fill analysis suggested varying degrees of missing studies across outcomes: EF (5 studies), FS (4 studies), and angiogenesis (2 studies), but none for fibrosis. These findings underscore the importance of considering publication bias when interpreting meta-analytic results in this field.

Sensitivity analyses

To assess the robustness of the pooled estimates and explore sources of substantial heterogeneity, we performed leave-one-out sensitivity analyses for outcomes with I2 > 50%, as well as subgroup analyses by intervention type (e.g., NP, EV, NP + cargo, Combine).

Angiogenesis

Overall heterogeneity was high (I2 = 98%, p < 0.00001). In the Combine subgroup (He et al. 2025; Liu et al. 2025; Wang et al. 2025 (1); Wang et al. 2025 (2)), I2 was 99%. Removing Wang et al. (1) 2025 and Liu et al. 2025 reduced I2 to 0%, while the overall effect remained significant (MD = 7.77, 95% CI: 3.41–10.82). No single study disproportionately drove the overall effect, but the high heterogeneity was largely attributable to these two outlying studies.

Fibrosis

Overall heterogeneity was extremely high (I2 = 99%, p < 0.00001). In the Combine subgroup (Liu et al. 2025; Wang et al. 2025 (1); Wang et al. 2025 (2)), I2 was 96%. Exclusion of Wang et al. (1) 2025 reduced I2 to 0% in this subgroup, with the effect estimate remaining strongly significant (MD = −10.88, 95% CI: −12.92 to −8.83). No other individual study removal substantially altered heterogeneity or effect direction.

EF

Overall heterogeneity for EF was very high (, p < 0.00001). Subgroup analysis revealed notable variability across intervention types. In the Combine subgroup, which included cargo-based interventions, moderate heterogeneity was observed (, p = 0.07). Leave-one-out sensitivity analysis within this subgroup indicated that no single study disproportionately influenced the pooled effect, which remained strongly significant (MD = 27.69, 95% CI: 19.36 to 36.03, p < 0.00001).

In the NP subgroup, heterogeneity was substantial (, p = 0.0004). Sensitivity analysis showed that the removal of Wang et al. (2024) reduced to 30%, while the overall effect remained positive and significant. This suggests that certain NP studies contributed disproportionately to statistical heterogeneity, though not to the direction of the effect.

Discussion

The current systematic review and meta-analysis investigated the application of MNP with or without cargo in animal models of MI. This meta-analysis illustrated that MNP with or without cargo can enhance cardiac regeneration by elevating EF, FS, angiogenesis, and decreasing fibrosis. Over the past decades, various strategies have been developed to deliver therapeutic agents to the ischemic area of the myocardial infarcted heart [13]. Recently, MNP, as an advanced smart delivery platform, has gained attention [22]. However, their effectiveness on cardiac tissue remains a topic of debate. This is the first systematic review and meta-analysis conducted on the application of MNP in MI animal models. Morphological and functional outcomes were compared between the MNP transplantation group (with or without cargo) and the matched control groups (MI or sham). Our findings demonstrate that transplanted MNPs, with or without cargo, improved EF, FS, fibrosis, and angiogenesis. The same results were confirmed in the subgroup analysis of empty MNP, based on timing and species. MNPs can introduce therapeutic agents to the target area over time [59]. Unlike traditional cardiac patches, the structures of MNP provide precise, localized agent delivery by penetrating the cardiac epicardial layer and creating micro-channels in the myocardium with additional stress and local tissue injury. The dynamic movement of cardiac tissue may help diffuse cargo to surrounding areas, leading to minimal off-target delivery [14]. Additionally, the improvement in cardiac function may be attributed to the mechanical support provided to the infarcted myocardium by the backbone of the transplanted MNP [20]. This mechanical support effect can explain the observed favourable outcomes. Additionally, the conductivity of MNP could make a bridge between the infarcted myocardium and the healthy region to their electrical connection [55].

In the timing subgroup analysis, none of the outcomes were favourable at 1-week post-MNP transplantation. Although the outcomes were favourable in all 2, 3, and 4 weeks. The phenomena following MI can be categorized into three stages: Necrosis and inflammation in the first hours to day 4. Following myocyte necrosis, primarily neutrophils and subsequently monocyte/macrophage cells can infiltrate to scavenge debris and dead tissues [60, 61]. In the next steps, granulation or proliferation occurs up to 4–10 days post-MI. It was suggested that fibroblasts proliferate and transdifferentiate into cardiomyofibroblasts, followed by aberrant ECM production, such as collagen deposition [61, 62]. Eventually, the remodelling is initiated during weeks to months post-MI. The creation of fibrotic scar from granulation tissue maturation leads to adverse ventricular remodelling and cardiac function [60, 61]. However, 28 days is too short a time to evaluate the long-term cardiac changes post-MI [53]. In subgroup analysis based on animal species, p value of fibrosis in the rat group was on the borderline (p = 0.05); thus, the final decision was made based on CI.

In the cargo subgroup analysis, none of the outcomes in the MP and cytokine groups were favourable. The cell group showed improvement in angiogenesis but not in EF. Conversely, all outcomes in the factor, combine, NP, NP + cargo, and MP + cargo groups were favourable for cardiac repair. Similarly, most outcomes in the EV, EV + cargo, and drug groups were positive. Specifically, the EF and FS in the EV group improved, and fibrosis was reduced, although angiogenesis did not decrease. The EF and FS in both the EV + cargo and drug groups were also enhanced. However, a favourable effect on fibrosis was not shown.

In recent years, cell-based therapy has emerged as a promising approach to enhance neovascularization and cardiac regeneration following MI [63]. The cells mentioned in the cell subgroup include induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC)-derived cardiomyocytes (CMs) [46] and cardiac stromal cells (CSCs) [47]. Transplanted iPSC-derived CMs can integrate into the myocardium, forming functional grafts that improve cardiac function and remodelling [64]. Although their immaturity primarily restricts their mechanisms of action [65]. It has been shown that transplanting CSCs can reduce apoptosis and promote angiomyogenesis [66, 67]. Despite these benefits, the cell retention in the transplanted area is poor. However, this challenge has been addressed by the MNP strategy. Also noted that the proliferation of cells and their secretion capacity may be limited by encapsulation [68].

The factors used in the factor subgroup include L-Arginine (l-Arg) [57], bioengineered proteins including mussel adhesive protein (MAP), MAP-VEGF (Vascular endothelial growth factor), and MAP-RGD [43]. L-Arg reaction with inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) can produce in situ NO, which plays a key role in cardioprotective effects by decreasing reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels [57]. For the development of bioengineered proteins, Escherichia coli was used. Data showed appropriate characteristics, such as excellent interfacial adhesion, making it promising for biomedical applications [43]. For MNP transplantation on the cardiac surface, the use of such bio-glue can help retain MNP for a prolonged period [48]. Additionally, it has been shown that the fusion of peptides derived from VEGF (MAP-VEGF) and fibronectin (MAP-RGD) with MAP accelerates the proliferation and migration of endothelial cells in vitro by enhancing the retention and stability of original angiogenic growth factors. Transplantation of MNP containing referred peptides in a rat MI model delayed ventricular remodelling and decreased fibrosis [43].

Yuan et al. [49], Wang et al. [50], Huang et al. [51], and Mei et al. [52] utilized exosomes isolated from mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) in MNP transplantation. Exosomes are nanoscale carriers containing a bilayer membrane and various cargoes, including nucleic acids (mRNA, microRNA, DNA fragments), lipids, and proteins with different biological effects [69–71]. Specifically, MSC-derived exosomes participate in cardiac regeneration by promoting the proliferation of CMs, angiogenesis, and preventing fibrosis [72]. Yuan et al. subjected MI models to MNP containing miR-29b mimic loaded into exosomes, alleviating inflammation, decreasing infarct size, and thickening the wall of the left ventricular [49]. Of note, Huang et al. used small interfering RNA (siRNA) to silence Response 1 (EGR1), an upregulated transcription factor in the MI pathological environment involved in cellular stress and autophagy. Delivering exosome-loaded siRNA to damaged cardiac tissue via MNP helped promote cardiac function and decrease fibrosis [51]. It is important to note that the effectiveness of exosome therapy heavily depends on biodistribution, which MNP has demonstrated as a promising delivery route by increasing the accumulation and retention time of exosomes.

The nanoparticles (NPs) used in the current study include AuNPs, ZIF-8 [40], poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA) NPs [14], cerium dioxide NPs (CeNPs), CLM [48], dual complementary liposome NPs (DLC) [50], and zeolitic imidazolate frameworks-90 (ZIF-90) NPs [57]. Biocompatible AuNPs serve as electrical conductors and help restore the electrical signals of damaged cardiac tissues. Chen et al. administered miR-30d with AuNPs. The role of miR-30d in preventing adverse remodelling of infarcted tissue through cardiomyocyte survival and reduced scar formation has been previously demonstrated by the same group. Furthermore, the use of ZIF-8 NPs as non-viral vectors protected miR-30d from lysosomal degradation. Application of AuNPs along with miR-30d contributed to the electrophysical behaviour of this system [40]. PLGA, also known as “smart polymers,” provides an effective platform for the targeted and sustained release of agents and promotes the regeneration mechanisms of MI [14]. Hu et al. loaded human bone marrow MSC-secreted factors onto PLGA NPs. These NPs serve as a secondary controller for agent delivery beyond the MNP [14]. Additionally, the secretome modulates the immune system, including the polarization of macrophages, the activation of NK cells, T cells, and dendritic cells, as well as the induction of pyroptosis in non-immune cells and inflammation through paracrine mechanisms [73]. Meanwhile, CeNPs have been administered for their anti-inflammatory, reactive oxygen species scavenging, and antioxidant potential in the MI inflammatory condition. CLM, a micro-nano structure comprising curcumin and l-Arg loaded into mesoporous silica NPs along with CeNPs, was utilized in the bilayer MNP. Curcumin provides excellent sonosensitivity and nitric oxide release from l-Arg, which promotes angiogenesis, thromboprophylaxis, and anti-fibrotic effects [48]. Additionally, favourable therapeutic effect of l-Arg loaded into ZIF-90 NP on MI model was confirmed by Mao et al. [57].

Huang et al. utilized encapsulated IL-4 within PLGA MP as MNP cargo. Sustained release of IL-4 from this system makes polarization of macrophage from pro-inflammatory to anti-inflammatory state, thereby decreasing signalling of endothelial inflammatory and re-entry of cardiomyocyte into cell cycle [58].

Galunisertib, a TGF-β (transforming growth factor-beta) receptor I kinase inhibitor, was administered by Chen et al. as an antifibrotic drug for MI treatment [39]. This local use of Galunisertib has overcome the challenge of its broad administration and the risk of cardiac rupture [74]. Additionally, Huang et al. applied S-Adenosyl-L-homocysteine (SAH) to inhibit the methyltransferase-like 3 and 14 complex, which promotes the survival of CMs in the early stage and suppresses fibrosis formation in the late stage [42]. VEGF and interleukin-10 (IL-10) [46] were used for cytokine therapy to enhance revascularization and suppress inflammation, respectively. However, cytokine therapy alone, according to the pathological environment of the myocardium post-MI, may not be adequate to repair [75]. In the combination therapy group, an experiment conducted by Sun and co-workers treated mice MI models using MNP integrated with iPSC-derived CMs, VEGF, and IL-10, resulting in improved cardiac function preservation [46]. Application of exosomes, carrying VEGF-laden DLC NP as MNP therapeutic cargoes, with rapid VEGF release, is crucial for early vascular network reconstruction in MI [50]. Sequentially released methylprednisolone (an anti-inflammatory drug), IL-10, and VEGF were integrated into different parts of MNP and transplanted into MI models by He, F. et al., addressing various pathological stages post-MI and alleviating cardiac hypertrophy, inflammation, and promoting angiogenesis [53]. The coincidental use of the cell (cardiomyocyte) and EV could be seen in Liu et al.‘s research [54].

The existence of significant heterogeneity in some cases can be attributed to diversity in material, geometry, or stiffness of microneedles. Besides, the discrepancy in animal species used to induce MI may lead to this heterogeneity. Additionally, the time difference in outcome measurement and the variety of carried cargo could also play a role.

Limitations and perspectives directions

Based on current data, MNPs are a valid delivery platform for transplantation and can be recommended as an alternative therapy in a clinical setting after the conduction of clinical trials. Despite these promising findings, the field is new, and few studies have been conducted until now in the MI animal models. It is suggested that further research, particularly in large animal models, should be done. Although some studies were excluded from meta-analysis due to a lack of enough statistical information, such as the number and SD. Furthermore, there was still a high heterogeneity in the conducted meta-analysis.

Conclusion

The current systematic review and meta-analysis focus on assessing the potential therapeutic effects of MNPs with or without cargo compared to control groups in animal MI models. The results demonstrate that empty MNP transplantation can promote ischemic heart regeneration mainly by improving EF, FS, angiogenesis, and fibrosis reduction. These findings were consistent in subgroup analyses based on time and animal species. On the other side, treatment of infarcted myocardium with cargo-containing MNP, especially factor, NP + cargo, MP + cargo, and combination therapy, showed overall favourable therapeutic effects across all outcomes.
