N-Glycoproteomics Sample Preparation Using Commercially Available Cellulose Magnetic Particles
Authors: Ken Hanzawa, Miki Tanaka-Okamoto, Matej Nemcic, Yasuhide Miyamoto, Kazuki Nakajima
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGlycopeptide preparation is a critical step in bottom-up glycoproteomics. In this study, a robust method for N-glycopeptide preparation was developed using low-cost, commercially available magnetic particles. These particles function as a solid phase for hydrophilic interaction liquid chromatography (HILIC) to enrich N-glycopeptides from mixtures of N-glycopeptides and non-glycosylated peptides. Two types of magnetic particles, carboxylated polymer beads and cellulose resin, were compared. The latter demonstrated higher efficiency in N-glycopeptide recovery and non-glycopeptide removal. Biological samples often contain impurities such as salts and detergents that reduce efficiency and reproducibility of trypsin digestion and subsequent HILIC purification. To remove these impurities, we combined an optimized HILIC extraction protocol with a single-pot solid-phase-enhanced sample preparation protocol (SP3) for N-glycopeptide enrichment. Blood-derived samples (serum/plasma) were first tested. Compared with in-solution digestion, the method improved digestion efficiency and achieved N-glycopeptide recoveries comparable to those of conventional HILIC solid-phase extraction in a single tube. This method can be used for N-glycopeptide preparation not only from albumin-immunoglobulin-depleted serum but also from tissue extracts. Furthermore, the method has been successfully applied to glycoproteomic analysis of clinical serum and tissue samples from patients with gastric cancer, enabling detection of cancer-related alterations in glycoproteins.Keywords: glycopeptides, magnetic particles, solid-phase extraction, glycoproteomics, gastric cancer

Glycosylation is the most common post-translational modification of proteins and increasingly influences protein function in a site-specific manner. Changes in the glycosylation patterns of proteins have been reported in several diseases, including cancer (1). Cancer-related alterations leading to changes in the expression of tumor-associated carbohydrate antigens, such as CA19-9 and sialyl-Tn antigens, are routinely used as tumor markers in clinical practice. Bottom-up N-glycoproteomics is a promising approach for biomarker discovery because it provides qualitative and quantitative information on carrier glycoproteins, glycosylation sites, and glycan composition.

However, the approach is limited by the poor ionization efficiency of glycopeptides in mass spectrometry measurements. The presence of abundant residual non-glycosylated peptides (non-glycopeptides) suppresses glycopeptide ionization; therefore, non-glycosylated peptides must be removed from the sample. Hydrophilic interaction liquid chromatography (HILIC) is a common approach for broad glycopeptide enrichment (non-specific to glycan structures) (2). In HILIC solid-phase extraction, the glycan moiety promotes the separation of glycopeptides from non-glycopeptides. HILIC stationary phases have been used for glycopeptide purification (3). ZIC-HILIC (sulfobetaine) is widely used, and the addition of trifluoroacetic acid (TFA), an ion-pairing reagent, to organic solvents enhances the removal efficiency of non-glycopeptides (4, 5, 6). Polysaccharides, such as cellulose, exhibit excellent performance as HILIC solid phase for purifying glycans and glycopeptides (7, 8). Furthermore, HILIC solid-phase extraction (SPE) with magnetic beads, including ZIC-HILIC and amino group-modified magnetic particles, enriches glycopeptides efficiently like that of conventional SPE (9, 10). Magnetic particles with various surface structures have also been developed for glycopeptide enrichment (3). However, reports on readily available and cost-effective methods are limited.

In conventional proteomics, a magnetic bead-based preparation method called single-pot solid-phase–enhanced sample preparation (SP3) is widely used as one of the most efficient protein preparation methods for biological samples, including blood, cells, and tissues (11, 12, 13). This protocol is suitable for automation to improve the sample preparation throughput (14). The original SP3 method uses a mixture of hydrophilic and hydrophobic Sera-Mag Speedbead carboxylated particles, which are cost-effective and commercially available polymer-based magnetic particles. When added to the sample solution, these magnetic particles clean-up proteins before protease digestion and peptides after digestion. This protein clean-up before digestion has been suggested to be driven by protein aggregation on beads by the addition of an organic solvent, termed protein aggregation capture (PAC), which is less affected by the surface chemistry of the particles, indicating that a variety of other particle types can be used (15, 16). Therefore, digestion of proteins captured on particle surfaces (on-bead digestion) achieves higher protease digestion efficiency than conventional in-solution digestion (15). PAC followed by on-bead digestion using the SP3 method can be applied to glycoproteomic sample preparation (17, 18).

An HILIC-related mechanism is presumed to underline peptide clean-up after digestion on magnetic beads (19). In this method, a technique that uses carboxylate-modified magnetic beads solely for peptide clean-up after in-solution protein digestion, termed the SP2 method, has been reported, showing that phosphorylated peptides and glycopeptides can also be recovered (20). To perform this process in a single tube, the peptide clean-up step can be achieved by directly adding acetonitrile (95% final concentration for proteomics) after PAC-based bead SP3 preparation. Further investigation may enable the development of a more SP3-specialized protocol for N-glycopeptide preparation, which would streamline multi-sample glycoproteomic analysis.

Low-cost cellulose magnetic particles are commercially available for automated nucleic acid purification (21, 22). These cellulose-type magnetic particles may also be available for glycopeptide removal. Therefore, in this study, we developed a cost-effective, high-throughput N-glycoproteomic sample preparation protocol. The preparation protocols were optimized using standard proteins, blood (serum and plasma), and tissue samples. The method was evaluated by analyzing serum and gastric tissue samples from patients with gastric cancer. This study provides new insights into N-glycopeptide preparation, including throughput improvements and adaptation for future automation.

Experimental procedures

Materials

Chemicals were purchased from FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation, Tokyo Chemical Industry, and Sigma-Aldrich, unless otherwise stated. The solvents used for liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) were purchased from Kanto Chemical. ReliaPrep Resin (#A175B) and Trypsin/Lys-C Mix, MS Grade (#V5072) were purchased from Promega. Sera-Mag SpeedBead carboxylate-modified [E3] and Sera-Mag SpeedBead carboxylate-modified [E7] magnetic particles were purchased from Cytiva (Marlborough, MA).

Human serum (pooled) for preliminary and method development studies was purchased from Cosmo Bio. Human plasma (pooled) for preliminary and method development studies was purchased from Rockland Immunochemicals, Inc. The rat brain tissue was purchased from Rockland Immunochemicals, Inc, which maintains ethical, welfare, and regulatory standards.

Serum and tissue samples from patients with gastric cancer (n = 10, males 8, females 2, mean age 64.5 years) and cancer-free volunteers as normal controls (n = 20, males 14, females 6, mean age 69.5 years) were obtained from the Osaka International Cancer Institute (Supplemental Table S1). The local ethics committees of the Osaka International Cancer Institute and Gifu University approved the study (Protocol: 1802215371-4). The study was complied with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients and volunteers.

Blood was drawn from participants into vacuum blood-collection tubes (INSEPAK, Sekisui Medical, 1 cm × 10 cm, 7 ml, Storage tube, 2.0 ml polypropylene tube, BIO-BIK). The tubes were centrifuged at 3200 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C. Sera were aliquoted into 1 ml Eppendorf safe-lock tubes and stored without any additives at −80 °C until further handling and analysis during a maximum of 8 years. Samples were subjected to a maximum of one freeze–thaw cycle over the entire study.

Human Serum Albumin (HSA) and Immunoglobulin (Ig) Depletion of Serum

Depletion of HSA and Ig from the serum was performed using a High-Select HSA/Ig Depletion Resin (Thermo Fisher Scientific) packed in MicroSpin Empty Columns (Cytiva). Before use, the shipping solution of the Depletion Resin (phosphate-buffered saline) was replaced with 20 mM HEPES-NaOH buffer, pH 7.4, containing 150 mM NaCl and 0.09% (w/v) NaN3. Using the manufacturer’s protocol, the protein-containing flow-through fractions were collected from 10 μl of serum or plasma.

In-Solution Digestion of Serum, Plasma, and Standard Proteins

Serum or plasma (non-depleted, up to 2.5 μl; HSA/Ig-depleted, up to 10 μl) or standard protein (up to 100 μg) was dried in a Protein LoBind tube (Eppendorf). The sample was redissolved in 50 μl of 200 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.5, with 4.8 mM sodium deoxycholate (SDC) and 4.8 mM sodium N-lauroylsarcosinate (SLS) to promote solubilization. Next, after adding a solution (10 μl) of 50 mM tris(2-carboxyethyl)phosphine (TCEP)-HCl and 200 mM chloroacetamide (CAA), the solution was heated at 95 °C for 10 min for reduction, carbamidomethylation, and denaturation. The sample solution was then diluted to 100 μl with water, CaCl2 [2 mM final concentration, for trypsin stabilization (23, 24, 25)], and 1 μg of Trypsin/Lys-C Mix (Promega) and incubated at 37 °C for 16 h for digestion.

PAC On-Bead Digestion With Magnetic Particles of Serum and Plasma Proteins

Serum or plasma (non-depleted, up to 2.5 μl; HSA/Ig-depleted, up to 10 μl) was dried in a Protein LoBind tube (Eppendorf). The sample was redissolved in 100 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.5 (100 μl) with 2% sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS), 10 mM TCEP-HCl, and 40 mM CAA. The solution was heated at 95 °C for 10 min for denaturation, reduction, and carbamidomethylation. After cooling the solution to room temperature, 30 μl of a 5% (w/v; 1.5 mg) suspension of magnetic particles (Sera-Mag Speedbead carboxylate-modified particles or ReliaPrep Resin) and 520 μl of ethanol to a final concentration of 80% (v/v) were added. Sera-Mag Speedbead carboxylate-modified particles were used as a 1:1 mixture of hydrophilic [E3] and hydrophobic [E7] types. The solution was incubated in a ThermoMixer (Eppendorf) at 25 °C for 10 min at 1500 rpm to induce PAC. The supernatant was then removed using a magnetic rack. To wash the particle-protein aggregates, 800 μl of 80% ethanol was added and removed after 2 min of incubation. This washing step was repeated three times to remove the particle–protein aggregates. After carefully removing the ethanol, the magnetic particles were resuspended in 100 μl of digestion solution containing 100 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.5, 2.4 mM SDC, 2.4 mM SLS, 2 mM CaCl2, and 1 μg of Trypsin/Lys-C Mix. The suspension was incubated in a ThermoMixer at 37 °C for 16 h at 1000 rpm for on-bead protein digestion.

Preparation of Tissue Sample Homogenates

Tissue samples were stored at −80 °C. Rat brain tissue was crushed into powder while frozen with dry ice, and a portion of the powder was used for subsequent preparation. For human clinical gastric tissue samples, cancer-containing tissue (cancer tissue) and adjacent normal tissue were isolated from the bulk tissues of surgical specimens obtained from patients with gastric cancer. The tissues were cut into small pieces while frozen with dry ice, and a portion was used for further preparation. Tissue was homogenized in a tissue lysis buffer containing 100 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.5), 2% (w/v) SDS, 1% (v/v) Triton X-100, 0.5% (w/v) SDC, 100 mM NaCl, 1× cOmplete, EDTA-free protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche) with 2% volume of Benzonase (Millipore), and 2 mM MgCl2 using a BioMasher II with a PowerMasher (Nippi). After adding the lysis buffer and further homogenizing the tissue mixture, debris was precipitated by centrifugation for 10 min at 15,000×g and 4 °C. The supernatant was collected as a tissue homogenate solution. A small aliquot was reserved for the BCA assay, and the remaining portion was stored at −80 °C after adding EDTA-2Na to a final concentration of 2 mM.

Protein Digestion of Tissue Samples by In-Solution Digestion

In-solution protein digestion of the tissue samples was performed after acetone precipitation. Briefly, tissue homogenates corresponding to 100 μg of protein were diluted to 100 μl with the tissue lysis buffer without the protease inhibitor cocktail. Proteins were precipitated by adding cold acetone (400 μl). After incubation at −20 °C for 60 min, the samples were centrifuged at 15,000×g at 4 °C for 10 min, and the supernatant was discarded. Residual acetone was dried at room temperature for 30 min. The precipitate was redissolved in 100 μl of 8 M urea containing 100 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.5, and reduction and carbamidomethylation were performed using DTT and IAA. The solution was diluted with 100 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.5 and CaCl2 (2 mM final concentration) to reduce the final urea concentration to less than 1 M. For digestion, 2 μg of Trypsin/Lys-C Mix was added, the mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 16 h.

Protein Digestion of Tissue Samples by PAC On-Bead Digestion With Magnetic Particles

Tissue homogenate corresponding to 100 μg of protein was diluted to 100 μl with tissue lysis buffer without the protease inhibitor cocktail containing TCEP-HCl and CAA (final concentrations of 10 mM and 40 mM, respectively). The solution was heated at 95 °C for 10 min to achieve protein denaturation, reduction, and carbamidomethylation. After cooling the solution to room temperature, 20 μl of a 5% (w/v; 1 mg) suspension of magnetic particles (Sera-Mag Speedbead carboxylate-modified particles or ReliaPrep Resin) and 480 μl ethanol were added to a final concentration of 80% (v/v). The mixture was incubated in a ThermoMixer (Eppendorf) at 25 °C for 10 min at 1000 rpm. The supernatant was then removed using a magnetic rack. For washing the particle–protein aggregates, 800 μl of 80% ethanol was added and removed after 2 min of incubation. The washing step was repeated three times. After carefully removing the ethanol, the magnetic particles were resuspended in 100 μl of digestion solution containing 100 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.5, 2.4 mM SDC, 2.4 mM SLS, 2 mM CaCl2, and 1 μg of Trypsin/Lys-C Mix (Promega). The suspension was incubated in a ThermoMixer at 37 °C for 16 h with shaking at 1000 rpm for on-bead protein digestion.

Standard Conditions of HILIC N-Glycopeptide Enrichment by Magnetic Particles

After spinning down the digested sample solution, a glycopeptide loading solution (acetonitrile containing 1% TFA) was added to achieve final acetonitrile concentrations of 90% for the Sera-Mag Speedbead carboxylate-modified particles and 82.5% for the ReliaPrep Resin. To improve glycopeptide enrichment, an ion-pairing reagent, TFA, more hydrophobic than formic acid was added to the solution. For in-solution digestion samples, magnetic particles were added before loading the glycopeptides. As a standard, 5 μl of 4 μM sialylglycopeptide (SGP; Tokyo Chemical Industry) was added. The solution was incubated in a ThermoMixer at 25 °C for 30 min at 1500 rpm for glycopeptide loading onto the magnetic particles. The supernatant was removed using a magnetic rack. After washing, which was repeated twice, the magnetic particles were incubated with 800 μl of wash solution (for the Sera-Mag particles, 90% acetonitrile with 1% TFA; for ReliaPrep Resin, 82.5% acetonitrile with 1% TFA) in the ThermoMixer at 25 °C for 30 min at 1500 rpm, and the supernatant was removed. The glycopeptides were eluted from the particles by adding 300 μl of elution solution (20 mM ammonium formate in 30% acetonitrile) and incubating in a ThermoMixer at 25 °C for 10 min at 1000 rpm. The glycopeptides were collected on a magnetic rack and transferred to a new LoBind tube. The solution was then evaporated using a centrifugal evaporator. The glycopeptides were reconstituted with 50 μl of 1% acetic acid in water and filtered using a Millex LH filter (4-mm; Millipore) coupled to a 0.5 ml LoBind tube by centrifugation at 2500×g for 2 min. The samples were stored at −20 °C for several weeks or at −80 °C for a long time until LC-MS analysis.

Additional Reversed-phase SPE of Gastric Tissue Glycopeptides

To avoid impairing performance in the LC-MS system after injection of gastric tissue extract, an additional purification step for N-glycopeptides was required, as described in the result section. PAC on-bead digestion and HILIC purification were performed as described above. Glycopeptides were then eluted from the magnetic particles using 300 μl of 0.1% formic acid in water and loaded onto a homemade reverse-phase column. The reverse-phase column was prepared by suspending the reverse-phase material (Wakosil 50C18; FUJIFILM Wako, Osaka, Japan) in 80% ethanol and packing 50 mg of the material into a 300 μl tip (Gilson) with glass wool placed at the bottom as a filter. For packing, the tip was placed on top of a 2 ml tube using an adapter (GL Sciences, Fukushima, Japan) and centrifuged at 1000×g for 5 min. Before sample loading, the column was conditioned sequentially with 250 μl acetonitrile, 250 μl of 0.1% formic acid in 50% acetonitrile, and 250 μl of 0.1% formic acid in water. This conditioning step was repeated twice, with centrifugation at 1000×g for 5 min. Samples were slowly passed through the column by centrifugation (300×g for 5 min), and the column was then transferred to a new LoBind 2 ml tube. The glycopeptides were eluted sequentially with 150 μl of 0.1% formic acid in 30% acetonitrile and 300 μl of 0.1% formic acid in 50% acetonitrile. The elute was then collected and evaporated using a centrifugal evaporator. Reconstitution, filtration, and storage were performed as described previously.

Nano-LC-MS for N-Glycoproteomics

Glycopeptide samples were diluted in 0.1% formic acid in water and transferred to ProteoSave autosampler vials (AMR) or 96-well low-protein-binding PCR plates (Sarstedt, Nümbrecht, Germany). LC-MS analysis used a Vanquish-Neo UHPLC system coupled to an Orbitrap Exploris 240 mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Samples trapped on a PepMap Neo Trap Cartridge (0.3 × 5 mm, 5 μm; Thermo Scientific) and separated on an emitter-integrated capillary column (C18 Nano HPLC capillary column 75-3-15; 0.075 × 150 mm, 3 μm; Nikkyo Technos) were used, respectively. The separation column was mounted on a Nanospray Flex ES072 Ion Source (Thermo Scientific) with a PRSO-V2 column oven set to 40 °C (Sonation). Solvent A was 0.1% formic acid in water and solvent B was 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile (Kanto Chemical, Tokyo, Japan). After sample loading, solvent B was maintained at 0% for 2 min, linearly increased to 1% for 0.5 min, and then to 44.5% for 58 min. The ratio of solvent B was then increased to 95% for 5 min, maintained for 4 min for washing, and then decreased to 0% for 0.5 min. The flow rate was maintained at 0.3 μl/min during separation and 0.4 μl/min during washing and equilibration. To reduce carry-over, two short blank runs were performed before and after each sample. In the first run, 5 to 15 μl of a 20% formic acid–50% trifluoroethanol mixture was injected to wash the trap-column, and the post-loading separation step was omitted, and column washing was performed immediately (26). In the second run, 5 μl of 0.1% formic acid in water was injected, followed by a short gradient and column wash. Both the trap-column and separation column were equilibrated with solvent A. Detailed settings for sample loading, separation gradient, washing, and equilibration are provided in Supplemental Table S2.

MS was performed in positive ion mode, with a spray voltage of 1800 V and an ion transfer capillary temperature of 275 °C. Full MS scans were acquired at resolution of 120,000, scan range 500 to 1800 m/z, user-defined lock mass m/z 667.17640 (15 ppm), RF lens offset 70%, AGC target 300% (3e6), and maximum injection time 50 ms. MS2 scans were performed in data-dependent mode, with 1.5 s cycle time, 1.6 m/z isolation window, and normalized higher-energy collisional dissociation (HCD) collision energies of 20, 30, and 40 (27). The spectra were recorded at a resolution of 30,000 over an m/z scan range of 150 to 2000, with an AGC target of 100% (1e5), maximum injection time of 75 ms, intensity threshold of 5.0e3, charge states of 2 to 8, and dynamic exclusion of 15 s (10 ppm). For clinical samples (serum and gastric tissues), slightly modified MS settings were used: full MS scan range 650 to 1800 m/z, user-defined lock mass m/z 667.17640 (12 ppm), dynamic exclusion 20 s (10 ppm), MS2 AGC target 50% (5e4), and MS2 maximum injection time 100 ms. List of the data files is shown in Supplemental Table S4.

Data Analysis

N-glycopeptides and peptides were identified and quantified using Proteome Discoverer 3.0 (Thermo Fisher Scientifics) combined with the Byonic node (v5.8.6; Protein Metrics, Cupertino, CA). Reference proteomes for humans (UP000005640_9606.fasta, 20,572 protein sequences; downloaded on Mar 17, 2025) and rats (UP000002494_10116, 22,366 protein sequences; downloaded on Mar 18, 2025) were obtained from UniProt. For Byonic searches, the mass tolerance settings were 6 ppm for precursors and 20 ppm for fragments. Enzymatic cleavage was set as a fully specific C-terminal R and K, allowing up to 2 missed cleavages. Cysteine carbamidomethylation was set to “fixed,” while methionine oxidation, N-terminal glutamine to pyro-glutamine, N-terminal cysteine ammonia-loss, and N-terminal acetylation were set to “common 1.” N-glycan modification was set to “rare 1,” and a built-in glycan database, “132 human,” was applied for all searches, unless otherwise noted. Precursor isotope off by x was set to “No error check.” A protein false discovery rate (FDR) was set to 1% or 20 reverse counts. Search results were filtered using the Peptide Groups and Peptide Spectral Matches (PSMs) tabs: Byonic score >150; Delta mod score >10; posterior error probability (PEP) 2D < 0.05; and FDR 2D < 0.01. In the Peptide Groups tab of the results, peptides displayed as “High” in the “Found in Sample” column were counted as their identifications. Label-free quantification of (N-glyco-) peptides was performed using a combination of Minora Feature Detector, Feature Mapper, and Precursor Ions Quantifier nodes embedded in Proteome Discoverer 3.0. Peptides of unique + razor were used for quantification. Precursor abundance was based on intensity. In the Peptide Groups tab of the result output by the software, raw (unnormalized) abundance values were used for further quantitative analysis of the peptides, and peptides displayed as “High” in the “Found in Sample” column were counted as their identifications.

For clinical samples, normalized peak abundance values were used for comparison between clinical samples. Search settings were slightly modified: some multi-fucosylated glycan compositions were removed from the glycan database (Supplemental Table S3), and the mass tolerance for fragments was narrowed (10 ppm). In addition, peptide group data were filtered based on the following criteria: Modifications do not contain “oxidation,” “ammonia-loss,” and “pyro-glu”; Master Protein Description does not contain “immunoglobulin”; only for serum samples; and Glycan Composition is “not blank” as well as more strict PEP 2D, <0.01. Additional data processing steps for clinical samples are described in the “Experimental Design and Statistical Rationale” section. Peptide group and PSM data of the results are shown in Supplemental Tables S5–S14.

Experimental Design and Statistical Rationale

We employed a case–control design comparing patients with gastric cancer (n = 10) with healthy control subjects (n = 20). The independent variable was disease status (cancer vs. control), and the dependent variables were the abundances of individual glycopeptides identified and quantified by Proteome Discoverer-Byonic from LC-MS and LC-MS/MS data. All 30 biological samples were analyzed using two independent glycoproteomic sample preparation methods, PAC and ISD, enabling direct comparison of method performance in terms of glycopeptide coverage, reproducibility, and differential abundance detection. For tissue analysis, matched cancer and adjacent normal tissue were collected from the same 8 (out of available 10) patients, constituting a paired within-subject design in which each patient served as their own biological control, eliminating inter-individual variability as a confounding factor. To minimize potential run-order effects during LC-MS analysis, sample injection order was randomized within each analytical batch. Serum and tissue samples were each processed and analyzed within a single analytical batch per method, eliminating the need for batch correction within individual differential abundance analyses and removing a potential source of systematic technical variation. As all statistical comparisons were performed within sample type and preparation method, rather than between analytical batches, batch effects were not expected to confound the primary analyses. No technical replicates were acquired; all statistical analyses were therefore based on biological replicates only. All downstream data analysis was performed computationally using pre-specified pipelines.

Raw glycopeptide abundances were filtered such that features present in fewer than 80% of serum samples or 75% of tissue samples were excluded prior to further analysis, reducing the influence of sparsely detected features and improving the robustness of downstream statistical analyses. Abundances were log2-transformed and median-normalized per sample to correct for potential between-sample loading differences. Missing values were imputed using the MinProb method (q = 0.01, tune.sigma = 0.3), which is appropriate for the missing-not-at-random data characteristic of LC-MS glycoproteomics, where missingness is concentrated among low-abundance features (28), as assessed by inspection of data distributions and missingness patterns.

Differential abundance analysis was performed using the limma framework with empirical Bayes moderation (29), which stabilizes per-feature variance estimates by borrowing information across all quantified glycopeptides and is specifically suited to studies with small and unequal group sizes (30, 31). For serum samples, the model included age and sex as covariates to account for potential confounding. For tissue samples, a paired design with patient blocking via duplicate correlation was used, which inherently controls for fixed individual characteristics, including age and sex, while substantially increasing statistical power by removing inter-patient variance from the error term (30, 32). Multiple testing correction was applied using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure at FDR of 5%. No explicit outlier removal was performed; all samples passing quality control criteria were retained for analysis. Empirical Bayes moderation was subsequently applied to stabilize feature-wise variance estimates and improve inference in the presence of limited replication (30, 31).

A formal a prior power calculation was not performed because reliable estimates of effect sizes and variance parameters for site-specific glycopeptide abundances were not available prior to the study, which limits the validity of conventional pre-study power estimation in exploratory high-dimensional glycoproteomics analyses (33). In addition, sample size was constrained by clinical sample availability. Statistical power was addressed through variance stabilization using empirical Bayes moderation, inclusion of paired analyses where applicable, control of major biological confounders, and FDR control.

Site-specific glycosylation analysis was performed by first calculating the relative abundance of each glycoform as a percentage of the total glycopeptide abundance observed at the corresponding glycosylation site within each sample. Differential site-specific glycosylation was then assessed using linear modeling with empirical Bayes variance moderation (limma), in which log2-transformed relative abundances were modeled as the response variable and cancer status as the primary explanatory variable. For tissue samples, paired analyses were performed using patient blocking via duplicate correlation, whereas serum analyses were conducted using unpaired models adjusted for age and sex. As a targeted secondary analysis intended to characterize glycosylation remodeling at disease-associated sites, site-specific examination was restricted to glycosylation sites for which at least one glycopeptide exhibited significant differential abundance in the primary analysis (Benjamini–Hochberg-adjusted p < 0.05). This approach reduced the multiple-testing burden and focused interpretation on glycosylation sites exhibiting evidence of disease-associated abundance changes.

All analyses were performed in R (version 4.4.0) (34) using the limma (v3.62.2) (30), imputeLCMD (v2.1) (35), and impute (v1.80.0) (36) packages. Figures were generated using ggplot2 (37).

Results

HILIC Enrichment of N-Glycopeptides Using Standard Proteins

We investigated the enrichment performance of commercially available and cost-effective magnetic particles: Sera-Mag Speedbead carboxylate-modified particles (styrene-acrylate polymer-based beads) and ReliaPrep Resin (cellulose-based particles). A standard hen egg yolk SGP spiked with bovine serum albumin tryptic-digested peptides was selectively recovered using HILIC extraction with magnetic particles (Fig. 1A). In HILIC enrichment, the selection of an appropriate acetonitrile concentration is essential; thus, SGP recovery rates were compared under specific acetonitrile conditions. The HILIC recovery rates of SGP with ReliaPrep Resin were higher than those with Sera-Mag particles, and glycopeptide recovery was achieved even at lower acetonitrile concentrations (Fig. 1B). By contrast, the recovery rates of SGP with ReliaPrep Resin were similar to those with non-magnetic microcrystalline cellulose resin, which has been used as the gold-standard (Supplemental Fig. S1) (7, 8). Direct glycopeptide enrichment with magnetic particle-based HILIC was evaluated via in-solution digestion of human IgG (Fig. 1C). The N-glycopeptide enrichment procedure is shown in Supplemental Fig. S2A. The effect of acetonitrile concentration (with 1% TFA addition) on glycopeptide recovery and non-glycopeptide removal was tested using human IgG digests (Supplemental Fig. S2B). ReliaPrep Resin showed a low non-glycopeptide removal efficiency at acetonitrile concentrations >85%, whereas a high removal rate was observed at 80% (Supplemental Fig. S2C), but the glycopeptide recovery rate was reduced (Supplemental Fig. S2B). Therefore, an acetonitrile concentration of 80% to 85% is preferable for glycopeptide purification using ReliaPrep Resin (Fig. 1C). Sera-Mag particles required an acetonitrile concentration of 90% for sufficient glycopeptide recovery and non-glycopeptide removal, but the recovery rates were lower than those of ReliaPrep Resin.

Plasma N-Glycopeptide Preparation Using HILIC Enrichment Combined With In-Solution or PAC On-Bead Digestion

HILIC enrichment is affected by sample hydrophilicity and impurities such as salts and detergents. The PAC on-bead digestion method can adapt for direct HILIC enrichment by removing salt and reagents during pre-digestion. Herein, we compared HILIC glycopeptide enrichment directly after in-solution digestion or PAC on-beads digestion using two magnetic particle types (Fig. 2, A and B).

In-solution digestion was performed in Tris buffer with an SDC/SLS mixture, a surfactant that does not inhibit protease digestion (trypsin/Lys-C) (38). SDC and SLS, which were added to the digestion buffer, are phase-transfer surfactants that dissolve in organic solvents under acidic conditions and were removed under acidic HILIC conditions (38). After digestion with trypsin/Lys-C, magnetic particles were added as HILIC solid phases to remove non-glycopeptides (Fig. 2A, left).

For the PAC method, samples were dissolved in Tris buffer with SDS as a denaturing agent, and reductive alkylation was performed (14). Magnetic particles and ethanol were added, and the proteins were captured onto the particles and cleaned to remove the salts, reagents, and SDS. Subsequently, the aggregated proteins on the magnetic particles were suspended in a buffer containing SDC/SLS, as in the in-solution method, and digested with trypsin/Lys-C (12). After on-bead digestion, the samples underwent HILIC for N-glycopeptide enrichment. Thus, the digestion efficiency of the PAC method improved compared with that of the in-solution method (Fig. 2A, right).

Both Sera-Mag Speedbead carboxylate-modified particles and ReliaPrep Resin were applicable to plasma N-glycopeptides, but the degree of purification differed. For peptide identification and abundance in preparations with Sera-Mag beads, the in-solution digestion method resulted in a higher number of residual non-glycopeptides (Fig. 2, C–E). By contrast, the PAC method yielded a higher number of N-glycopeptide identifications. These results suggest that prewashing the sample with PAC improves the efficiency of direct HILIC purification with Sera-Mag beads.

When using ReliaPrep Resin, the effect of acetonitrile concentration on HILIC extraction was optimized, which showed a similar tendency to IgG digests; 82.5% was a suitable concentration to recover glycopeptides and remove non-glycopeptides during purification (Supplemental Fig. S3). Both in-solution digestion and PAC methods showed comparable N-glycopeptides and lower residual non-glycopeptide identification and abundance than PAC preparation using Sera-Mag particles. Thus, ReliaPrep Resin is suggested to be adaptable to various sample types. The identification numbers of glycopeptides in triplicate were reproducible at approximately 75% for all methods (Supplemental Fig. S4). In addition, to assess the enrichment efficiency of glycopeptides independently of the database search, GlyCounter software was used to analyze the glycan-diagnostic oxonium ions in the MS2 spectra (39) (Supplemental Fig. S5). The proportion of MS2 spectra classified as “likely to be glycopeptides” was higher in the PAC method using Sera-Mag beads and in both the in-solution digestion and PAC methods using ReliaPrep Resin than in the in-solution digestion method using Sera-Mag beads, confirming the trend observed in the Byonic database search (Supplemental Fig. S5A). The ratio of MS2 spectrum counts of each diagnostic oxonium ions also showed a similar trend (Supplemental Fig. S5B). For glycan composition, the in-solution method using Sera-Mag beads showed a slightly lower ratio of glycopeptides corresponding to multi-antennary glycans than other methods, suggesting that residual non-glycopeptides may hinder the detection of glycopeptides with larger glycans (Fig. 2F). For glycosylation site identification, Sera-Mag beads contained slightly more missed cleavages than ReliaPrep Resin, even in the PAC method (Fig. 2G, light blue). No differences were observed in the N-glycoprotein identification numbers (Fig. 2H). In addition, glycopeptide enrichment with formic acid instead of TFA as an ion-pairing reagent in the PAC method was tested. Both Sera-Mag beads and ReliaPrep Resin successfully enriched the glycopeptides, and the latter particles showed higher glycopeptide enrichment efficiency (Supplemental Fig. S6). Therefore, PAC-based SP3 subsequent to HILIC enrichment using cellulose magnetic beads improved N-glycopeptide preparation from non-depleted plasma samples.

PAC On-Bead Digestion Improved Glycopeptide Enrichment Efficiency in High-Abundant Protein-Depleted Samples

Another issue in bottom-up glycoproteomics is the wide dynamic range of blood protein concentration, ranging from albumin at mg/ml to cytokines at trace amounts. In proteomic and glycoproteomic analyses of plasma and serum, immunoaffinity depletion of abundant proteins is performed to enhance protein identification (40, 41, 42). However, affinity purification requires buffered saline, and salts are concentrated in depleted samples. These factors may affect the efficiency of subsequent PAC on-bead digestion. Moreover, the commercial immunoaffinity depletion resin used in this study contains phosphate-buffered saline as a shipping buffer; however, phosphate is poorly soluble in organic solvents including ethanol. Thus, we used depletion resins that had been resuspended in HEPES-buffered saline, which has high solubility in the ethanol used in the PAC method.

In this study, we evaluated the effects of serum albumin and Ig depletion (HSA-Ig-dep) and PAC on-bead digestion using cellulose magnetic particles (Fig. 3). In the non-depleted samples, the difference between the in-solution and PAC on-bead digestion was minimal, with PAC showing slightly improved non-glycopeptide removal (similar to Fig. 2). In the depleted samples, no significant difference was noted in the number of total N-glycopeptides compared to that in the non-depleted samples, whereas the proportion of Ig-related N-glycopeptides substantially decreased, and the proportion of N-glycopeptides derived from other glycoproteins increased (Fig. 3). The difference between in-solution digestion and PAC was more prominent in the depleted samples, where PAC increased the number and abundance of identified glycopeptides and removed residual non-glycopeptides.

Sample Preparation for Tissue N-Glycoproteomics

We evaluated the applicability of the magnetic particle-based glycopeptide preparation method for tissue samples. In tissue (glyco-)proteomics, proteins are isolated and solubilized in extraction buffers containing protease inhibitors, salts, and strong detergents, such as SDS, which reduce enzyme digestion efficiency and mass spectrometric detection. To minimize these effects on subsequent steps, we evaluated the PAC on-bead digestion method using rat brain homogenates as a model and compared it with in-solution digestion that included a pre-clean-up step (Fig. 4A). For in-solution digestion, proteins underwent acetone precipitation to remove detergents and salts, followed by redissolution with urea and carbamidomethylation. N-glycopeptide enrichment was performed using ReliaPrep Resin after protein digestion. PAC on-bead digestion was performed similarly to that for the serum and plasma samples described above.

To compare magnetic particles, we tested the performance of two types of beads prepared using the PAC method. All the tested methods yielded detectable N-glycopeptides (Fig. 4, B–G). However, the ratio of glycopeptides with missed cleavages was higher in the in-solution method (Fig. 4E), along with a slightly lower glycopeptide abundance (Fig. 4D). The PAC method with Sera-Mag Speedbead carboxylate-modified particles resulted in a significantly higher amount of residual non-glycopeptides, indicating a lower efficiency of glycopeptide purification (Fig. 4, B–D). The glycopeptide enrichment efficiency was also assessed by GlyCounter based on the diagnostic fragment ions in the MS2 spectra (Supplemental Fig. S7). The proportion of MS2 spectra classified as “likely glycopeptide” and that containing glycan diagnostic ions was higher in both preparation methods using ReliaPrep Resin than in the PAC method using Sera-Mag, supporting the above results. These results suggest that PAC on-bead digestion followed by direct HILIC purification using ReliaPrep Resin is better suited for tissue N-glycopeptide preparation. Regarding glycan types, oligomannose- and complex-/hybrid-types were predominant, with trace amounts of the paucimannose-type glycans (Hex (1–3)HexNAc (2)) also detected (Fig. 4G).

N-Glycoproteomics of Serum From Patients With Gastric Cancer

To test the feasibility of our approach, we applied a magnetic particle-based preparation method to serum N-glycoproteomics of clinical specimens. N-glycopeptides were prepared from HSA- and Ig-depleted serum of patients with gastric cancer (n = 10) and non-cancer controls (n = 20; Supplemental Table S1) using the PAC method with ReliaPrep Resin magnetic cellulose. First, the abundance of N-glycan types in the identified glycopeptides was compared between cohorts. Six main glycan groups were defined: bi-antennary and branched types with or without fucosylation, oligomannose, and other (Fig. 5A). The relative abundance of N-glycan compositions corresponding to branched (tri- or tetra-antennary), fucosylated structures was higher in patients with gastric cancer (7.4%) than in healthy individuals (6.1%), but no statistical significance was observed.

Unsupervised principal component analysis (PCA) based on N-glycopeptide abundance showed partial clustering between patients with gastric cancer and non-cancer controls; however, three patients (G1, G8, and G9) were positioned outside the control group in the PCA score plot (Fig. 5B). Several glycopeptides exhibited an increasing trend in patients with gastric cancer, with four N-glycopeptides significantly elevated (adjusted p < 0.05; Fig. 5C, Supplemental Tables S15 and S17). Glycopeptides elevated in patients with cancer were derived from acute-phase proteins (Fig. 5D), including α1-acid glycoprotein 1 (AGP1), hemopexin, and clusterin, and consisted exclusively of mono- and di-fucosylated glycoforms. The MS2 spectra of these glycopeptides in patients with gastric cancer mainly contained antennary fucose, as diagnostic ions at m/z 512 and 803, corresponding to NeuAc1HexNAc1Hex1dHex1 and HexNAc1Hex1dHex1, respectively (Supplemental Fig. S8). Furthermore, a comparison of site-specific glycosylation changes at N56 and N93 of AGP1, N145 of clusterin, and N64 of hemopexin revealed a trend toward increased fucosylation and decreased non-fucosylation in patients with cancer (Supplemental Fig. S9A). By contrast, 23 glycopeptides displayed significantly decreased absolute abundance in the serum samples of patients with cancer (Fig. 5C). Site-specific glycan composition analysis revealed altered glycosylation patterns in several of these glycopeptides (Supplemental Fig. S9C, Supplemental Table S21), including decreased sialylation (e.g., histidine-rich glycoprotein N125), reduced fucosylation (α2-macroglobulin N55), and decreased non-fucosylated tri-antennary structures accompanied by non-significant increase in bi-antennary and fucosylated tri-antennary structures (leucine-rich α2-glycoprotein N325).

Furthermore, to evaluate the magnetic particle-based sample preparation, we compared the samples with those prepared using conventional methods that do not employ magnetic particles (Supplemental Fig. S10). The conventional preparation consisted of in-solution digestion followed by HILIC glycopeptide enrichment using non-magnetic microcrystalline cellulose particles (referred to as the “PAC” and “ISD” methods; Supplemental Fig. S10A). PCA of samples prepared using the two workflows showed comparable separation between the non-cancer control group and patients with gastric cancer (Supplemental Fig. S10, B and C). By contrast, samples clustered distinctly according to the preparation method, suggesting that the two workflows generated systematically different glycoproteomics profiles (Supplemental Fig. S10C). Examination of the principal component loadings indicated that this separation was driven by differences in the abundance of multiple glycopeptides (Supplemental Tables S15, S17 and S19).

These results indicate that the magnetic particle-based glycopeptide preparation method detected cancer-associated glycoprotein changes comparably to the conventional workflow, although systematic differences were observed between the quantitative values obtained using the two methods. Notably, the conventional method identified a greater number of glycopeptides displaying significant alterations between the control group and patients with gastric cancer (Supplemental Fig. S10, D and E, Supplemental Tables S15, S17 and S19). Similar to the magnetic particle-based method, the conventional workflow identified glycopeptides elevated in patients with cancer that were derived from acute-phase proteins, including fucosylated AGP1 glycoforms (Supplemental Fig. S10F). In addition, the conventional workflow detected increased levels of oligomannose-type glycopeptides derived from complement C3 and α1-antichymotrypsin. Site-specific glycan composition analysis of glycopeptides exhibiting significantly altered levels revealed largely consistent trends between the two preparation methods (Supplemental Fig. S9).

N-Glycoproteomics of Gastric Cancer Tissue

We performed tissue N-glycoproteomics analysis of samples from surgical specimens using cancer and normal tissues adjacent to the cancer (n = 8; Supplemental Table S1). N-glycopeptides were prepared from gastric tissue extracts using both magnetic bead-based PAC and the conventional ISD method to enable direct comparison of downstream analytical performance.

During gastric tissue glycoproteomics analyses, we observed a marked deterioration in nano-LC column performance that warranted further investigation. Injection of N-glycopeptides prepared from gastric tissue significantly impaired nano-LC performance (Fig. 6A, left), resulting in markedly distorted chromatographic peak shapes. A performance test using plasma N-glycopeptides before and after gastric tissue injection confirmed substantial deterioration of chromatographic behavior. By contrast, injections of rat brain tissue digests did not produce this effect. However, porcine gastric mucin digests impaired performance in a similar manner, suggesting that the effect could be attributed to gastric tissue-derived contaminants that adsorb to the reversed-phase packing material of the trap-column in the nano-LC system (Supplemental Fig. S11). Column cleaning via injection of trifluoroethanol and a high concentration of formic acid partially restored LC-MS performance. Thus, an additional purification step was introduced after HILIC purification for the eluted N-glycopeptides by reversed-phase spin SPE using a C18 spin tip (Fig. 6B). Such reversed-phase-purified glycopeptide injections from gastric tissue samples did not impair LC-MS system performance (Fig. 6A, right). Consequently, this additional SPE step was incorporated into subsequent glycoproteomic analyses of clinical gastric tissue samples.

For the quantitative glycoproteomic analysis of tissue samples, we compared the abundance of N-glycan types across the identified glycopeptides according to the following groups: bi-antennary, tri- or tetra-antennary, hybrid/mono-antennary, oligomannose, paucimannose, and others (Fig. 6C). The relative abundance of N-glycan compositions corresponding to paucimannose glycans (HexNAc2Hex1–3) was significantly increased (adjusted p = 0.02) in cancer tissues (7.5%) compared with that in adjacent normal tissues (4.4%). Oligomannose-type glycans were enriched in cancer tissues, although this increase did not reach significance. By contrast, complex-type glycans exhibited a decreasing trend. PCA score plots showed clear separation between cancer and normal tissue samples, with the exception of one sample (Fig. 6D), although this separation became less distinct when the 95% confidence intervals were considered. In terms of absolute abundance, a total of 249 and 256 N-glycopeptides were significantly upregulated and downregulated in cancer tissues, respectively (adjusted p < 0.05; Fig. 6E, Supplemental Tables S16 and S18). Additionally, Figure 6F shows site-specific glycosylation changes of representative significantly altered glycopeptides, including CEACAM5, CEACAM6, and periostin. At the CEACAM5 N152 site, a decrease in Man9-oligomannose and conversely, an increase in the relative abundance of Man7-oligomannose and the bi-antennary, asialo, complex-type N-glycans were observed Supplemental Fig. S13, Supplemental Table S22). The glycosite at CEACAM6 N224 displayed an increase in Man3-pauci-mannose and a decrease in the structures corresponding to the disialyl bi-antennary complex-type glycan. Meanwhile, periostin glycopeptides exhibited increased relative abundance of glycans corresponding to small complex-type as well as disialylated fucosylated, bi-antennary structures, along with decreased abundance of the complex-type with HexNAc ≥5 compositions at the N599 site. MS2 spectra of these glycopeptides suggested that the ““HexNAc > Hex”” compositions (e.g. HexNAc5Hex4Fuc1) were derived from HexNAc2, probably the cancer-related LacdiNAc (GalNAcβ1-4GlcNAc-) structure detected as a diagnostic ion at m/z 407 (Supplemental Fig. S12).

In addition to the PAC workflow, tissue samples were analyzed using the conventional ISD approach without magnetic particles, enabling direct comparison between the two preparation methods (Supplemental Fig. S14A). PCA score plots of the samples prepared using the two workflows showed similar separation of the non-cancer control group and patients with gastric cancer (Supplemental Fig. S14, B and C). However, samples clustered distinctly according to the preparation workflow, indicating systematic differences between the glycoproteomic profiles generated by the two methods (Supplemental Fig. S14C), similarly to the observations with the serum samples. Notably, the magnetic particle-based workflow displayed a greater number of glycopeptides exhibiting significant alterations (Supplemental Fig. S14, D and E, Supplemental Tables S16, S18 and S20). These findings demonstrated that magnetic particle-based sample preparation is an effective approach for characterizing N-glycoproteomic alterations in cancer tissues.

Discussion

Glycopeptide Preparation Based on SP3 Using Cost-Effective Cellulose Magnetic Particles

In this study, streamlined glycopeptide enrichment was achieved using the SP3 proteomic preparation method utilizing commercially available magnetic particles. The magnetic particle preparation used for PAC on-bead digestion was continuously used for post-digestion glycopeptide enrichment. The digestion efficiency and subsequent direct HILIC purification were improved by desalting PAC before digestion (ISD versus PAC, Fig. 2). However, carboxylate polymer beads, which were used in the original SP3 method, remain a potential source of recovery and purity for glycopeptide enrichment. Therefore, we selected cellulose magnetic particles that allow glycopeptide purification from serum with no additional purification, simplifying the operation.

The use of cellulose-based ReliaPrep Resin is critical for glycopeptide preparation. The cellulose particles exhibited higher glycopeptide recovery and non-glycopeptide removal efficiencies (Fig. 1 and Supplemental Figs. S1 and S2). Being electrically neutral, cellulose particles are less likely to cause ionic interactions with peptides and are thus probably more suitable for peptide removal. By contrast, carboxylate polymers interact with peptide chains via anion exchange. Tryptic peptides usually contain basic amino acids (Lys/Arg) at the C-terminus along with an N-terminal amino group that can interact with their carboxyl groups. This is advantageous for total peptide recovery, as reported in the original SP3 report (40, 41), and may have resulted in less efficient removal of non-glycopeptides. By contrast, as alternative cellulose particles with electrically neutral hydroxyl groups, dextran-coated magnetic beads are commercially available. For example, the performance of Qbeads-Hydroxyl beads was improved in a manner similar to that for ReliaPrep Resin (Supplemental Fig. S1). However, the cost performance was more than 100-fold lower than that of ReliaPrep Resin, indicating that magnetic bead-based purification using ReliaPrep Resin is a cost-effective alternative method to that using non-magnetic cellulose SPE.

Regarding the solvent content for HILIC, adding a TFA ion pair to the loading solution improves non-glycopeptide removal efficiency during glycopeptide enrichment (4). In this study, for post-digestion N-glycopeptide enrichment using cellulose-magnetic resins, 82.5% acetonitrile containing 1% TFA was used as the loading and washing solution in the optimal protocol. Additionally, adding formic acid as an alternative ion-pairing additive for HILIC extraction was evaluated (43). This preparation with ReliaPrep Resin showed a relatively high glycopeptide enrichment efficiency, whereas the preparation with Sera-Mag particles showed a lower performance (Supplemental Fig. S6, conditions 5 and 6). These results suggest that cellulose-based ReliaPrep Resin is more widely adaptable to various sample conditions.

Sample Type for Glycopeptide Preparation

We demonstrated glycopeptide preparation using plasma/serum, brain, and gastric tissue as biological samples. In plasma/serum glycoproteomics, the removal of abundant proteins in the blood has enabled in-depth detection, which may be useful for the discovery of disease markers (40, 41). The use of albumin- and Ig-depleted serum increased the number of identified N-glycopeptides compared to using non-depleted samples (Non-dep versus HSA & Ig-dep in Fig. 3). The PAC-based SP3 method showed better performance in depleted samples when using HSA/Ig depletion resin resuspended in HEPES-buffered saline, as described above (ISD versus PAC in Fig. 3). This suggests that SP3-based glycopeptide preparation methods may be useful for studying minor glycoproteins in blood samples.

In sample preparation for tissue glycoproteomics by SP3, detergents can be used to solubilize a wide range of proteins from tissues owing to detergent compatibility (14). In the case of rat brain extracts, the lysis process using stronger SDS following PAC on-bead digestion provided equivalent or higher glycopeptide enrichment efficiency than acetone precipitation and in-solution digestion methods (Fig. 4, B–D). The use of ReliaPrep Resin particles was also critical for tissue sample preparation, which showed higher residual non-glycopeptides (Fig. 4, B–D, right). Tissue appears to be less efficient for glycopeptide enrichment than blood, and cellulose magnetic beads are more effective in this context. Moreover, samples prepared from human gastric tissue extracts showed contamination and impaired performance in LC-MS (Fig. 6B). To avoid this, an additional purification step using a C18 solid-phase spin tip was required after HILIC preparation. Therefore, this method can be used for preparing N-glycopeptides not only from plasma/serum but also from various tissue extracts.

N-Glycoproteomic Analysis of Gastric Cancer

By applying the glycopeptide preparation method to clinical serum samples, we identified cancer-associated alterations in N-glycopeptides in individual patients with gastric cancer. The increased glycopeptides possessed fucosylated complex-type glycan compositions, with MS2 spectra suggesting the presence of mainly antennal fucosylation, presumably (±sialyl) Lewis structures (Fig. 5D, Supplemental Fig. S8). Site-specific glycosylation analysis of AGP1 (N56 and N93) revealed a trend toward increased relative abundance of N-glycan species with three or four antennae containing fucose in patients with cancer, consistent with that reported previously (44). Antennary fucosylation on AGP appears to occur specifically through α1,3-linkages, which are believed to be synthesized by hepatic α1,3-fucosyltransferases (45, 46, 47). Glycopeptides with reduced levels were also detected in patients with cancer. For some glycopeptides, a trend toward a decreased number of sialic acids (i.e., a decrease in di-sialyl and an increase in mono-sialyl) was observed (Supplemental Fig. S9). Elevated serum sialidase levels have been reported in patients with cancer (48, 49) and may contribute to the observed alterations in glycan sialylation. However, since sialic acid is relatively labile, the possibility that these differences arose from variations in sample storage conditions cannot be excluded. A reduction in a fucosylated glycopeptide (N55) was identified in α2-macroglobulin, and an overall decrease in fucosylation at this glycosylation site was also observed. Although observed at different glycosylation sites (N396 and 1424) within the same glycoprotein, decreased core-fucosylation has been reported in patients with pancreatic cancer and pancreatitis (50). Such glycoproteins with altered serum glycosylation are presumably produced in the liver; hence, further validation is needed to discriminate their expression between cancer types and other inflammatory diseases in a practical setting. Moreover, further studies are required to detect minor glycoproteins in blood samples using either an in-depth approach (e.g., additional purification steps) or alternative target glycoproteomics of known cancer-associated proteins.

In tissue N-glycoproteomics, tumor-containing and adjacent non-tumor regions obtained from the same patient with gastric cancer were compared. The overall glycan composition of glycopeptides in the tumor-containing region showed significant alterations, including reduced levels of the complex-type and increased levels of the pauci-mannose-type structures (Fig. 6C). In particular, the increase in pauci-mannose glycans is consistent with previous results from glycan analyses of cancer tissues (51, 52, 53, 54). Representative glycopeptides, namely, CEACAM5, CEACAM6, and periostin glycopeptides, shown in Figure 6F, were chosen based on their level of significance and relevance to gastric cancer (42, 55, 56, 57, 58). Each of these glycoproteins has established roles in gastric cancer progression, providing biological context for the glycosylation changes observed at their individual sites. CEACAM6 promotes invasion and peritoneal metastasis in gastric cancer through epithelial–mesenchymal transition via PI3K/AKT signaling (59), and elevated CEACAM6 expression is associated with poorer survival in advanced disease (60). The reciprocal change at CEACAM6 N224, characterized by an increase in pauci-mannose structures alongside a decrease in disialylated complex-type glycans, is more difficult to interpret given the limited availability of site-specific glycoform data for CEACAM6 in gastric cancer. Given the established contribution of glycosylation to CEACAM stability and protease resistance, this alteration represents a plausible candidate for future mechanistic investigation (61).

We also observed cancer-related LacdiNAc (GalNAcβ1-4GlcNAc-) structure in the N-glycan antenna on periostin (Fig. 6F). Periostin, secreted primarily by cancer-associated fibroblasts, promotes epithelial–mesenchymal transition, stemness, and metastasis in gastric cancer (62), and cancer-associated fibroblast-derived periostin has specifically been shown to drive gastric cancer stem cell behavior and liver metastasis (63). Changes in periostin N599 in cancer tissues showed an increase in glycans with the complex-/hybrid-type compositions, including typical disialylated, fucosylated glycans. (Fig. 6F, Supplemental Fig. S13). Conversely, changes in periostin N599 in cancer tissues showed a decrease in the glycans containing HexNAc2 units, presumed to be LacdiNAc. In gastric tissue, mucin O-glycans containing LacdiNAc, which are found in normal regions, are decreased in cancerous regions (64, 65). In breast cancer tissue, decreased expression of LacdiNAc-containing N-glycans compared to that in normal tissue has been reported, and enhanced LacdiNAc expression in breast cancer cells has been shown to suppress tumor growth (66). The shift from LacdiNAc to (sialyl) LacNAc in the glycan antenna may affect cell adhesion, migration, and invasion through interactions between the glycans on periostin and galectins or selectins (67). This suggests that the observed glycoform shift may contribute to the known role of periostin in gastric cancer metastasis, although this relationship was not directly tested herein.

In addition, coordinated glycosylation changes across multiple extracellular matrix components including fibronectin, decorin, biglycan, lumican, and collagens as well as altered glycosylation of lysosomal membrane glycoproteins including LAMP1, LAMP2, and CD63 were observed (Supplemental Table S16). These changes suggest remodeling of glycan processing in the Golgi and endoplasmic reticulum of tumor cells, impaired Golgi maturation in cancer cells (68), and systematic reprogramming of the tumor matrisome-glycosylation landscape (69), consistent with known cancer-associated glycosylation changes (70). Nevertheless, interpretation of these findings should be approached with caution because the present analysis was performed using bulk cancer-containing tissue specimens. Consequently, it was not possible to determine whether the observed glycosylation changes originated from the cancer cells themselves or from the surrounding stroma of infiltrating cells.

Evaluation of Magnetic Particle-Based Sample Preparation Methods in Clinical N-Glycoproteomics

To assess magnetic particle-based sample preparation method investigated herein, a preparation method combining conventional techniques that do not use magnetic particles (in-solution digestion followed by HILIC concentration using non-magnetic cellulose resin) was also performed and compared for clinical samples (Supplemental Figs. S10 and S14). PCA plots from both methods showed similar distributions of cancerous and non-cancerous samples, suggesting similar applicability of the magnetic particle method. Nevertheless, clear differences between the conventional and magnetic particle methods were also observed. These discrepancies likely arise from multiple differences in sample preparation, including the degree of protein denaturation and protein recovery during PAC purification prior to digestion, digestion efficiency between in-solution and on-bead digestion, and differences in glycopeptide recovery between non-magnetic microcrystalline cellulose and magnetic cellulose particles during HILIC enrichment.

In the serum, the PAC-based workflow identified 1984 glycopeptides across 384 glycosylation sites from 150 glycoproteins, while the conventional ISD workflow achieved comparable coverage (1928 glycopeptides, 375 sites, and 148 glycoproteins) (Supplemental Table 13). This analytical depth is comparable to that reported by Sun et al. (1961 glycopeptides and 168 glycoproteins) (71) and the PAC-based MuPPE platform (2640 glycopeptides and 137 glycoproteins) (72). In contrast, a ZenoTOF-based HILIC workflow identified 6901 site-specific glycans across 807 glycosylation sites from over 400 glycoproteins (73), while an automated HILIC platform applied to a larger gastric cancer cohort (>278 subjects) identified over 20,000 site-specific glycans (45). These differences likely reflect advances in MS instrumentation, workflow automation, and cohort size rather than the enrichment strategy itself.

Similarly, the magnetic particle-based workflow identified 6812 intact N-glycopeptides across 1399 glycosylation sites from 686 glycoproteins in gastric cancer tissues, whereas the conventional ISD workflow achieved comparable coverage (5964 glycopeptides, 1350 sites, and 672 glycoproteins) (Supplemental Table S9). Recent tissue N-glycoproteomic studies of prostate cancer and hepatocellular carcinoma reported 7022 glycopeptides across 1894 glycosylation sites from 1354 glycoproteins (74) and 5375 glycopeptides across 985 glycosylation sites from 575 glycoproteins (75), respectively, demonstrating comparable analytical depth. Greater coverage has been achieved in pancreatic cancer (20,038 glycopeptides, 4460 glycosylation sites, and 3437 glycoproteins) (76); however, this study employed extensive offline fractionation together with dedicated glycoproteomic acquisition and data analysis workflows, requiring substantially greater analytical complexity and instrument time. Collectively, these comparisons indicate that the present workflow provides broad serum and tissue N-glycoproteome coverage while maintaining a streamlined, automation-compatible sample preparation procedure suitable for routine clinical cohort studies.

This study has two main limitations. First, our work demonstrated only the practicability of the glycopeptide preparation method using a relatively small clinical cohort, warranting further validation in large sample sets. Second, gastric tissue samples containing mucins required an additional cleanup step using reversed-phase SPE tips; however, this procedure is currently not compatible with our automated preparation workflow. Despite these limitations, the proposed method enables efficient analysis of large sample cohorts and may provide new opportunities for high-throughput glycoproteimics in glycobiology research.

In conclusion, we demonstrated that the SP3-based N-glycopeptide preparation method using commercially available cellulose magnetic beads is a robust and convenient approach for N-glycoproteomics. Preparation using cellulose magnetic particles exhibited higher glycopeptide recovery and non-glycopeptide removal efficiencies in the preparation of various sample types. In particular, the SP3-based preparation was suitable for performing a continuous process from pre-cleanup by PAC to highly efficient glycopeptide enrichment based on HILIC, and hence, the foundation of the method can be adapted for automation. However, seamless and fully-automated sample preparation is paramount in a large cohort, and hence further development of an automated robotic protocol is required to establish an automated glycoproteomics system.
