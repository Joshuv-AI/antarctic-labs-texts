Phenotypic, genomic, and functional characterization of Corynebacterium sp. nov. isolated by droplet-based cultivation from vaginal swabs of a preeclampsia patient
Authors: Junjie Yuan, Jing Wang, Hui Wang, Zhenzhen He, Zhongju Chen, Yating Hu, Xuan Zhou, Shaozhen Yan, Lijie Wei, Mengzhou He, Yi Jiang, Rui Su, Huihui Yu, Shaoshuai Wang, Weidong Tan, Wencheng Ding, Ling Feng, Zerui Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMembers of the genus Corynebacterium are widely distributed across human mucosal surfaces and are increasingly recognized as important components of the female reproductive tract microbiota. However, their diversity is underestimated due to limitations of conventional cultivation approaches.ResultsIn this study, a novel bacterial strain, designated TJG_1T, was isolated from vaginal swab samples of a pregnant woman with preeclampsia (PE) using droplet-based microfluidic single-cell cultivation. Strain TJG_1T was characterized as a Gram-positive, non-spore-forming, non-motile short rod. Phylogenetic analysis based on the 16 S rRNA gene sequence placed the strain within the genus Corynebacterium, with the highest sequence similarity to Corynebacterium coyleae (97.39%). Whole-genome sequencing revealed a genome size of 2,481,283 bp with a G + C content of 61.52 mol%. Average nucleotide identity (ANI) and digital DNA–DNA hybridization (dDDH) values between strain TJG_1T and its closest relatives were below the accepted species thresholds, supporting its classification as a novel species. Functional genome annotation identified genes associated with metabolism, transport systems, putative antimicrobial resistance, and predicted host interaction-related traits. In vitro assays showed that neither bacterial metabolites nor live cells significantly affected trophoblast cell viability or oxidative stress-related gene expression under the tested conditions. Although alterations in cell migration behavior were observed following exposure to live bacteria, these changes were not accompanied by significant differences in migration-related gene expression.ConclusionsStrain TJG_1T represents a novel species within the genus Corynebacterium. This study highlights the utility of droplet-based cultivation for recovering previously underrepresented vaginal bacteria and expands current knowledge of Corynebacterium diversity in the female reproductive tract.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-026-05255-6.

Introduction

The genus Corynebacterium was first established by Lehmann and Neumann in 1896 and currently comprises more than 160 validly published species [1]. Members of this genus are widely distributed in diverse environments as well as human-associated habitats, including the skin, respiratory tract, gastrointestinal tract, and urogenital tract [2].

Although Corynebacterium diphtheriae is the best-characterized pathogenic species within the genus, increasing evidence indicates that non-diphtherial Corynebacterium species may also participate in human health and disease, particularly as opportunistic pathogens or context-dependent colonizers [3–5]. Several Corynebacterium species, including Corynebacterium coyleae, have been detected in the female urogenital tract and urinary microbiota and some have also been associated with urinary tract infections, bacteremia, and soft tissue infections [6–8]. However, the diversity and ecological distribution of Corynebacterium species in the vaginal microbiota of pregnant women remain incompletely characterized.

A major limitation in recovering vaginal Corynebacterium species is that traditional cultivation methods preferentially enrich fast-growing and competitive bacteria while failing to recover low-abundance or slow-growing organisms [9]. Droplet-based microfluidic single-cell cultivation has recently emerged as an effective strategy for isolating rare and previously uncultured microorganisms by physically compartmentalizing individual cells in picoliter-scale droplets and minimizing interspecies competition [10, 11].

In this study, we applied droplet-based microfluidic cultivation to isolate a previously undescribed Corynebacterium strain, designated TJG_1T, from the vaginal microbiota of a pregnant woman with PE. Using a polyphasic taxonomic approach integrating phenotypic characterization, phylogenetic analysis, and comparative genomics, we systematically characterized this strain and evaluated its preliminary interactions with trophoblast cells (HTR-8/SVneo) in vitro. These findings expand current knowledge of Corynebacterium diversity in the female reproductive tract.

Materials and methods

Inclusion criteria and sample collection

Vaginal swab samples were obtained from a single pregnant woman diagnosed with PE at the Department of Perinatal Medicine, Tongji Hospital, Huazhong University of Science and Technology. PE was diagnosed according to the diagnostic criteria of the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) [12]. The participant had no history of chronic hypertension or other pre-existing comorbidities before pregnancy. Vaginal secretion samples were collected by a trained obstetrician under sterile speculum examination using sterile swabs from the posterior vaginal fornix. Briefly, after gentle insertion of a sterile speculum, two sterile swabs were pressed against the posterior vaginal fornix for approximately 3 s. After collection, swabs were immediately placed into Amies transport medium (Hopebio, Qingdao, China), transported to the laboratory on ice, and processed within 2 h.

High-throughput picoliter droplet-based single-cell cultivation

Microfluidic devices used in this study were designed and fabricated as previously described [13]. Vaginal swab samples were eluted in Columbia medium (Becton Dickinson, Franklin Lakes, USA) and subsequently filtered through a 20 μm pore-size filter to remove large particles and debris. Bacterial cell density was estimated using a cell-counting chamber (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, USA) and adjusted according to a Poisson distribution (λ < 0.3), to maximize the probability of encapsulating single bacterial cells within individual droplets.

The bacterial suspension was introduced as the dispersed aqueous phase at a flow rate of 600 µL/h, while the continuous oil phase (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, USA) was infused at 1200 µL/h. Approximately 1 × 10⁶ monodisperse droplets were generated over a 30 min period, yielding a total volume of 300 µL, with an estimated > 3 × 10⁵ droplets containing single bacterial cells. The droplets were collected and incubated statically at 37℃ for 7 days. Bacterial growth within droplets was examined using an inverted microscope. To recover bacteria from the droplets, an equal volume of 1 H,1 H,2 H,2 H-perfluoro-1-octanol (Aladdin Bio-Chem Technology, Shanghai, China) was added to break the emulsion, followed by vortexing and centrifugation to separate the oil phase. The aqueous phase was collected, serially diluted, and spread onto Columbia blood agar plates (Huankai Microbial, Guangzhou, China). Plates were incubated at 37℃ for 12–36 h to obtain isolated colonies.

16 S rRNA gene sequence and strain identification

The nearly full-length 16 S rRNA gene was amplified from genomic DNA using the universal bacterial primers 27 F and 1492R. PCR products were sequenced by Sanger sequencing (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China). To determine the phylogenetic affiliation, the 16 S rRNA gene sequence of strain TJG_1T was compared against the EzBioCloud database (https://www.ezbiocloud.net/) to retrieve sequences of closely related type strains. Whole-genome ANI values were calculated using EzBioCloud online tools ANI calculator [14] and dDDH value was calculated by the Genome-to-Genome Distance Calculator (GGDC) 3.0 online service [15].

Whole-genome sequencing, assembly, and annotation

Single colony was picked from Columbia blood agar plates after 18 h of incubation and inoculated into a 12mL culture tube for scale-up cultivation. Bacterial cells in the logarithmic growth phase were collected by centrifugation and submitted to Shanghai Majorbio Bio-pharm Technology (Shanghai, China) for whole-genome sequencing.

Raw sequencing reads were quality-filtered using fastp. Quality control procedures included removal of adapter sequences, trimming of 5’-end bases containing non-ACGT characters, trimming of low-quality bases (quality score < Q20) from read ends, removal of reads containing ≥ 10% ambiguous nucleotides, and discarding reads shorter than 25 bp after trimming. The resulting high-quality reads were retained as clean data.

Genome assembly was performed using SOAPdenovo2 [16], and the optimal assembly was selected based on standard assembly metrics. Coding sequence (CDS) were predicted using Glimmer, transfer RNA (tRNA) genes were identified using tRNAscan-SE, and ribosomal RNA (rRNA) genes were detected using Barrnap. Functional annotation of predicted CDSs was conducted through sequence alignment against the Clusters of Orthologous Groups (COG), Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), and Comprehensive Antibiotic Resistance Database (CARD) databases.

Phylogenetic analysis

Phylogenetic analyses were conducted based on both 16 S rRNA gene sequences and whole-genome data using the Majorbio Cloud Platform (https://www.majorbio.com), with the Genome Taxonomy Database (GTDB) as the reference database. For 16 S rRNA gene-based analysis, up to 20 closely related strains at the genus and species levels were selected based on sequence similarity. For whole-genome-based phylogenetic reconstruction, 19 phylogenetically related strains were selected, and 31 conserved single-copy core genes were extracted and aligned. Phylogenetic trees were constructed using the neighbor-joining (NJ) method implemented in MEGA version 6.0 [17], and the robustness of tree topology was evaluated using bootstrap analysis with 1,000 replicates.

Phenotypic characterization

Purified colonies were cultured on Columbia blood agar plates at 37℃ under aerobic conditions. Colony morphology was assessed by stereomicroscopy based on shape, color, texture, size, margin characteristics, and hemolytic activity. Gram staining was performed using a commercial staining kit (BKMAMLAB, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and cellular morphology was examined under oil immersion at 100× magnification with representative images recorded. For growth curve analysis, bacterial cultures were incubated at 37℃ with shaking at 150 rpm using a Growth Curve Analyzer (Bioscreen C, Oy Growth Curves Ab, Helsinki, Finland). Optical density at 600 nm (OD600) was measured over 26 h, and all experiments were performed in triplicate. Biochemical characteristics of strain TJG_1T were determined using the VITEK 2 Gram-Positive Identification Card (bioMérieux, Marcy-l’ Étoile, France) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. For scanning electron microscopy (SEM), cells grown to the logarithmic phase were collected by centrifugation, fixed in an electron microscopy fixative at room temperature for 30 min, and then sent to Baiqiandu Technology (Wuhan, China) for further processing and imaging. The minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) of strain TJG_1T were determined using the VITEK 2 Gram-Positive AST card (bioMérieux, Marcy-l’ Étoile, France), following the manufacturer’s standardized protocol.

Host-microbe interaction assays

The human extravillous trophoblast cell line HTR-8/SVneo (ATCC, USA) was used for host–microbe interaction studies. For the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay, HTR-8/SVneo cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 2 × 10³ cells per well and incubated at 37℃ with 5% CO2 for 12 h to allow cell attachment. The culture medium was then replaced with antibiotic-free medium containing sterile-filtered bacterial culture supernatants at a final concentration of 10% (v/v) or live bacterial cells at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 10:1. Following 24 h of incubation, cell viability was assessed by measuring absorbance at 450 nm according to the manufacturer’s instructions. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

HTR-8/SVneo cell migration assay

HTR-8/SVneo were seeded in 12-well plates and cultured until reaching approximately 90–95% confluence. Adherent monolayers were scratched with a sterile 200 µL pipette tip. Cells were gently washed with PBS to remove detached cells and debris. The medium was then replaced with antibiotic-free culture medium containing live bacteria at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 10:1. Images were captured at 0 and 24 h using an inverted microscope. The scratch area was measured using ImageJ software.

RT-qPCR analysis

Total RNA was extracted using FastPure Cell/Tissue Total RNA Isolation Kit (Vazyme, China) and reverse-transcribed into complementary DNA using the HiScript III All-in-one RT SuperMix for qPCR (Vazyme, China). Quantitative PCR (qPCR) was performed using Taq Pro Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, China) on a Bio-Rad real-time PCR system. Gene expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method and are presented as fold changes relative to the control group. All primers used in this study are listed in Table S2.

Statistical analyses

GraphPad Prism software (v.8.0.2) was used for statistical analysis. Each assay and experiment were performed in at least three biological replicates.

Results

Workflow for droplet-based isolation and strain recovery

A droplet-based microfluidic workflow was developed for the isolation of individual bacterial cells from complex vaginal microbial communities (Fig. 1). Vaginal secretion samples were collected using sterile swabs and resuspended in culture medium for microfluidic processing. The suspension was introduced into a droplet microfluidic device as the aqueous phase and emulsified at a flow-focusing junction by a continuous oil phase, generating highly monodisperse water-in-oil droplets. By adjusting the initial cell density according to Poisson distribution principles, encapsulation conditions approximating single-cell occupancy were achieved, enabling efficient compartmentalization of individual bacterial cells.

Each droplet (~ 30 pL) functioned as an independent microreactor that provided a physically enclosed and isolated microenvironment for the proliferation of individual bacterial cells into microcolonies. Following incubation, droplets were demulsified, serially diluted, and plated onto Columbia blood agar plates. Droplet-derived bacteria subsequently formed well-isolated colonies, which were picked, purified, and subjected to downstream identification and phenotypic characterization. Our use of droplet-based single-cell cultivation enabled the isolation of 70 bacterial strains from 3 vaginal swabs. Among the recovered strains, there were 19 unique species spanning three major phyla: Firmicutes (71.4%), Actinobacteria (20%), and Proteobacteria (8.6%) (Table S3). To exclude potential contamination, blank control swabs (Fig. S1A) processed in parallel under identical experimental conditions yielded no detectable amplification of 16 S rRNA gene fragments (Fig. S1B), supporting the reliability of the isolation procedure.

Morphological, physiological and biochemical characteristics

The morphological, physiological, and biochemical characteristics of strain TJG_1T were systematically investigated (Fig. 2). After 24 h of aerobic incubation on Columbia blood agar plates, strain TJG_1T formed circular, smooth, slightly convex, and moist colonies with entire margins. Colonies were milky white and approximately 1–2 mm in diameter. No hemolysis activity was observed, indicating a non-hemolytic phenotype (Fig. 2A).

Gram staining revealed that strain TJG_1T was Gram-positive (Fig. 2B). Microscopic examination revealed irregular short rod-shaped cells occurring singly or in small aggregates, consistent with a coryneform morphology. Scanning electron microscopy further showed that cells lacked flagella and spores, indicating a non-motile and non-sporulating phenotype (Fig. 2C). Cell dimensions were estimated from electron micrographs to be approximately 1.0–1.5 μm in length and 0.5–0.7 μm in width, and no distinct surface appendages were observed.

Growth curve analysis showed an initial lag phase of approximately 6 h, followed by exponential growth and entry into the stationary phase at around 22 h, with no obvious decline in cell density during the remaining observation period (Fig. 2D).

Biochemical characterization showed that strain TJG_1T was positive for arginine dihydrolase, leucine arylamidase, alanine arylamidase, proline arylamidase, tyrosine arylamidase, and L-lactate alkalinization. In contrast, most carbohydrate utilization tests, including D-ribose, D-mannose, sucrose, D-maltose, and several enzymatic activities such as urease, amylase, and β-galactosidase, were negative (Table 1). Additionally, antimicrobial susceptibility testing showed that strain TJG_1T was resistant to benzylpenicillin, oxacillin, erythromycin, clindamycin, and levofloxacin, while exhibiting intermediate susceptibility to moxifloxacin. In contrast, the strain remained susceptible to gentamicin, linezolid, daptomycin, teicoplanin, vancomycin, tigecycline, rifampicin, and trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole (Table 2).

Identification and phylogenomic analysis of strain TJG_1T

Phylogenetic and genome-based analyses were performed to clarify the taxonomic status of strain TJG_1T. The nearly complete 16 S rRNA gene sequence (1,423 bp) was obtained and deposited in GenBank (accession number PX973266). Sequence comparison showed that strain TJG_1T shared 16 S rRNA gene sequence similarities of 94.44–97.39% with validly published members of the genus Corynebacterium, with the highest similarity to Corynebacterium coyleae. This value is below the generally accepted species delineation threshold of 98.65%, suggesting that strain TJG_1T represents a putative novel species.

Whole-genome comparisons further supported its taxonomic distinctiveness. ANI values between strain TJG_1T and its three closest phylogenetic relatives were all below the 95% species delineation thresholds. In addition, dDDH values were below the accepted 70% cutoff for species definition (Table S1). Phylogenetic reconstruction based on the 16 S rRNA gene (Fig. 3A) and phylogenomic analysis using concatenated alignments of 31 conserved single-copy core genes (Fig. 3B) consistently placed strain TJG_1T within the genus Corynebacterium. Together, the phylogenetic, genomic and phenotypic evidence support the designation of strain TJG_1T as a novel species within the genus Corynebacterium.

Genomic features and functional annotation of strain TJG_1T

Whole-genome sequencing and functional annotation were performed to characterize the genomic features of strain TJG_1T. The complete genome of strain TJG_1T consists of 2,481,283 bp with a G + C content of 61.52 mol%, containing 2,336 predicted genes, including 3 rRNA genes and 48 tRNA genes. Raw sequencing data have been deposited in the Sequence Read Archive (SRA) under accession number SRR37168060. A circular genome map of strain TJG_1T is shown in Fig. 4, displaying genes encoded on the forward and reverse strands, GC content, GC skew, and genomic coordinates. KEGG annotation assigned 1,600 coding sequences to six major functional categories (Fig. 5A), among which metabolism-related genes were the most abundant (1,287 genes), whereas genes associated with organismal systems were the least represented (48 genes). COG analysis classified 1,748 genes into 23 functional categories (Fig. 5B). Genes involved in translation, ribosomal structure and biogenesis constituted the largest category, followed by genes associated with amino acid transport and metabolism, transcription, and inorganic ion transport and metabolism. Notably, several genes were annotated in pathways categorized under “human diseases”. However, these annotations were inferred based on sequence homology and should not be interpreted as evidence of direct pathogenic potential.

Prediction of virulence-associated traits and antibiotic resistance

Genome-based analyses were performed to predict potential virulence-associated traits and antimicrobial resistance profiles of strain TJG_1T. Annotation against the Pathogen–Host Interactions (PHI) database identified approximately 500 genes associated with pathogen–host interaction phenotypes (Fig. 6A), among which attenuated virulence-related homologs represented the largest category. Homologs associated with hypervirulence, chemical resistance, and chemical sensitivity were less abundant. CARD annotation identified putative resistance determinants associated with multiple antibiotic classes, including macrolides, fluoroquinolones, peptides, tetracyclines, lincosamides, rifamycins, penams, glycopeptides, and streptogramins (Fig. 6B). These predictions were based on genome annotation and do not represent experimentally validated resistance phenotypes. Transport-related genes were further classified into seven major functional categories according to the Transporter Classification Database (TCDB) (Fig. 6C). Most annotated transporters belonged to primary active transporters and electrochemical potential-driven transport systems.

Analysis of virulence factors based on the Virulence Factor Database (VFDB) [18] identified homologs associated with multiple virulence-related functional categories (Fig. 6D), with nutritional/metabolic and immune modulation-associated genes being predominant. As these findings are prediction-based, they indicate potential functional traits inferred from homology rather than experimentally confirmed virulence properties.

Effects of strain TJG_1T on the viability, migration, and oxidative stress of extravillous trophoblast cells

The effects of sterile-filtered culture supernatants and live cells of strain TJG_1T on extravillous trophoblast cells (HTR-8/SVneo) were evaluated in vitro (Fig. S2). CCK-8 assays showed that neither bacterial supernatants (Fig. S2A) nor live bacteria (Fig. S2B) significantly affected cell viability after 24 h of exposure compared with the control group. The expression levels of migration- and oxidative stress-related genes, including MMP2, NRF2, SOD, and NF-κB, were further analyzed by quantitative real-time PCR following treatment with bacterial supernatants (Fig. S2C) or live cells (Fig. S2D). No significant differences in gene expression were observed under the tested conditions. To further assess the effects of live bacteria on trophoblast migration, a wound healing assay was performed following co-culture with strain TJG_1T (Fig. S2E). Compared with the control group, exposure to live bacteria significantly reduced the migratory capacity of HTR-8/SVneo cells. Although no corresponding changes in MMP2 expression were detected, the observed phenotypic differences suggest that strain TJG_1T may influence trophoblast cell behavior through mechanisms that warrant further investigation. These observations were obtained under the in vitro conditions used in this study and do not exclude the possibility of context-dependent host–microbe interactions.

Discussion

In this study, we successfully isolated and characterized a novel Corynebacterium strain, TJG_1T, from the vaginal microbiota of a pregnant woman with PE using droplet-based microfluidic cultivation. Conventional cultivation methods are often limited in their ability to recover low-abundance or slow-growing organisms from polymicrobial communities. By physically compartmentalizing individual bacterial cells into picoliter droplets, the droplet-based approach reduced interspecies competition and enabled efficient recovery of previously underrepresented taxa. Polyphasic taxonomic analyses consistently supported the classification of strain TJG_1T as a novel species within the genus Corynebacterium. The 16 S rRNA gene similarity to its closest relative, Corynebacterium coyleae, was below the accepted species threshold. This conclusion was further supported by ANI and dDDH values below the recognized genomic cutoffs for species delineation.

Genome annotation suggested that strain TJG_1T harbors a diverse repertoire of metabolic pathways, transporter systems, and putative antimicrobial resistance-associated genes. In addition, homology-based annotation identified genes associated with host interaction and virulence-related functional categories. However, it is important to emphasize that these annotations are based on sequence similarity and should not be interpreted as direct evidence of pathogenic potential without experimental validation.

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing revealed that strain TJG_1T exhibited variable responses across different antibiotic classes, with partial concordance between genome-predicted resistance determinants and phenotypic resistance profiles. Notably, the correspondence between predicted resistance determinants and phenotypic susceptibility profiles was not uniform. This discrepancy is not unexpected, as the presence of resistance-associated genes does not necessarily confer functional resistance, which may depend on gene expression, regulatory context, and environmental conditions. These findings underscore the importance of integrating genomic predictions with experimental validation to accurately assess antimicrobial resistance potential.

To preliminarily assess host interactions, we evaluated the effects of both bacterial metabolites and live cells on trophoblast cells. Under the experimental conditions tested, neither bacterial metabolites nor live cells significantly affected cell viability or oxidative stress-related gene expression under the tested conditions. However, live bacterial exposure reduced trophoblast migratory capacity in the wound healing assay, despite the absence of significant changes in migration-related gene expression. Although subtle morphological alterations were observed following live bacterial exposure, these findings were qualitative and were not further quantified. Therefore, the biological significance of these observations remains unclear.

Importantly, strain TJG_1T was isolated from a single participant with PE, the present data do not support a causal relationship between strain TJG_1T and PE, and further investigations will be required. The observed lack of significant effects in vitro further suggests that this strain does not exert overt cytotoxic or stress-inducing effects under baseline experimental conditions. Future studies involving larger clinical cohorts, multi-omics profiling, and disease-relevant host models will be necessary to clarify its ecological role and potential clinical significance.

Conclusion

Polyphasic characterization supports the designation of strain TJG_1T as a novel species within the genus Corynebacterium and expands the known diversity of vaginal-associated corynebacteria. Droplet-based microfluidic cultivation enabled the recovery of previously underrepresented taxa and provides an effective strategy for isolating rare bacteria from complex vaginal microbial communities.
