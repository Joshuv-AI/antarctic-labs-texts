Neural association between mental symptoms and facial emotion recognition in deficit schizophrenia: an fNIRS study
Authors: Yanqin Hu, Guijuan Yang, Huangzhiheng Zhang, Yang Li, Gang Yuan, Jiming Duan, Tingting Liu, Houfeng Zhao, Juan Qiao, Congju He
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe dysfunction of the prefrontal cortex (PFC) is closely related to the emotion recognition deficits in schizophrenia. Compared with the non-deficit schizophrenia (NDSZ), deficit schizophrenia (DSZ) has a more severe deficit in emotion recognition; however, the underlying neurobiological mechanism is unclear. Thus, this study aimed to investigate PFC activation patterns and their correlations with clinical scale scores during facial emotion recognition (FER) between the DSZ and NDSZ using functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS).MethodsThis study included 38 DSZ patients, 49 NDSZ patients, and 39 healthy controls (HCs). FER was evaluated using a standardized visual emotion discrimination task, while PFC activation was monitored via fNIRS. To identify channels(CHs) with statistically significant differences in oxygenated hemoglobin (HbO) changes across the three groups, we performed one-way ANOVA followed by post hoc Bonferroni tests for pairwise comparisons. These analyses explored the relationship between HbO concentration changes in the PFC and clinical scale scores during FER tasks.ResultsDuring anger, sadness, contempt, and happiness recognition tasks, the DSZ group showed significantly reduced HbO activation in the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DlPFC; Brodmann area 9, BA9) and the frontopolar cortex(BA10), compared with the NDSZ group. Similarly, decreased activation patterns were observed in BA9 and BA10 in the DSZ group during anger and happiness recognition tasks compared with the HCs. Furthermore, during contempt recognition, reduced activation was found in BA9 compared with the HCs. Correlation analyses revealed moderate negative associations (i.e., an inverse relationship) between activation levels in these prefrontal regions (BA9/BA10) and negative symptoms (i.e., diminution of emotional expression and motivation) scores in DSZ patients across these four emotion tasks. However, no significant correlations were found in NDSZ patients and HCs.ConclusionCompared with the NDSZ patients, DSZ patients exhibited more severe FER impairments, demonstrating a different cognitive deficit pattern.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-025-07558-w.

Background

Schizophrenia (SZ) is a severe psychiatric disorder marked by cognitive dysfunction, positive/negative symptoms, and behavioral/perceptual disturbances [1, 2]. Within this heterogeneous disorder, deficit schizophrenia (DSZ) represents a clinically distinct subtype defined by primary and persistent negative symptoms, accounting for approximately 32.64% of schizophrenia cases [3]. Compared with non-deficit schizophrenia (NDSZ) patients, DSZ individuals exhibit poorer long-term outcomes, including more severe cognitive impairment [4], persistent functional deficits, and lower remission rates [5]. Impairments in facial emotion recognition (FER) could potentially account for this discrepancy. While both DSZ and NDSZ patients show FER impairments, previous evidence indicates these deficits are significantly more pronounced in DSZ [6]. This impairment may stem from distinct neurobiological mechanisms in DSZ, as evidenced by more severe structural and functional abnormalities in brain regions critical for emotion processing, including the prefrontal cortex (PFC) [7, 8]. The clinical significance of these FER differences is underscored by their strong correlation with functional outcomes [9]. Although the clinical differences between DSZ and NDSZ patients are identifiable at the time of the first disease episode [10], the underlying neurobiological mechanisms have not been fully explored. Investigating FER differences between DSZ and NDSZ may therefore reveal subtype-specific pathophysiological mechanisms driving their divergent clinical trajectories.

Recent studies have revealed patterns of FER impairment in SZ patients [11, 12]. Notably, clinical symptoms and cognitive processes were differentially associated with positive-valence and negative-valence emotions (e.g., fear, anger) among patients with chronic SZ [13]. The recognition deficit in SZ patients exhibits a stable dissociation phenomenon across different stages of the disease, from the first episode to the chronic phase [14, 15]. While impaired negative-valence emotions recognition represents a well-replicated finding, the specific profile remains controversial. Zhu et al. reported relatively intact sensitivity to sadness and fear [16], while others document significant deficits across all negative-valence emotions [17–19]. A recent meta-analysis of 23 Chinese studies (N = 1894 SZ patients) revealed significant FER impairments across all basic emotions [20]. In addition, individuals with SZ exhibit hypoactivation in the frontotemporal lobe region, characterized by a topographical distribution that is specific to the disease [21]. Functional imaging studies have revealed that reduced PFC gray matter volume is associated with FER deficits in SZ [22]. Furthermore, the structural and functional abnormalities of the prefrontal lobe, especially medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) and dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DlPFC), are closely related to the defect of emotion recognition in patients with SZ [23, 24]. In SZ patients, reduced gray matter volume and diminished functional activity in the prefrontal regions often indicate impaired abilities to evaluate emotional information, inhibit downstream neural pathways, and make accurate judgments [25]. Dysfunction in two key circuits related to the PFC—namely the prefrontal-limbic circuit and the prefrontal-striatal circuit, characterized by reduced PFC activation—may constitute the neural basis underlying emotion recognition deficits in SZ [25, 26]. More importantly, a different neural foundation is also observed between DSZ and NDSZ. Compared with NDSZ patients, those with DSZ exhibit more severe functional deficit. Specifically, they demonstrate weaker intramodule connectivity within the salience network and stronger intermodule connectivity between the sensorimotor network and both the frontoparietal and visual networks [27]. These patterns suggest that DSZ and NDSZ patients have a different basis for neurological functional characteristics in functional connectomics. Furthermore, the more severe impairment in the prefrontal-limbic circuit observed in DSZ indicates that the neurobiological mechanism underlying FER is different from that in NDSZ [28].

Functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) relies on neurovascular coupling to non-invasively quantify cortical hemodynamic responses through oxygenated hemoglobin (HbO) and deoxygenated hemoglobin (HbR) concentration changes, offering superior motion tolerance over functional magnetic resonance imaging for psychiatric populations [21, 29]. Recent studies have found that SZ patients have a lower total hemoglobin concentration in the prefrontal lobe in the resting state [29] and task state [30] compared to healthy controls (HCs). The flexibility of fNIRS has demonstrated the advantages of its application in studies of task state aspects in SZ patients compared to functional magnetic resonance imaging [21].

Given the well-documented impairment of PFC function in SZ patients during FER tasks, we primarily monitored brain activity in the PFC. Based on the Brodmann area (BA) classification, we segmented the measurable PFC regions into several key areas: the dorsolateral PFC (encompassing BA9 and BA46), the frontopolar cortex (BA10), the orbitofrontal cortex (BA11), and the inferior frontal gyrus (BA47). These regions underpin the five functional sub-divisions (bilateral DLPFC, bilateral VLPFC, and mPFC) commonly used in neuroimaging studies. Functionally, the DLPFC (BA9/46) plays a crucial role in FER by contributing to working memory maintenance, goal-directed attention (particularly in regulating attention allocation to emotional features), and cognitive flexibility in shifting between different emotional categories. The VLPFC (comprising primarily BA47 and parts of BA45/44) participates in controlling automatic reactions to emotional stimuli and evaluating emotional valence, showing sensitivity in the rapid assessment of threatening facial expressions. The mPFC (which includes portions of BA10 and BA11) is involved in emotion regulation and the updating of goal-directed behaviors, processes that are especially critical for interpreting the social significance of emotions. Thus, these prefrontal regions, defined by both their Brodmann architecture and functional profiles, play significant and complementary roles during the processing of emotion recognition [31].

Previous behavioral and neuroimaging studies have consistently shown that DSZ patients exhibit more severe impairments in performing FER with abnormal alterations in PFC function compared to NDSZ patients [6, 28]. However, a critical gap remains. While these studies describe aberrant neural activity patterns and behavioral impairments in isolation, few have directly investigated the relationship between specific neural activity patterns during FER and clinical variables. Therefore, this study will focus on these five brain areas to systematically characterize their distinct contributions to FER deficits in SZ patients. In this study, building upon established experimental methods and theoretical frameworks from previous research, we aim to employ fNIRS to investigate the characteristic cerebral hemodynamic alterations of FER dysfunction in DSZ by fNIRS and to explore potential associations among cerebral hemodynamic profiles of DSZ patients and psychiatric symptom severity during recognition processes of different emotions.

Materials and methods

Participants

Between January 2024 and March 2025, we recruited 38 DSZ inpatients, 49 NDSZ inpatients, and 39 HCs from Chongqing Mental Health Center. Patient inclusion criteria required: [1] Right-handed; [2] Age in the range of 18 to 55 years old; [3] Meet the diagnostic criteria for SZ in the American Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fourth Edition (DSM-IV) and the Chinese version of the Structured Clinical Interview for DSM-IV; [4] Presence of persistent psychotic symptoms and treatment with antipsychotic drugs for at least 12 months. The Chinese version of the Schedule for the Deficit Syndrome was used to determine the major criteria for distinguishing the DSZ and NDSZ groups [32]. And the HCs inclusion criteria required: [1] Do not meet the diagnostic criteria of mental disorders or diseases in DSM-IV; [2] Right-handed; [3] Age in the range of 18 to 55 years old; [4] Communication is not hampered by a lack of basic grasp of the interview’s content. Exclusion criteria for all participants included: [1] History of neurological disorders or severe endocrine/metabolic diseases; [2] Comorbid DSM-IV Axis I or II disorders; [3] Poor adherence to study requirements (failure to complete or comply with trial procedures). This study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Chongqing Mental Health Center.

Behavioral assessment

All participants completed clinical and behavioural assessments on the day of the fNIRS examination by 2 psychiatrists with professional training. The Brief Psychiatric Rating Scale (BPRS) was used to assess the severity of psychotic symptoms in participants [33]. Positive and negative symptoms were specifically assessed using the standard Scale for the Assessment of Positive Symptoms (SAPS) and Scale for the Assessment of Negative Symptoms (SANS), respectively. Lower score indicated less severity. The Chinese version of the Schedule for the Deficit Syndrome was used to differentiate between the DSZ group and the NDSZ group, which contains two parts. The first part was to assess the primary negative symptoms, i.e., the six negative symptoms were assessed in three dimensions (severity, primary, and stability). And the second part was to summarize the results of the first part, and to assess whether the patients’ clinical manifestations met the four dimensions of the diagnostic criteria of the DSZ. All assessing psychiatrists were blinded to group membership, and inter-rater reliability was maintained at a high level through consistent training.

Functional near-infrared imaging system

In our study, we employed the ETG-100 multi-channel fNIRS device (ETG-100, Hitachi Medical Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) to measure prefrontal hemodynamic changes. The system utilized two wavelengths of near-infrared light (780 and 830 nm) as described in previous studies [34, 35]. The device was equipped with a single 3 × 5 probe consisting of 8 sources and 7 detectors arranged in an alternating pattern with an inter-optode distance of 3 cm. The configuration generated 22 measurement channels (CHs), where each CH was defined as the midpoint between a source-detector pair.

The spatial registration followed NIRS-KIT’s standardized pipeline [36]: (1) Manufacturer-provided probe geometry (3 × 5 array) was coregistered to MNI space using the Colin27 template via built-in NFRI-based transformation [37]; (2) CHs were labeled via AAL atlas [38].

Through virtual registration and parameter transformation, we obtained the Montreal Neurological Institute coordinates for each fNIRS CH. Anatomically, each CH corresponded to cortical regions approximately 0–1.5 cm beneath the scalp surface [39]. To facilitate neuroanatomical interpretation of the results, CHs were grouped into regions of interests based on their corresponding BAs. This probabilistic mapping of each CH to the AAL atlas and BAs was conducted using the specific probe geometry and standard head-atlas registration protocols. Each CH was assigned to BA for which it had the highest probability of overlap (see Supplementary Table S3 for details). The resulting regions of interests were as follows: DLPFC(BA9, CHs 1, 2, and 3; BA46, CHs 4, 5, 9, 14, and 18), frontopolar cortex (BA10, CHs 6, 7, 8, 10,11, 12, 13, and 16), Drbitofrontal area (BA11, CHs 15, 17, 20, and 21), and Inferior prefrontal gyrus (BA47, CHs 19 and 22).

Optical signal variations in each CH were sampled at 10 Hz. The PFC was covered by all 22 CHs. The bilateral DLPFC included left-sided CHs 1, 5, 6, and 10 and right-sided CHs 4, 8, 9, and 13. The bilateral ventrolateral prefrontal cortex comprised left-sided CHs 14 and 19 and right-sided CHs 18 and 22. The mPFCcovered CHs 2, 3, 7, 11, 12, 15, 16, 17, 20, and 21. CH placements were determined based on the international 10–20 anatomical cranial correction system. For details, see Figs. 1 and 2. According to previous researches, in the cerebral blood flow of SZ patients, changes in HbO concentration are more perceptive than changes in HbR concentration [21]. Hence, we focused more on the signal changes of HbO.

FER tasks

The Japanese and Caucasian Facial Expressions of Emotion. (JACFEE, http://www.humintell.com/jacfee/) database [40] was used to test participants’ ability to process FER. The JACFEE images have been validated cross-culturally, including in East Asian populations, demonstrating acceptable recognition accuracy rates [41, 42]. In this study, the testing set consisted of 56 images (excluding presentation and practice pictures). Eight pictures of each of the 7 emotions (including disgust, surprise, sadness, happiness, fear, contempt, and anger) were selected, for a total of 56 pictures. 40 pictures depicted negative-valence emotions, while 16 pictures depicted positive emotions. This imbalance reflects the database’s structure and the greater diversity of basic negative-valence emotions.

Crucially, the imbalance between negative and positive stimuli does not confound our results because our statistical analyses were performed at the level of individual emotion categories (e.g., happiness vs. fear vs. anger), not on the aggregated categories of ‘positive’ and ‘negative’ emotion. Therefore, the number of trials per emotion category (8 per emotion) was balanced, ensuring that the accuracy and neural response estimates for each discrete emotion are equally reliable and directly comparable.

Before the formal test, a unique set of 28 images was used as training data to ensure all participants understood the task rules. During training, performance feedback (correct/incorrect) was provided after each trial. To prevent familiarity effects, all training images were completely excluded from the subsequent testing phase. The remaining 28 test images were used in the formal experiment. The 28 test images were fully randomized into a unique sequence for each participant using E-Prime 2.0 to minimize order effects. Stimuli were presented on a 14-inch laptop screen at a viewing distance of approximately 60 cm. In the block design, a 12-second blank screen was displayed before the FER test. Each stimulus lasted 8 s, followed by a 12-second interstimulus interval, forming a 20-second cycle repeated 28 times [43].

Participants wore fNIRS probes and viewed the stimuli. They were instructed to select one of seven emotions that matched the displayed image and to confirm their choice. No feedback was provided during the testing phase. The reaction time and accuracy for each trial were automatically recorded by E-Prime. Correct responses were scored as 1 point, while incorrect or timed-out responses received 0 points.

fNIRS signal analysis

NIRS data preprocessing and visualization were performed using MATLAB-based NIRS_KIT toolbox [37]. The preprocessing pipeline began with visual inspection of raw data to remove apparent sudden noise. This inspection was conducted by researchers blinded to the participants’ group assignments. Sudden noise was identified as simultaneous, abrupt amplitude changes exceeding 50% of the surrounding baseline across multiple wavelengths. Raw optical intensity data were then converted to optical density before being transformed into hemoglobin concentration changes (HbO and HbR) using the modified Beer-Lambert Law [44]. In the data viewer’s CH control panel, abnormal CHs were marked for exclusion based on the following quantitative criteria: [1] coefficient of variation >15%; [2] cardiac pulsation amplitude < 5 times the standard deviation of baseline noise; [3] gain values < 40%; [4] signals with standard deviation near zero (flatlining). CHs identified as invalid were simply excluded from all subsequent analyses; no spatial interpolation was performed to avoid introducing estimation errors. Following this process, the average number of CHs retained per participant was 20.82 for the HC group, 20.45 for the NDSZ group, and 20.37 for the DSZ group. The proportion of excluded CHs did not differ significantly between groups (p >0.05), ensuring that subsequent group comparisons were not confounded by data quality disparities. After preprocessing, the block and event average calculation was performed using the Block Average module in the NIRS_KIT toolbox.

To enhance signal stability, the first 10-second segments of raw data were truncated. Then, we used the last 10 s of the pre-task period as the baseline. The preprocessing pipeline included the following key steps: [1] Detrending: Baseline drift removal using first-order polynomial detrending [45, 2]. Motion Artifact Correction: Prior to the application of the TDDR algorithm, the quality of each CH was assessed through visual inspection and the quantitative exclusion criteria outlined above (e.g., coefficient of variation, gain values). While we did not employ the scalp coupling index specifically, our multi-criteria approach ensured that only CHs with robust physiological signals were retained for further analysis. The TDDR algorithm was then applied to the retained CHs to correct for motion artifacts without the need for fixed amplitude thresholds [46, 3]. Bandpass Filtering: To isolate the low-frequency oscillations inherent to the hemodynamic response, the data were bandpass-filtered between 0.01 and 0.2 Hz using a zero-phase shift finite impulse response (FIR) filter [47]. This range was chosen based on established practices in fNIRS literature [48]. The lower cutoff (0.01 Hz) removes very slow baseline drifts, while the upper cutoff (0.2 Hz) is set below the fundamental frequency of typical respiratory oscillations (which commonly occur at 0.2–0.3 Hz) and well below the cardiac frequency, thus effectively minimizing these specific physiological confounds.

Consistent with established evidence demonstrating the superior sensitivity of HbO in detecting regional cerebral blood flow variations compared to HbR [21], our analysis focused exclusively on HbO concentration signals.

A two-level generalized linear model framework was implemented: [1] Individual-Level Analysis: (a) Task-related reference time series were constructed using the canonical hemodynamic response function from NIRS_KIT toolbox [49]. (b) Observed signals were modeled as linear combinations of target regressors (experimental conditions), nuisance covariates, and error terms. The toolbox’s default GLM implementation does not include an autoregressive model for prewhitening. (c) Set the contrast vector to obtain channel-wise parameter estimation results for each condition.

[2] Group-Level Analysis: (a) One-way ANCOVA was performed to compare HbO concentration changes between groups, controlling for sex, age, education, illness duration, marital status, and family history. (b) Post hoc analyses with Bonferroni correction assessed between-group differences.

Data analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 26.0 and NIRS-KIT tool which is equipped with MATLAB 2023b (MathWorks) [50]. Categorical variables were analyzed using chi-square tests. Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation. For data passing normality tests, between-group comparisons were performed using: [1] Independent samples t-tests for two-group comparisons [2]. One-way ANOVA for three-group comparisons, with Bonferroni-adjusted post hoc tests for significant differences. Multiple comparisons of HbO concentration changes were addressed using Bonferroni correction [51]. We conducted Pearson or Spearman correlation analyses (Benjamini/Hochberg method was used to control the false discovery rate, adjusted p < 0.05) to investigate the correlation between clinical scales scores and HbO concentration changes. A two-tailed p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Demographic and clinical information

Table 1 shows the demographic and clinical characteristic results of the two-sample t-test, chi-square test, and one-way ANOVA with a post hoc Bonferroni test among the groups (the DSZ, NDSZ, and HC groups). There was no significant difference in marital status (p = 0.665) and age (p = 0.192) among the three groups. Similarly, duration of illness (p = 0.998), medication dosage (p = 0.508), and treatment duration (p = 0.909) did not differ significantly between the DSZ and NDSZ groups. In contrast, sex (p < 0.001), family history (p = 0.004), and education years (p < 0.001) exhibited statistically significant differences across groups. Compared with the HC group, both the DSZ and NDSZ groups had a significantly higher proportion of males (both p < 0.001) and family history (both p < 0.001). The HC group had significantly more years of education than both the DSZ and NDSZ groups (both p < 0.001). Compared with the NDSZ group, the DSZ group had significantly lower SAPS scores (including all subscales; p < 0.001), but higher SANS scores (all subscales except attention; p < 0.001). No significant differences were observed in BPRS or the SANS attention subscale scores.

JACFEE test indicators

There were significant differences found in accuracy (p ≤ 0.01) and reaction time (p ≤ 0.01) for all FER tasks among the DSZ group, NDSZ group, and HC group. Specifically, the DSZ group demonstrated significantly lower accuracy than the NDSZ group in recognizing anger (p = 0.002), sadness (p = 0.021), and contempt (p = 0.003). Compared with the HCs, both patient groups showed lower recognition accuracy for anger (p < 0.001), fear (p < 0.001), happiness (p < 0.010), surprise (p = 0.001), disgust (p < 0.001), sadness (p < 0.001), and contempt (p < 0.001). During all FER tasks, reaction time was significantly longer in both DSZ and NDSZ groups relative to HC (p < 0.01), with no statistically significant difference observed between the two patient groups. Changes in [HbO]/[HbR] during FER tasks

Table S2, Table S4 and Fig. 4 displayed the means and standard deviations of the HbO and HbR concentration changes during FER tasks and the results of Bonferroni-corrected one-way ANOVA among the three groups. There were statistically significant differences among the three groups in 8 CHs (CH 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 11, and 16; all adjusted p < 0.05), while the differences were not statistically significant in the other CHs.

Table 2; Fig. 5 showed the results of the post-hoc modified Bonferroni correction. For sadness, the DSZ group exhibited significantly reduced HbO activation compared with the NDSZ group in the DLPFC (BA9: CH 2, p = 0.0034; CH 3, p = 0.0022), the frontopolar cortex (BA10: CH 6, p = 0.0021; CH 8, p = 0.0013). For anger, the DSZ group exhibited significantly reduced HbO activation compared with the HC group in the frontopolar cortex (BA10, CH 11: p = 0.0066; CH 16: p = 0.0326), as well as significantly reduced HbO activation compared with the NDSZ group in the frontopolar cortex (BA10, CH 6: p = 0.0236) and the DLPFC (BA9, CH 3: p = 0.0429). For contempt, the DSZ group exhibited significantly reduced HbO activation compared with the NDSZ group in the DLPFC (BA9: CH 2, 3; BA46, CHs 4, all p < 0.01), and the frontopolar cortex (BA10: CH 6, 8, 11, 16; all p < 0.05), and compared with HCs in the DLPFC (BA9:CH 2; p < 0.001). For happiness, the DSZ group exhibited significantly reduced HbO activation compared with the NDSZ group in the frontopolar cortex (BA10: CH 6, p = 0.0008; CH 8: p = 0.0027; CH 11: p = 0.0109), and compared with HCs in the DLPFC (BA9: CH 3: p = 0.0419;) and the frontopolar cortex (BA10: CH 6, p = 0.0258; CH 11, p = 0.0240). For sadness, the DSZ group exhibited significantly increased HbR activation compared with the NDSZ group in the DLPFC (BA9: CH 8, p = 0.0119), as well as significantly increased HbR activation compared with the HCs in the DLPFC (BA9: CH 8, p = 0.0004). Correlation between clinical scores and [HbO] differences

We analyzed the HbO concentration changes of CHs with differences between groups and their associations with clinical scale scores. Correlation analyses revealed significant inverse relationships between negative symptoms severity and prefrontal hemodynamic responses, specifically in the DSZ group during various emotion recognition tasks. During the sadness recognition task, SANS total scores were inversely correlated with HbO concentration changes in the DLPFC (BA9, CH 2: r = -0.404, p = 0.012, adjusted p = 0.048), while SANS affective flattening scores were negatively correlated with activation in the frontopolar cortex (BA10, CH 8: r = -0.447, p = 0.005, adjusted p = 0.024). During the anger recognition task, negative correlations were observed between SANS alogia scores and the frontopolar cortex activation (BA10, CH 16: r = -0.476, p = 0.002, adjusted p = 0.010), as well as between total SANS scores and the frontopolar cortex activation (BA10, CH 11: r = -0.418, p = 0.009, adjusted p = 0.045). During the contempt recognition task, a negative correlation was found specifically between affective flattening scores and the frontopolar cortex activity (BA10, CH 8: r = -0.459, p = 0.004, adjusted p = 0.028). During the happiness recognition task, total SANS scores were inversely correlated with HbO concentration changes in the frontopolar cortex (BA10; CH 8: r = -0.512, p = 0.001, adjusted p = 0.004; CH 11: r = -0.416, p = 0.009, adjusted p = 0.036). No significant correlations were found between HbO concentration changes in CHs and scale scores in the DSZ group during other emotion recognition tasks. Additionally, neither the NDSZ group nor the HC group showed significant correlations between clinical scale scores and CHs activation levels during FER tasks. Discussion

Deficits in FER are regarded as a characteristic of SZ, significantly impacting patients’ social function [52]. Previous studies on FER have rarely explored the subtype-specific neural functional impairments in DSZ. In this study, to evaluate the characteristic deficits in DSZ patients during FER tasks, we selected a highly homogeneous DSZ patient cohort and employed the JACFEE emotion recognition test. Our findings revealed differences among DSZ patients, NDSZ patients, and HCs in both prefrontal cortex (PFC) hemodynamics and behavioral performance (accuracy and reaction time) when identifying different facial emotions. Furthermore, we analyzed the correlation between HbO concentration changes in PFC and clinical symptom severity in DSZ and NDSZ patients. Notably, HbO concentration changes in PFC were significantly negatively correlated with the severity of negative symptoms. These findings suggest that the fNIRS signals acquired during the JACFEE task may reveal a neurophysiological profile in DSZ patients.

Performance on the FER tasks showed significant differences among the three groups [6]. DSZ patients exhibited marked impairments in recognizing both positive and negative-valence emotions, consistent with previous findings [53]. Our study revealed that both DSZ and NDSZ patients performed worse than HCs, demonstrating lower accuracy and longer reaction times. Furthermore, DSZ patients showed significantly poorer accuracy compared to NDSZ patients in recognizing anger, fear, sadness, and contempt [54]. Previous research suggests that this deficit in negative-valence emotions recognition remains stable throughout the illness course and may serve as a trait marker of SZ [41]. This is consistent with our findings. Broadly speaking, FER is a core component of social cognition. Compared with the NDSZ patients, DSZ patients exhibit greater severity of negative symptoms and more marked social cognitive deficits. These deficits are closely associated with negative symptoms, as well as multidimensional neurocognitive dysfunctions, including attention, learning, memory, information processing speed, cognitive flexibility, and executive function [6]. Thus, our finding that DSZ patients performed worse than NDSZ patients in recognizing these emotions can be explained by these underlying impairments.

FNIRS provided an effective means for assessing PFC activation level in this study. We observed significantly reduced activation in BA9 (DLPFC) and BA10 (frontopolar cortex, part of the mPFC) among DSZ patients compared with both NDSZ patients and HCs during sadness, anger, contempt, and happiness recognition tasks. The abnormal function of BA9/BA46 and BA10 may represent a shared neural substrate underlying both emotional and cognitive deficits in SZ, playing a critical role in FER [55]. Specifically, hypoactivation in BA9/BA46 may reflect an impaired ability to sustain or integrate emotional experiences. During the emotion maintenance task, reduced BA9/BA46 activation in SZ patients was associated with negative symptoms, including affective flattening and alogia [56]. Clinically, therapeutic interventions targeting the left BA9/46, including transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS), repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS), and accelerated intermittent theta burst stimulation (iTBS), have demonstrated efficacy in improving emotion recognition and negative symptoms in SZ patients [57, 58]. This indirectly supports the hypothesis of left BA9/46 hypoactivation and dysfunction in SZ.

Genetic neuroimaging studies further reveal aberrant right BA9/46 activation in SZ patients during facial working memory tasks [59], potentially reflecting its pivotal role in cognitive control. Meanwhile, BA10 (frontopolar cortex) is crucial for emotional perception and contextual integration. During emotion recognition tasks, SZ patients with high negative schizotypy exhibit weakened functional connectivity between the amygdala and BA10 compared to those with low schizotypy [60]. Given that BA10 does not operate in isolation, dysregulation within the prefrontal (BA9/10/46) -limbic circuit is considered a fundamental neural basis for social cognitive deficits in SZ.

Normally, fear, disgust, and anger processing rely on coordinated activation of the amygdala, insula, and anterior cingulate cortex, respectively, alongside BA10 engagement [61]. However, in DSZ patients, BA10 dysfunction extends beyond hypoactivation and weakened limbic connectivity to include structural abnormalities [62], which may manifest as impaired affective empathy. This dual structural-functional impairment correlates with poor emotion recognition performance, suggesting that DSZ patients struggle to integrate facial emotional cues with contextual information due to BA9/46 and BA10 dysfunction. Notably, no significant activation differences were detected between NDSZ and HCs in these regions prefrontal regions (BA9, BA10, and BA46). This may be attributed to: [1] Less severe PFC dysfunction in NDSZ patients (milder negative symptoms and lower SANS scores); [2] Compensatory overactivation in PFC when processing highly salient emotional stimuli, potentially masking underlying deficits [63].

Correlation analyses revealed moderate negative associations between activation levels in BA10 and left BA9/46 and negative symptoms scores in DSZ patients during sadness, contempt, anger, and happiness recognition tasks. This finding aligns with prior research demonstrating significant correlations between negative symptoms and emotion recognition deficits in SZ, particularly for negative-valence emotions [14, 20]. Damage to BA10 and BA9/46 in DSZ patients may be associated with primary negative symptoms (e.g., affective flattening). These results suggest that more severe negative symptoms were associated with greater impairment in negative-valence emotions recognition in DSZ patients. Given that the perception and integration of facial negative-valence emotions critically depend on intact PFC function (especially BA10 and BA9/46), our findings are consistent with this neural mechanism. Recent studies support distinct neurocognitive impairment patterns between DSZ and NDSZ patients, indicating that prefrontal (BA9, BA10, and BA46) abnormalities differ qualitatively rather than merely in severity [64, 65]. DSZ patients exhibit widespread neurocognitive dysfunction, which may explain their uniformly worse FER performance compared to NDSZ patients. This distinction also accounts for the absence of significant correlations between PFC activation levels and negative symptoms severity in NDSZ patients versus HCs.

Our findings have several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design of this study precludes any causal inference regarding the relationship between PFC dysfunction and DSZ. While our findings highlight robust associations, longitudinal studies are necessary to determine whether these fNIRS signatures represent a precursor to, or a consequence of, the illness. Second, all the SZ patients were already receiving antipsychotic medication before enrollment, with variations in drug types, dosages, and treatment durations, making it difficult to completely rule out potential effects of pharmacological interventions on brain function. Third, our fNIRS system’s limited CH configuration only allowed PFC assessment. This technical constraint prevented evaluation of other relevant regions (e.g., temporal/parietal lobes and limbic system), potentially biasing results interpretation. Future studies with larger sample sizes should employ more advanced techniques (e.g., combining functional magnetic resonance imaging, electrophysiology, and genetic approaches) to further investigate social cognitive functions in DSZ patients across different illness stages.

Conclusion

In summary, individuals with DSZ and NDSZ may exhibit varying degrees of impairment in FER. Furthermore, compared to NDSZ patients, those with DSZ are likely to demonstrate worse performance during FER tasks, characterized by reduced activation in the DLPFC and frontopolar area, which is associated with the severity of negative symptoms.
