Introduction

Upper tract urothelial carcinoma (UTUC) is a recognized component of the Lynch syndrome tumor spectrum, and identification of patients who may benefit from genetic evaluation is encouraged [1]. However, increasing evidence suggests that UTUC exhibits genetic heterogeneity beyond mismatch repair (MMR)‐associated pathways [2].

In Japan, syndrome‐specific genetic testing (SSGT), targeting MMR genes, remains the predominant approach. Although SSGT is effective in detecting classical Lynch syndrome, it may limit identification of pathogenic variants outside predefined hereditary syndromes. In contrast, multigene panel testing (MGPT) is increasingly being adopted internationally, supported by precision oncology frameworks emphasizing broader genomic assessment [3].

Despite these developments, universal MGPT is not always feasible because of cost, interpretation challenges, and resource availability. Therefore, the key clinical question is not whether MGPT should be universally applied, but how to identify patients in whom SSGT alone may be insufficient. Establishing clinical indicators for referral to genetic evaluation remains a challenge.

Case Presentation

A 41‐year‐old man was referred for evaluation of a left renal lesion incidentally detected during a health checkup. Imaging revealed a tumor in the left renal pelvis with hilar lymph node enlargement (Figure 1). Ureteroscopy was attempted; however, insertion was impossible because a narrow ureter was associated with incomplete duplicated ureter branching near the left ureteral orifice. Ureteroscopic biopsy was therefore not performed. Selective left renal pelvic urine cytology was suspicious for malignancy. The clinical stage was documented as cT3‐4N1M0 based on preoperative imaging.

Contrast‐enhanced computed tomography at diagnosis. (a) A tumor was observed in the left renal pelvis. (b) Left hilar lymph node enlargement was also detected. Arrows indicate the primary tumor and enlarged lymph node, respectively.

Neoadjuvant dose‐dense methotrexate, vinblastine, doxorubicin, and cisplatin chemotherapy was administered for three cycles, resulting in a partial response. Robot‐assisted left nephroureterectomy with lymph node dissection was subsequently performed. Histopathological examination revealed invasive high‐grade urothelial carcinoma (ypT3), with lymphovascular invasion. Surgical margins were negative (RM0), and no residual nodal metastasis was observed (ypN0).

Adjuvant nivolumab therapy was initiated. After 14 cycles, a papillary bladder tumor was detected. Transurethral resection, followed by a single intravesical chemotherapy instillation of 30 mg pirarubicin, was performed. Histopathology showed noninvasive low‐grade urothelial carcinoma (pTa, LVI0).

The patient requested genetic testing because of concern regarding hereditary cancer risk in his children. His family history included multiple malignancies (Figure 2): gastric, liver, and pancreatic cancers in his father; breast cancer in his mother and multiple maternal relatives; and gastric cancer in a maternal uncle. This suggested a hereditary cancer predisposition but did not meet criteria for a single syndrome, such as Lynch syndrome.

Although SSGT targeting MMR genes was initially considered, germline MGPT was selected primarily because of young age at UTUC diagnosis, diverse cancer types within the family, multiple affected relatives, and a family history not typical of Lynch syndrome. Following genetic counseling and shared decision‐making, germline MGPT was performed using ACTRisk (Canon Medical Systems Corporation, Tochigi, Japan), a 67‐gene hereditary cancer panel, using peripheral blood DNA. No pathogenic variants were detected in MMR genes. Instead, a germline BRCA2 frameshift variant (NM_000059:c.994del) was identified and classified as pathogenic according to the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics guidelines [4].

After identification of the germline BRCA2 pathogenic variant, surveillance was initiated. Prostate‐specific antigen was measured every 3 months and was 2.0 ng/mL. Imaging every 3–6 months for UTUC surveillance also assessed pancreatic lesions. Weekly male breast self‐examination during bathing was advised. No BRCA2‐associated malignancy has been detected to date.

Discussion

This case highlights a clinically relevant limitation of relying solely on SSGT for selected patients with UTUC. Although Lynch syndrome is the most recognized hereditary condition associated with UTUC, increasing evidence indicates genetic heterogeneity beyond MMR‐associated pathways [1, 2].

This case does not establish BRCA2 as a causative factor in UTUC. Rather, it illustrates the value of comprehensive germline evaluation in selected patients whose clinical and family histories are not adequately explained by a single hereditary syndrome. The decision to pursue MGPT in this patient was mainly influenced by young age at diagnosis, coexistence of gastrointestinal and breast cancers in the family, and the absence of a family history pattern typical of Lynch syndrome.

Solely relying on syndrome‐specific or phenotype‐driven assessment may lead to under‐recognition of clinically significant germline variants [5]. Therefore, urologists should consider consulting a genetic specialist when family history is atypical, mixed, or difficult to classify. This approach should be balanced against cost‐effectiveness and available resources.

Population‐based genomic data have shown that BRCA2 pathogenic variants are associated with an increased risk of urothelial carcinoma, particularly bladder cancer [6]. In addition, clinically significant germline variants, including BRCA2, are present in patients with urothelial carcinoma and may not be identified using family history alone [5]. However, evidence specific to UTUC remains limited. From a practical standpoint, MGPT should not be considered a universal replacement for SSGT, but rather a complementary tool following appropriate clinical assessment and specialist referral.

Real‐world data from Asia suggest that germline testing provides clinically meaningful information when applied to selected contexts [7]. Identifications of a germline BRCA2 pathogenic variant may have implications beyond etiologic interpretation, including surveillance for prostate and pancreatic cancers and genetic counseling for at‐risk relatives [8]. Although DNA damage response pathways are increasingly recognized as therapeutic targets in genitourinary cancers, the clinical implications of BRCA2 alterations in UTUC remain to be clarified [9].

To support clinical decision‐making, we propose a simplified algorithm to guide referral for genetic evaluation in UTUC (Figure 3). The clinical indication in Table 1 and the proposed algorithm are exploratory and hypothesis‐generating, because they are based on a single case and existing literature. Further validation in larger UTUC cohorts is required.

Conclusion

This case highlights the value of comprehensive germline evaluation in selected patients with UTUC and atypical or mixed family histories, rather than establishing a causal relationship between BRCA2 and UTUC. A selective MGPT strategy combined with early referral to genetic specialists may improve identification of clinically actionable germline variants in routine urological practice.
