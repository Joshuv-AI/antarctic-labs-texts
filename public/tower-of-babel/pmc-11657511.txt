Implementing and sustaining dementia care coordinators across integrated care systems: a realist evaluation
Authors: Ruth Abrams, Johanna Spiers, Jill Maben, Wendy Grosvenor, Morro Touray, Heather Gage
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGlobally, dementia care is under strain. Rising rates across ageing populations, coupled with overstretched health and care systems, mean that people living with dementia and their carers are missing out on crucial support. Addressing dementia care is a key priority for the UK government. This has led to a period of care transformation, including the implementation of new dementia support services across integrated care systems (ICS). However, little is known about how these new services work. This evaluation identifies how a dementia care coordinator service, implemented in the largest ICS in England, works for people living with dementia, their carers and the workforce.MethodsA realist evaluation using mixed methods was carried out between 2022 and 2024. This involved a repeat survey with dementia care coordinators, carried out a year apart, alongside 57 interviews with coordinators, service managers, healthcare practitioners, people living with dementia and their carers. A realist logic of analysis was applied across all data sets.ResultsThree broad concepts were identified including (1) workforce design and organisational culture, (2) meeting the needs of people living with dementia and their carers and (3) connecting to services and integrating care. A total of 23 context-mechanism-outcome configurations (CMOCs) across these concepts highlighted that whilst tensions exist between the service and wider system, dementia care coordinators ultimately act as bridge builders, connecting people to much-needed support. However, services like this could become a victim of their own success due to increasing caseloads and the risk of staff burnout.ConclusionsThe dementia care coordinator service is capable of supporting people who are pre- and post-dementia diagnosis despite the care system being under strain. This is a direct result of the bridge building work of the dementia care coordinators. Our findings support evidence-based recommendations for those wanting to implement and sustain a system-wide service and provide evidence for policy makers to consider increased funding for this service nationwide.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12916-024-03806-0.Keywords: Dementia, Service delivery, Integrated care systems, Workforce, Care coordination, Realist evaluation

Background

Dementia is a progressive condition with care and support needs that change over time [1]. Supporting people living with dementia and their carers is a major global health challenge. Ageing populations and rising dementia diagnosis rates mean that a dementia diagnosis occurs somewhere in the world every 3.2 s [2]. The number of people living with dementia worldwide is predicted to double in 2024 [3], and by 2030, global dementia rates will be 78 million [2, 4]. In the UK, the number of people living with dementia currently stands at one million [5], which is expected to rise to 1.7 in England and Wales by 2040 [6]. This presents both a current and future challenge [4] which is particularly difficult in healthcare systems where resources are stretched and there is a shortage of staff [7].

Rising dementia rates mean that planning for and ensuring appropriate dementia care is essential [2, 4]. Indeed, it is a key priority for both NHS England and the UK government, being central to two Prime Ministerial Challenges on Dementia [8, 9] and the NHS Long Term Plan [10]. Consequently, dementia care in the UK is undergoing a period of transformation, which includes a growing prevalence of dementia care coordination services and a focus on intervention in the community [11]. New services are encouraged to align with the National Institute of Clinical Excellence (NICE) recommendation that service users have a single point of contact (ideally a health or social care worker) for all their dementia-related needs [12], a recommendation that has also been made by the Alzheimer’s Society [13]. However, further work is needed to understand the current evidence base given that services have been reported to be fragmented [14], hard to navigate [15] and, at times, poor quality [16]. In particular, following COVID-19, some dementia services have closed whilst others continued, which may have widened health inequalities [17]. People living with dementia must have their needs met, since symptoms may accelerate when this does not happen [18].

Staff associated with dementia care coordinator services have a variety of job titles, including dementia support workers [19], dementia care navigators [20], dementia advisors [21, 22] and dementia care coordinators (DCCs) [23]. There is some variation in these roles. They are largely non-clinical [20], although some authors have reported clinical staff taking on these roles [24]. Although these roles are varied, being filled by people from a range of backgrounds and education levels [25], they tend to offer advice and education about dementia [20], connect service users with local groups [20, 21], facilitate access to organisations such as social services [26], coordinate care with healthcare professionals [26] and assist with decision making [24]. Whilst they carry out similar functions to social prescribers, they are different in that they focus exclusively on one condition. Several authors have investigated the effectiveness of dementia care coordinator roles. Whilst some positive outcomes for people living with dementia and their carers have been found [24, 27], results are mixed and inconclusive. This may be because of the ill-defined nature of the role and an interest in different outcomes [20, 24–26, 28]. This evaluation, therefore, aims to identify what about such a service works, for whom, how and under what circumstances [29].

Methods

Service and setting

In 2022, Kent County Council and the Kent and Medway Integrated Care Board (ICB) jointly commissioned the DCC service to serve a population of 1.868 million across Kent and Medway, in the south-east of England. The Integrated Care System (ICS) includes 42 primary care networks (PCNs), that is, local groupings of general practices serving between 30,000 and 50,000 people each. There are approximately 27,000 people living with dementia (PLWD) in Kent and Medway, a number that is expected to increase to 38,000 by 2030 [30].

Two service providers were commissioned to implement the service across the ICS, with one DCC allocated to each of the 42 PCNs, for two years, commencing April 2022. Low dementia diagnosis rates and fragmented post-diagnostic support are issues of local concern in Kent and Medway. The service was therefore designed to be proactive, improving diagnosis rates and supporting PLWD and their carers to navigate the care system including pre, during and post diagnosis (i.e. they stop providing care at the point of end of life, or care home entry). The DCC role is non-clinical, providing consistent long-term support tailored to individuals’ needs. DCCs receive referrals from health and care workforces across the ICS and self-referrals. They conduct needs assessments, make referrals to other local services (such as dementia cafes or respite care) and work with healthcare professionals including GPs, admiral nurses and psychiatrists to support and improve quality of life for PLWD and their carers.

Theoretical framework and design

To respond to the research aim, a realist evaluation was undertaken [29, 31]. This approach was taken because it can help to make sense of a complex intervention, in this case the DCC service. Realist evaluation involves asking what is (and is not) working about an intervention (or in this case, service), for whom, how and under what circumstances [31]. These questions are addressed by testing (refuting, confirming or refining) taken-for-granted assumptions about the intervention and refined through data collection. The contexts in which interventions are delivered impact on their various outcomes, intended or unintended [32]. Outcomes are triggered by underlying, unseen, causal mechanisms which are inherent to different contexts [33]. When these elements are arranged into configurations (i.e. Context + Mechanism = Outcome heuristics (CMOCs)), they can explain different details and elements of what is and is not working about an intervention. The endpoint of realist evaluation is to provide evidence-based CMOCs which are encapsulated in an overall explanation called a final programme theory [34].

PPI and stakeholder involvement

Two groups were established at the outset of the project including a service user and carer group and a professional healthcare expert group. Both groups met four times across the 24-month project. The service user and carer group comprised of two people living with dementia and four carers. Members advised on how best to engage people with dementia, for example, by suggesting dementia-friendly wording to be used on questionnaires and interviews. This group also helped to ensure that our findings were produced in plain English and any recommendations for service change acceptable to people with dementia and their carers. The professional healthcare expert group comprised three clinicians from Kent and Medway and a person previously employed by the DCC service. They also provided input on the interview topic guides, advised on recruitment and tailored the ongoing implementation of service improvement recommendations.

Data collection

Prior to study commencement, a favourable ethical opinion from NHS Research Ethics Committee (22/IEC08/0036) was received. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before data collection, and only those with the capacity to consent were included. The identity of all participants is protected with pseudonyms.

Recruitment and data collection: survey with DCCs

In collaboration with our professional healthcare expert group, and our service user and carer group, the research team developed an online survey to gather DCCs’ background, demographics and experiences of the job including training; joined up working with GPs and other professionals; support and how they spent their working time (patient-facing, patient-related non-patient facing, administration). Questions were developed based on existing literature and guided by the initial programme theory [35].

In February 2023 (time point 1, T1), an online survey was distributed by service providers to all employed DCCs across the ICS (n = 42). Reminders were sent where appropriate. The survey included multiple choice and open text box questions. Job satisfaction was measured using a Likert scale and burnout using the Maslach Burnout Inventory [36]. Thirty-one DCCs (73.8% response rate) completed the survey.

This survey was issued again in March 2024 (time point 2, T2) with additional items to capture information on how DCCs engage in self-care, suggestions for engaging GPs in joined up working and which healthcare workers could engage in joined up working. This survey was distributed to 45 DCCs. A total of 33 (73.3%) DCCs responded. Seventeen participants completed both surveys (Table 1).

Data analysis

In line with a realist approach, we developed a set of 35 initial CMOCs formulated as If/Then/Because statements [34] (see Additional file 1. Table S1). These statements were based on conversations with the service commissioner; project team meetings; stakeholder conversations with service users, carers and health professionals; case studies from service providers and findings from a DCC focus group in 2022 (IPT; 37).

Data from the qualitative interviews were coded by JS and RA, using the NVivo12 software. Analysis was inductive, deductive (i.e. informed by the initial CMOCs [37]) and retroductive, supported by the teams’ knowledge about the service. JS and RA discussed analysis throughout the process, refining and re-ordering conceptual labels, with continuous reference to the initial CMOCs throughout to see where evidence could be further tested or refuted (see Additional file 1: Table S1).

Qualitative data from T1 and T2 were exported into Excel and given conceptual labels. Any new concepts arising from T2 data were cross checked with qualitative interview data [38], and codes from across the entire body of data were amended, added to and re-ordered to allow for our new understanding. Quantitative data from both T1 and T2 of the survey were analysed using descriptive statistics and equally subsumed into the conceptual labels emerging from qualitative interview (and survey) analysis. Results from all elements of data collection are presented together in this paper.

During our initial analysis of both the survey and qualitative data, we returned to the list of CMOCs, noting relevant points to refute or refine our initial ideas [39]. This process of building on, refining and refuting CMOCs continued throughout analysis and writing. Two of the authors (RA and JS) discussed all CMOCs throughout the project, with some being removed and others merged, leading to a final list of 23 CMCOs (see Additional file 1. Table S2) that made up our final programme theory.

Bridge building as a golden thread

A key concept that arose during the data analysis process was that DCCs and the services they operate within make a difference by building bridges across communities, services, professionals, systems and one another. To help elucidate this conceptual finding, we draw on findings from Drennan et al. [40], who positioned certain professionals (in their case, Physician Associates) as boundary spanners, as well as the work of Nasir et al. [41]. These professionals act in ways that help connect disparate groups (service users, healthcare professionals, local dementia groups and so on) who could benefit from working collaboratively [42]. They are often innovative workers with good communication and people skills, rather than clinical knowledge [43]. They can promote good communication, trust and sustainable working relationships between individuals and groups [42–44] and are ideally placed to help navigate complex problems (such as the growing need for dementia care) whose solutions will require input from many sources [43]. For the purposes of our paper, we refer to DCCs as bridge builders, a finding we will return to in the discussion.

Results

This section presents findings that have been divided into three concepts that span (1) workforce design and organisational culture, (2) meeting the needs of people living with dementia and their carers and (3) connecting to services and integrating care. These concepts house our CMOCs, illustrated in our final programme theory (Fig. 1). Each concept holds tensions that demonstrate what about the DCC service is both working and not working well. Exemplar evidence is below, and in our supplementary material.

Final programme theory

Workforce design and organisational culture

Personal characteristics and knowledge

As reported in both surveys and interviews, DCCs want to do this job because they are inherently people-focused and want to make a difference in people’s lives. Conducting home visits and seeing people face-to-face were components of the role that often resulted in job satisfaction (CMOC 1, CMOC 2).

‘You actually feel like you’re doing some good out there, you know, because people really appreciate you visiting and appreciate advice on how to cope with it day to day. So there’s a lot of job satisfaction with this role, definitely’. (DCC20, interview).

DCCs also identified being able to respond appropriately as a crucial component of their skillset. Being organised and using their initiative helped them to proactively cope with the high workloads they faced and connect service users with support (CMOC 1).

‘It’s just managing your time […], I’ve got fifty tasks to do, but ten of them are really important, so I’m going to do those first’. (DCC12, interview).

DCCs reported going above and beyond, spending their free time looking up resources, seeking non-dementia specific groups which might be open to dementia members and working longer hours than paid for.

‘I went out to all the art groups in the area and spoke to them and said look, I’m talking to people with dementia, would it be okay if they joined your group, they’ve done art before, they’d like to continue to do art’. (DCC20, interview).

Many DCCs reported receiving positive feedback from service users regarding the service they offered, which appeared to enhance job satisfaction (CMOC 3). Indeed, job satisfaction was reported to be high across both surveys: at T1, 94% of respondents reported job satisfaction; at T2, 93% did. Data from interviewed participants confirmed this finding.

‘She was like, ‘I am really, really grateful.’ I was like, ‘That’s okay,’ got in the car and whoo! So, no, I was really happy with that outcome’. (DCC38, interview participant).

DCCs also highlighted in both the surveys and interviews that dementia-specific knowledge (supported by employed-led training) helped them to feel more confidence in their care delivery (CMOC 4). This was particularly the case if a DCC did not come from a healthcare background.

‘We do get quite intensive training, but I think that’s really good because it’s beneficial. I don’t come from a healthcare background […] but I found that very beneficial because you really are forearmed, and especially when people want to know a little bit more about the different types of dementia’. (DCC20, interview).

Data suggests that the combination of being people-focused, organised, able to use initiative and knowledgeable in dementia are personal characteristics that enable many DCCs to build bridges within and across services and communities (Table 3).

Teamwork

DCCs described how, when working well in a team with other DCCs, they felt supported. Access to their peers meant that DCCs felt better able to share information and respond to service user needs effectively (CMOC 5).

‘A lot of what you learn is on the job as you’re going. […] If I’m unsure about something, there’s always somebody there to speak to about things’. (DCC40, interview).

‘I have good colleagues. So if there are issues, […] you can check amongst yourselves, you don’t have to give names and stuff, but you can run situations past […], what would you do, blah, blah. Having a good team is really good’. (DCC13, interview).

Information sharing, peer-to-peer learning and support appeared to heighten DCCs’ understanding of resources, groups or activities available in the local area as well as how to respond in challenging situations. DCCs appeared to be skilled at building bridges within their team and across to other teams and services (Table 4).

CMOC relating to teamwork

High caseloads, understaffing and low salaries

Employing the right people for the role, training them and fostering an environment where information can be shared are all aspects of the DCC workforce design and culture which are working well. However, DCCs struggled with high caseloads. Some survey participants reported working more than their contracted hours. For full-time staff, nine of 21 (T1) and five of 24 (T2) DCCs stated they worked more than 9 h a day. For part-time staff, one of 10 (T1) and one of five (T2) DCCs worked more than 10% over their contracted hours.

Between T1 and T2, the proportion of time spent on home visits decreased (from 32 to 21% of all work time), whilst the proportion of time spent on non-patient facing activities and meetings increased, demonstrating that as workload increased, the time DCCs were able to spend caring for service users decreased. Qualitative participants perceived their high caseloads as due to high referral rates into the service, coupled with the service’s understaffing.

‘From my point of view, that is the real main difficulty. I don’t think there’s enough co-ordinators. […] But that couldn’t have been foreseen, you know? […] We never thought it would be so successful so quickly’. (SM04, interview participant).

This meant that DCCs were more likely to suffer burnout (CMOC 6), as demonstrated by the survey findings, where rates of burnout—defined by Maslach and colleagues [36] as negative feelings about work or exhaustion at least once a week—increased from 26 to 38%.

‘You’re split in half all the time, aren’t you really? You’re trying to do this and you’re trying to do that as well. It’s just really hard. It’s just the workload is immense, you know, there’s just so many people that that need help’. (DCC20, interview participant).

Some DCCs had already left the role, whilst others were considering doing so.

‘Quite a few people are leaving. I’m keeping my eye out for a role. The trouble is, I absolutely love this role, I love the support I’m providing, and I really don’t want to leave, but, at the same time, it’s not sustainable, to be doing all these hours’. (DCC07, interview participant).

Healthcare professionals also recognised this as a risk.

‘The most stressful thing is being desperate to do a job well and not being able to, and people are likely to leave if they don’t feel they can provide the service that they want to’. (HCP02, interview participant).

Many participants expressed a need for the DCC team to expand so it could cope with the workload (CMOC 7).

‘I think, and I can speak from my colleagues within the mental health team, I think we all just feel that these poor guys [DCCs] […] either need more coordinators or less work’. (HCP05, interview participant).

Additionally, DCCs and SMs reported that DCCs were often not paid adequately for the work they did, which could result in feelings of not being valued. This was another factor feeding workforce attrition (CMOC 8).

‘Quite a lot of people have left because of the salary. […] And it’s a shame, because they get used to the job and they start doing a great job and we’re all working together, and then people are like, oh, I’ve had enough, it’s just not enough pay, so I’ve got to go because I can earn better money somewhere else’. (DCC35, interview participant).

In addition to not feeling valued, the low salary was perceived as problematic because it did not match the skills that DCCs were learning.

‘But that is one of the biggest things for these dementia coordinators, that training programme. It’s so upskilled these people, which has had a negative impact, because now their salary does not meet that skill’. (SM06, interview participant).

Thus, even when the right people have been recruited for the role and a trained and working in a team that shares knowledge, DCCs with high caseloads who are working in understaffed teams are more likely to struggle with burnout and may leave the role (Table 5).

Meeting the needs of people living with dementia and their carers

Being consistently there

In line with the NICE guidelines [12], people living with dementia and their carers often saw DCCs as a single, trusted point of contact who they could speak to about anything related to dementia. This was achieved through their always being just a phone call away (CMOC 9).

‘I have somebody I can fall back on and to give me advice. […] You know, and I think that that is… well it makes a difference’. (PLWD01, interview participant).

Service users valued this responsiveness because it empowered them to take informed steps and, at times, enhanced their access to care such GPs appointment or community activities. These outcomes were reassuring for service users.

‘That level of reassurance to someone and family carers is massive, rather than not knowing… if someone leaves your house and then you think, ‘well, what do I do now? I don’t know who to go to next.’ (SM08, interview participant).

Importantly, this continuity of care was beneficial for family carers as well as people living with dementia, since it meant that carers could draw on DCC’s expertise and emotional support to be able to carry on with their caring responsibilities (CMOC 10).

‘I’ve always been a reasonably fit and healthy person, by the time she [PLWD] went into the care home, I was on blood pressure tablets and antidepressants, and I was really struggling to cope […] I think [my DCC] played a big part in me getting through all that, because she was always there, you know? […] She always rang me back, or if I emailed her, she always got in touch straight away, and she always was really, really helpful’. (FC08, interview participant).

Thus, data suggests that when service users feel that they will receive continuity of care from trusted DCCs who are consistently there for them, this will facilitate supportive care, thereby providing a bridge between DCC and service user (Table 6).

Caring for carers

Whilst the DCC service is intended to support people living with dementia in the first instance, many family carers also reported feeling supported by DCCs. Carers were supported by DCCs signposting them to specific carer local support, e.g. respite care, and DCCs educating them about dementia stages and symptoms (CMOC 11). A knowledgeable DCC can also build bridges for carers to access practical help, respite care or emotional support, resulting in feelings of relief and a reduction in carer burden and social isolation:

‘The son called up and said, I’m still not coping, the care agency isn’t helping, I just feel like I’m not getting this break, that I think a care home would be the best opportunity. I said, let’s speak to social services, let’s get a Kent Card in place […] within a week he noticed the change in his mum. He felt relieved […] he wasn’t stressed any more’. (DCC12, interview participant).

Additionally, DCCs can offer tailored advice and education about dementia to help carers (CMOC 12). For example, several DCCs described offering carers explanations of how dementia had impacted the senses, memory and abilities of those carers’ loved ones.

‘I explained to her how his [PWLD] senses were and how dulled they are compared to ours, and therefore what we see, feel, think and touch, everything is different’. (DCC20, interview participant).

Based on this knowledge, DCCs suggested strategies for managing dementia symptoms, improving carers’ quality of life and enabling them to feel more competent.

‘I went and did a training course thing on how to talk to people with dementia, and just things that you would think was common sense, but they’re really not, when you’re dealing with someone with dementia, and […] that was really helpful. Really helpful to understand how to talk to her, you know, how to talk to [PLWD] and […] how to behave with her’. (FC08, interview participant).

Thus, data suggests that as well as helping people living with dementia, DCCs can also support their carers (Table 7).

Mitigating crises

In accounts from both family carers and DCCs, we heard reports of crises managed and thus mitigated (CMOC 13). This included de-escalating and finding solutions to challenges such as: the person with dementia repeatedly urinating on the carpet/being unable to locate the toilet in the night, a PWLD getting lost, carers feeling severely overwhelmed, visits to A&E and serious physical or mental health challenges.

‘I had a call from a family where the daughter was in tears, you know, my mum’s not sleeping, she’s keeping my dad up all night, she hasn’t slept for days. She’s wrapping up knives and putting them in her purse, not because she wants to hurt anybody, but because she’s so scared. She’s trying to get out of the house, but they had like really steep steps down, concrete steps down […] and then a really busy road. […] So I went there that day and then did a rapid support referral, also contacted the GP to get them to contact urgently […] so it was about getting some antibiotics very quickly, getting the support put in place, getting night sits put in place so the husband can sleep’. (DCC41, interview participant).

Thus, because service users felt confident in their DCCs’ expertise, and could access it in a timely way, their needs were quickly met, major issues were resolved through appropriate service utilisation, and their quality of life improved. This was because of the bridge built not only between a DCC and service user but also between a DCC and the wider health and social care services (Table 8).

Diagnosis rates

Accounts from our qualitative interviews indicate that some DCCs proactively discussed diagnosis with service users, encouraging them to seek, and booking them in for, a diagnosis if they had not already done this (CMOC 14).

‘Every single client that I go to see, if they haven’t started the diagnosis process, I always do […] try and encourage the people to do that’. (DCC17, interview participant).

DCC participants listed various ways in which they tried to ensure that they could have these conversations with service users in the community. These included being present at memory cafes and dementia groups (including those for carers), running drop-in sessions and/or having leaflets available at GP surgeries, attending bigger events such as dementia roadshows and advertising via social media. Thus, DCCs provide a bridge between service users, and the diagnostic process (Table 9).

Unmet needs

Whilst there are many ways in which DCCs met service users’ needs, there are some areas where it was harder for DCCs to support people living with dementia and their carers. For example, several interviewees discussed people whose dementia symptoms were more advanced, and/or who lived alone, with no carer. It was harder for DCCs to support such people (CMOC 15). This might be because the DCCs could not get an accurate picture of the care needed, perhaps because the people living with dementia could not answer questions accurately. At other times, DCCs could not make contact or the PLWD did not remember that care was in place.

‘It’s those isolated people who […] may have the symptoms of dementia, but nobody around them to support them… […] Nobody to notice. They might not notice themselves. So, they’re going to be the people who are hidden, might not communicate with their GP surgery. So, I don’t know how we tackle that’. (SM04, interview participant).

In these instances, it was difficult to form bridges because service users’ needs were greater than the service’s capacity.

Potential users of the DCC service include those from a diverse range of backgrounds, cultures and ages, including those living with early onset dementia (i.e. those younger than 65) as well as those from minoritised cultures and communities. Some DCCs and service managers reported that it was harder to support people from these groups (CMOC 16):

‘[I: Are there any groups you find difficult to reach?] I’d say elders of the Sikh population. […] We have traveller communities that are very difficult to engage with. Any type of community where you’ve got almost closed family, it’s almost like […] it’s private, we’re dealing with it. You know, no outsiders. I think you get that in most communities, so it’s finding our way in’. (SM03, interview participant).

Those with early onset dementia may not ‘see’ themselves in the type of services a DCC may refer onto (e.g. local memory cafes), or they may not want to see that far ahead into their dementia progression (CMOC 17).

‘[PLWD] really struggles to identify with the various groups because she kind of goes along and is ten, twenty years younger than most of the people there, which has definitely caused some frustration to her because she feels hey, I’m off to see the old people and they’ve got dementia, and of course [she] isn’t processing that as her own diagnosis’. (FC009, interview participant).

As such, even when DCCs work hard to consistently be there, some bridges cannot be built, and some service users’ needs will go unmet (Table 10).

Connecting to services and integrating care

Knowledge about and availability of local services

DCCs signpost to community resources, thus effectively building bridges for service users to access beneficial services. These included dementia groups such as memory cafes, music and art groups; practical support such as occupational therapy services; medical help such as access to doctors’ appointments, treatments or walking aids; financial support such as benefits and respite care so that carers can have a break.

‘You may notice that they’re not managing to meet their care needs, so we will refer and get the ball rolling for a care needs assessment. If they need mobility equipment, we can still do all those sorts of things’. (DCC22, interview participant).

When this worked well, it was often because a DCC had cultivated holistic knowledge about, trust in and positive relationships with their local community, which again placed them in a unique position to broker and integrate an individualised approach to care (CMOC 18).

‘I think having knowledge of local services and as good links as possible with all those teams that you’ve just mentioned. Because […] you can’t do everything on your own […] you can’t sort [a] person’s… health problems out, social problems out in isolation. So it’s having good access to local resources’. (DCC8, interview participant).

This was a consistent finding across the qualitative interviews and survey data. Local knowledge enabled DCCs to feel more confident in their service delivery.

‘Understanding more about services available within the area I support [has helped me to deliver support]’. (T2 survey participant).

Importantly, DCCs, HCPs and service users reported that use of good quality services had beneficial health impacts in terms of slowing the progression of the dementia:

‘The activities that they do and they enjoy, i.e. knitting, crocheting, that sort of stuff. Because the whole time that they continue to it, the better it is for the patient’. (HCP07, interview participant).

However, much local variation existed regarding availability and accessibility of what a DCC could refer service users onto. Issues included high costs and fewer GPs/nurses in some areas. In other areas, there were no clubs to refer onto, or services were inaccessible due to poor transport or other local infrastructures (CMOC 19).

‘Now that we’re going into other areas of [location] we’ve not really got anything of our own. So we’ve got no clubs, or activities, we’re relying on other organisations to send people to those sorts of things, so if we could perhaps set up one or two things of our own that would be good’. (DCC10, interview participant).

Additionally, when local services themselves are overstretched, referring service users onto those services might result in them simply being placed on another waiting list (CMOC 20).

‘I did the referral to Crossroads… and there can be a year wait. I know someone who waited an actual year for them. But obviously, it was someone who was coping really well’. (DCC17, interview participant).

When DCCs knew that service users might have to face long waitlists, they did what they could to anticipate these delays and try to ensure that care was still delivered (CMOC 21).

‘And then if there’s anything obvious it’s easy to do straightway, to tell them what’s available, and if it isn’t I can kind of them a bit of a push and say, this might be nice, or perhaps you could talk to this person… […] so I try and encourage them to at least go on their list, so they’re known to them in case they need them in the future’. (DCC10, interview participant).

Some participants reported improved care when DCCs engaged in this form of proactive care:

‘She was asking how things were, and I told her same as before. Nothing’s changed, I’ve not heard nothing from social services. She said she’d chase them up, which she did, because they rung and I’ve got an appointment with them’. (FC07, interview participant).

Thus, service users living in areas where local authority community services are over-stretched or non-existent cannot have their needs met in the way that service users in other areas can. Where DCCs can think ahead and use the connections they have to sidestep waiting lists and lack of availability, this is appreciated by service users (Table 11).

Joined up working

Effective joined up working with local health and social care professionals was reported across our surveys and qualitative data. DCCs connected well with other health professionals including admiral nurses, memory clinics, mental health services, GPs and social services. Effective joined up working included examples of information sharing at multi-disciplinary team meetings (MDTs), proactive referrals and trusting working relationships, where DCCs’ expertise was drawn on to support the person living with dementia and their carer. Joined up working appeared to lead to improved outcomes for service users, including helping them to make appointments, providing support for carers connecting service users to the memory clinic without having to go via a GP (CMOC 22).

‘One to three times in the week it’s [inaudible] around the GP surgery, which I do enjoy, especially the MDT. Again, because we’re all talking to all the different services and trying to plan the best plan of action for that individual’. (DCC38, interview participant).

In this way, DCCs and other HCPs built networks of trust, and bridges between one another, as they shared their expertise and worked reciprocally towards the shared goal of better care for service users. Notably, the benefits were not just for people living with dementia and their carers. HCP participants also reported positive outcomes when joined up working with the DCC service was prioritised, such as a reduction in both clinical and administrative workload as well as the assurance that PLWD and their carers were being better supported.

‘Gathering that knowledge, that collateral information from dementia coordinators is exquisite. […] It gives me an insight into what the functioning of this person has been, what challenges the carer has faced along the way by the time they reach me […]. So I think that is really helpful. And it has also helped me in terms of discharging patients who have mild cognitive impairment back to the GP to create the space for more referrals to come in’. (HCP04, interview participant).

Interview data suggested it might be easier for DCCs to establish these positive working relationships with other HCPs if they already knew them or were well-known in the area. This was supported by a small but non-significant increase in perceptions of joined up working from T1 to T2 of the survey, as reported below. Building relationships if they are not pre-existing is therefore harder but important for collaborative working.

‘I think really we couldn’t do what we do unless we’re talking to everybody else basically. We need everybody else’s input’. (DCC20, interview participant).

There were also some reports of less successful joined up working. When asked about joined up working with GPs and other health professionals at T2, 57% of survey participants agreed or strongly agreed their role feels joined up with GPs, compared to 75% who felt the role was joined up with other health professionals. These figures were a small increase on T1 findings, where 52% felt the role was joined up with GPs and 68% felt it was joined up with other healthcare professionals. These figures, along with the qualitative findings, show that GPs were harder to engage than other healthcare professionals (CMOC 23).

‘Some of my colleagues, they haven’t been given access to their [GP] surgeries, so it’s really kind of impossible if… we understand and we appreciate everyone’s busy at the surgery and that’s why us having access to the surgeries, we can check things that maybe they can’t, because they’re busy. But if you don’t have access, then you can’t get anywhere’. (DCC24, interview participant).

At T2 of the survey, we asked DCCs who had had difficulty engaging with GPs why they thought that was. Replies to this qualitative, open-box question included that GPs often did not communicate well, did not have the physical space to offer DCCs when they went into surgeries and could not smoothly share admin duties. DCCs also suggested that GPs might not fully understand the service or have time to engage with it. For context, many healthcare professionals, including GPs, are working in an over-stretched system, meaning many are time-poor [7, 45].

‘GP practices are full-on. I’m lucky that I’ve moved from a really big practice to a small practice, so I had that little bit more time than your average GP practice. At my previous practice, I probably wouldn’t be doing what I’m doing now [engaging with the DCC service] because it would’ve been too time-consuming’. (HCP07, interview participant).

DCCs also reported that some GPs make inappropriate referrals into the service, that is, referring people who either do not have dementia or those who do not want care (CMOC 23).

‘Some referrals we get can be a bit ambiguous. I think sometimes, when the doctor gets to the point where they’re not sure what to do, they think, we’ll try the dementia coordinators. Like if someone has a series of UTIs you think, investigate those first, because that’s very likely what it is’. (DCC10, interview participant).

We suggest that these less positive joined up working outcomes result from a lack of understanding about what the service can do for HCPs, which, as seen above, includes reducing their workload. When DCCs are faced with barriers to collaboration with HCPs, they may struggle to build the beneficial bridges that they need to deliver timely interventions to support (Table 12).

Discussion

Summary of findings

In answer to the question of what works (and does not work), for whom and under what circumstances, the DCC service works well when DCCs are knowledgable in dementia care, have good communication and people skills, are well-supported and have the resources to be able to prioritise urgent cases. The service works well for people living with dementia and their carers who feel they have continuity of care with a consistent, accessible, trusted point of contact and when it delivers tailored care which can help to support PLWD and reduce carers’ burden. The service works best when it is well-staffed and operates in areas that have high-quality, accessible community support that DCCs can refer on to. DCCs are in a unique position to educate and empower service users, and can have difficult conversations about getting a diagnosis, managing dementia symptoms/behaviours and planning for the future. Importantly, however, factors including rising caseloads and low staffing levels can lead to overwhelm and possible burnout in DCCs. There are also groups for whom the service could better support, including those living alone, and/or with advanced dementia, as well as those from minoritised groups. There are also issues with wider service provision in terms of services that DCCs can refer into, which may still see service users waiting to access care.

This evaluation explored what about a dementia care coordinator service works (and does not work), for whom and under what circumstances, in the largest ICS in England. An intention of the DCC service was to provide proactive care. Whilst DCCs can do this at times, the help they provide is also reactive. Another intention of the DCC service was to increase dementia diagnosis rates. Our evidence suggests that DCCs might be able to do this, through the contact they have with service users pre diagnosis. However, the wider system needs to be able to accommodate increasing users and additional metrics are required to demonstrate this. Furthermore, the DCC service was set up to offer long-term support to people living with dementia and their carers from pre, during and post diagnosis, which includes offering needs assessments and tailored care. DCCs can do these things, but again, rising caseloads and waiting lists mean that the components of the role that DCCs enjoy and receive satisfaction from (e.g. face to face contact) may be compromised. DCCs act as bridge builders, connecting service users to local dementia services. However, whilst DCCs are knowledgeable about local services, they cannot make these connections when services are over-subscribed or do not exist.

There are areas that require attention to ensure service sustainability. Based on the data and analysis presented above, we found emerging evidence that the DCC service may become a victim of its own success. When high caseloads and rising numbers of people being diagnosed with dementia are combined with other issues we found, such as understaffing, low salaries and staff leaving, many DCCs felt overwhelmed. When DCC caseloads felt unmanageable, then DCCs were, and still are, more likely to burn out and/or leave the role regardless of their job satisfaction. As caseloads continue to grow, both the quality of the support that can be offered to service users and DCCs’ job satisfaction may decrease. Furthermore, if local dementia services are inaccessible or poor quality, or if healthcare professionals and other social support workers are too stretched to be able to collaborate with DCCs, the service will suffer.

Comparison to existing research

Emerging evidence regarding dementia care coordinator roles indicates that in some cases, they can improve outcomes for both people living with dementia and their carers [24, 27]. In line with guidelines from NICE and the Alzheimer’s society [12, 13], participants in our study reported that having one consistent point of contact was beneficial. Face-to-face work, such as the care provided by DCCs, has been shown to be important for people living with dementia and their carers [17], and this is something that also provides job satisfaction. As mentioned previously, if the needs of people living with dementia are not met, their symptoms may accelerate [18], highlighting the significance of these dementia services. DCCs need knowledge of and experience with dementia [20, 26] and to be empathic people with good person and communication skills [20, 25, 26]. We found that growing caseloads and understaffing could lead to burnout for DCCs. Similarly, others have suggested that caseload management is essential for high-quality offering from dementia care coordination services [26, 46].

For roles that span boundaries, such as that seen within the DCC service, good communication and people skills are essential so that staff can build sustainable, trusting relationships with various individuals and organisations [42–44]. We identified a range of characteristics that successful DCCs demonstrate, drawing parallels with other studies which describe such individuals as ‘trusted, credible, clear communicators [who] understand the cultures of both communities they serve’ [44]. DCCs’ ability to de-escalate and mitigate crises due to their expertise and the bridges they can build with local organisations is crucial. Contemporary healthcare has many boundaries that need to be crossed and linked holistically to offer the best outcomes [47]. The ability of DCCs to mitigate crises demonstrates their ability to use their knowledge, cross-organisational thinking and people skills [43]. We suggest that this ability may, in part, be due to the trust and communication that DCCs have developed between themselves, service users and other organisations as part of their bridge-building work [42].

In theory, such skills can be utilised to build bridges with harder-to-reach groups such as those with young onset dementia or those living alone so their needs can be met in future. However, when local services, such as those the DCCs might recommend to service users, are insufficient or lacking, this can increase health inequalities for people living with dementia [17]. Additionally, this essential bridge building work is contingent on DCCs having adequate time and resources. We know that UK health and social care systems are over-stretched [7] and that similar factors have led to burnout and attrition in groups such as GPs [45, 48], junior doctors [49, 50] and nurses [51]. Thus, it is unsurprising that DCCs, who are working with similarly reduced resources, face similar outcomes. This is a finding that may also be important to consider in the broader context of social prescribing and burnout risk. These findings, however, help address current calls for further evidence for the efficacy of dementia care coordinator services [20].

Strengths and limitations

The use of a realist approach to evaluate the DCC service can be considered a strength, since it allows for the exploration of nuanced and contextual elements of any given intervention [29, 31]. Furthermore, we collected extensive data from a wide range of participants and analysed that data in a collaborative manner within our team, resulting in robust findings and a set of transferable recommendations (see Table 13). Given the rising numbers of dementia cases and need for care solutions, the applicable nature of our findings is a clear strength.

Recommendations

Despite these strengths, the study also has limitations. The DCC service is delivered by two third-sector organisations. Alongside our evaluation, both providers were collecting their own data, including from service users (i.e. engaging them in focus groups). We speculate that, when it came to our data collection, this may have caused confusion when we approached DCCs, healthcare professionals and service users, given that we were an independent and unknown team. This may have affected our ability to recruit people living with dementia and their carers; ideally, we would have recruited more of these participants. Secondly, this evaluation did not take place in a static environment. We know that during the 2-year period of our evaluation, service providers implemented their own improvements including exit interviews with DCCs who had left the role, recruiting ‘area leads’ who focus on supervising and supporting DCCs, setting up a triage system, organising drop-ins with memory clinics, improved training and peer-support and increasing DCCs’ salary. We do not, however, know the implications of these improvements because they took place at staggered time points throughout and after our data collection. Thirdly, the research was constrained by its limited resources, which prevented the use of interpreters and the inclusion of those who do not have English as their first language. Finally, to ensure participants’ anonymity, our survey was confidential. This meant that although we could see from the consent forms that 17 of the people who filled in T2 had the same names as people who had filled in T1, we could not link people’s answers to specific names. Although this was a strength in some ways as it guaranteed confidentiality and potentially facilitated participation, it was also a limitation as it meant we could not connect individual answers to specific participants and so could only generalise, for example, what answers had changed over time, rather than make specific comparisons. It might also have been beneficial to analyse whether DCCs with less experience (i.e. less than two years) reported different experiences to those with more (i.e. more than 10 years), but the anonymous and qualitative nature of our two analytical methods prevented this.

There is now a need for further research into the relationship between implementation and impact, as noted by others [20]. For example, we speculate that whilst the service may, in some areas, put extra pressure on other areas of the health and social care system due to referrals from DCCs, if these referrals are appropriate, then we hypothesise that this may provide cost savings in the long run. Conclusions

This realist evaluation provides evidence to demonstrate that a DCC service may be a much-needed welcome intervention capable of supporting people living with dementia and their carers pre, during and post-diagnosis, at a time when the care system is fragmented and under strain. In a world of limited resourcing for health and social care, for a service of this nature to be sustainable, attention must now be paid to staffing and local provisions, to enable coordinators to continue doing what they do best—building bridges across services and systems for service users.
