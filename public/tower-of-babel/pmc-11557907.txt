Life-Course Multidisciplinary Psychosocial Predictors of Dementia Among Older Adults: Results From the Health and Retirement Study
Authors: Sayaka Kuwayama, Wassim Tarraf, Kevin A González, Freddie Márquez, Hector M González, Jennifer Tehan Stanley
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectivesIdentifying predictors of dementia may help improve risk assessments, increase awareness for risk reduction, and identify potential targets for interventions. We use a life-course psychosocial multidisciplinary modeling framework to examine leading predictors of dementia incidence.Research Design and MethodsWe use data from the Health and Retirement Study to measure 57 psychosocial factors across 7 different domains: (i) demographics, (ii) childhood experiences, (iii) socioeconomic conditions, (iv) health behaviors, (v) social connections, (vi) psychological characteristics, and (vii) adverse adulthood experiences. Our outcome is dementia incidence (over 8 years) operationalized using Langa–Weir classification for adults aged 65+ years who meet criteria for normal cognition at the baseline when all psychosocial factors are measured (N = 1 784 in training set and N = 1 611 in testing set). We compare the standard statistical method (Logistic regression) with machine learning (ML) method (Random Forest) in identifying predictors across the disciplines of interest.ResultsStandard and ML methods identified predictors that spanned multiple disciplines. The standard statistical methods identified lower education and childhood financial duress as among the leading predictors of dementia incidence. The ML method differed in their identification of predictors.Discussion and ImplicationsThe findings emphasize the importance of upstream risk and protective factors and the long-reaching impact of childhood experiences on cognitive health. The ML approach highlights the importance of life-course multidisciplinary frameworks for improving evidence-based interventions for dementia. Further investigations are needed to identify how complex interactions of life-course factors can be addressed through interventions.Keywords: Cognitive aging, Lifespan models, Machine learning, Random Forest

Translational Significance: Cognitive aging research remains discipline-dependent, and modeling strategies that can point to critical predictors using multidisciplinary life-course approaches are needed to better identify potential intervention points. Our findings indicate that predictors of dementia cover the lifespan and multiple disciplines and illustrate how data-driven and standard statistical techniques can be complementary in identifying the complex risk and protective profiles leading to dementia. We provide a roadmap for future research that can expand on the proposed modeling of life-course multidisciplinary data.

In the United States, the number of individuals ages 65 years and older with Alzheimer’s disease and related dementias (ADRD) was estimated to be 6.5 million in 2022 and is projected to double in the next 3 decades, impacting substantially the patients, caregivers, and healthcare system (1,2). As such, there is a pressing public health need to improve evidence-based interventions by identifying novel modifiable risk and protective factors that may delay or prevent the onset of ADRD.

The landmark Lancet Commission study highlighted the importance of interventions across the life course and reported 12 potentially modifiable risk factors that account for 40% of worldwide dementias (3). However, the report focused mostly on biological and behavioral factors and did not provide insights regarding psychosocial factors, critical in neurocognitive aging. The current study complements findings from the Lancet Commission report by uncovering contributing psychosocial factors.

Efforts to characterize the psychosocial risk and protective factors for ADRD have evolved considerably over the past 2 decades and particularly so since establishing the National Alzheimer’s Project Act in 2011 (4). For instance, we now have ample evidence showing that adverse childhood experiences, lower educational attainment, lower educational quality, as well as disadvantaged socioeconomic conditions, among other factors, are critical contributing risk factors in cognitive aging (5,6). ADRD risks are also shaped by behaviors, including smoking, physical activity, and alcohol consumption patterns (7,8), and psychological traits, such as conscientiousness and a positive outlook on life (9,10). More recently, and more so following the COVID-19 pandemic, epidemiological work has increasingly highlighted social experiences, including loneliness, poor social engagement, and low social support, as major contributing modifiable factors (11,12). These risks, however, do not operate independently and are often intertwined and interact in shaping the natural course that can lead to the development and onset of disease in later stages of life, including neurocognitive diseases.

Yet, much of the current work has focused on small sets of factors within disciplinary boundaries. Siloed work is useful for scientific discovery; however, what is lacking are more wholistic investigations that examine multiple psychosocial factors across the lifespan and point to critical factors that can guide risk identification and aid in constructing risk profiles to guide multipronged interventions. The National Institute on Aging (NIA) has provided a framework for integrative investigations over the life course and across different disciplines that “broadens the scope of the search for malleable targets for interventions” (3). This is pertinent to neurodegenerative disorders due to their multiple etiologies and complex arrays of risks and causes. Therefore, our study aims to identify prominent predictors of dementia by modeling life-course psychosocial factors informed by the NIA framework. Results from this work have the potential to better align existing findings and to highlight critical venues for interventions to best allocate scarce health and healthcare resources.

To do so, we adopt 2 statistical approaches with distinctive analytical strengths, namely standard statistical method (logistic regression) and data-driven supervised machine learning (ML (13); detailed in Methods section) method. Identification of predictors for disease outcomes has been largely implemented through standard statistical methods (eg, to identify factors associated with dementia incidence evaluated by statistical significance). But increasingly, data-driven ML methods have been suggested as alternatives (eg, to identify factors based on contributions to prediction performance) (14–18). While studies comparing the predictors identified by the above 2 methods are limited, the arguments on their respective and distinctive strengths in modeling predictors of health outcomes have been discussed (15,17). Particularly, the advantage of the standard statistical method (logistic regression) is the clear interpretability of linking prespecified individual factors to an outcome. The direction and magnitude of associations with the outcome can be estimated for each of the considered factors (17), which can then be used to highlight differential strengths of associations and potential intervention points. Whereas the advantage of the ML method is its ability to model a large number of factors simultaneously (19), which then allows the assessment of relative predictive contributions of a wide range of factors and the selection of arrays of factors that can guide multipronged interventions (20,21). This advantage further extends when nonlinear ML methods, such as Random Forest (RF; detailed in Methods section), are employed due to their ability to approximate more realistic risk profiles of complex syndromes, such as dementia (14,16). They do so by capturing nonlinear relationships of factors with the outcome (eg, quadratic relationships and interactions between factors) (14–17).

Using the 2 methodological approaches described above, first, we identify predictors that reflect the diverse and potentially interrelated psychosocial features underlying dementia incidence. We expect that these predictors will span multiple disciplines regardless of the modeling methods. Second, we compare the predictors identified from the 2 methods. We expect that the standard statistical method and ML method will identify different sets of predictors due to their distinctive approaches. In discussion, we bridge the evidence generated from these 2 methods and highlight ways to interpret the findings and facilitate translatability of the results. As we argued above, identifying these predictors (or factors) is an essential first step for better stratification of risk and to generate profiles, including ones that incorporate social determinants of health, that can aid in clinical decision-making and downstream personalized medicine that goes beyond the narrow focus on biology.

Research Design and Methods

Data

We used data from participants 65 years and older from the biennial Health and Retirement Study (HRS), a nationally representative study of U.S. adults aged 51 years and over. Detailed descriptions of the HRS and its sampling framework have been published elsewhere (22). We used the 2006 wave as the baseline year to assess predictors of dementia incidence 8 years later (by 2014). Our chosen timeline (8 years) was guided by 2 considerations: (i) average life expectancy following dementia onset (23), and (ii) average age of onset of dementia and the increased risk of dementia incidence after age 70 (24,25). Given these considerations, all individuals in our target subsample would have satisfied the criteria of being above age 70 by 2014 while preserving as many participants as possible in our sample by staying in line with the average life expectancy for non-Hispanic Whites, non-Hispanic Blacks, and Hispanics (the 3 major represented groups in HRS) in the United States. Doing so allows us to reduce loss to death and attrition and increases our sensitivity to capturing younger cases of dementia in all 3 race/ethnicity groups of interest. Additionally, we used data from the 2008 (baseline) and 2016 (dementia assessment) waves to validate the predictive ability of our ML models. Both sets of data were synthesized using the Cross-Wave Tracker File, RAND HRS Fat File, HRS Core data, and Cross-Wave Childhood Health and Family Aggregated Data, all available on the HRS website.

Analytic Samples

Our starting sample was n = 4 711 participants aged 65 years and older who were alive and responded at the baseline wave (2006) and participated in the Psychosocial study (a split half random sample of the HRS; the other half, interviewed in 2008, was used for the validation dataset). Out of these participants, n = 2 872 were alive and responded at the time of dementia assessment (8 years later). We excluded individuals who were not cognitively healthy (Dementia or Cognitive Impaired but Not Dementia using Langa–Weir (LW) classifications; see details below) at the baseline wave (excluded n = 511) and that had missing values for any of the predictors of interest (excluded n = 554) at the baseline wave. Finally, we also excluded n = 23 individuals whose race/ethnicity was “Non-Hispanic Other” due to a small number of observations. Our final analytic sample for the modeling (in the standard statistical approach and training phase of our ML approach; henceforth, training data) was n = 1 784 individuals. We followed the same procedure detailed above using the 2008/2016-wave data and included n = 1 611 in the testing phase of our ML approach (henceforth testing data). A detailed presentation of the inclusion/exclusion criteria is presented in Supplementary Figure 1.

Measures

Outcomes

Current HRS does not include adjudicated dementia diagnosis but provides research classifications for cognitive impairment and dementia determined using researcher-developed algorithms. Such effort includes the LW Classification of Cognitive Function, an algorithm that provides a grouping of participants into 3 categories: (i) Normal Cognition, (ii) Cognitive Impaired but Not Dementia (CIND), and (iii) Dementia (26). Briefly, LW classification is derived using a threshold-based algorithm applied to a summary score of cognition and/or functional limitations. Specifically, the summary score is computed using measures assessing cognition from the core HRS interview for the respondents and proxy measures of cognition and functional limitation for the proxy respondents. The thresholds in the LW algorithm were determined to approximate the prevalence of cognitive impairment and dementia in the Aging, Demographics, and Memory Study (ADAMS), a substudy of the HRS that conducted dementia assessment and thus provides dementia diagnosis for a subset of HRS participants. The detailed descriptions and features of the LW classifications have previously been published (26). Given our focus, we generated a binary variable to distinguish between individuals meeting the criteria for LW dementia versus not (Normal and CIND according to LW classifications).

Psychosocial predictors

Our choice of predictors is guided by constructs indicated through the NIA framework. A similar set of variables spanning similar disciplines has been used recently in a study examining mortality (27). All variables were collected at the 2006 (modeling/training data) and 2008 (testing data) waves, corresponding with the wave in which the respondents completed their psychosocial module. The variables covered 7 disciplines and included 57 individual indicators. Demographic factors included age, gender, race/ethnicity, and nativity; childhood experiences included father’s occupation, paternal and maternal education, father’s unemployment, financial help recipient during childhood, relocation due to financial duress during childhood, and childhood psychosocial adversity; socioeconomic conditions included total household income, total household wealth, job status, job history, education, recent financial difficulty (28), renting status, Medicaid, neighborhood disorder (29), and neighborhood cohesion (29); health behaviors included alcohol consumption pattern, current smoking status, history of smoking, moderate activity, vigorous activity, and sleep problem. Social connections included marital status, positive and negative social interactions with children, family, and friends (30,31). Psychological characteristics included big 5 personality traits (agreeableness, conscientiousness, extraversion, neuroticism, and openness) (32), anger expression (anger-in, anger-out) (33), cynical hostility (34,35), hopelessness (36,37), loneliness (38–40), life satisfaction (41,42), optimism (43), pessimism (43), positive and negative affect (44–47), purposefulness (48,49), religiosity (50), perceived constraints and mastery (51,52), and anxiety (53,54). Finally, adverse adulthood experiences included traumatic events (eg, death of a child) (55), daily discrimination (56), and major discrimination (56). A detailed explanation of how these factors were processed and coded is presented in Supplementary Material Section 2.

Data Preprocessing

Our data presented a significant difference in the number of observations between the 2 categories of the outcome (ie, dementia incidence was approximately 6%). This imbalance is widely acknowledged in ML modeling techniques and is suspected to lead to biased estimates (57). To mitigate the negative impact of the imbalanced data, we used, in line with existing recommendations and practice, a random oversampling technique from the R package ROSE that increased the number of “caseness” (dementia incidence) instances in the training data. Briefly, this step involves randomly selecting examples from the minority (dementia incidence) class and replicating them to generate synthetic samples (58). The resulting training data contained 50% of instances belonging to dementia incidence and no dementia incidence group, respectively.

In using the standard statistical method to identify predictors of dementia incidence, we constructed a series of logistic regression models for our binary outcome (ie, dementia incidence). Specifically, we first fit 4 independent logistic models using each of the demographic factors as the exposure to estimate their odds ratios (ORs). Next, in line with the traditional approach to modeling exposures, we focused on each of the risk factors independently. Therefore, we fit an additional 53 independent logistic regressions using each of the factors as the exposure while adjusting for the demographic characteristics. After obtaining the ORs for all factors of interest, we used the R package forestploter to illustrate the ORs as well as the 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the significant predictors to allow visual interpretations of findings (Figure 1). The predictors of dementia incidence in the context of the standard statistical method will be interpreted as having significant (p < .05) OR estimates.

The decisions on the reference group of the categorical variables affect the labeling of a factor as protection or risk; however, the magnitude of associations, though in the opposite direction, are largely preserved, and inferences are independent of these decisions.

Next, we used the ML method to identify predictors of dementia incidence. Briefly, the process involves 3 steps: (i) train the ML model using the training data, (ii) validate the ML model’s generalizability to unseen data (testing data), and (iii) evaluate factors’ predictive contributions using the model trained and validated in the previous 2 steps. Accordingly, we first trained the ML model to learn the relationships between all factors of interest and the outcome (ie, fit the model) using the training data. To implement this step, we used the RF classifier from the R package Tidymodels. RF is an ensemble method that utilizes many decision trees (59), which are a type of algorithm that derives sets of split conditions that classify observations into different classes of the outcome (eg, dementia vs no dementia) (60). To adjust the hyperparameters (ie, model options) to achieve the highest performance possible, we used the R package Tune and implemented cross-validation. Briefly, this method simulates training-testing datasets within the training data, which allows estimations of the models’ performance with different selections of hyperparameters on “unseen” data. The model with the selected hyperparameters was then tested for its generalizability to unseen data using the testing dataset. As a metric of the model performance, we used the Area Under the Receiver-Operating Characteristic Curve (AUC). The Receiver Operation Characteristic (ROC) curve is a plot of true positive rate against false positive rate across all possible classification thresholds (61). AUC is the area under the ROC curve and can be interpreted as a measure of how well a model can discriminate between classes (eg, dementia vs no dementia) (62). Finally, using the RF model constructed above, we identified the predictors of dementia incidence via permutation variable importance and visualized in Figure 2 using the R package vip. Permutation variable importance is a technique that allows us to evaluate the contribution of factors in predicting the outcome by computing the performance decline in AUC when the values of each factor are permuted (ie, shuffled thus disrupting the relationship between the factor and outcome) (63). The larger loss in the AUC denotes a higher predictive contribution of the factors, while no change or increase in the AUC indicates that the factor being evaluated does not contribute to predicting the outcome. The computed declines (as well as no change or increases) in the AUC were then used to assign each factor the importance score. For example, the importance score of 0.05 was given to the factor with a decline of 0.05 in the AUC (ie, inverse). Thus, predictors in the context of the ML method are identified as having positive importance scores, where higher scores indicate the larger contribution of a factor in predicting dementia incidence.

Predictors of 8-year dementia incidence using Random Forest (RF). All factors were modeled simultaneously in the RF model. Permutation importance score was calculated using R vip package on the testing (2008/2016 waves) data. Lastly, we compared the identified predictors of dementia incidence between the standard statistical method (ie, factors having significant ORs) and the ML method (ie, factors having positive permutation importance scores).

Results

The characteristics of our modeling sample (n = 1 784) are shown in Table 1. The mean age was 72.0 ± 5.6, 61% were female, nearly half of the analytic sample had education of some college and above, and 94% were born in the United States. Those meeting criteria for LW dementia were older (76.1 ± 6.3), had a higher proportion of Hispanic and non-Hispanic Black adults, and had a lower proportion of having high educational attainment (ie, some college and above) relative to those that did not meet criteria for LW dementia. Additional distributional characteristics are available in Supplementary Table 1.

Predictors identified using the logistic regressions (ie, standard statistical method) are presented in Figure 1, along with their corresponding ORs and 95% CIs. Out of 57 variables, 42 variables spanning all disciplines considered were significantly associated with higher or lower odds of dementia incidence. Adjusting for demographic factors, influential risk factors of dementia incidence (ie, according to ORs) included lower occupational prestige of father’s occupation (Manual/operator/military/other; reference group = Managerial/Professional), lower education (Less than high school, High school; reference group = College and above), lower job status prestige (Not working; reference group = Farming/construction/maintenance/production/transportation/military), receipt of financial help during childhood, and renting (Renting; reference group = Other). Similarly, influential protective factors against dementia incidence included lower recent financial difficulty (Very, Not very, Not at all, Somewhat; reference group = Completely), higher job status prestige (Management/business/financial/professional; reference group = Farming/construction/maintenance/production/transportation/military), consistent job history (Worked 1+ job for 5+ years, Worked but no 5+ years; reference group = Never/do not know), and higher maternal and paternal education (Mother’s education: 13+; Father’s education: 13+; reference group = <12).

Using the adopted RF model (ie, ML approach; AUC = 0.74), predictors of dementia incidence (Figure 2) spanned all considered disciplines. Notably, influential predictors (according to the permutation importance scores) included psychological characteristics (positive affect, anxiety, anger-in, negative affect, pessimism), socioeconomic conditions (job status, neighborhood disorder), social connections (negative relationship with family, positive relationship with friends), and adverse adulthood experiences (major discrimination).

Finally, we compared the predictors identified by RF and the factors significantly associated with dementia incidence using the standard statistical method (Supplementary Table 2). In summary, 25 variables were identified in common, spanning all disciplines of interest. Of note, 11 factors (eg, anger-in) were additionally identified as contributing factors by RF, whereas 17 factors were significantly associated with dementia incidence by the standard statistical method (eg, recipient of financial help recipient during childhood, education, recent financial difficulty) were not contributing predictors in RF. The lists of factors corresponding to these results are presented in Supplementary Table 2.

Discussion and Implications

This study investigated psychosocial predictors of dementia incidence using a life-course multidisciplinary framework applied to nationally representative longitudinal data. A total of 57 factors (modeled independently with the standard approach and simultaneously with the ML approach) were considered as potential predictors for 8-year dementia incidence. In line with our first objective, we corroborated that predictors of dementia incidence span multiple disciplines regardless of the modeling approach. We also showed, in accordance with the second objective, that the ML method and the standard statistical method identify different sets of predictors with some overlap, signaling different opportunities for interventions with respect to psychosocial factors. A clear thread from the resulting evidence is the importance of using a life-course multidisciplinary framework and the potential need to consider both the standard statistical and ML methods for a comprehensive assessment of the risk for dementia incidence.

It is critical to identify predictors to inform actions that lead to modifications and to better inform resource allocations for effective interventions. The fundamental directives of the Lancet report focus on research on health and behavioral risk factors that explain about 40% of dementia risk, which is a laudable first step. Yet, what is omitted from such focus is that nearly three-fifths of the risk for dementia remains unaccounted for, and the component factors of that risk can complicate health and behavioral issues by acting synergistically to augment the risk for disease. The NIA framework, while focused on understanding disparities in risk for disease, provides a multidimensional framework for thinking about risk in a more holistic way. Our intent was to widen the focus to factors that reach beyond the biobehavioral focus of the Lancet report by focusing on multidisciplinary psychosocial factors embedded within the comprehensive NIA framework with the aim to identify potentially targetable factors.

The factors identified by the standard statistical method expressed through risks or protections, included demographic such as age and sex; childhood experiences (eg, financial insecurity in childhood, maternal, and paternal education); socioeconomic conditions (eg, educational attainment, occupational status), and health behaviors (eg, moderate activity). A range of psychological characteristics (eg, pessimism, positive affect), social connections (eg, positive/negative relationship with family) as well as adverse adulthood experiences (major discrimination) were also identified. The ML method also revealed factors across the spectrum of considered disciplines as predictors of dementia incidence. These predictors included demographic indicators: age and nativity; childhood experiences: father’s education; socioeconomic conditions: job status, neighborhood disorder; health behaviors: moderate activity, current smoker; social connections: positive relationship with friends and children; psychological characteristics: positive affect, anxiety; and adverse adulthood experiences: major and daily discrimination.

These results affirm that predictors of dementia span a multidisciplinary continuum, regardless of the modeling approach (ie, standard statistical method and ML method). In line with the NIA framework and its expanded focus, this shows the complex psychosocial roots of the pathways that lead to dementia. The complex origins of dementia have been discussed in existing literature (3). However, our work adds to this body of scientific evidence by showing that modeling frameworks that do not allow evaluation of such complexity may yield limited information and potentially misidentify targets for modifications. Unraveling how different exposures across the life-course influence, or interact over time to influence, cognitive aging is an ongoing research priority and part of the goals set by the National Plan to Address Alzheimer’s Disease (64). Findings from this study help advance these efforts by showcasing the use of multimodular data, such as HRS, despite its limitations, that allow for deep life-course risk characterization. Yet, continued efforts for data harmonization and deeper characterization of the biopsychosocial exposome over the life course are needed.

Second, we expected that we would observe differences in identified predictors across the adopted statistical approaches (standard statistical method vs ML method). Our results indicate that there were both overlaps and notable differences across these approaches with considerable implications for interpretation. For example, several factors identified by the standard statistical method (logistic regressions) had low to no importance in the RF model. Notably, all early life and early adulthood risk factors had no or low predictive utility in RF, including father’s occupation, and receipt of financial help during childhood as well as participant’s educational attainment. Factors including health behaviors (current smoker) and social connections (marital status, positive relationship with friends and children) were not identified as significant with logistic regressions but were predictive in the ML context.

Importantly, the leading predictors identified through the standard statistical method emphasized (based on magnitude of ORs; strength of associations) the importance of childhood experiences and adulthood socioeconomic conditions. The ML method (based on the permutation importance scores) placed a stronger emphasis on psychological characteristics, social connections, and adverse adulthood experiences but lower emphasis on childhood experiences. Accordingly, from a substantive perspective, our results regarding the leading predictors from both methods identify targets for early life interventions focused on childhood experiences and later life safety net measures that can help maintain socioeconomic well-being. They also point to the critical role of buffering against adverse adulthood experiences (eg, through racial/ethnic identity development, social support seeking, and anger expression and suppression (65)), the significance of promoting positive (eg, positive affect), and alleviating negative (eg, anxiety) psychological traits, and the increasingly recognized importance of promoting later life social connection for healthy cognitive aging.

So, what are the implications of such findings? Unsurprisingly, when investigating predictors of dementia incidence using the multidisciplinary framework, the standard statistical method and ML method led to different insights for such interventions. Yet, the insights from both, as suggested by the literature (15,17,20,21), may be important. On the one hand, the strength of the standard statistical method is the ability to quantify the effect of a unique risk factor on the outcome, which allows us to make statements such as “odds of dementia incidence increase by 2.06 for the participants whose education is less than high school compared to those that went to college and above.” The results from such models are useful in informing actions specific to a hypothesized risk; however, the models require assumptions including linear associations of a specific exposure and outcome (17), and the estimated associations do not account for other factors that potentially interact with the factor being evaluated. Additionally, the standard techniques while theoretically suitable for adjusting for bias due to confounding can potentially lead to biased estimates if the covariates are inappropriately chosen and if the model does not reflect the underlying relationship of considered variables. Including all the exposures in one model is also prohibitive due to the potential for multicollinearity and the complexity associated with interpretation. Epidemiologists suggest using causal techniques (eg, Directed Acyclic Graphs) to inform modeling risk leading to less biased results, but the focus in such cases is always on establishing causality with respect to a defined exposure; single cause focus can be fundamentally reductive especially when dealing with complex psychosocial antecedents of disease. Therefore, the standard statistical method can potentially miss some factors (eg, traumatic events identified by RF) in more complex contexts (ie, when interacted or modeled nonlinearly) and may label them as not important, as indicated by our results. While this is acceptable when you have clear hypotheses and evidence to contextualize the associations within a prespecified set of variables or when you are conducting causal modeling, it might lead to imprecise information and misdirected recommendations for interventions. This is a critical shortcoming in assessing the predictors through a life-course multidisciplinary framework for complex syndromes such as dementia.

The ML method (eg, RF) complements and helps address some of these shortcomings due to its focus on maximizing the predictive capacity of a model and does so by capturing complex relationships among a wide range of factors (14–17) and allows us to make statements such as “positive affect is one of the most influential predictors of dementia incidence after taking nonlinear relationships among factors into consideration.” Assessing risk using such models has a clear advantage in extracting predictors that may be overrated or undermined when the risks are individually assessed through the standard statistical method (14–17). However, drawing inferences solely from the ML method (eg, concluding that education is not important in reducing risks for future abnormal cognitive aging, as indicated by RF) may also lead to incorrect recommendations and misguided interventions.

The user, as such, must deploy each model within the constraints of what it can achieve given its assumptions and limitations. It is possible that, by not employing the insights generated from both approaches, novel recommendations into predictors can be overlooked. The implications for public health can be substantial. If one is to only focus on the standard statistical method then the decision might be to center the focus on early life interventions (eg, aimed at reducing childhood socioeconomic disparities and promoting educational attainment) as foundational to set the course for productive and healthy aging, including cognitive aging. This is a reasonable course of action given the large amount of research substantiating the critical role of upstream factors in setting the developmental course for successful aging, including healthy cognitive aging. The RF method on the other hand offers an alternative interpretation that suggests that these issues are less critical than what their implications and their developmental sequala might be. As such, focusing on contemporaneous psychosocial areas (eg, psychological characteristics, social connections) that appear to be influential in facilitating healthy cognitive aging when modified might yield more proximate benefits and should therefore provide more tangible results in the lives of aging individuals.

Our results don’t yield clear recommendations on which method to use but rather a cautionary note on the complexity of psychosocial risks in disease pathways independent of modeling choice. While we were initially focused on the advantages of employing the 2 methods, our results have shown that the choice of the modeling approach can have critical implications for risk identification and translational guidance and that it may be crucial to employ both the standard and ML methods to allow comprehensive evaluation of risks and provide insights into critical intervention points.

Strength and Limitations

This study should be evaluated in the context of several strengths and weaknesses. Three primary strengths are noted. First, we used a life-course psychosocial multidisciplinary framework in identifying influential predictors of dementia. Our study showcased the benefit of employing this framework by identifying the predictors of dementia incidence that spanned life course and were multidisciplinary. Second, our use of ML method differs from many other studies that have employed ML as a prediction tool in clinical settings. Many of these studies do not attempt to identify predictors since the focus is on enhancing predictive performance. Others assess the predictors, however, only to increase the clinicians’ confidence in using the predictive tool or to confirm the validity of the prediction tool by comparing against existing knowledge (16). However, potential use case of identified predictors in interventions have been suggested (14,20,21). We suggest that the evaluation of predictors from ML can be a tool to aid in substantive interpretation. Third, we advanced current literature by using both standard statistical (logistic regression) and ML (RF) methods in evaluating predictors of dementia and showed that ML method and standard statistical method can complement each other in identifying and better understanding the contributions of complex risk and protective profiles to cognitive aging outcomes. It is also important to note that, despite the consensus view in the literature on the importance of comparing the 2 methods, studies that attempted these comparisons often employed logistic regressions modeling all factors of interest simultaneously and derived predictors using ML techniques such as permutation variable importance. This use of logistic regression is arguably an ML approach, disregarding the modeling assumptions in the standard statistics with limitations to the interpretability of relationships of factors with the outcome. Thus, the implementations of comparison using the traditional definition of standard statistical method (adopting the single exposure focus), capitalizing on its advantages, are sparse in the existing literature. In aggregate, employing both standard statistical and ML methods in the life-course multidisciplinary framework enabled us to leverage multiple psychosocial factors en masse and detect and identify key predictors in a large array of factors. This approach can be applied to other complex datasets with biomarker and clinical information (eg, health records) to provide better scientific coherence to understanding and mitigating intractable health outcomes.

This study has several limitations. First, the analytic sample included in our work was small. The exclusion criteria leading to our analytic samples largely resulted from our approach to focus on a subset population of interest (ie, adults 65 years and older with healthy cognition at baseline whose psychosocial data were available). To minimize the negative influence of having a small sample, we capitalized on the longitudinal structure of the HRS and validated our model performance using training and testing datasets. The reduction in sample size could be improved by data imputation for a portion of the exclusion that resulted from having missing values in considered predictors (n= 554 in training, n= 546 in testing data). Future work should explore the impact of incorporating data imputation, particularly in RF methods. Second, we relied on a research threshold-based operationalization of dementia which may be less precise than clinical adjudicated diagnoses. Moreover, previous study has shown that the performance of the research-based dementia classification is disproportionally worse for subgroups such as racial/ethnic minorities, older adults, and less-educated participants. Still, our dementia outcome has been validated and extensively used in literature (26). Alternatives include research regression-based operationalizations of dementia such as by Crimmins, Kim, Langa and Weir (26); Hurd, Martorell, Delavande, Mullen and Langa (66); and Gianattasio, Wu, Glymour, and Power (67). We also focused on an 8-year discrete window for assessing dementia incidence and the exact time of onset and diagnosis was not available. Future work should validate these findings in the context of shorter as well as longer assessment ranges and consider the exact onset of dementia diagnoses to extend our current findings. Third, our study focused on psychosocial factors, and biological factors were not considered. Additional work should build on our models and findings by incorporating a larger risk and protective factors pool informed by an expanded biopsychosocial framework. Fourth, we relied on the permutation method to assess predictor importance which could undervalue the importance of individual predictors in the presence of correlated measures. Variance inflation factor (VIF) analysis showed no strong evidence of multicollinearity, and standard correlation analysis for continuous variables showed only moderate correlation across variables. While our investigation of correlations using the VIF analysis did not show any strong correlations, results from the permutation importance methods are not conclusive and need further investigations. An alternative approach to tackle this issue by accounting for factors’ correlations has been discussed (68). Fifth, the current study focused on a narrow selection of ML techniques. Other approaches (eg, support vector machines, gradient boosting, and neural networks) should also be investigated (69). Additionally, given the limited scope of the work, we did not attempt to further investigate specific nonlinear relationships and interactions between factors. These suggested relationships should be further assessed (eg, through partial dependence plots) (70). Sixth, we did not test a logistic regression model (standard statistical method) that accounts for all 57 factors simultaneously. Notably, in a sensitivity analysis in the ML context that used a generalized linear model LASSO (for the binary dementia outcome), including all 57 factors, we had worse predictive performance compared to the adopted RF model. Seventh, we used terminology such as “predictive” factors in line with standard use in ML methods and to reflect the temporal arrangement of the variables in the domains of interest relative to the outcome. However, readers are cautioned against causal interpretation as more work is required to validate our results in the context of other classification methods for dementia as well as other model constructions and inputs. Lastly, the HRS has a complex design and requires the inclusion of probability weights to allow for correct representation of the target population (22); this work did not account for the complex design of the HRS as there are no clear methodological guidelines to account for these designs using ML approaches.

Conclusion

We used the standard statistical and ML methods to model life-course multidisciplinary psychosocial data from the HRS and identified influential and targetable predictors of dementia incidence in adults 65 years and older. Our results (i) emphasize the complex psychosocial etiologies of dementia as well as the importance of a life-course multidisciplinary framework for understanding dementia risk and (ii) highlight the importance of assessing predictors using both standard statistical and ML methods to enhance the interpretability of risk and protective factors for dementia and identify target interventions.
