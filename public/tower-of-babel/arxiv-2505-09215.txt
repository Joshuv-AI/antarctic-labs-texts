Optimum and Adaptive Complex-Valued Bilinear Filters
Bernhard Plaimer, Matthias Wagner, Oliver Lang, and Mario Huemer

Abstract
The identification of nonlinear systems is a frequent task in digital signal processing. Such nonlinear systems may be grouped into many sub-classes, whereby numerous nonlinear real-world systems can be approximated as bilinear models. Therefore, various optimum and adaptive bilinear filters have been introduced in recent years. Moreover, in many applications such as communications and radar, complex-valued bilinear systems in combination with complex-valued signals may occur. Hence, in this work, we investigate the extension of real-valued bilinear filters to complex-valued bilinear filters. First, we derive complex-valued bilinear filters by applying two or four real-valued bilinear filters, and compare them with respect to their computational complexity and performance. Second, we introduce novel fully complex-valued bilinear filters, such as the cbwf3 ( cbwf3 ), the cbls3 ( cbls3 ) filter, the cblms3 ( cblms3 ) filter, the cbnlms3 ( cbnlms3 ) filter, and the cbrls3 ( cbrls3 ) filter. Finally, these filters are applied to identify complex-valued miso ( miso ) systems and complex-valued Hammerstein models.

The cost function for the

\displaystyle J_{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda^{k-i}\bar{e}_{i}\bar{e}_{i}^{*}

In this section, we identify a communication transmitter impairment followed by a linear channel. In the complex-valued baseband, this cascade can be modeled as a complex-valued Hammerstein system: a memoryless front-end followed by a linear time-invariant channel, as shown in
Figure 11
.

Figure 11:
Block diagram of a communication transmitter in combination with a linear channel.

In a typical transmitter, bits are mapped to complex-valued symbols, shaped by a pulse-shaping filter, upconverted by an
iq
modulator, amplified by a
pa
, and then transmitted over the channel. In the simplified example considered here, both the pulse shaper and the
pa
are modeled as linear. The only memoryless distortion arises from
iq
imbalance in the modulator, which maps the complex-valued baseband symbols to

\displaystyle u_{k}=g_{1}x_{k}+g_{2}x_{k}^{*}

(132)

with
g_{1}=\frac{1+g_{\text{T}}e^{-\text{j}\phi_{\text{T}}}}{2}\in\mathbb{C}
and
g_{2}=\frac{1-g_{\text{T}}e^{\text{j}\phi_{\text{T}}}}{2}\in\mathbb{C}

Valkama et al. [2001]
,
Tockner et al. [2024]
. The amplitude imbalance factor is denoted by
g_{\text{T}}\in\mathbb{R}
, whereas
\phi_{\text{T}}\in\mathbb{R}
represents the phase imbalance. Now, this signal is convolved with the impulse response of the channel, which is modeled as a complex-valued
fir
(
fir
) filter
\mathbf{h}\in\mathbb{C}^{L}
. The output signal is

\displaystyle y_{k}=\mathbf{h}^{H}\begin{bmatrix}x_{k}&x_{k}^{*}\\
\vdots&\vdots\\
x_{k-L+1}&x_{k-L+1}^{*}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}g_{1}\\
g_{2}\end{bmatrix}=\mathbf{h}^{H}\mathbf{X}_{k}\mathbf{g}\text{.}

(133)

From (
133
) it can be seen, that fully complex-valued bilinear filters are suitable structures for identifying such a system.
On the other hand, because in general (
133
) cannot be represented with a
2\mathbb{R}
bilinear filter or a
4\mathbb{R}
bilinear filter, the identification with a
2\mathbb{R}
bilinear filter or a
4\mathbb{R}
bilinear filter is expected to perform worse. The channel impulse response
\mathbf{h}
with the length
L=64
shall model a multipath propagation environment and has been chosen similar as in
Hofbauer [2016]
. The amplitude imbalance and the phase imbalance were set to
g_{\text{T}}=1.15
and
\phi_{\text{T}}=\frac{\pi}{18}
, respectively. For the
cbnlms
filter,
\alpha_{\mathbf{g}}=\alpha_{\mathbf{h}}=0.5
and
\delta_{\mathbf{g}}=\delta_{\mathbf{h}}=10^{-4}
, were chosen. Furthermore, for the
4rbnlms
filter the parameters were set to
\alpha_{\mathbf{h}_{i}}=\alpha_{\mathbf{g}_{i}}=0.0001
and
\delta_{\mathbf{h}_{i}}=\delta_{\mathbf{g}_{i}}=\delta_{\mathbf{h}}
, for
i=1,\dots,4
. For the
2rbnlms
filter
\alpha_{\mathbf{g}_{\operatorname{Re}}}=\alpha_{\mathbf{g}_{\operatorname{Im}}}=\alpha_{\mathbf{h}_{\operatorname{Re}}}=\alpha_{\mathbf{h}_{\operatorname{Im}}}=0.0017
and
\delta_{\mathbf{g}_{\operatorname{Re}}}=\delta_{\mathbf{g}_{\operatorname{Im}}}=\delta_{\mathbf{h}_{\operatorname{Re}}}=\delta_{\mathbf{h}_{\operatorname{Im}}}=\delta_{\mathbf{h}}
were chosen. The parameters for the
cbrls
filter were set to
\lambda=0.95
,
\mathbf{P}_{\mathbf{h},0}=10\mathbf{I}^{M\times M}
, and
\mathbf{P}_{\mathbf{g},0}=10\mathbf{I}^{L\times L}
. Because the Hammerstein system in this example can also be identified with standard complex-valued adaptive filters, we use the complex Volterra series in
Crespo-Cadenas et al. [2017]
to implement an
nlms
-based Volterra filter, which serves as the complex-valued nonlinear baseline. The parameters for both, the
cbrls
filter and the complex-valued Volterra filter were chosen such that they achieve approximately the same steady-state performance as the
cbnlms
filter. The parameters for the
2rbnlms
and the
4rbnlms
were chosen to achieve a similar speed of convergence. For the input as well as for the noise signal, proper complex-valued Gaussian distributed noise with
x_{k}\sim\mathcal{CN}\left(0,1\right)
and
n_{k}\sim\mathcal{CN}\left(0,0.0001^{2}\right)
, respectively, was chosen.
The
ise
results of this simulation are shown in
Figure 12
. Using either a
2\mathbb{R}
bilinear filter or
4\mathbb{R}
bilinear filter yields a high
ise
, while the other three filters achieve substantially lower
ise
with comparable steady-state performance. As expected, the
cbrls
filter is the fastest. Notably, the
cbnlms
filter is faster than the complex-valued Volterra filter, likely due to its smaller number of parameters to estimate.

CONCLUSION

In this work, we introduced several novel complex-valued bilinear filters for the application in modeling and identifying complex-valued bilinear systems. First, complex-valued
2\mathbb{R}
and
4\mathbb{R}
bilinear filters were discussed. Next, several fully complex-valued bilinear filters, including the
cbwf
, the
cbls
filter, the
cblms
filter, the
cbnlms
filter, and the
cbrls
filter were introduced. In addition, the update equations for mixed complex-valued-real-valued bilinear filters were presented. Furthermore, for the
wf
and the
lms
-based filters, investigations on the convergence behavior were conducted. Those filters were used to identify complex-valued

Appendix A
Proof of convergence for the
cbwf

Assuming statistical independent and identically distributed columns
\mathbf{x}_{i,k}
of
\mathbf{X}_{k}
, the filter will converge after one iteration step, which is proven in the following. Utilizing this assumption the covariance matrix
\mathbf{R}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}}
can be written as a block-diagonal matrix

\displaystyle\mathbf{R}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}}=\mathbf{I}^{M\times M}\otimes\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}\text{,}

(134)

where
\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}=\operatorname{E}\left[\mathbf{x}_{i,k}\mathbf{x}_{i,k}^{H}\right]\in\mathbb{C}^{L\times L}
.
Inserting (
134
) into (
50
) yields

\displaystyle\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{g},n-1}=\sum_{m=1}^{M}|\hat{g}_{n-1,m}|^{2}\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}\text{,}

(135)

with
\hat{g}_{n-1,m}
as the
m
th element of
\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{n-1}
.
Inserting (
7
) into the definition of
\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{X}y}
leads to

\displaystyle\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{X}y}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{mat}_{M}\left[\mathbf{R}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}}\mathbf{f}^{*}\right]

(136)

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}g_{1}^{*}\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}\mathbf{h}&\cdots&g_{M}^{*}\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}\mathbf{h}\end{bmatrix}\text{,}

(137)

with
g_{m}
as the
m
th element of
\mathbf{g}
.
By inserting (
135
) and (
137
) into (
48
), one obtains

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{h}}_{n}=

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{i=1}^{M}|\hat{g}_{n-1,i}|^{2}\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}\right)^{-1}

(138)

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{i=1}^{M}\hat{g}_{n-1,i}g_{i}^{*}\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}\right)\mathbf{h}\text{.}

This can be simplified to

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{h}}_{n}=\frac{\mathbf{g}^{H}\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{n-1}}{\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{n-1}^{H}\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{n-1}}\mathbf{h}\text{.}

(139)

As next step we use
\hat{\mathbf{h}}_{n}
to calculate

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{n}=\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{h},n}^{-1}\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{X}y}^{H}\hat{\mathbf{h}}_{n}\text{.}

(140)

Inserting (
134
) into (
51
) yields the diagonal matrix

\displaystyle\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{h},n}=\hat{\mathbf{h}}_{n}^{T}\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}^{*}\hat{\mathbf{h}}_{n}^{*}\mathbf{I}^{M\times M}\text{.}

(141)

With (
137
) and (
141
) the estimate
\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{n}
follows to

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{n}=\frac{\mathbf{h}^{H}\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}\hat{\mathbf{h}}_{n}}{\hat{\mathbf{h}}_{n}^{H}\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}\hat{\mathbf{h}}_{n}}\mathbf{g}\text{.}

(142)

Inserting (
139
) leads to

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{n}=\frac{\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{n-1}^{H}\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{n-1}}{\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{n-1}^{H}\mathbf{g}}\mathbf{g}\text{.}

(143)

From (
139
) one can see that

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{h}}_{1}=\underbrace{\frac{\mathbf{g}^{H}\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{0}}{\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{0}^{H}\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{0}}}_{\nu}\mathbf{h}

(144)

is already a scaled version of the true but unknown vector
\mathbf{h}
. Moreover, since it is only possible to estimate
\mathbf{h}
up to a complex-valued scalar
\nu
, this is already the best estimate possible. Furthermore, the first estimate

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{1}=\frac{1}{\nu^{*}}\mathbf{g}

(145)

is also already a scaled version of
\mathbf{g}
. Consequently, the Kronecker product produces the true corresponding linear coefficient vector:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{1}\otimes\hat{\mathbf{h}}_{1}^{*}=\mathbf{f}\text{.}

(146)

The proof continues by showing that the sequences do not change for
n>1
, i.e.,

\hat{\mathbf{h}}_{n}=\hat{\mathbf{h}}_{1}

and

\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{n}=\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{1}

for
n\geq 1
.
From (
139
), it is easy to see that if
\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{n-1}=\frac{1}{\nu^{*}}\mathbf{g}
, then

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{h}}_{n}=\nu\mathbf{h}\text{.}

(147)

Similarly, from (
143
) one can see that

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{n}=\frac{1}{\nu^{*}}\mathbf{g}\text{.}

(148)

Hence, convergence is reached after one iteration step.

Note that if
\mathbf{R}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}}
does not fulfill (
134
),
\hat{\mathbf{h}}_{1}
and
\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{1}
would not just be scaled versions of
\mathbf{h}
and
\mathbf{g}
. Because of this, in the general case, the
cbwf
does not converge after one iteration step.
Similarly, if the statistical properties
\mathbf{R}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}}
or
\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{X}y}
are not known and therefore have to be estimated, convergence in one iteration step is not possible.

Appendix B
Proof of convergence for the
cblms
filter

In the following, similarly as in the real-valued case
Ciochina et al. [2017]
and assuming (
134
) with
\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}=\sigma_{x}^{2}\mathbf{I}^{L\times L}
, step-size boundaries will be derived in order to guarantee convergence. Therefore, with a scalar
\nu\in\mathbb{C}
, the error vectors

\displaystyle\Delta\mathbf{h}_{k}=\nu\mathbf{h}-\hat{\mathbf{h}}_{k}

(149)

and

\displaystyle\Delta\mathbf{g}_{k}=\frac{1}{\nu^{*}}\mathbf{g}-\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{k}\text{,}

(150)

are introduced. These errors show the difference between the current estimates
\hat{\mathbf{h}}_{k}
and
\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{k}
and scaled versions of the true vectors
\mathbf{h}
and
\mathbf{g}
, respectively. Inserting (
68
) into (
149
) yields

\displaystyle\Delta\mathbf{h}_{k}=\Delta\mathbf{h}_{k-1}-\mu_{\mathbf{h}}e_{k}^{*}\mathbf{X}_{k}\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{k-1}\text{.}

(151)

Calculating the second-order moment of
||\Delta\mathbf{h}_{k}||
results in

\displaystyle\operatorname{E}\left[||\Delta\mathbf{h}_{k}||_{2}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{E}\left[||\Delta\mathbf{h}_{k-1}||_{2}^{2}\right]-

(152)

\displaystyle\quad\;\,2\mu_{\mathbf{h}}\operatorname{E}\left[\operatorname{Re}\left[e_{k}\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{k-1}^{H}\mathbf{X}_{k}^{H}\Delta\mathbf{h}_{k-1}\right]\right]+

\displaystyle\quad\;\,\mu_{\mathbf{h}}^{2}\operatorname{E}\left[|e_{k}|^{2}\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{k-1}^{H}\mathbf{X}_{k}^{H}\mathbf{X}_{k}\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{k-1}\right]\text{.}

For a more compact notation

\displaystyle a_{\mathbf{h},k}=\operatorname{E}\left[\operatorname{Re}\left[e_{k}\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{k-1}^{H}\mathbf{X}_{k}^{H}\Delta\mathbf{h}_{k-1}\right]\right]

(153)

and

\displaystyle b_{\mathbf{h},k}=\operatorname{E}\left[|e_{k}|^{2}\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{k-1}^{H}\mathbf{X}_{k}^{H}\mathbf{X}_{k}\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{k-1}\right]

(154)

are introduced, which simplifies (
152
) to

\displaystyle\operatorname{E}\left[||\Delta\mathbf{h}_{k}||_{2}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{E}\left[||\Delta\mathbf{h}_{k-1}||_{2}^{2}\right]-

(155)

\displaystyle\quad\;\,2\mu_{\mathbf{h}}a_{\mathbf{h},k}+\mu_{\mathbf{h}}^{2}b_{\mathbf{h},k}\text{.}

By inserting (
62
) and (
3
) into (
153
), and assuming statistical independence between the noise
n_{k}
and the input signal matrix
\mathbf{X}_{k}
, (
153
) can be simplified to

\displaystyle a_{\mathbf{h},k}=

\displaystyle\operatorname{Re}\left[\operatorname{E}\left[\mathbf{h}^{H}\mathbf{X}_{k}\mathbf{g}\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{k-1}^{H}\mathbf{X}_{k}^{H}\Delta\mathbf{h}_{k-1}\right]-\vphantom{\operatorname{E}\left[\hat{\mathbf{h}}_{k-1}^{H}\mathbf{X}_{k}\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{k-1}\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{k-1}\mathbf{X}_{k}^{H}\Delta\mathbf{h}_{k-1}\right]}\right.

(156)

\displaystyle\left.\operatorname{E}\left[\hat{\mathbf{h}}_{k-1}^{H}\mathbf{X}_{k}\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{k-1}\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{k-1}\mathbf{X}_{k}^{H}\Delta\mathbf{h}_{k-1}\right]\right]\text{.}

Furthermore, using reformulated versions of (
149
) and (
150
) leads to

\displaystyle a_{\mathbf{h},k}=

\displaystyle\operatorname{Re}\left[\operatorname{E}\left[\nu^{*}\mathbf{h}^{H}\mathbf{X}_{k}\Delta\mathbf{g}_{k-1}\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{k-1}^{H}\mathbf{X}_{k}^{H}\Delta\mathbf{h}_{k-1}\right]+\vphantom{\operatorname{E}\left[\Delta\mathbf{h}_{k-1}^{H}\mathbf{X}_{k}\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{k-1}\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{k-1}\mathbf{X}_{k}^{H}\Delta\mathbf{h}_{k-1}\right]}\right.

(157)

\displaystyle\left.\operatorname{E}\left[\Delta\mathbf{h}_{k-1}^{H}\mathbf{X}_{k}\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{k-1}\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{k-1}\mathbf{X}_{k}^{H}\Delta\mathbf{h}_{k-1}\right]\right]\text{.}

Utilizing the property
\mathbf{v}^{H}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}=\operatorname{tr}\left[\mathbf{v}\mathbf{v}^{H}\mathbf{A}\right]
, the second part of the right-hand side of (
157
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle a_{\mathbf{h},k}=

\displaystyle\operatorname{Re}\left[\operatorname{E}\left[\nu^{*}\mathbf{h}^{H}\mathbf{X}_{k}\Delta\mathbf{g}_{k-1}\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{k-1}^{H}\mathbf{X}_{k}^{H}\Delta\mathbf{h}_{k-1}\right]+\vphantom{\operatorname{E}\left[\Delta\mathbf{h}_{k-1}^{H}\mathbf{X}_{k}\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{k-1}\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{k-1}\mathbf{X}_{k}^{H}\Delta\mathbf{h}_{k-1}\right]}\right.

(158)

\displaystyle\left.\operatorname{tr}\left[\operatorname{E}\left[\Delta\mathbf{h}_{k-1}\Delta\mathbf{h}_{k-1}^{H}\mathbf{X}_{k}\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{k-1}\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{k-1}\mathbf{X}_{k}^{H}\right]\right]\right]\text{.}

Furthermore, assuming
\Delta\mathbf{h}_{k-1}
is uncorrelated to
\mathbf{X}_{k}\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{k-1}
, yields

\displaystyle a_{\mathbf{h},k}=

\displaystyle\operatorname{Re}\left[\operatorname{E}\left[\nu^{*}\mathbf{h}^{H}\mathbf{X}_{k}\Delta\mathbf{g}_{k-1}\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{k-1}^{H}\mathbf{X}_{k}^{H}\Delta\mathbf{h}_{k-1}\right]+\vphantom{\operatorname{E}\left[\Delta\mathbf{h}_{k-1}^{H}\mathbf{X}_{k}\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{k-1}\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{k-1}\mathbf{X}_{k}^{H}\Delta\mathbf{h}_{k-1}\right]}\right.

(159)

\displaystyle\left.\operatorname{tr}\left[\operatorname{E}\left[\Delta\mathbf{h}_{k-1}\Delta\mathbf{h}_{k-1}^{H}\right]\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{g},k-1}\right]\right]\text{.}

Now, by incorporating (
134
) with
\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}=\sigma_{x}^{2}\mathbf{I}^{L\times L}
, this can be further simplified to

\displaystyle a_{\mathbf{h},k}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Re}\left[\nu^{*}\sigma_{x}^{2}\mathbf{h}^{H}\operatorname{E}\left[\Delta\mathbf{h}_{k-1}\right]\operatorname{E}\left[\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{k-1}^{H}\Delta\mathbf{g}_{k-1}\right]\right]+

(160)

\displaystyle\quad\;\,\sigma_{x}^{2}\operatorname{E}\left[||\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{k-1}||_{2}^{2}\right]\operatorname{E}\left[||\Delta\mathbf{h}_{k-1}||_{2}^{2}\right]\text{.}

Using the approximation
\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{k-1}^{H}\mathbf{X}_{k}^{H}\mathbf{X}_{k}\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{k-1}\approx L\sigma_{x}^{2}\operatorname{E}\left[||\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{k-1}||_{2}^{2}\right]
, (

) can be rewritten to

\displaystyle b_{\mathbf{h},k}=L\sigma_{x}^{2}\operatorname{E}\left[||\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{k-1}||_{2}^{2}\right]\operatorname{E}\left[|e_{k}|^{2}\right]\text{.}

(161)

To simplify this, it can be shown that the error from (

Appendix C
Derivation of the
crbnlms
filter

In this section, one possible method of how to normalize the step-size of the
crblms
filter is shown. Similar as in
Section 3.6
for the
cbnlms
filter, the a posteriori errors

\displaystyle\bar{e}_{k}

\displaystyle=y_{k}-\hat{\mathbf{h}}^{H}_{k}\mathbf{X}_{k}\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{k-1}

(180)

and

\displaystyle\tilde{e}_{k}

\displaystyle=y_{k}-\hat{\mathbf{h}}^{H}_{k-1}\mathbf{X}_{k}\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{k}

(181)

are introduced. Inserting (
118
) into (
180
) yields

\displaystyle\bar{e}_{k}

\displaystyle=e_{k}\left(1-\mu_{\mathbf{h}}\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{k-1}^{H}\mathbf{X}_{k}^{H}\mathbf{X}_{k}\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{k-1}\right)\text{.}

(182)

Setting this error to zero and assuming
e_{k}\neq 0
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mu_{\mathbf{h}}=\frac{1}{\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{k-1}^{H}\mathbf{X}_{k}^{H}\mathbf{X}_{k}\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{k-1}}\text{.}

(183)

Using similar arguments as in
Section 3.6
, the final update equation for

follows as

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{h}}_{k}

\displaystyle=\hat{\mathbf{h}}_{k-1}+\frac{\alpha_{\mathbf{h}}\mathbf{X}_{k}\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{k-1}}{\delta_{\mathbf{h}}+\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{k-1}^{T}\mathbf{X}_{k}^{H}\mathbf{X}_{k}\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{k-1}}e_{k}^{*}\text{.}

Since the a posteriori error in (

\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{k}
. Setting (

. A meaningful step-size is the one that minimizes

\displaystyle\left|\tilde{e}_{k}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=y_{k}y_{k}^{*}-2\operatorname{Re}\left[y_{k}^{*}\hat{\mathbf{h}}_{k-1}^{H}\mathbf{X}_{k}\right]\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{k}+

(185)

\displaystyle\quad\,\,\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{k}^{T}\mathbf{X}_{k}\hat{\mathbf{h}}_{k-1}\hat{\mathbf{h}}_{k-1}^{H}\mathbf{X}_{k}\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{k}\text{.}

After inserting (
119
) the gradient follows to

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\left|\tilde{e}_{k}\right|^{2}}{\partial\mu_{\mathbf{g}}}=-2\operatorname{Re}\left[e_{k}^{*}\hat{\mathbf{h}}_{k-1}^{H}\mathbf{X}_{k}\right]\operatorname{Re}\left[e_{k}\mathbf{X}_{k}^{H}\hat{\mathbf{h}}_{k-1}\right]+

(186)

\displaystyle 2\mu_{\mathbf{g}}\operatorname{Re}\left[e_{k}\hat{\mathbf{h}}_{k-1}^{T}\mathbf{X}_{k}^{*}\right]\mathbf{X}_{k}^{H}\hat{\mathbf{h}}_{k-1}\hat{\mathbf{h}}_{k-1}^{H}\mathbf{X}_{k}\operatorname{Re}\left[e_{k}\mathbf{X}_{k}^{H}\hat{\mathbf{h}}_{k-1}\right]\text{.}

Setting this to zero yields the optimal step-size

\displaystyle\mu_{\mathbf{g}}=\operatorname{Re}\left[e_{k}^{*}\hat{\mathbf{h}}_{k-1}^{H}\mathbf{X}_{k}\right]

(187)

\displaystyle\frac{\operatorname{Re}\left[e_{k}\mathbf{X}_{k}^{H}\hat{\mathbf{h}}_{k-1}\right]}{\operatorname{Re}\left[e_{k}\hat{\mathbf{h}}_{k-1}^{T}\mathbf{X}_{k}^{*}\right]\mathbf{X}_{k}^{H}\hat{\mathbf{h}}_{k-1}\hat{\mathbf{h}}_{k-1}^{H}\mathbf{X}_{k}\operatorname{Re}\left[e_{k}\mathbf{X}_{k}^{H}\hat{\mathbf{h}}_{k-1}\right]}\text{.}

Again similar as in

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{k}=\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{k-1}+\alpha_{\mathbf{g}}2\operatorname{Re}\left[e_{k}\mathbf{X}_{k}^{H}\hat{\mathbf{h}}_{k-1}\right]

(188)

\displaystyle\frac{\operatorname{Re}\left[e_{k}^{*}\hat{\mathbf{h}}_{k-1}^{H}\mathbf{X}_{k}\right]\operatorname{Re}\left[e_{k}\mathbf{X}_{k}^{H}\hat{\mathbf{h}}_{k-1}\right]}{\delta_{\mathbf{g}}+\operatorname{Re}\left[e_{k}\hat{\mathbf{h}}_{k-1}^{T}\mathbf{X}_{k}^{*}\right]\mathbf{X}_{k}^{H}\hat{\mathbf{h}}_{k-1}\hat{\mathbf{h}}_{k-1}^{H}\mathbf{X}_{k}\operatorname{Re}\left[e_{k}\mathbf{X}_{k}^{H}\hat{\mathbf{h}}_{k-1}\right]}\text{.}
