HILL: the efficacy and safety of hepatic arterial infusion chemotherapy with the FOLFOX regimen combined with lenvatinib and the PD-L1 inhibitor durvalumab in unresectable hepatocellular carcinoma: a prospective, single-arm, phase 2 clinical trial
Authors: Shao-Hua Li, Zhi-Jun Zuo, Liang-He Lu, Long-Zhou Xu, Qiao-Xuan Wang, Rong-Ce Zhao, Jie Mei, Ji-Bin Li, Lie Zheng, Rong-Ping Guo, Wei Wei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Despite numerous available treatment options, the overall prognosis for patients with unresectable hepatocellular carcinoma (uHCC) remains poor. This study aimed to evaluate the efficacy and safety of combining hepatic arterial infusion chemotherapy with FOLFOX (FOLFOX-HAIC), lenvatinib, and the PD-L1 inhibitor durvalumab in this population, with all uHCC patients receiving the triple-combination regimen as initial therapy. The primary objective was progression-free survival (PFS), and secondary outcomes included objective response rate (ORR), disease control rate (DCR), overall survival (OS), and safety. From August 2021 to September 2023, 40 uHCC patients were enrolled, with a median follow-up of 23.1 months (95% confidence interval [CI], 19.0–23.8); 3 deaths and 12 disease progressions were recorded. The median PFS was 15.8 months (95% CI, 8.3–23.3) with a 6-month PFS rate of 77.5% (95% CI, 63–90%), and 1- and 2-year OS rates were 97.5% and 94.0%, respectively. Per modified RECIST criteria, ORR was 75.0% (9 CR/pCR, 21 PR) and DCR was 95.0% (8 SD); median time to response (TTR) was 2.2 months and median duration of response (DOR) was 10.4 months. Seven patients (17.5%) underwent R0 conversion surgery, 3 (42.9%) achieving pCR. Safety was favorable: 85.0% had grade 1–2 adverse events, with grade 3 elevated alanine aminotransferase and thrombocytopenia each occurring in 2 patients (5.0%). No grade 4 adverse events were observed. Collectively, the triple-combination therapy of FOLFOX-HAIC, lenvatinib, and durvalumab exhibits promising therapeutic efficacy, favorable disease control, and a well-tolerated safety profile, providing a potential new treatment option for patients with uHCC. Trial number: NCT04961918.

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) represents a highly aggressive global health challenge, ranking as the sixth most commonly diagnosed malignancy and the third leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide.1 A particularly alarming clinical reality is that approximately 50% of HCC patients are initially diagnosed at intermediate or advanced stages, when tumors are frequently deemed unresectable due to multifocal bilobar involvement, macrovascular invasion, or inadequate future liver remnant.2

Over the past decade, the therapeutic landscape for advanced HCC has evolved substantially. The REFLECT trial established lenvatinib as a first-line targeted therapy, demonstrating comparable overall survival to sorafenib but superior progression-free survival and objective response rate.3,4 Subsequently, the IMbrave150 and HIMALAYA trials revolutionized treatment paradigms by introducing combination strategies—specifically atezolizumab plus bevacizumab and durvalumab plus tremelimumab—as preferred first-line options.5–7 More recently, the LAUNCH trial demonstrated that combining lenvatinib with transarterial chemoembolization (TACE) significantly improved objective response rates and overall survival compared to lenvatinib monotherapy, with 15.3% of patients achieving salvage liver resection.8 Moreover, hepatic arterial infusion chemotherapy (HAIC) using the FOLFOX regimen has emerged as a promising locoregional therapy, with studies reporting an objective response rate of 47.8% and superior survival outcomes compared to TACE in patients with advanced HCC.9,10

Despite these advances, a substantial proportion of HCC patients fail to derive long-term survival benefit from current systemic therapies due to intrinsic or acquired drug resistance.11 The highly vascularized nature of HCC makes antiangiogenic agents a therapeutic cornerstone; however, tyrosine kinase inhibitor monotherapy rarely achieves complete responses, and durable remissions remain uncommon.12,13 Immune checkpoint inhibitors have demonstrated remarkable antitumor effects across multiple malignancies, yet single-agent activity in HCC is modest, with objective response rates typically below 20%.5–7 The integration of locoregional treatments with systemic therapies has therefore emerged as a critical strategy to enhance patient outcomes.14 This approach is conceptually anchored in the “conversion therapy” paradigm—defined as treatment administered with the explicit intent of downstaging initially unresectable HCC to enable potentially curative resection.2 Supporting this concept, a recent prospective phase 2 trial of the LEN-TAP regimen (lenvatinib, TACE, and a PD-1 inhibitor) demonstrated dramatically higher salvage resection and objective response rates compared to TACE alone, along with significant improvements in survival outcomes.7

Nevertheless, several critical challenges persist. First, TACE-induced hypoxia can trigger compensatory upregulation of pro-angiogenic factors such as vascular endothelial growth factor and fibroblast growth factor, paradoxically promoting tumor revascularization and progression.13 Second, patients with macrovascular invasion—a common feature in advanced HCC—often demonstrate limited tolerance to TACE and may experience hepatic decompensation.7 Third, while anti-PD-1 antibodies have been extensively studied in combination regimens, the integration of anti-PD-L1 agents such as durvalumab with locoregional therapy and targeted agents remains insufficiently explored. To address these limitations, we hypothesized that substituting HAIC for TACE and incorporating durvalumab into a triple combination regimen with lenvatinib could enhance therapeutic efficacy while improving tolerability. This hypothesis is supported by the distinct advantages of each component: HAIC delivers chemotherapeutic agents directly into the hepatic artery, achieving higher intra-tumoral drug concentrations with reduced systemic toxicity and avoiding embolization-related hypoxia9,10; furthermore, the synergy among FOLFOX-induced immunogenic cell death, lenvatinib-mediated vascular normalization and immune microenvironment modulation, and durvalumab-enhanced T-cell activation may generate a potent “triple combination” effect capable of deepening tumor responses and increasing conversion rates.

In this prospective, single-arm, phase 2 study, we evaluated the efficacy and safety of upfront FOLFOX-HAIC combined with lenvatinib and durvalumab in treatment-naïve patients with unresectable HCC. To our knowledge, this represents the first investigation to integrate HAIC with both lenvatinib and an anti-PD-L1 agent in this population. Our regimen addresses key unmet needs: it leverages HAIC’s superior response over TACE while circumventing hypoxia-driven resistance; incorporates durvalumab’s favorable safety and unique immunomodulatory properties; and builds upon lenvatinib’s status as the highest-objective-response-rate targeted monotherapy. By achieving an objective response rate of 75.0% and an R0 conversion resection rate of 17.5% with manageable toxicity, this strategy redefines the therapeutic goal in unresectable HCC from palliation toward potential cure. These findings provide a compelling foundation for future randomized controlled trials and offer a new and effective treatment option for patients with advanced unresectable HCC.

Results

Patient characteristics and treatment administration

Between August 2021 and September 2023, a total of 125 patients were screened, and 40 patients were enrolled (Fig. 1). Among the enrolled patients, 2 (5.0%) were classified as BCLC stage B and 38 (95.0%) as BCLC stage C. All 33 (82.5%) HBsAg-positive patients received antiviral therapy with entecavir or tenofovir, initiated at least 7 days prior to the start of durvalumab and continued throughout the study period. No episodes of HBV flare or virological reactivation, as defined by AASLD criteria, were observed during treatment. Two patients were positive for HCV antibodies and further evaluated for HCV RNA. Both of them received direct-acting antiviral agent (DAA)-based therapy during anticancer treatment, and sustained virological response (SVR) was achieved in both cases. Radiological or histological evidence of cirrhosis was observed in 9 out of 40 patients (22.5%). All included patients underwent at least two cycles of HAIC and durvalumab and received at least two months of lenvatinib treatment, and these patients were incorporated into the safety evaluation. Ultimately, 40 patients underwent a total of 130 cycles of HAIC, with each patient receiving a median of 4 cycles (range: 2-6). Lenvatinib treatment had a median duration of 8.3 months, with an interquartile range of 4.9 to 12.7 months.

Efficacy analysis

The study was censored on April 28, 2024. The median follow-up duration was 23.1 months (95% CI: 19.0-23.8), over which time 3 deaths and 12 instances of disease progression were recorded. The median PFS was 15.8 months (95% CI: 8.3-23.3), with a 6-month PFS rate of 77.5% (95% CI: 63-90%). The 1- and 2-year OS rates were 97.5% and 94.0%, respectively. Survival outcomes are presented in Fig. 2. Based on the mRECIST criteria, 9 patients attained either complete response (CR) or pathological complete response (pCR), while 21 achieved partial response (PR), yielding an objective response rate (ORR) of 75.0%. When including the 8 patients who maintained stable disease (SD), the disease control rate (DCR) reached 95.0% (Table 2, Supplementary Table S1). The median time to response (TTR) was 2.2 months (95% CI, 1.9-2.6), while the median duration of response (DOR) was 10.4 months (95% CI, 8.3-13.8). Survival outcomes were evaluated based on the best overall response according to mRECIST, comparing CR/PR versus SD/PD and CR/PR/SD versus PD. The median PFS was 16.5 months versus 4.6 months (CR/PR vs SD/PD) and 16.2 months versus 1.7 months (CR/PR/SD vs PD). The 2-year OS was 92.1% versus 100% (CR/PR vs SD/PD) and 93.6% versus 100% (CR/PR/SD vs PD). PFS differed significantly between response groups in both comparisons (p < 0.001), whereas OS differences were not statistically significant (Supplementary Fig. S1). A sensitivity analysis according to RECIST 1.1 criteria resulted in an ORR of 57.5% (23/40) and a DCR of 90% (36/40) (Table 2, Supplementary Table S1). The median TTR was 2.1 months (95% CI: 1.6-2.6), and the median PFS was 15.8 months (95% CI: 8.3-23.3) under RECIST1.1 criteria (Supplementary Fig. S2). Among all 40 participants, none presented with ascites or esophageal/gastric varices of grade 2 or higher, while 11 patients exhibited splenomegaly on imaging. Of these patients, 7 (17.5%) underwent subsequent conversion surgery, and all of them achieved R0 resection, with 3 of them (42.9%) reaching pCR based on pathological examination. The median time from enrollment to fulfillment of curative resection criteria was 5.6 months (range: 2.2–7.6) (Supplementary Table S2). All patients who underwent surgery had unilobar tumors and no evidence of extrahepatic metastases at baseline. This cohort showed a trend toward longer median PFS (not reached vs. 15.8 months; both p = 0.13) and a higher 2-year overall survival rate (100% vs. 92.6%; p = 0.46) than those who did not receive surgery (Supplementary Fig. S3). Safety analysis

The safety evaluation indicated that the combination therapy was generally well tolerated, with 34 patients (85.0%) reporting mild adverse events (grade 1–2). The most frequently observed grade 3–4 adverse events were increased alanine aminotransferase levels and thrombocytopenia, each occurring at grade 3 in two patients (5.0%). Six (15.0%) patients experienced serious AEs or immune-related AEs that required hospitalization or steroid treatment (Supplementary Table S5). Moreover, no AE-related deaths occurred during hospitalization. Details of the AEs are shown in Table 3. The median Child‒Pugh score remained stable at 5 from baseline to posttreatment, with only one patient progressing from Child‒Pugh class A to class B and no progression to class C. Similarly, the median ALBI score improved slightly from –2.75 to –2.47, indicating minimal deterioration in hepatic function. Discussion

Despite rapid advances in systemic therapy, the outcome of uHCC remains unsatisfactory. The locoregional therapies continue to play a critical role in the comprehensive management of this patient population.14 Our group has previously reported that HAIC with the FOLFOX regimen, as well as comprehensive HAIC-based treatment strategies, significantly improves the prognosis of uHCC.10,15 However, it remains unclear which systemic therapeutic agent would be the optimal partner for HAIC. Lenvatinib has shown superior efficacy among first-line TKIs for uHCC, coupled with a manageable adverse event profile. Separately, the anti-PD-L1 agent durvalumab has also demonstrated promising efficacy and safety in prior studies.16 Therefore, we conducted this prospective, single-armed, phase 2 trial to evaluate the efficacy and safety of FOLFOX-HAIC combined with lenvatinib and durvalumab in uHCC, with the aim of establishing a more effective and better-tolerated treatment strategy.

In a study previously reported by our team, among patients with unresectable hepatocellular carcinoma (largest diameter ≥ 7 cm) without macrovascular invasion or extrahepatic spread, the FOLFOX-HAIC group (n = 159) demonstrated an ORR of 46%, a median PFS of 9.6 months, a median OS of 23.1 months, and an incidence of serious adverse events of 19%.9 In the present study, FOLFOX-HAIC combined with lenvatinib and durvalumab significantly enhanced the efficacy, achieving an ORR of 75.0% and a CR rate of 22.5% according to mRECIST criteria. Even in this cohort with advanced tumors, the conversion surgery rate was 17.5% (7/40). Notably, 42.9% (3/7) of these patients attained pCR. Meanwhile, this combination treatment strategy also provided a substantial survival benefit, with a median PFS of 15.8 months; the 6-month PFS rate was 77.5% (95% CI 63–90%), and the 1- and 2-year OS rates were 97.5% and 94.0%, respectively. In the EMERALD-1 trial, 25.8% of patients were BCLC stage A, 57.3% were stage B, and 16.1% were stage C. The combination of TACE with durvalumab and bevacizumab yielded an ORR of 43.6% and a median PFS of 15.0 months.17 The LEAP-012 trial reported an ORR of approximately 60% and a median PFS of 14.6 months with the combination of TACE, lenvatinib, and pembrolizumab.18 In the CARES-005 trial, TACE combined with camrelizumab and apatinib achieved an ORR of 65.0% and a median PFS of 11.0 months.19 By comparison, our triplet regimen achieved a higher ORR and longer median PFS than the above study. The high rate of intrahepatic tumor control can be attributed to the locoregional efficacy of HAIC, while prolonged survival reflects the durable clinical benefit and long-term effects of systemic agents, particularly immunotherapy.11 Although PFS significantly differed across response categories (CR/PR versus SD/PD and CR/PR/SD versus PD), no statistically significant difference in OS was observed. The potential reasons for the lack of difference in OS may largely be attributed to the impact of subsequent therapies after disease progression, as well as the relatively short follow-up duration, with a median follow-up of 23.1 months and only 3 death events occurring by the end of the study.

Our previous studies have revealed that HAIC has fewer adverse effects than TACE.10 Among tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) used for HCC, lenvatinib is recognized for a relatively manageable safety profile. Meanwhile, as an anti-PD-L1 agent, durvalumab’s adverse event spectrum is often considered comparable to, although potentially distinct from, that of anti-PD-1 therapies. The results of the present study showed that 34 (85.0%) patients had mild adverse events (AEs) (grade 1-2). Moreover, the most common grade 3-4 AEs were elevated alanine aminotransferase and thrombocytopenia, both of which were manageable with appropriate interventions. No patient developed hepatic decompensation (including ascites, hepatic encephalopathy, or variceal bleeding) during the treatment period. This overall favorable safety profile might be attributed to our active dose adjustment of lenvatinib and durvalumab, as well as the strategy of on-demand HAIC treatment.

As an early-stage trial, this study aimed to evaluate the preliminary efficacy and safety of the combined regimen. The original protocol was planned for 36 participants; however, the rate of treatment completion was slightly lower than expected. To ensure sufficient statistical power and with approval from the ethics committee, the sample size was increased to 40 participants. Post hoc power analysis based on the actual enrollment demonstrated a statistical power of 83.8%, which is considered adequate for exploratory studies. Furthermore, a sensitivity analysis excluding the last four participants still yielded consistent results, supporting the robustness of the findings (Supplementary Table S7, Supplementary Fig. S5).

The present study also has some limitations. First, it was a single-arm, phase 2 trial with a small sample size. Therefore, our findings require validation in a prospective, large-scale, phase 3 randomized controlled trial. Second, all enrolled patients had well-preserved liver function (Child‒Pugh A). The safety and tolerability of the combination regimen in patients with more advanced liver dysfunction remain to be evaluated. Third, the quality of life data were not collected, as this was not included as an endpoint in the protocol. Moreover, since the majority of patients had evidence of HBV infection, external validation in cohorts with other underlying etiologies is necessary. Finally, due to the single-arm design of this study, it remains uncertain whether the observed efficacy of this combination regimen is attributable to an additive or synergistic effect. We will incorporate translational analyses, such as biomarker exploration, to better elucidate the underlying mechanisms in subsequent Phase 3 clinical trials.

In summary, the regimen comprising FOLFOX-HAIC, lenvatinib, and durvalumab demonstrates encouraging therapeutic outcomes and an acceptable safety profile in patients with unresectable hepatocellular carcinoma. Confirmation of these findings through a phase 3 randomized controlled trial is necessary.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This phase 2, single-arm, prospective clinical trial was carried out at the Sun Yat-sen University Cancer Center (SYSUCC) in Guangzhou, China. Patients aged 18 to 75 years who were diagnosed with unresectable HCC (uHCC) according to the AASLD practice guidelines; treatment-naive; Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group Performance Score 0-1; and with adequate hematologic, hepatic, and renal functions were eligible for inclusion. Patients eligible for any form of curative therapy, including surgical resection, local ablation, or stereotactic body radiotherapy, were excluded from the study. Exclusion criteria also included significant functional deficits in major organs, such as the heart, brain, lungs, and kidneys, or combined with severe infection or other serious associated diseases; allergy to the study drugs; previous antitumor therapy; pregnant or lactating women; a history of other malignant tumors, organ transplantation, neurological or psychiatric diseases, human immunodeficiency virus infection, esophageal or gastric variceal bleeding, or cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events within the past 30 days, drug addicts or drug abusers.

This study was carried out in full accordance with relevant local legal requirements and the ethical standards outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided written informed consent prior to their involvement in the study. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) and Institutional Ethics Committee (IEC) of SYSUCC (IRB-approved number: B2021-166-01). Additionally, the trial was prospectively registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (identifier: NCT04961918) and reported following the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guidelines.

Trial design and treatment

The present study was an open-label, single-arm trial without blinding or randomization. A statistical expert (Ji-Bin Li) conducted the data analysis and reviewed the results. The HAIC procedure was conducted in accordance with previous studies.10,15,20 Following successful percutaneous catheterization of the femoral artery, angiography of the superior mesenteric artery and the hepatic artery was performed. Once the arteriographic findings verified eligibility, the catheter was advanced into the targeted branch of the hepatic artery. Subsequently, patients with a temporary indwelling catheter were transferred to the inpatient ward. No permanent port system was implanted. The catheter was linked to an infusion pump for continuous delivery of chemotherapy agents: oxaliplatin at a dose of 135 mg/m² administered from hour 0 to 3 on day 1; leucovorin at 400 mg/m² infused between hours 3 and 4.5; fluorouracil at 400 mg/m² delivered from hour 4.5 to 6.5; followed by a prolonged infusion of fluorouracil at 2400 mg/m² over 46 hours, spanning from day 1 to day 3. Throughout the treatment period, patients remained on bed rest. Upon completion of chemotherapy, the catheter was removed, and a firm pressure dressing was applied at the puncture site to ensure hemostasis. The infusion of durvalumab was given intravenously 12 hours after the end of chemotherapy at a dose of 500 mg in 250 ml of 0.9% saline in 1 hour. Concurrently, lenvatinib was administered orally at 8 mg per day. HAIC was performed, and durvalumab was infused once every 3 to 4 weeks. The dose of lenvatinib was adjusted according to our prior clinical experience in HCC, whereas the dose of durvalumab used in non-small cell lung cancer was also referenced.16 After 2 cycles of HAIC and durvalumab, the subsequent treatment was determined according to tumor response evaluation. All patients who tested positive for hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) received standard antiviral treatment. Patients with positive hepatitis C virus (HCV) antibodies received further evaluation for HCV RNA. Those confirmed to have active HCV infection were treated with interferon or sufosbuvir and velpatasvir. As the measurement of hepatic venous pressure gradient (HVPG) was not performed routinely in China, the presence and severity of portal hypertension at baseline were assessed based on indirect evidence from imaging and medical records when determining eligibility for conversion surgery.

Endpoints and follow-ups

The primary endpoint was progression-free survival (PFS), defined as the interval from the date of enrollment to the date of either diagnosis of tumor progression or death from any cause, depending on which occurred first. The secondary endpoints included overall survival (OS), defined as the duration from the date of enrollment to death due to all causes; the object response rate (ORR), defined as the percentage of complete response (CR) or partial response (PR), which was maintained for at least 4 weeks after initial radiological confirmation; and the disease control rate (DCR), calculated as the sum of ORR and the proportion of patients with stable disease (SD). All contrast-enhanced CT or MRI scans were reviewed by two independent radiologists blinded to treatment allocation. Discrepancies in assessment were resolved by a third senior radiologist. Patients underwent imaging follow-up every 6 weeks (±4 days) during cycles 1–6 of triple therapy and thereafter every 8 weeks until disease progression, surgical intervention, or death. The best overall response was assigned once between baseline and the first progression/death. Patients who remained free of tumor progression or death at the time of data cutoff were considered event-free and administratively censored as alive on the date of their last documented follow-up. Adverse events (AEs) were monitored continuously throughout the study period and classified using the National Cancer Institute’s Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE), version 4.03.21 All patients were followed up at an interval of 2 to 3 months and monitored twice daily during the hospitalization phase. At home, patients were advised to promptly notify the research team by telephone if any severe AEs occurred. Other AEs were documented during routine scheduled follow-up visits. All participants were followed up every 2 to 3 months.15,20,22,23 During every follow-up appointment, patients underwent a physical examination, blood tests (such as measurements of serum alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) levels and liver function), and contrast-enhanced abdominal imaging via computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging. Tumor response was evaluated based on the mRECIST criteria, with a sensitivity analysis performed using the RECIST 1.1 criteria. If potential tumor progression was observed, additional diagnostic procedures, such as hepatic arteriography or tissue biopsy, were carried out. When disease progression was confirmed, further management was determined by our center’s multidisciplinary team and implemented accordingly.

Statistical analysis

The sample size estimation was based on assumptions that the 6-month PFS rate of unresectable hepatocellular carcinoma treated with HAIC alone was approximately 40%,10 while the combination of HAIC with lenvatinib and durvalumab was expected to improve to 60% over HAIC alone, with an expected enrollment time of 12 months and a follow-up time of 12 months. With α set at 0.05 (two-sided) and β at 0.2, the sample size was calculated using PASS software based on the Weibull distribution with a shape parameter of 1. The One-Sample Log-rank Test module in PASS yielded a required sample size of 31 cases. Considering a potential dropout and protocol deviation rate of approximately 15%, a total of 36 patients were deemed necessary.

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS software, version 24.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA), with a significance level set at P < 0.05 for two-sided tests.
