Government Digital Transformation and the utilization of Basic Public Health Services by China’s migrant population
Authors: Haowen Jia
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis research delves into the ramifications of Government Digital Transformation (GDT) on Basic Public Health Services (BPHS) utilization among China’s migrant populace. Within the framework of escalating digital incorporation into public services, it becomes imperative to assess how GDT initiatives could ameliorate healthcare access for migrants, a group habitually confronted with systemic obstacles. Such scrutiny is crucial for the formulation of evidence-based policies that harness digital innovations to mitigate healthcare disparities.MethodsThe analysis employs data from the 2018 China Migrants Dynamic Survey, matched with details on the National Pilot Cities for Information Benefit to the Public policy at the prefecture city level, yielding a dataset comprising 113,905 observations. To gauge BPHS utilization among migrants, two indicators were developed: the establishment of health records and registration with family doctors. The influence of GDT on BPHS access was estimated utilizing Ordinary Least Squares regression for the initial analysis, incorporating a comprehensive array of individual and household attributes, alongside province fixed effects, to ensure a thorough evaluation.ResultsBaseline regression analyses revealed that GDT is significantly correlated with an enhanced probability of migrants establishing health records and registering with family doctors, with increases of 7.53% and 2.75%, respectively. Subsequent robustness checks, including sensitivity analyses with alternate dependent variables, the Propensity Score Matching method, Double/Debiased Machine Learning method, and placebo tests, further substantiated these findings. Additionally, the analysis explored mechanisms through which GDT facilitates BPHS access, identifying health information accessibility as a significant mediator in promoting healthcare engagement among migrants. Notably, both individual and regional heterogeneity were observed in GDT’s impact: female migrants, those with lower educational levels, older individuals, and rural hukou holders experienced more pronounced benefits, while the effects of GDT were also stronger in cities with lower GDP and smaller populations. These findings suggest that GDT is particularly effective in bridging healthcare access gaps in economically or demographically constrained areas.ConclusionsThe outcomes underscore that GDT significantly enhances BPHS utilization among migrants, positioning digital governance as an instrumental mechanism in bridging healthcare access gaps. The observed differential impacts across various subgroups and regions emphasize the importance of customized digital health strategies that cater to the diverse needs of migrant communities. For instance, targeted interventions in lower-GDP or smaller cities could maximize the benefits of GDT in under-resourced areas. Contributing to the body of empirical evidence on digital governance and public health, this research advocates for the expanded deployment of GDT initiatives to foster more equitable health service provision for migrant demographics, promoting inclusivity within a rapidly urbanizing society.

Background

Government Digital Transformation (GDT) represents a pivotal shift towards leveraging digital technologies to revamp public sector operations and service delivery, aiming to enhance efficiency, accessibility, and citizen satisfaction [1]. Within the realm of public health services, GDT seeks to transcend traditional barriers, facilitating seamless access to vital health information and services [2]. The importance of Basic Public Health Services (BPHS) cannot be understated, as they constitute the foundation for promoting public health and well-being, ensuring that essential preventive, curative, and rehabilitative services reach the broader population [3]. In the context of China’s rapidly evolving healthcare system, GDT emerges as a critical enabler, poised to address the multifaceted challenges of delivering comprehensive healthcare services across its vast and demographically diverse population [4]. By integrating digital solutions into the healthcare framework, GDT promises to bridge the gap between healthcare providers and recipients, particularly benefiting marginalized groups such as the migrant population, who often face significant hurdles in accessing standardized health services [5].

China’s migrant population, characterized by their mobility from rural to urban areas in search of better employment and living conditions, constitutes a significant and growing segment of the national demographic. This population faces unique challenges in accessing BPHS, challenges that are deeply rooted in the socio-economic and systemic fabric of the country. The Hukou household registration system, which ties access to public services including healthcare to one’s registered domicile, inherently disadvantages migrants who move away from their registered locations [6]. Consequently, despite contributing to the urban economy, migrants often find themselves excluded from the comprehensive health services available to local urban residents. This exclusion is compounded by additional barriers such as lower socioeconomic status, lack of awareness of available health services, and the transient nature of their employment and living conditions, which collectively hinder their access to and utilization of BPHS [7, 8].

Despite the burgeoning interest in GDT as a catalyst for improving public services, there exists a notable gap in the empirical literature specifically examining its impact on health service utilization among migrant populations [9]. This oversight is significant given the potential of GDT to address and mitigate the longstanding disparities in healthcare access faced by migrants, who are often marginalized within the healthcare system [10]. The intersection of digital governance and public health presents a unique opportunity to leverage technology in bridging the access gap, making the investigation into GDT’s effectiveness not just timely but imperative [8]. With migrants facing barriers such as the Hukou system restrictions, socioeconomic constraints, and mobility issues, GDT initiatives could offer innovative solutions to these entrenched problems [11]. Therefore, this study is necessitated by the urgent need to explore how digital interventions can democratize health service access, ensuring that the benefits of technological advancements in healthcare are equitably distributed [12]. By filling this gap in the literature, the research aims to illuminate the pathways through which digital transformation can enhance healthcare accessibility for one of the most vulnerable segments of the population, thereby contributing to the broader goals of health equity and social inclusion [13–15].

The primary objective of this study is to meticulously analyze the effects of GDT on the utilization of BPHS among China’s migrant population. This research seeks to shed light on the extent to which digital governance initiatives can ameliorate the challenges faced by migrants in accessing essential health services, thereby contributing to the reduction of health disparities. Within this overarching goal, the study is specifically designed to uncover the nuances of GDT’s impact, recognizing that the migrant population is not monolithic. Hence, a secondary but critical objective involves exploring the heterogeneity in GDT’s influence across different subgroups within the migrant community, such as variations based on gender, age, educational background, and Hukou status. This dual-focused approach aims not only to provide a comprehensive understanding of GDT’s overall efficacy in enhancing BPHS accessibility but also to identify which segments of the migrant population benefit most from such digital interventions.

The theoretical framework of this study is anchored at the confluence of digital inclusion, public service delivery, and health equity theories, offering a holistic lens through which to examine the impact of GDT on enhancing access to BPHS among migrant populations. Digital inclusion theory emphasizes the pivotal role of equitable access to digital technologies in bridging social and economic divides, suggesting that GDT could serve as a catalyst for reducing disparities in healthcare access [16]. Concurrently, theories of public service delivery highlight the government’s responsibility in ensuring the availability and quality of essential services, including healthcare, which is further underscored by health equity theories that advocate for removing systemic barriers to achieve equitable health outcomes for all segments of society [1, 17]. This integrated theoretical approach underscores the potential of GDT to not only improve service delivery efficiency but more importantly, to address the nuanced challenges faced by migrants in accessing healthcare, thereby contributing to the broader goal of health equity [18–20].

Related literature includes three main areas that contextualize this study’s analysis of GDT on BPHS utilization among migrants. First, a comparison with international GDT initiatives helps underscore both the unique characteristics of China’s digital transformation efforts and the potential broader applicability of its findings. Globally, countries have adopted various digital governance strategies to improve public service delivery [21, 22], yet China’s approach is distinct in its scale and focus on bridging urban-rural divides, especially for underserved groups like migrants [23–25]. This comparison illustrates how China’s GDT efforts are designed to address specific socio-economic challenges unique to a rapidly urbanizing society, while still contributing valuable lessons on digital inclusivity that may be relevant for other nations pursuing similar goals in healthcare accessibility.

Secondly, considering the interdisciplinary nature of GDT, insights from health economics and information systems are instrumental in understanding its broader impact. In health economics, research emphasizes the cost-effectiveness and accessibility benefits of digital health interventions, particularly in resource-constrained settings [26–28]. From the information management perspective, studies highlight the importance of factors such as digital infrastructure, data management, and user engagement in ensuring the success of digital health platforms [29, 30]. Together, these interdisciplinary perspectives suggest that GDT’s effectiveness in health systems extends beyond policy alone; it also depends on technological and economic considerations, which play crucial roles in facilitating sustainable and impactful digital transformations.

Finally, examining the relationship between migrant workers’ health and their social integration provides a critical context for understanding GDT’s broader implications. Research in regions with high migrant populations has shown that access to health services significantly influences migrants’ social integration and well-being [31–33]. Improved healthcare access has been linked to greater social stability, as healthier migrants are better able to participate economically and socially within their host communities. In this way, China’s GDT initiatives, by enhancing healthcare access for migrants, could potentially support social integration goals, suggesting that the benefits of GDT may extend beyond healthcare outcomes to foster more cohesive communities and improved social cohesion.

This study seeks to contribute to the existing academic literature by providing empirical analysis in an area where such evidence is notably scarce, particularly regarding the specific impact of GDT on BPHS utilization among migrants. By filling this gap, the research aims to enrich the discourse on digital governance and its role in public health, offering insights into how digital platforms can be leveraged to enhance healthcare accessibility for marginalized groups [9, 10]. Additionally, the findings are expected to have implications for public health policy, especially in the formulation and implementation of digital health initiatives tailored to the needs of migrant populations [8, 34]. By identifying the differential impact of GDT across various migrant subgroups, the study underscores the importance of designing targeted digital health strategies that are sensitive to the diverse needs and challenges within this demographic [12, 35]. Such nuanced policy interventions can not only ensure more effective utilization of digital health services but also contribute to the reduction of health disparities, aligning with the broader objectives of promoting public health equity and inclusivity [6, 13, 36, 37].

Methods

Data source

Our study examines the impact of GDT on the utilization of BPHS by China’s migrant population. At the micro-level, the data employed in this research derive from the 2018 China Migrants Dynamic Survey (CMDS), released by the National Health Commission of China. The CMDS represents a specialized monitoring survey dataset targeted at the Chinese migrant population. The scope of the 2018 CMDS encompassed 31 provinces, autonomous regions, and municipalities across mainland China, with a sample size of approximately 150,000 individuals, offering substantial representativeness at the national level. Importantly, the CMDS encompasses a wealth of individual characteristics and household socio-economic factors, including detailed information on the accessibility of BPHS. Utilizing CMDS data to analyze the access to BPHS among China’s migrant population aligns with the consistent approach in the existing literature [37–41].

Regarding the selection of GDT policies, this paper incorporates the National Pilot Cities for Information Benefit to the Public (NPCIBP) policy, emblematic of China’s approach. Initiated in 2014, the NPCIBP selected 80 cities as pilot sites. The policy underscores the potential of information technology to revolutionize the provision of public services and enhance societal welfare. At its core, the NPCIBP aims to construct robust e-governance platforms, facilitating access to a variety of public services, including education, employment, and notably for this paper, public health services [42]. The policy has been instrumental in promoting equitable and inclusive access to public health services, thereby enabling migrants and local residents alike to enjoy comparable levels of service, underscoring the convenience of online services.

By matching the micro-level CMDS data with information on the NPCIBP policy at the prefecture-level, we constructed the dataset required for our analysis. Following data cleaning and variable selection processes, the final sample size incorporated into the regression analysis amounted to 113,905.

Definition of variables

Table 1 presents the definitions and basic descriptive statistics of the variables used in this study. The explanatory variable, GDT, is binary, assigned a value of 1 for respondents residing in areas impacted by the NPCIBP policy, reflecting approximately 53% of our total sample. Two dependent variables are chosen to measure the acquisition of BPHS: Establishment of Health Record (EHR) and Registration with a Family Doctor (RFD). A value of 1 is assigned if the respondent has established a health record or registered with a family doctor, respectively. Table 1 shows that 27.1% of the sample has established health records and 12.4% registered with family doctors. These percentages suggest a relatively modest uptake of BPHS among the migrant population.

Although the RFD policy was introduced in China in the early stages of health service reform, its implementation has faced significant challenges, resulting in uptake levels that have not met initial expectations [43–46]. Various factors, such as resource limitations, regional disparities, and awareness gaps among migrant populations, have contributed to this modest uptake. Despite these challenges, analyzing BPHS acquisition—particularly EHR and RFD—remains a standard approach in studying the health service utilization of China’s migrant population. This approach is widely adopted in research using CMDS data, as it effectively captures the basic public health service engagement levels in this demographic [37, 47–49].

To ensure a precise estimation of GDT’s impact on BPHS access, a set of control variables is incorporated, reflecting individual and household characteristics. Individual characteristics include gender, age, squared age (to account for the non-linear effects of age), marital status, education level, Ethnicity, type of household registration, and the duration away from the household registration location. Household characteristics encompass household size, number of children, number of elderly, and the log-transformed values of household’s annual total income and expenditure. The definitions and descriptive statistics of these variables are presented in Table 1.

Robustness checks

Firstly, to address potential biases arising from omitted variables in the baseline regression, this study employs Propensity Score Matching (PSM) to mitigate estimation biases due to sample selection. Specifically, a 1-to-1 nearest neighbor matching is conducted based on individual and household characteristics, using the matched sample for regression analysis. The matched sample size is 59,694. This method aims to maximize the comparability of the treatment and control group samples, enhancing the robustness of the findings.

Thirdly, this study addresses potential model misspecification issues present in the baseline regression, which originally employed Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) for estimation. Here, we adopt the cutting-edge Double/Debiased Machine Learning (DML) approach for robustness checks, a method that significantly mitigates the concerns of model misspecification through the use of machine learning techniques [50–52]. Specifically, we employ three machine learning algorithms: Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LGBM), Decision Tree (DT), and Logistic Regression (LR). During the process, we also include quadratic and cubic terms of individual and household characteristic variables, and employ 5-fold cross-validation to ensure the reliability of our estimations. These procedures are implemented using the doubleml command in Python.

Finally, the study conducts a placebo test to further verify the robustness of the baseline regression results. The methodology involved randomly assigning the occurrence of the NPCIBP policy across regions according to the actual proportions observed in the sample. Regressions were run using these randomly assigned samples, and the results were saved. This process was repeated 500 times, and the coefficients from these regressions were plotted on a density graph. If the distribution of the estimated coefficients from the random assignment is centered around zero, it indicates that the policy’s impact is not due to random chance and thus passes the placebo test.

Discussion

The empirical analysis conducted within this study has elucidated the significant role of GDT in enhancing the utilization of BPHS by China’s migrant population. Key findings indicate that GDT initiatives are associated with increased likelihood of migrants establishing health records and engaging with family doctors, as indicated by the robust positive coefficients in the baseline regressions and reinforced through various robustness checks, including propensity score matching and machine learning methods. These results underscore the pivotal impact of digital governance in potentially bridging the healthcare access divide for migrants. Notably, the heterogeneity analysis revealed that GDT’s facilitative effects are more pronounced for female migrants, those with lower educational attainment, older individuals, and those hailing from rural Hukou backgrounds, signaling that digital interventions may be particularly beneficial for traditionally underserved subpopulations.

The findings of this study provide empirical support for the theoretical frameworks of digital inclusion, public service delivery, and health equity, particularly within the context of migrant populations. The positive impact of GDT on BPHS utilization among migrants underscores digital inclusion theory’s premise that equitable access to digital technologies can help bridge social and economic divides. The pronounced benefits observed among subgroups with lower education levels, women, and older migrants highlight GDT’s role in extending access to historically underserved groups, thereby reducing disparities in healthcare access. In line with public service delivery theory, the results affirm that digital governance enhances the government’s ability to meet its responsibility in providing essential services, making healthcare more accessible and responsive to migrants’ needs. Finally, the study contributes to health equity theory by demonstrating that GDT can help mitigate structural barriers to healthcare for migrant populations, supporting a more inclusive public health framework that accommodates diverse demographic and regional needs. These findings extend the applicability of these theories by emphasizing the importance of digital tools in enhancing health equity for mobile, vulnerable populations within rapidly urbanizing contexts.

The theoretical implications of this study’s findings provide a valuable lens through which to view the intersections of digital governance and health equity. The observed enhancement in the utilization of BPHS among migrants due to GDT offers empirical support to the theory that digitization acts as an equalizer in public service delivery, aligning with the perspectives suggesting that digital platforms can diminish traditional barriers to service access, such as geographic location, socioeconomic status, and information asymmetry [58, 59]. This study challenges and extends the technology acceptance model by demonstrating that, in the context of public health, the perceived usefulness of digital services may indeed translate into higher usage rates, particularly among marginalized groups [60]. Moreover, it corroborates the propositions of diffusion of innovations theory, wherein digital governance serves as an innovation that has diffused through policy initiatives, leading to increased social and health benefits [61]. The findings provide a nuanced contribution to the equity-focused view of e-governance, arguing that when designed inclusively, digital tools can foster equitable health outcomes and can be particularly impactful for groups that may otherwise be excluded from traditional models of healthcare delivery [62, 63]. The implications of this study encourage a re-examination of existing theories, advocating for a broader consideration of digital governance’s role in enhancing equity, especially within heterogeneous populations [64, 65].

The policy implications emerging from this study are both significant and actionable. The empirical evidence underscores GDT’s capacity to enhance access to BPHS, highlighting the importance of integrating digital strategies into health sector reforms. Given the differential responses observed among various migrant subgroups, targeted digital literacy programs would be especially beneficial. For instance, older migrants, who may have lower digital literacy, would benefit from tailored educational initiatives that improve their ability to navigate digital health services. Similarly, targeted efforts to simplify digital interfaces for migrants with lower education levels could help bridge accessibility gaps, ensuring that digital health services are more user-friendly for all literacy levels. Digital platforms could also be adapted to provide information in accessible formats, thereby enhancing health service reach and engagement among populations with diverse educational backgrounds.

In addition to individual-level interventions, regional differences in economic development and population size suggest a need for localized policy approaches. Policymakers in lower-GDP or smaller-population areas, where GDT’s impact on BPHS utilization was most significant, should prioritize investments in digital health infrastructure to maximize benefits for underserved populations. These areas may also benefit from regionally tailored health education programs that address specific health needs and resource constraints. In contrast, larger or economically developed cities could focus on enhancing digital service quality and integrating advanced technologies, such as telemedicine, to provide comprehensive healthcare access. By aligning digital health initiatives with both demographic and regional characteristics, policymakers can promote a more inclusive and equitable health system that addresses the diverse needs of China’s migrant populations.

The study presents significant advancements in the field of digital governance and health service utilization by addressing crucial gaps in the existing literature. Prior research in this domain has largely been theoretical, with a paucity of empirical evidence to substantiate the theories postulated about the benefits of digital governance. Furthermore, there has been a noticeable deficiency in the examination of heterogeneous effects among different demographic groups, particularly within transient populations like migrants. This study breaks new ground by providing robust empirical support to the theoretical assertions that GDT positively impacts the utilization of BPHS among migrants [9, 66]. It extends the understanding of digital governance beyond a one-dimensional perspective to reveal how its benefits are not uniformly distributed but are instead more pronounced within certain subgroups, such as women, older individuals, and the less educated [10, 67]. By bringing empirical insights into a discussion that has been largely theoretical, this study not only validates the theories of digital inclusivity and equity but also challenges them, calling for a nuanced approach to understanding and implementing digital governance strategies [19, 68]. This empirical investigation thus marks a novel contribution by shedding light on the specific needs and responses of diverse groups within the migrant population, offering a more intricate and comprehensive picture of the dynamics at play in the intersection of digital transformation and public health [69, 70].

This study acknowledges several limitations that could impact the interpretation of our findings. First, the binary measurement of GDT is relatively simplistic, as it does not capture the multidimensional nature of digital transformation. A binary variable may overlook variations in e-government services, the quality of digital infrastructure, or levels of digital literacy among citizens, which could influence BPHS utilization differently across regions. Future research would benefit from developing a more nuanced GDT index to assess the specific aspects of digital transformation that most significantly impact BPHS uptake. Additionally, the cross-sectional nature of the data limits our ability to examine the long-term effects of GDT on BPHS utilization. Longitudinal data would allow for a more dynamic analysis, revealing how GDT’s impact on public health service access may evolve over time.

Second, potential measurement errors in the dependent variables (EHR and RFD) and the untested assumption that migrants in GDT-affected cities are engaging with digital platforms pose additional limitations. While we assume that digital access fosters greater BPHS utilization among migrants, direct measurement of digital platform engagement was not available in this study. Future research could address this gap by incorporating indicators of digital engagement, such as platform usage frequency and types of digital health services accessed, to assess the robustness of this assumption. Moreover, using panel data in future studies would allow for an exploration of how GDT affects both immediate and long-term health service outcomes among migrant populations, offering a deeper understanding of the sustained impact of digital transformation policies.

Conclusions

This study systematically examined the impact of GDT on BPHS utilization among China’s migrant population, demonstrating that GDT initiatives significantly improve engagement with essential health services, such as the EHR and RFD. Our analysis revealed that the benefits of GDT are particularly pronounced among specific subgroups, including women, migrants with lower education levels, older individuals, and those from rural backgrounds, emphasizing GDT’s potential to mitigate health disparities. At the regional level, our findings indicate that GDT’s effects are stronger in cities with lower GDP and smaller populations, suggesting that digital health initiatives may provide the greatest marginal benefit in economically or demographically constrained areas. By addressing these variations in impact, this study contributes empirical evidence to the field, highlighting the role of digital governance in addressing the diverse needs of a mobile and underserved population.

In addition to these heterogeneity insights, the study explored mechanisms through which GDT enhances BPHS access, identifying HIA as a significant mediator. The results suggest that digital platforms facilitate migrants’ access to public health information, thereby bridging informational gaps and increasing healthcare engagement. These findings not only support the integration of digital strategies into public health reforms but also underscore the importance of targeted interventions, such as digital literacy programs, to maximize the accessibility and effectiveness of GDT initiatives across different migrant demographics. Policymakers can leverage these insights to design inclusive digital health strategies that cater to both individual and regional needs, ultimately contributing to a more equitable health system.

Future research should aim to address some limitations identified in this study. First, using longitudinal data could reveal how GDT impacts BPHS utilization over time, allowing for the assessment of long-term outcomes. Additionally, a more nuanced measure of GDT, incorporating factors like digital infrastructure quality and citizen digital literacy, would enable a deeper understanding of how specific components of digital transformation influence health access. Finally, further studies could explore the interaction between digital health services and social integration, examining how improved healthcare access influences migrants’ economic and social participation. By expanding on these areas, future research can contribute to a more robust understanding of digital governance’s potential in enhancing public health equity.
