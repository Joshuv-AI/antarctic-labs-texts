Encapsulated citrus peel essential oil in pegylated mesoporous silica nanoparticles for postharvest control of Botrytis cinerea in strawberry
Authors: Farzaneh Jokarshourijeh, Leila Ma’mani, Farzaneh Ahmadpanah, Naghme Save-Shemshaki, Leila Soleimanpour
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Nanotechnology-based pesticide delivery systems provide controlled release of agrochemicals along with enhanced stability, target specificity, and reduced environmental impact. Strawberries (Fragaria × ananassa), valued for their flavor, nutritional content, and antioxidant properties, face a short shelf-life and significant postharvest losses due to Botrytis cinerea (B. cinerea)-induced decay, posing a challenge to producers. This study reports the development of a biocompatible, botanical nano-formulated fungicide (BNF) to manage B. cinerea, as a resistant post-harvest pathogen. BNF, encapsulating citrus peel essential oil (CEO) within a biocompatible inorganic-organic matrix consisting of silica nanoporous and polyethylene glycol (PEG), was characterized using scanning electron microscopy (SEM), transmission electron microscopy (TEM), Thermogravimetric Analysis (TGA), Gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) analysis, and Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) and Barrett-Joyner-Halenda (BJH) methods. To evaluate the efficacy of our treatments against B. cinerea, Signum (BASF), a commercial fungicide, was used as a positive control. Its antifungal efficacy was assessed at four different concentrations (0, 5000, 7000, and 10000 mg L−1, when BNF contains 10% of the active ingredient) and Signum (1000 mg L−1) against B. cinerea on strawberry flowers and postharvest fruits. Results indicate that BNF at 5000, 7000, and 10,000 mg L⁻¹ significantly inhibited the growth of B. cinerea (p ≤ 0.01) and reduced fruit spoilage. Notably, BNF at a concentration of 10,000 mg L⁻¹ achieved 74.03% inhibition, closely comparable to Signum (77.52% inhibition), while the water-treated control (BNF0) showed only minimal inhibition (13.01%). These findings demonstrate BNF’s potential as an effective and sustainable alternative to conventional fungicides for managing B. cinerea in strawberry production, with a recommended dose of 10,000 mg L⁻¹ for optimal efficacy. This study demonstrates that BNF is a promising eco-friendly alternative to synthetic fungicides for controlling B. cinerea, enhancing strawberry shelf life, and reducing chemical residues, with potential utilization in other crops.

Introduction

The global agricultural sector faces a critical challenge in developing sustainable crop protection strategies that balance efficacy with environmental and food safety concerns1. While synthetic pesticides have been instrumental in ensuring food security by controlling pests and enhancing crop yields2,3, their persistent residues pose significant risks to human health and ecosystems4. This is particularly problematic in postharvest management, where conventional fungicides leave undesirable residues on ready-to-consume produce5. Such concerns have driven research efforts toward biological alternatives, among which plant essential oils have emerged as promising candidates due to their antimicrobial properties and favorable safety profiles6,7.

Among postharvest pathogens, B. cinerea represents a significant challenge. This necrotrophic fungus, ranked as the second most economically significant fungal pathogen worldwide8,9, causes devastating gray mold disease in strawberries, with yield losses exceeding 80% under favorable conditions10,11. The pathogen’s broad host range and destructive capacity result in estimated global economic losses of 10 − 100 billion dollars annually12,13. The current reliance on chemical fungicides has contributed to widespread resistance development and environmental contamination14. For instance, strawberry growers in Florida spend approximately $1000 per acre per season on these treatments15. As an alternative, citrus peel essential oil has demonstrated notable antifungal activity against B. cinerea, primarily attributed to its high concentrations of bioactive compounds such as limonene. However, the practical application of this volatile oil is limited by its poor stability and rapid degradation7.

Essential oils (EOs), though valued for their broad-spectrum bioactivities, suffer from limited practical applicability due to high volatility, low water solubility, and pronounced physicochemical instability. Exposure to light, heat, and humidity accelerates oxidative degradation, leading to loss of active components and diminished efficacy. These limitations also compromise formulation stability under environmental conditions. To overcome these challenges, nanotechnology offers innovative encapsulation systems that enhance the stability and controlled release of essential oils16,17. Recent advancements in nanotechnology offer promising solutions for sustainable agriculture and food science14,18,19. By leveraging nanoencapsulation techniques for fertilizer and pesticide delivery20,21, researchers aim to enhance efficacy of agrochemicals, improving agro-systems and reducing losses from biotic and abiotic stresses while boosting crop yields. Nanomaterials have enabled the development of next-generation pesticides with slow-release properties22, environmental compatibility, and improved efficacy in agricultural productivity23. Among these, silicon-based materials, particularly mesoporous silica nanoparticles (MSNs), have gained attention due to their biocompatibility, chemical stability, and adaptability24,25.

Since the discovery of MCM-41 by Mobil, MSNs have attracted significant research interest26. Their unique properties—such as high surface area (approximately 1000 m²g⁻¹), large pore volume (approximately 1 cm³g⁻¹), adjustable pore size (2–50 nm), uniform particle distribution, and open-pore structures make them ideal for adsorption, immobilization, and targeted delivery applications. These advancements pave the way for precision agriculture, enabling targeted delivery of agrochemicals to optimize resource use and minimize environmental impact. MSNs, stabilized by strong Si–O bonds, exhibit tunable porosity, surface area, and pore sizes and can be synthesized cost-effectively27,28. Their applications in agriculture include mitigating UV-B stress29, detoxifying heavy metals30, alleviating salinity stress31, and reducing dehydration effects32, making them a valuable tool for sustainable farming33,34. These nanoparticles disperse easily in water, making them suitable for impregnation in aqueous solutions. One of the earliest agricultural applications of MSNs involved loading imidacloprid onto these nanoparticles for effective termite control35. This pioneering application underscores the versatility of MSNs, opening avenues for further exploration in pest management and crop protection strategies. In recent years, MSNs have been widely utilized for the controlled encapsulation and release of drugs and chemicals36. Additionally, inorganic nanoparticles have been employed in DNA and gene delivery into plant and animal cells. Such multifaceted applications highlight the potential of MSNs to bridge agricultural and biomedical fields, advancing both food security and therapeutic innovations37. MSNs is selected for encapsulating essential oils due to its distinctive structural and functional properties that improve delivery and controlled release processes. Its highly ordered nanoporous structure offers a large surface area and adjustable pore sizes, facilitating efficient encapsulation and sustained release of active compounds such as essential oils38. The synthesis and characterization of MSNs-based delivery nanosystems enable customized drug release profiles, making it suitable for therapeutic applications39. Also, The feasibility of encapsulating essential oils in functional silica nanocontainers is demonstrated, highlighting their capability to protect volatile compounds and regulate release rates effectively40. For example, the practical use of cinnamon oil encapsulated in silica nanoparticles confirms enhanced stability and insecticidal activity, emphasizing the material versatility and effectiveness in preserving bioactivity41.

Beyond their established efficacy in controlling B. cinerea in strawberry production, essential oil-loaded MSNs offer diverse potential in sustainable agriculture and other fields. These nanocarriers can effectively target phytopathogens like Fusarium spp.42,43 or act as eco-friendly biopesticides against insect pests, particularly when derived from sustainable silica sources44. In food preservation, MSNs loaded with essential oils such as cinnamon or lemongrass significantly extend shelf life by inhibiting microbial proliferation45,46. Biomedically, they enable targeted delivery of therapeutic essential oils for antimicrobial or anti-inflammatory applications47. Collectively, these multifunctional attributes position mesoporous silica as a superior and versatile platform for the encapsulation and controlled release of essential oils. Therefore, by leveraging the advantages of polyethylene glycol (PEG)- is a water soluble, FDA-approved and nontoxic reagent, and MSNs, this study focuses on the development nano-encapsulated citrus peel essential oil using PEGylated MSNs as a sustainable bio-fungicide for control of B. cinerea in strawberries. The study aims to optimize the design and fabrication of BNF via PEGylation of MSNs, focusing on loading efficiency, stability, and evaluating its antifungal efficacy against B. cinerea in controlled greenhouse trials. Its established performance against B. cinerea makes it an ideal benchmark for comparing the effectiveness of novel treatments in this study.

Experimental section

Materials and equipments

Tetraethyl ortho silicate (TEOS), triton x-100 (50%) and Pluronic, P123 were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich and Citrus essential oil were purchased from Jahad Daneshgahi Alborz, Iran. The necessary solutions were prepared using deionized water (DW). The particle size distribution of BNF was determined using dynamic light scattering (DLS, Brookhaven, USA). For this analysis, solid nanomaterials were dispersed in deionized water and sonicated in an ultrasonic bath for 10 min to ensure uniform suspension. Morphology and structural characteristics were examined using a JEOL 1200 EX Transmission Electron Microscope (TEM) (JEOL Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) operating at an accelerating voltage of 80 kV. A 10 µL aliquot of the sample (diluted 1:100) was placed on a 400-mesh copper grid coated with a carbon support film (Agar Scientific, Stansted, UK). For scanning electron microscopy (SEM), the nanoparticles were dispersed in ethanol, air-dried, stored under vacuum for 24 h, and then imaged. Topographical features of BNF were further investigated using atomic force microscopy (AFM; DMEDs 95 − 50, Denmark) under ambient conditions. For this purpose, a sonicated suspension of solid nanomaterials was used. Signum, a commercial fungicide containing boscalid and pyraclostrobin, was selected as a positive control due to its well-documented broad-spectrum activity and efficacy as a protectant and systemic fungicide, which effectively suppresses fungal pathogens through synergistic inhibition of spore germination, mycelial growth, and disease progression.

Synthesis of encapsulated citrus peel essential oil using pegylated mesoporous silica nanoparticles (BNF)

MSN synthesis

4.0 g of n-butanol was added to a clear solution of P123 (4.0 g) and 35 wt% HCl (7.9 g) in DW (144 g), and allowed to stir for 1 h at 35 °C. Then, TEOS (8.6 g) was poured to the clear solution followed by vigorous stirring at 35 °C for 24 h. The mixture was aged at 100 °C for 24 h under static conditions. The resulting white precipitate was filtered without additional washing and dried under vacuum overnight. The template was removed by calcination at 550 °C for 6 h.

PEG600–silane reagent synthesis

PEG600–silane was synthesized via a hydrogen-transfer nucleophilic addition reaction between the terminal hydroxyl group of PEG600 and the isocyanate group of 3-(triethoxysilyl)propyl isocyanate, as described preciously48.

PEGylated MSN synthesis

A solution of 0.1 g PEG600–silane in 30 mL ethanol was added dropwise to a vigorously stirring solution containing 25 mg of MSN in 30 mL of ethanol/water. After stirring for 24 h, the mixture was filtered, thoroughly washed, and the white residue was dried at 100 °C under vacuum for 12 h to obtain PEGylated MSN.

Encapsulation of citrus peel essential oil

A solution of 150 mg of citrus peel oil CEO in acetone was added to a mixture containing 500 mg of PEGylated MSN in DW, and was stirred overnight. The solid was then centrifuged, washed thoroughly with ethanol, and dried in a vacuum oven to give BNF. In the following, the amount of CEO for BNF was determined by 25% by elemental analysis.

Preparation of BNF solution sample for application

To prepare the samples for use, the required amounts of BNF were dispersed in a combination of water and tween 80 (100:0.5, respectively), and sonicated for 10 min. The antifungal efficacy of BNF was assessed at 0, 5000, 7000, and 10,000 mg L-1 concentrations against B. cinerea on strawberry flowers and fruits. To convenience, the treatments were BNF0 (distilled water, 0 mg L⁻¹, serving as the control with no BNF enhancement), BNF0.5 (5000 mg L⁻¹, representing a low-dose BNF), BNF0.7 (7000 mg L⁻¹, an intermediate-dose treatment for BNF), BNF1 (10000 mg L⁻¹, a high-dose BNF treatment).

Loading efficiency

The encapsulation efficiency of PEGylated MSN was evaluated, revealing that approximately 80% of the CEO was successfully encapsulated. To confirm the presence and integrity of the encapsulated CEO, gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) analysis was performed. GC-MS was used to analyze the chemical composition of the extracted CEO from BNF, and the results were compared with the standard CEO profile. The method allowed the identification and quantification of CEO components, confirming the successful encapsulation and preservation of its chemical structure. The encapsulation process played a key role in ensuring the stability and sustained releases of CEO components within the nanoformulation.

Greenhouse treatment of strawberry plants

A greenhouse experiment was conducted to evaluate the efficacy of BNF in controlling B. cinerea infections, including flower blight, deformities, and fruit rot in strawberry plants.

The study involved four treatment groups: BNF (at four concentrations), Signum as reference chemical-fungicide, an infected control, and a healthy control. The experiment utilized a completely randomized design with four replications, each consisting three plants. As soon as flowering began, a spore spray was applied to simulate pathogen challenge. Under favorable conditions for B. cinerea (temperatures around 15–24 °C and high humidity)49, infection can establish. After 48 h, a fungicide spray was applied to control the onset of gray mold. At the flower initiation stage, strawberry plants were inoculated with a B. cinerea spore suspension (2 × 106 spores/mL). Foliar spraying of BNF and Signum were performed at seven-day intervals until fruit formation.

To evaluate the effect of the treatments, the number of infected and healthy flowers was counted. This evaluation was carried out for four weeks until the fruit formation stage. Fruit evaluation was carried out based on the percentage of infection severity, comparison with the healthy control, fruit shelf life after harvest, and adverse effects of the substances on plant growth and production for each treatment.

Postharvest treatment of strawberry fruits

Strawberry cultivar: Camarosa (Fragaria × ananassa). Grown in greenhouse using a soilless substrate containing coco-peat (coir) for optimal drainage and root health50. When the fruit was ripe, it was red, and healthy, similarly sized fruits were selected.

To assess the efficacy of BNF in prolonging fruit shelf life and controlling disease, strawberry fruits were inoculated with a B. cinerea spore suspension and treated with BNF after 24 h. The treated fruits were stored under controlled conditions: 24 h at 20 °C, followed by 72 h at 10 °C, and an additional 24 h at 25 °C. The effects of BNF were evaluated based on disease incidence and control effectiveness.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SAS 9.4. A one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed, and Duncan’s multiple range test (p ≤ 0.01) was used to identify significant differences between treatment means.

Results and discussions

Preparation and characterization of BNF

The BNF was synthesized and evaluated to control of B. cinerea in strawberries fruits (Scheme 1) characterized to evaluate its morphological, structural, and size distribution properties using various techniques, including SEM, TEM, AFM, BET/BJH, TGA, and DLS. TEM and SEM analyses (Fig. 1a and b) revealed that BNF particles, utilizing MSNs (specifically KIT-6) as a carrier, exhibited a uniform, spherical morphology with a well-distributed structure. TEM analysis estimated the well-defined, cubic lattice honeycomb-like network with uniform nano-channels (Fig. 1a). SEM images further verified the spherical and uniform structure, indicating a robust MSN-based network that enhances the encapsulation and controlled release of CEO (Fig. 1b). X-ray diffraction (XRD) was employed to confirm the ordered mesoporous frameworks of nanocarrier and BNF. The XRD patterns clearly correspond to the reflections of the 3D cubic symmetry of the Ia3d space group. Specifically, a sharp peak accompanied by a distinct shoulder peak, appears at 2θ = 0.88° and 2θ = 0.96°, attributed to the (211) and (220) diffractions, respectively. Additionally, a low-intensity, higher-order diffraction peak, indicating the (332) reflection, is observed at 2θ = 1.67°. Apart from the expected reduction in peak intensity, no significant changes were noted during the post-modification processes. The positions of the XRD peaks remained essentially unchanged, confirming the retention of KIT-6’s mesoporous structure after functionalization.

AFM imaging (Fig. 1c) demonstrated a uniform surface topology with high dispersity of the BNF particles. The smooth and evenly distributed morphology observed via AFM indicates a well-dispersed nanoformulation, attributed to the MSN carrier’s ability to prevent aggregation and ensure stable dispersion in aqueous solutions, this is critical for achieving effective fungicidal activity and prolonged stability. DLS analysis (Fig. 1d) showed a narrow size distribution, with the average particle size falling within the nanometer range. This finding aligns with the SEM and TEM results, confirming the consistency of particle size and suitability for nanoencapsulation.

BET and BJH methods are used to determine the surface area, pore volume, and pore size distribution of silica nanoporous-based carrier before and after CEO loading via nitrogen adsorption-desorption isotherms (Fig. 1e). The PEGylated MSN nanocarrier exhibited a high specific surface area of 524.6 m²g−1, a pore diameter of 4.12 nm, and a total pore volume of 0.485 cm³g−1, as determined by BET and BJH methods. The substantial reductions in surface area and pore diameter indicate that the cargo (CEO) occupies a portion of the mesoporous nano-channels. However, the unchanged shape of the hysteresis loop in the N2 adsorption–desorption isotherm suggests that the pore structure, characteristic of the cubic Ia3d symmetry of PEGylated MSN, remains intact during CEO-uploading to produce BNF. TGA results for BNF revealed two distinct weight loss peaks including one at 110 °C, attributed to water desorption, and another at 395 °C, corresponding to the decomposition of the organic groups. These results indicate a PEG loading of 40% and CEO loading approximately about 9%, further validated by GC analysis (Fig. 1f).

These characterization results confirm the successful formulation of BNF, with uniform morphology, nanoscale particle size, and high dispersity, making it a promising candidate for fungicidal applications. The high surface area and tunable porosity of PEGylated MSNs facilitate efficient loading of CEO, with approximately 80% encapsulation efficiency as determined by GC-MS analysis. GC-MS analysis of the CEO extracted from BNF revealed a characteristic terpenoid profile predominantly composed of monoterpene hydrocarbons (MH), accompanied by minor fractions of oxygenated monoterpenes (MO) and sesquiterpenes (Table 1). The analysis identified 17 major components accounting for 99.81% of the total essential oil composition, indicating excellent chromatographic resolution and compositional clarity. Among the identified components, limonene was the most abundant compound (87.08%), aligning with its typical dominance in essential oil from the citrus genus.

The high concentration of this monoterpene hydrocarbon is particularly significant, considering its well-established antifungal mechanisms, including cell membrane disruption, interference with ergosterol biosynthesis, and induction of reactive oxygen species (ROS) formation. This suggests a potent fungicidal potential of BNF based on its limonene-rich profile. In addition to limonene, several secondary monoterpene hydrocarbons were detected, including β-myrcene (0.82%), α-pinene (0.52%), β-pinene (0.75%), and sabinene (0.25%). While these compounds are present in relatively lower concentrations, they may exert synergistic antifungal effects through complementary mechanisms of action such as disruption of fungal signaling pathways or inhibition of spore germination. The oxygenated monoterpene fraction, comprising 6.43% of the total composition, included bioactive constituents such as cis- and trans-limonene oxide isomers (combined 3.40%), α-terpineol (1.62%), and terpinen-4-ol (0.41%). These compounds are widely reported for their broad-spectrum antifungal properties, including inhibition of hyphal growth and interference with fungal enzymatic systems. Although the sesquiterpene content was minimal (0.98%), the presence of β-bisabolene and α-bergamotene may play supportive roles in enhancing the overall chemical stability and bioavailability of the essential oil within the nano-formulated system. This compositional analysis demonstrates that CEO selected for encapsulation possesses an appropriate phytochemical profile as the principal fungicidal agent. while the high limonene content suggests particular efficacy against a broad spectrum of fungal pathogens, the complementary contributions of minor constituents offer potential for multifaceted antifungal activity, reinforcing the oil’s suitability for integration into nanoformulations aimed at fungal pathogen control.

Evaluating of BNF against Botrytis cinerea

As schematically illustrated in Fig. 2 and summary of the structural characteristics (Table 2), following the successful synthesis and characterization of BNF, the potential of this nanoformulation in controlling blossom blight disease caused by B. cinerea infection in strawberry was evaluated under greenhouse conditions at four weeks. The antifungal activity of BNF was assessed at four concentrations including 0, 5000, 7000, and 10,000 mg L−1. According to Fig. 2, which depicts the effect of treatments on B. cinerea blight incidence in strawberry blossoms, the lowest level of B. cinerea infection, or equivalently the highest proportion of healthy blossoms, was observed in the healthy control across all four weeks of evaluation. The lowest efficacy was recorded in the BNF0. In the first and second weeks, following the healthy control, the Signum and BNF1 treatments exhibited the highest efficacy, with no significant difference between them. However, in the third and fourth weeks, the Signum treatment performed slightly better than BNF1. Additionally, in the first week, the BNF0 and BNF0.5 treatments showed no significant difference. However, in the second, third, and fourth weeks, the BNF0.5 treatment demonstrated significantly greater efficacy in controlling B. cinerea blight compared to the BNF0 treatment. According to the results presented in Fig. 3, which illustrates the effect of treatments on controlling strawberry fruit blight, the highest control efficacy, or equivalently the lowest fruit blight incidence, was observed in the healthy control. Following the healthy control, the Signum treatment exhibited the highest efficacy, showing no significant difference compared to the BNF1 treatment. The lowest efficacy was recorded in the BNF0 treatment. Due to the absence of contamination in the healthy control, the highest percentage of healthy fruits (90%) was observed in this treatment. These findings highlight the promising potential of BNF as an effective alternative to conventional chemical fungicides in managing B. cinerea. Particularly at higher concentrations, BNF demonstrates strong antifungal activity, closely mirroring the performance of Signum. This suggests that the developed BNF could serve as a sustainable, eco-friendly option for postharvest disease management in strawberries and potentially other crops.

The efficacy assays demonstrated that BNF effectively inhibited B. cinerea on both blossoms and fruits, with its antifungal performance showing a clear concentration-dependent trend. Notably, BNF1 (10000 mg L−1) exhibited efficacy comparable to that of Signum (1000 mg L⁻¹), a conventional chemical fungicide, suggesting that BNF can serve as a promising eco-friendly alternative to conventional synthetic treatments.

In this study, the antifungal activity of BNF was evaluated at concentrations of 0, 5000, 7000, and 10,000 mg L⁻¹ in postharvest controlling of B. cinerea in strawberry fruits, with results compared to those of Signum (1000 mg L⁻¹) and a healthy control group (Fig. 4).

BNF demonstrated significantly enhanced antifungal efficacy at concentrations of 7000 and 10,000 mg L⁻¹, where it achieved inhibition levels comparable to Signum. This suggests that the BNF is highly effective in suppressing B. cinerea, particularly at higher doses. The MSN-based delivery system plays a key role in improving foliar adhesion and cellular uptake of CEO, which is further supported by the uniform particle size of 45–50 nm observed in TEM analysis. These findings highlight the potential of BNF as a sustainable and efficient bio-fungicidal agent for postharvest disease management, offering a viable alternative to conventional chemical fungicides while reducing environmental impact.

The performance of BNF represents a considerable improvement over conventional botanical formulations, whereas field efficacies typically range from 40% to 60%. Furthermore, BNF offers distinct environmental advantages, including reduced chemical residues and compatibility with sustainable agricultural practices51. The successful development of BNF as an effective bio-fungicide against B. cinerea opens promising avenues for its integration into current strawberry production systems. BNF can be applied via foliar sprays during the flowering stage to protect blossoms or as postharvest dips to extend fruit shelf life- methods that are fully compatible with existing equipment such as boom sprayers and dipping tanks commonly used by growers. Preliminary observations indicate good tank-mix stability with standard adjuvants; however, formal compatibility testing is required to ensure consistent performance under diverse field conditions.

The nanoscale properties of BNF may enhance foliar penetration and surface coverage, potentially allowing for reduced application frequency compared to conventional fungicides. This could lead to lower labor and input costs for producers. Nevertheless, practical implementation will require optimization of application rates and timing, particularly to align with integrated pest management (IPM) strategies. Integration the BNF with cultural practices- such as proper ventilation and sanitation —could further enhance disease control efficacy. These attributes position BNF as a practical, eco-friendly, and sustainable tool for strawberry producers, though field trials under commercial conditions may be essential to validate its efficacy, application protocol, and ease of integration into real-world farming practices.

PEGylated MSN nanocarrier enhances the practical applicability of BNF by ensuring stability in aqueous dispersions, as demonstrated by DLS and AFM analyses. Moreover, it enables targeted delivery to infection sites—critical for effective foliar and postharvest applications. The nanoencapsulation approach successfully addressed two major limitations of botanical fungicides: environmental instability and poor bioavailability. As demonstrated, unformulated limonene undergoes rapid oxidative degradation, which compromises its antimicrobial activity52. Based on literature, our formulation overcomes the challenge of rapid essential oil volatilization by enhancing stability and enabling slow release53 of limonene, a crucial advantage for sustained antifungal efficacy in strawberry field applications.

TEM and SEM characterizations revealed uniformly distributed nanoporous nature that significantly enhance both foliar adhesion and cellular uptake of bioactive compounds, consistent with previous reports on nano-encapsulated essential oils54. GC-MS analysis confirmed the preservation of the natural chemical diversity of citrus peel oil within the nano-delivery system, suggesting a valuable multi-site mode of action. This complex mixture may simultaneously disrupt fungal cell membranes55,56, impair mitochondrial function, and interfere with cell wall integrity15, thereby establishing multiple barriers against resistance development. Recent studies on encapsulation further support this strategy, showing enhanced antimicrobial activity of nano-formulated d-limonene compared to its bulk counterparts57. The economic implications are particularly compelling.

Given that B. cinerea control accounts for approximately 60% of fungicide expenditures in crops such as wild blueberries58, and strawberry growers spend an estimated $1,000 per season on preharvest treatments15, BNF’s nanoscale properties offer the potential to improve penetration and coverage, thus reducing application frequency and overall costs compared to conventional fungicides. This economic benefit is further strengthened by growing consumer demand for residue-free produce59,60, and increasing regulatory restrictions on conventional synthetic fungicides, collectively creating a favorable environment for the adoption of sustainable alternatives like BNF. These findings are further supported by our prior work conducted on the use of mineral-based formulations to control B. cinerea in strawberry crops, which similarly emphasized the importance of formulation in enhancing antifungal efficacy and environmental stability61.

The performance of BNF aligns with recent studies on nano-encapsulated essential oils for agricultural applications. For example, lemongrass and clove oils encapsulated in MSNs demonstrated up to threefold higher antifungal activity against Gaeumannomyces graminis var. tritici compared to their free counterparts. This enhancement was attributed to sustained release over 4–5 weeks, which significantly improved in vivo efficacy42. Similarly, MSN encapsulation of Ammoides pusila essential oil within MSN enhanced its inhibition of Fusarium avenaceum and suppressed enniatin production, further optimization was achieved through chitosan coatings, which fine-tuned release kinetics and bioavailability43.

PEGylated MSNs further amplify these benefits by enabling controlled release, reducing essential oil volatility, and ensuring sustained bioactivity44,63. This strategy is supported by parallel studies utilizing MSNs derived from sustainable sources — for example, clove oil-loaded MSNs synthesized from paddy straw nanosilica achieved 40% loading efficiency and demonstrated effective pest control, underscoring the scalability and environmental compatibility of agricultural waste-derived nanocarriers44. BNF’s potential as a sustainable alternative to synthetic fungicides such as Signum aligns with the expanding global demand for biopesticides, which offer target specificity, biodegradability, and minimal residue64,65. Regulatory frameworks, including those of the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA), already recognize citrus-derived compounds — such as limonene — as approved active ingredients in biopesticide formulations for postharvest antifungal application65.

As a nanobiopesticide, BNF enhances the bioavailability, potentially mitigating the high dosage requirements observed in this study (10,000 mg L⁻¹) through optimization of surface functionalization (e.g., PEGylation) or pore architecture38,44. Beyond agriculture, MSN-based delivery systems have shown promise in food preservation: coatings incorporating cinnamon or lemongrass essential oils extended the shelf life of sourdough bread by 10.5 days while maintaining a 95% antifungal index46— a compelling indicator of BNF’s potential to similarly prolong the postharvest quality of perishables like strawberries, though direct validation in this context remains pending. Further reinforcing BNF’s applicability, sugarcane molasses-derived essential oil encapsulated in MSNs achieved 80% encapsulation efficiency and delivered sustained antimicrobial activity66— highlighting the versatility of MSN platforms in stabilizing and enhancing the efficacy of diverse natural bioactives for postharvest management.

Safety remains a paramount concern for nanoparticles such as MSN-based bio-fungicides, particularly when applied to edible crops. MSNs composed of amorphous silica are generally recognized as safe (GRAS) by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA), exhibiting low cytotoxicity and favorable biodegradability profiles63,67. In vivo mouse studies confirm their biocompatibility, with no evidence of hepatotoxicity or organ accumulation at doses up to 100 mg/kg, and efficient clearance via renal and hepatic pathways68,69. Surface modification with polyethylene glycol (PEGylation) further enhances biocompatibility by minimizing immunogenicity and particle agglomeration — a strategy well-established in biomedical nanocarrier design for clinical translation67. However, dose- and size-dependent toxicity, such as oxidative stress or intestinal inflammation at higher exposures, highlights the need for optimized formulations62,70.

Ocular toxicity studies indicate reactive oxygen species (ROS)-mediated damage at high doses, though such risks are likely negligible in agricultural contexts, where topical application and low exposure doses predominate. Environmental assessments further suggest minimal ecological impact due to the chemical inertness and gradual degradation of MSNs in soil; nevertheless, potential effects on soil microbial communities warrant deeper investigation47,63. Although our study did not include direct toxicological or ecotoxicological assays, the existing body of evidence supports the potential low-risk profile of BNF for agricultural deployment47,67,68.

Despite these promising results, our study has limitations that warrant cautious interpretation. This study has not assessed the postharvest quality parameters-including strawberry taste, texture, or shelf life extension, nor environmental safety, such as ecotoxicity or soil microbial effects, limiting claims about BNF’s practical and ecological benefits62,70. These omissions constrain our ability to fully substantiate BNF’s agronomic and ecological benefits. Moreover, the relatively high dosage required for BNF efficacy (10000 mg L⁻¹) compared to synthetic benchmarks like Signum highlights a key area for optimization. Strategies such as advanced PEGylation, hybrid polymer coatings (e.g., chitosan), or pore-size engineering may significantly enhance delivery efficiency and reduce effective concentrations38,43. Additionally, natural variability in citrus peel essential oil composition — influenced by cultivar, season, and extraction method — may compromise batch-to-batch reproducibility and performance consistency. Addressing these gaps will be essential to translate BNF from a promising laboratory prototype into a commercially viable, sustainable tool for postharvest disease management. By doing so, BNF could play a transformative role in mitigating fungicide resistance, reducing synthetic chemical reliance, and supporting the global sustainability of high-value crops such as strawberries64,65.

Conclusion

This study establishes BNF as a groundbreaking sustainable bio-fungicide for postharvest management of B. cinerea in strawberries. By leveraging MSNs as a nanocarrier, BNF delivers citrus peel essential oil with enhanced stability and controlled release, achieving efficacy comparable to conventional fungicides such as Signum (74.03% vs. 77.52% inhibition). This breakthrough addresses key challenges in production and postharvest disease control while aligning with global demands for residue-free produce and sustainable agricultural practices. The findings demonstrate the antifungal efficacy of BNF at concentration of 10,000 mg L⁻¹ achieved 74.03% inhibition, (BNF contains 10% of active ingredient) against B. cinerea, providing a foundation for nanotechnology-enabled solutions in agriculture, with potential for eco-friendly applications pending further studies on strawberry quality and environmental safety. Future research should prioritize multi-season field trials to validate performance across diverse climates and cropping systems, assess biodegradability of PEGylated-MSN in soil environments, and optimize scalable, cost-effective production methods for commercial deployment.
