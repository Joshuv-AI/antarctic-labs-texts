Predictive role of health literacy and e-health literacy on the self-care of patients undergoing CABG using open heart surgery
Authors: Mohammad Hasanzadeh Naeini, Ghazanfar Rafiee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCoronary artery bypass graft surgery is a critical intervention for treatment of CAD. Self-care involves the knowledge and skills used daily to maintain health and self-esteem. Health literacy empowers patients to engage in health-related behaviors and perform appropriate self-care behaviors. This study aimed to assess how H-literacy and e-health literacy predict self-care in patients undergoing CABG surgery.MethodsA cross-sectional descriptive-analytical study was conducted using convenience sampling on 192 patients who provided written informed consent. The inclusion criteria included subjects who were at least 18 years of age and underwent CABG procedure for the first time, at least one month after the operation, were free of hearing or speech impairments, no history of mental illness, ability to read and write, have access to the Internet via a mobile device. The exclusion criteria included the performance of concomitant surgical procedures concurrently with CABG surgery, such as heart valve replacement, and the occurrence of acute surgical complications, including bleeding, infection, and acute kidney failure. Data collected through four questionnaires including a demographic information questionnaire, a health literacy questionnaire, an electronic health literacy questionnaire, and a self-care questionnaire.Results192 participants between ages 40 and 85, with a mean age of 63.35 ± 9.87 years, including 114 males and 184 females were studied. Notably, only 9.4% of the participants had obtained an education beyond the level of a diploma. A significant direct relationship was identified between health literacy and self-care (p < 0.001; r = 0.344), while no significant relationship was observed for e-health literacy (p = 0.255; r = 0.082). The findings of multiple linear regression indicated that health literacy had a positive influenced on self-care (coefficient = 0.92, p < 0.001), while e-health literacy did not (p < 0.059).ConclusionsThe findings indicate that health literacy is a significant predictor of self-care in coronary artery bypass graft surgery patients which highlights the need for healthcare officials to enhance public health literacy through educational initiatives.

Background

Coronary artery bypass graft (CABG) surgery is regarded as one of the most efficacious and pivotal techniques in the management of coronary artery diseases (CADs). In many cases, this surgical procedure is the sole means of treating and prolonging the lives of patients with CAD. Annually, over 8 million such surgeries are performed globally, with over 40,000 cases occurring in Iran [1]– [2]. It is anticipated that the demand for this surgical procedure will increase by approximately 5% annually on a global scale. By 2026, it is projected that the number of this surgery will exceed 1.3 million per year [3]. In the postoperative period, patients may experience a range of adverse effects, including chest discomfort, sleep disturbances, changes in appetite, respiratory issues, lower extremity edema, cardiac palpitations, paresthesia in the upper extremities, abdominal bloating, and weight loss [4]. The capacity of patients to avert complications and potential outcomes, in addition to adhering to dietary therapy, hinges on their ability to engage in self-care management [5].

Self-care is defined as activities that individuals learn through curiosity, education, and personal experience and perform to maintain their lives, health, and well-being. The objective of self-care is to equip individuals with the capacity to assume full responsibility for their health [6]. Engaging in suitable and appropriate self-care activities is vital for fostering positive health outcomes. These activities can assist in enhancing a patient’s adjustment, reducing role or performance disruptions in cardiac surgery patients, and preventing frequent hospitalizations [3].

The most significant factor influencing self-care behaviors is patients’ health literacy [7]. Health literacy has been shown to empower patients to engage in health-related behaviors and perform appropriate self-care behaviors [8]. In the context of chronic illness, health literacy and e-health literacy have emerged as powerful predictors of self-care behaviors [9]– [10]. Health literacy is a key factor in the development of skills and competencies that are necessary for the comprehension, evaluation, and utilization of health information and concepts, which in turn enhance the quality of life. Limited health literacy hinders a patient’s capacity to access fundamental information, accurately assess health risks, or grasp complex information, which ultimately affects their ability to engage in self-care [11].

Conversely, the advent of digital media and communication technology has facilitated greater access to information, with an increasing number of individuals seeking health-related data online [12]. The abundance of health information resources available on the Internet and the ease of access to them are prompting a gradual shift in the sources of health information used by people. Traditional sources of health information, such as newspapers, periodicals, and physicians’ offices, are being replaced by the Internet [13]. E-health literacy is a significant factor that can influence the efficacy of health information searches, as well as the nature of the information sought and satisfaction with the information obtained [14].

Individuals who demonstrate proficiency in e-health literacy are capable of employing web-based search strategies to ascertain reliable health information. E-health literacy enhances the efficacy of health services, curtails costs, and elevates the quality of services across diverse health sectors [15]. E-health resources serve as the primary source of health information pertaining to medicine and cardiovascular care. The Roadmap of the Global Heart Foundation identifies the potential impact of e-health on heart digital health by 2030, suggesting that it could reduce premature mortality caused by non-communicable diseases by one-third [16, 17].

To mitigate the incidence of adverse effects, it is imperative to enhance patients’ capacity for self-care following CABG surgery. This can be achieved by identifying a reliable and scientific source to address health-related inquiries and provide accurate and effective information. In this regard, the importance of health literacy and e-health literacy as a general health goal is increasingly recognized as a means of improving quality of life, enhancing interaction with the healthcare system, and ultimately reducing hospital visits and treatment costs, particularly in the 21 st century. The present study aimed to determine the predictive role of health literacy and e-health literacy in the self-care of patients undergoing CABG surgery at Yazd hospitals.

Methods

Study population

The population of this cross-sectional descriptive-analytical study comprised of patients undergoing CABG surgery using open-heart surgery in hospitals in Yazd. The sampling method was conducted through convenience. Despite its limited capacity for generalization, the convenience sampling method boast notable advantages, including its accessibility, ease of use, expeditiousness, and cost-effectiveness.

Subjects inclusion and exclusion criteria

A total of 192 participants who provided written informed consent, were at least 18 years of age, underwent CABG procedure for the first time, were at least one-month post-CABG operation, and free of hearing or speech impairments, had no history of mental illness, as determined by the patient’s own statements, the ability to read and write, access to the Internet via a mobile device, and ability to pay for and connect to the Internet participated.

Furthermore, participants who underwent concomitant surgical procedures in conjunction with CABG surgery, such as heart valve replacement, and the occurrence of acute surgical complications, including bleeding, infection, and acute kidney failure, which have the potential to adversely impact patients’ capacity for self-care during the initial postoperative period following CABG surgery were excluded from the study. Following the requisition of approval from the ethics committee of the University of Medical Sciences, as well as the procurement of the requisite ethical code and permits from the local University of Medical Sciences, the researcher proceeded to present the study at local hospitals where CABG surgery using open heart surgery was performed. Subsequently, the requisite arrangements were made to access the archive of patients’ documents, and information was collected on patients who had undergone CABG surgery. Due to constraints imposed by the number of patients and CABG surgeries, the sampling period was extended over a 6-month time-frame. On a monthly basis, the archives were referenced and information on new patients was obtained.

The data was collected from patients in two ways: first, given the fact that patients who have undergone CABG surgery must be periodically visited by their surgeon, the researcher was present in the clinics of the aforementioned hospitals. Patients who met the inclusion criteria and signed a written informed consent form, participated in the study. The researcher first formally introduced himself and elucidated the study’s design and objectives of the study to the participants. Subsequently, the participants were administered questionnaires, and the researcher was available to address any ambiguities or queries they might have had regarding the content. Second, in order to expedite the data collection process and to account for the willingness of some patients to participate in the study by telephone and at home, written informed consent was obtained from patients in the clinic. Patients who consented to participate were presented with each questionnaire item in a clear and impartial manner and they responded in a patient manner. In the event that a patient exhibited indications of fatigue, the scheduled discussion was deferred. The administration of the questionnaire necessitated a 30-minute allocation of patients’ time, while each telephone conversation was 45 min in duration. The confidentiality and non-disclosure of patients’ private information were guaranteed. The study data was collected through four questionnaires: a demographic information questionnaire, a health literacy questionnaire, an electronic health literacy questionnaire, and a self-care questionnaire.

Data collection

The demographic information questionnaire was utilized to collect participants’ demographic information, including gender, marital status, education level, economic status, employment status, place of residence, underlying medical conditions (diabetes, hypertension and thyroid disorders), history of previous surgeries, history of percutaneous coronary intervention (percutaneous coronary Intervention, formerly known as angioplasty with stent), and the duration of coronary artery disease. The collection of participants’ health literacy data entailed the calculation of the total score subject sample using the Likert scale items in the “basic skills dimension” (very easy = 5, quite difficult = 1) and “decision-making skills dimension” (always = 5 to never = 1), through the shortened form. The adult health literacy assessment tool developed by Tavossi et al. in 2022, was employed, which had a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.91 for the total items, 0.84 for basic skills, and 0.84 for decision-making skills [17]. In order to collect E-health literacy data from the patients, Norman et al.’s electronic health literacy questionnaire (2006) was utilized. This instrument focuses on eight distinct components, with each component being scored on a Likert scale ranging from very low (option 1) to very high (option 5). The questionnaire assessed participants’ knowledge and understanding of accessing health information sources on the Internet. The questionnaire inquired about patients’ awareness of, access to, utilization of, and confidence in the reliability of health-related resources, as well as their ability to discern between reliable and unreliable health-related internet resources [18]– [19]. In order to collect self-care data from patients, the researcher employed the self-care of patients undergoing CABG questionnaire, which was developed by Pooladi et al. in 2017. The questionnaire was divided into three sections: awareness, which included 14 items on a Likert scale where correct answers received 2 points, wrong answers received 0 points, and the answer, “I don’t know” received 1 point). The second section, titled “Attitude”, contained seven questions on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from “completely agree” to “completely disagree”. The third section, entitled “Application,” comprised (20 questions on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from “always” to “never”. The questionnaire exhibited a coefficient reliability Cronbach’s alpha of 0.7, indicating its internal consistency and reliability [20]. Prior to the implementation of the study, authorization was formally obtained from the designers of the questionnaire via electronic mail. Participants were subsequently administered questionnaires that pertained to their demographic profiles, health literacy, literacy, e-health, and self-care. To ensure the confidentiality of the participants, each completed questionnaire was assigned a unique code, thereby facilitating the identification of samples.

Statistical analysis

Quantitative variables were determined through the use of measures such as number, percentage, mean, and standard deviation. Qualitative variables were assessed in terms of frequency (as a percentage). The Shapiro-Wilk test was employed to ascertain the normality of the data distribution. In instances where the data exhibited non-normal distributions, non-parametric tests, such as the Mann-Whitney and Kruskal-Wallis tests, were utilized. In order to assess and compare participants’ health literacy levels, chi-square, t-tests, and analysis of variance (ANOVA) tests were conducted. The correlation between health literacy, e-literacy, and patients’ self-care behaviors was analyzed through correlation analysis. Multiple linear regression was utilized to identify variables such as age, education attainment and income. These variables were examined in relation to health literacy and e-health literacy, as well as education attainment, income, H-literacy, and e-health literacy in relation to the mean score of self-care. The present study sought to ascertain the extent to which health literacy and e-health literacy predict self-care behaviors. Statistical analysis was carried out using IBM SPSS statistics version 23 software (IBM Corp, Amonk, NY, USA) with a significance level set at 5%.

Results

A total of 192 patients, aged ranging in age from 40 to 85 years with a mean age of 63.35 ± 9.87 years, participated in this study. Of the participants, 114 (59.4%) were male, 184 (95.8%) were married, and 154 (80.2%) resided in urban areas. A mere 9.4% of patients had obtained an education beyond the level of a diploma, and 103 patients (53.6%) had a history of hypertension, diabetes mellitus, or both, while only 27 patients (14.1%) had a history of thyroid disorders. A total of 36 patients (18.8%) had a history of stent placement, and angioplasty. The mean duration of diagnosis prior to the need for CABG using open heart surgery, as determined by angiography, was 2.77 ± 3.79 months, with a range of 1 to 24 months. The ANOVA test further demonstrated a statistically significant difference in the mean health literacy scores among patients with varying educational backgrounds (p-value = 0.001). Specifically, patients with no literacy skills exhibited the lowest average health literacy score, while those with higher education demonstrated the highest. A similar outcome was observed in the e-health literacy score analysis, which also exhibited a statistically significant difference based on educational levels (p-value = 0.001). Patients with no literacy skills demonstrated the lowest e-health literacy score, while those with higher education levels exhibited the highest.

Furthermore, the lowest mean score for self-care was observed among patients with low income, while patients with high income exhibited the highest mean score. Discussion

The study found that the patients’ mean the health literacy score was moderate. This outcome is likely influenced by the education attainment, income, residing area, and age of the study participants. Several factors have been associated with health literacy levels. People with higher levels of education attainment, better jobs, and higher incomes tend to have better access to health information and resources with which to act. The strongest association was found between high education attainment and high health literacy. Low income, undesirable occupation, and specific race/ethnicity were also consistently associated with low health literacy [21]. In Iran, a correlation has been observed between health literacy scores in older adult patients and several demographic and socioeconomic factors, including monthly income, educational attainment, and the educational attainment of the patient’s spouse [22]. Adults with limited health literacy have been shown to receive reduced information from disease prevention and control materials, and they may face diminished access to screening and a reduced capacity to effectively manage their health conditions [23].

Patients residing in urban areas exhibited significantly higher health literacy scores compared to those residing in rural areas, indicating a distinct disparity between the two groups. These differences can be attributed to a multitude of factors, including but not limited to rurality, socioeconomic status, and healthcare resource availability. A 2010 study conducted in Jiangsu province, China, identified rural residency as a key predictor of low health literacy [24], while a recent study in Hebei province revealed that urban health literacy levels were 19% compared to just 7.94% in rural areas [25]. Furthermore, the findings of Wang W. et al. (2019) demonstrated that health literacy levels were significantly lower in rural populations across central China [26], This finding is congruent with the observations made in Iran [27]. The observed discrepancy may be attributed to various challenges confronting rural residents, including underdeveloped economies, inadequate access to healthcare services and information, and limited awareness of healthy lifestyles. Collectively, these factors contribute to the overall poor health literacy of rural populations [26, 28].

The mean health literacy score of patients lacking formal education was significantly lower than that of patients with higher levels of education suggesting that education level is associated with health literacy. Additionally, a positive and significant correlation was observed between health literacy and academic achievement in these two groups of patients, indicating that education may play a role in improving health literacy. Many studies have found that an individual’s health literacy is directly related to their education level. This is because education provides individuals with a greater number of skills that can help them understand complex situations, such as navigating the healthcare system [29]. Education has been identified as a modifiable risk factor that policymakers can target to improve health literacy.

Furthermore, a statistically significant inverse relationship was observed between the mean total score of patients’ health literacy and age, indicating that older patients exhibited a lower level of health literacy. While age is not considered a modifiable risk factor in itself, it is associated with lower health literacy. Nair et al. (2023) mentioned that inadequate health literacy was also significantly associated with advanced age [30]. These findings are consistent with the findings of multinational studies, which demonstrated comparable inadequate health literacy levels in China (37%), Malaysia (25%), and Brazil (22%) [31]– [32]. In these countries, inadequate health literacy has been demonstrated to be strongly associated with advanced age [31, 33]. Studies has indicated that cognitive decline can occur for up to a period of six weeks following cardiac surgery, exhibiting an incidence ranging from 20% to 50%. In contrast, a slight decline in cognitive function lasting from six months to a year, has been observed with an incidence of 10% to 30% [34]. As individuals age, they may encounter changes in their cognitive, visual, or auditory abilities, which can potentially impact their health literacy skills. Physical impairments, such as hearing and vision loss, may also contribute to a diminished capacity to process health information. Available evidence shows that age is related to cognitive impairment. Thus, older people find it difficult to understand or judge more complicated aspects related to health [35]. In Saudi Arabia, low health literacy has been observed to be associated with advanced age, specific geographical locations within the country, prior marital status, diminished educational attainment, constrained financial resources, and infrequent visits to healthcare providers, with individuals reporting a mean of three such visits in the preceding year [36]. However, advanced age has been demonstrated to be associated with a higher risk of low health literacy [37].

The study found that patients exhibited an average level of self-care ability, with a significant difference in mean self-care scores between patients with formal education, who scored higher, and those without formal education. Moreover, a direct and significant correlation was identified between self-care scores and education level. The findings underscore the crucial role of patient awareness in promoting self-care practices, which is a crucial factor in mitigating adverse health outcomes and reducing the frequency of hospitalizations. Bakhshayesh et al. (2023) observed that patients with higher levels of education exhibited higher mean scores in terms of self-care behaviors when compared to those with less formal education [38]. People with inadequate health literacy face challenges in engaging in self-care practices and adhering to medical directives [39]. It has been demonstrated that patients who engage in a greater number of self-care activities following coronary heart surgery demonstrate a reduction in postoperative complications, a decrease in the necessity for additional medical interventions, and a concomitant reduction in financial expenditures. This phenomenon has been demonstrated to result in an acceleration in patient recovery.

Furthermore, a significant difference in mean self-care scores was identified between low-income and high-income patients. Moreover, a direct and significant relationship was identified between patients’ total self-care scores and their income. Conversely, a statistical inverse relationship was noted between patients’ health literacy scores and their age, indicating that older patients demonstrated lower self-care abilities.

The study’s findings indicated that patients demonstrated inadequate e-health literacy, with individuals possessing lower levels of education exhibiting significantly lower e-health literacy scores compared to their higher-educated counterparts. Additionally, e-health literacy exhibited a positive correlation with being the family head, being employed, and having a higher income. Limited e-health literacy can result in adverse outcomes, including restricted access to information, ineffective disease management, diminished trust in the healthcare system, feelings of loneliness and isolation, decreased quality of life, and increased financial strain on both patients and the healthcare system.

The findings indicated a significant direct relationship between e-health literacy and self-care behaviors, indicating that an enhancement in e-health literacy is concomitant with an increase in self-care behaviors. A study conducted by Cho and Ha in South Korea on patients with hypertension revealed a significant direct association between e-health literacy and the patients’ self-care behaviors and health awareness [40]. The findings of a study showed that, while no significant correlation was observed between education attainment and self-care behaviors an increase in education level was associated with an enhancement in self-care behaviors [41].

Open-heart surgery has experienced challenging for patients in Saudi Arabia, frequently resulting in adoption of moderate self-care behaviors following discharge [42]– [43]. Studies have demonstrated that heightened cognitive deterioration subsequent to cardiac surgery has the potential to impede an individual’s capacity to engage in self-care activities. Previous research has demonstrated that patients who have undergone cardiac surgery exhibit suboptimal self-care behaviors following discharge [44]– [45].

In addition to ensuring timely prescription refills and adhering to medication schedules, common self-care behaviors among cardiac patients after discharge include avoiding physical exertion, monitoring incisions for infection, and promptly notifying healthcare providers of any concerning symptoms. A nutrient-rich diet is imperative for recovery, as is gradually resuming daily activities like walking to facilitate physical rehabilitation. Regular follow-up appointments are essential for ongoing health assessment, and emotional support from family and friends has been shown to significantly enhance the healing process [42]– [43]. Nevertheless, the implementation of self-care behaviors, including physician contact during instances of nausea and vomiting, the integration of a reminder system to facilitate medication adherence, the application of diverse techniques to enhance sleep quality, and daytime rest, was found to be among the least frequently engaged in by patients post-discharge [42].

Inadequate self-care practices have been demonstrated to result in suboptimal health outcomes and recurrent hospitalizations [46]. Conversely, patients who demonstrate sufficient capacity for self-care are likely to exhibit an enhanced quality of life and a reduced risk of mortality [47].

The quality of patients’ participation in self-care behaviors may be a contributing factor to the elevated rate of readmissions to the hospital following cardiac surgery [48]. Prolonged hospitalization due to impaired cardiac surgery patients’ ability to perform their roles and function in daily life is a common occurrence, which can be attributed to a lack of adherence to proper self-care behaviors. Proper self-care behaviors are crucial for promoting positive health outcomes, and include increasing adaptability and reducing impairment in role or functioning. Unfortunately, patients may not participate in the required self-care behaviors during the first few months after CABG surgery and/or valve replacement [1]. It is important to note that, in contrast valve replacement surgery, which is performed on patients of all ages ranging from infants to the elderly, the population of this study consisted exclusively of patients undergoing CABG using open heart surgery, who were predominantly middle-aged to elderly. This discrepancy in age groups may have contributed to the observed differences in self-care and health literacy among CABG surgery patients.

Limitations

The present study was conducted exclusively on patients who were undergoing CABG surgery. The study did not include other patients undergoing heart surgery, including those underwent valve replacement which may limit the generalizability of the results to all cardiac surgeries. A further limitation of this study was the use of a self-reporting tool which may have introduced reporting bias. Furthermore, the evaluation of patients’ self-care was conducted at least one month after surgery, which may have allowed patients to obtain information that could limit the generalizability of the results. The interpretation of the results may be influenced by the characteristics of the care givers, including their age, sex, education, and economic status. It is recommended that future studies include all patients undergoing open-heart surgery, follow up with patients for at least one year after surgery, and assess the health literacy and e-health literacy of patients before surgery.

Conclusions

The findings of the study indicated that patients who underwent CABG surgery exhibited an average level of health literacy and self-care abilities. A direct and statistically significant relationship was observed between these two variables, indicating that patients with higher health literacy demonstrated superior self-care abilities post-surgery. However, the study revealed that patients demonstrated limited e-health literacy. Additionally, a statistically significant direct relationship was observed between health literacy and e-health literacy. Nevertheless, no statistically significant correlation was identified between e-health literacy and the self-care practices of patients following CABG surgery. The results indicated that health literacy can predict patients’ ability to engage in self-care. Therefore, healthcare officials and policymakers should implement strategies to enhance the general population’s health literacy through public education and training programs. Prioritizing patients’ e-health literacy will not only facilitate access to information, but also guarantee the provision of accurate and reliable data for informed decision-making, facilitate communication with medical professionals through online platforms, enhance patient satisfaction, and ultimately improve treatment outcomes.
