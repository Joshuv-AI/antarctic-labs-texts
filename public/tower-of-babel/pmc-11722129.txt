A glucocorticoid spike derails muscle repair to heterotopic ossification after spinal cord injury
Authors: Kylie A. Alexander, Hsu-Wen Tseng, Hong Wa Lao, Dorothée Girard, Valérie Barbier, Jacobus P.J. Ungerer, Brett C. McWhinney, Selwin G. Samuel, Whitney Fleming, Ingrid G. Winkler, Marjorie Salga, François Genêt, Sébastien Banzet, Marc J. Ruitenberg, Jean-Pierre Lévesque
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryWhy severe injury to the central nervous system (CNS) triggers the development of large neurogenic heterotopic ossifications (NHOs) within periarticular muscles remains unknown. We report that spinal cord injury (SCI) triggers a rapid corticosterone spike in mice, which is causal for NHO development because treatments with corticosterone or the synthetic glucocorticoid (GC) receptor (GR) agonist dexamethasone are sufficient to trigger heterotopic ossification and upregulate the expression of osteoinductive and osteogenic differentiation genes in injured muscles even without SCI. The central role for GR signaling in causing NHO is further demonstrated in mice deleted for the GR gene (Nr3c1), which no longer develop NHO after SCI. Furthermore, administration of clinical GR antagonists inhibits NHO development in mice with SCI. This study identifies endogenous GC as causing pathological NHO after CNS injury and suggests that GR antagonists may be of prophylactic use to prevent NHO development in victims of severe CNS injuries.

Introduction

Effective skeletal muscle repair after injury requires two populations of muscle-resident stem cells working in concert: (1) myogenic stem cells called satellite cells (SCs) that regenerate myoblasts and myofibers1 and (2) mesenchymal progenitor cells (MPCs) called fibro-adipogenic progenitors (FAPs) that support SCs2 similar to the support provided by bone marrow stromal stem cells to hematopoietic stem cells. Rapidly after muscle injury, SCs and FAPs proliferate followed by timely apoptosis of FAPs induced by tumor necrosis factor (TNF) secreted by infiltrating inflammatory macrophages to prevent the onset of muscle fibrosis.3 Adequate function of the central nervous system (CNS) is essential for muscle repair because severe CNS damage caused by spinal cord injury (SCI), traumatic brain injury (TBI), or cerebral stroke cause the formation of heterotopic bones called neurogenic heterotopic ossifications (NHOs) in injured muscles in humans4,5,6 and mice.7 The molecular “switch” that corrupts effective muscle repair to osteogenesis and NHO formation in response to severe CNS injury is unknown, leading to a lack of effective prophylactic pharmacological treatments.8,9 The only effective treatment is surgical resection once NHOs are diagnosed,4,10 an invasive and challenging procedure when NHO encase the whole joint and adjacent large nerves and blood vessels. There is therefore an urgent unmet clinical need to identify prophylactic treatments targeting the mechanisms driving NHO pathogenesis.

We have developed a mouse model of NHO induced by the combination of a spinal cord transection between thoracic vertebras T11–T13 together with a muscle injury caused by intramuscular injection of cardiotoxin (CDTX).7 NHOs exclusively develop in CDTX-injured muscles only if there is a concomitant SCI.7,11 These NHO develop over 1–3 weeks exclusively in the CDTX-injured muscle even when CDTX is injected in the mobile front limb above the SCI.7 This is similar to SCI and brain-injured patients who can develop NHO in the non-paralyzed shoulder/elbow.7 In this mouse model, mineralized foci are detected as early as 3 days post injury with the progressive formation over 3 weeks of mineralized woven heterotopic bone nodules formed by osterix+/osteocalcin+ osteoblasts via intramembranous ossification.7,12 Genetic lineage tracing of SCs and FAP progenies in vivo has demonstrated that, following SCI, FAPs in the injured muscle are re-programmed and do not undergo apoptosis but continue proliferating to ultimately differentiate into osteoblasts that produce NHO.12

Phagocytic macrophages are key drivers of NHO development.7 SCI acts in part by exacerbating macrophage-mediated inflammation in injured muscles13 with excessive release of inflammatory cytokines oncostatin M (OSM),14 IL-1α, and IL1-β13 and overstimulation of the JAK1/2-STAT3 signaling pathway downstream of the OSM receptor.15 Deletion of the gene Il1r1 encoding the IL-1 receptor13 or Osmr encoding the OSM receptor,14 or administration of a JAK1/2 tyrosine kinase inhibitor ruxolitinib, attenuates NHO development following SCI in this mouse model.15

However, how a severe CNS injury switches myogenic regeneration to osteogenesis, as observed in CNS-injured patients or mice with SCI developing NHO, remains unknown.8 In this SCI-NHO mouse model, adrenal hormones released are necessary for NHO development, as bilateral adrenalectomy abrogates NHO formation after SCI.11 We further found that SCI causes a significant increase in blood epinephrine 24 h post-surgery, whereas chemical sympathectomy or treatment with β-blocker propranolol partially inhibited NHO formation, thus suggesting that enhanced sympathetic adrenergic signaling after SCI may contribute to NHO pathogenesis.11 However, chemical sympathectomy and β-blockers reduced NHO formation by only 50% in contrast to complete abrogation following bilateral adrenalectomy.11 This partial inhibition implies that additional adrenal hormones play an important role in NHO pathogenesis. Herein, we identify increased adrenal glucocorticoids (GCs) in the blood in response to SCI as a targetable trigger of NHO development.

Results

Increased blood GCs following SCI

We first measured corticosterone, the physiological endogenous GC in rodents, and its precursor 11β-deoxycorticosterone (which has negligible GC activity) in the blood of mice taken between 7.00 a.m. and 8.00 a.m. (corresponding to zeitgeber times Zt0 and Zt1), at the nadir of corticosterone circadian oscillation in mice.16 Despite an expected increase in blood GC levels caused by the stress of sampling blood, we detected a significant elevation in plasma corticosterone and 11β-deoxycorticosterone at 24 h post SCI (Figure 1A) compared to CDTX-mediated muscle injury with sham surgery, which had no significant effect on plasma GC concentrations at these time points (Figure 1A). GC receptor agonists promote HO development in injured muscles in the absence of SCI

To demonstrate a direct causal effect of elevated corticosterone on NHO development, we administered corticosterone in drinking water to mice with a muscle injury but without SCI (Figure 1B). Mice were treated with corticosterone either from day −2 to +7 post CDTX, or a delayed treatment starting from day +2 to +7 post muscle injury, which was chosen because this delayed time would be after the corticosterone plasma peak observed at 24 h after SCI (Figure 1A). Despite high inter-individual variability, corticosterone in drinking water increased plasma corticosterone concentrations similar to SCI plus muscle injury without corticosterone treatment (Figure 1C). As previously reported,7 heterotopic ossifications (HOs) did not develop in mice with muscle injury alone. In sharp contrast, 62% (18 out of 29) of mice with corticosterone in their drinking water from day −2 to +7 developed HOs (HO volume >0.5 mm3) in injured muscles (Figure 1D). This different outcome in HO development was significant by both Mann-Whitney and Fisher’s exact tests. Delaying the administration of corticosterone until 2 days post muscle injury resulted in the development of smaller HOs in only a few mice (Figure 1D), and HO volumes did not significantly differ from mice administered control water (p = 0.198). Immature HO formation was evident histologically in mice treated with corticosterone from day −2 to +7, with Masson’s stain illustrating abundant collagen I deposition among F4/80+ macrophages and osterix+ osteo-lineage cells at 21 days post CDTX (Figures 1E and S1). This establishes that the administration of exogenous corticosterone is sufficient to trigger HO development in injured muscles in the absence of SCI.

As corticosterone in water is consumed ad libitum with inherent mouse-to-mouse variability in drinking patterns, we next injected the synthetic and selective long-acting GC receptor (GR) agonist dexamethasone daily from day 0–7 post muscle injury. As expected, dexamethasone treatment significantly decreased total T cells and B cells in lymph nodes, spleen, bone marrow, and muscle (Figure S2). Interestingly, dexamethasone administration decreased monocyte/macrophages in lymphoid organs but did not alter their number in injured muscles, where NHOs develop, nor in the bone marrow, where they are formed (Figure S2). Expression of mRNA encoding specific monocyte/macrophage markers such as Adgre1 (F4/80 antigen), Fcgr1 (CD64), P2ry6, or Clec4a3 in injured muscles was also unaltered by dexamethasone treatment (Table S1). Dexamethasone increased granulocytes in both bone marrow and injured muscle (Figure S2) suggesting that dexamethasone had contrasted effects on adaptive versus innate immune cells. Most importantly, dexamethasone treatment induced HO development in CDTX-injured muscles in 9 out of 13 (69%) mice (Figure 1F), similar to corticosterone in drinking water.

As we have previously reported that SCI and CDTX muscle injury have to occur within 3 h of each other to trigger NHO development,11 we investigated whether a similar synchrony was required for dexamethasone treatment to induce HO formation in injured muscles. Indeed, by delaying the start of dexamethasone treatment after CDTX muscle injury, we found a similar temporal dependence, whereby GC administration was required within the first 6 h of muscle injury to elicit significant HO formation (Figure 1G).

Likewise, daily injection of dexamethasone in mice with SCI plus muscle injury further increased NHO volumes compared to mice with SCI and muscle injury injected with saline (Figure 1H).

We also validated these results with human NHO biopsy samples by culturing CD56− PDGRα+ muscle MPCs sorted from the muscles surrounding NHO from 5 different patients. We have previously established that these muscle MPCs, analogous to FAPs in the mouse muscle, are the cells of origin of NHO in both humans and mice.12 Muscle CD56−PDGRα+ MPCs were cultured in osteogenic conditions with ascorbic acid, β-glycerophosphate and increasing concentrations of cortisol, the physiological GC in humans. Cortisol significantly increased mineralization of human muscle MPC from all patients, suggesting that physiological GCs promote muscle mineralization in both species (Figure 1I).

Post-natal deletion of the GR gene prevents NHO development

We next produced mice to allow the post-natal deletion of the Nr3c1 gene encoding the GR17 to avoid the perinatal lethality observed with germinal Nr3c1 knockout.18 To this end, Nr3c1fl/fl mice were crossed with mice containing a tamoxifen-inducible recombinase CreERT2 knocked into the ROSA26 trap locus19 to allow efficient tamoxifen-inducible deletion of floxed Nr3c1 alleles in all cells. Both male and female Nr3c1fl/fl x ROSA26CreERT2/WT mice and control Nr3c1WT/WT x ROSA26CreERT2/WT mice (4–7 weeks of age) were gavaged daily with tamoxifen to delete the floxed Nr3c1 alleles and then left to rest for an additional 2 weeks before undergoing SCI and CDTX muscle injury (Figure 2A). Genomic PCR before and after tamoxifen treatment demonstrated excised Nr3c1Δ allele in 100% of Nr3c1fl/fl x ROSA26CreERT2/WT mice treated with tamoxifen, which did not occur in any Nr3c1WT/WT x ROSA26CrerERT2/WT mice or before tamoxifen treatment (Figure 2B). We designed reverse-transcription PCR (RT-PCR) primers to amplify from exon 2–3 and exon 2–4 boundaries to measure the proportion of mRNA for intact Nr3c1fl allele vs. recombined Nr3c1Δ allele lacking exon 3. In muscles from SCI + CDTX mice 21 days post injury, approximately 50% Nr3c1fl mRNAs were recombined after tamoxifen treatment (Figure 2C). Remarkably, at 7 and 21 days post SCI and CDTX muscle injury, none of 10 tamoxifen-treated Nr3c1fl/fl x ROSA26CreERT2/WT (Nr3c1Δ/Δ) mice developed NHO whereas all 12 tamoxifen-treated control Nr3c1WT/WT x ROSA26CrerERT2/WT mice did develop NHO (Figure 2D). CDTX-injured muscles from Nr3c1WT/WT x ROSA26CreERT2/WT control mice, 21 days post-surgery contained numerous NHO foci (Figure 2E), with F4/80+ macrophages intercalated among osterix+ osteo-lineage cells on NHO surfaces (Figure 2E) as expected. In contrast, injured muscles from Nr3c1Δ/Δ mice had no NHO foci, less F4/80+ expression, and no osterix+ osteoblast lineage cells (Figure 2E). This demonstrates that GR-mediated signaling above a certain threshold is necessary for NHO formation post SCI.

Out of the 14,911 detected expressed genes in CDTX-injured muscles, 759 were significantly upregulated (log2(fold change) ≥ 0.7; false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05) and 749 downregulated (log2(FC) ≤ −0.7; FDR < 0.05) in the dexamethasone-treated group (Figures 3C and 3D; Table S1). Preliminary DAVID (Database for Annotation, Visualization, and Integrated Discovery) analysis of genes significantly upregulated in injured muscles from dexamethasone-treated mice revealed a significant enrichment of the “Response to glucocorticoid” (6.5-fold, FDR = 1.4 × 10−3) and “Cushing syndrome” pathways (2.5-fold enrichment, FDR = 3.2 × 10−2), whereas many pathways enriched in the genes significantly downregulated were related to adaptive immunity and B and T cells (Table S2), consistent with decreased B and T cells of lymphocytes in injured muscles by flow cytometry (Figure S2D). Interestingly, the “Spinal cord injury” signature from Wikipathways was also significantly enriched (3.5-fold, FDR = 1.6 × 10−4) in injured muscles from dexamethasone-treated mice (Table S2).

We then performed a more sensitive gene set expression analysis (GSEA) using Gene Ontology, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), and Hallmark collections. Many downregulated gene sets were related to lymphocytes and acquired immunity (Figures 3E–3G and S4) with most downregulated genes including genes specific for T (Cd3d, Cd8b1, Cd4, Trbc2, Cd8a, Cd5, Lck, Tgtp2, Cd3e, Cd3g, Itk, and Il2rb), B (Cr2, Ighg2b, Iglc3, Cd19, Cd79a/b, Ighd, Blk, Ikzf3, Cd22, and Pax5), and natural killer cells (Klra9, Klre1, Klrk1, Nkg7, and Ncr1). On the other hand, many significantly upregulated gene sets were related to inflammatory, TNF, and IL-6/JAK/STAT3 signaling pathways (Figures 3F–3H and 4A). These results are very reminiscent of our previous GSEA analysis that identified these gene sets enriched in injured muscles of mice with SCI, which develop NHO.13 In particular, we could identify a common signature of enhanced inflammatory and macrophage genes that were upregulated in injured muscles of both dexamethasone-treated mice without SCI, which develop non-NHO, and mice with SCI instead of dexamethasone, which develop NHO.13 This common gene signature includes Chil3, Ccl2, Il1b, Il6, Il1r1, Cd163, Osmr, Osm, Mrc1, and Csf1, which were all upregulated (Figures 4A and 5). Importantly, dexamethasone also upregulated Osm, Il1b, and their respective receptors Osmr and Il1r1, which we have previously demonstrated to play an important role in promoting NHO development following SCI.13,14 Quantitative RT-PCR (qRT-PCR) on whole hamstring muscle extracted 4 days post muscle injury validated that Osm, Il1b, Osmr, and Il1r mRNA were significantly upregulated by dexamethasone treatment (Figures S5A–S5D). qRT-PCR on monocytes/macrophages sorted from injured muscles 4 days post injury also showed significant increase in Osm, Il1b, and Il1a following dexamethasone treatment (Figures S5E–S5G) further illustrating the pro-inflammatory effect of GCs in injured muscles. By delaying the starting point of mifepristone treatment after SCI and muscle injury, we established that mifepristone treatment must be started within a 48-h therapeutic window after SCI as mifepristone effectively inhibited NHO formation when started 5, 10, 24, or 48 h post-surgery (Figure 7E).

As mifepristone is also a PGR antagonist, we confirmed that the day 0–2 post-surgery treatment regimen was also effective in male mice (Figure 7F), which have 40-fold lower progesterone blood concentrations compared to females.42

As certain inbred strains of mice display different immune bias (e.g., Th1 and Th2-biased responses), we administered mifepristone to BALB/c mice, and the treatment was also just as effective in reducing NHO bone volumes (Figure 7G).

RUNX2 is a transcription factor essential for osteoblast differentiation from MPCs.37,43 Runx2, which was also significantly upregulated in SCI + CDTX groups between 2 and 4 days post-surgery, was significantly reduced after mifepristone treatment (Figure 7H). Likewise, mifepristone treatment trended toward decreased STAT3 phosphorylation in injured muscles (Figure S6B), which is associated with NHO development.15 As we have previously demonstrated that blockade of STAT3 phosphorylation via administration of JAK1/2 tyrosine kinase inhibitor ruxolitinib attenuated NHO development, we investigated the efficacy of combining mifepristone with ruxolitinib. Ruxolitinib treatment alone resulted in a 20% reduction in NHO volumes at day 7 and 35% at day 21, but this did not reach statistical significance by one-way ANOVA (significant by Mann-Whitney test at day 21 p = 0.037) (Figures S6C and S6D). More importantly, there was also no significant difference between mifepristone alone vs. mifepristone + ruxolitinib treatment groups at either time point (Figures S4C and S4D). Overall, this suggests that there is no additive contribution of further JAK1/2-STAT3 signaling to GR signaling in promoting NHO development as GR agonism already activates JAK1/2 STAT3 signaling (Figure S6A).

As SCI blocks the apoptosis of proliferating FAPs in the injured muscle,12 we measured FAP apoptosis in the injured muscles of SCI mice treated with mifepristone or vehicle. Mifepristone did not re-establish FAP apoptosis in injured muscles (Figure 7I). We did not detect any changes in expression of muscle repair genes within the first 4 days of muscle injury (Myog: myogenin, Myh3: myosin heavy polypeptide 3, and Mstn: myostatin) or the pro-fibrotic gene Tgfb3 (Figure S7), which was recently shown to be involved in burn-induced HO.44 Overall, these data suggest that GR antagonism does not rescue FAP apoptosis but may instead alter the pro-osteogenic environment and program within injured muscles following SCI.

Nr3c1 mRNA was abundantly expressed by inflammatory monocyte/macrophage subsets and granulocytes within injured muscles 4 days post-surgery, as well as in muscle progenitor populations: FAPs and SCs (Figure S8A). In contrast, Pgr was mostly absent and/or expressed at very low levels in all cell types aforementioned, compared to the ovary/uterus, which are well known to abundantly express Pgr (Figure S8B). Overall, these suggest that the effect of mifepristone is via GR and not PGR antagonism.

We then examined the effect of mifepristone treatment in a contusive model of SCI instead of a total transection of the spinal cord. A contusion of the spinal cord with a defined force concomitantly with CDTX-induced muscle injury also leads to NHO development.11 While NHO bone volumes at day 21 in this T9 contusion model are much lower than what is seen at day 21 in mice with a total SCI transection, NHO is only present in CDTX-injured muscles in mice with a T9 contusion (Figure S9A). In addition, mifepristone treatment for the first 2 days post-surgery reduced NHO bone volumes at day 21 compared to the vehicle-treated group (p = 0.029 by one-way ANOVA and p = 0.052 by Mann-Whitney test) (Figure S9A). Importantly, we also examined whether mifepristone treatment altered locomotor function in open field using the Basso Mouse Scale (BMS). The BMS score incorporates parameters such as ankle movement, stepping, stability, and coordination, to grade the level of hindlimb locomotion ability.45 T9 SCI mice receiving either mifepristone, CDTX, or a combination of both showed a similar level of functional recovery, reaching a BMS score of 2.5 at 21 dpi, which indicates that they were capable of extensive ankle movement, and placing their paws on the ground (Figure S9B). Overall, this suggests that short-term GR antagonist treatment does not worsen neuroinflammation nor locomotory function.

As a retrospective study in victims of SCI suggested that prophylaxis with non-steroid anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID) and cyclooxygenase inhibitor indomethacin reduces the incidence of NHO development,46 we performed a side-by-side comparison of the efficacy of a 2-day course of mifepristone versus indomethacin. While mifepristone markedly decreased NHO volumes at day 7 post injury, indomethacin had no effect (Figure S10). In agreement with the well-known side effects of NSAIDs, we noted the signs of gastrointestinal bleeding (bloody stools) in most indomethacin-treated mice, which was absent in mifepristone-treated mice.

To definitively rule out a possible effect of PGR antagonism with mifepristone, we administered the non-steroid selective GR antagonist relacorilant (CORT125134, Figure S8D), currently in clinical trials to treat Cushing syndrome and adrenal carcinomas.47,48,49 Similar to mifepristone, relacorilant significantly reduced NHO volumes (Figures 7J and 7K). Muscles from vehicle-treated mice contained numerous NHO foci with F4/80+ macrophages intercalated among osterix+ osteo-lineage cells on NHO surfaces 21 days post-surgery (Figure 7L). In sharp contrast, relacorilant-treated mice had significantly fewer NHO foci, less F4/80+ macrophages, and mostly no osterix+ osteo-lineage cells (Figure 7L).

Discussion

GCs are typically considered as immunosuppressive and “anti-inflammatory,” and stable synthetic GR agonists are prescribed to treat chronic and acute inflammation as well as lymphoid malignancies.50,51,52 We demonstrate herein that physiological GCs released by adrenal glands in response to SCI as well as treatment with the synthetic GR agonist dexamethasone are sufficient to trigger HO development in injured muscles even in the absence of SCI injury. Furthermore, additional treatment with GR agonist dexamethasone further increased NHO development after an SCI. Our results are consistent with a previous report showing that dexamethasone treatment also increased HO volumes in a mouse model of HO induced by injection of the potent osteogenic growth factor BMP7.53 Importantly, we find that a spike in GC is required to happen within 6 h of the muscle injury to subsequently trigger HO development, similar to SCI.11 This is in stark contrast to the effects of GC agonist treatment on a genetic form of HO fibrodysplasia ossificans progressiva, which is caused by ACVR1 gene gain-of-function mutations, where prednisone is frequently administered to reduce “HO flare-ups” in patients.54 This is also in sharp contrast to the effects of long-term treatments with GR agonists on the skeletal bones, which cause GC-induced osteoporosis (GIOP) by attenuating osteoblast differentiation and promoting osteoblast and osteocyte apoptosis.55,56,57 However, it is well known that physiological levels of endogenous GCs are required for adequate bone mass accrual and osteoblast differentiation.58 Indeed, mice with conditional deletion of Nr3c1 in osteoblasts56 or with osteoblast-specific overexpression of 11β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase-2 (gene Hsd11b2), which dehydrogenates biologically active endogenous corticosterone and cortisol into inactive 11-dehydrocorticosterone and cortisone, respectively,59 both display a marked osteopenia in skeletal bones. Therefore, physiological levels of GCs are necessary to ensure the maturation of osteoblasts from skeletal stem cells residing at the endosteum and periosteum of skeletal bones.60

Importantly, we find that GC signaling via the GR is necessary for NHO development after SCI as there was no NHO formation following post-natal deletion of the GR gene Nr3c1. This phenocopies the absence of NHO development in mice with bilateral adrenalectomy.11 Interestingly, RT-PCR on whole injured muscles showed that 50% Nr3c1fl mRNA was recombined after tamoxifen treatment of Nr3c1fl/fl x ROSA26CreERT2 mice.

These functional in vivo results were supported by unsupervised RNA sequencing of whole injured muscles. Indeed, dexamethasone treatment significantly upregulated the expression of genes associated with osteoblast differentiation such as Runx2, Sox11, Tnc, Spp1, Suco, Col12a1, or genes encoding pro-osteogenic growth factors such as Bmp3, Wnt7b, Wnt10a, Gdf5, Gdf6, Ccn1, Ccn2, and Ccn4. Importantly, expression of the fibrillar collagen tyrosine kinase receptor Ddr2, which has recently been shown to be expressed in MPCs from tendon and promote HO formation after extended body burn and tenotomy,61 was also significantly induced in injured muscles following dexamethasone treatment. Together, these findings show that high GC signaling produces an osteoinductive environment in injured muscles that drives osteogenic differentiation of FAPs and HO development. Unexpectedly, expression of Sp7 encoding the osteoblast transcription factor osterix was downregulated in injured muscles by a 4-day treatment with dexamethasone, and this potentially reflects the negative effects of long-term dexamethasone treatment on osteoblast differentiation and survival as observed in GIOP. This is consistent with the observation that Sp7 mRNA is also reduced in CDTX-injured muscles at day 4 after SCI and CDTX injury compared to CDTX muscle injury alone before bouncing back on day 7.62 It is however important to consider that (1) the peak of endogenous GC in response to SCI is brief, with corticosterone plasma concentrations returning to basal levels 48 h post injury, and (2) SP7/osterix protein is abundant in injured muscles 21 days post injury combined with GC treatment (Figure 1D), similar to injured muscles with SCI.7,14,15,63 In the experiments described herein, dexamethasone and corticosterone were administered for a maximum of 7 days post muscle injury whereas osteoblasts were detected in NHO 3 weeks post-surgery. At 3 weeks post-surgery, the initial and brief endogenous GC spike during the first 48 h post-injury did not counteract the late and robust expression of osterix protein in osteoblast-like cells in muscles forming NHO as shown herein (Figures 1E, 2E, and 7L) and in previous reports.7,14,15,63,64

Expectedly, we found that dexamethasone treatment caused a dramatic reduction in the number of B and T lymphocytes in lymphoid tissues (lymph nodes and spleen). We also found a decrease in T cells and a trend toward lower B cells in muscle following dexamethasone treatment, which aligns with the marked downregulation of genes associated with acquired immunity and of genes specific of B and T lymphocytes in injured muscles. Remarkably though, a 4-day dexamethasone treatment significantly increased gene sets associated with inflammation, JAK/STAT3 signaling, chemokines, and coagulation with upregulation of genes encoding inflammatory cytokines OSM and IL-1β, which are mostly expressed by activated macrophages,13,14 and their cognate receptors OSMR and IL1R1, which we have already shown to be important drivers of NHO development and are upregulated in the injured muscle after SCI.13,14 This is consistent with the reported increase in expression of genes encoding CD163, IL1R1, GP130, various chemokine receptors, and complement cascade components observed in human peripheral blood mononuclear cells stimulated in vitro with dexamethasone.65 While the pro-inflammatory effect of GCs in injured muscles seems counterintuitive, we further demonstrate that the monocyte/macrophages within injured muscles significantly express more pro-inflammatory cytokine genes such as Osm, Il1b, and Il1a after dexamethasone treatment in vivo. It has also recently emerged that the anti-inflammatory effects of GCs on inflammatory macrophages involve their ability to restart oxidative phosphorylation in mitochondria and enhance itaconate production in a context-dependent manner because the GC anti-inflammatory mechanism is blocked by hypoxia.66 Importantly, injured skeletal muscles are hypoxic,67 which may explain in part why GCs do not dampen inflammation in injured muscles leaving the inflammation caused by injury unchecked. Our results are also consistent with several reports on the pro-inflammatory effects of GCs in specific contexts. For instance, GR signaling in Cx3cr1-expressing macrophages drives the secondary neuroinflammation at the site of spinal cord contusion.68 In a rat model of unpredictable stress, which augmented plasma corticosterone levels, stress enhanced the activation of nuclear factor κB in the frontal cortex and hippocampus after intravenous lipopolysaccharide (LPS) injection, with increased IL-1β and TNF expression, compared to control non-stressed mice, an effect blunted by mifepristone treatment.69 GCs also exacerbate the neurotoxic effect of excitotoxic insults with kainic acid and intravenous LPS with increased expression of IL-1α, IL-1β, and TNF in the hippocampus and cerebral cortex.70,71 There is also a temporal relationship between GC administration and the immune challenge on inflammatory output, as GCs potentiate pro-inflammatory responses in the liver and hippocampus if administered before LPS inflammatory challenge, whereas if GC administration is delayed to after the LPS challenge, GCs suppress the inflammatory response.72 In summary, GCs can be pro-inflammatory, but this effect is highly dependent on the dose, duration of GC exposure, the timing of GC exposure versus inflammatory challenge, as well as the organ/tissue considered.73

We also demonstrate that the acute administration of two structurally unrelated GR antagonists mifepristone and relacorilant in the first 48 h after SCI markedly reduced NHO formation, consistent with our observation that deletion of the Nr3c1 gene encoding the GR prevents NHO formation after SCI. Of note, although treatment with GR antagonists reduced the expression of the master osteogenic transcription factor Runx2 in the injured muscle, it did not re-establish FAP apoptosis. Therefore, while GR antagonists were effective at toning down the osteogenic program that is initiated by SCI in injured muscles and thereby decreasing NHO formation, they did not appear to re-establish normal muscle repair.

As dexamethasone treatment upregulated the expression of inflammatory pathways that signal via JAK/STAT3, which we have previously shown to contribute to NHO development after SCI,15 we tested a combination therapy approach targeting both GR and JAK/STAT3 signaling. However, combination therapy was no more effective than GR antagonist treatment alone, possibly because mifepristone treatment alone was sufficient to reduce STAT3 phosphorylation.

Of note, although NHO volumes were smaller in the contusive NHO model, GR antagonist treatment also decreased NHO volumes but was unable to improve motor locomotion recovery. This suggests that GR antagonists can reduce NHO development post SCI but they do not improve nor adversely affect motor recovery.

Our findings contrast to the well-established anabolic and catabolic effects of GR agonists on skeletal bones.57,74 Basal GC levels are required for the development of skeletal bones in the cranium during fetal development75 whereas chronic GC excess is detrimental to skeletal bone health, as excessive and continuous GR agonism can result in GIOP by reducing bone formation by osteoblasts and increasing bone degradation by osteoclasts.57 The effect of GCs on NHO development may be mediated by several cell types including macrophages as well as muscle FAPs, osteoblast progenitors, and possibly endothelial cells, which all express GR. We are currently undertaking studies with cell-specific conditional deletion of Nr3c1 to provide further insights into which cell type(s) mediate the promoting effect of endogenous GCs on NHO formation.

In respect to the relevance to the human pathology, this mouse model mimics the spontaneous nature of human NHO as it does not rely on genetic mutations or administration of exogenous BMPs or transgene expression and only requires an SCI combined with a muscle injury.7,8 In contrast to the human pathology, this mouse model does not develop NHO specifically in periarticular muscles but rather in CDTX-injured muscles, with faster kinetics. As high GC plasma concentration is a common feature in victims of SCI,76 TBI,77 and extended severe body burns,78 which can all lead to HO development,6,79 excessive endogenous GC release could potentially be the common driver of traumatic HO and hence be a common therapeutic target to reduce the incidence of these various forms of traumatic neurogenic and non-NHO.

In conclusion, our findings show that a spike in endogenous GCs is a key driver of NHO development in mice, whereby a peak corticosterone release subsequent to an SCI leads to heterotopic ossification in damaged muscles. In addition, treatment of mice with GR antagonists significantly attenuated NHO development, providing a proof of principle that acute treatment with GR antagonists following the occurrence of an SCI may represent a potential prophylactic treatment to prevent the development of this pathology. GR antagonist treatment in our mouse model was superior to NSAID indomethacin, the only prophylactic treatment reported in the clinical setting46 and without the side effects associated with NSAIDs such as bloody stools. Importantly, on the flip side, our data also suggest that acute “anti-inflammatory” treatments with GR agonists have the potential to drive and/or exacerbate detrimental NHO development and therefore this should be avoided in victims of CNS injuries. This is clinically relevant, as to date, acute high dose of GR agonist methylprednisolone within 6 h of the occurrence of an SCI is still used, albeit controversially, as an option to dampen neuroinflammation.80,81 Our results further question the use of GR agonists for the acute treatment of SCI. Finally, our data highlight the key role of steroid neuro-endocrine mediators in orchestrating the adequate proliferation and differentiation of tissue-specific stem cells during tissue repair.

Limitations of the study

In this study, GR agonists, antagonists, and conditional deletion of the GR gene were employed in a mouse model of SCI-induced NHO. Although dexamethasone increased mineralization of MPCs sorted from muscles surrounding patients’ NHO, suggesting similar mechanisms between humans and mice, we did not perform a prospective correlative study to demonstrate a significant association between high blood cortisol in acute phase following SCI and subsequent NHO development in patients. Our team has just started such clinical study at the time of publication of this article. Furthermore, the safety profile of GR antagonists (mifepristone or relacorilant) has not been established in victims of SCI; therefore, their clinical evaluation and particularly the identification of the optimal therapeutic window after SCI (which is within 24 h after SCI in mice) warrants further investigation. Another limitation is that we did not test whether endogenous GCs were similarly involved in the pathogenesis of NHO following TBI. This will also need further investigation.

Experimental model and study participant details

Animals

All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of The University of Queensland (2017AE000050, 2021AE000155 and 2021AE000537) and housed at the Translational Research Institute under specific pathogen-free conditions, fed a standard diet chow (Specialty Feeds, Perth, Australia) with ad libitum water access and simulated diurnal cycle with light on from 7.00a.m. to 7.00p.m. and light off from 7.00p.m. to 7.00a.m.

C57BL/6J mice were obtained from either the Animal Resource Center (Perth, Australia) or Ozgene ARC (Perth, Australia).

Generation of Nr3c1 (GR) conditional knock-out mice

Nr3c1tm2Gsc (Nr3c1fl/fl) mice with a floxed exon 3 of the Nr3c1 gene17 were extensively backcrossed into the C57BL/6 genetic background and were kindly provided by Jan Tuckermann (University of Ulm) and Hong Zhou (ANZAC Research Institute, University of Sydney).

B6.129-Gt(ROSA)26Sortm1(cre/ERT2)Tyj/J (ROSA26CreERT2) mice19 backcrossed in the C57BL/6J background were purchased from Jackson Laboratory (strain # 008463).

Nr3c1fl/fl x ROSA26CreERT2/WT mice were generated by firstly crossing Nr3c1fl/fl mice with Rosa26CreERT2 mice, followed by intercrossing of F1 mice. Pups from each generation were genotyped by genomic PCR for the presence of Nr3c1WT, Nr3c1fl, ROSA26WT, and ROSA26CreERT2 alleles using primers GR1 5′-GGCATGCACATTACTGGCCTTCT-3′ and GR8 5′-CCTTCTCATTCCATGTCAGCATGT-3′ with 35 cycles at 94°C for 30s, 58°C for 30s, and 60°C for 60 s. The Nr3c1fl allele band migrated at 275bp, and wildtype Nr3c1 allele at 250bp.17,82 PCR reaction primers 5′-CTGGCTTCTGAGGACCG-3′ with 5′-CCGAAAATCTGTGGGAAGTC-3′, wildtype Rosa26 allele and 5′-CGTGATCTGCAACTCCAGTC-3′ with 5′-AGGCAAATTTTGGTGTACGG-3′ to detect Rosa26CreERT2 allele were used in 30 cycles at 94°C for 30s, 61°C for 30s, and 60°C for 60s. Wildtype Rosa26 allele band migrated as 198bp band and the Rosa26CreERT2 allele as a 150bp band. All PCR products were separated on 2% agarose gels run in 1X Tris-acetate EDTA (TAE) buffer, stained with fluorescent DNA dye SYBR Safe (ThermoFisher) and visualized on a Vilber Fluorescence Gel Doc apparatus.

Subsequently the progeny of these crosses (Nr3c1fl/WT x ROSA26CreERT2/WT) we intercrossed to generate mice homozygous for the Nr3c1 floxed allele and heterozygous for Rosa26CreERT2 allele, again selected via genotyping (Nr3c1fl/fl x ROSA26CreERT2/WT), control mice were bred separately to maintain a wild-type Nr3c1 allele but heterozygous for Rosa26CreERT2 (Nr3c1WT/WT x ROSA26CreERT2/WT).

SCI-induced NHO mouse model

7-10-week-old male and female mice were anesthetized (100 mg/kg ketamine, 10 mg/kg xylazine, and 1% isofluorane), and shaved from the dorsal area (between the neck and hindlimbs) and the skin area overlying the projected surgery site disinfected with 70% ethanol. A laminectomy was performed on the dorsal spine and the spinal cord was transected with a scalpel blade between T11 and T13, followed by an intramuscular injection of CDTX at 0.32 mg/kg in the hamstring muscles in a final volume of 50μL (Latoxan, France) as previously described.12,13 Mice subsequently received a subcutaneous injection of ciprofloxacin (10 mg/kg), buprenorphine (0.075 mg/kg) and recovered on a heat pad. As SCI causes complete paraplegia, mouse bladders were expressed manually twice daily throughout the experiments. Mice were given Bactrim (800 mg/L, Roche) in drinking water following surgical procedures as prophylaxis for bladder infections. Although we have observed that NHO development is similar in both males and females, females were used for all experiments utilizing C57BL/6 and BALB/c mice in this study for ethical considerations because manual bladder expression is much easier in females and the risk of bladder infection or urethra swelling and blockage is therefore much reduced compared with males.83 However, in experiments using Nr3c1 conditional knock outs, mixed sex cohorts were used to reduce the breeding and production of total number of animals needed for this study, in accordance with the Australian Code for the Care and Use of Animals for Scientific Purposes.

Contusive spinal cord injury model

This model used female mice for the same ethical considerations as described above in the SCI-induced NHO mouse model. Ten-week-old C57BL/6 female mice were anesthetized with intraperitoneal injections of zolazepam (50 mg/kg; Virbac) and xylazine (10 mg/kg; Troy Laboratories). Once anesthetized, animals were shaved from the dorsal area and the skin area overlying the projected surgery site disinfected with 70% ethanol. A 2-3cm rostrocaudal midline incision was made to expose the vertebral column, and the paravertebral muscles were split and pulled aside. The ninth thoracic (T9) target vertebra was identified based on anatomical landmarks,84 followed by dorsal laminectomy to expose the spinal cord. The spinal column was immobilized through the placement of stabilizing clamps on the transverse processes of T8 and T10. Using the force-controlled Infinite Horizon spinal impactor device (Precision Systems and Instrumentation), severe contusive spinal cord injury (100 kdyne) was inflicted on the T9 spinal cord. Next, the dorsal paravertebral muscle layers were sutured using 6-0 polyglactin dissolvable sutures (Ethicon), and the skin was closed with Michel wound clips (Kent Scientific). Before regaining consciousness, animals were placed in a veterinary recovery chamber (Pece Services, Cat# V1200DT). Post-operated mice were subcutaneously administrated with buprenorphine (0.5 mg/kg in Hartmann’s sodium lactate, Baxter Healthcare) for analgesia and antibiotic gentamicin (1.0 mg/kg) to prevent infection. Bladders were manually checked and voided twice daily until the experimental endpoint.

Clinical samples of NHO biopsies

All five human NHO samples used in the study were surgical waste obtained following NHO excision from patients with SCI, TBI, or cerebral stroke and patients’ characteristics (gender, age, etiology) are in Table S3. Our previous experiments with MPCs sorted from the muscles surrounding patients NHO biopsies have shown that 5 independent clinical samples from 5 patients are sufficient to detect a significant statistical effects on mineralization activity when MPCs are stimulated with OSM or IL-1.13,14 MPCs from each donor were cultured in identical conditions to test their calcium mineralization potential in response to stimulation by the same range of dexamethasone and cortisol concentrations.

All patients gave their prior informed consent, and the experimental protocol was approved by the Comité de Protection des Personnes (CPP approval 09025) and by the Commission Nationale Informatique et Liberté (CNIL approval Eyo1066211J).

Isolation of human PDGFRα+ CD56− FAPs from muscles surrounding patients’ NHO

Muscle surrounding NHOs was collected from the surgical waste The surgeries to resect NHO were performed at Raymond Poincaré Hospital (Garches, France). MPCs were isolated and cultured as we previously published.13 Briefly, muscles dissected from NHO were firstly trypsinized and the cell suspension incubated with biotinylated anti-human PDGFRα and anti-human CD56-PE monoclonal antibodies or with isotypes IgG1-PE and mouse Normal Goat IgG Biotinylated Control in PBS 2% FCS, 2mM EDTA for 30 min, followed by APC-Cy7 streptavidin. MPCs were washed and incubated with 7-amino actinomycin D for 30 min, washed and filtered using a 30μm cell strainer (Sysmex) and then sorted with a FACSAria III SORP sorter (BD Biosciences). PDGFRα+ CD56− MPCs were cultured in α-MEM with 20% fetal calf serum (FCS) and 1% antibiotics at 3,000 per cm2.

In vitro osteogenic differentiation and quantification of mineralization

To induce osteogenic differentiation, the media was replaced by α-MEM supplemented with 10% FCS, 1% antibiotics, 12.8 μg/mL ascorbic acid, and 2.15 mg/mL β-glycerophosphate (Sigma-Aldrich) and either 10−7 M dexamethasone (D1179, Sigma-Aldrich) or 10−6 to 10−8 M hydrocortisone (Sigma-Aldrich). Cells were cultured for 14 days. At the end of the differentiation process, cells were washed in PBS, fixed in 70% ethanol, washed in water, and stained in 20 g/L Alizarin red S (A5533, Sigma-Aldrich). Excess stain was removed during three washing steps. Alizarin red S dye was extracted with 0.5 N hydrochloric acid and 5% sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) and quantified by spectrophotometry at 405 nm.

Method details

Excision of Nr3c1 floxed alleles in mice

Nr3c1fl/fl x ROSA26CreERT2/WT and control Nr3c1WT/WT x ROSA26CreERT2/WT mice were gavaged 125 mg/kg tamoxifen dissolved in peanut oil daily for four consecutive days between 4 and 7 weeks of age, followed by a 2-week rest. Confirmation of Nr3c1 exon 3 excision after tamoxifen treatment was verified by genomic PCR for the presence of the excised allele band using GR1 5′-GGCATGCACATTACTGGCCTTCT-3′ and GR4 5′-GTGTAGCAGCCAGCTTACAGGA-3′ primers with 35 cycles at 94°C for 30s, 58°C for 30s, and 60°C for 60 s.

In addition, RT-PCR primers were designed to quantify intact Nr3c1fl mRNA (289bp amplicon) vs. recombined Nr3c1Δ (125bp amplicon) mRNA by RT-PCR. RNA was isolated from whole hamstrings via homogenization in Trizol (Life Technologies), using a TissueRuptor (Qiagen) and after chloroform separation, RNA was isolated from aqueous phase. Reverse transcription of 1μg of RNA was performed using the SensiFAST cDNA Synthesis Kit (ThermoFisher) as per manufacturer’s instructions. An RT-PCR was then performed using cDNA diluted 1/10 and 5′-ACTTCGCAGGCCGCTCAGTG-3′ and 5′-CGATAGCGGCATGCTGGACA-3′ primers to amplify from Nr3c1 exon 2 to exon 4 (94°C 2min, 34 cycles of 94°C 30s, 60°C 30s, 72°C 60s, followed by final extension at 72°C 5min). Band intensities were quantified by densitometry using ImageJ software version 1.54days (NIH, Bethesda, MD), using the Gel Analysis program. Briefly, the box tool was used to select each individual band. Using the ‘Analyze Gels’ function the intensities (pixels) for each band were exported. As fluorescent intercalant dye intensity is proportional to both DNA fragment length and quantity, the ratio of recombined Nr3c1Δ was calculated by dividing the pixel intensity of the recombined 125bp Nr3c1Δ band x 289 by the pixel intensity of the intact 289 bp Nr3c1fl band x 125 to normalize band intensity for their different lengths and levels of dye incorporation on a molar basis.

Post CDTX injection treatments

C57BL/6 female mice were given drinking water containing either corticosterone (Merck, 200 mg/L in 0.6% ethanol in water) or control water with 0.6% ethanol. All mice received an intramuscular injection of CDTX at 0.32 mg/kg in a final volume of 50 μL in the hamstring muscles. Mice subsequently received a subcutaneous injection of buprenorphine (0.075 mg/kg). Corticosterone-containing drinking water was replaced every 2 days. Alternatively, C57BL/6 mice were injected subcutaneously with injectable dexamethasone sodium phosphate at 10 mg/kg or saline control once daily from day 0–7 post-CDTX injection or otherwise specified.

Post SCI+CDTX mouse treatments

Mifepristone (Sigma Aldrich) was administered by gavage at 50 mg/kg once daily in vehicle (10% final dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) in peanut oil), or vehicle control between either day 0–7, day 0–2 or day 3–5 post-surgery. Relacorilant/CORT125385 (ChemieTek) was gavaged at 60 mg/kg bi-daily in vehicle (12% final DMSO in peanut oil), or vehicle control from day 0–2 post-surgery, a dose previously shown to reduce hyperglycemia in mouse models of polycystic ovary syndrome,85 liver steatosis in high fat diet fed mice,86 and dexamethasone-induced hyperinsulinemia and immunosuppression and gene expression in liver, adrenal and pituitary glands.87 Dexamethasone sodium phosphate was administered by subcutaneous injection at 10 mg/kg or saline control once daily from day 0–5 post-surgery. Ruxolitinib phosphate (LC Laboratories) was gavaged at 60 mg/kg between days 2–7 post-surgery twice daily, a dose shown to attenuate NHO in this model.15 Ruxolitinib was first dissolved in DMSO then diluted in vehicle (0.1% Tween 20, 5 mg/mL hydroxypropyl methylcellulose in water), control mice were administered vehicle. Indomethacin (SelleckChem) was gavaged at 2.5 mg/kg between days 0–2 post-surgery twice daily, a dose previously shown to be effective in mice to inhibit prostaglandin synthesis.88 Indomethacin was first dissolved in DMSO then diluted in vehicle (0.1% Tween 20, 5 mg/mL hydroxypropyl methylcellulose in water), control mice were administered vehicle.

Blood collection for mass spectrometry

For plasma isolation, blood samples for all mass spectrometry measurements were taken from mice by terminal cardiac puncture at days 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 and 7 post-surgery. All surgeries and harvests (inclusive of naive mice) were performed between 7:00-9:00a.m. (Zt0 to Zt2) to avoid day-to-day circadian GC concentration fluctuations. Blood was collected in tubes containing EDTA (4 mM final for 1 mL blood collected) and plasma was isolated by centrifugation (1000g for 10 min), plasma was subsequently transferred into new tubes and spun again (1000g for 10 min) and plasma stored at −80°C until use.

Plasma corticosterone and 11β-deoxycorticosterone concentrations were quantified using an automated on-line Solid Phase Extraction (SPE) which was coupled to a UPLC-MS/MS system. Plasma was diluted 1:1 with deuterated internal standards (d8-corticosterone and d8-11 deoxycorticosterone) in a ZnSO4/acetonitrile solution in 2 mL in a 96 well plate. After centrifugation, a 20μL aliquot of the supernatant was injected into the online SPE equipped UPLC-MS/MS system. The online sample manager loads the sample onto an OASIS XBridge C18 cartridge (Waters Corporation, Milford MA, USA) which is subsequently washed to remove interferences and the purified extract is eluted directly on to the ACQUITY UPLC BEH C18, 2.1 × 50 mm, 1.7 μm column. Quantification was performed by monitoring 2 transitions for each analyte using a Waters Xevo TQD mass spectrometer, with an assay time between injections of 4.0 min. The assay analytical range was up to 500 and 50 nM for corticosterone and 11β-deoxycorticosterone respectively. The Limit of quantitation (LOQ) with a CV of 20% was 0.5 nM for corticosterone and 0.05 nM for 11β-deoxycorticosterone. The inter-run imprecision across 4 levels for the 2 analytes were all <9%.

Tissue collection for histology and immunohistochemistry

Tissue samples from mice were collected between weeks 1–3 post-surgery. Mice were euthanized by CO2 asphyxiation and hind limbs were fixed 4% paraformaldehyde and decalcified in 14% EDTA. Decalcified hind limbs were embedded in paraffin and 5μm sections cut and placed on SuperFrost Plus slides (Menzel, Germany). Sections were deparaffinized and re-hydrated with xylene and graded ethanol and washed in Tris-buffered saline for immunohistochemistry. Immunohistochemistry using rat anti-mouse F4/80 monoclonal antibody (mAb), rabbit anti-mouse Osterix/Sp7 mAb, or purified isotype control antibodies rabbit IgG or rat IgG2b was performed using a 3 step procedure using species specific biotinylated F(ab)’2 secondary antibodies (biotinylated goat anti-rat IgG or goat anti-rabbit IgG) and horseradish peroxidase conjugated streptavidin (VECTASTAIN Elite ABC-Peroxidase Kit, ImmPACT DAB Peroxidase (HRP) Substrate) to detect primary antibodies. Hematoxylin and eosin staining was performed using a standard histology protocol. For Masson’s trichrome, 5μm sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated, then stained for 10 min in Weigert’s iron hematoxylin, rinsed under tap water for 10 min, developed in 1% acid alcohol, 1% hydrochloric acid in water for 15 to 30 s, rinsed in tap water (3 min) then distilled water, followed by staining in Biebrich scarlet-acid fuchsine solution for 10 to 15 min (Biebrich scarlet, 1% aqueous, acid fuchsine, 1% glacial acetic acid). Slides were then washed in distilled water and differentiated in 5% phosphomolybdic −5% phosphotungstic acid solution for 10 to 15 min or until collagen is not red. Slides were transferred into aniline blue solution for 5 to 10 min and rinsed in 1% acetic acid for 2 to 5 min, dehydrated, and mounted in resinous mounting medium.

Leukocyte isolation for flow cytometry

Leukocytes from lymph node, spleen, bone marrow, and muscle were isolated from mice for flow cytometric analysis at 4 days post-surgery. BM was isolated from a femur using 1 mL of PBS containing 2% FCS. Muscle leukocytes were isolated from mice as described.15 Hamstring muscle was cut into 1-mm pieces and a maximum of 0.5 g of tissue was used for each digestion as per the manufacturer’s instructions (Miltenyi Biotec). Dissociation of spleen was performed in 3mL PBS +2% FCS using a GentleMACS Dissociator tissue homogenizer (Miltenyi Biotec) using “spleen 3” setting, twice. Lymph nodes were dissociated using a 5mL syringe head in 1mL PBS with 2% FCS. Total leukocytes were then isolated into different cell populations using a CytoFLEX benchtop flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter Life Sciences) using the following mAbs; APC-Cy7 anti-mouse B220, FITC anti-mouse Ter119, PerCPCy5.5 anti-mouse CD3ε, Pacific Blue anti-mouse Ly6C, APC anti-mouse F4/80, PECy7 anti-mouse Ly6G, brilliant violet 510 anti-mouse/human CD11b, brilliant violet 785 anti-mouse CD45, and BD Horizon Fixable Viability Stain 700. All flow cytometry files were then analyzed using Flow Jo software version 10.5 (Becton, Dickinson and Company, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA).

GR intracellular staining for flow cytometry

For intracellular GR staining, blood and muscle cells were isolated as described above. Surface staining for muscle progenitor populations was firstly completed with the following monoclonal antibodies: FITC anti-mouse Ter119, PECy7 anti-mouse Sca1, Brilliant Violet 785 mouse CD45, PE mouse CD34, and Brilliant Violet 421 mouse CD31. Surface staining for blood and muscle leukocyte populations was firstly completed with the following conjugated monoclonal antibodies: FITC mouse CD3ε, PerCPCy5.5 mouse CD11b, PECy7 anti-mouse Ly6G, PE anti-mouse F4/80, APC-Cy7 anti-mouse B220 and Brilliant Violet 785 mouse CD45.

After surface staining, cells were washed (autoMACS Running Buffer), centrifuged and cell pellets resuspended for fixation and permeabilization using FIX & PERM Cell Permeabilization Kit, (ThermoFisher) as per manufacturer’s instructions. Once fixed and permeabilized, cells were incubated with either 1:80 dilution of rabbit anti-mouse GR monoclonal antibody or an equal concentration of rabbit mAb IgG isotype control (Cell Signaling Technologies) for 40 min on ice in PBS with 5 mM EDTA and 1% saponin to enable antibody access to nucleus and chromatin.89 Cells were washed, centrifuged and incubated with Cy5-conjugated F(ab)’2 donkey anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) cross absorbed against other species (Jackson ImmunoResearch) in PBS/EDTA with 1% saponin and 5 μg/mL purified normal donkey IgG for another 40 min on ice. Analysis of staining was carried out on a CytoFLEX benchtop flow cytometer. All flow cytometry files were then analyzed using Flow Jo software version 10.5 (Becton, Dickinson and Company, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA).

Muscle cell isolation and sorting for RNA extraction

As mentioned above, leukocytes were isolated from mouse hamstring muscles 4 days post-injury using a skeletal muscle dissociation kit (Miltenyi Biotech) as per manufactures instructions. Leukocyte populations were then sorted using a BD FACS Aria Fusion using the fluorescent mAbs specific for cell surface markers CD45, TER-119/Erythroid Cells, CD45R/B220, CD3ε, F4/80, CD11b, Ly6C, Ly6G, CD48 and cell viability as previously described.15

Muscle progenitor cells were isolated from hamstring muscles of naive C57BL/6 muscles using the muscle dissociation kit as above. Populations were sorted using a BD FACS Aria Fusion according to the following phenotypes: Satellite cells: CD45− lineage-(Ter119, B220, CD3ε, CD11b, Gr1)- CD31– CD34+ Sca1– integrin α7+; FAPs CD45– Lin– CD31– CD34+ Sca1+ integrin α7- as previously described.12

Muscle monocyte/macrophages were isolated from hamstring muscles of C57BL/6 muscles with a CDTX muscle injury and administered daily saline or dexamethasone (10 mg/kg daily), using the muscle dissociation kit and populations were sorted using a BD FACS Aria Fusion according to the following phenotype: Live (FVS700-negative) CD45+ Ter119- CD11b+ Ly6G− F4/80+. All cells were directly sorted into 1 mL Trizol LS (ThermoFisher).

RNA extraction and quantitative RT-PCR

For RNA isolation from bulk tissues, frozen muscle samples and ovaries (positive controls for Pgr mRNA expression) were homogenized in Trizol (Life Technologies), using a TissueRuptor (Qiagen) and after chloroform separation, RNA was isolated from aqueous phase. Reverse transcription of 1μg of RNA was performed using the SensiFAST cDNA Synthesis Kit (ThermoFisher) as per manufacturer’s instructions. Quantification of mRNA expression was performed using a single-step reverse transcription quantitative real-time RT-PCR using TaqMan fast PCR Master Mix and TaqMan gene expression assays specified in key resources table. Normalization of Ct values was performed using the expression of house-keeping gene Hprt or Rps20 and presented as ratio to house-keeping gene.

RNA sequencing of injured muscles

Mice were administered CDTX (0.32 mg/kg) via an intramuscular injection under anesthesia followed by daily intraperitoneal injection of saline or 10 mg/kg dexamethasone for 4 days. Following live μCT assessment to measure HO volume on day 4, hamstring muscles were harvested and RNA were isolated with Trizol as previously described.13 RNA samples were quantified using nanodrop and 10 μg of total muscle RNA samples were treated with DNA-free reagents (Invitrogen, cat # AM1906) according to manufacturer’s protocol.

The University of Queensland Sequencing Facility performed the library preparation and sequencing was performed. RNA-Seq libraries were produced using an Illumina Stranded mRNA Library Prep Ligation kit (Illumina, #20040534) and IDT for Illumina RNA UD Indexes (Illumina, 20040553) using the manufacturer’s protocol (Illumina, document # 1000000124518 v02, April 2021). In brief, a total of 80 ng of total RNA was enriched for mRNA using oligo-dT beads. Enriched poly-A+ RNA was heat fragmented and then reversed transcribed into first strand cDNA using reverse transcriptase and random primers. This cDNA was converted into dsDNA in the presence of dUTP to prevent subsequent amplification of the second strand, which maintains the strand orientation of the original mRNA. The 3′ ends of the cDNA were adenylated and pre-index anchors ligated. Libraries were amplified with 13 PCR cycles incorporating unique indexes for each sample to produce libraries ready for sequencing. Library concentrations were quantified using the PerkinElmer LabChip GX Touch with the DNA High Sensitivity Reagent kit (PerkinElmer, CLS760672) and then pooled in equimolar ratios.

Libraries were sequenced using the Illumina NovaSeq 6000. The library pool was diluted and denatured according to the NovaSeq protocol (Document # 1000000106351) and sequenced to generate paired-end 102 bp reads using a 100 cycle NovaSeq reagent Kit v1.5 (Illumina, 20028401). Fastq files were made using bcl2fastq2 (v2.20.0.422), including trimming the first cycle of each insert read due to expected low diversity.

Differential gene expression analysis was performed on Galaxy Australia platform (usegalaxy.org.au). To this end, sequencing reads were aligned to mouse reference genome mm10, using HISTA2 (Galaxy Version 2.2.1+galaxy0).90 Aligned reads were counted using featureCounts (Galaxy Version 2.0.1+galaxy2)91 and differentially expressed genes were subsequently analyzed with limma-voom (Galaxy Version 3.50.1+galaxy0).92,93 Normalized counts were presented in log2 of counts per million, CPM) and lowly expressed genes (log2(CPM) < 0.5) or genes expressed in only one sample were filtered out before further analyses. Principal component analysis was conducted using the prcomp function in Rstudio (v1.14656). Volcano plot was generated using EnhancedVolcano package https://github.com/kevinblighe/EnhancedVolcano). For DAVID analysis,94 lists of genes significantly (p < 0.05; FDR <0.0.05) up-regulated (log2(FC) ≥ 0.7) and down-regulated (log2(FC) ≤ −0.7) were established and pasted in the DAVID web page for further gene enrichment analysis using a minimum gene threshold of 4 and EASE score of 0.05. GSEA95,96 was performed using GO, Hallmark and KEGG gene sets (MSigDB molecular signature database v7.0)97,98 using GSEA software desktop (v4.0.3). Genes were ranked based on Log2FC∗(1-log(FDR)) and analyzed using Run GSEA Preranked tool. Leading edges of selected gene sets were generated using complexHeatmap function.99

In vivo FAP annexin V staining

C57BL/6 females underwent surgery (SCI or SHAM) followed by an intramuscular injection of CDTX and hamstring muscles harvested 3 days post-surgery. Muscle cells were isolated as described above and stained with the following surface markers: CD45-BV785, biotinylated anti-lineage (CD5, CD3ε, B220, CD11b, Gr1, Ter119), CD34-e660, CD31-BV421, Sca1-PECy7 and anti-ITGA7-PE antibodies followed by streptavidin (SAV)-BUV395. After surface staining, cells were washed in 1X PBS and then with annexin binding buffer (10mM HEPES pH = 7.4, 150mM NaCl, 5mM MgCl2, 5mM KCl, 1.8mM CaCl2). Cells were subsequently incubated with Annexin V-FITC (1/50 dilution) in binding buffer for 20 min at RT. After washing off excess Annexin V, cells were resuspended in binding buffer with a 1/5 volume of 7-amino actinomycin D. Analysis of staining was carried out using BD LSR Fortessa X20 cytometer.

Whole mouse muscle protein extraction for western blot

Muscle tissue samples were homogenized using IKA T10 basic homogenizer in chilled protein extraction buffer (0.3M NaCl, 10 mM EDTA, 30 mM Tris-HCl, 1%Triton X-100, 20 mM NaF, 0.1% NaN3, 1 mM Na3VO4, 10 mM β-glycerophosphate, cOmplete Protease Inhibitor, 10 mM levamisole) for 3 rounds of 20 s at maximum speed followed by incubation with mixing at 4°C for 15 min. After centrifugation (15,000 g at 4°C for 20 min), and protein concentrations in supernatants were measured with a BCA assay following manufacturer’s instructions.

Total 50 μg protein per mouse muscle were separated on a Bis-Tris pre-cast 4–12% acrylamide gel (ThermoFisher) and transferred onto a Hybond C Extra membranes (Amersham Biosciences) using the iBlot 2 Transfer stacks (Thermofisher) at 15V for 7 min. Membranes were blocked with Odyssey Blocking buffer for 1 h followed by incubation overnight at 4°C with 1/500 rabbit anti-phospho-STAT3 (Tyr705) XP mAb clone D3A7 (Cell Signaling) or 1/500 rabbit anti-total-STAT3 mAb clone 79D7 (Cell Signaling). After 3 washes in Tris-buffered saline (TBS) pH = 7.4 with 0.1% Tween 20, membranes were incubated with 1/7,500 IRDye 800CW-conjugated donkey anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) (LI-COR Biosciences) for 45 min at 4°C followed by 3 washes in TBS-0.1%Tween 20. Bands were detected in an Odyssey scanner (LI-COR Biosciences). After signal detection, primary and secondary antibodies on membrane were removed using Restore PLUS western blot stripping buffer (ThermoFisher Scientific).

Protein bands were quantified in Studio Lite Software (Version 5.2). Boxes of equal area were drawn for each sample and background intensity was subtracted from matching specific band intensity. For each independent sample (biological replicates) pSTAT3 band intensity was divided by the value obtained for total STAT3.

NHO volume quantification

All NHO volumes were quantified ex vivo or in vivo or using the Inveon micro positron emission tomography/computed tomography (μPET-CT) multimodality system (Siemens Medical Solutions Inc.) as we previously described13,63 except for Figures 1G, 2D, 7E, S9, and S10 where NHO volumes were measured in vivo using the Molecubes β-Cube and X-Cube μPET-CT system (Molecubes). For samples measured using the Inveon, parameters were: 360° rotation, 180 projections, 500 m exposure time, 80 kV voltage, 500 μA current, and an effective pixel size of 36 μm. 3D reconstitutions were performed using the Inveon Research Workplace software (Siemens Medical Solutions Inc.). For samples measured with Molecubes β-Cube, 3D reconstitutions and NHO volumes were quantified using VivoQuant 2021 (Invicro, Needham, MA, USA). To quantify NHO volumes, the region of interest (ROI) was drawn around the muscles containing NHO, and these were then carefully checked from three dimensions to ensure adjacent long bones were not included in the ROI. Calcified NHO regions were defined as above the threshold of 450 Hounsfield units (HU). To prevent bias for NHO bone volume quantification, one investigator scanned the mice and blinded the samples for another investigator to analyze.

Behavioral assessment of neurological and locomotor recovery

General overground locomotor performance and recovery were assessed by placing the mice in an open-field box with large circular area (0.9 m in diameter) and a transparent 30 cm high wall for 4 min. Locomotor performance was scored using the Basso Mouse Locomotor score (BMS) as described elsewhere.45 In brief, The BMS scale ranges from complete paralysis (score of 0) to normal locomotion (score of 9). It considers parameters including ankle movements, paw placement, stepping, coordination, trunk stability, and tail position. To prevent bias, more than one investigator was involved and at least one investigator was blinded to animal conditions until the experimental endpoint.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All data are presented as mean ± SD. Statistically significant differences were calculated using either two-sided Mann-Whitney test for 2 population comparisons, or one-way or two-way ANOVA, or non-parametric Friedman test for multiple comparisons using Prism software (version 8.0; GraphPad Software), with p values and numbers per group (n = number of animals or number of patients) specified in figure legends for individual experiments.
