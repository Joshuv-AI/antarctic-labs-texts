The Global Burden of Aortic Aneurysm and Its Attributable Risk Factors, 1990–2021: Results from the Global Burden of Disease Study 2021
Authors: Saeid Safiri, Fatemeh Amiri, Kuljit Singh, Reza Aletaha, Mark J. M. Sullman, Ali‐Asghar Kolahi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsAortic aneurysm (AA) is a potentially fatal aortic disorder, particularly when complicated by rupture. Using Global Burden of Disease 2021 estimates, this study reported worldwide changes in AA mortality, disability‐adjusted life years (DALYs), and modeled risk‐attributable burden from 1990 to 2021.MethodsWe reported Global Burden of Disease 2021 estimates and summarized findings using counts, percentages, and age‐standardized rates.ResultsIn 2021, the global age‐standardized death rate for AA was 1.9 per 100,000, representing a 26.7% decrease since 1990. The global age‐standardized DALY rate was 36.5 per 100,000, reflecting a 25.1% reduction. The highest national DALY rates per 100,000 were estimated in Armenia (192.8), Montenegro (178.9), and Nauru (114.5), while the lowest were in Saudi Arabia (5.1), Tajikistan (5.7), and Sri Lanka (6.7). The largest increases in age‐standardized DALY rates occurred in Georgia (357.4%), Uzbekistan (320.6%), and Sudan (209.4%), whereas the greatest decreases occurred in Australia (−70.6%), Bermuda (−66.6%), and Canada (−65.9%). For both sexes, DALY rates were highest in the 95+ years group, indicating concentration of fatal burden at advanced age. Smoking, high systolic blood pressure, and high body mass index were the leading modeled contributors to global AA DALYs, accounting for 37.3%, 17.1%, and 8.0%, respectively.ConclusionThis report identified marked variation in AA burden, potentially related to differences in screening, diagnosis, treatment access, and broader health‐system capacity. The results support targeted prevention, risk‐factor control, and improved vascular care, especially in resource‐limited settings. Reducing smoking and high systolic blood pressure may help lessen the future AA burden.

Introduction

In clinical terms, an aortic aneurysm (AA) refers to abnormal widening of the aorta, usually defined when the affected segment reaches more than 150% of the expected normal vessel diameter [1]. This condition poses a significant risk of rupture and sudden death, and ranks among the most challenging cardiovascular conditions to manage [2]. Mortality rates for AA are notably high, with over 80% of cases proving fatal once the aneurysm ruptures, and approximately 32% of patients die before reaching a hospital. Although abdominal aortic aneurysms are the most frequently observed, thoracic aortic aneurysms also significantly impact global morbidity and mortality [2, 3]. However, in the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) framework, AA is reported as an aggregated cause category; therefore, the present analysis cannot distinguish abdominal aortic aneurysm from thoracic aortic aneurysm, despite differences in their epidemiology, screening strategies, management, and prognosis. The development of AA reflects the interaction of inherited susceptibility, environmental exposures, and lifestyle‐related influences. The key risk factors identified by epidemiological studies include advanced age, male sex, smoking, and hypertension [4, 5, 6].

Currently, no effective pharmacological treatments are available for AA, and although aneurysm repair continues to carry perioperative risks, advances in contemporary surgical and endovascular techniques have markedly improved morbidity and mortality outcomes [7]. Despite its high mortality rate, AA has received less attention from public health initiatives compared to other cardiovascular conditions, such as coronary heart disease and myocardial infarction. Historically, AA mortality rates have trended upwards. Studies noted an increase in the United States between 1979 and 1991, where AA accounted for 1%–2% of all deaths among men aged 65 and older [8]. Globally, the AA prevalence between 1991 and 2001 ranged from 1.9% to 18.5% [9, 10, 11]. However, these figures may underestimate the true prevalence, as many AA cases develop asymptomatically, complicating precise tracking. Consequently, mortality data often provide a more reliable estimate of the disease burden. The GBD 2019 analysis reported an 82% increase in deaths and a 67% increase in DALYs due to AA between 1990 and 2019, and attributed much of this burden to high systolic blood pressure and smoking [12].

The AA burden varies considerably between regions. GBD 2017 estimates showed that the DALY numbers and age‐standardized DALY rates were greatest in regions with higher sociodemographic index (SDI) levels. By contrast, low‐ and middle‐SDI regions generally had smaller overall AA burdens, but showed larger increases in DALY rates, which may partly reflect expanding diagnostic capacity. Additionally, rising smoking rates and increasingly sedentary lifestyles in low‐ and middle‐income countries may contribute to a future rise in aneurysm cases [13]. Dietary factors, particularly excessive sodium intake, have also been implicated due to their associations with hypertension and vascular remodeling [14].

Due to the high mortality associated with AA and the benefits of early detection, several countries have implemented screening programs targeting selected high‐risk populations. Current guideline‐based evidence generally supports a single abdominal ultrasound examination to screen for abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA) in men aged 65–75 with a lifetime history of smoking. In men without this exposure, screening may be considered selectively, whereas routine screening is not advised for women unless specific risk factors are present [2]. The 2022 ACC/AHA Guideline for Aortic Disease similarly emphasizes a risk‐based approach and does not recommend population‐wide screening based on age alone [2]. In contrast, no established population‐based screening program exists for thoracic aortic aneurysm due to its lower prevalence and greater diagnostic complexity. Furthermore, the implementation and availability of screening programs vary across healthcare systems depending on local resources and disease burden. In low‐resource settings, substantial barriers to preventive care and diagnostic services remain, highlighting the need for improved awareness and access to vascular healthcare [2, 13].

Previous analyses using estimates generated through the GBD study have described the worldwide epidemiology of AA and the contribution of attributable risks, including reports from earlier GBD cycles [12, 13]. Nevertheless, analyses based on the latest GBD 2021 estimates are still needed to provide a more current and detailed account of changes over time and geographic differences in AA burden. For example, sex‑specific estimates have not always been provided, and the regional contributions of risk factors have not been fully presented [15]. Accordingly, we analyzed GBD 2021 estimates from 1990 to 2021 to report global AA mortality and DALY patterns across age, sex, and location, and to describe the risk‐related burden associated with available modifiable factors.

Methods

Overview

The present research used estimates from GBD 2021, conducted by the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation (IHME), to examine the burden of AA between 1990 and 2021. GBD 2021 provides internally consistent health estimates for 204 countries and territories, organized into 7 super‐regions and 21 regions. The analytical procedures applied in GBD 2021 follow the established GBD framework, with updates to data processing and modeling described in the main GBD 2021 methodological publications [16, 17]. The present research is a secondary analysis of modeled estimates from GBD 2021. No primary epidemiological data were collected; instead, we extracted and analyzed publicly available GBD 2021 estimates for AA. For the present analysis, we extracted GBD 2021 estimates for deaths, DALYs, age‐standardized rates, percentage changes in age‐standardized rates over the measurement period, and population‐attributable fractions for available risk factors. Estimates were extracted by year, sex, age group, country or territory, GBD region, and SDI category. We summarized these estimates globally, regionally, and nationally and generated figures to display temporal, geographic, age‐specific, sex‐specific, and SDI‐related patterns. We did not re‐estimate the underlying GBD models.

Case Definition and Data Inputs

Aortic aneurysms are characterized by full‐thickness dilatation of the aorta, typically resulting from atherosclerosis, elevated blood pressure, or blood vessel inflammation. Estimates of cause‐specific mortality encompass both abdominal and thoracic aortic aneurysms [16]. The IHME utilized vital registration data to model cause‐specific mortality for AA. ICD‐8 (International Classification of Diseases) data that were inconsistent with the overall time series and producing implausible trends were also excluded.

In the GBD 2021 framework, AA is coded as a combined disease category covering thoracic aortic aneurysm (TAA) and abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA), using the relevant ICD‐9 and ICD‐10 mortality and morbidity codes. The GBD framework does not generate separate estimates for AAA and TAA; therefore, all results presented herein represent the aggregated burden of both entities. This is a fundamental characteristic and limitation of the input data and the resulting estimates.

Modeling Strategy

A standard CODEm approach was employed to model AA deaths. For GBD 2021, significant changes were made to the redistribution and noise reduction methods. Specifically, a novel strategy was implemented to redistribute deaths coded to hypertension, drawing on data sources that included information on the sequence of clinical events preceding death. This update increased the number of deaths reallocated to cardiovascular diseases. The method for noise reduction was also revised; it was applied after redistribution to address both stochastic variation across time and space and the occurrence of small numbers of deaths in each location/year/age/sex. The updated empirical Bayesian noise reduction algorithm employs grouped data by region and data type as prior information, providing a more accurate reflection of regional patterns. A detailed description of these noise reduction updates by IHME is available in the previous publication [16].

Compilation of Results

The ICD codes for AA are as follows: ICD‐10 (I71–I71.9) and ICD‐9 (441–441.9). Years of life lost (YLLs), which quantify premature mortality, were estimated for AA by multiplying deaths in each age group by the corresponding remaining life expectancy from the GBD standard life table. Disability‑adjusted life years (DALYs) are typically estimated as the sum of YLLs and years lived with disability (YLDs); however, YLDs were not calculated due to the lack of robust data on non‑fatal health states. Consequently, the DALYs reported in this study consist entirely of YLLs. This should be considered when interpreting DALY estimates and when comparing them with those of other diseases that include a non‑fatal component. The uncertainty around each estimate was quantified using the standard GBD draw‐based approach. At each stage of estimation, 1000 draws were used to carry forward uncertainty arising from the underlying data sources, adjustments for residual non‐sampling variability, and measurement error. The 95% uncertainty intervals (UIs) were then calculated from the distribution of these draws, using the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles as the lower and upper limits, respectively.

Attributable burden was assessed for the risk factors included for AA in the GBD comparative risk assessment framework: lead exposure, high systolic blood pressure, high body mass index, high sodium intake, low vegetable intake, low fruit intake, and smoking. These risk factors were included because they are available within the GBD risk‐factor estimation framework for AA. Definitions and relative risks for these risk factors concerning AA are available elsewhere [17]. Country and regional rankings are presented descriptively and should be interpreted in light of the uncertainty intervals, particularly where intervals overlap.

R statistical software, Version 4.2.3, was used to tabulate the data and generate the figures. For the SDI analysis, smoothing spline models were fitted to describe variation in AA burden across socio‐demographic index (SDI) values. No formal hypothesis testing was performed; therefore, no a priori significance level was specified, and one‐sided or two‐sided testing was not applicable. Analyses stratified by year, age, sex, location, and SDI were descriptive and based on the standard GBD analytical framework.

This study was a secondary analysis of publicly available, aggregated GBD 2021 estimates and did not involve individual‐level participant data. Ethical approval was granted by the Ethics Committee of Shahid Beheshti University of Medical Sciences, Tehran, Iran, under approval code IR.SBMU.RETECH.REC.1404.254. Informed consent was not required.

Results

Global Burden and Trends

In 2021, the global burden of AA was an estimated 153,927 deaths (95% UI: 138,413–165,739), with an age‐standardized rate of 1.9 per 100,000 (95% UI: 1.7–2.0), reflecting a 26.7% reduction (95% UI: −31.1 to −23.1) from 1990 to 2021. Because YLDs were not estimated for AA in GBD 2021, all DALY estimates reported in the results consist entirely of YLLs and should be interpreted as reflecting fatal burden. Additionally, the global DALYs attributable to AA amounted to 3,107,762 (95% UI: 2,857,320–3,353,858), with an age‐standardized rate of 36.5 per 100,000 (95% UI: 33.5–39.5), indicating a 25.1% decrease (95% UI: −30.5 to −20.3) over the same period (Table 1).

Regional‐Level

In 2021, the regions with the highest age‐standardized mortality rates from AA were High‐income Asia Pacific (4.4, 95% UI: 3.7–4.8), Tropical Latin America (4.0, 95% UI: 3.7–4.3), and Eastern Europe (3.8, 95% UI: 3.5–4.1). Conversely, the regions with the lowest age‐standardized death rates were East Asia (0.5, 95% UI: 0.4–0.6), North Africa and the Middle East (0.9, 95% UI: 0.8–1.0), and Andean Latin America (0.9, 95% UI: 0.8–1.1). Regions experiencing the largest increases in age‐standardized death rates included Central Asia, South Asia, and the High‐income Asia Pacific. Central Asia recorded a marked rise of 110.1% (95% UI: 71.5–150.9), South Asia saw an increase of 71.6% (95% UI: 27.9–136.1), and High‐income Asia Pacific recorded a 56.9% rise (95% UI: 44.1–67.3). In contrast, the most substantial reductions in age‐standardized death rates from 1990 to 2021 were found in Australasia, High‐income North America, and Western Europe. Australasia saw a 67.6% decrease (95% UI: −70.5 to −65.2), High‐income North America had a reduction of 60.6% (95% UI: −61.9 to −59.5), and Western Europe experienced a 46.2% decline (95% UI: −48.9 to −44.2) (Table 1). Figures S1 and S2 provide the corresponding estimates separately for males and females, including the 2021 death rates and the changes in these rates since 1990.

In 2021, the highest age‐standardized DALY rates were found in Eastern Europe (91.3, 95% UI: 83.9–98.5), Tropical Latin America (91.1, 95% UI: 85.5–95.5), and High‐income Asia Pacific (79.7, 95% UI: 71.8–84.5). Conversely, the regions with the lowest age‐standardized DALY rates were East Asia (13.3, 95% UI: 10.6–17.0), North Africa and the Middle East (20.0, 95% UI: 17.3–23.1), and Andean Latin America (20.1, 95% UI: 16.8–24.3). Between 1990 and 2021, the greatest increases in age‐standardized DALY rates for AA were estimated in Central Asia, South Asia, and High‐income Asia Pacific. Central Asia recorded the largest increase, with an 80.9% rise (95% UI: 47.1–115.6); corresponding increases were 63.6% (95% UI: 22.3–125.7) in South Asia and 51.0% (95% UI: 41.2–61.0) in High‐income Asia Pacific. Over the same period, the largest decreases were estimated in Australasia, High‐income North America, and Western Europe. The age‐standardized DALY rate decreased by 69.3% (95% UI: −71.7 to −67.1) in Australasia, by 56.8% (95% UI: −58.2 to −55.7) in High‐income North America, and by 47.5% (95% UI: −49.6 to −45.6) in Western Europe. These regional estimates are presented in Table 1, and the corresponding sex‐specific DALY rates and temporal changes are provided in Figures S3 and S4.

Country‐Level

In 2021, age‐standardized death rates per 100,000 from AA varied substantially across nations. The highest were recorded in Armenia (9.2, 95% UI: 7.–6–0.8), Montenegro (8.7, 95% UI: 6.6–11.3), and Nauru (6, 95% UI: 3.5–9.1). In contrast, the lowest were observed in Saudi Arabia (0.2, 95% UI: 0.2–0.3), Tajikistan (0.3, 95% UI: 0.2–0.3), and Afghanistan (0.3, 95% UI: 0.2–0.4) (Table S1 and Figure 1A). Between 1990 and 2021, age‐standardized death rates from AA showed notable variation between countries. The three countries with the most substantial increases were Uzbekistan (385.9%, 95% UI: 225.1%–595.0%), Georgia (374.2%, 95% UI: 260.2%–510.0%), and Morocco (231.7%, 95% UI: 143.2%–358.2%). Conversely, the countries with the greatest reductions in age‐standardized mortality rates during the same period were Guam (−74.4%, 95% UI: −80.0 to −67.6%), Australia (−69.0%, 95% UI: −72.0 to −66.3%), and Canada (−65.7%, 95% UI: −69.1 to −62.4%). These country‐level patterns are shown in Figure 1B and Table S1.

In 2021, age‐standardized DALY rates due to AA showed substantial national disparities. The highest rates per 100,000 population were recorded in Armenia (192.8, 95% UI: 159.7–228.2), Montenegro (178.9, 95% UI: 134.8–230.0), and Nauru (114.5, 95% UI: 82.5–151.9). Conversely, the lowest were noted in Saudi Arabia (5.1, 95% UI: 3.7–6.7), Tajikistan (5.7, 95% UI: 4.1–7.8), and Sri Lanka (6.7, 95% UI: 4.6–9.4) (Table S2 and Figure 2A). Regarding changes from 1990 to 2021, Georgia exhibited the greatest increase, with a rise of 357.4% (95% UI: 242.4%–498.6%), followed by Uzbekistan, with an increase of 320.6% (95% UI: 199.2%–472.1%), and Sudan, with a 209.4% increase (95% UI: 44.5%–469.8%). Conversely, the largest decreases were found in Australia (−70.6% [95% UI: −73.3 to −68.2%], Bermuda (−66.6% [95% UI: −72.0 to −59.9%]), and Canada (−65.9% [95% UI: −69.0 to −62.7%]) (Table S2 and Figure 2B).

Sex‐ and Age‐Pattern

In 2021, global mortality patterns for AA varied substantially by sex and age, with both mortality counts and age‐specific death rates increasing sharply at older ages. In younger and middle‐aged populations, males mostly had higher mortality counts and age‐specific rates than females, indicating a greater fatal burden among men in these age groups. This pattern changed in the oldest age groups when considering absolute deaths: from 85 to 89 years onward, female death counts were equal to or greater than male counts, and the number of female deaths reached a peak in the 90–94 years age range. This likely reflects the larger number of women surviving to advanced ages. However, the age‐specific death rate pattern did not fully mirror the absolute death counts; in the 95+ years group, the largest age‐specific mortality rates were estimated in both sexes, with rates slightly lower among females than males (Figure 3A).

In 2021, the global distribution of DALYs due to AA also varied by age and sex, with both DALY counts and age‐specific DALY rates increasing markedly at older ages. Because YLDs were not estimated for AA in GBD 2021, the DALYs reported here consist entirely of YLLs and therefore reflect fatal burden. Males had higher DALY counts and age‐specific DALY rates than females through younger and middle adult age groups, indicating a larger fatal burden among men in these age groups. In older age groups, female DALY counts increased and, from the 85–89 years age group onward, equalled or exceeded male counts, with the highest female DALY counts observed in the 90–94 years age group. This pattern should be interpreted in relation to the larger number of women surviving to very old ages, rather than as evidence of higher age‐specific risk in women. The highest age‐specific DALY rates for both sexes were observed in the 95+ years group, underscoring the concentration of fatal AA burden at advanced ages (Figure 3B).

Association Between Burden and Socio‐Demographic Index

Risk Factors

Attributable DALY fractions were assessed for the risk factors available in the GBD comparative risk assessment framework. Within the GBD comparative risk assessment framework, the largest modeled attributable fraction of age‐standardized AA DALYs was for smoking, which accounted for 37.3% of the global burden, followed by high systolic blood pressure at 17.1% and high BMI at 8.0%. Because risk exposures may overlap, attributable fractions should not necessarily be interpreted as mutually exclusive or additive. Regionally, the burden attributable to smoking was highest in East Asia (50.9%), Eastern Europe (46.6%), and North Africa and the Middle East (45.4%). For high systolic blood pressure, Eastern Europe had the highest burden at 20.7%, closely followed by Central Europe at 20.6% and Central Asia at 20.3%. Regarding high body mass index, Eastern Europe, North Africa, and the Middle East all recorded burdens of 11.7%, while Central Europe was slightly lower at 11.5% (Figure 5).

Discussion

Drawing on GBD 2021 modeled estimates, this analysis characterizes changes in the worldwide fatal burden of AA between 1990 and 2021, considering deaths, DALYs, age and sex distributions, regional and national patterns, SDI‐related variation, and the contribution of available risk factors. Our findings highlight substantial global disparities in the burden of AA. Despite notable developments in screening and treatment, AA remains a serious and fatal health issue. While age‐standardized rates for deaths and DALYs decreased worldwide, their absolute numbers have risen since 1990, exceeding 153,000 deaths and 3 million DALYs in 2021. The burden rose with SDI levels up to about 0.77 and decreased thereafter. Regionally, high‐income areas, especially North America and Europe, exhibited a heavier burden than low‐income regions, though high‐income regions showed the most substantial reductions in mortality and DALY rates. The burden rose with age, with the 95+ age group recording the highest age‐specific death and DALY rates across both sexes. Our assessment of risk factors revealed associations with high sodium intake, lead exposure, dietary risks, high BMI, high systolic blood pressure, and smoking, with variations observed across different regions. These findings, which build upon existing knowledge, may enhance the understanding of AA epidemiology and inform future public health discussions.

The regional distribution observed in the present analysis is broadly consistent with earlier GBD‐based reports, although the updated 2021 estimates provide a clearer picture of how patterns have changed over time. Several high‐income regions, particularly High‐income North America and Western Europe, continued to show a comparatively high AA burden, but they also experienced some of the largest reductions in age‐standardized mortality and DALY rates from 1990 to 2021. In 2021, the highest age‐standardized mortality rates were estimated in High‐income Asia Pacific, Tropical Latin America, and Eastern Europe, while the lowest were estimated in East Asia, North Africa, and the Middle East. Temporal changes also varied substantially across regions, with Central Asia showing the largest increase in age‐standardized mortality and DALY rates, while Australasia, High‐income North America, and Western Europe showed the greatest decreases.

Previous GBD studies using 2019 and 2017 data indicated declines in age‐standardized mortality and DALY rates in high‐income areas, which may partly reflect better healthcare along with improved screening, preventive, and treatment measures [2, 13]. A modeling analysis by Song et al. also noted regional differences, with high‐income countries bearing the highest burden. South‐East Asia reported a high AA burden, possibly linked to elevated smoking rates in that region. In contrast, Africa had the lowest rates, although data availability may have affected the accuracy of these findings [18]. A population‐based study in Sweden by Zommorodi et al. found that lower‐income, less‐educated communities experienced higher rates of ruptured AA, whereas wealthier regions had fewer ruptures. This suggests that the higher mortality rates in low‐income regions could be associated with more frequent ruptures rather than a higher prevalence of AA [19].

Socioeconomic factors and healthcare access are likely important contributors to regional disparities. In lower‐income areas, limited access to regular screening may result in underdiagnosis, leading to lower reported prevalence but increased mortality from aneurysm rupture. This underreporting can obscure the true AA burden in these regions. Additionally, limited access to effective treatment often results in poorer outcomes after rupture. The costs associated with treatment, including surgery and expensive medications, may deter patients in these regions from seeking necessary care, posing serious public health concerns [20, 21, 22, 23]. In contrast, the advanced screening programs in North America and Western Europe employ modern diagnostic tools to monitor high‐risk populations and detect AA at earlier stages, which has been associated with a reduction in the rupture rates and associated deaths and DALYs [6, 24, 25, 26].

Taken together, the regional findings suggest that many high‐income and high‐SDI settings have recorded marked declines in AA death and DALY rates over the past 30 years. These reductions may reflect better access to diagnostic imaging, screening of selected high‐risk groups, improvements in perioperative care, management of modifiable risk factors, and wider availability of open and endovascular repair, although these explanations cannot be confirmed directly from the GBD estimates. The 2021 data also showed clear variation by age and sex. Among younger and middle‐aged adults, men generally had higher numbers of deaths and higher age‐specific death rates than women. At more advanced ages, particularly from 85 years onward, the absolute number of deaths among women equalled or exceeded that among men, probably because more women survive into these older age groups. This pattern should therefore be interpreted as a difference in absolute burden rather than as evidence that women have higher age‐specific risk. For both sexes, age‐specific death and DALY rates rose with age and were highest in the 95+ years group. These findings are broadly consistent with previous GBD 2019 estimates and with established demographic risk patterns reported in earlier epidemiological studies [1, 2, 5].

Furthermore, the higher AA burden in men may be attributed to lifestyle factors, genetic predispositions, and biological vulnerability to vascular diseases, as noted in pathophysiological studies, including research by Annunziata et al. [27]. Additionally, variations in disease presentation, surgical outcomes, and management between genders highlight the need for gender‐specific approaches in AA care [28, 29]. In the aging population, it is also important to consider that chronological age is often secondary to biological frailty. Frailty has emerged as a critical determinant of clinical outcomes in AA patients, influencing both the feasibility of surgical intervention and the risk of perioperative mortality [30].

Previous research has identified several contributors to AA incidence and mortality. Studies have identified hypertension, smoking, age, and genetic susceptibility as key risk factors, illustrating AA's complex etiology [4, 5]. In addition, GBD 2019 data identified smoking, hypertension, diet, sodium intake, and lead exposure as potential contributors [2]. Other studies suggest factors such as age, sex, smoking, and air pollution increase disease susceptibility [31, 32, 33]. Our findings using GBD 2021 data largely align with these established risk factors, showing higher AA rates among older adults and men, who are more frequently exposed to risks such as smoking and hypertension. In addition to previously studied risk factors, we identified high BMI as a contributor, particularly in Eastern Europe and the North Africa and Middle East regions. Recent studies have also investigated genetic factors, identifying specific genes that may influence aneurysm risk and progression. A family history of AA suggests that genetic counseling and targeted screening could be beneficial for high‐risk individuals [34].

Although lifestyle changes, such as smoking cessation and blood pressure control, have shown promise in reducing AA risk, the overall burden remains substantial. This suggests that current interventions may not effectively reach all populations or may lack the necessary impact [31, 35]. Advances in diagnostic and surgical methods, alongside updated management guidelines, have improved AA care, particularly in high‐income areas. For example, endovascular aneurysm repair (EVAR) and advanced imaging techniques are associated with lower AA‐related mortality rates [36, 37, 38]. The selection of these interventions, particularly EVAR in older populations, is increasingly guided by frailty assessments to optimize patient outcomes [39]. Additionally, machine learning models show potential for further improving AA management [40]. These technologies may facilitate earlier detection and treatment and may have contributed to reduced mortality and DALYs in high‐income regions. Compared with previous studies, our findings suggest that reductions in the overall burden may be associated with improved medical management, lifestyle changes, and preventive measures. Nevertheless, persistently elevated DALY rates in several regions indicate that stronger health‐system capacity, wider access to diagnostic services, and timely availability of surgical and endovascular care remain important priorities, especially in low‐ and middle‐income settings.

The updated estimates presented here may support prevention planning by characterizing major modeled contributors to AA burden, particularly smoking and high systolic blood pressure. However, several limitations are present. Firstly, data quality varies across regions, with high‐income countries generally collecting more reliable data than low‐income areas, where underreporting or misclassification may lead to less accurate burden estimates. Secondly, GBD data did not include AA incidence or prevalence, which are crucial metrics for effective disease management. Future research should prioritize the collection of incidence and prevalence data. Additionally, underdiagnosis in regions with limited healthcare access indicates that the actual AA burden may be higher than reported. Although GBD employs robust modeling tools to mitigate these issues, reliance on estimates in data‐scarce regions could introduce bias. Furthermore, data collection during the COVID‐19 pandemic may also have disrupted reporting systems, further affecting accuracy. Additionally, the study did not differentiate between AA subtypes, such as abdominal and thoracic aneurysms, which may have distinct epidemiological patterns. Because the GBD framework aggregates abdominal and thoracic aortic aneurysms, the observed regional and temporal patterns may reflect different mixtures of aneurysm subtypes across populations. This limits direct clinical interpretation, because AAA and TAA differ in epidemiology, screening pathways, management, and prognosis. Finally, this study does not address the role of genetics or familial history. Moreover, the GBD dataset does not contain frailty measures, which prevents assessment of how biological vulnerability affects the observed mortality and DALY trends at an individual level. Given the ecological design and reliance on modeled GBD estimates, the observed patterns, including the risk‐attributable fractions, describe population‐level associations rather than causal relationships at the individual level. Future research should investigate genetic factors and frailty indices to inform personalized screening and prevention strategies, especially as populations age and the AA burden increases with rising life expectancy.

Conclusion

This study examined global AA mortality and DALY patterns from 1990 to 2021, with emphasis on regional and demographic variation. Although high‐income regions have seen substantial declines in AA‐related mortality and DALYs, some low‐ and middle‐income countries have shown stagnation or increases in these measures. Young and middle‐aged men bore a larger fatal burden, although women aged 85 and older had comparable or higher absolute death counts. The asymptomatic nature of AA until rupture remains an important public health challenge. Improved screening strategies and appropriate resource allocation may help support earlier detection and management. Further reductions in AA burden may require stronger control of modifiable risks, particularly smoking and hypertension, alongside broader access to timely diagnosis and specialist vascular care.
