A Task-Oriented Evaluation Framework for Text Normalization in Modern NLP Pipelines
Md Abdullah Al Kafi, Raka Moni, Sumit Kumar Banshal

Abstract
Text normalization is an essential preprocessing step in many natural language processing (NLP) tasks, and stemming is one such normalization technique that reduces words to their base or root form. However, evaluating stemming methods is challenging because current evaluation approaches are limited and do not capture the potential harm caused by excessive stemming; therefore, it is essential to develop new approaches to evaluate stemming methods. To address this issue, this study propose a novel, task-oriented approach to evaluate stemming methods, which considers three aspects: (1) the utility of stemming using Stemming Effectiveness Score (SES), (2) the impact of stemming on downstream tasks using Model Performance Delta (MPD), and (3) the semantic similarity between stemmed and original words using Average Normalized Levenshtein Distance (ANLD), thus providing a comprehensive evaluation framework. We apply our evaluation framework to compare two stemmers for Bangla (BNLTK) and English (Snowball), and our results reveal a significant issue, prompting us to analyze their performance in detail. While the Bangla stemmer achieves the highest SES (1.67) due to effective word reduction (CR = 1.90), SES alone is insufficient because our proposed safety measure, ANLD, reveals that this high SES is due to harmful over-stemming (ANLD = 0.26), which correlates with the observed decrease in downstream performance.In contrast, the English stemmer achieves a moderate SES (1.31) with a safe meaning distance (ANLD = 0.14), allowing its word reduction to contribute positively to downstream performance; therefore, it is a more reliable stemmer. Our study provides a valuable tool for distinguishing between potential efficiency gains (high SES) and meaning preservation (low ANLD). Normalization tools should be evaluated for meaning-safety thresholds before optimizing for word reduction, as this approach ensures that the stemming method is both practical and safe.

Introduction

Assessment of text normalisation is complex and inconsistent, and existing studies typically assess only one aspect - either the amount of reduction in vocabulary size or the performance on a specific task. This does not capture the trade-off between computational savings, task performance and preservation of meaning, because an aggressive stemmer may achieve a high reduction in vocabulary size, but this can come at the cost of meaning and hence degrade performance on other tasks. Conversely, a small increase in task performance may be insignificant if it is not statistically significant, therefore a standardised measure that captures all these aspects simultaneously is needed, thus enabling a more comprehensive evaluation of text normalisation techniques.

Take the overly aggressive stemmer as an example, we might think that it’s good that it aggressively reduces vocabulary size, but at what cost, because the ”win” is at the expense of meaning. Will this ”win” harm downstream tasks, or on the flip side, will it result in a small bump in classification performance, and is this bump meaningful, or just statistical noise? The evaluation of normalization, as done in the literature, is not capable of answering these questions.

The literature problem is the lack of a standard for assessing normalization in a multi-dimensional fashion incorporating efficiency and performance and semantic integrity, and we fill the literature gap by introducing and validating a task-oriented framework for assessing normalization that simultaneously accounts for efficiency and performance and semantic integrity.

The proposed framework consists of a multi-step evaluation:
it includes Macro-Level Effectiveness, which quantifies the trade-off between vocabulary reduction and document-level semantic preservation, using the Stemming Effectiveness Score (SES) proposed by us,
Downstream Task Impact, which assesses the practical effect on model performance, using the Model Performance Delta (MPD), along with statistical significance testing,
and Micro-Level Semantic Fidelity, which quantifies the extent of word-level distortion and error, using the Average Normalized Levenshtein Distance (ANLD).
We demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed framework by means of a comparative case study on English and Bangla, a morphologically rich language, and we show that the proposed framework is highly effective in extracting insights that a single-metric assessment would miss.
By using all components together, we show how a seemingly ”effective” normalization method can be destructive at the same time, therefore providing a complete picture to a researcher to take an evidence-based decision.

Background and Related Work

Text normalization has been a standard preprocessing technique in NLP for decades, and it has been used to reduce vocabulary size, speed up computation, and increase performance in information retrieval tasks. However, in the context of today’s NLP pipelines, its utility is more questionable, because while normalization reduces lexical variation, it also potentially hides meaning, especially in morphologically rich languages. Therefore, the trade-off between the two competing objectives requires a principled and reliable evaluation framework that jointly accounts for practical utility and linguistic safety.

3
Methodology

Our proposed evaluation framework provides a multidimensional assessment of text normalization techniques, covering intrinsic compression, semantic preservation, micro-level distortion, and downstream model sensitivity. We applied our approaches to three datasets: Bangla morphological, Bangla sentence, and an external corpus from Mendeley Data.

3.1
Dataset Preparation

Two main parallel datasets are used in this study, allowing both word-level and sentence-level evaluation. Together, they enable analysis of morphological distortion, semantic drift, and performance sensitivity across normalization methods.
In this experiment, two publicly available datasets published in Mendeley and adapted from the Bangla Text Sentence Dataset (BTSD)
14
;
9
were used and adapted. The example of the datasets is shown in Figure
1
.
The first corpus contains several columns, including Bangla text, English translation, complexity class, voice, and other metadata. For the present study, only the Bangla text, its English translation, and the complexity class (simple, compound, complex) were considered, as these components share similar structural characteristics suitable for benchmarking classification performance. The sentence-level complexity of the dataset enables the examination of stemming and lemmatization effects in both languages, with Bangla exhibiting greater inflectional variation across sentence structures than English.The dataset is nearly perfectly balanced across the three classes, containing 888 simple, 892 compound, and 879 complex sentences. This justifies the use of Accuracy as our main metric, as the controlled conditions allow it to be a good representative of the overall model performance, and the parallel corpus also allows us to keep syntactic complexity and semantic content in check across languages, which helps isolate the effect of morphological richness in the preprocessing. Each sentence is written as a single entry to reduce confounding factors such as context, style or topic, making the experiment more controlled and less noisy. In addition to the dataset described, we also consider a parallel corpus for sentiment classification, which consists of 9,163 examples, comprising 4,139 positive, 2,826 negative and 2,189 neutral examples. The class imbalance is intentional and realistic, because it is for the purpose of testing the performance of our proposed frameworks with skewed distributions.

3.2
Proposed Framework

The purpose of this framework is to evaluate normalization methods from four complementary perspectives: macro-level compression, downstream performance, document-level semantic preservation, and micro-level string fidelity, and together, the five evaluations provide a holistic evaluation that avoids the pitfalls of single metric-based evaluation and reveal both the advantages and the hidden pitfalls of each normalization strategy.

3.2.1
Component 1: Compression Ratio

The first part of the framework addresses the macro-level effects of stemming, which we quantify by measuring vocabulary reduction. The percentage reduction in vocabulary is defined as:

CR=\frac{\text{\# Unique Words Before Stemming}}{\text{\# Unique Words After Stemming}}

(1)

Here, the symbol “#” represents the total count of unique tokens.

•

CR>1.0
: represents a more successful vocabulary compression; a larger reduction percentage indicates more aggressive stemming.

•

CR=1.0
: indicates that there is change in vocabulary size (ineffective normalization).

•

CR<1.0
: represents vocabulary expansion, a rare anomaly where the process increases the number of unique tokens (e.g., due to erratic tokenization).

3.2.2
Component 2: Model Performance Delta (MPD)

In order to measure the impact of stemming on actual model performance, we utilize the Model Performance Delta (MPD) adapted from Harman
20
. Which calculates the change in model performance based on normalization, thereby giving us a better understanding of whether stemming has a positive or negative impact on overall results.

MPD=\text{Metric}_{\text{stemmed}}-\text{Metric}_{\text{orig}}

(2)

Positive
MPD
values indicate an improvement of the model after normalization, and negative MPD values indicate that the model became worse. In our experimental setup, we always combine MPD with statistical significance tests, such as p-values, in order to distinguish actual effects from random noise; thus, any statement about the effect of stemming is supported by statistical evidence rather than by random variation

3.2.3
Component 3: Information Retention Score (IRS)

IRS measures how well the meaning of the document is preserved after stemming by computing the semantic similarity between the original document and its stemmed/normalized version, and we compute this by calculating the cosine similarity between the two aggregated embedding vectors. Higher IRS means that the stemmed text is closer in meaning to the original document.

IRS=\cos(\theta)=\frac{V_{\text{orig}}\cdot V_{\text{stemmed}}}{\|V_{\text{orig}}\|\,\|V_{\text{stemmed}}\|}

(3)

Here,
V_{\text{orig}}
and
V_{\text{stemmed}}
are the sentence/document level vectors obtained by averaging the contextualized token embeddings, which were generated using transformer-based models, specifically
csebuetnlp/banglabert
for Bangla and
distilbert-base-uncased
for English.

3.2.4
Component 4: Stemming Effectiveness Score (SES)

The Stemming Effectiveness Score (SES) is a score that combines the compression ratio (CR) and semantic similarity (SS) into a single score, which provides an intuitive measure of stemming effectiveness that rewards methods that increase the compression ratio at the expense of semantic similarity and penalizes methods that decrease the compression ratio at the expense of semantic similarity. It is defined as:

SES={(IRS)}*{(CR)}

(4)

SES (Stemming Evaluation Score) allows the capture of the trade-off between vocabulary reduction and meaning retention because it favors those stemmers with the best vocabulary reduction (CR ¿ 1) and semantic similarity (IRS close to 1). The higher the SES score, the better the stemming method in reducing the feature space while retaining the meaning of the text. However, the SES score should not be used alone, because a stemmer that is too aggressive could lower CR by a large margin and score a high SES score while destroying morphology and nuance. Therefore, use SES along with the ANLD score to ensure that the stemming is not morphologically destructive.

3.2.5
Component 5: Average Normalized Levenshtein Distance (ANLD)

The final component is a measure of micro-level semantic fidelity, which we use the Average Normalized Levenshtein Distance (ANLD) to estimate, because this metric can highlight situations in which the semantics appear to be preserved at a coarse level, but the underlying morphology has been changed in a severe and potentially harmful way. We calculate the Levenshtein distance between the original word and its stemmed variant, normalize this distance by the length of the original word, and average this value across the corpus. An ANLD of 0 implies that the two forms are identical, while a score of 1 means that the transformation is maximal. This is an important metric, because it can highlight situations in which the semantics appear to be preserved at a coarse level, such as a strong IRS, but the underlying morphology has been changed in a severe and potentially harmful way.

3.2.6
Experimental Setup

We validate our evaluation framework by conducting a downstream text classification task using 4 commonly used machine learning models (Logistic Regression, Naive Bayes, Support Vector Machine, Random Forest), and for all models, we use the default hyperparameters provided by scikit-learn to maintain consistency and prevent any bias caused by model specific tuning. For semantic comparison, we compute Information Retention Scores (IRS) using sentence- or document-level embeddings from transformer models and mean pooling, while the other metrics, Model Performance Delta (MPD), Stemming Effectiveness Score (SES), Average Normalized Levenshtein Distance (ANLD) and Compression Ratio (CR) are computed separately for each normalization. Therefore, together, these metrics offer an all-round understanding of how each normalization behaves across intrinsic properties, semantics and downstream task performance.

Step 3: Information Retention Score and SES

In the third step of the evaluation framework, we focus on measuring how much semantic information is preserved after normalization (

To address this, we compute the Information Retention Score (IRS), which quantifies semantic similarity between the original and normalized text using contextual embeddings. We further combine IRS with Compression Ratio (CR) to obtain the Stemming Effectiveness Score (SES), providing an integrated measure of both semantic preservation and vocabulary reduction.

The IRS results indicate that BNLTK is more faithful to the document-level meaning than Snowball, which is expected given its less aggressive normalization strategy, and in conjunction with its higher compression ratio, BNLTK has the best SES, indicating the best overall balance between vocabulary reduction and the original meaning. However, both the IRS and SES metrics are based on transformer embeddings, which capture only coarse contextual meaning and do not detect fine-grained morphological changes, and this can lead to overestimation of the performance of a normalization method, and neglects issues such as over-stemming. Therefore, the IRS and SES metrics are not sufficient to guarantee true linguistic fidelity, and their limitations motivate the use of a micro-level metric to detect subtle morphological disruptions, which we introduce in the following using the Average Normalized Levenshtein Distance (ANLD).

4.4
Step 4: Average Normalized Levenshtein Distance (ANLD)

To evaluate micro-level fidelity, we use Average Normalized Levenshtein Distance (ANLD), which measures the average amount of character-level change introduced by each normalization method, and ANLD is a value between 0 and 1. An ANLD of 0 means that the original surface form was completely preserved, while an ANLD of 1 means that the original surface form was completely transformed. While embedding-based metrics such as IRS indirectly measure the impact of morphological distortion, ANLD directly measures how much of the surface form has been transformed, making it particularly well-suited for detecting destructive over-stemming.

6
Conclusion

In this study, we propose a multi-dimensional evaluation framework for text normalization in NLP pipelines that evaluates a text normalizer’s performance in three dimensions: macro-level efficiency (SES), micro-level semantic fidelity (ANLD), and downstream sensitivity (MPD), and using this framework, we study the effects of stemming and lemmatization in English and Bangla text normalization and observe a clear trade-off between compression and meaning. Our experimental results provide strong evidence for why SES may be misleading on its own, because BNLTK Bangla stemmer, which achieved the highest SES (1.67) due to its compression power (CR = 1.90), was actually efficient because of significant semantic distortion (ANLD = 0.26) and loss of downstream accuracy. In contrast, the English Snowball stemmer achieved the lowest SES (1.31) and the safest semantic distance (ANLD = 0.14) while yielding consistent performance gains, therefore, we conclude that efficiency should be gated by semantic safety.

The results of this work demonstrate that overly aggressive compression is detrimental to downstream performance when the distortion exceeds the ANLD distortion threshold, however, ANLD should be seen as a qualifying check before SES optimization. In this work, we use traditional machine learning classifiers to keep the results isolated to the morphological effects, and in future work, we plan to apply this framework to transformer-based models (e.g. BERT, GPT) to see if contextual embeddings can recover the meaning loss caused by aggressive stemming.
