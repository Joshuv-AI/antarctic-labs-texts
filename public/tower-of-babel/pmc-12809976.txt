Unleashing the potential role of tumor-associated NK cells as a novel immunotherapeutic target in triple-negative breast cancer
Authors: Jianyu Pang, Yongzhi Chen, Hui Wang, Yuheng Tang, Qi Qi, Yingjie Sun, Silin Zhong, Shuxiong Luo, Jianglin Wu, Limin Xu, Xuhong Zhou, Wenru Tang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Breast cancer is the most prevalent malignancy among women worldwide, with triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) comprising 10–20% of cases. Although immunotherapy has recently emerged as a promising treatment, its efficacy remains limited in immunodeficient subtypes such as TNBC. Tumor-associated NK cells (TaNKs), an immunologically exhausted NK cell subset, have not yet been characterized in TNBC. Elucidating the TNBC tumor microenvironment through TaNKs may therefore provide novel diagnostic and therapeutic insights. Here, we employed single-cell transcriptomics to characterize TaNKs, which are broadly distributed across cancers. TaNKs displayed profound functional impairment, with diminished cytotoxicity and heightened stress responses, thereby compromising immune surveillance. Bulk RNA-seq analysis revealed that elevated TaNKs abundance correlated with poor prognosis in TNBC patients. Using machine learning, we established a TaNKs feature score (TaNKFS) incorporating six genes (HSPA1B, TUBB2A, BAG3, NR4A2, IER2, and MYADM), which demonstrated strong prognostic value and effectively quantified TaNKs levels in TNBC. Notably, immune checkpoint inhibitors showed minimal benefit in cases with high TaNKFS. Furthermore, deep learning-based drug screening and high-throughput molecular docking identified twelve candidate compounds targeting TaNKFS, including six clinically available agents (Staurosporine, Bexarotene, Cyclophosphamide, Vancomycin, Heroin, and Fluorouracil). Synergy prediction further suggested multiple effective combination regimens. In summary, this study delineates the role of TaNKs in TNBC, establishes a prognostic biomarker, and proposes novel therapeutic strategies integrating machine learning and deep learning approaches.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13058-025-02182-8.

Introduction

The incidence of breast cancer, the most prevalent malignant tumor among women worldwide, continues to rise, partly due to factors such as population aging, lifestyle changes, and environmental influences [1, 2]. Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) is a distinct subtype of breast cancer characterized by the lack of expression of estrogen receptors (ER), progesterone receptors (PR), and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) [3]. Recent studies indicate that TNBC accounts for approximately 10–20% of all breast cancer cases and is more commonly diagnosed in younger women [4]. Due to the absence of these receptors, TNBC is resistant to hormonal and targeted therapies, making its treatment particularly challenging. Recent advancements in multidrug combination therapy and immunotherapy have shown promise in managing TNBC. For instance, the combination of adriamycin and tretinoin not only enhances the antitumor effect but also significantly reduces cardiotoxic side effects in mouse models [5]. In the realm of immunotherapy, immune checkpoint inhibitors such as PD-L1 inhibitors have gained widespread use. However, the efficacy of immunotherapy in TNBC is limited due to the heterogeneity of TNBC tumors and the generally low level of immune cell within these tumors [6]. Consequently, there is an urgent need for current research to delve deeper into the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) in TNBC and to develop novel targeted therapeutic strategies.

Natural Killer (NK) cells are a crucial subset of lymphocytes that circulate throughout the body and rapidly mobilize to sites of infection or tumor formation when needed [7]. They express a variety of activating and inhibitory receptors on their surface, allowing them to interact with target cells and regulate their activation and antitumor functions [8]. NK cells are classically categorized into two main subtypes: CD56bright NK cells and CD56dim NK cells. CD56bright NK cells, which express high levels of CD56 (encoded by the NCAM1 gene), possess high cytotoxic potential but exhibit low cytolytic activity, and constitute only 5–10% of NK cells in peripheral blood circulation. In contrast, CD56dim NK cells, which express high levels of CD16 (encoded by the FCGR3A gene), make up 90% of NK cells in peripheral blood and are characterized by both high cytotoxicity and cell-killing activity. Recent studies involving single-cell mapping of NK cells across various cancers have identified additional subtypes with distinct functional characteristics, including HSP40 + tumor-associated NK cells (TaNKs) [9]. TaNKs represent a dysfunctional NK cell subtype, marked by reduced cytotoxicity, increased stress activity, elevated expression of inhibitory receptors (such as CD158a and CD158b), and lower levels of cytotoxic granules like granzyme B (GZMB) and perforin. Pan-cancer studies have shown that high levels of TaNKs are associated with poor prognosis across multiple cancers, including breast cancer. However, the specific distribution and role of TaNKs within the TNBC subtype remain unclear. Therefore, investigating NK cell heterogeneity in TNBC tumors is crucial not only for understanding the molecular mechanisms underlying immunotherapy resistance but also for identifying new therapeutic targets.

In recent years, the advent of single-cell transcriptome sequencing (scRNA-seq) technology has provided researchers with an unprecedented opportunity to delve into the complexity and diversity of cells under both physiological and pathological conditions [10]. This cutting-edge technology addresses several limitations of traditional sequencing methods by enabling high-throughput gene expression analysis at the single-cell level. As a result, scRNA-seq can reveal variations in cell types, functions, and subpopulations, as well as track cellular dynamics during development, disease progression, and therapeutic interventions. In oncology, scRNA-seq has become an invaluable tool for studying tumor heterogeneity, analyzing immune cell component, and screening for drug targets. Through scRNA-seq data, researchers have uncovered distinct expression profiles and functional disparities among different cell subpopulations within tumors, laying the groundwork for more individualized and precise cancer therapies [11]. Simultaneously, Artificial Intelligence (AI) technology has demonstrated remarkable potential across various disciplines, emerging as a focal point in science and technology. In the medical field, AI is extensively applied to support physicians in diagnosing diseases, predicting disease risks, and formulating personalized treatment strategies. For instance, the application of deep learning algorithms in medical image analysis has achieved significant success in accurately diagnosing and predicting conditions such as tumors and cardiac lesions [12]. In summary, this study comprehensively analyzed the single-cell transcriptional profiles of triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) with a focus on the heterogeneity of NK cell subpopulations by integrating scRNA-seq data. It explored the transcriptional variations, functional distinctions, and differentiation statuses among different NK cell subpopulations, thereby elucidating the mechanisms underlying TNBC’s resistance to immunotherapy. Moreover, the study investigated the correlation between NK cell heterogeneity and the prognosis of TNBC patients, utilizing machine learning algorithms to identify genetic features for the development of prognostic diagnostic models, potentially enhancing clinical diagnostic approaches. Additionally, deep learning techniques were employed to predict drug-target interactions and synergistic effects for promising gene features, offering novel strategies for targeted and combination therapies in TNBC. Methods

Data sources used for analysis

The 10X Genomics scRNA-seq data of TNBC tumor samples were organized and downloaded in GEO, including GSE143423, GSE176078, GSE148673 and GSE161529. The Bulk RNA-seq data used in the study mainly originated from the TCGA database (TCGA-BRCA) and the METABRCA (METABRCA-TNBC) database. Additionally, the data cohorts used for external validation were GSE58812 and GSE173839, respectively.

Data integration analysis

Gene expression matrices from four scRNA-seq datasets were imported into R and subjected to quality control. The four gene sets were subsequently integrated into a unified Seurat object. Batch correction was performed on the integrated object using the Harmony algorithm, accounting for sample grouping to minimize technical variability introduced by different experimental conditions across the datasets. UMAP dimensionality reduction and cell clustering (resolution = 0.5) were then applied to the integrated object (Figure S2A). Initial cell annotation was performed automatically using SingleR, followed by manual refinement to ensure accuracy. The cell marker genes used in this study are listed in Table S1A.

To further explore cellular diversity, a second-level annotation was performed after the primary categorization. This included extracting lymphoid immune cells (Figure S2B), myeloid immune cells (Figure S2C), and both normal and malignant epithelial cells (Figure S2D). The same analysis pipeline was applied to the immune cell subtypes, with adjusted resolution parameters: 0.7 for lymphoid immune cells and 0.6 for myeloid immune cells. In contrast, CopyKAT was employed to distinguish normal from malignant epithelial cells.

Exploration of function and differentiation characteristics based on the heterogeneity of NK subgroups

After isolating the NK cell subsets, dimensionality reduction and clustering were performed according to the standard analysis pipeline, with a resolution set to 0.7 (Figure S2E). Cell annotation was carried out based on marker genes of major NK subtypes as reported in the literature (Table S1B). Following the completion of the third-level annotation of NK subsets, functional exploration was conducted through functional scoring and pseudotime analysis of differentiation states. Functional scoring was performed using the AUCell algorithm, utilizing the characteristic gene set listed in Table S2. For pseudotime analysis, Monocle was employed to calculate the differentiation trajectory. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) of the NK subsets (P-value < 0.05, LogFC threshold = 0.25, Min.pct = 0.1) were used to construct this trajectory. Dimensionality reduction of the data was performed using DDRTree. Finally, each NK cell subset was positioned along the differentiation trajectory to examine variations in their differentiation timelines.

CIBERSORT-based reverse convolution for NK cells components

The marker gene expression profiles of NK cell subsets and the quality-controlled bulk RNA-seq tissue sequencing data were collected. For the NK cell subsets, the average expression levels of the top ten most highly expressed genes for each subset were extracted. For the bulk RNA-seq data, negative values were excluded, and genes with low expression were filtered out. Subsequently, CIBERSORT deconvolution was performed on the TCGA-TNBC, METABRCA-TNBC, and GSE58812 datasets, with parameters set to 1000 permutations and quantile normalization applied.

Extraction of transcriptional characteristics of TaNKs

For the scRNA-seq, DEGs between TaNKs and other NK cell subtypes were calculated with parameters set to only.pos = TRUE, min.pct = 0.1, logfc.threshold = 0.25, and p_val < 0.05. To this end, the SCENIC algorithm was utilized to identify transcription factors with heightened activity within the TaNKs subpopulations. The DEGs and transcription factors were combined, and duplicate genes were removed, resulting in a set representing the transcriptional characteristics of TaNKs in the scRNA-seq dimension. For the bulk RNA-seq, the abundance of TaNKs in METABRCA-TNBC was combined with the gene expression profile of METABRCA-TNBC. The Correlation coefficient between each gene and the abundance of TaNKs was calculated. Genes meeting the threshold (p-value < 0.05, correlation > 0.1) were identified as the transcriptional characteristics of TaNKs in the bulk RNA-seq dimension. Finally, to accurately pinpoint the characteristic information of TaNKs, the intersection of the gene characteristics from both dimensions was taken, and a Venn diagram was created.

Screening for prognostic gene signatures of the TaNKs

Univariate Cox regression was employed to assess the impact of individual variables on survival time or survival rate, without accounting for the influence of other factors. This method utilizes the Cox proportional hazards model, which associates an individual’s risk of death with their gene expression levels, thereby estimating the extent of the gene expression level’s influence on mortality risk. For each gene characteristic variable of TaNKs in the METABRCA-TNBC dataset, a formula related to survival time and status was constructed. Cox regression analysis was subsequently performed for each gene characteristic variable of TaNKs. The hazard ratio (HR), 95% confidence interval, and p-value were extracted from the analysis. The HR quantifies the relative magnitude of survival risk between two groups. Specifically, if the HR is greater than 1, it suggests that the gene variable proactively affects prognosis. Conversely, if the HR is less than 1, it indicates a protective effect on prognosis. Finally, gene characteristic variables of TaNKs with an HR > 1 were identified as having a detrimental impact on prognosis. The results from the univariate Cox regression were visualized using a forest plot.

Machine learning modeling for dimensionality reduction of prognostic gene characteristics

Gene characteristics of TaNKs with an HR > 1 from the univariate Cox regression analysis were selected for further dimensionality reduction. The gene expression profiles of the training set (METABRCA-TNBC), validation set 1 (TCGA-TNBC), and validation set 2 (GSE58812) were filtered based on these characteristics and used as features for training the machine learning model. Based on the abundance of TaNKs, the high abundance group was assigned a label of 1, and the low abundance group was labeled as 0. These labels served as the labels for training the machine learning model. After merging the datasets, the data were exported and imported into Python for further processing and transformation.

Following data preprocessing, machine learning models including Support Vector Machine (SVM), Random Forest Classifier (RFC), and Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) were constructed. Hyperparameter tuning was performed using grid search with a random seed of 1004 and tenfold cross-validation. The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was plotted, and the area under the curve (AUC) was used as the evaluation metric. For SVM, data preprocessing included standardization of the three datasets to improve prediction accuracy and generalization. A linear kernel was chosen to obtain the weight parameters for each gene characteristic. For RFC and XGBoost, after model parameter optimization and training, the “feature_importances_” function was used to export the importance indices of the gene characteristics for further analysis. The SVM model weights, along with the importance indices from RFC and XGBoost, were sorted in descending order by absolute value. The top 20 gene characteristics with the highest values were extracted, and the intersection of these three sets was taken as the final result of the machine learning-based dimensionality reduction.

Multivariate cox model construction of the TaNKs feature and external validation

The intersection of gene characteristics identified by the three machine learning algorithms was used as input to construct a multivariate Cox model. All intersecting gene characteristics were correlated with survival time and survival status. By considering the interaction of multiple gene characteristics on prognosis, the contribution of each gene characteristic to survival could be assessed more accurately. Gene characteristics with an HR > 1 were selected, and their regression coefficients were incorporated as parameters in the TaNKFS prognostic model.

To validate TaNKFS, time-dependent ROC analysis was performed to evaluate the model’s accuracy in predicting 1-year, 3-year, and 5-year survival rates. Additionally, predicted survival rates, as determined by TaNKFS, were dichotomized into high and low groups based on the median, and survival differences were assessed using Kaplan–Meier (KM) curves. The predicted survival rates were then ranked in ascending order, and the survival/death status was aligned with a heatmap of prognostic gene expression to evaluate the prognosis associated with high TaNKFS levels. The Correlation between the model genes, TaNKFS, and TaNKs abundance was also analyzed to evaluate the model’s utility in predicting TaNKs abundance.

Application of TaNKFS in clinical practice

To facilitate the clinical application of TaNKFS, we developed a Nomogram to predict the 1-year, 3-year, and 5-year survival rates of patients based on TaNKFS. The nomogram also includes a calibration curve to evaluate the predictive accuracy of the model.

Using TaNKFS-based survival analysis grouping, we compared the expression of 11 immune checkpoint inhibitor targets (PD-L1, CTLA-4, PD-1, LAG-3, TIM-3 HAVCR2, TIMD4, IDO-1, TIGIT, OX40, GITR, and BTLA) between the high and low TaNKFS groups in the METABRCA-TNBC cohort. Furthermore, we applied the GSVA algorithm to assess the T cell exhaustion score in the same cohort. The gene list used for this analysis is provided in Table S3A.

The correlations between these molecules and TaNKFS were also assessed to determine the potential efficacy of current immunotherapy drugs for TaNKFS patients. Further validation was conducted using the immunotherapy dataset GSE173839 (Sample distribution statistics are shown in Table S3B) to compare TaNKFS levels between the response and non-response groups in both chemotherapy and immunotherapy cohorts. The Wilcoxon test was applied for pairwise comparisons, with a p-value < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Screening of potential targeted drugs using drug databases and molecular docking

The human protein structures (receptors) for HSPA1B, TUBB2A, BAG3, NR4A2, IER2, and MYADM were retrieved from the UniProt database. To minimize experimental errors, the predicted PDB structures from AlphaFoldDB were utilized. Subsequently, the Comparative Toxicogenomics Database (CTD) was searched for small molecule compounds (ligands) associated with these six genes, and the relevant compounds were exported. Small molecule compounds targeting the reduction of these genes were then screened, with duplicates removed.

Molecular docking simulations were performed using AutoDock, where receptors and ligands were docked, and the binding affinity was assessed based on the docking binding energy. According to previous studies, a docking binding energy below 0 indicates an interaction between the ligand and receptor, with smaller values signifying stronger binding affinity. Finally, potential targeted drugs were visualized using PyMOL.

Integration of DeepPurpose and AutoDock for large-scale prediction of protein-molecule interactions

DeepPurpose employs various deep learning models with an end-to-end training strategy, where model parameters are updated using the backpropagation algorithm and optimizers, allowing the model to progressively converge and improve prediction accuracy [13]. This study primarily utilizes DeepPurpose for large-scale prediction of drug-target interactions.

First, protein sequences for HSPA1B, TUBB2A, BAG3, NR4A2, IER2, and MYADM were obtained from the UniProt database. Simultaneously, the SMILES molecular structures of 804,959 compounds reported in the literature were gathered as ligand inputs [14]. These protein sequences and ligand data were then combined, resulting in 4,829,754 protein–ligand combinations for analysis. DeepPurpose was used to perform the prediction task, with the Convolutional Neural Network model, as recommended by the developers of DeepPurpose, selected for final predictions.

The prediction results were sorted based on binding energy to identify high-affinity compounds for each gene. The top 500 compounds with the highest predicted binding scores were selected for further docking using AutoDock to validate prediction accuracy. Finally, compounds with the best molecular docking binding energies, meeting drug screening criteria, were chosen as potential targeted compounds.

DeepSynergy predicts drug synergy

To design combination therapies that maximize antitumor efficacy while minimizing adverse effects, we employed DeepSynergy, a deep learning-based framework for predicting drug synergy between candidate targeted agents and small molecules [15]. DeepSynergy applies neural network models to learn molecular representations by mapping chemical structures into low-dimensional feature vectors, thereby capturing similarities and differences among compounds. Based on these representations, the model predicts both the efficacy of single agents and the synergistic potential of drug combinations.

The framework is trained on large-scale experimental datasets encompassing diverse tissues and cell lines, and leverages iterative deep neural network optimization to enhance predictive accuracy. Consequently, DeepSynergy enables reliable assessment of the therapeutic potential of drug pairs across distinct cellular contexts.

In the present study, we applied DeepSynergy to breast cancer cell lines, including EFM192B, KPL1, MDAMB436, OCUBM, T47D, and ZR751. First, PubChem CID identifiers of candidate targeted agents were collected as input. The corresponding SMILES molecular structures were retrieved from the PubChem database using an in-house Python script. Following data preprocessing, DeepSynergy was executed to compute synergy scores for multiple drug combinations across the selected cell lines. Notably, higher synergy scores indicate stronger drug-drug interactions, implying enhanced therapeutic efficacy.

Statistical analysis

R version 4.1.1 was used for statistical analysis. Nonparametric tests were used for statistical tests between different groups, and Log Rank Test was used to test for significant differences in survival probability between samples, with P-value < 0.05 indicated statistical significance (*P < 0.05; **P < 0.01; ***P < 0.001; ****P < 0.0001). Spearman Rank Correlation Analysis was used to calculate correlations.

scRNA-seq revealed significant cellular diversity within TNBC tumors. We integrated data from four GEO databases, including profiles from 24 TNBC patients, yielding high-quality data from 106,282 cells and 35,304 genes. Lymphoid lineage immune cells, such as NK cells, T cells, B cells, and plasma cells, were further categorized into eight distinct subpopulations (Fig. 1B): plasma cells, NK cells, CD8 + T cells, CD4 + T cells, Exhausted T cells (Tex), Regulatory T cells (Treg), Natural Killer T cells (NKT), and B cells. Myeloid immune cells (Fig. 1C), including macrophages, dendritic cells, neutrophils, and mast cells, were subdivided into 10 distinct subpopulations.

We used a copy number variant algorithm to differentiate cancer cells from normal epithelial cells (Fig. 1D). Interestingly, although epithelial cells and cancer cells were initially indistinguishable, the copy number analysis revealed that malignant epithelial cells (cancer cells) exhibited non-diploid copy numbers, distinguishing them from normal epithelial cells. Despite this, the distinction between normal epithelial and cancer cells remained complex, suggesting that these cell types share overlapping features. In addition, Fig. 1E displays marker genes for these cell types, confirming the accuracy of the classification results.

Interestingly, cancer cells were predominantly detected in only one sample (Fig. 1F). The absence of widespread cancer cells in other samples may reflect the inherent tumor heterogeneity in TNBC. This suggests that the cancerous transformation of epithelial cells could be confined to specific stages of tumor development, or that the detection method was not able to capture the full spectrum of tumor cells across different patients. It is also noteworthy that NK cells are present in the majority of patients, but their detection is limited in samples with a higher proportion of epithelial/cancer cell components. This observation suggests that the immune microenvironment within TNBC tumors may progressively deteriorate as the tumor advances.

To validate these annotations, functional studies were performed. As shown in Fig. 2D, CD56dim NK cells exhibited high cytotoxicity, consistent with their role in immunotoxicity. NK cell depletion analysis revealed a decline in immune function, increasing susceptibility to infections and immune escape by tumors (Fig. 2E). Exhausted NK cells displayed elevated inflammation scores (Fig. 2F), suggesting over-activation, which may contribute to tumor immune evasion. TaNKs, characterized by heightened stress levels (Fig. 2G), showed signs of immune imbalance, potentially leading to functional depletion and tumor progression. Additionally, TaNKs and Exhausted NK cells exhibited higher HLA inhibitory receptor activity, whereas CD56bright and CD56dim NK cells displayed higher HLA-activating receptor activity, enhancing their responsiveness to external stimuli (Fig. 2H).

Pseudotime analysis of NK subpopulation differentiation further validated these findings (Fig. 2I and J). Naive NK cells were positioned at the early stage of differentiation, while CD56bright and CD56dim NK cells were located at a more differentiated stage with heightened immune activity. Exhausted NK cells and TaNKs, positioned near the end of the differentiation axis, exhibited declining immune function, marked by excessive pro-inflammatory factor secretion. This dysregulation disrupts the immune microenvironment and facilitates tumor immune escape. The expression trends of the top ten genes from each NK subtype, mapped onto the differentiation axis (Fig. 2K), confirmed the accuracy of the subpopulation annotations. Genes highly expressed in Naive NK cells clustered at the early end of the differentiation axis, while genes highly expressed in TaNKs were found at the late end of the axis, further validating their respective differentiation states.

This study not only systematically characterized the heterogeneity of NK cells in the TIME of TNBC, but also verified the existence of TaNKs, a cell subpopulation commonly found in various cancers in TNBC, an immunodeficient tumor.

TaNKs are strongly linked to adverse prognosis in TNBC

To further investigate the presence of TaNKs in TNBC, we conducted deconvolution analysis using bulk RNA-seq data from three independent cohorts (Fig. 3). Specifically, we reverse-calculated the proportions of various NK subtypes in the bulk RNA-seq datasets, leveraging data from their corresponding single-cell RNA-seq counterparts. Unfortunately, the results revealed significant variability in the proportion of each NK subtype across the different bulk RNA-seq datasets, likely due to noise introduced by pooled samples and experimental batch differences. Notably, NK subtype signals were detected in the METABRCA-TNBC dataset (Fig. 3A and B). In contrast, the TCGA-TNBC dataset showed elevated levels of exhausted NK cell signals (Fig. 3D and E), while the GSE58812 dataset exhibited nearly undetectable CD56dim NK cell signals (Fig. 3G and H). These discrepancies highlight substantial variability in NK cell content among TNBC patients, further emphasizing the tumor’s heterogeneity and immunodeficiency. Survival analysis (Fig. 3C, F, I) showed that TaNKs were associated with significantly poorer survival in the high-level group across all datasets. In contrast, naive NK cells (Figure S3A) exhibited lower survival in the high-level group, while CD56bright (Figure S3B), exhausted NK cells (Figure S3C) and CD56dim (Figure S3D) showed varied survival trends across datasets, likely due to the mixed tissue nature of Bulk RNA-seq. High levels of TaNKs exacerbate poor prognosis, aligning with studies suggesting their role in promoting tumor immune escape, inflammation, and angiogenesis.

In summary, TaNKs are strongly associated with poor prognosis in TNBC, aligning with their behavior observed across the pan-cancer context. Consequently, a deeper exploration of the genetic characteristics of prognosis-related TaNKs will facilitate the development of molecular therapeutic strategies for immunodeficient tumors, including TNBC. To this end, we extracted TaNKs-related genetic features from both scRNA-seq and bulk RNA-seq datasets (Fig. 4A). Through scRNA-seq, we identified 367 upregulated gene features by analyzing DEGs and transcription factors. Moreover, transcription factors, such as p53, play critical regulatory roles in cancer initiation and progression. Therefore, identifying transcription factors that are upregulated in TaNKs is as crucial as analyzing DEGs. For bulk RNA-seq, Correlation analysis of the METABRCA-TNBC cohort revealed 3,103 gene features associated with TaNKs. Cross-referencing both datasets, we identified 95 candidate gene features. Univariate Cox regression analysis (Fig. 4B) identified 40 features with hazard ratios (HR) > 1, linked to poor prognosis. Based on TaNKs clustering across three cohorts, we developed three machine learning models: RFC (Fig. 4C), SVM (Fig. 4D), and XGBoost (Fig. 4E). These models demonstrated an average AUC > 0.7 across all three cohorts. TaNKFS is an effective tool for prognostic assessment in TNBC and for evaluating TaNKs abundance

After several rounds of screening, 10 TaNKs-related gene features strongly associated with TaNKs and poor prognosis in TNBC patients were identified. Multivariate Cox regression was then employed to construct the final prognostic model (Fig. 5A). This approach not only assessed the impact of each individual gene feature on patient prognosis but also integrated the prognostic information from multiple genes, enhancing the model’s generalizability. The TaNKs-related gene-based prognostic score (TaNKFS) serves as a robust indicator for assessing TaNKs abundance and predicting prognosis in TNBC patients. Correlation analysis (Fig. 5B) demonstrated that TaNKFS, as a composite measure of six genes, exhibited a strong correlation with TaNKs abundance (R = 0.53), surpassing the correlation of any individual gene with TaNKs abundance. This suggests that TaNKFS can effectively evaluate TaNKs abundance in TNBC. Among the six components, TUBB2A displayed the strongest correlation with TaNKFS (R = 0.75), suggesting that it may serve as a key driver of the composite score.

Multiple analyses consistently showed a strong association between TaNKFS and poor prognosis. Time-dependent ROC curves across three cohorts (Fig. 5C: METABRCA-TNBC, 5F: TCGA-TNBC, and 5I: GSE58812) revealed AUC values greater than 0.6 for predicting 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival, confirming its predictive power. Kaplan–Meier survival analyses (Fig. 5D: METABRCA-TNBC, 5G: TCGA-TNBC, and 5J: GSE58812) indicated significantly lower survival rates in the high TaNKFS group, further validating its accuracy in predicting poor prognosis. Due to limited data availability, the association between TaNKFS and progression-free survival (PFS) was assessed only in the TCGA-TNBC cohort (Figure S4A). The analysis indicated that, although the difference in PFS between the high and low TaNKFS groups was not statistically significant, there was a trend toward poorer PFS in the high TaNKFS group. Additionally, the distribution of patient survival status showed a clear trend: as TaNKFS increased, the proportion of deceased patients also increased (Fig. 5E: METABRCA-TNBC, 5H: TCGA-TNBC, and 5K: GSE58812). The gene expression heatmap further confirmed that TaNKFS positively correlates with the expression levels of these six genes, suggesting that their overexpression is associated with poor prognosis. This is corroborated by the KM curves (Figure S4D–G). In addition, differential expression analysis across three independent cohorts (Figure S5A–C) demonstrated that all six genes were significantly upregulated in the high TaNKFS group, further supporting these findings.

Assessment of the clinical value of TaNKFS

To enhance the clinical application of TaNKFS in diagnosis and treatment, we developed a Nomogram to calculate patient survival rates based on TaNKFS. As shown in Fig. 6A, the Nomogram utilizes the expression levels of six genes to estimate the 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival probabilities for patients. This tool allows clinicians to integrate pathological features with intuitive data, facilitating trend observation and supporting decision-making. As a result, it offers valuable guidance for clinical trials and therapeutic strategies. Given the role of TaNKs in promoting tumor immune evasion and their prognostic significance, exploring the relationship between TaNKs signatures (TaNKFS) and immunotherapy may help refine treatment strategies. We assessed the relative expression of 11 common immune checkpoint inhibitor targets (Fig. 6C). The results revealed a notable downregulation of nearly all immune checkpoints in the high TaNKFS group. Correlation analysis (Fig. 6D) further confirmed significant negative correlations between TaNKFS and all immune checkpoints. In addition, the T cell exhaustion score was highly expressed in the low TaNKFS group (Fig. 6E) and showed a significant negative correlation with TaNKFS (Fig. 6F, R = − 0.46), further supporting the immune predictive value of TaNKFS. These findings suggest that tumors with higher TaNKs content may exhibit reduced immune checkpoint expression, implying that conventional immune checkpoint inhibitors may be less effective in high-TaNKs tumors, including TNBC.

To further validate these findings, we compared TaNKFS levels between responders and non-responders in breast cancer immunotherapy cohorts, which were treated with either chemotherapy or immunotherapy (Fig. 6G). The analysis revealed significantly elevated TaNKFS levels in non-responders to immunotherapy, indicating that patients with higher expression of this marker may have poorer responses to immunotherapy. While not statistically significant, non-responders in the chemotherapy group also exhibited a trend toward higher TaNKFS levels. In summary, both conventional chemotherapy and immune checkpoint inhibitor therapies appear to have limited efficacy in TNBC patients with high TaNKs content, highlighting the need for more targeted and precise treatment approaches.

Multidimensional screening of potential target drugs for TaNKFS

Given the limited therapeutic benefit of immune checkpoint inhibitors in TNBC patients with high TaNKs abundance, there is an urgent need for the development of novel targeted therapies. Our previous research identified that genes constituting the TaNKFS: HSPA1B, TUBB2A, BAG3, NR4A2, IER2, and MYADM, are overexpressed in TNBC with high TaNKs abundance and are strongly associated with poor prognosis. To identify potential therapeutic candidates, we screened the CTD, a widely utilized repository of documented gene-compound interactions, and integrated this with high-throughput computational simulations to select compounds capable of effectively reducing the expression levels of these six genes, without posing risks to human health or the environment.

Molecular docking simulations revealed high-affinity drugs for all six genes. For instance, BAG3 exhibited strong binding with the potent anticancer agent Staurosporine (Fig. 7A, − 5.00 kcal/mol), while IER2 displayed a significant interaction with the retinoid drug Bexarotene, used to treat cancers such as cutaneous T-cell lymphoma (Fig. 7B, − 8.57 kcal/mol). Cyclophosphamide, a classic antitumor drug, showed high affinity for HSPA1B (Fig. 7C, − 9.34 kcal/mol), while MYADM interacted strongly with Vancomycin (Fig. 7D, − 341.06 kcal/mol). Other noteworthy interactions included NR4A2 with heroin (Fig. 7E, − 18.20 kcal/mol) and TUBB2A with Fluorouracil (Fig. 7F, − 3.18 kcal/mol). These findings provide novel insights into repurposing existing drugs to target TaNKs-associated genes.

In addition to CTD screening, we employed Deep Leaning technology to predict potential target-specific small molecules from a library of 804,959 compounds, resulting in 4,829,754 compound-gene combinations. These predictions were further validated through high-throughput molecular docking simulations. Notably, BAG3 exhibited strong interaction with compound CID 2327752, while HSPA1B showed strong binding with compound CID 24705, with docking energies of − 5.34 kcal/mol and − 10.08 kcal/mol, respectively (Table S4). MYADM demonstrated the highest binding affinity with compound CID 155931 (− 7.29 kcal/mol), and NR4A2 showed favorable interaction with compound CID 4283 (− 18.5 kcal/mol). These compounds present additional molecularly targeted therapeutic options for tumors with high TaNKs content.

Through multidimensional screening combining CTD and DeepPurpose predictions, this study identified several promising candidate drugs for further investigation. However, potential toxicity concerns must be considered; future research should explore combination therapies to enhance efficacy while minimizing adverse effects.

Deep learning discovers drug synergies

We identified twelve potential TaNKFS-targeting compounds through two independent approaches. The next step involves using DeepSynergy to predict the synergistic effects of compound combinations, analyzing all possible pairings, and calculating their synergy values across various breast cancer cell lines. Higher synergy values indicate a more pronounced therapeutic enhancement when compounds are used in combination.

We first analyzed interactions among 95 TaNKs-associated genes via a protein–protein interaction network (Figure S5D), focusing on the Degree values of six TaNKFS genes. HSPA1B exhibited the highest Degree value of 25 (Table S5A), while TUBB2A showed the strongest correlation with TaNKFS (Fig. 5B, R = 0.75) and a degree value of 11 (Table S5A). These findings suggest that simultaneously targeting HSPA1B and TUBB2A may enhance efficacy against tumors with high TaNKs content.

Subsequently, we screened for the top five synergistic compound combinations involving HSPA1B and TUBB2A (Table S5B). These combinations represent promising therapeutic strategies for TNBC with high TaNKs content. We also identified the top three synergistic drug combinations across distinct breast cancer cell lines (Table S5C). The combination of cyclophosphamide and vancomycin frequently emerged in these results, suggesting it may offer stronger targeting effects and superior anti-TNBC efficacy compared to cyclophosphamide monotherapy.

Interestingly, the compound combinations identified in the T47D cell line were predominantly composed of compounds discovered through deep learning-based big data screening. Most of these compounds targeted HSPA1B, BAG3, and TUBB2A, suggesting that these combinations could provide novel strategies for treating tumors with high TaNKs content. This could expand clinical treatment options and offer new directions for precision therapy in TNBC.

Discussion

Breast cancer remains one of the most prevalent malignant tumors among women, with its global incidence continuing to rise [16]. Among its subtypes, TNBC is particularly challenging due to the absence of ER, PR, and HER2 expression, which contributes to its poor prognosis [17]. Although traditional treatments such as chemotherapy and radiotherapy can partially control TNBC, they are limited by significant side effects and drug resistance [18]. Emerging targeted therapies and immunotherapies, including PD-1 and PD-L1 inhibitors, have shown limited efficacy in TNBC, likely due to the low immune cell level and a “cold” TIME [19, 20]. Our scRNA-seq analysis reveals that epithelial/cancer cells make up the majority of the TIME in most TNBC samples (Fig. 1F), while other cell types, including immune cells, are present in relatively lower abundance. This underscores the importance of understanding TIME and identifying novel therapeutic targets to improve TNBC outcomes.

NK cells, critical components of the immune system, can recognize and destroy tumor cells lacking MHC-I molecules, making them promising targets for immunotherapy [21]. Advances in NK cell-based therapies, including CAR-NK cells and stem cell-derived NK cells, have shown initial promise. However, their application to tumors with TIME deficiencies, such as TNBC, requires further investigation [22–25]. scRNA-seq has become a powerful tool for dissecting cellular diversity, revealing transcriptional and functional heterogeneity, and identifying resistance mechanisms in the TIME [26–28]. A groundbreaking study published in Cell has uncovered novel mechanisms of NK cells within the TIME, identifying a previously uncharacterized subtype, termed TaNKs [9]. This discovery has significant implications for understanding disease progression and prognosis in TIME-deficient tumors, including TNBC. TaNKs, a dysfunctional subset of NK cells, are primarily characterized by reduced cytotoxicity, heightened stress responses, increased expression of inhibitory receptors (such as CD158a and CD158b), and diminished levels of cytotoxic granules, including GZMB and perforin. These cells are also linked to T cell dysfunction. Furthermore, research indicates that elevated TaNKs levels are strongly associated with poor prognosis in multiple cancers, including breast cancer. Our research provides evidence for the presence of TaNKs in the breast cancer subtype, TNBC. Through further subpopulation analysis of NK cells (Fig. 2), we confirmed the existence of TaNKs and validated their characteristics, including impaired immune function and elevated stress responses. Furthermore, TaNKs signaling was detected across three distinct TNBC bulk RNA-seq datasets, and a strong correlation was found between TaNKs levels and poor prognosis in TNBC.

The application of machine learning in medical diagnostic prediction has also seen significant progress, offering healthcare professionals more accurate and efficient diagnostic and predictive tools. By analyzing large datasets of medical and clinical features, machine learning techniques can uncover patterns and trends that aid in predicting disease risk, progression, and treatment outcomes [29–32]. For example, Andre Esteva and colleagues successfully used machine learning algorithms to analyze imaging data from breast cancer patients, predicting patient survival and the risk of disease recurrence [33]. In our study, we pioneered the development of TaNKFS using machine learning methodologies, demonstrating its strong correlation with TaNKs abundance in TNBC. This framework can be used to assess TaNKs levels within TNBC tumors (Fig. 5). Additionally, TaNKFS serves as a valuable tool for evaluating prognostic survival rates in TNBC patients (Nomogram, Fig. 6A).

Each gene in the TaNKFS model plays a distinct role in tumorigenesis and immune regulation. HSPA1B, a heat shock protein, supports tumor progression by regulating protein folding, cell survival, and metastasis [34, 35]. TUBB2A, a key microtubule component, influences cancer cell proliferation and invasion, with evidence linking it to cell morphology and chemotherapeutic response [36–38]. BAG3, an anti-apoptotic protein, modulates pathways such as p53 and caspases, helping tumor cells survive under stress [39]. NR4A2, a transcription factor, promotes tumor growth and metastasis in several cancers by regulating cell cycle and survival pathways [40–42]. IER2, involved in gene expression related to cell proliferation and invasion, is associated with poor clinical outcomes in various cancers [43]. MYADM, a membrane-associated protein, enhances tumor progression by modulating cytoskeletal organization and promoting metastasis [44, 45]. Collectively, these genes contribute to the aggressive nature of TNBC and its resistance to conventional therapies. Understanding their roles provides new opportunities for developing targeted therapies to improve TNBC outcomes.

Deep learning, a subset of machine learning, is increasingly being utilized in drug discovery and development, particularly in targeted drug screening. This approach has shown great promise, as deep learning models can rapidly and accurately predict drug-target interactions by analyzing large datasets of drug compounds and targets, thus offering new methods for targeted drug design and discovery [46–49]. In our study, a total of 12 potential compounds were screened using six genes from the TaNKFS, combined with deep learning and high-throughput molecular docking techniques. These included six known drugs already in clinical use: Staurosporine, a kinase inhibitor with pro-apoptotic effects [50, 51]; Bexarotene, a Retinoid X receptor agonist that modulates apoptosis and cell signaling [52–54]; Cyclophosphamide, a DNA-damaging agent used in multiple cancers [55]; Vancomycin, a glycopeptide antibiotic with potential anti-tumor effects [56]; Heroin, an opioid with possible applications in cancer pain management [57–59]; and Fluorouracil, a chemotherapeutic agent that disrupts DNA synthesis and angiogenesis [60–62]. We then conducted combination therapy predictions for these twelve compounds. Notably, the combination of Cyclophosphamide and Fluorouracil has demonstrated potent anticancer effects in clinical practice [63, 64], while Cyclophosphamide and Vancomycin exhibited potential enhanced antitumor efficacy in TNBC cell lines.

Despite promising results, this study has several limitations. Although the performance of the TaNKFS model and the feasibility of the targeted drug regimen were demonstrated through various methodologies, these findings have not yet been validated through basic experimental studies. The primary challenge lies in the inherent dependence of TaNKs on the tumor microenvironment. Once removed from this environment, these cells rapidly deteriorate, making their purification and subsequent experimental validation particularly difficult. To address this in future studies, TaNKs could be directly extracted from mouse tumor tissues with high TaNKs content for drug combination validation. Additionally, the CD56dim NK cells identified in this study exhibited strong cytotoxicity and potential tumor-immunocidal abilities, warranting further investigation. Specifically, screening for membrane proteins with stronger interactions between CD56dim NK cells and tumor cells would be valuable. Moreover, designing anchor antibodies based on tumor antigens and deep learning could enable targeted recruitment of CD56dim NK cells to tumor sites, thereby enhancing their tumor-killing efficacy.

Conclusion

This study addresses a critical gap in the understanding of TaNKs in TNBC, confirming their immunodeficient characteristics and association with poor prognosis. Building on these findings, the machine learning model TaNKFS was developed to assess TNBC prognosis and TaNKs abundance. Additionally, novel targeted combination therapies were proposed based on the constituent genes of TaNKFS. This research provides new insights into the clinical diagnosis, treatment, and fundamental studies of TNBC.
