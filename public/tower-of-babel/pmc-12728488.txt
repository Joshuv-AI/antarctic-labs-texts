Efficacy and safety of combining bevacizumab and fractionated stereotactic radiotherapy for extensive brain metastases in patients with non‐small cell lung cancer: a prospective phase II study (GASTO‐1053)
Authors: Rui Zhou, Shiyang Zheng, Daquan Wang, Fang Dong, Hongmei Zhang, Tao Zhang, Qiaoting Luo, Biaoshui Liu, Hui Liu, Jun Zhang, Fangjie Liu, Bin Wang, Likun Chen, Yonggao Mou, Kangqiang Peng, Bo Qiu, Hui Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe prognosis for non‐small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) patients with extensive brain metastases (BMs) treated with radiotherapy alone remains poor. Based on the synergistic potential of radiotherapy and angiogenesis inhibitors, we initiated this phase II study to assess the efficacy and safety of combining bevacizumab (Bev) with fractionated stereotactic radiotherapy (FSRT) in managing extensive BMs in NSCLC patients who had stable extracranial disease.MethodsPatients with extensive BMs from NSCLC, deemed unsuitable for stereotactic radiosurgery, were prospectively enrolled following multidisciplinary tumor board evaluation. Patients received FSRT (40 Gy in 10 fractions or 30 Gy in 5 fractions) in combination with Bev (7.5 mg/kg on day 1 prior to FSRT and on day 21 post‐FSRT). The primary endpoint was intracranial progression‐free survival (IPFS). Secondary endpoints included overall survival, progression‐free survival, quality of life (QOL), and toxicities. For comparison, NSCLC patients with extensive BMs treated with whole‐brain radiotherapy (WBRT) plus FSRT or FSRT alone were matched 1:1 with the study group (Bev + FSRT) using the propensity score matching.ResultsOne hundred and six patients were included in the Bev + FSRT group, with a median follow‐up duration of 35.8 months. The median IPFS was 18.3 months (95% confidence interval, 15.2‐23.3 months). The Bev + FSRT group showed a significant improvement in IPFS compared to both the WBRT + FSRT group (9.6 months, P < 0.001) and the FSRT alone group (8.9 months, P < 0.001). Treatment was well tolerated, with grade 1 radiation necrosis in 1 patient. Bev + FSRT treatment significantly reduced tumor volume (P < 0.001), peritumoral edema volume (P = 0.004), and vascular leakage (P < 0.001). Furthermore, QOL was significantly improved after Bev + FSRT treatment, particularly in patients with symptomatic extensive BMs.ConclusionThese findings support the combination of Bev and FSRT as a safe and effective treatment strategy for extensive BMs in NSCLC patients, offering improved intracranial disease control and symptom relief while avoiding the neurotoxicity associated with WBRT. A randomized trial is warranted to validate the findings of the current study.Trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov, NCT04345146. BACKGROUND

In patients with non‐small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), the brain is the most common site of metastasis. Approximately 20%‐40% of NSCLC patients develop brain metastases (BMs) during their disease course, resulting in significantly reduced life expectancy and diminished quality of life [1]. Given the limited brain penetration of many chemotherapeutic and targeted agents, radiotherapy remains the primary treatment for BMs. Previously, whole‐brain radiotherapy (WBRT) in combination with steroids was widely used to manage BMs in NSCLC patients [2, 3]. However, numerous studies have demonstrated that WBRT would lead to neuropsychological sequelae and deterioration in quality of life [1, 4, 5, 6]. Over the past decade, randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have compared stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS) alone with the combination of WBRT and SRS in patients with BMs, and have found no survival advantage with the combined approach over SRS alone [5, 7]. Given the adverse effects and lack of survival benefits associated with the combined treatment regimen, SRS has increasingly become the preferred treatment option for patients with 1 to 4 BMs.

The National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidelines recommend SRS as a cognitive‐sparing alternative to WBRT for limited BMs. However, ambiguity persists in NCCN guidelines regarding the distinction between “limited” and “extensive” BM disease based on lesion number or tumor volume [8, 9]. A clear dose‐response relationship has been established, with higher SRS doses improving 1‐year local control rates (88%‐93%) compared to lower doses (67%‐74%) [10]. However, this benefit comes at the cost of increased toxicity—specifically, the risk of radiation necrosis in large BMs (≥3 cm) treated with single‐fraction SRS can reach 24%‐38% within 12 months [11, 12]. Consequently, fractionated stereotactic radiotherapy (FSRT), delivering 27‐35 Gy over 3‐5 fractions, is recommended for large lesions or those located near critical structures [12, 13]. Emerging evidence suggests that FSRT provides superior local control compared to SRS while also reducing the incidence of radiation necrosis (24%‐38% for SRS vs. 12%‐23% for FSRT at one year), particularly in patients with extensive BMs [12, 13]. Overall, current data support FSRT as a preferred approach for multifocal disease, though standardized definitions of BM burden are needed to optimize treatment selection.

Although FSRT achieves superior local control with a lower risk of radiation necrosis in extensive BMs, intracranial recurrence remains a crucial limitation, occurring in 15%‐40% of patients within 12 months of initial radiotherapy [12, 14]. To improve intracranial disease control, extensive efforts have been dedicated to investigating the synergy between radiotherapy and angiogenesis inhibitors, aiming to disrupt the vicious circle between angiogenesis and radio‐resistance by normalizing tumor vasculature [15, 16]. Bevacizumab (Bev), a humanized monoclonal antibody targeting vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), is widely recognized for its anti‐angiogenic effects and has been approved as a first‐line treatment option for metastatic NSCLC worldwide [17, 18]. Preclinical studies have demonstrated that the concomitant administration of bevacizumab with radiotherapy could remodel the neo‐vasculature structure, making it less “anarchic” during a critical period (named “normalization window”) [19, 20]. During this phase, hypoxia could be decreased, thereby enhancing the anti‐tumor activity of radiation. The BRAIN [21] and EOLE [22] studies evaluated the efficacy and safety of bevacizumab in combination with chemotherapy as the first‐line treatment for NSCLC patients with asymptomatic and untreated BMs, demonstrating promising efficacy and an acceptable safety profile. The REBECA trial introduced the combination of bevacizumab and WBRT as a promising treatment strategy to enhance the control of BMs [23]. To date, no comprehensive prospective studies have evaluated the efficacy and safety of FSRT combined with bevacizumab for extensive BMs in NSCLC patients.

Building on these findings, FSRT was selected over WBRT for several key reasons: 1) accumulating evidence suggests that hippocampal‐avoidant FSRT better preserves neurocognitive function compared to WBRT, particularly in patients with stable extracranial disease; 2) preclinical data indicate that bevacizumab promotes vascular normalization when combined with high‐precision radiotherapy; and 3) modern FSRT platforms can deliver ablative doses to all lesions, even with high tumor burden. Based on these considerations, we hypothesized that adding bevacizumab to FSRT could improve in‐field control while reducing intracranial recurrence, potentially eliminating the need for WBRT in NSCLC patients with extensive BMs. Consequently, we initiated the prospective phase II trial to evaluate the efficacy and safety of combining bevacizumab with FSRT for managing extensive BMs in NSCLC patients with stable extracranial disease.

METHODS

Study population

Between January 2020 and January 2022, patients with extensive BMs deemed unsuitable for SRS or surgery by multidisciplinary team evaluation were recruited from Sun Yat‐sen University Cancer Center (Guangzhou, Guangdong, China). Key inclusion criteria comprised: (1) age ≥18 years; (2) histologically confirmed NSCLC; and (3) at least one of the following imaging‐based criteria on contrast‐enhanced brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI): (a) ≥3 measurable intracranial lesions with at least one lesion ≥2 cm in maximal diameter; or (b) 1‐2 measurable intracranial lesions with at least one lesion ≥3 cm in maximal diameter; or (c) cumulative intracranial tumor volume ≥15 cm3; or (d) presence of lesions located within 1 cm of the brainstem; (4) stable extracranial disease status at the time of enrollment; (5) Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status score of 0‐2; (6) adequate bone marrow, hepatic and renal functions. Main exclusion criteria included: (1) inability to complete a MRI scan with contrast; (2) contraindications to bevacizumab, such as a prior cardiac and/or thromboembolic event, or uncontrolled hypertension; (3) prior resection of cranial metastasis; (4) prior cranial radiotherapy; (5) leptomeningeal metastases; (6) prior malignancies, except for curable non‐melanoma skin cancer or cervical in situ.

The study was approved by the institutional review board and conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Guangdong Association Study of Thoracic Oncology (approval number: A2019‐007). The trial was registered on ClinicalTrials.gov under the identifier NCT04345146. All participants supplied written informed consent before commencing the trial.

Treatment

Patients were fixed using head immobilization and patient‐specific polyurethane foam immobilization devices in the supine position. Computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance (MR) scans were conducted for each patient. The gross tumor volume (GTV) and organs at risk (OARs) were manually contoured on the simulation CT imaging with the visual support of the fused MR imaging. The planning target volume (PTV) for GTV covered the GTV with an isotropic margin of 3 mm. Radiotherapy was delivered with daily cone beam CT (CBCT) image guidance to verify the position. Two FSRT schedules were used: 40 Gy in 10 fractions or 30 Gy in 5 fractions. Fractionation selection depended on lesion characteristics, including size, location, proximity to critical structures, and total intracranial tumor burden. Generally, the 10‐fraction regimen was preferred for larger lesions (>20 cm3) or those located near critical structures (e.g., brainstem, optic apparatus) to spare normal tissues, while the 5‐fraction regimen was selected for smaller lesions distant from critical structures [24]. The radiotherapy plans ensured that the prescribed dose covered 99% of GTV and 95% of PTV, and the maximal dose was required to be less than 110% of the prescribed dose. The dose constraints for OARs are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

Bevacizumab was administered intravenously on day 1 (one week before FSRT) and day 21 at a dose of 7.5 mg/kg (Supplementary Figure S1). Mannitol and dexamethasone were prescribed to mitigate cranial tumor‐related symptoms when needed.

As for the systemic therapies, patients were stratified into two distinct situations: (1) Symptomatic patients: These patients initiated treatment with bevacizumab plus FSRT (Bev + FSRT) within one week after enrollment. For those with epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) mutations or anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK) rearrangements, targeted drugs were continued concurrently with Bev + FSRT. Patients without EGFR mutations or ALK rearrangements were re‐evaluated three weeks after completing Bev + FSRT by both radiation and medical oncologists to determine the appropriate timing for the resumption of systemic treatment. (2) Asymptomatic patients: Those with EGFR mutations or ALK rearrangements initiated Bev + FSRT treatment concurrently with the ongoing targeted drug therapy. Patients without EGFR mutations or ALK rearrangements started Bev + FSRT three weeks after the last dose of systemic treatment and were re‐evaluated three weeks post‐Bev + FSRT to determine when systemic therapy should be resumed.

Toxicity and adverse events

Toxicities were assessed according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) Version 5.0 [25], from the initiation of treatment through 12 months after completing Bev + FSRT. Monitoring was conducted at predefined intervals: 1) weekly during treatment: symptoms, physical examinations, vital signs (with special attention to blood pressure 24‐72 hours after administering bevacizumab), neurocognitive assessments, and laboratory tests (complete blood counts, liver and renal function, and urinalysis); 2) monthly for the first 6 months post‐treatment, with continued clinical and neurocognitive assessments; 3) every 3 months through 12 months post‐treatment, with clinical and neurocognitive assessments, and contrast‐enhanced brain MRI to detect treatment‐related changes and radiological signs of toxicity. Toxicity monitoring schedules and management algorithms are detailed in the Supplementary Table S2.

Radiation necrosis was diagnosed according to the consensus criteria of the Chinese Multidisciplinary Collaboration Group of Radiation‐induced Brain Injury [26], defined as new or enlarging contrast‐enhancing lesions accompanied by at least one of the following: 1) MR diffusion‐weighted imaging (DWI)/apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC): low signal on DWI with elevated ADC values; 2) MRI perfusion: relative cerebral blood volume < 0.6; 3) MR spectroscopy: elevated lactate/choline ratios; or 4) histopathology if possible. All grade ≥3 toxicities were independently adjudicated by both a radiation oncologist and a neurologist.

Response evaluation, quality of life, and follow‐up

One month after completing Bev + FSRT, treatment response was assessed using contrast‐enhanced MRI with diffusion imaging. All patients were subsequently followed every 3 months for the first 2 years, every 6 months for years 3‐5, and annually thereafter. Imaging was reviewed by a senior radiation oncologist and a radiologist. The radiographic response of BMs was assessed using the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) guideline version 1.1 [27]. In‐field tumor progression was defined as a minimum 20% increase in the sum of the longest diameters (SLD) of measurable target lesions compared to the baseline SLD. Any in‐field tumor progression or the appearance of new brain lesions was denoted as progressive disease.

The quality of life (QOL) was evaluated using the European Organization for Research and Treatment of Cancer QOL questionnaire‐core 30 (QLQ‐C30) [28]. To evaluate changes over time, enrolled patients completed the QOL questionnaire at enrollment and at 1‐, 3‐, and 6‐months following treatment completion. As per the QLQ‐C30, high scores in the global health status and functional scales represent a favorable condition, while high scores in the symptom scales indicate a poor status.

MRI imaging, regions of interest delineation, and quantitative parameters acquisition

The patients underwent dynamic contrast‐enhanced MRI (DCE‐MRI) scans both before and one month after treatment. The imaging protocol included conventional brain MR images, DWI, and DCE images, utilizing a 3.0 Tesla magnetic scanner (Discovery MR750w, GE Healthcare, Milwaukee, WI, USA). The regions of interest (ROIs), including the tumor and edema regions, were manually delineated by both an experienced radiation oncologist and an experienced radiologist. Specifically, the tumor and edema regions were delineated on contrast‐enhanced T1‐weighted and T2‐weighted images, respectively. For patients with multiple tumors, each lesion was delineated as a separate ROI.

DWI images were subsequently transferred to the AW4.4 workstation (GE Healthcare) for analysis. The Functool software was utilized to process the DWI images and generate ADC maps. The mean ADC values with standard deviations within the ROIs were automatically calculated. Furthermore, quantitative DCE parameters, including plasma volume (Vp), extracellular space volume (Ve), transfer volume constant from plasma volume (Ktrans), and transfer rate constant (Kep), were automatically derived using the Extend Tofts model for both the tumor regions and normal brain tissue.

Post‐hoc propensity‐matched analysis

To compare the efficacy and toxicities of the study group (Bev + FSRT group), NSCLC patients with extensive BMs who received either WBRT combined with FSRT (WBRT + FSRT group) or FSRT alone (FSRT alone group) were selected using the propensity score matching method. Data were collected from the databases of multiple medical centers, including Sun Yat‐sen University Cancer Center, Sun Yat‐sen University Cancer Center Gansu Hospital (Lanzhou, Gansu, China), Air Force Hospital of Southern Theater Command of the People's Liberation Army (Guangzhou, Guangdong, China), and the First Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University (Chongqing, China). All patients in the three groups had stable extracranial disease status.

To minimize potential confounding factors, a 1:1 propensity score matching approach was performed to balance the baseline characteristics among the three groups. Propensity scores were estimated using a logistic regression model that included the following covariates: age, sex, Karnofsky Performance Status (KPS) score, presence of extracranial metastases, symptomatic brain metastases, and EGFR mutations or ALK rearrangements status. Matching was conducted using caliper matching with a caliper width of 0.2.

In the WBRT + FSRT group, patients received a total dose of 25‐30 Gy in 10‐15 fractions to the whole brain, along with a simultaneous integrated boost of 40‐50 Gy to the metastatic brain lesions. In the FSRT alone group, a total dose of 30‐50 Gy in 5‐10 fractions was delivered to the metastatic brain lesions. Mannitol and dexamethasone were routinely administered in the WBRT + FSRT group and selectively used in the FSRT alone group for patients with symptomatic brain metastases. Neither the WBRT + FSRT group nor the FSRT alone group received bevacizumab within three months before or after radiotherapy.

Statistical analysis

The primary endpoint of this study was intracranial progression‐free survival (IPFS), which was calculated from the completion of radiotherapy until the first occurrence of either intracranial progression or death. We assumed that the median IPFS could be improved from the previously reported 9.4 months [29] to 14 months in the current study. To detect this expected improvement with 90% statistical power at a two‐sided significance level of 0.05, a sample size of 85 patients was required. Assuming a dropout rate of 20%, our intended enrollment was set at 106 patients. Secondary endpoints included overall survival (OS), progression‐free survival (PFS), QOL, and toxicities. All time‐to‐event endpoints (IPFS, OS, and PFS) were measured from the completion of radiotherapy.

Descriptive statistics were applied to analyze the baseline characteristics and treatment‐related toxicities. Median values were compared using the Wilcoxon test, and categorical variables were compared using the Chi‐square test. The QOL scores at different time points were compared using the paired‐sample t‐test. Survival rates were estimated using the Kaplan‐Meier method, and differences in survival curves were compared through the log‐rank test. The proportions of patients receiving salvage treatments were reported with 95% confidence intervals (CI) calculated using the Clopper–Pearson exact method based on the binomial distribution. A competing risk analysis for intracranial progression was performed using R software (version 4.3.0, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). The probability of intracranial progression, conditional on the patient not experiencing a competing risk of death by time t, was calculated using the Fine and Gray competing risks regression model. A P value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Patient characteristics and treatment details

Between January 2020 and January 2022, a total of 108 NSCLC patients with extensive BMs were recruited. After excluding 2 patients with contraindications to bevacizumab, 106 patients were allocated to the treatment protocol (intent‐to‐treat [ITT] population) (Figure 1). Overall, the median age was 59 years (range, 29‐87 years). The majority of patients were male (71/106, 67.0%) and presented with symptomatic brain metastases (63/106, 59.4%). Adenocarcinoma was the predominant histological type (90/106, 84.9%), and EGFR mutations or ALK rearrangements were detected in 63 (59.4%) patients. Most patients (69/106, 65.1%) had no extracranial metastases at enrollment. Detailed baseline clinical characteristics are summarized in Table 1.

All patients in the study group completed Bev + FSRT as per protocol. Detailed systemic treatment regimens are summarized in Supplementary Table S3. Among the 63 patients with EGFR mutations or ALK rearrangements, 53 (84.1%) had received targeted therapy prior to FSRT and continued it throughout the Bev + FSRT treatment period. The remaining 10 (15.9%) patients initiated targeted therapy after completing Bev + FSRT. Among the 43 patients without EGFR mutations or ALK rearrangements, 15 (34.9%) patients had received chemotherapy and/or immunotherapy before Bev + FSRT due to the concurrent extracranial metastases, and resumed systemic treatment after completing Bev + FSRT. Another 23 (53.5%) patients initiated systemic treatment after Bev + FSRT, and the remaining 5 (11.6%) patients did not receive any systemic therapy.

Survival outcomes

Survival analyses were conducted in the ITT population. At the time of data cut‐off, the median follow‐up time was 35.8 months. The trial met its primary endpoint, with a median IPFS of 18.3 months (95% CI, 15.2 to 23.3 months; Figure 2A). In the context of the competing risk of death, the estimated probability of intracranial progression at 1 and 2 years was 20.0% (95% CI, 12.3% to 27.7%) and 30.0% (95% CI, 21.1% to 39.0%), respectively (Supplementary Figure S2). Regarding secondary endpoints, the median OS was 26.8 months (95% CI, 22.4 months to not reached [NR]; Figure 2B), and the median PFS was 8.4 months (95% CI, 6.7 to 17.3 months; Figure 2C).

Univariate analysis was conducted in the Bev + FSRT group to evaluate the impact of various variables on OS, PFS, and IPFS. EGFR mutation or ALK rearrangement status and the presence of extracranial metastases were significant prognostic factors for OS and PFS (Supplementary Figure S3A‐B). However, no specific factor was found to be significantly associated with IPFS (Supplementary Figure S3C).

Specifically, we analyzed the IPFS, OS, and PFS in the Bev + FSRT group stratified by the presence or absence of EGFR‐sensitive mutation or ALK rearrangement (Supplementary Figure S4). The median IPFS was comparable between patients with and without these genetic alterations (18.3 months [95% CI, 9.1 to 28.5 months] vs. 18.0 months [95% CI, 15.2 to 25.1 months], P = 0.835). However, the median OS and PFS were significantly longer in patients with EGFR‐sensitive mutation or ALK rearrangement than in those without such alterations (median OS, NR vs. 21.3 months [95% CI, 15.1 to 34.8 months], P = 0.010; median PFS, 16.5 months [95% CI, 8.3 to 23.3 months] vs. 6.6 months [95% CI, 5.1 to 10.2 months], P = 0.032).

Safety and QOL

In the Bev + FSRT group, 6 patients were lost to follow‐up. Therefore, toxicity and QOL were analyzed in the remaining 100 patients. Among them, 4 (4.0%) patients experienced acute headache during radiotherapy, with 3 classified as Grade 1 and 1 as Grade 2. Two (2.0%) patients reported Grade 1 nausea/vomiting. Only one (1.0%) patient in the Bev + FSRT group developed Grade 1 radiation necrosis. Furthermore, bevacizumab‐related toxicities were observed in 5 (5.0%) patients (Grade 1 proteinuria, 2 patients; Grade 1 hematuria, 1 patient; reversible mild hypertension, 2 patients). No instances of meningeal or parenchymal hemorrhage were detected by MRI in the Bev + FSRT group through the follow‐up period. Detailed characteristics are shown in Supplementary Table S4.

The QLQ‐C30 scores of the Bev + FSRT group are depicted in Figure 3 and Supplementary Table S5. Compared with baseline (mean score: 87.94 points), significant improvements in the overall QOL scores were observed at 1 month (mean score: 92.41 points; mean change: 4.47 points [95% CI, 2.74 to 6.20 points]; P < 0.001), 3 months (mean scores: 93.24 points; mean change: 5.15 points [95% CI, 3.13 to 7.18 points]; P < 0.001), and 6 months (mean scores: 92.90 points; mean change: 4.02 points [95% CI, 0.78 to 7.27 points]; P < 0.001) post‐treatment. Improvements were also observed in the functional scale scores. Notably, the improvement in QOL was more pronounced in patients with symptomatic BMs compared with asymptomatic patients.

MR imaging and DCE quantitative parameters

At 1 month after Bev + FSRT treatment, a significant reduction in cumulative tumor volume was observed (median volume decreased from 8.51 cm3 [interquartile range (IQR), 5.43‐25.24 cm3] at baseline to 4.75 cm3 [IQR, 2.78‐13.57 cm3]; mean change, ‐6.66 cm3 [95% CI, ‐8.64 to ‐4.69 cm3]; P < 0.001; Supplementary Table S6). A significant shrinkage of cumulative peritumoral edema volume was also noted (median volume from 32.33 cm3 [IQR, 7.57‐80.72 cm3] at baseline to 10.61 cm3 [IQR, 2.88‐35.04 cm3]; mean change, ‐27.95 cm3 [95% CI, ‐38.64 to ‐17.27 cm3]; P < 0.001; Supplementary Table S6). Satisfactory disease control, including brain lesions and peritumoral edema, was observed in the Bev + FSRT group compared to both the WBRT + FSRT group and the FSRT alone group (Figure 4).

Moreover, Cox regression analysis identified the reduction in Ktrans as a significant prognostic factor for IPFS (hazard ratio, 0.136 [95% CI, 0.036 to 0.520]; P = 0.004).

Failure patterns and salvage treatments

During the follow‐up period, a total of 32 (30.2%) patients in the Bev + FSRT group experienced intracranial progression. Intracranial progression was categorized into three subtypes: progression only at the previously irradiated site (3/106, 2.8%), progression only at new sites (21/106, 19.8%), and concurrent progressions at both previously irradiated and new sites (8/106, 7.5%). Following the occurrence of intracranial progression, 10 patients (9.4%; 95% CI, 4.6% to 16.7%) received salvage radiotherapy for new lesions, 21 patients (19.8%; 95% CI, 12.6% to 28.3%) switched to alternative systemic chemotherapy or targeted therapy, and 1 patient (0.9%; 95% CI, 0.02% to 5.11%) received salvage surgery for the previously irradiated lesion.

Post‐hoc propensity‐matched analysis

From the databases of multiple medical centers, we retrieved 679 NSCLC patients with extensive BMs and stable extracranial disease status who were treated with either WBRT plus FSRT or FSRT alone. Baseline clinical characteristics of patients prior to matching are detailed in Supplementary Table S7. Subsequently, through propensity score matching, 106 patients were selected as the WBRT + FSRT group, and 106 patients as the FSRT alone group, respectively (Figure 1). Baseline characteristics were comparable across the three groups (Table 1).

In the comparative analysis of therapeutic efficacy, significant differences in IPFS were observed among the Bev + FSRT group, the WBRT + FSRT group, and the FSRT alone group (P < 0.001, Figure 2A). The Bev + FSRT group demonstrated a markedly superior outcome compared to both the WBRT + FSRT group (median IPFS, 9.6 months; 95% CI, 7.2 to 12.8 months) and the FSRT alone group (median IPFS, 8.9 months; 95% CI, 7.1 to 11.1 months).

The median OS for the WBRT + FSRT group and the FSRT alone group was 24.7 months (95% CI, 17.4 to 43.4 months) and 24.5 months (95% CI, 16.9 to 31.3 months), respectively. There were no significant differences in OS among the three groups (P = 0.383, Figure 2B). The median PFS for the WBRT + FSRT group and the FSRT alone group was 7.5 months (95% CI, 5.6 to 9.6 months) and 7.1 months (95% CI, 5.3 to 9.1 months), respectively. The Bev + FSRT group showed a slightly longer PFS compared to both the WBRT + FSRT group and the FSRT alone groups (P = 0.002, Figure 2C).

Higher rates of radiation necrosis were observed in the FSRT alone group (6/106, 5.7%) and the WBRT + FSRT group (8/106, 7.5%) than in the Bev + FSRT group. In the FSRT alone group, 5 cases were classified as Grade 1 and 1 case as Grade 2. In the WBRT + FSRT group, 6 patients were classified as Grade 1 and 2 cases as Grade 2. The rate of Grade ≥1 radiation necrosis was lower in the Bev + FSRT group compared with historical controls, but the difference was not significant (P = 0.08).

DISCUSSION

The current study evaluated the efficacy and safety of combining bevacizumab with FSRT in NSCLC patients with extensive BMs. Compared to the historical WBRT + FSRT group and the FSRT alone group, the Bev + FSRT group demonstrated a significant improvement in IPFS, with a median IPFS of 18.3 months. The median OS and PFS in the Bev + FSRT group were 26.8 months and 8.4 months, respectively. Treatment‐related toxicities were mild, with only one patient experiencing Grade 1 radiation necrosis. Significant reductions in tumor volume and peritumoral edema were observed after Bev + FSRT treatment, leading to pronounced improvements in neurological symptoms and QOL for patients with symptomatic BMs.

Due to the superior preservation of neurocognitive function without compromising survival outcomes, SRS has emerged as a preferred treatment approach over WBRT for BMs. Previous trials have demonstrated that higher doses of SRS improve local control but also increase the risk of radiation necrosis [10, 12]. Therefore, dose escalation with SRS is generally not recommended for large or extensive BMs. FSRT, delivering radiation in multiple fractions, allows for a higher cumulative dose over the treatment course. The intervals between fractions also allow for the repair of sublethal radiation damage, particularly in radiosensitive brain tissue, thereby reducing the incidence of radiation necrosis [12, 13, 30]. Based on these advantages, the FRST regimen was adopted in the present study to enhance the local control of BMs while minimizing toxicities.

With a median follow‐up of 35.8 months, the combination of bevacizumab and FSRT achieved a 1‐year intracranial control (IC) rate of 80% and a median IPFS of 18.3 months. Supplementary Table S8 contextualizes these outcomes against historical studies: Ma et al. [31] reported a 1‐year IC of 40.7% with FSRT alone in patients with large BMs, while the RTOG 9508 trial (WBRT regimen) [32] demonstrated a 1‐year control rate of 74% in NSCLC patients with multiple BMs. In a study conducted by Matsuyama et al. [33], 299 NSCLC patients with 1 to 5 brain lesions received FSRT with a fixed biologically effective dose of approximately 80 Gy. The median local progression‐free survival and intracranial relapse‐free survival were 14.9 months and 4.4 months, respectively. Another study conducted by Porte et al. [14] analyzed the outcomes of NSCLC patients with BMs treated with stereotactic radiotherapy (single‐fraction, 15‐18 Gy; multi‐fraction, 18‐27 Gy in 3‐5 fractions) combined with immunotherapy. The results showed that the 1‐year regional progression‐free rate was 40.1% and the median time to local relapse was 4.14 months. Compared with these data, our study demonstrated satisfactory intracranial disease control. The Bev + FSRT cohort showed a statistically significant improvement in IPFS compared with both WBRT + FSRT and FSRT alone groups. Additionally, the Bev + FSRT group exhibited a lower incidence of out‐of‐field failure, supporting its potential as an alternative to WBRT in patients with extensive BMs. Preclinical studies have shown that bevacizumab leads to transient enhancement of intra‐tumoral oxygenation by normalizing tumor vasculature, particularly within the first 4‐5 days [19]. During this window, the introduction of radiotherapy shows synergistic effects [19]. Therefore, the observed improvements in tumor control and radiological responses in the Bev + FSRT group may be attributed to this synergistic mechanism—where bevacizumab disrupts the vicious circle between angiogenesis and radio‐resistance by normalizing tumor vasculature.

The longer life expectancy resulting from oncologic interventions emphasizes the importance of improving patients’ QOL and social functioning. Our study demonstrated significant improvements in both QOL and neurological symptoms with the introduction of bevacizumab, particularly in symptomatic patients with extensive BMs. These improvements were primarily attributable to substantial tumor shrinkage and reduction of peritumoral edema. Previous studies have shown that the use of bevacizumab results in a reduction in edema volume in most patients previously treated with brain radiotherapy [34, 35]. Consistent with these findings, our study observed a substantial decrease in peritumoral edema. Moreover, DCE‐MRI analysis revealed a significant decrease in the mean Ktrans value within the tumor lesions after treatment. As a quantitative biomarker, the Ktrans value reflects the leakage of contrast agents through abnormal tumor capillaries [36, 37]. Consistent with findings by Wang et al. [38], who demonstrated a positive correlation between the Ktrans value and the peritumoral edema index in lung cancer patients with BMs, our results further reinforce the pathophysiological relevance of vascular permeability in tumor‐associated edema. These findings indicate that the addition of bevacizumab to FSRT may improve vascular function and reduce leakage, thereby contributing to the resolution of edema and improvements in QOL. Moreover, the decrease in Ktrans value was identified as a significant prognostic factor for IPFS, indicating its potential as an early imaging biomarker of treatment response.

Radiation necrosis is one of the most common complications associated with brain radiotherapy. It represents inflammation resulting from tissue death and breakdown, which could cause mass effect and/or surrounding edema that compresses healthy brain tissue, thereby leading to cognitive decline, neurological deficits, and diminished QOL. A meta‐analysis conducted by Lehrer et al. [12] reported that the incidence of radiation necrosis in patients with large BMs after FSRT was 7.1% (95% CI, 4.4% to 11.3%). In a randomized placebo‐controlled trial conducted by Levin et al. [39], seven patients with biopsy‐proven radiation necrosis were treated with bevacizumab at a dose of 7.5 mg/kg every three weeks, resulting in an average reduction in radiation necrosis volume of 59% on T1‐weighted images and 63% on fluid‐attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) images. The incidence of radiation necrosis in our study cohort was remarkably low, with only 1 out of 100 patients (1.0%) experiencing this adverse event, compared to higher rates observed in both the FSRT alone and WBRT + FSRT groups. Several established risk factors for radiation necrosis—including age, histological subtype, KPS score, cumulative tumor volume, and prior radiation exposure—were well‐balanced across all three treatment groups. Furthermore, given that the median time to the onset of radiation necrosis typically ranges from 6 to 24 months after radiotherapy [40], the follow‐up durations in all three groups (Bev + FSRT: 35.8 months; FSRT alone: 33.8 months; WBRT + FSRT: 43.4 months) were sufficient to detect the delayed toxicity events. These results further support the potential therapeutic benefit of early integration of bevacizumab with FSRT to prevent radiation necrosis. Additionally, our study addressed concerns about the risk of brain hemorrhage associated with the combined treatment of bevacizumab and FSRT. MRI analyses showed no evidence of meningeal or parenchymal hemorrhagic lesions in the entire cohort. The favorable toxicity profile of the current regimen contributed to maintaining QOL and preserving favorable neurological function, which is crucial for subsequent salvage treatment, including salvage radiotherapy for new brain lesions as well as alternative systemic treatment.

A study conducted by Zindler et al. [41] identified extracranial metastases and age as negative prognostic factors in NSCLC patients with BMs who received SRS. Sperduto et al. [42] and Nieder et al. [43] also emphasized the prognostic significance of extracranial metastases and the number of BMs. Similarly, we found that patients with EGFR/ALK wild‐type status or concurrent extracranial metastases had poorer OS and PFS, but comparable IPFS. These findings suggest that the combination of bevacizumab and FSRT is effective for intracranial disease control across gene statuses and highlight the importance of optimizing systemic therapy regimens to achieve long‐term survival in NSCLC patients with extensive BMs. Currently, several studies have proposed that combining immune checkpoint inhibitors with radiotherapy might further improve treatment outcomes in patients with BMs [44, 45, 46, 47]. A multicentric retrospective study demonstrated that combined stereotactic radiotherapy and immunotherapy improved IPFS compared to stereotactic radiotherapy alone [48]. Among advanced patients who had been treated with immunotherapy, the addition of stereotactic radiotherapy and immune maintenance therapy was associated with improved OS compared with stereotactic radiotherapy alone. Despite the potential benefit of combining immunotherapy and radiotherapy in wild‐type NSCLC with BMs, the incidence of radiation necrosis was reported to be as high as 20% [48]. Therefore, the combination of bevacizumab and FSRT in NSCLC patients with extensive BMs appears to be valuable even in the era of immunotherapy, due to the unique pharmacological effects of bevacizumab.

Several limitations of our study should be acknowledged. First, although the trial employed a prospective design, the control groups were constructed retrospectively using propensity score matching. While this approach, combined with rigorous matching, strengthens the robustness of our findings, the comparison with non‐randomized historical controls remains a limitation and may introduce unmeasured confounding factors. Accordingly, the efficacy signals—particularly the magnitude of benefit—should be interpreted with caution and validated in a randomized setting. Second, the entire cohort exclusively consisted of patients with primary tumors of NSCLC, and we intentionally excluded participants with other cancer types. Thus, the generalizability of our results to different cancer types may require further exploration. Third, all enrolled patients had stable extracranial diseases. Accordingly, our findings are most applicable to NSCLC patients with extensive BMs who have achieved systemic disease control. The efficacy and safety of this regimen in patients with progressive extracranial disease remain to be determined and warrant further investigation. Fourth, although the median follow‐up of 35.8 months is relatively long and provides confidence in the intermediate‐term safety profile of this regimen, the possibility of rare late toxicities—such as delayed vascular events or subtle neurocognitive decline—cannot be completely excluded. Ongoing long‐term follow‐up of this cohort will be essential to further clarify these risks. Lastly, it is noteworthy that a significant proportion (63/106, 59.4%) of enrolled patients had EGFR mutations or ALK rearrangements, which might potentially benefit from targeted therapies. However, the significant improvement in IPFS with Bev + FSRT was observed after PSM adjustment for these genetic alterations across groups, suggesting an independent treatment effect of the bevacizumab‐containing regimen.

CONCLUSIONS

In this single‐arm prospective phase II trial, the combination of bevacizumab and FSRT for managing NSCLC patients with extensive BMs demonstrated satisfactory results in achieving effective intracranial disease control and alleviating BM‐related symptoms. Patients maintained satisfactory QOL following this combined therapeutic approach, with a low rate of cerebral radiation necrosis. These findings suggested that the combination of bevacizumab and FSRT holds promise as a viable alternative to WBRT in the management of NSCLC patients with extensive BMs. Although propensity score matching was applied, the absence of a direct control group precludes definitive conclusions regarding the efficacy of this combination regimen compared to FSRT alone. These findings should be interpreted as hypothesis‐generating; future phase III randomized trials are warranted to validate the findings of the current study.
