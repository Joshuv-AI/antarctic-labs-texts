TET1-Mediated DNA hydroxymethylation of KLF6 promotes LPS-induced pulmonary fibrosis by activating the S1PR3/RhoA/ROCK signaling pathway

Introduction

Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome (ARDS) is a severe clinical condition characterized by acute hypoxemia and increased permeability of the alveolar-capillary membrane, typically resulting from various insults such as infections, trauma, or inhalation injuries [1]. The pathophysiological process of ARDS involves extensive pulmonary inflammation, which can ultimately lead to pulmonary fibrosis—a complication associated with high mortality rates and long-term respiratory dysfunction. Recent studies have highlighted the critical role of endothelial cells and their transition to a mesenchymal phenotype (Endothelial-to-Mesenchymal Transition, EndMT) in the development of pulmonary fibrosis associated with ARDS [2]. Despite the acknowledged importance of EndMT in pulmonary fibrosis, the precise regulatory mechanisms and potential therapeutic targets remain largely undefined, necessitating further investigation to inform clinical treatment strategies.

Emerging evidence demonstrates that epigenetic modifications—such as DNA demethylation, RNA modifications, and transcription factor dysregulation—play critical roles in the pathogenesis of lung diseases [3, 4]. This highlights the urgent need to understand how epigenetic dysregulation contributes to ARDS in order to identify novel therapeutic targets. DNA methylation and demethylation, as the core epigenetic mechanisms, determine the fate and function of cells by precisely regulating gene switches without changing the DNA sequence. A key player in this process is Ten-Eleven Translocation methylcytosine dioxygenase 1 (TET1), a member of the TET protein family, which plays an instrumental role in DNA demethylation processes by catalyzing the conversion of 5-methylcytosine (5mC) to 5-hydroxymethylcytosine (5hmC), thereby influencing gene expression and cellular fate decisions [5]. Emerging evidence highlighted TET1 as a regulator of inflammatory processes and oncogenesis. For example, TET1 facilitated tumorigenesis in hepatocellular carcinoma through epigenetic activation of oncogenic pathways [6]. In hypoxic brain injury, TET1 contributed to neuroinflammatory processes via dysregulation of neurodevelopmental signaling [7]. Despite these insights, the direct role of TET1 in ARDS-related pulmonary fibrosis remains unclear, and the underlying mechanisms by which TET1 influences pulmonary fibrosis have yet to be extensively elucidated.

Krüppel-like factor 6 (KLF6) is a zinc finger transcription factor involved in various biological processes, including cell proliferation, differentiation, and apoptosis [8]. In the context of lung injury, elevated expression of KLF6 has been associated with exacerbated inflammatory responses and the progression of fibrosis. Chen et al. demonstrated that inhibition of KLF6 significantly ameliorated pathological changes in lung tissues of mice with acute lung injury, reducing inflammation and apoptosis, thereby highlighting the critical role of KLF6 in regulating pulmonary inflammatory responses and fibrosis [9]. Furthermore, Kim et al. found that KLF6 promoted pro-inflammatory gene expression by repressing miR-223 in macrophages, underscoring its essential role in inflammatory regulation [10]. Our bioinformatics analyses identified CpG islands within the promoter region of the KLF6 gene, suggesting that its expression may be regulated by DNA methylation patterns. These results indicate that TET1-mediated DNA demethylation could control KLF6 expression in lung tissues, thereby playing a crucial role in ARDS-associated pulmonary fibrosis.

Sphingosine-1-phosphate receptor 3 (S1PR3) is a member of the Sphingosine-1-Phosphate receptor family, which mediates various cellular processes including migration, proliferation, and inflammatory responses [11, 12]. In lung injury models, upregulation of S1PR3 expression has been closely associated with exacerbated inflammatory responses and dysfunction of endothelial cells. Research indicated that S1PR3 activated the Ras Homolog Family Member A (RhoA)/Rho-Associated Coiled-Coil Containing Protein Kinase (ROCK) signaling pathway, leading to cytoskeletal rearrangements and disruption of the endothelial barrier integrity, thus contributing to endothelial dysfunction and the progression of pulmonary fibrosis [13]. Additionally, Wu et al. demonstrated that mechanical stress-induced overexpression of S1PR3 led to the cleavage of vascular endothelial-cadherin (VE-cadherin), thereby compromising endothelial barrier stability and promoting inflammation through NF-κB activation [14]. Bioinformatics predictions suggested that KLF6 had a potential binding site with the S1PR3 gene promoter region, indicating that KLF6 may regulate S1PR3 transcriptional activation. However, whether KLF6 can enhance S1PR3 expression to modulate endothelial cell function and thereby promote pulmonary fibrosis remains to be resolved.

In light of the above, this study aims to probe the mechanism by which TET1-mediated demethylation of KLF6 promotes pulmonary fibrosis. We hypothesize that TET1 facilitates the hydroxymethylation of the KLF6 gene, leading to its upregulation. The increased expression of KLF6 may subsequently activate S1PR3 transcription, which in turn engages the RhoA/ROCK signaling pathway to drive EndMT and pulmonary fibrosis in the LPS-induced pulmonary fibrosis model. Understanding this molecular mechanism hopes to provide valuable insights into the pathogenesis of ARDS-associated pulmonary fibrosis and identify novel therapeutic targets for improving clinical outcomes in ARDS patients.

Materials and methods

Cell culture and treatment

Human pulmonary microvascular endothelial cells (HPMECs) were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (#C-12281, USA) and cultured in Endothelial Cell Growth Medium (EGM-2; #CC-3162, Lonza, Switzerland) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; #10270-106, Gibco, USA) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (#15140-122, Gibco) in a humid chamber of 5% CO₂ at 37 °C. For experiments, cells in passages 3–6 were used. To induce EndMT and apoptosis, HPMECs were treated with lipopolysaccharide (LPS; #L2880, Sigma-Aldrich) at a dose of 1 µg/mL for varying durations as specified in the corresponding experimental sections.

Transfection

All gene manipulation vectors used in this study, including shRNA, overexpression constructs and mutant, were commercially procured from GenePharma (Shanghai, China). HPMECs were transfected with these vectors using Lipofectamine 3000 (#L3000001, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. For TET1 knockdown experiments, cells were transfected with either TET1-specific shRNA (sh-TET1) or non-targeting control shRNA (sh-NC). To investigate KLF6 and S1PR3 overexpression effects, cells were transfected with either KLF6 overexpression vector (oe-KLF6) or S1PR3 overexpression vector (oe-S1PR3), along with their respective non-targeting control vector (oe-NC). To assess the functional role of TET1, cells were transfected with a full-length TET1 overexpression vector (TET1-FL) or a catalytically inactive TET1 mutant (TET1-CDmut). The TET1-CDmut construct harbors two point mutations: histidine at position 1672 is replaced by tyrosine (H1672Y), and aspartic acid at position 1674 is replaced by alanine (D1674A), which abolishes the catalytic activity of TET1 [15]. Transfection efficiency was validated 48 h post-transfection through RT-qPCR and Western blot analysis to confirm successful gene modulation at both mRNA and protein levels.

RT-qPCR

Total RNA isolation from HPMECs was carried out with TRIzol reagent (#15596018, Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the recommended protocol. Spectrophotometric measurements (Nanodrop, Thermo Fisher Scientific) were used to determine RNA concentration and purity. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized using SuperScript IV Reverse Transcriptase (#18090050, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) was performed using SYBR Green Master Mix (#4309155, Applied Biosystems, USA) on an ABI 7500 Fast Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems). Gene expression was normalized to β-actin using the 2−ΔΔCt method. Primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

Protein extraction and Western blot

Proteins were extracted from HPMECs using RIPA buffer (#89900, Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail (#11836170001, Roche, Switzerland). BCA assay kit (#23227, Thermo Fisher Scientific) was applied to determine the protein content. After loading 30 µg protein per sample on 10% SDS-PAGE, separated proteins were blotted onto PVDF membranes (#IPVH00010, Millipore, USA). Membrane blocking was achieved by 1 h incubation with 5% fat-free milk/TBST at room temperature and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against TET1 (#40142, CST, USA), KLF6 (#39–6900, Invitrogen, USA), S1PR3 (#ASR-013-200UL, Alomone Labs, Israel), CD31 (#MA5-37858, Invitrogen), VE-cadherin (#36–1900, Invitrogen), Alpha-Smooth Muscle Actin (α-SMA; #14–9760-82, Invitrogen), Vimentin (#PA5-27231, Invitrogen), Collagen I (#PA1-26204, Invitrogen), RhoA (#MA1-134, Invitrogen), ROCK1 (#28999, CST), and β-actin (#4970, CST) at a dilution of 1:1000. Following TBST washes, the membranes were probed with horseradish peroxidase-linked secondary antibodies (#7074 or #7076, CST) at 1:10,000 dilution for 1 h at room temperature. Signal detection was performed using ECL substrate (Tanon, Shanghai, China), and band intensity analysis was conducted through ImageJ software.

Active RhoA activity assay

Active RhoA (GTP-bound) was measured using the Active Rho Pull-Down and Detection Kit (#16116, Thermo Fisher Scientific) following the manufacturer’s protocol. HPMECs cultured to logarithmic growth phase were serum-starved in a serum-free medium containing the ROCK inhibitor Y-27,632 for 24 h to minimize basal RhoA activity. Following stimulation, cells were lysed using the provided lysis buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors to preserve RhoA activity. Protein concentrations were determined using the Bradford assay. Equal amounts of protein (1 mg) from each sample were incubated with the Glutathione S-Transferase-Rho Binding Domain (GST-RBD; Rho-binding domain) agarose beads included in the kit overnight at 4 °C with gentle rotation to selectively precipitate active RhoA. The beads were subsequently washed three times with the wash buffer to eliminate non-specific protein interactions. Bound active RhoA was eluted by boiling the beads in SDS-PAGE sample buffer for 5 min. Eluted proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE, transferred to PVDF membranes, and probed with the provided monoclonal RhoA antibody. Detection was performed using HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies and chemiluminescence reagents according to standard Western blot protocols.

Dot blot assay

Global DNA methylation and hydroxymethylation levels were assessed using a Dot Blot Assay Kit (#ab233486 for 5mC and #ab233487 for 5hmC, Abcam, UK) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, genomic DNA was extracted from HPMECs using the QIAamp DNA Blood Mini Kit (#51104, Qiagen, Germany) and denatured at 95 °C for 5 min. Denatured DNA samples (1 µg) were spotted onto a nitrocellulose membrane using a semidry transfer apparatus. Membranes were air-dried, blocked with 5% BSA in TBST for 1 h, and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against 5mC or 5hmC. After washing, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies and developed using an ECL detection system. Signal intensity was quantified using ImageJ software.

Methylation-specific PCR (MSP)

The methylation status of the KLF6 promoter was analyzed using the EZ DNA Methylation-Gold Kit (#D5001, Zymo Research, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, genomic DNA was bisulfite converted and amplified using primers specific for methylated (M) and unmethylated (U) KLF6 promoter regions (Table S2). Amplification products were separated by 2% agarose gel electrophoresis and visualized under UV light.

Chromatin Immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assay

The ChIP experiments were carried out with CST’s SimpleChIP Enzymatic Chromatin IP Kit (#9003, CST). Briefly, chromatin in HPMECs was first cross-linked using 1% formaldehyde and subsequently subjected to enzymatic cleavage. Immunoprecipitation was performed using anti-TET1 (#40142, CST) or anti-KLF6 (#39–6900, Invitrogen) antibodies, with IgG serving as the negative control. Purified DNA was subjected to PCR using primers specific for the KLF6 or S1PR3 promoter regions.

Dual-Luciferase reporter assay

To validate the interaction between KLF6 and the S1PR3 promoter, dual-luciferase reporter assays were conducted. The S1PR3 promoter region containing the wild-type KLF6 binding site was cloned into the pGL4.10 luciferase reporter vector (#E6651, Promega, USA), while the mutant version was cloned into a separate vector. HPMECs were co-transfected with either the wild-type or mutant luciferase reporter plasmid along with a KLF6 knockdown vector or control vector using Lipofectamine 3000. After 48 h, luciferase activity was measured using the Dual-Luciferase® Reporter Assay System (#E1910, Promega) and normalized to Renilla luciferase activity.

5hmC analysis assay

5hmC levels at the KLF6 promoter were quantified using the EpiMark 5-hmC and 5-mC Analysis Kit (#E3317S, NEB, USA) [16]. Briefly, 1 µg of genomic DNA was treated with T4 β-glucosyltransferase in the presence of 100 µM UDP-glucose at 37 °C for 1 h to convert 5hmC to 5-glucosylhydroxymethylcytosine. After column purification, genomic DNA was digested with methylation-sensitive restriction enzymes HpaII (cuts unmethylated CCGG) or MspI (cuts CCGG regardless of 5mC but not 5hmC). The digested DNA was then amplified by PCR using KLF6 promoter-specific primers, and the products were resolved on a 2% agarose gel to determine methylation patterns.

hMeDIP-qPCR

Genomic DNA (2 µg) was extracted from HPMECs and fragmented to 200–500 bp using a Covaris Focused-ultrasonicator. The DNA was heat-denatured at 95 °C for 10 min and immediately cooled on ice. 5% of the denatured DNA was reserved as input control. The remaining DNA was incubated with 0.5 µl of anti-5hmC antibody (#MA5-42319, Invitrogen) at 4 °C overnight with gentle rotation. Protein G magnetic beads (#88848, Thermo Fisher Scientific) were then added and incubated for 2 h at 4 °C to capture the antibody–DNA complexes. After extensive washing, the bound DNA was eluted and purified using a PCR purification kit (#28104, Qiagen). The enrichment of 5hmC at the KLF6 promoter was analyzed by qPCR using specific primers. Relative enrichment was calculated as the percentage of input.

Cell-Counting-Kit-8 (CCK-8)

Cell viability was assessed using the CCK-8 (#CK04, Dojindo Molecular Technologies, Japan) kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. HPMECs were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 10⁴ cells/well and treated with LPS and/or transfected with shRNAs or overexpression vectors as described. At designated time points, 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well and incubated for 2 h. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (ELX800, BioTek, USA).

TUNEL

Apoptotic cells were detected using the TUNEL Assay Kit (#25879, CST) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, HPMECs were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100, and incubated with a TUNEL reaction mixture. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Cells were imaged under a fluorescence microscope (#DM5000B, Leica, Germany), and apoptotic nuclei were grayscale-measured using ImageJ software.

Immunofluorescence staining

Cellular expression of α-SMA was examined using immunofluorescence techniques, as well as CD31 and TET1 in tissue samples. For HPMECs, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100, and blocked with 5% BSA for 1 h at room temperature. Cells were treated with anti-α-SMA primary antibodies (#PA5-85070, Invitrogen) at a 1:100 dilution for overnight incubation at 4 °C. After washing, HPMECs were incubated with Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:500, #R37116, Invitrogen) for 1 h at room temperature. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (#R37606, Invitrogen). For tissue samples, sections were incubated with primary antibodies against CD31 (1:200, #MA5-37858, Invitrogen) and TET1 (20 µg/mL, #PA5-72805, Invitrogen), followed by the same secondary antibody and DAPI counterstaining protocol. Microscopic visualization of fluorescence was performed on a Leica DM5000B platform.

Immunohistochemistry staining

Following euthanasia, pulmonary tissues were collected and immersion-fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde before paraffin embedding. Sequential processing included sectioning (4 μm), xylene deparaffinization, alcohol rehydration, and microwave-mediated antigen retrieval in citrate buffer (pH 6.0). A 15-minute incubation with 3% hydrogen peroxide was performed to suppress endogenous peroxidase. Primary antibody incubation (α-SMA, 1:200, #PA5-85070; Collagen I, 1:200, #PA1-26204; both Invitrogen) was conducted at 4 °C for 16 h. Following thorough washing, tissue sections were treated with HRP-linked secondary antibodies (Invitrogen #31460, 1:500 dilution) for 1 h at ambient temperature. Chromogenic detection was achieved using diaminobenzidine (DAB), followed by nuclear counterstaining with hematoxylin. Images were captured using a light microscope (#DM5000B, Leica).

Animal model and treatment

Male C57BL/6 mice (8 weeks old) obtained from Hunan SJA Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd.‌ were randomly divided into four groups (n = 6 per group): Control, LPS, LPS + sh-NC, and LPS + sh-TET1. To induce pulmonary fibrosis, mice received intraperitoneal injections of LPS (5 mg/kg in a total volume of 50 µL; #L2880, Sigma-Aldrich) daily for five consecutive days, as previously described [17]. To achieve TET1 knockdown, a lentiviral vector carrying shRNA targeting TET1 (5 × 10⁷ plaque-forming units per mouse; GenePharma) was administered via intratracheal injection five days prior to the first LPS injection [18]. Mice were euthanized 14 days after the initial LPS injection, and lung tissues were collected for subsequent analyses. All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Hunan Provincial People’s Hospital (The First Affiliated Hospital of Hunan Normal University) (No. 2025058).

Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) staining

Lung tissue sections were stained with H&E to evaluate histopathological changes. Sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and stained with hematoxylin for 5 min, followed by eosin for 2 min. Slides were then dehydrated, cleared, and mounted with coverslips. Histopathological changes were evaluated under a light microscope, and lung injury scores were assigned based on several criteria, including immune cell infiltration, pulmonary edema, and alveolar damage, as previously described by Lu et al. [19].

Masson trichrome staining

Pulmonary fibrosis was assessed using Masson trichrome staining. Paraffin-embedded lung sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and stained with Masson’s trichrome solution according to the manufacturer’s protocol (#HT15, Sigma-Aldrich). Collagen fibers were stained blue, nuclei were stained black, and cytoplasm was stained red. Fibrosis levels were quantified using the Ashcroft scoring system.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). All in vitro experiments were performed in triplicate. To compare two independent groups with normally distributed data, we applied a two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test. Comparisons among multiple groups were assessed using one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post-hoc test. All computations were executed using GraphPad Prism (version 8.0, California).

Results

TET1 knockdown ameliorated LPS-induced EndMT and apoptosis in HPMECs

To elucidate the role of TET1 in LPS-induced EndMT and apoptosis in HPMECs, we first assessed TET1 expression upon LPS stimulation. Treatment of HPMECs with 1 µg/mL LPS led to a significant upregulation of TET1 mRNA and protein levels, as demonstrated by RT-qPCR and Western blot analyses, with peak expression observed at 4 h post-treatment (Fig. 1A and B). Concurrently, Dot blot assays revealed that LPS treatment increased global 5hmC levels while decreased 5mC levels, indicating active DNA demethylation (Fig. 1C). Then, HPMECs were transfected with either a non-targeting control shRNA (sh-NC) or a TET1-targeting shRNA (sh-TET1). RT-qPCR and Western blot analyses confirmed that sh-TET1 effectively reduced TET1 expression at both the mRNA and protein levels compared to control and sh-NC groups (Fig. 1D and E). Further experiments demonstrated that LPS significantly upregulated TET1 expression in HPMECs treated with 1 µg/mL LPS for 4 h, whereas co-transfection with sh-TET1 markedly suppressed this upregulation (Fig. 1F and G). Functional assays using the CCK-8 method indicated that LPS treatment significantly impaired cell viability, whereas TET1 knockdown via sh-TET1 effectively restored cell viability in LPS-treated HPMECs (Fig. 1H). TUNEL assay showed that LPS exposure led to a substantial increase in apoptotic cell populations, which was significantly alleviated by TET1 knockdown (Fig. 1I). Western blot analysis indicated a marked reduction in CD31 and VE-cadherin expression coupled with increased α-SMA, Vimentin, and Collagen I levels following LPS treatment (Fig. 1J). Importantly, TET1 knockdown reversed these effects, restoring endothelial marker levels and attenuating mesenchymal marker expression. Immunofluorescence staining for α-SMA further confirmed these findings, showing elevated α-SMA expression following LPS treatment, which was significantly mitigated by TET1 knockdown (Fig. 1K). Overall, the results reveal an essential function of TET1 in facilitating LPS-mediated EndMT and apoptosis in HPMECs.

TET1 promoted KLF6 expression through mediating DNA demethylation of the KLF6 promoter in HPMECs

Current scientific literature emphasizes the vital involvement of transcription factor KLF6 in regulating various cellular processes including inflammation and apoptosis. KLF6 knockdown could reduce inflammatory damage and promote cell viability in type II alveolar epithelial cells during acute lung injury [9]. Herein, treatment with 1 µg/mL LPS for 4 h resulted in a significant upregulation of KLF6 mRNA and protein levels, as demonstrated by RT-qPCR and Western blot analyses (Fig. 2A and B). Bioinformatics analysis revealed the presence of CpG islands within the promoter region of the KLF6 gene, suggesting potential regulation by DNA methylation (Fig. 2C). MSP assay indicated that LPS treatment decreased the methylation level of the KLF6 promoter while increasing its demethylation (Fig. 2D). TET1 was found to bound to the promoter region of KLF6, as evidenced by chromatin immunoprecipitation (Fig. 2E). To further investigate the role of TET1 in KLF6 regulation, HPMECs were transfected with sh-TET1 for TET1 knockdown. MSP analysis demonstrated that TET1 knockdown resulted in enhanced methylation of the KLF6 promoter, underscoring the role of TET1 in maintaining KLF6 gene demethylation (Fig. 2F). Correspondingly, reduction of TET1 expression led to a significant decrease in KLF6 mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 2G and H). Furthermore, TET1 knockdown significantly reduced the levels of 5hmC at the KLF6 promoter region, a finding corroborated by semi-quantitative PCR, which confirmed the decrease in 5hmC modifications (Fig. 2I). To further confirm that TET1 regulates KLF6 expression via its dioxygenase activity, we constructed full-length TET1 (TET1-FL) and a catalytically inactive TET1 mutant (TET1-CDmut) in HPMECs. RT-qPCR analysis showed that TET1 knockdown significantly reduced KLF6 expression compared to the control group. Reintroduction of full-length TET1 restored KLF6 expression, whereas the catalytically inactive TET1 mutant failed to upregulate KLF6 (Fig. S1A). Consistently, hMeDIP-qPCR demonstrated that TET1 knockdown decreased 5hmC enrichment at the KLF6 promoter. Supplementation with full-length TET1 increased 5hmC levels at the KLF6 promoter, while the TET1-CDmut did not affect 5hmC enrichment (Fig. S1B). Collectively, these results demonstrate that TET1 promotes KLF6 expression through demethylation in HPMECs.

Overexpression of KLF6 reversed the effects of TET1 knockdown on LPS-induced EndMT and apoptosis in HPMECs

To investigate whether TET1 affects LPS-induced EndMT and apoptosis in HPMECs by regulating KLF6 expression, we conducted a series of experiments. Initially, LPS (1 µg/mL) treatment for 4 h significantly upregulated KLF6 mRNA and protein levels in HPMECs, which was reversed by TET1 knockdown, as shown by RT-qPCR and Western blot assays (Fig. 3A and B). Then, RT-qPCR and Western blot assays confirmed that oe-KLF6 significantly increased KLF6 expression at both the mRNA and protein levels compared to the control and oe-NC groups (Fig. 3C and D). Subsequently, RT-qPCR and Western blot analyses demonstrated that LPS treatment elevated KLF6 levels, and TET1 knockdown via sh-TET1 effectively reversed this upregulation. Importantly, co-transfection with oe-KLF6 in the sh-TET1 background further reinstated KLF6 expression levels (Fig. 3E and F). The CCK-8 analysis revealed a significant decrease in cellular viability following LPS exposure. While TET1 knockdown partially restored cell viability under LPS treatment, KLF6 overexpression counteracted this restorative effect by reducing cell viability again (Fig. 3G). Similarly, LPS exposure markedly increased apoptotic cell populations in TUNEL assays. Although TET1 knockdown attenuated this apoptotic response, subsequent KLF6 overexpression in TET1-silenced cells reversed the protective effect and elevated apoptosis rates (Fig. 3H). Analysis of EndMT markers by Western blot revealed that LPS treatment decreased endothelial markers (CD31 and VE-cadherin) and increased mesenchymal markers (α-SMA, Vimentin, and Collagen I). TET1 knockdown reversed these LPS-induced changes by restoring endothelial markers and reducing mesenchymal markers. However, KLF6 overexpression in TET1-knockdown cells re-established the EndMT phenotype (Fig. 3I). Immunofluorescence staining for α-SMA also showed that TET1 knockdown reduced LPS-induced α-SMA upregulation, which was abolished by KLF6 overexpression (Fig. 3J). Collectively, these results indicate that KLF6 overexpression effectively reverses the protective effects of TET1 knockdown on LPS-induced EndMT and apoptosis in HPMECs.

KLF6 activated the S1PR3/RhoA/ROCK signaling pathway in LPS-induced HPMECs

Next, we sought to identify the downstream target of KLF6 to clarify its role in LPS-induced HPMECs. S1PR3 is a G-protein-coupled receptor implicated in multiple lung pathologies—including pulmonary fibrosis [20]. Bioinformatic analyses using the JASPRA database predicted that S1PR3 could be a direct downstream target of KLF6 (Fig. 4A), and this prediction was experimentally validated through ChIP assay, which confirmed the direct binding of KLF6 to the S1PR3 promoter (Fig. 4B). Then HPMECs was tranfected sh-KLF6 to knockdown KLF6. RT-qPCR and Western blot confirmed that sh-KLF6 effectively decreased KLF6 expression at both the mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 4C and D). The Dual-luciferase reporter assay revealed that knockdown of KLF6 reduced the luciferase activity of wild-type S1PR3, while this effect of reducing luciferase activity disappeared when the S1PR3 promoter sequence mutated (Fig. 4E). The above further proves the transcriptional regulatory effect of KLF6 on S1PR3. Subsequent analyses revealed that LPS treatment significantly upregulated S1PR3 mRNA and protein levels in HPMECs, as demonstrated by RT-qPCR and Western blot analyses (Fig. 4F and G). Furthermore, the knockdown of KLF6 led to a significant reduction in S1PR3 mRNA and protein levels, indicating that KLF6 positively regulates S1PR3 expression (Fig. 4H and I). Previous studies indicated that S1PR3 activation can trigger the RhoA/ROCK pathway, a signaling cascade implicated in endothelial dysfunction and fibrotic changes in the lung [21]. Indeed, Western blot analysis revealed that silencing of KLF6 suppressed the RhoA/ROCK signaling pathway by downregulating active-RhoA and ROCK1 (Fig. 4I). Furthermore, HPMECs were subjected to LPS with or without sh-KLF6. RT-qPCR analysis showed that LPS significantly increased S1PR3 expression, which was effectively reversed by KLF6 knockdown (Fig. 4J). Correspondingly, Western blot analysis confirmed that the LPS-induced upregulation of S1PR3, active-RhoA, and ROCK1 was attenuated by sh-KLF6 (Fig. 4K). Overall, these findings reveal a mechanism whereby KLF6 enhances S1PR3 expression by directly binding to its promoter region, thereby activating the RhoA/ROCK signaling pathway in LPS-induced HPMECs.

Overexpression of S1PR3 reversed the effects of KLF6 knockdown on LPS-induced EndMT and apoptosis in HPMECs

To investigate whether KLF6 regulates LPS-induced EndMT and apoptosis in HPMECs through S1PR3, we performed the following experiments. Initially, HPMECs were transfected with either oe-NC or an S1PR3 overexpression vector (oe-S1PR3). RT-qPCR and Western blot analyses confirmed that oe-S1PR3 significantly elevated S1PR3 mRNA and protein levels compared to control and oe-NC groups (Fig. 5A and B). Next, we performed KLF6 knockdown and/or S1PR3 overexpression treatments followed by LPS exposure (1 µg/mL for 4 h). In LPS-treated HPMECs, knockdown of KLF6 reduced S1PR3 levels, this effect was reversed by S1PR3 overexpression (Fig. 5C and D). Functionally, KLF6 knockdown improved cell viability and reduced apoptosis in LPS-induced HPMECs, while these protective effects were abolished by S1PR3 overexpression (Fig. 5E and F). Western blot showed that KLF6 knockdown restored endothelial markers (CD31, VE-cadherin) and reduced mesenchymal markers (α-SMA, Vimentin and Collagen I) in LPS treated HPMECs, while S1PR3 overexpression negated these changes (Fig. 5G). Immunofluorescence staining of α-SMA further supported these findings (Fig. 5H). Collectively, these results demonstrate that overexpression of S1PR3 effectively reverses the protective effects of KLF6 knockdown on LPS-induced EndMT and apoptosis in HPMECs.

Knockdown of TET1 attenuated LPS-induced pulmonary fibrosis in mice by downregulating KLF6 and deactivating the S1PR3/RhoA/ROCK signaling pathway

To evaluate the effect of TET1 knockdown on LPS-induced pulmonary fibrosis, C57BL/6 mice were daily injected with LPS (5 mg/kg) for five days. TET1 knockdown was achieved via intratracheal delivery of shRNA-expressing lentivirus five days before LPS treatment. H&E staining revealed that TET1 knockdown alleviated LPS-induced lung injury and immune cell infiltration and edema (Fig. 6A). Masson trichrome staining further demonstrated that LPS significantly promoted pulmonary fibrosis, which was reduced by TET1 knockdown (Fig. 6B). Both transcriptional and protein analyses verified that TET1 knockdown abolished the LPS-mediated upregulation of TET1 in pulmonary tissues (Fig. 6C and D). Immunofluorescence staining revealed that LPS decreased CD31 and increased TET1 in the lung tissues, these effects were reversed by TET1 knockdown (Fig. 6E). Immunohistochemical analysis showed that knockdown of TET1 effectively reversed the LPS-induced upregulation of α-SMA and Collagen I (Fig. 6F). Western blot analysis demonstrated that TET1 knockdown restored VE-cadherin levels and attenuated the expression of Vimentin and Collagen I (Fig. 6G). Moreover, TET1 knockdown suppressed LPS-induced increase of KLF6, S1PR3, active-RhoA, and ROCK1 (Fig. 6H). Collectively, these results demonstrate that TET1 knockdown mitigates LPS-induced lung injury and fibrosis in ARDS mice by downregulating KLF6 and inactivating the S1PR3/RhoA/ROCK signaling pathway.

Discussion

ARDS remains a critical clinical challenge characterized by severe inflammation, endothelial barrier dysfunction, and pulmonary fibrosis [22]. The progression to pulmonary fibrosis in ARDS is closely associated with EndMT, a process where endothelial cells acquire mesenchymal characteristics, leading to increased fibrogenesis and exacerbation of lung injury [23, 24]. Recent studies have underscored the significance of EndMT in ARDS, suggesting that targeting this pathway may offer therapeutic benefits [23, 25, 26]. Although pulmonary fibrosis is classically viewed as a chronic process, mounting evidence shows that a single severe insult can initiate persistent fibrogenesis [27]. Intratracheal LPS triggers an acute innate-immune storm that releases TGF-β, IL-6, and TNF-α; these mediators persist beyond the initial neutrophilic phase, activate resident fibroblasts, and prompt extracellular-matrix deposition. Critically, LPS also induces EndMT in pulmonary endothelial cells [2], thereby converting a proportion of the vascular lining into matrix-synthesizing mesenchymal cells. Consequently, the LPS model is widely adopted to recapitulate the acute-to-fibrotic continuum of ARDS and to dissect the molecular switches that govern this transition. Our data extend this paradigm by demonstrating that knockdown of TET1 ameliorated LPS-induced EndMT and apoptosis in HPMECs. Furthermore, we revealed a novel mechanism wherein TET1 promoted KLF6 expression through DNA demethylation, subsequently activating the S1PR3/RhoA/ROCK signaling pathway, thereby exacerbating EndMT and pulmonary fibrosis during pulmonary fibrosis.

TET proteins, also known as Ten-Eleven Translocation proteins, are involved in facilitating DNA demethylation. This family includes three members: TET1, TET2, and TET3 [28]. In particular, TET1 is known to catalyze the conversion of 5mC to 5hmC, facilitating DNA demethylation and influencing gene expression [29, 30]. TET1 has been implicated in various organ injuries and fibrotic diseases. For instance, TET1 was shown to promote M1 macrophage polarization and proinflammatory responses in THP-1 cells, highlighting its detrimental role in inflammatory processes associated with tissue injury [31]. Furthermore, Lin et al. revealed that TET1 aggravated neuroinflammation in cerebral ischemia/reperfusion injury by modulating m5C RNA modifications in microglia, specifically by stabilizing RelB mRNA and shifting microglial polarization toward the proinflammatory M1 phenotype [32]. In idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, Zhang et al. demonstrated that miR-30a inhibited TET1 expression, leading to decreased mitochondrial fission and attenuating pulmonary fibrosis, highlighting TET1’s involvement in fibrogenesis [33]. However, the role of TET1 in lung injury has not been extensively explored. Our findings provided novel insights by showing that TET1 knockdown improved LPS-induced EndMT and apoptosis in HPMECs. This positions TET1 as a potential therapeutic target to alleviate fibrosis in lung injury or ARDS.

TET1 can promote target gene expression by maintaining DNA in a hypomethylated state through 5-hydroxymethylcytosine modifications [34, 35]. In our study, we revealed that TET1 enhanced KLF6 expression by demethylating its promoter region. Possessing zinc finger structural domains, KLF6 serves as a transcriptional regulator participating in the growth, differentiation, and death of cells [36]. KLF6 has been implicated in various pulmonary diseases. Chen et al. found that inhibition of KLF6 reduced inflammation and apoptosis in type II alveolar epithelial cells in acute lung injury [9]. Additionally, Zou et al. reported that KLF6 regulated the LOX-1/TGF-β1 axis to mediate EMT, thereby promoting diabetic pulmonary fibrosis [37]. Our data demonstrated that TET1-induced upregulation of KLF6 promoted LPS-induced EndMT and apoptosis in HPMECs, establishing a direct link between TET1 activity, KLF6 expression, and pulmonary microvascular endothelial cell dysfunction.

S1PR3 has been shown to mediate LPS-induced inflammation and lung injury. Peng et al. demonstrated that inhibition of S1PR3 using a selective antagonist protected the lungs against LPS-induced ARDS by suppressing pulmonary inflammation and improving mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation [13]. Similarly, Sun et al. identified S1PR3 as a novel biomarker in acute lung injury, showing that elevated plasma S1PR3 levels were linked to disease severity and mortality in both murine models and human patients [38]. Activation of S1PR3 by its ligand sphingosine-1-phosphate (S1P) could lead to the activation of RhoA, which subsequently activates ROCK [39, 40]. Piao et al. reported that S1PR3 deficiency attenuated atherosclerosis by disrupting the S1P/S1PR3/RhoA/ROCK signaling axis [21]. Additionally, Liu et al. demonstrated that S1PR3 in hippocampal neurons enhanced synaptic plasticity and mitigated depressive behaviors through the downregulation of the RhoA/ROCK1 signaling pathway [41]. The above indicates RhoA/ROCK as the downstream pathway of S1PR3. Our study illustrated that KLF6 upregulated S1PR3 expression, which in turn activated RhoA/ROCK pathway, leading to enhanced EndMT and apoptosis in LPS-induced HPMECs.

Of course, in addition to vascular endothelial cell EndMT, macrophage polarization, and inflammation are also actively involved in regulating ARDS-associated pulmonary fibrosis. Macrophages can adopt pro-inflammatory (M1) or anti-inflammatory (M2) phenotypes, influencing the progression of lung injury [42]. Li et al. demonstrated that TREM2 inhibits LPS-induced acute lung injury by suppressing macrophage ferroptosis, suggesting that modulation of macrophage function is crucial in ARDS treatment [43]. TET1 has been reported to regulate macrophage polarization and inflammation. Zhou et al. found that exosomes derived from induced pluripotent stem cells suppressed M2-type macrophages during pulmonary fibrosis via the miR-302a-3p/TET1 axis, highlighting TET1’s role in macrophage-mediated fibrogenesis [44]. However, whether TET1/KLF6/S1PR3/RhoA/ROCK axis influences pulmonary fibrosis through modulation of macrophage functions remains unclear, which deserves further exploration in the future.

In conclusion, our findings elucidate a novel TET1/KLF6/S1PR3/RhoA/ROCK signaling axis that contributes to LPS-induced EndMT and apoptosis in LPS-induced pulmonary fibrosis. However, the clinical relevance of these findings remains to be established. At present, it remains unclear whether TET1 or KLF6 expression levels are altered in human ARDS or pulmonary fibrosis patient samples. Future investigations analyzing patient-derived tissues will be crucial to establish whether dysregulation of this pathway contributes to disease pathogenesis in humans. Such studies should also examine the correlation between TET1/KLF6 expression and disease severity, EndMT marker levels, and patient prognosis. These analyses would not only validate the translational significance of our results, but also provide valuable insights into the potential of TET1/KLF6 as biomarkers or therapeutic targets for ARDS-related pulmonary fibrosis. Overall, our work provides novel insights into the molecular mechanisms of pulmonary fibrosis and paves the way for future therapeutic research.
