Interaction between skew-representability, tensor products, extension properties, and rank inequalities
Kristóf Bérczi, Boglárka Gehér, András Imolay, László Lovász, Carles Padró, Tamás Schwarcz

Abstract
Skew-representable matroids form a fundamental class in matroid theory, bridging combinatorics and linear algebra. They play an important role in areas such as coding theory, optimization, and combinatorial geometry, where linear structure is crucial for both theoretical insights and algorithmic applications. Since deciding skew-representability is computationally intractable, much effort has been focused on identifying necessary or sufficient conditions for a matroid to be skew-representable. In this paper, we introduce a novel approach to studying skew-representability and structural properties of matroids and polymatroid functions via tensor products. We provide a characterization of skew-representable matroids, as well as of those representable over skew fields of a given prime characteristic, in terms of tensor products. As an algorithmic consequence, we show that deciding skew-representability, or representability over a skew field of fixed prime characteristic, is co-recursively enumerable: that is, certificates of non-skew-representability – in general or over a fixed prime characteristic – can be verified. We also prove that every rank-3 matroid admits a tensor product with any uniform matroid and give a construction yielding the unique freest tensor product in this setting. Finally, as an application of the tensor product framework, we give a new proof of Ingleton’s inequality and, more importantly, derive the first known linear rank inequality for folded skew-representable matroids that does not follow from the common information property. Keywords: Extension properties, Freest products, Matroids, Polymatroid functions, Rank inequalities, Skew-representability, Tensor product

Introduction

The class of representable matroids serves as a fundamental bridge between linear algebra and combinatorial structures. While linear and algebraic representations have attracted most of the attention, alternative forms of matroid and polymatroid representation have gained increasing relevance, particularly due to applications in information theory. Accordingly, several notions of representability have been developed. A matroid is
linearly representable
, or simply
representable
, over a field
\mathbb{F}
if the elements of its ground set correspond to vectors in a vector space over
\mathbb{F}
in such a way that the rank of every set equals the dimension of the spanned subspace. Some polymatroids admit similar representations via collections of vector subspaces. More generally,
skew-representable
matroids and polymatroids are defined via analogous representations over skew fields.

Skew fields are sometimes called
division rings
in the literature.
Given a positive integer
k
, a matroid with rank function
r
is said to be
k
-folded representable
or
k
-folded skew-representable
if the polymatroid with rank function
k\cdot r
is representable or skew-representable, respectively. In an
algebraic representation
of a matroid over a field
\mathbb{F}
, the ground set is mapped to elements of a field extension, and the rank of each set corresponds to its transcendence degree over
\mathbb{F}
. A matroid admitting such a representation is called
algebraic
, and every representable matroid is known to be algebraic
[
52
]
. A polymatroid function is
entropic
if its rank function arises from the joint Shannon entropies of random variables
[
21
,
22
]
. Both representation by
partitions

[
48
]
and by
almost affine codes

[
63
]
characterize entropic matroids, and representable polymatroids – in particular, representable and folded representable matroids – are entropic
[
13
]
. A polymatroid function is
almost entropic
if it can be approximated arbitrarily closely by entropic ones. Almost entropic matroids are particularly important in the study of secret sharing schemes. Moreover, it was recently shown that algebraic matroids are also almost entropic
[
49
]
, extending the relevance of the extension properties of almost entropic polymatroids to the algebraic setting.

Given the broad range of representability notions and the nontrivial connections among them, several natural questions arise:
Can one decide algorithmically whether a given matroid admits a particular type of representation? If such a representation does not exist, is there, in some sense, an elegant proof of non-representability? If a representation exists, can its structural or algebraic complexity be effectively bounded or described?
While these questions are interesting on their own, their broader significance lies in the structural understanding they may offer of the matroid classes discussed above, with potentially far-reaching algorithmic implications. A prime example of this phenomenon is the
matroid parity problem
, which is generally intractable
[
32
]
; yet, Lovász’s celebrated result
[
43
,
41
,
42
]
provides an efficient solution in the case of representable matroids. As observed in
[
42
]
, the proof in fact extends to matroids satisfying the so-called
double circuit property
. Dress and Lovász
[
17
]
showed that algebraic matroids have this property, and Hochstättler and Kern
[
28
]
later extended this to pseudomodular matroids. However, designing a polynomial-time algorithm requires a compact representation of such matroids – once again highlighting the crucial role of representations.

Assuming a rank or independence oracle access to a matroid, one can decide whether it is representable over every field using Seymour’s decomposition theorem
[
62
]
. However, Truemper
[
64
]
showed that most representability problems – such as determining representability over a specific field or fields with a given characteristic – are computationally intractable. As a result, extensive research has focused on identifying necessary or sufficient conditions for representability
[
29
,
13
,
35
,
1
]
. Given two matroids
M_{1}=(S_{1},r_{1})
and
M_{2}=(S_{2},r_{2})
, a matroid
M=(S_{1}\times S_{2},r)
is a
tensor product
of
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
if
r(Y_{1}\times Y_{2})=r_{1}(Y_{1})\cdot r_{2}(Y_{2})
holds for all
Y_{1}\subseteq S_{1}
and
Y_{2}\subseteq S_{2}
. Since any two matroids representable over the same field admit a tensor product via the Kronecker product of their representing matrices, this operation appears to be a promising tool for studying representability. Yet research in this direction remained limited after Las Vergnas
[
38
]
showed that tensor products do not always exist – for example, the Vámos matroid and the rank-2 uniform matroid on three elements admit none. Progress resumed only recently, when Bérczi, Gehér, Imolay, Lovász, Maga, and Schwarcz
[
4
]
observed a rather surprising connection between matroid representability and the existence of certain tensor products. Motivated by this observation, the present paper studies the interplay between skew-representability, tensor products, extension properties, and rank inequalities in matroids.

1.1
Related work and motivation

A full overview of results and applications related to the representability of matroids and polymatroids is beyond the scope of this paper. Instead, we briefly outline the main motivations for our work.

Information and rank inequalities.

For each positive integer
n
, polymatroid functions on a ground set
S
with
n
elements form a polyhedral convex cone
\Gamma_{n}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2^{S}}
. It is determined by the
Shannon inequalities
, that is, the linear inequalities derived from the polymatroid axioms. The
entropy region

\Gamma^{*}_{n}
is the subset of
\Gamma_{n}
corresponding to entropic polymatroids. Its closure
\overline{\Gamma}^{*}_{n}
, consisting of the rank functions of almost entropic polymatroids, is a convex cone
[
68
, Theorem 1]
. This cone is characterized by the so-called
linear information inequalities
. The first non-Shannon linear information inequality, discovered by Zhang and Yeung
[
69
]
, proved that
\overline{\Gamma}^{*}_{n}\subsetneq\Gamma_{n}
for every
n\geq 4
. Moreover,
\overline{\Gamma}^{*}_{n}
is not a polytope for
n\geq 4
, that is, it cannot be described by any finite set of linear inequalities
[
47
]
.

Linear rank inequalities
characterize the convex cone
L_{n}
spanned by the rank functions of representable polymatroids on
n
elements. The first non-Shannon linear rank inequality was discovered by Ingleton
[
29
]
, providing a necessary condition for a matroid to be representable. Mayhew, Newman, and Whittle
[
50
]
used Ingleton’s inequality to show that there are infinitely many excluded minors for the class of matroids representable over any given infinite field. Since then, infinite families of linear rank inequalities have been identified
[
13
,
35
]
. More recently, Bérczi, Gehér, Imolay, Lovász, Maga, and Schwarcz
[
4
]
showed that the existence of a tensor product with the uniform matroid
U_{2,3}
is not only a necessary condition for representability, but also implies Ingleton’s inequality for polymatroid functions.

Characteristic-dependent linear rank inequalities
– that is, inequalities that hold for matroids representable over fields of fixed characteristic – have also attracted considerable interest. The first such result is due to Blasiak, Kleinberg, and Lubetzky, who used the dependencies of the Fano and non-Fano matroids
[
6
]
. Additional inequalities were introduced by Dougherty, Freiling, and Zeger
[
15
]
, motivated in part by applications in network coding. Since then, the topic has been further developed in several works, including
[
14
,
16
,
44
,
59
,
58
,
56
,
57
]
. The area involves many open questions:
Can we give a complete characterization of certain classes of matroids using rank inequalities? More generally, how can new linear rank inequalities – either general or characteristic-dependent – be generated?

Extension properties.

Interestingly, all of the currently known linear rank inequalities that are not characteristic-specific can be derived from the so-called
common information property

[
13
,
1
]
, or more generally, from related
extension properties
. These properties appear in multiple contexts. In information theory, they capture the idea that two sources share a structural core; in matroid theory, they describe when two matroids on the same ground set can be seen as projections of a joint matroid; and for polymatroids and entropy functions, they concern the existence of joint functions extending given marginals. Extension properties often yield necessary conditions for representability, whether of matroids or polymatroids. Many known information inequalities arise from such extension properties of almost entropic polymatroids, including the
copy lemma
and
Ahlswede-Körner
extensions
[
2
,
33
]
. The Ingleton-Main and Dress-Lovász extension properties, introduced in
[
7
]
based on earlier results
[
30
,
17
]
, provide necessary conditions for algebraic matroid representability. The above motivates to the following problem:
Are there non characteristic-specific linear rank or information inequalities that do not follow from known extensions properties?

Rank-
3
matroids.

Rank-3 matroids play a key role in questions related to representability. One of the classical examples of nonrepresentable matroids is the rank-3 non-Desargues matroid
[
29
]
(see also
[
52
, Chapter 6.1]
): it follows from Desargues’s theorem in projective geometry that this matroid is not representable over any skew field. Later, Lindström
[
39
]
also showed it to be non-algebraic. Another classical example is the rank-3 non-Pappus matroid: it follows from Pappus’s theorem that it is not representable over any field, while it is representable over a skew field
[
29
]
. In general, the proof of Kühne, Pendavingh, and Yashfe
[
37
]
shows that skew-representability of rank-3 matroids is already undecidable (see
2.5
). Every matroid of rank 3 satisfies all known extension properties, so no previously known linear rank inequality can certify the non-representability of any rank-3 matroid. It is also known that the characteristic set of any matroid coincides with the characteristic set of some matroid of rank three
[
34
,
46
]
. Thus the question naturally appears:
Is there any alternative to linear rank inequalities for distinguishing non-skew-representable rank-3 matroids from representable ones?

Symmetric powers.

Tensor products of matroids can also be considered in higher orders. When all
k
components in the product coincide with a fixed matroid
M
, the resulting structure on ordered
k
-tuples from
S
defines the
k
-th power
of
M
. Restricting to unordered
k
-tuples leads to the study of
symmetric powers
, where the matroid structure respects coordinate permutations. This idea, first studied by Lovász
[
40
]
and Mason
[
45
]
, revealed that not every matroid admits such symmetric constructions. A connection between symmetric powers and rigidity theory was recently identified by Brakensiek, Dhar, Gao, Gopi, and Larson
[
8
]
, who showed that symmetric tensor matroids are dual to certain rigidity matroids. Building on this relationship, Jackson and Shin-ichi
[
31
]
defined abstract symmetric tensor matroids as a dual concept to abstract rigidity matroids and studied their structural properties. In particular, they verified Graver’s maximality conjecture
[
24
]
for the generic
d
-dimensional rigidity matroid on
K_{n}
when
n-d\leq 6
. This conjecture asserts that among all abstract
d
-rigidity matroids on the complete graph
K_{n}
, there is a unique maximal element under the weak order of matroids, namely the generic
d
-dimensional rigidity matroid. The conjecture raises the following question:
Which matroids admit a
k
-th (respectively, symmetric) power that is maximal in the weak order, for every nonnegative integer
k
?

Applications in information theory.

Linear programming problems involving rank and information inequalities have been used to address various questions in information theory. One example is the search for lower bounds in secret sharing
[
54
,
51
,
3
]
. Nevertheless, it is not clear how to select the appropriate inequalities for such problems, and, moreover, many relevant inequalities are still unknown. Better results have been obtained using the strategy proposed in
[
19
]
, where, instead of linear and rank inequalities, constraints derived from extension properties are used in linear programming problems. This approach has yielded several results in secret sharing, matroid representation, and related areas
[
1
,
2
,
25
,
26
]
. Though these methods have led to the solution of some long-standing open problems, their applicability is quite limited due to computational constraints. Thus one may ask:
Is it possible to find better results by using constraints derived from other properties of representable or entropic polymatroids?

1.2
Our results and techniques

In this paper, we introduce a fundamentally new approach to studying representability and extension properties of matroids and polymatroids. Our method builds on tensor products, offering a perspective distinct from earlier techniques and unifying seemingly unrelated results. It is truly remarkable that such a simple operation captures deep structural properties and reveals previously unexplored connections. Several natural questions remain, suggesting a promising direction for further research.

As a first step toward understanding skew-representable matroids,
Section

3
focuses on modular extensions. Roughly speaking, a matroid is
fully modular extendable
if it can be embedded into a – possibly infinite – matroid whose pairs of flats satisfy the submodular rank inequality with equality. The main result of this section is a characterization of matroids whose connected components are either of rank
3
or skew-representable, not necessarily over the same field (Theorem
3.4
). The proof relies on the Veblen–Young theorem on representations of projective spaces. As a corollary, we show that a connected matroid of rank at least
4
is skew-representable if and only if it is fully modular extendable (Corollary
3.5
).

In
Section

4
, we prove several statements related to tensor products, modular extensions, and quotients of matroids and polymatroid functions, presented as a series of shorter lemmas (Lemmas
4.1
–
4.14
). Among other topics, we analyze how the tensor product behaves under basic operations such as direct sums and minors, and we also prove key results that will help extend matroid results to polymatroids. Since this section is quite technical, we suggest that first-time readers skip the proofs and simply skim the statements.

Section

5
is devoted to one of the main results of the paper: a characterization of skew-representability via tensor products – a connection that is far from obvious and highlights the surprising power of tensor products in this context. We establish this characterization in several steps. In
Section

5.1
, we start by showing a link between the existence of a tensor product of a matroid with the uniform matroid
U_{2,3}
and the existence of an extension in which a given pair of flats forms a modular pair (Theorem
5.1
). In particular, such an extension exists for any pair of flats whenever the corresponding tensor product exists (Corollary
5.4
).

.
Then, in

5.4

5.16

5.17
).

As rank-
3
matroids satisfy all known extension properties, distinguishing non skew-representable rank-
3
matroids from skew-representable ones is of particular interest;
Section

6
focuses on this problem. The results of Section

is not representable over any skew field, then there exists some

exists. We prove that every rank-
3
matroid has a tensor product with every uniform matroid (Theorem

), implying that the value of such a

is always at least
2
. Furthermore, our construction yields the unique
freest
tensor product: any set that is dependent in our construction is necessarily dependent in every tensor product of the matroids.

Motivated by their fundamental role in studying matroid and polymatroid function representations,
Section

7
focuses on linear rank inequalities. Since Ingleton’s foundational work, there has been extensive research aimed at finding new inequalities. Here, we introduce a novel method that can be applied broadly to derive both existing and new inequalities. As an illustration, in
Section

7.1
, we give a simpler proof of Ingleton’s inequality (Theorem
7.1
). Using a similar approach, we derive two inequalities satisfied by polymatroid functions that admit a tensor product with the rank function of the Fano and non-Fano matroids, respectively, in

7.2
(Theorem
7.5
). As a consequence, we obtain linear rank inequalities satisfied by polymatroid functions that are skew-representable over some skew field of characteristic 2, and by those representable over some skew field of characteristic different from 2, respectively (Corollary
7.6
). Finally, we prove the other main result of the paper: the first known linear rank inequality valid for folded skew-representable matroids that does not follow from the common information property (Theorem
7.9
and Corollary
7.13
). This result represents the culmination of our observations obtained through the tensor product framework and demonstrates its power to reveal structural properties beyond existing methods.

Organization

The rest of of the paper is organized as follows. In

Section

Preliminaries

General notation.

We denote the sets of
reals
and
integers
by
\mathbb{R}
and
\mathbb{Z}
, respectively, and add
+
as a subscript when considering positive values only. For
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, we use
[k]\coloneqq\{1,\dots,k\}
while
[0]=\emptyset
by convention. Given a ground set
S
, a set
X\subseteq S
and an element
y\in S
, the sets
X\setminus\{y\}
and
X\cup\{y\}
are abbreviated as
X-y
and
X+y
, respectively. If
S=S_{1}\times S_{2}
for some sets
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
, then for any
X\subseteq S_{1}
,
a\in S_{1}
,
Y\subseteq S_{2}
and
b\in S_{2}
, we use
X^{b}=X\times\{b\}
and
{}^{a}Y=\{a\}\times Y
.

Set functions and tensor product.

A set function
\varphi\colon 2^{S}\to\mathbb{R}
is called
increasing
if
\varphi(X)\leq\varphi(Y)
whenever
X\subseteq Y
, and
submodular
if
\varphi(X)+\varphi(Y)\geq\varphi(X\cap Y)+\varphi(X\cup Y)
for all
X,Y\subseteq S
. The latter is equivalent to the inequality
\varphi(X\cup Z)-\varphi(X)\geq\varphi(Y\cup Z)-\varphi(Y)
for all
X\subseteq Y\subseteq S
and
Z\subseteq S\setminus Y
. We call
\varphi
a
polymatroid function
if it is increasing, submodular, and
\varphi(\emptyset)=0
. If furthermore
\varphi(X)\leq k\cdot|X|
holds for some
k\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
and for all
X\subseteq S
, then
\varphi
is a
k
-polymatroid function
.

Let
\varphi_{1}\colon 2^{S_{1}}\to\mathbb{R}
and
\varphi_{2}\colon 2^{S_{2}}\to\mathbb{R}
be polymatroid functions defined over ground sets
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
, respectively. Then
\varphi\colon 2^{S_{1}\times S_{2}}\to\mathbb{R}
is a
tensor product
of
\varphi_{1}
and
\varphi_{2}
if
\varphi
is also a polymatroid function and
\varphi(X_{1}\times X_{2})=\varphi_{1}(X_{1})\cdot\varphi_{2}(X_{2})
holds for all
X_{1}\subseteq S_{1}
and
X_{2}\subseteq S_{2}
. Since the tensor product may not be uniquely defined, we denote the
set of tensor products
of
\varphi_{1}
and
\varphi_{2}
by
\varphi_{1}\otimes\varphi_{2}
. It is worth noting that the tensor product may not be integer-valued even if
\varphi_{1}
and
\varphi_{2}
are.

Given polymatroid functions
\varphi
and
\psi
, we define the set
T_{k}(\varphi,\psi)
to consist of all polymatroids that can be obtained by performing a sequence of
k
(left-associative) tensor products of
\varphi
with
\psi
. That is,
T_{1}(\varphi,\psi)=\varphi\otimes\psi
, and
T_{k}(\varphi,\psi)=\bigcup_{\varphi^{\prime}\in T_{k-1}(\varphi,\psi)}\varphi^{\prime}\otimes\psi
for
k\geq 2
. We say that
\varphi
is
k
-tensor-compatible with
\psi
if
T_{k}(\varphi,\psi)
is non-empty. We denote by
\mathcal{T}_{k}(\psi)
the
set of all polymatroid functions that are
k
-tensor-compatible with
\psi
, that is,
\mathcal{T}_{k}(\psi)=\{\varphi\mid T_{k}(\varphi,\psi)\neq\emptyset\}
.

Let
\psi\colon 2^{T}\to\mathbb{R}
be a set function over some finite ground set
T
, and let
\mathcal{Q}=(T_{1},\dots,T_{q})
be a partition of
T
into
q
(possibly empty) parts. The
quotient
of
\psi
with respect to
\mathcal{Q}
is the set function
(\psi/\mathcal{Q})
over
T_{\mathcal{Q}}=\{t_{1},\dots,t_{q}\}
defined by
(\psi/\mathcal{Q})(X)=\psi(\bigcup_{t_{i}\in X}T_{i})
for
X\subseteq T_{\mathcal{Q}}
. Observe that taking a quotient preserves the properties of being zero on the empty set, increasing, and submodular; hence, the quotient of a matroid rank function is an integer-valued polymatroid function. Helgason
[
27
]
showed the converse: every integer-valued polymatroid function can be obtained as the quotient of a matroid rank function.

Proposition 2.1

(Helgason)
.

Let
\varphi\colon 2^{S}\to\mathbb{Z}
be an integer-valued polymatroid function. Let
S_{\varphi}
denote the set obtained from
S
by taking
\varphi(s)
copies of each
s\in S
, and let
\theta\colon S_{\varphi}\to S
be the natural mapping that assigns each copy to its original element. For
Z\subseteq S_{\varphi}
, define
r_{\varphi}(Z)=\min\{\varphi(\theta(X))+|Z\setminus X|\mid X\subseteq Z\}
. Then
M_{\varphi}=(S_{\varphi},r_{\varphi})
is a matroid, and
\varphi(Y)=r_{\varphi}(\theta^{-1}(Y))
for all
Y\subseteq S
; that is,
\varphi=r_{\varphi}/\mathcal{Q}
, where
\mathcal{Q}=(\theta^{-1}(s_{1}),\dots,\theta^{-1}(s_{q}))
.

For a polymatroid function
\varphi
, we denote the corresponding matroid provided by Proposition
2.1
by
M_{\varphi}=(S_{\varphi},r_{\varphi})
.

Matroids.

For basic definitions on matroids such as independent sets, bases, circuits, loops, and rank, we refer the reader to
[
52
]
; see also
[
4
]
for a quick introduction. Recall that a
uniform matroid
of rank

over a ground set of size
n
has rank function
r(X)=\min\{|X|,r\}
and is denoted by
U_{r,n}
.

A matroid is
simple
if it has no loops and parallel elements. Given a matroid
M=(S,r)
on a finite ground set
S
, we denote by
\si(M)
the matroid obtained by deleting all loops and retaining exactly one element from each parallel class; we refer to
\si(M)
as the
simplification of
M
A

is a set
F\subseteq S
such that
r(F+e)=r(F)+1
holds for every

. We refer to a flat of rank
2
as a
line
. For a set
X\subseteq S
, the unique smallest flat containing
X
is called the
closure
of
X
and is denoted by
\mathrm{cl}_{M}(X)
; we dismiss the subscript
M
when the matroid is clear from the context. A pair
F_{1},F_{2}
of flats is called
modular
if
r(F_{1})+r(F_{2})=r(F_{1}\cap F_{2})+r(F_{1}\cup F_{2})
. A matroid is
modular
if any pair of its flats is modular. For sets
X,Y\subseteq S
, we say that
X

spans

Y
in
M
if
r(X\cup Y)=r(X)

Modular extensions.

We call every integer-valued polymatroid function
\varphi\colon 2^{S}\to\mathbb{Z}

0
-modular extendable
. For

with respect to

\varphi^{\prime}\colon 2^{S^{\prime}}\to\mathbb{Z}

such that there exists

, and

are irrelevant, and we may take the quotient of
\varphi^{\prime}
with respect to the partition consisting of the singletons in

For

and

if it is

. If the integrality assumption is dropped throughout, the corresponding properties are called

-fractionally modular extendable

We adapt the notation from set functions to matroids in the following sense: define a matroid

.

4

Projective spaces.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a – not necessarily finite – set, and
\mathbb{P}=(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{L})
be a pair where
\mathcal{L}\subseteq 2^{\mathcal{P}}
. We call the elements of
\mathcal{P}

points
, the elements of
\mathcal{L}

and we say that a point

if it satisfies the following axioms:

(P1)

For any two lines, there is at most one point that is incident to both of them.

(P2)

For any two distinct points
p,q\in\mathcal{P}
, there is exactly one line that is incident to both of them, denoted
\overline{pq}
.

(P3)

If the lines
\overline{ab}
and
\overline{cd}
intersect for distinct points
a
,
b
,
c
and
d
, then so do the lines
\overline{ac}
and
\overline{bd}
.

(P4)

Every line is incident to at least three points.

Note that the above definition allows for the degenerate case where
\mathcal{P}
consists of a single element and
\mathcal{L}
is empty. Two projective spaces
\mathbb{P}_{1},\mathbb{P}_{2}
are
isomorphic
, denoted by
\mathbb{P}_{1}\cong\mathbb{P}_{2}
, if there is a bijection between their point sets that preserves lines when applied in either direction. Let
\mathbb{F}
be a skew field and
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
. For

let
v\sim w
if there exists a scalar
\lambda\in\mathbb{F}
such that
\lambda\cdot v=w
. Let
\mathbb{F}\mathbb{P}^{n}
denote the projective space whose lines are the images of the two-dimensional subspaces of
\mathbb{F}^{n+1}
under
\sim
, called the
n
-dimensional projective space over
\mathbb{F}
. A projective space is said to be
skew-representable
if there exist a skew field
\mathbb{F}
and an integer
n
such that
\mathbb{P}\cong\mathbb{F}\mathbb{P}^{n}
. The
projective geometry

PG(n,p)
is the matroid whose ground set consists of the 1-dimensional subspaces of
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n+1}
, and whose independent sets are those collections of points that correspond to linearly independent vectors in
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n+1}
. Equivalently,
PG(n,p)
is the matroid represented by the columns of a matrix over
\mathbb{F}_{p}
whose columns form a set of representatives of the 1-dimensional subspaces of
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n+1}
.

If
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{L})
satisfies axioms

-
(P3)
and any line contains at least two points, then we call
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{L})

\bigoplus_{j\in J}\mathbb{P}_{j}
is defined as the pair
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{L})
where
\mathcal{P}=\bigcup_{j\in J}\mathcal{P}_{j}
and
\mathcal{L}=(\bigcup_{j\in J}\mathcal{L}_{j})\cup(\bigcup_{j\neq k\in J}\mathcal{P}_{j}\times\mathcal{P}_{k}
), where
\mathcal{P}_{j}\times\mathcal{P}_{k}\coloneqq\{\{a_{j},a_{k}\}\ |\ a_{j}\in\mathcal{P}_{j},a_{k}\in\mathcal{P}_{k}
}. Note that the direct sum of projective spaces is not necessarily a projective space, but it is always a generalized projective space. Conversely, every generalized projective space arises as such a direct sum, as formalized in the following result, which summarizes two exercises from
[
5

Proposition 2.6
.

[
5

For every generalized projective space
\mathbb{P}=(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{L})

\{\mathbb{P}_{j}\}_{j\in J}
of projective spaces such that
\mathbb{P}=\bigoplus_{j\in J}\mathbb{P}_{j}

Proof.

Given two points
a,b\in\mathcal{P}
, we say that
a\sim b
if
a=b
or the line
\overline{ab}
contains at least three points. We claim that this induces an equivalence relation on
\mathcal{P}
. To see this, assume that
a\sim b
and
b\sim c
for distinct
a,b
and
c
. We need to show that
a\sim c
holds as well. If
\overline{ab}=\overline{bc}
then the statement is trivial, hence we may assume that
\overline{ab}\neq\overline{bc}
.

Let
d
be a third point on
\overline{ab}
and
e
be a third point on
\overline{bc}
. If
d=e
, then
(P2)
implies
\overline{ab}=\overline{bd}=\overline{be}=\overline{ac}
, a contradiction. Thus

. Now the lines
\overline{ad}
and
\overline{ce}
intersect in

(P3)
,
\overline{ac}
and
\overline{de}
must also intersect. We denote the intersection by

\overline{ab}\neq\overline{bc}
, the line
\overline{de}
contains neither

is different from both

By the above,
\sim
gives a partition of
\mathcal{P}
into equivalence classes
(\mathcal{P}_{j})_{j\in J}
. For any two points

that are not in the same class,
\overline{pq}
must be the two-element set

. Therefore,
\mathbb{P}
is indeed the direct sum of projective spaces
\mathbb{P}_{j}=(\mathcal{P}_{j},\mathcal{L}_{j})

\mathcal{L}_{j}
consists of the lines containing at least two points from
\mathcal{P}_{j}
.

\mathbb{F}=(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{L})

whose flats are exactly the subspaces of
\mathbb{P}
; we denote this finitary matroid by
M_{\mathbb{P}}
. It is not difficult to check that if
\mathbb{P}=\bigoplus_{j\in J}\mathbb{P}
, then
M_{\mathbb{P}}=\bigoplus_{j\in J}M_{\mathbb{P}_{j}}
.

We will also use the following classical result of Veblen and Young
[
66
]
on representations of projective spaces; see also
[
5
, Theorem 3.4.2]
for a more recent treatment.

Proposition 2.7

(Veblen and Young)
.

Any projective space of dimension at least
3
is isomorphic to a projective space defined over a skew field.

3
Fully modular extendable matroids

The goal of this section is to establish a connection between fully modular extendability and skew-representability of matroids. While every projective space
\mathbb{P}
naturally gives rise to a matroid
M_{\mathbb{P}}
whose flats are its subspaces, our first result shows a partial converse: the flats of any modular matroid can be identified with the subspaces of a generalized projective space.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
M=(S,r)
be a simple, modular, finitary matroid of rank at least 3, and let
\mathcal{L}
denote the lines of
M
. Then,
\mathbb{P}=(S,\mathcal{L})
is a generalized projective space, the subspaces of which are exactly the flats of the matroid.

Proof.

For any pair of distinct
F_{1},F_{2}\in\mathcal{L}
, we have
2+2=r(F_{1})+r(F_{2})\geq r(F_{1}\cap F_{2})+r(F_{1}\cup F_{2})\geq r(F_{1}\cap F_{2})+3
, which implies
r(F_{1}\cap F_{2})\leq 1
. Since
M
is simple,
|F_{1}\cap F_{2}|\leq 1
follows, showing
(P1)
. To see
(P2)
, by the simplicity of
M
, observe that the unique rank-
2
flat that contains a given pair
x,y\in S
is
\mathrm{cl}(\{x,y\})
. Now consider two pairs of distinct elements
x,y\in S
and
z,w\in S
, and assume that
\mathrm{cl}(\{x,y\})\cap\mathrm{cl}(\{z,w\})\neq\emptyset
. By the modularity and simplicity of
M
, we get

\displaystyle r(\mathrm{cl}(\{x,y,z,w\}))+r(\mathrm{cl}(\{x,y\})\cap\mathrm{cl}(\{z,w\}))

\displaystyle=r(\mathrm{cl}(\{x,y\})\cup\mathrm{cl}(\{z,w\}))+r(\mathrm{cl}(\{x,y\})\cap\mathrm{cl}(\{z,w\}))

\displaystyle=r(\mathrm{cl}(\{x,y\})+r(\mathrm{cl}(\{z,w\})

\displaystyle=r(\mathrm{cl}(\{x,z\})+r(\mathrm{cl}(\{y,w\})

\displaystyle=r(\mathrm{cl}(\{x,z\})\cup\mathrm{cl}(\{y,w\}))+r(\mathrm{cl}(\{x,z\})\cap\mathrm{cl}(\{y,w\}))

\displaystyle=r(\mathrm{cl}(\{x,y,z,w\}))+r(\mathrm{cl}(\{x,z\})\cap\mathrm{cl}(\{y,w\})).

Therefore,
1\leq r(\mathrm{cl}(\{x,y\})\cap\mathrm{cl}(\{z,w\}))=r(\mathrm{cl}(\{x,z\})\cap\mathrm{cl}(\{y,w\}))
, and so
(P3)
is satisfied.

For any flat
F
and any
x,y\in F
, the closure
\mathrm{cl}(\{x,y\})
is also contained in
F
, so the flats of the matroid form subspaces of
\mathbb{P}
. The rank function of the matroid associated with the generalized projective space agrees with
r
on rank-1 and rank-2 flats and is modular, so it matches
r
on all flats. If there were a projective subspace that is not a flat, it would lie strictly between two flats whose ranks differ by one – that is, between two subspaces of consecutive ranks – which contradicts the definition of rank via maximal chains of subspaces.
∎

Recall that a matroid is called fully modular extendable if it is
k
-modular extendable for every
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
. Roughly speaking, this means that for every
k
, the matroid can be extended
k
times while satisfying the required rank conditions. However, note that the definition does not guarantee that the extensions for different values of
k
are compatible in the sense that any matroid obtained after
k
extensions can always be further extended by one more step. In principle, it could happen that for each finite
k
a different sequence of extensions is required. The main message of the following lemma is that there exists a universal sequence of extensions that works simultaneously for all
k
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
M=(S,r)
be a fully modular extendable matroid. Then, there exists a finitary matroid
M^{\prime}=(S^{\prime},r^{\prime})
with
S\subseteq S^{\prime}
such that
M
is the restriction of
M^{\prime}
to
S
and
M^{\prime}
is modular.

Proof.

Since
M
is
1
-modular extendable, for any pair
A,B\subseteq S
, there exists a set
Z
and a matroid
M^{\prime}=(S\cup Z,r^{\prime})
such that
r^{\prime}(A\cup Z)=r(A)
,
r^{\prime}(B\cup Z)=r(B)
, and
r^{\prime}(Z)=r(A)+r(B)-r(A\cup B)
. That is, there is an extension that is modular on the flats generated by
A
and
B
and whose rank equals the rank of the original matroid. Recall that we may assume that
|Z|\leq r(S)
. The basic idea is that, given there are only finitely many matroids on at most
|S|+r(S)
elements and
M
is fully modular extendable, at least one of these extensions is fully modular extendable as well, allowing us to continue the extension process. We formalize this as follows.

Let
S_{0}=S
, let
\mathcal{P}_{0}
be the list of all pairs of subsets of
S_{0}
, and set
\mathcal{M}_{0}={M}
. For
i\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, let
(A_{i},B_{i})
be the
i
-th pair in the list
\mathcal{P}_{i-1}
, and let
S_{i}\supseteq S_{i-1}
be a set such that
|S_{i}\setminus S_{i-1}|=r(S)
. Define
\mathcal{M}_{i}
to be the collection of all one-step extensions of matroids in
\mathcal{M}_{i-1}
on the ground set
S_{i}
with respect to the pair
(A_{i},B_{i})
. Set
\mathcal{P}_{i}
to be the list obtained from
\mathcal{P}_{i-1}
by adding all pairs in
2^{S_{i}}\times 2^{S_{i}}\setminus 2^{S_{i-1}}\times 2^{S_{i-1}}
to the end of the list
\mathcal{P}_{i-1}
.

Now let
\mathcal{P}=\bigcup_{i=0}^{\infty}\mathcal{P}_{i}
, where the union is taken so that each pair in
\mathcal{P}
retains its unique index received upon first appearing on a list. We define an infinite rooted tree
T
whose vertices are partitioned into levels, with the
i
-th level consisting of the matroids in
\mathcal{M}_{i}
. Two vertices
N_{1}\in\mathcal{M}_{i}
and
N_{2}\in\mathcal{M}_{i+1}
are connected by an edge if and only if
N_{2}
is a one-step extension of
N_{1}
. The tree
T
is locally finite, that is, all vertices of
T
have finite degree, as each level contains only finitely many matroids. So by Kőnig’s lemma
[
36
]
, there exists an infinite path in
T
starting at
M
. Let
M_{0}=M
together with
M_{j}=(S_{j},r_{j}),
where
j\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, be an infinite path in
T
, where
M_{j}\in\mathcal{M}_{j}
. Set
S^{\prime}=\bigcup_{i=0}^{\infty}S_{i}
and define
r^{\prime}(Y)=\sup\{r_{j}(X)\mid j\in\mathbb{Z}_{+},\,X\subseteq Y\cap S_{j}\}
for all
Y\subseteq S^{\prime}
. The rank of the ground set remains unchanged throughout the process, so
r^{\prime}(S^{\prime})=r(S)
. To prove that
r^{\prime}
is modular on flats, take any two flats and choose a pair of finite generating sets. This pair must occur in
\mathcal{P}
at some index
i
, so by the construction in step
i
, the rank function is already modular on the corresponding flats, and this property is maintained under further extensions.
∎

Finally, we will use the fact that, for modular extensions, it suffices to study the components of the matroid.

Lemma 3.3
.

For a nonnegative integer
k
, a matroid is
k
-modular extendable if and only if each of its components is
k
-modular extendable.

Proof.

It is clear that any restriction of a
k
-modular extendable matroid is
k
-modular extendable, so it suffices to show that the direct sum
M=(S,r)
of any two
k
-modular extendable matroids
M_{1}=(S_{1},r_{1})
and
M_{2}=(S_{2},r_{2})
is
k
-modular extendable. We proceed by induction on
k
. The case
k=0
is immediate; assume that
k\geq 1
. Let
A,B\subseteq S
and define
A_{i}\coloneqq A\cap S_{i}
and
B_{i}\coloneqq B\cap S_{i}
for
i\in\{1,2\}
. Since each
M_{i}
is
k
-modular extendable, there exists a
(k-1)
-modular extendable matroid
M^{\prime}_{i}=(S^{\prime}_{i},r^{\prime}_{i})
and a subset
Z_{i}\subseteq S^{\prime}_{i}
such that
S_{i}\subseteq S^{\prime}_{i}
,
M^{\prime}_{i}|S_{i}=M_{i}
,
r^{\prime}_{i}(A_{i}\cup Z_{i})=r_{i}(A_{i})
,
r^{\prime}_{i}(B_{i}\cup Z_{i})=r_{i}(B_{i})
, and
r^{\prime}_{i}(Z_{i})=r_{i}(A_{i})+r_{i}(B_{i})-r_{i}(A_{i}\cup B_{i})
. Then
M^{\prime}\coloneqq M^{\prime}_{1}\oplus M^{\prime}_{2}=(S^{\prime},r^{\prime})
is
(k-1)
-modular extendable by the induction hypothesis, and
M^{\prime}|S=M
. For the subset
Z\coloneqq Z_{1}\cup Z_{2}
, we have
r^{\prime}(A\cup Z)=r(A)
,
r^{\prime}(B\cup Z)=r(B)
, and
r^{\prime}(Z)=r(A)+r(B)-r(A\cup B)
. This shows that
M
is
k
-modular extendable.
∎

Now we are ready to prove the main result of the section, which establishes a connection between fully modular extendability and skew-representability.

Theorem 3.4
.

Given a matroid
M=(S,r)
, the following are equivalent.

(i)

M
is fully modular extendable.

(ii)

\si(M)\cong(M_{\mathbb{P}_{1}}\oplus\dots\oplus M_{\mathbb{P}_{q}})|S
for some projective spaces
\mathbb{P}_{1},\dots,\mathbb{P}_{q}
and finite subset
S
of the ground set of
M_{\mathbb{P}_{1}}\oplus\dots\oplus M_{\mathbb{P}_{q}}
.

(iii)

Each connected component of
M
is either of rank
3
or skew-representable.

Proof.

We prove the implications
\ref{it:i}\Rightarrow\ref{it:ii}\Rightarrow\ref{it:iii}\Rightarrow\ref{it:i}
.

\ref{it:i}\Rightarrow\ref{it:ii}
: The matroid
\si(M)
is also fully modular extendable. Hence, by
3.2
, there exists a finitary modular matroid
M^{\prime}=(S^{\prime},r^{\prime})
such that
\si(M)
is a restriction of
M^{\prime}
to a finite subset. By taking the simplification, we can assume that
M^{\prime}
is also simple. By Lemma
3.1
, the elements and rank-
2
flats of
M^{\prime}
form a generalized projective space
\mathbb{P}
, whose subspaces are precisely the flats of
M^{\prime}
. Proposition
2.6
then gives a decomposition
\mathbb{P}=\bigoplus_{j\in J}\mathbb{P}_{j}
, where each
\mathbb{P}_{j}
is a projective space. Since
\si(M)
is the restriction of
M^{\prime}
to a finite set
S
that intersects only finitely many of these projective spaces, the implication follows.

\ref{it:ii}\Rightarrow\ref{it:iii}
: Clearly, if each connected component of
\si(M)
is either of rank
3
or skew-representable, then the same holds for
M
as well. Therefore, the implication directly follows from the Veblen-Young theorem, Proposition
2.7
.

\ref{it:iii}\Rightarrow\ref{it:i}
: By Lemma
3.3
, it suffices to show that each component of
M
is fully modular extendable. For rank-
3
components, this holds by
[
1
, Proposition 3.18]
. Consider now a connected, skew-representable matroid
M
with a representation over
\mathbb{F}^{n}
. We construct a representation of
\si(M)
by modifying the given representation: remove all copies of the zero vector, and from each set of parallel vectors – that is, vectors lying in the same 1-dimensional subspace – retain only one representative. The resulting set contains no zero vector and includes at most one representative from each 1-dimensional subspace of
\mathbb{F}^{n}
. Therefore, the natural projection map from
\mathbb{F}^{n}\setminus{0}
to the projective space
\mathbb{F}\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
is injective on this set. Hence,
\si(M)
can be identified with a restriction of the finitary matroid represented by
\mathbb{F}\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
. By adding back one loop for each removed zero vector and parallel elements for each deleted nonzero vector to this matroid, we obtain a modular finitary matroid of which
M
is a restriction, proving that
M
is fully modular extendable.
∎

As a corollary, we get that for connected matroids of rank at least
4
, skew-representability is equivalent to fully modular extendability.

Corollary 3.5
.

Let
M
be a connected matroid of rank at least
4
. Then,
M
is skew-representable if and only if it is fully modular extendable.

Remark 3.6
.

Ingleton
[
29
]
observed that every simple rank-3 matroid can be embedded in some projective plane, and Welsh
[
67
]
asked whether this plane can always be taken to be finite; see also
[
52
, Problem15.9.2]
. In our setting, this question translates to asking whether the procedure described in the proof of Lemma
3.2
terminates after finitely many steps.

4
Properties of tensor products and modular extensions

The goal of this section is to establish several technical results concerning tensor products and modular extensions. While these lemmas support later arguments, they also capture structural properties that are of independent interest. For this reason, we present them together in a dedicated section, where they may also serve as a standalone toolkit for future work.

4.1
Tensor products

This section aims to analyze how the tensor product behaves under basic operations on matroid and polymatroid functions. Using
2.3
, we show that the existence of a tensor product is preserved under taking minors.

Lemma 4.1
.

If two matroids admit a tensor product, then every minor of one has a tensor product with every minor of the other.

Proof.

Let
M_{1}=(S_{1},r_{1})
and
M_{2}=(S_{2},r_{2})
be matroids and
M=(S_{1}\times S_{2},r)
be a tensor product of
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
. It suffices to show that every minor
M_{1}^{\prime}
of
M_{1}
has a tensor product with
M_{2}
; the statement then follows by repeating the proof with
M_{2}
and
M_{1}^{\prime}
. If
M_{1}^{\prime}
is a restriction of
M_{1}
, then the assertion is trivial. So it suffices to consider the case when
M^{\prime}_{1}=M_{1}/A
for some
A\subseteq S_{1}
.

Let
M^{\prime}=M/(A\times S_{2})
and let
r^{\prime}
denote the rank function of
M^{\prime}
. We claim that
M^{\prime}
is a tensor product of
M^{\prime}_{1}
and
M_{2}
. To see this, we need to verify that
r^{\prime}(X\times Y)=r^{\prime}_{1}(X)\cdot r_{2}(Y)
holds for all
X\subseteq S_{1}\setminus A
and
Y\subseteq S_{2}
, where
r^{\prime}_{1}
denotes the rank function of
M^{\prime}_{1}
. By
2.3
, when applied to
X_{1}=A
,
X_{2}=A\cup X
,
Y_{1}=S_{2}
and
Y_{2}=Y
, we get

\displaystyle r^{\prime}(X\times Y)

\displaystyle=r((X\times Y)\cup(A\times S_{2}))-r(A\times S_{2})

\displaystyle=r((A\cup X)\times Y)-r(A\times Y)

\displaystyle=r_{1}(A\cup X)\cdot r_{2}(Y)-r_{1}(A)\cdot r_{2}(Y)

\displaystyle=r^{\prime}_{1}(X)\cdot r_{2}(Y),

concluding the proof of the lemma.
∎

The next operation we consider is the direct sum of matroids.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
M_{1}^{1}=(S_{1}^{1},r_{1}^{1})
,
M_{1}^{2}=(S_{1}^{2},r_{1}^{2})
and
M_{2}=(S_{2},r_{2})
be matroids. Then,
M_{1}^{1}\oplus M_{1}^{2}
has a tensor product with
M_{2}
if and only if
M_{1}^{i}
has a tensor product with
M_{2}
for
i=1,2
.

Proof.

Assume first that
N=((S_{1}^{1}\cup S_{1}^{2})\times S_{2},r)
is a tensor product of
M_{1}^{1}\oplus M_{1}^{2}
and
M_{2}
. Then, for every
X\subseteq S_{1}^{i}
and
Y\subseteq S_{2}
, we have
r(X\times Y)=r_{1}^{i}(X)\cdot r_{2}(Y)
, hence
N|(S_{1}^{i}\times S_{2})
is a tensor product of
M_{1}^{i}
and
M_{2}
for
i=1,2
.

For the other direction, assume that
N_{i}
is a tensor product of
M_{1}^{i}
and
M_{2}
for
i=1,2
. Let
N=N_{1}\oplus N_{2}=((S_{1}^{1}\cup S_{1}^{2})\times S_{2},r)
denote their direct sum. Then, for every
X\subseteq S_{1}^{1}\cup S_{1}^{2}
and
Y\subseteq S_{2}
, we have
r(X\times Y)=r((X\cap S_{1}^{1})\times S_{2})+r((X\cap S_{1}^{2})\times S_{2})=r_{1}^{1}(X\cap S_{1}^{1})\cdot r_{2}(Y)+r_{1}^{2}(X\cap S_{1}^{2})\cdot r_{2}(Y)=(r_{1}^{1}(X\cap S_{1}^{1})+r_{1}^{2}(X\cap S_{1}^{2}))\cdot r_{2}(Y)
, hence
N
is a tensor product of
M_{1}^{1}\oplus M_{1}^{2}
and
M_{2}
.
∎

While the previous lemma focused on tensor products of non-connected matroids, the next addresses the connected case and shows that the tensor product of two connected matroids is connected.

Lemma 4.3
.

The tensor product of two connected matroids is connected.

Proof.

Let
M_{1}=(S_{1},r_{1})
and
M_{2}=(S_{2},r_{2})
be connected matroids and
M=(S_{1}\times S_{2},r)
be a tensor product of
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
. We need to show that for any
x_{1},y_{1}\in S_{1}
and
x_{2},y_{2}\in S_{2}
,
(x_{1},x_{2})
and
(y_{1},y_{2})
lie in the same connected component of the circuit hypergraph of
M
. Since
M_{1}
is connected and
M|S^{x_{2}}_{1}
is isomorphic to
M_{1}
, there exists a circuit
C_{1}
in
M
such that
(x_{1},x_{2}),(y_{1},x_{2})\in C_{1}
. Similarly, since
M_{2}
is connected and
M|^{y_{1}}S_{2}
is isomorphic to
M_{2}
, there exists a circuit
C_{2}
in
M
such that
(y_{1},x_{2}),(y_{1},y_{2})\in C_{2}
. This concludes the proof of the lemma.
∎

With respect to the existence of a tensor product, a simple reduction allows us to assume that all matroids are simple, that is, without loops or parallel elements.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
M_{1}=(S_{1},r_{1})
and
M_{2}=(S_{2},r_{2})
be matroids. For
i=1,2
, let
L_{i}
denote the set of loops of
M_{i}
and let
\mathcal{P}_{i}=\{P_{1}^{i},\dots,P_{q_{i}}^{i}\}
denote the partition of
S_{i}\setminus L_{i}
into parallel classes of
M_{i}
. For any matroid
N
on ground set
\mathcal{P}_{1}\times\mathcal{P}_{2}
, let
\widetilde{N}
denote the matroid on
S_{1}\times S_{2}
obtained from
N
by replacing each element
(P_{j}^{1},P_{k}^{2})\in\mathcal{P}_{1}\times\mathcal{P}_{2}
by a parallel class on
P^{1}_{j}\times P^{2}_{k}
, and adding the elements of
(S_{1}\times L_{2})\cup(L_{1}\times S_{2})
as loops. Then,

M_{1}\otimes M_{2}=\{\widetilde{N}\mid N\in\si(M_{1})\otimes\si(M_{2})\}.

In particular, two matroids admit a tensor product if and only if their simplifications do.

Proof.

Assume first that
P=(S_{1}\times S_{2},r)
is a tensor product of
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
. Then, for any pair
(e_{1},e_{2})\in S_{1}\times S_{2}
such that
e_{1}\in L_{1}
or
e_{2}\in L_{2}
,
r(\{(e_{1},e_{2})\})=r_{1}(\{e_{1}\})\cdot r_{2}(\{e_{2}\})=0
, thus
(e_{1},e_{2})
is a loop of
P
. Similarly, for
j\in[q_{1}]
and
k\in[q_{2}]
,
r(P_{j}^{1}\times P_{k}^{2})=r_{1}(P_{j}^{1})\cdot r_{2}(P_{k}^{2})=1
, thus
P_{j}^{1}\times P_{k}^{2}
is a parallel class of
P
as the direct product of a flat of
M_{1}
and a flat of
M_{2}
is a flat of
P
by
2.3
. These together with the fact that
P\in M_{1}\otimes M_{2}
imply that
N\coloneqq\si(P)\in\si(M_{1})\otimes\si(M_{2})
and
P=\widetilde{N}
.

To see the other direction, let
N\in\si(M_{1})\otimes\si(M_{2})
,
X_{1}\subseteq S_{1}
, and
X_{2}\subseteq S_{2}
. For
i\in\{1,2\}
, define
X^{\prime}_{i}\coloneqq\{P^{i}_{j}\in\mathcal{P}_{i}\mid P^{i}_{j}\cap X_{i}\neq\emptyset\}
. Then,
r_{\widetilde{N}}(X_{1}\times X_{2})=r_{N}(X^{\prime}_{1}\times X^{\prime}_{2})=r_{1}(X^{\prime}_{1})\cdot r_{2}(X^{\prime}_{2})=r_{1}(X_{1})\cdot r_{2}(X_{2})
, thus
\widetilde{N}
is a tensor product of
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
.
∎

Our next lemma shows that if
\varphi
is a tensor product of
\varphi_{1}
and
\varphi_{2}
, and
\varphi_{1}
is modular on some set, then the corresponding part of
\varphi
can be expressed as a direct sum.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
\varphi\colon 2^{S_{1}\times S_{2}}\to\mathbb{R}
be a tensor product of polymatroid functions
\varphi_{1}\colon 2^{S_{1}}\to\mathbb{R}
and
\varphi_{2}\colon 2^{S_{2}}\to\mathbb{R}
. If
\varphi_{1}(X)=\sum_{x\in X}\varphi_{1}(x)
for some
X\subseteq S_{1}
, then
\varphi(W)=\sum_{x\in X}\varphi(W\cap{\vphantom{S_{2}}}^{x}\!S_{2})
for all
W\subseteq X\times S_{2}
.

Proof.

Let
X=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\}
denote the elements of
X
, and for
i\in[k]
, define
X_{i}=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{i}\}
. For ease of discussion, we define
X_{0}=\emptyset
. Since
\varphi
is a polymatroid function, we have
\varphi(W)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}\varphi(W\cap{\vphantom{S_{2}}}^{x_{i}}\!S_{2})
. For the reverse inequality, observe that, since
\varphi
is the tensor product of
\varphi_{1}
and
\varphi_{2}
, we have
\varphi(X\times S_{2})=\varphi_{1}(X)\cdot\varphi_{2}(S_{2})=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\varphi_{1}(x_{i})\cdot\varphi({\vphantom{S_{2}}}^{x_{i}}\!S_{2})/\varphi_{1}(x_{i})=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\varphi({\vphantom{S_{2}}}^{x_{i}}\!S_{2})
. By submodularity, for each
i\in[k]
, we get

\varphi({\vphantom{S_{2}}}^{x_{i}}\!S_{2})\geq\varphi(W\cap{\vphantom{S_{2}}}^{x_{i}}\!S_{2})+\varphi(W\cup(X_{i}\times S_{2}))-\varphi(W\cup(X_{i-1}\times S_{2})).

Since
W\subseteq X\times S_{2}
, summing these inequalities over
i\in[k]
yields

\varphi(X\times S_{2})=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\varphi({\vphantom{S_{2}}}^{x_{i}}\!S_{2})\geq\sum_{i=1}^{k}\varphi(W\cap{\vphantom{S_{2}}}^{x_{i}}\!S_{2})+\varphi(X\times S_{2})-\varphi(W),

which concludes the proof of the lemma.
∎

In our proofs, we will frequently use the following statement, which roughly states that if we extend the ground set in one factor of the tensor product without increasing its rank in the given matroid, then the rank in the tensor product remains unchanged.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
\varphi\colon 2^{S_{1}\times S_{2}}\to\mathbb{R}
be a tensor product of polymatroid functions
\varphi_{1}\colon 2^{S_{1}}\to\mathbb{R}
and
\varphi_{2}\colon 2^{S_{2}}\to\mathbb{R}
.
Then, for all subsets
X,X^{\prime}\subseteq S_{1}
,
Y,Y^{\prime}\subseteq S_{2}
, and
Z\subseteq S_{1}\times S_{2}
,

\displaystyle\varphi(((X\cup X^{\prime})\times Y)\cup Z)

\displaystyle\leq\varphi((X\times Y)\cup Z)+(\varphi_{1}(X\cup X^{\prime})-\varphi_{1}(X))\cdot\varphi_{2}(Y),

\displaystyle\varphi((X\times(Y\cup Y^{\prime}))\cup Z)

\displaystyle\leq\varphi((X\times Y)\cup Z)+\varphi_{1}(X)\cdot(\varphi_{2}(Y\cup Y^{\prime})-\varphi_{2}(Y)).

In particular, if
\varphi_{1}(X\cup X^{\prime})=\varphi_{1}(X)
then

\varphi(((X\cup X^{\prime})\times Y)\cup Z)=\varphi((X\times Y)\cup Z),

and if
\varphi_{2}(Y\cup Y^{\prime})=\varphi_{2}(Y)
then

\varphi((X\times(Y\cup Y^{\prime}))\cup Z)=\varphi((X\times Y)\cup Z).

Proof.

We prove the first inequality; the second follows analogously. As
\varphi
is the tensor product of
\varphi_{1}
and
\varphi_{2}
, we have
\varphi((X\cup X^{\prime})\times Y)-\varphi(X\times Y)=(\varphi_{1}(X\cup X^{\prime})-\varphi_{1}(X))\cdot\varphi_{2}(Y)
. Hence, since
\varphi
is increasing and submodular, we get

\displaystyle\varphi(((X\cup X^{\prime})\times Y)\cup Z)

\displaystyle\leq\varphi((X\times Y)\cup Z)+\varphi((X\cup X^{\prime})\times Y)-\varphi((X\times Y)\cup((X^{\prime}\times Y)\cap Z))

\displaystyle\leq\varphi((X\times Y)\cup Z)+\varphi((X\cup X^{\prime})\times Y)-\varphi(X\times Y)

\displaystyle=\varphi((X\times Y)\cup Z)+(\varphi_{1}(X\cup X^{\prime})-\varphi_{1}(X))\cdot\varphi_{2}(Y),

proving the statement.
∎

We will need the following connection between the matroid and polymatroid cases. Recall that for an integer valued polymatroid function
\varphi
, we denote by
M_{\varphi}
the matroid provided by Proposition
2.1
.

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
\varphi\colon 2^{S_{1}\times S_{2}}\to\mathbb{Z}
be a tensor product of polymatroid functions
\varphi_{1}\colon 2^{S_{1}}\to\mathbb{Z}
and
\varphi_{2}\colon 2^{S_{2}}\to\mathbb{Z}
. Then,
M_{\varphi}=(S_{\varphi},r_{\varphi})
is a tensor product of
M_{\varphi_{1}}=(S_{\varphi_{1}},r_{\varphi_{1}})
and
M_{\varphi_{2}}=(S_{\varphi_{2}},r_{\varphi_{2}})
.

Proof.

Let
\theta_{1}\colon S_{\varphi_{1}}\to S_{1}
,
\theta_{2}\colon S_{\varphi_{2}}\to S_{2}
, and
\theta\colon S_{\varphi}\to S_{1}\times S_{2}
denote the mappings corresponding to
\varphi_{1}
,
\varphi_{2}
, and
\varphi
, respectively, as provided by Proposition
2.1
. Note that, by construction, the matroids thus obtained do not contain loops. Since
|\theta^{-1}((x,y))|=\varphi((x,y))=\varphi_{1}(x)\cdot\varphi_{2}(y)=|\theta_{1}^{-1}(x)|\cdot|\theta_{2}^{-1}(y)|
, the elements of the ground set
S_{\varphi}
can naturally be identified with the elements of
S_{\varphi_{1}}\times S_{\varphi_{2}}
, where the elements in
\theta^{-1}((x,y))
are in bijection with the elements in
\theta_{1}^{-1}(x)\times\theta_{2}^{-1}(y)
. Therefore, we may think of
M_{\varphi}
as a matroid over ground set
S_{\varphi_{1}}\times S_{\varphi_{2}}
, and assume that
\theta((x,y))=(\theta_{1}(x),\theta_{2}(y))
for all
x\in S_{\varphi_{1}}
,
y\in S_{\varphi_{2}}
.

To verify the statement, it suffices by Proposition
2.2
to show that the following equalities hold:
r_{\varphi}({\vphantom{Y_{2}}}^{e_{1}}\!Y_{2})=r_{\varphi_{1}}(e_{1})\cdot r_{\varphi_{2}}(Y_{2})
for each
e_{1}\in S_{\varphi_{1}}
and
Y_{2}\subseteq S_{\varphi_{2}}
;
r_{\varphi}(Y_{1}^{e_{2}})=r_{\varphi_{1}}(Y_{1})\cdot r_{\varphi_{2}}(e_{2})
for each
Y_{1}\subseteq S_{\varphi_{1}}
and
e_{2}\in S_{\varphi_{2}}
; and
r_{\varphi}(S_{\varphi_{1}}\times S_{\varphi_{2}})=r_{\varphi_{1}}(S_{\varphi_{1}})\cdot r_{\varphi_{2}}(S_{\varphi_{2}})
. Recall that
r_{\varphi}(Y_{1}^{e_{2}})=\min\{\varphi(\theta(W))+|Y_{1}^{e_{2}}\setminus W|\mid W\subseteq Y_{1}^{e_{2}}\}
and
r_{\varphi_{1}}(Y_{1})=\min\{\varphi_{1}(\theta_{1}(W_{1}))+|Y_{1}\setminus W_{1}|\mid W_{1}\subseteq Y_{1}\}
. Let
\pi\colon Y_{1}^{e_{2}}\to Y_{1}
be the natural projection, that is,
\pi((x,e_{2}))=x
for all
x\in Y_{1}
. Clearly,
\varphi(\theta(W))+|Y_{1}^{e_{2}}\setminus W|=\varphi_{1}(\theta_{1}(\pi(W)))+|Y_{1}\setminus\pi(W)|
, hence
r_{\varphi}(Y_{1}^{e_{2}})=r_{\varphi_{1}}(Y_{1})\cdot r_{\varphi_{2}}(e_{2})
. The equality for
e_{1}
and
Y_{2}
can be proved similarly. Finally, since
r_{\varphi_{1}}(S_{1})=\varphi(S_{1})
,
r_{\varphi_{2}}(S_{2})=\varphi(S_{2})
, and
r_{\varphi}(S)=\varphi(S)=\varphi_{1}(S_{1})\cdot\varphi_{2}(S_{2})=r_{\varphi_{1}}(S_{1})\cdot r_{\varphi_{2}}(S_{2})
, the lemma follows.
∎

4.2
Modular extensions

As one of our goals is to relate tensor products and modular extendability, it is important to understand how the latter behaves under certain operations. In particular, when extending results from matroids to polymatroid functions, two main difficulties arise. First, by Helgason’s result (
2.1
), every integer-valued polymatroid function can be expressed as a quotient of a matroid rank function, making it natural to ask how modular extendability behaves under this operation. It turns out that
k
-modular extendability is indeed preserved under taking quotients.

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
M=(S,r)
be a
k
-modular extendable matroid and
\mathcal{Q}=(S_{1},\dots,S_{q})
be a partition of
S
. Then, the polymatroid function
\varphi=r/\mathcal{Q}
is
k
-modular extendable.

Proof.

We prove the lemma by induction on
k
. For
k=0
, the result is trivial. Hence, assume that
k\geq 1
and that the statement holds for all smaller values of
k
. Let
S_{\mathcal{Q}}=\{s_{1},\dots,s_{q}\}
denote the ground set of
\varphi
, and let
\theta\colon S\to S_{\mathcal{Q}}
be the mapping that assigns each
s\in S
to
s_{i}
if
s\in S_{i}
. Let
A,B\subseteq S_{\mathcal{Q}}
. By the assumption,
M
has a one-step extension
M^{\prime}=(S^{\prime},r^{\prime})
with respect to
A^{\prime}=\theta^{-1}(A)
and
B^{\prime}=\theta^{-1}(B)
that is
(k-1)
-modular extendable. That is,
S\subseteq S^{\prime}
and there exists
Z^{\prime}\subseteq S^{\prime}
satisfying
S^{\prime}\setminus S\subseteq Z^{\prime}
,
r^{\prime}(X)=r(X)
for all
X\subseteq S
,
r^{\prime}(A^{\prime}\cup Z^{\prime})=r(A^{\prime})
,
r^{\prime}(B^{\prime}\cup Z^{\prime})=r(B^{\prime})
, and
r^{\prime}(Z^{\prime})=r(A^{\prime})+r(B^{\prime})-r(A^{\prime}\cup B^{\prime})
. We may assume that
Z^{\prime}=S^{\prime}\setminus S
, as otherwise we can add parallel copies of the elements in
Z\cap S
.

Let
S_{q+1}=Z^{\prime}
and
\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}=(S_{1},\dots,S_{q},S_{q+1})
. We claim that
\varphi^{\prime}=r^{\prime}/\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}
is a one-extension of
\varphi
with respect to
A
and
B
. To see this, let
Z=\{s_{q+1}\}
. Then
\varphi^{\prime}(X)=r^{\prime}(\theta^{-1}(X))=r(\theta^{-1}(X))=\varphi(X)
for
X\subseteq S
,
\varphi^{\prime}(A\cup Z)=r^{\prime}(A^{\prime}\cup Z^{\prime})=r(A^{\prime})=\varphi(A)
,
\varphi^{\prime}(B\cup Z)=r^{\prime}(B^{\prime}\cup Z^{\prime})=r(B^{\prime})=\varphi(B)
, and
\varphi^{\prime}(Z)=r^{\prime}(Z^{\prime})=r(A^{\prime})+r(B^{\prime})-r(A^{\prime}\cup B^{\prime})=\varphi(A)+\varphi(B)-\varphi(A\cup B)
. By induction,
\varphi^{\prime}
is
(k-1)
-modular extendable, concluding the proof of the lemma.
∎

Second, to extend the results to real-valued polymatroid functions, one needs to consider whether the pointwise limit of
k
-modular extendable functions preserves this property. Our next result answers this question in the affirmative.

Lemma 4.9
.

Let
(\varphi_{n}\colon n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+})
be a sequence of
k
-fractionally modular extendable polymatroid functions over the same ground set
S
, and let
\varphi
be a polymatroid function on
S
such that
\varphi_{n}(X)\to\varphi(X)
for all
X\subseteq S
. Then,
\varphi
is also
k
-fractionally modular extendable.

Proof.

We prove the lemma by induction on
k
. For
k=0
, the result is trivial. Assume now that
k\geq 1
and that the claim holds for all smaller values of
k
. For any pair
A,B\subseteq S
, each function
\varphi_{n}
in the sequence admits a one-step extension
\varphi_{n}^{\prime}
with respect to
A
and
B
that is
(k-1)
-fractionally modular extendable. Recall that for every such one-step extension
\varphi_{n}^{\prime}
, we may assume its ground set has size at most
|S|+1
, and the function values are uniformly bounded since the value on the entire ground set remains fixed at
\varphi(S)
. Therefore, from this sequence we can select a subsequence
(\varphi^{\prime}_{i_{n}}\colon n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+})
, all defined on the same ground set
S^{\prime}
, for which the values
\varphi^{\prime}_{i_{n}}(X)
converge for every subset
X\subseteq S^{\prime}
. Denote the pointwise limit by
\varphi^{\prime}
. By construction,
\varphi^{\prime}
is a polymatroid function. Moreover, the inductive hypothesis implies that
\varphi^{\prime}
is
(k-1)
-fractionally modular extendable.
Finally, because
\varphi^{\prime}
is the pointwise limit of one-step extensions with respect to
A
and
B
, it follows that
\varphi^{\prime}
is itself a one-step extension of
\varphi
with respect to
A
and
B
. This completes the proof.
∎

4.3
Skew-representability

If two matroids are representable over the same field
\mathbb{F}
, then they admit a tensor product representable over
\mathbb{F}
, which can be constructed via the Kronecker product of their representing matrices. Surprisingly, the analogous statement does not hold for skew-representable matroids; see Remark
4.16
for an example. Nevertheless, a similar result holds if one of the matroids is representable over the center of
\mathbb{F}
. This follows essentially from the fact that
r(A\otimes B)=r(A)\cdot r(B)
whenever the entries of
B
lie in the center of the skew field. To see this, we sketch one of the standard proofs of this result in the commutative setting. We omit the proof of the following result, as the argument given in, e.g.,
[
23
]
carries over to our setting without modification.

Lemma 4.10

(Mixed-product property)
.

Let
\mathbb{F}
be a skew field, and
A\in\mathbb{F}^{m\times n}
,
B\in\mathbb{F}^{p\times q}
,
C\in\mathbb{F}^{n\times k}
, and
D\in\mathbb{F}^{q\times r}
be matrices such that each entry of
B
commutes with each entry of
C
. Then,

(A\otimes B)(C\otimes D)=(AC)\otimes(BD).

The proof of the following lemma is essentially the same as in the commutative case. However, since we could not find a suitable reference and the case over skew fields can sometimes behave in surprising ways, we include a proof here as well.

Lemma 4.11
.

Let
\mathbb{F}
be a skew field and
A\in\mathbb{F}^{m\times n}
and
B\in Z(\mathbb{F})^{p\times q}
be matrices. Then,
r(A\otimes B)=r(A)\cdot r(B)
.

Proof.

Assume first that both
A
and
B
are invertible. Let
I_{\ell}
denote the
\ell\times\ell
identity matrix. Then,
4.10
implies that

I_{mp}=I_{m}\otimes I_{p}=(AA^{-1})\otimes(BB^{-1})=(A\otimes B)(A^{-1}\otimes B^{-1}),

showing that
A\otimes B
is invertible well.

We turn to the proof of the general case. Let
A^{\prime}
and
B^{\prime}
be invertible submatrices of
A
and
B
having size
r(A)\times r(A)
and
r(B)\times r(B)
, respectively. Then,
A^{\prime}\otimes B^{\prime}
is an invertible submatrix of
A\otimes B
having size
(r(A)\cdot r(B))\times(r(A)\cdot r(B))
, implying
r(A\otimes B)\geq r(A)\cdot r(B)
. To see the other direction, we may assume that the first
r(A)
columns of
A
form a basis of the right column space of
A
and the first
r(B)
columns of
B
form a basis of the column space of
B
. Consider the set of columns of
A\otimes B
with index set
H\coloneqq\{(i-1)\cdot q+j\mid i\in[r(A)],j\in[r(B)]\}
. As the entries of
A
and
B
commute and the first
r(B)
columns of
B
form a basis of the column space of
B
, for each
i\in[n]
columns
\{(i-1)\cdot q+j\mid j\in[r(B)]\}
span columns
\{(i-1)\cdot q+j\mid j\in[q]\}
in the column space of
A\otimes B
, thus columns corresponding to
H
span columns
\{(i-1)\cdot q+j\mid i\in[r(A)],j\in[q]\}
. Similarly, for each
j\in[q]
columns
\{(i-1)\cdot q+j\mid i\in[r(A)]\}
span columns
\{(i-1)\cdot q+j\mid i\in[n]\}
in the column space of
A\otimes B
, thus columns corresponding to
H
span all the columns. This shows that
r(A\otimes B)\leq|H|=r(A)\cdot r(B)
, finishing the proof.
∎

While two matroids that are representable over the same skew field
\mathbb{F}
do not necessarily have a tensor product representable over
\mathbb{F}
, the following lemma shows that a slightly weaker statement holds.

Lemma 4.12
.

Let
\mathbb{F}
be a skew field,
\varphi_{1}\colon S_{1}\to\mathbb{Z}
an
\mathbb{F}
-representable polymatroid function, and
\varphi_{2}\colon 2^{S_{2}}\to\mathbb{Z}
a
Z(\mathbb{F})
-representable polymatroid function. Then,
\varphi_{1}
and
\varphi_{2}
admit a tensor product
\varphi
which is representable over
\mathbb{F}
. Moreover, if
\varphi_{1}
and
\varphi_{2}
are matroid rank functions, then
\varphi
is the rank function of an
\mathbb{F}
-representable matroid.

Proof.

Let
A\in\mathbb{F}^{m\times n}
be a matrix and
\mathcal{P}=\{P_{s_{1}}\mid s_{1}\in S_{1}\}
a partition of
[n]
representing
\varphi_{1}
, and
B\in Z(\mathbb{F})^{p\times q}
a matrix and
\mathcal{Q}=\{Q_{s_{2}}\mid s_{2}\in S_{2}\}
a partition of
[q]
representing
\varphi_{2}
. Define
R_{(s_{1},s_{2})}\coloneqq\{(q-1)\cdot i+j\mid i\in P_{s_{1}},j\in Q_{s_{2}}\}
for
(s_{1},s_{2})\in S_{1}\times S_{2}
and observe that
\mathcal{R}=\{R_{(s_{1},s_{2})}\mid(s_{1},s_{2})\in S_{1}\times S_{2}\}
is a partition of
[nq]
. Consider the polymatroid function
\varphi\colon 2^{S_{1}\times S_{2}}\to\mathbb{Z}
represented by the matrix
A\otimes B\in\mathbb{F}^{mp\times nq}
and the partition
\mathcal{R}
. Then, for
X_{1}\subseteq S_{1}
and
X_{2}\subseteq S_{2}
,
\varphi(X_{1}\times X_{2})
equals the rank of the submatrix
A\otimes B
formed by the columns with indices from
\bigcup_{(s_{1},s_{2})\in S_{1}\times S_{2}}R_{(s_{1},s_{2})}
. This submatrix is the same as
A^{\prime}\otimes B^{\prime}
where
A^{\prime}
is the submatrix of
A
formed by its columns with indices
\bigcup_{s_{1}\in X_{1}}P_{s_{1}}
and
B^{\prime}
is the submatrix of
B
formed by its columns with indices
\bigcup_{s_{2}\in X_{2}}Q_{s_{2}}
. Applying
4.11
, we get
\varphi(X_{1}\times X_{2})=r(A^{\prime}\otimes B^{\prime})=r(A^{\prime})\cdot r(B^{\prime})=\varphi_{1}(X_{1})\cdot\varphi_{2}(X_{2})
, finishing the proof.
∎

Remark 4.13
.

We observe that the previous proof implies a stronger corollary for the product of multiple polymatroid functions using the associativity of Kronecker products. For a positive integer
k
and polymatroid functions
\varphi_{i}\colon 2^{S_{i}}\to\mathbb{R}
for
i\in[k]
, we define the
associative tensor product
of the
k
-tuple
(\varphi_{1},\dots,\varphi_{k})
recursively. For
k=1
, we define it to be
\varphi_{1}
, while for
k\geq 2
, we define it to be a polymatroid function
\varphi\colon 2^{S_{1}\times\dots\times S_{k}}\to\mathbb{R}
such that for each
i\in[k-1]
,
\varphi
is a tensor product of an associative tensor product of
(\varphi_{1},\dots\varphi_{i})
and an associative tensor product of
(\varphi_{i+1},\dots,\varphi_{k})
. In particular, if
k=2
, we get back the definition of
\varphi_{1}\otimes\varphi_{2}
, while for
k=3
we get a polymatroid function that is simultaneously a tensor product
(\varphi_{1}\otimes\varphi_{2})\otimes\varphi_{3}
and a tensor product
\varphi_{1}\otimes(\varphi_{2}\otimes\varphi_{3})
. If
\varphi_{1}\colon 2^{S_{1}}\to\mathbb{Z}
is
\mathbb{F}
-representable and
\varphi_{i}\colon 2^{S_{i}}\to\mathbb{Z}
is
Z(\mathbb{F})
-representable for
i\in\{2,\dots,k\}
, then it is not difficult to check that the proof of
4.12
implies the existence of an associative tensor product of
(\varphi_{1},\dots,\varphi_{k})
since taking the Kronecker product of matrices is an associative operation. We can also analogously define the associative tensor product of a
k
-tuple of matroids.

Finally, we give a sufficient condition for two matroids to have a tensor product that is representable over some skew field of a given characteristic.

Lemma 4.14
.

Let
\mathbb{F}
be a skew field of characteristic
p
,
\varphi_{1}\colon 2^{S_{1}}\to\mathbb{Z}
a polymatroid function representable over
\mathbb{F}
, and
\varphi_{2}\colon 2^{S_{2}}\to\mathbb{Z}
a polymatroid function representable over all infinite fields of characteristic
p
.
Then,
\varphi_{1}
and
\varphi_{2}
admit a tensor product
\varphi
which is representable over a skew field of characteristic
p
. Moreover, if
\varphi_{1}
and
\varphi_{2}
are matroid rank functions, then
\varphi
is a matroid rank function as well.

Proof.

Let
C
denote the center of
\mathbb{F}
.
Consider the rational function field
\mathbb{F}(x)
in a central indeterminate
x
(see
[
11
]
for definitions). Note that
\mathbb{F}(x)
has characteristic
p
and its center is
C(x)
, see
[
11
, Proposition 2.1.5]
for details. The polymatroid function
\varphi_{1}
is representable over
\mathbb{F}(x)
since
\mathbb{F}(x)
is an extension of
\mathbb{F}
, and
\varphi_{2}
is representable over
Z(\mathbb{F}(x))=C(x)
since
C(x)
is an infinite field of characteristic
p
. Therefore,
4.12
implies that
\varphi_{1}
and
\varphi_{2}
have a tensor product that is representable over
\mathbb{F}(x)
.
∎

Remark 4.15
.

4.14
implies that if
M
and
N
are regular matroids, then for each field
\mathbb{F}
they admit an
\mathbb{F}
-representable tensor product. However, these tensor products are not necessarily the same across fields:
M
and
N
do not necessarily admit a regular tensor product. In particular, it can be shown that any two binary matroids admit a unique binary tensor product, and the unique tensor product
(U_{2,3}\otimes U_{2,3})\otimes U_{2,3}
is representable over a field
\mathbb{F}
if and only if
\mathbb{F}
has characteristic 2. As we will see in
7.14
, the tensor product
U_{2,3}\otimes U_{2,3}
is unique and is the cographic matroid of
K_{3,3}
.

Remark 4.16
.

The non-Pappus matroid is a fundamental rank-3 simple matroid on 9 elements that violates Pappus’s Theorem in projective geometry and is therefore not representable over any field. However, it is skew-representable over the quaternions. Interestingly, we can show that the non-Pappus matroid admits a tensor product with itself, yet none of the matroids obtained in this way is representable over the quaternions – we omit the proof due to its length.

5
Skew-representability through tensor products

This section is devoted to one of the main results of the paper: a characterization of skew-representability in terms of tensor products. In
Section

5.1
, we establish a connection between a matroid being
1
-tensor-compatible with the uniform matroid
U_{2,3}
and being
1
-modular-extendable. In
Section

5.2
, we show that
k
-tensor-compatibility for every
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
implies fully modular extendability.
Section

5.3
presents the main result, providing a tensor product characterization of skew-representable connected matroids, as well as matroids representable over skew fields of fixed prime characteristic. As a consequence, we obtain that both skew-representability and representability over a skew field of fixed prime characteristic are co-recursively enumerable for connected matroids. Finally, in
Section

5.4
, we extend parts of this framework to polymatroid functions, relying on Helgason’s theorem and a linear program formulation for iterated tensor products.

5.1
Tensor with
U_{2,3}
and 1-modular extendability

The next theorem establishes a connection between a matroid having a tensor product with
U_{2,3}
and being
1
-modular extendable; see
[
53
]
for a different proof.

Theorem 5.1
.

Assume that
M=(S_{1},r_{1})
and
U_{2,3}=(S_{2},r_{2})
admit a tensor product
N=(S_{1}\times S_{2},r)
, and let
A,B\subseteq S_{1}
. Then,
N
has a minor
N^{\prime}
such that
N^{\prime}
is a one-step extension of
M
with respect to
A
and
B
.

Proof.

Let
S_{2}=\{u,v,w\}
denote the elements of
U_{2,3}
. We consider the following independent sets in
M
. Let
B_{0}
be a maximum independent set of
B
, and let
A_{0}
be an independent set of
A
such that
A_{0}\cup B_{0}
is a maximum independent set of
A\cup B
. Finally, let
C
be an independent set of
A
such that
A_{0}\cup C
is a maximum independent set of
A
. Consider the minor
N^{\prime}=(S_{1}^{u}\cup C^{v},r^{\prime})
of
N
obtained as
N^{\prime}=N/(A^{v}_{0}\cup B_{0}^{w})|(S_{1}^{u}\cup C^{v})
. Note that, by Lemma
4.5
and by the choice of
A_{0}
and
B_{0}
, we have
r(A_{0}^{v}\cup B_{0}^{w})=r(A_{0}^{v})+r(B_{0}^{w})=|A_{0}|+|B_{0}|=r(A^{x}\cup B^{x})
for all
x\in\{u,v,w\}
.

Claim 5.2
.

r^{\prime}(X)=r(X)
for all
X\subseteq S_{1}^{u}
.

Proof of claim..

By definition,
r^{\prime}(X)\leq r(X)
for all
X\subseteq S_{1}^{u}
. We first show that it suffices to verify the statement for
X=S_{1}^{u}
. Indeed, suppose that
X\subseteq S_{1}^{u}
and that
r^{\prime}(X)<r(X)
, that is,
r(X\cup A_{0}^{v}\cup B_{0}^{w})-r(A_{0}^{v}\cup B_{0}^{w})<r(X)
. Then, since
r
is submodular and increasing, we get

\displaystyle r^{\prime}(S_{1}^{u})

\displaystyle=r(S_{1}^{u}\cup A_{0}^{v}\cup B_{0}^{w})-r(A_{0}^{v}\cup B_{0}^{w})

\displaystyle\leq r(X\cup A_{0}^{v}\cup B_{0}^{w})+r(S_{1}^{u})-r(X)-r(A_{0}^{v}\cup B_{0}^{w})

\displaystyle=r(S_{1}^{u})+r^{\prime}(X)-r(X)

\displaystyle<r(S_{1}^{u}).

This would imply
r^{\prime}(S_{1}^{u})<r(S_{1}^{u})
as well. Now assume that
X=S_{1}^{u}
. Then, by Lemmas
4.5
and
4.6
, we get

\displaystyle r^{\prime}(S_{1}^{u})

\displaystyle=r(S_{1}^{u}\cup A_{0}^{v}\cup B_{0}^{w})-r(A_{0}^{v}\cup B_{0}^{w})

\displaystyle=r(S_{1}^{u}\cup A_{0}^{v}\cup A_{0}^{w}\cup B_{0}^{w})-r(A_{0}^{v}\cup B_{0}^{w})

\displaystyle=r(S_{1}^{u}\cup A_{0}^{w}\cup B_{0}^{w})-r(A_{0}^{v}\cup B_{0}^{w})

\displaystyle=r(S_{1}^{u})+r(A_{0}^{w}\cup B_{0}^{w})-r(A_{0}^{v}\cup B_{0}^{w})

\displaystyle=r(S_{1}^{u}),

concluding the proof of the claim.
∎

Finally, we verify that
r^{\prime}
assigns the correct values to the relevant sets.

Claim 5.3
.

r^{\prime}(A^{u}\cup C^{v})=r(A^{u})
,
r^{\prime}(B^{u}\cup C^{v})=r(B^{u})
and
r^{\prime}(C^{v})=r(A^{u})+r(B^{u})-r(A^{u}\cup B^{u})
.

Proof of claim..

By Lemmas
4.5
and
4.6
, together with the facts that
A_{0}\cup C
is a maximum independent set in
A
and
A_{0}\cup B_{0}
is a maximum independent set in
A\cup B
, we get

\displaystyle r^{\prime}(A^{u}\cup C^{v})

\displaystyle=r(A^{u}\cup C^{v}\cup A_{0}^{v}\cup B_{0}^{w})-r(A_{0}^{v}\cup B_{0}^{w})

\displaystyle=r(A^{u}\cup A^{v}\cup A^{w}\cup B_{0}^{w})-r(A_{0}^{v}\cup B_{0}^{w})

\displaystyle=r(A^{u}\cup A_{0}^{w}\cup B_{0}^{w})-r(A_{0}^{v}\cup B_{0}^{w})

\displaystyle=r(A^{u})+r(A_{0}^{w}\cup B_{0}^{w})-r(A_{0}^{v}\cup B_{0}^{w})

\displaystyle=r(A^{u}).

Similarly,

\displaystyle r^{\prime}(B^{u}\cup C^{v})

\displaystyle=r(B^{u}\cup C^{v}\cup A_{0}^{v}\cup B_{0}^{w})-r(A_{0}^{v}\cup B_{0}^{w})

\displaystyle=r(B^{u}\cup A^{v}\cup B_{0}^{v}\cup B_{0}^{w})-r(A_{0}^{v}\cup B_{0}^{w})

\displaystyle=r(B^{u}\cup A_{0}^{v}\cup B_{0}^{v})-r(A_{0}^{v}\cup B_{0}^{w})

\displaystyle=r(B^{u})+r(A_{0}^{v}\cup B_{0}^{v})-r(A_{0}^{v}\cup B_{0}^{w})

\displaystyle=r(B^{u}).

Finally, using that
B_{0}
is a maximum independent set of
B
as well, we get

\displaystyle r^{\prime}(C^{v})

\displaystyle=r(C^{v}\cup A_{0}^{v}\cup B_{0}^{w})-r(A_{0}^{v}\cup B_{0}^{w})

\displaystyle=r(A^{v}\cup B_{0}^{w})-r(A_{0}^{v}\cup B_{0}^{w})

\displaystyle=r(A^{v})+r(B_{0}^{w})-r(A_{0}^{v}\cup B_{0}^{w})

\displaystyle=r(A^{u})+r(B^{u})-r(A^{u}\cup B^{u}),

and the claim follows.
∎

By Claims
5.2
and
5.3
,
N^{\prime}
is a one-step extension of
M
with respect to
A
and
B
, concluding the proof of the theorem.
∎

5.3
Characterization of skew-representability

Using Corollaries
3.5
and
5.6
, we now give a characterization of representability over skew fields of certain characteristic. We will need the following simple observation.

Lemma 5.7
.

Any connected matroid
M=(S,r)
of rank at least
2
contains
U_{2,3}
as a minor.

Proof.

Let
x,y\in S
be such that
r(\{x,y\})=2
. Since the matroid is connected, there exists a circuit
C
such that
\{x,y\}\subseteq C
and
|C|=r(C)+1\geq r(\{x,y\})+1=3
. Any circuit of size at least
3
contains
U_{2,3}
as a minor, so the lemma follows.
∎

We are ready to state the main result of the section.

Theorem 5.8
.

Let
N
be a connected skew-representable matroid of rank at least two. Let
C
be the skew characteristic set of
N
and assume that for each
p\in C
,
N
is representable over all infinite fields of characteristic
p
. Then, for any matroid
M
,
M
is the direct sum of matroids each of which is representable over a skew field with characteristic in
C
if and only if
M
is
k
-tensor-compatible with
N
for each
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
.

Proof.

Assume first that
M=M_{1}\oplus\dots\oplus M_{q}
such that for each
i\in[q]
,
M_{i}
is representable over a skew field having characteristic
p_{i}\in C
. It follows from
4.14
by induction on
k
that for each
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
there exists a matroid
P_{k,i}\in T_{k}(M_{i},N)
which is representable over a skew field of characteristic
p_{i}
. It follows from
4.2
by induction on
k
that
P_{k,1}\oplus\dots\oplus P_{k,q}\in T_{k}(M,N)
for each
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
. This shows that
M
is
k
-tensor-compatible with
N
for each
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
.

To see the other direction, let
M_{0}
be a component of
M
. We need to show that
M_{0}
is representable over a skew field having characteristic in
C
. We may assume that
M_{0}
has rank at least two as rank one matroids are regular.
For each
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, there exists a matroid
P_{k}\in T_{k}(M_{0},N)
. By the definition of
T_{k}(M_{0},N)
, for each
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, there is a matroid
P^{\prime}_{k}
such that
P^{\prime}_{k}\in M_{0}\otimes N
and
P_{k}\in T_{k-1}(P^{\prime}_{k},N)
. As there are only finitely many matroids in
M_{0}\otimes N
, there exists a matroid
P^{\prime}\in M_{0}\otimes N
such that
P^{\prime}=P^{\prime}_{k}
for infinitely many values of
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
. In particular,
T_{k-1}(P^{\prime},N)\neq\emptyset
holds for infinitely many values of
k
. Since

implies

for each
\ell\geq 2
, it follows that
P^{\prime}
is
k
-tensor-compatible with
N
for each
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
. By Lemma
5.7
, each connected matroid of rank at least two contains
U_{2,3}
as a minor, thus
P^{\prime}
is also
k
-tensor-compatible with
U_{2,3}
for each
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
by
4.1
. Then,
5.6
implies that
P^{\prime}
is fully modular extendable.
As both
M_{0}
and
N
are connected and have rank at least 2,
P^{\prime}\in M_{0}\otimes N
is a connected matroid by
4.3
and it has rank at least 4. Therefore,
3.5
shows that
P^{\prime}
is representable over a skew field
\mathbb{F}
. As
P^{\prime}
contains
N
as a restriction, the characteristic of

must be in the skew characteristic set

of

. Since
M_{0}
is a restriction of

, we conclude that
M_{0}
is also representable over
\mathbb{F}
. This finishes the proof of the theorem.
∎

Choosing

5.4
Polymatroid functions and fractional modular extendability

We have seen that modular extendability is closely connected to tensor-compatibility with the uniform matroid
U_{2,3}
. This naturally raises the following question:
Do analogous statements hold for fractional modular extendability?
The aim of this section is to answer this in the affirmative. The key tool will be Helgason’s result (Proposition
2.1
), which allows us to reduce the problem to the matroid setting. However, to handle rational-valued functions, we must also use an argument that relies on the polyhedral description of tensor products.

Theorem 5.16
.

Let
\varphi\colon 2^{S}\to\mathbb{R}
be a polymatroid function which is
k
-tensor-compatible with the rank function of
U_{2,3}
for some
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
. Then,
\varphi
is
k
-fractionally modular extendable.

Proof.

Assume first that
\varphi
is an integer-valued polymatroid function. Consider the matroid
M_{\varphi}=(S_{\varphi},r_{\varphi})
and the mapping
\theta\colon S_{\varphi}\to S
provided by Proposition
2.1
. By Lemma
4.7
,
M_{\varphi}
is
k
-tensor-compatible with
U_{2,3}
, and therefore
k
-modular extendable by Theorem
5.5
. It then follows from Lemma
4.8
that
\varphi
is
k
-modular extendable as well. If
\varphi
is rational-valued, then let
q\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
be such that
\varphi^{\prime}=q\cdot\varphi
is integer-valued. By the previous reasoning,
\varphi^{\prime}
is
k
-modular extendable, and so
\varphi
is
k
-fractionally modular extendable.

Now consider the case when
\varphi
is a real-valued polymatroid function. The high-level idea of the proof is to construct a sequence
(\varphi_{n}\colon n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+})
of rational-valued,
k
-tensor-compatible polymatroid functions over
S
that converge to
\varphi
pointwise. By the previous case, each member of the sequence is
k
-fractionally modular extendable; hence, the
k
-fractional modular extendability of
\varphi
follows by Lemma
4.9
. To formalize the argument, we denote the ground set and the rank of
U_{2,3}
by
T
and
r
, respectively. Let
S_{0}=S
, and for
i\in[k]
, let
S_{i}=S_{i-1}\times T
. For every integer
0\leq i\leq k
, we assign a variable
x^{i}_{A}
to every set
A\subseteq S_{i}
. Given an
\varepsilon>0
, choose a
0<\varepsilon_{A}<\varepsilon
for each
A\subseteq S_{0}
such that
\varphi(A)+\varepsilon_{A}
is a rational number, and consider the following linear program.

\displaystyle x^{i}_{\emptyset}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{for }0\leq i\leq k,

(LP(
\varepsilon
))

\displaystyle x^{i}_{B}

\displaystyle\geq x^{i}_{A}

\displaystyle\text{for all }A\subsetneq B\subseteq S_{i},\ 0\leq i\leq k,

\displaystyle x^{i}_{A}+x^{i}_{B}

\displaystyle\geq x^{i}_{A\cap B}+x^{i}_{A\cup B}

\displaystyle\text{for all }A,B\subseteq S_{i},\ 0\leq i\leq k,

\displaystyle x^{i}_{A\times B}

\displaystyle=x^{i-1}_{A}\cdot r(B)

\displaystyle\text{for all }A\subseteq S_{i-1},\ B\subseteq T,\ 1\leq i\leq k,

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{A}

\displaystyle\geq|x^{0}_{A}-\varphi(A)|

\displaystyle\text{for all }A\subseteq S_{0}.

The first three constraints ensure that, for a solution,
x^{i}
corresponds to a polymatroid function over the ground set
S_{i}
; the fourth constraint ensures that
x^{i}
is a tensor product of
x^{i-1}
and
r
. Therefore, the
x^{0}
components define a set function that is
k
-tensor-compatible with
r
. In addition, the last constraint forces the values of
x^{0}
to be close to those of
\varphi
.

By the assumption that
\varphi
is
k
-tensor-compatible with
r
, the system
(LP(

ε

))
defines a nonempty bounded polyhedron; in particular, it has a solution for any
\varepsilon>0
. Let
\varphi_{n}
denote the set function corresponding to the
x^{0}
components of a vertex of this polyhedron for
\varepsilon=1/n
. Since
(LP(1/n))
is a rational polytope,
\varphi_{n}
is a rational-valued polymatroid function. By construction,
\varphi_{n}(A)\to\varphi(A)
for all
A\subseteq S
, hence the theorem follows by Lemma
4.9
.
∎

Similarly to the integer setting, we get the following as a corollary.

Corollary 5.17
.

Let
\varphi\colon 2^{S}\to\mathbb{R}
be a polymatroid function which is
k
-tensor-compatible with the rank function of
U_{2,3}
for each
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
. Then,
\varphi
is fully fractionally modular extendable.

Proof.

It suffices to show that
\varphi
is
k
-fractionally modular extendable for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, which follows by Theorem
5.16
.
∎

6
Uniform and rank-3 matroids

As we have seen in
Section

1.1
, skew-representability of rank-3 matroids is of particular interest.
5.9
shows that for any connected matroid that is not representable over any skew field, there exist some
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
such that
M
is not
k
-tensor-compatible with
U_{2,3}
. In this section we show that for matroids of rank 3, we always have to take
k\geq 2
even if
U_{2,3}
is replaced by any other uniform matroid, that is, any rank-3 matroid is 1-tensor-compatible with any uniform matroid. We also show that any rank-3 matroid
M
and any uniform matroid
U_{k,n}
admit a tensor product that is
freest
among all such tensor products: that is, there exists
P\in M\otimes U_{k,n}
such that any set that is independent in any other tensor product
P^{\prime}\in M\otimes U_{k,n}
is also independent in
P
. Equivalently,
P
is maximal under the weak order among all matroids in
M\otimes U_{k,n}
– a notion similar to the one appearing in Graver’s conjecture.

Theorem 6.1
.

Every rank-3 matroid has a freest tensor product with every uniform matroid.

Proof.

Consider the rank-
3
matroid
M
on ground set
S
with rank function
r
and closure operator
\mathrm{cl}
. By
4.4
we may assume that
M
is simple. Recall that a line in a matroid is a flat of rank
2
. Let
\mathcal{L}
denote the set of lines of
M
, let
\mathcal{P}\coloneqq\{\{L_{1},L_{2}\}\mid L_{1},L_{2}\in\mathcal{L},L_{1}\cap L_{2}=\emptyset\}
, and consider pairwise distinct new elements
e_{P}
for
P\in\mathcal{P}
which are also distinct from the elements of
S
. For a line
L\in\mathcal{L}
, define
{L}_{\bullet}\coloneqq L\cup\{e_{\{L,L^{\prime}\}}\mid L^{\prime}\in\mathcal{L},\{L,L^{\prime}\}\in\mathcal{P}\}
.
As
|{L}_{\bullet}|\geq 3
and
|{L}_{\bullet}\cap{L}_{\bullet}^{\prime}|=1
for any two
L,L^{\prime}\in\mathcal{L}
,
L\neq L^{\prime}
, by
[
52
, Proposition 1.5.6]
there is a simple rank-3 matroid
{M}_{\bullet}
on ground set
{S}_{\bullet}\coloneqq S\cup\{e_{P}\mid P\in\mathcal{P}\}
with family of non-independent lines
\{{L}_{\bullet}\mid L\in\mathcal{L}\}
.
Note that
{M}_{\bullet}|S=M
. Let
{r}_{\bullet}
denote the rank function and
{\mathrm{cl}}_{\bullet}
the closure operator of
{M}_{\bullet}
.

To motivate our construction of a tensor product of
M
with a uniform matroid
N
, first we show the following claim. As the proof of the claim does not use the uniformity assumption, we present it for a general matroid
N
.

Claim 6.2
.

Let
N
be a matroid on ground set
[n]
and
P\in M\otimes N
be any tensor product of
M
and
N
. Then, for any basis
A_{1}^{1}\cup\dots\cup A_{n}^{n}
of
P
, the following statements hold:

(a)

\sum_{i=1}^{n}|A_{i}|=3\cdot r_{N}([n])
and
A_{i}
is an independent set of
M
for each
i\in[n]
,

(b)

\bigcap_{i\in C}{\mathrm{cl}}_{\bullet}(A_{i})=\emptyset
for any circuit
C
of
N
,

(c)

\bigcup_{i\in C^{*}}A_{i}
is a generator of
M
for any cocircuit

of
N
.

Proof of claim..

It is clear that
(a)
holds.
Observe that
(b)
is equivalent to the conjunction of the conditions

\displaystyle\bigcap_{i\in C}\mathrm{cl}(A_{i})=\emptyset\text{ for each circuit $C$ of $N$},and

(1)

\displaystyle\{i\in[n]\mid L_{1}\subseteq\mathrm{cl}(A_{i})\text{ or }L_{2}\subseteq\mathrm{cl}(A_{i})\}\text{ is independent in $N$ for any disjoint lines $L_{1},L_{2}$ of $M$}.

(2)

Indeed, (
1
) is equivalent to that
\bigcap_{i\in C}{\mathrm{cl}}_{\bullet}(A_{i})\cap S=\emptyset
for each circuit
C
of
N
, while (
2
) is equivalent to
\bigcap_{i\in C}{\mathrm{cl}}_{\bullet}(A_{i})\setminus S=\emptyset
for each
C
circuit of
N
. To see the latter, observe that it is equivalent to the condition that for any two disjoint lines
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
of
M
,

is independent in

, and also observe that this set coincides with
\{i\in[n]\mid L_{1}\subseteq\mathrm{cl}(A_{i})\text{ or }L_{2}\subseteq\mathrm{cl}(A_{i})\}
.

To show (
1
), assume that there exists a circuit
C
of
N
and an element

.
For each
i\in[n]
, let
A^{\prime}_{i}
be an independent set of
M
which is maximal in

and contains

.
Since

, we have
|A^{\prime}_{i}|=|A_{i}|
and
A_{i}+e\subseteq\mathrm{cl}(A^{\prime}_{i})

A^{\prime}\coloneqq(A^{\prime}_{i_{0}}-e)^{i_{0}}\cup\bigcup_{i\in C\setminus\{i_{0}\}}(A^{\prime}_{i})^{i}

is a tensor product of

and

spans
(e,i_{0})

Hence

spans

as

spans

in
M
. This is a contradiction as
|A^{\prime}|<|A|
and
A
is a basis of
P
.

We next show that (
2
) is satisfied. Let
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
be two disjoint lines of
M
and let

for

. We have to show that

is independent in

.
Define
X_{1}\coloneqq(L_{1}\times(I_{1}\cup I_{2}))\cup(S\times I_{2})

.
We will use the submodular inequality
r_{P}(X_{1}\cap X_{2})+r_{P}(X_{1}\cup X_{2})\leq r_{P}(X_{1})+r_{P}(X_{2})
.
We have

\displaystyle r_{P}(X_{1})

\displaystyle=r_{P}((L_{1}\times(I_{1}\cup I_{2}))\cup(S\times I_{2}))

\displaystyle\leq r_{P}(L_{1}\times(I_{1}\cup I_{2}))+r_{P}(S\times I_{2})-r_{P}(L_{1}\times I_{2}))

\displaystyle=2r_{N}(I_{1}\cup I_{2})+3r_{N}(I_{2})-2r_{N}(I_{2})

\displaystyle=2r_{N}(I_{1}\cup I_{2})+r_{N}(I_{2}),

and similarly
r_{P}(X_{2})\leq 2r_{N}(I_{1}\cup I_{2})+r_{N}(I_{1})
. Since
X_{1}\cup X_{2}\supseteq(L_{1}\cup L_{2})\times(I_{1}\cup I_{2})
and
L_{1}\cup L_{2}
is a generator in

,
r_{N}(X_{1}\cup X_{2})=3r_{N}(I_{1}\cup I_{2})
. Therefore,

\displaystyle r_{P}(X_{1}\cap X_{2})

\displaystyle\leq r_{P}(X_{1})+r_{P}(X_{2})-r_{P}(X_{1}\cup X_{2})

\displaystyle\leq(2r_{N}(I_{1}\cup I_{2})+r_{N}(I_{2}))+(2r_{N}(I_{1}\cup I_{2})+r_{N}(I_{1}))-3r_{N}(I_{1}\cup I_{2})

\displaystyle=r_{N}(I_{1})+r_{N}(I_{2})+r_{N}(I_{1}\cup I_{2}).

On the other hand, if
i\in I_{1}\cap I_{2}
, then

, thus
|A_{i}|=3
. If

then

and
|A_{1}|=2
. Similarly, if

and
|A_{2}|=2
. As

, these imply that

|X_{1}\cap X_{2}\cap A|=3\cdot|I_{1}\cap I_{2}|+2\cdot|I_{1}\setminus I_{2}|+2\cdot|I_{2}\setminus I_{1}|=|I_{1}|+|I_{2}|+|I_{1}\cup I_{2}|.

As
A
is a basis of
P
, we obtain

|I_{1}|+|I_{2}|+|I_{1}\cup I_{2}|=|X_{1}\cap X_{2}\cap A|\leq r_{P}(X_{1}\cap X_{2})\leq r_{N}(I_{1})+r_{N}(I_{2})+r_{N}(I_{1}\cup I_{2}),

thus
I_{1}
,
I_{2}
, and
I_{1}\cup I_{2}
are independent in

. This shows (
2
).

Finally, assume that there exists a cocircuit

of
N
such that
\bigcup_{i\in J}A_{i}
is not a generator of
M
, that is,
\bigcup_{i\in C^{*}}A_{i}\subseteq L
for a line

of
M
. This implies that

. Using the submodularity of

,

\displaystyle r_{N}((S\times([n]\setminus C^{*}))\cup(L\times[n]))

\displaystyle\leq r_{N}(S\times([n]\setminus C^{*}))+r_{N}(L\times[n])-r_{N}(L\times([n]\setminus C^{*}))

\displaystyle=3\cdot(r_{N}([n])-1)+2\cdot r_{N}([n])-2\cdot(r_{N}([n])-1)

\displaystyle=3r_{N}([n])-1,

contradicting
A
being a basis of
N
. This finishes the proof of the claim.
∎

Consider a uniform matroid

on ground set

as the family of subsets of

satisfying

. The following easy observation will be used later.

The statement follows by observing that each

-element subset

intersects

and each

-element subset

intersects

.
∎

Next we show two technical claims that will be used in proving that

.

for

otherwise. Since

.
For an index

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}|A^{\prime}_{i}|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i\in J_{1}\triangle J_{2}}(s_{i}+1)+\sum_{i\in J_{1}\cap J_{2}}s_{i}+\sum_{i\in[n]\setminus(J_{1}\cup J_{2})}(s_{i}+2)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}s_{i}+2n-|J_{1}|-|J_{2}|

\displaystyle=3\cdot k-2.

.

Claim 6.5
.

,

7
Rank inequalities

In this section, we focus on linear rank inequalities and introduce a new method for deriving them. As an illustration, we first present a simpler proof of Ingleton’s inequality in
Section

7.1
. Building on this approach,
Section

7.2
derives two inequalities for polymatroid functions that admit tensor products with the rank functions of the Fano and non-Fano matroids, respectively, leading to characteristic-dependent linear rank inequalities for polymatroid functions that are skew-representable over skew fields of characteristic 2 or different from 2. The main result of the section appears in
Section

7.3
, where we derive a characteristic-independent linear rank inequality that does not follow from the common information property – to the best of our knowledge, the first such inequality.

7.1
Ingleton’s inequality

Ingleton
[
29
]
observed that the rank function of any skew-representable polymatroid function
\varphi\colon 2^{S}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfies the following inequality:

\displaystyle\varphi(A\cup B)+\varphi(A\cup C)+\varphi(A\cup D)+\varphi(B\cup C)+r(B\cup D)\geq

(Ing)

\displaystyle\varphi(A)+\varphi(B)+\varphi(A\cup B\cup C)+\varphi(A\cup B\cup D)+\varphi(C\cup D)

The goal of this section is to present a new proof of the inequality using tensor products. While several proofs exist – including two that rely on tensor product techniques
[
53

4
]
– our argument is more intuitive and offers a framework that leads to new linear rank inequalities. Our proof relies on a similar argument to that of Las Vergnas
[
38

, who showed that the Vámos matroid
V_{8}
and the uniform matroid

do not admit a tensor product. Since any skew-representable matroid admits a matroid tensor product with the uniform matroid
U_{2,3}
, it suffices to verify the following, more general statement that was first shown in
[
4
]
. The reason for including the proof is twofold: it is significantly simpler than the one given there, and the idea will serve as a starting point for deriving new linear rank inequalities in Sections
7.2
and
7.3
.

7.3
Beyond known inequalities

As we have seen earlier, rank-3 matroids are particularly interesting from the viewpoint of skew-representability, since they all satisfy the common information property – which therefore cannot be used to determine whether such a matroid is skew-representable or not. This motivates the study of one of the classical rank-3 matroids: the non-Desargues matroid. The
non-Desargues matroid
is a simple rank-3 matroid on 10 elements that arises from the classical Desargues configuration in projective geometry by omitting one line. We denote the ground set of the matroid by
\{d,a_{1},a_{2},a_{3},b_{1},b_{2},b_{3},c_{1},c_{2},c_{3}\}
with set of lines

\big\{\{d,a_{1},b_{1}\},\{d,a_{2},b_{2}\},\{d,a_{3},b_{3}\},\{a_{1},a_{2},c_{3}\},\{a_{1},a_{3},c_{2}\},\{a_{2},a_{3},c_{1}\},\{b_{1},b_{2},c_{3}\},\{b_{1},b_{3},c_{2}\},\{b_{2},b_{3},c_{1}\}\big\},

see also Figure
4
.

The non-Desargues matroid is not skew-representable, so by
5.9
, there exists
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
such that it is not
k
-tensor-compatible with
U_{2,3}
. By
6.1
, this value of
k
must be at least 2. We show that the non-Desargues matroid is not 2-tensor-compatible with
U_{2,3}
; more strongly, it does not even admit a tensor product with the graphic matroid of the complete graph on four vertices. We will see in
7.16
that this is a stronger statement, as the existence of the latter tensor product would imply the former. Using the framework developed in Sections
7.1
and

Since the non-Desargues matroid satisfies the common information property, this is, to the best of our knowledge, the first characteristic-independent inequality that does not follow from that property.

8
Discussion and open problems

In this paper, we introduced a tensor product framework to study skew-representability of matroids and polymatroid functions. In particular, we provided a characterization of skew-representable matroids, as well as of those representable over skew fields of a given prime characteristic, in terms of tensor products, yielding co-recursively enumerable certificates for these problems. We gave a construction for the freest tensor product of a rank-3 and a uniform matroid. As an application of our framework, we derived new linear rank inequalities, including the first known inequality for folded skew-representable matroids that goes beyond the common information property. We close the paper by mentioning some open problems.

1.

Theorem
5.1
shows that if a matroid is 1-tensor-compatible with
U_{2,3}
, then it is also 1-modular extendable. It would be interesting to see whether the converse holds: Does
1
-modular extendability imply
1
-tensor-compatibility with
U_{2,3}
?

2.

A key difference between the tensor product defined for matroids and for polymatroid functions is that, in the latter case, the product function may take fractional values, whereas in the former, we want the result to be a matroid. This naturally raises the question of whether, for matroids, the two interpretations differ in terms of existence: Is there a pair of matroids that does not admit a matroid tensor product, but whose rank functions, viewed as polymatroid functions, do admit a tensor product?

3.

5.9
implies that a connected matroid is skew-representable if it is
k
-tensor-compatible with
U_{2,3}
for every
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
. It remains open whether this result can be extended to the folded skew-representable case: Is every connected matroid whose rank function is
k
-tensor-compatible with the rank function of
U_{2,3}
for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, in the polymatroid sense, folded skew-representable?

4.

The proofs of Theorems
7.1
,
7.5
and
7.9
relied on a similar idea: generating certain sets in a specific order. However, there is a considerable freedom in the choosing this order, and repeating the same argument with a different choice also lead to linear rank inequalities. This raises a natural question: Do all inequalities obtained in this way are essentially the same, or can different orders lead to genuinely new inequalities?

5.

Theorem
6.1
shows that every rank-3 matroid admits a tensor product with any uniform matroid. In contrast, Theorem
7.9
implies that not every rank-3 matroid admits a tensor product with
M(K_{4})
. This matroid belongs to the class of sparse paving matroids, each of which can be constructed by removing some bases from a rank-
r
uniform matroid such that the intersection of any two removed bases has size at most
r-2
. This raises the natural question of whether
M(K_{4})
is, in some sense, minimal with respect to the nonexistence of tensor products with rank-3 matroids: Is there a rank-3 sparse paving matroid on six elements that is freer than
M(K_{4})
but still fails to admit a tensor product with every rank-3 matroid?
