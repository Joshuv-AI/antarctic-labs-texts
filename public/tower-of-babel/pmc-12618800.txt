Fc-Modified IVIG with Enhanced Blood–Brain Barrier Penetration Ameliorates Cognitive Deficits and Neuropathology in A53T α-Synuclein Transgenic Mice
Authors: Jie Zhu, Ya-ru Huang, Mei-qi Wang, Shi-yu Liang, Xiao-lin Yu, Gui-feng Zhang, Rui-tian Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractParkinson’s disease (PD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder marked by degeneration of nigrostriatal dopaminergic neurons together with α-synuclein (α-syn) aggregation, neuroinflammation, and oxidative stress. Although intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) contains naturally occurring antibodies against α-syn oligomers, it has failed to demonstrate therapeutic efficacy in PD models, likely due to limited delivery across the blood–brain barrier (BBB). Enhancing BBB penetration of IVIG could therefore substantially improve its therapeutic potential in PD. Here, we developed a brain-targeted IVIG formulation (IVIGᴬ) by site-specifically conjugating Angiopep-2 (Ang-2) to the Fc glycan site (Asn297) of IVIG, enabling receptor-mediated transcytosis across the BBB via low-density lipoprotein receptor-related protein-1 (LRP1). Compared with unmodified IVIG, IVIGᴬ exhibited significantly enhanced brain accumulation. Moreover, systemic administration of IVIGᴬ markedly improved motor and cognitive performance in A53T α-syn transgenic mice by reducing phosphorylated α-syn aggregates, preserving dopaminergic neurons and synaptic integrity, and attenuating neuroinflammation, oxidative stress, and complement activation. Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder characterized by the degeneration of nigrostriatal dopaminergic neurons and intraneuronal α-synuclein (α-syn) aggregates (Lewy pathology) (Calabresi et al. 2023). According to the World Health Organization (WHO), > 8.5 million people were living with Parkinson’s disease in 2019, with ~ 5.8 million disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) and ~ 329,000 deaths; prevalence has roughly doubled over the past 25 years (WHO, 2022; WHO, 2023). A central obstacle to disease-modifying therapy is delivery across the blood–brain barrier (BBB), which severely limits brain exposure to large biologics such as intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG).

Despite substantial research efforts, no disease-modifying therapies have been approved to date. A major hurdle lies in the effective delivery of therapeutics to the central nervous system (CNS) due to the restrictive nature of the BBB. Approximately 98% of small-molecule drugs and virtually all large-molecule biologics fail to penetrate the BBB at therapeutically relevant concentrations, which may partly explain the lack of clinical success of several anti-α-syn monoclonal antibodies, including prasinezumab and cinpanemab (Geerts et al. 2023). Therefore, overcoming the obstacle posed by the BBB remains a fundamental challenge in the development of effective biologic therapies for PD. IVIG is a therapeutic polyclonal antibody preparation with broad immunomodulatory activity and a long history of clinical use in immune-mediated neurological disorders (Bayry et al. 2023; Shock et al. 2020). IVIG contains antibodies capable of binding both amyloid-β and α-syn aggregates, suggesting potential therapeutic relevance for Alzheimer’s disease (AD) and PD (Huang et al. 2019; Shock et al. 2020; Wang et al. 2016). However, phase III clinical trials of IVIG in AD did not show cognitive or functional benefit compared to placebo, and IVIG did not demonstrate consistent neuroprotective effects on preclinical PD models (Rabenstein et al. 2019; Li et al. 2023). One of the primary barriers to CNS efficacy is IVIG’s poor BBB permeability. IgG (~ 150 kDa) crosses the intact BBB at extremely low levels (< 0.1% of plasma concentrations), resulting in predominantly peripheral activity and negligible brain exposure (Pardridge et al. 2023; St-Amour et al. 2013). Consequently, strategies that enhance IVIG brain delivery are urgently needed to realize the therapeutic potential in neurodegenerative diseases.

Angiopep-2 (Ang-2) is a 19-amino-acid peptide derived from the Kunitz domain of aprotinin that has been developed as a BBB-shuttling ligand to facilitate drug delivery to the brain (di Polidoro et al. 2022). Ang-2 targets low-density lipoprotein receptor-related protein-1 (LRP1), which is abundantly expressed on brain microvascular endothelial cells, enabling receptor-mediated transcytosis across the BBB (di Polidoro et al. 2022). Prior studies have validated Ang-2 as an effective molecular “Trojan horse” to transport macromolecules, including biologics, into the CNS via LRP1-mediated mechanisms (Georgieva et al. 2014; Habib et al. 2022). In this study, we used the A53T α-syn transgenic mouse model of synucleinopathy, which recapitulates intraneuronal α-syn inclusions with Lewy-like pathology, sensorimotor and motor deficits, and nigrostriatal dysfunction—providing a relevant platform to evaluate target engagement and disease-modifying potential (Giasson et al. 2002; Martin et al. 2006). We generated a brain-targeted IVIG derivative (IVIGᴬ) by site-specifically conjugating Ang-2 to the Fc glycan residue (Asn297) of native IVIG, and systematically evaluated the brain uptake and therapeutic efficacy of IVIGᴬ in A53T α-syn transgenic mice.

Materials and Methods

IVIG Modification

Commercial IVIG (5% solution) was purchased from Shandong Taibang Biological Products Co., Ltd., and subjected to site-specific conjugation with Ang-2 to enable brain-targeted delivery via LRP1-mediated transcytosis (Yang et al. 2023). As previously described (Kline et al. 2015), the Fc glycan residues (Asn297) of IVIG were selectively oxidized using 10 mM sodium periodate (Sigma, Cat# 311448) at 4 °C for 1 h in the dark. The oxidized IVIG was purified by Sephadex G-25 gel filtration and subsequently reacted with hydrazide-functionalized Ang-2 peptides (GL Biochem; sequence: Hydrazide–GGS–TFFYGGSRGKRNNFKTEEY–GGS–HHHHHH) in sodium acetate buffer (pH 5.5) at a molar ratio of 1:8 for 24 h at 4 °C. To stabilize the hydrazone linkage, sodium cyanoborohydride (NaBH₃CN, 10 mM) was added and incubated at 4 °C for an additional 2 h. Following conjugation, the reaction mixture was dialyzed using a 50 kDa molecular weight cutoff membrane to remove unreacted peptides. His-tagged conjugates were subsequently purified by Ni–NTA affinity chromatography to selectively eliminate unconjugated IVIG. The resulting Ang-2-modified IVIG (referred to hereafter as IVIGᴬ) was used in all subsequent experiments. Detailed characterization of IVIGᴬ, including its LRP1-binding affinity, BBB penetration efficiency, and preserved immunological activity, is presented in the following sections.

Dot Blot Analysis

To assess the binding affinity of IVIG to LRP1, unmodified IVIG and IVIGᴬ (1 μg each) were dotted onto dry nitrocellulose (NC) membranes and air-dried at room temperature for 30 min. The membranes were then blocked with 5% non-fat milk at room temperature for 1 h and divided into two groups. One group was incubated with diluted LRP1 Cluster II protein (MedChemExpress, Cat# HY-P702520, 1 μg/mL), followed by goat anti-human LRP1 Cluster II antibody (Novus, Cat# AF2368-SP, 1:1,000), and then HRP-conjugated donkey anti-goat IgG antibody (Abcam, Cat# ab97120, RRID: AB_10679892, 1:5,000). The other group was directly incubated with HRP-conjugated rabbit anti-human IgG Fc antibody (Abcam, Cat# ab125909, 1:10,000) as a loading control. After washing, signals were developed using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and visualized with the Amersham Imager 680 system (GE Healthcare). All images were acquired under identical exposure settings, and no selective or non-uniform adjustments were applied. Only uniform linear adjustments of brightness and contrast were made equally across the entire blot for presentation. Cropped images were prepared with sufficient space above and below the bands, and the uncropped original blots with full protein ladders are provided in the Supplemental Materials.

C1q Binding Activity

The C1q binding activity of IVIGA and unmodified IVIG was evaluated using an indirect ELISA assay (Emin et al. 2022). Ninety-six-well plates (Corning Costar) were pre-coated with 2 μg/well of C1q protein (Abnova, Cat# ABN-P8124) in carbonate-bicarbonate buffer (pH 9.6) overnight at 4 °C. After washing with PBS containing 0.05% Tween-20 (PBST), plates were blocked with 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in PBST for 1 h at 37 °C. IVIGA or IVIG was diluted in PBST containing 1% BSA to prepare a concentration gradient ranging from 0.75 μg/mL to 200 μg/mL, and 100 μL of each dilution was added to the wells and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. After washing, bound IgG was detected using horseradish peroxidase HRP-conjugated rabbit anti-human IgG secondary antibody (Abcam, Cat# ab6759, RRID: AB_955434, 1:10000), followed by incubation with TMB substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for color development. The reaction was terminated with 1 M H₂SO₄, and absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a SpectraMax iD5 microplate reader (Molecular Devices).

In vitro Uptake Assay in bEnd.3 Cells

Mouse brain endothelial bEnd.3 cells were purchased from Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China; Cat# CL-0598). The supplier provided short tandem repeat (STR) profiling authentication data and corresponding spectra to verify the identity of the cell line. All experiments were performed using cells within 15 passages after thawing. Routine PCR-based assays were conducted to confirm that the cells were free of mycoplasma contamination, and only negative cultures were used for subsequent studies.

Cells were maintained in high-glucose Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, Gibco) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (100 U/mL penicillin, 100 μg/mL streptomycin). They were cultured at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂. The medium was replaced every 2–3 days, and cells were passaged at 70–80% confluence using a 0.25% trypsin–EDTA solution (Gibco). For uptake experiments, cells were seeded in 24-well plates containing sterilized glass coverslips at a density of 5 × 104 cells/well and allowed to adhere overnight before treatment.

The following day, cells were incubated with IVIGA or unmodified IVIG at a final concentration of 500 μg/mL (diluted in serum-free DMEM) for 4 h at 37 °C. After incubation, cells were washed three times with PBS, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, and blocked with PBS containing 5% BSA and 0.1% Triton X-100. Cells were then incubated with Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated anti-human IgG secondary antibody (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat#A48276, RRID: AB_2896339, 1:200) for 1 h at room temperature to detect internalized IVIG. To visualize cell membranes, cells were counterstained with the lipophilic dye DiI (Solarbio, Cat#D8700). Coverslips were mounted with anti-fade mounting medium and imaged using a Leica SP8 confocal laser scanning microscope equipped with LAS X software. All groups were acquired under identical imaging conditions, with exposure times and detector gain settings kept constant. Intracellular human IgG (hIgG) fluorescence signals were quantified using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health) by an investigator blinded to the experimental groups. Uniform linear adjustments of brightness and contrast were applied equally to the entire image, and no selective or non-uniform modifications were performed.

In vivo Biodistribution and Brain Uptake

Based on a modified protocol previously reported for assessing brain uptake of IVIG (St-Amour et al. 2013), C57BL/6 male mice (6–8 weeks old) were randomly assigned to six time-point cohorts (0, 1, 3, 6, 12, and 24 h; n = 6 per cohort). Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane and intravenously injected via the tail vein with 25 mg of Cy5-labeled IVIGᴬ or unmodified IVIG. Fluorescent labeling was performed using Cy5 NHS ester (Solarbio, Cat# C0460) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. At 0 (immediately after injection), 1, 3, 6, 12, and 24 h post-injection, mice were re-anesthetized and perfused transcardially with PBS. Whole brains were collected and subjected to ex vivo fluorescence imaging using the IVIS Spectrum imaging system (PerkinElmer). Fluorescence intensity was quantified as radiant efficiency using Living Image software (PerkinElmer).

At the 3-h time point, brains were collected for ex vivo imaging and cryosectioning. Tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde at 4 °C overnight, then cryoprotected in 30% sucrose. Samples were embedded in OCT compound and sectioned coronally at 20 μm thickness using a cryostat for immunofluorescence staining. To assess the brain distribution of hIgG, sections were blocked with PBS containing 5% BSA and 0.1% Triton X-100, followed by co-incubation with Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated anti-human IgG secondary antibody (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat#A48276, RRID: AB_2896339, 1:200) and the rabbit endothelial marker CD31 (Abcam, Cat#ab28364, RRID: AB_726362, 1:100). After washing, appropriate fluorescently labeled secondary antibodies (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat#A-31573, RRID: AB_2536183, 1:500) were applied, and nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Images were acquired using a Leica SP8 confocal laser scanning microscope equipped with LAS X software. Excitation was provided by appropriate laser lines, and all groups were imaged under identical acquisition parameters, with exposure times and detector gain settings kept constant. Fluorescence intensity of hIgG in the vicinity of CD31⁺ blood vessels was quantified using ImageJ software by an investigator blinded to experimental groups. All images were processed uniformly, and only linear adjustments of brightness and contrast were applied equally to the entire image; no selective or non-uniform modifications were performed.

Anti-Inflammatory Effects of IVIGA in BV2 Cells

BV2 microglial cells were purchased from Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China; Cat# CL-0493). The supplier provided short tandem repeat (STR) profiling authentication data and corresponding spectra to verify the identity of the cell line. All experiments were performed with cells within 15 passages after thawing. Cells were routinely tested for mycoplasma contamination by PCR, and only negative cultures were used for experimentation.

Cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, Gibco) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (100 U/mL penicillin, 100 μg/mL streptomycin) at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂. The medium was changed every 2–3 days, and cells were subcultured at 70–80% confluence using 0.25% trypsin–EDTA solution (Gibco). For anti-inflammatory experiments, cells were seeded in 12-well plates at a density of 2 × 105 cells/well and allowed to adhere overnight.

To evaluate whether Ang-2 modification alters the anti-inflammatory properties of IVIG, we adapted and modified a previously reported method (Domínguez-Soto et al. 2018). Briefly, BV2 microglial cells were pretreated with IVIGA or unmodified IVIG (500 μg/mL, diluted in serum-free DMEM) for 2 h, followed by stimulation with lipopolysaccharide (LPS, Solarbio, Cat# L8880, 100 ng/mL) for 24 h. Culture supernatants were then collected and analyzed for tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) and interleukin-1β (IL-1β) concentrations using mouse ELISA kits (BioLegend) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a SpectraMax iD5 microplate reader (Molecular Devices), and cytokine concentrations were quantified from standard curves to compare the anti-inflammatory efficacy of IVIGA with that of unmodified IVIG.

Animals

Male A53T α-syn transgenic mice (12 months old), originally obtained from the Jackson Laboratory (strain B6; C3-Tg (Prnp-SNCA*A53T)83Vle/J), were bred and maintained under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions at the Laboratory Animal Facility of Tsinghua University. Male-only cohorts were pre-specified based on pilot data and prior reports indicating more robust and less variable phenotypes in males within this age window for the behavioral and nigrostriatal endpoints used, thereby increasing assay sensitivity; additionally, a single-sex design reduces estrous cycle-related variance in open-field/anxiety and neuroinflammatory readouts and is consistent with Reduction/Refinement under the 3R framework (Tullo et al. 2025; Pestana et al. 2024; Hubrecht et al. 2019). Age-matched male wild-type (WT) littermates lacking the A53T transgene were used as controls. All mice were housed in individually ventilated cages (IVCs) under a 12-h light/dark cycle at a controlled temperature (22 ± 2 °C), with ad libitum access to food and water. All animal procedures were conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee.

A total of 18 mice were enrolled, comprising A53T males (n = 12) and age-matched male WT littermates (n = 6).

Drug Administration

Considering the relatively short half-life of human IVIG in mice (Bard et al. 2012; St-Amour et al. 2013), an adjusted dosing regimen was adopted to optimize its exposure in the CNS. A53T mice were randomized to receive either brain-targeted IVIGᴬ (A53T + IVIGᴬ, n = 6) or PBS (A53T + PBS, n = 6); WT littermates received PBS (WT, n = 6). Brain-targeted IVIGᴬ was administered via tail vein injection at a dose of 400 mg/kg body weight every 3 days for 4 weeks. All injections were performed under brief isoflurane anesthesia to minimize stress. Mice were closely monitored after each administration for general health status and behavioral abnormalities. Behavioral assessments and tissue collection were conducted two weeks after the final injection.

Behavioral Assessments

Behavioral testing was completed within two weeks after the final administration, using the following group sizes: A53T + IVIGᴬ (n = 6), A53T + PBS (n = 6), and WT (n = 6). Experimenters were blinded to the treatment groups throughout the procedures. To minimize stress and fatigue, a minimum interval of one day was maintained between each behavioral test, allowing sufficient recovery time for the animals.

Hindlimb Clasping Test

Hindlimb clasping test served as a simple and sensitive indicator of neurological deficits in neurodegenerative disease models (Guyenet et al. 2010). Mice were gently lifted by the tail and suspended in the air for 1 min to observe hindlimb posture. A 0–3 scoring scale was used to assess motor impairment, as previously described (Guyenet et al. 2010):

0 = hindlimbs fully splayed,

0 = hindlimbs fully splayed,

1 = slight retraction but still partially extended,

1 = slight retraction but still partially extended,

2 = marked retraction without full clasping,

2 = marked retraction without full clasping,

3 = hindlimbs tightly clasped against the abdomen.

3 = hindlimbs tightly clasped against the abdomen.

Rotarod Test

Motor coordination and balance were evaluated using a mouse fatigue rotarod apparatus, as previously described (Shiotsuki et al. 2010). The experiment was conducted over three consecutive days, with each mouse undergoing both training and testing sessions daily. During the training phase, the rotarod rotated at a constant speed of 4 rpm for 5 min. In the testing phase, the rotation speed was linearly accelerated from 4 to 40 rpm over a 5-min period. Each mouse was subjected to three consecutive trials per day, and the latency to fall was recorded for each trial.

Open Field Test

Open field test is widely employed to evaluate locomotor activity and anxiety-like behavior in rodents (Zha et al. 2016). Mice were individually placed in the center of a 50 cm × 50 cm open-field arena and allowed to explore freely for 10 min. Behavioral parameters, including total distance traveled, time spent in the center zone, and rearing frequency, were recorded and analyzed using an automated video tracking system (SMART v3.0, Panlab).

Pole Test

Motor coordination and bradykinesia were assessed using the pole test, as previously described (Wang et al. 2024). Mice were individually placed head-upward near the top of a vertical rough-surfaced pole (50 cm in height, 1 cm in diameter). The time taken to turn completely downward (T-turn) and the total time to descend to the base (T-descend) were recorded. Each mouse received three trials per session, with at least 5 min of rest between trials. Mice were habituated to the apparatus one day prior to testing. For each animal, the average of three trials was calculated and used for analysis.

Novel Object Recognition Test

Recognition memory was evaluated using a two-day Novel Object Recognition (NOR) protocol, as previously described (Lueptow et al. 2017). On Day 1 (habituation phase), mice were placed in the arena and exposed to two identical objects to allow exploration. On Day 2 (test phase), one of the familiar objects was replaced with a novel object. The time spent exploring the novel versus the familiar object was recorded, and the discrimination index was calculated as an indicator of recognition memory performance.

Y-Maze Test

The Y-maze test was conducted to assess spatial working memory in mice, using a maze composed of three identical arms (30 cm × 8 cm × 15 cm) (Zha et al. 2016). The procedure consisted of a training phase and a test phase. During the training phase, one arm was blocked, and the mice were allowed to freely explore the remaining two arms for 5 min. After a 2-h retention interval, the test phase was performed by removing the barrier to allow access to all three arms. Mice were reintroduced into the maze and allowed to explore freely for 5 min. The number of entries into the previously blocked (novel) arm and the time spent in it were recorded. Increased exploration of the novel arm was interpreted as an indicator of preserved spatial recognition memory (Zha et al. 2016).

Tissue Collection and Processing

Following completion of the behavioral battery, all mice (N = 18; A53T + IVIGᴬ, n = 6; A53T + PBS, n = 6; WT, n = 6) were deeply anesthetized and transcardially perfused with ice-cold PBS. Brains were removed within ≤ 2 min on a pre-chilled stage and bisected along the midline. Hemispheres were allocated consistently across all animals: the right hemisphere for histology (IHC/IF; n = 6 mice per group) and the left hemisphere for molecular assays (WB and qPCR; n = 6 mice per group for each assay). Biological replicates were defined at the mouse level; no pooling was performed.

Regional sampling (atlas-guided coronal blocks).

SN (coronal block): coronal blocks were collected from approximately bregma − 2.0 to − 5.0 mm to encompass SN across relevant midbrain levels.

SN (coronal block): coronal blocks were collected from approximately bregma − 2.0 to − 5.0 mm to encompass SN across relevant midbrain levels.

STR (coronal block): coronal blocks targeting the dorsal striatum (caudate–putamen) were collected at approximately bregma + 0.0 to + 2.0 mm.

STR (coronal block): coronal blocks targeting the dorsal striatum (caudate–putamen) were collected at approximately bregma + 0.0 to + 2.0 mm.

From the left-hemisphere SN block, an SNpc-enriched piece of ~ 10 mg was immediately transferred into TRIzol and homogenized in-tube under RNase-free conditions for qPCR. The remaining left-hemisphere SN and STR material was snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 °C for Western blot/biochemical analyses. The right hemisphere was immersion-fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde at 4 °C overnight, cryoprotected in 30% sucrose, embedded in OCT, and coronally sectioned for IHC/IF.

To mitigate the inclusion of neighboring tissue inherent to block sampling, ROIs were stringently defined by anatomical landmarks on sections for IHC/IF, and molecular readouts were chosen for high relevance to SN/STR.

Immunohistochemistry and Immunofluorescence

Immunohistochemistry and immunofluorescence staining were performed on frozen brain sections (20 μm thick) prepared as described above. For immunohistochemistry analysis, sections were washed with PBS, incubated in 3% hydrogen peroxide in methanol for 10 min to quench endogenous peroxidase activity, and then blocked with PBS containing 5% BSA and 0.1% Triton X-100 for 1 h at room temperature.

Sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against phosphorylated α-syn at serine 129 (pS129 α-syn) (Abcam, Cat# ab51253, RRID: AB_869973, 1:100), tyrosine hydroxylase (TH) (Abcam, Cat# ab137869, AB_2801410, 1:100), dopamine transporter (DAT) (Abcam, Cat# ab128848, AB_2665470, 1:100), glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) (Cell Signaling Technology, Cat# 3670, AB_561049, 1:100), or Iba1 (GeneTex, Cat# GTX100042, AB_1240434, 1:100). After PBS washes, sections were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 1:500) for 1 h at room temperature. Color development was performed using DAB substrate solution (Solarbio) following the manufacturer's instructions. Hematoxylin counterstaining was applied to visualize nuclei. Bright-field images were captured using an Olympus IX73 inverted microscope equipped with a 10 × objective and DP80 digital camera at room temperature. Image acquisition was performed with cellSens software, and positive cell quantification was carried out by a blinded investigator in anatomically matched regions from 6 per group (biological replicates) using ImageJ.

For immunofluorescence staining, sections were blocked in PBS containing 5% BSA and 0.3% Triton X-100 for 1 h and incubated overnight at 4 °C with anti-postsynaptic density protein 95 (PSD95) (Abcam, Cat# ab238135, RRID: AB_2895158, 1:100) and anti-synaptophysin (SYP) (Abcam, Cat# ab8049, RRID: AB_2198854, 1:500). After washing, sections were incubated with Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated donkey anti-mouse IgG (H + L) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# A-11001; RRID: AB_2534069) and Alexa Fluor 647-conjugated donkey anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# A-31573; RRID: AB_2536183) at a 1:500 dilution for 1 h at room temperature, followed by nuclear staining with DAPI. Fluorescence images were obtained using a Leica TCS SP8 confocal microscope equipped with 40 × and 63 × oil-immersion objectives and hybrid detectors (HyD). Excitation was provided by laser lines at 405 nm (DAPI), 488 nm (Alexa Fluor 488), and 633 nm (Alexa Fluor 647) using a Leica SP8 confocal microscope. Image acquisition was performed with Leica LAS X software under identical settings for all groups, with exposure times and detector gain maintained constant across all samples. Fluorescence intensity and colocalization analyses were conducted with ImageJ software. All images were processed uniformly, and only linear adjustments of brightness and contrast were applied equally to the entire image; no selective or non-uniform modifications were performed.

Nissl Stain

Nissl staining was performed on frozen brain sections prepared as described above to evaluate neuronal integrity in the SN. Coronal sections (20 μm thick) were stained using a commercial Nissl staining kit (Solarbio) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, sections were incubated in cresyl violet solution, differentiated with 95% ethanol, dehydrated through graded ethanol, cleared in xylene, and coverslipped with neutral resin.

Bright-field images were acquired using an Olympus IX73 inverted microscope equipped with a 10 × objective and DP80 digital camera at room temperature. Image acquisition was performed using an Olympus microscope equipped with cellSens software under identical exposure settings for all groups. All images were processed uniformly without selective or non-linear adjustments. Nissl-positive neurons in anatomically defined regions of the substantia nigra (SN) were manually counted using ImageJ software by an investigator blinded to the experimental groups. Quantification was performed on 6 mice per group (biological replicates), and imaging parameters were kept constant across all samples to ensure comparability.

Statistical Analysis

All data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), with individual data points overlaid in the figures. For animal behavioral experiments, each group comprised six mice. For cell-based assays, experiments were performed independently at least three times, and the mean from each independent experiment was entered into the statistical tests.

For animal behavioral data, results are shown in absolute units; for proportion-type measures (e.g., open-field center time as a percentage of total time), percent values are also reported. For Western blot analyses, signals were normalized to β-actin; for phosphorylation endpoints, both p- and total proteins were β-actin–normalized and summarized as the p/total ratio. Fluorescence readouts were linearly rescaled by a single constant to ~ 0–10 for display only (axes labeled, e.g., “a.u. × 103”); all hypothesis tests used unscaled raw values. Colocalization and DAB IHC were expressed as % of ROI area for display (overlap/ROI × 100; positive/ROI × 100); statistical tests were performed on the corresponding proportions (area/ROI). Assay kits were shown in native units (e.g., pg/mL, µM), with optional single-factor display-only rescaling. qPCR used the ΔCt/ΔΔCt method; plots show 2^ − ΔΔCt (A53T + PBS = 1), and statistics used ΔCt.

An a priori power analysis (Python, statsmodels.stats.power) was conducted to determine the target sample size for the primary comparison, aiming for ≥ 80% power at α = 0.05; this informed the planned group size for behavioral experiments (six per group) unless otherwise noted.

Angiopep-2 Modified IVIG Showed Enhanced BBB Penetration, Brain Accumulation with Preserved Functional Activity

Ang-2 has been shown to facilitate brain delivery of macromolecules via LRP1-mediated transcytosis (Demeule et al. 2008; di Polidoro et al. 2022). To improve the brain delivery of IVIG and preserve antibody integrity and function, we employed a site-specific chemical conjugation strategy to attach Ang-2 peptides to the Fc glycan residue (Asn297) of IVIG. Specifically, Ang-2 peptides bearing N-terminal hydrazide (–NHNH2) groups were covalently linked to oxidized Fc glycans using sodium periodate-mediated coupling, generating a brain-targeted IVIG formulation (IVIGᴬ) (Fig. 1A). To confirm successful conjugation and targeting capability, dot blot analysis was performed using recombinant LRP1 protein dotted onto NC membranes. IVIGᴬ exhibited robust binding to LRP1, whereas unmodified IVIG showed negligible interaction, indicating that IVIG was efficiently modified and IVIGᴬ had acquired the ability to bind LRP1 (Fig. 1B). To assess whether Ang-2 conjugation affected Fc-mediated function, we evaluated binding of C1q to the Fc region of IVIGᴬ, which was commonly used as a surrogate marker for Fc structural integrity and immune competence (Georgakopoulos et al. 2011; Wilkinson et al. 2021). C1q binding is a surrogate marker of Fc integrity. Therefore, ELISA analysis demonstrated comparable C1q binding across a concentration range of 0.75 to 200 μg/mL for both IVIGᴬ and unmodified IVIG, indicating that the Fc functionality of IVIGᴬ remained intact after modification (Fig. 1C). We next investigated whether Ang-2 facilitated IVIG uptake in brain endothelial cells. As Fc receptors are minimally expressed on brain endothelium, unmodified IVIG is poorly internalized by endothelium (Anami et al. 2022; Demeule et al. 2008). Consistently, bEnd.3 cells showed minimal uptake of native IVIG. However, IVIGᴬ was substantially internalized by bEnd.3 cells (Fig. 1D, E), supporting receptor-mediated internalization.

We then examined the in vivo brain-targeting efficiency of IVIGᴬ. C57BL/6 mice were intravenously injected with 25 mg of Cy5-labeled IVIGᴬ or unmodified IVIG, and brain tissues were harvested at 0, 1, 3, 6, 12, and 24 h post-injection following transcardial perfusion with PBS. Ex vivo fluorescence imaging demonstrated 5.4-fold higher brain accumulation of IVIGᴬ than unmodified IVIG, based on the partial AUC within the peak window (Fig. 1F, G). Immunofluorescence analysis of brain sections collected 3 h post-injection further demonstrated widespread parenchymal distribution of IVIGᴬ beyond CD31⁺ vessels, whereas unmodified IVIG remained largely restricted to the vasculature (Fig. 1H, I), supporting enhanced transcytosis of IVIGᴬ into the brain. Lastly, we examined whether Ang-2 conjugation altered the anti-inflammatory activity of IVIG. In LPS-stimulated BV2 microglial cells, both IVIGᴬ and IVIG significantly reduced TNF-α and IL-1β secretion, with no significant differences between the two groups (Fig. 1J, K), suggesting that Ang-2 modification did not compromise IVIG’s immunomodulatory properties.

IVIGᴬ Improves Motor Coordination and Cognitive Performance in A53T α-Syn Transgenic Mice

To assess the therapeutic efficacy of IVIGᴬ in vivo, 12-month-old A53T α-syn transgenic mice were intravenously administered IVIGᴬ (A53T + IVIGᴬ) or PBS (A53T + PBS) every 3 days for a duration of four weeks. WT littermates receiving PBS served as baseline controls (WT). n = 6/group. Behavioral assessments were performed two weeks after the final injection to evaluate treatment outcomes (Fig. 2A). To determine the effect of IVIGᴬ on motor dysfunction and muscle strength, the hindlimb clasping test, rotarod test, open-field test, and pole test were conducted. In the hindlimb clasping test, IVIGᴬ treatment showed a tendency to reduce clasping severity in A53T α-syn transgenic mice, but the difference did not reach statistical significance (Kruskal–Wallis followed by Mann–Whitney U test, P = 0.082) (Fig. 2B, C). In the rotarod test, IVIGᴬ-treated mice displayed prolonged latency to fall across three consecutive training days, indicative of improved motor coordination (Fig. 2D). Similarly, open-field analysis revealed that IVIGᴬ-treated mice exhibited enhanced locomotor and exploratory behavior, characterized by increased total distance traveled, greater time spent in the center zone, and a higher number of rearing events compared to PBS-treated controls (Fig. 2E–H). Consistently, IVIGᴬ treatment improved performance in the pole test, as evidenced by reduced time to turn and to descend (Fig. 2I, J). To further evaluate cognitive function, we conducted the NOR and Y-maze tests. In the NOR test, IVIGᴬ-treated mice demonstrated a significantly higher discrimination index, suggesting improved recognition memory (Fig. 2K). In the Y-maze test, IVIGᴬ-treated mice spent more time in and made more entries into the novel arm compared to transgenic controls (Fig. 2L–N). Collectively, these results indicate that IVIGᴬ treatment significantly ameliorates both motor and cognitive deficits in A53T α-syn transgenic mice.

IVIGᴬ Reduces α-Syn Aggregate Burden and Preserves Dopaminergic Neurons and Synaptic Integrity in A53T α-Syn Transgenic Mice

To evaluate the neuroprotective efficacy of IVIGᴬ, we assessed α-syn pathology in the SN and STR of A53T α-syn transgenic mice (Fig. 3A). Immunohistochemical staining revealed pronounced accumulation of pS129 α-syn in both SN and STR of A53T α-syn transgenic mice, which was significantly reduced following IVIGᴬ treatment (Fig. 3B, C). Western blot analysis further confirmed that levels of h-α-syn and pan-α-syn were markedly decreased in IVIGᴬ-treated animals (Fig. 3D–F). Given that degeneration of dopaminergic neurons in the SN is a pathological hallmark of PD (Guatteo et al. 2022), we next assessed dopaminergic neuron survival via Nissl staining, and TH and DAT expression via immunohistochemistry (Fig. 4A). Compared to A53T controls (A53T + PBS), IVIGᴬ-treated mice showed a significant increase in Nissl-positive areas and elevated expression of TH and DAT in the SN (Fig. 4B–E). We also assessed synaptic integrity by co-immunostaining of synaptic markers PSD95 and SYP. A53T α-syn transgenic mice showed reduced PSD95 and SYP immunofluorescence intensity and a lower PSD95–SYP colocalization fraction (colocalized area/total area). In contrast, IVIGᴬ treatment restored both markers and significantly increased the colocalized area fraction (Fig. 4F–I). Consistently, western blot results showed significantly increased protein levels of PSD95, TH, DAT, and SYP in the SN of the IVIGᴬ-treated mouse group (Fig. 4J–N). Together, these results demonstrate that IVIGᴬ attenuated α-syn levels, protected dopaminergic neurons and synapses in the brains of A53T α-syn transgenic mice.

IVIGᴬ Attenuates Neuroinflammation, Oxidative Stress, and Complement Activation in Dopaminergic Brain Regions of A53T α-Syn Transgenic Mice

Neuroinflammation is a prominent pathological feature of PD, characterized by the activation of microglia and astrocytes and the subsequent release of pro-inflammatory cytokines in the brain (Liddelow et al. 2017; Tan et al. 2020). To assess the anti-inflammatory effects of IVIGᴬ, we examined glial activation, gliosis, and neurotoxic cytokine production in the SN and STR of A53T α-syn transgenic mice (Fig. 5A). Immunohistochemical analysis revealed marked activation of astrocytes (GFAP⁺) and microglia (Iba1⁺) in both SN and STR of A53T α-syn transgenic mice, which was significantly attenuated by IVIGᴬ treatment (Fig. 5B-F). Moreover, ELISA results demonstrated that IVIGᴬ treatment significantly reduced levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α and IL-1β, in the SN and STR (Fig. 5G, H). Given the critical role of the NF-κB signaling pathway in PD-related neuroinflammation (Bassani et al. 2015; Isik et al. 2023), we further assessed key components of this pathway. Western blot analysis revealed that IVIGᴬ treatment significantly downregulated phosphorylated IKKβ, phosphorylated p65, and phosphorylated JNK expression in the SN (Fig. 5I-L). In addition to neuroinflammation, aberrant oxidative stress and complement activation are also implicated in PD pathology (Aryal et al. 2020; Olufunmilayo et al. 2023). Kit-based detection of oxidative stress markers showed that IVIGᴬ treatment significantly decreased levels of NO₂⁻ and H₂O₂ in the SN and STR in IVIGᴬ-treated A53T mice relative to A53T + PBS (Fig. 5M, N). Moreover, qPCR analysis revealed that IVIGᴬ treatment markedly suppressed the expression of complement cascade genes, including C1q, C2, C3, and C4 in the SN of A53T α-syn transgenic mice (Fig. 5O). Collectively, these results demonstrate that IVIGᴬ exerts broad neuroprotective effects in the A53T α-syn transgenic mice by suppressing glial activation, inhibiting pro-inflammatory signaling, reducing oxidative stress, and attenuating complement system activation.

Discussion

PD affects > 8.5 million people worldwide and accounts for ~ 5.8 million DALYs and ~ 329,000 deaths, underscoring the urgent need for disease-modifying strategies (WHO, 2022; WHO, 2023). Antibody-based therapies, including α-syn-targeted monoclonal antibodies and pooled IVIG, aim to neutralize pathogenic α-syn species and modulate neuroinflammation; however, efficacy has been limited in part by inadequate delivery to the brain (Rabenstein et al. 2019). A key barrier is transport: IgG (~ 150 kDa) traverses the intact BBB at extremely low levels, resulting in negligible parenchymal exposure (Pardridge et al. 2023; St-Amour et al. 2013). To overcome this limitation, we conjugated IVIG to Ang-2, a 19-amino-acid peptide that binds LRP1 and mediates receptor-dependent transcytosis across brain endothelium, generating IVIGᴬ (Demeule et al. 2008). In vivo, IVIGᴬ increased brain accumulation and improved motor and cognitive phenotypes in A53T mice while reducing α-syn pathology, neuroinflammation, oxidative stress, and complement activation. These findings support the conclusion that enhancing BBB transport is necessary to realize therapeutic effects from antibody-based interventions in synucleinopathies.

LRP1 has dual roles at the BBB: it is well recognized for abluminal clearance from brain to blood, yet it also exhibits luminal endothelial expression that can support ligand influx when engaged by appropriate shuttles (Demeule et al. 2008; di Polidoro et al. 2022; Georgieva et al. 2014; Habib et al. 2022). By conjugating Ang-2 to IVIG, we leveraged this targeted pathway; other routes (e.g., FcRn-associated recycling, fluid-phase transcytosis, pathology-related permeability) may contribute marginally but are unlikely to account for the magnitude of uptake we observed. The resulting chemistry is mild and site-specific at the Fc glycan (Asn297), aiming to preserve Fc function while conferring BBB shuttling—providing a pragmatic, scalable way to raise intraparenchymal IgG exposure without de novo antibody engineering.

We designed this therapeutic study to establish proof-of-concept for brain-targeted delivery, given that unmodified IVIG is expected to have very limited CNS exposure and therefore a low a priori likelihood of detectable effect. Accordingly, our primary objective was to test whether enabling BBB transport can translate into measurable benefit. We agree, however, that adding an unmodified IVIG arm would be informative for quantifying the incremental contribution of Ang-2–mediated delivery. To that end, we have pre-specified a randomized follow-up (vehicle vs IVIG vs IVIGᴬ under identical dose and schedule) to isolate the causal impact of BBB targeting and to calibrate exposure–response.

IVIGᴬ’s benefits likely reflect both direct engagement of α-syn species and broader immunomodulation. Unmodified IVIG has generally failed to produce consistent benefits in PD models (St-Amour et al. 2012), and isolating α-syn–reactive natural autoantibodies can help but remains technically complex and costly (Huang et al. 2019; Folke et al. 2022). Our findings indicate that delivery is the decisive variable: Angiopep-2 conjugation increased in vivo brain entry by 5.4-fold relative to unmodified IVIG (Fig. 1G). Consistent with enhanced parenchymal exposure, IVIGᴬ treatment was associated with lower pS129 α-syn burden, preservation of dopaminergic and synaptic markers, and attenuation of NF-κB signaling, oxidative stress, and complement activation. This pleiotropic profile accords with the polyclonal repertoire of IVIG and suggests that achieving adequate brain exposure can convert largely peripheral activity into CNS efficacy (Pardridge et al. 2023).

We nonetheless recognize additional, non-mutually exclusive determinants that can limit antibody efficacy and that we now explicitly discuss. Disease stage/timing likely matters: once intracellular aggregation, synaptic dysfunction, and neuroinflammation are established, neutralizing extracellular α-syn may yield attenuated benefit; we will test early- versus late-intervention cohorts. Target accessibility remains constrained because most pathogenic α-syn is intracellular or membrane-associated; delivery improves but does not abolish this barrier. Conformer/epitope heterogeneity (PTMs, truncations, strain-like diversity) can reduce binding; multi-epitope/polyspecific approaches may be advantageous. PK/PD and regimen require optimization to sustain parenchymal exposure and occupancy; exposure–response will guide dose/interval. Fc effector functions can aid aggregate clearance but also risk pro-inflammatory activation; Fc-tuning will be explored. Finally, the immunogenicity of human IVIG in mice did not confound short-term outcomes here and in prior reports (Loeffler et al. 2012; Dubey et al. 2020), but in future studies, we will monitor for anti-drug immune responses during prolonged dosing.

Design choices and limitations are transparently noted. We used male-only A53T cohorts because the phenotype in this age window is more robust and less variable, improving assay sensitivity and aligning with Reduction/Refinement; however, this limits generalizability and precludes sex-by-treatment inference. We will perform sex-inclusive replication powered for interaction across key endpoints (Y-maze novel arm metrics, pole test latencies, open-field center time/rearing, and representative histology/molecular markers) using the same dose/timeline and reporting both sex-stratified and pooled effects. The behavioral n = 6/group was chosen via a priori power planning for primary endpoints; observed effects on primary outcomes were large with high achieved power, whereas two exploratory measures were under-powered (hindlimb clasping: d ≈ 1.68, power ≈ 0.75; open-field total distance vs WT: power ≈ 0.58). We therefore label these as limited and will replicate with n≈10–13/group to increase precision and sensitivity. We used a single-cohort workflow (behavior first, terminal assays thereafter) with a ~ 2-week interval after the last dose to complete testing and avoid acute dosing confounds; this may underestimate peak tissue engagement and allow interval disease progression. Planned work will stagger terminal timepoints (24–72 h, 1 week, 2 weeks post-dose) and measure brain/CSF exposure to map the exposure–effect window; where helpful, separate behavior/tissue cohorts will be considered. Finally, atlas-guided coronal blocks ensured complete inclusion of SN/striatum across levels but inevitably admitted limited neighboring tissue; we mitigated this through strict ROI definition for IHC/IF and region-relevant molecular readouts. Future studies may add finer dissections or spatial approaches.

In sum, Ang-2 conjugation markedly increases IVIG brain delivery and is associated with broad therapeutic benefits in a transgenic synucleinopathy model, spanning behavior, α-syn burden, dopaminergic neuron integrity, synaptic markers, and inflammatory, oxidative, complement pathways. These findings provide mechanistic and functional proof-of-concept that targeted BBB transcytosis can convert an otherwise CNS-limited biologic into a disease-modifying candidate for PD. More broadly, BBB targeting is necessary but not sufficient. Combining enhanced delivery with earlier intervention, rational epitope breadth, Fc-tuning, exposure-guided dosing, and sex-inclusive, multi-model validation is likely required for clinical translation. This strategy highlights the potential of IVIGᴬ as a clinically translatable therapy for PD and presents Ang-2–mediated antibody delivery as a versatile platform for treating a broad range of neurodegenerative diseases.
