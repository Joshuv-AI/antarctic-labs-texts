Coverage and social and behavioural drivers of routine childhood immunisation in selected regions in the Philippines: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Paulyn Jean Acacio-Claro, Maria Margarita Lota, Evalyn Roxas, Suzanna Vidmar, Alvin Abeleda, Soledad Natalia Dalisay, Madilene Landicho, Yoshiki Fujimori, Jan Zarlyn Rosuello, Micaella Dato, Anna Marie Demeterio, Jessica Kaufman, Florian Vogt, Vicente Belizario, Margie Danchin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionLike many countries globally, childhood immunisation coverage is below government targets in the Philippines and has worsened during the COVID-19 pandemic, leading to outbreaks of vaccine-preventable diseases. This study aimed to identify the social and behavioural drivers of routine childhood immunisation and preferred caregiver information sources to improve uptake in the Philippines.MethodsA quantitative, cross-sectional study, as part of a larger mixed-methods study, was undertaken in three regions in the Philippines with low immunisation coverage in 2023. Data on Behavioural and Social Drivers (BeSD) of immunisation, preferred information sources and vaccination status of children under 5 years were collected, analysed and reported according to the WHO BeSD framework.ResultsThe study included 653 caregivers and 950 children, with 65% of children fully immunised, 31.8% partially immunised and 2.5% being zero-dose or having received no vaccines. Key reasons for complete vaccination included the protection vaccines offer, healthcare worker recommendations and vaccines being free of charge. For partial vaccination, top reasons were intercurrent illness, inaccessible vaccination sites and parental belief that the child was too young to receive vaccines. The most common reason for non-vaccination was also intercurrent illness. The predictors of complete vaccination were older age of caregivers (46–59 years) (prevalence ratio (PR)=1.29; 95% CI 1.12 to 1.48) and low concerns about vaccine safety (PR=1.13; 95% CI 1.03 to 1.25). Other Christian denominations, compared with Catholics, were less likely to have fully immunised children (PR 0.82; 95% CI 0.69 to 0.97). Preferred vaccine information sources included the Department of Health, healthcare workers and mass media.ConclusionsWe found that only 65% of children were fully immunised in 2023, with both access and acceptance factors impacting uptake of routine childhood vaccines. Improving service accessibility and education of healthcare workers on vaccine safety and effectiveness and more targeted messaging from health authorities and mass media about the benefits of vaccination could increase uptake in the Philippines, with learnings from this study applicable globally.

Introduction

Childhood vaccines have provided the greatest contribution to improved infant survival over the past 50 years with 154 million deaths averted—95% among children <5 years and 61% due to measles vaccines.1 Vaccines contribute to 14 of the 17 Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), especially health (SDG3), and play a central role in achieving them.2 However, the immunisation-related indicators for SDG3 lag behind in many regions of the world, including the Asia-Pacific region. The COVID-19 pandemic caused substantial backsliding in vaccine confidence and coverage globally,3 with an increase in zero-dose children who receive no vaccines and account for nearly half of the vaccine preventable deaths.4 According to the latest WHO and UNICEF data,5 third-dose diphtheria, tetanus and pertussis (DTP3) coverage is estimated at 85% globally, first-dose measles/rubella (MR1) at 84% and zero-dose children have increased from 13.9 million in 2022 to 14.3 million in 2024. As such, improving childhood immunisation coverage is a global priority and critical to meeting the Immunisation Agenda 2030 (IA2030) targets of 90% coverage and no more than 6.5 million zero-dose children globally by 2030. Central to achieving these goals is accurate, country-specific data on coverage and drivers of vaccination to inform effective strategies.

The Philippines is ranked 5th out of the top 10 zero-dose countries with one million children who received no vaccines in 2021.6 Despite the implementation of a national vaccination programme for over four decades, childhood vaccine coverage rates have consistently fallen below the 95% target set by government7 and have been 60%–69% over the last 5 years.8 The national vaccination programme in the Philippines, established in 1976,9 was expanded to include school-aged children, adolescents and adults >60 years in 2016. National full immunisation coverage was estimated to be 59% in 202210 with notable decreases in uptake of measles containing vaccine (MCV), oral polio vaccine (OPV) and DTP, which have contributed to outbreaks of these diseases in 2024.11

Wide disparities in coverage rates exist among different regions in the Philippines,7 reflecting urban–rural and socioeconomic differences. Caregiver attitudes, beliefs and experiences with vaccines influence vaccine confidence and acceptance.12 13 Public trust and confidence in vaccines has been eroded by anti-vaccine groups14 and vaccine safety scares such as the Dengvaxia controversy, where primary school deaths were attributed to a new dengue vaccine in 2017.15 16 Supply issues such as vaccine stock-outs5 and a myriad of access issues impact vaccine uptake7. However, drivers of undervaccination for routine childhood vaccines post COVID-19 are not well understood in the Philippines17 and the government has prioritised collecting these data to increase childhood vaccine coverage rates.15

To understand the context-specific modifiable reasons for low childhood vaccine uptake and inform targeted strategies, the WHO recommends using the Behavioural and Social Drivers (BeSD) of Vaccination framework.18 The framework measures both access and acceptance factors through the following domains: thinking and feeling (the way people think about vaccine safety and effectiveness etc), social processes (influence of family and friends and provider recommendations), motivation (intention to vaccinate and vaccine hesitancy), and practical issues (practical barriers to accessing immunisation services).18 Understanding the diverse drivers that impact uptake is needed to design effective immunisation programmes.19

This study aimed to identify the social and behavioural drivers of routine childhood immunisation according to the WHO BeSD framework and preferred information sources of caregivers of children under 5 years to inform strategies to improve vaccine uptake in the Philippines. Findings from this study are already being used by the Department of Health (DOH), policy makers and programme planners to improve programme implementation, develop tailored approaches to increase vaccine uptake and strengthen primary healthcare (PHC) service delivery.20

Methods

Study sites and population

In consultation with the DOH, Regions IV-B (MIMAROPA) and V (Bicol) in Luzon, and VIII (Eastern Visayas) in Visayas were identified as priority regions with low COVID-19 and routine childhood vaccine coverage. These locations are home to Indigenous Peoples such as the Mangyan in Luzon, and other Lumad tribal groupings such as the Mamanwa in Visayas.21 The majority of residents in all three regions belong to the lowest wealth quintile and the main sources of livelihood are agriculture, gold mining, tourism, livestock and fisheries.10

Between April and December 2023, a multistage stratified cluster sampling was undertaken to select the respondents included in the study. The sampling design aligned with the WHO Vaccination Coverage Cluster Surveys methodology to reduce bias and improve accuracy and precision of survey results.22 Within each of the three regions, two provinces, four municipalities and eight ‘barangays’, the smallest community unit, were selected in consultation with relevant regional DOH offices and rural PHC facilities to target low coverage areas (online supplemental Appendix A). Prior to data collection, community health workers went from house to house in the barangays to recruit caregivers aged 18 years and above who cared for at least one child aged 5 years and below and invite them to take part in the study. All eligible caregivers able to be accessed during home visits were asked to present in the communal areas near the local health facility for a facilitated face-to-face survey for data collection. This process was continued until the quota per barangay was obtained to meet the sample size per province. As caregivers are often not home, every attempt was made to return and invite all caregivers to optimise representativeness.

Study design and variables

This study is part of a larger mixed methods research project in the Philippines. Quantitative analysis of immunisation coverage data and qualitative key informant interviews of health officials and healthcare workers (HCWs) and focus group discussions among caregivers were also conducted to obtain a deeper understanding of the social and behavioural drivers of childhood23 and COVID-19 vaccination for children24 and adults.25

For this paper, we report the quantitative data from the cross-sectional, paper-based survey questionnaires informed by the WHO BeSD surveys. Questions pertaining to vaccine confidence and hesitancy were derived from the Centers for Disease Control Vaccine Confidence Survey Question Bank26 and the WHO-Strategic Advisory Group of Experts Vaccine Hesitancy Matrix Questions.27 Additional questions on attitudes and perceptions regarding childhood immunisation from the Pediatric Infectious Disease Society of the Philippines-validated questionnaire on Parents’ Attitude About Childhood Vaccines.28 The questionnaire was translated and pretested before the face-to-face surveys of caregivers, guided by study personnel, were conducted in the barangays.

The following sociodemographic characteristics were collected from primary caregivers: age in years, sex (male or female), civil status (single, married, live-in, separated or widow), religion (Catholic, other Christian denomination, Islam and others), educational attainment (elementary, high school or college level) and employment status (unemployed, student, pensioner, self-employed or employed). The reasons for completion or not of each child’s vaccination as well as caregiver preferred vaccine information sources were also collected. The caregivers were asked to indicate their agreement (strongly agree/agree, neutral, disagree/strongly disagree) with several BeSD statements regarding vaccine-preventable diseases (VPDs) and vaccines provided during routine childhood immunisation. Data on the number of children 0–5 years in the household, relationship of caregiver to child and parent-reported vaccination status of the child according to vaccines given (received all, some or none) were collected. Parent-reported child vaccination status was verified through review of the child’s health facility-issued immunisation records card when available.

The outcome used in this study was age-appropriate immunisation status of the child based on caregiver’s report and/or immunisation records. Categories used were: (a) fully immunised child (FIC) defined as completing a single dose of Bacille Calmette-Guerin (BCG) vaccine, three doses of OPV, DTP3, Haemophilus influenzae type B and Hepatitis B vaccines, and two doses of MCV on or before 1 year of age,29 (b) partial immunisation, and (c) zero-dose child defined as a child who received no vaccines. In case of differences between reported status and immunisation records, the category used was based on the records. The completed questionnaires were encoded in an MS Excel database with data validation checks and macros for quality control.

Sample size

We aimed to recruit a total of 198 participants per region, using a full immunisation coverage rate of 10.3% (based on lowest coverage in one of the study sites), a design effect of 2 for cluster sampling within the barangay and a desired absolute precision of 6%.30 A buffer of 10% was added to account for loss and exclusions to obtain a sample of 27 or 28 per barangay, 54 or 55 per municipality leading to a total of 109 per province, 218 per region or a total sample size of 654 for all three regions.

Data analysis

A mixed effects logistic regression was performed on routine immunisation uptake separately for each sociodemographic characteristic and BeSD statement, allowing for clustering by caregiver. The BeSD domains examined were: thinking and feeling, social processes and practical issues. The motivation domain could not be examined because items such as intention to vaccinate data were not collected.

Success was defined as a FIC and failure as a partially immunised or zero-dose child. In line with this, BeSD statements were analysed with a positive framing. For statements reflecting positive perceptions about vaccination, agreement was the comparator with neutral/disagreement as the reference category. For negative statements, disagreement was the comparator and neutral/agreement was the reference category. Prevalence ratios (PRs) and 95% CIs were calculated. For caregiver sociodemographic characteristics, most of which have more than two categories, these values were tabulated. For BeSD statements, these were presented in forest plots.

A secondary analysis was performed on the youngest child in each household to explore caregivers’ beliefs by their most recent vaccination behaviour. The percentage of agreement with positive statements and disagreement with negative statements for each vaccination category (none, partial and FIC) was presented.

Analysis was performed using Stata V.15.1.31

The majority of caregivers were female (97.1%), aged 18–45 years (90.9%), in a live-in or married relationship (90.2%) and Catholic (83.9%). Most were parents (90.0%) and had one to two children (94.7%). Differences across the three regions were noted. The caregivers provided immunisation data for a total of 950 children. We were able to verify 42.4% of the children’s vaccination status using immunisation cards, with the remainder (57.6%) based on primary caregiver report alone. The most common reasons given for not presenting their immunisation cards were lost or forgotten cards and competing priorities. We verified that 91 (13.2%) children who were reported to be fully immunised by their caregivers had incomplete age-appropriate vaccination status on their immunisation cards and that 18 (8.0%) children reported as having incomplete vaccination were fully immunised.

Based on the caregiver reports and/or immunisation records, 618 (65.1%) were fully immunised, 302 (31.8%) were partially vaccinated, 24 (2.5%) were zero-dose, and six (0.6%) had no immunisation data. In Region V, 72.3% of children were fully immunised and there were no zero-dose children reported. In Regions IV-B and VIII, two municipalities had 13.5% (Municipality 1 IV-B) and 6.2% (Municipality 9, VIII) zero-dose children respectively and there was lower FIC coverage, with 58.1% and 65.1% FIC, respectively (online supplemental Appendix B).

In comparing the vaccination status and caregiver characteristics of those with and without immunisation cards, we found that children with unverified data had significantly older, employed and less educated caregivers and were likely from Region VIII. Complete immunisation was also higher among unverified reports (online supplemental Appendix C).

Reasons for complete, incomplete or zero-dose vaccination

Among 618 FIC, the most common reasons given for complete vaccination included the primary caregivers’ perception that vaccines protect the child (72.3%) (thinking and feeling domain), recommendation from HCWs (52.8%) (social processes domain), and vaccines being free of charge (31.1%) and accessibility of vaccination sites (21.0%) (practical issues domain). For 302 children with incomplete vaccination, the three most common barriers were from the thinking and feeling domain, namely, concerns that the child was sick on the day of vaccination (22.5%), perception that the child was too young (10.6%) and fear of acquiring COVID-19 in vaccination sites (5.0%). Common reasons related to practical issues included inaccessibility of vaccination sites (10.9%), vaccine supply (5.3%) and poor service quality (4.3%). Having a home birth (6.3%) and no recommendations from HCWs (3.6%) within the social processes domain were also cited. Among the 24 children with zero dose status, the most common reasons given for non-vaccination were that the child was sick on the day of vaccination (33.3%), having a home birth (8.3%), fear of acquiring COVID-19 at vaccination sites (8.3%), and no recommendations from family, friends, neighbours and others (8.3%).

Sources of vaccine-related information

The preferred sources of vaccine-related information reported by 653 primary caregivers were DOH announcements (72.4%), local government units (LGUs)/health centres and HCWs (54.5%) and mass media outlets (28.5%). Other less common sources of information included social media (11.0%), international health agencies such as the WHO and CDC (10.3%), as well as family and friends (9.8%).

Factors associated with childhood vaccine uptake

Age and religion of caregiver were found to have the largest association with child immunisation status. Older caregivers with ages 46–59 years were more likely to have FIC compared with the youngest cohort (PR 1.29; 95% CI 1.12 to 1.48). Compared with Catholic caregivers, those belonging to other Christian denominations were less likely to have their children fully immunised (PR 0.82; 95% CI 0.69 to 0.97). We found no evidence of an association with other caregiver sociodemographic characteristics and vaccination status of children in their care (table 2).

Among the negative BeSD statements, perception of vaccine safety was found to have the largest association with childhood vaccination uptake. The prevalence of FIC was higher among primary caregivers who disagreed with the statement ‘I worry about the safety of vaccines’ (PR: 1.13; 95% CI 1.03 to 1.25) compared with those who agreed or were neutral to this statement. Caregivers who disagreed with statements about a preference for fewer vaccines being given, getting natural immunity from diseases rather than vaccines and vaccines causing negative side effects also had higher point prevalences compared with those who agreed or were neutral to these statements. Conversely, the point prevalence was lower for caregivers who disagreed with the statements that a child cannot be vaccinated if he/she is sick, illnesses like polio/tuberculosis (TB)/pneumonia are not severe for children and there are too many vaccines given to children, compared with those who agreed or were neutral. We cannot, however, conclude that there is a true difference in the prevalence of FIC between caregivers who disagreed with these statements and those who did not (figure 1b).

Discussion

This study identified the key drivers of low routine childhood vaccine coverage in the Philippines post-COVID-19. We estimated full immunisation coverage for children <5 years across three selected regions in the Philippines to be 65.1%, well below the government target of 95%. The predictors of having an FIC were older age of caregivers (46–59 years), Catholic religion and low concerns about vaccine safety. Protection from VPDs, vaccines being free, recommendations from HCWs and good accessibility of vaccination sites were key parental drivers for complete vaccination. In contrast, intercurrent illness on scheduled immunisation days, inaccessibility of vaccination sites and having a home birth were core drivers for incomplete or non-vaccination. The preferred vaccine information sources included the DOH, HCWs and mass media.

Vaccine coverage in three regions

Our data are comparable to DOH data in recent years, with notable declines during the pandemic.8 10 This may be attributed to negative perceptions of COVID-19 vaccines, fuelled by misinformation on social media and in online anti-vaccination chat groups which gained large followings during the pandemic.32 The pandemic also disrupted vaccine supply33 34 and impacted access by deterring caregivers from using vaccination services and restricting movement or travel.33 Other declines in coverage prior to COVID-19 have occurred mostly due to vaccine safety scares such as the Dengvaxia controversy in 2017,15 where first dose measles coverage declined from 80% to 75% between 2016 and 2018 and precipitated large outbreaks in 2018–2019.35 Reported deaths in children vaccinated with Dengvaxia diminished confidence in the dengue vaccine and other routine childhood vaccines.15

Only 42.4% of children had immunisation cards to verify vaccination status in this study. Results showed that complete immunisation was higher among children with unverified status and there were significant differences in some caregiver characteristics. These imply that caregivers who did not present vaccination cards may have overreported their child’s vaccination status or differed systematically from those who brought records, potentially leading to an overestimation of true immunisation coverage. Such findings underscore the need for digital immunisation records and health information to improve accuracy and inform catch-up of under or non-immunised children. A digital monitoring tool for tracking vaccine coverage, utilisation and wastage was piloted in the Philippines in 2023 and provided high quality data for decision-making and improved vaccine delivery.36 The digitisation of vaccination records is supported by DOH and would enhance reporting systems, facilitate evaluation of vaccine programme performance and development of targeted interventions to improve uptake, supported by robust measurement of the social and behavioural drivers of vaccination.

Determinants of childhood vaccination

We found that the provision of free vaccines, good accessibility of vaccination sites and understanding of the protection offered by routine childhood vaccines underpinned complete vaccination of children, similar to other studies.37 38 As HCWs appear to be trusted sources of information, and recommendations by HCWs are a leading driver for complete immunisation, a key strategy to improve uptake is to build HCW capacity to communicate effectively about vaccines to highlight the benefits and risks of vaccines and address concerns.39 This has occurred in the Philippines with the DOH-led Bakuna Champions programme that trains HCWs at different levels of the health systems, barangay health workers and community members to be advocates for vaccination. The programme has not yet been formally evaluated,40 but HCWs have experienced burnout and trauma during the COVID-19 pandemic. Ongoing capacity building and training of HCWs will require strong DOH support and investment to optimise implementation.41 New strategies were implemented during COVID-19 to minimise disruptions to service delivery and improve patient experiences, but future pandemic planning, preparedness and management is needed to ensure ongoing workforce resilience.41

We found that caregiver belief that children cannot be vaccinated when they are sick with an intercurrent illness and concern about multiple vaccines being given at the same time were drivers of partial vaccination, as reported elsewhere.38 These findings highlight the need for parental education on the safety of vaccination during mild viral illnesses with or without low-grade fever.42 Home birth was also identified as a reason for incomplete or non-vaccination. Despite policies prohibiting home births in the Philippines, these still occur especially among rural women. Several socio-demographic factors, women’s reduced decision-making power,43 as well as limited local knowledge and practices about childbirth44 have been found to influence a woman’s decision to deliver at home and likely contribute to inequalities in accessing maternal and child health services, including vaccination. Thus, children delivered at home are an important group to monitor and may benefit from reminders by HCWs for doses or recall for missed doses.

Access issues were reported as important drivers of under or non-vaccination as found in other studies.45 46 In municipalities in remote areas, residents who live far from the health facilities and have to travel long distances, usually by foot, or pay for transportation, may not be able to access vaccination services due to the added costs. Strategies such as incentivising vaccination, outreach activities of HCWs and integrating vaccination with delivery of other child health programmes/interventions may increase uptake. The quality of the vaccination service, especially friendliness of healthcare centre staff, or supply-related issues also deter caregivers from bringing their children for vaccination. Such avoidance of healthcare services for PHC including immunisation has been one of the unintended effects of the pandemic,34 which persists in present times. Using validated tools, such as the BeSD tools, in low-income settings to accurately identify both access and acceptance barriers to inform tailored and cost-effective solutions as access factors are often overlooked.18 47

We found three predictors of complete immunisation. First, older age (46–59 years) of caregivers was associated with complete immunisation, even though this group was small. This has been found in other studies,48 49 but the association of caregiver’s age with childhood vaccination status varies,12 50 suggesting other factors may be at play. Second, religion was a predictor, as children from other Christian denominations had reduced uptake compared with children of Catholic caregivers. In the Philippines, the Catholic Church has historically supported public health initiatives, including vaccination, especially through partnerships with the DOH during the COVID-19 pandemic,51 compared with some evangelical and fundamentalist Christian groups who have expressed vaccine hesitancy or distrust.52 We did not find other caregiver sociodemographic characteristics to be significant predictors of uptake, although other studies have found that sex, education46 49 and working status49 were significantly associated with routine childhood vaccine uptake. This may be because almost all caregivers in our study were female, with fathers often not coming to vaccine clinics, and thus fathers and other male caregivers were under-represented.53 The lack of association with education highlights that vaccine concerns exist at both ends of the spectrum. Lastly, we found that reduced vaccine safety concerns were associated with complete immunisation, as reported in many other studies.19 54 HCWs play a crucial role in addressing parents’ concerns about vaccine safety and in building and sustaining vaccine confidence. Dialogue-based interventions to support HCW conversations in addressing vaccine safety concerns were effective in other Asia-Pacific countries. In Fiji, training health workers and community influencers to effectively communicate about vaccination promoted vaccine confidence and increased intention to vaccinate.55 Health promotion and education campaigns that emphasise the acceptability and safety of childhood vaccines may be utilised in the Philippines to increase caregivers’ level of knowledge and confidence in vaccines.

Some of our study sites that were near coastal areas or had mountainous terrains may have had more issues with delivery and accessibility of services. Some municipalities with more zero-dose children included indigenous groups who were less supportive of childhood vaccination as well as anti-vaccination groups. Other municipalities had differences in political and logistical support for programme implementation due to devolution of the Philippine health system. There were study sites with more supportive local chief executives (LCEs) and/or bigger healthcare budgets compared with others. In the Philippines, primary responsibility for health service delivery was transferred to LGUs headed by LCEs.56 Thus, political commitment of leaders is crucial to the success of vaccination and other health programmes.

We found that caregivers obtain most of their information about immunisation from credible sources such as government and trained HCWs, similar to results of other studies.57 58 While there was consensus among caregivers that they received enough information, understanding of such information may be low. In this study, social media was a less popular source of information compared with mass media outlets, probably because the caregivers were more familiar with traditional forms of communication such as television, radio and newspapers. Poor internet connectivity in some study sites may have also affected access to social media. In general, however, use of social media and other online platforms has greatly increased during the pandemic and contributed to the ‘infodemic’ or overwhelming volume of mis and disinformation.59 Thus, caregivers who relied on social media platforms were more hesitant to vaccinate their children compared with those who used more scientific sources.59

Strengths and limitations of study

This study had a robust sampling design, aligned with the recommended methodology for conducting vaccination coverage surveys, and included key fieldwork procedures such as house-to-house identification and revisits to non-responders to reduce non-response bias. It also has a large sample size to identify the access and acceptance drivers of vaccination uptake in three low coverage regions. We used an adapted validated survey questionnaire from WHO and added items based on expert opinion and local contextual knowledge. Results and recommendations of the study have already been presented to regional and national DOH offices to inform programme planning.

There were some limitations noted. Since the study sites were selected through ‘consultation’ with DOH to target low-coverage areas rather than through random methods as sampling frames were not available, results cannot be generalised to a broader population. Statements/perceptions pertaining to the practical issues domain were more limited compared with the other two domains. We were able to identify supply and accessibility issues and service quality concerns, but these findings may be biased toward more accessible populations and could underestimate the true extent of inaccessibility among remote households. The inclusion of equity measures such as socioeconomic status, geographic disadvantage and access to services could have provided a lens for measuring disparities in immunisation coverage. An overestimation of complete immunisation coverage and possible selection bias may have also occurred due to missing immunisation card data and caregiver characteristics which differed systematically between those with and without cards. Moreover, respondents who were more motivated or more likely to vaccinate their children may have been more willing to participate, further contributing to selection bias. Since the survey was conducted face to face, social desirability bias may have also been present. The length and extensiveness of the questionnaire may have caused survey fatigue among some caregivers, leading them to answer some questions less accurately. Although every effort to ensure good comprehension of the questions was made, this may have introduced bias in their responses and underestimated the association of other factors with vaccination uptake.

The percentages of agreement and disagreement with statements by caregivers whose youngest child is unvaccinated need to be considered in light of the small number of unvaccinated children. We cannot be as confident about these estimates as we can for the partially and fully vaccinated groups.

Conclusions

This study estimated that only 65.1% of children were fully immunised across three selected regions in the Philippines in 2023, well below the government’s target of 95%. Important drivers of complete vaccination were the perception of the safety and benefits of vaccination for the child, positive HCW recommendations and provision of a funded vaccine programme. Older caregiver age, Catholic religion and low vaccine safety concerns were significant predictors of uptake. Intercurrent illness at the time of vaccination and poor accessibility of vaccination sites were important drivers for under or non-vaccination.

Younger caregivers, those that have a home birth, and those with vaccine safety concerns are appropriate targets for health promotion and education to increase childhood vaccination uptake. Additionally, strengthening the capacity of HCWs in communicating and engaging with caregivers, particularly on safe vaccination during mild intercurrent illness as well as the benefits and risks of vaccines, is needed. A collaborative and multisectoral (eg, health, government and other sectors) approach is needed to address vaccine concerns through HCW capacity building and addressing practical issues such as inaccessibility of vaccination sites, poor supply or service quality. Innovative strategies such as vaccine delivery systems with less complicated logistics, digitisation of vaccination records to ensure timely data collection and analyses alongside understanding of the BeSD of vaccination are needed to inform targeted and cost-effective interventions and bring vaccination services closer to unreached caregivers.60 There is also a need for advocacy, communication and training for health authorities and mass media, whether traditional or online, to ensure accurate information dissemination which supports vaccination and other public health programmes (online supplemental Appendix D).
