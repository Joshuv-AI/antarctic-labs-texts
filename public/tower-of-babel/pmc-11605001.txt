The dynamics of Bovini exploitation strategies on the Central Plains of China from the Middle Neolithic to the Bronze Age
Authors: Xiaochen Pei, Fawei Liang, Yanpeng Cao, Yunan Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBos and Bubalus are important Bovini resources worldwide and were widely exploited on the Central Plains of China during the Neolithic and Bronze Ages. However, distinguishing between Bos and Bubalus remains were challenging due to their similar morphological traits, which leaves the interaction between them poorly understood. This study is the first to combine Zooarchaeology by Mass Spectrometry (ZooMS) with zooarchaeological methods to identify Bos and Bubalus in China at the Tuchengwang (5600–4300 cal. BP), and Pingliangtai (4200–3900 cal. BP) sites. This was accompanied by carbon and nitrogen isotope analyses of bone collagen to discuss their diets on the Central Plains. Our findings indicate that these exploitation strategies are dynamic. Aurochs (Bos primigenius) and water buffaloes (Bubalus mepistopheles) were exploited before the introduction of cattle (Bos taurus), and this exploitation continued into the Bronze Age. The exploitation of local wild Bovini resources may have influenced the adoption of cattle during the End Neolithic. By this time, cattle predominated in Bovini remains at most sites, but the adoption of cattle was not a phenomenon of unison, and their exploitation strategies became more diverse, especially in ritual practices. Wild water buffaloes had evolved into privilege goods by the Bronze Age, especially during the late Shang. Bovini, including Bubalus and Bos, are among the most important animal resources worldwide. In ancient China, Bubalus included wild (Bubalus mepistopheles) and domesticated water buffaloes (Bubalus bubalis), whereas Bos included aurochs (Bos primigenius), cattle (Bos taurus), and zebu (Bos indicus). Wild water buffalo remains, predominantly Bubalus mepistopheles, are widespread in Neolithic and Bronze Age China, especially in rice cultivation areas along the Yangtze River. The domestication of water buffalo in China remains debatable1–3, but the ancient DNA evidence suggests that Bubalus mepistopheles is not the direct ancestor of the modern domesticated water buffalo (Bubalus bubalis)4. Aurochs were primarily distributed in northern China, and their population experienced a rapid decline during the Holocene, ultimately leading to extinction5–7. Zebu, now mainly found in southern China, is thought to have been domesticated in the Indus Valley8,9 and did not spread to central and northern China during the early Bronze Age10. Zooarchaeological and ancient DNA evidence indicate that cattle, initially domesticated in the Fertile Crescent during the Pre-Pottery Neolithic B period11–13, were introduced to the Central Plains of China during the End Neolithic (4300–3900 BP)10,14,15, a key region of social complexity development in China. They quickly adapted to local subsistence16,17, especially in the pig-millet agricultural system on the Central Plains and made significant contributions to the formation of Chinese civilization during and after the Bronze Age. Cattle provided important subsistence resources, bone raw materials, traction for agriculture and transportation, and served important ritual functions, including divination and sacrifice18–20.

Zooarchaeological and ancient DNA evidence show that ancient people exploited different Bovini resources simultaneously at some Neolithic and Bronze Age sites on the Central Plains of China21–25. The coexistence of different Bovini species, mostly Bos and Bubalus, occupying different ecological niches, indicates the complex dynamics of human-animal interactions and the changes in animal populations influenced by cultural and environmental factors. These dynamics can be systematically considered within the structural framework of the “anthroposystem”26,27 to discuss the human-animal-environment interactions and relationships. Identifying Bovini skeletal remains and exploring their diets and natural habitats are helpful in recognizing the diverse utilization strategies of ancient humans and the mechanisms behind the introduction and adoption of cattle in Neolithic China.

However, distinguishing different Bovini remains is challenging due to their similar morphological traits and the limited preservation of bones. As a result, many fragmented bones have conservatively been identified as “large bovine” at many sites21,25. The difficulty in separating water buffalo from other Bovini has also limited the scope of stable isotope analysis, which is routinely used to reconstruct human or animal diets at archaeological sites in China28. On the Central Plains of China, the isotopic data is limited to discussions of local regions29,30 and lack comprehensive comparisons of the diets and living environments of different Bovini species across the region. Consequently, the Bovini exploitation strategies before, during, and after the introduction of cattle remain unclear. Examples include how local Bovini resources were exploited before cattle were introduced, whether this exploitation influenced cattle adoption, whether cattle adoption was a uniform phenomenon across all sites, and whether there were changes in the exploitation strategies of other Bovini species.

ZooMS (Zooarchaeology by Mass Spectrometry) analysis, which is based on variations in the peptide mass fingerprinting of bone collagen for species identification, offers advantages such as lower costs and less stringent sample preservation requirements compared to ancient DNA analysis31. It has been successfully applied to the identification of Bovids in Africa32,33. Bovina and Bubalina can be distinguished using specific peptide markers COL1α2 484–498 (Bovina m/z 1427, Bubalina m/z 1455) and 757–789 (Bovina m/z 3033, Bubalina m/z 3075). Other peptide markers shared by both Bovina and Bubalina are located at m/z 1105, 1192, 1208, 1532, 1580, 1648, 2131, 2792, 2853 and 2869 in the MALDI-TOF -MS data32,33. Therefore, given the low taxonomic richness of Bovini at Neolithic and Bronze Age sites on the Central Plains of China, Bos (within the tribe Bovina) and Bubalus (within the tribe Bubalina) can be successfully identified using collagen fingerprints.

Additionally, based on the identification of Bos and Bubalus remains, the carbon and nitrogen isotopes of bone collagen can be used to estimate the contribution of different foods. Herbivore diets are mainly influenced by two factors: the local vegetation of their habitats and the fodder intentionally supplemented by humans. Most natural plants used for the free grazing of Bovini exhibit C3 photosynthetic pathways in China34,35. However, the proclivities of water buffaloes in wetlands where C4 plants are abundant are responsible for enriched carbon isotope values and elevated nitrogen isotope values in bone collagen36,37. In addition to the influence of natural vegetation on the Central Plains of China during the Neolithic and Bronze Age, human feeding practices introduced domestic C4 cereals (foxtail millet and broomcorn millet) into the diets of cattle. Enrichment in carbon isotopes has been interpreted as a signature of the increasing management of cattle28, though whether water buffaloes were subjected to human husbandry remains understudied based on stable isotope analysis.

Taking advantage of ZooMS analysis for identifying Bovini remains, we first combined zooarchaeological and ZooMS methods to identify and analyze Bovini specimens from two archaeological sites on the Central Plains of China: Tuchengwang (Late Neolithic, 5600 − 4300 cal. BP, Fig. 1:1) and Pingliangtai (End Neolithic, 4200 − 3900 cal. BP, Fig. 1:2). Water buffalo bones were selected for stable carbon and nitrogen isotope analyses to explore their diets. Subsequently, we reviewed published zooarchaeological data from the Central Plains, spanning from the Middle Neolithic to the Bronze Age, to discuss the diversity and dynamics of Bovini exploitation strategies before, during, and after the introduction of cattle. Through these analyses, we aim to gain new insights into ancient human-animal-environment relationships and the folk taxa of Bovini established by ancient people.

Archaeological contexts and research materials

The materials for this study were obtained from two archaeological sites: Tuchengwang and Pingliangtai. Chronologically, the Pingliangtai site was later than Tuchengwang, forming a continuous chronological sequence that witnessed the introduction of cattle into the Central Plains. Geographically, both sites are situated in the eastern part of the Central Plains of China, sharing similar environmental backgrounds, with wetlands distributed around them. The Central Plains of China span the fertile lands of Henan, southern Shanxi, and the Wei River Valley in Shaanxi, a key region for the development of social complexity development in ancient China.

Tuchengwang site

The Tuchengwang site is located in Luohe City, Henan Province, in the central-eastern part of the Central Plains of China, covering an area of approximately 110,000 m2. Accelerator Mass Spectrometry (AMS)14C radiocarbon dating of bones place the occupation of the site between 5600 and 4300 cal. BP, corresponding the Late Neolithic (6000–4300 BP). Archaeological findings indicate the presence of settlement structures characterized by pile-dwelling38. Geographic archaeological surveys suggest that the site was situated near the Ying River, with evidence of ancient wetlands nearby, dating from the early to Middle Holocene, which persisted until approximately 1900 years ago39.

More than 4000 mammal bones were recovered through sieving with 10 mm meshes during excavation. Among these 11 bone and tooth fragments from Bovini remains (Number of Identified Specimens (NISP) = 11) were found at Tuchengwang40. Based on comparison with the reference collection at Peking University, two bone fragments showed closer resemblance to water buffalo than to cattle and were confidently identified as water buffalo. The remaining nine, less well-preserved bone and tooth fragments, which lack distinguishing morphological features, were classified as unidentified Bovini.

Pingliangtai site

Pingliangtai is a Neolithic walled site located in Zhoukou City, Henan Province, in the eastern part of the Central Plains of China. The geographic and climatic context indicates that Pingliangtai was surrounded by seasonally flooded wetlands at the End Neolithic41,42. Most remains excavated from the period (End Neolithic at Pingliangtai), dating to 4200–3900 cal. BP, correspond to the timeframe when cattle were introduced43,44.

During the 2016 and 2019 excavations, more than 10,000 animal bones from the End Neolithic were uncovered through dry sieving with 10 mm meshes at Pingliangtai. Of these 515 bone fragments were identified as Bovini. Over 300 fragments were confidently identified as cattle (Bos taurus) based on their morphological features and measurement data. Additionally, 21 fragments were identified as “Bos taurus?” due to their close resemblance to cattle, but with a lack of absolute certainty. Additionally, two were identified as water buffalo. However, 105 fragments were highly fragmented and lacked sufficient morphological evidence to distinguish between Bos and Bubalus, leading to their identification as unidentified Bovini.

We applied ZooMS to 165 bone, tooth, and horn core fragments that could not be confidently identified as Bos or Bubalus from the Tuchengwang (n = 11) and Pingliangtai (n = 154) sites. Additionally, carbon and nitrogen isotope analyses are performed on bones identified as water buffalo.

Based on the ZooMS identification results, nearly all Bovini bones at Tuchengwang were identified as Bubalina, and six specimens were identified as Bubalina at Pingliangtai (Fig. 2). Bubalus mephistopheles is the most widespread water buffalo species in China entering the Holocene45. Therefore, we identified Bubalina specimens from Tuchengwang and Pingliangtai as Bubalus mephistopheles. Additionally, 132 of the 154 samples analyzed at Pingliangtai were identified as Bovina. Considering the zooarchaeological context of Bovini in China, these could be cattle (Bos taurus), aurochs (Bos primigenius), or zebu (Bos indicus). However, ZooMS does not differentiate between these species. Based on morphological characteristics and osteometric data, the Bovini remains found at Pingliangtai are predominantly cattle (Bos taurus), with no evidence of aurochs, and zebu did not spread into China during this period. Therefore, we infer that the Bovina specimens identified through ZooMS analysis at Pingliangtai were likely primarily cattle.

At Tuchengwang, water buffalo (NISP ≥ 9) accounted for at least 0.42% of the total identified mammal assemblage. These water buffalo bones were mostly found in ash pits, often mixed with refuse from various daily activities. These remains included parts of the skull (two loose teeth), the front limb (a humerus fragment and a metacarpal fragment), the hind limb (a femur fragment, a calcaneus epiphysis), and the foot (a complete first phalanx and two complete third phalanges). The distribution of skeletal elements suggests that full water buffalo carcasses were butchered and consumed on-site. No human modifications have been observed on the bones. Determining the age of death based on bone epiphyseal fusion was relatively imprecise. However, in the absence of teeth found at sites, the epiphyseal fusion condition of the bones can still provide some information about the age of death. Based on the state of long bone epiphyseal fusion at Tuchengwang (Table 2), two bones indicated that the individuals died after 0–1 years, one died after 1–1.5 years, and one died before reaching 3.5 years. If these bones belonged to a single individual, the individual was slaughtered before 3.5 years. However, if they represent different individuals, determining their specific age at death becomes more difficult. Although it is not possible to determine the age of death precisely, considering that water buffalo bones are fragmented and often mixed with other daily refuse, we speculate that water buffalo were used for meat consumption.

Based on morphological and ZooMS identification, we found that ancient people at Pingliangtai exploited both water buffaloes and cattle, with cattle being the dominant species. Cattle accounted for 9.11% (NISP = 515) of the total identified mammals, while water buffaloes accounted for only 0.11% (NISP = 6). These species were frequently found together in the same feature, such as in house middens. The skeletal remains of the water buffalo included the front limb (three metacarpal fragments) and hind limb (one femur fragment, one tibia fragment, and one metatarsal fragment), five of which were selected for bone working. All skeletal parts of the cattle, from head to toe, were present at the site, suggesting that both processing and consumption procedures took place there. The kill-off pattern, based on epiphyseal fusion data ( n = 227) (Table 3) and dental eruption and wear of the mandible (Minium Number of Individuals (MNI) = 9) (Table 4), suggests that a small number of cattle were culled before one year of age. Additionally, bone-working traces were found on 57 bones that were used to produce common artifacts, such as arrowheads. Notably, one cattle scapula was selected for divination, a practice closely associated with royal authority and elite rituals during the Bronze Age21,48.

Stable isotope results

All the measured samples (n = 6) yielded high-quality collagen. The amount of carbon (C%) ranged from 34.34 to 44.32%. The amounts of Nitrogen (N%) ranged from 11.52 to 15.87%. The C: N atomic ratio varied between 3.24 and 3.48. All three indices met the quality control threshold of Vaiglova et al.51, indicating acceptable carbon and nitrogen isotopic results.

The δ13C values ranged from − 12.70‰ to −11.15‰ at Tuchengwang and Pingliangtai. Four of the δ15N values ranged from 5.27‰ to 5.90‰. Two others showed higher δ15N values of 7.46‰ and 7.78‰ (Table 5). Discussion

Water buffalo diets on the Central Plains revealed by isotopic evidence

Modern water buffaloes mainly inhabit swampy areas along rivers and grasslands, concentrated in tropical and subtropical regions56. The Tuchengwang and Pingliangtai sites are situated in the Western Huang-Huai Alluvial Plain in the southeastern part of the Central Plains, a transitional region between subtropical and warm temperate zones. The presence of water buffaloes unearthed from these sites may be related to the Holocene climatic background and the local environment around the sites. The climate was relatively warm during the Holocene Climatic Optimum and vegetation flourished57. Additionally, a recent geoarchaeological drilling program revealed the presence of wetland environments around the Tuchengwang and Pingliangtai sites39,41, that provided suitable habitats for water buffaloes.

Carbon and nitrogen isotopic data from animal bones provide evidence of their diets and living environments. Together with the results of this study, we reviewed the carbon and nitrogen isotopic data of buffalo remains on the Central Plains from the Middle Neolithic to the Bronze Age and compared them with the isotopic results of cattle and herbivores at the same site to reveal their diets and feeding practices of ancient people (Fig. 3). For the Neolithic period in China, the average δ13C values of common cereals, millet and rice, are − 11.3‰ and − 24.9‰, respectively. Wild C3 plants have an average δ13C value of ca. -25‰. Humans and animals that feed on 100% millet- and rice-based foods (or other wild foods) have δ13C values around − 6.3‰ and − 20‰, respectively16.

The δ13C and δ15N values of buffalo from the study sites and the reviewed data of the same site: (a) Study site of the Middle Neolithic Age: Baijia29; (b) Study site of the Late Neolithic Age: Qingtai52; study site of the End Neolithic Age: Kangjia54, Dongying30; (c) Study site of the Neolithic-Bronze Age transition: Xinzhai28,53; (d) Study site of the Early Bronze Age: Yanshishangcheng55.

Generally, similar ranges of isotopic data were observed among individual buffaloes at different time periods. However, discrepancies were observed among different herbivore species. During the Middle and Late Neolithic, the carbon isotopic results of water buffaloes in Baijia (Fig. 1:4) and Qingtai (Fig. 1:5), as well as the data from Tuchengwang in this study, were more enriched than those of cervids. Relatively higher δ15N values were also observed. During the End Neolithic and Neolithic-Bronze Age transition, the carbon isotopic data of water buffalo at Kangjia (Fig. 1:8), Dongying (Fig. 1:9) and Xinzhai (Fig. 1:18), as well as Pingliangtai in this study, distributed between − 16‰ and − 10‰, showing similar range with that of Caprinae and cattle, although three of the cattle specimens have more enriched carbon isotopic values. During the Early Bronze Age at Yanshishangcheng, the carbon isotopic data of the cattle varied between ca. −11‰ and − 6‰; water buffalo and Caprinae have more depleted carbon isotope varying from ca. -15‰ to -12‰.

In previous discussions of water buffalo diets, the enriched carbon isotopic results in Baijia and Kangjia were explained as the intake of wild millet or the diets influenced by humans29,54. Diets supplemented with millets and other C4 weeds (e.g., Setaria viridis), or the grazing of hygrophilous plants, such as sedges (Cyperus, a C4 plant), have been suggested for buffaloes in Dongying30. At Xinzhai, it was explained as the capture of wild buffaloes and intentional feeding with millets for consumption during feasting53; cattle at the same site were also suggested to be fed by large amounts of millet or millet byproducts28.

Comparing the above data and previous studies, we suggest that wetland environments may have been the main factor influencing the isotopic data, considering the relatively enriched carbon isotopic data and elevated nitrogen isotopic data compared to those of the cervids as a natural herbivore baseline. Signatures of the wetland living environment were observed until the End Neolithic. A wetland environment, where C4 plants are dominated by Cyperaceae, was also revealed by pollen records in Henan58. The slightly enriched carbon isotopic results of water buffalo during the Late Neolithic period compared to those of the Middle Neolithic period may be explained by the changing environment of wetlands, where more C4 plants appeared or the intake of terrestrial C4 weeds.

Accompanied by the introduction of cattle and sheep on the Central Plains during the End Neolithic, a wider distribution of carbon isotopic values of the water buffalo could be observed. The coincidence of the isotopic values of water buffalo, cattle, and Caprinae does not rule out the possibility of intentionally human feeding of millets with water buffalo. However, the relatively similar distributions of the isotopic signatures of water buffaloes from the Middle Neolithic to the End Neolithic revealed that their constant diets and stable living environment most likely relied on wetlands. Until the Early Bronze Age, with the increasing management of cattle and Caprinae, and the income of C4 millets revealed by more enriched carbon isotopic values, the carbon isotopic values of the water buffalo remained enriched. In general, although human management of cattle and Caprinae continually increased during the Neolithic and Early Bronze Ages, carbon and nitrogen isotopic data do not support similar water buffalo exploitation strategies. Traditional views in the last century suggested Bubalus mephistopheles as domesticated livestock59,60. By analyzing the skeletal morphology and mortality profile of the buffalo population, recent studies mostly supported that Bubalus mephistopheles is a wild animal1,2. Ancient DNA evidence also shows that native water buffaloes (Bubalus mepistopheles) did not genetically contribute to the later domesticated water buffaloes in China4. Therefore, based on the analysis and comparison of the isotopic data in this study, water buffalo remains found at many sites may have been occasionally hunted by ancient people.

Dynamics of cattle and water buffalo exploitation strategies and human-animal-environment relationships from Middle neolithic to bronze age

To gain a comprehensive understanding of the diverse exploitation strategies of cattle and water buffalo on the Central Plains of China, we review published data spanning from the Middle Neolithic to the Bronze Age and consider them within the structural framework of the “anthroposystem”.

During the Middle (9000–7000 BP) and Late Neolithic (7000 − 4300 BP), Bovini bones labeled as “Bovine”, “Bos sp.” or “Bubalus sp.” in faunal reports can be found at many sites (Fig. 1: 3–10, Table S1), and the proportion (NISP) of Bovini in identified mammal assemblages is low at most sites. However, the proportion of water buffaloes was relatively high in Baijia, and the NISP% in the identified mammal assemblage was over 20%61 (Fig. 1:4, Fig. 3). Additionally, Bos and Bubalus were simultaneously found at some sites (Fig. 1: 3,7,9,10, Table S2) during the Middle and Late Neolithic. Owing to the limited details of the published data and the small number of Bovini remains, it is challenging to obtain more information to discuss their detailed exploitation strategies. Nevertheless, given ancient DNA evidence4 and the analysis of the diet of water buffaloes discussed in the previous section, we suppose that opportunistic hunting of locally available wild Bovini animals is possible, which reflects the intensive interaction between ancient people and local wild Bovini, which may have contributed to the rapid adoption of cattle on the Central Plains during the End Neolithic, especially as they can offer similar products.

Similar to the Middle and Late Neolithic, the coexistence of Bos and Bubalus is commonly observed at the End Neolithic (4300–3900 BP) sites (Fig. 1: 8–9, 12–19, Table S3), indicating that ancient people exploited them simultaneously. However, evidence from zooarchaeological and ancient DNA analyses indicate that Bos were predominantly cattle (Bos taurus), which were domesticated in the Fertile Crescent and spread into the Central Plains during the End Neolithic. They became widespread as soon as they were introduced62, and this did not lead to the disappearance of the exploitation of other Bovini resources, which may be related to the previous exploitation of wild Bos and Bubalus. Cattle and water buffaloes were exploited for meat and bone working, such as the evidenced from Pingliangtai and Zhoujiazhuang (Fig. 1:12). However, there were differences in their exploitation strategies across different sites during this period.

On the one hand, the adoption of cattle was not a phenomenon of unison. Cattle were predominant in the Bovini remains at most sites; however, at Kangjia (Fig. 1:8) and Qiangaozhuang (Fig. 1:13), the proportions of water buffalo exceeded that of cattle (Fig. 4), possibly influenced by the environment and habitus. The environments surrounding Kangjia and Qiangaozhuang are both suitable for water buffalo63,64. Additionally, there was also a relatively high proportion of water buffaloes at Baijia, a Middle Neolithic site adjacent to Kangjia (Fig. 1), suggesting that when new animal resources emerged and similar local animals were still available, people continued to exploit familiar local resources. On the other hand, the exploitation strategies for cattle are more diverse. Only cattle were used for ritual practices, such as the oracle bones made from cattle scapulae unearthed at the Taosi (Fig. 1:11) and Zhoujiazhuang sites, as well as the discovery of complete cattle skeletons at Shantaisi (Fig. 1:14) and Pingliangtai, indicating that cattle were considered ritually significant animal, a phenomenon that was widespread on the Central Plains during the End Neolithic. Interestingly, both the scapulae of cattle and aurochs were used for divination at Zhoujiazhuang, as evidenced by ancient DNA analysis, while water buffaloes were not involved in this ritual practice during the End Neolithic22. This observation implies that ancient people exhibited a deep understanding of different Bovini species, and cattle and aurochs (Bos) were potentially perceived as more closely related than the water buffalo (Bubalus) from their perspective.

The coexistence pattern of Bos and Bubalus observed during the Neolithic continued at Bronze Age sites (Fig. 1: 20–24, Table S4). Further developments have been made in the exploitation strategies for cattle and water buffalo. The exploitation strategies for cattle become more diversified, with clear evidence of their use in traction65,66. Moreover, they continued to play crucial roles in ritual practices such as divination and sacrifice, and their bones became the primary raw material in bone workshops of Bronze Age urban centers18,20,67–70. However, a new phenomenon of water buffaloes emerged during this period, particularly in late Shang. Water buffalo and cattle remains were deposited simultaneously in sacrificial contexts at Yanshishangcheng (Fig. 1:21), and the water buffalo remains unearthed at Yinxu (Fig. 1:24) were primarily concentrated in palace-temple area. Water buffalo hunted by the royal elite were also seen as privilege goods, as evidenced by the oracle bones made from water buffalo ribs71. Additionally, water buffalo images were discovered on bronze, jade, and ceramic artifacts at Yinxu72,73. These findings indicate that water buffaloes were involved in ritual practices and were endowed with political significance during the Bronze Age, likely due to their ferocity as wild animals and the rise of an elite class.

Conclusion

In this study, morphologically unidentified Bovini bones from Tuchengwang (5600 − 4300 cal. BP), and Pingliangtai (4200–3900 cal. BP) on the Central Plains of China were identified using ZooMS analysis, and selected buffalo bones were subjected to stable carbon and nitrogen isotope analyses, enhancing our understanding of Bovini exploitation strategies and the diets of water buffaloes. We integrated published data spanning the Middle Neolithic to the Bronze Age within the structural framework of the “anthroposystem” to gain a deeper understanding of the diversified and dynamic exploitation strategies of cattle and water buffaloes. The results emphasized that wild Bovini resources, such as water buffaloes and aurochs, were exploited before the introduction of cattle, and continued to be exploited during the Bronze Age. The exploitation of local Bovini resource may have contributed to the rapid adoption and differences in the adoption of cattle during the End Neolithic. Nevertheless, cattle predominate in the Bovini remains at most sites, and their exploitation strategies are more diverse, with domestic cattle being more frequently involved in ritual activities than water buffaloes. During the Bronze Age, the exploitation strategy of cattle became more diversified, and water buffaloes were imbued with special significance and became a privilege good, especially during the late Shang.

Our analysis demonstrates that ZooMS provides a new window for understanding the exploitation strategies of animal that are difficult to identify morphologically. ZooMS analysis is expected to be applied more widely in species identification, which can help us gain a deeper understanding of human-animal-environment relationships and the classification of Bovini established by ancient people from an emic perspective.

Methods

ZooMS analysis

Following Buckley et al.31 with slight modifications, approximately 20 mg of bone fragments from each specimen was demineralized overnight with 0.6 M hydrochloric acid. After centrifugation and rinsing with 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate three times, the acid-insoluble fraction was gelatinized by heating at 65 °C in 100 µL 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate for 3 h. After centrifugation, 50 µL of the supernatant was digested with 1 µL of trypsin solution (0.4 µl/µg) and incubated overnight at 37 °C. The supernatant was added with 1 µL of 5% trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) to stop the digestion. The samples were desalted using C18 Ziptip (Millipore ZTC18S096) prepared with a bed volume of 50% acetonitrile (ACN), 0.1% TFA, and 0.1% TFA for desalting. After washing with 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid (TFA), the peptides were eluted with 50% ACN/0.1% TFA.

1 µL solution of each sample was mixed with 1 µL a-cyano-4-hydroxycinnamic acid matrix solution (1% in ACN/H2O 1:1 v/v). After spotting onto the target plate and allowing it to dry, each sample was analyzed at least three times using an AB Sciex 5800 MALDI-TOF/TOF Mass Spectrometer in the reflectron mode with a mass range of 800–3200 m/z. The spectra were calibrated against an adjacent spot containing four peptides (Angiotensin II, M + H+ at m/z 1046.542; subatance P, M + H+ at m/z 1347.735; P14R, M + H+ at m/z 1533.858; and ACTH fragment, M + H+ at m/z 2465.199). The mass spectra were analyzed using mMass spectrometry (V.5.5.0) following the method of Wang et al.74. The peptide markers selected for the identification of Bovini were compared with those reported by Janzen et al.33.

Stable isotope analysis

Specimens identified as water buffaloes from Tuchengwang and Pingliangtai were selected for stable carbon and nitrogen isotope analyses. Collagen was extracted following Ambrose75,76. The bone fragments were first cleaned by ultrasonication. Samples were treated with 0.5 M hydrochloric acid (HCl) for 1–3 days until fully demineralized and spongy. After rinsing with ultrapure water, the demineralized samples were soaked in 1% sodium hydroxide (NaOH) to remove humic contaminants. To prepare the gelatinized collagen, the samples were treated with HCl (pH 2–3) overnight at 95℃. After centrifugation, the supernatant was freeze-dried to obtain lyophilized collagen.

The stable carbon and nitrogen isotope values were measured at the Environmental Stable Isotope Laboratory, Institute of Environment and Sustainable Development of Agriculture, Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences, using an Isoprime 100 IRMS (Elementar, UK) coupled with an Elementar Vario (Elementar, UK), and calibrated with USGS40 (δ13CVPDB = -26.39 ± 0.04‰, δ15Na.i. r = -4.52 ± 0.06‰) and USGS41a (δ13CVPDB = + 36.55 ± 0.08‰, δ15Nair = + 47.55 ± 0.15‰) reference materials. For every 12 samples, a laboratory reference-Gelatin from bovine skin (δ13CVPDB = -14.7 ± 0.2‰; δ15N = 7.0 ± 0.2‰) was inserted for calibration and to monitor stability. The isotope results were analyzed as the ratio of the heavier isotope to the lighter isotope (13C/12 or 15 N/14N) and expressed as ‘δ’ in parts per 1000 or per mil (‰) relative to internationally defined standards for carbon (Vienna Pee Dee Belemnite, VPDB) and air nitrogen. The measurement errors were less than ± 0.2‰ for both δ13C and δ15N values.

Zooarchaeology

All animal remains unearthed at Tuchengwang, and Pingliangtai were recovered using a 10 mm mesh, and no wet sieving was used. The identification work was completed in the laboratory of the School of Archaeology and Museology at Peking University, using modern reference collections from the Zooarchaeological Laboratory at Peking University and published atlases22,77–80. The measurements were performed according to von Den Driesch81, and the pathologies and bone surface modifications (artificial and other marks) were recorded. Age profiles were estimated based on the eruption and wear of mandible teeth and bone epiphyseal fusion, with reference to46,49,50. The NISP and MNI were used to quantify faunal assemblages. By integrating the results of morphological identification and ZooMS analysis, we compared species frequencies, deposit contexts, body part representations, kill-off patterns and modifications to discuss the exploitation strategies of different Bovini species. We reviewed published faunal data from other sites across the Central Plains of China, dating from the Middle Neolithic to the Bronze Age. This broader dataset allowed us to discuss the change and continuity of Bovini exploitation strategies and human-animal-environment relationships over time.
