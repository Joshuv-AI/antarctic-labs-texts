Pest risk modelling for climate-smart vegetable production in East Africa
Authors: Marc Marin Agbodjan, Komi Mensah Agboka, John Odindi, Elfatih Abdel-Rahman, Onisimo Mutanga, Frank Chidawanyika, Chrysantus Tanga, Daniel Munyao Mutyambai, Sevgan Subramanian, Bonoukpoè Mawuko Sokame, Angelita Acebes-Doria
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAgricultural productivity in East Africa is increasingly threatened by climate-sensitive insect pests. This study investigates the occurrence of key vegetable pests: Brevicoryne brassicae, Plutella xylostella, Maruca vitrata, and Liriomyza mik. A comparative species distribution modelling evaluated pest risk projections under climate change. Four variable selection strategies were tested: case 1 (correlation and variance inflation factor [VIF] filtering across all predictors), case 2 (correlation and VIF filtering of bioclimatic variables merged with other predictors), case 3 (filtering based on environmental values at species occurrence point to approximate realized niches), and case 4 (dimensionality reduction using principal component analysis). These strategies were combined with 3 algorithms: generalized linear model, boosted regression tree, and maximum entropy. In total, 640 spatially occurrence records were analyzed with bioclimatic, elevation, normalized difference vegetation index, solar radiation, wind speed, and land use predictors. Future projections were generated under shared socioeconomic pathways, SSP2-4.5 and SSP5-8.5 (2041–2060), using 4 general circulation models. Model performance varied by species and strategy: case 3 performed best for B. brassicae and P. xylostella, case 1 was most effective for L. mik, and case 2 performed best for M. vitrata. Current suitability maps highlighted high risk zones in central and western Kenya, southwestern Uganda, and Rwanda’s northern province. Projections indicated consistent expansions for P. xylostella and L. mik, while B. brassicae and M. vitrata exhibited localized and uncertain shifts. These findings provide decision-ready pest risk maps that can inform climate-smart pest management, including targeted surveillance, resistant crop varieties, and biological control strategies across in East African vegetable systems.Keywords: species distribution modeling, vegetable pest risk mapping, climate change, ensemble modeling

Introduction

Agricultural productivity in sub-Saharan Africa is increasingly threatened by a combination of abiotic and biotic stressors. Insect pests remain among the most critical biotic threats, especially in smallholder farmers that lack access to timely surveillance and predictive pest management tools (FAO 2021). In East Africa, agriculture contributes over 33% to the regional gross domestic product and supports nearly 80% of the region’s population through food, employment, and income (The World Bank 2013, Ouma et al. 2016). Among the horticultural crops, cabbage, kale, amaranths, and cowpea are particularly important for dietary diversity and livelihood support (Tenkouano 2011, Ebert 2014) yet are highly susceptible to attack from both native and invasive insect pests (Okoma et al. 2025).

Four of the most economically damaging insect pests affecting cabbage, kale, and cowpea are the cabbage aphid Brevicoryne brassicae (Hemiptera: Aphididae), the diamondback moth Plutella xylostella (Lepidoptera: Plutellidae), the legume pod borer Maruca vitrata (Lepidoptera: Crambidae), and leaf miners Liriomyza mik (Diptera: Agromyzidae) (Seif and Nyambo 2013, Abdel Galil et al. 2020). These pests have short generation times, high reproductive rates, and broad host ranges, often resulting in rapid infestations and reinfestations (Nematollahi et al. 2014, Abdel Galil et al. 2020). Their economic impacts are severe; for example, P. xylostella alone causes annual yield losses estimated at 31% in Kenya, translating to around US$7.9 million (Seif and Nyambo 2013). Similarly, B. brassicae can induce losses of up to 80%, particularly when damage occurs during critical growth stages, as the aphid also transmits vector for turnip mosaic virus, exacerbating yield and quality reduction (Byamungu et al. 2019, Shonga and Getu 2021). The dynamics of these pests are tied to the ecological suitability of cropping environments, which determines whether their populations can establish, persist, and reach damaging levels. Climate change is expected to alter this suitability through shifts in temperature and rainfall patterns, thereby creating new opportunities for outbreaks. Given the urgency of the threat posed by these insect pests, understanding their spatial distribution under current and future climate conditions is essential for identifying high risk areas to prioritize surveillance and intervention during outbreaks.

Species distribution models (SDMs) are among the most widely used tools for this purpose, as they effectively estimate habitat suitability by linking species occurrence data with environmental predictors (Elith and Leathwick 2009, Franklin 2010). Although climate envelope approaches are sometimes criticized for ignoring nonclimatic factors such as species interactions and evolutionary adaptations, they remain a widely used and valuable tool for projecting spatially explicit pest risk maps that support integrated pest management (IPM), targeted surveillance, and extension planning (Venette et al. 2010, Srivastava et al. 2019). In pest management, SDMs can serve as early warning tools by identifying suitable zones, supporting regional monitoring networks, and guiding proactive interventions such as push–pull technology deployment, biopesticides, and parasitoid release to improve pest control efficacy, whether on-farm or as an area-wide IPM programs. Moreover, SDMs can integrate future climate scenarios, making them invaluable for forecasting pest dynamics under ongoing climate change (Peterson et al. 2011). However, the accuracy, interpretability, and generalizability of SDMs are highly dependent on the selection and preprocessing of input variables, particularly because many models rely on machine learning algorithms that are highly sensitive to parameter tuning and input data structure (Zbinden et al. 2025). Poor variable selection can introduce overfitting, multicollinearity, and ecological misrepresentation, ultimately reducing model reliability when applied across space or time (Dormann et al. 2013, Merow et al. 2014). To address these challenges, several variable selection strategies are commonly applied in SDMs. Correlation filtering and variance inflation factor (VIF) analysis are widely used to remove highly collinear predictors and improve model interpretability. Dimensionality reduction techniques such as principal component analysis (PCA) transform correlated variables into orthogonal components representing major environmental gradients. Other approaches focus on selecting predictors based on environmental conditions at species occurrence locations to approximate the realized ecological niche of the species. For instance, Cobos et al. (2019) systematically assessed heuristic methods for variable selection in ecological niche models and found that different strategies yield significantly different model outputs, particularly under presence-only data constraints. Similarly, Aguirre-Gutiérrez et al. (2013) demonstrated that model performance varies not only with algorithm choice but also with the evaluation metric and the method of variable selection, underscoring the importance of fit-for-purpose approaches. Despite these contributions, few studies have empirically compared variables selection performance in pest risk modeling, particularly within the African context, and even fewer have explicitly assessed the consistency of future habitat projections across multiple general circulation models (GCMs). Additionally, climate projections, which critically shape future habitat suitability estimates, are significantly influenced by the choice of GCMs (Porfirio et al. 2014, Goberville et al. 2015). Within the crop protection context, such risk maps are not only methodological outputs, but actionable tools that strengthen early warning systems, reduce reliance on chemical pesticides, and build resilience against climate-sensitive pests.

This study addresses the urgent need for reliable pest risk maps to support climate-smart pest management in East African vegetable systems. Focusing on 4 key insect pests of East African vegetable production the cabbage aphid (B. brassicae), the diamondback moth (P. xylostella), the legume pod borer (M. vitrata), and the leaf miner (L. mik) this study applies a comparative SDM framework. Specifically, the study aims to evaluate the influence of 4 distinct variable selection strategies on model performance under climate change scenarios, including (i) correlation + dendrogram + VIF filtering applied to the full environmental stack (case 1), (ii) correlation filtering restricted to bioclimatic variables and subsequently merged with other environmental predictors (case 2), (iii) filtering based on environmental values extracted at occurrence points representing the realized niche (case 3), and (iv) dimensionality reduction using PCA (case 4). The study further assesses the consistency and robustness of future habitat suitability projections by comparing outputs across 4 GCMs under 2 shared socioeconomic pathways (SSP2-4.5 and SSP5-8.5) for the mid-century period (2041–2060).

This dual approach highlights how variable selection strategies and differences among climate model projection uncertainty influence pest distribution outcomes, providing spatially explicit insights that support pest monitoring, inform IPM implementation, and guide climate-smart agricultural policy in East Africa.

Materials and Methods

Study Area

The study was focused on Kenya, Uganda, and Rwanda covering a total area of approximately 850,500 km2 (Fig. 1). The region spans a wide range of climatic and environmental zones, from arid and semiarid lowlands to humid highland ecosystems, offering diverse conditions for agriculture and pest dynamics. Kenya features arid and semiarid regions in the north and east, and more humid, agriculturally productive highlands in the central and western zones. Rainfall follows a bimodal pattern, with annual totals ranging from 950 to 3,000 mm. Temperatures vary from 10 °C in the highlands to over 30 °C in the lowlands and coastal areas. These conditions support a variety of crops, including maize, beans, cabbage, kale, and tropical fruits. Uganda lies within a tropical belt, with elevations ranging from 900 to 1,500 m. Annual rainfall ranges from 900 to 2,200 mm, with temperatures typically between 16 and 28 °C. Bimodal rainfall supports crops such as coffee, bananas, maize, and various vegetables, especially in the Lake Victoria basin and southwestern highlands. Rwanda is characterized by a hilly landscape with elevations averaging around 1,500 m. Rainfall ranges from 800 to 1,600 mm annually, and temperatures generally vary between 15 and 27 °C. The western highlands are cooler and wetter, while the eastern lowlands are drier and warmer, with key crops including potatoes, cassava, beans, and leafy vegetables. The region’s climatic variability and mixed cropping systems create a suitable context for modeling insect pest distributions, particularly those affecting vegetable crops under current and future climate conditions.

Geographical occurrence records of four key vegetable pest species across agroclimatic zones in Kenya, Uganda, and Rwanda. Agroclimatic zones were derived from the FAO/IIASA Global Agro-Ecological Zones version 4 (GAEZ v4) data portal.

Data Collection

Species Occurrence Data

To ensure robust ecological inference and reduce model overfitting, species occurrence data were compiled across Africa rather than being limited to Kenya, Uganda, and Rwanda. This wider geographic sampling increases environmental representativeness and enhances model generalizability, especially when projecting into new environmental conditions. The occurrence records for the pest species, B. brassicae (n = 121), P. xylostella (n = 657), M. vitrata (n = 1,041), and L. mik (n = 562), were downloaded from the Global Biodiversity Information Facility (GBIF; https://www.gbif.org) on 22 May 2025 (GBIF 2025). Duplicate records and entries without geographic coordinates were removed, resulting in 2,381 georeferenced points. These were validated using Google Earth to ensure spatial accuracy. To reduce spatial sampling bias, the dataset was rarefied to retain only 1 occurrence per 5 km × 5 km grid cell, matching the resolution of the environmental variables (Banda et al. 2024). After filtering, 640 high-quality occurrence points were retained for modeling, distributed as follows: B. brassicae (n = 37), P. xylostella (n = 234), M. vitrata (n = 272), and L. mik (n = 97).

Environmental Predictor Variables

A total of 25 environmental predictors variables were used to model pest species distribution: 19 bioclimatic variables (BIO1–BIO19), elevation, normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI), wind speed, solar radiation, land use land cover (LULC), and agricultural area. These variables were selected because they capture the key climatic, topographic, and land-use factors known to influence insect pest development, survival, dispersal, and host plant availability. Bioclimatic variables represent temperature and precipitation regimes that strongly regulate insect physiology, phenology, and life cycles (Bale et al. 2002, Deutsch et al. 2008). Elevation accounts for broad environmental gradients associated with climate, habitat structure, and vegetation patterns that influence insect distribution (Korner 2007). NDVI was included as a proxy for vegetation productivity and host plant availability, which are critical determinants of herbivorous insect abundance and distribution (Pettorelli et al. 2005). Wind speed and solar radiation influence insect dispersal capacity, activity levels, and microclimatic conditions affecting development and survival (Chapman et al. 2015). LULC and agricultural area were incorporated to represent cropping patterns and habitat suitability associated with cultivated landscapes where these pests occur (Fahrig et al. 2011, Tscharntke et al. 2012). Together, these predictors provide a comprehensive representation of the environmental conditions shaping pest distributions and are widely used in species distribution modeling studies. All layers were resampled to 5 km × 5 km resolution to match the native scale (∼4.6 km) of the TerraClimate data and ensure consistency after bioclim variables derivation. Processing and clipping to the African extent were done in R using the raster and terra packages.

Bioclimatic variables

The 19 bioclimatic variables were derived from monthly temperature and precipitation layers using the TerraClimate dataset (Abatzoglou et al. 2018), which provides high-resolution (∼4.6 km) global climate data from 1958 to 2024. These variables were computed in Python (v3.12) using the “rioxarray” package (v0.16.0), following standard formulas based on monthly averages, extremes, and seasonality to capture biologically meaningful climate gradients (Booth et al. 2014). For future projections, the bioclimatic variables were sourced from WorldClim v2.1 (Fick and Hijmans 2017), covering the mid-century period (2041–2060) under SSP2-4.5 and SSP5-8.5 scenarios for 4 GCMs: HadGEM3-GC31-LL, EC-Earth3-Veg, INM-CM5-0, and UKESM1-0-LL. These models were chosen based on their validated historical performance in reproducing observed climate patterns and their robust representation of climate variability within East Africa, as recommended by Omay et al. (2023) and Pickler and Mölg (2021).

Elevation

Elevation data were obtained from the Shuttle Radar Topography Mission at 30 m resolution and resampled to 5 km. Elevation plays a critical role in shaping local climatic gradients including temperature, precipitation, and vegetation patterns which directly influence pest development, reproduction, and host plant availability (Farr et al. 2001).

Normalized difference vegetation index

Vegetation greenness was represented using the NDVI, sourced from MODIS MOD13Q1 data (Didan 2015) and resampled from 250 m to 5 km. NDVI captures vegetation density and crop health, providing an indirect indicator of host plant availability.

Wind speed

Monthly average wind speed data were obtained from the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts (Haiden et al. 2021). Wind influences pest dispersal, especially for mobile or airborne species.

Solar radiation

Surface solar radiation was acquired from the NASA POWER project (NASA 2013) at 0.5° (∼50 km) spatial resolution, representing incoming shortwave energy that affects microclimate and pest metabolic rates.

Land use land cover

LULC data were incorporated during the modeling phase to represent natural, agricultural, and developed land classes. These data help capture anthropogenic landscape modifications that affect habitat suitability for pest species. LULC layers were derived from the MODIS MCD12Q1 product (Friedl and Sulla-Menashe 2019), originally at 500 m resolution and resampled to 5 km × 5 km for consistency with other predictors.

Agricultural area

A continental-scale agricultural area map from the Soil4Africa project (https://www.soils4africa-h2020.eu/s4a-maps-agricultural-land-in-africa) was used postodeling to refine predictions. It enabled binary classification of pest presence in cultivated and noncultivated areas, helping identify agricultural zones at risk.

Species distribution model accuracy is significantly influenced by selection of appropriate predictor variables. These strategies reflect common practices in SDM workflows but differ in how they address background correlation structures, ecological realism, and multicollinearity.

Species Distribution Modeling and Ensemble Classification

Species distribution modeling was performed for each insect pest species using the “sdm” package in R (Naimi and Araújo 2016), with species presence and randomly generated background (pseudoabsence) points. The same pseudoabsence sampling strategy was applied across all algorithms to ensure comparability among models and avoid introducing algorithm-specific variability in pseudoabsence selection during ensemble construction, while acknowledging that pseudoabsence number and selection strategy can influence SDM performance (Barbet-Massin et al. 2012). Three algorithms were selected for the modeling, boosted regression trees (BRT), maximum entropy (MaxEnt), and generalized linear models (GLMs). These methods were chosen for their strong performance and complementarity in ecological modeling. BRT captures complex nonlinear interactions (Elith et al. 2008), MaxEnt is effective with presence-only data (Phillips et al. 2017), and GLM offers a robust statistical baseline (Guisan et al. 2002). Each model was trained using 5-fold cross-validation repeated 3 times. Ensemble predictions were then generated using a true skill statistics (TSS) weighted approach, where only models with TSS >0.7 were retained. This threshold represents a commonly accepted level of model performance in ecological niche modeling, indicating acceptable discrimination capacity between presences and absences (Allouche et al. 2006). In the ensemble construction, each algorithm contributed proportionally to its performance, with TSS scores serving as ensemble weights rather than equal averaging across models. The output of this ensemble process was a continuous suitability map for each species and variable selection case.

To ensure a robust and ecologically meaningful classification, continuous ensemble predictions were converted into binary presence-absence maps using a threshold that maximize the sum of sensitivity and specificity, consistent with the TSS criterion. This approach provides a balanced tradeoff between omission and commission errors and is recommended over arbitrary or prevalence-based methods (Liu et al. 2016). Model performance was evaluated; models were assessed using both discrimination and calibration metrics. Discrimination was evaluated using area under the curve (AUC), interpreted cautiously due to its limitations under presence-only conditions. Calibration was assessed using prevalence or deviance with lower values indicating better fit. Additional classification metrics, including accuracy, balanced accuracy, and Cohen’s Kappa were derived from confusion matrices based on binary maps. To maintain consistency across temporal projections, the same TSS-derived threshold for each species and variable selection case was applied to future suitability maps. Binary maps also formed the basis for subsequent range shift maps.

To ensure rigorous and transparent model evaluation, empirically supported thresholds from the SDM literature were applied to interpret performance metrics and guide final model selection:

AUC values between 0.8 and 0.98 were considered indicative of strong discriminatory power without overfitting. Values greater or equal to 0.99 were treated as potential signs of model overfitting in presence-only context (Araújo and New 2007, Merow et al. 2014, Radosavljevic and Anderson 2014).

The final modeling case for each pest species was selected by prioritizing models that exhibited AUC within the acceptable range but not exceeding 0.99, had low deviance, and scored highest on classification metrics, particularly balanced accuracy and Kappa. This multimetric approach aligns with best practices in SDM evaluation, which advocate combining ensemble accuracy, calibration and classification skill rather than relying on AUC alone (Lobo et al. 2008, Qiao et al. 2015). Finally, all binary map outputs were masked using the agricultural area layer to restrict predictions to cultivated zones.

Future Projections, Model Agreement, and Range Dynamics

Future habitat suitability maps were generated for each species by projecting the selected ensemble models (as determined in the previous section) into mid-century (2041–2060) climate conditions under SSP2-4.5 and SSP5-8.5 scenarios. These 2 scenarios were selected because they represent intermediate and high greenhouse gas emission pathways, allowing the assessment of pest distribution under contrasting climate change trajectories commonly used in climate impact studies (IPCC, 2023). Each scenario was run across 4 GCMs, resulting in a set of continuous suitability maps for every combination of species, GCMs, and SSP. Each projection was thresholded into binary maps using the same TSS-optimized from the current suitability models, ensuring consistency in classification across temporal periods.

Model uncertainty and convergence were assessed, by calculating a GCM agreement score for each grid cell under each SSP, ranging from 0/4 (no agreement among GCMs) to 4/4 (complete agreement). Agreement was then classified into 3 robustness levels: 0/4 or 4/4 GCM highly robust (100% agreement), 1/4 and 3/4 GCM agreement was classified as moderately robust (75% agreement), and 2/4 GCM agreement indicated uncertainty (50% agreement). These robust maps highlight areas of high confidence in future predictions versus areas of high intermodel uncertainty where GCMs showed divergence. In addition, after independent evaluation each SSP, a combined consensus suitability map was produced by overlaying the final binary suitability maps from SSP2-4.5 and SSP5-8.5. This resulted in a 3-class classification per species: suitable (suitable in both SSPs), unsuitable (unsuitable in both SSPs), and uncertain (suitable in 1 SSP but unsuitable in the other). This SSP-based consensus map provides a more nuanced view of future suitability by capturing scenario-related uncertainty.

Finally, range dynamics were computed by comparing the current binary habitat suitability maps with the SSP-based consensus map. This comparison allowed for the identification of areas of habitat gain (currently unsuitable but projected to become suitable), habitat loss (currently suitable but projected to become unsuitable), stable habitat (areas consistently suitable or unsuitable across both periods), and uncertain transitions (mixed outcomes across SSPs). These spatial analyses provided insights into potential range expansions, contractions, and areas requiring adaptive management under climate change scenarios. All these analyses were made using the “raster” and “terra” package in R (Hijmans 2025). The overall workflow is presented in Fig. 2 and was designed to compare variable selection strategies, identify the best-performing SDMs based on predictive accuracy, and predict pest habitat suitability under current and future climate scenarios.

The performance of species distribution models differed across the 4 variable selection strategies, as reflected in variations in AUC, deviance, balanced accuracy, and Kappa values among the modeling cases, revealing species-specific responses to predictor configurations (Fig. 3). For B. brassicae, case 3, which employed realized niche-based filtering, delivered the highest ensemble AUC (0.956), balanced accuracy (0.896), and Kappa (0.151), indicating strong model reliability and ecological relevance. Although case 4 showed marginally higher accuracy (0.865), its higher deviance (0.357) suggested weaker model calibration (Fig. 3A). P. xylostella also performed best under case 3, achieving a balanced accuracy of 0.945 and an acceptable AUC of 0.978, while the perfect AUC of 1.000 in case 1 raised concerns of overfitting (Fig. 3B). In the case of L. mik, case 1 yielded the most robust results, with an AUC of 0.978, low deviance (0.148), and high balanced accuracy (0.930), whereas case 2, despite its high accuracy, suffered from severe overfitting (deviance = 1.282) (Fig. 3C). For M. vitrata, case 2 provided the best overall performance, combining a high AUC (0.933), low deviance (0.193), excellent accuracy (0.938), and the highest Kappa value (0.354), reflecting a well-balanced model (Fig. 3D).

Current Habitat Suitability of 4 Insect Species

The spatial patterns of current habitat suitability differed among the 4 pest species, with some species showing broad continuous suitability gradients and others exhibiting more localized or fragmented suitable areas (Fig. S1). B. brassicae, P. xylostella, and L. mik exhibited broad continuous gradients of low to high suitability (0.2–0.8), predominantly concentrated in central and western Kenya, which corresponded with large zones classified as suitable in the binary maps (Fig. 4), using the best-performing variable selection strategy identified for each species in the previous section. These species displayed minimal suitability across most of Uganda and Rwanda, where only fragmented patches were identified as suitable. In contrast, M. vitrata showed more localized and scattered areas of low to high suitability (0.2–0.8), especially along western Uganda and Rwanda, and also the Kenyan coast, reflecting in its binary classification. Unsuitable zones dominated the eastern and northeastern regions across all 4 species.

Future Agreement and Robustness Under SSP2-4.5 and SSP5-8.5

Projected agreement among the 4 GCMs under the SSP2-4.5 scenario revealed consistent spatial consensus under both SSP2-4.5 and SSP5-8.5 scenarios (Figs S2 and S3). B. brassicae displayed suitability areas with high consensus to full agreement (3/4 to 4/4 GCMs) predominantly across central and western Kenya, northern and eastern Uganda, and most of Rwanda. Unsuitable zones appeared in western and central Uganda as well as in eastern and coastal Kenya, while uncertain areas (2/4 GCMs) were fragmented in Kenya and more widespread in eastern Uganda. P. xylostella showed strong GCM convergence, with nearly complete agreement (4/4 models) across central and western Kenya, southern Uganda, and eastern Rwanda. M. vitrata exhibited a more fragmented pattern, with high (4/4) to moderate (3/4) model consensus concentrated in western and southern Uganda, nearly all of Rwanda, and scattered zones in central Kenya. A wide swath of low agreement (1/4) extended from central Kenya to the coastal region. In contrast, L. mik presented broad regions of strong consensus across central and western Kenya and much of Rwanda, while large areas of Uganda were marked by model uncertainty and low agreement (1/4, 2/4 GCMs) under SSP2-4.5 but uncertain and high agreement (2/4, 3/4) under SSP5-8.5

Model robustness under future projections (Figs S4 and S5) largely mirrored the spatial patterns observed in the agreement maps. P. xylostella showed high robustness (100% GCM agreement) across most of its suitable and unsuitable zones, indicating strong model consistency. M. vitrata had relatively small pockets of low robustness, mostly concentrated in western Uganda and parts of central Kenya. In contrast, L. mik and B. brassicae displayed more heterogeneous patterns, with large portions of Uganda and central Kenya showing moderate (75%) and low (50%) robustness, reflecting model uncertainty to moderate robustness in these areas.

Final Consensus and Range Shift Under SSP2-4.5 and SSP5-8.5

The final consensus binary maps (Figs S6 and S7) illustrate the spatial distribution of areas predicted as suitable and unsuitable for each pest species under both SSP2-4.5 and SSP5-8.5 scenarios. The predicted suitable areas remain relatively consistent across the 2 scenarios for all species, with minor spatial expansions or contractions observed. Discussion

This study contributes to the methodological and applied discourse on climate-driven pest risk modeling in East Africa by demonstrating that variable selection and ensemble modeling critically influence the reliability, ecological realism, and transferability of SDMs. The findings offer both methodological advancements and spatially explicit evidence that can inform surveillance systems, adaptive management strategies and policy decisions aimed at building climate-resilient agricultural systems.

Methodological insights: Variable selection strategies and ensemble modelling

The comparative assessment revealed that no single variable selection strategy universally optimizes SDM performance across species. Each approach presented tradeoffs between statistical rigor, ecological interpretability, and predictive transferability. Realized niche filtering (case 3) generated the most reliable predictions for B. brassicae and P. xylostella, benefiting from its ability to match predictors closely to known species niches. However, this method risks underestimating potential distributions when important predictors are absent from the occurrence dataset, which may constrain future projections. Bioclimatic filtering (case 2) and the full environmental stack (case 1) yielded the strongest performance for M. vitrata and L. mik, respectively. While these strategies allowed inclusion of biologically relevant predictors, they carried a higher risk of multicollinearity, particularly when applied to sensitive algorithms such as GLMs. PCA (case 4) effectively reduced multicollinearity and data dimensionality but occasionally compromised ecological interpretability, as principal components tend to represent statistical variance rather than biologically meaningful gradients (Aguirre-Gutiérrez et al. 2013, Zurell et al. 2020). These strategies reflect deeper philosophical divisions in SDM, whether to prioritize statistical neatness or ecological nuance. The findings align with Dormann et al. (2013) and Merow et al. (2014), who argued that poor variable selection can lead to overfitting, multicollinearity, or ecological misrepresentation. Additionally, Cobos et al. (2019) showed that different selection methods yield significantly different SDM outputs, particularly under presence-only constraints.

The use of an ensemble framework integrating MaxEnt, GLM, and BRT further enhanced predictive robustness by leveraging complementary modeling strengths. MaxEnt handled larger predictor sets well, aided by regularization techniques, and delivered strong fits for species with broader ecological ranges. GLMs, while statistically interpretable, required prefiltered variables due to their sensitivity to collinearity, but provided stable prediction where species-environment relationships were clearly defined. BRTs captured nonlinear patterns and variable interactions effectively, with regularization mitigating overfitting risks and allowing nuanced delineation of niche boundaries. By integrating these modeling philosophies Maxent’s machine learning, GLM’s parametric precision, and BRT’s decision-tree complexity the ensemble produced predictions that were both ecologically realistic, and statistically robust, particularly when coupled with case-specific predictor sets. Algorithmic disagreements across models served as internal uncertainty checks, identifying regions requiring cautious interpretation or field validation (Araújo and New 2007, Qiao et al. 2015). In high agreement zones, predictions were deemed more trustworthy. This aligns with recommendations from Marmion et al. (2009), who emphasized algorithmic complementarity in improving SDM performance.

Overall, this framework demonstrates that SDM quality depends not only on data quality or algorithm choice but also critically on variable preselection logic, algorithm-ecology fit, and the ensemble design used to balance overfitting and generalizability. The comparative framework contributes practical guidance on how variable filtering, species-specificity, and dimensionality reduction interact with model architecture to shape pest risk outcomes.

Current and future habitat suitability across East Africa

Under current climate conditions, habitat suitability patterns for the 4 pests aligned with known agroclimatic zones and host crop distributions in Kenya, Uganda, and Rwanda. P. xylostella and B. brassicae were most suitable in cool, high-altitude areas such as Kenya’s central highlands (Nyeri, Kericho), southwestern Uganda, and Rwanda’s Northern Province. These areas are dominant in brassica cultivation, which supports these pests’ lifecycle under moderate temperatures and adequate moisture (Furlong et al. 2013). Conversely, L. mik and M. vitrata showed broader lowland and mid-altitude suitability. Liriomyza wide distribution corresponds to its generalist, polyphagous feeding strategy, while M. vitrata favored warm, humid legume-growing zones (Foba et al. 2015, Rodrıguez-Castañeda et al. 2017, Srinivasan et al. 2021), especially around Lake Victoria and parts of eastern Uganda and southern Rwanda. These baseline patterns highlight the need for locally adapted monitoring systems that reflect crop distribution and pest biology.

Future projections under SSP2-4.5 and SSP5-8.5 revealed species-specific responses to climate change. P. xylostella displayed consistent upward and latitudinal expansion into highland areas, indicating that zones currently marginal may soon face regular infestations. This suggests that brassica farmers in traditionally cooler zones, such as central Kenya and Uganda’s highlands, will become increasingly exposed, underscoring the urgency of resistant cultivars and early warning systems. In Rwanda, the Eastern and Southern provinces showed over 7,500 km2 each of full gain, while Uganda’s Western and Northern regions recorded over 15,000 km2. Kenya’s Samburu, Laikipia, and Baringo counties all surpassed 4,000 km2 of full gain suitable area. These changes are attributed to warming temperatures rendering cooler zones climatically suitable, enabling the diamondback moth to colonize previously marginal areas. L. mik exhibited the most widespread expansion across all 3 countries. Rwanda’s Eastern Province had over 10,000 km2 of full gain, and every region in Uganda showed >20,000 km2 expansion. Kenya’s Kitui, Taita Taveta, Narok, and Kajiado counties all exceeded 10,000 km2 of gain, with negligible loss or uncertainty. This robust signal, consistent across all models and both scenarios, underscores the pest’s high climate adaptability and capacity for widespread encroachment (FAO 2008), needing priority inclusion in regional IPM programs and long-term monitoring networks. B. brassicae showed more heterogeneous projections. In Uganda, the Northern region showed extreme uncertainty (∼40,000 km2), while gain and loss co-occurred in Kenya’s highland and midland counties. Moderate full gain was observed in Samburu and Kajiado (∼3,000 km2 each), but complete loss appeared in Kericho, Embu, and Nandi. These findings align with the aphid’s sensitivity to heat extremes and moisture variability (Soh et al. 2018). M. vitrata presented the most scenario-dependent patterns. While full gain occurred in Rwanda’s Eastern and Southern provinces (∼2,500 km2) and select Kenyan counties (Kwale, Kilifi, Nyandarua, Machakos), substantial uncertainty dominated in Uganda’s Western/Central regions and parts of Kenya (Nakuru, Taita Taveta). Losses were concentrated in highland counties like Muranga and Nyeri. These reflect the pest’s dependency on flowering legumes and specific rainfall timing (Ba et al. 2019), suggesting that future risks will be highly localized and closely linked to cropping calendars.

By applying both multi-GCM and dual-scenario consensus mapping, the results identified robust expansion zones and high-uncertainty regions (Dale et al. 2017). For P. xylostella and L. mik, range expansion was consistent across GCMs and SSPs, indicating strong confidence in increased pest pressure (Ma et al. 2021). For B. brassicae and M. vitrata, responses were more variable, suggesting vulnerability to rainfall shifts, crop phenology changes, and heat thresholds (Jayabal and Kennedy 2021). Overall, these projections strengthen the availability of reliable pest risk maps for East African vegetable systems. By clearly distinguishing expansion zones from uncertainty hotspots, the results provide a basis for targeted surveillance, resource prioritization, and adaptive management. High-confidence expansion zones, such as the western Kenyan highlands and southwestern Uganda, highlight areas where proactive measures ranging from early warning systems to resistant crop varieties and biological control should be scaled up. In addition, these spatial projections can support climate-smart pest management interventions, including the strategic deployment of biopesticides and parasitoid-based biological control programs. Identifying areas of emerging or persistent suitability allows pest management agencies and extension services to prioritize regions where preventive biological control releases and environmentally friendly pest control technologies may be most effective. For example, parasitoid releases have proven effective in regulating populations of key vegetable pests such as P. xylostella, while microbial biopesticides (eg Bacillus thuringiensis) are widely used in integrated pest management programs targeting brassica pests (Furlong et al. 2013, Srinivasan et al. 2021). Integrating SDM-based risk maps with monitoring networks and extension systems could therefore improve the spatial targeting of biological control and biopesticide distribution, enhancing the effectiveness of climate-smart pest management strategies across East African vegetable production systems. Conversely, uncertainty hotspots in regions like northern Uganda and central Kenya indicate where flexible monitoring frameworks are most critical. In this way, the spatially explicit maps serve as decision-support tools that align directly with the goal of advancing climate-smart pest management and safeguarding farmer livelihoods under changing climatic conditions.

Limitations and future research

Despite methodological rigor, some limitations persist. First, species presence data sourced from GBIF are subject to sampling bias, while spatial thinning mitigated autocorrelation, true absences and sampling effort remain uneven. Second, we treated land use and cropping patterns as static, which may not reflect future agricultural expansion or shifts in crop types that influence pest dynamics. Third, correlative SDMs lack mechanistic components such as pest phenology, dispersal, and interactions with natural enemies or competitors, which constrain their predictive realism.

Future research should integrate dynamic crop distribution, pest lifecycle parameters, and farmer adaptation practices. Ground validation and participatory risk assessments with farmers and extension systems are also crucial for translating these projections into actionable planning tools. Finally, quantifying the economic implications of range shifts would enhance their relevance for agricultural policy and climate adaptation investment.

Conclusion

This study presents a comprehensive assessment of the current and future spatial distributions of four vegetable pests, B. brassicae, P. xylostella, M. vitrata, and L. mik in East Africa under climate change scenarios. By comparing 4 variable selection strategies and employing ensemble modeling across multiple GCMs, the research highlights the critical influence of predictor choice on model accuracy and ecological relevance. The findings show that no single variable selection method optimizes model performance across species, reinforcing the importance of context-specific strategies to improve SDM robustness. Spatial outputs revealed species-specific habitat suitability patterns, with P. xylostella and L. mik projected to expand under future conditions, while B. brassicae and M. vitrata exhibited more localized and uncertain shifts. By providing decision-ready risk maps aligned with integrated pest management, this study contributes directly to climate-smart pest management strategies in East African vegetable systems, offering a pathway to reduce yield losses and strengthen farmer resilience under climate change. The study also underscores the need for adaptive planning to accommodate uncertainty, particularly in regions with inconsistent model agreement. Future research should focus on validating model outputs with field data, integrating pest phenology and socioeconomic vulnerability, and accounting for dynamic land use and crop phenology. Overall, this research supports more informed pest risk management and strengthens the evidence base for agricultural resilience in the face of climate change.
