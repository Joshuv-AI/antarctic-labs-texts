SUMOylation in cancer: molecular mechanisms and therapeutic implications
Authors: Yu Ding, Fei Li, Jia-Mei Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Small Ubiquitin-like Modifier modification (SUMOylation), a critical post-translational modification, plays a dual role in cancer by regulating both tumor cell-intrinsic properties and the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME). Based on a synthesis of direct mechanistic, functional/preclinical, and correlative evidence, it promotes immune evasion through multiple mechanisms: suppressing antigen presentation, limiting immunopeptidome diversity, regulating immune checkpoint molecules (with direct evidence for PD-L1 and TIGIT, while support for other checkpoints remains indirect), and modulating immune cell differentiation and function. Pharmacological inhibition of SUMOylation (e.g., TAK-981, ML792) reverses these immunosuppressive effects by restoring antigen presentation, activating type I interferon responses, enhancing CD8 + T cell cytotoxicity, and reducing regulatory T cells. Notably, while prospective validation is still lacking, SUMOylation-related gene signatures hold promise as prognostic markers and correlative candidate indicators of immunotherapy response. Furthermore, preclinical models demonstrate that SUMOylation inhibitors synergize with immune checkpoint blockade to enhance anti-tumor efficacy, although this synergistic effect has not yet been validated in clinical settings. This review consolidates current knowledge on the immunologic and translational dimensions of SUMOylation—from molecular mechanisms to therapeutic implications—into a single narrative focused on how SUMOylation shapes the TIME and its implications for cancer immunotherapy.

Introduction

Small Ubiquitin-like Modifier modification (SUMOylation) is a critical post-translational modification (PTM) process involving the covalent attachment of SUMO proteins to lysine residues of target proteins via an E1, E2, E3 enzymatic cascade [1, 2]. This modification plays a central role in maintaining normal cellular functions by regulating protein localization, stability, activity, and interactions, encompassing fundamental biological processes such as chromatin organization, DNA repair, transcriptional regulation, signal transduction, and protein trafficking [3, 4]. Its dynamic equilibrium is crucial for cellular homeostasis, and dysregulation of this pathway has been firmly linked to the pathogenesis and progression of various diseases, including cancer [1–3].

SUMOylation exhibits a dual role in cancer: on one hand, its aberrant activation can promote tumor cell progression by activating key oncogenic pathways and reprogramming metabolic processes [5–7]. On the other hand, SUMOylation remodels the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) to mediate immune evasion [3, 4, 7, 8]. This dual regulation of both intrinsic tumor cell properties and the TIME underscores the complex role of SUMOylation in malignant progression. In light of these findings, SUMOylation inhibition holds promise as a candidate combination strategy with cancer immunotherapy. Here, we provide a narrative review that consolidates the immunologic and translational dimensions of SUMOylation focused on how SUMOylation shapes the TIME and its implications for cancer immunotherapy, covering molecular mechanisms, therapeutic strategies, current challenges, and future directions.

Literature search strategy is as follows: to assemble the evidence base for this narrative review, we searched PubMed and Web of Science databases up to April 2026 using combinations of the following search terms: “SUMOylation” OR “SUMO” OR “TAK-981” OR “subasumstat” OR “ML792” in combination with “cancer” OR “tumor immunity” OR “immune checkpoint” OR “tumor microenvironment” OR “biomarker” OR “immunotherapy”. We prioritized peer-reviewed original research articles and authoritative reviews published in English. Given the relatively young age of the immuno-oncology field of SUMOylation, we included all relevant studies that provided direct mechanistic evidence (e.g., identification of SUMOylated substrates or modification sites), functional/preclinical evidence using SUMO pathway inhibitors, or correlative biomarker analyses from clinical cohorts. Reference lists of retrieved articles were hand-screened to identify additional relevant studies. While this review is not systematic, we aimed to provide comprehensive coverage of the key discoveries through April 2026, with particular emphasis on studies that explicitly linked SUMOylation to immune regulation in cancer. Furthermore, considering the heterogeneity across different levels of evidence, we have indicated the level and type of evidence in each paragraph/finding below, so that readers can interpret these findings/data correctly.

Molecular mechanisms of SUMOylation in regulating the TIME

As a key post-translational modification, SUMOylation exerts multi-layered regulatory effects within the TIME through the covalent attachment of SUMO proteins to lysine residues on target proteins. Regulation of immune checkpoint by SUMOylation

SUMOylation modulates the stability, trafficking, and function of various immune checkpoints, thereby influencing immune evasion and immunotherapy resistance. A recent review article has comprehensively summarized the regulatory roles of post-translational modifications (PTMs) on immune checkpoint. However, it detailed SUMOylation modification sites only for PD-L1 and TIGIT. For other immune checkpoints (such as TIM-3, CTLA-4, VISTA, etc.), the regulation was primarily described through other PTM mechanisms. This suggests that the regulatory role of SUMOylation in other immune checkpoints requires further validation [9].

- Pharmacological inhibition of SUMOylation (e.g., using TAK-981) can restore MHC-I APM expression, enhance tumor cell susceptibility to CD8 + T cells, and improve therapeutic response to immune checkpoint blockades (ICBs) [8, 10, 11]. More specifically, MYC-induced SUMOylation of SAFB suppresses the MHC-I/APM pathway [8]. This is direct mechanistic evidence. However, the specific molecular mechanism linking SUMOylation of SAFB to MHC-I inhibition has not been clearly studied.

Regulation of cytokine signaling pathways by SUMOylation

SUMOylation modulates cytokine-mediated immune responses by modifying key proteins in signaling pathways, which are all validated by direct mechanistic evidence.

- Interferon Signaling: In myeloid cells, SUMOylation, in concert with MORC3, maintains heterochromatin to basally suppress IFNB1 expression; inhibiting SUMOylation triggers IFN-I-dependent anti-tumor immune activation, involving three-dimensional genome remodeling and alterations in histone modifications [12]. In T cells, the SUMO-activating enzyme inhibitor TAK-981 enhances type I interferon responses and promotes IFNγ secretion [13].

- NF-κB and JAK-STAT Pathways: SUMOylation influences inflammatory cytokine production and immune cell activation by modifying components of signaling axes such as NF-κB and JAK-STAT [14, 15].

Impact of SUMOylation on immune cell function and differentiation

SUMOylation reshapes the cellular composition of the TIME by regulating immune cell development, activation, and differentiation status, which are also supported by functional/preclinical evidence from pathway inhibition.

- T Cell Function: Inhibiting SUMOylation sustains T cell activation (upregulating CD69, CD38), reduces regulatory T (Treg) cell differentiation, and enhances the cytotoxic function of CD8 + T cells (e.g., IFNγ secretion) [13]. In a chronic lymphocytic leukemia model, TAK-981 boosts the anti-tumor activity of both CD4 + and CD8 + T cells [13].

- Depleting Treg cells: In pancreatic cancer models, TAK-981 improves survival through a T cell-dependent mechanism, with single-cell transcriptomic analysis revealing the depletion of Treg cells within the TIME [16].

- Inhibiting Immune Cell Infiltration: The molecular mechanisms by which SUMOylation drives immune cell infiltration are multifaceted. SUMOylation within tumor cells suppresses the type I interferon pathway, thereby limiting the recruitment of effector T cells [17]. Activated SUMOylation could also suppress MHC-I mediated antigen presentation, thereby reducing immune cell infiltration [11]. Pharmacologic inhibition of SUMOylation relieves these constraints, leading to increased infiltration of CD8 + T cells, B cells and NK cells into the tumor bed, as demonstrated in models of pancreatic cancer [18].

In summary, by integrating the three-dimensional regulatory network of immune checkpoints, cytokine signaling, and immune cell function, SUMOylation may function as one of several molecular nodes that influence the TIME, providing a testable theoretical framework for targeted intervention.

Other immune escape mechanisms

Moreover, tumor cells employ SUMOylation to remodel immunogenicity and evade immune surveillance, and SUMOylation within immune cells could also attenuate their immune functions:Inhibition of Antigen Processing and Presentation (supported by direct mechanistic evidence): As previously mentioned, SUMOylation reduces tumor antigen presentation by downregulating MHC-I APM components, rendering tumor cells invisible to CD8 + T cells. Pharmacological inhibition of SUMOylation can relieve the repression of MHC-I by the transcriptional repressor SAFB, restoring antigen presentation capacity and enhancing CD8 + T cell killing [10, 11].Interference with Immune Cell Function: SUMOylation can suppress NK cell activation and T cell cytotoxicity. Mechanistically, SUMOylation of STAT1, PTEN, and PPARγ within immune cells impairs JAK/STAT and PI3K/Akt signaling, leading to reduced STAT1 phosphorylation, diminished interferon-stimulated genes expression, and ultimately compromised T cell-mediated antitumor immunity [8]. Moreover, functional evidence reveals that SUMOylation inhibition depletes Tregs via type I interferon responses, thus remodeling the TIME [16, 21].

Technological advances in studying SUMOylation-immune interactions

Novel tools for detecting SUMOylation in immune cells (Methodological/Technical Evidence)

Two cyclic peptides, derived from phage display technology and capable of specifically binding to the C-terminal dodecapeptide of SUMO1, have been developed for constructing highly sensitive nanochannel devices. By conjugating these peptides to polyethylene terephthalate conical nanochannel membranes, these devices enable the detection of SUMO1 C-terminal peptides and proteins, allow monitoring of the time course of SENP1-catalyzed deSUMOylation reactions, and facilitate cellular imaging. This technology provides a new tool for precise SUMO1 recognition, proteomics analysis, and drug development [23].

Model systems for studying SUMOylation in tumor-immune crosstalk (Methodological/Technical Evidence)

Preclinical mouse models have served as powerful platforms for dissecting the role of SUMOylation in tumor-immune crosstalk. In hepatocellular carcinoma, treatment with SUMOylation inhibitors (TAK-981 and ML792) in subcutaneous and orthotopic mouse models, combined with single-cell RNA sequencing, mass cytometry, flow cytometry, and multiplex immunofluorescence, revealed that SUMOylation inhibition reduces exhausted CD8 + T cells while enhancing cytotoxic NK cells and M1 macrophages, thereby reshaping the TIME [24]. Similarly, in an orthotopic pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma model, SUMOylation inhibition with TAK-981 was shown to exert T cell-dependent anti-tumor effects, with single-cell transcriptomics revealing activation of interferon responses in lymphocytes and remodeling of immune cell composition within the TIME [18]. These studies collectively suggest that in vivo tumor models, combined with multi-omics profiling, can serve as a valuable experimental framework for understanding how SUMOylation modulates anti-tumor immunity. However, as with computational approaches, findings from these model systems remain correlative and require direct biochemical and functional validation to establish mechanistic causality.

Computational approaches to predict SUMOylation-immune networks (Methodological/Technical Evidence)

A critical appraisal of these methods is warranted. While powerful, these approaches have inherent limitations. Transcriptomic methods, including single cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq), infer pathway states indirectly and do not directly measure SUMO conjugation events. Computational predictions of SUMOylation sites or networks require validation with direct biochemical assays (e.g., mass spectrometry, co-immunoprecipitation). Similarly, readouts from pharmacologic inhibition (e.g., TAK-981) reflect the global consequences of blocking the SUMO pathway but do not, by themselves, establish target-specific SUMOylation networks. The gap between transcriptomic correlates and direct proteomic or biochemical evidence of SUMOylation must be clearly recognized.

With these considerations, computational models based on multi-omics data and machine learning algorithms are advancing the systematic dissection of SUMOylation-immune networks. In ovarian cancer research, using multi-omics data from the TCGA- ovarian cancer cohort, machine learning analysis identified 22 SUMOylation-related genes (SRGs) significantly associated with prognosis. Further integration with single-cell sequencing data pinpointed 5 key SRGs, whose expression correlated with tumor cell metabolism, epithelial-mesenchymal transition signaling, and chemotherapy resistance. At the single-cell level, high expression of these SRGs was confirmed to be associated with suppressed CD8 + T cell cytotoxic function [25]. In a pan-cancer analysis, based on bulk and single-cell RNA sequencing data from 1,445 breast cancer and 113 normal tissues, computational subtyping classified breast cancer into two distinct SUMOylation-related molecular subtypes. These subtypes exhibited significant differences in prognosis, immune infiltration characteristics (e.g., CD8 + T cell and macrophage abundance), and drug sensitivity. These findings were validated across 21 malignant tumor cohorts, offering correlative evidence that could inform future hypothesis-driven studies on personalized immunotherapeutic strategies targeting SUMOylation [26]. Additionally, studies propose leveraging artificial intelligence (AI) to assist in screening novel SUMOylation inhibitors, aiming to enhance the precision of targeted therapies [8].

Therapeutic targeting of SUMOylation for cancer immunotherapy

Pharmacological inhibitors of the SUMOylation cascade (Functional evidence from SUMOylation inhibition)

Preclinical research has focused on developing small molecule inhibitors targeting the SUMOylation pathway. TAK-981, a SUMO-activating enzyme inhibitor, activates type I interferon responses, enhances IFNγ secretion by CD4 + and CD8 + T cells, boosts CD8 + T cell cytotoxic function, and reduces Treg cell differentiation in patient-derived T cells and mouse models of chronic lymphocytic leukemia [13]. In an orthotopic mouse model of pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), TAK-981 significantly prolongs survival, an effect dependent on T cell activation [16]. Furthermore, ML792 treatment and UBE2I knockdown suppress tumor growth, increase anti-tumor immune cell infiltration, and induce cancer cell death in cholangiocarcinoma (CCA), with relatively low toxicity to normal biliary epithelial cells [27]. Collectively, these broad-spectrum SUMOylation inhibitors function by remodeling the immune cell landscape within the TIME in preclinical models [10, 16].

Precision modulation of specific SUMOylation targets (Functional evidence with direct molecular mechanism)

While broad-spectrum SUMOylation inhibitors have demonstrated efficacy in preclinical models, mechanism-guided targeting of specific SUMOylation nodes may offer greater therapeutic specificity. A key example is the transcriptional repressor SAFB: MYC-induced SUMOylation of SAFB suppresses MHC-I APM expression, and inhibiting this modification relieves the repression, thereby enhancing tumor antigen presentation and CD8 + T cell killing [11]. In CCA, direct targeting of the SUMOylation enzymes SAE1 and UBE2I (via knockdown or drugs such as ML792 and S-adenosylmethionine) specifically inhibits tumor growth while increasing anti-tumor immune cell infiltration, with minimal toxicity to normal biliary epithelial cells [27]. Additionally, reversing SUMOylation-mediated suppression of immunoproteasome function restores the diversity of the tumor antigenic peptide repertoire and enhances T cell immune surveillance [10]. These examples illustrate that understanding the precise molecular substrates of SUMOylation may inform the development of more selective intervention regimens [8, 27]. However, clinical translation of these approaches remains in early stages, and their therapeutic benefit in patients has not yet been established.

Current challenges

Tissue-specific effects of SUMOylation in immune regulation (Non-cancer and cross-context evidence)

The immunomodulatory effects of SUMOylation exhibit significant tissue specificity, posing a major challenge for targeted therapies. This specificity cautions against broad, pan-cancer generalizations of findings derived from a limited set of tumor contexts, including pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, CCA, bladder cancer, and lymphoid malignancies, etc. In the spleen, SUMO1 specifically regulates TNFα production and metabolic acidosis during endotoxemia; its absence exacerbates inflammation through transplanted splenocytes [28]. In the kidney, SUMOylation stabilizes IκBα by modifying specific lysine residues (K21/K22), inhibiting excessive NF-κB activation and thereby mitigating inflammation in sepsis-associated acute kidney injury (SA-AKI); proximal tubule-specific knockout of Ubc9 (the SUMO E2 enzyme) significantly aggravates kidney injury and apoptosis [29]. Within the central nervous system (CNS), SUMOylation in microglia influences HIV immune evasion by regulating key proteins that maintain viral latency [30]. Furthermore, the regulatory roles of SUMOylation in Treg differentiation, epigenetic regulation, and metabolic reprogramming [31] highlight the heterogeneity among immune cell types. This tissue and cell-type specificity necessitates the development of precise targeting strategies. Of note, the evidence in this section derives primarily from non-tumor tissues (spleen, kidney, CNS) and sepsis models, illustrating general principles of SUMOylation-mediated immune regulation rather than tumor-specific mechanisms.

Balancing therapeutic efficacy and immune-related adverse events (Functional evidence through SUMOylation inhibition)

Targeting SUMOylation for immunotherapy faces the core challenge of balancing efficacy with toxicity. Evidence from lung cancer patients undergoing ICBs suggests that a dynamic range exists: mild reduction in SUMOylation enhances T cell and NK cell activation, whereas excessive inhibition (rate of change between −50% and −100%) leads to increased lymphocyte mortality and is significantly linked to treatment resistance. This bidirectional effect indicates that the magnitude of SUMOylation inhibition directly correlates with both therapeutic efficacy and safety [20]. Preclinical studies show that combining TAK-981 with ICB improves tumor outcomes but is associated with dose-dependent toxicity [8]. This bidirectional effect is also observed in non-tumor contexts, such as sepsis models, where TAK-981 enhances bacterial clearance and improves survival, but SUMO1 deficiency amplifies inflammatory damage [28, 29], further illustrating the general principle that the net effect of SUMOylation modulation depends on the magnitude of inhibition. Therefore, developing tissue-targeted delivery systems or utilizing AI to screen novel inhibitors [8] are critical directions for minimizing systemic toxicity and preserving immune cell function.

Identification of candidate biomarkers (Biomarker evidence)

Identifying candidate biomarkers to predict response to SUMOylation-targeted immunotherapy is a key focus and challenge in current research. Multiple studies suggest that molecules related to the SUMOylation pathway hold biomarker potential [32, 33]. Emerging clinical trial information (clinical evidence)

To date, the clinical translation of SUMOylation inhibitors is still in its early stages. The most advanced compound, TAK-981 (subasumstat), has been evaluated in Phase I/II clinical trials. Notably, a Phase I trial (NCT03648372) investigated TAK-981 as a single agent and in combination with pembrolizumab in patients with advanced solid tumors or lymphomas [36]. Results indicated manageable safety profiles and preliminary, hypothesis-generating evidence of anti-tumor activity, with ongoing biomarker analyses to identify responsive patient populations. However, these trials were primarily designed to assess safety and pharmacokinetics; they were not designed to establish clinical efficacy, nor did they provide definitive proof of clinical benefit or validate predictive biomarkers. TAK-981 has also been studied in several other trials [NCT04074330 (anti-CD20), NCT04776018 (anti-CD38), NCT04065555 (anti-EGFR or anti-PD-L1), and NCT04381650 (anti-PD-1)], while other SUMOylation inhibitors, such as ML792, remain primarily in preclinical development. Collectively, no SUMOylation-targeted therapy has yet demonstrated clinical benefit in patients with cancer, and substantial preclinical and translational work remains before this approach can be considered a clinically viable strategy.

Future perspectives and research directions

Developing isoform-specific SUMOylation inhibitors (Future perspectives with computational evidence)

Current SUMOylation inhibitors (e.g., TAK-981), despite showing potential for remodeling the TIME in preclinical studies, exhibit dose-dependent toxicity [8]. Future efforts must focus on developing isoform-specific SUMOylation inhibitors to precisely target specific SUMO protein isoforms (e.g., SUMO1/2/3). Pan-cancer analyses reveal significant heterogeneity in the expression of SRGs across different tumors; for instance, in breast cancer, two molecular subtypes with distinct prognoses and immune infiltration characteristics can be identified: Subtype 1 (high expression of certain SRGs, poor prognosis) and Subtype 2 (high expression of a different set of SRGs, good prognosis) [26]. These subtype differences correlate with immune cell infiltration patterns and therapeutic response, suggesting that designing isoform-selective inhibitors could optimize efficacy and reduce toxicity. Additionally, combining artificial intelligence to screen novel SUMOylation inhibitors, or developing antibody–drug conjugates to achieve precise restoration of specific protein functions within the TIME, is warranted [8].

Exploring spatial regulation of SUMOylation in tumor niches (Future perspectives with preclinical evidence)

The spatiotemporal heterogeneity of the TIME significantly influences SUMOylation function. Studies indicate that the hypoxic microenvironment can regulate PTMs through metabolic reprogramming processes such as activation of the hexosamine biosynthesis pathway, thereby shaping an immunosuppressive TIME [37]. Single-cell transcriptomic analysis has confirmed that inhibiting SUMOylation alters the interaction networks of multiple cell types within the PDAC TIME [16]. Future research should integrate spatial multi-omics technologies (e.g., spatial transcriptomics, imaging mass spectrometry) to dissect the dynamic modification patterns of SUMOylation in different tumor regions (e.g., invasive front, perivascular areas). Particular attention should be paid to regulatory mechanisms at the three-dimensional genome level; for example, the heterochromatic state maintained by SUMOylation in coordination with MORC3 suppresses innate immune responses in myeloid cells, while inhibiting SUMOylation induces three-dimensional genome remodeling and activates anti-tumor immunity [12].

Conclusion: towards a SUMOylation-informed immunotherapy paradigm

Key mechanistic insights and therapeutic implications

Direct mechanistic evidence (e.g., biochemical SUMOylation assays) supports SUMOylation-mediated regulation of APM components and immune checkpoint molecules such as PD-L1 and TIGIT; Functional/preclinical evidence from SUMOylation inhibition (e.g., TAK-981, ML792) indicates broader effects on cytokine signaling pathways (type I interferon, NF-κB, JAK-STAT), immune cell differentiation (reduced Tregs, enhanced CD8 + T cell cytotoxicity), and remodeling of the TIME; Correlative biomarker and computational evidence (e.g., SRG signatures, SUMO scores) has identified associations between SUMOylation pathway activity and patient prognosis or immunotherapy response; however, these findings are retrospective and have not yet been prospectively validated as predictive biomarkers.

Drawing from evidence in specific tumor contexts such as PDAC, CCA, and lymphoma, the integrated model suggests that SUMOylation may function as one of several molecular nodes that influence the TIME, providing a theoretical foundation for targeted intervention.

These mechanistic insights carry significant therapeutic implications. Pharmacological inhibition of SUMOylation (e.g., using the SUMO E1 enzyme inhibitor TAK-981, or SUMOylation inhibitors 2-D08 and ML792) can reverse the aforementioned immunosuppressive effects: it relieves the inhibition of MHC-I APM, enhancing tumor antigen presentation and susceptibility to CD8 + T cell killing [11]; it unveils a targetable immunopeptidome and reshapes the immune cell landscape [10]; it activates type I interferon responses, sustains T cell activation (increasing CD69/CD38 expression), reduces Treg differentiation, and enhances CD8 + T cell cytotoxic function [13]. In various tumor models (e.g., PDAC [16, 21], CCA [27], bladder cancer [35], osteosarcoma [7]), SUMOylation inhibition, either as a single agent or combined with ICB, demonstrates significant anti-tumor activity. Its efficacy relies on remodeling the TIME. Notably, SUMOylation scores or SRG expression patterns correlate with immunotherapy response in cancer; however, these studies are retrospective and correlative. Therefore, these signatures currently serve as prognostic indicators rather than validated predictive biomarkers [20, 26, 35]. Whether they can prospectively identify patients likely to benefit from SUMOylation‑targeted immunotherapy remains to be determined.

Proposed roadmap for clinical translation of SUMOylation-based immunotherapy

- A key future direction involves the development of more precise SUMOylation inhibitors to potentially maximize efficacy and minimize off-target effects [3, 8]. Concurrently, the combination of SUMOylation inhibitors with ICB represents a mechanistically plausible hypothesis to be tested in future trials. Preclinical evidence from model systems suggests the potential for synergistic effects. Future clinical studies will be required to systematically investigate the safety and, subsequently, the efficacy of different combination regimens. To address dose-dependent toxicity observed in preclinical studies, the exploration of targeted delivery systems (such as antibody-conjugated SUMOylation inhibitors) or AI-assisted screening for novel inhibitors warrants further investigation [8, 41].- Identifying Predictive Biomarkers and Stratifying Patients: Achieving precision medicine requires establishing a robust biomarker system. Research has revealed that SRGs expression profiles (e.g., SUMO score) or specific molecules (e.g., ALKBH5 nuclear expression) are significantly correlated with TIME characteristics and ICB response [26, 35, 42], although these findings still await prospective validation. The predictive value of these biomarkers must be prospectively validated in controlled clinical studies before they can guide patient stratification.- Advancing Clinical Research and Translational Validation: Implementing the roadmap requires robust clinical research. Early-phase clinical trials (Phase I/II) should be prioritized in tumor types characterized by aberrant SUMOylation activation associated with poor prognosis (e.g., CCA [27], PDAC [16, 21], bladder cancer [35]) to assess the safety, pharmacokinetics, and preliminary efficacy of single agents and combination therapies, particularly with ICB. In-depth investigation of tissue-specific effects is crucial, clarifying the similarities and differences in how SUMOylation regulates the TIME across different organs/tumor types. Simultaneously, utilizing advanced model systems (such as humanized mouse models, organoid co-cultures) and single-cell technologies to deeply analyze the mechanisms by which SUMO inhibition remodels immune cell composition, functional status, and intercellular interaction networks in the human TIME [16, 21] will provide a theoretical basis for clinical trial design. Exploring spatial omics technologies to dissect the differential regulation of SUMOylation in various tumor regions (e.g., invasive front, perivascular areas, immune-rich/desert zones) and its impact on local immune status (spatial regulation) will aid in understanding the heterogeneity of treatment responses and developing more precise intervention strategies.

In conclusion, based on the current preclinical evidence base, targeting SUMOylation represents a mechanistically plausible strategy to overcome the immunosuppressive TIME and resistance to immunotherapy. However, clinical translation remains in early stages; no SUMOylation-directed therapy has yet demonstrated clinical benefit in controlled trials, and no predictive biomarker has been prospectively validated. Substantial mechanistic, translational, and clinical work remains before this approach can be considered a clinically viable immunotherapeutic strategy. Further mechanistic understanding, development of precise modulatory tools, optimization of combination regimens, and identification of benefiting populations will be required to determine whether this approach can ultimately improve outcomes for cancer patients in clinical practice.
