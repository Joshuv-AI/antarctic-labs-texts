Influence mechanisms of rock mass discontinuity density on mining-induced land subsidence
Authors: Wanxin Pan, Yaqiang Gong, Wenbin Deng, Guangli Guo, Yuyang Wan, Naseer Muhammad Khan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Surface subsidence induced by underground coal mining, together with the associated damage to surface infrastructure and a range of ecological impacts such as hydrological disturbance and vegetation degradation, collectively hampers the sustainable development of coal resource-based cities worldwide. What remains unclear, however, is how discontinuity density influences mining-induced surface subsidence (mining subsidence), which may render conventional analogy-based analysis methods inadequate for interpreting the anomalously low surface subsidence observed in the Ordos coalfield in China. In this study, we demonstrate that the stiffness of the overburden, represented by the uniaxial compressive strength of intact rock (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\sigma\:}_{ci}$$\end{document}), is not a sufficient indicator for subsidence estimation, and the discontinuity density may play a more influential role. To substantiate this claim, a series of UDEC models with varied discontinuity density and rock stiffness were built and calibrated, and the modeling results were compared with practical cases to assess their validity. The results indicate: (1) increasing discontinuity density leads to a systematic shift in the dominant overburden failure mode from intact rock deformation to block sliding, thereby reducing the reliability of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\sigma\:}_{ci}$$\end{document} as an indicator for subsidence estimation; (2) the applicability of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\sigma\:}_{ci}$$\end{document}-based analogy methods is conditional, being reliable primarily when discontinuity densities are comparable; and (3) higher discontinuity density reduces the width-to-depth ratio required for critical mining, which helps to explain the delayed and attenuated subsidence observed in deep coal mining areas of Ordos, China. The study provides useful insights for subsidence prediction and mining design, and contributes to a better understanding of subsidence mechanisms relevant to the sustainable development of coal resource-based cities.

Introduction

After coal is extracted from underground, the original stress balance of the surrounding rock is disrupted, leading to movement and deformation of the overburden1,2. As these deformations propagate upward, they ultimately manifest at the ground surface as mining subsidence. Such surface subsidence is commonly accompanied by damage to surface infrastructure as well as eco-hydrological disturbances, including groundwater table decline, vegetation degradation, and land desertification. These impacts impose long-term constraints on land resource utilization and urban development, posing a major challenge to the sustainable development of coal resource-based cities worldwide. To address these issues, extensive research efforts have been devoted to mining subsidence, and considerable progress has been achieved in elucidating its general principles3–5. However, the movement patterns of the overburden are highly contingent on geological and mining conditions, and in some cases exhibit substantial variability or deviation from conventional expectations6. Therefore, further investigation into overburden behavior under different conditions is necessary to refine the theoretical framework of mining subsidence and to better support the sustainable development of coal resource-based cities.

Scholars have conducted extensive research on the mechanisms of strata movement and the patterns of surface deformation, which has yielded abundant results in the past7–15 and recently16–21, yet an increasing amount of engineering practice indicates that the empirical laws derived from eastern coal mines are not entirely applicable to the western regions as China’s coal mining focus shifts westward22,23. For instance, in the deep coalfields of Ordos, Inner Mongolia, an abnormal phenomenon has been observed: surface subsidence is markedly smaller than expected24. To account for this, some researchers25 argue that the prevailing evaluation indices for subsidence degree, which predominantly emphasize the stiffness of intact rock specimens measured through uniaxial compressive strength (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\sigma\:}_{ci}$$\end{document}), may fail to reflect the destabilizing role of discontinuities within the overburden. Such discontinuities, they contend, are highly likely to constitute the decisive factor behind the unusually surface subsidence observed in Ordos. In this regard, discontinuities were given great emphasis in both China’s national engineering rock mass classification standard and the Geological Strength Index (GSI) system26. Specifically, the former one assesses rock mass quality on the basis of two principal parameters: rock stiffness and rock mass integrity (quantified through the density of structural planes such as joints, fractures, and bedding planes, which can collectively be referred to as discontinuity density). The latter one, on the other hand, holds that rock mass mechanical behavior is primarily governed by two factors: blockiness, defined by the spacing of structural planes as well as the size and geometry of rock blocks (can also collectively be referred to as discontinuity density), and the condition of the discontinuities. We can see that the two systems diverge in the specific metrics adopted but both converge on one essential point: the discontinuity density. The question then immediately arises as to what is the impact of the discontinuity density on mining subsidence and which one is more influential (the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\sigma\:}_{ci}$$\end{document} or the discontinuity density), which are not fully understood and merit further study. It should also be noted that numerous studies, conducted by researchers of differing perspectives such as the mechanics27–29, rock engineering30–32, and geological structures33–35, have investigated the influence of discontinuity density on rock mass stability. But few studies have posed attention on its effect on mining subsidence, which probably is the reason for the confusion on the understanding of the anomalous surface subsidence observed in the deep coalfields of Ordos.

To bridge the gap, we first introduced the abnormal mining subsidence phenomena in Sect. 2, and then developed a set of calibrated UDEC models to investigate the relationship between discontinuity density and mining subsidence in Sect. 3. The modeling results and case analysis provide evidence supporting our proposed hypothesis (Sect. 4), which contributes to the theoretical framework of mining subsidence estimation by analogy-based method, and offers new insights in understanding the role of discontinuity density for large-scale strata movement.

Previous explanations for the abnormal subsidence phenomena and the limitations

In the previous research25, we have provided a preliminary explanation for the abnormal subsidence phenomena from the perspective of the GSI system26. Deviation from this condition is the fundamental cause of the aforementioned misjudgment.

Collectively, it can be observed that the GSI system suggests a greater importance of the GSI value over the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\sigma\:}_{ci}$$\end{document} in estimating the deformation modulus of rock masses.

Following this line of reasoning, in our previous study25, we also constructed FLAC3D models in which the rock mass properties were set to equivalent values corresponding to three representative GSI levels (i.e., 30, 50, and 75) to investigate the relationship between mining subsidence and GSI values. The results supported the proposition that the influence of the GSI indicator is significantly greater than that of the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\sigma\:}_{ci}$$\end{document} indicator in estimating mining subsidence. However, it should be emphasized that FLAC3D is based on the concept of an equivalent continuum, which inherently fails to capture the intrinsically discontinuous nature of rock strata characterized by bedding planes, joints, and fractures, particularly cross-joints between layers. Specifically, in FLAC3D, rock beds do not physically fracture but are represented through plastic flow. Although interfaces can be introduced into the model to separate rock layers, they cannot realistically simulate the fragmentation and separation of numerous rock blocks. Moreover, the FLAC3D software itself strongly discourages the inclusion of an excessive number of interfaces and recommends using 3DEC or UDEC instead. Therefore, it can be concluded that the previous study had certain limitations, particularly in its inability to directly clarify the role of discontinuity density (essentially equivalent to the GSI value, as will be explained later) in controlling mining-induced subsidence, as well as the coupled effects of discontinuity density and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\sigma\:}_{ci}$$\end{document}.

To refine our previous study, this paper further employs UDEC to directly investigate the relationships among discontinuity density, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\sigma\:}_{ci}$$\end{document}, and mining-induced subsidence. It should be noted that in the following analysis, the GSI value is represented by discontinuity density, as the two are essentially similar in nature for deeply buried rock masses. As illustrated in Fig. 1, the GSI value is estimated based on two fundamental aspects of rock mass quality: structure and surface conditions. The surface conditions primarily refer to rock masses exposed to the combined effects of temperature variation, moisture, ice, wind, biological activity, and chemical agents such as oxygen and acidic solutions, which are mainly observed in near-surface outcrops37,38. In contrast, deeply buried rock masses located in closed or semi-closed environments experience fundamentally different types and intensities of alteration. The tremendous pressure exerted by the overlying strata expels water from loose sediments, resulting in tighter intergranular contacts, while mineral precipitation from groundwater acts as a cementing agent, gradually transforming the sediments into solid sedimentary rocks through an extremely slow process39. Consequently, the variability in the condition of discontinuities within such deep rock masses is greatly constrained, and their characteristics tend to be relatively uniform. Under these conditions, the GSI value of the overburden can, to a considerable extent, be regarded as equivalent to the structural blockiness—in other words, to the discontinuity density.

Method

Based on the above considerations, UDEC was employed to simulate the underground coal mining process, owing to its advantages in modeling the discontinuous deformation of jointed rock masses and its proven success in previous studies40–42. The details are presented below.

Model setup

Basic setup

The basic model, illustrated in Fig. 2, represents a domain with a total length of 800 m, comprising the coal seam with thickness of 5 m and density of 1300 kg/m3, overlain by the overburden of 100 m thick with density of 2500 kg/m3. The left, right, and bottom boundaries are constrained with fixed displacements, whereas the top boundary represents the ground surface and remains unconstrained. The open-off cut is positioned 100 m from the left boundary to minimize boundary effects on the simulation results. During the simulation, the working face advances in increments of 20 m, and ground surface subsidence is recorded throughout this process. The total advancement distance of the working face is 600 m, ensuring that the entire model undergoes sufficient mining-induced deformation.

The choice of discontinuity configuration

In actual rock masses, discontinuities are ubiquitously developed, with spacings ranging from several millimeters to several meters. However, due to current limitations in computational capacity and efficiency, it is impractical to explicitly reproduce the complete discontinuity network within rock masses when simulating large-scale engineering problems such as mining-induced overburden movement. The discontinuities explicitly incorporated in this study, with transverse and longitudinal spacings ranging from 10 × 10 m to 2 × 2 m as summarized in Table 2, mainly represent those characterized by large apertures, strong connectivity, and pronounced persistence, which exert a significant controlling effect on the macroscopic movement of the overburden. For discontinuities below this scale, i.e., those with small apertures, limited persistence, and indistinct traces, their influence on rock mass damage and mechanical degradation was considered following the concepts proposed by Hoek43 and Packulak et al.44. Rather than being explicitly modeled, their effects were implicitly incorporated by assigning equivalent rock mass mechanical properties to the blocks in UDEC. This treatment is also consistent with actual geological conditions, since even the smallest block size adopted in this study (2 × 2 m) would rarely correspond to truly intact rock in nature, let alone blocks with dimensions of 10 × 10 m. At such modeling scales, the UDEC blocks are more reasonably regarded as rock masses containing internal discontinuities. In other words, the blocks in our UDEC model do not represent intact rock, instead, they represent sub-rock masses partitioned by explicitly modeled large-scale discontinuities, while still containing smaller-scale internal discontinuities that are not explicitly modeled. The corresponding mechanical parameters are provided in Sect. 3.2.

Mesh sensitivity analysis

The accuracy with which the M-C failure criterion captures plastic flow is highly sensitive to the density of the mesh division. To strike an appropriate balance between computational efficiency and simulation accuracy, the present study determines an optimal meshing scheme through a mesh-sensitivity analysis. The procedure is outlined as follows.

Fig. 3Comparison of block mesh density gradients. Line colors correspond to those in Fig. 4.

Comparison of block mesh density gradients. Line colors correspond to those in Fig. 4.

Fig. 4Curves of surface subsidence under different mesh densities.

Curves of surface subsidence under different mesh densities.

Using Model 4 in Table 2 as a reference, the quadrilateral area-division method was adopted to construct five sets of gradient models, each with a distinct mesh element size (defined by the length × width of sub-quadrilateral elements). The mesh sizes considered were: 10 × 10 m (single block), 5 × 5 m (4 sub-elements), 3.3 × 3.3 m (9 sub-elements), 2.5 × 2.5 m (16 sub-elements), and 2 × 2 m (25 sub-elements). The corresponding mesh densities for each case are illustrated in Fig. 3. Excavation simulations were subsequently performed on all five mesh models, and the evolutionary relationship between surface subsidence and working-face advance distance was obtained (see Fig. 4). We can see that once the element size is reduced to ≤ 5 × 5 m, the subsidence curves corresponding to different mesh sizes converge, suggesting that under these conditions the influence of mesh division on subsidence prediction becomes faint. Accordingly, based on the criterion of mesh convergence, this study adopts 5 × 5 m as the final meshing scheme.

Results and discussion

Strata failure characteristics under the coupling effects of discontinuity density and rock stiffness

In the rigid block model, blocks are assumed to be non-deformable, and the patterns of overburden failure are governed primarily by the sliding of blocks along discontinuities (see Fig. 5a). By contrast, in the M-C block model (specifically refer to the liner elastic, perfectly plastic model with the M-C failure criterion), blocks are allowed to undergo elastoplastic deformation, so that overburden failure is jointly influenced by inter-block sliding and the elastoplastic deformation of the blocks themselves (see Fig. 5b and c). Understanding the mechanical distinctions between these two models is essential for interpreting how different constitutive assumptions lead to contrasting subsidence behaviors. Therefore, in what follows, we compare the evolution of surface subsidence under varying discontinuity densities to clarify the mechanisms responsible for these differences.

Fig. 5Schematics of instability mechanisms for rigid blocks and deformable blocks.

Schematics of instability mechanisms for rigid blocks and deformable blocks.

We can first see from Fig. 6 that as the discontinuity density increases from 10 × 10 m to 6 × 6 m, the instantaneous subsidence rate (the ratio of the maximum surface subsidence at a given working-face advance stage to the mining thickness) in the rigid block model remains consistently lower than that in the M-C block model for the same advance distance of the working face. Taking the discontinuity density of 10 × 10 m as an illustrative case, when the working face advances 160 m, Model 1 (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\sigma\:}_{ci}$$\end{document} = 20 MPa), Model 2 (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\sigma\:}_{ci}$$\end{document} = 50 MPa), Model 3 (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\sigma\:}_{ci}$$\end{document} = 80 MPa), Model 4 (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\sigma\:}_{ci}$$\end{document} = 80 MPa), and Model 5 (rigid block) yield the instantaneous subsidence rates of 0.73, 0.61, 0.49, 0.06 and 0.01, respectively. This result aligns with the well-established understanding that surface subsidence values of hard overburden with higher \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\sigma\:}_{ci}$$\end{document} are lower than those of weak overburden with lower \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\sigma\:}_{ci}$$\end{document}. The underlying reason is that when discontinuity density is relatively low, for instance, with a spacing of 10 × 10 m in this study, the overburden behaves more like a continuous medium. Under such conditions, block sliding is limited, while block failure is dominated by elastoplastic deformation rather than sliding. Consequently, the regulatory role of intact rock stiffness in controlling mining-induced subsidence becomes particularly pronounced.

We can also see that, as the discontinuity density increases from 10 × 10 m to 2 × 2 m, an anomalous phenomenon emerges. Specifically, under normal circumstances, the rigid block model, which represents an idealized medium with infinitely stiff blocks, should produce smaller surface subsidence compared with deformable models such as the M-C block model, because, intuitively, stiffer objects deform less. However, this expected trend is reversed when the discontinuity density becomes sufficiently high. We can observe that, for the same advance distance of the working face, the instantaneous subsidence rate in the rigid block model rises rapidly and even surpasses that of the M-C block model when the discontinuity density increases from 4 × 4 m to 2 × 2 m. For instance, at the discontinuity density of 4 × 4 m and the working face advance of 120 m, Model 16 (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\sigma\:}_{ci}$$\end{document} = 20 MPa), Model 17 (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\sigma\:}_{ci}$$\end{document} = 50 MPa), Model 18 (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\sigma\:}_{ci}$$\end{document} = 80 MPa), Model 19 (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\sigma\:}_{ci}$$\end{document} = 150 MPa), and Model 20 (rigid block) exhibit instantaneous subsidence rates of 0.99, 0.98, 0.82, 0.29, and 0.17, respectively. At this stage, although the rigid block model still shows lower instantaneous subsidence rate than the M-C block models with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\sigma\:}_{ci}$$\end{document} = 20, 50 and 80 MPa, its instantaneous subsidence rate has already exceeded that of the M-C block model with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\sigma\:}_{ci}$$\end{document} = 150 MPa. When the discontinuity density further increases to 2 × 2 m, this reversal becomes more pronounced. For example, at a working face advance of 60 m, Model 21 (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\sigma\:}_{ci}$$\end{document} = 20 MPa), Model 22 (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\sigma\:}_{ci}$$\end{document} = 50 MPa), Model 23 (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\sigma\:}_{ci}$$\end{document} = 80 MPa), Model 24 (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\sigma\:}_{ci}$$\end{document} = 150 MPa), and Model 25 (rigid block) exhibit instantaneous subsidence rates of 0.19, 0.11, 0.09, 0.07, and 0.44, respectively, indicating that the rigid block model completely surpasses all M-C block models in terms of surface subsidence.

To further interpret this counterintuitive result, it is necessary to examine the failure morphology of the overburden under different modeling assumptions. In the M-C block models (see Fig. 7a-d), bed separation occurs at depths of 8 m, 24 m, 34 m, 44 m below the surface. Although the overburden above these separations is curved and sagging, it does not fracture or collapse, forming a supporting structure that prevents significant surface subsidence. In contrast, in the rigid block model (see Fig. 7e), the entire overburden collapses along the fracture surfaces on both sides of the coal wall, tipping into the goaf. Unlike the M-C block model, the overburden does not form a distinct supporting structure, resulting in more severe surface subsidence.

To explain the observed transition, Fig. 8 is drawn to better examine the mechanisms of rock mass failure under different discontinuity densities. Under conditions of low discontinuity density, the rigid block model and the deformable M–C block model exhibit comparable behaviors, with the overburden in both displaying strong continuity (as illustrated in Fig. 8a and b). Fundamentally, this is attributed to the sparse distribution of discontinuities, which restricts the relative sliding and separation of rock blocks. On a microscopic level, this condition fosters strong embedment and interlocking between blocks, significantly inhibiting the coalescence of discontinuities and block detachment. Macroscopically, this constraint impedes the upward propagation of slip failure from the roof to the overlying strata, thereby preserving the overall continuity of the overburden. In this scenario, the gravitational load of the upper strata is primarily transferred to the flanking coal walls through a pressure arch structure, and the strata undergo continuous deformation manifesting as bending and sagging toward the goaf, defined by a virtual absence of open discontinuities or block separation. The stability of these strata is predominantly governed by block stiffness: higher stiffness enhances the bearing capacity of the pressure arch, thereby reducing the magnitude of strata bending, the strata composed of relatively hard rock even show almost no noticeable bending when the mined-out area is relatively small. This explains the traditional view that “surface subsidence values of hard overburden with higher \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\sigma\:}_{ci}$$\end{document} are lower than those of weak overburden with lower \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\sigma\:}_{ci}$$\end{document}”.

Fig. 8The influence of discontinuity density on the overburden failure in both constitutive models.

The influence of discontinuity density on the overburden failure in both constitutive models.

As the overburden discontinuity density increases, the rock mass structure becomes increasingly discretized. This discrete nature significantly intensifies block sliding failure, a phenomenon that proves fatal to the stability of the overburden within the rigid block model. This instability arises because, although the rigid blocks can transmit overhead pressure to the coal walls on both sides through mutual squeezing, the blocks themselves do not undergo the compressive deformation necessary to facilitate mutual interlocking. Consequently, under the influence of shear or tensile stresses, the blocks separate easily. This separation causes the already dense discontinuities to coalesce, forming “fault-like” fracture surfaces (as indicated by the purple dashed lines in Fig. 8c). Macroscopically, shear failure of the overburden occurs along these fracture surfaces flanking the coal walls. When the discontinuity density is sufficiently high, these fracture surfaces propagate continuously to the ground surface, manifesting a scenario similar to that in Fig. 7e: the entire overburden separates along these surfaces and topples toward the goaf, resulting in a sharp increase in surface subsidence.

In contrast to the rigid block model, the deformable M-C block model exhibits a more moderate failure process under high discontinuity density. Although overburden failure in this scenario is more pronounced than in low-density cases and resembles the rigid model through the coalescence of discontinuities into fracture surfaces (Fig. 8d), a critical distinction, however, is that the overburden in the deformable M-C model successfully maintains a stable supporting structure. Consequently, unlike the rigid model, the fractures do not propagate to the surface, and the overburden avoids integral collapse or toppling. This stability is attributed to the inherent deformability of real rocks, which allows for the adjustment of contact surface morphology during rotation and subsidence. This adaptability enables sustained interlocking, effectively inhibiting the further propagation of fracture surfaces and mitigating the severity of overall shear failure within the rock mass. Consequently, from a macroscopic perspective, the overburden in the deformable model is more prone to forming a stable supporting structure and results in reduced surface subsidence compared to the rigid block model. Furthermore, similar to conditions of low discontinuity density, the strength of this supporting structure depends primarily on block stiffness. This ensures that the M–C block models representing overburden composed of rocks with these four different stiffness levels still conform to the conventional understanding that “greater rock stiffness results in reduced surface subsidence.”

Furthermore, it is crucial to emphasize that under conditions of high discontinuity density, although rock stiffness continues to influence overburden failure to some extent, for example, as reflected by the different locations of bed separations in Fig. 7a-d, its impact on final mining subsidence is significantly diminished. Figure 6 provides compelling evidence for this trend: as discontinuity density increases, the disparity among the surface subsidence curves of the four M–C block models gradually decreases. Notably, when the discontinuity density increases to 2 × 2 m, the four curves nearly coincide. This indicates that as discontinuity density rises, the influence of rock stiffness (represented by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\sigma\:}_{ci}$$\end{document}) becomes secondary, while sliding failure along discontinuities emerges as the dominant controlling factor for mining subsidence. Given the higher computational efficiency of the rigid block model, which eliminates the need for iterative calculations of internal block deformation, it can be considered a viable alternative modeling approach under high discontinuity density conditions. In such scenarios, the impact of rock stiffness on rock mass failure is markedly weakened, rendering discontinuity density the more critical parameter.

In summary, the aforementioned comparison elucidates a seemingly paradoxical phenomenon observed in our experimental results: under high discontinuity density conditions, the rigid block model, which possesses the greatest block stiffness, paradoxically exhibits the highest surface subsidence. This behavior is attributed to its fundamental difference from the M-C constitutive model, which is capable of representing the inherent deformability of real rock blocks, and further reveals the dual and opposing effects of rock deformability on overburden failure. On one hand, deformability reduces the macroscopic stiffness of the overburden, thereby enhancing strata bending and surface subsidence; on the other hand, it allows contact surfaces between rock blocks to adjust their morphology during rotation and settlement, promoting interlocking between adjacent blocks and thereby inhibiting the development of through-going fracture surfaces and the discretization of the rock mass. Specifically, under low discontinuity density conditions where the rock mass is dominated by continuous deformation, the former effect (weakening of macroscopic overburden stiffness) is more pronounced. This results in greater strata bending and surface subsidence in the deformable M-C block model compared to the rigid block model. In contrast, under high discontinuity density conditions where rock mass discretization is intensified, the latter effect (facilitating rock interlocking) begins to become evident. This helps to suppress discontinuity coalescence and the integral sliding failure of the overburden, ultimately resulting in lower surface subsidence in the deformable M-C block model than in the rigid block model.

Effect of discontinuity density and rock stiffness on critical mining

The influence of discontinuity density and rock stiffness on critical mining is shown in Fig. 9, where we can see that both of them affect the width-to-depth ratio (i.e., the ratio of the extraction width to the mining depth) required to reach the critical mining state (i.e., the minimum mining geometry at which surface subsidence reaches its maximum and then stabilizes). Specifically, we find that the higher the discontinuity density, the smaller the width-to-depth ratio required to reach the critical mining state. For instance, in the model with overburden composed of rock with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\sigma\:}_{ci}$$\end{document} = 150 MPa (the green columns in Fig. 9, corresponding to Models 4, 9, 14, 19, and 24), when the discontinuity density increases from 10 × 10 m to 2 × 2 m, the critical width-to-depth ratio drops sharply from 3.2 (Model 4) to 1.2 (Model 24), representing a 62.5% decrease. This can be attributed to the fact that, as discontinuity density increases, the overburden becomes increasingly discretized and behaves as an assemblage of smaller, loosely interlocked rock blocks. These blocks can more readily rotate, slide, and adjust under mining-induced stress relief, allowing the overburden to reach the critical mining state more rapidly. In other words, the higher the discontinuity density, the lower the structural integrity of the rock mass, and the earlier the critical mining state is reached.

Further analysis shows that the influence of intact rock stiffness on critical mining progressively diminishes as discontinuity density increases. Specifically, at the discontinuity density of 10 × 10 m, increasing \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\sigma\:}_{ci}$$\end{document} from 20 MPa to 150 MPa raises the critical width-to-depth ratio from 1.7 to 3.2, an increase of 88.2%. By contrast, when discontinuity density is 2 × 2 m, the increase is only 20% (from 1.0 to 1.2). Under such high discontinuity density conditions, the influence of intact rock stiffness on mining subsidence is substantially weakened. This is because when the rock mass is highly fractured, the overall deformation of the overburden is mainly governed by the relative displacement and rotation of the rock blocks rather than by the elastic or plastic deformation of the intact rock itself. As a result, the mechanical contrast between hard and soft rocks becomes less significant, and the subsidence behavior is controlled mainly by the spatial configuration and connectivity of discontinuities rather than by the intrinsic stiffness of the rock material.

Compared with practical cases, we note that under high discontinuity-density conditions (e.g., discontinuity spacing of 4 × 4 m and 2 × 2 m in this study, corresponding to Models 16–19 and 20–24), the critical width-to-depth ratio ranges from approximately 1.0 to 1.7, which is broadly close to the empirical range of 1.2–1.4 reported in the classic textbook45. By contrast, under low density conditions (e.g., discontinuity spacing of 10 × 10 m and 8 × 8 m, corresponding to Models 1–4 and 6–9), the critical width-to-depth ratio increases to 1.8–3.2, which agrees well with field measurements from the Shaanxi–Inner Mongolia mining areas25.

Therefore, when we recall the comparative analysis in Sect. 2, it becomes evident that a crucial premise was previously overlooked. Meaningful comparison of overburden stiffness between different mining regions should be made under conditions of similar discontinuity density. Only by controlling for this factor can the relative effect of rock stiffness on subsidence be accurately assessed.

From an engineering perspective, the implication of this finding is not that existing subsidence prediction or analogy-based methods should be replaced by the present numerical model. Rather, discontinuity density, or its equivalent expression in terms of structural blockiness or rock mass integrity, should be introduced as an additional but essential similarity criterion when analogy-based assessment is conducted. In practical comparisons between a target mining area and a reference case, similarity in mining depth, seam thickness, panel size, mining method, and intact-rock strength alone may not be sufficient. The structural integrity of the overburden should also be examined. When the reference and target cases differ substantially in discontinuity density or equivalent rock mass quality, an analogy based mainly on intact-rock strength may lead to unreliable subsidence estimates.

For new mining areas where detailed discontinuity statistics are not yet available, discontinuity density can be preliminarily evaluated using accessible geological information, such as borehole core logging, RQD, core recovery, regional structural surveys, and approximate GSI or rock mass integrity classifications. Although such preliminary information may not be sufficient for precise subsidence prediction, it can help determine whether the selected reference case is structurally comparable to the target area. If clear differences in discontinuity conditions are identified, the analogy result should be treated with caution and supplemented by additional numerical sensitivity analysis or further field investigation.

Limitations and future prospects

While this study employed UDEC to directly investigate the relationship between discontinuity density and mining-induced subsidence and yielded new insights into the mechanical mechanisms involved, certain limitations remain due to the constraints of current computational and modeling capabilities. Acknowledging these limitations helps to clarify the scope of the present findings and indicates directions for future improvement.

It should be firstly noted that, given the inherent complexity of geological conditions and rock mass structures, fully reproducing all aspects of mining-induced overburden movement is beyond the reach of existing numerical modeling technology. The present model therefore represents a balance between computational feasibility and geological realism. Several simplifications inevitably affect the depth of our research. Firstly, discontinuity spacing was not refined below 2 × 2 m, and the simulated overburden thickness was limited to 100 m due to computational constraints. Although this assumption allows the model to remain computationally tractable, it does not directly reflect the conditions of deep coalfields such as the Ordos Basin discussed in this study. Secondly, the actual discontinuity networks, which are typically chaotic and anisotropic in nature, were idealized as orthogonal vertical and horizontal sets (even though such an approach remains very common in current research practices). Thirdly, it must be acknowledged that the 2D nature of the UDEC simulations cannot fully capture the three-dimensional spatial effects of panel advancement. Furthermore, the present models incorporate certain simplifications with respect to other geological conditions, such as the omission of hydro-mechanical coupling associated with groundwater and a simplified representation of the initial in-situ stress field, all of which may influence discontinuity strength and overall rock mass stability.

Looking forward, continuous advances in computing power46, numerical modeling techniques47–49, and emerging technologies50–55 will enable higher-resolution and more realistic simulations. These developments will facilitate a more comprehensive understanding of overburden behavior and provide a stronger theoretical foundation for predicting and managing mining-induced subsidence in support of sustainable resource development.
