Some remarks on L-equivalence for cubic fourfolds and hyper-Kähler manifolds
Simone Billi, Lucas Li Bassi

Abstract
We prove that if two very general cubic fourfolds are L-equivalent then they are isomorphic, and we observe that there exist special cubic fourfolds which are L-equivalent but not isomorphic. When the cubic fourfolds are very general in certain Hassett divisors, we prove that if they are L-equivalent then they are also Fourier-Mukai partners. We also provide further examples in support of the fact that L-equivalent hyper-Kähler manifolds should be D-equivalent, as conjectured by Meinsma.

Introduction

1.1
L-equivalence

The
Grothendieck ring of varieties

K_{0}(\Var_{k})
over a field
k
is the free abelian group generated by isomorphism classes of varieties over
k
, modulo the relations

[X]=[X\setminus Z]-[Z]

for every closed subvariety
Z\subset X
.
The ring structure is given by the product

[X]\cdot[Y]:=[X\times_{k}Y],

which is commutative with unit the class of a point
[\Spec(k)]
.
The class of the affine line

\mathbb{L}:=[\mathbb{A}^{1}_{k}]\in K_{0}(\Var_{k})

is called the
Lefschetz motive
.
The class of a variety in the Grothendieck ring of varieties is a motivic invariant, it also carries information about the birational geometry of the variety and about its derived category.

It was proved by Larsen and Lunts
[
31
]
that the quotient
K_{0}(\Var_{k})/\mathbb{L}
is isomorphic to the free abelian group generated by the stably birational classes of smooth projective varieties. Two varieties
X,Y
are called
D-equivalent
if there is an equivalence
D^{b}(X)\cong D^{b}(Y)
of their bounded derived categories of coherent sheaves. If two varieties are D-equivalent then results of Kawamata
[
27
]
and Kontsevich
[
28
]
prove that their classes are equal in the completion (with respect to the dimension filtration) of the localization
K_{0}(\Var_{k})[\mathbb{L}^{-1}]
. It is then interesting to study the localization
K_{0}(\Var_{k})\to K_{0}(\Var_{k})[\mathbb{L}^{-1}]
, with the difficulty that Borisov proved
[
9
]
that the class
\mathbb{L}
is a zero-divisor in
K_{0}(\Var_{\mathbb{C}})
. This lead Kuznetsov and Shinder to the following definition.

Definition 1.1

(
[
29
]
)
.

Two varieties
X
and
Y
over
k
are
L-equivalent
if there exists a positive integer
r
such that

\mathbb{L}^{r}\cdot([X]-[Y])=0\in K_{0}(\Var_{k}).

It was conjectured by Kuznetsov–Shinder
[
29
, Conjecture 1.6]
that simply connected algebraic varieties that are D-equivalent should be also L-equivalent, independently this was also conjectured by Ito-Miura-Okawa-Ueda
[
26
, Problem 1.2]
without the assumption of simply connectedness.
By a result of Orlov, if two varieties with ample canonical class or anticanonical class are D-equivalent then they are isomorphic. In particular, they are clearly L-equivalent. On the other hand, when the canonical bundle is trivial, this implication is proved to be false in
[
14
,
26
,
37
]
including the simply connected case. Indeed, there exist examples of abelian varieties and projective hyper-Kähler manifolds that are D-equivalent but not L-equivalent. A variant of this conjecture
[
26
, Problem 7.2]
is still an open case and it was proved to be true for abelian varieties in
[
12
]
. The opposite implication is not entirely clear in general, and Meinsma conjectures
[
37
, Conjecture 1.5]
that two L-equivalent projective hyper-Kähler manifolds must be D-equivalent.

2
Hodge lattices and L-equivalence

2.1
Hodge lattices

A
lattice
is a finitely generated free abelian group
L
with a non-degenerate symmetric bilinear form
b\colon L\times L\to\mathbb{Z}
. For elements
v,w\in L
we will write
v^{2}:=b(v,v)
and
v\cdot w:=b(v,w)
. A lattice
L
is called
even
if for any
v\in L
we have
v^{2}\in 2\mathbb{Z}
, it is called
odd
otherwise. We denote by
O(L)
the group of
isometries
, i.e. isomorphisms preserving the bilinear form. We consider the dual lattice
L^{\vee}:=\Hom(L,\mathbb{Z})\cong L\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{Q}
, which naturally contains
L
, and let

D(L):=L^{\vee}/L

be the
discriminant group
. It is a torsion group endowed with a symmetric bilinear form, which takes values in
\mathbb{Q}/2\mathbb{Z}
when
L
is even. We moreover let

\disc(L):=|D(L)|\in\mathbb{Z}

be the
discriminant
of
L
, which is also equal to the absolute value of the determinant of a Gram matrix for
b
. The length of the discriminant group
l(D(L))
is the smaller number of generators for
D(L)
, it does not exceed the rank of
L
. A lattice
L
is called
unimodular
if
\disc(L)=1
, or equivalently
L=L^{\vee}
.
The
genus
of a lattice is the data of its signature, parity and discriminant form. The genus is clearly an isometry invariant, but there are non-isometric lattices with the same genus.

For an integer
n\in\mathbb{Z}
we denote by
L(n)
the lattice obtained by
L
multiplying the bilinear form by
n
. We can allow also rational numbers
q\in\mathbb{Q}
and consider
L(q)
, whose bilinear form takes rational values a priori, and it is a lattice if its quadratic form takes integer values.

An injective morphism of lattices
L\hookrightarrow M
is called an
embedding
, in this case we can see
L\subset M
as a sublattice of
M
and denote by
L^{\perp}\subset M
its orthogonal complement. An embedding of lattices
L\subset M
is called
primitive
if the quotient
M/L
is torsion-free, it is called of
finite index
if the quotient
M/L
is finite and in this case
M
is called an
overlattice
of
L
.

Following
[
38
]
, a primitive embedding
L\subset M
determines a finite index embedding
L\oplus L^{\perp}\subseteq M
and the group

G_{L,M}=\frac{M}{L\oplus L^{\perp}}

is embedded isometrically in
D(L)
and anti-isometrically in
D({L^{\perp}})
. The group
G_{L,M}
is called the
gluing subgroup
. It follows that

|G_{L,M}|^{2}=\frac{\disc(L)\cdot\disc(L^{\perp})}{\disc(M)}.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
L\subseteq\Lambda
be a primitive sublattice of a unimodular lattice
\Lambda
. Let
T
be a primitive sublattice of
L
, assume there exist
n\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
such that
T(n)
admits a primitive embedding in
L
. If
2\rk T>\rk\Lambda
then
n=\pm 1
.

Proof.

Assume by contradiction that
n\not=\pm 1
. Then
D({T(n)})
contains a subgroup isomorphic to
(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^{\rk T}
, generated by an integral basis of
T(n)
multiplied by
\frac{1}{n}
. Since
\Lambda
is unimodular, from (
1
) we have that the primitive embedding
T(n)\subset\Lambda
induces an isomorphism
D({T(n)})\cong D(N)
, where
N=T(n)^{\perp}\subset\Lambda
. This means that
\rk T\leq\rk N=\rk\Lambda-\rk T
which implies
2\rk T\leq\rk\Lambda
, a contradiction.
∎

Definition 2.2
.

1)

A polarized torsion-free integral Hodge structure is called a
Hodge lattice
.

2)

A Hodge structure
H
of weight
2n
is called of
K3 type
if it is torsion free,
\dim_{\mathbb{C}}H^{n+1,n-1}=1
and
H^{n+i,n-i}=0
for
i=2,\dots,n
.

3)

A Hodge lattice of K3 type is called
irreducible
if it admits no primitive proper Hodge sublattice of K3 type.

The polarization of a torsion-free Hodge structure gives a lattice structure which is compatible with the Hodge structure. In particular, a polarized Hodge structure of K3 type has the structure of a Hodge lattice. The main example of irreducible Hodge lattice will be the transcendental part of a Hodge structure of K3 type.
For a Hodge lattice
H
, we denote by
\End(H)
the algebra of Hodge endomorfisms, by
\Aut(H)
the group of Hodge automorfisms and by
O_{Hdg}(H)
the group of Hodge isometries.

The following very useful and technical result about Hodge lattices will be fundamental.

Proposition 2.3

(
[
36
, Proposition 1.1 and Lemma 3.1]
)
.

Let
T
and
T^{\prime}
be irreducible Hodge lattices of K3 type. Assume that
\End(T)=\mathbb{Z}
and that there is an isomorphism of Hodge structures
T\cong T^{\prime}
. Then there exists
q\in\mathbb{Q}
such that
T^{\prime}(q)
is integral and there is a Hodge isometry
T\cong T^{\prime}(q)
.

If
H
is a polarizable Hodge structure of K3 type and weight
2n
, we consider the
algebraic part

A(H):=H^{n,n}\cap H
. Let
T(H)
be the minimal integral Hodge sub-structure of
H
such that

\bigoplus_{i=1}^{n}H^{n+i,n-i}\subset T(H)_{\mathbb{C}},

we call it the
transcendental part
. We also set

G(H):=\frac{H}{A(H)\oplus T(H)}.

After fixing a polarization, we can consider the orthogonal complement
A(H)^{\perp}
, in this case
(A(H)^{\perp})^{n+i,n-i}=H^{n+i,n-i}
for any
i=1,\dots,n
so that
A(H)^{\perp}=T(H)
if
H
is torsion-free. If
H
is torsion free and polarized, then it has the structure of a Hodge lattice. In particular,
A(H)
and
T(H)
are primitive sublattices and we get a finite index Hodge embedding
A(H)\oplus T(H)\subseteq H
, so that
G(H)=G_{A(H),H}
is the gluing subgroup.

We will be mainly interested in the case of Hodge structures of K3 type.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
H
and
H^{\prime}
be polarized Hodge structures of K3 type of weight
2n
, assume that
H
and
H^{\prime}
are isometric as lattices and that there is a Hodge isometry
f\colon T(H)\cong T(H^{\prime})
. Then there exists a Hodge isometry
h\colon H\cong H^{\prime}
restricting to
f
if and only if there is an isometry
g\colon A(H)\cong A(H^{\prime})
such that
f
and
g
induce the same isometry from
G(H)
to
G(H^{\prime})
.

Proof.

On the lattice-theoretical level, this is precisely
[
38
, Corollary 1.5.2]
. We then observe that such an
h
is Hodge if and only
f
is. In fact, since
f=h_{\mid T(H)}
then
f
is Hodge from
T(H)
to
T(H^{\prime})
if and only if
h
is, moreover we have
g(A(H)_{\mathbb{C}})=A(H^{\prime})_{\mathbb{C}}
and also
H_{\mathbb{C}}=A(H)_{\mathbb{C}}\oplus T(H)_{\mathbb{C}}
since
H
is polarized (of K3 type), similarly for
H^{\prime}
.
∎

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
H
and
H^{\prime}
be polarized Hodge structures of K3 type of weight
2n
such that
H
and
H^{\prime}
are isometric as lattices and that
\rk A(H)\geq l(D({A(H)}))+2
. Assume that there is a Hodge isometry
f\colon T(H)\cong T(H^{\prime})
and that
A(H)
and
A(H^{\prime})
are even indefinite and in the same genus. Then there is an isometry
g\colon A(H)\cong A(H^{\prime})
and a Hodge isometry
h\colon H\cong H^{\prime}
restricting to
f
and

.

Proof.

By
2.4
it suffices to find an isometry
g\colon A(H)\cong A(H^{\prime})
inducing the same isometry

as

. From the fact that

and

are even indefinite and in the same genus with
\rk A(H)\geq l(D({A(H)}))+2
, we know by

38

that they are isometric and that the map

is surjective. Hence, we can always find the wanted isometry

.
∎

2.2
Implications of L-equivalence

As in
[
14
]
, we consider the additive category
\HS_{\mathbb{Z}}
of integral polarizable Hodge structures and for any
n
the subcategory
\HS_{\mathbb{Z},n}
of integral polarizable Hodge structures of weight
n
.
We consider the abelian group
K_{0}(\Hdg_{\mathbb{Z}})
generated by elements of
\Hdg_{\mathbb{Z}}
and subject to the relations

[H_{1}\oplus H_{2}]=[H_{1}]+[H_{2}]

for any
H_{1},H_{2}\in\Hdg_{\mathbb{Z}}
.
There is a homomorphism of additive groups

\Hdg_{\mathbb{Z}}\colon K_{0}(\Var_{\mathbb{C}})\to K_{0}(\HS_{\mathbb{Z}})

where
\Hdg_{\mathbb{Z}}([X])=[H^{*}(X,\mathbb{Z})]
, that factors through the localization
K_{0}(\Var_{\mathbb{C}})[\mathbb{L}^{-1}]
, see
[
14
]
. A similar definition can be given for
\Hdg_{\mathbb{Z},n}
.

For the following, we readapt the definitions and results given in
[
36
, Section 4]
.
Consider an integral polarizable Hodge structure
H\in\HS_{\mathbb{Z},2n}
of weight
2n
.
There is an additive endofunctor
\mathcal{T}\colon\Hdg_{\mathbb{Z},2n}\to\Hdg_{\mathbb{Z},2n}
associating to a Hodge structure its transcendental part. In particular, if
[H]=[H^{\prime}]
then
[T(H)]=[T(H^{\prime})]
.
Moreover, we have a group homomorphism

\mathcal{G}\colon K_{0}(\HS_{\mathbb{Z},2n})\to\mathbb{Q}^{*}

such that
\mathcal{G}([H])=|G(H)|
.

Recall that we have

|G(H)|^{2}=\frac{\disc(A(H))\cdot\disc(T(H))}{\disc(H)}.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
H
and
H^{\prime}
be unimodular Hodge lattices of K3 type, and let
T
and
T^{\prime}
be their transcendental parts. Assume
[H]=[H^{\prime}]\in\HS_{\mathbb{Z}}
, then
\disc(T)=\disc(T^{\prime})
.

Proof.

Since the Hodge lattice
H
is unimodular, we have
G(H)\cong D(T)
and hence
\mathcal{G}([H])=|D(T)|=\disc T
. Now,
\disc(T)=\mathcal{G}([H])=\mathcal{G}([H^{\prime}])=\disc(T^{\prime})
.
∎

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
T
and
T^{\prime}
be Hodge lattices of K3 type. Assume that
\End(T)=\mathbb{Z}
and that
[T]=[T^{\prime}]\in K_{0}(\HS_{\mathbb{Z}})
. If
\disc T=\disc T^{\prime}
then there is a Hodge isometry
T\cong T^{\prime}(\pm 1)
.

Proof.

Since
\End(T)=\mathbb{Z}
, by
[
14
, Theorem 2.3]
we have that there exists an isomorphism of Hodge structures
T\cong T^{\prime}
.
In this case there is a Hodge isometry
T\cong T^{\prime}(q)
by
2.3
, implying

\disc T=q^{\rk T}\disc T^{\prime}

from which we deduce that

since
\disc T=\disc T
.
∎

T(Y)\cong T(Y^{\prime})
. In particular, we have a Hodge isometry
H^{4}_{prim}(Y,\mathbb{Z})\cong H^{4}_{prim}(Y^{\prime},\mathbb{Z})
and, up to composing it with
-\mathrm{id}
, by
2.4
this extends to a Hodge isometry
H^{4}(Y,\mathbb{Z})\cong H^{4}(Y^{\prime},\mathbb{Z})
mapping
\eta_{X}
to
\eta_{Y^{\prime}}
. Hence, by the Torelli Theorem for cubic fourfolds
[
44
]
we can conclude that
Y\cong Y^{\prime}
.
∎

One might wonder if a similar statement holds for special subic fourfolds. This happens not to be the case, for this we recall a construction given by Fan-Lai
[
15
]
.

A very general cubic fourfold
Y\in\mathcal{C}_{20}
contains a Veronese surface
V\subset\mathbb{P}^{5}
and the system of quadrics containing
V
determines a Cremona transformation

F_{V}\colon\mathbb{P}^{5}\dashrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{5}.

The indeterminacy locus of the birational inverse
F_{V}^{-1}
is a Veronese surface
V^{\prime}\subset\mathbb{P}^{5}
projective equivalent to
V
.

Proposition 3.4

(
[
15
, Theorem 1.1]
)
.

Let
Y\in\mathcal{C}_{20}
be a very general cubic fourfold containing a Veronese surface
V
and let
Y^{\prime}
be the proper image of
Y
via
F_{V}
. Then
Y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}_{20}
is a cubic fourfold containing
V^{\prime}
, and it is the unique cubic fourfold such that
Y
and
Y^{\prime}
are FM partners but
Y\not\cong Y^{\prime}
.

We recall that two cubic fourfolds
Y
and
Y^{\prime}
are called
Fourier-Mukai (FM) partners
if there is Fourier-Mukai equivalence
\mathcal{A}_{Y}\cong\mathcal{A}_{Y^{\prime}}
. Here
\mathcal{A}_{Y}:=\langle\mathcal{O},\mathcal{O}(1),\mathcal{O}(2)\rangle^{\perp}\subset D^{b}(Y)
, see
[
30
]
.

The previous examples are then easily seen to be L-equivalent.

Proposition 3.5
.

There exist special cubic fourfolds
Y
and
Y^{\prime}
which are L-equivalent, but not isomorphic (and in particular not D-equivalent). Moreover,
Y
is a FM partner of
Y^{\prime}
and we can choose
Y
so that
\End(T(Y))=\mathbb{Z}
.

Proof.

Let
Y\in\mathcal{C}_{20}
be a very general cubic fourfold, containing a Veronese surface
V,
and
Y^{\prime}
be the proper image of the Cremona transformation
F_{V}
. The indeterminacy locus of
F_{V}
is a Veronese surface
V\subset Y
and the indeterminacy locus of the inverse
F_{V}^{-1}
is a projective equivalent Veronese surface
V^{\prime}
. Thus, we can write the following relation

[Y]-[Y^{\prime}]=([Y\setminus V]+[V])-([Y^{\prime}\setminus V^{\prime}]+[V^{\prime}])=0\in K_{0}(\Var_{\mathbb{C}})

since
[V]=[V^{\prime}]
and
[Y\setminus V]=[Y^{\prime}\setminus V^{\prime}]
from the above. In particular,
Y
and
Y^{\prime}
are L-equivalent and by
3.4
they are FM partners and not isomorphic. Since
Y
is very general in
\mathcal{C}_{20}
, we have
\End(Y)=\mathbb{Z}
by
2.8
.
∎

For a cubic fourfold
Y
, we consider the lattice
\widetilde{H}(\mathcal{A}_{Y},\mathbb{Z}):=K_{top}(\mathcal{A}_{Y})
which is isometric to
U^{\oplus 4}\oplus E_{8}(-1)^{\oplus 2}
.
We denote by
A(\mathcal{A}_{Y})
and
T(\mathcal{A}_{Y})
the algebraic and transcendental sublattices, respectively. Moreover,
\widetilde{H}(\mathcal{A}_{Y},\mathbb{Z})
is endowed with a Hodge structure such that there is a Hodge isometry
T(Y)(-1)\cong T(\mathcal{A}_{Y})
, see
[
2
]
.

Theorem 3.6
.

Consider a very general cubic fourfold
Y\in\mathcal{C}_{d}
, for
d
not divisible by
9
. If
Y^{\prime}
is a cubic fourfold which is L-equivalent to
Y
, then
Y
and
Y^{\prime}
are FM partners.

Proof.

By
[
24
, Theorem 1.5 iii)]
, this is equivalent to prove that there is a Hodge isometry
\widetilde{H}(\mathcal{A}_{Y},\mathbb{Z})\cong\widetilde{H}(\mathcal{A}_{Y^{\prime}},\mathbb{Z})
.

Firstly, we observe that by
[
1
, Proposition 2.8]
we have a Hodge isometry
T(Y)(-1)\cong T(\mathcal{A}_{Y})
. From
[
15
, Lemma 2.4]
we know that the isometry class of
T(Y)
is determined by
d
, and the discriminant group
D({T(Y)})
is cyclic when
d
is not divided by
9
. Hence, in our hypothesis
D({T(\mathcal{A}_{Y})})
is cyclic and also
D({A(\mathcal{A}_{Y})})
is so from the fact that
D({T(\mathcal{A}_{Y})})
is anti-isometric to
D({A(\mathcal{A}_{Y})})
because
\widetilde{H}(\mathcal{A}_{Y},\mathbb{Z})
is unimodular.

Since
Y
is very general in
\mathcal{C}_{d}
, we have that
\End(T(Y))=\mathbb{Z}
by
2.8
and also
\rk T(Y)=21
. In this case
3.2
gives a Hodge isometry
T(Y)\cong T(Y^{\prime})
, in particular this implies that
D({A(\mathcal{A}_{Y})})
and
D({A(\mathcal{A}_{Y^{\prime}})})
are isometric. From the fact that
l(D({A(\mathcal{A}_{Y})}))=1
and
A(\mathcal{A}_{Y})
is indefinite with
\rk A(\mathcal{A}_{Y})=3
, we can apply
2.5
to get a Hodge isometry
\widetilde{H}(\mathcal{A}_{Y},\mathbb{Z})\cong\widetilde{H}(\mathcal{A}_{Y^{\prime}},\mathbb{Z})
.
∎

Remark 3.7
.

Note that by a conjecture of Huybrechts
[
25
, Conjecture 3.21]
for two cubic fourfolds being FM partners implies being birational. A well known counter-example to the converse are two general Pfaffian cubic fourfolds, i.e. two general cubic fourfolds in
\mathcal{C}_{14}
, which are birational since both rational by
[
5
]
and in general not FM partners by
[
24
, Theorem 1.5 iii)]
. The same example shows that birationality does not imply L-equivalence for cubic fourfolds by virtue of
Theorem 3.6
.

4
Hyper-Kähler manifolds

A hyper-Kähler manifold
X
will be a compact Kähler manifold which is simply connected and such that
H^{0}(X,\Omega^{2}_{X})=\sigma_{X}\cdot\mathbb{C}
for an everywhere nondegenerate holomorphic 2-form
\sigma_{X}
. Since L-equivalence is for algebraic varieties, we will consider projective hyper-Kähler manifolds. In this case
H^{2}(X,\mathbb{Z})
is a torsion-free polarized Hodge structure of K3 type of weight
2
, where the lattice structure is given by the Beauville-Bogomolov-Fujiki form of signature
(3,b_{2}(X)-3)
. The algebraic part coincides with the Néron-Severi lattice
\NS(X)=H^{1,1}(X)\cap H^{2}(X,\mathbb{Z})
and it transcendental part is given by
T(X)=\NS(X)^{\perp}
, in analogy to the case of K3 surfaces (see for example
[
23
, Lemma 3.1]
). Also, since
X
is projective, the signature of
\NS(X)
is of type
(1,-)

[
20
, Proposition 26.13]
. One can refer for example to
[
20
,
13
]
for generalities about hyper-Kähler manifolds.

Manifolds of
K3
[n]
type are hyper-Kähler manifolds that are deformation equivalent to the Hilbert scheme of
n
points on a K3 surface
[
6
]
. Manifolds of
OG10 type
are hyper-Kähler manifolds that are deformation equivalent to the symplectic resolution of a moduli spaces of sheaves on a K3 surfaces as in
[
41
]
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
X
and
X^{\prime}
be L-equivalent projective hyper-Kähler manifolds of the same deformation type such that
\disc H^{2}(X,\mathbb{Z})=p
is a prime, then
\disc T(X)=c\cdot\disc T(X^{\prime})
with
c\in\{1,p,\frac{1}{p}\}
.

Proof.

We have
m=|G(X)|=|G(X^{\prime})|
, moreover
m|\disc T(X)
and
m|\disc\NS(X)
and similarly for
X^{\prime}
. Observing that from (
1
)

pm^{2}=\disc T(X)\cdot\disc\NS(X)

we deduce that
\disc T(X)\in\{m,pm\}
, similarly for
X^{\prime}
.
∎

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
X
and
X^{\prime}
be L-equivalent projective hyper-Kähler manifolds of the same deformation type and assume that
\disc H^{2}(X,\mathbb{Z})=p
a prime number,
\End(T(X))=\mathbb{Z}
and
\rk T(X)\not=4
. If there is a primitive embedding
H^{2}(X,\mathbb{Z})\subseteq\Lambda
in a unimodular lattice
\Lambda
with
2\rk T(X)>\rk\Lambda
, then there is a Hodge isometry
T(X)\cong T(X^{\prime})
.

Proof.

Since
\End(T(M))=\mathbb{Z}
, then by
2.7
together with
4.1
there is a Hodge isometry
T(X)\cong T(X^{\prime})(q)
for
q\in\{\pm 1,\pm p,\pm\frac{1}{p}\}
. Up to exchanging
X
and
X^{\prime}
, we can assume
q\in\mathbb{Z}
. From the hypothesis that there is a primitive embedding
H^{2}(X,\mathbb{Z})\subseteq\Lambda
in a unimodular lattice
\Lambda
and
2\rk T(X)>\rk\Lambda
, we can apply
2.1
to deduce that
q=\pm 1
. Since
X
and
X^{\prime}
are projective, then
T(X)
and
T(X^{\prime})
have signature
(2,-)
and by
2.9
with
\rk T(X)\not=4
we get a Hodge isometry
T(X)\cong T(X^{\prime})
.
∎

Remark 4.3
.

We recall the following:

1.

For
X
of OG10 type, we have that
H^{2}(X,\mathbb{Z})\cong U^{\oplus 3}\oplus E_{8}(-1)^{\oplus 2}\oplus A_{2}(-1)
as lattices. In particular,
\disc H^{2}(X,\mathbb{Z})=3
and there is a primitive embedding in the unimodular lattice
\Lambda=U^{\oplus 5}\oplus E_{8}(-1)^{\oplus 2}
of rank
26
. Hence,
4.2
can be applied for
\rk T(X)>13
.

2.

For
X
of K3
[2]
type, we have that
H^{2}(X,\mathbb{Z})\cong U^{\oplus 3}\oplus E_{8}(-1)^{\oplus 2}\oplus[-2]
as lattices. In particular,
\disc H^{2}(X,\mathbb{Z})=2
and there is a primitive embedding in the unimodular lattice
\Lambda=U^{\oplus 4}\oplus E_{8}(-1)^{\oplus 2}
of rank
24
. Hence,
4.2
can be applied for
\rk T(X)>12
.

4.1
Hyper-Kähler manifolds from a cubic fourfold

4.1.1
The Fano variety of lines

We firstly consider the Fano variety of lines
F(Y)
associated to a cubic fourfold
Y
, this is a projective hyper-Kähler manifold of K3
[2]
type
[
5
]
.

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
Y
and
Y^{\prime}
be L-equivalent cubic fourfolds, then their Fano varieties of lines

and
F(Y^{\prime})
are L-equivalent.

Proof.

We know by
[
39
, Lemma 2.1]
that
Y^{[2]}
and

are L-equivalent. Moreover, by
[
17
, Theorem 5.1]
we have that

\mathbb{L}^{2}([F(Y)]-[F(Y^{\prime})])=([Y^{[2]}]-[Y^{\prime[2]}])+[\mathbb{P}^{4}]([Y]-[Y^{\prime}])\in K_{0}(\Var_{\mathbb{C}}).

The first part of the statement follows multiplying by
\mathbb{L}^{m}
where
m
is big enough so that
[Y^{[2]}]-[Y^{\prime[2]}]
and
[Y]-[Y^{\prime}]
are annihilated, recalling that the Grothendiek ring of varieties is commutative.
∎

In particular, if
Y
and
Y^{\prime}
are cubic fourfolds as in
3.4
then by

and
4.4
we get that

and

are L-equivalent. However, it was already known by
[

that

.

4.1.2
The LSV manifolds

We now treat the case of projective hyper-Kähler manifolds that are birational to the Laza–Saccà–Voisin (LSV) manifold
J(Y)
(or its twisted version

) for a cubic fourfold
Y
. These are hyper-Kähler manifolds of OG10 type obtained as compactifications of the fibration (twisted) intermediate Jacobian of hyperplane section of cubic fourfolds, we refer to
[

,
45
]
for their construction.

Proposition 4.5
.

Let
X
be a manifold of OG10 type birational to
J(Y)
(or
J^{t}(Y)
) for a very general cubic fourfold
Y
. Let
T^{\prime}
be a cubic fourfold and
X^{\prime}
be another manifold of OG10 type birational to
J(Y^{\prime})
(or
J^{t}(Y^{\prime})
). If
X
is L-equivalent to
X
, then
Y\cong Y^{\prime}
and
X
is birational to
X^{\prime}
.

Proof.

We have that
T(X)
is Hodge anti-isometric to
T(Y)
, see
[
32
,
42
]
and
[
7
, Proposition 5.1]
. Since
Y
is very general we know by
2.8
that
\End(T(X))=\mathbb{Z}
. In this case we have
\rk T(X)=22
and by
4.3
we can apply
4.2
to deduce that there is a Hodge isometry
T(X)\cong T(X^{\prime})
. In particular, we obtain a Hodge isometry
T(Y)\cong T(Y^{\prime})
and by the Torelli Theorem for cubic fourfold
[
44
]
with the fact that

and

are very general, we get

.
In conclusion, we have that
X
and
X^{\prime}
are birational to

(or

).
∎

We have that if

is very general then
\NS(J(Y))\cong U
and
\NS(J^{t}(Y))\cong U(3)
, moreover there are rational Hodge isometries
T_{\mathbb{Q}}(J(Y))\cong T_{\mathbb{Q}}(J^{t}(Y))\cong T_{\mathbb{Q}}(Y)(-1)
. This implies that there exists

such that there is a Hodge isometry

, since there is an isometry

we then have a Hodge isometry

.

We then wonder if

and

can be L-equivalent, and notice that by

they are not birational.

4.1.3
Moduli spaces on the Kuznetsov component

We finally treat the case of hyper-Kähler manifolds that are constructed from moduli spaces of Bridgeland (semi-)stable objects in the Kuznetsov component
\mathcal{A}_{Y}
of a cubic fourfold
Y
.

The first stability condition on
\mathcal{A}_{Y}
was constructed in
[
4
]
. We distinguish the connected component
\Stab^{\dagger}(\mathcal{A}_{Y})\subset\Stab(\mathcal{A}_{Y})
of the manifold of stability conditions containing such a condition, and for
a Mukai vector
v\in\widetilde{H}(\mathcal{A}_{Y},\mathbb{Z})
and a stability condition
\sigma\in\Stab^{\dagger}(\mathcal{A}_{Y})
, denote by
M_{v}(\sigma)
the moduli space of
\sigma
-semistable objects in
\mathcal{A}_{Y}
with Mukai vector
v\in A(\mathcal{A}_{Y})
. We also recall that there are always two algebraic classes
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\in A(\mathcal{A}_{Y})
spanning a primitive sublattice isometric to
A_{2}
, and when
Y
is very general we have
A(\mathcal{A}_{Y})\cong A_{2}
.

Proposition 4.6

(
[
3
,
33
]
)
.

Let
v\in\widetilde{H}(\mathcal{A}_{Y},\mathbb{Z})
and
\sigma\in\Stab^{\dagger}(\mathcal{A}_{Y})
be a
v
-generic stability condition.

1.

If
v
is primitive, then
M_{v}(\sigma)
is hyper-Kähler of K3
[n]
type with
n=\frac{v^{2}+2}{2}
.

2.

If
v=2v_{0}
with
v_{0}
primitive and
v_{0}^{2}=2
, then
M_{v}(\sigma)
admits a symplectic resolution
\widetilde{M}_{v}(\sigma)
via blow-up which is a hyperkähler manifold of OG10 type.

For this reason, we will call a vector
v\in\widetilde{H}(\mathcal{A}_{Y},\mathbb{Z})
of
O’Grady type
if
v=2v_{0}
for a primitive vector
v_{0}
with
v_{0}^{2}=2
.

Most of the propositions proved in the previous sections rely on finding some connection between the L-equivalence and Hodge theoretic properties of cubic fourfolds. This is why the following lemma is particularly useful in order to deal with
M_{v}(\sigma)
for some
\sigma
in
\Stab^{\dagger}(\mathcal{A}_{Y})
and
v\in\widetilde{H}(Y,\mathbb{Z})
.

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
\sigma
be a
v
-generic stability condition in
\Stab^{\dagger}(\mathcal{A}_{Y})
for either a primitive or a O’Grady type Mukai vector
v\in\widetilde{H}(Y,\mathbb{Z})
. Then
T(M_{v}(\sigma))\simeq T(Y)(-1)
.

Proof.

We know by
[
19
, Proposition 2.8]
and
[
3
, Theorem 1.6]
that in both cases there exists a Hodge isometry
H^{2}(M_{v}(\sigma),\mathbb{Z})\simeq v^{\perp}\subset\widetilde{H}(\mathcal{A}_{Y},\mathbb{Z})
. Moreover, by
[
1
, Proposition 2.8]
there exists another Hodge isometry
\langle\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\rangle^{\perp}=A_{2}^{\perp}\simeq H^{4}_{prim}(Y,\mathbb{Z})(-1)
where the first orthogonal complement is taken in the Mukai lattice of
\mathcal{A}_{Y}
. As
v\in\widetilde{H}(\mathcal{A}_{Y},\mathbb{Z})
lies in the algebraic part we deduce from both the isometries that
T(M_{v}(\sigma))\simeq T(Y)(-1)
. In case
v
is of O’Grady type, then
\widetilde{M}_{v}(\sigma)
is given by a blow-up on the singular locus of
M_{v}(\sigma)
, so that
T(\widetilde{M}_{v}(\sigma))\simeq T(M_{v}(\sigma))\simeq T(Y)(-1)
.
∎

The following lemma tells us that when
Y
is very general, a primitive Mukai vector
v\in A(\mathcal{A}_{Y})
is determined by its square up to isometry.

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
v,w\in A_{2}
be two primitive elements with the same square. Then
v^{\perp}\simeq w^{\perp}
as lattices.

Proof.

Let
v=ae_{1}+be_{2}
for a basis
\{e_{1},e_{2}\}
of
A_{2}
. Then a generator of
v^{\perp}
will be a primitive element
u=xe_{1}+ye_{2}
such that
(2a-b)x+(2b-a)y=0
. We call
p:=(2a-b)
and
q:=(2b-a)
. The diophantine equation
px+qy=0
has solutions

x=k\frac{q}{g},\ y=-k\frac{p}{g},\qquad g:=\gcd(p,q),\quad k\in\mathbb{Z}.

The only primitive solutions are thus given by
k=\pm 1
. In both cases we get

u^{2}=\frac{2}{g^{2}}\left(q^{2}+p^{2}+pq\right)=\frac{6}{g^{2}}\left(a^{2}+b^{2}-ab\right)=\frac{3}{g^{2}}v^{2}.

Note that
p\equiv q\equiv 2(a+b)\mod 3
. Moreover,
g\mid(2p+q)=3a
and
g\mid(2q+p)=3b
. As
v
is primitive we know that
\gcd(a,b)=1
, therefore
g\mid 3
, i.e.
g\in\left\{\pm 1,\pm 3\right\}
. Now, we have the identity
a^{2}+b^{2}-ab\equiv(a+b)^{2}\mod 3
. Therefore,

0\equiv v^{2}=2(a^{2}+b^{2}-ab)\mod 3\iff 0\equiv a+b\equiv p\equiv q\mod 3\iff g=\pm 3.

This shows that the square of the primitive element
u
that generates the orthogonal complement of a primitive vector
v\in A_{2}
is
u^{2}=\frac{v^{2}}{3}
if
v^{2}\equiv 0\mod 3
or, else,
u^{2}=3v^{2}
. In both cases it is uniquely determined by the square of
v
.
∎

Remark 4.9
.

If
v=kv_{0}
for
k\in\mathbb{Z}
and
v_{0}
primitive we get that an element
u\in v^{\perp}\iff u\in v_{0}^{\perp}
, so the same result of
4.8
holds for
v=kv_{0}
,
w=kw_{0}
with
v_{0},w_{0}
primitive vectors with the same square.

Now we are ready to state and prove the following proposition in the same fashion of
4.5
.

Proposition 4.10
.

Let
Y
and
Y^{\prime}
be two cubic fourfolds and assume that
Y
is very general. Let
v\in A(\mathcal{A}_{Y})
and
w\in A(\mathcal{A}_{Y^{\prime}})
be either primitive with same square or of O’Grady type. Let
\sigma_{Y}\in\Stab^{\dagger}(Y)
and
\sigma_{Y^{\prime}}\in\Stab^{\dagger}(Y^{\prime})
be a
v
-generic and a
w
-generic stability condition, respectively. Suppose that
M_{v}(\sigma_{Y})
and
M_{w}(\sigma_{Y^{\prime}})
are L-equivalent, then
Y\cong Y^{\prime}
and
M_{v}(\sigma_{Y})
is birational to
M_{w}(\sigma_{Y^{\prime}})
. In case
M_{v}(\sigma_{Y})
and
M_{w}(\sigma_{Y^{\prime}})
are of K3
[n]
type, then they are also D-equivalent.

Proof.

By
4.7
we get that
T(M_{v}(\sigma_{Y}))
is Hodge anti-isometric to
T(Y)
and
T(M_{w}(\sigma_{Y}))
to
T(Y^{\prime})
. Since
Y
is very general we know by
2.8
that
\End(T(X))=\mathbb{Z}
. Since
Y
is very general, we have
T(Y)=H_{prim}^{4}(Y,\mathbb{Z})
and similarly for
Y^{\prime}
. From the fact that
T(Y)
and
T(Y^{\prime})
are isometric of signature
(22,2)
, by
2.7
and
2.9
we get Hodge isometries
T(Y)\simeq T(M_{v}(\sigma_{Y}))(-1)\simeq T(M_{w}(\sigma_{Y^{\prime}}))(-1)\simeq T(Y^{\prime})
. From the first and the last Hodge isometries we get a Hodge isometry
H^{4}(Y,\mathbb{Z})\cong H^{4}(Y^{\prime},\mathbb{Z})
. The Torelli theorem for cubic fourfolds implies then then
Y\simeq Y^{\prime}
. Moreover, we have
A(\mathcal{A}_{Y})\simeq A(\mathcal{A}_{Y^{\prime}})\simeq A_{2}
and by
[
1
, Proposition 2.8]
we get Hodge isometries
A_{2}^{\perp}=T(\mathcal{A}_{Y})\cong H^{4}_{prim}(Y,\mathbb{Z})(-1)\simeq H^{4}_{prim}(Y^{\prime},\mathbb{Z})(-1)\cong T(\mathcal{A}_{Y^{\prime}})
.

As
v,w\in A_{2}
share the same square and are either primitive or of O’Grady type we deduce from
4.8
, with the fact that
O(A_{2})\to O(D({A_{2}}))
is surjective, that they have isomorphic embeddings in
A_{2}
. So, we can deduce the following chain of Hodge isometries

H^{2}(M_{v}(\sigma_{Y}),\mathbb{Z})\simeq v^{\perp}\simeq w^{\perp}\simeq H^{2}(M_{w}(\sigma_{Y^{\prime}}),\mathbb{Z}).

(2)

If
v
and
w
are of O’Grady type then the Hodge isometry of (
2
) induces a Hodge isometry
H^{2}(\widetilde{M}_{v}(\sigma_{Y}))\simeq H^{2}(\widetilde{M}_{w}(\sigma_{Y^{\prime}}))
. Moreover, by
[
40
]
the monodromy group of a manifolds of OG10 type
X
is given by
O^{+}(H^{2}(X,\mathbb{Z}))
, thus, up to composing by
-\mathrm{id}
we can assume that the Hodge isometry is monodromy. The Hodge theoretic Torelli theorem for hyper-Kähler manifolds
[
34
, Theorem 1.3]
implies that
M_{v}(\sigma_{Y})
is birational to
M_{w}(\sigma_{Y^{\prime}})
.

If
v
and
w
are primitive, then
M_{v}(\sigma_{Y})
and
M_{w}(\sigma_{Y^{\prime}})
are of type
K3^{[n]}
with
n=\frac{v^{2}+2}{2}

4.2
Hyper-Kähler manifolds from K3 surfaces

4.2.1
Desingularizations of moduli spaces of sheaves on a K3

We firstly consider the case where the hyper-Kähler manifolds are birational to desingularizations of moduli spaces of sheaves on K3 surfaces.

Proposition 4.13
.

Let
X
be a manifold of OG10 type with
\NS(X)=U\oplus A_{2}(-1)
,
\disc T(X)=1
and
\End(T(X))=\mathbb{Z}
. Let
X^{\prime}
be another manifold of OG10 type which is L-equivalent to
X
, then
X
and
X^{\prime}
are birational. Moreover,
X
and
X^{\prime}
are D-equivalent.

Proof.

Since
\disc T(X)=1
, the primitive vector
\sigma\in A_{2}(-1)\subset\NS(X)
with
\sigma^{2}=-6
and of divisibility
3
in
A_{2}(-1)
has then divisibility
3
also in
H^{2}(X,\mathbb{Z})
.
By
[
16
, Theorem 3.13]
we have that
X
is birational to a moduli space of sheaves on a K3 surface. Moreover, we have
\rk T(X)=20>13
and by
4.2
there is a Hodge isometry
T(X)\cong T(X^{\prime})
in view of
4.3
.
In particular,
T(X^{\prime})
is also unimodular and from this it follows that
\NS(X^{\prime})
has the same discriminant form as
\NS(X)
(which is the one of
H^{2}(X,\mathbb{Z})
), hence
\NS(X)
and
\NS(X^{\prime})
are in the same genus.
Since the two lattices are even indefinite and
\rk\NS(X)\geq l(\NS(X))+2
, we can extend the Hodge isometry
T(X)\cong T(X^{\prime})
to a Hodge isometry
H^{2}(X,\mathbb{Z})\cong H^{2}(X^{\prime},\mathbb{Z})
by
2.5
. Up to composing it with
-\mathrm{id}
, the Hodge isometry can be taken to be monodromy by
[
40
, Theorem 5.4]
and the Torelli Theorem for hyper-Kähler manifolds
[
34
, Theorem 1.3]
implies that
X
and
X^{\prime}
are birational.

In particular, they are birational to a moduli space of sheaves on a K3 surface and then by
[
21
, Theorem 4.5.1]
they are D-equivalent.

∎

4.2.2
Hilbert schemes of points on a K3 surface

We now consider manifolds of K3
[n]
type that are birational so Hilbert schemes of points on a K3 surface.

Proposition 4.14
.

Let
X
birational to
S^{[n]}
for a K3 surface
S
with
U\subseteq\NS(S)
, and assume that
\End(T(X))=\mathbb{Z}
and
\rk T(X)\not=4
. If
X^{\prime}
is a manifold of K3
[n]
type which is L-equivalent to
X
and
\disc T(X)=\disc T(X^{\prime})=m
is coprime to
2(n-1)
, then
X
and
X^{\prime}
are D-equivalent.

Proof.

Since
\disc T(X)=\disc T(X^{\prime})
,
\End(T(X))=\mathbb{Z}
and
\rk T(X)\not=4
, then by
2.7
and
2.9
we have a Hodge isometry
T(X)\cong T(X^{\prime})
.

Firstly, we want to show that
D({\NS(X)})
is isometric to
D({\NS(X^{\prime})})
.
By hypothesis, the discriminant group
D({T(X^{\prime})})
does not contain any subgroup isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}/2(n-1)\mathbb{Z}
so that the primitive embedding
T(X^{\prime})\subset H^{2}(X^{\prime},\mathbb{Z})
has trivial embedding subgroup and then the discriminant of the orthogonal complement
\NS(X^{\prime})
is given by
D({\NS(X^{\prime})})\cong D({T(X^{\prime})})(-1)\oplus D({[-2(n-1)]})
, which is isometric to the discriminant form of
\NS(X)\cong\NS(S)\oplus[-2(n-1)]
from the fact that
D(\NS(S))\cong D(T(S))(-1)\cong D(T(X))(-1)
.

From the fact that
U\subseteq\NS(X)
we have
\rk\NS(X)\geq l(\NS(X))+2
.
Moreover, since
\NS(X)
and
\NS(X^{\prime})
have the same genus we are in the place to apply
2.5
to get a Hodge isometry
H^{2}(X,\mathbb{Z})\cong H^{2}(X^{\prime},\mathbb{Z})
. Up to composing with
-\mathrm{id}
the Hodge isometry can be chosen to be orientation-preserving. Moreover, since
\End(T(X))=\mathbb{Z}
and
O(\NS(X))\to O(D(\NS(X)))
is surjective with the fact that
D({\NS(X)})\cong D({T(X)})(-1)\oplus D({[-2(n-1)]})
, the Hodge isometry can be chosen to act as
\pm 1
on the discriminant and hence by
[
34
, Corollary 9.9]
we have that
X
and
X^{\prime}
are birational. Now, by
[
35
, Theorem 1.2]
we know that they are also D-equivalent.

∎
