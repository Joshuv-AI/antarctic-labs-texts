Prophylactic bi-level non-invasive positive pressure ventilation during monitored anesthesia care for transfemoral TAVI in a morbidly obese patient with obstructive sleep apnea: a case report
Authors: Saad Ahmed Naved, Madiha Khan, Saad Rasool, Muhammad Zameer Rajput
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTranscatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) is increasingly performed under monitored anesthesia care (MAC); however, its use in patients with morbid obesity and obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) remains challenging because of the risk of hypoventilation and airway compromise. Introduction

Transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) is an established minimally invasive therapy for severe aortic stenosis, with the increasing adoption of a “minimalist” approach using monitored anesthesia care (MAC) [1] This strategy is associated with faster recovery and fewer pulmonary complications than general anesthesia (GA) [2]. However, its application in patients with morbid obesity and obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) remains challenging because of the risk of airway obstruction and hypoventilation under sedation [3, 4].

Noninvasive ventilation (NIV) is widely used in the perioperative setting to improve oxygenation and reduce respiratory complications, particularly in high-risk patients [5]. The use of non-invasive ventilation during awake transcatheter aortic valve procedures has been previously described, including by Guarracino et al., [6] who demonstrated its feasibility in high-risk respiratory patients. Despite this, its intraoperative use during MAC, particularly as a planned prophylactic strategy, remains less well characterized in TAVI, with limited guidance on its role in supporting spontaneous ventilation.

We report the successful anesthetic management of transfemoral TAVI performed under MAC using a structured approach of planned bilevel NIPPV, dexmedetomidine-based sedation, and regional analgesia in a patient with a triple anesthetic challenge comprising severe OSA, anticipated difficult airway, and severe aortic stenosis.

Case presentation

A 67-year-old woman (weight 112.5 kg, height 157 cm, BMI 45.6 kg/m²) presented with progressively worsening dyspnea and orthopnea. Her medical history included poorly controlled diabetes mellitus (HbA1c 12.7%), hypertension, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, morbid obesity, and a prior left mastectomy for breast carcinoma. She had a markedly reduced functional capacity. Airway assessment revealed a short neck, large facial structure, and mildly restricted neck extension, indicating an anticipated difficult airway. Echocardiography revealed severe calcific aortic stenosis (aortic valve area ~ 1 cm²), concentric left ventricular hypertrophy, mild pulmonary hypertension, and preserved left ventricular ejection fraction (~ 60%). Coronary angiography revealed mild coronary artery disease. Preoperative sleep study demonstrated severe obstructive sleep apnea with an apnea–hypopnea index (AHI) of 56.3 events/hour. The study recorded multiple obstructive, central, and mixed apneic episodes, with a nadir oxygen saturation of 76% and an average nocturnal saturation of 91%. During the titration phase, the application of noninvasive ventilation resulted in a marked reduction in respiratory events. Given the severe symptomatic aortic stenosis and high surgical risk, transfemoral TAVI was selected over surgical valve replacement. In the cardiac catheterization laboratory, standard ASA monitoring was performed along with invasive arterial (left radial) and central venous access (right internal jugular vein). A difficult airway setup was prepared. A clear plan for immediate conversion to general anesthesia, including an airway control strategy, was established prior to the procedure. Bi-level non-invasive positive pressure ventilation (NIPPV) was initiated prior to the procedure and continued intraoperatively to optimize oxygenation and maintain airway patency. A well fitted oronasal mask was used to deliver bilevel NIPPV (iVolve F1A, BMC Medical Co., China). The mask was secured with adjustable headgear and connected to a standard single-limb NIV circuit with an exhalation port. Bilevel ventilation was applied with IPAP of 10–12 cm H₂O and EPAP of 4–5 cm H₂O, titrated to maintain adequate ventilation and oxygenation. The fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO₂) was initially set at 100% and titrated to maintain SpO₂ >94%. Careful positioning and draping allowed maintenance of mask seal without interfering with procedural access or monitoring. Owing to the configuration of the bilevel NIPPV interface, integration of side stream or inline end-tidal CO₂ monitoring was not possible. Ventilation was therefore closely monitored clinically through continuous assessment of respiratory rate, chest excursion, patient responsiveness, and oxygen saturation trends. Transesophageal echocardiography was not utilized in this case, allowing uninterrupted application of the bilevel NIPPV interface. Sedation was maintained using a dexmedetomidine infusion (0.4 µg/kg/h) without a loading dose and supplemented with titrated fentanyl boluses (20 µg) targeting Ramsay Sedation Score of 2–3 while preserving spontaneous ventilation and airway patency. The patient was positioned in a semi-ramped posture to optimize respiratory mechanics and airway patency. Bilateral fascia iliaca blocks were performed under ultrasound guidance using 20 ml of 0.25% bupivacaine on each side to facilitate femoral access and reduce the opioid requirements. Anticoagulation was achieved using intravenous heparin (10,000 IU). A temporary pacing wire was inserted into the right internal jugular vein. Vascular access was obtained via a percutaneous transfemoral approach via right common femoral artery using a vascular closure device. A 26-mm self-expanding transcatheter aortic valve (Evolut PRO+) was successfully deployed under fluoroscopic and transthoracic echocardiographic guidance. Final angiography confirmed appropriate valve positioning and hemostasis. The procedure was completed without airway intervention or conversion to general anesthesia. The hemodynamic parameters remained stable throughout the procedure, without significant vasopressor requirements. Postoperatively, the patient was transferred to the surgical intensive care unit on bi-level NIPPV support and was successfully weaned off within a few hours. Her recovery was uneventful, with early mobilization and good tolerance of oral intake. She was discharged home in stable condition after a four-day hospital stay with planned cardiology and endocrinology follow-ups. The patient reported satisfaction with her perioperative care, noting improved breathing and minimal discomfort postoperatively.

Discussion and conclusions

The present case highlights an important contemporary challenge in transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI), that is, achieving a balance between the benefits of MAC and the perceived safety of general anesthesia in high-risk patients. While MAC is increasingly favored as part of a minimalist approach due to reduced pulmonary complications and shorter recovery, its application in patients with morbid obesity and OSA remains limited because of concerns regarding airway compromise and hypoventilation. This case is noteworthy in demonstrating that a structured perioperative respiratory strategy can allow the safe implementation of MAC, even in this high-risk subgroup. Current literature supports the use of noninvasive ventilation (NIV) in the perioperative setting, particularly in high-risk patients, where it improves oxygenation and reduces reintubation, pulmonary complications, and hospital stay [7]. However, this evidence is strongest for postoperative or therapeutic use. The intraoperative use of non-invasive ventilation during sedation has been previously described, including in transcatheter aortic valve procedures by Guarracino et al. [6] Nonetheless, data specifically addressing structured prophylactic planned intraoperative application during MAC remain limited. The physiological benefits of bi-level noninvasive positive pressure ventilation (NIPPV), including improved oxygenation, reduced work of breathing, and potential reduction in left ventricular afterload, make it an attractive adjunct in patients with combined cardiac and respiratory comorbidities. However, the hemodynamic effects of noninvasive ventilation (NIV) in severe aortic stenosis are nuanced and require careful consideration. While positive-pressure ventilation may reduce venous return and ventricular preload—potentially precipitating hypotension in this preload-dependent population—it can simultaneously decrease left ventricular afterload and improve cardiac performance [8]. The net hemodynamic effect is therefore highly patient- and setting-dependent, influenced by intravascular volume status, ventricular function, and airway pressure levels. In the present case, NIPPV was applied judiciously using low-pressure settings with vigilant hemodynamic monitoring, allowing respiratory support without compromising cardiovascular stability.

Bilevel NIPPV was preferred over high-flow nasal oxygen, as it provides both inspiratory support and expiratory positive airway pressure, thereby improving ventilation and maintaining airway patency, particularly in patients with coexisting COPD and severe OSA [9, 10]. These considerations highlight that the choice of modality should be individualized based on patient physiology and procedural context. The primary challenge in such patients is maintaining adequate ventilation while avoiding airway instrumentation. Sedation further compounds this risk by promoting upper airway collapse and hypoventilation. Dexmedetomidine-based sedation, as used in this case, is advantageous because of its minimal respiratory depression and preservation of spontaneous ventilation [11]. In addition, the use of regional analgesia reduces the opioid requirements and enhances procedural tolerance, further supporting respiratory stability. Despite these advantages, the use of NIPPV under sedation is not without limitations, including the risks of upper airway obstruction, mask leak, patient–ventilator asynchrony, and delayed recognition of respiratory failure, necessitating vigilant monitoring and readiness for conversion to GA [12]. This case demonstrates the feasibility of integrating bi-level non-invasive positive pressure ventilation (NIPPV) into monitored anesthesia care for TAVI in high-risk patients. This study contributes to the existing literature by demonstrating its use during MAC rather than its conventional role as a rescue or postoperative intervention. It supports a physiology-driven, individualized approach in which respiratory support, sedation strategy, and regional analgesia are integrated to minimize perioperative risk.

In keeping with the evolving minimalist TAVI paradigm, this case demonstrates a structured approach for extending monitored anesthesia care (MAC) to carefully selected high-risk patients. Appropriate patient selection is fundamental and may favor cooperative patients with preserved hemodynamic stability, significant respiratory comorbidity, anticipated difficult airway, and prior tolerance to noninvasive ventilation. Conversely, patients with severe hemodynamic instability, intolerance to NIV, or anticipated inability to maintain airway patency may be better managed under general anesthesia. This case highlights the potential role of planned bilevel NIPPV in safely facilitating MAC in selected high-risk TAVI patients.

Although the individual components of this strategy are well established, this case demonstrates their effective integration in a uniquely high-risk clinical setting characterized by the triple anesthetic challenge of severe obstructive sleep apnea, morbid obesity, and critical aortic stenosis. The combined use of planned bilevel noninvasive ventilation, dexmedetomidine-based sedation, and regional analgesia enabled successful transfemoral TAVI under monitored anesthesia care while maintaining spontaneous ventilation and avoiding invasive airway manipulation. Further studies are required to define its role in perioperative practices.
