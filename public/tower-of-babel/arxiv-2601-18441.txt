Abstract
We study the document exchange problem under multiple substring edits. A substring edit in a string 𝐱 {\mathbf{x}} occurs when a substring 𝐮 {\mathbf{u}} of 𝐱 {\mathbf{x}} is replaced by an arbitrary string 𝐯 {\mathbf{v}} . The lengths of 𝐮 {\mathbf{u}} and 𝐯 {\mathbf{v}} are bounded from above by a fixed constant. Let 𝐱 \mathbf{x} and 𝐲 \mathbf{y} be two binary strings that differ by multiple substring edits. The aim of document exchange schemes is to construct an encoding of 𝐱 \mathbf{x} with small length such that 𝐱 \mathbf{x} can be recovered using 𝐲 \mathbf{y} and the encoding. We construct a low-complexity document exchange scheme with encoding length of 4 ​ t ​ log ⁡ n + o ⁡ ( log ⁡ n ) 4t\log n+o(\log n) bits, where n n is the length of the string 𝐱 \mathbf{x} . The best known scheme achieves an encoding length of 4 ​ t ​ log ⁡ n + O ⁡ ( log ⁡ log ⁡ n ) 4t\log n+O(\log\log n) bits, but at a much higher computational complexity. Then, we investigate the average length of valid encodings for document exchange schemes with uniform strings 𝐱 \mathbf{x} and develop a scheme with an expected encoding length of ( 4 ​ t − 1 ) ​ log ⁡ n + o ⁡ ( log ⁡ n ) (4t-1)\log n+o(\log n) bits. In this setting, prior works have only constructed schemes for a single substring edit.

Introduction

The document exchange problem, also known as data synchronization, involves two nodes
A
and
B
storing strings
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}
, respectively, where the string
\mathbf{y}

differs slightly from
\mathbf{x}
. The goal is for node

to recover
\mathbf{x}
by receiving a small amount of information from
A
and leveraging the knowledge of
\mathbf{y}
. We consider the scenario in which
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}
are binary strings and node

computes an encoding of the string
\mathbf{x}
, denoted by

, and communicates it to

. The encoding is designed in such a way that node

is able to recover
\mathbf{x}
from
\mathbf{y}
and
\mathsf{Enc}(\mathbf{x})
. The length of
\mathsf{Enc}({\mathbf{x}})
is termed

.
Since the goal is for node

to obtain
\mathbf{x}
with minimum communication between the nodes, the redundancy should be made as small as possible. See Fig.

and
\mathbf{y}
differ by a number of insertions, deletions, or substitutions. Such operations are called IDS edits. The minimum number of IDS edits needed to change
\mathbf{x}
into

.

Early works on document exchange under this model can be found in

For constant

[
17

A systematic binary code capable of correcting

This construction utilizes a technique called syndrome compression introduced in
[

]
. Codes correcting combinations of insertions, deletions and substitutions are studied extensively in the literature (see, for instance,

However, many of the known code constructions are non-systematic and cannot be directly used to obtain document exchange schemes.

The authors of
[
20
]
introduced the substring edit model defined as follows. For a given constant

, a

substring edit applied to a string

results in a substring

of

being replaced by another string

such that the lengths of
\mathbf{u}
and

are bounded from above by

In this work, we focus on constructing schemes for the document exchange problem with
t
many

-substring edits, where

[

many

-substring edits,

. Those codes are constructed using a technique called distributed graph coloring and have redundancy

and
O(n^{10t})
, respectively. Our goal is to construct schemes with similar redundancy and lower complexities.

In addition to the worst-case document exchange explained above, we consider the average-case document exchange. In the average case, multiple encoding functions with different redundancy can be used to encode
\mathbf{x}
. We consider the case where
{\mathbf{x}}
is drawn uniformly at random from the set of all binary strings of length
n
. The goal is to reduce the average redundancy of the used encodings. Under a
single

k
-substring edit, the authors of
[
13

construct a scheme with average redundancy

-substring edit error with the smallest known redundancy of
\log n+O(\log\log n)

[
13
]
. However, document exchange schemes for multiple

-substring edits in the average case remain unexplored.

Our contributions can be summarized as follows:

(I)

Using the syndrome compression technique, we construct a worst-case document exchange scheme with redundancy
4t\log n+o(\log n)
bits with encoding and decoding complexities of
O(n^{2t+1})
and
O(n^{t+1})
, respectively.

(II)

For the average-case document exchange problem, we construct a scheme with average redundancy of
(4t-1)\log n+o(\log n)
bits. The main idea is to divide the space of input strings into two parts, which allows for different redundancies. We subsequently demonstrate that the majority of the strings come from the part that requires a small redundancy.

II-A

Edit Models

We now introduce the various edit models required to describe our problem setting and results.

For any string
\mathbf{x}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
, a valid arbitrary location
i\in[1,n+1]
and an arbitrary symbol
\omega\in\{0,1\}
, a single
insertion-deletion-substitution (IDS) edit
is the operation where either
w
is inserted in the
i
-th location,
x_{i}
is substituted with
\omega
, or
x_{i}
is deleted from the string. Such an operation would produce as output either
(x_{1},\cdots,x_{i-1},\omega,x_{i},\cdots,x_{n})
,
(x_{1},\cdots,x_{i-1},\omega,x_{i},\cdots,x_{n})
, or
(x_{1},\cdots,x_{i-1},y,x_{i},\cdots,x_{n})
, respectively. Multiple IDS edits imply that
\mathbf{x}
undergoes several subsequent single IDS edits.

A special case of multiple IDS edits is substring edits. For any
\mathbf{x}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
, let
{\mathbf{u}}
be a substring of
{\mathbf{x}}
with location
i\in[1,n-{\ell}({\mathbf{u}})+1]
. A
k
-substring edit
is the operation where
\mathbf{u}
is deleted and a string
\mathbf{v}
is inserted in the same location
i
, and
{\ell}(\mathbf{u}),{\ell}(\mathbf{v})\leq k
. Hence, the resulting string would be
((x_{1},\cdots,x_{i-1}),{\mathbf{v}},(x_{i+{\ell}({\mathbf{u}})},\cdots,x_{n}))
.

Example 1
.

Consider
\mathbf{x}=(1110{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}0010}100110)
and
\mathbf{y}=(1110{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}10011}100110)
. From
\mathbf{x}
one can get
\mathbf{y}
through a
5
-substring edit with
\mathbf{u}=({\color[rgb]{1,0,0}0010})
and
\mathbf{v}=({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}10011})
.

If a
k
-substring edit operation has been applied on
\mathbf{x}
subsequently over
t
rounds, the string
\mathbf{x}
is said to have undergone
t

k
-substring edits. We will primarily focus on the document exchange under this edit model, which is also considered in
[
12
]
. Note that
(t-1)

k
-substring edit is a special case of
t

k
-substring edit, where the deleted and inserted strings are

We will now introduce some notions connected to application of the edit models on input strings. For any string
\mathbf{x}
, the
edit ball
of
\mathbf{x}
under
t

k
-substring edits
\mathcal{B}_{t}^{\mathrm{str}(k)}({\mathbf{x}})
is the set of strings that can be produced from

by applying at most
t

k
-substring edits. For two equal length strings
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}
, we say they are
confusable under
t

k
-substring edits
if both strings can produce the same output string under

k
-substring edits, i.e.,

t

k
-substring edits
of
\mathbf{x}\in\{0,1\}^{n}

under
t

-substring edits, namely
\mathcal{C}_{t}^{\mathrm{str}(k)}(\mathbf{x})=\{{\mathbf{y}}\in\{0,1\}^{n}:\mathcal{B}_{t}^{\mathrm{str}(k)}({\mathbf{x}})\cap\mathcal{B}_{t}^{\mathrm{str}(k)}({\mathbf{y}})\neq\emptyset\}

Further, we define the

by applying

For a general statement on the confusion ball which holds true for any choice of

.

II-B

Problem Statement

In this paper, for our document exchange schemes, we focus on the multiple-substring edit model, in which strings
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}
differ by
t

k
-substring edits. Such a scenario is formally described below.

Definition 1

(Document Exchange)
.

Consider a string
\mathbf{x}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
and a string
\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{B}_{t}^{\mathrm{str}(k)}(\mathbf{x})
. An
encoding function
with redundancy
s
,
\mathsf{Enc}:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}^{s}
, produces a valid
document exchange scheme
if
{\mathbf{x}}
can be deterministically determined by solely observing
\mathbf{y}
and
\mathsf{Enc}(\mathbf{x})
.

The complexities of computing the encoding function and determining
\mathbf{x}
from
\mathbf{y}
and
\mathsf{Enc}(\mathbf{x})
are referred to as the
encoding
and
decoding
complexities, respectively.

Definition 2

(Average-case Document Exchange)
.

Let the sets
\mathcal{P}_{1},\cdots,\mathcal{P}_{r}
form a partition of
\{0,1\}^{n}
and let
\mathsf{Enc}_{j}:\mathcal{P}_{j}\to\{0,1\}^{s_{j}}
,
j\in[1,r]
be
r
different encoding functions each with redundancy
s_{j}
. The
r
encoding functions produce a valid
average-case document exchange scheme
if any
{\mathbf{x}}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
can be deterministically determined by solely observing a string
{\mathbf{y}}\in\mathcal{B}_{t}^{\mathrm{str}(k)}(\mathbf{x})
,
r
and
\mathsf{Enc}_{r}({\mathbf{x}})
. Assuming all strings
{\mathbf{x}}
have the same probability of being chosen, the average redundancy of the scheme is defined as
\bar{s}=\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{r}|\mathcal{P}_{j}|s_{j}}{n}.

By using different encoding functions for different input strings, we may be able to reduce the expected redundancy overall all strings. The paper
[
13
]
considers document exchange under one substring edit for cases when
p_{0}\neq p_{1}
, where
p_{0}
and
p_{1}
are the probabilities corresponding to each bit of
{\mathbf{x}}
being
0
or
1
. However, we do not consider such scenarios.

III
Document exchange under multiple substring edits

In this section, we will present the document exchange scheme under
t

k
-substring edits. Using the syndrome compression technique
[
17
]
, we construct an encoding function for a document exchange scheme with redundancy
4t\log n+o(\log n)

Syndrome compression is a general technique for constructing low-redundancy error correcting codes as described formally below.

Lemma 1

(
[
18
, Section 2]
)
.

Consider an arbitrary error model and for
{\mathbf{c}}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
, let
\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{c})
represent the confusion ball of
\mathbf{c}
under the edit model in consideration.

Define a function
f:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}^{R}
satisfying

1.

f(\mathbf{c})\neq f(\mathbf{c}^{\prime})
, for any
\mathbf{c},\mathbf{c}^{\prime}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
and
\mathbf{c}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{c})
, and,

2.

R\leq O((\log\log n)\log n)
.

We refer to such a function
f
as a
labeling function
.
Then, for any fixed
\mathbf{x}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
, there exists an integer

a_{\mathbf{x}}\leq 2^{\log{|\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{x})|}+o(\log n)}

such that

f(\mathbf{x})\not\equiv f(\mathbf{y})\pmod{a_{\mathbf{x}}},\quad\forall\ \mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{x}).

The time complexity for finding
a_{\mathbf{x}}
corresponding to a given
\mathbf{x}
is at most
O(|\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{x})|^{2})
.

The syndrome compression technique requires that the labeling function maps to different outputs for all mutually confusable strings. Our edit model here is
t

k
-substring edits, which are a special case of multiple IDS edits. Hence, we choose the labeling function from
[
17
]
, capable of correcting multiple IDS edits.

Lemma 2

(
[
17

)
.

For any
\tau
, constant w.r.t.
n
, there exists a labeling function
f:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}^{R}
, where

R~\leq[(\tau^{2}+1)(2\tau^{2}+1)+2\tau^{2}(\tau-1)]\log n+o(\log n),

such that for any
\mathbf{c},\mathbf{c}^{\prime}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
and
\mathbf{c}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}_{\tau}^{\mathrm{IDS}}(\mathbf{c})
, it holds that
f(\mathbf{c})\neq f(\mathbf{c}^{\prime})
.

Further, the complexity of computing the function

is at most
O(n)

[
17
]
.
As
t

k
-substring edits are equivalent to at most
2tk

in

-substring model.

For any
{\mathbf{x}}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
and a labeling function

\mathsf{Enc}({\mathbf{x}})=(f({\mathbf{x}})\mod{a_{\mathbf{x}}},~a_{\mathbf{x}})

a_{\mathbf{x}}=O(|\mathcal{C}({\mathbf{x}})|)

\max_{{\mathbf{x}}\in\{0,1\}^{n}}|\mathcal{C}({\mathbf{x}})|

Lemma 3
.

For any string
\mathbf{x}\in\{0,1\}^{n}

t,k

\displaystyle|\mathcal{C}_{t}^{\mathrm{str}(k)}(\mathbf{x})|=O(n^{2t}).

By definition, for any

\mathcal{B}_{t}^{\mathrm{str}(k)}(\mathbf{y})\cap\mathcal{B}_{t}^{\mathrm{str}(k)}(\mathbf{x})\neq\emptyset.

Let

(1)

Using the above results, we now present our document exchange scheme under

Theorem 1

(Document Exchange Scheme)
.

Consider
\mathbf{x}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
and let
t,k
be fixed constants and
f
be the labeling function in Lemma
2
. Then, for
a_{\mathbf{x}}
as in Lemma
1
and any
{\mathbf{y}}\in\mathcal{B}_{t}^{\mathrm{str}(k)}(\mathbf{x})
, the following encoding function

\displaystyle\mathsf{Enc}(\mathbf{x})=\bar{f}(\mathbf{x})\triangleq(f(\mathbf{x})\mod{a_{\mathbf{x}}},~a_{\mathbf{x}})

produces a valid document exchange scheme with redundancy
4t\log n+o(\log n)
bits and complexity of encoding and decoding as
O(n^{2t+1})
and
O(n^{t+1})
, respectively.

Proof.

Determining
{\mathbf{x}}
correctly:
By definition, the set of strings of length
n
that are
t

k
-substring edits of
\mathbf{y}
is a subset of
\mathcal{C}_{t}^{\mathrm{str}(k)}(\mathbf{x})
. From Lemma
1
, for any string
\mathbf{z}\in\mathcal{C}_{t}^{\mathrm{str}(k)}(\mathbf{x})
, it holds that
f(\mathbf{x})\not\equiv f(\mathbf{z})\pmod{a_{\mathbf{x}}}
. Thus, given
\mathsf{Enc}(\mathbf{x})=(f(\mathbf{x})\mod{a_{\mathbf{x}}},~a_{\mathbf{x}})
, we can uniquely identify
\mathbf{x}
by iteratively computing
f(\hat{\mathbf{x}})\mod{a_{\mathbf{x}}}
for all
\hat{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathcal{B}_{t}^{\mathrm{str}(k)}({\mathbf{y}})
.

Decoding complexity:
Given
{\mathbf{y}}
, using an analogous bound to the one in
Eq.

2
(replacing
2t
in
Eq.

2
by
t
), it can be seen that computing the set
\mathcal{B}_{t}^{\mathrm{str}(k)}({\mathbf{y}})
requires
O(n^{t})
operations, because there are at most
O(n^{t})
many strings in this set. Further, the complexity of calculating
f(\hat{\mathbf{x}})
is
O(n)

[
17
,
19
]
. Thus, the overall decoding complexity is at most
O(n^{t+1})
.

Encoding complexity:
To compute
\mathsf{Enc}({\mathbf{x}})
, from Lemma
1
, we know that we need to find an appropriate

, such that
f(\mathbf{x})\not\equiv f(\mathbf{y})\pmod{a_{\mathbf{x}}},\quad\forall\ \mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{C}_{t}^{\mathrm{str}(k)}(\mathbf{x})
. Therefore, we need to compute the set
\mathcal{C}_{t}^{\mathrm{str}(k)}(\mathbf{x})
, which takes
O(n^{2t})
operations. For each
{\mathbf{y}}\in\mathcal{C}_{t}^{\mathrm{str}(k)}(\mathbf{x})
, we need to calculate
f({\mathbf{y}})
, which takes
O(n)
operations. The value of

needed for the property
f({\mathbf{x}})\not\equiv f({\mathbf{y}})\mkern 4.0mu({\operator@font mod}\mkern 6.0mua_{\mathbf{x}})
to hold can be computed in parallel to computing

at no extra cost, see
[
18
]
. The reason is that one can start with

and increase it whenever we find a

with
f({\mathbf{y}})\equiv f({\mathbf{x}})\mkern 4.0mu({\operator@font mod}\mkern 6.0mua_{\mathbf{x}})
. Hence, the overall complexity of calculating
\mathsf{Enc}(\mathbf{x})
is
O(n^{2t+1})
.

Redundancy:
From
Lemmas

1
and
3
, one needs at most
2t\log n+o(\log n)
bits to store
a_{\mathbf{x}}
and
f({\mathbf{x}})\mod a_{\mathbf{x}}
. Thus, the total length of
\mathsf{Enc}(\mathbf{x})
is
4t\log n+o(\log n)
bits.

k
scales with
n
. However, the complexities of encoding and decoding are
O(n^{8t})
and
O(n^{10t})
, respectively, which are much higher than those demonstrated by our scheme. Furthermore, both schemes have the same dominant term in the redundancy.

IV
Average-case document exchange under multiple substring edits

We present our average-case document exchange scheme with an average redundancy of
(4t-1)\log n+o(\log n)
bits.

The main idea behind average-case document exchange is that the redundancy of the
\mathsf{Enc}
function of a worst-case document exchange scheme is determined by the largest confusion ball. However, only a few strings have a large cardinality of the confusion ball. The remaining majority have a small cardinality of the confusion ball and will require encodings with smaller redundancy.

We consider strings
{\mathbf{x}}
that are
pattern-dense
, i.e., each substring of
{\mathbf{x}}
of length
\delta
must contain a fixed string
{\mathbf{p}}
, which we refer to as
pattern
.

Definition 3

(Pattern-dense strings)
.

For some fixed pattern
\mathbf{p}\in\{0,1\}^{\leq\delta}
, any string
\mathbf{x}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
is
(\mathbf{p},\delta)
-dense
if every substring of
\mathbf{x}
of length
\delta
contains the pattern
{\mathbf{p}}
as a substring.

The goal is to demonstrate that most of the strings in
\{0,1\}^{n}
are pattern-dense. We then show that such strings require an encoding with a small redundancy.

We now demonstrate that for any fixed pattern
{\mathbf{p}}
and large enough
\delta
, the number of strings in
\{0,1\}^{n}
which are not
(\mathbf{p},\delta)
-dense is vanishingly small, as
n\to\infty
.

Lemma 4
.

Let
{\mathbf{x}}
be sampled uniformly at random from
\{0,1\}^{n}
and let
\delta=\alpha\log n
for some fixed constant
\alpha
. Then, for any pattern
{\mathbf{p}}
with length constant with respect to
n
,

\displaystyle\Pr(\mathbf{x}\text{ is not }(\mathbf{p},\delta)\text{-dense})=O\left(\frac{1}{n^{\frac{\lfloor\alpha/{\ell}(\mathbf{p})\rfloor}{2^{{\ell}(\mathbf{p})}}-1}}\right).

Proof.

If
\mathbf{x}
is not
(\mathbf{p},\delta)
-dense, it means that there exists at least one index
i
, such that
p\neq(x_{j},x_{j+1},\cdots,x_{j+{\ell}(\mathbf{p})-1}),\forall j\in[i,i+\delta-1]
. Thus,

\displaystyle\Pr(\mathbf{x}\text{ is not }(\mathbf{p},\delta)\text{-dense})

\displaystyle=\Pr\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{n-\delta+1}\{{\mathbf{p}}\neq(x_{j},x_{j+1},\cdots,x_{j+{\ell}(\mathbf{p})-1}),\forall j\in[i,i+\delta-1]\}\right)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\leq}}\sum_{i=1}^{n-\delta+1}\Pr\left(\mathbf{p}\neq(x_{j},x_{j+1},\cdots,x_{j+{\ell}(\mathbf{p})-1}),\forall j\in[i,i+\delta-1]\right)

\displaystyle=(n-\delta+1)\cdot\Pr\left(\mathbf{p}\neq(x_{j},x_{j+1},\cdots,x_{j+{\ell}(\mathbf{p})-1}),\forall j\in[1,\delta]\right)

\displaystyle=(n-\delta+1)\cdot\bigcap_{j=1}^{\delta}\Pr(\mathbf{p}\neq(x_{j},x_{j+1},\cdots,x_{j+{\ell}(\mathbf{p})-1}))

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{\leq}}(n-\delta+1)\cdot\prod_{j=1}^{\lfloor\delta/{\ell}(\mathbf{p})\rfloor}\Pr(p\neq(x_{(j-1)\cdot{\ell}(\mathbf{p})+1},\cdots,x_{j\cdot{\ell}(\mathbf{p})}))

\displaystyle=(n-\delta+1)\cdot\prod_{j=1}^{\lfloor\delta/{\ell}(\mathbf{p})\rfloor}(1-\Pr(p\neq(x_{(j-1)\cdot{\ell}(\mathbf{p})+1},\cdots,x_{j\cdot{\ell}(\mathbf{p})})))

\displaystyle=(n-\delta+1)\cdot\left(1-\frac{1}{2^{{\ell}(\mathbf{p})}}\right)^{\lfloor\delta/{\ell}(\mathbf{p})\rfloor}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(c)}}{{\leq}}(n-\delta+1)\cdot\exp\left(\frac{-\lfloor\delta/{\ell}(\mathbf{p})\rfloor}{2^{{\ell}(\mathbf{p})}}\right)

\displaystyle=O\left(\frac{1}{n^{\frac{\lfloor\alpha/{\ell}(\mathbf{p})\rfloor}{2^{{\ell}(\mathbf{p})}}-1}}\right).

Here,
(a)
follows from the union bound;
(b)
holds because the probability of the intersection of all events in a set is upper bounded by the probability of the intersection of any subset of events, which for a subset containing mutually independent events equals the product of their probabilities. Finally,
(c)
holds as
\forall x\in\mathbb{N},(1-x)\leq e^{-x}
.
∎

In
[
13
]
, for some choice of
{\mathbf{p}}
and
\delta
, Li
et al.
demonstrate a scheme capable of correcting one
k
-substring edit for any
(\mathbf{p},\delta)
-dense string. A description is provided below.

Lemma 5

(
[
13
, Theorem 19]
)
.

Consider any constant
k
,
\delta=k2^{2k+3}\log n
and
{\mathbf{p}}=0^{k}1^{k}
. Then, there exist functions
h
and
\psi
, such that any
(\mathbf{p},\delta)
-dense string
\mathbf{x}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
can be uniquely decoded from

({\mathbf{y}},\mathbf{h(\mathbf{x})},\mathbf{\psi(\mathbf{x})}),

for any
\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{B}_{1}^{\mathrm{str}(k)}(\mathbf{x})
and where
{\ell}(\mathbf{h(\mathbf{x})})\leq\log n
and
{\ell}(\psi(\mathbf{x}))=o(\log n)
. The overall complexity of computing
\mathbf{h(\mathbf{x})}
,
\mathbf{\psi(\mathbf{x})}
and the decoding process is at most
O(n\ \mathrm{poly}\log n)
.

For document exchange schemes using syndrome compression or distributed graph coloring, the redundancy depends on the size of the confusion ball. In our scheme, we partition
\{0,1\}^{n}
into two parts
\mathcal{P}_{1}
and
\mathcal{P}_{2}
such that all
{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathcal{P}_{1}
are
({\mathbf{p}},\delta)
-dense strings and all
{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}
are not. This partitioning allows us to encode with small average redundancy, provided we also convey the partition index of
{\mathbf{x}}
with
\mathsf{Enc}({\mathbf{x}})
. As such, we can define a
restricted confusion ball
as follows.

Definition 4
.

Consider a fixed pattern
\mathbf{p}
and a parameter
\delta
. Then, for every
(\mathbf{p},\delta)
-dense string
\mathbf{x}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
, the
restricted confusion ball
is defined as,

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{C}}_{t}^{\mathrm{str}(k)}({\mathbf{x}})\triangleq\{{\mathbf{y}}\in\mathcal{C}_{t}^{\mathrm{str}(k)}({\mathbf{x}}):{\mathbf{y}}\text{ is }(\mathbf{p},\delta)\text{-dense}\}.

The idea of partitioning the confusion ball based on prior information about the string
{\mathbf{x}}
is connected to the so-called
pre-coding technique
described in
[
19
]
. There, Song
et al.

[
19
]
allow the encoding of strings that are codewords of some selected code. This prior information is used to restrict the confusion ball and obtain a smaller redundancy. However, a coding scheme based on pre-coding does not directly translate to an average-case document exchange scheme, because codewords are sparsely distributed in the ambient space.

We take inspiration from this technique and consider a family of codes to encode the strings in
\mathcal{P}_{1}
. In particular, we consider the family of one
k
-substring edit correcting codes proposed in
[
13
]
and using
{\mathbf{p}}=0^{k}1^{k}
, we show that all strings in
\mathcal{P}_{1}
have a reduced size of the restricted confusion ball.

Lemma 6
.

For any constant
k
,
\delta=k2^{2k+3}\log n
and
{\mathbf{p}}=0^{k}1^{k}
, the size of the restricted confusion ball of any
(\mathbf{p},\delta)
-dense string
\mathbf{x}\in\{1,0\}^{n}
can be reduced to

\displaystyle|\tilde{\mathcal{C}}_{t}^{\mathrm{str}(k)}(\mathbf{x})|=O(n^{2t-1}).

by leveraging the fact that
{\mathbf{x}}
is
({\mathbf{p}},\delta)
-dense and using
\mathbf{h(\mathbf{x})}
and
\psi(x)
as described in Lemma
5
.

Proof.

The proof is similar to that of Lemma
3
. Since
\mathbf{x}
is known to be
(\mathbf{p},\delta)
-dense, in such a case, we need to only consider the restricted confusion ball
\tilde{\mathcal{C}}_{t}^{\mathrm{str}(k)}(\mathbf{x})
. Since
\tilde{\mathcal{C}}_{t}^{\mathrm{str}(k)}(\mathbf{x})\subset\mathcal{C}_{t}^{\mathrm{str}(k)}(\mathbf{x})
, and, from Lemma
3
,
\mathcal{C}_{t}^{\mathrm{str}(k)}(\mathbf{x})\subseteq\mathcal{B}_{2t}^{str}(\mathbf{x})
, it holds that,

\tilde{\mathcal{C}}_{t}^{\mathrm{str}(k)}(\mathbf{x})\subset\mathcal{B}_{2t}^{\mathrm{str}(k)}(\mathbf{x})\cap\{\mathbf{y}\in\{0,1\}^{n}:\mathbf{y}\text{ is }(\mathbf{p},\delta)\text{-dense}\}.

Now, from Lemma
5
, given
\mathbf{h(\mathbf{x})}
and
\mathbf{\psi(\mathbf{x})}
, one can uniquely decode
\mathbf{x}
from any string that is one
k
-substring edit away from
\mathbf{x}
. Thus, the size of the restricted confusion ball of
\mathbf{x}
is bounded from above by the number of strings in
\mathcal{B}_{2t-1}^{\mathrm{str}(k)}(\mathbf{x})
that uniquely decode to an
n
-length
(\mathbf{p},\delta)
-dense string. Hence, using arguments similar to Lemma
3
, we have,

|\tilde{\mathcal{C}}_{t}^{\mathrm{str}(k)}(\mathbf{x})|\leq|\mathcal{B}_{2t-1}^{\mathrm{str}(k)}(\mathbf{x})|=O(n^{2t-1}).\qed

The above result on restricted confusion ball sizes allows us to reduce the redundancy for most strings. With this at hand, we present our average-case document exchange scheme.

Theorem 2

(Average-case Document Exchange)
.

Let any string
\mathbf{x}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
,
t,k
be fixed constants, and
f
be the labeling function in Lemma
2
. Then, for
a_{\mathbf{x}}
as in Lemma
2
and any
{\mathbf{y}}\in\mathcal{B}_{t}^{\mathrm{str}(k)}(\mathbf{x})
, the following encoding produces an average-case document exchange scheme

\displaystyle\mathsf{Enc}(\mathbf{x})=\begin{cases}(\mathbf{h(\mathbf{x})},\mathbf{\psi(\mathbf{x})},\bar{f}(\mathbf{x})),\text{ if }{\mathbf{x}}\text{ is }(\mathbf{p},\delta)\text{-dense}\\
\bar{f}(\mathbf{x}),\text{ otherwise,}\end{cases}

with expected redundancy
(4t-1)\log n+o(\log n)
bits, where
{\mathbf{p}}=0^{k}1^{k}
,
\delta=k2^{2k+3}\log n
, and
\mathbf{h(\mathbf{x})},\mathbf{\psi(\mathbf{x})}
are as described in Lemma
5
.

Proof.

Since the structure of
\mathsf{Enc}(\mathbf{x})
is different depending on whether
\mathbf{x}
is
(\mathbf{p},\delta)
-dense or not, we can clearly distinguish between the two cases.

If
\mathbf{x}
is
(\mathbf{p},\delta)
-dense, we only need to consider the restricted confusion ball
\tilde{\mathcal{C}}_{t}^{\mathrm{str}(k)}(\mathbf{x})
. Given
\mathbf{h(\mathbf{x})}
and
\mathbf{\psi(\mathbf{x})}
, from Lemma
6
, we have

|\tilde{\mathcal{C}}_{t}^{\mathrm{str}(k)}(\mathbf{x})|=O(n^{2t-1}).

We use steps similar to Theorem
1
, and note that since the size of
\mathsf{Enc}(\mathbf{x})
now depends on the size of the restricted confusion ball, the redundancy is at most
(4t-2)\log n+o(\log n)
bits. Since the length of
h({\mathbf{x}})
is
\log n
and the length of
\mathbf{\psi(\mathbf{x})}
is
o(\log n)
, and they both also need to be communicated, the total redundancy of the encoding becomes at most
(4t-1)\log n+o(\log n)
bits.

Alternatively, if
{\mathbf{x}}
is not
(\mathbf{p},\delta)
-dense, we employ the procedure described in Theorem
1
. Now, from Lemma
4
, substituting
\alpha=k2^{2k+3}
and
{\ell}(\mathbf{p})=2k
, we have,

\Pr(\mathbf{x}\text{ is not }(\mathbf{p},\delta)\text{-dense})=O\left(\frac{1}{n^{3}}\right)

. Thus, on average, the length of
\mathsf{Enc}(\mathbf{x})
will be at most
(4t-1)\log n+o(\log n)
bits.
∎

As the redundancy of both syndrome compression and distributed graph coloring methods depends solely on the sizes of the confusion balls, we could construct an average-case document exchange scheme with a similar expected redundancy of
(4t-1)\log n+O(\log\log n)
using the distributed graph coloring method instead of syndrome compression. However, the encoding and decoding complexities would be much higher for such a scheme.

V
Conclusion

In this work, we constructed a document exchange scheme for the
t

k
-substring edit model with redundancy
4t\log n+o(\log n)
and encoding and decoding complexities of
O(n^{2t+1})
and
O(n^{t+1})
, respectively. Further, we addressed the previously unexamined case of
t

k
-substring edits in the average-case document exchange and proposed a scheme with average redundancy
(4t-1)\log n+o(\log n)
bits.
