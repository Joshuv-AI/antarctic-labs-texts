Strontium-incorporated thermoresponsive hydrogel platform co-delivering graphene oxide-mannose nanocomplexes: An osteoimmunomodulatory strategy for periodontitis therapy
Authors: Linxin Chen, Jiayi Cai, Lin Liu, Geyuan Zheng, Sihang Chen, Maotuan Huang, Linyu Xu, Wenxiu Yuan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Periodontitis is an immune-mediated disease that destroys tooth-supporting tissues, yet existing treatments cannot synchronize inflammation resolution with bone regeneration. To address this unmet need, we report an injectable, strontium-incorporated an injectable thermoresponsive hydrogel (composed of chitosan, 0.6% gelatin, and β-glycerophosphate) platform engineered for the delivery of graphene oxide-mannose nanocomplexes (designated GM-Sr@Hydrogel). Upon injection, this platform undergoes rapid thermoresponsive stiffening, enabling the localized and controlled release of Sr2+ and GO-DM nanocomplexes within the periodontal microenvironment. The osteoimmunomodulatory capacity of this system was rigorously evaluated in a ligature-induced periodontitis model in vivo and further dissected using an in vitro inflammatory and osteogenic co-culture system. Our results demonstrate that the GM-Sr@Hydrogel functions as a potent osteoimmune regulator, evidenced by significant attenuation of periodontal inflammation and robust preservation of alveolar bone mass. Mechanistically, in vitro, this multifunctional platform concurrently promotes the osteogenic differentiation of periodontal ligament cells (PDLCs), restrains their pathological inflammatory activation, and impairs the maturation trajectory of dendritic cells (DCs) in a pro-inflammatory niche. Collectively, these findings delineate a novel osteoimmunomodulatory strategy for preserving periodontal homeostasis and establish the GM-Sr@Hydrogel as a promising translational platform for the management of periodontitis.

Introduction

Periodontitis ranks among the two most prevalent oral diseases worldwide and is characterized as a chronic inflammatory condition initiated by the invasion of plaque-derived microorganisms into the periodontal tissues [1]. This pathological process culminates in the progressive destruction of the tooth-supporting tissues, including the gingiva, periodontal ligament, alveolar bone, and cementum. Epidemiological investigations indicate that over 700 million individuals globally suffer from severe forms of periodontitis [2]. Beyond its substantial detriment to patients’ quality of life, the disease imposes a considerable socioeconomic burden [3]. Although the host immunity triggered by the periodontal microbiota can defend against bacterial invasion, its imbalance or excessive activation can instead exacerbate the destruction of periodontal tissues [4,5]. Arron et al. first introduced the critical concept of “osteoimmunology” in 2000, elucidating the bidirectional interaction between the skeletal and immune systems and highlighting how immune aberrations can precipitate disturbances in bone homeostasis [6]. This conceptual framework offers a novel paradigm for the management of bone regeneration in periodontitis [7,8]. A dedicated focus on the periodontal osteoimmune microenvironment—specifically the crosstalk and regulatory network between key immune constituents and skeletal lineage cells—promises to yield biologically grounded insights into the etiology and therapeutic targeting of periodontitis.

Despite this evolving conceptual framework, the translation of osteoimmunological principles into effective clinical therapies remains challenging. Current therapeutic strategies for periodontitis often fail to achieve continuous inflammation control and predictable alveolar bone regeneration [9,10]. Current clinical management of periodontitis follows a multi-modal sequence, beginning with non-surgical scaling and root planing (SRP). Although SRP is the gold standard for biofilm removal, it often fails to fully decontaminate deep defects and root bifurcations, frequently necessitating surgery [11]. Open flap debridement improves access but causes postoperative morbidity and gingival recession without inherently restoring lost tissues [12]. Regenerative procedures such as guided tissue regeneration and bone grafts, aim to reconstruct the periodontal attachment, yet their success is limited by strict case selection, unpredictable outcomes, and dependence on a compromised regenerative capacity in a persistently inflamed environment [13]. Host modulation therapies target the dysregulated immune response but are constrained by systemic side effects and unproven clinical efficacy [14]. Hence, a critical unmet need remains for a strategy that simultaneously resolves inflammation and actively promotes coordinated periodontal regeneration. A major limitation lies in the inability of locally administered agents to maintain a stable, therapeutic concentration within the dynamic periodontal microenvironment [15,16]. To address this, the development of a drug delivery platform that integrates sustained-release kinetics with intrinsic bioactivity is of paramount importance. Hydrogels have emerged as highly promising candidates for periodontal tissue reconstruction owing to their tunable degradation profiles, customizable mechanical attributes, and versatile loading capacities [17,18]. A dual-functional antibacterial hydrogel loaded with curcumin nanoparticles has been shown to augment the antioxidant and anti-inflammatory capabilities of macrophages via the glutathione metabolic pathway, thereby exerting potent immunomodulatory effects and ameliorating periodontitis progression [19]. Similarly, Shen et al. demonstrated that the incorporation of dental pulp stem cell-derived exosomes within a hydrogel matrix accelerated the healing of alveolar bone and periodontal epithelium in murine models of periodontitis [20]. These findings underscore the utility of hydrogel-based delivery systems in facilitating the transport of appropriate therapeutic agents while mitigating the risk of premature clearance, thus enabling the controlled release of therapeutic molecules.

To realize the therapeutic potential of osteoimmunomodulation, the selection of appropriate bioactive cargo for hydrogel-based delivery is critical. Among the various bioactive agents amenable to hydrogel-based delivery, strontium (Sr) represents a particularly compelling candidate for osteoimmunomodulation [21]. As a necessary trace element for bones and teeth, strontium plays a crucial role in promoting cell growth and differentiation during tissue regeneration [22]. Strontium ranelate, comprising two divalent strontium ions (Sr2+) as the active pharmaceutical ingredient, is clinically indicated for the management of postmenopausal osteoporosis [23,24]. Moreover, accumulating evidence supports the therapeutic potential of strontium in periodontitis [25]. The strategic incorporation of Sr2+ into a hydrogel matrix, designed to achieve its stable release within the periodontal niche, represents a promising direction for modulating the osteoimmune metabolic microenvironment characteristic of periodontitis. In addition to ionic modulation of bone metabolism, the incorporation of nanomaterials with intrinsic immunoregulatory properties offers a complementary strategy to enhance hydrogel functionality. Graphene oxide (GO), a key oxidized derivative of graphene, is distinguished by an abundance of oxygen-containing functional groups, which markedly enhance its hydrophilicity, biocompatibility, and specific surface area relative to pristine graphene [26,27]. Notably, GO exhibits inherent immunomodulatory activity [28]. Dendritic cells (DCs) have been shown to internalize GO, a process that leads to the upregulation of surface CD83 expression and concomitant inhibition of DC activation. Furthermore, GO interferes with the function of immunoproteasome subunits involved in antigen processing within DCs and promotes the generation of IL-2+ and MHC I+ regulatory T (Treg) cells [29]. Additional studies indicate that GO coatings can influence macrophage polarization and the secretion of inflammatory cytokines via Toll-like receptor engagement, attenuating inflammatory responses under pathological conditions while priming a pro-osteogenic environment under homeostatic conditions [30]. The facile functionalization of GO further enhances its utility, enabling it to serve as an effective platform for the sustained loading and release of bioactive molecules while itself constituting a promising biomedical material. While GO provides a versatile platform, its immunomodulatory effects can be further refined through surface functionalization. To potentiate the immunomodulatory capacity of the GO-based complex, we further incorporated D-mannose (DM), a monosaccharide abundant in various fruits and plants [31]. Recent investigations have revealed that mannose exerts potent anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory effects [32,33]. Zhang et al. first reported the capacity of mannose to induce Treg cells and suppress type I diabetes and airway inflammation in murine models [33]. The multifaceted biological activities of mannose encompass interference with pathogenic microbial colonization, inhibition of glucose metabolic pathways, and modulation of mannose receptor (MR)-mediated phagocytosis. Notably, mannose receptors are highly expressed on the surface of DCs and play a critical role in antigen recognition [34]. Accordingly, the functionalization of biomaterials with mannose provides a targeted means to engage DCs via mannose receptor interactions, thereby influencing DC-mediated osteoimmune responses.

Herein, we sought to engineer a thermosensitive hydrogel delivery system modified with strontium and co-loaded with a graphene oxide-mannose complex (GM-Sr@Hydrogel), capitalizing on the distinct yet synergistic properties of Sr2+, GO, and DM. This system undergoes thermoresponsive stiffening to form a solid, highly plastic gel, thereby enabling the efficient delivery and controlled release of both Sr2+ and the GO-DM complex for the treatment of periodontitis. Employing an in vivo periodontitis model, an in vitro inflammatory microenvironment, and an osteogenic induction system, we investigated the osteoimmunomodulatory effects of the GM-Sr@Hydrogel. The GM-Sr@Hydrogel system elicited multifaceted therapeutic effects in the context of periodontitis, as evidenced by enhanced osteogenic differentiation of periodontal ligament cells (PDLCs), concurrent suppression of their inflammatory phenotype, and inhibition of dendritic cells (DCs) maturation within an inflammatory microenvironment. Our findings provide critical insight into a novel osteoimmunomodulatory strategy for safeguarding periodontal homeostasis and identify the GM-Sr@Hydrogel as a promising therapeutic platform for periodontitis management.

Materials and methods

Synthesis of graphene oxide-D-mannose nanocomplexes (GM) and strontium-incorporated hydrogel carried by GM (GM-Sr@Hydrogel)

The graphene oxide (GO) nanosheet (XFNANO, China) was weighed, prepared with sterile water, stirred at 500 rpm for 60 min, and dispersed under ultrasonic conditions using a probe sonicator at 200 W for 30min (pulse: 2s on/3s off) under ice-bath cooling to form a nanoscale uniform GO solution with 0.1% (w/v, g/ml). The TNF-α expression test experiment in DCs to determine whether the obtained GO solution was contaminated by endotoxins. D-mannose (DM) (Sigma-Aldrich, M2069) was prepared into a solution with sterile deionized water, and in a sterile environment, continuously was stirred using a magnetic stirrer. The DM solution was dropped into and mixed on a spiral mixer for 48 h upside down to assemble a nano well-proportioned graphene oxide-D-mannose nanocomplexes (GM) solution.

For the fabrication of the thermosensitive hydrogel, 2.0% (w/v, g/ml) chitosan (Sigma-Aldrich, USA, 419419) with 0.6% (w/v, g/ml) gelatin (Sigma-Aldrich, USA, G2500) were dissolved in 0.1 M acetic acid under ice-bath cooling. Chitosan was selected as the polymer backbone for this thermosensitive system because its sol-gel transition at physiological temperature, excellent biocompatibility, and biodegradability. The 16% (w/v, g/ml) β glycerol 2-phosphate disodium salt hydrate (β-GP) (Sigma-Aldrich, USA, G9422) solution was sterilized by filtration through 0.22 μm (Millipore, USA). According to previous studies [35,36], the thermosensitive chitosan/gelatin/GP hydrogel was produced in a laminar flow hood. In brief, the GP solution was added drop by drop into the chitosan and gelatin solution under stirring on ice. The 0.5% (w/v, g/ml) strontium chloride (Sigma-Aldrich, USA, 255521) solution was mixed with the above gelatin solution. Finally, added 0.1% (w/v, g/ml) genipin (Sigma-Aldrich, USA, G4796). After the above steps were completed, the strontium modified thermosensitive hydrogel was synthesized. The reinforced thermosensitive hydrogel was used as a controlled release system of GM nanocomplexes. The GM nanocomplexes were added into the hydrogel under stirring in an ice bath. Encapsulation efficiency (EE) was determined by quantifying unentrapped GM in the supernatant after gelation (PBS, 37 °C, 30 min) at 230 nm, and in vitro release was assessed by measuring released GM at the same wavelength in PBS (37 °C) with periodic medium replacement (1–72 h), fitting the cumulative profile to the Higuchi model. Post-release stability was evaluated by DLS of GM in the release medium at 12–72 h compared with freshly prepared GM, and cellular uptake was visualized by confocal microscopy after incubating PDLCs with 25% extract of FITC-GM-loaded hydrogel for 24 h and DAPI counterstaining. The GM-Sr@Hydrogel system was stored at 4 °C for further research.

Characterization of the GM-Sr@Hydrogel

To test the fluidity of the hydrogel, Sr@Hydrogel and GM-Sr@Hydrogel, the synthesized hydrogels were placed in syringes and the syringes were pushed to observe whether them could flow smoothly from the needle of the syringe. At the same time, draw the pattern on a plane and their plasticity was observed when they completely solidified at 37 °C. The flowable hydrogel, Sr@Hydrogel and GM-Sr@Hydrogel were injected into a screw-top bottle. To determine if solidification had occurred, the bottle was tilted every 30 s to observe if the hydrogel was flowing or if the position of the mixture changed. The time when the hydrogel, Sr@Hydrogel and GM-Sr@Hydrogel stopped flowing at 37 °C was recorded as the gelling time. Comprehensive rheological analyses, including temperature-ramp and time-sweep measurements were performed at 37 °C. The GM-Sr@Hydrogel precursor solution was subjected to a dynamic temperature sweep from 25 °C to 45 °C at a constant frequency of 1 Hz (ω = 6.283 rad/s) and a heating rate of approximately 1 °C/min. The storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G″) were recorded as a function of temperature and time. The hydrogel, Sr@Hydrogel and GM-Sr@Hydrogel were prepared and allowed to gel for 12 h. Subsequently, the hydrogels were freeze-dried at −80 °C using a lyophilizer (BETA1-8, Germany) for 48 h. The freeze-dried samples were made brittle by liquid nitrogen and adhered to the sample platform using conductive glue. Finally, gold powder was sprayed on the surface and then observed using a scanning electron microscope (SEM, Hitachi S-4800, Japan). Morphological examination and energy spectrum scanning were performed on the samples.

To explore the stability of the hydrogel, Sr@Hydrogel and GM-Sr@Hydrogel, they were immersed in PBS. The hydrogels were subjected to gelation and complete freeze-drying treatment. The hydrogels were weighed, placed in PBS, and shaken vigorously at 37 °C. The swelling performance of the hydrogels were evaluated by comparing the changes in their mass before and after immersion at different times. The swelling ratio was calculated using the following formula: Sw% = (Wt-W0)/W0, where Sw represents the change in mass of the hydrogel before and after soaking, W0 is the initial mass, and Wt is the final mass, respectively. Additionally, their degradation behavior characteristics were tested. In summary, the gel-like hydrogel, Sr@Hydrogel and GM-Sr@Hydrogel were weighed, placed in PBS, and incubated at 37 °C. After the predetermined time, the remaining hydrogels were removed from PBS and weighed again, and then dried. Finally, the mass changes of the hydrogels were calculated using the following formula: De% = (Dt-D0)/D0, where De represents in mass of the hydrogel before and after degradation, D0 is the initial mass, and Dt is the massed at a certain time. To investigate the mechanical performance optimization of strontium on the hydrogel, we conducted a compression assessment of the hydrogel in its gel state by electronic universal testing machine (China Meters Industrial System, CMT6103). In vitro retention was evaluated using the buccal retention model developed by Madsen et al. [37]. Fresh porcine buccal mucosa was mounted on an inclined support (∼20°) at 37 °C. GM-Sr@Hydrogel was injected onto the mucosal surface and allowed to gel. Retention under shear was assessed by continuous irrigation with pre-warmed artificial saliva (OriLeaf, China; 37 °C, pH 7.4) delivered via a gravity-driven infusion set at 1 mL/min onto the gel-filled mucosa. Retention was evaluated by photographic documentation at defined time intervals. During the gelation-dissolution transition of the hydrogel, to assess the viscosity of the GM-Sr@Hydrogel, we took images of the morphology of the hydrogel.

Animal experiments

All experimental procedures were approved by the ethics committee, Fujian Medical University, with the approval number being IACUC FJMU 2025-Y-2208. Sprague Dawley rats (8-week-old, weighing an average of 200 g, male) were purchased from Wushi Experimental Animal Limited Company (Fuzhou, China). All experimental animals were raised and managed under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions, with a 12-h/12-h light-dark cycle and adequate food and water supply. Animals were anaesthetized with pentobarbital sodium (100 mg/kg, injected intraperitoneally). According to the previous research [38], periodontitis (PD) was induced by placing the 3.0-silk ligature in the crevice of the bilateral second upper molars to accumulate the bacterial biofilm. Soft food was feed for 2 days after the operation, and the loading silks were checked daily. If the silks have fallen, they were promptly reloaded. For experiments assessing the effect of the GM-Sr@Hydrogel on PD, animals were randomly and equally allocated to five groups by the random isolation table (n = 6 per group): Sham, PD, PD + Hydrogel, PD + Sr@Hydrogel, PD + GM-Sr@Hydrogel. After local cleaning and drying, periodontal ligament injections were administered to the maxillary second molar using a microinjector [39]. Rats in the PD + Hydrogel, PD + Sr@Hydrogel, or PD + GM-Sr@Hydrogel group received periodontal ligament injection of 20 μl Hydrogel, Sr@Hydrogel or GM-Sr@Hydrogel every three days. Animals in the Sham and PD were treated with equal volume PBS. Visual inspection after injection and at subsequent monitoring time points to confirm that the hydrogel formed a coherent gel in situ and did not cause gingival bulging or excessive material extrusion. Animals were sacrificed according to the schematic diagram after different interventions for analyses (Fig. 2A). Macroscopic hydrogel remnants were observed at the time of sacrifice and tissue harvesting to confirm whether the material had been fully cleared.

Morphometric analysis

Animals were sacrificed by anesthetic overdose, the harvested maxillary tissues were separated, fixed by 4% paraformaldehyde for 48 h and stored in 70% ethanol at 4 °C before being processed. The morphology of the maxillary molars and the condition of periodontal tissue were observed under a stereomicroscope (Nikon, SMZ18).

Micro-computed tomography

Micro-CT scanning of the maxillae of mice was applied by NEMO Micro CT (filter Al 0.2 mm, 70 kV, 114 μA) (PINGSENG Healthcare Kunshan Inc, China) with the resolution of 10 μm to detect the change of alveolar bone. Three‐dimensional (3D) images were reconstructed by Cruiser (PINGSENG Healthcare Kunshan Inc, China) for morphological evaluation. The distance between the cemento-enamel junction (CEJ) and the alveolar bone crest (ABC) was measured with ImageJ (American National Institutes of Health).

Histology and immunofluorescence staining

The maxillae of rats were decalcified using 0.5 M EDTA for 3 weeks, dehydrated, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 5-μm used for staining. The organs of rats were dehydrated, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 5-μm used for staining. According to the manufacturer recommendations, the sections were stained with hematoxylin-eosin staining (HE). Observe the inflammatory infiltration situation of gingiva and the absorption of alveolar bone under the microscope.

For immunofluorescence analysis, after antigen retrieval and blocking treatment, frozen sections, and fixed periodontal ligament cells (PDLCs) were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies target in OPN (ABclonal, 1:200), RUNX-2(ABclonal, 1:200), IL-1β (ABclonal, 1:200), iNOS (ABclonal, 1:200), IL-6 (ABclonal, 1:200), and CD80 (Proteintech, 1:200). The secondary antibodies included donkey anti-mouse Alexa Fluor 488/555/647 and donkey anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor 488/555/647 (all from Thermo Fisher Scientific). The cell nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (Sigma, D9542), the images of the samples were observed using confocal microscope and analyzed using ImageJ software. Sections were blinded and scored by two experienced researchers, and the average scores were used for statistical analyses.

Isolation and culture of periodontal ligament cells (PDLCs) and dendritic cells (DCs)

Periodontal ligament cells (PDLCs) were isolated from periodontal ligament tissue as described previously [40]. Human PDLCs were isolated from the healthy periodontal ligament of premolars extracted for orthodontic indications, with no evidence of periodontal disease. All experiments were performed using cells between passages 3 and 5. These cells were cultured in α-MEM medium (Gibco) supplemented with 1% penicillin-streptomycin and 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; BioInd, Kibbutz, Israel). The culture conditions are 37 °C, 5% humidity of carbon dioxide environment, and 95% humidity. When the cells reached 70% confluence, the cells were passaged by the trypsin. PDLCs at passage 3-6 were used. According to the previous research [41], the bone marrow cells suspension was obtained by rinsing, and then density gradient centrifugation was performed to obtain the bone marrow mononuclear cells. The cells were incubated with red blood cell lysis buffer (APPLYGEN, China) and washed. Then, the cells were inoculated in a medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum (Australian FBS), 1% penicillin-streptomycin, 2 mM l-glutamine, HEPES buffer, and supplemented with 20 ng/mL recombinant mouse GM-CSF (Perprotech, USA) and 10 ng/mL recombinant mouse IL-4 (Perprotech, USA). The medium was changed half a day later, and the medium was changed half again on the fourth day. The non-adherent dendritic cells (DCs) were collected for the experiments.

Calcein/PI cell viability and cytotoxicity assay

According to the Calcein/PI manufacturer recommendations (BIYONTIME, C1371S), prepared the Calcein/PI working fluid. PDLCs were seeded in 24-well plates, cultured with the specified culture medium containing certain concentrations of the extract (0%, 25%, 50%, 75%, and 100% of the Hydrogel, Sr@Hydrogel, and GM-Sr@Hydrogel) for 24 h. The PDLCs were labeled with the Calcein/PI working fluid for 30 min. The images were acquired with confocal microscopy.

CCK-8 cell viability assay

PDLCs were plated in 96-well plates at 3500 cells per well in 100 μl of culture medium supplemented with 10 μl of CCK-8 reagent (MCE) and incubated at 37 °C for 2.5h following indicated treatments. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm using the microplate reader (Synergy H1; BioTek).

The Hydrogel, Sr@Hydrogel, and GM-Sr@Hydrogel precursor were first gelled in 96-well plates at 37 °C to form a stable hydrogel layer. PDLCs were then seeded directly onto the surface of the pre-formed hydrogels and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h or 48 h. Untreated PDLCs seeded on standard tissue culture plastic served as the control. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

Inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) analysis of Sr2+ release

The GM-Sr@Hydrogel was gelled in PBS (pH 7.4) at 37 °C for 24 h to prepare the 25% extract—identical to the conditions used for all in vitro osteogenic and immunomodulatory assays. The Sr2+ concentration in the extract was determined by ICP-MS.

ALP staining

PDLCs were seeded in 6-well plates in osteogenesis-inducing medium with the extract (containing 25% concentrations) of Sr@Hydrogel (Sr) and GM-Sr@Hydrogel (Sr-GM). Sr is represented as the extract of Sr@Hydrogel, Sr-GM is represented as the extract of GM-Sr@Hydrogel. After 7 and 14 days of osteogenesis-induction, the cells were fixed. According to the instructions, ALP staining was performed (Beyotime Biotechnology), and stained plates were photographed and inspected with a light microscope.

Alizarin red staining

PDLCs were seeded in 6-well plates in osteogenesis-inducing medium with the extract (containing 25% concentrations) of Sr@Hydrogel (Sr) and GM-Sr@Hydrogel (Sr-GM). Sr is represented as the extract of Sr@Hydrogel, Sr-GM is represented as the extract of GM-Sr@Hydrogel. After 21 days of osteogenesis-induction, the cells were fixed. According to the instructions, alizarin red staining was performed (Beyotime Biotechnology), and stained plates were photographed and inspected with a light microscope.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

After the treatments, total RNA was extracted with Trizol (Takara, Tokyo, Japan). The first-strand cDNA was prepared using HiScript II Q RT SuperMix for qPCR (Vazyme Biotech co., China). qRT-PCR was performed using SYBR Premix Ex TaqII (Vazyme Biotech co., China) in CFX96 Real-Time System (Bio-Rad). GAPDH RNA was used as a housekeeping control. The primer sequences are listed in (Supplementary Table 1). The relative levels of genes expression were normalized to GAPDH and calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt.

Western blotting assay

The PDLCs or DCs lysates were extracted using RIPA buffer (Beyotime, P0013B) for 30 min containing PMSF (Beyotime, ST505). The concentrations of the proteins were detected by BCA protein assay kit (Beyotime, #P0012). The samples were heated at 100 °C for 5min in sample buffer, separated on 10% SDS-polyacrylamide gels, and transferred to PVDF membranes (Bio-Rad). The membranes were blotted with 5% BSA and incubated with primary antibody at 4 °C overnight. The membranes were washed in TBST solution and incubated with the HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. The antibody-antigen complexes were visualized with ECL reagents (Millipore, WBKLS0100). The following primary antibodies were applied, ALP (ABclonal, 1:1000), BSP (ABclonal, 1:1000), OPN (ABclonal, 1:1000), COL1(ABclonal, 1:1000), IL-1β (ABclonal, 1:1000), iNOS (ABclonal, 1:1000), IL-6 (ABclonal, 1:1000), TNF-α (ABclonal, 1:1000), CD80 (Proteintech, 1:1000), and GAPDH (Servicebio, 1:5000).

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay

The ELISA was performed according to standard protocols (HUABIO, EM0004, EM0010). The culture supernatants of DCs were obtained under different treatments. Briefly, 96-well microtiter plates were added with 50 μL per well of detection buffer solution. 10 μL of serially diluted samples or standards were added to the wells. Then add 50 μL of the detection antibody to each well. A sealing film was used to seal the plate and incubated it at room temperature in the shaker for 1 h. After removal of the coating solution, the plates were then washed four times, followed by the addition of 100 μL per well of a horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated detection antibody, and incubated for 30 min at 37 °C. After another four washes, the enzymatic reaction was developed by adding 100 μL of chromogenic substrate solution and incubated for 15 min in the dark at room temperature. The reaction was terminated by the addition of 100 μL of stop solution and the absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. The antigen concentration was determined by interpolation from a standard curve generated with known concentrations of the purified antigen. DCs were also incubated with the GO solution or LPS, and TNF-α secretion was measured by ELISA.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as the mean ± standard error of at least three independent experiments. Statistically significant differences were evaluated using two-tailed Student's t tests for comparison between two groups or by one-way analysis of variance followed by the Tukey's test for multiple comparisons. All statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 8. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Rational design and multi-faceted characterization of a strontium-incorporated hydrogel platform co-delivering graphene oxide-mannose nanocomplexes (GM-Sr@Hydrogel)

As shown in Fig. 1A, we first synthesized the graphene oxide-D-mannose complex (GM) through dynamic self-assembly technology, synthesized blank thermos-responsive hydrogel (composed of chitosan, 0.6% gelatin, and β-glycerophosphate) and strontium-modified thermos-responsive hydrogel, and then loaded GM into the strontium-modified thermos-responsive hydrogel to construct strontium-modified, GM-loaded hydrogel system (GM-Sr@Hydrogel). To further evaluate the characteristics of temperature sensitivity and liquidity, we developed the small bottle inversion experiment. The results showed that the Hydrogel, the Sr@Hydrogel, and the GM-Sr@Hydrogel all had good fluidity and could smoothly pass through the syringe at 4 °C, which meets the requirements of periodontal membrane injection therapy (Fig. 1B). And three hydrogels remained liquid at 4 °C and became non-flowing gels at the bottom of the inverted bottle after a few minutes at 37 °C. Besides, all the hydrogels exhibited excellent plasticity, making them suitable for on-site repair of local irregular cartilage injuries. No significant difference in gelation time was observed among the Hydrogel, the Sr@Hydrogel, and the GM-Sr@Hydrogel groups (Fig. 1C). The gelation times were 8.97 ± 0.08 min, 8.98 ± 0.12 min, and 8.98 ± 0.07 min, respectively. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 1, upon heating from 25 °C to 45 °C, G′ increased dramatically from 29 Pa to 482 Pa (a >16-fold increase), while G″ increased only modestly from 9 Pa to 22 Pa in GM-Sr@Hydrogel. Correspondingly, tan(δ) decreased from 0.31 to 0.047. This obvious thermal-responsive stiffening indicated that the material has significantly transitioned from its initial state of weakly structured viscoelastic fluid to a highly elastic and solid-dominated state. Afterwards, the surface morphology of the gelated hydrogels were evaluated by scanning electron microscopy (SEM), and it was found that the modification with Sr and the loading of GM did not alter the spatial pore size of the thermosensitive hydrogel (Fig. 1D). The microscopic observations confirmed the successful immobilization of Sr2+ in Sr@Hydrogel and GM-Sr@Hydrogel evidenced by rougher pore walls, compared to the Hydrogel. Notably, energy spectrum scanning corroborated these findings, confirming a substantial increase in Sr loading and a strong correlation between Sr distribution and the porous structure of the scaffold (Fig. 1D). As shown in Fig. 1E, when the three hydrogels entered the equilibrium solution, their swelling rates significantly increased within 5 min and stabilized approximately 10 min later. Furthermore, after 12 days, the degradation rates of all three hydrogels at room temperature exceeded 40% (Fig. 1F). No remarkable differences in the swelling rate and the degradation rate were observed among the Hydrogel, the Sr@Hydrogel, and the GM-Sr@Hydrogel. Compared with the Hydrogel group, the compressive modulus and compressive strength of the Sr@Hydrogel group both increased, indicating that the Sr@Hydrogel had a stronger ability to absorb compressive energy than the Hydrogel, and strontium modification enhanced the mechanical properties of hydrogels (Supplementary Fig. 2). Furthermore, it remained localized on the mucosal surface for over 3 h under continuous irrigation, with a retention rate of 73% at 3 h (Supplementary Fig. 3A). When undergoing the stretching process, the GM-Sr@Hydrogel exhibited excellent stickiness (Supplementary Fig. 3B). Besides, encapsulation efficiency of GM-Sr@Hydrogel was 89.82 ± 2.60%, confirming that the majority of GM nanocomplexes were entrapped within the network rather than surface-adsorbed. The cumulative GM nanocomplexes release reached 82.6% at 72 h, following diffusion-controlled kinetics without a pronounced burst, functionally confirming stable entrapment (Supplementary Fig. 4). DLS analysis showed that the hydrodynamic diameter of released GM (404.1–454.7 nm) did not differ significantly from freshly prepared GM (441.5 ± 63.07 nm), demonstrating post-release stability (Supplementary Fig. 5). Confocal microscopy revealed prominent intracellular green fluorescence in PDLCs after 24 h incubation with FITC-GM-loaded hydrogel extract, confirming efficient cellular internalization (Supplementary Fig. 6). Taken together, these findings confirmed that the construction of the GM-Sr@Hydrogel system was successful. GM-Sr@Hydrogel attenuates periodontitis via suppression of alveolar bone resorption

Periodontitis manifests as gingival inflammation, attachment loss, alveolar bone resorption. To explore whether the GM-Sr@Hydrogel combat periodontitis through rescue alveolar bone resorption, we constructed an animal model induced by tight ligation at the cervical margin of the second molar using orthodontic wire. As the schematic diagram tells (Fig. 2A), following periosteum injection of the Hydrogel, the Sr@Hydrogel, and the GM-Sr@Hydrogel per 3 days, the assessment of the therapeutic effects was performed by stereomicroscope observation, 3D reconstruction of the maxillary second molar, and immunofluorescence staining (Fig. 2B–D). Gross morphological observations revealed that the PD group exhibited periodontal pocket formation and severe root exposure compared with the Sham group (Fig. 2B). However, treatment with Sr@Hydrogel and GM-Sr@Hydrogel explicitly ameliorated the bone loss and the formation of periodontal pockets. Among them, the GM-Sr@Hydrogel group showed the most pronounced recovery of periodontal alveolar morphology, while the Hydrogel group seemed to be no therapeutic effect of periodontitis (Fig. 2B). Moreover, Micro-CT 3D-reconstruction showed that distinct alveolar bone loss and trabecular destruction in the PD group and the PD + Hydrogel group (Fig. 2C). Notably, the GM-Sr@Hydrogel treatment effectively restored alveolar bone and trabecular structure, achieving a therapeutic effect superior to that of the Sr@Hydrogel alone, closely approximating the healthy bone phenotype of the Sham group. The state of the bone trabeculae below the root bifurcation was consistent with the results shown from Micro-CT (Supplementary Fig. 7). Through statistically analysis of the length from cemento-enamel junction to alveolar bone crest distance (CEJ-ABC), the Sr@hydrogel and the GM-Sr@hydrogel interventions could evidently reverse the reduction in the CEJ-ABC in the mesial, distal and root bifurcation regions caused by PD (Fig. 2C). And the improvement effect of the GM-Sr@hydrogel on the height of the alveolar bone is more significant. To further determine the effect of the GM-Sr@hydrogel on the bone remodeling of the alveolar bone in periodontitis, we applied immunofluorescence staining to assess the expression of osteogenic-related factors, including OPN and RUNX-2. Immunofluorescence staining showed that, compared to the Sham group, the number of OPN+ and RUNX-2+ cells in pericementum apparently dropped in the PD group and the PD+Hydrogel group (Fig. 2D), nevertheless the treatment of the Sr@hydrogel and the GM-Sr@hydrogel reversed the decrease. It is noteworthy that, compared with the Sr@hydrogel group, there was more pronounced upregulation in the number of the two types of positive cells in the GM-Sr@hydrogel group. Overall, these data indicated that the therapeutic potential of the GM-Sr@Hydrogel in periodontitis via rebalance bone resorption and bone formation.

GM-Sr@Hydrogel ameliorates periodontal inflammatory infiltration to alleviate periodontitis

Excessive inflammatory activation precedes and promotes osteoclastogenesis-osteogenesis imbalance and subsequent alveolar bone destruction in periodontitis. To further elucidate whether the GM-Sr@Hydrogel system exerts direct anti-inflammatory activity to interrupt the inflammatory cascade upstream of bone loss, hematoxylin-eosin (HE) staining is employed to evaluate the inflammatory state of the periodontal tissues. As displayed in Fig. 3A, it revealed distinct differences in periodontal inflammatory infiltration across groups in gingival tissues and periodontal ligament. The Sham group exhibited intact periodontal structure with minimal inflammatory cell infiltration and well-preserved periodontal ligament (PDL) fibers. In contrast, the PD group showed severe inflammatory cell infiltration in the gingival, accompanied by destruction of PDL fibers and alveolar bone resorption (Fig. 3A). The Hydrogel produced no significant therapeutic benefit. Nevertheless, the Sr@Hydrogel group exhibited significantly attenuated inflammatory cell accumulation and improved PDL fiber integrity, while GM-Sr@Hydrogel was superior. Immunofluorescence staining offered cellular perspective to detect the expression of pro-inflammatory mediators IL-1β and iNOS (Fig. 3B). Quantitative analysis demonstrated that the PD group exhibited a dramatic increase in the percentage of IL-1β+ and iNOS+ positive cells in the periodontal tissues, compared with the Sham group (P < 0.05). However, the number of IL-1β+ and iNOS+ cells significantly decreased after the treatment with the Sr@Hydrogel, while the GM-Sr@Hydrogel treatment achieved the more robust reduction (Fig. 3B). Comprehensive biocompatibility evaluation of GM-Sr@Hydrogel: negligible cytotoxicity in vitro and no observable toxicity in vivo at certain concentrations

Since the therapeutic potential of the GM-Sr@Hydrogel had been proved, there are some obstacles to become competent biological agent, such as the biocompatibility. To evaluate the in vivo biocompatibility of the GM-Sr@Hydrogel, HE staining was performed on the organs including the liver, kidney, and heart of animals (Fig. 4A). In Fig. 4A, it showed that all the groups displayed normal organ structure. Hepatocytes were arranged in a regular radial pattern around the central vein, with intact cellular morphology, abundant eosinophilic cytoplasm, and centrally positioned, uniformly round nuclei without signs of swelling or pyknosis. There were no significant performance of hepatocellular necrosis, inflammatory cell infiltration, or fatty degeneration. In kidney, the proximal and distal renal tubules displayed intact epithelial lining and preserved brush borders, with no tubular dilation, atrophy, or inflammatory infiltration. Besides the heart kept well-preserved myocardial fibers with no pathological damage. These above data indicated that the excellent in vivo biocompatibility of the GM-Sr@Hydrogel, with no systemic toxicity to vital organs. Additionally, in vitro cytocompatibility was further evaluated via Calcein/PI live/dead staining and CCK-8 cell proliferation assay on PDLCs (Fig. 4B and C). As shown in Fig. 4B and Supplementary Fig. 8, all three hydrogel formulations (Hydrogel, Sr@Hydrogel, GM-Sr@Hydrogel) maintained high cell viability across concentration gradients ranging from 0% to 50%, with few PI-positive dead cells observed. Treated with 75% and 100% concentration, Calcein+ cells reduced, and PI+ cells emerged. Consistent with this, CCK-8 results demonstrated that cell viability remained stable in all groups at 0%, 25%, and 50% concentration, and no significant cytotoxicity was detected (P > 0.05). However, 75% and 100% concentration imposed apparent toxicity (P < 0.05) (Fig. 4C). Besides, the cytocompatibility of the Hydrogel, Sr@Hydrogel, and GM-Sr@Hydrogel under direct contact conditions were evaluated by seeding PDLCs onto pre-formed hydrogel. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 9, these three groups showed no significant difference in cell activity compared to the Con group in 24 h or 48 h. These results demonstrate that the hydrogels were non-cytotoxic even when cells are cultured directly on the hydrogel surfaces. Sr-GM boosts the ability of osteogenesis in periodontal ligament cells (PDLCs)

While in vivo studies revealed that the potent of the GM-Sr@Hydrogel in suppressing bone resorption and inflammation, therefore, in vitro experiments were firstly conducted to validate its osteogenic efficacy in PDLCs. The amount of strontium of the 25% GM-Sr@Hydrogel extract was tested by ICP-MS. As shown in Supplementary Table 2, the Sr2+ concentration in the 25% extract was determined to be 2.12 ± 0.03 mM. In the ALP staining (an indicator reflecting early bone formation) assay, there was prominently higher activity of PDLCs in in vitro osteogenic induction environment (Fig. 5A). On the 7th day, the ALP activity of the Osteoinduction + Sr group showed no significant change compared to the Osteoinduction group, while the ALP activity in the Osteoinduction + Sr-GM group increased significantly. Interestingly, on the 14th day, compared with the Osteoinduction group, the ALP activity in the Osteoinduction + Sr group and the Osteoinduction + Sr-GM group showed a remarkable increase (Fig. 5A). Meanwhile, the Alizarin Red staining was used to observe the formation of mineralized nodules in PDLCs under different osteogenic induction conditions. After 21 days of osteogenic induction, compared with the Con group, the alizarin red staining in the Osteoinduction group was deeper. Furthermore, compared with the Osteoinduction group, the number of mineralized nodules in the Osteoinduction + Sr group increased slightly, while the number in the Osteoinduction + Sr-GM group increase markedly (Fig. 5B). Next, we tested the expression of osteogenic-related genes after the application of the extract solution of the hydrogels. At the mRNA level, the expressions of key osteogenic transcription factors and functional markers, including Osterix, Alp, Runx-2, and Ocn in PDLCs in the Sr-GM group were drastically upregulated (Fig. 5C). On the 21st day after osteogenic induction, we conducted protein-level detection of the genes related to osteogenic differentiation in the PDLCs. Noteworthy, there was an obvious upregulation in the protein expression of osteogenic related genes such as COL1, ALP, OPN, BSP, and OCN in PDLCs in the osteogenic induction microenvironment (Fig. 5D). Compared with the Osteoinduction group, the expression of the osteogenic related genes sharply upregulated in the Osteoinduction + Sr group and in the Osteoinduction + Sr-GM group. Likewise, in the Osteoinduction + Sr-GM group, the PDLCs demonstrated the best osteogenic differentiation ability. Sr-GM suppresses the pro-inflammatory phenotype of PDLCs and restores inflammatory homeostasis

To further investigate the anti-inflammatory efficacy of the GM-Sr@Hydrogel, we evaluated the inhibitory effect on inflammatory phenotype of PDLCs in the LPS-induced inflammatory microenvironment at transcriptional, translational, and cellular levels (Fig. 6). As shown in Fig. 6A, the mRNA level of key pro-inflammatory cytokines, including Il-6, Il-8, and Tnf-α in PDLCs stimulated by LPS remarkably upregulated, indicating the successful establishment of the inflammatory microenvironment in vitro model. However, the Sr-GM treatment significantly reduced the mRNA expression of the three genes in PDLCs under inflammatory conditions. Similarly, the intervention of LPS apparently increased the protein expression levels of inflammatory related genes such as IL-1β and iNOS (Fig. 6B). Besides, the Sr treatment and the Sr-GM treatment markedly reduced the protein levels of IL-1β in LPS-stimulated PDLCs, returned to levels approaching the control evidenced by quantitative statistics (Fig. 6B). Meanwhile, compared with the Sr group, the PDLC cells in the Sr-GM group exhibited a more significant inhibitory effect on the protein levels of iNOS. The Sr-GM group exhibited a numerically lower protein expression of IL-1β, although the difference did not reach statistical significance. Furthermore, immunofluorescence staining supported these results at the cellular level. LPS stimulation induced a robust increase in the fluorescence intensity of IL-6 and IL-1β in PDLCs, which was significantly attenuated by the Sr treatment and the Sr-GM treatment. Compared to the Sr group, the Sr-GM treatment resulted in a further numerical reduction but did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 6C). Consistent with the results in vivo, the GM-Sr@Hydrogel performed the Sr@Hydrogel in inflammation-inhibitory potency. Sr-GM acts as an osteoimmune modulator by restraining dendritic cells (DCs) maturation

Building on the pro-osteogenic and anti-inflammatory effects of the GM-Sr@Hydrogel demonstrated above, we further explored its immunomodulatory mechanism by evaluating its impact on dendritic cells (DCs) maturation, a key driver of inflammatory bone destruction in periodontitis (Fig. 7). The GO solution and GM nanocomplex solution were confirmed to be free of biologically significant endotoxin contamination, as evidenced by a negative TNF-α expression test in DCs, thereby excluding endotoxin-driven artefacts from the observed immunomodulatory effects (Supplementary Fig. 10). As presented in Fig. 7A, morphological observation revealed that LPS stimulation induced typical mature morphology of DCs, characterized by elongated, branched dendrites and an irregular cell shape, indicating activated immunological activity. In contrast, the treatment of the Sr-GM effectively reversed these morphological changes, with DCs retaining a more rounded, immature phenotype like the Con group. Consistent with the morphological findings, it showed that LPS obviously upregulated the mRNA expression of DCs maturation markers (Cd86 and Il-12p40) and pro-inflammatory cytokines (Il-1β) (Fig. 7B). In addition, compared with the LPS group, the mRNA level of Cd86, Il-12p40, and Il-1β in DCs in the Sr-GM group declined distinctly. Western blot results further confirmed these findings at the protein level. LPS stimulation markedly increased the protein expression of pro-inflammatory mediators (TNF-α, IL-1β, and iNOS) and the maturation marker CD80 in DCs (Fig. 7C). Nevertheless, the Sr treatment and the Sr-GM treatment potently inhibited the expression of all these proteins, and the inhibitory effect of the Sr-GM treatment was stronger than that the Sr treatment. The secretion of inflammatory-related factors like IL-6 and TNF-α on the DCs supernatant was detected through ELISA experiment. As shown in Fig. 7D, the secretion level of IL-6 and TNF-α all significantly elevated in the supernatant of the LPS group compared to the Con group. However, after the intervention with Sr and Sr-GM, the secretion of IL-6 and TNF-α in the DCs supernatant was significantly reduced. Furthermore, compared to the LPS + Sr group, the LPS + Sr-GM group exhibited a more significant inhibitory effect on the secretion of IL-6. In vivo, immunofluorescence staining of periodontal tissues showed a substantial increase in CD80+ mature DCs infiltration of periodontal ligament tissues in periodontitis rats compared to the Sham group (Fig. 7E). Treatment with the Sr@Hydrogel partially reduced DCs infiltration, while the GM-Sr@Hydrogel treatment significantly and profoundly decreased the number of CD80+ DCs in the PDL, effectively inhibiting local DCs maturation in the periodontal microenvironment. Discussion

In this study, we innovatively developed an injectable, strontium-incorporated thermosensitive hydrogel system encapsulating nanoscale GM complexes (designated GM-Sr@Hydrogel) and demonstrated its dual osteogenic and immunomodulatory efficacy. By employing an in vivo model of periodontitis, an in vitro inflammatory microenvironment, as well as the osteogenic induction system, the GM-Sr@Hydrogel functioned as an osteoimmune regulator, effectively attenuating periodontal inflammation, and suppressing alveolar bone resorption. Specifically, this system enhanced the osteogenic differentiation of periodontal ligament cells (PDLCs), while mitigating inflammatory phenotype, and concurrently impeded the maturation of dendritic cells (DCs) under inflammatory microenvironment (Fig. 8).Fig. 8Schematic illustration of a strontium-incorporated thermoresponsive hydrogel platform co-delivering graphene oxide-mannose nanocomplexes to alleviate periodontitis via osteoimmune regulation. Periodontitis is an inflammatory bone-resorptive disorder driven by complex host–microbe interactions. Vashishta demonstrated that F. alocis promotes alveolar bone loss through TLR2-dependent signaling, while dysregulated neutrophil activity causes collateral tissue damage via inflammatory mediators [42]. Cullinan et al. further showed that IL-10 polymorphisms influence disease progression rates, underscoring the genetic contribution to susceptibility [43]. Despite these insights, investigations have largely been confined to single-dimensional perspectives, and the precise crosstalk between the immune compartment and osseous tissue during periodontitis remains incompletely understood. Clinically, current treatments can disrupt biofilms and transiently suppress inflammation but fail to regenerate lost alveolar bone, partly owing to inadequate local retention of therapeutic agents and the emergence of drug resistance with repeated administration [[44], [45], [46], [47], [48], [49]]. Although advanced biomaterials and drug delivery systems offer potential solutions, their efficacy is frequently compromised by oral physiological challenges—gingival crevicular fluid flow, mastication, and gravity—that limit local retention [[50], [51], [52]]. Hydrogels are particularly compelling in this context: their three-dimensional hydrophilic network absorbs biological fluids and adheres to both soft and hard tissues, making them well-suited for intraoral application [51,53]. In the present study, we successfully introduced an injectable hydrogel, thermosensitive hydrogel system whose adaptive plasticity within the dynamic periodontal microenvironment establishes a platform for controlled and targeted therapy.

Notably, hydrogels are widely used for both local and systemic delivery of diverse therapeutic factors [54,55]. Strontium (Sr) constitutes a principal component of bones and teeth [56,57]. In aged osteoporotic murine models, strontium supplementation has been shown to activate the Wnt signaling pathway, thereby promoting the generation of osteoblasts [58]. Consistent with these findings, data from C Letícia Macedo Marins's study, strontium ranelate could effectively mitigate bone loss caused by ligature-induction in estrogen deficiency [59]. Collectively, the above data thus provide a hint concerning as whether strontium incorporated into the hydrogel system can create a microenvironment conducive to bone remodeling for the periodontium. To address this, we integrated Sr into the synthesized thermosensitive hydrogel. It is noteworthy that, relative to the unmodified Hydrogel, the hydrogel functionalized by Sr (Sr@ Hydrogel) exhibited no appreciable alterations in key physicochemical properties, including fluidity, swelling kinetics, degradation profile, and pore architecture. Intriguingly, however, in the in vivo model of periodontitis, the Sr@ Hydrogel distinctly promoted alveolar bone remodeling and concurrently suppressed the expression of the pro-inflammatory markers IL-1β and iNOS within periodontal ligament. The result of ICP-MS indicated Sr concentration was within the established 1-5 mM range that has been documented to activate osteogenic signaling pathways and promote osteoblast differentiation [[60], [61], [62]]. Furthermore, in the in vitro bone induction microenvironment simulation, the Sr@ Hydrogel upregulated the expression of osteogenic-related genes in PDLCs.

Dendritic cells (DCs) are central to osteoimmune regulation [63], as evidenced by their protective role against bisphosphonate-related osteonecrosis and their capacity to exacerbate apical periodontitis via NLRP3 inflammasome-mediated cytokine release [64,65]. The carbon-based nanomaterial graphene oxide (GO) possesses notable immunomodulatory properties. Previous studies have revealed that DCs could internalize GO and subsequently modulates their abilities for antigen presentation to T-cells [29]. In a comprehensive study by Parker et al., GO flake lateral dimension critically governed DC ability to activate Ag-specific CD4 or CD8 T cells [66]. Consequently, the regulation strategy of osteoimmunology targeting DC's functions is expected to become an encouraging approach to novel therapies of periodontitis. A close immunoregulatory interplay exists between D-mannose (DM) and dendritic cells, primarily attributable to the high expression of mannose receptors on the surface of DCs [67,68]. These receptors exhibit specific recognition and binding affinity can specifically for carbohydrate moieties such as mannose and fucose. The ligand-receptor-mediated bridge via mannose moieties provides a possibility for the targeted regulation of DCs. Importantly, as a naturally occurring monosaccharide, DM presents unparalleled advantages over more complex targeting ligands in terms of both material modification feasibility and biocompatibility [69]. Meanwhile, GO is replete with functional groups, which confer a high specific surface area and substantial capacity for further functionalization [70]. Based on the recent reports, firstly, a GM complex was constructed by assembling GO with DM in this study. GM nanocomplexes were prepared via a spontaneous assemble approach without the use of chemical crosslinkers. It is primarily driven by hydrogen bonding between the hydroxyl groups of D-mannose and the oxygen-containing functional groups of GO, supplemented by CH–π and van der Waals interactions. This non-covalent strategy preserves the native structure and bioactivity of D-mannose while providing a stable complex for subsequent hydrogel incorporation. Subsequently, we loaded GM to the Sr@hydrogel. After multi-faceted characterization, it confirmed that the loading of GM did not compromise the intrinsic physicochemical properties of the hydrogel, and it still retained excellent fluidity, plasticity, spatial porosity, and biodegradability. The GM-Sr@Hydrogel was based on the CS/β-GP system. This system underwent thermal reinforcement, which is consistent with the results of previous studies [71,72]. Moreover, the GM-Sr@Hydrogel exhibited no discernible toxicity toward major organs, affirming its biological safety profile. Using imaging and histological evaluations, our study verified that the GM-Sr@hydrogel significantly attenuated periodontitis via suppression of alveolar bone resorption and inflammatory phenotype. Notably, compared with the Sr@hydrogel, the GM-Sr@hydrogel conferred superior therapeutic efficacy in the treatment of periodontitis. Consistent with these in vivo findings, under the osteogenic induction conditions and in the inflammatory microenvironment in vivo, the GM-Sr@hydrogel exhibited a stronger osteogenic induction and anti-inflammatory performance on PDLCs. Furthermore, we evaluated the influence of the GM-Sr@hydrogel on the differentiation of DCs. Our data revealed that the GM-Sr@Hydrogel functioned as an effective osteoimmune modulator by restraining the maturation of DCs. In the current study, ELISA analysis of DCs-conditioned medium established the DCs-to-PDLCs paracrine pathway by which GM-Sr@Hydrogel. It is also important to consider the possibility of a reciprocal PDLCs-to-DCs signaling axis. LPS-stimulated PDLCs have been reported to release soluble factors such as IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-6 [[73], [74], [75]], which are known to promote DCs maturation [76,77]. It is plausible that GM-Sr@Hydrogel-treated PDLCs, driven toward an osteogenic phenotype, may simultaneously acquire a paracrine profile that reinforces the suppression of DCS maturation. Dedicated experiments employing Transwell co-cultures is warranted in future studies to dissect the contribution of PDLCs-derived factors to DCs modulation. Extract-based protocols were chosen to isolate the paracrine signaling mechanisms underlying the observed osteogenic and immunomodulatory effects. While this does not fully replicate the direct cell–matrix contact that would occur upon in situ injection, it effectively models the predominant in vivo exposure mode—diffusion of soluble factors from the gel into the surrounding tissue. Complementary direct-contact and 3D encapsulation studies are planned to further elucidate the contribution of cell adhesion and matrix-mediated signaling to the overall regenerative response. Collectively, these findings delineate a framework wherein GM-Sr@Hydrogel coordinates PDLCs and DCs modulation to alleviate periodontitis.

Several limitations of the present study warrant acknowledgment. First, in the study, while we have demonstrated that the GM-Sr@Hydrogel could effectively suppress DC maturation and concomitantly promote osteogenic differentiation while mitigating inflammatory responses in PDLCs, the potential crosstalk between these two cellular populations in the context of periodontitis remains unresolved. Elucidating the interactive communication networks and underlying regulatory pathways through multi-omics analyses will constitute a critical focus of our future investigations. Secondly, although our findings provide promising preliminary evidence supporting the therapeutic applicability of the GM-Sr@Hydrogel in periodontitis, this work represents only an initial step toward clinical translation. Specifically,the current formulation is not yet optimized for routine clinical use. The current 3-day injection interval, while deliberately chosen to ensure maximal therapeutic exposure, would justify for this proof-of-concept study by both the release kinetics and literature precedent, would be clinically impractical. The current formulation therefore represents a first-generation proof-of-concept prototype rather than a clinically ready platform, and further optimization is required before translation can be considered. With further formulation refinement to suppress the initial burst release and prolong the release duration, a single injection coincident with scaling and root planing, or a weekly regimen integrated into the initial phase of periodontal therapy, appears clinically feasible. The minimally invasive injectable nature of the hydrogel, combined with such a reduced dosing frequency, would be expected to achieve patient comfort and treatment compliance comparable to, if not better than, existing non-surgical periodontal procedures. Beyond optimizing the dosing regimen, subsequent studies in large animal models will be essential to establish optimal delivery protocols. Moreover, additional retention studies in a true periodontal pocket-mimicking model are required before translational claims can be fully substantiated. More importantly, comprehensive, longitudinal investigations conducted from multiple perspectives will be necessary to fully characterize the long-term biocompatibility, systemic toxicity, and in vivo degradation pathways of the GM-Sr@Hydrogel system. Future studies will incorporate both the recombinant Factor C (rFC) assay, which is less susceptible to GO interference than LAL, and the TET assay as complementary orthogonal methods to rigorously certify the endotoxin-free status of our GO prior to any translational development.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we innovatively constructed a strontium-modified hydrogel system incorporating nanoscale GM complexes (GM-Sr@Hydrogel), which effectively alleviates periodontal inflammation and mitigates alveolar bone resorption through the coordinated modulation of periodontal ligament cells (PDLCs) and dendritic cells (DCs) (Fig. 8). The GM-Sr@Hydrogel system enhanced the osteogenic differentiation of PDLCs while concurrently suppressing their inflammatory phenotype and impeded DC maturation within an inflammatory microenvironment. To the best of our knowledge, this study represents the first application of a strontium-functionalized hydrogel loaded with GM complexes for the treatment of periodontitis. Collectively, our findings provide valuable insight into a novel osteoimmunology regulation strategy for maintaining periodontal homeostasis and identify GM-Sr@Hydrogel as a potential therapeutic candidate for periodontitis management.
