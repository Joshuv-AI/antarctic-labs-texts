Neurogenic diarrhoea after superior mesenteric artery divestment or resection for pancreatic adenocarcinoma following preoperative chemotherapy: international multicentre retrospective study
Authors: Thomas F Stoop, Chiara Croce, Hiroyuki Ishida, Mahsoem Ali, Ammar A Javed, Asif Halimi, Atsushi Oba, Bas Groot Koerkamp, Carl-Stephan Leonhardt, Cornelius A Thiels, Elena Rangelova, Ernesto Sparrelid, Ira Sotirova, Jennifer L Leiting, Jun Tauchi, Kosuke Kobayashi, Marit Klos, Lysanne P M Beuk, Luana Genova, Manuela Mastronardi, Michael L Kendrick, Nabiha A Mughal, Olivier R Busch, Oskar Franklin, Patrik Larsson, Ulla Klaiber, Richard D Schulick, Rutger T Theijse, Oliver Strobel, Poya Ghorbani, Roeland F de Wilde, Salvador Rodriguez Franco, Toshitaka Sugawara, Johanna W Wilmink, Wells Messersmith, Marc G Besselink, Christopher L Wolfgang, Yosuke Inoue, Mark J Truty, Marco Del Chiaro

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe increased use of superior mesenteric artery (SMA) divestment has been a driver behind the increased resection rate for locally advanced pancreatic cancer following preoperative therapy. This has also resulted in an increase in the rate of postoperative neurogenic diarrhoea, but knowledge about postoperative neurogenic diarrhoea is lacking. The aims of this study were to determine the incidence, risk factors, management, and outcomes of neurogenic diarrhoea after SMA divestment/resection for pancreatic adenocarcinoma.MethodsThis multicentre retrospective study included consecutive patients undergoing pancreatic adenocarcinoma resection, including SMA divestment/resection, after preoperative chemotherapy with or without radiotherapy between 2016 and 2022. Primary endpoints were the incidence of postoperative neurogenic diarrhoea, associated factors, treatment strategies, and outcomes. Secondary endpoints were the rate of adjuvant chemotherapy and the impact of neurogenic diarrhoea on overall survival (OS).ResultsOverall, 291 patients who underwent pancreatic resection with concomitant SMA divestment (240, 82%) or resection (51, 18%) were included in the study. Postoperative neurogenic diarrhoea was diagnosed in 193 patients. The adjusted risk of developing neurogenic diarrhoea was 59% (95% confidence interval (c.i.) 46 to 73) after ≤ 180° SMA divestment, 80% (95% c.i. 67 to 93) after > 180° SMA divestment, and 77% (95% c.i. 66 to 88) after SMA resection. Postoperative neurogenic diarrhoea was treated with non-opioid drugs (29, 15%), non-opium opioids (140, 73%), opium tincture (69, 36%), and/or octreotide (49, 25%), and normalized in 51% of patients. After adjusting for confounders, neurogenic diarrhoea was not significantly associated with OS (hazard ratio 0.83; 95% c.i. 0.55 to 1.25).ConclusionNeurogenic diarrhoea occurred in two-thirds of patients following resection of pancreatic adenocarcinoma with concomitant SMA divestment/resection, particularly after > 180° SMA divestment or SMA resection. Although challenging to manage, neurogenic diarrhoea is not associated with impaired OS. Evidence-based treatment strategies are needed for adequate management.

Introduction

The introduction of (m)FOLFIRINOX (combination of 5-fluorouracil with leucovorin, irinotecan, and oxaliplatin, eventually modified in dosing) and gemcitabine–nab-paclitaxel has changed the perspective on patients with localized pancreatic adenocarcinoma with major vessel involvement including the superior mesenteric artery (SMA)1,2. Consequently, 61% of patients with borderline resectable pancreatic cancer and 23% of patients with locally advanced pancreatic cancer undergo resection after either neoadjuvant or induction therapy3, potentially contributing to better overall survival (OS)4,5. Depending on signs of ingrowth into the SMA, the extent of circumferential involvement, and surgeon preference, an SMA divestment or resection can be performed when aiming for a radical resection6. Because of the high morbidity and mortality associated with SMA resection7–10, divestment of periarterial tissue from the SMA is increasingly being used11–16. In this procedure, the tumour mass, including the perineural sheet, is peeled off the artery. With both approaches (SMA divestment and resection), patients are at risk of developing neurogenic diarrhoea.

The pathophysiology of postoperative neurogenic diarrhoea concerns the (partial) removal of the nerve plexus around the SMA, increasing the parasympathetic tone of the bowel and leading to increased peristalsis17,18. Evidence regarding the incidence of, risk factors for, and the management and outcomes of neurogenic diarrhoea is scarce11–13,19–21. Considering the clinical relevance of neurogenic diarrhoea after SMA divestment on postoperative nutritional status, functional recovery, quality of life, and the chance to start with adjuvant chemotherapy, more insight is needed into its incidence, risk factors, management, and outcomes to counsel patients and for decision-making. The aim of this international multicentre study was to investigate the incidence, risk factors, management, and outcomes of postoperative neurogenic diarrhoea after either SMA divestment or resection in patients with pancreatic adenocarcinoma.

Methods

This retrospective international cohort study is reported according to the STROBE guidelines22. This study was approved by the Colorado Multiple Institutional Review Board (COMIRB; #23-2376) from the University of Colorado and the Ethics Committee of the Amsterdam UMC (#2023.0660). The requirement for informed consent was waived because of the retrospective nature of the study.

Study design and patients

All consecutive adult (age ≥ 18 years) patients who underwent a pancreatic resection with concomitant SMA divestment or resection following preoperative chemotherapy (with or without radiotherapy) for pancreatic adenocarcinoma with any SMA involvement on cross-sectional imaging at diagnosis (2016–2022) were included in the study.

The primary study endpoints were the incidence of postoperative neurogenic diarrhoea, factors associated with neurogenic diarrhoea, treatment strategies to manage neurogenic diarrhoea, and treatment outcome (that is, normalization of neurogenic diarrhoea). Secondary endpoints were the proportion of patients that started with adjuvant chemotherapy and the impact of neurogenic diarrhoea on OS.

Definitions

Radiological response following preoperative chemotherapy was defined using Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumours (RECIST)23. Serum levels of the tumour marker carbohydrate antigen 19-9 (CA19-9) were determined before and after preoperative chemotherapy (with or without radiotherapy). Serum CA19-9 levels ≥ 37 U/ml were considered to be elevated. Patients’ performance status (PS) at time of surgery was assessed according to the Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) criteria24.

Extended resections were defined in accordance with the International Study Group of Pancreatic Surgery criteria25,26. Arterial divestment was defined as either periadventitial or subadventitial. In-hospital or 30-day major morbidity was defined as a Clavien–Dindo grade ≥IIIa complication27, and clinically relevant pancreatic surgery-specific complications (that is, delayed gastric emptying, postpancreatectomy haemorrhage, postoperative pancreatic fistula, chyle leak, and bile leak) were dichotomized into none/grade A versus grade B/C28–32. Organ failure was defined as a > 24-hour requirement for mechanic respiratory support, dialysis, and/or inotropic agents. In addition to in-hospital/30-day major morbidity, the 90-day mortality rates were determined.

The 8th edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer staging manual33 was used determine tumour node metastasis (TNM) categories. Microscopic radicality was classified following either the 0-mm rule (R0, ≥ 0-mm margin clearance; R1, direct margin involvement), 1-mm rule (R0, ≥ 1-mm margin clearance; R1, < 1-mm margin clearance), or the variant 1-mm rule (R0, ≥ 1-mm margin clearance; R1, < 1-mm margin clearance or direct margin involvement)34, according to a specific centre’s rules.

Postoperative neurogenic diarrhoea was defined as watery defaecation (> 5 times/day) without evidence of bacterial enteritis and if diarrhoea occurred immediately after a meal, despite pancreatic enzyme replacement therapy19. Because of the retrospective design of this study, the frequency of more than five watery stools per day was not required for the diagnosis of neurogenic diarrhoea: the persistence of diarrhoea after exclusion of other causes and optimal pancreatic enzyme administration was considered sufficient for diagnosis. Normalization of neurogenic diarrhoea was defined as a reduction in frequency to < 5 times/day or, if the frequency was not reported, a statement in the reports that the diarrhoea normalized. Information was retrieved from electronic patient records.

In the event of cancer recurrence, the first location of recurrence was registered (that is, locoregional, distant, or both). Locoregional disease recurrence was defined as suspected recurrence at the operation site and/or locoregional lymphadenopathy. OS was measured from time of surgery, because the findings of this study should facilitate clinical decision-making at time of surgery.

Statistical analysis

Data analyses were performed by a statistician (M.A.) using R version 4.3.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) and Stata® version 17 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA). Statistical significance was set at two-tailed P < 0.050. Logistic regression analysis was performed to investigate factors predictive of postoperative neurogenic diarrhoea. Cox regression analysis was used to investigate the difference in OS between patients with and without neurogenic diarrhoea, adjusted for prespecified confounders and predictive factors. For more details about the statistical analyses35–40, see supplementary methods.

Results

Overall, 291 patients who underwent resection for a pancreatic adenocarcinoma with concomitant SMA divestment (240, 82%) or SMA resection (51, 18%) after preoperative chemotherapy with or without radiotherapy were included in the study. Patients were recruited from 10 centres across different countries: three centres in the USA (143 patients), three centres in Sweden (31 patients), two centres in the Netherlands (42 patients), one centre in Japan (57 patients), and one centre in Austria (18 patients). Postoperative neurogenic diarrhoea was diagnosed in 193 patients (64%).

Baseline characteristics

The median age of patients was 64 years (interquartile range (i.q.r.) 57–71) years, with most patients (188, 65%) having an ECOG PS at of 0. Before the start of preoperative chemotherapy, SMA involvement > 180° was seen in 98 patients (34%), with a median tumour size of 33 (i.q.r. 27–41) mm on cross-sectional imaging. The most common preoperative chemotherapy regimen was (m)FOLFIRINOX (209, 72%), followed by gemcitabine–nab-paclitaxel (73, 25%), with a median treatment duration of 4 (i.q.r. 3–6) months. In all, 102 patients (35%) received radiotherapy before surgery (Table 1).

Surgical procedures and outcome

Pancreatoduodenectomy was the most commonly performed pancreatic resection (172, 59%), followed by left pancreatectomy (63, 22%) and total pancreatectomy (56, 19%). Portomesenteric venous resection was performed in 184 patients (63%), with the majority of procedures being segmental resections. Extended resections other than from the SMA and portomesenteric vein were performed in 239 patients (82%), including resection of the adrenal gland (68, 23%), coeliac axis (59, 20%), and hepatic artery (22, 8%).

The unadjusted risk of developing neurogenic diarrhoea was higher after SMA resection than SMA divestment (42 (82%) versus 151 (63%) patients, respectively; P = 0.008). Among the 240 patients who underwent divestment of the SMA, the extent of divestment was described for 170 (71%). The unadjusted risk of neurogenic diarrhoea did not differ significantly after SMA divestment > 180° versus ≤ 180° (51 (76%) versus 66 (64%) patients, respectively; P = 0.098). In-hospital/30-day major morbidity occurred in 118 patients (41%), including in 73% (37) and 34% (81) of patients after SMA resection and divestment, respectively (P < 0.001). The rates of major morbidity after ≤ 180° versus > 180° SMA divestment were similar (37 (36%) versus 25 (37%) patients, respectively; P = 0.854). Postpancreatectomy haemorrhage grade B/C occurred in 25% (13) and 9% (21) of patients after SMA resection and divestment, respectively (P < 0.001). Overall, the 90-day mortality rate was 3%, and was similar between the SMA resection and divestment groups (2 (4%) versus 7 (3%), respectively; P = 0.707; Table 2). Postoperative neurogenic diarrhoea

Compared with ≤ 180° SMA divestment, SMA divestment > 180° was associated with a higher risk of postoperative neurogenic diarrhoea (odds ratio (OR) 3.09; 95% confidence interval (c.i.) 1.33 to 7.18), whereas the association of SMA resection with neurogenic diarrhoea was not significant (OR 2.20; 95% c.i. 0.68 to 7.10; Table 3). The unadjusted risk of developing postoperative neurogenic diarrhoea was 64% (95% c.i. 54 to 73) after ≤ 180° SMA divestment, 75% (95% c.i. 65 to 85) after > 180° SMA divestment, and 82% (95% c.i. 70 to 90) after SMA resection. The adjusted risk of developing postoperative neurogenic diarrhoea was 59% (95% c.i. 46 to 73) after ≤ 180° SMA divestment, 80% (95% c.i. 67 to 93) after > 180° SMA divestment, and 77% (95% c.i. 66 to 88) after SMA resection. Other factors associated with an increased risk of developing postoperative neurogenic diarrhoea were a higher body mass index and longer preoperative chemotherapy treatment. Preoperative stereotactic body radiotherapy was associated with a lower likelihood of developing postoperative neurogenic diarrhoea compared with no preoperative radiation (OR 0.23; 95% c.i. 0.09 to 0.56; Fig. 1).

Postoperative neurogenic diarrhoea was managed with non-opioid drugs (29, 15%), non-opium opioid drugs (for example, loperamide or codeine; 140, 73%), opium tincture (69, 36%), and/or octreotide in (49, 25%; Fig. 2). Overall, 61 patients with neurogenic diarrhoea needed enteral and/or parenteral feeding support at any time because of intestinal loss caused by neurogenic diarrhoea (data missing for 7). The median follow-up in patients with neurogenic diarrhoea was 20 (i.q.r. 12–34) months. Neurogenic diarrhoea normalized in at least 51% of patients (95; data missing for 5) after a median of 22 (i.q.r. 17–70) days after surgery (data missing for 27). Normalization after antidiarrhoeal treatment occurred in 12% of patients (5 of 41) after SMA resection, 59% of patients (29 of 49) after > 180° SMA divestment, and in 63% of patients (40 of 64) after ≤ 180° SMA divestment (P < 0.001; Figs S1 and S2). When comparing treatment patterns between patients without and with normalization of neurogenic diarrhoea, possible differences were noted in the use of bulking agents (6 (7%) versus 23 (25%) patients, respectively; P < 0.001) and opium tincture (21 (24%) versus 47 (51%) patients, respectively; P < 0.001).

Oncological outcome

After excluding patients who died within 90 days after the index surgery, 133 patients (47%) received adjuvant chemotherapy (43% and 55% of patients with and without postoperative neurogenic diarrhoea, respectively; P = 0.053). The most common adjuvant chemotherapy regimen was (m)FOLFIRINOX (32%). Among patients treated with adjuvant chemotherapy, the use of adjuvant (m)FOLFIRINOX did not differ significantly between patients with and without postoperative neurogenic diarrhoea (26 versus 43%, respectively; P = 0.056). Details regarding adjuvant chemotherapy regimens are provided in Table S2.

During a median follow-up of 40 (i.q.r. 25–67) months, 168 patients (58%) died. The risk of cancer recurrence at 1, 3, and 5 years was 41% (95% c.i. 36 to 48), 68% (95% c.i. 63 to 74), and 72% (95% c.i. 66 to 78), respectively. Locations of cancer recurrence are provided in Table S2. The median OS was 28 (i.q.r. 25–34) months, with 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS rates of 79% (95% c.i. 74 to 84), 41% (95% c.i. 35 to 48), and 28% (95% c.i. 22 to 36), respectively (Fig. S3a). After adjustment for confounders, postoperative neurogenic diarrhoea was not significantly associated with OS (hazard ratio 0.83; 95% c.i. 0.55 to 1.25). Results of Cox regression analysis are provided in Table 4 and Fig. S3b shows adjusted OS curves.

Discussion

In this international multicentre study, the rate of postoperative neurogenic diarrhoea among 291 patients who underwent SMA divestment or SMA resection during resection of advanced pancreatic adenocarcinoma was 66%. The adjusted risk of developing neurogenic diarrhoea was 59% (95% c.i. 46–73) after ≤ 180° SMA divestment, 80% (95% c.i. 67–93) after > 180° SMA divestment, and 77% (95% c.i. 66–88) after SMA resection. The management neurogenic diarrhoea was diverse and included non-opioid drugs, non-opium opioids, opium tincture, and/or octreotide. The rate of adjuvant chemotherapy was lower among patients with than without postoperative neurogenic diarrhoea (43 versus 55%; P = 0.053), although the difference was not statistically significant and this lower proportion of patients started with adjuvant chemotherapy in case of postoperative neurogenic diarrhoea was not associated with impaired OS. Postoperative neurogenic diarrhoea normalized in half the patients.

An observational single-centre study19 reported that the incidence of postoperative neurogenic diarrhoea was 58% among 117 patients with pancreatic adenocarcinoma who underwent SMA divestment during pancreatic surgery; the lower incidence in that study than in the present study (66%) could be explained by the inclusion only of patients with (borderline) resectable pancreatic cancer, probably requiring lesser SMA divestment, in the previous study. In the present study, > 180° SMA divestment was associated with an increased risk of neurogenic diarrhoea compared with ≤ 180° SMA divestment. Unlike ≤ 180° SMA divestment, no association was seen between neurogenic diarrhoea and SMA resection, which is likely explained by the limited sample size of the SMA resection group, underlined by the broad 95% c.i. for the hazard ratio. After SMA resection, only 12% of patients experienced normalization of diarrhoea over time. In contrast, neurogenic diarrhoea normalized more often in patients who underwent SMA divestment, ranging from 59% after > 180° SMA divestment to 63% after ≤ 180° SMA divestment. The seemingly limited difference in normalization rates between SMA divestment ≤ 180° and > 180° suggests a capacity for recovery even when no or only limited innervation via the SMA nerve plexus of the bowel is left.

Other factors that were independently associated with postoperative neurogenic diarrhoea were a lower body mass index before the start of preoperative chemotherapy and a longer duration of preoperative chemotherapy, probably due to impaired nutritional status and chemotherapy-induced diarrhoea in the preoperative setting41. It is likely that the impact of these factors is limited, considering the regeneration of the intestinal mucosa after completion of preoperative chemotherapy and the fact that generally only fit patients are selected for advanced pancreatic cancer surgery2.

Preoperative treatment with stereotactic body radiotherapy was associated with a lower likelihood of developing postoperative neurogenic diarrhoea than both conventional external beam radiotherapy and no radiotherapy, probably because the high target dose of radiation reduces tumour invasion of the nerve plexus around the SMA42.

There was a tendency for patients with postoperative neurogenic diarrhoea to be less likely to start adjuvant chemotherapy than patients without neurogenic diarrhoea (43 versus 55%; P = 0.053); this is likely explained by impaired nutritional status and a delay in functional recovery. Another factor contributing to the lower likelihood of starting adjuvant chemotherapy is that patients with postoperative neurogenic diarrhoea received more cycles of preoperative chemotherapy than those without diarrhoea.43

The striking result that neurogenic diarrhoea normalized in only half the patients should be interpreted with caution. Due to retrospective data collection and the fact the patients in this study were operated on in tertiary referral centres, the evaluation of neurogenic diarrhoea and treatment effect was difficult to assess. The 51% normalization rate of neurogenic diarrhoea during the survival-based follow-up of 42 months is prone to underestimation due to the lack of time-dependent analysis. The large variability in treatment patterns highlights the limited evidence regarding different therapeutic options, as is known from the management of high-output enterostomies44,45. Patients without normalization of neurogenic diarrhoea seemed to be treated less frequently with bulking agents (7 versus 25%) and opium tincture (24 versus 51%). This suggests room for improvement, because Inoue and et al.21 demonstrated that intractable neurogenic diarrhoea after > 180° SMA divestment occurred only in 7% of patients, taking into account that treatment with opioids was more often required to control the diarrhoea, than in patients with less extensive SMA divestment.

Optimization of the management of neurogenic diarrhoea is essential to reduce deteriorations in nutritional status and subsequent postoperative functional recovery, as well as impairments in quality of life46,47. Until more level 1 evidence is available, a standardized work-up was recently proposed within the PREOPANC-4 implementation programme (see Fig. 3)48,49. In addition to the use of a structured therapeutic step-up approach, early awareness is key to diagnosing neurogenic diarrhoea in a timely manner, starting with the first therapeutic intervention, with eventual optimization of pancreatic enzyme supplementation and rapid dose escalation strategies. Adequate documentation about the presence, severity, and treatment response of postoperative neurogenic diarrhoea is key.

This study has several limitations that should be kept in mind when interpreting the study findings. It was difficult to differentiate between neurogenic diarrhoea and other (contributing) causes of diarrhoea, such as exocrine insufficiency, dumping syndrome, and bacterial overgrowth. In addition, the retrospective nature of data collection may have resulted in an underestimation of the incidence of neurogenic diarrhoea. The precise frequency of diarrhoea (over time) was not always reported. A pragmatic approach was used in this study, allowing the diagnosis of neurogenic diarrhoea without having the guarantee that the daily frequency was more than five times, and this could have influenced the rate of normalization found in this study. It was not feasible to reliably collect information regarding the timing of starting a specific antidiarrhoeal drug. Thus, it was not feasible to perform a regression analysis on factors associated with normalization of neurogenic diarrhoea. In addition, information regarding the side of SMA divestment (that is, left or right) was not available in patients who underwent a divestment of ≤ 180°, and this may be relevant to the likelihood of developing neurogenic diarrhoea. Stratifying the extent of SMA divestment into quartiles was not feasible due to the retrospective study design and sample sizes. Cox regression analysis on the association of neurogenic diarrhoea with OS was limited by the variability in definitions of radical resection among centres.

In conclusion, this study provides urgently needed evidence about a complication that is becoming more common with the increase in advanced pancreatic cancer surgery. In this study, neurogenic diarrhoea occurred in two-thirds of patients undergoing pancreatic surgery with concomitant SMA divestment or resection during advanced pancreatic cancer surgery following preoperative chemotherapy, particularly after > 180° SMA divestment and SMA resection. Although challenging to manage, postoperative neurogenic diarrhoea is not associated with impaired OS. Evidence-based treatment strategies are needed for adequate management of neurogenic diarrhoea. Prospective studies are needed to provide a clear definition of the condition and to analyse possible risk factors.
