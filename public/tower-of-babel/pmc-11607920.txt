Tracheal stenosis misdiagnosed as asthma: a case report
Authors: Naveed Ur Rehman Siddiqui, Ayaz Ur Rehman, Areeba Sultan, Awais Abbas, Moghira Iqbal Uddin Siddiqui, Qalab Abbas
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Tracheal stenosis has various etiologies, including trauma from surgery, intubation, accidents, inhalation injuries, or inflammatory diseases such as Wegener’s granulomatosis, sarcoidosis, or systemic lupus erythematosus [1]. However, the vast majority of cases result from prolonged intubation or tracheostomy [2]. Even when high-volume, low-pressure cuffed tubes are used, airway stenosis can occur in up to 11% of intubated or tracheostomy patients, even after less than 24 h of intubation [3]. When tracheal stenosis occurs, it is often difficult to diagnose and manage. The common presentation of tracheal stenosis includes general respiratory symptoms of wheezing, dyspnea, exercise intolerance, stridor, or cough. In children, an otherwise mild respiratory infection can lead to increased work of breathing. If a dyspneic patient has a history of intubation or tracheostomy, tracheal stenosis should be considered [4]. These symptoms may not initially suggest an airway stenosis and may be misdiagnosed as asthma, a far more common condition, especially in pediatric population. By the time the child becomes symptomatic, the tracheal stenosis usually progresses to involve 30–50% of the lumen diameter.

Case presentation

We present the case of a 10-year-old Asian boy, weight 41 kg, with known history of adrenal insufficiency but no prior documented history of asthma, who presented in the emergency department with complaints of cough, wheezing, and increased work of breathing for 1 day. Ten months prior, he had been admitted with adrenal crisis and shock, requiring intubation for 3 days. He was successfully extubated without complications and discharged home. However, he had multiple visits to the emergency department in the months following his intubation, presenting with cough, wheezing, and increased work of breathing. He was treated presumptively as a case of acute severe asthma due to bilateral expiratory wheezing and respiratory distress. No other respiratory diagnoses were previously documented, and he showed partial but temporary responses to asthma treatments during these visits.

During this visit, he presented with similar symptoms, including increased work of breathing requiring pediatric intensive care unit (PICU) admission, as his condition did not respond to initial management. On examination, he was tachypneic with harsh vesicular breathing and bilateral wheeze on auscultation. Laboratory testing revealed leukocytosis with a white blood cell count of 14.4k/L and a left shift. The differential showed 89% neutrophils and 6.3% lymphocytes. Hemoglobin was 11.3 g/dL, platelets were 327k/L, and arterial blood gas analysis showed pH of 7.42, partial pressure of carbon dioxide of 32.9 mmHg, partial pressure of oxygen of 75 mmHg, bicarbonate of 20.90 meq/L, and base excess of −2.8 meq/L. Although the patient’s chest X-ray did not show typical signs of hyperinflation, asthma was presumed based on the clinical presentation of bilateral wheezing, increased work of breathing, and a history of multiple similar episodes that responded to bronchodilator therapy.

He was managed for acute severe asthma with inhaled beta agonists and anticholinergic, systemic steroids, and magnesium sulfate followed by terbutaline infusion, along with trial of noninvasive bilevel positive pressure ventilation through face mask. However, his symptoms remained refractory, and he progressed to hypoxic respiratory failure requiring intubation. Rapid sequence intubation was attempted using a video laryngoscope, but we were unable to pass the endotracheal tube beyond the vocal cords. Initially, an age-appropriate uncuffed endotracheal tube was used, followed by multiple attempts with smaller sized tubes, all of which were unsuccessful. A size 2.5 laryngeal mask airway was inserted, and bag and mask ventilation was provided. The child was rushed to the operating room (OR), where an emergent tracheostomy was done, and through this, we were able to manage airway adequately.

Subsequent bedside bronchoscopy revealed grade III tracheal stenosis (Fig. 1). He was weaned off mechanical ventilation successfully, with no further asthma treatment, and the tracheostomy remained in place. The patient was later discharged with a follow-up plan for open resection and anastomosis or laser treatment 3–4 weeks post discharge. Carbon dioxide laser excision of subglottic stenosis was performed after 3 weeks, and the patient was successfully decannulated from the tracheostomy tube. His postprocedure course remained uneventful, and he was discharged on oral medications.

Discussion

Acquired tracheal stenosis is frequently misdiagnosed as an acute exacerbation of asthma in the clinical setting [5]. This case stands out owing to the combination of severe tracheal stenosis (grade III) and the recurrent misdiagnosis of asthma over an extended period, even in the absence of typical signs such as hyperinflation on chest X-ray. The patient had multiple emergency visits before the correct diagnosis was made, underscoring how tracheal stenosis can persist undiagnosed in children, especially when there is a history of past intubation and no prior asthma diagnosis. The delay in diagnosis in this case, despite persistent symptoms, emphasizes the importance of considering alternative diagnoses early in the evaluation of children with refractory wheezing, particularly when the clinical response to asthma therapy is inadequate.

While the subglottis is not typically the narrowest part of the airway in a 10-year-old, subglottic stenosis can still occur owing to factors such as prolonged intubation, which the patient experienced for 3 days. Injury can be caused by infection or mechanical trauma, usually from endotracheal intubation, but it can also result from blunt, penetrating, or other trauma. Factors implicated in the development of subglottic stenosis related to endotracheal intubation include the size of the endotracheal tube relative to the child’s larynx, the duration of intubation, motion of the endotracheal tube while the child is intubated, and multiple extubation failures requiring reintubations. Sex hormones have been implicated as a possible cause owing to the female preponderance afflicted with this condition, but several reports were not able to confirm the presence of estrogen receptors in biopsies [6].

A study by Schweiger et al. indicated that children who were intubated and inadequately sedated had a higher risk of developing subglottic stenosis. The study, which included children aged 30 days to 5 years who underwent endotracheal intubation, found that the incidence of undersedation was significantly higher in those who developed subglottic stenosis compared with those who did not (15.8% versus 3.65%) [7].

Tracheal stenosis grading is based on the percentage of airway narrowing. Grade I involves less than 50% narrowing, grade II is 51–70% narrowing, grade III represents 71–99% narrowing, and grade IV refers to complete obstruction of the airway. In our case, the patient’s grade III stenosis indicates severe narrowing of 71–99%, which significantly impacted airway management and required urgent tracheostomy.

Flow-volume loops (FVL) remain the most effective method for detecting upper airway obstruction (UAO), even before symptoms develops [8]. A flow-volume loop could have been beneficial in differentiating between intrathoracic obstruction, such as asthma, and extrathoracic obstruction, such as subglottic stenosis, particularly while the patient was on noninvasive bilevel positive airway pressure (NIV BiPAP) support.

In this case, the decision to perform a tracheostomy was critical, as it effectively bypassed the subglottic stenosis and secured the airway. However, if the stenosis had been located distal to the subglottis, a tracheostomy might not have adequately addressed the airway obstruction. Bronchoscopy was performed to assess the extent of the tracheal stenosis following the patient’s emergency tracheostomy. This procedure allowed for direct visualization of the stenosis, providing critical information about the location and severity of the airway blockage. If the stenosis had been too severe to evaluate via bronchoscopy, a computed tomography (CT) scan could have been used as an alternative to help identify the narrowing. A thorough endoscopic evaluation in the operating room is essential to guide further management and treatment planning [9].

Treatment via an endoscopic or open surgical approach is dependent on the patient’s symptoms, the site of involvement, and the degree of stenosis. The ideal treatment plan must be individualized and balanced between a more definitive but invasive surgical resection with end-to-end anastomosis or more conservative endoscopic treatments, which have a greater risk of recurrence [10]. Useful algorithms for treatment selection are now available, using features of the case including the length of the stenosis, lumen diameter, cartilage involvement, and prior treatment attempts.

Conclusion

Tracheal stenosis can be easily misdiagnosed as asthma, particularly for inpatients with history of intubation. When a patient presents with signs and symptoms of respiratory distress, tracheal stenosis should be considered in the differential diagnosis and the airway should be secured promptly in a secure environment by an anesthesia/otolaryngologist.
