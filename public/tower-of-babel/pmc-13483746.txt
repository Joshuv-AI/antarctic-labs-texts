Clinical efficacy and safety of interventional embolization combined with early rehabilitation in the management of post-traumatic shoulder periarthritis
Authors: Biaorui Su, Yajun Hong, Zhenyu Li, Shandan Ouyang, Cong Yu, Fangming Geng, Jia Niu, Jin Peng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatients with refractory post-traumatic shoulder periarthritis(PTSP) have markedly reduced quality of life owing to persistent pain and functional restrictions. Thus, exploring a safe, effective treatment regimen is essential.AimThis study compared the efficacy and safety of TAE plus early rehabilitation versus rehabilitation alone for conservatively refractory post-traumatic shoulder periarthritis.MethodsThis study enrolled patients with conservatively refractory post-traumatic shoulder periarthritis admitted between September 2023 and January 2025. Using 1:1 propensity score matching, 60 patients were selected and divided into two groups: the control group (n = 30) received 12 weeks of structured rehabilitation, while the study group (n = 30) received TAE and the same systematic rehabilitation training for 12 weeks from the second day after TAE. Primary outcomes included nocturnal pain (NRS) and Constant-Murley scores; secondary measures were active ROM (forward flexion, external rotation), EQ-5D index, periarticular effusion on MRI, and adverse events.ResultsAfter PSM, the baseline data of the two groups were balanced and comparable (P > 0.05). At all postoperative time points, the study group had notably greater improvements in all indicators than the control group (P < 0.05). At 6 months, its NRS score dropped to 1.8 ± 1.2, significantly lower than the control group’s 4.5 ± 1.8 (P < 0.001); Constant-Murley score rose to 86.3 ± 8.7, much higher than the control’s 69.8 ± 11.4 (P < 0.001). Forward flexion/external rotation ROM reached 148° ± 18°/75.0° ± 10.3°,significantly better than the control’s 132.5° ± 20.1°/62.0° ± 9.8° (P < 0.001); EQ-5D index hit 0.82 ± 0.12, higher than the control’s 0.65 ± 0.14 (P < 0.001).MRI showed 89.8% of the study group had effusion reduction/disappearance, versus 52.3% in the control (P < 0.001). No serious adverse events occurred.ConclusionIn this exploratory retrospective study, TAE plus early systematic rehabilitation was associated with greater pain relief, improved joint function, and better quality of life compared with rehabilitation alone. These findings are hypothesis-generating and require confirmation in prospective studies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12893-026-03875-3.Keywords: Post-traumatic shoulder periarthritis, Transcatheter arterial embolization, Rehabilitation treatment, Management of pain, Joint function

Introduction

The shoulder is the largest ball-and-socket joint in the human body and provides the greatest range of motion, enabling essential functions such as upper-limb weight-bearing, rotation, and precise movement. Therefore, the structural stability and functional integrity of the shoulder are critical for activities of daily living and work performance. However, post-traumatic shoulder periarthritis (PTSP) develops in approximately 23%–35% of patients following shoulder trauma, including impact injuries, falls, and sports-related injuries [1, 2]. PTSP is a refractory condition characterized by chronic inflammation around the joint capsule, synovial hyperplasia, abnormal angiogenesis, and fibrotic adhesion. Clinically, patients often present with persistent nocturnal pain (incidence up to 89%), restricted active range of motion (forward flexion reduced by 30%–50% compared with the healthy side), and diminished quality of life [3, 4]. With population aging and increasing sports injuries, the incidence of PTSP is rising, leading to substantial socioeconomic burden.

Currently, conservative management remains the first-line treatment for PTSP and mainly includes oral nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), physical therapy, and intra-articular glucocorticoid injections [5]. However, these modalities offer only short-term symptomatic relief and do not halt disease progression; long-term use is associated with gastrointestinal and cardiovascular risks, cartilage degeneration, and high recurrence rates [6–8]. For patients refractory to ≥ 3 months of conservative therapy, arthroscopic capsular release may be considered, but it is invasive, requires prolonged recovery, and carries risks of complications (e.g., rotator cuff tear, infection), particularly in elderly or comorbid patients [9–11].

Transcatheter arterial embolization (TAE) has emerged as a novel approach for chronic musculoskeletal pain. The proposed mechanism of TAE involves superselective catheterization of abnormal blood vessels supplying the inflamed area, followed by injection of embolic agents to reduce local blood flow. This is hypothesized to decrease the delivery of inflammatory mediators and provide analgesic effects, although the precise mechanisms remain under investigation [12]. A systematic review by Aly et al. [13] reported that 82% of patients with chronic shoulder or elbow pain achieved ≥ 50% pain reduction at 6 months after TAE, with a low complication rate. In patients with PTSP, TAE significantly reduced pain scores and improved range of motion. However, TAE alone does not address capsular fibrosis or adhesive contracture; without timely rehabilitation, 20%–25% of patients may experience recurrent loss of mobility within 3–6 months [14, 15].

Rehabilitation alone aims to restore range of motion gradually, but its effectiveness in PTSP is often limited by severe baseline pain [11]. have shown that up to 40%–50% of patients discontinue training due to pain exacerbation, and even with adherence, Constant-Murley scores at 6 months typically reach only 65–70, below the normal functional level (> 90). In addition, persistent abnormal neovascularization and ongoing inflammation may impair the resolution of joint effusion, with improvements of less than 50% [16, 17]. Thus, neither treatment alone may adequately address the combination of pain and functional limitation in refractory PTSP. There is an urgent need for a combined strategy that achieves both rapid inflammation control and efficient functional recovery.

This exploratory, retrospective propensity score-matched (PSM) cohort study examined the association of TAE followed by early systematic rehabilitation, compared with rehabilitation alone, with clinical outcomes in a cohort of patients diagnosed with conservatively refractory post-traumatic shoulder periarthritis. All enrolled patients had documented shoulder trauma, moderate-to-severe pain, marked range of motion limitation, and MRI evidence of periarticular effusion. Given the retrospective design and the lack of formal phenotypic subtyping, the primary aim was hypothesis generation rather than confirmatory inference.

Materials and methods

Study design

This was a retrospective cohort study. Patients with post-traumatic shoulder periarthritis who failed to respond to conservative treatment and received care at our facility between September 2023 and January 2025 were identified through the electronic medical record system. Patients were assigned to two groups based on their actual treatment: the control group received structured rehabilitation alone for 12 weeks, and the study group received TAE followed by the same 12-week rehabilitation program starting on the second day after TAE. A total of 120 patients met the initial inclusion criteria. After excluding 6 who refused to participate, 2 with incomplete follow-up data, 1 who underwent shoulder surgery during the study period, and 1 who received additional analgesic interventions outside the protocol, 110 patients remained in the initial cohort. To reduce selection bias and confounding, retrospective PSM was performed. A logistic regression model was constructed using age, gender, body mass index (BMI), type of trauma, duration of conservative treatment, and disease duration as matching variables. Patients were matched 1:1 using the nearest neighbor method with a caliper width of 0.25 standard deviations. After matching, 60 patients (30 per group) with balanced baseline characteristics were included in the final analysis. Importantly, the interventions had already been completed before the matching process; the PSM was applied only to the retrospectively collected data to create comparable groups for outcome comparison. Ethical statement

The study was approved by the hospital ethics committee. The principles of the Declaration of Helsinki (DH) were strictly followed, and all study procedures complied with international ethical standards. For all participants, informed consent was provided personally. clinical practice an emergency arise that prevented self-consent, authorization was instead granted by a family representative or guardian. All data involved in the study were anonymized to ensure the privacy and confidentiality of the participants.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria: ① Age of 18–70 years old; ② Clinical diagnosis of post-traumatic shoulder periarthritis based on: (a) ICD-10 code M75.0 (adhesive capsulitis of shoulder); (b) a documented history of ipsilateral shoulder trauma (fall, impact injury, or sports injury) preceding symptom onset; and (c) persistent shoulder pain and active range of motion limitation (forward flexion < 100° or external rotation < 30° compared with the unaffected side) for ≥ 3 months despite standard conservative treatment (including NSAIDs, physical therapy, and/or intra-articular injections) [18, 19]; ③ Moderate-to-severe shoulder pain (NRS ≥ 4); ④ MRI evidence of periarticular effusion and/or abnormal neovascularization as defined in the MRI assessment section. We emphasize that these MRI features are not diagnostic of any specific pathological entity; they were used only as exploratory surrogate markers to suggest possible active inflammatory changes, not as confirmation of a particular diagnosis.; ⑤ Agreement to receive the assigned treatment and complete ≥ 6 months of follow-up. No formal phenotypic subtyping (e.g., pain-dominant vs. stiffness-dominant) was performed due to the retrospective design.

Exclusion criteria: ① Combined with structural lesions such as rotator cuff tear, fracture, infection or tumor; ② With previous history of shoulder surgery; ③ Severe heart, liver, kidney dysfunction or coagulation dysfunction; ④ Allergy to contrast media or contraindication to TAE; ⑤ Systemic diseases such as nervous system diseases or rheumatoid arthritis affect shoulder joint function; ⑥ Pregnant or lactating women; ⑦ Lost to follow-up or with incomplete follow-up data.

Treatment methods

The treatment methods described below reflect the actual interventions received by patients based on clinical decision-making at the time of care. All data were collected retrospectively from medical records.

Early systematic rehabilitation training [21]

From the second day after TAE, an individualized rehabilitation program was designed by professional physiotherapists. The total treatment duration was 12 weeks and was divided into three stages with supervised training performed five times per week for 45–60 min per session. Patients were also instructed to complete a standardized home exercise program once daily for 30 min.

The first stage (1–4 weeks, pain relief and passive activity) : ① Shoulder cold compress (15 min each time, twice a day) to reduce postoperative inflammation; ② Passive joint movement training: The procedures included forward flexion of shoulder joint (with the assistance of therapist, slowly raise to the limit of pain tolerance, maintain 5 s, 10 times/group), external rotation (elbow flexion 90° close to the trunk, therapist assisted forearm external rotation to the maximum Angle, maintain 5 s, 10 times/group), pendulum exercise (leaning forward, the affected limb naturally drops, swing clockwise and counterclockwise 10 times/group, 2 groups); ③ Scapular stability training: Stand against the wall, bend both elbows 90° against the wall, and slowly move the elbows up and down (10 times/group, 2 groups).

The second stage (5–8 weeks, active activity and muscle strength strengthening period) : ①Active joint activity training: complete shoulder joint forward flexion independently (dumbbell: Hebei Kangda Fitness Equipment Co., LTD., size 0.5 kg/ each, 1 kg/ each, model KD-YL 001), slowly raise to 120°, 12 times/group, 2 groups; External rotation (elastic band: TheraBand Company, United States, specification medium resistance (Latex-Free), model 10453, length 1.5 m) fixed on the door handle, the affected limb external rotation against elastic band resistance, 12 times/group, 2 groups; ②Muscle strength training: rotator cuff muscle group training (side horizontal dumbbell lifting 0.5 kg for 3s, 10 times/group), deltoid muscle training (straight arm forward dumbbell lifting 0.5 kg, 10 times/group), rest for 1 min between each group; ③Joint mobilization: Maitland manipulation grade ⅰ-ⅱ was used to loosen the shoulder joint to improve the range of motion of the joint, 15 min each time.

The third stage (9–12 weeks, functional recovery and endurance training) : ① Functional training: such as combing hair, dressing, lifting and other daily life movement simulation training (20 min each time); ② Endurance training: brisk walking for 30 min (with the natural swing of the shoulder), or riding a bicycle for 20 min (adjusting the height of the handlebars to a comfortable position of the shoulder); ③ Stretching training: static stretching of shoulder muscles (such as stretching the anterior deltoid bundle with cross-hugging shoulders for 20 s, 3 times/side).

Rehabilitation adherence metrics: Adherence was monitored via attendance records for supervised sessions (5 times/week, total 60 sessions) and patient-completed daily logs for home exercises (once/day, total 84 days). The overall adherence rate was calculated as (number of completed sessions / prescribed sessions) × 100%. Patients with ≥ 80% adherence were classified as highly adherent. Control group (early systematic rehabilitation training only)

The rehabilitation training program was identical to that of the study group, consisting of 12 weeks of staged and systematic rehabilitation initiated from the second day after enrollment, with the same frequency, duration, and training content. No TAE was performed in this group. If patients experienced significant shoulder pain at enrollment, short-term oral celecoxib capsules were permitted (200 mg per dose, twice daily, for up to 7 days) to minimize discomfort, while avoiding prolonged pharmacological intervention that could affect outcome assessment.

Indicators of observation

Baseline data were collected prior to treatment, including demographic characteristics, clinical variables, pain and functional scores, joint range of motion, quality of life index, assessment of changes in effusion on MRI, and grading of joint capsular effusion.

Main outcome measures

NRS for nocturnal pain [22]: the score range was 0-10 points, with 0 as no pain and 10 as the most severe pain. The shoulder pain score of the patient in the resting state at night was recorded, and the lower the score, the more obvious the pain relief.

MRI assessment of abnormal neovascularization: MRI was performed on a 3.0T scanner (GE Signa HDxt) using T2-weighted fat-suppressed sequences and contrast-enhanced T1-weighted sequences. Contrast-enhanced images were acquired after intravenous administration of a gadolinium-based contrast agent (0.1 mmol/kg gadopentetate dimeglumine). Abnormal neovascularization was defined as the presence of contrast-enhancing tortuous vessels within or adjacent to the joint capsule, rotator interval, or peri-articular soft tissues. All images were independently assessed by two experienced musculoskeletal radiologists blinded to clinical data. Discrepancies were resolved by consensus. Inter-rater reliability was evaluated on a random subset of 20 patients (10 per group). Cohen’s kappa for effusion grade was 0.86 (95% CI: 0.79-0.93), and for abnormal neovascularization it was 0.91 (95% CI: 0.84-0.98). The intraclass correlation coefficient for continuous MRI measurements (e.g., effusion thickness) was 0.94 (95% CI: 0.89-0.97).

Sample size calculation

In this study, the 6-month nocturnal pain NRS score was used as the primary basis for sample size estimation. Based on the results of a multicenter, open-label feasibility trial conducted by Okuno et al. [20], patients with refractory nocturnal shoulder pain treated with TAE demonstrated a nocturnal pain NRS score of 1.6 ± 2.2 at 6 months post-procedure. Combined with the clinical practice of our team and previous literature reports [26], we estimated that the rehabilitation-only group would have a 6-month nocturnal pain NRS score of approximately 4.5 ± 1.8. Based on these parameters, the effect size (Cohen’s d) was calculated to be approximately 1.44. Sample size estimation was performed using G*Power 3.1 software, with α = 0.05 (two-sided) and statistical power (1–β) = 0.80. A two-sample independent t-test indicated that 24 patients per group were required. Considering a potential 10%–15% loss to follow-up in this retrospective study, the target sample size was increased to 30 patients per group, yielding a total of 60 cases, which corresponded to the actual number of patients included in this study and ensured adequate statistical power.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis performed using IBM SPSS25.0, and Lucidchart was used to to construct the study flow chart. Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test, homogeneity of variance was evaluated using Levene’s test. Normally distributed continuous data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (x̄ ± s) and compared using the independent samples t-test. Non-normally distributed data are summarized as median (interquartile range) [M(IQR)] and analyzed with the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables are expressed as frequency (percentage) [n (%)], with group comparisons made using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate, the latter being applied when expected cell frequencies were below 5. In order to reduce confounding bias, PSM was used to balance the baseline data between the groups. logistic regression was used to construct a propensity score model, which was matched 1:1 according to the nearest neighbor method, and the caliper value was 0.25. Baseline data after matching were compared using the corresponding methods described above. The results of NRS score, Constant-Murley shoulder function score, ROM of shoulder joint, EQ-5D life quality index and the change of fluid around joint capsule (MRI evaluation) were expressed by x̄±s. The incidence of adverse reactions was compared by χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test (when the expected frequency was less than 5). All statistical tests were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Comparison of baseline information

A total of 110 patients with PTSP who failed to respond to conservative treatment were included in this study. To reduce selection bias and confounding inherent to retrospective analyses, 1:1 propensity score matching PSM was performed based on age, gender, BMI, trauma type, duration of conservative treatment, and course of disease. Finally, 30 cases in the control group and 30 cases in the study group with balanced baseline characteristics were obtained, providing standardized research objects for the subsequent comparison of efficacy and safety. As shown in Table 1, before PSM, most of the indicators were not significantly different (P > 0.05), but some indicators (such as the EQ-5D index) had approached the significance level (P = 0.056), indicating some selection bias. After PSM, the standardized mean differences across the comparative groups were all less than 0.1, and the maximum standardized mean difference (SMD) was only 0.083 (well below the strict equilibrium criterion of 0.1 and the conventional acceptable threshold of 0.25). This result showed that PSM effectively eliminated the systematic differences between the two groups in demographic characteristics, clinical basic conditions, disease severity and imaging findings, and significantly reduced the selection bias and confounding bias inherent in retrospective studies, thus establishing good baseline comparability between the two groups.In the study group, TAE was technically successful in all 30 patients (100%). Mean fluoroscopy time was 24.5 ± 6.8 min, mean DAP was 32.1 ± 9.4 Gy·cm2, and mean contrast volume was 85.2 ± 12.6 mL. No procedure-related complications (e.g., vessel dissection, contrast extravasation) occurred.

Discussion

PTSP is a common refractory disease in orthopedics. Its core pathological features include chronic inflammation, fibrosis and abnormal vascular proliferation around the joint capsule after trauma, which often result in persistent pain and functional impairment [27]. By comparing the efficacy of TAE combined with early rehabilitation and rehabilitation alone, this study found that the combined treatment was associated with improvements in pain, function, range of motion, and quality of life, without increasing the risk of adverse events. Given the retrospective design and the lack of formal phenotyping, the findings should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating, not as support for a disease-modifying claim.

The term PTSP is recognized as a historical label that lacks specificity in contemporary shoulder taxonomy. It may encompass a heterogeneous group of conditions, including secondary frozen shoulder (adhesive capsulitis triggered by trauma), post-traumatic capsular contracture, and chronic pain syndromes with varying degrees of synovitis and fibrosis [28]. To provide a reproducible, albeit exploratory, case definition for this retrospective study, we applied the following operational criteria: (1) a documented history of ipsilateral shoulder trauma preceding symptom onset; (2) clinical features consistent with adhesive capsulitis, specifically active forward flexion < 100° or external rotation < 30° compared with the unaffected side, persisting for ≥ 3 months despite standard conservative treatment (NSAIDs, physical therapy, and/or intra-articular injections); and (3) MRI evidence of periarticular effusion and/or abnormal neovascularization. We emphasize that these MRI findings are not diagnostic of any specific pathological entity; they were used only as exploratory surrogate markers to suggest ongoing inflammatory activity, as described in previous studies [29].

In this study, TAE combined with a structured rehabilitation program demonstrated significant advantages across multiple clinical outcomes. The pain and functional impairment observed in post-traumatic shoulder periarthritis are generally associated with chronic inflammation of the periarticular soft tissues, abnormal angiogenesis, and inflammation-induced peripheral nerve sensitization. Although conventional rehabilitation therapy can improve local blood circulation and enhance joint range of motion through targeted exercise training, it has limited ability to halt the ongoing inflammatory process driving disease progression. TAE reduces blood flow to targeted areas by selectively embolizing abnormal neovessels. It is hypothesized that this may decrease local concentrations of inflammatory mediators, thereby contributing to pain relief, although direct evidence in human shoulder tissues is lacking [12]. This finding aligns with systematic reviews reporting significant pain reduction after TAE (e.g., Aly et al. [13] observed a decrease from 7.5 to 1.2 at 6 months; Digge et al. [30] summarized consistent evidence with a favorable safety profile). Nocturnal pain is one of the most distressing symptoms in patients with post-traumatic shoulder periarthritis and can severely impair sleep quality as well as hinder the rehabilitation process. The NRS score of the study group decreased to 3.2 ± 1.5 at 1 month after surgery, which was significantly lower than that of the control group (4.8 ± 1.7), and this advantage continued to expand over time, and the difference between the two groups was 2.7 points at 6 months after surgery(P < 0.001). This result is highly consistent with the trend of pain relief after TAE reported by Epelboym et al. [31]in their systematic review, who observed substantial pain reduction following TAE, supporting its potential as an analgesic intervention for chronic musculoskeletal conditions. The more rapid and complete pain reduction observed in the study group may reduce fear of pain and improve rehabilitation adherence. In terms of shoulder joint function recovery, the combined treatment showed more comprehensive advantages. As an internationally recognized gold-standard tool for assessing shoulder function, the Constant–Murley score provides a comprehensive evaluation across four domains: pain, activities of daily living, strength, and range of motion [23]. The score of the study group was 86.3 at 6 months after operation, compared with 69.8 in the control group, and closer to the normal shoulder function level (above 90), likely reflecting the combined effects of pain relief and structured rehabilitation. In particular, the forward flexion Angle of the shoulder joint in the study group was 148.0 ± 18.0°, which was close to the physiological range of 150° in normal adults. The external rotation Angle of the study group was 75.0 ± 10.3°, which was 21.0% higher than that of the control group.These results align with the findings reported by Okuno et al. [32]on interventional embolization for osteoarthritis, who found that the functional recovery of the joint was significantly accelerated after vascular embolization. Wang et al. [33]reported a 21% improvement in joint mobility with early rehabilitation. The field of shoulder surgery has paid increasing attention to perioperative rehabilitation. The latest expert consensus clearly states that “rehabilitation should be started as soon as possible under the premise of pain control for post-traumatic shoulder dysfunction”, and systematically puts forward the three-stage progressive rehabilitation principle of “passive movement → active movement → functional training“ [16], This was consistent with our staged protocol (1 to 4 weeks of passive mobilization and 5 to 8 weeks of strength training) initiated 2 days after surgery. The conclusion that “early painless activity can reduce the risk of joint adhesion by 40%” in the consensus provides theoretical support for the choice of rehabilitation time in this study. The significant improvement of quality of life represents the ultimate clinical value of the combined treatment strategy. The EQ-5D index is an internationally recognized health-related quality of life instrument, with scores ranging from 0 (death) to 1 (full health), and it provides a comprehensive assessment of patients’ physical function, psychological status, and social participation [34, 35]. The EQ-5D index of the study group was 0.82 ± 0.12 at 6 months after operation, compared with 0.65 ± 0.14 in the control group (P < 0.001). This difference likely reflects not only pain relief and functional gains but also improved psychological well-being. Six months after operation, MRI indicated that 89.8% of the patients had reduced or resolved effusion, This finding is consistent with the proposal by Wengert et al. [36]that “MRI effusion grading may reflect changes in inflammation”. At the same time, it was confirmed by quantitative analysis of MRI with Yoon et al. [37]that abnormal vascular proliferation around the shoulder joint capsule was positively correlated with the volume of effusion, and the disappearance rate of vascular signal was 91% at 8 weeks after embolization. These MRI findings (reduced effusion) are consistent with the hypothesis that TAE may be associated with decreased local inflammatory activity. However, effusion reduction is not a direct measure of synovial inflammation, and any causal link between TAE and alteration of the underlying inflammatory process remains unproven. At most, these observations suggest that TAE could create a more favorable local environment for rehabilitation, but this interpretation is speculative. By alleviating pain early, TAE may enable patients to engage more effectively in rehabilitation, potentially improving adherence and outcomes.

The safety of any novel therapeutic technique must be adequately demonstrated before it can be widely adopted. In this study, the safety profile of TAE combined with rehabilitation was comparable to that of rehabilitation alone, with a total adverse event rate of 10.0% in both groups, and no serious adverse events observed. In the study group, there were two cases of mild puncture-site hematoma and one case of transient shoulder numbness, all of which are recognized minor complications associated with interventional procedures. This result is consistent with Kishore et al. [12]who reported that the complications of TAE were mild, transient and of low incidence, mainly puncture site problems such as hematoma or transient shoulder numbness/pain. The muscle strain and joint swelling in the control group reflected the risk of training due to poor pain control during rehabilitation alone. From a risk–benefit perspective, TAE combined with a structured rehabilitation program demonstrated favorable outcomes in this cohort. However, given the retrospective design of the study, these findings should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of clinical efficacy. For patients with poor response to conservative treatment for more than 3 months, TAE combined with rehabilitation therapy may merit further evaluation in prospective clinical trials.

To our knowledge, this is the first PSM exploratory study comparing TAE plus early rehabilitation versus rehabilitation alone in refractory PTSP. However, given the observational design, these findings should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than conclusive. The observed effect sizes (Cohen’s d > 1.5 for pain and function at 6 months) are large, although causal inference is limited by the retrospective design. Regarding costs, adding TAE increases upfront procedural expenses (estimated ¥15,000–¥20,000 vs. ¥3,000–¥5,000 for rehabilitation alone). Whether this incremental cost is justified requires formal cost-effectiveness analysis in prospective trials. Nonetheless, for patients who have failed prolonged conservative treatment, potential savings from reduced long-term disability may offset initial expenses. Future randomized controlled trials with health economic evaluation are needed to confirm these findings.

Despite the positive results of this study, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, treatment allocation was not randomized and was substantially influenced by clinician preference, perceived severity, and patient expectations. Due to the retrospective design, there is an inherent risk of confounding by indication, expectation effects, and performance bias. Although propensity score matching was used to minimize measured confounding, unmeasured confounders (e.g., patient motivation, adherence to rehabilitation, clinician bias in treatment allocation) may still exist and could partially account for the observed differences between groups. Moreover, behavioral and contextual factors (e.g., daily activity levels, psychosocial support, expectation of benefit) were not captured in the retrospective records and may have contributed to the outcomes. The large effect sizes observed (Cohen’s d > 1.5 for multiple outcomes) are exceptional for musculoskeletal interventions and raise the possibility of detection bias or overestimation; thus, these findings should be interpreted with caution and require confirmation in adequately powered randomized controlled trials. Second, the use of MRI effusion grading as a surrogate marker for “inflammation control” lacks construct validity for post-traumatic shoulder conditions and has not been validated as a surrogate for inflammation control or disease activity. Effusion volume is non-specific, highly variable, and poorly correlated with pain or stiffness severity. In this study, the assessment was not blinded, and the grading criteria, while based on published methods [29], have not been formally validated for this condition. Moreover, the clinical relevance of a shift from “moderate” to “mild” effusion is unclear, and effusion reduction should not be equated with disease resolution. Future studies should incorporate validated imaging biomarkers (e.g., dynamic contrast-enhanced MRI or quantitative perfusion parameters) and blinded assessments to better capture inflammatory changes and their relationship to clinical outcomes. Third, the sample size was relatively small, and follow-up limited to 6 months, precluding conclusions about long-term efficacy and durability of the treatment effect. Fourth, the study population was exclusively Chinese, which may limit the generalizability of our findings to other ethnic or geographic populations. Fifth, we did not formally distinguish between potential phenotypic subtypes (e.g., pain-dominant vs. stiffness-dominant) due to the limitations of retrospective data. This lack of formal phenotyping is a limitation because the pathophysiological mechanisms—and therefore the potential response to TAE—could differ across subtypes. Future prospective studies should incorporate validated clinical phenotyping tools to enable subgroup analyses. Sixth, subgroup analyses were not performed to assess whether treatment effects differ across patient characteristics (e.g., age, trauma type, baseline severity), which may have provided insights to support more personalized treatment strategies. These limitations underscore the need for future multicenter randomized controlled trials with larger samples, longer follow-up, blinded outcome assessments, and standardized imaging protocols to validate the efficacy and safety of TAE combined with early rehabilitation in patients with post-traumatic shoulder periarthritis.

Conclusion

In this exploratory retrospective observational study of patients with conservatively refractory post-traumatic shoulder periarthritis, TAE combined with early systematic rehabilitation was associated with greater pain relief, better functional recovery, and improved quality of life compared with rehabilitation alone. These associations were observed without an increase in serious adverse events. However, owing to the absence of phenotypic stratification, and the potential for unmeasured confounding, the results should be regarded as hypothesis-generating only. They do not establish causality, nor do they support a disease-modifying role for TAE. Prospective randomized controlled trials with phenotypically characterized populations are required to determine whether TAE confers any true clinical benefit and, if so, which patient subgroups are most likely to respond.
