Insights into collapse in ambulances (CIA): a retrospective analysis of interfacility transfers from Emergency Medical Services (EMS) of Sindh, Pakistan
Authors: Kamran Idris, Zainab Mubeen, Zeeshan Noor Shaikh, Aswad Latif, Tehmina Fatima Warsi, Ahmad Khubaib, Tarique Quadir Lakhiar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEmergency Medical Services (EMS) is the cornerstone of healthcare service delivery, especially in lower and middle-income countries (LMICs). Given the scarcity of literature, the study determines the rate of patient collapse in ambulances (CIA), in association with history, complaints, and other contributing factors. CIA is a novel terminology coined by the study team, referring to out-of-hospital deaths in ambulances.MethodsThe study employed a retrospective approach from EMS records in Sindh to determine the patient collapse rate from July 2023 to February 2024. The dataset included interfacility transfers in which patients were transported between health facilities. Data collected via reports of ambulance response and transfer reports were extensively studied to determine the rate of CIA, response time, types of cases, types of transfers, and need for transfers, along with patient condition, diagnosis, and procedures conducted en route to transfer.ResultsThe findings reported a rate of CIA as 0.019% (n = 37) out of 195,638 cases, with the average response time for all the cases being less than 10 min, highlighting the consistent service delivery. In line with the presenting complaints, the majority of CIAs resulted from abnormal breathing (32.4%) and cardiac/respiratory arrest (32.4%). All the CIA cases were either serious or life-threatening, transferred to the need for a higher level of care. All the requests were made from the public sector secondary hospital to a similar setup or tertiary care, thereby highlighting shortcomings in the existing model of healthcare service delivery in Sindh.ConclusionTo prevent the collapse of serious and life-threatening patients in ambulances regardless of uncontrolled risks, it is important to obtain comparative data as well as measure the performance of EMS and ambulance crews for healthcare improvement. In addition, addressing the healthcare inequities through a systems approach is vital to improve patient outcomes at both the prehospital and hospital levels through the provision of essential care before patient transfer.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-026-14759-w.

Background

Health Systems is a collaboration of organizations, institutions, resources, and people intending to improve, restore, and maintain population health, through six building blocks known as service delivery, health workforce, information systems, medicines and technologies, financing, and leadership and governance [1, 2]. Within the building block of service delivery, the Emergency Medical Services (EMS) are the primary contact points for health-related emergencies and are globally acknowledged as a foundation of the healthcare system. The primary aim of an EMS is the delivery of coordinated, efficient, and immediate prehospital care/medical assistance in the event of acute or life-threatening events, to avert premature mortality and long-term morbidity [3]. The main components of an emergency service provider include access to emergency care, provision of care in the community, care en route to the hospital, and care upon arrival at the hospital [4, 5]. Efforts are in line to establish strengthened EMS and triage systems, as timely provision of emergency care can significantly reduce the burden of disease, particularly in LMICs [6–10].

In this context, the Government of Sindh enacted the Sindh Emergency Rescue Bill of 2023 to ensure readiness to respond promptly, providing rescue and emergency medical treatment to those affected, and counseling on measures to prevent or mitigate emergencies [11]. The operations were executed by a non-profit organization known as Sindh Integrated Emergency and Health Services (SIEHS) that operates through a public-private partnership [11, 12], recognized as the Accredited Centre of Excellence (ACE) by the International Academy of Emergency Medical Dispatch (IAED). The provincial EMS complies with the Medical Priority Dispatch System (MPDS) or ProQA software - a universal standard for emergency medical dispatchers (EMDs) taking calls for a broad range of field and triage responses developed by IAED [12, 13]. With advanced medical supplies, life-saving drugs, and qualified paramedics and nurses, SIEHS provides immediate pre-hospital emergency care and teleconsultation on a round-the-clock basis to those suffering from acute health trauma [12]. The organization operates on a three-tiered system, including Basic Life Support; Advanced Life Support (ALS) with Automated External Defibrillator (AED) machines and cardiac monitors; and Advanced Cardiac Life Support (ACLS) with cardiac monitors and ventilators [12].

Given the substantial impact of EMS on mortality rates and complications, assessing the performance of prehospital emergency services is therefore crucial for estimating the health situation in the region [4]. The existing body of literature lacks data on the incidence of mortalities en route to hospitals, creating a significant gap in the holistic understanding of critical EMS operations. This study aims to address this gap and contribute insights into the collapse in ambulances, patients’ medical conditions, and other contributing factors during transfer. Addressing this gap will help pinpoint areas for improvement at both the EMS and health system levels, design targeted interventions to prevent prehospital patient collapse, and optimize resource allocation and patient outcomes.

Methods

Operational definition

In our study, the term Collapse in Ambulance (CIA) is as an operational definition: a patient experiencing sudden clinical deterioration in physiological function that necessitates urgent medical intervention, with the patient not exhibiting signs of life during ambulance transfer. The term was devised for analytical purposes for the specific data and does not represent a standard clinical classification in the literature. These incidents are identified by the paramedical staff/Emergency Medical Technicians (EMTs) in ambulances. Following the collapse, patients are transferred to a nearby health facility for further confirmation of death by the physician.

Study objectives

The primary aim of this study is to determine the rate of CIA operated by the provincial entity of Sindh, Pakistan, from July 2023 to February 2024, in relation to the patient’s medical condition or other contributing factors during transfer.

Study design and setting

The study is conducted using a retrospective analysis, focusing on the frequency of CIA events in relation to the patient’s medical condition (ProQA codes assigned by EMDs and treatments administered during transfer to the health facility. The data sources included organizational records from all EMS stations located in Karachi, Sukkur, and Hyderabad, in which routine data is recorded using ISO-certified, validated reports. The dataset comprised of interfacility transfers with most patients being referred from health facilities.

Eligibility

We studied ambulance, critical transfer, and care transfer reports of all patients transferred between July 2023 to February 2024. Incomplete reports or data outside the study period were excluded from the study.

Data collection

The study investigated all the patient reports from EMS stations located in Karachi, Sukkur, and Hyderabad. This data provided comprehensive information on the initial emergency response, including responses from the EMDs, patient identification, history and assessment, Chest-Defibrillation-Epinephrine (CDE) procedures, physical assessment, treatments/medicines administered, and other elements related to communication with the hospital and the patient’s condition upon arrival at the hospital. Based on the information provided. ProQA codes were assigned by EMDs to reflect the initial patient condition prior to ambulance arrival while EMTs assessed the patients during transfer. This may lead to differ in clinical evaluation subject to the changes in patient status between the call and physical assessment.

Data analysis

The study employed the data obtained from routine reports filled out by EMDs during calls and after interventions while EMTs (who are present inside the ambulance) also fill out the ambulance form, which confirms CIA cases. We performed a descriptive analysis to determine the frequency of CIA cases in comparison to other interventions. Through screening of organizational records, CIA cases were filtered and exposed to further examination using MS Excel v.2021. This analysis encompassed factors such as patient age and gender, response time, transfer location, patient history, and comorbidities, presenting complaints, type of transfer, collapse site, ProQA code, CDE procedures, and, where available, feedback from patient attendants. With the help of the tracker (which is also inside the ambulance), location and time were also incorporated where applicable.

Results

We conducted a retrospective analysis using records from the EMS organization. During the study period, a total of 195,638 ambulance transfers requiring prehospital emergency care were made, of which 61% (n = 119,038) were serious, and 39% (n = 59,328) were life-threatening. In addition, 30% (n = 35610) of serious and 20% (n = 11764) of life-threatening cases were addressed with an average response time of less than 10 min. Conversely, the remaining 26% (n = 30459) of serious and 31% (n = 18433) of life-threatening cases received a response from EMS within 20 min. As shown in Fig. 1, the study reported 37 cases (0.019%) that resulted in ambulance collapses witnessed by EMTs from the stations of Karachi (n = 13, 35.1%) and Thatta (n = 10, 27%) during the specified duration. Patient demographics and complaints

The patient demographics of CIA cases are presented in Table 2, indicating a slight predominance of males (n = 20, 54%) over females (n = 17, 46%). The most commonly reported age groups were 0–15 years (n = 15, 40.5%) and above 60 years (n = 8, 21.6%). The patient history revealed prevailing comorbidities, most commonly cardiovascular disorders (n = 9, 24.3%), hypertension (n = 6, 16.2%), and diabetes mellitus (n = 4, 10.8%). Other miscellaneous conditions in the patient history were pneumonia and respiratory distress syndrome, common in newborns owing to their premature delivery and low birth weight. Other conditions included breathing difficulties/shortness of breath, diabetic foot ulcers, kidney and liver disorders, as well as self-inflicted injury or poison ingestion in some cases. Among the chief complaints documented by EMDs, conditions reported were shortness of breath (n = 22), respiratory distress syndrome (n = 4), road traffic accidents (n = 2), self-inflicted injuries (n = 2), myocardial infarction (n = 2), as well as cardiac arrest, diabetic foot, hypotension, pleural effusion, stroke, and febrile seizures. It is important to note that these conditions frequently overlapped, with some patients presenting with two or three of these complaints simultaneously.

EMS response

Table 3 further presents ambulance response, including both EMDs’ assignment of priority codes according to MPDS/ProQA and the subsequent response from EMS personnel within the EMS system. The primary causes of the CIA identified from the initial emergency call response were abnormal breathing and cardiac/respiratory arrest. At the same time, the most commonly assigned Pro-QA codes were 33-C-2 and 33-D-1, interpreted as Abnormal breathing (n = 12, 32.4%) and suspected cardiac and respiratory arrest (n = 12, 32.4%), respectively. Less common reasons included acute severe pain (33-C-5), not alert (6-D-2), significant hemorrhage or shock (33-C-3), changing color (6-D-3), and abdominal pain (6-D-4). The mean ambulance response time from the attendant call to the arrival of the ambulance and paramedical staff at the scene was 9.24 ± 10.54 min [95% confidence interval (CI) 6.0–12.8 min]. It is worth highlighting that nearly all the ambulance transfers were from hospital to hospital (n = 36, 97.3%), the reasons for ambulance transfers were predominantly due to the need for a higher level of care (n = 33, 89%), and ventilator support (n = 3, 8.1%), referred by a health facility. In addition, the majority of patients were transferred from secondary health facilities to either a similar level of care (n = 10, 27%) or tertiary-level facilities (n = 12, 32%), while some were directed to the private sector (n = 7, 19%). The details on transfer types and reasons are also presented in Fig. 2. During the transit, the patients received multiple interventions, including cardiopulmonary resuscitation (n = 29), Automated External Defibrillator (AED) for (n = 6) patients, and epinephrine doses were administered to (n = 27) patients. In cases where an AED was not attached, the prime reasons were refusals from attendants for female patients, orders of Do-Not-Resuscitation, and the age of children (0–10 years) [Supplementary Table 3]. The majority of CIAs (n = 29, 78.4%) occurred during transit within the ambulance. In contrast, the remaining (n = 8, 21.6%) were safely transferred to the destination health facility; however, they collapsed upon arrival at the Emergency Department.

Following the collapse of ambulances, the patient attendants were contacted at least three times for service-related feedback as part of routine practice, on different days and at different times, to ensure transparency. The majority of attendees rated their experience with the ambulance service, treatment, and EMT performance during transit as Satisfactory (54%). However, despite multiple attempts, patient attendants were unreachable, and contact could not be established in 46% of the cases. The key findings are also summarized in Fig. 3 – Patient Collapse in Ambulances.

Discussion

In Pakistan, EMS operate on the Anglo-American model, in which paramedics or EMTs provide pre-hospital care, and patients are transported to hospital emergency departments, with fewer interventions during transit [14]. Given the scarcity of literature on EMS at both the national and international levels [15], this study was conducted by the sole established organization delivering prehospital emergency care in Sindh via well-equipped ambulances to evaluate the overall collapse rate compared with routine interventions, in relation to associated patient conditions. Currently, there is no universally accepted term for patient collapse during ambulance transfers. The term “CIA” was used in this study as an operational descriptor to identify events in EMS data systematically. The approach creates a window for preliminary examination of the phenomenon, thereby highlighting the need to conduct further research to establish clinical standard definitions and reporting frameworks.

During the study period, approximately 195,638 patients were transported by ambulance, of whom more than 90% were high-priority transfers (serious or life-threatening cases), according to organizational data. Among these interventions, 37 cases (0.019%) resulted in a patient collapsing in the ambulance, as witnessed by EMTs. They were subsequently confirmed as deceased upon arrival at the destination or a nearby health facility by the on-duty physician. As the term “collapse in ambulance” is devised by the study researchers, it complies with the concept of ‘deaths occurring in an ambulance. However, both terms are subject to underreporting and lack extensive research, particularly since EMS organizations do not typically report deaths occurring during transit. Remarkably, a surveillance study conducted in different hospitals in Karachi reported a 4% death rate among patients who arrived at the hospital via ambulances, with the most common reason being chest pain and shortness of breath [10]. This observed rate significantly surpasses the one reported in our findings; there are multiple contextual variations. Similar challenges have been described in the literature, in which patient deterioration during ambulance transfer remains underreported; however, it is a significant concern in prehospital care. The study results may contribute to generating evidence on adverse events during ambulance transfers.

The ambulance transfers that resulted in CIA were mostly handled by EMS stations in Karachi and the Thatta region. The demographic data reveal that patients who collapsed in ambulances were predominantly male and mostly under 15 years old or above 60 years old. This pattern highlights the vulnerability of these age groups to complicated medical emergencies both before and during the transit. Among the patients who collapsed, the most common medical history was cardiovascular diseases, hypertension, and diabetes mellitus, further presenting with shortness of breath, respiratory distress syndrome, and pneumonia (associated with low birth weight and premature birth). Other non-communicable conditions, including stroke, road traffic accidents, thalassemia, chronic kidney and liver issues, and self-inflicted injuries, were also prevalent. During transit, the chief patient complaints were shortness of breath with chest pain, difficulty breathing, and gasping. The previous studies consistently report similar findings, indicating that individuals with cardiovascular and respiratory diseases are often subject to premature deaths in both pre-hospital care in ambulances and hospital settings [15]. Per the patient’s chief complaints highlighted in our study, the European Emergency Data Project also outlines critical emergencies where EMS can offer assistance, including cardiac arrest, respiratory difficulties, trauma, chest pain, and stroke [16].

Out of all the cases that resulted in collapse, the majority of them were categorized as serious (requiring rapid assessment and urgent on-scene intervention or transport), where patients exhibited symptoms such as altered consciousness, shortness of breath, hemorrhage, shock, acute onset of cardiac issues, severe pain, necessitating an emergency response. The remaining incidents were life-threatening cases (requiring immediate resuscitation or intervention) and were attributed to sudden cardiac or respiratory arrest. The study findings reported an average response time of less than 10 min, which complies with international standards. For instance, the NHS targets an average response time of 7 min for life-threatening calls and 15 min for serious calls [17]. while the NFPA sets a benchmark of 9 min for the arrival of an ALS unit at an emergency medical incident [18]. The difference between dispatch-level categorization (ProQA coding) and in-transit clinical assessment is noteworthy. While ProQA coding is based on the initial information received during the emergency call, EMT assessments reflect the actual state of the patient. Hence, there is a possibility that the state of the patients might have worsened or improved over the time gap between the emergency call and the arrival of the ambulance, especially in those cases where the response time was prolonged. However, the retrospective nature and the structure of the available information did not enable the tracking of such changes. In addition, almost all transfers were from a health facility to a hospital setting, primarily due to the need for a higher level of care and ventilator support unavailable at the patient pick-up location, predominantly from secondary health facilities to a similar level or to a tertiary care facility.

Such predominance is an important factor, as interfacility transfers usually involve clinically unstable patients with complex medical conditions or a deteriorating status before transfer. This may reflect health system challenges, such as a lack of availability of specialized or critical care, ultimately resulting in referrals and reliance on ambulance transfers. Subsequently, comparisons with other EMS systems should be interpreted cautiously due to variations in EMS structures, medical complexity, and reporting definitions.

As the transfer of critical patients via ambulances poses multiple risks, it is therefore important to emphasize discussing the background characteristics in service delivery. The trend in transfers reported in this study is attributed to compromised functionality of the public sector, ranging from primary to DHQ levels, as also highlighted in the Sindh Health Sector Strategy 2012–2020 [19]. The health system in Sindh operates through different tiers: BHUs, RHCs, THQs, and DHQs. However, the facility assessment conducted in 2015-16 revealed unsatisfactory performance outcomes, poor service utilization, patient dissatisfaction, a workforce shortage, inadequate infrastructure in operating theatres, emergency and inpatient areas, non-functional equipment, and a severe shortage of equipment and unavailability of essential medications [20]. These issues were further aggravated by the floods of 2022, during which 13% of the damage occurred to health facilities, as reported by the Post-Disaster Needs Assessment conducted by the Government of Pakistan and supported by various international organizations. The floods led to service disruptions across primary and secondary healthcare, with estimated losses of $109 million for the health sector. Adding to the challenges in service delivery is the scarcity of hospital beds in the province, with a ratio of 0.75 beds per 1000 population, far below the SDG standards of 3.4 beds per 1000 population [20].

The findings of the analysis of ambulance transfers highlight significant disparities in patient outcomes, with a notable trend of higher collapse rates in ambulances for both serious and life-threatening cases, suggesting deficiencies in patient stabilization and immediate care at public health facilities (frequently the patient pick-up point). Addressing service delivery issues is therefore imperative to enhance the overall quality and effectiveness of healthcare services at all levels, thereby ensuring better patient outcomes and experiences. Regarding treatment during transit, cardiopulmonary Resuscitation (CPR) was performed on 29 patients, an Automated External Defibrillator (AED) was used on 4 patients, and epinephrine was administered in 27 cases. These interventions may overlap, as some patients may have received a combination of them. As part of routine organizational activity, feedback from most attendees indicated satisfaction with the organizational services, while some were unreachable despite multiple attempts to contact them.

Given the core importance of pre-hospital care in the healthcare system, its effectiveness and outcomes depend on healthcare infrastructure and service delivery, high-quality care, the availability of supplies, and strong referral mechanisms to respond to emergencies before deterioration occurs. Further, a well-functioning emergency medical service, such as the study site, ensures timely interventions and plays a vital role in preventing adverse outcomes and improving overall patient well-being. Therefore, investing in and strengthening prehospital care services at the provincial level is essential for enhancing the resilience and responsiveness of the healthcare system as a whole. There is need for future research involving several EMS agencies in order to advance knowledge about EMS performance; further, using a continuum of care model that encompasses pre-transfer stabilization and in-transfer management would enable the examination of the influences on deterioration of patients.

Strengths

This study creates a foundation for research from the lens of EMS services in Pakistan, presenting the rate of CIA in contrast to routine ambulance transfers. The low rate of CIAs during the study period underscores consistent organizational performance in delivering prehospital emergency care. As a significant portion of ambulance transfer requests is aimed at addressing the need for a higher level of care, mostly from secondary health facilities, potential shortcomings in initial point-of-care and service delivery are discussed. The analysis delves deeply into routinely collected data, offering comprehensive findings.

Limitations

The study used retrospective organizational records from a single EMS organization; the findings have limited generalizability to other prehospital settings, populations, and standard clinical procedures. Further, reliance on data that is collected on a routine basis may introduce documentation limitations and potential selection bias. Although response time is one of the major indicators of EMS performance, this paper did not study the detailed relationship between time intervals and outcomes. The lack of research on the CIA may have introduced organizational bias, addressed through a rigorous data analysis process and cross-verification of findings by two researchers separately. Additionally, the study was primarily descriptive and did not include inferential testing, confidence intervals, or multivariate modeling to determine independent predictors. Future studies with larger datasets should incorporate advanced statistical methods to identify factors associated with patient deterioration during transfers and to assess the effects of response time intervals.

Conclusion

This study presents a substantially low rate of CIAs; however, the findings cannot be compared, as, to the researcher’s knowledge, this is one of the few novel studies at both the national and international levels. The response time reported in this study complies with international standards. It highlights the efficiency of EMS in providing prompt emergency medical services to serious and life-threatening cases in Sindh, Pakistan. The findings also shed some light on system inadequacies, as transfer requests were made primarily due to the need for a higher level of care from the secondary tier of the health system. This is also corroborated by the literature, where health facility and needs assessments in Sindh revealed several shortcomings in the existing health care model, such as the unavailability of medical and diagnostic equipment, medical technologies, and a health workforce, as well as compromised governance and leadership. Addressing these inequities through a systems approach is therefore crucial to prevent patient collapse in ambulances and to improve patient outcomes at both the prehospital and hospital levels by providing essential care in serious, life-threatening cases [20].
