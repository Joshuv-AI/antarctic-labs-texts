Valve–in–valve transcatheter mitral valve replacement via totally endoscopic endoclamp–assisted approach after failed bioprosthetic mitral valve: a case report
Authors: J Nader, A Roussiaux, J E Ricci, C Piot
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEndoscopic Endoclampassisted MViV can expand the armamentarium for complex redo mitral surgery, offering excellent early clinical and haemodynamic results in selected patients. A 50 year-old man with heavy alcohol and tobacco use and two previous mitral operations (repair, then bioprosthetic replacement eight years earlier) presented with progressive dyspnoea (NYHA III). Transthoracic and transoesophageal echocardiography revealed severe mixed stenosis/regurgitation of a 33 mm Medtronic Mosaic bioprothesis. No endocarditis was found preoperatively. After shared decisionmaking, minimally invasive videoassisted right minithoracotomy under endoaortic balloon occlusion (Endoclamp®) was planned. Intraoperatively, the degenerated valve was firmly embedded in the native annulus and could not be safely explanted. A 29 mm SAPIEN 3 Ultra transcatheter heart valve was therefore deployed inside the surgical frame under direct vision on cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB). Postdeployment echocardiography showed a mean gradient of 8 mmHg without paravalvular leak or LVOT compromise. The patient was extubated on postoperative day 2; aside from temporary difficulties in respiratory weaning, recovery was uneventful, and he was discharged home on day 9.Keywords: Mitral valve-in-valve, Minimally invasive mitral surgery, Endoclamping, Bioprosthetic degeneration

Introduction

Bioprosthetic mitral valve degeneration typically presents 8–15 years after implantation and traditionally mandates redo sternotomy with explantation and replacement. Redo surgery is, however, associated with higher operative bleeding, mortality, prolonged recovery, and increased transfusion requirements. Recent studies and metaanalyses have demonstrated that transcatheter Mitral Valve-in-Valve (MViV) yields comparable—or superior—early outcomes with shorter hospital stay, particularly in highrisk cohorts [1–3]. Yet most series describe transseptal or transapical percutaneous access; reports of a totally endoscopic surgical approach, especially using an endoaortic balloon, remain scarce. We present herein such a case of per operative multidisciplinary strategic decision of MViV. We discuss the technical nuances, and review the current literature.

Case presentation

Clinical history and examination

A 50 year-old male (body mass index 28,6 kg/m²) presented with six months of worsening exertional dyspnea (NYHA class II - III) and intermittent palpitations. Past medical history included active alcohol (~60 g/day) and tobacco (35 pack-years) consumption, ischemic heart disease with a chronic occlusion of right coronary artery, mitral regurgitation treated 8 years ago with minimally invasive mitral valve repair at age 42, than 3 weeks later, a bioprosthetic replacement using a Medtronic Mosaic 33 mm valve, for early repair failure. He was implanted with an internal cardiac defibrillator (ICD) for dilated cardiomyopathy with left ventricule dysfunction.

Transthoracic and transesophageal echography revealed severe dysfunction of the mitral bioprosthesis (mean gradient 14 mmHg, EOA 1 cm²), mild pulmonary hypertension (RVSP 45 mmHg), preserved LVEF (53%).

Hemoculture and PET-scan confirmed the absence of infectious endocarditis. Cardiac CT-scan did not find any new coronary stenosis, without any aortic aneurysm.

Given the patient’s age, comorbidities, and desire to avoid a third sternotomy, a minimally invasive right thoracotomy approach was proposed after heart-team discussion. Informed consent was obtained, including the potential need for conversion to full sternotomy for mitral replacement. The patient’s first prosthesis choice was mechanical heart valve. Percutaneous Valve-in-Valve intervention was not considered regarding the young age.

At the induction of anesthesia and before dressing the patient for surgery, we disactivated the ICD. The patient went through a spontaneous cardiac arrest necessitating 2 min cardiopulmonary rescucitation (CPR) before the rhythm was back to normal.

The intervention was conducted through a 5 cm right infraaxillary minithoracotomy in the 4th intercostal space with one 10 mm port for 3D 4K camera and another 5 mm port for left ventricle drainage. Cardiopulmonary bypass was established via femoral arterial and venous cannulation, using vacuumassisted venous drainage. Aortic occlusion was performed using endoaortic balloon catheter (Endoclamp®) and cardiac arrest was obtained using antegrade cold crystalloid cardioplegia.

After liberating the wall of the left atrium, we opened it at the same place the last atriotomy was performed. The bioprosthetis was exposed. All leaflets were extensively calcified and remodeled without any additional images. The stent posts were firmly adherent to the native annulus. When trying to have a better exposition to get the bioprothesis explanted, the endoballoon moved and the heart was again perfused. We positioned once more time the ballon in the ascending aorta under echographical guidance and administered a second flush of cardioplegia.

Considering the risk of annular disruption and all the difficulties since the initial cardiac arrest to the myocardial protection issue, we proceeded, after heart-team emergent discussion, with valve-in-valve strategy.

We excised all the 3 leaflets and implanted then, a 29 mm SAPIEN 3 transcatheter heart valve, chosen based on the MViV instructions. Crimped on highpressure balloon (volume 33 mL), the valve was deployed under direct visualization guidance, referring to the top of the three commissures in order to align the free ventricular top of the THV valve with these hinge points. Immediate TOE did not show any paravalvular leakage without any LVOT obstruction. Cross clamp time was 113 min. CBP time was longer because of respiratory difficulties for the weaning.

The patient was then transferred to the intensive care unit where he was extubated 12 h after surgery and managed with noninvasive ventilation. No arrhythmias or renal dysfunction occurred. Discharged on postoperative day 9 with anticoagulation using novel oral anticoagulant. The pre-discharged TTE found a preserved LVEF and a good function of the bioprosthesis (mean gradient 8 mmHg, no PVL).

Discussion

The case was rendered more complex by the unexpected prebypass cardiac arrest that occurred while the anesthetist temporarily deactivated the patient’s ICD to avoid electromagnetic interference. Although the rhythm was rapidly restored after CPR, the episode raised concerns about myocardial reserve and the safety of a prolonged crossclamp time needed for complete explantation. Faced with a densely pannused valve that resisted atraumatic removal, the surgical team deliberately prioritized shortening ischaemic time over anatomic completeness. Realtime discussion among the surgeon, anaesthetist, and cardiologist resulted in switching to a valve-in-valve (ViV) strategy that preserved haemodynamic stability while still delivering a durable solution.

Redo mitral surgery is technically demanding owing to adhesions and annular calcification. MViV, originally developed for highrisk patients via percutaneous routes, has demonstrated lower operative mortality and stroke rates compared with conventional redo surgery [1]. Our case extends these findings by combining a totally endoscopic approach with Endoclamp technology, offering:

Cosmesis and reduced trauma relative to sternotomy.

Previous authors have described endoscopic explant and-replace techniques, yet reports of endoscopic MViV remain limited.

Endoaortic balloon occlusion enabled rapid access to the left atrial path and to cardioplegic arrest without aortic manipulation reducing the hemorragic risk of freeing pericardial adherences to the aorta and right ventricle. Endoscopic surgery allowed perfect coaxial alignment of the transcatheter valve, minimising risk of paravalvular leak and leftventricular outflow tract (LVOT) obstruction while maintaining a short clamp time.

The ICD device was reenabled before ICU discharge and paddles were kept on standby and temporary epicardial pacing wires placed, recognising the patient’s heightened arrhythmic vulnerability.

Our patient’s uneventful course supports the safety of this hybrid strategy.

This experience suggests that totally endoscopic Endoclampassisted MViV can act as an effective bailout in redo cases where a sudden event or hostile annulus makes full bioprothetic explantation unsafe, especially in high-profile implanted bioprothesis. Centres performing minimally invasive mitral surgery should anticipate such scenarios and establish an algorithm that includes pre- and intraoperative CT/TEE based confirmation of ViV feasibility (annular diameter, neoLVOT) and availability of appropriately sized balloonexpandable valves in the hybrid OR. The absence of infective endocarditis is crucial.

To our knowledge, few reports describe a totally endoscopic, endoclamp-assisted valve-in-valve mitral replacement [4] performed as a rescue strategy during failed explantation attempts. This hybrid approach may represent a viable bridge between open surgery and percutaneous TMVR, particularly in anatomically hostile reoperations.”

Conclusion

Endoscopic Endoclampassisted MViV offers a versatile rescue option when explantation of a degenerated mitral bioprosthesis proves unsafe. Larger series are warranted to validate durability and longterm outcomes.
