Decline and Resurgence of Cardiovascular–Kidney–Metabolic Syndrome–Related Deaths Involving Malnutrition in the United States, 1999 to 2023
Authors: Shuai Jin, Kaide Xia, Hao Lu, Dianmei Yang, Jing Wu, Ying Huang, Mei Chen, Junwen Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCardiovascular–kidney–metabolic syndrome (CKM) contributes substantially to death, but the extent of malnutrition documentation among CKM–attributed deaths is unclear. We quantified temporal trends and disparities in US CKM‐related deaths involving malnutrition from 1999 to 2023.MethodsUsing Centers for Disease Control and Prevention Wide‐Ranging Online Data for Epidemiologic Research Multiple Cause of Death data, we identified deaths with CKM as the underlying cause and malnutrition as a contributing cause. We calculated age‐adjusted mortality rates (AAMRs) per 100 000 using the 2000 US standard population, assessed trends with joinpoint regression, quantified malnutrition involvement using an AAMR‐based share, and modeled the death‐count–based share with weighted binomial regression.ResultsNational AAMRs followed a U‐shaped pattern, declining from 2.31 in 1999 to about 0.9 in the early 2010s, then increasing to 3.78 (95% CI, 3.71–3.85) in 2023 (average annual percentage change, 2.1%). Despite declining overall CKM death, the AAMR‐based share increased from 0.67% to 1.40%. Adults aged ≥65 years accounted for most deaths and had higher AAMRs than younger adults, although younger decedents showed faster adjusted annual increases in malnutrition documentation. Black adults had the highest AAMRs throughout, whereas relative increases in malnutrition involvement were steeper among White adults. The West had higher baseline odds and the most rapid acceleration.ConclusionsMalnutrition involvement in CKM‐attributed deaths increased over time despite declining overall CKM deaths, highlighting malnutrition as an underrecognized component of end‐of‐life vulnerability in CKM and supporting integration of nutritional assessment and supportive care in high‐burden populations and regions.

Cardiovascular–kidney–metabolic syndrome (CKM), recently defined by the American Heart Association, describes the tightly interlinked clustering of metabolic risk factors, chronic kidney disease (CKD), and cardiovascular disease. 1 , 2 In this study, we operationalize “CKM‐related death” using death certificates in which a CKM condition is coded as the underlying cause of death, reflecting the primary disease initiating the chain of events leading to death, rather than merely a comorbidity. In the United States, population‐based surveillance shows that CKM‐related death remains substantial and has reaccelerated in recent years despite advances in prevention and treatment, with particularly high rates among older adults and rural residents. 3 , 4 , 5 This escalating burden underscores CKM as a critical public health priority and highlights the need to better understand the factors that drive CKM‐related death. 4

To date, efforts to elucidate the drivers of CKM death have focused predominantly on external socioenvironmental determinants. Recent population‐based studies have mapped how social determinants of health, such as food insecurity, social deprivation, and income inequality, serve as powerful accelerators of premature CKM death at the county or individual level. 4 , 6 , 7 , 8 Other work has extended this perspective to environmental stressors, demonstrating that extreme heat risk is associated with higher CKM‐related death, even after accounting for cardiometabolic risk factor prevalence. 7 , 9 Collectively, these analyses portray individuals with CKM as being disproportionately exposed to and harmed by structural and environmental disadvantages. However, they pay comparatively less attention to intrinsic biological and nutritional vulnerabilities that may critically shape survival once CKM is established. 8 , 9

Although CKM is often framed as a pathology of metabolic excess, including obesity, hyperlipidemia, and type 2 diabetes, 5 a substantial subset of patients paradoxically transition to a catabolic state characterized by malnutrition and protein–energy wasting. 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 In advanced CKD and heart failure, chronic inflammation, neurohormonal activation, congestion, and anorexia can drive a shift from metabolic surplus to cachexia, a phenomenon underpinning the so‐called “obesity paradox” or “reverse epidemiology.” 15 , 16 , 17 Multiple clinical studies have demonstrated that malnutrition is an independent and powerful predictor of death among patients with heart failure and renal insufficiency, sometimes outweighing traditional cardiovascular risk factors. 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 , 18 , 19 However, despite this evidence, the population‐level epidemiology of the “double burden” in which CKM conditions constitute the underlying cause of death and malnutrition is recorded as a contributing cause remains poorly characterized. Importantly, “CKM‐related death involving malnutrition” in death certificate data is not equivalent to “dying mainly from malnutrition”; rather, it indicates that malnutrition was documented as a co‐occurring condition that may reflect frailty, catabolism, or advanced disease severity at the end of life. Because coding practices and diagnostic recognition may evolve over long periods, International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD‐10)–based ascertainment of malnutrition may be subject to temporal and regional variation; therefore, rigorous trend and disparity analyses should explicitly compare CKM deaths with versus without malnutrition to contextualize malnutrition's contribution. Accordingly, it is unclear how frequently malnutrition is implicated in CKM‐related deaths relative to CKM deaths without malnutrition, how its contribution has changed over time (including whether malnutrition involvement has increased faster than overall CKM death), and whether its impact varies across demographic and geographic subgroups.

To address this knowledge gap, we leveraged the national Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) Wide‐Ranging Online Data for Epidemiologic Research (WONDER) Multiple Cause of Death database to conduct a 25‐year, population‐based analysis of CKM‐related death involving malnutrition in the United States. Specifically, we aimed to quantify temporal trends in age‐adjusted mortality rates, characterize demographic and geographic disparities, and describe state‐level variations. Crucially, unlike prior surveillance, we explicitly contrasted CKM deaths with versus without malnutrition and quantified malnutrition involvement using both an age‐adjusted mortality rate (AAMR)‐based share (ShareA) and a death‐count–based share (ShareD). Furthermore, we used multivariable binomial regression models to ascertain whether the rising malnutrition burden persists after adjusting for demographic shifts (age, sex, race) and geographic factors. By focusing on deaths in which CKM conditions are the underlying cause and malnutrition is a colisted contributing cause, this study provides descriptive epidemiologic evidence on the evolving intersection between cardiometabolic disease and undernutrition. These findings may help inform targeted strategies for risk identification and supportive care, such as systematic nutritional screening and multidisciplinary rehabilitation (including exercise or cardiac rehabilitation) in high‐risk CKM populations, while recognizing that death certificate data cannot determine the effectiveness of specific interventions or establish causality.

METHODS

Ethics Statement

This study used publicly available, deidentified CDC WONDER Multiple Cause of Death data and was considered exempt from institutional review board review; informed consent was not required.

Data Availability

CDC WONDER Multiple Cause of Death data are publicly available.

Transparency and Openness Promotion Statement

The analytic methods and materials needed to reproduce the results, including CDC WONDER query specifications, ICD‐10 code lists, variable definitions, and R code for data processing and statistical analyses, will be made publicly available (eg, GitHub/Zenodo) upon publication.

Data Source and Study Design

We conducted a retrospective, population‐based observational study using the CDC WONDER Multiple Cause of Death database from 1999 through 2023. 20 , 21 This nationwide resource compiles information from death certificates filed in the 50 US states and the District of Columbia, including the underlying cause of death and up to 20 additional multiple causes.

To rigorously distinguish the distinct burden of malnutrition, we analyzed 2 nested cohorts derived from death certificate records: (1) CKM overall, defined as deaths in which a CKM condition was listed as the underlying cause of death; and (2) CKM with malnutrition, defined as the subset of deaths with CKM as the underlying cause of death in which malnutrition was recorded in any multiple‐cause‐of‐death field. Additionally, we derived a complementary cohort of CKM deaths without malnutrition (by subtracting malnutrition‐involved deaths from overall CKM deaths). We further quantified malnutrition involvement using both ShareA and ShareD.

Case Definitions and Covariates

Decedents were identified using ICD‐10 codes. We defined CKM–related deaths as those encompassing major cardiovascular, renal, and metabolic diseases listed as the underlying cause of death. 4 To quantify nutritional depletion, we identified malnutrition using codes E40 to E46 recorded as a contributing cause. 22 All ICD‐10 codes are provided in Table S1.

For the derived metric AAMR‐based malnutrition involvement share (ShareA), we defined ShareA as (AAMR for CKM with malnutrition/AAMR for CKM overall)×100. Uncertainty was quantified using a delta‐method approximation combining the standard errors of the numerator and denominator. In parallel, we defined the death‐count–based malnutrition involvement share (ShareD) among CKM‐attributed deaths as (deaths with malnutrition/deaths overall). ShareA served as a descriptive, rate‐based measure, including for joinpoint analyses, whereas ShareD was used as the binomial regression outcome.

Demographic and geographic covariates included sex (female, male), age group (<65 years, ≥65 years), and race (White, Black, Other race[s]). Race was obtained from US death certificates according to the Office of Management and Budget standards and is reported by funeral directors on the basis of an informant, often next of kin, or, if unavailable, observation. In CDC WONDER, bridged‐race categories are available for 1999–2020, whereas single‐race categories are available for 2018–2023. To maintain comparability across calendar periods and reduce instability caused by sparse counts and data suppression, race was analyzed as White, Black, and Other race(s). “Other race(s)” included all available non‐White and non‐Black categories in CDC WONDER, including American Indian or Alaska Native and Asian or Pacific Islander in the bridged‐race data, and other non‐White/non‐Black race categories in the single‐race data, where available. Accordingly, race was not self‐identified and may be subject to misclassification. County‐level urbanization was defined according to the 2013 National Center for Health Statistics Urban–Rural Classification Scheme. Due to data availability constraints following suppression criteria, urbanization analyses were restricted to the period 1999 to 2020. 23 Geographic variation was evaluated by US census region (Northeast, Midwest, South, and West) and by state. 24

Statistical Analysis

We calculated annual AAMRs per 100 000 population, standardized to the 2000 US standard population. Rates based on <10 deaths were suppressed in accordance with CDC guidelines. 25

Temporal trends were assessed using log‐linear joinpoint regression implemented via the joinpoint regression (Joinpoint Regression Program, version 5.0.2; National Cancer Institute). We specified up to 2 joinpoints to identify significant changes in slope and to estimate annual percentage changes and average annual percentage changes (AAPCs) with 95% CIs. For the derived ShareA metric, joinpoint models were fit assuming constant variance, consistent with modeling a derived proportion.

To evaluate adjusted associations between calendar time and malnutrition involvement, we fitted multivariable binomial regression models. The outcome was ShareD, as defined above, weighted by the total number of CKM deaths (trial size). Models included natural cubic splines for calendar year (4 df) and were adjusted for sex, age group, race, census region, and urbanization. Robust SEs were clustered by calendar year. In secondary models, we included interaction terms between time and subgroup indicators to test whether temporal trajectories differed significantly by demographic group.

All statistical tests were 2‐sided, with a P value <0.05 considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed in R version 4.3.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

RESULTS

National Trends

Between 1999 and 2023, CKM underlying‐cause deaths involving malnutrition increased from 4927 to 10 328, and the AAMR rose from 2.31 (95% CI, 2.24–2.37) to 3.78 (95% CI, 3.71–3.85) per 100 000. The temporal pattern of malnutrition‐involved CKM death was biphasic, with an early decline followed by a marked resurgence: joinpoint analysis identified slope changes around 2009 and 2014, with rates reaching a nadir in the early 2010s and then increasing sharply through 2023 (Figure 1B). Over the full period, the AAPC for malnutrition‐involved CKM death was 2.10% (95% CI, 1.61–2.53; P<0.001; TableTable).

In contrast, overall CKM underlying‐cause death (regardless of contributing causes) declined substantially from 1999 to 2023, with AAMR decreasing from 343.25 (95% CI, 342.53–343.97) to 269.57 (95% CI, 269.00–270.13) per 100 000 and an overall AAPC of −0.85% (95% CI, −1.15 to −0.53; P<0.001; Table S2). The trajectory for CKM deaths without malnutrition was similar (AAMR, 340.95–265.79 per 100 000; AAPC, −0.88% [95% CI, −1.18 to −0.57]), indicating that the national decline in CKM deaths was driven primarily by nonmalnutrition CKM deaths (TableTable).

Despite the overall decrease in CKM deaths, the relative contribution of malnutrition to CKM deaths increased over time, as reflected by ShareA. National ShareA increased from 0.67% (95% CI, 0.65–0.69) in 1999 to 1.40% (95% CI, 1.38–1.43) in 2023, with an AAPC of 3.04% (95% CI, 2.86–3.23; P<0.001) and joinpoints around 2008 and 2013 (Figure 1C; Table S3). This indicates an increasing rate‐based contribution of malnutrition‐involved deaths within CKM deaths over time. Together, these findings show that while overall CKM death has declined nationally, malnutrition involvement has become increasingly prominent within CKM deaths in the past decade.

Demographic Disparities in Malnutrition‐Involved CKM Deaths

Malnutrition‐involved CKM death demonstrated a similar biphasic (“U‐shaped”) trajectory in both sexes, characterized by early declines followed by renewed increases starting in the mid‐2010s (Figure 2A). In 2023, men had a slightly higher AAMR than women (3.87 versus 3.68 per 100 000), and the long‐term increase was modestly steeper in men (AAPC, 2.39% versus 1.92%; TableTable). However, supplementary analyses of proportionate contribution revealed a distinct contrast: ShareA was consistently higher in women than in men throughout the study period (1999–2023) (Figure S1A; Table S3). This suggests that while men have a higher absolute burden of fatal CKM, women with CKM are disproportionately affected by nutritional involvement.

The burden of malnutrition‐involved CKM death was overwhelmingly concentrated among older adults (Figure 2B). Across the study period, AAMRs among individuals aged ≥65 years were orders of magnitude higher than those aged <65 years, reaching 18.30 per 100 000 in 2023 compared with 0.25 per 100 000 among younger individuals (TableTable). Despite the substantially lower absolute burden, the younger group exhibited the fastest relative increase over the full period (AAPC, 2.24%), whereas increases among older adults were smaller in relative terms (AAPC, 1.20%). Joinpoint patterns indicated earlier nadirs and subsequent increases in older adults, while rates among younger adults remained low with more limited slope changes.

Notably, ShareA patterns diverged by age: ShareA increased among older adults but decreased modestly among younger adults (Figure S1B; Table S3). To contextualize this finding, we examined overall CKM mortality trends (Figure S2; Table S2). While overall CKM underlying‐cause death declined in older adults, it increased among younger adults over time. Thus, the decreasing ShareA in younger adults does not reflect a lower nutritional burden but rather indicates that the rise in malnutrition‐involved deaths occurred in parallel with an even faster rise in overall CKM death in this age stratum.

Urbanization and Census‐Region Patterns

Across racial and ethnic strata, malnutrition‐involved CKM deaths exhibited a broadly biphasic trajectory, with declines through the late 2000s followed by renewed increases thereafter (Figure 3A). Black individuals consistently had the highest absolute AAMRs over time (peak, 4.83 per 100 000), whereas White individuals accounted for the largest absolute number of deaths and showed a pronounced rebound in the most recent decade (Table). In supplementary analyses of contribution, ShareA increased in all racial and ethnic groups from 1999 to 2023. Notably, the relative pace of increase was fastest among White (AAPC, 3.45%) and Other (AAPC, 3.42%) groups compared with Black individuals (AAPC, 2.09%), indicating a narrowing of the relative gap in malnutrition involvement (Table S3; Figure S3A).

Both metropolitan and nonmetropolitan counties experienced early declines, followed by a subsequent rebound in malnutrition‐involved CKM AAMRs, with turning points occurring around the early to mid‐2010s (Figure 3B). Nonmetropolitan areas had higher AAMRs at baseline, but the disparity attenuated over time; by 2020, AAMRs were similar between metropolitan and nonmetropolitan counties (2.17 versus 2.12 per 100 000) (Table). Consistent with this convergence, ShareA rose significantly in metropolitan counties (AAPC, 1.90%) but remained relatively stable in nonmetropolitan counties (AAPC, 0.05%; P=0.873) over 1999 to 2020 (Table S3; Figure S3B). This stability in rural ShareA occurred despite a long‐term decline in overall CKM AAMRs in nonmetropolitan counties (Figure S4), suggesting distinct drivers of malnutrition burden in urban centers.

All 4 census regions demonstrated a comparable biphasic pattern with nadirs in the early 2010s and accelerated increases in recent years but with substantial differences in level and slope (Figure 3C). By 2023, the West had the highest AAMR (4.58 per 100 000), followed by the Midwest (4.07) and the South (4.00) (Table). ShareA increased in every region from 1999 to 2023. The steepest rise occurred in the West (AAPC, 4.64%), followed by the Northeast (3.10%) and Midwest (2.61%) (Table S3; Figure S3C), indicating a geographically widespread but unevenly accelerating contribution of malnutrition to CKM death.

State‐Level Heterogeneity in Burden and Trends

State‐level analyses revealed marked geographic heterogeneity in malnutrition‐involved CKM deaths (Figure 4; Table S4). In 2023, AAMRs varied substantially across states, spanning an ≈6‐fold difference (1.6–10.2 per 100 000). The highest contemporary burdens were concentrated in several Mountain West and South‐Central states, specifically Utah (10.23 per 100 000) and Oklahoma (8.14 per 100 000), whereas northeastern states consistently remained at the lower end of the distribution (eg, New York, ≈1.61; and Connecticut, ≈1.63 per 100 000; Figure 4A).

Long‐term trends also differed considerably by geography. While many states experienced significant increases, the velocity of this rise was uneven. Particularly rapid accelerations were observed in the western region (eg, Oregon: AAPC, 7.27%; Idaho: AAPC, 6.49%; Arizona: AAPC, 4.89%) and selected midwestern states (eg, Minnesota: AAPC, 6.86%). In contrast, others showed stable or modestly declining trajectories (eg, Illinois: AAPC, −0.14%; New Jersey: AAPC, −0.52%) (Figure 4B). Because CDC WONDER suppresses strata with <10 deaths, state‐specific AAPCs reflect each state's valid reporting window and should be interpreted in light of the first and last available years shown in Table S4.

Supplementary analyses highlighted a diverging pattern between total burden and malnutrition involvement. While overall CKM deaths (regardless of malnutrition) generally declined across most states (Figure S5; Table S5), the proportion of CKM deaths complicated by malnutrition (ShareA) increased broadly (Figure S6). By 2023, ShareA reached as high as ≈4.06% in Utah, with elevated contributions also clustered in South Dakota (≈2.97%), Idaho (≈2.68%), and Colorado (≈2.66%). Notably, several states exhibited exponential‐like growth in ShareA (eg, Minnesota: AAPC, 7.98%; Utah: AAPC, 7.99%), indicating that the rising involvement of malnutrition is a geographically widespread phenomenon that is outpacing the background trends of CKM death (Table S6).

Adjusted Differences and Temporal Dynamics in Malnutrition Documentation

To quantify adjusted differences and temporal change in malnutrition documentation among CKM‐attributed deaths, we fitted domain‐specific weighted binomial regression models of ShareD, weighting by total CKM deaths (trial size) and adjusting for nonlinear calendar time. Effects are reported at the median study year (2011), and time‐by‐subgroup interactions represent relative annual change in odds versus the reference group.

At the median year, men had lower odds of malnutrition documentation than women (adjusted odds ratio [aOR], 0.680 [95% CI, 0.662–0.698]; P<0.001) with no differential temporal change by sex (interaction aOR, 1.001 [95% CI, 0.998–1.005]; P=0.434). Younger adult decedents had markedly lower odds than older decedents (aOR, 0.263 [95% CI, 0.240–0.288]; P<0.001) but demonstrated a faster increase over time (interaction aOR, 1.027 [95% CI, 1.015–1.038]; P<0.001). Relative to Black decedents, White decedents had lower odds at the median year (aOR, 0.899 [95% CI, 0.861–0.939]; P<0.001), whereas the Other group had higher odds (aOR, 1.084 [95% CI, 1.032–1.138]; P=0.001); both White and Other showed faster temporal increases than Black (interaction aORs, 1.016 and 1.013, respectively; both P<0.001). Regionally, the West had higher odds than the South (aOR, 1.140 [95% CI, 1.091–1.191]; P<0.001) and the most rapid acceleration (interaction aOR, 1.025 [95% CI, 1.019–1.031]; P<0.001), while the Northeast remained lower without differential change. In 1999 to 2020, metropolitan counties had lower odds than nonmetropolitan counties (aOR, 0.922 [95% CI, 0.893–0.953]; P<0.001) but a steeper increase (interaction aOR, 1.022 [95% CI, 1.017–1.028]; P<0.001; Table S7; Figure 5).

DISCUSSION

In this 25‐year, nationwide analysis of US deaths, we found that deaths in which CKM conditions were the underlying cause and malnutrition was a contributing cause followed a pronounced U‐shaped temporal pattern, with an early period of decline followed by a renewed and accelerating increase in recent years. This resurgence was largely concentrated among older adults and was unevenly distributed across populations, with Black individuals bearing the highest overall burden, while recent relative increases were driven disproportionately by White individuals and were especially pronounced in the Western census region, alongside substantial geographic heterogeneity across states. By leveraging a comprehensive, nationally representative multiple cause‐of‐death data set and systematically examining demographic, urban–rural, regional, and state‐level patterns, this study provides a detailed characterization of the evolving and geographically clustered burden of CKM‐related deaths involving malnutrition—one that is not fully captured by traditional, obesity‐centric views of cardiometabolic disease.

Importantly, our analytic framework distinguishes deaths “dying from CKM” (CKM as the underlying cause of death) from deaths “dying from malnutrition.” 26 In this study, malnutrition was captured as a contributing condition among CKM‐attributed deaths, reflecting clinical recognition of nutritional depletion or wasting at the time of death rather than attribution of death primarily to malnutrition. 27 Accordingly, our findings should be interpreted as an increasing co‐occurrence (involvement) of malnutrition within CKM deaths and not as evidence that malnutrition caused CKM deaths. 28

The U‐shaped pattern we observed, with early declines followed by renewed increases, likely reflects the interplay of improvements in cardiovascular care and evolving population‐level vulnerabilities. The initial decline coincided with widespread adoption of evidence‐based cardiovascular therapies and risk‐factor control, which contributed to long‐term reductions in cardiovascular deaths in the early 2000s. 29 More recently, however, national gains in life expectancy have stalled or reversed in parts of the United States, driven in part by worsening cardiometabolic outcomes and other chronic conditions. 29 Our data indicate that CKM‐related deaths with colisted malnutrition are part of this broader shift. The upturn was particularly pronounced in the West, while substantial geographic heterogeneity was observed across the Northeast, Midwest, South, and individual states. 4 , 8 , 30 At the same time, the overwhelming concentration of events in older adults suggests that as more people survive earlier cardiovascular events and live longer with CKM, an increasing fraction progress into advanced stages of heart failure and CKD where vulnerability to malnutrition and wasting is greatest. 31 , 32 Although we cannot establish causality, this pattern is consistent with a transition from early cardiometabolic risk to late‐stage, frailty‐driven death.

To clarify whether rising malnutrition involvement simply mirrored secular changes in overall CKM death, we directly compared CKM overall death with CKM death involving malnutrition and quantified the proportional contribution (ShareA) of malnutrition within CKM death over time. 33 The increase in ShareA indicates that malnutrition accounts for an expanding fraction of CKM deaths beyond what would be expected from overall CKM mortality trends alone, supporting malnutrition involvement as an increasingly prominent vulnerability phenotype within CKM‐attributed deaths. 34

Our findings underscore the importance of moving beyond a narrow focus on metabolic excess in CKM and recognizing the contribution of “internal” biological fragility. CKM is often conceptualized in terms of obesity, dyslipidemia, and type 2 diabetes, yet a substantial subset of patients ultimately enter a catabolic state characterized by protein–energy wasting and loss of lean body mass. 35 , 36 , 37 In advanced CKD and heart failure, chronic inflammation, neurohormonal activation, congestion, and anorexia can collectively induce a shift from metabolic surplus to cachexia. 38 , 39 Multiple clinical studies and meta‐analyses have demonstrated that malnutrition, assessed by tools such as the Controlling Nutritional Status score, geriatric nutritional risk index, or protein–energy wasting scores, is an independent and powerful predictor of death among patients with heart failure and in those receiving dialysis, often outweighing traditional cardiovascular risk factors. 40 , 41 , 42 Although we could not directly measure body composition or nutritional biomarkers in this study, the presence of malnutrition as a contributing cause of death in a growing number of CKM‐related deaths is consistent with this trajectory. These observations suggest that clinical paradigms focused predominantly on weight reduction and metabolic risk control may be incomplete for patients with advanced CKM, in whom nutritional vulnerability, frailty, and functional decline may emerge as dominant correlates of poor prognosis. Incorporating systematic screening for malnutrition and frailty into CKM management, even among patients with high body mass index, may therefore be critical to reducing deaths in this population. 43 , 44 , 45

The demographic and geographic disparities we observed provide additional context for targeting interventions. Black adults consistently experienced the highest age‐adjusted mortality rates, reflecting the accumulated effects of structural racism, higher prevalence of CKM risk factors, and barriers to high‐quality care. 46 , 47 , 48 At the same time, the recent slope of increase was steeper among White adults, suggesting a changing distribution of risk and a broadening of vulnerability that echoes recent reports of rising cardiometabolic deaths among socioeconomically disadvantaged White populations. 49 , 50 Historically, nonmetropolitan counties in the United States have experienced higher cardiovascular deaths than metropolitan counties, and in many national analyses the rural–urban gap has persisted or even widened over time. 51 , 52 In contrast, in our study of CKM‐related deaths involving malnutrition, we observed more rapid declines in nonmetropolitan counties during the earlier part of the study period, leading to a partial narrowing of the rural–urban gap by 2020. Nonetheless, rates in many rural areas remain high, and recent national studies show that rural counties have borne a disproportionate increase in cardiovascular deaths in the past decade, especially among younger adults. 52 , 53 , 54 State‐level analyses identified several western and North‐Central states, such as Utah and South Dakota, as contemporary hotspots in terms of current burden or recent growth in CKM–malnutrition deaths. These patterns may also be consistent with broader literature linking adverse lifestyle and nutrition‐related environments to cardiometabolic risk and death. 55 , 56 These patterns may reflect the combined influence of rurality, health care access, nutrition environments, and broader structural disadvantage, consistent with prior literature on cardiovascular mortality disparities across place and population groups. 30 , 57 , 58 They indicate that both historically marginalized groups and emerging high‐risk populations require attention.

Because CKM is a recently formalized construct, we operationalized CKM death using a prespecified, consistently applied set of ICD‐10 codes for underlying causes of death across 1999 to 2023. 1 This approach minimizes definitional drift over time within our surveillance framework, although changes in diagnostic patterns and coding practices remain possible. 59

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, death certificate data are subject to misclassification and underascertainment, particularly for conditions such as malnutrition that may be underrecognized or inconsistently coded. Our estimates therefore likely represent a conservative lower bound of the true burden. Second, the ecological nature of the data precludes individual‐level adjustment for important clinical covariates, such as body mass index, albumin, inflammatory markers, ejection fraction, or stage of kidney disease, and prevents differentiation between starvation‐related malnutrition and inflammation‐ or disease‐driven cachexia. Third, changes in coding practices or awareness over time could influence the likelihood that malnutrition is recorded as a contributing cause of death. However, the consistent rise in ShareA, the temporal heterogeneity across multiple subgroups, and the marked geographic variation observed in our analyses argue against coding changes alone as a sufficient explanation, although evolving documentation may have contributed in part to the observed increases. Finally, our focus on deaths with CKM as the underlying cause and malnutrition as a contributing cause does not capture all clinically relevant intersections between CKM and malnutrition, such as cases in which malnutrition is listed as the underlying cause.

From a clinical standpoint, our findings support consideration of routine malnutrition and frailty screening within CKM care pathways, particularly in advanced heart failure and CKD, paired with referral to dietitians and multidisciplinary management. 60 , 61 While our study cannot evaluate treatment effects, the results motivate further work to determine whether targeted nutritional strategies (including dietary counseling, oral supplementation, and, in selected cases, enteral/parenteral support) and exercise‐based interventions such as cardiac rehabilitation can improve outcomes in high‐risk CKM syndrome populations. 62

CONCLUSIONS

Despite these limitations, our findings highlight malnutrition involvement as an increasingly prominent component of CKM‐attributed deaths in the United States, with substantial demographic and geographic heterogeneity. Clinically, these results underscore the need to better integrate nutritional vulnerability and functional decline into risk assessment for advanced CKM syndrome. From a public health perspective, they support prioritizing surveillance and supportive resources in high‐burden regions and states. Future studies should evaluate whether targeted nutritional support and rehabilitation‐oriented interventions can mitigate adverse outcomes among CKM syndrome populations at highest risk.
