EEG-based biomarker for the identification of mild cognitive impairment via coherency analysis and iterative dynamic time warping
Authors: Minho Choi, Jang-Han Bae, Kahye Kim, Kyu Yeong Choi, Sarang Kang, Kun Ho Lee, Jaeuk U. Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Mild cognitive impairment (MCI) is an early stage of dementia, and its timely diagnosis is vital for preventing or delaying disease progression, which strongly impacts older adults’ health and quality of life. Non-intrusive MCI screening can combine cognitive assessments with physiological biomarkers. However, studies on practical, non-invasive biomarkers suitable for daily life remain limited, highlighting the need for new indicators. This study proposes a novel biomarker derived from event-related potentials (ERPs). Electroencephalography was recorded from the prefrontal cortex during an auditory oddball task. ERPs were segmented into epochs based on standard and target stimuli. Iterative dynamic time warping was applied to reduce noise and correct epoch variability, including non-linear timing distortions. The imaginary part of beta-band coherency was then extracted from ERP epochs to form the biomarker. Data from 1,619 participants (1,142 cognitively normal and 477 with MCI) were analyzed. Results demonstrated significant differences (p < 0.001) between control and MCI groups. Additionally, the biomarker showed low correlation with previously studied biomarkers, indicating it may capture new aspects of MCI-related physiological changes. These findings suggest the biomarker could improve non-intrusive MCI screening and provide insights into underlying neural alterations, supporting the development of more accurate and convenient diagnostic tools.

Introduction

Dementia causes a gradual decline in cognitive function that can considerably interfere with daily life. It can be categorized into subtypes, such as Alzheimer’s disease (AD), vascular dementia, Lewy body dementia, frontotemporal dementia, and mixed dementia, depending on its cause1. Among them, AD is the most common type, accounting for 60% of all cases2. The occurrence of dementia is strongly correlated with aging, and its incidence is rapidly increasing along with the global growth of the elderly population3,4. The global prevalence of dementia reached 55 million people in 2019, resulting in a societal cost of over US$ 1.31 trillion5. The number of dementia patients is expected to rise to 152 million by 20506. Additionally, dementia is the seventh leading cause of death worldwide and has a substantial impact on our lives7. However, there is no medical treatment that can completely cure dementia, making its prevention and proper management crucial. Mild cognitive impairment (MCI) is recognized as the prodromal stage of dementia. It is a condition in which an individual can perform daily activities but experiences mild cognitive deficit. Up to 15.56% of the population aged 50 and above may be affected by MCI, and over 46% of those with MCI may progress to dementia within 3 years8. Therefore, MCI testing and detection in the elderly are necessary for the prevention and delay of dementia, which can contribute to public health and the reduction of medical expenses.

Neuroimaging is known as an effective tool for diagnosing MCI. It uses magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) or positron emission tomography to analyze temporal lobe atrophy or cerebral blood flow and glucose hypometabolism in the brain9. These methods enable objective and direct identification of neurological changes but require specialized examination equipment and expert interpretation. As a more cost-effective and convenient testing tool, various questionnaire-based methods have been developed, including the mini mental state examination (MMSE) and the Montreal cognitive assessment10. This approach improves accessibility to the MCI test, making it useful as an initial screening tool. However, it may be affected by the participant’s subjective perception and psychological factors, and it has limitations in obtaining detailed information about the participant’s neurophysiological condition. Additionally, repeated assessments may lead to a learning effect, potentially influencing the reliability of the results. To complement this, physiological information such as bio-signals, voice, and eye-tracking can be utilized11–13. Among these, studies using electroencephalography (EEG), which measures the electrical activity of the brain, have been widely conducted to assess cognitive function in subjects. One study recorded resting-state EEG with 19 channels in an eyes-closed condition from 72 normal and 150 MCI participants. Functional lagged linear connectivity was analyzed, revealing reduced alpha-band connectivity in MCI14.15 investigated the distribution of permutation entropy to classify normal, MCI, and dementia groups, each consisting of 85 individuals. It was possible to distinguish the three groups using EEG signals recorded from the TP8 electrode with eyes closed and from the C3, C4, T8 and O2 electrodes with eyes open. The frontal beta asymmetry of resting-state EEG was extracted from the EEG data of subjects (58 normal and 41 MCI) in another study16. MCI participants exhibited higher asymmetry values. When incorporated into logistic regression alongside demographic information and computerized cognitive tests, it was able to distinguish normal and MCI with an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve of 0.933.

Although the effectiveness of EEG in MCI assessment has been confirmed in these studies, EEG measurements can increase patient discomfort; multiple electrodes must be attached to the head, and their contact status must be checked, often requiring the use of gel that requires cleaning afterward. To minimize the burden of EEG measurement, some studies have used dry-contact electrodes or reduced the number of channels.17 collected data from 40 subjects using a portable EEG system and analyzed event-related potential (ERP) elicited by a visual oddball task. The results showed that the peak amplitude of P300 was lower in the MCI group, with a statistically significant difference from the normal group. Resting-state EEG data from 496 elderly individuals were obtained using a device with two dry-contact electrodes placed in the prefrontal regions18. The authors compared MMSE scores with EEG parameters, such as median frequency, peak frequency, and alpha-to-theta ratio, and confirmed a moderate correlation with a Pearson correlation coefficient (PCC) of 0.758. The same device was utilized in subsequent studies. Several useful behavioral and ERP measures were identified from the data of 407 normal and 160 MCI participants19.20 expanded the data to include 1,754 subjects and conducted ERP component analyses. The research revealed that the MCI group exhibited greater variability in P300 latency to target auditory stimuli, and statistical differences were observed between the two groups in the phase locking values and coherence of the beta band in response to standard auditory stimuli. Another study examined intra-individual ERP variability in relation to MCI21. It developed a response variance curve (RVC) to quantify ERP variability across epochs and introduced biomarkers derived from RVC, including peak amplitude, latency, and area under the curve. However, research utilizing EEG with these simple measurement methods employing a small number of dry-contact electrodes remains limited. Further identification of biomarkers is needed for the effective detection of MCI and its practical application.

This study proposes a novel EEG biomarker to complement existing research and provide a more practical MCI assessment. For this biomarker, it is assumed that eyes-closed EEG is measured using two dry-contact electrodes at the prefrontal regions and that ERPs are generated through two types of auditory stimuli. In ERP experiments, when the same stimulus is repeated multiple times, neural responses ideally should remain consistent. However, in reality, the latency of responses may vary slightly across individual ERP trials. This variability is primarily due to changes in neural processing speed, fluctuations in cognitive attention, and individual differences in neuro-physiological conditions. These phenomena can obscure the underlying features when performing ERP analysis, potentially hindering the identification of meaningful biomarkers. The dynamic time warping (DTW) technique can be employed to reduce the variability of jitter in ERP epochs by optimally aligning signals. As an algorithm designed to align two time-series signals22, DTW compresses or extends a signal to achieve an optimal shape with minimal temporal differences from a target signal. While DTW primarily addresses latency variability, its application may also mitigate certain high-frequency fluctuations, potentially reducing some noise components23. The proposed method modifies it to an iterative algorithm, considering the increased EEG variability with aging and enlarged noise components associated with the use of dry electrodes20,24. The devised iterative DTW (IDTW) transforms ERP epochs for each auditory stimulus. Then, the imaginary part of coherency (IPC) for the beta band of ERPs is obtained to analyze the interaction and connectivity between two-channel EEG signals while reducing the influence of volume conduction. Volume conduction refers to the phenomenon where neural activity from a single brain region simultaneously affects multiple EEG channels25. It can cause false connectivity unrelated to actual functional connectivity or distort connectivity-related metrics, making proper consideration of volume conduction necessary in EEG connectivity analysis. To address this, the proposed biomarker is based on IPC, which is relatively robust to the effects of volume conduction. This novel EEG biomarker, designed with consideration for ERP variability and volume conduction issues, is described in detail in this study. Its validation results are presented based on analyses using a large dataset, including statistical comparisons between cognitively normal and MCI groups. Additionally, this study examines the correlation between previously introduced biomarkers and the proposed biomarker to determine whether it provides new information about MCI that is not captured by existing biomarkers. Methods

Data acquisition

The studies involving humans were approved by the Institutional Review Board of Chonnam National University Hospital (IRB No. CNUH-2019-279). All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. The participants provided their written informed consent to participate in this study. The participants were recruited from the Gwangju Alzheimer’s and Related Dementias Cohort Research Center between 2019 and 2022, with a total of 1,754 older adults aged 54 to 90 years. A clinical interview, MRI analysis, and questionnaire-based tests, including the Korean MMSE, 2nd Edition (KMMSE) and the Seoul Neuropsychological Screening Battery II (SNSB), were conducted to assess the cognitive status of the participants. Based on a comprehensive assessment of the test results, all participants were classified into cognitively normal (CN), subjective cognitive decline (SCD), amnestic MCI (aMCI), non-amnestic MCI (naMCI), and dementia groups26. SCD is characterized by subjective memory concerns but does not exhibit cognitive decline at the level of MCI and is considered to have normal cognitive function along with CN. aMCI and naMCI are subtypes of MCI, distinguished by the presence or absence of cognitive impairment in the memory domain27. In this study, CN and SCD were grouped as healthy controls (HC), while aMCI and naMCI were classified as MCI, and the differences between HC and MCI were analyzed.

A portable device, the neuroNicle FX2 (LAXTHA, Republic of Korea), was used to measure EEG. It has a sampling rate of 250 Hz and a resolution of 15 bits. Two channels of EEG (Fp1 and Fp2 according to the international 10–20 system) are obtained from the prefrontal cortex using dry-contact monopolar electrodes, with a reference electrode placed at the right earlobe. This device was designed in the form of a headband, making it easy to wear. With this feature, EEG can be easily measured during daily life using the device. In our experiments, EEG measurement was performed for about 5 minutes while each participant sat upright with their eyes closed in a quiet room. ERPs were elicited by an auditory oddball task during the EEG measurement. In the oddball task, participants heard two randomly generated auditory stimuli-standard stimuli at 750 Hz and target stimuli at 2,000 Hz-and were instructed to press a switch when the target stimulus was presented. The duration of all stimuli was 50 ms, the inter-stimulus interval was 1,000 ms, and the standard and target stimuli were presented 256 and 64 times, respectively, in each experiment for each subject. Pre-processing

The measured EEG is filtered using a band-pass filter of 0.1–30 Hz to remove unnecessary signal components in the proposed method. The filtered signal is then divided into epochs based on the time points at which auditory stimuli are generated. Each epoch includes an EEG segment from 200 ms before to 700 ms after an auditory stimulus, and the baseline of each epoch and channel is corrected for analysis. Three rules are applied to exclude inappropriate epochs for each participant’s data. An epoch with a noisy ERP may exhibit abnormally large amplitude or fluctuations, and any epoch exceeding an amplitude threshold is removed. The second rule uses the Interquartile Range (IQR) method, where epochs with a standard deviation (SD) value outside 1.5 times the IQR from the first and third quartiles are considered outliers. In the oddball task, it is assumed that participants press the switch only for target stimuli, and ERPs are elicited by these predetermined actions. Therefore, the timing of the switch press is monitored and epochs associated with unintended actions, such as pressing the switch during standard stimuli or failing to press it during target stimuli, are discarded from the analysis. If the number of remaining epochs for any stimulus type is less than eleven after this inspection, the data are considered insufficient for analysis and are excluded. After this process, some data may still exhibit high SD across all epochs due to noise. To eliminate the data with abnormal EEG variations, the mean SD of all remaining epochs for each participant’s data is calculated, and the three-sigma rule is applied to identify and remove such outliers28. Figure 1 shows the data exclusion process of this study. In addition to the data eliminated by the criteria mentioned above, data with missing information were removed. Data from dementia group were also excluded because this study focuses on the differences between HC and MCI. The remaining data from 1,142 HC and 477 MCI subjects were investigated. As a result, the alignment in FDTW is performed with respect to a noisy and temporally blurred template, which may limit its ability to accurately recover the underlying ERP components. In FDTW, each ERP epoch is aligned to the average ERP and then processed with a low-pass filter. Extraction of biomarkers

The functional connectivity analysis of EEG provides valuable information about the relationships and interactions between different brain regions29. Therefore, this analysis method have been employed in many studies related to MCI or cognitive decline30,31. However, volume conduction is one of the factors that hinder the accurate interpretation of actual connectivity between EEG signals, and its influence increases as the distance between electrodes decreases32. In our measurement setup, EEG signals from Fp1 and Fp2 are recorded using adjacent electrodes in the prefrontal region, making it crucial to consider volume conduction when performing connectivity analysis. IPC is one of the connectivity metrics that is robust to the volume conduction problem25. Coherency measures the linear relationship between two EEG signals and quantifies the degree of phase synchronization. Let the coherency between two signals, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x_1(t)$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x_2(t)$$\end{document}, be defined as Coh(f). Then, IPC, which is denoted as iCoh(f), is obtained from the imaginary part of the coherency as11\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} iCoh(f) = \text {Im}(Coh(f)) \text {.} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}Volume conduction causes a phase difference of zero or 180 degrees, primarily affecting the real part of coherency. Therefore, IPC is insensitive to this volume conduction artifact and can provide information about the synchronization of two time-lagged signals.

This study utilizes IPC to extract a biomarker, specifically the task-related difference in the mean of IPC (DiCohBI). Algorithm 3 outlines the overall process for obtaining the biomarker from ERPs after pre-processing. The ERPs are separated by stimulus type and EEG channel, and IDTW is applied. Subsequently, the beta-band components (13–30 Hz) are extracted from the IDTW-processed ERPs, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\boldsymbol{S_{jk}^I}$$\end{document}, using a finite impulse response filter. The beta band is known to contain information related to attention and cognitive processing. A previous study using data similar to that of the present research reported that connectivity analysis focusing on the beta band of the ERP provides useful information for distinguishing between MCI and HC groups20. Based on this findings, the beta component of the ERP is employed in the present study to construct the proposed biomarker. Then, IPC is calculated for two auditory stimuli. When constructing the IPC, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$iCoh\_BI_j(f)$$\end{document} for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\boldsymbol{B_{j1}^I}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\boldsymbol{B_{j2}^I}$$\end{document}, each \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\boldsymbol{B_{jk}^I}$$\end{document} can be averaged into a one-dimensional time signal, and the cross-spectrum of the two average ERPs is computed. However, in the proposed method, the cross-spectrum is calculated for each individual ERP, and the Welch periodogram is applied to average the results, thereby obtaining noise-robust outcomes while enhancing frequency resolution. The mean IPC values for standard and target stimuli are denoted as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$MiCohBI_s$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$MiCohBI_t$$\end{document}, respectively. The difference between these mean values is determined as DiCohBI to quantify the change in IPC in response to target stimuli relative to standard stimuli.

Statistical analyses

Data from 1,619 subjects were used to extract the proposed biomarker using MATLAB-based EEGLAB and HEREMES tools. The extracted data were then statistically analyzed with Python APIs, including SciPy and Statsmodels, to validate the usefulness of the proposed biomarker. The analysis includes an examination of biomarker differences between the HC and MCI groups. Biomarkers were derived under various conditions where FDTW was used instead of IDTW or neither IDTW nor FDTW was applied, to assess the effect of IDTW. To facilitate intuitive comparisons among the cases, the mean and SD of biomarkers were obtained using the Min-Max scaled values for each case. Variance homogeneity was tested using Levene’s test in the analysis. Based on the test result, the two sample t-test or Welch’s t-test was utilized to calculate the t-statistic and p-value. Additionally, a logistic regression analysis was performed to understand the relationship between the proposed biomarker and MCI, excluding the influence of covariates. In this analysis, all continuous variables were normalized using Min-Max scaling to a range between 0 and 1, defined as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(x - x_{\min })/(x_{\max } - x_{\min })$$\end{document}. Prior to scaling, outliers were identified and excluded using a three-SD criterion, whereby observations exceeding ±3 SD from the mean of the valid samples were removed to minimize the influence of extreme values. The odds ratio (OR), 95% confidence interval (CI), and p-value were calculated. Two adjusted models (Adjusted1 and Adjusted2) were investigated along with a crude model. The crude model used only the proposed biomarker as input, and Adjusted1 included demographic information, such as sex, age, and education years, as covariates. In Adjusted2, KMMSE scores were included in addition to the covariates used in Adjusted1. Previous studies have introduced various EEG-based biomarkers and behavioral measures related to MCI. A correlation analysis between the proposed biomarker and existing indices can help determine whether the proposed biomarker contains novel information associated with MCI. Accordingly, primary indices of studies based on similar data were selected and reproduced20,21. Table 2 presents the statistical information of the proposed biomarker and its variants for different conditions. In the table, DiCohBF represents the case where FDTW replaced IDTW, while DiCohB denotes the case where neither FDTW nor IDTW was applied. DiCohB had a p-value greater than 0.05, indicating no statistical significance between HC and MCI. This is because the connectivity analysis of ERP responses to each auditory stimulus was not properly conducted due to noise components and the variability of jitter across ERP epochs. FDTW could mitigate this problem. The difference in the mean of DiCohBF between the two groups enlarged, achieving statistical significance with a p-value of 0.0052. However, the absolute value of Cohen’s d was smaller than 0.2, and DiCohBF did not show a meaningful effect in distinguishing the two groups33. The proposed biomarker, DiCohBI, made the distinction between the two groups more pronounced; the difference in means increased, and SD decreased further, particularly in the MCI group. This change strengthened the statistical significance, and the p-value was less than 0.0001. The absolute value of Cohen’s d was 0.2245. While not large, this value was greater than 0.2, indicating a small effect size, and it could have clinical significance compared to DiCohBF. These results supported the advantages of IDTW as mentioned earlier. IDTW was able to preserve the benefits of FDTW while reducing unnecessary high-frequency fluctuations, thereby enhancing the reliability of the connectivity analysis. Discussion

The proposed biomarker is derived from two-channel prefrontal EEG signals recorded during an auditory oddball task, which are segmented into ERPs. IDTW is applied to emphasize common ERP components while reducing the effects of noise and jitter variation. From the refined ERPs, IPC is calculated, and the difference between the average IPC values for standard and target stimuli is defined as the proposed biomarker. To facilitate the physiological interpretation of the proposed biomarker, additional analyses were conducted by decomposing it into three components: ERP refinement using IDTW, beta-band IPC, and the difference in IPC between standard and target stimuli.

Figure 4 presents an example of average ERPs obtained using FDTW and IDTW, along with the average of the unprocessed raw ERPs for comparison. The average ERPs for the target and standard stimuli were calculated based on the ERPs measured at Fp1 and Fp2, respectively, using data from a participant in the HC group. In our previous study, two prominent ERP components, N100 and P200, were identified in the same dataset20. These components occurred between 60–200 ms and 180–300 ms after auditory stimuli, respectively. The amplitude of the ERP components was larger for target stimuli. These characteristics were also observed in the average of the raw ERPs, as indicated by the blue lines within the gray areas in the figure. After applying FDTW and IDTW, variations in ERP across epochs were reduced, leading to an increase in the amplitude of common ERP components. N100 and P200 (or overlap with P300) were more distinctly identified in the red and black lines. However, FDTW enlarged the overall oscillatory components of the signal, and this tendency was more pronounced in the standard stimulus, where the ERP amplitude was smaller. In contrast, IDTW exhibited fewer high-frequency fluctuations compared to FDTW and primarily emphasized characteristic ERP components such as N100 and P200. This is because IDTW repeatedly aligned each ERP and removed high-frequency components, allowing common ERP components to be more effectively reflected in the average ERP. This example demonstrates the advantages of IDTW over FDTW in a noisy environment. IDTW enables the derivation of ERP epochs with aligned common ERP components and reduced high-frequency fluctuations, which can be effectively utilized for biomarker extraction.Fig. 4Comparison of average ERPs resulting from FDTW and IDTW.

The prefrontal cortex is associated with higher-order cognitive functions, including working memory, attention, top-down regulation, and decision-making34. Beta-band activity in EEG is known to be associated with cognitive processing, attention, and long-range neural communication, and plays a crucial role in processing sensory stimuli. Due to these characteristics, beta-band features have been widely used in studies of cognitive decline35,36. In this study, IPC-based connectivity measures were employed to capture beta-band functional interactions within the prefrontal cortex while minimizing the effects of volume conduction. To assess the usefulness of frequency components other than the beta band, variants of the proposed biomarker were calculated for these components and compared (Table 5). In the table, DiCohI represents an index derived from raw ERPs after applying IDTW, rather than from specific frequency band components. DiCohTI and DiCohAI correspond to the biomarkers for the theta and alpha bands, respectively. When compared to the proposed biomarker (DiCohBI in Table 2), the differences between the HC and MCI groups for the three biomarkers were small, showing lower absolute values of Cohen’s d and t-statistic. P-values were larger than 0.05 for all biomarkers, and there was no statistical significance in distinguishing the two groups. In summary, the proposed biomarker is based on beta-band IPC, which reflects task-related neural responsiveness associated with cognitive processing. It is motivated by the observation that the average IPC responses to standard and target stimuli differ between HC and MCI groups during the auditory oddball task. Because target stimuli require increased cognitive and attentional processing compared to standard stimuli, both groups exhibited higher \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$MiCohBI_t$$\end{document} than \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$MiCohBI_s$$\end{document}. When considering only standard stimuli, the HC group showed higher \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$MiCohBI_s$$\end{document} than the MCI group, suggesting reduced functional connectivity and impaired predictive processing in MCI40. In contrast, for target stimuli, the MCI group showed higher \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$MiCohBI_t$$\end{document} than the HC group. In this study, this phenomenon is interpreted as a compensatory mechanism41,42. Specifically, when increased neural processing is required, the HC group can achieve adaptive responses through efficient prefrontal communication, whereas the MCI group may exhibit inefficient network connectivity due to neuronal degeneration, leading to increased compensatory activation. The proposed biomarker is designed to emphasize these contrasting patterns between standard and target conditions across groups. In addition, the proposed framework incorporates an IDTW-based ERP refinement step, which reduces noise and jitter variation, particularly relevant when using dry electrodes, thereby enhancing the extraction of common ERP components.

Limitation and future works

The data used in this study were collected exclusively from Korean participants within a relatively uniform clinical setting, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Although the proposed biomarker demonstrated significant discriminative performance between the HC and MCI groups, factors such as genetic background, cultural lifestyle differences, and variations in clinical diagnostic standards for MCI could potentially influence physiological signals and, consequently, the performance of the biomarker. Therefore, further validation using multi-national and multi-center datasets is necessary to more rigorously assess the robustness and generalizability of the proposed method. To achieve this, the following steps are required: (1) establishing collaborations with multiple centers across different countries to acquire data from participants with diverse ethnic and cultural backgrounds; (2) applying the proposed biomarker to multi-center datasets to analyze the effects of ethnicity, cultural background, and differences in signal acquisition procedures on its performance and stability; (3) Implementing data normalization techniques and calibration methods to account for variability across different clinical sites and recording equipment; and (4) Conducting a comprehensive assessment of the biomarker’s performance within a global clinical environment to ensure its reliability. Through these efforts, the clinical applicability and scalability of the proposed biomarker in real-world settings can be more clearly determined, further refining it into a more universal indicator for MCI screening.

Regarding the clinical utility of the proposed biomarker, it is important to note that while the differences between the HC and MCI groups were highly significant (p < 0.001), the observed effect size (Cohen’s d = -0.2245) is relatively small. This suggests that the proposed biomarker, when used as a standalone feature, may have limited utility for definitive clinical diagnosis. Nevertheless, the proposed biomarker captures a distinct aspect of MCI-related neurophysiological changes, as indicated by its low correlation with previously reported biomarkers. This complementary characteristic suggests that it may provide additional value when integrated with other cognitive or physiological measures. Therefore, rather than being used as a standalone indicator, the proposed biomarker is more appropriately applied as part of a multimodal framework for MCI detection. Various machine learning or deep learning methods can be explored, and future studies may also investigate model architectures that integrate the proposed biomarker with other EEG-based, demographic, or questionnaire-based indices to achieve synergistic effects.

Additionally, the analysis was conducted using data recorded from the prefrontal cortex in this study. However, patterns of coherency may vary when using data from other brain regions. To verify this, we plan to collect validation data with an extended set of channels in future work. Participants were classified into two groups: HC and MCI. Still, it is possible to further subdivide the MCI group into aMCI and naMCI, or to include the dementia group for extended analysis. Such an approach could provide additional insights into brain activity associated with the progression and characteristics of MCI. IDTW was devised to extract the proposed biomarker, and its benefits were demonstrated in the results. Nevertheless, further validation is necessary to determine whether the devised IDTW can serve as a general pre-processing method to enhance the reliability of various biomarker extractions. For instance, although IDTW is capable of reducing high-frequency fluctuations more effectively than FDTW, the removed components may carry useful information for other biomarkers. To explore this possibility, further analysis of the characteristics and potential trade-offs of IDTW is essential. Related research can be pursued in the future, through which IDTW could be utilized to improve the reliability of other biomarkers.
