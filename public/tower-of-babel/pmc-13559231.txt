Experience of biological implants in thoracic surgery for soft tissue and chest wall reconstruction in clean and contaminated environments
Authors: Hannah Jesani, Veena Surendrakumar, Hasanali Walji, Mostafa Ahmed, Luis Hernandez-Arenas, Laura Socci, Antonio Martin-Ucar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBiological mesh has become an integral tool in surgery for reconstructive procedures. Acellular dermal matrices derived from porcine or bovine collagen scaffolds provide a durable implant material for use in reconstruction of the chest wall, and soft tissues including the pericardium and diaphragm. We aimed to present our experience of biological meshes used for thoracic reconstructive procedures for a wide range of clinical indications by a single surgeon, comparing the effect of contaminated and clean surgical environments and implant materials.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed all patients who had undergone thoracic surgery reconstructive procedures under a single surgical team with biological mesh material over a 16-year period from March 2009 to April 2025. Data included indication for surgery, operative procedure, surgical site, biological implant material (cross-linked or non-cross-linked), postoperative infection, in-hospital mortality and reoperation rate. We directly compared the results between patients undergoing the procedure in infected/contaminated versus clean cases.ResultsIn total 152 consecutive procedures were performed utilising 212 patches. Median age was 65 years (range, 24–84 years) and majority treated were males 104/152 (68%). Indications for surgery included total pleurectomy for mesothelioma (38%), resection of extended thoracic malignancy (33%), surgery for trauma or related complications (19%), and benign conditions including adult congenital abnormalities (10%). Cross-linked biological mesh had a significantly reduced surgical site infection rate compared to non-cross-linked biological mesh (n=6; 7% vs. n=16; 23%, P=0.008). This remained significant following adjustment for key clinical confounding factors [adjusted odds ratio (OR) 0.289, 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.105–0.793, P=0.02]. Direct comparison of contaminated environments versus clean showed surgical site infections postoperatively were not significantly different (24% vs. 12% P=0.09). No biological patch necessitated explantation due to infection. In-hospital mortality and reoperation rates remained low at 4% and 9% respectively.ConclusionsOur study evaluates and supports the continued use of biological patches for thoracic reconstruction of soft tissues and chest wall. Biological patches have a role in a diverse range of surgical indications including extended resection of malignancy and traumatic injuries and can be feasibly used in high risk contaminated surgical fields.Keywords: Biological implants, chest wall reconstruction, diaphragm reconstruction, acellular dermal matrices

Highlight box

Key findings

• This 16-year single surgeon cohort (n=152, 212 patches) demonstrates biological patches can be feasibly used in thoracic reconstruction across a wide range of indications, including diaphragm, pericardial, and chest wall reconstruction. Overall surgical site infection rates were acceptable (14%), with low in-hospital mortality (4%) and reoperation rates (9%). Importantly, outcomes in contaminated or high-risk surgical fields were comparable to clean cases, with no statistically significant increase in infection (24% vs. 12%, P=0.09). Cross-linked biological meshes were associated with significantly lower infection rates compared to non-cross-linked materials (7% vs. 23%, P=0.008) which remained significant following adjustment for key clinical confounding factors (adjusted odds ratio 0.289, 95% confidence interval: 0.105–0.793, P=0.02).

What is known and what is new?

• Biological meshes, including acellular dermal matrices, are increasingly used in thoracic reconstruction due to their biocompatibility, tissue integration, and resistance to infection, particularly in contaminated environments where synthetic materials may be contraindicated. However, existing evidence is limited, with small cohorts and minimal comparative data between different biological mesh types.

• This study adds one of the largest longitudinal analyses in this field. It supports the application of biological meshes in complex and contaminated thoracic surgical reconstructive procedures.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Biological patches may be considered in selected contaminated or high-risk thoracic fields, but further prospective and multicentre studies are required to validate these findings and inform optimal material selection.

Introduction

Biological implants also known as biological meshes or acellular dermal matrices have become an integral tool in thoracic surgery, offering a versatile and durable solution for reconstructing defects in the chest wall, diaphragm and pericardium (1-3). Derived from acellular dermal matrices from porcine and bovine-derived collagen scaffolds these materials provide a biocompatible, non-synthetic alternative to traditional prosthetic materials. These materials have become increasingly used over their synthetic alternatives (4). Unlike synthetic meshes biological patches integrate with the host tissue allowing for neovascularization, remodelling and resistance to infection making them particularly useful in contaminated or high-risk surgical fields (4-6). One contraindication to use of synthetic patches is a contaminated, infected environment due to their high propensity to form a bacterial biofilm which resists treatment with solely intravenous (IV) antibiotics and necessitates mesh removal. Synthetic meshes recognised complications include mesh infection, mesh erosion, nerve damage and adhesion formation. This has been reported among many surgical specialties (7-9). The incidence of local infection following chest wall resection and reconstruction displays considerable disparity, reported range has been 2% to 23% (10-12).

The use of biological patches in thoracic surgery spans a range of indications including reconstruction following extended oncological resection, repair of traumatic injuries, and reinforcement of weakened or defective tissue. In chest wall reconstruction, biological meshes help restore the structural integrity while reducing risk of infection and foreign body reaction and can be utilised when replacing infected synthetic prosthesis (13). By providing stability biological implants aid in chest wall mechanics, ventilation, and protection of underlying organ structures (14). In diaphragmatic repair they provide a durable and flexible structure particularly in cases where the defect may be too large to close primarily. Similarly in pericardial reconstruction, biological patches offer a safe alternative for restoring pericardial integrity while minimizing adhesion formation and prevent cardiac herniation following extended resection (15).

This large cohort study aims to evaluate the short and long-term outcomes of patients who have undergone thoracic reconstruction with biological patches under a single surgeon. The study will assess the versatility of use, clinical outcomes and feasibility of biological patches in both clean and contaminated environments. Studies have compared biological implants and synthetic prosthesis for reconstruction and better understanding of the efficacy of these materials may further define their role and benefit in improving patient outcomes.

To our knowledge, this is the largest retrospective cohort analysis of patients who have undergone thoracic reconstruction using solely biological patches. By reviewing a comprehensive dataset spanning 16 years this study aims to provide a detailed evaluation of the clinical applications, long term outcomes and wide utilisation in thoracic surgical procedures in both clean and contaminated high risk surgical fields. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-0955/rc).

Methods

We conducted a retrospective cohort analysis of all consecutive patients who have undergone reconstruction of soft tissue or chest wall by a single surgeon. Procedures were performed over a 16-year period from March 2009 to April 2025 in 3 different hospitals. Data was inputted into a prospective clinical database. Surgeries included a diverse range of surgical indications and procedures, including extended pleurectomy and decortication for mesothelioma, extended thoracic malignancy resection, surgery for traumatic injuries to the chest wall or diaphragm, and other benign or congenital conditions.

Data included patient demographics, comorbidities, pre-operative lung function, surgical approach, procedure, biological patch type used for repair, indication, length of stay, reported complications, need for reoperation, and follow-up. Data were included from previously published manuscripts (6). This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the University Hospitals Coventry and Warwickshire Research (No. SE0406). All other participating hospitals were informed of and agreed to this study. As this study involved retrospective analysis of anonymized data as part of routine clinical care, individual informed consent for data collection was waived. Clinical images included in this manuscript have been fully anonymized. Informed consent for their use and publication was obtained from the respective patients.

Patient selection

All patients who received a biological collagen mesh during any thoracic procedure within the study period were included. Four different patch products were used during this study period. Patients were identified through review of operative records, from a single surgeon practice. Exclusion criteria included patients who underwent procedures where solely synthetic patch material was used for reconstruction or primary closure was utilised instead of patch repair. We did include patients who required simultaneous rib fixation using rigid prosthesis and biological mesh repair.

Data collection

Patient demographics, surgical indications, intraoperative details, and postoperative outcomes were collated from surgical logs, patient medical records and departmental prospective databases. Specific variables recorded included patient characteristics, surgical indication and procedure, complication rates, contamination of surgical fields, patch related complications, length of stay, reoperation, and long-term functional outcomes. Perioperative antibiotic administration was not consistently captured within the retrospective dataset and therefore could not be formally analysed. Institutional practice consisted of a single prophylactic IV antibiotic dose in elective cases at induction of anaesthesia, with continuation of antimicrobial therapy determined on an individual clinical basis according to operative findings and postoperative course. In the emergency setting with known contaminated surgical fields, perioperative IV antibiotics were administered during anaesthetic induction and continued during inpatient stay.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive analysis was used to summarize patient demographics, indications, and postoperative outcomes. Comparisons were made between patches used in contaminated and non-contaminated surgical fields. Statistical analysis was conducted to determine the association between patch use and surgical outcomes, including surgical field. Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 30 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Categorical variables were analysed using Pearson Chi-squared test. Student’s t-test was used for normally distributed continuous variables and Mann-Whitney U for non-parametric data. Binary logistic regression was used to assess the association between clinical indication (oncological, trauma and benign disease), urgency (elective and urgent/emergency), patch type (cross-linked and non-cross-linked), patch location (diaphragm involvement) and contaminated field effect on post-operative infection rates. Adjusted odds ratios (aORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated. A two-sided P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics

There were a total of 152 procedures utilising a total of 212 biological patches within this period. The majority treated were male with ratio 2:1 (104 male vs. 48 female). Median age was 65 years (range, 24–84 years). Some patients (57/152, 38%) received more than 1 patch during the procedures. The range of number of patches used per patient was 1–3. Median length of stay was 10 days (range, 1–104 days). There were 6 (4%) in hospital deaths in total. Pre-operative lung function analysis showed median forced expiratory volume in 1 second (FEV1) was 76% (range, 38–114%) and transfer factor for carbon monoxide (TLCO) was 76% (range, 46–128%). Operations were electively performed in 123 (81%) and 29 (19%) procedures were undertaken urgently or as an emergency. A total of 34 (22%) procedures were performed on patients with contaminated surgical environments and high risk for infection. Contaminated spaces were defined as patients with perforated viscus, reoperation, trauma with abdominal contents in the chest, patients with known bronchopleural fistulae, clinical and radiological evidence of infection, and where foreign bodies were present in the chest. The remaining 118/152 (78%) cases were performed in clean surgical fields.

Early postoperative outcomes

Post-operative surgical site infection rate among the total cohort was 14%. This included patients with surgical wound infection, post-operative pleural effusion with evidence of infection requiring intervention, and empyema. In the clean cohort (n=118), there were 2 wound infections, 3 empyema’s, and 9 infected pleural effusions. In the contaminated cohort (n=34), there were 3 wound infections, 4 empyema’s, and 1 infected pleural effusion. Following surgery, admission to the enhanced care [intensive care unit (ICU)/high dependency unit (HDU)] in the cohort was 37/152 (24%). The reoperation rate was 9% (n=14/152). Indications for reoperation included 4 patients requiring empyema debridement, 2 redo diaphragmatic hernia repairs, 2 diaphragmatic patch disruptions requiring early repair, 1 return to theatre for bleeding, 1 patient requiring patch release, 1 patient returned for repair of persistent air leak, 1 patient required oesophageal stenting from injury from previous Belsey fundoplication surgery, 1 patient underwent further chest wall mass resection and there was 1 revision of skin flap due to flap failure. Causes of in hospital mortality (n=6, 4%) was reported as complex bronchopleural fistula in 2 patients, pneumonia in 2 patients, 1 patient had respiratory failure and 1 patient developed sepsis from a splenic abscess. Patch-related outcomes

There were in total 6 (4%) patch related complications with 2 patch disruptions, 1 patch was too tight which necessitated patch release and 3 later recurrences of diaphragmatic or chest wall hernias. Patch disruptions required prompt reoperation for reinforcement of the biological patch which was kept in place using additional peri costal sutures. The case requiring patch release manifested following pericardial patch placement where the patient developed clinical signs of cardiac constriction requiring return to theatre. Via a video-assisted approach 2 sutures were released to allow for decompression and the patient stabilised. Two patients with recurrent diaphragmatic hernias underwent re-operation and successful re-repair.

Comparison of biological patch material

We performed a comparative analysis of the cross linked and non-cross linked biological patch cohorts shown in Table 6. This included baseline comparisons, post-operative surgical site infections and reoperation rates. Postoperative surgical site infection occurred in 6 of 81 patients (7%) receiving cross-linked mesh compared with 16 of 71 patients (23%) receiving non-cross-linked mesh. Pearson’s Chi-squared test demonstrated a statistically significant association between mesh type and postoperative infection (χ2(1)=6.995, P=0.008). Cross-linked mesh was associated with significantly reduced odds of postoperative infection compared with non-cross-linked mesh (OR 0.275, 95% CI: 0.101–0.748). There was also an increased length of hospital stay in the non-cross-linked group.

Multivariable logistic regression and sensitivity analyses

Multivariable binary logistic regression was performed to primarily evaluate factors associated with postoperative surgical site infection in order to adjust for confounding factors, shown in Table 7. After adjustment for surgical field contamination and operative urgency, cross-linked mesh remained independently associated with reduced odds of postoperative infection compared with non-cross-linked mesh (adjusted OR 0.289, 95% CI: 0.105–0.793, P=0.02). Reconstruction in a contaminated surgical field was associated with increased odds of infection (adjusted OR 2.509, 95% CI: 0.837–7.524, P=0.10), although this did not reach statistical significance. Operative urgency was not independently associated with postoperative infection (elective surgery adjusted OR 1.528, 95% CI: 0.402–5.801, P=0.53).

An exploratory sensitivity analysis was performed with additional adjustment for surgical indication (oncology, trauma and benign pathology). The overall indication variable was not independently associated with postoperative infection (Wald χ2=3.096, df =2, P=0.21). Cross-linked mesh remained associated with reduced odds of postoperative infection compared with non-cross-linked mesh (adjusted OR 0.376, 95% CI: 0.126–1.126), although this association no longer reached statistical significance following adjustment for indication (P=0.08). The direction and magnitude of effect remained broadly consistent with the primary model (adjusted OR 0.289, 95% CI: 0.105–0.793, P=0.02), suggesting that the observed protective association of cross-linked mesh was not fully explained by differences in surgical indication.

We also performed a sensitivity analysis of patch site effect on post-operative infection rates with additional adjustment for diaphragm involvement. Cross-linked mesh remained independently associated with reduced odds of postoperative infection compared with non-cross-linked mesh (adjusted OR 0.257, 95% CI: 0.086–0.769, P=0.02). Diaphragm involvement was not independently associated with postoperative infection (adjusted OR 1.367, 95% CI: 0.441–4.237, P=0.59).

This analysis has showed cross-linked biological patches had a reduced surgical site infection rate compared to non-cross-linked material, in univariate analysis (n=6; 7% vs. n=16; 23%, P=0.008). When adjusted multivariate analysis was performed including case urgency, patch site location and contaminated space, the significance of reduced post-operative infection rate with cross-linked patches compared to non-cross-linked persisted.

Discussion

Thoracic reconstruction of the soft tissues and chest wall plays a vital role in the multidisciplinary management of both malignant and non-malignant intrathoracic pathology. These procedures often involve high-risk surgical environments and carry significant potential for morbidity. Over the last 15 years, there has been a gradual paradigm shift in the use of biological patches for reconstruction, particularly in contaminated or complex surgical fields where synthetic materials may not be suitable (6,13,14). This large, single-surgeon cohort study represents a comprehensive evaluation of outcomes following thoracic reconstruction using biological patch materials.

Our results demonstrate that biological patches were successfully used in a diverse range of thoracic procedures and anatomical locations, with reasonable complication and reoperation rates considering the extent of surgery. Importantly, a substantial portion of procedures (22%) were performed in contaminated or high-risk surgical environments, underscoring the versatility of biological patches in complex thoracic reconstruction. When comparing clean and contaminated space surgical site infective complications including post-operative empyema, wound infection, and pleural effusion with evidence of infection the proportion was higher in the patients with contaminated spaces (24% vs. 12%), however this difference did not reach statistical significance (P=0.09). The increased physiological burden and microbial load present in these complex cases makes them challenging to treat and despite this, outcomes remained acceptable. No biological patch-related infections necessitated immediate removal or re-operation due to infection, highlighting the robustness and tensile strength of biological materials in managing infected fields and integrating into the host, supporting the practical feasibility of biological patch use in contaminated settings. We recognise the diverse pathologies treated in this cohort including mesothelioma, emergency traumatic injuries and elective congenital cases likely represent distinct immunological and inflammatory environments, each of which may influence tissue healing, susceptibility to infection, and biological patch incorporation. Consequently, outcomes relating to biological patch integration and postoperative infective complications should be interpreted within the context of this clinical heterogeneity. We have addressed this by performing adjusted multivariate binary logistic regression analysis.

Although urgent procedures are generally considered to carry a higher risk of postoperative infection, operative urgency was not independently associated with surgical site infection in the present study. This finding should be interpreted cautiously given the relatively small number of urgent cases (n=29) and the wide CI surrounding the estimate. Furthermore, urgency and field contamination were closely related within the cohort, potentially limiting the ability to distinguish their independent effects in multivariable analysis.

Our data also demonstrates variability in infection rates between patch types. Porcine cross-linked collagen matrix was associated with the lowest rate of postoperative surgical site infective complications (7%), while non-cross-linked porcine mesh demonstrated higher rates (24%). The observed differences in surgical site infection rate between cross linked and non-cross-linked biological patches was statistically significant (7% vs. 23%, P=0.008). Material properties such as cross-linking, porosity and preparation method may influence their susceptibility to infection. Our multivariate analysis confirmed that with adjustment for surgical field contamination, and clinical urgency, cross-linked mesh had a significantly less surgical site infection rate (P=0.02). Additional exploratory sensitivity analysis with procedure indication still identified cross-linked mesh had a reduced risk of surgical site infection, however this no longer reached significance (P=0.08).

The principal structural difference between cross-linked and non-cross-linked biological patches lies in the collagen architecture of the extracellular matrix. Cross-linked patches undergo chemical treatment, commonly using agents such as glutaraldehyde, to create additional covalent bonds between collagen fibres, increasing resistance to enzymatic degradation and mechanical failure (16). This results in a denser, more rigid matrix which may confer greater resistance to mechanical stress, microbial invasion and enzymatic degradation. Non-cross-linked patches, by preserving a more natural matrix architecture, generally permit greater host fibroblast migration, angiogenesis, and constructive tissue remodelling, which may facilitate biological integration with the host tissue (17), however are considered higher risk of degradation particularly in mechanically demanding reconstructions. The structural differences between cross linked and non-cross-linked materials may influence their integration to host tissue. These findings are consistent with other surgical literature that emphasizes the infection-resistant properties of cross-linked matrices, particularly in contaminated environments (12,18,19). We recognise other confounding factors such as comorbidity data and other operative factors not included in our multivariate model may also have contributed to these differences.

Interestingly, the overall reoperation rate across the cohort was relatively low (9%) and did not significantly differ between cross linked and non-cross linked patch materials (P=0.41), suggesting that while infection risk varies, this does not necessarily translate into a higher need for surgical revision. Most reoperations were due to empyema debridement or mechanical complications, rather than direct patch failure. Only 6 (4%) cases of patch-related complications were reported, with 3 early complications including two patch disruptions and one case requiring patch release. There were also 3 late recurrences of herniation. This low rate of patch complications supports the longstanding durability of these materials, especially with our analysis over a substantial period.

Another key finding is that patients in the contaminated group experienced longer hospital stays (median 13 vs. 9 days, P=0.002), which again reflects the increased complexity and morbidity of these procedures. However, in-hospital mortality remained low at 4%, comparable across clean and contaminated groups, and consistent with or better than published series for complex thoracic reconstructions (12).

An additional consideration is the cost associated with biologic mesh implantation. Biological meshes are substantially more expensive than conventional synthetic materials (20). Our findings support the role of biologic mesh as a potential adjunct in complex or contaminated reconstructions rather than as a universal substitute for synthetic mesh as widespread routine use may not be feasible. While the present study was not designed to perform formal cost-effectiveness analysis, the clinical utility of biologic materials may be greatest in carefully selected high-risk scenarios, particularly in contaminated or potentially contaminated operative fields where synthetic mesh infection, explanation, fistulation, or chronic wound complications may carry considerable morbidity. Therefore, the decision to utilise biologic rather than synthetic material should likely remain individualised, considering patient comorbidity, degree of contamination, defect complexity, reconstructive goals, and surgeon experience. Further prospective comparative studies incorporating health economic evaluation are required to better define the patient populations in whom biologic mesh use is clinically and economically justified.

This study’s strengths lie in its comprehensive scope, long follow-up, and consistent surgical technique from a single operator. We have directly compared surgical environments at high risk of post-operative complication and patch failure and results support the use of biological patches in these complex cases.

Limitations of the study include its retrospective nature and lack of randomization. A limitation of this study is the potential for era bias given the long retrospective study period spanning 16 years. During this time, management strategies for complex soft tissue reconstruction and biologic mesh implantation evolved substantially, including changes in surgical technique, patient selection, biological mesh availability, cost and perioperative optimisation. These temporal changes may have influenced clinical outcomes independently of mesh type. A single surgeon study can also limit external validity.

In addition, comparison between cross-linked and non-cross-linked biologic meshes may be subject to temporal selection bias. Patch selection was likely influenced by availability, cost and clinical judgment and did change over time. Certain mesh types were preferentially used during different periods of the study, reflecting evolving institutional practice patterns, surgeon preference, and product availability. Consequently, patients receiving different mesh types may not represent directly comparable cohorts, and observed differences in outcomes may partially reflect differences in treatment era rather than intrinsic mesh characteristics alone. For the purposes of analysis, patch materials were grouped under a category cross-linked vs. non-cross-linked, and product-level subgroup analysis was not performed. This approach reflects real-world heterogeneity but limits evaluation of potential differences between individual manufacturers’ products.

Another limitation is that the observed associations between biologic mesh type and clinical outcomes may be influenced by other confounding variables including patient comorbidity, defect complexity and evolving perioperative management strategies which were not included in our multivariate analysis. Additionally, data on certain possible cofounding factors such as body mass index (BMI) was incomplete across the study cohort, limiting formal statistical assessment. Variation in postoperative antibiotic duration between patients which has not been formally analysed may have influenced infective outcomes and represents a limitation of this retrospective study. Consequently, causative interpretation of differences between groups should be made cautiously. Although, we did try to adjust for major confounders with multivariate and sensitivity analyses.

Given the heterogeneous anatomical indications for biologic patch reconstruction in this cohort (diaphragmatic, chest wall, and pericardial repairs), no single site-specific validated quality-of-life instrument was applicable to assess post-operative function. Therefore, future prospective studies should incorporate validated health-related quality-of-life measures such as EQ-5D and SF-36 Health survey to enable cross-domain comparison. Post-operative lung function was also not routinely performed, and this would be interesting for future work to assess.

These limitations are inherent to long-term retrospective analyses and should be considered when interpreting comparative outcome data. The study may have been underpowered to detect statistically significant differences in post-operative outcomes. Future prospective or multicentre studies with contemporaneous comparison groups would be valuable in validating these findings with further risk adjusted analysis, isolating the independent effects of mesh type on clinical outcomes and defining optimal material selection.

Conclusions

In conclusion, biological patches were feasible for thoracic reconstruction in an extensive and versatile range of complex surgical scenarios within this large single-surgeon experience including contaminated surgical fields. Infection, reoperation and in-hospital mortality rates were acceptable considering the complexity of the cohort and no biological patch required removal due to infection. Cross-linked materials were associated with a lower rate of postoperative infectious complications compared with non-cross-linked materials when adjusted for key clinical confounding factors; however, this finding should be interpreted cautiously because of the retrospective design, potential selection bias and lack of adjustment for all potential confounding factors. Biological patches may be considered in selected contaminated or high-risk thoracic fields, but further prospective, multicentre and comparative studies are required to define optimal biological material selection and establish their role relative to alternative reconstructive strategies.
