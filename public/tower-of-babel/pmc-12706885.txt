Understanding the Ethiopian policy landscape on abortion services using a health policy triangle framework
Authors: Negash Wakgari, Delayehu Bekele, Stuart J. Watson, Mekitie Wondafrash, Zoe Bradfield, Gizachew A. Tessema
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe World Health Organization emphasises the importance of prioritising women’s health and rights in abortion policies. In 2005, the Ethiopian government revised the legal framework to expand the circumstances under which abortion is legally permitted. This study investigated and described the current policy landscape in Ethiopia over the last two decades.MethodsInformed by the Walt and Gilson health policy triangle framework, this study employed a complementary document content analysis and key informant interviews. In our study, a policy document was defined as a document that involved laws, policies, policy directives, national strategies, clinical guidelines, and working documents. To identify relevant policy documents, we searched the Ethiopian Federal Ministry of Health (FMoH) website, national academic institution platforms, and the grey literature. We also contacted stakeholders working at the FMoH to identify if there were relevant documents that had not been published online. Furthermore, we undertook key informant interviews with individuals actively involved in policy reform or implementation processes. Participants were selected using purposive sampling techniques. A semi-structured questionnaire was used during the interviews, and both deductive and inductive thematic analyses were employed. Findings are presented under four predetermined themes: context, actors, process, and content.ResultsEight policy documents were included in the final synthesis and analysis. Political transition, the government’s desire to avert high maternal deaths, and civil society pressures were key contextual factors that triggered the policy reforms. The Federal government, civil society and a few international organisations were frequently mentioned as actors in policy reforms. Following the 2005 legal reform that lessened the hurdles for receiving safe abortion services, the FMoH initiated a range of implementation programmes aimed at expanding abortion services to primary facilities and private sectors. Despite the enabling legal framework, access to abortion services remains challenging due to persistent stigma, lack of awareness about the legal provisions, and the burden placed on healthcare providers associated with policy dilemmas.ConclusionsEthiopia has made significant strides in liberalising abortion laws, but access to safe abortion services remains a major challenge. Comprehensive strategies that align with international reproductive health rights are recommended to improve policy implementation.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12939-025-02722-w.

Introduction

Abortion remains a contentious global issue affecting women’s reproductive health rights [1, 2]. Due to restrictive and inconsistent policies, unsafe abortion has been identified as one of the most preventable causes of maternal death [3]. For example, the World Health Organization (WHO) reported that unsafe abortions cause up to 13.2% global maternal deaths annually [4]. In 2012, the WHO made a strong recommendation that countries’ abortion policies should prioritise women’s health and rights, and suggested that policy blockades that impede access to abortion care should be removed [5, 6]. Effective national delivery of abortion care extends beyond health service delivery alone; it involves a broader structural and legal environment that plays a role in shaping abortion services. Thus, laws and policies related to abortion are integral components of the overall context within which individuals seek and receive such care [4, 7].

The WHOs abortion care guidelines, published in 2022, emphasise the significance of ensuring access to equitable abortion services [4]. The guidelines highlight the importance of supportive policy frameworks for ensuring reliable abortion services and creating an enabling environment for individuals seeking abortion care. Recognising that a person’s whole environment influences their access to care and the quality of their experience, the guideline considers broader factors such as social, cultural, and legal contexts [8].

Effective public discourse on this issue needs to consider the complexities involved and respect the diversity of perspectives on reproductive rights and healthcare [7, 9–11]. The provision of safe abortion services provides women with the option to make decisions about their reproductive health, potentially reducing the associated physical and emotional trauma. Moreover, supporting the right to safe and voluntary abortion is often framed as an essential component of reproductive autonomy given it allows women to make decisions about their bodies, lives, and futures based on their circumstances and values [12, 13].

Despite these recommendations, 1.64 billion women of reproductive age reside in countries around the world where safe abortion is criminalised [1, 5, 14]. Most countries with the strictest abortion policies are located in low- and middle-income countries, leading to increased risks associated with unsafe abortions [15, 16]. Such restrictive laws and policies not only elevate the risks associated with abortions but also impact the collection and dissemination of official statistics regarding these procedures. In nations with stringent abortion policies, providing comprehensive abortion care becomes ethically and morally challenging. This often results in dilemmas for healthcare providers and contributes to the stigma associated with abortion, which results in poor outcomes for women [17]. Research indicates that ambiguous abortion laws and policies intensify dilemmas between adhering to legal regulations and providing essential care, affecting the healthcare system and care outcomes [15, 18–20].

Abortion policies may affect the healthcare system, healthcare providers, and women’s health [13, 21]. For example, the impact of abortion laws on healthcare providers involves complex interactions with professional identity, patient care, communication practices, and the potential conflicts arising from aligning personal or professional values with legal obligations. This holistic perspective is essential for a comprehensive understanding of the implications of abortion laws for healthcare professionals and women’s health [22]. The lack of access to adequate reproductive health services and restrictive abortion policies are identified as contributing factors to the prevalence of unsafe abortions [23, 24]. In regions where abortion is restricted, the potential for negative mental health outcomes may be higher due to the circumstances surrounding the procedure [25, 26].

A retrospective case study conducted to outline actors and processes involved in policy reform lacked a clear methodological approach to document analysis [27]. It did not consider health policy frameworks in the methods and address abortion policy contents well. This case study focused only on the 2005 legal reform and not all ongoing development in the area as it is based on the data prior to 2012. In addition, as results of the former study were drawn from interviews conducted in 2007 and 2012, while several abortion policies were developed and implemented after this period, describing the current Ethiopian abortion policy landscape is crucial to improve and keep maternal health on track [28, 29]. This approach provides a comprehensive and methodologically rigorous examination of abortion policy within the broader context of reproductive health rights and the implications of policy development and implementation, contributing to evidence-based policymaking and practice.

Methods

Study design and setting

We conducted a complementary policy document review and key informant interviews [30]. This study was conducted in Ethiopia, the second-most populous African country with an estimated population of more than 135 million [31]. The Ethiopian health system is a three-tier system with primary, secondary, and tertiary levels of care. At the community level, there is a primary care, which focuses on preventive care, health promotion, maternal and child health services, and treating common illnesses. Secondary and tertiary levels provide inpatient and ambulatory services and a more extensive range of specialised medical and surgical services [32–34]. Maternal mortality decreased from 673 per 100,000 live births in 2005 to 401 per 100,000 live births in 2019 [28, 35]. The contraceptive prevalence rate is 41%, and about one-quarter of pregnancies end up with abortion in Ethiopia [28, 36].

Procedure and analytical frameworks

The READ (Ready your materials, Extract data, Analyse data, and Distil your findings) approach was adopted to collect policy documents and obtain data from the document systematically [30]. Walt and Gilson’s health policy triangle framework [37] was used to underpin our analysis for the policy and programs landscape in Ethiopia [38]. This framework identifies four key components: Namely policy context, actors, process, and content [39] (Fig. 1). In our study, context was defined as national or international legal, political, economic, social, and cultural factors that could trigger the development of existing policies. The actors involved individuals, policymakers, communities, groups, institutions, or the government during the specific policy development. The process describes how these policies were formulated and brought into implementation. Content refers to the matters in the evaluated policy documents, including what areas of the issue the policy addresses [40, 41].Fig. 1Policy triangle framework, adapted from Walt and Gilson, 1994 [37]

Policy triangle framework, adapted from Walt and Gilson, 1994 [37]

Policy documents eligibility criteria

In this study, we define abortion policy documents as official documents, including the laws, legal framework, policies, policy directives, strategies, and implementation documents such as clinical guidelines, training manuals, and regulations, as well as draft working documents [30]. We included policy documents published since 2005 as this marks the time when the Ethiopian government changed its legal framework to broaden the circumstances for undertaking abortion services in health facilities.

Document identification and searching

Policy documents were sourced through comprehensive searches across Ethiopian government and academic websites, as well as grey literature published up until March 2025. First, websites for the Ethiopian Ministry of Health, national academic institutions’ platforms, development agencies, civil society groups, and non-governmental organizations actively involved in maternal health programs were searched. Key terms like ‘safe abortion,’ ‘induced abortion,’ ‘legal abortion,’ ‘comprehensive abortion care,’ ‘policy,’ ‘laws,’ ‘legislations,’ ‘strategy,’ ‘guidelines,’ and ‘Ethiopia’ were meticulously explored in various combinations in the identified websites. Additionally, we contacted stakeholders at the FMoH to acquire policy documents that were not readily available or identified through online searches.

Data extraction

Twelve documents were initially identified; four were deemed ineligible, and hence the remaining eight were included in the final analysis. We summarised the documents chronologically to outline the key milestones in the policy development process. Then, relevant information or data points were systematically collected and transferred into predefined policy models to ensure the consistency and relevance of the extracted information. Predefined checklists were utilised to understand the policy context, actors, process, and contents and to extract the data. Literature and health policy triangle framework were reviewed to develop the checklists [30, 37, 42]. Data checklists are provided as an additional file 1.

Key informants’ recruitment

Purposive sampling techniques were applied to identify key informants based on their involvement during policy and guideline development, interests, and availability. A letter of support for this study from the Saint Paul Institute for Reproductive Health and Rights was submitted to the FMoH and the Head of the respective organisation participating in the research to get further permission and list potential participants in their institution. With the support of the FMoH and the head of the respective organisation participating in the research, we listed the names of individuals who participated in abortion law reforms in 2005 or were involved in the policy implementation process. The first author contacted key informants listed as potential participants. Priority was given to individuals who had participated in policy development since 2005 or those involved in policy implementation. All participants who were approached and expressed interest were interviewed. Informed consent was obtained prior to proceeding with the interview.

Data collection tools and procedures

A semi-structured questionnaire was developed by reviewing the literature and health policy triangle framework [37, 39, 43]. The interview guide primarily focuses on key aspects of national abortion policies, process, context, content, and the perceived impact of abortion policies on women’s access to abortion services. The tool was also translated into Amharic to ensure ease of understanding for participants who speak the language. During the interviews, a voice-recorder was utilised to capture the respondents’ voices.

Analysis

Deductive and inductive thematic analysis was utilised to manage and interpret the collected data [44, 45]. Specifically, data extracted from policy documents underwent categorisation into thematic areas aligned with the health policy triangle framework, encompassing content, actors, process, and context dimensions. Additionally, the data obtained from the key informant interviews underwent thematic analysis, facilitating triangulation with secondary data sources for a comprehensive understanding and validation of findings.

The data from the policy document was analysed and evaluated using several key data quality dimensions, including completeness, internal consistency, external comparisons, and external consistency. In addition, triangulation with documents, member checking, and peer debriefing were used to enhance qualitative rigour [30, 46–48], and “Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research” in writing was considered [49].

Themes

Findings are presented under four predetermined themes: context, actors, process, and content, based on the methodological framework.

Context

The context of the Ethiopian abortion law reform is described under this theme. These are described in two sub-themes: Political transition and maternal health policies, and Legal and Policy reforms on women’s rights and abortion post-1991.

Political transition and maternal health policies

The transition from the Derg government to the new political regime led by the Ethiopian People’s Revolutionary Democratic Front (EPRDF) in 1991 marked a significant shift in Ethiopia’s political landscape [50, 51]. The EPRDF initially created a more conducive environment for civil society to operate and advocate for change. This shift in political leadership allowed various stakeholders, including civil society organisations, to engage with the government on women’s rights and healthcare issues. Over time, this collaboration led to the implementation of policies and programmes aimed at reducing maternal mortality, including efforts to address unsafe abortion practices through legal reforms, increased access to reproductive healthcare services, and public awareness campaigns [27]. One participant explained the impacts of policy reform, saying: “Before the policy reform, young girls were admitted and suffered from unsafe abortion complications, and some of them died. You know, 32% of maternal deaths were attributed to unsafe abortion before the policy reform” [P#3].

Before the legal reform, abortion was performed only to save a woman’s life, leading to high rates of maternal mortality, with a massive portion attributed to complications from unsafe abortions [52–54]. The maternal mortality ratio was 687 per 100,000 live births in 2005 [55] and significantly reduced after reforms to 412 in 2016 [56] and 267 in 2020 [6]. One participant supported this: “While I was attending my education at Tikur Anbesa Hospital, there was a septic ward … .where postabortion complicated cases were admitted. That ward was foul-smelling. You observe women admitted with complications … It was painful. That septic ward is closed nowadays; you do not see complications as before” [P#4].

Legal and policy reforms on women’s rights and abortion in post-1991

The reform was supported by the government’s commitment to global human rights treaties, such as the Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination Against Women [57] and the Protocol to the African Charter on Human and Peoples’ Rights on the Rights of Women in Africa, also known as the Maputo Protocol [58]. These international agreements served as a crucial basis for the amendments to the penal code. A participant explained the context as follows: “I feel there was strong willingness from the governments … also there were civil societies from Ethiopia who were feeding the policymakers the attributes of unsafe abortion to maternal deaths. The EPRDF was progressive and open to change at the time; they had a strong willingness for any changes in all aspects, not only abortion policy … I do not think that there was external pressure for abortion policy reforms” [P#1].

Ethiopia’s healthcare system has significantly expanded following the political reforms of 1991 [59, 60]. This involved expanding primary care services and the concept of task sharing, which involves delegating certain healthcare tasks to new cadres of healthcare workers [61, 62]. The new government developed the 1995 constitutions [63], which gave priority to women’s empowerment; in line with this, there was a formidable internal willingness to revise the 1957 penal code [64]. A participant described the process as follows: “It was an entire change, not only reforming abortion policy, the EPRDF developed the new constitution and amended several national policies to align with the 1995 constitutions. This was a great opportunity to liberalise abortion policy” [P#2]. A detailed contextual description can be found in Bridgman-Packer, D., and Kidanemariam, S. (2018) [65].

Actors

Stakeholders involved in the law reform are discussed in this theme under two sub-themes: Multisectoral collaboration in Policy reform and religious opposition and public debate in abortion law reform.

Multisectoral collaboration in policy reform

The law’s revision was driven by collaboration among representatives from the medical, political, and women’s rights sectors. These groups united to gather data and present compelling evidence to policymakers regarding the health and societal impacts of unsafe abortion. Their goal was to propose measures aimed at reducing maternal deaths and upholding the rights enshrined in Ethiopia’s constitution, notably Article 35 [63, 65]. The contribution of individuals and professional associations to policy reform was highlighted. One participant mentioned the contribution of the Ethiopian Society of Obstetrics and Gynaecologist (ESOG) as follows: “ESOG generated the evidence, they used health rationale to convince policymakers, they reported and published several admitted with unsafe abortions, their contribution to maternal death and the burden to the hospitals” [P#5].

The government, national civil society organisations such as ESOG and the Ethiopian Women Lawyers Association (EWLA), and a few international organisations were frequently mentioned as actors in policy reform. One participant said: “Besides the government’s willingness to reform policies, national civil society such as ESOG, EWLA, and few international non-governmental organisations (NGOs), including IPAS-Ethiopia, MSIE, strongly supported policy reform” [P#4]. One participant summarised the government’s position on women’s empowerment and policy reform: “The Ethiopian government was progressive at the time; they were revising several policies and were open to change, particularly regarding women’s empowerment and rights …” [P#5].

Religious opposition and public debate in abortion law reform

All major religious groups, such as leaders from the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church, Muslims, and Evangelicals, hold official opposition to abortion, except in cases where it is deemed necessary to save the life of the woman [66]. The interview participant also explained as follows: “In the initial draft of the penal code, the committee proposed abortion on demand, sparking significant opposition from religious leaders … Subsequently, as the first draft of the penal code was made open for public comments, a vigorous debate ensued through government media platforms between proponents and opponents of the proposed reform. Those against the reform disseminated misleading information via the media, formed an alliance with religious leaders, and invested considerable effort in undermining support for abortion policy reform”[P#7]. Another participant supported this: “Some international NGOs, such as USAID and religious leaders, were against abortion policy reform” [P#4].

Process

The procedures of policy reform undertaken, progress, and policy implementation challenges are addressed under this theme. Three sub-themes: Legal evolution and Policy reform; progress in safe abortion Policy and reproductive health reforms; and challenges in the implementation of Ethiopia’s abortion Policy were used to describe the theme.

Legal evolution and policy reform

Ethiopia’s abortion law from 1957 allowed abortion only in cases of “grave and imminent danger to the life of the woman” and mandated approval from two physicians [64]. This requirement posed a significant challenge to safe abortion services and a more receptive environment created to improve women’s health and rights in the 1991 political transition [27]. This period saw the adoption of progressive national health policies and the drafting of a new constitution in 1995, signalling a commitment to women’s rights and freedoms [63]. This is supported by qualitative findings. “I am usually interested in describing it as the mechanism of abortion law reforms: it took about five years. The Ethiopian Parliament initiated an overhaul of the Penal Code in 2000 and ordered the Ministry of Justice to develop the first draft of the abortion policy. The initiation was mainly due to the development of the new constitution in 1995. Article 35 of the constitution discusses women’s rights, so the government needed to reform the abortion policy to make it align with the new constitution” [P#6]. In 2003, several organisations formed the Advocacy Working Group (AWG) to coordinate their efforts to liberalise the country’s abortion laws [67, 68] and established a legal framework successfully in 2005 [69, 70].

Progress in safe abortion policy and reproductive health reforms

In 2005, Ethiopia liberalised its abortion laws, resulting in a remarkable change in abortion policies and a notable decline in maternal morbidity attributed to unsafe abortions [28, 71]. After the penal code was liberalised, the FMoH initiated a bold implementation programme aimed at facilitating the introduction and expansion of safe abortion services. This program involved several key components, including publishing the technical and procedural guidelines for safe abortion services in 2006 and its subsequent editions [29, 72].

There was a need to capacitate midlevel professionals to provide quality abortion services in all health facilities, including private institutions. To do this, the Ministry developed the “First Trimester Comprehensive Abortion Care Training Manual” in 2013 and the revised version in 2018 [73]. This was a significant step in improving access to safe abortion services, particularly in rural areas where access to physicians may be limited. In 2021, the Ministry developed a training manual for the second trimester of abortion care for the first time [74].

In the same fashion, the FMoH developed a national reproductive strategy aimed at addressing the reproductive and sexual health needs of Ethiopia’s diverse population in 2006, 2016, and the most recent one in 2021 [59, 75]. In addition, the Ministry developed its first National Adolescent and Youth Reproductive Health (AYRH) strategy in 2006, second in 2016, and recently in 2021 [76, 77].

The Ethiopian Ministry of Health technical working group developed self-care intervention guidelines [78] that were aligned with the WHO self-care guidelines [79]. This Reproductive, Maternal, Neonatal, Child, Adolescents, and Youth Health-Nutrition (RMNCAYH-N) guideline was finalised in, 2024 and is awaiting the Ministry’s endorsement. A standard treatment guideline for hospitals was initially developed in 2004 and revised in 2010 and 2014 [80].

Challenges in the implementation of Ethiopia’s abortion policy

Despite progress, ensuring universal access to safe abortion remains a significant challenge in Ethiopia. One participant depicted the recent policy implementation challenges as follows: “The global gag rule followed by COVID-19 destroyed the healthcare system providing abortion. Safe abortion service is not like treating malaria; it demands psychological and value clarification. By its nature, abortion services require support. Institutions providing abortion services are financially handicapped, and the service is deteriorating. You need money to train providers. Nowadays, the number of public health facilities providing safe abortion care is dramatically declining due to a lack of support. Private facilities are struggling with it, anti-choice groups are boldly working, and providers are stigmatised” [P#5].

Cultural, religious, and provider values must be revised to implement the policy fully. Though training was provided for health care providers, there is still peer pressure against the providers, and the service is stigmatised. One participant said: “Value clarification is important. Some providers decline abortion services after providing services for a year. They mention peer pressure in the facility as a reason. Sometimes, they give names and tag abortion providers. As a Ministry, following abortion law reforms, we took several actions; we developed the first technical and procedural guidelines in 2006, prepared training materials for safe abortion services, which capacitating providers followed to provide quality abortion care … .then we focused on commodities and supplies” [P#3].

Study participants depicted that lack of awareness about abortion policies and implementation guides among healthcare providers is due to providers’ high turnover and the silent strategy that the FMoH is following to implement the policy: “Policy reform is not only essential for addressing the issue but also requires effective popularisation and dissemination. It has been approximately twenty years since we liberalised this policy, yet there remains a lack of consensus among actors. We need a plan to promote awareness of our abortion policy right now. Additionally, there is a concerning rate of provider turnover; despite training efforts, retention remains low. It is evident that the Ministry is quietly pursuing the implementation of the abortion policy, likely to mitigate the influence of anti-choice groups” [P#4]. Content

Ethiopian abortion policy contents are summarised in this theme. These are explained by two sub-themes: Legal framework and limitations of Ethiopia’s abortion Policy and implementation challenges and gaps in Policy documents. In addition, the details of the Ethiopian Abortion legal framework and implementation guides are presented as an additional file (additional file 2).

Legal framework and limitations of Ethiopia’s abortion policy

The 1995 Ethiopian constitution article 35 pointed out that women are entitled to affirmative measures to remedy historical inequality and discrimination and have the right to full consultation in formulating national development policies, especially those affecting women’s interests. Overall, this article emphasises women’s rights, promotes their well-being in various aspects of life, and founded the 2005 Ethiopian abortion law reform [63].

Abortion was discussed in articles 545 to 552 of the Ethiopian penal code. Article 551 discussed circumstances under which abortion was legally permitted, while the remaining articles described the action or punishment for a crime against abortion. The penal code allowed abortions to save a woman’s life (Art. 551/1/b), to maintain physical (Art. 551/1/b) and mental health (Art. 551/1/d), for pregnancy due to rape or incest (Art. 551/1/a), and for foetal impairment (Art. 5551/1/c/b) [70]. In line with this, one participant said: “We did it; in our context, it was difficult to move beyond this. All actors, including the government, tried to make abortion on demand. You know, you always need to understand your context. We have good abortion policies today, relative to some countries” [P#1]. In support of this statement, another participant stated as follows: “If we succeed in establishing abortion on demand, we must anticipate opposition from groups such as religious leaders who may strongly oppose this change and push for a return to the previous policy …” [P#7].

Article 545 outlined the legal framework regarding the intentional termination of pregnancy. Article 546 described the punishment level as follows: if a pregnant woman intentionally procured abortion, she is punishable with simple imprisonment, and any person who assists the pregnant woman in procuring the means of abortion or aids her in the abortion process will also face punishment and can be considered either a principal criminal or an accomplice and will be subjected to simple imprisonment. One participant commented this as follows: “Abortion requires considering social and religious perspectives, not only healthcare. Some say Ethiopian abortion policies are liberalised; no, it is still restrictive, but under restricted policies nowadays, we do have certain criteria to consider abortion relative to the former penal code” [P#5]. Another participant said: “It was a great opportunity to remove abortion from the penal code. Abortion is reproductive healthcare. Why in the penal code? It needs to be in the right place. We missed the opportunity to remove it from the penal code, and we intend to have the current policy to mediate the opposition groups’ feelings” [P#6].

Article 547 established the legal consequences for individuals involved in performing or assisting in abortions on another person, considered various circumstances such as consent, coercion, and the capacity of the pregnant woman to understand her actions. Similarly, Article 548 addressed aggravated cases of abortion where the procedure is performed outside the circumstances provided by law. Articles 549 and 550 addressed the attempt to procure an abortion on a woman wrongly supposed to be pregnant and the consideration of extenuating circumstances such as extreme poverty in determining punishment for abortion-related offenses, respectively.

Article 552 outlined the procedure for abortion under specific conditions, the penalty for violating the procedure, and the implementation plan. This article stated that the FMoH is mandated to issue a directive shortly outlining the procedure for abortion under the conditions specified in article 551, and the directive should ensure that abortion is carried out in a manner that does not adversely affect the interests of pregnant women. However, the existing policy did not fully solve the problem, and women could not make their own decisions due to moral and ethical dilemmas, stigma, a lack of awareness about eligibility for safe abortion services, and a vague policy [81–84]. This is also indicated in the qualitative findings, as the senior staff commented on. “We do not have the system to create awareness about abortion policy. Usually, partners take this responsibility and are not observed by the government. Full policy implementation requires community awareness of the policy. Let alone the society, you can see some providers are not aware of the 2005 abortion policy. We are facing challenges in this regard. As it is a very contentious issue, the government wants to refrain from entertaining it in the media (Radio or Television). Still, it is highly stigmatised” [P#2].

One notable aspect of the article was its recognition of a woman’s testimony as sufficient evidence in cases of rape or incest. This provision helps to streamline the legal process for obtaining an abortion in such circumstances, acknowledging the sensitive nature of these situations and the importance of trusting the woman’s account [51, 63, 65, 70]. A participant provided an overview of the current legal status of Ethiopian abortion policies as follows: “The 2005 Ethiopian abortion laws take health considerations into account but did not address socio-economic factors, which encompass various elements such as income level, family size, marital status, and occupation. The inclusion of underage or minor cases does not fully encompass this complexity. Currently, abortion is generally treated as a criminal act under Ethiopian law … There remains a lack of widespread awareness regarding the specifics of these policies; many professionals are aware of the criminalization of abortion but lack clarity on permissible circumstances. Our penal code outlined legal consequences not only for women seeking abortions but also for providers and anyone aiding or facilitating abortion procedures” [P#3].

Implementation challenges and gaps in policy documents

The FMoH developed the “Technical and Procedural Guidelines” in 2006 and its second edition in 2014, incorporating medical abortion, second-trimester abortion, task shifting and sharing, and healthcare tier that was not described in detail in the first edition [29]. The main components of this guideline were procedures available for abortion, the legal framework surrounding abortion services, care before, during, and after the procedure, and referral procedures, healthcare skills and performance, and abortion services based on the level of healthcare facility or provider offering the service.

After eight years, the ministry drafted the third edition in 2022 and is awaiting endorsement and launch by officials. The rationale for developing the third edition of the guideline is stated to align with the recommendations made by recent WHO abortion care guidelines and the second national health sector transformation plan (HSPT-II), reproductive health (RH) strategies, other national relevant guidelines, and to accommodate the progressive changes made in the health system and update with scientific evidence. This procedural guideline included additional components such as provider-assisted self-care medical abortion, mobile health team approach for hard-to-reach areas, and safe abortion care in the humanitarian situation, considering the recent Ethiopian context [85].

The guidelines outlined the specific types of healthcare facilities authorised to perform abortion procedures at different gestational ages, ensuring that appropriate levels of care are provided based on the stage of pregnancy. Also indicated is that a woman who is eligible for pregnancy termination should obtain the service within three working days. These guidelines authorised all healthcare facilities to provide safe abortion care services. However, this needs to be implemented, as reported during interviews with senior staff. A participant said that “though the guidelines state that all healthcare facilities (100%) should provide safe abortion care, as reported in 2016 emergency obstetric and newborn care assessments, only 41% of the facilities have been providing the service” [P#6].

The technical and procedural guidelines lacked the mechanism of dissemination and popularising the reformed policy to the community to make women in remote areas access the services. The mechanism to mitigate legal and moral value conflicts that healthcare providers may face while interpreting policies to offer abortion services was not addressed.

The “First Trimester Comprehensive Abortion Care Training Manual” was developed in 2013 and 2018 [73], spoke to each other and referred to the criminal code and technical and procedural guidelines. The manuals contained details about women-centred abortion care and reproductive health rights, value clarification and attitude transformation, abortion laws in Ethiopia, technical and procedural aspects of surgical and medication abortion, logistics, post-abortion care, and skill assessment checklists.

The “Second Trimester Abortion Care Training Manual” [74] was aimed to realise a skilled second-trimester medical abortion healthcare workforce and advocate for women’s reproductive health rights. The manuals contained details about medical abortion, reproductive health rights, value clarification, and attitude transformation, abortion laws, technical and procedural aspects of medication abortion, logistics, and skill assessment checklists.

The draft RMNCAYH-N self-care guidelines developed by the Ministry and awaiting endorsement were aligned with the WHO self-care guidelines [79]. This draft guideline indicated that self-care for medical abortion is up to 10 weeks based on the eligibility for abortion as stated in the policy [86]. This guideline recognised the self-use of abortion medication (misoprostol and mifepristone) that was included in national essential medicine lists in 2015 by an authority [87]. It discussed the use of mifepristone and misoprostol medication without the direct supervision of a provider. According to these guidelines, services should be provided after gestational week determination and counselling by a trained provider in a healthcare facility [86].

A standard treatment guideline developed for hospitals restated abortion legal frameworks [80]. However, the guideline recognised and listed essential medicines for medication abortion but did not cite necessary references. This standard guideline under treatment objectives highlighted that providers must ensure compliance with legal requirements for abortions, including exploring the reasons for abortion requests to ensure they align with legal criteria. The more healthcare workers try to explore the reasons for abortion requests, the more they may discourage women from getting abortion services, and these statements may create spaces for professionals to deter women from receiving care.

Ethiopia has developed three national reproductive health strategies since abortion law reforms [59, 75]. This strategy promotes reproductive health services in the country. The strategic plan for RH for 2021–2025 in Ethiopia aimed to reduce maternal mortality to 277 maternal deaths per 100,000 live births. The 2021–2025 strategic plan also contained “zero preventable maternal deaths and morbidities,” which may include unsafe abortion [75]. The recent national RH strategy aimed to improve the reproductive health of women and their families through twelve strategic directions. The strategic plan also decided on performance targets to increase the proportion of health centres and hospitals providing “safe abortion care as per the law and guidelines from 43% to 100%.” Ensuring capacity building and strengthening safe abortion services at all levels and increasing women’s involvement in decision-making processes related to RH services [75].

Similarly, following the reform of abortion laws, the Ministry developed the National Adolescent and Youth Reproductive Health (AYRH) strategy aimed at having healthy and productive Ethiopian adolescents and youth. This strategic plan outlined the contribution of unsafe abortion to maternal deaths and puts enhancing abortion services for adolescents and youth as a priority intervention [76, 77]. In the recent AYRH strategic plan of Ethiopia, unsafe abortion was identified as among the top three health issues for ages 15 to 19 and was planned to reduce the pregnancy-related death rate for the specific age group. However, the document did not highlight why unsafe abortion is still a problem after two decades of abortion law reform, the knowledge gaps in abortion policies among young people, or the procedures to be taken to avoid abortion stigma in society at this age [77]. There is still an enormous awareness gap in the community about the existing abortion policies, leading to implementation gaps. Supported by qualitative findings: “Still, our community does not have information about the criteria by which abortion is allowed. So still, you may see abortion performed outside clinics, and clandestine abortion is being performed; collaboration is needed to create awareness among the community regarding the policy” [P#1]. Discussion

This study analysed the Ethiopian Policy landscape, focusing on the provision of abortion services and implementation of safe abortion programs following the significant reforms introduced in 2005. Prior to these reforms, Ethiopia had highly restrictive abortion policies that only allowed abortion to save the life of the woman [52, 65]. These restrictions were contributed to high rates of unsafe abortions, and thereby high rates of maternal mortality particularly for women in rural areas [53, 55]. The 1991 political transition and the new constitution in 1995 signalled a commitment to women’s reproductive rights [50, 51, 63]. The 2005 legislative reform was largely driven by the government’s recognition of the need to reduce maternal deaths and by sustained pressure from civil society organisations [69, 88, 89]. While the political transition marked a positive shift towards women’s rights and healthcare in Ethiopia, challenges persisted in the implementation of the reforms [52, 81].

Despite the passing of two decades since the enactment of the law, public awareness regarding the legal status of abortion remains limited, which was reflected when only 5% of women have comprehensive knowledge about abortion policies [90]. In line with this finding, despite liberal abortion policies in Zambia, poor knowledge of abortion laws hinders access to abortion care [91]. Evidence also indicated that popularising and disseminating abortion legal context is necessary to improve service access [92]. Moreover, while legal reforms allow broader access to abortion care, some facilities were not providing abortion services in Ethiopia. This finding is similar to the studies conducted in South Africa [93, 94] and Zambia [95, 96]. Limited resources, inadequate infrastructure, a lack of trained healthcare providers, and stigma have contributed to gaps in service provision [97–99].

Ethiopian abortion law article 551/2 addressed situations where grave and imminent danger necessitates immediate intervention. Healthcare providers were authorised to terminate pregnancy for women whose medical conditions warrant immediate intervention. This empowers providers to make timely and informed decisions regarding managing urgent medical situations. What matters is the way providers interpret conditions that warrant immediate abortion, which could be influenced by their knowledge, skill, and attitude toward safe abortion services. This is keeping providers as gatekeepers and decision-makers of services, overlooking women’s reproductive health rights [4, 82, 83, 97, 100]. The law, while providing some latitude for abortion-seeking women, creates conflicting concerns and burdensome responsibilities for providers and may allow delegitimising and stigmatising abortion services [83, 101].

Though abortion is reproductive healthcare [4, 5, 18], it is still in the 2005 Ethiopian penal code and punishable [70]. It is debatable and unclear how the three key elements of woman-centred abortion care [29] -choice, access, and quality of care can be respected from a reproductive health rights perspective, while abortion care is in the penal code and not fully considered as a healthcare [4, 5]. In addition, this policy obliges women to consider distorting the truth or lying to obtain access to abortion services. If a woman’s case does not fall within the regulatory framework, the woman will resort to telling service provider what they think is required to get access to abortion services, leading to potential moral injury [102]. Ultimately, recognising the complexity and deeply personal nature of decisions surrounding abortion underscores the importance of supporting individuals in making choices that align with their values, circumstances, and well-being, which would be addressed by availing abortion based on informed consent and on request without requiring a reason [8, 52].

The current Ethiopian abortion policies can be criticised for not entirely considering economic or social reasons and not making abortion available on request [52]. Examples such as South African abortion policies which were more liberalised compared to Ethiopian abortion policies, included social and economic circumstances and the right to access first-trimester abortion care upon request [103].

The Ethiopian abortion law simplifies the process for age-based abortions by requiring only the stated age of the individual seeking the abortion, eliminating the need for additional documentation or proof of age [52, 65, 70]. This may still be a challenge to access services, particularly in traditional and illiterate communities with diverse educational backgrounds where individuals may not be aware of the policy framework. Therefore, the guide could contribute to age-based discrimination against women’s rights and requiring women to know about ‘the right thing to say’ to access care [4, 97, 104, 105].

The Ethiopia abortion policy emphasises that the woman requesting safe abortion services does not necessarily need to be in a state of ill health at the time of the request. Instead, it is the responsibility of the healthcare provider in charge to assess the woman’s condition and determine, in good faith, whether the continuation of the pregnancy or the birth of the foetus poses a threat to her health or life. Health is viewed as a state of wholeness where individuals are not only physically well but also mentally, socially, and spiritually balanced. It is described as a relative state, implying that it is subjective and varies among individuals, creating confusion about legal frameworks as reported in former study in sub-Saharan Africa [105]. Health encompasses more than just the absence of disease and includes the ability to function optimally in various aspects of life [106–108]. This policy assigns considerable decision-making power and accountability to individual practitioners. As a result, these practitioners may grapple with ethical challenges when making decisions related to abortion, considering factors such as the well-being of the woman seeking abortion, legal requirements, and their own moral beliefs and values [82, 83, 97]. Therefore, it is crucial for legal frameworks to be comprehensive, effectively implemented, and aligned with international human rights principles to advance women’s health [109–111].

Moreover, it also provides specific guidance for providers when addressing cases where a pregnant woman’s physical or mental deficiency, or her minority status, renders her physically and mentally unfit to bring up the child. It emphasises the importance of assessing women for any disabilities that may impact their ability to care for themselves and their children. Due to the multiple types and complex nature of the disability, providers may focus only on physical disability and miss intellectual, emotional or psychological disability, keeping providers at the forefront of decision-making on behalf of women [112, 113]. Diagnosing some types of disability may also require additional skill [114, 115], adding another unnecessarily significant responsibility for some professions providing abortion services, reflecting the gap in the policy [97]. Ethiopian practitioners involved in abortion-related matters experience moral dilemmas due to the significant authority, burden, and responsibility placed on them by the law, led to personal judgments and interpretations [82, 83]. This is in line with the study conducted in Rwanda [20] and the Zambian context, where ambiguous abortion policies are limiting access to safe abortion services [96].

Strengths and limitations

The strengths of this study lie in the robust application of the methodology, which scaffolded an intentional review of the policy landscape, further expounded by key informant interviews. Some limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, although we have extensively searched government websites and approached relevant individuals working at the FMoH to obtain policy documents that were not readily available through online searches, this study may not include all existing abortion policy documents, owing to the variable nature of document cataloguing and the sensitive nature of this research topic. Second, while triangulating document analysis with qualitative interviews strengthened the interpretation, the number of key informants was relatively small and limited to those who were available and willing to take part in the study. The recruitment of more representatives from diverse sectors may have extended the information we received regarding the context and process of developed policy documents. Finally, while we anticipate participants would remember major events/discussions, there may be a recall bias associated with the evidence in the policy process and context-related information for older policies.

Conclusions

While Ethiopia has made significant strides in liberalising abortion laws, effective implementation, awareness, and access to safe services remain major challenges. Despite expanding circumstances allowing for pregnancy termination, Ethiopian abortion policies are critiqued for not entirely considering economic and social reasons; not making abortion available on request; keeping providers at the forefront of decision-making, removing women’s bodily autonomy and adding unnecessary responsibility on abortion providers. Addressing these challenges requires comprehensive strategies that align with international reproductive rights and recognise abortion as reproductive healthcare, which requires further review of gaps in abortion policy contents and service provision. Continued reform is needed to align national policy to WHO international standards.
