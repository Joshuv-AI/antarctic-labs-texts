Subjective Successful Aging in the Presence of Mobility Disability
Authors: Madina Khamzina, Wendy A Rogers, Nicholas G Castle
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectivesResearchers often define successful aging as freedom from disability and disease, yet the perceptions of older adults living with disability challenge this conception, demonstrating that they can indeed age successfully. This paper adapts a framework of successful aging with disability, basing it on the subjective assessment of key components contributing to success among older adults living with mobility disability due to multiple sclerosis.Research Design and MethodsEmploying a qualitative, theory-grounded methodology, we conducted semistructured interviews with 20 individuals aged 60–75 who live with mobility disability attributed to multiple sclerosis. The open-ended questions explored their perspectives on the aging process, their definition of successful aging, and the coping strategies they employ in navigating challenges associated with age and their condition.ResultsDespite facing mobility disabilities, the majority (16 out of 20) expressed a sense of successful aging, identifying 5 key themes: accepting reality, maintaining a positive attitude, fostering independence, nurturing a social life, and preserving cognitive abilities. They achieved successful aging by focusing on alternatives, relying on external support, having a good attitude and faith, and accepting their challenges.Discussion and ImplicationsThe themes identified in this research contribute to redefining successful aging in future studies and facilitating the development of interventions aimed at improving the quality of life for older adults coping with mobility disability.Keywords: Mobility disability, Multiple sclerosis, Perceptions of older adults, Qualitative research methods

Scientifically driven views on successful aging often overlook the experiences and perspectives of individuals living with disabilities. One early view on successful aging by Rowe and Kahn required “disability-free” aging implying that successful aging is not attainable for those with disability (Rowe & Kahn, 1997). However, as demographics shift toward a larger aging population, including individuals with disabilities, there is a notable focus on reshaping and advocating the idea of framing successful aging in the presence of disability. This focus is supported by studies that explored subjective perceptions of successful aging emphasizing the discrepancy between scientific definitions and lay perspectives, underscoring the significance of attitude, adaptation, social connection, and psychological resilience for attaining successful aging (Knight & Ricciardelli, 2003; Montross et al., 2006; Phelan et al., 2004; Romo et al., 2013; Strawbridge et al.,2002; von Faber et al., 2001). These findings emphasize the need to reapproach the framework of successful aging by including multidimensional factors that contribute to successful aging especially among those with disability.

The distinction between “aging with disability” and “aging into disability” underscores the complexity of experiences among older adults with disabilities and emphasizes the need for tailored approaches to successful aging within this population. People who are aging with disability developed their disability prior to age 60, maybe at birth or during childhood or during adulthood but prior to age 60. As they age, they have additional age-related changes in perception, cognition, and mobility that can exacerbate their disabilities (Mitzner et al., 2018). Individuals who developed disabilities later in life, largely due to age-related changes, are described as aging into disability (Verbugge & Yang, 2002). In the literature scholars often emphasize a lifespan perspective, acknowledging that individuals who are aging with disabilities continue to age and face unique challenges associated with both aging and disability simultaneously (Yeung & Breheny, 2022).

In the United States, mobility disability, affecting approximately one in seven older adults aged 65 and older, is the most prevalent form of disability, with significant implications evident in conditions like multiple sclerosis (MS), where impairment of walking emerges as a prominent consequence, affecting individuals’ autonomy and participation in various aspects of life (Baird et al., 2018; Finlayson & Van Denend, 2003; Kister et al., 2013; Kohn et al., 2014; Okoro et al., 2018). Consistent with the principles of Selective Optimization with Compensation model of successful aging (Baltes & Baltes, 1990), older adults with MS may prioritize their strength and abilities to compensate for their weaknesses and confront challenges. For instance, older adults with MS can establish realistic and meaningful goals encompassing physical, cognitive, emotional, and social well-being, striving to achieve them despite challenges posed by their condition, thereby enhancing their sense of control, autonomy, and overall life satisfaction as they age.

Broader qualitative research underscores that achieving successful aging, characterized by higher levels of subjective well-being and self-rated health, remains feasible even in the presence of disability (Montross et al., 2006; Pruchno et al., 2010; Romo et al., 2013; Strawbridge et al., 2002; Teater & Chonody, 2020). In line with this, the International Classification of Functioning, Disability, and Health framework emphasized the enhancement of overall health and functionality in individuals with disabilities. This involves the elimination of environmental barriers (e.g., inaccessible built environments) and the provision of facilitators (e.g., access to assistive equipment and technology; WHO, 2001). Such measures aim to improve the interaction between individual behaviors, such as participation in various life situations, health conditions, and the broader physical, social, and attitudinal environmental factors (Mitra & Shakespeare, 2019; Mitzner et al., 2018; WHO, 2001).

The absence of models addressing successful aging among individuals with disabilities underscores a crucial gap in comprehending the multifaceted dynamics of aging within this demographic, emphasizing the need for deeper exploration to foster a more inclusive scientific understanding of successful aging across diverse older adult populations.

Proposing a Framework

Using a qualitative research approach, our goal was to explore the multifaceted nature of self-perception, integrating subjective well-being and self-rated health, which allowed for better understanding of the complex interplay between self-health perceptions and overall well-being among older adults. We believe that incorporating these two components within the framework contributes to objective-free standards and a more comprehensive conceptualization of successful aging within the context of disability.

In addition, our framework recognizes the importance of social and physical engagement. Since the early introduction of the Rowe and Kahn (1997) model, social activity has been identified as one of the dimensions of successful aging that contributes to subjective well-being and overall health, aligning with the indicators of successful aging (Carr & Weir, 2019; Cosco et al., 2014; Lin et al., 2020). In the broader disability literature, social engagement has shown associations with positive psychological functioning (Muller et al., 2012). Engaging in regular physical activity is linked to improved overall health, higher levels of life satisfaction, and enhanced quality of life among older adults, largely attributed to its positive influence on overall health, life satisfaction, and quality of life, as well as its association with reduced risks for multiple chronic conditions and disability (Cho & Cheon, 2023; Cho et al., 2018; Keysor, 2001; Netz et al., 2005; Szychowska & Drygas, 2022).

Our proposed framework, depicted in Figure 1 (Khamzina, 2022), relies on subjective well-being and self-rated health as two key indicators of successful aging, forming the cornerstone of its components. Additionally, we posit two modifiable behaviors, engagement in social and physical activities, as factors that contribute to successful aging. We conducted a population-based analysis of the framework as well as the impact of modifiable factors in an earlier study (Khamzina, 2022). The focus herein was a qualitative analysis to elaborate on this framework of successful aging among those who experience mobility disability due to MS. Our proposed framework emphasizes modifiable behaviors, but it is essential to recognize potential limitations in its alignment with the qualitative analysis. Our intention was to establish a foundation for future research by proposing this framework, recognizing the need for further refinement and enhancement as additional themes emerged from the qualitative analysis. Through in-depth interviews, in this study we explored: (1) the role of social and physical activity for successful aging; (2) emerging areas of older adults’ perceptions and beliefs on successful aging; and (3) strategies used by older adults to adjust to challenges.

Research Design and Methods

Participants

Older adults aged 60 and older were recruited from the Rehabilitation and Engineering Research Center on Technologies to Support Aging-in-Place for People with Long-Term Disabilities participant registry. The inclusion criteria were individuals who (1) are aged 60 and above; (2) self-identify themselves as having mobility disability for at least one decade (i.e., either through use of mobility aid or report having serious difficulty walking or climbing stairs); and (3) speak English.

The study was approved by the University of Illinois Urbana–Champaign and flyers were distributed via email to older adults in a registry. Participants who expressed interest were contacted via email. Eligible older adults were prescreened via a brief phone call, scheduled for interviews if they could use a computer and Zoom, gave verbal consent before interviews, and received a $20 gift card upon completion.

Data Collection

Participants were interviewed individually between July and August 2022 virtually via Zoom due to the continuing coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic. Interviews lasted between 45 and 95 min. The researcher read and entered the participants’ answers into REDCap. Interviews were audio- and video-recorded and further transcribed using Zoom platform.

The interview process encompassed a comprehensive array of questions, both quantitative and qualitative, open-ended explorations related to self-appraisal of health, subjective well-being, social activity, physical activity, and successful aging (full script available from Khamzina, 2022). The interview began with questions covering sociodemographic and health data, adapted from the TechSAge Background Questionnaire (Remillard et al., 2020), gathering pertinent descriptive information on participants aging with long-term sensory and mobility disabilities, self-rated health, memory, functional limitations, and sensory aids usage (Remillard et al., 2020).

The Subjective Well-Being section of the interview was adapted from the National Health and Aging Trends Study (NHATS) population survey’s well-being section. It included 11 items that assess positive and negative affect, sense of control, and psychological well-being (Kim et al., 2016).

The social activity part was adopted from the NHATS study and included six items related to the participation in different social activities such as visiting friends, religious services, participation in clubs/classes, going out, work, and volunteering work in the past month. Frequency of participation was measured on a scale from never (0 days), seldom (1–2 days), sometimes (3–4 days), to often (5–7 days).

Questions regarding physical activity were adapted from the Physical Activity Scale for Individuals with Physical Disabilities (Washburn et al., 2002), comprising seven items assessing participation in various physical activities, tailored for individuals with mobility disabilities. Responses were facilitated by including options such as physical therapy, wheeling, pushing, wheelchair racing, and wheelchair push-ups, with frequency measured on a scale ranging from never (0 days) to often (5–7 days) over the past week.

The last part of the interview examined the perceptions and experiences of successful aging as defined by older adults with mobility disability. This qualitative part included 18 open-ended questions that were adopted from the study by Knight and Ricciardelli (2003). This final part of the interview probed reasoning and perception on adjustments due to disability and changes with age, and personal experience of aging.

Measures

For directional consistency seven items related to subjective well-being (questions on feeling cheerful; full of life; life having meaning and purpose; feeling confident; liking living situation; finding way to do what the person wants; having easy time adjusting to change) were reverse-coded and ranged from 1 to 41 with higher scores representing better well-being. The appropriateness of using a single well-being score for analyzing NHATS data was previously supported by a confirmatory factor analysis and tested the construct validity of the single factor construct (Kim et al., 2016). Responses for the social activity part were coded as “never” = 0 and “seldom” or “sometimes” or “often” = 1 to denote a continuous variable from 0 to 6. To calculate the frequency responses were coded as “never” = 0 and “seldom” or “sometimes” or “often” = 1 to denote a continuous variable from 0 to 7. To calculate the frequency of physical activity participation, responses were coded as “never” = 0 and “seldom” or “sometimes” or “often” = 1 to denote a continuous variable from 0 to 7.

Data Analysis

An inductive thematic analysis was employed to assess older adults’ perceptions of their aging experience through a qualitative explorative design, utilizing data extracted from transcriptions (Braun & Clarke, 2006). Coders initially familiarized themselves with the data by reviewing transcribed text, generating meaningful units independently, and labeling them with codes in Excel spreadsheets. Interrater reliability, aiming for an 80% agreement level, was calculated based on code agreement, with discrepancies resolved through discussion. Codes were then grouped into themes based on similarities and study objectives, and named for clarity.

Results

Participant Characteristics

We recruited 25 older adults with mobility disabilities, primarily stemming from MS, with the analysis focusing on the 20 community-dwelling individuals specifically affected by MS-related mobility issues (Table 1). The majority (80%, n = 16) were retired and lived with a spouse or partner, while 60% (n = 14) were married. Participants’ ages ranged from 60 to 75, with a mean age of 69 (standard deviation [SD] = 4.62). Sixty percent (n = 12) reported incomes of $75,000 or more, and 90% (n = 18) had obtained at least a bachelor’s degree. Nineteen participants relied on walking aids such as canes, walkers, or crutches for independent mobility, with the most common aid being a cane/stick (70%, n = 14). On average, participants engaged in 3.35 (SD = 4.94) social activities and 4.20 (SD = 1.38) physical activities.

Participants mainly engaged in visiting friends or family (85%, n = 17) and going out for enjoyment (80%, n = 16) as their primary social activities, with all participating in stationary activities (e.g., reading, watching TV). Overall, substantial activity levels were reported, with 80% engaging in walking, wheeling, or pushing outside their homes; 80% (n = 16) in light sport activities (e.g., bowling, darts); 15% (n = 3) in moderate sport activities (e.g., double tennis); 25% (n = 5) in strenuous sports or recreational activities (e.g., wheelchair racing, swimming); and 75% (n = 15) exercising specifically to increase muscle strength (e.g., wheelchair push-ups, lifting). Roughly half (45%, n = 9) of participants ranked their health as either “good” or “very good” but none ranked their health as “excellent.” The mean of subjective well-being score was 33.68 (SD = 4.77) with a range from 24 to 40 points.

Perceptions Related to Framework of Successful Aging

Self-rated health

After ranking their health, participants elaborated on their self-health assessment, with most emphasizing physical well-being, including aspects such as health concerns, pain, overall physical condition, and symptoms. Participants who rated their health as “good” or higher highlighted positive aspects of their lives despite facing disease-related mobility limitations: “No real day-to-day worries or pressing issues or problems … I have multiple sclerosis is mild, I feel that my health is good” (Male, 70). Meanwhile, among participants who rated their health as “poor” or lower, participants cited health issues and pain as the reasons behind their ranking. Some participants (n = 4) mentioned that they assessed their health based on their ability or inability to perform various day-to-day activities or overall functioning: “I am unable to drive or walk” (Female, 69) or “It is my ability to do anything without assistance” (Male, 60). Some older adults (n = 3) compared their health to that of others when assessing their health score: “Generally, my health is worse than my peers” (Male, 70) or “I have great shape compared to my friends” (Male, 62).

Subjective well-being

Participants’ subjective well-being was assessed using a single open-ended question: “When you think about your well-being, what comes to your mind?” Their responses encompassed various aspects, including their abilities to perform tasks, independence, family support and interactions, absence of pain, psychological well-being, financial resources, and mental health. Independence notably emerged as a crucial factor contributing to enhanced well-being. For older adults coping with mobility disability, independence was characterized by the ability to make autonomous decisions and perform tasks to the best of their abilities: “I am satisfied that am able to do the things I need to do; I am not a bother to other people” (Male, 60).

Social activity

Despite facing age- and health-related obstacles, nearly all respondents (90%) perceived social activity as either “important” or higher. Their perceptions of social participation encompassed several areas, notably well-being, sense of belonging, and meaning. Participants described how social activities contributed to their well-being, evoking feelings of joy, happiness, and positive experiences: “It is good for your body and mind and your general well-being” (Female, 69). Additionally, being part of a community or interacting with others who shared similar interests fostered a sense of belonging and meaning. They expressed how contributing to a greater community made them feel more socialized, happier, and more active. Few noted that otherwise they felt lonely and aged much quicker: “It is important, I stay active groups on Facebook. If I do not do it, I hibernate quicker” (Male, 66).

Physical activity

The purpose of this qualitative section was to explore thoughts and perceptions on the following questions: “Do you think physical activity is important to you and why?” As observed for social activity, most participants (90%) perceived physical activity as “important” or higher. When participants were prompted to elaborate on the significance of physical activity, two key areas emerged. The first related to the importance for the physical health, which involved aspects such as health improvement, pain management, mobility maintenance, prevention of health deterioration, cognitive stimulation, muscle strengthening, and overall movement: “If you do not move you lose if, if I do not exercise, I end up in a wheelchair” (Male, 65). The second aspect was its significance for mental health encompassing emotional independence, happiness, focus, and mental health maintenance: “It makes a big difference long term for memory, mental health …” (Male, 70).

Meaning of Successful Aging

Eighty percent of participants reported aging successfully when asked directly, prompting a thematic analysis of their explanations and thoughts regarding the meaning of successful aging.

Accepting the reality

Overall, participants largely emphasized the importance of self-acceptance (i.e., acceptance of limitations and challenges), combined with embracement of what is currently available and feasible physically and mentally. One participant stated, “I’m not happy that I can’t do the things that I’ve always been able to, but I suspect that’s true for any 72-year-old … I can’t hike like I used to but there’s nothing I can do about it” (Male, 72). Participants often emphasized the importance of feeling well and being as healthy as possible despite their health challenges due to disability, highlighting the significance of engaging in physical activities and maintaining healthy habits for the quality of life: “I tell my kids all the time that they can have all the wealth in the world, but the richest man is the healthiest man. It doesn’t matter how much money you have you can’t buy health and if your health is bad, you can’t do all the things you really want to do” (Male, 65).

The process of acceptance could also be related to acceptance of physical and mental challenges, such as “… not to be restricted very much by your deficits including mental or physical deficits” (Male, 70). For some this was an inevitable part of aging: “… accepting that we are not here to stay forever. We’re all headed towards death” (Female, 71).

One participant highlighted his acceptance process by comparing his current health condition to what could have been worse, for example, “I manage my difficulty walking but other than the multiple sclerosis and the arthritis I really don’t have diabetes I don’t have high blood pressure pressure, I don’t have high cholesterol … all my numbers are good” (Female, 73).

Positive attitude

Being happy and enjoying life, as well as having an overall positive attitude, were frequently emphasized. Many participants described their experiences as accepting the past and moving forward with a realistic but positive attitude. For example, one described successful aging as trying to avoid being a cranky old person: “If you can age and still be happy, not be a curmudgeon, then you’re doing pretty good” (Female, 81). Participants mentioned that successful aging is being young at heart: “I think it is about being happy. Whatever the aches and pains be the main limiting factor, trying to prevent your world from becoming smaller and smaller and staying young at heart” (Female, 64).

Being independent

This theme encompassed the importance of autonomy such as making decisions on their own or doing things on their own. Physical limitations are barriers, but to remain performing things they used to do or used to like was one of the central thoughts: “In general, it is about living your life, doing all the things that you like, doing and feeling good” (Female, 65).

“… be independent, living along, not rely on people” (Female, 65). This autonomy might require external support, which was discussed later as one of the coping strategies with losses.

Social life

Successful aging as a need to be part of a society, help others, and connect with friends help them to remain was illustrated in the following quotes: “be involved in helping others” (Male, 66). “You’re able to be part of a group you derive joy, positive and self-confidence” (Female, 69). The responses involved engaging with family members they resided with, communicating with friends via calls, and fostering connections through hobbies or volunteer work. During the interviews conducted amidst COVID-19 restrictions, many participants highlighted the difficulty in sustaining social activity and connecting with their support network. They noted that the limitations imposed by the pandemic exacerbated their already constrained social interactions due to disability. Consequently, feelings of loneliness and social isolation emerged as significant barriers, further complicating the pursuit of successful aging.

Cognitive health

Some participants (n = 3) stressed that aging successfully was about maintaining cognitive health. One of the respondents replied that due to physical limitations he does not consider himself as aging successfully; however, he added that from the cognitive health perspective he thinks he is aging successfully: “I know I contradict myself because I stressed my illness and did not account for cognition. Probably I should not put a definite ‘no’ to my answer to aging successfully because it could ‘yes’ from overall perspective and ‘no’ from the point of view of mobility” (Male, 70). Other responses elaborated successful aging as “maintaining your brain functions, not falling prey to dementia or Alzheimer” (Female, 69).

Coping Strategies With Age- and Disability-Related Losses

Participants shared strategies and experiences in adjusting to life changes due to age and disability, attributing challenges primarily to disability rather than age. Four main themes emerged: focusing on alternatives, extrinsic factors, maintaining a positive attitude and faith, and accepting challenges.

Focusing on alternatives

Focusing on alternatives implied compensating for losses, adjusting, and finding alternative ways to age, to exercise, and to be active with mobility disability. One detailed answer clearly elaborated on this: “If you start overdoing it today, you’ll have less of it tomorrow, because you used it today and you tend to push things a little more than you probably should, just like I’m not willing to go quietly into that long night … you still think you’re 35 in your head, you may be just watching rather than participating” (Male, 70).

Another participant was specific about the way they share responsibilities within family members, so that everyone is still able to do their own part the best they can: “I can’t vacuum, I don’t have a fluffy mop to do the floor, but I can do paperwork, pay the bills, work on the computer, being able to do that part of my life has helped me greatly” (Female, 75).

Extrinsic factors

Participants highlighted diverse strategies for coping with mobility challenges, relying on various extrinsic factors including mobility aids and seeking support from their social circle, including friends, spouses, counseling, physical therapists, household aids, and technology. For instance, one participant, a 69-year-old woman, attributed her successful adjustment to disability-related changes to frequent counseling sessions, the encouragement of supportive friends and family, and the effective use of assistive devices:

I got really great little scooter that I can lift myself and put it in out of the car and zips along at five miles an hour and I could use that for photography, also I’ve done a lot of physical therapy trying to improve my gait which hasn’t been successful in that I haven’t I still can’t walk very well, but that’s one of my adapting strategies that doesn’t fix your problems … it makes it better makes me able to walk better than it would, without it. (Female, 69)

Positive attitude and faith

Adopting a good attitude and maintaining faith emerged as a third coping strategy with losses associated with age and disability. This approach encompassed several elements, including cultivating a positive mindset, advocating for oneself in decision making, avoiding self-pity, maintaining faith in a higher power, persevering, and continuously striving for the best. The participants’ sentiments were echoed in the following quotes:

Well, I would say my faith in God and sort of learning and having the attitude that I really don’t have a choice, you kind of have to adapt to continue on. (Female, 65)

It’s very appropriate for aging to allow yourself to be angry about the loss of things and allowing yourself to grieve. (Female, 65)

Acceptance

The fourth strategy was accepting the challenges. This involved making the conscious decision to acknowledge the challenge and take action, alongside embracing the potential of leading life in a different manner. Few were able to acknowledge the losses; they could not completely overcome them. Others highlighted that acceptance was an ongoing challenge, akin to navigating through the stages of grief daily:

… something come up with MS is grieving. You grieve for the loss of things you used to be able to do. So, accepting and completely getting over I guess are two different things. (Male, 65)

Another participant illustrated how some older adults facing mobility disabilities struggle with accepting their losses:

I had to get a new scooter and gave my old one to a friend of mine who has MS and she’s still not able to mentally accept the fact that she should be using it. So, I feel grateful that you know I’ve accepted it. (Female, 72)

Discussion

Our qualitative study, conducted through personal interviews, elucidated the perceptions of older adults with mobility disability due to MS on the components of our proposed framework as well as their perceptions on successful aging. Additionally, we identified the strategies they employed to navigate challenges posed by aging- and disability-related losses.

Older adults with mobility disabilities due to MS approached successful aging from a perspective that emphasized adaptation and acceptance of limitations. Despite facing significant challenges posed by both age and disease 80% of participants felt they were aging successfully. This adaptive approach closely aligns with the Selection, Optimization, and Compensation model proposed by Baltes and Baltes (1990), wherein individuals adapt to aging-related changes by selecting and optimizing resources while compensating for losses. Participants emphasized the importance of personal accomplishments rather than solely evaluating their physical status, highlighting a pivotal aspect of successful aging within the context of disability, which closely aligns with the concepts of life satisfaction (Havighurst, 1961; Ryff, 1982). The experiences of older adults in our sample align with the findings from the study by Wallack et al. (2016), which emphasized social engagement, attitude and outlook on life, and healthy lifestyle choices and habits as modifiable factors that can be positively influenced through targeted self-management strategies.

Research has consistently demonstrated the association between cognitive health and successful aging (Rowe & Kahn, 1997; von Faber et al., 2001), with higher cognitive function predicting better outcomes in areas such as physical health, social engagement, and overall quality of life. In our study only a few participants highlighted the importance of being cognitively healthy to perceive themselves as aging successfully. In the literature cognitive health sometimes associated with social engagement, emphasizing the significance of social interaction for keeping their minds stimulated (Carr & Weir, 2019).

Furthermore, our data shed light on the coping strategies employed by older adults with mobility disabilities to navigate the challenges of aging- and disability-related losses, particularly during the COVID-19 pandemic. The revealed subthemes of coping strategies reflect previous literature findings (Molton & Yorkston, 2017; Romo et al., 2013) highlighting the relevance and applicability of these strategies in the context of mobility disability due to MS. Thus, maintaining a positive attitude, which emerges as a significant subtheme of coping mechanism, resonates with existing literature suggesting that positive thoughts and emotions contribute to overall well-being and good health (Patnaik, 2013). Research suggests that individuals who maintain a positive attitude are more likely to experience successful aging, highlighting the importance of this coping strategy (Badache et al., 2021; Nur Amalina & Yarina, 2017; Rashid et al., 2012).

Trusting in God and faith were noted in perceptions of successful aging by other older adults in other studies (Cernin et al., 2011; Parker et al., 2002; Romo et al., 2013). Similarly, for many individuals in our study, faith and acceptance provide comfort, strength, and resilience in navigating life’s uncertainties. By accepting their disabilities and finding meaning in their experiences, individuals can cultivate inner peace and resilience, enhancing their overall well-being and quality of life.

In our subtheme, relying on extrinsic factors we included factors such as seeking support from external sources including family, friends, caregivers, and community resources as well as leveraging assistive devices. In our study, we found that a significant portion (70%) of participants encountered serious difficulty walking or climbing stairs, with 30% reporting being unable to walk. The most frequently utilized mobility aid was a cane or stick, with 70% relying on this tool for assistance. Thus, the utilization of mobility aids plays a pivotal role in facilitating successful aging among older adults, particularly those facing mobility challenges. People who successfully accommodate these devices can uphold their activity levels and handle tasks independently, without relying on assistance from others (Freedman et al., 2017). This capacity to adapt and maintain quality of life represents a departure from the traditional view of successful aging and could potentially be explored as an additional component within the framework of successful aging with disabilities for future research. In fact, previous literature has proposed a shift from the term “successful aging” to “successful accommodation” (Freedman et al., 2017).

Despite facing limitations such as inaccessible infrastructure and transportation, older adults with disabilities prioritized social engagement, recognizing its importance for overall well-being and successful aging. This aligns with Rowe and Kahn’s model, which emphasized active engagement with life as essential for successful aging (Rowe & Kahn, 1997). Participants encountered additional challenges in leaving their homes due to social distancing mandates during the COVID-19 pandemic. Nonetheless, our study participants recognized the importance, even the necessity, of social engagement during these times and managed to connect with friends and family members who did not reside with them. The presence of a robust social network and proximity to loved ones can significantly contribute to overall well-being and foster successful aging. Interestingly, older adults with disabilities may place an even higher value on social engagement compared to their counterparts without disabilities, potentially due to facing fewer limitations (Jang et al., 2004). Social engagement is pivotal for healthy aging among individuals with disabilities, as suggested by the Health Belief Model, which highlights the influence of perceived life satisfaction and a sense of belonging on one’s inclination to remain socially active (Champion & Skinner, 2008; Couture et al., 2020). Amid pandemic restrictions, finding alternative methods such as leveraging technology becomes essential to combat social isolation and promote healthy aging.

Our study also highlighted the discrepancy between subjective perceptions of successful aging and self-rated health assessments among older adults with mobility disabilities. Whereas half of those deemed successful agers rated their health as either “fair” or “poor,” indicating that subjective successful aging did not necessarily correlate with positive self-health assessments, 45% rated their self-rated health as good or very good—a phenomenon often referred to as the “disability paradox” (Albrecht & Devlieger, 1999; Krahn et al., 2009). Perceptions of health and well-being may not fully capture the aging experience, especially among individuals contending with disabilities (Whitley et al., 2016). Participants highlighted the importance of physical activity in managing function and preventing health decline, noting a decrease in intensity as they aged or faced disabilities, yet remaining motivated by health benefits to prevent further deterioration, consistent with literature linking physical activity to successful aging. Our participants echoed this sentiment, noting that despite their mobility disabilities, remaining physically active contributed significantly to their overall well-being and successful aging experience (Szychowska & Drygas, 2022).

Our sample, comprised solely of older adults with mobility disabilities due to MS, may not represent all diverse mobility disability groups, potentially differing from those with other disabilities. However, the sample was chosen for theoretical reasons to apply the framework of successful aging with disability among the most common type of disability, mobility disability, and the most common cause for disability, MS. Our sample was quite small, which might also limit the generalizability of the findings. Many respondents reported higher income levels ($75k or more) and living with a spouse/partner, potentially influencing their perceptions of aging experiences and their ability to address health challenges compared to those from lower socioeconomic backgrounds. Finally, although we cannot assert theoretical saturation, especially among Black/African Americans, our study offered valuable insights into the concept of successful aging among an increasingly growing segment of older adults with disabilities.

Our study highlights a high level of perceived successful aging among older adults with mobility disability due to MS, emphasizing the need to explore broader implications for practice and policy in aging. Future research should extend this understanding to refine the definition of successful aging for those with other disabilities. Our framework elucidates the role of physical and social engagement in successful aging among individuals with disabilities and holds promise for tailored interventions to optimize their well-being and quality of life across various disability types.

One key implication from the policy perspective is the need to ensure access to essential resources such as mobility aids and allied health services, including physical therapy, counseling, and personal care assistance. Many individuals with disabilities face challenges in accessing these services due to financial constraints or inadequate insurance coverage. Therefore, policies aimed at expanding coverage for these services through Medicaid, Medicare, and private insurance can greatly enhance accessibility and affordability for individuals in need. Moreover, there is an opportunity to provide tailored physical activity equipment for older adults with disabilities, accommodating various mobility levels and disabilities. Investing in specialized equipment can create inclusive and accessible fitness environments, promoting greater physical activity participation both at home and outdoors for this population.

An important implication is that perception of successful aging may differ from conventional health-based metrics, suggesting a need for a more holistic and patient-centered approach by interventionalists and policymakers to promote healthy aging. Our findings highlight the feasibility of achieving successful aging through subjective evaluations, even if individuals do not fit previous research-defined definitions. Participants’ narratives illustrate belief in successful aging by embracing diverse psychological values and deploying coping strategies to manage disabilities, adapt to change, and overcome health challenges and physical limitations.
