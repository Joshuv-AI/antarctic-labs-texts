Correction of Ineffective Erythropoiesis and Normalization of Iron Homeostasis After Exagamglogene Autotemcel in Transfusion‐Dependent β‐Thalassemia

Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTExagamglogene autotemcel (exa‐cel) is a one‐time, ex vivo, CRISPR‐Cas9 gene edited cell therapy approved for patients with transfusion dependent β‐thalassemia (TDT) aged 12–35 years. In a Phase 3 study (CLIMB THAL‐111), exa‐cel treatment resulted in reactivation of fetal hemoglobin and increases in total hemoglobin, leading to transfusion independence in 91% of participants. Here, we report on the impact of exa‐cel treatment on measures of ineffective erythropoiesis and iron homeostasis, which were secondary and exploratory endpoints in CLIMB THAL‐111 and the CLIMB‐131 long‐term follow‐up study. Prior to exa‐cel infusion, all participants were receiving regular red blood cell transfusions and iron chelation therapy. At time of data cut (April 2025), 98% of participants (55 of 56) had been transfusion independent for ≥ 12 months and 38 (68%) had discontinued iron removal therapy (mean duration off iron removal therapy 19.4 months). Following transfusion independence and cessation of iron removal therapy, erythroferrone concentrations decreased and hepcidin levels normalized in all participants, indicating correction of ineffective erythropoiesis and restoration of iron homeostasis. Further supporting this finding, improvements and trends toward normalization were seen in key erythropoiesis biomarkers, including erythropoietin levels, reticulocyte counts, and soluble transferrin receptor concentrations. Iron overload biomarkers, including ferritin, liver iron concentration, and cardiac T2*, decreased and then remained stable throughout follow‐up, even after cessation of iron removal therapy. These results demonstrate restoration of effective erythropoiesis and iron homeostasis after exa‐cel infusion in the setting of transfusion independence following reactivation of fetal hemoglobin. (CLIMB THAL‐111 and CLIMB‐131; Clinical Trials.gov numbers NCT03655678 and NCT04208529).

Introduction

β‐thalassemia is an inherited blood disorder caused by mutations in the β‐globin gene which reduce or abrogate production of β‐chains in adult hemoglobin and alter the α:β globin‐chain ratio in red blood cells (RBCs), resulting in ineffective erythropoiesis and anemia [1, 2]. A proportion of patients with β‐thalassemia require lifelong RBC transfusions for survival [3] and are classified as having transfusion‐dependent thalassemia (TDT), whereas those not requiring regular transfusion are classified as having non‐transfusion dependent thalassemia (NTDT) [4, 5, 6].

During normal, steady‐state effective erythropoiesis, renal peritubular cells secrete erythropoietin in response to hypoxia, stimulating bone marrow RBC progenitor proliferation and maturation into reticulocytes, which are released into circulation (Figure 1). In ineffective erythropoiesis, RBC progenitors undergo apoptosis in the bone marrow during maturation, failing to produce mature RBCs and resulting in a compensatory expansion of the RBC progenitor pool. This elevates levels of erythroferrone, a hormone specifically secreted by RBC progenitors, which in turn suppresses hepcidin in a feedback loop intended to increase iron bioavailability for RBC production. However, in ineffective erythropoiesis, this response results in abnormal and deleterious tissue iron accumulation and systemic iron overload, since the body has no physiological means of excreting excess iron [7]. At the molecular level, hepcidin binds to the membrane protein ferroportin causing its internalization and degradation which subsequently inhibits intestinal uptake of dietary iron and the mobilization of iron from tissue stores (hepatocytes and reticuloendothelial tissues) [8, 9]. Hepcidin production is regulated by several factors including erythropoietic activity, hypoxia, inflammation, and body iron content [9]. Increased ineffective erythropoietic activity, anemia and the resulting hypoxia, suppress hepcidin, resulting in increased absorption of dietary iron and abnormal tissue iron deposition, including in non‐storage organs (heart and endocrine organs). Repeated RBC transfusions can decrease the ineffective erythropoiesis associated with thalassemia, although incompletely [10]. However, repeated RBC transfusions contribute to additional and more rapid iron overload. Thus, for patients with β‐thalassemia, iron overload results from both the ineffective erythropoiesis associated with the disease as well as from repeated RBC transfusions.

Clinically, iron overload can cause severe tissue injury, leading to serious cardiac (impaired contractility and arrhythmias), hepatic (fibrosis, cirrhosis, hepatocellular carcinoma), and endocrine (hypothyroidism, hypoparathyroidism, hypogonadism, diabetes mellitus) complications and end organ damage which is a major contributor to increased morbidity and premature mortality in patients with TDT [7, 11, 12]. Tissue iron is measured using magnetic resonance techniques (R2 or R2*), with the liver iron content representing the best proxy for total body iron. Tissue iron levels guide chelation therapy, which is necessary in all patients with TDT and some with NTDT [13]. Serum ferritin levels are also used as a biomarker of total body iron content, although this is not ideal since ferritin levels are affected by many confounding factors other than iron stores and may not always directly correlate to tissue iron content.

Exagamglogene autotemcel (exa‐cel) is a non‐viral cell therapy that reactivates the synthesis of fetal hemoglobin (HbF) via ex vivo CRISPR‐Cas9 gene editing of autologous CD34+ hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (HSPCs) at the erythroid‐specific enhancer of the BCL11A gene. In the Phase 3 CLIMB THAL‐111 study of exa‐cel in patients with TDT aged 12–35 years, infusion of exa‐cel led to transfusion‐independence in 91.4% of participants, with mean total hemoglobin in the normal range of 13.1 g/dL and mean HbF of 11.9 g/dL with pancellular distribution [14]. The results of CLIMB THAL‐111 raised the hypothesis that in addition to transfusion independence, exa‐cel treatment may also correct ineffective erythropoiesis observed in TDT by reactivating expression of HbF leading to both effective erythropoiesis and normal iron homeostasis.

To address this question, we analyzed key biomarkers of erythropoiesis and iron homeostasis, both before and after discontinuation of iron removal therapy, collected during the exa‐cel Phase 3 CLIMB THAL‐111 and the long‐term follow‐up CLIMB‐131 clinical trials, in which participants had up to 6 years of follow‐up.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

CLIMB THAL‐111 was a Phase 3, open‐label, single dose study of exa‐cel in patients aged 12–35 years with a confirmed diagnosis of TDT and history of ≥ 100 mL/kg/year or 10 units/year of packed RBC transfusions for consecutive 2 years before screening. Following completion of the study, participants were offered enrollment in a 13‐year long‐term follow‐up study (CLIMB‐131; Clinical Trials.gov, NCT04208529).

Full details on the manufacturing, conditioning, and infusion procedures for exa‐cel in patients with TDT can be found in a previously published report by Locatelli et al. [14] The protocol provided recommendations for restarting iron removal therapy following exa‐cel infusion, including restarting iron chelation therapy not earlier than 3 months (for deferasirox or deferoxamine) or 6 months (for deferiprone) following exa‐cel infusion and permitted phlebotomy. Iron removal therapy with iron‐chelating drugs and/or phlebotomy was at the treating physician's discretion. Iron removal therapies could be discontinued thereafter if participants achieved transfusion independence and had hemoglobin levels > 10 g/dL, serum ferritin < 500 μg/L, and/or liver iron concentrations (LIC) < 5 mg/g. Additional details on trial eligibility, the myeloablative busulfan conditioning and iron removal therapy following exa‐cel are provided in the Appendix.

Outcomes

The primary endpoint of the Phase 3 CLIMB THAL‐111 study was transfusion independence which was defined as the proportion of participants who maintained a weighted average hemoglobin ≥ 9 g/dL without RBC transfusion for ≥ 12 months. Secondary endpoints included the duration of transfusion independence, total and fetal hemoglobin concentrations over time, changes in markers of iron overload, and proportion of participants receiving iron removal therapy over time. Exploratory endpoints included assessments of ineffective erythropoiesis (including erythroferrone) and bone marrow analysis (myeloid: erythroid ratio) compared with baseline over time, and changes in hepcidin concentrations. In CLIMB‐131, the primary endpoint was safety and all‐cause mortality. Secondary endpoints related to iron overload included liver iron concentration, cardiac iron content, and ferritin over time. Other endpoints in CLIMB‐131 included change in erythropoietin over time through 5 years after exa‐cel infusion, assessments of ineffective erythropoiesis, and changes in biomarkers of ineffective erythropoiesis.

Data Collection for Biomarker Assessments

Participants provided serial blood samples that were assessed by local laboratories for RBC counts (starting from Day 2 after exa‐cel infusion), biomarkers of iron homeostasis and iron overload, including ferritin (starting 30 days after exa‐cel infusion), serum iron (starting 90 days after exa‐cel infusion), and transferrin (starting 90 days after exa‐cel infusion). The ratio of myeloid to erythroid cells (M:E) was assessed locally at the investigating site using bone marrow aspirate after exa‐cel infusion. Erythropoiesis biomarkers from participants, including erythropoietin, erythroferrone, hepcidin, and soluble transferrin receptor, were assessed centrally at study visits starting at Day 90 after exa‐cel infusion in CLIMB THAL‐111.

Statistical Analysis

Analyses of safety and efficacy endpoints included all participants who received an infusion of exa‐cel, defined as the full analysis set (FAS). As previously reported, a sample size of 45 patients was determined to be sufficient to provide at least 95% statistical power to rule out a response rate of 50% when the true response rate is 80% for both the primary and key secondary endpoint in CLIMB THAL‐111 with a 1‐sided alpha of 2.5% [14]. For assessments of changes in parameters of iron overload, including changes from baseline in LIC, cardiac iron content as assessed by T2* MRI, and serum ferritin level, all participants in the FAS were included and data were summarized as continuous variables over time. Similarly, for changes in markers of erythropoiesis, including erythropoietin, reticulocyte counts, and soluble transferrin receptor, all participants in the FAS were included and data were summarized as continuous variables. For these secondary and exploratory endpoints, no statistical hypothesis testing was conducted. Baseline was defined as the most recent non‐missing measurement collected before the start of mobilization.

Ethical Conduct of Studies

Each participant or legal guardian provided written informed consent, and an independent data monitoring committee monitored safety. Results

Participant Demographics and Baseline Clinical Characteristics

A total of 56 participants received exa‐cel in the CLIMB THAL‐111 clinical study with a median duration of follow‐up of 46.2 months (mean 45.0; range 16.0–75.2 months) across the CLIMB THAL‐111 and CLIMB‐131 studies (data cut April 10, 2025). Overall, 36% of participants were adolescents (12–17 years of age) and 64% were adults (18–35 years of age). At baseline, prior to exa‐cel infusion, all participants were transfusion‐dependent receiving a mean 37.0 (SD, 12.0) units of packed RBCs annually and had mild iron overload with an average liver iron concentration of 4.7 (SD, 3.3) mg/g and normal cardiac iron content (mean T2* 34.1 [SD, 8.8] ms) (Table 1). Before exa‐cel infusion, all participants were receiving iron chelation therapy.

Changes in Total and Fetal Hemoglobin Following Exa‐cel

Following exa‐cel infusion, increases were seen in total and fetal hemoglobin by Month 2, with HbF being the dominant contributor to total hemoglobin by Month 3 in a pancellular distribution. Total hemoglobin and HbF levels plateaued at Month 6 and were sustained through follow‐up, with mean total hemoglobin at normal or near normal levels (Figures 2 and S1). Restoration of HbF synthesis led to 55 out of 56 participants (98%) achieving transfusion independence for ≥ 12 months across CLIMB THAL‐111 and CLIMB‐ 131.

All participants resumed iron removal therapy, with 30% undergoing chelation alone, 34% phlebotomy alone, and 36% a combination of both (Table S1). The median time to restart iron chelation was 6.6 months (range, 2.0 to 30.1) and to start phlebotomy was 9.4 months (range, 2.9 to 37.0) after exa‐cel infusion. The median duration of iron removal therapy was 18.2 months (range, 1.0 to 51.6). At the time of the data cut, 38 participants (68%) had discontinued iron removal therapy for at least 6 months with a median duration free from iron removal therapy of 19.4 months (range 8.2 to 64.7).

Assessment of Biomarkers of Erythropoiesis

To understand the impact of exa‐cel treatment on ineffective erythropoiesis, levels of erythropoietin, erythroferrone, reticulocyte counts, and soluble transferrin receptor were evaluated. Erythropoietin levels initially increased after exa‐cel infusion, reflecting the expected post‐transplant hematopoietic recovery response. Mean erythropoietin levels subsequently decreased over time, consistent with increases in total Hb levels after exa‐cel and reached stable, steady state levels by Month 9 that were slightly elevated above the reference range (Figures 3A and S2). We tested the relationship between hemoglobin level and erythropoietin concentration, and found that, as expected, participants who achieved hemoglobin levels at the lower end of the observed total hemoglobin range had higher erythropoietin levels (Figure S3). Given that erythroferrone levels are elevated in patients with TDT due to underlying ineffective erythropoiesis, we assessed changes in erythroferrone after exa‐cel infusion. Levels of erythroferrone decreased starting at Month 6 after exa‐cel infusion (mean at Month 6 was 12.81 μg/L [SE, 3.83]), reaching normal to near normal levels by Month 15 (mean 7.22 μg/L [SE, 0.82]; reference range for upper limit of normal 2.26 μg/L to 6.32 μg/L) which were then maintained through Month 60 at normal or near normal levels (Figure 3B). Participants with lower total hemoglobin showed less decrease in erythroferrone concentrations than those with higher total hemoglobin (Figure S4), consistent with erythropoietin findings. Mean reticulocyte counts, which transiently increased after exa‐cel infusion consistent with hematopoietic recovery in the peri‐transplant period, subsequently stabilized at mildly elevated levels throughout the follow‐up period. Notably, as expected, reticulocyte counts stabilized at higher levels in splenectomized participants (Figures 3C, S8, S9; Table 1). Mean soluble transferrin receptor, also a biomarker of erythropoietic activity, remained stable and slightly above the reference range throughout follow‐up (Figures 3D and S5). Participants who had bone marrow examinations had local assessment of M:E ratios. Although not standardized and not available for the entire follow‐up period, M:E ratios also showed trends toward normalization over the follow‐up period (Figures S6 and S7).

Assessment of Biomarkers of Iron Overload and Iron Homeostasis

Given that ongoing ineffective erythropoiesis disturbs iron homeostasis, we also assessed iron biomarkers, including serum ferritin, liver iron content (LIC; by liver MRI R2*), cardiac iron content (CIC; by MRI cardiac T2*), and hepcidin. In patients with β‐thalassemia and tissue iron overload, serum ferritin, LIC, and CIC are elevated and hepcidin maybe suppressed when ineffective erythropoiesis resumes between RBC transfusions. As can be seen in Figure 4, as expected, tissue iron stores in the liver and heart (LIC and cardiac T2*) and serum ferritin concentrations generally decreased while participants were on iron removal therapy. However, after cessation of iron removal therapy, tissue iron concentrations remained stable over time, consistent with the absence of iron re‐accumulation after transfusion independence (Figure 4A–C). Crucially, hepcidin levels were not suppressed, remaining stable and in the normal range or above the upper limit of normal in all participants, including following discontinuation of iron removal therapy (Figure 4D). This result is consistent with normal iron homeostasis and effective erythropoietic activity after exa‐cel infusion.

Discussion

The goal of exa‐cel treatment is to achieve a functional cure for TDT, which includes transfusion independence at a stable hemoglobin level, normalization of ineffective erythropoiesis, and correction of dysregulated iron metabolism without ongoing tissue iron loading. If achieved, this would result in freeing patients from the heavy time and financial burden of regular transfusion/chelation therapy and would be expected to ameliorate the attendant morbidity and mortality otherwise associated with ongoing ineffective erythropoiesis and iron overload. Here, we describe results from the CLIMB THAL‐111 study and the CLIMB‐131 extension study assessing the impact of exa‐cel treatment on biomarkers of erythropoiesis and iron homeostasis. Exa‐cel led to normal or near normal levels of total hemoglobin that were durable, with 98% of participants achieving transfusion independence for a year or longer. After transfusion independence and discontinuation of iron removal therapy, participants had decreases in erythroferrone concentrations and normalized hepcidin levels, with improvements and trends toward normalization in erythropoietin levels, reticulocyte counts, and soluble transferrin receptor concentrations, indicating correction of ineffective erythropoiesis. Specific biomarkers of iron overload, including ferritin, liver iron concentration, and cardiac T2*, showed stable trajectories after cessation of iron removal therapy consistent with the absence of ongoing tissue iron accumulation after exa‐cel. These results demonstrate effective erythropoietic activity and restored iron homeostasis, with no iron reaccumulation after exa‐cel infusion.

Erythropoietin secretion is responsive to tissue extractable oxygen as sensed in the kidney and is strongly correlated with total hemoglobin levels [15, 16, 17]. Following an initial rise in erythropoietin immediately after exa‐cel infusion, most likely reflecting the expected erythropoietic recovery post‐transplant, erythropoietin levels decreased, reaching a steady state by Month 9 post‐infusion mirroring total hemoglobin levels. Notably, steady state levels of erythropoietin in this study were slightly higher than the normal reference range, likely reflecting the impact of high HbF levels on renal oxygen sensing as high HbF is positively correlated with increased erythropoietin [18, 19]. This has been suggested to be the result of the higher hemoglobin oxygen affinity and release dynamics associated with HbF compared with adult Hb which could produce an exaggerated erythropoietin response at equivalent levels of circulating hemoglobin [19, 20]. This compensatory response is similar to the adaptive response observed at high altitudes, where reduced atmospheric oxygen as opposed to altered oxygen affinity also stimulates the production of erythropoietin and increased reticulocyte response. Importantly, participants in the lowest range of observed hemoglobin levels in the current study had slightly higher erythropoietin levels, indicating normal and preserved oxygen sensing feedback and erythropoietin stimulation in these participants.

Both reticulocyte counts and soluble transferrin receptor levels also stabilized at slightly elevated levels after exa‐cel infusion. There could be two potential reasons for this. First, it is well established that fetal RBCs, which also express high levels of HbF, have a shorter lifespan than adult RBCs [21], being estimated to be approximately 70 days [22] compared to approximately 120 days for adult RBCs [23]. While the lifespan of HbF‐containing RBCs in adolescents and adults who have received exa‐cel (or have HPFH) has not been directly assessed, this difference could contribute to the slightly elevated biomarkers of erythropoiesis observed in this study, reflecting the higher and normal physiologic erythropoietic drive associated with elevated HbF in the context of participants maintaining normal or near normal total hemoglobin levels. Of note, previous studies have also shown that individuals with HPFH have HbF levels which can be as high or higher as those observed after exa‐cel infusion, and can also have elevated total hemoglobin levels and slightly increased reticulocyte counts [24, 25], though this has not been well studied. Second, reticulocyte counts were more elevated in splenectomized individuals (n = 16) compared to individuals with an intact spleen (n = 40). Increases in reticulocyte counts following splenectomy have been reported in other studies [26], likely deriving from lack of reticulocyte sequestration in the splenic circulation. In the context of slightly elevated levels of reticulocytes and soluble transferrin receptor, we also saw an improving trend in the M:E ratio, although not centrally assessed and thus standardized and not available from all participants. This suggests that decreasing numbers of erythroid precursors are required following exa‐cel due to resolution of ineffective erythropoiesis. Overall, this data suggests that the slightly elevated erythropoietin and reticulocyte levels observed reflect the expected physiology of elevated HbF and are in the context of the splenectomy status of many participants.

Erythroferrone is secreted by developing RBC precursors, both in the bone marrow and in extramedullary sites such as the liver and spleen. A more ineffective erythropoietic process drives greater expansion of the RBC precursor cell pool, and thus higher levels of circulating erythroferrone [27]. After exa‐cel infusion, erythroferrone levels decreased and reached normal or near‐normal values over time, indicating restoration of effective erythropoiesis following exa‐cel. The increased erythropoietic activity should be considered when interpreting these data. Taken together, the changes in these erythropoiesis biomarkers suggest that exa‐cel treatment substantially ameliorates the ineffective erythropoiesis that is a hallmark of β‐thalassemia.

Dysregulation of iron homeostasis is a direct result of ineffective erythropoiesis, with elevated erythroferrone levels suppressing hepcidin secretion and thus leading to increased intestinal iron absorption and redistribution of iron from storage sites to other tissues, such as the liver, heart, and endocrine organs, which would otherwise not be expected to load iron. Gradual improvements in biomarkers of iron overload have been reported in patients with TDT following allogenic hematopoietic stem cell transplants [28] as well as in patients given beti‐cel gene addition therapy [29], including decreases in LIC and serum ferritin concentrations over time. While these studies suggest improved or restored iron homeostasis is possible with these treatments, correlations between changes in iron biomarkers over time and key biomarkers of ineffective erythropoiesis, such as erythroferrone, were not conducted. In addition, these studies did not meticulously evaluate these parameters before and after cessation of iron removal therapy, and thus the full impact of these other therapies on erythropoiesis and iron overload cannot be completely assessed.

A key finding of this current analysis following exa‐cel was that once iron levels had been stabilized with iron removal therapy, levels did not increase when iron removal therapy was discontinued. This was evidenced by stable liver and cardiac iron content on MRI and stable serum ferritin over time. There was one participant who had higher liver iron concentration (> 30 mg/g) at the time of IRT discontinuation which was the result of an inability to complete an adequate course of iron removal therapy due to an intercurrent pregnancy. Furthermore, hepcidin normalized in all participants and did not trend down after discontinuation of iron removal therapy, which would otherwise be expected if there were ongoing ineffective erythropoiesis. These results indicate normalization of iron homeostasis following exa‐cel treatment with no iron reaccumulating after discontinuation of iron removal therapy. Overall, this data supports that exa‐cel treated patients are not converted to non‐transfused β‐thalassemia states (NTDT).

There are limitations to the current study. This was an open‐label, single‐arm study of a gene‐edited cell therapy and therefore there was no comparator group which does limit the ability to fully interpret biomarker data. However, results for several iron biomarkers reported in this study are consistent with results reported in patients with TDT who underwent allogeneic stem cell transplantation [30]. While this study had a small patient population and a limited follow‐up period for some participants, the 13‐year, long‐term follow‐up study CLIMB‐131 should provide further insights into the durable impacts of exa‐cel treatment on ineffective erythropoiesis and iron homeostasis and the potential to halt progression of β‐thalassemia complications, including marrow expansion and bone deformities, development of extramedullary hematopoiesis, excess tissue iron deposition, and vascular and iron overload‐related complications.

In conclusion, biomarker data from participants in the CLIMB THAL‐111 and CLIMB‐131 trials provide supportive evidence that following exa‐cel treatment, there is both correction of ineffective erythropoiesis and restoration of iron homeostasis, further confirming the potential for exa‐cel to deliver a one‐time functional cure to patients with TDT.
