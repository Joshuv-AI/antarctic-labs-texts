Effectiveness of an Information Platform‐Based Multidisciplinary Enhanced Recovery After Surgery Program on Recovery After Transcatheter Aortic Valve Implantation: A Non‐Concurrent Controlled Study
Authors: Shaoping Lin, Cuicui Feng, Lian Lin, Jiezhen Liu, Meizhen Guan, Jiaxin Li, Fangfang Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimTo evaluate the implementation effectiveness of a multicomponent multidisciplinary Enhanced Recovery After Surgery pathway integrated with an information platform for patients undergoing transcatheter aortic valve implantation.DesignA single‐center quasi‐experimental study with a non‐concurrent historical control group.MethodsA single‐center quasi‐experimental study with a non‐concurrent historical control group included 154 patients. Introduction

Aortic stenosis (AS) is a major age‐related valvular disorder, and its burden is increasing with population aging. A meta‐analysis of population‐based studies estimated that among adults older than 75 years, the prevalence of AS is approximately 12.4%, while severe AS affects approximately 3.4% (Osnabrugge et al. 2013). Transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) has substantially changed the management of severe symptomatic AS, evolving from a treatment primarily for patients at prohibitive or high surgical risk to an established option for selected intermediate‐ and low‐risk patients (Mack et al. 2019; Popma et al. 2019). Despite the less invasive nature of TAVI and substantial improvements in procedural outcomes, recovery remains clinically important because many TAVI recipients are older, frail, multimorbid and physically deconditioned. Thus, procedural success alone does not necessarily ensure rapid restoration of mobility, independence, or health‐related quality of life.

The conceptual foundation of Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) can be traced to the multimodal ‘fast‐track’ surgical approach proposed by Kehlet in the 1990s. In 1997, Kehlet emphasized that postoperative morbidity and prolonged convalescence arise partly from the physiological stress response to surgery and proposed coordinated multimodal interventions rather than reliance on any single perioperative measure (Kehlet 1997). This concept was subsequently developed into a structured enhanced‐recovery framework by the ERAS Study Group. In 2005, Fearon and colleagues published a consensus‐based multimodal perioperative protocol for colorectal surgery that helped formalize the modern ERAS model (Fearon et al. 2005). ERAS subsequently expanded across surgical specialties as a multidisciplinary, evidence‐based approach incorporating patient education, nutritional optimization, standardized perioperative care and early mobilization (Ljungqvist et al. 2017). Application of these principles to TAVI has developed more recently. A TAVI‐specific multidisciplinary ERAS pathway was proposed in 2016 (Sola et al. 2016), and contemporary evidence continues to indicate that structured ERAS implementation in TAVI remains an evolving area of practice (Yılmaz Ak et al. 2025).

Nurses have a central role in translating ERAS principles into daily TAVI care because they provide continuous assessment, patient education, mobilization support, discharge preparation and coordination across the transition from hospital to home. This role is particularly relevant for older patients whose frailty, functional limitations, cognitive burden and multimorbidity may complicate recovery. However, access to structured rehabilitation after TAVI remains limited. In a multicenter cohort of 3372 patients undergoing transcatheter aortic valve replacement, only 30.6% participated in cardiac rehabilitation within 90 days after discharge, with participation ranging from 5% to 60% across hospitals (Sukul et al. 2023). This gap indicates that successful procedural treatment is not consistently followed by structured recovery support.

From a nursing perspective, the remaining research gap is not simply whether ERAS can be applied to TAVI, but how nurses can operationalize its principles across the entire care transition. Existing TAVI‐oriented ERAS approaches have primarily emphasized peri‐procedural and inpatient optimization (Sola et al. 2016; Yılmaz Ak et al. 2025), whereas the integration of nursing assessment, structured patient education, progressive mobilization, individualized rehabilitation guidance, discharge preparation and continuing post‐discharge communication within a single care pathway has been less clearly evaluated. Digital information platforms may provide a practical mechanism for nurses to standardize education, reinforce rehabilitation plans, identify unresolved recovery needs and maintain communication after discharge. Nevertheless, evidence remains limited regarding the implementation of such a nurse‐integrated, multidisciplinary pathway and its association with functional recovery, independence and healthcare utilization after TAVI.

Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the implementation effectiveness of a multicomponent multidisciplinary ERAS care pathway integrated with an information platform for patients undergoing TAVI. The intervention was evaluated as an integrated care package extending from hospitalization to home rather than as a test of any single ERAS, digital‐health, or professional‐support component. From a nursing‐practice perspective, the study specifically examined whether a structured pathway incorporating education, functional assessment, rehabilitation support, discharge coordination and continuing communication could support recovery across the hospital‐to‐home transition. We hypothesized that implementation of this comprehensive pathway would be associated with better procedural knowledge, reduced early frailty, improved functional recovery, shorter hospitalization and better follow‐up functional outcomes compared with conventional care.

Patients and Methods

Study Participants

This was a single‐center, quasi‐experimental study with a non‐concurrent historical control group. Group allocation was determined by the calendar period in which patients underwent TAVI rather than by random assignment. The control group comprised 76 patients who underwent TAVI from 2023 through August 2024 and received conventional nursing care. Following implementation of the information platform‐based multidisciplinary ERAS program in September 2024, 78 patients undergoing TAVI were included in the intervention group and received the ERAS program in addition to conventional nursing care.

Clinical, procedural and echocardiographic variables were retrospectively retrieved from the electronic medical record, catheterization laboratory records and institutional echocardiography database. To assess measurable changes in TAVI practice between the two calendar periods, additional variables included NYHA functional class, STS‐PROM score, aortic valve morphology, vascular access, prosthesis platform and model, balloon predilatation and post‐dilatation, procedure duration, device success, new permanent pacemaker implantation, discharge mean transvalvular gradient and moderate‐or‐greater paravalvular regurgitation. Device success and prosthetic valve hemodynamic outcomes were classified according to contemporary VARC‐3 principles (Généreux et al. 2021).

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Research Ethics Committee Guangdong Provincial People's Hospital, Guangdong Academy of Medical Sciences (approval No. GDREC2020048H(R1)) before study implementation and was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided written informed consent before participation. Participation was voluntary, and participants were informed that they could withdraw from the study without affecting their subsequent treatment or nursing care. Personal identifiers were removed from the analytic dataset, and study data were stored and analysed using coded identifiers to protect participant confidentiality. Access to patient‐related information transmitted through the digital platform was restricted to authorized members of the multidisciplinary care team.

The study was reported in accordance with the Transparent Reporting of Evaluations with Nonrandomized Designs (TREND) statement; the completed TREND checklist is provided in File S1.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

The eligibility criteria were established with reference to contemporary recommendations for TAVI and previous studies of mobile‐guided rehabilitation in patients undergoing TAVI (Vahanian et al. 2022; Shen et al. 2024). The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) a clinical indication for TAVI established by the multidisciplinary Heart Team in accordance with contemporary valvular heart disease guidelines (Vahanian et al. 2022); (2) completion of TAVI during the index hospitalization; (3) ability to independently use a smartphone, with sufficient reading and comprehension skills to access the information platform; and (4) clear consciousness and the ability to communicate effectively. The digital‐literacy and communication requirements were necessary because the intervention involved repeated access to educational materials, rehabilitation guidance and online communication through the information platform and were consistent with eligibility considerations reported in previous mobile‐guided TAVI rehabilitation research (Shen et al. 2024).

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) critical illness or major clinical instability that precluded participation in postoperative rehabilitation; (2) major cerebrovascular disease or active malignant disease that substantially interfered with rehabilitation or the planned follow‐up; (3) severe cognitive impairment, psychiatric disorder, or speech disorder that prevented participation in the intervention or completion of outcome assessments; and (4) inability to complete the planned 12‐month follow‐up (Shen et al. 2024).

Intervention Methods

Control Group

Patients in the control group received conventional perioperative nursing care. Before TAVI, nurses provided routine procedural education and completed standard preoperative preparations. After the procedure, unnecessary catheters and tubes were removed as early as clinically appropriate; patients were encouraged to begin mobilization, and nurses provided guidance regarding breathing exercises, nutrition and rehabilitation activities. After discharge, patients received routine follow‐up and were advised to participate in outpatient cardiac rehabilitation.

Intervention Group

The intervention was developed with reference to established ERAS principles for cardiac procedures and published evidence on perioperative and rehabilitation care after TAVI (Sola et al. 2016; Yılmaz Ak et al. 2025; Engelman et al. 2019; Brocki et al. 2022; Li et al. 2023; Ashikaga et al. 2023). The program was delivered by a multidisciplinary team comprising physicians, a head nurse, rehabilitation therapists and nurses and incorporated an information platform throughout the transition from hospitalization to home.

During hospitalization, the platform was used to provide structured education on TAVI, self‐care, nutrition, pain assessment and individualized rehabilitation. Rehabilitation therapists assessed patients' physical and nutritional status and developed individualized rehabilitation plans, while nurses delivered corresponding educational and exercise materials and assessed patients' understanding. Postoperatively, the multidisciplinary team reassessed patients daily and coordinated early catheter removal when clinically appropriate, pain management, nutritional management and progressive mobilization, including bedside activity, standing and walking, in accordance with the patient's clinical condition (Sola et al. 2016; Yılmaz Ak et al. 2025; Engelman et al. 2019; Li et al. 2023).

After discharge, the information platform supported continuation of the rehabilitation plan through educational materials, rehabilitation check‐ins, online exercise appointments and video‐based group sessions. Patients could also submit questions through the platform, and rehabilitation therapists adjusted exercise content and intensity according to follow‐up assessments. These components were intended to maintain continuity of rehabilitation and professional support beyond hospitalization, consistent with previous TAVI‐specific mobile‐guided and telerehabilitation approaches (Shen et al. 2024; Brocki et al. 2022; Ashikaga et al. 2023). The intervention was analysed as a single multicomponent care package because the ERAS elements, digital delivery, multidisciplinary contact and additional rehabilitation support were introduced concurrently and were not independently allocated. Accordingly, the study was not designed to estimate the component‐specific effects of the digital platform, ERAS content, or additional professional contact. Outcome Measures

Knowledge of TAVI Under ERAS. Knowledge was assessed using a study‐specific questionnaire because existing validated cardiac rehabilitation knowledge instruments, such as the Coronary Artery Disease Education Questionnaire–Short Version, were developed primarily for patients with coronary artery disease and do not assess TAVI‐specific procedural knowledge or the ERAS‐related educational content delivered in the present program (Ghisi et al. 2016). Previous studies evaluating educational interventions in patients undergoing TAVI have similarly used procedure‐specific questions to assess patients' understanding of TAVI‐related information (Brand et al. 2025). The questionnaire used in the present study comprised 37 items covering four domains aligned with the educational content of the intervention, including TAVI procedural knowledge, infection prevention and self‐management, ERAS nutritional management and ERAS functional exercise. Each correct response was scored 1 point, whereas an incorrect or ‘do not know’ response was scored 0, yielding a total score ranging from 0 to 37; higher scores indicated greater knowledge. The questionnaire demonstrated high internal consistency (Cronbach's α = 0.905) and a validity coefficient of 0.929.

Early Frailty and Functional Capacity

The Fried frailty phenotype was employed to assess frailty across five dimensions (Fried et al. 2001). Frailty scores ≥ 3 were classified as frailty, scores of 1–2 as pre‐frailty and a score of 0 as robust. The Barthel Index (BI) was used to assess independence in activities of daily living, with higher scores indicating greater functional independence (Mahoney and Barthel 1965).

Length of Hospital Stay

This included intensive care unit length of stay, postoperative hospital length of stay, and total hospital length of stay.

Follow‐Up Functional Outcomes

The 12‐Item Short Form Health Survey (SF‐12) was used to assess health‐related quality of life, with higher scores indicating better perceived health status (Ware Jr et al. 1996). Functional exercise capacity was assessed using the 6‐min walk test (6MWT) according to standardized procedures (ATS Committee on Proficiency Standards for Clinical Pulmonary Function Laboratories 2002).

Cardiac and Prosthetic Valve Assessment

Transthoracic echocardiography was performed before TAVI, during postoperative hospitalization and at 1, 3, 6 and 12 months. LVEF was recorded as a measure of left ventricular systolic function (Lang et al. 2015) and was analysed separately from health‐related quality of life and exercise capacity. Because LVEF does not directly measure prosthetic valve performance, discharge mean transvalvular gradient and moderate‐or‐greater paravalvular regurgitation were additionally evaluated as valve hemodynamic outcomes according to VARC‐3 principles (Généreux et al. 2021).

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were assessed for normality separately within each study group using the Shapiro–Wilk test together with visual inspection of Q–Q plots. Variables showing no substantial departure from normality were summarized as mean ± standard deviation and compared between groups using independent‐samples t‐tests. Variables with non‐normal distributions were summarized as median (Q1, Q3) and compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Age and the Barthel Index showed approximately normal distributions in both groups and were therefore analysed using independent‐samples t‐tests. Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages [n (%)] and compared using Pearson's χ 2 test.

Because the intervention and control groups were enrolled during different calendar periods, post hoc sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the potential influence of measurable temporal changes in TAVI practice. Total hospital length of stay was analysed using a generalized linear model with a gamma distribution and log link. Repeated LVEF measurements were evaluated using generalized estimating equations with an identity link, exchangeable working correlation structure and robust standard errors. Adjusted models included age, sex, baseline LVEF, NYHA Class III/IV, bicuspid aortic valve morphology, vascular access, prosthesis platform and procedure duration. Study group, follow‐up time and the group‐by‐time interaction were included in the longitudinal LVEF model. Because treatment period and intervention status were intrinsically linked, these analyses were considered sensitivity analyses for measured temporal differences rather than complete control of calendar‐time confounding. A two‐sided p value < 0.01 was considered statistically significant; 99% confidence intervals were reported for adjusted sensitivity analyses.

Results

Participant Characteristics

A total of 154 patients were included, comprising 76 patients in the control group and 78 in the intervention group. The mean age was 73.37 ± 6.86 years in the control group and 73.35 ± 7.06 years in the intervention group (p = 0.984). Men accounted for 28.95% and 42.31% of the groups, respectively (p = 0.084). NYHA Class III/IV was present in 76.32% and 73.08% of patients (p = 0.644), and bicuspid aortic valve morphology was observed in 46.05% and 50.00%, respectively (p = 0.624). Median STS‐PROM scores were 5.80% (3.50%, 8.60%) and 5.50% (3.30%, 8.20%) (p = 0.568).

Procedural characteristics showed no statistically significant between‐period differences at the prespecified 0.01 level. Transfemoral access was used in 88.16% of control‐period and 93.59% of intervention‐period procedures (p = 0.241), while self‐expanding prostheses were used in 89.47% and 92.31%, respectively (p = 0.541). The overall distribution of prosthesis models did not differ statistically between periods (p = 0.388). Device success was achieved in 94.74% and 96.15% of patients (p = 0.673), and new permanent pacemaker implantation occurred in 11.84% and 10.26%, respectively (p = 0.754). At discharge, the median mean transvalvular gradient was 10.00 (8.00, 13.00) mmHg versus 9.00 (7.00, 12.00) mmHg (p = 0.094), and moderate‐or‐greater paravalvular regurgitation occurred in 7.89% versus 5.13% (p = 0.486) (Table 1).

Knowledge Outcomes

Before the intervention, no statistically significant between‐group differences were observed in TAVI procedural knowledge (5.00 [4.00, 7.00] vs. 6.00 [5.00, 7.25], p = 0.111), ERAS nutritional management knowledge (4.00 [1.00, 6.00] vs. 5.00 [2.00, 7.00], p = 0.069), or ERAS functional exercise knowledge (5.00 [4.00, 6.00] vs. 5.00 [4.00, 6.00], p = 0.997). After the intervention, the intervention group had higher scores for TAVI procedural knowledge (10.00 [9.00, 10.00] vs. 7.00 [6.00, 8.00], p < 0.001), ERAS nutritional management (10.00 [9.00, 10.00] vs. 8.00 [6.00, 9.00], p < 0.001), and ERAS functional exercise (9.00 [8.00, 9.00] vs. 6.00 [5.00, 8.00], p < 0.001) than the control group (Table 2).

Postoperative frailty status differed between groups (χ 2 = 27.755, p < 0.001). Length of Stay

The intervention group had a shorter median ICU stay than the control group (3.50 [2.00, 5.00] vs. 5.00 [4.00, 7.00] days, p < 0.001). Postoperative hospital stay was also shorter in the intervention group (8.00 [6.00, 9.25] vs. 11.00 [9.00, 16.00] days, p < 0.001), as was total hospital stay (14.00 [11.00, 17.00] vs. 20.00 [15.00, 24.25] days, p < 0.001) (Table 4). In the post hoc gamma‐regression sensitivity analysis adjusting for age, sex, baseline LVEF, NYHA functional class, aortic valve morphology, vascular access, prosthesis platform and procedure duration, total hospital stay remained shorter during the intervention period (adjusted ratio, 0.74; 99% CI, 0.64–0.86; p < 0.001).

At the post‐intervention assessment, the intervention group had a higher SF‐12 score than the control group (98.00 [93.75, 100.00] vs. 93.00 [90.00, 98.25], p < 0.001). The corresponding 6‐min walk distances were 346.00 (318.50, 368.75) m and 320.00 (224.00, 351.00) m, respectively (Table 5).

In the post hoc adjusted GEE analysis of longitudinal LVEF, the group‐by‐time interaction was attenuated and did not meet the prespecified significance threshold (p = 0.028). At 12 months, the adjusted between‐group difference in LVEF was 2.1 percentage points (99% CI, −0.4 to 4.6; p = 0.031).

Discussion

In this single‐center non‐concurrent implementation study, patients managed after introduction of the multicomponent information platform‐based multidisciplinary ERAS pathway showed greater procedural knowledge, less early frailty, greater independence in activities of daily living, shorter hospitalization and more favourable follow‐up functional and health‐status outcomes than patients receiving conventional care. The overall pattern of findings suggests that extending structured rehabilitation, education and multidisciplinary support from hospitalization into post‐discharge care may facilitate recovery after TAVI. However, the apparent difference in longitudinal cardiac function was less robust after accounting for measurable patient and procedural differences between the two treatment periods. These findings should therefore be interpreted as the implementation effect of the integrated care pathway as a whole rather than as evidence for the independent effectiveness of the digital platform, ERAS content, or any individual intervention component.

Functional recovery is particularly important after TAVI because recipients are commonly older adults with frailty, multimorbidity and reduced physical reserve. The 6‐min walk test is an established standardized measure of functional exercise capacity and is widely used to evaluate changes in physical performance (Holland et al. 2014). Multidisciplinary geriatric rehabilitation has also been explored in older patients with cardiovascular disease as an approach to restoring function and independence after acute treatment (van Dam van Isselt et al. 2018). In the TAVI population specifically, previous reviews have highlighted the potential role of cardiac rehabilitation in improving postoperative physical capacity and functional recovery (Zou et al. 2023). A subsequent systematic review and meta‐analysis found that exercise‐based cardiac rehabilitation after TAVI was associated with improvements in both exercise tolerance and functional independence, although the underlying studies were generally small and heterogeneous (Oz et al. 2023). Functional independence is clinically relevant beyond its role as a rehabilitation endpoint: impaired Barthel Index scores have been associated with increased mortality, neurological complications, cardiac decompensation and failure of fast‐track care after TAVI (El Garhy et al. 2020). Similar prognostic associations between reduced Barthel Index scores and mortality have been observed in older patients with heart failure (Katano et al. 2022). Nevertheless, a recent systematic review of comprehensive geriatric assessment and related interventions around TAVI found mixed results and substantial methodological limitations in the available studies (Chin et al. 2024). Taken together, the concurrent findings across frailty, walking capacity and activities of daily living in our cohort are clinically coherent with the existing rehabilitation literature, but they do not establish which specific component of the pathway produced the observed functional benefit.

Health‐related quality of life is another important outcome after TAVI because successful valve implantation does not necessarily translate into equivalent gains in symptoms, functional status and perceived health for every patient. A systematic review of TAVI studies demonstrated that functional status and quality of life generally improve after the procedure, while also documenting considerable heterogeneity in the magnitude of patient‐reported benefit (Kim et al. 2014). More recent prospective evidence similarly indicates substantial variability in clinically meaningful improvement in physical dimensions of health‐related quality of life during the first year after TAVI (Sævik et al. 2025). The favourable health‐status findings in the present study are therefore compatible with a broader recovery model in which physical rehabilitation, education, self‐management support and continuity of professional contact may act together. Importantly, the present study cannot establish that improvement in health status was specifically mediated by the information platform, because neither mediation effects nor individual intervention components were assessed separately.

Digital delivery may be particularly useful for maintaining rehabilitation support after hospital discharge, but the TAVI‐specific evidence remains preliminary. Brocki et al. evaluated real‐time home‐based telerehabilitation in older adults recently discharged after TAVI and found that patients who completed the intervention valued supervised exercise and real‐time feedback, while limited information‐technology skills and low retention substantially constrained feasibility (Brocki et al. 2022). Ashikaga et al. subsequently evaluated home‐based cardiac telemonitoring rehabilitation after TAVI and demonstrated that such an approach could be delivered safely in a small single‐center cohort, although the sample size and historical comparison limited conclusions regarding comparative effectiveness (Ashikaga et al. 2023). These studies support the feasibility of selected components of digitally supported rehabilitation but do not establish that digital delivery is intrinsically superior to equivalent rehabilitation delivered through conventional professional contact. Our findings extend this implementation literature by evaluating an information‐supported pathway spanning both hospitalization and post‐discharge care, while similarly requiring caution regarding attribution of benefit to the technology itself.

Hospital length of stay is an important pathway‐level outcome after TAVI, but it is also especially susceptible to changes in institutional practice over time. Large observational data from the United States demonstrated progressive shortening of hospitalization as TAVI experience accumulated, with mean length of stay declining substantially between 2012 and 2015 (Arora et al. 2018). More recently, the FAST‐TAVI II cluster‐randomized trial showed that implementation of a structured package of peri‐procedural quality‐of‐care measures increased early discharge and reduced length of stay after transfemoral TAVI without an accompanying increase in short‐term mortality or cardiovascular readmission (Durand et al. 2024). The absolute hospitalization duration in our cohort remained longer than that reported in contemporary international fast‐track pathways, likely reflecting differences in local admission practices, peri‐procedural management, rehabilitation organization and discharge criteria. Although the adjusted sensitivity analysis suggested that the association between ERAS‐pathway implementation and shorter hospitalization was not fully explained by the measured patient and procedural characteristics, the non‐concurrent design prevents complete separation of the intervention effect from secular changes in care.

The same temporal limitation is particularly important when interpreting cardiac‐function findings. Operator experience, vascular‐access strategy, prosthesis selection, implantation technique and peri‐procedural management may evolve over successive TAVI periods even within the same institution. Although the measured procedural and prosthetic‐valve characteristics were broadly similar between the control and intervention periods, unmeasured changes in practice remain possible because calendar period and intervention status were intrinsically linked. In addition, LVEF should not be interpreted as a direct measure of prosthetic valve performance. LVEF primarily reflects global left ventricular systolic function, whereas contemporary standardized assessment of prosthetic valve function incorporates valve‐specific hemodynamic and structural measures, including transvalvular gradients and paravalvular regurgitation (Généreux et al. 2021). The attenuation of the longitudinal LVEF difference after adjustment for baseline and procedural characteristics therefore argues against attributing this outcome specifically to the ERAS pathway. We consequently regard the LVEF findings as exploratory and do not interpret them as evidence that the intervention improved the mechanical or hemodynamic performance of the implanted prosthesis.

The information platform should likewise be considered one delivery component within a broader complex intervention rather than an independently evaluated treatment. In the intervention period, patients received not only digital educational materials but also additional multidisciplinary contact, structured rehabilitation supervision, professional encouragement, individualized guidance and continuing post‐discharge support. The updated Medical Research Council framework for complex interventions emphasizes that observed effects may arise from interactions among individual intervention components, implementation context, mechanisms and resource requirements rather than from a single identifiable element (Skivington et al. 2021). Consequently, the present study estimates the effectiveness of implementing the complete resource‐enhanced care package under routine clinical conditions. It cannot determine whether similar outcomes could have been achieved by allocating the same personnel time and professional attention to another form of supportive intervention, nor can it isolate the relative contributions of ERAS principles, digital delivery, rehabilitation supervision, or increased human contact. A resource‐matched active comparator or factorial/component‐focused design would be required to address these questions.

Several limitations should therefore be considered when interpreting these findings. First, this was a single‐center study, and the results may not be directly generalizable to centers with different patient populations, staffing models, digital infrastructure, peri‐procedural protocols, or discharge practices. Second, the intervention was delivered in addition to conventional care and increased the overall intensity of professional support. Because no attention‐matched or resource‐equivalent active control was included, specific effects attributable to ERAS content or the information platform cannot be separated from nonspecific effects of additional professional attention and rehabilitation support. Third, the non‐concurrent design intrinsically links intervention status to calendar period. Although additional procedural characteristics were examined and adjusted sensitivity analyses were performed, residual confounding related to changes in TAVI technology, operator experience, procedural strategy and peri‐procedural management cannot be eliminated. The attenuation of the longitudinal LVEF findings after adjustment illustrates the importance of this limitation. Fourth, follow‐up was limited to 12 months and therefore cannot establish the longer‐term sustainability of the observed functional and health‐status outcomes. Fifth, procedural knowledge was assessed using a study‐specific questionnaire rather than an externally validated TAVI‐specific knowledge instrument; despite acceptable internal psychometric performance in the present cohort, its external measurement properties and generalizability remain uncertain. Finally, the study was not designed to evaluate healthcare resource consumption or cost‐effectiveness, which is particularly relevant because the intervention required additional multidisciplinary personnel input.

Future studies should therefore employ concurrent controlled designs, preferably multicenter randomized studies, to separate intervention effects from calendar‐time changes in TAVI practice. An active comparator receiving a similar frequency and intensity of professional contact would be particularly informative for determining whether the ERAS‐specific and digital components provide incremental benefit beyond increased attention and rehabilitation support. Factorial or component‐focused studies could further identify the elements that contribute most strongly to functional recovery, adherence and patient‐reported outcomes. Future evaluations should also incorporate standardized prosthetic‐valve hemodynamic endpoints, longer follow‐up, implementation fidelity, healthcare‐resource use and formal cost‐effectiveness analyses to determine whether this integrated pathway provides sustainable clinical value across different TAVI care settings.

Implications for Nursing Practice

The findings have several practical implications for nurses involved in the care of patients undergoing TAVI. First, recovery planning should extend beyond procedural preparation and routine discharge education. Nurses can incorporate structured assessment of frailty, independence in activities of daily living and functional exercise capacity into the peri‐procedural pathway to identify patients who may require additional rehabilitation support. Reassessment before discharge can further inform individualized education, mobility goals and follow‐up needs.

Second, patient education and rehabilitation guidance can be organized as a continuous nursing process rather than as isolated inpatient activities. Standardized educational content regarding the procedure, self‐management, nutrition, activity progression and recovery expectations can be initiated before TAVI, reinforced during hospitalization and continued after discharge. An information platform may facilitate consistent delivery of these materials and communication with patients, but it should complement rather than replace individualized nursing assessment and direct professional support.

Third, nurses can serve as coordinators between patients, physicians and rehabilitation professionals during the hospital‐to‐home transition. Structured post‐discharge check‐ins may be used to review rehabilitation progress, reinforce adherence to individualized activity plans, identify barriers to recovery and facilitate timely referral to appropriate members of the multidisciplinary team when additional assessment is required.

Finally, the present intervention should be implemented as an integrated care pathway rather than assuming that the digital platform alone produces better outcomes. Because the intervention also involved greater multidisciplinary contact and rehabilitation support, healthcare organizations adopting a similar model should consider nursing workload, staffing, digital literacy, implementation fidelity and local rehabilitation resources. Prospective evaluation of these factors is needed before widespread implementation.

Conclusion

Implementation of a multicomponent information platform‐based multidisciplinary ERAS pathway was associated with better early functional recovery and shorter hospitalization after TAVI in this single‐center non‐concurrent study. The findings represent the overall effect of a resource‐enhanced care package and do not identify the contribution of individual intervention components. Although the association with shorter hospital stay persisted after adjustment for measured patient and procedural characteristics, the longitudinal LVEF difference was attenuated and residual confounding from temporal changes in TAVI practice cannot be excluded. Concurrent controlled studies using resource‐matched comparators are needed to determine the specific and comparative effectiveness of this care model.
