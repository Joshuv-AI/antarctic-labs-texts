Frames of dying: a qualitative study of end-of-life experiences of terminal Arab patients
Authors: Nidaa Natour Hleihel, Daniel Sperling
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPalliative care (PC) at the end of life (EOL) seeks to alleviate suffering and enhance dignity and quality of life, mostly for patients facing terminal illness. Yet within Israel’s Arab population, cultural, religious and linguistic dynamics deeply shape the experience of illness, care preferences and trust in the healthcare system. Care provision often fails to address these sociocultural complexities. This study aims to explore and understand the views and attitudes of terminal Israeli Arab patients regarding PC, their dying experiences and the meanings they attach to them as shaped by cultural, religious and socio-political factors.MethodsA descriptive qualitative study was pursued based on an interpretive-phenomenological approach. Data were collected through 14 in-depth semi-structured interviews conducted in Arabic with 13 terminally ill Arab patients and one family proxy between July 2023 and July 2025. Participants were recruited via convenience and purposive sampling to reflect sample diversity. Interpretive Phenomenological Approach and thematic analysis organized by the Atlas.Ti. software was used to analyze the data.ResultsFour central themes were identified: 1. God decides, but the System Fails – Religious acceptance of death coexists with mistrust in the healthcare system and perceived medical neglect; 2. Faith, Control, and Family: Navigating the EOL journey Decision making at the EOL is shaped by spiritual frameworks, patient's reluctance to accept PC and to engage in EOL discussions, and strong familial involvement, at times overriding patient autonomy; 3. Post-Mortem Concerns– Patients, especially parents, expressed intense anxiety regarding the fate of their dependents after death; 4. The Spiritual Architecture of the EOL Journey – Faith served as a moral and existential framework, influencing perceptions of suffering, sedation and the dying process.ConclusionsEOL experiences of terminal Arab patients are shaped by deeply rooted religious worldviews, family dynamics and perceptions of structural inequalities. Understanding these elements is vital to improving culturally sensitive PC that fosters trust, spiritual dignity and family-centered decision-making among minority populations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12904-025-01975-0.

Background

Palliative care (PC) is a comprehensive medical approach addressing serious, chronic or terminal illnesses, by alleviating physical suffering, pain, dyspnea and fatigue, enhancing quality of life, and providing emotional, psychological and occasionally spiritual support while upholding patient dignity, autonomy and individual preferences [1]. This multidimensional approach regards patients as whole persons, requiring interventions encompassing physical, emotional, social and, when appropriate, spiritual care, especially in the vulnerable context of end-of-life (EOL) decision-making [2].

In Israel, the Dying Patient Act was passed in 2005. The integrates several religious and ethical values, including the principle of sanctity of life, enhancing patients’ quality of life, preventing pain and suffering, and promoting patient autonomy [3–5]. Section 23 of the Act specifically acknowledges the rights of a dying patient (defined as those whose life expectancy does not exceed 6 months) and his or her family to receive universal PC services, when appropriate. In 2009, the Ministry of Health published clinical and administrative guidelines regulating the operation of PC services throughout the country. In addition, PC services have been added to the “health basket,” which is a pre-determined and publicly funded list of health services. As a result, every Israeli citizen has the right to access free PC services whenever they require it [6, 7]. Along with these developments, pain management and PC are officially recognized as a medical specialty in 2010. As of today, PC services in Israel are provided at three levels of care: hospital-based care, community services, and at the hospice. The purpose of this three-levels system is to provide services to various patients, and especially to oncology patients, after they are diagnosed with incurable disease [8].The awareness and the integration of PC within public healthcare in Israel have significantly expanded in recent years, embedding universal services, acknowledged by law, across community, home, and institutional settings [8, 9]. Despite this progress, the availability of these services varies considerably, depending on institutional capacities, local infrastructure and resource allocation. Recent qualitative findings indicate that among Arab healthcare professionals in Israel, cultural and religious considerations often complicate clinical referrals to PC, particularly at EOL stages [10]. In addition, critical shortages in trained healthcare professionals and cultural competence training plague many regions [7, 9, 11, 12]. As a result, disparities persist across population groups, rooted in differences in geographic access, cultural and linguistic adaptability, lack of knowledge, misconceptions and the social stigma associated with death [13–15].These barriers disproportionately impact minority communities, particularly the Arab population [16].

Arab citizens of Israel, comprising approximately 21% of the population, are a heterogeneous group which includes Muslims, Christians and Druze, each characterized by distinct cultural values, community frameworks, and religious traditions [17–19] In the Arab community, the extended family frequently plays a dominant role in making healthcare decisions, often surpassing the autonomy of the individual patient, and practices such as withholding terminal diagnoses from patients motivated by protection, modesty or spiritual considerations are not uncommon [14, 20].

Research shows that Arab patients are referred to PC services less often than Jewish patients, despite their potentially greater needs for EOL support [21, 22]. This disparity is rooted in systemic factors, including inaccessible information in Arabic, lack of culturally tailored services, misperceptions of the proposed care, such as equating PC with ’ceasing treatment’, and fears of stigmatization related to discussing death [15] Significantly, palliative care is included in the national Healthcare Services Basket and provided at no cost to all residents, including Israeli Arab citizens, through HMOs and hospitals [9]formally securing universal access [23] Importantly, such disparity should not be interpreted as intentional discrimination. Rather, it likely reflects systemic and structural barriers, such as language limitations, inadequate cultural competence among providers, and unequal healthcare resource distribution inadvertently restricting access for Arab patients [22, 24].

Crucially, cultural and religious dimensions greatly shape attitudes toward PC. In Arab communities, death is often shrouded in taboo, with conversations around it viewed as ominous or detrimental to one’s mental well-being [25–27]. The belief that “all is in the hands of God”, and that outcomes may involve miracles, discourages open prognostic discussion and advance planning [14, 28]. Similarly, opioid use and sedation are often perceived by members of these communities as hastening death rather than providing relief, further fueling reluctance to access these treatments [29] A recent quantitative cross-sectional study with 435 Israeli Arab participants aged 50 + found that overall knowledge of PC was limited, however, Arab Christians participants reported a higher knowledge score than Muslims and Druze participants. Among Muslims, those who identified as, highly religious expressed stronger intentions to seek PC as than less religious Muslims, highlighting how religiosity levels affect care intentions [30]. These findings underscore that the Arab community is not monolithic, and that religious variation meaningfully informs preferences and behaviors in EOL care [14].

Given the complex cultural, religious and socio-political environment shaping EOL experiences for Israeli Arab patients, this study undertakes an interpretive-phenomenological exploration of the subjective meaning-making processes of terminally ill individuals approaching death. Rather than relying on structural or theoretical models, our methodology employs rich, in-depth interviews to illuminate how religiosity, family structures and cultural value systems fundamentally inform fears, decisions regarding care and perceptions of suffering. In doing so, the study fills a significant gap in the literature discussing the Israeli context, and advances the international discourse around culturally informed PC [30].

In culturally diverse Arab populations, religiosity, family structures, and cultural value systems are profoundly shaped by religious teachings, religious laws, communal expectations and individuals’ perceived ability to pursue PC, based on their self-efficacy and the practical availability of supportive resources in their environment.

Specifically, attitudes refer to patients’ evaluative beliefs about PC, such as viewing PC as a surrender of hope or associating it with imminent death, or that PC may generate emotional resistance [31–33]. Subjective norms describe patients’ perceptions of social expectations and pressures from significant others such as(family members, community figures or religious leaders), who may discourage discussions of dying or EOL care planning [14, 15]. Finally, perceived behavioral control (PBC) refers to patients’ sense of how possible and feasible it is for them to access and use PC, constrained by systemic barriers, including the lack of Arabic-speaking professionals, poor service availability in peripheral areas, and the absence of culturally responsive communication and education [34, 35]. Collectively, these theoretical elements highlight the critical need to understand how religious, cultural and structural factors interact to shape Arab patients’ intentions to engage with PC services, an inquiry this study seeks to address.

Methods

Design

This study aims to explore and understand the views and attitudes of terminal Israeli Arab patients regarding PC, their dying experiences and the meanings they attach to them, as shaped by cultural, religious and socio-political factors. As such, the study seeks to uncover how sociocultural dynamics shape decision-making regarding PC utilization. More broadly, its findings aim to inform culturally responsive policies and clinical practices in improving access, communication and trust in PC services among Arab minority populations in Israel.

This study adopted a descriptive qualitative research design following the Interpretive Phenomenological Approach (IPA). Semi-structured, in-depth interviews were conducted with terminally ill Arab patients in Israel from Muslim, Christian and Druze backgrounds, in an attempt to explore their perceptions, attitudes and lived experiences related to PC at EOL.

IPA was selected for this study for its suitability in examining how individuals interpret and give meaning to complex, emotionally sensitive and culturally embedded experiences [36]. This approach enables a nuanced understanding of how Arab patients navigate personal, familial, spiritual and cultural dimensions in face of terminal illness, and how these factors shape their dying experiences, the meanings they give to them and their intentions to seek PC.

Participants and sampling

A total of 14 individuals participated in the study, including 13 terminally ill Arab patients and one family member of a dying patient. Participants were recruited from multiple medical departments, including internal medicine, surgical and gynecology units, oncology outpatient clinics, and cardiac intensive care units in four hospitals located in different regions across Israel: Galilee Medical Center (northern Galilee), Carmel Medical Center (Haifa district), Hillel Yaffe Medical Center (Central/Hasharon region), and Hadassah Medical Center (Jerusalem district). In the Israeli context, patients with terminal illness who are managed in the community are most often already integrated into PC services, whereas preliminary consultations with clinicians prior to the study indicated that most initial referrals to PC are made by hospital physicians. Because one of our key inclusion criteria was that participants had not yet received PC, hospitals were therefore the most appropriate setting to identify terminal Arab patients at this specific pre-PC stage, when they had not yet been exposed to palliative services and could offer first-hand accounts of their familiarity with PC and their perceptions, expectations and concerns regarding it. Convenience and purposive sampling methods [37] were used to ensure variation in gender, age, religion and geographic distribution.

Two additional hospitals, Soroka Medical Center (serving primarily the Bedouin Arab population in the south) and the English Hospital in Nazareth were initially included in the recruitment plan. However, due to difficulties in recruiting patients willing and able to participate in emotionally intense interviews, limited availability of eligible individuals, significant disruptions caused by the outbreak of two armed conflicts in Israel during the data collection period, which affected access to hospitals and participant engagement, departmental barriers and staff constraints, recruitment of more patients, especially from these two remaining centers, was ultimately discontinued.

Participants’ inclusion criteria were: [1] self-identified as Arab citizens of Israel; [2] following a physician-confirmed terminal diagnosis; [3] able to give informed consent to participation in the study; [4] with sufficient cognitive, physical and emotional capacity to participate in an in-depth, semi-structured interview; and [5] had not engaged in prior consultation or care involving a palliative care team, either in the hospital or in community-based settings. During the recruitment process, two additional individuals were identified, but ultimately not included in the study. One was a patient who reported experiencing significant physical weakness and chose not to participate; and in the other case, it was determined upon further inquiry that the patient had already been involved in a prior palliative care discussion with the clinical team, and therefore did not meet the inclusion criteria.

Recognizing the pivotal role of the family in Arab culture both in caregiving and in decision-making regarding treatment and research participation, in one exceptional case we allowed a close family member to be interviewed as a proxy informant when the patient appeared too frail while gave his consent to participate. Out of the total, we successfully interviewed 13 patients and, in one case, a family member. In that instance, the patient chose not to participate due to fatigue and weakness, but wanted his family member, who was fully informed regarding his attitudes, concerns and perceptions regarding EOL and PC be interviewed. This interview was analyzed with caution and used primarily to contextualize the themes, rather than as a direct substitute for the patient’s own voice, and we acknowledge this proxy interview as a methodological limitation of the study.,.

Data collection

After obtaining institutional review board (IRB) approvals from all participating hospitals in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki of all participating medical institutions, the research team collaborated with clinical staff across departments to present the study’s objectives and identify eligible participants. Information regarding the study was shared during clinical staff briefings and through direct communication with department heads, physicians and social workers.

Eligible participants were referred by clinical teams based on their ability to provide rich, reflective narratives relevant to the study’s aims. Healthcare professionals were asked to assess patients’ emotional and cognitive readiness to participate and to initiate initial contact. Once interest was expressed, the first author approached each participant directly, explained the study in Arabic, answered any questions, and obtained written informed consent prior to the interview.

Data collection was conducted between July 2023 and July 2025 using semi-structured, in-depth interviews. The extended time frame reflecting disruptions and recruitment challenges related mainly to the outbreak of war and ongoing security emergencies in Israel during this period as well as the complicity of the inclusion criteria All interviews were conducted in Arabic by author 1 who is a native Arabic speaker, a researcher and a member of the Arab community in Israel. This shared cultural and linguistic background helped to establish trust, ensure cultural sensitivity and facilitate open conversations on topics often considered taboo, such as death, dying, spirituality and EOL preferences. Given the participants’ physical and emotional state, the interviews were relatively short but rich, lasting between 20 and 124 min, with an average duration of 47 min. When interviews were at the shorter end of this range (for example, when patients experienced fatigue or discomfort, fluctuating levels of alertness, pain, or emotional distress, as well as by explicit requests from patients to pause or terminate the interview or interruptions in the hospital environment, and clinical procedures that restricted the time available for the interview), the semi-structured guide was used more flexibly that it has been used, namely the interviewer prioritized key domains such as the illness experience, understanding of prognosis, perceptions and meanings of PC and EOL, and the role of religion, family and trust in the healthcare system, while less central probes from the structured interview guide were omitted or condensed as needed. Some of the interviews were conducted at the patient’s bedside in the hospital ward, when patients were too weak to move around, while others were conducted in a separate, quiet and private room, usually reserved for medical staff or ambulatory/day patients in the hospital. In bedside interviews, privacy was maintained by use of curtains between beds, and patients were often in quiet, uncrowded rooms due to their medical condition. Family members were usually present from the beginning of the interview or joined it in the middle, and often tended to participate actively, although efforts were made to hear the patients on their own. Throughout, the interviewer gave deliberate priority to the patient’s voice, ensuring that, despite the presence of family, the patient had sufficient opportunity to express their feelings, perspectives, understanding of PC, and preferences regarding the end of life. These methodological choices are consistent with recommended practices in qualitative research on EOL issues, which emphasize the patient’s ability and willingness, cultural sensitivity, and the ethical obligation to maintain the integrity of patient-centered data [38, 39].

Before entering the empirical field, both authors shared their positions and set of beliefs concerning the key issues to be analyzed in the study. Following each interview, the researchers wrote a reflexive journal entry documenting key impressions, emotional responses and contextual factors. These notes were later used to enrich the interpretive depth of the analysis and contextualize participants’ narratives. The authors’ more advanced reflections following the study completion served to further conceptualize their experience in qualitatively researching a delicate and complex issue, involving cultural issues and engaging in responsive cultural inquiry [40].

The interviews were recorded and transcribed in Arabic by a professional Arabic – language Editor specializing in transcription and translation, and then translated into Hebrew by the same professional to allow comprehensive analysis by both researchers [41]. Selected quotations were further translated into English by the research team. All the translated transcripts were subsequently reviewed and validate by a bilingual member of the research team, to verify accuracy and to preserve cultural and linguistic nuance as well as to maintain the original tone meaning [42].

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Research Ethics Committee at the authors’ academic institution (removed for double-blind review), as well as from the ethics committees of all participating medical institutions.

Before signing the informed consent form, a detailed explanation of the study was provided to participants in Arabic, often in the presence of family members, who were encouraged to ask questions and clarify any concerns prior to the interview, fostering transparency and mutual understanding. While the interviews were conducted primarily with the patient as the central respondent, in six cases, family members requested to be present during the conversation, based on the interviewer’s professional clinical experience and field observations in this study, family members’ presence generally appeared to facilitate rather than inhibit patients’ participation. In practice, their presence typically had a calming and supportive effect: patients appeared more relaxed, maintained eye contact with the interviewer and were able to sustain their narrative within the limits of their medical condition. While, at time, relatives helped clarify specific points or add information on certain topics, we recognize that their presence might also have limited the expression of some more private or sensitive views that patients would have voiced differently in a one-to-one setting. For this reason, we highly reflected on these interviews, and treated family members’ contributions as supplementary and interpreted patients’ accounts with awareness of this context. Since we did not systematically document whether accompanying relatives consisted of the head of the family or the primary decision-maker, we acknowledge that intra-familial hierarchies may have influenced the narrative, but to very limited extent provided patients’ ability to participate in the interview was completely free and voluntary.

Interview guide

A semi-structured interview guide was developed collaboratively by the two researchers, specifically for this study, grounded in an extensive review of contemporary literature, and informed by Ajzen’s Theory of Planned Behavior (1991), (see appendix 1). The interview guide was designed to explore the experiences, beliefs and intentions of terminal Arab patients regarding PC at the EOL, while remaining sensitive to cultural and religious nuances. The guide included six sections: [1] Sociodemographic and medical background: participants were asked about their age, gender, marital status, number of children, education level, religious affiliation, level of religiosity, income status, diagnosis and treatment history. This section established context and allowed the interviewer to adapt subsequent questions appropriately; [2] Perception and attitudes regarding PC: participants were invited to share their understanding of PC, their views regarding it, and their willingness to seek such care. Example questions included: “Has someone you know received or is receiving palliative care?“, “What have you heard about that care.?“, “Please share your perceptions, thoughts or impressions of it?“; [3] Death anxiety: to explore emotional responses to death, participants were asked, for example, “When the topic of death is raised, which feelings arise for you?“, “Which close people do you share these feelings with?“, “Which health professionals do you share them with?“; [4] Dying anxiety: questions in this section focused on how participants imagined their final days and their emotional preparation for death, for example, “When speaking of end of life, how do you describe or imagine your own dying process?“, “What emotions arise when you share these thoughts?“, “Could you try to tell me what specifically scares you most about this process?“; [5] External support systems and interpersonal relationships: this section focused on the rational and spiritual context and began with an open question, “With whom do you typically discuss your concerns for example: about health status, seeking care, palliative care, religious permissibility)?“, “Do you feel a desire to share your dilemmas with others?“, “Is there anything that prevents you from sharing? What is it?” As the interview progressed, the interviewer used standardized prompts to ask specifically about the role of family members, close friends and religious or spiritual leaders, in order to explore family dynamic, perceived social support and pattern of spiritual consultation; and [6] Closing and participant reflection: at the end of the interview, participants were encouraged to raise additional topics, ask questions, or share any reflections that emerged during the conversation.

A pilot phase was conducted with two terminally ill Arab patients. Based on their feedback, the guide was revised to improve its clarity, cultural resonance and emotional sensitivity. The final version of the guide was both conceptually grounded and culturally responsive, enabling meaningful exploration of terminal Arab patients’ views and decision-making processes related to PC at the EOL.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed by both researchers to increase trustworthiness [43] using Interpretive Phenomenological Analysis (IPA) [36, 44]. The researchers read through the transcripts multiple times to ensure deep familiarity with the data, paying close attention to language, tone and cultural nuance. Initial codes were then generated inductively, reflecting key ideas, recurring expressions and underlying meanings within participants’ narratives.

A total of 122 codes were developed through this process. The two researchers reviewed the coded data, then engaging in a collaborative analysis process, meeting to discuss, refine and interpret the emerging codes. These discussions involved sharing reflections, identifying conceptual overlaps and clarifying cultural or emotional significances. Through iterative dialogue and consensus-building, the researchers grouped the codes into broader themes. The analytic process was supported by the use of the ATLAS.ti software (v. 9), which facilitated systematic coding, theme development and organization of large volumes of qualitative data.

While each theme addresses a distinct facet of the participants’ experiences, divided in itself into several categories, when seen together, they form a comprehensive picture of the socio-cultural and existential contexts in which PC decisions are made and dying experiences obtain meaning.

Theme 1 God decides, but the system fails: illness perceptions and medical inequity

This theme, which is further divided into two large categories, explores how dying Arab patients interpret the meaning of their illness, and how these interpretations are shaped by religious beliefs, experiences with the healthcare system, and a pervasive sense of ethnic marginalization. Participants described a complex emotional and cognitive process that included religious acceptance on the one hand, and deep frustration, disappointment and mistrust of medical institutions on the other.

Illness as a divine decree

For most participants, the terminal diagnosis was seen not only as a biological event, but also as a decree from God. This view reflects a deeply embedded spiritual framework in which participants expressed a deep faith that God is the one who decides about everything, including illness, suffering and the timing of death. Faith played a central role in helping participants internalize their illness, cope with it emotionally, and seek meaning in their suffering. As Ahmad said, “God gave us the soul, and he is the one who has the right to do with it as he will “. (Ahmad, male, 65 years old, Muslim] Others referred to faith from different points of view: “It’s God’s decision. It’s his right, he created us. When he created us, it was with his knowledge and his power. It’s simply nothing, we’re just particles on this earth, on this ball called Earth”. (Samer, male, 60 years old, Muslim. “The illness returned a year ago, after we found out that my youngest son has a heart problem. I became extremely stressed… Even though I know it’s in God’s hands and not ours, it definitely made the situation worse” (Sari, male, 38 years old, Druze).

Perceived failures in medical care and ethnic discrimination

Despite feeling unable to control their situation, many participants shared their expectations from the healthcare system to monitor it and care for them with dignity. However, they voiced dissatisfaction, even bitterness, toward the existing healthcare system. For some, this dissatisfaction was linked to clinical decisions and everyday interactions that they perceived as being influenced by their Arab identity.

Specifically, they reported frustration from the medical care they received and attributed it, despite being unable to support it, to the fact that they were Arabs:

Last time, after the test results came back bad, after eight months they weren’t good…. I was hoping to see improvement, but things declined. So, I argued with the professor there. I told him: either I’m dying or I’ll come to commit suicide one of the two. You’re reducing my meds, doing this to me… The results clearly got worse. I asked him to return me to the original treatment. And he did without arguing. He saw I was yelling, fighting for it…. I felt there was some kind of racism. (Fawzy, male, 42 years old, Muslim)

She [a nurse] actually made it clear…. She moved him from his private room and kept treating him harshly, both in actions and words. It felt like it was because he’s Arab……. Yes… even the way she spoke to him was different. She said things like: [in a loud voice] ‘What? You’re out all night and then come here during the day just to sleep?!’ The way she spoke was disgusting. (Alaa, male, 38 years old, Christian)

These accounts, voiced by both Muslim and Christian participants and across different hospital settings, illustrate how changes in treatment, room allocation and the tone of communication were interpreted as ethnically biased. While not all participants explicitly used the language of “racism,” those who did framed such experiences as part of a broader pattern of unequal treatment of Arab patients.

These perceptions were not framed as isolated incidents, but rather as symptoms of a deep-rooted mistrust in the system: a context where even sincere and clinically sound recommendations for PC were perceived as abandonment. In this light, care intended to relieve suffering and preserve dignity was instead interpreted by some as a healthcare providers giving up on them and as evidence of institutional inequity.

This theme thus reveals narratives that go beyond individual experiences of illness and dying, reflecting a deeper identity-based process in which Arab patients navigate the tension between religious acceptance of their condition and a sense of alienation from the healthcare system.

Theme 2: faith, control, and family: navigating the EOL journey

This theme examines how Arab patients with terminal illness steer their emotional, spiritual and practical responses to their condition. Rather than regarding their medical diagnosis as a straightforward trajectory of decline, many participants described a complex and dynamic process related to it, involving resistance, reinterpretation and adaptive coping strategies. Such responses included engaging in religious rituals, pursuing alternative therapies, experiencing denial, seeking second medical opinions and involving family members in care decisions. They were all deeply shaped by cultural frameworks and driven by a strong desire to maintain hope and a sense of control. The theme is divided into four large categories presented below.

“Treat your Ill with charity”: religious practices and meaning-making

This theme explores how religious beliefs shape participants’ approaches to terminal illness emotionally, spiritually and practically. Rather than viewing their illness solely as a medical or physical experience, many participants framed it as a moment for religious reflection and an opportunity for moral and spiritual elevation. Faith was not merely a source of comfort, but a motivating force for specific practices and ways through which participants make meaning in the face of suffering and uncertainty. Acts such as prayers, giving charity (sadaqa), and surrendering to God’s will, offered patients both existential grounding and a sense of spiritual agency.

Within Islamic tradition, one widely known saying captures this reported orientation: “Treat your ill with charity” (داووا مرضاكم بالصدقات). This phrase reflects a belief that charitable giving is not only morally virtuous but also spiritually efficacious, so that it can lead to healing, protection from harm or divine forgiveness. Under this worldview illness is not accidental but connected to divine will, and charity becomes a way to seek God’s favor, reduce suffering or atone for one’s sins.

In one interview, the topic of charitable acts emerged as part of a broader moral worldview. The participant described giving part of his own land to his neighbor so that the neighbor’s elderly mother-in-law could access the home more easily. This act, while not involving a direct cash payment, clearly had a material and economic value. Hence, was described as a spiritual offering intended to please God:

I did what I felt I had to do… and more. … My neighbor didn’t have direct passage; I told him: your wife’s mother is old, she shouldn’t have to walk all the way around to visit her daughter. I want to give you two meters so she can enter easily. … I love to do good and cast it into the sea… One day, we’ll be rewarded for it. It’s one of the best things I’ve done. I don’t regret it. I don’t boast about it, I’ve always been like this… We do this for God. He eases many things for us. God encouraged us to do such things, we do it for Him. (Samer, male, 60 years old, Muslim)

This account exemplifies how sadaqa is understood not only as financial charity, but as a generous act performed sincerely for God. The participant linked his behavior to divine instruction and reward, viewing it as part of a reciprocal moral universe in which doing good brings spiritual protection, healing or forgiveness. In the context of terminal illness, such acts became especially meaningful, reaffirming dignity, demonstrating faith and fostering a sense of purpose amid vulnerability.

Seeking control: alternative care and denial

Despite participants’ strong religious faith and belief that their fate rests in God’s hands, they simultaneously expressed a need to regain a sense of control over there. While religion offers meaning, it was often accompanied with actions demonstrating participants’ need for controlling their situation. This paradoxical experience manifested in various ways: Some participants sought treatment for their illness in other hospitals, pursued herbal remedies, or stopped curative treatment when they felt it was no longer effective or tolerable. What unified these diverse actions was the underlying need to exercise agency in the decision-making process itself to actively determine what treatment to pursue and how to manage their condition, rather than passively accepting a predetermined medical path.

Sari shared:

I’ve stopped receiving medication and treatments, because the treatments no longer help me. I went through three treatments with no improvement. My condition only got worse. I started with biological therapy and then chemotherapy. Now I’ve stopped all treatments. I told the doctor I can’t do it anymore. As long as I’m getting treatment, my condition just keeps getting worse. (Sari, male, 38 years old, Druze)Mariam, another participant said:

Mariam, another participant said:

No, I refuse to be referred to palliative care. I want to get rid of this disease on my own terms. At one point, I consulted with someone who would prepare a natural remedy for me… I want to go back to being the person I used to be. I am actively looking for alternative treatments… I want to be released and go home. (Mariam, female, 35 years old, Muslim)

Language barriers to medical Understanding

Language barriers emerged as a key factor affecting participants’ ability to actively engage in medical processes, particularly in sensitive conversations related to PC and EOL decisions. The language challenge expressed by participants stemmed not only from limited proficiency in Hebrew, which is the spoken language in medical settings, but more critically from the difficulty understanding complex medical terminology and professional discourse.

Many participants described relying on family members as communication intermediaries. Hence, the participants’ spouses, children or siblings often accompanied participants to medical appointments, not merely to offer emotional support, but also to translate, explain and ensure comprehension. In such cases, family members also became active in medical decision-making processes.

In addition, participants shared that they clearly preferred their care be provided by Arabic-speaking physicians or those with a similar cultural background. Shared language was perceived as a source of comfort, trust and emotional ease, enabling more open and empathetic interactions. Even participants who were fluent in Hebrew expressed the need to receive explanations in their native language when it came to serious medical issues. Thus, Fatma said:

I know Hebrew, but I don’t understand all the medical language… there are words I don’t get. I can’t just say ‘yes, yes’ when I don’t understand anything. So, if there’s something I need to understand, I bring my son… or his wife. (Fatma, female, 70 years old, Muslim)

The findings explore how communication in a familiar, clear language fosters connection, confidence and greater patient agency in the medical setting. As one participant explained: “My wife talks to him [the doctor] … I speak a little Hebrew… but I don’t understand everything. Yes, I prefer a doctor who speaks Arabic, yes…” (Kamel, male, 48 years old, Muslim).

On the other hand, a few participants did not view language as a barrier. Some were fully proficient in Hebrew, while others had become accustomed to interacting with Hebrew-speaking medical teams over the years. Still, these minority cases underscored the significant contrast with the experiences of many others, highlighting the need for culturally and linguistically adapted communication.

Family Involvement in Decision-Making: A Multifaceted Phenomenon Decision-making by family members in cases of serious illness emerged in the participants’ narratives as a widespread, multidimensional phenomenon within the Arab community. It encompasses a range of roles, motivations and power dynamics which shape the clinical process in significant ways.

While patients occasionally claimed that they were the primary decision-makers regarding their care, the actual dynamics within families often revealed a more complex picture. In many cases, family members exerted substantial influence over treatment decisions, at times even overriding the patient’s explicitly stated preferences. The caregivers’ influence on such decisions was not always distributed evenly among all family members. Frequently, one family member emerged as the dominant figure, although not acknowledged as such by law, assuming responsibility as coordinator, interpreter and key decision-driver. This typically occurred when the patient lacked full language proficiency, had limited understanding of their medical condition, or was emotionally unprepared to engage with the clinical reality. It was often driven by a sense of urgency, protection or responsibility, and reflected underlying power dynamics shaped by cultural norms, emotional bonds and family hierarchy. This was particularly evident in the account of one participant who found himself leading the decision-making process in the face of familial denial:

I asked the rest of the family what do you understand from this? That the doctors opened the abdomen and closed it because they couldn’t intervene. What more can you understand from that? Even the dumbest person in the world would get it… But they simply wouldn’t accept it. So, I started looking for the best-known surgeons in the world, and eventually concluded they’re all at Hadassah Medical Center. (Alaa, male, 38 years old, Christian)

This example illustrates how clarity and acceptance by one family member can contrast sharply with denial among others, placing the burden of action and advocacy on a single individual.

The family dynamics, while often rooted in care and concern, could occasionally limit the patient’s autonomy and blur the boundaries between support and control in EOL care planning, shaping treatment trajectories according to familial beliefs or emotional readiness rather than the patient’s wishes. One participant, for example, expressed interest in receiving institutional hospice care, yet acknowledged that her son objected to that: “As for me, I don’t avoid the idea… Yes, I would like it. But my son, the one you saw here, refuses”. (Fatma, female, 70 years old, Muslim)

Theme 3: Post-Mortem concerns: fear for the family left behind and the soul’s Barzakh fate

Although illness and EOL experiences were central in the interviews, many participants were equally, or even more, concerned with what would happen after their death, particularly regarding the wellbeing of their children and other close family members. These were not abstract existential anxieties, but expressions of concrete and immediate fears rooted in socio-economic vulnerability, single parenthood and strong familial obligations. For many, the emotional burden of dying was compounded by the terror of leaving behind dependents without sufficient financial, emotional or structural protection. Crying intensely, one participant shouted:

My children… my children… I’m afraid to leave them because of my illness… They don’t want to go to their father… They want to stay at my parents’ house. I asked my mother: ‘Where will my children go?’ She told me they’ll stay with her. And I have money I saved with my sister, I told her to keep it for my children. (Mariam, female, 35 years old, Muslim)

In the transition from life to death, participants focused not only on concern for those left behind, but also expressed concerns for their souls after death, especially in relation to the torments of the grave and divine reckoning. In Islamic culture, and in Muslim communities, the doctrine of Barzakh (برزخ, the intermediate state between death and resurrection in Islamic eschatology) holds that this transition phase grants the soul either rest or suffering, corresponding to its deeds when the person was alive. This theological assumption may explain the depth of anxiety and mental preparation for death. In our study, it found particularly vivid expression in the interviews, as one of the participants shared:

Here, they bury someone and leave immediately… But what is more important the people who came to comfort or to stay a little longer with the deceased? We Muslims believe that the soul returns to the grave to be questioned by the angels of death, and it will be very moved if at least one of its family members remains by its side. So, I said to Adam, my grandson: Please stay for half an hour after the burial, read the Quran by the grave while the angels are questioning. Anyone who wants to leave can do so, you will be there. (Fatma, female, 70 years old, Muslim)

These examples illustrate how dying was not experienced as an isolated, personal event, but rather as a relational crisis threatening to disrupt family continuity and spiritual well‑being in profound ways. On one level, the fear for family members who remain underscores the social and emotional instability that death introduces; and on another, the belief in Barzakh (برزخ) and the post‑mortem interrogation reflects a spiritual dimension in which the individual grapples with accountability before God - a dual concern encompassing both collective family vulnerability and the soul’s fate.

Theme 4: the spiritual architecture of the EOL journey

This theme is structured around two interlocking dimensions underscoring the foundational role of faith in shaping EOL experiences among participants.

Spiritual and religious framing of the dying process

Faith emerged as more than a coping mechanism, it served as the spiritual architecture upon which many participants constructed their understanding of illness, dying and the meaning of their final life stage. Rather than being limited to private belief or ritual observance, religion functioned as a comprehensive interpretive framework shaping how individuals experienced bodily decline, made decisions, related themselves to others and oriented themselves toward the unknown.

Spiritual framing offered coherence and moral direction amid uncertainty. For some, illness was not merely a medical event but a divinely orchestrated test, a means of purification, or an invitation to return to what truly matters. As one participant reflected: “This pain the pain of dying strikes with particular force those who have strayed from what truly matters and failed to honor what is sacred in the eyes of God”. (Fatma, female, 70 years old, Muslim)

In this framing, suffering was not only to be relieved but also spiritually integrated. Several participants described renewed engagement with religious practices following diagnosis or illness that is not necessarily rooted in deep theology, but as a way to reconnect with the values they considered sacred.

I wasn’t very traditional, and I wasn’t that religious… but since I found out about the illness, I started holding on to my religious practices. Not deeply, but at least I make sure to pray and do things like that. (Fawzy, male, 42 years old, Muslim)

Spiritual resistance to sedation and morphine in EOL

Faith also influenced how participants approached medical interventions. Some expressed concerns regarding pain management practices, such as sedation, viewing them as incompatible with divine will or as undermining the spiritual significance of consciously enduring suffering. These theological perceptions often shaped participants’ openness to treatment choices and revealed subtle tensions between the use of pharmacological pain relief and the belief in surrendering to God’s will in the natural process of dying. Specifically, participants expressed fear toward certain PC interventions, particularly the use of morphine and sedation. These practices were not always perceived by participants as legitimate medical means for pain relief or symptom management, but rather as accelerating death or interfering with divine will. Such concerns were often rooted in cultural beliefs and religious frameworks. As one participant explained, sedation was seen not as a compassionate act, but a spiritual transgression:

Sedation? In my view, it’s a lack of faith. I want to physically experience my dying process because it comes from God. If God didn’t want it, He wouldn’t have created it. When I choose something that goes against God’s will, for me, that’s a lack of faith. (Samer, male, 60 years old, Muslim)

This perspective reflects a deeper moral and theological worldview, in which enduring suffering is seen as meaningful, even sacred, and medical interventions addressing human suffering, a legitimate and accepted practice in PC, may be perceived as morally problematic. These attitudes underscore the need for culturally sensitive dialogue when discussing PC options, especially in communities where faith and medical ethics are closely intertwined. In the context of this theme, such attitudes suggest that faith was not a discrete aspect of EOL experiences but a cross-cutting structure framing the entire journey. As such, they shaped how participants faced decline, navigated uncertainty and imbued the process of dying with moral and existential meaning.

Discussion

This study provides new insights into how terminally ill Arab patients in Israel perceive and experience PC at the EOL. The four themes that emerged in the study reveal not only personal accounts of coping with dying and death and with the participants’ physical and existential suffering, but also broader cultural, theological and structural processes that shape their meaning. This section situates these findings within four major issues, discussed below.

Faith and perceived behavioral control

A key finding in this study is the dual role of faith in shaping patients’ experiences. On the one hand, illness was presented as a divine decree, echoing deep theological traditions rooted in Islam, Christianity and Druze spirituality. These perceptions often reduced patients’ sense of control and choice, framing suffering as a test or divine will. On the other hand, faith also empowered patients by offering frameworks of meaning and concrete practices, such as prayers, charitable acts (sadaqa), and ritual observance, all of which created a sense of moral responsibility and agency at EOL.

Applying Ajzen’s Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB), these findings complicate the concept of perceived behavioral control, referring to the individual’s perception of the ease or difficulty of performing a particular behavior, shaped by both past experiences and anticipated obstacles [44]. As shown in this study, patients’ perceived control was not limited to their ability to receive medical treatment or care, also being spiritual and symbolic. Seeking alternative treatments, negotiating with doctors, or even denying the terminal nature of the disease, all reflected attempts to regain control in ways consistent with cultural and religious worldviews.

In addition, our findings indicate that faith functions not only as a source of meaning and spiritual resilience but also, in some cases, as a contributing factor to patients’ reluctance to engage with PC services. This dual role has been described in previous literature, where religious belief supports psychological endurance and aids existential adjustment, but may simultaneously reinforce expectations for divine intervention or miraculous cure, leading to delayed acceptance of symptom-focused care or avoidance of discussions around dying [28, 33].Viewed through the lens of TPB, this suggests that perceived behavioral control is mediated by spiritual convictions: while faith may enhance patients’ sense of agency via religious practices, it may also foster perceptions that accepting palliative care equates to diminishing hope or relinquishing spiritual responsibility for fighting the illness. Accordingly, clinicians must navigate the fine balance between validating religiously grounded coping strategies and clarifying that palliative care is not antithetical to faith rather, it can coexist with spiritual perseverance and may reinforce dignity and moral preparedness at the EOL [45].

Such understanding is consistent with previous studies in Muslim and Christian contexts, where religious belief re-defines illness as part of a moral-spiritual narrative rather than a purely medical event [46, 47]. The current study expands the existing literature on this topic by showing how perceived control can coexist with submission to divine authority, revealing a paradox of control at the EOL, suggesting that EOL decision-making cannot be fully understood through biomedical or psychological frameworks alone, and that it is important to incorporate cultural and religious dimensions into the understanding of health, illness and EOL decision-making. In addition, our findings highlight that misconceptions surrounding PC and pain management, such as perceiving PC as abandonment or equating opioid-based analgesia with hastening death, further reduced willingness to accept symptom-oriented care. These perceptions align with previously reported stigma towards PC in Israel and in other settings [16, 33], and underscore the critical role of healthcare providers in actively correcting misinformation, normalizing pain management as an essential component of care, and communicating PC as supportive rather than curative. Such educational and communicative efforts are particularly important in culturally sensitive contexts, where religious beliefs shape attitudes toward suffering, but do not inherently contradict the provision of compassionate symptom relief.

However, our findings also align with contemporary scholarship highlighting that dynamics such as death denial and tensions around patient autonomy are not derived solely from religious worldviews. Rather, they constitute broader, cross-cultural features of the EOL process. Studies across diverse populations have demonstrated that denial functions as an adaptive coping mechanism, enabling patients to preserve hope and maintain psychological stability when confronting terminal illness [28, 29] Similarly, debates around autonomy at the EOL have been identified as universal, often reflecting the interplay between individual identity, emotional readiness, and the existential threat posed by terminal diagnoses, irrespective of religious affiliation [48, 49] Importantly, these dynamics are further contextualized by the broader sociopolitical reality in Israel: longstanding tensions linked to the Arab–Israeli conflict have been shown to affect Arab patients’ perceptions of institutional trust and may amplify reluctance to engage in EOL discussions or accept palliative interventions [24, 50, 51]. In this sense, religious and cultural interpretations do not generate mistrust in isolation but intersect with historical and political experiences of marginalization, shaping how symptom management, including opioid-based analgesia, and PC are perceived. Within the present study, religious frameworks shaped the interpretation of these dynamics for example, linking endurance of suffering to spiritual reward or equating acceptance of palliative care with surrendering control, but did not create them in isolation. Rather, religious belief provided culturally embedded meaning to coping responses that are broadly observed across EOL clinical settings.

Subjective norms and the complexity of family dynamics

The second issue evolving from the findings concerns family involvement in EOL decision-making, which underscores the centrality of subjective norms under TPB. As found in this study, patients often deferred to family members, whether willingly or under pressure, as linguistic and cultural intermediaries. In several cases, family influence overrode patients’ stated preferences, highlighting how caregiving norms of protection and responsibility may conflict with individual autonomy.

While these findings align with the existing literature, where medical decisions are embedded in family hierarchies and communal values [48, 52, 53], the accounts in this study highlight a unique tension: family members acted out of love and concern, yet their interventions could blur the line between support and control of the dying patient. Patients were sometimes silenced or overruled in decisions about PC, revealing a collision between Western bioethical emphasis on autonomy and local cultural imperatives of interdependence [48, 49].

Within this relational context, some participants and their families explicitly framed PC as “losing hope” or “giving up the fight by the healthcare providers,” so that accepting PC was perceived less as an additional layer of support and more as a symbolic transition from striving for cure to preparing for death a perception that echoes prior evidence of PC stigma and negative stereotypes that reduce willingness to use such services [33].

This finding challenges universalist assumptions in PC ethics [49, 54] placing primary weight on patients’ individual autonomy and assuming that family involvement in PC corresponds to patient choice [55, 56]. Instead, it underscores the need for culturally adapted frameworks of family involvement shaping the patient’s relational autonomy, thereby respecting patients’ embeddedness in familial networks while safeguarding their dignity and voice. Importantly, the patient-family dynamics in Arab society were amplified by linguistic barriers, which shifted agency to family members who could translate and deliver the medical information for the patient in ways that were unknown either to the patient or the medical team. Relying on family as language and cultural mediators not only followed a practical necessity, but also served as a culturally sanctioned process reconfiguring the decision-making power.

Trust, discrimination, and the healthcare system

A particularly salient theme emerging from this study was the coexistence of religious acceptance of death with profound mistrust of healthcare institutions. Participants frequently described neglect, indifference or perceived discrimination, often attributing these experiences to their Arab identity. These findings resonate with prior scholarship demonstrating that minority groups in various contexts experience systemic inequities and interpersonal microaggressions that undermine their trust in medical care [50, 51, 57]. Reflecting on this issue as an Arab and a native Arabic speaker (author 1), it was evident to me that even subtle acts of exclusion provoked powerful emotional responses. Accepting or declining PC in such a context was therefore not perceived merely as a medical decision, but as part of a fractured and historically charged relationship between the Arab community and healthcare institutions.

Yet these narratives must be situated within a paradoxical reality, whereby a significant portion of the healthcare professionals in Israel are Arab. Hence, 25% of all physicians, 27% of nurses, and nearly half (49%) of all pharmacists in Israel are Arabs [35]. Broader assessments suggest that approximately 40% of the entire healthcare workforce across professional and auxiliary roles is Arab. The relative overrepresentation re- their demographic share (approximately 21% of the general population) positions the Arab minority as indispensable to the functioning of the Israeli health system [58]. The simultaneous presence of mistrust in this system on the one hand and the strong professional representation in it on the other can be explained in the following manner. While the prominence of Arab healthcare workers suggests that patients may encounter many caregivers from their own community, speaking their language and sharing cultural frames of reference, participants’ accounts underscore that such a representation does not equal their recognition. This disjuncture is further illuminated by studies showing that many Arab physicians in Israel tend to view their careers primarily as individual professional integration and as breaking a “glass ceiling,” rather than as representing “the Arab sector”. They are integrated into institutions whose leadership, norms and policy frameworks are largely shaped by the Jewish majority, so that their numerical visibility does not necessarily translate into structural equality, redistribution of power, or a felt sense of institutional recognition among Arab patients [59] The institutional culture in general and decision-making at EOL specifically are perceived by Arab patients as inequitable and exclusionary. Even when treated by Arab professionals, the systemic backdrop of marginalization means that clinical encounters are often interpreted through a lens of ethnic inequality. These patterns resonate with international evidence indicating that structural barriers, socio-economic deprivation and racialization shape access to palliative and EOL care in many regions worldwide, including Latin America, sub-Saharan Africa and other marginalized populations, where only a minority of those in need actually receive PC [60–63].

This paradox reveals that improving PC for minority patients requires more than numerical diversity within the workforce. It calls for a cultural transformation that acknowledges and actively addresses perceptions of inequity. Rebuilding trust will require not only Arabic-speaking clinicians, but also honest institutional commitments to equity, dignity and transparency in the framing and delivery of PC. Without such systemic changes, the presence of Arab professionals may be experienced by patients as insufficient to counterbalance broader structures of exclusion.

Post-Mortem concerns and the spiritual architecture of dying

A distinctive contribution of this study is its illumination of patients’ concerns at EOL that extended beyond their own lives into the fate of their families and souls. Fear for dependents, particularly children, underscored the socio-economic vulnerabilities of Arab families, where illness and death threaten not only emotional continuity but also financial stability. These anxieties deepen our understanding of the EOL as a relational crisis rather than an individual event [64, 65].

Equally salient was the theological concern with Barzakh, the intermediate state of the soul, and the moral weight of conscious suffering. Patients feared sedation and morphine, interpreting them not only as clinical interventions but also as moral compromises that could deprive them of the spiritual integrity of dying. Such a belief aligns with more studies on religion and health, where suffering is often framed as spiritually meaningful [14, 66, 67]. In this study, enduring pain was viewed as a final act of surrender to God, an interpretation reconfiguring the ethical calculus of pain management. For patients, agreeing to sedation was not just a clinical choice but also an existential and theological decision. These findings emphasize the need for clinicians to treat pain management not simply as a biomedical necessity, but also as a spiritual dialogue. In line with established PC principles, effective pain management should integrate physical, psychological, social and spiritual dimensions of suffering, recognizing that analgesia decisions are embedded in patients’ values and belief systems [68]. Thus, PC teams should engage in culturally sensitive conversations which acknowledge these frameworks rather than dismissing them as irrational or irrelevant to pain management.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size is relatively small and the interview durations were generally brief (typically up to approximately thirty minutes), reflecting the considerable challenges involved in recruiting Arab patients with terminal illness who were emotionally and cognitively capable of participating in in‑depth interviews. These challenges were amplified by the broader context of the study period, during which many key hospital staff were mobilized for military reserve duty and medical centers faced substantial overloads, leaving limited time and capacity to identify and approach eligible participants. While qualitative research does not aim to represent the population of which the participants are members [69, 70], such a limitation calls for further examination and evaluation of our rich findings to other individuals in the broader Arab population.

Second, the inclusion criteria focused solely on patients with a confirmed terminal diagnosis who had not yet been referred to PC, with the purpose of having an open and first-hand conversation regarding PC with the participants. In practice, this required physicians to define patients as “dying” under the Israeli Dying Patient Act while they were still pre-PC, a combination that many clinicians found difficult to operationalize in routine care; in several cases, patients initially identified as potentially eligible were already under the care of palliative teams and therefore had to be excluded. As such, this may have excluded from our study patients with an advanced (but not terminal) illness, who may also have benefited from PC and held different attitudes. Future studies should consider including patients with broader clinical statuses.

Third, although the Arab population in Israel is religiously diverse, our sample consisted predominantly of Muslim participants, with Christian and Druze patients under-represented. As a result, the findings primarily reflect the experiences of Muslim patients and may not fully capture potential intra-group differences in EOL experiences and attitudes toward PC among other Arab religious communities. Future research should purposively include larger numbers of Christian, Druze and other Arab minorities to explore such differences.

Fourth, the sample includes one exceptional proxy interview with a close family member of a terminally ill patient who appeared too frail while gave his consent to participate directly. Though this interview offers contextual insight into family perspectives on EOL and PC, we acknowledge that a family member cannot fully represent the patient’s own attitudes and experiences, so that the inclusion of this interview into our data may affect their overall integrity and homogeneity, this interview was analyzed with caution and used primarily to contextualize, rather than to substitute for, the patient’s voice. Additionally, in six interviews, family members were present at the patient’s request or cultural preference. While we respected participants’ wishes and cultural norms regarding family presence during sensitive conversations, we acknowledge that the presence of family members may have influenced what patients felt comfortable expressing. Patients may have withheld certain views or feelings they would have shared in a one-on-one setting, particularly if a family decision-maker or head of household was present. The potential impact of family dynamics on participant responses including power hierarchies and cultural expectations around family roles in medical decision-making represents an important limitation. We have analyzed the methodological and ethical implications of conducting interviews in the presence of family members in recent study [40].

Fifth, although participants were recruited from various geographical regions across Israel, the study did not include individuals from the Bedouin population, mainly because the majority of these individuals live in Southern Israel that was greatly affected by the Hamas attack on October 7th and the security emergencies at the time of conducting this study. As this group faces unique cultural, social and structural challenges, the involvement of members of this community in future research is required in order to understand the great diversity of the Arab community in Israel.

Lastly, while author 1, who conducted the interviews, is a native Arabic speaker and shared a cultural background with the participants, which facilitated trust and culturally sensitive dialogue, this may also have influenced participants’ responses or introduced interpretive bias. To mitigate this, the research process incorporated a reflexive approach and collaborative analysis meetings between the two researchers [71, 72].

Conclusion

This study improves our understanding of the EOL experiences of Arab patients in Israel by demonstrating how cultural, religious and structural factors influence perceptions of PC. The findings complicate Ajzen’s concept of perceived behavioral control by showing that patients’ sense of control extends beyond biomedical choices to include spiritual and symbolic dimensions. This produces a paradox in which the pursuit of control coexists with submission to divine authority. The importance of family caregivers not only highlights the significance of subjective norms in decision-making processes, but also reveals tensions where familial influence differs from patients’ own preferences. This emphasizes the need to reconcile relational autonomy with the ethical importance of the patient’s voice. Narratives of mistrust towards healthcare institutions reveal that the mere presence of Arab professionals is insufficient without systemic reforms addressing inequities in recognition and care delivery. Finally, patients’ reflections on Barzakh and conscious suffering show that dying is experienced as a moral and relational process, rather than just a clinical event. Together, these insights contribute to the existing literature and emphasize the need for culturally informed, patient-centered PC frameworks.
