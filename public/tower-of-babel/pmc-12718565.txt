Healthcare use and costs of functional somatic disorder in Denmark: a population-based cohort study (DanFunD)
Authors: Marie Weinreich Petersen, Kaare Bro Wellnitz, Eva Oernboel, Tina Birgitte Wisbech Carstensen, Thomas Meinertz Dantoft, Anne Ahrendt Bjerregaard, Torben Joergensen, Per Fink
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionStudies investigating the economic burden of functional somatic disorder (FSD) in general populations are lacking. The current study incorporated data from a randomly sampled general population to examine healthcare use and costs in FSD using comprehensive national register data.MethodsThe study included 9656 adults from the DanFunD general population cohort study. FSD was identified via self-reported questionnaires and verified by diagnostic interviews conducted by a physician. Data on healthcare were sourced from the comprehensive Danish central registries in the period 10 years before and 4 years after DanFunD inclusion. Healthcare use was investigated with Poisson and Zero-inflated Poisson regression models. Healthcare costs were investigated with linear regression models. Analyses comprised sex- and age-adjusted regression models with non-parametric bootstrap resampling (10 000 repetitions).ResultsIndividuals with FSD used more healthcare facilities and it costed more compared with individuals without FSD. For example, the incidence rate ratio of having used healthcare services for participants with interview-diagnosed severe FSD was 1.62 (95% CI 1.37 to 1.94) in the 10-year period before DanFunD inclusion, and the annual healthcare costs were 1038 € (339 € to 1846 €) higher. Individuals with FSD also had a higher use of healthcare than individuals with other severe physical disease, but in most cases, no differences were found for costs.ConclusionsFSD carries a significant economic burden on society due to increased healthcare use and costs compared with individuals without FSD, and for most, also for individuals with other severe physical disease.Trial registration numberNCT05811663.Keywords: Epidemiology, Public Health, Epidemiologic Factors

Introduction

Functional somatic disorder (FSD) is a common condition characterised by persistent patterns of physical symptoms that cannot be better explained by other physical or mental conditions. The unifying diagnostic construct of bodily distress syndrome (BDS) may be used to operationalise the FSD diagnosis.1 Additionally, various other names are used for FSD, such as irritable bowel syndrome, fibromyalgia/chronic widespread pain, chronic fatigue syndrome and other functional somatic syndromes.

FSD may cause severe impairment in physical and mental health, lower social status and poor labour market participation.2,4 FSD cannot be diagnosed through clinical or paraclinical tests, but it is a clinical diagnosis relying on identifying its characteristic symptom pattern. This makes diagnosing FSD challenging. Due to limited familiarity with FSD among healthcare professionals, patients often consult multiple specialists with little or no success before receiving an accurate diagnosis. Besides being costly, this delay in proper diagnosis and treatment increases the risk of the condition becoming chronic.5

In 2005, it was estimated that FSD accounted for 3% of hospitalisations and 10–20% of healthcare expenses in Denmark.6 A Danish primary care study also showed that patients with FSD incur higher annual healthcare costs compared with those with other conditions.7 Additionally, a population-based study reported increased healthcare use and costs for individuals with irritable bowel syndrome.8 Outside Denmark, clinical studies have shown that FSD leads to increased direct and indirect healthcare costs9 10 with a dose-response relationship to the severity of the disorder.11 A Canadian population-based study found that children, adolescents and young adults with a first diagnosis of somatic symptom disorder and related disorders had higher healthcare use and costs.12 Even though these previous studies provide valuable knowledge to the field of FSD, their methodology may challenge the generalisation of their results, ie, inclusion of highly selected patient samples, a specific focus on only one functional somatic syndrome and the establishment of the diagnosis by means of self-report.

Studies investigating healthcare use and costs of FSD in general population-based samples using solid methodology are highly lacking. Additionally, Denmark has a universal tax-funded healthcare system with residency-based entitlement. A significant advancement in this field was the introduction of the Danish civil registration number (CPR number) in 1968.13 The CPR number has enabled individual-level data recording across various registries and the possibility of lifelong follow-up. Denmark boasts a comprehensive network of population-based registries and databases that routinely collect data from primary and secondary healthcare as well as pharmaceutical sales, among other sources. Compared with non-Nordic countries, this unique infrastructure has significantly enhanced the quality and scope of registry-based research in Denmark, a unique opportunity to investigate the use and costs of healthcare.14

The objectives of the present study were to describe and investigate the healthcare use and healthcare costs for individuals with FSD compared with (1) individuals without FSD and (2) individuals with other severe physical disease.

Methods

Study participants

The DanFunD (Danish Study of Functional Disorders) baseline cohort was included in the present study.15 Participants were randomly drawn from the Danish Civil Registration system,16 and exclusion criteria were as follows: not born in Denmark, not being a Danish citizen and pregnancy.

The DanFunD baseline cohort comprises a total of 9656 (33.7% of the invited participants) men and women aged 18–76 years born in Denmark and living in the Western part of greater Copenhagen. All participants filled in questionnaires about, among others, physical symptoms, psychological factors, social life, etc. A stratified subsample (n=2450) of the participants was invited to participate in a diagnostic interview (the Research Interview for Functional Somatic Disorders, RIFD) performed by trained family physicians.17 This sub-sample included every tenth participant and all participants with high symptom scores on the DanFunD baseline symptom questionnaires. A total of 1590 (64.9%) participants accepted and participated in the interview. Hence, in this study, FSD was measured through self-reported questionnaires in the total sample of 9656 participants and through diagnostic interviews in the stratified subsample of 1590 participants.

Ethics statement

This study was performed in line with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Approval was granted by the Ethical Committee of the Capital Region (H-3-2011-081 and H-3-2012-015). Written informed consent was obtained from all individual participants included in the study.

Independent variables

Definition of functional somatic disorder

FSD was defined as the unifying diagnostic construct of bodily distress syndrome (BDS). BDS divides patients into two subgroups: a single-organ subgroup, ie, patients with symptoms from one or two of four symptom clusters (cardiopulmonary, gastrointestinal, musculoskeletal and general symptoms/fatigue), and a multi-organ subgroup, ie, patients with symptoms from at least three of four symptom clusters.1 18 The diagnostic construct has been validated and confirmed in several clinical and population-based studies and has been shown to encompass a range of specialty-specific functional somatic syndromes, such as irritable bowel syndrome, fibromyalgia/chronic widespread pain and chronic fatigue syndrome.19,22 These conditions are well-known and used within somatic medical practice. Therefore, we present data using both the FSD (BDS) diagnosis and three commonly used functional somatic syndrome diagnoses, irritable bowel (IB),23 chronic widespread pain (CWP)24 and chronic fatigue (CF)25 in the present paper.

Assessment of functional somatic disorder

Individuals with FSD were identified with the self-reported Bodily Distress Syndrome Checklist,26 including bothersome physical symptoms within the last 12 months. Additionally, a stratified subsample of participants with a clinical diagnosis of FSD was identified with the RIFD interview, developed to be used as a second phase tool after a respondent’s self-report in symptom questionnaires.17 The RIFD interviews were performed by three trained primary care physicians over the telephone. The physicians assessed whether a specific symptom pattern was due to a FSD rather than another physical or mental condition. The RIFD interview has shown good criterion validity for identifying individuals with FSD.17

Cases with IB,23 CWP24 and CF25 were identified with self-reported validated symptom questionnaires, including bothersome symptoms within the last 12 months.

Individuals without FSD constituted the control group in analyses on FSD while individuals without IB, CWP, and CF constituted the control group in analyses on IB, CWP and CF.

Severe physical disease

Individuals reporting to have at least one of the following diagnoses, such as cancer, myocardial infarction, other heart disease, obstructive pulmonary disease and stroke, were categorised as having a severe physical disease and served as a second comparison group (objective 2). Diseases were identified with one item “Has a doctor ever told you that you have/had…” from the questionnaire battery.

Primary outcomes

Overall healthcare use and costs were calculated based on healthcare resources in primary care, in- and outpatient general hospital care, in- and outpatient mental healthcare and medication.

Use of primary care

Use of primary care was extracted from the Danish National Health Service Register for Primary Care.27 The register contains data from all providers in primary care and includes information about all Danish citizens, providers and health services since 1990. In the current study, this category included the total number of services (all kinds of consultations, ie, face-to-face consultations, telephone consultations, laboratory tests etc.) in general practice, medical specialists (doctors on house calls, ophthalmologist, ear, nose and throat specialist, other medical specialist), physiotherapists, chiropractors and psychologists.

Use of in- and outpatient general hospital and mental healthcare

Use of both in- and outpatient general hospital and mental healthcare was extracted from the National Patient Registry.28 The register contains information on all patients from Danish general hospitals since 1977 and since 1995 on psychiatric inpatients and emergency department and outpatient speciality clinic contacts. Information includes administrative data, diagnoses, treatments and examinations. In the current study, this category included number of bed days and outpatient visits in general hospitals and mental healthcare.

Use of medication

Use of medication was extracted from the National Prescription Registry.29 The register contains information on prescriptions redeemed in Denmark from community pharmacies since 1995. In the current study, prescription medication was classified as number of prescriptions.

Healthcare costs

Except for a few services (e.g. dental healthcare), the Danish healthcare system is entirely funded through taxation, allowing Danish residents to receive medical care for free. Healthcare costs investigated in the current study were sourced from national health registers.27,29 These cost data are organised using Diagnosis Related Group (DRG) codes, reflecting the average costs for specific procedures or hospital stays for both in-patient and out-patient treatments at Danish hospitals. Additionally, the data includes actual reimbursement figures for primary care and specialist services, as well as public expenditures for medication.

Statistical analyses

Registry data of both healthcare use and healthcare costs were obtained during a period covering 10 years before and 4 years after each participant’s participation in the DanFunD baseline investigation. Separate analyses for each period were conducted.

Descriptive statistics for the annual healthcare use and costs were shown with medians and IQRs.

Individuals with FSD (regardless of whether they also had another severe physical disease) were compared with individuals without FSD (regardless of whether they also had another severe physical disease); the latter as the reference (i.e. controls). Individuals with FSD (and without any other severe physical disease) were compared with individuals with a severe physical disease (and without an FSD), the latter as the reference group.

To account for outliers, non-parametric bootstrap resampling with 10 000 repetitions was performed, maintaining number of individuals in the diagnostic groups. Results from the 10 000 bootstrapped analyses on both use and costs were reported as medians and 95% percentile bootstrapped CIs.

All analyses were adjusted for sex and age. Age was modelled as natural cubic splines with five knots at the 5th, 27.5th, 50th, 72.5th and 95th percentiles according to the recommendations by Harrell.30 Weights were included in the analyses to account for emigration, death and participants younger than 18 years.

Healthcare use

For the analyses of direct healthcare use, sets of regression analyses were conducted (see below) with healthcare variables as the dependent variable. In all analyses, healthcare use was estimated as number of healthcare use per year in the two time periods and included as rounded variables (see sensitivity analysis below).

Overall use of healthcare, outpatient general hospital care, primary care and medication was analysed with Poisson regression models. Incidence rate ratios (IRR) with 95% percentile bootstrapped CI were used as a measure of association.

Use of inpatient general hospital care and in- and outpatient mental healthcare was analysed specifically with zero-inflated Poisson (ZIP) regression to account for a large number of zeroes (ie, no use of the specific category of healthcare). For the zero-inflated part of the ZIP models, ORs with 95% percentile bootstrapped CI were used as a measure of association (ie, OR between not being an extra zero for individuals with FSD compared with individuals without FSD). For the Poisson part of the ZIP models, IRR with 95% percentile bootstrapped CI was used as a measure of association (ie, the ratio of incidence rates among individuals with FSD compared with individuals without FSD).

Healthcare costs

For the analyses of healthcare costs, sets of normal linear regression analyses were conducted. Costs related to total healthcare, in- and outpatient general hospital care, in- and outpatient mental healthcare, primary care and medication, respectively, were included as dependent variables. As data were not normally distributed, the 50% percentile with 95% percentile bootstrapped CI was used as a measure of association. Median costs per year within each period before and after the DanFunD baseline investigation were estimated for all groups and converted to Euro, year 2010 values, using Statistics Denmark’s DRG codes and a currency conversion rate of €1=7.45 Danish kroner.

Sensitivity analyses

One assumption of the Poisson regression model is that the outcome is Poisson distributed and, thus, consists of positive integer values. Initially, the healthcare use variables did not comprise continuous scales with integer values. We therefore made several sensitivity analyses in which these outcome variables were rounded, floored and ceiled, respectively. Results from the sensitivity analyses with floored and ceiled variables largely equalled the results from the sensitivity analyses with rounded outcome variables. Hence, it was reliable to continue with rounded healthcare use variables for the main analyses.

Results

Study participants

Complete sample

The median age of the 9656 DanFunD study participants was 54 years (IQR=44 to 64). A total of 53.9% were women and 67.2% were employed at the time of DanFunD inclusion. Moreover, 1543 had FSD; 1447 single-organ type and 96 multi-organ type, 337 had IB, 442 had CWP, and 823 had CF. 1023 participants reported having severe physical disease, 1588 did not report any severe physical disease but fulfilled the criteria of a FSD (including IB, CWP and CF), and 458 had both a severe physical disease and a FSD (including IB, CWP and CF).

Stratified subsample of interviewed participants

The median age of the stratified subsample of 1590 participants who participated in the diagnostic research interview was 54 years (IQR=44 to 63), 59.3% were women and 63.5% were employed at the time of investigation. At inclusion, 412 had FSD; 326 single-organ type and 86 multi-organ type (IB, CWP and CF were not diagnosed with diagnostic interviews but with symptom questionnaires only). 239 participants had another severe physical disease, 319 did not report any severe physical disease but fulfilled the criteria of an FSD, and 83 had both a severe physical disease and a FSD.

Healthcare costs

At a glance, individuals with FSD, IB, CWP and CF had a higher total healthcare cost per year than individuals without these conditions (table 1). Even though numbers were lower for individuals with interview-diagnosed FSD, they still stood out with higher costs than individuals without FSD (table 1).

In the adjusted analyses, compared with controls, individuals with FSD, IB, CWP and CF according to symptom questionnaires had increased healthcare costs (table 2), and this was also the case for the multi-organ type established with diagnostic interviews (table 3). For example, participants with multi-organ FSD established with symptom questionnaires had a median mean annual healthcare cost that was 4208 € (3050 to 5605 €) higher than individuals without FSD in the 10 year period before DanFunD inclusion. For interview-diagnosed multi-organ FSD, the median mean annual healthcare costs were 1038 € (339 to 1846 €) higher than individuals without FSD in the same period.

Compared with individuals with severe physical disease, the multi-organ type of FSD established with symptom questionnaires had higher healthcare costs in the 10 year period before DanFunD inclusion (table 2). In both time periods, those with questionnaire-based single-organ FSD had lower healthcare costs, and CWP had lower healthcare costs in the 10 year period before DanFunD inclusion. Otherwise, no differences were found.

For individuals with interview-diagnosed FSD, no differences were found in healthcare costs for the multi-organ type, whereas individuals with single-organ FSD had lower healthcare costs compared with individuals with severe physical disease (table 3).

Description of the costs of specific healthcare services is displayed in online supplemental tables S1–S6. A general picture was increased costs of general hospital in- and outpatient healthcare, primary care, prescription medication and psychiatric outpatient healthcare compared with individuals without FSD. However, only costs in primary healthcare remained markedly higher also when compared with individuals with severe physical disease.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to use random population sampling and national register data to examine healthcare utilisation and costs in functional somatic disorder (FSD), defined by both symptom questionnaires and diagnostic interviews using various criteria. We found significantly higher healthcare use and costs in individuals with FSD compared with those without, and this persisted to some degree when comparing to individuals with other severe physical diseases.

Previous population-based studies have shown similar increased healthcare use and costs for individuals with irritable bowel syndrome,8 functional somatic syndromes,10 somatisation31 32 and medically unexplained symptoms.9 11 12 Additionally, a primary care study found increased annual healthcare costs for patients with FSD compared with patients with other diseases.7 The higher healthcare utilisation and costs among individuals with FSD may be attributed to both the condition itself and other factors. Physicians may be unfamiliar with the condition, which can result in delayed or missed diagnoses.33 As patients seek answers for their symptoms, they may consult multiple medical specialists and undergo numerous examinations in search of a diagnosis. Since the majority (99 %) of the Danish population is enlisted with a general practitioner, who serves as the gatekeeper to the secondary healthcare system, this process typically involves multiple referrals between general practitioners to specialised secondary care. Our results reflect this, showing that individuals with FSD have a high number of visits to primary care, whereas those with other severe physical diseases tend to use more secondary healthcare services. Additionally, a previous register-based study indicated that patients with severe FSD had higher comorbidity of general medical conditions compared with healthy controls,34 which may also contribute to the increased direct healthcare utilisation observed in our study. The higher healthcare use and costs associated with questionnaire-based FSD, compared with FSD diagnosed through clinical interviews, may be explained by misclassification due to unexcluded differential diagnoses. These alternative diagnoses, which are typically ruled out during diagnostic interviews, could account for the increased healthcare utilisation among questionnaire-based cases.35

In 2021, the average Dane used 18.7 healthcare services—far fewer than individuals with FSD in our study—highlighting the substantial societal burden of FSD. Effective treatments for FSD are available; for instance, psychotherapy has been shown to improve both physical and mental health outcomes.36 A Danish randomised controlled trial found that cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) significantly enhanced physical and mental well-being as well as social functioning in patients with severe FSD, with benefits sustained in the long term.37 Moreover, the treatment yielded total savings of €7185 per patient 3 years post-intervention, largely due to reduced healthcare utilisation.38 These findings emphasise that timely recognition and appropriate management of FSD can both improve patient outcomes and lower healthcare costs. Given that mental disorders are risk factors for developing FSD, prevention and early intervention should be prioritised in healthcare planning. This includes improving education and training for health professionals to facilitate earlier diagnosis, enhance detection strategies and prevent progression or chronification of symptoms. Equally important is fostering coordinated care across primary, mental health and specialist services to ensure continuity and integration of treatment. Strengthened policy focus and continued research are needed to optimise management pathways, support implementation of effective interventions and ultimately reduce the societal burden of FSD. Prioritising FSD in healthcare planning, through improved education, early diagnosis, and coordinated care across settings, is essential. Continued research and policy support are needed to strengthen FSD care.

Limitations and strengths

A major strength of the study was the use of high-quality, longitudinal data from central Danish registries covering primary and secondary care and prescriptions. Combined with large-scale questionnaire data, diagnostic interviews and weighted analyses over a 14-year period, this ensured robust case identification and accounted for factors like emigration, immigration and death.

Some limitations also have to be addressed: First, the response rate was 33.7% for the questionnaire sample and 64.9% for the interviewed sample due to invited participants declining to participate. Although the risk of selection bias was substantially lower than in clinical studies (since participants were randomly drawn from the general population), we cannot rule it out entirely. Unfortunately, it was not possible to statistically adjust for non-response, for example through post-survey weighting. However, a non-responder analysis suggested that selection bias did not have a noticeable impact on social parameters.4 Second, as the Danish health system is almost entirely tax-funded, the findings from this study may not directly apply to countries with fragmented or insurance-driven systems. Also, private sector data were not included in the estimation which potentially caused underestimation of healthcare use and costs FSD. Third, high comorbidity with physical diseases may complicate attribution of costs solely to FSD, but diagnostic interviews and comparisons support a link. Fourth, we did not take into account the presence of psychiatric disorders in this study. FSD and psychiatric disorders often co-exist; hence, future research could preferably investigate to what extent the increased healthcare use in FSD may be driven by these comorbidities. Finally, FSD was measured at a single time point, which limits the ability to draw conclusions about causality or temporal relationships. Future studies would benefit from incorporating repeated assessments to better capture changes over time and strengthen causal inference.
