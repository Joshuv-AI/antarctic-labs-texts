Association between adherence to the Mediterranean diet and risk of sarcopenia: a systematic review and dose-response meta-analysis of observational studies
Authors: Chenping Xi, Jiaqi Wang, Xiaolin Liu, Caijuan Si, Xiaohua Ke, Huiting Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEpidemiological studies have investigated the potential association between adherence to the Mediterranean diet and risk of sarcopenia, with inconsistent results.ObjectiveThe current systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted to synthesize available evidence regarding the association between adherence to the Mediterranean diet and risk of sarcopenia.MethodsThis review followed PRISMA guidelines and was registered in PROSPERO (CRD420251145903). A comprehensive literature search was conducted in four electronic databases, including ISI Web of Science, PubMed, Embase, China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI) from database inception to July 2025. Random-effects model was applied to calculate the pooled odd ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the highest versus lowest categories of Mediterranean diet score in relation to sarcopenia risk. The Cochran’s Q test and I-squared (I2) statistic were used to detect the sources of heterogeneity among the included studies.ResultsSeven observational studies (3 cohort and 4 cross-sectional studies) with 9,709 participants were included. The results showed that high adherence to the Mediterranean diet was significantly associated with a reduced risk of sarcopenia (OR = 0.81; 95%CI:0.71–0.92, P = 0.002), with substantial heterogeneity (I2 = 70.2%; Pheterogeneity=0.002). Dose-response analysis showed an inverse linear association between adherence to the Mediterranean diet and risk of sarcopenia (OR = 0.873; 95% CI:0.846–0.901, Pdose−response<0.001, Pnonlinearity=0.369). Subgroup analyses revealed an inverse association between adherence to the Mediterranean diet and risk of sarcopenia in the subgroups of sample size < 1000 (OR = 0.34, 95%CI: 0.20–0.58, P < 0.001), questionnaire/food record (OR = 0.83; 95%CI:0.77–0.89, P < 0.001), and posteriori method defining Mediterranean diet(OR = 0.35; 95%CI: 0.19–0.62, P < 0.001), with less evidence of heterogeneity.ConclusionsOur study suggested that high adherence to the Mediterranean diet was associated with a 19% reduced risk of sarcopenia. Further large prospective studies and randomized controlled trials are needed to affirm these findings.Trial registrationPROSPERO CRD420251145903.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40795-026-01434-w.

Introduction

Sarcopenia is a condition characterized by the accelerated loss of muscle mass and strength, which in turn leads to a decline in physical performance [1]. In the latest systematic review and meta-analysis by Sánchez-Sánchez JL et al. [2], the prevalence of sarcopenia was approximately 10%, and the variation in reported prevalence varies largely depending on the diagnostic method or study population. Of relevant note, mounting epidemiological evidence shows that sarcopenia is associated with increased risk of multiple adverse health outcomes, such as falls, frailty, physical disability, longer hospitalisation, and all-cause mortality [3, 4]. Additionally, with global ageing, sarcopenia is also regarded as a major challenge to society and healthcare systems [5]. As far as we know, aging, physical inactivity, malnutrition, smoking, diabetes, metabolic imbalance, chronic inflammation, social factors and dietary factors are contributing factors to the risk of sarcopenia [6]. Therefore, preventive measures to reduce the incidence of sarcopenia are of high priority.

In the past few decades, epidemiological studies have shown that dietary factors play an important role in the prevention of sarcopenia [7], and mostly examined the associations between intakes of individual foods or nutrients and risk of sarcopenia [8–10]. However, in practice, people do not take nutrients alone but consume meals containing many combinations of foods and nutrients [11]. In this context, the study of dietary pattern has been increasingly used in nutritional epidemiology, to take into account the combined effects of foods and potentially facilitate nutritional recommendations [12]. The Mediterranean diet, characterizing by high consumption of fruits, vegetables, nuts, legumes, whole grains and extra-virgin olive oil; moderate consumption of poultry, fish and alcohol; and low consumption of red and processed meats [13], represented a healthy dietary pattern usually consumed in the populations bordering the Mediterranean sea [14]. To date, accumulating epidemiological evidence has shown the beneficial effects of adherence to the Mediterranean diet on multiple noncommunicable diseases, such as cardiovascular diseases, non-alcohol fatty liver disease and some types of cancers [15–17]. Moreover, recent studies also found that higher adherence to the Mediterranean diet was associated with a lower risk of all-cause mortality [18, 19]. Still, little is known with regard to the clear relationship between adherence to the Mediterranean diet and risk of sarcopenia. Nonetheless, based on the characteristics of the Mediterranean diet, it could be an ideal dietary pattern to reduce the risk of sarcopenia.

More recently, there have been increasing attentions in nutritional research regarding the relation between adherence to the Mediterranean diet and sarcopenia risk [20]. A large volume of epidemiological studies mainly focus on individual components of sarcopenia, e.g. walking speed, muscle strength, handgrip strength, physical performance, in relation to the Mediterranean diet [21–23]. Thus far, only seven observational studies have emerged to explore the relationship between adherence to the Mediterranean diet and the risk of sarcopenia [24–30], with inconsistent conclusions. Whilst some studies have reported that adherence to the Mediterranean diet is associated with a lower risk of sarcopenia [24, 25, 27, 29, 30], other studies reported the null findings [26, 28]. Furthermore, there is currently no systematic review and meta-analysis exploring the association between adherence to the Mediterranean diet and sarcopenia risk. Therefore, to address the critical knowledge gap, we performed a comprehensive systematic review and meta-analysis of observational studies to clarify the association between adherence to the Mediterranean diet and risk of sarcopenia.

Methods and materials

Literature search strategy

This study was conducted in strict accordance with the updated 2020 Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [31]. The study protocol has been registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) under the registration number CRD420251145903. Comprehensive literature searches in PubMed, ISI Web of Science, Embase and CNKI from the earliest available date until July 2025, were carried out using a combination of with related free-text terms: (“sarcopenia”[all fields] OR “muscle mass” [all fields] OR “muscle strength”[all fields] OR “muscular atrophy”[all fields] OR “gait speed”[all fields] OR “grip strength”[all fields]) AND (“MedDiet”[all fields] OR “Mediterranean diet” [all fields] OR “Mediterranean”[all fields] OR “dietary pattern”[all fields] OR “dietary score”[all fields] OR “dietary adherence”[all fields]). Furthermore, the bibliographies of the final retrieved articles and relevant reviews on this topic was also manually screened to lessen the chance of missing any eligible articles. Unpublished studies or grey literature were not eligible in this meta-analysis. All published studies were limited to those performed with humans and written in English and Chinese. No restriction was applied regarding publication date. The literature search was carried out by two independent reviewers (CPX and JQW). The complete search strategy in each database can be found in Supplementary Appendix 1.

Selection of studies

Studies were eligible if they fulfilled all the following inclusion criteria: (1) human observational studies, e.g. cross-sectional, case-control and longitudinal cohort studies, published in English and Chinese; (2) adults aged 18 years and older; (3) the exposure of interest was the Mediterranean diet; (4) the outcome of interest was sarcopenia; (5) reported the adjusted estimates of RRs, HRs, ORs of outcome risk and corresponding 95% CIs (or sufficient data for calculating effect estimates); (6) diagnosed sarcopenia was confirmed by medical records review, or self-report on a previous physician-made diagnosis of sarcopenia; (7) If the data in retrieved article lacked sufficient detail, the corresponding author will be contacted via email. Moreover, studies were excluded if they: (1) were randomized controlled trials, reviews, editorials, case reports, comments and conference abstracts; (2) were published in other languages than English and Chinese; (3) reported the association between individual components of sarcopenia, e.g. muscle strength, handgrip strength, physical performance and adherence to the Mediterranean diet; (4) reported the association between individual nutrient, food group and sarcopenia risk; (5) did not provided HRs, RRs or ORs with 95%CIs; (6) were irrelevant topic articles. Disagreements regarding study selection were resolve by discussion or, if need, by consulting the corresponding author (HTZ). Data extraction

Two independent authors (CPX and JQW) extracted the following data from each of the included studies: first author’s name, year of publication, study design, study region, sample size, number of sarcopenia cases, mean age/age range of individuals, dietary assessment method, definitions of sarcopenia, HRs/RRs/ORs with their corresponding 95%CIs of sarcopenia across categories of Mediterranean diet score, and confounding factors in the fully adjusted model. Any discrepancies about data extraction were resolved by consensus or discussion with the third author (XLL).

Study quality assessment

We evaluated the methodological quality of each included study based on the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS), which was designed for non-randomized studies in meta-analyses [32].The NOS scores ranged from 0 to 9 “stars” and consisted of three primary domains: population selection (maximum of 4 stars), comparability of the groups (maximum of 2 stars), and exposure (maximum of 3 stars). We considered NOS scores of 0–3, 4–6 and 7–9 as low, medium and high quality, respectively [33]. Similarly, if the two reviewers (XLL and XHK) were unable to reach a consensus on the quality assessment, the third author was consulted.

Statistical analysis

For main analyses in this meta-analysis, we used the ORs and 95%CIs as the effect estimates. A random-effects model was used to summarize the ORs and 95%CIs of the highest versus lowest categories of Mediterranean diet score for sarcopenia. Then, the adjusted ORs and 95%CIs of all included studies were summarized, and an overall estimate of the effect size was calculated. Results were analyzed separately when one study included multiple sarcopenia assessment methods.Cochran’s Q test and I2 statistic were used to evaluate the inter-study heterogeneity. P values of Cochran’s Q-test ≤ 0.10 or I2 > 50% indicated significant heterogeneity across studies, and a random-effects model (DerSimonnian and Laird method) was used [34]. In case of significant heterogeneity (I2 > 50%), subgroup and sensitivity analyses were carried out to further identify the potential sources of heterogeneity. In our analyses, subgroup analyses were carried out based on study design (cross-sectional or cohort studies), study area (Western countries or other), sample size (≥ 1000 or < 1000), dietary assessment method (FFQ or other), definition of Mediterranean diet (priori or posteriori), and definition of sarcopenia (EWGSOP or AWGS/FNIH). To clarify the robustness of the results, sensitivity analysis was performed by excluding one study at a time from the overall risk estimates. Potential publication bias was evaluated by the visual inspection of the funnel plot and quantified by the Begg’s and Egger’s tests [35]. If publication bias was observed, the Duval and Tweedie trim-and-fill method was used to adjust for the funnel plot asymmetry by filling missing research data [36]. We modeled Mediterranean diet scores by using a restricted cubic spline with three knots at fixed percentiles (10%, 50% and 90%) of the distribution [37]. A two-stage generalized least-squares trend estimation model was utilized to explore potential linear and nonlinear dose-response association between adherence to the Mediterranean diet and sarcopenia risk [38]. All data analyses were carried out in STATA version 17.0 (Stata Corporation, College Station, TX, US).Statistical tests were two-tailed, and P-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant unless specified elsewhere.

Results

Literature search

We identified a total of 4,665 articles through electronic database searches and reference lists. After removing 849 duplicates, 3,816 potential eligible articles were remained for title and abstract screening. Subsequently, 3,796 articles were excluded based on the reviewing of the titles and abstracts of retrieved articles. The remaining 20 articles underwent full-text review for potential eligibility, and 13 articles were further excluded for the following reasons: systematic review (n = 2), reported the association between individual components of sarcopenia, e.g. walking speed, muscle strength, handgrip strength, physical performance and Mediterranean diet (n = 5); did not mention Mediterranean diet score (n = 5); reported the same participants (n = 1). Finally, seven studies [24–30] involving 9,709 participants met all inclusion criteria and were included in the quantitative synthesis. The flowchart of the study selection is shown in Fig. 1.

Study characteristics

The characteristics of all eligible studies are shown in Table 2. The seven included studies were published between 2015 and 2025, and encompassed a total of 9,709 participants. Of all included studies, four were cross-sectional studies [24, 27, 29, 30], and three were cohort studies [25, 26, 28]. Two studies were conducted in Italy [25, 29], two in Iran [27, 30], one in Spain [24], one in China [26], and one in Sweden [28]. The sample size of included studies ranged from 250 to 2,963. The age of participants across studies ranged from ages 45 to above.The follow-up duration for the cohort studies ranged from 4.0 to 16.0 years. Most of included studies used FFQ to collect dietary information [25–27, 29, 30], one study used dietary questionnaire [24], and one study used food record [28]. Five studies included participants of both sexes [24–27, 29], one study included men [28], and remaining one study included only women [30].The study by Álvarez-Bustos et al. was included in the analysis of two definitions of sarcopenia. Among the included studies, six studies used the European Working Group on Sarcopenia in Older People (EWGSOP) criteria to define sarcopenia [24, 25, 27–30], one study used the Foundation for the National Institutes of Health (FNIH) criteria to define sarcopenia [24], and one study used the Asian Working Group for sarcopenia (AWGS) criteria to define sarcopenia [26]. Five studies used Mediterranean diet scale to calculate the Mediterranean diet score [24–26, 28, 29] and the remaining two studies used principal component analysis to calculate Mediterranean diet score [27, 30]. Álvarez-Bustos et al. [24] identified the mean score for the Mediterranean diet was 7.5, Chan et al. [26] identified 3.9 in sarcopenic participants and 4.1 in non-sarcopenic participants, Mazza et al. [29] identified 30 scores. The NOS quality assessment of the included studies is shown in Table 3. Six of the included studies were regarded as high quality [24–26, 28–30], and the remaining one was regarded as medium quality [27].

Mediterranean diet adherence and sarcopenia

Seven articles including 1,341 sarcopenia cases and 9,709 participants, were included to assess the association between adherence to the Mediterranean diet and risk of sarcopenia. Combining 9 effect sizes from 7 articles [24–30], Fig. 2 showed that highest vs. lowest adherence to the Mediterranean diet was associated with a reduced risk of sarcopenia (OR = 0.81; 95%CI:0.71–0.92, P = 0.002). Significant heterogeneity was seen in the included studies(I2 = 70.2%, Pheterogeneity=0.002), and therefore the effect size was assessed using a random-effects model.

Dose-response analysis

Five studies (2 cohort and 3 cross-sectional studies) [25, 27–30] were included in the linear dose-response analysis of adherence to the Mediterranean diet and risk of sarcopenia (Fig. 3). Pooled analysis showed an inverse linear association between adherence to the Mediterranean diet and risk of sarcopenia (OR = 0.873; 95%CI: 0.846–0.901, Pdose−response<0.001, Pnonlinearity= 0.369).

Subgroup analyses

Given significant heterogeneity of this study (I2 = 70.2%, P = 0.002), subgroup analyses were performed to further explore the potential sources of heterogeneity (Table 4). Results from subgroup analyses showed a significant inverse association between adherence to the Mediterranean diet and sarcopenia in sample size < 1000 (OR = 0.34, 95%CI: 0.20–0.58, P < 0.001), with no evidence of heterogeneity (I2 = 0.0%, P = 0.554). When we analyzed separately by dietary assessment method, the results showed an inverse association between adherence to the Mediterranean diet and sarcopenia risk in subgroup of questionnaire/food record(OR = 0.83; 95%CI:0.77–0.89, P < 0.001). There was less evidence of heterogeneity between studies (I2 = 1.6%, P = 0.362). Additionally, there was an inverse association between sarcopenia and adherence to the Mediterranean diet defined by a posteriori method (OR = 0.35; 95%CI: 0.19–0.62, P < 0.001), with less evidence of heterogeneity (P = 0.353; I2 = 4.0%).

Publication bias and sensitivity analyses

No evidence of asymmetry was observed by visual inspection of funnel plots (Supplementary Fig. 1). Moreover, no significant publication bias was detected in statistical tests (highest compared with lowest category of Mediterranean diet: Begg’s test P = 0.118; Egger’s test P = 0.622). As shown in Supplementary Fig. 2, the results of sensitivity analysis showed that the association between adherence to the Mediterranean diet and sarcopenia risk was robust and not affected by the exclusion of any single study or a couple of studies.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first and most comprehensive dose-response meta-analysis to date examining the correlation between adherence to the Mediterranean diet and risk of sarcopenia with 9,709 participants and 1,341 sarcopenia cases. In this systematic review and meta-analysis including seven observational studies, we found that adherence to the Mediterranean diet was significantly associated with a reduced risk of sarcopenia. Based on linear dose-response analysis, each 1-score increase in the Mediterranean diet score was associated with a 13% reduced risk of sarcopenia (OR = 0.87; 95%CI: 0.85–0.90; P < 0.001). Additionally, sensitivity analyses did not change our results. Our findings reinforce the existing evidence regarding the beneficial effect of adhering to the Mediterranean diet on sarcopenia, supporting the adoption of adherence to the Mediterranean diet for the prevention of sarcopenia.

With the dramatically rapid rate of aging worldwide, sarcopenia, as an age-related disease, has become a serious public health concern in older adults and is associated with increased adverse health outcomes, including falls, fracture, longer hospitalization, and mortality [3, 4].Thus far, extensive prior studies have explored the association between different dietary patterns and the risk of sarcopenia [24, 39, 40]. However, most previous studies have used a posteriori methods to examine the association between specific dietary patterns. e.g. sweet, vegetable and animal food patterns and sarcopenia [26, 40]. In contrast, a priori dietary patterns, e.g. Mediterranean diet and Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension (DASH) dietary pattern have rarely been studied in relation to sarcopenia [24, 25, 41]. Still, the impact of the Mediterranean diet on sarcopenia has also received much attention. Notably, the current research results regarding the association between adherence to the Mediterranean diet and risk of sarcopenia remain controversial. For instance, Chan et al., found no significant association between adherence to the Mediterranean diet and risk of sarcopenia in a prospective cohort [26]. In contrast, a cross-sectional study performed in Spain reported that adherence to the Mediterranean diet was associated with a decreased risk of sarcopenia [24]. Consistent with previous cross-sectional studies [24, 27], we found that adherence to the Mediterranean diet was associated with a reduced risk of sarcopenia. These discrepant findings in previous studies may be attributed the following reasons. First, dietary assessment methods of included studies are different. For example, Álvarez-Bustos et al.used dietary questionnaire to collect dietary information [24], Karlsson et al. used food record [28], and remaining studies used FFQ [25–27, 29, 30]. Second, significant differences in dietary habits were found in different countries. Four of included studies were performed in Western countries [24, 25, 28, 29], and the remaining three studies in Asian countries [26, 27, 30]. It is well-known that there are obvious differences in dietary habits between Eastern and Western countries [42]. Third, discrepancies in definition of Mediterranean diet may contribute to the differing results. Hashemi et al. used a posteriori method to identify Mediterranean pattern [27], whereas Cacciatore et al. used a priori methods to identify Mediterranean diet [25]. Fourth, differences in the definition of sarcopenia might explain part of these discrepant results. In our analyses, six included studies used the EWGSOP criteria to define sarcopenia [24, 25, 27–30], one study used the FNIH) criteria to define sarcopenia [24], and one study used the AWGS criteria to define sarcopenia [26]. In a word, discrepancies in dietary assessment method, definitions of Mediterranean diet and sarcopenia, and dietary habits across Eastern and Western countries may explain the different results.

Although the explicit mechanism underlying the association between adherence to the Mediterranean diet and sarcopenia remains unclear, there are several possible reasons that may explain the observed associations. First, antioxidant nutrients and dietary fiber abundant in vegetables and fruits, two crucial components of the Mediterranean diet, are thought to have beneficial effects. Yao et al. in a recently published study, found that high intake of antioxidant nutrients, e.g. vitamins A, E, selenium and oils, may protect against sarcopenia [43]. Meanwhile, a previous systematic review also showed that high intake of a diet high in fiber, such as Mediterranean diet might increase the beneficial gut bacteria [44], which is inversely associated with sarcopenia [45]. Second, Mediterranean diet is characterized by a low dietary inflammatory index, highlighting its potential anti-inflammatory effects [46]. A previous meta-analysis reported that a high dietary inflammatory index could increase the risk of sarcopenia [5]. Third, high adherence to the Mediterranean diet was significantly associated with a reduced risk of diabetes [47], established risk factor for sarcopenia [6]. Fourth, people with high adherence to the Mediterranean diet have a significantly lower ultra-processed food (UPF) consumption than those with low adherence [48]. Indeed, UPF consumption has been associated with an increased risk of sarcopenia [49].Finally, individuals adhering to the Mediterranean diet are more likely to have a healthier lifestyle, which may help reduce the age-related decline in muscle mass and function [50]. Taken together, these mechanisms discussed above may explain the protective effect of high adherence to the Mediterranean diet on sarcopenia risk.

In this meta-analysis, we also observed substantial heterogeneity between included studies (I2 = 70.2%, P = 0.002). Although inter-study heterogeneity has been common in published meta-analyses [11, 38], it is necessary to explore the potential sources of high heterogeneity. Therefore, subgroup analyses were carried out regarding study design, study area, sample size, dietary assessment method, definition of Mediterranean diet, and definition of sarcopenia.The results of subgroup analyses showed that differences in sample size, dietary assessment method and definition of Mediterranean diet might partly explain the sources of heterogeneity. When subgroup analyses were performed in studies with sample size < 1000, heterogeneity decreased from 70.2% to 0.0%. Similarly, when stratified by dietary assessment method and definition of Mediterranean diet separately, the results showed that heterogeneity decreased from 70.2% to 1.6% and 4.0%, respectively. Significant heterogeneity could be attributed to the follows reasons. First, the differences in the definitions of the Mediterranean diet among different countries, such as Italy and China, may lead to significant heterogeneity. Second, applying different diagnostic methods for sarcopenia might have led to significant heterogeneity. For example, Karlsson et al. used the EWGSOP criteria to define sarcopenia [28], while Chan et al. used the AWGS criteria to define [26]. Third, in all the included studies, recall bias caused by dietary assessment methods, e.g. FFQs, should also be considered. Moreover, residual confounding and unmeasured variables in observational studies represented an important source of between-study heterogeneity. Finally, inconsistent adjustment for confounding variables across studies contributed substantially to the observed heterogeneity in pooled estimates.

Strengths and limitations

This meta-analysis has several notable strengths. First, to the best of our knowledge, this is the first systematic review and meta-analysis to date to assess the association between adherence to the Mediterranean diet and sarcopenia risk. Our findings reinforce the existing evidence regarding the beneficial effect of adhering to the Mediterranean diet on sarcopenia, supporting the adoption of Mediterranean diet for the primary prevention of sarcopenia. Second, sarcopenia cases were diagnosed through medical records or geriatric clinicians, avoiding misdiagnosis bias. Third, we strictly screened the existing articles according to the predetermined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Fourth, there was no evidence of publication bias in the funnel plot and statistical tests, including Begg’s and Egger’s tests for publication bias were also non-significant. Fifth, subgroup and sensitivity analyses were conducted to further explore potential sources of heterogeneity, improving the accuracy of our results. Finally, the dose-response analysis was performed between adherence to the Mediterranean diet and sarcopenia, which strengthened the relationship.Despite these strengths, several limitations should be considered in this meta-analysis. First, four of included studies used the cross-sectional design, which precludes definitive conclusion on the causal association between adherence to the Mediterranean diet and sarcopenia risk. Meanwhile, the effect estimates were ORs and 95%CIs in this study.The inclusion of cross-sectional studies may limit causal inference, although subgroup analyses support the robustness of the findings. Thus, more prospective cohort studies or randomized controlled trials are required to further confirm the role of the Mediterranean diet in preventing sarcopenia. Second, most of the included studies used FFQs to collect dietary data, carrying an inherent recall bias. Third, heterogeneity in the used definitions of sarcopenia and Mediterranean diet may introduce variability, and affect the strength of the observed association. Fourth, even if some potential confounding factors have been adjusted, the possibility of residual confounding from certain unmeasured factors still could not be excluded. Moreover, our analyses did not extensively search grey literature, which might introduce potential selective reporting bias. Fifth, significant heterogeneity (I2 = 70.2%) was found in this meta-analysis, which indicated that high variability in other measures might impact our results. Although subgroup and sensitivity analyses were conducted to detect potential sources of heterogeneity, we were unable fully explain the sources of heterogeneity between studies. Sixth, limited studies investigating the association between adherence to the Mediterranean diet and sarcopenia risk, might reduce the statistical power and robustness of the results. Seventh, Finally, four of the included studies were performed in Western countries and three studies in Asian countries, resulting in population bias and thus potentially limiting the generalizability of our findings. Thus, more studies, particularly in different populations, are required to confirm the association between adherence to the Mediterranean diet and sarcopenia risk.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the results of this meta-analysis provide evidence that adherence to the Mediterranean diet is significantly associated with a decreased risk of sarcopenia. Our findings add to the current knowledge that healthy dietary patterns, such as the Mediterranean diet, can provide a promising strategy for preventing sarcopenia. To further verify the research findings, high-quality prospective cohort studies and clinical trials are needed to confirm the observed association between adherence to the Mediterranean diet and sarcopenia risk.
