Prenatal and early life phthalate exposure and early life stress: no interactions with adolescent internalizing symptoms
Authors: Mariah DeSerisy, Gabriella Meltzer, Arin Balalian, Shabnaz Siddiq, Autumn M Clemons, Julie B Herbstman, Virginia A Rauh, Frederica P Perera, Amy E Margolis, Pam Factor-Litvak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPrenatal and early life exposure to phthalates has been increasingly implicated in adverse neurodevelopmental outcomes. However, associations between phthalate exposure in early life and later adolescent mental health remain understudied, particularly when also considering co-exposure to early life psychosocial stressors. We examined the independent and combined associations of prenatal and early life (ages three, five, and seven) phthalate exposure with adolescent internalizing symptoms in a longitudinal cohort of 563 Black and Latiné mother–child dyads living in a low-income urban neighborhood. We further explored whether early life stress modified these associations or predicted internalizing symptoms independently. Exposure to eleven common phthalates was assessed using urinary metabolites measured in the third trimester of pregnancy and in children ages three, five, and seven. Early life stress was operationalized by a composite measure of material hardship and maternal demoralization measured concurrently with phthalate exposure. Youth self-reported anxiety and depression symptoms were measured between ages 13–15 with the Revised Children’s Manifest Anxiety Scale and Center for Epidemiological Studies Depression Inventory, respectively. Bayesian kernel machine regression assessed effects of the phthalate mixture on anxiety and depression symptoms. False discovery rate-corrected, pooled linear regressions tested individual phthalate effects, stratified by sex, and tested early life stress as an effect modifier. We did not detect significant associations between prenatal or postnatal phthalate exposure—either independently or jointly—and adolescent depression or anxiety; early life stress did not modify these associations or independently predict symptomology. However, uncertainty in effect estimates necessitates future research to clarify if these null effects hold in larger samples. If replicated, our findings suggest that there may be important, as yet unknown resilience factors protecting youth from long term effects of early phthalate exposure.Keywords: Phthalates, Depression, Anxiety, Early life stress, Adolescent

Introduction

Phthalates are nearly ubiquitous, endocrine disrupting chemicals with known neurotoxicant effects. Sources of exposure include household and consumer goods, e.g., cosmetics, detergents, food packaging, medical devices, pharmaceutical coatings, adhesives and paint, and building materials and textiles (Engel et al., 2021; Jurewicz et al., 2013). Critically, phthalates can cross the placental barrier to disrupt endocrine-dependent fetal development, and potentially contribute to alterations in brain development that increase vulnerability to internalizing symptoms, such as anxiety or depression, later in life (Wang and Qian, 2021). Postnatal phthalate exposure can also alter metabolic and hormonal function, with documented associations between exposure and internalizing symptoms (Wang and Qian, 2021). Further, and consistent with their endocrine disrupting properties, phthalate exposure appears to operate in sex-specific ways (Wang and Qian, 2021). Importantly, internalizing symptoms also exhibit well-documented sex differences, which emerge during adolescence and often coincide with puberty (Kessler et al., 2005; Merikangas et al., 2010). Despite this, few studies have directly examined sex-specific effects of phthalates on children’s internalizing symptoms. Moreover, although co-exposure to multiple phthalates is common (Li et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025; Zhu et al., 2024), limited research has examined mixtures of phthalates across prenatal and postnatal exposure windows.

Internalizing symptoms are common, affecting as many as 30% of youth (Beesdo-Baum and Knappe, 2012; Costello et al., 2003; Merikangas et al., 2010; Perou et al., 2013). The emergence of problematic internalizing symptoms tends to correspond with the onset of puberty, with girls and women demonstrating higher rates than boys and men (Farhane-Medina et al., 2022; Kessler et al., 2005; Kessler and McRae, 1981; Merikangas et al., 2010; Salk et al., 2017). These well-documented developmental and sex-specific patterns raise questions about early risk factors that shape vulnerability before symptom onset. Animal evidence indicates that phthalates exhibit anti-androgenic properties, and prenatal and early life exposure may disrupt sexually dimorphic neurodevelopment, including increased anxiety-like behavior in males during puberty that is linked to decreased testosterone and altered neurotransmitter function (Carbone et al., 2019, 2013; Gray et al., 2000). In humans, prenatal phthalate exposure has been associated with elevated internalizing symptoms in early childhood, with the most consistent effects observed in boys (Colicino et al., 2022; Daniel et al., 2020; England-Mason et al., 2020; Philippat et al., 2017; Tsai et al., 2023). Together, perturbation of androgen-dependent brain maturation during sensitive periods may alter sex-typical trajectories of neurodevelopment, potentially increasing vulnerability to internalizing symptoms in boys.

Despite strong evidence from the clinical literature suggesting that elevated internalizing symptoms in early childhood are associated with risk for later anxiety and depression (Bakken et al., 2023; Finlay-Jones et al., 2024; Klinge et al., 2025), few studies have evaluated whether associations between early life phthalate exposure and childhood internalizing symptoms persist into adolescence in sex-specific ways. In one longitudinal study of seven to sixteen-year-old Mexican American children (N = 334), prenatal exposure to low molecular weight phthalates was weakly associated with internalizing symptoms among adolescents; sex-specific associations between phthalate metabolites and internalizing symptoms were not reported (Hyland et al., 2019). In another study of Taiwanese children ages eight to fourteen, prenatal MEHP was positively associated with internalizing symptoms (N = 153); sex-specific effects again were not reported (Huang et al., 2019). Finally, among 546 Greek children, prenatal exposure to high molecular weight phthalates was associated with greater odds of adverse internalizing symptom trajectory membership among girls, based on four repeated symptom assessments from ages four to fifteen years (Mouatsou et al., 2024). This limited evidence points to the potential for enduring effects of phthalates on adolescent internalizing symptoms; however, mixed findings underscore the need to clarify which phthalate esters, developmental exposure windows, and sex-specific patterns are most strongly associated with risk.

In a separate line of research, developmental studies document increased risk of internalizing problems associated with stressful life experiences (Lecarie et al., 2022; March-Llanes et al., 2017). Individuals at high risk of phthalate exposure are also at heightened risk for exposure to social stressors (e.g., Brelsford et al., 2017). Combined exposure to neurotoxicant chemicals and social stressors has been linked to increased risk for neurodevelopmental problems (Bolton et al., 2013; DeSerisy et al., 2023; Margolis et al., 2025; Monk et al., 2019). Co-exposure to phthalates and social stress may act in concert to increase risk for internalizing symptoms through overlapping dopaminergic and stress-response neuroinflammation pathways (Cheng et al., 2022; Dutcher et al., 2020; Huang et al., 2022a; Huang et al., 2022b; Monk et al., 2019).

Herein, we extend prior research to examine whether prenatal and postnatal phthalate exposure is associated with self-reported internalizing symptoms at ages 13 to 15 in a cohort of predominantly low-income, Black and Latińe mother–child dyads. As prior research links phthalate exposure to internalizing symptoms in youth, we hypothesized that prenatal and postnatal phthalate exposure would be linearly associated with greater internalizing symptoms in adolescence, with the strongest effects observed in boys. We further hypothesized that phthalate-related increases in adolescent internalizing symptoms would be moderated by exposure to early life stressors, such that higher combined exposure would be associated with more symptoms. We note that early life stress (ELS) is operationalized as exposure to material hardship (i.e., lack of access to basic needs) and maternal demoralization (i.e., psychological distress and difficulty coping). These measures assess maternal experiences of stress during the child’s early life, and so we refer to them as ELS because they reflect the shared experience of stress between mother and child (Smith and Pollak, 2020), as has been done in our prior work (DeSerisy et al., 2023; Pagliaccio et al., 2020).

Materials and Methods

Participants

Detailed demographic and recruitment information regarding the Columbia Center for Children’s Environmental Health (CCCEH) Mothers and Newborns prospective birth cohort have been previously published (Perera et al., 2006). In brief, Black and Latińe women residing in Washington Heights, Harlem, or the South Bronx in New York City (NYC) were recruited between 1998 and 2006 through local prenatal care clinics. Recruited women were non-users of tobacco products or illicit drugs, between the ages of 18 and 35, free of diabetes, hypertension, or known HIV, and had initiated prenatal care by the 20th week of pregnancy. The full cohort included data from 727 mother–child dyads. Of the 727 dyads enrolled in the Mothers and Newborns cohort, 563 had available data for any of the predictors and outcomes of interest (i.e., phthalate metabolites, specific gravity, ELS, anxiety symptoms, depression symptoms) and so contributed data to the multiply imputed analytic datasets used in the present analyses. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Columbia University, and mothers provided informed consent for themselves and their children at every study visit. Children provided assent beginning at age seven. Latińe mothers were more likely to be excluded from the analytic sample than Black mothers (See Supplementary table 1).

Study Timeline

Longitudinal study visits began in the third trimester of pregnancy and occurred approximately every two years thereafter. The current study leverages data collected during five visits: “prenatal” (approximately mother’s third trimester of pregnancy, range: 23.86–41.00 gestational weeks), age three (age range: 22–––68 months; mean age = 42.85 months), age five (age range: 4 – 6 years; mean age = 4.43 years), age seven (age range: 6 – 8 years; mean age = 6.52 years), and age 14 (age range: 13 –15 years; mean age = 13.69). Early life stress measures (maternal demoralization and material hardship) and phthalate exposure were collected prenatally (maternal urine) and at child ages three, five, and seven. Adolescent self-reported anxiety and depression symptoms were collected once at child age 14.

Measures

Phthalate exposure:

A spot urine sample was collected from the women (n = 392) during the third trimester (mean ± SD, 33.02 ± 3.88 weeks gestation; median, 32.43 weeks) and from the children at ages 3 (n = 387), 5 (n = 460), and 7 (n = 336). Samples were analyzed for the following eleven phthalate metabolites at the CDC using solid phase extraction coupled with high performance liquid chromatography–isotope dilution tandem spectrometry as described by Kato et al., (2005): monoethyl phthalate (MEP), mono-isobutyl phthalate (MiBP), mono(3-carboxypropyl) phthalate (MCPP), monobenzyl phthalate (MBzP), mono(2-ethylhexyl) phthalate (MEHP), mono(2-ethyl-5-hydroxyhexyl) phthalate (MEHHP), mono(2-ethyl-5-oxohexyl) phthalate (MEOHP), and mono(2-ethyl-5-carboxypentyl) phthalate (MECPP), Monobutyl phthalate (MBP), Mono-carboxy-isooctyl phthalate (MCOP), and Mono-carboxynonyl phthalate (MNCP). Specific gravity was measured in the urine samples using a handheld refractometer and was used to control for urinary dilution (Atago PAL 10-S; Bellevue, WA; Hauser et al., 2004). Using standard practice, phthalate metabolite concentrations below the limit of detection (LOD) values were replaced with LOD/√2. In this analysis, we did not include phthalates where greater than 70% of the sample was < LOD. Concentrations of phthalate metabolites were adjusted for urinary specific gravity (SG) using the following formula: Pc=P [(1.017 – 1)]/SG – 1)], where Pc represents the specific gravity-corrected phthalate concentration (micrograms per liter), P represents the measured concentration in urine, and 1.017 is the mean SG of all samples measured (United States Environmental Protection Agency, 2015).

Maternal Demoralization:

Maternal demoralization was measured using the Psychiatric Epidemiology Research Instrument–Demoralization (PERI-D; Dohrenwend et al., 1980), a 27-item scale measuring eight composite domains of non-specific psychological distress (perceived physical health, sadness, poor self-esteem, dread, anxiety, confused thinking, hopelessness/helplessness, and psychophysiological symptoms).

Material Hardship:

Material hardship reflects families’ level of unmet basic needs (Mayer and Jencks, 1989). Material hardship was assessed by a verbal interview with the parent conducted by a trained research assistant, e.g., “In the past year has there been a time when you: 1. couldn’t afford to buy food?; 2. couldn’t afford a place to stay?; 3.couldn’t afford gas/electricity?; or 4. couldn’t afford clothing?”. Each item on the questionnaire was rated yes/no [1/0] to indicate having experienced (or not) an unmet need.

Early Life Stress Composite Score:

Psychosocial stress measures were administered via interview with a trained research worker. For the current analyses, these measures were combined to create a single ELS composite score. Specifically, item responses from both scales were rescaled to range from 0 to 1, with higher values indicating greater stress exposure. Responses were averaged within each domain (maternal demoralization and material hardship), and the two domain scores were then averaged with equal weighting to create an age-specific ELS composite at the prenatal, age 3, age 5, and age 7 assessments. Following multiple imputation, the four age-specific ELS composite scores were subsequently averaged across childhood to create a single overall ELS score for each participant (range = 0–1; mean = 0.34; median = 0.32). For analyses examining effect modification by overall early life stress, the overall ELS composite was dichotomized at the sample median to create high- and low-ELS groups. High and low ELS groups were used in analyses testing if ELS moderated associations between phthalate exposure and adolescent anxiety and depression scores. To test effects of individual exposure windows, we additionally examined age-matched phthalate and ELS effects (i.e., effects of phthalate and ELS exposure occurring at the same time point). Composite scores at each age point were dichotomized using a median split to create high and low ELS exposure groups at each age; age-specific ELS groups were used in Bayesian kernel machine regression (BKMR) and regression analyses accounting for exposure windows.

The Revised Children’s Manifest Anxiety Scale (RCMAS; Reynolds and Richmond, 1985, 1978; White and Farrell, 2001) is a 37-item measure of youth anxiety symptoms. Items are rated on a 0–1, presence or absence scale. Total scores are generated by summing across 28 anxiety items, ignoring the 9 social desirability items. Scores can range from 0 to 28. In the current sample, scores ranged from 0 to 24. Total scores above 19 may indicate clinically significant anxiety symptoms (Stallard et al., 2001). Continuous total scores were used in all analyses.

The Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES-D; Radloff, 1977) is a 20-item self-report measure of depression symptoms. Items are rated on a 0–3 scale indicating the frequency of experience (e.g., rarely or none of the time to most or all the time). Total scores are generated by reverse scoring positively worded items and then summing across all items. Scores range from 0 to 60, with scores above 15 indicating significant levels of depression symptoms (Weissman et al., 1980). In the current sample, scores ranged from 0 to 39. Continuous total scores were used in all analyses.

Analytic Plan

All data analysis was conducted in RStudio version 4.1.1 (RStudio Team, 2022). BKMR was conducted using code developed by Devick 2019 for multiply imputed data (bkmr_MI; Devick, n.d). Missing data were handled by multiple imputation by classification and regression trees (mice package; van Buuren and Groothuis-Oudshoorn, 2011). Given prior findings of sex-specific effects, all primary analyses were conducted separately in males and females using sex assigned at birth extracted from the medical record. Corresponding sensitivity analyses examining exposure effects in the entire sample (i.e., males and females together) were additionally conducted; in these analyses, sex assigned at birth was included as a covariate. Details of all model specifications can be found in the supplement. Correlation tables tested associations between phthalate metabolites across age points and between phthalate metabolites within each age point. BKMR analyses examined effects of 1) prenatal phthalates, 2) postnatal phthalates (operationalized as the average phthalate exposure across childhood calculated separately for each metabolite [ages three, five, and seven years]), and 3) combined prenatal and postnatal phthalate exposure models (operationalized as prenatal exposure and the childhood average phthalate exposure included as distinct mixture components) on adolescent anxiety or depression symptoms, each in separate models (3 models for girls, 3 models for boys, 3 models for combined sample). Although BKMR has been successfully implemented in modest sample sizes (Bobb et al., 2015; Pesenti et al., 2023), to address potential power limitations false discovery rate (FDR)-corrected, linear regression models were estimated within each multiply imputed dataset and pooled using Rubin’s rules to test associations between phthalate metabolites and anxiety or depression symptoms, controlling for confounders.

To complement traditional null hypothesis significance testing, we conducted Bayesian follow-up analyses (BayesFactor; Morey, n.d). Bayes factors (BF10) were computed to quantify the relative evidence for the alternative hypothesis versus the null hypothesis, with values > 3 indicating moderate evidence for an effect and values < 0.33 indicating moderate evidence for the null (Kass and Raftery, 1995; Lakens et al., 2020). This approach allowed us to interpret null results more robustly, accounting for the strength of evidence rather than relying solely on p-values.

To explore if associations between phthalate exposure and anxiety/depression symptoms differed by ELS, BKMR analyses were then run as described above for prenatal, postnatal, and prenatal and postnatal phthalate exposure in each of the stratified high and low ELS exposure groups separately. To test for specificity of effects based on exposure windows, BKMR analyses were additionally conducted using age-matched phthalate and ELS exposures (e.g., prenatal phthalate exposure in high and low prenatal ELS exposure groups; age three phthalate exposure in high and low age three ELS exposure groups, etc.). Associations between the ELS composite score (average across childhood and each age point independently) and anxiety or depression symptoms were also tested via FDR-corrected pooled linear regression, controlling for confounders. Finally, to examine developmentally sensitive windows and effect modification by ELS, additional pooled linear regression models included age-matched phthalate metabolites, concurrent ELS measures, and their interaction term.

Confounders for all analyses were identified by directed acyclic graph (Fig. 1). Of note, due to initial data collection procedures, mothers who used tobacco products, alcohol, or illicit drugs during pregnancy were screened out of the study (Perera et al., 2006). Thus, these variables were not included as confounders in our analyses. Final confounders included in all analyses were: child age at anxiety/depression visit, maternal ethnoracial identification, maternal report of breast-feeding, maternal age at child’s birth, maternal body mass index from mother’s medical record and obtained closest to delivery date, maternal years of education, and quality of the home environment defined as total score from the HOME scale and assessed at the three year visit (Evans et al., 2001). Sex assigned at birth was included as a covariate only in whole-sample (combined male and female) sensitivity analyses.

Phthalates exposures across development

Phthalate exposures were highly correlated across development in both boys and girls and across the entire sample (Figs. 2–7). Consistent with prior reports of individuals living in the context of socioeconomic disadvantage, geometric means of exposure were generally higher than those reported in the general population (Table 2; He et al., 2024). Phthalate metabolites deriving from the same parent compound (e.g., MEOHP and MEHHP) demonstrated the highest correlations. High and low molecular weight phthalates were also generally highly positively correlated within exposure class but less positively correlated, or un-correlated, across classes (i.e., high molecular weight phthalates were correlated with each other but not with low molecular weight phthalates and vice versa).

Neither phthalate mixtures nor their interaction with early life stress predicted adolescent internalizing symptoms

Results of BKMR analyses examining associations between the prenatal, postnatal, and combined prenatal and postnatal phthalate mixtures and adolescent anxiety and depression symptoms are described in Supplementary table 3; prenatal phthalate mixture effects are shown in Fig. 8. Neither the prenatal, the average postnatal, nor the combined prenatal and postnatal phthalate mixture was associated with adolescent anxiety or depression symptoms. Credible intervals were wide, indicating considerable uncertainty in the estimated exposure contrasts. Univariate analyses did not indicate nonlinear associations between individual phthalates or the phthalate mixture and anxiety or depression symptoms. Bivariate analysis did not indicate any interaction effects between phthalates in the mixture. Posterior inclusion probabilities were generally low (PIP < 0.5), indicating that no single phthalate strongly contributed to any mixture-outcome association. Results were similar for girls, boys, and analyses pooled across sexes.

Phthalate metabolites did not predict adolescent internalizing symptoms

After FDR correction, no associations were statistically significant in sex-stratified analyses or in the full sample for either outcome (Supplementary table 6–7). Bayesian model comparisons similarly provided limited support for associations between individual phthalates and internalizing symptoms. For anxiety, most Bayes factors indicated weak evidence (BF10 < 3), with a small number of moderate effects that were not consistent across sex or exposure window. For depression, evidence was also generally weak, with the exception of age seven MCNP, which showed strong evidence in females (BF10 = 11.83) and in the full sample (BF10 = 17.21). Overall, results did not indicate robust associations between individual phthalate metabolites and adolescent internalizing symptoms.

The interaction between phthalate metabolites and early life stress did not predict adolescent internalizing symptoms

Neither average nor concurrent ELS by phthalate metabolite interactions survived FDR-correction (Supplementary tables 8–11). Bayesian analyses indicated a few age- and sex-specific signals: for anxiety, age 3 MIBP × age 3 ELS (BF10 = 5.59, full sample), age 7 MCPP × age 7 ELS (BF10 = 9.97, boys), and age 7 MEHP × age 7 ELS (BF10 = 4.18, full sample); for depression, only age 7 MCNP × age 7 ELS showed strong evidence (11.83, girls; BF10 = 17.21, full sample). All other interactions were weakly supported (BF10 < 3). Main effects of phthalate exposure or ELS were not associated with anxiety or depression symptoms in any models.

Early life stress did not predict adolescent internalizing symptoms

Associations between ELS exposure and adolescent anxiety symptoms are presented in Supplementary table 12. After FDR correction, no associations remained statistically significant in sex-stratified analyses or in the full sample. In uncorrected analyses, average ELS was associated with anxiety symptoms in girls (β = 9.26, p = 0.017) and in the full sample (β = 7.55, p = 0.026). Prenatal and age seven stress were also nominally associated with anxiety symptoms in the full sample (ps < 0.05), and age five stress was nominally associated with anxiety symptoms in girls (p = 0.023). Bayesian model comparisons provided converging evidence for associations involving average ELS. In the full sample, there was strong evidence for an association between average ELS and anxiety symptoms (BF10 = 91.84), and in girls, the evidence was moderate-to-strong (BF10 = 27.05). Prenatal and age seven stress in the full sample also showed moderate evidence (BF10s = 16.34 and 14.67, respectively), whereas other comparisons yielded only anecdotal or weak evidence (BF10s < 5).

Associations between ELS exposure and depression are presented in Supplementary table 13. After FDR correction, no associations remained statistically significant in sex-stratified analyses or in the full sample. In uncorrected analyses, average ELS was associated with depressive symptoms in the full sample (β = 5.78, p = 0.035). No other ELS time points were significantly associated with depressive symptoms. Bayesian model comparisons indicated moderate evidence for an association between age seven stress and depressive symptoms in the full sample (BF10 = 6.37). All other comparisons yielded only anecdotal evidence (BF10s < 3), suggesting limited overall support for associations between ELS exposure and adolescent depressive symptoms.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is one of the first studies to examine associations between early life phthalate exposures and internalizing symptoms in adolescence, a period of high risk for the emergence of such symptoms. Contrary to our hypotheses, we did not detect significant associations between prenatal or postnatal phthalate exposure, individually or combined on adolescent anxiety or depression symptoms in terms of both mixtures of total phthalates and average concentrations of individual phthalates. There was also no significant effect modification of early life stress on these results. Across analyses, results indicated considerable uncertainty in the exposure effect estimates, meaning that further research in larger samples is necessary to clarify these associations.

Consistent with prior reports of elevated phthalate exposures among youth living in socioeconomically disadvantaged contexts (He et al., 2024; Welch et al., 2023), urinary phthalate metabolite concentrations in the present cohort were generally higher than those reported in nationally representative samples of U.S. children ages six to eighteen (He et al., 2024). Although the results should be considered in light of differences in participant age and study characteristics, across shared metabolites, concentrations were approximately 1.5- to 5.4-fold higher among children aged seven in the present cohort, with the exception of MCOP, which was modestly lower (Supplementary table 14). Such differences may be driven by social determinants of health, including consumer product use, diet, and differences in the built environment (James-Todd et al., 2016). Consistent with this hypothesis, exposure to phthalates deriving from the same parent compound and those of similar molecular weight classes were generally highly correlated both within individuals over time and between individuals at the same time point. In contrast, correlations between low- and high-molecular-weight phthalates were substantially weaker, suggesting that these classes likely arise from different exposure sources (Luís et al., 2021). Together, these findings indicate that children likely experienced multiple, relatively consistent sources of phthalate exposure across childhood.

Phthalate exposure was not associated with internalizing symptoms in any exposure window, nor did ELS exposure modify associations between phthalate exposures and internalizing symptoms. In line with a two-hit hypothesis of toxicant exposure on children’s neurodevelopment (Barrett and Padula, 2019; Margolis et al., 2025; Padula et al., 2020), we hypothesized that phthalate effects would be exacerbated by ELS because both phthalates and ELS target androgen-dependent neural circuitry. First, animal models indicate that phthalate exposure during early life (i.e., prenatal and pre-pubertal) causes alterations in androgen-dependent GABA-ergic circuitry and greater anxiety-like behavior in adulthood in males. Both neuroendocrine and behavioral function were reversed via treatment of phthalate-related testosterone or GABA function (Carbone et al., 2019, 2013). Second, phthalate exposure causes with disruptions in dopamine signaling (M.-L. Huang et al., 2022; W. Huang et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2016), an androgen-dependent neurotransmitter heavily implicated in anxiety and depressive disorders (Moraga-Amaro et al., 2014; Nikolaus et al., 2019). Critically, ELS effects on neurodevelopment also operate through GABA- and dopamine-dependent pathways (for review: Cheng et al., 2022; Dutcher et al., 2020). Specifically, ELS is associated with alterations in reciprocal inhibition pathways through which GABA and dopamine interact (Bouarab et al., 2019; Hanson et al., 2021; Karst et al., 2023; Lukkes et al., 2017), and thus may contribute to chronic neuroinflammation and altered dopamine function implicated internalizing disorders in adulthood (Crowley et al., 2016; Nikolaus et al., 2019; Troubat et al., 2021; Won and Kim, 2020). In fact, increasing clinical evidence in humans supports targeting GABA-ergic neuroinflammation and dopamine function to improve clinical outcomes, especially among treatment resistant cases (for review: Lucido and Dunlop, 2025; Protti et al., 2020; Réus et al., 2023; Sah and Singewald, 2025). As such, it is plausible that phthalate-related alterations in GABA and dopamine function in early life increases susceptibility to ELS-related neuroinflammation and altered dopamine function, ultimately leading to increased internalizing symptoms, particularly in adolescence when these symptoms commonly emerge. These hypotheses, however, were not supported by the study results.

Null results may have been driven by the study’s small sample size. Although BKMR has been successfully applied with small samples of environmental and health data (e.g., Bobb et al., 2018; Pesenti et al., 2023), it is possible that we were underpowered to observe effects (Gibson et al., 2019). Supporting this hypothesis, credible intervals in BKMR models and Bayes factors in linear models indicated considerable uncertainty in effect estimates. This suggests that variability in phthalate exposure and/or our relatively small sample size may have contributed to null results. It is also possible that the powerful adverse effects of ELS may have superseded or masked any neurotoxicity induced by phthalate exposure. Consistent with this, we observed very high Bayes factor values in linear models of average ELS, prenatal ELS, and age seven ELS predicting anxiety symptoms. However, these results should be interpreted with caution because effects did not survive multiple comparisons correction in classical statistical models. Finally, associated with the enduring effects of systemic racism and environmental injustice, individuals living in marginalized communities are at greatest risk of exposure to multiple chronic chemical and psychosocial stressors (e.g., Brelsford et al., 2017), as well as their related mental health sequelae, including anxiety and depression symptoms (Ridley et al., 2020; Wang and Narcisse, 2025). Consistent with these reports and highlighting the vulnerability of youth living in marginalized contexts, the average reported depression score in our sample fell within the at-risk range (Poulin et al., 2005; Weissman et al., 1980). Phthalate exposures levels were also considerably higher in this sample than in the general population, indicating that ceiling effects of the exposure may also have contributed to null results (He et al., 2024; Welch et al., 2023). Future research leveraging animal models and larger samples of economically and socially diverse youth, such as the Environmental influences on Child Health Outcomes (ECHO) Consortium (LeWinn et al., 2022), are necessary to parse out etiologic mechanisms of the shared and unique neurotoxic effects of combined and sequential phthalate and early life stress exposure.

This study has several strengths. Participants came from a relatively large, longitudinal birth cohort that recruited mothers from low socioeconomic status, racial/ethnic minority communities that have historically been excluded from neurodevelopmental and environmental public health research. The researchers also had access to cutting-edge technologies that enabled them to accurately ascertain phthalate metabolite concentrations in individuals’ biospecimens, avoiding issues of exposure misclassification. In addition, adolescent self-reported depression and anxiety symptoms were assessed using diagnostic tools that have also been widely validated, including among Black and Latińe adolescents (Cobb and Martínez, 2023; Pina et al., 2009). This study also has several limitations. Given the geographic clustering of participants in the Washington Heights/Inwood neighborhood and relative homogeneity in terms of ethnoracial identification and socioeconomic status, there was limited variation in levels of adolescent depression and anxiety, as well as in neighborhood and household-level physical and social environmental characteristics that may have influenced their phthalate and ELS exposures. Mothers recruited were between 18–35 years old during pregnancy, potentially limiting generalizability of these findings. Lastly, we did not have a concurrent measurement of phthalate exposure at the time we ascertained adolescent participants’ mental health and thus we could not measure concurrent effects of phthalate exposure on adolescent anxiety or depression. Future studies should leverage longitudinal samples with concurrent exposure measurements to capture these effects.

Conclusions

When compared to the general population, prenatal and postnatal phthalate exposures and depression were elevated in this cohort of Black and Latińe youth living in low-income urban environments (He et al., 2024; Poulin et al., 2005; Welch et al., 2023). Despite these elevated exposure and symptom levels, we did not detect associations between prenatal or early childhood phthalate exposure and adolescent internalizing symptoms, nor did we observe significant modification of these associations by ELS. These null findings may reflect methodological limitations such as small sample size and ceiling effects of both exposure and outcomes. This study adds to a growing body of research examining the influence of combined and sequential chemical and psychosocial exposures in shaping youth mental health outcomes. Future research leveraging larger datasets with geographically and ethnoracially diverse participants is warranted to better elucidate these associations.
