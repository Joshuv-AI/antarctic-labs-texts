Partitioned Internal Auditory Canal: Association With Cochlear Aperture Anomalies
Authors: Levent Sennaroğlu, Emel Tahir
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveTo investigate the association between partitioned internal auditory canal (IAC) and other inner ear abnormalities.MethodsThe temporal bone computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) findings of ears with partitioned IAC were retrospectively analyzed. The IAC was considered narrow if its mid‐portion diameter < 2 mm. Cochlear aperture (CA) was classified as normal (diameter more than 1.5 mm), stenotic (diameter < 1.5 mm), or atretic. The MRI images examined the cochlear nerve (CN). The CN's diameter was compared with the ipsilateral facial nerve to determine if it was normal, hypoplastic, or aplastic.ResultsPartitioned IAC was observed in 1.1% of ears with normal CA, 14.9% of ears with stenotic CA, and 20.9% of ears with atretic CA. Forty‐eight right and 37 left ears with partitioned IAC were examined. The most common CA anomaly accompanying partitioned IAC was CA stenosis, while more than half of the cochleae were of normal size. IAC was mostly narrow in ears with partitioned IAC. When the CN was examined, partitioned IAC was almost always accompanied by CN aplasia (more frequently) or hypoplasia.ConclusionThis study demonstrates that CN deficiency, associated with CA abnormalities, may cause the aberrant location of the meatal segment of the facial nerve in a separate bony canal. Partitioned IAC is an inner ear malformation characterized by CN deficiency.Level of Evidence4

Introduction

Internal auditory canal (IAC) anomalies are uncommon and can occur in conjunction with other inner ear malformations (IEMs) [1, 2] Atresia, stenosis, dilatation, aberrant direction, and duplications are all typical IAC abnormalities. A partitioned IAC or IAC duplication is an unusual malformation of the IAC associated with sensorineural hearing loss [1]. This abnormality has been documented as an individual occurrence in otherwise normal patients and a common feature in other central nervous system malformations [3]. Partitioned IAC is identified by two canals separated by a complete or incomplete bony septum. The inferiorly placed canal proceeds to the cochlea and/or vestibule. The superiorly situated canal continues with the bony canal of the labyrinthine segment of the facial nerve. It can be considered to be the bony canal of the meatal segment of the facial nerve [2].

It is important to use the most appropriate terminology. The term “IAC duplication” does not describe the pathology correctly, as the canal is divided rather than duplicated. In this pathology, there is a complete or incomplete separation between the compartments of the IAC, separating the facial nerve from the cochlear or cochleovestibular nerve. Therefore, the term “partitioned IAC” is preferred throughout this manuscript as it more precisely reflects the underlying anatomy.

It is also important to look at it from the perspective of the facial canal as well. When the facial nerve segments are described, the most appropriate terminology for this situation of the meatal segment is “separate facial canal.” When we describe IAC, it can be “normal,” “narrow,” “consisting of only facial nerve,” or “partitioned.” The latter means there is a separate bony septum between the facial nerve and the cochleovestibular nerve (separate facial nerve canal).

In such a situation, the partitioned IAC has two compartments: one is a separate channel for the facial nerve, and the other compartment belongs to the cochleovestibular nerve. Together, the two compartments unite and form the IAC.

Currently, cochlear implantation (CI) can be performed in cochlear nerve (CN) hypoplasia and some selected IEMs [4] Here, the clinical significance of this anomaly emerges because cochlear implantation is contraindicated in CN aplasia or the presence of a nonfunctioning nerve. Besides, it is necessary to clarify the pathophysiology of these anomalies and their radiological characteristics. Partitioned IAC is an extremely rare abnormality, with only about 50 cases documented in the literature, and the etiology is currently unknown. We have noticed that partitioned IAC is encountered more in cases of CA stenosis/atresia and CN deficiency. Therefore, this study is organized to investigate the relationship between partitioned IAC and CA abnormalities. This study aims to investigate imaging characteristics of partitioned IAC and its relationship with other inner ear malformations and explain the findings from a developmental perspective.

Materials and Methods

Patients

The medical records of patients with inner ear malformations who had been diagnosed in our clinic and whose temporal bone computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scans were available were included in the study. Patients were identified through systematic review of our institutional inner ear malformation database. In the first step, a total of 1225 patients in the database were screened. All available temporal bone CT images with adequate quality were reviewed for IAC and CA status. Cases in which the IAC could not be assessed due to the nature of the anomaly (common cavity, Michel deformity, rudimentary otocyst) or insufficient image quality were excluded. A total of 1522 ears met the inclusion criteria and were analyzed for CA status and the presence of partitioned IAC. From this cohort, 91 ears with partitioned IAC were identified. Of these, 85 ears (48 right, 37 left) from 65 patients had both CT and MRI available for detailed analysis and constitute the primary study population. Institutional ethical board approval was obtained (SBA‐ 24/7437‐date: April 02, 2024). Of the 48 right ears with partitioned IAC, 22 were male (45.8%) and 26 were female (54.2%), with a mean age of 2.94 ± 3.24 years (range, 1–17 years). Of the 37 left ears with partitioned IAC, 15 were male (40.5%), and 22 were female (59.5%), with a mean age of 2.46 ± 1.85 years (range, 1–10 years). Partitioned IAC was bilateral in 20 patients and unilateral in the remaining 45 patients.

Radiological Assessment

CT imaging was obtained with 0.5‐mm slice thickness in axial and coronal sections (Somatom Plus 4 Volume Zoom; Siemens, Erlangen, Germany). All CT images were reviewed independently by the first author (L.S.) and the second author (E.T.) using standard temporal bone‐window settings. Discrepancies were resolved by consensus. The MRI was performed by 3‐T scanners (a Magnetom Allegra, Siemens, or an Ingenia, Royal Philips, Amsterdam, the Netherlands). T2‐weighted axial (0.5–0.8‐ mm slice thickness) and oblique sagittal reformat sequences were used for nerve evaluation. Unqualified images due to low resolution, inappropriate plans, or artifacts were excluded.

Inner ear measurements were performed using the Centricity Picture Archiving Communication System (PACS) by General Electric (GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL, USA). The focus of interest was the existence of partitioned IAC, IAC diameter, inner ear malformation type, and CA structure. On the axial images, the width of the CA was measured at its mid‐portion at the mid‐modiolar level. The IAC was also measured, and it was categorized as “narrow” when its midportion diameter on CT axial scans was less than 2 mm [5]. Partitioned IAC was defined as two separate bony canals that exist for the vestibulocochlear nerve and facial nerve inside the IAC. IAC atresia was defined as the absence of a visible lumen on any available CT section.

The cochlear aperture (CA) architecture was classified as normal (diameters more than 1.5 mm), stenotic (diameters less than 1.5 mm), or atretic (0 mm) (Figure 1A,B) [6, 7, 8]. CA abnormalities were further grouped as follows: Isolated aperture atresia: The CA is atretic, but the cochlear structure and dimensions are normal (Figure 1B). According to the abovementioned classification, ears with Type 4 meatal segment, that is, partitioned IAC, were included in the study.

Beforehand, our institution's entire inner ear malformation database was assessed according to CA types, and the IAC was checked to see whether or not it was partitioned. Afterward, the images of ears with partitioned IAC were analyzed and evaluated according to CN status and whether the cochlea was hypoplastic if an MRI was available.

Statistical Analysis

CA, CN, and IAC distributions were given as numbers and percentages. Pearson's chi‐squared test was used to analyze the partitioned IAC distributions according to CA types. Odds ratio (OR) and CI calculations were performed. Analyses were performed using R software, and graphs were drawn using Microsoft Excel. p values below 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 1522 ears with adequate temporal bone CT imaging were analyzed for IAC and CA status. Partitioned IAC was identified in 91 ears during the initial screening. Of these, 85 ears (48 right, 37 left) had complete imaging data available for detailed analysis (Tables 1 and 2). On the right side, 48 ears with partitioned IAC were examined; 34 (70.8%) normal and 14 (29.2%) hypoplastic cochleas were detected. Twelve (25.0%) atretic, 30 (62.5%) stenotic, and 6 (12.5%) normal CA were encountered. As a result of IAC examination, 39 (81.3%) narrow, 3 (6.3%) normal, 3 (6.3%) incomplete formations, and 3 (6.3%) IAC containing only CN were encountered. When we examined the CN status, 39 (83.0%) CN aplasia, 8 (17.0%) CN hypoplasia were seen, and 1 (2.1%) ear could not be evaluated because MR was insufficient. In summary, in right ears with IAC partition, CA was usually atretic/stenotic and CN was almost always deficient, although the cochlea was often normal in size.

Discussion

This rare IAC abnormality is characterized by the presence of a complete or incomplete bony septum that splits the IAC into two separate canals. Vincenti et al. proposed the term “partitioned IAC” rather than “duplicated” IAC [2]. We also believe the term “partitioned” is more accurate because the IAC is divided into two compartments rather than duplicated in this abnormality. This is why we preferred to use the term “partitioned” instead of “duplication” throughout the paper.

According to our findings, partitioned IAC is frequently detected in conjunction with CA abnormalities. This necessitated a review of CA and its abnormalities in this study. The CA (bony CN canal) is the bony canal that connects the IAC to the cochlear modiolus [10, 11]. It is responsible for transporting CN fibers from the spiral ganglion to the CN [12]. Sennaroglu et al. categorized CA anomalies as a separate group of inner ear malformation (IEM) as they can occur in the cochlea with completely normal anatomy except the aperture [13].

The term “isolated aperture atresia” refers to a malformation in which the CA is not visible even though all other cochlear structures are normal. The CN is typically deficient (absent or hypoplastic). A narrow or atretic IAC can be identified concurrently. If the mid‐portion or mid‐modiolar width of CA is < 1.5 mm, this is referred to as “isolated aperture stenosis.” Again, the dimensions and shape of the cochlea are normal. The CN is typically aplastic [7].

It is worth noting that isolated CA stenosis involves the CA and IAC, with no other malformations in the cochlea or vestibule solely. The authors of this article have previously described isolated aperture stenosis [7, 14]. Developmentally, this anomaly is different from IP or other anomalies. The cochlea has 2.5 turns, and the interscalar septa have formed, but the CA remains stenotic. In this case series, CA is stenotic and atretic in the majority of cases. Although the structure of the cochlea is normal, the narrow CA and its association with maldeveloped IAC suggest that the CA may be developmentally part of the IAC.

The IAC embryogenesis differs from that of the inner ear [15]. The IAC is formed by the ossification of the mesoderm surrounding the facial and vestibulocochlear nerves, whereas the inner ear is formed by otic placode ossification [16]. The facial and vestibulocochlear nerves emerge from the facioacoustic primordium, a cluster of cells originating from the neural crest, before separating into two different nerves [16]. During the fifth and sixth months of pregnancy, the mesoderm around the facial and vestibulocochlear nerves chondrifies and then ossifies to create the IAC [17]. This mesenchyme surrounds the entire otocyst at the beginning of the sixth week, and it proceeds to chondrify and ossify until the 16th week [18]. To summarize, the cochlea develops prior to the IAC. CN is created initially within the cochlea, followed by IAC ensheathing these nerves. Although direct causal evidence cannot be established from this retrospective imaging series, it is plausible that CN deficiency may impair the mesodermal stimulus required for normal IAC growth, potentially contributing to anomalies such as partition. CA, a natural extension of IAC, may also remain atretic or stenotic. Despite the fact that the majority of ears with partitioned IAC had normal cochlear structure, IAC was narrow in a high proportion of cases, CA was atretic or stenotic in a high percentage of cases, and CN was deficient in almost the entire sample (Figure 4). This demonstrated that, even if the cochlea develops normally, the nerve structure and canal may not have normal development. Therefore, we can conclude that partitioned IAC and CA anomalies are mesodermal ossification disorders of the vestibulocochlear and facial nerve.

As reported in the literature, partitioned IAC frequently causes narrow IAC in our series. The diameter of the canal cannot reach normal dimensions due to nerve hypoplasia resulting from irregular canal division. Properly developing nerve structure stimulates the surrounding mesodermal structures and the development of the end organ (cochlea).

Chan et al. showed that partitioned IAC might be associated with a rare neurological syndrome called pontine tegmental cap dysplasia. In their case, unilateral partitioned IAC is accompanied by bilateral CN aplasia [19]. Defective neuronal migration and dysfunctional axonal guidance have been implicated in the pathophysiology of this disease [20]. In light of the case of Chan et al., and consistent with the high rate of CN aplasia in our own series (83% right ears, 69.4% left ears), impaired neurogenesis represents a plausible unifying mechanism for partitioned IAC. Although causality cannot be inferred from retrospective data alone, the consistent co‐occurrence of CN deficiency and partitioned IAC across this large cohort supports this hypothesis and warrants prospective investigation.

According to our findings, 20.9% of the ears with atretic CA and 14.9% of the ears with stenotic CA showed partitioned IAC. The probability of IAC being partitioned significantly increases in stenotic and atretic CA compared with ears with normal CA. This suggests that CA anomalies and partitioned IAC structures are anomalies that continue with each other.

Although “partitioned IAC” and “separate facial canal” describe the same pathology, we think it is more appropriate to use each term in different specific situations. When individual segments of the facial canal are described in a report, it is more appropriate to use the term “separate facial canal” as the latter clearly describes the meatal segment of the facial nerve. Using the term “partitioned IAC” does not explicitly describe the meatal segment of the facial nerve. In this situation, the facial nerve is separated from the CN by a bony septum within the IAC. On the other hand, when the IAC is being defined, the term “separate facial canal” is not specific to the IAC. In this situation, “partitioned IAC” is more descriptive of IAC. To describe the anomalous pathways of the facial nerve radiologically, we can explain that the facial nerve is separated from the vestibulocochlear nerve in the meatal segment by a bony canal. When we focus on the internal acoustic canal, we can understand that it is partitioned into two with a bony extension inside. To clarify, the term “partitioned IAC” encompasses a “separate facial canal” where the meatal segment of the facial nerve is in an aberrant position. In this case, the IAC is divided into two separate compartments: (1) The canal of the facial nerve (separate facial canal) and (2) The compartment of the cochleovestibular nerve. Instead of “duplicated,” which refers to two different canals, the term “partitioned” is more appropriate. It is still controversial whether CT or MRI is optimal in patients undergoing cochlear implantation [21, 22]. In order to reduce the duration and cost of the examination, it is sometimes preferable to use a single modality. At first glance on CT, this partition anomaly can be perceived and missed only as stenosis. Assuming that the CN is hypoplastic, a cochlear implant may be planned mistakenly. Partitioned IAC, on the other hand, was almost always related to CA atresia or stenosis in our series. CA atresia/stenosis strongly suggests CN aplasia or unfunctional hypoplasia. While simple IAC stenosis and CN hypoplasia may benefit from a cochlear implant, a separate facial canal with CN hypoplasia/aplasia may not. Therefore, in complex cases such as partitioned IAC, both CT and MRI should be obtained to carefully analyze the contents of each compartment before the decision between CI and ABI.

Because of the rarity of partitioned IAC, a prospective cross‐sectional study design was not feasible; thus, this can be accepted as a limitation of this study. Only recently have a few case reports with a limited number of patients been reported in the literature on IAC stenosis. This study is the most extensive case series published so far. Furthermore, an influential association was discovered between a particular inner ear malformation and partitioned IAC. Along with technological advancements, we can now understand more about the radiological evaluation of IAC and this unrecognized abnormality. Regarding histopathological correlations: the first author (L.S.) has previously conducted extensive histopathological studies of inner ear malformations, and these data have been published in detail [13]. However, at the time the current database was assembled, the clinical significance of the separate facial canal within the context of partitioned IAC had not yet been fully recognized; systematic histopathological sampling specifically targeting this anomaly was therefore not performed in those archival specimens.

The present study offers several topics for future research. Prospective studies with standardized pre‐ and postimplantation imaging protocols, molecular genetic analyses targeting genes involved in neuronal guidance and mesodermal ossification, and long‐term audiological outcome data in patients with partitioned IAC will be essential to clarify the pathophysiology and refine candidacy criteria for cochlear implantation versus auditory brainstem implantation in this population. Neurotrophic mechanisms and the development of neural structures must also be included in future studies on inner ear malformations. The discovery of molecular mechanisms that disrupt neuronal regeneration will occur in future molecular treatments to prevent inner ear malformations. Genes affecting neuron development will be targeted when intrauterine genetic treatments are available.

Conclusion

The most important finding of this study is the association between CA stenosis/atresia and separate facial canal formation. When the CA aperture is atretic/stenotic, the CN is deficient. As a result, the IAC is not developed correctly, increasing the possibility of a separate facial nerve canal resulting in a partitioned IAC. It is essential to perform CT and MRI in these cases and evaluate the neural structures in each compartment before making a decision about CI or ABI.
