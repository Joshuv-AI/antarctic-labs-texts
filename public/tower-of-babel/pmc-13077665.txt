COVID‐19 Antiviral Treatment: Examining Knowledge, Access, Trust in Sources of Information and Willingness to Use Among Essential Workers: A Cross‐Sectional Survey of Cohort Participants
Authors: Jenae Sanders, Jade James‐Gist, Melisa M Shah, Nathan Graff, Olivia Almendares, Tiffany Israel, Yuwei Zhu, Bryan Blette, Sarah Hamid, Hope King, H Keipp Talbot, Carlos G Grijalva, Melissa Briggs‐Hagen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsDespite evidence of effectiveness, oral antivirals for COVID‐19 are underused, with significant variation across populations. This study examines COVID‐19 treatment awareness, knowledge, access, trusted sources, and willingness to use among diverse essential workers in Tennessee during May 18, 2023–June 1, 2024.MethodsParticipants enrolled in a prospective community cohort study completed an enrollment survey on demographics, medical history, and vaccination status. Quarterly cross‐sectional surveys assessed knowledge, awareness, access, trust, and willingness to use COVID‐19 antivirals. Knowledge, access, and trust were measured using five‐point Likert‐type scales, and knowledge was converted into a score based on number of correct responses. Awareness and willingness were assessed using yes/no/unsure responses. Responses from survey respondents with risk factors for severe COVID‐19 were assessed. Descriptive analyses and χ 2 tests assessed differences across demographic and health characteristics. Multivariable log‐binomial regression modeling was used to estimate unadjusted and adjusted prevalence ratios for key outcomes by demographic and health characteristic with 95% confidence intervals.ResultsOf 1289 respondents with risk factors for severe COVID‐19, over 50% were aware of COVID‐19 antivirals; 69% were willing to take antivirals if recommended by their healthcare provider. Adjusted models showed older respondents had higher awareness, knowledge, access and trust in doctors. Black non‐Hispanic respondents, those with lower education, and those with fewer vaccinations were less likely to be aware of antivirals, answer knowledge questions correctly, or report willingness to use antivirals if recommended. Hispanic respondents reported less awareness, knowledge, and lower trust in doctors as a good source of information about COVID‐19 antivirals.ConclusionAlthough willingness to use COVID‐19 antivirals was relatively high among diverse essential workers with risk factors for severe COVID‐19, there was variation based on demographic and health factors. This study identifies key gaps, providing a foundation for developing new approaches to improve access to antiviral treatments.Keywords: attitudes, healthcare access, knowledge, SARS‐CoV‐2, trust, willingness

Background and Aims

COVID‐19 oral antiviral treatments nirmatrelvir‐ritonavir and molnupiravir are recommended for high‐risk patients to reduce the risk of severe COVID‐19 and related complications [1, 2]. These medications decrease the rate of severe disease in high‐risk individuals with mild‐to‐moderate COVID‐19, including vaccinated individuals, when administered within 5 days of symptom onset [1, 3, 4]. Despite their proven effectiveness, many eligible patients do not receive these treatments. One study from 2022 found only 28.4% of eligible adults received a prescription for COVID‐19 antivirals [1].

Previous studies have identified demographic differences in COVID‐19 treatment uptake, with underrepresented populations less likely to receive antiviral treatment [5, 6]. Social vulnerability is a measure that reflects how communities with various sociodemographic characteristics may be negatively impacted as a result of medical or environmental emergencies [7]. Studies have highlighted differences in dispensing rates by location, with persons living in high vulnerability ZIP codes being less likely to receive antivirals than those in low vulnerability areas [8]. Distribution of testing sites and availability of healthcare providers who can prescribe medication may have contributed to these differences.

Attitudes towards safety, benefits, and trust in the healthcare system can also have a significant impact on willingness to receive treatment [9, 10]. Previous studies have found that negative experiences with healthcare services among underrepresented patients may contribute to differences in treatment [10, 11]. Given these findings, it is important to investigate factors associated with awareness of and readiness to receive antiviral treatment among diverse persons.

Essential workers are a key population of interest, as their critical roles in maintaining public health, safety, and essential daily operations necessitate in‐person work that is indispensable to the functioning of society. Essential workers are required to show up in person to meet critical needs of their sector, and multiple studies have shown that they were disproportionately impacted by COVID‐19, especially early during the pandemic. Options for mitigating exposure to infections in the workplace differ widely by sector [12] and a national report evaluating health department data from late 2021 found that outbreaks in facilities with large numbers of essential workers were reported almost as often as outbreaks in health care facilities [13]. Multiple studies found higher rates of early COVID‐19 infections among essential workers than the general population [14], with ongoing effects for many workers [15]. Understanding the perspectives and motivations of this important population is key.

In this study, we sought to understand knowledge, awareness, access, trust in sources and willingness to use COVID‐19 antivirals among a diverse group of essential workers at risk for severe COVID‐19. Understanding gaps in perceived knowledge, access, and willingness to receive treatment, and how these vary across populations, can inform healthcare providers efforts to improve treatment uptake among patients at increased risk for severe outcomes.

Methods

Study Design/Participants

This study was reported following the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) Statement guidelines for reporting observational studies [16]. The Viruses and Infections in Essential Workers (VIEW) study is a community‐based cohort study consisting of demographically diverse essential workers in Tennessee. Essential workers were defined as people required to work outside of their home an average of at least 20 h per week; healthcare workers were not included as they were enrolled in a separate cohort. Participants were enrolled during November 22, 2022–January 31, 2024, and underwent weekly nasal swabs and symptom screening, as well as quarterly surveys as part of a prospective cohort study designed to assess COVID‐19 vaccine effectiveness.

The target study size was set to provide 80% power to detect hypothesized COVID‐19 vaccine effectiveness (the primary outcome of the cohort study), based on projected infection risk and vaccination coverage. Participants were recruited through local community outreach and communications, utilizing an array of essential worker organizations and associations. Inclusion criteria included residing in middle Tennessee (within approximately 100 miles from Nashville), and either being an essential worker (excluding healthcare workers) or living with an essential worker. Participants could enroll in person or remotely, and the study was designed so that all study procedures could be completed remotely if desired. All participants provided written consent. Study consent and materials were available in English and Spanish.

The main analysis in this manuscript focuses on VIEW cohort participants at increased risk for severe COVID‐19, potentially eligible for outpatient COVID‐19 antiviral treatment. This was defined as meeting one of the following criteria: (1) aged 18–49 years with at least one comorbidity associated with progression to severe COVID‐19, or (2) aged 50 years and older [17]. Although determination of who should receive COVID‐19 antivirals is a clinical decision, both underlying medical conditions and age have been demonstrated to be significant predictors of severe COVID‐19, with the risk increasing progressively with age for adults [2].

Data Collection and Definitions

During enrollment, participants provided contact details, demographic information, history of underlying medical conditions, vaccination status, and history of prior SARS‐CoV‐2 infection. Demographic variables included age, sex, race and ethnicity, education, employment status, marital status, health insurance status, and residential address.

Age (in years) was assessed as a continuous variable and categorized into four age groups: 18–34, 35–49, 50–64, and 65 and older. Race and ethnicity were consolidated into five categories: Asian non‐Hispanic (NH), Black NH, White NH, Native NH/other/unknown, or Hispanic/Latino. Insurance status was categorized as employer/outside/other insurance, Medicaid/Medicare, or uninsured. Vaccination status was defined as completed one or more booster doses, completed primary series only, or did not complete primary series (including unvaccinated and partially vaccinated) at the time of the participant's latest quarterly survey. All reported vaccine doses were verified using the state registry, electronic health records, or vaccine card images. COVID‐19 prior infection was self‐reported at enrollment (yes/no).

Census‐tract level social vulnerability index (SVI) values were based on participant address. The SVI incorporates themes such as socioeconomic status, household composition and disability, minority status and language, and housing and transportation [7]. Each participant was assigned an SVI value categorized as Very Low (0.0–0.19), Low (0.20–0.39), Moderate (0.40–0.59), High (0.60–0.79), or Very High (0.80–1.0) [7].

Survey Design

Beginning in February 2023, all cohort participants were asked to complete quarterly surveys that captured updates to their vaccination status as well as a range of knowledge, attitudes, and practices. The survey was updated in May 2023 to include questions about participants' awareness of two FDA‐authorized antiviral treatments, nirmatrelvir‐ritonavir or molnupiravir, for mild to moderate COVID‐19 (using the brand names Paxlovid and Lagevrio). To evaluate knowledge, participants were asked five questions regarding when antivirals were indicated, who benefitted from them, and how they were beneficial. Access was assessed with two questions: one regarding where to find information about antivirals, and another about where to obtain antivirals if needed. Trust in sources of information, such as public health officials, websites, doctors, and pharmacists, was evaluated through five questions asking whether participants considered each source reliable for COVID‐19 antiviral information. Participants' willingness to receive COVID‐19 treatment if recommended by a healthcare provider was measured using a single question. The question refers to the participant's readiness to use antiviral treatment after it has been prescribed by a healthcare provider, as both antiviral medications require a prescription (Supporting Information S1: Figure 1).

Nearly all questions were asked using a five‐point Likert‐type scale (from 1 to 5; 1 = fully disagree, 2 = disagree, 3 = uncertain, 4 = agree, 5 = fully agree). Awareness of antivirals and willingness to take antivirals if recommended by a doctor or pharmacist required responses with yes/no/unsure.

Data Analysis

For each participant, we assessed their most recent response to each of the quarterly survey questions, as of June 1, 2024. Descriptive analyses were conducted on all survey variables to summarize participant characteristics. Survey responses were analyzed overall and stratified by key demographic and health variables, including age group, sex, race and ethnicity, educational attainment, employment status, marital status, insurance status, underlying conditions, vaccination status at the time of survey completion, self‐reported history of COVID‐19, and SVI.

For analysis purposes, the Likert scale was dichotomized by combining “strongly agree” and “agree” as one category, and “disagree”, “strongly disagree”, and “uncertain” as another. For the knowledge score, responses of agree/strongly agree were considered correct for true statements, while responses of disagree/strongly disagree were considered correct for false statements, with a knowledge score value ranging from zero to five based on the number of correct responses. Answering at least three out of five questions correctly was used as a binary outcome for the regression modeling.

We compared proportions of respondents answering “yes” for awareness, access, and willingness outcomes, “agree” or”strongly agree” for trust outcomes, or with a knowledge score of 60% or higher using χ 2 tests; a p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Multivariable log binomial regression modeling was performed to identify associations between participant characteristics and key dependent variables including awareness of antiviral treatments, knowledge about antivirals, ability to access antivirals if needed, trust in one's doctor as a reliable source of information, and willingness to take treatment if recommended by a doctor or pharmacist. All hypothesis testing was two‐sided.

Variables were selected for model inclusion based on existing literature and plausible impact on the outcomes of interest. These included age, sex, race and ethnicity, level of education, marital status, insurance status, number of underlying conditions, vaccination status, self‐reported prior COVID‐19, and SVI [18, 19, 20]. Multicollinearity was assessed by calculating variance inflation factors; no significant multicollinearity was found within any of the models. Unadjusted and adjusted prevalence ratios with their corresponding 95% confidence intervals were reported. Planned assessments of subgroups were conducted through examination of two‐way interaction terms for race and ethnicity and sex; race and ethnicity and age; sex and age; and race and ethnicity and education in each model, but none were statistically significant.

Statistical analysis was performed using the R software version 4.4.1 2023.03.1.

Results

Overall Survey Results

Of 1996 participants enrolled by January 31, 2024, 1644 (82.4%) responded to at least one quarterly survey about COVID‐19 antivirals by June 1, 2024. Study participants were mostly female (68%), demographically diverse (2% Asian, 22% Black NH, 10% Hispanic/Latino, 9% Native NH/other/unknown, and 56% White NH), with a median age of 41.2 years (IQR: 30.7,50.9). Among survey respondents, a total of 1289 (78.4%) had risk factors for severe COVID‐19, which made them potentially eligible for treatment (Table 1).

Awareness of COVID‐19 Antivirals

Among respondents with risk factors for severe COVID‐19, 54% reported being aware of at least one of two specific COVID‐19 antivirals [nirmatrelvir‐ritonavir (Paxlovid) or molnupiravir (Lagevrio)] (Figure 1). Awareness of COVID‐19 antivirals varied across multiple demographic categories (Supporting Information S1: Table 1). The greatest differences by percentage were observed by age (71% among respondents aged 65 and older and 37% among respondents aged 18–34 years), and race and ethnicity (61% among White NH and 24% among Hispanic respondents).

After adjusting for demographic and clinical factors, awareness of COVID‐19 antivirals was higher among older respondents (adjusted prevalence ratio [aPR] 1.90, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.53–2.37 comparing age 65 and older to age 18–34 years]) and was lower among Black NH (aPR 0.80, CI [0.70–0.91]) and Hispanic respondents (aPR 0.48, 95% CI [0.34–0.67]) compared to White NH respondents. Lower awareness was also noted among less vaccinated respondents as compared to those with one or more booster doses, those with high SVI, and those with lower educational attainment (Figure 2, Supporting Information S1: Table 2).

Knowledge About COVID‐19 Treatment

Only 47% of respondents correctly answered at least three out of five COVID‐19 antiviral knowledge questions (Figure 1). The greatest differences in knowledge were found when stratifying respondents by age, race and ethnicity, educational attainment, and vaccination status (Supporting Information S1: Table 3).

In the adjusted analysis, Black NH (aPR 0.79, 95% CI [0.68–0.92]) and Hispanic (aPR 0.72, 95% CI [0.53–0.98]) respondents were less likely than White respondents to answer three or more COVID‐19 antiviral knowledge questions correctly. Respondents who did not complete the primary series (aPR 0.49, 95% CI [0.36–0.68]) or had completed the primary series only (aPR 0.60 95% CI [0.49–0.75]) were less likely to answer correctly than those who reported one or more booster doses. Older age group, higher level of education, having two or more underlying conditions, and self‐reported history of prior COVID‐19 were all associated with increased knowledge about antivirals (Figure 2, Supporting Information S1: Table 4).

Access to Receive Antiviral/Treatment

Two‐thirds of respondents knew where to obtain information about antiviral pills and over 50% knew how to access them if needed (Figure 1). The greatest differences in access by percentage were reported by respondents who were younger (18–34 years) compared to older (65 years and older) (41% vs. 75%), Hispanic/Latino as compared to Asian NH (41% vs. 69%), and uninsured as compared to privately insured (34% vs. 59%) (Supporting Information S1: Table 5).

In the adjusted model, Black non‐Hispanic (NH) and Hispanic/Latino respondents were less likely than White NH respondents to report knowing how to access antiviral pills (aPR 0.85, 95% CI: 0.75–0.97; and aPR 0.79, 95% CI: 0.62–1.00, respectively), although the association for Hispanic/Latino respondents did not reach statistical significance. Respondents who had completed only the primary vaccination series were less likely to report knowing how to access antiviral pills compared with those who had received booster doses (aPR 0.76, 95% CI: 0.64–0.90). Similarly, single respondents were less likely than married respondents to report knowledge of access (aPR 0.87, 95% CI: 0.76–0.99). Reported access to antiviral pills was higher among older respondents and those with a greater number of underlying conditions (Figure 2; Supporting Information S1: Table 6).

Trust in Sources of Information on COVID‐19 Antiviral Treatment

When asked about sources of information on COVID‐19 antivirals, most participants considered their doctors (78%) pharmacist (72%), and public health officials (69%) to be good sources. In contrast, only 8% considered social media platforms a reliable source (Figure 1). Respondents aged 65 years and older were the most likely to trust doctors (92% vs. 72% among those age 18–34 years), whereas trust was lowest among respondents who had not completed the primary series (56% vs. 85% among those who had received booster doses) (Supporting Information S1: Table 7).

These differences persisted in the adjusted model. Respondents who had not completed the primary series (aPR 0.70, 95% CI [0.60–0.81]) and those who had completed the primary series only (aPR 0.81, 95% CI [0.73–0.9]) were less likely to report doctors as trusted sources than those who completed one or more booster doses. Hispanic respondents were also less likely than White NH respondents to report doctors as trusted sources (aPR 0.80 (95% CI [0.69–0.93]). By contrast, respondents aged 50 years and older were more likely than those aged 18–34 years to report trust in doctors for COVID‐19 antiviral information. (Figure 2, Supporting Information S1: Table 8).

Willingness to Receive Antiviral Treatment

Most respondents (69%) reported being willing to take an oral antiviral if recommended by their doctor or pharmacist (Figure 1). The percentage of participants willing to take antivirals was highest among Asian respondents (83%). Participants who didn't complete the primary series or were uninsured had the lowest percentage, both at 56% (Supporting Information S1: Table 5).

In the adjusted model, several factors were associated with willingness to receive COVID‐19 antiviral treatment if recommended. Respondents who had not completed the primary series (aPR 0.42, 95% CI [0.33–0.55]) and those who had received only the primary series (aPR 0.75, 95% CI [0.66–0.85]) were less likely to report willingness to receive treatment than those who had received boosters. Lower educational attainment, Black NH race/ethnicity, and female sex were also associated with lower willingness (Figure 2, Supporting Information S1: Table 9).

Discussion

This study found that most essential workers with risk factors for severe COVID‐19 disease surveyed were willing to take COVID‐19 antivirals if recommended by their healthcare provider. Not surprisingly, older individuals and those with more than two underlying conditions were more likely to answer knowledge questions correctly and report knowing how to access antivirals. However, Black NH survey respondents, those with lower education and those who had received fewer COVID‐19 vaccine doses were all less likely to be aware of antivirals, answer antiviral knowledge questions correctly, or report willingness to use antivirals if recommended by a doctor or pharmacist. Hispanic/Latino respondents were less likely to be aware of antivirals, answer knowledge questions correctly, or agree with statements describing doctors as a good source of information on antivirals. These findings are concerning given that some of these same factors have also been associated with an increased risk of severe disease [21, 22].

A recent nationwide survey found low awareness of Paxlovid and, even among those familiar with the medication, misconceptions about its benefits and side effects [9]. Similar to our findings, this study found lower awareness and misperceptions were more prevalent among medically vulnerable and disadvantaged populations who could potentially benefit from treatment. These groups included unvaccinated adults, individuals with lower education levels, and Black and Hispanic or Latino adults. Simplifying antiviral information and using clear, accessible language can help bridge knowledge gaps. Providers should engage patients with risk factors for severe COVID‐19 to enhance their understanding of treatment options.

During the pandemic, initiatives such as Test‐to‐Treat aimed to improve access to antivirals by offering timely access to diagnostic tests and antiviral prescriptions through pharmacists and telehealth services [23]. However, studies have shown that the distribution of testing and treatment sites posed challenges, with persistent lower access to antiviral treatments in rural areas and those with higher social vulnerability [18, 24, 25]. In this study, reported trust in doctors and pharmacists as reliable sources of information about COVID‐19 treatment was lowest among Hispanic/Latino respondents and individuals with lower vaccination status. Building trust in healthcare providers and pharmacists is crucial, particularly among Hispanic/Latino respondents and individuals with lower vaccination status. Culturally tailored education and outreach efforts could help address these concerns. Studies have also found that Black and Hispanic individuals have also faced barriers in accessing telemedicine services for COVID‐19 care [26, 27]. Findings from this study point to the need to continue to explore new models to improve awareness and willingness among populations at increased risk for severe outcomes.

Our analysis found no significant association between willingness to take antivirals (if prescribed) and either insurance status or social vulnerability index. For most of the study period, nirmatrelvir‐ritonavir and molnupiravir were available at no cost to patients. However, in November 2023, both drugs became commercially available, potentially increasing the cost to patients. Out of pocket costs for a full course of treatment may now be as high as $700–1500 for uninsured patients. While patient assistance programs aim to improve affordability, differences in treatment access may have worsened under this new paradigm. Ongoing monitoring of treatment uptake and factors influencing factors is crucial to addressing these challenges.

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the generalizability of results may be constrained because cohort participants may differ from the broader population and may be more likely to engage in healthcare. Additionally, self‐reported data are subject to response bias, including the possibility that participants provided answers they perceived as socially desirable. Another limitation is that this analysis relied exclusively on the most recent survey response from each participant, which precludes assessment of longitudinal changes in awareness, knowledge, or willingness to accept treatment over time. Furthermore, the study did not evaluate actual uptake of nirmatrelvir‐ritonavir or molnupiravir, which may differ from stated willingness. The survey item assessing willingness to use treatment also did not capture reasons for declining therapy, limiting insight into potential barriers. Despite these limitations, the study provides valuable information on COVID‐19 antiviral treatment awareness, knowledge, access, trust, and willingness to receive treatment among a diverse cohort of essential workers. Future research should incorporate longitudinal analyses to examine trends in willingness, assess the predictive validity of stated willingness for actual antiviral use, and explore qualitative factors influencing treatment decisions. Comparative studies across different populations will also be critical to determine whether these findings are consistent in other settings.

Effective antiviral use requires active patient participation. For treatment to effectively prevent progression to severe disease, patients with mild COVID‐19 symptoms need to engage their healthcare system, know or learn about treatment options, receive a prescription, obtain and start treatment, all within a couple of days of symptom onset. Providers may consider educating their patients with risk factors for severe COVID‐19 about treatment options even before they become ill. Asking patients about their willingness to receive treatment and addressing any barriers to receiving treatment may also be important to further reduce treatment disparities.

Essential workers are crucial in keeping vital services running during emergency responses or pandemics, but they face a higher risk of exposure to infectious diseases. This study found that while there's a high willingness to receive antiviral treatment, there are significant gaps in knowledge and willingness to receive treatment among multiple demographic groups at increased risk for severe COVID‐19. These gaps can lead to poor health outcomes and exacerbate existing health disparities. Understanding the complex factors influencing awareness, perceptions, and acceptance of COVID‐19 treatment is crucial for improving health outcomes. By identifying the factors associated with differing understandings of COVID‐19 treatment, we can inform targeted efforts to address these gaps. This can include tailored education initiatives, community outreach programs, and policy interventions aimed at increasing access to treatment. Ensuring everyone has the opportunity to make informed decisions about their health is vital. By addressing the identified gaps and disparities, we can ultimately contribute to improved health outcomes and a more resilient essential worker community. Moving forward, collaborative efforts between policymakers, healthcare providers, and community leaders will be essential to developing effective strategies for promoting COVID‐19 treatment.
