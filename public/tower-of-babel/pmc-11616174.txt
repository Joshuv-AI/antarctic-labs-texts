Efficacy and safety of combining anti-angiogenic therapy, radiotherapy, and PD-1 inhibitors in patients with driver gene-negative non-small cell lung cancer brain metastases: a retrospective study
Authors: Xianwen Zhang, Qian Sun, Rujun Chen, MengDie Zhao, Feng Cai, Zhen Cui, Hao Jiang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe efficacy and safety of anti-angiogenic combination therapy in patients with driver gene-negative non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) with brain metastases (BM) are uncertain.MethodsEighty-eight records of driver gene-negative patients with NSCLC treated with craniocerebral radiotherapy (RT) and programmed death factor-1 (PD-1) inhibitors between May 2021 and May 2023 were collected. Based on whether anti-angiogenic therapy (AT) is combined or not, patients are categorized into the AT group and the non anti-angiogenic therapy (NAT) group. The NAT group patients received craniocerebral RT and PD-1 inhibitor and those in the AT group received craniocerebral RT and PD-1 inhibitor with ≥ 4 cycles of AT. Comparing the clinical efficacy and safety in these two patient cohorts was the main goal of the study.ResultsBy May 1, 2024, the iORR was 94.0% and 63.2% for AT and NAT group, respectively. The 1- and 2-year iLPFS for AT and NAT group were 93.6%, 80.9% and 69.7%, 36.4%, respectively. The 1- and 2-year iDPFS were 86.7%, 56.3% and 59.1%, 48.3%, respectively. The 1- and 2-year OS were 82.0%, 36.6% and 68.4%, 34.6%, respectively. Compared to the standard treatment (RT and PD-1 inhibitors), the addition of AT prolonged the median iLPFS (NR vs. 22.0 months, hazard ratio [HR] = 11.004, P < 0.001) and the median iDPFS (NR vs. 20.0 months, HR = 8.732, P = 0.003), but was not significant in the extension of the OS (21.0 vs. 19.0 months, HR = 1.601, P = 0.206). Multivariable analysis showed that combination therapy with AT is significantly associated with prolonged iLPFS (HR = 4.233, P = 0.002) and iDPFS (HR = 2.824, P = 0.007), whereas only GPA score is significantly associated with improved OS (HR = 0.589, P = 0.019). The incidence of hypertension in the AT group showed an increasing trend, and no significant increased risk of radiation-induced brain necrosis was found. No drug-related intracranial hemorrhage events occurred.ConclusionCombining AT, RT, and PD-1 inhibitors can substantially improve iLPFS and iDPFS for patients with driver gene-negative NSCLC with BM; however, it is not significantly associated with better OS.

Background

Lung cancer has the highest overall incidence (12.4%) and mortality (18.7%) rates among all cancers worldwide [1]. In China, approximately 30% of cancer deaths originate from lung cancer [2], and the incidence is still increasing compared with the decreasing incidence in some Western countries [3]. Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) accounts for about 85% of all lung cancers, and the brain is the most common site of metastasis, with approximately 50% of patients with NSCLC developing brain metastases (BM) during disease progression. Approximately 10–20% of newly diagnosed patients with NSCLC already have BM, and patients with NSCLC have BM for a median of 11 months [4, 5]. Additionally, BM are usually associated with a shorter survival time and poor quality of life; the median overall survival (OS) is 3–15 months [6]. Improving the survival of patients with BM in NSCLC has been the focus of many studies.

Surgery and craniocerebral radiotherapy (RT) can quickly relieve the central nervous system symptoms at a relatively effective local control rate, which is still the main method of treatment for patients with symptomatic BM. Local treatment should not be delayed for patients with asymptomatic BM [7]. Owing to the improvement of comprehensive anti-tumor treatment regimens, patients with lung cancer survive significantly longer. Concurrently, advances in imaging technology have led to an increase in the diagnosis of multiple BM, thereby limiting the role of surgical treatment in this context. Moreover, studies have shown that surgery alone is not sufficient to maintain local tumor control, and postoperative adjuvant RT is still needed to reduce local recurrence [7, 8]. Therefore, combination therapy based on RT is the preferred method for patients with BM, especially the application of stereotactic radiotherapy (SRT) which reduces the risk of neurocognitive dysfunction caused by whole brain radiotherapy (WBRT) [9].

In the past 2 decades, the application of targeting specific driver gene mutations drugs such as EGFR and ALK has significantly improved the prognosis of driver gene positive NSCLC [10, 11]. May benefit from its ability to cross the blood-brain barrier, even patients with BM at initial diagnosis can achieve effective intracranial disease control, with intracranial objective response rate (iORR) ranging from 42% ~ 100% and a median OS of up to 47 months [12, 13]. However, the treatment of driver gene-negative patients with NSCLC BM remains challenging. Crossing the blood-brain barrier is difficult, and chemotherapy drugs alone have limited effects on BM, even when combined with WBRT, the efficacy is not significantly improved, which limits the application of chemotherapy in patients BM [14]. The application of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), such as programmed death factor-1 (PD-1) / programmed death factor ligand-1(PD-L1) inhibitors, has opened up a new therapeutic pathway for patients with driver gene-negative NSCLC BM. The comprehensive data from prospective and retrospective analyses indicate that ICIs monotherapy improves the survival of patients with BM, with a median OS of 9.7–17 months [15–17]. However, ICIs therapy still has considerable clinical limitations: with a highly heterogeneous iORR of 9–70% in patients with NSCLC and BM, significantly influenced by the level of PD-L1 expression and the type of tumor. The iORR with monotherapy is usually < 30% [18, 19]. Studies have shown that the combination of ICIs and craniocerebral RT increases blood-brain barrier permeability, allowing ICIs to enter brain tissue and improve intracranial efficacy. However, there are few studies that include symptomatic BM patient, the number of beneficiaries is still limited, and there is still an unmet need for clinical treatment [20, 21].

The occurrence of tumor BM depends on vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) [22], which is highly expressed in BM lesions and associated with poor prognosis [23]. Previous studies have shown that anti-angiogenic drugs can normalize tumor blood vessels and increase RT sensitivity by downregulating the expression of VEGF and improving hypoxia reperfusion [24, 25]. Bevacizumab is a recombinant humanized monoclonal antibody targeting VEGF. It has been found to have anti-tumor proliferation effects in established mouse models of BM, indicating that bevacizumab can penetrate the blood-brain barrier [26]. Anlotinib is a novel multi-target tyrosine kinase inhibitor that inhibits angiogenesis. Animal studies have shown that craniocerebral RT can enhance anlotinib’s ability to cross the blood-brain barrier [27]. Currently, VEGF is also considered to have immunosuppressive effects, with VEGF levels higher in non-responders to anti-PD-1 therapy than in responders. Furthermore, AT seems to have the potential to overcome ICIs resistance [28, 29]. A real-world study found that compared to ICIs + chemotherapy, AT + ICIs and AT + ICIs + chemotherapy significantly reduced the risk of disease progression and death in patients with advanced NSCLC. The median PFS of the three groups were 3.5, 5.1, and 7.5 months, respectively, whereas the median OS was 21.8, 33.1, and 37.3 months, respectively [30]. However, this study only included a small number of patients with BM and did not provide details related to craniocerebral RT. The need to explore whether the efficacy of this combination therapy can be expanded to patients with BM remains urgent.

Therefore, for patients with driver gene-negative NCSLC BM, the combination of RT and ICIs with AT shows notable potential as an effective treatment strategy. We conducted a retrospective analysis to evaluate the efficacy and safety of this triple combination therapy.

Methods

Patients

From May 2021 to May 2023, we enrolled 88 patients with driver gene-negative NSCLC with BM at the First Affiliated Hospital of Bengbu Medical University who met the eligibility requirements. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Age between 18 and 80 years old; (2)clear histopathological diagnosis with no mutations in driver genes; (3) controlled primary tumors, with no anti-tumor treatment administered within the last 6 months; (4) detailed imaging evaluation before and during BM treatment, including chest or abdominal computed tomography, emission computed tomography, and cranial enhanced magnetic resonance imaging (MRI); at least one measurable intracranial lesion; if accompanied by extracranial metastasis, there was one extracranial metastasis and ≤ three metastatic lesions. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) long-term use of steroids for autoimmune diseases or contraindications to other RT, ICIs, and AT ; (2) failure to complete the RT course or < 4 cycles of ICIs and AT; (3) interval between RT and ICIs > 3 months or between ICIs and AT > 2 weeks; (4) history of other malignancies; (5) local surgery or other systemic treatments after BM diagnosis; (6) incomplete follow-up data.

Depending on whether AT was combined or not, they were divided into two groups as follows: AT group: AT + craniocerebral RT + PD-1 inhibitors; NAT group: craniocerebral RT + PD-1 inhibitors.

RT pattern

Clinicians should choose the RT segmentation mode based on the intracranial tumor site and size, number of BM, histological type, general condition, and estimated survival time. The three main types in this study were: SRT ( 51–52.5 GY/15–17 F, 50–60 GY / 10–12 F), WBRT ( 30 GY / 10 F, 40 GY / 20 F), and SRT + WBRT (different combinations of the two).

PD-1 inhibitors and AT

All ICIs are programmed death factor-1 (PD-1) inhibitors, the most commonly used PD-1 inhibitors were pembrolizumab (2 mg/kg, q3w), sintilimab (200 mg/time, q3w), tislelizumab (200 mg/time, q3w), and camrelizumab (200 mg/time, q3w). The AT drugs included anlotinib (10–12 mg, d1–14 days, q3w) and bevacizumab (7.5 mg/kg, q3w).

Study endpoints

The initial post-treatment follow-up should occur between 4 and 8 weeks after the BM therapy, and subsequent evaluations should be conducted every 3 to 6 months, continuing until May 2024. Relevant clinical information was collected for all patients, including sex, age, smoking history, histological type, number of BM, peritumoral edema status, the maximum diameter of BM, central nervous system symptom, extracranial lesions, and the Graded Prognostic Assessment (GPA) score [13]. Tumor response rate was assessed using the modified Response Evalu- ation Criteria in Solid Tumours (mRECIST1. 1) criteria, categorized as complete response (CR), partial response (PR), stable disease (SD), or progressive disease (PD). The following endpoints were assessed: (1) the iORR, the total CR and PR rates for intracranial reactions; (2) intracranial local progression-free survival (iLPFS), the time from BM treatment initiation to the progression of intracranial primary lesions; (3) intracranial distant progression-free survival (iDPFS), the time from BM treatment initiation to the appearance of a new lesion in the skull; and (4) OS, the time between BM treatment initiation and the last follow-up or death.Toxicity grading is based on the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events v4.0 grading criteria.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables that follow a normal distribution were described using mean and standard deviation, continuous variables that did not follow a normal distribution were described using median and interquartile range, and categorical variables were described using frequency. We compared iORR and adverse reactions between two groups using chi square test and Fisher’s exact test. The Kaplan–Meier method was used to evaluate iLPFS, iDPFS, OS, and log-rank tests were performed. Univariable and multivariable analyses were conducted using the Cox proportional hazards model, all statistical tests were two-sided, and P ˂ 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All data were analyzed using SPSS software (version 29.0; IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) and GraphPad Prism (version 10.22; GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA).

Results

Patient characteristics

This study included 88 patients in total; 38 patients (43%) had RT + PD-1 inhibitors, and 50 (57%) had AT + RT + PD-1 inhibitors. There were 65 males (74%) and 23 females (26%). Patients had a mean age (std, range) of 60.4 (9.4, 32–80) years.Seventy-six patients (86%) had intracranial symptoms, with a median peritumoral edema diameter (Q₁, Q₃) of 1.23 (0.74, 2.18) cm and median maximal BM diameter (Q₁, Q₃) of 2.24 (1.80, 3.02) cm. Fifty-five patients (63%) had a BM number ≤ 3, with a median BM number (Q₁, Q₃) of 3.00 (1.00, 4.75) .Sixty-one patients (69%) were diagnosed with a pathological type of adenocarcinoma, and 27 patients (31%) had another pathological type of squamous cell carcinoma. Extracranial metastases were present in 21 patients (24%), 12 of whom (57%) had asymptomatic bone metastases, 4 of whom (19%) had adrenal metastases, 3 of whom (14%) had lung metastases, 1 of whom (5%) had liver metastasis and 1 of whom (5%) had abdominal lymph node metastasis. WBRT was given to 13 patients (15%), SRT was given to 54 patients (61%), and WBRT + SRT was given to 21 patients (24%). The original lung cancer GPA dataset included age, number of BM, KPS score and extracranial metastases is used to predict the prognosis of NSCLC patients with BM. In 52 patients (59%), the GPA was ≥ 2.5, with a median GPA (Q₁, Q₃) of 2.5 (2.00, 3.00). The baseline characteristics of the two groups patients were similar. Intracranial response

Compared to 8 patients (21%) who achieved CR and 16 patients (42%) who achieved PR in the NAT group, 16 patients (32%) achieved CR, and 31 patients (62%) achieved PR in the AT group. The iORR of patients was considerably increased by combination of AT (94% vs. 63%, P < 0.001)(Table 2).

Prognostic factors for iLPFS and iDPFS

As of May 2024, the median follow-up time was 16 months (range 5–33 months). A total of 24 patients (27%) had local intracranial progression, and 30 (34%) had distant intracranial metastasis as identified by intracranial MRI and mRECIST1.1criteria. Among the 24 patients with local intracranial progression, 7 were from the AT group and 17 from the NAT group. Excluding three patients who received only symptomatic supportive therapy, the remaining received additional anti-tumor treatment: eight received chemotherapy and AT, nine received chemotherapy, one received AT, one received local surgery, one had local surgery and chemotherapy, and one received WBRT and chemotherapy. Among the 30 patients with distant intracranial metastasis, 19 were from the NAT and 11 from the AT group. Excluding two patients who received only symptomatic support, the remaining received additional anti-tumor treatment: ten received chemotherapy, two received AT, four had chemotherapy and AT, six received local RT, three received local RT and AT, and three adjusted ICIs and AT drug therapy type. Thirteen patients had local intracranial progression and distant intracranial metastasis: two from AT group and 11 from the NAT group.

The 1- and 2-year iLPFS for AT and NAT group were 93.6%, 80.9% and 69.7%, 36.4%, respectively. The 1- and 2-year iDPFS were 86.7%, 56.3% and 59.1%, 48.3%, respectively. Compared to the standard treatment (RT and PD-1 inhibitors), the addition of AT prolonged the median iLPFS (NR vs. 22.0 months, hazard ratio [HR] = 11.004, P < 0.001) (Fig. 1A) and the median iDPFS (NR vs. 20.0 months, HR = 8.732, P = 0.003) (Fig. 1B). Univariable analysis showed a relationship between the number of BM (HR = 1.288, 95% CI: 1.108–1.497, P < 0.001), combination of AT (HR = 3.946, 95% CI: 1.635–9.526, P = 0.002) with iLPFS (Table 3), and a relationship between peritumoral edema (HR = 1.256, 95% CI: 1.010–1.562, P = 0.041), the combination of AT (HR = 2.892, 95% CI: 1.372–6.098, P = 0.005) with iDPFS (Table 4). Multivariable analysis showed that combined AT (HR = 4.233, 95% CI: 1.721–10.411, P = 0.002) and the number of BM (HR = 1.272, 95% CI:1.066–1.516, P = 0.007) were significantly correlated with prolonged iLPFS (Table 3). Combined AT (HR = 2.824, 95% CI: 1.335–5.971, P = 0.007) was significantly associated with prolonged iDPFS (Table 4).

Prognostic factors for OS

As of May 2024, 55 patients (63%) had died. The 1- and 2-year OS for AT and NAT group were 82.0%, 36.6%, and 68.4%, 34.6%, respectively. Although the OS of the AT group was slightly longer than that of the NAT group, it was not related to the addition of AT (21.0 vs. 19.0 months, HR = 1.601, P = 0.206). (Fig. 2). Univariable analysis showed a relationship between the number of BM (HR = 1.244, 95% CI: 1.116–1.387; P < 0.001), GPA (HR = 0.533, 95% CI: 0.401–0.708, P < 0.001) with OS, whereas multivariable analysis showed a significant correlation between higher GPA scores and longer OS (HR = 0.589, 95% CI: 0.379–0.916, P = 0.019) (Table 5).

Safety

The most common acute adverse reactions in the two groups of patients included decreased appetite and fatigue. The incidence of hypertension in the AT group was significantly higher than that in the NAT, and the difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.061). Most of the adverse reactions were grade 1–2. Two additional adverse reactions that need attention are radiation-induced brain necrosis and bleeding. In the AT and NAT groups, there were 3 cases (including 2 cases of rectal bleeding and 1 case of hemoptysis) and 2 cases of bleeding (1 case of gum bleeding and 1 case of nasal bleeding), respectively. After stopping the medication, the bleeding improved and did not recur after reducing the drug dosage. No cerebral hemorrhage was observed in all patients. There were six and seven cases of radiation-induced brain necrosis in the AT and NAT groups, respectively. Only one case in the NAT group had grade 3 radiation-induced brain necrosis, the symptoms were relieved after hormone combined with Bevacizumab. Additionally, three patients experienced grade 3 adverse reactions: hypertension, bone marrow suppression, and rash, with no drug-related deaths occurred. Discussion

The discovery of ICIs significantly changed the treatment approach for patients with advanced NSCLC without driver gene mutations, improved survival outcomes, and was effective for intracranial lesions [18, 31]. However, owing to the limited iORR of monotherapy and delayed response (usually 3–4 months after treatment initiation), and false progression, studies have suggested combining ICIs with RT, AT, or other systemic therapies to enhance efficacy and overcome drug resistance [32–34].

A meta-analysis showed that for patients with NSCLC BM, RT combined with anti-tumor drugs had the best efficacy. The iORR was highest (81%) when RT was combined with ICIs, with a median iPFS of 7.04 months. In contrast, RT plus chemotherapy had the worst iORR (46%) and median iPFS (5.7 months). ICIs combined with chemotherapy had immediate results, with an iORR of 56% and a median iPFS of 6.9 months. The median iPFS for ICIs alone was significantly lower at 2.29 months [35]. An international multicenter study found that intraoperative radiation therapy combined with immunotherapy or targeted therapy has a synergistic effect in BM, achieving a higher 2-year local control rate (93.7%) and 1-year iDPFS (60%). In this study, a patient diagnosed with renal cell carcinoma ceased using cabozantinib—a multi-target inhibitor of angiogenesis, C-MET, RET, and other pathways—after seven months of treatment due to grade 3 radiation-induced brain necrosis. Notably, the patient subsequently found relief from the symptoms of radiation-induced brain necrosis by using bevacizumab, after dexamethasone had failed to provide alleviation [36]. Given that only 24.3% of the patients in this study underwent targeted therapy in conjunction with stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS) and ICIs, and considering the diversity of targeted drugs, including tyrosine kinase inhibitors and anti-angiogenic agents, it is indeed worthwhile to further investigate whether the AT therapy to RT combined with ICIs could enhance intracranial efficacy while not increasing the incidence of radiation-induced brain necrosis.

A preclinical study in a murine lung cancer model found that RT increased CD8 + T cells, CD4 +T cells, dendritic cells, myeloid-derived suppressor cells, and PD-L1 expression in tumor cells. It also increased PD-1 expression on CD8 + and CD4 + T cells. However, AT treatment significantly reduced PD-1 expression induced by RT on these T cells, suggesting a weak reversal of the immunosuppressive environment. Compared with single or double ICIs therapy, RT combined with anti-PD-L1 therapy can inhibit tumor growth, improve survival, prolong the immune memory effect, and prevent tumor recurrence. Similar and more significant effects were observed when anti-VEGF therapy was added to RT and anti-PD-L therapy, providing a basis for new combination therapies [37].

The dual effects of immune activation and immune suppression induced by the combination therapy of RT and ICIs have also been observed in other studies. The introduction of AT therapy can not only suppress tumor growth and metastasis via direct antitumor effects, but also bolster antitumor immune responses and foster a microenvironment conducive to radiation therapy. This is accomplished by enhancing T cell infiltration into tumors, decreasing immunosuppressive cytokines, and fostering the normalization of tumor vasculature to enhance oxygenation and reperfusion [37–40]. These dynamic interactions provide a theoretical basis for RT, ICIs, and AT combined antitumor therapy.

A meta-analysis showed PD1/PDL1 inhibitors combined with RT and AT achieved a higher disease control rate of 92% and CR rate of 48% compared to monotherapy or dual therapy in the treatment of solid tumors [41]. Wang et al. retrospectively analyzed 308 patients with NSCLC BM who received PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitor based monotherapy or combination therapy. The results showed that compared with PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitor plus chemotherapy or PD-1/PD-L1 monotherapy, PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitor combined AT therapy was associated with the longest PFS (7.7 vs. 3.2 vs. 2.5 months; P = 0.0251) and OS (29.2 vs.15.8 vs. 8.3 months; P = 0.0001) [42]. A phase IB clinical study showed that the iORR of anlotinib combined with sintilimab in the treatment of patients with advanced untreated NSCLC is 72.7%, the disease control rate is 100%, and the median PFS is 15 months, achieving encouraging sustained efficacy and safety [43]. However, only 18% of asymptomatic BM patients were included in the study, so the effect of RT was not evaluated. Yang et al. conducted a retrospective analysis and found that compared with ICIs monotherapy and ICIs combination chemotherapy, AT combined with ICIs and chemotherapy significantly improved the survival and reduced the risk of death in patients with driver gene-negative NSCLC BM, with median OS of 11.4, 16.2, and 23.6 months, respectively (P < 0.001). In this study, 81.3% of patients had peritumoral edema and 56.9% had central nervous system symptoms. The addition of craniocerebral RT significantly improved central nervous system symptoms, and 62.7% of patients achieved CR or PR, thereby significantly reducing the risk of death caused by SD and PD [44] .

However, due to the exclusion of BM from large-scale studies or the inclusion of only a small number of patients with stable or asymptomatic BM, the potential benefits of combining AT with craniocerebral RT and ICIs for the treatment of BM are inferred based on the treatment model of advanced NSCLC. Whether asymptomatic or symptomatic patients with BM should be randomly assigned to receive only drug therapy, only RT, or drug plus RT (regardless of stratification based on PD-L1 status) is a highly controversial issue.There are currently multiple prospective clinical studies (NCT05021328, NCT02681549, NCT03175432, NCT05522660, NCT05522660, NCT05812534 NCT05703269) underway to explore the optimal combination mode between AT and ICIs, RT and ICIs with or without AT, including the timing of combination therapy, dose fractionation mode of RT, etc., which will provide more authentic evidence to support the results of this study.

Although there is currently a lack of prospective data to evaluate this triple combination approach, AT combined with ICIs is increasingly being offered to BM patients receiving RT at different times in clinical practice.Our research revealed that the integration of AT with PD-1 inhibitors and craniocerebral RT markedly enhances iLPFS and iDPFS among patients with driver gene-negative NSCLC BM, especially 86% of symptomatic BM patients included in this study, offering a potential treatment combination strategy for this patient group. The study also indicated a trend towards OS extension in patients undergoing AT treatment, although this did not achieve statistical significance. This outcome may be attributed to the retrospective design of the study, which was susceptible to variations in patient tumor status and physician treatment preferences. Furthermore, patients experiencing intracranial progression were subjected to a variety of systemic treatments, including AT, without a standardized approach. Consequently, the findings of this study should be considered with appropriate caution.

Peritumoral edema in BM affects patients’ quality of life, for patients with NSCLC and BM, peritumoral edema < 1 cm is associated with better prognosis and a lower risk of recurrence [45, 46]. A phase III study (NCT02366143) analyzed 1202 untreated metastatic NSCLC patients and showed that the atezolizumab + bevacizumab + chemotherapy group had better OS than that of the atezolizumab + chemotherapy group, with a lower incidence of new BM [47]. This suggests that the combination of bevacizumab and atezolizumab can delay the occurrence of new BM. Patients with BM often require hormone therapy owing to brain tissue edema, including peritumoral edema caused by tumors and radiation-induced brain edema caused by treatment, which affects the efficacy of ICIs. Bevacizumab combined with ICIs treatment can alleviate peritumoral edema, improve symptoms, reduce hormone use, and promote the improvement of immunotherapy efficacy [48]. Similar reports have also been made in the study of anlotinib, where AT drugs effectively replace hormones and avoid the effects of immune suppression [49]. Similarly, we found that patients with larger peritumoral edemas have a heightened risk of developing intracranial distant metastasis. Notably, increasing AT therapy appears to reduce this risk.

The sequence of RT, ICIs, and AT is a key issue that urgently needs resolution. Owing to safety concerns with craniocerebral RT and systemic treatment combination, some patients may only receive systemic treatment without RT, especially ICIs, which acts slowly. This delay can cause patients to miss the optimal window for achieving lasting intracranial reaction, leading to progression, neurological deficits, poor quality of life, and even death. A large retrospective study involving 380 patients with malignant melanoma BM found that patients who received SRT or surgical combined with ICIs treatment significantly improved the OS of asymptomatic and symptomatic malignant melanoma BM patients compared to those who did not receive local treatment (p = 0.009) [50]. Additionally, a retrospective analysis of patients with metastatic rectal cancer who received local radiotherapy found that the addition of RT significantly improved PFS (median: 5.0 vs. 3.6 months; p = 0.04) and OS (median: 15.2 vs. 7.2 months; p = 0.01) compared to patients who did not receive local radiotherapy. Among patients receiving RT, those who received RT sequential/simultaneous ICIs and AT treatment had better PFS than that of patients who received AT and ICIs sequential RT (median: 7.1 vs. 6.2 vs. 3.5 months; p = 0.004) [51]. Hall J et al. found that synchronous treatment with SRS/SRT combined with ICIs (within 1 month before and after) had a lower local control failure rate compared to asynchronous treatment (3.0% vs. 8.4%, P = 0.01) [52]. At present, multiple studies have obtained similar results, that is, patients who receive craniocerebral RT and ICIs or AT synchronous treatment can achieve better therapeutic effects. However, there is no unified standard for defining the time interval of synchronous treatment, and most of them are recognized as within 3 months [53–55].

An important consideration for AT combined with craniocerebral RT and ICIs treatment is the potential increased risk of adverse events, most notably the occurrence of radiation-induced brain necrosis and cerebral hemorrhage.Some studies have shown that symptomatic radiation-induced brain necrosis is more common in patients receiving RT alone than in patients receiving ICIs and RT. This may be due to the longer survival time of patients receiving combined ICIs treatment, which is higher than the median time of occurrence of radiation-induced brain necrosis, resulting in higher reported radiation-induced brain necrosis [53, 56]. Compared to NSCLC, radiation-induced brain necrosis is more common in malignant melanoma [57]. The study by Hall J et al. showed that there was no significant difference in the occurrence of radiation-induced brain necrosis between synchronous treatment with SRS/SRT combined with ICIs (within 1 month before and after) and asynchronous treatment (5.7% vs. 8.1%, P = 0.314). Further analysis of the factors related to radiation-induced brain necrosis revealed that the occurrence of G2 + radiation-induced brain necrosis is associated with PD-L1 ≥ 50% and metastatic tumor diameter ≥ 20 millimeters [52]. Cabanie et al. also showed that the volume of intracranial metastases, rather than the time interval between SRT and ICIs treatment, is the main factor affecting brain necrosis [58]. An international multicenter study also reached a similar conclusion. The incidence rate of symptomatic radiation-induced brain necrosis after SRS combined with ICIs for one year and two years was 4.8% and 7.2% respectively, which was not related to the time of combining ICIs, but related to the volume of brain tissue receiving 12GY radiation [59]. A meta-analysis found that patients with SRS ≥ 25 Gy had an increased risk of radiation-induced necrosis compared to those with SRS ≤ 24 Gy (p = 0.158) [41]. The preliminary results of the ongoing RADREMI trial (NCT04047602) show that, with concurrent ICIs, the 6-month radiation necrosis and local control rates in SRS patients with reduced dose were 0% and 98%, respectively [60]. Therefore, for patients receiving SRS treatment, it is recommended to use ICIs simultaneously to achieve maximum therapeutic effect. In the selection of RT dose, clinicians may also be more inclined to use low-dose SRS while ensuring clinical efficacy to reduce the risk of radiation-induced brain necrosis.

Another major issue with combining AT on this basis is the potential risk of cerebral hemorrhage. A meta-analysis involving 8713 patients showed that the use of bevacizumab did not significantly increase the risk of intracranial hemorrhage [61]. The combination of AT with craniocerebral RT and AT with ICIs has been reported to not increase the risk of intracranial hemorrhage [42, 62]. At present, there is a lack of prospective data to evaluate the safety of this triple therapy. However, in clinical practice, the use of RT as a local treatment in combination with ICIs and AT is increasing. Our study found that triple therapy did not significantly increase the risk of radiation-induced brain necrosis and intracranial hemorrhage, providing clinical experience for the safety of combination therapy. Nonetheless, this study is a retrospective analysis, and there is no standardized treatment for the population selection, order of combination, and radiation dose of the three combination therapy. Prospective studies are still needed to further explore the efficacy and safety of combination therapy, providing reliable evidence for clarifying the optimal mode of BM treatment.

Limitations

Our study had the following limitations: first, it is a retrospective analysis, and there was an inherent selection bias in the patient population. In clinical practice, which patients receive combined AT is influenced by the patient’s general condition, symptoms of BM, and the doctor’s treatment choice. Second, the sample size of this study was relatively small, and the diversity of PD-1 inhibitors and AT drugs involved may affect the generalizability of the results. Third, there was a lack of sufficient event occurrence in this study, which to some extent affects the accuracy and reliability of statistics. Large scale research and extended follow-up time are needed to observe whether combined AT is superior to NAT. Fourth, further research is needed to determine whether there is a difference in PD-L1 expression levels between the two groups and whether the expression level of PD-L1 is related to efficacy and adverse reactions. Fifth, the radiation dose pattern in the study is not uniform, making it difficult to evaluate which radiation dose gradient is best combined with systemic therapy.Sixth, although the time interval between RT and ICIs/AT in this study was within 3 months, there is a lack of standardized treatment sequence for combination therapy. Therefore, the research results need to be interpreted with caution, and there is an urgent need for large-scale studies, especially prospective studies, to evaluate the efficacy and safety of AT + RT + PD-1 inhibitors in patients with NSCLC BM, especially in recruiting symptomatic/progressive BM patients.

Conclusion

Our study provides a new clinical treatment strategy. The treatment mode of AT combined with RT and PD-1 inhibitors can significantly improve iLPFS and iDPFS in patients with driver gene-negative NSCLC BM. This approach is beneficial for improving OS, with adverse reactions remaining within an acceptable range. However, further exploration is needed on the optimal combination mode of combination therapy, timing and dose segmentation of intracranial radiotherapy, selection of beneficiaries, and potential predictive biomarkers.
