Influence of different photosensitizers on the internal bleaching response of aPDT-induced coronal discoloration: an in vitro study
Authors: Merve Işık Aydın, Merve Nur Yılmaz, Selin Bıçaklıoğlu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Purpose this study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of the conventional walking bleaching (CWB) method in treating coronal discoloration caused by different photosensitizers used in antimicrobial photodynamic therapy (aPDT). Methods sixty extracted maxillary central incisors were randomly allocated into four groups (n = 15): methylene blue (MB), toluidine blue (TB), curcumin (CUR), and a negative control (NC). In the NC group, neither photosensitizer application nor light activation was performed. Following root canal treatment and aPDT procedures, intracoronal bleaching was performed using the conventional walking bleaching (CWB) technique with 35% hydrogen peroxide. Color measurements were obtained at baseline, one month after aPDT, and on days 4, 8, and 12 of bleaching. Color differences were calculated using the CIEDE2000 (ΔE00) formula. Data were analyzed using Robust Mixed ANOVA with Holm-adjusted multiple comparisons (α = 0.05). Results no significant main effect of photosensitizer type was observed on the overall ΔE00 values (p = 0.097), whereas time had a significant effect on bleaching response (p < 0.001). A significant photosensitizer × time interaction was also identified (p = 0.003). Although no differences were observed among the groups at ΔE002, the TB group exhibited significantly lower ΔE00 values than the MB, CUR, and NC groups during the later evaluation periods (ΔE003 and ΔE004), indicating a reduced bleaching response over time. Conclusion different photosensitizers produced comparable levels of overall bleaching response following aPDT-induced coronal discoloration. However, the temporal bleaching pattern differed among the photosensitizers, with TB-induced discoloration demonstrating a lower bleaching response during the later stages of treatment. These findings suggest that the bleaching behavior of aPDT-related discoloration may vary according to the photosensitizer used.

Introduction

Bacteria are recognized as the principal etiological factor of pulpal and periapical lesions, as they produce toxins that cause irritation in the pulp and periapical tissues [1]. Root canal therapy (RCT) aims to establish conditions favorable for apical healing by eliminating microbial infection from the root canal system [2]. However, conventional chemomechanical preparation may not completely eradicate microorganisms, particularly those penetrating the dentinal tubules, because of the complex anatomy of the root canal system, which may contribute to post-treatment reinfection [3]. To overcome these limitations, several studies have demonstrated that the adjunctive use of antimicrobial photodynamic therapy (aPDT) in addition to conventional chemomechanical preparation is effective in reducing the intracanal bacterial load [4].

Photosensitizers (PS) used in aPDT can selectively accumulate in target cells or tissues and produce a therapeutic effect upon activation by low-energy light at an appropriate wavelength. The antimicrobial action of aPDT is based on the generation of reactive oxygen species following light activation of the PS [5]. In endodontics, various PSs with different chemical structures have been used to enhance intracanal disinfection. Among these, methylene blue (MB) and toluidine blue (TB), which belong to the phenothiazine class, are the most commonly used agents [4, 6]. In recent years, curcumin (CUR), a natural compound, has emerged as an alternative to synthetic PSs in dental aPDT applications [7, 8]. However, despite its potential to improve root canal disinfection, there is a significant disadvantage in that the PS used in aPDT can cause undesirable tooth discoloration [9].

Tooth discoloration constitutes a significant problem for patients and may negatively impact satisfaction after treatment. Intracoronal discolorations that give rise to aesthetic concerns can, under appropriate conditions, be corrected relatively simply and effectively in teeth treated with RCT using internal bleaching methods [10]. In recent years, the most widely used internal bleaching technique has been the conventional walking bleaching (CWB) method, performed with 30-35% hydrogen peroxide (HP) [11]. This method is a frequently preferred, proven, conservative, and cost-effective treatment option to improve the color of teeth exhibiting intracoronal discoloration after RCT [12].

Color determination in dentistry can be performed using visual assessment, shade guides, and device-assisted methods. Among these, spectrophotometers are widely used because of their high standardization and repeatability [13]. Although the CIELAB color difference formula (ΔE*ab) has long been used for color analysis, current literature recommends the use of the CIEDE2000 (ΔE00) formula because it provides results that are more consistent with human visual perception [14, 15]. In dentistry, the 50:50 perceptibility and acceptability thresholds (AT and PT) for the CIEDE2000 system are reported to be ΔE00 = 0.8 and ΔE00 = 1.8, respectively [15]. Therefore, the use of spectrophotometric analysis and the CIEDE2000 system is important for the objective and reliable evaluation of tooth discoloration.

To the best of our knowledge, no previous study has evaluated the effectiveness of the CWB method on intracoronal discolorations caused by different PSs used during aPDT. Therefore, in the present study, we comparatively investigated the bleaching efficacy of CWB on discoloration induced by different PSs. The null hypothesis was that the type of PS used would not influence the bleaching effectiveness of the CWB method.

Materials and methods

This in vitro study design was approved by the Non-Interventional Research Ethics Committee of Bolu Abant İzzet Baysal University (date: August 2025; no: 2025/368).

Determination of sample size

Sample size analysis was conducted using the G*Power v3.1.2 (Heinrich Heine University, Düsseldorf, Germany) software, based on the ΔE color change values reported by Figueiredo et al. [9], who used a similar methodology. The ΔE00 color difference value was considered the primary outcome variable. Using the effect size calculated from the previous study (Cohen’s f = 0.30), a 5% significance level (α = 0.05), and 80% statistical power (1 - β = 0.80), the analysis determined that at least 15 samples would be required for each of the PS groups. An additional negative control (NC) group containing 15 specimens was included to provide a reference for color changes occurring in the absence of PS application and light activation.

Selection of samples

Sixty extracted maxillary central incisors with similar crown dimensions were selected for this study. The cervico-occlusal and mesiodistal dimensions of the crowns were measured using a digital caliper with a precision of 0.01 mm, and teeth showing more than 10% deviation from the mean values were excluded. Crown integrity, complete root development, and the presence of a single canal were confirmed radiographically. After the removal of the soft and hard tissue remnants, the samples were disinfected in 0.5% chloramine-T solution (Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany) for 48 h.

Root canal preparation

Access cavities were prepared on the palatal surfaces under water cooling using a high-speed handpiece and a diamond round bur. After locating the canal orifices, a #10 K-file was inserted to the apex, and the working length was established 1 mm short of the apical foramen. Root canal preparation was completed using ProTaper Next X1, X2, and X3 NiTi rotary instruments (Dentsply Maillefer, Ballaigues, Switzerland) operated with an X-Smart Plus endodontic motor (Dentsply Maillefer, Ballaigues, Switzerland) according to the manufacturer’s recommended torque and speed settings. Following each instrument change, the canals were irrigated with 2 mL of 2.5% sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl; Wizard, Rehber Kimya, Istanbul, Turkey). Final irrigation was performed with 2 mL of 17% ethylenediamine tetraacetic acid (EDTA; Promida, Eskişehir, Turkey), followed by saline solution. In all irrigation procedures, a 30-gauge side-vented IrriFlex ® needle (Produits Dentaires SA, Vevey, Switzerland) was used, and the canals were dried with paper points.

Fabrication of the composite reference frame

To ensure standardization during all measurements, a fixed reference frame was created on the buccal surfaces of the teeth using a flowable composite resin. For this purpose, an impression of the contact surface of the spectrophotometer tip was obtained using a silicone-based impression material, and a model was subsequently fabricated by pouring chemically cured acrylic resin into the impression. The resulting model was positioned at the center of the buccal surface of each tooth and stabilized with a flowable composite resin, thereby creating a standardized measurement area that allowed the spectrophotometer tip to be positioned at the same location during each measurement [13].

Photodynamic therapy procedure

After root canal preparation and composite reference frame completion, the samples were numbered from 1 to 60. Randomization was performed using a computer-generated randomization sequence (www.random.org), and the samples were randomly allocated into four groups (n = 15): MB, TB, CUR, and NC.

NC

No PS was applied and no light activation was performed. The specimens underwent root canal obturation and internal bleaching procedures according to the same protocol used in the experimental groups.

MB

A total of 1 mL (0.1 mg/mL, 313 µM) MB (Merck KGaA) was applied to the root canal of each specimen. Prior to activation, MB (Merck KGaA) was left in the root canal for 5 min [16]. For activation, the LED Fotosan 630 device (CMS Dental A/S, Copenhagen, Denmark) and the Endo tip (1 mm² diameter; CMS Dental A/S) were used. The Endo tip (CMS Dental A/S) was placed in the canal to a length 1 mm short of the working length. Then, activation was performed for 60 s by applying light at a wavelength of 620-640 nm (peak: 630 nm) and intensity of 2-4 mW/cm². After each use, the Endo tip (CMS Dental A/S) was disinfected with ethanol. The MB remaining in the canal was removed by irrigation with 5 mL of 2.5% NaOCl solution.

CUR

CUR (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) was prepared from a 10.2% stock solution. To obtain the working solution, 1 mL of the stock solution was diluted with 9 mL of a solvent containing 0.05% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), resulting in a final curcumin concentration of 1.02%. DMSO was used to enhance the solubility of curcumin because of its limited aqueous solubility. The selected concentration was based on previously published studies demonstrating the antimicrobial efficacy of CUR-mediated aPDT [17]. A volume of 0.1 mL of the prepared working solution was introduced into the root canal, and the specimens were incubated in the dark for 5 min prior to light activation. After application, the remaining CUR was removed by irrigating with 5 mL of 2.5% NaOCl solution.

TB

1 mL (0.1 mg/mL, 327 µM) TB (Merck KGaA) was applied to the root canal and allowed to sit for 5 min. The activation process was performed in the same manner as in the MB group. After application, the remaining TB was removed by irrigating with 5 mL of 2.5% NaOCl solution.

After completion of the aPDT procedure, a gutta-percha cone compatible with the ProTaper Next X3 file (Dentsply Maillefer) was placed at the working length, and the tug-back was checked. After the canals were dried with paper points, a resin-based root canal sealer was applied, and obturation was performed using the single-cone technique. Color measurements were performed and recorded before the procedure and 1 month after aPDT application.

Bleaching procedure

The excess gutta-percha at the canal orifice was removed with a steel round bur so as to remain 1 mm below the enamel-cementum junction, and a 2 mm thick layer of glass ionomer cement was applied on top. The pulp chamber of all samples was filled, according to the manufacturer’s instructions, with 35% HP gel Opalescence ® Endo (Ultradent Products Inc., South Jordan, UT, USA) using a mini black syringe. A teflon tape and temporary filling material were applied on top, and the access cavities were sealed. The samples to which the bleaching agent was applied were stored in distilled water at 37 °C for 4 days. At the end of the period, the temporary filling material was removed, the bleaching agent was cleaned by rinsing with distilled water, and fresh bleaching agent was placed in the pulp chamber. This procedure was repeated on days 4, 8, and 12 for all samples [18].

Tooth color assessment

To ensure standardization, all color measurements were performed under standardized D65 lighting conditions using the same spectrophotometer by a single restorative dentist (M.N.Y.). To minimize measurement bias, all specimens were coded, and the examiner was blinded to group allocation throughout the study.

CIE systems provide standardization in color measurements. In this study, color measurements were performed using the Vita Easyshade Advance spectrophotometer (VITA Zahnfabrik, Bad Sackingen, Germany). To ensure the repeatability of the measurements, all assessments were made from the same point on a fixed composite frame [18]. Before each measurement, the device was calibrated, and the measurements were repeated three times under white light, recording the average L*, a*, and b* values. These parameters were measured at baseline and at all subsequent time points to calculate color change. Statistical analysis

The analysis of the data was conducted using R version 4.5 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). The Shapiro-Wilk test was employed to evaluate the normal distribution. For ΔE00 values that did not exhibit normal distribution across group, time, and group x time interaction, a Robust Mixed ANOVA was utilized. Multiple comparisons were performed with the Holm correction. Descriptive statistics for quantitative variables are presented as trimmed mean ± standard error (Table 1). The significance threshold was established at p < 0.05.

Results

The Robust Mixed ANOVA revealed no significant main effect of PS group on the overall ΔE00 values (p = 0.097). The overall trimmed mean ΔE values were 4.51 for the MB group, 3.01 for the TB group, 4.03 for the CUR group, and 3.72 for the NC group (Table 1).

A significant main effect of time was observed (p < 0.001). The overall trimmed mean ΔE values increased from 1.72 at ΔE001 (T0-T1) to 3.71 at ΔE002 (T1-T2), reaching 4.95 at ΔE003 (T1-T3) and 4.93 at ΔE004 (T1-T4). Pairwise comparisons demonstrated no significant difference between ΔE003 and ΔE004, whereas all other time-point comparisons were statistically significant (Table 1).

A significant group × time interaction was identified (p = 0.003), indicating that the bleaching response over time differed according to the PS used. At ΔE001 (T0-T1), the MB group exhibited significantly higher ΔE values than the TB, CUR, and negative control groups, whereas no significant differences were observed among the latter three groups. At ΔE002 (T1-T2), no significant differences were detected among the groups. At both ΔE003 (T1-T3) and ΔE004 (T1-T4), the TB group demonstrated significantly lower ΔE values than the MB, CUR, and NC groups. No significant differences were observed among the MB, CUR, and NC groups at ΔE003, whereas at ΔE004 the CUR and NC groups showed comparable ΔE values (Table 1; Fig. 3).

Discussion

It is known that some materials used during RCT can cause discoloration in dental hard tissues. When this occurs particularly in anterior teeth, it may lead to aesthetic problems and create negative psychosocial effects on patients [20]. It has also been reported that the PS agents used during aPDT, which is applied to enhance the disinfection of the root canal system in the course of RCT, may cause pigmentation in dental hard tissues, resulting in intracoronal discoloration after treatment [21]. Although various methods for sealing dentinal tubules and different approaches for removing PS agents have been tried to prevent this discoloration, it has been reported that none of these methods can completely eliminate the discoloration [9, 13, 22]. Previous studies have primarily focused on discoloration caused by PSs, whereas data regarding the reversal of these discolorations using bleaching techniques remain limited. Therefore, the present study comparatively evaluated both the severity of discoloration induced by different PSs (MB, TB, and CUR) and the bleaching response achieved using the CWB technique with 35% HP. Although no significant overall effect of PS type on bleaching response was observed, the significant group × time interaction demonstrated that the bleaching progression differed according to the PS used. Therefore, the null hypothesis was partially rejected.

It has been reported that phenothiazine-derived PSs such as MB and TB, which are commonly used during aPDT procedures, can cause significant discoloration in dental hard tissues [21]. In a study evaluating the discoloration caused by different PS agents, MB and TB were also reported to cause the highest level of staining [23]. In contrast, it was stated that indocyanine green did not lead to clinically significant discoloration [24] and aPDT applications using CUR may be safe in terms of color stability [25]. However, since these studies examined the effects of PS agents on resin-based composite discs, it suggests that they may not fully reflect the discoloration occurring in natural tooth tissue. In contrast, the present in vitro study demonstrated no significant differences in the degree of intracoronal discoloration among the MB, TB, and CUR groups. These discrepancies may be attributed to differences in the materials used, study designs, the physicochemical properties of the PSs, dentinal penetration, variability in evaluation methods, and differences in the irrigation protocols used for PS removal.

According to the 50:50% perceptibility and acceptability thresholds proposed by Paravina et al. [26] for the CIEDE2000 color system, color differences below ΔE00 = 0.8 are generally considered imperceptible, whereas values exceeding ΔE00 = 1.8 are regarded as clinically unacceptable. Although no statistically significant differences were observed among the PSs regarding the initial degree of discoloration, evaluation according to the CIEDE2000 perceptibility and acceptability thresholds provides additional clinical context. All three PSs produced color changes exceeding the perceptibility threshold (ΔE00 = 0.8). While the mean discoloration values for the MB and CUR groups remained below the acceptability threshold (ΔE00 = 1.8), the TB group showed a mean value slightly above this threshold. These findings suggest that, although different PSs produced comparable initial discoloration, the reversibility of that discoloration following internal bleaching may differ. Therefore, the susceptibility of PS-induced discoloration to bleaching should be considered when selecting PSs for use in esthetically sensitive teeth. In the present study, discoloration induced by TB exhibited a less favorable bleaching response than that induced by MB and CUR during the later stages of bleaching.

Various irrigation protocols have been investigated to improve the removal of PSs following aPDT [21]. Ramalho et al. [27] reported that passive ultrasonic irrigation methods using saline, 2.5% NaOCl, and 17% EDTA failed to completely prevent color change of root dentin during the removal of 0.01% MB. Similarly, Figueiredo et al. [9] demonstrated that, although the Endo-PTC cream used in combination with 2.5% NaOCl reduced the staining potential of MB and TB at 0.01% concentration, it did not eliminate it entirely. Collectively, these findings indicate that intracoronal discoloration following aPDT cannot be completely prevented, particularly in anterior teeth where esthetic demands are high. Accordingly, the present study evaluated the effectiveness of the CWB technique using 35% HP for the management of intracoronal discoloration induced by different PSs under standardized experimental conditions.

To the best of our knowledge, no previous study has evaluated the effectiveness of internal bleaching for the management of aPDT-induced coronal discoloration. One of the principal findings of the present study was the significant group × time interaction observed during the bleaching period. Although no significant overall effect of PS type on bleaching response was detected, the temporal bleaching pattern differed among the groups. No significant differences were observed during the early stage of bleaching (ΔE002); however, at the later evaluation periods (ΔE003 and ΔE004), the TB group exhibited significantly lower ΔE00 values than the MB, CUR, and NC groups. These findings indicate that TB-induced discoloration showed a less favorable response to the CWB protocol over time. The lower bleaching response observed in the TB group may be associated with the physicochemical characteristics of TB and its interaction with dental hard tissues. Previous studies have shown that phenothiazine-derived PSs, such as MB and TB, can induce clinically relevant discoloration following aPDT [9]. Differences in molecular structure, diffusion behavior, and binding affinity to dentinal components may influence the susceptibility of discoloration to oxidation by HP-based bleaching agents. However, the present study did not include chemical, microscopic, or spectroscopic analyses to investigate these mechanisms. Therefore, these explanations remain speculative and should be interpreted with caution. Further studies incorporating structural and chemical analyses are needed to clarify the mechanisms underlying the reduced bleaching response observed in the TB group.

Several internal bleaching techniques and bleaching agents have been proposed for the management of discoloration in endodontically treated teeth. Among these, sodium perborate, HP, carbamide peroxide, and combinations of these materials have been widely investigated. Previous studies have reported that the effectiveness of internal bleaching may vary depending on the bleaching agent, concentration, application protocol, and the nature of the discoloration [28–30]. In the present study, the CWB technique using 35% HP was selected because it represents one of the most commonly used and well-documented approaches for the treatment of intracoronal discoloration. Internal bleaching methods based on HP have been shown to be effective in eliminating pigment-related intracoronal discolorations that penetrate into the dentin [31]. Amer [28] highlighted that HP-based CWB is a well-established and conservative treatment approach for the management of discoloration in endodontically treated teeth. Similarly, high-concentration HP has been reported to exert its bleaching effect by oxidizing pigment molecules within dentin into smaller and less chromatic compounds [29, 30, 32]. Taken together, these findings support the use of 35% HP as an effective and conservative internal bleaching agent, while also suggesting that the bleaching outcome may vary according to the characteristics of the discoloration being treated.

While the initial discolorations were statistically similar, the clinical relevance of these findings emerges when considering the subsequent management of such staining. From a clinical perspective, if different PSs provide comparable antimicrobial efficacy, their susceptibility to reversal by internal bleaching may represent an additional consideration during material selection. In the present study, although no significant overall effect of PS type on bleaching response was observed, TB-induced discoloration exhibited a less favorable bleaching response during the later stages of the bleaching period. Therefore, in esthetically demanding clinical situations, the bleaching behavior of PS-induced discoloration may also be considered when selecting an appropriate photosensitizer.

However, this study has several limitations. Firstly, the study was conducted under in vitro conditions, and the biological and functional conditions of the oral environment could not be fully simulated. Although the sample size was determined based on an a priori power analysis, the relatively small number of specimens in each group may have limited the detection of subtle differences and increased the risk of Type II error. In addition, only maxillary central incisors, three different PSs, a single bleaching agent (35% HP gel), and a single internal bleaching protocol (CWB) were evaluated. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted with caution and cannot be directly generalized to other tooth types, bleaching agents, or alternative bleaching techniques. Furthermore, color changes were evaluated over a relatively short follow-up period, and post-bleaching color stability was not assessed in the long term. Future in vitro and clinical studies should investigate different PSs, bleaching agents, and longer follow-up periods, while also evaluating the long-term behavior of aPDT-induced discoloration and its response to various internal bleaching protocols.

Conclusion

Within the limitations of this study, different PSs produced comparable overall bleaching responses following aPDT-induced coronal discoloration. However, the temporal bleaching pattern differed among the groups, with TB-induced discoloration exhibiting a less favorable bleaching response during the later stages of treatment. These findings suggest that the bleaching behavior of aPDT-related discoloration may vary according to the PS used.
