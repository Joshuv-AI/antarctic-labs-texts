In vivo imaging analyses to explore the integration of multimodal aversive sensory information in the central amygdala
Authors: Xinliang Zhou, Yuta Sakuragi, Chiaki Katagiri, Hiroshi Nomura, Masabumi Minami
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe negative emotions elicited by diverse aversive sensory stimuli induce avoidance behaviors and adaptive autonomic/hormonal responses, which are crucial for biological defense and homeostasis. The central amygdala (CeA), which receives a range of sensory information, plays important roles in stress and emotional responses. However, it remains unclear whether diverse sensory information from different sensory systems, such as the pain, olfactory, and gustatory systems, is received by the same group of CeA neurons and then integrated to generate negative emotions, or whether different neural pathways are used to generate negative emotions without converging on a specific group of CeA neurons. To clarify this issue, we conducted in vivo Ca2+ imaging to measure CeA neuron activity in response to aversive pain, olfactory, and gustatory stimuli. The results revealed that 144 of the 263 CeA neurons identified were activated by one or more types of aversive sensory stimuli: 70.8% of the activated neurons responded to only one stimulus, 24.3% to any two stimuli, and only 4.9% to all three, suggesting that the specificity of CeA neurons to aversive stimuli is relatively high, while some neurons respond multiple aversive stimuli. Introduction

The negative emotions elicited by various aversive sensory stimuli such as pain and predator odors induce avoidance behaviors and adaptive autonomic/hormonal responses, which are crucial for biological defense and homeostasis. The central amygdala (CeA), which receives a diverse range of sensory information, plays important roles in stress and emotional responses1–6. Aversive pain, olfactory, gustatory, and visual stimuli induce c-fos mRNA expression and/or c-Fos protein production in the CeA, indicating increased neuronal activity in response to these stimuli1,4–6. Bilateral excitotoxic lesions of the CeA suppressed both intraplantar formalin-induced and intraperitoneal acetic acid-induced conditioned place aversion, suggesting the involvement of the CeA in the negative affective component of pain2. Studies using optogenetics have shown that the neural pathway from the parabrachial nucleus to the CeA plays an important role in pain-induced negative emotions3. Neural circuits within the CeA involved in eliciting fear responses to predator odors have also been identified4. A more recent study revealed that short-term acute bright light exposure exerts an anxiogenic effect in mice through the activation of the intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cell–CeA pathway6. However, it remains unclear whether diverse sensory information from different sensory systems, such as the pain, olfactory, gustatory, and visual systems, is received by the same group of CeA neurons and then integrated to generate negative emotions or whether they use different neural pathways to generate negative emotions without converging on a specific group of neurons. As a first step toward clarifying this issue, we utilized an in vivo Ca2+ imaging technique7–9 for real-time measurement of the activities of CeA neurons in response to aversive sensory stimuli via the pain, olfactory, gustatory, and visual systems.

Results

Responses of CeA neurons to aversive sensory stimuli

To quantify the responses of CeA neurons to aversive pain, olfactory, gustatory, and visual stimuli, neuronal activity in the CeA of head-fixed mice was recorded via in vivo Ca2+ imaging. We injected an adeno-associated virus (AAV) into the CeA to deliver the Ca2+-sensitive fluorescent protein GCaMP6m, which is controlled by the human synapsin 1 promoter, and implanted a gradient-index (GRIN) lens above the injection site to observe fluorescence signals (Fig. 1A,B). After allowing at least 5 weeks for recovery from surgery and sufficient expression of GCaMP6m, the mice were head-fixed for in vivo Ca2+ imaging and exposed to aversive stimuli of different modalities: tail pinch (pain stimulus), 2-methylthiazoline (2MT; fear-inducing olfactory stimulus4), quinine solution (bitter taste stimulus), and bright light (anxiety-inducing visual stimulus6). In each trial, the tail pinch was applied first, followed by 2MT, quinine solution, and bright light in a random order (Fig. 1C). These four stimuli were applied with an interstimulus interval of 60 ± 30 s, and ten trials were conducted with an intertrial interval of 1–2 min. The fluorescence intensity of GCaMP6m in the CeA was recorded 10 s before and after the start of each sensory stimulation. The raw fluorescence image data were analyzed with CaImAn10, and a total of 174 neurons from 8 mice were identified. To make a heat map (Supplementary Fig. S1A−D), the z-score for each identified neuron was calculated from 2 s before to 8 s after each sensory stimulus, using the average fluorescence intensity in the 2-s period immediately before the stimulus as a baseline and averaged over 10 trials. To determine whether each neuron was activated by each stimulus, we used a permutation test to compare the mean ΔF values during the periods 0–2 s before (pre) and 1–3 s after (post) the start of stimulus application. The original data set of pre and post mean ΔF values were obtained from the 10 trials, and the pre and post values were systematically shuffled to obtain approximately 1,000 shuffled data sets. The null distribution was obtained by calculating the mean of differences between pre and post values of each shuffled data set and compared with the mean of differences between pre and post values of the original data set to determine whether the neuron was significantly (P < 0.05) activated (Supplementary Fig. S1E–H).

A GRIN lens was implanted above the injection site, and fluorescence images were acquired through the GRIN lens. (B) Representative image of GCaMP6m expression and GRIN lens placement in the CeA. (C) Schedule for Ca2+ imaging and aversive sensory stimulation.

CeA neurons activated by multiple sensory stimuli

Since 5.2% of neurons were activated by visual stimulation, a much smaller number compared to those of neurons activated by the other three types of aversive sensory stimuli (Supplementary Fig. S1E–H), it was decided that subsequent experiments would be conducted and analyzed using imaging data of pain, olfactory, and gustatory stimuli. In addition, imaging data of 89 neurons from the second cohort of mice (n = 6) were added to the analysis to further improve the accuracy of the experiment. In total, imaging data of 263 neurons from 14 mice were used for analysis, yielding the heat map shown in Fig. 2A. Figure 2B shows that 23.2%, 29.3%, and 20.9% of CeA neurons were activated by aversive pain, olfactory, and gustatory stimuli, respectively. In total, 54.8% of CeA neurons were activated by one or more sensory stimuli, and 45.2% were not activated (i.e., inhibited or unresponsive) by any sensory stimulus. Among the activated neurons (n = 144), 70.8% (n = 102) were activated by only one type of sensory stimulus, of which 27, 48, and 27 neurons were selectively activated by pain (tail pinch), olfactory (2MT), and gustatory (quinine) stimuli, respectively, 24.3% (n = 35) were activated by two of the three sensory stimuli, and 4.9% (n = 7) were activated by all three stimuli (Fig. 2C,D). Panels a–g of Fig. 2E show the time courses of the mean z-score values before and after each sensory stimulus in CeA neuron groups a–g, which were activated by the different combinations of sensory stimulation as shown in Fig. 2C.

Discussion

In this study, we conducted in vivo Ca2+ imaging to determine whether the sensory signals of different modalities converge onto the same group of cells in the CeA. Among the 263 CeA neurons identified, 144 (54.8%) were activated by one or more types of aversive sensory stimuli. Among the activated neurons (n = 144), 70.8% (n = 102) were activated by only one type of sensory stimulus, of which 27, 48, and 27 neurons were selectively activated by pain (tail pinch), olfactory (2MT), and gustatory (quinine) stimuli, respectively, 24.3% (n = 35) were activated by two of the three sensory stimuli, and 4.9% (n = 7) were activated by all three stimuli.

The types of CeA neurons involved in the induction of negative emotions by aversive sensory stimuli have been reported. With respect to pain stimulation, Wilson et al. reported increased excitability of protein kinase C-delta (PKCδ)-expressing CeA neurons in a mouse model of neuropathic pain and demonstrated that chemogenetic inhibition of PKCδ-expressing CeA neurons alleviated nerve injury-induced hyperalgesia11. In another study, Chen et al. demonstrated that chemogenetic inhibition of PKCδ-expressing CeA neurons reduced mechanical hyperalgesia in mice with formalin-induced pain12. Furthermore, Lin et al. demonstrated that in a mouse model of chronic muscle pain, the neuronal excitability of somatostatin (SOM)-expressing CeA neurons was enhanced, and that chemogenetic inhibition of SOM-expressing CeA neurons alleviated chronic pain and anxiety/depression-like behaviors13. These findings suggest that both PKCδ-expressing and SOM-expressing CeA neurons are involved in the induction of negative emotions by pain stimuli. With respect to olfactory stimulation, Isosaka et al. performed in vivo fiber photometry of Ca2+ signals to demonstrate that exposure to 2MT can decrease the neuronal activity of serotonin 2 A receptor-expressing CeA neurons, the majority of which also express SOM4. They also showed that chemogenetic activation and inhibition of these neurons can downregulate and upregulate 2MT-induced freezing behaviors, respectively. These findings suggest that SOM-expressing CeA neurons regulate 2MT-induced fear responses. Concerning gustatory stimulation, in a recent report by Hamada et al., in vivo Ca2+ imaging revealed that bitter taste (quinine) increased the neuronal activity of a portion of both PKCδ- and SOM-expressing CeA neurons, suggesting the involvement of these types of CeA neurons in the processing of aversive gustatory information14. As noted above, studies examining the types of CeA neurons activated by aversive sensory stimulation are emerging, but very few studies have applied aversive sensory stimulation via multiple modalities. In this study, we utilized a synapsin promoter to induce the expression of GCaMP6m in all types of CeA neurons. Further studies using Cre mouse lines, which enable the expression of GCaMP6m in a neuron-type-selective manner, are needed to elucidate the neural mechanisms underlying the processing of aversive sensory stimulation of multiple modalities in the CeA.

It is quite difficult to accurately compare the intensities (saliency/aversiveness) across stimuli of different modalities. Therefore, we used pain (tail pinch), olfactory (2MT), and gustatory (quinine) stimuli, by which approximately similar proportions (20–30%) of CeA neurons are activated, to examine the percentages of CeA neurons that are activated by multiple aversive sensory stimuli. Since only 5.2% of CeA neurons were activated by the light stimulation, we did not include the imaging data of light stimulation session in the subsequent analysis. The results of this study demonstrate that when the percentage of neurons responding to each aversive stimulus is between 20 and 30%, 70.8% of the activated neurons responded to only one stimulus, 24.3% to any two stimuli, and only 4.9% to all three, suggesting that the specificity of CeA neuron responses to aversive stimuli is relatively high, while some neurons respond multiple aversive stimuli. Aversive sensory information from different sensory pathways may be partially integrated in the CeA, and such integrated information produces appropriate emotional responses. To elucidate the details of the specificity of CeA neuron responses to sensory stimuli, further studies are needed to examine the responses of CeA neurons to not only aversive but also rewarding and emotionally neutral stimuli.

Materials and methods

Animals

Male C57B6/J mice (7 weeks old) were purchased from Japan SLC Inc. (Hamamatsu, Japan). The animals were group-housed at a constant ambient temperature (23 ± 1 °C) under a 12-h light/dark cycle (lights on at 07:00), with food and water available ad libitum. Animal experiments were performed with the approval of the Animal Experiment Ethics Committee at the Hokkaido University (approval number: 22–0056) and according to the Hokkaido University Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. The experimental protocols were conducted in accordance with the Fundamental Guidelines for Proper Conduct of Animal Experiments and Related Activities in Academic Research Institutions (Ministry of Education, Culture, Sports, Science and Technology, Notice No. 71 of 2006), the Standards for Breeding and Housing of and Pain Alleviation for Experimental Animals (Ministry of the Environment, Notice No. 88 of 2006), and the Guidelines on the Method of Animal Disposal (Prime Minister’s Office, Notice No. 40 of 1995). Animal use follows the recommendations of the ARRIVE guidelines.

Virus injection and GRIN lens implantation

Before surgery, 125 µL of carprofen (1 mg/mL), 100 µL of dexamethasone (50 µg/mL), and 100 µL of gentamicin (1 mg/mL) were administered intraperitoneally. The mice were then anesthetized with 1–2% isoflurane and fixed on a stereotaxic frame (SR-6 M-HT; Narishige, Tokyo, Japan). To express GCaMP6m, AAVdj-hsyn-GCaMP6m (1.35 × 1013 virus molecules/mL, 0.5 µL; UNC Vector Core, Chapel Hill, NC, USA) was injected into the CeA (anteroposterior, −1.25 mm; mediolateral, + 2.85 mm; dorsoventral, −4.8 mm)15 using a 30-G infusion cannula. A GRIN lens (cylindrical shape, 0.6 mm in diameter, 7.3 mm in length; Inscopix, Palo Alto, CA, USA) was subsequently implanted 250 μm dorsal to the injection site using a one-axis motorized stereotaxic micromanipulator (MDS-1; Narishige), with an implantation rate of 10 μm/s for the first 3.05 mm and 1 μm/s for the remaining 1.5 mm. To anchor the lens, three screws were inserted into the skull, and the lens was fixed to the skull via adhesive self-curing resin cement (Super-Bond; Sun Medical, Moriyama, Japan). A head-fixing frame (CF-10; Narishige) was fixed horizontally via acrylic resin (Unifast; GC Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). To protect the lens and avoid scattering and reflection of the excitation light, a plastic tube was placed around the lens and fixed with black acrylic resin (Ortho-Jet; Lang Dental, Wheeling, IL, USA). During the 7-day post-surgery period, gentamicin (1 mg/mL) was intraperitoneally administered at a volume of 100 µL once a day, and carprofen (50 µg/mL) and dexamethasone (1.5 µg/mL) were added to the drinking water. After surgery, the mice were housed individually and allowed to recover for > 5 weeks before Ca2+ imaging.

In vivo calcium imaging and aversive sensory stimuli

Ca2+ imaging experiments were performed in a sound-attenuated room. Before the experiments, the animals were habituated to the experimental apparatus over 2 days. Briefly, the mice were fitted for > 10 min with a head-fixing unit, a mask for odor presentation, and a liquid dispenser (OPR-HFAJ; O’Hara & Co., Tokyo, Japan) without stimulation. On the day of the experiment, Ca2+ imaging began after the mice had been fitted with the experimental apparatus for > 10 min. Recording was conducted via an upright one-photon macro zoom fluorescence microscope (MVX10; Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). GCaMP6m was excited via a 470-nm light-emitting diode (LED) (M470L5; Thorlabs, Newton, NJ, USA), and fluorescence signals were detected via a complementary metal oxide semiconductor (CMOS) camera (ORCA-Fusion BT; Hamamatsu Photonics K.K., Hamamatsu, Japan). During the recordings, the mice were exposed to aversive stimuli of four different modalities. For pain stimulation, the tails of the mice were pinched with forceps for approximately 2 s. For olfactory stimulation, an odor presentation device (SD-2020; O’Hara & Co.) was used to provide 2MT (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) over 3 s. Specifically, airflow was constantly given to the mouse nasal region at a rate of 2 L/min, and during olfactory stimulation (3 s each), a mixture of 1.4 L/min airflow and 0.6 L/min airflow passed through a glass bottle containing 2MT, a volatile liquid. For gustatory stimulation, a solution delivery device (OPR-7300; O’Hara & Co.) was used to provide 12 µL of 10 mM quinine hydrochloride solution (Nacalai Tesque, Kyoto, Japan). For visual stimulation, light was provided for 3 s via a white LED (5 V, 1 KΩ). Supplemental Movies S1-4 show that the presentation of these sensory stimuli elicited aversive behaviors. In each trial, tail pinch was initially performed, followed by stimulation with 2MT, quinine solution, and bright light in a random order (Fig. 1C). These four stimuli were applied with an interstimulus interval of 60 ± 30 s, and ten trials were conducted with an intertrial interval of 1–2 min. The fluorescence intensity of GCaMP6m in the CeA was recorded for 20 s, beginning 10 s prior to the onset of stimulation, at a rate of 15 Hz.

Histological analysis

After the imaging experiments, the mice were deeply anesthetized via the intraperitoneal administration of 0.07 mL pentobarbital (50 mg/mL) and perfused with phosphate-buffered saline followed by 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in 0.1 M phosphate buffer. Brains were removed and postfixed in 4% PFA overnight at 4 °C. Cryoprotection was performed in 30% sucrose solution at 4 °C for 48–72 h before the samples were frozen in tissue-embedding compound (FSC 22 Clear; Leica Biosystems, Richmond, IL, USA). Coronal sections (40 μm thick) were prepared via a cryostat (CM3050S; Leica Biosystems GmbH, Wetzlar, Germany). The localization of the implanted GRIN lens and GCaMP6m expression were checked via a fluorescence microscope (4× magnification; BZ-X700, Keyence, Osaka, Japan) (Supplementary Fig. S2). Data from mice with a misplaced GRIN lens or incorrectly localized GCaMP6m expression were excluded from the subsequent analyses.

Data analysis

The imaging data were analyzed via CaImAn, an open-source tool for scalable calcium imaging data analysis. The raw image data were motion corrected, and fluorescence signals were extracted via a CNMF-E algorithm. The automatically extracted candidate neurons were manually inspected, and those with typical shapes and signal spikes were confirmed as neurons. To determine whether each neuron was activated by each stimulus, we used a permutation test to compare the mean ΔF values during the periods 0–2 s before (pre) and 1–3 s after (post) the start of stimulus application. ΔF (t) was calculated as F(t) − F₀(t), where the running baseline F₀(t) was estimated from an automatically determined low percentile within a 33.3-s sliding window. The original data set of pre and post mean ΔF values were obtained from the 10 trials, and the pre and post values were systematically shuffled to obtain approximately 1,000 shuffled data sets. The null distribution was obtained by calculating the mean of differences between pre and post values of each shuffled data set and compared with the mean of differences between pre and post values of the original data set to determine whether the neuron was significantly (P < 0.05) activated. To make a heat map (Fig. 2A), the z-score was calculated from 2 s before to 8 s after each sensory stimulus, using the average fluorescence intensity in the 2-s period immediately before the stimulus as a baseline and averaged over 10 trials. Neurons responding to multiple sensory stimuli were identified according to the method described by Hamada et al.14.
