Changes in Outpatient and Telehealth Visits Among Undocumented Patients
Authors: Rahael Borchers, Francesco Sergi, Robert Rodriguez, Shaokui Ge, Elaine C. Khoong, Melanie Molina
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This cross-sectional study reports on changes in outpatient and telehealth visits among patients likely to be undocumented after the implementation of anti-immigration policies.

Introduction

Approximately 14 million people in the US are undocumented immigrants, and an additional 12 million live in households with at least 1 undocumented family member.1 Undocumented immigrants and their families often face challenges accessing health care because of lack of insurance coverage, language barriers, and discrimination.2,3,4 Policies that impede undocumented immigrants’ access to health care have serious consequences for public health and increase health care costs.5 For example, a study has shown that emergency-only hemodialysis for undocumented patients leads to higher morbidity, mortality, and health care expenditures than standard outpatient dialysis.6

Prior research has shown that anti-immigrant policies influence access to care and health care–seeking behaviors among immigrant patients. Between 2016 and 2020, federal immigration policies and anti-immigrant rhetoric suppressed health care use among immigrant communities.7,8,9 By 2024, California completed an incremental expansion of Medicaid to income-eligible residents regardless of immigration status and age.10 However, in January 2025, anti-immigrant rhetoric escalated and new federal policies intensified immigration policy enforcement, curtailed access to asylum, rescinded the protected areas policy (allowing immigration enforcement in sensitive locations such as medical facilities), and proposed to rescind the 2022 Public Charge rule (threatening to limit visa eligibility based on use of public benefits).11 The impact of these changes on immigrant outpatient and telehealth use remains unknown. This study examines outpatient health care use among patients likely to be undocumented and patients likely with legal status in a large safety-net system before and after 2025 federal policy changes.

Methods

In this cross-sectional study, we analyzed electronic health record (EHR) visit data from an urban public safety-net system in California that serves more than 100 000 patients annually to compare monthly rates of outpatient in-person visit completion and outpatient telehealth use from January to June 2024 (pre–federal policy changes) with those from January to June 2025 (post–federal policy changes). This study was approved by the University of California, San Francisco Institutional Review Board with a waiver of informed patient consent because the study used deidentified data. This study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guideline.

We included outpatient encounters for patients age 18 years and older at the time of medical record extraction, across primary care, urgent care, and specialty outpatient clinics. Since immigration status is not recorded in the EHR, we classified patients into 2 proxy groups using expert consensus: (1) patients with a non–English primary language (NEPL patients) to model undocumented status and (2) patients with English as their primary language (EPL patients) with Social Security number (SSN) to model legal status. Patients not meeting either definition were excluded from the analysis. We also excluded patients with invalid SSNs. Additional analyses were conducted comparing NEPL patients without SSNs with alternative reference groups (NEPL patients with SSN and EPL patients without SSN) to confirm the robustness of our classification approach (eTable 1 in Supplement 1). We adjusted for sex and assessed effect modification using interaction terms.

We assessed the total number of visits, changes in completion rates of outpatient in-person visits, and proportion of telehealth visits (as a percentage of total outpatient visits) as primary outcomes. In this health system, telehealth visits are primarily conducted via telephone without video, which does not require internet access or advanced technology. We excluded visits classified as left without being seen because of the small visit count. We summarized 6-month temporal trends and assessed month-specific differences.

Statistical Analysis

We described visit characteristics by proxy group and year and reported absolute counts and proportion of total visits. We used a mixed Poisson regression to estimate incidence rate ratios (IRRs) for completed outpatient visits and telehealth visit counts by month, comparing both proxy groups. The model included year, documentation status proxy, and their interaction as fixed effects and months nested within years as random effects to account for seasonal variation and other external influences. We estimated overall 6-month and month-specific IRRs comparing January to June 2025 with corresponding months in 2024. We conducted additional descriptive analyses in April 2026 including July through December 2024. As a sensitivity analysis, we repeated the analyses using only Medicaid-insured patients, as California’s Medicaid expansion likely added to this sample with undocumented patients. Given their presumed socioeconomic status, we anticipated that the effects of federal anti-immigration policies might be more pronounced in this subgroup. Given the large sample size, our outcomes are reported using IRRs and 95% CIs to emphasize effect estimates and precision. Statistical analyses were conducted using RStudio version 2025.09.0, build 387 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

In-Person Visits

Across the study period, patients likely to be undocumented had higher in-person visit completion rates than patients likely with legal status (IRR, 1.15; 95% CI, 1.13-1.16). However, month-specific analyses found lower in-person visit completion rates for patients likely to be undocumented in every month after January 2025, with decreases ranging from 5% to 12%. Significant decreases were observed in March 2025 (IRR, 0.88; 95% CI, 0.83-0.93) and June 2025 (IRR, 0.93; 95% CI, 0.87-0.99) compared with the same months in 2024 (Figure 1). There was no clinically significant change in in-person visit completion rates between 2024 and 2025 (IRR, 1.00; 95% CI, 0.99-1.02), and differences between immigration status proxy groups were consistent across years (eTable 3 in Supplement 1). In-person visit completion rates were similar across sex groups, although the higher completion rates among likely undocumented patients were slightly attenuated among female patients (interaction IRR, 0.98; 95% CI, 0.96-1.00).

Telehealth

In 2024, telehealth visits accounted for approximately 7% of outpatient visits, with similar rates between the 2 groups (IRR, 0.98; 95% CI, 0.92-1.03). From 2024 to 2025, the proportion of telehealth visits decreased slightly among patients likely with legal status (IRR, 0.95; 95% CI, 0.93-0.98) (5% relative decrease), while increasing for patients likely to be undocumented (5.6% relative increase). In 2025, patients likely to be undocumented had a 12% higher relative proportion of telehealth visits compared with patients likely with legal status (IRR, 1.12; 95% CI, 1.04-1.20) (Figure 2). Although the absolute change was small, the change was still clinically significant in visit distribution for health systems. In-person and telehealth trends were consistent in the subanalysis restricted to Medicaid-insured patients (eResults in Supplement 1). Female patients had substantially higher telehealth use compared with male patients (IRR, 1.49; 95% CI, 1.44-1.55), with variation across immigration status proxy groups, including larger differences among patients classified as likely undocumented (eTable 4 in Supplement 1).

Discussion

In this cross-sectional analysis of outpatient visits, we found that patients likely to be undocumented had higher in-person visit completion rates compared with patients likely with legal status but experienced month-specific decreases. Despite overall decreasing trends in telehealth visits in 2025, patients likely to be undocumented increased their proportion of telehealth visits, particularly during months when in-person visit rates decreased, suggesting that telehealth served as an alternative pathway when patients avoided in-person care. Female patients had substantially higher telehealth use across groups, with larger differences observed among patients classified as likely undocumented, suggesting that sex may influence how patients use telehealth to access care.

Our study found that, even in a state that expanded insurance coverage to undocumented patients, those likely to be undocumented avoided in-person visits and increased telehealth use during periods of high federal immigration enforcement activities. These patterns coincide with reports of intensified Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) activity in the Central Valley and Southern California.12 These findings align with prior studies linking immigration enforcement climates to reduced clinic show rates7,8,9 and suggest that fear of detention and deportation may discourage undocumented patients from seeking in-person care, contributing to increases in telehealth use.13 This study is among the first to show changes in health care use among immigrants since the new federal policy changes in 2025 and quantify telehealth use in this population.

Limitations

This study has limitations. These include the proxy classification, which may misclassify individuals’ documentation status in both directions. We acknowledge that language preferences may act as a confounder in telehealth use patterns, and future work should explore the intersection of language and immigration status in shaping telehealth access and use. Our study was limited to a single health system, and findings may not be generalizable to areas that lack longstanding sanctuary policies or have higher ICE activity. Findings may also not be generalizable across outpatient visit types, and future work could analyze differences across outpatient specialties. While we drew temporal associations, we cannot attribute causality between specific federal policies and the observed use patterns. Month-specific changes in outpatient care use may reflect responses to broader immigration enforcement climates, but qualitative research is needed to explore this further. Future work should evaluate interventions that improve access for immigrant communities while preserving quality of care, such as leveraging telehealth to mitigate barriers to in-person care, as well as addressing the digital divide and language-access issues to maximize telehealth effectiveness.

Conclusions

Results of this study suggest that the policy implications are substantial, as intensified federal immigration enforcement and California’s January 2026 freeze on Medicaid enrollment for adults with “unsatisfactory immigration status,” as defined by the California Department of Healthcare Services,14 further exacerbate barriers to preventive care for immigrant patients.15 We anticipate that these policy changes, combined with anti-immigrant rhetoric, may drive patients to reduce preventive outpatient visits and increase inpatient and acute care services, leading to a sicker patient population and higher health care costs. To mitigate these impacts, health system leaders should ensure that patients feel safe enough to seek care, while also maintaining their safety within clinical spaces, addressing gaps in insurance coverage, and expanding telehealth services.
