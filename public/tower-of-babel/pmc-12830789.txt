Oxindole based sulfonyl derivatives synthesized as potent inhibitors of alpha amylase and alpha glucosidase along with their molecular docking study
Authors: Muhammad Taha, Munther Alomari, Nizam Udin, Fazal Rahim, Imad Uddin, Syahrul Imran, Khalid Mohammed Khan, Syed Adnan Ali Shah, Adeeb Shehzad
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDiabetes mellitus, a persistent metabolic disorder, impedes the proper metabolism of proteins, carbohydrates, and lipids, leading to various physiological complications. A spectrum of synthetic alpha-glucosidase inhibitors is employed to mitigate glucose levels; however, prolonged use of these medications has been associated with a range of adverse effects. The current study particularly focuses on piperidin-indolin based sulfonyl derivatives, a class of heterocyclic compounds to assess the inhibitory efficacy of these synthesized compounds against α-amylase and α-glucosidase enzymes. All compounds showed excellent inhibitory activity in the range between 1.90 ± 0.10 to 16.80 ± 0.30 µM (amylase) and 1.20 ± 0.01 to 15.40 ± 0.30 µM (glucosidase). Limited structural activity relationship has been established for all compounds which suggest compound 16 has many folds better potential then standard drug. Molecular docking revealed that the most active compounds established stable hydrogen-bonding and hydrophobic interactions within the catalytic pockets of α-amylase and α-glucosidase, consistent with key active-site residues known to mediate inhibition. Molecular dynamics simulations further confirmed the stability of the ligand-enzyme complexes, particularly the α-glucosidase–compound 7 system, which maintained a Cα RMSD range of 1.5–2.2 Å throughout 200 ns. Introduction

Diabetes mellitus, a persistent metabolic disorder, impedes the proper metabolism of proteins, carbohydrates, and lipids, leading to various physiological complications that culminate in hyperglycaemia1. Currently, diabetes affects approximately 4.4% of the global populace, a significant increase from 2.8% in 2000, and about 589 million adults aged 20–79 years are living with diabetes worldwide (~ 1 in 9 adults)2. A recent studies estimated the global diabetes prevalence (all ages) at about 828 million adults in 20223. The number of people with diabetes worldwide is projected to reach approximately 1.31 billion by 20504.

A spectrum of synthetic alpha-glucosidase inhibitors is employed to mitigate elevated blood glucose levels. However, prolonged use of these medications has been associated with a range of adverse effects, such as hypoglycaemia, headaches, nausea, and dizziness5,6. Underscoring the urgency for the development of innovative, efficacious, and safer alternatives. Consequently, researchers are increasingly concentrating on heterocyclic compounds, attributed to their efficiency, accessibility, and reduced side effects7.

Indole based compounds have studied for their antidiabetic potential which indicate better interaction with enzymes8. Some Indole sulphonamide-based analogues have shown better inhibitory potential against amylase and glucosidase. The activity mainly depends upon the structural variation on the phenyl ring9,10. On the other hand, sulfonyl substituted compounds have shown to possess excellent biological properties11,12. Keeping in mind the better potential of both indole and sulfonyl moieties, we have combined the two structures which will further enhance their activities. Treatments for type 2 diabetes include acarbose, voglibose, and miglitol. These are responsible of regulating the enzymes α-amylase and α-glucosidase. However, their high cost and side effects have limited their use13. Diabetes affects 5% of people worldwide, making it challenging for medical practitioners to treat it without causing negative side effects. Consequently, there has been a surge in the development and synthesizing these agents, leading scientists to compete in creating novel, effective, and safe therapeutic agents for the management of diabetes14–18.

Recent studies have highlighted oxindole scaffolds, as privileged structures for α-glucosidase and α-amylase inhibition, with several oxindole-based hybrids showing low-micromolar activity and favorable binding interactions with the enzyme active sites19. Moreover, piperidine linkers are well-recognized pharmacophores that enhance aqueous solubility and facilitate optimal spatial orientation of bioactive moieties for target engagement11. Despite these advances, no systematic study has yet explored oxindole–piperidine–sulfonyl hybrids as dual α-amylase and α-glucosidase inhibitors, with comprehensive structure–activity relationships and docking validation. The present work addresses this gap by correlating substituent effects with enzyme inhibition and elucidating their molecular interactions through in silico modelling.

Our research group has reported several heterocyclic analogs for their pharmacological potential20–25. Rationale of the current study particularly focuses on indole-based sulfonyl derivatives, a class of heterocyclic compounds (Fig. 1). The primary objective is to assess the inhibitory efficacy of these synthesized compounds against α-amylase and α-glucosidase enzymes. The indole moiety, amongst various heterocyclic structures, has garnered substantial interest due to its ‘privileged’ status, which is attributed to its broad biological relevance.

Biological activities

In search of potent anti-diabetic agents, we have synthesized sixteen analogues. All analogues were screened for their α-amylase and α-glucosidase potential in the presence of standard drug acarbose (IC50 = 12.80 ± 0.10/12.90 ± 0.10 µM). All analogues showed excellent inhibitory activity in the range between 1.90 ± 0.10 to 16.80 ± 0.30 µM (amylase) and 1.20 ± 0.01 to 15.40 ± 0.30 µM (glucosidase) respectively (Table 1). Limited structural activity relationship has been established for all compounds which suggest that variation in the activities is mainly attributed to the change in the substituents on the phenyl ring.

Molecular Docking studies

Molecular docking studies were performed to examine the binding interactions of the most active oxindole–piperidine–sulfonyl derivatives (compounds 7, 9, and 15) with α-glucosidase and α-amylase. These compounds were chosen based on their low IC₅₀ values, indicating strong inhibitory activity. Docking simulations were carried out using AutoDock Vina to explore the active site interactions and rationalize the observed biological activities. The docking results showed a clear correlation between the experimental IC₅₀ values and the calculated binding free energies (ΔG), where lower IC₅₀ values corresponded to more negative ΔG values, suggesting stronger and more favorable binding interactions (Table 2). For α-glucosidase, compound 7 showed the most potent inhibition with an IC₅₀ value of 1.20 ± 0.01 µM and a binding free energy of −9.3 kcal/mol. Compound 15 also exhibited strong inhibition with an IC₅₀ value of 2.50 ± 0.10 µM and ΔG of −8.6 kcal/mol, while compound 9 showed comparatively weaker inhibition with an IC₅₀ value of 3.20 ± 0.20 µM and ΔG of −8.0 kcal/mol. These results indicate that compound 7 forms the most stable enzyme–inhibitor complex through multiple hydrogen-bonding and hydrophobic interactions at the catalytic pocket. Compound 15 forms slightly weaker but still stable interactions, while compound 9 interacts less efficiently with the key residues such as His239, His279, and Arg312. A similar trend was observed for α-amylase. Although the binding free energies for α-amylase were slightly less negative than those for α-glucosidase, the relative ranking of inhibitory strength remained consistent across both enzymes. This pattern suggests that similar structural features, particularly the trifluoromethyl and sulfonyl groups, contribute to effective dual inhibition by enabling complementary hydrogen, halogen, and π-type interactions within both active sites. Overall, compound 7 can be identified as the most promising dual inhibitor of α-glucosidase and α-amylase, exhibiting the lowest IC₅₀ values and the most favorable docking scores among the compounds tested. Compound 15 shows comparable but slightly weaker binding, while compound 9 displays the least favorable energy and interaction pattern. The docking results are in good agreement with the experimental enzyme inhibition data, confirming the reliability of the predicted binding modes and providing valuable structural insight for future design and optimization of oxindole–piperidine–sulfonyl derivatives.

As for the binding interaction analysis, docking results for all compounds were primarily compared to acarbose binding interactions (Fig. 7). Docking for acarbose against a-glucosidase showed that acarbose binds strongly to the active site of α-glucosidase mainly through hydrogen bonds, which is expected because of its highly polar sugar-like structure. The docking results show eight hydrogen bonds in total, with distances between 1.71 and 3.01 Å, and two additional weak hydrophobic contacts involving Arg312 and His239. These interactions create a stable and tightly bound complex inside the enzyme’s catalytic pocket. The strongest hydrogen bonds are formed with Lys155 and His279, both showing very short bond distances (1.71 Å and 1.77 Å). These residues act as the main anchors that hold acarbose in place and help mimic the natural substrate’s transition state. Asn241 forms three hydrogen bonds (3.00, 2.16, and 2.76 Å), making it another key residue that stabilizes the sugar rings of acarbose. Arg312 contributes both a hydrogen bond (3.01 Å) and a hydrophobic alkyl interaction (5.03 Å), while HIS239 and Pro240 form weaker carbon-type hydrogen bonds around 2.9 Å, with His239 also providing a π-alkyl contact.

Molecular interaction profiles of acarbose with α-glucosidase and α-amylase showing key hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic contacts at the catalytic sites

The comparison between acarbose and the fluorinated analogs (compounds 7 and 15) reveals distinct yet complementary binding patterns within the α-glucosidase active pocket (Fig. 8). Acarbose interacts primarily through an extensive hydrogen-bonding network, dominated by strong conventional hydrogen bonds with key catalytic residues such as Lys155, His279, Asn241, and Arg312. These residues anchor acarbose tightly via multiple short hydrogen bonds (1.7–3.0.7.0 Å), reflecting a highly polar and transition state interaction typical of carbohydrate inhibitors. In contrast, both compound 7 and compound 15 display a hybrid interaction pattern combining halogen bonding, hydrogen-bond-like contacts, and π-based hydrophobic stabilization, attributed mainly to their trifluoromethyl and aromatic scaffolds.

(a) Molecular docking interaction of compound 15 against α-glucosidase. (b) Molecular docking interaction of compound 7 against α -glucosidase. (c) Molecular docking interaction of compound 9 against α -glucosidase

For compound 7, the trifluoromethyl group contributes multiple halogen bonds, notably with the backbone oxygens of Ser156 (2.73 Å) and Phe157 (3.11 Å), as well as an additional halogen contact between another fluorine and Phe157 (O) (2.78 Å). A unique hydrogen bond-like interaction is formed between fluorine and the side-chain amide (HD2) of Asn412 (2.34 Å), while a weaker halogen bond occurs with ASN14 (OD1) (3.68 Å). The CF₃ carbon is further stabilized through hydrophobic interactions with LYS155 (alkyl) and Phe311 (π-alkyl), suggesting that the fluoro-substituted moiety replaces the dense hydrogen-bond network seen in acarbose with a combination of halogen and hydrophobic stabilization. The extended aromatic ring in compound 7 enables a π-donor hydrogen bond with Arg312 (NH) (2.89 Å) and an additional π-alkyl interaction with the same residue, indicating that Arg312 maintains its central role across all ligands. The sulfone group also interacts with Arg312 through a carbon hydrogen bond, while the indolin-2-one ring forms a weak carbon hydrogen bond with His239 (3.02 Å) and is further stabilized by π-alkyl interactions with Pro240 and Leu218. This combination of halogen and aromatic interactions allows compound 7 to achieve strong binding despite fewer conventional hydrogen bonds compared with acarbose.

Compound 15 follows a similar trend, with its fluorine atoms engaging in halogen and hydrogen-bond-like interactions that complement its aromatic stabilization. One fluorine forms a halogen bond with Asn412 (CO) (3.40 Å), another interacts with the side-chain amino group of Lys155 (2.94 Å) and the imidazole NH of His239 (2.32 Å), and a third forms a halogen bond with the carbonyl oxygen of Ser156 (3.09 Å) (Fig. 8b). The CF₃ carbon is stabilized by hydrophobic alkyl interactions with LYS155 and π-alkyl contact with Phe311, closely resembling the stabilization pattern seen in compound 7. The extended aromatic system in compound 15 interacts hydrophobically with ARG312 and forms π-π stacking with Phe157, while Phe157 also engages in a T-shaped π-π interaction with the indolin-2-one moiety. These interactions collectively produce a balanced network of halogen, hydrogen, and π contacts that substitute for the extensive hydrogen-bond network characteristic of acarbose. In general, while acarbose relies almost exclusively on polar hydrogen-bonding contacts to achieve strong affinity, compounds 7 and 15 incorporate halogen bonding and aromatic stacking to compensate for fewer hydrogen donors and acceptors. Arg312, Lys155, His239, and Phe157 appear as recurrent residues across all three ligands, underscoring their importance in ligand recognition and stabilization. Piperidine provides further support by forming π-alkyl interactions with imidazole sidechain of His279 and His239 (Fig. 8b). The introduction of fluorine in compounds 7 and 15 enhances binding through halogen bonding and increased hydrophobic complementarity, suggesting that fluorinated scaffolds can provide favorable electrostatic and dispersion contributions within the same active site framework that accommodates acarbose.

The docking results for compound 9 indicate that the bulky ortho-trifluoromethyl substituent strongly influences its binding orientation within the α-glucosidase active site (Fig. 8c). Due to steric effects, this group occupies a slightly different binding cavity compared to the other fluorinated analogs, leading to a distinct network of halogen and aromatic interactions. One of the fluorine atoms is positioned to potentially form a halogen bond with the carbonyl backbone oxygen of either Pro309 (3.10 Å) or Ser308 (2.88 Å), while another fluorine atom interacts with the backbone oxygen of Pro309 (2.90 Å). These halogen bonds help anchor the trifluoromethyl group firmly within the pocket and compensate for the lower number of conventional hydrogen bonds compared to acarbose.

The extended aromatic ring of compound 9 contributes to its stabilization through an electrostatic π-cation interaction with the imidazole NH of His279, which mimics the catalytic anchoring behavior observed for acarbose but via a different interaction type. This aromatic system is further supported by a hydrophobic π-alkyl interaction with the alkyl side chain of Arg312, highlighting the key role of this residue in accommodating both polar and hydrophobic ligands. The sulfone (SO₂) group strengthens the complex by forming a strong hydrogen bond with the amino sidechain of Arg312 (2.33 Å) and a carbon-hydrogen bond with the imidazole NH of His239 (2.83 Å). These cooperative contacts involving His239 and Arg312 are crucial for maintaining the correct ligand orientation near the catalytic site. The piperidine ring introduces additional hydrophobic stabilization through a π-alkyl interaction with His239, while the oxygen of the indolin-2-one moiety forms a carbon-hydrogen bond with the alkyl side chain of Pro240 (2.69 Å).

Furthermore, the indolin-2-one ring participates in π-π T-shaped stacking with Phe157 and a π-alkyl interaction with Ala278, enhancing the overall rigidity and complementarity of the bound pose.

As for docking of acarbose against a-amylase enzyme, the results showed a dense hydrogen-bonding network that is consistent with its highly polar and carbohydrate-like structure. The ligand forms nine hydrogen bonds in total, including seven conventional and two carbon-type hydrogen bonds, along with three hydrophobic contacts (two alkyl and one π-alkyl interaction). The hydrogen bond distances range between 1.84 and 3.57 Å, indicating strong and directional polar interactions that dominate the overall binding stabilization. Several catalytic and substrate-recognition residues of α-amylase participate in these interactions. The residues Thr163, Asp147, Ile148, Arg161, Asp300, and Glu233 form multiple short conventional hydrogen bonds, contributing to a stable and well-anchored binding pose. Among them, Thr163 forms both a conventional hydrogen bond (1.84 Å) and a carbon-hydrogen bond (3.18 Å), suggesting a dual stabilizing role. The acidic residues Asp147, Asp300, and Glu233 serve as major hydrogen bond donors and acceptors, providing strong electrostatic attraction to the hydroxyl-rich acarbose scaffold. The very short contacts with Glu233 (1.89 Å) and Asp300 (1.98 Å) indicate high interaction strength, likely contributing to the inhibitor’s affinity at the catalytic center. The residue Arg161 forms a strong conventional hydrogen bond (2.07 Å), which may help stabilize the transition-state-like conformation of acarbose within the pocket. Gly306 contributes through a weaker carbon-hydrogen bond (3.57 Å), supporting the ligand’s extended orientation. The hydrophobic stabilization is mainly contributed by Leu162 and Ile235, both forming alkyl interactions at 5.30 Å and 4.32 Å, respectively, while His201 participates in a π-alkyl interaction (4.94 Å) that further secures the central ring system of the ligand.

The docking comparison between acarbose and the fluorinated analogs (compounds 7, 9, and 15) against α-amylase reveals notable variations in their binding mechanisms and residue preferences within the catalytic pocket (Fig. 9). Acarbose, as expected from its polyhydroxylated structure, exhibits a strong hydro8gen-bond-driven binding mode. It forms nine hydrogen bonds (1.84–3.57 Å) with several catalytic residues, including Thr163, Asp147, Ile148, Arg161, Glu233, and Asp300, along with weaker carbon-hydrogen and hydrophobic interactions involving Leu162, Ile235, and His201. The presence of multiple short and directional hydrogen bonds, particularly with Glu233 and Asp300, underlines acarbose’s high affinity and its ability to mimic the natural substrate transition state of α-amylase. In contrast, compound 7 interacts through a mixed network of halogen, hydrogen, and hydrophobic interactions (Fig. 9b). Its para-trifluoromethyl substituent forms a halogen bond with the carbonyl backbone of Thr163 (3.47 Å), while the central carbon contributes hydrophobic alkyl and π-alkyl contacts with Leu165 and Trp59, respectively. The sulfone (SO₂) moiety engages in a π-sulfur interaction with Trp58, and its oxygen atom forms a strong hydrogen bond with the imidazole sidechain of His305 (2.12 Å). Additional stabilization arises from a carbon-hydrogen bond between the piperazine moiety and Asp300, while the oxygen of the indolin-2-one ring interacts with His201 (2.23 Å). Two π-alkyl interactions with Ile235 and Ala307 further reinforce the binding. Overall, compound 7 maintains both polar and hydrophobic interactions that mimic the stabilizing pattern of acarbose, though the number of strong hydrogen bonds is fewer and partially replaced by halogen-based contacts.

(a) Molecular docking interaction of compound 15 against α-amylase. (b) Molecular docking interaction of compound 7 against α-amylase. (c) Molecular docking interaction of compound 9 against α-amylase

For compound 9 (Fig. 9c), the bulky ortho-trifluoromethyl group does not directly participate in binding but instead affects the ligand’s orientation within the pocket. The extended aromatic ring engages in a π–π stacking interaction with Trp59, contributing to hydrophobic stabilization near the catalytic site. The sulfone oxygen forms a conventional hydrogen bond with the imidazole sidechain of His305 (2.40 Å), while the piperazine moiety forms a carbon-hydrogen bond with the same residue (2.57 Å). Additionally, the carbonyl group of the indolin-2-one moiety forms a strong hydrogen bond with the imidazole NH of His201, supported by a π-alkyl contact with Ala307. This interaction network suggests that compound 9, though less dependent on halogen bonding, relies heavily on π-stacking and polar stabilization involving His201 and His305, which are key residues also contacted by acarbose and compound 7. Compound 15 shows a distinct yet related binding pattern. Its fluorine atom forms a halogen bond with the carbonyl group of Tyr62 (2.94 Å), while the central carbon forms a hydrophobic π-alkyl interaction with Leu165 (Fig. 9a). The sulfone moiety’s sulfur atom does not participate significantly in binding, but one of its oxygen atoms forms a strong hydrogen bond with the imidazole NH of His305 (2.36 Å). The piperazine ring forms a carbon-hydrogen bond with the carbonyl group of Asp300 (2.70 Å), enhancing polar stabilization. Compared to compounds 7 and 9, compound 15 engages fewer aromatic interactions but maintains consistent hydrogen bonding to catalytic residues such as His305 and Asp300, like the pattern observed in acarbose.

Comparison suggests that acarbose binds predominantly through multiple strong hydrogen bonds to acidic residues (Asp147, Glu233, Asp300), while the fluorinated analogs achieve binding stability through a combination of halogen bonds, π-stacking, and hydrophobic interactions, often centered around His305, His201, and Trp59. The consistent involvement of His305 and His201 across all three fluorinated compounds highlights these residues as key anchoring points for inhibitor design within α-amylase. While acarbose relies on a dense hydrogen-bonding network for high affinity, compounds 7, 9, and 15 utilize fluorine-mediated and aromatic interactions to balance polarity with hydrophobic complementarity.

Molecular dynamics

The Protein Cα RMSD trajectory displayed a clear and interpretable pattern over the 200 ns simulation period (Fig. 10). The plot began with a rapid increase in RMSD during the first 5–10 ns, rising from near 0 Å to approximately 1.8 Å, corresponding to the relaxation of the docked complex as it adjusted to the solvated and thermally equilibrated environment. This initial rise reflects normal structural adaptation of α-glucosidase to the simulation conditions.

RMSD plot of compound 7 in complex with a-glucosidase for 200ns

After this equilibration phase, the RMSD curve entered a stable plateau region between 1.5 Å and 2.2 Å, which persisted for most of the trajectory, roughly from 10 ns up to about 190 ns.

Minor oscillations were observed within this interval, typically within ± 0.3 Å, indicating routine backbone flexibility without large-scale structural rearrangements. These subtle fluctuations are consistent with local conformational breathing of surface loops and side chains rather than global unfolding events. Toward the final 10–15 ns, the RMSD profile remained steady, showing no progressive drift or rising trend. The absence of cumulative deviation toward the end of the trajectory suggests that the complex maintained equilibrium and did not experience any late-stage destabilization or denaturation effects. This long, stable plateau is characteristic of a well-equilibrated and conformationally stable protein-ligand complex.

The MM-GBSA energy profile of the α-glucosidase-compound 7 complex over the 200 ns molecular dynamics trajectory provides detailed insight into the thermodynamic stability and the energetic factors sustaining ligand binding (Fig. 11). The smoothed ΔGbind curve, obtained using a five-frame moving average, showed an average binding free energy of approximately − 25 kcal mol⁻¹, indicating a favourable and energetically stable complex. Although the raw ΔGbind data exhibited visible fluctuations, these variations were mainly the result of compensatory shifts among individual energy components, particularly the solvation and electrostatic terms, rather than a genuine loss of binding strength. Throughout the trajectory, the van der Waals (vdW) contribution remained consistently negative, typically between − 35 and − 45 kcal mol⁻¹. This stability highlights the importance of hydrophobic and dispersion interactions that anchor compound 7 within the enzyme’s active site. The relatively flat vdW profile confirms that the ligand stayed tightly packed and correctly oriented in the hydrophobic pocket without undergoing significant positional rearrangement. The Coulombic energy component also contributed favourably, varying between − 5 and − 10 kcal mol⁻¹. These modest changes correspond to transient adjustments in polar or charged residues surrounding the ligand, reflecting local flexibility rather than structural instability. Together, the vdW and Coulombic terms represent the primary stabilizing forces maintaining the integrity of the complex. The solvation components, ΔGSolv_GB and ΔGSolv_SA, were positive, representing the energetic cost of desolvating the ligand and neighbouring residues during complex formation. ΔGSolv_GB showed the largest variation, at times exceeding + 20 kcal mol⁻¹, and accounted for much of the apparent noise observed in the total ΔGbind trajectory. In contrast, ΔGSolv_SA remained relatively stable and smaller in magnitude, suggesting a consistent contribution from solvent-accessible surface effects. The opposing trends of vdW and Solv_GB reflect the typical enthalpy-solvation compensation pattern, where the gain from nonpolar packing counterbalances the desolvation penalty. The smoothed ΔGbind trend revealed three distinct dynamic regions. During the first 30 ns, the system underwent equilibration, with ΔGbind gradually decreasing as the complex adjusted to a stable conformation.

MM-GBSA analysis for ΔG binding and other component energies for compound 7 over 200ns simulation against α-glucosidase

Between 30 and 150 ns, the binding free energy remained nearly constant at around − 25 kcal mol⁻¹, indicating a well-maintained equilibrium and persistent ligand retention within the catalytic pocket. In the final 50 ns, the trajectory displayed a slight increase toward − 22 kcal mol⁻¹, which may reflect minor solvent rearrangement or slow breathing motions of the binding site, although the complex remained energetically stable throughout this phase. This behaviour is consistent with the RMSD data, which showed that the protein backbone maintained its conformational integrity throughout the 200 ns simulation.

The RMSD profile for the compound 7-α-amylase complex shows a clear equilibration and stable binding behaviour throughout the simulation period (Fig. 12). An initial rise in Cα RMSD is observed during the early stages of the run, reflecting structural relaxation of the enzyme from its starting conformation. This increase plateaus relatively quickly, stabilising within the range of approximately 1.4 to 1.6 Å. The RMSD then fluctuates minimally around this mean value, with only minor transient deviations that remain below 2.0 Å. The absence of a progressive drift or large amplitude fluctuations suggests that the protein backbone maintains structural integrity and does not undergo significant conformational rearrangement upon ligand binding. These characteristics indicate that the complex remains stable under simulation conditions, supporting that compound 7 fits well within the α-amylase active site and does not disrupt the enzyme global fold. The stable RMSD trajectory therefore reinforces the reliability of the docking pose and suggests favourable binding compatibility between compound 7 and α-amylase.

The MM-GBSA trajectory shows consistently favourable binding for the complex, with an average ΔG bind − 36.68 kcal mol⁻¹ and a most favourable snapshot near − 48.39 kcal mol⁻¹ (Fig. 13). The spread is moderate and does not indicate progressive destabilization, which matches the stable RMSD behaviour. Decomposition indicates that van der Waals interactions are the primary driver of affinity. The vdW term averages − 35.83 kcal mol⁻¹ and often reaches values near − 44 kcal mol⁻¹, highlighting strong shape complementarity and close packing between the ligand and the α-amylase pocket. Synthesis protocol for sulfonyl oxindole derivatives (1–16)

The 1-(1-(Aryl)phenylsulfonyl)piperidine-4-yl)indoline-2-one compounds were synthesized by adding 1-(piperidin-4-yl)indolin-2-one (1mmol, 0.216 g) along with aryl sulfonyl chloride (1mmol) 15 mL pyridine as solvent. After completion of reaction, the solvent was evaporated, ppt was washed with cold water and dried to afford crude product which was recrystallized in methanol to get pure products (1–16).

α-Amylase and α-Glucosidase inhibitory activity

The inhibitory activities of the synthesized compounds against α-amylase and α-glucosidase were evaluated following previously reported protocols with slight modifications28. All experiments were conducted in triplicate and repeated three times independently.

α-Amylase assay

A 10 µL aliquot of each compound (1–100 µg/mL in DMSO) was mixed with 490 µL of phosphate buffer (pH 6.8) and 250 µL of enzyme solution (0.5 U/mL) and incubated at 37 °C for 10 min. The reaction was initiated by adding 250 µL of 1% soluble starch solution and incubated for another 10 min. The reaction was terminated by adding 1 mL of DNS reagent, and the mixture was heated at 100 °C for 5 min, cooled, and the absorbance measured at 540 nm.

α-Glucosidase assay

Inhibition criteria for glucosidase were established by using a modified version of a previously published work28. 10 µL of test compound (1–100 µg/mL) was mixed with 490 µL of phosphate buffer (pH 6.8) and 250 µL of 5 mM p-nitrophenyl α-D-glucopyranoside, followed by pre-incubation at 37 °C for 5 min. Subsequently, 250 µL of α-glucosidase solution (0.15 U/mL) was added, and the mixture was incubated for 15 min at 37 °C. The reaction was stopped by adding 2 mL of 200 mM Na₂CO₃, and absorbance was recorded at 405 nm.

Molecular Docking

In this study, AutoDock Vina29 was utilized to dock the small molecules into the active site of the protein. A homology model of α-glucosidase that was constructed based on the crystal structure of isomaltase from Saccharomyces cerevisiae (PDB ID: 3A4A) using SwissModel30. While for α-amylase enzyme, the crystal structure (PDB ID: 4W93) was downloaded from protein databank website. The structure of α-amylase enzyme was optimized by removing water molecules, adding hydrogen atoms, adding charge, and repairing end residues. For α-glucosidase enzyme, the grid map was generated based on 0.375 Å spacing between grid points, and the center of the grid box was placed at coordinate x = 14.885107, y = −11.366557, and z = 18.413239. The dimensions of the active site box were set at 60 × 60 × 60 Å. While for α-amylase enzyme, the grid map was generated using 0.375 Å spacing between grid points, and the center of the grid box was placed at coordinate x = −9.632488, y = 4.340907, and z = −23.107256. Protein-ligand interactions were visualized and analyzed using Discovery Studio Visualizer 2016.

Molecular dynamics

The molecular dynamics (MD) simulation of the α-glucosidase-compound 7 complex was performed to investigate the structural stability, conformational flexibility, and dynamic behaviour of the binding interaction under physiological conditions. The system was prepared using the Protein Preparation Wizard in Schrödinger Maestro, where missing hydrogen atoms were added, protonation states were assigned according to pH 7.0, and crystallographic water molecules beyond 5 Å from the ligand were removed. The complex was minimized using the OPLS4 force field to eliminate steric clashes and to achieve a relaxed starting structure for the simulation. The minimized system was solvated in an orthorhombic box of TIP3P water molecules, maintaining a 10 Å buffer distance from the protein surface. Sodium and chloride ions were added to neutralize the system and achieve a physiological salt concentration of 0.15 M NaCl. Periodic boundary conditions were applied in all directions. Long-range electrostatic interactions were treated using the particle mesh Ewald (PME) method, and covalent bonds involving hydrogen atoms were constrained using the SHAKE algorithm, allowing an integration time step of 2.0 fs. The simulation protocol consisted of energy minimization followed by gradual heating and equilibration. The system was heated from 0 K to 300 K over 1 ns under the NVT ensemble while restraining the heavy atoms of the protein. Subsequently, equilibration was carried out under the NPT ensemble at 300 K and 1 bar using the Berendsen thermostat and barostat for 2 ns to ensure stable temperature and pressure conditions. The production phase of the simulation was conducted for 200 ns under constant pressure and temperature using the Desmond MD engine. Coordinates were saved every 10 ps, resulting in 20,000 trajectory frames for analysis. Trajectory stability was monitored through the root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) of the protein Cα atoms and the root-mean-square fluctuation (RMSF) of individual residues to evaluate conformational convergence and flexibility. The RMSD profile confirmed system equilibration, while the RMSF pattern identified flexible regions at the loop domains adjacent to the binding site. The simulation maintained constant total energy and temperature throughout the 200 ns run, confirming a stable and equilibrated system suitable for energetic analysis.

Conclusion

At present, the greatest threat to global human health is diabetes, a persistent metabolic disorder leading to various physiological complications. Various synthetic alpha-glucosidase and alpha-amylase inhibitors is employed to mitigate glucose levels; however, side effects are the main issue associated with them. We synthesized sixteen oxindole based sulfonyl compounds and characterized through different spectroscopic techniques. All compounds were screened for their inhibitory efficacy against α-amylase and α-glucosidase enzymes. Molecular docking study was performed to investigate the binding affinity. From our present in silico study, we observed that four of the selected compounds, namely, 7, 9, 15 and 16 could be promising inhibitors of the selected enzymes, and a possible treatment option against diabetes. The study suggests that 16 has many folds the best potential. All other compounds showed better inhibitory activity in the range between 1.90 ± 0.10 to 16.80 ± 0.30 µM (amylase) and 1.20 ± 0.01 to 15.40 ± 0.30 µM (glucosidase). Limited structural activity relationships have been established for all compounds. Molecular docking analyses supported these experimental findings by revealing that the most active compounds formed stable hydrogen-bonding and hydrophobic interactions within the catalytic regions of α-amylase and α-glucosidase. The observed binding conformations were consistent with known active site residues, supporting the inhibitory mechanism at the molecular level. To further validate the docking predictions, molecular dynamics simulations were conducted on the most potent compound–enzyme complexes. The α-glucosidase–compound 7 complex demonstrated structural stability throughout a 200 ns simulation, with Cα RMSD values remaining within 1.5–2.2 Å, confirming that ligand binding did not perturb the overall protein fold. The MM-GBSA analysis further quantified the binding energetics, indicating a mean ΔGbind of approximately − 25 kcal mol⁻¹. The decomposition of energy components showed that van der Waals and lipophilic interactions dominated the stabilizing contributions, while electrostatic and solvation effects were moderately favorable.

Building on these promising results, future work will focus on in vivo evaluation of the most active compounds in diabetic animal models to confirm pharmacodynamic efficacy, and molecular dynamics simulations to validate binding stability and drug-likeness. These efforts aim to advance this compound class toward preclinical development as next-generation antidiabetic agents.

Limitation of the current study

Although the synthesized oxindole–piperidine–sulfonyl derivatives demonstrated promising in vitro α-amylase and α-glucosidase inhibitory activities still several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the present study is restricted to enzymatic and computational evaluations; in vivo pharmacological validation in diabetic animal models is still required to confirm efficacy and bioavailability. Only sixteen analogues were synthesized, providing a limited chemical diversity for comprehensive SAR analysis. Finally, the study employed docking-based interaction analysis without co-crystallized structural confirmation, which may limit the precision of the predicted binding modes.
