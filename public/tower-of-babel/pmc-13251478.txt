We thank the authors for their comments. However, we interpret the findings differently and address their points individually below. Please note that the cited article1 did not include great saphenous vein (GSV) pressure measurements; rather, the present comments refer instead to our subsequent studies.2,3

(1) Catheter tip orientation

(1) Catheter tip orientation

Tip orientation may affect absolute pressure but not simultaneously measured pressure gradients (PGs), as all catheters were oriented identically. Any directional effect would cancel out when calculating the gradient. Even then, any such correction would be negligible. For example, reducing an observed ambulatory pressure of ∼30 mmHg by 10% (∼3 mmHg) would require, by the Bernoulli equation, a linear velocity of ∼90 cm/s—comparable to peak aortic velocities (70-120 cm/s), whereas GSV velocity during walking is ∼20 cm/s. This corresponds to a dynamic pressure of ∼0.16 mmHg. Thus, catheter orientation cannot explain the observed gradients.

(2) Transducer dynamics

(2) Transducer dynamics

Pressure was analyzed at 33 Hz. Disposable transducers such as Edwards TruWave have a natural frequency of ∼40 Hz for a standard fluid-filled system. All channels were recorded simultaneously, so any damping or phase lag would affect them equally and therefore cancel out in the relative PG.

(3)Pressure gradient and flow

Pressure gradient and flow

A transient gradient (milliseconds) may not produce sustained flow, but a gradient persisting for a few tenths of a second, as observed during swing phase, does. The proposed interpretation is internally inconsistent. The authors assume anterograde GSV flow without a substantial longitudinal gradient, yet they question flow through perforating veins despite the larger, sustained gradient observed between the GSV and intramuscular veins. Flow depends on PG magnitude and duration, and on the total effective cross-sectional area under Poiseuille’s law. Anatomic studies demonstrate many small perforators beyond those visible on ultrasound.4,5 Their cumulative effective cross-sectional area likely equals or exceeds that of the GSV trunk. More importantly, perforators are distributed throughout the superficial venous network, so each drains a local territory through a short direct route into the deep system. In contrast, longitudinal GSV transport requires convergence of flow from a large network into a single axial conduit over distance, increasing hydrodynamic resistance. Thus, during locomotion, distributed perforator outflow is mechanically more efficient than transport along the GSV trunk.

(4)Ultrasound limitations

Ultrasound limitations

As noted in the letter, ultrasound B-flow is a qualitative modality that visualizes flow within B-mode imaging. It does not provide velocity measurements and depends strongly on echo signal intensity. Accordingly, interpreting B-flow images as evidence of flow direction is questionable, especially during locomotion, when it is inherently less reliable because it is prone to motion artifacts. Nevertheless, the reported observation of antegrade flow in thigh perforators supports our concept, as described in our publication.3
