Strengthening access to antihypertensive medicines through HEARTS in the Americas: the Grenada experience
Authors: Kerry-Ann Renaud-Thomas, Larissa Mark, Camille Isaac, Taraleen Malcolm, Shawn Charles
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjective.To describe how Grenada leveraged the HEARTS in the Americas standardized clinical pathway to strengthen procurement of a single-pill fixed-dose antihypertensive and align prescribing and procurement across public and private sectors.Methods.Grenada adopted a national hypertension clinical pathway specifying a single-pill fixed-dose combination of telmisartan/amlodipine with chlorthalidone as preferred therapy. Implementation strategies included integration of pathway medicines into the essential medicines list and procurement systems; engagement of clinical and procurement leadership; dissemination to private providers; and engagement with private pharmacies. A descriptive analysis was conducted using predefined indicators aligned with adoption, feasibility, acceptability, and public–private alignment using survey data, program documents, procurement records, and routine reports.Results.Integration of the standardized regimen into procurement processes supported availability of telmisartan/amlodipine and chlorthalidone in public primary care facilities, although supply gaps persisted. Adoption of a single national pathway supported standardized prescribing, forecasting, and centralized procurement. Dissemination to private clinicians and pharmacies contributed to uptake in the private sector, with pharmacies independently procuring pathway medicines, supporting continuity of care across sectors, although alignment remained incomplete. Facilitators included early stakeholder engagement, alignment between clinical and procurement leadership, and regimen simplification. Limitations included data system gaps and supply chain constraints.Conclusions.A standardized clinical pathway can strengthen procurement and promote public–private alignment in hypertension care. Addressing supply chain constraints remains essential for sustained access and continuity of treatment, with lessons applicable to other small island developing states implementing HEARTS.

Hypertension is a leading contributor to cardiovascular disease and premature mortality globally and in the Americas, with a high burden in small island developing states (SIDS). In the Caribbean, noncommunicable diseases place increasing pressure on health systems, underscoring the need for strengthened primary health care and reliable access to affordable, evidence-based essential medicines (1). Despite the availability of effective antihypertensive therapies, fragmented prescribing practices and supply chain inefficiencies often limit treatment continuity. Additionally, when evidence‑based clinical treatment guidelines are not established, patient management is highly variable and driven by clinician preference, contributing to fragmented care, increased costs, and poorer outcomes.

The HEARTS technical package, developed by the World Health Organization (WHO) and adapted for the Americas by the Pan American Health Organization (PAHO), promotes standardized, evidence-based management of cardiovascular disease in primary care (2). A core component is the use of a standardized treatment protocol and simplified medication regimens to reduce variability in care and improve outcomes (2).

However, misalignment between clinical guidelines, essential medicines lists, and supply chain management systems often limits consistent access to recommended therapies (3, 4). In many settings, fragmentation of health systems, particularly limited coordination between public and private sectors, disrupts continuity of care, especially for patients with chronic conditions who obtain medicines across different sectors (5, 6). Standardized regimens can simplify forecasting and procurement and improve medicine availability.

In Grenada, implementation of HEARTS focused on adopting a standardized hypertension clinical pathway and strengthening systems to ensure availability of recommended medicines. As a SIDS, Grenada faces structural constraints that shape medicine access, including limited procurement volumes, supplier concentration, dependence on importation, and exposure to supply disruptions.

Grenada implemented the HEARTS initiative within its public primary health care system in 2024. Antihypertensive medicines are available through public primary health care services free of charge for individuals aged under 16 years and over 60 years, and at a highly subsidized cost for those aged 16–60 years. There is no national health insurance. Private-sector care is largely financed through out-of-pocket payments, with reimbursement available only to individuals with private health insurance.

Approximately 95% of fixed-dose combination (FDC) antihypertensive products used in the public sector are procured through the Organisation of Eastern Caribbean States Pharmaceutical Procurement Service (OECS PPS), which provides access to quality-assured medicines through pooled procurement.

Prior to rollout, the Ministry of Health led a phased process beginning in six primary care facilities, including a rapid assessment of hypertension care gaps, stakeholder consultations across public and private sectors, and adaptation of HEARTS protocols to the local context. Although initially introduced in six sites, shared healthcare workforce enabled rapid diffusion across the wider primary care system. Early provider training and consensus-building supported uptake.

A standardized national hypertension clinical pathway was adopted, specifying first-line treatment with a single-pill FDC of telmisartan/amlodipine, with chlorthalidone included in the regimen. The pathway aimed to simplify prescribing, improve procurement efficiency, and support continuity of care across sectors. As a SIDS with centralized public procurement, few private pharmacies, and frequent cross-sector care utilization, Grenada provided an appropriate setting to examine how standardized clinical pathways influence medicine availability and public–private alignment under constrained pharmaceutical market conditions.

The aim of this article is to describe how Grenada leveraged the HEARTS in the Americas standardized clinical pathway to support procurement of a single-pill fixed-dose antihypertensive and align prescribing and medicine procurement and availability across public and private sectors. By applying an implementation science lens, this article contributes to the HEARTS literature by documenting how standardized clinical pathways can be operationalized to align prescribing and procurement activities across sectors in a small island health system.

METHODS

To illustrate the pathways through which standardized clinical guidance under HEARTS may influence medicine availability and continuity of hypertension care, we developed a logic model linking clinical pathway adoption to procurement alignment and public–private medicine availability (Figure 1). The model reflects the conditions of a SIDS, where limited procurement volumes, mixed public–private service utilization, and concentrated supplier markets shape the feasibility and sustainability of standardized treatment regimens.

A descriptive mixed-methods implementation evaluation assessed the adoption, feasibility, and acceptability of a standardized hypertension clinical pathway and associated medicine procurement practices under the HEARTS initiative in Grenada. An implementation focus approach was used to support system learning rather than causal inference.

Implementation focused on strengthening medicine availability and alignment across sectors. Strategies included: (1) integration of pathway medicines into the national Essential Medicines List and procurement processes; (2) engagement of clinical leaders, pharmacists, and procurement units to align prescribing and supply systems; (3) dissemination of the pathway to private providers; and (4) engagement with private pharmacies to encourage procurement of pathway medicines. These strategies promoted pathway adoption at policy, provider, and facility levels while reducing fragmentation between public and private care delivery.

Strategies were implemented sequentially, beginning with pathway consensus and policy endorsement, followed by integration into the Essential Medicines List and procurement systems, and subsequently by provider and pharmacy engagement activities. Orientation activities included sensitization events, continuing medical education, and technical support.

All public primary health care facilities implementing the HEARTS clinical pathway were eligible for inclusion in the evaluation. Private pharmacies were identified using national pharmacy listings and professional networks and were invited to participate in the medicine availability survey on a voluntary basis. Clinical providers were recruited through HEARTS implementation activities and facility leadership, focusing on primary care physicians, internal medicine specialists, and family nurse practitioners (FNP) involved in hypertension management across primary care and hospital settings. Given the small size of the national health system, the evaluation aimed to capture a substantial proportion of eligible facilities, pharmacies, and providers rather than achieve statistical representativeness, consistent with its implementation-focused objectives and reflecting the structural realities of conducting health systems evaluations in a SIDS.

Data sources included: (1) national policy and program documents (e.g., Essential Medicines List updates and HEARTS implementation reports); (2) procurement and stock records from public facilities; (3) structured survey administered to pharmacists in public and private sectors; and (4) structured survey administered to clinical providers involved in hypertension management.

Survey instruments were aligned with HEARTS operational guidance and implementation outcome constructs. Both surveys were administered electronically. The pharmacy survey assessed medicine availability, stockout frequency and duration, procurement lead times, and sourcing challenges. The provider survey used Likert-scale items to assess acceptability, usability, and perceived impact of the standardized clinical pathway.

A descriptive analysis was conducted using program documents, procurement records, survey data, and routine reports. No patient-level data were used. Data were collected between March and April 2026. The analysis was guided by implementation science frameworks, particularly constructs of adoption, feasibility, and acceptability as described by Proctor et al (7). Indicators were mapped to these constructs, and the study logic model.

Indicators

Indicators were selected to balance conceptual relevance with data feasibility in a paper-based health information environment (Table 1). Priority was given to indicators that could be measured using routinely available program, pharmacy, and procurement data, while acknowledging constraints in prescription-level and patient outcome measurement.

Public–private alignment was assessed across three complementary dimensions: (1) formulation consistency (availability of the same FDC products and strengths in both sectors); (2) regimen completeness (the concurrent availability of all medicines required to implement the standardized pathway); and (3) temporal availability (absence of prolonged or asynchronous stockouts across sectors). This multidimensional definition recognizes that continuity of hypertension care in mixed health systems depends not only on whether medicines are stocked, but on whether patients can access the same complete regimen reliably across care settings and over time.

Quantitative indicators were summarized descriptively, while qualitative information was synthesized to contextualize implementation processes and outcomes. Data relied primarily on self-reported surveys and program records, which may be subject to recall or reporting bias. However, triangulation across multiple data sources was used to enhance credibility and consistency of findings.

Ethical considerations

This evaluation was conducted as part of routine HEARTS implementation in Grenada using aggregated program data and anonymized voluntary survey responses. No patient-level or personally identifiable data were collected. In accordance with national guidelines, ethics committee approval was not required.

RESULTS

The results present findings from the implementation evaluation of the standardized hypertension clinical pathway in Grenada, drawing on program records, policy documents, and surveys of pharmacies and clinical providers. Findings are organized according to key implementation outcomes: adoption, feasibility, acceptability, and public–private alignment. Results emphasize system-level processes related to pathway uptake, medicine availability, and supply chain performance, rather than patient-level outcomes. Findings are context-specific and not intended to infer causality.

Adoption

All six health centers are currently implementing the HEARTS clinical pathway and are designated as formal reporting sites. These centers serve as primary hubs for service delivery. The 29 satellite medical stations also implement the HEARTS approach, as they operate under the same trained staff and policy directives. However, they have not yet been formally designated as reporting sites.

The FDC of telmisartan/amlodipine (80 mg/10 mg and 40 mg/5 mg) and chlorthalidone (12.5 mg and 25 mg) were added to Grenada’s Essential Medicines List in the third quarter of 2024, with procurement commencing in October of that year. These medicines became available for use in the public sector in January 2025, one month prior to the official launch of the initiative.

Providers from both public and private sectors were invited to participate in all HEARTS-related activities, including sensitization sessions, consensus-building workshops, continuing medical education activities, launch and implementation workshops. Attendance records indicate that 53% of registered physicians participated in the HEARTS-related activities.

A total of 14 pharmacies responded to the medicine availability survey: 11 private and 3 public facilities, representing response rates of 34.4% (11/32) and 10.3% (3/29), respectively. Efforts were made to increase pharmacy participation through email and telephone follow-up and redistribution of the survey. The public facilities respondents were Central Medical Stores, one hospital, and one major primary care dispensary which is representative. Stocking of the pathway medicines varied by sector and product (Table 2). Notably, all three public facilities (100%) stocked the FDC, compared to 8 of 11 (72.7%) private pharmacies, while the overall readiness – carrying both medicines – was comparable across sectors (63.6% private; 66.7% public).

Feasibility

Medicine availability was a significant feasibility concern. Only 4 of 14 pharmacies (28.6%) reported no stockouts of pathway medicines in the previous 12 months; the remaining 10 pharmacies (71.4%) experienced at least one stockout event (Figure 2). Telmisartan 80 mg/amlodipine 10 mg was the most frequently affected product, reported by five pharmacies (35.7%), with the higher-dose FDC (telmisartan 80 mg/amlodipine 10 mg) disproportionately affected relative to the other formulations. No medicine quality concerns were identified during implementation.

Among the 10 pharmacies that experienced stockouts, 9 provided valid duration data. One pharmacy that reported stockouts across all pathway medicines did not indicate a duration. Seven (77.8%) reported supply interruptions of 60 days or longer, representing a clinically significant threat to continuity of antihypertensive treatment.

Procurement lead times varied considerably across facilities, ranging from 24 hours to approximately 60 days. The most-reported lead times were in the range of 2–3 weeks, although several pharmacies noted reliance on secondary or smaller suppliers when primary distributors were unable to fulfill orders. Qualitative responses highlighted that major suppliers frequently did not carry the generic pathway medicines, causing pharmacies to resort to the brand-name equivalents (Twynsta) or alternative suppliers, resulting in extended and unpredictable lead times.

Acceptability

Thirteen clinical providers completed the provider satisfaction survey: 11 physicians (84.6%) and 2 FNPs (15.4%). Grenada’s public health system employs only two FNPs, both of whom participated in the survey. Twelve respondents (92.3%) were based in primary care facilities, while there was one hospital-based respondent. Most respondents reported using the HEARTS pathway for 7–12 months (53.8%), followed by more than one year (38.5%), and 3–6 months (7.7%), indicating sustained pathway usage.

Provider satisfaction with the HEARTS clinical pathway was high. Twelve of 13 respondents (92.3%) reported being satisfied or very satisfied: 8 (61.5%) reported being satisfied and 4 (30.8%) very satisfied. One respondent (7.7%) was neither satisfied nor dissatisfied, and no respondents reported dissatisfaction. Twelve of 13 (92.3%) providers indicated that they would recommend continued use of the HEARTS pathway in Grenada, with 1 respondent unsure.

All 13 respondents (100%) agreed or strongly agreed that the pathway was easy to understand, easy to apply, and supported standardized hypertension management. Confidence in managing hypertension and workflow efficiency were each endorsed by 11 respondents (84.6%), with 1 disagreeing on workflow. Notably, two respondents (15.4%) disagreed that recommended medications were practical and available, consistent with the supply gaps mentioned above (Table 3).

Clinician-reported impact on patient outcomes produced the most cautious responses of the acceptability indicators. Eight respondents (61.5%) agreed or strongly agreed that the pathway improved patient outcomes such as blood pressure control and follow-up rates, while five (38.5%) neither agreed nor disagreed; no respondents disagreed.

Public–private medicine availability alignment

Medicine availability was partially aligned across sectors. All 3 public sector facilities stocked the FDC versus 8 of 11 (72.7%) private pharmacies, while stocking of both pathway medicines was comparable (66.7% public, 63.6% private), suggesting similar but incomplete readiness across sectors.

Both surveys identified medicine supply as the main systemic challenge to pathway implementation, with barriers – including distributor unavailability, reliance on alternative suppliers, and extended procurement lead times – not confined to either sector. The disproportionate stockout pattern for telmisartan 80 mg/amlodipine 10 mg underscores the need for targeted procurement strategies for high-dose FDC combinations.

DISCUSSION

This study describes Grenada’s experience implementing a standardized hypertension clinical pathway under the HEARTS initiative, with a focus on strengthening medicine procurement and promoting alignment across public and private sectors. Findings demonstrate high pathway adoption and clinician acceptability, alongside gains in medicine availability, while highlighting persistent supply chain constraints affecting both sectors.

A key strength was the rapid adoption of the pathway within the public sector, supported by integration into national policy instruments, including the Essential Medicines List. Early inclusion of the FDC of telmisartan/amlodipine and chlorthalidone, with procurement initiated prior to the formal launch, reflects strong alignment between clinical guidance and procurement systems. This policy–procurement linkage ensures recommended therapies are both endorsed and available for routine use, facilitating consistent implementation. Global evidence demonstrates that essential medicines policies are effective only when aligned with financing, procurement, and supply systems, enabling uninterrupted availability at points of care (8–10).

Sustainability is supported by routine procurement of HEARTS medicines and continued use of the standardized treatment protocol, maintaining alignment between prescribing practices and medicine availability.

The findings demonstrate meaningful private sector engagement. A substantial proportion of private pharmacies reported stocking pathway-recommended medicines, and availability across sectors suggests emerging alignment in treatment options. This is important in Grenada, where patients frequently access care across sectors. Availability of the same medicines across sectors supports continuity of care and reduces fragmentation. However, incomplete availability indicates only partial alignment.

Despite these gains, medicine availability and supply chain performance were the main implementation challenges. Many pharmacies reported prolonged stockouts often exceeding 60 days. The higher-dose FDC (telmisartan 80 mg/amlodipine 10 mg) was disproportionately affected, suggesting vulnerabilities in procurement or supply of specific formulations. These findings underscore that inclusion in the Essential Medicines List and initial procurement are necessary but not sufficient for sustained availability. Evidence from implementation of the WHO Model List of Essential Medicines further shows that weak integration between policy and purchasing contributes to stockouts and variable service delivery, while stronger alignment improves health system performance and equity of access (11, 12).

Ongoing supply chain strengthening, including forecasting, supplier engagement, and procurement planning, is required to ensure reliability. Patients are currently dispensed a one-month supply of medicines with up to three refills. Although 90-day prescribing could improve continuity of care and reduce patient visits, current storage, inventory management, and pharmacy information system limitations may increase pressure on procurement and the risk of stockouts unless supply chain capacity is strengthened. Notably, while the public Central Medical Stores reported no stockout, both of the public pharmacy respondents (one hospital and one major primary health care dispensary) reported stockouts, highlighting distribution and last-mile supply chain challenges within the public sector.

Variability in procurement lead times and reliance on secondary suppliers further indicate structural supply chain constraints, including limited availability among major distributors. Substitution with brand-name equivalents, such as Twynsta, reflects adaptability but also raises cost and access concerns, highlighting the importance of supplier engagement in standardized treatment implementation.

Stockout patterns reflect structural supply chain vulnerabilities characteristic of SIDS. Higher-dose FDCs were disproportionately affected likely due to lower patient volumes and slower turnover reducing incentives to suppliers to stock these formulations. This may result in inconsistent availability of specific strengths, undermining the continuity of standardized regimens.

Market structure further shapes availability. Limited procurement volumes, dependence on importation, and concentrated supplier networks constrain competition and reduce supply flexibility. Responses indicating that major distributors frequently did not carry pathway-recommended medicines suggest that small markets may be deprioritized in supplier portfolios, particularly for generic products with lower profit margins. Pharmacies therefore relied on secondary suppliers or branded equivalents, contributing to longer and less predictable procurement lead times.

Parallel public and private procurement also posed challenges. While a single national pathway supported alignment in prescribing, procurement systems remained only partially harmonized. Public sector procurement is centralized and planned through formal tendering cycles, while private pharmacies operate independently and respond to market availability and distributor offerings. Without coordinated forecasting or pooled procurement, this can perpetuate uneven availability across sectors. These findings suggest that, in SIDS, standardized clinical pathways should be complemented by supply chain strategies addressing turnover, supplier engagement, and cross-sector coordination.

Grenada’s experience aligns with reports from other HEARTS implementing countries and small island settings, where standardized clinical pathways improve prescribing consistency but expose supply chain vulnerabilities (13–15). Limited procurement volumes, reliance on few suppliers, and challenges in FDCs are common constraints suggesting these issues are systemic rather than program specific, underscoring the importance of addressing market and procurement dynamics alongside clinical standardization efforts.

Clinician acceptability was high, with strong agreement on ease of use, clinical applicability, and support for standardized care. The simplified regimen facilitated integration into routine practice and improved provider confidence. However, more cautious responses regarding patient outcomes likely reflect the absence of routine clinical outcome data and recognition that blood pressure control also depends on medicine availability and adherence.

Importantly, findings from providers and pharmacies indicate that supply constraints are systemic rather than sector specific. Addressing supply chain bottlenecks will require coordinated, system-wide strategies rather than isolated interventions (8, 12, 16, 17). Targeted efforts to improve availability of higher-dose FDCs, strengthen procurement planning, and engage suppliers are particularly important.

A key limitation was the inability to conduct a prescription audit due to the absence of electronic health information systems. Clinical data are primarily paper-based, limiting systematic tracking of prescribing patterns and pathway adherence. Additionally, reliance on aggregated program records and self-reported survey data may introduce recall and reporting bias. Although aggregate HEARTS data are collected through DHIS2, a facility-level Excel tool piloted for patient-level data showed inconsistent site compliance. A DHIS2 tracker is planned to support routine reporting of patient-level outcomes, including longitudinal blood pressure control.

A further limitation of the study is that information on medicine pricing and private-sector market characteristics was not systematically collected, limiting the ability to fully assess cost dynamics and market influences on medicine availability and procurement behavior. Furthermore, the evaluation did not include patient-level outcomes, cost data, or longitudinal supply chain performance measures, precluding assessment of clinical effectiveness or economic impact. Finally, the sample represents a substantial proportion of relevant system actors in a SIDS context, but findings may not be generalizable to larger or structurally different systems. Despite these limitations, the evaluation provides relevant implementation insights for small island health systems implementing standardized treatment pathways under routine conditions.

This experience offers several lessons for SIDS implementing HEARTS. First, standardized clinical pathways can align clinical practice with procurement systems, as emphasized in global HEARTS guidance (2, 3). Second, early and deliberate engagement of private stakeholders supports system alignment and continuity of care. Third, sustained attention to supply chain performance is essential to translate policy and clinical guidance into reliable access to medicines. Routine administration of the survey is proposed to improve participation over time and support ongoing monitoring of medicine availability and supply chain performance.

Grenada’s implementation demonstrates that while standardized clinical pathways can drive adoption, acceptability, and initial alignment across sectors, maintaining these gains depends on strengthening medicine supply systems. Addressing procurement and supply chain challenges is key to realizing the full benefits of HEARTS in improving hypertension management. These findings are relevant to other SIDS with similar market constraints and mixed public–private systems.
