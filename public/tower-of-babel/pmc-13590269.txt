Methodological Concerns Regarding Codeine and Oxycodone Safety Comparisons in Hip Osteoarthritis [Response to Letter]
Authors: Jeongmin Lee, Yujin Kim, Eunjung Choo, Sooyoung Shin, Yeo Jin Choi
Published: 2026

Dear editor

We appreciate Shan et al for their interest and for the opportunity to further clarify methodological consideration regarding our study.1 We agree that observational studies utilizing administrative claims data require cautious interpretation due to residual confounding, covariate balance, competing events, and censoring.2,3 However, a few points raised in the Letter do not entirely align with the analytical approach, limitations, or interpretations reported in our paper, and we appreciate this opportunity to provide clarifications.

First, we agree that this study was not able to capture clinically relevant measures related to severity of hip osteoarthritis (OA), including pain intensity, functional limitation, mobility and radiographic disease stage, and these limitations were specifically acknowledged in the Limitations of the article. We have stated that residual confounding cannot be completely excluded despite extensive covariate adjustment and matching strategies and clearly identified information on pain severity, functional limitation, and OA stage was unavailable, which may have influenced outcomes as well as opioid selection. Therefore, we did not assume that propensity score matching (PSM) completely removed confounding by indication, and we consistently interpreted our findings as observational associations and emphasized the need for future controlled studies incorporating clinical, lifestyle, and genetic factors.

Second, the Letter raises concern regarding the residual imbalance in concomitant tramadol use after PSM (standardized mean difference [SMD]=0.381). While we admit that this imbalance requires cautious interpretation, we would like to clarify that we neither ignored this discrepancy nor suggested that tramadol-induced confounding has been fully resolved. In the Results, we reported the residual imbalance in tramadol use (SMD=0.381), along with those observed for asthma (SMD=0.130) and osteoporosis (SMD=0.101), and these variables were included as covariates in the multivariable Cox proportional hazard models, followed by subgroup analyses stratified by tramadol exposures. These subgroup findings are particularly relevant to the fracture-related results, which provided the lower hazard of femoral fracture associated with codeine regardless of concomitant tramadol use and across multiple patient and treatment characteristics. On the contrary, outcomes pertaining to overall and non-femoral fractures demonstrated heterogeneity according to concomitant NSAID and tramadol use. Thus, while residual confounding associated with tramadol cannot be completely excluded, the findings of this study suggest the association was not limited to a particular tramadol-use group. Nevertheless, these findings should be interpreted with appropriate consideration of the residual imbalance in tramadol use.

Third, Shan et al raised concerns regarding the reporting of our sensitivity analysis utilizing inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW). We agree that presenting additional post-weighting balance diagnostics, including covariate SMDs and weight distributions, would improve methodological transparency. These diagnostics were assessed as part of the IPTW analysis but were not reported in the article. The IPTW analysis was conducted as a sensitivity analysis to examine whether the primary findings remained robust using an alternative PS approach. The Results from IPTW analysis were generally consistent with the primary analyses. Hence, the omission of detailed weighting diagnostics from the article should be regarded as a reporting limitation of the sensitivity analyses rather than a limitation of the analysis itself or evidence against the validity of the primary findings.

Fourth, we respectfully disagree with the assertion that the presence of death as a competing event constitutes a violation of the “core Cox regression assumptions” and invalidates our fracture risk comparisons. In competing-risk settings, cause-specific Cox regression and Fine-Gray subdistribution hazard regressions are both well-established approaches that address fundamentally different research questions.2,4 While cause-specific Cox regression addresses etiologic questions, Fine-Gray regression evaluates cumulative incidence function in the presence of competing events.2,4 Therefore, Fine-Gray regression may provide complementary information rather than serving as a mandatory substitute for cause-specific Cox regression. Moreover, in the current article, Schoenfeld residual test was conducted to evaluate the proportional hazard assumption for the Cox regression models, and no evidence of violation was observed. Although all-cause mortality was comparable between the treatment groups, we acknowledge that this does not replace a formal competing-risk analysis. Hence, competing-risk modeling could offer complementary insights; however, the absence of Fine-Gray modeling does not invalidate our reported cause-specific hazard estimates.

Fifth, regarding potential informative censoring from medication switching, participants were followed until the earliest occurrence of the outcome of interest, medication switching, or the end of the study period. We acknowledge that censoring at switching could introduce bias if medication switching was associated with subsequent outcome risks. Inverse probability of censoring weighting (IPCW) can serve as a valuable sensitivity analysis to address such dependent censoring under the assumptions of adequate measurement of relevant predictors and accurate specification of the censoring model.5 However, potentially important predictors of treatment switching, including pain severity, treatment response, and adverse effects, are not captured in the Health Insurance Review and Assessment (HIRA) database.6 Therefore, residual dependent censoring could remain even with weighting. Although informative censoring remains a potential limitation, the absence of IPCW alone does not establish whether, or to what extent, such censoring affected the observed associations.

Lastly, we would like to clarify the interpretation of the results related to femoral fracture. Contrary to the Letter’s assertion that we overinterpreted the lower femoral fracture hazard associated with codeine as a “bone-protective drug effect”, we made no such claim in the article. We consistently describe the fracture outcomes as showing heterogeneous patterns. Moreover, our conclusion also referred to these as “distinct fracture patterns” and did not attribute the observed inverse association to an intrinsic skeletal protective effect of codeine. Indeed, the Discussion considered the OA severity, fall patterns and mobility as potential explanations, while warranting caution against interpreting the result as a direct protective effect. Furthermore, as pain severity, mobility and radiographic OA stage are not recorded in the HIRA database, the proposed explanation regarding OA advancement by Shan et al cannot be directly verified. Therefore, this should be considered as an alternative explanation rather than an established mechanism.

We appreciate Shan et al for commenting methodological considerations inherent to observational population-based cohort study. We agree that residual confounding, post-weight balance assessment, competing-risk analyses, and informative censoring warrant cautious interpretations and further studies. However, we respectfully disagree with the characterization that these issues invalidate the findings of our study. Several limitations raised in the Letter were already acknowledged in our article, while additional analyses, including multivariable adjustment, tramadol-stratified subgroup analyses, IPTW sensitivity analysis and duration-related analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of the observed association in the primary analysis. Above all, our study did not claim elimination of tramadol-related confounding or a bone-protective effect of codeine. Hence, the findings should be interpreted as observational comparative safety signals that require cautious interpretation and further validation, rather than as causal evidence favoring one opioid over another.
