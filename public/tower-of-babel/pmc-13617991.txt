Heterogeneous slab mantle hydration controls the chemical architecture of the Kamchatka arc
Authors: Qiqi Xue, Shuo Chen, Jianke Fan, Jie Li, Dapeng Zhao, Kaj Hoernle, Maxim Portnyagin, Weidong Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Fluids released from subducting slabs play an important role in arc magmatism and global volatile cycles, yet resolving their lithological origins and spatial distribution remains a major challenge. Here, we integrate molybdenum (Mo) isotope systematics with improved P-wave tomography across the Kamchatka arc to trace the origins and transport pathways of slab-derived fluids. Arc lavas exhibit δ98/95Mo values systematically higher than the depleted mantle and correlate positively with fluid-mobile element enrichment. We identify a distinctive “dumbbell-shaped” isotopic pattern cospatial with prominent low-velocity zones in the mantle wedge. The anomalies further align with regions of marked velocity reductions in the incoming Pacific Plate, indicating that the fluid budget of the volcanic front is directly inherited from heterogeneous serpentinization of the subducting lithospheric mantle prior to subduction. Our results demonstrate that preexisting slab hydration exerts first-order control on subduction zone chemical architecture, providing a predictive framework for understanding global volatile cycling.

INTRODUCTION

Subduction zones are the primary sites of chemical exchange between Earth’s surface and its deep interior (1, 2). The release of H2O-rich fluids from the subducting slab drives flux melting in the overlying mantle wedge and exerts a profound influence on arc magmatism, seismicity, continental crust formation, and ore genesis (3–6). Despite the centrality of this process, identifying the specific fluid source lithologies [altered oceanic crust (AOC), sediment, or serpentinized mantle] and mapping their spatial distribution across an arc remain substantial challenges (5, 7). This ambiguity arises because arc magmas commonly integrate overlapping and variably fractionated geochemical signatures, complicating efforts to isolate the influence of individual slab components.

The Kamchatka subduction zone offers an ideal natural laboratory to overcome these ambiguities. Previous studies have suggested that its arc magmas are largely mafic and sourced from a mantle wedge with subordinate sedimentary contamination (8, 9), enabling the clear isolation of slab-derived signals. Furthermore, Kamchatka presents a unique geodynamic dichotomy: at ∼55°N, the Hawaiian-Emperor Seamount Chain (HESC) subducts beneath the Kamchatka Peninsula. North of this latitude, the subduction angle becomes substantially shallower, decreasing from ∼55° to ∼35° (Fig. 1A) (10). To the south, the subduction angle of the slab remains relatively stable, averaging about 55° (10, 11). This natural experiment enables us to clarify whether the geochemical signature of an arc is a function of the incoming material composition or the geodynamic architecture of the system, or a combination of both.

Previous studies have suggested that hydrous fluids and/or aqueous melts released from the subducting slab trigger partial melting in the mantle beneath Kamchatka (8, 12–17). However, the dominant source of these fluids remains debated. Stable isotopes, such as O, Li, and Tl isotopes, have been used to argue for contributions from altered HESC crust-derived slab fluids (12, 14) or progressive dehydration of AOC and melting of an HESC-dominated mélange (16). In contrast, geochemical modeling of trace elements has suggested that aqueous melts derived from subducted sediments and oceanic crust drive mantle melting beneath Kamchatka (15). Recent thermodynamic-geochemical models and combined Mo-Pb isotope constraints instead point to dehydration of subducting serpentinized mantle as the dominant fluid source (13, 17). Notably, most existing constraints are derived from the northern Kamchatka arc, where the influence of the HESC is strong. A comprehensive framework that includes the typical Pacific lithosphere to the south remains limited. Furthermore, geochemical tracers alone are insufficient to spatially localize fluid release without independent geophysical constraints on the subducting slab and the mantle wedge architecture.

Here, we resolve this uncertainty by integrating new Mo isotope data from the southern Kamchatka arc with existing northern datasets and improved P-wave tomography. Molybdenum is highly fluid-mobile under oxidizing conditions and exhibits notable isotopic fractionation during subduction processes, with isotopically heavy Mo preferentially transferred by slab fluids, making it an ideal tracer for slab-to-wedge transport (18–25). By integrating this sensitive isotopic tracer with seismic imaging, we provide a unified, physically constrained model for the volatile cycling that resolves the interplay between the slab architecture and chemical transport across the Kamchatka arc.

RESULTS

The Kamchatka arc is part of the Circum-Pacific Volcanic Belt (Fig. 1A). Its volcanism results from the subduction of the 90- to 105-million-year old (Ma) Pacific plate beneath the Eurasian plate at a present rate of ∼9 centimeters per annum (cm/a) (26). To the south, the arc merges into the Kuril arc, whereas to the north, it terminates at the Aleutian to Kamchatka arc junction, bounded by a series of transform faults (10). The Quaternary volcanism in Kamchatka is distributed across two primary zones: the Sredinny Range (SR) and the Eastern Volcanic Front (EVF), with the latter extending northward into the Central Kamchatka Depression (CKD) (7). We focus on lavas from the southern EVF, including Gorely, Mutnovsky, Ksudach, and Kambalny (Fig. 1A). This segment provides a crucial reference, as it is situated south of the subducting HESC and, thus, is unaffected by its associated compositional and thermal perturbations, which complicate the northern arc sector (17).

The analyzed lavas are predominantly basaltic to andesitic (fig. S1) and display typical arc-like trace element patterns, characterized by enrichment in fluid-mobile elements (e.g., K, Rb, Ba, Cs, Sr, Pb, and Mo) and depletion in high–field strength elements (HFSEs) (e.g., Nb, Ta, and Ti; fig. S2). These samples also display systematically higher Mo concentrations (0.43 to 2.89 μg/g; mean = 1.19 ± 0.23 μg/g, 2 SE) and δ98/95Mo values (−0.24 to 0.02‰; mean = −0.10 ± 0.03‰, 2 SE) relative to the average value of depleted mid-ocean ridge basalt (MORB) ([Mo] = 0.30 ± 0.03 μg/g; δ98/95Mo = −0.22 ± 0.03‰) (27–29). The trace element patterns and Mo isotope systematics of these southern lavas closely parallel the lavas reported for the northern arc, but stand in stark contrast to those of the SR lavas (Fig. 2, fig. S2, and data S2) (17).

DISCUSSION

Tracing the source of slab-derived fluids beneath Kamchatka arc

Isolating the slab-derived signal requires the exclusion of effect of magmatic and crustal processes. Within the EVF-CKD samples, no systematic relationships are observed between δ98/95Mo and indices of crystal fractionation such as MgO, (Dy/Yb)N, or K/Rb (fig. S3), indicating that magmatic differentiation has not overprinted the primary Mo isotope signatures in the arc-front lavas. Although previous studies have shown that Mo isotopes can be notably fractionated during low degrees (<1%) of mantle melting, the Kamchatka arc magmas are inferred to form by moderate to high degrees of melting (5 to 14%) (7), under which Mo isotope fractionation is predicted to be negligible (29, 30). Furthermore, the lack of correlation between δ98/95Mo or MgO and tracers of crustal assimilation (e.g., Nb/La and Ta/U) suggests that crustal assimilation has not overprinted the primary slab signals (figs. S3 and S4). In addition, the absence of any clear relationship between δ98/95Mo and mantle enrichment proxies (e.g., Nb/Zr) (Fig. 3A) further indicates that the observed Mo isotopic variability in the arc-front lavas is not inherited from preexisting mantle wedge heterogeneity. Instead, δ98/95Mo correlates positively with fluid-sensitive element ratios such as Mo/Ce and Pb/Ce (Fig. 2). These coupled geochemical relationships confirm that the Mo isotopic signatures in Kamchatka arc-front lavas are primarily controlled by the flux of isotopically heavy, slab-derived fluids.

Our arc-wide comparison of Mo isotope systematics reveals a notable first-order coherence across the Kamchatka arc. Both the HESC-influenced northern EVF/CKD segments and the HESC-unaffected southern EVF exhibit higher δ98/95Mo signatures than depleted mantle, which correlate consistently with indices of fluid-mobile element enrichment (e.g., Mo/Ce and Pb/Ce; Fig. 2, A and B). This regional coherence implies that the isotopically heavy Mo signature is a baseline feature of the Kamchatka subduction zone, operating independently of localized HESC-related perturbations. Spatially, the δ98/95Mo distribution follows a distinct “dumbbell” pattern, peaking at the northern and southern ends of the studied arc segment while remaining relatively suppressed in the central segment (∼54°N) (Fig. 2E).

This spatial pattern is independently corroborated by boron isotopes. Compiled δ11B data of melt inclusions and bulk rocks from the similar volcanic segments exhibit arc-parallel variations that closely mirror those of Mo isotopes, with peak values approaching +7‰ in the northern and southern sectors of the arc (Fig. 2H). Such coupled enrichments in δ11B and fluid-mobile elements (B/Nb) are widely recognized as robust tracers of serpentinite-derived slab fluids (fig. S5) (5). Notably, the central segment (∼54°N) exhibits suppressed δ11B values in these independent compilations, mirroring the reduced δ98/95Mo signatures observed at the same latitude. This multiproxy convergence, further supported by weaker seismic anomalies in both the mantle wedge and incoming plate at ∼54°N, lends confidence to the dumbbell pattern that goes well beyond any single dataset, despite the currently limited Mo isotope coverage of the central segment. While Pb and other large-ion lithophile elements likely reflect mixed contributions from AOC and sediments (15), the systematically heavy Mo isotope compositions together with elevated δ11B are consistent with a dominant contribution from serpentinite dehydration fluids (5, 31).

Based on these isotopic tracers alone, however, it is not possible to unambiguously distinguish whether the serpentinite fluids originate from the subducting lithospheric mantle or from forearc mantle wedge serpentinite (5, 22). The low P-wave velocity (low-Vp) anomalies in our tomography resolve this ambiguity, as they extend to lithospheric mantle depths in the incoming plate at the northern and southern ends of the studied arc segment, well below the subduction interface (Fig. 2). In conjunction with the pressure-temperature conditions along the Kamchatka slab geotherm, which predict antigorite breakdown primarily at 80 to 120 km depth, consistent with the volcanic front position, this provides an argument for subducting serpentinized mantle as the dominant fluid source beneath the Kamchatka volcanic front (13, 32).

Our improved P-wave tomography offers a complementary geophysical constraint on the spatial distribution of this fluid flux. We identify a clear spatial coupling between these isotopic patterns and low-Vp anomalies at ∼50 km depth, situated within the mantle wedge below the arc crust (Fig. 2, D and G, and fig. S6). Specifically, the strongest low-Vp anomalies coincide with the northern and southern termini of the EVF/CKD, where lavas exhibit the highest δ98/95Mo and highest δ11B values (Fig. 2, C to H). We interpret these anomalies as magmatic-fluid conduits that track the peak dehydration flux from the deeper slab (∼100 to 120 km depths), which are generally consistent with previous local tomographic models with higher resolution (33–35). At these depths, released fluids facilitate localized flux melting, and the low-Vp anomalies thus reflect the integrated effect of high pore-fluid pressure and melt generation. In contrast, the central EVF segment (∼54°N) is characterized by weaker seismic anomalies and correspondingly reduced geochemical enrichment (Figs. 1B and 2, C to H).

The segmented architecture of the Kamchatka arc appears to be inherited from the hydration state of the incoming Pacific plate. Our tomography of the trench-slope region—imaging the incoming slab at a depth of ∼20 km below the seafloor (Fig. 2, C and F)—reveals notable Vp reductions at the northern and southern ends of the studied arc segment extending well into the lithospheric mantle. In contrast, the central segment (∼54°N) lacks these intense Vp reductions, suggesting a relatively hydration-poor lithospheric mantle. Because seismic velocity of the incoming subducting slab is primarily sensitive to serpentinization, this provides direct physical representation of the slab’s heterogeneous hydration state upon entering the trench. The tectonic origin of this presubduction hydration heterogeneity, whether related to seamount subduction, bend faulting, or fracture zones, lies beyond the scope of the present study. Our central conclusion does not depend on identifying this mechanism. Given that the volcanic front’s δ98/95Mo and δ11B signatures peak precisely where the incoming plate is most hydrated, we conclude that the fluid budget of the arc is directly inherited from the primary hydration state of the subducting lithosphere.

This slab spatial heterogeneity is subsequently transferred to the mantle wedge via reactive channelized flow (36), where fluids released from the slab-serpentinite breakdown ascend through the overlying mafic slab (7, 37, 38), efficiently scavenging Mo before fluxing the mantle wedge (Fig. 4) (17, 19, 23). Quantitative mass balance modeling (Materials and Methods) confirms that a modest addition (0.1 to 2.5% by mass) of this serpentine-derived fluid to the mantle wedge is sufficient to reproduce both the isotopically heavy Mo signatures and the positive δ98/95Mo-Mo/Ce correlation observed along the volcanic front (fig. S7A). To further constrain the relative contributions of different slab components, we integrate radiogenic Nd isotope constraints following the approach of (39). In δ98/95Mo–143Nd/144Nd space (fig. S7B), the Kamchatka arc lavas plot along the serpentinite fluid mixing array rather than the sediment mixing trajectory, demonstrating that bulk sediment contribution is negligible, consistent with previous Pb isotope mass balance results showing that the sediment comprises less than 0.2% by weight of the slab-derived component (16). The geochemical architecture is therefore best described as a multicomponent system in which serpentinite-derived fluids set the first-order isotopic framework, with AOC and/or sediment accounting for only second-order variability. Precisely quantifying the contributions of each slab component in Mo-Nd isotope space would require more robust Mo and Nd partitioning data and better-constrained isotope reservoir compositions specific to the Kamchatka arc.

The source of back-arc lavas in Kamchatka

In sharp contrast to the fluid-dominated arc front, lavas from the SR record a different petrogenetic regime. They display higher Nb/Zr, lower 143Nd/144Nd, and lower δ98/95Mo (Fig. 3), indicating derivation from an enriched mantle source (14, 40, 41). Similar geochemical distinctions between arc-front and back-arc mantle sources have been recognized in other western Pacific subduction systems (42, 43). Willbold and Messling (17) proposed that this component represents an enriched asthenospheric inflow facilitated by the structural disruption of the subducted HESC lithosphere at depth (∼300 to 400 km), creating a localized tear. However, to the best of our knowledge, direct geophysical evidence connecting this geochemical signature to deep mantle structure has been lacking. Our P-wave tomography provides this missing constraint. Beneath the SR, we identify a prominent low-Vp anomaly within the upper mantle at depths of ∼200 to 300 km (Fig. 1C). In addition, our tomography reveals discontinuous high-velocity anomalies with NW-SE in the gaps beneath the southern end of the Kamchatka arc and its offshore area at depths of ∼350 to 400 km (Fig. 3, C to E, and fig. S6). This possibly suggests a pronounced, deep “slab tear” or slab window. Similar features have been also revealed beneath the northern end of the Kamchatka Trench, indicating toroidal mantle flow around the slab edge beneath Kamchatka (11, 44). Therefore, we propose that slab tears or slab windows existing beneath Kamchatka enable the active, buoyant upwelling of hot, enriched asthenospheric material into the overlying subduction-modified mantle wedge (Figs. 3E and 4). The low δ98/95Mo values and enriched mantle signatures are the expected geochemical expression of this mantle source. Additionally, the SR lavas also display negative correlations between δ98/95Mo and (Dy/Yb)N and (Sm/Yb)N, with no resolvable correlation with MgO (fig. S3), indicating that these isotopic variations are not controlled by crystal fractionation. Since (Dy/Yb)N and (Sm/Yb)N are sensitive to garnet involvement in the mantle source rather than to differentiation processes, this trend is consistent with mixing between a depleted mantle wedge and the enriched mantle component described above.

Collectively, our geochemical and seismic data reveal a vertically and laterally partitioned architecture of the Kamchatka subduction system. Shallow dehydration of the serpentinized slab mantle governs the volcanic front, creating a dumbbell pattern of fluid flux and isotopic signatures dictated by the “hydration memory” of the incoming plate. In contrast, deep asthenospheric upwelling through slab windows or toroidal mantle flow around slab edges drives back-arc magmatism in the SR. These results demonstrate that the primary hydration state and structural integrity of the subducting lithosphere serve as first-order controls on the chemical architecture of subduction zones (Fig. 4). The interpretation demonstrated here may have broader applicability. In the Lesser Antilles, Cooper et al. (5) showed through boron isotope systematics and shear-wave velocity tomography that along-arc variations in magmatism and seismicity reflect the heterogeneous distribution of serpentinized mantle in the subducting plate, inherited from fracture zone hydration at the slow-spreading Mid-Atlantic Ridge, rather than differences in plate age or convergence rate. A similar link between presubduction lithospheric hydration and arc chemical architecture is documented here for the faster-spreading Pacific plate at Kamchatka, indicating that the degree and spatial pattern of presubduction hydration, rather than spreading rate alone, exerts primary control on arc chemical architecture.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Trace elements analysis

Trace element concentrations of whole-rock samples were determined using an Agilent 7700e inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometer (ICP-MS) at Wuhan SampleSolution Analytical Technology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China. The sample digestion procedure was as follows: (i) Powdered samples were dried in an oven at 105°C for 12 hours; (ii) ∼50 mg of dried powder was accurately weighed into a Teflon digestion vessel; (iii) 1 ml of HNO3 and 1 ml of HF were added slowly; (iv) the sealed vessel was placed in a stainless-steel pressure jacket and heated at 190°C for >24 hours; (v) after cooling, the solution was evaporated to near dryness on a hotplate at 140°C, followed by addition of 1 ml of HNO3 and re-evaporation to dryness; (vi) 1 ml of HNO3, 1 ml of ultrapure water, and 1 ml of 1 ppm (parts per million) indium internal standard solution were added, and the vessel was resealed and heated again at 190°C for >12 hours; (vii) the final solution was transferred to a polyethylene bottle and diluted to 100 g with 2% HNO3 prior to analysis. The analytical precision is better than 5% (relative standard deviation) for most trace elements. Analytical accuracy was monitored through repeated measurements of the geological reference materials (GRMs) of GSR-3, JA-2 and JB-3, which agrees with the reference values (data S1).

Mo isotope analysis

Chemical separation of Mo was carried out at Guizhou Tongwei Analytical Technology Co., Ltd. (Guizhou, China), following the procedure described in (45). Approximately 0.1 to 0.2 g of powdered sample was spiked with a mixed double-isotope tracer (97Mo-100Mo) and digested in HF + HNO3 at 150°C for 3 days. Molybdenum was purified using N-benzoyl-N-phenylhydroxylamine (BPHA) resin. Isotopic measurements were performed using a Neptune Plus MC-ICP-MS at Guangzhou Tuoyan Analytical Technology Co., Ltd., Guangzhou, China, with instrumental mass bias corrected using the double-spike method. Molybdenum isotopic compositions are reported as δ98/95Mo relative to NIST SRM 3134, using the following relation:δ98/95Mo=[(Mo98/Mosample95)/(Mo98/MoNIST SRM 313495)−1]

The analytical precision and accuracy were monitored through the analysis of procedural blanks and a suite of international reference materials (data S2). The total procedural blank for Mo was 0.017 ± 0.034 ng (2 SD; n = 3), which is negligible relative to the mass of Mo processed. The external reproducibility of δ98/95Mo for the NIST SRM 3134 standard was 0.00 ± 0.05‰ (2 SD; n = 37). Accuracy was verified by measuring the IAPSO seawater standard (δ98/95Mo = 2.09 ± 0.05‰) and a series of GRMs, including JB-3 (−0.20 ± 0.03‰), GSR-7 (−0.05 ± 0.05‰), GSR-9 (−0.12 ± 0.03‰), and GSR-10 (−0.17 ± 0.04‰). These results, along with their measured Mo concentrations for JB-3 (1.04 μg/g), GSR-7 (0.26 μg/g), GSR-9 (0.49 μg/g), and GSR-10 (0.08 μg/g), are in agreement with previously reported values within error (46–49).

Mo systematic mixing model

The serpentinite fluid end-member compositions used in the mixing model are constrained as follows. Mo and Ce concentrations in the depleted MORB mantle (DMM) are taken from (50), with an average δ98/95Mo of −0.22‰ from (28). Following the approach in (23), the fluid Mo concentration is calculated using a zone refining model with a rock/fluid ratio of 50, which simulates the progressive enrichment of Mo in a slab-derived fluid during channelized flow through a MORB-type oceanic crust overlying the serpentinized mantle lithosphere. The Mo partition coefficient between fluid and eclogite is calculated after (19, 24) at 600°C and 2.61 GPa under oxidizing conditions (FMQ + 4) and 5 wt % NaCl, assuming 2 wt % rutile in the residual source and a Cpx:Gt ratio of 70:30. Rutile preferentially incorporates isotopically light Mo, such that fluids passing through the eclogitic crust acquire isotopically heavy Mo signatures relative to the residual slab. The partition coefficient for Nd is from (37) at 700°C and 4 GPa. 143Nd/144Nd of DMM is from (51). Two serpentinite fluid end-members are considered to bracket the range of possible fluid compositions: fluid 1, with δ98/95Mo = +0.25‰, estimated from this study and (17), and fluid 2, with δ98/95Mo = 0‰. Weighted average sediment compositions outboard of the Kamchatka arc (Mo, Ce, and Nd concentrations and Nd isotopes) are from (16). As Mo isotope data for sediments outboard of Kamchatka are currently unavailable, we adopt the average sediment Mo isotope composition outboard of the Mariana arc from (20) as a proxy. Detailed parameters are presented in data S4.

Seismic data

In this study, abundant P-wave arrival-time data are used to obtain the tomographic model. These data are collected from the International Seismological Centre (ISC)–EHB bulletins from 1964 to 2021 (www.isc.ac.uk/isc-ehb/search/arrivals/), in which all the events are well relocated using the EHB algorithm (52). Then, the data are carefully sifted to constitute the final dataset to perform the isotropic tomographic inversion. For the local and regional earthquakes, the selection criteria are as follows: (i) Each earthquake was recorded at more than five stations in the study region, and (ii) the absolute values of raw travel-time residuals are <2.5 s. The teleseismic events are selected based on the following criteria: (i) The epicentral distance between a teleseismic event and a station is in the range of 30° to 90°; (ii) the absolute values of relative travel-time residuals are <3.0 s; and (iii) each event was recorded at more than five stations in the study region. As a result, our dataset contains 307,763 arrival times of 14,459 local events and 109,140 relative travel-time residuals of 14,875 teleseismic events recorded at 280 stations (fig. S8).

Seismic tomography

The tomography method (53) used to determine the three-dimensional (3D) Vp model beneath the Kamchatka subduction zone. For the inversion, 3D grid nodes are arranged in the study volume with a lateral grid interval of 1.0°. In the vertical direction, the grid meshes are set at depths of 20, 50, 100, 150, 200, 250, 300, 350, and 400 km. The 1D initial model is integrated from the CRUST1.0 model (54), the WPSP01P model (55), and the IASP91 Earth model (56), similar to our previous works (57, 58). Velocity discontinuities, such as the Conrad, Moho, and 410- and 660-km discontinuities, are also included in the 1D initial Vp model. Using this initial 1D Vp model, we relocate all the local and regional earthquakes. Then, we conduct the tomographic inversions using the LSQR algorithm (59) with damping and smoothing regularizations. Considering the trade-off between the root-mean-square travel-time residual and the norm of the 3D Vp model, we find the optimal damping and smoothing parameters to be 100.0 and 10,000.0, respectively, after conducting many tomographic inversions with different values of the damping and smoothing parameters.

Resolution analysis

Two kinds of resolution tests are performed to evaluate the resolution and reliability of the main features of our Vp tomographic model, including checkerboard resolution tests (CRTs) and restoring resolution tests (RRTs). In the CRTs for the isotropic tomography, every two neighboring grid nodes are assigned +3% and −3% Vp anomalies to construct a 3D input checkerboard model. Then, synthetic travel times were calculated for the checkerboard model with the same distribution of seismic stations and events as in the real dataset, and random noise (−0.2 to +0.2 s) with a standard deviation of 0.1 s was added to the synthetic travel times to simulate the picking errors of the observed data. Last, the synthetic travel times were inverted to obtain an output model, which was then compared with the input checkerboard model to investigate the resolution of the tomographic images.

Figures S9 and S10 show the CRT results for the tomography with lateral grid intervals of 1.0° and 1.5°, respectively, which indicate that the tomography images have a lateral resolution of 1.0° at depths of 20 to 250 km beneath the Kamchatka Island and its surrounding areas and have a lateral resolution of 1.5° at depths of 20 to 400 km beneath the main study region.

To further appraise the reliability of the main features of our Vp model, a number of RRTs were performed (60). In these tests, most steps are the same as those of the CRTs except for the input model, which is derived from the Vp patterns revealed by this study. Our tomography shows lacks of prominent low-Vp anomalies beneath the Kamchatka costal area at ∼54°N at depths of 20 to 50 km (Fig. 2 and fig. S6), and then, we constructed two RRTs to confirm this feather. The input models in these RRTs include continuous or discontinuous low-Vp anomalies beneath the Kamchatka costal area at depths of 20 to 50 km. The RRT results shown in figs. S11 and S12 indicate that the features with the lack of prominent low-Vp anomalies near the Kamchatka costal area at ∼54°N at depths of 20 to 50 km are robust.

Our isotropic tomography also reveals discontinuous high-velocity anomalies beneath the southern Kamchatka arc at depths of 350 to 400 km, and distinct low-Vp anomalies (fig. S6) exist in the area that lacks prominent high-velocity anomalies. To confirm the credibility of these features, we performed three RRTs, whose results (figs. S13 to S15) show that the above features are quite robust.
