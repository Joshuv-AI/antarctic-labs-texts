Data-driven discovery of dynamical models in biology Preprint: APS/123-QED
Bartosz Prokop, Lendert Gelens

Abstract
Dynamical systems theory describes how interacting quantities change over time and space, from molecular oscillators to large-scale biological patterns. Such systems often involve nonlinear feedbacks, delays, and interactions across scales. Classical modeling derives explicit governing equations, often systems of differential equations, by combining mechanistic assumptions, experimental observations, and known physical laws. The growing complexity of biological processes has, however, motivated complementary data-driven methods that aim to infer model structure directly from measurements, often without specifying equations a priori. In this review, we survey approaches for model discovery in biological dynamical systems, focusing on three methodological families: regression-based methods, network-based architectures, and decomposition techniques. We compare their ability to address three core goals: forecasting future states, identifying interactions, and characterizing system states. Representative methods are applied to a common benchmark, the Oregonator model, a minimal nonlinear oscillator that captures shared design principles of chemical and biological systems. By highlighting strengths, limitations, and interpretability, we aim to guide researchers in selecting tools for analyzing complex, nonlinear, and high-dimensional dynamics in the life sciences.

Introduction

I.1
Dynamical systems across science

The world around us is constantly changing. From planetary orbits that govern the change of seasons, to turbulent weather patterns, to the molecular choreography of living cells: all are examples of
dynamical systems
, in which the state of a system evolves over time according to underlying rules.

A dynamical system describes how a set of interacting quantities changes in time, and is often represented by a system of
ordinary differential equations
(ODEs). An ODE specifies how the rate of change of a quantity depends on the current state of the system. In vector form, a general continuous-time dynamical system can be written as
Strogatz 2000
:

\frac{d}{dt}X(t)=X_{t}(t)=\mathrm{f}(X(t),\beta(t)),

(1)

where
X
is the state vector containing the variables
x_{i}
with
i=1,\dots,N
,
\mathrm{f}
is the vector field governing the dynamics, and
\beta
is the set of parameters that may depend on time
t
.
In experimental or computational studies, we often work with discrete-time representations:

X_{k+1}=\mathrm{F}(X_{k}),

(2)

where
X_{k}
is the system state at time
t_{k}
and
X_{k+1}
at
t_{k+1}
, updated by the map
\mathrm{F}
.

As illustrated in Fig.
1
A, small parameter changes can profoundly alter the qualitative behavior of a system, for example shifting it from a stable fixed point to sustained oscillations through a bifurcation. Mathematical models of dynamical systems allow us to predict future states, identify interactions between variables, and understand the mechanisms producing observed behavior. This modeling framework has long been central to physics and engineering, and is now also essential in the life sciences.

I.3
Two illustrative oscillators in chemistry and biology

To illustrate how dynamical systems arise in both chemistry and biology, we examine two illustrative oscillators.
The first occurs in the
Belousov-Zhabotinsky
(BZ)
reaction
, initially described by Belousov in the 1950s
Winfree 1984
and belonging to the family of (bio)chemical systems arising from non-equilibrium thermodynamics. Belousov observed periodic color changes in a mixture of bromate, cerium, and malonic acid, a phenomenon initially dismissed by the scientific community for apparently contradicting established chemical principles
Winfree 1984
. Zhabotinsky later reproduced the reaction, and Field, Körös, and Noyes elucidated its underlying mechanism
Field et al. 1972
. Experiments by these authors are shown in Fig.
1
B, alongside a Petri dish demonstration of the classical BZ reaction producing temporally changing wave patterns.
Over the years, numerous mathematical models of the BZ reaction have been proposed, ranging from detailed mechanistic descriptions involving many intermediate species to reduced descriptions capturing only the dominant dynamical features
Gyorgyi et al. 1990
;
Tyson 2013
;
Tyson 1977
;
Epstein and Pojman 1998
.
Here, we focus on the
Oregonator
model developed by Field and Noyes
Field and Noyes 1974
and written in a normalized form by Tyson
Tyson 1985
(Fig.
1
C), a minimal set of nonlinear ODEs that retains only the three key intermediates and their essential interactions. We present this model because it captures the core feedback structure responsible for oscillations while remaining analytically and computationally tractable. In compact form:

\begin{split}u_{t}&=p[u(1-u)-v(u-q)],\\
v_{t}&=p^{\prime}(uv+gv-2fw),\\
w_{t}&=u-w,\end{split}

(3)

where
u=[\text{HBrO}_{2}]
,
v=[\text{Br}^{-}]
, and
w=[\text{Ce}^{4+}]
represent the concentrations of the key intermediates. This model will later serve as a benchmark for evaluating data-driven methods.

Biochemical oscillations play equally central roles in biology. One key example is the eukaryotic cell cycle, which drives cellular division and underpins the development of all living organisms. The canonical somatic cycle consists of interphase (G1, S, G2) followed by mitosis (M-phase), during which DNA is replicated and the cell divides into two genetically identical daughters
Alberts et al. 2008
. In early embryonic cycles, such as those in
Xenopus
embryos, the gap phases are absent and cells alternate rapidly between DNA synthesis and mitosis.
Extensive work with
Xenopus
extracts has uncovered a core biochemical oscillator: cyclin B is periodically synthesized and binds to Cdk1, forming a complex that triggers mitotic entry; active Cdk1 then activates the anaphase-promoting complex/cyclosome (APC/C), which promotes cyclin B degradation, thus closing a negative feedback loop
King et al. 1996
;
Newport and Kirschner 1984
;
Pomerening et al. 2005
;
Yang and Ferrell Jr 2013
(Fig.
1
D-E). In large embryonic cells and in cell-free extracts, the mitotic state propagates as spatial waves across the cytoplasm, appearing as target patterns
Chang and Ferrell Jr 2013
;
Nolet et al. 2020
;
Afanzar et al. 2020
;
Puls et al. 2024
;
Deneke et al. 2016
or as spiral waves
Cebrián-Lacasa et al. 2024
(Fig.
1
D). Such patterns are analogous to those observed in the BZ reaction, illustrating that similar spatio-temporal dynamics can emerge in both chemical and biological systems.
Many mathematical models of the cell cycle oscillator have been developed, from large-scale mechanistic descriptions to reduced, analytically tractable forms
Ferrell et al. 2011
;
Novak and Tyson 1993
;
Pomerening et al. 2005
;
Yang and Ferrell Jr 2013
;
Tyson and Novak 2015
;
De Boeck et al. 2021
;
Rombouts and Gelens 2021
.
Here, we present a recent model by Parra-Rivas
et al.

Parra-Rivas et al. 2023
, which integrates two bistable switches
Mochida et al. 2016
;
Rata et al. 2018
;
Kamenz et al. 2021
and successfully reproduces early embryonic oscillations both qualitatively and quantitatively (Fig.
1
B). We focus on this model because it explicitly incorporates only the three core molecular components (cyclin B, Cdk1, APC/C) and their interactions (Fig.
1
E):

\displaystyle\begin{split}u_{t}&=k_{s}-\left(d_{1}+d_{2}w\right)u,\\
v_{t}&=e_{v}^{-1}\left(\frac{s_{u}}{s_{y}}u-v+b_{v}s_{v}v^{2}-s_{v}^{2}v^{3}\right),\\
w_{t}&=e_{w}^{-1}\left(\frac{s_{uv}}{s_{w}}uv-w+b_{w}s_{w}w^{2}-s_{w}^{2}w^{3}\right),\end{split}

(4)

where
u=[\text{cyclin B}]
,
v=[\text{Cdk1}_{a}]
, and
w=[\text{APC/C}]
denote the state variables.

Despite their very different molecular compositions, the Oregonator and the cell cycle oscillator share fundamental design principles: feedback loops involving activation and inhibition that lend themselves to mathematical modeling. Tyson formulated both models for the Oregonator and early cell cycle models, using the deep conceptual parallels between chemical and biochemical oscillators
Tyson 2022
.
Oscillations in both systems arise from common dynamical principles. In many systems, a stable fixed point describes the long-term state; persistent oscillations emerge when parameters cross a bifurcation threshold that destabilizes this fixed point
Strogatz 2000
. The most common example is the Hopf bifurcation, in which the system transitions to a stable closed trajectory in state space: a
limit cycle
(Fig.
1
A). Three elements are often required
Novák and Tyson 2008
: (i) a negative feedback loop to reset the system; (ii) a time delay preventing immediate resetting; and (iii) sufficient nonlinearity to drive the system away from fixed points. In the BZ reaction, inhibitory bromide feedback, intermediate chemical conversion steps, and autocatalytic terms fulfill these roles. In the cell cycle, APC/C-mediated cyclin degradation provides negative feedback, multi-step processes and bistable switches impose delays, and ultrasensitive/bistable activation processes ensure strong nonlinearity. Rather than tracking every molecular interaction in detail, some researchers have also argued to focus on these functional dynamic features, such as ultrasensitivity, bistability, and time delays, and to represent systems through their bifurcation structures and response curves
Tyson and Novak 2020
;
De Boeck et al. 2021
;
Parra-Rivas et al. 2023
.
This perspective emphasizes the universal building blocks of biological regulation, complementing mechanistic models while keeping the dynamics analytically and conceptually tractable.

I.4
Understanding dynamical systems

Mathematical models of dynamical systems are powerful because they enable us to
predict
,
explain
, and
interpret
complex behaviors. Yet what it means to “understand” a dynamical system differs across disciplines. Physicists may seek exact laws that fully reproduce a system’s evolution; meteorologists often prioritize accurate forecasts without complete mechanistic knowledge; biologists typically focus on identifying key components and their interactions
Kitano 2002
.

Ideally, one would infer the full governing equations
\mathrm{f}(X(t),\beta)
from data. Such a model immediately provides three complementary outcomes (Fig.
1
F):

Forecasting

Predicting future states from current observations, with or without a mechanistic model.

Interactions

Identifying the main connections among system variables. These can be visualized as a network diagram, and when the precise functional forms are known, formalized as a dynamical system.

States

Revealing the system’s repertoire of possible behaviors: its attracting stationary solutions (fixed points), oscillatory limit cycles and other attractors, and the transitions between them.

In practice, recovering the full equations is often infeasible. Instead, data-driven methods may aim directly at one or more of these three goals. These aspects are strongly interconnected: the same dataset can, in principle, inform all three, but different approaches emphasize one or another. Together, they form complementary routes toward a better understanding of dynamical systems in biology.

I.5
From classical to data-driven approaches

The study of dynamical systems has a long history. In the Western scientific tradition, early modeling efforts, from Ptolemy’s geocentric geometry to Kepler’s elliptical orbits, sought to capture planetary motion. The 17
th
-century development of calculus by Newton and Leibniz allowed physical laws to be expressed as differential equations, exemplified by Newton’s inverse-square law for gravity
Strogatz 2000
. Euler and Lagrange extended this to a general formalism for ODE-based modeling.
In the late 19
th
century, Poincaré shifted focus from exact solutions to qualitative questions such as stability, laying the foundations of nonlinear dynamics
Arnold and Vogtmann 1978
. This revealed that many natural systems—across physics, engineering, biology, climate science, and economics—are inherently nonlinear and complex.
For low-dimensional, well-characterized systems, the classical approach (derive equations from observation and intuition) remains powerful. But as the number of variables grows and interactions become uncertain, as in many biological systems, this approach becomes increasingly challenging.
In recent decades, a complementary
data-driven
paradigm has emerged
Brunton and Kutz 2022
. While all models are ultimately data-based, here the goal is to infer governing rules directly from measurements, with minimal prior assumptions. Early developments included statistical time-series methods for nonlinear and chaotic dynamics
Takens 1981
;
Abarbanel et al. 1990
;
Casdagli 1989
;
Mindlin and Gilmore 1992
;
Grassberger et al. 2012
;
Ghadami and Epureanu 2022
;
Bucci et al. 2023
. The modern wave of data-driven methods has been fueled by two trends: the rise of “Big Data” in the natural sciences
Hey et al. 2009
, and massive increases in computational power.
The history of machine learning is intertwined with these advances. Neural-network concepts date back to the Neocognitron
Fukushima 1980
, inspired by the visual cortex
Hubel and Wiesel 1962
; Hopfield networks introduced associative memory
Hopfield 1982
; Boltzmann machines
Ackley et al. 1985
laid the groundwork for modern architectures. A decisive leap came in 2012 with deep convolutional networks trained on the ImageNet dataset
Krizhevsky et al. 2017
, triggering rapid adoption of ML across disciplines.
This opportunity is clearly illustrated in biology, where advances in live-cell imaging, single-cell sequencing, and spatial transcriptomics now generate massive datasets describing molecular and cellular dynamics in space and time. These datasets are too complex for manual model derivation but ideally suited for modern machine learning, which can extract predictive structure even without full mechanistic knowledge
Alber et al. 2019
;
Hey et al. 2009
.
Such data come in two forms. In some cases, genuine time series are available, for example from live-cell imaging or longitudinal sampling. In other cases, most prominently single-cell sequencing, only population snapshots are obtained. Here, temporal information must be inferred rather than observed.
Pseudotime
methods address this challenge by assigning cells a latent temporal coordinate based on transcriptomic similarity, thus reconstructing differentiation or oscillatory progression trajectories from static data
Trapnell et al. 2014
;
Setty et al. 2016
;
Saelens et al. 2019
;
Alpert et al. 2022
. These approaches reveal cellular states and branching topologies when destructive sampling precludes direct time series.
In this review, however, we focus on methods that learn from true time-resolved data. Specifically, we discuss regression-, network-, and decomposition-based approaches that infer governing dynamical models rather than pseudotemporal orderings.

I.6
Scope of this review

In the following, we survey data-driven methods for analyzing dynamical systems, with an emphasis on oscillatory dynamics relevant to biology. We categorize existing methods into regression-based, network-based, and decomposition approaches, discussing their principles, representative algorithms, and limitations. As a unifying benchmark, we apply all methods to the Oregonator model (Eq. (
3
)), which captures essential nonlinear oscillatory behavior while remaining analytically and computationally tractable. Code for these examples can be found on Gitlab
Prokop and Gelens 2025
. Our goal is to provide researchers from biology, chemistry, and related fields with an overview of available tools, guidance on their applicability, and perspectives on future directions for data-driven model discovery in the natural sciences.

However, we do not cover probabilistic frameworks such as Bayesian inference or related approaches, which are already well established in biology and continue to offer important insights
Kwiatkowska et al. 2008
;
Jianyong Sun et al. 2012
. Instead, our focus is on complementary methodologies that emphasize dynamical structure, interpretability, and connections to Koopman theory, and that remain less familiar to many life science researchers despite their growing potential.

II
A unifying framework for data-driven methods in biological dynamical systems

II.1
From linear systems to operator thinking

When studying a dynamical system in the form of Eq. (
1
), linear systems are the simplest to analyze because they can be solved explicitly and decomposed into independent modes.
In vector form:

X_{t}(t)=\mathrm{A}X(t),

(5)

with solution

X(t+t_{0})=\mathrm{V}e^{\Lambda t}\mathrm{V}^{-1}X(t_{0}),

(6)

where
\mathrm{A}=\mathrm{V}\Lambda\mathrm{V}^{-1}
,
\Lambda
contains the eigenvalues
\lambda_{i}
, and
\mathrm{V}
the eigenvectors
v_{i}
.
Transforming to the eigenvector coordinates
\mathrm{z}=\mathrm{V}^{-1}X
decouples the dynamics:

z_{t,j}(t)=\lambda_{j}z_{j}(t),

(7)

so each mode evolves independently. The real parts of
\lambda_{i}
determine stability; imaginary parts set oscillation frequencies.

Box I: Example of Koopman linearization

To illustrate how Koopman theory transforms a nonlinear system into a linear one by lifting with polynomial functions
Iacob et al. 2023
, consider one of the simplest two-dimensional nonlinear system by Ref.
Brunton et al. 2022
:

\begin{split}x_{t,1}&=\mu x_{1},\\
x_{t,2}&=\delta(x_{2}-x_{1}^{2})\end{split}

(8)

with
\delta<\mu<0
.
The quadratic terms
x_{1}^{2}
and
x_{1}
prevent direct diagonalization in
(x_{1},x_{2})
coordinates.

If we augment the state with a new observable
y_{3}=x_{1}^{2}
and define
\mathrm{y}=(x_{1},x_{2},y_{3})
, the system becomes

\mathrm{y}_{t}=\begin{pmatrix}\mu&0&0\\
0&\delta&-\delta\\
0&0&2\mu\end{pmatrix}\mathrm{y},

(9)

which is fully linear in the augmented coordinates.
Here, the nonlinear interaction has been absorbed into an extra dimension, revealing a structure that can be analyzed with standard linear tools.

Most biological systems are nonlinear and cannot be diagonalized in this way.
However, Koopman operator theory offers a unifying perspective: any nonlinear system can be recast as a linear system in a (possibly infinite-dimensional or Hilbert) space of observables.
Originally developed by Koopman and von Neumann in the 1930s
Koopman 1931
;
Koopman and Neumann 1932
, this approach defines functions
g(X)
whose evolution is governed by a linear operator
\mathcal{K}
:

\mathrm{Z}_{t}=\mathcal{K}\mathrm{Z},\quad\mathrm{Z}=g(X).

(10)

If a finite set of observables captures all relevant dynamics, we can apply the same eigen-analysis as for a purely linear system.
The central challenge for data-driven approaches is to discover such observables automatically from data. Figure
2
a illustrates this transformation in schematic form, and Fig.
2
b highlights how many modern algorithms can be interpreted as finite-dimensional approximations to
\mathcal{K}
(for examples see Info Box
II.1
).

Koopman theory provides a conceptual bridge between classical linear analysis and modern data-driven approaches.
Most methods for studying nonlinear dynamical systems can be interpreted as different strategies for approximating the action of
\mathcal{K}
, whether explicitly, implicitly, or in restricted subspaces of observables.

In this review, we categorize data-driven methods primarily by their underlying methodology:

regression-based approaches
,
network-based architectures
, and
linearization or decomposition techniques
.
This categorization is intentional. Other classification schemes exist, such as distinguishing between
white-box
(mechanistic) and
black-box
(phenomenological) models, or between supervised and unsupervised learning. However, these often miss the methodological constraints and opportunities that come with approximating
\mathcal{K}
. In white-box models, parameters and structure have a clear physical or biochemical meaning, allowing for mechanistic interpretation. In black-box models, parameters are tuned for predictive accuracy, often at the expense of interpretability. Supervised learning maps known inputs to outputs, while unsupervised learning instead searches for latent structure or dynamics in raw data.

Moreover, interpretability is not determined solely by whether a model is labeled as white-box or black-box.
For example, a sparse regression model (often considered white-box) can yield uninterpretable results if applied to high-dimensional, noisy data with an ill-chosen basis; conversely, a neural-network-based approach (typically called black-box) can be made interpretable by constraining architectures or incorporating physics-informed features
Shiguihara et al. 2021
;
Kitson et al. 2023
.
Thus, by focusing on methodology rather than philosophical labels, we gain a clearer view of each approach’s strengths and limitations (see Table
1
) in addressing the core aspects introduced before: forecasting, interaction inference, and state identification (Fig.
1
F).

Regression-based methods

use regression to test whether symbolic model equations or inferred connections between variables can explain observed data. Unlike classical modeling, where equations are specified
a priori
, these methods select or refine equations during the fitting process. Their symbolic form often aids interpretability, but performance can degrade in high-dimensional settings due to the curse of dimensionality.

Network-based methods

employ architectures such as neural or Bayesian networks
Shiguihara et al. 2021
;
Kitson et al. 2023
to capture complex nonlinear relationships. They often learn suitable observables to approximate
\mathcal{K}
in high-dimensional latent spaces, producing accurate forecasts, but sometimes limiting direct interpretability.

Decomposition methods

apply mathematical transformations to extract dominant spatio-temporal modes directly from data, without requiring explicit governing equations. Many of these modes correspond to Koopman modes, enabling low-dimensional or linear representations of otherwise complex, nonlinear systems.

In the following, we examine representative methods from each category, applied to synthetic data from the Oregonator model introduced earlier in the introduction.
The Oregonator serves as a well-studied (bio)chemical oscillator with dimensionality and nonlinearity features that mirror common challenges in biological systems: hidden variables, multiple coupled time scales, and nonlinear feedbacks.

III
Regression-based methods

Regression-based approaches are among the most widely used data-driven tools for identifying dynamical systems.
They aim to infer how changes in one measured variable can be explained by others through explicit functional relationships
Brunton and Kutz 2022
.
In general, the regression problem can be expressed as:

Y=f(X,\beta),

(11)

where
Y
are observations,
X
the independent variables (often past values of system states), and
\beta
model parameters.
The task is to select a model structure
f
and parameters
\beta
such that
f(X)
best predicts the observed data (see Info Box
III
).

Box II: A primer on regression

Regression methods minimize the mismatch between data and model predictions, typically using error norms such as

\displaystyle E_{2}

\displaystyle=\left(\tfrac{1}{n}\sum_{k}|f(x_{k})-y_{k}|^{2}\right)^{1/2}\quad\text{(least squares)},

(12)

\displaystyle E_{1}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{n}\sum_{k}|f(x_{k})-y_{k}|\quad\text{(mean absolute error)},

(13)

\displaystyle E_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\max_{k}|f(x_{k})-y_{k}|\quad\text{(maximum error)}.

(14)

Each metric emphasizes different aspects:
\ell_{2}
minimizes average error and is most common;
\ell_{1}
is more robust to outliers;
\ell_{\infty}
targets worst-case deviations, relevant in control.

For example, fitting
f(x_{k})=\beta_{2}x_{k}+\beta_{1}
to clean data yields similar fits across all norms.
With an outlier,
\ell_{2}
is distorted,
\ell_{\infty}
focuses on the outlier, and
\ell_{1}
remains comparatively robust.
Thus, regression outcomes depend not only on model structure
f
but also on the chosen error metric, which balances accuracy, robustness, and worst-case guarantees.

Regression-based approaches can be grouped into three classes: causality methods (e.g., Granger causality, GOBI), polynomial methods (NARMAX, SINDy), and evolutionary approaches (Symbolic Regression, AI-Feynman).

Building on this, we now turn to specific regression-based approaches used for data-driven model discovery in dynamical systems. Such methods can be grouped into three broad classes: those emphasizing
causal inference
, those relying on
polynomial bases
, and those employing
evolutionary algorithms
(see Fig.
3
).
Despite methodological differences, all regression-based approaches share two important characteristics.
First, they typically produce
symbolic and interpretable
models, either in the form of interaction networks or explicit differential equations.
Second, they cannot on their own identify whether all relevant state variables are included in the data, i.e., whether
X
truly spans the system’s underlying state space.
These shared strengths and limitations shape how regression-based methods can be applied in biological contexts, as we illustrate below.

III.1
Causal methods

Causality approaches aim to infer whether one variable helps explain the future of another
Wisdom 1960
.
In biology, this is often used to reconstruct interaction networks from time series, such as gene expression or ecological dynamics
Saint-Antoine and Singh 2020
.

A classical method is
Granger Causality
(GC)
Granger 1969
, which compares prediction accuracy with and without including past values of another variable.
Formally, a baseline model predicts future
Y
from its own history:

Y_{t}=\alpha_{0}+\alpha_{1}Y_{t-1}+\alpha_{2}Y_{t-2}+\dots+\eta_{t},

(15)

while an extended model adds past values of
X
:

Y_{t}=\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}Y_{t-1}+\dots+\gamma_{1}X_{t-1}+\gamma_{2}X_{t-2}+\mu_{t}.

(16)

If prediction improves,
X
is said to “Granger cause”
Y
.

Although simple and widely applied
Runge et al. 2019
, GC performs poorly in nonlinear, oscillatory, or synchronously coupled systems, where it can yield spurious full networks
Nawrath et al. 2010
;
Guo et al. 2008
;
Stokes and Purdon 2017
.

Recent alternatives address these issues.

General ODE-Based Inference
(GOBI)
Park et al. 2023
, for example, uses regulation-detection scores to prune false positives and extends to higher-order motifs (e.g.,
u\to w\leftarrow v
).
From a Koopman perspective, causal inference compares predictive subspaces in observable space, approximating parts of the operator
\mathcal{K}

Rupe et al. 2024
;
Di Antonio and Vinci 2024
.

III.2
Polynomial methods

Polynomial regression methods reconstruct dynamical equations directly.
Two key representatives are
Nonlinear Autoregressive Moving Average models with Exogenous Inputs
(NARMAX) and
Sparse Identification of Nonlinear Dynamics
(SINDy).

NARMAX.

Originally developed in control theory, the NARMAX inputs model fits discrete-time dynamics using predefined polynomial bases
Billings and Mao 1998
.
The general form is:

\displaystyle X_{k}

\displaystyle=F\!\Big(X_{k-1},\dots,X_{k-n_{x}},

\displaystyle\qquad\;Y_{k-1},\dots,Y_{k-n_{y}},

\displaystyle\qquad\;\eta_{k-1},\dots,\eta_{k-n_{\eta}}\Big)+\eta_{k}

(17)

where
F
is expanded in polynomial, rational, or radial basis functions
Chen et al. 1990
;
Hong et al. 2008
.
NARMAX prioritizes predictive accuracy and controllability over interpretability.
It has been widely used in biomechanics, physiology, and biochemical signal prediction
Kukreja et al. 2003
;
Khodadadi et al. 2023
;
Mendes and Kell 1998
;
Krishnanathan et al. 2012
.

SINDy.

SINDy
Brunton et al. 2016a
however emphasizes parsimony and interpretability.
It constructs a candidate library
\Theta(X)
and performs sparse regression:

X_{t}=\Theta(X)\xi.

(18)

The method assumes parsimony, meaning that most coefficients
\xi
are zero, identifying only a minimal subset.
This can be interpreted as approximating the Koopman generator, with
\Theta
serving as lifting functions
Brunton and Kutz 2022
.

SINDy has been applied in ecology
Hirsh et al. 2022
;
Fasel et al. 2022
;
Sandoz et al. 2023
;
dos Anjos et al. 2023
;
Gutierrez-Vilchis et al. 2023
;
Messenger et al. 2024
, cellular dynamics
Mangan et al. 2016
;
Messenger et al. 2022
;
Brummer et al. 2023
;
Naozuka et al. 2025
, and other domains, though mostly on synthetic rather than experimental data.

Box III: Polynomial regression with SINDy applied to oregonator data

To illustrate the opportunities and pitfalls of regression-based approaches, we examine the Sparse Identification of Nonlinear Dynamics (SINDy) method on data from the Oregonator model of the Belousov–Zhabotinsky reaction
Tyson 1977
. This system is a widely used testbed because it is nonlinear, oscillatory, and has a known ground-truth model.

Ideal conditions.
We first simulate the Oregonator with high temporal resolution (
\Delta t=0.001
) and no added noise. The term library contains all monomials up to order two,
\Theta=[u,v,w,u^{2},v^{2},w^{2},uv,uw,vw]
,
and we use ridge regression with a cutoff
\xi_{\text{thres}}=0.01
. Under these conditions, SINDy exactly recovers the original model:

\displaystyle\begin{split}u_{t}&=-42.241u-8198.303v-0.500u^{2}-99.979uv,\\
v_{t}&=-0.592u-83.999v+0.600w-1.000uv,\\
w_{t}&=0.333u-0.333w,\end{split}

(19)

and simulations of the inferred equations reproduce the true dynamics beyond the training data range.

Adding noise.
With 5% Gaussian noise, identification fails: the inferred equations no longer reproduce the oscillations. The reason is that SINDy performs regression on the estimated derivatives
X_{t}
, which strongly amplifies measurement noise in
X
. Numerous strategies have been proposed to mitigate this issue, including smoothed differentiation schemes and noise-aware extensions of SINDy
Delahunt and Kutz 2022
;
Cortiella et al. 2022
;
Prokop and Gelens 2024
;
Messenger and Bortz 2021
;
Fasel et al. 2022
. Nevertheless, high-quality, temporally resolved data remain a strict requirement in practice.

Partial observability.
In real experiments, not all system variables are measurable. For the BZ reaction, only
v
and
w
are typically accessible. Restricting SINDy to these two variables with a second-order library fails to reproduce the oscillatory dynamics. Even when extending the library up to the 8
th
order, the method cannot approximate the true dynamics. From a Koopman perspective, this failure occurs because the necessary lifting functions are missing: without the unobserved state
u
, the system cannot be properly linearized in observable space.

Incomplete libraries.
Finally, when all three state variables are available but the library only includes first-order terms
\Theta=[u,v,w]
, the inferred model again fails. Without nonlinear terms, the algorithm lacks the expressive power to approximate the Koopman operator.

In summary, polynomial methods like SINDy provide interpretable models and are particularly powerful when domain knowledge informs the basis function selection.
Yet, they still depend on predefined libraries, which can limit discovery in highly nonlinear biological systems.
A further limitation is their sensitivity to data quality: because regression is typically performed on estimated derivatives, noise or insufficient temporal resolution can severely distort the results.
Several studies have proposed strategies to mitigate these issues, such as improved differentiation schemes and noise-aware extensions of SINDy
Delahunt and Kutz 2022
;
Cortiella et al. 2022
;
Prokop and Gelens 2024
;
Messenger and Bortz 2021
.
We illustrate these challenges in more detail in Info Box
III.2
, where SINDy is tested on the Oregonator model under increasingly realistic experimental conditions.

III.3
Evolutionary methods

Polynomial regression methods rely on predefined functional libraries, which can limit their ability to capture unknown dynamics.
Evolutionary algorithms, in particular
Symbolic Regression
(SR), overcome this by simultaneously discovering both the structure and parameters of candidate models
Eiben and Smith 2015
;
Schmidt and Lipson 2009
.
In SR, models are encoded as hierarchical
expression trees
(see Fig.
3
C), where internal nodes represent operators (e.g.,
+,\times,\sin
) and leaves correspond to variables or constants.
These trees evolve over successive generations through three main operations:
mutation
(replacing a subtree with a randomly generated one),
recombination
(swapping subtrees between two parents), and
selection
(retaining models with lower prediction error or higher parsimony).
This evolutionary search enables the discovery of both novel structures and parameter values without relying on a fixed functional library.

The flexibility of SR makes it powerful, but also computationally expensive, prone to overfitting, and sensitive to noise due to the vast search space.
Recent developments have sought to mitigate these challenges by incorporating domain-specific constraints. A prominent example is
AI-Feynman

Udrescu and Tegmark 2020
, which uses physical priors such as dimensional consistency, parsimony, compositionality, symmetry, and separability to restrict the search. By narrowing the solution space in this way, AI-Feynman was able to rediscover dozens of canonical physics equations directly from data, demonstrating how carefully chosen constraints can both accelerate convergence and enhance interpretability.

In biology, such universal rules are harder to define, but analogous constraints can be introduced by exploiting biochemical priors, conservation laws, or known regulatory motifs. Applications of SR in biology already span gene regulatory and biochemical networks
Beauregard et al. 2019
;

, ecological dynamics
Chen et al. 2019
;
Haldar et al. 2024
, and mechanistic tissue models
Hou et al. 2024
, though the success of these approaches often depends on constraining the search with system-specific knowledge. Conceptually, SR can be seen as approximating Koopman lifting functions in a highly flexible but relatively unstructured way, trading generality for computational efficiency. When guided by appropriate priors, however, evolutionary methods hold particular promise for interpretable model discovery in complex biological systems.

III.4
Limitations in biological applications

Across subclasses, regression-based methods face three recurring challenges:

1.

Strict data requirements:
derivative estimation amplifies noise; dense, high-resolution time series are often essential. Biological data often violate these assumptions.

2.

Dependence on prior knowledge:
success depends on access to all relevant state variables and informed choices of libraries or operator sets. Without such priors, spurious or uninterpretable models often result.

3.

Scaling issues:
high-dimensional biological networks exacerbate the curse of dimensionality and limit applicability.

These challenges are not merely theoretical but are consistently observed in practice. For instance, applying SINDy to the Oregonator model shows that under ideal conditions—complete observability, sufficiently rich libraries, and low-noise data—the method can recover the governing equations exactly. However, as conditions become more realistic, with limited observability, noisy measurements, or incomplete prior knowledge, SINDy rapidly fails to reproduce the dynamics
Prokop and Gelens 2024
. This underscores that successful regression-based discovery in biology hinges critically on data quality, careful feature selection, and substantial domain expertise (for more details see Table
2
).

Thus, while regression-based discovery has excelled in proof-of-concept and low-dimensional systems, its application to realistic biological complexity remains challenging. Hybrid approaches that combine regression with mechanistic priors or dimensionality reduction hold promise for overcoming these limitations.

III.5
Other regression-based methods

Beyond polynomial expansions and sparse regression, several other machine learning techniques have been applied to dynamical systems.
Kernel methods such as
support vector machines
(SVMs) or kernel-based NARMAX extend linear regression into nonlinear feature spaces via kernel functions (e.g., Gaussian or polynomial kernels)
Suykens 2001
;
Suthaharan 2016
;
Chen et al. 2004
.
In this way, SVMs or other kernel methods can model nonlinear state-to-state mappings
Gilpin et al. 2020
and have been used for forecasting and classification of dynamical regimes
Ben-Hur et al. 2008
, including inference of specifically gene regulatory
Gillani et al. 2014
or generally biological (metabolic, gene-gene interaction etc.) networks
Vert et al. 2007
.
Similarly,
Gaussian process regression
(GPR) provides a flexible, nonparametric Bayesian framework for learning dynamical systems from data
Rasmussen and Williams 2005
.
GPR models offer probabilistic predictions with uncertainty quantification and have been applied in systems biology to capture noisy dynamics of gene networks and signaling pathways
Gao et al. 2008
;
Kristjanson Duvenaud 2014
;
Frigola et al. 2014
.
However, both SVMs and GPR typically act as black-box predictors rather than interpretable dynamical models, and they scale poorly to very high-dimensional or long time-series data.
As such, they are often used as complementary tools for model identification tasks.

IV
Network-based methods

Regression-based methods (Sec.
III
) rely on explicit symbolic structures to represent dynamics.
By contrast, network-based methods take a different approach:
artificial neural networks
(ANNs) serve as flexible, high-dimensional function approximators capable of capturing nonlinear dynamics directly from data.
Their strength lies in expressivity: they can approximate arbitrarily complex mappings without predefined equations, making them well-suited for systems where governing rules are unknown or partially observed.
This flexibility comes at a cost, however, since neural networks typically act as “black boxes,” demanding large amounts of data and offering limited interpretability.
Where regression methods produce explicit equations (e.g. sparse polynomials in SINDy), neural networks discover latent representations that may or may not align with interpretable observables.
As in the regression case, this distinction can be understood through Koopman operator theory: neural networks can be viewed as learning finite-dimensional approximations of Koopman embeddings, but often without explicit interpretability.

Box IV: A primer on neural networks

The basic architecture of a neural network (NN) consists of input nodes, hidden layers, and output nodes. Here we show an example of a fully connected feed-forward neural network with two hidden layers.

In the linear case, each layer corresponds to a matrix multiplication:

\displaystyle X^{(1)}

\displaystyle=A_{1}X,

(20a)

\displaystyle X^{(2)}

\displaystyle=A_{2}X^{(1)},

(20b)

\displaystyle Y

\displaystyle=A_{3}X^{(2)},

(20c)

leading to the composite mapping

Y=A_{3}A_{2}A_{1}X,

(21)

or, more generally,

Y=\prod_{j=1}^{l}A_{j}X.

(22)

Since the composition of linear functions is linear, such networks cannot represent nonlinear systems.
Nonlinear activation functions overcome this limitation:

\displaystyle f_{a}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1+e^{-x}}

\displaystyle\text{(sigmoid)},

(23a)

\displaystyle f_{a}(x)

\displaystyle=\tanh(x)

\displaystyle\text{(tanh)},

(23b)

\displaystyle f_{a}(x)

\displaystyle\text{(ReLU)}.

(23c)

With these, the network becomes a nested nonlinear function:

Y=f_{a,l}\big(A_{l}\cdot f_{a,l-1}(A_{l-1}\cdots f_{a,1}(A_{1}X))\big).

(24)

Training consists of optimizing the weights
A_{l}
by minimizing a loss function, typically

\mathcal{L}=\left\|\textit{NN}(X)-Y\right\|^{2}_{2},

(25)

with regularization (e.g.,
\ell_{1}

The universal approximation theorem guarantees that sufficiently large NNs can approximate any continuous function

feed-forward neural networks

IV.1
Feed-forward neural networks

Among neural architectures (Fig.
4
), the FFNN is the simplest, mapping a current state
X_{k}
to the next state
X_{k+1}

Narendra and Parthasarathy 1992
;
Suykens et al. 1996
.
Formally, the loss function is

\mathcal{L}=\left\|X_{k+1}-\mathit{NN}(X_{k})\right\|_{2}^{2},

(26)

so that the network approximates the evolution operator
\mathrm{F}
.

FFNNs have been applied to oscillatory and chaotic systems
Sandberg et al. 2001
;
Chattopadhyay et al. 2020
and, in biology, to brain development timing
Nagarajan and Jonkman 2013
, gene regulatory networks
Choo et al. 2020
;
Chen and Li 2022
, and intercellular signaling
Nilsson et al. 2022
.
From a Koopman perspective, FFNNs can be viewed as implicitly discovering nonlinear observables, akin to polynomial libraries in SINDy but in a black-box manner.

A common limitation is error accumulation over long trajectories, sometimes leading to implausible predictions such as negative concentrations

;

;

.
Extensions such as physics-informed (PINNs)

Another direction uses FFNNs not for forecasting but for uncovering static geometric features of phase space.
The CLINE method (Computational Learning and Identification of Nullclines)

trains a FFNN to approximate inverse relations of the form

\displaystyle\begin{split}u_{t}&=f(u,v)\rightarrow u=f_{u}^{-1}(v,u_{t}),\\
v_{t}&=g(u,v)\rightarrow u=g_{u}^{-1}(v,v_{t}).,\end{split}

(27)

using measured trajectories and their derivatives.
By evaluating conditions such as

or

, CLINE recovers nullcline structures directly from data, without requiring a mechanistic model.
Because nullclines define the intersections and stability structure of a system’s phase portrait, this approach provides mechanistic insights into dynamics that go beyond time-series prediction.

IV.2
Recurrent neural networks

RNNs extend FFNNs by introducing loops that provide memory, making them natural tools for temporal and sequential data

.
Each hidden state
h_{k}
depends on both the current input
X_{k}
and the previous hidden state
h_{k-1}
:

\displaystyle h_{k}

\displaystyle=f_{a,1}(W_{x}X_{k}+W_{h}h_{k-1}+b_{h}),

(28a)

\displaystyle X_{k+1}

\displaystyle=f_{a,2}(W_{\text{out}}h_{k}+b_{\text{out}}),

(28b)

where
W_{x}
,
W_{h}
, and
W_{\text{out}}
are trainable weight matrices and
f_{a}
are activation functions.
This recursive structure allows RNNs to capture dependencies across time, unlike FFNNs which only map one state to the next.

RNNs have been applied to chaotic attractors
Pathak et al. 2017
, oscillatory systems
Cestnik and Abel 2019
, and system identification tasks
Vlachas et al. 2018
.
In biology, they have been used to model gene regulatory dynamics
Jia et al. 2019

Baranwal et al. 2022
, and microbial population behavior

;
Vidal-Saez and Garcia-Ojalvo 2025
.

A widely used special case is
reservoir computing
(RC), in which the recurrent reservoir is fixed and only the output weights are trained:

\displaystyle h_{k}

\displaystyle=f_{a}(W_{\text{in}}X_{k}+W_{\text{res}}h_{k-1}),

(29a)

\displaystyle X_{k+1}

\displaystyle=W_{\text{out}}h_{k}.

(29b)

Because only

is optimized, RC reduces computational cost and avoids instability while retaining expressive power

;
Tanaka et al. 2019
;
Gauthier et al. 2021
.
From a Koopman perspective, both RNNs and RCs can be interpreted as flexible ways of encoding time-delay coordinates, analogous to explicit lag terms in regression-based approaches.

To illustrate opportunities and pitfalls of FFNNs, we train a network on synthetic data from the Oregonator model aiming to reproduce the oscillatory dynamics.

Ideal conditions.
Training a network with three hidden layers (128 nodes each, ReLU activation) on high-resolution data from the Oregonator model we are able to reproduce the qualitative dynamical behavior Within the training domain.

Different initial conditions.
However, it fails to generalize to unseen initial conditions that lie outside the training domain.
Thus, the network is only able to reproduce the dynamics within a narrow set of conditions, requiring additional measurements to capture the general dynamics of a system.

Different architectural choices.
Additionally, using different architectural choices affects the ability of the network to reproduce the dynamics.
Using a different activation energy (ReLU to Tanh) or reducing the size of the network disallows to capture any of the oscillatory features of the Oregonator.

With linear activations, this reduces to a

(PCA)

.

In dynamical systems, AEs provide a way to reduce high-dimensional dynamics into low-dimensional latent coordinates, or to approximate Koopman eigenfunctions

;
Sondak and Protopapas 2021
.
Applications in biology include cell fate dynamics
Maizels et al. 2024

Wehmeyer and Noé 2018
, and high-dimensional omics data
Liu et al. 2021
.

A prominent extension is the
variational autoencoder
(VAE)

, which assigns a probabilistic prior to the latent space:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{VAE}}=\mathbb{E}_{q(Z|X)}[\|X-\hat{X}\|^{2}]+\beta\,D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q(Z|X)\|p(Z)),

(32)

where

This enforces smooth latent spaces and promotes generalizable embeddings that can approximate Koopman-invariant coordinates

;

.

As with regression-based polynomial expansions, the latent variables discovered by AEs or VAEs can be seen as approximate lifting functions.
The main advantage is that these are learned directly from data rather than specified a priori.
The drawback is sensitivity to latent dimensionality: too few dimensions miss essential modes, while too many cause overfitting

;
Zeng et al. 2023
.

IV.4
Limitations and outlook

Three major challenges persist across NN-based methods:

1.

Lack of interpretability:
the high-dimensional embeddings rarely align with mechanistic biological meaning, in contrast to regression-derived symbolic models.

2.

Dependence on architecture:
performance depends strongly on architecture and hyperparameters, often requiring costly trial-and-error.

3.

Limited generalization:
generalization beyond the training domain is weak, particularly with noisy or undersampled biological data.

To highlight these challenges in more detail, we tested the ability of a FFNN to capture the oscillatory dynamics of the Oregonator model in Info Box
IV.3
.

Domain-informed variants such as PINNs, BINNs, and Koopman-inspired VAEs offer one path forward, balancing flexibility with interpretability — though at the cost of introducing strong priors.
Taken together, regression and network-based methods represent complementary ends of a spectrum: the former emphasizes symbolic interpretability, the latter emphasizes flexible approximation.
Both can be unified under the Koopman framework, but each has distinct strengths and weaknesses for biological applications.

IV.5
Other neural methods

Beyond the architectures discussed above, several additional neural approaches are increasingly relevant for modeling dynamical systems.

Neural ODEs
replace discrete mappings with continuous-time formulations, embedding differential equations directly into the network structure and enabling end-to-end training of time-continuous models
Chen et al. 2018
.

Graph neural networks
(GNNs) extend standard architectures to relational data, making them suitable for systems with explicit interaction graphs such as gene regulatory or protein–protein interaction networks
Sanchez-Gonzalez et al. 2018
;
Bronstein et al. 2021
.
Transformer-based architectures are also being explored for dynamical prediction, where self-attention mechanisms capture long-range temporal dependencies more effectively than recurrent units
Vaswani et al. 2017
;
Zhang et al. 2023
.
While these methods are more established in the machine learning community, their application to biology is still emerging.
They therefore complement FFNNs, RNNs, and AEs by providing powerful alternatives for modeling dynamical systems, particularly when complex interactions or long-range dependencies play a key role.

V
Decomposition methods

After showing that NNs can approximate the Koopman operator via high-dimensional embeddings (Sec.
IV
), we now turn to decomposition-based methods that allow for a more direct approximation of the Koopman operator from measured data.
The most widely used is
Dynamic Mode Decomposition
(DMD), introduced by Schmid
et al.

Schmid 2010
to extract spatio-temporally coherent structures from high-dimensional data, originally in fluid dynamics
Brunton and Kutz 2022
.
Conceptually, DMD is related to
proper orthogonal decomposition
(POD)
Berkooz et al. 1993
, which identifies dominant spatial structures, but DMD additionally encodes their temporal evolution.
Rowley and Mezić
et al.

Rowley et al. 2009
showed that DMD can be interpreted as a finite-dimensional approximation of the Koopman operator using only measured state variables.

V.1
Dynamic Mode Decomposition

The core idea in DMD is to analyze how successive snapshots of the system state are related.
Given two sets of snapshots,

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}X(t_{1})&X(t_{2})&\dots&X(t_{m-1})\end{bmatrix},\quad

(33)

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}X(t_{2})&X(t_{3})&\dots&X(t_{m})\end{bmatrix},

(34)

DMD seeks a linear operator
A
such that

\mathcal{X}^{\prime}\approx A\mathcal{X},

(35)

or, with equidistant sampling,

X_{k+1}\approx AX_{k}.

(36)

The operator
A
thus provides a finite-dimensional approximation of the Koopman operator
\mathcal{K}
.
Computationally, one solves

A=\operatorname*{argmin}_{A}\left\|\mathcal{X}^{\prime}-A\mathcal{X}\right\|_{F}=\mathcal{X}^{\prime}\mathcal{X}^{\dagger},

(37)

where
\mathcal{X}^{\dagger}
is the Moore–Penrose pseudo-inverse.

For high-dimensional systems with
n\gg m
, computing the full
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is intractable.
Instead, one projects onto a reduced system
\tilde{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
obtained from the
singular value decomposition
(SVD) of
\mathcal{X}
.
Tu
et al.

Tu et al. 2014
demonstrated that
\tilde{A}
faithfully captures the essential eigenvalues of
A
, making the method computationally feasible.
The number of retained modes
r
must be chosen carefully: too few lose dynamical information, too many amplify noise.

A key limitation of (exact) DMD is that it relies on linear observables, which rarely suffice for nonlinear dynamics.

Extended DMD
(eDMD)
Williams et al. 2015
overcomes this by lifting the data into a higher-dimensional feature space via nonlinear functions
g
, and applying DMD there:

g(X_{k+1})\approx Kg(X_{k}),

(38)

where
K
provides a finite-dimensional approximation of the Koopman operator in the lifted space.
This approach parallels the role of basis libraries in regression-based methods such as SINDy (Sec.
III
).

In biological contexts, both DMD and eDMD have been applied to neural dynamics in healthy
Brunton et al. 2016b
and epileptic
Karabiber Cura and Akan 2021
states, microbial ecosystems
Balakrishnan et al. 2020
, metabolic dynamics
Skantze et al. 2023
, blood flow
Habibi et al. 2020
, and tumor growth
Bourantas et al. 2014
.
These studies highlight the potential of decomposition approaches to reveal coherent spatio-temporal patterns from complex biological data.

V.2
Limitations of decomposition methods

Despite their appeal, decomposition approaches face two main challenges in biological settings:

1.

Requirement for sufficiently rich dynamics.

As DMD and eDMD fit global linear operators, the data must capture diverse dynamical regimes to yield meaningful approximations.
When dynamics are dominated by switching, noise, or short transients — common in biological datasets — these methods fail to generalize.
While noise-robust variants exist
Chen et al. 2012
;
Hemati et al. 2017
, they still require high temporal resolution rarely available in biology.

2.

Dependence on prior knowledge for interpretability.

The success of eDMD depends on choosing appropriate nonlinear lifting functions
g
, a challenge shared with regression approaches like SINDy.
Without careful selection, the resulting modes may lack mechanistic meaning, undermining biological interpretability.

We evaluate these challenges in more detail by applying eDMD to the Oregonator model in Info Box
V.3
.
In summary, decomposition methods such as DMD and eDMD provide a principled way to approximate the Koopman operator directly from data and have demonstrated utility in biological systems.
However, their reliance on rich data and appropriate lifting functions limits their interpretability and robustness, particularly in noisy, nonlinear biological contexts.

V.3
Other decomposition methods

While DMD and eDMD dominate the literature, other decomposition techniques are also relevant for dynamical systems.
POD provides low-rank spatial modes but ignores temporal evolution, making it useful as a preprocessing tool for dimensionality reduction
Berkooz et al. 1993
.

Nonnegative matrix factorization
(NMF)
Lee and Seung 1999
has been applied to extract interpretable, additive components in gene expression dynamics and imaging data
Devarajan 2008
;
Stein-O’Brien et al. 2018
.
Tensor decomposition methods, such as
Tucker decomposition
or the related CANDECOMP/PARAFAC
Tucker 1966
, extend these ideas to multiway data (space–time–condition) and are increasingly used for multi-omics trajectories
Kolda and Bader 2009
.
These approaches complement DMD by offering different trade-offs between interpretability, temporal structure, and computational cost.

Closely related to DMD are decomposition techniques that not only aim to reconstruct the system’s dynamical behavior but also provide meaningful interpretations of the recovered dimensions, following the Takens’ embedding theorem
Takens 1981
.

Box VI: Extended DMD on Oregonator data

We evaluate decomposition approaches on Oregonator trajectories using eDMD evaluating its ability to reproduce the oscillatory behavior.

Ideal conditions.
When applying eDMD to high-resolution data covering multiple periods and sufficient lifting (polynomial lifting up to sixth order with 67 dimensions used), eDMD captures the qualitative dynamical behavior.

Insufficient lifting.
However, when not enough lifting functions are provided, e.g. with polynomial lifting up to 3 order that includes all terms included in the Oregonator model, eDMD fails to reproduce the oscillations.

Low quality data.
When applying eDMD on just a few periods with low resolution, a scenario often encountered when observing biological systems, and despite a sufficient amount of lifting dimensions, eDMD fails.

Added noise.
The ability to reproduce dynamics with eDMD is limited when providing noisy, high-quality data with the exact same set up as in ideal conditions. The introduction of noise hinders the identification of relevant dynamical modes in the measured data.

According to the theorem, all the information of a dynamical system is encoded in each measurement, meaning that even unobserved dimensions can be unfolded and a unique measurement-based coordinate system defined, for example, using the
Hankel Alternative View of Koopman
(HAVOK) method
Brunton et al. 2017
;
Champion et al. 2019
. Such approaches are particularly useful when obtaining closed representations of chaotic systems via eDMD proves challenging. In principle, they allow reconstruction of missing state variables from partial observations, a scenario frequently encountered in biological systems. Identifying suitable embeddings from experimental data, however, is nontrivial
Tan et al. 2023
, though it has already facilitated the application of SINDy in studies of tumor growth dynamics
Brummer et al. 2023
.

VI
Future of data-driven methods for dynamical systems in biology

With this, we have provided an overview of existing data-driven methods for studying dynamical systems.
Yet all of these approaches exhibit certain limitations that can hinder their application to real-world experimental data, particularly in biology.
This raises important questions: what is the future of data-driven science in the study of (biological) dynamical systems?
And what are the most promising recent developments?

Figure 5:
Examples of hybrid methods integrating regression- and network-based approaches.
UDEs combine mechanistic equations with NNs; SDL translates NN structures into symbolic expressions; CLINE extracts nullclines which can be turned into equations via SINDy or SR; and SINDy with AEs balances interpretability with dimensionality reduction.

VI.1
Experimental frontiers enabling data-driven modeling

Some of the most pressing limitations arise not from methodology but from experimental constraints.
Biological systems are often high-dimensional, noisy, and only partially observable.
Advances in microscopy now offer ever higher spatio-temporal resolution, enabling detailed observations of cellular and subcellular processes
Balasubramanian et al. 2023
.
Novel sensors, including fluorescent reporters and FRET-based probes, further enhance the specificity and sensitivity of dynamic biochemical measurements
Hickey et al. 2022
;
Specht et al. 2017
.
Similarly, innovations in multi-omics promise richer molecular time series, although current techniques remain too slow and often destructive, complicating temporal reconstruction
Bressan et al. 2023
;
Velten and Stegle 2023
. Recent pipelines such as DANSE (Dynamic Analysis of multi-omics data) have begun to address this challenge by explicitly integrating time-resolved and multi-omics measurements into unified modeling frameworks, providing practical tools for high-dimensional dynamic inference
Jansen Klomp et al. 2025
.
Another emerging direction is the use of synthetic surrogates: minimal artificial cells that reproduce aspects of biological dynamics while offering full experimental control
Buddingh’ and Van Hest 2017
;
Jiang et al. 2022
.

These experimental advances will generate increasingly complex datasets.
The challenge for data-driven methods is to use them effectively, motivating approaches that can both handle high-dimensional data and produce interpretable insight.

VI.2
Pseudotime-based analyses: strengths and caveats

An additional class of approaches, widely used in single-cell biology, infers temporal orderings from static population-level data.

Pseudotime
methods assign cells a latent temporal coordinate based on transcriptomic similarity, thereby reconstructing trajectories of differentiation or oscillatory progression when true longitudinal measurements are unavailable
Trapnell et al. 2014
;
Setty et al. 2016
.
Extensions such as RNA velocity
La Manno et al. 2018
and optimal-transport trajectory analysis
Schiebinger et al. 2019
provide directionality and coarse rate information.
These approaches have become indispensable for mapping cell states, branching topologies, and rare transitions, especially in developmental contexts.

From the perspective of this review, pseudotime excels at identifying dynamical states (Fig.
1
F), but is much weaker for forecasting and characterizing interactions.
Because pseudotime is not physical time, derivatives are ill-posed and regression-based or decomposition methods that rely on evenly spaced time series (e.g., SINDy, DMD) cannot be directly applied.
Similarly, mechanistic inference is limited: pseudotime primarily recovers topology rather than explicit governing equations or reaction rates.
Nevertheless, pseudotime can serve as a valuable complement by highlighting where dynamic changes occur, thereby guiding targeted acquisition of true time series data for downstream mechanistic modeling.

The field of pseudotime analysis is rapidly evolving, with close to a hundred tools developed to date.
Saelens
et al.

Saelens et al. 2019
benchmarked 45 such methods across hundreds of real and synthetic datasets, concluding that no single approach is universally optimal.
Instead, performance depends strongly on dataset size, dimensionality, and trajectory topology.
Thus, while we do not evaluate pseudotime methods in the same way as regression-, network-, or decomposition-based approaches, we note that they provide a complementary entry point to dynamical analysis when destructive sampling prevents longitudinal measurements.

VI.3
Hybrid methods: combining strengths

A clear trend in recent years has been the integration of regression-based and network-based methods.
As highlighted in Table
3
, these two groups are complementary: regression emphasizes interpretability but struggles with high-dimensional data, while neural networks excel in flexibility but often act as black boxes.
Hybrid methods aim to exploit the strengths of each.

Other combinations are possible, although explicit integrations with decomposition methods remain rare.
Instead, decomposition is often embedded within network architectures such as autoencoders (Sec.
V
), forming the basis of many modern hybrids
Lusch et al. 2018
;
Iwata and Kawahara 2020
.
Initial applications to biological systems have shown promising results
Yuan et al. 2021
.
Here we highlight several representative directions (Fig.
5
).

Universal Differential Equations

Universal differential equations (UDEs)
Rackauckas et al. 2020
directly embed neural networks into mechanistic models.
Known components of a system are described by classical differential equations
f_{\mathrm{m}}
, while unknown or poorly understood parts are represented by a NN:

X_{t}=f_{\text{m}}(X,\beta_{\text{m}})+\textit{NN}(X,\beta_{\text{NN}}).

(39)

This allows training end-to-end while retaining temporal consistency.
In biology, UDEs are attractive because many systems are partially characterized: known feedback loops can be modeled explicitly, while residual terms capture missing biology
Philipps et al. 2024
.
Applications range from gene regulatory networks
Kana and Bhattacharya 2021
to epidemic dynamics
Rojas-Campos et al. 2023
;
Kuwahara and Bauch 2024
and ecological interactions
Buckner et al. 2024
.

Symbolic Deep Learning

Symbolic deep learning (SDL)
Cranmer et al. 2020
pursues a different strategy: rather than embedding NNs in equations, it translates trained networks into symbolic expressions.
Typically, GNN first learns interaction rules; then symbolic regression (e.g., pySR) extracts interpretable equations.
This approach has been applied to biochemical oscillations
Boddupalli et al. 2023
;
Ahmadi Daryakenari et al. 2024
, population dynamics
Boddupalli et al. 2023
, and pharmacokinetics
Ahmadi Daryakenari et al. 2024
, combining the representational power of NNs with the interpretability of symbolic models.

CLINE-SINDy/SR

Recently, CLINE has been proposed as a model-free method to uncover geometric phase-space features such as nullclines
Prokop et al. 2025
.
Once identified, these structures can be converted into explicit equations via SINDy (with suitable libraries) or symbolic regression (requiring fewer priors).
This reduces the task from full temporal fitting to geometric curve fitting, improving robustness.
CLINE-SR has demonstrated the ability to identify strongly nonlinear functions using experimental biochemical data
Prokop et al. 2025
, highlighting the potential of hybrid nullcline-based approaches.

SINDy with Autoencoders

SINDy struggles with high-dimensional data, while AEs excel at compressing dynamics into lower dimensions.
Their combination
Bakarji et al. 2022
;
Bakarji et al. 2023
leverages both: AEs discover reduced coordinates, and SINDy identifies interpretable governing equations within that latent space.
This balance has been used, for example, to analyze
C. elegans
locomotion
Voina et al. 2024
.
Additionally, this approach can be combined with methods aimed at uncovering missing state variables — for instance, delay embedding techniques suggested by Takens
Takens 1981
— which reconstruct the high-dimensional system from limited observations.
Such integrations make SINDy–AE frameworks particularly attractive for biological and biochemical systems where measurements are sparse and incomplete.

Beyond these frameworks, other emerging approaches combine neural architectures and dynamical landscapes. For example, neural flow maps extend neural ODEs toward improved time-series modeling in biological systems
Xu et al. 2025
, while recent work on reconstructing Waddington-type landscapes directly from data
Cislo et al. 2025
;
Fontaine et al. 2025
highlights how high-dimensional learning can be linked to classical concepts in developmental biology.

VI.4
Future targets of data-driven methods in biology

What, then, should be the goal of data-driven methods for dynamical systems in biology?
As discussed in Section
I.4
, the primary objective is to enable a deeper understanding of dynamical systems in the context of underlying biological processes.
This understanding may relate to the ability to forecast system behavior, uncover hidden interactions, or determine the relevant state variables in a biological system.

Most of the methods presented throughout this review are capable of addressing one or more of these goals.
However, in biology, it is especially important to have tools that can holistically capture, describe, and integrate all the mechanisms that underlie the system of interest.

In our opinion, and as suggested by the comparative overview in Table
1
, this task can best be fulfilled by symbolic mathematical models in the form of differential equations.
Such models explicitly describe interactions, mechanisms, and their influence on the system’s state.
When chosen appropriately, they are capable not only of forecasting system behavior but also to go beyond and explore ’what-if’ scenarios.
A ’good’ model is thus able to predict
unknown
biology
in silico
even before experiments are conducted.
Of course, these predictions must ultimately be validated experimentally.
This does not imply that such a model is ’correct’, as George Box famously noted in 1976: ’All models are wrong, but some are useful’.
But there are numerous examples where mathematical modeling has been essential to uncovering the mechanisms of biological systems, and the importance of mathematics in biology has been widely recognized
Gunawardena 2014
;
Paluch 2015
;
Goldstein 2018
;
Phillips 2015
;
Mogilner et al. 2006
.
Recent methodological advances illustrate how this vision is evolving.
New approaches such as neural flow maps
Xu et al. 2025
extend neural ODEs toward more expressive representations of time series, while pipelines like DANSE integrate multi-omics dynamics
Jansen Klomp et al. 2025
.
At the same time, Waddington’s epigenetic landscape metaphor has led to machine learning methods that reconstruct developmental landscapes directly from data
Cislo et al. 2025
;
Fontaine et al. 2025
.
Together, these efforts highlight how diverse research lines are converging on hybrid models that bridge mechanistic interpretability and data-driven flexibility.

Looking ahead, the future of data-driven biology will not be dominated by a single methodology but by hybrid approaches that combine the interpretability of symbolic equations, the flexibility of neural networks, and the robustness of probabilistic frameworks.
Such methods may never yield a “correct” model in the absolute sense, but — echoing Box’s dictum — they can be profoundly
useful
.
By distilling governing equations from data, uncovering hidden state variables, and integrating mechanistic priors with machine learning, the next generation of models will not only describe what is known but also predict the unknown, guiding experiments and shaping new biological theory.
