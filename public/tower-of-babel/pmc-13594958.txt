Probable autosomal dominant Alport syndrome associated with a novel COL4A4 variant: A case report
Authors: Alice Doubinsky, Martina Marangoni, Lidia Ghisdal, Rita Ghaleb, Marie Van Eycken, Tess Van Meerhaeghe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlport syndrome is a hereditary glomerular basement membrane disorder caused by pathogenic variants in COL4A3, COL4A4, or COL4A5. Autosomal dominant forms are increasingly recognized but remain underdiagnosed due to their mild and heterogeneous presentation. We report the case of a 38-year-old woman referred for persistent proteinuria and microscopic hematuria with preserved renal function. Extensive immunological and infectious work-up was negative. Kidney biopsy revealed minimal abnormalities on light microscopy and negative immunofluorescence. Electron microscopy demonstrated irregular thinning of the glomerular basement membrane (GBM), with focal thickening, lamellation, and segmental splitting of the lamina densa, without immune-type deposits. These findings suggested a collagen IV-related nephropathy. Whole-exome sequencing identified a previously undescribed heterozygous missense variant in COL4A4 (c.4556C > G; p.Thr1519Arg). According to ACMG/AMP criteria, the variant is currently classified as a Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS), fulfilling PM1, PM2, and PP3 evidence criteria. Nevertheless, the combination of the patient's phenotype, characteristic ultrastructural findings, and compatible family history strongly supports its clinical relevance and suggests that it represents the most likely molecular explanation for the disease. Clinical, ultrastructural, and genetic findings supported a diagnosis of probable autosomal dominant Alport syndrome. This case highlights the diagnostic value of integrating electron microscopy and genetic testing in adults with persistent hematuria, proteinuria, and non-specific biopsy findings.Keywords: Alport syndrome, Collagen IV nephropathy, COL4A4, Genetic testing, Hematuria

Introduction

Alport syndrome (AS) is an inherited nephropathy caused by pathogenic variants in COL4A3, COL4A4, or COL4A5, encoding the α3, α4, and α5 chains of type IV collagen [1], [2]. These chains assemble into the α3α4α5(IV) heterotrimeric network, which is essential for the structural integrity and function of the glomerular basement membrane (GBM) [3]. Disruption of this network leads to progressive GBM abnormalities and chronic kidney disease.

Three inheritance patterns are recognized: X-linked, autosomal recessive, and autosomal dominant [4], [5]. Autosomal dominant Alport syndrome (ADAS), typically associated with heterozygous variants in COL4A3 or COL4A4, is increasingly identified due to the widespread use of next-generation sequencing [6], [7]. Its phenotype is generally milder than X-linked disease and is characterized by persistent microscopic hematuria, variable proteinuria, and slower renal function decline [8], [9]. Extrarenal manifestations are less frequent [10].

Because affected individuals may present only with isolated hematuria or moderate proteinuria for many years, ADAS remains frequently underrecognized in adult nephrology practice. Kidney biopsy findings may also be subtle or non-specific, making diagnosis challenging. Detailed clinicopathological correlation and careful interpretation of novel variants are therefore essential to improve diagnosis and genotype–phenotype understanding [[11], [12]]. Here, we report the case of a 38-year-old woman with probable ADAS associated with a previously undescribed heterozygous COL4A4 missense variant.

Case presentation

A 38-year-old woman was referred for persistent proteinuria (1–1.5 g/g) and microscopic hematuria. The exact age at first detection of urinary abnormalities was not available; however, hematuria had been present for several years before referral. Urinary sediment confirmed persistent hematuria; however, detailed characterization of dysmorphic erythrocytes or acanthocytes was not available. Renal function was preserved (creatinine 0.71 mg/dL; eGFR 107 mL/min/1.73 m2, CKD-EPI 2021). Blood pressure was well controlled under renin–angiotensin system blockade.

Autoimmune screening, including antinuclear antibodies, antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies, anti-GBM antibodies, and anti-PLA2R antibodies, was negative. Complement levels were normal. Viral serologies were negative. Glycemic status was normal.

Renal imaging showed kidneys of normal size and morphology without cysts or urinary tract abnormalities. Ophthalmologic and audiometric evaluations were normal.

Kidney biopsy revealed no significant abnormalities on light microscopy in a sample containing 6–7 glomeruli, one of which was globally sclerotic (Fig. 1). The non-sclerotic glomeruli were within normal histological limits (Fig. 2). Tubulointerstitial fibrosis was minimal (<10%). Tubules were unremarkable, and arterioles showed mild medial thickening. Immunofluorescence was negative.

Electron microscopy demonstrated irregular thinning of the GBM with focal thickening, lamellation, and segmental splitting of the lamina densa. No immune-type electron-dense deposits were identified. Limited podocyte foot process effacement was present. These findings were suggestive of a collagen IV-related nephropathy.

Whole-exome sequencing with targeted analysis of 467 genes associated with inherited kidney diseases, including COL4A3, COL4A4, and COL4A5, identified a heterozygous missense variant in COL4A4 (NM_000092.5:c.4556C > G; p.(Thr1519Arg)). No other clinically significant sequence variants were detected, and copy-number variant analysis did not identify any clinically relevant exon-level deletions or duplications. Family history revealed a maternal aunt with longstanding microscopic hematuria (Fig. 3), suggesting a possible autosomal dominant inheritance pattern. No information regarding proteinuria, chronic kidney disease, hearing impairment, ocular abnormalities, dialysis, or kidney transplantation was available in affected relatives. However, segregation analysis could not be performed because the patient was no longer in contact with her family, preventing genetic testing of relatives.

The identified variant was absent from the gnomAD v4 population database and had not previously been reported in the literature. It affects a highly conserved threonine residue located within the C-terminal non-collagenous (NC1) domain of COL4A4. Multiple computational prediction tools supported a deleterious effect (18/20 concordant algorithms; REVEL score = 0.97; CADD score = 25.2). According to ACMG/AMP criteria, the variant fulfilled PM1 (location within a functionally important protein domain), PM2 (absence from population databases), and PP3 (multiple computational predictions supporting a deleterious effect) and was therefore classified as a Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS, Class 3). Although the available evidence is insufficient for classification as likely pathogenic because segregation and functional studies are lacking, the overall clinical, ultrastructural, and genetic findings strongly support this variant as the most likely molecular explanation for the patient's disease.

Renin–angiotensin system blockade had been initiated before the genetic diagnosis and was continued throughout follow-up. An SGLT2 inhibitor was added in November 2024. Before SGLT2 inhibitor initiation, ACR was 2079 mg/g and UPCR was 2.58 g/g. Thereafter, albuminuria progressively decreased to 1512 mg/g in January 2025, 1311 mg/g in May 2025, and 987.2 mg/g in January 2026. At the latest follow-up in June 2026, ACR was 1108 mg/g and UPCR 1.28 g/g. Kidney function remained preserved throughout follow-up, with eGFR ranging from 103 to 117 mL/min/1.73 m2 (Table 1). Although this temporal evolution occurred after SGLT2 inhibitor initiation, the contribution of SGLT2 inhibition cannot be distinguished from that of concomitant ARB therapy or the natural variability of proteinuria in a single patient.

The diagnosis of probable autosomal dominant Alport syndrome was based on persistent hematuria, proteinuria, characteristic GBM abnormalities on electron microscopy, identification of a heterozygous COL4A4 variant, and a compatible family history.

Discussion

Although this report describes a single patient, its value lies less in defining a new phenotype than in illustrating the diagnostic pathway leading to recognition of autosomal dominant Alport syndrome in an adult with subtle histological findings. Such patients remain underdiagnosed in routine nephrology practice because light microscopy may be non-diagnostic and clinical manifestations are often mild. This case illustrates the diagnostic challenges of ADAS in adults, characterized by persistent hematuria, moderate proteinuria, preserved renal function, without evidence of extrarenal manifestations at the current age of 38 years. [8], [9]. Although generally milder than X-linked disease [10], heterozygous COL4A4 variants may be associated with chronic kidney disease, particularly in patients with persistent proteinuria [13], [14].

Beyond the identification of a novel COL4A4 variant, this case illustrates an important diagnostic issue in adult nephrology: patients with ADAS may remain undiagnosed for years because of mild clinical manifestations and non-specific findings on light microscopy. The combination of persistent hematuria, moderate proteinuria, subtle ultrastructural abnormalities, and genomic analysis allowed recognition of a probable inherited collagen IV disorder [16].

Histopathological findings were subtle. Light microscopy and immunofluorescence were non-diagnostic, whereas electron microscopy demonstrated abnormalities highly suggestive of a collagen IV-related nephropathy, including GBM thinning, focal thickening, lamellation, and segmental splitting of the lamina densa [3], [15]. In autosomal dominant forms, the classical “basket-weave” appearance may be incomplete or focal, potentially contributing to delayed diagnosis [8]. Published cohorts indicate considerable phenotypic variability among individuals carrying heterozygous COL4A4 variants, ranging from isolated microscopic hematuria to proteinuric chronic kidney disease and, less commonly, kidney failure. Although genotype–phenotype correlations remain incomplete, variants involving the NC1 domain have been associated with both mild and more progressive renal phenotypes, suggesting that this region is functionally important but does not determine clinical severity alone.

Whole-exome sequencing with targeted analysis of 467 genes associated with inherited kidney diseases identified a heterozygous missense variant in COL4A4 (NM_000092.5:c.4556C > G; p.(Thr1519Arg)). The variant is absent from the gnomAD v4 population database and, to our knowledge, has not been previously reported in ClinVar, HGMD Professional 2026.1, or the published literature. It affects a highly conserved residue located within the C-terminal non-collagenous (NC1) domain of COL4A4. Two alternative substitutions affecting the same amino acid residue (p.(Thr1519Met) and p.(Thr1519Pro)) have been reported in ClinVar but remain classified as variants of uncertain significance. The p.(Thr1519Met) substitution has also been reported in association with chronic kidney disease and is listed in HGMD as a DM? (“possible disease-causing mutation”) variant. However, this designation does not constitute evidence of pathogenicity according to ACMG/AMP criteria and therefore cannot be used to apply ACMG criterion PM5. In contrast, variants affecting neighbouring residues (p.(Cys1513Tyr) and p.(Phe1517Cys)) have been reported as pathogenic in patients with autosomal recessive Alport syndrome, supporting the functional importance of this region of the NC1 domain [3, PMID: 24052634, 31515789].

The substitution of threonine by arginine at position 1519 introduces a larger positively charged amino acid in place of a smaller polar residue and may theoretically affect local molecular interactions required for correct NC1 domain folding or α3α4α5 collagen IV protomer assembly [3]. Several pathogenic variants affecting the NC1 domain of COL4A4 have been associated with variable phenotypes, ranging from isolated hematuria to progressive kidney disease, supporting the clinical heterogeneity of variants affecting this region [5], [8], [13], [14]. However, in the absence of functional studies, the biological consequences of this substitution remain hypothetical and require further investigation.

Historically, individuals carrying heterozygous COL4A3 or COL4A4 variants were considered to have thin basement membrane nephropathy, often viewed as a benign condition. Contemporary evidence indicates an increased lifetime risk of chronic kidney disease and focal segmental glomerulosclerosis [5], [8], [11]. Early renin–angiotensin system blockade may delay disease progression [17]. The role of SGLT2 inhibitors in Alport syndrome remains under investigation, although their nephroprotective effects in proteinuric CKD provide a biological rationale for their use. A recent retrospective study by Toso et al. reported reduced proteinuria and improved eGFR slope in adults with Alport syndrome treated with SGLT2 inhibitors [18].

Genetic diagnosis enables cascade screening and informs living donor evaluation [[5], [19], [20]]. In adults presenting with persistent hematuria and non-diagnostic biopsy findings, exome sequencing can significantly improve diagnostic yield [6], [7].

Similar to previously reported ADAS cohorts, our patient exhibited isolated renal involvement without hearing or ocular abnormalities. The preserved renal function at age 38 is consistent with the generally mild phenotype associated with heterozygous COL4A4 variants, although long-term renal risk remains variable.

Conclusion

We report a novel heterozygous COL4A4 missense variant (c.4556C > G; p.(Thr1519Arg)) identified in a patient with clinical and ultrastructural features consistent with probable autosomal dominant Alport syndrome. This case highlights the importance of electron microscopy and genetic testing in adults with persistent hematuria, proteinuria, and subtle biopsy findings. Although the identified COL4A4 variant is currently classified as a Variant of Uncertain Significance according to ACMG/AMP criteria, the combination of the patient's phenotype, characteristic ultrastructural findings, absence of alternative molecular diagnoses following analysis of 467 kidney disease genes, and compatible family history strongly supports its role as the most likely molecular explanation for the disease. Further segregation and functional studies will be required to establish pathogenicity definitively. Reporting such cases with detailed phenotypic correlation may contribute to improved interpretation of rare COL4A4 variants and refinement of genotype–phenotype correlations.
