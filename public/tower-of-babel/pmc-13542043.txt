Transthyretin Amyloid Cardiomyopathy in Women: Epidemiology, Diagnostic Delay, and Clinical Implications
Authors: Avni Madhani, Bhavya Ancha, Kevin M. Alexander, Joban Vaishnav
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Transthyretin amyloid cardiomyopathy (ATTR-CM) is an underdiagnosed cause of heart failure, resulting from the deposition of misfolded transthyretin protein in the myocardium. While historically considered to be a male-predominant disease, an increasing number of studies have shown that ATTR-CM may be under-recognized in women, likely due to differences in disease presentation compared with men. Notably, left ventricular wall thickness ≥ 12 mm is frequently used as a marker for ATTR-CM workup; however, women with ATTR-CM often have a lower left ventricular wall thickness than men and may not meet this threshold despite significant myocardial involvement. Moreover, hormonal differences between male and female patients may result in distinct cardiac phenotypes. Socioeconomic factors disproportionately impacting women, such as increased caregiving burden, symptom dismissal, and fewer financial resources, are likely additional contributing factors. As a result, diagnostic delays up to several years have been noted in women. Improving the diagnosis of ATTR-CM in women requires a multifactorial approach, including reforming screening guidelines to reflect sex-specific differences in disease presentation and increasing awareness of ATTR-CM in the community. Furthermore, given the historical underrepresentation of women in clinical studies on ATTR-CM, further investigation is necessary to better understand the prevalence, treatment efficacy, and outcomes of ATTR-CM in women.

Introduction

Transthyretin amyloid cardiomyopathy (ATTR-CM) results from the deposition of misfolded transthyretin (TTR) protein in the myocardium due to wild-type or variant (hereditary) forms. ATTR-CM is underdiagnosed and has multiple cardiac manifestations, including heart failure with preserved or reduced ejection fraction (HFpEF or HFrEF), atrial fibrillation, and conduction disease [1, 2].

Although considered a male-predominant disease, ATTR-CM may be more prevalent in women than previously recognized. A study of 108 patients with HFpEF who underwent endomyocardial biopsy found that 40% of patients with cardiac amyloidosis were women [3]. Additionally, ATTR-CM diagnosis is often delayed in women compared with men. One study showed that women < 70 years old had a 5-year delay from symptom onset [4].

Increased recognition of ATTR-CM in women is necessary to prevent diagnostic delay and improve outcomes (Fig. 1). This review will (1) detail the prevalence and pathophysiology of ATTR-CM in women, (2) describe differences in disease presentation and outcomes for women, (3) provide an overview of the inclusion of women in clinical studies, and (4) discuss reasons for diagnostic delays and suggestions to improve diagnosis among women with ATTR-CM.Fig. 1Drivers of delayed diagnosis of transthyretin amyloid cardiomyopathy (ATTR-CM) in women. *These suggested features are based on a limited number of studies examined in this review and need further validation. Prevalence

Overall

The overall prevalence of ATTR-CM is evolving, driven by increased disease awareness and the availability of non-invasive diagnostic modalities and disease-modifying therapies. In the United States, approximately 50,000–150,000 individuals have ATTR-CM [2, 5–7]. Prospective studies highlight substantial underdiagnosis [8]. In an enriched, high-risk cohort of patients with HFpEF and suggestive echocardiographic features of infiltrative disease, 29% were ultimately diagnosed with ATTR-CM [9]. Wild-type ATTR-CM (ATTRwt-CM) accounts for most cases, with a reported prevalence of 37–95% across cohorts, while variant (hereditary) ATTR-CM (ATTRv-CM) demonstrates greater variability (5–58%) [7]. Overall, ATTR-CM is estimated to affect approximately 1% of the heart failure population or 1 in 6000 individuals in the general population [10]. Among TTR variants, the V122I (p.V142I) mutation is the most common in the US, particularly among Black individuals, with a carrier prevalence of 3.5% (1.5 million individuals) and an estimated penetrance of 39% [2, 8, 11, 12].

Prevalence by Sex

ATTR-CM demonstrates a male predominance, with large registries reporting 60–70% of patients as male, with some cohorts approaching 90% [5, 13–17]. ATTRwt-CM shows the strongest male predominance, while ATTRv-CM demonstrates a lower male predominance and relatively higher female representation [14, 15, 18]. In a systematic review by Bruno et al. of 4669 pooled patients with ATTR-CM, only 17% were female. Female representation was less than 30% in wild-type disease but exceeded 30% in variant disease [19]. Another study by Masri et al. reported that 91% of patients with wild-type disease and 71% of patients with variant disease were male [14]. Sex distribution may differ among specific genotypes. The Transthyretin Amyloidosis Outcomes Survey (THAOS) investigated TTR genetic variants in symptomatic and asymptomatic patients and found a male:female prevalence ratio of 1:1 in V30M, 1.4:1 in T60A, and 2.9:1 in V122I [20]. Additionally, cohorts with neuropathy-predominant V30M ATTR have shown that inheritance patterns, including the sex of the transmitting parent and affected offspring, may influence disease expression and sex differences in prevalence. In a study of 114 families with V30M ATTRv in Sweden, the mean anticipation was 11.7 years, with the greatest risk of disease and anticipation observed among male offspring and with maternal transmission [21]. Thus, an interplay among genotype, ancestry, inheritance, penetrance, and age likely shapes sex distribution in ATTR-CM.

Despite evidence of male predominance, multiple studies describe potential underdiagnosis in women [19, 22–28]. Prospective and screening-based cohorts have demonstrated that the prevalence gap between male and female patients narrows with systematic ascertainment [29, 30]. For example, the Screening for Cardiac Amyloidosis with Nuclear Imaging in Minority Populations (SCAN-MP) study found that 5.2% of female and 8.2% of male participants with heart failure had ATTR-CM. Though prevalence remained higher in male participants, the difference did not reach statistical significance [30]. Increasing awareness and improved diagnostics have contributed to greater detection of ATTR-CM among female patients over time, with female prevalence reaching 20% in another recent cohort [4, 15]. Female representation also increases with age, supporting a non-uniform sex distribution [4]. These findings are consistent with a prior review by Aimo et al., which proposed that sex-specific differences in disease presentation may contribute to the underdiagnosis and misdiagnosis of women with ATTR-CM [31]. Thus, the observed male predominance in ATTR-CM likely reflects a combination of biological differences, referral patterns, and historical underdiagnosis in women rather than a uniform sex-specific disease distribution.

Pathophysiology

ATTR-CM arises from the extracellular deposition of misfolded TTR in the myocardium, derived from wild-type or variant tetramers [8, 32]. Although circulating TTR concentrations have prognostic value, serum TTR levels measure total circulating TTR without differentiating between stable or aggregated species and thus should be interpreted cautiously [31, 33]. Lower serum TTR has been associated with adverse outcomes in both ATTR-CM and general heart failure, and a rise in serum TTR (ΔTTR) following TTR stabilization with acoramidis has emerged as a marker of treatment response and improved survival in follow-up studies of ATTRibute-CM [34–39]. However, the applicability of ΔTTR as a sex-neutral treatment-response marker remains unclear. Female patients have lower baseline circulating TTR concentrations and comprise only 10% of the original ATTRibute-CM study population [39–41]. Thus, whether ΔTTR carries equivalent prognostic meaning across sexes, specifically given a lower female baseline, has not been established, with further validation needed.

Although sex-stratified pharmacodynamic data remain limited, sex-related biological factors may modulate ATTR-CM pathogenesis. Animal models demonstrate that dihydrotestosterone upregulates TTR transcription to a greater extent than estradiol [42]. Clinical data further support sex-based differences in myocardial involvement. In cohorts with ATTRv-CM, female patients exhibit less severe cardiac phenotypes assessed by echocardiographic parameters, suggesting reduced susceptibility to myocardial infiltration [43]. Female patients with increased left ventricular (LV) wall thickness are often post-menopausal and older, indicating that hormonal transitions may affect disease onset [44]. Collectively, these findings raise the possibility that the hormonal milieu and sex-specific biological modifiers may contribute to delayed-onset and distinct cardiac phenotypes in female patients.

Social Factors Determining Health Outcomes in Women

Although ATTR-CM-specific data are limited, social determinants of health likely contribute to gender disparities in diagnosis and treatment. Given that ATTR-CM mostly occurs in older individuals, many affected women likely face age-related economic and social vulnerabilities. Among adults older than 65 years, 41% of women are widowed compared with 13% of men, and a substantial proportion live on incomes below $20,000 [45–48]. These circumstances likely hinder access to advanced imaging, referral to specialized centers, and disease-modifying therapy. Women are more likely than men to have their symptoms dismissed and to experience delayed specialist referrals [49–54]. Importantly, the musculoskeletal manifestations of ATTR-CM frequently present years prior to cardiac manifestations, representing an opportunity for early referral that may be missed [55]. Additionally, women often serve as caregivers across the life course, a role associated with delayed care-seeking [51, 56]. Although these factors have not been systematically studied in ATTR-CM, they likely contribute to diagnostic and therapeutic disparities observed between men and women.

Clinical Manifestations and Diagnostics

Differences in Biomarkers

Several studies have examined differences in biomarkers, echocardiographic parameters, and associated manifestations of ATTR-CM between men and women. Specific biomarkers, imaging findings, and concurrent pathologies to prompt workup for ATTR-CM are outlined in Fig. 2, and many of these remain consistent among men and women. Common biomarkers used in the workup of ATTR-CM include N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP)/B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP), troponin, and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR). Multiple studies have demonstrated no difference in NT-proBNP/BNP between men and women with ATTR-CM [15, 18, 57–59]. Two studies that demonstrated higher NT-proBNP/BNP values in women included older women with a more advanced New York Heart Association (NYHA) class and a higher UK National Amyloidosis Centre (NAC) stage [60, 61]. Regarding troponin, many studies show no difference in levels between men and women, but data are limited and inconsistent [15, 18, 57–60]. Differences in Echocardiography

Echocardiographic findings often raise initial suspicion for ATTR-CM, with some suggestive features, including increased LV wall thickness, preserved ejection fraction (EF), diastolic dysfunction, low stroke volume (SV) index, and decreased global longitudinal strain (GLS) with apical sparing [62]. An LV wall thickness ≥ 12 mm is often used as a marker to pursue further workup [8, 63]. Several studies have shown that women with ATTR-CM have a lower interventricular septum (IVS) thickness and/or posterior wall (PW) thickness compared with men (Table 1). This difference is often attenuated when the IVS and PW thickness are indexed to body surface area (BSA) and/or height (Table 2) [20, 57, 64]. Thus, part of the difference in non-indexed values may be because the normal threshold for wall thickness is lower in female individuals [65]. Some studies have even demonstrated a higher indexed IVS and PW thickness in women compared with men [15, 60]. Furthermore, women with ATTR-CM are more likely than men to have IVS thickness < 12 mm [18, 57, 59, 66]. In a study of 1,062 patients from the French National Reference Centre for Cardiac Amyloidosis, 7% of women had IVS thickness < 12 mm compared with 2% of men. This proportion increased to 15% in women ≤ 77 years of age, which likely contributes to diagnostic challenges among younger women [18]. Similarly, in the West German Amyloidosis Center cohort of 240 patients, 20% of women and 3% of men had IVS thickness < 12 mm [57]. A lower LV thickness cutoff in women is further supported by Shiwani et al., who found that the values for LV hypertrophy in a cohort of healthy participants ranged from 10 to 15 mm vs. 12 to 17 mm in Women with ATTR-CM also have a higher LV ejection fraction (LVEF) and lower LV end-diastolic diameter than men (Table 1) [15, 57, 59, 60, 68]. An analysis from THAOS showed that women with ATTR-CM had an average LVEF of 53% compared with 48% in men and an LV diastolic diameter of 42 mm compared with 46 mm in men [68]. Women with ATTR-CM may also have lower SV, lower LV mass index (LVMI), and a higher E/e’ ratio than men, although variations exist among studies regarding these parameters [15, 20, 57, 60].

Differences in Nuclear Scintigraphy, Biopsy, and Cardiovascular Magnetic Resonance

Limited data on differences between men and women exist for other imaging and diagnostic modalities in ATTR-CM, including cardiovascular magnetic resonance (CMR), technetium-99 m pyrophosphate (PYP) scintigraphy, and biopsy. Nuclear scintigraphy (i.e., PYP scans) and endomyocardial biopsies are the diagnostic modalities for ATTR-CM. A study by Takashio et al. found a lower PYP heart-to-contralateral lung ratio of 1.6 in women vs. 1.9 in men, with this value differentiating ATTR-CM from light chain amyloidosis [1, 61]. Fewer women in this study underwent an endomyocardial biopsy or extracardiac biopsy compared with men [61]. Further studies on PYP parameters and biopsy sensitivity are warranted in women. With regards to CMR, a study by Yoshimura et al. found that women with ATTRwt-CM had a significantly higher LVEF, lower extracellular volume fraction, and a lower late gadolinium enhancement score than men. However, no significant differences in LV mass, global longitudinal strain, native T1, or myocardial T2 value were observed between men and women [69].

Differences in Cardiac and Non-cardiac Manifestations

ATTR-CM often presents with a number of other cardiac and non-cardiac conditions including arrhythmias, conduction abnormalities, aortic stenosis, carpal tunnel syndrome, trigger finger, joint arthroplasty, spinal stenosis, and spontaneous biceps tendon rupture [2]. These co-existing conditions can help increase diagnostic suspicion for ATTR-CM. Most studies have not shown differences in rates of carpal tunnel syndrome between men and women with ATTR-CM [18, 20, 58–61, 68]. An analysis of patients with ATTRwt-CM from THAOS found that men had a higher rate of lumbar spinal stenosis at 7% vs. 1% in women [68]. However, several other studies have demonstrated no difference in the prevalence of lumbar spinal stenosis in men and women [18, 59]. The THAOS study of patients with ATTRwt-CM also found similar rates of biceps tendon rupture, Achilles tendon rupture, joint arthroplasty, rotator cuff repair, and trigger finger between men and women [68]. The presence of atrial fibrillation, bundle branch blocks, first-degree atrioventricular block, and high-degree/complete atrioventricular block has also been found to be similar in men and women [57–61]. THAOS demonstrated that women with ATTRv-CM have a higher rate of sensory abnormalities, whereas men have a higher rate of autonomic impairment. This difference was not found in patients with ATTRwt-CM [20, 68]. Other studies assessing neuropathic symptoms did not find a significant difference between men and women [18, 57].

Inclusion in Trials/Studies

Inclusion of Women in Staging Systems

Women have been historically underrepresented in clinical studies on the diagnostics, staging, and treatment of ATTR-CM (Table 3). The three primary staging systems for ATTR-CM include the NAC, Mayo Clinic, and Columbia staging systems. The Mayo Clinic staging system includes NT-proBNP and troponin T for risk stratification. Out of the cohort of 360 patients in this study, 9% (33 patients) were female [26]. NAC staging includes NT-proBNP and eGFR. The test cohort used to develop the staging parameters included 15% female patients, and the validation cohort had 19% female patients [70]. The Columbia staging system adds diuretic dose and NYHA class to the NAC stage and incorporates 16% female patients in the cohort [71]. Thus, caution must be used when applying these scores to female patients. Inclusion of Women in Treatment Trials

Three treatments (tafamidis, acoramidis, and vutrisiran) are currently FDA-approved for ATTR-CM in the United States. While there have been several clinical trials investigating treatments for ATTR-CM, women have been underrepresented. The ATTR-ACT and ATTRibute-CM trials, which demonstrated the efficacy of tafamidis and acoramidis, respectively, included 10% female patients [39, 73]. Similarly, the HELIOS-B trial, which investigated vutrisiran, included 8% female patients [74]. The APOLLO-B trial investigating patisiran, which was ultimately not FDA-approved for ATTR-CM, included 11% female patients [75].

Women are also underrepresented in ongoing trials for the development of anti-TTR antibodies. In the phase 1B trial investigating cliramitug (NI006), one female patient was part of the cohort of 40 patients [76]. The phase 2 trial testing coramitug included seven female patients out of 104 patients [77].

Inclusion of Women in Cohort Studies

Several large studies that have improved understanding of the epidemiology, prevalence, and diagnostics of ATTR-CM have been conducted. The NAC cohort of 1732 patients included 14% female patients over 21 years, with the number of female patients diagnosed with ATTR-CM rising from 2001 to 2020 [15]. THAOS, which is the largest disease registry for ATTR amyloidosis, enrolled a total of 6718 participants from 33 countries in a 16-year span [78]. In an interim analysis that included 6135 patients enrolled through 2022, 29% of patients with cardiac and/or neurologic phenotypes were female, and 59% of asymptomatic gene carriers were female [13]. The SCAN-MP study, which is the largest prospective screening study for ATTR-CM in Black and Caribbean Hispanic individuals, had a recruitment target of 50% female individuals and met this target with an overall 51% female cohort [30, 79]. Female inclusion in large cohorts has increased in more recent studies, representing a promising shift.

It is important to note that trial eligibility criteria have likely limited the inclusion of women in ATTR-CM studies. All treatment trials required a left ventricular wall thickness of at least 12 mm, leading to the underrepresentation of women [39, 73–77]. Additionally, a number of staging and cohort studies used characteristic echocardiographic parameters as part of their inclusion criteria, which have traditionally represented male-specific parameters [15, 26]. Thus, the extent to which the findings of these studies apply to women remains unclear.

Disease Course and Outcomes

Diagnostic Delay

Women with ATTR-CM are diagnosed approximately 3 years later than men [15]. This pattern is observed across multiple cohorts [25, 57, 80]. Data from the THAOS cohort further highlight this disparity, with the greatest diagnostic delay of 5 years observed in women aged < 70 years compared with an average of 2 years after symptom onset in the overall population [4]. Several factors may underlie delayed recognition in women, including the use of non-indexed LV thickness thresholds for workup and provider bias of ATTR-CM as a male-prevalent disease.

Mortality and Heart Failure Hospitalizations

Heart failure due to ATTR-CM carries a poor prognosis in the absence of treatment, with a median survival of approximately 5 years [2]. Sex-specific differences in outcomes remain variable across cohorts. In a study of 1732 patients from the UK NAC, structural and functional cardiac phenotypes were largely similar between male and female patients, with no significant differences in mortality by genotype [15]. In a large study from a French referral center, the 5-year overall survival was similar between male and female patients, but younger female patients had a worse prognosis compared with age-matched male patients [18]. Other multi-center studies have identified male sex as a predictor of worse survival [14]. These conflicting data likely reflect heterogeneity in study populations in addition to the underrepresentation of women in cohorts.

Access to Treatment

Disease-modifying therapy with tafamidis has been shown to significantly improve outcomes in ATTR-CM [2]. A limited number of studies on treatment rates with tafamidis in men and women show varying results. A recent study of patients with ATTRwt-CM from 14 European heart centers showed lower rates of tafamidis initiation in women [81]. However, in the West German Amyloidosis Center cohort, 94% of patients received stabilizer therapy, with no observed differences between men and women [57]. Limited data on acoramidis and vutrisiran use exist due to their relatively recent approval.

Disease Progression on Treatment

The underrepresentation of women in ATTR-CM studies and clinical trials limits robust conclusions regarding treatment response and disease progression in women [2, 8, 31]. Major cohort studies of ATTR-CM progression at Columbia Medical Center and the NAC included only 9% and 14% female patients, respectively [82, 83]. A cohort study of 240 patients from the West German Amyloidosis Center demonstrated that women maintained higher LVEF on tafamidis therapy compared with men [57]. However, functional capacity measured by 6-min walk distance was slightly lower in women after 1-year, while NYHA class did not differ between men and women [57]. A study including 1,055 patients with ATTRwt-CM on tafamidis did not indicate differences in treatment efficacy or mortality between men and women [81]. In a large study from a French referral site, the protective effect of tafamidis appeared attenuated in women [18]. Given variations in conclusions from different studies, differences in disease progression on treatment between men and women remain incompletely characterized.

Advanced Therapies

Data on advanced therapies in ATTR-CM, including heart transplantation, remain limited due to the small number of patients studied [8, 84]. Among 31 patients with ATTR-CM or light-chain amyloidosis who underwent heart transplantation at Stanford between 2004 and 2017, 71% were male [84]. These disparities are consistent with broader trends in advanced heart failure care. Female patients comprise approximately 25% of heart transplant waitlist candidates, with little change in representation over time [85, 86].

Conclusion and Future Considerations

Various expert consensus guidelines have recommended LV wall thickness cutoffs for ATTR-CM workup that may underdiagnose women. The 2020 American Heart Association (AHA) and 2022 AHA/American College of Cardiology (ACC)/Heart Failure Society of America guidelines include LV wall thickness ≥ 14 mm as a threshold to increase ATTR-CM suspicion [87, 88]. The 2021 European Society of Cardiology guidelines use an unexplained LV thickness ≥ 12 mm as part of the ATTR-CM echocardiographic diagnostic criteria [63]. Notably, the 2025 ACC guidelines are the first to indicate that women may not meet the threshold of LV wall thickness ≥ 12 mm [89].

Several studies have offered recommendations for modifying predictive echocardiographic parameters to improve ATTR-CM detection in women. For example, indexing LV wall thickness to BSA and/or height and creating indexed thresholds may improve recognition of ATTR-CM in women [15, 20, 57, 60, 64]. Another study suggested the use of LVMI indexed to BSA ≥ 95 g/m2 in women [66]. In the presence of biomarker evidence or other associated manifestations of ATTR-CM, proposed echocardiographic features to increase suspicion for ATTR-CM in women include LVEF > 50%, an IVS or PW thickness indexed to height > 9 mm/m (with the IVS thickness indexed to height more commonly being > 10 mm/m), and an IVS thickness or PW thickness indexed to BSA > 8.5 mm/m2 (Fig. 1). However, these criteria are based on a limited number of studies described in Tables 1 and 2 and must be further validated. A prospective screening study of women with HFpEF, adapted from the design of SCAN-MP, could help characterize the prevalence of ATTR-CM and echocardiographic features in women [30].

Additionally, socioeconomic factors, such as limited access to and utilization of care, likely preclude the diagnosis of ATTR-CM in women. Community outreach and patient education programs may improve patient awareness and encourage seeking care for characteristic symptoms. Furthermore, increasing awareness among non-cardiology providers, such as primary care providers and orthopedists, may help mitigate some of these challenges, as orthopedic manifestations frequently present years before ATTR-CM [55]. Strategies for expanding recognition among providers include increasing focus on ATTR-CM in medical education and sessions on ATTR amyloidosis in society meetings for various specialties.

A multifaceted approach is required to improve recognition of ATTR-CM in women (Fig. 3). Future studies investigating female-specific imaging parameters and risk scores are warranted. Increased recruitment of women in treatment, staging, and cohort studies is necessary for further disease understanding along with evolution of guidelines.
