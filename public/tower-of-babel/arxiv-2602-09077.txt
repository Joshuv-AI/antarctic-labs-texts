Fixed-grid sharp-interface numerical solutions to the three-phase spherical Stefan problem
Yavkreet Swami, Jacob Barajas, Amneet Pal Singh Bhalla

Abstract
Many metal manufacturing processes involve phase change phenomena, which include melting, boiling, and vaporization. These phenomena often occur concurrently. A prototypical 1D model for understanding the phase change phenomena is the Stefan problem. There is a large body of literature discussing the analytical solution to the two-phase Stefan problem that describes only the melting or boiling of phase change materials (PCMs) with one moving interface. Density-change effects that induce additional fluid flow during phase change are generally neglected in the literature to simplify the math of the Stefan problem. In our recent work [ 1 ] , we provide analytical and numerical solutions to the three-phase Stefan problem with simultaneous occurrences of melting, solidification, boiling, and condensation in Cartesian coordinates. The problem was set in a semi-infinite domain with two moving interfaces—melt and boiling fronts. The solid, liquid, and vapor phases of the PCM have distinct thermophysical properties, including density, thermal conductivity, and heat capacity. All relevant jump conditions, including density and kinetic energy, were accounted for in the three-phase Stefan problem. Our current work builds on our previous work to solve a more challenging problem: the three-phase Stefan problem in spherical coordinates for finite-sized particles. There are three moving interfaces in this system: the melt front, the boiling front, and the outer boundary which is in contact with the atmosphere. Although an analytical solution could not be found for this problem, we solved the governing equations using a fixed-grid sharp-interface method with second-order spatio-temporal accuracy. Using a small-time analytical solution, we predict a reasonably accurate estimate of temperature (in the three phases) and interface positions and velocities at the start of the simulation. To derive small-time analytical solutions, low- and high-density contrasts between phases are considered separately. Our numerical method is validated by reproducing the two-phase nanoparticle melting results of Font et al. [ 2 ] . Lastly, we solve the three-phase Stefan problems numerically to demonstrate the importance of kinetic energy terms during phase change of smaller (nano) particles. In contrast, these effects diminish for large particles (microns and larger).

Introduction

The Stefan problem is concerned with the evolution of an interface between two phases while simultaneously going through a phase change, such as melting a solid metal. In 1890, Josef Stefan introduced these problems in the context of sea ice formation in a set of four papers. The foundation of Stefan’s work traces back to 1831 when a similar problem was faced by Lamé and Clapeyron while studying the solidification of Earth’s crust
[
3
,
4
]
.

Although a large body of research has been conducted to solve the Stefan problem analytically and numerically, the vast majority of studies incorporate a number of restrictive assumptions that are often justified for mathematical convenience. These include constant latent heat, constant phase change temperature, constant thermal properties (thermal conductivity, specific heat) in each phase and an equal density in both phases
[
5
,
6
]
. During boiling, there is a density change of 1000 or more between the liquid and vapor phases, making the assumption of equal density across phases the most unreasonable. Furthermore, most Stefan problems considered in the literature have only two phases, either solid and liquid or liquid and vapor.

In metal manufacturing processes, such as welding and metal additive manufacturing (AM), a metal substrate or powder undergoes rapid melting, boiling, and vaporization under the action of a high-intensity laser or heat source. The standard two-phase Stefan problem cannot describe such a process. In our recent work
[
1
]
we introduced a three-phase Stefan problem set in half-space and Cartesian coordinates to model the simultaneous melting, solidification, boiling and condensation of a phase change material. The semi-infinite planar geometry allowed us to obtain an analytical solution to the three-phase problem that accounts for density and kinetic energy jumps during phase change. A fixed-grid sharp interface method was also presented to solve the three-phase Stefan problem with two moving interfaces in a Cartesian coordinate system. Analytical solutions were used to initialize the simulation in our prior work. The numerical method was demonstrated to be second-order accurate in both space and time.

This paper extends our previous work on the three-phase planar Stefan problem to the three-phase spherical Stefan problem while retaining appropriate density variation and kinetic energy contributions. This brings us closer to our long-term goal of developing and validating fully-resolved computational fluid dynamics (CFD) algorithms to simulate metal AM processes. In the manufacturing industry, the spherical Stefan problem has practical relevance to the production of metallic powders and spherical nanoparticles. As such, there has been extensive research in the literature regarding the spherical Stefan problem in two-phase form; see references
[
7
,
8
,
9
,
10
,
11
]
. This work contributes to the literature by introducing the three-phase version of the spherical Stefan problem and a numerical method to solve it.

The modeled system considered here is a finite-sized spherical particle exposed to the atmosphere. Unfortunately, the finite-sized spherical geometry does not permit a similarity solution. Thus, to initialize the numerical solution for the sharp-interface method, we rely on small-time analysis of the governing equations and boundary conditions to predict the temperature in the three phases and the interface positions and velocities at the start of the simulation. Our small-time analysis of the three-phase Stefan problem can be viewed as an extension of the small-time technique introduced by Myers et al.
[
11
]
for modeling the two-phase nanoparticle melting. Myers et al. considered low density contrasts between the solid and liquid phases in their small-time analysis. The small-time analyses presented in this work also consider the large density contrasts between the phases.

In Sec.
2
of our work, we mathematically formulate two-phase and three-phase spherical Stefan problems while retaining density and kinetic energy jump terms in the Stefan conditions. Governing equations and boundary conditions are non-dimensionalized and small-time analytical solutions for temperature and interface positions and velocities are derived for both two- and three-phase Stefan problems. Sec.
3
presents a sharp-interface numerical method using a second-order, finite difference/volume discretization, with explicit tracking of moving interfaces using an immersed-boundary (IB) method
[
12
]
. We then validate the proposed methodology in Sec.
5
by reproducing the two-phase nanoparticle melting results of Font et al.
[
2
]
. Font el al. transform the spherical heat equations to Cartesian equations and immobilize the moving boundaries by mapping them on a fixed rectilinear domain. In contrast, we solve the governing equations directly in spherical coordinates on a fixed axisymmetric grid which allows the interfaces to freely move through the underlying mesh. Lastly, we apply the method to numerically solve the three-phase Stefan problem to quantify the influence of density and kinetic-energy terms on interface evolution. It is found that the kinetic energy terms significantly affect the melt times of smaller particles, whereas for large particles their effect diminishes significantly.

2.1
Two phase spherical Stefan problem

We consider a simplified scenario where the PCM melts only during phase change. This is achieved by setting
T_{\infty}
greater than
T_{m}
but less than
T_{v}
. A similar two-phase spherical Stefan problem has been considered in the literature
[
2
]
. The two-phase problem serves to (i) introduce the non-dimensional parameters of the problem; (ii) introduce a small-time analytical solution method; and (iii) validate our fixed-grid sharp-interface numerical method with a coordinate transformed numerical solution reported in the literature.

To find the evolution of temperature in the solid (
T^{\rm S}(r,t)
) and liquid (
T^{\rm L}(r,t)
) phases as a result of melting, we need to solve the temperature
equation in the two domains. This is obtained by expressing the internal energy
e
in terms of temperature (see Eqs. (
2a
) and (
2b
)), and
neglecting terms related to pressure work and viscous dissipation in Eq. (
1c
). The resulting temperature equation in the solid and liquid phases reads as

\displaystyle\rho^{\rm S}C^{\rm S}\left(\frac{\partial T^{\rm S}}{\partial t}+u^{\rm S}\,\frac{\partial T^{\rm S}}{\partial r}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\kappa^{\rm S}}{r^{2}}\,\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\left(r^{2}\frac{\partial T^{\rm S}}{\partial r}\right),

\displaystyle 0\leq r<R_{1}(t)

(3a)

\displaystyle\rho^{\rm L}C^{\rm L}\left(\frac{\partial T^{\rm L}}{\partial t}+u^{\rm L}\,\frac{\partial T^{\rm L}}{\partial r}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\kappa^{\rm L}}{r^{2}}\,\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\left(r^{2}\frac{\partial T^{\rm L}}{\partial r}\right).

\displaystyle R_{1}(t)<r\leq R_{b}(t)

(3b)

Five boundary conditions are needed to solve Eqs. (
3
) to determine
T^{\rm S}(r,t)
,
T^{\rm L}(r,t)
, and
R_{1}(t)
.

The temperature
T_{m}^{\rm I}
at the S-L interface differs from the bulk melting temperature
T_{m}
due to the curvature and surface energy/tension effects. The two temperatures are related by the Gibbs-Thomson relation

T_{m}^{\rm I}(R_{1}(t))=T_{m}-\frac{\sigma_{\rm SL}T_{m}}{\rho^{\rm S}L_{m}}\,\frac{2}{R_{1}(t)}.

(4)

Here,
\sigma_{\rm SL}
denotes the surface energy coefficient between the solid and liquid phases. At any time
t
during the melting process, the temperature in the liquid and solid phase near the melt front is the same:
T^{\rm S}(R_{1}(t),t)=T^{\rm L}(R_{1}(t),t)=T_{m}^{\rm I}(R_{1}(t))
. The outer boundary is maintained at an ambient temperature of
T^{\rm L}(R_{b}(t),t)=T_{\infty}
. Solid phase temperature at the center of the sphere satisfies the homogeneous Neumann boundary condition
\left.\frac{\partial T^{\rm S}}{\partial r}\right|_{r=0}=0
. The Stefan condition (and the fifth boundary condition) for the melt front is obtained by applying the Rankine-Hugoniot jump condition to the energy equation across the solid-liquid interface

\kappa^{\rm S}\frac{\partial T^{\rm S}}{\partial r}\Bigg|_{R_{1}}-\kappa^{\rm L}\frac{\partial T^{\rm L}}{\partial r}\Bigg|_{R_{1}}=\rho^{\rm S}u_{1}\bigg[(C^{\rm L}-C^{\rm S})(T_{m}^{\rm I}-T_{r})+L_{m}-\frac{1}{2}\bigg(1-(\rho^{\rm SL})^{2}\bigg)({u_{1}})^{2}\bigg].

(5)

We refer the reader to our prior work
[
1
]
for the derivation of the Stefan condition(s) for the two- and three-phase Stefan problems.

Radii
R_{1}(t)
and
R_{b}(t)
are related through mass conservation of the PCM:

\displaystyle\frac{4}{3}\pi\rho^{\rm S}R_{0}^{3}=\frac{4}{3}\pi\rho^{\rm S}R_{1}^{3}(t)+\frac{4}{3}\pi\rho^{\rm L}(R_{b}^{3}(t)-R_{1}^{3}(t))

\displaystyle\hookrightarrow\,

\displaystyle R_{b}^{3}(t)=\rho^{\rm SL}R_{0}^{3}+(1-\rho^{\rm SL})R_{1}^{3}(t).

(6)

The velocity in the solid phase is zero, i.e.,
u^{\rm S}(\Omega^{\rm S},t)\equiv 0
, whereas in the liquid phase is a function of time and position.
u^{\rm L}
is obtained by integrating the mass balance Eq. (
1a
) in the liquid domain. This yields

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\left(r^{2}u^{\rm L}\right)=0,

(7a)

\displaystyle\hookrightarrow\,

\displaystyle u^{\rm L}(r,t)=\frac{A(t)}{r^{2}},

(7b)

in which
A
is a yet to be determined function of time. Applying the Rankine-Hugoniot jump condition across the solid-liquid interface for the mass balance equation relates
A(t)
to the interface position
R_{1}(t)
and velocity
u_{1}(t)

\displaystyle(\rho^{\rm L}-\rho^{\rm S})\frac{{\rm d}R_{1}}{{\rm d}t}=\rho^{\rm L}u^{\rm L}\bigg|_{R}{}_{1},

(8a)

\displaystyle\hookrightarrow\,

\displaystyle u^{\rm L}\bigg|_{R}{}_{1}=\frac{A}{R_{1}^{2}}=\left(1-\rho^{\rm SL}\right)\frac{{\rm d}R_{1}}{{\rm d}t},

(8b)

\displaystyle\hookrightarrow\,

\displaystyle u^{\rm L}(r,t)=\frac{R_{1}^{2}\left(1-\rho^{\rm SL}\right)}{r^{2}}u_{1}.

(8c)

In order to determine the relevant parameters of the problem, we non-dimensionalize the governing equations and the boundary conditions. Non-dimensional variables are indicated with a hat
\widehat{(.)}
symbol. We define

t=\tau\widehat{t},\quad r=R_{0}\,\widehat{r},\quad T=T_{r}+\Delta T\,\widehat{T},

(9)

in which the time, length, and temperature scales are
\tau
,
R_{0}
, and
\Delta T
, respectively. For externally imposed temperature condition, the temperature scale can be defined as
\Delta T=T_{\infty}-T_{r}
. The length and time scales determine the velocity scale of the problem, i.e.,
u=\frac{R_{0}}{\tau}\widehat{u}
. The time scale
\tau=R_{0}^{2}/\alpha^{\rm L}
is chosen based on (thermal) diffusion in the liquid phase; this is not a unique choice. Here,
\alpha^{\rm L}=\kappa^{\rm L}/\rho^{\rm L}C^{\rm L}
is the thermal diffusivity of the liquid phase. The length scale
R_{0}
is taken to be the initial radius of the particle. By using the above scales, the non-dimensional temperature equation in the liquid and solid phases reads as

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\widehat{T}^{\rm S}}{\partial\widehat{t}}+\widehat{u}^{\rm S}\,\frac{\partial\widehat{T}^{\rm S}}{\partial\widehat{r}}

\displaystyle=\alpha^{\rm SL}\,\frac{1}{\widehat{r}^{2}}\,\frac{\partial}{\partial\widehat{r}}\left(\widehat{r}^{2}\frac{\partial\widehat{T}^{\rm S}}{\partial\widehat{r}}\right),

\displaystyle 0\leq\widehat{r}<\widehat{R}_{1}(\widehat{t})

(10a)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\widehat{T}^{\rm L}}{\partial\widehat{t}}+\widehat{u}^{\rm L}\,\frac{\partial\widehat{T}^{\rm L}}{\partial\widehat{r}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\widehat{r}^{2}}\,\frac{\partial}{\partial\widehat{r}}\left(\widehat{r}^{2}\frac{\partial\widehat{T}^{\rm L}}{\partial\widehat{r}}\right).

\displaystyle\widehat{R}_{1}(\widehat{t})<\widehat{r}\leq\widehat{R}_{b}(\widehat{t})

(10b)

Here,
\alpha^{\rm SL}=\alpha^{\rm S}/\alpha^{\rm L}
denotes solid-to-liquid thermal diffusivity ratio,
\widehat{R}_{1}=R_{1}/R_{0}
, and
\widehat{R}_{b}=R_{b}/R_{0}
.

The boundary condition equations are also non-dimensionalized using the same scales. To wit, the non-dimensional version of the interface temperature Eq. (
4
) reads as

\widehat{T}_{m}^{\rm I}=\widehat{T}_{m}-\frac{\Gamma_{m}}{\widehat{R}_{1}},

(11)

in which
\widehat{T}_{m}^{\rm I}=(T_{m}^{\rm I}-T_{r})/\Delta T
,
\widehat{T}_{m}=(T_{m}-T_{r})/\Delta T
, and
\Gamma_{m}=(2\sigma_{\rm SL}T_{m})/(\rho^{\rm S}L_{m}R_{0}\Delta T)
. Proceeding analogously, the non-dimensional version of the Stefan condition Eq. (
5
) reads as

\kappa^{\rm SL}\left.\frac{\partial\widehat{T}^{\rm S}}{\partial\widehat{r}}\right|_{\widehat{R}_{1}}-\left.\frac{\partial\widehat{T}^{\rm L}}{\partial\widehat{r}}\right|_{\widehat{R}_{1}}=\rho^{\rm SL}\beta_{m}\left[1+\gamma_{m}\widehat{T}_{m}^{\rm I}-\frac{1}{2}\bigl(1-(\rho^{\rm SL})^{2}\bigr)\delta_{m}\widehat{u}_{1}^{2}\right]\widehat{u}_{1}.

(12)

Here,
\kappa^{\rm SL}=\kappa^{\rm S}/\kappa^{\rm L}
denotes solid-to-liquid thermal conductivity ratio,
\beta_{m}=L_{m}/(C^{\rm L}\,\Delta T)
is the (liquid) Stefan number,
\gamma_{m}=(C^{\rm L}-C^{\rm S})\Delta T/L_{m}
is the inverse Stefan number, and
\delta_{m}=R_{0}^{2}/(L_{m}\tau^{2})
is a measure of kinetic energy of the melt front relative to the latent heat of melting.

2.2
Small-time analytical solution to the two-phase spherical Stefan problem

In order to initialize the numerical simulation we need to understand the behavior of the system (interface velocity, phase temperature, etc.) at the initial stages of the melting process. At the initial times of the phase change process, it is reasonable to expect that the melt front will stay close to the outer boundary, i.e.,
\widehat{R}_{1}\approx 1
, and that the initial thickness of the liquid layer will also be small. Based on these assumptions, we re-scale the spatial and temporal coordinates. Specifically, utilizing the smallness parameter
0<\varepsilon\ll 1
, we define small-time position and time coordinates indicated with a tilde
\widetilde{(.)}
symbol:

\widehat{r}=1-\varepsilon\widetilde{r},\quad\widehat{R}_{1}=1-\varepsilon\widetilde{R}_{1},\quad\widehat{R}_{b}=1-\varepsilon\widetilde{R}_{b},\quad\widehat{t}=\varepsilon\widetilde{t}.

(13)

Furthermore, the small-time velocity of the interface is assumed to be constant,
{\rm{d}}\widetilde{R}_{1}/{\rm{d}}\widetilde{t}=P
. Thus, the dimensionless position and velocity of the interface at the initial times can be written as

\displaystyle\widehat{R}_{1}(\widehat{t})=1-\varepsilon P\widetilde{t}=1-P\widehat{t},

(14a)

\displaystyle\widehat{u}_{1}=\frac{{\rm d}\widehat{R}_{1}}{{\rm d}\widehat{t}}=-P.

(14b)

\widetilde{r}
,
\widetilde{R}_{1}
,
\widetilde{t}
, and
P
are assumed to be
\mathcal{O}(1)
.
\widetilde{R}_{1}
and
\widetilde{R}_{b}
are related to each other through Eq. (
6
) as follows:

\displaystyle\widehat{R}_{b}^{3}

\displaystyle=\rho^{\rm SL}+(1-\rho^{\rm SL})\widehat{R}_{1}^{3},

\displaystyle\widehat{R}_{b}^{3}

\displaystyle=\rho^{\rm SL}+(1-\rho^{\rm SL})(1-\varepsilon\widetilde{R}_{1})^{3},

\displaystyle\widehat{R}_{b}^{3}

\displaystyle\approx\rho^{\rm SL}+(1-\rho^{\rm SL})(1-3\varepsilon\widetilde{R}_{1}),

\displaystyle\widehat{R}_{b}^{3}

\displaystyle=1-3\varepsilon(1-\rho^{\rm SL})\widetilde{R}_{1},

\displaystyle\widehat{R}_{b}

\displaystyle=\bigg[1-3\varepsilon(1-\rho^{\rm SL})\widetilde{R}_{1}\bigg]^{\frac{1}{3}},

\displaystyle 1-\varepsilon\widetilde{R}_{b}

\displaystyle\approx 1-\varepsilon(1-\rho^{\rm SL})\widetilde{R}_{1},

\displaystyle\implies\widetilde{R}_{b}

\displaystyle=(1-\rho^{\rm SL})\widetilde{R}_{1}.

(15)

Next we apply these small-time scalings to the dimensionless temperature Eq. (
10b
) for the liquid phase. Various terms in the temperature equation scale as

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\widehat{T}^{\rm L}}{\partial\widehat{t}}=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\,\frac{\partial\widetilde{T}^{\rm L}}{\partial\widetilde{t}},

(16a)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\widehat{T}^{\rm L}}{\partial\widehat{r}}=-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\,\frac{\partial\widetilde{T}^{\rm L}}{\partial\widetilde{r}},

(16b)

\displaystyle\widehat{u}^{\rm L}=\frac{(1-\rho^{\rm SL})\widehat{R}_{1}^{2}}{\widehat{r}^{2}}\widehat{u}_{1}=\frac{(1-\rho^{\rm SL})(1-\varepsilon\widetilde{R}_{1})^{2}}{(1-\varepsilon\widetilde{r})^{2}}(-P),

(16c)

\displaystyle\widehat{r}^{2}=(1-\varepsilon\widetilde{r})^{2}=1-2\varepsilon\widetilde{r}+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{2}),

(16d)

\displaystyle\widehat{r}^{2}\frac{\partial\widehat{T}^{\rm L}}{\partial\widehat{r}}=(1-2\varepsilon\widetilde{r}+\cdots)\left(-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\,\frac{\partial\widetilde{T}^{\rm L}}{\partial\widetilde{r}}\right)=-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\,\frac{\partial\widetilde{T}^{\rm L}}{\partial\widetilde{r}}+\mathcal{O}(1),

(16e)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\widehat{r}}\left(\widehat{r}^{2}\frac{\partial\widehat{T}^{\rm L}}{\partial\widehat{r}}\right)=-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\,\frac{\partial}{\partial\widetilde{r}}\left(-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\,\frac{\partial\widetilde{T}^{\rm L}}{\partial\widetilde{r}}+\mathcal{O}(1)\right)=\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\,\frac{\partial^{2}\widetilde{T}^{\rm L}}{\partial\widetilde{r}^{2}}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right),

(16f)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\widehat{r}^{2}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\widehat{r}}\left(\widehat{r}^{2}\frac{\partial\widehat{T}^{\rm L}}{\partial\widehat{r}}\right)=\left[1+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)\right]\left[\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\,\frac{\partial^{2}\widetilde{T}^{\rm L}}{\partial\widetilde{r}^{2}}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)\right]=\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\,\frac{\partial^{2}\widetilde{T}^{\rm L}}{\partial\widetilde{r}^{2}}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right).

(16g)

According to the magnitude of the solid-liquid density ratio
\rho^{\rm SL}\approx 1
or

\widehat{u}^{\rm L}
(Eq. (
16c

\displaystyle\widehat{u}^{\rm L}

\displaystyle\approx(1-\rho^{\rm SL})(-P)\approx P=\mathcal{O}(1),

(17a)

\displaystyle\widehat{u}^{\rm L}

\displaystyle\approx\left(1-\frac{\widetilde{\rho}^{\rm SL}}{\varepsilon}\right)(-P)\approx\frac{\widetilde{\rho}^{\rm SL}}{\varepsilon}P=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right),

2.2.1
Small-time low-density-ratio solution (LDRS) to the two-phase spherical Stefan problem

Substituting Eqs. (
16

17a

10b

\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\,\frac{\partial\widetilde{T}^{\rm L}}{\partial\widetilde{t}}-\frac{P}{\varepsilon}\,\frac{\partial\widetilde{T}^{\rm L}}{\partial\widetilde{r}}=\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\,\frac{\partial^{2}\widetilde{T}^{\rm L}}{\partial\widetilde{r}^{2}}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right),

\frac{\partial^{2}\widetilde{T}^{\rm L}}{\partial\widetilde{r}^{2}}=0.

(19)

The general solution of Eq. (

\widetilde{T}^{\rm L}(\widetilde{r},\widetilde{t})=C_{1}(\widetilde{t})\,\widetilde{r}+C_{2}(\widetilde{t}).

\widetilde{T}^{\rm L}(\widetilde{R}_{b},\widetilde{t})=\widehat{T}_{\infty}

\widehat{T}_{\infty}=(T_{\infty}-T_{r})/\Delta T
. The interface temperature condition requires
\widetilde{T}^{\rm L}(\widetilde{R}_{1},\widetilde{t})=\widetilde{T}_{m}^{\rm I}

\widetilde{T}_{m}^{\rm I}=\widehat{T}_{m}-\Gamma_{m}
. Imposing the two temperature conditions to Eq. (
20

\displaystyle C_{1}\widetilde{R}_{b}+C_{2}

\displaystyle=C_{1}(1-\rho^{\rm SL})\widetilde{R}_{1}+C_{2}=\widehat{T}_{\infty},

(21a)

\displaystyle C_{1}\widetilde{R}_{1}+C_{2}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{T}_{m}^{\rm I}.

(21b)

Solving for
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
from the equations above yields small-time temperature in the liquid phase as

\displaystyle\widetilde{T}^{\rm L}(\widetilde{r},\widetilde{t})=\frac{\widehat{T}_{\infty}-\widetilde{T}_{m}^{\rm I}}{\rho^{\rm SL}}\left(1-\frac{\widetilde{r}}{\widetilde{R}_{1}}\right)+\widetilde{T}_{m}^{\rm I},

(22a)

\displaystyle\hookrightarrow\,

\displaystyle\widehat{T}^{\rm L}(\widehat{r},\widehat{t})=\frac{\widehat{T}_{\infty}-\widetilde{T}_{m}^{\rm I}}{\rho^{\rm SL}}\left(1-\frac{1-\widehat{r}}{1-\widehat{R}_{1}}\right)+\widetilde{T}_{m}^{\rm I}.

(22b)

Note that Eq. (
22b
) is obtained from Eq. (
22a
) by substituting
\widetilde{r}=(1-\widehat{r})/\varepsilon
and
\widetilde{R}_{1}=(1-\widehat{R}_{1})/\varepsilon
.

Analogously, the leading-order term in the solid phase’s temperature equation is

\frac{\partial^{2}\widetilde{T}^{\rm S}}{\partial\widetilde{r}^{2}}=0.

(23)

Since
\frac{\partial\widehat{T}^{\rm S}}{\partial\widehat{r}}\bigg|_{\widehat{r}=0}=0
, and
\widehat{T}^{\rm S}(\widehat{R}_{1},\widehat{t})=\widehat{T}_{m}^{\rm I}
, at initial times the solid phase has a uniform temperature throughout its domain per Eq. (
23
):

\widetilde{T}^{\rm S}(\widetilde{r},\widetilde{t})=\widehat{T}^{\rm S}(\widehat{r},\widehat{t})=\widetilde{T}_{m}^{\rm I}.

(24)

In order to determine the small-time position and velocity of the interface using Eqs. (
14
), the value of
P
needs to be determined. This is obtained by substituting the small-time solutions
\widehat{T}^{\rm L}
and
\widehat{T}^{\rm S}
into the Stefan Eq. (
12
)

\displaystyle\kappa^{\rm SL}\left.\frac{\partial\widehat{T}^{\rm S}}{\partial\widehat{r}}\right|_{\widehat{R}_{1}}-\left.\frac{\partial\widehat{T}^{\rm L}}{\partial\widehat{r}}\right|_{\widehat{R}_{1}}=\rho^{\rm SL}\beta_{m}\left[1+\gamma_{m}\widehat{T}_{m}^{\rm I}-\frac{1}{2}\bigl(1-(\rho^{\rm SL})^{2}\bigr)\delta_{m}\widehat{u}_{1}^{2}\right]\widehat{u}_{1},

\displaystyle\hookrightarrow\,

\displaystyle\frac{\widehat{T}_{\infty}-\widetilde{T}_{m}^{\rm I}}{\rho^{\rm SL}}\left(\frac{1}{P\widehat{t}}\right)=\rho^{\rm SL}\beta_{m}\left[1+\gamma_{m}\widetilde{T}_{m}^{\rm I}-\frac{1}{2}\bigl(1-(\rho^{\rm SL})^{2}\bigr)\delta_{m}P^{2}\right]P.

(25)

Eq. (
25
) is a quartic equation in
P
that needs to solved numerically using a root finding algorithm. In our numerical experiments we take a small value of the initial nondimensional time
\widehat{t}_{\rm init}\leq 10^{-3}
. Once
P
is determined by solving Eq. (
25
), the small-time low-density-ratio solution (LDRS) to the two-phase Stefan problem is complete.

2.2.2
Small-time high-density-ratio solution (HDRS) to the two-phase spherical Stefan problem

Substituting Eqs. (
16
) and Eq. (
17b
) into Eq. (
10b
)

\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\,\frac{\partial\widetilde{T}^{\rm L}}{\partial\widetilde{t}}-\frac{\widetilde{\rho}^{\rm SL}}{\varepsilon^{2}}P\,\frac{\partial\widetilde{T}^{\rm L}}{\partial\widetilde{r}}=\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\,\frac{\partial^{2}\widetilde{T}^{\rm L}}{\partial\widetilde{r}^{2}}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right),

(26)

yields the leading-order term that determines the small-time temperature in the liquid phase:

\frac{\partial^{2}\widetilde{T}^{\rm L}}{\partial\widetilde{r}^{2}}=G\frac{\partial\widetilde{T}^{\rm L}}{\partial\widetilde{r}}.

(27)

Here,
G=-P\,\widetilde{\rho}^{\rm SL}
. Integrating Eq. (
27
) twice with respect to
\widetilde{r}
gives the general solution of the form

\displaystyle\widetilde{T}^{\rm L}(\widetilde{r},\widetilde{t})

\displaystyle=C_{3}(\widetilde{t})\,\frac{\exp(G\widetilde{r})}{G}+C^{^{\prime}}_{4}(\widetilde{t}),

(28a)

\displaystyle=C_{4}+C_{3}\bigg[\widetilde{r}+\frac{G\widetilde{r}^{2}}{2}+\cdots\bigg],

(28b)

in which we have retained up to quadratic term of the exponential function.
C_{3}
and
C_{4}
functions are determined by imposing the boundary conditions:
\widetilde{T}^{\rm L}(\widetilde{R}_{b},\widetilde{t})=\widehat{T}_{\infty}
and
\widetilde{T}^{\rm L}(\widetilde{R}_{1},\widetilde{t})=\widetilde{T}_{m}^{\rm I}
. This yields

\displaystyle\widetilde{T}^{\rm L}(\widetilde{r},\widetilde{t})=\widehat{T}_{\infty}-(\widehat{T}_{\infty}-\widetilde{T}_{m}^{\rm I})\frac{(\widetilde{R}_{b}-\widetilde{r})+\frac{G}{2}(\widetilde{R}_{b}^{2}-\widetilde{r}^{2})}{(\widetilde{R}_{b}-\widetilde{R}_{1})+\frac{G}{2}(\widetilde{R}_{b}^{2}-\widetilde{R}_{1}^{2})},

(29a)

\displaystyle\hookrightarrow\,

\displaystyle\widehat{T}^{\rm L}(\widehat{r},\widehat{t})=\widehat{T}_{\infty}-(\widehat{T}_{\infty}-\widetilde{T}_{m}^{\rm I})\frac{(1-\rho^{\rm SL})^{2}(1-\widehat{R}_{1})^{2}-(1-\widehat{r})^{2}}{(1-\rho^{\rm SL})^{2}(1-\widehat{R}_{1})^{2}-(1-\widehat{R}_{1})^{2}}.

(29b)

Eq. (
29b
) is obtained from Eq. (
29a
) by substituting
\widetilde{r}=(1-\widehat{r})/\varepsilon
and
\widetilde{R}_{1}=(1-\widehat{R}_{1})/\varepsilon
, and taking the limit
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
. The temperature in the solid domain remains the same as written in Eq. (
24
).

Finally, substituting the small-time HDRS
\widehat{T}^{\rm L}
and
\widehat{T}^{\rm S}
into the Stefan Eq. (
12
) gives us the equation for calculating
P
for the high density ratio case:

\frac{\widehat{T}_{\infty}-\widetilde{T}_{m}^{\rm I}}{\big[1-(1-\rho^{\rm SL})^{2}\big]}\left(\frac{2}{P\widehat{t}}\right)=\rho^{\rm SL}\beta_{m}\left[1+\gamma_{m}\widetilde{T}_{m}^{\rm I}-\frac{1}{2}\bigl(1-(\rho^{\rm SL})^{2}\bigr)\delta_{m}P^{2}\right]P.

(30)

2.3
Three phase spherical Stefan problem

For the the three-phase Stefan problem there are three interfaces present in the domain: solid-liquid interface
R_{1}(t)
, liquid-vapor interface
R_{2}(t)
, and outer free boundary
R_{b}(t)
with the condition
0<R_{1}(t)<R_{2}(t)<R_{b}(t)
. The dimensional form of the temperature equation in the solid, liquid, and vapor phases reads as

\displaystyle\rho^{\rm S}C^{\rm S}\left(\frac{\partial T^{\rm S}}{\partial t}+u^{\rm S}\,\frac{\partial T^{\rm S}}{\partial r}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\kappa^{\rm S}}{r^{2}}\,\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\left(r^{2}\frac{\partial T^{\rm S}}{\partial r}\right),

\displaystyle 0\leq r<R_{1}(t)

(31a)

\displaystyle\rho^{\rm L}C^{\rm L}\left(\frac{\partial T^{\rm L}}{\partial t}+u^{\rm L}\,\frac{\partial T^{\rm L}}{\partial r}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\kappa^{\rm L}}{r^{2}}\,\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\left(r^{2}\frac{\partial T^{\rm L}}{\partial r}\right),

\displaystyle R_{1}(t)<r<R_{2}(t)

(31b)

\displaystyle\rho^{\rm V}C^{\rm V}\left(\frac{\partial T^{\rm V}}{\partial t}+u^{\rm V}\,\frac{\partial T^{\rm V}}{\partial r}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\kappa^{\rm V}}{r^{2}}\,\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\left(r^{2}\frac{\partial T^{\rm V}}{\partial r}\right).

\displaystyle R_{2}(t)<r\leq R_{b}(t)

(31c)

Using the same scales for length, time, and temperature as defined in Sec.
2.1
, the dimensionless form of Eqs. (
31
) reads as

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\widehat{T}^{\rm S}}{\partial\widehat{t}}+\widehat{u}^{\rm S}\,\frac{\partial\widehat{T}^{\rm S}}{\partial\widehat{r}}

\displaystyle=\alpha^{\rm SL}\,\frac{1}{\widehat{r}^{2}}\,\frac{\partial}{\partial\widehat{r}}\left(\widehat{r}^{2}\frac{\partial\widehat{T}^{\rm S}}{\partial\widehat{r}}\right),

\displaystyle 0\leq\widehat{r}<\widehat{R}_{1}(\widehat{t})

(32a)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\widehat{T}^{\rm L}}{\partial\widehat{t}}+\widehat{u}^{\rm L}\,\frac{\partial\widehat{T}^{\rm L}}{\partial\widehat{r}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\widehat{r}^{2}}\,\frac{\partial}{\partial\widehat{r}}\left(\widehat{r}^{2}\frac{\partial\widehat{T}^{\rm L}}{\partial\widehat{r}}\right),

\displaystyle\widehat{R}_{1}(\widehat{t})<\widehat{r}<\widehat{R}_{2}(\widehat{t})

(32b)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\widehat{T}^{\rm V}}{\partial\widehat{t}}+\widehat{u}^{\rm V}\,\frac{\partial\widehat{T}^{\rm V}}{\partial\widehat{r}}

\displaystyle=\alpha^{\rm VL}\,\frac{1}{\widehat{r}^{2}}\,\frac{\partial}{\partial\widehat{r}}\left(\widehat{r}^{2}\frac{\partial\widehat{T}^{\rm V}}{\partial\widehat{r}}\right).

\displaystyle\widehat{R}_{2}(\widehat{t})<\widehat{r}\leq\widehat{R}_{b}(\widehat{t})

(32c)

The dimensionless form of the Gibbs-Thomson relation that defines temperature at the boiling front reads as

\widehat{T}_{v}^{\rm I}=\widehat{T}_{v}-\frac{\Gamma_{v}}{\widehat{R}_{2}},

(33)

in which
\widehat{T}_{v}^{\rm I}=(T_{v}^{\rm I}-T_{r})/\Delta T
,
\widehat{T}_{v}=(T_{v}-T_{r})/\Delta T
, and
\Gamma_{v}=(2\sigma_{\rm LV}T_{v})/(\rho^{\rm L}L_{v}R_{0}\Delta T)
. The dimensionless form of the solid-liquid interface temperature
\widehat{T}_{m}^{\rm I}
remains the same as written in Eq. (
11
). The temperature at the outer boundary is maintained at
T_{\infty}
, so that the condition
\widehat{T}^{\rm V}(\widehat{R}_{b},\widehat{t})=\widehat{T}_{\infty}
holds at all times. By applying the Rankine-Hugoniot jump condition to the energy equation, we obtain the Stefan condition for the boiling front:

\displaystyle\kappa^{\rm L}\frac{\partial T^{\rm L}}{\partial r}\Bigg|_{R_{2}(t)}-\kappa^{\rm V}\frac{\partial T^{\rm V}}{\partial r}\Bigg|_{R_{2}(t)}=\bigg(\rho^{\rm L}u_{2}-(\rho^{\rm L}-\rho^{\rm S})u_{1}\bigg)\Bigg[(C^{\rm V}-C^{\rm L})(T_{v}^{\rm I}-T_{r})+L_{v}-\frac{1}{2}\Big\{(1-\rho^{\rm SL})u_{1}\Big\}^{2}+

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\frac{1}{2}\Big\{(\rho^{\rm LV}-\rho^{\rm SV})u_{1}+(1-\rho^{\rm LV})u_{2}\Big\}^{2}-\Big\{(\rho^{\rm LV}-\rho^{\rm SV})u_{1}+(1-\rho^{\rm LV})u_{2}-(1-\rho^{\rm SL})u_{1}\Big\}u_{2}\Bigg].

(34)

Our prior work
[
1
]
provides a detailed derivation of the Stefan conditions for the three-phase case. Eq. (
2.3
) when non-dimensionalized with the scaling parameters defined previously gives

\left.\frac{\partial\widehat{T}^{\rm L}}{\partial\widehat{r}}\right|_{\widehat{R}_{2}}-\kappa^{\rm VL}\left.\frac{\partial\widehat{T}^{\rm V}}{\partial\widehat{r}}\right|_{\widehat{R}_{2}}=\beta_{v}\Big(\widehat{u}_{2}-(1-\rho^{\rm SL})\widehat{u}_{1}\Big)S_{v},

(35)

in which
S_{v}
is defined as

\displaystyle S_{v}

\displaystyle=\Bigg[1+\gamma_{v}\widehat{T}_{v}^{\rm I}-\frac{1}{2}(1-\rho^{\rm SL})^{2}\delta_{v}\,\widehat{u}_{1}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\delta_{v}\Big\{(\rho^{\rm LV}-\rho^{\rm SV})\widehat{u}_{1}+(1-\rho^{\rm LV})\widehat{u}_{2}\Big\}^{2}-

\displaystyle\quad\quad\delta_{v}\Big\{(\rho^{\rm LV}-\rho^{\rm SV})\widehat{u}_{1}+(1-\rho^{\rm LV})\widehat{u}_{2}-(1-\rho^{\rm SL})\widehat{u}_{1}\Big\}\widehat{u}_{2}\Bigg].

(36)

Here,
\kappa^{\rm VL}=\kappa^{\rm V}/\kappa^{\rm L}
denotes vapor-to-liquid thermal conductivity ratio,
\beta_{v}=L_{v}/(C^{\rm L}\,\Delta T)
is the (vapor) Stefan number,
\gamma_{v}=(C^{\rm V}-C^{\rm L})\Delta T/L_{v}
is the inverse Stefan number, and
\delta_{v}=R_{0}^{2}/(L_{v}\tau^{2})
is a measure of kinetic energy of the boiling front relative to the latent heat of vaporization. The dimensional and dimensionless version of the Stefan equation for the melt front remains the same as written in Eqs. (
5
) and (
12
), respectively.

The velocity in the solid phase is zero, i.e.,
u^{\rm S}(\Omega^{\rm S},t)\equiv 0
, whereas in the liquid phase remains the same as written in Eq. (
8c
). The velocity in the vapor phase
u^{\rm V}
is obtained by integrating the mass balance Eq. (
1a
) in the vapor domain. This yields

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\left(r^{2}u^{\rm V}\right)=0,

(37a)

\displaystyle\hookrightarrow\,

\displaystyle u^{\rm V}(r,t)=\frac{B(t)}{r^{2}},

(37b)

in which
B
is a yet to be determined function of time. Applying the Rankine-Hugoniot jump condition to the mass balance equation across the liquid-vapor interface relates
B(t)
to the interfacial positions
R_{1}(t)
and
R_{2}(t)
, and interfacial velocities
u_{1}(t)
and
u_{2}(t)
:

\displaystyle(\rho^{\rm L}-\rho^{\rm V})\frac{{\rm d}R_{2}}{{\rm d}t}=(\rho^{\rm L}u^{\rm L}-\rho^{\rm V}u^{\rm V})\bigg|_{R}{}_{2},

(38a)

\displaystyle\hookrightarrow\,

\displaystyle(\rho^{\rm L}-\rho^{\rm V})\frac{{\rm d}R_{2}}{{\rm d}t}=\rho^{\rm L}(1-\rho^{\rm SL})\frac{R_{1}^{2}}{R_{2}^{2}}\frac{{\rm d}R_{1}}{{\rm d}t}-\rho^{\rm V}\frac{B}{R_{2}^{2}},

(38b)

\displaystyle\hookrightarrow\,

\displaystyle B(t)=\bigg[(\rho^{\rm LV}-\rho^{\rm SV})R_{1}^{2}\frac{{\rm d}R_{1}}{{\rm d}t}-(\rho^{\rm LV}-1)R_{2}^{2}\frac{{\rm d}R_{2}}{{\rm d}t}\bigg],

(38c)

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle u^{\rm V}(r,t)=\bigg[(\rho^{\rm LV}-\rho^{\rm SV})\frac{R_{1}^{2}}{r^{2}}u_{1}-(\rho^{\rm LV}-1)\frac{R_{2}^{2}}{r^{2}}u_{2}\bigg],

(38d)

\displaystyle\hookrightarrow\,

\displaystyle\widehat{u}^{\rm V}(\widehat{r},\widehat{t})=\bigg[(\rho^{\rm LV}-\rho^{\rm SV})\frac{\widehat{R}_{1}^{2}}{\widehat{r}^{2}}\widehat{u}_{1}-(\rho^{\rm LV}-1)\frac{\widehat{R}_{2}^{2}}{\widehat{r}^{2}}\widehat{u}_{2}\bigg].

(38e)

Radii
R_{b}(t)
,
R_{1}(t)
, and
R_{2}(t)
are related to each other through the mass conservation of the PCM (similar to the two-phase case). Alternatively, Eq. (
38d
) can be used to derive the relationship between the three radii:

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}R_{b}}{{\rm d}t}=u^{\rm V}(R_{b},t)=\bigg[(\rho^{\rm LV}-\rho^{\rm SV})\frac{R_{1}^{2}}{R_{b}^{2}}\frac{{\rm d}R_{1}}{{\rm d}t}-(\rho^{\rm LV}-1)\frac{R_{2}^{2}}{R_{b}^{2}}\frac{{\rm d}R_{2}}{{\rm d}t}\bigg],

(39a)

\displaystyle\hookrightarrow\,

\displaystyle R_{b}^{2}\,{\rm{d}}R_{b}=(\rho^{\rm LV}-\rho^{\rm SV})R_{1}^{2}\,{\rm{d}}R_{1}-(\rho^{\rm LV}-1)R_{2}^{2}\,{\rm{d}}R_{2},

(39b)

\displaystyle\implies\,

\displaystyle R_{b}^{3}=a(R_{1}^{3}-R_{0}^{3})-b(R_{2}^{3}-R_{0}^{3})+R_{2}^{3},

(39c)

\displaystyle\hookrightarrow\,

\displaystyle\widehat{R}_{b}^{3}=a(\widehat{R}_{1}^{3}-1)-b(\widehat{R}_{2}^{3}-1)+\widehat{R}_{2}^{3},

(39d)

in which we have used the initial condition
R_{1}=R_{2}=R_{b}=R_{0}
at
t=0
while integrating Eq. (
39b
), and defined
a=(\rho^{\rm LV}-\rho^{\rm SV})
and
b=\rho^{\rm LV}
in Eqs. (
39c
) and (
39d
).

2.4
Small-time analytical solution to the three-phase spherical Stefan problem

Having discussed the governing equations and the boundary conditions for the three-phase Stefan problem, we derive the small-time analytical solution to initialize the simulation. At the initial times we assume
\widehat{R}_{1}\approx\widehat{R}_{2}\approx\widehat{R}_{b}\approx 1
. By using this ansatz, same as the one used for the two-phase case, we define small-time position and time coordinates using
0<\varepsilon\ll 1
:

\widehat{r}=1-\varepsilon\widetilde{r},\quad\widehat{R}_{1}=1-\varepsilon\widetilde{R}_{1},\quad\widehat{R}_{2}=1-\varepsilon\widetilde{R}_{2},\quad\widehat{R}_{b}=1-\varepsilon\widetilde{R}_{b},\quad\widehat{t}=\varepsilon\widetilde{t}.

(40)

A constant small-time velocity is assumed for the two interfaces, such that
{\rm{d}}\widetilde{R}_{1}/{\rm{d}}\widetilde{t}=P
and
{\rm{d}}\widetilde{R}_{2}/{\rm{d}}\widetilde{t}=Q
. As a result, the initial positions and velocities of the two interfaces can be expressed as follows:

\displaystyle\widehat{R}_{1}(\widehat{t})=1-\varepsilon P\widetilde{t}=1-P\widehat{t},

(41a)

\displaystyle\widehat{R}_{2}(\widehat{t})=1-\varepsilon Q\widetilde{t}=1-Q\widehat{t},

(41b)

\displaystyle\widehat{u}_{1}=\frac{{\rm d}\widehat{R}_{1}}{{\rm d}\widehat{t}}=-P,

(41c)

\displaystyle\widehat{u}_{2}=\frac{{\rm d}\widehat{R}_{2}}{{\rm d}\widehat{t}}=-Q.

(41d)

Small-time radii
\widetilde{R}_{1}
,
\widetilde{R}_{2}
, and
\widetilde{R}_{b}
are related to each other via Eq. (
39d
). Following steps similar to those used to arrive at Eq. (
15
),
\widetilde{R}_{b}
for three-phase reads as

\widetilde{R}_{b}=a\widetilde{R}_{1}+(1-b)\widetilde{R}_{2}.

(42)

According to the magnitude of the liquid-vapor and solid-vapor density ratio
\rho^{\rm LV}
and
\rho^{\rm SV}
, respectively, the vapor domain velocity
\widehat{u}^{\rm V}
(Eq. (
38e
)) scales as

\displaystyle\widehat{u}^{\rm V}

\displaystyle\approx\mathcal{O}(1),

[low density ratio scaling]

(43a)

\displaystyle\widehat{u}^{\rm V}

\displaystyle\approx\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right),

[high density ratio scaling]

(43b)

in which the large liquid-vapor (similarly solid-vapor) density ratio is scaled as
\rho^{\rm LV}=\widetilde{\rho}^{\rm LV}/\varepsilon
with
\widetilde{\rho}^{\rm LV}\sim\mathcal{O}(1)
. Therefore, we obtain two different small-time solutions for low and high liquid-vapor/solid-vapor density ratios.

2.4.1
Small-time low-density-ratio solution (LDRS) to the three-phase spherical Stefan problem

Proceeding analogously and assuming low density ratio between the three phases, the leading-order terms for the temperature equation in the three phases read as

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}\widetilde{T}^{\rm S}}{\partial\widetilde{r}^{2}}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\in\Omega^{\rm S}

(44a)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}\widetilde{T}^{\rm L}}{\partial\widetilde{r}^{2}}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\in\Omega^{\rm L}

(44b)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}\widetilde{T}^{\rm V}}{\partial\widetilde{r}^{2}}

\displaystyle=0.

\displaystyle\in\Omega^{\rm V}

(44c)

The small-time solution for temperature in the solid domain corresponds to Eq. (
24
). For the small-time liquid and vapor temperature solutions,
\widetilde{T}^{\rm L}(\widetilde{r},\widetilde{t})=D_{1}(\widetilde{t})\,\widetilde{r}+D_{2}(\widetilde{t})
and
\widetilde{T}^{\rm V}(\widetilde{r},\widetilde{t})=E_{1}(\widetilde{t})\,\widetilde{r}+E_{2}(\widetilde{t})
, respectively, the unknown functions (of time)
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
, and
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
are determined by imposing appropriate boundary conditions. These are
\widetilde{T}^{\rm L}(\widetilde{R}_{1},\widetilde{t})=\widetilde{T}_{m}^{\rm I}
,
\widetilde{T}^{\rm L}(\widetilde{R}_{2},\widetilde{t})=\widetilde{T}_{v}^{\rm I}
,
\widetilde{T}^{\rm V}(\widetilde{R}_{2},\widetilde{t})=\widetilde{T}_{v}^{\rm I}
, and
\widetilde{T}^{\rm V}(\widetilde{R}_{b},\widetilde{t})=\widehat{T}_{\infty}
. Here, the leading-order interface temperatures are denoted
\widetilde{T}_{m}^{\rm I}=\widehat{T}_{m}-\Gamma_{m}
and
\widetilde{T}_{v}^{\rm I}=\widehat{T}_{v}-\Gamma_{v}
. Solving for the unknowns, the small-time temperature solution in the three phases (in terms of non-dimensional variables) reads as

\displaystyle\widehat{T}^{\rm S}(\widehat{r},\widehat{t})

\displaystyle=\widetilde{T}_{m}^{\rm I},

\displaystyle 0\leq\widehat{r}<\widehat{R}_{1}(\widehat{t})

(45a)

\displaystyle\widehat{T}^{\rm L}(\widehat{r},\widehat{t})

\displaystyle=\frac{\widetilde{T}_{v}^{\rm I}-\widetilde{T}_{m}^{\rm I}}{\widehat{R}_{2}-\widehat{R}_{1}}(\widehat{r}-\widehat{R}_{2})+\widetilde{T}_{v}^{\rm I},

\displaystyle\widehat{R}_{1}(\widehat{t})<\widehat{r}<\widehat{R}_{2}(\widehat{t})

(45b)

\displaystyle\widehat{T}^{\rm V}(\widehat{r},\widehat{t})

\displaystyle=\frac{\widehat{T}_{\infty}-\widetilde{T}_{v}^{\rm I}}{(a-b)+b\widehat{R}_{2}-a\widehat{R}_{1}}(\widehat{R}_{2}-\widehat{r})+\widetilde{T}_{v}^{\rm I}.

\displaystyle\widehat{R}_{2}(\widehat{t})<\widehat{r}\leq\widehat{R}_{b}(\widehat{t})

(45c)

The small-time solutions written is Eqs. (
45
) are complete once the values of
P
and
Q
are determined. This can be achieved by substituting the small-time solutions into the Stefan Eqs. (
12
) and (
35
) for the melt and boiling fronts, respectively. Thus, we have the following two coupled equations in
P
and
Q
, which are solved numerically:

\displaystyle\frac{\widetilde{T}_{v}^{\rm I}-\widetilde{T}_{m}^{\rm I}}{(P-Q)\widehat{t}}

\displaystyle=\rho^{\rm SL}\,\beta_{m}\left[1+\gamma_{m}\widetilde{T}_{m}^{\rm I}-\frac{1}{2}\bigl(1-(\rho^{\rm SL})^{2}\bigr)\delta_{m}\,P^{2}\right]P,

(46a)

\displaystyle\frac{\widetilde{T}_{v}^{\rm I}-\widetilde{T}_{m}^{\rm I}}{(P-Q)\widehat{t}}+\,\frac{\kappa^{\rm VL}(\widehat{T}_{\infty}-\widetilde{T}_{v}^{\rm I})}{\big(aP-bQ\big)\widehat{t}}

\displaystyle=\beta_{v}\Big(-Q+(1-\rho^{\rm SL})P\Big)\,S_{v}.

(46b)

S_{v}
in Eq. (
46b
) is evaluated using small-time interface speeds
\widehat{u}_{1}=-P
and
\widehat{u}_{2}=-Q
and interface temperature
\widehat{T}_{v}^{\rm I}=\widetilde{T}_{v}^{\rm I}
as

\displaystyle S_{v}

\displaystyle=\Bigg[1+\gamma_{v}\widetilde{T}_{v}^{\rm I}-\frac{1}{2}(1-\rho^{\rm SL})^{2}\delta_{v}\,P^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\delta_{v}\Big\{(\rho^{\rm LV}-\rho^{\rm SV})P+(1-\rho^{\rm LV})Q\Big\}^{2}-

\displaystyle\quad\quad\delta_{v}\Big\{(\rho^{\rm LV}-\rho^{\rm SV})P+(1-\rho^{\rm LV})Q-(1-\rho^{\rm SL})P\Big\}Q\Bigg].

(47)

Using the numerical solution of Eqs. (
46
), the small-time prediction of melt and boiling front positions are
\widehat{R}_{1}=1-P\widehat{t}_{\rm init}
and
\widehat{R}_{2}=1-Q\widehat{t}_{\rm init}
, respectively, and that of the outer free boundary is
\widehat{R}_{b}=1-(aP+(1-b)Q)\widehat{t}_{\rm init}
. A more accurate prediction of the initial outer boundary location can be obtained from the small-time estimates of
\widehat{R}_{1}
and
\widehat{R}_{2}
through the use of Eq. (
39d
):
\widehat{R}_{b}=\sqrt[3]{a(\widehat{R}_{1}^{3}-1)-b(\widehat{R}_{2}^{3}-1)+\widehat{R}_{2}^{3}}
. For a physically correct small-time solution, these predictions should satisfy the condition
\widehat{R}_{1}<\widehat{R}_{2}<\widehat{R}_{b}
. Furthermore, if Eq. (
39d
) is used then
\widehat{R}_{b}\in\mathbb{R}
. For the three-phase case of Sec.
5.2
, our empirical tests show that as long as
\rho^{\rm L}/10\lesssim\rho^{\rm V}\leq\rho^{\rm L}
, we obtain physically correct small-time predictions of the interface positions. Additionally, the two estimates of
\widehat{R}_{b}
are quite close to each other for this range of
\rho^{\rm V}
. Assuming a low density ratio, Myers et al.
[
11
]
introduced the small-time solution technique in the context of two-phase melting of a nanosphere induced by a convective boundary condition at the exterior boundary. The authors considered tin and gold nanoparticles with a small solid-to-liquid density ratio of
\rho^{\rm SL}=1.0287
and
\rho^{\rm SL}=1.1156
, respectively. To obtain physically correct small-time solutions for large density ratios (
\rho^{\rm SL}>10
or
\rho^{\rm LV}>10
) we use the correct scaling for the liquid and vapor phase velocity as written in Eqs. (
17b
) and (
43b
), respectively. This is analyzed in the next section.

2.4.2
Small-time high-density-ratio solution (HDRS) to the three-phase spherical Stefan problem

If the density ratio between the three phases is large, then the leading order terms for the temperature equation in the three phases read as

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}\widetilde{T}^{\rm S}}{\partial\widetilde{r}^{2}}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\in\Omega^{\rm S}

(48a)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}\widetilde{T}^{\rm L}}{\partial\widetilde{r}^{2}}

\displaystyle=M\frac{\partial\widetilde{T}^{\rm L}}{\partial\widetilde{r}},

\displaystyle\in\Omega^{\rm L}

(48b)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}\widetilde{T}^{\rm V}}{\partial\widetilde{r}^{2}}

\displaystyle=N\frac{\partial\widetilde{T}^{\rm V}}{\partial\widetilde{r}},

\displaystyle\in\Omega^{\rm V}

(48c)

in which
M
and
N
are
\mathcal{O}(1)
constants. Proceeding analogously to Sec.

and imposing appropriate boundary conditions on the three interfaces, the small-time HDRS for temperature in the three phases reads as

\displaystyle\widehat{T}^{\rm S}(\widehat{r},\widehat{t})

\displaystyle=\widetilde{T}_{m}^{\rm I},

\displaystyle 0\leq\widehat{r}<\widehat{R}_{1}(\widehat{t})

(49a)

\displaystyle\widehat{T}^{\rm L}(\widehat{r},\widehat{t})

\displaystyle=\widetilde{T}_{v}^{\rm I}-\big(\widetilde{T}_{v}^{\rm I}-\widetilde{T}_{m}^{\rm I}\big)\frac{(1-\widehat{R}_{2})^{2}-(1-\widehat{r})^{2}}{(1-\widehat{R}_{2})^{2}-(1-\widehat{R}_{1})^{2}},

\displaystyle\widehat{R}_{1}(\widehat{t})<\widehat{r}<\widehat{R}_{2}(\widehat{t})

(49b)

\displaystyle\widehat{T}^{\rm V}(\widehat{r},\widehat{t})

\displaystyle=\widehat{T}_{\infty}-\big(\widehat{T}_{\infty}-\widetilde{T}_{v}^{\rm I}\big)\frac{\big\{a(1-\widehat{R}_{1})+(1-b)(1-\widehat{R}_{2})\big\}^{2}-(1-\widehat{r})^{2}}{\big\{a(1-\widehat{R}_{1})+(1-b)(1-\widehat{R}_{2})\big\}^{2}-(1-\widehat{R}_{2})^{2}}.

\displaystyle\widehat{R}_{2}(\widehat{t})<\widehat{r}\leq\widehat{R}_{b}(\widehat{t})

(49c)

\displaystyle\frac{2P(\widetilde{T}_{v}^{\rm I}-\widetilde{T}_{m}^{\rm I})}{(P^{2}-Q^{2})\widehat{t}}

\displaystyle=\rho^{\rm SL}\,\beta_{m}\left[1+\gamma_{m}\widetilde{T}_{m}^{\rm I}-\frac{1}{2}\bigl(1-(\rho^{\rm SL})^{2}\bigr)\delta_{m}\,P^{2}\right]P,

(50b)

Here,

)

. This is even for vapor density as low as

3
A fixed-grid sharp-interface technique to solve the three-phase Stefan problem numerically

Figure 2:
Schematic of the 1D grid used to discretize the spherical heat equations using the sharp interface technique. Irregular cells
j
and
j+1
,
p
and
p+1
, and
N
abut interfaces separating phases
A
and
B
, and phases
B
and
C
, and outer free boundary, respectively. The cell centers are marked with
(\bullet)
, and the evolving interfaces and free boundary are marked with dashed lines (

).

In this section, we present a fixed-grid, sharp-interface method to solve the dimensionless heat/temperature Eqs. (
32a
)-(
32c
) in the evolving solid, liquid and vapor domains. Stefan conditions and interface temperature conditions on the evolving solid-liquid and liquid-vapor interfaces are imposed sharply in this approach. We describe the discretization process for the three-phase case as it is more general. In the following discussion we drop the
\widehat{(.)}
symbol from the dimensionless variables for the ease of notation.

The one-dimensional domain
\Omega:=0\leq r\leq R_{\rm max}
is discretized into
N_{\rm max}
cells of uniform size
\Delta r
, such that
N_{\rm max}=\lceil R_{\rm max}/\Delta r\rceil
. Here,
R_{\rm max}=(\rho^{\rm SV})^{\frac{1}{3}}
is the maximum radius of the sphere, when all of the initial solid particle (of unit initial radius) has evaporated. The discrete temperature
T_{i}
and velocity
u_{i}
are both stored at the
i^{\rm th}
(with
i=1,\ldots,N
) cell-center having a coordinate
r_{i}=(i-\frac{1}{2})\Delta r
.
N_{\rm max}
number of cells are computed based on user-defined
N_{\rm min}
number of cells that are needed to resolve the initial thickness of the liquid and vapor layers,
\Delta_{0}^{\rm L}
and
\Delta_{0}^{\rm V}
, respectively. Specifically, we define
\Delta r:=\textrm{min}(\Delta_{0}^{\rm L},\Delta_{0}^{\rm V})/N_{\rm min}
to control the grid resolution. We compute the initial liquid and vapor layers’ thicknesses by
\Delta_{0}^{\rm L}=R_{2}-R_{1}=(P-Q)t_{\rm init}
and
\Delta_{0}^{\rm V}=R_{b}-R_{2}=(bQ-aP)t_{\rm init}
.

The heat equation is discretized using a second-order finite difference stencil. We treat conduction/diffusion implicitly and convection explicitly. The heat equation is discretized only for regular grid cells that do not contain or abut the moving interfaces (
s_{1}=R_{1}
,
s_{2}=R_{2}
, and
s_{3}=R_{b}
). In contrast, irregular cells containing or abutting the interface impose interface temperature conditions. With
n
denoting the time level, and
\Delta t
denoting the time-step size, the discrete form of the heat equation for a regular grid cell
i
at (midpoint) time
t=(n+\frac{1}{2})\Delta t
reads as

\displaystyle\frac{T_{i}^{n+1}-T_{i}^{n}}{\Delta t}+\bigg(u_{i}\frac{\widebar{T}_{i+\frac{1}{2}}-\widebar{T}_{i-\frac{1}{2}}}{\Delta r}\bigg)^{n+\frac{1}{2}}=\frac{\alpha_{i}}{r_{i}^{2}}\frac{1}{\Delta r}\bigg[

\displaystyle r_{i+\frac{1}{2}}^{2}\frac{T^{n+1}_{i+1}-T^{n+1}_{i}}{2\Delta r}-r_{i-\frac{1}{2}}^{2}\frac{T^{n+1}_{i}-T^{n+1}_{i-1}}{2\Delta r}

\displaystyle+r_{i+\frac{1}{2}}^{2}\frac{T^{n}_{i+1}-T^{n}_{i}}{2\Delta r}-r_{i-\frac{1}{2}}^{2}\frac{T^{n}_{i}-T^{n}_{i-1}}{2\Delta r}\bigg].

(51)

Here,
\widebar{T}
denotes the CUI (cubic upwind interpolated)
[
13
]
limited temperature field on the left (
i-\frac{1}{2}
) and right (
i+\frac{1}{2}
) faces of the regular grid cell
i
. Because we store the discrete values of temperature at cell centers, the discretized form of the Laplace operator in spherical coordinates does not suffer from the
r=0
singularity. For the first grid cell with index
i=1
, we directly impose the homogeneous Neumann boundary condition on its left face. An extrapolation of the form
\phi^{n+\frac{1}{2}}=\frac{3}{2}\phi^{n}-\frac{1}{2}\phi^{n-1}
is performed to evaluate the velocity and temperature at time level
n+\frac{1}{2}
for the convective term. As the simulation advances, the number of unknown discrete temperatures
N
varies based on the position of the outer boundary
s_{3}=R_{b}(t)
. We do not solve for temperature locations beyond
R_{b}(t)
as those are assumed to be at a constant value of
T_{\infty}
. We stop the simulation when the solid is almost completely melted, but has not yet fully evaporated. Therefore, at any instant the number of unknowns (equivalently the number of cells)
N
in the system of equations are less than
N_{\rm max}
.

Grid cells adjacent to the interface, say
j
and
j+1
, are indicated as irregular in Fig.
2
. We do not discretize the heat equation for irregular cells. To complete the system of equations for
N
unknown temperatures, we need additional equations for the irregular cells. One-sided quadratic extrapolation is used to calculate the temperature at the interface by associating cells
j-2
,
j-1
, and
j
with phase
A
and cells
j+1
,
j+2
, and
j+3
with phase
B
. The one-sided extrapolated temperature at the interface is equated to the interface temperature
T^{\rm I}
(i.e.,
T_{m}^{\rm I}
or
T_{v}^{\rm I}
) to close the system of equations:

\displaystyle T_{A}\bigg|_{r=s}=a_{1}T_{A,j}+a_{2}T_{A,j-1}+a_{3}T_{A,j-2}=T^{\rm I},

(52a)

\displaystyle T_{B}\bigg|_{r=s}=b_{1}T_{B,j+1}+b_{2}T_{B,j+2}+b_{3}T_{B,j+3}=T^{\rm I}.

(52b)

Similarly, for the outer boundary at
s_{3}=R_{b}
, we impose the temperature boundary condition using the three neighboring temperatures in phase C as

T_{C}\bigg|_{r=s_{3}}=c_{1}T_{C,N}+c_{2}T_{C,N-1}+c_{3}T_{C,N-2}=T_{\infty}.

(53)

The coefficients
a_{k}
,
b_{k}
, and
c_{k}
appearing in Eqs. (
52
) and (
53
) are determined by fitting a one-dimensional, second-order Lagrange polynomial to the neighboring nodes. Phase
A
, for example, has coefficients
a_{k}
as follows:

\displaystyle a_{1}=\frac{\left(s-r_{j-1}\right)\left(s-r_{j-2}\right)}{\left(r_{j}-r_{j-1}\right)\left(r_{j}-r_{j-2}\right)},

(54a)

\displaystyle a_{2}=\frac{\left(s-r_{j}\right)\left(s-r_{j-2}\right)}{\left(r_{j-1}-r_{j}\right)\left(r_{j-1}-r_{j-2}\right)},

(54b)

\displaystyle a_{3}=\frac{\left(s-r_{j}\right)\left(s-r_{j-1}\right)}{\left(r_{j-2}-r_{j}\right)\left(r_{j-2}-r_{j-1}\right)}.

(54c)

A similar process is followed for irregular cells
p
and
p+1
that abut the second interface, and cell
N
that abuts the outer boundary.

The discrete Eqs. (
3
), (
52
), and (
53
) form a system of
N
equations that are inverted using a sparse direct solver to calculate the
N
unknown temperatures. At this stage, only the velocities of the two interfaces,
u_{1}^{n+1}
and
u_{2}^{n+1}
need to be determined. Calculating them requires evaluating the one-sided derivatives of temperature
T^{n+1}
required for the right hand side of Stefan Eqs. (
12
) and (
35
). A one-sided derivative of temperature at an interface may be expressed in terms of neighboring nodal temperatures in phase
A
as follows:

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}T_{A}}{{\rm d}r}\Bigg|_{r=s}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{3}c_{A,j}T_{A,j},

(55a)

\displaystyle\hookrightarrow\frac{{\rm d}T_{A}}{{\rm d}r}\Bigg|_{r=s}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{3}c_{A,j}\left(T_{A}\bigg|_{s}+\frac{{\rm d}T_{A}}{{\rm d}r}\bigg|_{s}(r_{j}-s)+\frac{1}{2}\frac{{\rm d^{2}}T_{A}}{{\rm d}{r}^{2}}\bigg|_{s}(r_{j}-s)^{2}+\mathcal{O}\left((r_{j}-s)^{3}\right)\right).

(55b)

Eq. (
55b
) follows from Eq. (
55a
) through the use of the Taylor series expansion to express neighboring nodal temperatures in terms of the interface temperature and its derivatives. Ignoring higher order derivatives in Eq. (
55
), and equating coefficients of
T_{A}
,
\frac{{\rm d}T_{A}}{{\rm d}r}
, and
\frac{{\rm d^{2}}T_{A}}{{\rm d}{r}^{2}}
on both sides of the equation, yields a
3\times 3
system of equations for the coefficients
c_{A,j}

\begin{bmatrix}1&1&1\\
\Delta r_{1}&\Delta r_{2}&\Delta r_{3}\\
(\Delta r_{1})^{2}&(\Delta r_{2})^{2}&(\Delta r_{3})^{2}\\
\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}c_{A,1}\\
c_{A,2}\\
c_{A,3}\\
\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\
1\\
0\\
\end{bmatrix}.

(56)

Here,
\Delta r_{j}=(r_{j}-s)
. To calculate
\frac{{\rm d}T_{B}}{{\rm d}r}\bigg|_{r=s}
for phase
B
, a similar procedure is followed. Once the positions of melt and boiling fronts are updated using
u_{1}^{n+1}
and
u_{2}^{n+1}
, the position of the outer boundary is updated using Eq. (
39d
).

6
Conclusions and discussion

In this work, we presented a mathematical model for two- and three-phase spherical Stefan problems that consistently accounts for variation in thermophysical properties, including density between various phases of a phase change material. The Stefan conditions consider a jump in kinetic energy across the phase change interface, which is typically ignored in the literature. We also presented a fixed-grid, sharp-interface method for simulating two- and three-phase spherical Stefan problems which was demonstrated to be second-order in spatiotemporal accuracy. Small-time analytical solutions were derived to initialize the simulation. Large and small density contrasts between the phases are considered to derive the appropriate small-time solutions. The numerical method and the small-time analysis are validated against the results reported in the literature for the two-phase case before utilizing them to solve the three-phase problems. For the three-phase Stefan problem, we quantified the influence of kinetic energy terms (
\delta_{m}
and
\delta_{v}
) on the time evolution of melt and boiling fronts, as well as on the outer free boundary of a nanoparticle. It is found that including kinetic energy terms predicts a longer melting time for the particle than when it is not. The melt front dynamics exhibited greater sensitivity to kinetic energy terms than the boiling front. For the larger particles of size micrometers and above, the kinetic energy terms do not affect the phase change dynamics. In summary, the present study tackles a rather unexplored, but physically relevant heat and mass transfer problem, which is the three-phase version of the classical Stefan problem.
