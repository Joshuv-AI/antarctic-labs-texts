Clinical Management of Antirheumatic Drug Exposure During Therapeutic Plasma Exchange
Authors: Ziyan Ding, Jing Li, Xue Zhong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTherapeutic plasma exchange (TPE) is being increasingly utilized in the clinical management of severe rheumatic immune diseases, providing an effective means for rapidly removing pathogenic autoantibodies and inflammatory mediators. However, the non-selective nature of this technique can also lead to the unintended clearance of concomitantly administered antirheumatic drugs, potentially compromising therapeutic efficacy and disease control. Therefore, effective management of potential drug removal process during TPE and the implementation of individualized risk assessment are crucial for optimizing treatment outcomes in patients undergoing TPE. The variability in the extent of drug removal during TPE is primarily determined by their distinct pharmacokinetic characteristics, necessitating the establishment of a systematic, evidence-based strategy for adjusting drug administration regimens in patients receiving TPE treatment. This review synthesizes current evidence from 65 studies on the removal of antirheumatic drugs during TPE, identifying key determinants influencing clearance rates, including volume of distribution, protein binding, molecular size, and elimination half-life. Our analysis reveals that the risk of drug removal exists as a continuous spectrum: large monoclonal antibodies (e.g., rituximab, natalizumab), characterized by a large molecule size, low volume of distribution, with which mostly confined to the vascular space, are cleared with high efficiency. This finding supports the clinical recommendation of administering such drugs after TPE. For drugs with limited direct evidence, we propose a predictive model based on fundamental pharmacokinetic parameters to estimate their removal risk and guide clinical decision-making. Based on this evidence, we have constructed a stratified clinical management framework. It aims to maintain effective therapeutic drug exposure levels during chronic TPE therapy and to provide a rationale for the judicious application of TPE in overdose scenarios. Implementing this pharmacokinetic-informed, risk-adapted individualized strategy is important for ensuring treatment continuity, enhancing patient safety, and advancing empiricism-based therapy towards precision medicine.Keywords: Therapeutic Plasma Exchange, Antirheumatic Drugs, Drugs Removal, Management and Risk Assessment

Introduction

Autoimmune rheumatic diseases affect approximately 10% of the global population, especially in women, people aged 50 and over, and those with specific genetic backgrounds [1, 2], with the global annual incidence and prevalence of these diseases increasing by 19.1% and 12.5%, respectively [3, 4]. Among these patients, roughly 2–5% may be candidates for therapeutic plasma exchange (TPE) during their disease course, given that TPE is typically reserved for severe, refractory, or life-threatening manifestations like catastrophic antiphospholipid syndrome [5] and so on [6]. Nowadays, with advancements in medical technology, therapeutic plasma exchange (TPE) has become a vital treatment option for patients with autoimmune rheumatic diseases [7]. Rheumatic immune diseases, such as systemic lupus erythematosus, rheumatoid arthritis, systemic sclerosis, and idiopathic inflammatory myopathy, involve complex immune mechanisms that often limit the effectiveness of conventional pharmacotherapy [8].TPE can alleviate symptoms and enhance the quality of life by removing pathological factors, antibodies, and inflammatory mediators [9]. During TPE, the patient’s blood is circulated extracorporeally. Plasma and its pathogenic factors are removed using exchange equipment and replaced with equal volumes of fresh - frozen plasma or albumin solution before reinfusion [7].Although TPE is often used as an adjunct therapy alongside pharmacotherapy, it is important to note that this process can remove harmful substances from plasma while inadvertently eliminating drugs present in the bloodstream. The extent of drug removal during TPE varies based on several factors, including the drug’s properties, patient characteristics, and the technical aspects of the TPE procedure [10].Commonly used antirheumatic drugs, such as glucocorticoids, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (DMARDs), and biologics, may be rapidly cleared during TPE. This may lead to subtherapeutic drug levels, potentially reducing treatment effectiveness and exacerbating the disease. Recent studies have increasingly explored the effects of plasma exchange on drug removal, particularly regarding antibiotics [11]. However, there remains a relative lack of not only such data on the removal of antirheumatic drugs, but also studies applying population pharmacokinetics modelling to guide antirheumatic drug dose adjustment in patients receiving TPE.

This review summarizes relevant data on the removal of antirheumatic drugs during TPE and develops a framework to predict the risk of drug removal. It aims to provide practical guidance for antirheumatic drug dose adjustment during TPE, ensuring these patients safety and efficacy. By integrating pharmacokinetic parameters with clinical practice, this work lays the foundation for future population pharmacokinetics modelling of antirheumatic drugs in patients undergoing TPE. This not only contributes to enhancing treatment strategies for rheumatic immune diseases but also identifies current research gaps and suggests new directions for future studies, including the development of model-informed dose optimization approaches.

Methods of Literature Search

A systematic search was conducted in PubMed, Cochrane Library, Embase, Web of Science, CNKI, and Wanfang Data databases from inception to June 6, 2025. A search strategy combining Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms and free-text keywords was applied. The search terms included any one of the following: “Pharmacokinetics”, “Drug Clearance”, “Drug Dialysability”, “Drug Distribution”, “Drug Elimination”, “Drug Excretion”, “Plasma Concentration”, “Drug Concentration”, “Drug Dose”, “Drug Monitoring”, “Drug remov*”, in combination with “Plasmapheresis” or “Plasma Exchange”. All retrieved records were initially screened by title and abstract to exclude irrelevant and duplicate articles. Then full texts of the remaining articles were reviewed with relevant information extracted. Books, reviews, animal studies, and editorials were excluded. Manual searches of reference lists from relevant reviews and included studies were also performed to maximize the identification of pertinent literature.

Data extracted included drug category, drug name, molecular weight, protein binding affinity, volume of distribution, elimination half-life, clearance, study type (overdose or therapeutic dosing), timing of therapeutic plasma exchange (TPE) relative to drug administration, number of subjects, post-TPE drug clearance ratio (after/before), proportion of total drug cleared by TPE, and replacement solution used.

Besides, the therapeutic index (TI) is critical for assessing whether TPE-mediated drug removal has clinical significance. However, identification of narrow therapeutic index drugs (NTIDs) remains controversial. The current FDA definition [12] (less than 2-fold difference in LD50/ED50) is derived from animal data and lacks clinical relevance, and existing NTID lists are inherently arbitrary. Nonetheless, regulatory frameworks exist: FDA defines NTIDs as drugs where small dose/concentration changes may cause serious therapeutic failure or life-threatening adverse reactions [12, 13]; the Chinese NTID consensus (2025) [14] lists [33] NTIDs based on five characteristics. Among the drugs in our tables, tacrolimus, cyclosporine, methotrexate, and mycophenolate mofetil are formally recognized as NTIDs, all strongly recommended. For these drugs, even modest TPE-induced clearance increases may necessitate supplemental dosing or intensive TDM. Table 4 summarizes their TDM target ranges and relevant pharmacogenetic biomarkers. Most other antirheumatic drugs (biologics, JAK inhibitors, NSAIDs) lack formal NTID designation. However, as even aspirin exhibits NTI characteristics at high antirheumatic doses, illustrating that NTID designation is dose- and indication- dependent.

Among the included studies, approximately one half of the reports addressed drug removal by TPE during therapeutic use, whereas about one quarter involved the application of TPE in the setting of drug overdose; the remaining one quarter did not clearly specify the clinical context. Acknowledging that drug pharmacokinetics can differ significantly between therapeutic and overdose contexts [15], and that a portion of the literature involves overdose scenarios, this analysis primarily focuses on evidence derived from therapeutic drug use to enhance the relevance of recommendations for standard clinical practice. For each drug, we evaluate its likelihood of being removed by TPE based on integrated pharmacokinetic and clinical evidence, while for those without TPE clearance data we adapt PK parameters to estimat their likehood. This analysis and synthesis culminate in practical, evidence-based clinical recommendations for drug management in patients undergoing TPE to minimize risks from unintended drug clearance or suboptimal exposure.

Corticosteroids

Corticosteroids (CS), synthetic analogs of adrenal steroid hormones, are first-line immunomodulatory agents for numerous autoimmune diseases due to their potent anti-inflammatory and immunosuppressive effects [83]‚ despite the development of targeted antibody therapies [84].Characterized by high protein binding (77–95%) and a moderate volume of distribution (Vd 0.6–1.41 L/kg),data for prednisone and prednisolone [16] (Table 1) indicate that despite these features, therapeutic plasma exchange (TPE) still removes a small but measurable fraction of certain corticosteroids (e.g., 0.74–0.83% for prednisolone), attributed to their confined intravascular distribution. Key determinants of inter-agent variability, such as lipophilicity (log P) and molecular surface area, influence clearance, distribution, and protein binding [85]. While specific clearance data for agents like dexamethasone and methylprednisolone are lacking, their high protein binding and moderate Vd (Table 2) suggest minimal removal by TPE—a conclusion supported by comparative pharmacokinetic studies [86]. Based on these pharmacokinetic characteristics and its potential toxicity, routine post-TPE dosing is not recommended. Consider supplementation only if TPE is performed near peak concentration or with large exchange volume.

Immunosuppressants

This heterogeneous class demonstrates variable susceptibility to TPE, primarily dictated by molecular size and distribution characteristics.

Calcineurin Inhibitors (Cyclosporine, Tacrolimus)

Calcineurin inhibitors (cyclosporine and tacrolimus) suppress T-cell-mediated immunity by inhibiting calcineurin following binding to their respective intracellular immunophilins, thereby blocking cytokine production [87]. These highly lipophilic agents undergo extensive CYP3A-mediated metabolism and are substrates for P-glycoprotein, leading to significant pharmacokinetic variability [88]. They exhibit extensive tissue distribution and high binding to erythrocytes and plasma proteins. Consequently, therapeutic plasma exchange (TPE) removes only a negligible fraction of cyclosporine (0.2–0.3%) and minimally affects tacrolimus levels, despite differences in their volumes of distribution (Cyclosporine exhibits a significantly larger Vd compared to tacrolimus). Notably, in a heart-transplant case, the elimination efficiency of tacrolimus from TPE appeared to be negative values [26]. This may be caused by the combined effects of assay variability, minor hemodynamic fluctuations during the procedure, and the rapid re-equilibration of tacrolimus from its predominant erythrocyte reservoir into the plasma compartment, which have also underscored that the net removal of tacrolimus by TPE is negligible. In all, routine TPE does not meaningfully enhance their elimination, and no dose adjustment is required. However, aggressive TPE may be considered as a rescue intervention in life-threatening overdose scenarios.

Antimetabolites

Mycophenolate Mofetil/Acid

Mycophenolate Mofetil (MMF) is a pro-drug of Mycophenolic acid (MPA). MPA potently and selectively inhibits lymphocyte proliferation by reversibly blocking inosine monophosphate dehydrogenase (IMPDH), an enzyme critical for purine synthesis in lymphocytes [89]. Despite high protein binding (> 97%), therapeutic plasma exchange (TPE) clears a substantial portion (19–70%) of the drug from the plasma per session, possibly related to procedural timing and the marked inter-individual variability in MPA levels (up to 10-fold) [90], partly influenced by genetic factors such as UGT1A9 polymorphisms [91]. However, due to extensive tissue distribution, this removes only a minor fraction (0.44–2.8%) of the total body burden (Table 1). The impact on overall exposure is generally not clinically significant, and routine dose adjustment is not required. However, if TPE is performed within 4 h of drug administration—encompassing the time to peak concentration for both mycophenolate mofetil (~ 1–2 h) and enteric-coated mycophenolate sodium (~ 4 h)[92]—post-TPE dosing is advisable to maintain therapeutic levels.

Azathioprine(AZA)

AZA (a prodrug of 6-mercaptopurine) inhibits purine synthesis and lymphocyte proliferation [93, 94]. Characterized by moderate volume of distribution and the requirement for hepatic conversion to active metabolites, TPE does not significantly alter its systemic exposure, as summarized in Table 3. Although there is no accurate evidence about the influences of TPE, factors such as variable gastrointestinal absorption of AZA [95] and genetic influences on its metabolism [96] with its pharmacokinetic features are all support the conclusion. Routine post-TPE additional dosing is not recommended since it is slightly influenced by TPE.

Methotrexate (MTX)

MTX, a folate antagonist that exerts its immunosuppressive effect primarily by inhibiting dihydrofolate reductase, is a cornerstone in the treatment of various autoimmune diseases97. When assessing its removal during therapeutic plasma exchange (TPE), its pharmacokinetic profile is pivotal. Despite a moderate protein binding (50–60%) and a relatively low volume of distribution (0.4–0.8 L/kg), TPE clears only a minimal fraction (0.39–2%) of the total body burden. This inefficacy is attributed to MTX’s rapid intracellular distribution and active renal tubular secretion, which maintain a low plasma-to-tissue concentration ratio, rendering extracorporeal removal largely ineffective.

Clinical management should therefore not rely on TPE for MTX detoxification. Dosing should continue as scheduled post-TPE to prevent disease flare. The potential utility of TPE in this context is limited to managing complications, such as providing renal support in cases of acute kidney injury secondary to high-dose therapy, which may indirectly aid excretion [98]. The focus must remain on therapeutic drug monitoring and renal function assessment, not on attempting enhanced clearance via TPE.

Alkylating Agent

Cyclophosphamide (CYP) acts as a DNA-alkylating agent [99]. Its pharmacokinetic properties—moderate Vd and hepatic conversion to active metabolites—limit its efficient removal by TPE, as summarized in Table 3. Although precise evidence is lacking, available data support that TPE does not significantly alter systemic exposure. Therefore, routine post-TPE additional dosing is not recommended due to its potential toxicity.

Biologic Agents (Monoclonal Antibodies)

This subclass is highly susceptible to removal by TPE. Their large molecular size (> 100 kDa), minimal extravascular distribution (low Vd, typically < 0.2 L/kg), and proteinaceous nature make them ideal targets for plasma extraction.

Anti-CD20 (Rituximab)

Rituximab exerts its therapeutic effects by binding to CD20 on B-lymphocytes, leading to B-cell depletion [100]. As a monoclonal antibody with large molecular size of ~ 150 kD and predominantly intravascular distribution, this biologic agent is efficiently removed by therapeutic plasma exchange (TPE), with per-session clearance rates of 45–77% (Table 1). Consequently, standard administration after TPE is recommended to prevent significant drug loss.

In specific clinical scenarios, additional measures are needed. For rituximab, a supplemental dose is advised if TPE must closely follow its infusion. This tailored approach is further supported by the considerable interindividual pharmacokinetic variability of rituximab, which is influenced by factors such as disease phenotype, FCGR genotype, and immunogenicity [100].

Anti-IL-6R (Tocilizumab), Anti-C5 (Eculizumab)

Two additional monoclonal antibodies—Tocilizumab (IL-6 signal inhibition) [101], and Eculizumab (complement C5 blockade) [49]—exhibit the characteristic pharmacokinetic profile of large molecular size (> 145 kDa), and low volume of distribution (< 0.3 L/kg). Although specific clearance data during therapeutic plasma exchange (TPE) are not yet established, these shared properties allow the inference that they are highly susceptible to efficient removal by TPE. Consistent with agents of this class, TPE is expected to markedly accelerate their elimination (e.g., reducing the half-life of eculizumab to 1–6 h). Therefore, post-TPE administration is uniformly recommended to maintain effective plasma concentrations, with supplemental dosing considered in particular when multiple TPE sessions are planned.

Notably, while interindividual pharmacokinetic variability exists—such as the nonlinear clearance of Eculizumab [102]—these variations do not alter the fundamental susceptibility of these agents to TPE-mediated removal, nor do they undermine the core principle of post-TPE dosing. In clinical practice, therapeutic drug monitoring combined with individualized management remains essential to ensure efficacy, especially when serial TPE is required.

Immunomodulators

Antimalarials

Hydroxychloroquine, which exerts immunomodulatory effects in autoimmune diseases through mechanisms such as interfering with lysosomal activity and Toll-like receptor signaling [103], share a pharmacokinetic profile that renders therapeutic plasma exchange (TPE) highly ineffective (Table 2). This is primarily due to their characteristically low plasma protein binding (40–55%) and, most critically, an extremely large volume of distribution (Vd > 10 L/kg, exceeding 100 L/kg for chloroquine). These properties indicate extensive tissue sequestration, leaving a negligible fraction (< 1%) of the total body burden in the vascular compartment accessible for removal by TPE. Therefore, for patients on chronic therapy, no dosage adjustment is required during or after TPE, as the procedure will not meaningfully alter systemic drug exposure or therapeutic efficacy. This principle extends to overdose scenarios, where evidence-based guidelines (EXTRIP) strongly advise against using TPE due to its futility in enhancing clearance [104]. Management should focus solely on aggressive supportive care.

In summary, the vast tissue distribution of antimalarials creates a significant pharmacokinetic barrier to extracorporeal removal. TPE is pharmacokinetically ineffective, and its use does not warrant any change to existing dosing regimens.

Intravenous Immunoglobulin (IVIg)

IVIg is a pooled polyclonal antibody preparation that exerts immunomodulatory effects via multiple mechanisms, including Fc receptor blockade and anti-inflammatory actions [53]. Despite differing mechanisms, this immunomodulator shows a critical pharmacokinetic profile that dictates their management during therapeutic plasma exchange (TPE): they are large protein molecules (MW: ~300 kDa) with a very low volume of distribution (Vd: IVIg 0.042–0.107 L/kg). This confirms the predominant confinement to the intravascular space, making it highly susceptible to efficient removal by TPE.

Consequently, the primary clinical risk management strategy is strict scheduling. This is critical for IVIg, for which administration before or during TPE is strictly contraindicated; it must be infused only after the entire TPE course is finished [53]. Adherence to this sequence is non-negotiable to ensure therapeutic efficacy and avoid the complete, wasteful elimination of these high-cost biological agents.

Analgesics

The effect of TPE on NSAIDs is inconsistent, influenced by their high protein binding and varying Vd.

Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), which primarily exert their therapeutic effects by inhibiting cyclooxygenase (COX) enzymes, are cornerstone therapies for inflammatory pain [105]. Their near-ubiquitous high plasma protein binding (> 90%) makes them potential targets for therapeutic plasma exchange (TPE). However, the actual efficiency of TPE-mediated clearance is predominantly determined by their volume of distribution (Vd). Agents with a low Vd (typically < 0.3 L/kg) are more effectively removed, while clearance is less efficient and unpredictable for those with a higher or more variable Vd.

Aspirin, Ibuprofen, Diclofenac (Table 1):

This subset has direct quantitative evidence from studies, enabling precise risk assessment. Aspirin has high protein binding (80–90%) and a very low Vd (0.1–0.2 L/kg). Pharmacokinetic data (n = 6) show a wide, unpredictable range of total body clearance by TPE (0.65–32%), attributable to its complex, nonlinear kinetics [106]. TPE is not an efficient or reliable method for salicylate removal. Post-TPE dosing is advised to maintain therapeutic effect. Ibuprofen [107] presents an ideal profile for TPE: very high protein binding (> 95%) and a low Vd (0.11–0.19 L/kg). Clinical data (n = 30) confirm effective clearance, with an average plasma concentration reduction of ~ 35% and ~ 17% of total burden removed. TPE is a viable intervention for ibuprofen, though rebound may occur. Dose supplementation post-TPE should be considered. Diclofenac [108] is > 99% protein-bound, but its Vd is more variable (0.12–0.55 L/kg). Correspondingly, TPE clearance data (n = 4) show high inter-individual variability (1.1–55%). Clearance by TPE is possible but unpredictable. Post-procedure dosing is considered based on clinical assessment, routine dosing is not recommended as the evidence is equivocal.

Meloxicam, Naproxen, Carprofen (Table 2)

For these agents, direct TPE data are lacking, but their pharmacokinetics and animal studies strongly suggest high clearance potential. Meloxicam [109] (human Vd ~ 0.14 L/kg, protein binding 99.4%) and Naproxen [110] (Vd 0.16 L/kg, protein binding > 99%) share a profile of very high binding and low Vd. Veterinary evidence (e.g., a 49% plasma concentration reduction for carprofen in dogs) supports high TPE efficacy. TPE is inferred to be a highly effective rescue modality for overdose of these drugs. In patients on chronic therapy, post-TPE re-dosing is likely necessary. Carprofen (Vd 0.18 L/kg), though rarely used in humans, serves as a reinforcing pharmacokinetic model [111]. Canine studies demonstrated substantial clearance post-TPE, validating the principle for high-binding, low-Vd NSAIDs. Consequently, TPE could be considered a plausible adjunct for severe intoxication by NSAIDs sharing these properties.

In summary, for low-Vd NSAIDs (ibuprofen, meloxicam, naproxen), TPE can significantly reduce plasma concentrations, necessitating post-procedure dose supplementation to maintain therapy. For agents with unpredictable clearance (aspirin, diclofenac), TPE is not a standard detoxification method, and therapeutic dosing should be resumed post-TPE with monitoring. TPE may be considered as an adjunct in severe, refractory overdoses of select NSAIDs based on their specific pharmacokinetics.

Other Antirheumatic Drugs: Pharmacokinetic-Based Risk Assessment for Removal

For antirheumatic drugs lacking direct quantitative data on removal by Therapeutic Plasma Exchange (TPE), their extracorporeal clearance potential can be predicted based on core pharmacokinetic (PK) parameters. The key determinants are molecular size, volume of distribution (Vd), and the extent of protein binding, with specific parameters for relevant drugs systematically summarized in Table 3.

Biologic agents, including various monoclonal antibodies (e.g., adalimumab, infliximab, secukinumab, belimumab) and fusion proteins (e.g., abatacept, etanercept), carry the highest risk of removal due to their large molecular weight (> 100 kDa) and very low Vd (typically < 0.2–0.3 L/kg). This profile indicates predominantly intravascular distribution and high plasma concentration, making them highly susceptible to efficient clearance by TPE.

The likelihood of removal for conventional synthetic antirheumatic drugs is more variable. Agents characterized by “high protein binding (> 80%) and a low Vd (< 0.2–0.3 L/kg),” such as the active metabolite sulfapyridine of sulfasalazine and the active metabolite of leflunomide, share a PK profile similar to TPE-removable NSAIDs, suggesting a theoretical potential for significant clearance. Conversely, for drugs with a substantially larger Vd (> 1.0–2.0 L/kg), such as JAK inhibitors (tofacitinib, baricitinib), extensive tissue distribution means the fraction present in plasma is minimal, resulting in predictably low TPE clearance efficiency and a negligible impact on total systemic exposure.

In summary, the propensity of a drug to be removed by TPE is not arbitrary but is dictated by its intrinsic pharmacokinetic properties. This parameter-based analysis provides the necessary evidence foundation for systematically understanding the factors affecting TPE removal efficiency and for subsequently formulating pharmacokinetically-informed clinical practice guidelines.

Integrating Pharmacokinetic Principles with Clinical Practice

Factors Affecting Drug Removal by TPE

Therapeutic plasma exchange (TPE), while aimed at removing pathological circulating factors, may inadvertently deplete concomitantly administered drugs, thereby posing a risk to therapeutic efficacy for the primary disease [10]. The extent of this iatrogenic drug clearance is not uniform but is governed by a confluence of factors, which can be systematically categorized into three primary domains: the intrinsic pharmacokinetic (PK) properties of the drug, the technical parameters of the TPE procedure, and the critical timing of drug administration relative to the TPE session [112]. A comprehensive understanding of these determinants, summarized in Fig. 1, is essential for anticipating and mitigating unintended drug loss in clinical practice.

Key factors influencing drug removal by TPE

Distribution

Among pharmacokinetic parameters, volume of distribution (Vd) and protein binding (Pb) are critical determinants of a drug’s susceptibility to removal by TPE [112]. These properties reflect the extent to which a drug remains within the central plasma compartment, thereby influencing its accessibility to extracorporeal removal. The Vd represents the apparent volume in which a drug is distributed throughout the body [113]. A smaller Vd indicates limited tissue distribution and a higher proportion of the drug remaining in plasma, resulting in increased susceptibility to TPE-mediated clearance. In general, drugs with a Vd < 0.2–0.3 L/kg are considered more amenable to removal by TPE [114].

Many drugs bind to plasma proteins, primarily albumin and α1-acid glycoprotein [115]. Drugs with high protein-binding affinity, typically defined as a binding fraction (fb) > 80%, tend to remain predominantly in the plasma compartment, thereby increasing their likelihood of removal during TPE. The fundamental principle underlying TPE is that higher proportion of drug resides in blood/plasma relative to tissue distribution would facilitate greater extracorporeal drug clearance [114]. In addition, protein binding may be influenced by several clinical factors, including (1) disease states such as renal or hepatic failure, (2) inflammatory conditions resulting from trauma, surgery, or infection, and (3) factors affecting protein metabolism, such as nutritional status, all of which may alter the extent of drug–protein interactions [10].

Eliminate

Endogenous clearance and elimination half-life are also critical pharmacokinetic parameters affecting the efficiency of drug removal by TPE [116]. Drugs with lower endogenous clearance persist longer in the systemic circulation [117], thereby increasing their susceptibility to TPE-mediated removal. For example, studies have shown that 6.9% of ceftazidime was removed in patients with a clearance of < 50 mL/min, compared with 4.6% in those with a clearance of > 50 mL/min [118]. Similarly, a longer elimination half-life prolongs the duration during which a drug remains in plasma [119], increasing the opportunity for removal by TPE. In contrast, drugs with high endogenous clearance or short half-lives are eliminated more rapidly, limiting the effectiveness of TPE.

It has been suggested that when endogenous clearance is < 4 mL/min, or when drug metabolism is impaired due to renal or hepatic dysfunction, the likelihood of drug removal by TPE is increased, as a greater proportion of the drug remains available in the systemic circulation [112].

Clinical Relevance of Vd and Clearance for Dosing

Beyond their role in predicting TPE-mediated removal, Vd and systemic clearance(CL) are fundamental pharmacokinetic parameters that directly guide clinical dosing. Specifically, Vd determines the loading dose needed to achieve therapeutic concentrations (Drugs with a large Vd require higher loading doses to rapidly achieve its target) [113, 120, 121]. During TPE, extracorporeal removal adds an additional clearance pathway, effectively increasing total CL. Therefore, knowing a drug’s baseline Vd and CL is essential for predicting the impact of TPE on drug exposure and for rationally adjusting maintenance doses post-TPE, especially for drugs with a narrow therapeutic index, because even a modest increase in CL due to TPE may necessitate supplemental dosing to maintain efficacy and prevent subtherapeutic levels for them.

Multicompartment Model

The multicompartment pharmacokinetic model of a drug may further influence its removal during TPE [112]. Drugs exhibiting multicompartment distribution undergo two distinct phases: a distribution phase, during which the drug moves from plasma (central compartment) to peripheral tissues, and an elimination phase, during which the drug redistributes back into plasma for clearance [122]. The efficiency of TPE depends on the phase during which the procedure is performed. If TPE is initiated during the distribution phase, higher plasma concentrations facilitate more effective removal. In contrast, performing TPE during the elimination phase generally results in lower removal efficiency, as a larger fraction of the drug resides in peripheral tissues. Therefore, for drugs with multicompartment pharmacokinetics, the timing of TPE relative to drug administration is critical.

Others

Additional factors influencing drug removal during TPE include the duration and frequency of TPE sessions [123], the volume of replacement fluid [7, 123, 124]. Typically, a TPE session lasts 2–3 h; prolonging the procedure or increasing its frequency may enhance drug removal. The replacement fluid volume is also a key determinant. Previous studies have demonstrated that replacement volumes of 1.0, 1.5, and 2.0 times the plasma volume can remove approximately 63%, 78%, and 86% of macromolecular plasma components, respectively. Accordingly, a replacement volume of 1–1.5 times the plasma volume is generally recommended, which allows for removal of approximately 63%–78% of plasma constituents, while larger volumes may further increase drug clearance. Finally, the timing of drug administration relative to TPE plays a crucial role in determining drug loss [125]. Administering drugs after TPE generally results in minimal removal, whereas administration shortly before TPE increases the likelihood of clearance. Available evidence suggests that, whenever feasible, drugs should be administered at least 4–6 h after plasma exchange to compensate for drug loss. If post-TPE administration is not possible, the distribution half-life of the drug (if available) may be used to estimate the proportion of drug that has redistributed into the extravascular space at the time of TPE initiation.

A Clinical Management Framework Based on Risk Stratification

Building on the drug-specific evidence and mechanistic principles summarized above, this section proposes a pragmatic, tiered framework for managing antirheumatic drugs during TPE. The framework is divided into two complementary components: risk stratification (Fig. 2A) and clinical implementation (Fig. 2B), with an integrated decision pathway illustrated in Fig. 2. This strategy aims to proactively prevent futile clearance, actively manage predictable removal, and correctly identify scenarios requiring no intervention.

Framework Diagram for Stratified Management Strategy of Antirheumatic Drugs During TPE

Risk Stratification Based on Pharmacokinetic Parameters

The risk of clinically significant drug removal by TPE can be stratified into three tiers based on core pharmacokinetic parameters, primarily volume of distribution (Vd), molecular weight, and protein binding(Fig. 2A).

High-Risk Drugs

The first tier predominantly comprises large-molecule biologics. This class includes all monoclonal antibodies (e.g., rituximab, tocilizumab), and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIg). Their defining characteristics are a high molecular weight (> 150 kDa) and an exceptionally low Vd (< 0.2 L/kg), confining them almost entirely to the vascular space. Standard TPE can remove 60–85% of their circulating concentration per session.

Moderate-to-Low-Risk Drugs

The second tier encompasses chemically diverse conventional synthetic antirheumatic agents, requiring finer pharmacokinetic discernment One subgroup, including certain NSAIDs (e.g., ibuprofen, meloxicam, naproxen) and specific chemotherapeutic/immunosuppressive agents (e.g., mycophenolic acid), exhibits significant plasma clearance by TPE due to high protein binding despite a low-to-moderate Vd Another subgroup includes two distinct pharmacokinetic scenarios: drugs with a very large Vd (e.g., antimalarials like hydroxychloroquine, calcineurin inhibitors like cyclosporine and tacrolimus) where TPE contribution is trivial (< 1%); and drugs with very high intrinsic clearance (e.g., methotrexate) where the body’s inherent eliminative capacity far outweighs any TPE-mediated removal.

Clinical Implementation Strategies

Based on the above risk stratification, the following management strategies are recommended (Fig. 2B).

For high-risk drugs, management is non-negotiable a mandatory sequential strategy: a strict “post-TPE administration” protocol must be enforced. IVIg must never be infused before or during TPE and should only be initiated after all planned TPE sessions are complete. Subcutaneous or intravenous injections of interferon-beta and therapeutic monoclonal antibodies should be scheduled after an individual TPE session or upon completion of an entire TPE cycle. For patients requiring serial TPE post-transplant or as part of a specific regimen (e.g., after basiliximab), supplemental dosing may be necessary if more than three TPE sessions occur within a short period (e.g., one month), guided by trough level monitoring.

For moderate-to-low-risk drugs, we can choose a monitoring and adjustment strategy. For theses in the significant-clearance subgroup, post-TPE administration with potential dose supplementation is recommended. For drugs in the trivial-clearance subgroup, routine dose adjustment is generally unnecessary. Of note, for methotrexate overdose, the role of TPE is not enhanced clearance but renal support in the context of concurrent acute kidney injury.

General implementation principles must accompany this stratified approach. Optimizing administration timing by scheduling doses after TPE sessions, whenever clinically feasible, is a universal tactic to minimize unintended drug loss. Therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM) is invaluable for agents with a narrow therapeutic index (e.g., mycophenolic acid, tacrolimus) [126], as officially recognized by the FDA [12] and Chinese NTID consensus [14]. For these drugs, even a modest increase in clearance due to TPE may necessitate dose adjustments to maintain efficacy and prevent toxicity. For other antirheumatic drugs without formal NTID designation, TDM is not routinely required but may be considered on a case-by-case basis, especially when clinical response is suboptimal or toxicity is suspected. As emphasized in Sect. 3, the same drug may behave as an NTID at higher doses (e.g., aspirin for rheumatoid arthritis), so clinical judgment should always prevail. Furthermore, the choice of replacement fluid (e.g., albumin solution vs. fresh frozen plasma) [127] may influence protein binding equilibria and free drug concentrations, a consideration particularly relevant for highly protein-bound drugs.

Application in overdose scenarios

When considering TPE as a rescue intervention for drug overdose, the following stepwise approach is recommended. First, confirm life-threatening toxicity (e.g., refractory arrhythmias, status epilepticus) unresponsive to maximal medical management including specific antidotes. Second, perform a critical pharmacokinetic feasibility screen using the offending drug’s Vd serves as the primary gatekeeper. A Vd > 1–2 L/kg generally predicts TPE inefficacy and should preclude its use, while a Vd < 0.2–0.3 L/kg indicates technical feasibility. Third, consult specific evidence (like the clinical practice pathway in this study) and guidelines (like EXTRIP) [104]. For instance, data robustly support TPE for theophylline overdose, while strongly advising against it for chloroquine poisoning. The decision then proceeds to a comprehensive risk-benefit analysis and individualized judgment, weighing the potential benefit of rapid concentration reduction against the procedural risks of TPE (invasiveness, hemodynamic shifts, resource intensity) and the phenomenon of post-TPE rebound from tissue stores. The availability of superior extracorporeal modalities (e.g., hemoperfusion) must also be considered. Finally, upon proceeding, implement early intervention with adequate exchange volume (e.g., 1.5 plasma volumes) and guide therapy by serial drug concentration measurements.

Limitations and Strength

This systematic review possesses inherent limitations that must be acknowledged. The primary constraint is the variable quality and nature of the underlying evidence, which predominantly consists of case reports, small observational studies, and data extrapolated from overdose scenarios or animal models, rather than robust prospective clinical trials. These factors introduce potential biases and limit the certainty of some recommendations, particularly for newer agents. Furthermore, the proposed management and decision pathways, while grounded in pharmacokinetic principles, require prospective clinical validation to assess their impact on hard patient outcomes. Additionally, no mathematical model based on population pharmacokinetics(popPK) exists to guide dose adjustment during or after TPE. Currently, evidence on drug removal is largely descriptive, and recommendations remain empirical rather than predictive. Future studies should develop popPK models incorporating TPE-specific covariates to enable individualized dosing for these patients. What’s more, while Table 4 provides TDM guidance for the NTIDs identified in this review, quantitative therapeutic index values are not available for most other antirheumatic drugs. NTID lists vary by jurisdiction and are inherently arbitrary; therefore, clinical judgment remains essential Despite these constraints, the study’s strengths are substantial. Its principal contribution is the first comprehensive synthesis and analysis of TPE clearance data across the entire spectrum of antirheumatic drugs, transforming scattered evidence into a structured, actionable knowledge base. More importantly, it establishes a clear, mechanism-driven predictive framework and clinical practice pathway, which dual focus on both risk mitigation and therapeutic application, centered on core pharmacokinetic parameters like volume of distribution, offers a practical tool for advancing precision medicine at the intersection of apheresis and rheumatology

Conclusion

Therapeutic plasma exchange is a critical intervention in managing severe rheumatic diseases, but it exerts a complex and significant influence on the pharmacokinetics of concurrently administered antirheumatic drugs. This systematic review demonstrates that the risk of drug removal by TPE is not unpredictable but is primarily governed by core pharmacokinetic parameters. Based on this, we successfully synthesized available evidence into a clear, hierarchical management framework to help clinical practice. In summary, implementing pharmacokinetics-informed management strategies of antirheumatic drugs during TPE can help in mitigating unintended drug loss and guiding rational detoxification, which is essential for ensuring continuity of care for the primary disease, improving outcomes in critical care, and safeguarding patient safety. These improvements contribute to precision medicine in apheresis, with therapeutic drug monitoring being a key future direction
