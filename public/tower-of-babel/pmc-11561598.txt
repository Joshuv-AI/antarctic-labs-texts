Effects of Microheterogeneous Wettability and Pore-Throat Structure on Asphaltene Precipitation in Tight Sandstone: Results from Nuclear Magnetic Resonance
Authors: Zhen Zhou, Yiqian Qu, Xin Cheng, Yuting Cheng, Shuqi Lan, Zhengqi Yang, Liancheng Wei, Wei Sun, Hanning Wu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe pore throat structure and microheterogeneous wettability of tight sandstone reservoirs are complex, which leads to varying asphaltene precipitation locations, contents, and distributions in different pores during CO2 flooding. Clarifying the heterogeneous wettability of different pore throat structures and their effects on asphaltene precipitation and adsorption is crucial for improving CO2 displacement efficiency. A series of experiments were conducted in this study, including X-ray diffraction (XRD), cast thin section (CTS), field emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM), high-pressure mercury intrusion (HPMI), environmental scanning electron microscopy (E-SEM), nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR), and CO2 flooding experiments, to investigate the pore structure complexity of tight sandstone reservoirs of the Yanchang Formation in the Ordos Basin, China. Furthermore, we investigated the variations in microheterogeneous wettability across diverse pore-throat structures and elucidated the impact of heterogeneous wettability on asphaltene precipitation during CO2 flooding. The findings indicate that the type and configuration of pore throats are crucial factors influencing microheterogeneous wettability. The intergranular pores are dominated by mixed wetting, and most of the dissolution pores exhibit oil wetting. The surface of Illite shows drop-like water under E-SEM, which is mainly oil wetting, whereas the surface of chlorite shows film-like water, which is water wetting. The configuration of chlorite intercrystalline pores and intergranular pores shows water wetting, whereas the configuration of Illite intergranular pores and dissolution pores shows oil wetting. During the CO2 flooding process, asphaltene tends to be adsorbed in the intercrystalline Illite with dissolution pores, reducing the dissolution pore volume and blocking small pores, and the displacement efficiency becomes low. In addition, asphaltene precipitation also occurs in the pore configuration of chlorite intercrystalline and intergranular pores, causing a wetting reversal on hydrophilic mineral surfaces. This reversal increases the pore throat structure complexity but has less of an impact on the flooding efficiency. A high Illite content is more likely to lead to asphaltene precipitation, significantly influencing small pore structure and the oil displacement efficiency.

Introduction

The large-scale development of tight oil, a significant alternative resource, has been underway in China alongside the exploitation of unconventional oil resources. CO2 flooding has emerged as a reliable strategy for enhancing oil recovery from tight reservoirs.1,2 The CO2 injected into reservoirs has many effects, including reducing interfacial tension, reducing crude oil viscosity, extracting light hydrocarbons, etc., which can greatly increase recovery.3 During CO2 flooding, interactions among CO2, fluid, and rock in a reservoir can produce asphaltene and inorganic precipitation, impacting flooding efficiency.4−9 Asphaltene may be adsorbed on the surface of the oil-wetting pore. The pore throat structure of tight sandstone reservoirs is intricate, featuring mixed wettability.10,11 This complexity leads to heterogeneous asphaltene precipitation across various pore throat structures.12−15 Understanding the intricate pore structure and heterogeneous wettability in tight reservoirs is crucial. Hence, a deep analysis of the interactions among these factors is crucial for developing oil in tight reservoirs.

Currently, CO2 displacement experiments in tight sandstone reservoirs include laboratory and numerical simulations. Laboratory simulations include flooding experiments, which compare variations in core physical properties and asphaltene content pre- and post-CO2 displacement.16−18 Numerical simulation experiments provide results of CO2–fluid–rock interactions from a microscopic perspective.19−22 Previous studies on CO2 flooding in tight sandstone reservoirs have focused mainly on macroscopic reservoir evaluations.23,24 Nevertheless, they neglected a thorough examination of asphaltene precipitation and adsorption mechanisms at the microscopic pore scale and their influence on flooding efficiency.25,26 Environmental scanning electron microscopy (E-SEM) facilitates direct observation of reservoir wettability and determines the distribution of asphaltenes within microscopic pore throats using energy spectrum analysis. This technology enables the direct observation of the wettability of rocks at the microscopic level.27 Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) quantitatively assesses changes in pore structure and wettability induced by asphaltene precipitation.28 The methodologies combined enable the exploration of the relationships among asphaltene precipitation, adsorption, and wettability within pore throat structures at the microscale.

Regarding asphaltene precipitation, scholars believe that in the process of CO2 oil displacement, the precipitation produced first deposits in large pores and then in small pores, which can cause blockage. Large and homogeneous pore throats have higher CO2 oil displacement efficiency, which is closely related to the contact volume between CO2 and oil.29,30 On a microscopic level, Ding et al.31 identified through numerical simulations that surface roughness in pore throats reduces the CO2 displacement efficiency. Lin et al.32 studied the interaction between CO2, formation water, and rocks, emphasizing that injecting CO2 pressure increases its acidity, thereby exacerbating the corrosive effect of carbonic acid on minerals. However, this corrosion will decrease with increasing temperature. In addition to the interaction between fluids and rocks, the issue of asphaltene adsorption is more worthy of our attention. Taheri-Shakib et al.33 pointed out that asphaltene precipitation plays an important role in affecting CO2 oil displacement efficiency, and its adsorption on the surface of carbonate rocks is related to the hydrogen bonds between molecules. Similarly, Zhang et al.34 also found that half of the asphaltene precipitation occurred in low-permeability areas, and classified asphaltene sedimentation into low velocity, corner, and trap sedimentation modes. Mohammed et al.35 conducted indoor simulations and modeling to determine the adsorption factors of asphaltene, highlighting the influence of minerals on the nonuniform deposition of asphaltene.

Although previous studies have investigated reservoir damage caused by CO2 displacement in tight reservoirs, the mechanisms of asphaltene precipitation and adsorption onto pore walls remain unclear. In particular, research into the relationships among pore structure heterogeneity, wettability, and asphaltene precipitation distribution is lacking. Previous research has revealed that the most important factor is not the crude oil properties but the microheterogeneous wettability. Clay content exerts a significant influence on the intricate pore throat structure. Together, these factors lead to the heterogeneous wettability of the reservoir, and the adsorption of asphaltene can also cause changes in wettability. With an increase in mining, the reservoir environment becomes increasingly complex. Therefore, further exploration is needed in this area.

This study focuses on representative tight sandstone samples obtained from the eighth section of the Yanchang Formation in the Ordos Basin. The mineral composition and pore structure of the reservoir were quantitatively analyzed via techniques such as X-ray diffraction (XRD), casting thin section (CTS), high-pressure mercury intrusion (HPMI), and field emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM). The heterogeneous wettability and pore structure pre- and post-CO2 flooding were clarified by combining nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) and environmental scanning electron microscopy (E-SEM). Furthermore, the relationships among the asphaltene precipitation content, distribution, and heterogeneous wettability alterations in different pore throat structures were studied. This comprehensive research revealed the influence of the heterogeneous wettability of tight sandstone reservoirs on asphaltene precipitation and adsorption, thereby offering opinions for optimizing CO2 injection strategies for enhanced recovery.

Materials and Methodology

Samples Background

Twelve samples from two regions in the southern part of the Shaanbei slope zone were used (Figure 1a). The Triassic Yanchang Formation is a part of the lacustrine delta sedimentary system, with ten sections36 (Figure 1b). All tight sandstone samples were taken from the Chang 8 section, including eight from Ganquan and four from Baibao. The depths of the Ganquan area samples ranged from 1533.30 to 1694.00 m, and those of the Baibao area samples ranged from 2046.34 to 2060.77 m.

Methodology

All of the samples were tight cylindrical sandstone. XDR, FE–SEM, CTS, and E-SEM were performed on both ends of the sample, and HPMI and CO2 flooding were performed on the remaining parts.

The porosities of the samples were measured with a KX-23A instrument. The permeability was measured via a DX-07G gas permeability tester.

XRD analysis was implemented via D8 equipment (temperature: 24 °C, relative humidity: 35%). We determined mineral composition based on the experimental steps of Ufer et al.37

The CTS and FE-SEM analyses were completed via Leica and a 400 FEG scanning electron microscope. A 0.02 mm sample slice was used for the CTS. A thumb-sized block sandstone sample was used for SEM. Both techniques were employed to examine the pore structure and morphology effectively.

HPMI was conducted following with a pressure range of 0.03–241.00 MPa. HPMI was a method of the intrusion of mercury into washed oil cores to study the distribution of pore throats in the core.

A MesoMR12-060H-I NMR instrument was used in this study. This device has a closed mouth-shaped magnet and a maximum rock sample test size of / 60 mm × H 100 mm cylinder. A quantitative study was conducted on the pore structure of 12 typical samples pre- and post-CO2 flooding.

The PVT analyzer manufactured by the RUSKA Company was employed to determine the minimum mixing pressure for the CO2 crude oil system, identified as 16 MPa, with a reactor volume of 380 mL.

Physical simulation experiments were carried out, combined with the implementation standard SY/T6490-2007 with a temperature of 25 °C and a humidity of 38% (Figure 4). The experimental steps are as follows,

Discussion

Distribution Characteristics of Pore Throats

The intricate pore structure of tight sandstone reservoirs significantly influences asphaltene precipitation.30,41−43 The pore geometry, distribution of pore size, and connectivity within the samples all indicate the complexity inherent in their pore throat structure.

Based on results from XRD, CTS, FE-SEM, NMR, and HPMI analyses, pore throat structures can be categorized into three types. Samples Q4, Q5, and Q8 are residual intergranular pore-dominated types. Samples Q3, Q6, and Q7 are dissolution pore-dominated types, and Sample Q1 is both intergranular and dissolution pore-dominated types.

The intergranular pore-dominated types are primarily configured by intergranular and chlorite intercrystalline pores. This type has many intergranular pores and a few dissolution pores (Figure 10A-3). Figure 10 shows that a large amount of chlorite developed around the intergranular pores (Figure 10A-4). The average content of chlorite is 58.70% (Table 1). The saturated water T2 NMR spectrum reveals that the signal amplitudes of both large and small pores were high and that chlorite played a positive role. The movable fluid porosity measures 1.59% (Table 3), with a high proportion attributed to small pores (Figure 10A-1). The mercury intrusion curve reveals a median radius of 0.049 μm for these samples. Low entry pressure and a maximum mercury saturation of 81.14% (Table 2), indicate strong pore connectivity.

The dissolution pore-dominated types are primarily configured by Illite intercrystalline and dissolution pores. This pore type exhibits the development of dissolution pores (Figure 10B-3), with abundant Illite intercrystalline pores. Illite was also distributed around the feldspar dissolution pores (Figure 10B-4), with an average content of 49.70% (Table 1). In this type of saturated water T2 spectrum, the peak of the macropores was not obvious, with a prevalence of small pores over large ones. Signal amplitudes are significantly lower compared to the residual intergranular pore-dominated samples (Figure 10B-1), and the porosity of the movable fluid was 3.64% (Table 3). The median radius of the mercury intrusion curve measures 0.062 μm with a maximum mercury saturation of 79.06% (Table 2). The mercury intrusion pressure of Q7 was lower than that of Q3 and Q6 (Figure 10B-2), indicating that the distribution of macropores in sample Q7 was greater than that in samples Q3 and Q6, which was consistent with the NMR results.

Both the intergranular and dissolution pore-dominated types exhibit a configuration of dissolution pores, intergranular pores, and Illite intercrystalline pores. In contrast to the dissolution pore-dominated samples, this type shows the development of intergranular pores with fewer intragranular pores (Figure 10C-3). Illite was distributed around dissolution pores (Figure 10C-4), averaging 49.6% content (Table 1). The saturated water T2 spectrum revealed more macropores than did the dissolution-dominated samples (Figure 10C-1). The distribution frequencies of macropores and small pores are similar with a movable fluid porosity of 1.16% (Table 3). In the mercury intrusion curve, this type exhibits a low initial mercury intrusion pressure, followed by a sudden increase in intrusion pressure. The mercury intrusion saturation was high (Figure 10C-2). The median radius of the mercury intrusion curve measures 0.09 μm, with a maximum mercury saturation of 79.06% (Table 2). This indicated that the pore connectivity of the sample was strong and that the small pores were relatively homogeneous.

Hence, the sequence of the pore throat matching relationships, ranked from superior to inferior based on physical properties and connectivity, is as follows across the three sample types: residual intergranular pore-dominated types, both intergranular pore-dominated and dissolution pore-dominated types, and dissolution pore-dominated types.

Analysis of Heterogeneous Wettability and Influencing Factors for Different Pore Throat Structures

The NMR T2 spectrum of saturated water can define the pore distribution in the reservoir. In this paper, the low point between the two peaks of the saturated water T2 spectrum is used as the boundary to distinguish between large and small pores.44,45 The NMR curves of the 12 samples show changes in the saturated water and oil signal positions within the pores due to varying mineral compositions and wettability heterogeneity. The manganese water adhered to the water-wetting core pore walls when the saturated MnCl2-solution core was displaced with crude oil, leading to differences in the T2 spectra. The relaxation time of the water-wetting pores detected by NMR decreases, resulting in a leftward shift of the boundary of the saturated oil NMR T2 spectrum. This reflects heterogeneous wettability in the reservoir, with small pores being more water-wetted and large pores being more oil-wetted (Figure 11d). E-SEM revealed membrane-like and droplet-like water on mineral surfaces (Figure 9), indicating mixed wetting conditions.46−49

During the geological history of sedimentation in tight sandstone reservoirs, tight reservoirs have larger pores and smaller throats; more crude oil was permeated into the larger pores. In comparison, less crude oil was permeated into the smaller pores. Under the long-term aging of crude oil, the minerals within larger pores gradually become oil-wetting, whereas those in the smaller pores are mostly water-wetting.11 Therefore, tight sandstone reservoirs exhibit heterogeneous wetting.

The T2 spectra of the residual intergranular pore-dominated samples indicate no change in the inflection points between the peaks. The T2 spectrum under oil flooding to a bound water state shows a decrease in the signal amplitude between large and small pores. This suggests partial water wetting in the large and small pores of the sample (Figure 11a).

In dissolution pore-dominated samples, the NMR T2 curve of saturated oil shows that the amplitude of macropores increases, whereas that of small pores decreases. This suggests strong oil wetting in large pore throats and strong water wetting in small pores. In sample Q3, some MnCl2 solution adhered to the small pores due to water wetting, causing the two peaks of the NMR T2 curve in the saturated oil state to shift to the left, resulting in a shorter relaxation time for the small pores (Figure 11b).

In both the intergranular pore-dominated samples and the dissolution pore-dominated samples, the water wetting in the small pores is more obvious, and the wettability change is the same as that in the dissolution pore-dominated samples. This is also due to the adhesion of some MnCl2 solution to the water-wetting pore wall of the small pores, which causes the two peaks of the T2 spectrum in the saturated oil state to shift to the left, resulting in a shorter relaxation time for the small pores (Figure 11c). Unlike the former, this type of sample developed more macropores.

According to Table 1, residual intergranular pore-dominated samples exhibit strong water wetting in both large and small pores. The average chlorite content in this type of pore is 58.73%, and the average Illite content is 8.90%. Moreover, the dissolution pore-dominated samples exhibit large pore oil wetting, and the small pores are water wetting. Among them, dissolution pore-dominated samples have an average Illite content of 48.70% and an average chlorite content of 20.23%. Both the intergranular and dissolution pore-dominated samples also exhibit characteristics of both large pore oil wetting and small pore water wetting. These types of samples have an Illite average content of 49.6%. The average content of chlorite is 25.50%.

The combined E-SEM results (Figure 9) of various pore throat types indicate that oil wetting in the study area samples is linked to Illite, whereas water wetting is associated with the chlorite content.

Relationships Between Heterogeneous Wettability and Precipitation in Different Pore Throats

Clay minerals are closely related to reservoir properties.50−52 The chlorite content is positively correlated with the porosity and permeability (Figure 12a,b), indicating that chlorite has a positive effect on reservoir properties. On the other hand, the correlation between Illite and porosity is not significant (Figure 12c). Still, it is negatively correlated with permeability (Figure 12d), indicating that pore throat connectivity and reservoir properties are deeply influenced by illite. Among the three types, the precipitation amounts generated by CO2 flooding are arranged from high to low as dissolution pore-dominated types, both intergranular pore and dissolution pore-dominated types, and residual intergranular pore-dominated types, with Illite contents of 49.70, 49.60, and 13.80% and chlorite contents of 20.33, 25.50, and 58.73%, respectively (Table 1). Illite negatively affects reservoir properties and has a significant influence on the CO2 oil displacement efficiency (Figure 13). Chlorite has a positive effect on reservoir properties and has a relatively small impact on the CO2 oil displacement efficiency (Figure 13).

The results of this study revealed that samples with residual intergranular pores were dominated by higher chlorite contents but lower precipitation contents after CO2 flooding. In contrast, the dissolution pore-dominated samples and both the intergranular pore- and dissolution pore-dominated samples have high illite contents and precipitation contents (Figure 13). The observation of asphaltene precipitation around illite–pore interfaces via E-SEM indicates that Illite plays a more significant role in precipitation than chlorite does. Owing to the water wetting of chlorite and the oil wetting of Illite, the precipitation generated during CO2 flooding will be adsorbed on the pore wall due to the oil wetting of Illite, whereas chlorite is water wetting, and precipitation does not easily occur. Most of them will be displaced out with crude oil, and a small portion will remain in the pores, causing changes in micro wettability. Therefore, owing to the heterogeneous wettability of different pore throat structures, precipitation is more likely to adsorb on the surface of oil-wetting Illite, affecting the recovery rate.

Relationships between Adsorbed Precipitation and CO2 Flooding Efficiency in Different Pore Throat Structures

Variations in wettability due to different pore throat structures led to distinct distributions of asphaltene precipitation. The content and distribution of asphaltene precipitation also affect the efficacy of CO2 flooding. The changes in the physical properties, asphaltene precipitation content, and oil displacement efficiency after CO2 flooding are shown in Table 4. Among the three types, the content of asphaltene precipitation decreases from high to low and is categorized into dissolution pore-dominated types, both intergranular pore-dominated and dissolution pore-dominated types, and residual intergranular pore-dominated types. The flooding efficiencies are 21.25, 38.53, and 54.52%, respectively (Figure 13). Notably, the dissolution pore-dominated samples and both the intergranular and dissolution pore-dominated samples exhibit higher levels of asphaltene precipitation following CO2 flooding, primarily due to the presence of Illite. However, the impact of asphaltene precipitation on dissolution pore-dominated samples surpasses that on intergranular pore- and dissolution pore-dominated samples. Therefore, the oil wetting of Illite in different pore throat structures affects the content and distribution of asphaltene precipitation. An increase in asphaltene precipitation content can block pores, thereby affecting oil displacement efficiency.

The following conclusions can be drawn: During CO2 mixed-phase flooding, dominant flow channels develop in macropores, facilitating the easier displacement of crude oil from these regions. In contrast, the displacement of crude oil from small pores proves more challenging. Therefore, asphaltene precipitates preferentially in the macropores (Figure 14a).

Second, the efficacy of CO2 flooding correlates closely with the pore complexity. The dissolution pore-dominated samples and both the intergranular and dissolution pore-dominated samples developed a large amount of Illite (Figure 13). The precipitation induced by CO2 flooding is influenced by the oil wetting of Illite within the reservoir. However, in terms of flooding efficiency, dissolution pore-dominated samples are significantly more affected than both intergranular pores and dissolution pores. This disparity arises from the development of many macropores of the latter type (Figure 14c). So, more precipitates adhere to the surfaces of these macropores during the CO2 flooding process, which has a smaller influence on flooding efficiency.

On the other hand, dissolution pore-dominated samples are prone to blocks due to the development of small pores, thereby affecting the oil recovery efficiency (Figure 14b). Analysis of the NMR T2 curve of sample Q7 flooding CO2 revealed that illite’s oil wetting leads to continuous asphaltene precipitation adsorption on pore walls, resulting in small pore blockage (Figure 14b). The transformation of macropores into small pores, induced by adsorbed precipitates, results in a reduction in flooding efficiency (Figure 14b). Finally, asphaltene also precipitates in intergranular pore-dominated samples, causing changes in wettability, increasing the intricacy of the pore throat structure, and having a relatively small effect on the oil recovery efficiency (Figure 14a).

Conclusions

The dissolution pore-dominated samples have the most complex pore throat structure. Intergranular pore-dominated pore types consist of mainly intergranular pores and chlorite intercrystalline pores. The dissolution pore-dominated types primarily feature Illite intercrystalline pores and dissolution pores. Both intergranular and dissolution pore-dominated types are characterized by a combination of dissolution pores, intergranular pores, and illite intercrystalline pores.

The different pore throat structures show heterogeneous wetting via NMR, with larger pores exhibiting more oil wetting and smaller pores exhibiting more water wetting. According to the results of E-SEM, the heterogeneous wettability is influenced by clay minerals, whereas chlorite is water-wetting and illite is oil-wetting.

The NMR results pre- and post-CO2 flooding indicate that during the CO2 mixed-phase flooding process, CO2 preferentially forms a flow channel in the macropores, and the crude oil of the large pores is preferentially displaced, whereas the crude oil in the small pores does not move easily. Therefore, asphaltene precipitates preferentially deposit in the macropores and then deposit in small pores. The content of asphaltene precipitates correlates with the content of Illite present, where higher Illite content leads to increased precipitation.

The results of E-SEM and NMR indicate that the configuration of chlorite intercrystalline and intergranular pores involves water wetting. In contrast, the Illite intergranular and dissolution pore configurations exhibit oil wetting. During the CO2 flooding process, asphaltene tends to adsorb within the Illite intercrystalline pores configuration, which reduces the dissolution pore volume and blocks small pores, thereby reducing the displacement efficiency.

In all experiments, asphaltene precipitation induced by CO2 flooding primarily affects the structure of the small pores. In dissolution pore-dominated samples, asphaltene precipitation tends to block small pores, hindering oil recovery. Both the intergranular pore and dissolution pore-dominated samples exhibit more developed macropores, where asphaltene adsorption gradually reduces the pore size with less impact flooding efficiency. In the intergranular pore-dominated samples, asphaltene precipitates are more easily carried away by crude oil, and some asphaltene precipitates are also retained in pore throats, causing a reversal of wettability. This phenomenon increases pore throat complexity but minimally affects flooding efficiency. Therefore, microheterogeneous wettability influences the adsorption of asphaltene onto pore walls, potentially causing wettability reversal as asphaltene adsorbs.
