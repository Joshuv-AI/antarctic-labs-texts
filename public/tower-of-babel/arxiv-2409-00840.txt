Longitudinal magnetoresistance in graphene with random Rashba spin-orbit interaction
S. Kudła 1 , V. K. Dugaev 1 , J. Barnaś 2,3 , A. Dyrdał 2

Abstract
We consider longitudinal electronic transport in a graphene monolayer, with an external in-plane magnetic field parallel to the electric field, and in the presence of extrinsic spatially fluctuating spin-orbit Rashba interaction. Our main interest is focused on the longitudinal magnetoresistance. We show, that for the spin-orbit correlation length ξ \xi between 10 − 6 10^{-6} and 10 − 5 10^{-5} cm, scattering processes on the Rashba spin-orbit fluctuations lead to a negative magnetoresistance. For smaller correlation lengths, ξ < 10 − 6 \xi<10^{-6} cm, we find a small and positive magnetoresistance. The description is based on an effective model of graphene Hamiltonian, that is valid for low energy states around the Dirac points. In turn, to derive the electron scattering rate and the longitudinal conductivity and magnetoresistivity, we employ the linear response theory and Green’s function formalism.

II
Model

Bearing in mind the main objective of this paper, as described above in the Introduction, we
consider graphene in an external magnetic field applied in the graphene plane and in the presence of spatially fluctuating extrinsic Rashba spin-orbit interaction.
The corresponding Hamiltonian describing low-energy electron states in the vicinity of the Dirac point
K
has the following form:

H=H_{0}+H_{\mathbf{B}}+H_{R}.

(1)

The term
H_{0}
,
H_{0}=v\,\mbox{\boldmath$\tau$}\cdot{\mathbf{k}}
, describes pristine graphene with
{\mathbf{k}}=(k_{x},k_{y})
being 2D wavevector, while
\tau
stands for the Pauli matrices acting in the sublattice space. The second term,
H_{\mathbf{B}}=\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$\Delta$}
, is the Zeeman term describing coupling with the external in-plane magnetic field. Here,
\sigma
are the Pauli matrices in the spin space, and we assume
\mbox{\boldmath$\Delta$}=(\Delta,0,0)
,
where
\Delta=g\mu_{B}B/2
and the external magnetic field is along the axis
x
. Note, that by assuming the in-plane magnetic field, we avoid diamagnetic orbital effects. Therefore, in Eq. (
1
) we take into account only the Zeeman coupling.

The last term,
H_{R}
, in Eq. (
1
) stands for the Rashba spin-orbit Hamiltonian,

H_{R}=\lambda({\bf r})\,(\sigma_{y}\tau_{x}-\sigma_{x}\tau_{y}),

(2)

where
\lambda({\bf r})
is the randomly fluctuating in space Rashba parameter,
with vanishing mean value,
\left\langle\lambda({\bf r})\right\rangle=0
, and the correlation function given by the formula
[
47
]
,

C_{\lambda\lambda}\left(\bf{r-r}^{\prime}\right)\equiv\langle\lambda({\bf r})\,\lambda({\bf r^{\prime}})\rangle=\left\langle\lambda^{2}\right\rangle F({\bf r-r^{\prime}}),

(3)

where
F({\bf r-r^{\prime}})
is a certain correlation function. In the following, the Fourier transform of
F({\bf r})
will be assumed in the form
F(\mathbf{q})=\xi^{2}\,e^{-\xi^{2}q^{2}}
, where
\xi
is a characteristic correlation length.

As the external magnetic field is oriented along the axis
x
(which is in the graphene plane), it is convenient to perform a unitary transformation in the spin space,
H\to U^{\dagger}HU
, with the transformation matrix defined as

\displaystyle U=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&i\\
1&-i\end{array}\right),

which transforms the spin operators in the following way:
U^{\dagger}\sigma_{x}U=\sigma_{z}
and
U^{\dagger}\sigma_{y}U=-\sigma_{x}
.
Accordingly, we obtain the transformed Hamiltonian in the following form:

\displaystyle H_{0}+H_{\mathbf{B}}={\rm{diag}}(H_{0\uparrow},H_{0\downarrow}),

(7)

where

\displaystyle H_{0\sigma}=v\,\mbox{\boldmath$\tau$}\cdot{\bf k}+\Delta_{\sigma},

(8)

with
\Delta_{\uparrow,\downarrow}=\pm\Delta
, while the Rashba term upon transformation acquires the form,

\displaystyle H_{R}={\rm{diag}}\left(H^{\mathrm{(1)}}_{R\uparrow},H^{\mathrm{(1)}}_{R\downarrow}\right)+H^{\rm{(2)}}_{R},

(9)

where

\displaystyle H^{\mathrm{(1)}}_{R\uparrow,\downarrow}=\mp\lambda({\bf r})\,\tau_{y},

(10)

and

\displaystyle H^{\mathrm{(2)}}_{R}=-\lambda({\bf r})\,\sigma_{x}\tau_{x}.

(11)

Note that the states labeled here as
\uparrow
(
\downarrow
) are in fact the states with spin oriented along (opposite to) the external magnetic field.

As follows from Eqs. (
9
), (
10
) and (
11
), after the unitary transformation one can clearly distinguish two components of the electron coupling with the fluctuating Rashba field.
One of them
corresponds to spin-conserving inter-sublattice scattering, and the other one describes inter-sublattice spin-flip scattering processes. In the following, the effects of random Rashba field are treated perturbatively as a source of electron scattering. To emphasize the role of scattering on Rashba spin-orbit fluctuations, we exclude other scattering processes, which however can be effectively included by a constant contribution to the imaginary part of the self-energy, if necessary.

Figure 1:
(a) The electron energy spectrum of the transformed Hamiltonian. The Dirac cones are spin split and their two components are shifted up and down in energy scale.
(b) Schematic of possible scattering processes from the spin-up Fermi circle in weak (left) and strong (right) magnetic fields. The scatterings
{\bf k}\to{\bf k^{\prime}}
(spin-conserved scattering) or
{\bf k}\to{\bf k^{\prime\prime}}
(spin-flip scattering) are possible if
\Delta k<1/\xi
, where
\Delta k=|{\bf k^{\prime}}-{\bf k}|
or
\Delta k=|{\bf k^{\prime\prime}}-{\bf k}|
, respectively.

The Green’s function of the Hamiltonian
H_{0\sigma}
(
\sigma=\uparrow,\downarrow
) has the following form:

G_{0\sigma}(\varepsilon,{\bf k})=\frac{\varepsilon-\Delta_{\sigma}+\mu+v\,\mbox{\boldmath$\tau$}\cdot{\bf k}}{\prod\limits_{n=1,2}[\varepsilon-\varepsilon_{n\sigma}({\bf k})+\mu+i\delta\,{\rm sgn}\,\varepsilon]},

(12)

where
\varepsilon_{1\sigma}({\bf k})=\varepsilon_{1\sigma}(k)=\Delta_{\sigma}+vk
and

\varepsilon_{2\sigma}({\bf k})=\varepsilon_{2\sigma}(k)=\Delta_{\sigma}-vk
are the eigen states of Hamiltonian (
7
) with
k=|{\bf k}|
, while
\mu
is the chemical potential.
Note, that there is a
2\times 2
unit matrix in front of the first three terms in the nominator, which according to our notation (also in the following) is not indicated explicitly.
The energy spectrum is shown schematically in Fig.
1
.
Linear dependence of the electron energy
\varepsilon
on
k
in graphene leads to the linear in
\varepsilon
dependence of the density of states,
\rho(\varepsilon)\sim\varepsilon
. Accordingly, the magnetic-field-induced shift of spin subbands (like presented in Fig. 1) does not affect the total density of states
\rho_{\rm tot}=\rho_{\uparrow}+\rho_{\downarrow}
.
The external magnetic field applied in the graphene plane shifts the chemical potential in order to conserve the particle number. When the chemical potential in the absence of magnetic field, denoted as
\mu_{0}
, obeys the condition
\mu_{0}>\Delta
, then the
chemical potential varies with the field as
\mu=\sqrt{\mu_{0}^{2}-\Delta^{2}}
. In turn, when
\mu_{0}<\Delta
, then the chemical potential is independent of the field,
\mu=\mu_{0}
.

III
Relaxation processes due to random Rashba field

To find the electron relaxation time, we need to calculate first the appropriate self-energy. Then, the relaxation times will be determined from the imaginary part of the self-energy.

III.1
Self-energy

The self energy due to scattering of electrons from fluctuating spin-orbit interaction is given by the formula

\Sigma_{\mathbf{k}}=\int\frac{d^{2}{\bf k}^{\prime}}{(2\pi)^{2}}H_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}^{R}G_{0}(\varepsilon,\mathbf{k}^{\prime})H_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\mathbf{k}}^{R},

(13)

where
H_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}^{R}
is the Bloch representation of the Hamiltonian (
9
). Note, the self energy is here a
4\times 4
matrix. Due to the form of Eq. (
9
), one can factorize the self-energy as
\Sigma={\rm{diag}}\left(\Sigma_{\uparrow},\Sigma_{\downarrow}\right)
, and present
\Sigma_{\sigma}
as a sum of the contributions from spin-conserving and spin-flip scattering processes (see also Fig.
2
) in the following form:

\displaystyle\Sigma_{\sigma}(\varepsilon,{\bf k})=\langle\lambda^{2}\rangle\int\frac{d^{2}{\bf k}^{\prime}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,F(|{\bf k-k^{\prime}}|)\tau_{y}G_{0\sigma}(\varepsilon,{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}})\tau_{y}

\displaystyle+\langle\lambda^{2}\rangle\int\frac{d^{2}{\bf k}^{\prime}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,F(|{\bf k-k^{\prime}}|)\,\tau_{x}G_{0\bar{\sigma}}(\varepsilon,\mathbf{k}^{\prime})\tau_{x},\,\,

(14)

where we introduced the notation:
\bar{\sigma}=\downarrow
when
\sigma=\uparrow
, and
\bar{\sigma}=\uparrow
when
\sigma=\downarrow
.

Figure 2:
Self energy due to the scattering from fluctuating Rashba coupling. The left (right) part corresponds to spin-conserving (spin-flip) scattering processes.

The self energy
\Sigma_{\sigma}(\varepsilon,{\bf k})
is a
2\times 2
matrix in the sublattice space, and its imaginary part determines the electron relaxation rates. Thus, the relaxation rate of electrons with spin
\sigma
is described by the
2\times 2
matrix
\gamma_{\sigma}(\varepsilon,{\bf k_{\sigma}})
, which is the imaginary part of
\Sigma_{\sigma}(\varepsilon,{\bf k}_{\sigma})
,
with
|{\bf k}_{\sigma}|=k_{\sigma}
being the solution of the equation
\varepsilon_{n\sigma}(k)-\mu=\varepsilon
for the two eigenstates
n=1,2

In the following we neglect the real part of self energy as it leads to a small disorder-induced correction to the electron dispersion relations.

Let us take
\mu>|\Delta_{\sigma}|
and calculate
\gamma_{\sigma}(0,{\bf k_{\sigma}})
, corresponding to electrons at the Fermi level (see Fig.
1
). In this case,
we find

\displaystyle\gamma_{\sigma}(0,{\bf k_{\sigma}})=\frac{\xi^{2}\langle\lambda^{2}\rangle}{4v}\Big\{e^{-2\xi^{2}k_{\sigma}^{2}}k_{\sigma}\Big[I_{0}(2\xi^{2}k_{\sigma}^{2})

\displaystyle+\frac{\mbox{\boldmath$\tau$}_{1}\cdot{\bf k}_{\sigma}}{k_{\sigma}}\,I_{1}(2\xi^{2}k_{\sigma}^{2})\Big]+e^{-\xi^{2}(k_{\sigma}^{2}+k_{\bar{\sigma}}^{2})}\,k_{\bar{\sigma}}\Big[I_{0}(2\xi^{2}k_{\sigma}k_{\bar{\sigma}})

\displaystyle-\frac{\mbox{\boldmath$\tau$}_{1}\cdot{\bf k}_{\sigma}}{k_{\sigma}}\,I_{1}(2\xi^{2}k_{\sigma}k_{\bar{\sigma}})\Big]\Big\},\hskip 14.22636pt

(15)

with
k_{\sigma}=\mu_{\sigma}/v
,
\mu_{\sigma}=\mu-\Delta_{\sigma}
, and
\mbox{\boldmath$\tau$}_{1}=(-\tau_{x},\tau_{y})
.
Apart from this,

I_{0}(z)
and
I_{1}(z)
in Eq. (
15
) are the modified Bessel functions,

I_{0}(z)=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}e^{z\cos\theta}d\theta,\;

I_{1}(z)=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}e^{z\cos\theta}\cos\theta\,d\theta.

III.2
Momentum relaxation time

The imaginary part of self energy is a matrix.
To find the relaxation time of electron in a certain state
|n\sigma{\bf k}\rangle
, we should solve the eigenvalue equation, which takes into account the self energy part,

\displaystyle\det\big[H_{0\sigma}({\bf k})-\mu+i\gamma_{\sigma}(\varepsilon,{\bf k}_{\sigma})-\varepsilon\big]=0.

(16)

From now the dependence of
\gamma_{\sigma}
on
{\bf k}
is not indicated explicitly and this dependence will be restored when necessary.
Since we assume that the effect of
\gamma_{\sigma}
is relatively weak, we can consider it as a small correction to the solutions
\varepsilon=\varepsilon_{1\sigma}(k)-\mu
and
\varepsilon=\varepsilon_{2\sigma}(k)-\mu
(i.e., the solutions of Eq. (
16
) for
\gamma_{\sigma}=0
). Correspondingly, from Eq. (
16
) we obtain the following equation:

\displaystyle\det\left[\Delta_{\sigma}+v\mbox{\boldmath$\tau$}\cdot{\bf k}-\mu+i\gamma_{\sigma}\big(\varepsilon_{1,2\sigma}-\mu)-\varepsilon\right]=0,

(17)

where we choose
\gamma_{\sigma}(\varepsilon)=\gamma_{\sigma}\big(\varepsilon_{1\sigma}-\mu)
for the solution
\varepsilon\simeq\varepsilon_{1\sigma}(k)-\mu
or
\gamma_{\sigma}(\varepsilon)=\gamma_{\sigma}\big(\varepsilon_{2\sigma}-\mu)
for the second solution
\varepsilon\simeq\varepsilon_{2\sigma}(k)-\mu
.

In the first case (i.e., for
n=1
), we take
\varepsilon=+0
(which corresponds to electron states at the Fermi level, see Fig.
1
) and use
\gamma_{\sigma}(0)
from Eq. (13). Then we get

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{1\sigma}(k)=\Delta_{\sigma}+vk_{\sigma}+iw^{\prime}_{\sigma}-iw^{\prime\prime}_{\sigma}\,\frac{k_{x\sigma}^{2}-k_{y\sigma}^{2}}{k_{\sigma}}\,,

(18)

where we introduced the following notation:

\displaystyle w^{\prime}_{\sigma}=\frac{\xi^{2}\langle\lambda^{2}\rangle}{4v}\Big\{e^{-2\xi^{2}k_{\sigma}^{2}}k_{\sigma}I_{0}(2\xi^{2}k_{\sigma}^{2})

\displaystyle\hskip 71.13188pt+e^{-\xi^{2}(k_{\sigma}^{2}+k_{\bar{\sigma}}^{2})}\,k_{\bar{\sigma}}I_{0}(2\xi^{2}k_{\sigma}k_{\bar{\sigma}})\Big\},

(19)

\displaystyle w^{\prime\prime}_{\sigma}=\frac{\xi^{2}\langle\lambda^{2}\rangle}{4vk_{\sigma}}\Big\{e^{-2\xi^{2}k_{\sigma}^{2}}\,k_{\sigma}\,I_{1}(2\xi^{2}k_{\sigma}^{2})

\displaystyle\hskip 71.13188pt-e^{-\xi^{2}(k_{\sigma}^{2}+k_{\bar{\sigma}}^{2})}\,k_{\bar{\sigma}}\,I_{1}(2\xi^{2}k_{\sigma}k_{\bar{\sigma}})\Big\}.

(20)

From Eq. (
18
) we find the following expression for the anisotropic relaxation time
\tilde{\tau}
,

\displaystyle\frac{\hbar}{2\tilde{\tau}_{1\sigma}({\bf k}_{\sigma})}\equiv\gamma_{1\sigma}(0,{\bf k}_{\sigma})=w^{\prime}_{\sigma}-w^{\prime\prime}_{\sigma}\,\frac{k_{x\sigma}^{2}-k_{y\sigma}^{2}}{k_{\sigma}}\,.

(21)

Similarly, one can find the relaxation time of a hole with energy
\varepsilon=-2\mu
. For this purpose we have to use Eq. (
14
) and calculate
\gamma_{\sigma}(-2\mu)
. As a result we find
\gamma_{\sigma}(-2\mu)=-w^{\prime}_{\sigma}+w^{\prime\prime}_{\sigma}\,(k^{2}_{x\sigma}-k^{2}_{y\sigma})/k_{\sigma}
.
Taking into account that

\hbar/2\tau_{2\bar{\sigma}}=-\gamma_{2\bar{\sigma}}(-2\mu)
we come to equality
\tilde{\tau}_{2\bar{\sigma}}({\bf k})=\tilde{\tau}_{1\sigma}({\bf k})
, as one might expect from the electron-hole symmetry.

Importantly, the anisotropy of relaxation time is related to the orientation of external magnetic field.
When
k_{\bar{\sigma}}\to k_{\sigma}
we get
w^{\prime\prime}_{\sigma}\to 0
, and the relaxation anisotropy disappears. Specifically, for
\mu\gg\Delta_{\sigma}

(i.e., when the magnetic field is relatively weak) one can neglect this anisotropy, taking
\gamma_{1,2\sigma}({\bf k}_{\sigma})=w^{\prime}_{\sigma}
, and the electron relaxation time related to the scattering from fluctuating Rashba SO interaction is
\tilde{\tau}_{\sigma}(k_{\sigma})\simeq\hbar/2w^{\prime}_{\sigma}
. In the following we analyze the
\tilde{\tau}_{1\sigma}({\bf k})
and suppress the index 1.

\uparrow

\downarrow

Consider first the numerical data on the relaxation time in the spin-

\tilde{\tau}_{\uparrow x}
, shown in Fig.

1/\xi
, see Fig.

\uparrow
-spin subband reduces to a point and there is no spin-flip scattering in the system. When the chemical potential is slightly larger than
\Delta

IV
Electronic transport and magnetoresistance

Figure 5:
Kubo diagrams for the conductivity and diagrams representing equations for the vertex functions.

The charge current density, as a response of the system to the electromagnetic field described by the vector potential
{\bf A}(t)={\bf A}_{\omega}e^{-i\omega t}
,
can be written (due to the gauge transformation
{\bf k}\to{\bf k}-e{\bf A}/\hbar c
) in the linear response formalism as

\displaystyle j_{x}(\omega)=-i\sum_{\sigma}{\rm Tr}\int\frac{d^{2}{\bf k}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int\frac{d\varepsilon}{2\pi}\,J_{\mathbf{k}\sigma x}(\varepsilon,\omega)

\displaystyle\times G_{\mathbf{k}\sigma}(\varepsilon+\hbar\omega)\,H^{\mathbf{A}}_{\sigma}\,G_{\mathbf{k}\sigma}(\varepsilon),

(22)

where

\displaystyle H^{\bf{A}}_{\sigma}=-\mathbf{j}_{\sigma}\cdot\mathbf{A}_{\omega}

(23)

describes coupling of the system to an external electromagnetic field,
{\bf A}_{\omega}={\bf E}_{\omega}/i\omega
, within the minimal coupling approach,
the charge current density,
\mathbf{j}_{\sigma}
, is defined as

j_{\sigma i}=ev_{\sigma i}=\frac{e}{\hbar}\,\frac{\partial H_{0\sigma}}{\partial k_{i}}=\frac{ev}{\hbar}\tau_{i},

(24)

whereas
J_{\mathbf{k}\sigma x}(\varepsilon,\omega)
is the renormalized velocity vertex function (see Fig.
5
(a)) defined as

J_{\mathbf{k}\sigma x}(\varepsilon,\omega)=\frac{ev}{\hbar}\Gamma_{\mathbf{k}\sigma x}(\varepsilon,\omega),

(25)

with the vertex function
\Gamma_{\mathbf{k}\sigma x}
given below.

We assume the electric field along the axis
x
, i.e., parallel to the magnetic field, and focus on the longitudinal conductivity.
Taking into account the relation
j_{x}(\omega)=\sigma_{xx}E_{\omega x}
, one finds

\displaystyle\sigma_{xx}(\omega)=\frac{e^{2}v^{2}}{\hbar^{2}\omega}\,{\rm Tr}\sum_{\sigma}\int\frac{d^{2}{\bf k}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\frac{d\varepsilon}{2\pi}\,\Gamma_{{\bf k}\sigma x}(\varepsilon,\omega)

\displaystyle\times G_{\bf k\sigma}(\varepsilon+\hbar\omega)\,\tau_{x}\,G_{\bf k\sigma}(\varepsilon).

(26)

The vertex function
\Gamma_{{\bf k}\sigma x}(\varepsilon,\omega)
is presented in Fig.
5
and takes the following form:

\displaystyle\Gamma_{{\bf k}\sigma x}(\varepsilon,\omega)=\tau_{x}+\langle\lambda^{2}\rangle\int\frac{d^{2}{\bf k}^{\prime}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,F(|{\bf k-k^{\prime}}|)\,\tau_{y}\,G_{{\bf k}^{\prime}\sigma}(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle\times\Gamma_{{\bf k}^{\prime}\sigma x}(\varepsilon,\omega)\,G_{{\bf k}^{\prime}\sigma}(\varepsilon+\hbar\omega)\,\tau_{y}

\displaystyle+\langle\lambda^{2}\rangle\int\frac{d^{2}{\bf k}^{\prime}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,F(|{\bf k-k^{\prime}}|)\,\tau_{x}G_{{\bf k}^{\prime}\bar{\sigma}}(\varepsilon)\,

\displaystyle\times\Gamma_{{\bf k}^{\prime}\bar{\sigma}x}(\varepsilon,\omega)\,G_{{\bf k}^{\prime}\bar{\sigma}}(\varepsilon+\hbar\omega)\,\tau_{x}\,.\hskip 14.22636pt

(27)

In the limit of
\omega\to 0
, the main contribution to the current is from the Fermi surface,
\varepsilon\to 0
. In this limit, the equation for the vertex function reads

\displaystyle\Gamma_{{\bf k}\sigma x}=\tau_{x}+\langle\lambda^{2}\rangle\int\frac{d^{2}{\bf k}^{\prime}}{(2\pi)^{2}}F(|{\bf k-k^{\prime}}|)\,\tau_{y}G^{A}_{{\bf k}^{\prime}\sigma}\Gamma_{{\bf k}^{\prime}\sigma x}G^{R}_{{\bf k}^{\prime}\sigma}\tau_{y}

\displaystyle+\langle\lambda^{2}\rangle\int\frac{d^{2}{\bf k}^{\prime}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,F(|{\bf k-k^{\prime}}|)\,\tau_{x}G^{A}_{{\bf k}^{\prime}\bar{\sigma}}\,\Gamma_{{\bf k}^{\prime}\bar{\sigma}x}\,G^{R}_{{\bf k}^{\prime}\bar{\sigma}}\,\tau_{x},\hskip 14.22636pt

(28)

where we used the notation,
\Gamma_{{\bf k}\sigma x}\equiv\Gamma_{{\bf k}\sigma x}(0,0)
and
G^{R,A}_{{\bf k}\sigma}\equiv G^{R,A}_{{\bf k}\sigma}(0)
. The derivation of the vertex function is presented in Appendix
A
.

As a result, in the limit of
\omega\to 0
we find the following expression for the static (dc) conductivity:

\displaystyle\sigma_{xx}=\frac{e^{2}v^{2}}{2\pi\hbar}\,{\rm Tr}\sum_{\sigma}\int\frac{d^{2}{\bf k}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,\Gamma_{{\bf k}\sigma x}\,G^{R}_{\bf k\sigma}\,\tau_{x}\,G^{A}_{\bf k\sigma}\,,

(29)

which includes the vertex function
\Gamma_{{\bf k}\sigma x}
.
To solve the self-consistent vertex equations we postulate the solution in the following form:

\displaystyle\Gamma_{{\bf k}\sigma x}=\tau_{x}\,g_{1\sigma{\bf k}}+\tau_{y}\,g_{2\sigma{\bf k}}+g_{3\sigma{\bf k}}\,,

(30)

where
g_{i\sigma{\bf k}}
are certain functions of
{\bf k}
. The explicit forms of
g_{i\sigma{\bf k}}
are given in the Appendix
A
.

For
\mu
significantly larger than
\Delta_{\sigma}
, when the anisotropy of relaxation time is negligible and
\gamma_{\sigma}
becomes constant, we find

\displaystyle\sigma_{xx}=\frac{e^{2}v^{2}}{\pi\hbar}\sum_{\sigma}\int\frac{d^{2}{\bf k}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,\mathcal{D}_{\sigma}\left[(\mu_{\sigma}^{2}+v^{2}k_{x}^{2}-v^{2}k_{y}^{2})g_{1\sigma{\bf k}}\right.

\displaystyle\left.+2v^{2}k_{x}k_{y}g_{2\sigma{\bf k}}+2\mu_{\sigma}vk_{x}g_{3\sigma{\bf k}}\right],\hskip 14.22636pt

(31)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\sigma}=\left[(\mu_{\sigma}-vk+i\gamma_{\sigma})(\mu_{\sigma}+vk+i\gamma_{\sigma})\right.\hskip 56.9055pt

\displaystyle\times\left.(\mu_{\sigma}-vk-i\gamma_{\sigma})(\mu_{\sigma}+vk-i\gamma_{\sigma})\right]^{-1}.\hskip 14.22636pt

(32)

Then we get

\displaystyle\sigma_{xx}=\frac{e^{2}}{8\pi^{2}\hbar}\sum_{\sigma}\frac{\mu_{\sigma}}{\gamma_{1\sigma}}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\;d\phi\;\big(\cos^{2}\phi\,g_{1\sigma k_{\sigma}}

\displaystyle+\sin\phi\cos\phi\,g_{2\sigma k_{\sigma}}+\cos\phi\,g_{3\sigma k_{\sigma}}\big),

(33)

where we denoted
k_{x}=k\cos\phi\;
,
k_{y}=k\sin\phi
, so that
g_{n\sigma k}
are functions of angle
\phi
.
When the random Rashba field is small,
g_{1\sigma k}\to 1
,
g_{2\sigma k}=g_{3\sigma k}\to 0
, and for
\mu
significantly larger than
\Delta_{\sigma}
, we finally get

\displaystyle\sigma_{xx}^{0}=\frac{e^{2}}{8\pi\hbar}\sum_{\sigma}\frac{\mu-\Delta_{\sigma}}{\gamma_{\sigma}+\gamma_{0}}\;,

(34)

where to avoid singularity when
\gamma_{\sigma}\to 0
), we have added in the denominator a constant term
\gamma_{0}
, which takes into account scattering by other defects. Such scattering becomes dominant when Rashba spin-orbit fluctuations disappear. However, in the following numerical calculations we assumed the parameters of Rashba field fluctuations large enough, to make the scattering on spin-orbit fluctuations dominant, and thus we neglect other scattering processes.

The effect of magnetic field is usually described quantitatively by the relevant magnetoresistance (MR), which is defined by the standard formula,

{\rm{MR}}(B)=\frac{\rho_{xx}(B)-\rho_{xx}(0)}{\rho_{xx}(0)}\simeq\frac{\sigma_{xx}(0)}{\sigma_{xx}(B)}-1.

(35)

The magnetoresistance in conventional systems (with scalar scattering potential of impurities) is usually positive. Negative magnetoresistance appears in some specific cases, like due to weak localization or due to spin disorder, or in Kondo systems.
Here we show that negative magnetoresistance also appears due to scattering on fluctuating in space Rashba interaction.

V
Summary and conclusions

We have analysed transport properties of graphene in an in-plane external magnetic field, with randomly fluctuating Rashba spin-orbit interaction. Our main objective was to study the longitudinal magnetoresistance, and the
considerations were limited to low energy states in the vicinity of the Dirac points. To find the conductivity and then the longitudinal magnetoresistance, we used Kubo approach with the Green function formalism. We have calculated electron relaxation time due to scattering by Rashba field fluctuations and also included the vertex corrections.

Numerical results based on the derived analytical formulas clearly show, that for the
correlation length
\xi
between
\xi=5\times 10^{-6}
cm and
\xi=10^{-5}
cm, the
electrical conductivity of graphene with fluctuating Rashba interaction in external magnetic field applied in the graphene plane increases with increasing magnetic field. The corresponding resistivity becomes reduced by the external magnetic field, i.e. magnetoresistance is negative. This is in agreement with experimental observations
[
61
,
62
]
.
