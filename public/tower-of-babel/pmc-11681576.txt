Lipoprotein(a) and Cardiovascular Disease in People Living With HIV: The Next Horizon
Authors: David B Hanna, Carlos J Rodriguez
Published: 2024

HIV infection is now a well‐established risk factor for cardiovascular disease, with numerous studies showing an increased risk for subclinical atherosclerosis, myocardial infarction, and heart failure among people living with HIV (PWH) compared with those without HIV, 1 and the Randomized Trial to Prevent Vascular Events in HIV (REPRIEVE) trial demonstrating the value of statins in reducing atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease risk in PWH. 2 Over the years, numerous circulating biomarkers of inflammation, immune dysfunction, and cardiac dysfunction have been found to be elevated in PWH. 3 , 4 Known pathways leading to coronary heart disease (CHD) development are affected by the inflammatory aspects of HIV infection, namely, low‐level inflammation and immune dysfunction that persist even with potent antiretroviral therapy (ART), which produce elevated CHD risk among PWH who are not on ART and among PWH who use ART but who have inadequately suppressed HIV viral loads or low CD4+ T‐cell counts. 1 , 5

Elevated lipoprotein(a) [Lp(a)] is an established risk factor for atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease independent of low‐density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol. This may be explained by the carriage by Lp(a) of oxidized phospholipids, which bind to apo(a), itself a protein bound to Lp(a), leading to inflammation, thrombosis, and atherogenesis. 6 Thus, given the inflammatory role of Lp(a), it is plausible that it may further contribute to CHD risk among PWH. Furthermore, it is known that PWH are more likely to have mixed dyslipidemia, 7 which portends a smaller and more atherogenic LDL particle. Thus, LDL levels alone may not account for residual CHD risk among PWH, and looking beyond LDL to Lp(a) among PWH is important.

The article by Kikuchi et al. in this issue of the Journal of the American Heart Association (JAHA) examines some of these questions, focusing on the potential role of Lp(a) in coronary artery endothelial function in the context of HIV infection. 8 While endothelial dysfunction is a well‐known early precursor on the atherosclerosis pathway, it has been less studied in HIV. 9 The work by Kikuchi et al. is the latest of a series of studies by the same group that have built an evidence base for a role of HIV‐associated endothelial function. 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 In the current study, the authors recruited from a health system in Baltimore, Maryland, a sample of 117 age‐ and sex‐matched adults either with HIV or without HIV, all without clinical CHD. They report that Lp(a) levels are higher among PWH compared with individuals without HIV despite almost 100% of PWH being on ART. They identify an association between Lp(a) levels and reduced change in coronary cross‐sectional area in response to isometric hand grip exercise on stress cardiac magnetic resonance imaging, which is reflective of localized epicardial coronary endothelial vasoreactivity, but only among persons with HIV and not among individuals without HIV. In contrast, they did not find an association of Lp(a) levels with change in coronary blood flow, which is reflective of more global macrovascular and microvascular endothelial vasoreactivity. Of note, slightly over half of the study population were women, who continue to be understudied in HIV research.

An important limitation of the current study is that Black individuals were significantly more represented among the PWH group versus those without HIV. It is known that Black individuals have higher Lp(a) levels 15 and worse endothelial function than non‐Hispanic Whites. Prior studies have found that vascular endothelial function is impaired and nitric oxide bioavailability is reduced in non‐Hispanic Black individuals compared with non‐Hispanic White individuals. 16 , 17 , 18 It is also unclear how hyperlipidemia was defined in the study by Kikuchi et al., leaving uncertainty about how traditional lipid components such as LDL cholesterol, high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol, and triglycerides may interact with Lp(a) to modify CHD risk.

Despite all this, the current study moves the field forward towards solidifying the role of Lp(a) in PWH. Lp(a) is an atherogenic lipoprotein that has been studied predominantly in populations without HIV. Not surprisingly, given the interrelation of inflammation, HIV, and Lp(a), Kikuchi et al. found that Lp(a) levels were more elevated among PWH versus those without HIV. The current study highlights the need for continued work and progress in this field of Lp(a) and PWH that will impact clinical cardiovascular care and may eventually lead to an Lp(a)‐lowering trial among PWH. Indeed, the authors themselves showed in a small 6‐week clinical trial (N=15) that evolocumab both reduces LDL and Lp(a) levels and improves coronary cross‐sectional area response to isometric hand‐grip exercise among PWH. 6 The localization of the differences observed among PWH to epicardial coronary endothelial vasoreactivity, a known marker and precursor to CHD, seems plausible given that Lp(a) is believed to be a factor in epicardial CHD but not for microvascular disease and emphasizes the HIV‐specific effects on CHD and the potential role that inflammation and Lp(a) plays in this. The advent of Lp(a)‐lowering treatments presents a specific, potentially intervenable pathway. The findings by Kikuchi et al. are particularly relevant given that there are several pharmacologic intervention trials to directly lower Lp(a) levels in the CHD population: the Olpasiran Trials of Cardiovascular Events and Lipoprotein(a) Reduction [OCEAN(a)] 19 (results anticipated in 2026) and the Lp(a) HORIZON (Assessing the Impact of Lipoprotein(a) Lowering With TQJ230 on Major Cardiovascular Events in Patients With CVD) outcomes trial 20 (results anticipated in 2025). Of course, just as was done with statins, analogous to the REPRIEVE trial, the safety and efficacy of these Lp(a)‐lowering agents will need to be tested in PWH.

Given that the current study is cross‐sectional and with a limited sample size, larger longitudinal studies are needed to corroborate these findings and to provide more insight regarding the distribution of Lp(a) among PWH and its association with cardiovascular events, which could further tease out whether Lp(a) mediates the role of HIV in coronary endothelial function, and what Lp(a) thresholds portend greater CHD risk among PWH. Further analyses will be needed to test the association of Lp(a) with coronary endothelial function and CHD events in relation to T‐cell count, viral load, and ART regimen. Future studies will also be needed to determine whether Lp(a) adds value when incorporated into CHD risk prediction approaches among PWH. Integral to such studies will be well‐characterized study cohorts that can account for the immense variability among PWH both in the United States and around the world due to differences in age, sex, ethnicity and genetic ancestry, history of use of different ART regimens (including the earliest medications that had significant cardiotoxic effects, as well as the current integrase inhibitor‐based regimens), and other major confounders. Carefully designed studies like the work by Kikuchi et al. will continue to play an important role in providing evidence for new mechanistic pathways that contribute to the complex manifestation of long‐term chronic disease in PWH.
