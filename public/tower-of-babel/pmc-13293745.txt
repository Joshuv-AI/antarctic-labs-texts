Predicting Knee osteoarthritis progression using explainable machine learning and clinical imaging data
Authors: Rayan Harari, Stacy E. Smith, Sara M. Bahouth, Lawrence Lo, Meera Sury, Ming Yin, Lena F. Schaefer, William Wells, Jamie E. Collins, Jeffrey Duryea
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo evaluate explainable AI models for predicting Knee osteoarthritis (KOA) progression using quantitative MRI and clinical data, for two outcomes: a composite endpoint (radiographic plus pain progression) and a radiographic-only progression endpoint.MethodWe analyzed 600 participants from the Foundation for the National Institutes of Health Osteoarthritis Biomarkers Consortium (FNIH), part of case–control cohort in the Osteoarthritis Initiative (OAI). The participants were grouped as composite progressors (n = 194), radiographic-only (n = 103), pain-only (n = 103), and non-progressors (n = 200). Input features included demographic data, Kellgren–Lawrence grade, joint space width, WOMAC pain score, and quantitative volume MRI measurements of KOA-related, including cartilage, bone marrow lesions, osteophytes, effusion-synovitis (ES), and Hoffa's synovitis (HS), at baseline and as 24-month change. Data were split into stratified 80% training and 20% held-out test sets, with 10-fold cross-validation used for model tuning within the training set. Five classifiers (random forest, XGBoost, logistic regression, decision tree, and multilayer perceptron). We applied multiple explainability methods, including Gini importance, SHAP values, regression coefficients, and permutation importance.ResultsRadiographic progression was most accurately predicted using longitudinal change features (random forest AUC = 0.87). Baseline features alone also yielded strong performance (AUC = 0.80). Composite progression was more difficult to predict (AUCs = 0.66–0.70). Across models, key expandability factors included medial femoral cartilage loss, BMLs in the medial tibia and femur, medial osteophyte, and ES volumes.ConclusionExplainable machine learning using quantitative MRI enable interpretable prediction of KOA progression. This is the first study in the FNIH/OAI cohort to integrate longitudinal quantitative MRI features with model-agnostic explanations across multiple classifiers.

Introduction

Knee osteoarthritis (KOA) is a leading cause of disability worldwide [1], affecting an estimated 654 million people over the age of 40 as of 2020 [2], with incidence and prevalence expected to rise substantially due to aging populations and increasing rates of obesity and sedentary lifestyles [3]. The disease contributes to substantial individual and societal burden, including chronic pain, functional impairment, reduced quality of life, and significant healthcare costs [4].

A major clinical challenge in KOA is its heterogeneous progression [5]. While some patients remain stable for years, others experience rapid deterioration in both joint structure and symptoms. Furthermore, the correlation between radiographic changes and patient-reported symptoms is often weak [6]. Many individuals with severe joint space narrowing report minimal pain, while others with significant pain exhibit only mild imaging abnormalities [[7], [8], [9]]. This discordance complicates both clinical decision-making and the evaluation of therapeutic efficacy in clinical trials [10]. For instance, disease-modifying osteoarthritis drug trials often fail to demonstrate benefit due to reliance on structural endpoints alone, even when patients report symptomatic relief [11].

Conventional diagnostic tools, particularly radiography, remain widely used due to accessibility and cost-effectiveness, but they are limited in sensitivity, particularly for early cartilage loss and soft tissue abnormalities [12]. The Kellgren-Lawrence (K/L) grading system is commonly used for classification, yet it lacks granularity and is susceptible to inter-observer variability [13]. MRI, in contrast, provides a more detailed view of joint structures, including cartilage, subchondral bone, menisci, and bone marrow lesions (BMLs) [12]. However, MRI is less accessible and time-intensive, and standalone MRI biomarkers often lack sufficient predictive power to inform prognosis at the individual level [14].

Because KOA progression reflects multiple biological processes, single-modality assessments are limited [10]. ML methods can integrate clinical and imaging data and identify complex relationships not captured by conventional statistical approaches. Prior KOA studies have shown promise in predicting radiographic or symptomatic progression [[15], [16], [17]], but many rely on semi-quantitative imaging, single-timepoint features, or less interpretable models [18], [19]. Here, we instead evaluate fully quantitative MRI biomarkers and longitudinal change features across multiple model classes to characterize prognostic signal rather than identify a single optimal algorithm.

To address these gaps, the Foundation for the National Institutes of Health (FNIH) Osteoarthritis Biomarkers Consortium Project proposed a robust, data-driven framework that categorizes patients into four mutually exclusive progression groups based on both radiographic and pain changes over time [20]. These standardized definitions provide a valuable reference point for training and evaluating predictive models in KOA research. In this study, we develop and evaluate explainable ML models to predict KOA progression using a well-characterized cohort from the FNIH initiative. We focus on two key objectives: (1) distinguishing radiographic and symptomatic progressors from other subtypes using both baseline and longitudinal data; and (2) identifying the most important imaging and clinical features contributing to predictions, using explainability tools. By integrating multimodal data with transparent algorithms, our goal is to improve prognostic modeling in KOA and support more personalized care and targeted trial design.

Methods

Study population and data source

This retrospective analysis used data from the FNIH Osteoarthritis Biomarkers Consortium Project, a curated case-control study embedded within the larger Osteoarthritis Initiative (OAI) [21], [22]. The dataset included 600 participants selected based on the availability of clinical, radiographic, and MRI scans. These groups were derived from longitudinal changes in medial tibiofemoral joint space width (JSW) and Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index (WOMAC) pain scores [23]. Radiographic progression was defined as a JSW loss of ≥0.7 mm from baseline to 24, 36, or 48 months, and pain progression as a persistent increase in WOMAC pain score between 24 and 60 months. Institutional review boards at each OAI site gave approval. The FNIH cohort represents an enriched case–control sample designed to support biomarker discovery rather than population-level risk estimation. Therefore, model discrimination can be evaluated reliably, whereas probability calibration may differ in settings with lower progression prevalence. As in prior analyses of this cohort, discordant progressors (pain-only or radiographic-only) were treated as non-events in the composite classification to maintain a clinically conservative definition of progression.

Clinical and imaging variables

The study included a combination of demographic, clinical, radiographic, and quantitative MRI-derived variables. Demographic variables included age, sex, race, and body mass index. Clinical variables included baseline WOMAC pain scores and information on the use of medications related to KOA management. Radiographic assessment involved Kellgren-Lawrence (K/L) grading and measurements of minimum JSW at baseline. We used the MRI Osteoarthritis Software Scoring, a semi-automated software tool to measure compartment-specific cartilage (Cart [24]) bone marrow lesion (BML [25]), osteophyte (Ost [26]), effusion-synovitis (ES [27]), and Hoffa's synovitis (HS [27]) volumes (Table 1). Cartilage volume was measured in the central weight-bearing region of the medial femur. MRI Osteoarthritis Software Scoring measures peripheral osteophytes in the central portion of the tibiofemoral joint. We measured ES on axial slices in the same region as the patella and Hoffa’s synovitis (HS) in a central portion of the Hoffa's fat pad. Measurements were made in the medial and lateral femorotibial compartments, and the patella joint for BMLs. Data preprocessing

All radiographic and MRI assessments followed standardized protocols established by the OAI. Before model training, continuous variables were standardized using z-score normalization fitted on the training data and applied to the test data to prevent information leakage. Categorical variables were encoded numerically. Longitudinal change features were calculated as the difference between baseline and 24-month measurements for each participant (24-month value − baseline value). This calculation was performed at the participant level and did not use outcome labels or cohort-level fitted parameters. All subsequent preprocessing procedures, including z-score normalization and hyperparameter tuning, were performed using training data only and then applied to the held-out test data.

Classification framework

Two predictive analysis frameworks were used: Cross-sectional analysis used only baseline imaging and clinical variables, and Longitudinal analysis used the baseline to 24-month changes. For each analysis type, we performed two binary classification tasks (Table 2). These tasks reflect real-world clinical interest in identifying patients with both symptomatic and structural progression (primary), or any radiographic worsening (secondary). Machine Learning models and training procedure

Five supervised machine learning algorithms were used for classification: RF, Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), LR with L2 regularization, decision tree, and multilayer perceptron (MLP). To enhance reproducibility, model training was initialized with a random seed of 42. Data were partitioned into stratified training (80%) and held-out test (20%) sets. Hyperparameter tuning was conducted using 10-fold cross-validation within the training set only, and final model performance was evaluated on the untouched test set. Feature selection was not performed, as the objective was to assess the prognostic value of a predefined panel of quantitative imaging biomarkers rather than derive parsimonious predictor sets. All data analysis and modeling were conducted in Python using scikit-learn (v0.24.2), XGBoost (v1.5.0), TensorFlow (v2.6.0), SHAP (v0.39.0), and pandas (v1.3.4).

Supplemental Table S1 provides details of hyperparameters for each model. Model performance was evaluated based on accuracy (overall proportion of correct predictions), precision (proportion of true positives among predicted positives, i.e., positive predictive value), sensitivity (proportion of true positives identified among all actual positives, i.e., sensitivity), F1-score (harmonic mean of precision and sensitivity), and AUC-ROC (area under the receiver operating characteristic curve, reflecting the model's ability to discriminate between classes across thresholds). Given the class imbalance inherent in the dataset, stratified folds were used to preserve class proportions in each training and validation split. Details of model training parameters are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Model explainability

To improve interpretability and clinical relevance, explainability techniques were applied to all models. For tree-based models (RF, XGBoost, decision tree), feature importance was computed using Gini impurity scores. SHAP values were calculated for XGBoost models to quantify the contribution of individual features to each prediction. LR coefficients were examined to determine the direction and strength of feature associations with the target outcome. For the MLP model, permutation importance was used to estimate the impact of each variable on predictive performance. These approaches allowed for comparison of key predictors across models and supported identification of robust imaging and clinical biomarkers of progression.

Longitudinal models (baseline→24-month change)

Model's Performance: Using longitudinal change features yielded improved AUCs over the baseline-only model (Fig. 1B; Table 4). LR and XGBoost reached AUC = 0.70, RF 0.69, and MLP 0.68; accuracies were 0.68–0.69. Sensitivity remained modest for most models (0.26–0.31).

Explainability: In RF (Fig. 2C, bottom-left), change features dominated: cartilage – medial femur – change, ES – total – change, and OSTP – medial femur – change were top-ranked, together with BML – medial femur – change and BML – total – change. XGBoost SHAP (Supplementary Fig. 2–S, left) showed that greater loss of cartilage (more negative change), larger BML – medial tibia – change, and increases in ES shifted predictions toward progression, while smaller cartilage loss was protective. LR coefficients (Supplementary Fig. 4–S, left) featured large negative coefficients for cartilage – medial femur – change, reinforcing the protective association of preserved cartilage; positive coefficients were observed for OSTP – medial femur – change and ES – total – change. MLP permutation importance (Supplementary Fig. 8–S, left) highlighted cartilage – medial femur – change, Age, and OSTP – medial femur – change, consistent with the RF/XGBoost pattern.

Longitudinal models (baseline→24-month change)

Model's Performance: Longitudinal radiographic classification maintained strong performance (Fig. 1D; Table 4). RF achieved the highest discrimination with AUC = 0.87, accuracy 0.76, precision 0.97, and sensitivity 0.53. MLP and Decision Tree followed (AUC 0.76 and 0.76 [0.76 rounded from 0.764]; accuracies 0.74 and 0.73; sensitivities 0.53 and 0.54). XGBoost and LR yielded AUC 0.80 and 0.75, respectively.

Explainability: RF Gini (Fig. 2D, bottom-right) again placed cartilage – medial femur – change at the top, followed by OSTP – medial tibia – change, OSTP – medial femur – change, and BML – medial tibia – change; ES – total – change was also influential. XGBoost SHAP (Supplementary Fig. 2–S, right) exhibited the same directionality: greater cartilage loss, increases in BML (especially tibial medial) and OSTP (medial compartment) drove predictions toward radiographic progression; smaller loss (or gain) in cartilage – medial femur shifted predictions away from progression. LR coefficients (Supplementary Fig. 4–S, right) showed a large negative coefficient for cartilage – medial femur – change and positive coefficients for ES – total – change and OSTP – medial tibia – change. MLP permutation importance (Supplementary Fig. 8–S, right) prioritized cartilage – medial femur – change, Age, ES – total – change, and OSTP – medial tibia – change, consistent with RF and XGBoost.

Summary of dominant predictors across tasks

Across models and tasks, the most consistent predictors were medial femoral cartilage, medial tibial/femoral BML burden, osteophyte burden, and ES. Longitudinal change in these features was particularly informative for radiographic progression. Because several imaging biomarkers are biologically related and statistically correlated, we focused on convergence across explainability methods rather than single-feature rankings to identify stable predictor patterns.

Sensitivity analyses separating structural and pain progression

To further isolate predictors of structural and symptomatic worsening, we performed pairwise sensitivity analyses comparing radiographic-only progressors with non-progressors (G2 vs G4) and pain-only progressors with non-progressors (G3 vs G4). Longitudinal models achieved good discrimination for structural progression (best AUC = 0.82), driven primarily by cartilage loss and increasing BML burden. In contrast, models showed limited discrimination for pain-only progression (AUC = 0.40–0.47). This suggests that structural MRI features alone incompletely capture mechanisms underlying persistent pain. Detailed ROC curves and feature rankings are provided in Supplementary Fig. 9–S.

Predictive value of quantitative MRI beyond clinical variables

To evaluate whether quantitative MRI features provided prognostic information beyond standard clinical predictors, we compared three modeling strategies across all classification tasks: (1) clinical variables only, (2) clinical variables combined with MRI features using logistic regression, and (3) clinical + MRI features using the best-performing machine learning model (Figure 3). Across all tasks, clinical-only models showed limited discriminative ability, with AUC values ranging from 0.47 to 0.48. Incorporating quantitative MRI features improved performance, particularly for radiographic outcomes (AUC up to 0.73). The highest discrimination was achieved by machine learning models that integrated clinical and imaging features, most notably for longitudinal radiographic progression (RF AUC = 0.87). ROC curves showed consistent improvement when quantitative MRI features are added to clinical predictors, with the largest gains observed for radiographic progression. Machine learning models provided additional discrimination beyond logistic regression in longitudinal analyses.

Discussion

In this study, we developed and evaluated explainable machine learning models to predict KOA progression using both baseline and longitudinal imaging and clinical data from the FNIH cohort. We evaluated cross-sectional (baseline) and longitudinal (baseline-to-24-month change) feature sets for two tasks: a ‘primary’ composite endpoint (radiographic + pain) and a ‘secondary’ radiographic endpoint (any radiographic progression). Our findings show the feasibility of distinguishing progressors, particularly those with radiographic change, with reasonable accuracy and interpretability. Performance was highest for radiographic-only progression and improved when longitudinal change features were included (RF AUC = 0.87; Fig. 1D; Table 4). By contrast, the composite (pain + structure) outcome remained harder to separate, with cross-sectional AUCs in the mid-0.60s (Fig. 1A; Table 4). Sensitivity analyses further reinforced this dissociation, with imaging features predicting structural but not isolated pain progression. Importantly, additional analyses confirmed that MRI-derived features provided incremental predictive value beyond clinical variables alone. This supports their role as independent prognostic biomarkers. Model explanations converged similar biological signals across methods: medial femoral cartilage level and loss, BML burden in the medial tibia and femur, medial-compartment osteophytes, and ES (Fig. 2; Supplementary Fig. 1–8). The contribution of this work lies in investigating a reproducible predictive structure for established imaging biomarkers rather than proposing a novel machine learning algorithm. Because the cohort is enriched for progression, predicted probabilities should not be interpreted as population risk estimates without recalibration in representative clinical datasets.

Comparison with prior FNIH/OAI work: Our findings are directionally consistent with earlier analyses using the same FNIH cohort, which are comparable to or higher than statistics reported in recent FNIH analyses using quantitative MRI using traditional statistical approaches(range ∼0.69–0.82) [[28], [29], [30], [31]]. Using mostly MOAKS-based semi-quantitative imaging features, Hunter et al. reported higher discrimination for radiographic progression than for composite outcomes and identified medial compartment cartilage, BMLs, osteophytes, and synovitis as key contributors [31]. With fully quantitative MRI (MOSS) measures, Smith et al. (2025) observed c-statistics in the high-0.6 to ∼0.8 range, again higher for radiographic progression than for composite [30]. Our model (RF) AUC = 0.87 for longitudinal radiographic progression is higher than prior quantitative-MRI models (which reported high-0.7 to ∼0.82), whereas our composite-endpoint AUCs (0.66–0.70) are similar to the modest discrimination seen previously.

The lower performance observed for composite progression likely reflects the partial dissociation between structural pathology and pain, a known feature of osteoarthritis. Imaging biomarkers capture tissue-level degeneration but may not fully represent neurobiological or inflammatory contributors to symptoms, which underscores the need for multimodal prediction strategies. The convergence of cartilage loss, medial BML burden, and osteophyte progression suggests the presence of structural phenotypes that may warrant investigation in future subgroup analyses.

Consistent with prior studies, ensemble models such as RF and XGBoost outperformed linear models, particularly for structural progression tasks [32], [33]. Our dual-task framework extends earlier ML analyses in OAI that focused on either radiographic or pain outcomes alone [34], [35]. Given the moderate sensitivity observed across models, these approaches may be better suited for high-specificity use cases such as identifying candidates for disease-modifying trials rather than broad screening.

What the explainable ML adds: A central aim of this study was to move beyond purely predictive models and provide transparent explanations of model behavior. Prior OA prediction studies have also used interpretable machine learning approaches, which identify SHAP-based explanations in OAI-derived models and partial dependence plots to examine feature contributions in OA incidence prediction [[36], [37], [38]]. In this study, across RF, XGBoost, logistic regression, and MLP models, explainability analyses consistently identified the same structural predictors: cartilage – medial femur, medial tibial and femoral BML burden, medial osteophytes, and ES. The direction of effect was also consistent across methods: greater cartilage volume and smaller cartilage loss were associated with lower progression risk, whereas increasing BMLs, osteophytes, and synovitis were associated with higher risk. Longitudinal change features carried the strongest signal for structural outcomes, particularly cartilage loss and increasing BML or osteophyte burden. ES repeatedly appeared among influential predictors, consistent with prior reports linking inflammatory activity with structural worsening, whereas HS showed less consistent importance.

Feature-importance and SHAP-based explanations should be interpreted as summaries of model behavior rather than evidence of causal mechanism. These approaches identify variables that improve model discrimination but do not establish biological pathways, particularly when predictors are correlated. Post-hoc explanations of non-linear models are not equivalent to causal inference and can be unstable under feature correlation and distribution shift; therefore, we avoid mechanistic claims and interpret these results as tools for model transparency, debugging, and hypothesis prioritization, consistent with cautions raised by prior studies [39] regarding the limits of explaining black-box models in high-stakes settings. The primary contribution of this study therefore lies in clarifying the prognostic structure of quantitative MRI biomarkers within multivariable models rather than proposing a new algorithm. Local explanation methods may support clinical interpretation by identifying the dominant features contributing to risk for an individual patient, although prospective evaluation is needed to determine their impact on decision-making. Across five classifiers and multiple explanation techniques, these predictors showed consistent ranking and directionality, suggesting that their prognostic relevance is robust to model choice. Task-specific patterns also emerged: longitudinal cartilage loss dominated prediction of structural progression, whereas baseline features provided weaker separation and imaging markers showed limited ability to discriminate pain-only progression.

Interpreting the composite endpoint: The modest AUCs for the primary (composite) task are consistent with the literature and likely reflect the structure–symptom gap in KOA. Imaging explains structural progression well, but persistent pain reflects additional processes (central sensitization, activity patterns, comorbidity, psychosocial factors) not captured by the structural MRI features used here. This argues for multimodal models (e.g., PROs, activity/sensor data, biochemical markers) when the clinical question centers on symptomatic progression.

Clinical and trial-design implications: The ability to rank predictors and show their direction of effect has potential practical value. The modest discrimination observed for the composite endpoint (AUC 0.66–0.70) should be interpreted in the context of the additional cost and acquisition burden associated with quantitative MRI. These results are unlikely to support broad population screening when MRI would be obtained solely for prediction. A more practical application may be trial enrichment or longitudinal monitoring of patients already undergoing MRI evaluation, particularly given the stronger performance observed for radiographic progression. For trial enrichment, longitudinal change in medial femoral cartilage, medial BML/OSTP, and ES offers a basis to identify knees at higher risk of radiographic worsening. For clinical monitoring, patient-level explanations have the potential to clarify why a model flags risk (cartilage loss vs. BML growth vs. synovitis), which can support targeted follow-up or adjunctive management. Models incorporating 24-month change are not intended for baseline screening but may support risk trajectory assessment by identifying knees with accelerated structural deterioration. Such information could be particularly valuable for trial enrichment or monitoring disease-modifying interventions.

Methodological considerations: Several methodological considerations should be noted. The enriched case-control design of the FNIH dataset increases power for biomarker discovery but does not reflect progression prevalence in general KOA populations; discrimination metrics may therefore not translate directly to population screening without recalibration. Feature correlation among imaging biomarkers can influence tree-based importance rankings, so we emphasized convergence across multiple explainability approaches. Sensitivity remained modest across most models, particularly in cross-sectional analyses, consistent with prior KOA prognostic studies [40]. A strength of the study is the standardized FNIH outcome framework, which supports comparison across studies and may reduce label noise [41]. External validation in population-based cohorts will be needed to assess calibration, threshold performance, and clinical utility. Correlation analysis of the top-ranked predictors showed strong relationships among osteophyte measures and between medial tibial and medial compartment BML measures (Supplementary Fig. 10–S). These findings suggest that several highly ranked variables represent related structural processes rather than independent biomarkers.

Limitations and next steps: This study has limitations. The FNIH dataset is enriched for progressors, which may affect model generalizability to broader OAI cohorts. External validation in independent cohorts will be essential to establish transportability before clinical use. Symptom prediction will likely require non-imaging modalities. Some variables (e.g., HS [30]) may benefit from re-definition or improved measurement. Future work should test multimodal models, extend quantitative cartilage beyond the medial femur, and evaluate calibration and decision-curve utility for concrete use-cases (e.g., enrichment thresholds). Further, while model explainability was prioritized, not all techniques capture non-linear interactions or temporal trends equally well. The lack of external validation also reflects a broader limitation in OA imaging research: few independent cohorts include harmonized longitudinal MRI biomarkers, comparable progression definitions, and sufficient follow-up. Progress will require shared quantitative imaging pipelines and cross-cohort feature harmonization to support transportability testing. Lastly, while ML models showed reasonable performance, they remain prediction tools and not diagnostic instruments.

Conclusions

To our knowledge, this is the first study to apply explainable ML models to predict KOA progression using fully quantitative MRI-based features in the FNIH cohort. Models leveraging longitudinal changes in cartilage, BMLs, OSTPs, and ES achieved strong discrimination of structural progression (AUC up to 0.87), with consistent feature rankings across five ML classifiers. Although predictive performance was lower for symptom-based outcomes, our results support the use of explainable ML to predict KOA progression, particularly when leveraging longitudinal imaging data. These findings extend prior FNIH/OAI studies by integrating interpretability techniques (e.g., SHAP, permutation, and coefficients) with established imaging biomarkers, which can support future efforts in trial enrichment, risk stratification, and multimodal modeling for KOA progression. This study should be viewed as a prognostic modeling investigation grounded in quantitative imaging rather than an exercise in algorithmic novelty. The models provide a structured way to evaluate how established biomarkers jointly relate to progression risk while maintaining transparency regarding their limitations. The findings therefore inform both future biomarker research and the design of multimodal prediction frameworks.
