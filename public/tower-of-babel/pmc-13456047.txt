Advanced freeze–thaw production of fungal chitosan and its Ag–Cu nanocomposite: enhanced antimicrobial activity and in vitro cytotoxicity evaluation
Authors: Hillary Onyeka Abugu, Eray Tatlıcı, Ayfer Kılıç, Elif Apohan, Hatice Çağlar Yılmaz, Özfer Yeşilada, Sema Erdemoğlu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Antimicrobial resistance continues to undermine the effectiveness of conventional treatments and so, in this study, fungal chitosan was produced from Pleurotus ostreatus through chitin extraction followed by freeze–thaw-assisted deacetylation and subsequent stabilization and capping of Ag–Cu nanoparticles. The as-synthesized nanocomposites were characterized and evaluated for their antimicrobial and cytotoxic properties. Fourier-Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR), X-Ray Diffraction (XRD), Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM), Energy-Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (EDX), elemental mapping, and zeta potential analysis confirmed successful formation of the fungal chitosan and Ag–Cu nanocomposite. Average crystallite sizes of 9.49, 30.60, 22.25, and 23.74 nm were obtained for the reference chitosan (RCS), synthesized chitosan (OCS), chitosan–Ag–Cu (OCS3) composite, and Ag–Cu (NCS2) nanoparticles respectively. Microscopic and elemental analyses revealed a compact fibrillar and heterogeneous porous morphology together with homogeneous elemental distribution in the composite, confirming successful incorporation of Ag and Cu throughout the chitosan matrix indicating effective stabilization of the metallic phase. All the formulations exhibited positive zeta potential values, suggesting favorable colloidal stability. Biological evaluation demonstrated showed that OCS3 exhibited strong activity against Escherichia coli ATCC 25922, Pseudomonas aeruginosa ATCC 27853, Enterococcus faecium NJ-1, Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 29213, and Candida albicans ATCC 90028, outperforming fungal chitosan alone. The L929 fibroblasts and MCF-7 breast cancer cells cell-culture studies further revealed pronounced differences in selectivity and cytotoxicity of the formulations. NCS2 gave the most balanced combination of antiproliferative activity and biological discrimination, unlike the OCS which showed the most favorable selectivity profile and OCS3 displayed the highest cytotoxic potency but limited selectivity toward cancer cells. The observation therefore demonstrate that in addition to being an effective platform for functionalization and stabilization of biopolymer and Ag–Cu composite, freeze–thaw-derived fungal chitosan is a sustainable alternative to conventional chitosan sources.

Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance is a threat, which surfaced just after the invention of antimicrobial drugs and this has increased over the years, challenging the very foundation of modern medicine. There is a rise in the antimicrobial resistance due to the improper use, overuse (especially in undeveloped and developing countries), rise of resistant infections and reduced pace in the development of new therapies.1 About 10 million global critical health crisis projected by 2050, with an annual 700 000 fatalities.1

Due to its wider scope of applications and the vast expectations for future demands in the global systems, nanotechnology/nanomaterials are gaining wider acceptability.2 Several materials have been assessed or synthesized for the purpose of solving the problem of antimicrobial resistance including nanocomposites such as VS2/MoS2,3 cobalt-doped VS2 nanosheets.4 These nanocomposites have proven to be commendable antimicrobial properties against both Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacteria. This global-growing demand for efficient antimicrobial drugs and reduced environmental impact, introduced interests in biocompatible and sustainable polymers in medicine of which chitosan has been at the forefront. Chitosan is a biocompatible non-toxic biopolymer that causes minimal immune reactions, mainly extracted from living organisms such as fungi, animals, algae, and bacteria for specific applications.5 Being the second most abundant natural polymer just after cellulose, chitin is the most important product that gives chitosan as its derivative.6 Chitosan is obtained from chitin (a semi-crystalline biopolymer mostly formed by N-acetyl-β-d-glucosamine) through deacetylation after deproteinization, depigmentation, demineralization and removal of water. Both chitosan and chitin are large biopolymers with their molecular size heavily dependent on the production method and source precursor.7 Chitosan from different precursors have been reported to have different properties ranging from seasonal availability of the precursor, allergy risks and harsh conditions during chemical processing for crustacean-derived chitosan to consistent quality, milder extraction conditions and vegan-friendly for fungi-derived chitosan.8,9 Since the chitosan's antimicrobial activity, solubility and other properties are greatly determined by its deacetylation degree, deacetylation process is crucial step in obtaining chitosan from fungi.10 With the application of classical protocol, most especially on fungi-sourced precursor, achieving an effective deacetylation is rather very difficult and so, a deacetylation approach that is much feasible, easier and effective process that gives room for an enhanced product efficacy need to be embraced.11 Freeze–thaw method in the deacetylation of chitin involves alternating freeze and thaw process. In this procedure, the crystals of the ice influence the biopolymer's intramolecular and intermolecular hydrogen bonds, disordering the polymer structure and rendering it more prone to reaction.11–13

Several reports on the antimicrobial efficacy of chitosan from different precursors have been put forward.14–16 Also, chitosan mono-, bi- and tri-metallic nanocomposites have been fabricated,17–20 but their potency is often insufficient for aggressive pathogens.

Since the method of deacetylation of the chitin and the precursor heavily influences the chitosan properties, there is need to re-examine the chitosan–metal composites such as the chitosan–silver–copper composite. Silver nanoparticles have been established to possess high antimicrobial efficacy through the destruction of bacteria, fungi and viruses by releasing Ag+. This destruction is achieved through DNA binding, membrane disruption and enzyme inhibition mechanism.21,22 It also leads to the depolarization of the membrane of bacteria and alters its permeability without visible alteration in the membrane structure.22 Copper oxide also is a well-known antimicrobial agent and by reducing electron density with a high concentration of small particles in some regions of the cytoplasm and the cell wall, it causes the rupture of microbial cell wall especially in E. coli.22,23 Since metallic nanoparticles offer alternative to failing antimicrobial agents; harnessing biocompatible, efficient and sustainable deacetylation method and synergistic chitosan–bimetallic composite can bring a relieve in the quest for an effective antimicrobial agent. In this study, an advanced and efficient freeze–thaw deacetylation procedure was employed to obtain chitosan from Pleurotus ostreatus. The antimicrobial activities of the resulting fungal chitosan (OCS), chitosan–Ag–Cu composite (OCS3), and Ag–Cu nanoparticles (NCS2) were evaluated against several pathogenic bacteria and yeast species. Their cytotoxic effects were also evaluated.

Materials and methods

Reagents

All the reagents were used as received and were of analytical grade. Zn(NO3)2, AgNO3 and Cu(SO4)2 were made by Merck (Darmstadt, Germany). NaOH was from Sigma-Aldrich (Czech Republic). High molecular weight reference chitosan was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (CAS: 9012-76-4, Pcode:102407218, Iceland). The deionized water (DI) used in the experimental studies was obtained from an Elga Purelab water purification system and was used to prepare all the aqueous solutions. Nutrient agar (Hi-media), Sabouraud dextrose agar (Difco), Mops broth (Sigma-Aldrich), Mueller Hinton Broth (Hi-media), ethanol and acetic acid (Merck) were used for antimicrobial assessment.

Apparatus

The supernatants were separated using a Hettich Universal 320R (Germany) model centrifuge which has maximum 9000 rpm and a temperature range of −20 to 40 °C. For mixing, a Berghof, BLH-800, (Germany) model stirrer was used. Protherm, PLF series (Turkey) furnace was used to dry of the materials. The functional groups of the synthesized materials were analyzed using Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy with attenuated total reflectance (FTIR-ATR; PerkinElmer Spectrum One). The FTIR has a resolution of >0.5 cm−1, 20 scans per second, operating range of 650–4000 nm, sensitivity of 30 000 : 1 (peak-to-peak) in a 1 minute scan and the library is Spectrum Software. Phase composition and crystallite size were determined by X-ray diffraction (XRD) using a Rigaku D/MAX-2200 diffractometer. Surface morphology, particle size, and elemental composition were examined by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) using a LEO EVO 40 instrument equipped with a BALTEC SCD 050 sputter coater. The SEM operating conditions are: electron high tensile of 20 kv, secondary electron detector, working distance of 10–13 mm and system vacuum of 1.83 × 10−5. The zeta potential and hydrodynamic diameter of the synthesized materials and bacterial cells were measured using a Malvern Zetasizer Nano series instrument, model Nano-ZS. For the antimicrobial activity evaluation, Ultrasonic bath (El-ma sonic p60H), Mc Farland (Biosan), Incubator (Nuve), 8-channel pipette (Brand) were used.

Preparation of fungal biomass, chitin extraction and deacetylation of the chitin

Preparation of fungal biomass

P. ostreatus were obtained from local markets in Malatya-Türkiye. The fresh sample were cut into smaller pieces and oven dried at 40 °C until a constant weight was achieved. Chitin extraction and subsequent chitosan production were performed by modifying the method described by Ban et al.11 The experimental procedures were carried out as shown in Fig. 1.

The experimental processes for the production of chitin and chitosan from P. ostreatus.

Extraction of chitin

To remove impurities, 100 g of dried mushroom biomass was homogenised in 96% ethanol for 20 min. The supernatant was separated via centrifugation at 9000 rpm for 10 min, and this extraction-centrifugation cycle was repeated three times. Subsequently, the residue was treated with 1000 mL of a solution containing 5% HCl and 0.5% Na2S2O5, followed by incubation at room temperature for one hour. After centrifugation, the residue was washed with deionized water until a neutral pH (∼7.0) was reached.

For deproteinization, the neutralized residue was suspended in 1000 mL of 2% NaOH and heated at 56 °C for 2 h. The mixture was then centrifuged and washed four times with deionized water. To ensure decolourisation, the insoluble residue was reacted with a mixture of 3% (v/v) H2O2 and 750 mL of 0.1 M NaOH at 45 °C under gentle stirring (300 rpm) for 30 min. The solid phase was separated by centrifugation and washed repeatedly with deionized water until neutral pH was achieved. A representative portion of the obtained solid material was dried and designated as chitin (Fig. 1).

Advanced freeze–thaw deacetylation of chitin

Chitosan was prepared by deacetylation of the extracted chitin. Briefly, the remaining chitin was treated with 400 mL of 50% NaOH, and the resulting mixture was subjected to repeated freeze–thaw treatment. The suspension was frozen in liquid nitrogen, thawed at room temperature, and then frozen again. This freeze–thaw cycle was repeated four additional times. Following these cycles, the solution was heated at 104 °C for 10 min. The resulting suspension was cooled to 25 °C, and the solid phase was separated by centrifugation at 9000 rpm for 20 min. Subsequently, 1 M HCl was added dropwise to the separated phase until the pH reached 8.5. Chitosan precipitation occurred within 10–20 min, after which the precipitate was collected by centrifugation at 9000 rpm for 20 min. The recovered material was washed with deionized water until near-neutral pH was achieved and then lyophilized to obtain the final chitosan product (OCS) (Fig. 1).

Synthesis of chitosan/Ag–Cu nanocomposite and Ag–Cu bimetal nanoparticles

The chitosan–Ag–Cu composite (OCS3) was synthesized through reduction and stabilization process following the method by Songping and Shuyuan,24 with a slight modification (Fig. 2).

Synthesis processes of chitosan/Ag–Cu nanocomposite.

Briefly, about 1.00 g of the chitosan was dissolve in 100 mL of 1% acetic acid and stirred at room temperature for 6 h and a homogenous slurry was obtained. Thereafter, 50 mL of the slurry chitosan solution was mixed with 50 mL of 0.1 M solution of AgNO3 and CuSO4. The pH of the mixture was adjusted to about 9 using 0.1 M solution of NaOH and the mixture was heated for 20 minutes while stirring at 250 rpm. A gradual colour change from brownish to dark solution was observed. Afterwards, the heating was stopped and the stirring continued for 4 h. The mixture was then cooled and centrifuged at 9000 rpm for 20 minutes. The centrifuged sample was then washed with deionized water and ethanol before drying at 50 °C for 12 h. It is worth noting that the dried sample was not calcined in order to avoid degradation of the chitosan capping layer. For comparison, Ag–Cu nanoparticles without chitosan (NCS2) were synthesized using the same procedure in the absence of chitosan.

Characterization of the synthesized materials

For characterization and antimicrobial activity assay, the samples were coded as follows: RCS for reference chitosan, OCS for synthesized chitosan, NCS2 for Ag–Cu nanoparticles, and OCS3 for the chitosan–Ag–Cu composite.

Degree of deacetylation

The degree of deacetylation was determined by acid–base titration following the method of Atanda et al.28 Briefly, 0.50 g of chitosan was dissolved in 30 mL of 0.1 M HCl and stirred at room temperature for 30 min. Three drops of methyl orange were then added as the indicator, and the solution was titrated with 0.1 M NaOH. The endpoint was identified by a color change from pink to orange-yellow. The moisture content previously determined for the sample was used as the water content of chitosan. The percentage of free NH2 groups was then calculated using eqn (4).45DD, % = NH2%/9.94% × 100%

The theoretical NH2 content of chitosan was taken as 9.94%, calculated as (16/161) × 100, which represents the proportion of free amino groups in the chitosan structure by weight. In the calculation, C1 denotes the molar concentration of HCl, C2 the molar concentration of NaOH, V1 the volume of HCl used, V2 the titre volume of NaOH, G the sample weight in grams, and W the moisture content of chitosan expressed as a percentage. The factor 0.016 represents the mass of NH2 equivalent to 1 mL of 1 M HCl.29

FTIR, XRD, SEM and zeta potential analysis of the materials

The samples were characterized by FTIR-ATR in attenuated total reflectance mode to identify the functional groups present on the material surface. Spectra were recorded at room temperature over the range of 500–4000 cm−1 with a resolution of 4 cm−1. The crystallographic structure of the synthesized chitosan was analyzed by XRD. The average crystallite size was calculated using the Scherrer equation (eqn (6)),6where λ is the X-ray wavelength, D is the average crystallite size, β is the full width at half maximum expressed in radians, and θ is the Bragg angle. The XRD is Cu-Kα1 radiation with a wavelength of 1.5405 Å emitted from a Cu target to obtain XRD patterns. It uses the Jade 6+ crystal analysis program and library for phase analysis and it scans from 2 to 80 2θ degrees. The XRD model is Rigaku-D/Max 22000 (Tokyo, Japan).

Surface morphology, particle size, elemental composition, and elemental distribution of the materials were examined by SEM coupled with energy-dispersive X-ray analysis (SEM-EDX) and elemental mapping. In addition, zeta potential and hydrodynamic diameter measurements were used to evaluate the dispersion behaviour, stability, and possible interactions of the synthesized antimicrobial materials with microbial cell surfaces. For zeta potential analysis, chitosan and chitosan–metallic composites were dispersed in 0.1% potassium chloride solution, and measurements were performed at 25 °C, pH range of 2–12, with a maximum conductivity of 200 mS cm−1. All zeta potential measurements were carried out in triplicate, and the results were expressed as mean ± zeta deviation (n = 3).

Average molecular weight of chitosan

The method by Yigit et al.30 was followed to ascertain the average molecular weight of the synthesized chitosan (OCS) using IKA (ROTAVISC LO-VI S000, Germany). First, the chitosan was dried to a constant weight and then 1% w/v was made with 0.2 m NaCl and 0.1 m acetic acid. The mixture was stirred and filtered through 0.45 µm from which 0.1 to 0.8 g dl−1 was prepared. At 24.4 °C, viscosity of the solvent was determined (ηs), while ensuring the Torque was above 10% at a revolution per minute (rpm) of 150. Using the same conditions, the viscosity of the diluted chitosans were determined and relative, specific and reduced viscosities calculated using eqn (1)–(3).7Relative viscosity, ηRel = η/ηs8Specific viscosity ηSep = ηRel − 19Reduced viscosity ηRed = ηSep/bwhere ηRel is the relative viscosity, ηSep is the specific viscosity, ηRed is the reduced viscosity, b is the chitosan concentration in g dl−1. From the Huggins plot (ηRed against b), the intrinsic viscosity [η] was estimated (extrapolated to get the y-intercept) and used to calculate the viscosity-average molecular weight (Mw) through the application of Mark–Houwink eqn (10). K and a are the constants which are characteristic of a particular polymer–solvent system at a particular intrinsic viscosity and temperature of the polymer in that solvent. Both parameters are temperature and chitosan's degree of deacetylation dependent and are valid for a certain range of molecular weights.3110[η] = KMwa

In this study, 0.2 M NaCl/0.1 M acetic acid was used as the chitosan solvent at 24 °C, with a and K Mark–Houwink constants values being 0.93 and 1.81 × 10−3 dm3 g−1 respectively.

Antimicrobial activity assay

The antimicrobial activities of OCS, OCS3 and NCS2 were evaluated against selected pathogenic microorganisms, including the Gram-negative bacteria Escherichia coli ATCC 25922 and Pseudomonas aeruginosa ATCC 27853, the Gram-positive bacteria Enterococcus faecium NJ-1 and Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 29213, and the yeast Candida albicans ATCC 90028. All test microorganisms were obtained from stock cultures maintained in the Biotechnology Laboratory of İnönü University, Malatya.

For inoculum preparation, bacterial and yeast strains were first cultured on nutrient agar and Sabouraud dextrose agar plates, respectively, and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. After incubation, the microbial suspensions were prepared and adjusted to the 0.5 McFarland standard.

Minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) values were determined using the broth micro dilution method in sterile 96-well microplates. Mueller–Hinton broth was used for bacterial assays, whereas RPMI-MOPS broth was used for the yeast assay. Briefly, 100 µL of the appropriate broth medium was dispensed into each well. Stock solutions of the test compounds were then prepared and serially diluted by a twofold dilution method. Subsequently, 100 µL of the standardized microbial suspension was added to each well. The microplates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h for bacteria and 48 h for yeast. The MIC value was defined as the lowest concentration of the test compound that completely inhibited visible microbial growth. The results were further confirmed by the addition of resazurin dye.32 Fluconazole and gentamycin were used as control antibiotics and the antimicrobial activities were ascertained using MIC values on yeast and bacteria.32,33

Cell lines and culture conditions

The human breast cancer cell line (MCF-7) and the mouse healthy fibroblast cell line (L929) were obtained from Prof. Dr Ahmet KOÇ (the Department of Medical Biology, Faculty of Medicine, İnönü University Malatya, Turkey). MCF-7 and L929 cells were cultured in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 1% penicillin–streptomycin. The cells were incubated in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2 at 37 °C.34

In vitro cytotoxicity evaluation using the MTT assay

%10 ethyl alcohol and 1% acetic acid were used to prepare the stock solutions of the newly synthesized compounds, and further dilutions were prepared using fresh culture medium (the combined solvent concentration in the final culture medium was less than 0.1%). Cytotoxicity of the compounds and Cis-platin (control) was investigated by using the MTT cell proliferation assay. First, 5 × 106 cells were cultivated in 96-well plates containing 100 µL of medium for 24 h to allow the attachment of cells to surface of the plate. Following this incubation, five different concentrations of the newly synthesized compounds (5, 10, 20, 40, and 80 µg mL−1) were added to the wells to determine their IC50 values. The plates were then incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator for 24, 48, and 72 h. After the designated incubation periods, 20 µL of MTT solution was added into each well, and the plates were further incubated for 2 h. After the supernatant was carefully removed from the plates, 100 µL of DMSO was added into each well, and they were shaken at 150 rpm for 5 min in the dark at room temperature to solubilize the formazan crystals. Then, the homogenized formazan crystals were read at a wavelength of 570 nm using a microplate reader. The IC50 values of the compounds and cisplatin at 24, 48, and 72 h were calculated using the Microsoft Office Excel program. Each concentration was tested in twelve replicates, and the IC50 values (mg mL−1) were determined as the concentration that reduced the absorbance by 50% relative to the untreated control wells.32 Cisplatin was included as a positive control in the assay.

Results and discussion

Characterisation results of the synthesised materials

Solubility and degree of deacetylation

The OCS exhibited a solubility of 91.6% and a degree of deacetylation of 89.7%, confirming the successful conversion of chitin to chitosan. Both parameters are widely regarded as key indicators of chitosan quality, since they strongly influence its physicochemical behavior and potential applications. In particular, chitosan solubility is closely associated with molecular weight, degree of deacetylation (DD), and biological source.35 Solubility in dilute acetic acid, typically at 1% or 2%, is commonly used as an indirect indicator of the extent of deacetylation in the biopolymer.36

The degree of deacetylation reflects the relative proportion of N-acetyl-d-glucosamine and d-glucosamine units in the polymer backbone, and this structural feature directly affects the solubility of chitosan in acidic media.37 A degree of deacetylation above 85% is generally considered favorable for obtaining good chitosan solubility, although the exact value may vary depending on the extraction procedure and precursor source.38 In the present study, the DD value of 89.7% is therefore indicative of efficient deacetylation and is consistent with the high solubility observed.

Previous studies have reported chitosan solubility values of 70%, 60%, and 78% for chitosan derived from shrimp shell, crab, and fish scales, respectively,39 whereas Metin et al.40 reported a solubility of 94.15% for chitosan extracted from blue crab (Callinectes sapidus) shells. The solubility obtained in the present work is therefore comparatively high and suggests that the extracted fungal chitosan possesses favorable physicochemical characteristics. More broadly, materials with a degree of deacetylation above 50% are generally classified as chitosan rather than chitin, and the DD is known to influence crystallinity, ion-exchange capacity, reactivity of amino groups, viscosity, tensile properties, immune activity, and flocculation behavior.41 Taken together, the present solubility and DD values indicate that the extracted material has the characteristic properties expected of well-formed chitosan.

Moisture and ash content

The OCS exhibited a moisture content of 1.1%, indicating low residual water content and suggesting favorable storage stability. Low moisture levels are generally considered a desirable quality attribute in chitosan, as they are associated with improved shelf life and reduced susceptibility to physicochemical deterioration during storage.42,43 Moisture content in chitosan has also been reported to vary depending on the biological source and extraction conditions, which may account for differences observed among studies.36,44

The ash content of the OCS was 1.1%, compared with 0.4% for the commercial chitosan standard (RCS). Ash content is commonly used as an indicator of the efficiency of inorganic residue removal during extraction, with lower values generally reflecting higher material purity.45,46

Although some studies suggest that an ash content of approximately 1% is acceptable for chitosan of good quality,45,47 the slightly higher value observed for the OCS relative to the RCS likely reflects differences in precursor composition and extraction origin. Taken together, these results indicate that the extracted fungal chitosan possessed low moisture content and an ash level within an acceptable range, supporting its suitability for subsequent characterization and nanocomposite synthesis.

Viscosity-average molecular weight

The degree of deacetylation and molecular weight are some of the most important features of polymer in determining its applications. Chitosan's molecular weight directly influences the solubility, viscosity and ultimately the biological activities. Chitosan with lower molecular weights are known to have a better antimicrobial effect.31,48 Tsai et al.,48 observed that 12 kDa low molecular weight chitosan performed better than 228 kDa medium molecular weight chitosan against E. coli, Bacillus cereus, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Staphylococcus aureus, Saccharomyces cerevisiae and Salmonella sp. Though there are reports of medium molecular weight chitosan (194 kDa) possessing better antibacterial effect against Gram-positive microorganism than lower molecular weight chitosan (5–41 kDa), as a result of its greater thickness of the peptidoglycan layer, which prevents the access of the chitooligosaccharides to the cell. Although the peptidoglycan layer prevents the chitooligosaccharides from accessing the cell, the antimicrobial effect of the higher molecular weight chitosan is due to the formation of a chitosan film which inhibit the absorption of nutrients by the microorganism.49 A study by Chang et al.,50 observed that pH has an appreciable influence on chitosan performance. The study reported that chitosan from 300 to 3.3 kDa, molecular weight under acidic pH condition, and recorded increase in activity with an increased molecular weight, regardless of the temperature and the bacteria tested but, at neutral pH, the chitosan activity increased with an increase in molecular weight.

The OCS showed a viscosity-average molecular weight of 858.87 kDa which can be classed as medium molecular weight chitosan following the report of Gonçalves et al.,51 where low, medium and high molecular weight chitosan have molecular weights of <100, 100–1000 and >1000 kDa respectively. Kasaai et al.,52 obtained chitosan molecular weight of 35–2220, while Santoso et al.,53 reported 198.64 kDa from water soluble chitosan obtained from fresh exoskeleton (carapace). The Mark–Houwink–Sakurada equation is not without shortcomings. As a result of the fact that chitosan is known to be very polydisperse, the application of Huggins plot (Fig. 3) and subsequent Mark–Houwink–Sakurada equation does not give a true representation of the average molecular weight of chitosan chain.52 Also, because of the electrostatic repulsion of the charged group which is dependent on the degree of acetylation and as well as the stiffness of chitosan chain, which eventually makes the empirical constants (a and K) vary significantly, the molecular weight obtained may not be the true representation of the chitosan.52,54

The close similarity between the spectra of OCS and RCS confirms the successful extraction of chitin from P. ostreatus and its subsequent deacetylation to chitosan. In particular, the broad absorption band in the 3200–3500 cm−1 region, assigned to overlapping O–H and N–H stretching vibrations, together with the bands corresponding to aliphatic C–H stretching, amide I, amide II and C–O–C stretching, demonstrates that the synthesized material retained the typical polysaccharide structure of chitosan, in agreement with previous reports on fungal-derived chitosan.55–57 The band around 1424 cm−1, attributed to CH3 bending and N–H deformation, further supports the structural integrity and purity of the obtained chitosan.57 These findings are also consistent with the established distinction between chitin and chitosan, since deacetylation reduces the proportion of N-acetyl-d-glucosamine units and increases the relative abundance of amino-containing glucosamine residues.58Fig. 3 and Table 1 also show that incorporation of Ag–Cu nanoparticles into the chitosan matrix caused clear spectral changes in OCS3 relative to OCS, particularly the shift of the amide I band from 1655 to 1642 cm−1 and the amide II band from 1596 to 1582 cm−1, together with reduced band intensity. These changes indicate coordination of Ag and Cu species with the amino and carbonyl functional groups of chitosan, confirming successful formation of the Ag–Cu/chitosan nanocomposite. Such coordination restricts the mobility of the polymer chains and modifies the local chemical environment surrounding these functional groups, thereby enhancing the structural integrity and stability of the nanocomposite. The strong interaction between the metal nanoparticles and the chitosan matrix is also expected to promote a more uniform distribution of nanoparticles and improve the availability of active surface sites, which may contribute to the enhanced antimicrobial performance observed for the nanocomposite. Likewise, the marked reduction in intensity and shift of the C–O–C band from 1072 cm−1 in OCS to 1035 cm−1 in OCS3 suggest that the glycosidic environment of the polymer backbone was altered by nanoparticle incorporation, supporting chemical interaction rather than simple physical entrapment. This modification indicates that the polymer backbone actively participates in stabilizing the nanoparticles, reducing their tendency to aggregate and producing a more robust nanocomposite structure. In contrast, NCS2 lacked the characteristic polysaccharide bands observed in RCS and OCS and instead displayed a distinct peak at 1384 cm−1, which may be associated with residual precursor species or surface-active vibrations of the inorganic phase. Taken together, the FTIR data presented in Fig. 3 and Table 1 confirm both the successful production of fungal chitosan and the formation of a coordinated chitosan–Ag–Cu nanocomposite system.

XRD analysis

The XRD patterns shown in Fig. 5 provide clear evidence of the structural differences among RCS, OCS, OCS3, and NCS2. The OCS exhibited a semi-crystalline structure, characterized by diffraction peaks superimposed on a broad amorphous background, which is consistent with previous reports on fungal-derived chitosan.55,59

XRD patterns of RCS, OCS, OCS3, and NCS2.

As seen in Fig. 5, the RCS displayed the typical broad reflections at approximately 2θ = 10° and 20° whereas OCS retained the major reflection near 20° and additionally showed sharp peaks at 31.7° and 45.4°. These more intense reflections indicate a higher degree of crystallinity in the extracted fungal chitosan (OCS) compared with the commercial reference (RCS), which may be related either to a more ordered molecular arrangement or to trace mineral residues remaining after extraction and demineralization. The average crystallite sizes calculated from the Scherrer equation were 9.49 nm for RCS and 30.60 nm for OCS, further supporting the more ordered crystalline arrangement of the synthesized chitosan. This difference is reasonable because chitosan is known to occur in different polymorphic forms depending on its biological source and extraction method, and such variation directly affects hydrogen-bonding networks and crystallinity.60–63

Fig. 5 also clearly shows that incorporation of Ag–Cu significantly altered the diffraction behavior of both OCS3 and NCS2. Compared with OCS, both samples exhibited changes in peak position and reduced diffraction intensity, indicating structural modification following metal incorporation. In OCS3, the reduced intensity of the chitosan-related reflections suggests partial loss of crystallinity, likely resulting from disruption of the intermolecular hydrogen-bonding network and coordination of Ag and Cu species with the amino and hydroxyl functional groups of chitosan, as reported previously.64,65 This reduction in crystallinity reflects the transformation of portions of the polymer from a highly ordered arrangement to a more disordered structure, thereby increasing the accessibility of functional groups that can interact with surrounding molecules. Such structural modification is beneficial for antimicrobial applications because it facilitates exposure of coordinated metal nanoparticles and promotes their interaction with microbial cell surfaces.

The diffraction peaks of NCS2 were consistent with Ag and Cu phases indexed to ICDD cards 65-2871, 41-1402, and 04-0836, while the OCS3 pattern matched silver- and copper-containing phases assigned to ICDD cards 41-1402, 43-0997, 08-0256, and 03-0879, confirming the successful incorporation of both metals into the composite structure. The presence of these crystalline metallic phases demonstrates that the synthesis method preserved the crystallinity of the Ag–Cu nanoparticles while simultaneously integrating them within the chitosan matrix, providing structurally stable active phases responsible for the functional properties of the nanocomposite. The average crystallite sizes of OCS3 and NCS2 were 22.25 and 23.74 nm, respectively, confirming that both materials remained within the nanoscale range. The relatively small crystallite sizes provide a high surface-area-to-volume ratio, thereby increasing the number of accessible active sites for interaction with bacterial cells and enhancing the surface reactivity of the nanocomposites. Furthermore, the slightly smaller crystallite size observed for OCS3 suggests that the chitosan matrix restricted crystal growth during nanoparticle formation, producing finer and more uniformly dispersed crystallites. Besides, the disappearance of the characteristic chitosan peak in OCS3, together with peak broadening and slight peak shifts visible in Fig. 5, provides evidence of strong interfacial interactions between the Ag–Cu phase and the chitosan matrix, possibly accompanied by lattice distortion or surface-induced stress.66 Peak broadening indicates the formation of small crystallites and the presence of structural disorder, while the slight peak shifts reflect changes in the local crystal environment arising from metal–polymer interactions. These structural changes demonstrate that Ag–Cu nanoparticles were chemically integrated into the chitosan network rather than merely physically mixed, resulting in a nanocomposite with enhanced structural stability and potentially improved antimicrobial performance. Under the alkaline reaction conditions, Ag+ and Cu2+ ions were likely stabilized by the chitosan suspension, facilitating electron transfer and metal ion reduction without visible Ag2O precipitation, and thereby promoting formation of the final chitosan–Ag–Cu composite.67

SEM and EDX analysis

The SEM micrographs presented in Fig. 6 were recorded at magnifications ranging from 500× to 20 000×, while EDX and elemental mapping analyses in Fig. 7 and 8 respectively, were used to determine the elemental composition and surface distribution of the materials.

The OCS exhibited a soft, compact, fibrillar surface with a heterogeneous porous structure, broadly resembling the morphology of the RCS. In addition, OCS showed relatively flat regions together with fragmented or piecemeal areas, resulting in an overall irregular and partially amorphous surface morphology, consistent with previously reported features of chitosan.68 Compared with RCS, the OCS sample appeared more rugged, which is in agreement with its higher crystallinity as indicated by the XRD results. In contrast, NCS2 displayed a distinct morphology composed of flake-like and semi-spherical metallic clusters with a high surface area-to-volume ratio, confirming the effectiveness of the chemical reduction process. The most notable morphological transformation was observed in OCS3, where the chitosan surface was uniformly decorated with finely dispersed Ag–Cu nanoparticles. The absence of large, isolated metallic aggregates suggests strong interfacial interaction and good chemical compatibility between the chitosan matrix and the metal nanoparticles, which are important for the structural stability and potential synergistic antimicrobial performance of the final nanocomposite system.

EDX analysis of the OCS confirmed the presence of the expected biopolymer-associated elements, namely C, N, and O, in varying proportions, as shown in Fig. 7. The peaks around 2.1 (OCS), 2.5 (RCS), 2.2 (OCS3) and 2.2 KeV (NCS2) are the characteristic X-ray emission line of gold (Au) used as coating to prevent build up of static electrical charge from the electron beam and heat damage to the biological component which may result when a biological material (chitosan in this case) come under an intense electron beam.69,70

The elemental composition of OCS was broadly comparable to that of the commercial RCS, indicating that the fungal-derived extraction process yielded chitosan of high purity. In the NCS2 sample, the EDX spectrum confirmed a predominantly metallic composition, with normalized weight percentages of approximately 90.17% Cu and 9.83% Ag, demonstrating effective reduction of the metal precursors. More importantly, the EDX profile of the OCS3 nanocomposite provided clear evidence of successful integration of the biopolymer and metallic phases, as indicated by the simultaneous detection of C, O, Ag, and Cu signals. The relatively high metal loading in OCS3, with Ag and Cu contents of approximately 39.10% and 35.16% by weight, respectively, is likely to contribute to the enhanced antimicrobial performance of the composite. In addition, the marked reduction in the relative intensity of the nitrogen signal in OCS3 compared with OCS suggests substantial coverage of the chitosan surface by metallic nanoparticles, which is consistent with the surface morphology observed in the SEM micrographs.

For the composite materials, EDX and elemental mapping further (Fig. 7 and 8) revealed a homogeneous distribution of the constituent elements across the surface, supporting the successful incorporation of the metallic components within the chitosan matrix rather than simple surface segregation. This observation is consistent with the findings of Boucheta et al.66 who reported a similarly uniform elemental distribution in a chitosan-functionalized Ag–Cu-ferrite nanocomposite developed as a high-performance electrochemical sensor for ascorbic acid detection.

As shown in Fig. 8, EDX elemental mapping revealed that the hemispherical particles, with an average diameter of approximately 30 nm, were homogeneously distributed in both the OCS3 and NCS2. In the OCS3 composite, carbon, nitrogen, and oxygen exhibited a continuous and uniform distribution, representing the chitosan matrix, while silver and copper were also distributed densely and homogeneously throughout the analyzed area. The strong overlap of the Ag and Cu signals with the nitrogen- and oxygen-rich regions provides visual evidence that the metal species were successfully anchored to the functional groups of the chitosan backbone. In addition, the absence of large metallic hotspots indicates that the chitosan matrix effectively limited nanoparticle agglomeration and promoted uniform dispersion. For the NCS2, the elemental maps similarly showed a dense and homogeneous coexistence of Ag and Cu, supporting the formation of a bimetallic phase. The merged elemental maps of both samples further confirmed the structural homogeneity and compositional integrity of the synthesized materials. Such uniform dispersion of Ag–Cu nanoparticles within the chitosan matrix is particularly important, as it provides the structural basis for consistent synergistic antimicrobial activity and more controlled ion release behavior in potential biomedical applications.

The data presented in Table 2 further suggest that chitosan coating influenced the surface charge of the Ag–Cu particles, as evidenced by the increase in zeta potential after chitosan incorporation. This is particularly important because zeta potential is a key parameter governing nanoparticle surface charge, colloidal stability, and interactions with microbial cell surfaces.71 As also reflected in Fig. 9, particles with zeta potential values above +30 mV or below −30 mV are generally considered more stable in suspension, and such enhanced stability may contribute to higher antimicrobial activity by reducing particle agglomeration and preserving a larger active surface area.71,72Fig. 9 also clearly demonstrates the strong pH dependence of the zeta potential, with values gradually shifting from positive to negative as the pH increased from 2 to 12. This trend is consistent with previous reports on chitosan–metal composites and reflects the ionization behavior of surface functional groups, particularly amino groups, which become protonated or deprotonated depending on the pH of the surrounding medium.73–76

Similar pH-responsive behavior was reported by Salazar-Bryam et al.77 who observed increasingly negative zeta potential values and enhanced antimicrobial activity with increasing pH in stabilized silver nanoparticle systems. As indicated by the trend in Fig. 9 changes in pH alter the balance between positive and negative surface charges, thereby affecting electrostatic repulsion, particle aggregation, and dispersion behavior in aqueous media.76,78 When the pH deviates from the isoelectric point, electrostatic repulsion becomes dominant and favors colloidal stabilization, whereas near the isoelectric point, where the zeta potential approaches zero, van der Waals attraction and hydrogen bonding promote aggregation and eventual precipitation.75,79,80 Taken together, the results presented in Fig. 9 and Table 2 indicate that the chitosan-based Ag–Cu nanocomposite possesses a pH-responsive surface charge profile that is likely to play an important role in its colloidal stability and antimicrobial performance.

Another important parameter influencing antimicrobial performance is the particle size distribution (Fig. 10), which was assessed here by hydrodynamic diameter. In general, particles with smaller hydrodynamic diameters, typically within the 10–100 nm range, are expected to show better antimicrobial activity because of their larger effective surface area, improved intracellular uptake, greater membrane penetration, and enhanced colloidal diffusion and stability in liquid media.81,82 Several studies have further shown that hydrodynamic size may be a more relevant predictor of antimicrobial behavior than crystallite size or dry particle size, since it reflects the actual state of the particles in suspension.83–85 As summarized in Table 2, the hydrodynamic diameters of OCS and the OCS3 were 1320 and 1148 d.nm, respectively, with corresponding polydispersity index values of 1.00 and 0.230. The reduction in hydrodynamic diameter after Ag–Cu incorporation suggests a more compact or better-dispersed particle system, whereas the markedly high polydispersity index of chitosan indicates the possible presence of large aggregates or impurities dominating the light scattering signal.

Hydrodynamic diameter distribution graph of OCS, OCS3 and NCS2.

Polydispersity index is a dimensionless parameter describing the width of particle size distribution, with values of 0.05–0.08 indicating monodisperse systems, 0.08–0.7 indicating moderate to narrow size distributions, and values above 0.7 indicating broad size distributions that may not be ideally suited for standard dynamic light scattering analysis.86 Accordingly, the composite sample, with a polydispersity index of 0.230, exhibited a more acceptable and narrower size distribution than chitosan alone. These values are also lower than those reported by Dananjaya et al.67 who found average hydrodynamic sizes of 1776 ± 23 nm for low-molecular-weight chitosan and 240.1 ± 23.6 nm for a chitosan–Ag nanocomposite, suggesting that the present system achieved comparatively smaller and potentially more favorable dispersions for antimicrobial application.

Proposed mechanism of the composite formation

The formation of the chitosan–metal composite is proposed to proceed through coupled reduction and stabilization processes arising from the interaction of hydroxyl and amino functional groups on the biopolymer surface with metal ions, as illustrated in Fig. 11.

Proposed mechanism of the chitosan–metal complex formation.

The chitosan serves multiple roles during this process, acting as a structural matrix, shape-directing agent, reducing agent, stabilizer, and size-controlling medium.87 Through electrostatic attraction, complexation, and coordinated chelation, chitosan binds metal ions and forms a stable biopolymer–metal complex.88 This interaction is facilitated by the ione-pair electrons on the hydroxyl and primary amine groups of the chitosan backbone, which readily coordinate with positively charged metal ions. For metallic nanoparticles in the zero-valent state to form, the coordinated metal ions must subsequently be reduced.89,90 In the present system, chitosan is proposed to participate directly in this reduction step by donating electrons from its –NH2 groups and neighboring –OH groups, thereby converting metal ions into their zero-valent metallic state. For instance, Cu2+ or Ag+ may initially interact with hydroxyl ions to form CuO or Ag2O intermediates, which are then reduced by chitosan-associated hydroxyl groups, accompanied by oxidation of these groups to aldehydes or acids, ultimately yielding Cu0 or Ag0 species.91,92

Another proposed mechanism involves nucleation and growth control, in which the reduced metal atoms aggregate to form stable nuclei. The subsequent growth of these nuclei is regulated by chitosan through a capping mechanism, whereby chitosan molecules adsorb onto the surface of the growing particles and form a protective shell.93 This stabilization is further supported by electrostatic repulsion arising from protonated amino groups –NH3+ and by steric hindrance provided by the bulky chitosan chains, both of which help prevent the nanoparticles from aggregating into larger clusters.94 The final chitosan–metal composite is therefore proposed to consist of metal nanoparticles trapped or embedded within the three-dimensional chitosan network.91,95 In this configuration, the molecular weight of the biopolymer influences the final particle size, while the chitosan matrix acts as a scaffold governing the stability and morphology of the composite.87,91 Stronger interactions, particularly at higher chitosan concentrations, may further restrict precursor attachment and thereby favor the formation of smaller and more uniform composite particles.

The proposed formation mechanism of the chitosan–Ag–Cu bimetallic complex is also consistent with hard and soft acid base theory.96,97 According to this framework, Ag+, as a soft acid, has a strong affinity for the lone-pair electrons on the nitrogen atoms of chitosan amino groups and is therefore expected to coordinate preferentially at these sites. In contrast, Cu2+ which is generally classified as a hard to borderline acid, preferentially interacts with oxygen-containing groups such as hydroxyl functionalities within the chitosan structure. This selective coordination behavior is supported by the distinct shifts and intensity changes observed in the characteristic –NH2 and –OH bands in the FTIR spectra, indicating the participation of both nitrogen- and oxygen-based functional groups in metal binding.

Antimicrobial activity

The increasing resistance of microorganisms to commonly used antibiotics has created an urgent need for the development of new antimicrobial agents. Among the most promising candidates, metallic nanoparticles, particularly silver and copper nanoparticles, have attracted considerable attention because of their broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity. When combined in bimetallic form, Ag–Cu nanoparticles often exhibit enhanced antimicrobial efficacy due to synergistic interactions that improve their effects on microbial cells. Their performance can be further strengthened when incorporated into a natural biopolymeric matrix such as chitosan, which provides structural support, stabilization, and improved dispersion of the metallic phase.98 Chitosan, the most important derivative of chitin, the second most abundant natural polymer, is a biodegradable biopolymer composed mainly of d-glucosamine and N-acetyl-d-glucosamine units.99,100 Fungal chitosan (OCS) extracted from P. ostreatus was used as a natural biopolymeric matrix for loading Ag–Cu bimetallic nanoparticles. The antimicrobial activities of OCS, NCS2 and OCS3 were evaluated against E. coli, P. aeruginosa, E. faecium, S. aureus, and C. albicans. The results showed that OCS alone exhibited negligible antimicrobial activity against the tested microorganisms (Fig. 12).

Determination of MIC values by resazurin-based 96-well plate method.

Chitosan is known to be a cationic biopolymer that can interact with negatively charged microbial surfaces, thereby altering membrane permeability and facilitating nanoparticle contact or penetration into the cells.101,102 However, its intrinsic antimicrobial activity is strongly influenced by factors such as molecular weight, degree of deacetylation, and the susceptibility of the target microorganism.99,103,104 Previous studies have also reported that low-molecular-weight commercial chitosan may show no or only moderate antimicrobial activity against P. aeruginosa and S. aureus, with MIC values of 50 and 100 µg mL−1, respectively.99,102 Therefore, the limited activity observed for OCS alone in the present study is consistent with the known variability in the antimicrobial performance of chitosan.

Ag and Cu nanoparticles are known to differ in both antimicrobial activity and cytotoxicity, and these differences can influence the performance of bimetallic systems.105 Several studies have demonstrated that the coexistence of Ag and Cu can produce a synergistic effect, resulting in improved antimicrobial efficacy compared with the individual metals alone.106 For example, Joshi et al.107 reported that Cu co-loaded with Ag on TNTs was more effective than TNTs loaded with either Ag or Cu separately, while de Videira et al.108 showed that Ag–Cu nanoalloys exhibited higher antimicrobial activity than either AgNPs or CuNPs alone. In addition, the Ag : Cu ratio has been identified as an important factor influencing antimicrobial performance, since variations in this ratio can lead to significant differences in biological activity. In particular, Wang et al.109 demonstrated that porous Ag–Cu nanoalloys with an Ag : Cu ratio of 1 : 1 showed superior antibacterial efficacy against both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria compared with monometallic Ag or Cu nanoparticles. These findings support the view that both the coexistence and relative proportion of Ag and Cu are critical determinants of the antimicrobial behavior of bimetallic nanomaterials.

As can be seen from Table 3 in this study; NCS2 exhibited moderate antimicrobial activity, with MIC values ranging from 125 to 250 µg mL−1, whereas OCS3 showed markedly improved activity, with MIC values between 15.62 and 62.5 µg mL−1. This clear reduction in MIC after incorporation into the fungal chitosan matrix indicates that chitosan loading substantially enhanced the antimicrobial performance of the bimetallic nanoparticles. A previous study reported the MIC value of 128 µg mL−1 for Ag–Cu bimetallic nanoparticles against S. mutans110 which is higher than the lower MIC values observed for the OCS3 in the present work. These findings suggest that fungal chitosan not only serves as an effective carrier for metallic nanoparticles but also contributes to improved dispersion and bioactivity of the composite system. Overall, the chitosan–Ag–Cu nanoparticle composite formulation appears to be a promising antimicrobial material with potential for future medical applications. The control drugs (fluconazole and gentamycin) showed stronger antimicrobial effects in relation to the OCS3 performances.

Table 4 shows the cytotoxic activity of OCS, NCS2 & OCS3 against L929 fibroblasts and MCF-7 breast cancer cells with their calculated selectivity indices (SI). OCS had the most favourable selectivity profile among all investigated formulations. While cytotoxic effects measurable against MCF-7 cells were seen at every exposure time tested, an IC50 value for L929 fibroblasts was unable to be determined within the conditions tested. The ability to remain biologically active while showing no noticeable toxicity toward the non-malignant cell model provides support for fungal chitosan as a candidate compound with anticancer activity that also maintains a relatively safe biological profile. This behavior is noteworthy because selective biological activity remains one of the principal challenges in the development of nanomaterial-based therapeutic systems. Similar observations were reported by Qi et al.111 who observed that both chitosan nanoparticles and copper-loaded chitosan nanoparticles inhibited the proliferation of BEL7402 hepatocellular carcinoma cells while exhibiting negligible toxicity toward normal L-02 liver cells. In that study, IC50 values of 15 and 6 µg mL−1 were reported for chitosan nanoparticles and chitosan–copper nanoparticles, respectively. Yao et al.112 have observed notably that, cytotoxicity of chitosan-based nanoparticles was found to be strongly affected by nanoparticle formulation, including composition, surface modification. These results therefore support the concept that whole fungal chitosan intrinsically has potential biological relevant anticancer properties and also retains low toxicity towards healthy cells.

The response was significantly different after Ag–Cu nanoparticles introduction into chitosan matrix. OCS3 demonstrated the greatest antiproliferative activity against MCF-7 cells, especially during the early phases of exposure (IC50 = 2.69 µg mL−1 after 24 h), which is otherwise notably lower than many previously reported chitosan-based systems and shows how greatly biological activity can be enhanced by silver–copper interaction, though the selectivity significantly decreased with the increase of activity. The IC50 values obtained for MCF-7 and L929 cells remained remarkably similar throughout the incubation period, resulting in SI values close to unity. This behavior indicates that OCS3 affected malignant and non-malignant cells to a comparable extent and therefore lacked meaningful biological discrimination despite its high potency.

The biological model of OCS3 was consistent with previously established mechanisms occurring with Ag- and Cu-containing nanomaterials. Upon internalization in cells, these NPs have potential to participate in production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), interfere with mitochondrial function, induce oxidative stress, damage nucleic acids and proteins and activate apoptosis related signaling pathway. This observation is noted in previous studies like Sanpui et al.113 who have shown that the delivery of Ag+ alone and chitosan-encapsulated Ag-NPs induced apoptosis in cancer cells at low concentrations. Likewise, Gounden et al.114 and Özdamar et al.115 respectively observed increased cytotoxic activity of chitosan-modified silver nanoparticles against breast cancer cell models, and olive leaf extract-loaded chitosan nanoparticles had stronger antiproliferative activity on MCF-7 and A549 cells than free extract. Taken together, these studies suggest that the activated OCS3 activity is due to oxidative stress induced by nanoparticulate material complexes and increased cellular interaction through the chitosan matrix.

Based on a comparison made with OCS3, where NCS2 exhibited an overall more balanced biological profile, NCS2 produced lower IC50 values than OCS3 in MCF-7 cells throughout the incubation period, although its cytotoxic potency was less. The maximum selectivity index, 4.60, was after 48 h and the SI value at 1.86 remained even at 72 h. The findings show that NCS2 had an advantage over cancer cells and was highly proliferating with little anti-proliferative activity. It's also very appealing that it has better potency and specificity, rather than being too toxic to all cells at once. Differential therapy agents that can distinguish cancer from normal tissue have more robust margins for use clinically as well. NCS2 has a similar biological activity to that reported previously for silver and copper-based polysaccharide nanocomposites as anticancer agents. Solairaj et al.116 reported that Ag- and Cu-containing chitin nanocomposites inhibited MCF-7 cell proliferation through mechanisms involving oxidative stress, membrane disruption, depletion of intracellular antioxidant defenses, and apoptosis induction. Similar processes may plausibly contribute to the activity observed here, although direct mechanistic investigations were beyond the scope of the present study. Thus, the information at hand supports this conclusion oxidative stress mediated pathways are significant contributors to the biological effects of NCS2.

One other aspect that can be observed from this study, however, are no trends towards an increase with respect to times. OCS3 had its greatest impact after 24 h instead of after a longer period of time whereas OCS was more active at 24 and 72 h as opposed to 48 h. This is a result of such complexity, which implies that cell reactions depend on many variables instead just duration as well. Changes in nanoparticle aggregation state, temporal variations in metal-ion release, interactions with serum proteins, intracellular trafficking pathways, and adaptive responses of surviving cell populations may all contribute to these fluctuations. Similar formulation-dependent and time-dependent variations have been described for chitosan-based nanomaterials. Thus there is a need for prudence while considering toxicity from time spent being exposed to it. In addition to this, comparing it also shows how selective is needed for a drug candidate evaluation as well. Although cisplatin had a high cytotoxicity to MCF-7 cells, it also showed high toxicity to L929 fibroblasts at an early stage of exposure. From a biochemical point-of-view then it is more about being selective and not so much potent that way. NCS2 seems to be an optimal balance of potency and specificity among the metal-containing compounds while OCS3 is very potent and broadly cytotoxic. The differences highlight how therapeutic activity can't just rely on IC50 measurements, but also take considerations of specificity as well as biology.

In general, the results in Table 4 show that biological performance of these chitosan-based systems is highly structure-dependent. Metallic nanoparticles are not sufficient for explaining these results as well; it is more about what kind (of) nanostructure used, their properties like size/dispersion etc. OCS was the most selective formulation, NCS2 offered the best balance of cytotoxicity and selectivity, and OCS3 had the highest antiproliferative activity. Further research must be made to understand how such disparities are caused by studying processes like reactive oxygen production; mitochondrial membranes' polarization; caspases' activity; apoptotic process's signal transduction; DNA strand breakage response mechanisms etc. These researches will give us a lot of information about how these differences are caused, as well as what can be done with them medically going forward.

Notwithstanding the promising antimicrobial effect of Ag–Cu/fungal chitosan nanocomposite, the potential toxicity associated with metal-based nanocomposites cannot be rebuffed. The toxicological profile of this sort of materials are influenced by a lot of factors such as the particle size, metal ion release, exposure duration and even it dosage. Therefore, comprehensive in vitro and in vivo biocompatibility and cytotoxicity studies are required before considering clinical or biomedical applications.

Conclusion

The rapid emergence of antimicrobial resistance following the introduction of antimicrobial drugs has become a major threat to modern medicine and has intensified the global search for effective and sustainable antimicrobial materials. In this context, the potential of chitosan bimetallic composite was exploited.

The freeze–thaw approach was used for chitin deacetylation, which is an alternating freezing and thawing steps, proved to be an effective strategy for producing fungal chitosan with favorable physicochemical properties. The obtained chitosan (OCS) showed a solubility of 91.6% and a degree of deacetylation of 89.7%, confirming successful chitosan formation and indicating that the applied method produced a highly processable material. These two parameters are particularly important because chitosan solubility is strongly influenced by molecular weight, degree of deacetylation, and biological source, and together they provide key evidence for the quality and functionality of the extracted biopolymer.

The moisture content (1.1%) of the OCS was comparable to values reported in previous studies, further supporting the quality of the extracted material. Low moisture content is generally regarded as a desirable characteristic of chitosan because it is associated with improved shelf stability, although variations in moisture content are known to depend strongly on the biological source of the biopolymer. The viscosity-average molecular weight (858.87 kDa) fell under medium molecular weight polymer while the structural characterization further confirmed the successful formation of chitosan and its metal-containing derivatives. The OCS, RCS, OCS3, and NCS2 all exhibited characteristic functional groups, including N–H, O–H, CO, and C–H, consistent with the expected polysaccharide structure and with literature reports.

XRD analysis showed that the OCS possessed a semi-crystalline structure with diffraction peaks superimposed on an amorphous domain. In the NCS2 sample, the Ag-related peaks were consistent with ICDD cards 65-2871 and 41-1402, while the Cu-related peak corresponded to ICDD card 04-0836. For the OCS3, the observed peaks matched ICDD cards 41-1402 for AgH, 43-0997 for Ag2O, 08-0256 for CuH, and 03-0879 for CuO and Cu2O. The average crystallite sizes obtained for RCS, OCS, OCS3, and NCS2 were 9.49, 30.60, 22.25, and 23.74 nm, respectively.

In addition, SEM analysis showed that the synthesized chitosan had a soft, compact, fibrillar, and heterogeneous porous surface morphology similar to that of the RCS. EDX and elemental mapping further confirmed the presence of the expected biopolymer-associated elements, namely C, N, and O, together with a homogeneous elemental distribution across the composite surface, indicating successful incorporation of the different components within the material. The positive zeta potential values obtained (20.3 to 50.3 mV) for the nanoparticles suggest improved colloidal stability, which is likely to contribute to enhanced antimicrobial performance by limiting particle agglomeration and maintaining effective surface interaction with microbial cells. The OCS3 exhibited significant antimicrobial activity against all tested bacterial and yeast strains. Overall, these findings demonstrate that incorporation of Ag–Cu nanoparticles into fungal chitosan markedly enhances its antimicrobial potential and supports the suitability of fungal chitosan as a promising carrier matrix for metallic nanoparticles with OCS having a remarkable selectivity profile against cancer cells.
