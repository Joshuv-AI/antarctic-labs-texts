Research on a long-haul transmission-line distributed vibration signal denoising method based on dual-fiber sensing and weakly supervised learning
Authors: Ziyi Zhao, Fang Tian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLong-haul distributed fiber-optic vibration monitoring for overhead transmission lines enables continuous sensing along the entire corridor. However, as the sensing distance increases, signal attenuation and the accumulation of environmental/system noise lead to a pronounced degradation in signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), thereby reducing the reliability of feature extraction and condition assessment. To address the difficulty of acquiring “clean-label” samples in field environments and the limited capability of conventional filtering in suppressing in-band noise, this paper proposes a dual-fiber-sensing-based weakly supervised denoising method for long-distance distributed vibration signals. By serially connecting two fibers within the same OPGW, two DAS observations corresponding to the same spatial locations are obtained, and their noise discrepancies are exploited to construct Noise2Noise (N2N) training pairs. Building on this, an N2N-Attention-U-Net network incorporating an attention-gating mechanism is developed to achieve end-to-end adaptive denoising of DAS spatiotemporal blocks. A 60-km field dataset collected from a 220-kV overhead transmission line in Anshan, Liaoning Province, China, is used to conduct comparative experiments against bandpass filtering, Wiener filtering, and the jDAS model, with evaluations performed from multiple perspectives including spatiotemporal maps, frequency–space distributions, time-domain waveforms at representative sensing points, and frequency–time evolution patterns. The results demonstrate that deep-learning-based approaches achieve superior overall performance; in particular, the proposed method substantially reduces the noise floor and stripe-like artifacts while better preserving weak spectral lines and time-varying spectral structures. Further validation on out-of-training-set samples shows that the proposed method maintains the best overall denoising performance with favorable generalization capability and robustness, providing high-fidelity preprocessed data to support subsequent condition recognition in long-distance transmission-line vibration monitoring.Keywords: Distributed acoustic sensing, Overhead transmission line monitoring, Noise2Noise, Deep learning, Weakly supervised denoisingSubject terms: Medical humanities; Science, technology and society

Introduction

Overhead transmission lines are critical infrastructure enabling long-distance power transmission and interregional grid interconnection; their operational safety and supply reliability directly affect grid stability and socioeconomic continuity. Constrained by large geographic spans and complex corridor environments, transmission corridors often traverse regions characterized by high altitude, severe cold, and strong winds, making timely perception of full-line operating conditions and risk early warning essential1–3.

Because most lines are located in outdoor environments, manual inspection suffers from low efficiency, high difficulty, and significant safety risks. Conventional point sensors are limited by restricted coverage, insufficient long-term reliability, and high installation/maintenance costs, and they often require modifications to line components, making them unsuitable for long-distance, full-line online monitoring4,5. Distributed optical fiber sensing can leverage existing fibers in optical ground wire (OPGW) to achieve continuous along-line measurements, offering long-range coverage, immunity to electromagnetic interference, and full spatiotemporal online sensing, thereby providing a viable pathway for transmission-line condition monitoring6–9.

With ongoing performance improvements in distributed acoustic sensing (DAS), it shows broad prospects for monitoring and early warning of icing10, galloping11, sag12, and wind-induced vibration13. However, existing studies have largely been limited to scaled-down models or validation on a small number of spans, and remain insufficient for full-line, engineering-grade applications14–16. A key bottleneck is that long-distance distributed sensing signals attenuate with increasing propagation distance; combined with the cumulative effects of environmental and system noise, this leads to a reduced signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) along the line and degraded robustness of feature extraction and state inversion, thereby limiting algorithm applicability and accuracy at the full-line scale17. Therefore, high-fidelity denoising methods tailored to real operating conditions are urgently needed to enhance SNR while preserving informative components, thereby supporting engineering deployment.

Bandpass/bandstop filtering is a commonly used technique for DAS denoising. It preserves signals within the frequency band of interest while suppressing out-of-band components, but it is generally ineffective at removing random noise that overlaps with the target band18. In real-world transmission lines, noise is often broadband; thus, a relatively wide passband is typically adopted in practice to avoid waveform distortion, which in turn constrains the achievable denoising performance. In addition, the choice of filter type and order entails trade-offs among magnitude-response ripple, transition-band sharpness, and phase response19. Partition-wise parameter tuning across thousands of channels further incurs substantial computational and maintenance costs.

In recent years, deep learning has introduced a new data-driven paradigm for DAS signal denoising. Existing approaches can be broadly categorized into supervised, weakly supervised, and self-supervised learning20–23. Supervised learning relies on paired noisy/clean samples and learns a mapping from noisy observations to clean signals; it has been applied to denoising DAS seismic signals24,25. However, for field OPGW measurements, environmental noise is typically strong and highly stochastic, making it difficult to obtain clean reference labels.Weakly supervised learning, without requiring clean samples, constructs training pairs from noisy observations and suppresses random noise under loss constraints; in practice, its performance can approach or even match that of supervised learning. A representative method is Noise2Noise (N2N), which uses different noisy realizations of the same underlying signal as the input and target for training. Its limitation is that, in some scenarios, it is difficult to obtain multiple independent noisy observations of the same signal26–28. Self-supervised learning, by contrast, does not depend on additional noisy replicas or clean targets; it typically performs denoising via structured masking and reconstruction. For example, the jDAS model based on the J-invariance principle drops a subset of channels during training and learns to inpaint them using information from other channels, thereby achieving denoising. Nevertheless, when the noise level is high and the channel count is extremely large, the performance gains may be limited29,30.

Motivated by these challenges, this paper proposes a weakly supervised denoising method for long-haul transmission-line distributed vibration sensing based on dual-fiber measurements. By serially connecting idle fibers inside the OPGW of an in-service overhead line, two DAS observations at the same spatial locations are obtained, providing a natural pair of noisy replicas for weakly supervised training. Building on this setup, we develop an N2N–Attention–U-Net denoising model, where measurements from one fiber are used as the network input and measurements from the other fiber serve as the training target, enabling effective denoising of long-distance distributed vibration signals. Compared with conventional filtering and existing deep-learning-based denoising strategies, the proposed method achieves superior denoising performance and improved generalization capability.

Experimental site

A long-haul transmission-line vibration monitoring field test was conducted on a 220 kV overhead transmission line in Anshan, Liaoning Province, China, as illustrated in Fig. 1. The line is equipped with an optical ground wire (OPGW). One spare communication fiber within the OPGW was selected as the sensing channel, and the phase variations of the distributed Rayleigh backscattered light along the link were continuously measured based on distributed acoustic sensing (DAS), so as to capture the vibration response of the line.

On-site photograph of the monitored 220 kV transmission line.

The transmission-line vibration monitoring system used in this study is a Φ-OTDR, featuring a sensing range of 60 km, a spatial resolution of 5 m, and a sampling rate of 800 Hz. The interrogator was installed in the substation next to the communication cabinet, and a redundant communication fiber was routed out and connected to enable along-line RBS phase measurements on the OPGW fiber. The field deployment and system architecture are shown in Fig. 2.

F-OTDR deployment site and monitoring principle.

Φ-OTDR extends conventional OTDR by monitoring phase variations in Rayleigh backscattering (Rayleigh backscatter, RBS) within the fiber, thereby enabling dynamic strain sensing31. The system configuration is illustrated in Fig. 2. A narrow-linewidth laser serves as the light source, and its output is split into two paths by a coupler. In the sensing arm, the light is modulated into optical pulses by an acousto-optic modulator (AOM), amplified by an erbium-doped fiber amplifier (EDFA), and then launched into the sensing fiber through an optical circulator. As the pulsed light propagates along the sensing fiber, Rayleigh scattering is generated at each location. When external disturbances act on the fiber, localized strain is induced in the perturbed segment, leading to a corresponding phase change in the Rayleigh backscattered light from that region. A portion of the phase-encoded Rayleigh backscatter propagates backward, is routed by the circulator into a second coupler, and interferes with the reference light that is directly delivered to the same coupler via the reference arm. The mixed optical fields are subsequently split into two outputs and fed into a balanced photodetector. By monitoring variations in the detector output current, the phase changes of the received Rayleigh backscatter can be demodulated. The electrical signal is then acquired by a data acquisition card and transferred to the processing terminal for subsequent signal analysis.

U-Net is a classical encoder–decoder convolutional neural network architecture originally developed for medical image segmentation, whose core objective is to produce pixel-/point-wise predictions at the same resolution as the input. The network exhibits a U-shaped topology consisting of a contracting path (encoder) and an expanding path (decoder): the encoder progressively enlarges the receptive field and extracts high-level semantic representations through stacked convolutions and down-sampling operations, while the decoder gradually restores the spatial resolution via up-sampling and outputs results with the same size as the input. A key design of U-Net is the use of skip connections, which concatenate (or add) the feature maps from the encoder to the corresponding decoder layers at each scale, enabling the decoder to exploit both deep semantic information and shallow fine-grained details during reconstruction, thereby significantly improving localization accuracy and boundary fidelity.

The principle of the proposed N2N-Attention-U-Net model is illustrated in Fig. 3.

Principle of the attention U-Net model.

To enhance the network’s capability in representing target regions and suppress interference from irrelevant background features, an attention gating mechanism (Attention Gate, AG) is introduced into the classical U-Net encoder–decoder framework in this study. The overall architecture still employs a multi-scale down-sampling encoder to extract features and conveys low-level detail information to the decoder via skip connections for fine reconstruction. The difference is that Attention U-Net does not directly fuse encoder and decoder features; instead, an AG is embedded at each skip connection to adaptively reweight and select encoder features. When certain spatiotemporal regions are dominated by noise, their weights are reduced, thereby preventing noise from being directly injected into the reconstruction path through skip connections. Conversely, when continuous structures consistent with true vibrations emerge, the responses of the corresponding regions are strengthened to improve structural fidelity. Combined with the Noise2Noise training objective, the network achieves effective denoising of DAS spatiotemporal blocks without requiring clean labels by minimizing the loss function.

Results analysis

Dataset construction

In this study, the training data were collected using a distributed acoustic sensing (DAS) system deployed at a substation of a 220 kV overhead transmission line in Anshan, Liaoning Province, China. Figure 4 presents the DAS measurements over the entire 60 km sensing distance. As shown, the measured signals exhibit a pronounced mirror-symmetric distribution with respect to the 30 km position. This symmetry arises because two optical fibers inside the OPGW were connected in series, thereby mapping the approximately 30 km physical transmission line onto an equivalent 60 km fiber sensing link and producing a symmetric response along the distance axis. To construct the input–target sample pairs required for N2N training, the distance–time (spatiotemporal) matrix corresponding to the latter 30 km was flipped along the spatial dimension, as illustrated in Fig. 4b. Specifically, the first 30 km segment was used as the model input, while the spatially flipped second 30 km segment served as the training target, enabling weakly supervised paired learning of the same line disturbance response under different noise realizations.

DAS measurement results of OPGW vibrations.

Since the two optical fibers are packaged within the same OPGW cable and deployed along the same transmission line, the line vibration and external disturbances at the same spatial location are coupled into both fibers through the same cable structure. After spatial alignment, the two DAS observations are expected to exhibit high consistency in their dominant spatiotemporal patterns and spectral-line distributions, thereby reflecting the same underlying signal.

Meanwhile, the two channels differ in aspects such as the microscopic distribution of Rayleigh scattering centers and coherent fading characteristics, phase drift, local optical loss, and intra-cable differences in stress coupling to the fiber cores. These factors introduce channel-specific noise floors and stripe-like artifacts, and may also lead to a certain degree of amplitude mismatch and local registration errors.

It should be noted that the key prerequisite for weakly supervised Noise2Noise training is not that the two observations satisfy an ideal i.i.d. condition, but rather that they share the dominant signal while their noise realizations are not fully correlated. Under this setting, the network tends to learn the stable and consistent common structures present in both observations, and suppresses random perturbations that appear only in a single channel or exhibit weak correlation as noise. Therefore, the aforementioned mismatches and partially correlated noise may cause the training conditions to deviate from the ideal case and introduce some bias, but they do not invalidate the effectiveness of using such data as approximate weakly supervised paired samples.

A 24-h continuous online monitoring experiment was conducted on the overhead transmission line. The first 22 h of data were used for model training, and the remaining 2 h were used for testing. The out-of-training samples were selected from an independent time period that was not involved in training, and are therefore temporally separated from the training samples. Since the on-site environment of transmission lines is time-varying, factors such as wind conditions, background disturbance intensity, local mechanical responses, and noise distributions may change across different time periods. This setting can thus, to some extent, evaluate the model’s ability to adapt to temporal distribution shifts and previously unseen vibration patterns.To construct trainable paired samples, the input data and the target data were segmented following a unified rule into array blocks of size 128 × 128. During training, the loss function was used to quantify the discrepancy between the model output fw(·)and the noisy target data, and the network parameters were iteratively updated using the Adam optimizer. The model was trained for 40 epochs, and the learning rate was gradually decayed from 10–3 to 10–5 according to a predefined schedule to balance rapid convergence in the early stage and fine optimization in the later stage.

The training curves of the jDAS model and the proposed model are shown in Fig. 5. It can be seen that the loss of the proposed model decreases to near its minimum by the 10th epoch, indicating a faster convergence speed than the conventional jDAS model.

Comparison of multiple denoising methods

To benchmark the proposed N2N-Attention-U-Net method, we additionally apply a bandpass filter, Wiener filtering, and the jDAS model to the same dataset for denoising, and comparatively analyze the denoising performance of different approaches. The bandpass filter is implemented as a fourth-order Butterworth filter, which is applied to the raw signals at each sensing point with a passband of 0.5–100 Hz. For Wiener filtering, a 11 × 11 local statistics window is adopted. As a classical linear minimum mean-square error (MMSE) estimator, Wiener filtering aims to obtain the optimal linear estimate of the underlying clean signal in the presence of additive noise. In practice, it typically estimates the signal and noise power based on local statistical characteristics and then constructs an adaptive filtering gain to suppress noise and reduce the mean-square error. Meanwhile, the jDAS baseline shares the same network architecture as our method, i.e., an Attention-U-Net, and is trained on the same set of training samples. During training, a random masking operation is applied to the DAS channels of each sample to emulate missing-channel conditions and measurement uncertainty.

To ensure fair comparability with band-pass filtering, Wiener filtering, and jDAS, we conducted preliminary tuning experiments for all baseline methods on this dataset, and determined the final parameter settings under the same data split and consistent evaluation criteria. The selection was made by balancing two factors: (i) the degree of noise-floor suppression during periods without significant disturbances, and (ii) the preservation of representative spectral lines and time-varying spectral structures to avoid over-smoothing.

For band-pass filtering, we compared several candidate combinations of cutoff frequencies and filter orders, and finally selected a 4th-order Butterworth filter with a passband of 0.5–100 Hz, as a compromise between suppressing out-of-band noise and reducing distortion within the target band. For Wiener filtering, we evaluated different local statistical window sizes and ultimately adopted an 11 × 11 window to balance noise suppression and detail preservation. As a deep-learning baseline, jDAS was trained under the same conditions as our method—using the same training data, backbone architecture, number of epochs, optimizer, and learning-rate schedule—while retaining only the difference in its self-supervised masking mechanism. Key hyperparameters such as the masking ratio were selected through preliminary experiments to identify the most stable configuration on this dataset.This procedure ensures that each compared method was tuned on the dataset within a reasonable parameter range, so that the reported results reflect a reasonable or approximately optimal performance level for each method on the considered task.The denoising results for the DAS signals obtained using different methods are shown in Fig. 6.

As shown in Fig. 7b, the frequency–space distribution after bandpass filtering remains highly consistent with the raw result within 0–100 Hz. This is because the bandpass filter primarily suppresses components above 100 Hz and lacks the capability to discriminate noise components that lie in the same frequency band as the informative signal, making it difficult to further reduce the low-frequency noise floor. Overall, the proposed method not only significantly reduces the noise floor, but also better preserves key frequency-domain features, thereby achieving a relatively more favorable balance between noise suppression and effective signal retention..

As shown in Fig. 9a–e, the time–frequency denoising performance of different methods can be visually compared. Figure 9a indicates that the raw signal at this sensing point exhibits a high noise floor over the entire frequency band, superimposed with a stable narrowband component at 60 Hz. After applying the 0–100 Hz bandpass filter (Fig. 9b), the energy in the high-frequency region is truncated as a whole and the contrast of low-frequency components is enhanced to some extent; however, the in-band noise remains pronounced. The Wiener-filtered result (Fig. 9c) effectively suppresses the background noise floor and improves the discernibility of low-frequency textures, yet it shows a stronger tendency toward non-selective attenuation of high-frequency components. In addition to the substantial weakening or even removal of components around the power-line frequency, part of the transient high-frequency energy is simultaneously suppressed, leading to an over-smoothed representation of time-varying details. By contrast, jDAS (Fig. 9d) reduces the noise floor while largely preserving the dominant 60 Hz component and its neighboring structures, suggesting a certain degree of feature fidelity; nevertheless, its overall denoising strength and detail enhancement remain limited. Furthermore, the proposed N2N-Attention-U-Net (Fig. 9e) achieves more pronounced noise suppression while more faithfully retaining the time–frequency evolution of the original signal. For example, the two spike-like features occurring in the 1–2.5 s interval remain clearly distinguishable after applying the proposed method, indicating that the model achieves a relatively more favorable balance between noise suppression and feature fidelity.

Based on the above observations, considering multiple perspectives including spatiotemporal representations, frequency–space distributions, time-domain waveforms at representative sensing points, and frequency–time evolution characteristics, it can be concluded that the bandpass filter yields the most limited denoising performance. Constrained by its fixed-band suppression mechanism, it cannot effectively discriminate noise components that overlap with informative signal components in the same frequency band. Although Wiener filtering can lower the noise floor and highlight low-frequency content, its pronounced non-selective attenuation of high-frequency components results in the weakening or even loss of useful high-frequency vibration features. In contrast, both jDAS and the proposed N2N-Attention-U-Net exhibit substantially improved noise suppression and better structural fidelity, suppressing stripe-like and random noise while retaining the principal time–frequency characteristics. Nevertheless, jDAS remains less effective in preserving subtle yet physically meaningful spectral features, which may be mistakenly treated as noise and suppressed.

This is mainly because the characteristic frequency peaks required for sag monitoring are concentrated within the range of 3–10 Hz, while bandpass filtering cannot effectively suppress noise components that overlap with the useful signals in this frequency band. Therefore, its improvement in sag inversion accuracy is relatively limited.

In comparison, Winner filtering reduces the monitoring errors to some extent, but the improvement remains limited. After denoising with jDAS and N2N, the sag monitoring errors are significantly reduced, indicating that deep-learning-based denoising methods can more effectively enhance the identifiability of key frequency peaks. Among them, the proposed N2N method achieves a more pronounced error reduction, demonstrating that the dual-fiber weakly supervised denoising strategy can effectively improve the accuracy of transmission-line sag monitoring based on distributed vibration signals.

Conclusion

This study targets key practical challenges in long-haul DAS-based vibration monitoring of overhead transmission lines, including the distance-dependent degradation of signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), the difficulty of obtaining clean ground-truth labels, and the limited capability of suppressing in-band noise. To address these issues, we propose a dual-fiber-sensing-based weakly supervised denoising framework. By exploiting the co-located observations enabled by serially connecting two fibers within the OPGW, we construct natural noisy replica pairs, thereby providing a feasible data foundation for Noise2Noise (N2N) training.

At the model level, an Attention Gate mechanism is incorporated into a U-Net encoder–decoder architecture to form the proposed N2N-Attention-U-Net, enabling adaptive feature selection and noise suppression for DAS spatiotemporal blocks. While effectively suppressing random noise, the proposed architecture further enhances the fidelity of continuous structures and weak features that are consistent with true vibration responses.

The comparative experiments show that band-pass filtering mainly suppresses out-of-band components and has limited capability to distinguish noise that overlaps with the effective signal within the same frequency band. Wiener filtering can reduce the noise floor, but it carries the risk of non-selectively attenuating useful mid- and high-frequency components. Both jDAS and the proposed method can significantly improve the clarity of spatiotemporal and time–frequency textures; among them, the proposed method performs better in reducing the noise floor, removing stripe artifacts, and preserving weak spectral lines, resulting in relatively better overall performance.

Generalization tests on out-of-training-set samples further demonstrate that the proposed method maintains stable denoising performance and favorable feature fidelity under unseen operating conditions, indicating stronger generalization capability and providing higher-quality inputs for subsequent vibration-feature extraction, event identification, and condition assessment of transmission lines.
