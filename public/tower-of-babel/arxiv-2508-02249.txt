A Threshold Phenomenon for the Shortest Lattice Vector Problem in the Infinity Norm
Stefan Kuhlmann, Robert Weismantel

Abstract
One important question in the theory of lattices is to detect a shortest vector: given a norm and a lattice, what is the smallest norm attained by a non-zero vector contained in the lattice? We focus on the infinity norm and work with lattices of the form 𝑨 ​ ℤ n \bm{A}\mathbb{Z}^{n} , where 𝑨 \bm{A} has integer entries and is of full column rank. Finding a shortest vector is NP-hard [ 45 ] . We show that this task is fixed parameter tractable in the parameter Δ \Delta , the largest absolute value of the determinant of a full rank submatrix of 𝑨 \bm{A} . The algorithm is based on a structural result that can be interpreted as a threshold phenomenon: whenever the dimension n n exceeds a certain value determined only by Δ \Delta , then a shortest lattice vector attains an infinity norm value of one. This threshold phenomenon has several applications. In particular, it reveals that integer optimal solutions lie on faces of the given polyhedron whose dimensions are bounded only in terms of Δ \Delta .



1
Introduction

A fundamental algorithmic problem in the geometry of numbers with numerous applications to other areas of mathematics and computer science is the shortest lattice vector problem.
This problem is easy to state. Let
\bm{A}\in\mathbb{Z}^{m\times n}
be a matrix of full column rank. The lattice
\bm{A}\mathbb{Z}^{n}
consists of all integral combinations of the columns of
\bm{A}
, i.e.,

\bm{A}\mathbb{Z}^{n}:=\left\{\bm{y}\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}:\bm{y}=\bm{A}\bm{x}\text{ for }\bm{x}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\right\}.

Given such a matrix
\bm{A}\in\mathbb{Z}^{m\times n}
of full column rank and a norm
\|\cdot\|
, a natural question is to determine a non-zero vector in the lattice
\bm{A}\mathbb{Z}^{n}
that attains the smallest norm:
\min_{\bm{z}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\backslash\{\bm{0}\}}\|\bm{A}\bm{z}\|
.
In this paper, the underlying norm is the infinity norm, which is given by
\|\bm{x}\|_{\infty}:=\max\{|x_{1}|,|x_{2}|,\ldots,|x_{n}|\}
for

\bm{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then the shortest vector problem in the infinity norm is as follows

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{z}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\backslash\{\bm{0}\}}\|\bm{A}\bm{z}\|_{\infty}.

(SVP)

It has been shown by van Emde Boas that (
SVP
) is NP-hard
[
45
]
. Hardness and algorithmic results for (
SVP
) and approximate versions of (
SVP
), including in the Euclidean norm, play an important role in lattice-based cryptography; see
[
33
]
for a comprehensive survey on the topic.
Many algorithms for solving the shortest lattice vector problem exactly or approximately have been devised
over the past decades. It is beyond the scope of this paper to discuss the literature in detail. Rather let us refer to several milestones and references on the topic.

It began with the remarkable work of Lenstra, Lenstra, Lovász
[
32
]
, who introduced the notion of a “reduced basis”. One important property of such a basis is that its first vector is approximately a shortest lattice vector with respect to the Euclidean norm. This provides a bound on the length of a shortest vector that can be utilized by an exhaustive search procedure to solve (
SVP
) in the Euclidean norm in running time
2^{\mathcal{O}(n^{3})}
. Kannan
[
27
,
28
]
refined this approach and improved the running time for computing a shortest lattice vector significantly to
2^{\mathcal{O}(n\log n)}
; see also
[
21
,
23
,
24
,
36
]
for further modifications and improvements of Kannan’s algorithm.
The first randomized algorithm to compute a shortest lattice vector in single exponential time in the Euclidean norm was developed by Ajtai, Kumar, Sivakumar
[
5
]
. The authors introduced the technique of “randomized sieving”.
Roughly speaking, their method samples exponentially many lattice points and combines them to generate shorter vectors with positive probability; see
[
18
,
43
]
for excellent surveys on this topic.
The randomized sieving technique has been the subject of intensive investigation. In recent years, it has been modified, generalized, improved and can be applied to arbitrary norms. It gives exact randomized algorithms for (
SVP
) that run in single exponential time and require single exponential space; see
[
14
,
34
,
39
,
42
]
,
[
3
]
for the fastest algorithm in the Infinity norm, and
[
1
,
2
,
4
]
for state-of-the art results in the Euclidean Norm based on “discrete Gaussian sampling”.
Another appealing approach to solve (
SVP
) in the Euclidean norm was proposed by Micciancio and Voulgaris
[
35
]
. Their method is based on the “Voronoi cell” of a lattice. It provides us with the first deterministic single exponential time algorithm for (
SVP
) in the Euclidean norm.

The methods to tackle (
SVP
) discussed so far all measure complexity in terms of the dimension
n
. In this paper, we ask the following: can we say something more when we also fix the parameter
\Delta
defined to be the largest absolute value among all full rank subdeterminants of the given matrix
\bm{A}
? In other words, we fix a constant
\Delta
and work with lattices defined by
\Delta
-modular matrices. This is made formal below.

Definition 1
.

A matrix
\bm{A}\in\mathbb{Z}^{m\times n}
of full column rank is called
\Delta
-modular if
\left|\det\bm{B}\right|\leq\Delta
for all full rank submatrices
\bm{B}
of
\bm{A}
and there exists at least one full rank submatrix
\bm{B}
of
\bm{A}
such that
\left|\det\bm{B}\right|=\Delta
.

It is a major open problem in integer programming whether a linear discrete optimization problem is solvable in polynomial time when the underlying matrix is
\Delta
-modular and
\Delta
is constant; see
[
10
,
11
,
16
,
20
,
30
,
37
,
38
]
for some partial progress in this direction. Inspired by this question, we ask ourselves whether (
SVP
) is solvable in polynomial time when the lattice
\bm{A}\mathbb{Z}^{n}
is determined by a
\Delta
-modular matrix
\bm{A}
and
\Delta
is constant. We are not aware of any FPT algorithm for (
SVP
) parameterized by
\Delta
prior to this work.
Our algorithmic result below is possible because of a threshold phenomenon:
if the dimension
n
is sufficiently large, then for a
\Delta
-modular matrix
\bm{A}
a shortest non-zero lattice vector
\bm{A}\bm{z}
attains the smallest possible value
\|\bm{A}\bm{z}\|_{\infty}=1
.

Theorem 1
.

Let
\bm{A}\in\mathbb{Z}^{m\times n}
be
\Delta
-modular. Suppose that
n\geq\lceil(\Delta-1)/2\rceil\cdot(\Delta-1)+1
. Then there exists
\bm{z}^{*}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\backslash\{\bm{0}\}
such that
\|\bm{A}\bm{z}^{*}\|_{\infty}=1
.

It is open what the correct lower bound on
n
in Theorem
1
should be. To make this more formal, let
\mathcal{M}_{\Delta}
denote the set of
\Delta
-modular matrices with full column rank. We consider the function

\displaystyle f(\Delta):=\max_{\bm{A}\in\mathcal{M}_{\Delta}}\left\{n\in\mathbb{N}:\|\bm{A}\bm{z}\|_{\infty}\geq 2\;\forall\bm{z}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\backslash\{\bm{0}\}\right\}.

(1)

By definition, Theorem
1
remains true whenever
n\geq f(\Delta)+1
.
Hence, the value
f(\Delta)
can be viewed as a threshold dimension. It can also be interpreted as a variant of Minkowski’s convex body theorem parameterized in
\Delta
: Define
\mathcal{Q}:=\{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:-\bm{1}\leq\bm{A}\bm{x}\leq\bm{1}\}
. Whenever
n\geq f(\Delta)+1
, the convex body
\mathcal{Q}
contains a non-zero integer vector, i.e.,
\mathcal{Q}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}\backslash\{\bm{0}\}\neq\emptyset
.

Theorem
1
and the lower bound construction below imply

\displaystyle\Delta-1\leq f(\Delta)\leq\lceil(\Delta-1)/2\rceil\cdot(\Delta-1).

For
\Delta\in\{1,2,3\}
, the upper and lower bound match.

Proposition 1
.

Let
\Delta\geq 2
. There exists a
\Delta
-modular matrix
\bm{A}\in\mathbb{Z}^{\binom{\Delta}{2}\times(\Delta-1)}
such that
\|\bm{A}\bm{z}\|_{\infty}\geq 2
for all
\bm{z}\in\mathbb{Z}^{\Delta-1}\backslash\{\bm{0}\}
.

The proof of Theorem
1
is constructive. It gives rise to an algorithm that computes the shortest non-zero lattice vector in the lattice
\bm{A}\mathbb{Z}^{n}
with respect to the infinity norm,
which runs in polynomial time in
m
and
n
for fixed
\Delta
.

Theorem 2
.

Let
\Delta\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 1}
,
\bm{A}\in\mathbb{Z}^{m\times n}
have full column rank and
n\geq f(\Delta)+1
. Then one can solve (
SVP
) or return a full rank submatrix
\bm{B}
of
\bm{A}
with
\left|\det\bm{B}\right|>\Delta
. This can be done
in
\mathcal{O}(mn^{2}\Delta^{3})
time.

Theorem
2
implies an FPT algorithm for (
SVP
) parameterized by
\Delta
: Given some matrix
\bm{A}\in\mathbb{Z}^{m\times n}
, one can first compute the Hermite normal form in (strongly) polynomial time, cf.
[
29
]
, to verify that
\bm{A}
has full column rank or restrict to a submatrix of full column rank. The next step is to check whether this submatrix has rank at most
f(\Delta)
or not. In the latter case, one runs the algorithm that provides a proof of Theorem
2
. Otherwise, one can use an algorithm that solves (
SVP
) in polynomial time when
n
is a constant. It will become evident in Section
5
that the FPT algorithm uses polynomial space and exponents in the running time stated in Theorem
2
can be improved by incorporating fast algorithms for Gauss-Jordan elimination and matrix multiplication. In fact, Theorem
2
does not require
\Delta
to be constant. It also applies when
\Delta\leq\sqrt{2n}
.

Theorem
1
has applications beyond algorithmic results. It can be used to derive novel results in the theory of integer programming.
However, to apply Theorem
1
, with
f(\Delta)
, to problems in integer programming, requires us to assume that
f(\Delta)
is a monotonously increasing function. It is not clear whether this is true. Hence, we replace
f(\Delta)
by the following canonical monotonously increasing function

\displaystyle F(\Delta):=\max_{i\in[\Delta]}f(i).

(2)

Observe that
\Delta-1\leq F(\Delta)\leq\lceil(\Delta-1)/2\rceil\cdot(\Delta-1)
remains true.
Given a polyhedron
\mathcal{P}=\{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\bm{A}\bm{x}\leq\bm{b}\}
, then Theorem
1
already implies that the vertices of the integer hull of
\mathcal{P}
, lie on a face of
\mathcal{P}
whose dimension is bounded by a function depending solely on
\Delta
.

Theorem 3
.

Let
\bm{A}\in\mathbb{Z}^{m\times n}
be
\Delta
-modular,
\bm{b}\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}
, and
\mathcal{P}=\{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\bm{A}\bm{x}\leq\bm{b}\}
. Then the vertices of the convex hull of
\mathcal{P}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}
lie on faces of
\mathcal{P}
of dimension at most
F(\Delta)
.

If we set
\Delta=1
in Theorem
3
, the matrix
\bm{A}
is unimodular and we recover that vertices of the convex hull of
\mathcal{P}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}
coincide with vertices of
\mathcal{P}
, a fact that follows from a result by Hoffman and Kruskal
[
26
]
. When
\Delta=2
, the vertices of the integer hull lie on faces of dimension zero or one. This also follows from a known result due to Veselov and Chirkov
[
46
]
. For
\Delta\geq 3
, upper bounds of this type were not known previously.
Theorem
3
can be applied to obtain novel upper bounds on the number of non-zero entries of optimal solutions of integer optimization problems in standard form. This topic is discussed below.

2
Sparse Integer Optimal Solutions

The proof of Theorem
3
is based on the threshold phenomenon for the shortest lattice vector problem that is made precise in Theorem
1
. This section is devoted to show that this threshold phenomenon also gives new bounds on the sparsity of integer optimal solutions for optimization problems in standard form. For this to make sense, we assume that
\bm{A}\in\mathbb{Z}^{m\times n}
has full row rank. It is called
\Delta
-modular if
\bm{A}^{\top}
is
\Delta
-modular according to Definition
1
. Given such a matrix
\bm{A}
and
\bm{b}\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}
,
\bm{c}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, an integer optimization problem in standard form is of the form

\max\left\{\bm{c}^{\top}\bm{x}\colon\bm{A}\bm{x}=\bm{b},\,\bm{x}\geq\bm{0},\,\bm{x}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\right\}.

(3)

It is an important question to detect an optimal solution of smallest support, i.e., an optimal solution
\bm{z}^{*}
with the smallest number of strictly positive values among
z^{*}_{1},\ldots,z^{*}_{n}
.

The problem of bounding the support of solutions for integer optimization problems has been studied quite intensively in the past decade. A first bound in terms of
m
and determinants of
\bm{A}
is due to Aliev et al.
[
8
, Theorem 1]
. Their result gives an upper bound on the support of an optimal integer solution of
m+\log_{2}(\sqrt{\det\bm{A}\bm{A}^{\top}})
; see also
[
6
, Theorem 3]
for a refinement of the latter statement.
A problem with these bounds, for our purposes, is that the expression
\det\bm{A}\bm{A}^{\top}
cannot be bounded solely by the parameter
\Delta
, the largest full rank subdeterminant of
\bm{A}
.
For instance, the matrix
\bm{T}\in\mathbb{Z}^{(n-1)\times n^{2}}
obtained from deleting a row of the oriented incidence matrix of a complete graph satisfies
\det\bm{T}\bm{T}^{\top}=n^{n-2}
by Kirchhoff’s matrix tree theorem; see
[
17
, Section 7.2]
. However,
\bm{T}
is totally unimodular and thus there always exist integer solutions with at most
n-1
non-zero entries, compared to the bound
n-1+\log_{2}(n^{(n-2)/2})
.
Nevertheless, using the Cauchy-Binet formula on
\det\bm{A}\bm{A}^{\top}
, bounds in terms of
m
and
\Delta
on the support of an optimal integer solution are available; see
[
31
, Theorem 4]
and
[
22
, Section 3.1]
.
Let us also mention that in the special case when the columns of
\bm{A}
span
\mathbb{R}^{m}
, one can show an upper bound of
2m+\log_{2}(\Delta)

[
6
, Theorem 2]
.

Below, we establish the first upper bound on the support of an optimal integer solution in form of one times
m
plus a function in
\Delta
. More precisely, our bound on the support of an optimal integer solution is
m+F(\Delta)
; see (
2
) for the definition of
F(\Delta)
. Given that there exists an optimal solution to the LP relaxation of (
3
) with support at most
m
, the support bounds for the continuous and the integer optimal solution differ essentially by this threshold value
F(\Delta)
and are independent of
m
.
To the best of our knowledge, prior to the result below, upper bounds of the form
m
plus a function in
\Delta
are only available when
m=1

[
9
, Theorem 1.2]
or in an asymptotic setting
[
40
]
. For
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we define
\supp(\bm{x}):=\{i\in[n]:x_{i}\neq 0\}
.

Theorem 4
.

Let
\bm{A}\in\mathbb{Z}^{m\times n}
have full row rank,
\bm{b}\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}
, and
\bm{c}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
.
If (
3
) has an optimal solution, then there exists an optimal solution
\bm{z}^{*}
such that

\displaystyle\left|\supp(\bm{z}^{*})\right|\leq m+F(\Delta).

Similar to Theorem
3
, Theorem
4
extends known results for
\Delta\in\{1,2\}
to arbitrary values of
\Delta
.
We also provide a first non-trivial lower bound on the number of non-zero entries of integer solutions in terms of
m
and
\Delta
. This bound even holds for totally
\Delta
-modular matrices, i.e., matrices in which
k\times k
submatrices have a determinant bounded by
\Delta
in absolute value for all
k\in[m]
.

Proposition 2
.

Let
\Delta\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 1}
. There exists a totally
\Delta
-modular matrix
\bm{A}\in\mathbb{Z}^{m\times n}
with full row rank and
\bm{b}\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}
such that

\displaystyle\left|\supp(\bm{z}^{*})\right|=m+\Delta-1

for all optimal integer solutions
\bm{z}^{*}
of (
3
) with respect to all
\bm{c}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
.

For the sake of completeness, let us briefly mention that there is also a line of research investigating upper bounds on the support of optimal solutions of (
3
) in terms of
m
and
\|\bm{A}\|_{\infty}
, the largest entry of
\bm{A}
in absolute value. In this regime, a first upper bound is due to Eisenbrand and Shmonin
[
19
, Theorem 1]
, which has been improved to
2m\log_{2}(2\sqrt{m}\|\bm{A}\|_{\infty})
by Aliev et al.
[
8
, Theorem 1]
. This bound is known to be tight, up to the constants
2
in front of
m
and in the logarithm
[
12
,
13
]
. Hence, in contrast to Theorem
4
, bounds of the form
m
plus a function in
\|\bm{A}\|_{\infty}
cannot exist.

3
Proofs of the Upper and Lower Bounds on
f(\Delta)

The proof of Theorem
1
requires us to analyze subdeterminants of
\bm{A}\cdot\bm{B}^{-1}
, where
\bm{A}
has full column rank and
\bm{B}
is some invertible full rank submatrix of
\bm{A}
. This will be accomplished by applying the following lemma.
Throughout the paper, we use the following notation: for a matrix
\bm{A}\in\mathbb{Z}^{m\times n}
and sets
I\subseteq[m]
and
J\subseteq[n]
, we denote by
\bm{A}_{I}
or
\bm{A}_{I,\cdot}
the submatrix consisting of the rows indexed by
I
, by
\bm{A}_{\cdot,J}
the submatrix consisting of the columns indexed by
J
, and by
\bm{A}_{I,J}
the submatrix given by the rows indexed by
I
and columns indexed by
J
.

Lemma 1
.

Let
\bm{A}\in\mathbb{Z}^{m\times n}
have full column rank and
\bm{B}
be an invertible full rank submatrix of
\bm{A}
. Let
I\subseteq[m]
and
J\subseteq[n]
with
|I|=|J|
. Let
\bm{a}_{i_{1}},\ldots,\bm{a}_{i_{|I|}}
be the rows of
\bm{A}
indexed by
I
and
\bm{b}_{j_{1}},\ldots,\bm{b}_{j_{n-|J|}}
the rows of
\bm{B}
not indexed by
J
. Then we have

\displaystyle\left|\det(\bm{A}\cdot\bm{B}^{-1})_{I,J}\right|=\frac{\left|\det(\bm{a}_{i_{1}},\ldots,\bm{a}_{i_{|I|}},\bm{b}_{j_{1}},\ldots,\bm{b}_{j_{n-|J|}})\right|}{\left|\det\bm{B}\right|}.

Proof.

We append the rows
\bm{b}_{j_{1}},\ldots\bm{b}_{j_{n-|J|}}
to
(\bm{A}\cdot\bm{B}^{-1})_{I,J}
and the columns of
\bm{B}^{-1}
that are not indexed by
J
. By definition, we have that
\bm{b}_{j_{k}}^{\top}\bm{B}^{-1}
is a standard unit vector for all
k\in[n-|J|]
. This implies that the new rows are standard unit vector rows. Therefore, by applying Laplace expansion along these rows, we have

\displaystyle\det(\bm{A}\cdot\bm{B}^{-1})_{I,J}

\displaystyle=\pm\det\left((\bm{a}_{i_{1}},\ldots,\bm{a}_{i_{|I|}},\bm{b}_{j_{1}},\ldots,\bm{b}_{j_{n-|J|}})^{\top}\bm{B}^{-1}\right)

\displaystyle=\pm\frac{\det(\bm{a}_{i_{1}},\ldots,\bm{a}_{i_{|I|}},\bm{b}_{j_{1}},\ldots,\bm{b}_{j_{n-|J|}})}{\det\bm{B}}

∎

If we select the invertible submatrix
\bm{B}
with largest determinant in absolute value in Lemma
1
, we obtain the result below.

Corollary 1
.

Let
\bm{A}\in\mathbb{Z}^{m\times n}
be
\Delta
-modular and
\bm{B}
an full rank submatrix of
\bm{A}
with
\left|\det\bm{B}\right|=\Delta
. Then
\bm{A}\cdot\bm{B}^{-1}
has all its subdeterminants bounded by
1
in absolute value. In particular, for any column
\bm{r}
of
\bm{B}^{-1}
, we obtain that
-1\leq\bm{A}\bm{r}\leq 1
.

We only need Corollary
1
to prove Theorem
1
. Lemma
1
will be used in Section
5
.

Proof of Theorem
1
.

Let
\bm{B}
be an
n\times n
submatrix of
\bm{A}
that has determinant
\Delta
. Set
\bm{B}^{-1}=(\bm{r}_{1},\ldots,\bm{r}_{n})
and
\Lambda=\bm{B}^{-1}\mathbb{Z}^{n}
. If we have
\bm{r}_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
for some
i\in[n]
, we get
-1\leq\bm{A}\bm{r}_{i}\leq 1
by Corollary
1
and the claim follows. So suppose that
\bm{r}_{i}\notin\mathbb{Z}^{n}
for all
i\in[n]
. Consider the
2n
vectors
\pm\bm{r}_{1},\ldots,\pm\bm{r}_{n}
. These vectors are contained in
\Lambda\backslash\mathbb{Z}^{n}
. To construct integral vectors, we sort them by their residue classes in
\Lambda/\mathbb{Z}^{n}
. There are
\Delta-1
possible residue classes since
\left|\det\bm{B}\right|=\Delta
and
\bm{r}_{i}\notin\mathbb{Z}^{n}
for all
i\in[n]
. Our lower bound on
n
and the pigeonhole principle guarantee that there exists at least one residue class that contains at least
\Delta
elements. Select the vectors corresponding to these
\Delta
elements. The pigeonhole principle also ensures that we only need to select at most one of
\bm{r}_{i}
and
-\bm{r}_{i}
for all
i\in[n]
. Therefore, after reordering and resigning if necessary, we may assume that
\bm{r}_{1},\ldots,\bm{r}_{\Delta}
are in the same residue class. This implies that

1.

\bm{r}_{i}-\bm{r}_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\backslash\{\bm{0}\}
for all
i,j\in[\Delta]
with
i\neq j
and

2.

\bm{r}_{1}+\ldots+\bm{r}_{\Delta}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\backslash\{\bm{0}\}
.

The vectors above are non-zero because
\bm{B}^{-1}
is invertible and since each vector
\bm{r}_{i}
corresponds to a column of
\bm{B}^{-1}
. We claim that one of them satisfies
-1\leq\bm{A}\bm{x}\leq 1
, which then proves the statement. To show this, we use the fact that

\displaystyle-\bm{1}\leq\bm{A}\bm{r}_{i}\leq\bm{1}

(4)

for all
i\in[\Delta]
, which holds by Corollary
1
.
Fix a pair
i,j\in[\Delta]
with
i\neq j
and consider
\bm{r}_{i}-\bm{r}_{j}
. Suppose
-\bm{1}\leq\bm{A}(\bm{r}_{i}-\bm{r}_{j})\leq\bm{1}
does not hold, i.e., there exists a row
\bm{a}^{\top}
of
\bm{A}
with the property
|\bm{a}^{\top}(\bm{r}_{i}-\bm{r}_{j})|>1
. This implies
|\bm{a}^{\top}(\bm{r}_{i}-\bm{r}_{j})|\geq 2
since
\bm{a},\bm{r}_{i}
, and
\bm{r}_{j}
have integer entries. As
-\bm{1}\leq\bm{A}\bm{r}_{i}\leq\bm{1}
and
-\bm{1}\leq\bm{A}\bm{r}_{j}\leq\bm{1}
by (
4
), we can assume without loss of generality that
\bm{a}^{\top}\bm{r}_{i}=1
and
\bm{a}^{\top}\bm{r}_{j}=-1
. Next we claim that
\bm{a}^{\top}\bm{r}_{k}=0
for all
k\in[\Delta]\backslash\{i,j\}
.
For the purpose of deriving a contradiction, suppose that there exists an index
k
such that
|\bm{a}^{\top}\bm{r}_{k}|>0
. Again, without loss of generality we can assume that
\bm{a}^{\top}\bm{r}_{k}>0
. Next, consider the integer vector
\bm{r}_{i}-\bm{r}_{k}
. Since
\bm{a}^{\top}(\bm{r}_{i}-\bm{r}_{k})=1-\bm{a}^{\top}\bm{r}_{k}
is an integer and
\bm{a}^{\top}\bm{r}_{k}\leq 1
by (
4
), we conclude that
\bm{a}^{\top}(\bm{r}_{i}-\bm{r}_{k})=0
. This holds if and only if
\bm{a}^{\top}\bm{r}_{k}=1
.
Applying the arguments from above to
\bm{r}_{i}-\bm{r}_{k}
, we obtain another row
\tilde{\bm{a}}^{\top}
of
\bm{A}
such that
|\tilde{\bm{a}}^{\top}(\bm{r}_{i}-\bm{r}_{k})|=2
and, without loss of generality,
\tilde{\bm{a}}^{\top}\bm{r}_{i}=1
and
\tilde{\bm{a}}^{\top}\bm{r}_{k}=-1
. However, then the matrix
\bm{A}\cdot\bm{B}^{-1}
contains the submatrix

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\bm{a}^{\top}\bm{r}_{i}&\bm{a}^{\top}\bm{r}_{k}\\
\tilde{\bm{a}}^{\top}\bm{r}_{i}&\tilde{\bm{a}}^{\top}\bm{r}_{k}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
1&-1\end{pmatrix}.

(5)

This submatrix has determinant
2
in absolute value, which contradicts that
\bm{A}\cdot\bm{B}^{-1}
has all subdeterminants bounded by
1
; cf. Corollary
1
.
This shows the claim that
\bm{a}^{\top}\bm{r}_{k}=0
for all
k\in[\Delta]\backslash\{i,j\}
.
In summary, we get, for each pair
i,j\in[\Delta]
with
i\neq j
, a unique row
\bm{a}^{\top}
of
\bm{A}
such that

\begin{array}[]{lll}\bm{a}^{\top}\bm{r}_{k}=0&\text{ for all }&k\in[\Delta]\backslash\{i,j\}\\
|\bm{a}^{\top}(\bm{r}_{k}-\bm{r}_{l})|\geq 2&\iff&k=i\text{ and }l=j\text{ for all }k,l\in[\Delta].\end{array}

(6)

This fact is used as follows:
Consider the integer vectors
\bm{r}_{i}-\bm{r}_{i+1}
for
i\in[\Delta-1]
. Suppose that none of these vectors satisfy
-1\leq\bm{A}\bm{x}\leq 1
. It follows that, for all
i\in[\Delta-1]
, there exists a unique row
\bm{a}_{i}^{\top}
of
\bm{A}
with the properties (
6
). This leads, up to multiplying rows with
-1
, to the submatrix

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\bm{a}_{1}^{\top}\bm{r}_{1}&\dots&\bm{a}_{1}^{\top}\bm{r}_{\Delta}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\bm{a}_{\Delta-1}^{\top}\bm{r}_{1}&\dots&\bm{a}_{\Delta-1}^{\top}\bm{r}_{\Delta}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1&-1&&&\\
&1&-1&&\\
&&\ddots&\ddots&\\
&&&1&-1\end{pmatrix}

of
\bm{A}\cdot\bm{B}^{-1}
.
As a final step, we take the integer vector
\bm{r}_{1}+\ldots+\bm{r}_{\Delta}
into consideration. Select an arbitrary row
\bm{a}^{\top}
of
\bm{A}
. We obtain a
\Delta\times\Delta
submatrix of
\bm{A}\cdot\bm{B}^{-1}
of the form

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\bm{a}^{\top}\bm{r}_{1}&\dots&\bm{a}^{\top}\bm{r}_{\Delta}\\
\bm{a}_{1}^{\top}\bm{r}_{1}&\dots&\bm{a}_{1}^{\top}\bm{r}_{\Delta}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\bm{a}_{\Delta-1}^{\top}\bm{r}_{1}&\dots&\bm{a}_{\Delta-1}^{\top}\bm{r}_{\Delta}\end{pmatrix}=\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}\bm{a}^{\top}\bm{r}_{1}&\dots&\dots&\dots&\bm{a}^{\top}\bm{r}_{\Delta}\\
1&-1&&&\\
&1&-1&&\\
&&\ddots&\ddots&\\
&&&1&-1\end{pmatrix}}_{=:\bm{D}}.

(7)

From Corollary
1
it follows that all subdeterminants of
\bm{A}\cdot\bm{B}^{-1}
are at most
1
in absolute value. This applies in particular to the submatrix
\bm{D}
. We use this fact and Laplace expansion along the row given by
\bm{a}^{\top}
to obtain the relation

\displaystyle 1\geq\left|\det\bm{D}\right|=\left|\bm{a}^{\top}\bm{r}_{1}+\ldots+\bm{a}^{\top}\bm{r}_{\Delta}\right|.

This holds for all rows of
\bm{A}
and hence verifies the statement. ∎

Proof of Proposition
1
.

Let
D:=(V,A)
be the directed graph given by nodes
V:=\{1,\ldots,\Delta\}
and arcs
A:=\{(i,j):i<j\text{ for }i,j\in[\Delta]\}
.
Let
\bm{T}^{\prime}
be the arc-node incidence matrix of
D
. Consider the matrix
\bm{T}
that arises from
\bm{T}^{\prime}
by deleting the last column.
Define the invertible matrix

\displaystyle\bm{B}:=\begin{pmatrix}1&&&\\
&\ddots&&\\
&&1&\\
\Delta-1&\cdots&\Delta-1&\Delta\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{Z}^{(\Delta-1)\times(\Delta-1)}

and let
\bm{A}:=\bm{T}\cdot\bm{B}
. Since
\bm{T}\in\mathbb{Z}^{\binom{\Delta}{2}\times(\Delta-1)}
is totally unimodular and has full column rank, cf.
[
44
, Chapter 19]
, the matrix
\bm{A}
has full column rank as well and is
\Delta
-modular. We claim that
\min_{\bm{z}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\backslash\{\bm{0}\}}\|\bm{A}\bm{z}\|_{\infty}\geq 2
, which proves the result.

Let
\bm{\lambda}=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{\Delta-1})^{\top}\in\mathbb{Z}^{\Delta-1}
and
\bm{z}=\bm{B}^{-1}\bm{\lambda}\in\mathbb{Z}^{\Delta-1}
. We have
\bm{A}\bm{z}=\bm{T}\bm{\lambda}
. Observe that
\bm{T}
contains the unit matrix, which is given by the rows that correspond to the arcs incident to the vertex
\Delta
, whose corresponding column we deleted. Therefore we obtain
\|\bm{A}\bm{z}\|_{\infty}\geq\|\bm{\lambda}\|_{\infty}
. Hence, it suffices to study the case when
\|\bm{\lambda}\|_{\infty}=1
. The definition of
\bm{B}
implies

\displaystyle\bm{B}^{-1}\bm{\lambda}\in\mathbb{Z}^{\Delta-1}\Leftrightarrow\lambda_{1}+\ldots+\lambda_{\Delta-1}\equiv 0\mod\Delta.

So we have
\lambda_{1}+\ldots+\lambda_{\Delta-1}=0
since
\left|\lambda_{1}+\ldots+\lambda_{\Delta-1}\right|\leq\Delta-1
by
\|\bm{\lambda}\|_{\infty}=1
. This shows that there exist two indices
i,j\in[\Delta-1]
such that
\lambda_{i}=1
and
\lambda_{j}=-1
. Then the row
\bm{t}
of
\bm{T}
that corresponds to the arc
(i,j)
gives
\bm{t}^{\top}\bm{\lambda}=\pm 2
, which implies
\|\bm{A}\bm{z}\|_{\infty}=\|\bm{T}\bm{\lambda}\|_{\infty}\geq 2
.
∎

4
Proofs of the Polyhedral Results

We begin with the proof of Theorem
3
, which requires us to apply Theorem
1
to lower-dimensional subspaces. More precisely, we need to investigate vectors satisfying
-\bm{1}\leq\bm{A}\bm{x}\leq\bm{1}
and
\bm{A}_{I}\bm{x}=\bm{0}
for some
I\subseteq[m]
such that the rows of
\bm{A}_{I}
are linearly independent. To work with the appropriate determinants corresponding to such a subspace, we introduce, for a given
I\subseteq[m]
, the refined parameter

\displaystyle\Delta_{I}:=\max\left\{\left|\det\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\bm{A}_{I}\\
\bm{A}_{J}\end{array}\right)\right|:J\subseteq\left[m\right],\;\left|J\right|=n-\left|I\right|\right\}.

We refer the reader to
[
15
, Lemma 1]
or
[
7
, Lemma 6]
for examples on how determinants behave when restricting to subspaces or faces of polyhedra. The key property of
\Delta_{I}
for our purposes is that
\Delta_{I}\leq\Delta
, by definition, and thus
F(\Delta_{I})\leq F(\Delta)
by the monotonicity of
F(\Delta)
.

Proof of Theorem
3
.

We will show the contraposition of the claim, i.e., every integer vector in
\mathcal{P}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}
that is not contained in a face of
\mathcal{P}
with dimension at most
F(\Delta)
is not a vertex of the convex hull of
\mathcal{P}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}
.
Let
\bm{y}\in\mathcal{P}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}
be a vector that does not lie on a face of
\mathcal{P}
of dimension at most
F(\Delta)
. Let
K\subseteq[m]
be the index set for the tight inequalities of
\bm{A}\bm{y}\leq\bm{b}
, that is,
\bm{A}_{K}\bm{y}=\bm{b}_{K}
. Select a set
I\subseteq K
such that the rows of
\bm{A}_{I}
are linearly independent and
\rank\bm{A}_{I}=\rank\bm{A}_{K}
. Since
\bm{y}
does not lie on a face of
\mathcal{P}
of dimension at most
F(\Delta)
, we have
\rank\bm{A}_{I}\leq n-(F(\Delta)+1)
. Consider the face
\mathcal{F}:=\mathcal{P}\cap\{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\bm{A}_{I}\bm{x}=\bm{b}_{I}\}
of
\mathcal{P}
. As
\rank\bm{A}_{I}\leq n-(F(\Delta)+1)
, we obtain
\dim(\mathcal{F})\geq F(\Delta)+1\geq F(\Delta_{I})+1
. Therefore, we can apply Theorem
1
to
-\bm{1}\leq\bm{A}\bm{x}\leq\bm{1}
and
\bm{A}_{I}\bm{x}=\bm{0}
, which gives us
\bm{z}^{*}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\backslash\{\bm{0}\}
such that
-\bm{1}\leq\bm{A}\bm{z}^{*}\leq\bm{1}
and
\bm{A}_{I}\bm{z}^{*}=\bm{0}
. This implies that
\bm{y}\pm\bm{z}^{*}\in\mathcal{F}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}\subseteq\mathcal{P}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}
. We conclude that
1/2\cdot(\bm{y}+\bm{z}^{*})+1/2\cdot(\bm{y}-\bm{z}^{*})=\bm{y}
. Hence,
\bm{y}
is a convex combination of integer vectors in
\mathcal{P}
. This contradicts that
\bm{y}
is a vertex of the integer hull.
∎

Our next goal is to apply Theorem
3
to obtain Theorem
4
.
This involves switching between inequality form and standard form while preserving determinants.
To do so, we use a known result concerning orthogonal lattice bases stated below; see
[
41
, Theorem 4.2]
for a proof and
[
47
]
for an earlier reference, as cited in
[
22
]
.
The result also follows from a classical identity which relates the Plücker coordinates of a Grassmannian to the Plücker coordinates of the dual Grassmannian; cf., for instance,
[
25
, Book III, Chapter XIV, Theorem I]
.
In the statement, we write
\bar{I}:=[n]\backslash I
for the complement of
I\subseteq[n]
. Also, the expression
\gcd\bm{A}
denotes the greatest common divisor of the full rank subdeterminants of
\bm{A}
. Note, for the remainder of this section,
\bm{A}\in\mathbb{Z}^{m\times n}
is a matrix of full row rank instead of full column rank.

Lemma 2
.

Let
\bm{A}\in\mathbb{Z}^{m\times n}
have full row rank. Let
\bm{W}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n\times(n-m)}
have full column rank such that
\bm{A}\cdot\bm{W}=\bm{0}
. Then we have

\displaystyle 1/\gcd\bm{A}\cdot\left|\det\bm{A}_{\cdot,I}\right|=1/\gcd\bm{W}\cdot\left|\det\bm{W}_{\bar{I},\cdot}\right|

for all
I\subseteq[n]
with
|I|=m
.

Let us briefly discuss this result. Denote by
\bm{a}_{1}^{\top},\ldots,\bm{a}_{m}^{\top}
the rows of
\bm{A}
, similarly by
\bm{w}_{1},\ldots,\bm{w}_{n-m}
the columns of
\bm{W}
, and let
L:=\lin\{\bm{a}_{1},\ldots,\bm{a}_{m}\}
be the linear space spanned by
\bm{a}_{1},\ldots,\bm{a}_{m}
. The assumption
\bm{A}\cdot\bm{W}=\bm{0}
in Lemma
2
states that the linear space
\lin\{\bm{w}_{1},\ldots,\bm{w}_{n-m}\}
is orthogonal to
L
, that is,
L^{\perp}=\lin\{\bm{w}_{1},\ldots,\bm{w}_{n-m}\}
. Observe that Lemma
2
applies to all bases of
L^{\perp}
given by integer vectors. This gives us the freedom to choose a suitable basis.
In the following proof, we select
\bm{w}_{1},\ldots,\bm{w}_{n-m}
to be a basis of the lattice
L^{\perp}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, which implies
\gcd\bm{W}=1
and therefore that
\bm{W}
is a
(\Delta/\gcd\bm{A})
-modular matrix provided that
\bm{A}
is
\Delta
-modular.

Proof of Theorem
4
.

Let
\bm{z}^{*}
be a solution of (
3
) such that
\bm{z}^{*}
is a vertex of the integer hull of
\mathcal{S}:=\{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\bm{A}\bm{x}=\bm{b},\bm{x}\geq\bm{0}\}
. Consider the polyhedron
\bm{z}^{*}-\mathcal{S}=\{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\bm{A}\bm{x}=\bm{0},\bm{x}\leq\bm{z}^{*}\}
. Observe that
\bm{0}
is a vertex of the integer hull of
\bm{z}^{*}-\mathcal{S}
as
\bm{z}^{*}
is a vertex of the integer hull of
\mathcal{S}
. As above, let
L
be the linear space spanned by the rows of
\bm{A}
. We apply Lemma
2
: Choose
\bm{W}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n\times(n-m)}
such that the columns of
\bm{W}
form a basis of
L^{\perp}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}
. By Lemma
2
, the matrix
\bm{W}
satisfies
\gcd\bm{W}=1
and is a
(\Delta/\gcd\bm{A})
-modular matrix. We set
\delta:=\Delta/\gcd\bm{A}
.
Consider the polyhedron
\mathcal{P}:=\{\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n-m}:\bm{W}\bm{y}\leq\bm{z}^{*}\}
. The right hand side of
\mathcal{P}
corresponds one-to-one to vectors in
\bm{z}^{*}-\mathcal{S}
. More precisely,
the linear map defined by
\bm{y}\mapsto\bm{W}\bm{y}
is an isomorphism that maps
\mathcal{P}
onto
\mathcal{S}
and its restriction to
\mathbb{Z}^{n-m}
maps one-to-one to
L^{\perp}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}
as
\gcd\bm{W}=1
.
Therefore,
\bm{0}\in\mathcal{P}
is a vertex of the integer hull of
\mathcal{P}
. From Theorem
3
, it follows that
\bm{0}
lies on a face of
\mathcal{P}
of dimension at most
F(\delta)
. So there are at least
(n-m)-F(\delta)
tight inequalities, indexed by elements in
I\subseteq[n]
, such that
\bm{0}=\bm{W}_{I}\bm{0}=\bm{z}^{*}_{I}
. We use this to get

\displaystyle\left|\supp(\bm{z}^{*})\right|\leq n-\left|I\right|=n-\left((n-m)-F(\delta)\right)=m+F(\delta)\leq m+F(\Delta)

as
\delta\leq\Delta
and thus
F(\delta)\leq F(\Delta)
.
∎

It is possible to use Lemma
2
to transfer the construction in Proposition
1
into standard form. However, this requires some minor technical modifications. Hence we present below a concrete example and give an ad-hoc proof that no integer solution with fewer than
m+\Delta-1
non-zero entries exists.

Proof of Proposition
2
.

Let
m=(\Delta-1)^{2}+1
and
n=m+\Delta-1
. By
\bm{I}_{l}
, we denote the
l\times l
unit matrix and
\bm{1}_{l}
is the all-ones vector with
l
entries for
l\in\mathbb{N}
. Consider the following system of linear equations

\displaystyle\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}\bm{I}_{\Delta-1}&\bm{I}_{\Delta-1}&&\\
\bm{T}&&\bm{I}_{m-\Delta}&\\
-\bm{1}_{\Delta-1}^{\top}&&&\Delta\end{pmatrix}}_{=:\bm{A}}\bm{x}=\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}2\cdot\bm{1}_{\Delta-1}\\
\bm{1}_{m-\Delta}\\
1\end{pmatrix}}_{=:\bm{b}},

where
\bm{T}\in\mathbb{Z}^{(m-\Delta)\times(\Delta-1)}
is the node-arc incidence matrix of a complete directed graph with
\Delta-1
nodes. First observe that
\bm{A}\in\mathbb{Z}^{m\times n}
has full row rank as the last
m
columns give an invertible submatrix. Next we claim that the matrix
\bm{A}
is totally
\Delta
-modular:
Since
\bm{T}
is a arc-node incidence matrix, we know that
\bm{T}
is totally unimodular; cf.
[
44
, Chapter 19]
. Adding unit vector as rows and then unit vector columns to
\bm{T}
preserves totally unimodularity. So we obtain that the submatrix
\bm{A}^{\prime}
given by the first
m-1
rows of
\bm{A}
is totally unimodular. Consider an
m\times m
submatrix
\bm{B}
of
\bm{A}
that contains the last column. Applying Laplace expansion along the last column and the fact that
\bm{A}^{\prime}
is totally unimodular tells us that the determinant of
\bm{B}
equals
0
or
\pm\Delta
. Similarly, if
\bm{B}
is an
m\times m
submatrix of
\bm{A}
that does not contain the last column of
\bm{A}
, we can apply Laplace expansion along the last row of
\bm{B}
and obtain that
\left|\det\bm{B}\right|\leq\Delta-1
as
\bm{A}^{\prime}
is totally unimodular. We established that
\bm{A}
is
\Delta
-modular. To see that
\bm{A}
is totally
\Delta
-modular, just append the last unit vector to
\bm{A}
and observe that the new matrix remains
\Delta
-modular. However, since the resulting matrix contains a unit matrix and is
\Delta
-modular, all
k\times k
subdeterminants are bounded by
\Delta
in absolute value for
k\in[m]
.

We continue with showing that no sparse integer-valued solution exists.
The all-ones vector
\bm{1}
is a non-negative integer solution to
\bm{A}\bm{x}=\bm{b}
. For the remainder of this section, we show that there is no other non-negative integer solution, which implies the claim as
\left|\supp(\bm{1})\right|=n=m+\Delta-1
.
Suppose that
\bm{z}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}_{\geq 0}
satisfies
\bm{A}\bm{z}=\bm{b}
. Our first claim is that
z_{n}=1
. Consider the last equation of
\bm{A}\bm{z}=\bm{b}
, which states

\displaystyle\Delta\cdot z_{n}-1=z_{1}+\ldots+z_{\Delta-1}.

If
z_{n}=0
, then the right hand side has to be negative, which is not possible. So we have
z_{n}\geq 1
. If
z_{n}\geq 2
, we get that
2\Delta-1\leq z_{1}+\ldots z_{\Delta-1}
. By averaging, we know that there has to be an index
i\in[\Delta-1]
such that
z_{i}\geq 3
. Consider the
i
-th equation of
\bm{A}\bm{z}=\bm{b}
. This equation tells us

\displaystyle 2=z_{i}+z_{\Delta-1+i}\geq 3+z_{\Delta-1+i},

which implies that
z_{\Delta-1+i}
is negative, a contradiction. So we conclude that
z_{n}=1
. Our next claim is that
z_{i}\geq 1
for all
i\in[\Delta-1]
. Suppose that
z_{i}=0
for some
i\in[\Delta-1]
. Since
\Delta-1=z_{1}+\ldots+z_{\Delta-1}
by the last equation, we get from averaging again that there exists
j\in[\Delta-1]
such that
z_{j}\geq 2
. Consider the equation
z_{j}-z_{i}+z_{k}=1
for some
k\in\{2\Delta-1,\ldots,n-1\}
, which corresponds to the rows of
\bm{A}
that contain the arc-node incidence matrix
\bm{T}
. We get

\displaystyle 1=z_{j}-z_{i}+z_{k}\geq 2+z_{k},

a contradiction to
z_{k}
being non-negative. Hence, we deduce that
z_{i}\geq 1
. This holds for all
i\in[\Delta-1]
. As
\Delta-1=z_{1}+\ldots+z_{\Delta-1}\geq\Delta-1
, we have
z_{i}=1
for all
i\in[\Delta-1]
. Plugging in the value
1
for each
z_{1},\ldots,z_{\Delta-1},z_{n}
and rearranging the corresponding columns to the right hand side in
\bm{A}\bm{z}=\bm{b}
leaves us with the equations
z_{l}=1
for all
l\in\{\Delta,\ldots,n-1\}
. We conclude that
\bm{z}=\bm{1}
is the only integer valued solution for
\bm{A}\bm{x}=\bm{b}
with
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}_{\geq 0}
.
∎

5
An algorithm for the (
SVP
)

This section describes how to turn the proof of Theorem
1
into an algorithm for solving (
SVP
). Recall that we assume
n\geq f(\Delta)+1
throughout this section.

To illustrate the idea, suppose that
\bm{B}
is an invertible full rank submatrix of
\bm{A}
with determinant
\Delta
in absolute value. Let
\bm{B}^{-1}=(\bm{r}_{1},\ldots,\bm{r}_{n})
. If
\bm{r}_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
for some
i\in[n]
, then
\bm{A}\bm{r}_{i}
is a shortest lattice vector by Corollary
1
. Otherwise, there exist
\Delta
columns of
\pm\bm{B}^{-1}
that are contained in the same residue class in
\bm{B}^{-1}\mathbb{Z}^{n}
. Let
\bm{r}_{1},\ldots,\bm{r}_{\Delta}
denote these columns.
Then the proof of Theorem
1
ensures that one of the following test vectors

1.

\bm{r}_{i}-\bm{r}_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\backslash\{\bm{0}\}
for all
i,j\in[\Delta]
with
i\neq j
and

2.

\bm{r}_{1}+\ldots+\bm{r}_{\Delta}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\backslash\{\bm{0}\}

corresponds to a shortest lattice vector. The remaining issue is how to obtain such a submatrix
\bm{B}
? Unfortunately, the task of finding a submatrix
\bm{B}
with largest subdeterminant in polynomial time for fixed
\Delta
is a major open problem.
To circumvent this difficulty, we consider a sequence of invertible submatrices
\bm{B}_{(l)}
and work with
\bm{B}^{-1}_{(l)}=(\bm{r}^{(l)}_{1},\ldots,\bm{r}^{(l)}_{n})
. As above, we generate test vectors of the form:

1.

\bm{r}^{(l)}_{i}-\bm{r}^{(l)}_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\backslash\{\bm{0}\}
for all
i,j\in[\Delta]
with
i\neq j
and

2.

\bm{r}^{(l)}_{1}+\ldots+\bm{r}^{(l)}_{\Delta}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\backslash\{\bm{0}\}
.

The key insight is that either one of these test vectors already corresponds to a shortest lattice vector or they jointly provide a certificate that there exists a full rank submatrix
\bm{B}_{(l+1)}
of
\bm{A}
with
\left|\det\bm{B}_{(l+1)}\right|>\left|\det\bm{B}_{(l)}\right|
. This observation gives rise to an iterative procedure.
This procedure either terminates with a shortest lattice vector or it generates a submatrix whose determinant is larger than
\Delta
in absolute value.
The latter output is a contradiction if we suppose that
\bm{A}
is
\Delta
-modular. Hence, the procedure can be viewed as a partial recognition algorithm for testing whether
\bm{A}
is
\Delta
-modular.

We next introduce some notation for a lighter presentation of the algorithm. Let
n\geq f(\Delta)+1
and
\bm{B}_{(l)}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n\times n}
be an invertible matrix with
\left|\det\bm{B}_{(l)}\right|\leq\Delta
and
\bm{B}^{-1}_{(l)}=(\bm{r}^{(l)}_{1},\ldots,\bm{r}^{(l)}_{n})
such that
\bm{r}^{(l)}_{i}\notin\mathbb{Z}^{n}
for all
i\in[n]
. Let

H_{(l)}\subseteq\pm\{\bm{r}^{(l)}_{1},\ldots,\bm{r}^{(l)}_{n}\}
be a set satisfying

H_{(l)}=\{\bm{h}^{(l)}_{j_{1}},\ldots,\bm{h}^{(l)}_{j_{\Delta}}\}
,
\bm{h}^{(l)}_{j_{i}}-\bm{h}^{(l)}_{j_{k}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, at most
\bm{r}_{i}
or
-\bm{r}_{i}
is in
H_{(l)}
for all
i,k\in[\Delta]
.

Recall from the proof of Theorem
1
that
n\geq f(\Delta)+1
and
\bm{r}^{(l)}_{i}\notin\mathbb{Z}^{n}
ensure the existence of such a set, though, it is in general not unique. Furthermore, let

\displaystyle J_{(l)}:=\left\{j\in[n]:\bm{r}^{(l)}_{j}\in H_{(l)}\text{ or }-\bm{r}^{(l)}_{j}\in H_{(l)}\right\}=\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{\Delta}\}

be the set of indices that correspond to columns of
\pm\bm{B}^{-1}_{(l)}
that are contained in
H_{(l)}
. Observe that
|J_{(l)}|=|H_{(l)}|=\Delta
since
H_{(l)}
contains at most one of
-\bm{r}^{(l)}_{i}
and
\bm{r}^{(l)}_{i}
for all
i\in[n]
.
Finally, the set
H_{(l)}
allows us to define a set of test vectors

\displaystyle T_{(l)}:=\left\{\bm{h}-\bm{h}^{\prime}:\bm{h},\bm{h}^{\prime}\in H_{(l)}\right\}\backslash\{\bm{0}\}\cup\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{\Delta}\bm{h}^{(l)}_{k}\right\}.

Some of the test vectors in
T_{(l)}
are used explicitly during the algorithm in Steps
10
and
11
. We denote them by

\displaystyle\bm{t}_{k}:=\bm{h}^{(l)}_{j_{k}}-\bm{h}^{(l)}_{j_{k+1}}\text{ for }k\in[\Delta-1]\text{ and }\bm{s}:=\sum_{k=1}^{\Delta}\bm{h}^{(l)}_{k}.

Equipped with this notation, the algorithm can be described as follows:

Algorithm 1

Polynomial Time Algorithm for the (
SVP
) when
n\geq f(\Delta)+1

1:

Input:
Full column rank matrix
\bm{A}\in\mathbb{Z}^{m\times n}
,
n\geq f(\Delta)+1
, and
\Delta\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 1}
.

2:

Output:
Either
\bm{y}\in\bm{A}\mathbb{Z}^{n}
such that
\|\bm{y}\|_{\infty}=1
or a full rank submatrix
\bm{B}
of
\bm{A}
with
\left|\det\bm{B}\right|>\Delta
.

3:

Find some invertible full rank submatrix
\tilde{\bm{B}}
of
\bm{A}
. Initialize
l=0
,
\bm{B}_{(0)}:=\tilde{\bm{B}}
.

4:

If
\left|\det\bm{B}_{(l)}\right|>\Delta
, return
\bm{B}_{(l)}
.

5:

Calculate
\bm{B}_{(l)}^{-1}=(\bm{r}^{(l)}_{1},\ldots,\bm{r}^{(l)}_{n})
.

6:

If
|\bm{a}^{\top}_{k}\bm{r}^{(l)}_{j}|>1
for some
k\in[m],j\in[n]
, replace the
j
-th row of
\bm{B}_{(l)}
with
\bm{a}^{\top}_{k}
. Set this matrix to be
\bm{B}_{(l+1)}
, increment
l
and go to
4
.

7:

If
\bm{r}^{(l)}_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
for
j\in[n]
, return
\bm{y}:=\bm{A}\bm{r}^{(l)}_{j}
.

8:

Compute
H_{(l)}
,
J_{(l)}
, and
T_{(l)}
as described above.

9:

If
-\bm{1}\leq\bm{A}\bm{t}\leq 1
for
\bm{t}\in T_{(l)}
, return
\bm{y}:=\bm{A}\bm{t}
.
Otherwise, collect, for every
\bm{t}\in T_{(l)}
a row
\bm{a}_{\bm{t}}^{\top}
of
\bm{A}
such that
|\bm{a}^{\top}_{\bm{t}}\bm{t}|\geq 2
.

10:

If
\bm{a}^{\top}_{\bm{t}_{k}}
does not satisfy (
6
) for
k\in[\Delta-1]
, compute
i\in\{j_{k},j_{k+1}\}
,
j\in J_{(l)}\backslash\{j_{k},j_{k+1}\}
such that
\sign(\bm{a}^{\top}_{\bm{t}_{k}}\bm{h}^{(l)}_{i})=\sign(\bm{a}^{\top}_{\bm{t}_{k}}\bm{h}^{(l)}_{j})
. Replace the rows of
\bm{B}_{(l)}
indexed by
i,j
with the rows
\bm{a}^{\top}_{\bm{t}_{k}}
and
\bm{a}^{\top}_{\bm{h}^{(l)}_{i}-\bm{h}^{(l)}_{j}}
.
Set this matrix to be
\bm{B}_{(l+1)}
, increment
l
and go to
4
.

11:

Replace the rows of
\bm{B}_{(l)}
indexed by
J_{(l)}
with the rows
\bm{a}_{\bm{t}_{1}}^{\top},\ldots,\bm{a}_{\bm{t}_{\Delta-1}}^{\top},\bm{a}^{\top}_{\bm{s}}
. Set this matrix to be
\bm{B}_{(l+1)}
, increment
l
and go to
4
.

Proof of Theorem
2
.

The correctness of Algorithm
1
follows by construction. We show that it terminates after finitely many iterations and give a running time analysis.

Termination:
It suffices to check that we increment index
l
finitely many times. In fact, we will prove that
l\leq\Delta
. To reach a new increment, we have to update the matrix
\bm{B}_{(l)}
. Our claim is that
1+\left|\det\bm{B}_{(l)}\right|\leq\left|\det\bm{B}_{(l+1)}\right|
for all
l\geq 0
. Assuming this holds, we obtain

\displaystyle\left|\det\bm{B}_{(\Delta)}\right|\geq 1+\left|\det\bm{B}_{(\Delta-1)}\right|\geq\ldots\geq\Delta+\left|\det\bm{B}_{(0)}\right|\geq\Delta+1

since
\bm{B}_{(0)}
is invertible and integer-valued. Hence, if
l=\Delta
, the algorithm terminates with a full rank submatrix of
\bm{A}
with determinant larger than
\Delta
in absolute value.
It remains to show that

1+\left|\det\bm{B}_{(l)}\right|\leq\left|\det\bm{B}_{(l+1)}\right|

for all
l\geq 0.

We update the matrix
\bm{B}_{(l)}
in Steps
6
,
10
, and
11
. In each of these steps we consider a submatrix of
\bm{A}\cdot\bm{B}_{(l)}^{-1}
of the form
(\bm{A}\cdot\bm{B}_{(l)}^{-1})_{K,I}
, where
K\subseteq[m]
is the index set of new rows of
\bm{A}
that we add to obtain
\bm{B}_{(l+1)}
and
I\subseteq[n]
is the index set of rows of
\bm{B}_{(l)}
that we replace. In each of the three steps, we claim that
1<|\det(\bm{A}\cdot\bm{B}_{(l)}^{-1})_{K,I}|
holds. Suppose that this is true. Then Lemma
1
implies that

\displaystyle 1<\left|\det(\bm{A}\cdot\bm{B}^{-1}_{(i)})_{K,I}\right|=\frac{\left|\det\bm{B}_{(l+1)}\right|}{\left|\det\bm{B}_{(l)}\right|}

and, therefore,
1+\left|\det\bm{B}_{(l)}\right|\leq\left|\det\bm{B}_{(l+1)}\right|
follows as both determinants are integers.
It remains to verify the inequality

\displaystyle 1<\left|\det(\bm{A}\cdot\bm{B}_{(l)}^{-1})_{K,I}\right|

(8)

for each of Steps
6
,
10
, and
11
.

In Step
6
, we have
|\det(\bm{A}\cdot\bm{B}_{(l)}^{-1})_{K,I}|=|\bm{a}^{\top}_{k}\bm{r}^{(l)}_{j}|>1
by construction, which already settles this case.
Thus, we can assume that
-\bm{1}\leq\bm{A}\bm{r}^{(l)}_{i}\leq\bm{1}
for all
i\in[n]
in Steps
10
and
11
.
In particular, the arguments from the proof of Theorem
1
apply.

Consider Step
10
. As
\bm{a}^{\top}_{\bm{t}_{k}}
does not satisfy (
6
) for some
k\in[\Delta-1]
, there exists an index
j\in J_{(l)}\backslash\{j_{k},j_{k+1}\}
such that
\bm{a}^{\top}_{\bm{t}_{k}}\bm{h}_{j}\neq 0
. As discussed in the proof of Theorem
1
, we may assume that
\bm{a}^{\top}_{\bm{t}_{k}}\bm{h}_{j_{k}}=1
and
\bm{a}^{\top}_{\bm{t}_{k}}\bm{h}_{j_{k+1}}=-1
. Moreover, since
\bm{a}^{\top}_{\bm{t}_{k}}\bm{h}_{j}\neq 0
, we can assume that
\bm{a}^{\top}_{\bm{t}_{k}}\bm{h}_{j}=1
. Then
(\bm{A}\cdot\bm{B}_{(l)}^{-1})_{K,I}
coincides with submatrix (
5
), up to permuting columns, whose determinant is
2
in absolute value. This gives the inequality (
8
) in that case.

Therefore, in Step
11
, we can assume that property (
6
) holds. Then the submatrix
(\bm{A}\cdot\bm{B}_{(l)}^{-1})_{K,I}
corresponds to the matrix presented in (
7
), up to changing rows and columns and multiplying by
-1
. Since
|\bm{a}^{\top}_{\bm{s}}\bm{s}|>1
, the calculations carried out in the proof of Theorem
1
below (
7
) show that (
8
) holds.

Running time:

Step
3
takes
\mathcal{O}(mn^{2})
time using Gauss-Jordan elimination. The next steps, Steps
4
to
7
, can be done in time
\mathcal{O}(mn^{2})
as well using Gauss-Jordan elimination and standard matrix multiplication. Computing the involved sets and checking whether a test vector is a shortest lattice vector in Steps
8
and
9
requires
\mathcal{O}(mn\Delta^{2})
time since
|T_{(l)}|=\mathcal{O}(\Delta^{2})
. In Step
10
, we need to check
\mathcal{O}(\Delta)
rows of
\bm{A}
for property (
6
). The test for property (
6
) takes
\mathcal{O}(\Delta)
time. Once this is accomplished, we need to perform the updates in Step
10
and Step
11
. Together, this can be done in time
\mathcal{O}(\Delta^{2})
. Combining everything and using the fact that
0\leq l\leq\Delta
, the total running time equals

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(mn^{2})+(\Delta+1)\cdot\left(\mathcal{O}(mn^{2})+\mathcal{O}(mn\Delta^{2})+\mathcal{O}(\Delta^{2})\right)=\mathcal{O}(mn^{2}\Delta^{3}),

where we use that
n\leq m
.
∎
