Quantum thermalization and average entropy of a subsystem Journal: Physics Letters A
Smitarani Mishra, Shaon Sahoo

Abstract
Page’s seminal result on the average von Neumann (VN) entropy does not immediately apply to realistic many-body systems which are restricted to physically relevant smaller subspaces. We investigate here the VN entropy averaged over the pure states in the subspace ℋ E \mathcal{H}_{E} corresponding to a narrow energy shell centered at energy E E . We find that the average entropy is S ¯ 1 ≃ ln ⁡ d 1 \overline{S}_{1}\simeq\ln d_{1} , where d 1 d_{1} represents first subsystem’s effective number of states relevant to the energy scale E E . If d E = dim ( ℋ E ) d_{E}=\dim{(\mathcal{H}_{E})} and D D ( D 1 D_{1} ) is the Hilbert space dimension of the full system (subsystem), we estimate that d 1 ≃ D 1 γ d_{1}\simeq D_{1}^{\gamma} , where γ = ln ⁡ ( d E ) / ln ⁡ ( D ) \gamma=\ln(d_E)/\ln(D) for nonintegrable (chaotic) systems and γ < ln ⁡ ( d E ) / ln ⁡ ( D ) \gamma<\ln(d_E)/\ln(D) for integrable systems. This result can be reinterpreted as a volume-law of entropy, where the volume-law coefficient depends on the density-of-states for nonintegrable systems, and remains below the maximal possible value for integrable systems. We numerically analyze a spin model to substantiate our main results.



1
Introduction

Understanding how thermodynamic behavior emerges from the microscopic rules of quantum mechanics remains a central question in statistical physics
[
13
,
6
,
28
]
. One particularly fruitful avenue of investigation concerns the entanglement structure of many-body quantum systems, especially in the context of thermalization
[
14
,
27
]
. When a large quantum system is in a pure state, the entanglement entropy of its small subsystem often mimics the thermodynamic entropy
[
8
,
15
,
17
,
21
,
9
,
26
,
36
]
, suggesting a deep connection between quantum entanglement and statistical mechanics.

The von Neumann (VN) entropy quantifies the bipartite quantum entanglement of a pure state
[
16
]
. This VN entropy of typical pure states provides a key insight into the statistical thermodynamical properties of the quantum systems. For example, the VN entropy of typical eigenstates of quantum many-body Hamiltonians may help us determine whether a system is integrable or chaotic (nonintegrable)
[
4
,
18
]
; this in turn determines how the system thermalizes
[
6
,
23
,
5
]
. In a seminal work, Page first provided an exact formula for the VN entropy of a Haar-random pure state
[
24
]
. Later, this formula was rigorously proved by others
[
32
,
10
,
31
]
. In another important work, Vidmar and coworkers reported the exact bounds for the VN entropy of typical eigenstates (for translationally invariant quadratic fermionic Hamiltonians
[
35
]
). Many important works subsequently reported the VN entropy of typical pure states from different ensembles or of typical eigenstates of physical Hamiltonians
[
4
,
3
,
2
,
11
,
12
]
.

In this work, we investigate the VN entropy of a subsystem when the full quantum system is constrained to a physically relevant subspace corresponding to a narrow energy shell- in other words, when the system resides in a random pure state with a fixed energy scale. Our study is motivated by the following objectives:

1.

To provide a complementary understanding or alternative perspective of quantum thermalization, which is traditionally understood through frameworks such as canonical typicality
[
14
,
27
]
and the Eigenstate Thermalization Hypothesis or ETH
[
6
,
7
,
33
]
.

2.

To explore how the average subsystem entropy scales with the density of states (DOS) of the full system. This result offers a compelling connection between the VN entropy and the thermodynamic (Boltzmann) entropy of an equilibrium quantum system.
To the best of our knowledge, Ref.
[
29
]
provided the first numerical evidence that the average VN entropy over a subspace,
\overline{S}_{1}
, scales logarithmically with the DOS at the corresponding energy, i.e.,
\overline{S}_{1}\propto\ln{(\mathrm{DOS})}
.

3.

To examine the scaling of average VN entropy with subsystem size. It is well established that highly excited states typically obey a volume law for entanglement entropy
[
20
,
1
,
22
,
4
]
. The value of the proportionality coefficient in the volume law can serve as an indicator of the system’s dynamical character - whether the system is nonintegrable (chaotic) or integrable
[
18
]
.

We present a simple analytical model to compute the average von Neumann (VN) entropy associated with a narrow energy shell, focusing on the leading-order contribution and neglecting subleading corrections. As we shall see, this leading term already captures the essential features relevant to the questions addressed in this work. One of our main findings is the following: as mentioned earlier, highly excited states typically obey a volume-law scaling of entanglement entropy. In nonintegrable systems, the coefficient of this volume law is generally constant, whereas in integrable systems, it may depend on the system size
[
18
,
19
]
. However, since we consider typical pure states sampled from a narrow energy shell, instead of full spectrum, this volume-law coefficient is not constant — even for nonintegrable systems. Instead, it depends on the DOS, which is energy-dependent. In the case of integrable systems, the coefficient is non-universal and remains below the maximal value possible.

To substantiate our main result, we also perform numerical calculations on a one-dimensional spin-1/2 chain with next-nearest neighbor interactions.

2
Average entropy at an energy scale

If a quantum system of Hilbert space dimension
D_{1}D_{2}
is in a Haar-random pure state, then the average VN entropy of the subsystem of Hilbert space dimension
D_{1}
is

\mathbb{E}({S}_{1})=\left(\sum_{k=D_{2}+1}^{D_{1}D_{2}}\frac{1}{k}\right)-\frac{D_{1}-1}{2D_{2}}.

(1)

This general result was first conjectured by Page
[
24
]
and later proven by others
[
32
,
10
,
31
]
. In the thermodynamic limit (with
1\ll D_{1}\ll D_{2}
), it becomes

\mathbb{E}({S}_{1})\simeq\ln(D_1).

(2)

Here we outline a method for calculating the average VN entropy (main term) corresponding to a subspace associated with an energy scale. We argue that, in this case, a result analogous to Eq.
2
can be obtained.

2.1
Average entropy in terms of a parameter
d_{1}

Consider the subspace spanned by the eigenkets of the full system corresponding to a narrow energy shell:
\mathcal{H}_{E}=\mathrm{Span}\left\{\ket{n}:E_{n}\in(E+\frac{\Delta E}{2},E-\frac{\Delta E}{2}]\right\}
, where
E_{n}
is the eigenvalue corresponding to the eigenket
\ket{n}
of the full system. The shell width
\Delta E
, is arbitrary, but is macroscopically small and microscopically large. A general state in the subspace can be written as
\ket{\psi}=\sum^{\prime}_{n}c_{n}\ket{n}
with
\sum^{\prime}_{n}|c_{n}|^{2}=1
. Here
\sum^{\prime}_{n}
denotes a summation over
d_{E}
energy eigenkets in
\mathcal{H}_{E}
, where
d_{E}=\dim{(\mathcal{H}_{E})}
. The reduced density matrix for the subsystem 1 is

\rho_{1}=\mathrm{Tr}_{2}(\ket{\psi}\bra{\psi})={\sum_{n,m}}^{\prime}c_{n}c^{*}_{m}\mathrm{Tr}_{2}(\ket{n}\bra{m}),

(3)

where the trace is taken with respect to a basis of the subsystem 2. When the full system is in pure state
\ket{\psi}
, the VN entropy of the subsystem 1 is given by

S_{1}(\rho_{1})=-\mathrm{Tr}_{1}(\rho_{1}\ln{\rho_1}),

(4)

where the trace is taken with respect to a basis of the subsystem 1. To obtain
\overline{S}_{1}
, i.e., the average VN entropy corresponding to the subspace
\mathcal{H}_{E}
, we need to consider all possible states
\ket{\psi}
and associated reduced density matrices
\rho_{1}
(see Eq.
3
). This, in principle, can be done by integrating
S_{1}(\rho_{1})
over the surface of the
2d_{E}
-dimensional unit sphere in the coefficient space (there are
d_{E}
coefficients
c_{n}
, each having real and imaginary parts). If
dK
is the surface element on the unit sphere, we have:

\overline{S}_{1}=\frac{\int S_{1}(\rho_{1})dK}{\int dK}.

(5)

Performing this calculation can be challenging. One can somewhat simplify this calculation by noting that the leading term in the average VN entropy when averaged over random pure states and when averaged over energy eigenstates are the same
[
24
,
35
]
. This suggests that, for calculating the leading term, we can replace Eq.
5
by the following average:

\overline{S}_{1}\simeq\frac{1}{d_{E}}{\sum_{n}}^{\prime}S_{1}(\rho_{1}^{(n)}),

(6)

where
\rho_{1}^{(n)}=\mathrm{Tr}_{2}(\ket{n}\bra{n})
. We recall that
d_{E}=\dim{(\mathcal{H}_{E})}
. Evaluating even Eq.
6
is not straightforward. Since
S_{1}\leq\ln{(D_1)}
, we can in general write Eq.
6
as

\overline{S}_{1}\simeq\ln d_{1},

(7)

where
d_{1}
is some parameter and, clearly,
d_{1}\leq D_{1}
. Primary objective of our study is to find the value of
d_{1}
.

2.2
Physical interpretation of the parameter
d_{1}

To obtain a physical interpretation of
d_{1}
, we note the following. When we randomly choose pure states (of the full system) without restriction on the energy scale
E
, then all the energy scales associated with subsystem 1 are important. Accordingly, all states (
D_{1}
in number) of subsystem 1 would contribute to its average VN entropy. This is an intuitive way to understand the result in Eq.
2
.
When attention is restricted to the pure states in the subspace
\mathcal{H}_{E}
associated with a narrow energy shell centered at energy
E
, all the states of subsystem 1 are no more equally significant. The parameter
d_{1}
in Eq.
7
can be interpreted as the effective number of significant (orthonormal) states of subsystem 1 when the full system is restricted to the subspace
\mathcal{H}_{E}
. We can also view
d_{1}
as the dimension of the first subsystem’s effective Hilbert space associated with the subspace
\mathcal{H}_{E}
.

To better understand
d_{1}
, we consider a thermodynamically large system with
1\ll l_{1},l_{2}
, where
l_{1}
(
l_{2}
) is the size of subsystem 1 (subsystem 2). Here, the size of a subsystem can simply be taken as its number of sites. In the thermodynamic limit, we ignore the interaction energy between the subsystems to calculate the leading term in
\overline{S}_{1}
. This can be justified when the system is large and the Hamiltonian is local (i.e., interactions are local). It is expected that, for the large system considered here, the states of subsystem 1 corresponding to the energy scale
\overline{E}_{1}=\frac{l_{1}}{l_{1}+l_{2}}E
would be the most important. Thus the parameter
d_{1}
represents the effective number of (orthonormal) states of subsystem 1 corresponding to its energy scale
\overline{E}_{1}
.

2.3
Understanding subsystem energy scale

It is possible to intuitively understand why the states of subsystem 1 corresponding to the energy scale
\overline{E}_{1}=\frac{l_{1}}{l_{1}+l_{2}}E
would be the most important when the full system is at the energy scale
E
(fixed). In the following, we first explain this (semi-) classically, then, elucidate it quantum mechanically using the perturbation theory.

If the full system is restricted in the energy shell centered at energy
E
(fixed), then
E_{1}+E_{2}=E
. Here
E_{1}
(
E_{2}
) is the energy of the subsystem 1 (subsystem 2). We note that, although the energy of the full system is almost fixed, the energy of a subsystem is not. For example, for the subsystem 1,
0<E_{1}<E
. To know the most probable value of
E_{1}
, one needs to look at the probability density function or PDF corresponding to subsystem 1. If
p_{1}(E_{1})
denotes the PDF, it can be shown that
[
25
]
:

p_{1}(E_{1})\propto e^{-\frac{(E_{1}-U_{1})^{2}}{2k_{B}T^{2}C_{1}}},

(8)

where
k_{B}
is the Boltzmann constant,
T
is the equilibrium temperature of the system and
C_{1}
is the specific heat at constant volume (for the subsystem 1). In the expression of
p_{1}(E_{1})
,
U_{1}
is the thermodynamic internal energy of subsystem 1. Since the energy of the full system is approximately
E
, its thermodynamic internal energy would be
U\simeq E
. Since
U
is an extensive quantity, the internal energy corresponding to subsystem 1 would be
U_{1}=\frac{l_{1}}{l_{1}+l_{2}}U\simeq\overline{E}_{1}
(here, we ignore the interaction energy between the subsystems). It is clear from Eq.
8
that, the PDF is a Gaussian distribution centered at
E_{1}=U_{1}
. If the size of subsystem 1 is large, then the peak will be very sharp (similar to the delta function). This result explains why it is expected that the states of subsystem 1 corresponding to the energy scale
\overline{E}_{1}
would be the most significant in the thermodynamic limit.

To understand the energy scale of subsystem 1 using the perturbation theory, let us consider that the total Hamiltonian of the full system is
H_{T}=H_{1}+H_{2}+H_{I}
, where
H_{1}
(
H_{2}
) is the Hamiltonian of the subsystem 1 (subsystem 2), and
H_{I}
represents the interactions between the subsystems. Let
\ket{1,n}
(
\ket{2,m}
) be an eigenket of
H_{1}
(
H_{2}
) with an eigenvalue
E_{1,n}
(
E_{2,m}
). Without the interaction term, the tensor product of these kets will be an eigenket of
H_{T}
. For further discussion, we adopt the following notation:
E_{n,m}=E_{1,n}+E_{2,m}
and
\ket{n,m}=\ket{1,n}\otimes\ket{2,m}
. Consider now the following set of tensor product states:
\mathcal{T}_{E}=\left\{\ket{n,m}:E_{n,m}\in(E+\frac{\Delta E}{2},E-\frac{\Delta E}{2}]\right\}
. All the kets in
\mathcal{T}_{E}
are zeroth-order eigenkets of
H_{T}
. One can use it as a basis to represent any state of the full system at the energy scale
E
. Using perturbation theory, one gets the following eigen energies and eigenkets with first order corrections
[
30
]
:

\begin{split}\widetilde{E}_{n,m}&=E_{n,m}+\bra{n,m}H_{I}\ket{n,m}\\
\widetilde{\ket{n,m}}&=\ket{n,m}+{\sum_{k,l}}^{\prime}\frac{\bra{k,l}H_{I}\ket{n,m}}{E_{n,m}-E_{k,l}}\ket{k,l},\end{split}

(9)

where the summation
\sum^{\prime}_{k,l}
is over all the states in
\mathcal{T}_{E}
except
\ket{n,m}
. Of-course, this result makes sense only when
|\bra{k,l}H_{I}\ket{n,m}|\ll|E_{n,m}-E_{k,l}|
. Now to see why the energy scale
\overline{E}_{1}
is important for Subsystem 1, we rewrite the denominator in Eq.
9
. If we take the unperturbed energy
E_{n,m}\sim\overline{E}_{1}+\overline{E}_{2}
, then the denominator can be written as
(\overline{E}_{1}-E_{1,k})+(\overline{E}_{2}-E_{2,l})
. It is now not difficult to see that a state
\ket{1,k}
of subsystem 1 will be insignificant for our problem if
|\overline{E}_{1}-E_{1,k}|
is large in general.

2.4
Estimation of
d_{1}
when
l_{1}\sim l_{2}

Here we estimate the parameter
d_{1}
assuming that the sizes of the subsystems are of the same order, that is,
l_{1}\sim l_{2}
. For a thermodynamically large system, the average VN entropy and the VN entropy of a typical pure state are the same in the leading order
[
2
]
. Thus we estimate the average entropy by calculating the entropy of a typical pure state. In the following, we first re-look in to Eq.
7
as the VN entropy of a typical state.

As mentioned in the previous subsection, any state of the full system at the energy scale
E
can be represented by a linear combination of kets in
\mathcal{T}_{E}
. Accordingly, we write a typical state at energy scale
E
in the following way:

\ket{\psi}={\sum_{n,m}}^{\prime}c_{n,m}\ket{n,m},

(10)

where the summation
\sum^{\prime}_{n,m}
is over tensor product states in
\mathcal{T}_{E}
. Here
c_{n,m}
are random numbers with the usual normalization constraint:
\sum^{\prime}_{n,m}|c_{n,m}|^{2}=1
. The elements of the reduced density matrix of subsystem 1 are given by:

(\rho_{1})_{k,l}=\sum_{m=1}^{d_{2}}c^{*}_{k,m}c_{l,m},

(11)

where
d_{2}
is the number of (orthonormal) kets of subsystem 2 at the energy scale
\overline{E}_{2}=\frac{l_{2}}{l_{1}+l_{2}}E
. If we take the random numbers
c_{n,m}
to be of the same order, i.e.,
|c_{n,m}|\sim\frac{1}{\sqrt{d_{1}d_{2}}}
, then all the diagonal elements of
\rho_{1}
would be of the order
(\rho_{1})_{i,i}\sim\frac{1}{d_{1}}
. The off-diagonal elements of
\rho_{1}
will be vanishingly small in the thermodynamic limit. This shows that the VN entropy of a typical state at the given energy scale
E
is
S_{1}\simeq\ln{d_1}
, similar to Eq.
7
.

We now note that if
d_{1}
(
d_{2}
) is the number of (orthonormal) states of the subsystem 1 (subsystem 2) at the energy scale
\overline{E}_{1}
(
\overline{E}_{2}
), and if
d_{E}
is the number of eigenstates of the full system at the energy scale
E
, then

d_{1}d_{2}\simeq d_{E}.

(12)

This relation is supposed to work for nonintegrable (chaotic) systems. For integrable systems, due to additional constraints on the system, one would get
d_{1}d_{2}<d_{E}
, since the full system would not be able to access the full subspace
\mathcal{H}_{E}
. When
l_{1}\sim l_{2}
, it is expected that
d_{1}\sim d_{2}
. Hence,
d_{1}^{2}\simeq d_{E}
. This yields from Eq.
7
,
\overline{S}_{1}\simeq\frac{1}{2}\ln{d_E}
for nonintegrable system. Since
d_{E}\propto
DOS, the leading term in the average VN entropy becomes:

\overline{S}_{1}\simeq\frac{1}{2}\ln{(\textrm{DOS})}.

(13)

For integrable system, the the average VN entropy would be less that the maximum possible value:
\overline{S}_{1}<\frac{1}{2}\ln{(\textrm{DOS})}
.

2.5
Estimation of
d_{1}
when
l_{1}\ll l_{2}

Discussion in the previous subsection reveals how the average VN entropy scales with the DOS, but does not tell us how it scales with the subsystem size (
l_{1}
). For this we consider the limit where the subsystem sizes are quite different. In the following, we consider the thermodynamic limit with
l_{1}\ll l_{2}
.

The Schmidt decomposition of the typical state (Eq.
10
) shows that the VN entropy of this state depends on
d_{1}
, since
d_{1}\ll d_{2}
following our assumption that
l_{1}\ll l_{2}
. Therefore, in the current case also
\overline{S}_{1}\simeq\ln{d_1}
. We now discuss a way to estimate
d_{1}
.

S_{1}
) for the individual eigenkets and

N=16
and subsystem size
l_{1}=6
.

We now consider a parameter
\eta
that may be called the effective Hilbert space dimension associated with a single site. The parameter is taken in such a way that
\eta^{l_{1}}=d_{1}
and
\eta^{l_{2}}=d_{2}
. This is similar to
d^{l_{1}}=D_{1}
and
d^{l_{2}}=D_{2}
where
d
is the Hilber space dimension of a single spin (for spin-1/2 system,
d=2
). This shows that

\frac{\ln d_{1}}{\ln d_{2}}=\frac{\ln D_{1}}{\ln D_{2}}.

(14)

We here assume that the Eq.
12
is valid for nonintegrable systems even when
l_{1}\ll l_{2}
.
Now starting from Eq.
12
, and using Eq.
14
, we get,

\ln d_{E}\simeq\ln d_{1}(1+\frac{\ln d_{2}}{\ln d_{1}})=\ln d_{1}(1+\frac{\ln D_{2}}{\ln D_{1}})=\ln d_{1}\frac{\ln D}{\ln D_{1}}.

(15)

We recall that
D_{1}D_{2}=D
, the dimension of the Hilbert space of the full system. Rearranging the terms, we get:

\ln d_{1}\simeq\ln D_{1}\left(\frac{\ln d_{E}}{\ln D}\right).

(16)

This equation can be recast as:

d_{1}\simeq D_{1}^{\gamma},

(17)

with
\gamma=\frac{\ln d_{E}}{\ln D}
for nonintegrable (chaotic) systems.
This is the expression of
d_{1}
that appears in Eq.
7
. For the integrable system,
d_{1}d_{2}<d_{E}
, as noted earlier. It is easy to check that, even for this case, one can write
d_{1}=D_{1}^{\gamma}
, but now
\gamma<\frac{\ln d_{E}}{\ln D}
. It is interesting to note here that, as
\gamma\to 1
, we recover the result of Page as appear in Eq.
2
.

3
Numerical verifications

To numerically verify the result presented in Eq.
7
, with
d_{1}
given in Eq.
17
, we rearrange the terms and express the average VN entropy in terms of the DOS of the full system and the size of the subsystem 1.
Starting from Eq.
7
, we get:

\overline{S}_{1}\simeq\ln d_{1}=\left(\frac{\ln d_{E}}{\ln D}\right)\ln D_{1}.

(18)

Since
\frac{\ln D_{1}}{\ln D}=\frac{l_{1}}{l_{1}+l_{2}}
, we obtain the following leading term in the average entropy:

\overline{S}_{1}\simeq\ln d_{1}\simeq l_{1}\left(\frac{\ln\textrm{(DOS)}}{l_{1}+l_{2}}\right),

(19)

where again we used the fact that
d_{E}\propto\textrm{DOS}
. For integrable system, one can again argue that the average VN entropy less than the maximum value presented in Eq.
19
.

We see from the above equation that, for a large system of fixed size
l_{1}+l_{2}
,
\overline{S}_{1}\propto\ln\textrm{(DOS)}
for given subsystem size
l_{1}
, and
\overline{S}_{1}\propto l_{1}
for given energy (or DOS). The second relation is simply the volume-law of entropy; the coefficient is now not constant but depends on DOS (or the energy
E
). For a spin model discussed below, we numerically test our results discussed above.

For numerical calculations, we take a spin-1/2 one-dimensional system with next-nearest neighbor interactions, as described by the following Hamiltonian:

H=\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\vec{S}_{i}\cdot\vec{S}_{i+1}+\Delta_{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N-2}S^{z}_{i}S^{z}_{i+2}.

(20)

This model system is integrable when
\Delta_{2}=0
and is nonintegrable when
\Delta_{2}>0

]
. In our calculations, we take a 16-site system as the full system (open chain).

First, we plot the VN entropy (
S_{1}
) of the individual eigenkets of the full system for

= 0.0, 0.2 and 0.5. We take the 6-site left-end part of the full system as subsystem 1. The result can be found in Fig.

\overline{S}_{1}
over a small energy interval and plot it against

, where this plot is shown separately for the left and the right halves of the spectrum. The linearity of these plots verifies that
\overline{S}_{1}\propto\ln{\textrm{(DOS)}}
for given subsystem size
l_{1}
(see Eq.

). We also note from the figure that the slope is largest for the nonintegrable case (
\Delta_{2}=0.5
) but is smaller than that for the integrable case (
\Delta_{2}=0.0
), expected from our earlier discussion. It may be noted that, for the integrable case, the linear fit is not always as good as the fit for the nonintegrable case. This shows that the proportionality constant in
\overline{S}_{1}\propto\ln{\textrm{(DOS)}}
can depend on the energy scale for integrable systems.

Last, we verify the volume law for this spin model. We plot
\overline{S}_{1}
against the subsystem size
l_{1}
(for the fixed energy where the DOS is maximum). As predicted by Eq.

, we see a linear plot in Fig.

- implying that
\overline{S}_{1}\propto l_{1}
. For our calculations, we choose

(where the system is nonintegrable).

4
Conclusion

Although the average von Neumann (VN) entropy for Haar-random pure states is well known (Page’s result), it is important to know the average for physically relevant subspaces. In this Letter, we analyze the average von Neumann (VN) entropy corresponding to an energy scale (for generic quantum local Hamiltonians). We find that the average entropy of a part of a system (here, subsystem 1) is
\overline{S}_{1}\simeq\ln{d_1}
, where
d_{1}=D_{1}^{\gamma}
(
D_{1}
is the Hilbert space dimension of the subsystem 1). We argue that
\gamma=\frac{\ln{d_E}}{\ln{D}}
for nonintegrable (chaotic) systems, and
\gamma<\frac{\ln{d_E}}{\ln{D}}
for integrable systems, where
D
is the Hilbert space dimension of the full system and
d_{E}
is the subspace dimension corresponding to the energy scale. This result can be reinterpreted as the volume-law of entropy where the coefficient of volume is dependent on the density of states (DOS) for nonintegrable system and the coefficient is less than the maximum possible value for integrable system. We substantiate our results by numerical analysis of a one-dimensional spin chain. Our results provide a complementary understanding of quantum thermalization which is traditionally explained in the frameworks such as canonical typicality and the Eigenstate Thermalization Hypothesis (ETH).
