Near-infrared spectroscopy: Continuous, objective surveillance for face transplant success
Authors: Alexander Lugilde-Guerbek, Antonio Bulla, Victor Andreu-Sola, Bernat López-Masramon, José Antonio López Martínez, Carlos Pueyo-Morillo, Alex Arteaga, Sara Orois, Andrés Iglesias-Álvarez, Manuel Cuadros-Ramírez, Juan P. Barret
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryFace transplantation carries a critical risk of vascular occlusion. Traditional monitoring relies on subjective clinical signs, which may detect compromise after irreversible ischemia. We report the first application of continuous near-infrared spectroscopy (NIRS) for postoperative monitoring in a partial face transplant recipient. Over ten days, NIRS identified 51 subclinical perfusion events (hemodynamic, positional, or mixed), enabling prompt non-surgical rescue. Regional oxygen saturation (rSO₂) drops preceded clinical signs, guiding fluid resuscitation and patient repositioning. No graft loss occurred. Continuous NIRS oximetry provides objective, real-time data that complements clinical examination and may improve allograft survival.

Introduction

Since the first face transplant in 2005, vascularized composite allotransplantation (VCA) has become a reconstructive option for severe disfigurement.1 However, vascular occlusion remains the most significant threat; early series reported graft loss in 15% of cases, some directly due to anastomotic failure.2 Conventional monitoring relies on intermittent clinical assessment (color, temperature, turgor) and handheld Doppler, both lacking continuous, objective data. Near-infrared spectroscopy (NIRS) addresses this gap by providing non-invasive, real-time tissue oximetry.3, 4 Motivated by prior vascular events in our center’s first two face transplants (both monitored without NIRS, leading to reintervention),5 we introduced continuous NIRS for the third case to enable subclinical detection and earlier non‑surgical rescue

Materials and methods

A 60-year-old woman underwent partial face transplantation (nose, maxilla, perioral, chin) following septic shock necrosis. Bilateral end-to-end anastomoses of facial arteries (9–0 nylon) and veins (2.0/2.5 mm couplers) were performed. To proactively manage the risk of vascular thrombosis and potential surgical re-exploration, continuous NIRS monitoring was implemented for this case.

From postoperative day (POD) 0 to 10, regional oxygen saturation (rSO₂) was monitored using the INVOS™ 7100C system (Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN, USA). Two sensors were applied: one on the VCA skin paddle and a control sensor on the native forehead (Fig. 1). A critical event was defined as a ≥ 15% reduction in VCA rSO₂ from the daily baseline mean. Results

Fifty-one critical events were identified over ten days. Hemodynamic events (n = 35) were most common. Hypotension-induced rSO₂ drops occurred on POD 1, 2, and 6, detected exclusively by NIRS before clinical signs (Fig. 2). Paradoxical drops during hypertensive spikes (n = 28 events on POD 4, 7, 8) suggested venous congestion from rapid arterial inflow against compromised outflow. Position-dependent changes were observed as early as the intraoperative phase; macroscopically, specific rotations or flexions of the hemiface mechanically compromised outflow, causing immediate visible congestion and localized oximetry drops that resolved upon realigning the graft (Fig. 1B). Positional events (n = 5) resolved with head-of-bed (HOB) adjustment; NIRS guided HOB elevation from 0° to 7° (POD 2), then 15–30° (POD 4). Rather than relying on empirical protocols, the INVOS system provided objective, real-time feedback that safely titrated the angle of head elevation, allowing the clinical team to maximize venous drainage without crossing the threshold into arterial hypoperfusion. Attempts to reach 45° (POD 5–7) were reversed due to NIRS-detected desaturation, returning to 30° until POD 10. Mixed events (n = 4) required both repositioning and hemodynamic support. Seven transient events of unknown etiology (including nocturnal episodes possibly REM-related) self-corrected. No vascular compromise or graft loss occurred. Discussion

This case suggests that continuous NIRS oximetry provides superior, real-time perfusion surveillance in facial VCA. NIRS detected 51 subclinical events, all reversed with conservative management, avoiding unnecessary surgical exploration. Compared with our prior two face transplants (monitored without NIRS, both complicated by vascular events requiring re-operation), NIRS enabled earlier detection of hypoperfusion, particularly from systemic hypotension and positional changes. The control sensor on native forehead was essential to rule out systemic confounders. The VCA proved highly labile – susceptible to both hemodynamic fluctuations and mechanical positioning – reinforcing the need for continuous monitoring. NIRS also guided safe HOB elevation during rehabilitation, reducing team uncertainty.

Limitations include the single-case design and the use of a moving baseline that may mask slow declines. NIRS samples only 1–2 cm depth and should remain an adjunct to clinical examination and Doppler. Nevertheless, this first report of INVOS-based NIRS in a facial allograft shows feasibility and value for extending flap oximetry to VCA.
