Severe mitochondrial cardiomyopathy with m.3303C > T variant in the mitochondrial tRNALeu(UUR) gene: A study of large family analysis
Authors: Shuichi Yatsuga
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe m.3303C > T variant in the mitochondrial tRNALeu(UUR) gene is a rare cause of mitochondrial cardiomyopathy (MCM). We report a Japanese family with this variant across three generations, demonstrating a wide clinical spectrum ranging from asymptomatic carriers to lethal infantile cardiomyopathy. The proband was a newborn male infant who developed severe hypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy, generalized hypotonia, and profound lactic acidosis (43.9 mmol/L) shortly after birth. Despite intensive care, the patient died at 11 days of age due to multiorgan failure. Autopsy revealed diffuse cardiac hypertrophy and abnormal mitochondria. Genetic analysis showed 98% heteroplasmy of the m.3303C > T variant in multiple tissues. Family screening revealed variant loads ranging from 6% to 100% in seven relatives, with clinical manifestations only appearing when the variant load exceeded 90%. One maternal uncle with 100% muscle heteroplasmy died at the age of 7 years from mitochondrial myopathy. Other family members with variant loads below 90% were asymptomatic. This study demonstrates that the m.3303C > T variant exhibited, within this family, an exceptionally high threshold effect, with severe phenotypes manifesting only at very high heteroplasmy levels (>90%), and highlights the importance of family screening for genetic counseling.Keywords: Mitochondrial DNA, tRNALeu(UUR), M.3303C > T variant, Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, Heteroplasmy, Threshold effect, Lactic acidosis, Infantile cardiomyopathy

Introduction

To date, more than 400 mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) changes have been identified as pathogenic [1], [2]. Pathogenic variants in mtDNA often cause complications, such as cardiac disorders, generalized myopathy, and neurological disorders. However, cardiac disorders as the main feature in infants are rare, potentially leading to lethal infantile cardiomyopathy [3], [4]. The m.3303C > T variant (OMIM 590050) is generally identified after the diagnosis of mitochondrial cardiomyopathy. Because this variant is rare, the clinical spectrum and threshold effects of its pathogenicity have not been fully elucidated [3], [4].

Recent advances in next-generation sequencing (NGS) technology have revolutionized the diagnostic approach to mitochondrial diseases, enabling a comprehensive analysis of both mtDNA and nuclear genes associated with mitochondrial dysfunction [5], [6]. These technological improvements have facilitated earlier diagnosis, reduced the need for invasive muscle biopsies in selected cases, and improved genetic counseling for affected families [7], [8]. The more common m.3243 A > G variant in the same tRNALeu(UUR) gene has been extensively studied and has been shown to demonstrate heteroplasmy-dependent clinical manifestations, with threshold effects typically manifesting at 60–90% variant loads [9], [10]. Understanding the threshold characteristics of different mtDNA variants is crucial for prognosis and family counseling [11].

We investigated the clinical presentation by surveying a three-generation Japanese family with an m.3303C > T variant, aiming to better define the genotype-phenotype correlation and threshold effect of this rare variant.

Case report

The proband (IV-2) was a newborn male infant (Fig. 1). His gestational age was 37 weeks, birth weight was 2195 g, and birth length was 46 cm. He was born as the first child of healthy, unrelated Japanese parents. Soon after birth, the patient developed tachypnea, generalized hypotonia, and severe lactic acidosis (Table 1). Echocardiography confirmed the diagnosis of hypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy. Although intensive care was provided, the patient died at 11 days of age due to multiorgan failure. An autopsy was performed with parental consent. At the macroscopic level, the heart showed diffuse hypertrophy with an atrial septal defect, and the liver weight was increased without an abnormal structure. Microscopically, cardiomyocytes contained numerous vacuoles, and hepatocytes showed fatty infiltration on hematoxylin-eosin staining. Some cardiomyocytes contained granular deposits, as observed on modified Masson's trichrome staining. Both the skeletal muscle and heart showed no ragged-red fibers. Electron microscopy revealed abnormally structured mitochondria in the heart, liver, and skeletal muscles. DNA analysis was performed on several organs, which demonstrated a heteroplasmic m.3303C > T variant in the mitochondrial tRNALeu(UUR) gene; the variant load was 98% in the heart, skeletal muscle, kidney, and bladder (Fig. 1).

One year after his death, his younger sister was born. Her heteroplasmic m.3303C > T variant was 84% in urine. She remained healthy with no clinical symptoms at 3 years of age. An echocardiographic assessment conducted at an external facility revealed no indications of cardiac hypertrophy or dysfunction. Additionally, the serum creatine kinase level was within normal limits. All maternal family members, except one patient (III-7), were healthy. The maternal uncle (III-7) died of mitochondrial myopathy with psychomotor retardation at 7 years of age. Regrettably, detailed cardiac evaluation data for patient III-7 are unavailable, as he passed away in the 1990s and comprehensive medical records were not preserved. We obtained his muscle tissue (III-7), which had been stored and performed PCR-RFLP analysis at another hospital. The variant load was 100% in the muscle. The variant loads of other family members are shown in Fig. 1. The proband's mother (III-6) showed 70% in urine, 65% in blood, 60% in nails, 45% in hair, and buccal mucosa; the maternal grandmother's sister (II-1) showed 58% in blood, 34% in urine, 26% in buccal mucosa, and 6% in hair; the grandmother (II-2) showed 60% in urine; and the maternal aunt (III-3) showed 74% in urine, all without clinical health problems. The mother (III-3) underwent an echocardiographic assessment at an external facility, which revealed no indications of cardiac hypertrophy or dysfunction.

Materials and methods

Initial mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) analysis was conducted on autopsy tissue, specifically cardiac muscle, from the proband. This tissue was chosen due to its manifestation of the most severe pathological alterations and the anticipated presence of the highest variant load. Individuals II-1, II-2, III-3, III-6, III-7, IV-2, and IV-3 had urine, muscle, blood, hair, and buccal mucosa collected for analysis of the m.3303C > T variant. DNA was extracted from the collected samples using standard proteinase K and phenol-chloroform methods. The m.3303C > T variant was identified through PCR-RFLP, as detailed below. Comprehensive mtDNA sequencing was not undertaken. Although this study was conducted circa 2010, the cost of next-generation sequencing in Japan at that time was prohibitively high, rendering it unfeasible within the constraints of available research funding. Consequently, PCR-RFLP remained the standard and cost-effective diagnostic method for detecting known pathogenic variants. Haplogroup analysis was not performed. The m.3303C > T variant was analyzed by PCR-RFLP using two different primers and restriction endonucleases:

1)A 76 bp fragment, including nucleotide position 3303, was amplified by PCR using primers at mtDNA position (5′-3′) 3277–3300 and 3353–3333. The amplification conditions were as follows: 95 °C for 1 min, followed by 30 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s and 68 °C for 15 s. After PCR amplification, the PCR products were digested with the restriction endonuclease HpaI (New England Biolabs, Inc.), separated through 3% agarose gel electrophoresis, and visualized with ethidium bromide under UV light. The normal PCR product yielded two fragments (53 and 23 bp). Because the variant abolishes the HpaI digestion site, the 76 bp fragment remains uncut in the patients.

Initial PCR-RFLP analysis was conducted at the referring institution. For the maternal uncle (III-7) and the proband's sister (IV-3), PCR-RFLP analysis was additionally performed at our institution, and a representative gel is shown in Fig. 2. According to the records obtained, band intensities were quantified using densitometry, with adjustments made for molecular size differences between digested fragments to ensure precise heteroplasmy estimation. Specifically, band intensities were normalized to fragment size prior to calculating heteroplasmy percentages, taking into account the proportionally stronger ethidium bromide fluorescence signal from larger DNA fragments. Normal controls confirmed the absence of the variant in wild-type mtDNA, and known positive controls validated the digestion pattern.

Heteroplasmy levels, ascertained through two independent PCR-RFLP methods (HpaI and MseI), demonstrated concordant results, with a difference of less than 5% across all samples, thereby affirming the accuracy of the quantification. The pathogenicity of the m.3303C > T variant was assessed using MitoTip [12], a computational predictor specifically designed for mitochondrial tRNA variants. This tool integrates multiple sources of information, including phylogenetic conservation, RNA secondary structure analysis, and clinical databases. Furthermore, the variant was corroborated as a reported pathogenic variant in MITOMAP (https://www.mitomap.org), a comprehensive database of mitochondrial DNA variants.

Results

The proband exhibited severe hypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy immediately postpartum. Upon admission, laboratory analyses (Table 1) indicated significant lactic acidosis (43.9 mmol/L), elevated creatine kinase levels (7126 U/L), and signs of multiorgan involvement, including hepatic dysfunction (AST 234 U/L, ALT 27 U/L) and renal impairment (BUN 12.7 mg/dL, creatinine 1.4 mg/dL). Blood glucose levels were within normal range (92 mg/dL). Despite intensive care interventions, the progression of multiorgan failure resulted in mortality at 11 days of age. Autopsy findings indicated widespread cardiac hypertrophy accompanied by an atrial septal defect. Microscopic analysis revealed numerous vacuolations within cardiomyocytes and fatty infiltration in hepatocytes. Electron microscopy identified abnormally structured mitochondria in cardiac muscle, skeletal muscle, and liver tissue.

Molecular genetic analysis identified the heteroplasmic m.3303C > T variant in the mitochondrial tRNALeu(UUR) gene. In the proband, the variant load was 98% in the heart, skeletal muscle, kidney, and bladder. Family screening revealed a wide range of heteroplasmy levels across three generations (Fig. 1). The maternal uncle (III-7) exhibited a 100% variant load in skeletal muscle and succumbed to mitochondrial myopathy with psychomotor retardation at the age of 7. The proband's mother (III-6, 70% in urine, 65% in blood) and younger sister (IV-3, 84% in urine) both underwent echocardiographic assessments at external institutions, which revealed no indications of cardiac hypertrophy or dysfunction. The sister's serum creatine kinase level was within normal limits, and she remains asymptomatic at the age of 3. Among the asymptomatic family members, the following heteroplasmy levels were observed: the maternal grandmother's sister (II-1) exhibited 58% in blood and 34% in urine; the grandmother (II-2) showed 60% in urine; and the maternal aunt (III-3) demonstrated 74% in urine. All individuals with heteroplasmy levels below 90% remained asymptomatic. PCR-RFLP analysis performed at another institution confirmed the m.3303C > T variant, with 84% heteroplasmy in the urine of the proband's sister (IV-3) and 100% in the skeletal muscle of the maternal uncle (III-7) (Fig. 2). The proband's father did not carry the variant, consistent with maternal inheritance.

The m.3303C > T variant is cataloged in MITOMAP as a confirmed pathogenic variant linked to mitochondrial cardiomyopathy and myopathy. MitoTip [12] analysis assigned a pathogenicity score of 92.7 out of 100, signifying a very high probability of pathogenicity. This elevated score is indicative of the variant's position at a highly conserved nucleotide site within the tRNALeu(UUR) gene and its documented association with severe clinical phenotypes across multiple families. The heteroplasmy levels, ascertained through two independent PCR-RFLP methods (HpaI and MseI), exhibited concordant results, with a discrepancy of less than 5% across all samples, thereby affirming the accuracy of the quantification.

Discussion

We report a three-generation Japanese family with the m.3303C > T variant in the mitochondrial tRNALeu(UUR) gene. The clinical spectrum in individuals varied by variant load, ranging from the most severe lethal hypertrophic cardiomyopathy in our proband and psychomotor retardation in the uncle to being free from any clinical symptoms.

Although lethal infantile hypertrophic cardiomyopathy associated with high heteroplasmy levels of this variant has been previously documented [[3], [4]], our study represents the first comprehensive three-generation family analysis demonstrating an exceptionally high pathogenic threshold (>90%). This research offers several novel contributions: 1) an analysis of seven family members with heteroplasmy levels ranging from 6% to 100%, representing the broadest range reported within a single family for this variant; 2) documentation of the precise pathogenic threshold through systematic family screening, with clinical manifestations occurring only when the variant load exceeded 90%; 3) long-term follow-up data (3–25 years) indicating that carriers with less than 90% heteroplasmy remain asymptomatic; and 4) a systematic comparison with the more common m.3243 A > G variant, revealing variant-specific threshold characteristics.

The m.3303C > T variant is situated within the mitochondrial tRNALeu(UUR) gene. A prevalent variant in this gene is m.3243 A > G [13]. Recent investigations have thoroughly characterized the m.3243 A > G variant, revealing that heteroplasmy levels of 10–30% are linked to diabetes, 50–90% to encephalomyopathies including MELAS, and 90–100% result in Leigh syndrome or perinatal lethality [[9], [10], [14], [15]]. Cardiac involvement in individuals carrying the m.3243 A > G variant exhibits diverse manifestations, such as hypertrophic and dilated cardiomyopathy, with cardiac dysfunction correlating with the variant load in skeletal muscle [[16], [17]]. Recent studies utilizing iPSC-derived cardiomyocytes have shown that high m.3243 A > G variant loads (>60%) impair mitochondrial respiration, although the downstream effects vary among patients, accounting for phenotypic heterogeneity [18]. In contrast, our family analysis indicates that the m.3303C > T variant demonstrates a significantly higher threshold compared to m.3243 A > G, both biochemically and clinically [3], [4]. In our three-generation family study, clinical symptoms were absent with <90% of mtDNA variant load; however, individuals exhibited reduced lifespan with >90% of mtDNA variant load. This is in stark contrast to the m.3243 A > G variant, where clinical manifestations can occur at considerably lower heteroplasmy levels.

The m.3303C > T variant has been documented in multiple families since its initial identification by Silvestri et al. (1994) [3]. Campos et al. (2001) [19] reported a Spanish family exhibiting mitochondrial myopathy, cardiomyopathy [19], and psychiatric disorders associated with this variant. Goldstein et al. (1999) [4] described maternally inherited mitochondrial cardiomyopathy linked to this variant. More recent studies have reported both neonatal-onset severe cardiomyopathy and adult-onset manifestations [[20], [21]]. A recent familial study by Fałek et al. (2024) [21] documented siblings with neonatal cardiomyopathy and severe metabolic acidosis who succumbed in infancy, with their oligosymptomatic mother and asymptomatic maternal aunt identified as carriers. These findings align with our observations, illustrating significant phenotypic variability within families harboring the same variant. Our findings corroborate previous reports by demonstrating severe cardiac phenotypes at very high heteroplasmy levels. However, our study advances the understanding by characterizing a high threshold (>90%) within this single family through a systematic three-generation family analysis encompassing heteroplasmy levels from 6% to 100%. Notably, both the mother (70% heteroplasmy) and sister (84% heteroplasmy) in our family remain asymptomatic with normal cardiac function, supporting a high threshold for clinical manifestation in this family. While based on a single family and not generalizable as a definitive threshold, this observation is consistent with previous reports and may aid genetic counseling.

The mortality rate is elevated in cases of mitochondrial cardiomyopathy compared to those without cardiac involvement among infants and children hospitalized with mitochondrial diseases [22]. Within pediatric cardiomyopathy registries, mitochondrial disorders constitute a significant portion of metabolic cardiomyopathies, with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) being the most prevalent presentation, accounting for approximately 40% of mitochondrial cardiomyopathies [[22], [23]]. Recent guidelines underscore the necessity of distinguishing mitochondrial HCM from sarcomeric HCM, as they differ in natural history, prognosis, and therapeutic strategies [24]. The Pediatric Cardiomyopathy Registry indicates a 5-year survival rate of merely 42% in cases of mitochondrial disease-related HCM presenting after one year of age, in contrast to 94% in idiopathic cases [19]. Children with mtDNA variants frequently exhibit heart-associated symptoms; however, heart disease as the primary symptom is relatively uncommon. Previous studies indicate that heart disease as the primary symptom manifests in early onset after birth as lethal infantile cardiomyopathy [3], [4]. In our case, severe cardiomyopathy also manifested postnatally, and the patient succumbed at 11 days of age. We posit that mitochondrial diseases, where cardiomyopathy is the predominant symptom, are exceedingly severe and carry a poor prognosis, particularly when associated with the m.3303C > T variant at very high heteroplasmy levels.

The observed variability in phenotypic expression within this family, even among individuals exhibiting similar levels of heteroplasmy, suggests the potential involvement of nuclear genetic modifiers or environmental factors. Previous research has indicated that the nuclear genetic background can affect the phenotypic severity of mtDNA variants [25], [26]. Although nuclear gene panel sequencing was not conducted in our study, the maternal inheritance pattern and the clear correlation between heteroplasmy levels and phenotypic severity strongly indicate that the m.3303C > T variant constitutes the primary molecular defect. Future investigations incorporating comprehensive nuclear genome analysis may elucidate potential modifying factors that could account for subtle phenotypic variations within families.

The experiences of this family underscore significant challenges in the realm of genetic counseling for mitochondrial disorders. At the time of the maternal uncle's (III-7) death in the 1990s, comprehensive genetic counseling services were not widely accessible in Japan. Consequently, the family was not informed about the hereditary nature of the condition or the associated recurrence risks until the birth of the proband. Following the proband's demise, genetic counseling was provided to the family, elucidating the maternal inheritance pattern, recurrence risk, and available options for future pregnancies. The subsequent birth of a daughter (IV-3) with 84% heteroplasmy in urine raised pertinent questions regarding her long-term prognosis. Although she remains asymptomatic at age 3, with normal cardiac function and creatine kinase levels, the exceptionally high threshold (>90%) observed in this family offers some reassurance. Of note, her heteroplasmy differed among tissues (100% in buccal mucosa versus 84% in urine). Urinary epithelial cells have been proposed as a readily accessible and clinically informative tissue for mtDNA variant detection [27]; however, urinary heteroplasmy may show day-to-day variability, so values should be interpreted with caution when used for risk assessment. Nonetheless, given the proximity to this threshold, ongoing cardiac and developmental surveillance is imperative and has been arranged, including annual echocardiographic evaluations and developmental assessments. The findings from this family bear significant implications for prenatal counseling. Prenatal diagnosis through amniocentesis or chorionic villus sampling can ascertain fetal heteroplasmy levels, though several critical caveats must be communicated to families: 1) The >90% threshold specific to this variant must be carefully explained when interpreting prenatal testing results. 2) Tissue-specific heteroplasmy variation implies that amniocyte heteroplasmy may not accurately reflect cardiac or muscle heteroplasmy. 3) The threshold may exhibit slight variability between individuals due to potential genetic modifiers. 4) Long-term follow-up is essential even for individuals with heteroplasmy below the apparent threshold. For reproductive counseling, preimplantation genetic testing (PGT-M) represents an option for at-risk families, although the technical challenges of accurate heteroplasmy quantification in single blastomeres must be considered.

The advent of next-generation sequencing (NGS) has revolutionized the diagnostic approach to mitochondrial diseases [5], [6], [7], [8]. Contemporary NGS-based methodologies facilitate the simultaneous analysis of the entire mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) genome alongside hundreds of nuclear genes implicated in mitochondrial function, achieving detection sensitivity for heteroplasmy as low as 5–10% with adequate read depth [6], [7]. This development marks a substantial improvement over traditional Sanger sequencing, which typically yielded genetic diagnoses in only 11% of patients with mitochondrial disorders [5]. Recent guidelines from the United Kingdom advocate for NGS as the preferred first-line diagnostic strategy, potentially obviating the need for invasive muscle biopsies in numerous cases [7]. Nevertheless, in our study conducted prior to the widespread availability of NGS, we employed PCR-RFLP, which remains a validated and cost-effective technique for known variants when family screening is necessary. The concordance between two independent PCR-RFLP methods (difference < 5%) confirmed the accuracy of our heteroplasmy quantification.

This study acknowledges several limitations. Because most analyses were performed more than a decade ago, largely at the referring institution, much of the primary data—including electron microscopy images and gel images for most family members—could not be retrieved. The electron microscopy findings were documented in the original clinical records, but the images themselves were no longer available. Nevertheless, the original PCR-RFLP gel for the proband's sister (IV-3) and the maternal uncle (III-7), performed at our institution, was located in the archives of the referring hospital and is presented here as representative primary data (Fig. 2). Firstly, detailed echocardiographic parameters, such as interventricular septal thickness, left ventricular posterior wall thickness, left ventricular mass, and ejection fraction, were unavailable for asymptomatic family members, as these assessments were conducted at external institutions. Nonetheless, the absence of cardiac symptoms and normal clinical echocardiography findings in carriers with less than 90% heteroplasmy (mother at 70%, sister at 84%) support the high pathogenic threshold of this variant. Secondly, biochemical analysis of the mitochondrial respiratory chain was not conducted. The diagnosis was established through molecular genetic testing using PCR-RFLP, the standard diagnostic approach in the 1990s when this study commenced. The clear genotype-phenotype correlation observed in this family, along with consistency with previously reported cases, strongly supports the pathogenic role of the m.3303C > T variant. Thirdly, detailed cardiac evaluation data for the deceased maternal uncle (III-7) were unavailable, as he passed away in the 1990s and comprehensive medical records were not preserved. Clinical records indicated that psychomotor retardation and progressive muscle weakness were the primary clinical manifestations. Fourthly, additional family members declined genetic testing or were unavailable for sampling, limiting the scope of family screening. This decision was respected in accordance with ethical principles of genetic counseling. Fifthly, nuclear gene panel sequencing was not performed to exclude potential genetic modifiers. However, the maternal inheritance pattern, the clear correlation between heteroplasmy levels and phenotypic severity, and consistency with previously reported families support that the m.3303C > T variant is the primary molecular defect. Sixthly, single-cell analysis of skeletal and cardiac muscle fibers would provide valuable insights into the biochemical threshold at the cellular level. However, such analysis was not feasible due to tissue preservation limitations and represents an important future direction. Despite these limitations, this study provides valuable insights into the exceptionally high pathogenic threshold (>90%) of the m.3303C > T variant through comprehensive three-generation family analysis spanning heteroplasmy levels from 6% to 100%. These findings have important clinical implications for genetic counseling, prenatal diagnosis, and long-term management of affected families.

In conclusion, our study demonstrated that the m.3303C > T variant shows clinical heterogeneity, ranging from asymptomatic to the most severe form of hypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy, manifesting only when this variant accumulates at very high percentages (>90%), according to its spatial distribution of this. This exceptionally high threshold effect, demonstrated through systematicthree-generation family analysis, has important implications for genetic counseling, prenatal diagnosis, and reproductive decision-making in affected families. Our findings underscore the importance of comprehensive family screening and tissue-specific heteroplasmy assessment in understanding the variable expressivity of mtDNA variants.
