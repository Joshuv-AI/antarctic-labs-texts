Monotonicity of the Laplace Transform for Tomography in Dissipative Systems
Antonello Tamburrino 1,2 , Antonio Corbo Esposito 1 , Gianpaolo Piscitelli 3

Abstract
. This paper addresses the problem of tomography for the interior of dissipative materials, with a focus on Magnetic Induction Tomography (MIT), a proven technique for imaging the interior of conductive materials using low-frequency electromagnetic fields. Processing MIT data is mathematically challenging because of the non-linear and ill-posed nature of the underlying inverse problem. On the other hand, the Monotonicity Principle is recognized as the basis for developing effective approaches. In this framework, the paper presents a principle of monotonicity for the Transfer Operator in Magnetic Induction Tomography, i.e. the operator mapping the Laplace transform of the applied source onto the Laplace transform of the measured quantity. Specifically, it is proved that the Transfer Operator satisfies a Monotonicity Principle when evaluated on a proper real semi-axis of the complex plane. The description of the related (real-time) imaging method is also given. Keywords: Magnetic Induction Tomography, Monotonicity Principles, Transfer Function, Inverse Problems, Imaging. AMS subject classifications: 35K05, 35K10, 35R30, 78A46.

1.
Introduction

Tomography is a technique used to create a 3D image of an object by taking measurements from the outside. It works by sending a signal (like an X-ray) into the object and measuring how it comes out the other side. This can be used to image various structures, such as bones, organs, and even materials.

There are two main types of tomography: hard-field and soft-field. Hard-field tomography utilizes rather energetic fields that travel in straight lines, like X-rays. This makes it easier to create an image of the interior, but it involves ionizing radiation, expensive equipment, and safety issues, among other concerns. In contrast, soft-field tomography uses less energetic fields that can be bent by the material. This makes it more versatile because no ionizing radiation is involved; however, it comes with an increase in the level of difficulty in processing the data to form an image of the interior.

The tomographic method treated in this work is Magnetic Induction Tomography (MIT), also known as Eddy Current Tomography. Magnetic Induction Tomography relies on
the capability of low-frequency electromagnetic fields to penetrate to some extent the interior of conducting materials. In this tomographic method, a set of electrical currents that circulate in the source region
\Omega_{S}
induces electrical currents in the conducting domain under imaging
\Omega_{C}
(see
Figure

1
). The spatial distribution of the electrical resistivity
\eta
affects the reaction field measured externally to
\Omega_{C}
and, therefore, starting from the reaction field, an attempt is made to reconstruct the electrical resistivity into the interior of the conducting domain.

Figure 1.

\Omega_{S}
induces electrical currents in the conducting domain under imaging
\Omega_{C}
. The spatial distribution of the electrical resistivity
\eta
affects the reaction field measured in the source region
\Omega_{S}

In practical scenarios where it suffices to recover only the support of an anomaly in a known background, non-iterative imaging methods are advantageous due to their real-time capabilities. The fundamental idea in this category of methods involves calculating an indicator, specifically a function of space, that takes distinct values when assessed within or outside the anomalous region. Several non-iterative imaging techniques within this context are recalled here.

Pioneering studies on non-iterative approaches are due to Colton and Kirsch (Linear Sampling Method
[
8
]
, and Factorization Method
[
27
]

and Brühl’s extension of the Factorization Method to electrical resistance tomography
[

These methods rely on sophisticated mathematical constructions that require meticulous numerical implementations.
A robust numerical convergence criterion for infinite series, particularly sensitive to noise, is essential, as indicated in
[
6

.
Kusiak, Sylvester, and Potthast proposed a non-iterative method to find the so-called convex scattering support, a convex subset of the convex hull of the support of an inhomogeneity in a medium
[
28

,
36

In
[
19

18
]
, Hanke, Griesmaier, and Sylvester proposed non-iterative methods for the Helmholtz equation for localizing one or several scatterers from far-field data at one fixed incident field and frequency. The MUSIC algorithm (MUltiple SIgnal Classification), well-established in signal processing, gained attention for its potential application in imaging, as highlighted by Devaney
[
11
,
12
]
. Ammari and Lesselier
[

extended the MUSIC algorithm to locate small inclusions buried in a half-space, leveraging scattered amplitude data. Although capable of detecting multiple inclusions simultaneously, this method is limited to cases where inclusions are small and well-separated.

The Monotonicity Principle Method (MPM), which is the main subject of this paper, is a non-iterative imaging method introduced by Tamburrino and Rubinacci in 2002 for electrical resistance tomography (ERT)
[
47
]
.
MPM is based on a proper monotonic relation between the unknown interior material property and the measured external data. The first evidence of this monotonous relationship was found by Gisser, Isaacson, and Newell in 1990 (see
[
17
]
).
However, its value as the foundation for a new family of imaging methods was not realized until 2002
[
47
]
.

MP-based tomography presents several advantages. First, unlike other methods such as Gauss-Newton and similar (see, for instance,
[
34
]
and
[
30
]
), it does not require costly and time-consuming repetitive calculations of the forward problem, making it ideal for
real-time imaging
. Additionally, MP-based algorithms can be easily broken down into smaller tasks, allowing for
parallel processing
and further reduced computational time. Second, the test based on the Monotonicity Principle is
exact
not only in an ideal setting consisting of a continuum of probes
[
21
]
but even for a discrete and finite distribution of probes
[
47
]
. Third, MP-based tomography methods are
fully nonlinear
, in the sense that no approximations of the nonlinear operator mapping the unknown quantities into the measured data are introduced, unlike for the (linear) Born approximation
[
3
,
42
,
52
,
54
,
55
]
. Moreover, although MP does not linearize the unknown-data operator, it does not suffer
[
16
]
from the presence of
local minima
and false solutions when the inverse problem is cast in terms of minimization
[
35
,
38
,
53
]
.
Fourth, the test required by MPM can be implemented
without any approximation
also in a real-world setting consisting of a finite number of noisy measurements, as opposed to other non-iterative methods, such as the Factorization Method, based on a range test that requires checking if a series is convergent or not (see
[
6
,
7
]
). Fifth, MPM can be
regularized
, resulting in a stable and robust imaging method against noise (
[
16
]
,
[
33
]
). In
[
10
]
and
[
9
]
, the Monotonicity Principle has been introduced for nonlinear problems, under quite general assumptions on the material property. The related imaging method, together with realistic numerical examples, can be found in
[
32
]
and the converse in
[
33
]
.

MPM, initially applied in the context of ERT for elliptic problems, was later extended to parabolic partial differential equations (PDEs) governing Magnetic Induction Tomography in both the small skin-depth regime
[
43
]
and the large skin-depth regime
[
48
]
, with experimental validations in
[
44
]
. The extension of MPM to hyperbolic PDEs, which governs inverse wave propagation scattering problems, provided upper and lower bounds for unknown objects under the constraint of a finite number of measurements (limited aperture data)
[
47
]
. In
[
21

MPM remains an active research topic, showing promise in various applications. Garde et al.
[
16
]
formulated a monotonicity-based shape reconstruction scheme, incorporating regularization against noise and modeling error. Two reconstruction algorithms were proposed and validated using numerical examples with simulated complete electrode model (CEM) data and experimental measurements. In the medical imaging domain, a novel bimodal imaging technique was presented combining breast microwave radar (BMR) and electrical impedance tomography (EIT) methods
[
14
,
15
]
. This technique used a priori information from BMR images to estimate the location of dense breast regions and employed the monotonicity of the impedance matrix to reconstruct the tissue distribution in the breast region.

In
[
2
]
, Aykroyd et al. investigated the performance of MPM in ERT problems, proposing a modified algorithm that integrates Bayesian modeling and Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) estimation for configurations with a limited number of electrodes and low noise levels. Wallinger et al.
[
51
]
combined MPM with Gauss–Newton-based iterative algorithms for electrical capacitance tomography in two-phase scenarios. MPM provided the initial estimate for the Gauss–Newton method, refining the boundaries of anomalies. Harrach et al.
[
20
]
developed a monotonicity-based imaging method by combining frequency-difference EIT with ultrasound-modulated EIT, eliminating the need for numerical simulations or forward models.

The monotonicity of time constants in pulsed Magnetic Induction Tomography, initially introduced and numerically validated in
[
45
]
, led to subsequent publications that developed an imaging method based on the monotonicity of time constants
[
40
,
49
,
39
,
46
]
. These studies demonstrated real-time reconstruction capabilities to profile anomalies within practical 3D structures.

The mathematical framework underlying the treated problem is modeled by a parabolic system (see
Section

2.2
), a mathematical context in which it is generally challenging to identify the correct operator that exhibits the MP. The original content of this paper consists of (i) introducing the Transfer Operator
\mathcal{H}_{\eta}
, a new operator to treat inverse problems in parabolic PDEs, and (ii) proving the Monotonicity Principle for
\mathcal{H}_{\eta}
.

The Transfer Operator
\mathcal{H}_{\eta}
is the operator that maps the Laplace transform of the driving source to the Laplace transform of the measured data. Specifically,
\mathcal{H}_{\eta}
evaluated for a complex
p
is a kind of transfer “function” mapping the Laplace transform of the applied current density to the Laplace transform of the measured electrical field, as discussed in
Section

3
, i.e.,

(1)

\mathcal{H}_{\eta}(p):\mathbf{J}_{s}(\mathbf{x},p)\rightarrow\mathcal{P}_{S}\mathbf{E}_{R}(\mathbf{x},p),

where
\eta
is the electrical resistivity of the conducting domain under tomographic imaging,
\mathbf{J}_{s}(\mathbf{x},p)
is the Laplace transform of the applied source current density evaluated at
p\in\mathbb{C}
,
\mathbf{E}_{R}(\mathbf{x},p)
is the Laplace transform of the measured electrical field, and
\mathcal{P}_{S}
is a projector in a proper functional space.
The Transfer Operator represents the measured data. It generalizes the concept of generalized impedance (in the complex plane) borrowed from Circuit Theory.

The Monotonicity Property for the Transfer Operator
\mathcal{H_{\eta}}
proved in this paper is:

(2)

\eta_{1}\preceq\eta_{2}\implies\mathcal{H}_{\eta_{1}}\left(p\right)\preceq\mathcal{H}_{\eta_{2}}\left(p\right),

for any
p
real and in the region of convergence for
\mathcal{H}_{\eta_{1}}
and
\mathcal{H}_{\eta_{2}}
(see
Theorem

13
for details). In (
2
)
\preceq
stands for the Loewner order. The impact of a Monotonicity Property in terms of imaging methods was first recognized in
[
47
]
for elliptic PDEs in the context of Electrical Resistance Tomography. In this paper, the discovery of the new form of the Monotonocity Property of (
2
) allows us to apply the imaging methods developed in the framework of MPM to the operator
\mathcal{H}_{\eta}
. The foundation of the imaging method comes from the following equivalent form of (
2
):

(3)

\mathcal{H}_{\eta_{1}}\left(p\right)\not\preceq\mathcal{H}_{\eta_{2}}\left(p\right)\implies\eta_{1}\not\preceq\eta_{2}.

The Relation (
3
) allows one to infer a relation between
\eta_{1}
and
\eta_{2}
, starting from the measured data, i.e., the Transfer Operator evaluated at
p
.

This paper, which introduces a new operator (
\mathcal{H_{\eta}}
) to treat imaging problems for parabolic PDEs, generalizes the findings from the finite-dimensional approximation of
[
41
]
to the continuous case. This generalization requires methods and techniques that are completely different from those previously used for the discrete setting. We refer to
[
41
]
for an informal introduction to the proposed approach and for preliminary numerical examples that show the effectiveness of the imaging method based on the Monotonicity Property (
3
) of the operator
\mathcal{H_{\eta}}
.

2.
Mathematical Model for Magnetic Induction Tomography

In this article, we address the issue of determining the configuration of one or multiple anomalies located within the confines of a conductive region
\Omega_{C}
. This is achieved through the utilization of electromagnetic fields under magneto-quasi-stationary (MQS) conditions, where the displacement current is considered negligible
[
4
,
5
,
22
,
50
]
. The anomalous region, denoted as
V\Subset\Omega_{C}
, may exhibit various topologies and shapes, potentially comprising multiple components, and possesses a resistivity distinct from that of the surrounding conducting domain
\Omega_{C}
. The objective of recovering the shape of the anomaly or anomalies belongs to the area of research, famously known as the inverse obstacle problem. An exemplary scenario is found in Magnetic Induction Tomography (MIT), where the focus lies on detecting ”small” defects within an otherwise homogeneous material.

The MIT probing system is highly conceptual yet universally applicable, encompassing all conceivable scenarios within this broad framework. In detail, the domain under consideration, denoted as
\Omega_{C}
, is imaged using a set of various source current densities
\mathbf{J}_{S}
circulating within the source domain
\Omega_{S}
, as shown in
Figure

3
. The observed parameter is the (projected) reaction electric field
\mathbf{E}_{R}
generated by the current density
\mathbf{J}
induced within the conductive domain
\Omega_{C}
.

2.1.
Notations and Settings

We consider the standard scenario where a conducting domain
\Omega_{C}
is surrounded by an insulating material. Consequently, the normal component of the current density
\mathbf{J}
evaluated on the surface of the conducting domain
\Omega_{C}
is assumed to be vanishing.

Figure 3.
Schematic of a typical eddy current problem: a conducting domain
\Omega_{C}
and the source region
\Omega_{S}
. In
\Omega_{S}
a source electrical current density
\mathbf{J}_{s}
is applied and the (reaction) electric field
\mathbf{E}_{R}
produced by the current density induced in
\Omega_{C}
is measured. The operator (Transfer Operator) mapping the applied source current density
\mathbf{J}_{s}
into
\mathbf{E}_{R}
is the measured data.

We recall from
[
13
,
31
]
the classical functional space
H_{\rm div}(\Omega_{C})

(4)

H_{\rm div}(\Omega_{C})=\left\{\mathbf{u}\in L^{2}(\Omega_{C},\mathbb{R}^{3})\;|\;{\rm div}(\mathbf{u})\in L^{2}(\Omega_{C},\mathbb{R}^{3})\right\},

equipped with the scalar product

\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\rangle_{H_{\rm div}(\Omega_{C})}=\int_{\Omega_{C}}\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x})\cdot\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}+\int_{\Omega_{C}}{\rm div}(\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x}))\ {\rm div}(\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{x}))\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x},

and we define the functional space
H_{L}\left(\Omega_{C}\right)
(see
[
5
]
and
[
46
]
) as

(5)

H_{L}\left(\Omega_{C}\right)=\left\{\mathbf{v}\in H_{\rm div}(\Omega_{C})\;|\;\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v}=0\text{ in }\Omega_{C},\right.\left.\mathbf{v}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}=0\text{ on }\partial\Omega_{C}\right\},

equipped with the scalar product inherited from
H_{\rm div}(\Omega_{C})
. Since
H_{L}\left(\Omega_{C}\right)
is a divergence free space, it turns out that the inherited scalar product reduces to the standard
L^{2}\left(\Omega_{C}\right)
inner product:

(6)

\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\rangle_{C}=\int_{\Omega_{C}}\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x})\cdot\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}.

Similarly, it is possible to define
H_{\rm div}(\Omega_{S})
and
H_{L}\left(\Omega_{S}\right)
.

Moreover, we set

L^{\infty}_{+}(\Omega_{C}):=\{\theta\in L^{\infty}(\Omega_{C})\ |\ \theta\geq c_{0}\ \text{a.e. in}\ \Omega_{C},\ \text{for a positive constant}\ c_{0}\}.

2.2.
Model Problem

In this section, a summary of the mathematical model for the Eddy Current problem is provided.

Let
{\bf E}
,
{\bf B}
,
{\bf H}
, and
{\bf J}
be the electric field, the magnetic flux density, the magnetic field, and the electric current density, respectively. Under the Magneto-Quasi-Stationary approximation, Maxwell’s equations reduce to (see, e.g.,
[
50
]
):

(7)

\displaystyle{\rm{curl}}\ {\mathbf{E}}(\mathbf{x},t)=-\partial_{t}{\bf B}(\mathbf{x},t)\qquad\qquad\textrm{in}\ \mathbb{R}^{3}\times[0,+\infty[,

(8)

\displaystyle{\rm{curl}}\ {\bf H}(\mathbf{x},t)={\bf J}(\mathbf{x},t)+{\bf J}_{S}(\mathbf{x},t)\quad\ \textrm{in}\ \mathbb{R}^{3}\times[0,+\infty[,

(9)

\displaystyle{\rm{div}}\ {\bf B}(\mathbf{x},t)=0\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\ \textrm{in}\ \mathbb{R}^{3}\times[0,+\infty[,

(10)

\displaystyle{\bf J}(\mathbf{x},t)=\begin{cases}\eta^{-1}(\mathbf{x})\ {\bf E}(\mathbf{x},t)\qquad\quad\ \ \textrm{in}\ \Omega_{C}\times[0,+\infty[\\
0\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad\ \textrm{in}\ (\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\Omega_{C})\times[0,+\infty[,\end{cases}

(11)

\displaystyle{\bf B}(\mathbf{x},t)=\mu_{0}{\bf H}(x,t)\qquad\qquad\qquad\ \textrm{in}\ \mathbb{R}^{3}\times[0,+\infty[,

(12)

\displaystyle|{\bf B}(\mathbf{x},t)|=O(|\mathbf{x}|^{-3})\ \textrm{uniformly for $t\in[0,+\infty[$, as}\ |\mathbf{x}|\to+\infty,

where
\eta\in L_{+}^{\infty}(\Omega_{C})
denotes the electrical resistivity of the conductor,
\mu_{0}
is the magnetic permeability of the free space and
{\bf J}_{S}
represents the prescribed source current density. Moreover, it is assumed that there are no magnetic materials in the system. Equations (
7
), (
8
), and (
9
) have to be interpreted in the weak sense, to automatically account for the discontinuities of the fields across material interfaces
[
5
]
.

The problem described by (
7
)–(
12
) admits an equivalent integral formulation (see
[
4
]
). Specifically, the integral formulation in the weak form for the Eddy Current problem is to find
\mathbf{J}\in L^{2}\left(0,T;H_{L}\left(\Omega_{C}\right)\right)
such that

(13)

\begin{split}\langle\mathbf{w},\eta\mathbf{J}(\cdot,t)\rangle_{C}=-\langle\mathbf{w},\partial_{t}\mathcal{A}_{cc}\left[\mathbf{J}\right(\cdot,t)]\rangle_{C}-&\langle\mathbf{w},\partial_{t}\mathbf{A}_{s}(\cdot,t)\rangle_{C},\end{split}

for all
\mathbf{w}\in H_{L}\left(\Omega_{C}\right)
and almost all
0\leq t\leq T
. Here
\eta
is the point-wise resistivity,
\mathcal{A}_{cc}
is a spatial integral operator defined as

(14)

\mathcal{A}_{cc}:\mathbf{v}\in H_{L}\left(\Omega_{C}\right)\rightarrow\frac{\mu_{0}}{4\pi}\int_{\Omega_{C}}\frac{\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{y})}{\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\|}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}\in L^{2}\left(\Omega_{C};\mathbb{R}^{3}\right),

and
\mathbf{A}_{s}
is the magnetic vector potential due to the assigned source current density
\mathbf{J}_{S}
. To be more precise,

(15)

\mathbf{A}_{s}(\cdot,t)=\mathcal{A}_{cs}\left[\mathbf{J}_{s}(\cdot,t)\right],

where the operator
\mathcal{A}_{cs}
is defined as

(16)

\mathcal{A}_{cs}:\mathbf{v}\in H_{L}\left(\Omega_{S}\right)\rightarrow\frac{\mu_{0}}{4\pi}\int_{\Omega_{S}}\frac{\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{y})}{\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\|}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}\in L^{2}\left(\Omega_{C};\mathbb{R}^{3}\right).

For a comprehensive discussion on the functional spaces associated with Maxwell’s equations, we refer to
[
5
,
4
]
.

To state and prove the next result, it is convenient to introduce an additional operator

(17)

\mathcal{A}_{sc}:\mathbf{v}\in H_{L}\left(\Omega_{C}\right)\rightarrow\frac{\mu_{0}}{4\pi}\int_{\Omega_{C}}\frac{\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{y})}{\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\|}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}\in L^{2}\left(\Omega_{S};\mathbb{R}^{3}\right).

We have the following Proposition whose demonstration is a straightforward consequence of the definitions of corresponding operators.

Proposition 1
.

The operator
\mathcal{A}_{sc}
is the adjoint of
\mathcal{A}_{cs}
, i.e.

(18)

\langle\mathcal{A}_{sc}\mathbf{w}_{c},\mathbf{w}_{s}\rangle_{S}=\langle\mathbf{w}_{c},\mathcal{A}_{cs}\mathbf{w}_{s}\rangle_{C},\ \forall\mathbf{w}_{c}\in H_{L}\left(\Omega_{C}\right),\mathbf{w}_{s}\in H_{L}\left(\Omega_{S}\right).

Remark 2
.

Taking into account (
15
), and with the help of
Proposition

1
, the second term on the r.h.s. of (
13
) can be written as

(19)

\langle\mathbf{w},\partial_{t}\mathbf{A}_{s}(\cdot,t)\rangle_{C}=\langle\mathcal{A}_{sc}\left[\mathbf{w}\right],\partial_{t}\mathbf{J}_{S}(\cdot,t)\rangle_{S}.

2.3.
Uniqueness of Weak Solution

In this section, the uniqueness of the weak solution of (
13
) is proved by using classical techniques. Let
\mathbf{J}_{1},\;\mathbf{J}_{2}\in L^{2}\left(0,T;H_{L}\left(\Omega_{C}\right)\right)
be two weak solutions of (
13
). Then,
\mathbf{J}_{0}=\mathbf{J}_{1}-\mathbf{J}_{2}
in
L^{2}\left(0,T;H_{L}\left(\Omega_{C}\right)\right)
solves the variational problem

(20)

\begin{split}\langle\mathbf{w},\eta\mathbf{J}_{0}(\cdot,t)\rangle_{C}=-\langle\mathbf{w},\partial_{t}\mathcal{A}_{cc}\left[\mathbf{J}_{0}\right(\cdot,t)]\rangle_{C},\end{split}

for every test function
\mathbf{w}\in H_{L}\left(\Omega_{C}\right)
and almost every
0\leq t\leq T
with initial condition
\mathbf{J}_{0}(\mathbf{x},0)=0
for almost every
\mathbf{x}\in\Omega_{C}
. Using arguments of
[
46
]
, it can be proved that if
\mathbf{J}_{0}\left(\mathbf{x},t\right)\in L^{2}\left(0,T;H_{L}\left(\Omega_{C}\right)\right)
is a solution of the homogeneous system (
20
), then it can be expressed as

(21)

\mathbf{J}_{0}\left(\mathbf{x},t\right)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}i_{n}\left(t\right)\ {\bf j}_{n}(\mathbf{x})\quad\textrm{in}\ \Omega_{C}\times[0,T],

where
\mathbf{j}_{n}
s are the normalized solutions of the following Generalized Eigenvalue Problem

(22)

\langle\mathcal{A}_{cc}\mathbf{j}_{n},\mathbf{w}_{c}\rangle_{C}=\tau_{n}(\eta)\langle\eta\mathbf{j}_{n},\mathbf{w}_{c}\rangle_{C},\ \forall\mathbf{w}_{c}\in H_{L}\left(\Omega_{C}\right),

and
i_{n}
s are solutions of

(23)

\displaystyle r_{n}i_{n}+l_{n}i_{n}^{\prime}=0\ \forall n\in\mathbb{N},

where
r_{n}=\left\langle\eta{\bf j}_{n},{\bf j}_{n}\right\rangle_{C}
,
l_{n}=\left\langle\mathcal{A}_{cc}{\bf j}_{n},{\bf j}_{n}\right\rangle_{C}
and time constants
\tau_{n}=l_{n}/r_{n}
are finite, real, non-negative, ordered in decreasing order and converging to zero. Since
\mathbf{J}_{0}(\cdot,0)\equiv 0
and
\{\mathbf{j}_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
is a complete
\eta
-orthonormal set in
H_{L}\left(\Omega_{C}\right)
, the initial condition
i_{n}(0)=0
for all
n\geq 1
is obtained. Since
i_{n}(t)=i_{n}(0)e^{-t/\tau_{n}}
from (
23
), it follows that
i_{n}\left(t\right)=0
for every
0\leq t\leq T
and
n\geq 1
. This proves that
\mathbf{J}_{0}=0
for almost every
x\in\Omega_{C}
and
0\leq t\leq T
and, hence, the uniqueness of the weak solution of the system (
13
).

2.4.
Projection Operators

Since
H_{L}\left(\Omega_{C}\right)
is a closed subspace of
L^{2}\left(\Omega_{C};\mathbb{R}^{3}\right)
, its inner product naturally induces a projection operator

(24)

\mathcal{P}_{C}:\mathbf{w}\in L^{2}\left(\Omega_{C};\mathbb{R}^{3}\right)\rightarrow\mathbf{w}_{c}\in H_{L}\left(\Omega_{C}\right),

where
\mathbf{w}_{c}=\argmin_{\mathbf{u}\in H_{L}\left(\Omega_{C}\right)}\langle\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{u},\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{u}\rangle_{C}
. It is worth noting that
\mathbf{w}_{c}=\mathcal{P}_{C}\mathbf{w}
is the unique element of
H_{L}\left(\Omega_{C}\right)
such that

(25)

\langle\mathbf{w},\mathbf{u}\rangle_{C}=\langle\mathbf{w}_{c},\mathbf{u}\rangle_{C},\ \forall\mathbf{u}\in H_{L}\left(\Omega_{C}\right).

Similarly, one can define
\mathcal{P}_{S}
, the projection operator over
H_{L}\left(\Omega_{S}\right)
.

Therefore, (
13
) can be cast in the operator form

(26)

\mathcal{P}_{C}\left(\eta\mathcal{I}+\mathcal{A}_{cc}\partial_{t}\right)\mathcal{P}_{C}\mathbf{J}=-\mathcal{P}_{C}\mathcal{A}_{cs}\partial_{t}\mathbf{J}_{S},

where
\mathcal{I}
is the identity operator, and (
15
) has been for the rightmost term.

The projection operator
\mathcal{P}_{C}
applied to
\mathbf{J}
is
not
strictly required but makes clear the symmetric structure of the operator on the l.h.s. of (
13
).

2.5.
Measured Quantities

Let
\mathbf{E}_{R}(\mathbf{x},t)
be the reaction electric field, i.e., the electric field produced by the current density
\mathbf{J}
. The quantity measured is the projection of the reaction electric field
\mathbf{E}_{R}(\mathbf{x},t)
on
H_{L}\left(\Omega_{S}\right)
. We denote it by
\mathcal{P}_{S}\mathbf{E}_{R}
.

Since
\mathbf{E}_{R}(\mathbf{x},t)=-\partial_{t}\mathbf{A}_{R}(\mathbf{x},t)-\nabla\varphi_{R}(\mathbf{x},t)
, where
\mathbf{A}_{R}=\mathcal{A}_{sc}\mathbf{J}
is the magnetic vector potential and
\varphi_{R}
is the electric scalar potential (see
[
22
]
), by exploiting
\int_{\Omega_{S}}\nabla\varphi_{R}\cdot\mathbf{J}^{\prime}\mathrm{d}V=0
for every
\mathbf{J}^{\prime}\in H_{L}\left(\Omega_{S}\right)
, it turns out that

(27)

\displaystyle\langle\mathbf{E}_{R}(\cdot,t),\mathbf{w}_{s}\rangle_{S}

\displaystyle=-\langle\partial_{t}\mathbf{A}_{R}(\cdot,t),\mathbf{w}_{s}\rangle_{S}

(28)

\displaystyle=-\langle\partial_{t}\mathcal{A}_{sc}\left[\mathbf{J}(\cdot,t)\right],\mathbf{w}_{s}\rangle_{S}

for every
\mathbf{w}_{s}\in H_{L}\left(\Omega_{S}\right)
,
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{S}
representing the usual inner product defined on
L^{2}\left(\Omega_{S}\right)
.
As a consequence, it results

(29)

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{S}\mathbf{E}_{R}

\displaystyle=-\mathcal{P}_{S}\partial_{t}\mathbf{A}_{R}

(30)

\displaystyle=-\mathcal{P}_{S}\mathcal{A}_{sc}\partial_{t}\mathbf{J}.

Equation (
27
) suggests another observable quantity, namely
\mathbf{A}_{R}
or, more precisely, its projection
\mathcal{P}_{S}\mathbf{A}_{R}
:

(31)

\mathcal{P}_{S}\mathbf{A}_{R}\\
=\mathcal{P}_{S}\mathcal{A}_{sc}\mathbf{J}.

2.6.
Structure of the Solution

In
[
46
]
, it has been shown that if
\mathbf{J}\left(\mathbf{x},t\right)\in L^{2}\left(0,T;H_{L}\left(\Omega_{C}\right)\right)
is a solution of (
13
), then it can be expressed as:

(32)

\mathbf{J}\left(\mathbf{x},t\right)=\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}i_{n}\left(t\right)\ {\bf j}_{n}(\mathbf{x})\quad\textrm{in}\ \Omega_{C}\times[0,T],

where the
\mathbf{j}_{n}
s are the solutions of (
22
) and the
i_{n}
s are the solutions of

(33)

\displaystyle r_{n}i_{n}+l_{n}i_{n}^{\prime}=\mathcal{E}_{n}\ \forall n\in\mathbb{N},

where
\mathcal{E}_{n}=-\langle\partial_{t}{\bf A}_{S},{\bf j}_{n}\rangle_{C}
and
\tau_{n},

r_{n},

l_{n}
are the same as in (
23
).

By linearity and (
32
), the solution of (
13
) can be written as

(34)

\mathbf{J}\left(\mathbf{x},t\right)=\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}c_{n}e^{-t/\tau_{n}}{\bf j}_{n}(\mathbf{x})+\mathbf{J}_{F}(\mathbf{x},t)\ \textrm{in}\ \Omega_{C}\times[0,T],

where the series on the r.h.s. corresponds to the source-free response, while
\mathbf{J}_{F}
is the forced response, i.e., a particular solution of (
13
) related to the specific prescribed source
\mathbf{J}_{S}
. The initial condition
\left(\mathbf{J}(\mathbf{x},t=0))\text{ prescribed}\right)
is imposed by the constants
c_{n}
s:

(35)

c_{m}=\frac{\langle\mathbf{J}(\mathbf{x},0)-\mathbf{J}_{F}(\mathbf{x},0),\eta\mathbf{j}_{m}\rangle_{C}}{\langle\mathbf{j}_{m},\eta\mathbf{j}_{m}\rangle_{C}},\ \forall m\in\mathbb{N}.

Proposition 3
.

It is worth noting that

(36)

\left\lVert\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}c_{n}e^{-t/\tau_{n}}{\bf j}_{n}(\mathbf{x})\right\rVert_{C}=\mathcal{O}\left(\mathrm{e}^{-t/\tau_{1}}\right),\textrm{ when }t\rightarrow+\infty.

Proof.

Indeed:

\begin{split}\left\lVert\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}c_{n}e^{-t/\tau_{n}}{\bf j}_{n}(\mathbf{x})\right\rVert_{C}&\leq\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}|c_{n}|e^{-t/\tau_{n}}\left\lVert{\bf j}_{n}(\mathbf{x})\right\rVert_{C}\\
&\leq\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\left|\frac{\langle\mathbf{J}(\mathbf{x},0)-J_{F}(\mathbf{x},0),\eta\mathbf{j}_{n}\rangle_{C}}{\langle\mathbf{j}_{n},\eta\mathbf{j}_{n}\rangle_{C}}\right|e^{-t/\tau_{n}}\left\lVert{\bf j}_{n}(\mathbf{x})\right\rVert_{C}\\
&\leq\frac{1}{\eta_{L}\left\lVert{\bf j}_{n}(\mathbf{x})\right\rVert_{C}}\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\left|\langle\mathbf{J}(\mathbf{x},0),\eta\mathbf{j}_{n}\rangle_{C}-\langle J_{F}(\mathbf{x},0),\eta\mathbf{j}_{n}\rangle_{C}\right|e^{-t/\tau_{n}}\\
&\leq\frac{1}{\eta_{L}\left\lVert{\bf j}_{n}(\mathbf{x})\right\rVert_{C}}\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}(\eta_{U}\lVert\mathbf{J}(\mathbf{x},0)\rVert_{C}\lVert\mathbf{j}_{n}\rVert_{C}+\eta_{U}\lVert J_{F}(\mathbf{x},0)\rVert_{C}\lVert\mathbf{j}_{n}\rVert_{C})e^{-t/\tau_{n}}\\
&\leq\frac{\eta_{U}}{\eta_{L}}\left(\lVert\mathbf{J}(\mathbf{x},0)\rVert_{C}+\lVert J_{F}(\mathbf{x},0)\rVert_{C}\right)\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}e^{-t/\tau_{n}},\end{split}

where
\eta_{U}
and
\eta_{L}
are the upper and lower bounds of
\eta
, respectively.
Let
\{\tau_{n_{j}}\}_{j\in\mathbf{N}}
be a subsequence that contains all time constants without any repetition. Considering that the time constants are eigenvalues of (
22
) and that the operator
\mathcal{A}_{cc}
is a linear operator, it is easily seen that the algebraic multiplicity is lower than the dimension
d
of the host space for each connected component (see, e.g.,
[
37
, Chap. XIII]
and
[
23
, Chap. 1]
).
In the present case, the algebraic multiplicity of the eigenvalues is not larger than
3k
, since
d=3
is the dimension of the host space, where
k
is the number of connected components. Thus

\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}e^{-t/\tau_{n}}\leq 3k\sum_{j=1}^{+\infty}e^{-t/\tau_{n_{j}}}<+\infty,

because the series to the right of the first inequality is convergent by the ratio test. Indeed,
e^{-t/\tau_{n_{j+1}}}/e^{-t/\tau_{n_{j}}}<1
since
\tau_{n_{j+1}}
is strictly smaller than
\tau_{n_{j}}
.

Finally, it results that

\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}e^{-t/\tau_{n}}=m_{1}e^{-t/\tau_{1}}+e^{-t/\tau_{1}}\sum_{j=m_{1}+1}^{+\infty}e^{-t\left(1/\tau_{n}-1/\tau_{1}\right)},

where
m_{1}
is the multiplicity of
\tau_{1}
. The series on the r.h.s. is convergent for any
t>0
, as can be verified by using the ratio test. Moreover, since the series at the r.h.s. is monotonically decreasing, the convergence at an arbitrary
t>0
, gives the conclusion (
36
).
∎

3.
The Transfer Operator

This section is organized into three main parts.
In

3.1
, the forced response is derived for problem (

is exponential in time, and the Transfer Operator is introduced. In

3.1.
Exponential waveforms

In this section, we analyze the existence of the forced response of the system (
13
), when the applied current density
\mathbf{J}_{S}
is exponential in time, i.e. we assume

(37)

\mathbf{J}_{S}(\mathbf{x},t)=\mathbf{j}_{S}(\mathbf{x})e^{\lambda t},

where

15

16

Due to the linearity of the system, the forced response

\mathbf{J}_{F}(\mathbf{x},t)=\mathbf{j}(\mathbf{x})e^{\lambda t},

(40)

\left\langle\mathbf{w}_{c},\left(\eta\mathcal{I}+\lambda\mathcal{A}_{cc}\right)\mathbf{j}\right\rangle_{C}=-\lambda\langle\mathbf{w}_{c},\mathcal{A}_{cs}\mathbf{j}_{S}\rangle_{C},\ \forall\mathbf{w}^{\prime}_{c}\in H_{L}\left(\Omega_{C}\right).

The measured fields
\mathbf{A}_{R}
and

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}_{R}(\mathbf{x},t)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{a}_{R}(\mathbf{x})e^{\lambda t}

(42)

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{R}(\mathbf{x},t)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{e}_{R}(\mathbf{x})e^{\lambda t},

where, thanks to (

(43)

\displaystyle\langle\mathbf{e}_{R},\mathbf{w}_{s}\rangle_{S}

\displaystyle=-\lambda\langle\mathcal{A}_{sc}\mathbf{j},\mathbf{w}_{s}\rangle_{S},

(44)

\displaystyle\langle\mathbf{a}_{R},\mathbf{w}_{s}\rangle_{S}

\displaystyle=\langle\mathcal{A}_{sc}\mathbf{j},\mathbf{w}_{s}\rangle_{S}.

In operator form (
40

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{C}\left(\eta\mathcal{I}+\lambda\mathcal{A}_{cc}\right)\mathcal{P}_{C}\mathbf{j}

(46)

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{S}\mathbf{e}_{R}

\displaystyle=-\lambda\mathcal{P}_{S}\mathcal{A}_{sc}\mathbf{j},

(47)

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{S}\mathbf{a}_{R}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{P}_{S}\mathcal{A}_{sc}\mathbf{j}.

Therefore, if the operator appearing on the l.h.s. of (

) is invertible (see

(48)

\mathcal{P}_{S}\mathbf{e}_{R}=\mathcal{H}_{\eta}\mathbf{j}_{S},

where
\mathcal{H}_{\eta}:H_{L}\left(\Omega_{S}\right)\to H_{L}\left(\Omega_{S}\right)

\mathcal{H}_{\eta}=\lambda\mathcal{P}_{S}\mathcal{A}_{sc}\left[\mathcal{P}_{C}\left(\eta\mathcal{I}+\lambda\mathcal{A}_{cc}\right)\mathcal{P}_{C}\right]^{-1}\lambda\mathcal{P}_{C}\mathcal{A}_{cs}.

Combining (
34
), (
36
), (
42
), (
48
) and (

(50)

\mathcal{P}_{S}\mathbf{E}_{R}=\mathcal{O}\left(\mathrm{e}^{-t/\tau_{1}(\eta)}\right)+\mathcal{H}_{\eta}\mathbf{j}_{S}\,e^{\lambda t},\textrm{ for }t\rightarrow+\infty.

When
\lambda>-1/{\tau_{1}(\eta)}
, the second term on the r.h.s. of (

and can be experimentally measured.

3.2.
Laplace Transform

Taking the Laplace transform of (
13
), it results that

(51)

\langle\mathbf{w}^{\prime}_{c},\left(\eta\mathcal{I}+p\mathcal{A}_{cc}\right)\mathbf{J}(\cdot,p)\rangle_{C}=-p\langle\mathbf{w}^{\prime}_{c},\mathcal{A}_{cs}\left[\mathbf{J}_{S}(\cdot,p)\right]\rangle_{C},\ \forall\mathbf{w}^{\prime}_{c}\in H_{L}\left(\Omega\right),

(52)

\begin{split}\mathbf{J}(\cdot,p)&=\mathcal{L}\left[\mathbf{J}(\cdot,t)\right](p)\\
\mathbf{J}_{S}(\cdot,p)&=\mathcal{L}\left[\mathbf{J}_{S}(\cdot,t)\right](p),\\
\end{split}

being
\mathcal{L}
the Laplace transform operator, and

Similarly,

(53)

\displaystyle\langle\mathbf{E}_{R}(\cdot,p),\mathbf{w}_{s}\rangle_{S}

\displaystyle=-p\langle\mathcal{A}_{sc}\left[\mathbf{J}(\cdot,p)\right],\mathbf{w}_{s}\rangle_{S},

(54)

\displaystyle\langle\mathbf{A}_{R}(\cdot,p),\mathbf{w}_{s}\rangle_{S}

\displaystyle=\langle\mathcal{A}_{sc}\left[\mathbf{J}(\cdot,p)\right],\mathbf{w}_{s}\rangle_{S}.

Equations (

), and (

), (

)
and (

), respectively, for

. Therefore, the operator

defined in (

\mathbf{J}_{S}(\mathbf{x},p)\rightarrow\mathcal{P}_{S}\mathbf{E}_{R}(\mathbf{x},p)
evaluated at

. Hereafter we will use the symbol
\mathcal{H}_{\eta}
, for this operator, i.e.

(55)

\mathcal{H}_{\eta}:p\in\mathbb{C}\mapsto\mathcal{H}_{\eta}(p)\in\mathfrak{L}(H_{L}\left(\Omega_{S}\right),H_{L}\left(\Omega_{S})\right).

3.3.
Existence of

In this Section, we prove that the solution of (

\lambda
is larger than
-1/\tau_{1}(\eta)
where

Proposition 4
.

The operator
1

\mathcal{P}_{C}\eta\mathcal{P}_{C}

Lemma 5
.

For any

4.
Monotonicity of Transfer Function

In this section, it is proved that the operator
\eta\in L^{\infty}_{+}(\Omega_{C})\rightarrow\mathcal{H}_{\eta}(-1/\tau)
, mapping the material property
\eta
to
\mathcal{H}_{\eta}
, satisfies the Monotonicity Principle for
\tau
negative or greater than a proper positive constant. The subscript
\eta
in
\mathcal{H}_{\eta}
makes explicit the specific electrical resistivity on which it is evaluated (
49
).

We recall that:

(a)

\eta_{1}\leq\eta_{2}
means that

\eta_{1}(x)\leq\eta_{2}(x)\text{ for a.e. }x\in\Omega_{C}
;

(b)

\mathcal{A}\preceq\mathcal{B}
means that
\mathcal{B}-\mathcal{A}
is a positive semi-definite operator.

The main Theorem relies on the two following Lemmas.

Lemma 10
.

The mapping
\eta\in L^{\infty}\left(\Omega_{C}\right)\mapsto\mathcal{P}_{C}\eta\mathcal{P}_{C}
is monotone, that is,

(66)

\eta_{1}\leq\eta_{2}\Longrightarrow\mathcal{P}_{C}\eta_{1}\mathcal{P}_{C}\preceq\mathcal{P}_{C}\eta_{2}\mathcal{P}_{C}.

Proof.

The proof is trivial. Indeed, if
\eta_{1}(x)\leq\eta_{2}(x)\text{ for a.e. }x\in\Omega_{C}
, then it follows that

(67)

\displaystyle\left\langle\mathbf{w}_{c},\eta_{1}\mathbf{w}_{c}\right\rangle_{C}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega_{C}}\eta_{1}\left|\mathbf{w}_{c}(x)\right|^{2}\text{d}V(x)

(68)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Omega_{C}}\eta_{2}\left|\mathbf{w}_{c}(x)\right|^{2}\text{d}V(x)

(69)

\displaystyle=\left\langle\mathbf{w}_{c},\eta_{2}\mathbf{w}_{c}\right\rangle_{C},\ \forall\mathbf{w}_{c}\in H_{L}\left(\Omega_{C}\right).

∎

Lemma 11
.

Given two linear, invertible and symmetric operators
\mathcal{A}_{1}
and
\mathcal{A}_{2}
defined on the space
X
, an operator
\mathcal{C}
defined on the space
X
, and a linear operator
\mathcal{B}
defined from the space
Y
to the space
X
, it turns out that:

(a)

\mathcal{A}_{1}\preceq\mathcal{A}_{2}\Longleftrightarrow\mathcal{A}_{2}^{-1}\succeq\mathcal{A}_{1}^{-1}
;

(b)

\mathcal{A}_{1}\preceq\mathcal{A}_{2}\Longleftrightarrow\mathcal{A}_{1}+\mathcal{C}\preceq\mathcal{A}_{2}+\mathcal{C}
;

(c)

\mathcal{A}_{1}\preceq\mathcal{A}_{2}\Longrightarrow\mathcal{B}^{{\dagger}}\mathcal{A}_{1}\mathcal{B}\preceq\mathcal{B}^{{\dagger}}\mathcal{A}_{2}\mathcal{B}
.

Proof.

The proofs are trivial.
∎

Hereafter, for the sake of simplicity, we define
\lambda_{1}(\eta)=-\tau_{1}^{-1}(\eta)
. It is worth noting that
\lambda_{1}(\eta)
is a pole of the Transfer Operator
\mathcal{H}_{\eta}
. In general,
-\tau_{k}^{-1}(\eta)
is a pole for
\mathcal{H}_{\eta}
, for arbitrary
k
.

The Monotonicity Principle for the Transfer Operator
\mathcal{H}_{\eta}
evaluated on the real axis is stated below.

Theorem 12
.

Let
\alpha,\beta\in L_{+}^{\infty}(\Omega_{C})
be two electrical resistivities, and let
\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}
and
\mathcal{H}_{\beta}
be defined as in (
49
), then

(70)

\alpha\leq\beta\implies\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}\left(\lambda\right)\preceq\mathcal{H}_{\beta}\left(\lambda\right),\ \forall\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\textrm{ and }\lambda>\max\{\lambda_{1}(\alpha),\lambda_{1}(\beta)\}.

Proof.

First, the Transfer Operators
\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}(\lambda)
and
\mathcal{H}_{\beta}(\lambda)
are well defined for
\lambda>\max\{\lambda_{1}(\alpha),\lambda_{1}(\beta)\}
, thanks to
Theorem

9
.
Then
\alpha\leq\beta
implies
\mathcal{P}_{C}\alpha\mathcal{P}_{C}\preceq\mathcal{P}_{C}\beta\mathcal{P}_{C}
, thanks to
Lemma

10
. Then, it results that

(71)

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{C}\alpha\mathcal{P}_{C}\preceq\mathcal{P}_{C}\beta\mathcal{P}_{C}\Longleftrightarrow\

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{C}(\alpha\mathcal{I}+\lambda\mathcal{A}_{cc})\mathcal{P}_{C}\preceq\mathcal{P}_{C}(\beta\mathcal{I}+\lambda\mathcal{A}_{cc})\mathcal{P}_{C}

(72)

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\

\displaystyle\left[\mathcal{P}_{C}(\alpha\mathcal{I}+\lambda\mathcal{A}_{cc})\mathcal{P}_{C}\right]^{-1}\succeq\left[\mathcal{P}_{C}(\beta\mathcal{I}+\lambda\mathcal{A}_{cc})\mathcal{P}_{C}\right]^{-1},

where we have exploited (a) and (b) from
Lemma

11
. Finally, (
70
) follows from (
72
) combined with (c) from
Lemma

11
, for
\mathcal{A}_{1}=\left[\mathcal{P}_{C}(\beta\mathcal{I}+\lambda\mathcal{A}_{cc})\mathcal{P}_{C}\right]^{-1}
,
\mathcal{A}_{2}=\left[\mathcal{P}_{C}(\alpha\mathcal{I}+\lambda\mathcal{A}_{cc})\mathcal{P}_{C}\right]^{-1}
, and
\mathcal{B}=-\lambda\mathcal{P}_{C}\mathcal{A}_{cs}
.

∎

5.
A Non-Iterative Imaging Method Based on Monotonicity

This section shows how to convert the MP of
Theorem

12
into an imaging method. Conversions of this type (from an MP to an imaging method) have been known since the paper
[
47
]
. Hereafter, it is provided for the sake of completeness and with reference to an inverse obstacle problem consisting in the reconstruction of the shape of a conductive inclusion embedded in a conductive material
\Omega_{C}
.

Let
A\subset\Omega_{C}
be the region occupied by an inclusion with electrical resistivity
\eta_{i}
embedded in a conductive background that occupies the region
\Omega_{C}
. The electrical conductivity of the background is indicated as
\eta_{BG}
. The overall electrical resistivity is

(73)

\eta_{A}(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}\eta_{i}&\hbox{ in }&A,\\
\eta_{BG}&\hbox{ in }&\Omega_{C}\setminus A.\end{array}\right.

Hereafter, it is assumed that

(74)

\eta_{i}>\eta_{0}.

It is easy to observe that if
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
are two possible inclusions in
\Omega_{C}
, then (
73
) and (
74
) imply that

(75)

A_{1}\subset A_{2}\implies\eta_{A_{1}}\leq\eta_{A_{2}}.

Theorem

12
together with equation (
75
) provides the monotonicity of the Transfer Operator for the inverse obstacle problem, as started below.

Theorem 13
.

Let
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
be two inclusions embedded in the conducting material
\Omega_{C}
. Under assumptions (
73
) and (
74
), it follows that

(76)

A_{1}\subset A_{2}\implies\mathcal{H}_{A_{1}}\left(\lambda\right)\preceq\mathcal{H}_{A_{2}}\left(\lambda\right),\ \forall\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\textrm{ and }\lambda>\max\{\lambda_{1}(\eta_{A_{1}}),\lambda_{1}(\eta_{A_{2}})\}.

Imaging the shape of an anomaly via the Monotonicity Principle can be achieved as originally proposed in
[
47
]
. Specifically, the following proposition holds.

Proposition 14
.

Let
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
be two inclusions embedded in the conducting material
\Omega_{C}
. Under assumptions (
73
) and (
74
). If there exists a real number
\lambda
greater than
\max\{\lambda_{1}(\eta_{A_{1}}),\lambda_{1}(\eta_{A_{1}})\}
such that

\mathcal{H}_{A_{1}}\left(\lambda\right)\not\preceq\mathcal{H}_{A_{2}}\left(\lambda\right),

then
Theorem

13
implies that

A_{1}\not\subset A_{2}.

The concept of monotonicity in transfer functions allows the development of an imaging algorithm that does not require iterative calculations. Hereafter, the key steps of the method are summarized for completeness.

First, let
\Omega_{C}
be covered by means of a set of “small” subdomains called test elements, i.e.,
\Omega_{C}=\{T_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N}
. Second, check via
Proposition

14
if a given test domain
T_{j}
is suspected not to be completely included in the unknown anomaly
A\subset\Omega_{C}
. If there exists a
\lambda
such that
\mathcal{H}_{T_{j}}\left(\lambda\right)\not\preceq\mathcal{H}_{A}\left(\lambda\right)
, then discard the test domain
T_{j}
.
Third, take as a reconstruction of
U
the union of those test domains that have not been discarded during the second step.

Summing up, the reconstruction rule is

(77)

A^{U}=\bigcup_{j}\left\{T_{j}\ |\ \mathcal{H}_{T_{j}}(\lambda)\preceq\mathcal{H}_{A}(\lambda),\ \forall\lambda>\lambda_{j}\right\},

where
\lambda_{j}=\max\{\lambda_{1}\left(T_{j}\right),\lambda_{1}\left(A\right)\}
is a proper threshold.

When the unknown anomaly
A
is the union of some or all
T_{j}
s, then
A^{U}
is a rigorous upper bound:

(78)

A\subseteq A^{U}\subseteq A^{U}\left(\lambda^{*}\right),

where
A^{U}\left(\lambda^{*}\right)
is the estimated reconstruction at the prescribed parameter
\lambda^{*}
, i.e.,

(79)

A^{U}\left(\lambda^{*}\right)=\bigcup_{j}\left\{T_{j}\ |\ \mathcal{H}_{T_{j}}(\lambda^{*})\preceq\mathcal{H}_{A}(\lambda^{*})\right\}.

There is another way to estimate
A
using the same principle, but focusing on the search for the test elements that contain the true anomaly
A
. Specifically, this reconstruction rule is

(80)

A^{L}=\bigcap_{j}\left\{T_{j}\ |\ \mathcal{H}_{T_{j}}(\lambda)\succeq\mathcal{H}_{A}(\lambda),\ \forall\lambda>\lambda_{j}\right\}.

In other words, the final reconstruction of
A
is obtained by finding the common regions between all the test elements that might contain
A
.

When the unknown anomaly
A
is the intersection of some of the
T_{j}
s, then
A^{L}
is a rigorous lower bound:

(81)

A^{L}\left(\lambda^{*}\right)\subseteq A^{L}\subseteq A,

where
A^{L}\left(\lambda^{*}\right)
is the estimated reconstruction at the prescribed parameter
\lambda^{*}
, i.e.,

(82)

A^{L}\left(\lambda^{*}\right)=\bigcap_{j}\left\{T_{j}\ |\ \mathcal{H}_{T_{j}}(\lambda^{*})\succeq\mathcal{H}_{A}(\lambda^{*})\right\}.

6.
Conclusions

In this paper, the inverse obstacle problem is studied within the framework of Magnetic Induction Tomography, which involves the detection of small defects in a conducting material using low-frequency electromagnetic fields. From the mathematical point of view, this is a non-linear and ill-posed inverse problem, in which the underlying physics is governed by a parabolic PDE.

The proposed strategy is based on the search for a Monotonicity Property that, when available, can be translated into a non-iterative imaging method. In this setting (parabolic PDE), it is very challenging to recognize the proper operator showing the Monotonicity Property and to prove the related monotonicity. The main contribution of this study is that a Monotonicity Property for parabolic PDEs emerges when considering the Laplace transforms of a proper quantity that can be measured, the Transfer Operator
\mathcal{H}_{\eta}
. Specifically, the introduction of the Laplace transform, by hiding the temporal dependence of the problem, naturally gives rise to the Transfer Operator
\mathcal{H}_{\eta}
, which satisfies a proper Monotonicity Principle:

(83)

\alpha\leq\beta\implies\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}\left(\lambda\right)\preceq\mathcal{H}_{\beta}\left(\lambda\right).

Subsequently, an imaging method based on this Monotonicity Principle provides the solution for the inverse obstacle problem in real-time operations.
