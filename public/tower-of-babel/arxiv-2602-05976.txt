The Signed Wasserstein Barycenter Problem
Matt Jacobs majaco@ucsb.edu, Bohan Zhou bhzhou@ucsb.edu

Abstract
Barycenter problems encode important geometric information about a metric space. While these problems are typically studied with positive weight coefficients associated to each distance term, more general signed Wasserstein barycenter problems have recently drawn a great deal of interest. These mixed sign problems have appeared in statistical inference setting as a way to generalize least squares regression to measure valued outputs and have appeared in numerical methods to improve the accuracy of Wasserstein gradient flow solvers. Unfortunately, the presence of negatively weighted distance terms destroys the Euclidean convexity of the unsigned problem, resulting in a much more challenging optimization task. The main focus of this work is to study properties of the signed barycenter problem for a general transport cost with a focus on establishing uniqueness of solutions. In particular, when there is only one positive weight, we extend the uniqueness result of 58 to any cost satisfying a certain convexity property. In the case of arbitrary weights, we introduce the dual problem in terms of Kantorovich potentials and provide a sufficient condition for a stationary solution of the dual problem to induce an optimal signed barycenter.

Introduction

In Euclidean geometry, affine combinations provide a natural mechanism to interpolate and extrapolate between points. The collection of all such combinations, the affine span, is rooted in several fundamental ideas in linear algebra such as the basis and dimension. A natural question arises if one could consider an analogous concept in the space of probability measures. Given a collection of probability measures
\{\mu_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
supported on a open subset
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and an affine combination of weights
\{a_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}\subset\mathbb{R}
such that
\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}=1
, we define the signed barycenter as a probability measure that minimizes the weighted transport cost:

\bar{\nu}\in\argmin_{\nu}\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}K_{c}(\mu_{i},\nu),

where
K_{c}(\mu_{i},\nu)
represents the cost of transporting input probability measure
\mu_{i}
to another probability measure
\nu
, subject to a ground cost function
c\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
. When all weights
\{a_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
are nonnegative and
K_{c}(\mu_{i},\nu)=\frac{1}{2}W_{2}^{2}(\mu_{i},\nu)
, where
W_{2}(\cdot,\cdot)
is the quadratic Wasserstein distance, the minimizer
\bar{\nu}
is known as the
Wasserstein barycenter

(
1
)
. Allowing negative weights leads to a natural extension under the name
generalized Wasserstein barycenter
studies by
58

(

;
19

. To avoid ambiguity, we adopt the term
signed barycenter
to refer to the minimizer of the above problem.

The seminar work by
1

It provides a notion of a “weighted average” of input probability measures through a nonlinear interpolation of measures, shedding light on analyzing one kind of Fréchet mean of input data, which arises naturally from a broad range of mathematical and statistical research. Owing to its central role as a notion of mean, it invites numerous applications, including matching problems
(

, texture mixing
(

, shape interpolation
(

(

(

(

(

Despite its broad applicability, the classical Wasserstein barycenter is defined through convex combinations and is therefore inherently limited to interpolation. This restriction precludes the study of extrapolation and the affine span in the space of probability measures. Negative coefficients occur naturally in many applications. For example, in
(
33
)
, the counterfactual quantile function of incomes in a treated state following a minimum-wage policy change is constructed using quantile functions from control states. From an economic perspective, the contribution of a control state can exhibit negative correlations. Moreover, the conditional Wasserstein barycenter problem studied in
(

One may also regard the signed barycenter as a map from input measures and weights

(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{m};a_{1},\ldots,a_{m})\mapsto\bar{\nu}=\Gamma_{\textbf{a}}(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{m})

into a response measure. This can be interpreted as a form of parametric statistical inference in the space of probability measures, where both predictor variables
(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{m})
and response variable
\bar{\nu}
are distributions. Statistical inference models where both the sample and parameter spaces are functional spaces, rather than Euclidean, have gained increasing attention with the rise of functional data analysis. The distribution-to-distribution regression, as one way to infer the distributional response from distributional predictor, becomes canonical since the introduction of the Wasserstein metric. See

Here we focus on the case when the space of distributions are endowed with the Wasserstein metric. Works in

Therefore these models are essentially generalized from the classical simple linear regression and transformed into a function-to-function regression by the optimal transport maps. While recent works
(

have extended to multivariate distributions, with Gaussian distribution as a tractable special case. Such extensions typically rely on the existence of optimal transport maps, and often on their explicit forms. By contrast, a multiple multivariate regression built on the signed barycenter problem does not require the corresponding reformulation into a function-to-function regression. To the best of our knowledge, this perspective has not been systematically explored in the existing literature, although a thorough statistical analysis is left for future study.

Our model is also a key component in the high order scheme for the Wasserstein gradient flow. The renowned JKO scheme

Main Assumptions.

Throughout this work, we assume that
\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
are open sets and the cost function
c\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\to[0,\infty)
satisfies the following properties.

1.

c\in C^{2}(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})
and is bi-twisted, i.e.,
y\mapsto\nabla_{x}c(x,y)
and
x\mapsto\nabla_{y}c(x,y)
are injective.

2.

The sets
\left\{x\in\mathcal{X}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}c(x,y^{\prime})\leqslant b\right\}
and
\left\{y\in\mathcal{Y}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}c(x^{\prime},y)\leqslant b\right\}
are compact for any
b\in\mathbb{R}
and
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
.

3.

There exists a point
(x_{0},y_{0})\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
such that for any
\varepsilon>0
there exists
B_{\varepsilon}>0
such that

(1-\varepsilon)c(x_{0},y)-B_{\varepsilon}c(x,y_{0})\leqslant c(x,y)\leqslant(1+\varepsilon)c(x_{0},y)+B_{\varepsilon}c(x,y_{0})

(1)

and

(1-\varepsilon)c(x,y_{0})-B_{\varepsilon}c(x_{0},y)\leqslant c(x,y)\leqslant(1+\varepsilon)c(x,y_{0})+B_{\varepsilon}c(x_{0},y)

(2)

for all
(x,y)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
.

In particular, we may pick
c(x,y)=\mathinner{\!\left\lvert x-y\right\rvert}^{p}
when
\mathcal{X}=\mathcal{Y}=(\mathbb{R}^{d},{\left\lVert{\cdot}\right\rVert}_{p})
.

From the above assumptions, for
(x_{0},y_{0})\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
identified in Assumption
3
, it is natural to define the spaces

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\textup{cost}}(\mathcal{X})

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\left\{\mu\in\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{X})\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\int_{\mathcal{X}}c(x,y_{0})\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)<\infty\right\},

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\textup{cost}}(\mathcal{Y})

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\left\{\nu\in\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{Y})\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\int_{\mathcal{X}}c(x_{0},y)\,\mathrm{d}\nu(y)<\infty\right\},

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\textup{cost}}(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\left\{\pi\in\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\int_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}}\big(c(x,y_{0})+c(x_{0},y)\big)\,\mathrm{d}\pi(x,y)<\infty\right\}.

Readers may recall that these spaces impose finiteness requirements, analogous to those in
8
.

Main Results

In this paper, we study (empirical) signed barycenter problems from both primal approach in the space of probability measures, and dual approach in the space of potential functions.

Primal approach
Under our main assumptions on the cost function
c
and sets
\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}
, we establish in
Theorem

7
the existence of the signed barycenter, which generalizes the existence result of
58
that only considered the quadratic cost. We then analyze properties of the signed barycenter functional on the space of probability measures. The presence of negative weights fundamentally change the nature of the problem: the functional is no longer Euclidean convex, and, the signed barycenter need not be absolutely continuous, as observed in
58
. Furthermore, for general costs, we also lose the tool of
\lambda
-convexity along generalized geodesic that plays a crucial role in previous literature (see for example
23
;
24
;
58
) for the quadratic cost.

These obstacles sum up and motivate a dual approach. Nevertheless, a notable exception arises when the barycenter functional involves exactly one positive weight. In this setting, we prove in
Theorem

15
that, if there is a convex interpolation for the cost function, then there exists an explicit continuous interpolating curve in the space of probability measures, such that the barycenter functional is convex along this curve. This result does not require that the transport cost induces a metric for the space of probability measures, and extends the
\lambda
-convexity of the barycenter functional along generalized geodesics under the quadratic cost: the case of one positive and one negative weight are studied in
24
, and the case of one positive and multiple negative weights in
58
.

Dual approach
We propose a new dual formulation for the signed barycenter problem in a general setting, allowing a general cost function and more than two inout measures. This approach starts from a new treatment on the congruence condition (see
Proposition

14
) satisfied by dual potentials. In contrast to Toland’s duality used in
23
;
24
for the Wasserstein extrapolation, i.e., the difference between two 2-Wasserstein terms, their duality modifies Brenier’s theorem to impose a strong convexity constraint on the Kantorovich potentials.

When at least one input marginal with positive weight is absolutely continuous, the resulting dual problem takes the form of a min-max problem, due to the presence of negative weights. We study the dual functional, and its stationary and saddle points. By the convex-concave property, we characterize the signed barycenter in terms of saddle points in
Theorem

21
. The uniqueness of the signed barycenter follows up in
Theorem

22
, under additional absolutely continuous marginal associated with a positive weight. From computational perspective, a stationary point is generally more tractable in the min-max problem. We therefore propose a sufficient condition for global optimality in
Theorem

25
, paving avenue to future numerical computation of the signed barycenter. This dual framework have been applied for computing the classical barycenter
(
39
)
as well.

2.2.1
Primal problem and dual problem

Given two probability measures
\mu,\nu

\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}

3.1
Properties of the barycenter functional

Now that we have demonstrated the existence of minimizers, we turn to the task of characterizing the minimizers by studying various notions of differentibility and critical points. As is standard in optimal transport, there are at least two distinct and useful notions of paths between measures. The simplest paths are those given by convex interpolation, namely if one has measures
\nu_{0},\nu_{1}\in\mathbb{P}_{\textup{cost}}(\mathcal{Y})
, we can consider the interpolation
\nu_{t}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=t\nu_{1}+(1-t)\nu_{0}
. On the other hand, we can also consider paths along generalized geodesics with respect to a base point in
\mathbb{P}_{\textup{cost}}(\mathcal{Y})
. Namely, if one has an abstract probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{M},\rho)
, with sufficiently rich structure, we can interpolate between
\nu_{0}
and
\nu_{1}
by finding a curve
Y\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}[0,1]\times\Omega\to\mathcal{Y}

such that
t\mapsto Y(t,z)
is
C^{1}
and
\nu_{t}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}={Y_{t}}_{\#}\rho
matches the endpoints at
t=0,1
. Since there is no natural choice of a distinguished basepoint for the general form of our problem, when considering generalized geodesics we will work with the abstract space
(\Omega,\mathcal{M},\rho)
. However, we will see that when at least one of the measures
\mu_{i}
is absolutely continuous, we can choose it instead as the basepoint and obtain a sharper characterization of differentiability properties along generalized geodesics.

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\mathscr{B}(\nu_{t_{n}^{-}})-\mathscr{B}(\nu)}{t_{n}^{-}}

3.2
The special case of only one positive weight

We now turn our attention to the special case where only one of the weights is positive, i.e.,
I_{+}=\{1\}
. In this case we can guarantee convexity and uniqueness properties of the signed barycenter problem, as long as the cost function satisfies a certain convexity condition. This generalizes the result of
58
for the quadratic cost only.

When there is only one positive weight and the cost is quadratic, this special case has already been noted in prior work
(
24
;
58
)
. As explained in
24
, an advantage in this setting is that
\mathscr{B}(\nu)
is 1-convex along generalized geodesics with base measure
\mu_{1}
. This follows from
6
, which implies that
\frac{a_{1}}{2}W_{2}^{2}(\cdot,\mu_{1})
is
a_{1}
-convex along generalized geodesics with base measure
\mu_{1}
and
-\frac{\mathinner{\!\left\lvert a_{i}\right\rvert}}{2}W_{2}^{2}(\cdot,\mu_{i})
is
(-\mathinner{\!\left\lvert a_{i}\right\rvert})
-convex along generalized geodesic with any base measure.

Theorem 15
.

If there exists a continuous function
\theta\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}^{2}\times[0,1]\to\mathcal{Y}
such that
\theta(x_{1},y_{0},y_{1},0)=y_{0}
,
\theta(x_{1},y_{0},y_{1},1)=y_{1}
, and for all
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m},y_{0},y_{1})\in\mathcal{X}^{m}\times\mathcal{Y}^{2}

t\mapsto\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}c\big(x_{i},\theta(x_{1},y_{0},y_{1},t)\big)

(11)

is (strictly) convex, then for any
\nu_{0},\nu_{1}\in\mathbb{P}_{\textup{cost}}(\mathcal{Y})
there exists an explicit continuous interpolating curve
\nu_{t}
such that

t\mapsto\mathscr{B}(\nu_{t})

is (strictly) convex. Consequently, if
t\mapsto\mathscr{B}(\nu_{t})
is strictly convex, then the signed barycenter is unique.

Remark 16
.

When
c
is the quadratic cost or other costs based on the Euclidean distance, it is most natural to choose the standard convex interpolation
\theta(x_{1},y_{0},y_{1},t)=ty_{1}+(1-t)y_{0}
. In particular, the standard convex interpolation in the quadratic cost leads to the fact that

is strictly convex. This recovers the generalized geodesic convexity and uniqueness results from
58

However, for general costs, often the most natural choice is the family of curves satisfying the equation

\nabla_{x}c\big(x_{1},\theta(x_{1},y_{0},y_{1},t)\big)=t\nabla_{x}c(x_{1},y_{1})+(1-t)\nabla_{x}c(x_{1},y_{0}),

as these are the curves that induce generalized geodesics between probability measures with respect to the cost
c
.

Proof.

For
j=0,1
, let
\pi^{j}\in\Pi(\mu_{1},\nu_{j})
be the optimal plan transporting
\mu_{1}
to
\nu_{j}
. By the Gluing Lemma there exists a measure
\gamma\in\Pi(\mu_{1},\nu_{0},\nu_{1})
such that
P^{0}_{\#}\gamma=\pi^{0}
and
P^{1}_{\#}\gamma=\pi^{1}
where
P^{j}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}^{2}\to\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
is the projection map
P^{j}(x_{1},y_{0},y_{1})=(x_{1},y_{j})
for
j\in\{0,1\}
. We then define two maps

and use them to define pushforward measures.

Define
\nu_{t}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=Q_{\theta,t\,\#}\gamma
. Every coupling
\tilde{\pi}\in\Pi(\mu_{i},\nu_{t})
can be represented as
\tilde{\pi}=\tilde{Q}_{\theta,t\,\#}\Gamma_{i}
for some

. Therefore, for any

Since we can glue each of these measures along

, it follows that

\sum_{i=2}^{m}|a_{i}|K_{c}(\mu_{i},\nu_{t})=\inf_{\Gamma\in\Pi(\mu_{2},\ldots,\mu_{m},\gamma)}\int_{X^{m}\times\mathcal{Y}^{2}}\sum_{i=2}^{m}|a_{i}|c\big(x_{i},\theta(x_{1},y_{0},y_{1},t)\big)\,\mathrm{d}\Gamma.

(12)

Next, we observe that

K_{c}(\mu_{1},\nu_{t})\leqslant\int_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}^{2}}c\big(x_{1},\theta(x_{1},y_{0},y_{1},t)\big)\,\mathrm{d}\gamma(x_{1},y_{0},y_{1})

(13)

Thus, combining (
12
) and (
13
) and the convexity of the curve

, we obtain the inequality

\displaystyle\mathscr{B}(\nu_{t})

\displaystyle\leqslant\sup_{\Gamma\in\Pi(\mu_{2},\ldots,\mu_{m},\gamma)}\int_{\mathcal{X}^{m}\times\mathcal{Y}^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}c\big(x_{i},\theta(x_{1},y_{0},y_{1},t)\big)\,\mathrm{d}\Gamma(x_{2},\ldots,x_{m},x_{1},y_{0},y_{1})

Finally, if (

for all

and

there must exist some

such that

∎

4
The Dual problem

In this section, we develop a new dual formulation to the signed barycenter problem (
9
), which is different from the multimarginal approach
(
1
;
26
)
. As in the classical OT, the dual formulation explores the optimization over the space of functions, which is typically more tractable analytically. The numerical solver based on the dual formulation is more practical and stable, see
36
.

Since we have seen the special case where only one of the weights is positive in
Section

3.2
, we will assume throughout this section there are at least two marginals associated with positive weights, i.e.,
I_{+}\setminus\{1\}\neq\emptyset
. We also assume that at least one marginal associated with a
positive weight
is absolutely continuous, and let
\mu_{1}\in\mathbb{P}_{\textup{cost}}^{\textup{AC}}(\mathcal{X})
without loss of generality.

From
Section

2.2.2
, we may replace each term in (
9
) by its own dual formulation:

\displaystyle a_{i}K_{c}(\mu_{i},\nu)=\sup_{f_{i}}a_{i}[\int_{\mathcal{X}}f^{c}_{i}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{i}+\int_{\mathcal{Y}}f_{i}\,\mathrm{d}\nu],\quad\textup{for\penalty\ }i\in I_{+};

\displaystyle a_{i}K_{c}(\mu_{i},\nu)=\inf_{f_{i}}a_{i}[\int_{\mathcal{X}}f^{c}_{i}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{i}+\int_{\mathcal{Y}}f_{i}\,\mathrm{d}\nu],\quad\textup{for\penalty\ }i\in I_{-}.

Here optimizations are over
c
-concave functions. For the remainder of this paper, all dual formulations are assumed to be restricted to
c
-concave functions. Potentials
f_{i}
for

are referred as maximizing variables, and
f_{i}
for

are minimizing variables. We also denote the tuple of dual potentials by

. The optimizers
\bar{f}_{[I]}=(\bar{f}_{1},\ldots,\bar{f}_{m})
are referred as (optimal)

\nu
to
\mu_{i}

We have seen these Kantorovich potentials satisfy a balance equation in

To study the signed barycenter problem, the first concern is whether the strong duality

\mathscr{D}
if

\sup_{f_{[I_{+}\setminus\{1\}]}}\inf_{f_{[I_{-}]}}\mathscr{D}(f)=\inf_{f_{[I_{-}]}}\sup_{f_{[I_{+}\setminus\{1\}]}}\mathscr{D}(f)=\mathscr{D}(\bar{f}_{[I_{+}\setminus\{1\}]},\bar{f}_{[I_{-}]}).

Any saddle point is a stationary point by definition.

We consider the linearized functional of
\mathscr{D}
at
\bar{f}_{[I]}
, defined by

\overline{\mathscr{D}}(f_{[I\setminus\{1\}]};\bar{f})=\sum_{i\in I\setminus\{1\}}\int_{\mathcal{X}}a_{i}f_{i}^{c}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{i}+\int_{\mathcal{X}}a_{1}\bar{f}_{1}^{c}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{1}+a_{1}\int_{\mathcal{Y}}(\bar{f}_{1}-f_{1})\,\mathrm{d}\bar{\nu}.

(20)

where
\bar{f}_{1},f_{1}
are redundant potentials as in (
16
) and
\bar{\nu}={\left(T_{\bar{f}^{c}_{1}}\right)}_{\#}\mu_{1}
as in (
18
). Apparently,
\overline{\mathscr{D}}(\bar{f}_{[I\setminus\{1\}]};\bar{f})=\mathscr{D}(\bar{f}_{[I\setminus\{1\}]})
. Notably,
\overline{\mathscr{D}}(f;\bar{f})
is convex w.r.t minimizing variables and concave w.r.t maximizing variables, thanks to
Lemma

5
. This facilitates us to characterize the stationary points to
\mathscr{D}
via the characterization of the saddle points to
\overline{\mathscr{D}}
.

Proposition 19
.

\bar{f}_{[I\setminus\{1\}]}
is a saddle point to
\overline{\mathscr{D}}(f_{[I\setminus\{1\}]};\bar{f})
if and only if for every
i\in I\setminus\{1\}
, there exists
\pi_{i}\in\Pi(\mu_{i},\bar{\nu})
such that

\pi_{i}(x_{i},y)(\bar{f}^{c}_{i}(x_{i})+\bar{f}_{i}(y)-c(x_{i},y))=0.

(21)

Furthermore, for every
i\in I

K_{c}(\mu_{i},\bar{\nu})=\int_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}}c(x_{i},y)\,\mathrm{d}\pi_{i}(x_{i},y)=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\bar{f}^{c}_{i}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{i}+\int_{\mathcal{Y}}\bar{f}_{i}\,\mathrm{d}\bar{\nu}=D_{c}(\mu_{i},\bar{\nu}).

(22)

Proposition 20
.

\bar{f}_{[I\setminus\{1\}]}
is a stationary point to
\mathscr{D}(f)
if and only if
\bar{f}_{[I\setminus\{1\}]}
is a saddle point to
\overline{\mathscr{D}}(f_{[I\setminus\{1\}]};\bar{f})
.

Combine
Proposition

19
and
Proposition

20
, the stationary point
\bar{f}_{[I\setminus\{1\}]}
to
\mathscr{D}
satisfies (
21
) and (
22
). Consequently,
\mathscr{D}(\bar{f})=\mathscr{B}(\bar{\nu})
. However, it is still not sufficient to show the strong duality (
17
), since we do not yet know if
\inf\sup\mathscr{D}(f)=\mathscr{D}(\bar{f})
.

4.2
Properties of saddle points

In this section, we will show how to construct the signed barycenter, from the saddle point of the dual problem, thereby ensuring the strong duality
\inf\mathscr{B}(\nu)=\sup\inf\mathscr{D}(f)
. And we also propose sufficient conditions for the uniqueness of the signed barycenter.

Theorem 21

(Any saddle point induces a signed barycenter)
.

Suppose
\mu_{1}\in\mathbb{P}_{\textup{cost}}^{\textup{AC}}(\mathcal{X})
and
\bar{f}_{[I\setminus\{1\}]}
is a
c
-concave saddle point to
\mathscr{D}(f)
. Then

\bar{\nu}={T_{(-\sum_{i=2}^{m}\frac{a_{i}}{a_{1}}\bar{f}_{i})^{c}}}_{\#}\mu_{1}
defined in (
18
) is a signed barycenter to (
9
), and the strong duality (
17
) holds. Furthermore, for any marginal
\mu_{i}\in\mathbb{P}_{\textup{cost}}^{\textrm{AC}}(\mathcal{X})
,
\bar{\nu}={T_{\bar{f}_{i}^{c}}}_{\#}\mu_{i}
.

Proof.

Apply
Lemma

17
,

\displaystyle\mathscr{B}(\bar{\nu})

\displaystyle\geqslant\inf_{\nu}\mathscr{B}(\nu)=\inf_{\nu}\inf_{f_{[I_{-}]}}\sup_{f_{[I_{+}\setminus\{1\}]}}a_{1}K_{c}(\mu_{1},\nu)+\sum_{i=2}^{m}a_{i}\int_{\mathcal{X}}f_{i}^{c}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{i}+\int_{\mathcal{Y}}(\sum_{i=2}^{m}a_{i}f_{i})\,\mathrm{d}\nu

\displaystyle\geqslant\sup_{f_{[I_{+}\setminus\{1\}]}}\inf_{f_{[I_{-}]}}\mathscr{D}(f)=\mathscr{D}(\bar{f}_{[I_{+}\setminus\{1\}]},\bar{f}_{[I_{-}]})=\sum_{i=2}^{m}\int_{\mathcal{X}}a_{i}\bar{f}^{c}_{i}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{i}+\int_{\mathcal{X}}a_{1}\bar{f}_{1}^{c}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{1}.

The saddle point
\bar{f}_{[I\setminus\{1\}]}
is also a stationary point, thanks to the characterization of stationary points to
\mathscr{D}(f)
in
Section

4.1
, from (
22
) we have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}}c(x_{i},y)\,\mathrm{d}\pi_{i}(x_{i},y)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\bar{f}^{c}_{i}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{i}+\int_{\mathcal{Y}}\bar{f}_{i}\,\mathrm{d}\bar{\nu};

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}}c(x_{1},y)\,\mathrm{d}\pi_{1}(x_{1},y)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\bar{f}^{c}_{1}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{1}+\int_{\mathcal{Y}}\bar{f}_{1}\,\mathrm{d}\bar{\nu}.

where
\pi_{i}\in\Pi(\mu_{i},\bar{\nu})
is optimal. Plug in these to continue the above inequality,

\displaystyle\mathscr{B}(\bar{\nu})

\displaystyle\geqslant\sum_{i=2}^{m}\int_{\mathcal{X}}a_{i}\bar{f}^{c}_{i}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{i}+\int_{\mathcal{X}}a_{1}\bar{f}_{1}^{c}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{1}+a_{1}[\int_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}}c(x_{1},y)\,\mathrm{d}\pi_{1}(x_{1},y)-\int_{\mathcal{X}}\bar{f}^{c}_{1}\mathrm{d}\mu_{1}-\int_{\mathcal{Y}}\bar{f}_{1}\mathrm{d}\bar{\nu}]

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}}a_{1}c(x_{1},y)\,\mathrm{d}\pi_{1}(x_{1},y)+\sum_{i=2}^{m}\int_{\mathcal{X}}a_{i}\bar{f}^{c}_{i}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{i}-a_{1}\int_{\mathcal{Y}}(-\sum_{i=2}^{m}\frac{a_{i}}{a_{1}}\bar{f}_{i})\,\mathrm{d}\bar{\nu}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}}a_{1}c(x_{1},y)\,\mathrm{d}\pi_{1}(x_{1},y)+\sum_{i=2}^{m}\int_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}}a_{i}c(x_{i},y)\,\mathrm{d}\pi_{i}(x_{i},y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}K_{c}(\mu_{i},\bar{\nu})=\mathscr{B}(\bar{\nu}),

which completes the strong duality, i.e.,
\inf\mathscr{B}(v)=\sup\inf\mathscr{D}(f)
.
∎

Given a saddle point
\bar{f}_{[I\setminus\{1\}]}
, the follow theorem states that for
i\in I_{+}\setminus\{1\}
, the OT plan between
any
signed barycenter and
\mu_{i}
belongs to the
c
-superdifferential of
\bar{f}_{i}
. This is reminiscent of a related property in the classical theory of optimal transport
(
7
, Remark 1.15)
. We emphasize that this property holds only for potentials corresponding to a
positive weight
. With additional absolutely continuous marginal associated with a
positive weight
, we obtain the uniqueness of the signed barycenter from the perspective of dual approach, comparing with the uniqueness result from
Theorem

15
.

Theorem 22

(Uniqueness of the signed barycenter)
.

Suppose
\mu_{1}\in\mathbb{P}_{\textup{cost}}^{\textup{AC}}(\mathcal{X})
and
\bar{f}_{[I\setminus\{1\}]}
is a
c
-concave saddle point to
\mathscr{D}(f)
. Any signed barycenter
\hat{\nu}
(including
\bar{\nu}
induced from
\bar{f}_{[I\setminus\{1\}]}
) to
\mathscr{B}
necessarily satisfies that for
i\in I_{+}\setminus\{1\}
, there exists
\pi_{i}\in\Pi(\mu_{i},\hat{\nu})
such that
\pi_{i}(x_{i},y)(c(x_{i},y)-\bar{f}_{i}^{c}(x_{i})-\bar{f}_{i}(y))=0
.

If there is
\mu_{i}\in\mathbb{P}_{\textup{cost}}^{\textup{AC}}(\mathcal{X})
for some
i\in I_{+}\setminus\{1\}
. Then the signed barycenter is unique.

Proof.

Let
\bar{\nu}={T_{(-\sum_{i=2}^{m}\frac{a_{i}}{a_{1}}\bar{f}_{i})^{c}}}_{\#}\mu_{1}
be the signed barycenter induced from the saddle point
\bar{f}_{[I\setminus\{1\}]}
and
\hat{\nu}
be another signed barycenter such that
\mathscr{B}(\hat{\nu})=\mathscr{B}(\bar{\nu})
.

For
i\in I\setminus\{1\}
, let
\hat{f}_{i}
be the Kantorovich potential such that
K_{c}(\mu_{i},\hat{\nu})=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\hat{f}_{i}^{c}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{i}+\int_{\mathcal{Y}}\hat{f}_{i}\,\mathrm{d}\hat{\nu}=\sup_{f}\int_{\mathcal{X}}f^{c}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{i}+\int_{\mathcal{Y}}f\,\mathrm{d}\hat{\nu}
. Then

\displaystyle\mathscr{B}(\hat{\nu})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=2}^{m}a_{i}[\int_{\mathcal{X}}\hat{f}_{i}^{c}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{i}+\int_{\mathcal{Y}}\hat{f}_{i}\,\mathrm{d}\hat{\nu}]+a_{1}K_{c}(\mu_{1},\hat{\nu})

\displaystyle\geqslant\sum_{i\in I_{+}\setminus\{1\}}a_{i}[\int_{\mathcal{X}}\bar{f}_{i}^{c}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{i}+\int_{\mathcal{Y}}\bar{f}_{i}\,\mathrm{d}\hat{\nu}]+\sum_{i\in I_{-}}a_{i}[\int_{\mathcal{X}}\hat{f}_{i}^{c}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{i}+\int_{\mathcal{Y}}\hat{f}_{i}\,\mathrm{d}\hat{\nu}]+a_{1}K_{c}(\mu_{1},\hat{\nu})

\displaystyle\geqslant\inf_{\nu}\sum_{i\in I_{+}\setminus\{1\}}a_{i}[\int_{\mathcal{X}}\bar{f}_{i}^{c}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{i}+\int_{\mathcal{Y}}\bar{f}_{i}\,\mathrm{d}\nu]+\sum_{i\in I_{-}}a_{i}[\int_{\mathcal{X}}\hat{f}_{i}^{c}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{i}+\int_{\mathcal{Y}}\hat{f}_{i}\,\mathrm{d}\nu]+a_{1}K_{c}(\mu_{1},\nu)

\displaystyle=\mathscr{D}(\bar{f}_{[I_{+}\setminus\{1\}]},\hat{f}_{[I_{-}]})\geqslant\inf_{f_{[I_{-}]}}\mathscr{D}(\bar{f}_{[I_{+}\setminus\{1\}]},f_{[I_{-}]})=\mathscr{D}(\bar{f}_{[I_{+}\setminus\{1\}]},\bar{f}_{[I_{-}]})=\mathscr{B}(\bar{\nu}),

which implies that all above inequalities are equalities.

As a result, for
i\in I_{+}\setminus\{1\}
,
\bar{f}_{i}
is the Kantorovich potential to
K_{c}(\mu_{i},\hat{\nu})
. Thanks to the optimality condition in
Theorem

4
, there exists
\pi_{i}\in\Pi(\mu_{i},\hat{\nu})
such that
\pi_{i}(x_{i},y)(c(x_{i},y)-\bar{f}^{c}_{i}(x_{i})-\bar{f}_{i}(y))=0
. Note that
\bar{\nu}
satisfies a stronger condition as this equation holds for any
i\in I\setminus\{1\}
.

If, in addition, there exists an absolutely continuous marginal
\mu_{i}
associated with a positive weight, denoted by
\mu_{i}\in\mathbb{P}_{\textup{cost}}^{\textup{AC}}(\mathcal{X})
. Then the above property can be rephrased in terms of OT maps. Namely,
T_{\bar{f}^{c}_{i}}
is the optimal transport map from
\mu_{i}
to
\hat{\nu}
. On the other hand, thanks to
Theorem

21
,
T_{\bar{f}^{c}_{i}}
is the optimal transport map from
\mu_{2}
to
\bar{\nu}
. Thus
\hat{\nu}=\bar{\nu}
.

∎

4.3
A criteria for upgrading stationary points to saddle points

In practice, it is often more tractable to obtain a stationary point. For instance, via (
19
) when all input measures are absolutely continuous. We are therefore interested in determining whether this stationary point is a saddle point, in which case it possesses the properties discussed in
Section

4.2
and induce the signed barycenter.

The objective value of the dual problem (
15
) is the same whether
f_{[I\setminus\{1\}]}
are
c
-concave or merely bounded continuous, since replacing each
f_{i}
by
f_{i}^{cc}
increases the objective value when
f_{i}
is a maximizing variable and decrease it when
f_{i}
is a minimizing variable. Thus we may consider another dual functional

D(f_{[I_{+}\setminus\{1\}]},g_{[I_{-}]})=\sum_{i=2}^{\ell}\int_{\mathcal{X}}a_{i}f_{i}^{c}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{i}+\sum_{i=\ell+1}^{m}\int_{\mathcal{X}}a_{i}g_{i}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{i}+a_{1}\int_{\mathcal{X}}(-\sum_{i=2}^{\ell}\frac{a_{i}}{a_{1}}f_{i}+\sum_{i=\ell+1}^{m}\frac{\mathinner{\!\left\lvert a_{i}\right\rvert}}{a_{1}}g_{i}^{c})^{c}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{1},

which is essentially the composition of (
15
)

\mathscr{D}(f_{[I_{+}\setminus\{1\}]},f_{{I_{-}}})=\sum_{i=2}^{\ell}\int_{\mathcal{X}}a_{i}f_{i}^{c}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{i}+\sum_{i=\ell+1}^{m}\int_{\mathcal{X}}a_{i}f_{i}^{c}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{i}+a_{1}\int_{\mathcal{X}}(-\sum_{i=2}^{\ell}\frac{a_{i}}{a_{1}}f_{i}+\sum_{i=\ell+1}^{m}\frac{\mathinner{\!\left\lvert a_{i}\right\rvert}}{a_{1}}f_{i})^{c}\mathrm{d}\mu_{1},

with the change of variables
g_{i}=f_{i}^{c}
. Notably,

\sup_{f_{[I_{+}\setminus\{1\}]}}\inf_{f_{[I_{-}]}}\mathscr{D}(f_{[I_{+}\setminus\{1\}]},f_{[I_{-}]})=\sup_{f_{[I_{+}\setminus\{1\}]}}\inf_{g_{[I_{-}]}}D(f_{[I_{+}\setminus\{1\}]},g_{[I_{-}]}),

regardless the optimizations are among
c
-concave or bounded continuous functions.

Given a
c
-concave stationary point
\bar{f}_{[I\setminus\{1\}]}
, to show it is a saddle point, since
\mathscr{D}
is concave w.r.t the maximizing variables, it suffices to check that

\mathscr{D}(\bar{f}_{[I_{+}\setminus\{1\}]},\bar{f}_{[I_{-}]})=\inf_{f_{[I_{-}]}}\mathscr{D}(\bar{f}_{[I_{+}\setminus\{1\}]},f_{[I_{-}]})

holds. To establish this, we define
\bar{g}_{i}=\bar{f}_{i}^{c}
for
i\in I_{-}
and show the following holds

•

g_{[I_{-}]}\mapsto D(\bar{f}_{[I_{+}\setminus\{1\}]},g_{[I_{-}]})
is convex along curves in
(C_{b}(\mathcal{X}))^{m-\ell}
.

•

\mathscr{D}(\bar{f}_{[I_{+}\setminus\{1\}]},\bar{f}_{[I_{-}]})=D(\bar{f}_{[I_{+}\setminus\{1\}]},\bar{g}_{[I_{-}]})=\inf_{g_{[I_{-}]}}D(\bar{f}_{[I_{+}\setminus\{1\}]},g_{[I_{-}]})
.

under adequate conditions.

Now, we pick those maximizing variables in the stationary point and consider
h\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathcal{Y}\times(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}^{|I_{-}|})\to\mathbb{R}
defined by

h(y;x_{1},\mathbf{x}^{-})\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=a_{1}c(x_{1},y)-\sum_{i\in I_{-}}|a_{i}|c(x_{i},y)+\sum_{i\in I_{+}\setminus\{1\}}a_{i}\bar{f}_{i}(y).

(23)

Proposition 23
.

Let
\mu_{1}\in\mathbb{P}_{\textup{cost}}^{\textup{AC}}(\mathcal{X})
and
\bar{f}_{[I\setminus\{1\}]}
be a
c
-concave stationary point of
\mathscr{D}
. If
h
is uniformly bounded from below and
h(y;x_{1},\mathbf{x}^{-})
defined in (
23
) is convex for every
(x_{1},\mathbf{x}^{-})\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}^{|I_{-}|}
, then
D(\bar{f}_{I_{+}\setminus\{1\}},g_{[I_{-}]})
is convex along curves in the set of bounded functions, i.e., for any bounded continuous functions
g_{[I_{-}]}
, define
g_{i,t}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=tg_{i}+(1-t)\bar{f}_{i}^{c}
for
i\in I_{-}
, and

t\mapsto D(\bar{f}_{2},\ldots,\bar{f}_{\ell},g_{\ell+1,t},\ldots,g_{m,t})

is convex.

Remark 24
.

The functional
\mathscr{D}
is in general not necessarily jointly convex w.r.t the minimizing variables. However, at a stationary point, if the weighted cost and maximizing potentials satisfy the convexity criteria, the functional
\mathscr{D}
is convex w.r.t the minimizing variables in some sense.

Proof.

Note that
\int_{\mathcal{X}}a_{i}g_{i}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{i}
is linear for
i\in I_{-}
, thus our goal is equivalent to show

t\mapsto\int_{\mathcal{X}}a_{1}\Big(-\sum_{i\in I_{+}\setminus\{1\}}\frac{a_{i}}{a_{1}}\bar{f_{i}}+\sum_{i\in I_{-}}\frac{\mathinner{\!\left\lvert a_{i}\right\rvert}}{a_{1}}g_{i,t}^{c}\Big)^{c}(x_{1})\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{1}(x_{1})

is convex.

Expanding the integrand in terms of the
c
-transforms, we see that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{X}}a_{1}\Big(-\sum_{i\in I_{+}\setminus\{1\}}\frac{a_{i}}{a_{1}}\bar{f_{i}}+\sum_{i\in I_{-}}\frac{\mathinner{\!\left\lvert a_{i}\right\rvert}}{a_{1}}g_{i,t}^{c}\Big)^{c}(x_{1})\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{1}(x_{1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\inf_{Y}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\Big(a_{1}c(x_{1},Y(x_{1}))+\sum_{i\in I_{+}\setminus\{1\}}a_{i}\bar{f}_{i}(Y(x_{1}))-\sum_{i\in I_{-}}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert a_{i}\right\rvert}g_{i,t}^{c}(Y(x_{1}))\Big)\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{1}(x_{1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\inf_{Y}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\Big(a_{1}c(x_{1},Y(x_{1}))+\sum_{i\in I_{+}\setminus\{1\}}a_{i}\bar{f}_{i}(Y(x_{1}))+\sum_{i\in I_{-}}\sup_{x_{i}}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert a_{i}\right\rvert}\big(g_{i,t}(x_{i})-c(x_{i},Y(x_{1}))\Big)\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\inf_{Y}\sup_{\gamma\in\Pi(\mu_{1},\cdot)}\int_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}^{|I_{-}|}}\Big(h(Y(x_{1});x_{1},\mathbf{x}^{-})+\sum_{i\in I_{-}}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert a_{i}\right\rvert}g_{i,t}(x_{i})\Big)\,\mathrm{d}\gamma(x_{1},\mathbf{x}^{-}),

where
Y\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}
and
\gamma
is a joint coupling over
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}^{|I_{-}|}
, whose first marginal is
\mu_{1}
. If we choose some compact, convex set
K\subset\mathcal{Y}
and restrict
Y
to take values in
K
, then the last line is bounded from above by

\inf_{Y\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathcal{X}\to K}\sup_{\gamma\in\Pi(\mu_{1},\cdot)}\int_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}^{|I_{-}|}}\Big(h(Y(x_{1});x_{1},\mathbf{x}^{-})+\sum_{i\in I_{-}}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert a_{i}\right\rvert}g_{i,t}(x_{i})\Big)\,\mathrm{d}\gamma(x_{1},\mathbf{x}^{-}).

Clearly, the space of maps
Y\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathcal{X}\to K
is a weakly compact subset of
L^{1}(\mu_{1})
. Since
h
is convex with respect to the
y
variable, it follows that the functional is weakly lower semicontinuous with respect to
Y
. It is also clear that the functional is concave with respect to
\gamma
and weakly upper semicontinuous with respect to the weak convergence of
\gamma
.

Thus, by Sion’s minimax theorem, we can interchange the order of inf and sup. Defining

H_{K}(x_{1},\mathbf{x}^{-})\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\inf_{y\in K}h(y;x_{1},\mathbf{x}^{-}),

we have

D(\bar{f}_{2},\ldots,\bar{f}_{\ell},g_{\ell+1,t},\ldots,g_{m,t})\leqslant\sup_{\gamma\in\Pi(\mu_{1},\cdot)}\int_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}^{|I_{-}|}}\Big(H_{K}(x_{1},\mathbf{x}^{-})+\sum_{i\in I_{-}}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert a_{i}\right\rvert}g_{i,t}(x_{i})\Big)\,\mathrm{d}\gamma(x_{1},\mathbf{x}^{-}).

We then have the following string of inequalities

\displaystyle\sup_{\gamma\in\Pi(\mu_{1},\cdot)}\int_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}^{|I_{-}|}}\Big(H_{K}(x_{1},\mathbf{x}^{-})+\sum_{i\in I_{-}}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert a_{i}\right\rvert}g_{i,t}(x_{i})\Big)\,\mathrm{d}\gamma(x_{1},\mathbf{x}^{-})

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\sup_{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{0}\in\Pi(\mu_{1},\cdot)}t\int_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}^{|I_{-}|}}\Big(H_{K}(x_{1},\mathbf{x}^{-})+\sum_{i\in I_{-}}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert a_{i}\right\rvert}g_{i}(x_{i})\Big)\,\mathrm{d}\gamma_{1}(x_{1},\mathbf{x}^{-})

\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt+(1-t)\int_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}^{|I_{-}|}}\Big(H_{K}(x_{1},\mathbf{x}^{-})+\sum_{i\in I_{-}}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert a_{i}\right\rvert}\bar{f}_{i}^{c}(x_{i})\Big)\,\mathrm{d}\gamma_{0}(x_{1},\mathbf{x}^{-})

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}Y_{1}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathcal{X}\to K\\
Y_{0}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathcal{X}\to K\end{subarray}}\sup_{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{0}\in\Pi(\mu_{1},\cdot)}t\int_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}^{|I_{-}|}}\Big(h(Y_{1}(x_{1});x_{1},\mathbf{x}^{-})+\sum_{i\in I_{-}}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert a_{i}\right\rvert}g_{i}(x_{i})\Big)\,\mathrm{d}\gamma_{1}(x_{1},\mathbf{x}^{-})

\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt+(1-t)\int_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}^{|I_{-}|}}\Big(h(Y_{0}(x_{1});x_{1},\mathbf{x}^{-})+\sum_{i\in I_{-}}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert a_{i}\right\rvert}\bar{f}_{i}^{c}(x_{i})\Big)\,\mathrm{d}\gamma_{0}(x_{1},\mathbf{x}^{-})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}Y_{1}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathcal{X}\to K\\
Y_{0}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathcal{X}\to K\end{subarray}}t\int_{\mathcal{X}}\Big(a_{1}c(x_{1},Y_{1}(x_{1}))+\sum_{i\in I_{+}\setminus\{1\}}a_{i}\bar{f}_{i}(Y_{1}(x_{1}))-\sum_{i\in I_{-}}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert a_{i}\right\rvert}g_{i}^{c}(Y_{1}(x_{1}))\Big)\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{1}(x_{1})

\displaystyle\hskip 34.14322pt+(1-t)\int_{\mathcal{X}}\Big(a_{1}c(x_{1},Y_{0}(x_{1}))+\sum_{i\in I_{+}\setminus\{1\}}a_{i}\bar{f}_{i}(Y_{0}(x_{1}))-\sum_{i\in I_{-}}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert a_{i}\right\rvert}\bar{f}_{i}^{c}(Y_{0}(x_{1}))\Big)\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{1}(x_{1})

Letting
K
approach
\mathcal{X}
, we have now shown that

t\mapsto D(\bar{f}_{2},\ldots,\bar{f}_{\ell},g_{\ell+1,t},\ldots,g_{m,t})

is convex.

∎

In the following theorem, we will assume that the signed barycenter is absolutely continuous. And we will provide a sufficient condition in
Proposition

27
.

Theorem 25

(Sufficient condition for a saddle point)
.

Let
\mu_{1}\in\mathbb{P}_{\textup{cost}}^{\textup{AC}}(\mathcal{X})
and
\bar{f}_{[I\setminus\{1\}]}
be a
c
-concave stationary point of
\mathscr{D}
. Suppose
h
is uniformly bounded from below and
h(y;x_{1},\mathbf{x}^{-})
is convex for every fixed
(x_{1},\mathbf{x}^{-})\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}^{|I_{-}|}
. Assume that
\bar{\nu}={T_{(-\sum_{i=2}^{m}\frac{a_{i}}{a_{1}}\bar{f}_{i})^{c}}}_{\#}\mu_{1}
defined in (
18
) is absolutely continuous, then
\bar{f}_{[I\setminus\{1\}]}
is a saddle point of
\mathscr{D}
.

Proof.

Let
H(x_{1},\mathbf{x}^{-})\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\inf_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}h(y;x_{1},\mathbf{x}^{-})
and it is bounded from below by our assumption. For any continuous function
\psi\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathcal{X}^{|I_{-}|}\to\mathbb{R}
, we introduce a new
H
-based transform

\psi^{H}(x_{1})\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\sup_{\mathbf{x}^{-}\in\mathcal{X}^{|I_{-}|}}H(x_{1},\mathbf{x}^{-})-\psi(\mathbf{x}^{-}),

and for any bounded continuous function
g_{i}
for
