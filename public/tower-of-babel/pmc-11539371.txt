Key anatomical and clinical points about the superior cluneal nerves
Authors: Thomas Fichtner Bendtsen, Siska Bjørn, Thomas Dahl Nielsen
Published: 2024

Dear Editor,

We write in response to Dr. Ottestad's editorial on superior cluneal nerve-related pain due to entrapment or direct nerve injury [1]. While we appreciate the attention brought to this underrecognized source of cutaneous neuropathic pain in the superior gluteal region, we would like to offer some clarifications regarding key anatomical details mentioned in the editorial.

First, it was stated that the medial, intermediate, and lateral branches of the superior cluneal nerve (originating from the lateral branches of the dorsal rami of the spinal nerves T12-L3) course over the longissimus and iliocostalis muscles near the spine before crossing the iliac crest and gluteus medius distally. In fact, all branches of the superior cluneal nerve pass through the lumbar portion of the iliocostalis, emerging from its lateral half or even its anterolateral margin, descending only a few centimeters before penetrating the thoracolumbar fascia and crossing the iliac crest subcutaneously [2]. This anatomical distinction has relevant clinical implications for optimal diagnostic block.

Second, the editorial suggests that the superior cluneal nerves, particularly the medial branch, can be targeted for radiofrequency ablation around the posterior superior iliac spine (PSIS), referencing Gálvez and colleagues [3]. However, PSIS is actually at the level of the S2 segment of the sacrum [4], while Gálvez et al. targeted the nerve branches at the intersection of the superior cluneal nerves and the iliac crest, approximately 7 cm lateral to the spinous process of vertebra L4, which corresponds to the superior aspect of the iliac crest [4]. The vertical distance from the PSIS to L4 is roughly 5–7 cm. This discrepancy likely stems from a mislabeling of the target on the iliac crest as ‘PSIS’ in Gálvez et al.’s Fig. 1 [3].

Third, the editorial mentions that a positive radiating Tinel's sign at the PSIS indicates superior cluneal neuralgia. However, Tinel's sign is not intended for application over bony structures like the PSIS, as the target per definition should be a specific superficial nerve of an appreciable size. In our opinion, a radiating response is better elicited by palpation of the PSIS rather than tapping. Of note, the PSIS and posterior sacroiliac ligaments are innervated by the posterior sacral plexus, which is derived from the dorsal rami of L4-S4 [5]. Therefore, using anatomical proxy targets such as bony and ligamentous landmarks around the PSIS for radiofrequency ablation is more likely to affect branches of the posterior sacral plexus rather than the superior cluneal nerve.

Finally, while superior cluneal neuralgia may occasionally result from entrapment where the nerves pierce the thoracolumbar fascia or cross the iliac crest, the nerve block technique described by Nielsen et al. (2019) offers a more reliable method for targeting the superior cluneal nerves before they pierce the thoracolumbar fascia [2]. This ultrasound-guided approach is safe and easy to perform with a high success rate. Thus, it optimizes anesthetic coverage while minimizing procedural risks.

We hope these clarifications contribute to a more accurate understanding of superior cluneal nerve pain and that they enhance future discussions on the subject.
