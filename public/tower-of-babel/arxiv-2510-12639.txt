Contraction and entropy production in continuous-time Sinkhorn dynamics
Anand Srinivasan, Jean-Jacques Slotine

Abstract
. Recently, the vanishing-step-size limit of the Sinkhorn algorithm at finite regularization parameter ε \varepsilon was shown to be a mirror descent in the space of probability measures. We give L 2 L^{2} contraction criteria in two time-dependent metrics induced by the mirror Hessian, which reduce to the coercivity of certain conditional expectation operators. We then give an exact identity for the entropy production rate of the Sinkhorn flow, which was previously known only to be nonpositive. Examining this rate shows that the standard semigroup analysis of diffusion processes extends systematically to the Sinkhorn flow. We show that the flow induces a reversible Markov dynamics on the target marginal as an Onsager gradient flow. We define the Dirichlet form associated to its (nonlocal) infinitesimal generator, prove a Poincaré inequality for it, and show that the spectral gap is strictly positive along the Sinkhorn flow whenever ε > 0 \varepsilon>0 . Lastly, we show that the entropy decay is exponential if and only if a logarithmic Sobolev inequality (LSI) holds. We give for illustration two immediate practical use-cases for the Sinkhorn LSI: as a design principle for the latent space in which generative models are trained, and as a stopping heuristic for discrete-time algorithms.

2.1.
Entropic optimal transport

We recall the
entropy-regularized
optimal transport problem.
Let
X,Y=\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\mathcal{P}(X),\mathcal{P}(Y)
the corresponding sets of probability measures.
Let
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(X),\nu\in\mathcal{P}(Y)

\Pi(\mu,\nu)\subset\mathcal{P}(X\times Y)
denote the set of

between these marginals, i.e. those joint probability measures

(1)

\displaystyle\int_{X\times Y}f(x)\pi(dx,dy)

\displaystyle=:\int_{X}f(x)\pi^{X}(dx)=\int_{X}f(x)\mu(dx)

(2)

\displaystyle\int_{X\times Y}g(y)\pi(dx,dy)

\displaystyle=:\int_{Y}g(y)\pi^{Y}(dy)=\int_{Y}g(y)\nu(dy),

for all bounded measurable

Equations (

\displaystyle{\rm OT}_{\varepsilon}(\mu,\nu)

\displaystyle=\min_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[c]+\varepsilon H(\pi|\mu\otimes\nu),

(4)

\displaystyle H(\pi|\tilde{\pi})

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\int_{X\times Y}d\pi\log\left(\frac{d\pi}{d\tilde{\pi}}\right)&{\rm if}\ \pi\ll\tilde{\pi}\\
+\infty&{\rm otherwise}\end{cases}

where

\pi
is absolutely continuous with respect to
\tilde{\pi}

The existence of the minimizer (denoted throughout as
\pi_{*}

[
12

The Fenchel-Rockafellar dual form of

(

[

\displaystyle{\rm OT}_{\varepsilon}(\mu,\nu)

\displaystyle=\sup_{f\in C(X),g\in C(Y)}\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[\frac{f}{\varepsilon}]+\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[\frac{g}{\varepsilon}]-\mathbb{E}_{(\mu\otimes\nu)}\left[\exp\left(\frac{-c+(f\oplus g)}{\varepsilon}\right)\right]

2.4.
Definitions

Definition 2.1

(Conditional expectation operator)
.

Let
(X\times Y,\pi)
be the probability space.
Define

(17)

\displaystyle(P_{\pi}f)(y)

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[f|Y=y]=\int_{X}f(x,y)\pi(dx|y)\in L^{2}(\pi^{Y}),

which is an orthogonal projection (after canonically embedding
L^{2}(\pi^{Y})
back in
L^{2}(\pi)
via
h(x,y):=h(y)
, which we will assume throughout), since for all
f,g\in L^{2}(\pi)
,

(1)

P_{\pi}
is a projection by the tower property

(18)

\displaystyle P_{\pi}P_{\pi}f

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[f|Y]|Y]=E_{\pi}[f|Y]=P_{\pi}f,

(2)

P_{\pi}
is self-adjoint by

(19)

\displaystyle\langle P_{\pi}f,g\rangle_{L^{2}(\pi)}

\displaystyle=\iint_{X\times Y}g(x,y)\pi(dx|y)\int_{X}f(x^{\prime},y)\pi(dx^{\prime},dy)=\langle f,P_{\pi}g\rangle_{L^{2}(\pi)}.

(3)

P_{\pi}
is bounded and a contraction; by Jensen’s inequality,

(20)

\displaystyle\left\lVert P_{\pi}f\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\pi)}^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[(\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[f|Y])^{2}]\leq\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[f^{2}|Y]]=E_{\pi}[f^{2}]=\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\pi)}^{2}.

(4)

P_{\pi}
is the orthogonal projection onto the closed subspace

(21)

\displaystyle\im P_{\pi}

\displaystyle=\{g\in L^{2}(\pi)\ |\ \exists h\in L^{2}(\pi^{Y})\ {\rm s.t.}\ g(x,y)=h(y)\ {\rm for}\ \pi-{\rm a.e.}(x,y)\}.

Similarly, define the projection
Q_{\pi}:L^{2}(\pi)\to L^{2}(\pi^{X})

(22)

\displaystyle(Q_{\pi}f)(x)

\displaystyle:=E_{\pi}[f|X=x]=\int_{Y}f(x,y)\pi(dy|x)

which also has properties (1), (2), and (3) above with (4) being

(23)

\displaystyle\im Q_{\pi}

\displaystyle=\{g\in L^{2}(\pi)\ |\ \exists f\in L^{2}(\pi^{X})\ {\rm s.t.}\ g(x,y)=f(x)\ {\rm for}\ \pi-{\rm a.e.}(x,y)\}.

Definition 2.2

(Numerical range)
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{B}(H)
be a bounded linear operator on a Hilbert space
H
.
Its
numerical range

W(T)
is the subset of the complex plane

(24)

\displaystyle W_{H}(T)

\displaystyle=\{\frac{\langle v,Tv\rangle_{H}}{\langle v,v\rangle_{H}}\ |\ v\in H\},

which is equivalently the map of the unit sphere
\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{H}=1
under
v\mapsto\langle v,Tv\rangle_{H}
.

Definition 2.3

(Coercivity)
.

We call an operator
T
as in
2.2

\lambda
-
coercive
in a real Hilbert space
H
if
\inf W_{H}(T)=\lambda>0
.

(57)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}H(\pi_{t}^{Y}|\nu)

\displaystyle=-\int_{X\times Y}g_{t}(y)(Q_{\pi_{t}}^{\perp}g_{t})(x,y)\pi_{t}(x,y)dxdy

(58)

\displaystyle=-\langle g_{t},Q_{\pi_{t}}^{\perp}g_{t}\rangle_{L^{2}(\pi_{t})}=-\left\lVert Q_{\pi_{t}}^{\perp}g_{t}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\pi_{t})}^{2}

since
Q_{\pi_{t}}
(and therefore
Q_{\pi_{t}}^{\perp}
) is an orthogonal projection on
L^{2}(\pi_{t})
by
2.1
, giving the result.
∎

Remark 4
.

Notice that the expression in (
58
) can be written as, for some function
g\in L^{2}(\pi^{Y})
,

(59)

\displaystyle\langle g,(I-Q_{\pi})g\rangle_{L^{2}(\pi)}=\langle g,P_{\pi}(I-Q_{\pi})g\rangle_{L^{2}(\pi^{Y})}=\langle g,(I-P_{\pi}Q_{\pi})g\rangle_{L^{2}(\pi^{Y})}

since
P_{\pi}g=g
for
g
which is just a function of
y
.
This motivates the definition of the operator

(60)

\displaystyle(T_{\pi}g)(y)

\displaystyle:=E_{\pi}[E_{\pi}[g(Y)|X]|Y=y]=(P_{\pi}Q_{\pi}g)(y),

which maps
L^{2}(\pi^{Y})\to L^{2}(\pi^{Y})
(compare with the domains of
P_{\pi},Q_{\pi}
in
2.1
which are the whole of
L^{2}(\pi)
).

T_{\pi}
is self-adjoint on
L^{2}(\pi^{Y})
since, for
f
also in
L^{2}(\pi^{Y})
,

(61)

\displaystyle\langle f,T_{\pi}g\rangle_{\pi^{Y}}

\displaystyle=\langle f,Q_{\pi}g\rangle_{\pi}=\langle Q_{\pi}f,g\rangle_{\pi}=\langle T_{\pi}f,g\rangle_{\pi^{Y}}.

Moreover,
T_{\pi}\bm{1}=\bm{1}
, so
T_{\pi}
satisfies the necessary conditions to be a symmetric (reversible) Markov operator (§1.6.1,
[
11
]
).
Its stationary measure is
\pi^{Y}
, since for every
f\in L^{2}(\pi^{Y})
,

(62)

\displaystyle\int_{Y}\pi^{Y}T_{\pi}fdy

\displaystyle=\int_{X}\pi^{X}Q_{\pi}fdx=\int_{Y}f\pi^{Y}dy

hence
T_{\pi}^{*}\pi^{Y}=\pi^{Y}
.
Note furthermore that by a calculation following from (
56
),

(63)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\pi_{t}^{Y}}{\partial t}=-\pi_{t}^{Y}P_{\pi_{t}}(I-Q_{\pi_{t}})\log\frac{d\pi_{t}^{Y}}{d\nu}=-\pi_{t}^{Y}(I-T_{\pi_{t}})\log\frac{d\pi_{t}^{Y}}{d\nu}.

Hence, defining
L_{\pi_{t}}:=(I-T_{\pi_{t}})
, we see that the marginal dynamics (
63
) can be written in the Onsager-gradient flow form

(64)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\pi_{t}^{Y}}{\partial t}=-\pi_{t}^{Y}L_{\pi_{t}}\frac{\delta F}{\delta\pi^{Y}}(\pi_{t}^{Y}),

with
K_{\pi}:=\pi^{Y}L_{\pi}
the (nonlocal,
\pi
-dependent)
Onsager operator
.

Definition 3.1

(Sinkhorn Dirichlet form)
.

In analogy with diffusion processes, let us define the Dirichlet form (§1.7.1,
[
11
]
) associated to
K_{\pi}
using an “integration by parts” formula

(65)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\pi}(f,g)

\displaystyle:=\langle f,L_{\pi}g\rangle_{L^{2}(\pi^{Y})},

which for equal arguments is exactly

(66)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\pi}(g,g)

\displaystyle=\langle g,(I-Q_{\pi})g\rangle_{L^{2}(\pi)}=\left\lVert(I-Q_{\pi})g\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\pi)}^{2},

the entropy production rate of the Sinkhorn flow (Theorem
3
), taking
g=\log\frac{d\pi^{Y}}{d\nu}
.

As in the theory of diffusion processes, an explicit bound for the entropy production rate (
54
) is given by a “Poincaré constant,” or spectral gap, for the Dirichlet form
\mathcal{E}_{\pi}
.

Lemma 1

(Poincaré inequality for
\mathcal{E}
)
.

For all
g\in L^{2}(\pi^{Y})
, we have

(67)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\pi}(g,g)

\displaystyle\geq(1-C(\pi))\left\lVert g-\langle g,\bm{1}\rangle\bm{1}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\pi^{Y})}^{2}=(1-C(\pi))\mathrm{Var}_{\pi^{Y}}(g)

for some constant
C(\pi)\in[0,1]
depending only on
\pi
.

Proof.

Let
g\in L^{2}(\pi^{Y})
and
g=\tilde{g}+c\bm{1}
with
\tilde{g}\in L^{2}_{0}(\pi^{Y})
where
L^{2}_{0}(\pi^{Y})
is the mean-zero subspace as defined in (
15
).
Then,

(68)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\pi}(g,g)

\displaystyle=\langle g,L_{\pi}g\rangle_{\pi^{Y}}=\langle\tilde{g},L_{\pi}\tilde{g}\rangle_{\pi^{Y}}=\left\lVert\tilde{g}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\pi^{Y})}^{2}-\langle\tilde{g},T_{\pi}\tilde{g}\rangle_{\pi^{Y}}

since
T_{\pi}\bm{1}=\bm{1}
,
L_{\pi}\bm{1}=0
and
L_{\pi}
is self-adjoint on
L^{2}(\pi^{Y})
.
Moreover,
L_{0}^{2}(\pi^{Y})
is an invariant subspace of
T_{\pi}
:

(69)

\displaystyle\langle T_{\pi}\tilde{g},\bm{1}\rangle_{\pi^{Y}}

\displaystyle=\langle\tilde{g},\bm{1}\rangle_{\pi^{Y}}=0.

Hence, denote the restriction to
L_{0}^{2}(\pi^{Y})
by
T_{\pi}^{0}
, which remains self-adjoint.
Thus, letting
f\perp\bm{1}
denote that
f\in L_{0}^{2}(\pi^{Y})\setminus\{0\}
,

(70)

\displaystyle\left\lVert T_{\pi}^{0}\right\rVert

\displaystyle=\sup W_{L^{2}(\pi^{Y})}(T_{\pi}^{0})=\sup_{f\perp\bm{1}}\frac{\langle f,T_{\pi}^{0}f\rangle_{\pi^{Y}}}{\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\pi^{Y})}^{2}}=\sup_{f\perp\bm{1}}\frac{\left\lVert Q_{\pi}f\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\pi)}^{2}}{\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\pi^{Y})}^{2}}=:C(\pi).

Since
Q_{\pi}
is an orthogonal projection,
C(\pi)\leq 1
.
Hence,

(71)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\pi}(g,g)

\displaystyle\geq(1-C(\pi))\left\lVert\tilde{g}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\pi^{Y})}^{2},

which gives the result.
∎

Corollary 1
.

Along the Sinkhorn dynamics (
10
), (
11
),

(72)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}H(\pi_{t}^{Y}|\nu)

\displaystyle\leq-(1-C(\pi_{t}))\mathrm{Var}_{\pi_{t}^{Y}}(\log\frac{d\pi_{t}^{Y}}{d\nu})

with
C(\pi_{t})
the Poincaré constant in Lemma
1
.
Furthermore,
C(\pi_{t})<1
whenever the regularization parameter
\varepsilon>0
.

Proof.

Inequality (
72
) as well as
C(\pi)\in[0,1]
is an immediate consequence of Theorem
3
.
Now, suppose
C(\pi)=1
; then from (
70
), there exists some
f_{*}\perp\bm{1}
(using the same notation as we have defined there) such that

(73)

\displaystyle\left\lVert Q_{\pi}f_{*}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\pi)}^{2}=\left\lVert f_{*}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\pi^{Y})}^{2}=\left\lVert f_{*}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\pi)}^{2}\iff\left\lVert(I-Q_{\pi})f_{*}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\pi)}^{2}=0,

since
Q_{\pi}
is an orthogonal projection in
L^{2}(\pi)
.
This holds iff
f_{*}=Q_{\pi}f_{*}

\pi
-a.e.
In other words, the
X
-measurable function
h_{*}(x):=(Q_{\pi}f_{*})(x)
is such that
f_{*}(y)=h_{*}(x)
for
\pi
-a.e.
(x,y)
.
But, since
\varepsilon>0
, the Sinkhorn initial condition (
7
) satisfies
\pi_{0}>0
Lebesgue-a.e.; hence, along the Sinkhorn flow (
11
),
\pi_{t}>0
a.e.
Thus,
f_{*}
must be a.e. constant on
Y
, yet
f_{*}\perp\bm{1}
so
f_{*}=0
, which is a contradiction.
Hence,
C(\pi)<1
if
\varepsilon>0
.
∎

Corollary
1
shows the role that positive entropy regularization
\varepsilon>0
plays in convergence of the Sinkhorn flow.
Lastly, we now give a sharp condition for exponential entropy production.
Notice that
\mathcal{E}_{\pi}(g,g)
in (
66
) with argument
g=\log\frac{d\pi^{Y}}{d\nu}
is exactly the “Fisher information”

(74)

\displaystyle I_{\pi}(\omega|\nu)

\displaystyle:=\mathcal{E}_{\pi}(\log\frac{d\omega}{d\nu},\log\frac{d\omega}{d\nu}),

so that
\frac{d}{dt}H(\pi_{t}^{Y}|\nu)=-I_{\pi_{t}}(\pi_{t}^{Y}|\nu)
, which mirrors precisely the formula from the theory of diffusion processes
[
11
]
.
It follows that the entropy production (
54
) corresponds to an exponential entropy production if and only if a “log-Sobolev” inequality (which is a Polyak-Lojasiewicz inequality for the Lyapunov function
H
) holds:

Definition 3.2

(Logarithmic Sobolev inequality)
.

A pair
(\pi,\nu)
is said to satisfy a
log-Sobolev inequality
(in the sense of (
74
), (
65
)) with constant
\lambda>0
if

(75)

\displaystyle H(\pi^{Y}|\nu)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2\lambda}I_{\pi}(\pi^{Y}|\nu).

Corollary 2

(Exponential entropy decay in the Sinkhorn flow)
.

If for given
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(X),\nu\in\mathcal{P}(Y),\lambda>0
and all
\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\cdot)
, the pair
(\pi,\nu)
satisfies the log-Sobolev inequality (Definition
3.2
) uniformly with rate
\lambda
, then

(76)

\displaystyle H(\pi_{t}^{Y}|\nu)

\displaystyle\leq e^{-2\lambda t}H(\pi_{0}^{Y}|\nu)

along the continuous-time Sinkhorn flow (
10
), (
11
).

Proof.

By Theorem
3
, definitions (
65
), (
74
), and by hypothesis,

(77)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}H(\pi_{t}^{Y}|\nu)

\displaystyle=-\langle\log\frac{d\pi_{t}^{Y}}{d\nu},(I-Q_{\pi})\log\frac{d\pi_{t}^{Y}}{d\nu}\rangle_{L^{2}(\pi_{t})}

(78)

\displaystyle=-\langle\log\frac{d\pi_{t}^{Y}}{d\nu},(I-T_{\pi})\log\frac{d\pi_{t}^{Y}}{d\nu}\rangle_{L^{2}(\pi^{Y}_{t})}

(79)

\displaystyle=-I_{\pi_{t}}(\pi_{t}^{Y}|\nu)

(80)

\displaystyle\leq-2\lambda H(\pi_{t}^{Y}|\nu)

and the result follows by application of the Grönwall inequality.
∎

Besides the theoretical significance of the above results, let us give for illustration two simple computational use-cases of the Sinkhorn LSI.

Example 1

(Latent space design for generative models)
.

In generative models trained using
{\rm OT}_{\varepsilon}
-type losses (e.g. the Sinkhorn divergence,
[
15
]
), the choice of the latent space
\phi(Y)
for training may be guided by the LSI constant (
75
) of the pushforward data marginal
\phi_{\#}\nu
; larger (uniform) LSI constants yield faster exponential convergence of inner Sinkhorn solves.
Similarly, in generative models based upon the Schrödinger bridge (e.g.
[
3
]
), a larger LSI constant in the latent space
\phi(Y)
can improve training stability and convergence rates.

Example 2

(Adaptive stopping heuristic for discrete Sinkhorn)
.

Practical uses of the Sinkhorn algorithm often use a fixed number
L
of iterations; we illustrate how
a priori
bounds for the entropy production rate can be used to set
L
.
While the entropy production identity (
54
) holds for the continuous-time Sinkhorn flow, the per-iterate entropy drop is first-order consistent with (
54
):

(81)

\displaystyle\frac{H(\pi_{n_{\rm odd}+2}^{Y}|\nu)-H(\pi_{n_{\rm odd}}^{Y}|\nu)}{\gamma}

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{dt}H(\pi_{t}^{Y}|\nu)+O(\gamma)

for step size
\gamma>0
(where
n_{\rm odd},n_{\rm even}
correspond to alternating steps of the original Sinkhorn algorithm (
7
)).
If one has an LSI
3.2
of rate
\lambda
for the marginal
\nu
, then

(82)

\displaystyle H(\pi_{n_{\rm odd}+2k}^{Y}|\nu)

\displaystyle\leq e^{-2\lambda\gamma k}H(\pi_{n_{\rm odd}}^{Y}|\nu)+O(k\gamma^{2})

(which can also be adapted for variable step-sizes).
Hence for given tolerance
\tau>0
and error
H_{0}
measured at some
n_{\rm odd}
, one can plan for

(83)

\displaystyle n

\displaystyle\geq\left\lceil\frac{1}{2\lambda\gamma}\log\frac{H_{0}}{\tau}\right\rceil

number of iterates, at which point
H_{0}
can be re-measured and checked for within tolerance, else re-start the iteration with a new estimate
n
in (
83
).
We note that as the classical Sinkhorn corresponds to
\gamma=1

[
7
]
, (
83
) is merely a heuristic to avoid computing
H
on every step.
In a
variable-step-size Sinkhorn algorithm with
\gamma\ll 1
(e.g. Definition 1,
[
7
]
), (
83
) provides a valid estimate.
