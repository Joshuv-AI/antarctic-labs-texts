GEMDAT: a Python toolkit for site-resolved diffusion analysis in solid-state molecular dynamics
Authors: Anastasia K Lavrinenko, Theodosios Famprikis, Victor Landgraf, Jouke R Heringa, Stef Smeets, Victor Azizi, Simone Ciarella, Marnix Wagemaker, Alexandros Vasileiadis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMolecular dynamics (MD) simulations have become essential for understanding diffusion mechanisms in solid-state materials such as ionic conductors, fuel cells, and gas sensors, yet most existing studies and software tools extract only standard metrics, leaving much of the information contained in the trajectories unused. Here we introduce GEMDAT, a user-friendly Python toolkit for site-resolved diffusion analysis of MD simulations of solid-state materials (https://github.com/GEMDAT-repos/GEMDAT). Beyond mean-squared displacements, radial distribution functions, and Arrhenius-based activation energies, GEMDAT provides jump rates, attempt frequencies, site-specific activation energies, rotational diffusion, and percolation. Our tool provides access to vibrational amplitudes, site geometries, and site occupancies—quantities that are also directly comparable to experimental diffraction data. Migration sites can be defined manually or identified automatically from the trajectory. A built-in caching approach, together with rapid visualization capabilities, makes the workflow fast and interactive. We demonstrate GEMDAT on a series of case studies involving crystalline Li- and Na-ion conductors, plastic crystals, amorphous structures, and surface configurations, showing how the code extracts atomic-level structural features and connects them to macroscopic transport properties, thereby guiding the optimization and development of solid-state materials.Subject terms: Chemistry, Materials science, Physics

Introduction

Mass transport plays a fundamental role in determining the performance and functionality of solid-state materials and devices used in batteries, semiconductors, fuel cells, and gas sensors1–4. Species diffusion through a material directly affects charge transport, reactivity, reaction rates, and structural stability. In solid-state materials, diffusion typically happens through thermally activated jumps between atomic sites1,5,6. Mobile species occupy these low-energy stable or metastable sites until they gain sufficient energy to jump over the barrier to neighboring sites. Mapping these sites and tracking individual jumps provides critical insights into how features, such as structural disorder, element doping, introducing vacancies, size of bottlenecks, and local symmetry, govern mass transport7–9.

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations become essential for exploring mass transport mechanisms across diverse material classes6,10,11. Classical MD enables large-scale simulations over long timescales, while ab initio MD (AIMD) provides quantum-mechanical accuracy at a higher computational cost11,12. More recently, machine-learned interatomic potentials (MLIPs), such as moment tensor potentials (MTP)13, Gaussian approximation potentials (GAP)14, and pretrained models like PET-MAD15, MACE16, and CHGNet17 have bridged these approaches, enabling near-first-principles accuracy with classical-level efficiency across chemically diverse systems.

Despite these advances in simulation capabilities, the tools available for post-analysis of MD trajectories often fail to fully extract the information they contain. General-purpose MD trajectory analysis libraries such as MDAnalysis18 and MDTraj19 were designed primarily for biomolecular systems, with data models that organize atoms into chains, residues, and molecular topologies, and analysis modules targeting protein structure, hydrogen bonding, and membrane dynamics. Although MDAnalysis provides mean-squared-displacement calculations, it lacks support for crystallographic site identification, ionic hop detection, and the VASP trajectory formats widely used in solid-state research. MDTraj similarly provides no diffusion analysis, site-specific metrics, or support for crystallographic formats. Tools such as VMD20 and OVITO21 offer great visualization and basic statistical metrics, but do not support site-specific diffusion analysis or jump-event detection. On the materials science side, Pymatgen22 and its pymatgen-analysis-diffusion add-on23 provide diffusion coefficient extraction, Arrhenius fitting, van Hove correlation functions, and site occupancy analysis within a crystal structure. However, pymatgen-analysis-diffusion does not detect discrete ionic jump events, construct site-to-site transition matrices, or compute jump-based activation energies. Its support is also largely limited to VASP-formatted trajectories, restricting applicability to classical MD simulations from other codes. The site-analysis package24 provides four site geometries (spherical, Voronoi, polyhedral, and dynamic Voronoi) that can deform dynamically within the framework, but it focuses specifically on site-projection analysis and does not compute transport properties such as diffusion coefficients, conductivities, and activation energies or perform analysis of site geometry, rotational diffusion, and percolation.

Here, we present a generalized molecular dynamics analysis tool (GEMDAT), an open-source Python toolkit designed to fill these gaps by providing comprehensive, site-resolved diffusion analysis for solid-state materials. Beyond standard tracer diffusivities and mean-squared displacements, GEMDAT provides analysis of vibrational amplitude, site occupancies, sizes, and geometry, jump rates and site-specific energy barriers, radial distribution functions, collective motion, autocorrelation functions and rotational diffusion, and jump network and percolation analysis.

We demonstrate the application of GEMDAT on several case studies of solid-state crystalline ionic conductors, plastic crystals, amorphous structures, and surface configurations—materials our research group has extensive experience with. While some GEMDAT functions benefit from a crystalline framework, which allows for applying symmetry operations and improving statistics, the code is equally capable of analyzing non-crystalline materials.

The code documentation and interactive Jupyter notebook tutorials are publicly available for the research community, and we encourage researchers to contribute to further development of GEMDAT and expand its capabilities through shared analysis workflows.

This article is organized as follows: the “Results” section demonstrates GEMDAT’s code structure, its capabilities, and application to different structural families of solid-state materials. These case studies include argyrodites (Li7−xPS6−xBrx), sodium-ion conductors and plastic crystals (Na3−xWxSb1−xS4, Na3PS4), irreducible antifluorites (Li2+xS1–xNx), amorphous materials (LixSi), and surface configurations (LixCu)—all representing disordered ion conductors. Some GEMDAT functions work particularly well with crystalline frameworks, where symmetry operations can be applied to improve statistics, but the code is equally capable of analyzing non-crystalline materials. The “Methods” section introduces physical principles underlying GEMDAT’s calculations and simulation details for the provided case studies.

Results

Code architecture

The architecture and workflow of GEMDAT are shown in Fig. 1. GEMDAT accepts two primary inputs (shown in dashed boxes): MD trajectory files from atomistic simulations (VASP, LAMMPS, GROMACS) or from Python packages (ASE, Pymatgen) and a definition of atomic sites available for occupation. These sites can be derived from Pymatgen Structure objects, CIF files, or automatically extracted from high-density regions in the trajectory. Once loaded, GEMDAT creates a Trajectory object and saves a pickle cache file to speed up subsequent analyses (see the “Methods” subsection “Trajectory loading and analysis”). The workflow supports two types of analysis—trajectory-based and site-based—indicated by black and blue arrows in Fig. 1, respectively. The trajectory object can be filtered to isolate mobile species and analyze displacement statistics, vibrational amplitudes, and attempt frequencies. Orientational analysis of polyanion rotations provides insights into rotational dynamics (see the “Methods” subsection “Orientations and rotational analysis”). Radial distribution functions characterize bonding and coordination environment, with the option to perform site-resolved analysis when atomic sites are available (see the “Methods” subsection “Radial distribution function (RDF)”). The Volume class constructs 3D density grids from trajectory, allowing users to visualize diffusion pathways and calculate activation energies (see the “Methods” subsection “Volume-based analysis”). When crystallographic sites are defined (see the “Methods” subsection “Site definition”), GEMDAT identifies ion transitions between them, extracts discrete jump events, and computes jump diffusivity, jump conductivity, and site-specific (or jump) activation energies (see the “Methods” subsection “Jump analysis”). These jump activation energies and the jump network serve as input for graph percolation analysis, which reveals active sites connected to the percolating network and determines percolation activation energies (see the “Methods” subsection “From jump library to percolation analysis”). Shape analysis characterizes the site geometry and the distribution of mobile ions around atomic sites (see the “Methods” subsection “Shape analysis”). All analysis modules support visualization through both Plotly (backend = 'plotly') for interactive exploration and Matplotlib (backend ='matplotlib') for static figures (Fig. 2).

Representative timings for trajectory reading, mean squared displacement, and radial distribution function calculations are compared with MDAnalysis, Pymatgen, and ASE across trajectories of varying size—from a short trajectories in Table S1, to a long VASP trajectory (104 atoms, 120,000 frames) and a large GROMACS trajectory (17,836 atoms, 10,001 frames) in Table S2—providing an estimate of the computational cost of each analysis type and of the feasibility of applying GEMDAT to the longer and larger trajectories now accessible with MLIPs. We note that GEMDAT’s automatic pickle caching significantly reduces trajectory-loading times on subsequent runs, which is particularly beneficial during iterative analysis.

It is worth noting that for trajectories obtained under variable-cell conditions, several modules of GEMDAT should be applied with caution. The site-assignment procedure (see the “Methods” subsection “Site definition”) relies on a static reference structure (or on density maxima extracted from a fixed-cell trajectory) and therefore assumes that the host sublattice does not undergo significant translational drift or anisotropic distortion. The voxel-based probability density (see the “Methods” subsection “Volume-based analysis”) and the derived free-energy landscape are constructed on a discretised grid of fixed dimensions, and would need to be reformulated in fractional coordinates, or recomputed on a cell-averaged reference, to remain meaningful when the lattice vectors fluctuate. Likewise, the ionic conductivity computed via the Nernst–Einstein relation (Eq. (4)) requires a particle density that becomes time-dependent under variable-cell conditions and must therefore be averaged consistently over the trajectory.

Role of disorder in argyrodites

The simulation data and analysis presented in this section are reused from ref. argyrodites8. Here, we focus on how GEMDAT’s analysis modules were applied to extract site-resolved transport properties of argyrodites and connect them to site disorder, configurational entropy, and local environments.

Li7−xPS6−xBrx argyrodites crystallize in the cubic F4®3m space group (Fig. 3a). PS43− tetrahedra occupy Wyckoff 4b positions, while halide ions at 4a sites and sulfide ions (not bonded to P5+) at 4d positions form the anion sublattice30. Due to similar ionic radii, Br− and S2– ions can exchange positions and occupy both 4a and 4d sites31. Li+ transport occurs through a network of tetrahedral interstitial sites, including T5 (48h), T2 (48h), and T4 (16e) positions, which form cage-like substructures around the 4d sites (Fig. 3b). Ion diffusion proceeds via three distinct jump mechanisms (Fig. 3c): short-range doublet (T5–T5) and intracage (T5–T2) jumps within individual cages, and long-range intercage (T2–T2 and T5–T4–T5) jumps between neighboring cages that are typically the rate-limiting steps for macroscopic transport8,32.

GEMDAT-calculated site-specific activation energies for T5–T5 doublet and T5–T4 intercage jumps showed that higher bromine occupancy at 4d sites decreases activation energy for intercage jumps but increases it for doublet jumps, causing a switch in the rate-limiting step (Fig. 5a–c). To further quantify structural changes between studied materials, we used GEMDAT shape analysis, which measured average distances between 4a/4d anion sites and surrounding lithium positions, representing cage radii (Fig. 5d–f). We found that higher bromine occupancy at 4d sites led to expansion of the 4d-centered cages, shortening of intercage distances, and an increase in doublet jump distances. Maximum conductivity occurred when 4a and 4d cages achieved similar radii (Fig. 5e), creating a uniform electrostatic force distribution.

Rotational analysis of plastic crystals

Na3PS4 has been shown to transition to a plastically crystalline (i.e., “rotor-“) phase upon heating beyond 500 °C33,34. The rotor-phase polymorph (γ-Na3PS4) exhibits distinct librational and rotational mobility of the PS43− molecular anion, along with a distinct stepwise increase in sodium ionic conductivity33,35. MD simulations can be used to capture and describe rotational mobility, which is of particular interest in understanding the interplay between rotational and translational dynamics in such rotor-phase electrolytes36.

Figure 7a shows the probability density of sulfur from AIMD simulations, demonstrating the increased librational amplitude of PS43− in γ-Na3PS4 compared to the low-temperature β-Na3PS4 polymorph (both simulated at 900 K). In β-Na3PS4, the sulfur atoms vibrate in well-defined, solid-like, approximately spherical energy minima (“sites”). In γ-Na3PS4, the probability density of sulfur is significantly more diffuse, indicating significant mobility and positional disorder, consistent with the exceedingly high displacement parameters emerging from fitting of diffraction data33.

Comparison of sulfur probability density in β- and γ-Na3PS4 from AIMD simulations at 900 K.

a Volumetric probability density in the respective simulation boxes in yellow. PS43− tetrahedra shown as stick tetrahedra, Na+ omitted for clarity. b Orientational probability density of P–S bonds in spherical coordinates.

Another convenient way to capture the orientational behavior of molecular units is by tracking bond orientations. GEMDAT creates a trajectory of vectors (here, P–S bond vectors) and can conveniently produce plots of the orientational probability function: a function on the surface of a sphere representing the probability of the bonds pointing in a particular direction. Figure 7b presents the orientational probability functions for β- and γ-Na3PS4 as functions of polar angles in a rectilinear projection. For β-Na3PS4, four sharp maxima are observed along the <111> directions of the cubic cell, indicative of a single, well-defined orientation for all tetrahedra, and consistent with the reported crystallographic model of β-Na3PS4 (I4®3m)34. For γ-Na3PS4, eight diffuse maxima are observed. The diffuse nature of the maxima is indicative of the large orientational mobility of the P–S bonds (and by extension the PS43− anions). The eight maxima represent two sets of four, each set representing a tetrahedral orientation, consistent with the two equally represented orientations for PS43− anions in the crystallographic model of γ-Na3PS4 (Fddd)33.

The data presented in Fig. 7 is consistent with the rotor-phase description of γ-Na3PS4 but does not prove per se that PS43− are dynamically reorienting (i.e., rotationally diffusing). To capture reorientations, one can determine the time-dependent self-correlation of bond orientations via the orientational self-correlation function, ζ(t), which quantifies the degree of reorientation as a function of time. GEMDAT conveniently computes ζ from bond vector trajectories, as shown in Fig. 8.

Orientational self-correlation function, ζ(t) (Eq. (8)), of P–S bonds from AIMD simulations of β- and γ-Na3PS4 at 900 K.

The shaded region represents the standard deviation of the orientational self-correlation function across all individual P–S bonds in the supercell.

For β-Na3PS4, ζ quickly decays and plateaus at a value close to 1 (~0.97). This is indicative of low-amplitude libration of the P–S bonds about their equilibrium orientation, as generally expected in any solid at finite temperature. Orientational correlations are conserved in time: the orientation of a P–S bond at any given time does not significantly differ from its previous orientation. In contrast, for γ-Na3PS4, ζ decays to 0 over time, indicating loss of orientational correlation, consistent with large-amplitude molecular reorientations over time (i.e., rotor-phase behavior). The decay of ζ can be fitted to an exponential decay to quantify a characteristic time for PS43− reorientations, which is of the order of 50 ps at 900 K, consistent with the value reported by Sau and Ikeshoji from classical MD simulations35.

Site shape and geometry analysis of sodium-ion conductors

Partial aliovalent W6+ substitution in Na2.875W0.125Sb0.875S4 stabilizes the cubic β-Na3PS4-type structure (Fig. 9a) while introducing substantial sodium disorder through vacancy creation37. This structural complexity, characterized by anharmonic ionic distributions and partial site occupancies, poses significant challenges for conventional crystallographic refinement.

We used GEMDAT shape analysis to examine the sodium-ion density distribution from a 50 ps AIMD simulation at 500 K and identify the optimal crystallographic model for the Na2.875W0.125Sb0.875S4 structure. Analysis using the conventional Wyckoff 6b site assignment (0, 0, 0.5)34,37 (Fig. 9a) captured a sodium occupancy of 0.95, which agrees well with the nominal stoichiometry. However, this assignment required a large site radius of 1.5 Å, which exceeds the default threshold of twice the vibrational amplitude (A = 0.51 Å for Na⁺). GEMDAT shape analysis further revealed anisotropic sodium distribution, showing a two-dimensional diamond-like shape (Fig. 9c). This finding is consistent with previous diffraction and computational studies of Na3PS4-type structures37,38. To enable direct comparison with experimental crystallographic data and extract quantitative anisotropic atomic displacement parameters (ADPs), we fitted the three-dimensional sodium coordinate distributions to Gaussian models using the scikit-learn39 GaussianMixture module, a probabilistic framework that assumes data points are generated from a mixture of Gaussian distributions with unknown parameters (Fig. 10). For the Wyckoff 6b position, we fitted a one-component three-dimensional Gaussian mixture to extract the covariance matrix, which directly relates to the anisotropic displacement parameter tensor U. The covariance matrix was converted to anisotropic displacement parameter using the following equation40: B=8π2U. This analysis yielded an isotropic displacement parameter Biso ≈ 10.6 Å2 with strong anisotropy, as reflected in the eigenvalues of the B tensor, showing significantly larger displacements in the ab-plane than in the c-direction (Fig. 10c). Such large displacement parameters, while capturing the observed probability density distribution, are relatively big for conventional thermal motion at 500 K, indicating either local disorder or insufficient crystallographic site definition.

Projections of sodium coordinates distribution (in blue) with 3D Gaussian fit.

General fit is shown in a solid red line, while Gaussian components are shown in dashed lines. a One-component 3D Gaussian to represent a crystallographic model with Wyckoff 6b position. b Four-component 3D Gaussian to represent a crystallographic model with Wyckoff 24f position. c Projected coordinate densities with anisotropy derived from a one-component Gaussian mixture. d Projected coordinate densities with anisotropy derived from a four-component Gaussian mixture.

As an alternative, we evaluated the Wyckoff 24f site model (Fig. 9b), in which sodium is represented by four discrete, partially occupied positions at the diamond vertices. We fitted a four-component 3D Gaussian mixture model (Fig. 10b) to extract optimal 24f site parameters from the AIMD-derived probability density distribution. The fitting results in the components with equal statistical weights, which reflect the symmetry-equivalent nature, and an isotropic displacement parameter Biso ≈ 6.0 Å2, significantly smaller than that for the Wyckoff 6b model. The mean positions extracted from the fit—representing the centers of the four Gaussian components—directly correspond to fractional coordinates (0.406, 0, 0.5) for the Wyckoff 24f sodium sites (Fig. 9b), matching the crystallographic model reported in previous studies34,37,38 and validating the GEMDAT analysis methodology.

Percolation analysis in disordered irreducible antifluorites

The simulation data and analysis presented in this section are reused from ref. 9. Here, we focus on demonstrating the GEMDAT-based workflow for extracting jump-specific activation energies and performing percolation analysis to examine Li+ ion mobility in ordered and disordered configurations of irreducible antifluorite electrolyte Li9S3N.

In the anion-disordered lithium-rich antifluorite structure, Li+ primarily occupies tetrahedral sites, with octahedral sites only partially occupied9,41. Using GEMDAT jump analysis, three jump pathways were identified from the AIMD trajectories: tetrahedral-to-octahedral (tet-oct) through triangular three-anion bottlenecks, octahedral-to-tetrahedral (oct-tet) through the same triangular bottlenecks, and tetrahedral-to-tetrahedral (tet-tet) jumps through linear two-anion bottlenecks (Fig. 11).

To capture all possible local jump environments, we generated multiple independent disordered configurations with a random S/N distribution while maintaining a 3:1 stoichiometry. We used GEMDAT to extract jump-specific activation energies and characterize each jump by its initial site coordination, destination site coordination, and bottleneck composition. The disordered S/N arrangement enabled 91 distinct jump types (Fig. 11f–h), compared to only 6 in the ordered phase (Fig. 11b–d). The resulting distribution of jump-specific activation energies (Fig. 12a) revealed that certain local environments created by disorder enabled low-energy pathways with activation barriers below 0.35 eV, while the minimum barrier in ordered Li₉S₃N exceeded 0.43 eV. Low-energy jumps generally occurred in the nitrogen-rich bottlenecks, which can be attributed to the smaller ionic radius and higher charge density of N3−, creating wider migration channels.

Building on these GEMDAT-extracted activation energies, we developed a percolation model9 operating on 5 × 5 × 5 supercells (1500 Li sites)—system sizes that exceed AIMD accessibility yet can be analyzed using only jump activation energies from smaller AIMD cells. The model represents the lithium sublattice as a graph where Li sites are nodes and jumps are edges. Two sites are connected only when both forward (i → j) and backward (j → i) jumps have activation energies below the threshold Ecutoff. The network is considered percolating if it spans the entire supercell.

At an energy cutoff of Ecutoff = 0.4 eV, ordered Li9S3N showed no percolating pathways. Lack of tet-oct and tet-tet jumps below this barrier created “inactive” sites that are not connected to the percolation network and cannot participate in long-range diffusion. At the same energy cutoff, disordered Li9S3N exhibited percolating networks with a significantly larger amount of lithium sites connected to it (green sites in Fig. 12b). The percolation-energy diagrams provided further quantitative insights (Fig. 12c), plotting the fraction of Li sites connected to percolation networks as a function of the energy threshold. The minimum percolation threshold was 0.34 eV for disordered compared to 0.43 eV for ordered Li9S3N, reflecting the availability of low-energy jumps created by nitrogen-rich environments. These simulated percolation energies aligned closely with experimental conductivity activation energies (0.39 eV disordered, 0.47 eV ordered)9.

Lithium diffusion in amorphous silicon

The diffusion of lithium in bulk amorphous silicon was investigated to provide an atomistic rationale for the rate-limiting kinetics governing the electrochemical lithiation of Si anodes. Although Si offers a theoretical capacity approximately ten times that of graphite, its practical performance is constrained by Li transport through the disordered host network42,43. Prior computational work has established that the heterogeneous void distribution in Si yields a broad spectrum of migration barriers42, yet the relationship between local structural topology and macroscopic diffusivity remains incompletely understood, motivating the present analysis.

The diffusive behavior of the Li and Si sublattices in the amorphous Li36Si64 supercell was characterized by analyzing the MSD extracted from the AIMD trajectories using GEMDAT (Fig. 13). The tracer diffusion coefficient was determined as 1.22 × 10−10 m2 s−1. This value falls within the range reported for amorphous LixSi at low-to-intermediate lithiation levels from both AIMD42 and experimental tracer diffusion measurements44, and is several orders of magnitude larger than diffusivities measured in crystalline Si at room temperature45, underscoring the kinetic advantage of the amorphous structure for Li transport.

Analysis of Li trajectories using GEMDAT identified preferred Li-occupancy sites within the amorphous Si matrix (Fig. 13a, b). Li atoms were found to vibrate locally about their equilibrium positions with characteristic displacements of 1.0–1.4 Å (Fig. 13c), giving rise to a number of distinct sites. The proximity of these distances suggests that adjacent preferred sites are separated by flat energy barriers, permitting frequent short-range hops while the atoms remain trapped within the same structural void of the Si network over extended timescales. This is in qualitative agreement with the picture established by previous studies42,44, who showed that Li preferentially occupies large ring interstices in the disordered Si network and that local structural topology governs the accessibility of neighboring sites.

The mean activation energy per Li jump event, obtained from the GEMDAT transition rate analysis, is 0.197 ± 0.013 eV. This value falls within the reported range of microscopic barriers in bulk Si (~0.1–2.4 eV)42 and reflects the relatively low-energy hops between nearby sites observed in the AIMD trajectories.

At first glance, this is much lower than the macroscopic activation energy of ~1.4 eV measured in isotopic tracer experiments44. However, this difference does not indicate a discrepancy but rather arises from the fundamentally different transport regimes probed by the two approaches. AIMD captures intrinsic short-range hopping between locally connected sites, which involves low activation barriers. In contrast, macroscopic diffusion depends on the formation of continuous percolation pathways across the network. This long-range transport is limited by deep trapping events associated with undercoordinated or strained Si environments, which dominate transport over experimental timescales and thus determine the effective activation energy44.

Consistent with this picture, the simulations reveal pronounced local mobility without the emergence of long-range diffusion. Frequent short-range hopping occurs between nearby sites (Fig. 13b), showing short-range connectivity (Fig. 13c), yet this motion does not translate into macroscopic transport, as evidenced by the very low MSD (Fig. 13d) and the limited network connectivity shown in Fig. 13a.

Lithium diffusion on copper surface

The mobility of lithium on copper surfaces was investigated to elucidate the mechanistic origin of the reversible Li uptake observed experimentally in Cu current collectors during electrochemical plating and stripping46. Although Li incorporation into Cu has been reported previously47,48, the absence of lattice parameter changes in operando X-ray diffraction measurements46 suggested that transport proceeds preferentially via grain boundary pathways rather than bulk lattice diffusion, further supported by the quantification of grain boundary versus lattice diffusion coefficients in Cu49. Understanding Li surface mobility is therefore essential for rationalizing current collector degradation and informing strategies to suppress Li trapping in next-generation Li-metal batteries50,51.

The MD trajectory data from ref. 46. were reanalyzed using GEMDAT for four Li atoms co-adsorbed on the Cu(111) surface. In the original study, two distinct adsorption sites were identified from DFT calculations: the energetically favorable p2 positions, where Li maximizes its distance from the three nearest surface Cu atoms, and the less stable p1 positions, lying ~100 meV higher in energy46. Reanalysis of the transition statistics extracted from the AIMD trajectories confirmed that Li atoms reside predominantly at p2 sites, with only transient events through the p1 positions (Fig. 14). No measurable energy barrier was found for migration between adjacent p2 sites, indicating an essentially flat potential energy surface governing lateral Li transport along the Cu(111) surface. The tracer diffusion coefficient extracted from the MSD is 4.49 × 10−8 m2 s−1 (Fig. 14d), confirming that Li diffusion on this surface is rapid. Taken together, these results support the interpretation that grain boundary surfaces in the Cu current collector provide facile Li transport pathways, offering a microscopic rationalization for the reversible Li uptake observed experimentally during electrochemical plating and stripping46,49.

Discussion

The research examples presented throughout this work demonstrate how GEMDAT enables the extraction of mechanistic atomic-scale insights from MD simulations and connects them to macroscopic transport properties. Its general analysis framework can be applied across diverse solid-state material families. Beyond common metrics such as mean-squared displacement and radial distribution functions, GEMDAT offers analytical tools that were previously inaccessible or available only through custom scripts tailored to a specific material.

GEMDAT’s trajectory analysis uses atomic sites to discretize ion motion into individual jump events and calculates diffusional properties, including site occupancy and geometry, jump rates, jump diffusivity, and site-specific activation energies. Analysis of the jump network and mechanism reveals how local coordination environments and bottleneck compositions control migration barriers. In addition to jump analysis, GEMDAT also investigates rotational dynamics, site geometry, and percolation pathways. Rotational analysis provides insights into the orientational disorder of polyanions and rotational diffusion. Analysis of atomic site geometry characterizes anisotropic ion distributions that define the shape of sites and allows the extraction of atomic displacement parameters that can be directly compared to experimental diffraction data. Percolation analysis identifies which sites are connected to the percolation network and examines how structural disorder creates low-energy pathways that enable macroscopic conductivity.

The demonstrated applicability of GEMDAT across structurally diverse and highly disordered lithium and sodium conductors establishes it as a versatile tool for analyzing MD in solid-state materials. Its modular and customizable architecture ensures that new analysis methods can be easily incorporated as the field advances. By providing general, reproducible workflows for extracting transport properties at the atomic level from MD trajectories, GEMDAT contributes to the understanding of the fundamental principles governing ionic transport in solid-state materials.

Methods

Trajectory loading and analysis

The Trajectory class in gemdat.trajectory loads MD simulation data from multiple packages using Pymatgen’s trajectory readers as backend. The Trajectory.from_vasprun() method reads VASP XML files, Trajectory.from_lammps() reads LAMMPS trajectory files requiring structure and temperature inputs, Trajectory.from_gromacs() reads GROMACS trajectory and topology files requiring temperature input and supporting extraction of time-series energy data from GROMACS EDR file, and Trajectory.from_ase_trajectory() reads ASE trajectory file requiring temperature input. Upon first loading, GEMDAT automatically saves a pickle cache file, significantly reducing loading time for subsequent analysis. The Trajectory object stores a time series of atomic positions for all species, along with simulation cell lattice parameters, species information, timestep data, and simulation temperature. The underlying data is stored as NumPy arrays, enabling efficient memory access and standard Python indexing and slicing. Periodic boundary conditions are automatically handled when computing displacements and distances.

Filtering the trajectory to isolate mobile ion species is a critical preprocessing step for extracting diffusion metrics. The Trajectory.filter(list_of_species) method creates a new trajectory with only the specified species. Before identifying discrete jumps, GEMDAT analyzes continuous trajectory properties. The TrajectoryMetrics class in gemdat.metrics computes fundamental diffusion properties from atomic displacements without requiring the definition of atomic sites. The TrajectoryMetricsStd class provides block analysis52,53 for error estimation. The Trajectory class also provides built-in plotting methods for preliminary analysis.

The Trajectory class additionally provides an interface to the Bayesian diffusion analysis implemented in the kinisi package29,56. kinisi likewise computes the MSD through overlapping sliding-window sampling but additionally estimates the variance of the observed MSD by rescaling the variance of the observed squared displacements by the number of numerically independent subtrajectories in the simulation. The kinisi DiffusionAnalyzer then models the resulting MSD as a multivariate normal distribution with an analytically derived covariance matrix and samples the diffusion coefficient via Bayesian regression. This approach provides an efficient estimate of the diffusion coefficient with an accurate quantification of its statistical uncertainty. A more detailed description of the methodology can be found in ref. 29.

The RDF is determined by calculating the distance between all species pairs ∣ri(t)−rj(t)∣, for each time step t and binning them into a histogram. M is the total number of time steps. The radial_distribution_between_species(specie_1, specie_2) function provides RDFs between two species or lists of species, requiring only trajectory data. The radial_distribution(transitions, floating_specie) function computes site-resolved RDFs, calculating pair distributions of mobile ions around each atom type while separating mobile species by the site type they occupy. This returns a dictionary of RDFData objects, one for each site-pair type. This function requires a Transitions object created by combining the trajectory and the crystallographic sites structure (see Methods: Site Definition).

Volume-based analysis

The Volume class in gemdat.volume converts trajectories into discretized 3D grids that represent the time-averaged probability density of finding given species at each position. This probability density distribution provides a continuous view of diffusion pathways and enables automatic extraction of atomic sites. A volume object can be constructed using volume = trajectory.to_volume(resolution = 0.1), where the resolution parameter controls the voxel size in Å. The probability density Pξ is obtained directly from the MD trajectory by accumulating, for each voxel ξ, the number of times the diffusing species is found within that voxel across all trajectory frames. The resulting occupancy histogram is normalized by the total number of counts, yielding a spatially resolved probability density of the mobile ion. Note that voxels are not necessarily cubic, as their shape is determined by the lattice parameters of the simulation cell.

The Volume class stores the 3D density array and lattice information and provides multiple analysis capabilities. Density data is interactively visualized as isosurfaces using volume.plot(), revealing regions of high occupancy. When atomic sites are provided, they are overlaid on the density map to correlate ion diffusion with site positions (Fig. 2g). Density data can also be exported in CHGCAR format for visualization in VESTA61 or other crystallographic software.

To automatically extract site positions from local density maxima, the Volume.to_structure() method uses a watershed algorithm62, with the background_level parameter controlling peak sensitivity—higher values reduce spurious peaks in noisy regions. The resulting peak centers define a set of site positions that can be converted into a structure object for subsequent analyses.

The Volume class also calculates activation energies along diffusion pathways. The time-averaged probability density P(ξ) is first converted to a free energy landscape63:

Voxels that are frequently visited by the mobile species correspond to low-energy positions, whereas rarely visited voxels along connecting channels correspond to transition regions. The activation energy associated with a given migration pathway is then extracted as the difference between the maximum of Aξ along the path and the value of Aξ at the initial site. Optimal paths between sites are found using graph-based path-finding algorithms (Fig. 2h). The 3D grid is converted into a graph, in which voxels are nodes and edges connect neighboring voxels, with weights proportional to the free energy. Dijkstra’s64 or Bellman–Ford65,66 algorithm identifies the minimum energy path between specified sites. Multiple alternative pathways can be found by iteratively removing previously identified routes and re-running the search.

Site definition

Site-based diffusion analysis requires defining the positions between which diffusing ions jump. Sites can be determined directly from a Pymatgen Structure object, loaded from a CIF file, or extracted from trajectory density data (see the “Methods” subsection “Volume-based analysis”).

The Transitions class in gemdat.transitions assigns each mobile ion to its nearest site at each time step by defining a radius around each atomic position. If no site lies within the defined radius, the ion is assigned to index -1, indicating an interstitial or unassigned position. By default, the doubled vibrational amplitude is used as the site radius. The algorithm automatically prevents double-counting when sites overlap by reducing the radius to half the inter-site distance. The Transitions object stores two key matrices: the occupancy matrix denoting which site ion i occupies at time t, and the transition event matrix recording all site-to-site changes. These matrices enable comprehensive occupancy statistics and jump analysis through built-in methods.

Jump analysis

The Jumps class in gemdat.jumps takes a Transitions object (see the “Methods” subsection “Site definition”) as an input and extracts discrete jump events from the transitions data (Fig. 2i). There are two key parameters that help to distinguish these jump events from thermal vibrations. The distance threshold defines the inner sphere radius of the site core region. A jump is only registered when an atom leaves the inner sphere of its initial site and enters the inner sphere of the destination site. The site_inner_fraction parameter in Transitions specifies what fraction of the site radius determines this inner sphere. The time threshold specifies the minimum residence time required at the destination site to confirm that a transition has occurred and is controlled by the minimal_residence parameter in Jumps.

From Jump Library to percolation analysis

Jump network and jump activation energies are the basis for percolation analysis, which provides insights into long-range diffusion and the identification of transport pathways in complex materials. The methodology9 requires an initial characterization of the local structural environments surrounding each atomic site, followed by the construction of an environment-specific jump library extracted from MD simulations using GEMDAT (Fig. 2k). Characterization of the environment is based on the nearest-neighbor shell, although second- and higher-shell neighbors can also be included where relevant.

The model represents the lithium sublattice as a directed graph where diffusion atom sites are nodes and possible jumps between sites are directed edges, indicating the jump direction. The disordered local environment around sites is generated randomly and is highly dependent on the structure under investigation. Two sites are considered connected only when both forward (i → j) and backward (j → i) jumps exhibit randomly sampled environment-specific activation energies from their respective uncertainty distributions below a given energy threshold. A path is percolating if it spans the supercell by wrapping around the cell under periodic boundary conditions, i.e., it connects opposite sides of the supercell in at least one lattice direction, and the end point is also a valid start point. For each energy cutoff, the percolation analysis computes the fraction of sites participating in system-spanning clusters. The analysis requires statistical averaging over multiple random structure configurations to ensure convergence.

Due to high specificity to the investigating structure, percolation analysis is not included as a general GEMDAT module. However, our tutorials provide examples of the general analysis supported by GEMDAT, as well as system-specific code for the percolation model and graph analysis.

Shape analysis

The ShapeAnalyzer class in gemdat.shape characterizes the density-weighted distribution of mobile ions around atomic sites, providing descriptors of both occupancy and geometry (Fig. 2l). The ShapeAnalyzer is initialized from a reference atomic site structure. The .from_structure() constructor symmetrizes the input structure using space group operations, identifying all symmetrically equivalent site positions. This reduces the analysis to unique site types and improves statistics. At each time step, GEMDAT identifies all mobile ions within a specified radius of each site and transforms their coordinates to the site’s local coordinate system. Accumulated coordinates over the trajectory build statistics.

ShapeData objects are visualized using shape.plot(bins), creating 2D histograms projecting the displacement cloud around the site along three orthogonal directions. This reveals the time-averaged geometry of atomic distribution. To relate site geometry to crystal structure, crystallographic sites can be overlaid on plots using shape.plot(bins, sites). The ShapeAnalyzer.optimize_sites() method refines site positions by computing the centroid of each site cluster, improving site assignments when the input structure contains slightly misplaced sites.
