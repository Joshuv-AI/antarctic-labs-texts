Modelling and economic evaluation of a guar gum–enhanced electrocoagulation process optimized by BBD with sludge characterization for DB183 dye removal
Authors: Mohammad Mehralian, Maryam Khashij, Edris Bazrafshan, Milad Nasimi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study presented an electrocoagulation process integrated with guar gum coagulant aid as a natural and green material to degrade Disperse Blue183 dye as an environmental contaminant. Unlike traditional chemical coagulants (e.g., PAC, alum), GG is biodegradable, non-toxic, and capable of forming strong hydrogen bonding with dye molecules, thereby improving floc density and reducing secondary pollution. The operation variables of the process were optimized based on the aluminium plate as an electrode and dye removal with sludge characterization as an assessment indicator. Under optimum conditions (pH 9.0, time 35.0 min, and j 47.0 mA/cm2), electrocoagulation integrated with Guar Gum removed 96% of the Disperse Blue183 dye with a 150 mg/L of GG. The R2 coefficient of 0.98 showed that the model is significant using Box-Behnken Design. SEM showed that the floc was dense and FTIR indicated hydrogen bonding between Guar Gum and Disperse Blue183 dye in the electrocoagulation process. Economic evaluation displayed that the current technique cost 0.94 $.m− 3. These results confirm that Guar Gum coagulant aid can effectively promote floc structure in the electrocoagulation process and also provide a novel technique for practical applications to dye degradation from wastewater. Introduction

Today, population growth and urbanization increase the demand for resources, enhancing the growth of industry and thus the generation of wastewater. The various industries such as textile, leather, paper, cosmetic, and plastic industries use various organic dyes according to the product requirements1. The annual production of organic dyes was reported as 7 × 105 tons, containing hazardous organic pollutants2. Approximately 15% of the textile dyes produced are directly discharged into the environment, creating major ecological toxicity due to the high amount of carcinogens, mutagens, and genotoxins present; several dyes possess intense chemical stability and are not easily degradable3,4. Dyes may undergo biological and chemical transformations with the consequent release of more toxic by-products. As a result, even minimal traces of such contaminants can make water unsuitable for human consumption5,6. Also, turbidity generated by the presence of dye in water bodies reduces sunlight penetration and decreases dissolved oxygen rates, thus interfering with aquatic organisms7,8.

Proper treatment of dyed wastewater is therefore necessary before its release into water bodies. Various processes such as adsorption, oxidation, chemical coagulation, and, etc. have been applied to dyes removal from wastewater9–11. However, these procedures were expensive and involved operational concerns12. As a result, interest in the study of novel and low-cost processes increased to overcome these problems in reproducibility. Electrocoagulation is an effective technology that decreases sludge generation13. In this process, hydrogen bubbles and hydroxyl radicals are produced at the cathode, while metal ions from the anodes react with these radicals to form hydroxide flocs. The flocs act to aggregate the metal ions, precipitating them in aid of hydrogen gas. Efficiency and energy consumption are both affected profoundly by operating parameters14.

Recently, integrated techniques have been applied to enhance pollutant removal efficiency, minimize operating costs, overcome treatment limitations, reduce installation space, and management of sludge production3. The addition of a coagulant aid enhances the electrocoagulation process by improving the formation and settling of flocs, leading to better removal of the DB183 dye. This integration is optimized through methods like Box-Behnken Design (BBD) to find the best conditions for both the electrocoagulation and the coagulant aid to work together efficiently. Researchers have worked on the application of integrated methods to remove dye. Wang et al. applied an EC process integrated with polymeric aluminum chloride (PAC) and poly methyl acrylate (PMA) to increase the size of the flocs15. The EC with a chemical coagulation system was used to treat brewery wastewater by Abdulrazzaq et al.3. An EC process applied along with a coagulant aid can provide enhanced floc strength and pollutant removal. There is still limited information available regarding this technique. The coagulation technique involves the addition of coagulants directly into the wastewater. Inorganic compounds, such as aluminum sulfate, are widely used but have created high concentrations of aluminum that cause environmental problems16. On the other hand, organic coagulants propose advantages related to low toxicity, low sludge formation, and low cost besides being biodegradable and environmentally friendly4. Guar gum (GG) is a kind of natural material made from the endosperm of seeds called Cyamopsis and it has been known to be a green, eco-friendly, harmless material17,18.

Recently, using eco-friendly coagulants is one of the main fields that researchers have focus on to eliminate contaminants from wastewater. This study explores the combination of the EC process and GG as a green and natural substance for dye removal. This approach improves efficiency and highlights sustainability by using a biodegradable and eco-friendly material. Also, GG owing to its low cost, high availability, biodegradability, and strong bridging ability, offers a more practical and sustainable aid in process compared to other natural coagulants aid. In this study, Disperse Blue 183 (DB183) dye was employed as a model dye to evaluate the effectiveness of an integrated EC process with a GG coagulant aid (EC-GG). DB183 is a chemically stable and environmentally persistent compound. It is found in textile effluents, which are toxic and dangerous to aquatic life due to the compound’s low biodegradability and high toxicity. Therefore, it is essential to remove it from wastewater to protect the environment and human health19. The primary objective of this research is to investigate the EC-GG process, optimize its parameters using the BBD, assess its cost-benefit analysis, and characterize the structure of the generated sludge through this novel method.

EC-GG reactor setup

Figure 1 shows the experimental setup of the EC-GG reactor. The batch process of an EC reactor was carried out for 1000 mL of synthetic wastewater. In this regard, six aluminium electrodes were used with a dimension of 8 × 8 cm. Plate electrodes were placed vertically and parallel with an interelectrode distance of 1.5 cm. The DC power source was connected to apply the current for the EC process. Current density and voltage were measured regularly by a multimeter. The synthetic wastewater was stirred using a magnetic stirrer. The pH was adjusted with 0.1 N NaOH and H2SO4 solutions. GG powder was used as a coagulant aid varying from 10.0 to 200 mg/L. Samples were taken out from the EC-GG reactor and analyzed for the removal percentage of dye, COD, and turbidity. Before each run, the plates were cleaned with distilled water and replaced after every two runs. All experimental conditions were performed with three replicates. The efficiency of the EC-GG reactor and energy consumption were also investigated under optimum conditions.

Experimental schematic of EC-GG reactor.

Box-Behnken design

A BBD was executed to four independent parameters and DoE was performed to calculate the number of runs with various independent variables. The parameters include pH (A), time (B), current density (C), and dye concentration (D). Based on the variables, the number of runs was obtained by 29 runs using BBD.

Characterization of sludge

Analysis of scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images has been used to study the characteristics of sludge, highlighting details such as particle morphology and structure. For SEM analysis, representative portions of the sludge were carefully selected to ensure homogeneity at magnification ×100 μm (FE-SEM TESCAN MIRA4). SEM analysis was conducted on the sludge in a 1 cm x 1 cm area, which was coated with a thin gold layer for sputter-coating.

For FTIR analysis, the sludge samples were prepared to ensure good interaction with the infrared beam. The FTIR analysis was performed using a PerkinElmer Spectrum Two spectrometer. Spectra were recorded over the wavenumber range of 4000 to 400 cm-1. This range covers most of the fundamental vibrational frequencies of common functional groups found in organic and inorganic matter.

Interaction between variables affecting on EC-GG process

Figure 3a depicts the 3D interaction between the variables on the DB183 removal. The interaction of pH (A) and time (B) is presented in Fig. 3 a. The results illustrated that with pH increasing from 3.0 to 9.0, the removal rate increases. The elimination of DB183 dye increased significantly as the initial pH increased from 3.0 to 9.0. When the initial pH was set at 9.0, there was a 74% improvement in the removal of DB183 dye. This is attributed to Al+ 3 ions interact with hydroxide ions in the reaction. On the other hand, the metal species (Al+ 3, Al(OH)x3−, Al(OH)−4 ​) are highly amphoteric. A significant secondary coagulation region can occur at very high pH due to the formation of the highly reactive aluminate species Al(OH)4−​, which may complex directly with the dye. This is similar to Negash et al. findings on eliminating Reactive Red 12022. The interaction between time (B) and current density (C) on DB183 dye degradation rate was conducted by changing C from 8 to 47 mA/cm2 under different times from 10 to 60 min, and the percentage of DB183 dye degradation by different levels of both current density (mA/cm2) and time (min) was shown at Fig. 3b. According to Fig. 3b, the removal rate of DB183 dye increased as the current density increased at any given time. The increase in current density leads to a high concentration of Al3+ being generated from the anodic metal dissolution, resulting in an enhancement of the coagulation process and the release of more bubbles at the cathode, which leads to the flotation of contaminants23. According to Faraday’s law [(Eq. 6)], increasing the current density leads to a higher rate of anode dissolution. This increased aluminum hydroxide Al(OH)3 flocs as an adsorbent material, which improved dye removal efficiency5,12.

Where m, I, and t are the rate of Al dissolved in the reactor, the current density, and the time (s), respectively. M is the molecular weight of aluminum, and n = 3 also represents the number of electrons. The Faraday constant is denoted by the F. Different initial concentrations of DB183, such as 10.0, to 70.0 mg/L, were used to explain the impact of dye concentration on the degradation rate applying the EC-GG process for 60 min under an applied current density of 47.0 mA/cm2. In Fig. 3(c), the removal rate of DB183 was plotted with initial dye concentration and time. The degradation of DB183 dye decreased from 62% to 57% with the increase in dye concentration from 10.0 to 70.0 mg/L at a constant pH of 9.0 after 35.0 min of electrolysis. According to Faraday’s law, when the current density and time are constant, the same amount of metal species is dissolved from the aluminum anode, regardless of the dye concentration. As a result, a consistent concentration of aluminum hydroxide is obtained in the aqueous solution. This indicates that the presence of absorbent species in the solution is less significant. This explains the observed decrease in dye removal efficiency for DB183 in high-concentration solutions at a constant current density. These findings align well with existing literature on dyes and the same treatment process24,25. In the simultaneous EC and addition of coagulant aid, there are many advantages. Certain studies have been conducted on the application of the integrated system to remove contaminants26,27. According to studies, a synergistic effect has been identified as the primary mechanism for removing contaminants in this integrated treatment process. The increased removal efficiency associated with the improved flocculant structure was achieved through the combination of a coagulant aid and Al(OH)n coagulation. Furthermore, a combined treatment process led to the restoration of floc damage during floc fluctuation, resulting in the reconstitution of more stable flocs and enhanced removal of contaminants27. Wang et al. also reported that PAC assists in the regrowth of flocs after secondary breakage15. Compared to the simple EC process, the addition of a quantity of 5 g/L of banana peel (BP) in the electrocoagulation reactor resulted in faster decolorization. The highest removal of dye by adding BP was obtained at low current density values, although even at high current density values the separation process was significantly improved28. Watermelon rinds were also investigated by Kouniba et al. as a bio-coagulant for achieving maximum removal efficiency of 99.21% for turbidity in water29. In addition, avocado seed powder was used by Zourif et al. to remove of 94.34% for methylene blue, 95.28% for crystal violet, and 99.64% for turbidity30. In the present study, EC integrated with GG achieved 96% removal of DB183 dye under optimized conditions (pH 9.0, reaction time 35 min, current density 47 mA/cm², and 150 mg/L GG). When these results are compared quantitatively with those of other reported bio-coagulants, it becomes evident that the EC–GG process performs at least comparably, and in some cases more effectively, depending on the contaminant and operating parameters. For example, while banana peel generally shows efficiencies in the range of 75–95% for turbidity removal, and watermelon rind achieves between 72 and 99% depending on conditions, the optimized GG system provides consistently high dye removal at a moderate dosage. Similarly, although avocado seed powder has demonstrated high removal of certain dyes and turbidity, these studies were not conducted under electrocoagulation-integrated conditions, making direct comparison less straightforward. Overall, this quantitative comparison demonstrates that the EC–GG process is not only competitive with but also complementary to other bio-coagulants reported in the literature. Its high efficiency, combined with the advantages of sludge densification through electrocoagulation, highlights its potential for practical application to treat wastewater containing dyes. As a result, an integrated EC-GG process could be applicable for dense sludge structure generation.

GG dosage optimization

The effect of GG coagulant aid dosages on the removal efficiency of DB183 dye, COD, turbidity, and settling time as well as floc structure was investigated under the various concentrations of the GG (Fig. 4). Based on Fig. 4, GG addition enhanced the removal efficiency of dye, COD, and turbidity. The removal percentages of DB183 dye, COD, and turbidity were 96%, 93%, and 98% at 150 mg/L of GG, respectively. At 150 mg/L of GG, the time required for sludge settling was obtained 6 min. By increasing the GG amount, the agglomeration and the gravity influence became more pronounced. These are related to decreasing the settling time, resulting in better management of sludge production and lower concentrations of dye, COD, and turbidity. The bending of GG molecules in the wastewater took place due to attraction by the hydrogen bonds. GG had a long chain and was thus capable of connecting more solid particles that were suspended in the wastewater. Indeed, GG, can play a role in sludge reuse by improving its dewatering and thickening properties. This can make it easier to handle and transport sludge, reducing the need for landfill disposal and promoting its use as a fertilizer or soil amendment. This behavior is similar to other studies regarding different coagulant aids31. On the other hand, the excessive amount of GG led to disrupting the ability to bind floccules together, causing the formation of floccules with a lower sedimentation rate32.

SEM and FTIR results of the sludge structure

Based on the studies, GG powder has an irregular shape with a rough surface26. This means that it provides the surface area for more sites to allow for adsorption in coagulation-flocculation. Figure 5 (a-b) showed the morphology of the flocs using SEM analysis before and after the addition of GG. It can be observed from Fig. 5 that the flocs are neat and dense. It proves that the GG flocs are well-structured and compact. The capability of GG in the sludge settling time is due to its affinity for interaction with high polymeric groups. Significantly, GG has been applied in water/wastewater purification processes33. Additionally, the replacement of conventional inorganic coagulants like alum and PAC by natural GG coagulant aid is generally recognized as a safe and green product and hence its use would not disturb the ecosystem34.

SEM image of (a) flocs and (b) GG flocs after EC-GG process (magnification 1000×, scale bar 100 μm).

Figure 6 shows the FTIR spectra for the GG flocs formed during the EC-GG process. The active functional groups responsible for removing the DB183 dye were determined using FTIR analysis. A strong and broad peak at 3017.32 cm− 1 and a narrower peak at 1035.90 cm− 1 of O–H and C–O stretch confirmed the characteristic of the polymeric compound in the GG compound. The peak at 2902.13 cm− 1 was indicative of C–H stretching of aliphatic structures26. Accordingly, the dissociated carboxyl groups were represented by peaks from 1760 to 1600 cm− 1 and thus could play a role in coagulation through ion bridges and binding sites35. The FTIR analysis of GG flocs after the EC-GG process reflects the disappearance of the broad peak representing the alcohol groups, which is replaced by a free O-H stretch vibration at 3189.78 cm− 1. This is evidence of the importance of hydrogen bonding in the coagulation-flocculation process involving the GG powder and contaminants. In addition to SEM and FTIR analyses, sludge settling behavior was also examined, providing complementary insight into its physical properties as illustrated in Fig. 4. Finally, the sludge generated by EC alone is typically bulkier, with lower settling velocity and higher volume, which may cause operational challenges in separation and handling. In contrast, when GG is applied as a coagulant aid, the sludge exhibits denser and more compact flocs with improved settling characteristics and reduced overall sludge volume. This improvement is attributed to the bridging and adsorption properties of guar gum, which enhance floc formation and stability36.

Sludge generation

Figure 7 illustrates the sludge generation at different current densities (8, 27.5, and 47 mA/cm²). Based on the Fig. 7, sludge generation increases with the rise in current density for both EC-GG and EC alone. At the lowest current density of 8 mA/cm², sludge production is minimal, with EC alone producing slightly more sludge (~ 200 mg) compared to EC-GG (~ 150 mg). At 27.5 mA/cm², sludge generation rises significantly, reaching approximately 650 mg for EC alone and 600 mg for EC-GG. At the highest current density of 47 mA/cm², sludge generation peaks, with EC alone generating around 1100 mg and EC-GG producing about 950 mg. Overall, the data suggest that the presence of GG in the EC process slightly reduces sludge generation at all current densities tested, while sludge production consistently increases with increasing current density.

Mechanism of GG action

In recent years, researchers have been focused on eco-friendly wastewater purification materials derived from natural polymers. GG contains a large number of cis O–H groups in its backbone, which enables it to bind to organic matter through hydrogen bonding (Fig. 8). GG also serves as an effective coagulant aid for organic systems containing carboxyl and hydroxyl groups. As a natural polymer, GG is non-ionic and forms organic colloids, which typically undergo a coagulation-flocculation process by acting as a bridging agent between particles. It was also revealed from the literature reviews that the bridging mechanism occurred when high molecular weight GG and contaminants were stabilized and formed aggregates before settling. The natural coagulant would provide the required polymers to form the aggregates and flocs at the optimum dosage of GG26. Overall, the dye removal mechanism is best described as a synergistic effect where, in addition to hydrogen bonding, electrostatic interactions and polymer bridging play crucial roles in the effective aggregation and removal of dye molecules.

Economic evaluation

Economic evaluation shows that the EC-GG process costs $0.94 $ m− 3. Based on the different studies, electrocoagulation (EC) alone has typically achieved dye removal efficiencies in the range of 70–90% with treatment costs between about $1.0–1.5 m− 3, depending on the dye type and operating conditions37. When EC has been combined with natural coagulants such as chitosan or Moringa extracts, the removal efficiency has improved to around 85–93% with costs still generally above $1.0 m38,39. In comparison, this study demonstrated 96% dye removal with a treatment cost of $0.94 m− 3, indicating both higher efficiency and lower cost, which highlights the effectiveness of guar gum as a coagulant aid.

Conclusion

The laboratory-scale investigation utilized a combination of the EC and GG coagulant aid as a green and natural material to degrade the DB183 dye. Various parameters were studied to understand the impact of DB183 dye removal under the optimal GG dosage. In addition, the mechanism to remove contaminants was preliminarily shown by different characterization of sludge. In the EC-GG process, the concentration of Al3+ in the solution increased as j and t values increased. The DB183 dye removal rate with the EC process was 74% under the optimal conditions (dye concentration 40.0 mg/L, time 35.0 min, current density 47.0 mA/cm2, and pH 9.0). With the addition of 150 mg/L of GG as a coagulant aid, the DB183 dye removal rate reached 96%. The model indicated that pH had the most significant influence (p-value < 0.0001). The model’s R² was 0.98, with an R²pred of 0.90 and R²adj of 0.96, revealing a difference of less than 0.2. In the EC-GG process, the sludge structure was dense (settling time 6 min with 150 mg/L of GG), and operational costs were 0.94 $.m3. The formation of Al(OH)n(s) flocs through flocculation and the use of coagulant aid was crucial for the adsorption of suspended solids and the creation of metal hydroxide precipitation. Hence, the EC-GG integrated process is suggested as an economical, efficient, practical, and reusable approach for removing DB183 dye. Although the results demonstrate the promising efficiency of GG-assisted electrocoagulation for DB183 dye removal under controlled conditions, the use of synthetic wastewater represents a limitation of this study. Future work should validate the process with real textile effluents to ensure its effectiveness in practical applications.

Industrial scalability

The integration of GG with the EC process indicates favorable potential for industrial scalability, as the process relies on readily available, cost-effective coagulant aids and can be adapted to large-scale treatment units without major modifications in operational design. The optimization achieved through BBD further supports its practical feasibility by lowering energy demand, thereby reducing overall treatment costs. In addition, the characterization of the generated sludge presents opportunities for practical reuse, particularly in applications such as construction materials, which can minimize disposal problems and generate economic value. The proposed system provides effective dye removal and sustainable sludge management, making it preferred to treat wastewater.

Future studies

Further studies should evaluate the performance of the EC–GG process on real textile wastewater containing mixed dyes, salts, and impurities to validate its applicability under practical conditions. In addition, comprehensive toxicity, leachability, and reuse assessments of the produced sludge (e.g., as soil amendment or fertilizer) are recommended to confirm its environmental safety and potential valorization.
