Rifaximin alleviates irinotecan-induced diarrhea in mice model
Authors: Chengyi Huang, Huiyin Lan, Minghua Bai, Jinggang Chen, Shengkun Xu, Quanquan Sun, Qianping Chen, Wei Mao, Jin Jiang, Ji Zhu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIrinotecan is a chemotherapeutic drug widely used to treat solid tumors. However, its effectiveness is limited by the severely delayed onset of diarrhea. This study aimed to confirm the protective effects of the non-systemic oral antibiotic rifaximin on irinotecan-induced mucositis in mice model.Materials and methodsSix to eight week-old BALB/c mice were treated with saline, irinotecan (50 mg/kg, i.p. once daily), rifaximin (50 mg/kg, p.o. twice daily), or irinotecan + rifaximin for 9 consecutive days. Signs of diarrhea, bloody diarrhea, and body weight were monitored daily. Intestinal tissues were harvested for histopathological analysis and quantitative PCR. SN38 and SN38G concentration in intestine were detected using LC-MS analysis. Intestinal bacteria β-glucuronidase (BGUS) activity was detected using mouse feces. We performed 16S rRNA sequencing to investigate the gut microbiota composition. Gut permeability was tested in vivo by measuring the fluorescein isothiocyanate-dextran intensity in the serum.ResultsRifaximin reduced the frequency of delayed diarrhea and attenuated the severity of diarrhea caused by irinotecan in mice. Rifaximin significantly inhibited SN38 exposure in intestine and irinotecan-induced increase in BGUS activity. Rifaximin alleviated intestinal mucosal inflammation, prevented intestinal epithelial damage caused by irinotecan, and maintained gut barrier function. Moreover, the consecutive use of rifaximin did not cause a disorder in gut microbiota and reduced irinotecan-induced Firmicutes expansion. More importantly, rifaximin inhibited the expansion of some microbiota (such as Blautia, Eggerthella, and f_Enterobacteriaceae) and promoted an increase in beneficial microbiota (such as Lactobacillus intestinalis, Lachnospiraceae NK4A136 group, and f_Oscillospiraceae).ConclusionsPreventive use of rifaximin is a feasible method to protect against irinotecan-induced diarrhea.Keywords: Rifaximin, irinotecan, diarrhea, gut microbiota

Introduction

Irinotecan is a chemotherapeutic drug widely used to treat solid tumors, especially colorectal cancer. However, severe diarrhea and neutropenia are major dose-limiting toxicities of all irinotecan-based regimens. Studies have shown that 88% of patients develop diarrhea when using irinotecan, and 30% show severe diarrhea up to grade 3-4 [1,2]. This grade mainly refers to diarrhea that occurs more than 24 h (usually 5 days) after irinotecan administration, known as delayed diarrhea. It can be life-threatening when prolonged, owing to the risk of dehydration, electrolyte imbalance, hemodynamic collapse, or severe sepsis due to bacterial translocation [3]. However, there is currently no preventive treatment for irinotecan-induced delayed diarrhea, which places great emphasis on its prevention.

Intestinal bacteria play crucial roles in drug metabolism [4], especially for irinotecan. SN-38G, a hepatic metabolite of irinotecan, can be transformed to SN-38 through the β-glucuronidase (BGUS) activity of gut bacteria. Reactivation of SN-38 in the gut is considered the main mechanism of irinotecan-induced delayed diarrhea [5,6]. Moreover, irinotecan alters the composition of intestinal bacteria and causes gut dysbiosis. It can promote the proliferation of potentially harmful bacteria (Staphylococcus, Clostridium, and Escherichia coli) and inhibit the growth of potential probiotics (Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium) [7–9]. Hence, bacteria in the gut play an indispensable role in irinotecan-induced diarrhea.

Rifaximin is an intestinal antibiotic, a semi-synthetic derivative of rifamycin SV, which inhibits bacterial DNA synthesis by binding to the β-subunit site of DNA-dependent RNA polymerase [10]. Because the systemic absorption of rifaximin after oral administration is negligible (<0.4% of the administered dose), it only performs localized activity in the bowel [11]. As a broad-spectrum intestinal antibiotic, rifaximin exhibits high antibacterial activity against various Gram-positive and Gram-negative aerobic and anaerobic bacteria. Therefore, rifaximin can prevent SN-38G reactivation in the intestine by inhibiting intestinal bacteria that generate BGUS, such as E. coli. Previous studies in humans and in vitro models demonstrated that rifaximin did not significantly alter the gut microbiota, and led to a relative abundance of beneficial bacteria [12]. Additionally, it reduced bacterial virulence and prevented bacterial adherence and translocation to the gut mucosa [13,14].

Overall, altering the gut flora and maintaining a ‘drug-friendly’ intestinal microenvironment is a potentially feasible strategy to prevent irinotecan-induced diarrhea. Recognizing the necessity of prophylactic treatment for irinotecan-induced delayed diarrhea, we performed this study to determine whether rifaximin could reduce its occurrence, decrease the level of intestinal BGUS, prevent intestinal inflammation, and enhance gut barrier function by altering gut bacteria.

Materials and methods

Animal studies

Healthy 6–8-week-old female BALB/c mice were obtained from the Shanghai Slac Laboratory Animal Corporation (Shanghai, China). The mice were housed in conventional metabolic cages (n = 4/cage) and maintained on a 12-h light/dark cycle. All studies were conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the Animal Ethics Committee of Zhejiang Cancer Hospital, China.

Irinotecan was purchased from CSNpharm (CSN19463) as a hydrochloride salt (>99% purified high-performance liquid chromatography grade). Irinotecan was dissolved in DMSO to prepare a stock at a concentration of 250 mg/mL and stored at −20 °C. Rifaximin was purchased from CSNpharm (CSN11845), dissolved in DMSO at a concentration of 100 mg/mL, and stored at −20 °C. On the day of use, both mother liquors were diluted to the working concentrations.

Mice were divided into four groups of 12 animals each: vehicle, rifaximin only (RIF), irinotecan only (IRI), and rifaximin + irinotecan (RIF + IRI). Irinotecan (50 mg/kg per day) or saline were injected intraperitoneally for 9 consecutive days. Mice were orally administered rifaximin (50 mg/kg twice daily) or ddH2O 6 h apart (AM and PM) on the day of irinotecan administration. Specific rifaximin and irinotecan doses were based on published reports [15–18]. Mice were examined daily for signs of diarrhea (fecal staining of the skin and loose watery stool) and bloody diarrhea (black sticky stool), and their body weight was monitored daily [17]. On days 3 and 10 after treatment, feces and intestinal contents were collected for subsequent analyses. After sacrifice, ileum and colon samples were dissected. Histopathology

Ileum and colon tissues were obtained and washed with 0.9% saline solution. The obtained samples were fixed in formalin, embedded in paraffin for histological examination using 4 μm-thick sections, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). For immunohistochemical staining, 4 μm-thick paraffin sections were stained with antibodies against Ki-67 (Cell Signaling Technology, #12202) and β-catenin (Cell Signaling Technology, #8480S) overnight at 4 °C. The slides were then washed and stained with the appropriate secondary antibodies. The processed slides were examined under an Olympus microscope. Histological score (H-score) quantitative analysis of each immunohistochemically stained slide was performed using previously published methods [19].

We used ImageJ software v. 1.43 (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA) to measure villi length, defined as the length from the top of the villus to the point of intersection between two villi; five villi from one slice per mouse were randomly selected. Crypt counts were calculated using two H&E slices of the ileum in different locations per mouse under three random fields under a 10-fold objective lens.

Fecal BGUS activity assay

At day 3 of drug administration, naturally discharged and fresh stools were collected and stored at −80 °C. A BGUS Assay Kit (ZHI, #ZI-E027) was used to assess the fecal BGUS activity. The feces were weighed and homogenized in lysis buffer (1:10 w/v). The fecal suspension was then centrifuged at 8000 ×g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected in 1.5 mL Eppendorf tubes. The reaction mixture contained 50 μL of the collected supernatant, 100 μL of buffer 1, and 100 μL of buffer 2. After mixing, the mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. Then, 750 μL of buffer 3 was added to the mixture. BGUS activity was measured by detecting the presence of the hydrolysis product of phenolphthalein at 540 nm.

Quantitative reverse transcription PCR for inflammatory cytokines and tight junction proteins

Total RNA was extracted from the colon samples using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, USA). A PrimeScript RT reagent kit (Takara, Otsu, Japan) was utilized to reverse-transcribe 1 µg of RNA according to the measured concentration. Quantitative real-time PCR of inflammatory cytokines, GADPH, and the tight junction protein occludin was performed using a Super SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix kit (Esscience, China). Sequences of the specific primers used are listed in Supplemental Table 1.

Gut permeability measurement in vivo

On day 7 after drug administration, the mice were fasted, but allowed water for 4 h. Fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-dextran (4 kD, Sigma, #46944) was orally administered (50 mg/100 g body weight). Blood was collected 2 h after gavage, and the fluorescence of serum samples was measured using a fluorescence microplate reader (excitation, 490 nm; emission, 520 nm). The FITC-dextran intensity in each serum sample was calculated using a standard working curve [20].

Intestinal content collection

The ileal contents of mice were collected for sequencing in a sterile centrifugal tube after euthanasia at the end of the treatment. The centrifuge tube containing the sample was frozen in liquid nitrogen and then transferred to −80 °C for preservation.

Fecal DNA extraction, 16S rRNA gene sequencing, and data analysis

Total microbial genomic DNA was extracted from the collected samples using an E. Z. N. A. Soil DNA Kit (Omega Bio-tek, Norcross, GA, USA). Fecal DNA samples were amplified via PCR using barcoded primer pairs targeting the V3–V4 region of the 16S rRNA gene. PCR amplicons were sequenced using an Illumina MiSeq PE300 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). The resulting bacterial sequence fragments were analyzed using QIIME2 (version 2023.2). Bioinformatic analysis of the gut microbiota was performed using the Majorbio Cloud platform (https://cloud. majorbio. com). Further details are provided in the Supplementary Materials.

Sample preparation and LC–MS analysis for SN38 and SN38G

To remove debris, 0.1 g of each intestinal contents was mixed with 1 ml of phosphate buffered saline (PBS), homogenized, centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C and the supernatant was collected for further analysis. A 100 μL supernatants of fecal homogenate were mixed with 400 μL iced acetonitrile (containing 3 μM 10-Hydroxycamptothecin) in 1.5 ml Eppendorf tube, and then centrifuged at 16,000 g for 15 min at 4 °C. An aliquot of 400uL supernatant was transferred to a new 1.5 ml Eppendorf tube, and frozen in −80 °C for 30 min, and then lyophilized. The sample was reconstituted by adding 80 μL of 25% acetonitrile to the lyophilized powder and then mixing well. After centrifugation at 16,000 g for 15 min at 4 °C, 3 μL aliquot of supernatant was loaded to UPLC-ZenoTOF-MS/MS system.

Liquid chromatography (LC) was performed by a SHIMADZU Nexera series. For SN38 and SN38G determination, LC separation was achieved in a Phenomenex Kinetex F5, (2.1*150mm, 2.6um, 00 F-4723-AN) column with mobile phases of 0.1% (v/v) formic acid in water (A) and acetonitrile (B). High-resolution multiple reaction monitoring (MRM HR) in ZenoTOF-MS/MS system was used to determine the compounds, which focused on selective ions for SN-38 (393.1→349.2), SN-38G (569.0 →393.1), and IS (365.1→321.1). The relative concentration of SN-38 and SN-38G were determined by calculating the ratio between the peak area of individual compound and the peak area of internal standard.

Sample preparation and LC–MS analysis for short-chain fatty acids

The details of experimental process are provided in the Supplemental Materials.

Statistical analysis

Results are expressed as means ± SD/SEM. Tests were two-sided, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Differences between groups (n > 2) were compared using one-way ANOVA, followed by a Sidak post-hoc test. Unpaired t test was used to compare the difference between two groups. GraphPad Prism version 9 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA) was used for all analyses.

Results

Rifaximin delayed and suppressed the occurrence of irinotecan-induced diarrhea in mice

We conducted animal experiments to test the efficacy of rifaximin for the treatment of irinotecan-induced delayed diarrhea. Residual staining around the anus of mice was the most apparent sign of diarrhea. Diarrhea was first observed only in the IRI group on day 4, in accordance with a previous study [21], and more than 50% of the mice in the IRI group developed diarrhea on day 7 (Figure 1(b)); however, the first sign of diarrhea in the RIF + IRI group appeared on day 7, and fewer mice developed diarrhea in this group (Figure 1(b)). On days 8–10, more than 60% of the mice in the IRI group experienced bloody diarrhea, whereas only one mouse in the RIF + IRI group developed bloody diarrhea on day 10 (Figure 1(c)). Moreover, co-treatment with rifaximin and irinotecan significantly delayed the weight loss caused by irinotecan (Figure 1(d)). These findings demonstrate that rifaximin reduces the frequency of delayed diarrhea and attenuates the severity of diarrhea caused by irinotecan.

Rifaximin protected against gut epithelial damage caused by irinotecan and maintained the gut barrier function

A histological examination was conducted to determine whether rifaximin could protect against gut epithelial damage. Normal glandular and intact intestinal epithelial structures were observed in control and RIF groups, respectively. In contrast, obviously shorter villi and crypt loss, or even complete elimination of the top villi, along with a large number of inflammatory cells, were observed in the ileum and colon in the IRI group (Figure 2(a) and S1). The intestinal crypt is a self-renewing structure and a functional unit of the epithelium in the small intestine. The crypt counts of the ileum in the IRI group were significantly lower than those in the RIF + IRI group. Rifaximin significantly relieved the crypt damage caused by irinotecan, equalizing the intestinal crypt counts to those in the control and RIF groups (Figure 2(b)). We observed strikingly shortened villi in the IRI group compared with those in the control group (p < 0.001), and the addition of rifaximin significantly maintained the length of the villi (Figure 2(c)). To confirm the protective effect of rifaximin in gut epithelial cells, immunohistochemistry of the proliferative marker Ki-67 and cell membrane marker β-catenin was conducted (Figure 2(d)). Severe loss of proliferating cells and cell membrane integrity was observed in the IRI group, indicating the difficulty of recovery from the damage caused by irinotecan. However, a significant increase in the expression of both Ki-67 and β-catenin was observed after the addition of rifaximin. To quantify the immunohistochemistry results, Ki-67 and β-catenin H-scores were calculated in colon tissues (Figure 2(e)). The Ki-67 and β-catenin H-score in colon tissues in IRI group was lower compared with the other groups (p < 0.001), and co-treatment of rifaximin with irinotecan maintained the proliferation ability of epithelial cells and the cell membrane integrity at the same level as that of the control and RIF groups (p > 0.05).

Occludin is a transmembrane protein that maintains tight junction stability and barrier function [22,23]. Rifaximin increased occludin expression and prevented the irinotecan-induced decrease in occludin expression (Figure 2(d) and (e)). Next, we conducted a FITC-dextran assay to evaluate gut permeability in living mice. The results indicated that gut permeability increased significantly after continuous irinotecan treatment compared to that in the control group, whereas mice in the RIF + IRI group showed significantly reduced permeability compared to mice in the IRI group (Figure 2(f)).

Rifaximin prevented irinotecan-induced gut inflammation

To further explore local gut inflammation in mice receiving irinotecan and/or rifaximin, we examined the gene expression level of nine inflammatory cytokines in colon tissues: tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α, interferon (IFN)-γ, interleukin (IL)-17α, IL-18, IL-6, IL-10, IL-1β, prostaglandin E2 (PGE2), and thromboxane A2 (TXA2) (Figure 3(a)-1). The expression of IL-1β, IL-6, IL-10, IL-18, IFN-γ, PGE2, TNF-α, and TXA2 in the IRI group increased significantly compared with the control (p < 0.001), and adding rifaximin significantly inhibited the expression of these inflammatory cytokines (Figure 3(a)–(h)). No significant difference was measured in IL-17α between the control and IRI (p = 0.991) or IRI and RIF + IRI groups (p = 0.108) (Figure 3(i)).

Rifaximin inhibited the intestinal SN38 exposure and BGUS activity in feces

The intestinal concentration of SN38 and SN38G was examined in mice from IRI and RIF + IRI groups (Figure 4(a)). The results showed that the relative concentration of SN38 in RIF + IRI groups was significantly lower than IRI groups (p < 0.05), which indicated that rifaximin inhibited the exposure of SN38 in the mice intestine. Interestingly, the relative concentration of SN38G between IRI and RIF + IRI groups had no significant difference (p = 0.38). Then, BGUS activity in feces was investigated for each group (Figure 4(b)). The results showed that the BGUS activity in mice treated with irinotecan alone was dramatically higher than that in the control group (p < 0.05). Consecutive use of rifaximin significantly suppressed the elevation of BGUS activity induced by irinotecan (p < 0.05).

In order to investigate the effect of bacterial metabolites, we detected the short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) levels in intestinal contents in the four groups. The heat map (Fig. S2) and PCA analysis (Fig. S3) showed that the content and composition of SCFAs had a significant difference between rifaximin and non-rifaximin groups. The concrete quantitative comparation of various SCFAs among the four groups showed that the adding of rifaximin could inhibit the production of bacterial SCFAs in intestine (Fig. S4–7). The results above suggested that rifaximin works in alleviating irinotecan-induced diarrhea by inhibiting BGUS activity to prevent the exposure of SN38 in intestine instead of affecting bacterial SCFAs metabolites.

Long-term use of rifaximin did not cause gut microbial disturbance in mice

We performed 16S rRNA sequencing to further examine the effects of rifaximin on the gut bacteria. Alpha diversity, assessed using the ACE index of the microbial species, was analyzed in the four groups (Figure 5(a)). No significant differences were observed among the four groups (p = 0.19). Thus, as an intestinal antibiotic, rifaximin did not significantly affect the gut microbial community richness compared to the control group. To further explore the effects of rifaximin and irinotecan on the gut microbial composition, we constructed a hierarchical clustering tree at the species level (Fig. S8) and analyzed beta diversity using typing analysis (Figure 5(b)). The gut microbial composition showed an obvious difference between the irinotecan and non-irinotecan groups, which could be classified into two clusters depending on whether the mice were treated with irinotecan. In addition, rifaximin had little effect on gut microbial beta diversity compared to irinotecan (Figure 5(b)). Beta diversity difference analysis showed that sample dispersion in the IRI group was the largest among the four groups (p < 0.05) (Figure 5(c)). The results revealed that the gut bacterial composition varied greatly in the IRI group. Venn analysis showed that 48 species were shared by all groups, and each leaflet showed the number of species in the respective group, as detected via 16S rRNA gene sequencing (Figure 5(d)). Thus, long-term use of rifaximin did not significantly alter the gut microbial composition in mice, which maintained a similar level to that of the control.

Rifaximin protected against proteobacteria expansion induced by irinotecan at the phylum level

A striking Proteobacteria expansion at the phylum level was observed in mice treated with irinotecan compared to that in the control (Figure 6(a)). In the control group, 88.2% Firmicutes and 11.7% Bacteroidetes were observed in the distal ileum (Figure 6(a) and (b)). Firmicutes (87.6%) and Bacteroidetes (10.2%) were observed in rifaximin-treated mice (Figure 6(a) and (c)). In contrast, irinotecan-treated mice showed an obvious increase in Proteobacteria of up to 27.5%, consistent with a previous study (Figure 6(a) and (d)) [21]. However, Proteobacteria expansion was inhibited to only 6.3% in mice treated with both rifaximin and irinotecan, and the relative abundances of Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes were maintained at 64.84% and 28.89%, respectively (Figure 6(a) and (e)). Therefore, rifaximin protected against irinotecan-induced expansion of Proteobacteria at the phylum level.

Rifaximin protected against Lactobacillus intestinalis decrease and blautia expansion induced by irinotecan

The gut bacteria with statistically significant differences in mice among the four groups are shown at the family and genus levels (Figure 7(a) and (b)). The gut microbiota was significantly different from that in the control group at the species level (Figure 7(c)). Compared to the control, Lactobacillus and Lactobacillus intestinalis (L. intestinalis) were significantly reduced in mice in the IRI group, whereas f_Enterobacteriaceae and Blautia were significantly increased. To explore the effect of rifaximin on the gut microbiota, we compared the relative abundance between the two groups at the species level (Figure 7(d)). The potentially beneficial bacteria, L. intestinalis (p < 0.01), f_Oscillospiraceae (p < 0.01), and Lachnospiraceae NK4A136 group (p < 0.05), increased significantly in the RIF+IRI group compared with the IRI group; the potentially harmful bacteria, Blautia (p < 0.05) and Eggerthella (p < 0.01), showed a dramatic decrease in the RIF + IRI group. Although there was no statistically significant difference in f_Enterobacteriaceae between the two groups, a decreasing trend was observed after the addition of rifaximin.

Discussion

In this study, we confirmed that the non-systemic antibiotic rifaximin significantly alleviated irinotecan-induced delayed diarrhea. The proportion and severity of diarrhea in mice decreased significantly after co-treatment with rifaximin and irinotecan. Consecutive exposure to irinotecan for 9 days induced mucosal inflammation characterized by an increase in cytokine gene expression and an obvious increase in gut permeability. Epithelial and glandular structural damage and severe loss of proliferating cells were observed in mice treated with irinotecan alone. These phenomena were significantly ameliorated by the addition of rifaximin. Importantly, rifaximin inhibited the expansion of some potentially harmful bacteria and the reduction of some potential probiotics induced by irinotecan.

Studies have shown that irinotecan-induced DNA damage and apoptosis in intestinal epithelial cells generates oxidative stress and accelerates reactive oxygen species (ROS) formation. ROS directly aggravate gastrointestinal mucosa inflammation, activating the NOD/RIP2/NF-kB signaling pathway and triggering an inflammatory cascade, including IFN-γ, TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 [24,25]. Studies have reported that rifaximin is an intestine-specific human pregnane X receptor (PXR) agonist, which can repress NF-kB signaling cascade by activating PXR [26]. In our study, the expression of the inflammatory factors IFN-γ, TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, IL-10, PGE2, and TXA2 decreased significantly in the RIF + IRI group compared with the IRI group, which showed that rifaximin had a strong effect against irinotecan-induced inflammation. Besides acting on the NF-KB signal pathway, rifaximin can also inhibit the activation of inflammatory signals caused by intestinal bacteria translocation by maintaining the intestinal barrier [27].

Studies on treatment methods for irinotecan-induced delayed diarrhea have mainly focused on BGUS inhibitors, including specific inhibitors, non-specific inhibitors, and some natural extracts [15–17,21,28–30], which can alleviate gut damage by preventing SN-38 reactivation induced by intestinal BGUS. Consistent with these previous studies, elevated BGUS activity was observed in irinotecan-treated mice. In the present study, rifaximin significantly inhibited irinotecan-induced BGUS activity elevation and SN38 exposure in intestine. Given that rifaximin is an intestinal antibiotic, rifaximin may inhibit certain bacteria that produce BGUS to prevent the formation of SN38 in intestine. Chemotherapeutic drugs could induce gut microbiota disorder, which aggravates intestinal injury [4,9,31]. We investigated the intestinal microbiota composition in feces using clustering analysis. The microbial communities after irinotecan treatment were considerably different from those in the other groups, and they could be divided into two clusters depending on whether the mice received irinotecan. Rifaximin had little effect on the microbial composition at the species level. After irinotecan administration, Lactobacillus abundance decreased significantly, and Enterobacteriaceae and Blautia abundances increased significantly compared to the control group at the genus level.

Blautia plays a pivotal role in maintaining the environmental balance in the intestine and preventing inflammation by upregulating intestinal regulatory T cells and producing short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) [32,33]. However, studies have shown that patients with irritable bowel syndrome and ulcerative colitis have a higher abundance of Blautia in their feces than healthy individuals [34,35], which was also observed in mice treated with irinotecan. Rifaximin has been used clinically to treat irritable bowel syndrome and ulcerative colitis [36]. In our study, co-treatment with rifaximin and irinotecan significantly inhibited the expansion of Blautia induced by irinotecan. The concentration of SCFAs in rifaximin groups decreased strikingly compared with non-rifaximin groups, which suggested that rifaximin may do not work through affecting SCFAs. In addition to the marked reduction in Blautia, an obvious reduction in Enterobacteriaceae was observed following the addition of rifaximin. Enterobacteriaceae are associated with a wide range of clinical syndromes [37], and they share a close correlation with drug side effects due to their ability to produce BGUS (including E. coli and Klebsiella sp.) [38,39]. Although there was no statistical difference in the relative abundance of Enterobacteriaceae between the RIF + IRI and IRI groups, BGUS activity was significantly inhibited in the RIF + IRI group. Thus, rifaximin improved irinotecan-related gut damage by inhibiting the expansion of potentially harmful microbiota induced by irinotecan.

Importantly, some potentially beneficial gut microbiota expanded significantly in mice treated with rifaximin and irinotecan compared to those treated with irinotecan alone. The Lachnospiraceae NK4A136 group could maintain gut barrier integrity in mice and is negatively correlated with intestinal permeability [40,41]. Moreover, Oscillospiraceae was reported to maintain intestinal permeability in patients with alcoholic liver disease [42], and it was regarded as a candidate next-generation probiotic. In particular, L. intestinalis showed a dramatic increase in the RIF + IRI group compared to the IRI group. L. intestinalis is a newly discovered probiotic that exerts protective effects against dextran sodium sulfate-induced colitis in mice by targeting the C/EBPA-SAA1/2-Th17 axis [43]. L. intestinalis may be closely correlated with the promotion of gut development, including villus growth and crypt development [44].

Our study had several limitations. First, we did not use a tumor-bearing mouse model. We only focused on the effect of rifaximin on bowel protection; however, we did not examine the influence of co-treatment with rifaximin and irinotecan on tumor growth. Second, we did not investigate the gut microbiota in human samples from healthy donors or patients receiving irinotecan. The composition of gut microbiota in human was somewhat different compared with that in mice, which may lead to the difference of sequencing results. Finally, we did not explore the correlation between the gut microbiota and potential inflammation- related pathways. We only showed an overall anti-inflammatory effect of rifaximin and the specific pathways affected by rifaximin in irinotecan-induced diarrhea will be explored in the future. These limitations will be addressed in our future studies.

Conclusions

In summary, rifaximin protected against irinotecan-induced epithelial cell toxicity and maintained a favorable intestinal microenvironment in mice. These results provide evidence for the clinical application of rifaximin in the prevention of irinotecan-induced diarrhea.
