Free Semigroups of Large Critical Exponent
Aleksander Skenderi

Abstract
. For a convergence group equipped with an expanding coarse-cocycle, we construct finitely generated free subsemigroups, which we call Bishop–Jones semigroups , of critical exponent arbitrarily close to but strictly less than the critical exponent of the ambient group. As an application, we show that for any non-elementary transverse subgroup Γ \Gamma of a semisimple Lie group G G , there exist finitely generated free Anosov subsemigroups in the sense of Kassel–Potrie of critical exponent arbitrarily close to but strictly less than that of the ambient transverse group. Furthermore, we show that these semigroups admit quasi-isometric embeddings into the symmetric space X X of G G with certain additional coarse-geometric properties.



1.
Introduction

An important object in the study of discrete subgroups of Lie groups and discrete subgroups of isometries of Gromov hyperbolic spaces is the
critical exponent
. For concreteness, let
\Gamma\subset\mathrm{SO}^{\circ}(n,1)\cong\mathrm{Isom}^{+}(\mathbf{H}_{\mathbb{R}}^{n})
be a discrete subgroup of isometries of the real hyperbolic
n
-space and fix a basepoint
x\in\mathbf{H}_{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. The
critical exponent
of
\Gamma
is the abscissa of convergence of the
Poincaré series

\displaystyle Q(s)=\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}e^{-sd_{\mathbf{H}_{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}(x,\gamma x)},

i.e.,

\displaystyle\delta(\Gamma)=\inf\{s>0:Q(s)<\infty\}\in[0,\infty].

Equivalently, the critical exponent is given by

\displaystyle\delta(\Gamma)=\limsup_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\log\#\{\gamma\in\Gamma:d_{\mathbf{H}_{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}(x,\gamma x)\leq T\},

and thus it measures the asymptotic growth rate of the
\Gamma
-orbits in
\mathbf{H}_{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. We remark that the critical exponent is independent of the choice of basepoint
x\in\mathbf{H}_{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, hence is well-defined.

A classical theorem of Bishop–Jones in
[
2
]
shows that for any non-elementary Kleinian group
\Gamma\subset\mathrm{SO}^{\circ}(3,1)
, the Hausdorff dimension of its conical limit set is equal to its critical exponent. Showing that the Hausdorff dimension is bounded above by the critical exponent is well-known and not particularly difficult. The great novelty in their proof was to construct a well-behaved tree in
\mathbf{H}_{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
, related to the group
\Gamma
, and whose set of accumulation points lands inside of the conical limit set of
\Gamma
. One uses properties of the tree to construct a probability measure supported on its set of accumulation points, and then uses Frostman’s Lemma to obtain a lower bound on the Hausdorff dimension.

While this is a truly ingenious construction, the tree is not so easy to work with. Indeed, their construction is not
\Gamma
-equivariant, and hence does not play particularly well with the
\Gamma
-action on
\mathbf{H}_{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
. Motivated by the work of Bishop–Jones, we construct finitely generated free subsemigroups in a broad class of convergence groups. These semigroups, which we call
Bishop
–
Jones semigroups
, are large from the perspective of the critical exponent of the ambient group. Nevertheless, we do not prove an analog of Bishop–Jones’ theorem, since in the general abstract setting we work in, there is no (at least to the author) natural way to measure the Hausdorff dimensions of (conical) limit sets.

To better illustrate our result, we describe two applications of our main result Theorem
3.1
. The first application of Theorem
3.1
pertains to a class of discrete subgroups of semisimple Lie groups known as
transverse groups
. Transverse groups (called regular antipodal groups by Kapovich–Leeb–Porti
[
19
]
) have been widely studied in recent years by many authors; see for instance
[
5
]
,
[
6
]
,
[
19
]
, and
[
21
]
. The class of transverse groups include both Anosov and relatively Anosov groups, as well as all discrete subgroups of rank one Lie groups. Given a non-elementary transverse subgroup
\Gamma
of a connected semisimple real Lie group
G
without compact factors and with finite center, we show that its critical exponent (with respect to an appropriate linear functional on the partial Cartan subspace) can be approximated by the critical exponents of finitely generated free subsemigroups, which are Anosov in the sense of Kassel–Potrie
[
22
]
. Delaying definitions until section
4
, we prove the following.

Theorem 1.1

(Theorem
5.1
)
.

Suppose
\Gamma
is a non-elementary
P_{\theta}
-transverse group and
\phi\in\mathfrak{a}_{\theta}^{*}
is such that
\phi(\kappa(\gamma_{n}))\rightarrow\infty
for any sequence
\{\gamma_{n}\}
of pairwise distinct elements of
\Gamma
. Then there exists a sequence
\{\Gamma_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
of free
P_{\theta}
-Anosov subsemigroups of
\Gamma
so that

(1)

\delta^{\phi}(\Gamma_{n})<\delta^{\phi}(\Gamma)
for all
n\geq 1
, and

(2)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\delta^{\phi}(\Gamma_{n})=\delta^{\phi}(\Gamma)
.

We also show that the orbit of a Bishop–Jones semigroup embeds into the symmetric space
X=G/K
as a
(\mathcal{C},B)
-regular map in the sense of Kapovich–Leeb–Porti
[
19
]
by using work of Dey–Kim–Oh
[
10
]
. The precise statement of this result is Theorem
6.1
.

The second application is related to the classical critical exponent gap theorem of Corlette for discrete subgroups in Sp
(n,1)
and
\mathrm{F}_{4}^{-20}
. Denote by
\mathbf{H}_{\mathbb{H}}^{n}
the
n
-dimensional quaternionic hyperbolic space and
\mathbf{H}_{\mathbb{O}}^{2}
the Cayley hyperbolic plane (also known as the octonionic projective plane). These are connected, contractible, negatively curved Riemannian manifolds with normalized sectional curvatures between
-4
and
-1
. The simple Lie groups of real rank one Sp
(n,1)
and
\mathrm{F}_{4}^{-20}
are the orientation-preserving isometry groups of
\mathbf{H}_{\mathbb{H}}^{n}
and
\mathbf{H}_{\mathbb{O}}^{2}
, respectively. For a discrete subgroup
\Gamma
in either of these isometry groups, the critical exponent
\delta(\Gamma)
is defined analogously as in the real hyperbolic case. Corlette’s renowned gap theorem states the following.

Theorem 1.2

(Corlette, Theorem 4.4 of
[
8
]
)
.

(1)

If
\Gamma\subset\mathrm{Sp}(n,1)
,
n\geq 2
, is a discrete subgroup, then
\delta(\Gamma)=4n+2
or
\delta(\Gamma)\leq 4n
. Moreover,
\delta(\Gamma)=4n+2
if and only if
\Gamma
is a lattice.

(2)

If
\Gamma\subset\mathrm{F}_{4}^{-20}
is a discrete subgroup, then
\delta(\Gamma)=22
or
\delta(\Gamma)\leq 16
. Moreover,
\delta(\Gamma)=22
if and only if
\Gamma
is a lattice.

As a consequence of our main result, Theorem
3.1
, we show that there is no such gap phenomenon for discrete
subsemigroups
in Sp
(n,1)
and
\mathrm{F}_{4}^{-20}
. More precisely, we prove the following.

Theorem 1.3

(Theorem
5.2
)
.

(1)

Let
\Gamma\subset\mathrm{Sp}(n,1)
,
n\geq 2
, be a lattice. Then there exists a sequence
\{\Gamma_{m}\}_{m\geq 1}
of finitely generated free subsemigroups of
\Gamma
so that
\delta(\Gamma_{m})<\delta(\Gamma)=4n+2
for all
m\geq 1
and
\lim_{m\to\infty}\delta(\Gamma_{m})=4n+2
.

(2)

Let
\Gamma\subset\mathrm{F}_{4}^{-20}
be a lattice. Then there exists a sequence
\{\Gamma_{m}\}_{m\geq 1}
of finitely generated free subsemigroups of
\Gamma
so that
\delta(\Gamma_{m})<\delta(\Gamma)=22
for all
m\geq 1
and
\lim_{m\to\infty}\delta(\Gamma_{m})=22
.

We mention an interesting group-theoretic consequence of Theorem
1.3
. Consider the case when
\Gamma\subset\mathrm{Sp}(n,1)
,
n\geq 2
, is a lattice (an entirely analogous discussion holds when
\Gamma\subset\mathrm{F}_{4}^{-20}
is a lattice). Then the above theorem tells us that we can find a finitely generated free subsemigroup
\mathcal{T}
, with finite generating set
S
, so that
4n<\delta(\mathcal{T})<4n+2
. Let
\Gamma^{\prime}:=\langle S\rangle
be the group generated by
S
. Then
\delta(\Gamma^{\prime})\geq\delta(\mathcal{T})>4n
, so Corlette’s gap theorem implies that
\delta(\Gamma^{\prime})=4n+2
and
\Gamma^{\prime}
is a lattice with finite index in
\Gamma
. In particular, there is some relation among the elements of
S
when one is allowed to multiply by negative powers of these elements, even though there is no relation when multiplying together exclusively positive powers of these elements.

Remark 1.4
.

We remark that the analog of Corlette’s gap theorem is known to fail for discrete subgroups of
\mathrm{SO}(n,1)\cong\mathrm{Isom}(\mathbf{H}_{\mathbb{R}}^{n})
(see Sullivan
[
27
]
for examples when
n=3
and Kapovich–Kontorovich
[
16
]
for examples in all dimensions) and for discrete subgroups of
\mathrm{SU}(m,1)\cong\mathrm{Isom}(\mathbf{H}_{\mathbb{C}}^{m})
when
m=2,3
by work of Dey–Liu
[
11
]
.

Similarities and differences in the work of Yang

After I posted a first version of this paper to the arXiv, Wenyuan Yang informed me that Theorem
1.3
also follows from his earlier work “Statistically convex-cocompact actions of groups with contracting elements”
[
30
]
. Indeed, Theorem A of Yang’s paper shows that if a group
G
admits a proper action on a geodesic metric space with a contracting element, then there exists a sequence of free subsemigroups of
G
with critical exponent arbitrarily close to, but strictly less than, that of
G
. Thus his Theorem A also provides a proof of Theorem
1.3
. Nevertheless, our general framework used to prove our main result Theorem
3.1
is different from Yang’s and thus we obtain a new proof of Theorem
1.3
. I will now discuss in more detail the differences between Yang’s framework and the framework of this paper of expanding coarse-cocyles.

Both our results apply to many notable classes of groups, including hyperbolic and relatively hyperbolic groups. Moreover, Yang’s work applies for instance to groups acting properly and cocompactly on a CAT(0) space with rank-one elements, whereas the present work applies to groups acting properly on CAT(0) visibility spaces (that is, the action need not be cocompact; see section 1.2.6 of
[
3
]
for further details). Yang’s work also applies to certain small cancellation groups as well as to mapping class groups of closed hyperbolic surfaces. One notable difference in the results of our papers is that Yang’s general framework requires the group to act on a properly and by isometries on a
geodesic
metric space, whereas in our abstract setting the groups we consider come equipped with a convergence action on a compact space with a coarse-cocycle.

A notable difference in applications is that our setting applies to transverse subgroups of higher rank semisimple Lie groups, which need not be hyperbolic nor even relatively hyperbolic (see
[
6
]
for an intimate relation between transverse groups and so-called projectively visible subgroups of the automorphism group of a properly convex domain in real projective space). Due to the presence of flats in higher-rank symmetric spaces, it seems unlikely that one could build a suitable metric for which a transverse group acts by isometries on the symmetric space with a contracting element.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Convergence Groups and Expanding Coarse-Cocycles

When
M
is a compact metrizable space, a countable subgroup
\Gamma\subset\mathrm{Homeo}(M)
is called a (discrete)
convergence group
if for every sequence of pairwise distinct elements
\{\gamma_{n}\}\subset\Gamma
, there exist points
a,b\in M
and a subsequence
\{\gamma_{n_{k}}\}
so that
\gamma_{n_{k}}\big|_{M\smallsetminus\{b\}}
converges locally uniformly to
a
as
k\to\infty
. Blayac–Canary–Zhu–Zimmer recently developed a theory of Patterson–Sullivan measures associated to expanding coarse cocycles of convergence groups and used it to establish counting, mixing, and equidistribution results for a broad class of groups, which, in particular, includes relatively Anosov groups; see
[
3
]
and
[
4
]
. In this section, we describe the relevant concepts and results from their work that we will use later in this paper.

2.1.1.
The Basics of Convergence Groups

Given a convergence group
\Gamma\subset\mathrm{Homeo}(M)
, we define the following:

(1)

The
limit set

\Lambda(\Gamma)
is the set of points
x\in M
for which there exist
y\in M
and a sequence
\{\gamma_{n}\}
in
\Gamma
such that
\gamma_{n}\big|_{M\smallsetminus\{y\}}
converges locally uniformly to
x
.

(2)

A point
x\in\Lambda(\Gamma)
is called a
conical limit point
if there exist distinct points
a,b\in M
and a sequence
\{\gamma_{n}\}
in
\Gamma
so that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\gamma_{n}x=a
and
\lim_{n\to\infty}\gamma_{n}y=b
for all
y\in M\smallsetminus\{x\}
.

A convergence group
\Gamma
is said to be
non-elementary
if its limit set
\Lambda(\Gamma)
contains at least three points. In this case,
\Lambda(\Gamma)
is the smallest
\Gamma
-invariant closed subset of
M
(see Theorem 2S of
[
29
]
). For the remainder of this paper, we will only consider non-elementary convergence groups.

In Theorem 2B of
[
29
]
, Tukia shows that the elements in a convergence group
\Gamma\subset\mathrm{Homeo}(M)
can be classified as follows: every element
\gamma\in\Gamma
is either

(1)

loxodromic
, meaning that it has two fixed points
\gamma^{+}
and
\gamma^{-}
in the limit set
\Lambda(\Gamma)\subset M
and
(\gamma^{\pm 1})^{n}\big|_{M\smallsetminus\{\gamma^{\mp}\}}
converges locally uniformly to
\gamma^{\pm}
,

(2)

parabolic
, meaning that it has one fixed point
p\in\Lambda(\Gamma)
and
(\gamma^{\pm 1})^{n}\big|_{M\smallsetminus\{p\}}
converges locally uniformly to
p
, or

(3)

elliptic
, meaning it has finite order.

In
[
3
]
it was observed that the set
\Gamma\sqcup M
has a unique topology which makes it a compact metrizable space on which the natural action of
\Gamma
is a convergence group action. Any metric
d
that induces this topology is called a
compatible metric
. Given any
x\in\Gamma\sqcup M
, we write
B_{\epsilon}(x)
for the open ball in
\Gamma\sqcup M
centered at
x
of radius
\epsilon
with respect to the metric
d
. The following result, phrased more generally than in
[
3
]
, in fact follows from their original proof.

Lemma 2.1

(Part (3) of Proposition 2.3 of
[
3
]
)
.

Let
\Gamma\subset\mathrm{Homeo}(M)
be a convergence group. For any compatible metric
d
and any
\epsilon>0
, there exists a finite set
F\subset\Gamma
such that

\displaystyle\gamma\big((\Gamma\sqcup M)\smallsetminus B_{\epsilon}(\gamma^{-1})\big)\subset B_{\epsilon}(\gamma)

for every
\gamma\in\Gamma\smallsetminus F
.

We will need the following convergence group analog of a well-known result of Abels
-
Margulis
-
Soifer, which, in particular, states that elements in a strongly irreducible linear group are uniformly close to proximal elements (see
[
1
]
). We actually state a slightly strengthened version of Lemma 2.4 in
[
3
]
, but its proof is essentially exactly the same.

Lemma 2.2

(Lemma 2.4 of
[
3
]
)
.

Suppose
\Gamma\subset\mathrm{Homeo}(M)
is a convergence group and
d
is a compatible metric on
\Gamma\sqcup M
. Then there exists
\epsilon>0
and a finite set
F\subset\Gamma
with the following property: for any
\gamma\in\Gamma
, there is some
f\in F
so that
\gamma f
is loxodromic and

\displaystyle\min\{d((\gamma f)^{+},(\gamma f)^{-}),d(\gamma f,(\gamma f)^{-}),d((\gamma f)^{+},(\gamma f)^{-1}),d(\gamma f,(\gamma f)^{-1})\}>\epsilon.

2.1.2.
Properties of Expanding Coarse-Cocycles and GPS Systems

We first recall the notion of a coarse-cocycle.

Definition 2.3

(See page 2 of
[
3
]
)
.

Suppose
\Gamma\subset\mathrm{Homeo}(M)
is a convergence group. A function
\sigma:\Gamma\times M\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is called a
\kappa
-
coarse-cocycle
if:

(1)

For every
\gamma\in\Gamma
, the function
\sigma(\gamma,\cdot):M\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is
\kappa
-
coarsely continuous
; that is, for all
x_{0}\in M

\displaystyle\limsup_{x\to x_{0}}|\sigma(\gamma,x)-\sigma(\gamma,x_{0})|\leq\kappa.

(2)

The function
\sigma
satisfies a coarse version of the cocycle identity: for all
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\in\Gamma
and
x\in M
, we have

\displaystyle\Big|\sigma(\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2},x)-\Big(\sigma(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}x)+\sigma(\gamma_{2},x)\Big)\Big|\leq\kappa.

Notice that a
0
-coarse-cocycle is simply a continuous cocycle. We will be interested in coarse-cocycles satisfying certain additional properties.

Definition 2.4

(Definition 1.2 of
[
3
]
)
.

Suppose
\Gamma\subset\mathrm{Homeo}(M)
is a non-elementary convergence group and
d
is a compatible metric on
\Gamma\sqcup M
. A coarse-cocycle
\sigma:\Gamma\times M\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is said to be:

(1)

Proper
if
\sigma(\gamma_{n},\gamma_{n}^{+})\rightarrow\infty
whenever
\{\gamma_{n}\}\subset\Gamma
is a sequence of pairwise distinct loxodromic elements so that the sequence of pairs of repelling/attracting fixed points satisfies

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to\infty}d(\gamma_{n}^{-},\gamma_{n}^{+})>0.

(2)

Expanding
if it is proper and for every
\gamma\in\Gamma
, there is a number
||\gamma||_{\sigma}\in\mathbb{R}
, called the
\sigma
-
magnitude
of
\gamma
satisfying the following: for every
\epsilon>0
, there exists
C=C(\epsilon)>0
so that if
x\in M
,
\gamma\in\Gamma
, and
d(x,\gamma^{-1})>\epsilon
, then

\displaystyle\big|\sigma(\gamma,x)-||\gamma||_{\sigma}\big|\leq C.

Given an expanding coarse-cocycle
\sigma:\Gamma\times M\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, the
\sigma
-
Poincaré series
is

\displaystyle Q_{\sigma}(s)=\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}e^{-s||\gamma||_{\sigma}}\in[0,\infty]

and the
\sigma
-
critical exponent
is the abscissa of convergence of this series, that is,

\displaystyle\delta_{\sigma}(\Gamma)=\inf\{s>0:Q_{\sigma}(s)<\infty\}\in[0,\infty].

We will make frequent use of the following result in
[
3
]
, which establishes a number of useful properties related to expanding coarse-cocycles.

Proposition 2.5

(Proposition 3.3 of
[
3
]
)
.

Suppose
\Gamma\subset\mathrm{Homeo}(M)
is a convergence group,
d
is a compatible metric on
\Gamma\sqcup M
, and
\sigma
is an expanding
\kappa
-coarse-cocycle. Then:

(1)

For any finite subset
F\subset\Gamma
, there exists
C>0
such that: if
\gamma\in\Gamma
and
f\in F
, then

\displaystyle||\gamma||_{\sigma}-C\leq||\gamma f||_{\sigma}\leq||\gamma||_{\sigma}+C\ \ \ \mathrm{and}\ \ \ ||\gamma||_{\sigma}-C\leq||f\gamma||_{\sigma}\leq||\gamma||_{\sigma}+C.

(2)

\lim_{n\to\infty}||\gamma_{n}||_{\sigma}=\infty
for every escaping sequence
\{\gamma_{n}\}\subset\Gamma
.

(3)

For any
\epsilon>0
, there exists
C>0
such that: if
\alpha,\beta\in\Gamma
and
d(\alpha^{-1},\beta)\geq\epsilon
, then

\displaystyle||\alpha||_{\sigma}+||\beta||_{\sigma}-C\leq||\alpha\beta||_{\sigma}\leq||\alpha||_{\sigma}+||\beta||_{\sigma}+C.

We will use the following notion of a coarse Gromov–Patterson–Sullivan system to apply our construction of a Bishop–Jones semigroup in the abstract convergence group setting to the particular case of
P_{\theta}
-transverse groups acting on their limit sets.

Definition 2.6

(Definition 1.7 of
[
3
]
)
.

Suppose
\Gamma\subset\mathrm{Homeo}(M)
is a non-elementary convergence group and let
M^{(2)}=\{(x,y)\in M^{2}:x\neq y\}
. We say that
(\sigma,\bar{\sigma},G)
is a
\kappa
-
coarse Gromov–Patterson–Sullivan system
(or
GPS system
for short) if
\sigma,\bar{\sigma}:\Gamma\times M\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
are proper
\kappa
-coarse-cocycles,
G:M^{(2)}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a locally bounded function, and

\displaystyle\Big|\Big(\bar{\sigma}(\gamma,x)+\sigma(\gamma,y)\Big)-(G(\gamma x,\gamma y)-G(x,y)\Big)\Big|\leq\kappa

for all
\gamma\in\Gamma
and distinct
x,y\in M
. When
\kappa=0
and
G
is continuous, we call
(\sigma,\bar{\sigma},G)
a
continuous GPS system
.

The following result shows, among other things, that if
(\sigma,\bar{\sigma},G)
is a coarse GPS system, then
\sigma
and
\bar{\sigma}
are expanding coarse-cocycles.

Proposition 2.7

(Parts (1) and (2) of Proposition 3.4 of
[
3
]
)
.

If
\Gamma\subset\mathrm{Homeo}(M)
is a convergence group and
(\sigma,\bar{\sigma},G)
is a coarse GPS system, then:

(1)

The coarse-cocycles
\sigma
and
\bar{\sigma}
are expanding.

(2)

There exists
C>0
such that

\displaystyle||\gamma^{-1}||_{\bar{\sigma}}-C\leq||\gamma||_{\sigma}\leq||\gamma^{-1}||_{\bar{\sigma}}+C

for all
\gamma\in\Gamma
.

Example 2.8
.

To better illustrate this setting, we describe one very important motivating example, which will allow our general result to be applied to prove Theorem
1.3
. Let
X
be a proper geodesic Gromov hyperbolic space and
\Gamma\subset\mathrm{Isom}(X)
a discrete group of isometries of
X
. Then
\Gamma
acts as a convergence group on the Gromov boundary
\partial X
of
X
(see
[
12
]
or
[
29
]
). Fix a base point
o\in X
. Then we may define, for each
x\in X
, a
Busemann function

b_{x}:X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
via

\displaystyle b_{x}(q)=\limsup_{p\to x}d(p,q)-d(p,o).

The
Busemann coarse-cocycle

\beta:\Gamma\times\partial X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is then defined by

\displaystyle\beta(\gamma,x)=b_{x}(\gamma^{-1}(o)).

In general, this is only a coarse-cocycle. The classical
Gromov product
is the map
G:\partial X^{(2)}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
defined by

\displaystyle G(x,y)=\limsup_{p\to x,q\to y}d(o,p)+d(o,q)-d(p,q).

We then have that
(\beta,\beta,G)
is a coarse GPS system (which is not necessarily continuous). Moreover, one can take the
\beta
-magnitude function to be
||\gamma||_{\beta}=d(o,\gamma o)
, hence
\delta_{\beta}(\Gamma)
is precisely the usual critical exponent
\delta(\Gamma)
of the Poincaré series

\displaystyle Q(s)=\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}e^{-sd(o,\gamma o)}.

If
X
is CAT
(-1)
(for instance, if
X
is a rank one symmetric space), then
\beta
is a continuous cocycle and
(\beta,\beta,G)
is a continuous GPS system.

2.2.
Shadows and Coarse Patterson–Sullivan Measures

We now recall the definition of shadows from
[
3
]
. They will play an important role in our construction of Bishop–Jones semigroups.

Definition 2.9

(Shadow; see Definition 1.15 of
[
3
]
)
.

Suppose
\Gamma\subset\mathrm{Homeo}(M)
is a non-elementary convergence group and
d
is a compatible metric on
\Gamma\sqcup M
. Given
\epsilon>0
and
\gamma\in\Gamma
, the associated
shadow
is defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\epsilon}(\gamma):=\gamma\big(M\smallsetminus B_{\epsilon}(\gamma^{-1})\big).

Readers familiar with shadows in the classical setting of Gromov hyperbolic spaces may find this definition strange at first. However, Proposition
5.5
of
[
3
]
shows that when
X
is a proper geodesic Gromov hyperbolic space and
\Gamma\subset\mathrm{Isom}(X)
is discrete, then every shadow, in the sense of the above definition, in the Gromov boundary
\partial X
is contained in a classically defined shadow; likewise any classically defined shadow in
\partial X
is contained in a shadow in the convergence group sense.

The following are some important properties of shadows which will be needed later on in the paper.

Proposition 2.10

(Proposition 5.1 of
[
3
]
)
.

If
\epsilon>0
and
\sigma:\Gamma\times M\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is an expanding coarse-cocycle, then:

(1)

There exists
C_{1}>0
such that if
x\in\mathcal{S}_{\epsilon}(\gamma)
, then

\displaystyle||\gamma||_{\sigma}-C_{1}\leq\sigma(\gamma,\gamma^{-1}(x))\leq||\gamma||_{\sigma}+C_{1}.

(2)

If
\{\gamma_{n}\}\subset\Gamma
is an escaping sequence, then

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathrm{diam}\ \mathcal{S}_{\epsilon}(\gamma_{n})=0\ \ \mathrm{and}\ \ \lim_{n\to\infty}\inf_{x\in\mathcal{S}_{\epsilon}(\gamma_{n})}d(\gamma_{n},x)=0,

(3)

3.
A Bishop–Jones Semigroup for Convergence Groups with Expanding Coarse-Cocycles

3.1.
The Main Construction

Let
\Gamma\subset\mathrm{Homeo}(M)
be a discrete non-elementary convergence group equipped with an expanding coarse-cocycle
\sigma:\Gamma\times M\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. For
n\geq 0
, define

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\sigma,n}:=\{\gamma\in\Gamma\ :\ n\leq||\gamma||_{\sigma}<n+1\}.

Given a finite subset
S\subset\Gamma
, define

\displaystyle S^{0}:=\{\mathrm{id}\}\ \ \mathrm{and}\ \ S^{m}:=\{\gamma_{1}\cdots\gamma_{m}:\gamma_{i}\in S\ \ \mathrm{for}\ \ 1\leq i\leq m\}\ \mathrm{for}\ m\geq 1.

For
\gamma\in\bigcup_{m=0}^{\infty}S^{m}
, let

\displaystyle|\gamma|_{S}:=\min\{k\geq 1:\gamma=\gamma_{1}\cdots\gamma_{k},\ \mathrm{where}\ \gamma_{i}\in S\ \mathrm{for\ all}\ 1\leq i\leq k\},

if
\gamma\neq\mathrm{id}
and set
|\mathrm{id}|_{S}:=0
. Our main result is the following theorem.

Theorem 3.1

(Main Theorem)
.

For every
0<\delta<\delta_{\sigma}(\Gamma)
, there exists a free finitely generated semigroup
\mathcal{T}=\mathcal{T}_{\delta}\subset\Gamma
with finite generating set
S\subset\Gamma
, which we call a
\mathrm{Bishop}
–
\mathrm{Jones\ semigroup}
, with the following properties:

(1)

Its critical exponent satisfies

\displaystyle\delta_{\sigma}(\mathcal{T})\geq\delta.

(2)

Given any expanding coarse-cocycle
\phi:\Gamma\times M\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, there exist constants
B_{1}=B_{1}(\phi)>1
and
b_{1}=b_{1}(\phi)>0
so that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{B_{1}}|\gamma|_{S}-b_{1}\leq||\gamma||_{\phi}\leq B_{1}|\gamma|_{S}+b_{1}

for all
\gamma\in\mathcal{T}
.

(3)

With
B_{1}=B_{1}(\sigma)
as above, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{B_{1}}\log(\#S)\leq\delta_{\sigma}(\mathcal{T})\leq B_{1}\log(\#S).

In particular,
\delta_{\sigma}(\mathcal{T})<\infty
.

(4)

The critical exponent of a Bishop–Jones semigroup is strictly less than that of the ambient group, that is

\displaystyle\delta_{\sigma}(\mathcal{T})<\delta_{\sigma}(\Gamma).

(5)

Given any two expanding coarse-cocycles
\phi_{1},\phi_{2}:\Gamma\times M\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, there exist constants
B_{2}=B_{2}(\phi_{1},\phi_{2})>1
and
b_{2}=b_{2}(\phi_{1},\phi_{2})>0
so that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{B_{2}}||\gamma||_{\phi_{2}}-b_{2}\leq||\gamma||_{\phi_{1}}\leq B_{2}||\gamma||_{\phi_{2}}+b_{2}

for all
\gamma\in\mathcal{T}
.

Property (1) is established in Corollary
3.27
. Property (2) is proved in Lemma
3.29
. Notice that property (5) is an immediate consequence of property (2). Property (3) is established in Proposition
3.34
. By this property, to establish property (4) it suffices to consider the case when
\delta_{\sigma}(\Gamma)<\infty
. This is the content of Theorem
3.36
.

In section
3.2
, we provide the proofs of properties (1), (2), and (5), as well as discussing the relation between Bishop–Jones semigroups and the conical limit set of
\Gamma
. Section
3.3
is devoted to the proofs of properties (3) and (4).

The heart of the argument is the following proposition, whose proof occupies the remainder of this section. It is the key technical result needed to establish Theorem
3.1
. We emphasize that not only the statement of the proposition, but also its proof, will be used later on. Henceforth we fix a compatible metric
d
on
\Gamma\sqcup M
.

Proposition 3.2
.

For every
0<\delta<\delta_{\sigma}(\Gamma)
, there exist a finite subset
S\subset\Gamma
,
D_{0}>0
, and
t_{0}>0
so that: for any
m\geq 0
, if
\gamma\in S^{m}
, then the set
\gamma\cdot S\subset S^{m+1}
has the following properties:

(1)

If
\eta\in\gamma\cdot S
, then
\mathcal{S}_{\frac{t_{0}}{4}}(\eta)\subset\mathcal{S}_{\frac{t_{0}}{2}}(\gamma)
.

(2)

The sets
\{\mathcal{S}_{\frac{t_{0}}{4}}(\eta)\}_{\eta\in\gamma\cdot S}
are pairwise disjoint.

(3)

If
\eta\in\gamma\cdot S

||\eta^{-1}\gamma||_{\sigma}\leq D_{0}
.

(4)

\sum_{\eta\in\gamma\cdot S}e^{-\delta||\eta||_{\sigma}}\geq e^{-\delta||\gamma||_{\sigma}}
.

Moreover, the resulting subset
\mathcal{T}=\mathcal{T}_{\delta}
of
\Gamma
defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}:=\bigcup_{m=0}^{\infty}S^{m}

is a free subsemigroup of
\Gamma
with finite generating set

.

Before we can give the proof, we need to establish a few technical lemmas.

Lemma 3.3
.

For any
C>0
, there exists an integer
L=L(C)\geq 1
and a partition
\Gamma=P_{1}\sqcup\cdots\sqcup P_{L}
of
\Gamma
so that for any
1\leq j\leq L
and any pair of distinct elements
\alpha,\beta\in P_{j}
, we have
||\alpha^{-1}\beta||_{\sigma}\geq C
.

Proof.

Fix an enumeration
\Gamma=\{\gamma_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
of the elements of
\Gamma
. By part (2) of Proposition
2.5
, we have that
||\gamma_{n}||_{\sigma}\rightarrow\infty
as
n\rightarrow\infty
. Thus there exists an integer
N\geq 1
so that
||\gamma_{n}||_{\sigma}\geq C
for all
n\geq N
. Define a graph
\mathcal{G}
having vertex set the elements of
\Gamma
as follows. There is an edge between two elements
\alpha
and
\beta
of
\Gamma
if and only if
\alpha\neq\beta
and

\displaystyle\alpha^{-1}\beta\in\{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\dots,\gamma_{N-1},\gamma_{1}^{-1},\gamma_{2}^{-1},\dots,\gamma_{N-1}^{-1}\}.

Thus the degree of the graph
\mathcal{G}
is bounded above by
2N-2
. Applying a greedy coloring, we see that
\mathcal{G}
can be colored by
L:=2N-1
colors so that adjacent vertices have different colors. By construction, this coloring corresponds exactly to a partition
\Gamma=P_{1}\sqcup\cdots\sqcup P_{L}
of
\Gamma
so that for any

, if
\alpha,\beta\in P_{j}
are distinct elements, then

\displaystyle\alpha^{-1}\beta\notin\{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\dots,\gamma_{N-1},\gamma_{1}^{-1},\gamma_{2}^{-1},\dots,\gamma_{N-1}^{-1}\},

which implies that
||\alpha^{-1}\beta||_{\sigma}\geq C
, as desired.
∎

Lemma 3.4
.

Fix
x\in M
,
f\in\Gamma
, and
0<\epsilon^{\prime}<\epsilon
. There exists

so that for all

, if
\gamma\in\mathcal{A}_{\sigma,n}\cap B_{\epsilon^{\prime}}(x)
, then
\gamma f\in B_{\epsilon}(x)

Proof.

Suppose not. Then there is some sequence of integers

and a sequence
\{\gamma_{n_{j}}\}\subset\Gamma
so that
\gamma_{n_{j}}\in\mathcal{A}_{\sigma,n_{j}}\cap B_{\epsilon^{\prime}}(x)
but
\gamma_{n_{j}}f\notin B_{\epsilon}(x)
for all

. By passing to a subsequence
\{\gamma_{n_{j_{k}}}\}
of
\{\gamma_{n_{j}}\}
, we have that
\gamma_{n_{j_{k}}}\rightarrow a\in\overline{B_{\epsilon^{\prime}}(x)}\subset B_{\epsilon}(x)
and
\gamma_{n_{j_{k}}}^{-1}\rightarrow b\in M
as

. By part (1) of Proposition 2.3 of
[
3
]
, it follows that
\gamma_{n_{j_{k}}}|_{M\smallsetminus\{b\}}

\gamma_{n_{j_{k}}}|_{(\Gamma\sqcup M)\smallsetminus\{b\}}
converges locally uniformly to a. Since

(as

), this implies that
\gamma_{n_{j_{k}}}f\in B_{\epsilon}(x)

∎

The following lemma is immediate from part (3) of Proposition

, but we record it below as it will be convenient later.

Lemma 3.5
.

For every
\epsilon>0
, there exists
A=A(\epsilon)>0
so that if
\alpha,\beta\in\Gamma
and

\displaystyle e^{-||\alpha\beta||_{\sigma}}\geq Ae^{-||\alpha||_{\sigma}}e^{-||\beta||_{\sigma}}.

By part (3) of Proposition

, there exists a constant

\displaystyle||\alpha\beta||_{\sigma}\leq||\alpha||_{\sigma}+||\beta||_{\sigma}+C.

Hence

\displaystyle e^{-||\alpha\beta||_{\sigma}}\geq e^{-C}e^{-||\alpha||_{\sigma}}e^{-||\beta||_{\sigma}}=Ae^{-||\alpha||_{\sigma}}e^{-||\beta||_{\sigma}},

where we define

.
∎

Lemma 3.6
.

For every
\epsilon>0
and

\displaystyle 0\leq||\beta||_{\sigma}-||\alpha||_{\sigma}\leq M\ \ \mathrm{and}\ \ ||\alpha^{-1}\beta||_{\sigma}>C,

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\epsilon}(\alpha)\cap\mathcal{S}_{\epsilon}(\beta)=\emptyset.

Suppose not. Then there exists

, an integer

, and sequences

\displaystyle 0\leq||\beta_{n}||_{\sigma}-||\alpha_{n}||_{\sigma}\leq M\ \ \mathrm{and}\ \ ||\alpha_{n}^{-1}\beta_{n}||_{\sigma}>n,

Lemma 3.7

\displaystyle\alpha\mathcal{S}_{\frac{t}{4}}(\alpha^{-1}\beta)\subset\alpha\mathcal{S}_{\frac{t}{2}}(\alpha^{-1})\cap\mathcal{S}_{\frac{t}{8}}(\beta).

Suppose not. Then there exist sequences

(3.8)

\displaystyle x_{n}\in\alpha_{n}\mathcal{S}_{\frac{t}{4}}(\alpha_{n}^{-1}\beta_{n})\smallsetminus\Big(\alpha_{n}\mathcal{S}_{\frac{t}{2}}(\alpha_{n}^{-1})\cap\mathcal{S}_{\frac{t}{8}}(\beta_{n})\Big)

We now turn our attention to the Poincaré series
Q_{\sigma}(s)=\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}e^{-s||\gamma||_{\sigma}}
. Fix
0<\delta<\delta+\epsilon<\delta_{\sigma}(\Gamma)
. By definition, we have

\displaystyle Q_{\sigma}(\delta+\epsilon)=\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}e^{-(\delta+\epsilon)||\gamma||_{\sigma}}=\infty.

Since
\Gamma\sqcup M
is compact, for each

, there exists

\displaystyle\sum_{\eta\in\Gamma\cap B_{\frac{1}{n}}(x_{n})}e^{-(\delta+\epsilon)||\eta||_{\sigma}}=\infty.

Passing to a subsequence, we can assume that

t>0

\displaystyle\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma\cap B_{t}(x)}e^{-(\delta+\epsilon)||\gamma||_{\sigma}}=\infty.

Lemma 3.11
.

For any

and

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}\sum_{\eta\in\mathcal{A}_{\sigma,n}\cap B_{t}(y)}e^{-\delta||\eta||_{\sigma}}=\infty.

The general strategy is as in the proof of Lemma 8.7 of
[

y=\gamma x\in\Gamma x
. By part (1) of Proposition

\displaystyle||\xi||_{\sigma}-C

\displaystyle\leq||\gamma\xi||_{\sigma}\leq||\xi||_{\sigma}+C,\ \mathrm{hence}

\displaystyle e^{-C}e^{-||\xi||_{\sigma}}

\displaystyle\leq e^{-||\gamma\xi||_{\sigma}}\leq e^{C}e^{-||\xi||_{\sigma}}.

\displaystyle e^{-||\gamma\xi||_{\sigma}}\geq\frac{1}{D}e^{-||\xi||_{\sigma}}

n\geq N
so that

\displaystyle\sum_{\gamma\in\mathcal{A}_{\sigma,n}\cap B_{\frac{t_{0}}{2}}(x)}e^{-\delta||\gamma||_{\sigma}}\geq\frac{Le^{\delta\tilde{C}}}{A^{\delta}}.

Thus there exists
1\leq i\leq L
so that

\displaystyle\sum_{\gamma\in P_{i}\cap\mathcal{A}_{\sigma,n}\cap B_{\frac{t_{0}}{2}}(x)}e^{-\delta||\gamma||_{\sigma}}\geq\frac{e^{\delta\tilde{C}}}{A^{\delta}}.

Now let
f_{i}\in F
be such that the elements of
P_{i}f_{i}
are uniformly
\tilde{\epsilon}
-loxodromic. Define

\displaystyle S:=\Big(P_{i}\cap\mathcal{A}_{\sigma,n}\cap B_{\frac{t_{0}}{2}}(x)\Big)f_{i}.

By choosing a possibly larger
n\geq N
, the
\sigma
-magnitudes of the elements of
S
will be sufficiently large for Lemma
3.7
to hold, and furthermore Lemma
2.1
implies that for all
\gamma\in S\cup S^{-1}
, we have

(3.16)

\displaystyle\gamma\big((\Gamma\sqcup M)\smallsetminus B_{\epsilon_{0}}(\gamma^{-1})\big)\subset B_{\epsilon_{0}}(\gamma).

Since
\epsilon_{0}<t_{0}
, this immediately yields

(3.17)

\displaystyle\gamma\big((\Gamma\sqcup M)\smallsetminus B_{t_{0}}(\gamma^{-1})\big)\subset B_{t_{0}}(\gamma).

for all
\gamma\in S\cup S^{-1}
.

Notice that the set
S
contains at least two elements. Indeed,
\delta\tilde{C}>0
and
0<A<1
, so
e^{\delta\tilde{C}}/A^{\delta}>1
, while
e^{-\delta||\gamma||_{\sigma}}\leq e^{-\delta n}<1
for any
\gamma\in P_{i}\cap\mathcal{A}_{\sigma,n}\cap B_{\frac{t_{0}}{2}}(x)
. Hence the above inequality can hold only if
\#(P_{i}\cap\mathcal{A}_{\sigma,n}\cap B_{\frac{t_{0}}{2}}(x))\geq 2
, which is equivalent to
\#S\geq 2
.

By Lemma
3.4
, we have
S\subset B_{t_{0}}(x)
. If
\eta\in S
, then
\eta=\gamma f_{i}
for some
\gamma\in P_{i}\cap\mathcal{A}_{\sigma,n}\cap\mathcal{B}(x,\epsilon)
. Then part (1) of Proposition
2.5
yields

\displaystyle e^{-\delta||\eta||_{\sigma}}

\displaystyle=e^{-\delta||\gamma f_{i}||_{\sigma}}\geq e^{-\delta\tilde{C}}e^{-\delta||\gamma||_{\sigma}}.

Combining this with the above calculations, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{\eta\in S}e^{-\delta||\eta||_{\sigma}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\gamma\in P_{i}\cap\mathcal{A}_{\sigma,n}\cap B_{\frac{t_{0}}{2}}(x)}e^{-\delta||\gamma f_{i}||_{\sigma}}

(3.18)

\displaystyle\geq e^{-\delta\tilde{C}}\sum_{\gamma\in P_{i}\cap\mathcal{A}_{\sigma,n}\cap B_{\frac{t_{0}}{2}}(x)}e^{-\delta||\gamma||_{\sigma}}\geq\frac{1}{A^{\delta}}.

We will now show that the set
S
generates a finitely generated semigroup containing the identity and having all the desired properties. We claim that for any
m\geq 0
and
\gamma\in S^{m}
, the set
\gamma\cdot S\subset S^{m+1}
satisfies properties
(1)-(4)
of Proposition
3.2
. The subset of
\Gamma
defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}:=\bigcup_{m=0}^{\infty}S^{m}

is a finitely generated subsemigroup of
\Gamma
, and after we establish the aforementioned properties, we will be able to explain why
\mathcal{T}
is free (hence the sets in the above union are pairwise disjoint). Before proving by induction that
\gamma\cdot S
has the desired properties, we need some preliminary facts.

Fix distinct elements
\eta
and
\xi
of
P_{i}\cap\mathcal{A}_{\sigma,n}\cap B_{\frac{t_{0}}{2}}(x)
. Then
\eta f_{i}
and
\xi f_{i}
are distinct elements of
S
and without loss of generality we may assume
||\xi f_{i}||_{\sigma}\geq||\eta f_{i}||_{\sigma}
. We have

\displaystyle||\xi f_{i}||_{\sigma}-||\eta f_{i}||_{\sigma}

\displaystyle\leq\Big|||\eta f_{i}||_{\sigma}-||\eta||_{\sigma}\Big|+\Big|||\eta||_{\sigma}-||\xi||_{\sigma}\Big|+\Big|||\xi||_{\sigma}-||\xi f_{i}||_{\sigma}\Big|

(3.19)

\displaystyle\leq 2\tilde{C}+1.

Moreover, using (
3.15
) we obtain

\displaystyle||(\eta f_{i})^{-1}(\xi f_{i})||_{\sigma}

\displaystyle=||f_{i}^{-1}\eta^{-1}\xi f_{i}||_{\sigma}

\displaystyle\geq||\eta^{-1}\xi f_{i}||_{\sigma}-\tilde{C}

\displaystyle\geq||\eta^{-1}\xi||_{\sigma}-2\tilde{C}

(3.20)

\displaystyle\geq C.

Combining (
3.19
) and (
3.20
), Lemma
3.6
tells us that

(3.21)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\frac{t_{0}}{4}}(\eta f_{i})\cap\mathcal{S}_{\frac{t_{0}}{4}}(\xi f_{i})=\emptyset.

We now show that for all
m\geq 1
and any collection of elements
\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{m}\in S
, we have

(3.22)

\displaystyle d(\gamma_{m}^{-1}\cdots\gamma_{1}^{-1},x)>2t_{0}.

By Lemma
2.2
and the fact that
S\subset B_{t_{0}}(x)
, we have

(3.23)

\displaystyle d(\gamma^{-1},x)\geq d(\gamma^{-1},\gamma)-d(\gamma,x)>\tilde{\epsilon}-t_{0}>3t_{0}>2t_{0},

for any
\gamma\in S
. This proves the base case. Assume now that (
3.22
) holds for
m\geq 1
and let
\gamma_{m+1}\in S
. We want to show that
d(\gamma_{m+1}^{-1}\gamma_{m}^{-1}\cdots\gamma_{1}^{-1},x)>2t_{0}
. Set
\xi:=\gamma_{m}^{-1}\cdots\gamma_{1}^{-1}
. By the inductive hypothesis,

\displaystyle d(\xi,\gamma_{m+1})\geq d(\xi,x)-d(x,\gamma_{m+1})>2t_{0}-t_{0}=t_{0},

and therefore by (
3.17
)

\displaystyle\gamma_{m+1}^{-1}\xi\in\gamma_{m+1}^{-1}\big((\Gamma\sqcup M)\smallsetminus B_{t_{0}}(\gamma_{m+1})\big)\subset B_{t_{0}}(\gamma_{m+1}^{-1}).

Along with the base case of the induction, this gives

\displaystyle d(\gamma_{m+1}^{-1}\cdots\gamma_{1}^{-1},x)=d(\gamma_{m+1}^{-1}\xi,x)\geq d(x,\gamma_{m+1}^{-1})-d(\gamma_{m+1}^{-1},\gamma_{m+1}^{-1}\xi)>3t_{0}-t_{0}=2t_{0},

which concludes the proof of (
3.22
).

We are now in the position to verify properties
(1)-(4)
in the statement of the theorem.

Proof of Property (1).

Fix any
m\geq 0
and
\gamma\in S^{m}
. Let
\eta\in\gamma\cdot S
. Using what we just proved,

(3.24)

\displaystyle d(\gamma^{-1},\gamma^{-1}\eta)\geq d(\gamma^{-1},x)-d(x,\gamma^{-1}\eta)>2t_{0}-t_{0}=t_{0}

Thus by Lemma
3.7
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\frac{t_{0}}{4}}(\eta)=\gamma\big(\gamma^{-1}\mathcal{S}_{\frac{t_{0}}{4}}(\gamma\cdot(\gamma^{-1}\eta))\big)\subset\gamma(\gamma^{-1}\mathcal{S}_{\frac{t_{0}}{2}}(\gamma))=S_{\frac{t_{0}}{2}}(\gamma),

which verifies part
(1)
.
∎

Proof of Property (2).

Let
\xi,\eta\in\gamma\cdot S
be distinct children of
\gamma
. We want to show that

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\frac{t_{0}}{4}}(\xi)\cap\mathcal{S}_{\frac{t_{0}}{4}}(\eta)=\emptyset.

Without loss of generality, assume that
||\gamma^{-1}\eta||_{\sigma}\leq||\gamma^{-1}\xi||_{\sigma}
. By construction,
\gamma^{-1}\xi
and
\gamma^{-1}\eta
are both in
S
. Hence,

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\frac{t_{0}}{4}}(\eta)\cap\mathcal{S}_{\frac{t_{0}}{4}}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{S}_{\frac{t_{0}}{4}}(\gamma\cdot\gamma^{-1}\eta)\cap\mathcal{S}_{\frac{t_{0}}{4}}(\gamma\cdot\gamma^{-1}\xi)

\displaystyle=\gamma\cdot\Big(\gamma^{-1}\mathcal{S}_{\frac{t_{0}}{4}}(\gamma\cdot\gamma^{-1}\eta)\cap\gamma^{-1}\mathcal{S}_{\frac{t_{0}}{4}}(\gamma\cdot\gamma^{-1}\xi)\Big)

\displaystyle\subset\gamma\cdot\Big(\mathcal{S}_{\frac{t_{0}}{8}}(\gamma^{-1}\eta)\cap\mathcal{S}_{\frac{t_{0}}{8}}(\gamma^{-1}\xi)\Big)=\emptyset,

where the inclusion in the third line follows from Lemma
3.7
, and where the last set being the empty set follows from Lemma
3.6
. This verifies part
(2)
.
∎

Proof of Property (3).

Define

\displaystyle D_{0}:=\max_{\zeta\in S}||\zeta^{-1}||_{\sigma}.

Given
\eta\in\gamma\cdot S
, we have
\gamma^{-1}\eta\in S
, hence

\displaystyle||\eta^{-1}\gamma||_{\sigma}=||(\gamma^{-1}\eta)^{-1}||_{\sigma}\leq D_{0},

which proves part
(3)
.
∎

Finally, we prove part (4).

Proof of Property (4).

For any
\eta\in\gamma\cdot S
, (
3.24
) gives
d(\gamma^{-1},\gamma^{-1}\eta)>t_{0}
. So by (
3.14
), we have

\displaystyle e^{-||\eta||_{\sigma}}=e^{-||\gamma\cdot\gamma^{-1}\eta||_{\sigma}}\geq Ae^{-||\gamma||_{\sigma}}e^{-||\gamma^{-1}\eta||_{\sigma}}.

Combining this with (
3.18
), we conclude that

\displaystyle\sum_{\eta\in\gamma\cdot S}e^{-\delta||\eta||_{\sigma}}\geq A^{\delta}e^{-\delta||\gamma||_{\sigma}}\sum_{\eta\in\gamma\cdot S}e^{-\delta||\gamma^{-1}\eta||_{\sigma}}=A^{\delta}e^{-\delta||\gamma||_{\sigma}}\sum_{\xi\in S}e^{-\delta||\xi||_{\sigma}}\geq e^{-\delta||\gamma||_{\sigma}},

as desired.
∎

Having established all the properties, it remains to check that
\mathcal{T}
is a free semigroup. This means that if
\alpha\in\mathcal{T}
is any element that can be written both as
\beta_{1}\cdots\beta_{k}
and as
\gamma_{1}\cdots\gamma_{j}
where
\beta_{i},\gamma_{l}\in S
for all
1\leq i\leq k
and
1\leq l\leq j
, then we must have
k=j
and
\beta_{i}=\gamma_{i}
for all
1\leq i\leq k
. There are two cases to consider:

Case 1.
In this case, we have
k=j
. Then there is a largest integer
0\leq m\leq k
so that
\beta_{i}=\gamma_{i}
for all
0\leq i\leq m
(where we define
\beta_{0}=\gamma_{0}:=\mathrm{id}
). If
m=k
, then there is nothing to show, so suppose
m<k
. Then we obtain

(3.25)

\displaystyle\beta_{m+1}\beta_{m+2}\cdots\beta_{k}=\gamma_{m+1}\gamma_{m+2}\cdots\gamma_{k},

and
\beta_{m+1}\neq\gamma_{m+1}
. But then repeatedly applying property (1) (along with the fact that
\mathcal{S}_{\frac{t_{0}}{2}}(\alpha)\subset\mathcal{S}_{\frac{t_{0}}{4}}(\alpha)
for any
\alpha\in\Gamma
) yields

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\frac{t_{0}}{4}}(\beta_{m+1}\beta_{m+2}\cdots\beta_{k})\subset\mathcal{S}_{\frac{t_{0}}{2}}(\beta_{m+1}\beta_{m+2}\cdots\beta_{k-1})\subset\cdots\subset\mathcal{S}_{\frac{t_{0}}{2}}(\beta_{m+1})\subset\mathcal{S}_{\frac{t_{0}}{4}}(\beta_{m+1})

and likewise

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\frac{t_{0}}{4}}(\gamma_{m+1}\gamma_{m+2}\cdots\gamma_{k})\subset\mathcal{S}_{\frac{t_{0}}{2}}(\gamma_{m+1}).

But by property (2), we know that
\mathcal{S}_{\frac{t_{0}}{4}}(\beta_{m+1})
and
\mathcal{S}_{\frac{t_{0}}{4}}(\gamma_{m+1})
are disjoint, hence also
\mathcal{S}_{\frac{t_{0}}{4}}(\beta_{m+1}\beta_{m+2}\cdots\beta_{k})
and
\mathcal{S}_{\frac{t_{0}}{4}}(\gamma_{m+1}\gamma_{m+2}\cdots\gamma_{k})
are disjoint, which contradicts
(\ref{case1})
.

Case 2.
It remains to consider the case when
k\neq j
. Without loss of generality, suppose
k<j
. Let
m\geq 1
be the smallest integer so that
\beta_{m}\neq\gamma_{m}
. If
m<k
, then we can argue just as in the previous case. If however
\beta_{i}=\gamma_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq k
, then we obtain

\displaystyle\mathrm{id}=\gamma_{k+1}\cdots\gamma_{j}.

Again, property (1) allows us to conclude that

(3.26)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\frac{t_{0}}{4}}(\mathrm{id})=\mathcal{S}_{\frac{t_{0}}{4}}(\gamma_{k+1}\cdots\gamma_{j})\subset\cdots\subset\mathcal{S}_{\frac{t_{0}}{4}}(\gamma_{k+1}).

By property (2), we know that the elements of
\{\mathcal{S}_{\frac{t_{0}}{4}}(\eta)\}_{\eta\in S}
are pairwise disjoint. Recalling that the set
S
has at least two elements, property (1) then implies that the shadow
\mathcal{S}_{\frac{t_{0}}{4}}(\gamma_{k+1})
is a proper subset of
\mathcal{S}_{\frac{t_{0}}{4}}(\mathrm{id})
. Hence
(\ref{case2})
implies that
\mathcal{S}_{\frac{t_{0}}{4}}(\mathrm{id})
is a
proper
subset of itself, which is impossible. This concludes the proof of the second case, and with it the proof of the proposition.
∎

3.2.
Properties of Bishop–Jones Semigroups

In this section, we establish numerous useful properties of Bishop–Jones semigroups and prove properties (1), (2), and (5) of Theorem
3.1
.

One immediate corollary of Proposition
3.2
, which establishes property (1) in Theorem
3.1
, is the following result. Fix
0<\delta<\delta_{\sigma}(\Gamma)
and let
\mathcal{T}=\mathcal{T}_{\delta}
be the associated Bishop–Jones semigroup constructed in Proposition
3.2
.

Corollary 3.27
.

The critical exponent of
\mathcal{T}
satisfies

\displaystyle\delta_{\sigma}(\mathcal{T})\geq\delta.

Proof.

We need to show that

\displaystyle\sum_{\gamma\in\mathcal{T}}e^{-\delta||\gamma||_{\sigma}}=\infty.

Inductively applying property (4) of Proposition
3.2
, we see that

\displaystyle\sum_{\gamma\in S^{m}}e^{-\delta||\gamma||_{\sigma}}\geq e^{-\delta||\mathrm{id}||_{\sigma}}>0.

It follows that

\displaystyle\sum_{\gamma\in\mathcal{T}}e^{-\delta||\gamma||_{\sigma}}=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\sum_{\gamma\in S^{m}}e^{-\delta||\gamma||_{\sigma}}\geq\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}e^{-\delta||\mathrm{id}||_{\sigma}}=\infty,

which concludes the proof.
∎

The following technical lemma will be very useful when attempting to control the asymptotic behavior of
\mathcal{T}
and of
\mathcal{T}^{-1}:=\{\gamma^{-1}:\gamma\in\mathcal{T}\}
(see in particular Lemma
3.29
and Lemma
3.45
). Let
\epsilon_{0}
be as in item (ii) in the proof of Proposition
3.2
.

Lemma 3.28
.

For every
m\geq 1
and
\gamma\in S
, we have

\displaystyle S^{m}\subset(\Gamma\sqcup M)\smallsetminus B_{\epsilon_{0}}(\gamma^{-1}).

Proof.

We prove the claim by induction. Let
\alpha,\beta\in S
be arbitrary. By (
3.23
), we have
d(\alpha^{-1},x)>3t_{0}
. By construction, we have
S\subset B_{t_{0}}(x)
. Hence,

\displaystyle d(\alpha^{-1},\beta)\geq d(\alpha^{-1},x)-d(x,\beta)>3t_{0}-t_{0}=2t_{0}>\epsilon_{0}.

Since
\alpha
and
\beta
were arbitrary, this proves the claim for
m=1
. Suppose now that
S^{m}\subset(\Gamma\sqcup M)\smallsetminus B_{\epsilon_{0}}(\gamma^{-1})
for all
\gamma\in S
. Let
\alpha\in S^{m+1}
be arbitrary. Then
\alpha=\eta\beta
for some
\eta\in S
and
\beta\in S^{m}
. By the inductive hypothesis,
\beta\in S^{m}\subset(\Gamma\sqcup M)\smallsetminus B_{\epsilon_{0}}(\eta^{-1})
, so by (
3.16
) we obtain

\displaystyle\alpha=\eta\beta\in\eta\big((\Gamma\sqcup M)\smallsetminus B_{\epsilon_{0}}(\eta^{-1})\big)\subset B_{\epsilon_{0}}(\eta)\subset B_{t_{0}+\epsilon_{0}}(x).

Thus for any
\gamma\in S
we have

\displaystyle d(\gamma^{-1},\alpha)\geq d(\gamma^{-1},x)-d(x,\alpha)\geq 3t_{0}-t_{0}-\epsilon_{0}>\epsilon_{0}.

Since
\alpha\in S^{m+1}
was arbitrary, this shows that

\displaystyle S^{m+1}\subset(\Gamma\sqcup M)\smallsetminus B_{\epsilon_{0}}(\gamma^{-1})

for all
\gamma\in S
, as desired.
∎

In the following lemma, we establish property (2) of Theorem
3.1
.

Lemma 3.29
.

Given any expanding coarse-cocycle
\phi:\Gamma\times M\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, there exist constants
B_{1}=B_{1}(\phi)>1
and
b_{1}=b_{1}(\phi)>0
so that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{B_{1}}|\gamma|_{S}-b_{1}\leq||\gamma||_{\phi}\leq B_{1}|\gamma|_{S}+b_{1}

for all
\gamma\in\mathcal{T}
.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.28
, we know that for any
\gamma\in S
,
n\geq 1
, and
\alpha\in S^{n}
, we have
d(\gamma^{-1},\alpha)\geq\epsilon_{0}
. Let
C_{\phi}=C_{\phi}(\epsilon_{0})>0
be the constant provided by part (3) of Proposition
2.5
associated to the constant
\epsilon_{0}
and the coarse-cocycle
\phi
. Fix
m
sufficiently large so that

\displaystyle||\beta||_{\phi}\geq C_{\phi}+1

for all
\beta\in S^{m}
. For any
n\geq 0
, we can write
\gamma\in S^{n}
in the form

\displaystyle\gamma=\gamma_{1}\cdots\gamma_{q}\alpha,

where
n=qm+r
,
q=\left\lfloor{n/m}\right\rfloor
,
0\leq r\leq m-1
,
\gamma_{j}\in S^{m}
, and
\alpha\in S^{r}
. Using Lemma
3.28
, a calculation of the same sort as in the proof of Proposition
3.2
shows that

\displaystyle d(\gamma_{i}^{-1},\gamma_{i+1}\cdots\gamma_{q})\geq t_{0}-\epsilon_{0}>\epsilon_{0}

for all
1\leq i\leq q-1
. Let
C^{\prime}>0
be the constant provided by part (1) of Proposition
2.5
associated to the finite subset
\bigcup_{i=0}^{m-1}S^{i}
of
\Gamma
and the coarse-cocycle
\phi
. Combining all of the above facts, we obtain

\displaystyle||\gamma||_{\phi}

\displaystyle=||\gamma_{1}\cdots\gamma_{q}\alpha||_{\phi}

\displaystyle\geq||\gamma_{1}\cdots\gamma_{q}||_{\phi}-C^{\prime}

\displaystyle\geq||\gamma_{1}||_{\phi}+||\gamma_{2}\cdots\gamma_{q}||_{\phi}-C_{\phi}-C^{\prime}

\displaystyle\geq\cdots\geq||\gamma_{1}||_{\phi}+\cdots+||\gamma_{q}||_{\phi}-((q-1)C_{\phi}+C^{\prime})

\displaystyle\geq q(C_{\phi}+1)-((q-1)C_{\phi}+C^{\prime})

\displaystyle=q+C_{\phi}-C^{\prime}\geq q-C^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\frac{n}{m}-\Big(\frac{r}{m}+C^{\prime}\Big)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{m}|\gamma|_{S}-(C^{\prime}+1).

Now let
C^{\prime\prime}=C^{\prime\prime}(S)
be the constant provided by part (1) of Proposition
2.5
corresponding to the finite set
S
and coarse-cocycle
\phi
. Since
\gamma=\xi_{1}\cdots\xi_{n}
for some
\xi_{i}\in S
, we obtain

\displaystyle||\gamma||_{\phi}

\displaystyle=||\xi_{1}\cdots\xi_{n}||_{\phi}

\displaystyle\leq||\xi_{1}\cdots\xi_{n-1}||_{\phi}+C^{\prime\prime}

\displaystyle\leq\cdots\leq||\mathrm{id}||_{\phi}+nC^{\prime\prime}

\displaystyle=C^{\prime\prime}|\gamma|_{S}+||\mathrm{id}||_{\phi}.

Thus the lemma holds with
B_{1}=\max\{m,C^{\prime\prime}\}
and
b_{1}=\max\{||\mathrm{id}||_{\phi},C^{\prime}+1\}
.
∎

As we remarked earlier, property (5) in Theorem
3.1
is an immediate consequence of this lemma.

We conclude this section by examining the relation between Bishop–Jones semigroups and the conical limit set of the ambient group. This will be essential in the coming results. The next lemma shows that any two elements of the Bishop–Jones semigroup which differ starting from the first generation are a definite distance apart.

Lemma 3.30
.

There exists
r>0
so that for any two elements
\gamma_{1}=\alpha_{1}\cdots\alpha_{k}\in S^{k}
and
\gamma_{2}=\beta_{1}\cdots\beta_{j}\in S^{j}
of the Bishop–Jones semigroup with
\alpha_{1}\neq\beta_{1}
, we have
d(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})\geq r
.

Proof.

Suppose not. Then there exist sequences
\{\gamma_{n}\}
and
\{\eta_{n}\}
of elements in
\mathcal{T}
with
\gamma_{n}=\alpha_{1,n}\cdots,\alpha_{k(n),n}
and
\eta_{n}=\beta_{1,n}\cdots\beta_{j(n),n}
so that
\alpha_{1,n}\neq\beta_{1,n}
for each
n
, but
\lim_{n\to\infty}d(\gamma_{n},\eta_{n})=0
. After passing to sufficiently many subsequences, we may assume without loss of generality that:

(1)

there are elements
\alpha_{1}\neq\beta_{1}
of
S
so that
\alpha_{1,n}=\alpha_{1}
and
\beta_{1,n}=\beta_{1}
for all
n
, and

(2)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\gamma_{n}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\eta_{n}
.

By property (2) of Proposition
3.2
, we have
\mathcal{S}_{\frac{t_{0}}{4}}(\alpha_{1})\cap\mathcal{S}_{\frac{t_{0}}{4}}(\beta_{1})=\emptyset
. Repeatedly applying property (1) of Proposition
3.2
yields

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\frac{t_{0}}{4}}(\gamma_{n})\subset\mathcal{S}_{\frac{t_{0}}{2}}(\alpha_{1})\subset\mathcal{S}_{\frac{t_{0}}{4}}(\alpha_{1})\ \ \mathrm{and}\ \ \mathcal{S}_{\frac{t_{0}}{4}}(\eta_{n})\subset\mathcal{S}_{\frac{t_{0}}{2}}(\beta_{1})\subset\mathcal{S}_{\frac{t_{0}}{4}}(\beta_{1}),

for all
n
. But as
\{\gamma_{n}\},\{\eta_{n}\}\subset\Gamma
are escaping sequences, part (2) of Proposition
2.10
implies that

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\Big(\inf_{q\in\mathcal{S}_{\frac{t_{0}}{4}}(\alpha_{1})}d(\gamma_{n},q)\Big)\leq\lim_{n\to\infty}\Big(\inf_{q\in\mathcal{S}_{\frac{t_{0}}{4}}(\gamma_{n})}d(\gamma_{n},q)\Big)=0,\ \ \mathrm{and}

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\Big(\inf_{s\in\mathcal{S}_{\frac{t_{0}}{4}}(\beta_{1})}d(\eta_{n},s)\Big)\leq\lim_{n\to\infty}\Big(\inf_{s\in\mathcal{S}_{\frac{t_{0}}{4}}(\eta_{n})}d(\eta_{n},s)\Big)=0.

Since
\mathcal{S}_{\frac{t_{0}}{4}}(\alpha_{1})
and
\mathcal{S}_{\frac{t_{0}}{4}}(\beta_{1})
are closed subsets of
M
, we have
\lim_{n\to\infty}\gamma_{n}\in\mathcal{S}_{\frac{t_{0}}{4}}(\alpha_{1})
and
\lim_{n\to\infty}\eta_{n}\in\mathcal{S}_{\frac{t_{0}}{4}}(\beta_{1})
. But
\lim_{n\to\infty}\gamma_{n}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\eta_{n}
, so

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\frac{t_{0}}{4}}(\alpha_{1})\cap\mathcal{S}_{\frac{t_{0}}{4}}(\beta_{1})\neq\emptyset,

which is a contradiction. This concludes the proof.
∎

The next lemma shows that any geodesic sequence in
\mathcal{T}
limits to a point in
M
which is
\frac{t_{0}}{2}
-uniformly conical.

Lemma 3.31
.

Let
\{\gamma_{n}\}\subset\mathcal{T}
be a geodesic sequence in a Bishop–Jones semigroup
\mathcal{T}
, that is,
\gamma_{n+1}\in\gamma_{n}\cdot S
for all
n\geq 1
. Then
\lim_{n\to\infty}\gamma_{n}
exists. Moreover, denoting
w\in M
by this limit, we have
w\in\Lambda_{\frac{t_{0}}{2}}^{\mathrm{con}}(\Gamma)
.

Proof.

By repeatedly applying property (1) in Proposition
3.2
, we obtain the following decreasing nested sequence of shadows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\frac{t_{0}}{2}}(\gamma_{1})

\displaystyle\supset\mathcal{S}_{\frac{t_{0}}{2}}(\gamma_{2})\supset\cdots\supset\mathcal{S}_{\frac{t_{0}}{2}}(\gamma_{n})\supset\mathcal{S}_{\frac{t_{0}}{2}}(\gamma_{n+1})\supset\cdots.

We claim that the intersection

(3.32)

\displaystyle\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{S}_{\frac{t_{0}}{2}}(\gamma_{n}).

is a singleton. Since each shadow
\mathcal{S}_{\frac{t_{0}}{2}}(\gamma_{n})
is closed and compact, their intersection is nonempty. But by part (2) of Proposition
2.10
,
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathrm{diam}\ \mathcal{S}_{\frac{t_{0}}{2}}(\gamma_{n})=0
, hence
\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{S}_{\frac{t_{0}}{2}}(\gamma_{n})
is a singleton, which we denote by
\{w\}\subset M
. Again by part (2) of Proposition
2.10
, there exist
x_{n}\in\mathcal{S}_{\frac{t_{0}}{2}}(\gamma_{n})
so that
d(\gamma_{n},x_{n})\rightarrow 0
as
n\rightarrow\infty
. Notice also that
d(x_{n},w)\leq\mathrm{diam}\ \mathcal{S}_{\frac{t_{0}}{2}}(\gamma_{n})\rightarrow 0
as
n\rightarrow\infty
. Therefore,

\displaystyle d(\gamma_{n},w)\leq d(\gamma_{n},x_{n})+d(x_{n},w)\rightarrow 0\ \ \mathrm{as}\ \ n\rightarrow\infty,

which shows that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\gamma_{n}=w
. We conclude by Lemma
2.12
that
w\in\Lambda_{\frac{t_{0}}{2}}^{\mathrm{con}}(\Gamma)
.
∎

Let
E=E_{\delta}\subset\Lambda(\Gamma)
denote the set of accumulation points of
\mathcal{T}
. Since
\mathcal{T}
is infinite,
E
is nonempty. We now show that
E
is uniformly conical.

Lemma 3.33
.

We have
E\subset\Lambda_{\frac{t_{0}}{2}}^{\mathrm{con}}(\Gamma)
.

Proof.

Fix
u\in E
and let
\{\gamma_{n}\}
be a distinct sequence of elements of
\mathcal{T}
so that
\gamma_{n}\rightarrow u
. By a diagonal extraction argument, let us find a subsequence
\{\eta_{n}\}
of
\{\gamma_{n}\}
so that for all
k\leq n
,
\eta_{k}
and
\eta_{n}
share the same first, second, …, and
k
-th ancestors; that is, they are descendents of the same elements in
S
,
S^{2}
, all the way up to
S^{k}
. For clarity, we describe how we construct this subsequence.

In what follows, for each
k\geq 1
,
\phi_{k}:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
denotes a strictly increasing function. We first pass to a subsequence
\{\gamma_{\phi_{1}(1)},\gamma_{\phi_{1}(2)},\gamma_{\phi_{1}(3)},\dots\}
of
\{\gamma_{n}\}
so that all elements of this subsequence descend from the same element of
S
(or perhaps an element of this subsequence is this element of
S
). Next we pass to a subsequence
\{\gamma_{\phi_{2}\circ\phi_{1}(1)},\gamma_{\phi_{2}\circ\phi_{1}(2)},\gamma_{\phi_{2}\circ\phi_{1}(3)},\dots\}
of
\{\gamma_{\phi_{1}(n)}\}_{n\geq 1}
so that all elements of this subsequence descend from the same element of
S^{2}
(or perhaps an element of this subsequence is this element of
S^{2}
). Continue passing to such subsequences and then define

\displaystyle\eta_{n}:=\gamma_{\phi_{n}\circ\phi_{n-1}\circ\cdots\circ\phi_{1}(n)}.

Then the sequence
\{\eta_{n}\}
has the aforementioned property. Now consider the sequence
\{\alpha_{n}\}\subset\mathcal{T}
, where
\alpha_{n}\in S^{n}
is the ancestor of
\eta_{n}
in
S^{n}
. By construction,
\{\alpha_{n}\}
is a geodesic sequence. By Lemma
3.31
,
w:=\lim_{n\to\infty}\alpha_{n}
exists and moreover
w\in\Lambda_{\frac{t_{0}}{2}}^{\mathrm{con}}(\Gamma)
. It therefore suffices to show that
w=u
. Recall that
\eta_{n}\rightarrow u
and, by property (1) of Proposition
3.2
, that
\mathcal{S}_{\frac{t_{0}}{4}}(\eta_{n})\subset\mathcal{S}_{\frac{t_{0}}{2}}(\alpha_{n})
. Part (2) of Proposition
2.10
then yields

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\inf_{z\in\mathcal{S}_{\frac{t_{0}}{2}}(\alpha_{n})}d(u,z)\leq\lim_{n\to\infty}\inf_{z\in\mathcal{S}_{\frac{t_{0}}{4}}(\eta_{n})}d(u,z)=0,

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\inf_{z\in\mathcal{S}_{\frac{t_{0}}{2}}(\alpha_{n})}d(w,z)=0,\ \ \mathrm{and}

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathrm{diam}\ \mathcal{S}_{\frac{t_{0}}{2}}(\alpha_{n})=0.

Therefore there are
u_{n},w_{n}\in\mathcal{S}_{\frac{t_{0}}{2}}(\alpha_{n})
so that
d(u,u_{n})\rightarrow 0
,
d(w,w_{n})\rightarrow 0
, and
d(u_{n},w_{n})\rightarrow 0
. It follows that

\displaystyle d(u,w)\leq d(u,u_{n})+d(u_{n},w_{n})+d(w_{n},w)\rightarrow 0,

which shows that
u=w
. This concludes the proof.
∎

3.3.
Critical Exponent Gap for Bishop–Jones Semigroups

In this section, we prove properties (3) and (4) of Theorem
3.1
. We begin by establishing property (3).

Proposition 3.34
.

With
B_{1}=B_{1}(\sigma)>1
and
b_{1}=b_{1}(\sigma)>0
as in part (2) of Theorem
3.1
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{B_{1}}\log(\#S)\leq\delta_{\sigma}(\mathcal{T})\leq B_{1}\log(\#S).

In particular,
\delta_{\sigma}(\mathcal{T})<\infty
.

Proof.

Consider the Poincaré series of
\mathcal{T}
with respect to the word-length
|\cdot|_{S}
, that is the series

\displaystyle Q_{S}^{\mathcal{T}}(t):=\sum_{\gamma\in\mathcal{T}}e^{-t|\gamma|_{S}},

and let

\displaystyle\delta_{S}(\mathcal{T})

\displaystyle:=\inf\{t>0:Q_{S}^{\mathcal{T}}(t)<\infty\}=\limsup_{k\to\infty}\frac{1}{k}\log\{\gamma\in\mathcal{T}:|\gamma|_{S}\leq k\}

denote its critical exponent. Recall from the proof of Proposition
3.2
that
\#S\geq 2
. Since
\mathcal{T}
is a free semigroup, the number of elements of
\mathcal{T}
of word length
k\geq 0
is
(\#S)^{k}
. Thus we obtain

\displaystyle\delta_{S}(\mathcal{T})

\displaystyle=\limsup_{k\to\infty}\frac{1}{k}\log\big(\#\{\gamma\in\mathcal{T}:|\gamma|_{S}\leq k\}\big)

\displaystyle=\limsup_{k\to\infty}\frac{1}{k}\Big(\log\sum_{i=0}^{k}(\#S)^{i}\Big)

\displaystyle=\limsup_{k\to\infty}\frac{1}{k}\log\bigg(\frac{(\#S)^{k+1}-1}{\#S-1}\bigg)

\displaystyle=\limsup_{k\to\infty}\frac{1}{k}\log\big((\#S)^{k+1}-1\big)-\frac{1}{k}\log(\#S-1)

\displaystyle=\log(\#S).

It thus suffices to show that

(3.35)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{B_{1}}\delta_{S}(\mathcal{T})\leq\delta_{\sigma}(\mathcal{T})\leq B_{1}\delta_{S}(\mathcal{T}).

Fix an arbitrary
t>\delta_{S}(\mathcal{T})
. Then,
Q_{S}^{\mathcal{T}}(t)<\infty
. We claim that
Q_{\sigma}^{\mathcal{T}}(B_{1}t)<\infty
. By part (2) of Theorem
3.1

\displaystyle-B_{1}t||\gamma||_{\sigma}\leq-B_{1}t\bigg(\frac{1}{B_{1}}|\gamma|_{S}-b_{1}\bigg)=b_{1}B_{1}t-t|\gamma|_{S}

for all
\gamma\in\mathcal{T}
, hence

\displaystyle Q_{\sigma}^{\mathcal{T}}(B_{1}t)=\sum_{\gamma\in\mathcal{T}}e^{-B_{1}t||\gamma||_{\sigma}}\leq e^{b_{1}B_{1}t}Q_{S}^{\mathcal{T}}(t)<\infty,

as desired. We conclude that
\delta_{\sigma}(\mathcal{T})\leq B_{1}\delta_{S}(\mathcal{T})
, and the other inequality in (
3.35
) is proven entirely analogously. This concludes the proof.
∎

If
\delta_{\sigma}(\Gamma)=\infty
, then the above result gives
\delta_{\sigma}(\mathcal{T})<\delta_{\sigma}(\Gamma)
. Hence to conclude the proof of property (4) of Theorem
3.1
(and thus the proof of the entire theorem), it remains to consider the case when
\delta_{\sigma}(\Gamma)<\infty
. This is precisely the content of the following result.

Theorem 3.36
.

Suppose
\Gamma\subset\mathrm{Homeo}(M)
is a convergence group,
\sigma
is an expanding coarse-cocycle, and
\delta_{\sigma}(\Gamma)<\infty
. Fix
0<\delta<\delta_{\sigma}(\Gamma)
and let
\mathcal{T}=\mathcal{T}_{\delta}
be the corresponding Bishop–Jones subsemigroup of
\Gamma
. Then,

\displaystyle\delta_{\sigma}(\mathcal{T})<\delta_{\sigma}(\Gamma).

Remark 3.37
.

The proof of this theorem will be in the spirit of classical work of Dal’bo–Otal–Peigné (see Proposition 2 of
[
9
]
, and, for additional examples of this technique, Theorem 4.1 of
[
6
]
and Theorem 4.3 of
[
3
]
). When one proves such critical exponent gap results for subgroups, the following hypotheses are typically needed: the first is that the Poincaré series of the subgroup diverges at the critical exponent (in which case, the subgroup is said to be of
divergence type
), and the second is that the limit set of the subgroup is a proper subset of the limit set of the ambient group. As a step towards proving this theorem, we will show that the
\sigma
-Poincaré series of
\mathcal{T}
indeed diverges at
\delta_{\sigma}(\mathcal{T})
(that is,
\mathcal{T}
is a semigroup of divergence type). Moreover, denoting by
E
the set of accumulation points of
\mathcal{T}
and
E^{\prime}
the set of accumulation points of
\mathcal{T}^{-1}:=\{\gamma^{-1}:\gamma\in\mathcal{T}\}
, we show that
E\cup E^{\prime}
is a proper subset of
\Lambda(\Gamma)
. Thus no such assumptions are required in the statement of Theorem
3.36
. Establishing these facts is the crux of the proof of the theorem.

We begin by first showing that
\mathcal{T}
is of divergence type. The main idea is the following. For
R>0
, define

\displaystyle n(R):=\#\{\gamma\in\mathcal{T}:||\gamma||_{\sigma}\leq R\}.

Since the set
E
is uniformly conical, we can adapt an argument of Coornaert (see Theorem 7.2 of
[
7
]
) to show that there exists a constant
C\geq 1
so that

(3.38)

\displaystyle n(R)\geq\frac{1}{C}e^{\delta_{\sigma}(\mathcal{T})R}

for all
R\geq||\mathrm{id}||_{\sigma}
. We are then able to show that the Poincaré series

\displaystyle Q_{\sigma}^{\mathcal{T}}(s):=\sum_{\gamma\in\mathcal{T}}e^{-s||\gamma||_{\sigma}}

for
\mathcal{T}
diverges at the critical exponent
s=\delta_{\sigma}(\mathcal{T})
, which allows us to modify classical arguments to prove Theorem
3.36
.

Before we can adapt Coornaert’s argument, we need to address the following technical issue. In Coornaert’s proof, the Patterson–Sullivan measure of his subgroup is supported on its limit set. However in our case, a coarse-Patterson–Sullivan measure for
\Gamma
might not even assign positive measure to the set
E\subset\Lambda(\Gamma)
. Hence, we need to construct a measure
\nu
, supported on
E
, with some of the properties of a Patterson–Sullivan measure, in the sense which we now describe.

We obtain such a measure by performing a similar construction to that of Theorem
4.1
of
[
3
]
, which is an adaptation of the standard construction due to Patterson
[
25
]
and Sullivan
[
28
]
. Let
\delta:=\delta_{\sigma}(\mathcal{T})<\infty
and for
x\in\Gamma\sqcup M
let
\mathcal{D}_{x}
denote the Dirac measure at
x
. By Lemma 3.1 in
[
25
]
, there exists a non-decreasing function
\chi:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 1}
so that

(a)

For every
\epsilon>0
, there exists
R>0
such that
\chi(r+t)\leq e^{\epsilon t}\chi(r)
for any
r\geq R
and
t\geq 0
,

(b)

\sum_{g\in\mathcal{T}}\chi(||g||_{\sigma})e^{-\delta||g||_{\sigma}}=\infty
.

(when
\sum_{g\in\mathcal{T}}e^{-\delta||g||_{\sigma}}=\infty
, we may take
\chi\equiv 1
).

For
s>\delta
, we define a Borel probability measure on
\Gamma\sqcup M
by

\displaystyle\nu_{s}:=\frac{1}{Q_{\sigma}^{\chi}(s)}\sum_{g\in\mathcal{T}}\chi(||g||_{\sigma})e^{-s||g||_{\sigma}}\mathcal{D}_{g},

where
Q_{\sigma}^{\chi}(s):=\sum_{g\in\mathcal{T}}\chi(||g||_{\sigma})e^{-s||g||_{\sigma}}
, which is finite by property (a) above. Now fix
s_{n}\searrow\delta
so that
\nu_{s_{n}}\overset{\ast}{\rightharpoonup}\nu
.
Notice that
\nu
is a Borel probability measure supported on
E
. The following lemma provides an upper bound on the
\nu
-measure of certain shadows of elements of the Bishop–Jones semigroup
\mathcal{T}
.

Lemma 3.39
.

There exists a constant
C_{1}>1
so that

\displaystyle\nu(\mathcal{S}_{\frac{t_{0}}{2}}(\gamma))\leq C_{1}e^{-\delta||\gamma||_{\sigma}}

for all
\gamma\in\mathcal{T}
.

Proof.

Fix any
\gamma\in S^{n}\subset\mathcal{T}
. Recall from part (2) of Proposition
3.2
that if
\eta\in S^{n}\smallsetminus\{\gamma\}
, then
\mathcal{S}_{\frac{t_{0}}{2}}(\gamma)\cap\mathcal{S}_{\frac{t_{0}}{2}}(\eta)=\emptyset
. Thus there exists an open set
U\subset\Gamma\sqcup M
so that
\mathcal{S}_{\frac{t_{0}}{2}}(\gamma)\subset U
and
\overline{U}\cap\mathcal{S}_{\frac{t_{0}}{2}}(\eta)=\emptyset
for all
\eta\in S^{n}\smallsetminus\{\gamma\}
. Notice that

(3.40)

\displaystyle\#\{g\in\mathcal{T}\cap U:g\notin\gamma\mathcal{T}\}<\infty.

Indeed, if not, then after passing to a subsequence, there exists a sequence of distinct elements
\{g_{n}\}\subset\mathcal{T}\cap U
, all descending from the same element
\eta\in S^{n}\smallsetminus\{\gamma\}
, and such that
g_{n}\rightarrow x\in\overline{U}
. By part (2) of Proposition
2.10
, there exist
x_{n}\in\mathcal{S}_{\frac{t_{0}}{2}}(g_{n})
so that
x_{n}\rightarrow x
. Repeatedly applying part (1) of Proposition
3.2
gives
\mathcal{S}_{\frac{t_{0}}{2}}(g_{n})\subset\mathcal{S}_{\frac{t_{0}}{2}}(\eta)
, and since
\mathcal{S}_{\frac{t_{0}}{2}}(\eta)
is closed this implies that
x\in\overline{U}\cap\mathcal{S}_{\frac{t_{0}}{2}}(\eta)
, which is a contradiction. Thus (
3.40
) holds.

We now will collect various estimates which will be needed to give an upper bound on the
\nu
-measure of
\mathcal{S}_{\frac{t_{0}}{2}}(\gamma)
.

As in Lemma
3.29
, we have
d(\gamma^{-1},h)\geq\epsilon_{0}
for all
h\in\mathcal{T}
(where
\epsilon_{0}>0
is defined in the start of the proof of Proposition
3.2
). By part (3) of Proposition
2.5
, there is a constant
C=C(\epsilon_{0})>0
so that

(3.41)

\displaystyle e^{-s_{n}||\gamma h||_{\sigma}}\leq e^{\delta C}\cdot e^{-\delta||\gamma||_{\sigma}}\cdot e^{-s_{n}||h||_{\sigma}}

for all
h\in\mathcal{T}
and all
s_{n}
.

Now fix
\epsilon>0
and let
R=R(\epsilon)>0
be as in the first property of the function
\chi
defined above. By part (1) of Proposition
2.5
, there is a constant
C^{\prime}=C^{\prime}(\gamma)>0
so that

\displaystyle||h||_{\sigma}-C^{\prime}\leq||\gamma h||_{\sigma}\leq||h||_{\sigma}+C^{\prime}

for all
h\in\mathcal{T}
. Defining
F_{\epsilon}
to be the finite set
F_{\epsilon}:=\{h\in\mathcal{T}:||h||_{\sigma}<R(\epsilon)\}
, we then obtain

(3.42)

\displaystyle\chi(||\gamma h||_{\sigma})\leq\chi(||h||_{\sigma}+C^{\prime})\leq e^{\epsilon C^{\prime}}\chi(||h||_{\sigma})

for all
h\in\mathcal{T}\smallsetminus F_{\epsilon}
. Set

\displaystyle T_{\epsilon}:=\{g\in\mathcal{T}\cap U:g\notin\gamma(\mathcal{T}\smallsetminus F_{\epsilon})\},

and observe from (
3.40
) and the fact that
F_{\epsilon}
is finite that this set
T_{\epsilon}
is also finite. We are now in a position to obtain an upper bound on
\nu(\mathcal{S}_{\frac{t_{0}}{2}}(\gamma))
.

By Urysohn’s Lemma, there exists a continuous function
f:\Gamma\sqcup M\rightarrow[0,1]
so that
f\equiv 1
on
\mathcal{S}_{\frac{t_{0}}{2}}(\gamma)
and
f\equiv 0
on
(\Gamma\sqcup M)\smallsetminus U
. Using the above established facts, we obtain

\displaystyle\nu(\mathcal{S}_{\frac{t_{0}}{2}}(\gamma))

\displaystyle\leq\int f\ d\nu=\lim_{s_{n}\searrow\delta}\int f\ d\nu_{s_{n}}

\displaystyle=\lim_{s_{n}\searrow\delta}\frac{1}{Q_{\sigma}^{\chi}(s_{n})}\sum_{g\in\mathcal{T}\cap U}\chi(||g||_{\sigma})e^{-s_{n}||g||_{\sigma}}f(g)

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{s_{n}\searrow\delta}\frac{1}{Q_{\sigma}^{\chi}(s_{n})}\sum_{g\in\mathcal{T}\cap U}\chi(||g||_{\sigma})e^{-s_{n}||g||_{\sigma}}

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{s_{n}\searrow\delta}\frac{1}{Q_{\sigma}^{\chi}(s_{n})}\bigg(\sum_{g\in T_{\epsilon}}\chi(||g||_{\sigma})e^{-s_{n}||g||_{\sigma}}+\sum_{h\in\mathcal{T}\smallsetminus F_{\epsilon}}\chi(||\gamma h||_{\sigma})e^{-s_{n}||\gamma h||_{\sigma}}\bigg)

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{s_{n}\searrow\delta}\frac{1}{Q_{\sigma}^{\chi}(s_{n})}\bigg(\sum_{g\in T_{\epsilon}}\chi(||g||_{\sigma})e^{-\delta||g||_{\sigma}}+\sum_{h\in\mathcal{T}\smallsetminus F_{\epsilon}}\chi(||\gamma h||_{\sigma})e^{-s_{n}||\gamma h||_{\sigma}}\bigg)

\displaystyle=\lim_{s_{n}\searrow\delta}\frac{1}{Q_{\sigma}^{\chi}(s_{n})}\sum_{h\in\mathcal{T}\smallsetminus F_{\epsilon}}\chi(||\gamma h||_{\sigma})e^{-s_{n}||\gamma h||_{\sigma}}

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{s_{n}\searrow\delta}\frac{1}{Q_{\sigma}^{\chi}(s_{n})}e^{\epsilon C^{\prime}}\cdot e^{\delta C}\cdot e^{-\delta||\gamma||_{\sigma}}\sum_{h\in\mathcal{T}\smallsetminus F_{\epsilon}}\chi(||h||_{\sigma})e^{-s_{n}||h||_{\sigma}}

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{s_{n}\searrow\delta}e^{\epsilon C^{\prime}}\cdot e^{\delta C}\cdot e^{-\delta||\gamma||_{\sigma}}\cdot\frac{1}{Q_{\sigma}^{\chi}(s_{n})}\cdot Q_{\sigma}^{\chi}(s_{n})

\displaystyle=e^{\epsilon C^{\prime}}\cdot e^{\delta C}\cdot e^{-\delta||\gamma||_{\sigma}}.

Since
\epsilon>0
was arbitrary and the constant
C^{\prime}
depends only on
\gamma
and not on
\epsilon
, sending
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
gives

\displaystyle\nu(\mathcal{S}_{\frac{t_{0}}{2}}(\gamma))\leq e^{\delta C}\cdot e^{-\delta||\gamma||_{\sigma}}.

Notice that the constant
C
depends only on
\epsilon_{0}
, not on the particular choice of
\gamma\in\mathcal{T}
. Since
\gamma\in\mathcal{T}
was arbitrary, the lemma holds with
C_{1}:=e^{\delta C}
.
∎

We need one more observation before we can establish (
3.38
). Let
\{\gamma_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}\subset\mathcal{T}
be any geodesic ray in the Bishop–Jones semigroup
\mathcal{T}
starting at the identity element
\gamma_{0}=\mathrm{id}
. Let
t_{0}>0
be as in the proof of Proposition
3.2
and recall from (
3.24
) that we have
d(\gamma_{n}^{-1},\gamma_{n}^{-1}\gamma_{n+1})>t_{0}
for all
n\geq 1
, where
\gamma_{n}^{-1}\gamma_{n+1}\in S
(the set generating
\mathcal{T}
), by construction. Let
C=C(t_{0})
be the constant furnished by part (3) of Proposition
2.5
and define
D:=\big(\max_{s\in S}||s||_{\sigma}+C\big)/2
. Then,

\displaystyle||\gamma_{n+1}||_{\sigma}\leq||\gamma_{n}||_{\sigma}+||\gamma_{n}^{-1}\gamma_{n+1}||_{\sigma}+C\leq||\gamma_{n}||_{\sigma}+2D.

Given
R>||\gamma_{0}||_{\sigma}+D
, define

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}(R):=\{\gamma\in\mathcal{T}:R-D\leq||\gamma||_{\sigma}\leq R+D\}.

Let
n_{0}
be the largest integer so that
||\gamma_{n_{0}}||_{\sigma}<R-D
. Then,

\displaystyle R-D\leq||\gamma_{n_{0}+1}||_{\sigma}\leq||\gamma_{n_{0}}||_{\sigma}+2D<R+D,

hence
\gamma_{n_{0}+1}\in\mathcal{T}(R)
. In other words, we have shown that the set
\mathcal{T}(R)
contains an element of any geodesic ray in
\mathcal{T}
starting at
\gamma_{0}=\mathrm{id}
. We now prove the aforementioned estimate (
3.38
) needed for the proof of Theorem
3.36
.

Proposition 3.43
.

There exists a constant
C\geq 1
so that

\displaystyle n(R)\geq\frac{1}{C}e^{\delta_{\sigma}(\mathcal{T})R}

for all
R\geq||\mathrm{id}||_{\sigma}
.

Proof.

Fix
R>||\gamma_{0}||_{\sigma}+D=||\mathrm{id}||_{\sigma}+D
. By the proof of Lemma
3.33
, given any
u\in E
, there exists a geodesic ray
\{\gamma_{n}\}\subset\mathcal{T}
so that
\{u\}=\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{S}_{\frac{t_{0}}{2}}(\gamma_{n})
. By the previous discussion,
\gamma_{n}\in\mathcal{T}(R)
for some
n
. Since
u\in E
was arbitrary, we have
E\subset\bigcup_{\gamma\in\mathcal{T}(R)}\mathcal{S}_{\frac{t_{0}}{2}}(\gamma)
.
Let
\nu
be the Borel probability measure and
C_{1}>1
be the constant provided by Lemma
3.39
. Then
\nu
is supported on
E
and,

\displaystyle\nu(\mathcal{S}_{\frac{t_{0}}{2}}(\gamma))\leq C_{1}e^{-\delta_{\sigma}(\mathcal{T})||\gamma||_{\sigma}}

for all
\gamma\in\mathcal{T}
. Hence,

\displaystyle C_{1}n(R+D)e^{-\delta_{\sigma}(\mathcal{T})(R-D)}

\displaystyle\geq C_{1}\sum_{\gamma\in\mathcal{T}(R)}e^{-\delta_{\sigma}(\mathcal{T})(R-D)}

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{\gamma\in\mathcal{T}(R)}\nu\big(\mathcal{S}_{\frac{t_{0}}{2}}(\gamma)\big)

\displaystyle\geq\nu(E)=1,

and therefore

\displaystyle n(R+D)\geq\frac{1}{C_{1}}e^{\delta_{\sigma}(\mathcal{T})(R-D)}=\frac{1}{C_{1}e^{2D\delta_{\sigma}(\mathcal{T})}}e^{\delta_{\sigma}(\mathcal{T})(R+D)}\geq\frac{1}{C}e^{\delta_{\sigma}(\mathcal{T})(R+D)},

where
C=C_{1}e^{2D\delta_{\sigma}(\mathcal{T})}
. By enlarging
C
if necessary, we obtain

\displaystyle n(R)\geq\frac{1}{C}e^{\delta_{\sigma}(\mathcal{T})R}

for all
R\geq||\mathrm{id}||_{\sigma}
, as desired.
∎

Corollary 3.44
.

The Poincaré series
Q_{\sigma}^{\mathcal{T}}(s)=\sum_{\gamma\in\mathcal{T}}e^{-s||\gamma||_{\sigma}}
diverges at
s=\delta_{\sigma}(\mathcal{T})
.

Proof.

Notice that the Poincaré series

\displaystyle Q_{\sigma}^{\mathcal{T}}(s)=\sum_{\gamma\in\mathcal{T}}e^{-s||\gamma||_{\sigma}}

converges if and only if the series

\displaystyle F(s)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\big(n(k)-n(k-1)\big)e^{-sk}

converges (since the ratio of the two series is bounded between two strictly positive constants). The series
F(s)
converges if and only if the series

\displaystyle G(s)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}n(k)e^{-sk}

converges (because
n(k)=\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}n(i+1)-n(i)
). Thus it suffices to show that the series
G(s)
diverges at
s=\delta_{\sigma}(\mathcal{T})
. Let
s>\delta_{\sigma}(\mathcal{T})
. By Proposition
3.43
,

\displaystyle G(s)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}n(k)e^{-sk}

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{k=\lfloor||\mathrm{id}||_{\sigma}\rfloor+1}^{\infty}n(k)e^{-sk}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{e^{-(s-\delta_{\sigma}(\mathcal{T}))(\lfloor||\mathrm{id}||_{\sigma}\rfloor+1)}}{C}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}e^{-(s-\delta_{\sigma}(\mathcal{T}))k}

\displaystyle=e^{-(s-\delta_{\sigma}(\mathcal{T}))(\lfloor||\mathrm{id}||_{\sigma}\rfloor+1)}\cdot\frac{1}{C(1-e^{-(s-\delta_{\sigma}(\mathcal{T}))})}.

Hence
Q_{\sigma}^{\mathcal{T}}(s)
diverges at
s=\delta_{\sigma}(\mathcal{T})
, as desired.
∎

We now turn to showing that
E\cup E^{\prime}
is a proper subset of
\Lambda(\Gamma)
.

Lemma 3.45
.

The set
E\cup E^{\prime}
is a proper subset of
\Lambda(\Gamma)
.

Proof.

We use the same notation as we did throughout the proof of Proposition
3.2
. Let
y\in\Lambda(\Gamma)
and
\epsilon_{0}>0
be as in point (ii) in the beginning of the proof of Proposition
3.2
. We will show that
y\notin E\cup E^{\prime}
. By construction (see once again point (ii) in the proof of the aforementioned proposition), we have
y\in B_{s_{2}}(x)\smallsetminus\overline{B_{s_{1}}(x)}
. Notice that (
3.22
) says exactly that the set
\mathcal{T}^{-1}
lies a distance greater than
2t_{0}
from the point
x
. Thus
E^{\prime}\subset M\smallsetminus B_{2t_{0}}(x)
, and since
s_{2}<2t_{0}
, we have

.

It remains to show that

. By construction,

\displaystyle t_{0}+\epsilon_{0}<\frac{2}{3}d(y,x)+\frac{1}{12}d(y,x)=s_{1}.

We claim that

\displaystyle E\subset\overline{B_{t_{0}+\epsilon_{0}}(x)}\subset B_{s_{1}}(x).

Indeed, given
\alpha\in S^{m+1}
,
m\geq 1
, write
\alpha=\gamma\beta

\gamma\in S
and
\beta\in S^{m}
. By Lemma

), we have

\displaystyle\alpha\in\gamma\big((\Gamma\sqcup M)\smallsetminus B_{\epsilon_{0}}(\gamma^{-1})\big)\subset B_{\epsilon_{0}}(\gamma)\subset B_{t_{0}+\epsilon_{0}}(x).

It follows that
E\subset\overline{B_{t_{0}+\epsilon_{0}}(x)}\subset B_{s_{1}}(x)
. But as
y\notin\overline{B_{s_{1}}(x)}
, we see that

. Thus
y\in\Lambda(\Gamma)\smallsetminus(E\cup E^{\prime})
, concluding the proof.
∎

We now give the proof of Theorem
3.36
.

Proof of Theorem
3.36
.

By the preceding lemma,
E\cup E^{\prime}
is a proper closed subset of
\Lambda(\Gamma)
. Fix an open set
U\subset M

U\cap\Lambda(\Gamma)\neq\emptyset

\displaystyle N:=\#\{\gamma\in\mathcal{T}:\gamma U\cap U\neq\emptyset\}<\infty.

If not, then there exist a sequence of distinct elements
\{\gamma_{n}\}\subset\mathcal{T}
and a sequence

\gamma_{n}(\overline{U})\rightarrow a

By Theorem

and a

-Patterson–Sullivan measure
\mu

for
\Gamma

. We therefore have

\displaystyle e^{-C_{1}-\delta_{\sigma}(\Gamma)\sigma(\gamma^{-1},\cdot)}\leq\frac{d(\gamma_{*}\mu)}{d\mu}\leq e^{C_{1}-\delta_{\sigma}(\Gamma)\sigma(\gamma^{-1},\cdot)}

\mu

\delta_{\sigma}(\mathcal{T})=\delta_{\sigma}(\Gamma)

\Gamma

\displaystyle N+1

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{\gamma\in\mathcal{T}}\mu(\gamma U)=\sum_{\gamma\in\mathcal{T}}(\gamma^{-1})_{*}\mu(U)\geq e^{-C_{1}}\sum_{\gamma\in\mathcal{T}}\int_{U}e^{-\delta_{\sigma}(\Gamma)\sigma(\gamma,x)}\ d\mu(x)

4.
Background on Lie Theory

In this section, we record the necessary background on Lie theory, transverse groups, and Anosov semigroups that we will need in order to apply the results of the previous section to prove Theorems
5.1
and
5.2
in the coming section.

4.1.
Basics of Lie Theory

Let
G
be a connected semisimple real Lie group without compact factors and with finite center and let
\mathfrak{g}
denote its Lie algebra. Let
b
denote the Killing form of
\mathfrak{g}
and fix a Cartan involution
\tau
of
\mathfrak{g}
; that is, an involution of
\mathfrak{g}
for which the bilinear pairing
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
on
\mathfrak{g}
defined by
\langle X,Y\rangle:=-b(X,\tau(Y))
is an inner product. Then
\mathfrak{g}
decomposes as
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{k}\oplus\mathfrak{p}
, where
\mathfrak{k}
and
\mathfrak{p}
are the 1 and -1 eigenspaces of
\tau
. The subalgebra
\mathfrak{k}
is a maximal compact Lie subalgbra of
\mathfrak{g}
and we denote by
K\subset G
the maximal compact Lie subgroup of
G
whose Lie algebra is
\mathfrak{k}
.

Fix a maximal abelian subspace
\mathfrak{a}\subset\mathfrak{p}
, known as a
Cartan subspace
, which is unique up to conjugation. The Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
then decomposes as

\displaystyle\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{g}_{0}\oplus\bigoplus_{\alpha\in\Sigma}\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha},

which is called the
restricted root space decomposition
associated to
\mathfrak{a}
; in this decomposition, for
\alpha\in\mathfrak{a}^{*}
, we define

\displaystyle\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}:=\{X\in\mathfrak{g}:[H,X]=\alpha(H)X\ \ \mathrm{for\ all}\ H\in\mathfrak{a}\},

and call

\displaystyle\Sigma:=\{\alpha\in\mathfrak{a}^{*}\smallsetminus\{0\}:\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}\neq 0\}

the set of
restricted roots
. Now fix an element
H_{0}\in\mathfrak{a}
so that
\alpha(H_{0})\neq 0
for all
\alpha\in\Sigma
, and let

\displaystyle\Sigma^{+}:=\{\alpha\in\Sigma:\alpha(H_{0})>0\}\ \ \mathrm{and}\ \ \Sigma^{-}:=-\Sigma^{+}.

Notice that
\Sigma=\Sigma^{+}\sqcup\Sigma^{-}
. We write
\Delta\subset\Sigma^{+}
for the set of
simple restricted roots
, which, by definition, consists of all the elements of
\Sigma^{+}
which cannot be written as a non-trivial positive integer linear combination of elements in
\Sigma^{+}
. As
\Sigma
is an abstract root system on
\mathfrak{a}^{*}
, it follows that
\Delta
is a basis of
\mathfrak{a}^{*}
and every
\alpha\in\Sigma^{+}
is a non-negative integral linear combination of elements in
\Delta
. See for instance Chapter II of Knapp’s book
[
23
]
for more details.

4.1.1.
The Weyl Group, Opposition Involution, and Cartan Projection

The
Weyl group
of
\mathfrak{a}
is given by
\mathcal{W}:=N_{K}(\mathfrak{a})/Z_{K}(\mathfrak{a})
, where
N_{K}(\mathfrak{a})\subset K
is the normalizer of
\mathfrak{a}
in
K
and
Z_{K}(\mathfrak{a})\subset K
is the centralizer of
\mathfrak{a}
in
K
. The Weyl group is a finite group generated by reflections of
\mathfrak{a}
(with respect to the inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
) about the kernels of the simple restricted roots in
\Delta
. Hence,
\mathcal{W}
acts transitively on the set of
Weyl chambers
, which are the closures of the connected components of

\displaystyle\mathfrak{a}-\bigcup_{\alpha\in\Sigma}\ker\alpha.

We call the Weyl chamber

\displaystyle\mathfrak{a}^{+}:=\{X\in\mathfrak{a}:\alpha(X)\geq 0\ \ \mathrm{for\ all}\ \alpha\in\Delta\},

the
positive Weyl chamber
. In the Weyl group
\mathcal{W}
, there is a unique element
w_{0}
, called the
longest element
, with the property that
w_{0}(\mathfrak{a}^{+})=-\mathfrak{a}^{+}
. Thus the longest element allows us to define an involution
\iota:\mathfrak{a}\rightarrow\mathfrak{a}
,
H\mapsto-w_{0}\cdot H
, which is called the
opposition involution
. It induces an involution of
\Sigma
preserving
\Delta
, denoted by
\iota^{*}
, defined by
\iota^{*}(\alpha)=\alpha\circ\iota
for all
\alpha\in\Delta
. Moreover, if
k_{0}\in N_{K}(\mathfrak{a})
denotes a representative of the longest element
w_{0}\in\mathcal{W}
, then

(4.1)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Ad}(k_{0})\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}=\mathfrak{g}_{-\iota^{*}(\alpha)}

for all
\iota\in\Sigma
.

Let
\kappa:G\rightarrow\mathfrak{a}^{+}
denote the
Cartan projection
, that is
\kappa(g)\in\mathfrak{a}^{+}
is the unique element so that

\displaystyle g=k\exp(\kappa(g))k^{\prime}

for some
k,k^{\prime}\in K
. We note that
k,k^{\prime}\in K
need not be unique. Such a decomposition of
g\in G
is called a
KA^{+}K
decomposition (see Theorem
7.39
of
[
23
]
). Notice that since
\iota(-\mathfrak{a}^{+})=\mathfrak{a}^{+}
, we have
\iota(\kappa(g))=\kappa(g^{-1})
for all
g\in G
. Recall also that when
G=\mathrm{SL}(d,\mathbb{R})
, the Cartan projection is given by

\displaystyle\kappa(g)=\mathrm{diag}(\log\sigma_{1}(g),\dots,\log\sigma_{d}(g)),

where
\sigma_{1}(g)\geq\cdots\geq\sigma_{d}(g)>0
are the singular values of
g
.

4.1.2.
Parabolic Subgroups and Flag Manifolds

Given a subset
\theta\subset\Delta
, consider the subset of positive restricted roots
\Sigma_{\theta}^{+}:=\Sigma^{+}\smallsetminus(\mathrm{Span}(\Delta\smallsetminus\theta))
. We then define the
\textit{standard parabolic subgroup associated to}\ \theta
, denoted by
P_{\theta}=P_{\theta}^{+}\subset G
, to be the normalizer in
G
of

\displaystyle\mathfrak{u}_{\theta}=\mathfrak{u}_{\theta}^{+}:=\bigoplus_{\alpha\in\Sigma_{\theta}^{+}}\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}.

The
\textit{standard partial flag variety associated to}\ \theta
is

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\theta}=\mathcal{F}_{\theta}^{+}:=G/P_{\theta}.

We similarly define the
\textit{standard parabolic subgroup opposite to}\ P_{\theta}
, denoted by
P_{\theta}^{-}
, to be the normalizer in
G
of

\displaystyle\mathfrak{u}_{\theta}^{-}:=\bigoplus_{\alpha\in\Sigma_{\theta}^{+}}\mathfrak{g}_{-\iota^{*}(\alpha)},

and the
\textit{standard partial flag variety opposite to}\ \mathcal{F}_{\theta}
by

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\theta}^{-}:=G/P_{\theta}^{-}.

Using (
4.1
), we see that if
k_{0}\in N_{K}(\mathfrak{a})
is a representative of the longest element
w_{0}\in\mathcal{W}
, then

\displaystyle k_{0}P_{\theta}^{\pm}k_{0}^{-1}=k_{0}^{-1}P_{\theta}^{\pm}k_{0}=P_{\iota^{*}(\theta)}^{\mp}

We say that two flags
F_{1}\in\mathcal{F}_{\theta}^{+}
and
F_{2}\in\mathcal{F}_{\theta}^{-}
are
transverse
if the pair
(F_{1},F_{2})
is contained in the
G
-orbit of
(P_{\theta}^{+},P_{\theta}^{-})
in
\mathcal{F}_{\theta}^{+}\times\mathcal{F}_{\theta}^{-}
. Given any flag
F\in\mathcal{F}_{\theta}^{\pm}
, we denote by
\mathcal{Z}_{F}\subset\mathcal{F}_{\theta}^{\mp}
the set of flags that are not transverse to
F
. As the set of transverse pairs in
\mathcal{F}_{\theta}^{+}\times\mathcal{F}_{\theta}^{-}
is an open and dense subset,
\mathcal{Z}_{F}
is a closed subset with empty interior. Furthermore,
\mathcal{Z}_{F}=\mathcal{Z}_{F^{\prime}}
if and only if
F=F^{\prime}
.

If
\theta\subset\Delta
is
symmetric
, that is
\iota^{*}(\theta)=\theta
, then the fact that
k_{0}P_{\theta}k_{0}^{-1}=P_{\theta}^{-}
allows us to identify
\mathcal{F}_{\theta}
and
\mathcal{F}_{\theta}^{-}
via the map

\displaystyle gP_{\theta}^{-}\mapsto gk_{0}^{-1}P_{\theta}.

Thus it makes sense to say whether or not two elements in
\mathcal{F}_{\theta}
are transverse. Precisely, two flags
g_{1}P_{\theta}
and
g_{2}P_{\theta}
in
\mathcal{F}_{\theta}
are
transverse
if and only if there exists some
g\in G
so that
gg_{1}\in P_{\theta}
and
gg_{2}k_{0}\in P_{\theta}^{-}
. Abusing notation, given a flag
F\in\mathcal{F}_{\theta}
, we write
\mathcal{Z}_{F}\subset\mathcal{F}_{\theta}
for the set of flags that are not transverse to
F
.

When
\theta=\Delta
is the full set of simple restricted roots, its associated parabolic subgroup
P_{\Delta}
, which we denote simply by
P
, is a minimal (with respect to inclusion) parabolic subgroup called the
Borel subgroup
. The associated flag variety
\mathcal{F}_{\Delta}
, henceforth written simply
\mathcal{F}=G/P
, is the
Furstenberg boundary
.

4.1.3.
Partial Cartan Subspaces and the Partial Iwasawa Cocycle

Given a non-empty subset
\theta\subset\Delta
, we define

\displaystyle\mathfrak{a}_{\theta}

\displaystyle=\bigcap_{\alpha\in\Delta\smallsetminus\theta}\ker\alpha,\ \ \ \ \mathfrak{a}_{\theta}^{+}=\mathfrak{a}_{\theta}\cap\mathfrak{a}^{+},

\displaystyle A_{\theta}

\displaystyle=\exp(\mathfrak{a}_{\theta}),\ \mathrm{and}\ \ A_{\theta}^{+}=\exp(\mathfrak{a}_{\theta}^{+}).

Let

\displaystyle p_{\theta}:\mathfrak{a}\rightarrow\mathfrak{a}_{\theta}

denote the projection of
\mathfrak{a}
onto
\mathfrak{a}_{\theta}
invariant under all
w\in\mathcal{W}
fixing
\mathfrak{a}_{\theta}
pointwise. The dual space
\mathfrak{a}_{\theta}^{*}=\mathrm{Hom}(\mathfrak{a}_{\theta},\mathbb{R})
can in fact be identified with the subspace of
p_{\theta}
-invariant linear functionals on
\mathfrak{a}
:

\displaystyle\mathfrak{a}_{\theta}^{*}=\{\phi\in\mathfrak{a}^{*}:\phi\circ p_{\theta}=\phi\}.

Thus the
\textit{partial Cartan projection associated to}\ \theta

\displaystyle\kappa_{\theta}=p_{\theta}\circ\kappa:G\rightarrow\mathfrak{a}_{\theta}

has the property that, for all
\phi\in\mathfrak{a}_{\theta}^{*}
and
g\in G
,

(4.2)

\displaystyle\phi(\kappa_{\theta}(g))=\phi(\kappa(g)).

Let
N:=\mathrm{exp}(\mathfrak{u}_{\Delta})
. The
Iwasawa decomposition
is the statement that the map

\displaystyle K\times A\times N

\displaystyle\rightarrow G

\displaystyle(k,a,n)

\displaystyle\mapsto kan,

is a diffeomorphism; see Proposition 6.46 of
[
23
]
. In
[
26
]
, Quint used the Iwasawa decomposition to define the
Iwasawa cocycle

\displaystyle B:G\times\mathcal{F}\rightarrow\mathfrak{a},

which has the defining property that
gk\in K\cdot\mathrm{exp}(B(g,F))\cdot N
for all
(g,F)\in G\times\mathcal{F}
, where
k\in K
is such that
F=kP
. Intuitively, the Iwasawa cocycle can be thought of as a vector valued analog of the Busemann cocycle in the rank 1 setting, and was used by Quint to define higher-rank Patterson–Sullivan measures.

Given
\theta\subset\Delta
, we have
P\subset P_{\theta}
, hence the identity map on
G
induces a surjection
\Pi_{\theta}:\mathcal{F}\rightarrow\mathcal{F}_{\theta}
. We can then define the
partial Iwasawa cocycle
to be the map

\displaystyle B_{\theta}:G\times\mathcal{F}_{\theta}\rightarrow\mathfrak{a}_{\theta}

given by
B_{\theta}(g,F)=p_{\theta}(B(g,F^{\prime}))
for some (all)
F^{\prime}\in\Pi_{\theta}^{-1}(F)
. Quint showed that this is a well-defined cocycle, meaning that

\displaystyle B_{\theta}(gh,F)=B_{\theta}(g,hF)+B_{\theta}(h,F)

for all
g,h\in G
and
F\in\mathcal{F}_{\theta}
. See section
6
of
[
26
]
for more details.

Suppose now that
\theta\subset\Delta
is symmetric, that is,
\iota^{*}(\theta)=\theta
. Let
\mathcal{F}_{\theta}^{(2)}\subset\mathcal{F}_{\theta}\times\mathcal{F}_{\theta}
denote the subset of transverse flags. There is a smooth map
G_{\theta}:\mathcal{F}_{\theta}^{(2)}\rightarrow\mathfrak{a}_{\theta}
that satisfies

\displaystyle G_{\theta}(gF,gF^{\prime})-G_{\theta}(F,F^{\prime})=\iota\circ B_{\theta}(g,F)+B_{\theta}(g,F^{\prime}),

for all
g\in G
and
(F,F^{\prime})\in\mathcal{F}_{\theta}^{(2)}
. See for instance the end of section 2 of
[
20
]
.

4.1.4.
Critical Exponents

Let
\Gamma\subset G
be any discrete sub(semi)group and let
\theta\subset\Delta
. In higher rank, the (partial) Cartan projection should intuitively be thought of as a “vector valued distance,” and as such allows us to measure asymptotic growth rates of certain quantities related to
\Gamma
. More precisely, given
\phi\in\mathfrak{a}_{\theta}^{*}
, let
Q_{\Gamma}^{\phi}(s)
denote the Poincaré series

\displaystyle Q_{\Gamma}^{\phi}(s)=\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}e^{-s\phi(\kappa_{\theta}(\gamma))}.

As before, the
critical exponent

\delta^{\phi}(\Gamma)
is the abscissa of convergence of this Poincaré series, i.e.,

\displaystyle\delta^{\phi}(\Gamma)=\inf\{s>0:Q_{\Gamma}^{\phi}(s)<\infty\}\in[0,\infty].

Equivalently,

\displaystyle\delta^{\phi}(\Gamma)=\limsup_{T\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{T}\log\#\{\gamma\in\Gamma:\phi(\kappa_{\theta}(\gamma))<T\}.

4.2.
Transverse Groups

In this section, we recall the definition of a transverse subgroup of a connected semisimple real Lie group
G
with finite center and no compact factors. Transverse groups are a family of discrete subgroups of semisimple Lie groups, which contain both the class of Anosov and relatively Anosov groups, as well as all discrete subgroups of rank one Lie groups.

For the remainder of this section, we assume that
\theta\subset\Delta
is symmetric. Following the notation of
[
13
]
, we define a map

\displaystyle U_{\theta}:G\rightarrow\mathcal{F}_{\theta}

by fixing a
KA^{+}K
-decomposition
g=k_{g}\mathrm{exp}(\kappa(g))k_{g}^{\prime}
for each
g\in G
and setting
U_{\theta}(g)=k_{g}P_{\theta}
. If
\alpha(\kappa(g))>0
for all
\alpha\in\theta
, then
U_{\theta}(g)
is independent of the choice of
KA^{+}K
-decomposition (see for instance Theorem 1.1 in Chapter IX of
[
15
]
), and so
U_{\theta}
is continuous on the set

\displaystyle\{g\in G:\alpha(\kappa(g))>0\ \mathrm{for\ all}\ \alpha\in\theta\}.

We say that a subgroup
\Gamma
of
G
is
P_{\theta}
-
divergent
if

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\min_{\alpha\in\theta}\alpha(\kappa(\gamma_{n}))=\infty

for any sequence
\{\gamma_{n}\}
of distinct elements of
\Gamma
. A
P_{\theta}
-divergent group is discrete and its
\theta
-
limit set
is the set of accumulation points in
\mathcal{F}_{\theta}
of
\{U_{\theta}(\gamma):\gamma\in\Gamma\}
. Using Proposition 2.6 of
[
6
]
, one can show that
\Lambda_{\theta}(\Gamma)
is a closed,
\Gamma
-invariant subset of
\mathcal{F}_{\theta}
. A
P_{\theta}
-divergent subgroup
\Gamma\subset G
is called
P_{\theta}
-
transverse
if in addition any two distinct flags
F,G\in\Lambda_{\theta}(\Gamma)
are transverse. We remark that in the literature, divergent and transverse groups are sometimes called
regular
and
regular antipodal
, respectively (for instance, in the work of Kapovich–Leeb–Porti
[
19
]
). An essential feature of transverse groups is that they act on their limit sets as convergence groups.

Proposition 4.3

(Section 5.1 of
[
19
]
or Proposition 3.3 of
[
5
]
)
.

If
\Gamma
is
P_{\theta}
-transverse, then
\Gamma
acts on
\Lambda_{\theta}(\Gamma)
as a convergence group. Moreover, if
\Gamma
is non-elementary, then
\Gamma
acts on
\Lambda_{\theta}(\Gamma)
minimally, and
\Lambda_{\theta}(\Gamma)
is perfect.

The following proposition will allow us to apply Theorem
3.1
to the setting of transverse groups.

Proposition 4.4

(Part (1) of Proposition 10.3 of
[
4
]
)
.

Suppose
\Gamma
is non-elementary
P_{\theta}
-transverse,
\phi\in\mathfrak{a}_{\theta}^{*}
, and

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\phi(\kappa(\gamma_{n}))=\infty

for any sequence
\{\gamma_{n}\}
of pairwise distinct elements of
\Gamma
. Define cocycles
\sigma_{\phi},\bar{\sigma}_{\phi}:\Gamma\times\Lambda_{\theta}(\Gamma)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
by

\displaystyle\sigma_{\phi}(\gamma,F)=\phi(B_{\theta}(\gamma,F))\ \ \mathrm{and}\ \ \bar{\sigma}_{\phi}(\gamma,F)=\iota^{*}(\phi)(B_{\theta}(\gamma,F)).

Then
(\sigma_{\phi},\bar{\sigma}_{\phi},\phi\circ G_{\theta})
is a continuous GPS system for the action of
\Gamma
on
\Lambda_{\theta}(\Gamma)
. Moreover, one can take the magnitude functions to be

\displaystyle||\gamma||_{\sigma_{\phi}}=\phi(\kappa(\gamma))\ \ \mathrm{and}\ \ ||\gamma||_{\bar{\sigma}_{\phi}}=\iota^{*}(\phi)(\kappa(\gamma)).

In particular,
\delta^{\phi}(\Gamma)=\delta_{\sigma_{\phi}}(\Gamma)
.

4.3.
Anosov Semigroups

In this section, we discuss the notion of Anosov semigroups recently introduced by Kassel and Potrie in
[
22
]
. The motivation of their paper was to show how certain techniques in the study of discrete subgroups of Lie groups could be fruitful in understanding linear cocycles in dynamics, and vice-versa. One of the main topics of their paper was semigroup representations with a uniform gap in the exponential growth rate of eigenvalues and singular values. In particular, the authors wished to extend the notion of Anosov representations of discrete subgroups of Lie groups – initially introduced by Labourie in
[
24
]
and further developed by Guichard–Wienhard in
[
14
]
– to semigroups. However, it is not clear how to adapt the original definition of Anosov representations to this more general setting. Instead, motivated by the notion of dominated splittings for linear cocycles, Kassel–Potrie came up with the definition detailed below.

Let
\Lambda
be a semigroup with a finite generating subset
S
. That is, any element of
\Lambda
can be written as a product of elements of elements of
S
. A semigroup may or may not admit an identity element
\mathrm{id}
. For
\gamma\in\Lambda\smallsetminus\{\mathrm{id}\}
, define the
\textit{word length of}\ \gamma\ \textit{with respect to}\ S
to be

\displaystyle|\gamma|_{S}:=\min\{k\geq 1:\gamma=f_{1}\cdots f_{k},\ \mathrm{where}\ f_{i}\in S\ \mathrm{for\ all}\ 1\leq i\leq k\},

and set
|\mathrm{id}|_{S}:=0
. Note that if
S^{\prime}
is another finite generating set of
\Lambda
, then there exists a constant
M\geq 1
so that

(4.5)

\displaystyle M^{-1}|\gamma|_{S^{\prime}}\leq|\gamma|_{S}\leq M|\gamma|_{S^{\prime}}

for all
\gamma\in\Lambda
.

Definition 4.6
.

Let
G
be a connected semisimple real Lie group and
\theta\subset\Delta
a collection of simple restricted roots. A semigroup homomorphism
\rho:\Lambda\rightarrow G
is said to be
P_{\theta}
-
Anosov
if there exists constants
C,c>0
so that

(4.7)

\displaystyle\alpha(\kappa(\rho(\gamma)))\geq C|\gamma|_{S}-c

for all
\gamma\in\Lambda
and
\alpha\in\theta
.

If
\Lambda\subset G
is a semigroup and satisfies (
4.7
) with
\rho
being the inclusion
\Lambda\hookrightarrow G
, then we say that
\Lambda
is a
P_{\theta}
-
\textit{Anosov subsemigroup of}\ G
. Note that by (
4.5
), this definition is independent of the choice of finite generating set for
\Lambda
.

5.
Critical Exponents of Transverse Groups and Relations to Corlette’s Gap Theorem

In this section, we provide two applications of Theorem
3.1
. The first proves Theorem
1.1
from the introduction.

Theorem 5.1
.

Suppose
\Gamma
is a non-elementary
P_{\theta}
-transverse group and
\phi\in\mathfrak{a}_{\theta}^{*}
is such that
\phi(\kappa(\gamma_{n}))\rightarrow\infty
for any sequence
\{\gamma_{n}\}
of pairwise distinct elements of
\Gamma
. Then there exists a sequence
\{\Gamma_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
of free
P_{\theta}
-Anosov subsemigroups of
\Gamma
so that

(1)

\delta^{\phi}(\Gamma_{n})<\delta^{\phi}(\Gamma)
for all
n\geq 1
, and

(2)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\delta^{\phi}(\Gamma_{n})=\delta^{\phi}(\Gamma)
.

Proof.

By Proposition
4.4
,
(\sigma_{\phi},\bar{\sigma}_{\phi},\phi\circ G_{\theta})
is a continuous GPS system for the action of
\Gamma
on its limit set
\Lambda_{\theta}(\Gamma)
. In particular,
\sigma_{\phi}:\Gamma\times\Lambda_{\theta}(\Gamma)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is an expanding coarse-cocycle and we may consider the corresponding
\sigma_{\phi}
-magnitude defined by
||\gamma||_{\sigma_{\phi}}=\phi(\kappa(\gamma))
for all
\gamma\in\Gamma
. Moreover, for any subset
H\subset\Gamma
, we have
\delta^{\phi}(H)=\delta_{\sigma_{\phi}}(H).
Let
\{\delta_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
be a sequence of real numbers so that
\delta_{n}<\delta^{\phi}(\Gamma)
for all
n
and
\lim_{n\to\infty}\delta_{n}=\delta^{\phi}(\Gamma)
. For each
n\geq 1
, let
\Gamma_{n}
be the free Bishop–Jones subsemigroup of
\Gamma
associated to
\delta_{n}
provided by Theorem
3.1
. By properties (1) and (4) of Theorem
3.1
, we have

\displaystyle\delta_{n}\leq\delta^{\phi}(\Gamma_{n})<\delta^{\phi}(\Gamma),

for all
n\geq 1
, and therefore

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\delta^{\phi}(\Gamma_{n})=\delta^{\phi}(\Gamma).

This proves items (1) and (2) in the statement of the theorem. It only remains to prove that each
\Gamma_{n}
is
P_{\theta}
-Anosov. Fix an arbitrary
n\geq 1
and let
S_{n}
be the corresponding finite generating subset of
\Gamma_{n}
. Since
\Gamma
is
P_{\theta}
-transverse, it is in particular
P_{\theta}
-divergent, so
\lim_{m\to\infty}\alpha(\kappa(\gamma_{m}))=\infty
for any
\alpha\in\theta
and any sequence
\{\gamma_{m}\}
of distinct elements in
\Gamma
. Thus Proposition
4.4
tells us once again that
\sigma_{\alpha}:\Gamma\times\Lambda_{\theta}(\Gamma)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is an expanding coarse-cocycle for each
\alpha\in\theta
, and that we can choose the corresponding magnitude functions to be
||\gamma||_{\sigma_{\alpha}}=\alpha(\kappa(\gamma))
. By property (2) of Theorem
3.1
, for each
\alpha\in\theta
, there exist constants
A_{\alpha},a_{\alpha}>0
so that

\displaystyle\alpha(\kappa(\gamma))=||\gamma||_{\sigma_{\alpha}}\geq A_{\alpha}|\gamma|_{S_{n}}-a_{\alpha}

for all
\gamma\in\Gamma_{n}
. Setting
C:=\min\{A_{\alpha}:\alpha\in\theta\}>0
and
c:=\max\{a_{\alpha}:\alpha\in\theta\}>0
, we obtain

\displaystyle\alpha(\kappa(\gamma))\geq C|\gamma|_{S_{n}}-c

for all
\gamma\in\Gamma_{n}
and
\alpha\in\theta
, which says exactly that
\Gamma_{n}
is
P_{\theta}
-Anosov.
∎

We now prove Theorem
1.3
, which shows that there is no critical exponent gap for subsemigroups of lattices in
\mathrm{Sp}(n,1)
and
\mathrm{F}_{4}^{-20}
. Since the class of transverse groups contains all discrete subgroups of rank one Lie groups, this result follows immediately from the previous result. However, there is a proof which does not require the theory of transverse groups, which is the one we present below.

Theorem 5.2
.

(1)

Let
\Gamma\subset\mathrm{Sp}(n,1)
,
n\geq 2
, be a lattice. Then there exists a sequence
\{\Gamma_{m}\}_{m\geq 1}
of finitely generated free subsemigroups of
\Gamma
so that
\delta(\Gamma_{m})<\delta(\Gamma)=4n+2
for all
m\geq 1
and
\lim_{m\to\infty}\delta(\Gamma_{m})=4n+2
.

(2)

Let
\Gamma\subset\mathrm{F}_{4}^{-20}
be a lattice. Then there exists a sequence
\{\Gamma_{m}\}_{m\geq 1}
of finitely generated free subsemigroups of
\Gamma
so that
\delta(\Gamma_{m})<\delta(\Gamma)=22
for all
m\geq 1
and
\lim_{m\to\infty}\delta(\Gamma_{m})=22
.

Proof.

We prove only (1) as the proof of (2) is entirely analogous. Fix
n\geq 2
, let
\Gamma\subset\mathrm{Sp}(n,1)=\mathrm{Isom}^{+}(\mathbf{H}_{\mathbb{H}}^{n})
be a lattice, and fix a basepoint
o\in\mathbf{H}_{\mathbb{H}}^{n}
. The quaternionic hyperbolic space
\mathbf{H}_{\mathbb{H}}^{n}
is a proper geodesic Gromov hyperbolic space, and therefore, as in Example
2.8
,(\beta,\beta,G)
is a GPS system. Taking the
\beta
-magnitude function to be
||\gamma||_{\beta}=d(o,\gamma o)
, we have
\delta_{\beta}(\Gamma)=\delta(\Gamma)=4n+2
. The result then follows from parts (1) and (4) of Theorem
3.1
.
∎

6.
Quasi-Isometric Embeddings of Bishop–Jones Semigroups Into the Symmetric Space

Fix a non-empty, symmetric subset
\theta\subset\Delta
and let
\Gamma\subset G
be
P_{\theta}
-transverse. Write
X=G/K
for the symmetric space associated to
G
and let
o=[K]\in G/K
. Fix a
K
-invariant norm
||\cdot||
on
\mathfrak{g}
and a metric
d_{X}
on
X
induced from the Killing form on
\mathfrak{g}
, so that

\displaystyle d_{X}(go,ho)=||\kappa(g^{-1}h)||

for all
g,h\in G
. Given a linear functional
\phi\in\mathfrak{a}_{\theta}^{*}
, define a function
\textbf{d}_{\phi}:X\times X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
by

\displaystyle\textbf{d}_{\phi}(go,ho):=\phi(\kappa(g^{-1}h))=\phi(\kappa_{\theta}(g^{-1}h)).

Since the Cartan projection
\kappa:\mathfrak{a}^{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is bi-
K
-invariant, this is a well-defined left
G
-invariant function. We remark that sometimes the function
\mathbf{d}_{\phi}
is a distance function on the whole symmetric space, and sometimes it is not; see for example
[
17
]
and
[
18
]
.

Under some additional assumptions on
\phi
, we show that we may quasi-isometrically embed Bishop–Jones semigroups of
\Gamma
into
X
in such a way that, when restricted to the image of these semigroups, the function
\mathbf{d}_{\phi}
behaves like a metric, up to a uniform additive error. This is inspired by work of Dey–Kim–Oh in
[
10
]
and heavily relies on Theorem
6.4
due to the same authors. In what follows, for some fixed
\psi\in\theta
, let
\mathcal{T}
be a Bishop–Jones subsemigroup of
\Gamma
with respect to the expanding coarse-cocycle
\sigma_{\psi}:\Gamma\times\Lambda_{\theta}(\Gamma)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
of Proposition
4.4
. Delaying certain definitions for the moment, the main result of this section is the following.

Theorem 6.1

(Coarse Triangle Inequality)
.

There exists a
\theta
-admissible cone
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathfrak{a}^{+}
so that: if
\phi\in\mathfrak{a}_{\theta}^{*}
is positive on
\mathcal{C}_{\theta}\smallsetminus\{0\}
and
\phi(\kappa(\gamma_{n}))\rightarrow\infty
for any distinct sequence of elements
\{\gamma_{n}\}
in
\Gamma
, then there exists a constant
D=D(\phi,\mathcal{T})>0
so that for all
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\gamma_{3}\in\mathcal{T}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{d}_{\phi}(\gamma_{1}o,\gamma_{3}o)\leq\mathbf{d}_{\phi}(\gamma_{1}o,\gamma_{2}o)+\mathbf{d}_{\phi}(\gamma_{2}o,\gamma_{3}o)+D.

Before giving the proof, we recall some relevant notions and results from
[
10
]
. Set
\mathcal{W}_{\theta}=\{w\in\mathcal{W}:w(\theta)=\theta\}
.

Definition 6.2

(See Section 4 of
[
10
]
)
.

Define a closed cone
\mathcal{C}
in
\mathfrak{a}^{+}
to be
\theta
-
admissible
if the following three conditions hold:

(1)

The cone
\mathcal{C}
is opposition involution invariant:
\iota(\mathcal{C})=\mathcal{C}
,

(2)

\mathcal{W}_{\theta}\cdot\mathcal{C}=\bigcup_{w\in\mathcal{W}_{\theta}}\mathrm{Ad}_{w}\mathcal{C}
is convex,

(3)

\mathcal{C}\bigcap\big(\bigcup_{\alpha\in\theta}\ker\alpha\big)=\{0\}
.

For a
\theta
-admissible cone
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathfrak{a}^{+}
, we say that an ordered pair of distinct points
(x_{1},x_{2})
in
X
is
\mathcal{C}
-
regular
if for
g_{1},g_{2}\in G
with
x_{1}=g_{1}o
and
x_{2}=g_{2}o
, we have
\kappa(g_{1}^{-1}g_{2})\in\mathcal{C}
.

Definition 6.3

(Definition 4.2 of
[
10
]
)
.

Let
(Z,d_{Z})
be a metric space and
f:Z\rightarrow X
a map. For a cone
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathfrak{a}^{+}
and a constant
B\geq 0
, we say that
f
is
(\mathcal{C},B)
-
regular
if the pair
(f(z_{1}),f(z_{2}))
is
\mathcal{C}
-regular for all
z_{1},z_{2}\in Z
such that
d_{Z}(z_{1},z_{2})\geq B
. We simply say that
f
is
\mathcal{C}
-
regular
if it is
(\mathcal{C},B)
-regular for some
B\geq 0
.

The importance of this definition comes from the following result of Dey–Kim–Oh in
[
10
]
which roughly states that the image of a quasi-isometric embedding from a geodesic metric space into the symmetric space
X
that is
\mathcal{C}
-regular for a
\theta
-admissible cone
\mathcal{C}
has a metric-like function which satisifes a coarse version of the triangle inequality. This will play a crucial role in the proof of Theorem
6.1
; we write
\mathcal{C}_{\theta}=p_{\theta}(\mathcal{C})
.

Theorem 6.4

(Theorem 4.3 of
[
10
]
)
.

Let
Z
be a geodesic metric space and
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathfrak{a}^{+}
a
\theta
-admissible cone. Let
f:Z\rightarrow X
be a
\mathcal{C}
-regular quasi-isometric embedding. If
\phi\in\mathfrak{a}_{\theta}^{*}
is positive on
\mathcal{C}_{\theta}\smallsetminus\{0\}
, then there exists a constant
D=D_{\phi}\geq 0
such that for all
x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}\in f(Z)
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{d}_{\phi}(x_{1},x_{3})\leq\mathbf{d}_{\phi}(x_{1},x_{2})+\mathbf{d}_{\phi}(x_{2},x_{3})+D.

We can now give the proof of Theorem
6.1
.

Proof of Theorem
6.1
.

We view the Bishop–Jones semigroup
\mathcal{T}
as a geodesic metric space by equipping it with its “tree metric”
d_{\mathcal{T}}
, that is, the natural analog in the case of a finitely generated free semigroup of the metric defined on the Cayley graph of a finitely generated free group. By Theorem
6.4
, it suffices to construct a
\theta
-admissible cone
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathfrak{a}^{+}
and a
\mathcal{C}
-
\mathrm{regular}
quasi-isometric embedding
f:\mathcal{T}\rightarrow X
. Define
f:\mathcal{T}\rightarrow X
by
f(\gamma)=\gamma o
. We first show that
f
is a quasi-isometric embedding. That is, we need to show there exist constants
Q>1
and
q>0
so that

(6.5)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{Q}d_{\mathcal{T}}(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})-q\leq d_{X}(\gamma_{1}o,\gamma_{2}o)\leq Qd_{\mathcal{T}}(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})+q.

Let
S
denote the finite subset of
\Gamma
which generates
\mathcal{T}
as a free semigroup. Given
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\in\mathcal{T}
, let
\eta
be the most recent “ancestor” of both
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
. More precisely,
\eta\in S^{m}
where
m\geq 0
is the largest integer so that we may write both
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
as
\gamma_{1}=\eta\alpha_{1}\cdots\alpha_{k}
and
\gamma_{2}=\eta\beta_{1}\cdots\beta_{j}
, where the
\alpha_{i}
and
\beta_{l}
are in
S
. Notice that
\alpha_{1}\neq\beta_{1}
(as otherwise
m
would not be the largest integer with this property). Furthermore,
d_{\mathcal{T}}(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})=d_{\mathcal{T}}(\eta^{-1}\gamma_{1},\eta^{-1}\gamma_{2})
and

\displaystyle d_{X}(\gamma_{1}o,\gamma_{2}o)=||\kappa(\gamma_{1}^{-1}\gamma_{2})||=||\kappa((\eta^{-1}\gamma_{1})^{-1}\cdot(\eta^{-1}\gamma_{2}))||=d_{X}(\eta^{-1}\gamma_{1}o,\eta^{-1}\gamma_{2}o).

Hence to prove (
6.5
) in general, it suffices to prove it in the case when
\gamma_{1}=\alpha_{1}\cdots\alpha_{k}
and
\gamma_{2}=\beta_{1}\cdots\beta_{j}
are two arbitrary elements of the semigroup with
\alpha_{1}\neq\beta_{1}
. We begin with the easier inequality in (
6.5
), which is the upper bound on
d_{X}(\gamma_{1}o,\gamma_{2}o)
. First, notice that

\displaystyle d_{X}(o,\gamma_{1}o)

\displaystyle=d_{X}(o,\alpha_{1}\cdots\alpha_{k}o)

\displaystyle\leq d_{X}(o,\alpha_{1}o)+d_{X}(\alpha_{1}o,\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}o)+\cdots+d_{X}(\alpha_{1}\cdots\alpha_{k-1}o,\alpha_{1}\cdots\alpha_{k}o)

\displaystyle=||\kappa(\alpha_{1})||+||\kappa(\alpha_{1}^{-1}\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2})||+\cdots+||\kappa(\alpha_{k-1}^{-1}\cdots\alpha_{1}^{-1}\alpha_{1}\cdots\alpha_{k-1}\alpha_{k})||

\displaystyle=||\kappa(\alpha_{1})||+||\kappa(\alpha_{2})||+\cdots+||\kappa(\alpha_{k})||

\displaystyle\leq\Big(\max_{\zeta\in S}||\kappa(\zeta)||\Big)\cdot k

\displaystyle=\Big(\max_{\zeta\in S}||\kappa(\zeta)||\Big)\cdot d_{\mathcal{T}}(\mathrm{id},\gamma_{1}).

Set
L=\max_{\zeta\in S}||\kappa(\zeta)||
. Likewise,
d_{X}(o,\gamma_{2}o)\leq Ld_{\mathcal{T}}(\mathrm{id},\gamma_{2})
, hence

\displaystyle d_{X}(\gamma_{1}o,\gamma_{2}o)\leq d_{X}(\gamma_{1}o,o)+d_{X}(o,\gamma_{2}o)\leq L\big(d_{\mathcal{T}}(\gamma_{1},\mathrm{id})+d_{\mathcal{T}}(\mathrm{id},\gamma_{2})\big)=Ld_{\mathcal{T}}(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}),

which shows that the right-most inequality in (
6.5
) holds with
Q=L
and
q=0
. It remains to prove the other inequality. First, notice that the magnitude functions
||\cdot||_{\sigma_{\phi}}
and the symmetric space distance
d_{X}
are related via

\displaystyle||\alpha^{-1}\beta||_{\sigma_{\phi}}=|\phi(\kappa(\alpha^{-1}\beta))|\leq||\phi||_{\mathrm{op}}\cdot||\kappa(\alpha^{-1}\beta)||=||\phi||_{\mathrm{op}}\cdot d_{X}(\alpha o,\beta o),

hence

(6.6)

\displaystyle d_{X}(\alpha o,\beta o)\geq\frac{1}{||\phi||_{\mathrm{op}}}||\alpha^{-1}\beta||_{\sigma_{\phi}}

for all
\alpha,\beta\in\mathcal{T}
. By part (2) of Theorem
3.1
, there exist constants
A=A(\sigma_{\phi})>1
and
a=a(\sigma_{\phi})>0
so that

(6.7)

\displaystyle||\gamma||_{\sigma_{\phi}}\geq\frac{1}{A}|\gamma|_{S}-a=\frac{1}{A}d_{\mathcal{T}}(\mathrm{id},\gamma)-a

for all
\gamma\in\mathcal{T}
(we call these constants
A
and
a
instead of
B_{1}
and
b_{1}
as in part (2) of Theorem
3.1
to avoid causing confusion with the constants
B
and
b
of Lemma
6.10
). As before, let
\gamma_{1}=\alpha_{1}\cdots\alpha_{k}
and
\gamma_{2}=\beta_{1}\cdots\beta_{j}
be two arbitrary elements of
\mathcal{T}
with
\alpha_{1}\neq\beta_{1}
. Recall that we denote by
d
the metric on the compact space
\Gamma\sqcup\Lambda(\Gamma)
, and that this metric is
not

\Gamma
-invariant. By Lemma
3.30
, there is a constant
r>0
(independent of
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
) so that
d(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})\geq r
. Let
C=C(r)
be the constant provided by part (3) of Proposition
2.5
when taking
\epsilon=r
. Then
d(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})\geq r
implies that

(6.8)

\displaystyle||\gamma_{1}^{-1}\gamma_{2}||_{\sigma_{\phi}}\geq||\gamma_{1}^{-1}||_{\sigma_{\phi}}+||\gamma_{2}||_{\sigma_{\phi}}-C.

Applying Proposition
2.7
and part (5) of Theorem
3.1
to the GPS system
(\sigma_{\phi},\bar{\sigma}_{\phi},\phi\circ G_{\theta})
, we obtain a constants
D>1
and
M>0
so that

\displaystyle||\gamma^{-1}||_{\sigma_{\phi}}\geq\frac{1}{D}||\gamma||_{\sigma_{\phi}}-M

for all
\gamma\in\mathcal{T}
. Thus, (
6.8
) becomes

(6.9)

\displaystyle||\gamma_{1}^{-1}\gamma_{2}||_{\sigma_{\phi}}\geq\frac{1}{D}||\gamma_{1}||_{\sigma_{\phi}}+||\gamma_{2}||_{\sigma_{\phi}}-(M+C).

Combining (
6.6
), (
6.7
), and (
6.9
), we obtain

\displaystyle d_{X}(\gamma_{1}o,\gamma_{2}o)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{||\phi||_{\mathrm{op}}}||\gamma_{1}^{-1}\gamma_{2}||_{\sigma_{\phi}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{||\phi||_{\mathrm{op}}}\bigg(\frac{1}{D}||\gamma_{1}||_{\sigma_{\phi}}+||\gamma_{2}||_{\sigma_{\phi}}-(M+C)\bigg)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{||\phi||_{\mathrm{op}}}\bigg(\frac{1}{D}\Big(\frac{1}{A}d_{\mathcal{T}}(\mathrm{id},\gamma_{1})-a\Big)+\Big(\frac{1}{A}d_{\mathcal{T}}(\mathrm{id},\gamma_{2})-a\Big)-(M+C)\bigg)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{||\phi||_{\mathrm{op}}}\bigg(\frac{1}{AD}\big(d_{\mathcal{T}}(\gamma_{1},\mathrm{id})+d_{\mathcal{T}}(\mathrm{id},\gamma_{2})\big)-\Big(\frac{a}{D}+a+M+C\Big)\bigg)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{AD\cdot||\phi||_{\mathrm{op}}}\cdot d_{\mathcal{T}}(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})-\frac{\frac{a}{D}+a+M+C}{||\phi||_{\mathrm{op}}}.

Thus (
6.5
) holds with

\displaystyle Q=\max\{L,AD\cdot||\phi||_{\mathrm{op}}\}\ \mathrm{and}\ q=\frac{\frac{a}{D}+a+M+C}{||\phi||_{\mathrm{op}}},

which shows that the map
f:\mathcal{T}\rightarrow X
is indeed a quasi-isometric embedding.

In order to finish the proof, we need the following technical lemma.

Lemma 6.10
.

There exist
B,b>0
so that, for any two elements
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\in\mathcal{T}
with
d_{\mathcal{T}}(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})\geq B
, we have

(6.11)

\displaystyle\min_{\alpha\in\theta}||\gamma_{1}^{-1}\gamma_{2}||_{\sigma_{\alpha}}=\min_{\alpha\in\theta}\alpha(\kappa(\gamma_{1}^{-1}\gamma_{2}))\geq b||\kappa(\gamma_{1}^{-1}\gamma_{2})||.

Proof of Lemma
6.10
.

As in the verification of (
6.5
), it suffices to prove the lemma in the case when
\gamma_{1}=\alpha_{1}\cdots\alpha_{k}
and
\gamma_{2}=\beta_{1}\cdots\beta_{j}
are two arbitrary elements of the semigroup with
\alpha_{1}\neq\beta_{1}
. By part (5) of Theorem
3.1
, there exist constants
P>1
and
p>0
so that for all
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\in\mathcal{T}
, we have

\displaystyle\min_{\alpha\in\theta}||\gamma_{1}^{-1}\gamma_{2}||_{\sigma_{\alpha}}\geq\frac{1}{P}||\gamma_{1}^{-1}\gamma_{2}||_{\sigma_{\phi}}-p.

Hence it suffices to show that there exist
B,b>0
so that if
d_{\mathcal{T}}(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})\geq B
, then

(6.12)

\displaystyle||\gamma_{1}^{-1}\gamma_{2}||_{\sigma_{\phi}}=\phi(\kappa(\gamma_{1}^{-1}\gamma_{2}))\geq bP||\kappa(\gamma_{1}^{-1}\gamma_{2})||+Pp.

Let
A,a,M,C,D
be the constants of inequalities (
6.7
), (
6.8
), and (
6.9
), and let
Q,q
be the quasi-isometric embedding constants of (
6.5
). Note that
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
satisfy the assumptions of Lemma
3.30
, so we may apply each of the aforementioned inequalities. By (
6.5
), (
6.7
), and the fact that
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
only have the identity as a common ancestor, we have

(6.13)

\displaystyle\begin{split}||\gamma_{1}||_{\sigma_{\phi}}+||\gamma_{2}||_{\sigma_{\phi}}+\Big(2a+\frac{q}{AQ}\Big)&\geq\frac{1}{A}d_{\mathcal{T}}(\gamma_{1},\mathrm{id})-a+\frac{1}{A}d_{\mathcal{T}}(\mathrm{id},\gamma_{2})-a+2a+\frac{q}{AQ}\\
&=\frac{1}{A}d_{\mathcal{T}}(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})+\frac{q}{AQ}\\
&\geq\frac{1}{A}\bigg(\frac{1}{Q}d_{X}(\gamma_{1}o,\gamma_{2}o)-\frac{q}{Q}\bigg)+\frac{q}{AQ}\\
&=\frac{1}{AQ}||\kappa(\gamma_{1}^{-1}\gamma_{2})||.\end{split}

Fix a constant
H>D>1
. We claim that there exists a constant
B\geq 1
so that if
d_{\mathcal{T}}(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})\geq B
, then either

(6.14)

\displaystyle||\gamma_{1}||_{\sigma_{\phi}}\geq\frac{2a+\frac{q}{AQ}+M+C+HPp}{\frac{1}{D}-\frac{1}{H}},

or

(6.15)

\displaystyle||\gamma_{2}||_{\sigma_{\phi}}\geq\frac{2a+\frac{q}{AQ}+M+C+HPp}{1-\frac{1}{H}}.

Indeed, if not, then for every integer
n\geq 1
, there exist elements
\alpha_{n}
and
\beta_{n}
of
\mathcal{T}
having the identity as their only common ancestor, satisfying
d_{\mathcal{T}}(\alpha_{n},\beta_{n})\geq n
, but such that the sequence
\{||\alpha_{n}||_{\sigma_{\phi}}\}
is bounded above by the right-hand side of (
6.14
), while the sequence
\{||\beta_{n}||_{\sigma_{\phi}}\}
is bounded above by the right-hand side of (
6.15
). Since
d_{\mathcal{T}}(\alpha_{n},\beta_{n})\rightarrow\infty
as
n\rightarrow\infty
, there either exists a subsequence of
\{\alpha_{n}\}
or a subsequence of
\{\beta_{n}\}
which is escaping. But any such subsequence must have
\sigma_{\phi}
-magnitudes tending to infinity, which is a contradiction. We conclude that there must exist some
B\geq 1
so that
d_{\mathcal{T}}(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})\geq B
implies that either (
6.14
) or (
6.15
) holds.

Now if
d_{\mathcal{T}}(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})\geq B
, then combining (
6.9
), (
6.13
), (
6.14
), and (
6.15
), a direct computation shows that

\displaystyle||\gamma_{1}^{-1}\gamma_{2}||_{\sigma_{\phi}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{D}||\gamma_{1}||_{\sigma_{\phi}}+||\gamma_{2}||_{\sigma_{\phi}}-(M+C)

\displaystyle>\frac{1}{H}\bigg(||\gamma_{1}||_{\sigma_{\phi}}+||\gamma_{2}||_{\sigma_{\phi}}+2a+\frac{q}{AQ}+HPp\bigg)

\displaystyle>\frac{1}{AHQ}||\kappa(\gamma_{1}^{-1}\gamma_{2})||+Pp.

Thus (
6.12
) holds with
B
as above and
b=\frac{1}{AHQP}
, concluding the proof of the lemma.
∎

We now construct a
\theta
-admissible cone
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathfrak{a}^{+}
so that the quasi-isometric embedding
f:\mathcal{T}\rightarrow X
is
\mathcal{C}
-regular. Let
B,b>0
be the constants of Lemma
6.10
. Define

\displaystyle U_{0}:=\{\kappa(\gamma_{1}^{-1}\gamma_{2}):\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\in\mathcal{T},d_{\mathcal{T}}(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})\geq B\}\subset\mathfrak{a}^{+}.

Let
U\subset\mathfrak{a}_{\theta}^{+}
denote the convex hull of
p_{\theta}(U_{0})
and consider the asymptotic cone of
U
, that is

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{0}:=\overline{\bigcup_{u\in U}\mathbb{R}^{+}u}.

Since
U_{0}
is invariant under the opposition involution, so is
\mathcal{C}_{0}
. We claim that
\mathcal{C}_{0}-\{0\}\subset\mathrm{Int}(\mathfrak{a}_{\theta}^{+})
. Let
u\in U
. Then
u
is of the form
u=\sum_{i=1}^{k}t_{i}u_{i}
where
t_{i}\geq 0
for all
i
,
\sum_{i=1}^{k}t_{i}=1
, and
u_{i}\in p_{\theta}(U_{0})
for all
i
. By Lemma
6.10
, for any
\alpha\in\theta
we have

(6.16)

\displaystyle\alpha(u)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}t_{i}\alpha(u_{i})\geq b\sum_{i=1}^{k}t_{i}||u_{i}||\geq b\Big|\Big|\sum_{i=1}^{k}t_{i}u_{i}\Big|\Big|=b||u||.

Now let
x\in\mathcal{C}_{0}\smallsetminus\{0\}
be arbitrary. Then there exist sequences
\{\lambda_{n}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{+}
and
\{u_{n}\}\subset U
so that
