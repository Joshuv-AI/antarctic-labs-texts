Gender bias in fertility choices: how son preference increases the risk of short birth intervals in India
Authors: Soumen Barik, Dewaram A. Nagdeve, Priyanka Patel, Mayank Singh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundShort birth intervals are a persistent public health issue that elevate maternal and child health risks in India, where high patriarchy and son preference influence reproductive health outcomes among women. While prior research has not isolated or quantified the contribution of son preference to short birth interval (SBI). This study explores the impact of son preference on SBI in India.Data and methodsUsing nationally representative data from India’s National Family Health Survey (NFHS-5, 2019–21), we analyzed 117,135 married women aged 15–49 with at least two consecutive live births. We employed logistic regression models to estimate adjusted odds ratios (AORs) for SBI. Furthermore, the Fairlie decomposition technique was also applied to quantify the contribution of observed individual, household, and community-level characteristics in the differential length of birth interval among son preference and non-son preference women.ResultsWomen who had son preference had 16% higher odds of SBI (AOR = 1.16; 95% CI 1.12–1.20). The Fairlie decomposition revealed that 78.4% of this disparity was explained by measurable factors. Household wealth was the dominant contributor (64.7%), followed by fertility desire (30.4%), maternal BMI (16.3%), and household size (15.9%). Notably, maternal education and regional context acting as equalizing forces, reducing the explained gap by 18.8% and 24.8%, functioning as protective suppressors, however, 21.6% of the disparity remained unexplained, suggesting unmeasured patriarchal norms and reproductive coercion mechanisms.ConclusionSon preference’s influence on SBI in India is not rooted solely in cultural tradition but is statistically explained by observed characteristics. Policies that enhance women’s education, economic security, and access to family planning, especially in high-patriarchy regions, can disrupt the association between son preference and SBI. Integrating gender-transformative approaches into India’s reproductive health programs is essential to advance both maternal health (SDG 3) and gender equality (SDG 5).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s41182-026-00986-8.

Background

Birth intervals, which refer to the time between two consecutive births, play a crucial role in the health of both mothers and children [20, 48]. Optimal spacing between births enables women to recover from previous pregnancies, whereas both excessively short and excessively long intervals are associated with increased risks of adverse health outcomes for mothers, newborns, and young children [21, 29, 38, 39]. The World Health Organization recommends at least 24 months between a live birth and the next conception, which corresponds to approximately 33 months between two consecutive live births, assuming a gestation period of about nine months for a healthy pregnancy and birth [4, 19].

Studies have shown that SBI are associated with an increased risk of adverse maternal outcomes [21] including anaemia [1, 29], obstetric haemorrhage [54], hypertensive disorders of pregnancy [31], preeclampsia [68], uterine rupture among women with previous caesarean deliveries, and even maternal mortality [38, 39]. Short intervals also limit the time available for women to recover physically and emotionally between births [1], which may adversely affect maternal well-being and contribute to mental health challenges [20]. In the child health context, these factors are associated with preterm birth [69], low birth weight (LBW) [34], undernutrition [34], congenital anomalies [14] and child mortality [62]. Together, these outcomes exert broader pressures on maternal and child healthcare services, placing additional strain on health systems, education, and social support structures. They may also perpetuate cycles of poverty and inequality, particularly in settings with limited medical resources [66] and reducing the opportunities for economic growth and stability [36].

Strong son preference has traditionally been linked to shorter birth intervals and poorer health outcomes for daughters in households without sons [41]. In societies with a strong preference for sons, the sex of the previous child often influences the length of the next birth interval, as families tend to have shorter intervals after the birth of a girl compared to after the birth of a boy [18, 60]. In many Indian families, the desire to have at least one male child leads couples to continue childbearing with minimal spacing between births until a son is born [58]. This reproductive behaviour where families cease childbearing only after achieving the desired number of sons often results in short birth intervals, thereby compromising maternal and child health in the process [6, 7, 13]. India has long been characterized by patriarchal norms [12, 50, 63]. These norms take various forms, including son preference, restrictive gender roles, limited women's autonomy, and male-dominated kinship systems [23, 35, 43]. Son preference, a particularly pervasive expression of patriarchy, contributes to not only shorter birth intervals but also broader gender disparities in child health and survival [2, 56]. In families with no male children, daughters are often disadvantaged in access to nutrition, healthcare, and parental attention, highlighting the far-reaching impact of son preference on both fertility behaviour and gender equity [41, 72].

Numerous studies have examined SBI including its determinants [16, 61], the role of sex-selective abortion [58], son preference and sex composition [18] spatial hotspot and coldspot patterns [38, 39], and its association with child undernutrition [53], maternal health [29] and child health outcomes [38, 39]. Cross-national evidence confirms son preference accelerates fertility. A first-born daughter shortens subsequent intervals in Pakistan and among Chinese migrants [40, 70], while Turkish couples continue childbearing until a son arrives [5], and Kyrgyz women with only daughters face higher odds of parity progression [45]. Despite the extensive research on SBI in India, the specific contribution of son preference to SBI remains unexplored. Most of the prior studies identify the determinants of SBI in aggregate but do not isolate the effect of son preference or quantify how differences between women with and without son preference translate into SBI disparities. To the best of our knowledge, this study is among the first in India to both (i) examine the association between son preference and SBI using nationally representative NFHS data and (ii) apply a decomposition framework to partition the SBI gap into “explained” components (differences in characteristics) and “unexplained” components (differences in associations/coefficients). This study advances SDG 3 (Good Health and Well-being) and SDG 5 (Gender Equality) by identifying social drivers of SBI critical for maternal and child health. Findings inform India's reproductive policies via key levers: postpartum family planning integration, gender-transformative counseling, and rights-based services prioritizing autonomy. These disparities underscore the need to shift from supply-side interventions to address patriarchal norms limiting women's autonomy and perpetuating rapid childbearing. This evidence supports targeted interventions to disrupt the link between son preference and unhealthy birth spacing.

Data and methods

Data source

We analysed nationally representative data from the most recent round of the National Family Health Survey (NFHS-5, 2019–21), conducted by the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare, Government of India, and coordinated by the International Institute for Population Sciences (IIPS). NFHS-5 is a large-scale, multi-round household survey that provides reliable national- and state-level estimates on fertility, family planning, maternal and child health, and socioeconomic conditions in India. The survey collected detailed information on household characteristics and wealth; fertility, birth histories, and family planning; maternal and child health (including antenatal, delivery, and postnatal care, immunization, morbidity, and mortality); nutrition and anthropometry; anaemia and selected biomarkers; women’s empowerment and decision-making; domestic violence; and water, sanitation, and hygiene. The NFHS employs a stratified two-stage cluster sampling design to ensure representativeness at both national and state levels [37]. In the first stage, primary sampling units (PSUs) are selected using probability proportional to size, followed by systematic selection of households in the second stage. Sampling weights are provided to account for the complex survey design and non-response [37].

Sample selection procedure

The analytic sample presented in Fig. 1 was derived from the NFHS-5 (2019–21) women’s file (IR dataset), which included 724,155 women. We first restricted the sample to ever-married women (n = 542,830), excluding 181,285 never-married women. Among ever-married women, we further limited the sample to mothers with at least two children (n = 394,157), excluding 148,673 women with no or only one child. To capture recent birth intervals, we restricted the sample to mothers with at least one birth in the five years preceding the survey (n = 117,135), excluding 277,022 women with no recent births. The final analytic sample comprised 117,135 women, of whom 24,981 (20.33%) reported son preference and 92,154 (79.67%) did not. Multiple births and records with missing or inconsistent information on key variables were excluded. Variable description

Outcome variable

The primary outcome of this study was the birth interval (BI) between two consecutive live births. Following WHO and DHS guidelines, birth intervals were categorized into short and optimal intervals. A short birth interval (SBI) was defined as less than 33 months between two consecutive live births, while intervals of 33 months or more were considered optimal birth intervals (OBI). For analysis, SBI was coded as 1 if the preceding birth interval was less than 33 months and 0 otherwise.

Exposure variables

The primary exposure variable in this study was son preference, derived from respondents reported ideal number of sons and daughters in the NFHS among currently married women aged 15–49 years. A respondent was classified as having son preference if the ideal number of sons exceeded the ideal number of daughters, consistent with previous literature [7, 15]. A binary variable was constructed, where 1 indicated son preference (ideal number of boys > ideal number of girls) and 0 indicated no son preference (ideal number of boys ≤ ideal number of girls).

The selection of explanatory (or exposure) variables was guided by prior theoretical frameworks and empirical evidence from studies on son preference, fertility behaviour, and birth spacing in low- and middle-income countries, particularly India. Variables were grouped into three domains: women related factors like age (15–24; 25–34; ≥ 35 years), education (no education; primary; secondary; higher), age at marriage (< 18; 18–24; ≥ 25 years), age at first birth (< 20; 20–29; 30–49 years), BMI (normal; underweight; overweight/obese), exposure to family-planning messages (not exposed; exposed), number of living daughters (0; 1; 2; 3 +), fertility desire (wanted; undecided/no more/other), and current contraceptive use (no use; modern method; traditional method); household factors includes wealth index (poorest; poorer; middle; richer; richest), household size (1–4; 5–8; > 8 members), sex of household head (male; female), social group/caste (SC/ST: Scheduled Castes / Scheduled Tribes; OBC: Other Backward Classes; other), and religion (Hindu; Muslim; other); and Community factors are place of residence (urban; rural), region (East; West; North; South; Central; Northeast), mass media exposure (none; any), and perceived distance to a health facility (no problem; problem). These variables have been consistently identified in previous literature as key determinants of reproductive behaviour and short birth intervals. All selected variables were included in the multivariable models to account for potential confounding and to provide a comprehensive assessment of the association between son preference and short birth interval.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive analyses summarized socio-demographic characteristics and the prevalence of short birth intervals by son preference across India using frequencies, proportions, and means with standard deviations. Bivariate analyses assessed unadjusted associations between son preference and short birth intervals, as well as with covariates grouped into women’s, household, and community factors. To ensure the robustness of the regression estimates, we assessed multicollinearity among the independent variables using Variance Inflation Factor (VIF), and all VIF values were below 5, indicating no evidence of multicollinearity. We applied survey‐weighted logistic regression models with short birth interval (< 33 months) as the outcome and son preference as the primary exposure. A model-based analytical approach was used, in which four sequential models were fitted; the details of each model are described below.

Model 1 (crude): Son preference was included as the primary exposure variable to assess its unadjusted association with short birth interval. Model 2 (Adjusted for Individual Characteristics): The model was adjusted for son preference and women’s characteristics, including age, educational attainment, age at marriage, age at first birth, body mass index (BMI), exposure to family planning messages, number of living daughters, fertility desire, and current contraceptive use. Model 3 (Individual + Household-adjusted): In addition to the variables included in Model 2, household-level characteristics were incorporated, including wealth index, household size, sex of the household head, mass media exposure, and perceived distance to the nearest health facility. Model 4 (Fully adjusted): The final model further adjusted for community-level characteristics, including caste, religion, place of residence, and region.

To assess how much of the difference in the predicted probability of short birth interval (SBI) between women with and without son preference is attributable to observed characteristics versus differential effects, we applied the Fairlie decomposition for binary outcomes [27, 28]. Groups were defined by son preference: women with son preference (G = 1) and without son preference (G = 0).

Discussion

This study investigates the association between son preference and SBI in India, using data from the National Family Health Survey (NFHS-5). Results from multivariable logistic regression show that women reporting son preference had 16% higher odds of SBI (AOR = 1.16). Fairlie decomposition indicates that 78.4% of the observed gap in predicted SBI prevalence is accounted for by socioeconomic, demographic, and reproductive factors, particularly household wealth, fertility intentions, maternal BMI, and regional variation. These findings suggest that while son preference is a persistent cultural factor, the observed differences in birth spacing largely reflect structural and behavioral characteristics that may be addressed through targeted policies and programs.

Our findings that son preference was associated with higher odds of SBI are consistent with a well-established body of evidence showing how gender-biased fertility norms shape reproductive timing in patriarchal contexts. As noted by [2, 16, 40, 51, 57, 58], the cultural imperative to produce a son often drives the open-ended pregnancies, where women have minimal spacing between births until a male child is born. This pattern is especially pronounced in India, where sons are valued for economic security, lineage continuity, and support in old age [41, 58]. These observed patterns are associated with shorter interbirth intervals, which have been linked in prior research to maternal depletion, preterm delivery, and child mortality [21, 38, 39].

Our results show that women with three or more daughters had 69% higher odds of short birth intervals (AOR = 1.69), reflecting the intensified pressure to continue childbearing in the absence of the ideal number of sons. This pattern likely reflects the perception of sons as economic assets and future providers, which may influence women’s fertility behaviour, birth spacing, and contraceptive use [18, 40, 51]. In patriarchal systems, where men dominate decision-making and control power across social, household, and economic spheres, women’s reproductive autonomy is often undermined, particularly in joint families where there is pressure for rapid repeat births after the birth of a daughter [22, 67, 71]. Evidence from Pakistan shows women with daughters have shorter subsequent birth intervals, while those with sons were more likely to use contraception [40, 42]. Addressing son preference requires promoting gender equality, girls’ education, enforcing anti-discrimination laws, eliminating dowry, and strengthening social security so sons are no longer viewed as essential for economic or old-age security [51].

Maternal age proved to be a strong protective factor against SBI. Women aged 25–34 and ≥ 35 years had 77% and 93% lower odds of SBI, respectively, compared with those aged 15–24. This trend likely reflects the natural decline in fertility with age, as a reduced ovarian reserve, poorer egg quality, and menstrual irregularities together decrease the likelihood of conception [11, 17]. In India, the average age of menopause is approximately 46 years, and by the early 40 s, most women are less likely to have a healthy pregnancy [3]. Additionally, older women tend to have greater autonomy in reproductive decision-making and are more likely to have reached their desired family size and sex composition, leading to longer birth intervals [4, 75].

Women from larger households were more likely to have SBI, rising by 36% for 5–8 members and 72% for over 8 members. Large, often joint or extended families mainly based on an agrarian economy often limit women’s reproductive autonomy, as elders, especially mothers-in-law, strongly influence pregnancy and contraception [24, 52]. Patriarchal norms, lack of privacy, and pressure for quick births, especially after a daughter, reduce contraceptive use [40, 59]. Economic reliance on children for labour and old-age support, coupled with low maternal education, further increases short birth intervals [51, 58].

Maternal education emerged as a protective factor, reducing the odds of SBI by 15% (AOR = 0.85). Schooling enhances women’s awareness of reproductive health, improves access to family planning, and strengthens agency in fertility choices [9]. Decomposition results showed that education contributed negatively (–18.8%) to the SBI gap associated with son preference, suggesting that schooling helps mitigate gender-biased fertility behaviour [8] Evidence from Pakistan, shows that women with secondary and higher education had 25% and 31% lower risks of SBI, respectively, compared with women with no education [51]. Studies from Indonesia indicate that educated women are more likely to use family planning services and long-acting contraceptives effectively [46]. Additionally, they tend to marry later, naturally limiting their reproductive years and number of children.

Wealth status strongly influences birth spacing: women from the richest households have 49% lower odds of SBI than the poorest (AOR = 0.51). Greater financial resources enable access to contraception, postnatal care, and counselling on optimal birth spacing key supports for reproductive autonomy. This aligns with Chowdhury et al., [16], who found richer rural Indian women significantly less likely to experience SBI (OR = 0.84 and 0.60 for richer and richest groups, respectively). In contrast, poorer mothers, who are often less educated, unemployed, and financially constrained, face challenges in accessing healthcare for themselves and their children. Their limited income is largely spent on basic necessities, leaving little for medical expenses [64, 73]. Crucially, our decomposition analysis reveals that household wealth accounts for 64.7% of the explained son preference–SBI gap. Economic security appears to buffer against son preference likely by reducing dependence on sons for old-age support thereby weakening gender-biased fertility behaviour. Thus, wealth not only improves access to services but also mitigates cultural drivers of rapid childbearing [51]. Religion emerged as a significant factor associated with SBI, with Muslim women and those from other religious groups showing a slightly higher likelihood of experiencing SBI [32, 44, 74]. This pattern is consistent with prior studies and may be linked to comparatively lower use of contraceptives and modern birth control methods among these groups [30].

SBI in India exhibit stark regional disparities, closely tied to the country’s patriarchal geography. Women in the North, Central, and South face significantly higher odds of SBI, while those in the Northeast experience 37% lower odds. High-patriarchy states like Haryana, Rajasthan, and Uttar Pradesh enforce rigid gender norms and strong son preference, pressuring women into rapid successive pregnancies, especially after a daughter’s birth to secure a male heir [12], [63]. In agrarian regions, male-dominated land inheritance further entrenches this dynamic, as sons are seen as essential for property continuity [49]. Moreover, these same regions exhibit lower contraceptive prevalence compared to the national average of (67%), based on NFHS-5 data, such as Uttar Pradesh (62%), Gujarat (65%), and Bihar (56%) [37]. Limited contraceptive use contributes to higher fertility and shorter spacing between births [55]. In contrast, the Northeast especially Meghalaya operates under matrilineal traditions where lineage and inheritance flow through women, granting them greater reproductive autonomy leading to healthier birth spacing [10]. Similarly, southern states like Kerala, with historically matrilineal roots, high female literacy, and progressive gender norms, show lower patriarchy levels and longer interbirth intervals. Thus, SBI patterns reflect deeper sociocultural structures: where patriarchy is strong, reproductive autonomy is constrained, where gender norms are more equitable, women are better able to space births safely [12, 26].

Decomposition results clarify the observed contributions to the gap in predicted SBI prevalence between women with and without son preference. Desire for more children accounted for 30.4% of the explained gap, as women who wish to have more children tend to conceive again sooner, reducing the spacing between births. Similar findings have been reported by [16, 33], and Singh et al., [64], who noted that a strong desire for more children is associated with earlier subsequent pregnancies. This highlights the importance of addressing fertility intentions through family planning programs to promote healthy birth spacing [58]. Similarly, maternal BMI (16.3%) and household size (15.9%) also contributed substantially to the explained gap, reflecting biological and resource constraints that increase SBI risk among disadvantaged women. Underweight status likely reflects underlying socioeconomic disadvantage, including limited access to contraception and suboptimal maternal care, which may contribute to shorter birth intervals [47, 75], while larger households intensify competition for resources, accelerating completion of childbearing.

Notably, geographical region (–24.8%) and maternal education (–18.8%) reduced the explained gap, indicating their role as equalizing forces. Education empowers women with reproductive knowledge, contraceptive agency, and resistance to familial pressure for rapid childbearing [51]. The remaining 21.6% of the gap is unexplained by measured covariates likely reflecting unmeasured patriarchal norms and intra-household power structures that influence women’s fertility choices, particularly by husbands and mothers-in-law. These may include reproductive coercion, a form of control in which an individual interferes with a woman’s decisions regarding contraception, pregnancy, or abortion, such as pregnancy pressure and contraceptive sabotage. However, NFHS-5 does not directly measure pregnancy coercion, contraceptive sabotage, or male partner interference with contraceptive use. Therefore, these unobserved factors may partly explain the residual disparity [22, 67, 71]. These findings collectively indicate that structural interventions such as poverty alleviation, girls’ education, and regionally tailored family planning programs can effectively reduce the association between son preference and SBI, consistent with global evidence that short birth intervals are not inevitable but responsive to policy [25, 38, 39].

Conclusion

While son preference remains a deeply entrenched cultural norm in many parts of India, this study shows that observed differences in SBI were largely accounted for by socioeconomic and reproductive characteristics, rather than immutable cultural factors. Beyond the protective roles of women’s education and household wealth, reproductive policies can support postpartum family planning, gender-transformative counseling, and rights-based services prioritizing women’s autonomy. Addressing structural vulnerabilities such as poverty, limited education, and geographic barriers to care is critical to reduce inequities in birth spacing and ensure non-discriminatory, rights-based implementation. Key contributors to SBI include fertility desire, age at first marriage and birth, number of living daughters (particularly three or more), maternal underweight status, household size, and regional context, especially in Central, North, and Southern regions where son preference and SBI prevalence are higher. Causal pathways cannot be established due to the cross-sectional design.

Under India’s reproductive health initiatives, such as Mission Parivaar Vikas [16], the role of Accredited Social Health Activists (ASHAs) can be strategically expanded to address these drivers at the community level. ASHAs can deliver gender-sensitive counselling that explicitly acknowledges reproductive coercion, and son preference as a risk factor for rapid childbearing [65]. By identifying women with multiple daughters, delayed first birth, uneducated and underweight mothers, belonging to socially and economically disadvantaged groups, linking them to nutrition support, contraception, and gender-equity messaging ASHAs can help decouple son preference from unhealthy fertility behavior. Integrating gender-transformative content into ASHA training and incentivizing quality counselling (not just service delivery) will strengthen reproductive autonomy and promote optimal birth spacing. Such targeted, community-based strategies hold significant promise for advancing both maternal health (SDG 3) and gender equality (SDG 5) in India.

Strengths and limitations

A key strength of this study lies in its use of nationally representative, high-quality data from NFHS-5, which enables robust inference on the association between son preference and SBI across India’s diverse sociocultural landscape. By applying the Fairlie decomposition technique a method rarely used in reproductive health research in India we move beyond simple association to quantify how much of the SBI gap between women with and without son preference is accounted for by observable characteristics versus unmeasured structural or attitudinal factors. This analysis shows that nearly 78% of the disparity is explained by socioeconomic and reproductive variables, highlighting which factors contribute most to the observed differences. Furthermore, the study explicitly considers intersectional vulnerabilities such as women with multiple daughters, delayed first birth, uneducated and underweight mothers, and those from disadvantaged groups illustrating how gender bias compounds with social marginalization to influence SBI risk.

However, several limitations warrant caution. First, son preference is proxied indirectly through the actual and desired sex composition of mothers of the survey. This crude measure cannot capture the intensity, internalization, or household-level negotiation of son preference. Second, while NFHS-5 provides rich covariate data, it lacks information on critical qualitative dimensions such as spousal or in-law pressure, exposure to gender-equity messaging, or women’s agency in contraceptive decision-making that likely mediate the link between son preference and rapid childbearing. Third, given the cross-sectional design, these associations cannot be interpreted as causal. Potential sources of bias include unmeasured confounding factors such as reproductive coercion, intra-household power dynamics, and cultural practices not captured in NFHS-5. In addition, the restriction of the analytic sample to women aged ≥ 35 years with a recent birth may introduce selection bias and limit the generalizability of findings to younger reproductive-age women. Therefore, biological interpretations, such as age-related fertility decline, should be interpreted with caution given the potential influence of sample selection. Fourth, regarding interaction effects, we acknowledge their potential importance; however, the current analysis focuses on estimating main effects to ensure model parsimony and interpretability. Furthermore, a detailed examination of trends in sex composition and fertility intentions over time was beyond the scope of this study. Finally, recall bias in birth histories especially among older women or those with high parity may affect interval accuracy. Despite these constraints, the study offers a rare, empirically grounded map of actionable levers that, if targeted through gender-responsive health programming, could disrupt the cycle of son preference–driven reproductive risk in India.
