Towards Distillation-Resistant Large Language Models: An Information-Theoretic Perspective
Hao Fang ∗, Tianyi Zhang ∗, Tianqu Zhuang, Jiawei Kong, Kuofeng Gao, Bin Chen, Leqi Liang, Shu-Tao Xia, Ke Xu

Abstract
Proprietary large language models (LLMs) embody substantial economic value and are generally exposed only as black-box APIs, yet adversaries can still exploit their outputs to extract knowledge via distillation. Existing defenses focus exclusively on text-based distillation, leaving the important logit-based distillation largely unexplored. In this work, we analyze this problem and present an effective solution from an information-theoretic perspective. We characterize distillation-relevant information in teacher outputs using the conditional mutual information (CMI) between teacher logits and input queries conditioned on ground-truth labels. This quantity captures contextual information beneficial for model extraction, motivating us to defend distillation via CMI minimization. Guided by our theoretical analysis, we propose learning a transformation matrix that purifies the original outputs to enhance distillation resistance. We further derive a CMI-inspired anti-distillation objective to optimize this transformation, which effectively removes distillation-relevant information while preserving output utility. Extensive experiments across multiple LLMs and strong distillation algorithms demonstrate that the proposed method significantly degrades distillation performance while preserving task accuracy, effectively protecting models’ intellectual property.

Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) have recently demonstrated remarkable capabilities and have been deployed in a variety of practical applications, such as multi-agent systems
(
22
;
3
)
. Training such models demands substantial resources, including large-scale datasets, extensive computational budgets, and significant human effort, rendering them highly valuable intellectual property.

Figure 1
:
An illustration of the designed defense algorithm. We propose to learn a transformation matrix that post-processes output logits. By minimizing the conditional mutual information
\mathcal{I}(X;Z|Y)
, we successfully reduce the information beneficial for knowledge distillation within the teacher logits.

To safeguard their interests, commercial service providers typically isolate their proprietary models and only expose them through APIs. However, malicious attackers can exploit the model output texts or logits to perform knowledge distillation (KD)
(
8
;
10
;
5
)
, thus cheaply extracting model capabilities without any access to the weights or architecture.

To defend against such theft, early work has proposed LLM watermarking
(
11
;
27
;
14
)
as a mitigation strategy, yet they offer only post-hoc verification and cannot directly prevent the extraction of proprietary knowledge. More recent efforts attempt to perturb the token distribution and modify model outputs to hinder KD
(
16
;
13
)
. However, these methods focus exclusively on text-based distillation, leaving the more widely discussed logit-based distillation largely unaddressed.
Notably, a model’s output logits reveal the full probability distribution over tokens, conveying substantially richer information than the sampled token labels. Consequently, defending against such distillation is particularly challenging. This difficulty is further exacerbated by a growing body of work
(
20
;
5
;
21
)
that strengthens logit-based KD by more fully exploiting the rich information in these distributions. To address this challenge, we introduce an information-theoretic measure to quantify the distillation-relevant information in teacher logits and propose a transformation strategy to mitigate knowledge leakage.

Fundamentally, LLMs perform a classification task to predict the next token for an ongoing sequence. The information in an output vector can be interpreted in two aspects: (1) the predicted label for the current input (
i.e.
, the token corresponding to the maximum logit), and (2) additional contextual information beyond the label
(
26
)
.
Although different prefixes can yield the same next predicted token, their logits differ substantially due to varying contexts in semantics and syntax. Particularly, logit-based KD exploits such contextual information to transfer richer knowledge to the student model
(
5
)
.

To quantify the supervisory guidance from teacher outputs during distillation, we introduce conditional mutual information (CMI) as a principled measure of the conveyed information.
Formally, let
X,Y,Z
denote the input sequence, the ground-truth label, and the teacher logits for the given
X
, respectively.
Then
\mathcal{I}(X;Z|Y)
measures how much information
Z
provides about
X
conditioned on
Y
. This precisely aligns with the paradigm of logit-based KD that exploits information encoded in teacher logits beyond the label to enhance student performance. Therefore, a natural defense is to purify the teacher logits to minimize the conditional mutual dependence between
X
and
Z
.
To achieve this goal, we propose learning a transition matrix
\mathcal{M}
that post-processes the output logits (see Figure
1
), which we theoretically validate to reduce
\mathcal{I}(X;Z|Y)
. To effectively learn this matrix and decrease CMI, we further expand
\mathcal{I}(X;Z|Y)
and decompose the minimization into two sub-problems based on the Information Bottleneck (IB) principle
(
17
)
: (1) increasing the dependency between the logits
Z
and the ground-truth label
Y
, and (2) weakening the correlation between the input
X
and the logits
Z
.
Guided by this analysis, we devise an optimization objective with two corresponding loss terms.
We first derive a theoretical upper bound for the first subproblem, which formally justifies that minimizing the cross-entropy between
Z
and
Y
is a practical surrogate objective.
The latter sub-task aims to reduce the relevance of
X
and
Z
, yet directly computing or estimating this term for LLMs is intractable. To circumvent this difficulty, we note that under the KD scenario, the information in
Z
about
X
is ultimately leveraged through gradients propagated to the student during optimization. In this sense, these gradients serve as the practical carrier of the guidance provided by teacher logits.
Building on this insight, we employ a surrogate student model to simulate distillation and maximize the angular deviation between the gradients induced by the transformed logits
Z^{\prime}
and those from the original
Z
.
By iteratively optimizing the transformation
\mathcal{M}
with the proposed loss function, we effectively reduce the distillation-beneficial contextual information in teacher outputs, thereby strengthening their resistance against knowledge extraction attacks.

In summary, our contributions are as follows:

•

To the best of our knowledge, we are the first to defend LLMs against logit-based KD. We analyze the problem from an information-theoretic perspective and propose learning a matrix that post-processes output logits to minimize their CMI with the input.

•

Guided by theoretical analysis, we design two complementary loss functions to optimize the transformation matrix, which effectively removes the contextual information within the logits that could be exploited by attackers while preserving output utility.

•

Extensive experiments across various LLMs on three widely used datasets against four strong distillation algorithms demonstrate the outstanding defense performance of the proposed method.

Related Work

2.1
Knowledge Distillation for Large Language Models

Knowledge Distillation (KD) is a widely adopted paradigm for transferring knowledge from high-capacity teacher models to smaller and less capable student models. For large language models (LLMs), KD is usually realized through two paradigms: sequence-level (text-based) distillation and logit-based (response-level) distillation.

Sequence-level knowledge distillation trains the student model using texts generated by the teacher as hard-label supervision, treating the teacher-produced outputs as pseudo-labels and optimizing the student via standard maximum-likelihood estimation
(
10
)
.
In contrast, besides the SFT loss based on ground-truth labels, logit-based KD additionally uses the teacher’s soft output distributions to distill fine-grained predictive information by aligning the teacher and student distributions. The pioneering logit-based KD method
(
8
)
uses the Kullback-Leibler divergence (KLD) to measure the discrepancy between the student and the teacher distributions. Reverse KLD (RKLD) employs a reverse version of KL divergence by changing the reference distribution in KL from the teacher to the student, allowing the student to concentrate its limited capacity on the teacher’s high-probability regions
(
5
)
. AlphaNet
(
20
)
introduces
\alpha
-divergence, providing a tunable measure between KLD and RKLD to penalize over-estimation and under-estimation in KD. ABKD
(
21
)
further proposes a novel
\alpha\text{-}\beta
-divergence to balance hardness-concentration and confidence-concentration, generalizing FKLD and RKLD to a broader family of divergences.

2.2
Protecting LLMs from Knowledge Distillation

The great success of KD poses a significant threat to the protection of intellectual property in LLMs. To mitigate this issue, watermarking
(
11
;
14
)
seeks to embed an identifiable and verifiable signature into a model’s behavior or parameters, enabling the model owner to verify their ownership. Meanwhile, Fingerprinting
(
27
;
24
)
assigns a unique and traceable identity to each distributed model instance, enabling the model owner to attribute leaked or misused models to their source. Despite their usefulness, both approaches are inherently post hoc, indicating that they facilitate the identification of model stealing after it has occurred, but cannot prevent the extraction process itself.

Another research line attempts to directly solve this threat by modifying the token distribution during decoding to obtain generated texts that are resilient to distillation. The pioneering work
(
16
)
proposes an anti-distillation decoding strategy that actively poisons reasoning traces by injecting a learned perturbation into the token-level probability distribution. Concretely, a proxy student first computes gradients on a small hold-out set of teacher-generated sequences, yielding a fixed poisoning direction
\nabla\ell
. During inference, the teacher performs two forward passes for each candidate token with proxy-student parameters perturbed by
\pm\eta\nabla\ell
, and estimates the resulting finite-difference logit shift. This shift is then integrated into the teacher’s logits to form an adjusted sampling distribution. Sampling from this modified distribution implicitly steers any downstream student trained on these traces toward higher loss, thereby degrading the effectiveness of distillation.
DOGe
(
13
)
adopts a more direct approach that maximizes the divergence between teacher and student output distributions to make the teacher’s predictions harder to imitate. Specifically, DOGe fine-tunes the teacher’s final linear layer via the negative KL-divergence between the temperature-scaled softmax of the teacher logits and the detached softmax of a proxy student, enlarging the teacher–student discrepancy.

However, existing methods limit their scope in the sequence-level distillation scenarios, leaving the defense against the more widely discussed logit-based KD algorithms largely unexplored. To address this gap, this work makes an early step in investigating this issue from an information-theoretic perspective and proposes learning a transformation matrix as an effective post-processing solution.

3
Preliminary

3.1
Notation

Let
\mathcal{V}
be the vocabulary and
|\mathcal{V}|<\infty
.
Let
X\in\mathcal{V}^{*}
be the input sequence consisting of the user’s query and the ongoing generated response, and
Y\in\mathcal{V}
be the ground-truth label,
i.e.
, the next token for the current input
X
.

Let
f:\mathcal{V}^{*}\to\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{V}|}
be the large language model, and
Z=f(X)
be the logit vector for current input
X
. The next token prediction is sampled from the distribution
\sigma(Z)
, where
\sigma(\cdot)
denotes the softmax function.

3.2
Knowledge Distillation

Given a dataset
\mathcal{D}
consisting of prompt-answer pairs
(q,o)
, KD algorithms repeatedly query a teacher model
f_{\text{T}}(\cdot)
with
(q,o)
sampled from
\mathcal{D}
and train a student model
f_{\text{S}}(\cdot)
by mimicking the teacher’s output. Formally, the knowledge distillation loss can be expressed as:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{KD}}\triangleq\frac{1}{l}\sum_{t=1}^{l}\text{Dist}\Big(\sigma\big(f_{\text{T}}(q,o_{<t})\big)\Big\|\sigma\big(f_{\text{S}}(q,o_{<t})\big)\Big),

(1)

where
l
denotes the length of the ground-truth answer
o
, and
\text{Dist}(\cdot\|\cdot)
denotes the distance metric such as Kullback–Leibler divergence (KLD).
In general, the objective of logit-based distillation algorithms can be formulated as:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{total}}=(1-\alpha)\cdot\mathcal{L}_{\text{SFT}}+\alpha\cdot\mathcal{L}_{\text{KD}},

(2)

where
\alpha
is a factor set to
0.5
by default, and
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SFT}}
denotes the supervised fine-tuning (SFT) loss,
i.e.
, the negative log-likelihood of the predictive probability of the ground-truth label. Since the logits convey more contextual information than the predictive label alone, KD typically outperforms standard SFT on many downstream tasks. Based on this formulation, various improved algorithms have been proposed, including novel designs of distance metrics such as reverse KLD
(
5
)
and AB-KLD
(
21
)
.

3.3
Information Bottleneck

The Information Bottleneck (IB) theory
(
18
;
19
;
17
)
highlights that a well-trained model should compress redundant information from inputs while preserving key information for tasks. The IB objective is formulated as:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{IB}}=\mathcal{I}(X;Z)-\beta\cdot\mathcal{I}(Z;Y),

(3)

where
\mathcal{I}(\cdot;\cdot)
is mutual information, and
\beta>0
is a hyperparameter. A lower
\mathcal{L}_{\text{IB}}
indicates a better performance and higher robustness of a model.
Note that
Z
in IB represents the intermediate representation during inference. In this work, we instantiate
Z
as the logit vector.

Figure 2
:
An illustration of the proposed defense framework. As shown in the left subfigure, we introduce a surrogate student model as an adversary and simulate the KD process. We compute the KD loss
\mathcal{L}_{\text{total}}
and extract its gradients
\bm{g}
with respect to the student parameters to quantify the guidance signal carried by the teacher outputs. The right subfigure explains the loss computation used for training the transformation matrix. To diminish context information in teacher logits, we design to minimize the KLD between the transformed logits
Z^{\prime}
and the ground-truth label
Y
, while simultaneously maximizing the angular deviation between the gradients
\bm{g}
and
\bm{g}^{\prime}
calculated by the original and transformed logits, respectively.

4
Method

In this section, we first illustrate the rationality and benefits of minimizing CMI as a defense objective and present a theoretically-grounded post-processing strategy via a logit transformation matrix. Guided by theoretical insights, we design an optimization objective consisting of two complementary loss terms to train the matrix. An overview of the proposed method is illustrated in Figure
2
.

4.1
Conditional Mutual Information-Based Defense

The CMI
\mathcal{I}(X;Z|Y)
measures how much information
Z
reveals about
X
given the ground-truth label
Y
. The additional contextual information that
Z
carries beyond the label aligns exactly with the goal of logit-based distillation, as shown in
Equation

1
. Therefore, we propose to defend teacher knowledge by minimizing
\mathcal{I}(X;Z|Y)
, preventing such contextual information from being extracted. This objective can also be interpreted through entropy and uncertainty. Specifically,
\mathcal{I}(X;Z|Y)=\mathcal{H}(X|Y)-\mathcal{H}(X|Z,Y)
, which quantifies the reduction in uncertainty of
X
when
Z
is additionally observed. For a fixed training dataset,
\mathcal{H}(X|Y)
remains constant. Hence, minimizing
\mathcal{I}(X;Z|Y)
is equivalent to maximizing
\mathcal{H}(X|Z,Y)
,
i.e.
, making
Z
contain as little information about
X
as possible to increase the uncertainty about
X
given the label
Y
.

Remark 1
.

As an intuitive extreme case, if
Z
ideally degenerates to the one-hot label
Y
,
i.e.
, it contains no contextual information beyond the category, then logit-based knowledge distillation collapses to standard label-only supervised fine-tuning. In this case, the CMI attains its minimum value
\mathcal{I}(X;Z|Y)=\mathcal{I}(X;Y|Y)=0
.

To reduce the contextual information in teacher logits, we propose a lightweight post-processing strategy that further modifies output logits while keeping the teacher completely frozen. Concretely, we introduce a learnable matrix
\mathcal{M}
to transform the original teacher output into a refined one.
Before delving into the full training pipeline, we show that such a transformation admits an intriguing information-theoretic property that formally guarantees that the refined logits become more context-compressed:

Theorem 2
.

Let
X\in\mathcal{V}^{*}
be the current input,
Y\in\mathcal{V}
be the ground-truth next token,
Z=f(X)\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{V}|}
be the teacher logits,
\mathcal{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{V}|\times|\mathcal{V}|}
be the logit transformation matrix, and
Z^{\prime}=\mathcal{M}\cdot Z
be the refined logits. Then

Y\to X\to Z\to Z^{\prime}

forms a Markov chain, and their CMI values satisfy

\mathcal{I}(X;Z|Y)\geq\mathcal{I}(X;Z^{\prime}|Y).

Detailed proofs can be found in Appendix
A
. Theorem
2
ensures that matrix transformations are universally beneficial for reducing CMI, which facilitates the compression of contextual information exploited by attackers. To further reduce CMI while preserving the utility of outputs, we additionally design an effective optimization objective to better guide the training of the transformation matrix.

4.2
Transformation Matrix Optimization

Next, we analyze the proposed matrix training objective through the lens of the Information Bottleneck (IB) principle to resolve the optimization problem. In particular, the following property reveals that minimizing CMI actually induces an IB objective.

Theorem 3
.

The conditional mutual information
\mathcal{I}(X;Z|Y)
is a special case of Information Bottleneck objective with
\beta=1
in
Equation

3
, i.e.,

\mathcal{I}(X;Z|Y)=\mathcal{I}(X;Z)-\mathcal{I}(Z;Y).

(4)

A detailed derivation is provided in Appendix
A
.
This property is particularly appealing as it reveals that minimizing
\mathcal{I}(X;Z|Y)
inherits the benefits of the Information Bottleneck, balancing compression of redundant information in
X
while preserving task-relevant information in
Y
. The above characterization naturally decomposes the objective into two complementary sub-tasks:

•

Minimizing
\mathcal{I}(X;Z)
.
This compresses redundant contextual information in the teacher logits
Z
that originates from the input
X
, weakening the dependency between inputs and outputs. As a consequence,
Z
becomes less informative about
X
, which enhances the resistance against KD algorithms.

•

Maximizing
\mathcal{I}(Z;Y)
.
This preserves task-relevant information and maintains the model’s utility.
Notably, it encourages
Z
to concentrate toward its class centroid (
i.e.
, increasing the correct label’s logit while suppressing others), which also eliminates contextual information and brings defense benefits.

Training Loss Design.
Motivated by the two sub-tasks, we design two complementary loss functions to achieve the CMI minimization.
We begin with analyzing the second term
\mathcal{I}(Z;Y)
, which can be expanded into

\mathcal{I}(Z;Y)=\mathcal{H}(Y)-\mathcal{H}(Y|Z).

(5)

Note that
\mathcal{H}(Y)
is constant, thus maximizing
\mathcal{I}(Z;Y)
is equivalent to minimizing the conditional entropy
\mathcal{H}(Y|Z)
, which measures the residual uncertainty about the ground-truth label
Y
after observing logits
Z
.
Although directly computing
\mathcal{H}(Y|Z)
is intractable, we can derive a consistent upper bound as follows.

Lemma 4
.

Let
Y\in\mathcal{V}
be the ground-truth next token and
Z\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{V}|}
be the teacher logits. Let
p(\cdot|Z)
be the distribution of
Y
conditional on
Z
, and
\hat{p}(\cdot|Z)=\sigma(Z)
be its predictive distribution by the teacher model. Then the cross-entropy
\mathcal{H}(p,\hat{p})
can be decomposed into

\mathcal{H}(p,\hat{p})=\mathcal{H}(Y|Z)+\mathbb{E}_{Z}\left[\mathbb{KL}\Big(p(\cdot|Z)\Big\|\hat{p}(\cdot|Z)\Big)\right],

(6)

which further implies the upper bound

\mathcal{H}(p,\hat{p})\geq\mathcal{H}(Y|Z).

(7)

Remark 5
.

In classification tasks with discrete labels,
p(\cdot|Z)
degenerates into a one-hot distribution, making
\mathcal{H}(p,\hat{p})
equivalent to the standard cross-entropy loss, denoted as
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CE}}
. Therefore, minimizing
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CE}}
provides a tractable surrogate for reducing the conditional entropy
\mathcal{H}(Y|Z)
. In practice, optimizing
\mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}}
increases the logit corresponding to the true label. This intuitively aligns with the fact that if
\text{argmax}(Z)=Y
always holds, the conditional entropy attains its minimum,
\mathcal{H}(Y|Z)=0
.

For maximizing
\mathcal{I}(X;Z)
, previous studies on image classification typically compute its value
(
26
)
or estimate it via variational upper bounds
(
1
)
.
However, directly estimating
\mathcal{I}(X;Z)
for autoregressive LLMs is fundamentally infeasible, since both
X
and
Z
are in high-dimensional spaces, and their underlying distributions are difficult to approximate.
To handle this challenge, we note that in KD scenarios, the information of
X
residing in
Z
is ultimately exploited through how
Z
instructs the student’s output, which in practice materializes in the form of gradients that update the student’s parameters during training. Hence, the learning signals conveyed by the gradients serve as the effective carrier of the information of
X
residing in
Z
, which motivates the following proposition:

Proposition 6
.

Let
\theta
denote the parameters of a surrogate student model
f_{\textnormal{S}}(\cdot)
.
Under the standard KD setting, the information in
Z
about
X
that can be exploited during distillation is operationally conveyed through the gradients of the loss with respect to
\theta
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\bm{g}

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\theta}\frac{1}{l}\sum_{t=1}^{l}\Big[-(1-\alpha)\cdot\log\sigma\big(f_{\textnormal{S}}(q,o_{<t})\big)_{o_{t}}

(8)

\displaystyle+\alpha\cdot\mathbb{KL}\Big(\sigma\big(f_{\textnormal{T}}(q,o_{<t})\big)\Big\|\sigma\big(f_{\textnormal{S}}(q,o_{<t})\big)\Big)\Big].

Based on the gradients
\bm{g}
, we can characterize the information within teacher logits
Z
that is transmitted to the student during distillation. The optimization objective is accordingly reformulated as obtaining a transformed distribution
Z^{\prime}
whose induced gradients
\bm{g}^{\prime}
contain as little valid information as possible.
Since the original
\bm{g}
represents the correct and informative update direction under KD, we aim to disrupt this signal by maximizing the angular deviation between
\bm{g}
and
\bm{g}^{\prime}
, measured via cosine similarity. This strategy implicitly suppresses distillation-relevant information in
Z
through gradient distortion, significantly enhancing resistance to logit-based distillation attacks.

With the above loss terms, the training objective for learning the transformation matrix
\mathcal{M}
is formulated as:

\displaystyle

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{M}}=\mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}}+\lambda\cdot\mathcal{L}_{\text{grad}},

(9)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{l}\sum_{t=1}^{l}\log\sigma\big(f_{\textnormal{T}}(q,o_{<t})\big)_{o_{t}}+\lambda\cdot\frac{\langle\bm{g},\bm{g}^{\prime}\rangle}{\|\bm{g}\|_{F}\|\bm{g}^{\prime}\|_{F}},

where
\lambda>0
is the scaling factor of
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{grad}}
,
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
is the dot operation, and
\|\cdot\|_{F}
denotes the Frobenius norm.

Transformation Matrix Optimization.

The pseudocode of training algorithm is presented in Appendix
C
.
Notably, a direct implementation of
\mathcal{M}
as a dense
|\mathcal{V}|\times|\mathcal{V}|
matrix is computationally prohibitive due to the large vocabulary size. To mitigate this, we parameterize
\mathcal{M}
via a low-rank decomposition
\mathcal{M}=\mathbf{E}+\mathbf{AB}
, where
\mathbf{E}
denotes the identity matrix, and
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{V}|\times r}
and
\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times|\mathcal{V}|}
are learnable matrices with decomposition rank
r
.
Moreover, directly computing full gradients renders the optimization of
\mathcal{L}_{\text{grad}}
intractable due to their high dimensionality. To this end, we adopt the gradients of the student model’s second-to-last layer as a surrogate in the loss calculation, as they contain rich task-relevant information and closely align with the model’s predicting behaviors.
Inspired by LoRA, we initialize
\mathbf{A}
from
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
and initialize
\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{0}
, so that
\mathcal{M}
starts from
\mathbf{E}
and ensures
Z^{\prime}=Z
at the beginning of training. This initialization yields more stable gradients and improves optimization effectiveness
(
9
)
.

Experiments

5.1
Experimental Setup

Models and Datasets.

Note that logit-based KD requires teachers and students to share the same tokenization to ensure one-to-one correspondence in token semantics. Hence, we select models from the same series to avoid engineering issues from vocabulary mismatches and better focus on defensive analysis.
We consider two widely used LLM families, Qwen2.5
(
25
)
and Llama 3
(
4
)
.
For the Qwen family, we use Qwen2.5-7B as the teacher model and Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct as the surrogate student. Distillation is performed on multiple student models with different scales and capacities, including Qwen2.5-3B/1.5B/0.5B. For the Llama family, we use Llama-3.1-8B as the teacher model and Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct as the surrogate student. The defensive performance is then evaluated on student models Llama-3.2-3B and Llama-3.2-1B.

Following prior work
(
16
;
13
)
, we consider three widely used benchmarks, GSM8K
(
2
)
, MMLU
(
6
)
, and MATH
(
7
)
. GSM8K is a math problem dataset with annotated reasoning chains; we use the standard split with 7,473 training and 1,319 evaluation examples. MMLU is a large-scale benchmark for evaluating world knowledge and problem-solving.
We randomly sample 15,000 pairs as training data and evaluate on a test set of 14,042 examples. The MATH is a more challenging math benchmark, which contains 7,500 training examples with step-by-step solutions. Following
(
23
)
, we evaluate performance on the MATH-500
(
15
)
dataset.

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we investigate the challenging problem of defending LLMs against unauthorized logit-based KD. We characterize the guidance signals in the teacher logits via conditional mutual information, based on which we propose a post-processing approach that learns a transformation matrix using two theory-inspired loss functions. By refining the output logits, the proposed method degrades the performance of distilled student models while preserving teacher utility, effectively protecting the intellectual property. We highlight that, unlike prior defenses against sequence-level distillation, our method makes a pioneering step in defending against tricky logit-based KD techniques. We hope this work advances the community’s understanding of LLM distillation and motivates more robust distillation defenses.

Impact Statement

This paper presents one of the earliest systematic attempts to protect the intellectual property of large language models against logit-based knowledge distillation, a particularly challenging problem that has been largely overlooked by prior work. Experimental results demonstrate that the proposed method is effective across a wide range of settings while preserving the utility of the teacher model. Moreover, this work aims to deepen the community’s understanding of the mechanisms underlying knowledge distillation. Overall, we believe this research advances machine learning by improving the privacy and security of LLM. There are many potential societal consequences of this line of research, most of which are related to the secure deployment, responsible model sharing, and intellectual property protection of LLMs, and none that we believe require specific discussion here.

Data Construction

GSM8K is a math word-problem dataset with manually annotated reasoning chains, making it particularly suitable for knowledge distillation in reasoning-intensive tasks. We use the standard split, which contains 7,473 training examples and 1,319 evaluation examples.
MMLU is a widely used multiple-choice benchmark covering a broad range of subjects. Question

15 gallons of gas were equally divided into 5 different containers. Josey needed 1/4 of a container to run her lawnmower. How many pints of gasoline did Josey need?

Vallina Distillation

Let’s think step by step

The total number of gallons of gas is 15 * 4 = 60

The number of containers is 5

The number of gallons in each container is 60 / 5 = 12

The number of pints in each container is 12 * 2 = 24

The number of pints Josey needs is 24 * 1/4 = 6

The answer is
\boxed{6}

The number of pints of gasoline that Josey needs is 12 * 2 = 24

The answer is
\boxed{24}

Question

A Senate committee has 8 Republicans and 6 Democrats. In how many ways can we form a subcommittee of 5 members that has at least one member from each party?

Vallina Distillation

We can use the principle of complementary counting. There are a total of
\binom{14}{5}=2002
ways to choose a subcommittee of 5 members from the 14 senators. We can count the number of ways to choose a subcommittee of 5 members that has only Republicans or only Democrats. There are
\binom{8}{5}=56
ways to choose a subcommittee of 5 Republicans, and
\binom{6}{5}=6
ways to choose a subcommittee of 5 Democrats. Therefore, there are
56+6=62
ways to choose a subcommittee of 5 members that has only Republicans or only Democrats. Thus, the number of ways to choose a subcommittee of 5 members that has at least one member from each party is
2002-62=\boxed{1940}
. The answer is 1940

Defensive Distillation

We can count the number of ways to form a subcommittee of 5 members that has at least one member from each party by subtracting the number of ways to form a subcommittee of 5 members that has only Republicans from the number of ways to form a subcommittee of 5 members that has only Democrats.
There are
\binom{8}{5}=56
ways to choose 5 Republicans from 8 Republicans, and there are
\binom{6}{5}=6
ways to choose 5 Democrats from 6 Democrats.

Therefore, there are
56-6=\boxed{50}
ways to form a subcommittee of 5 members that has at least one member from each party.

The answer is: 50
