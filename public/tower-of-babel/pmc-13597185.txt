Treatment switching in oncology evidence synthesis: a systematic review of meta-analytical practices incorporating time-dependent confounding, censoring bias, and patient-level variability
Authors: Edward Kurnia Setiawan Limijadi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTreatment switching in the oncology trials introduced time-dependent confounding and informative censoring, which complicated the unbiased estimation of treatment effects in evidence synthesis.ObjectivesTo systematically assess the meta-analytical practices that are used for adjusting treatment switching in oncology, with an emphasis on handling time-dependent confounding, censoring bias, as well as patient-level variability.MethodsA systematic review was conducted using PRISMA 2020 guidelines. The databases searched systematically are MEDLINE, Embase, Cochrane Library and Web of Science, and the studies published between 2015 and the last search date were included (provide exact search date). The review was conducted with the reporting standards from the PRISMA 2020 statement, and because there was methodological heterogeneity, an analysis of the findings was conducted using a structured narrative synthesis. The eligible studies included randomized trials, simulation studies, observational studies, and health technology assessments, with the application of formal statistical methods to adjust for treatment switching.ResultsSix studies had met the inclusion criteria. Common methods involved rank-preserving structural failure time models (RPSFTM), marginal structural models (MSMs), and inverse probability of censoring weighting (IPCW). The modeling assumptions, the specification of the model, the switching mechanisms, the method of dealing with time-dependent confounders, and the method of dealing with censoring were shown to vary across the included studies and affect the results and estimates of the treatment effects. The reporting practices were varying, and there was limited integration into the meta-analytical frameworks.ConclusionAdjustment regarding the treatment switching is methodologically complex, having no universally optimal approach. Enhanced level of transparency, methodological standardization, along with the consideration of patient-level heterogeneity, is important to improve the validity of the oncology evidence synthesis.

Introduction

Treatment switching, in which the patients transition from the originally assigned intervention towards an alternative therapy in a clinical trial, is commonly observed in oncology studies, mainly in cases where ethical considerations and emerging standards of care are involved [1]. As such, switching may be justified clinically, but it has substantial challenges regarding estimating unbiased treatment effects, particularly when assessing overall survival, including other time-to-event consequences [2]. The conventional intention-to-treat analyses might underrate true treatment effects in the existence of switching, while naïve per-protocol or as-treated analyses can bring significant bias [3].

A key methodological concern is time-dependent confounding; thus, prognostic factors influencing both the probability of switching and clinical outcomes emerge over time [2]. Such factors are even affected by preceding treatment, which violates the standard assumptions of traditional regression methods. In parallel, the informative censoring might take place when the patients are censored artificially at switching time, which leads to biased estimates when the censoring mechanism is regarding prognosis [4]. Such issues are even compounded by the patient-level variability, which included heterogeneity in the disease progression, response to treatment, and switching behavior, that challenged the generalizability, along with the interpretability of treatment impact estimates [4].

For addressing these complexities, different advanced statistical methods have been developed. Such involved rank-preserving structural failure time models, converse probability of the censoring weighting, two-phase adjustment methods, as well as marginal structural models [5]. All the methods depend on explicit assumptions regarding the fundamental data-generating process, treatment assignment mechanisms, and the availability of covariate information. The comparative performance, reporting practices, and methodological consistency of such approaches across the oncology studies are characterized as insufficient.

Regardless of a growing body of literature that applied these techniques, a lack of comprehensive synthesis exists, which focuses on how treatment switching is dealt with in meta-analytical contexts, mainly regarding the time-dependent biases, as well as inter-individual variability [6]. Present reviews have largely emphasized the individual methods or particular case studies, having limited attention to the systematic comparison, transparency regarding assumptions, and implications for the evidence synthesis [7], [8]. Furthermore, the extent to which present meta-analytical practices sufficiently account for switching-based biases is unclear.

Thus, this systematic review aims at critically evaluating the meta-analytical practices in the oncology studies, which include the treatment switching adjustments, with a particular focus on handling time-dependent confounding, censoring bias, as well as patient-level variability. While synthesizing methodological approaches, evaluating their fundamental assumptions, and finding gaps in the current practice, this study focuses on providing evidence-based recommendations for improving the validity, along with the transparency of oncology evidence synthesis in the context of treatment switching.

Methods

Protocol and reporting standards

This systematic review was done according to the PRISMA 2020 statement for ensuring transparency, reproducibility, and comprehensiveness of reporting [9]. The systematic review was performed following the PRISMA 2020 statement to ensure a transparent and reproducible reporting of systematic reviews. The review protocol was not a prospectively registered study before initiation of the study in PROSPERO, OSF or another registry. The lack of prospective registration is a limitation, as the registration of the protocol may limit the chance of selective reporting and post hoc methodological decisions. Some minor methodological variations on the review process were recorded and explained following the manuscript.

Inclusion of both primary studies as well as evidence synthesis contexts ensured that the review evaluated translation of the methods into practice, not only theoretical application.

The review question as well as eligibility criteria were structured with the use of the Population–Concept–Context (PCC) framework, as recommended by the Joanna Briggs Institute for the evidence synthesis, including the heterogeneous study designs along with the methodological outcomes [11]. The population included patients with oncological conditions enrolled in studies in which the treatment switching took place. The concept paid heed to the statistical adjustment methods, which addressed the treatment switching, including approaches designed for mitigating the time-dependent perplexing, informative censoring, as well as patient-level variability. Moreover, the context included oncology research settings, along with the randomized trials, observational studies, and meta-analytical evidence synthesis. The PCC framework was chosen over the traditional PICO approach as the key objective of this review was the methodological evaluation instead of the comparative effectiveness, thus needing a wider and more flexible conceptual structure.

Eligibility criteria

Eligibility criteria referred to identifying the studies assessing the statistical approaches to handle the treatment switching in oncology. The study included the randomized controlled trials, observational studies, and methodological studies that explicitly addressed treatment switching and applied the formal adjustment methods for mitigating the switching-based bias. The inclusion of diverse study designs was justified through the methodological emphasis of the review, since treatment switching takes place across the experimental and real-world settings.

Studies were eligible when they included patients having any type of cancer, as well as reported treatment impact estimation in the presence of switching between the study arms or therapies. The study needed explicit usage of the statistical adjustment approaches, which included the structural models, for example, rank-preserving structural failure time models, inverse probability weighting methods, two-stage estimators, or the marginal structural models. Moreover, the studies that did not focus on the switching analytically or lacked adequate methodological detail were all excluded. Furthermore, the reviews, editorials, and commentaries that do not have original analyses were even excluded to ensure that the synthesis was based on the primary methodological applications.

Based on the methodological aspects of this review, eligible studies were divided into four categories: (1) applied clinical studies that used treatment-switching adjustment techniques, (2) simulation studies that compared the performance of the various adjustment techniques, (3) methodological framework studies that described the causal adjustment techniques, and (4) studies of health technology assessment that incorporated the adjusted estimates into decision-making. This categorization enabled us to distinguish between the empirical applications and conceptual contributions.

Definitions of core methodological constructs

For ensuring the conceptual clarity, core constructs were referred to a priori. Treatment switching is any post-randomization or even post-baseline change in the assigned treatment, which includes crossover or initiation of the alternative therapies [12]. Time-dependent confounding occurs when there is an additional covariate that changes over time, affects the likelihood of switching treatment and the likelihood of clinical outcomes, and is also affected by prior treatment exposure [4]. The informative censoring refers to censoring mechanisms regarding the prognosis or treatment response [13]. The patient-level variability included heterogeneity in the baseline characteristics, treatment response, and disease progression, as well as switching behavior, which may affect the causal effect estimation.

Information sources and search strategy

A comprehensive as well as systematic search strategy was made for identifying the studies that addressed treatment switching as well as its analytical handling in oncology. The electronic databases searched are MEDLINE (via PubMed), Embase, the Cochrane Library, and Web of Science. The inclusion of various databases was intended to maximize the sensitivity and reduce the risk of missing pertinent methodological studies.

The search strategy was developed with the use of a combination of controlled vocabulary, for example, MeSH terms, as well as free-text keywords. Keywords were chosen to reflect three basic domains: (1) treatment switching, (2) causal inference as well as statistical adjustment methods, and (3) oncology cases. Such a structure ensured a link with the PCC framework while minimizing retrieval bias.

Other methodological terms were also considered, such as the cross-over adjustment, switching adjustment, contamination adjustment, treatment cross-over, structural nested models, g-estimation, recensoring, counterfactual survival analysis, causal survival analysis, indirect treatment comparison, network meta-analysis and health technology assessment, to enhance the retrieval sensitivity. These terms were used in conjunction with oncology-related terms to find studies that might relate to treatment switching, but do not use the term ‘treatment switching’ in the abstract or title.

Grey literature sources such as the health technology assessment databases and conference proceedings were used to identify further methodological applications. Included studies and methodological reviews related to the reference were manually screened for additional eligible studies.

Search terms regarding the treatment switching were “treatment switching,” “crossover,” and “treatment crossover,” which were chosen for capturing the variations in terminology that are used across clinical trials as well as the methodological literature. Terms regarding the statistical adjustment methods were “inverse probability weighting,” “marginal structural model,” “rank-preserving structural failure time model,” “two-stage method,” as well as “causal inference,” which reflected the key analytical approaches used for addressing the switching-related bias. Such terms were selected depending on previous methodological literature

Study selection

To ensure the relevance and modern applicability of the evidence base, studies that were published within the last ten years were a part of this review. The time restriction was applied due to the methodological approaches to address the treatment switching in oncology, mainly the ones regarding time-dependent confounding, censoring bias, as well as patient-level variability, that have evolved substantially in past decade having advances in the causal inference techniques, greater availability of individual patient data (IPD), as well as improvements in terms of the computational methods. Limiting the search to a ten-year window allowed the review to capture both introductory developments and recent refinements in the statistical practices, thus reflecting present approaches used in the regulatory and health technology assessment settings. Additionally, this timeframe ensured suitable coverage of studies that applied core adjustment methods and avoided the inclusion of out-of-date or superseded analytical approaches. Thus, this period aligned with the progressive development of causal inference along with the survival analysis methodologies, making sure that the synthesis reflected evidence that is both methodologically robust and relevant for contemporary decision-making in oncology evidence synthesis.

As per the limited number of studies that directly addressed the treatment switching in the oncology evidence synthesis and meta-analytic frameworks, a small number of relevant methodological, simulation, applied, and HTA studies were included in the study. Such studies were selected depending upon their explicit focus on the treatment switching adjustment methods, for example, RPSFTM, or IPCW, and their significance to time-dependent confounding, censoring bias, as well as individual patient-level variability.

Data extraction

Data were extracted with the use of a standardized and piloted extraction form. The reviewers had independently extracted data, having discrepancies resolved by consensus, minimizing the errors and subjective interpretation. Extracted variables involved the study characteristics, details about the treatment switching, which included proportion, timing, as well as direction, statistical methods considered for adjustment, and the assumptions fundamental for each method. More data was collected on the kind of data used (individual patient data vs aggregate data), time-dependent confounding handling and censoring, and reporting of the sensitivity analyses as well as model diagnostics. Particular emphasis was put on capturing in what ways the studies addressed the patient-level variability and heterogeneity, and provided their importance in the causal inference. Table 5, attached in the appendix sections, shows the criteria for extracting data from the relevant studies.

Risk of bias and methodological appraisal

The level of risk-of-bias and methodological quality assessment was adapted to the type of included studies. ROBINS-I was not used across all studies, as it is used in intervention studies, which have clear intervention groups and outcomes, but not necessarily in a randomized way or in simulation studies, which do not fit the methodological framework papers. When assessing applied clinical analyses, the analyses of confounding control, completeness of switching information, assumptions and validity, missing data, outcome measure and transparency of reporting were used as the assessment points. For simulation studies, the characteristics of simulation scenarios, the specification of the parameters, and the interpretation of the simulation results were emphasized [18]. The assessment parameters for methodological framework studies were conceptual clarity, repeatability, transparency of the assumptions and applicability to oncology treatment-switching adjustment. Each assessor completed an independent evaluation, and differences of opinion were discussed.

Assessment of methodological assumptions and reproducibility

For every included study, the researcher systematically assessed whether core assumptions fundamental to the applied methods were stated explicitly, justified, and evaluated. These involved the assumptions of no unmeasured confounding, appropriate model specification, and independent censoring. Moreover, studies were categorized depending on the transparency and plausibility of such assumptions. Reproducibility was evaluated by assessing the availability of analytical code, the wholeness of model specification, and the availability of underlying data. Studies were classified as reproducible, partially reproducible, or non-reproducible depending on the predefined criteria.

Data synthesis

As per the substantial heterogeneity in study designs, analytical methods, outcome measures, and reporting practices, the quantitative meta-analysis was deemed unsuitable and was not conducted. Due to significant heterogeneity in study designs, analytical methods, outcomes, assumptions and reporting, it was decided not to conduct quantitative meta-analysis of these studies. Due to different methodological goals, data sources, treatment switching mechanisms and frameworks, the included studies were not pooled for meaningful statistics. A narrative synthesis was instead performed in a structured manner to compare adjustment methods, assumptions, implementation characteristics, diagnostic procedures and implications for evidence synthesis. This was deemed acceptable for assessing methodological evidence where the effect estimates weren't directly comparable between studies.

Reporting bias and sensitivity analyses

Because of the limited number of included studies (n = 6), formal assessment of publication bias with the use of funnel plots or statistical tests was not suitable. Instead, reporting bias was qualitatively evaluated based on transparency and comprehensiveness of methodological reporting.

The studies included were mostly methodological investigations and simulation studies, applied adjustment analyses, and health technology assessment reports; these studies were not considered to be suitable for the conventional GRADE framework, as they did not include comparative clinical intervention studies (Table 3). Results

Risk-of-bias assessment

The methodological quality assessment showed differences in the studies included depending on the purpose and design of the study (Table 5). Applied re-analysis studies, depending upon the individual patient data (IPD) from the randomized controlled trials, represented a moderate to high risk of bias, mainly because of the dependence on assumptions needed for causal adjustment methods like RPSFTM and IPCW. Such included assumptions related to no unmeasured confounding, precise specification of weighting models, as well as treatment effect homogeneity. Specifically, IPCW-based approaches were sensitive to the model specification, including the presence of extreme weights, which may bring instability in the estimates. In addition, heterogeneity in the reporting of covariates, censoring processes, and treatment switching mechanisms contributed to the concerns related to the comparability across studies.

Thus, as the methodological rigor was usually high, the main source of bias across the studies was linked with the assumptions fundamental to treatment switching adjustment methods, including their implementation in the applied oncology settings instead of the study design per se.

Overview of included studies

Overall, six studies were a part of the review, which comprised the applied re-analyses of randomized controlled trials, simulation-led comparative studies, health technology assessment (HTA) studies, methodological investigations, and tech-based guidance documents. The studies had been published between 2015 and 2025 and were conducted across the UK and other international settings. The included literature mainly focused on the evaluation of the statistical methods to adjust the treatment switching in oncology trials, which include Rank-Preserving Structural Failure Time Models (RPSFTM), two-stage adjustment approaches, and inverse probability of censoring weighting (IPCW) Many studies compared the multiple methods in the same analytical framework, which highlighted differences in the estimated treatment impacts when alternative assumptions were used. Simulation-based studies discovered the performance of such methods under different conditions of the treatment switching intensity, confounding structures, and censoring mechanisms.

Health technology assessment studies assessed the implications regarding the treatment switching adjustments for the survival estimation, including downstream decision-making, particularly in the economic evaluations. Such studies confirmed that the adjustment methods could substantially influence the survival estimates, which in turn affected the cost-effectiveness outcomes. The methodological guidance documents even emphasized the importance of applying the sensitivity analyses and carefully assessing the underlying assumptions when executing the switching adjustments in practice. Thus, the evidence base reflected a prevalence of the methodological and applied analytical studies instead of large-scale meta-analyses including the treatment switching adjustments, demonstrating a gap in integrating such methods in the formal evidence synthesis frameworks.

Thematic analysis

Theme 1: Methodological Foundations along with the Structural Assumptions in Treatment Switching Adjustment

The methodological approaches to address the treatment switching in the oncology trials are based on the causal inference frameworks relying heavily on the structural assumptions as well as counterfactual reasoning. The work by Hernán & Robins et al. introduced the concept of target trial emulation, which emphasized explicitly defining the eligibility criteria, treatment approaches, assignment processes, and follow-up periods, along with the outcomes for reducing bias when randomized trials are not feasible [17]. The framework underpinned most of the modern approaches to the treatment switching adjustments.

In a similar way, methods like augmented two-stage estimation as well as inverse probability weighting depend on the assumptions of no unmeasured confounding, including correct model specification [14]. This highlighted the need for structural validity in the causal estimation. The increased two-stage estimation approach demonstrated how the external data can be included for improving the precision; it is still based on the validity of the fundamental assumptions with regard to the post-randomization treatment behavior as well as censoring mechanisms.

Preceding systematic reviews emphasized that various statistical approaches are reinforced by different assumptions regarding the treatment switching mechanisms, which include monotonicity, consistency, and exchangeability [4]. Such assumptions are not even testable in practice; however, they critically affect the validity of projected treatment effects. Overall, this theme highlighted that the treatment switching adjustment methods are highly dependent on the structural assumptions, and the selection of the method should align with the plausibility of such assumptions in the case of the available data.

Theme 2: Confounding Adjustment and Time-Dependent Bias

A core methodological challenge found across the included studies refers to the presence of time-dependent confounding, which arises when the post-baseline variables affect the treatment switching and are affected by the prior treatment. Methods like the inverse probability of censoring weighting (IPCW), rank-preserving structural failure time models (RPSFTM), and marginal structural models (MSMs) are commonly used for addressing this issue. IPCW and MSM approaches explicitly adjusted for the time-varying confounders by reweighting observations for the creation of a pseudo-population, such that treatment assignment is independent of the measured confounders. Though such methods need correct specification regarding the treatment and censoring models.

The augmented two-stage estimation method even extended such approaches while incorporating external or any auxiliary information for improving the estimation efficiency [14]. Though its performance is quite sensitive to model misspecification, including the quality of external data sources. The HTA-focused study emphasized that time-dependent confounding is a key limitation in the real-world evidence synthesis, mainly when the observational data are used to supplement the trial findings [15]. Moreover, target trial emulation frameworks tend to attempt to mitigate such bias while aligning the observational analyses with the hypothetical randomized designs.

Across all the included studies, bias because of the time-dependent confounding and treatment switching is one of the most significant threats to validity, which reinforced the need for careful methodological selection, including sensitivity analyses.

Theme 3: Heterogeneity in terms of Methodological Approaches and Reporting Practices

The included systematic and scoping reviews demonstrated the significant heterogeneity in how treatment switching is handled across the studies. Differences are there not just in the statistical methods used but even in how such methods are reported as well justified. The systematic review highlighted that studies even lacked the standardized reporting of assumptions, model diagnostics, including sensitivity analyses, which complicated interpretation and reproducibility. In the same way, the scoping review identified variability in applying the adjustment techniques across the real-world studies, with inconsistent documentation of the switching mechanisms and analytical choices [15]. Simulation-based study involved in the evidence base showed that various methods performed variably, focusing on the original switching scenario, like the timing, proportion, and direction of switching [16]. It reinforced that no single method is generally optimal, and performance is quite context-dependent.

The detection bias study, which focused on the open-label trials, indirectly contributed to this theme while demonstrating how poor blinding and differential outcome assessment can affect the reported treatment effects, which may interact with the treatment switching adjustments in the meta-analytic contexts. Hence, this theme underscored the absence of standardized methodological approaches, including the reporting frameworks for the treatment switching adjustments, signifying a critical gap in the literature.

Theme 4: Implications regarding Health Technology Assessment (HTA) and Evidence Synthesis

The integration of the treatment switching adjustments into health technology assessment (HTA) frameworks is a recurrent theme across these included studies. HTA decisions depend on the accurate estimates of the treatment effectiveness, whereas treatment switching can bias such estimates if not addressed properly. The incorporation of causal inference methods like target trial emulation can enhance the robustness of evidence used in policy and reimbursement decisions [15]. While aligning the observational analyses with hypothetical randomized trials, decision-makers can enhance the interpretation of real-world data.

The systematic review even highlighted that treatment switching adjustments are highly important in oncology because of the ethical considerations that allowed crossover in trials, diluting the observed treatment effects [12]. Adjusting for the switching is thus important to estimate the accurate causal effect of the interventions. Moreover, failure to account for switching can significantly lead to biased estimates of survival benefits, which directly affects the cost-effectiveness analyses in HTA frameworks. Hence, such findings suggested that the methodological rigor in handling treatment switching is not just a statistical concern. It also has a policy-relevant issue affecting the regulatory and reimbursement decisions.

Theme 5: Variability in the Treatment Effects and Sensitivity to Model Assumptions

Another core theme that emerged from the included studies refers to the variability in estimated treatment effects, which depended on the selected adjustment method and fundamental assumptions. MSMs are a theoretically sound approach to overcome time-dependent confounding; however, there are two assumptions underlying their use: correct model specification and sufficient longitudinal data to satisfy the assumption of no violation of positivity conditions to support the model. Hence, it is important to note that MSMs cannot be considered as always preferable to other methods [16]. The included studies highlighted that the model-based approaches are mainly sensitive to assumptions regarding the treatment switching mechanisms as well as censoring processes. Violations of such assumptions can lead to biased or unstable estimates.

The study on the external data integration in the augmented two-stage estimation illustrated how the incorporation of the additional information can enhance the precision, but it also introduced dependency on the validity and compatibility of the external datasets[12]. Across these studies, sensitivity analyses are focused on as a critical component to evaluate robustness. Though reporting of these analyses is inconsistent. This theme underscores that treatment effect estimates in the presence of switching are integrally model-dependent, whereas conclusions must be interpreted with caution, mainly when various methods yield divergent results.

Comparative assumptions of treatment-switching adjustment methods

The studies that were included showed that there are significant differences among the treatment-switching adjustment methods with regard to their assumptions, data needed, and implementation issues. RPSFTM assumes that the treatment effects are constant across patient subgroups or disease phases, and that the recensoring procedures are valid, which may be problematic when the treatment effects differ between patient subgroups and/or disease phases. IPCW needs to properly specify switching and censoring models, to measure adequately prognostic covariates and to measure and assess positivity and extreme weights. Assumptions about unmeasured confounding, treatment history and time-varying covariates are involved in MSM approaches, and require the proper modeling of time-varying covariates, treatment history, and censoring mechanisms. Two-stage approaches need to be properly defined by switching eligibility criteria, post-switching information and a suitable selection of secondary baseline. Thus, there is no “best” method, and the choice of method is always subjective. The choice of adjustment approach should be dependent on clinical settings, switching mechanisms, patient-level data available and the likelihood of the assumptions.

Discussion

A systematic review was conducted to assess methodological approaches to dealing with treatment switching in oncology evidence synthesis. Due to the significant variability in study design, adjustments, data structures and reporting, there was insufficient homogeneity among studies to do a quantitative, meta-analysis-type review. Instead, the emphasis was on statistical models to deal with treatment switching, the presence of time-varying confounding, informative censoring, and patient-level variability.

In the studies included, the most common were rank-preserving structural failure time models, marginal structural model logic, inverse probability of censoring weighting, and two further two-stage variants. The overriding methodological concern is that post-randomization events like progression are normally the cause of switching, are prognostic, and influenced by a previous treatment, and they invalidate naive per-protocol comparisons, which causes time-dependent confounding and selection processes. In theory, IPCW and other weighting methods can be used to recover a pseudo population in which censoring at switch is conditionally independent, but a proper specification of the censoring model and sufficient measures of time-varying predictors are required to do so [19]. RPSFT style approaches, by contrast, do not explicitly model switching probability but use powerful structural assumptions regarding the manner in which treatment hastens or slows down survival time, which may be unrealistic when switching is late, or when treatment effects are disease-state specific, or when the intensity of exposure to treatment is meaningfully different across individuals [20].

One of the regularly observed outcomes was a great deal of variation in predicted treatment effects across methods and specifications, which has clinical and policy implications since small differences in survival often determine oncology decisions. The most obvious implication is that model-dependent instead of data-driven treatment effect estimates are made under switching and synthesis, and that interchange adjusted estimates can be deceptive. Sullivan et al. [12] demonstrated that inappropriate reporting about implementation details and sensitivity analyses is typical of switching adjusted analyses, and this is consistent with the results of the current review, in which heterogeneous reporting was observed and the inability to incorporate them into meta-analytical processes. Latimer et al. [21] in a real-world case study showed that various implementations of IPCW and two stage estimation could recover plausible effects, but RPSFTM did not do so and wide uncertainty was obtained on heavy switching, which shows that performance varies not only by method choice, but also by the operationalization of model steps, censoring choices, and covariate treatment by analysts.

The importance of patient-level heterogeneity is that switching decisions have been known to be affected by clinical status, adverse events, biomarkers, and health systems limitations, which can alter switching probability and treatment effect. Partially, the heterogeneity can be dealt with using weighting techniques, although the covariates of effects and prognostic factors require adequate time resolution. In most studies, the covariates required are not fully specified, and those that are specified are measured just a few times or not harmonized to form the secondary evidence. Structural models, including RPSFTM, may also fail in cases where early and late switchers respond differently to treatment, or post-progression survival is less homogenous across subgroups, due to the reduced defensibility of constant effect style assumptions [20]. Besides, cross-sectional evidence suggests that RPSFTM type adjustments are commonly applied in contexts with estimates that are more similar to sequential therapy efficacy than fundamental efficacy, and make results challenging to interpret when meta-analyses have to pool the results of heterogeneous switching regimes [22]. Patient-level variability was identified as an important factor to consider when addressing switching behavior and interpretation of treatment effects across the evidence included; however, this was measured and reported inconsistently. Several factors have been reported to cause variability, such as baseline prognosis, disease course, biomarker status, therapy response, adverse events, and availability of subsequent therapies. Future studies should use prospective data to establish clinical meaningful patient-level covariates and assess for treatment effect differentials in clinically meaningful patient sub-groups.

Another implication is regarding evidence synthesis and health technology assessment, where adjusted survival estimates are input into cost-effectiveness models and reimbursement decisions. The fact that the review found only a small amount of meta-analytical integration indicates that analysts are prone to a lack of congruence between trial-level adjustment outputs and synthesis models that are intended to deal with exchangeable study estimates. Recent target trial thinking research in HTA demonstrates that clear information on estimate, treatment approaches, follow up, and censoring guidelines are necessary in the application of real world data, and the matching of evidence to decision questions, conceptually relevant to switching because the application of switching effectively can form multiple embedded strategies within a single randomized trial [23]. Here, the lack of standardized reporting and access to patient-level information limits the viability of principled pooling across studies and promotes ad hoc decision-making, including picking up a single adjusted estimate without completely propagating structural uncertainty.

The findings can be summed up by saying that there is no universally optimal approach to the treatment switching adjustment in the evidence synthesis in oncology, yet it also shows certain priorities to be given to the practice reinforcement. The analysts are required to spell out the estimate clearly and explain why a certain adjustment is the right answer to the question of the decision and not a set of adjusted results without a consistent causal focus. The reporting ought to be based on the systematic guidelines, such as clear-cut assumptions, implementation options, diagnostic tests, and sensitivity analyses that trade key modeling decisions, since they are at the heart of interpretability and reproducibility [12], [14]. The patient-level data and time-varying covariates should be modeled in advance to minimize the use of assumptions that cannot be verified, and synthesis should reflect method uncertainty instead of assuming that adjusted estimates are certain.

Limitations

This review has many limitations. Only six studies had met the inclusion criteria, which reflected the highly specialized as well as methodologically intensive nature of the treatment switching adjustment in oncology. Moreover, the stringent eligibility requirements, mainly the need for explicit application of the advanced causal inference methods along with sufficient methodological transparency, had excluded a large proportion of the studies that discussed the treatment switching conceptually; however, they did not execute the formal adjustment techniques. In addition, the restriction to studies that were published between 2015 and 2025, though justified for capturing the contemporary methodological practices, may have excluded previous foundational work.

The heterogeneity in the study design, data sources, i.e., individual patient data vs aggregate data, as well as analytical methods, restricted the feasibility of the quantitative synthesis and direct comparison across the studies. The reliance on published reports introduced the potential reporting bias, mainly given the unpredictable documentation of the model assumptions, sensitivity analyses, and diagnostic procedures. The variability in how patient-level heterogeneity, including switching mechanisms, was reported constrained the capability of fully assessing the generalizability. Regardless of these limitations, the included studies represented a diverse range of methodological approaches, giving a focused as well as analytically robust synthesis of the current practices in terms of treatment switching adjustment.

Conclusion

Treatment switching is one of the critical methodological challenges in oncology evidence synthesis, mainly in the estimation of unbiased treatment effects for the time-to-event outcomes. This review demonstrated that, as advanced causal inference methods like the rank-preserving structural failure time models, marginal structural models, and inverse probability of censoring weighting provide theoretically strong solutions, their practical application is largely dependent on the unverifiable assumptions, including data quality. The findings highlighted that variation in terms of structural assumptions, handling of the time-dependent confounding, and approaches to the informative censoring can lead to substantially varying treatment effect estimates. Furthermore, poor methodological standardization and unpredictable reporting practices across the studies limited comparability and complicated the meta-analytic integration. The implications extended beyond methodological rigor, which affected the health technology assessments along with the clinical decision-making, where adjusted survival estimates directly informed the policy and reimbursement decisions. Significantly, the patient-level variability is insufficiently addressed, which further constrains the generalizability of findings. Hence, this review underscored the need for higher transparency, standardized reporting frameworks, including rigorous sensitivity analyses in the studies that address treatment switching. Future studies should focus on the development of standardized reporting formats, clear causal assumptions and comparative studies involving realistic oncology datasets to identify the most useful times to implement particular adjustment methods. Strengthening such aspects is vital for improving the validity, reproducibility, and interpretability of oncology evidence synthesis in the presence of treatment switching.
