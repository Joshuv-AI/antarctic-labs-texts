Differences in symptoms and clinical outcomes of COVID-19 infection across vaccination status and age groups during the national pandemic from December 2022 to February 2023 in China: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Nan Hua, Ange Wang, Fang Zheng, Wenjing Gao, Qin Zhang, Jing Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe end of the ‘dynamic zero-COVID’ policy on 7 December 2022 caused an immediate Omicron outbreak in China, which is the first nationwide COVID-19 wave in China. We aimed to assess the difference in COVID-related symptoms and clinical outcomes across vaccination status and age groups.MethodsThis was a cross-sectional study. Data were collected via online survey or telephone interview. The target population was adults who had been infected during this wave since December 2022 and were not hospitalized due to COVID-19 infection at the survey time. Information on COVID-19 infection status, COVID-related symptoms, COVID-related pneumonia, clinic visits, hospital admissions, and dose of vaccines received was collected. Data were analyzed by log-binomial regression and negative binomial regression.ResultsOf 2350 participants aged 18–98 years, 82% were COVID-infected or probably infected, 81% of the participants had received at least one dose of a vaccine and 79% had received two or more doses. The percentage of vaccination decreased with age. Compared to the unvaccinated group, receiving three or more doses of vaccine was associated with a lower risk of pneumonia (prevalence ratio, PR: 0.550, 95% CI: 0.377 to 0.805), clinic visits (PR: 0.629, 95% CI: 0.488 to 0.810), and hospital admission (PR: 0.293, 95% CI: 0.162 to 0.530). Compared to young adults (aged below 36 years), older adults (aged above 75 years) were more likely to have fatigue (PR: 1.224, 95% CI 1.078 to 1.389), had a longer length of time to symptom disappearance (odds ratio 3.784, 95% CI 2.657 to 5.387). Compared to adults aged below 56 years, those aged above 75 years were associated with a higher risk of having COVID-19 related pneumonia (PR 3.669, 95% CI 2.190 to 6.148), clinic visits (PR: 1.727, 95%: 1.237 to 2.412), and hospitalization (PR 6.656, 95% CI 2.609 to 16.980). After further adjustment for multimorbidity, the effect size of the association between age groups and clinical outcomes was reduced.ConclusionsOlder adults are at higher risk of severe COVID-related outcomes, and vaccination seems to protect against severe outcomes. Ongoing vaccination is necessary.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-11733-5.Keywords: COVID-19, Coronavirus, Vaccine, Symptoms, Older adults, Fatigue

Introduction

While the World Health Organization (WHO) ended COVID-19 as a public health emergency in May 2023 [1], it remains a public health threat globally, causing heavy medical costs and work productivity loss. By the end of June 2023, over 6.9 million related deaths have been reported worldwide [2]. The SARS-CoV-2 variant Omicron was reported to the WHO in November 2021[3]. Since then, the dominant global epidemic lineage has gradually changed from the Delta variant to the Omicron variant [4, 5]. Despite the relatively lower clinical severity of the Omicron variant [6–8], older adults are the high-risk group for severe outcomes [9].

During the global COVID pandemic since 2019, China had been implementing strict prevention and control measures, shifting from initial “First-level-response”, “Normalized-control” to “Dynamic-COVID-zero” policy [10]. Under these policies, mass population-level testing and quarantine measures were conducted, which effectively suppressed COVID-19 infection spread in regions in China [11]. On 7 December 2022, the dynamic zero-COVID policy was officially moved towards reopening [12]. Changes in prevention measures caused an immediate national outbreak, which was the most massive nationwide COVID outbreak wave in mainland China since 2019. During this period, the reported daily new cases peaked in late December 2022 and then gradually decreased [13]. Studies have estimated that around 80–90% of the population in mainland China was infected during this first nationwide wave [14, 15].

As older adults were a particularly vulnerable population for COVID-19 infection and adverse clinical outcomes, vaccination is the primary strategy for preventing severe conditions and death related to COVID-19 [16]. Pivotal clinical trials have demonstrated the effectiveness of COVID-19 vaccines in preventing severe clinical outcomes [17]. However, despite vaccines being provided freely, a substantial proportion of older adults in China have remained hesitant to receive them, mainly due to concerns or misconceptions about vaccine efficacy and safety [18, 19], presenting a significant public health challenge after the end of the Zero-COVID policy. During the first nationwide wave of COVID-19 in China, real-world data on age-specific clinical characteristics and the potential benefit of receiving multiple vaccine doses remained limited.

We conducted a survey among community-dwelling Chinese adults from January- February 2023. The aims of our study were: (1) to describe and compare COVID-19 symptom prevalence across age groups and vaccination status; (2) to evaluate the associations between vaccination status and clinical outcomes in Chinese adults; (3) to evaluate the associations between age groups and clinical outcomes of COVID-19 infection.

Methods

Participants and data collection

This was a cross-sectional study conducted from January 7 to February 28, 2023, during the first nationwide COVID-19 outbreak wave in China. To reduce potential information bias when participants recall their infection experience, the data collection was planned to be completed at the end of February 2023. To recruit as many participants over a relatively short period, we used a convenience sampling approach via online survey or phone interview. Individuals were eligible if they were aged ≥ 18 years, had been infected since December 2022, and were willing to participate in the study. As the study focuses on assessing post-infection clinical outcomes, the target population was adults infected during this first national wave (since December 2022) who had either recovered from acute infection or were in the post-acute phase at the survey time. Thus, individuals likely in the acute phase were excluded, including those (1) reporting being infected for less than one week; (2) currently hospitalized due to COVID-19 at the time of the survey. The online survey was created on the online survey platform Wenjuanxing (www.sojump.com). The survey link was distributed through WeChat social media and participants were also asked to share the survey link with their contacts. Considering the lower proportion of older adults using WeChat, we conducted a telephone survey at the Xiaoying District Community Health Service Center in Hangzhou, Zhejiang province, to recruit more older adult participants. A trained medical staff called the older residents (aged older than 60 years) from the directory of around 1500 residents aged older than 60 years who signed Family Doctor Services with Xiaoying District Community Health Service Center. If they met the inclusion criteria and were willing to participate in the study, they were further interviewed by the medical staff via phone.

A total of 2377 participants responded to the survey, including 1353 who responded to the online survey and 1024 to the phone interview. We processed the data using the following cleaning criteria: (1) aged below 18 years (n = 11); (2) submitted multiple questionnaires (n = 5); (3) reported logically contradicted data (n = 3); (4) had missing data of age (n = 1); and (5) had been recently infected for less than a week (n = 7). Thus, 2350 participants were included in the analysis.

Measurements

Demographic characteristics and vaccination status

Information on age, sex (male, female), educational level (primary school and below, junior high school, high school, graduate and above), province of living, current smoking status (yes, no), height, and weight was collected. BMI was calculated and then divided into four groups, including low weight (BMI < 18.5 kg/m2), normal weight (18.5 kg/m2 ≤ BMI < 24 kg/m2), overweight (24 kg/m2 ≤ BMI < 28 kg/m2), and obesity (BMI ≥ 28 kg/m2). Age was divided into four groups: below 36 years, 36–55 years, 56–75 years, and above 75 years. Participants were asked to report whether they have any of a list of chronic diseases, including hypertension, diabetes, coronary heart disease, angina, myocardial infarction, with arterial stent placement, stroke, Parkinson’s disease, chronic bronchitis, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, hematological disease, chronic kidney diseases, cancer, rheumatic autoimmune diseases. The number of medications taken for the reported chronic diseases (zero, one, two, three, above three) was also asked. The doses of COVID vaccines taken before December 2022 (zero, one dose, two doses, three doses, and above) were asked.

Infection status and length of time to symptom disappearance

The infection status of participants during this outbreak was determined by asking the question “have you been infected with COVID-19 since December 2022?” with four options: “I have been infected, confirmed by nucleic acid test or antigen test for COVID-19”, “I probably have been infected, because I was around people with confirmed infection and I experienced symptoms”, “I haven’t been infected or I don’t know if I’ve been infected because I didn’t have symptoms”, and “I am not sure about my infection status.” Participants who reported “I have been infected, confirmed by nucleic acid test or antigen test for COVID-19” were defined as infected individuals. Participants who reported “I probably have been infected, because I was around people with confirmed infection and I experienced symptoms” were defined as individuals with a high probability of being infected. Individuals who were infected or probably infected were asked to recall the length of time since infection (within 1 week; 1–2 weeks; 2–3 weeks; 3–4 weeks; above 1 month). Participants who reported COVID infection “within 1 week” were excluded from the analysis. Additionally, they were asked to report the length of time to symptoms disappearance (within 3 days; 3–5 days; 5–7 days; 1–2 weeks; above 2 weeks; symptoms still exist at the survey time; not sure). For those who reported their symptoms still existed at the survey time and the length of time since the infection was more than one month, their length of time to symptom disappearance was grouped as more than 4 weeks.

COVID-19-related symptoms, medication, and health service utilization

Participants reported their COVID-19-related symptoms. The online survey included 18 symptoms. The phone interview only collected information on 11 common symptoms. For the analysis, we thus included 11 symptoms that were both included in the online survey and phone interviews, including fever, cough, expectoration, sore throat, musculoskeletal pain, vomiting, diarrhea, lack of appetite, fatigue, shortness of breath, and chest tightness. The total number of symptoms was calculated. Medication usage for COVID-19 was collected, including cold medicine, antipyretic, traditional Chinese medicine, antibiotic, glucocorticoid, paxlovid, azvudine, molnupiravir, cough medicine, other medicines, and none. Two questions were asked about health service utilization, including ‘did you visit the outpatient or emergency department because of COVID-19’(yes/no), and ‘were you admitted to the hospital due to COVID-19 infection’ (yes/no).

Pneumonia status

Participants were asked to report whether they had a chest computed tomography (CT) (yes/no) due to COVID-19 infection. If reporting ‘yes’, we asked further whether they had pneumonia confirmed by CT (yes/no/not sure). Participants who reported that they received a chest CT and had a confirmed diagnosis of pneumonia were defined as individuals with pneumonia detected by CT. Participants who reported that they did not receive a chest CT or participants who received a chest CT and did not have a confirmed diagnosis of pneumonia or were unsure whether they had pneumonia from their chest CT results were defined as non-pneumonia individuals.

Statistical analysis

Regression analysis was restricted to those who reported that they had tested positive for COVID infection (n = 1565) or had a high probability of being infected (n = 349), with a total of 1914 participants. Associations of vaccination status and age groups with the presence of each symptom (yes, no), having pneumonia (yes, no), having clinic visits due to COVID (yes, no), and having hospital admission due to COVID (yes, no) were assessed by log-binomial regression, reporting prevalence ratio (PR) [20–22]. Associations of vaccination status and age groups with the number of symptoms (expressed as count data) were assessed by negative-binomial regression, reporting mean ratio. Associations of vaccination status and age groups with the length of time to symptom disappearance (expressed as ordinal data) were assessed by ordinal logistic regression, reporting odds ratio (OR). For the analysis using pneumonia and hospital admission as the outcomes, as very few people had pneumonia or were hospitalized due to COVID-19 in young adults, we regrouped age into three groups: below 56 years, 56–75 years, and above 75 years. For the analyses evaluating associations between age groups and clinical outcomes, we adjusted for sex, smoking status, BMI, educational level, and dose of COVID-19 vaccine in the multivariable models. To assess whether the associations between age groups and clinical outcomes were partially explained by multimorbidity, multimorbidity (number of chronic diseases) was then further adjusted in the multivariable model to see if the effect sizes were reduced. For the analyses evaluating the association between doses of vaccination and clinical outcomes, we adjusted for age groups, sex, smoking status, BMI, and educational status in the multivariable models. Statistical significance was defined as p-value < 0.05 (two-tailed). All statistical analyses were performed using Stata/SE Version 16.0.

Results

The study included 2350 participants aged 18–98 years (Table S1), with those aged above 55 years accounting for 54%. The participants were from 27 out of 32 provincial-level administrative divisions in mainland China, with the majority from Zhejiang Province (Table S2). Overall, 81% of participants had received at least one dose of a COVID vaccine, and 67% had received three or more doses. The proportion of people who were vaccinated with at least one dose was highest among the 18–35 age group (95%) and the 36–55 age group (95%), with the proportion decreasing with age. Among those older than 75 years, 59% were vaccinated with at least one dose (Table S1). Individuals who received two or more doses of vaccine were younger, more females, had higher education levels, and had fewer chronic diseases (Table S3).

Infection status and usage of medications due to COVID infection

During the national outbreak period, 67% reported that they were infected with COVID-19 confirmed by nucleic acid test or antigen test for COVID-19, and 15% reported that they should have been infected as they experienced symptoms but did not take any COVID-19 diagnostic testing (Supplementary Figure S1). Among 1914 participants infected or probable infected by COVID, the majority of them aged 36–55 years (29%) and 56–75 years (35%), were female (61%), and received three or more vaccines (68%). Those in the older age groups were more likely to have chronic diseases and take more medications for chronic diseases (Table 1).

Among the infected or probable infected participants, people tend to use antipyretic (52%), cold medicine (31%), and cough medicine (25%) to relieve symptoms. Only a small proportion of participants used new antiviral drugs against COVID-19 (2%), like paxlovid, azvudine, and molnupiravir (Supplementary Figure S2b). The most prevalent symptoms were fever (79%), cough (77%), fatigue (63%), expectoration (50%), lack of appetite (40%), musculoskeletal pain (40%), and sore throat (38%) (Supplementary Figure S2a). The prevalence of symptoms varies across age groups. Among individuals aged below 36 years, a higher proportion reported fever (86%), cough (86%), expectoration (62%), sore throat (58%), and vomiting (8%) after COVID-19 infection compared to other age groups. Among older adults aged above 75 years, fatigue (72%) and loss of appetite (53%) were more prevalent compared to the younger age group (Fig. 1).

Associations between vaccination status and presence of symptoms, number of symptoms, length of time to symptom disappearance

The multivariable models consistently showed that receiving two or three doses of the COVID-19 vaccine was associated with significantly lower prevalence of lack of appetite and expectoration and higher prevalence of sore throat, with similar effect sizes for two doses and three doses (Fig. 2). In addition, receiving two doses was associated with a higher prevalence of musculoskeletal pain, and receiving three doses was associated with a lower prevalence of diarrhea. No significant difference was found between the prevalence of other symptoms and vaccination status.

Compared with unvaccinated participants, those who received three or more doses tend to report slightly fewer symptoms (mean ratio 0.935, 95% CI 0.875 to 1.000) and slightly shorter length of time to symptom disappearance (OR: 0.640, 95% CI: 0.495 to 0.827) (Fig. 2). No difference in the number of symptoms and length of time to symptom disappearance was found for taking one or two doses of the vaccinations.

Associations between vaccination status and clinical outcomes

The multivariable regression showed that vaccination status was significantly associated with the risk of clinical outcomes. Receiving two doses of vaccine was associated with a lower risk of having pneumonia (PR: 0.413, 95% CI: 0.201 to 0.848). No significant difference in the risk of having clinic visits was observed between individuals who received two vaccine doses and those who were unvaccinated. Receiving three or more vaccine doses was associated with a lower risk of having pneumonia (PR: 0.550, 95% CI: 0.377 to 0.805) and a lower risk of clinic visits (PR: 0.629, 95% CI: 0.488 to 0.810) compared to the unvaccinated group (Table 2). Receiving two or more doses was associated with a significant reduction in the risk of hospital admission compared to the unvaccinated group. The adjusted PR for hospital admission was 0.272 (95% CI: 0.082 to 0.907) for the two-dose group and 0.293 (95% CI: 0.162 to 0.530) for the three or more doses group compared with the unvaccinated group (Table 2).

Associations between age groups and presence of symptoms, number of symptoms, length of time to symptom disappearance

Log-binomial regression showed that the prevalence of fever, cough, expectoration, sore throat, musculoskeletal pain, and vomiting was lower in the 56–75 years group and above 75 years group (Fig. 3). The prevalence of fatigue and lack of appetite was highest among the group aged over 75 years compared to other age groups. After adjusting for sex, BMI, smoking status, dose of vaccine, and education level, the prevalence of fatigue was 22% (PR: 1.224, 95% CI: 1.078 to 1.389) higher in the over 75 years group compared to the below 36 years group. In contrast, the prevalence of sore throat and vomiting decreases with age in a dose-response pattern.

The association between age groups and presence of COVID-related symptoms, number of symptoms, and length of time to symptom disappearance. The age group below 36 years was used treated as the reference group. The model was adjusted for age groups, smoking status, BMI, and educational level. The effect sizes for each symptom were estimated from log-binomial regression, expressed as prevalence ratio. The effect size for number of symptoms was estimated from negative-binomial regression, expressed mean ratio. The effect size for the length of time to symptom disappearance from ordered logistic regression, expressed odds ratio. *Data in bold indicate statistical significance; *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001

The multivariable analysis showed that the number of symptoms in the 56–75 years group was 12% lower (mean ratio: 0.883, 95% CI: 0.817 to 0.954) compared to the youngest age group. No significant difference was found between other age groups in the number of symptoms reported. The length of time to symptom disappearance varies across age groups (Supplementary Figure S2c), which was significantly longer in older age groups (Fig. 3). For example, older adults aged above 75 years were significantly more likely to report a longer length of time to symptom disappearance compared with those below 36 years, with an OR of 3.784 (95% CI: 2.657 to 5.387).

Associations between age groups and clinical outcomes

The multivariable regression analysis showed that the risk of pneumonia detected by CT, COVID-related clinic visits, and hospital admission was significantly higher among older adults (Table 3). Compared to those aged below 56 years, the risk of having pneumonia detected by CT was significantly higher for the aged 56–75 years group (PR: 1.968, 95% CI: 1.209 to 3.205) and those aged above 75 years (PR: 3.669, 95% CI: 2.190 to 6.148). Similarly, the risk of having COVID-related clinic visits (PR: 1.727, 95%: 1.237 to 2.412) and hospitalization (PR:6.656, 95% CI: 2.609 to 16.980) was significantly higher in the age group aged above 75 years than in the age group aged below 56 years. No significant difference in the above clinical outcomes was found between other age groups.

Further adjustment for multimorbidity in the multivariable regression reduced the effect sizes of the association between age groups and clinical outcomes. After adjusting for multimorbidity, the association between age groups and the risk of having clinic visits became insignificant. The PR for pneumonia and hospital admission was reduced from 3.669 (95% CI: 2.190 to 6.148) to 2.353 (95% CI: 1.267 to 4.370) and 6.656 (95% CI: 2.609 to 16.980) to 4.949 (95% CI: 1.649 to 14.850) (Table 3).

Discussion

We assessed the associations between several clinical outcomes across vaccination status and age groups during the recent massive nationwide outbreak in late December 2022 in China. We found that receiving three or more doses of vaccines was associated with fewer symptoms and a shorter length of time to symptom disappearance. Moreover, individuals receiving three or more doses of vaccines had a lower risk of pneumonia, clinic visits, and hospitalization. However, vaccination coverage among older adults was significantly lower than that of younger adults. After COVID-19 infection, older adults (the aged above 75 years group) were more likely to have fatigue, had a longer length of time to symptom disappearance, higher chance of having pneumonia, and had clinic visits and hospitalization. These findings suggest that older adults remain at high risk for negative outcomes after COVID-19 infection. Future public health policies should strongly recommend vaccination and enhance health education efforts to encourage high-risk populations, such as older adults, to complete the full vaccination schedule.

Our findings were consistent with phase 3 trials of the inactivated vaccine Sinovac-CoronaVac, which is one of the predominant vaccines the Chinese public received. The trials showed that the Sinovac-CoronaVac vaccine efficacy of two doses of protection against hospitalization ranged from 85% to 100% 17. A study [23] using real-world coverage data of BNT162b2 and CoronaVac from December 2021 to March 2022 among the Hong Kong population also showed higher effectiveness of two or more doses against severe COVID-19 disease and death for both brands of vaccines.

Since the global pandemic, China has developed and approved thirteen COVID-19 vaccines [24], and the approved vaccines were provided free to the public. The 2-dose inactivated vaccine was the most widely used in China [25, 26]. After finishing the primary vaccination process, receiving a booster dose was recommended. We showed that although 79% of the study population had received two or more doses of vaccine, the proportion of those receiving three or more doses of vaccine decreased with age, with 48% of those aged above 75 years receiving three or more doses. The figure was close to that released by the China CDC, which showed that until 28 November 2022, the percentage of taking booster vaccine of older adults aged over 80 years was 40%[27]. Low coverage and willingness to receive essential vaccines among older adults remain important public health challenges, including COVID-19 vaccines, Influenza vaccines, and pneumococcal vaccines [28, 29]. Future efforts should prioritize raising awareness about the importance of vaccination among older adults and expanding vaccine accessibility through community-based sites.

Interestingly, our study showed that people who had received three or more doses of the vaccine had a higher risk of sore throat. Similarly, previous studies found that compared with unvaccinated individuals, individuals who received three or more doses of vaccine were associated with increased risk of upper respiratory symptoms (e.g., sore throat, nasal discharge, sneezing) [30, 31]. The specific explanation for this finding requires further investigation.

We found that older adults were more likely to report having fatigue after infection, have a longer length of time to symptom disappearance, and have more severe clinical outcomes. Consistent with previous studies conducted in different countries over the two-year global pandemic period [32–35], we showed that older adults aged above 75 years had a significantly higher prevalence of pneumonia, clinic visits, and hospitalization. These findings that older adults tend to have worse clinical outcomes after COVID infection may be partly attributable to their higher burden of chronic diseases and age-related health decline [36–38]. Despite the evidence showing that Omicron is getting less severe over evolution [6–8], these findings suggest that older adults, especially those with multimorbidity, were still at higher risk of critical outcomes of COVID-19, which would lead to high demand for healthcare services.

The current study has several strengths. First, this study sample was large, which allowed us to compare clinical outcomes across age groups and vaccination status. Secondly, this was the first study to collect data on the clinical characteristics of all age groups of adults after the reopening in China in December 2022. There were several limitations. Firstly, we did not collect information on the brands of vaccines and the time they received their last dose, which limited us to further comparing the effectiveness between brands of vaccines. Secondly, individuals currently hospitalized due to COVID-19 at the survey time were not included, potentially resulting in a minor underestimation of the very severe cases with prolonged hospital stays. However, this accounted for only a small proportion of all infected individuals. In addition, as the survey was initiated in Zhejiang province and the majority of older adult participants were recruited from a community health care center in Hangzhou, Zhejiang, the study population may not be fully representative of the entire Chinese population. Compared to the overall Chinese population aged above 18 years, our sample included a higher proportion of females, middle-aged, and older adults, and individuals with higher educational level [39]. But based on a relatively large sample size, our main finding was that vaccination status was associated with clinical outcomes in a dose-response pattern, which was consistent with other studies conducted in other populations in different periods. Such evidence on consistency and dose-response effect supports the reliability of our findings. Therefore, while our study used a convenience sample of the target population, the findings were generalizable in interpretation in terms of the direction of the effects [40]. Future studies to recruit a nationally representative sample and to collect more information on socio-economic and geographic factors would be helpful for assessing potential effect modifiers of vaccine effectiveness.

Conclusions

In summary, our study provides novel real-world evidence that receiving three or more doses of vaccines seems to protect against adverse clinical outcomes, with the biggest effect size seen for hospitalization during this massive Omicron outbreak in China from December 2022 to February 2023. Older adults with multimorbidity remain at higher risk for severe clinical outcomes, thus requiring timely diagnosis and comprehensive treatment. These findings suggest that persistent efforts are needed to promote full vaccination and booster vaccination in older adults to ease the potential health service burden related to COVID-19.
