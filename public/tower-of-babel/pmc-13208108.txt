APKASS 2024 consensus statement on anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction, part Ⅲ: Return to play after anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction
Authors: Zuru Liang, Yuichi Hoshino, Gilbert Moatshe, Joon Ho Wang, Pakapon Issaragrisil, Stefan Mogos, Nadhaporn Saengpetch, Chanakarn Phornphutkul, Jin Goo Kim, Ryosuke Kuroda, Nattha Kulkamthorn, Hideyuki Koga, Shuhei Otsuki, Ryuichiro Akagi, Sang-Hak Lee, Karl Eriksson, Kyoung Ho Yoon, Bancha Chernchujit, Ekavit Keyurapan, Vasileios Raoulis, Rainer Siebold, Jonathan Patrick Ng, Patrick Shu-Hang Yung, Michael Tim-Yun Ong

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo establish a standardized set of criteria for Return to Play (RTP) after Anterior Cruciate Ligament (ACL) reconstruction based on a consensus among international experts.MethodsAn international group of ACL experts convened at the APKASS 2024 Consensus Meeting to address the variability in RTP criteria. Using a structured questionnaire, the experts evaluated six key areas: clinical assessment, functional testing, psychological factors, rehabilitation and reinjury prevention, surgery-related factors, and stakeholder insights. Data were analyzed using a predefined consensus threshold of 75% agreement.ResultsThe consensus identified several key recommendations for RTP, emphasizing the avoidance of purely time-based decision-making in favour of a more comprehensive approach that incorporates functional and psychological assessments, and sport-specific demands. Notable findings included the endorsement of manual stability tests (Lachman and Pivot Shift) as important components of evaluating knee stability, the critical role of psychological readiness, and the importance of a multifaceted approach involving various stakeholders in the RTP decision-making process.ConclusionThe APKASS 2024 Consensus Statement provides a comprehensive, expert consensus-based framework for RTP after ACL reconstruction and may help promote greater consistency in clinical practice. It highlights the need for a personalized approach that considers the unique recovery progress of athletes. Future research is encouraged to refine RTP protocols further and explore the integration of novel diagnostic and therapeutic technologies.Level of evidenceV (Expert opinion).Keywords: ACL reconstruction, Return to play, Consensus statement, Functional testing, Psychological readiness, Injury prevention

Introduction

Anterior Cruciate Ligament (ACL) injuries are among the most common and severe injuries in sports, significantly impacting an athlete's career and quality of life. The decision to return an athlete to play (RTP) after ACL reconstruction is complex and multifaceted, involving clinical, functional, and psychological assessments 1, 2, 3, 4. Despite numerous studies and guidelines, there remains substantial variability in RTP criteria and practices among healthcare professionals. This inconsistency can lead to varied outcomes for athletes, ranging from successful return to sport to increased risk of re-injury or premature end of sports careers.

The APKASS 2024 Consensus meeting aimed to provide expert consensus-based guidance on RTP criteria by integrating evidence-based practices with expert opinions from various disciplines involved in ACL treatment. The project gathered data through a comprehensive survey distributed among orthopaedic surgeons. The survey addressed key areas such as the role of clinical and functional assessments, psychological readiness, involvement of stakeholders in decision-making, and strategies for ACL rehabilitation and re-injury prevention.

This consensus meeting and the subsequent publication of its findings seek to bridge the gap in current RTP protocols by providing a clearer, more unified framework that can be adopted internationally. By doing so, the project hopes to enhance the safety and efficacy of sports medicine practices concerning ACL rehabilitation and reduce the incidence of re-injury among athletes. This article reports on the survey results, discusses the consensus reached among experts, and outlines recommendations for future practice and research in ACL injury management.

Methods

The expert panel for the ACL Consensus Meeting, held on October 24, 2024, at The Chinese University of Hong Kong, comprised 23 experienced orthopaedic surgeons specializing in knee sports injuries. This diverse group included 18 fellows from the Asia-Pacific Knee, Arthroscopy and Sports Medicine Society (APKASS) and 5 from the European Society of Sports Traumatology, Knee Surgery and Arthroscopy (ESSKA). These international clinical and research experts in ACL injury collaborated on a consensus-building initiative to address current controversies in ACL injury management and decisions related to RTP.

The advisory panel, comprising three postdoctoral research fellows (MW, ZL, MC), two experienced knee surgeons (JPN, MTYO), and a leading specialist in ACL injury management (PSHY), developed the survey questions and conducted a comprehensive literature review. The literature search, which excluded animal and cadaver studies, was performed using PubMed and Embase databases with search terms such as “anterior cruciate ligament,” “injury,” “rupture,” “return to sport,” and “return to play."

During the consensus meeting, the participants from ten countries collaborated on various RTP criteria through a structured paper questionnaire covering six domains, including clinical assessment criteria, functional testing, psychological factors, rehabilitation and reinjury prevention, surgery-related factors, and insights into current RTP criteria. Each section included multiple-choice items, checkboxes for multiple responses, and open-ended questions to collect qualitative data and specific insights. Participants indicated their level of agreement with the statements presented and selected all options that applied to their practices and opinions on RTP. Before survey administration, formal consensus was predefined as at least 75% agreement to ensure a reliable and well-supported agreement among this diverse group of experts.

Data from the survey were analyzed quantitatively and qualitatively. Quantitative responses were summarized using descriptive statistics to identify patterns and levels of consensus. Qualitative responses were analyzed thematically to extract deeper insights and inform discussion points for the consensus.

Discussion

APKASS ACL RTP consensus.

Section 1: clinical assessment criteria

Time post-surgery and RTP decision-making

A consensus was not achieved on a universal minimum time to return to RTP after ACL reconstruction. A group of experts (52.2%) recommend a minimum of 9 months to ensure adequate healing and reduce the risk of reinjury, consistent with evidence suggesting a 51% reduction in reinjury rates for each month RTP is delayed up to 9 months.5 However, some experts (34.8%) favour a shorter period of 6 months, and a few consider up to 12 months. Accordingly, RTP clearance should be individualized and based on objective criteria—including clinical assessment, functional performance testing, psychological readiness, concomitant procedures, and rehabilitation progression—rather than time alone. The diversity in opinions highlights the need for ongoing research to refine RTP timelines and validate objective clearance thresholds.

Abandoning purely time-based RTP models

A consensus was reached that purely time-based RTP clearance should be avoided (82.6% agreement). This indicates an agreement towards incorporating a more comprehensive set of criteria that includes functional and clinical assessments, psychological readiness, and sport-specific demands. Recovery after ACL reconstruction is highly individualized,6 with substantial variability in biological healing, neuromuscular control, and psychological readiness.4,7 While moving away from a time-only model, time remains relevant given graft healing and maturation,8 and delaying RTP to at least 9 months is associated with a substantially lower reinjury risk.5 Accordingly, time should be considered as one component within a multi-criterion RTP framework.

Stability assessments before RTP

A strong consensus was reached that routine stability assessment before RTP should include the Lachman test (95.7%) and the pivot-shift test (91.3%). The Lachman test, noted for its sensitivity and specificity in detecting ACL ruptures, is a cornerstone in clinical assessment protocols.9 Similarly, the Pivot shift test is highly regarded for its ability to demonstrate rotational knee stability, which is crucial for dynamic sports activities.10,11 Although instrumented laxity testing like the KT-1000 is less uniformly endorsed (60.9%), it is still significant for providing a quantitative measure of knee laxity 12, 13, 14. The integration of these tests represents a comprehensive approach to ensuring knee stability and functionality before clearing athletes for RTP, supporting a multifaceted evaluation strategy as recommended by contemporary research and clinical guidelines 15, 16, 17.

Multidisciplinary stakeholder involvement in RTP decisions

A consensus was reached that RTP decisions should involve multiple stakeholders: the patient (100%), parent/guardian for athletes under 18 (82.6%), surgeon (91.3%), team physician (82.6%), and physical therapist/athletic trainer (100%). This multidisciplinary approach supports a comprehensive evaluation of the athlete's readiness, incorporating medical safety and sport-specific demands 2,3,18, 19, 20. The involvement of parents for athletes under 18 aligns with ethical standards in healthcare, where parental consent and involvement are crucial in the medical decisions of underage athletes. While coaches are involved, according to 61% of experts, their role is seen as supportive, focusing on the athlete's technical readiness and integration into team activities. Although there might be concerns about potential conflicts of interest due to their commitment to team success,21 coaches are pivotal in sharing information on the athlete's performance as they return to practice, and their input to healthcare providers is valuable for assessing progress.20

Contextual factors influencing RTP decisions

A strong consensus was reached that RTP decisions should be adjusted based on contextual factors, including the type of sport and the level of competition (91.3% agreement). Factors like sport type (contact vs. non-contact) and competition level are crucial because they dictate the physical demands and risks associated with the sport.2 For instance, athletes engaged in high-contact sports may require more rigorous assessment and a cautious approach to RTP to mitigate risks of re-injury. Tailoring RTP strategies to these contextual factors is crucial for optimizing safety, performance, and long-term outcomes.

Impact of patient age on RTP decision-making

A consensus was not reached on how a patient's age should influence RTP decisions. The largest single group of respondents (39.1%) indicated a preference for extended rehabilitation periods for younger patients. This approach may be based on concerns about the long-term development and health of younger athletes, recognizing their ongoing growth and potential for developmental changes. Young athletes have a higher non-modifiable risk for reinjury after ACL reconstruction 22, 23, 24, 25. This risk is not only due to the physical demands of their sports but also to the higher likelihood of engaging in high-risk behaviours typical of sports that involve cutting, jumping, and pivoting movements.23,26 This demographic is more likely to return to their pre-injury level of sport 27, 28, 29, potentially increasing susceptibility to reinjury. Given these insights, it is evident that RTS strategies need to be tailored by age group. For younger athletes, extended rehabilitation might be preferable to address not only the immediate injury but also to account for their ongoing physical development and higher activity levels.

Section 2: functional testing

Functional testing and RTP

A unanimous consensus (100%) indicates that RTP testing should assess specific functional skills such as quality of movement, strength, range of motion, balance, and neuromuscular control. This comprehensive approach ensures athletes meet general physical standards and demonstrate specific capabilities required for their sport. Deficits in any of these areas can significantly increase the risk of reinjury.5 For instance, impaired balance and neuromuscular control are linked to higher rates of ACL injury 30, 31, 32. Similarly, an inadequate range of motion or strength asymmetries can predispose athletes to further injuries if unaddressed.30 While functional assessments are widely recommended in clinical practice 33, 34, 35, 36, implementation varies in both test selection and timing. This variability points to a need for more standardized approaches that can universally apply to different sports and injury types.

Primary criteria for RTP after ACL reconstruction

Experts advocate a multifaceted approach to RTP, emphasizing the integration of functional tests (100%), recovery timelines (95.7%), strength conditioning (95.7%), and sport-specific evaluations (82.6%). This broad-based assessment ensures a thorough readiness evaluation, aligning with literature highlighting functional performance as a critical indicator of recovery 33, 34, 35, 36. The consensus supports the importance of adequate healing time and muscle strength recovery before returning to play,30,37 while also factoring in sport-specific skills and a combination of objective and subjective clinical judgments.38 While important, the range of motion, neuromuscular control, psychological readiness, and patient/family education did not meet the consensus threshold. These findings suggest that while these factors are considered relevant by a majority, they do not form the core criteria for all practitioners. This may be due to varying emphasis on psychological and educational factors in different clinical practices or the subjective nature of these assessments.39

Strength assessment methods for RTP

A consensus was reached that isometric and isokinetic dynamometry should be used for strength assessment before RTP (91.3% agreement). These tests provide objective, quantitative data crucial for evaluating muscle strength, ensuring a safe and effective return to sports activities. Their ability to provide precise measurements of muscle force under standardized conditions contributes to high reliability, making them a frequent choice in clinical practice.40,41

Functional tests required before RTP

A unanimous consensus was reached on the necessity of single-leg hop tests before RTP (100% agreement). These tests are recognized for their effectiveness in assessing functional performance and symmetry between the injured and uninjured leg 42, 43. They are commonly used and recommended for determining readiness for sports activities and evaluating the risk of re-injury.15,34 Lower extremity strength tests also reached consensus (82.6%), highlighting their role in tracking strength recovery in muscles affected by ACL injuries. They are indicative of the ability to handle the physical demands of sports activities, thus reducing the risk of re-injury.34,38 Although frequently used, balance testing, sport-specific agility tests, and landing mechanics assessments did not meet the consensus threshold. These tests are valuable for assessing specific aspects of neuromuscular control and athletic readiness. Still, they might be considered less critical or too specialized depending on the sport or the athlete's level of competition. Their slightly lower usage rates could reflect variability in clinical settings or the specific demands of different sports.

Key benchmarks for rehabilitation progression and RTP readiness

A consensus was reached that standardized functional testing protocols (91.3%) and the Limb Symmetry Index (LSI) (82.6%) are key benchmarks for guiding rehabilitation and determining RTP readiness. Standardized Functional Testing Protocols are valued for their objectivity and ability to replicate sport-specific tasks, providing a reliable measure of an athlete's functional readiness.4,7 LSI, in particular, is used to evaluate the comparative strength and function of the injured limb against the uninjured limb, with a common threshold for RTP set at greater than 90%.5,44,45 Achievement of Sport-Specific Movement Patterns and Clinical Examination Findings are also commonly used (both 78.3%), emphasizing the importance of specific skills proficiency and health status as evaluated by health professionals.16 Other methods, such as Psychological Readiness Assessment and Time-Based Milestones, are frequently considered but do not meet the consensus criterion. Psychological readiness is increasingly recognized as important, yet it is less standardized compared to other measures.4,15

Defining successful RTP after ACL reconstruction

No consensus was reached on a single definition of successful RTP. The most frequently agreed-upon definitions include returning to the same sport at the preinjury level and patient satisfaction with their activity level (both 73.9%). Returning to the same sport at the preinjury level is often considered as the ideal outcome after ACL reconstruction. This definition implies not only physical recovery but also a restoration of preinjury skills and capabilities.3 Studies define successful RTP as returning to the same sporting level as preinjury, which is associated with higher satisfaction rates and a psychological sense of ‘full recovery’.46 The high importance placed on patient satisfaction reflects a patient-centered approach in modern rehabilitation. This outcome measure considers the patient's perspective on their recovery and is indicative of a broader understanding of success, which encompasses both physical and psychological well-being.47,48

Section 3: psychological factors

Assessing psychological readiness in RTP decisions

While a substantial majority (82.6%) of experts recognize the importance of psychological readiness in RTP decisions for ACL reconstruction patients, there is no consensus on a specific standard for psychological assessment. Most practitioners use validated tools like the ACL Return to Sport after Injury (ACL-RSI) Scale, Tampa Scale of Kinesiophobia, and the Knee Injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score. These tools are crucial for evaluating mental readiness49 but reflect a diverse array of practices, highlighting the need for more standardized guidelines to optimize RTP outcomes.

Crucial psychological factors for RTP after ACL reconstruction

A consensus was reached that confidence (91.3%) and fear of reinjury (87%) are pivotal psychological factors in RTP. Confidence is associated with a higher likelihood of returning to sport,50 while fear of reinjury is a notable barrier that can delay or prevent RTP.51,52 These factors emphasize the need for targeted psychological interventions to address both confidence and fear, enhancing the likelihood of a successful return to sport.15,53,54 While motivation, depression, and coping skills did not reach the consensus threshold, they still represent important components of the psychological landscape that influence RTP outcomes.

Addressing fear of reinjury during rehabilitation

A consensus was reached that fear of re-injury should be addressed during rehabilitation. Two main methods—counseling and education (78.3%), and gradual exposure to sport-specific activities (95.7%) receive significant support. Counseling and education help to mitigate fears by enhancing understanding and setting realistic expectations,55,56 while gradual exposure helps to rebuild physical strength and confidence in the injured limb, effectively reducing fear through controlled, progressive exposure.57

Recommended psychological assessments before RTP after ACL reconstruction

A consensus was not reached on a single psychological assessment tool. The ACL-RSI Scale, which assesses readiness for returning to sports, confidence, and fear of re-injury, nearly reached a consensus with 69.6% support. Although no single psychological assessment tool reached a consensus threshold, the ACL-RSI Scale is favoured for its specificity to ACL rehabilitation scenarios,52,58,59 indicating a need for further validation and potentially broader endorsement in clinical practice.

Section 4: rehabilitation and reinjury prevention

Emphasis on strength training during rehabilitation after ACL reconstruction

Experts reached a strong consensus (95.7%) on incorporating both quadriceps and hamstring strengthening in ACL rehabilitation. A balanced program targeting these complementary muscle groups—quadriceps for knee extension and hamstrings for knee flexion and restraint of anterior tibial translation—is fundamental for knee stability, successful RTP, and secondary injury prevention.4,60,61 Evidence indicates that comprehensive strengthening of both quadriceps and hamstring improves functional outcomes and reduces reinjury rates.62

Sports medical clearance before unrestricted training and competition after ACL reconstruction

Experts reached a strong consensus (95.7%) on the need for formal sports medicine clearance prior to unrestricted training and competition. Medical clearance ensures that athletes are physically and psychologically prepared to resume competitive sports, thereby significantly reducing the risk of re-injury.15 These evaluations are comprehensive, assessing not only physical readiness but also psychological factors, such as confidence and fear of reinjury.3,63

Essential components in an injury prevention programme before RTP after ACL reconstruction

Experts reached a consensus on including strength training (91.3%), agility training (87%), and plyometrics (82.6%) in injury prevention programmes prior to RTP. These components address various aspects of physical readiness and injury prevention: strength training improves muscle mass and neuromuscular control 60,64, 65, 66; agility training enhances quick, precise movements 66, 67, 68; and plyometrics increase joint stability and muscular power.67,69 While flexibility training fell slightly below the consensus threshold, it remains recognized for improving range of motion and reducing injury risk.70,71

Strategies for ongoing monitoring of potential Re-injury after RTP

Although no single post-RTP strategy for ongoing monitoring reached a consensus, a majority emphasized ongoing monitoring through strength testing, functional testing, and neuromuscular control evaluation. These methods help to detect re-injury risks by assessing muscle group strength,72,73 functional symmetry,73,74 and neuromuscular coordination 75, 76, 77. Regular neuromuscular training and continuous monitoring are recommended to identify and address any deficiencies that could lead to reinjury.76,78

Section 5: surgery-related factors

The role of extra-articular lateral tenodesis in RTP after ACL reconstruction

Experts reached a consensus (78.3% agreement) that extra-articular lateral tenodesis may be considered in certain cases to improve RTP outcomes. This surgical procedure is often considered for patients with high rotational knee laxity or participating in high-risk sports.79 It aims to provide additional lateral stability to the knee, crucial for preventing reinjury during dynamic activities.80 Extra-articular lateral tenodesis is recognized for improving biomechanical stability and potentially reducing the risk of reinjury post-ACL reconstruction.79,81,82 However, the decision to employ this technique should be based on individual patient's assessments, considering factors like knee laxity, activity level, and previous injuries.79 Future research should focus on refining patient selection criteria and further quantifying the benefits and risks associated with this procedure.

Influence of graft type on RTP decision making

A consensus was not reached on how graft type should influence RTP decision making, with the most common approach (56.5%) using the same RTP timeline regardless of graft type (autograft or allograft). The type of graft used in ACL reconstruction is a critical factor in post-operative rehabilitation and RTP timelines, though its influence on rehabilitation strategies varies.8,25,83,84 Bone-patellar tendon-bone (BPTB) autografts are often favoured for their strength and faster incorporation, which may support earlier RTP. In contrast, hamstring tendon (HT) autografts might require a more conservative timeline due to slower tissue-to-bone healing.85,86 A study found that ACL reconstruction using BTB autografts showed higher overall RTS rates than HT autografts 87, 88, 89. However, both graft types demonstrated similar rates for returning to preinjury performance levels and re-rupture rates. These findings highlight the diverse opinions and practices, emphasizing the need for further research to establish optimal RTP guidelines specific to different graft types.

Methods to enhance biological graft incorporation in ACL reconstruction

A consensus was not reached on the methods to enhance biological graft incorporation during ACL reconstruction to improve RTP, with 65.22% of experts still adhering to standard surgical techniques. Only a minority of experts reported using biological augmentation techniques such as PRP, stem cells, and bone marrow aspirate, which are investigated for their potential to accelerate healing and improve graft integration.8,83,90 Given the lack of consensus and mixed results in current literature, further research is necessary to evaluate the effectiveness of these advanced biological enhancement techniques.

Impact of concomitant Knee Injuries on RTP

A consensus was not reached on whether and how to modify RTP timelines in the presence of concomitant injuries. However, 69.57% of experts reported adjusting the RTP timeline based on concomitant injuries, such as meniscus repair, cartilage procedures, and MCL/LCL injuries. These injuries introduce complex variables into the rehabilitation and RTP process, necessitating extended healing times 91, 92, 93. Moreover, meniscus, cartilage and MCL injuries have been associated with lower RTS rates.91,94,95 Given the variability in the healing and functional recovery based on the type and extent of these additional injuries, further research is needed to explore how the concomitant injuries influence RTP decision-making and to identify strategies that optimize the RTP process.

Assessment methods for graft healing before RTP after ACL reconstruction

A consensus on a single method to assess graft healing before RTP was not reached, reflecting the diverse clinical practices. The most commonly reported methods are MRI assessment and clinical examination, reflecting a division in preference for imaging versus non-imaging techniques. While MRI provides detailed insights into graft characteristics,96 it does not necessarily predict clinical and functional outcomes, especially within the first year post-operation.97 Clinical examinations focus on symptomatic recovery and functional abilities, which are crucial for determining RTP readiness. This diversity in assessment methods highlights the need for a multifaceted approach that combines clinical assessments, functional testing, and imaging to make safe and effective return-to-sport decisions.

Section 6: insights regarding current RTP criteria

The survey results demonstrate evolving perspectives on RTP criteria after ACL reconstruction. There is a clear diversity of opinions, with no single perspective reaching a consensus on the adequacy of current guidelines. While a majority believes that existing RTP guidelines provide a solid foundation, they also express the need for updates and refinements to better align with recent advances in clinical practice and research. This suggests a shift towards more personalized and dynamic guidelines that can adapt to individual patient needs and incorporate the latest scientific findings.

Significant challenges identified in determining RTP criteria include the lack of standardized testing and variability in rehabilitation protocols. These challenges highlight the discrepancies in patient readiness assessments across different clinical settings and the diverse rehabilitation approaches that reflect varying philosophies, resources, and patient-specific factors. The absence of standardized testing and the variability in protocols complicate the creation of a consistent RTP pathway, underscoring the need for more uniform rehabilitation protocols and standardized, evidence-based testing methods.72 Addressing these challenges is essential for achieving consistent outcomes and safer RTP decisions.

Moreover, a consensus was reached on the need for further research in functional testing protocols (78.3%), whereas psychological readiness, long-term outcomes, and injury prevention strategies did not reach a consensus. This supports a multidisciplinary approach to refining RTP criteria, and current literature highlights inconsistencies in how functional tests are applied and interpreted, limiting their effectiveness for RTP decisions.72

This consensus should be interpreted in light of an important methodological limitation: it was derived from a single-round structured survey rather than a formal Delphi process. Accordingly, the findings are best viewed as expert opinion-based agreement and a practical framework to support RTP decision-making, rather than a fully refined or definitive consensus standard.

In conclusion, the responses highlight a field actively seeking improvement through updates to RTP guidelines, standardization of testing and rehabilitation protocols, and a multidisciplinary research approach. These efforts aim to enhance the specificity and reliability of RTP criteria, ultimately benefiting athletes recovering from ACL reconstruction by providing safer and more effective pathways back to sport.

Conclusion

The APKASS 2024 Consensus Statement on ACL Reconstruction represents a step toward improving consistency in RTP decision-making after surgery. This consensus, developed through collaboration among international experts and comprehensive analysis of existing practices and contemporary evidence, aims to enhance the safety, efficacy, and consistency of RTP protocols across varied clinical environments.

The consensus elucidates RTP decision-making's complex, multifaceted nature, weaving together clinical assessments, functional testing, psychological evaluations, sport-specific demands, and injury prevention. It recommends moving beyond traditional time-based models, advocating for a comprehensive, individualized approach that accounts for each athlete's specific recovery needs and readiness. Additionally, it highlights the significance of psychological factors, such as athlete confidence and fear of reinjury, proposing targeted interventions like counseling and gradual exposure to sport-specific activities to effectively manage these concerns. While recognizing the strides made, the consensus also identifies key areas where further research is essential. These include refining standardized functional testing protocols and evaluating tools for assessing psychological readiness. The potential integration of emerging technologies and biological assessments into the RTP criteria also demands more extensive exploration.

In summary, the APKASS 2024 Consensus on ACL Reconstruction provides a comprehensive, expert consensus-based framework for RTP, integrating the latest scientific evidence with practical clinical expertise. It sets a clear direction for future research and continuous improvement in the field and may help promote greater consistency in clinical practice. This consensus assists clinicians in making well-informed decisions while contributing to the goal of improving long-term health and performance outcomes for athletes after ACL reconstruction.
