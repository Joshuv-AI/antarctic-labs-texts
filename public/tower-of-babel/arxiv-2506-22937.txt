Tianrun Qiu, Changxin Chen, Sizhe Cheng, Yiming Yang, Yixiao Guo, Zhicong Lu, Yuxin Ma

Abstract
. Blind and low-vision (BLV) players encounter critical challenges in engaging with video games due to the inaccessibility of visual elements, difficulties in navigating interfaces, and limitations in sending interaction input. Moreover, the development of specialized accessibility features typically requires substantial programming effort and is often implemented on a game-by-game basis. To address these challenges, we introduce GamerAstra , a generalized accessibility framework that leverages a multi-agent design to facilitate access to video games for BLV players. It integrates multi-modal techniques including large language models and vision-language models, enabling interaction with games lacking native accessibility support. The framework further incorporates customizable assistance granularities to support varying degrees of visual impairment and enhances interface navigation through multiple input modalities. The evaluation through technical assessments and user studies indicate that GamerAstra effectively enhances playability and delivers a more immersive gaming experience for BLV players. These findings also underscore potential avenues for advancing intelligent accessibility frameworks in the gaming domain.

With the emergence of next-generation artificial intelligence (AI) models, particularly visual language models (VLMs), there is promise in exploring AI-human collaborative gaming assistance in the absence of official accessibility functionality support. Therefore, we introduce GamerAstra, a multi-agent AI framework designed to make games accessible without requiring programming skills or first-party support from game developers. GamerAstra encompasses four key features, realized through the collaborative efforts of ten agents with distinct functionalities:

(1)

Mental Map Building via Feedbacks from Multiple Agents
: The framework integrates feedback from multiple agents, including the State Detector, Image Recognizer, OCR, and VLMs, to enable BLV players to fully engage with the game’s narrative and visual elements, facilitating the construction of a mental map within the game. It also allows the system to adapt to varying levels of latency tolerance.

(2)

Enhanced Real-time Navigation and Complex Input Operation Support
: Our framework assists in identifying and interacting with UI elements, providing two interaction methods, i.e., keyboard navigation and hotkey actions, to accommodate different player preferences.

(3)

Generalizable Support for Diverse Games
: GamerAstra employs a multi-agent mechanism, utilizing technologies from lightweight image recognition to large vision-language models (VLMs), to perceive graphical content at varying levels of granularity. It supports new games without additional programming or official game developer support, achieved through configuration files for rapid adaptation.

(4)

Customizable Interaction Granularity for Various Impairment Degrees
: The framework offers tailored rules to address different granularity of players’ requirements ranging from low vision to complete blindness. This customization ensures a user-friendly and accessible experience for individuals with varying levels of visual impairment.

To demonstrate the effectiveness of the framework, the evaluation comprises two components: (1) a within-subject study involving eight BLV participants, which compares performance from baseline support tools to that achieved with GamerAstra, and (2) five quantitative evaluations to test the framework’s accuracy and adaptability. The results indicate that GamerAstra significantly enhances game playability, provides a more immersive gaming experience, and demonstrates robustness across multiple scenarios. Based on these findings, we explore future directions for developing an even more unified, intelligent, and fully real-time framework, which could further promote equity between BLV individuals and the general public within the gaming context. GamerAstra represents a significant step toward enabling BLV players to experience gaming on par with their sighted peers.

In summary, our contributions include the following:

•

A formative study that combined video analysis and interviews to identify six key accessibility challenges faced by BLV players.

•

A multi-agent accessibility framework,
GamerAstra
, that facilitates an inclusive gaming experience for BLV players without necessitating official game support or additional program development. Leveraging a multi-agent architecture, GamerAstra integrates four key features designed to enhance accessibility and engagement in game-play for BLV players.

•

A user-centric evaluation which includes a comparative study with BLV participants to highlight significant gains in playability and overall gaming experience, as well as a quantitative assessment to demonstrate the performance and adaptability across different games.

2.
Related-work

Our work builds upon recent research on BLV gaming and AI agents. In this section, we review key studies and outline our motivation based on their findings.

2.1.
Accessibility Applications using Large Language Models

With the rapid advancement of Large Language Models (LLMs) and Vision-Language Models (VLMs), along with their improved understanding capabilities, increasing research focuses on applying these technologies to enhance accessibility for BLV individuals.

2.1.1.
Accessibility for visual content understanding

Recent studies focus on leveraging these models to assist BLV individuals by providing detailed image descriptions, interpreting complex visual scenes, and enhancing access to visual information across various digital platforms. WorldScribe
(
Chang et al. 2024
)
offers context-aware live visual descriptions that progressively detail objects, while Immersive A/V
(
Jain et al. 2023
)
makes sports broadcasts accessible through sonification. VoxLens
(
Sharif et al. 2022
)
presents an interactive plug-in that leverages AI to make data visualizations more accessible. For videos, both NarrationBot-and-InfoBot
(
Stangl et al. 2023
)
and ShortScribe
(
Van Daele et al. 2024
)
focus on enhancing video content accessibility for BLV users, while DanmuA11y
(
Xu et al. 2025
)
focuses on transforming visually-centric barrages into accessible audio discussions, although both of them require pre-processing. While the former introduces a tandem visual dialogue system to provide richer, more interactive video descriptions, the latter uses models to generate hierarchical descriptions of short-form videos, helping users better understand and select content. Comics, which also pose unique accessibility challenges, are addressed by DCC
(
Kim et al. 2024
)
through dense captioning techniques.

Collectively, these works showcase the expanding role of advanced AI models in transforming visual content into accessible formats, empowering BLV users to engage more fully with visual media. However, the characteristics of being real-time, interface complexity, and the unfamiliarity of VLM with virtual environments hinder the existing work’s effectiveness in understanding games.

2.1.2.
Accessibility for performing visual tasks

Beyond understanding visual content, LLMs have also been instrumental in enabling BLV users to perform tasks traditionally requiring sight. GenAssist
(
Huh et al. 2023
)
allows BLV individuals to generate images using diffusion models, illustrating how AI can act as a “copilot” in completing visually demanding tasks. For editing videos, LAVE
(
Wang et al. 2024
)
leverages LLMs to assist BLV users with video retrieval, brainstorming, and sequencing. DesignChecker
(
Huh and Pavel 2024
)
aids BLV web developers in creating visually appealing websites, while A11yBoard
(
Zhang and Wobbrock 2022
)
makes digital art-boards accessible through gesture operations and multi-modal feedback. Similarly, VizAbility
(
Gorniak et al. 2024
)
enhances chart accessibility by integrating an LLM-based question-and-answer module, supporting BLV users to navigate and explore data more easily, while VisPhoto
(
Hirabayashi et al. 2023
)
improves photography with omnidirectional imaging and AI post-processing. Additionally, VisionTasker
(
Song et al. 2024
)
utilizes VLMs for mobile automation, though limited by slow speed and inefficacy in gaming operations.

These innovations show how AI tools increase BLV users’ independence, aiding in creative, professional, and daily activities. However, current methods lack real-time interactivity, immersion, and complex operation support for gaming, compounded by the absence of a control tree and insufficient precise coordinate control in VLMs.

3.
Formative Study

To better understand the specific challenges faced by BLV players, we carried out a formative study consisting of two key components: analyzing video contents and conducting one-on-one interviews. These processes explore how players currently navigate visual challenges, their preferred ways of interacting with assistive tools during game-play, and the additional support they would like to receive.

3.1.
Methods

3.1.1.
Video Analysis

A total of 50 videos were examined from 9 BLV players on YouTube
11
11

11

https://www.youtube.com
and 8 BLV players on BiliBili
12
12

12

https://www.bilibili.com
. To facilitate analysis, the YouTube players were labeled Y1 to Y9, while the BiliBili players were labeled B1 to B8. The videos provided insights into the strategies employed by BLV players and the accessibility challenges they encountered. The authors meticulously selected videos from these channels, focusing on videos that pertained to game experiences and reviews. The players’ critiques of the games as well as their reflections on past gaming experiences were extracted and documented from the results provided by the official speech-to-text service provided by the video websites. These data were then categorized and synthesized to identify the challenges.

3.1.2.
Questionnaire and Interview

To recruit potential participants for the interview, we distributed a questionnaire to members of two blind gaming communication groups, resulting in 15 valid responses (8 from low-vision players and 7 from blind players). The ages of the respondents ranged from 18 to 32 years (mean = 24.33, SD = 4.62), with an average gaming time of 28.8 hours per week (SD = 14.34, min = 10, max = 50). The questionnaire covered several key areas, including participants’ preferences in game genres, their preferred game control modalities, specific requirements for interface navigation functionalities, demands for visual descriptive content, challenges encountered during game-play, and the limitations of existing accessibility tools.

Among the valid responses, we successfully contacted 7 participants who expressed a willingness to participate in further one-on-one interviews (age mean = 24.86, SD = 3.64). Each participant received compensation equivalent to $15 in local currency. For the purpose of analysis, participants are referred to as P1 through P7 below. Of these, P3 and P5 are low-vision players, while P1, P2, P4, P6, and P7 are blind players. The interviews began by gathering participants’ personal background, e.g. age, visual impairment condition, and gaming duration, followed by inquiries into their primary games, use of accessibility tools, and identification of games with accessibility issues, including potential causes. We then asked their views on current accessibility limitations, particularly regarding controls, navigation, and visual interpretation. The session ended with an open discussion on broader gaming accessibility insights.

3.2.
General Findings

During the formative study, we gained deeper insights into the motivations and current status of the target players, which served to further solidify the key challenges and feature foundation for our framework design.

3.2.1.
Current Status

Firstly, although games tailored for BLV individuals do exist, they are relatively scarce and often do not match the quality of other titles (B1, B4, B7). These games, especially audio games, have also been criticized for failing to offer full game experience.

However, games that provide good support for BLV remain limited (Y1, B5), although many of BLV players have a passionate relationship with their games, and there are already many BLV players working as accessibility consultants (e.g. Y1, Y3, Y5) for game companies. The progress of BLV-adaptation has always been slow, given low profitability and high cost
(
Yuan et al. 2011
)
. Therefore, BLV players are also often required to discover whether a game is playable or not, and may be forced to become a
“game hacker”
(Y8,
(
Martinez et al. 2024
)
). Y2 expressed frustration with this situation, stating,
“We are ‘almost like a beggar and cannot be a chooser,’ because there are so few games accessible,”
and added,
“it is very rare to find a new game that I can play.”
Existing research also suggests that many BLV players feel disappointed due to the lack of accessibility support for the games they purchased
(
Martinez et al. 2024
)
.

Therefore, game experience of BLV players is limited, and can result in not being able to play popular video games with family and friends
(
Gonçalves et al. 2020
)
. Playing audio games with sighted friends is also not welcomed, as mentioned a quote in
(
Andrade et al. 2019
)
, “An audio game won’t appeal as much to sighted people”.

Moreover, although there are types of games that BLV players can play part of it, they may still need visual assistance from others (Y1, Y2, Y6, Y7, B2, B5,
(
Andrade et al. 2019
)
) and their independence is thus limited, which also reduce the level of interactivity and playfulness of the games
(
Gonçalves et al. 2023
)
. Assistance from sighted people is also often limited due to limited time availability, knowledge and sensitivity of their friends and relatives
(
Gonçalves et al. 2020
)
. At the same time, BLV players can be frustrated by not being able to play some parts of games (B1, B4), and this may even spark their sense of isolation (Y1).

To summarize, the limited availability of video games that BLV players can play smoothly without significant accessibility barriers and the dependency on sighted assistance in many gaming scenarios, where BLV players cannot independently navigate or interact with games due to inaccessible design.

3.2.2.
Motivation

By focusing on these issues, we seek to create a more inclusive environment that empowers BLV players to enjoy gaming. Currently, accessible support for games either requires direct official support or the existence of exposed interfaces and the involvement of developers with sufficient programming experience for mod development, as shown in Section
2.3
.

However, as the VLMs and other cutting-edge AI technologies becomes more mature and begins to be trained to play some simple games
(
Hu et al. 2024
)
, its ability to act as a copilot to help the BLV population play video games becomes worthy of being explored. Also as indicated by B3 and agreed by P1 and P4,
“Some content creators even write their own accessibility plugins, but these are usually targeted at specific games because they need to incorporate certain entities within those games. This leads to limited extensibility, a significant amount of work and requirement for proficient programming skills, so proposing a framework is necessary.”

Therefore, we aim to propose a universal and generalizable framework to address the extensibility issues for more video games that new games will be supported by merely writing prompts and adding image resources for scene comparison and analysis. Such a framework will also create opportunity to easily cater games simultaneously to satisfy players with different ability conditions, as suggested in
(
Gonçalves et al. 2023
)
.

3.3.
Key Challenges

After gathering findings that highlighted the potential value of an assistance framework, we further identify the specific challenges that this framework should address. Through analysis, we identified six major challenges faced by BLV players:

3.3.1.
C1: Inaccessible Game UI Components

Many participants highlighted the challenges posed by inaccessible user interfaces in games, particularly regarding that many games are completely incompatible with screen readers. Thus, menu navigation, selecting UI elements, clicking buttons, etc. in these games become inaccessible for the BLV crowd unless they memorize the combination of buttons used to accomplish each action with the help of a sighted friend. Y2 expressed their frustration:
“I have to memorize all the menus and sounds.”
This sentiment underscores the significant effort required to navigate complex game settings without adequate support. Worse still, this kind of memory is fragile, as
“once I accidentally press a key few more times, I will have no idea where it stops now”
(Y4). Y1 also noted that when trying to play a new game, he
“usually spent hours in the game just messing around with options,”
which is concluded by Y9 as
“guessing”
. P7 also indicated that not knowing whether an element is clickable makes him frustrated. Such experiences illustrate the trial-and-error nature of exploring game options, where players often feel lost and unsupported.

Moreover, when there are many image-based UI elements, games can become completely incomprehensible for BLV players. For example,
Black Myth: Wukong
uses a variety of icons to refer to skill points and clues, and these contents are completely inaccessible to visually-impaired players who can only use OCR (B1, B4), as agreed by all blind questionnaire respondents. The situation is even worse for more complex situations, as Y5 providing an example that
“[OCR] may list preference list items in a linear sequence and thus meaning you have a bunch of settings titles and another bunch of ‘on off 100 50’, which is not accessibility-ideal at all.”
Despite that, there are games where the positions of the operable buttons and the descriptions do not coincide, resulting in the inability to actually click them (P4, P6).

3.3.2.
C2: Lack of Real-time Feedback

BLV players struggle to access real-time updates, alerts, and notifications due to inaccessible interfaces. This forces them to mentally track game state changes, leading to cognitive overload and a diminished gaming experience. For example, Y1 mentioned such issue,
“Blind players have to take a lot of memorization in order to play,”
he said. Y3 and Y7 also suggested that the inability to listen to tutorial messages can make learning new games more challenging.

The need for real-time feedback is emphasized by the observation that players may miss crucial updates entirely. As Y5 mentioned,
“It would be much better if more audio cues were given to remind us to avoid attacks.”
Y6 also indicated that sound beacons should be introduced to enhance gaming experience. This demonstrates a clear gap in the current design. There are also games that have poorly developed narrator feature that may not read tool-tips, objectives or tutorial messages, or may only help navigate the initial menus (Y2 and B3). In addition, immediate feedback is also conducive to enhancing immersion, that
“a delay of just one second can have a significant impact on experience,”
according to P4. 80% of the questionnaire respondents believe that a faster response speed will essential for a good accessibility tool.

3.3.3.
C3: Difficulties in Spatial Operations

BLV players face significant barriers when performing tasks that require map navigation, precise alignment or drag-and-drop actions. For example, Y2 mentioned that
“for actions that require even a little bit more precision, I just get stuck.”
Y6 also highlights that,
“Games like World of Warcraft are hard because it needs mouse so I can only play with sighted friends.”
This problem has also been indicated in a survey
(
Gonçalves et al. 2023
)
, that BLV players often struggle with scenes that requires aligning elements precisely, e.g. aligning to interact with non-player characters (NPCs) in Stardew Valley, or finding exact entrance point of doors in Zelda, as supported by P1, P2 and 60% of questionnaire respondents. They also expressed a desire for more accessible audio helpers to help them understand game mechanics better. Current navigation often fails to process this, as Y3 states,
“Even when using navigation assistance mode, I still get stuck a lot.”
This may also be because some BLV players’ sense of space is weaker, as proposed by P4.

3.3.4.
C4: Barriers in Accessing Visual Elements with Complex Design

Many games rely heavily on visual elements, such as effects, cut scenes, and cosmetic features, which are inaccessible to BLV players. Complex visual designs, including color-coded indicators and graphical progress bars, further exacerbate the problem, as they cannot be interpreted without cues. This significantly diminishes the gaming experience for BLV players. As Y1 noted,
“Without good subtitles and audio cues, understanding of games can be limited.”
Meanwhile, Y5 expressed that
“not having audio description in games is disappointing because we do not have a sense of our surroundings and what characters are doing, which make the story incomplete.”
Y8 also agreed, stating that it is
“one of the most important undone things”
for accessibility tools, that
“we would not been able to enjoy the story without someone reading those things”
. 57% of questionnaire respondents also concur that
“the lack of visual cues affects the understanding of the plot.”

The granularity control of the audio feedback is also important, that if the feedback is overly simple, the BLV player may not understand why the operation is performed. For example, Gonçalves et al.
(
Gonçalves et al. 2023
)
pointed out that sometimes
“players knew they had to interact but often not knowing why”
. Moreover, although most (6 in 7) of the interviewees had never used or had not used extensively VLM-based image description tools for gaming, P1 has used some before and stated that the level of detail in descriptions was insufficient in complex scenarios, and thus could not satisfy him.

3.3.5.
C5: Excessive Assistance and Verbose Feedback

While current accessibility settings are designed to assist individuals who are blind or low-vision, their implementation may be excessively comprehensive, thus making them lose part of the gaming experience. As Y8 expressed,
“with accessibility you still want to make the player feel like doing something instead of feeling being helped.”
Y1 agreed on this, worrying that
“sometimes, accessibility settings make the game too easy”
. P1 also indicated that
“the existing scheme may interfere with the fun of gaming operations.”

Verbose feedback also limits BLV players’ gaming experience. Y2, P2, P3 and P5 pointed out that current OCR-based accessibility tools tend to provide verbose or repetitive responses, which sometimes even makes the results completely incomprehensible. This problem is particularly serious for low-vision players that 80% of them agree the that
“excessive feedback disrupts immersion,”
as mentioned by Y1. In addition, OCR may also output garbled characters, which frustrated P6 and P7. The issue of verbosity is further exacerbated by the inherent conflict between the two-dimensional nature of visual perception and the linear auditory channel in a one-dimensional manner. This discrepancy often leads to non-optimized audio reminders, resulting in auditory bandwidth limitations
(
Gonçalves et al. 2023
)
.

3.3.6.
C6: Insufficient Support for Low-Vision Players

Our study indicates that individuals with low vision generally require fewer, but more specifically tailored, forms of assistance compared to those who are blind. Current accessibility efforts often overlook low-vision players who are not completely blind, therefore, they face significant barriers due to the lack of tailored support in video games. B3, a player with cataract, often had to play like a completely blind person due to poor support for low-vision users. Y1, a player with light sense, also pointed out when playing a racing game,
“The racing line becomes invisible sometimes as someone who relies on color sense.”
Some low-vision players (P5 and 2 questionnaire respondents) even opt to disable built-in accessibility assistance in certain games, despite the inconvenience caused by visual impairment. They described these features as
“too noisy,”
likely due to excessively verbose auditory cues or feedback. This phenomenon highlights the need for more fine-grained and multi-leveled configuration design for accessibility features, offering differentiated support tailored to varying degrees of visual impairment.

4.
GamerAstra

To provide better assistance to video games without adequate accessibility support, we propose GamerAstra, a multi-agent framework for assisting game-play for BLV players. We first present an overview of the framework, including the functionality of the agents and their communication mechanisms, followed by the major user experience features supported by the framework. Additionally, a community contribution support tool is proposed to augment the overall functionality and effectiveness of the framework.

GamerAstra is a multi-agent framework where several agents work collaboratively, each specializing in a specific aspect. In the
detect

State Detector
,
OCR
, and
Item Recognizer
, to capture and interpret relevant information from the game environment. The State Detector continuously monitors the current conditions to trigger the OCR and Item Recognizer when predefined criteria are met. One is responsible for extracting textual data, while the other identifies key visual elements. Collectively, these outputs constitute the initial data stream, which is forwarded for subsequent analysis and processing.

Building on this input, during the
describe
phase, the
Change Comparator
analyzes changes in the data and identify ongoing user actions to activate the
VLM Describer
when specific instructions or contextual cues are detected. The
Voice Recognizer
can also be used to convey vocal questions to be answered using the VLM Describer. The VLM Describer then generates semantic feedback or descriptive interpretations based on output from the last phase, which are conveyed to the user through voice feedback.

Finally, the
act

Navigator
integrates results from the detect phase to simulate a user-friendly two-dimensional navigational interface that spatially maps actionable elements. During user engagement, these actions are further combined with hotkey inputs captured by the
Action Catcher
. The resulting fusion enables precise and context-aware in-game operations, thereby facilitating real-time decision-making and execution.

In addition to the core processing pipeline, the
support
modules, including the Game Adapter and Database Handler, operate concurrently to facilitate all stages of the process. These components are intricately integrated with the game’s internal systems, facilitating the efficient management of real-time data flow. They also provide adaptive prompts to the VLM Describer to enhance game-specific responsiveness, and dynamically retrieve and supply contextual data from the database to downstream agents. This coordinated architecture ensures continuous, real-time responsiveness across the
detect–describe–act cycle
.

4.2.
Feature 1: Mental Map Building via Feedbacks from Multiple Agents

In current practice, BLV players have limited or no accessibility assistance to play many games, which also prevents them from gaining a clearer understanding of the game, as stated in
C3, C4
. Therefore, our tool should provide appropriate descriptions that enable BLV players to explore visual elements, allowing them to better integrate into the plot and experience the emotions conveyed. Clear and vivid scene descriptions, real-time feedback, and real-time interpretation of visual cues may all help users construct a more comprehensive mental map of the game (
C2
). Furthermore, when users encounter uncertainties, the framework should function like a human co-pilot, offering interactive responses to questions and resolving doubts, thereby avoiding the drawbacks of traditional one-way information transmission, e.g. OCR.

In the early stages of implementation, we only utilized VLMs augmented with specially designed prompts, which can provide satisfiable explanations. But as highlighted by B3, P1, P4, P6, real-time feedback is essential to maintain immersion, while relying solely on VLMs leads to limitations in longer response time and possibly repetitive outputs, as showed in
C2
. Therefore, multiple feedback rates are provided, similar to WorldScribe
(
Chang et al. 2024
)
, together with human-evaluated real-time cues that do not require any processing time to further reduce latency and augment the system’s capability to emulate a human copilot.

To address this, we implemented a hybrid approach based on the SSIM
(
Wang et al. 2004
)
method to detect changes between screenshots cropped regularly to output with the necessary output:

•

No Audio for Minimal Changes
:
Audio prompts are suppressed when changes fall below a predefined threshold (
\Delta<\theta_{1}
), ensuring non-intrusive operation.

•

OCR-Only for Minor Changes
:
If changes satisfy
\theta_{1}\leq\Delta<\theta_{2}
, only instantaneous feedback using
OCR
and
Image Recognizer
is provided. This targets transient text modifications (e.g., dialogue boxes) while ignoring static background elements.

•

Localized VLM Analysis for Major Changes
:
For significant changes (
\Delta\geq\theta_{2}
), the latest and previous screenshots are compared. Differences are highlighted with red boxes on the latest screenshot, and a refined prompt directs the visual model to analyze only the content within boxes. Recent outputs from the visual model are also sent as historical context to further avoid redundancy.

Meanwhile, real-time interpretation of visual cues is equally important, which is achieved mainly via the real-time compare functionality. MobileNet
(
Sandler et al. 2019
)
is employed to ascertain the presence of specific visual cues on the game-play interface or to determine the current state of the game by comparing a cropped screenshot with saved interface slices, which typically uses about
2\text{ms}
for one round of comparison using Intel i7-11700 CPU. This approach enables BLV players to access real-time information conveyed through visual cues, requiring only that contributors provide the corresponding screenshot slices and the textual content to be announced upon triggering in advance. Moreover, this module can also serve as an indicator of state changes and can trigger the corresponding navigation content and hotkey list updates, which also facilitates mental map building via self-exploration.

Figure 3
.
Multiple types of feedback in SenrenBanka game, including responses of line changes, appearing of new characters, hotkeys and additional questions.

Moreover, given that our system owns multiple signal sources, information is systematically organized through a message queue simulating a bidding mechanism. The system prioritizes messages by recency and urgency, broadcasting the most relevant content while discarding outdated information, while evaluating the types of each message to determine whether it can be interrupted when necessary. Our methods also align with cognitive resource theory
(
Alister et al. 2024
)
and can mitigate cognitive overload.

Lastly, we have introduced two methods for BLV players to independently access additional information when feedback is insufficient, improving over the unidirectional information transmission of traditional tools to offer greater flexibility. First, users can offer voice queries, with transcribed text and screenshots processed by a VLM for precise responses. This process enables users to receive precise and contextually relevant responses, akin to having an human co-pilot by their side. Another approach involves hotkeys to retrieve specific details, as shown in section
4.3.2
, such as the last card played or current score.

This multi-faceted approach is capable to detect interface changes and ensure that outputs are fast, concise, vivid and non-intrusive to the players, as shown in Fig.
3
.

4.3.
Feature 2: Enhanced Real-time Navigation and Complex Input Operation Support

For BLV players, especially blind players, tasks like clicking buttons, executing menu selections (
C1, C4
), and performing actions requiring precise spatial awareness (
C3
) can be challenging. Meanwhile, not being able to perform complex actions themselves may result in over-assistance (
C5
). However, current VLMs have weak coordinate perception capabilities and cannot directly return precise coordinates in games, as unlike web pages or apps, games usually do not expose its elements tree, resulting in a lack of non-visual assistive information.

To solve this problem, P1 and P4 proposed middle-ware to connect to the navigation system of existing screen reader tools, while P3 and 73.3% of questionnaire respondents suggested creating a variety of shortcut keys to provide quick-access operations. Building upon these participant-derived insights, we have formulated a methodology using pure vision technologies, including pre-saved UI items for each scene, OpenCV
(
Olli-Pekka and OpenCV 2016
)
matching, OCR with coordinates, and YOLO
(
Jocher and Qiu 2024
)
, which can accurately identify the coordinates of buttons, UI elements, and core objects related to game operations, and can eliminate the need for trial-and-error or memorization to perform certain game actions. Moreover, since existing UI element annotation models always run very slowly and their output quality is not guaranteed
(
Wan et al. 2024
;
Hong et al. 2024
;
Wu et al. 2024
)
, we also built a pre-processing tool to semi-automatically parse all key UI elements and allow users to manually screen and correct them. Therefore, important UI elements can be added to the interface navigation feature in a real-time and error-free manner, which also makes text-less UI elements and items where the text and interaction area are not coincided accessible (P4, P6).

To utilize the information obtained from these sources, we propose two methods for user interaction, which are
keyboard arrow keys navigation
and
hotkey actions
to cater to the specific and divergent needs of different players on different occasions, as shown in Fig.

.

4.3.1.
Keyboard arrow keys navigation

Certain participants (P1, P4), emphasized that response speed is critical in their gaming experience. For these players, the ability to freely explore the user interface (UI) and interactive elements in real-time, akin to the functionality offered by screen readers, would likely yield a satisfactory gaming experience. The expectation put forward by P4 that
“what is needed is a seamless operation”
can therefore be met.

We built a virtual 2D navigation grid that dynamically integrates detected objects from all sources, allowing exploration via arrow keys through rows and columns, and to press
Enter
to execute the current item. According to the suggestion on P7, we made the navigation of page elements as consistent as possible with the original layout. Moreover, since
C3
shows that many BLV players lack spatial imagination ability, we add a reminder
“row [Current row index] [Total rows], column [Current column index] [Total columns]”
in every message to assist them while not being verbose, as supported by P6, and can even add blank placeholders to represent the exact spatial positional relationship when necessary. When objects detected in different agents overlaps, we keep items from the detection source that has higher credibility
(Saved UI > YOLO > Template Matching > OCR)
. In addition, when implementing, we found that when dialog boxes were present, OCR still recognized text information from the background, which caused the navigator to include elements actually non-interactive. To address this, a YOLO model was trained based on 12 dialog box images from supported games and 57 images from online sources to recognize dialog boxes and retain only the OCR results within the dialog box area, thus avoiding any impact on game understanding.

4.3.2.
Hotkey Actions

The research findings reveal that both P2, P3 and P6 identified keyboard shortcuts as the most efficient modality for executing routine operations. Moreover, MinecraftAccess and StardewAccess, as representative cases of accessibility modules, have both incorporated dozens of key bindings, which makes it believable that these hotkeys can enhance operational efficiency for advanced users.

Our system supports four types of hotkeys, which are:

•

Print Matching
:
Printing the result of related picture matching result via either YOLO / template matching.

•

VLM Describer
:
Using a specific prompt to give description on a given area via the current VLM, which can provide more detailed and focused result on the specific topic.

•

Coordinate Clicker
:
Clicking a predefined coordinate, typically some of the most important buttons on the current interface.

•

OCR Fetcher
:
Using OCR recognition method to fetch the text on a specific part on the interface in real-time, typically current score, current tools in use, etc.

The hotkeys are predefined in configurations to suit within specific interfaces inside the games and are designed to accept further additions from external contributors.

4.4.
Feature 3: Generalizable Support for Diverse Games

Current accessibility assistance in video games often requires either waiting for official updates or writing extensive code to create mods, to implement support. However, due to high development costs, games that are satisfiably accessible remains relatively scarce.

In comparison, GamerAstra offers a multi-agent framework that utilizes pure visual perception and VLMs, combined with game-specific prompts and other no-code customization modules, and is designed with future game support in mind to achieve better generalizability. Our system utilizes tailored configurations for controlling the functionalities of agents, along with pre-saved image resources for reminder messages and game state detection. This approach enables support for new games without mass amount of coding or any official support from the developers. Its structure is shown in Fig.
5
, while its full composition will be provided in Section
5.2
.

For each game, accessibility assistance providers can simply fill out a series of configurations to quickly enable accessibility adaptation. This also provides an opportunity for volunteers without programming experience but with a willingness to contribute, enabling them to develop accessibility features for new games and thereby promoting the advancement of game accessibility. Consider this scenario:
When a BLV player wishes to play a particular game, even if the developers have not planned for accessibility features, their family members or friends can independently provide the necessary support, ensuring an adequate gaming experience.
Moreover, when a new game lacks accessibility configurations, the
Game Adapter
agent will dynamically bridge this gap by employing LLMs to generate adaptive prompts and temporary configurations automatically, providing accessibility assistance even for unsupported games. Last but not least, considering tasks such as YOLO training and configuration editing, there may still be a certain cognitive burden for contributors who lacks computer skills. Therefore, we further propose an all-in-one tool that allows them to contribute in a fully interactive environment, which will be introduced in detail later.

4.5.
Feature 4: Customizable Interaction Granularity for Different Impairment Degrees

Based on our formative study, individuals with low vision often find accessibility plugins designed for fully blind users cumbersome and verbose, leading to reluctance in their use (
C5, C6
). Meanwhile, their degree of visual impairment prevents them from enjoying many types of games. Therefore, creating separate configuration files for both blind and low-vision players, while sharing a common set of basic prompts, is worth exploring. In our system, we have tailored two sets of rules for blind and low-vision users. Additionally, they can further customize these rules based on individual needs by nging the configuration manually, enabling a granular level of customization to tailor varying degrees of visual impairment and preserve the gaming challenge and fun, which is also considered by 4 questionnaire respondents (26.6%) as a major shortcoming of current accessibility tools.

Through interviews, we found that while almost every agent is meaningful in blind assistance mode, low-vision mode typically only requires these following agents:
Navigator
,
Describer
,
Item Recognizer
and
Voice Recognizer
. Moreover, since low-vision players are more capable to use a mouse, assisting them in using their mouse in the game is also essential. Similar to the practice applied by NVDA
(
Access 2025
)
while accessing common apps, a
Mouse Explorer
module specifically for low-vision players was implemented, which accesses output from OCR and Item Recognizer agent to support real-time reporting of the text or non-textual object that the mouse is currently near to in games. This is a typical example of assistance that only applies to low-vision players, whereas some accessibility mods, such as
Hearthstone Access
for
Hearthstone
, disables mouse input to support only keyboard access that the blind players utilize, which affects the convenience for low-vision players.

5.3.
Quantitative Performance Evaluation

This section presents a quantitative evaluation of our framework, focusing on accuracy, coverage of user intent, and system performance across diverse gaming scenarios in three distinct gaming environments that we support: Uno, SenrenBanka, and MySuika. The analysis leverages empirical data from model testing, user interface navigation, and comparative assessments of different operational modes.

5.3.1.
Detection Accuracy

We quantitatively evaluated the accuracy of core detection agents within the GamerAstra framework via three games using three core components: Dialog Detection (YOLO), Object Recognition (YOLO in MySuika fruit detection), and Template Matching (Uno card detection). A total of 57 dialog screenshots that are not included in the original training set (45 from Google searches, 12 from the three supported games), 160 fruit images (covering 9 categories), and 119 Uno game-play frames were tested. Accuracy was measured by successful detection rates and false positives, and the result is as follow:

•

Dialog Detection
: Achieved 100% accuracy on 12 game screenshots for supported games and 84.21% overall accuracy across 57 images, demonstrating robust generalization to unseen games. False positives were eliminated through strict non-dialog labeling.

•

Fruits Recognition
: Achieved 97.6% accuracy (160/164), while misclassifications (e.g. misclassify raspberry as an apple) occurred in 2 cases and another 2 wrong cases were caused by missing blueberries, which were very small.

•

Template Matching
: Attained 99.2% accuracy (118/119) in Uno card detection, with a single failure caused by color and character similarity (Blue-8 misidentified as Blue-3).

The above validates our system’s robustness for real-time gaming assistance, while strengths include low false positive rate and adaptability beyond static patterns.

5.3.2.
Coverage of User Intent

The supportability and executability of user intentions have always been key considerations. Therefore, we assessed intention coverage of our framework by testing navigation completeness and action execution in 45 different scenarios from: Uno (18), SenrenBanka (7), and MySuika (19). Tests evaluated whether all meaningful UI elements were detected and if user commands were correctly interpreted. The results are as follow:

•

UI Navigation
: 95.5% success rate (42/44), with failures in Uno due to delayed OCR processing and residual template matches.

•

Action Execution
: 98.5% success rate (66/67) in coordinate-based interactions. The failure case is due to inaccurate coordinates caused by fruit that is rotating too fast in MySuika. Other executions were all finished successfully.

GamerAstra demonstrates strong support for user intent, successfully identifying all UI navigation elements, responding seamlessly and executing user actions with high accuracy.

Comparison of Modes

To verify the importance of the external low-bar volunteer contribution database to our framework, we classified the completeness of game support on configuration into three levels. Additionally, we included the NVDA
(

(2)

(3)

We define “Understand” as the cognitive capacity to assimilate all requisite informational elements necessary for comprehending a game scene’s functional purpose, whereas “Execute” denotes the agent’s ability to freely actualize all designer-intended interactions within said scene with full cognitive awareness. Based on the results, the NVDA + OCR mode exhibits limited usability, while the General mode enhances usability in some interfaces, yet numerous non-operable elements persist due to the inability of VLMs to output accurate coordinates. The Self-learning General mode is capable of generating more accurate descriptions and accelerates VLM descriptions through stored data; however, it does not fundamentally resolve the issue of non-operability, resulting in no increase in successful scenarios. The Full Framework, by utilizing manually annotated UI elements and game configurations, supports all scenarios, achieves high playability and avoids cognitive interference.

In summary, tailored configurations and pre-established contexts remain pivotal in supporting a broader spectrum of games at present. By leveraging the contributions of accessibility volunteers, the existing system is already capable of assisting BLV players in engaging with a variety of games.

7.
Discussion

Our paper identifies key challenges for the BLV community in video gaming support. Our framework demonstrates the potential of a multi-agent approach to enhance video game accessibility, support more games, and help BLV individuals play like ordinary people, possibly even better. We will outline the next steps below.

7.1.
Potential for Supporting More Complex Games

The current framework demonstrates effectiveness in small and medium-sized games, yet modern AAA titles with open-world environments, intricate gaming mechanics and hundreds of roles and items are more complex. Since configuring these games requires more work, we have not conducted exhaustive tests yet. To address this, a
crowd-sourced ecosystem
could be further developed, where contributors collaboratively annotate game content (e.g. mapping terrain features, documenting roles and items’ properties, or scripting combat strategies) through an online platform, as exemplified by
YouDescribe

(
Pitcher-Cooper et al. 2023
)
, a crowd-sourced video description platform that helps BLV people. The platform can also further accelerate prompt engineering using AI techniques. However, data quality, privacy issues, and copyright issues of crowd-sourced annotation also require further discussion. To summarize, this paradigm shift from standalone assistance to community-powered accessibility could ultimately create a virtuous cycle, that as more players participate, the system’s capability to handle complex scenarios will also grow.

Moreover, path-finding in complex maps, which is not yet supported by the current framework, remains challenging using current technologies and requires future work, due to the limitations of VLMs and existing computer vision techniques in spatial perception and complex spatial understanding, as well as the high latency of VLM-based methods. This might become an important direction for future research. For example, U2 showed his strong desire for this feature during the evaluation.

7.2.
Exploring the Enhancement of Existing Accessibility Features

Currently, our framework is only tested and utilized for games completely without accessibility support. However, as suggested in C5, current accessibility support is also not satisfactory in several scenarios, limiting their gaming experience. Therefore, it will also be beneficial for them to also use the GamerAstra framework as a supplement. Moreover, as indicated in C6, existing accessibility modules also fail to satisfy low-vision players sometimes, forcing users to either endure inadequate adaptations or abandon assistive tools altogether, which underscores the necessity for customizable, multi-tiered visual assistance systems, and GamerAstra may also resolve this problem. Future work may focus on integrating GamerAstra with existing accessibility modules to enhance their functionality, ensuring seamless compatibility and user-centric design, which makes U5 and U6 extremely excited about it. U6 reported that as a blind person, he was unable to fully interact with certain elements in
Ace Attorney
and
The Invisible Guardian
, preventing him from completing these games. He hopes to use our tool to better explore them in the future.

7.3.
Towards an more Inclusive Future

To further enhancing accessibility in gaming for BLV players requires frameworks, prioritizing dynamic interaction, adaptive personalization, and emotive design might be worth delving into. First, current system may not react fast enough during fast-pace game-play scenes, e.g. fight scenes and racing scenes, due to the limitations of current techniques, while a more
dynamic system
using future techniques may further minimize latency to support these scenes. Low-vision players U4 and U8 have relatively fast operation speeds, thus they have higher expectations for such a fully real-time systems. Second, although our framework provides different profiles for both blind and low-vision people and supports manual configuration to suit one’s needs,
adaptive personalization
by employing machine learning approaches that tailor narration speed, cue complexity, and agent configuration to individual needs might also be worth exploring, as suggested by U6. Third,
emotive design
, including emotionally modulated synthetic voices and narration with emotions, may serve as an alternative to the emotional engagement that sighted players derive from visual effects to further enhance the emotional experience for BLV individuals, as shown in C4. It may also be beneficial to conduct longer-term observations to better optimize these features. This holistic approach may not only address functional barriers but also foster inclusive socio-emotional connections between BLV players and virtual worlds.

In the digital era, accessibility remains a formidable challenge. However, the ongoing attention from the HCI community and the advancements in the AI field are progressively enhancing the digital experience for the BLV population.

8.
Conclusion

We introduced
GamerAstra
, a multi-agent AI framework designed to enhance the accessibility of video games for BLV players, eliminating the need for programming skills or official developer support. Through a formative study, we identified six core challenges that impede gaming experiences for this community. GamerAstra effectively addresses these challenges by employing a multi-agent system that rapidly adapts to new games, provides tailored support for various levels of visual impairment, enhances navigation support, and integrates agent feedback to assist in mental model construction. Our evaluation, which includes comparative user studies and quantitative assessments, demonstrates that GamerAstra significantly improves gaming accessibility and user experience. These results underscore the potential of GamerAstra to transform gaming experiences for BLV players, making games more inclusive and enjoyable.
