On the fragility of laminar flow
Theodore D. Drivas, Daniel Ginsberg, Marc Nualart

Abstract
. Inviscid laminar flow is a stationary solution of the incompressible Euler equations whose streamlines foliate the fluid domain. Their structure on symmetric domains is rigid: all laminar flows occupying straight periodic channels are shear and on regular annuli they are circular [ 9 ] . Laminarity can persist to slight deformations of these domains provided the base flow is Arnold stable and non-stagnant (non-vanishing velocity) [ 4 ] . On the other hand, flows with trivial net momentum (and thus stagnate) break laminarity by developing islands (regions of contractible streamlines) on all non-flat periodic channels with up/down reflection symmetry [ 8 ] . Here, we show that stable steady states occupying generic channels or annuli and stagnate must have islands. Additionally, when the domain is close to symmetric, we characterize the size of the islands, showing that they scale as the square root of the boundary’s deviation from flat. Taken together, these results show that dynamically stable laminar flows are structurally unstable whenever they stagnate.



1.
Introduction

In this paper, we are interested in the existence of islands (regions of contractible streamlines) for steady fluid flow confined in periodic domains

(1.1)

D_{G,H}:=\{(x,y):\,x\in\mathbb{T},\,G(x)\leq y\leq H(x)\}.

Here,
G,H\in C^{2,\alpha}(\mathbb{T},\mathbb{R})
for
\alpha\in(0,1)
are any two boundary defining functions, with
G(x)<H(x)
, for all
x\in\mathbb{T}
.
The steady incompressible Euler equations (perfect fluids) read

(1.2)

\begin{cases}&\nabla^{\perp}\psi\cdot\nabla\omega=0\qquad\text{in}\qquad D_{G,H}\\
&\Delta\psi=\omega\qquad\qquad\ \ \text{in}\qquad D_{G,H}\\
&\partial_{\tau}\psi=0\qquad\qquad\ \ \text{on}\qquad\partial D_{G,H}\end{cases}

and
\partial_{\tau}
denotes the derivative tangent to
\partial D_{G,H}
.
There is a one parameter freedom in the above formulation which is fixed by prescribing
\int_{\partial D_{G,H}^{\mathsf{t}op}}\partial_{n}\psi{\rm d}\ell=\gamma
where
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
is a prescribed circulation, which is preserved on dynamical solutions in view of the Kelvin theorem. The circulation condition determines the cohomological character of the solution, e.g. the projection of
\psi
onto harmonic functions on the domain
D_{G,H}
. See
[
7
]
. A special but important class of solutions to (
1.2
) are those with a specified functional relationship between vorticity and streamfunction. Specifically, for any
F\in C^{1}
, and
c_{G},c_{H}\in\mathbb{R}
, solutions
\psi
to the following semi-linear elliptic problem

(1.3)

\begin{cases}\Delta\psi=F(\psi)&\text{ in }D_{G,H},\\
\psi(x,H(x))=c_{H}&\text{ for }x\in\mathbb{T},\\
\psi(x,G(x))=c_{G}&\text{ for }x\in\mathbb{T},\end{cases}

give rise to stationary configurations of the Euler equations in
D_{G,H}
, for some circulation
\gamma
.
We are interested in the structure of these steady states. In some cases, their streamlines
\{\psi={\rm const}\}
laminate the domain (see
[
4
]
and also
[
12
,
13
,
14
,
9
]
). In others, islands of contractible streamlines must exist (see
[
8
]
and also
[
6
,
16
]
).
The purpose of this paper is to show that stable stationary solutions
\psi
of the Euler equations confined to generic domains
D_{G,H}
possess islands.

Theorem 1.1
.

Fix
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
F\in C^{4}(\mathbb{R};\mathbb{R})
satisfying
F^{\prime}>-\lambda_{1}
, where
\lambda_{1}
is the first eigenvalue of the Dirichlet Laplacian in
D_{G,H}
. There exists an open dense set
\mathcal{B}\subset(C^{2,\alpha}(\mathbb{T}))^{2}
so that for each
(G,H)\in\mathcal{B}
, any solution
\psi
to (
1.3
) on
D_{G,H}
having stagnation points possess islands.

Thus, either the flow is laminar because the flow is non-stagnant
[
4
]
, or – if stagnation sets exist – then generically they must be centers of islands separated by hyperbolic stagnation points. We remark that the complement of
\mathcal{B}
is non-empty. Indeed, for any analytic
F
and analytic non-flat non-contractible curve
\Gamma_{0}
in
\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R}
, one can produce, by means of Cauchy-Kovalevskaya, laminar solutions
\psi
to (
1.3
) in
D_{\Gamma_{G},\Gamma_{H}}
such that
\nabla\psi=0
on
\Gamma_{0}
and
\Gamma_{G}
and
\Gamma_{H}
are non-flat level sets of
\psi_{0}
. Such a domain may be very thin but nevertheless provides examples of channels which support laminar flows with essentially any geometry. See also
[
10
, Theorem 4.12]
for such a construction for special
F
of finite regularity.

An important class of steady states for which Theorem
1.1
applies are Arnold stable steady states (see
[
2
,
1
]
) and highlights the drastic consequences of small boundary perturbations in stable fluid equilibria. Indeed, while any Arnold stable steady state in
\mathbb{T}\times[-1,1]
is a shear flow with closed non-contractible fluid trajectories, we show that arbitrarily small disturbances of the boundary induce a break in the streamline topology of Arnold stable equilibria with the appearance of islands. Note also that the existence of a stagnation point is guaranteed by Poincare’s last geometric theorem as soon as the boundary velocities point in different directions.

The ideas presented in this paper allow us also to conclude the appearance of islands for steady Euler solutions in generic annular domains posed in
\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R}
or also in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, no longer assuming that the boundaries are graphs in the periodic variable. Namely, steady Euler solutions that stagnate on generic annular domains must have islands. This extension is described briefly at the end of §
2
.

In some perturbative settings, we can understand more about the structure of the set
\mathcal{B}
. For instance, we consider small perturbations of the straight channel

(1.4)

G=-1+\varepsilon g,\quad H=1+\varepsilon h.

If furthermore
c_{H}=c_{G}
, then the set
\mathcal{B}
locally contains

(1.5)

\mathcal{B}^{\prime}=\{(-1+\varepsilon g,1+\varepsilon h)\ :(g,h)\in(C^{2,\alpha}(\mathbb{T}))^{2},\quad h^{\prime}(x)+g^{\prime}(x)\not\equiv 0\},

which is clearly open and dense in
(C^{2,\alpha}(\mathbb{T}))^{2}
nearby
(G,H)=(-1,1)
.

Once the existence of islands is ensured, a natural problem is to quantify their size in terms of the size of the boundary perturbation, which is of order
\varepsilon
. Since islands appear nearby local critical points of
\psi_{\varepsilon}
, they can be found to be arbitrarily small. Instead, we are interested in describing the maximal size they can reach in terms of
\varepsilon
. This is the purpose of our next result, which describes the maximal size of islands appearing in perturbations of the periodic channel
D_{-1,1}=\mathbb{T}\times(-1,1)
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
1.1
, there exists an open and dense set
\mathcal{B}_{0}\subset\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
so that for all
(g,h)\in\mathcal{B}_{0}
there exists
\varepsilon_{*}>0
such that the solution
\psi_{\varepsilon}
to (
1.3
) in
D_{-1+\varepsilon g,1+\varepsilon h}
possess islands whose maximal height is at least of order
\sqrt{\varepsilon}
, for all
0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{*}
.

The set
\mathcal{B}_{0}
contains boundary perturbations for which solutions to some elliptic equation have non-degenerate local maxima, and its precise description is postponed until Section
4
.

We now explain the heuristic reason for the generic existence of islands.
Consider any steady Euler solution
u=\nabla^{\perp}\psi
in
D_{G,H}
which has an interior critical point. If
\psi
does not have islands, then it is laminar and by Proposition
A.2
, its set of critical points is connected and belongs to some closed non-contractible level set
\Gamma_{0}
. In particular,
\nabla\psi=0
and
\psi=c
on
\Gamma_{0}
. If
\omega=F(\psi)
where
F
is analytic, then
\psi
is uniquely determined by
\Gamma_{0}
by the Cauchy-Kovalevskaya theorem. See
[
10
]
where such a construction is carried out to obtain laminar steady Euler solutions emanating from a singular streamline. Since the level sets of
\psi
are assumed to foliate the domain, they must do so monotonically on each side of
\Gamma_{0}
and at some point they must meet the top and bottom boundaries. If the boundaries do not coincide with level sets of
\psi
, then
\psi
has islands.

Since
\psi
is uniquely determined by the curve
\Gamma_{0}
,
c\in\mathbb{R}
and
F
, one would conjecture that there is a one-to-one correspondence between the singular streamline
\Gamma_{0}
and the boundary defining functions
(G,H)
for which the level sets
\{\psi=c_{G}\}
and
\{\psi=c_{H}\}
coincides with the domain boundaries, so that the image of the map
\Gamma_{0}\mapsto(\{\psi=c_{G}\},\{\psi=c_{H}\})
is nowhere dense. Indeed, there is one function-worth degree of freedom in
\Gamma_{0}
, but two defining functions
H
and
G
of the boundaries. In this regard, the image of the map has empty intersection with the set of boundary perturbations where one of them is constant, see Proposition
A.1
.
Likewise, Lemma
3.1
shows that for all
(g,h)\in\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
, the pair
(-1-\varepsilon g,1+\varepsilon h)
is not in the image of the map
\Gamma_{0}\mapsto(\{\psi=c_{G}\},\{\psi=c_{H}\})
, for
\varepsilon>0
small enough.

By continuity, the image of the map is closed, and hence it is nowhere dense if it has empty interior. Then, for any analytic
(G,H)
and
F
for which the solution
\psi
to (
1.3
) does not have islands, it is enough to show the existence of some
(g,h)
for which the solution
\psi_{\varepsilon}
of (
1.3
) in
D_{G+\varepsilon g,H+\varepsilon h}
develops islands, for
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small. This is precisely the main idea behind Theorem
1.1
and consists of finding the solution
\psi_{\varepsilon}
as the sum of
\psi
, a first correction of size
\varepsilon
and a second correction of size
\varepsilon^{2}
. The first order correction solves a linear equation in
D_{G,H}
with boundary conditions linearly prescribed by the pair
(g,h)
, see (
2.4
), and must be constant along
\Gamma_{0}
if
\psi_{\varepsilon}
does not have islands. Thus, finding an instance of
(g,h)
for which the first order correction is non-constant along
\Gamma_{0}
ensures the existence of islands for
\psi_{\varepsilon}
and proves that the image of the map is nowhere dense.

The outline of the paper is as follows. Section
2
provides some general criterion for the existence of islands, and applies them to prove Theorem
1.1
. Section
3
gives a proof in the case of small perturbations of the flat channel, where more information about the generic set of boundaries can be provided. Section
4
establishes a lower bound on the maximal height of the islands, proving Theorem
1.2
. Appendix
A
gives some criteria for existence of islands in some definite cases. Appendix
B
contains some technical estimates for estimating a remainder on an asymptotic expansion used to prove Theorem
1.1
.

2.
Islands for generic domains

In this section we show Theorem
1.1
. The first part of the section is devoted to proving the generic appearance of islands in
D_{\varepsilon}:=D_{G+\varepsilon g,H+\varepsilon h}
if they are absent in
D_{0}:=D_{G,H}
. Throughout the section, unless stated otherwise, we assume
\psi_{0}
is a laminar non-constant solution to (
1.3
). Let
\Gamma_{0}
be the unique (otherwise Proposition
A.2
applies) streamline of
\psi_{0}
on which
\nabla\psi_{0}=0
. Our first result shows that
\partial_{y}\psi_{0}
(and thus the fluid velocity) is not identically zero on the boundaries.

Lemma 2.1
.

If
\psi_{0}
is laminar, then
\partial_{y}\psi_{0}\not\equiv 0
on each connected component of
\partial D_{G,H}
.

Proof.

Assume
\partial_{y}\psi_{0}(x,G(x))=0
for all
x\in\mathbb{T}
. Since
\psi_{0}(x,G(x))=c_{G}
, we deduce that
\nabla\psi(x,G(x))\equiv 0
. Then,
\psi_{0}
is laminar and solves

(2.1)

\begin{cases}\Delta\psi_{0}=F(\psi_{0})&\text{ in }D_{G,H},\\
\psi_{0}=c_{0},\quad\nabla\psi_{0}=0&\text{ on }\Gamma_{0},\\
\psi_{0}(x,G(x))=c_{G},\quad\nabla\psi_{0}(x,G(x))=0&\text{ for }x\in\mathbb{T}\end{cases}

Hence, from Corollary 1.1 in
[
9
]
we deduce that
\psi_{0}
is a shear flow, that
G(x)=G_{0}
and
\Gamma_{0}=\{(x,y_{0}):x\in\mathbb{T}\}
for some
G_{0}<y_{0}<H(x)
, for some constant
G_{0}<\min H(x)
. By elliptic stability of
F
and
\nabla\psi_{0}=0
on
\partial D_{G_{0},\Gamma_{0}}
, we deduce that
\nabla\psi_{0}=0
in
D_{G_{0},\Gamma_{0}}
. Hence,
\psi_{0}
is constant in
D_{G_{0},\Gamma_{0}}
. Moreover, we see that either
H^{\prime}\equiv 0
or
\psi_{0}
is constant on
D_{G,H}
by using Lemma 3 in
[
8
]
. Since
\psi_{0}
is assumed non-constant on
D_{G,H}
, we have that
H=H_{0}
for some
H_{0}>G_{0}
. Then,
\psi_{0}(x,y)=\psi_{0}(y)
due to
\psi_{0}(x,H_{0})=c_{H}
and the elliptic stability of
F
. Therefore,
\psi_{0}(y)
satisfies
\psi_{0}^{\prime\prime}(y)=F(\psi_{0}(y))
, with
\psi_{0}(y_{0})=c_{0}=c_{G}
and
\psi_{0}^{\prime}(y_{0})=\psi_{0}^{\prime\prime}(y_{0})=0
due to continuity and
\psi_{0}=c_{G}
in
D_{G,\Gamma_{0}}
. Thus,
F(c_{0})=0
and by the uniqueness of solutions to the ODE we conclude that
\psi_{0}=c_{G}
is constant, reaching a contradiction.
∎

Next, we parametrize
\Gamma_{0}
by a smooth graph. For this, we first need to show that

Let

Hence,

Now, since

by a smooth curve, confer

Since
\nabla\psi_{0}(x,y_{0}(x))

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\partial_{x}^{2}\psi_{0}(x,y_{0}(x))+y_{0}^{\prime}(x)\partial_{xy}^{2}\psi_{0}(x,y_{0}(x)),

\partial_{x}^{2}\psi_{0}(x,y_{0}(x))=0

Asymptotic expansion of the streamfunction

In this subsection we further investigate the solution
\psi_{\varepsilon}
to (
1.3
) posed in
D_{G+\varepsilon g,H+\varepsilon h}
. The following result shows that
\psi_{\varepsilon}
is a linear in
\varepsilon
perturbation of

, uniquely determined by the boundary perturbations

, together with a correction term that is

small.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
(g,h)\in(C^{2,\alpha}(\mathbb{T}))^{2}
. There exists
\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that for all
0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{0}
there exists a unique solution
\psi_{\varepsilon}:D_{\varepsilon}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
to (

) in

. Moreover, there exist functions

with

The proof of Theorem

consists of finding the solution

to (

given by a precise first order approximation together with a correction that is

\begin{cases}\left(\Delta-F^{\prime}(\psi_{0})\right)\varphi=0&\text{ in }D_{0}\\

\varphi(x,G(x))=-g(x)\partial_{y}\psi_{0}(x,G(x))&\text{ for }x\in\mathbb{T}.\\
\end{cases}

To define
\psi_{\varepsilon}
as in (

), since
D_{\varepsilon}
may not be contained in
D_{0}
,
we first extend
\psi_{0}
and
\varphi

from
D_{0}
to a slightly larger region containing both

and
D_{\varepsilon}
. Since

, this can be done using a standard extension operator which may be found in
[

. We fix an open set
\widetilde{D_{0}}\Supset\overline{D_{0}}
so that for

small enough,

\overline{D_{\varepsilon}}\Subset{\widetilde{D}_{0}}
. There are

\widetilde{\psi}_{0}
and
\widetilde{\varphi}
of
\psi_{0}
and

defined on

\widetilde{D}_{0}
, coinciding with
\psi_{0}
and

on

, so that
\|\widetilde{\psi}_{0}\|_{C^{3}(\widetilde{D_{0}})}\lesssim\|\psi_{0}\|_{C^{3}(D_{0})}

. In particular, they are defined on

2.2.
Generic appearance of islands

We first
show that any singular streamline
\Gamma_{\varepsilon}
of
\psi_{\varepsilon}
on which
\nabla\psi_{\varepsilon}=0
must be
\varepsilon
close to the singular streamline
\Gamma_{0}
of
\psi_{0}
.

Lemma 2.3
.

For
\varepsilon>0
small enough, any singular streamline
\Gamma_{\varepsilon}
of
\psi_{\varepsilon}
is given by a graph in
x
, that is, there exists some
y_{\varepsilon}\in C^{1}(\mathbb{T})
such that

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\varepsilon}=\{(x,y_{\varepsilon}(x)):\,x\in\mathbb{T}\}.

Moreover, there holds
\|y_{\varepsilon}-y_{0}\|_{C^{1}(\mathbb{T})}\lesssim\varepsilon
.

Proof.

We begin by recalling that
F(c_{0})=\Delta\psi_{0}|_{\Gamma_{0}}=\partial_{y}^{2}(x,y_{0}(x))\left(1+(y_{0}^{\prime}(x))^{2}\right)\neq 0
and assume without loss of generality that
F_{0}:=F(c_{0})<0
, so that
\partial_{y}^{2}\psi_{0}(x,y_{0}(x))<0
for all

. By uniform continuity, we have that
\partial_{y}\psi_{0}(x,y)\leq\frac{F_{0}}{2}
for all

and all

, for some
\delta_{0}>0
sufficiently small. In particular, since

\displaystyle\partial_{y}\psi_{0}(x,y_{0}(x)+\delta)=\delta\int_{0}^{1}\partial_{y}^{2}\psi_{0}(x,y_{0}(x)+s\delta){\rm d}s,

for all
\delta\in\mathbb{R}
, we conclude that
\partial_{y}\psi_{0}(x,y_{0}(x)+\delta)\leq\delta\frac{F_{0}}{2}<0
and
\partial_{y}^{2}\psi_{0}(x,y_{0}(x)-\delta)>-\delta\frac{F_{0}}{2}
, for all
0<\delta<\delta_{0}
. Hence, for any fixed
\delta\in(0,\delta_{0})
we observe that

\displaystyle\partial_{y}\psi_{\varepsilon}(x,y_{0}(x)+\delta)

\displaystyle=\partial_{y}\psi_{0}(x,y_{0}(x)+\delta)+\left(\varepsilon\partial_{y}\varphi+\partial_{y}r_{\varepsilon}\right)(x,y_{0}(x)+\delta)

\displaystyle\leq\delta\frac{F_{0}}{2}-\varepsilon\|\varphi\|_{C^{1}(D_{\varepsilon})}-\|r_{\varepsilon}\|_{C^{1}(D_{\varepsilon})}<0

for
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small,
where
r_{\varepsilon}
is a remainder defined in (
2.3
). Likewise, we have
\partial_{y}\psi_{\varepsilon}(x,y_{0}(x)-\delta)>0
. In particular, this shows that for all
\varepsilon>0
small enough, for all
x\in\mathbb{T}
, there exists
y_{\varepsilon}(x)\in(y_{0}(x)-\delta,y_{0}(x)+\delta)
such that
\partial_{y}\psi_{\varepsilon}(x,y_{\varepsilon}(x))=0
. Since
\partial_{y}^{2}\psi_{\varepsilon}(x,y)=\partial_{y}^{2}\psi_{0}(x,y_{0}(x))+O(\varepsilon)<0
for all
x\in\mathbb{T}
and all
|y-y_{0}(x)|<\delta_{0}
, for
\varepsilon
sufficiently small, by the implicit function theorem we conclude that the mapping
x\mapsto(x,y_{\varepsilon}(x))
is a unique,
2\pi
-periodic and
C^{1}
curve, with
\|y_{\varepsilon}(x)-y_{0}(x)\|_{C(\mathbb{T})}\leq\delta
.

In fact, once we know
\|y_{\varepsilon}(x)-y_{0}(x)\|_{C(\mathbb{T})}\leq\delta
for all
\delta\in(0,\delta_{0})
by taking
\varepsilon>0
small enough, we can improve it to
\|y_{\varepsilon}(x)-y_{0}(x)\|_{C^{1}(\mathbb{T})}\lesssim\varepsilon
. Indeed, for all
x\in\mathbb{T}
we have that

(2.6)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\partial_{y}\psi_{\varepsilon}(x,y_{\varepsilon}(x))=\partial_{y}\psi_{0}(x,y_{\varepsilon}(x))+\varepsilon\partial_{y}\varphi(x,y_{\varepsilon}(x))+\partial_{y}r_{\varepsilon}(x,y_{\varepsilon}(x))

where further

(2.7)

\displaystyle\partial_{y}\psi_{0}(x,y_{\varepsilon}(x))=(y_{\varepsilon}(x)-y_{0}(x))\int_{0}^{1}(\partial_{y}^{2}\psi_{0})(x,y_{0}(x)+t(y_{\varepsilon}(x)-y_{0}(x))){\rm d}t.

Since
\partial_{y}^{2}\psi_{0}(x,y_{0}(x))\neq 0
, by uniform continuity we have that
\partial_{y}^{2}\psi_{0}(x,y)\neq 0
for all
|y-y_{0}(x)|\leq\delta_{0}
, for some
\delta_{0}>0
. For
\varepsilon
small enough such that
\|y_{\varepsilon}(x)-y_{0}(x)\|_{C(\mathbb{T})}\leq\delta_{0}
, we then have

\displaystyle\|y_{\varepsilon}(x)-y_{0}(x)\|_{C(\mathbb{T})}\lesssim\varepsilon\|\varphi\|_{C^{1}(D_{\varepsilon})}+\|r_{\varepsilon}\|_{C^{1}(D_{\varepsilon})}\lesssim\varepsilon.

Similarly, applying
\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}x}
to (
2.6
) and (
2.7
) we have that

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=(y_{\varepsilon}(x)-y_{0}(x))^{\prime}\int_{0}^{1}(\partial_{y}^{2}\psi_{0})(x,y_{0}(x)+t(y_{\varepsilon}(x)-y_{0}(x))){\rm d}t

\displaystyle\quad+(y_{\varepsilon}(x)-y_{0}(x))^{\prime}(y_{\varepsilon}(x)-y_{0}(x))\int_{0}^{1}(\partial_{y}^{2}\psi_{0})(x,y_{0}(x)+t(y_{\varepsilon}(x)-y_{0}(x))){\rm d}t

\displaystyle\quad+(y_{\varepsilon}(x)-y_{0}(x))\int_{0}^{1}(\partial_{xyy}^{3}\psi_{0}+(y_{0}^{\prime}(x))\partial_{y}^{3}\psi_{0})(x,y_{0}(x)+t(y_{\varepsilon}(x)-y_{0}(x))){\rm d}t

\displaystyle\quad+(y_{\varepsilon}(x)-y_{0}(x))^{\prime}\left(\varepsilon\partial_{y}\varphi(x,y_{\varepsilon}(x))+\partial_{y}r_{\varepsilon}(x,y_{\varepsilon}(x))\right)

\displaystyle\quad+(\partial_{x}+y_{0}^{\prime}(x)\partial_{y})\left(\varepsilon\partial_{y}\varphi(x,y_{\varepsilon}(x))+\partial_{y}r_{\varepsilon}(x,y_{\varepsilon}(x))\right).

Since
\|y_{\varepsilon}-y_{0}\|_{C(\mathbb{T})}\lesssim\varepsilon
and
\varepsilon\|\varphi\|_{C^{2}(D_{\varepsilon})}+\|r_{\varepsilon}\|_{C^{2}(D_{\varepsilon})}\lesssim\varepsilon
, taking
\varepsilon
small enough we conclude that
\|y_{\varepsilon}-y_{0}\|_{C^{1}(\mathbb{T})}\lesssim\varepsilon
. Finally, let
\Gamma_{\varepsilon}:=\{(x,y_{\varepsilon}(x)):x\in\mathbb{T}\}
and note that
\partial_{y}\psi_{\varepsilon}|_{\Gamma_{\varepsilon}}=0
and
\psi_{\varepsilon}
is periodic on
\Gamma_{\varepsilon}
. Hence, there exists
x_{0}\in\mathbb{T}
for which
\nabla\psi_{\varepsilon}(x_{0},y_{\varepsilon}(x_{0}))=0
. If
\psi_{\varepsilon}
is laminar, then
\nabla\psi_{\varepsilon}=0
on
\Gamma_{\varepsilon}
.
∎

We shall now see that if
\psi_{\varepsilon}
does not develop islands for any sufficiently small boundary perturbation
(G+\varepsilon g,H+\varepsilon h)
, the associated first order correction
\varphi=\varphi_{g,h}
that solves (

) must be constant on the singular streamline
\Gamma_{0}
of
\psi_{0}
.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
\{\varepsilon_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}\subset(0,1)
any sequence for which
\varepsilon_{n}\rightarrow 0
as
n\rightarrow\infty
and
\psi_{\varepsilon_{n}}
does not have islands. Then
\varphi
is constant on
\Gamma_{0}
.

Proof.

Let
\varepsilon>0
and assume that
\psi_{\varepsilon}
does not have islands. Then it is laminar and by Lemma
2.3
there exists a singular streamline
\Gamma_{\varepsilon}=\{(x,y_{\varepsilon}(x)):\,x\in\mathbb{T}\}
of
\psi_{\varepsilon}
, with
y_{\varepsilon}\in C^{1}(\mathbb{T})
and
\|y_{\varepsilon}-y_{0}\|_{C^{1}(\mathbb{T})}\lesssim\varepsilon
, where
\Gamma_{0}=\{(x,y_{0}(x)):\,x\in\mathbb{T}\}
denotes the unique singular streamline of
\psi_{0}
. Since
\psi_{\varepsilon}
is constant along
\Gamma_{\varepsilon}
, we deduce that

\displaystyle 0=\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}x}\psi_{\varepsilon}(x,y_{\varepsilon}(x))=\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}x}\left(\psi_{0}(x,y_{\varepsilon}(x))+\varepsilon\varphi(x,y_{\varepsilon}(x))+r_{\varepsilon}(x,y_{\varepsilon}(x))\right)

where further

\displaystyle\psi_{0}(x,y_{\varepsilon}(x))

\displaystyle=\psi_{0}(x,y_{0}(x))+\partial_{y}\psi_{0}(x,y_{0}(x))(y_{\varepsilon}(x)-y_{0}(x))

\displaystyle\quad+(y_{\varepsilon}(x)-y_{0}(x))^{2}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}t(\partial_{y}^{2}\psi_{0})(x,y_{0}(x)+st(y_{\varepsilon}(x)-y_{0}(x))){\rm d}s{\rm d}t

and also

\displaystyle\varphi(x,y_{\varepsilon}(x))=\varphi(x,y_{0}(x))+(y_{\varepsilon}(x)-y_{0}(x))\int_{0}^{1}(\partial_{y}\varphi)(x,y_{0}(x)+t(y_{\varepsilon}(x)-y_{0}(x)){\rm d}t.

Since
\psi_{0}(x,y_{0}(x))
is constant and
\partial_{y}\psi_{0}(x,y_{0}(x))=0
for all
x\in\mathbb{T}
, together with
\|y_{\varepsilon}-y_{0}\|_{C^{1}(\mathbb{T})}\lesssim\varepsilon
, we conclude that

\displaystyle 0=\varepsilon\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}x}\varphi(x,y_{0}(x))+O(\varepsilon^{2})

Therefore, for any sequence
\varepsilon_{n}\rightarrow 0
for which
\psi_{\varepsilon_{n}}
does not have islands, we must have
\varphi
constant along
\Gamma_{0}
.
∎

Once we have established that
\varphi
being non-constant along
\Gamma_{0}
gives rise to islands for
\psi_{\varepsilon}
for
\varepsilon>0
small enough, we next investigate the set of boundary perturbations
(g,h)
to
(G,H)
for which the solution
\varphi=\varphi_{g,h}
to (
2.4
) is not constant on
\Gamma_{0}
. For this, given
(g,h),\,(G,H)\in(C^{2,\alpha}(\mathbb{T}))^{2}
, and the solution
\varphi_{g,h}
to (
2.4
) posed in
D_{G,H}
with boundary values
(g,h)
, we introduce

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{G,H}=\{(g,h)\in(C^{2,\alpha}(\mathbb{T}))^{2}:\varphi_{g,h}|_{\Gamma_{0}}\neq\text{const}\}

We show that any solution
\psi_{0}
in
D_{G,H}
is an arbitrarily small perturbation away from developing islands.

Lemma 2.5
.

For all
(G,H)\in(C^{2,\alpha}(\mathbb{T}))^{2}
the set
\mathcal{B}_{G,H}
is open and dense.

Proof.

The open statement on
\mathcal{B}_{G,H}
follows from the fact that
\varphi_{g,h}|_{\Gamma_{0}}\neq\text{const}
is an open condition and
\varphi_{g,h}
has continuous dependence on
(g,h)
due to classical Schauder estimates.

As for the density statement, since
\varphi_{g,h}
is linear in the boundary values
(g,h)
, it is enough to show that
\mathcal{B}_{G,H}
is non-empty. Namely, we shall exhibit some
(g,h)\in(C^{2,\alpha}(\mathbb{T}))^{2}
such that
\varphi_{g,h}
is not constant along
\Gamma_{0}
. In this direction, let
g=0
,
h_{1}=1
,
h_{2}=\cos(x)
and assume that
(0,h_{i})\not\in\mathcal{B}_{G,H}
, for
i=1,2
. That is,
\varphi_{0,h_{i}}|_{\Gamma_{0}}=:\varphi_{i}
for some constants
\varphi_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
. We assume
\varphi_{i}\neq 0
. Then, for
h(x)=\varphi_{2}-\varphi_{1}\cos(x)
we have that
\varphi_{0,h}|_{\Gamma_{0}}=0
and
\varphi_{0,h}(x,G(x))=0
as well. Therefore, due to the elliptic stability of

we conclude that
\varphi_{0,h}=0
on
D_{G,\Gamma_{0}}
and thus
\nabla\varphi_{0,h}=0
on
\Gamma_{0}
. Since
\varphi_{0,h}
solves (
2.4
), by unique continuation (see
[
15
]
) we have
\varphi_{0,h}=0
in all
D_{G,H}
and thus by continuity
\varphi_{0,h}(x,H(x))=0
, a contradiction with
\varphi_{0,h}(x,H(x))=-\partial_{y}\psi_{0}(x,H(x))h(x)\neq 0
, since
\partial_{y}\psi_{0}(x,H(x))\neq 0
due to Lemma
2.1
and
h(x)\neq 0
. If
\varphi_{i}=0
for some
i=1,2
, we argue for

and we likewise reach

. Hence, we must have either
(0,h_{1})\in\mathcal{B}_{G,H}
or
(0,h_{2})\in\mathcal{B}_{G,H}
and therefore we conclude that
\mathcal{B}_{G,H}
is non-empty.
∎

2.3.
Genericity of Islands: Proof of Theorem
1.1

Define the set

(2.8)

\mathcal{B}=\{(G,H)\in(C^{2,\alpha}(\mathbb{T}))^{2}:\text{ the solution }\psi_{0}\text{ to }\eqref{eq:steadyEulerDve}\text{ in }D_{G,H}\text{ possess islands}\}.

We claim that it
is open and dense. The density of
\mathcal{B}
follows from Theorem
2.1
, Lemma
2.4
and Lemma
2.5
, since there is an open and dense set of boundary perturbations
(G+\varepsilon g,H+\varepsilon h)
to
(G,H)
such that the associated solution
\psi_{\varepsilon}
develops islands. Therefore, the theorem is proved once we show that
\mathcal{B}
is open in
(C^{2,\alpha}(\mathbb{T}))^{2}
. Fixing
(H,G)\in\mathcal{B}
, we have the following.

Proposition 2.2
.

There exists
\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that for all
(h,g)\in(C^{2,\alpha}(\mathbb{T}))^{2}
with
\|g\|_{C^{2,\alpha}(\mathbb{T})}+\|h\|_{C^{2,\alpha}(\mathbb{T})}\leq 1
, the solution
\psi_{\varepsilon}
in

Proof.

Let
\Gamma
denote a closed contractible streamline of
\psi_{0}
and let

. The proof is divided according to the nature of the contractible streamline
\Gamma
.

Case 1

Assume that
\nabla\psi_{0}\neq 0
on
\Gamma
. Since
\psi_{0}
is
C^{2}
, we can locally parametrize
\Gamma
by a
C^{1}
curve. Let

denote the normal unit vector pointing inside the set bounded by
\Gamma
and assume without loss of generality) that
\hat{n}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{e}_{2}
and

for some
\mathbf{x}\in\Gamma
.

By continuity,
\frac{1}{2}\partial_{y}\psi_{0}(\mathbf{x})<\partial_{y}\psi_{0}(\mathbf{x}+y\hat{n})<\frac{3}{2}\partial_{y}\psi_{0}(\mathbf{x})
, for all
0<y<\delta_{0}

small enough. Let

and observe that

\displaystyle\psi_{0}(\mathbf{x}+y_{1}\hat{n})<\psi_{0}(\mathbf{x})+\frac{3}{2}\partial_{y}\psi_{0}(\mathbf{x})y_{1}<\psi_{0}(\mathbf{x}+y\hat{n})<\psi_{0}(\mathbf{x})+\frac{1}{2}\partial_{y}\psi_{0}(\mathbf{x})y_{2}<\psi_{0}(\mathbf{x}+y_{2}\hat{n})

for all

lies between
\Gamma_{1}
and
\Gamma_{2}
, two closed and contractible streamline. Moreover,

\displaystyle\psi_{0}(\mathbf{x})+\frac{3}{2}\partial_{y}\psi_{0}(\mathbf{x})y_{1}<\psi_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x}+y\hat{n})<\psi_{0}(\mathbf{x})+\frac{1}{2}\partial_{y}\psi_{0}(\mathbf{x})y_{2}

for all

small enough and hence

still lies between
\Gamma_{1}
and
\Gamma_{2}

has islands.

Case 2

Assume now
\nabla\psi_{0}=0
somewhere in
\Gamma
. If there exists
\mathbf{x}\in\Gamma
such that

, by continuity there exists points inside the region bounded by
\Gamma
where
\nabla\psi_{0}\neq 0
. At least one of them belongs to a regular streamline where
\nabla\psi_{0}\neq 0
throughout (see
[
9
, Lemma 2.1]
) and Case 1 applies.

Case 3

Assume next that
\nabla\psi_{0}=0
and also

. Then, we can locally parametrize

by a

curve, see

and hence

in a small annular region bounded by

is monotone increasing there. Thus, we proceed as in Case 1 to show the existence of two closed and contractible stream-lines
\Gamma_{i}
of
\psi_{0}

\psi_{\varepsilon}

Case 4

We last consider

on
\Gamma
and

in the region enclosed by

∎

Islands in generic annular domains

We briefly comment on the setting where the Euler equations are considered in an annular domain

of
\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R}
defined to be the region between two closed non-contractible

curves
\Gamma_{h}
and
\Gamma_{g}
. As before, we consider solutions
\psi
to

(2.9)

\begin{cases}\Delta\psi=F(\psi)&\text{ in }D_{\Gamma_{g},\Gamma_{h}},\\
\psi=c_{h}&\text{ on }\Gamma_{h},\\
\psi=c_{g}&\text{ on }\Gamma_{g},\end{cases}

for any

, and

. We fix
\alpha\in(0,1)
and

satisfying

, where

now reads

.

There exists an open dense set
\mathcal{B}
in the product space of
C^{2,\alpha}
curves so that for each
(\Gamma_{g},\Gamma_{h})\in\mathcal{B}
, any solution
\psi

. It involves a precise understanding of any potential singular streamline

[

3.
Islands for small perturbations of the periodic channel

In this section we further investigate the appearance of islands for small perturbations of
D_{0}=\mathbb{T}\times(-1,1)
. We first assume that
c_{h}=c_{g}=0
(which just amounts to a possible shift in
F
if
c_{h}=c_{g}\neq 0
). This is the assumption of trivial homology which was studied in
[
8
]
. Under this assumption, we can relax the assumption on
F
so that the problem

(3.1)

\begin{cases}\Delta\varphi-F^{\prime}(\psi)\varphi=0&\text{ in }\mathbb{T}\times[-1,1],\\
\varphi(x,1)=0&\text{ for }x\in\mathbb{T},\\
\varphi(x,-1)=0&\text{ for }x\in\mathbb{T}.\end{cases}

admits only the trivial solution. In the end of the section we show how to treat the general case

.
Let
\psi_{0}(x,y):\mathbb{T}\times[-1,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be any non-zero solution to

(3.2)

\begin{cases}\Delta\psi_{0}=F(\psi_{0})&\text{ in }\mathbb{T}\times[-1,1],\\
\psi_{0}(x,\pm 1)=0&\text{ for }x\in\mathbb{T}.\end{cases}

Note that the existence of a critical point for
\psi_{0}
is already ensured by the trivial homology. Since
\partial_{x}\psi_{0}
is a solution to the linearised problem, which admits only the trivial solution, we obtain that
\psi_{0}(x,y)=\psi_{0}(y)
is a shear flow, and from Theorem 2.2 in
[
3
]
we deduce that
\psi_{0}(y)
is symmetric with respect to
y=0
and thus
\psi_{0}^{\prime}(0)=0
. Moreover, there holds
\psi_{0}^{\prime}(1)\neq 0
. Otherwise,
\Phi_{0}(y):=\psi_{0}^{\prime}(y)
satisfies

(3.3)

\begin{cases}\Phi_{0}^{\prime\prime}=F^{\prime}(\psi_{0})\Phi_{0}&\text{ in }[-1,1],\\
\Phi_{0}(\pm 1)=0,&\end{cases}

for which
\Phi_{0}\equiv 0
and hence
\psi_{0}\equiv 0
, a contradiction. Using the description of
\psi_{\varepsilon}
from Theorem
2.1
, the condition on
\varphi_{g,h}
from Lemma
2.4
now reads
\varphi(x,0)
is constant, since
\Gamma_{0}=\{y=0\}
. The next result determines
\varphi(x,0)
in terms of the boundary defining functions
(g,h)
. More precisely, for

(3.4)

\mathcal{B}^{\prime}=\{(g,h)\in(C^{2}(\mathbb{T}))^{2}:h^{\prime}(x)+g^{\prime}(x)\not\equiv 0\},

we have the following.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
(g,h)\in(C^{2,\alpha}(\mathbb{T}))^{2}
and let
\varphi(x,y)
be the solution to (
2.4
) in
D_{0}=\mathbb{T}\times(-1,1)
. Then,
\partial_{x}\varphi(x,0)\not\equiv 0
if and only if
(g,h)\in\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Firstly, we decompose
\varphi(x,y)=\varphi_{e}(x,y)+\varphi_{o}(x,y)
where
\varphi_{e}
and
\varphi_{o}
denote the even symmetric and odd symmetric parts of
\varphi
with respect to
y=0
. By definition, there clearly holds
\varphi_{o}(x,0)=0
and
\partial_{y}\varphi_{e}(x,0)=0
, for all

so that, in particular,
\varphi(x,0)=\varphi_{e}(x,0)
. Thus, we study
\varphi_{e}
, which is the unique solution to

(3.5)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\Delta\varphi_{e}-F^{\prime}(\psi_{0})\varphi_{e}=0&\text{ in }\mathbb{T}\times[0,1],\\
\varphi_{e}(x,1)=f(x)&\text{ for }x\in\mathbb{T},\\
\partial_{y}\varphi_{e}(x,0)=0&\text{ for }x\in\mathbb{T}.\\
\end{cases}

for

, because
\psi_{0}
is even. Now, if
\partial_{x}\varphi_{e}(x,0)=0
, for all
x\in\mathbb{T}
, let
\phi:=\partial_{x}\varphi
, which satisfies

(3.6)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\Delta\phi-F^{\prime}(\psi_{0})\phi=0&\text{ in }\mathbb{T}\times[0,1],\\
\phi(x,0)=0&\text{ for }x\in\mathbb{T},\\
\partial_{y}\phi(x,0)=0&\text{ for }x\in\mathbb{T}.\\
\end{cases}

From Lemma 3 in
[
8
]
, we have
\phi=0
in
\mathbb{T}\times[0,1]
and thus
\partial_{x}\varphi_{e}(x,1)=0
. Since
\psi_{0}^{\prime}(1)\neq 0
, we deduce that

must be constant. In other words,

(3.7)

\displaystyle h(x)+g(x)=c_{0},\quad\text{ for all }x\in\mathbb{T},

for some
c_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
. With this, the lemma is proved.
∎

Remark

(Possible existence of laminar flow)
.

and

Question 1
.

For all

g,h:\mathbb{T}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and
F
such that the solution
\psi
to (
1.3
) posed in
D_{-1-\varepsilon g,1+\varepsilon h}
does not have islands?

Supporting evidence can be found in the case of solutions close to the Couette flow, which solves (
1.3
) posed in
\mathbb{T}\times(-1,1)
with
F=-1
and
\psi_{0}=-\frac{1}{2}(y^{2}-1)
. Then, the equation for the first order correction now reads

\begin{cases}\Delta\varphi=0&\text{ in }\mathbb{T}\times[-1,1],\\
\varphi(x,1)=h(x),&x\in\mathbb{T}\\
\varphi(x,-1)=g(x),&x\in\mathbb{T}\end{cases}

whose solution is

\displaystyle\varphi(x,y)=\sum_{n\neq 0}\frac{1}{2\sinh(n)}\left(h_{n}-g_{n}\right)\sinh(ny)e^{inx}+\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{1}{2\cosh(n)}\left(h_{n}+g_{n}\right)\cosh(ny)e^{inx}

where
h(x)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}h_{n}e^{inx},
and
g(x)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}g_{n}e^{inx}.

It is instructive to note that for any singular streamline
\Gamma_{\varepsilon}
to exist, we must have
h_{n}+g_{n}=0
for all
n\neq 0
and also

\displaystyle y_{\varepsilon}(x)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\partial_{y}\varphi(x,y_{\varepsilon}(x))+\partial_{y}r_{\varepsilon}(x,y_{\varepsilon}(x))=\varepsilon\partial_{y}\varphi(x,0)+O(\varepsilon^{2})

so that
\frac{y_{\varepsilon}(x)}{\varepsilon}\rightarrow\partial_{y}\varphi(x,0)
as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
. In other words, the limiting rescaled
\Gamma_{\varepsilon}
converges to the curve
(x,\partial_{y}\varphi(x,0))
, which is uniquely prescribed by the boundary data
(g,h)
by

\displaystyle\partial_{y}\varphi(x,0)=\sum_{n\neq 0}\frac{nh_{n}}{\sinh(n)}e^{inx}

and clearly indicates there is a unique correspondence between boundary data and any potentially singular streamline. It is thus conceivable that there are non-flat domains which support laminar flows having stagnation sets, but we do not resolve this point. It is worth mentioning here
[
10
, Theorem 4.12]
, which shows the existence of Arnold stable steady laminar solutions of the Euler equations on narrow compact doubly connected non-circular domains that stagnate along the outer boundary and satisfy (
2.9
) for some non-linearity
F\in C^{2,\frac{1}{2}}
. It would be interesting to see if the solutions constructed in
[
10
]
can be extended to occupy larger domains.

3.1.
Islands for non-trivial homology

Here we show Theorem
1.1
for the case
c_{h}\neq c_{g}
. Since
\psi_{0}(x,1)=c_{h}
and
\psi_{0}(x,-1)=c_{g}
, the assumed elliptic stability of
F^{\prime}(\psi_{0})
gives
\psi_{0}(x,y)=\psi_{0}(y)
, as in the trivial homology setting. Hence, by the stagnation assumption, there exists some
y_{0}\in(-1,1)
such that
\psi_{0}^{\prime}(y_{0})=0
. We claim that such
y_{0}
is unique.

Indeed, let
y_{1}\in[-1,1]
,
y_{1}\neq y_{0}
be any other critical point. Assuming, say, that
y_{1}>y_{0}
, we have that
\Phi_{0}(y)=\psi_{0}^{\prime}(y)
satisfies

(3.8)

\begin{cases}\Phi_{0}^{\prime\prime}=F^{\prime}(\psi_{0})\Phi_{0}&\text{ in }[y_{0},y_{1}],\\
\Phi_{0}(y_{0})=\Phi(y_{1})=0,&\end{cases}

Then again, the elliptic stability of
F^{\prime}
shows that
\Phi_{0}(y)=0
for all
y\in[y_{0},y_{1}]
. In particular, this shows that
\psi_{0}(y)=\psi_{0}(y_{0})
and
F(\psi_{0}(y_{0}))=0
, for all
y\in[y_{0},y_{1}]
. Hence,
\psi_{0}(y)=\psi_{0}(y_{0})
, for all
y\in[-1,1]
, but this contradicts
c_{g}\neq c_{h}
.

We next observe that
\psi_{0}
must be symmetric about
y=y_{0}
. Indeed, since
F(\psi_{0}(y_{0}))\neq 0
and
\psi_{0}
has only one critical point, then
\psi_{0}
must attain either
c_{h}
or
c_{g}
only once inside. Say
\psi_{0}
attains
c_{h}
at some
y_{1}\in(-1,1)
. Then, in
(y_{1},1)
we have

(3.9)

\begin{cases}\psi_{0}^{\prime\prime}=F(\psi_{0})&\text{ in }[y_{1},1],\\
\psi_{0}(y_{1})=\psi(1)=c_{h},&\end{cases}

and from
[
3
, Theorem 2.2]
we deduce that
\psi_{0}
must be symmetric with respect to
y_{*}=\frac{1+y_{1}}{2}
, with
\psi_{0}^{\prime}(y_{*})=0
. By uniqueness, it must be
y_{*}=y_{0}
so that
y_{1}=2y_{0}-1
. Next, we argue as in the trivial homology setting, so that the analogue of Theorem
2.1
holds and we have that for all
(g,h)\in(C^{2,\alpha}(\mathbb{T}))^{2}
. the unique solution
\psi_{\varepsilon}:D_{\varepsilon}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
to (
1.3
) in
D_{\varepsilon}
can be written as

(3.10)

\psi_{\varepsilon}(x,y)={\psi_{0}}(y)+\varepsilon{\varphi}(x,y)+r_{\varepsilon}(x,y),

where
\varphi(x,y)
solves

(3.11)

\begin{cases}\left(\Delta-F^{\prime}(\psi_{0})\right)\varphi=0&\text{ in }\mathbb{T}\times[-1,1],\\
\varphi(x,1)=-h(x)\psi_{0}^{\prime}(1)&\text{ for }x\in\mathbb{T},\\
\varphi(x,-1)=g(x)\psi_{0}^{\prime}(-1)&\text{ for }x\in\mathbb{T},\\
\end{cases}

with
\|{\varphi}\|_{C^{2,\alpha}(D_{\varepsilon})}\lesssim 1
and
\|r_{\varepsilon}\|_{C^{2,\alpha}(D_{\varepsilon})}\lesssim\varepsilon^{2}
. The obvious modifications to Lemma
2.4
show that the presence of islands for
\psi_{\varepsilon}
for all
\varepsilon>0
small enough is conditional on
\varphi(x,y_{0})
being non-constant. Then, Theorem
1.1
is established once we prove

Lemma 3.2
.

There exists an open and dense set
\mathcal{B}\subset(C^{2,\alpha}(\mathbb{T}))^{2}
such that for all
(g,h)\in\mathcal{B}
, the solution
\varphi(x,y)
associated to (
3.11
) is non-constant along
y=y_{0}
.

Proof.

The open statement is straightforward due to classical Schauder estimates. As for the density statement, we observe that solutions
\varphi
to (
3.11
) are linear in
(g,h)
. Thus, it is enough to show there exists
(g,h)\in(C^{2,\alpha}(\mathbb{T}))^{2}
such that
\varphi(x,y_{0})
is non-constant. In this direction, let
g(x)\equiv g_{0}
for some
g_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
and
h^{\prime}(x)\neq 0
. We claim that
\partial_{x}\varphi(x,y_{0})\neq 0
.

Assume otherwise. Then,
\partial_{x}\varphi(x,y)
satisfies

(3.12)

\begin{cases}\left(\Delta-F^{\prime}(\psi_{0})\right)\partial_{x}\varphi=0&\text{ in }\mathbb{T}\times[-1,y_{0}],\\
\partial_{x}\varphi(x,y_{0})=0&\text{ for }x\in\mathbb{T},\\
\partial_{x}\varphi(x,-1)=0&\text{ for }x\in\mathbb{T},\\
\end{cases}

so that
\partial_{x}\varphi(x,y)=0
, for all
(x,y)\in\mathbb{T}\times[-1,y_{0}]
owing to the elliptic stability of
F^{\prime}
. On the other hand, since
\varphi(x,y_{0})
is constant, we can consider the even and odd reflections of
\varphi
with respect to
y=y_{0}
in the domain
\mathbb{T}\times(2y_{0}-1,1)
. As usual,
\varphi_{o}(x,y_{0})
is trivially zero and thus we have
\partial_{x}\varphi_{e}(x,y_{0})=0
and also
\partial_{x}\partial_{y}\varphi_{e}(x,y_{0})=0
. Again using Lemma 3 from
[
8
]
, we conclude that
\partial_{x}\varphi_{e}(x,y)\equiv 0
on
\mathbb{T}\times(y_{0},1)
and in particular this means that
\partial_{x}\varphi_{e}(x,1)=0
. However,

\displaystyle\varphi_{e}(x,1)=\frac{-\psi_{0}^{\prime}(1)h(x)+\varphi(x,2y_{0}-1)}{2}

and
-1<2y_{0}-1<y_{0}
. Since
\partial_{x}\varphi(x,2y_{0}-1)=0
, we conclude that
\partial_{x}\varphi(x,1)=-\frac{\psi_{0}^{\prime}(1)}{2}h^{\prime}(x)\neq 0
, for any non-constant
h
. Hence a contradiction and thus
\varphi(x,y_{0})
is not constant, namely
(g,h)\in\mathcal{B}
.
∎

4.
Size of Islands

In this section we consider
D_{0}=\mathbb{T}\times(-1,1)
and
D_{\varepsilon}=D_{-1-\varepsilon g,1+\varepsilon h}
, for some
(g,h)\in\mathcal{B}
given by (
3.4
) or Lemma
3.2
according to the homology. We locate islands of fluid and characterise their size in terms of the boundary perturbations. To do so, we first identify points in
D_{\varepsilon}
for which islands appear nearby. For all
\varepsilon>0
small enough, the condition
F(\psi_{0}(s))\neq 0
whenever
\psi_{0}^{\prime}(s)=0
provides the existence of some curve
\Gamma_{\varepsilon}=\{(x,y_{\varepsilon}(x)):\,x\in\mathbb{T}\}
for which
\partial_{y}\psi_{\varepsilon}=0
on
\Gamma_{\varepsilon}
. Such curve is
\varepsilon
close to the critical point
y=y_{0}
of
\psi_{0}
. Throughout the section, we assume that
F(\psi_{0}(y_{0}))<0
.

Lemma 4.1
.

There exists
\delta_{0}>0
such that
\psi_{\varepsilon}:\mathbb{T}\times[y_{0}-\delta_{0},y_{0}+\delta_{0}]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
attains its maximum in the interior. Moreover, its maximizers are close to global maximizers of
\varphi(\cdot,y_{0})
, for
\varepsilon>0
small enough.

Proof.

Proceeding as in the proof of Lemma
2.4
, there exists
\delta_{0}>0
small enough for which any global maximizer
(x_{\varepsilon},y_{\varepsilon})
of
\psi_{\varepsilon}
in
\mathbb{T}\times[y_{0}-\delta_{0},y_{0}+\delta_{0}]
is interior, since
\partial_{y}\psi_{\varepsilon}(x,y_{0}+\delta_{0})<0
,
\partial_{y}\psi_{\varepsilon}(x,y_{0}-\delta_{0})>0
and
\partial_{y}^{2}\psi_{\varepsilon}(x,y)<0
for all
(x,y)\in\mathbb{T}\times[y_{0}-\delta_{0},y_{0}+\delta_{0}]
, for
\varepsilon>0
small enough. Hence, for any
x\in\mathbb{T}
, there exists a unique
y=y_{\varepsilon}(x)\in(y_{0}-\delta_{0},y_{0}+\delta_{0})
such that
\partial_{y}\psi_{\varepsilon}(x,y_{\varepsilon}(x))=0
. By the implicit function theorem, since
\partial_{y}^{2}\psi_{\varepsilon}(x,y)<0
in
\mathbb{T}\times[y_{0}-\delta_{0},y_{0}+\delta_{0}]
we see that
\Gamma_{\varepsilon}=\{(x,y_{\varepsilon}(x)):\,x\in\mathbb{T}\}
is a
C^{1}
curve in
\mathbb{T}\times[y_{0}-\delta_{0},y_{0}+\delta_{0}]
.

Next, the map
x\mapsto\psi_{\varepsilon}(x,y_{\varepsilon}(x))
is
2\pi
-periodic, and thus attains its maximum and minimum. Let
(x_{\varepsilon},y_{\varepsilon})\in\Gamma_{\varepsilon}
be any such maximum, there holds

\displaystyle\psi_{\varepsilon}(x,y_{\varepsilon}(x))=\psi_{0}(y_{\varepsilon}(x))+\varepsilon\varphi(x,y_{\varepsilon}(x))+r_{\varepsilon}(x,y_{\varepsilon}(x))\leq\psi_{0}(y_{\varepsilon})+\varepsilon\varphi(x_{\varepsilon},y_{\varepsilon})+r_{\varepsilon}(x_{\varepsilon},y_{\varepsilon})=\psi_{\varepsilon}(x_{\varepsilon},y_{\varepsilon})

for all
x\in\mathbb{T}
. As
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
we have
x_{\varepsilon}\rightarrow x_{0}
up to a subsequence and then

\displaystyle\varphi(x,y_{0})\leq\varphi(x_{0},y_{0})+O(|x_{\varepsilon}-x_{0}|)+O(\varepsilon)

for all
x\in\mathbb{T}
. In particular, this shows that
x_{0}
must be a global maximum of
\varphi(x,y_{0})
. Therefore global maxima of
\psi_{\varepsilon}
in
\mathbb{T}\times[y_{0}-\delta_{0},y_{0}+\delta_{0}]
must converge to global maxima of
\varphi(\cdot,y_{0})
. Indeed, if
x_{0}
is not a global maximum of
\varphi(x,y_{0})
, let
x_{1}
denote a global maximum of
\varphi(x,y_{0})
and let
\varepsilon>0
small enough so that
O(|x_{\varepsilon}-x_{0}|)+O(\varepsilon)<\frac{\varphi(x_{1},y_{0})-\varphi(x_{0},y_{0})}{2}
. Then,

\displaystyle\varphi(x_{1},y_{0})\leq\varphi(x_{0},y_{0})+O(|x_{\varepsilon}-x_{0}|)+O(\varepsilon)<\frac{1}{2}\left(\varphi(x_{1},y_{0})+\varphi(x_{0},y_{0})\right)

from which we readily obtain
\varphi(x_{1},y_{0})<\varphi(x_{0},y_{0})
, a contradiction.
∎

Given the preceding lemma, understanding the maximizers of
\varphi(\cdot,y_{0})
and their possible degeneracy (whether
\partial_{x}^{2}\varphi
vanishes) is crucial in describing
\psi_{\varepsilon}(x,y)
for
(x,y)
nearby a maximum
(x_{\varepsilon},y_{\varepsilon})
. For this, let
(g,h)\in(C^{2,\alpha}(\mathbb{T}))^{2}
and let
\varphi(x,y):=\varphi_{g,h}(x,y)
denote the unique solution to (
2.4
). We define

(4.1)

\mathcal{B}_{0}=\{(g,h)\in(C^{2,\alpha}(\mathbb{T}))^{2}:\max\varphi(\cdot,y_{0})\neq 0\text{ and }\partial_{x}^{2}\varphi(x_{0},y_{0})<0\text{ for all }(x_{0},y_{0})=\text{arg}\max\varphi(\cdot,y_{0})\},

and we readily observe that
\mathcal{B}_{0}\subset\mathcal{B}
.

4.1.
Local Morse theory

Using Morse theory nearby suitable maxima of
\psi_{\varepsilon}
, the next result shows that boundary perturbations
(g,h)\in\mathcal{B}_{0}
of size
\varepsilon
generate islands of height
\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}
.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
(g,h)\in\mathcal{B}_{0}
. There exists
\delta_{1}>0
for which there exists
\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that for all
0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{0}
, the steady Euler solution
\psi_{\varepsilon}
to (
1.3
) possess islands whose height is comparable to
\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}
. More precisely, there exists a local maximum
(x_{\varepsilon},y_{\varepsilon})
of
\psi_{\varepsilon}
and constants
0<C_{1}\leq C_{2}
for which for all
0<\delta<\delta_{1}
, the level set

\displaystyle A_{\delta}:=\{\psi_{\varepsilon}-\psi_{0}(y_{0})=(1-\delta)(\psi_{\varepsilon}(x_{\varepsilon},y_{\varepsilon})-\psi_{0}(y_{0}))\}

is such that, for all
(x,y)\in A_{\delta}
,

\displaystyle C_{1}\delta\varepsilon\leq(y-y_{\varepsilon})^{2}+\varepsilon(x-x_{\varepsilon})^{2}\leq C_{2}\delta\varepsilon.

Since the proposition describes the level sets of
\Phi_{\varepsilon}:=\psi_{\varepsilon}-\psi_{0}(y_{0})
locally around
(x_{\varepsilon},y_{\varepsilon})
, the same conclusions can be drawn for the level sets of
\psi_{\varepsilon}=\Phi_{\varepsilon}+\psi_{0}(y_{0})
locally around
(x_{\varepsilon},y_{\varepsilon})
, since
\psi_{0}(y_{0})
is constant.

Proof.

Let
\delta_{0}>0
given by Lemma
4.1
and let
(x_{\varepsilon},y_{\varepsilon})
any global maximizer of
\psi_{\varepsilon}(x,y_{\varepsilon}(x))
(and thus of
\psi_{\varepsilon}
) in
\mathbb{T}\times[y_{0}-\delta_{0},y_{0}+\delta_{0}]
. There holds
0=\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}x}\big|_{x=x_{\varepsilon}}\psi_{\varepsilon}(x,y_{\varepsilon}(x))=\partial_{x}\psi_{\varepsilon}(x_{\varepsilon},y_{\varepsilon})=0
, so that
\nabla\psi_{\varepsilon}(x_{\varepsilon},y_{\varepsilon})=0
. Then,

\displaystyle\psi_{\varepsilon}(x,y)=\psi_{\varepsilon}(x_{\varepsilon},y_{\varepsilon})+\int_{0}^{1}(((x-x_{\varepsilon})\partial_{x}+(y-y_{\varepsilon})\partial_{y})\psi_{\varepsilon})(x_{\varepsilon}+t(x-x_{\varepsilon}),y_{\varepsilon}+t(y-y_{\varepsilon})){\rm d}t

and since
\nabla\psi_{\varepsilon}(x_{\varepsilon},y_{\varepsilon})=0
, we further expand the above as

(4.2)

\psi_{\varepsilon}(x,y)=\psi_{\varepsilon}(x_{\varepsilon},y_{\varepsilon})+(x-x_{\varepsilon})^{2}H_{xx}(x,y)+2(x-x_{\varepsilon})(y-y_{\varepsilon})H_{xy}(x,y)+(y-y_{\varepsilon})^{2}H_{yy}(x,y),

where the above functions are given explicitly by

(4.3)

H_{x^{i}x^{j}}(x,y)=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}t(\partial_{x^{i}}\partial_{x^{j}}\psi_{\varepsilon})(x_{\varepsilon}+st(x-x_{\varepsilon}),y_{\varepsilon}+st(y-y_{\varepsilon}))\,{\rm d}s{\rm d}t,\qquad x^{1}=x,x^{2}=y.

As
F(\psi_{0}(y_{0}))<0
we have
\frac{3}{4}F(\psi_{0}(y_{0}))<H_{yy}<\frac{1}{4}F(\psi_{0}(y_{0}))
everywhere in the domain provided both
|y-y_{\varepsilon}|
and
\varepsilon
are taken sufficiently small, since

\displaystyle H_{yy}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}t\psi_{0}^{\prime\prime}(y_{\varepsilon}+st(y-y_{\varepsilon})){\rm d}s{\rm d}t

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{1}t\left(\varepsilon\partial_{y}^{2}\varphi+\partial_{y}^{2}r_{\varepsilon}\right)(x_{\varepsilon}+st(x-x_{\varepsilon}),y_{\varepsilon}+st(y-y_{\varepsilon})){\rm d}s{\rm d}t

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}F(\psi_{0}(y_{0}))+O(|y-y_{\varepsilon}|)+O(\varepsilon)

We also record that

(4.4)

H_{xy}=\varepsilon h_{xy},\qquad H_{xx}=\varepsilon h_{xx},

for smooth functions
h_{xy},h_{yy}
uniformly bounded in
\varepsilon
. Next, with

(4.5)

w(x,y)=(y-y_{\varepsilon})\sqrt{|H_{yy}(x,y)|}-(x-x_{\varepsilon})\frac{H_{xy}(x,y)}{\sqrt{|H_{yy}(x,y)|}},

we note that
w(x,y)\sim y-y_{\varepsilon}+O(\varepsilon)(x-x_{\varepsilon})
since
H_{xy}\sim\varepsilon
by (
4.4
). Then, since
|H_{yy}|=-H_{yy}
, there holds

(4.6)

w(x,y)^{2}=-H_{yy}(x,y)(y-y_{\varepsilon})^{2}-2(x-x_{\varepsilon})(y-y_{\varepsilon})H_{xy}(x,y)-(x-x_{\varepsilon})^{2}\frac{H_{xy}(x,y)^{2}}{H_{yy}(x,y)},

and as a result, from (
4.2
) we have

\displaystyle\psi_{\varepsilon}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\psi_{\varepsilon}(x_{\varepsilon},y_{\varepsilon})-w(x,y)^{2}+(x-x_{\varepsilon})^{2}\left(H_{xx}(x,y)-\frac{H_{xy}(x,y)^{2}}{H_{yy}(x,y)}\right)

\displaystyle=\psi_{\varepsilon}(x_{\varepsilon},y_{\varepsilon})-w(x,y)^{2}+\frac{\mathrm{D}(x,y)}{H_{yy}(x,y)}(x-x_{\varepsilon})^{2}

\displaystyle\sim\psi_{\varepsilon}(x_{\varepsilon},y_{\varepsilon})-w(x,y)^{2}-\mathrm{D}(x,y)(x-x_{\varepsilon})^{2},

where
\mathrm{D}(x,y)=\det H(x,y)
. Expanding as in (
4.2
), we have

(4.7)

\mathrm{D}(x,y)=\mathrm{D}(x_{\varepsilon},y_{\varepsilon})+\int_{0}^{1}((x-x_{\varepsilon})\partial_{x}+(y-y_{\varepsilon})\partial_{y})\mathrm{D}(x_{\varepsilon}+t(x-x_{\varepsilon}),y_{\varepsilon}+t(y-y_{\varepsilon}))\,dt.

Now, from (
4.4
),

(4.8)

\mathrm{D}(x,y)=\varepsilon(h_{xx}H_{yy}-\varepsilon h_{xy}^{2}),

where
h_{xx},H_{yy},h_{xy}
are uniformly bounded in
\varepsilon
. On the other hand, since
\partial_{y}\psi_{\varepsilon}(x,y_{\varepsilon}(x))=0
for all
x\in\mathbb{T}
, then there holds

\displaystyle 0=\partial_{xy}^{2}\psi_{\varepsilon}(x,y_{\varepsilon}(x))+y^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}(x)\partial_{y}^{2}\psi_{\varepsilon}(x,y_{\varepsilon}(x)).

and thus

\displaystyle\mathrm{D}(x_{\varepsilon},y_{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle=\partial_{y}^{2}\psi_{\varepsilon}(x_{\varepsilon},y_{\varepsilon})\left(\partial_{x}^{2}\psi_{\varepsilon}(x_{\varepsilon},y_{\varepsilon})-(y_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(x_{\varepsilon}))^{2}\partial_{y}^{2}\psi_{\varepsilon}(x_{\varepsilon},y_{\varepsilon})\right)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon F(\psi_{0}(y_{0}))\partial_{x}^{2}\varphi(x_{\varepsilon},y_{\varepsilon})+O(\varepsilon^{2})

\displaystyle=\varepsilon F(\psi_{0}(y_{0}))\partial_{x}^{2}\varphi(x_{\varepsilon},y_{0})+O(\varepsilon)

Now, from Lemma
4.1
, for
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small,
x_{\varepsilon}
is (up to a subsequence) arbitrarily close to some maximizer
x_{0}
of
\varphi(\cdot,y_{0})
, so that

\displaystyle\mathrm{D}(x_{\varepsilon},y_{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle=\varepsilon F(\psi_{0}(y_{0}))\partial_{x}^{2}\varphi(x_{0},y_{0})+O(|x_{\varepsilon}-x_{0}|)+O(\varepsilon).

In particular, since
(g,h)\in\mathcal{B}_{0}
, we have that
\partial_{x}^{2}\varphi(x_{0},y_{0})<0
is independent of
\varepsilon
and thus we conclude that
\mathrm{D}(x_{\varepsilon},y_{\varepsilon})\sim\varepsilon
. As a result, together with (
4.7
) we find

(4.9)

D(x,y)\sim\varepsilon(1+|x-x_{\varepsilon}|+|y-y_{\varepsilon}|).

To properly describe the size of the island, let
\Phi_{\varepsilon}(x,y)=\psi_{\varepsilon}(x,y)-\psi_{0}(y_{0})
and recall that the connected component of
\{\Phi_{\varepsilon}(x,y)=\Phi_{\varepsilon}(x_{\varepsilon},y_{\varepsilon})\}
containing
(x_{\varepsilon},y_{\varepsilon})
consists only of the maximizer
(x_{\varepsilon},y_{\varepsilon})
of
\Phi_{\varepsilon}
. Note that

\displaystyle\Phi_{\varepsilon}(x_{\varepsilon},y_{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle=\psi_{0}(y_{\varepsilon})+\varepsilon\varphi(x_{\varepsilon},y_{\varepsilon})+r_{\varepsilon}(x_{\varepsilon},y_{\varepsilon})-\psi_{0}(y_{0})

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\varphi(x_{\varepsilon},0)+O(\varepsilon^{2})

so that
\Phi_{\varepsilon}(x_{\varepsilon},y_{\varepsilon})>0
if
\max\varphi(\cdot,0)>0
and likewise
\Phi_{\varepsilon}(x_{\varepsilon},y_{\varepsilon})<0
if
\max\varphi(\cdot,0)<0
, for
\varepsilon>0
small enough. Assume that
\max\varphi(\cdot,y_{0})>0
, and for
\delta>0
small we define the level set
\{\Phi_{\varepsilon}(x,y)=(1-\delta_{0})\Phi_{\varepsilon}(x_{\varepsilon},y_{\varepsilon})\}
(if instead
\max\varphi(\cdot,0)<0
one would consider the level set
\{\Phi_{\varepsilon}(x,y)=(1+\delta_{0})\Phi_{\varepsilon}(x_{\varepsilon},y_{\varepsilon})\}
). Along this set,
(x,y)
satisfies

(4.10)

\delta\Phi_{\varepsilon}(x_{\varepsilon},y_{\varepsilon})=w(x,y)^{2}-\frac{D(x,y)}{H_{yy}(x,y)}(x-x_{\varepsilon})^{2}\sim((y-y_{\varepsilon})+\varepsilon(x-x_{\varepsilon}))^{2}+D(x,y)(x-x_{\varepsilon})^{2},

while also

\displaystyle\Phi_{\varepsilon}(x_{\varepsilon},y_{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\varphi(x_{0},y_{0})+O(\varepsilon^{2})+O(\varepsilon|x_{\varepsilon}-x_{0}|)

with
\varphi(x_{0},y_{0})>0
. Thus (
4.10
) reads

(4.11)

\delta\varepsilon\varphi(x_{0},y_{0})\sim((y-y_{\varepsilon})+\varepsilon(x-x_{\varepsilon}))^{2}+\varepsilon(x-x_{\varepsilon})^{2}+\varepsilon(x-x_{\varepsilon})^{2}(|x-x_{\varepsilon}|+|y-y_{\varepsilon}|).

Provided
\delta
is taken small enough, this implies that
|x-x_{\varepsilon}|\lesssim\delta^{1/2}
along the streamline (if
|x-x_{\varepsilon}|
were much larger then we could not have
(x-x_{\varepsilon})^{2}+(x-x_{\varepsilon})^{3}\sim\delta
),
and so the above reads

(4.12)

\delta\varepsilon\sim(y-y_{\varepsilon})^{2}+\varepsilon(x-x_{\varepsilon})^{2},

which says that the streamline
\{\Phi_{\varepsilon}(x,y)=(1-\delta)\Phi_{\varepsilon}(x_{\varepsilon},y_{\varepsilon})\}
is close to an ellipse
(y-y_{\varepsilon})^{2}+\varepsilon(x-x_{\varepsilon})^{2}=C\delta\varepsilon
, which has height
\sim(\delta\varepsilon)^{1/2}
and width
\sim\delta^{1/2}
. It is also clear that for arbitrary
\delta<1
, the islands have height

\sim\varepsilon^{1/2}
(since if
|y-y_{\varepsilon}|\sim\varepsilon^{1/2-a}
for
a>0
we would have
\Phi_{\varepsilon}(x,y)\sim F(\psi_{0}(y_{0}))(y-y_{\varepsilon})^{2}+O(\varepsilon)\sim F(\psi_{0}(y_{0}))\varepsilon^{1-2a}<0
so we can’t have

. They could however (and, in general, will) have large width.
∎

4.2.
Genericity of elliptical islands

To determine that generic boundary perturbations

, we first investigate further properties of

. In particular, we study

when

Lemma 4.2
.

Let

and

such that

, for some

, and

. Let

\displaystyle\varphi(x,y)=-\beta\psi_{0}^{\prime}(1)\phi(y)\cos(k(x-x_{0})).

Here

\begin{cases}\phi^{\prime\prime}(y)-(k^{2}+F^{\prime}(\psi_{0}(y))\phi(y)=0&y\in[-1,1],\\

Let

\begin{cases}\phi^{\prime\prime}(y)-(k^{2}+F^{\prime}(\psi_{0}(y)))\phi(y)=0&y\in[-1,y_{0}],\\

∎

Remark
.

Under the assumption of trivial homology,

Appendix A
Islands of fluid in special cases

In this section, we discuss some cases for which one can ensure the existence of islands, irrespective of the size of the boundary perturbation. These results complement the generic and perturbative results of the main text and while we state them for periodic domains defined by graphs, an inspection of the proofs show that they are likewise true for general annular domains. For
g,h\in C^{2,\alpha}(\mathbb{T})
with
g(x)<h(x)
, set

(A.1)

D_{g,h}:=\{(x,y):x\in\mathbb{T},\,g(x)\leq y\leq h(x)\},

and for any
F\in C^{1}
and any
c_{h},c_{g}\in\mathbb{R}
, consider the solution
\psi
(if any), to

(A.2)

\begin{cases}\Delta\psi=F(\psi)&\text{ in }D_{G,H},\\
\psi(x,h(x))=c_{h}&\text{ for }x\in\mathbb{T},\\
\psi(x,g(x))=c_{g}&\text{ for }x\in\mathbb{T}.\end{cases}

The first result states that, in the presence of stagnation points, fixing one boundary to be horizontal and perturbing the other always produces islands.

Proposition A.1
.

Assume
g^{\prime}\equiv 0
and
h^{\prime}\neq 0
. Let
\psi
be any solution to (
A.2
) which stagnates. Then,
\psi
possess islands.

Proof.

Assume otherwise, namely
\psi
is laminar and non-constant. By criticality,
\nabla\psi=0
on some closed and non-contractible
\Gamma
. Then, we may consider
u=\nabla^{\perp}\psi
as a steady laminar Euler solution on
D_{g,\Gamma}
, the region in
D_{g,h}
bounded above by
\Gamma
. Given the overdetermined nature of the boundary conditions on
\Gamma
and the fact that the bottom boundary of
D_{g,\Gamma}
is flat, Corollary 1.1 in
[
9
]
shows that
\Gamma
is a flat horizontal curve as well and
\nabla\psi=0
there. Next, since
F
is Lipschitz, one can use Lemma 3 in
[
8
]
and the fact that
h^{\prime}\neq 0
to obtain that
\psi
is constant, a contradiction.
∎

When both
h
and
g
are non-trivial perturbations, the appearance of islands is ensured if solutions attain more than one critical value.

Proposition A.2
.

Let
g^{\prime}\neq 0
and
h^{\prime}\neq 0
. Suppose
\psi
is a solution to (
A.2
) whose set of critical points is disconnected. Then,
\psi
has islands.

Proof.

If
\psi
is laminar, there exists
c_{1},c_{2}
and closed non-contractible and disjoint level sets
\Gamma_{c_{i}}
such that
\psi|_{\Gamma_{c_{i}}}=c_{i}
and
(\nabla\psi)|_{\Gamma_{c_{i}}}=0
. Then,
\psi
is a steady laminar solution of Euler in
D_{\Gamma_{c_{1}},\Gamma_{c_{2}}}
, say, with overdetermined boundary conditions. Hence, Corollary 1.1 in
[
9
]
shows that
\Gamma_{c_{i}}
are flat horizontal curves where
\nabla\psi=0
. Hence,
[
8
, Lemma 3]
shows that, since
h^{\prime}\neq 0
and
g^{\prime}\neq 0
, then
\psi
must be constant in
D_{g,h}
, a contradiction.
∎

A first consequence of the proposition is for non-linearities
F
for which the associated solution
\psi_{0}
to (
A.2
) in
\mathbb{T}\times[-1,1]
has more than one critical point. Then, the solution associated to any non-trivial boundary perturbation will necessarily develop islands.

A second corollary of the proposition is that all eigenfunctions but the first of the Dirichlet Laplacian in
D_{g,h}
with at least one non-trivial boundary must have have islands. Indeed, since the first eigenfunction of the Dirichlet Laplacian does not change sign and the set of eigenfunctions is
L^{2}
orthogonal, all other eigenfunctions do change sign. In particular, they attain their maximum and minimum in
D_{g,h}
and the proposition applies. This situation is to be compared with that of
\mathbb{T}\times[-1,1]
, where there exists shear-flow eigenfunctions
\phi_{n}=\phi_{n}(y)
associated to infinitely many eigenvalues.

Finally, we remark the following result on partially “free-boundary” equilibria (see
[
5
]
):

Lemma A.1
.

If
g^{\prime}\neq 0
and
|\nabla\psi|=a\geq 0
on
\partial D_{g}
then, any stagnant solution
\psi
possess islands.

Proof.

Assume again towards a contradiction that
\psi
is laminar. Then, there existence of some curve
\Gamma
where
\nabla\psi=0
. Next, since
\psi
is a laminar steady solution of Euler in
D_{g}^{\Gamma}
and enjoys overdetermined boundary conditions for
\psi
, recall that
\nabla\psi=0
on
\Gamma
and
|\nabla\psi|=a\geq 0
on
\partial D_{g}
, Corollary 1.1 in
[
9
]
implies both
\partial D_{g}
and
\Gamma
are flat horizontal curves, contradicting
g^{\prime}\neq 0
.
∎

Estimates on the error function

This section is devoted to the proof of Proposition

Observe that both

\varepsilon

\partial D_{0}

.

Let

In particular, there holds

\displaystyle\|r_{\varepsilon}\|_{C^{2,\alpha}(D_{\varepsilon})}\lesssim\varepsilon^{2}\left(\|F\|_{C^{4}}+\|h\|_{C^{2,\alpha}}+\|g\|_{C^{2,\alpha}}\right)

\displaystyle X_{\varepsilon}=\left\{v\in C^{0,\alpha}(D_{\varepsilon}):\|v\|_{C^{0,\alpha}(D_{\varepsilon})}\leq\varepsilon\right\}

\displaystyle K_{\varepsilon}(v)(x,y):=\mathcal{N}(v)(x,y)-\beta_{\varepsilon}(x,y),\quad\widetilde{K}_{\varepsilon}(v):=(\Delta-F^{\prime}(\psi_{0}))_{\mathrm{D}}^{-1}K_{\varepsilon}(v),

where here

\begin{cases}\left(\Delta-F^{\prime}(\psi_{0})\right)w=f&\text{ in }D_{\varepsilon},\\

To apply the Banach fixed point argument, we first show that
\widetilde{K}_{\varepsilon}
maps

to itself.

Lemma B.1
.

There exists
\varepsilon_{*}>0
such that
\widetilde{K}_{\varepsilon}(v)\in X_{\varepsilon}
, for all

, for all
0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{*}

Proof.

Let
\varepsilon>0

[
11

\displaystyle\|\widetilde{K}_{\varepsilon}(v)\|_{C^{2,\alpha}}\leq c_{0}\|N(v)-\beta_{\varepsilon}\|_{C^{0,\alpha}}.

Here the Schauder constant

\varepsilon

\varepsilon
