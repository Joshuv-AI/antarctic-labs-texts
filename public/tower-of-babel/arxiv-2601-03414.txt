1.
Introduction

Finding the prime factorization of an arbitrary integer is believed to be a
difficult algorithmic problem, although its precise complexity is not known.
However, for the special case of factorials,
n!:=n\cdot\left(n-1\right)\cdot\cdots\cdot 2\cdot 1
,
n\geq 2
, we have the following complete and
elegant solution:

(1.1)

n!=\mathop{\textstyle\prod}\limits_{p\leq n}p^{\nu_{p}\left(n!\right)}

where the product is over all primes
p
between
2
and
n
and the
multiplicity of
p
, denoted
\nu_{p}\left(n!\right)
, is given by
Legendre formula (
[
1
]
, Theorem (6-9)):

(1.2)

\nu_{p}\left(n!\right)=\mathop{\textstyle\sum}\limits_{j=1}^{\infty}\left\lfloor\frac{n}{p^{j}}\right\rfloor=\mathop{\textstyle\sum}\limits_{j=1}^{L}\left\lfloor\frac{n}{p^{j}}\right\rfloor\text{; }L:=\left\lfloor\log_{p}n\right\rfloor\text{.}

An alternative form of this formula, which will be our main working tool, is
(
[
1
]
, Exercise 7 in Problems for Section (6.3)):

(1.3)

\nu_{p}\left(n!\right)=\frac{n-s_{p}\left(n\right)}{p-1}\text{,}

where
s_{p}\left(n\right)
denotes the sum of the base
p
digits of
n

(see first paragraph of Section
2.2
).

Here we study the relative sizes of the maximal prime powers divisors of
n!

(the powers
p^{\nu_{p}\left(n!\right)}
). We will prove:

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
p
be any prime. Then there exists some
n_{0}\left(p\right)\in\mathbb{N}
such that

q^{\nu_{q}\left(n!\right)}<p^{\nu_{p}\left(n!\right)}\text{; }\forall n\geq n_{0}\left(p\right)\text{ and for all primes }q>p\text{.}

Corollary 1.2
.

Let
m
be any positive integer and let
p_{1}<p_{2}<p_{3}<\cdots<p_{m}~=2<3<5<\cdots<p_{m}
be the first
m
prime
numbers ordered increasingly. Then there exists some
n_{0}\left(m\right)\in\mathbb{N}
such that

2^{\nu_{2}\left(n!\right)}>3^{\nu_{3}\left(n!\right)}>5^{\nu_{5}\left(n!\right)}>\cdots>p_{m}^{\nu_{p_{m}}\left(n!\right)}>q^{\nu_{q}\left(n!\right)}\text{; }\forall n\geq n_{0}\left(m\right)

for any prime
q
which is larger than
p_{m}
.

Proof.

This follows from Theorem (
1.1
) by taking
n_{0}\left(m\right)=\max\left\{n_{0}\left(p_{1}\right),\ldots,n_{0}\left(p_{m}\right)\right\}
.

Corollary 1.3
.

Let
q
be any odd prime, and
n\geq 2
any
integer. Then
2^{\nu_{2}\left(n!\right)}>q^{\nu_{q}\left(n!\right)}

with the single exception
q=n=3
.

Remark 1.4
.

Shortly after uploading the first version (arXiv:submit/7138707) of the
present paper, I have learned about a paper by Mehdi Hassani, published in
2005, with results similar to Theorem (
1.1
) and Corollary (
1.3
) (
[
4
]
Theorem (5.2) and Corollary
(5.3)). It can be checked that Theorem (
1.1
) and Corollary (
1.3
) of the present paper imply, respectively, Hassani’s
Theorem (5.2) and Corollary (5.3), but not the other way around. I am
grateful to Carlo Sanna for informing me about Hassani’s results.

Let
p
be a fixed prime. The prime successor of
p
, denoted
p_{\text{succ}}
, is the smallest prime which is strictly larger than
p
. As we shall
see, the proof of Theorem (
1.1
) shows that any positive integer
solution
n
of

\left\lfloor\log_{p}n\right\rfloor<\left(n-1\right)h_{p}\left(p_{\text{succ}}\right)-\frac{p-2}{p-1}\text{,}

where
h_{p}\left(x\right):=\frac{1}{p-1}-\frac{\log_{p}x}{q-1}
,
satisfies
q^{\nu_{q}\left(n!\right)}<p^{\nu_{p}\left(n!\right)}
for
any prime
q>p
. Moreover, there exists
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that the
above inequality holds true for all
n\geq n_{0}
. Note that since
p_{\text{succ}}
is uniquely determined by
p
, the minimal such
n_{0}
is a
function of
p
, and hence we refer to this minimal value as
n_{0}\left(p\right)
. It is easy to see that the existence of
n_{0}\left(p\right)

implies, in turn, the existence of a minimal positive integer
n_{\min}\left(p\right)
such that
p_{\text{succ}}^{\nu_{p_{\text{succ}}}\left(n!\right)}<p^{\nu_{p}\left(n!\right)}
for all
n\geq

n_{\min}\left(p\right)
. It is also evident that
n_{\min}\left(p\right)\leq n_{0}\left(p\right)
, but a small numerical example shows that this bound
need not be tight. For instance, if
p=2
, the proof of Corollary (
1.3
) gives
n_{0}\left(2\right)=21
, while
n_{\min}\left(2\right)=4
. The second result of the paper gives the exact value
of
n_{\min}\left(p\right)
in the special case
p_{\text{succ}}=p+2
,
(equivalently,
p
and
p_{\text{succ}}
are twin primes).

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
p
and
q=p+2
be prime twins. Then

n_{\min}\left(p\right)=\frac{p^{2}+p}{2}\text{.}

Section
2
is devoted to the proofs of Theorem (
1.1
), Corollary (
1.3
) and Theorem (
1.5
).
The arguments combine the use of (
1.3
), some
guess work based on numerical examples that were studied using the computer
algebra system GAP (
[
3
]
), and standard manipulations of
inequalities that also rely on elementary results from the calculus of real
one variable functions.

It seems appropriate to conclude the introduction with the following two
remarks. The initial motivation behind the question addressed by Theorem (
1.1
) arose in the context of finite group theory, where the maximal
prime power divisors of
n!
are the orders of the Sylow subgroups of the
finite Symmetric groups
S_{n}
. Here the author is happy to embrace the
following sentence, quoted from
[
2
]
: ”Moreover, any
simple question about
S_{n}
is worth studying(!).” The second remark
concerns the simple looking answer to the question addressed by Theorem (
1.5
). One wonders if this result can be generalized to other
primes (with
p_{\text{succ}}-p>2
), and if so, can
n_{\min}\left(p\right)
be written in polynomial form whose coefficients are parametrized
by
p_{\text{succ}}-p
?

2.
Proofs

2.1.
Proof of Theorem (
1.1
)

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
p<q\leq n
where
p
and
q
are
primes and
n
an integer. Then

q^{\nu_{q}\left(n!\right)}<p^{\nu_{p}\left(n!\right)}\Longleftrightarrow\left(\log_{p}q\right)\cdot\frac{n-s_{q}\left(n\right)}{q-1}<\frac{n-s_{p}\left(n\right)}{p-1}\text{.}

Proof.

Using the identity
q=p^{\log_{p}q}
we have

\displaystyle q^{\nu_{q}\left(n!\right)}<p^{\nu_{p}\left(n!\right)}

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow

\displaystyle\left(p^{\log_{p}q}\right)^{\nu_{q}\left(n!\right)}<p^{\nu_{p}\left(n!\right)}

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow

\displaystyle p^{\left(\log_{p}q\right)\cdot\nu_{q}\left(n!\right)}<p^{\nu_{p}\left(n!\right)}

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow

\displaystyle\left(\log_{p}q\right)\cdot\nu_{q}\left(n!\right)<\nu_{p}\left(n!\right)\text{.}

Substituting
\nu_{q}\left(n!\right)
and
\nu_{p}\left(n!\right)

from (
1.3
) in the last inequality, yields the
claim of the lemma.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
p
be a prime and set

(2.1)

h_{p}\left(x\right):=\frac{1}{p-1}-\frac{\log_{p}x}{x-1}=\frac{1}{p-1}-\frac{1}{\ln p}\frac{\ln x}{x-1}\text{; }\forall x\in[p,\infty)\text{.}

Then
h_{p}\left(x\right)
is differentiable and monotonically increasing
in its domain of definition
[p,\infty)
. It follows that for any prime
q>p
we have

h_{p}\left(q\right)\geq h_{p}\left(p_{\text{succ}}\right)>0\text{.}

Proof.

The differentiability of
h_{p}\left(x\right)
in
[p,\infty)
is clear.
In order to prove that it is monotonically increasing we compute its first
derivative in
[p,\infty)

h_{p}^{\prime}\left(x\right)=\frac{1}{\ln p}\frac{1}{\left(x-1\right)^{2}}\left(\ln x+\frac{1}{x}-1\right)\text{,}

and check that
h_{p}^{\prime}\left(x\right)
is positive in
[p,\infty)
. Clearly, for any
x\in[p,\infty)
we have
h_{p}^{\prime}\left(x\right)>0
iff
g\left(x\right):=\ln x+\frac{1}{x}-1>0
. For
p=2
we
have
g\left(2\right)=\ln 2+\frac{1}{2}-1

\geq 0.693-\frac{1}{2}>0
. For

p\geq 3
we have
\ln p>1
and hence
g\left(p\right)=\ln p+\frac{1}{p}-1>\frac{1}{p}>0
. Thus
g\left(p\right)>0
for all primes
p
. Now
g^{\prime}\left(x\right)=\frac{1}{x}-\frac{1}{x^{2}}
and
g^{\prime}\left(x\right)>0
for all
x\in[p,\infty)
since
p>1
. It
follows that
g\left(x\right)
is monotonically increasing in
[p,\infty)
, which together with
g\left(p\right)>0
shows that
g\left(x\right)>0

for all
x\in[p,\infty)
. Hence
h_{p}^{\prime}\left(x\right)>0

for all
x\in[p,\infty)
, and so
h_{p}\left(x\right)
is
monotonically increasing in
[p,\infty)
. This implies that for any
u>p

the function
h_{p}\left(x\right)
has a global minimum in
[u,\infty)
,
given by
h_{p}\left(u\right)
. The value
h_{p}\left(u\right)
is
strictly positive since
h_{p}\left(p\right)=0
, and
h_{p}\left(x\right)
is monotonically increasing in
[p,\infty)
. Choosing
u=p_{\text{succ}}

concludes the proof.

Proof of Theorem (
1.1
).

Let
p
be any prime. We have to prove the existence of
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
, which may depend on
p
, such that

q^{\nu_{q}\left(n!\right)}<p^{\nu_{p}\left(n!\right)}\text{; }\forall n\geq n_{0}\text{ and for all primes }q>p\text{ .}

Since
q^{\nu_{q}\left(n!\right)}\geq 1
we must have
n_{0}\geq p
.
Under this assumption
\nu_{p}\left(n!\right)>0
for all
n\geq n_{0}

and hence
q^{\nu_{q}\left(n!\right)}<p^{\nu_{p}\left(n!\right)}
is
trivially true for all primes
q>p
and all
n\geq n_{0}
with
q>n
. Hence
it suffices to show that there exist
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
n_{0}>p
and
q^{\nu_{q}\left(n!\right)}<p^{\nu_{p}\left(n!\right)}

for all
n\geq n_{0}
and for all primes
q
with
p<q\leq n
.

By Lemma (
2.1
) we have to prove the existence of
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

(2.2)

\left(\log_{p}q\right)\cdot\frac{n-s_{q}\left(n\right)}{q-1}<\frac{n-s_{p}\left(n\right)}{p-1}

for all
n\geq n_{0}
and for all primes
q
such that
p<q\leq n
.

Since
n
is positive we have
s_{q}\left(n\right)\geq 1
. This implies

(2.3)

\left(\log_{p}q\right)\cdot\frac{n-s_{q}\left(n\right)}{q-1}\leq\left(\log_{p}q\right)\cdot\frac{n-1}{q-1}\text{.}

On the other hand, the number of base
p
digits of
n
is
\left\lfloor\log_{p}n\right\rfloor+1
, and the largest digit of
n
in base
p
is
p-1
. Therefore

s_{p}\left(n\right)\leq\left(p-1\right)\left(\left\lfloor\log_{p}n\right\rfloor+1\right)\text{.}

This implies

(2.4)

\frac{n-\left(p-1\right)\left(\left\lfloor\log_{p}n\right\rfloor+1\right)}{p-1}\leq\frac{n-s_{p}\left(n\right)}{p-1}\text{.}

Combining (
2.3
) and (
2.4
) it follows that any prime
q
and
integer
n
satisfying

(2.5)

\left(\log_{p}q\right)\cdot\frac{n-1}{q-1}<\frac{n-\left(p-1\right)\left(\left\lfloor\log_{p}n\right\rfloor+1\right)}{p-1}\text{,}

and
p<q\leq n
, also satisfy (
2.2
). Hence our aim now is to prove
the existence of
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that (
2.5
) holds true for
all
n\geq n_{0}
and for all primes
q
such that
p<q\leq n
. We start
with the following equivalences:

\displaystyle\left(\log_{p}q\right)\cdot\frac{n-1}{q-1}<\frac{n-\left(p-1\right)\left(\left\lfloor\log_{p}n\right\rfloor+1\right)}{p-1}

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow

\displaystyle n\frac{\left(\log_{p}q\right)}{q-1}-\frac{\left(\log_{p}q\right)}{q-1}<\frac{n}{p-1}-\left(\left\lfloor\log_{p}n\right\rfloor+1\right)

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow

(2.6)

\left\lfloor\log_{p}n\right\rfloor<n\left(\frac{1}{p-1}-\frac{\left(\log_{p}q\right)}{q-1}\right)-\left(1-\frac{\left(\log_{p}q\right)}{q-1}\right)\text{.}

By the notation of Lemma (
2.2
) we have

\displaystyle h_{p}\left(q\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p-1}-\frac{\left(\log_{p}q\right)}{q-1}

\displaystyle 1-\frac{\left(\log_{p}q\right)}{q-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-\frac{1}{p-1}+\frac{1}{p-1}-\frac{\left(\log_{p}q\right)}{q-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{p-2}{p-1}+h_{p}\left(q\right)\text{.}

Applying this to (
2.6
) we get that (
2.6
) is equivalent to

(2.7)

\left\lfloor\log_{p}n\right\rfloor<\left(n-1\right)h_{p}\left(q\right)-\frac{p-2}{p-1}\text{.}

By Lemma (
2.2
),
h_{p}\left(q\right)\geq h_{p}\left(p_{\text{succ}}\right)>0
, and hence, any positive integer
n
satisfying

(2.8)

\left\lfloor\log_{p}n\right\rfloor<\left(n-1\right)h_{p}\left(p_{\text{succ}}\right)-\frac{p-2}{p-1}\text{,}

also satisfies (
2.7
) for all primes
q
such that
p<q\leq n
. Since
\left\lfloor\log_{p}n\right\rfloor\leq\log_{p}n
, any positive integer
n
satisfying

(2.9)

\log_{p}n<\left(n-1\right)h_{p}\left(p_{\text{succ}}\right)-\frac{p-2}{p-1}\text{,}

also satisfies (
2.8
). Now (
2.9
) is a special case of the
following inequality:

(2.10)

\log_{p}n<n\cdot a-b\text{.}

where
a=h_{p}\left(p_{\text{succ}}\right)
and
b=a+\frac{p-2}{p-1}
.
Note that
a
and
b
are functions of
p
, which are independent of
n
,
and that
a
is a positive real number by Lemma (
2.2
). Since
the r.h.s. of (
2.10
) is a linear function of
n
with a positive
slope, it eventually dominates
\log_{p}n
on the l.h.s. of (
2.10
), which means that there exists
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that Inequality (
2.10
) holds true for all
n\geq n_{0}
.

Proof of Corollary (
1.3
).

By the proof of Theorem (
1.1
) (see (
2.8
)) there exists
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\left(\ast\right)~\left\lfloor\log_{2}n\right\rfloor<\left(n-1\right)h_{2}\left(3\right)\text{; }\forall n\geq n_{0}\text{,}

and for this
n_{0}
,
2^{\nu_{2}\left(n!\right)}>q^{\nu_{q}\left(n!\right)}
for all primes
q>2
and all integers
n\geq n_{0}
. By (
2.1
) we have

(2.11)

h_{2}\left(2_{\text{succ}}\right)=h_{2}\left(3\right)=1-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\ln 3}{\ln 2}>0.2075>\frac{1}{5}

and hence we get that any
n
satisfying

\left(\ast\ast\right)~\left\lfloor\log_{2}n\right\rfloor\leq\frac{n-1}{5}\text{,}

also satisfies
\left(\ast\right)
. By inspection, the smallest integer
solution of
\left(\ast\ast\right)
is
n=21
, and
\left(\ast\ast\right)
also holds true for all
21\leq n\leq 31
. Now,
\left\lfloor\log_{2}n\right\rfloor
is a step function which is constant on each step (an
interval of the form
\left[2^{k},2^{k+1}-1\right]
) and increases by one
unit from step to step. On the other hand,
\frac{n-1}{5}
is a linear
function of
n
that increases by
\frac{2^{k}}{5}
along the step
\left[2^{k},2^{k+1}-1\right]
. Since
n=32
corresponds to
k=5
, and
\frac{2^{5}}{5}>6>1
, it is clear that
\left(\ast\ast\right)
holds also for all
n\geq 32
. We have thus shown that
2^{\nu_{2}\left(n!\right)}>q^{\nu_{q}\left(n!\right)}
holds true for all
n\geq 21
and all primes
q>2
.
It remains to consider the interval
2\leq n\leq 20
. Since this interval is
finite and the relevant values of
q
are the odd primes in this same
interval, namely the primes
3,5,7,11,13,17,19
, one can check ”by hand”,
e.g., an easy to write GAP (
[
3
]
) program, that
2^{\nu_{2}\left(n!\right)}>q^{\nu_{q}\left(n!\right)}
holds true for all integers
2\leq n\leq 20
and all primes
q\in\left\{3,5,7,11,13,17,19\right\}

with the single exception
q=n=3
.

2.2.
Proof of Theorem (
1.5
)

Let
n\geq 1
and
b\geq 2
be integers. Recall that the base
b

representation of
n
is the unique sequence

\left(n\right)_{b}:=\left(d_{l}\text{,}d_{l-1},\ldots,d_{1},d_{0}\right)

of non-negative integers (the base
b
digits of
n
)
d_{i}
,
0\leq i\leq l
such that
d_{i}\in\left\{0,1,\ldots,b-1\right\}
,
d_{l}\geq 1
,
and

n=d_{l}b^{l}+d_{l-1}b^{l-1}+\cdots+d_{1}b+d_{0}\text{.}

Furthermore we say that
n
is an
l+1
digit number in base
b
, and
s_{b}\left(n\right):=\mathop{\textstyle\sum}\limits_{i=0}^{l}d_{i}
.

Lemma 2.3
.

With notation as above,
n-s_{b}\left(n\right)=0
for all
1\leq n\leq b-1
, and
n-s_{b}\left(n\right)>0
for
all
n\geq b
. Furthermore,
\left(b-1\right)|\left(n-s_{b}\left(n\right)\right)
.

Proof.

The first claim is clear. For
n\geq b
we have:

n-s_{b}\left(n\right)=\mathop{\textstyle\sum}\limits_{i=0}^{l}d_{i}b^{i}-\mathop{\textstyle\sum}\limits_{i=0}^{l}d_{i}=\mathop{\textstyle\sum}\limits_{i=0}^{l}d_{i}\left(b^{i}-1\right)=\mathop{\textstyle\sum}\limits_{i=1}^{l}d_{i}\left(b^{i}-1\right)\overset{d_{l}\geq 1}{\geq}b^{l}-1>0\text{.}

Moreover,
\left(b-1\right)|\left(b^{i}-1\right)
for all
1\leq i\leq l
, so
\left(b-1\right)|\left(n-s_{b}\left(n\right)\right)
follows.

Let
p
be a prime such that
p+2
is also a prime. Set

q:=p+2\text{, }k:=\frac{p^{2}+p}{2}-1\text{.}

In order to prove Theorem (
1.5
) we have to prove that

q^{\nu_{q}\left(k!\right)}>p^{\nu_{p}\left(k!\right)}\text{;}

and

(2.12)

~~~~~~p^{\nu_{p}\left(n!\right)}>q^{\nu_{q}\left(n!\right)}\text{; }\forall n\geq k+1\text{.}

Lemma 2.4
.

\nu_{q}\left(k!\right)=\nu_{p}\left(k!\right)=\frac{p-1}{2}\text{,}

and consequently
q^{\nu_{q}\left(k!\right)}>p^{\nu_{p}\left(k!\right)}
.

Proof.

We calculate the exponents
\nu_{q}\left(k!\right)
and
\nu_{p}\left(k!\right)
using (
1.3
). We have

k=\left(\frac{p-1}{2}\right)\left(p+2\right)\Longrightarrow\left(k\right)_{p+2}=\left(\frac{p-1}{2},0\right)\Longrightarrow s_{p+2}\left(k\right)=\frac{p-1}{2}\text{.}

Similarly

k=\left(\frac{p-1}{2}\right)p+p-1\Longrightarrow\left(k\right)_{p}=\left(\frac{p-1}{2},p-1\right)\Longrightarrow s_{p}\left(k\right)=\frac{3}{2}\left(p-1\right)\text{.}

Substituting in (
1.3
) gives:

\nu_{q}\left(k!\right)=\frac{k-s_{q}\left(k\right)}{q-1}=\frac{\left(\frac{p-1}{2}\right)\left(p+2\right)-\frac{p-1}{2}}{p+1}=\frac{p-1}{2}

\nu_{p}\left(k!\right)=\frac{k-s_{p}\left(k\right)}{p-1}=\frac{\left(\frac{p-1}{2}\right)p+p-1-\frac{3}{2}\left(p-1\right)}{p-1}=\frac{p}{2}+1-\frac{3}{2}=\frac{p-1}{2}\text{.}

Having proved
\nu_{q}\left(k!\right)=\nu_{p}\left(k!\right)
,
q^{\nu_{q}\left(k!\right)}>p^{\nu_{p}\left(k!\right)}
follows from
q>p
.

Proving (
2.12
) requires more effort. First, (
2.12
) is equivalent
to

(2.13)

r\left(n,p\right):=\frac{n-s_{p}\left(n\right)}{n-s_{p+2}\left(n\right)}>\left(\frac{\ln\left(p+2\right)}{\ln p}\right)\left(\frac{p-1}{p+1}\right)\text{; }\forall n\geq k+1=\frac{p^{2}+p}{2}\text{,}

by Lemma (
2.1
). Note that
n-s_{p+2}\left(n\right)>0
by Lemma (
2.3
) (
p+2\leq n

since
n\geq k+1
and
p\geq 3
).

Lemma 2.6

Proof.

Lemma 2.9

The real function

f\left(x\right):=\left(\frac{\ln x}{\ln p}+1\right)\frac{1}{x}\text{; }\forall x\geq 1

is positive and monotonically decreasing.

Proof.

Hence, since

n=d_{1}p+d_{0}=e_{1}\left(p+2\right)+e_{0}=e_{1}p+2e_{1}+e_{0}\text{.}

Since
d_{0}<p
we must have
e_{1}\leq d_{1}\leq p-1
. Suppose that
e_{1}=d_{1}
. Since
n\geq k+1=\frac{p^{2}+p}{2}=\frac{p+1}{2}\cdot p
we
have
e_{1}=d_{1}\geq\frac{p+1}{2}
and hence
2e_{1}\geq p+1
, and we
obtain a contradiction since
n=d_{1}p+d_{0}=e_{1}p+2e_{1}+e_{0}\geq d_{1}p+p+1
but
d_{0}\leq p-1
. It follows that
e_{1}<d_{1}\leq p-1
.
Write
d_{1}=e_{1}+\delta
, then
\delta\geq 1
is an integer and
\frac{d_{1}}{e_{1}}=1+\frac{\delta}{e_{1}}
, so
\frac{d_{1}}{e_{1}}\geq\frac{p-1}{p-2}
is equivalent to
\frac{\delta}{e_{1}}\geq\frac{1}{p-2}
. Thus,
\left(\ast\right)
is true iff
\frac{\delta}{e_{1}}\geq\frac{1}{p-2}
.
This is the case since
e_{1}<d_{1}\leq p-1
implies
e_{1}\leq p-2
and
since
\delta\geq 1
. Note further that if
\frac{d_{1}}{e_{1}}=\frac{p-1}{p-2}
, which implies
r\left(n,p\right)=r\left(m,p\right)
, then
e_{1}<d_{1}\leq p-1
implies
d_{1}=p-1
and
e_{1}=p-2
. Therefore

. Since
e_{0}\geq 0
we have,
in this case,
m\leq n
.

(2). Suppose that

is a

-digit number in base
p
, or in other words,

By the assumption

. Furthermore

\displaystyle n

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d_{2}p^{2}+d_{1}p+d_{0}=e_{2}\left(p+2\right)^{2}+e_{1}\left(p+2\right)+e_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e_{2}p^{2}+\left(4e_{2}+e_{1}\right)p+4e_{2}+2e_{1}+e_{0}\text{.}

Since

we must have
e_{2}\leq d_{2}\leq p-1
. We
consider the following cases.

(2.1). Suppose that

. In this case

\left(\ast\ast\right)~d_{1}p+d_{0}=\left(4e_{2}+e_{1}\right)p+4e_{2}+2e_{1}+e_{0}\text{.}

Since
d_{0}\leq p-1
we have
4e_{2}+e_{1}\leq d_{1}
which is equivalent
to
e_{1}\leq d_{1}-4d_{2}\leq p-1-4d_{2}
. Since

this implies
that
d_{2}\leq\frac{p-1}{4}
. From
e_{1}\leq p-1-4d_{2}
and

and hence

\displaystyle 4e_{2}+2e_{1}+e_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 4d_{2}+2e_{1}+e_{0}\leq 4\cdot\frac{p-1}{4}+2p-10+p+1

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle p-1+2p-10+p+1=4p-10\text{.}

Thus, dividing
4e_{2}+2e_{1}+e_{0}
by
p\geq 11
with remainder we have

(2.19)

4e_{2}+2e_{1}+e_{0}=\alpha\cdot p+\beta\text{; }\alpha\in\left\{0,1,2,3\right\}\text{, }\beta\in\left\{0,\ldots,p-1\right\}\text{.}

Rewriting
\left(\ast\ast\right)
in terms of

d_{1}p+d_{0}=\left(4e_{2}+e_{1}+\alpha\right)p+\beta\text{.}

Hence
\beta=d_{0}
and
d_{1}=4e_{2}+e_{1}+\alpha
. Since we assume
e_{2}=d_{2}
Condition
\left(\ast\right)
reads

\displaystyle\frac{d_{2}\left(p+1\right)+2d_{2}+e_{1}+2d_{2}+\alpha}{d_{2}\left(p+1\right)+2d_{2}+e_{1}}\geq\frac{p-1}{p-2}\text{.}

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow

\displaystyle 1+\frac{2d_{2}+\alpha}{d_{2}\left(p+1\right)+2d_{2}+e_{1}}\geq 1+\frac{1}{p-2}

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow

\displaystyle\left(2d_{2}+\alpha\right)\left(p-2\right)\geq d_{2}\left(p+1\right)+2d_{2}+e_{1}

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow

\displaystyle d_{2}\left(p-7\right)+\alpha\left(p-2\right)-e_{1}\geq 0

If
\alpha>0
we see, using

and
p\geq 11
, that the l.h.s
is strictly positive. If
\alpha=0
we have to prove that

By (
2.19
),
4e_{2}+2e_{1}+e_{0}=4d_{2}+2e_{1}+e_{0}=\beta\leq p-1
,

Since

\displaystyle d_{2}\left(p-7\right)-e_{1}\geq d_{2}\left(p-7\right)-\frac{p-5}{2}

(2.2). Suppose that
