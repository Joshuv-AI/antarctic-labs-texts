Sulfur as a central integrator of plant–microbe interactions: from nutrient cycling to immune signalling and microbiome assembly
Authors: Büsra Elkatmis, Gözde Merve Türksoy, Enrique Rodríguez, Bilal Rahmoune, Anna Koprivova, Stanislav Kopriva, Karl-Josef Dietz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSulfur is an essential macronutrient that underpins plant growth, stress resilience, and immunity. Beyond its role in primary metabolism, sulfur is incorporated into a diverse array of secondary metabolites that mediate plant–microbe interactions. In this review, we summarize current knowledge on how microbial sulfur metabolism contributes to plant sulfur nutrition and how plant-derived sulfur-containing compounds shape microbial community assembly and disease outcomes. Microorganisms mobilize organic sulfur in soils through sulfatase activity, volatile sulfur production, and sulfoquinovose degradation, thereby enhancing plant sulfur availability, particularly under limiting conditions. Conversely, plants deploy sulfur-rich metabolites, including volatile organic compounds, glucosinolates, and the phytoalexin camalexin, to restrict pathogens, modulate beneficial associations, and structure rhizosphere communities. These compounds act not only as antimicrobial agents but also as ecological filters that balance defense with microbiome homeostasis. Emerging evidence indicates that sulfur availability and metabolic flux influence the composition and function of plant-associated microbiota, linking primary nutrient assimilation to immune regulation. By integrating insights from sulfur biochemistry, microbial ecology, and plant immunity, we highlight sulfur metabolism as a central node in plant–microbe interactions. Understanding the dynamic exchange of sulfur between plants and their microbiota will be essential for improving crop resilience and sustainable nutrient management in sulfur-limited agricultural systems.Keywords: Camalexin, glucosinolates, microbiome, plant defense, plant growth-promoting bacteria, S-containing metabolites, sulfur, volatiles

Introduction

Plants inhabit complex microbial ecosystems in which beneficial, commensal, and pathogenic microorganisms co-exist (Bulgarelli et al., 2013). Beneficial microorganisms contribute to plant performance by improving nutrient bioavailability (Hacquard et al., 2015), protecting plants against adverse environmental conditions (de Zelicourt et al., 2018) and suppressing pathogens (Duran et al., 2018; Carrion et al., 2019). In contrast, harmful microbes cause a large variety of plant diseases, with significant impacts on crop production (Strange and Scott, 2005). Plants evolved diverse mechanisms to communicate with the microbiota, selectively attracting beneficial organisms while defending against harmful ones. Many of these mechanisms rely on plant metabolites, several of which can fulfill both of these functions (reviewed in Sasse et al., 2018; Jacoby et al., 2020; Somssich et al., 2025). Among mineral nutrients, sulfur (S) occupies a uniquely versatile and often underappreciated position in these regulatory networks (Takahashi et al., 2011; Mukherjee et al., 2025a).

S is indispensable for primary metabolism as a constituent of cysteine, methionine, coenzymes, prosthetic groups, and the central redox buffer glutathione. The chemical flexibility and redox versatility of S, namely its ability to cycle between multiple oxidation states, underlies its central role in redox regulation and stress adaptation (Jacob and Anwar, 2008). Plants take up S as inorganic sulfate, reduce it, and incorporate it as sulfide into O-acetylserine (OAS), to form Cys, the first product of sulfate assimilation (Takahashi et al., 2011). Cys is incorporated into proteins, converted into Met and glutathione (GSH), or serves as an S donor for the synthesis of numerous S-containing metabolites, that contribute to innate immunity (Rausch and Wachter, 2005).

Bacterial S metabolism is likewise critical for plant interactions. For example, sulfation is required for the function of many Nod factors in Rhizobium symbiosis (Schwedock and Long, 1990), and sulfate assimilation genes CysH and CysM are necessary for the plant growth-promoting (PGP) effect of Pseudomonas fluorescens strain SS101 (Cheng et al., 2017). Similarly, disruption of S assimilation pathway reduces virulence in multiple fungal and bacterial pathogens (Shen et al., 2002; Zhao et al., 2022; Lee et al., 2023).

Recent advances in synthetic community approaches, isotope tracing, and multi-omics analyses have begun to reveal how tightly coordinated plant and microbial S metabolism governs both mutualistic and antagonistic interactions. Yet, critical mechanistic questions remain unresolved, particularly regarding S fluxes between partners, perception of S-containing volatiles, and ecological relevance under field conditions. Here, we summarize current knowledge on (i) microbial contributions to plant S nutrition, (ii) S-containing volatile organic compounds (VOCs) as mediators of long-distance plant–microbe communication, (iii) S-dependent plant defense metabolites in pathogen resistance and microbiome assembly, and (iv) the role of S metabolism in beneficial interactions and abiotic stress tolerance. We propose that S metabolism acts as a central integrator linking nutrient cycling, immune signaling, redox regulation, and microbial community dynamics. Since the most intensively studied S-containing specialized metabolites, glucosinolates and camalexin, are restricted to the Brassicales, this family, including the model plant Arabidopsis thaliana, is over-represented in the examples discussed. Nevertheless, many underlying mechanisms are conserved across plant species.

Beneficial impact of microbial sulfur metabolism on plants

Microbes in plant sulfur nutrition

In nature, S can be transformed between organic and inorganic forms and multiple oxidation states through both natural and anthropogenic processes (Z. Zhou et al., 2025). Biogenic transformations are major drivers of the global S cycle and have played essential roles in the evolution of life on Earth (Mateos et al., 2023). S is ubiquitous in terrestrial and marine environments, as well as in the atmosphere, thereby influencing climate dynamics (Z. Zhou et al., 2025). The S cycle has changed substantially due to anthropogenic activities. Since the industrial revolution, increasing S emissions led to acid rain and severe health and environmental problems. Following implementation of the Clean Air Act in 1970, S depositions decreased significantly and S has again become limiting in many agricultural systems (Hinckley et al., 2020). Because microorganisms drive key S transformations, biological S metabolism strongly impacts global S cycling, plant productivity, and ultimately human health (Mukherjee et al., 2025a).

Although plants take up S primarily as inorganic sulfate, most soil S occurs in organic forms that are not directly accessible to plants. Microorganisms can utilize these organic S sources and mineralize them, thereby providing S to plants (Kertesz and Mirleau, 2004). Three major pathways for mineralization of organic S are well established: (i) hydrolysis of sulfates and sulfonates by sulfatases (Kertesz et al., 2007); (ii) synthesis of the volatile compound dimethyl disulfide (DMDS) (Meldau et al., 2013); and (iii) metabolism of sulfoquinovose, the headgroup of plant sulfolipids (Snow et al., 2021).

Sulfatases are hydrolytic enzymes that operate via a unique catalytic mechanism involving formylglycine, generated post-translationally from Cys or Ser residues (Dierks et al., 1999). These enzymes enable bacteria to desulfurize diverse organic sulfates and sulfonates, acquiring both carbon and S (Kertesz, 2000; Barbeyron et al., 2016). Sulfatase-producing bacteria are common in the rhizosphere, and their activity often contributes directly to the PGP effects (Kertesz and Mirleau, 2004). Indeed, loss of the ability to utilize organic sulfates in the PGP bacterium Pseudomonas putida S-313R abolished its PGP effect, highlighting sulfatase activity as a key determinant of sulfate availability to plants.

A well-characterized S-dependent mechanism of bacterial PGP activity, particularly under low S supply, is the production of DMDS (Fig. 1) by Bacillus sp. B55 (Meldau et al., 2013). This strain colonizes wild tobacco Nicotiana attenuata and enhances plant growth via volatile emissions. S derived from DMDS can be incorporated into the plant organic matter, as demonstrated by tracer experiments (Meldau et al., 2013; and see below). In contrast to sulfate mineralization and DMDS production from various S-containing substrates, bacterial sulfoquinovose degradation (sulfoglycolysis) begins with a defined substrate, sulfoquinovose (Fig. 1), yet the specific form of S made available to plants remain unclear (Snow et al., 2021). Sulfoquinovose is among the most abundant organic S compounds in soil, with an estimated annual production of 10 billion tons (Sharma et al., 2022). Bacteria metabolize sulfoquinovose as a carbon source via distinct pathways, releasing S in forms such as sulfate, sulfite, sulfolactate, or dihydroxypropane-sulfonate (Snow et al., 2021). Although the biochemical details of these pathways are increasingly understood (Burchill et al., 2025), their quantitative contribution to plant S nutrition remains to be determined.

Beyond these defined pathways, numerous enzymatic reactions interconvert S compounds in the biosphere, including processes generating sulfide, oxidizing thiosulfate to sulfate, or producing dimethyl sulfide (Mukherjee et al., 2025a). Genes encoding these reactions are enriched in rhizosphere microorganisms compared with bulk soil, suggesting coordinated S metabolism between plants and microbes in the rhizosphere (Mukherjee et al., 2025a). Supporting this view, inoculation with mixed S-oxidizing and S-reducing bacteria increased nitrogen, phosphorus, and S accumulation in canola when combined with inorganic fertilizer (Aslam et al., 2025). Likewise, an 18-member synthetic rhizosphere bacterial community (SynCom) enhanced biomass of Arabidopsis and Brassica rapa var. parachinensis under S deficiency (Mukherjee et al., 2025b). This effect was attributed to extracellular GSH secreted by the microbiome, which alleviated S deficiency-induced oxidative stress and was confirmed by revealing that deletion of the GSH biosynthetic gene gshA abolished the PGP effect (Mukherjee et al., 2025b). However, for this and other microbial S transformations, ecological significance in natural ecosystems and potential for improving S use efficiency in the agricultural context remain to be elucidated.

Sulfur-containing bacterial volatile organic compounds and their effects on plants

Among the diverse microbial S transformations in the rhizosphere, the emission of S-containing VOCs represents a particularly dynamic interface between microbial metabolism and plant physiology. VOCs play important roles in plant communication with both beneficial and pathogenic microorganisms (Weisskopf et al., 2021). Bacterial VOCs belong to several classes of chemical compounds, including ketones, terpenes, nitrogen-containing volatiles, aromatic compounds, pyrazines, and many S-containing compounds (Weisskopf et al., 2021). Among these, S-containing VOCs (S-VOCs) are particularly reactive due to the redox properties of S and can act over short and long distances. They are by-products of bacterial primary and secondary metabolism and are derived mainly from H2S and methanethiol (Schulz and Dickschat, 2007). Many volatiles and some of their functions have been reported, but a comprehensive inventory of VOCs and an understanding of their functions are still far from being complete (Kemmler et al., 2025). It is particularly crucial to characterize the functions and biosynthesis of additional VOCs, as they represent important alternatives to the use of fertilizers and pesticides (Thomas et al., 2020).

Numerous studies have identified bacterial S-VOCs with beneficial properties. One of the most well-known S-VOC is DMDS, which has been found as a dominant compound in the VOC bouquets of various bacterial species, including Burkholderia ambifaria, Bacillus sp. B55, and Pseudomonas tolaasii (Groenhagen et al., 2013; Meldau et al., 2013; Lo Cantore et al., 2015). Treatment with bacterial VOCs, as well as DMDS as a pure compound in low concentrations, can promote the growth of Arabidopsis and other plant species. Similarly, a study on 40 bacterial strains isolated from desert plants such as agaves and cacti, revealed that S-VOCs, such as DMDS, dimethyl trisulfide (DMTS), and 3-(methylthio)-1-propanol, were dominant compounds in their VOCs and promoted the growth of Arabidopsis and Nicotiana benthamiana (Fig. 2) (Camarena-Pozos et al., 2019). Furthermore, S-VOCs were also prevalent in VOC bouquets of a 16-member SynCom derived from Arabidopsis roots, with compounds such as DMDS, 2-methyl-5-(methylthio)furan, and 2,4-dithiapentane showing a PGP activity in a pure form (Türksoy et al., 2025). Additionally, DMDS and benzothiazole were among the VOCs responsible for PGP activities of several Burkholderia strains on Malus domestica (Zeng et al., 2025). However, while these pure compounds show promise as biofertilizers in laboratory settings, the application in real field conditions as growth stimulants poses challenges. Concentrations that promote growth or protect against pathogens in the laboratory may not have the same effects in the field, and could even be toxic at higher doses. For instance, DMDS was found to be toxic at higher concentrations in some studies (Lo Cantore et al., 2015), and the field application of methanesulfoxide as a fungicide was toxic to other organisms (Joller et al., 2020). Therefore, it is crucial to establish systems that closely mimic natural conditions when using these VOCs as growth stimulants.

To overcome such obstacles, researchers have explored using communities instead of single strains, or establishing more natural systems with soil. Türksoy et al. (2025) demonstrated that a SynCom produced growth-promoting VOCs dominated by S-containing metabolites. Removing high DMDS-producing strains or even all strains with PGP VOC cocktails did not diminish the overall PGP property of the SynCom (Türksoy et al., 2025). Soil-based systems, where bacteria and soil/pot-grown plants are separated by filters have also been employed; however, they may lead to side effects such as CO2 overaccumulation (Park et al., 2015; Tahir et al., 2017). Even though such systems are difficult to implement in field conditions, the priming effects of VOCs offer an encouraging and simple approach for applying either pure volatile compounds or whole volatile blends from bacteria and bacterial communities.

Despite the identification of volatiles that promote growth and stress tolerance, there is still a lack of understanding regarding how plants perceive them as signals, or whether they might be assimilated in plant metabolism. So far, no receptor has been identified for VOCs, except for ethylene (Bleecker et al., 1988). Among the S-VOCs, only the DMDS mode of action has been studied in detail. DMDS emitted by Bacillus sp. B55 can serve as an S nutrient source in N. attenuata (Meldau et al., 2013). 35S isotope labeling revealed the uptake and incorporation of S from VOCs into plant proteins. The PGP effect was clearly evident in sulfate-deficient conditions, so the ecological relevance of this process might be limited to S-deficient soils (Meldau et al., 2013). The growth-promoting effect of DMDS is dependent on OAS (thiol) lyase (OAS-TL), since loss of cytosolic or both plastidic and mitochondrial OAS-TL isoforms abolished the growth-promoting effect (Türksoy et al., 2025). This indicates that DMDS is reduced to methanethiol and in a reaction with OAS it is incorporated into S-methylcysteine, in analogy with the methionine degradation pathway. Indeed, metabolite analysis of shoots of Arabidopsis exposed to the community VOCs revealed an increase in S-methylcysteine concentration (Türksoy et al., 2025).

S-VOCs not only promote growth and S metabolism, but also influence nutrient homeostasis in different plant species (Fig. 2). For instance, volatiles emitted by Serratia plymuthica and Fusarium culmorum affected nutrient levels in Zea mays in a glass jar system (Martín-Sánchez et al., 2020). Analogously, bacterial VOCs enhanced iron acquisition, biomass, rhizosphere acidification, and chlorophyll content of Medicago truncatula, under both iron-limiting and non-limiting conditions. DMDS was detected in their volatile blend, along with another S-containing compound, DMTS (Orozco-Mosqueda Mdel et al., 2013).

Additionally, S-VOCs exhibit antimicrobial properties. For instance, S-VOCs produced by Paraburkholderia graminis contributed to the suppressiveness of soils against fungal pathogens (Carrion et al., 2018). DMDS protected tobacco and corn plants against Botrytis cinerea and Cochliobolus heterostrophus (Huang et al., 2012), and exhibited fungicidal activity against Sclerotinia minor (Tyagi et al., 2020). In addition, DMDS application on fungal-infected tomato plants induced overexpression of defense-related genes PR1 and PR5, suggesting activation of the salicylic acid pathway (Tyagi et al., 2020). DMDS action is not restricted to fungal pathogens; it also inhibits growth of the oomycete Phytophthora infestans (De Vrieze et al., 2015). Among other S-VOCs, DMTS and S-methyl methanethiosulfonate (MMTS) were able to control potato infection by P. infestans (Chinchilla et al., 2019). Importantly, MMTS did not activate plant defenses prior to infection and directly inhibited the pathogen without being phytotoxic. However, its potential field application is limited, as it can be toxic to plants, bacteria, and nematodes, though it may still be used for direct oomycete control (Joller et al., 2020). DMTS has also been reported to reduce the incidence of Botryosphaeria dothidea, the causal agent of ring rot on apple fruit, and was effective in post-harvest protection. In addition to disease suppression, DMTS increased sugar content and vitamin C levels in apple fruit (Sun et al., 2022).

The protective action of VOCs can be attributed to their direct effects on pathogens and their ability to enhance plant innate immunity (Ryu et al., 2004; Erb et al., 2015). There is growing evidence that VOCs can prime plants to better withstand pathogen or herbivore attacks as well as to promote plant growth and yield. VOC blends containing S-VOCs have been particularly effective in priming plants for growth enhancement. For instance, Arabidopsis and Lactuca sativa treated with VOCs from a root-associated Microbacterium strain showed increased shoot biomass, flower number, and stem length upon transfer to soil. Transcriptomic analyses revealed that VOC treatment influenced plant S and nitrogen metabolism (Cordovez et al., 2018). Similarly, priming Arabidopsis with VOCs from a 16-member SynCom, which includes DMDS and many other S-VOCs, resulted in growth promotion and altered root colonization patterns (Türksoy et al., 2025). This priming affected root exudate composition, with an initial increase in exudation followed by a decrease. Furthermore, the concentration of S-containing defense compounds, indolic glucosinolates and camalexin, decreased, potentially to facilitate colonization (Türksoy et al., 2025). Interestingly, the plant-derived S-VOC, dipropyl disulfide, was also found to prime neighboring plants and influence root exudate composition, resulting in altered microbiome composition (Zhou et al., 2024).

These findings collectively suggest that bacterial S-VOCs, especially DMDS, play critical roles in plant–microbe interactions (Fig. 2). S-VOCs are emitted by various bacterial species and impact a wide range of plant species. While the biosynthesis of the VOCs is relatively well understood, the receptors in plants, signaling pathways, and detailed mechanisms of action remain largely unexplored. Discovering new bacterial VOCs with plant bioactivity is challenging due to issues with identification and availability of the compounds. Although DMDS may provide S for plant metabolism, this is not confirmed for other S-VOCs. It is also unclear if the positive effects observed in laboratory settings translate to natural ecosystems. Further research is needed to understand the mechanisms and potential agricultural uses of these S-VOCs.

Plant sulfur-containing metabolites interacting with microbiota

Role of plant sulfur-containing metabolites in pathogen defense

S has a long history of use as a phytosanitary agent dating back to ancient civilizations, such as the Sumerians and the Greeks. It is still used in modern pesticide formulations, such as fungicides, insecticides, acaricides, and nematicides (Umetsu and Shirai, 2020). The use of S in agriculture reflects the natural defense mechanisms of plants. Plants rely on S-containing compounds for their defense systems, reflected in the concept of S-induced resistance and S-enhanced defense (Rausch and Wachter, 2005; Bloem et al., 2015). These compounds, such as glucosinolates, S-containing phytoalexins, and S-VOCs, play a crucial role in plant immunity against a wide range of pathogens, parasites, and herbivores (Halkier and Gershenzon, 2006; Burow et al., 2008; Kunstler et al., 2020; Somssich et al., 2025). Their diverse structures and biosynthetic pathways make them powerful defense systems in a wide range of plant species.

Glucosinolates (Fig. 1), present in the Brassicales, are phytoanticipins, that are stored within plant tissue and act as pre-formed precursors of antimicrobial compounds. Tissue disruption or internal signaling pathways activate myrosinase-mediated hydrolysis of glucosinolates, generating bioactive breakdown products such as isothiocyanates, nitriles, and thiocyanates. These compounds provide defense against pathogens and herbivores (Halkier and Gershenzon, 2006; for details see below), and also play a crucial role in plant–insect chemical signaling and multitrophic interactions (Fig. 3) (Hopkins et al., 2009). Over 130 glucosinolate variants have been identified in plants (Blazevic et al., 2020), featuring a sulfated thioglucoside core linked to variable amino acid-derived side chains (Fig. 1). The chemical diversity of glucosinolates arises from side chain modifications that determine their biochemical properties and bioactivities of the degradation products (Halkier and Gershenzon, 2006). Glucosinolates are categorized into three groups based on the amino acids from which they are synthesized: aliphatic glucosinolates from Met, Val, Leu, Ile, and Ala, indolic glucosinolates from Trp, and benzenic glucosinolates from Phe or Tyr (Blazevic et al., 2020). The synthesis of glucosinolates involves three major stages—chain elongation of some precursor amino acids, core structure synthesis, and secondary modifications. Biosynthesis is tightly linked to S metabolism, providing S for the core glucosinolate structures (Mugford et al., 2009; Sonderby et al., 2010).

Organosulfur compounds in Allium species, such as garlic or onion, act as S-containing phytoanticipins, similar to glucosinolates. These species produce amino acid-based metabolites, such as alliin, isoalliin, and methiin, and store them in the cytosol (Abdalla and Mühling, 2019; Yoshimoto et al., 2022). Upon cellular damage, these compounds are metabolized by alliinase, a cysteine sulfoxide lyase normally localized in the vacuole. The main product formed is allicin, derived from alliin, which has direct anti-microbial effects, due to its redox activity and ability to easily penetrate cell membranes. Allicin can also rearrange into various S-VOCs, such as diallyl sulfides, dithiins, and ajoene (Nwachukwu et al., 2012). Alliinase is not specific to Allium species; it is also found in the medicinal herb Petiveria alliacea, where it activates petiveriin into phenylmethanesulfenic acid (PMSA). This compound can be further converted into thiosulfinate petivericin or phenylmethane thial-S-oxide (Musah et al., 2009). These and other S-containing compounds from P. alliacea exhibit biological activity against various pathogenic fungi and bacteria (Kim et al., 2006).

S-containing phytoalexins are the next major functional class of defense metabolites. Indolic phytoalexins specific to the Brassicales, such as camalexin, brassinin, and cyclobrassinin, have been well studied and also possess health-promoting activities (Chripkova et al., 2014; Abdalla and Mühling, 2019). However, not all S-containing phytoalexins contain the indole group, as seen, for instance, in nasturlexins (Fig. 1) (Pedras and To, 2015). In Arabidopsis and Camelina sativa, camalexin (Fig. 1) is synthesized from tryptophan through a series of reactions catalyzed by P450 enzymes (Bednarek, 2012; for details on camalexin function, see below). In other Brassica species, brassinin is derived from the degradation product of glucobrassicin, indol-3-ylmethyl-isothiocyanate (ITC) (Klein and Sattely, 2017). Elemental sulfur (S0) is also a phytoalexin, produced in economically important plants from various plant families. It is highly toxic to several ascomycetes, effectively controlling major diseases such as powdery mildew (Cooper and Williams, 2004).

The two S-containing amino acids, Cys and Met, are essential for maintaining plant health and activating defense mechanisms (Moormann et al., 2022). Cys residues are major targets for oxidative post-translational modifications, such as sulfenylation, S-nitrosylation, and disulfide bond formation. Being reversible, they fine-tune the activity of core immune proteins, including NADPH oxidases, cysteine-rich receptors, and kinases involved in pathogen-associated molecular pattern (PAMP) perception, making Cys-centered redox control a core element of plant immunity (Couturier et al., 2013). In Arabidopsis, Cys acts as both a metabolic precursor and an independent signaling molecule. Exogenous Cys application induces proteome responses similar to those caused by biotic stress, increases levels of the phytoalexin camalexin, and enhances resistance to Pseudomonas syringae pv. tomato DC3000 (Moormann et al., 2025). Thus, Cys serves as a precursor for secondary metabolites, but also contributes to a metabolic communication system that integrates danger perception, redox signaling, and defense activation, enhancing plant immunity. Cys and GSH play crucial roles in plant defense against fungal and bacterial pathogens by modulating redox homeostasis. After fungal infections by Blumeria graminis, an accumulation of GSH in the apoplast triggers resistance signaling, whereas mitochondrial GSH depletion in Arabidopsis leaves upon B. cinerea infection leads to reactive oxygen species (ROS)-mediated cell death that restricts pathogen spread (Simon et al., 2013). Additionally, Cys-rich proteins, such as membrane-associated thionins, exhibit strong antifungal activity against Cladosporium cucumerinum and B. cinerea, and are toxic to lepidopteran larvae such as Helicoverpa spp. (Kunstler et al., 2020).

Also Met plays a crucial role in coordinating metabolism and immune responses in plants. Its derivative, S-adenosylmethionine (SAM), is a key precursor of ethylene and polyamines, two major defense signaling molecules (Sauter et al., 2013). The Met cycle helps to balance growth and defense in plants. Marco et al. (2014) showed that overexpression of SAM decarboxylase 1 (SAMDC1) in Arabidopsis elevated spermine levels and constitutively up-regulated defense genes and jasmonic acid biosynthesis genes, conferring enhanced resistance to P. syringae pv. tomato DC3000 and pv. maculicola ES4326, and Hyaloperonospora arabidopsidis without growth penalties (Marco et al., 2014). Additionally, the methionine cycle is critical for antiviral resistance, and its modulation can increase viral susceptibility (Makinen and De, 2019).

The importance of S metabolism in enhancing immunity has been further demonstrated by in planta bacterial transcriptomics. During the early stages of pattern-triggered immunity, virulence genes were induced in P. syringae pv. tomato DC3000, but in later stages genes related to S starvation response were activated (Lovelace et al., 2018). Moreover, upon pathogen inoculation, plants induced transcription of genes for sulfate transporters, indicating a mechanism whereby plants withdraw sulfate from the apoplast to limit bacterial growth in leaves. In this respect, S resembles iron, which is a common subject of the tug of war between the host and the pathogen (Gerner et al., 2020).

Altogether, these mechanisms explain the empirical S-induced resistance in crops (Bloem et al., 2015). However, there is a metabolic cost associated with the S-mediated defense, highlighting trade-offs between immunity, growth, and symbiotic interactions. S is preferentially allocated to primary assimilation in the morning and to the glucosinolate pool in the evening (Huseby et al., 2013), supporting the idea of growth versus defense trade-off. The metabolic costs can be minimized by the activation of S-rich pathways only as a consequence of biotic stress, such as for the S-containing phytoalexins, or by a regulatory interconnection with primary S metabolism (Malhotra et al., 2023). Indeed, glucosinolates serve as a pool of S which can be recycled into primary products during seed germination and seedling establishment under limited S supply (Sugiyama et al., 2021). S deficiency induces accumulation of Sulfur Deficiency Induced (SDI1) and SDI2 proteins, which repress the transcription of genes involved in aliphatic glucosinolate synthesis (Aarabi et al., 2016). This reallocation of S towards primary metabolism compromises chemical defense but enhances protein synthesis. In line with this, pathogen-challenged S-limited Arabidopsis plants show compromised resistance to Alternaria brassicicola (Kruse et al., 2007). Collectively, these findings position S metabolism as a central resource allocation hub, mediating strategic trade-offs between growth, immunity, and symbiotic benefits under varying nutrient and biotic conditions.

Glucosinolates, central sulfur metabolites of the Brassicales, in plant–microbe interactions

Glucosinolate biosynthesis is strongly affected by environmental conditions, allowing plants to adjust defenses against biotic stresses and shape rhizosphere microbiome composition (Fig. 3) (Mitreiter and Gigolashvili, 2021; Kattel and Antonious, 2025). Upon pathogen attack, local biosynthesis of 4-methoxyglucobrassinin (4-methoxyindol-3-ylmethyl glucosinolate) is activated to prioritize defense mechanisms (Bednarek et al., 2009). This response is orchestrated by complex transcriptional and epigenetic regulation as well as redistribution through specialized membrane transporters, to optimize concentrations at infection sites (Xu et al., 2016). The transcriptional regulation is primarily dependent on two groups of transcription factors, MYB28/29/76 for aliphatic glucosinolates and MYB51/34/122 for indolic glucosinolates, which require interaction with MYC2/3/4, connecting the regulation to jasmonate response (Schweizer et al., 2013). Numerous studies emphasize glucosinolate biosynthesis as a key pivot in plant–pathogen co-evolution, tightly linked to S metabolism modulation, hormonal defense regulation (especially jasmonate and salicylate), and integration of biotic signals (Fig. 3) (Cacho et al., 2015; Kopriva and Gigolashvili, 2016; Liu et al., 2016; Sugiyama et al., 2021). Natural genetic variation in glucosinolate production under biotic stress is well documented in A. thaliana, where polymorphisms in genes such as AOP2, MYB28, and MAM3 determine aliphatic glucosinolate profiles, influencing pathogen and herbivore resistance through genotype–environment interactions (Chan et al., 2011; Kerwin et al., 2015). Transcriptomic studies revealed significant enrichment of transcription of glucosinolate biosynthesis genes and insect response pathways during infection, and highlighted regulation by biotic stress-related phytohormones (Liu et al., 2024). Accordingly, proteomic analysis revealed that in Arabidopsis leaves, methyl jasmonate (MeJA) up-regulates oxidative stress tolerance, glucosinolate metabolism, S assimilation, and defense pathways, while it represses photosynthesis and carbohydrate anabolism, thereby redirecting resources from growth to defense (Chen et al., 2011). These changes, coupled with root defense priming, maintain glucosinolate profile variation during attacks.

Glucosinolates themselves do not have direct antimicrobial activity; the antifungal, antibacterial, and anti-oomycete effects are attributed to their breakdown products, particularly ITCs (Bednarek et al., 2009; Clay et al., 2009; Fan et al., 2011). The details on the multifaceted function of glucosinolates in pathogen defense, their target organisms, mechanisms, and ecological significance can be found in multiple reviews and the references therein (Kunstler et al., 2020; Plaszko et al., 2021; Z. Liu et al., 2021; Eugui et al., 2022; W. Yang et al., 2024; Somssich et al., 2025). In short, the various glucosinolate degradation products act through multiple mechanisms, including disrupting cell membranes, triggering ROS accumulation, inhibiting enzymes, altering surface hydrophobicity, inhibiting motility, and repelling pathogens.

Besides their role in defense, glucosinolates and their breakdown products belong to an increasing number of plant secondary metabolites that are associated with shifts in the composition of plant-associated microbiota (Jacoby et al., 2021). For example, community structures in the rhizosphere changed when exposed to the degradation products of an additionally produced glucosinolate sinalbin (p-hydroxybenzyl glucosinolate) in a transgenic Arabidopsis overexpressing CYP79A1 (Bressan et al., 2009). Similarly, changes in rhizosphere bacterial community composition of Brassica napus correlated with the soil concentration of 2-phenylethyl-ITC, which is derived from gluconasturtiin (phenethyl glucosinolate; Rumberger and Marschner, 2004). Atypical myrosinases such as the PYK10 (BGLU23) and BGLU21 localized in endoplasmic reticulum (ER) bodies contribute to the production of bioactive metabolites from glucosinolates as evidenced from experiments with the Arabidopsis double mutant pyk10 bglu21 that showed altered bacterial and fungal root-associated communities compared with the wild type (Basak et al., 2024). Similar alterations were observed in the nai1 mutant, which lacks functional ER bodies, confirming the importance of these structures for Arabidopsis interaction with microbiota and suggesting that glucosinolate degradation occurs before root exudation (Basak et al., 2024). The myrosinase reactions can have diverse outcomes, depending on the presence of specifier proteins directing the reaction toward nitriles, epithionitriles, and thiocyanates (Kissen and Bones, 2009). Manipulation of the degradation pathway in the Arabidopsis triple nsp mutant, which does not produce nitriles, also caused distinct shifts in rhizosphere β-diversity, which was distinct from mutants impaired in glucosinolate biosynthesis (Chroston et al., 2024). These findings highlight that not only glucosinolates per se, but also the specific myrosinases and the type of glucosinolate hydrolysis products contribute to the assembly of root-associated microbial communities. Additionally, specific glucosinolates can enrich certain microbial taxa in leaf communities (Unger et al., 2024). Analyses of wild Arabidopsis populations revealed that sinigrin (allyl-glucosinolate) enriches specific taxa because they can use it as a carbon source, in contrast to glucoraphanin (4-methylsulfinylbutyl glucosinolate). Degrading sinigrin, certain strains of the Comamonadaceae and Oxalobacteraceae strains cross-feed other taxa, enabling formation of complex communities (Unger et al., 2024). While the role of glucosinolates in shaping plant-associated microbiota is increasingly recognized, similar principles appear to operate in animal systems.

Plant-derived glucosinolates and ITCs also impact the composition and function of the animal gut microbiota, as the animals feed on glucosinolate-producing plants. They enhance bacterial diversity, stimulate production of beneficial microbial metabolites, and modulate host immune responses, leading to anti-inflammatory and anticarcinogenic effects (Sikorska-Zimny and Beneduce, 2021). The most frequently studied dietary glucosinolates involved in these modulations are glucoraphanin and glucobrassicin (indol-3-ylmethyl glucosinolate). When plant myrosinase is inactivated through cooking, the gut microbiota can partially compensate by converting glucosinolates into bioactive ITCs, such as sulforaphane [4-(methylsulfinyl)butyl-ITC], through myrosinase-like activity (Dmytriv et al., 2025). Dietary supplementation with glucoraphanin in obese C57BL/6J mice fed a high-fat diet reduced body weight gain and improved glucose tolerance, compared with control mice (Bankole et al., 2024). This was accompanied by a restoration of gut eubiosis, a significant decrease in the Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes ratio, a hallmark of obesogenic dysbiosis, and broad remodeling of microbial diversity, community composition, and the plasma metabolome (Bankole et al., 2024). Controlled human intervention studies have shown that broccoli consumption alters gut microbiota β-diversity, reduces accumulation of Firmicutes, and increases Bacteroidetes, resulting in a lower Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes ratio, similar to glucoraphanin supplementation (Kaczmarek et al., 2019). Indeed, these shifts correlated with accumulation of glucosinolate-derived metabolites in plasma and urine. Consistently, in vitro experiments with human fecal microbiota fed broccoli extracts confirmed that the microbiota broke down glucosinolates, and corresponding ITCs accumulated in the extract (Zhang et al., 2022). Moreover, feeding the extract increased microbial diversity and caused marked restructuring of microbial communities (Zhang et al., 2022). While dietary glucosinolates are clearly implicated in shaping gut microbiota composition and function, most evidence derives from whole-food extracts or in vitro models. More research is needed to understand the specific effects of individual glucosinolates on the gut microbiota (Sikorska-Zimny and Beneduce, 2021). Additionally, controlled animal studies are necessary to disentangle the variability driven by microbiota and to elucidate the mechanisms underlying glucosinolate–microbiome–health interactions.

Further investigations are needed to assess the mechanisms by which glucosinolates and their degradation products shape microbiomes in the plant and animal kingdoms. Comparable effects of glucosinolates and derived products have been observed in the microbiota of plant rhizospheres and animal guts, suggesting that myrosinase-mediated degradation plays a key role in the interaction of glucosinolate-producing plants with their associated microbes as well as in effects on intestinal microbiota upon glucosinolate consumption by animals. Glucosinolate production and degradation have been linked to a decreased abundance of opportunistic taxa, such as Pedobacter, Caulobacter, Hirschia, and Ferruginibacter, and an enrichment of beneficial taxa including Pseudoxanthomonas and Brevundimonas (Chroston et al., 2024), similar to improvement of the Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes ratio in the gut microbiome after consuming broccoli extract (Zhang et al., 2022; Bankole et al., 2024). Together, these findings suggest that glucosinolates help to shift structurally and functionally dysbiotic communities towards more balanced microbial assemblages that benefit host health and ecosystem functioning. Although the underlying molecular mechanisms are not fully understood, these convergent outcomes highlight glucosinolates as promising cross-kingdom modulators of microbial community structure, stability, and function. Further research integrating isotope labeling, gnotobiotic systems, and genetic tools is needed to establish causality in these interactions, beyond correlative association.

Camalexin, a model S-containing phytoalexin

Camalexin was first isolated in Camelina sativa, and has since been widely studied for its antimicrobial effect against pathogens such as B. cinerea, P. syringae, Verticillium dahliae, Plasmodiophora brassicae, A. brassicicola, or Burkholderia glumae (Rogers et al., 1996; Nguyen et al., 2022). Loss of camalexin biosynthesis compromises resistance and, notably, can convert otherwise beneficial interactions into dysbiotic outcomes, as demonstrated for the endophytic fungus Piriformospora indica, and oomycetes in multi-kingdom SynComs (Nongbri et al., 2012; Wolinska et al., 2021). Camalexin acts as a phytoalexin, so that its biosynthetic pathway is activated upon recognition of microbe-associated molecular patterns (MAMPs) (Fig. 4) (Finnegan et al., 2016). After recognition of pathogens, calcium bursts activate Ca2+-dependent protein kinases (CPKs), CPK5 and CPK6, while mitogen-activated protein (MAP) kinase cascades activate MPK3 and MPK6. These kinases converge on the transcription factor WRKY33, the master regulator of camalexin synthesis genes (Zhou et al., 2020). Hormonal signals, particularly jasmonate and ethylene, further reinforce this transcriptional activation through interaction with ERF and MYB transcription factors, such as ERF1, ERF72, MYB51, and MYB122 (Frerigmann et al., 2015; Zhou et al., 2020, 2022; D. Wang et al., 2020; Li et al., 2022).

Beyond transcriptional regulation, recent evidence demonstrates epigenetic fine-tuning of camalexin biosynthetic genes. A bivalent chromatin state marked by the repression mark H3K27me3 and the activation mark H3K18ac modulates the timing and amplitude of the camalexin biosynthetic gene activation following elicitation (Zhao et al., 2021). In addition, core biosynthetic enzymes CYP79B2, CYP71A12, CYP71A13, GSTU4, and CYP71B15 assemble into a metabolic complex at the ER surface that allows channeling of tryptophan and its byproducts without the accumulation of intermediates (Mucha et al., 2019). While the precise mechanisms are still being studied, these discoveries pinpoint the complex multilevel regulatory mechanisms and the importance of induction–repression balance for the observed time-specific camalexin response against distinct pathogens. Due to its early discovery and prominent presence in the model species Arabidopsis, the camalexin-mediated immune response to pathogens is well understood and amply evidenced. Most new studies focus not on its antimicrobial function, but rather on the regulatory landscapes underlying the biosynthetic route activation, metabolite mobilization, and its relationship with the nutritional status of the plant.

Although the protective function of camalexin is well established, its precise modes of toxicity vary among target organisms and are not yet comprehensively understood. Proposed mechanisms include: (i) disruption of membrane integrity via binding and destabilization of the phospholipid bilayer (Fig. 4) observed in P. syringae and P. simiae (Rogers et al., 1996); (ii) inhibition of bacterial motility and chemotaxis implied by transcriptomics (J. Zhou et al., 2025); (iii) interference with protein function, such as binding to and inhibiting the AcrB efflux pump in Escherichia coli (Irianti et al., 2025); and (iv) inhibition of viral particle assembly (Liao et al., 2022). In response, distinct fungal pathogens have evolved various strategies to handle camalexin toxicity. Several species, including Rhizoctonia solani, Sclerotinia sclerotiorum, Leptosphera maculans, A. brassicicola, and B. cinerea, perform biotransformations, during which they metabolize camalexin into innocuous or less toxic derivatives by oxidation, hydrolysis, or glycosylation of the original compound (Fig. 4) (Pedras and Abdoli, 2013). Other strategies, found in B. cinerea, include the reduction of camalexin synthesis in the host by dampening the expression of the master regulator WRKY33 (Liu et al., 2017), as well as the expression of the ABC transporter BcatrB, which allows the active pumping of camalexin out of the fungal cytosol (Stefanato et al., 2009), effectively reducing its cytotoxic effect. Overall, while these reciprocal adaptations highlight camalexin as a focal point of plant–pathogen co-evolution, the specific mechanistics behind these effects remain understudied. Defining the particular response mechanisms in bacteria and fungi is essential for a full understanding of the plant–pathogen communication system.

Beyond its antimicrobial function, camalexin has a dual role in shaping plant microbiome composition and function (Koprivova et al., 2019). A genome-wide association study identified a tandem of two Arabidopsis P-450 genes, CYP71A27 and CYP71A28, that contribute to control of variation in sulfatase activity as a proxy for microbiome function (Koprivova et al., 2019). Mutants in core camalexin synthesis genes phenocopied the cyp71A27 mutant and camalexin complemented the reduction in soil sulfatase activity. Interestingly, camalexin was also found to be necessary for the PGP effect of beneficial strains, such as Paraburkholderia phytofirmans PsJN, Pseudomonas sp. CH267, and Pseudomonas simiae WCS417r (Koprivova et al., 2019). This is consistent with camalexin exudation from roots being triggered by elicitors (Millet et al., 2010). Interestingly, camalexin exuded from roots is synthesized in the leaves, implying systemic coordination between above-ground defense perception and below-ground microbiome regulation (Koprivova et al., 2023). This challenges the traditional view of phytoalexins as strictly local defense compounds and positions camalexin as a systemic mediator of microbial balance. Taken together, this evidence suggests that rather than acting solely as a toxin, camalexin appears to prevent microbial overproliferation and dysbiosis, thereby stabilizing beneficial associations.

Additionally, camalexin is part of the priming mechanism for protection against pathogen infection, known as induced systemic resistance (ISR) (Bakker et al., 2018). For instance, root colonization by Trichoderma virens and T. atroviride enhanced growth and induced the expression of defense pathways and camalexin accumulation, which reduced the severity of infection by B. cinerea (Contreras-Cornejo et al., 2011). Similarly, root colonization by the rhizobacterium Bacillus cereus AR156 activated genes for camalexin synthesis, effectively accumulating camalexin, which provided Arabidopsis with a wide-spectrum resistance against leaf infection by different pathogens (Li et al., 2024). Camalexin-mediated ISR against B. cinerea was also primed by the phyllospheric yeast Protomyces arabidopsidicola strain C29 (Wang et al., 2022). Camalexin and other tryptophan-derived compounds are also important for the ability of commensal bacteria to control fungal load in the rhizosphere, thereby promoting Arabidopsis growth and survival (Wolinska et al., 2021). This evidence shows that PGP colonization further benefits Arabidopsis by allowing it to turn post-infection camalexin synthesis into a constitutive resistance factor that represents an ample ecological advantage.

Taken together, camalexin exemplifies the multifunctionality of S-containing defense metabolites. It operates at the intersection of pathogen restriction, microbiome assembly, systemic signaling, and growth–defense balance. Rather than acting solely as a cytotoxic endpoint of immunity, camalexin appears to modulate microbial community dynamics in a manner that prevents dysbiosis while preserving beneficial interactions. Understanding how camalexin concentration, spatial distribution, and metabolic context shape these outcomes remains an important challenge.

The role of plant sulfur metabolism in interactions with beneficial microbes under abiotic stress

Many PGP bacteria enhance plant performance specifically under abiotic stress conditions (Muhammad et al., 2024). They employ various mechanisms to support plants under unfavorable environments, such as modulating phytohormones, enhancing nutrient uptake, activating systemic resistance against pathogen infection, and boosting antioxidant mechanisms in plants (Backer et al., 2018; Muhammad et al., 2024). For example, many PGP strains produce 1-aminocyclopropane-1-carboxylate (ACC) deaminase, an enzyme that breaks down ACC to reduce stress-induced ethylene production in plants, thereby alleviating stress symptoms (C.-H. Liu et al., 2021; Shekhawat et al., 2023). Additionally, certain PGP bacteria modulate enzymatic antioxidants such as superoxide dismutase, catalase, and ascorbate peroxidase, as well as non-enzymatic antioxidants, such as flavonoids, carotenoids, and GSH, to strengthen the defense of the plant under abiotic stress conditions (Q. Wang et al., 2020; Tian et al., 2025). Thus, beneficial microbes play a critical role in mitigating abiotic stress, and S-containing compounds, such as GSH, are often part of these mechanisms (Fig. 5) (Noctor and Foyer, 1998).

Indeed, S has irreplaceable functions in the plant antioxidant system, being essential for the synthesis of key molecules such as GSH, Cys, Met, and others that help to maintain redox balance and protect against abiotic stress (Kopriva et al., 2019). GSH is synthesized through a two-step process catalyzed by γ-glutamylcysteine synthetase and glutathione synthetase (Noctor et al., 2012). GSH is easily oxidized by, for example, ROS to glutathione disulfide (GSSG) and is thus essential for cellular redox homeostasis (Noctor and Foyer, 1998; Noctor et al., 2012). Various GSH-dependent proteins and enzymes, including glutathione peroxidases, glutaredoxins, and glutathione S-transferases (GSTs) further contribute to antioxidant defense in plants (Dixon et al., 2009; Cao et al., 2020; Vogelsang and Dietz, 2022). Recent studies revealed that beneficial microbes not only influence plant antioxidant systems but also impact redox homeostasis through S metabolism and GSH-dependent pathways, aiding plants in coping with abiotic stress (Guo et al., 2023; Shekhawat et al., 2024; Bisht et al., 2025).

In a typical example, a root endophytic bacterium Enterobacter sp. SA187 enhances plant growth under various abiotic stress conditions through multiple mechanisms. It modulates ethylene signaling by producing 2-keto-4-methylthiobutyric acid that is converted to ethylene (de Zelicourt et al., 2018). Additionally, after colonizing plants, SA187 strongly up-regulates genes related to S metabolism (Andres-Barrao et al., 2021). This helps to rescue salt hypersensitivity of Arabidopsis mutants in S metabolism, such as sultr1;2, apr2, or apk1 apk2, and indicates that coordinated regulation of S metabolism between plants and PGP microbes enhances plant salt stress tolerance (Andres-Barrao et al., 2021). SA187 also improves plant tolerance to high light stress by modulating iron and S acquisition (Shekhawat et al., 2024), leading to increased expression of genes related to photosynthesis and oxidative stress responses. Using a GRX1/ro-GFP2 reporter line, Shekhawat et al. (2024) demonstrated that SA187 strengthened redox homeostasis under high-light conditions through regulating iron homeostasis and redox balance.

Another common stress is high alkalinity, which alters water absorption, nutrient availability, and soil pH, impairing plant growth and development (S. Yang et al., 2024). Inoculation with Bacillus sp. Jrh14-10 enhanced Arabidopsis growth under high alkalinity stress by increasing total fresh weight, dry weight, primary root length, and lateral root number (Guo et al., 2023). Interestingly, similar positive effects were observed after exogenous addition of MgSO4, Cys, and GSH. Indeed, transcriptome analysis revealed that Jrh14-10 treatment up-regulated S metabolism genes, including APS3, APR1, APR2, APR3, SERAT3;2, and OASC, leading to increased production of Cy and other S-containing metabolites (Guo et al., 2023). Moreover, Jrh14-10 treatment reduced ROS levels under high alkalinity by enhancing activities of antioxidant enzymes (Guo et al., 2023).

Furthermore, PGP bacteria can improve plant performance under heavy metal stress (Ren et al., 2019). Inoculation with metal-resistant strains Microbacterium oxydans JYC17, Pseudomonas thivervalensis Y1-3-9, and Burkholderia cepacia J62 increased the biomass of B. napus L. grown in copper-contaminated soil and enhanced the levels of antioxidants ascorbic acid (AsA) and GSH in plant leaves. These two antioxidants are key components of the AsA–GSH cycle, which detoxifies hydrogen peroxide (Noctor and Foyer, 1998). The increased AsA and GSH levels following bacterial inoculation suggest that beneficial microbes may enhance antioxidant capacity to mitigate oxidative damage caused by heavy metal toxicity (Ren et al., 2019). In nature, however, plants are often exposed to multiple abiotic stresses, which intensifies their impact on growth. In such conditions, PGP bacteria can provide the greatest benefit. For instance, treatment with Bacillus amyloliquefaciens SN13 increased the growth of rice under drought, salt stress, and nutrient deficiency, by modulating antioxidant enzyme activities and reducing oxidative stress in plants (Bisht et al., 2025). While these studies highlight the importance of S metabolism in plant–microbe interactions under abiotic stress, further reserach is needed to directly demonstrate the contribution of beneficial bacteria to plant S uptake, transport, and metabolic integration.

Conclusions and future perspectives

Across nutritional, defensive, and ecological contexts, S emerges as a central integrator of plant–microbe interactions. S-containing compounds function as nutrients, redox regulators, antimicrobial agents, long-distance signals, and microbiome-shaping metabolites. This multifunctionality enables S metabolism to coordinate growth, immunity, and ecological balance under fluctuating environmental conditions. Despite substantial progress, several key gaps remain. First, the quantitative contribution of microbiota to plant S nutrition is largely inferred from transcriptional and metabolomic data rather than directly measured. Stable isotope-based S flux analyses are urgently needed to resolve microbial-to-plant S transfer under ecologically relevant conditions. Second, while the biosynthesis of S-VOCs is increasingly understood, their perception mechanisms in plants remain largely unknown. Identification of receptors and downstream signaling components represents a major frontier. A broader conceptual challenge lies in integrating S metabolism into systems-level models of plant immunity and microbiome assembly. S allocation is tightly balanced between primary metabolism and defense, reflecting trade-offs between growth and protection. Understanding how plants dynamically regulate this allocation in response to microbial signals and environmental stress will be critical for predictive modeling and crop improvement. Future research combining isotope tracing, synthetic microbial communities, genome editing, and ecological field studies will be instrumental in transforming our current descriptive understanding into a mechanistic framework. Harnessing S metabolism as a target for microbiome engineering and sustainable agriculture represents a promising avenue for improving plant resilience under both biotic and abiotic stress.
