Parenthood and Mental Health Among University Populations in Sub‐Saharan Africa: A Cross‐Sectional study

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsThis study examined the association between parenthood status and psychological well‐being among university staff and students in sub‐Saharan Africa (SSA).MethodsA cross‐sectional, web‐based survey was conducted across 22 universities in four SSA countries using convenience sampling. A total of 1189 university staff and students, including parents and non‐parents, participated. Mental health outcomes were assessed using the Depression, Anxiety, and Stress Scales (DASS‐21). Multinomial logistic regression models were used to examine associations between parenthood status and mental health outcomes after adjusting for sociodemographic and contextual factors.ResultsParticipants with children had lower odds of moderate depression (adjusted odds ratio [AOR] = 0.55; 95% CI 0.40–0.75) and severe anxiety (AOR = 0.65; 95% CI 0.50–0.86) compared with participants without children. Higher odds of severe mental health outcomes were observed among females and respondents from Malawi and Mozambique. Marital stability was associated with lower odds of severe depression and anxiety.ConclusionsParenthood status was associated with lower odds of depression and anxiety among university staff and students in SSA. These findings highlight important sociodemographic and contextual differences in mental health within university populations and underscore the need for targeted, culturally responsive mental health interventions.

Introduction

Mental health is broadly defined as the state of well‐being in which individuals realize their potential, manage life stresses, engage in productive work, and contribute to society. The World Health Organization (WHO) emphasizes that there is “no health without mental health,” highlighting its integral role in overall health [1]. The World Mental Health Report [2] indicates that one in eight individuals worldwide experiences a mental disorder, with prevalence influenced by factors like sex, age, and socio‐economic status. The COVID‐19 pandemic, for instance, amplified mental health issues, contributing to the global surge in conditions such as depression, anxiety, and stress worldwide [3].

In academic settings, the psychological well‐being of staff has become a focal point. Universities are multifaceted institutions, and the university population face challenges in balancing academic, administrative, and personal responsibilities. If these demands are not managed effectively, they can lead to work‐life imbalance, resulting in heightened psychological distress. Research indicates that the university population experiences greater psychological distress than the general population [4, 5]. These challenges are further intensified in university staff with children, as they juggle academic pressures with family obligations.

The psychological impact of parenthood has long been a subject of scholarly interest. While societal norms often present parenthood as a source of fulfillment, empirical evidence indicates a more complex relationship. Parenthood can lead to enhanced life satisfaction and well‐being for some individuals, offering a sense of purpose [6]. However, it also entails considerable emotional and financial stress and the difficulty of balancing work and family responsibilities, which can result in psychological strain and mental health disorders [7, 8, 9]. These strains affect both employed and unemployed parents. For university‐employed parents, these challenges are magnified by the specific pressures associated with academic and administrative roles, including demanding schedules and high job‐related stress [10]. This can lead to burnout, poor mental health, and decreased job satisfaction [11]. The COVID‐19 pandemic further exacerbated these burdens [12].

The relationship between parenthood and mental health is multifaceted and influenced by cultural and socio‐economic contexts. Some studies indicate that parents report better mental health and higher life satisfaction than non‐parents [13, 14, 15]. In contrast, other research suggests that childless individuals exhibit better mental health [16], while some studies find no significant difference between parents and non‐parents [17, 18].

Empirical studies in Western contexts have suggested that parenthood generally has a positive impact on mental health, with many parents reporting lower levels of stress. Notably, employed women, particularly full‐time workers, often experience mental health improvements post‐parenthood compared to men [7].

While extensive research has been conducted on the association between parenthood and mental health in Western countries [7, 9, 13, 16, 19]; there remains a significant gap regarding the psychological well‐being of the university population, particularly in African settings. In many African cultures, parenthood is shaped by communal values, traditional gender roles, and economic dynamics, which can affect the mental health of parents, especially those working in academia. Societal expectations often place caregiving responsibilities disproportionately on women [20], increasing stress for those in the university who try to balance academic demands with family obligations. These challenges are further exacerbated in environments that lack institutional support.

This study aims to examine the psychological well‐being of staff and students in the university community in Africa, particularly those with children, focusing on how cultural, social, and institutional dynamics influence their mental health outcomes. By analyzing both the positive and negative effects of parenthood, the research seeks to provide evidence‐based insights to inform policies and programs that support higher education institutions, promoting both employee well‐being and institutional efficiency. Also, the study findings will contribute to the development of strategies that foster academic excellence while also addressing the mental health needs of staff, especially those with parenting responsibilities.

Methods

This study recruited b, whichoth staff and students because they share common academic environments and stressors. A web‐based cross‐sectional survey was conducted between April 16 and November 18, 2024, across 22 SSA countries. The survey was distributed in both English and Portuguese to ensure accessibility for non‐English‐speaking participants, particularly in Mozambique. Ethical approval was obtained from multiple ethics committees, and informed consent was electronically acquired from all participants.

Participants were university staff, including academic and non‐academic staff, and students from various SSA institutions. A convenience sampling method was employed, and a snowballing technique encouraged participants to share the survey link. The final sample size of 1189 was determined based on a single population proportion statistical formula, ensuring sufficient statistical power for analysis.

The survey used the DASS‐21 (Depression, Anxiety, and Stress Scale‐21) to assess mental health outcomes. It included sociodemographic questions about age, gender, country, marital status, and academic qualifications. The DASS‐21 is a validated instrument capable of distinguishing symptoms of depression, anxiety, and stress. The survey was pretested on 30 participants to ensure validity, with Cronbach's alpha for the DASS‐21 scale being 0.82. The data collection method was designed for broad accessibility, utilizing Google Forms and distributing the survey via social media platforms and email. The study's inclusion criteria allowed participation from individuals with and without children, while excluding incomplete responses to the DASS‐21.

Patient and Public Involvement

This study adopted an inclusive approach, prioritizing the meaningful participation of university staff and students to enrich our understanding of the research issue from diverse perspectives. Quantitative data collection focused on the mental health challenges faced by staff and students (with and without children) through anonymous surveys, which encouraged honest responses and minimized social desirability bias. Since the study was designed for the university population, which also represents the broader public, individuals outside the university were not directly engaged. This approach enabled an exploration of the association between mental health and parenthood in academia.

Data Analysis

Exposure and Outcome Variables

The primary exposure of interest was parenthood status, defined as whether participants had at least one child (yes/no). The primary outcome was mental health status, assessed using the DASS‐21 which measures symptoms of depression, anxiety, and stress, with higher scores indicating greater psychological distress. For analytical purposes, scores were categorized into severity levels (e.g., normal, mild, moderate, and severe) based on established cut‐off points [21].

Potential Confounding Factors

Potential confounding factors examined in the study were based on previous literature as important determinants of mental health outcome [22, 23, 24, 25]. These included age, gender, marital status, faculty category, and country of residence. Age and gender are important because mental health can vary across life stages and between men and women [22]. Marital status was included as it reflects social support and living arrangements. Faculty category captures differences in job roles, work environments, and levels of work‐related stress [24]. Country of residence was also included to account for cultural, economic, and policy differences that may affect mental health outcomes. By including these potential confounders, our statistical analysis method isolated the independent effect of parenthood on mental health while reducing bias from sociodemographic and contextual factors [26].

Statistical Analysis

Sample characteristics were summarized using frequencies and proportions. Mental health outcomes of depression, anxiety, and stress were categorical. These outcomes were treated as nominal categorical variables.

Prior to model selection, we assessed the proportional odds (parallel lines) assumption using the Brant test which revealed that there were statistically significant for all three outcomes—stress (χ² = 19.70), anxiety (χ² = 61.02), and depression (χ² = 67.19), all with p < 0.05 indicating violation of the assumption and that the odds ratios were not consistent across thresholds. Such violations make ordinal logistic regression less reliable and could lead to incorrect or misleading results [27, 28]. Therefore, multinomial logistic regression was used, as it does not require this assumption and allows category‐specific estimates [29, 30].

Multinomial logistic regression was used to examine the association between parenthood status (exposure) and mental health outcomes (categorical DASS‐21 severity levels), with the “normal” category as the reference group. Multinomial logistic regression was employed because the outcome variable comprised multiple categorical levels that were treated as non‐ordinal and did not meet the proportional odds assumption required for ordinal logistic regression, which assumes that the relationship between covariates and outcome categories is constant across thresholds [30]. Both unadjusted and adjusted models were fitted. The adjusted models controlled for potential confounders, including age, gender, marital status, faculty category, and country of residence. We conducted several checks to make sure our results are reliable. These included testing how well the model fits the data, checking that the variables are not too highly correlated, and confirming that the model assumptions are met. We also ran additional analyses to ensure the results were consistent.

Results are presented as odds ratios (ORs) and adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. All analyses were performed using STATA/MP version 14 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA). All statistical tests or analyses were two‐sided.

Results

Participant Characteristics

A total of 1189 respondents participated in the study. Participants were predominantly female (55.8%), with the largest proportion from Nigeria (59.9%), followed by Ghana (21.4%), Mozambique (10.8%), and Malawi (7.9%). Most respondents were not married (81.7%) and identified as Christian (83.4%). Nearly all participants were of African descent (96.3%). Over half (54.3%) had secondary education, while 37.8% had undergraduate or postgraduate qualifications. Approximately 39.9% of participants resided in urban areas, and 35.9% did not disclose their location. For details of the participants' characteristics, see Table 1.

Multivariable Analysis

Adjusted multinomial regression results are shown in Table 4. After adjustment, participants with children had lower odds of moderate depression (AOR = 0.55, 95% CI 0.40–0.75) and severe anxiety (AOR = 0.65, 95% CI 0.50–0.86) compared with those without children. Participants from Malawi and Mozambique had substantially higher odds of moderate and severe stress and depression, as well as severe anxiety, compared with those from Ghana. The likelihood ratio test indicated that including depression, anxiety, and stress significantly improved model fit compared with the intercept‐only model (p < 0.001). Discussion

This study examined the association between parenthood status and mental health outcomes among university staff and students in SSA. The findings demonstrate substantial disparities in depression, anxiety, and stress across sex, marital status, and country of residence. Participants with children had lower adjusted odds of moderate and severe depression and lower odds of severe anxiety compared with those without children, highlighting important sociocultural and contextual influences on mental health within African academic communities.

Higher odds of adverse mental health outcomes were observed among females, unmarried individuals, and respondents from Malawi and Mozambique. These findings are consistent with prior evidence demonstrating that mental health outcomes in African settings are shaped by intersecting social, economic, and gender‐related factors [13, 20, 31, 32]. Participants with secondary education appeared to account for a disproportionately high burden of severe stress, depression, and anxiety. This pattern may reflect social and cultural pressures related to educational attainment, particularly challenges associated with transitioning from secondary education into tertiary education or employment. Previous studies suggest that unsuccessful educational transitions, coupled with socioeconomic stressors, may increase vulnerability to psychological distress among young adults [33, 34, 35]. Gender‐specific pressures during this transition period may further exacerbate mental health risks [36]. Similar findings have been reported in studies examining the preparedness of secondary school graduates for employment and higher education, particularly in low‐ and middle‐income settings.

Consistent with existing literature, female participants were more likely to experience severe stress, depression, and anxiety than males. These findings align with evidence indicating that women often face compounded pressures related to caregiving responsibilities, societal expectations, and biological factors that increase susceptibility to mental health disorders [37, 38, 39]. The disproportionate mental health burden observed among women in this study mirrors patterns reported in both African and global contexts.

Marital status also emerged as an important determinant of mental health. Unmarried individuals demonstrated higher odds of severe depression and anxiety compared with those who were married or in a relationship. This finding is consistent with Social Support Theory, which posits that marriage provides emotional stability, companionship, and financial security that can buffer against psychological distress [40, 41, 42]. In contrast, loneliness and social isolation, more common among unmarried individuals—have been strongly associated with depression, anxiety, and increased mortality risk [43].

Parenthood status was associated with lower adjusted odds of moderate and severe depression and lower odds of severe anxiety. This association may reflect the cultural significance of parenthood in many African societies, where children are often viewed as symbols of achievement, continuity, and social identity [44]. Communal child‐rearing practices common in many African contexts may also provide social support that buffers psychological distress [45]. Prior research has shown that parenthood can promote adaptive coping strategies and prioritization of life goals [9], whereas more individualized parenting models commonly observed in some high‐income settings have been associated with higher levels of parental stress and burnout [46].

Country‐specific patterns were evident. Participants from Malawi and Mozambique had higher odds of severe stress and depression, while Malawi also showed higher odds of severe anxiety, compared with Ghana. Nigeria accounted for the largest share of severe anxiety cases. These disparities are consistent with evidence linking mental health outcomes to differences in socioeconomic conditions, healthcare infrastructure, and access to mental health services across SSA [31, 32, 47, 48]. Higher mental health burdens in Malawi and Mozambique may reflect limited mental health resources, economic instability, and systemic barriers to care. In contrast, Ghana's comparatively lower burden of severe mental health outcomes may be attributable to stronger mental health governance frameworks and policy implementation [48].

Strengths and Limitations of the Study

The study has several strengths, including its collaborative, multi‐country design and inclusion of university staff and students—a population often underrepresented in mental health research in SSA. The use of a validated screening instrument and a relatively large sample size (N = 1189) enhanced the reliability of the findings and provided sufficient statistical power to examine associations across key sociodemographic groups.

However, the study also has limitations. The use of an online survey and convenience sampling may limit the generalizability of findings to all university staff and students in SSA. The reliance on self‐reported data introduces potential reporting bias, and cultural differences in perceptions of mental health may have influenced responses across countries. A small proportion of data was missing (≈2.9%), which may introduce minor bias if not completely at random [49], and the exclusion of non‐English‐speaking Francophone and Arabic‐speaking countries, along with the use of convenience and snowball sampling, introduces potential selection bias and limits regional generalizability. This may affect the representativeness and robustness of the findings [50]. In addition, despite adjustment for key confounders, residual confounding cannot be ruled out, as unmeasured or imprecisely measured variables may still influence the observed associations, a common limitation in observational studies including cross‐sectional designs [51, 52, 53, 54]. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted with caution, and causal inferences avoided. Also, parenthood in this study represents a simplified proxy and does not capture the complexity of family structures, caregiving intensity, or household dynamics. Given the cross‐sectional design, causal relationships cannot be established, and the observed associations may reflect reverse causation or unmeasured confounding. Moreover, this study used convenience and snowball sampling, which may introduce selection bias and limit representativeness. As a result, the findings may not be generalizable and should be interpreted with caution. Finally, the combined analysis of staff and students may mask important differences in mental health experiences; however, subgroup‐specific analyses were not feasible due to sample size constraints, therefore future research should include a stratified analyses by occupational role (staff vs students) to better understand heterogeneity in mental health outcomes.

Conclusion

This study highlights important associations between parenthood status and mental health among university staff and students in SSA. Parenthood was associated with lower odds of depression and anxiety, while adverse mental health outcomes were more prevalent among females, unmarried individuals, and participants from Malawi and Mozambique. These findings underscore the need for culturally and contextually tailored mental health strategies within university settings that address key sociodemographic vulnerabilities.

Relevance to Clinical Practice

The findings have important implications for mental health care delivery in SSA. Clinicians should consider family structure, marital status, and sociocultural context when assessing mental health outcomes among university populations. Younger and unmarried individuals and those without children may benefit from targeted mental health screening and support. Gender‐responsive and context‐specific interventions are particularly needed in settings with high burdens of severe mental health symptoms, such as Malawi and Mozambique. Incorporating culturally grounded, family‐centered approaches into routine mental health care may enhance service relevance and effectiveness.

Implications and Recommendations for Future Research

Future studies should employ longitudinal designs to explore causal pathways between parenthood and mental health outcomes and to assess how institutional support, number and age of children, and socioeconomic status mediate these associations. Expanding research to include Francophone and Arabic‐speaking SSA countries will be essential for developing regionally inclusive mental health policies and interventions.
