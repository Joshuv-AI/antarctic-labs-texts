The wreath matrix
Jan Petr, Pavel Turek

Abstract
Let k ≤ n k\leq n be positive integers and ℤ n \mathbb{Z}_{n} be the set of integers modulo n n . A conjecture of Baranyai from 1974 asks for a decomposition of k k -element subsets of ℤ n \mathbb{Z}_{n} into particular families of sets called “wreaths”. We approach this conjecture from a new algebraic angle by introducing the key object of this paper, the ( n , k ) (n,k) -wreath matrix M = M ⁡ ( n , k ) M=M(n,k) . As our first result, we establish that Baranyai’s conjecture is equivalent to the existence of a particular vector in the kernel of M M . We then employ results from representation theory to study M M and its spectrum in detail. In particular, we find all eigenvalues of M M and their multiplicities, and identify several families of vectors which lie in the kernel of M M .

2
The wreath matrix

Throughout the rest of the paper
n
and
k
are positive integers and
g=\gcd(n,k)
. From the definition of
(n,k)
-wreaths, we see that each wreath in
\mathcal{W}_{n,k}
contains
n/g
sets. Thus the collection of wreaths required in
Conjecture

1.2
has size
\frac{g\binom{n}{k}}{n}
.

We can rephrase
Conjecture

1.2
in terms of the largest independent set of a well-chosen graph
G_{\mathcal{W}_{n,k}}
. The vertex set of
G_{\mathcal{W}_{n,k}}
is
\mathcal{W}_{n,k}
and we put an edge between two distinct wreaths if they are not disjoint.

Then
Conjecture

1.2
is true if and only if there is an independent set of size
\frac{g\binom{n}{k}}{n}
in
G_{\mathcal{W}_{n,k}}
. Indeed, by a counting argument, a set of
\frac{g\binom{n}{k}}{n}
wreaths partitions
\mathbb{Z}_{n}^{(k)}
if and only if its wreaths are pairwise disjoint, that is they form an independent set in
G_{\mathcal{W}_{n,k}}
. Moreover, such an independent set would be the largest, again, by a counting argument.

The introduction of the wreath matrix is motivated by the Delsarte–Hoffman upper bound for the size of an independent set. The Delsarte–Hoffman bound has been widely used in extremal combinatorics, for instance, in Lovász’s proof
[
13
]
of the Erdős–Ko–Rado theorem
[
5
]
. To state it we need the following definition.

By a
pseudoadjacency matrix of a graph
G
we mean any real matrix
\widetilde{M}
indexed by
V(G)
which

•

is symmetric, and

•

satisfies
\widetilde{M}_{u,v}=0
whenever
\{u,v\}\notin E(G)
, and

•

whose row-sums are equal and positive (and therefore
f=(1,\ldots,1)
is an eigenvector).

The Delsarte–Hoffman bound (as stated, e.g., in
[
4
]
) is as follows:

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
G
be a graph on
N
vertices and let
\widetilde{M}
be a pseudoadjacency matrix of
G
. Let
\lambda_{1}
be the eigenvalue
of
\widetilde{M}
corresponding to the eigenvector
f=(1,1,\ldots,1)
, and let
\lambda_{\min}
be the minimum eigenvalue of
\widetilde{M}
. Let
S\subset V
be an independent
set of vertices of
G
. Then

|S|\leq\frac{-\lambda_{\min}}{\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{\min}}N.

Equality holds if and only if

1_{S}-\frac{|S|}{N}f

is a
\lambda_{\min}
-eigenvector of
\widetilde{M}
, where
1_{S}
denotes the indicator vector of
S
.

Analogously to the case of adjacency matrices, one gets the following:

Remark 2.2
.

If all entries of
\widetilde{M}
are non-negative, the maximum eigenvalue of
\widetilde{M}
is
\lambda_{1}
.

For our graph
G_{\mathcal{W}_{n,k}}
, we can obtain a pseudoadjacency matrix
\widetilde{M}
from the wreath matrix
M
by setting
\widetilde{M}=M-\frac{n}{g}I
. As
G_{\mathcal{W}_{n,k}}
is a transitive graph, the row-sums of
\widetilde{M}
are equal, and since it is connected and
\widetilde{M}
is non-negative, the row-sums are positive. Then it is easy to see that
\widetilde{M}
is indeed a pseudoadjacency matrix of
G_{\mathcal{W}_{n,k}}
.

Inspired by
Theorem

2.1
(and
Proposition

1.4
) we would like to understand the spectrum of
\widetilde{M}
. As
M
and
\widetilde{M}
differ only by a scalar matrix, we can study the spectrum of
M
instead. We start by computing the largest eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}
of
M
.

To achieve this, we introduce an action of the symmetric group on
\mathcal{W}_{n,k}
. Throughout the paper we write
S_{n}
for the symmetric group of permutations of
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
with its identity denoted as
\id
. It is easy to see that there is a well-defined action of
S_{n}
on
\mathcal{W}_{n,k}
given by
\tau\mathcal{F}_{\pi}=\mathcal{F}_{\tau\pi}
for any permutations
\tau
and
\pi
in
S_{n}
. The stabiliser of
\mathcal{F}_{\id}
consist of permutations which preserve the set partition
\mathbb{Z}_{n}=\cup_{i=1}^{\frac{n}{g}}P_{i}
, where
P_{i}=\{(i-1)g+1,(i-1)g+2,\dots,ig\}
, and act on the indices of
P_{i}
as elements of the dihedral group
D_{\frac{2n}{g}}
if
k\nmid n
or as elements of the symmetric group
S_{\frac{n}{k}}
if
k\mid n
. Thus, if
k\nmid n
, the stabiliser has size
\frac{2n(g!)^{n/g}}{g}
and is isomorphic to the
wreath product

S_{g}\wr D_{\frac{2n}{g}}
. If
k\mid n
, the stabiliser has size
(k!)^{n/k}(\frac{n}{k})!
and is isomorphic to
S_{k}\wr S_{\frac{n}{k}}
.
By the orbit-stabiliser theorem, the size of
\mathcal{W}_{n,k}
is
\frac{g(n-1)!}{2(g!)^{n/g}}
if
k\nmid n
or
\frac{n!}{(k!)^{n/k}(\frac{n}{k})!}
if
k\mid n
.

Lemma 2.3
.

The largest eigenvalue of
M
is
\frac{n(n-k)!k!}{2g(g!)^{n/g}}
when
k\nmid n
or
\frac{n^{2}(n-k)!}{k^{2}(k!)^{n/k-1}(\frac{n}{k})!}
when
k\mid n
.

Proof.

Let
T\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}^{(k)}
. There are
\frac{n(n-k)!k!}{g}
permutations
\pi\in S_{n}
such that
T\in\mathcal{F}_{\pi}
. Now, assume
k\nmid n
. Using the above size of the stabiliser of
\mathcal{F}_{\id}
, we conclude that there are
\frac{(n-k)!k!}{2(g!)^{n/g}}
wreaths which contain
T
. The largest eigenvalue equals the sum of entries of any row of
M
. The sum of a row labelled by
\mathcal{F}
is

\sum_{\mathcal{F}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{W}_{n,k}}|\mathcal{F}\cap\mathcal{F}^{\prime}|=\sum_{T\in\mathcal{F}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathcal{F}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{W}_{n,k}\\
T\in\mathcal{F}^{\prime}\end{subarray}}1=\sum_{T\in\mathcal{F}}\frac{(n-k)!k!}{2(g!)^{n/g}}=\frac{n}{g}\frac{(n-k)!k!}{2(g!)^{n/g}},

as required.

If
k\mid n
, there are
\frac{n(n-k)!}{k(k!)^{n/k-1}(\frac{n}{k})!}
wreaths which contain
T
. The largest eigenvalue is therefore

\sum_{\mathcal{F}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{W}_{n,k}}|\mathcal{F}\cap\mathcal{F}^{\prime}|=\sum_{T\in\mathcal{F}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathcal{F}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{W}_{n,k}\\
T\in\mathcal{F}^{\prime}\end{subarray}}1=\sum_{T\in\mathcal{F}}\frac{n(n-k)!}{k(k!)^{n/k-1}(\frac{n}{k})!}=\frac{n^{2}(n-k)!}{k^{2}(k!)^{n/k-1}(\frac{n}{k})!},

as required.
∎

For any
T\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}^{(k)}
we write
w_{T}
for the Boolean vector indexed by wreaths, which has ones at entries given by wreaths containing
T
. We also define a Boolean matrix
M_{T}=w_{T}w_{T}^{T}
. We directly see that
M_{T}
are rank one symmetric positively semi-definite matrices. Moreover,
M
equals the sum of matrices

where
T
runs over the subsets of
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
of size
k
. We immediately deduce the following.

Proposition 2.4
.

The matrix
M
is positively semi-definite.

We can conclude a bit more from the decomposition
M=\sum_{T\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}^{(k)}}M_{T}
. In particular, we see that the image of
M
is spanned by vectors
w_{T}
and therefore has dimension at most
\binom{n}{k}
. Moreover, a vector
v
lies in the kernel of

if and only if it is orthogonal to all vectors

. We end the section by proving

1.4

Action of the symmetric group

In the rest of the paper we assume

Recall that we write

By

We end the section with the first result about the eigenvalues of
M
. The key ingredient is Schur’s lemma
[
17
, Proposition 4]
, which implies that any endomorphism of

acts on each

by scalar multiplication. As in
Theorem

, the eigenvalues may not be pairwise distinct.

Proposition 3.4
.

The matrix
M
has
k+1
eigenvalues
\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3},\ldots,\lambda_{k}>\lambda_{0}=0
. The multiplicity of
\lambda_{1}
is
1
. The multiplicity of
\lambda_{l}
is
\binom{n}{l}-\binom{n}{l-1}
for
2\leq l\leq k
.

Proof.

Let
\alpha
be the linear endomorphism of
V
given by
M
. Clearly,
\alpha
commutes with the action of the symmetric group, in other words,
\alpha
is an endomorphism of the
\mathbb{C}S_{n}
-module
V
. Moreover, from the decomposition
M=\sum_{T\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}^{(k)}}M_{T}
, we know that
\alpha
has image
W=\Phi(V_{k})
and we can thus restrict
\alpha
to
W
, which yields a positive definite map. By Schur’s lemma,
\alpha
acts on each submodule
E_{l}
by scalar multiplication (where
l=0
or
2\leq l\leq k
). We know that the one-dimensional space
E_{0}
is the eigenspace of the largest eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}
. And for
2\leq l\leq k
if
\lambda_{l}
is the scalar by which
\alpha
acts on
E_{l}
, then
\lambda_{l}>0
and its multiplicity is the dimension of
E_{l}
which is
\binom{n}{l}-\binom{n}{l-1}
, as required.
∎

We now compute the eigenvalues

from

for

. As noted at the end of the previous section, the eigenspace corresponding to

is

.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
A
be a subset of
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
of size
l
such that
2\leq l\leq k
.

1.

For a set
J\subseteq A
we have

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}T\cap A=J\\
|T|=k\end{subarray}}w_{T}\equiv(-1)^{|A\setminus J|}w_{A}\mod{W_{l-1}}.

2.

For a non-negative integer
j\leq l
we have

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|T\cap A|=j\\
|T|=k\end{subarray}}w_{T}\equiv(-1)^{l-j}\binom{l}{j}w_{A}\mod{W_{l-1}}.

Proof.

Using the definition of
v_{C}\in V_{k}
for any set
C
of size at most
k
and the principle of inclusion and exclusion, we compute

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}T\cap A=J\\
|T|=k\end{subarray}}v_{T}=\sum_{J\subseteq C\subseteq A}(-1)^{|C\setminus J|}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}T\supseteq C\\
|T|=k\end{subarray}}v_{T}=\sum_{J\subseteq C\subseteq A}(-1)^{|C\setminus J|}\binom{n-|C|}{k-|C|}v_{C}.

The right hand side becomes
(-1)^{|A\setminus J|}\binom{n-l}{k-l}v_{A}
modulo
V_{l-1}
and we obtain the first part after applying
\Phi
. Summing the congruence over all sets
J\subseteq A
of a fixed size
j
yields the second part.
∎

For

and

we define the constant

as the inner product of vectors

and

whenever

and

are sets of sizes

and

, respectively, with an intersection of size

. Hence, we compute the following.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
2\leq l\leq k
. Then

\lambda_{l}=\sum_{j=0}^{l}(-1)^{l-j}\binom{l}{j}b_{l,j}.

Proof.

Recall that
M
acts by multiplication by

on

and

. For any set

of size

generates the module

, and thus

. Therefore

is the eigenvalue of

acting on

modulo

\displaystyle Mw_{A}

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{T\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}^{(k)}}M_{T}w_{A}\equiv\sum_{T\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}^{(k)}}\langle w_{T},w_{A}\rangle w_{T}\equiv\sum_{j=0}^{l}b_{l,j}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|T\cap A|=j\\

∎

We can now compute the coefficients

and eigenvalues of

.

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
2\leq l\leq k
and
0\leq j\leq l
be integers. Provided that
k
divides

, we have

\displaystyle b_{l,j}=\begin{cases}\frac{(n-2k)!}{(k!)^{n/k-2}(\frac{n}{k}-2)!}\binom{n-k-l}{k-l}&\textnormal{if }j=0,\\
0&\textnormal{if }0<j<l,\\
\frac{(n-k)!}{(k!)^{n/k-1}(\frac{n}{k}-1)!}&\textnormal{if }j=l.\\
\end{cases}

In particular, we have

\displaystyle\lambda_{l}=\frac{(n-k)!}{(k!)^{n/k-1}(\frac{n}{k}-1)!}+(-1)^{l}\frac{(n-2k)!}{(k!)^{n/k-2}(\frac{n}{k}-2)!}\binom{n-k-l}{k-l}.

Proof.

Let
A=[l]_{n}
and
T=\{l-j+1,l-j+2,\ldots,l-j+k\}
. Recall that
b_{l,j}
is the inner product of vectors
w_{A}
and
w_{T}
. Thus it counts pairs
(\mathcal{F},S)
, where
\mathcal{F}
is a wreath which contains sets
T
and
S
(of size
k
) with
A\subseteq S
.

Note that the sets of size
k
within a wreath are disjoint. Therefore, if
0<j<l
, and hence
|A\cap T|=j>0
and
|A\setminus T|=l-j>0
, there are no such pairs
(\mathcal{F},S)
. We thus have
b_{l,j}=0
whenever
0<j<l
.

Assume now
j=l
, that is,
A\subset T
. In this case, we have
S=T
, and therefore
b_{l,l}
counts the number of wreaths which contain
T
. This is computed in the proof of
Lemma

2.3
: as there are
\frac{n(n-k)!k!}{k}
permutations
\pi\in S_{n}
such that
T\in\mathcal{F}_{\pi}
and the stabiliser of
\mathcal{F}_{\id}
in
S_{n}
has size
(k!)^{n/k}(\frac{n}{k})!
, there are
\frac{(n-k)!}{(k!)^{n/k-1}(\frac{n}{k}-1)!}
wreaths which contain
T
.

Finally, assume
j=0
. Here, we have
b_{l,0}=\sum_{A\subset S}q_{S,T}
where
q_{S,T}
is the number of wreaths which contain
S
and
T
. If
S
and
T
are not disjoint, then
q_{S,T}=0
. If
S
and
T
are disjoint, as there are
\frac{n(n-k)(n-2k)!(k!)^{2}}{k^{2}}
permutations
\pi\in S_{n}
such that
S,T\in\mathcal{F}_{\pi}
, we get
q_{S,T}=\frac{(n-2k)!}{(k!)^{n/k-2}(\frac{n}{k}-2)!}
. The formula for
b_{l,0}
follows from noting that there are
\binom{n-k-l}{k-l}
ways to choose elements of
S\setminus A
from
\mathbb{Z}_{n}\setminus\left(A\cup T\right)
.

The value of
\lambda_{l}
comes from
Lemma

4.2
.
∎

Lemma 4.4

Let

\sum_{x+y=\gamma}\binom{x}{\alpha}\binom{y}{\beta}=\binom{\gamma+1}{\alpha+\beta+1}.

Both sides count the number of ways to place

\beta

\gamma+1

∎

Proposition 4.5
.

Let
2\leq l\leq k
and
0\leq j\leq l
be integers. Provided that
n
and
k
are coprime, we have

\displaystyle b_{l,j}=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2}l!k!(n-k-l)!\left(2\binom{k}{l+1}+(n-2k+1)\binom{k}{l}\right)&\textnormal{if }j=0,\\
j!(l-j)!(k-j)!(n-k-l+j)!\binom{k+1}{l+1}&\textnormal{if }0<j<l,\\
\frac{1}{2}l!(k-l)!(n-k)!\left(2\binom{k}{l+1}+\binom{k}{l}\right)&\textnormal{if }j=l.\\
\end{cases}

Proof.

Let
A=[l]_{n}
and
T=\{l-j+1,l-j+2,\ldots,l-j+k\}
. Recall that
b_{l,j}
is the inner product of vectors
w_{A}
and
w_{T}
. Thus it counts pairs
(\mathcal{F},S)
, where
\mathcal{F}
is a wreath which contains sets
T
and
S
(of size
k
) with
A\subseteq S
.

Let

be the least integer

such that
A\subseteq\{\pi(i+1),\pi(i+2),\dots,\pi(i+d)\}
for some

, where
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{F}_{\pi}
. Informally,

denotes the least number of consecutive places on

which contain

. Then for a given
\mathcal{F}
, there are

choices for
S
(provided

, otherwise there are none). We further let

be the number of wreaths

which contain
T
and have

. We now distinguish the cases

,

and
0<j<l
.

Suppose firstly that

. We claim that
q_{d,0}=\frac{1}{2}l!k!(n-k-l)!(n-k-d+1)\binom{d-2}{l-2}
for

.

To show this, we count the permutations

such that

contains
T
and has

, and then divide this number by

to account for rotations and reflections.
Such a permutation

is uniquely given by the following data: the position of the first (clockwise) element of
T
, the (clockwise) number of elements between the last element of
T
and the first element of
A
, the positions of the elements of
A
within the
d
consecutive elements, and the permutations of
T
,
A
and the remaining elements.
Vice versa, each valid choice of these parameters uniquely determines
\sigma
.

The number of choices for the position of the first element of
T
is
n
, for the number of elements between
T
and
A
it is
n-k-d+1
, for the placement of
A
within
d

\binom{d-2}{l-2}
, and the numbers of the final three permutations are

,

and

, respectively.

After putting all this together, we obtain the claimed formula for

. We compute

\displaystyle 2b_{l,0}

\displaystyle=\sum_{d=l}^{k}(k+1-d)l!k!(n-k-l)!(n-k-d+1)\binom{d-2}{l-2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{d=l}^{k}l!k!(n-k-l)!\left(2\binom{k+1-d}{2}\binom{d-2}{l-2}+(n-2k+1)\binom{k+1-d}{1}\binom{d-2}{l-2}\right)

\displaystyle=l!k!(n-k-l)!\left(2\binom{k}{l+1}+(n-2k+1)\binom{k}{l}\right),

using
Lemma

4.4
twice with
\alpha=2
and
1
,
\beta=l-2
and
\gamma=k-1
.

If

, similarly to the case

, we find that

. Thus we obtain

\displaystyle 2b_{l,l}

\displaystyle=\sum_{d=l}^{k}(k+1-d)^{2}l!(k-l)!(n-k)!\binom{d-2}{l-2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{d=l}^{k}l!(k-l)!(n-k)!\left(2\binom{k+1-d}{2}\binom{d-2}{l-2}+\binom{k+1-d}{1}\binom{d-2}{l-2}\right)

\displaystyle=l!(k-l)!(n-k)!\left(2\binom{k}{l+1}+\binom{k}{l}\right),

as desired.

Finally, for
0<j<l
, we claim that
q_{d,j}=j!(l-j)!(k-j)!(n-k-l+j)!\binom{d-1}{l-1}
.
Once again, we do so by computing the number of permutations
\sigma\in S_{n}
such that
\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}
contains
T
and has

, and divide this number by

.

This time,
\sigma
is uniquely determined by the following data: the position of the first (clockwise) element of
T
, the positions of the elements of
A
so that

and exactly
j

T
, and the permutations of

,
T\setminus A
,
A\setminus T
and the remaining elements.
Vice versa, each valid choice of these parameters uniquely determines
\sigma
.

Once more, the number of choices for the position of the first element of
T
is

. The numbers of the final four permutations are
j!
,
(k-j)!
,
(l-j)!
and
(n-k-l+j)!
, respectively.

It remains to justify that the remaining term is

. Let
D
be the set of the

consecutive elements.
From the first and the last element of

, exactly one lies in

, which gives the factor of

in

.
Without loss of generality, assume that it is the last element of

that lies in

. Then the position of the first element of

After putting all this together, we obtain the claimed formula for

. We compute

\displaystyle b_{l,j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{d=l}^{k}(k+1-d)j!(l-j)!(k-j)!(n-k-l+j)!\binom{d-1}{l-1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{d=l}^{k}j!(l-j)!(k-j)!(n-k-l+j)!\binom{k+1-d}{1}\binom{d-1}{l-1}

\displaystyle=j!(l-j)!(k-j)!(n-k-l+j)!\binom{k+1}{l+1},

which finishes the proof.
∎

Corollary 4.6

Let
2\leq l\leq k
be integers such that
k
and
n
are coprime. Then

\lambda_{l}=(-1)^{l}\frac{1}{2}l!k!(n-k-l)!(n-2k-1)\binom{k}{l}-\frac{1}{2}k!(n-k)!+l!\binom{k+1}{l+1}\sum_{j=0}^{l}(-1)^{l-j}(k-j)!(n-k-l+j)!.

Proof.

This is a combination of
Lemma

4.2
and
Proposition

4.5
after we rewrite

\displaystyle b_{l,0}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}l!k!(n-k-l)!\left(2\binom{k}{l+1}+(n-2k+1)\binom{k}{l}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}l!k!(n-k-l)!(n-2k-1)\binom{k}{l}+l!k!(n-k-l)!\binom{k+1}{l+1}

and

\displaystyle b_{l,l}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}l!(k-l)!(n-k)!\left(2\binom{k}{l+1}+\binom{k}{l}\right)

\displaystyle=l!(k-l)!(n-k)!\binom{k+1}{l+1}-\frac{1}{2}k!(n-k)!.

∎

Theorem

1.5
follows.

Proof of
Theorem

1.5
.

This is
Lemma

2.3
,
Proposition

3.4
and
Corollary

4.6
.
∎

We finish this section by describing some properties of
\lambda_{l}
when
2\leq l\leq k
for
k
and
n
coprime. First, we present explicit formulas for
l=2
and
l=3
.

Example 4.7
.

Assume that
k
and
n
are coprime. Then for
k\geq 2
we have

\lambda_{2}=\frac{1}{6}k!(n-k-2)!(n-2)\left((2k-1)n-(3k^{2}-k-1)\right)

and for
k\geq 3

\lambda_{3}=\frac{1}{4}k!(n-k-3)!(n-3)(n-2k)\left((k-1)n-(2k^{2}-2k-2)\right).

While the formula for the eigenvalues of
M
in
Corollary

4.6
does not seem to further simplify, there are a couple of interesting observations about the eigenvalues we can make.

Corollary 4.8
.

Let
2\leq l\leq k
be integers such that
k
and
n
are coprime. Then
\lambda_{l}=(n-k-l)!P_{k,l}(n)
, where
P_{k,l}
is a polynomial of degree
l
with leading term
\frac{(2k-l+1)k!}{2(l+1)}
.

Proof.

From
Proposition

4.5
, we see that
b_{l,j}=(n-k-l)!Q_{k,l,j}(n)
where
Q_{k,l,j}
is a polynomial of degree
1
if
j=0
and degree
j
otherwise. Moreover, the leading term of
b_{l,l}
is
\frac{l!(k-l)!}{2}\left(2\binom{k}{l+1}+\binom{k}{l}\right)=\frac{(2k-l+1)k!}{2(l+1)}
The result then follows from
Lemma

4.2
.
∎

We end the section by establishing some of the roots of
P_{k,l}
.

Proposition 4.9
.

Let
2\leq l\leq k
be integers such that
k
and
n
are coprime.

1.

One of the roots of
P_{k,l}
is
l
.

2.

If
l
is odd, then
2k
is a root of
P_{k,l}
.

Proof.

Using
Corollary

4.6
we compute that
P_{k,l}(l)
equals

\displaystyle P_{k,l}(l)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{l}\frac{1}{2}l!k!(l-2k-1)\binom{k}{l}-\frac{1}{2}k!(l-k)(l-1-k)\dots(1-k)

\displaystyle+l!\binom{k+1}{l+1}\sum_{j=0}^{l}(-1)^{l-j}(k-j)!(j-k)(j-1-k)\dots(1-k)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{l}\frac{1}{2}l!k!(l-2k-1)\binom{k}{l}-(-1)^{l}\frac{1}{2}k!l!\binom{k-1}{l}+(-1)^{l}l!(k-1)!\binom{k+1}{l+1}\sum_{j=0}^{l}(k-j)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{l}\frac{1}{2}(k-1)!l!\left(k(l-2k-1)\binom{k}{l}-k\binom{k-1}{l}+(2k-l)(l+1)\binom{k+1}{l+1}\right)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{l}\frac{1}{2}(k-1)!l!\binom{k}{l}\left(k(l-2k-1)-(k-l)+(2k-l)(k+1)\right)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{l}\frac{1}{2}(k-1)!l!\binom{k}{l}\left(kl-2k^{2}-k-k+l+2k^{2}-kl+2k-l\right)

\displaystyle=0,

thus (i) is proven.

Since
(n-k-l)!
is defined (and nonzero) for
n=2k
, we can compute that
\lambda_{l}
becomes
0
when we let
n=2k
in
Corollary

4.6
(for
l
odd) to establish (ii). The substitution yields

\displaystyle P_{k,l}(2k)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{l+1}\frac{1}{2}l!k!(k-l)!\binom{k}{l}-\frac{1}{2}(k!)^{2}+l!\binom{k+1}{l+1}\sum_{j=0}^{l}(-1)^{l-j}(k-j)!(k-l+j)!

\displaystyle=l!\binom{k+1}{l+1}\sum_{j=0}^{l}(-1)^{l-j}(k-j)!(k-l+j)!.

The change of variables
i=l-j
changes the final sum to

\sum_{i=0}^{l}(-1)^{i}(k-l+i)!(k-i)!=-\sum_{i=0}^{l}(-1)^{l-i}(k-i)!(k-l+i)!;

thus the sum is zero.
∎

5
Kernel of
M

Motivated by
Proposition

1.4
we would like to understand the kernel of
M
. Using
Proposition

3.4
, we know that its codimension is
\binom{n}{k}-n+1
. We can also list some elements in the kernel.

Let
a\leq n
be an integer greater or equal to
2
. We write
\tau_{i}
for transposition
(i\hskip 5.0pti+1)
and let
x_{a}=e_{\mathcal{F}_{\id}}-e_{\mathcal{F}_{\tau_{1}}}-e_{\mathcal{F}_{\tau_{a+1}}}+e_{\mathcal{F}_{\tau_{1}\tau_{a+1}}}
. For any wreath
\mathcal{F}
we further define
y_{a,\mathcal{F}}=\sum_{\sigma\in S_{a}}\sgn(\sigma)e_{\sigma\mathcal{F}}
. We can check that vectors
x_{a}
and
y_{a,\mathcal{F}}
lie in the kernel of
M
for suitable
a
.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
a\leq\frac{n}{2}
be a positive integer distinct from
1
and
k
. Then
x_{a}
lies in the kernel of
M
.

Proof.

We need to show that for any set
T\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}^{(k)}
, the vector
w_{T}
is orthogonal to
x_{a}
. Writing
H
for the subgroup of
S_{n}
generated by transpositions
\tau_{1}
and
\tau_{a+1}
, we can write
\langle w_{T},x_{a}\rangle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}h\in H\\
T\in\mathcal{F}_{h}\end{subarray}}\sgn(h)
. If
T
contains an even number of elements of
\{1,2\}
or an even number of elements of
\{a+1,a+2\}
, then the sum vanishes as the signs cancel out. Otherwise, since
a\neq k
, there is no
h\in H
such that
T\in\mathcal{F}_{h}
, and thus we also get zero.
∎

For vectors
y_{a,\mathcal{F}}
we use a stronger statement which provides a way to generate vectors in the kernel of
M
. To state it, we need some additional terminology. A
linear character
of a group
H
is a homomorphism
\rho:H\to\mathbb{C}
. We denote the restriction of
\rho
to a subgroup
K
of
H
by
\rho\big\downarrow_{K}^{H}
and the
trivial character
which sends all group elements of
H
to
1
by
\mathbf{1}_{H}
. The well-known
orthogonality of characters
implies that if
\rho
and
\eta
are distinct linear characters of
H
, then
\sum_{h\in H}\rho(h)\overline{\eta(h)}=0
, where the bar denotes the complex conjugate.

Additionally, for subset
T\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}^{(k)}
, we write
S_{n,T}
for the stabiliser of
T
in
S_{n}
(that is, the subgroup of
S_{n}
consisting of permutations
\pi
such that
\pi(T)=T
).

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
H
be a subgroup of
S_{n}
. Suppose that
\rho
is a linear character of
H
such that for any subset
T\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}^{(k)}
we have
\rho\big\downarrow_{H\cap S_{n,T}}^{H}\neq\mathbf{1}_{H\cap S_{n,T}}
. Then for any wreath
\mathcal{F}
, the vector
\sum_{h\in H}\rho(h)e_{h\mathcal{F}}
lies in the kernel of
M
.

Proof.

Let
T\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}^{(k)}
. We need to show that
w_{T}
is orthogonal to
\sum_{h\in H}\rho(h)e_{h\mathcal{F}}
. As mentioned above, the condition
\rho\big\downarrow_{H\cap S_{n,T}}^{H}\neq\mathbf{1}_{H\cap S_{n,T}}
implies
\sum_{h\in H\cap S_{n,T}}\rho(h)=0
. The inner product
\langle w_{T},\sum_{h\in H}\rho(h)e_{h\mathcal{F}}\rangle
equals

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}h\in H\\
T\in h\mathcal{F}\end{subarray}}\rho(h)=\sum_{S\in\mathcal{F}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}h\in H\\
T=hS\end{subarray}}\rho(h).

If there is no
h^{\prime}\in H
such that
T=h^{\prime}S
, then
\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}h\in H\\
T=hS\end{subarray}}\rho(h)
is zero. Otherwise, we use such
h^{\prime}
to rewrite
\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}h\in H\\
T=hS\end{subarray}}\rho(h)
as

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}h\in H\\
hT=T\end{subarray}}\rho(hh^{\prime})=\rho(h^{\prime})\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}h\in H\\
hT=T\end{subarray}}\rho(h).

As the stabiliser of
T
in
H
is
H\cap S_{n,T}
, the sum is also zero. This finished the proof.
∎

For readers with further algebraic interests, we mention a possible reformulation of
Lemma

5.2
.

Remark 5.3
.

By the Frobenius reciprocity, the condition
\rho\big\downarrow_{H\cap S_{n,T}}^{H}\neq\mathbf{1}_{H\cap S_{n,T}}
can be replaced by:

\rho
is not an irreducible constituent of the permutation character
\left(\mathbf{1}_{H\cap S_{n,T}}\right)\big\uparrow_{H\cap S_{n,T}}^{H}
.

We can now deduce that the vectors
y_{a,\mathcal{F}}
lie in the kernel of
M
quite easily.

Corollary 5.4
.

Let
a\leq n
be a positive integer greater than
2
. If
\mathcal{F}
is a wreath, then
y_{a,\mathcal{F}}
is in the kernel of
M
.

Proof.

We apply
Lemma

5.2
with
H=S_{a}
and
\rho=\sgn
. Since
a\geq 3
, any subgroup
S_{n,T}
with
T\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}^{(k)}
contains a transposition from
H
and hence
\rho\big\downarrow_{H\cap S_{n,T}}^{H}
does not coincide with the trivial character of
H\cap S_{n,T}
. The conclusion follows from
Lemma

5.2
.
∎

Concluding remarks

In 1991, Katona wrote about the wreath conjecture (“Sylvester’s conjecture” here refers to
Theorem

1.1
):
“It seems to be hard to settle this conjecture.
Sylvester’s conjecture was earlier attacked by algebraic methods and an algebraic way of thinking. Baranyai’s brilliant
idea was to use matrices and flows in networks. This conjecture, however, seems to be too algebraic. One does not
expect to solve it without algebra. (Unless it is not true.)”

These words support the potential of the wreath matrix as a novel algebraic approach to the wreath conjecture. Moreover, the authors believe that the wreath matrix itself is an interesting object worth further investigation.

One particular property that the authors are curious about is the following.

Question 5.5
.

Are the eigenvalues
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{k}
from
Theorem

1.5
all distinct?
