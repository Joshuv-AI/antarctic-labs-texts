Use of continuous temperature data from wearable devices for fever or infection management in acute hospital settings: a scoping review protocol
Authors: Yuwei Liu, Qimeng Zhao, Ka Li, Dawn Dowding
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionDespite the increasing interest in wearable devices for monitoring body temperature in acute hospital settings, their integration into routine clinical workflows remains limited. There is a growing need to map the existing evidence on the use of temperature data generated by wearable devices to enhance our understanding of how these data are monitored, presented and used in clinical practice.Methods and analysisA scoping review of relevant literature from January 2013 to February 2025 will be conducted following the Joanna Briggs Institute scoping review methodology. A search of Medline, Embase, Web of Science, CINAHL and IEEE Xplore Databases will be undertaken using the Population, Concept, Context framework to identify studies that use wearable devices to monitor patients’ body temperature in acute hospital settings to support the management of fever or infections. A pilot search has been conducted to identify key search terms and specific types of wearable devices, followed by analysis of retrieved literature. We will use a two-step screening process to identify eligible articles, starting with title/abstract screening, followed by full-text screening. A hand search of the reference lists and citation lists of eligible articles and identified reviews will be conducted for additional publications that meet the inclusion criteria. Primary studies published in English and Chinese will be included regardless of their design and type of publication. Information on the characteristics of wearable devices, clinical contexts of device usage, strategies of presenting and analysing temperature data, and their integration into clinical workflows will be extracted. In the article presenting the results of the scoping review, we will provide a descriptive synthesis of the findings, supported by visual representations, such as charts, tables and images to describe the current evidence base.Ethics and disseminationAs this is a scoping review, ethical approval is not required. The results will be presented in a peer-reviewed journal article and at healthcare or medical informatics conferences. The findings will provide insights into the interpretation of temperature data generated by wearable devices to support clinical decision-making and inform strategies to facilitate their incorporation into clinical practice.PROSPERO registration numberThis scoping review protocol is registered on Open Science Framework (https://osf.io/v6sp8).

Introduction

Infections, especially healthcare-associated infections (HCAIs), represent a significant global patient safety concern with high morbidity and mortality, affecting an estimated 5.4%–7.8% of patients hospitalised in acute care facilities across Europe1 and 12%–18% in the Eastern Mediterranean Region.2 Hospitalised patients are particularly vulnerable to infections due to factors such as weakened immune systems, invasive procedures, prolonged use of medical devices (eg, catheters and ventilators) and exposure to multidrug-resistant organisms. Fever is often one of the earliest and most noticeable symptoms of an underlying infection, making its timely recognition crucial for early diagnosis and intervention.3 Therefore, accurate and regular monitoring of body temperature is a fundamental aspect of fever detection and infection surveillance.

While many traditional temperature measurement methods are limited by their intermittent or invasive nature, the rapid advancement of wearable technologies has made non-invasive continuous temperature monitoring in clinical settings a more feasible option.4 Wearable devices are electronic sensors designed to be directly worn on the body to perceive and record various physiological parameters,5 such as smart watches, patches, rings, wristbands and other sensor-based technologies. These devices are characterised by wireless connectivity, real-time data acquisition, remote monitoring capabilities and advanced data analytics. In recent years, studies have explored the accuracy and feasibility of wearable devices for temperature monitoring across various acute hospital settings, including operating rooms, paediatrics, intensive care units (ICUs), surgical wards and oncology wards, highlighting a growing interest in the application of wearable devices in clinical practice.69

In the age of artificial intelligence, wearable devices hold significant potential to revolutionise body temperature management in acute hospital settings by enabling early detection of fever and data-driven personalised care for infections.10 The high-frequency temperature data generated by wearable devices contain rich physiological information, such as patterns, trends and circadian rhythms. When processed with advanced algorithms, this data may reveal subtle temperature fluctuations and thermal patterns that may serve as early indicators of infections, thereby facilitating timely and individualised interventions. For example, Verma et al11 developed a fever episode detection algorithm using continuous temperature monitoring with a wearable patch for patients at high risk of febrile neutropenia, identifying fever episodes an average of 4.3 hours earlier than clinical assessments. Ren et al12 reported that clustering temporal patterns in high-frequency time-series temperature data using unsupervised machine learning can distinguish fever caused by infection from non-infectious causes, achieving an area under curve (AUC) of 0.88 and potentially enabling earlier antibiotic cessation. Furthermore, the wireless and automated data transmission capabilities of wearable devices can streamline clinical workflows, reducing the time and effort required for nurses to manually measure vital signs.13

However, despite the significant potential, the clinical integration of wearable devices remains in its early stages. According to the WHO, the digital health interventions maturity life-cycle spans across the continuum from pre-prototype, prototype, pilot, demonstration, scale-up, to integrated and sustained implementation.14 The early stage from pre-prototype to pilot focuses on evaluating feasibility, usability and efficacy, which reflects the current focus of most research on the clinical application of wearable devices. Significant barriers remain before wearable devices can be further integrated into routine clinical practice, including poor data quality, data ownership and security concerns, limited data standards and interoperability with electronic health records, reimbursement policies and difficulties in the clinical interpretation of the large volumes of data generated by these devices.15 16 Due to these limitations, wearable devices may simply be used by healthcare professionals as tools to collect temperature data, but acquiring data does not mean it is being actively used. The broader potential of wearable technology remains largely underexplored. Furthermore, the high volume of data generated by these devices has introduced new challenges. The dataset can be overwhelming and lead to alert fatigue without proper filtering of artefacts in raw data and reliance on simplistic threshold-based alarms.17 18 On the other hand, the vast amount of data collected by wearable devices, if not properly gathered, stored and analysed, can become wasted medical information and resources.

To achieve deeper integration of wearable devices into clinical practice, there is a need to incorporate wearable-generated data into existing clinical workflows and facilitate the interpretation of these data to support clinical decision-making. Existing literature reviews in this field have identified the types of wearable devices available and assessed their accuracy through clinical validation studies.9 19 A more recent scoping review20 summarised the use of patient-generated health data (PGHD) from consumer-grade wearable devices by healthcare professionals outside clinical settings, leaving a gap in understanding how wearable device data is used within acute care hospitals. Building on current literature reviews, therefore, this study will aim to review existing evidence on the use of continuous temperature data from wearable devices for managing fever and infections in acute hospital settings.

Objectives

The aim of this scoping review will be to systematically explore the current applications of wearable devices for monitoring body temperature in acute hospital settings, with a focus on their role in fever and infection management. Specifically, this review will identify the available wearable devices, examine how temperature data generated by these devices is presented and used, and summarise the algorithms employed to process temperature data for diagnosing or predicting infections. Through these steps, this review seeks to provide a comprehensive understanding of the current state of integration of wearable temperature monitoring technologies into fever-related clinical practice.

To attain these objectives, the following research questions have been formulated:

What wearable devices have been used in acute hospital settings to monitor body temperature for the management of fever or infections?

Methods

Rationale for a scoping review

Given the novelty of wearable temperature monitoring technologies, we considered a scoping review appropriate to comprehensively map the extent and range of available evidence in this rapidly evolving field. Additionally, the scoping review provides flexibility to synthesise the heterogeneous information from diverse wearable devices, their varying applications and the different ways temperature data are used in acute hospital settings. Furthermore, this approach will serve as a foundation for future systematic reviews by identifying current research efforts, knowledge gaps and the feasibility of synthesising evidence on temperature thresholds or derived features for early infection detection.

Design

For the design of this protocol, we followed the best practice guidance and reporting items for the development of scoping review protocols developed by the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Scoping Review Methodology Group.21 The scoping review will be conducted in alignment with the JBI Manual for scoping reviews22 and the findings will be reported following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews guidelines.23 We have registered the review on the Open Science Framework (https://osf.io/v6sp8) to ensure transparency and reproducibility. We started the review in January 2025 and expect to complete it by February 2026.

Identify relevant studies

We adopted the PCC (Population, Concept, Context) framework to identify relevant research evidence. This framework will guide the development of our search strategy and eligible criteria by clearly defining the target population, the core concepts of interest and the contextual settings for locating evidence that aligns with the objectives of our scoping review.

Population

The population in focus is hospitalised patients. Although it is recognised that basal body temperature can vary across different age groups, this scoping review does not focus on making analytical comparisons of specific temperature values but rather on summarising how temperature data is monitored, presented and used. In addition, most existing wearable temperature monitoring devices are engineered with a universal design to accommodate a broad spectrum of users, including both adults and children. Therefore, to ensure the most comprehensive understanding of the available evidence, no limits will be placed on the age of the study population.

Concepts

Wearable devices

There are varied definitions for wearable devices. The Medical Subject Headings term for ‘wearable electronic devices’ refers to electronic implements that can be worn on the body, either as implants or accessories. Meanwhile, CINAHL Heading defines ‘wearable sensors’ as sensors worn on the body or embedded in clothing, allowing continuous or intermittent monitoring of physiological signals. Typically, a wearable device is characterised by five main features: wireless mobility, interactivity and intelligence, sustainability and durability, simple operation and miniaturisation, wearability and portability.5 In this review, we will focus on wireless wearable devices that are designed to be worn on the external body surface and are capable of continuous, non-invasive body temperature monitoring. Examples include wearable patches, smartwatches, smart rings, wristbands, chest straps and other wearable sensors or accessories, which can be either medical-grade, commercial-grade or research-grade devices.

Body temperature

Body temperature is a vital sign that reflects the balance between the body’s heat production and heat loss. Basal body temperature may vary slightly between individuals and throughout the day, typically falling within a range of 35.7°C to 37.3°C.24 Body temperature can be measured at various sites of the body, yielding significantly different values, particularly between skin temperature and core body temperature, which can differ by 2–4°C.25 In clinical practice, there is no specific site or method for measuring body temperature that is suitable for all situations.26 Instead, measurements taken from any body parts provide physiological information about patients, and each measurement method is tailored to specific clinical scenarios, aiming to balance the required level of accuracy with practical feasibility. Current wearable devices can directly detect skin temperature or estimate core temperature using embedded algorithms. Therefore, this study will not impose restrictions on the specific sites of body temperature measurement.

Fever

The National Institute for Health and Clinical Excellence defined fever as an elevation of body temperature above the normal daily variation.27 A concept often confused with fever is hyperthermia. According to the concept analysis,28 29 fever involves an abnormal increase in the body’s set-point temperature, typically caused by a pathologic process. In contrast, hyperthermia refers to an elevation in body temperature due to non-pathological factors, such as hyperthermia after exercise or induced hyperthermia therapies for cancer. Therefore, hyperthermia is not within our interest; however, considering the potential interchangeable use of the term in practice, we will include it in the search strategy but will carefully read the retrieved literature to identify studies relevant to this review. Some studies focus on evaluating increases in localised tissue temperature, such as measuring skin temperature to detect wound infections or monitoring foot temperature to identify signs of infection in diabetic patients. However, these studies do not meet our inclusion criteria, as fever is defined as a systemic, whole-body response. In addition, non-infectious diseases that gained the suffix ‘fever’ in their name are not within the scope of our interest; for example, familial Mediterranean fever or rheumatic fever.

Infections

Infections refer to the invasion and multiplication of microorganisms, such as bacteria, viruses, fungi or parasites in body tissues, which can trigger an immune response and present symptoms like fever, pain and fatigue. This scoping review primarily focuses on HCAIs, which are defined as infections acquired during or shortly after receiving healthcare, such as healthcare-associated pneumonia, surgical site infections, bloodstream infections, urinary tract infections and sepsis.1 Neutropenic fever, intrapartum fever and fever of unknown origin encountered in routine clinical practice are also considered eligible for inclusion due to their potential relevance to infections and often initiate a fever workup. Epidemic infectious diseases, such as COVID-19, dengue fever and malaria, will be included when patients use wearable devices during clinical treatment in acute hospital settings. However, monitoring the prevalence or transmission of these epidemic infectious diseases will be excluded, as this topic falls under public health rather than clinical practice.

Context

Acute hospital settings are healthcare facilities delivering short-term, hospital-based care for patients with acute illness or injury. No specific restrictions will be placed on settings such as surgical wards, medical wards, oncology wards, ICUs or emergency departments. Although wearable devices are increasingly used for continuing care after discharge, such as remotely monitoring postoperative complications or detecting fever in chemotherapy patients, monitoring typically takes place in communities, homes or outpatient clinics. However, the methods of data collection, storage and transmission in these settings differ significantly from those in acute hospital environments. Therefore, these studies will be excluded from consideration.

Literature search strategy

A search of the Medline, Embase, Web of Science, CINAHL and IEEE Xplore Databases will be undertaken to identify articles on the topic. The search process will be conducted iteratively, starting with a pilot search in each database based on predefined terms, followed by an examination of the returned literature to optimise the strategy. The predefined terms were identified via trial searches in Google Scholar to identify potentially useful phrases. Search terms will be organised by the PCC framework, combining the following topics ‘wearable*’ ‘body temperature’ ‘fever’ ‘infecti*’ ‘patient*’ and ‘acute care hospital’ with the ‘AND’ Boolean operator. Each topic contains a series of keywords and their synonyms, as well as relevant subject headings used in the databases, separated by the ‘OR’ Boolean operator. Specific brands of wearable devices with temperature monitoring functions identified from previous literature reviews4 9 will also be included in the search strategy.

The publication date will be limited from 1 January 2013, as bibliometric analysis indicates a rapid increase in research on wearable devices since then.30 31 Before 2013, studies mainly concentrated on the engineering aspects of sensor and wearable system development, while research since 2013 has focused on the application of wearable technologies in health monitoring.32 Therefore, 2013 is chosen as the starting point to highlight recent advancements and trends in the field. Furthermore, in the pilot search without restrictions on publication years, we assessed the search results and found no eligible studies published before 2013.

The searches will be verified using a predefined set of articles. The full search strategy has been reviewed by a professional medical librarian using the Peer Review of Electronic Search Strategies Checklist.33 The detailed search strategy conducted in one of the major databases (MEDLINE) is provided in table 1. After completing the electronic searches, we will conduct a supplementary manual search for additional studies. This will involve reviewing the reference lists of eligible articles and literature reviews that were identified in the search based on our criteria. We will also search for subsequent papers that have cited the eligible articles. For conference abstracts closely related to our research topic, we will perform a manual search using the title and main authors’ names to identify any full-text publications or follow-up work by the same authors. Specifically, we will search the conference abstract title in Google Scholar and at least one bibliographic database, and then use the author links to track the first or corresponding author’s publications. We will search for grey literature, including conference proceedings, preprints and dissertations, to identify additional eligible materials. The websites and databases we plan to search for grey literature will be listed in the article presenting the results of the scoping review.

Evidence screening and selection

The search results will be imported into Rayyan.ai (https://www.rayyan.ai/) for duplicate removal and online screening. Titles and abstracts will be screened to assess their eligibility based on the established inclusion criteria. Articles that meet these criteria, or those that potentially meet them but lack sufficient information (eg, unclear use of wearable devices for body temperature monitoring, uncertainty about whether the monitoring occurred in acute hospital settings), will proceed to full-text screening.

In both the title/abstract screening and full-text screening stages, the first reviewer (YL) will conduct the full literature review, while a random sample of 20% of the articles will be independently screened by the second reviewer (QZ). The reviewers’ decisions will be blinded to each other during the screening process. Both reviewers will discuss and conduct pilot screenings to ensure a consistent and accurate understanding of the inclusion and exclusion criteria. A flowchart (online supplemental figure 1) based on these criteria has been created to visualise the decision-making process for literature screening, providing structured support for determining article eligibility. Disagreements between the two reviewers will be resolved through discussion, and unresolved cases will be discussed with a third reviewer (DD) for final decision. The overall percentage of agreement and Cohen’s kappa coefficients will be reported to assess inter-rater agreement between the two reviewers.34 If a high level of agreement is not achieved (eg, lower than 90%),35 we will discuss our points of disagreement among all reviewers and further clarify the eligibility criteria. Reasons for exclusion will be systematically recorded and reported in the article presenting the results of the scoping review.

During the full-text screening, we will also try to identify if multiple articles could be derived from the same study. For conference abstracts and formally published articles, the formally published version will be retained. If multiple conference abstracts are available, the most recent version will be prioritised. All decisions regarding article selection will reach consensus among the reviewers, considering which papers best fit the inclusion criteria. All reviewers involved in literature screening have a background in nursing and previous research experience with digital health. The search and screening results will be reported using a Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses flow diagram.

Inclusion criteria

For the purpose of this scoping review, we are interested in studies using wearable devices to monitor patients’ body temperature in acute hospital settings to support the management of fever or infections, including their detection, diagnosis, treatment and prognosis in clinical practice. Primary research studies will be included regardless of their design (qualitative, quantitative or mixed methods) and type of publication, such as journal articles, research letters, dissertations, preprints and conference abstracts. Publications in English or Chinese will be included, as the research team can read and understand these languages.

Exclusion criteria

We will exclude studies that: (1) Use temperature monitoring tools which do not meet our definition of wearable devices, (2) Do not enrol hospitalised patients, (3) Monitor local tissue temperature for therapeutic purposes, (4) Focus on hypothermia or targeted temperature management, (5) Focus on the design or development of wearable devices, (6) Monitor body temperature in non-acute healthcare facilities, community or home, (7) Focus on the accuracy of wearable devices without further interpretations of temperature data, (8) Identified as non-primary research studies or retracted publications and (9) Not in English or Chinese. Literature reviews will be excluded from data extraction because typically they lack the methodological details required for our focus of interest and may lead to double counting of evidence. Instead, their reference lists will be screened to identify eligible primary studies. Table 2 presents detailed examples of the inclusion and exclusion criteria.

Data extraction

Data will be extracted from the full text and supplementary materials of each publication. An Excel data extraction sheet has been created to gather information across five modules: (1) Bibliographic and contextual attributes of the studies, (2) Characteristics of wearable devices, (3) Methods of transmitting, presenting and analysing temperature data and (4) Applications of temperature data. With reference to the WHO’s manual, ‘Monitoring and Evaluating Digital Health Interventions’,14 we will design data extraction items to assess the maturity of wearable devices in clinical applications. The temperature data analysis will be classified according to the descriptive, diagnostic, predictive and prescriptive data analytics framework.36 37 Table 3 presents the items that will be included in the data extraction.

We will employ an iterative approach for data extraction, updating the data-charting form when necessary. The data extraction form will be reviewed by another reviewer before formal data extraction. A second reviewer (QZ) will extract data from 20% of the included studies to evaluate the consistency and reliability of the data extraction process. Any discrepancies found will be discussed to reach a resolution. If the discrepancies cannot be resolved, a third reviewer (DD) will be consulted for further clarification. The completed data extraction form will be presented in the final scoping review. As suggested by the JBI methodology for scoping review,38 quality appraisal is not mandatory in a scoping review. Therefore, we will not assess the methodological quality of the papers included, as it is not the primary focus of our review.

Data analysis and presentation

In the final scoping review, we will provide a descriptive synthesis of the findings, supported by visual representations, such as charts, tables and images where appropriate. Images of the wearable devices will be included to provide a visual understanding of the technologies reviewed. A comprehensive summary of how temperature data is presented across studies will be organised using tables or charts. Temperature features and data processing algorithms will be outlined in tables for clarity and ease of comparison. Overall, the key findings from the research will be presented in a structured and visually engaging manner, to describe the current evidence base and enhance a better understanding of the data.

Stakeholder consultation

As consulting stakeholders is a recommended step in conducting a scoping review,39 we have consulted an expert from wearable device manufacturers who has extensive experience in implementing wearable devices in clinical practice during the development of this protocol. Suggestions such as technical characteristics of the wearable devices, regulatory compliance, interoperability and technological maturity assessment have been incorporated into the data extraction table template. We may further invite stakeholders to provide suggestions on the interpretation of the study results, to guide the iterative process of scoping review and help identify gaps not fully captured in the literature.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and/or the public have not been directly involved in the design of the review protocol or process.

Ethics and dissemination

Ethical approval is not required for this scoping review, as it will be based on previously published data and will not involve identifiable personal data. The findings of this review will be submitted for publication in a peer-reviewed journal and presented at healthcare or medical informatics conferences. All data extracted from included studies will be provided as supplementary materials.

Discussion

This protocol describes the methodology we will take to conduct a scoping review of published literature to evaluate the use of wearable devices in clinical temperature monitoring and their role in supporting the management of fever and infections. The conduction of this scoping review will strictly adhere to the existing guidance from JBI and reporting standards to ensure a methodologically robust, transparent and reproducible research process.

Building on existing scoping reviews that evaluate the feasibility of wearable devices in clinical applications, this scoping review will extend previous work by examining how temperature data generated by wearable devices are interpreted and used from a clinical perspective. This review will summarise the currently available wearable devices, data analytic methods and the maturity of wearable technology integration into clinical workflows. The findings are expected to inform data-driven clinical decision-making, as well as inform further research about temperature pattern mining and machine learning for PGHD.

One limitation of this study is that only publications in English and Chinese will be included, and there is no plan to search non-English language databases. This may lead to the absence of relevant studies published in other languages. Furthermore, the study will not conduct a quality appraisal of the included literature, which could affect the assessment of the reliability and validity of the findings. In addition, only 20% of the records will be screened and extracted independently by two reviewers, with the remaining 80% handled by a single reviewer. While this approach is methodologically acceptable,35 it still represents a limitation of this review, potentially increasing the risk of bias.
