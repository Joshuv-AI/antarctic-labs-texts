Responding to a warming planet: a scoping review of climate change literacy, perceived health impacts, and mental health outcomes among African students
Authors: Lidetu Demoze, Amensisa Hailu Tesfaye, Angwach Abrham Asnake, Alemayehu Kasu Gebrehana, Bizunesh Fantahun Kase, Mitkie Tigabie, Gelila Yitageasu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundClimate change represents an urgent global health and environmental challenge, with youth in Africa, who account for over 60% of the continent’s population, being disproportionately affected due to limited adaptive capacities, inadequate education, and fragile mental health systems. Climate change literacy, defined as the ability to obtain, process, and apply climate-related information, is crucial for fostering awareness, adaptive behaviours, and psychological resilience among students. Simultaneously, growing evidence points to the mental health consequences of climate change, including anxiety, distress, and emotional exhaustion. Given the growing urgency to address these converging challenges, a comprehensive understanding of the existing evidence is needed. This scoping review aims to systematically map the literature on climate change literacy, perceived health effects, and climate-related mental health outcomes among African students. It seeks to identify key themes, highlight gaps in knowledge, and inform future research, educational programming, and policy interventions aimed at building climate resilience among African youth.MethodsThis scoping review followed the PRISMA-ScR guidelines. A comprehensive literature search was conducted across seven databases: PubMed, Scopus, Embase, Web of Science, ScienceDirect, AJOL, and PsycINFO. Peer-reviewed primary studies published between January 2014 and March 2024 were included. Eligible studies involved African students at primary, secondary, or tertiary education level and reported on at least one of the following: climate change literacy, perceived health impacts of climate change, or mental health outcomes related to climate change. Two reviewers independently screened articles, extracted data, and charted key findings. The results were synthesized both numerically and thematically, with a narrative summary used to integrate and interpret the evidence.ResultsA total of 5,256 records were retrieved, and finally 23 studies included for final analysis. The 23 included studies, spanning 12 African countries and involving 20,531 participants, revealed considerable variability in students’ climate change literacy, health perceptions, and mental health responses. While university students generally exhibited moderate to high knowledge and positive attitudes, misconceptions and limited behavioural engagement persisted, particularly among primary and secondary school students. Students perceived climate change as a driver of vector-borne diseases, malnutrition, respiratory illnesses, and heat stress. Mental health impacts, including anxiety, depression, and emotional distress, were reported in several studies, yet remain underexplored. Key barriers to climate literacy and engagement included curriculum gaps, limited institutional support, and socio-cultural misconceptions, whereas facilitators included media exposure, peer dialogue, and experiential learning opportunities.ConclusionThis scoping review reveals substantial variability in climate change literacy, perceived health effects of climate change, and mental health outcomes of climate change among African students. While university students generally show higher awareness and engagement, misconceptions, weak curriculum integration, and limited behavioural action persist. Students increasingly recognize climate-related health risks such as vector- and waterborne diseases, malnutrition, and heat-related illnesses yet mental health impacts, including anxiety and emotional distress, remain underexplored. The review underscores the need for standardized, age-appropriate climate education integrated with public and mental health content. It calls for educator capacity building, experiential learning, institutional mental health support, and student empowerment through climate action platforms. A multi-sectoral approach is essential to develop informed, resilient, and engaged African youth to lead sustainable climate solutions.Trial registration A protocol for this scoping review was pre-registered with the Open Science Framework (OSF) (link: https://osf.io/mrbfn).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13690-025-01794-y.

Background

Climate change is a defining challenge of the 21 st century, posing multidimensional risks to ecosystems, economies, and human health [1]. The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) has underscored the disproportionate vulnerability of the Global Youth, particularly Africa, where adaptive capacities remain low [2]. Youth in Africa, comprising over 60% of the continent’s population, are especially at risk [3]. Their understanding, attitudes, and behavioural responses to climate change will shape the continent’s future adaptive capacity.

Climate change literacy, defined as the capacity to understand and act on climate-related information [4, 5]. Climate change literacy, encompassing knowledge, attitudes, and behaviours, plays a pivotal role in shaping individual and collective responses to environmental challenges [6]. Among students, it influences curriculum engagement, environmental activism, and risk perception [4]. At the same time, perceptions of climate-related health threats can contribute to psychological stress, especially in contexts where adaptive resources are limited [7].

Globally, there is a growing emphasis on integrating climate change education into curricula to equip students with the knowledge and skills to address environmental challenges. For instance, the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD’s) 2025 Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA) assessment focuses on students’ understanding of climate issues, aiming to measure their capacity to distinguish scientific evidence from misinformation and make informed decisions regarding environmental challenges [8].

In Africa, climate change education is less prevalent, and students often lack access to comprehensive information on environmental issues [9]. This gap in climate literacy hinders their ability to understand and respond to climate-related challenges effectively. Moreover, limited resources and infrastructural challenges further impede the integration of climate education into school curricula [9].

Climate change impacts on health include increased transmission of vector-borne diseases, malnutrition from food insecurity, and heat-related illnesses [10–13]. More recently, mental health effects, such as climate anxiety, ecological grief, and solastalgia, have emerged as pressing concerns, especially among youth [14, 15]. There is increasing recognition of the psychological impacts of climate change globally. Studies have shown associations between climate change awareness and mental health issues such as anxiety, depression, and stress. For instance, a scoping review found that higher levels of climate change perception are consistently associated with adverse mental health effects, including depression and anxiety [16]. In Africa, the mental health consequences of climate change are significant yet under-researched. A study in Kenya found that climate change experiences were significantly associated with mental health concerns and suicidality among high school students, with females reporting higher levels of worry and fear [17]. Furthermore, the psychological trauma from climate disasters, such as floods and droughts, has lasting effects on affected populations, often overlooked in relief efforts that prioritize physical health and safety [18].

This scoping review systematically maps the existing literature to examine the levels of climate change literacy, perceived health impacts, and climate-related mental health outcomes among students in Africa. The findings aim to inform curriculum development, education policy, and student support systems in African contexts in the era of climate change.

Methods

Protocol

We followed the PRISMA-ScR (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews) guidelines to ensure comprehensive and transparent reporting of the review (S1 File). All stages of the review process was thoroughly documented to support transparency, reproducibility, and quality assurance.

Eligibility criteria

The study employed a systematic approach to identify relevant literature based on predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. The criteria were designed to ensure the selection of studies that align with the research focus on African students and their climate literacy, perceived health impacts, and mental health outcomes related to climate change (Table 1).

Search strategy

A comprehensive and systematic search strategy was employed to identify relevant literature across different electronic databases: PubMed, Scopus, Embase, Science Direct, Web of Science, AJOL, PsycINFO. The search will use a combination of controlled vocabulary (e.g., MeSH terms) and free-text keywords related to the review’s core concepts: climate change, climate literacy, mental health, perceived health impacts, students, and Africa.

Keywords will include variations and combinations of the following terms:

“climate change”, “global warming”, “climate literacy”, “climate knowledge”, “climate anxiety”, “mental health”, “psychological distress”, “students”, “Africa”, and “health impacts”.

“climate change”, “global warming”, “climate literacy”, “climate knowledge”, “climate anxiety”, “mental health”, “psychological distress”, “students”, “Africa”, and “health impacts”.

Boolean operators (AND, OR) were used to structure and refine the search queries, and filters were applied to limit the results to English-language publications from 2014 to the present. Where available, truncation symbols and proximity operators were applied to enhance search sensitivity. A draft search string for PubMed was included in the appendix and was adapted for use across other databases (S2 File).

The search strategy was refined iteratively in consultation with the research team to ensure comprehensiveness and relevance. Additionally, reference lists of included studies and relevant reviews were manually screened to identify any studies that may have been missed through database searches.

Study selection

The study selection process was conducted in accordance with the PRISMA-ScR guidelines to ensure transparency and methodological rigor. Two independent reviewers (L.D and A.H.T) screened the titles and abstracts of all retrieved records to assess eligibility based on the predefined inclusion criteria. Full-text articles of potentially relevant studies were then reviewed independently by the same reviewers. Any disagreements or discrepancies during the screening process were resolved through discussion and consensus, with other three reviewers (A.A.A, A.K.G and G.Y)(Table 2).

Data charting framework

Data extraction

A structured form captured: study setting, design, sample characteristics, key concepts assessed (knowledge, attitudes, practice, health perceptions, and mental health impact), and major findings. Extracted data were analysed thematically and synthesized narratively. The data extraction tool was piloted on a sample of studies to ensure consistency and clarity before full extraction began. Extracted data were synthesized descriptively and presented in tabular and narrative formats aligned with the review objective. Three reviewers (LD, GY, and A.H.T) independently extracted the data, and any disagreements were resolved through discussion and additional review by other team members (A.A.A. B.F.K, and A.K.G).

Results

Searching and studies selection process

A total of 5,256 studies were initially identified through various electronic databases, including PubMed (n = 1930), Web of Science (n = 1547), Embase (n = 332), Scopus (n = 345), ScienceDirect (n = 757), AJOL (n = 267), and PsycINFO (n = 78). After removing 985 duplicate records, 4,271 records remained for title and abstract screening. Of these, 3,143 records were excluded. The remaining 1,128 full-text articles were retrieved and assessed for eligibility. No reports were excluded due to retrieval issues. After full-text screening, 1,105 studies were excluded for not meeting the inclusion criteria. Ultimately, 23 studies met the eligibility criteria and were included in the scoping review (Fig. 1).Fig. 1PRISMA-ScR flow diagram of study selection for this scoping review (n=23)

Study characteristics

The review included 23 studies conducted between 2014 and 2025 across 12 African countries, predominantly utilizing a cross-sectional study design. The total sample size across the 23 studies included in the scoping review is 20,531 participants. The smallest sample size among the studies was 22 participants in a qualitative case study conducted in Uganda [19], while the largest sample size was 10,084 participants from a study in Egypt [20].

Most of the studies were published recently, with a noticeable increase in publications from 2023 to 2025, indicating growing scholarly interest in this area in recent years. Ethiopia [21–23] contributed the highest number of studies with four studies, followed by Egypt [20, 24, 25], Ghana [26–28], Nigeria [29–31], and South Africa [32–34], each with three studies. Algeria [35], Cameroon [36], Eritrea [37], Kenya [17], Mozambique [38], Tanzania [39], and Uganda [19] each contributed one study(Fig. 2). The majority of the studies applied a cross-sectional design [20, 21, 23, 24, 26, 28, 29, 33, 40], while others employed descriptive correlational [25], descriptive survey [22, 30, 31], mixed-method [27, 34], qualitative case study [19], or exploratory research approaches [38].Fig. 2The number of studies from each country in the review

The sample sizes varied significantly across studies, ranging from as few as 22 participants in a qualitative case study in Uganda [19] to as many as 10,084 in a large-scale cross-sectional study conducted in Egypt [20]. In terms of educational levels, the majority of participants were university or college students, indicating a focus on tertiary education settings [19–21, 23–25, 27–29, 31, 34–37, 39, 40]. A smaller number of studies targeted secondary or high school students [17, 26, 27, 32, 33, 38], with only one study focusing on primary and middle school students [22] (Table 3).

Climate change literacy

Knowledge

The reviewed studies revealed significant variation in climate change knowledge among African students, influenced by factors such as educational level, curriculum content, teacher capacity, and access to credible information. University students generally demonstrated moderate to high levels of climate change awareness, while primary and secondary students exhibited limited understanding and persistent misconceptions. In Algeria, university students exhibited a medium level of knowledge, though misconceptions about the causes of climate change were prevalent [35]. In Cameroon, university students showed low to moderate knowledge, with some students displaying an inverse relationship between knowledge and perceived risk of climate change [36]. In Egypt, knowledge levels ranged from moderate to high among university and institute students, with one study reporting high awareness of climate-related health consequences [20, 24, 25].

In Eritrea, college students were reported to have good knowledge of climate change, despite the lack of formal climate education [37]. Ethiopian university students generally showed just above average to moderate knowledge, though performance varied, with some students performing poorly in specific climate science areas [21, 23, 40]. Primary and middle school students in Ethiopia demonstrated poor understanding of climate change impacts [22].

In Ghana, high school and university students displayed low to moderate awareness levels, with some students showing positive attitudes but gaps in formal education and misinformation being noted [26–28]. Among Mozambican high school students exhibited limited knowledge, partly due to the dominance of Western scientific narratives in education [38].

In Nigeria, university and tertiary education students were generally aware of climate change, but lacked depth in understanding its origins and effects [30, 31]. In South Africa, high school and university students displayed moderate to low knowledge, often accompanied by misconceptions, especially confusing the greenhouse effect with climate change [32–34].

In Tanzania, university students were reported to have a high level of climate change knowledge [39]. Similarly, in Uganda, university students demonstrated strong awareness of both local and global climate issues [19].

Attitudes

Based on the assessed studies, students’ attitudes toward climate change varied in strength and focus across different educational levels and regions in Africa. In Egypt, university students demonstrated a high attitude level, particularly in terms of personal responsibility for sustainable and eco-friendly actions [25]. In Ghana, university students showed moderate to high positive attitudes toward climate action, indicating growing environmental consciousness among higher education populations [28].

In South Africa, high school students exhibited moderate to low attitudes, with some confusion and lack of clarity about climate issues potentially undermining stronger engagement [32]. Another South African study among secondary school students reported moderate attitudes, with about two-thirds expressing concern and willingness to take action [33]. University students in South Africa also displayed moderate to high levels of concern, though their understanding was limited and gender disparities were noted [34]. Finally, in Uganda, university students showed high to moderate attitude levels, indicating strong awareness and engagement with climate change topics [19].

Practice and behaviours

Behavioural engagement was minimal. Tree planting, recycling, and awareness campaigns were the most cited activities. However, lack of resources, institutional support, and rural-urban disparities limited sustained engagement. University undergraduates in Egypt exhibit a moderate level of climate-change–related practices, such as energy-conservation habits and participation in sustainability activities [24].Ugandan university students report moderate-to-high engagement in eco-friendly behaviors, actively adopting actions like waste sorting, tree planting, and reduced single-use plastic [19].Conversely, South African high-school learners demonstrate low practical involvement in climate-action behaviors, with many students rarely translating their concern into day-to-day environmental practices [32].

Perceived health impacts of climate change

Students linked climate change to increased disease burden, particularly malaria, waterborne illnesses, malnutrition, and respiratory conditions. University students in Egypt reported a broad spectrum of perceived health effects due to climate change, including direct physical hazards, diarrheal and waterborne diseases, cardiovascular and respiratory problems, vector-borne diseases, and malnutrition [24]. Another Egyptian cohort recognized respiratory illnesses, waterborne diseases, and heat stress as the main health impacts associated with climate change [20].

Among Ethiopian university students, perceived health impacts included heat stroke, malaria, injuries from extreme weather, malnutrition, diarrheal and other waterborne diseases, skin infections like scabies and trachoma, skin cancer, and psychological conditions such as anxiety and depression [23].

A larger sample of Ethiopian university students identified a similarly wide array of perceived health effects: heat-related illnesses, injuries and deaths, respiratory and cardiovascular diseases, vector-borne diseases, malnutrition, waterborne diseases, and skin conditions, alongside mental health challenges such as anxiety and depression [40].

Ghanaian university students reported perceived health impacts in the form of water- and food-borne diseases linked to climate-related environmental changes [28].South African university students recognized air-quality-related illnesses, waterborne diseases, mental illness, and heat-related illnesses as key health concerns connected to climate change [34]. At the secondary school level in South Africa, students described difficulty concentrating during heat and an increase in aggressive behavior as health-related outcomes of climate stress [33].Ugandan university students experienced perceived health impacts in the form of mugginess and emotional strain as consequences of a warming climate [19].

Mental health outcomes of climate change

Climate change is increasingly recognized as not only an environmental crisis but also a significant threat to mental health. Six studies explored the mental health dimensions of climate change. Across the reviewed studies, students reported a variety of psychological responses to climate-related stressors, ranging from anxiety to depression, with variations observed by education level and geographic context.

University students in Egypt reported experiencing depression, anxiety, and stress as a result of concerns linked to climate change [25]. High school students in Kenya described experiencing extreme worry, suicidal thoughts, and anxiety in response to the perceived threats of climate change [17].

Nigerian university students disclosed a concerning cluster of mental health outcomes, such as anxiety, depression, suicidal ideation, and substance abuse [29].South African high school learners exhibited emotional responses of anxiety, fear, and hopelessness when confronted with climate change realities and media messaging [32].

South African university students reported mental illness associated with air-quality concerns and heat-related stressors [34]. In Uganda, university students described anxiety and emotional strain linked to climate change, reflecting both environmental and psychological burdens [19].

Barriers and facilitators to climate change literacy

Across various African countries, student experiences reveal a range of barriers that impede engagement and comprehension, as well as facilitators that support climate literacy and action. In Eritrea, barriers included the absence of formal climate education, policy ignorance, and common misconceptions, while facilitators included mass media exposure, personal interest, and basic education [37]. Among Ethiopian university students, barriers such as inadequate training, curriculum gaps, limited information sources, departmental disengagement, and regional disparities were highlighted. However, these were countered by facilitators like high baseline awareness, electronic media, peer and social networks, and experiential learning opportunities [23]. In another Ethiopian study, barriers included a lack of formal education in curricula, gender gaps, and institutional disparities, while facilitators were access to environmental and public health programs and reliable information sources [40].

In Ghana, key barriers were educational gaps, misinformation, and low parental education levels, while formal education and media played important roles in facilitating knowledge and shaping attitudes [28]. Among South African high school students, barriers included misconceptions from formal education, limited access to electronic media, apathy and fear driven by conflicting or alarmist narratives, and a lack of open discussions with parents and peers [32]. Facilitators, in contrast, were interactions with electronic media and peer or parental dialogue [32]. Another South African study found that barriers such as the absence of climate change content in the curriculum, complexity of climate language, socioeconomic challenges, and perceptions of climate change as a Western concern impeded understanding and engagement. Facilitators included targeted education, local engagement, and environmental modifications like planting trees and creating cooling spaces [33].

In Uganda, barriers to deeper climate understanding were skepticism about human contributions to climate change and a lack of experiential learning in the curriculum. Facilitators included the inclusion of environmental management content and the value of peer dialogue and group discussions in enhancing comprehension and engagement [19].

Discussion

This scoping review synthesised evidence from 23 studies conducted across 12 African countries to explore the current state of climate change literacy, perceived health impacts, and mental health outcomes among African students. The findings reveal a complex interplay between knowledge, attitudes, behaviours, and health-related outcomes, shaped by geographic, educational, cultural, and institutional factors. While the literature reflects increasing scholarly attention to climate change and health in educational contexts, it also underscores critical gaps and challenges that need urgent attention.

Climate change literacy: knowledge, attitudes, and behaviours

Overall, students demonstrated varying levels of knowledge about climate change, with university students generally showing higher awareness compared to primary and secondary school counterparts. In countries like Tanzania and Eritrea, students demonstrated relatively high knowledge levels, supported by media exposure and strong personal interest, even in the absence of formal curricula [37, 39]. By contrast, knowledge in Cameroon and Mozambique was low to moderate and hindered by the dominance of Western-centric science education and limited local contextualization [36, 38]. Misunderstandings such as confusing the greenhouse effect with climate change were especially prevalent in South African high school students, pointing to deficits in curriculum content and teaching methods [32]. These disparities underscore the urgent need for integrating locally relevant and scientifically accurate climate change education into school curricula across all levels. Enhancing climate literacy through context-sensitive education not only empowers students to make informed decisions but also strengthens community resilience and supports national efforts to address climate-related challenges.

Attitudes toward climate change were generally positive among university students, particularly in Egypt and Uganda, with many students acknowledging their personal responsibility in addressing environmental challenges [20, 25]. However, these positive attitudes were not uniformly observed across all settings, as high school students in South Africa showed moderate to low concern, often influenced by socioeconomic stressors and poor curriculum integration [32, 33]. This variation in attitudes highlights the critical role of both contextual socio-economic factors and educational quality in shaping young people’s engagement with climate issues. Promoting early and inclusive climate education, especially in disadvantaged settings, is essential not only to build environmental awareness but also to foster a sense of agency and long-term behavioral change necessary for effective climate action.

In terms of climate-related behaviors, the review found that practice often lagged behind both knowledge and attitude [19, 24]. Students from Uganda showed higher levels of eco-friendly engagement, including waste sorting and tree planting, driven by relevant curriculum content and peer discussions [19]. In contrast, behavioral practices were generally not assessed or found to be minimal in many other studies [24, 32].

Geographical disparities in climate change literacy observed across the reviewed studies have significant implications for climate resilience and policy implementation. Countries such as Tanzania and Eritrea, where students demonstrated relatively high literacy, may benefit from stronger media presence, community engagement, and policy attention to environmental issues. In contrast, low literacy levels in Cameroon and Mozambique may stem from limited curricular integration and contextualization of climate science.

This gap between awareness and action underscores the importance of embedding experiential learning and institutional frameworks that facilitate student involvement in environmental initiatives. Without clear opportunities and supportive structures, even well-informed and motivated students may be unable to translate their knowledge and attitudes into meaningful, sustained climate-positive behaviors.

Perceived health and mental health impacts

Students widely recognized the health impacts of climate change, especially in Egypt, Ethiopia, and South Africa, where risks like waterborne diseases, malnutrition, heat stress, respiratory issues, and vector-borne diseases were frequently mentioned [23, 24, 34]. Ethiopian university students reported a particularly comprehensive understanding of both physical and psychological health effects, suggesting greater exposure to climate-health linkages in health science programs [23, 40]. This heightened awareness among students, particularly those in health-related fields, underscores the importance of integrating climate change education into academic curricula to better prepare future professionals for emerging public health challenges associated with a changing environment.

Mental health outcomes associated with climate change emerged as a major concern in several studies, especially in Kenya, Nigeria, and South Africa [17, 29, 32]. Symptoms such as anxiety, depression, suicidal ideation, and emotional distress were commonly reported, often linked to feelings of helplessness, media exposure, and uncertainty about the future [25, 29, 32]. Despite their importance, these issues were underrepresented in the literature, signalling an urgent need for more focused research on climate-induced psychological stress [17, 19]. Addressing this gap is critical, as understanding the full scope of climate change’s impact on mental health can inform targeted interventions, policy-making, and resource allocation to support vulnerable populations coping with both environmental and psychological challenges.

The limited representation of mental health studies in the reviewed literature underscores a critical research and policy gap. Although a few studies reported climate-related anxiety, depression, and suicidal ideation among students, the evidence base remains sparse and geographically concentrated. This likely underrepresents the true burden of psychological distress associated with climate change in African youth. Future research should employ standardized tools to assess climate-related mental health outcomes and incorporate longitudinal and qualitative approaches to capture evolving emotional responses. Integrating mental health support into climate education programs is also essential to help students transform climate-related worry into agency and resilience.

Barriers and facilitators

Barriers to climate literacy and engagement included limited curriculum integration, gender disparities, departmental disengagement, and prevailing misconceptions [26, 37, 40]. Some students perceived climate change as a foreign or Western issue, further alienating them from meaningful involvement [33, 38]. Conversely, facilitators such as digital media, experiential learning, peer networks, and integration of environmental health content were instrumental in raising awareness and fostering engagement [19, 23, 32]. These findings highlight the complex interplay between structural, cultural, and educational factors that shape climate literacy and engagement. The persistence of gender disparities and misconceptions underscores the need for more inclusive and context-specific educational strategies that resonate with diverse student populations. Moreover, the perception of climate change as a distant or external issue suggests that localized framing and culturally relevant narratives could enhance relevance and personal connection. Facilitators such as digital media and peer networks demonstrate the potential of leveraging modern communication channels and social dynamics to enhance climate education. Therefore, future initiatives should prioritize interdisciplinary collaboration, curriculum reforms, and community-based approaches to overcome barriers and foster sustained climate action among youth.

Our findings underscore the urgent need for a multi-faceted response to address climate change literacy and its associated health and psychological impacts among African students. First and foremost, curriculum reform is essential there must be a move toward standardized, age-appropriate climate education that is integrated across all levels of schooling to ensure students receive consistent and relevant information [41]. Alongside this, capacity building is critical; teachers and educators need targeted training to effectively deliver climate science in ways that are engaging, accurate, and contextually appropriate [42, 43]. Furthermore, the growing evidence of climate-related emotional distress highlights the need for mental health integration within educational institutions school and university counselling services should explicitly address climate anxiety and offer support mechanisms tailored to these emerging challenges [44–46]. Lastly, youth empowerment must be prioritized by creating platforms for student-led climate initiatives, which not only foster leadership and agency but also transform climate-related fear and uncertainty into proactive and meaningful action [47–49].

Implications for policy, research, and practice

This review has several implications. First, climate change literacy must be recognised as a core component of health and environmental education. Ministries of education and health across Africa should prioritise the integration of comprehensive, context-specific climate education into school curricula. This includes age-appropriate, locally relevant content and teacher training modules.

Second, the psychosocial dimensions of climate change should be acknowledged in school health services and mental health programs. As climate anxiety rises, educational institutions should provide emotional support mechanisms and resilience-building programs tailored to youth.

Third, further researches are needed to address key knowledge gaps. Longitudinal studies assessing changes in literacy and mental health over time, and interventional studies evaluating the effectiveness of educational or community-based programs, are particularly needed. Future research should focus on the development and validation of standardized assessment tools for measuring climate literacy, attitudes, and climate related psychological outcomes among students. Such tools should capture the multidimensional nature of literacy spanning knowledge, attitudes, and behavioural competencies and be adaptable to diverse linguistic and cultural contexts across Africa. Establishing common metrics will enable cross-country comparisons, guide evidence-based curriculum reform, and support the evaluation of educational and psychosocial interventions designed to enhance youth resilience in a changing climate.

Strengths and limitations

This review is among the first to comprehensively map climate change literacy and health-related outcomes among African students. Its strengths include a broad geographic scope, inclusion of both physical and mental health dimensions, and attention to facilitators and barriers. However, limitations include the exclusion of non-English studies, potential publication bias, and the heterogeneity of study designs and measurement tools, which limited quantitative synthesis.

Conclusion

This scoping review reveals significant variability in climate change literacy, perceived health effects, and mental health outcomes among African students, with knowledge and engagement largely influenced by educational level, country context, and access to resources. While university students generally exhibit greater awareness and more proactive attitudes, widespread misconceptions, inadequate curriculum integration, and limited behavioral engagement persist across regions. The review also highlights a growing recognition among students of climate-related health impacts, especially vector-borne and waterborne diseases, malnutrition, and heat-related illnesses. However, mental health outcomes though deeply concerning remain underexplored, despite reports of climate-related anxiety, depression, and emotional distress among students in several countries.

Our findings underscore the urgent need for standardized, age-appropriate climate education across all educational levels in Africa, integrated with public and mental health dimensions. Addressing this complex challenge requires capacity building for educators, the inclusion of experiential learning opportunities, and the institutionalization of mental health support systems that respond to climate-related psychological stress. Furthermore, empowering students through platforms for climate action can help transform climate anxiety into constructive engagement and leadership.

Policy implementation should prioritize collaboration between the education, health, and environment sectors. Governments can integrate climate literacy indicators into national education frameworks and embed climate and mental health content in teacher training programs. Schools and universities should also engage community organizations and media to promote locally relevant climate communication.
