Insights into structure, toxicity and antibacterial potential of tungsten oxide–carbon nanocomposites coupled with antibiotics
Authors: Afshan Muneer, Chang Xu, Syed Qaswar Ali Shah, Amjad Islam Aqib, Muhammad Muddassir Ali, Abdul Ghaffar, Farid S. Ataya, Kun Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The Houbara bustard is a vulnerable bird species that often experiences higher morbidity and mortality due to infections caused by the multiple drug-resistant pathogens like Salmonella. Studies exploring alternative therapeutics are essential to strengthen antimicrobial stewardship. This study evaluated the antibacterial potential and toxicity of a tungsten oxide nanocomposite (WO3/C3N4, WNC) combined with antibiotics against multidrug-resistant (MDR) Salmonella enterica strains isolated from Houbara bustards. Preparations used in this study included WNC alone and in combination with ciprofloxacin, penicillin, ampicillin, and oxytetracycline. Characterization of WNC through FTIR showed absorption at 799 cm−1, indicating W–O–W stretching and confirming WO3 integration while the particle sizes, as identified through SEM, ranged from 20 to 300 nm. The XRD patterns showed fewer sharp peaks for the WNC, confirming its crystalline nature. The antibacterial potential of these products revealed lowest MICs in case of WNC + ciprofloxacin (16.74 ± 4.76 µg/mL) and WNC + oxytetracycline (30.69 ± 10.44 µg/mL), while WNC alone and with penicillin or ampicillin exceeded 100 µg/mL. The toxicity assay showed the WNC + ampicillin significantly reducing mitotic and phase indices in a dose-dependent manner (p < 0.05) at 24 and 48 h. All treatments showed significant (p ≤ 0.05) dose-dependent genotoxic effects. The study concluded that WNC-antibiotic combinations are effective against MDR Salmonella and relatively safe for host systems, supporting their potential as alternative antibacterial agents.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-23257-8.

Introduction

The Houbara bustard is a vulnerable bird species native to arid regions. Certain migratory birds including Houbara bustard have experienced increased mortality due to infections caused by the multiple drug-resistant pathogens. On the Arabian Peninsula, this species is recognised as highly endangered1 and that might owe to various health challenges. The rise in antimicrobial resistance is one the major factor among health challenges that may arise due to use of sub-therapeutic and therapeutic dosages for purposes such as growth promotion, routine disease prevention, and treatment2. These migratory birds are among the potential carrier of pathogens across international borders because they may fly great distances regardless of geographical boundaries. The presence of antibiotic-resistant genes (ARGs) and multidrug-resistant bacteria in these migratory birds seriously threaten human and animal health3.

One of the most significant bacteria causing gastroenteritis and septicemia in both humans and warm-blooded animals worldwide is Salmonella. Although Salmonella can induce a variety of illness syndromes, the most common ones are typhoid fever, bacteremia, and gastroenteritis4. The incidence of Salmonella infection varies generally among animal species5. Wild species are readily infected by Salmonella mainly though food and water. Wild bird species represent both a potential reservoir of Salmonella for zoonotic infection as well as a major source infection in livestock. It has been reported that in recent years, birds are more likely to act as a reservoir for microbial infection on farms rather than as the primary source of infection6.

In the fight against infectious diseases, antibiotics are a common and effective weapon7. Because of the extensive use of antibiotics in medical, veterinary, and agricultural operations, it is clear that antimicrobial resistance is an evolving problem that is now affecting both humans and animals8. The selection pressure posed by this overuse of the antibiotics has led to marked increase in microbial resistance to antibiotics9. Antibiotic-resistant bacteria have been reported in wild animals that live in isolated localities with little to no exposure to antimicrobial agents10. The concern about the transmission of disease and antibiotic resistance increases when animals grown in captivity are released into wild populations potentially introducing resistant strains into previously unaffected ecosystems11.

Beyond conventional antibiotics, several alternative strategies are now in practice including the use of phytochemicals, probiotics, prebiotics, and nanoparticles. Nanoparticles lead to increased production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) in biological systems and ROS can damage DNA, proteins, and cell membranes leading to oxidative stress, or cell death12. To overcome a wider range of infections, different types of metallic and non- metallic nanoparticles are being executed in a different way. Tungsten based nanoparticles attracted a lot of interest due to their antibacterial properties. Among the various nanoparticles studied, tungsten oxide (WO₃) nanoparticles are preferred due to their unique properties, including high-temperature stability and a large surface area. These characteristics have contributed to the growing interest in WO₃ nanoparticles as promising candidates for advanced antibacterial applications13. The nanoparticles offer several advantages over conventional antimicrobial techniques, including lower toxicity, higher stability and the ability to interact with various biomolecules simultaneously14. Instead of using alone when tungsten-based nanoparticles are coupled with the different antimicrobial drugs, their efficacy and targeting capacity is increased via multiple mechanisms of action15. On the other hands, it is equally important to assess toxicity adhered to these nanoproducts, and this requires efficient and environment friendly assay. Allium cepa (onion) assay, among various others, is widely used to assess cyto-genotoxicity of various chemicals and environmental contaminants16. Additionally, Allium cepa has proven to be an effective biomarker for evaluating the effects of nano and micro materials17. The comet assay is also widely used method for evaluating DNA damage and has beeneffective in assessing the genotoxicity of nanoparticles, owing to its reliability, simplicity, low cost, sensitivity and versatility18.

The current study, therefore, was designed to investigate the antibacterial potential of tungsten oxide nanocomposite coupled antibiotics against multiple drug resistant (MDR) Salmonella enterica and evaluation of cytotoxicity and genotoxicity adhered to these products.

Material and methodology

Chemicals and reagents used for the preparation of nanocomposite coupled antibiotics

Sodium tungstate dihydrate (Na2WO4. 2H2O), cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) (C19H42BrN), hydrochloric acid (HCl), D-glucose (C6H12O6), urea (CO (NH2)2), methanol (CH3OH), ethylene glycol (C2H6O2), polyvinyl propylene (PVP), ampicillin (C16H18N3NaO4S), oxytetracycline (C22H24N2O9), ciprofloxacin (C17H18FN3O3), penicillin (C16H18N2O4S) and ethanol (C2H5OH), wherein all the chemicals were purchased from the Sigma-Aldrich, USA. All the chemicals were used as received without any further purification. Distilled water was used for synthesis and washing purpose.

Synthesis of tungsten trioxide/graphitic carbon nitride (WO3/g-C3N4) nanocomposite

In order to obtain a colloidal solution, 0.15 g Graphitic Carbon Nitride (g-C3N4) was dispersed in 60 mL of ethylene glycol and stirred continuously for 40 min. Subsequently, 0.31 g of Tungsten Trioxide (WO3) was slowly added, and stirred for 1 h at room temperature. Resulted solution was autoclaved at 200 °C for 4 h. This product was washed and centrifuged while the precipitates were dried at 60 °C overnight for 12 h. After drying, the product was further calcined at 400 °C for 5 h. The resulting greenish color product was ground to a fine powder with a mortar and pestle.

Coating of drug on WO3/g–C3N4 nanocomposite

A total of 0.035 g of the drug was dissolved in 20 mL of deionized water while 1.5 g of WO3/g-C3N4 nanocomposite was separately dissolved in 20 mL of deionized water along a small amount of polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP) as a stabilizing agent. Both solutions were mixed with and stirred again for 24 h at room temperature. The product was centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 30 min until the nanocomposite was settled at the bottom of the centrifugation tube. The product was dried at 100 °C for 8 h and ground using a mortar and pestle.

Characterization

The crystalline structure of WO3/g-C3N4 nanocomposite (WNC) was investigated by using XRD Rigaku TTR (Tokyo, Japan) operating at 40 kV 150 mA in range 2θ area between 20 to 80° with intensity of (Cu Kα (λ) radiation around 0.15406 nm wavelength. Images scanning and elemental composition analysis was performed on MIRA3 TESCAN (Brno, Czech Republic). Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy analysis was performed using vibrational modes in coated nanocomposite on Perkin Elmer spectrum America (FTIR spectrometer) at room temperature in the range of 4000–500 cm−1.

The US-1100 double beam UV–Visible spectrophotometer LYNX, LM-56-1001AE, Pakistan was used to observe the absorbance of coated WO3/g-C3N4 nanocomposite. Raman spectrum collection was carried out using a Peak Seeker Pro-Agiltron Raman spectroscopy (USA) using a source 50 mW of laser light at 785 nm. For each measurement, 50 mg of the sample was placed on aluminum slides and analyzed at room temperature.

Cytotoxicity and genotoxicity of nanocomposite

Allium cepa anaphase telophase test

Small onions of 3–5 cm in size were immersed in varying concentrations of the test solution for 48 h. The onions were divided into three groups: (1) negative control onions, which were kept in distilled water; (2) positive control onions, which were exposed to 10 mg/mL of methyl methanesulfonate (MMS); and (3) the treated group onions which were exposed to solutions containing nanocomposite-coated antibiotics. The composites were further divided into WNC + Ampicillin, WNC + Oxytetracycline, WNC + Ciprofloxacin, WNC + Penicillin and alone WNC to find their comparative cytotoxicity and genotoxicity. Onion roots were treated with these composites at different concentrations (0.25 mg/mL, 0.5 mg/mL, 1 mg/mL, and 10 mg/mL) for 24 to 48 h at room temperature in the dark. After 24 h of exposure, roots were cut and fixed in a 3:1 ethanol:acetic acid (v/v) solution in an Eppendorf tube. The same procedure was repeated after 48 h of exposure19.

The fixed roots were rinsed with distilled water on the day of the experiment. Eight root tips were hydrolyzed by heating them in 1N HCl for 8–10 min. For each concentration, three slides were prepared. To prepare the root smear, the Schiff reagent was used for staining, applied for 25–30 min at room temperature in the dark (Cildir & Liman, 2020). Using a new razor blade, darkly pigmented root tips measuring 1–2 mm in length were squashed and put on glass slides. The roots on the slides were treated with one or two drops of 45% acetic acid. The slides were covered with coverslips and subsequently sealed with nail paint. Afterwards, the slides were examined at 40X using a light microscope. A total of 500 cells were counted in order to examine mitotic activity under the influence of various treatments19.

Comet assay

The comet assay was performed to assess DNA damage induced by antibiotic-coated nanoparticles in onion root cells according to19 with slight modifications. Antibiotics coated with nanocomposites at test dosages of 10, 1, 0.5, and 0.25 mg/mL were given to onions. For comparison, two controls were used, the MMS (10 mg/mL) served as a positive control and distilled water was used as a negative control. On the day of experiment, the cut root tips were crushed in a petri dish placed on an ice box to extract DNA using nucleus isolation buffer. The suspension was passed through a nylon filter. The extracted DNA was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 7 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was discarded. Phosphate buffer saline (PBS) was used to prepare low melting point agarose (LMPA) and 1% agarose with a normal melting point was used to pre-coat the slides. Following centrifugation, the resulting DNA pellet was mixed with 1.5% LMPA and pipetted onto the pre-coated slides.. After placing the cover slips, the sample slides were immersed in a electrophoresis buffer containing 300 mM NaOH and 1 mM EDTA for 20 min prior to electrophoresis The electrophoresis was run for 20 min at 25 V and 300 mA. Then, the slides were neutralized by using the Tris buffer (pH 7.5). The slides were stained with ethidium bromide and left in the dark for five minutes to visualize the comet tail using a fluorescence microscope. Cells were assigned a score based on the degree of DNA damage, ranging from 0 (no damage) to 4 (complete damage). To prevent bias, three slides were made for each concentration, and 50 cells were counted from each slide. The results were expressed as arbitrary units calculated according to a formula described by Jamil et al.20.

Antibacterial potential of nanocomposite

Isolation of Salmonella enterica

Cloacal samples were processed for the isolation of Salmonella enterica and a series of biochemical tests were performed for the identification. Further, the molecular analysis was applied to confirm the Salmonella enterica. Isolates selected for the antibacterial susceptibility assay of the nanocomposites were chosen based on their multidrug resistance profiles, particularly resistance to ampicillin, penicillin, oxytetracycline, and ciprofloxacin.

Minimum inhibitory concentration

The minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of the nanocomposites was assessed through broth microdilution assay using 90-well microtitration plate. Two-fold serial dilutions of the nanocomposites were prepared, starting from a concentration of 10 mg/mL, and added to each well, excluding the positive control. All wells were inoculated with the bacterial concentration (1–1.5 × 105 CFU/mL), except for the negative control. The negative control contained only nutrient broth, while the positive control contained bacteria and nutrient broth. The microtitration plate was incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. After incubation, the optical density (OD) was measured at a wavelength of 690 nm to determine the MIC21. The OD values were taken at 4, 8, 12, 16, 20, and 24 h to evaluate the antibacterial activity in time dependent manner.

Statistical analysis

The collected data were analyzed using both parametric and non-parametric tests. t-test was used for comparisons between two groups, while ANOVA was applied for comparisons among more than two groups in case means comparison was intended. Minitab version 17 and SPSS version 22 was applied on data at 5% probability, as and when needed.

Results

X-ray diffraction pattern

The X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of WO₃ and WO₃/g-C₃N₄ composites are presented in (Fig. 1). The diffraction peaks correspond to a mixture of WO₂ and WO₃ phases, indicating the presence of tungsten oxide nanoparticles with significant oxygen vacancies within the lattice. Several sharp peaks were observed, suggesting a high degree of crystallinity. The intensity of these peaks increased proportionally with the mass fraction of tungsten oxide in the samples. In contrast, the presence of smaller and broader peaks may be attributed to the amorphous regions within the WO₃ nanoparticles, which contribute to a reduction in peak sharpness. Importantly, no additional peaks were detected in the XRD pattern of the WO₃ nanoparticles, confirming the high purity of the synthesized product.Fig. 1XRD pattern: (a) WO3 nanoparticles, (b) WO3/g-C3N4 nanocomposite.

XRD pattern: (a) WO3 nanoparticles, (b) WO3/g-C3N4 nanocomposite.

The Diffraction peaks observed for WO3/g-C3N4 nanocomposite at 2-theta (θ) were 23.82, 26.12, 28.80, 30.77, 33.41, 35.40, 38.55, 42.35, 44.75, 49.99, 53.22, 55.19, 61.19, 74.40, 76.33, 78.34° with miller indices (002), (112), (113), (022), (331), (010), (232), (341), (432), (433), (414), (733), (724), (312), (330) and (412) respectively in XRD pattern. The peaks of WO3/g-C3N4 nanocomposite were not sharper as compared to the WO3 nanoparticles, indicating the crystalline nature of both products was different due to stacking of WO3 in the center of polymeric structure of g-C3N4. Peaks of WO3 were clearly observed in the diffraction pattern of nanocomposite because the small amount of WO3 is well-dispersed in the nanocomposite resulting in no observable change in the intensive peaks of crystalline WO3. Peaks of WO₃ were clearly observed in the diffraction pattern of the nanocomposite, with no significant shift or broadening in peak position or intensity, indicating that the crystalline structure of WO₃ remained unchanged after composite formation. This confirms that the WO₃ phase retained its crystalline structure without distortion or phase transformation upon integration into the composite matrix.

Scanning electron microscopy

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images of the WO₃/g-C₃N₄ nanocomposite are shown in (Fig. 2). The incorporation of WO₃ nanoparticles into the g-C₃N₄ matrix did not significantly alter the overall morphology of the nanocomposite. As seen in Fig. 2a, the material consists of numerous randomly arranged particles, indicating a heterogeneous structure. The aggregation of g-C₃N₄ sheets with WO₃ nanoparticles of varying crystal sizes results in a porous structure, which is favorable for substrate applications due to increased surface area. Figure 2b highlights an area (marked with a red circle) where WO₃ nanoparticles are visibly embedded within the g-C₃N₄ matrix. The surrounding region is densely populated with suspended nanoparticles, forming an oval or spheroid-like morphology. This structure may offer a high surface-to-volume ratio, providing abundant reactive sites. Figure 2c, captured at 100,000 × magnification, reveals well-defined particle boundaries. Larger particles are clearly distinguished from smaller nanoparticles, emphasizing the polydispersity of the sample. The average particle size of the nanocomposite was found to range between 20 and 300 nm, with an average diameter of approximately 120 nm. Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy

In Fig. 5a, many FTIR absorption bands are observed for pure WO3 nanoparticles at 3481, 3080, 1649, 1435, 1041 and 799 cm-1 wavenumbers. Band appeared at 1649 cm-1 is due to W-OH bending vibration mode. While band at 1435 cm-1 is assigned to the –OH stretching mode of water molecules. WO3 is the three-dimensional orientation of octahedral WO6, in which atoms of tungsten metal are situated at the center of the octahedron, and oxygen atoms are located as its apexes, leading to crystalline nature of particles. FTIR reveals various vibration modes due to the fundamental vibration of W–O, W = O and W-OH chromophores. In the FTIR spectrum of the WO₃/g-C₃N₄ nanocomposite (Fig. 5b), characteristic bands of both g-C₃N₄ and WO₃ are clearly observed at 3270, 2171, 1755, 1645, 1323, 1034 and 799 cm-1 wavenumbers. Bands at 3270, 2171, 1755, 1645, 1323, and 1034 cm⁻1 correspond to g-C₃N₄ structural features, while the band at 799 cm⁻1 is specific to WO₃. The 3270 cm⁻1 band arises from N–H stretching vibrations of terminal –NH₂ or = NH groups and O–H stretching from water molecules. The 2171 cm⁻1 band indicates C≡N stretching, related to cyanate formation during g-C₃N₄ synthesis. The band at 1755 cm⁻1 is due to C–N heterocyclic vibrations and possibly C = O stretching from glucose derivatives used in the process. The 1645 cm⁻1 band reflects C = N stretching of g-C₃N₄’s conjugated system, while 1323 cm⁻1 is attributed to C–N stretching in secondary amines. The band at 1034 cm⁻1 confirms the presence of s-triazine units, a key feature of g-C₃N₄. In contrast, the 799 cm⁻1 band corresponds to W–O–W stretching in WO₃, confirming its successful integration. The presence of both sets of bands without significant shifts indicates that WO₃ interacts with g-C₃N₄ without altering its core structure. This confirms the stability of the nanocomposite’s chemical framework.Fig. 5FTIR spectrum: (a) WO3 nanoparticles. (b) WO3/g-C3N4 nanocomposite.

FTIR spectrum: (a) WO3 nanoparticles. (b) WO3/g-C3N4 nanocomposite.

Discussion

The current study shows that in the XRD pattern of the WO3/g-C3N4 nanocomposite, diffraction peaks were observed at 2-theta (θ) values of 23.82, 26.12, 28.80, 30.77, 33.41, 35.40, 38.55, 42.35, 44.75, 49.99, 53.22, 55.19, 61.19, 74.40, 76.33, 78.34° corresponding to the miller indices (002), (112), (113), (022), (331), (010), (232), (341), (432), (433), (414), (733), (724), (312), (330) and (412) respectively. These peaks were similar to Meng et al.22 in which 2-theta (θ) values assigned to miller indices confirmed the synthesis of C14N6O5W. This study was also consistent with the findings of Rajkumar et al 23, who confirmed the presence of chitosan/polyvinyl alcohol-copper oxide nanocomposites (CS/PVA/CuONCs) in crystalline form by revealing diffraction peaks and their corresponding relative planes at strong peak positions 2θ for 18.65° (020), 26.11° (021), 28.56° (110), 34.81° (002), 36.17° (111), 39.83° (130), 44.85° (131), 47.29° (202), 50.42° (151), 55.03° (020), 57.75° (202), 64.13° (113), and 75.27° (222).

In the SEM image, g-C3N4 appears aggregated with various crystal size WO3 nanoparticles and overlapping with each other making a porous structure that is suitable as a substrate. It has been reported that g-C3N4 is a layer-like porous material containing a large number of holes, typically of large surface, exhibiting a lamellar structure24. The nanocomposites’ FTIR spectra showed distinctive bands. The intensity of certain bands increased when the cross-linking agent was present. The bands found in current study were in consistent with the findings of Rajkumar et al.23, who reported broad absorption bands in the range of 3376–3338 cm⁻1, corresponding to the stretching vibrations of hydroxyl groups on the surface of CuO nanoparticles. The band observed at 1649 cm⁻1 in this study is attributed to the bending vibration mode of W–OH groups, while the band at 1435 cm⁻1 corresponds to the stretching mode of water molecules associated with –OH groups. Absorption in the fingerprint region i.e., below 1000 cm-1 is due to interatomic vibration of metal oxide25. A weaker band observed at 3481 cm-1 represents the presence of symmetric stretching vibrations and intercalated water molecules W-OH…H2O26. Band at 3080 cm-1 is might be due to intermolecular hydrogen bonding O┈H existing among absorbed water molecules27.

Similar to the present investigation, Kumari et al.28 investigated the cytotoxic effects of benzyl butyl phthalate in combination with dibutyl-n-phthalate (DBP) and found that exposure resulted in an almost dose-dependent reduction of root growth. Consistent with findings of Jadhav et al.29, it was found that bisphenol A, an analogue of bisphenol S, caused a dose-dependent decrease in mitotic index in onion root tips (Fig. 7). When bisphenol A interacts with proteins involved in cell division, the mitotic index is inhibited. Similar to the current work, Liman et al.19 found that the mitotic index (MI) decreased in concentration-dependent ways (r = − 0.99). In line with the current work, Adesuyi et al.30 evaluated the endocrine disruptor amritaz, a pesticide, and found significant dose-dependent inhibition of onion root. The first plant structure to be impacted by harmful substances or chemicals is the root, and most susceptible structure to chemical contamination are the meristematic cells found on the terminals of the roots31,32. The gradual increase in chromosomal aberrations (CAs) highlights the genotoxic effects of clopyralid. Similarly, Liman et al.19 observed that the mitotic index (MI) showed a concentration-dependent decrease (r = − 0.99) at all WO3NP concentrations compared to the control group (negative). The control group exhibited higher mitotic index values, while the maximum WO3NP concentration resulted in the minimum mitotic index value (24.64 ± 0.72). Their study also reported that MI reductions became evident earlier at 12.5 mg/L compared to the positive control. Upon WO3NP exposure, an increase in the mitotic phases in a dose dependent manner was observed at all concentrations relative to the negative control, except in prophase. Consistent with our findings, Bucchianico et al.33 also reported no significant signs of cytotoxicity after 48 h of exposure. Conclusion

The study concluded tungsten oxide carbon nanocomposites coupled with antibiotics as well-formed products available as potential antibacterial candidates against pathogens. These products significantly modulated resistance in Salmonella enterica when coupled with penicillin, ampicillin, ciprofloxacin, and oxytetracycline. Nanocomposite coupled with ciprofloxacin was found highest effective in that it showed lowest minimum inhibitory concentration against MDR Salmonella enterica. These preparations, as overall, proved that the coupled products can be potential candidate to play role in antimicrobial stewardship. The toxicity assays revealed safer use of these preparations when tested on onion root cells. The study however requires to proceed further with in vivo trials and field trials to develop a well suited nanocomposite-antibiotics blend that can be a promising alternative to antimicrobials against multiple drug resistant pathogens.
