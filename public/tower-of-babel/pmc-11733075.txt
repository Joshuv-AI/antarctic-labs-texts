Climate change and human health in Alpine environments: an interdisciplinary impact chain approach understanding today's risks to address tomorrow's challenges
Authors: Giulia Roveri, Alice Crespi, Frederik Eisendle, Simon Rauch, Philipp Corradini, Stefan Steger, Marc Zebisch, Giacomo Strapazzon
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe European Alps, home to a blend of permanent residents and millions of annual tourists, are found to be particularly sensitive to climate change. This article employs the impact chain concept to explore the interplay between climate change and health in Alpine areas, offering an interdisciplinary assessment of current and future health consequences and potential adaptation strategies.Rising temperatures, shifting precipitation patterns and increasing extreme weather events have profound implications for the Alpine regions. Temperatures have risen significantly over the past century, with projections indicating further increases and more frequent heatwaves. These trends increase the risk of heat-related health issues especially for vulnerable groups, including the elderly, frail individuals, children and recreationists. Furthermore, changing precipitation patterns, glacier retreat and permafrost melting adversely impact slope stability increasing the risk of gravity-driven natural hazards like landslides, avalanches and rockfalls. This poses direct threats, elevates the risk of multi-casualty incidents and strains search and rescue teams.The environmental changes also impact Alpine flora and fauna, altering the distribution and transmission of vector-borne diseases. Such events directly impact healthcare administration and management programmes, which are already challenged by surges in tourism and ensuring access to care.In conclusion, Alpine regions must proactively address these climate change-related health risks through an interdisciplinary approach, considering both preventive and responsive adaptation strategies, which we describe in this article.Keywords: Environmental health, Epidemiology, Health systems, Public health, Prevention strategies

Introduction

The European Alps are found to be particularly sensitive to climate change with temperatures rising about twice as fast as the global average.1 Alpine regions face elevated climate change-related impacts due to their intricate orographical and ecological characteristics, high population density and high touristic load and associated economic activities.2 Accelerated warming induces shifts in species distributions, ecosystem forms, as well as modification of hydrological regimes and geomorphic processes.3 The interplay of these changes, in conjunction with shifts in precipitation patterns and an increase in extreme weather events could significantly influence the incidence of serious health events. Heat-related diseases,4 vector-borne diseases,5 as well as gravity-driven natural hazards6 can have a direct impact on the well-being of Alpine communities and tourists visiting mountain areas.

Impact chains are well-known conceptual models supporting climate risk assessment. They comprehensively link all components that contribute to specific risks within a particular area and/or sector, from a cause-effect standpoint. Effective strategies for risk reduction can be developed by integrating inputs through an interdisciplinary approach, leveraging insights from local experts and knowledge of natural and climate systems.7 8 The resultant impact chain serves as a valuable tool for outlining the most critical factors to address when devising adaptation strategies.9

By following the impact chain concept, this article explores the intricate interplay between climate change and human health in the Alpine regions with the aim of providing an interdisciplinary assessment of current and future risks for human health and health systems as well as delineating potential adaptation options. The work is based on the knowledge and analysis of three interconnected key components—the environmental, the socio-ecological and the economic systems (figure 1).

Alpine climate change trends

Rising temperatures and changes in precipitation patterns: ecological transformations, changes in snow cover, glacier retreat and permafrost melting

Mean annual temperatures in the Alps have risen by about +1.8°C over the past 150 years, with accelerated warming since the 1980s.1 To project future climate scenarios, different greenhouse gas concentrations pathways have been integrated into climate model simulations.10 11 According to the most recent and comprehensive climate assessment for the Alps, based on the EURO-CORDEX simulations, temperatures are expected to continue increasing over the next decades. By the end of the century, the average rise is projected to be between +2°C and +4°C compared with 1981–2010 means, depending on the scenario.1 12 In mountainous regions above 1000 metres above sea level (m a.s.l.) this increase may be more pronounced (by of up to 1°C) compared with lower elevations across all seasons.1

Rising temperatures and changes in precipitation patterns are causing significant shifts in the distribution, phenology and interactions of Alpine flora and fauna, which are further exacerbated by anthropogenic pressures. These ecological transformations and altered climate conditions also affect the life cycle and reproduction of disease vectors such as ticks (figure 1).13

The rise in temperatures has already led to rapid glacier retreat and permafrost melting in recent decades (figure 1), and these trends are also projected to continue in the face of future warming.14 Alpine glaciers are expected to lose about 45% of their area by 2050 and, under the high-emission scenario, almost disappear by 2100.15

Concerning precipitation, there has been a notable overall rise in total winter precipitation over the last century, particularly in the Northwestern Alps and high-elevation regions. This trend is accompanied by an escalation in daily precipitation intensity at both annual and seasonal scales.16 Changing precipitation patterns, especially when combined with rising temperatures, are expected to impact the occurrence of gravity-driven natural hazards (figure 1), as rainfall is a common preparatory and triggering factor for landslides and rockfalls.

Changes in snow cover distribution and characteristics, driven by higher temperatures and shifting precipitation patterns, affect snow avalanche phenomena (figure 1).17

Extreme weather events

High-temperature extremes have intensified and episodes of persistently hot conditions, hereafter ‘heatwaves’, have become more frequent over the past decades (figure 1).18 Climate projections for Europe indicate that many Alpine areas are expected to experience more than 50 additional heatwave days per year from 2071 to 2100 compared with 1981–2010. Figure 2 shows heatwave days calculated using the EURO heat project definition, which considers the maximum apparent temperature (Tapp). Tapp is a discomfort index derived from air and dew point temperatures.19 Concurrently, cold spells are expected to become less frequent and severe. Similarly, climate change is expected to influence the frequency, scale and geographical reach of other mountain hazards, including floods, flash floods and debris flows (figure 1).

Exposure and vulnerabilities

Human communities in Alpine environments and tourism influence

The Alpine region is currently home to approximately 14 million inhabitants, distributed across 200 000 km2. However, the population density in this area varies significantly. Due to the unique geomorphology of the Alps, they primarily retain a rural character, with only about 900 000 people (approximately 6.5% of the total Alpine population) living in cities of more than 100 000 inhabitants.20 This results in specific climate change-related risks affecting different population groups to varying degrees, depending on their specific vulnerabilities and living environments.

Similar to other European regions, Alpine communities are dealing with the challenge of ageing populations. As the ongoing demographic shift towards an ageing population continues, the prevalence of chronic diseases within this vulnerable demographic group is anticipated to rise concurrently (figure 1).

Next to the inhabitants of these regions, there is also a substantial and continuously growing number of exposed visitors. The Alps, a popular destination for winter and summer sports enthusiasts, attracts over 100 million tourists annually.21 The increasing influx of tourists strains the local healthcare system, especially as popular recreational activities like hiking, climbing and skiing carry significant risks of traumatic injuries (figure 1).22 23 Furthermore, sudden cardiac death (SCD) is the leading cause of non-traumatic deaths during downhill skiing and mountain hiking.24 It is estimated that among the annual population of 4–5 million hikers and skiers in the Alps, many have known cardiovascular diseases and are at increased risk for SCD.25

The increase in tourist numbers correspondingly has raised the frequency of mountain rescue missions, intensifying the exposure of search and rescue (SAR) personnel to mountain-related hazards and resulting in a surge in occupational accidents.26 Concurrently, in-hospital care also grapples with a surge in patients during peak tourist seasons and public holidays. Many visitors seek medical attention for non-emergency issues or minor ailments, straining the healthcare system, diverting resources from genuine emergencies and causing longer wait times for critical patients.27 Additionally, a pre-existing shortage of healthcare specialists, particularly in rural areas, compounds the pressure on the system.

Health-related risks in Alpine regions

Alpine regions present specific health risks. These risks are also related to their remote and/or difficult to reach locations, affecting both the access to healthcare services as well as emergency response times.22

Heat-related health consequences

Heat-related diseases, like heatstroke, heat exhaustion and dehydration have surged globally, with 37% of heat-related fatalities attributed to climate change.28 Between 2000 and 2020, global warming has been associated with an estimated temperature-related mortality increase in Europe.29 The anticipated increase in frequency and intensity of heatwaves, combined with demanding recreational activities in the mountains and a growing elderly and vulnerable population, may lead to higher heat-related morbidity and mortality rates in Alpine areas (figure 1). Urbanisation and limited green spaces in small Alpine cities can contribute to the formation of urban heat islands, increasing the vulnerability of sensitive populations like the elderly and children (figure 1). In Alpine countries like Austria and Switzerland, between 2071 and 2099, the anticipated proportions of deaths linked to the mean apparent temperature exceeding the comfort threshold are projected to be 1.74% and 1.96% respectively, under the high-emission scenario considered.29

The assessment of the impact of heat on the human body commonly uses the concepts of compensated and uncompensated heat stress. Compensated heat stress occurs when the body’s cooling mechanisms effectively counteract elevated heat, while uncompensated heat stress involves an inability to adequately dissipate heat, leading to potential health risks.30 Besides apparent temperature, there are several measures and indices to assess thermal comfort and the physiological response of the human body to environmental conditions.

The wet-bulb temperature (Twb), for instance, is a measure of air temperature that takes into account the cooling effect of evaporation. A Twb of 35°C has been theorised as the threshold above which heat stress becomes uncompensable.31 However, recent experimental studies have demonstrated that decompensated heat stress occurs in healthy adults at Twb significantly lower than 35°C, ranging from 25°C to 28°C in hot-dry environments to 30°C to 31°C in warm-humid environments.32 30 This holds true for healthy young adults and maybe even lower when considering more vulnerable populations, such as those expected to increasingly inhabit the Alps.

Although these thresholds are not yet widespread in the Alpine areas, their likelihood is expected to increase with rising temperatures. For instance, Casanueva et al33 project that heat stress, as indicated by Twb, could trigger more frequent heat warnings in Switzerland, rising from 1 to 4–6 heat spell events per year by the end of the century. Consequently, other Alpine countries may also experience a greater incidence of heat-related illnesses as climate change progresses. The Universal Thermal Climate Index (UTCI) is another state-of-the-art indicator that estimates the thermal stress the human body undergoes when exposed to outdoor conditions. The UTCI takes into account factors such as air temperature, humidity, wind speed and radiation.34 Death counts increase in conditions of moderate and strong stress, that is, when UTCI is above 26°C and 32°C.34 Based on the ERA5-HEAT data set,35 the UTCI in Bolzano, a city in the south-eastern Alps at 262 m a.s.l., has increased over the past decades. Average UTCI values in July (from 12:00 to 15:00 Coordinated Universal Time (UTC)) rose from 25.0°C between 1961 and 1990 to 26.2°C between 1991 and 2020 (figure 3). It is important to note that these values are derived from a 31 km grid and represent a spatial average over a broad area that includes the target location. Since Bolzano is located in a valley bottom near mountains, actual local UTCI values in July are likely higher. Similar to Twb, elevated UTCI values are more common in low-lying Alpine cities. Ongoing warming could further increase the likelihood of discomfort and expand the affected area. For scenarios involving significant physical activity, the Potential Equivalent Temperature (PET) may serve as a viable alternative to the UTCI.36 The PET considers varying activity levels and clothing insulation, offering a more precise representation of thermal stress for active individuals.37 However, the UTCI is more sensitive to humidity and wind speed compared with the PET, making it better suited for identifying potentially hazardous weather, especially in winter conditions. Additionally, while the PET is more responsive to mean radiant temperature in hot weather, the UTCI provides a more balanced assessment across different environments and seasons.38

However, while an overall increase of potential heat-stress conditions is expected in the Alpine countries in future decades, it is crucial to highlight that future projections concerning heat-related health often oversimplify and overlook the intricate complexities of the real scenario.39 Inter-individual factors of human thermoregulation, population diversity, including age, health and socioeconomic status are relevant factors to be considered for this scenarios.39 Quantifying the likelihood and severity of the identified risks should therefore be considered with caution to prevent unreliable and unrealistic estimates.

Gravity-driven hazards

The Alps can be subjected to gravity-driven hazards (eg, avalanches, landslides and rockfalls) due to changes in snow cover, glacier retreat and permafrost melting (figure 1). Avalanches are major natural events in snow-covered mountains, posing substantial threats to both people and infrastructure. The persistent influence of climate change intensifies concerns regarding the heightened frequency and unpredictability of avalanches, posing new risks for catastrophic events despite the existing preventive measure. Furthermore, since the occurrence of human-triggered avalanches is significantly influenced by the number and behaviour of winter recreationists, the continuous surge in tourist numbers serves as a significant external factor that could amplify avalanche incidents (figure 1). Changing temperature and precipitation patterns alter snowpack stability, augmenting the risk of avalanches and potentially increasing the frequency and severity of avalanche-related traumatic injuries (eg, due to a diminished snow cover and an increase in terrain roughness)17 8 as well as potentially increasing the burden of asphyxia-related death (eg, due to changes in snow characteristics) (figure 1).

Global warming has affected high Alpine trails and routes, making many of them highly dangerous. Nowadays, there is a scarcity of data in the literature that comprehensively addresses the prevalence of traumatic events and fatalities resulting from the impact of climate change. However, gravity-driven hazards, such as landslides, rockfalls6 and icefall have increasingly been reported in the Alpine regions. Classical ice climbs in the Alps face significant threats from rockfall and falling stones caused by the melting of rocks. The classic route up Mont Blanc (4809 m a.s.l.) registers an accident rate in the Grand Couloir du Goûter of 3.7 deaths per year since 1990. The heightened frequency of rockfalls in the couloir is one of the many factors contributing to the elevated accident rate in this area.40 The hazard posed by these climate change-induced events has resulted in a shift in mountain-related activities, both among recreationalists and alpine guides. A quantitative survey conducted among Alpine guides in the French and Italian Mont Blanc area, unveiled their recognition of the need to adapt their activities.41 Such gravity-driven hazards not only cause direct injuries and fatalities but also disrupt healthcare systems, further straining medical resources and response capabilities (figure 1). Landslides, for instance, can have profound and far-reaching effects on public health. In addition to the common fatalities resulting from trauma or suffocation due to entrapment, the impairment of vital infrastructure, including power, water, gas and sewage systems, can result in injuries and illnesses among the affected population (figure 1). These may encompass waterborne diseases, electrical injuries or lacerations caused by falling debris. Landslides impose also substantial mental health consequences, with post-traumatic stress disorder exhibiting a higher prevalence following landslides compared with other types of disasters (figure 1).42 Alpine regions are also exposed to ice-fall events that can lead to mass-casualty incidents (MCIs). In July 2022, the Marmolada Glacier disaster unfolded in the Dolomites region of the Northeastern Italian Alps, resulting in 11 fatalities.43 Mountain MCIs challenge SAR personnel in providing prompt medical care and can overwhelm the entire healthcare system (figure 1).

Changes in disease vector ecology

Climate change also expands habitats of disease-carrying vectors like ticks, altering their distribution and behaviour (figure 1). Warmer temperatures and altered precipitation patterns enhance breeding and survival rates.44 These changes introduce diseases previously associated with different climate zones45 and heighten the risk of endogenous vector-borne diseases. For instance, tick-borne diseases will increase, particularly impacting vulnerable populations. In recent years, Europe has seen around 85 000 confirmed cases of Lyme disease annually, emerging as a significant public health concern.46 During the last decade, the incidence of Tick-borne encephalitis (TBE) human cases in Europe increased significantly from 2412 in 2012 to 3604 confirmed cases in 2020.47 The TBE virus has also expanded its presence to previously unoccupied environments, including higher altitudes within the Alpine region.48 Since 2000, the incidence rate of TBE has been rising in Tyrol and reached 13 cases per 100 000 in 2013. In contrast, in the area of Lower Austria, the incidence continuously declined to 1.2 per 100 000.49

The ongoing decline in biodiversity driven by human activities, combined with the effects of climate change, contributes to the exacerbation of this phenomenon. A recent multiscale study conducted in Europe and validated in an Alpine area (ie, provinces of Trento and Belluno, Italy) reported a negative relationship between TBE occurrence and habitat richness (ie, the diversity of ecosystem types).50 This relationship highlights how ecological aspects play a crucial role in the natural hazard of TBE and how habitat richness might have an impact on human health. Limited resources and weakened healthcare systems may face challenges in managing these ecological-driven shifts.

Adaptation and mitigation strategies of future climate-change-related risks on health in Alpine regions

To address the complex environmental shifts and their impact on public health in Alpine regions, a multidisciplinary approach combining adaptation and mitigation strategies is crucial (table 1).7 These strategies must be integrated into daily awareness among the general population, healthcare professionals and stakeholders. Local monitoring and planning are urgently needed to keep pace with rapid changes.51

Preventive adaptational strategies

Primary prevention

Urgent local efforts in primary prevention are essential to mitigate climate-induced health risks. Evidence-based strategies should focus on raising awareness and building resilience among professionals, citizens and tourists. Preventive information campaigns targeting vulnerable populations can reduce health issues from heatwaves. In avalanche-prone areas, education and awareness for outdoor enthusiasts and SAR personnel are critical. Prioritising research and development of new safety equipment is also important.

Infrastructure and technology

Infrastructure planning should include protective structures for avalanches and landslides, along with advanced early warning systems and resilient communication technologies.52 Public healthcare facilities need to be adapted to enhance climate resilience and maintain services during climate-related disasters.

Vector-borne diseases

Anticipating the expansion of tick habitats and the rise in TBE, Lyme disease and other vector-borne infections, vaccination campaigns, surveillance and targeted vector control are vital. These measures, along with efforts to conserve natural habitats, require interdisciplinary collaboration and global cooperation.

Responsive adaptational strategies

Emergency response

Specialised training for SAR personnel is essential to handle mass-casualty events effectively, ensuring high-quality missions and on-site medical treatment.53 Disaster preparedness plans and early warning systems further enhance the effectiveness of emergency response. In the event of an MCI, it is crucial to establish efficient triage systems that not only enable fair and ethical decision-making but also help alleviate the psychological impact on those affected.53

Innovative technologies

Innovative technologies such as drones can help overcome challenges in remote mountainous areas by supporting patient searches, delivering medical equipment and establishing telemedical communication.54 During mass casualty events, drones can survey the scene, assess safety, and facilitate remote triage.

Implementing these strategies requires proactive planning, interdisciplinary collaboration and adequate resource allocation to effectively address the growing health risks posed by climate change in Alpine regions.

Conclusions

In summary, Alpine regions, with their unique geography and challenges, including external factors such as increasing tourism flows, environmental changes and an ageing population, present a wide range of health risks that require meticulous management. These risks span from immediate threats like heat-related diseases, trauma and MCI resulting from geohydrological disasters, to issues evolving over a longer period, such as changes in vector ecology and the increased risk of new infectious diseases. To address these risks, the implementation of both preventive and responsive adaptation strategies is essential to ensure the well-being of Alpine communities and visitors.
