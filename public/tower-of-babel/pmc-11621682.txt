Repeated intratracheal instillation effects of commonly used vehicles in toxicity studies with mice
Authors: In-Hyeon Kim, Je-Hein Kim, Se-Woong Park, Su-Jin Lim, Jeong Hwan Kim, Cheoljin Park, Jinsoo Lee, Sung-Hwan Kim
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntratracheal instillation (ITI) is considered the most pragmatic approach for investigating the potential toxicities of various respiratory exposure materials. Various respiratory exposure materials, including nanomaterials, hazardous air pollutants, fine dust, and household biocides, have raised public health concerns because of limited toxicological information and increasing consumption. Hence, toxicity studies using ITI in laboratory animals are important to accurately assess the human risks associated with these respiratory-exposed materials. However, data to adequately support the study design of ITI toxicity studies, particularly those examining the effects of commonly used vehicles following repeated exposure are insufficient. Therefore, in this study, we examined the effects of 16 types of commonly used vehicles in toxicity studies following 14-day repeated ITI in mice. General health endpoints (mortality, clinical signs, and body weight) were monitored throughout the study period, and terminal endpoints (gross observation, lung weight, bronchoalveolar lavage fluid analysis, and lung histopathological examination) were assessed after terminal sacrifice. Saline and phosphate-buffered saline elicited the least response, whereas corn oil (50 µL) showed the most severe toxicity findings. In addition, several commonly used vehicles, including distilled water, sodium carboxymethyl cellulose, dimethyl sulfoxide, ethanol, Tween 20, and Tween 80, induced mild-to-severe toxicity in the respiratory system. Based on the results of this study, some commonly used vehicles in toxicity studies should be used with caution when the ITI exposure route is considered. These results provide important background information on the effects of vehicles in ITI toxicity studies along with valuable insights for designing toxicity studies using respiratory exposure materials.Keywords: Respiratory exposure, Nanomaterials, Regulatory toxicology, Background dataSubject terms: Biological techniques, Natural hazards, Health occupations

Introduction

Public health concern including exposure to various respiratory hazards, such as nanomaterials, hazardous air pollutants, fine dusts, and household biocides have steadily increased1–4. The quantity of these materials has notably increased with rapid progression in science and industrialization. Moreover, their characteristic physicochemical properties render them susceptible to human exposure through the respiratory tract. Although previous studies have reported the toxicological effects of these respiratory hazardous materials, there is a considerable gap in toxicological data for human risk assessment5–9. Various respiratory exposure methods, including intratracheal instillation (ITI), inhalation exposure, and intranasal instillation, are used to expose the lungs of laboratory animals for the toxicological studies of these materials10–13. Each respiratory exposure method has its own technical advantages and disadvantages14,15.

ITI is a technique commonly used to expose the lungs of laboratory animals to respiratory hazardous materials. This method was first used in studies investigating the toxicological effects of coal tar in laboratory animals, such as rabbits and guinea pigs16. ITI is a cost-effective method of exposure, enabling immediate delivery of a predetermined quantity of test materials directly to the lungs of laboratory animals15. This method is feasible both invasively and non-invasively. In the invasive approach, test materials are introduced by surgically exposing the trachea on the ventral side of the neck and inserting a needle through the tracheal wall just below the larynx17. Alternatively, the noninvasive method involves inserting a catheter orally into the pharyngeal region18. ITI is simpler than the inhalation exposure method, which involves expensive nose-only or whole-body inhalation chambers. It also minimizes the risk to laboratory researchers via reduces their exposure to potentially unknown and highly toxic materials. Moreover, it facilitates the targeted administration of doses directly to the lungs within a short time while avoiding skin or oral exposure that may occur with inhalational exposure methods19.

The toxicological effects of various respiratory-exposed materials have been investigated in laboratory animals using ITI. Morimoto et al. summarized the usefulness and applicability of the ITI method for estimating nanoparticle-induced respiratory toxicity20. The potential toxicity of various nanoparticles, including titanium dioxide, cerium oxide, single-walled carbon nanotubes, and multi-walled carbon nanotubes, has been investigated in laboratory animals using ITI. Similarly, hazardous air pollutants and fine dust have been administered via ITI to assess their potential toxicity in human health risk assessment21–23. These results demonstrate the involvement of hazardous air pollutants and fine dust in the disruption of physiological homeostasis. Furthermore, commonly used household biocides such as triclosan, chloromethylisothiazolinone/methylisothiazolinone, and polyhexamethylene guanidine phosphate have been intratracheally instilled into laboratory animals to elucidate their potential toxicities24–27. Although these studies have effectively investigated the potential toxicological effects of various materials, the ITI method has limitations in broader applications. The selection of an appropriate vehicle is an important consideration in toxicity studies involving diverse test materials. However, information regarding the commonly used vehicles in ITI toxicity studies involving laboratory animals has been limited.

Therefore, in the present study, we aimed to examine the effects of commonly used vehicles in toxicity studies, including distilled water (DW), saline, phosphate buffered saline (PBS), dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, 5% and 10%), ethanol (5% and 10%), Tween 20 (0.1% and 0.5%), Tween 80 (0.1% and 0.5%), sodium carboxymethyl cellulose-Na (CMC, 1% and 2.5%), and corn oil (10, 30, and 50 µL), following a 14-day repeated ITI in mice. Although CMC and corn oil are more commonly used in oral toxicity studies than in ITI toxicity studies, they were included in this study to observe the potential repeat-dose effects with ITI, as they are frequently used vehicles. Additionally, the dilution ratios of vehicles were applied based on the most commonly used dilution ratios in general toxicity studies. To this end, we aimed to evaluate the general in-life and terminal health endpoints during the study period with mice according to the related Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development test guidelines for respiratory endpoints in repeated inhalation toxicity studies28,29.

Materials and methods

Animals and maintenance

Specific pathogen-free (SPF) C57BL male mice (7-week-old) were obtained from Orient Bio (Gyeonggi-do, Republic of Korea). The animals that seemed healthy during the acclimation period (at least five days) were used in this study. Healthy animals with similar mean body weights were assigned to experimental groups using the Pristima system (Xybion Medical Systems, NJ, USA). All animals were housed in an animal room controlled under standard conditions (temperature 20–26 °C, humidity 30–70%, light 12 h light/12 h dark cycle with 150–300 lx, and ventilation 10–20 air changes per hour), as previously described30. All animals were housed individually in stainless-steel cages (130 mm W × 170 mm L × 100 mm H) with autoclaved animal enrichments (wood block; Doongji Bio, Chungcheongnam-do, Republic of Korea). An irradiated standard rodent pellet diet (PMI Nutrition International, MO, USA) was provided ad libitum. Filtered and irradiated municipal tap water was provided ad libitum. No known contaminants were present in the diet, water, and animal enrichments at concentrations that would have interfered with the study results.

All animal procedures were conducted at the Korea Institute of Toxicology (KIT), which is fully accredited by the Association for Assessment and Accreditation of Laboratory Animal Care International. The Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of KIT reviewed and approved animal experimental procedures, ensuring compliance with the Animal Protection Act of Korea and the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals31.

Preparation of test vehicles

DW was prepared using Direct-Q® 3 UV Water Purification System (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany). PBS was obtained from Gibco Korea Ltd. (Seoul, Republic of Korea). Saline was obtained from Dai Han Pharmaceuticals Ltd. (Seoul, Republic of Korea). DMSO, ethanol, Tween 80, CMC, and corn oil were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich Korea Ltd. (Seoul, Republic of Korea). Tween 20 was obtained from Bio-Rad Korea Ltd. (Seoul, Republic of Korea).

For ITI, DW, PBS, saline, and corn oil were administered directly to the mice. DMSO, ethanol, Tween 20, Tween 80, and CMC were diluted for ITI as follows. DMSO and ethanol were diluted with DW to final concentrations of 5% and 10%, respectively. Tween 20 and 80 were diluted in DW to concentrations of 0.1% and 0.5%, respectively. CMC was diluted in DW to 2.5%, followed by sonication using a probe sonicator (VCX 500, Sonics & Materials, Inc., Connecticut, USA) for approximately 5 min. Additionally, a 1% CMC solution was prepared to further dilute 2.5% CMC.

Experimental groups and intratracheal instillation

All animals, except for the negative control group, received various types of vehicles via a non-invasive ITI method for 14 days. All animals, including negative control group, were conducted ITI under light isoflurane anesthesia. The anesthesia and administration procedures took approximately 3 min per animal, and the ITI was conducted at the same time each day. The dose volume was 50 µL, with the exception of two corn oil groups that received 10 and 30 µL. A total of 102 male SPF C57BL mice were divided into 17 groups (six animals per group). Two separate studies were conducted to examine the effects of commonly used vehicles in toxicity studies with mice. The first study comprised ten different groups, including the negative control, saline, 0.1% Tween 20, 0.5% Tween 20, 0.1% Tween 80, 0.5% Tween 80, 1% CMC, 2.5% CMC, and corn oil (10 and 30 µL). The second experiment comprised seven different groups, including DW, PBS, 5% DMSO, 10% DMSO, 5% ethanol, 10% ethanol, and corn oil.

In-life observations

All animals were monitored for mortality, general clinical signs, and body weight changes during the in-life period. Mortality was monitored daily throughout the entire in-life period. General clinical signs, including assessments of general appearance and behavioral changes, were observed once daily during the non-dosing period and twice daily (pre- and post-dosing) throughout the entire in-life period. Body weight measurement was conducted on dosing days 1, 2, 4, 8, 11, and 15.

Terminal observations

Following the 14-day ITI dosing period, all surviving animals were euthanized using isoflurane and humanely sacrificed by exsanguination of the vena cava and aorta. All animals were subjected to terminal observations, including gross observation, bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) analysis (cell count and clinical chemistry), lung weight measurements, and lung histopathological observations.

Gross observations were conducted for all euthanized animals, and external, abdominal, and thoracic cavity abnormalities were carefully examined. BALF was collected as previously described for the analysis of cell counts (total and differential) and clinical chemistry parameters (total protein, albumin, and lactate dehydrogenase [LDH])32. Briefly, 0.7 mL of PBS was inserted via the incised trachea using a polypropylene tube and retrieved after three washes to collect BALF. We confirmed that the volume of collected BALF was consistent between animals. Subsequently, 20 µL of BALF was used to evaluate the total cell count using a NucleoCounter® cell count analyzer (NC-250; Chemometec, Gydevang, Denmark). For differential cell counts in BALF, 230 µL of BALF was centrifuged (approximately 72 × g for 10 min) using a Shandon Cytospin 4 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) and stained with Diff-Quik (Sysmex, Kobe, Japan). Differential counts of alveolar macrophages, lymphocytes, neutrophils, and eosinophils were manually evaluated under a light microscope by analyzing 200 cells per slide. The remaining BALF was centrifuged (approximately 4000 × g for 10 min) to isolate the supernatant for the analysis of clinical chemistry parameters (total protein, albumin, and LDH) using a Toshiba 200FR NEO chemistry analyzer (Toshiba, Tokyo, Japan).

After BALF collection, the left lung was dissected for absolute lung weight measurement. The relative lung weight was calculated based on the terminal body weight at necropsy. The right lung was preserved for histopathological observation as described previously33. Briefly, the right lung was fixed in 10% buffered formalin and embedded in paraffin. Formalin was gently infused into the lungs via the trachea to achieve inflation. The lung was divided into 4-µm thick sections. Sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin for subsequent histopathological analysis. All sections were examined under a light microscope.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using either GraphPad InStat (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, USA) or the Pristima system (Xybion Medical System, USA) based on general statistical analysis methods used in previous toxicology studies34,35. Statistical analyses were conducted to compare the various dose groups with the vehicle control group. Homogeneity of variance was assessed using Bartlett’s test. For homogeneous data, differences among groups were evaluated using Analysis of Variance (ANOVA), followed by Dunnett’s test for inter-group comparisons. For heterogeneous data, the Kruskal-Wallis test was applied, and inter-group differences between the control and treated groups were assessed using Dunn’s rank-sum test. Comparisons were conducted between the various vehicle groups and the negative control group to identify statistically significant differences. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Body weights

The body weights during the study period are presented in Table 1. In several vehicle-treated groups, including 1% CMC, 2.5% CMC, corn oil (30 and 50 µL), saline, DW, and 10% ethanol groups, body weights were significantly lower than those in the negative control group. In the corn oil groups (30 and 50 µL), lower body weights (up to -25% from the negative control) were observed from dosing day eight to the day of terminal sacrifice. Similarly, in 1% CMC, 2.5% CMC, and 10% ethanol-treated groups, lower body weights (up to -13% from the negative control) were observed from dosing day 11 to the day of terminal sacrifice. Additionally, the saline and DW-treated groups showed lower body weights (up to -9% from the negative control) on the day of terminal sacrifice.

Specifically, red lung discoloration was observed in groups treated with 1% CMC, 2.5% CMC, 5% DMSO, and 10% DMSO. Histopathological observations

The histopathological observation results are presented in Fig. 1; Table 5. Several vehicle-treated groups, including 1% CMC, 2.5% CMC, corn oil (10 and 30 µL), 0.1% Tween 20, 0.5% Tween 20, 0.1% Tween 80, 0.5% Tween 80, 5% ethanol, and DW groups, showed histopathological findings in the lung tissue. Aggregates of foamy macrophages were observed in the lungs of groups treated with 1% CMC, 2.5% CMC, and corn oil (10 and 30 µL), whereas mucous cell hyperplasia in the bronchiolar epithelium was observed in the corn oil (30 µL) group. Additionally, the infiltration of perivascular mixed cells and/or alveolar neutrophils was observed in several groups, including 1% CMC, 2.5% CMC, corn oil (10 and 30 µL), 0.1% Tween 20, 0.5% Tween 20, 0.1% Tween 80, 0.5% Tween 80, 5% ethanol, and DW groups. Furthermore, mucous plugs in the lungs were observed in groups treated with 0.1% Tween 20, 0.5% Tween 20, 0.1% Tween 80, and 0.5% Tween 80, whereas thickened alveolar septa were observed in the corn oil (30 µL) group.

Discussion and conclusion

Various respiratory-exposed materials, including nanomaterials, hazardous air pollutants, dust, and household biocides, have raised public health concerns owing to their potential health risks. However, insufficient information regarding their toxicity is available to accurately assess the risks to humans. Therefore, toxicity studies with respiratory exposure materials using laboratory animals are necessary. The ITI method is considered the most realistic approach for such toxicity studies. Despite the use of the ITI method to investigate the potential toxicities of respiratory-exposure materials, limited background information is available on the repeated effects of commonly used vehicles in toxicity studies. To the best of our knowledge, only Numano et al. have reported the effects of commonly used vehicles following a single ITI exposure36.

Selecting an appropriate vehicle for administering test materials is a crucial process in toxicity studies using laboratory animals. Ideally, the vehicles used in these studies should maintain the homogeneity and stability of the test materials. Additionally, they should be well tolerated and not influence normal toxicity biomarkers in laboratory animals. Therefore, it is advisable to acquire accurate toxicological data on vehicles in the absence of previous experience or availability of published data for a specific vehicle, as this could potentially confound the interpretation of toxicity studies. Various types of vehicles, including hydrophilic solutions, emulsifying agents, surfactants, solvents, and lipid-based formulations are commonly used in toxicity studies. Thackaberry summarized the potential toxicities of commonly used vehicles in oral toxicity studies based on exposure duration, routes of exposure, concentrations, and laboratory animal species37.

In this study, mice were repeatedly exposed via ITI to 16 types of commonly used vehicles in toxicity studies, and the potential effects of these vehicles were observed. The corn oil (50 µL)-treated group showed the most severe toxicity among various vehicle-treated groups. All animals in this group died before the scheduled day of euthanization, and a corresponding decrease in body weight was observed during the in-life period. Additionally, the groups treated with a lower volume of corn oil (10 and 30 µL) also showed toxicities, including lower body weight, lung discoloration (pale) in gross observation, increased lung weight, difference in cell counts/clinical chemistry in BALF analysis, and/or lung histopathological abnormalities. Although corn oil, a lipid-based formulation, is sometimes used as a vehicle for lipophilic test materials in toxicity studies involving oral exposure38,39, it is not considered an appropriate respiratory exposure material because of the toxicity findings observed in this study.

CMC, an emulsifying agent, is commonly used as a vehicle for hydrophobic test materials in toxicity studies with oral exposure because of its low toxicity, poor bioavailability, and ability to create uniform suspensions40,41. The CMC-treated groups (1% and 2.5%) also showed relatively severe toxicities following repeated exposure to ITI. One animal in the 2.5% CMC group died (dosing day six), and a correlation with lower body weight was observed during the in-life period. Additionally, lung discoloration (red) on gross observation, increased lung weight, differences in cell counts/clinical chemistry in BALF analysis, and lung histopathological abnormalities were observed in the 2.5% CMC group, with similar but less severe findings in the 1% CMC group. Hence, CMC is an inappropriate vehicle because of the toxicity findings associated with respiratory exposure observed in this study.

Low concentrations of Tween 20 and Tween 80 are commonly used as surfactants in toxicity studies with oral exposure because of their low toxicity and the ability to improve suspensions37. Tween 20 (0.1% and 0.5%)- and Tween 80 (0.1% and 0.5%)-treated groups showed mild toxicities following repeated exposure to ITI. Particularly in histopathological observations, infiltration of perivascular mixed cells and mucous plugs in the lungs were observed in the Tween 20 (0.1% and 0.5%)- and Tween 80 (0.1% and 0.5%)-treated groups. Additionally, increased lung weight and/or differences in BALF cell counts were observed in the 0.5% Tween 20 and 0.5% Tween 80 groups. Hence caution should be exercised when using Tween 20 and Tween 80 as vehicles for repeated exposure to the respiratory system, given the toxicity findings observed in this study.

The DW-treated group showed lower body weight, increased lung weight, and infiltration of perivascular mixed cells in histopathological observations following 14-day repeated ITI. A previous study also reported similar histopathological abnormalities in the lungs after a single ITI with DW36. Therefore, DW is commonly used as a vehicle in ITI studies and is considered non-toxic; however, caution should be exercised when using DW as a vehicle for respiratory exposure. In contrast to the findings in the DW-treated group, the saline- and PBS-treated groups showed the lowest response following 14-day repeated ITI. These results are similar to those of a previous study that employed a single ITI procedure with saline or PBS36.

In other vehicle-treated groups, including ethanol (5% and 10%), DMSO (5% and 10%), and saline groups, statistically significant differences were observed for some endpoints. The lower body weight values observed in the 10% ethanol-treated group were attributed to the treatment however, it was not possible to definitively classify it as a toxicity finding because of the absence of corresponding findings in gross observation, lung weight evaluation, BALF analysis, or histopathological observations. Similarly, lung discoloration (red) in the DMSO (5% and 10%)-treated groups during gross observation was considered treatment-related. However, it was also uncertain to definitively classify it as a toxicity finding due to the lack of corresponding findings in other endpoints. However, the other observed findings were not considered treatment-related effects because they were transient and did not have dose-dependency.

The exposure route is a key factor that influences the effects of test materials. Exposure is a condition that allows external test materials to enter the body. Test materials can enter the body through various routes, such as food or water intake (oral route), application to the skin (dermal route), or air (inhalation) etc42. Different exposure routes lead to varying effects owing to differences in the physiological environmental conditions and physicochemical properties of the test materials, which affect the absorption rates43. For instance, vehicles commonly used in toxicity studies, such as corn oil (for durations up to one month), CMC, Tween, and DW, are considered non-toxic in oral toxicity studies37. However, these vehicles are considered inadequate for use with respiratory-exposure materials based on the observed toxicity findings in this study. Therefore, selecting an appropriate vehicle depending on the exposure route of the test materials is important for accurately interpreting the results of toxicity studies.

Based on the results of this study, some vehicles commonly used in toxicity studies were found to be inadequate for respiratory exposure. Among the various vehicles tested, saline and PBS elicited the lowest response following 14-day repeated ITI. However, other vehicles induce mild to severe toxicities in the respiratory system. These findings support the need for careful consideration of vehicle selection when designing toxicity studies using respiratory-exposure materials. Furthermore, additional information on vehicles, such as the longer duration of repeated exposure, effects on other laboratory animal species, and effects on juvenile animals, would provide valuable insights for designing toxicity studies using respiratory-exposure materials.
