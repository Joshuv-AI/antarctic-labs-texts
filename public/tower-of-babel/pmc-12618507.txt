Spirocyclic poly(vinylene ether ketone) membranes with enhanced microporosity for energy-efficient alcohol-hydrocarbon azeotrope separation
Authors: Jiaqi Li, Yijie Fang, Ruiyan Xiong, Weilin Feng, Hukang Guo, Fupeng Li, Mengxiao Zhang, Shuai Wang, Chuanjie Fang, Baoku Zhu, Young Moo Lee, Liping Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The energy-efficient separation of azeotropic organic mixtures remains a critical challenge in chemical manufacturing. Conventional thermal-driven processes like distillation and pervaporation face fundamental thermodynamic constraints, requiring energy-intensive phase transitions. We present a pressure-driven membrane solution using a spirocyclic poly(vinylene ether ketone) membrane with enhanced microporosity that achieves alcohol-selective permeation from alcohol-hydrocarbon azeotropes. The membrane demonstrates separation factors of 330 for ethanol/cyclohexane and 74 for ethanol/heptane systems, while reducing energy consumption by 2-3 orders of magnitude compared to conventional methods. Through multiscale characterization and molecular dynamics simulations, we elucidate the dual mechanism governing separation performance: differential surface adsorption affinity and restricted hydrocarbon diffusion within micropores of membrane. Here, we show a molecular sieving strategy bypasses traditional volatility-based separation paradigms, offering an approach for azeotrope fractionation with transformative potential for sustainable chemical processing.

Introduction

In pivotal industrial operations such as Fischer-Tropsch synthesis1,2 and fluid catalytic cracking3,4, alcohol byproducts inevitably form fully miscible azeotropic mixtures with hydrocarbon streams. The separation of these systems poses a persistent technological challenge, yet is indispensable for producing high-purity, high-value feedstocks required in specialty chemical manufacturing5. While distillation remains the predominant industrial separation method6, its utility is fundamentally constrained by the ubiquity of alcohol/hydrocarbon azeotropes7,8. Although entrainer-assisted distillation can modulate component volatility9,10, this strategy exacerbates energy intensity (accounting for >80% of total chemical separation energy costs11) and process complexity through mandatory liquid-vapor phase transitions (Fig. 1a). Membrane-based pervaporation12–15 partially circumvents distillation limitations but retains thermodynamic inefficiencies, requiring substantial energy input to overcome latent heat demands (Fig.1a). This inherent energy penalty renders pervaporation economically impractical for large-scale applications. Pressure-driven membrane technology offers a promising solution to these thermodynamic limitations through molecular discrimination based on size, shape, and physicochemical properties via dense permselective membranes16,17. Unlike traditional temperature-dependent methods, such processes operate without phase transitions, drastically reducing energy consumption18. Building on reverse osmosis principles from seawater desalination, organic solvent reverse osmosis (OSRO, Fig. 1a) has emerged as a transformative approach for precise molecular fractionation of small organics (< 200 Da), including xylene isomers and hydrocarbon fractions19–23. A seminal study by Lively and coworkers demonstrated exceptional separation efficiency for xylene isomers using carbon molecular sieve membranes via OSRO20. Recent advancements in membrane materials, module engineering, and transport mechanisms have further expanded OSRO’s applicability to complex organic mixtures22,24–26. It is noteworthy that only a few studies focus on the separation of azeotropic organic mixtures16,26–28, such as aromatic/Aliphatic hydrocarbons and methanol/toluene. This is primarily due to the significant challenges in precisely controlling the microstructure of polymeric membranes for specific azeotropic systems. Current limitations stem from the need for molecular-level control over pore architecture and functional group distribution—requirements that exceed conventional membrane design paradigms.

We present a pioneering strategy for energy-efficient azeotropic alcohol/hydrocarbon separation via organic solvent reverse osmosis (OSRO) using polymeric membranes with enhanced microporosity. The fundamental challenge lies in designing polymer-based membranes that simultaneously achieve molecular sieving precision and structural integrity under organic solvent conditions. While selective permeation requires controlled dissolution and diffusion of organic species, conventional polymeric membranes suffer from plasticization and swelling—phenomena that degrade separation performance and operational stability20–22. This explains why only a limited number of polymer-derived materials have demonstrated practical OSRO applicability to date. Polymers of intrinsic microporosity (PIMs)29, with their rigid ladder-like architectures, offer inherent microporosity that facilitates molecular transport21,22. However, their broad pore size distributions (< 2 nm)30–33 result in suboptimal separation accuracy for alcohol/hydrocarbon azeotropes. To overcome this limitation, we propose a molecular design paradigm shift: engineering membranes with a precisely tailored microporous architecture. This approach addresses the critical need for compact, size-selective polymer frameworks capable of discriminating between alcohol and hydrocarbon molecules with molecular-level precision.

In this work, building on the structural framework of PIM-1, we strategically modify its chemical architecture to enable precise alcohol/hydrocarbon separation. Spirocyclic moieties embedded in the polymer backbone critically inhibit chain packing, generating uniform micropores29,34,35. Prior work has shown that judicious insertion of flexible linkers between these spirocycles enhances chain alignment while preserving microporosity21,22. Leveraging these insights, we synthesize a spirocyclic poly(vinylene ether ketone) (SPVEK) via click chemistry36,37, fabricating thin-film composite (TFC) membranes on porous polyimide substrates (Fig. 1b). Rigorous characterization validates microporous architecture of the SPVEK membrane, organic solvent stability, and alcohol/hydrocarbon permselectivity. Complementary molecular dynamics (MD) simulations reveal the dual mechanism for azeotrope separation: alcohols exhibit preferential surface adsorption and internal diffusion, while hydrocarbons face steric hindrance effect within sub-nanometer channels (Fig. 1c). This work establishes a materials design blueprint for OSRO membranes, achieving azeotrope separation while operating at ambient temperatures—a transformative advance toward sustainable chemical separations.

Results

Solvent-Stable SPVEK with Enhanced Microporosity

We engineered a pressure-driven membrane for OSRO using an established TFC architecture (Fig. 1b). The selective layer comprises spirocyclic poly(vinylene ether ketone) (SPVEK), synthesized via a click reaction37 based the spirocyclic monomer 3,3,3’,3’-tetramethyl-1,1’-spirobisindane-6,6’-diol (SBI) and the aromatic monomer 1,4-dipropioloylbenzene (DPPB) (Fig. 1b, and Supplementary Fig. 1–6). Gel permeation chromatography (GPC) confirmed a weight-average molecular weight (Mw) of 20 kDa and a polydispersity index (PDI) of 2.4 (Supplementary Fig. 7). SPVEK exhibits good solubility in volatile solvents such as tetrahydrofuran and chloroform (Supplementary Table 1, Supplementary Fig. 8), enabling facile fabrication of uniform films. Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) revealed no detectable glass transition below the decomposition temperature of the SPVEK (Supplementary Fig. 9–10), attributable to its rigid architecture—comprising benzene rings and spirocyclic motifs—that restricts chain mobility. This behavior mirrors that of PIM-138,39, which shares analogous spirocyclic units, further validating the structural design’s role in enhancing thermal stability.

To engineer SPVEK with tailored microporous topology, we introduced vinylene ether ketone (VEK) units to enhance backbone flexibility while maintaining microporosity. MD simulations revealed conformational flexibility of the VEK segment, with kernel density estimation (KDE) mapping the dynamic distributions of interatomic distances (d) and angles (θ) between interatomic connection line and benzene ring (Fig. 2a, b, and Supplementary Fig. 11). Comparative analysis demonstrated broader conformational space of VEK (Δd ≈ 0.7 Å, Δθ ≈ 40°) relative to the rigid dioxane units in PIM-1 (Δd ≈ 0.16 Å, Δθ ≈ 10°) (Fig. 2a, b and Supplementary Fig. 11), confirming enhanced chain mobility. This structural distinction manifests in diminished N2 (77 K) and CO2 (273 K) adsorption capacities compared to PIM-1 (Fig. 2c, and Supplementary Fig. 12). To elucidate microporous architecture of SPVEK, we performed MD simulations using the Gromacs package40, constructing atomic-scale models of the dry polymer (Supplementary Fig. 13–15, Supplementary Table 2–4). Triplicate models per system ensured statistical reliability, with thermal equilibration achieved through 21-step annealing protocols. While SPVEK and PIM-1 exhibited comparable densities (~1.0 g cm−3), their pore topology diverged markedly (Fig. 2d, e, and Supplementary Fig. 15-16). Monte Carlo pore analysis revealed a maximum interconnected pore diameter (Df) of 2.06 Å for SPVEK versus 3.60 Å for PIM-1 (Supplementary Table 4)—directly explaining impermeability for SPVEK to N2 (3.64 Å kinetic diameter). This aligns with experimental N2 adsorption isotherms (Supplementary Fig. 12), validating the computational models. Crucially, micropores (1–6 Å) in SPVEK demonstrated narrow distribution versus wide distribution (2-11 Å) in PIM-1 (Fig. 2f).

Swelling studies confirmed structural resilience for SPVEK: exposure to ethanol/cyclohexane induced minimal mass changes (swelling degree < 1.5–10% vs. > 43–57% for PIM-1; Supplementary Table 5), while simulations revealed preserved micropores (1–6.5 Å post-swelling; Supplementary Fig. 17). This solvent stability establishes SPVEK as suited for maintaining size-sieving microporosity during azeotrope separations.

SPVEK membrane with enhanced microporosity for azeotrope resolution

The SPVEK membrane was fabricated through spin-coating of a chloroform polymer solution onto a crosslinked polyimide porous support, forming a TFC architecture with a defect-free selective layer (Fig. 1b). Cross-sectional scanning electron microscope image revealed a distinct interface between the dense SPVEK selective layer (∼170 nm thick) and the underlying crosslinked polyimide support (Fig. 1b, Supplementary Fig. 18). To evaluate separation performance, we focused on the ethanol/cyclohexane azeotrope—a system of broad industrial relevance in bioethanol dehydration and chemical production processes where cyclohexane serves as an entrainer15,41–43. Prior to testing, membranes underwent preconditioning through continuous mixture permeation for 48 h at 8 MPa transmembrane pressure to stabilize swelling-induced structural rearrangements. The preconditioning process and separation performance tests were conducted in a cross-flow filtration system (Supplementary Fig. 19) operated at 25 °C.

The SPVEK TFC membrane demonstrates exceptional ethanol/cyclohexane separation performance with concentration-dependent selectivity (Fig. 3a, b). Ethanol consistently dominates permeate streams (cyclohexane content < 3.5 wt.%) across feed ethanol concentrations spanning 10-90 wt.%. Remarkably, decreasing ethanol feed fraction from 90 wt.% to 10 wt.% enhances separation factors from 14 to 330, albeit with a concomitant flux reduction from 1.38 to 0.17 kg m-2 h-1 (Fig. 3b). This inverse correlation between selectivity and flux arises from ethanol-induced plasticization: swelling degree measurements reveal SPVEK undergoes 10% mass expansion in ethanol versus merely 1.5% in cyclohexane (Supplementary Table 5). At elevated ethanol concentrations, this preferential swelling enlarges micropores, compromising size-sieving precision. The permeate ethanol fraction consistently surpasses feed concentrations, revealing reverse osmosis characteristics through generation of counteracting osmotic pressure. Quantitative analysis of feed/permeate concentration gradients enabled calculation of apparent osmotic pressures (2.4–11.5 MPa range) that remarkably exceeded applied transmembrane pressures (8 MPa, Fig. 3c, Supplementary Fig. 20, 21). This abnormal osmotic pressure phenomenon may be related to the preferential adsorption of ethanol—adsorption experiments and molecular dynamics indicate that ethanol accumulates at the membrane-solvent interface and within the membrane (Supplementary Fig. 22, and Supplementary Table 6). We hypothesize that ethanol exhibits preferential adsorption at the feed solution interface and recalculate the activity and osmotic pressure using molecular simulation concentration data (at the feed solution-membrane interface). The resulting corrective osmotic pressure (Fig. 3c) appears more reasonable. This also reminds us that when evaluating osmotic pressure and the OSRO membrane process, attention should be paid to the influence of the membrane material’s specific adsorption of feed solution components. Additionally, the observed inverse relationship between ethanol feed concentration (10-90 wt%) and osmotic pressure (11.5-2.4 MPa) directly explains flux enhancement at higher ethanol fractions through increased effective driving force.

The separation performance of the SPVEK membrane exhibits strong pressure dependence, revealing a critical pressure threshold for optimal operation (Fig. 3d, and Supplementary Fig. 21). Increasing transmembrane pressure from 4 to 8 MPa drives separation factor surges from 10 to 84 - a 740% enhancement demonstrating pressure-tunable molecular discrimination. This pressure-activation behavior aligns with established OSRO mechanisms where elevated hydraulic forces overcome solution-diffusion limitations24,27. Flux analysis reveals a transport mechanism: ethanol permeance escalates from 0.16 to 1.37 kg m⁻² h⁻¹ with pressure, while cyclohexane flux remains suppressed (< 0.012 kg m⁻² h⁻¹) across all tested conditions (Supplementary Fig. 23). This striking disparity is likely attributed to ethanol dual advantageous characteristics: (1) enhanced intermolecular interaction capability with SPVEK, and (2) a substantially smaller kinetic dimensions (Supplementary Fig. 24), which facilitates selective microporous transport.

The permselectivity of the SPVEK membrane was systematically evaluated using industrially relevant azeotropic mixtures: ethanol/heptane, ethanol/benzene, methanol/toluene, and alcohol/ester mixtures (Fig. 3e). The SPVEK membrane demonstrated a separation factor (α) of 74 for ethanol/heptane. However, performance declined for ethanol/benzene (α ≈ 2) and methanol/toluene (α ≈ 6), attributed to membrane swelling in aromatic solvents (swelling degree > 180%, Supplementary Table 5). This result demonstrates the importance of anti-solvent plasticization in molecular sieving for membrane material design. Notably, the membrane exhibited a certain degree of selectivity for alcohol/ester mixtures (Fig. 3e, mixtures 4–7), highlighting potential for esterification process intensification. Long-term stability testing under industrial-relevant conditions (8 MPa, 30 days) revealed < 5% flux decline and stable permselectivity (Fig. 3f, and Supplementary Fig. 25), demonstrating exceptional operational durability. Remarkably, we found that during the OSRO process (driven solely by pressure at ambient temperature), the SPVEK membrane exhibited high separation performance (α = 330, Flux=0.17 kg m–2 h–1) comparable to most energy-intensive pervaporation processes (operated under heated and vacuum conditions, Supplementary Fig. 26). This establishes a benchmark for membrane-based azeotrope separation.

Two-dimensional density distribution plot of the SPVEK membrane (Fig. 4b, and Supplementary Fig. 29) revealed uniform polymer backbone distribution with localized variations, indicative of solvent-induced swelling during diffusion. To elucidate the permselectivity mechanism, we analyzed solvent density distribution along the transport direction (z axis, Fig. 4c, d). Ethanol exhibited preferential adsorption at the feed-side membrane surface (near z = 22.5 nm), with concentrations significantly exceeding the feed, while the concentration of cyclohexane is lower than the feed (Fig. 4d). Permeate-side analysis revealed that the ethanol/cyclohexane ratio at the interface is similar to that on the permeate side, with no significant selective adsorption. This is attributed to ethanol being the main component (> 96.5 wt.%) in the permeate. Coordination number analysis (Supplementary Fig. 31) revealed reduced yet non-isolated solvent clustering (< 5 Å) within membrane channels, suggesting cluster-mediated transport through confined pathways.

Analysis of molecular dynamics simulations revealed distinct transport behaviors through mean square displacement (MSD) calculations (Fig. 4e). The ethanol diffusion coefficient (2.20 × 10⁻8 cm2 s⁻1) significantly surpasses that of cyclohexane (0.73 × 10⁻8 cm2 s⁻1), confirming preferential ethanol permeation (Supplementary Table 9). Applying the solution-diffusion model, we quantified the selectivity components: diffusion selectivity (58.8%) and sorption selectivity (41.2%), which combine to produce the overall membrane selectivity. Molecular interaction analysis reveals that ethanol engages with SPVEK chains via hydrogen bonding (-33.26 kJ mol-1), whereas cyclohexane exhibits exclusively van der Waals interactions (-17.53 kJ mol-1, Fig. 4f, g; Supplementary Fig. 32-33). Stronger hydrogen bonding interactions lead to greater adsorption of ethanol on the membrane surface. Meanwhile, due to its larger kinetic dimensions, cyclohexane exhibits a three times lower diffusion coefficient within the membrane (Supplementary Fig. 24). The preferential adsorption of ethanol and the lower diffusion rate of cyclohexane within the membrane collectively determine high selectivity for ethanol/cyclohexane azeotrope separation, demonstrating the dual selectivity mechanism of SPVEK membranes.

Energy-efficient cascade processing for industrial separations

In membrane separation processes, separation efficiency is critically governed by membrane selectivity. Conventional membranes typically exhibit performance degradation with varying feed compositions, significantly limiting their operational flexibility. Remarkably, our SPVEK membrane demonstrates an inverse correlation between selectivity and ethanol concentration in the feed (Fig. 3b). This counterintuitive behavior, where selectivity increases as ethanol concentration decreases, suggests a molecular discrimination mechanism fundamentally different from conventional pervaporation membranes. To evaluate practical applicability, we engineered a two-stage cascade separation process (Fig. 5a) using an azeotropic ethanol/cyclohexane mixture (1:1 w/w). The first-stage permeate achieved > 95 wt.% ethanol purity, while the retentate concentrated to > 95 wt.% cyclohexane (Fig. 5b). Subsequent second-stage processing yielded ethanol purity exceeding 98 wt.% (Fig. 5c), demonstrating exceptional multi-stage scalability. Energy analysis reveals the transformative potential of our OSRO process (Fig. 5d). Compared to conventional distillation and pervaporation, our membrane system reduces energy consumption by 2–3 orders of magnitude18. Discussion

Our findings establish a facile click chemistry route for fabricating SPVEK membranes with enhanced microporosity that exhibit unprecedented separation selectivity for alcohol/hydrocarbon azeotropes. Multiscale investigations reveal that the record ethanol/cyclohexane selectivity (α = 330) stems from a dual mechanism combining thermodynamic and kinetic factors: preferential ethanol adsorption through hydrogen-bond interactions with the VEK structure, coupled with size-sieving effects from micropores that restrict hydrocarbon diffusion. Notably, the strategic incorporation of spirocyclic moieties not only creates molecular-sieving architecture but also effectively mitigates plasticization, enabling stable operation under realistic feed conditions – a critical advancement over conventional glassy polymers prone to swelling-induced performance decay.

Process modeling demonstrates the technology’s industrial relevance: A two-stage cascade system achieves 98 wt.% ethanol purity from azeotropic feeds while reducing energy intensity, representing a 98-99% reduction compared to distillation. This performance envelope suggests potential applicability across biofuel purification and petrochemical separation sectors where thermal processes dominate.

Three key limitations warrant further investigation: 1) Long-term stability under cyclic pressure/solvent exposure requires extended testing, 2) Scalable manufacturing of defect-free thin selective layers needs optimization, and 3) Competitive sorption effects in complex multicomponent feeds remain uncharacterized. Practical implementation would require module engineering to handle organic solvents and integration with existing separation trains – challenges being addressed through current collaborations with membrane manufacturers.

This work achieves the separation of azeotropes by decoupling selectivity from relative volatility, instead leveraging molecular-level design of sorption-diffusion hierarchies. The demonstrated energy efficiency gains could significantly advance sustainable chemical production paradigms, particularly when coupled with renewable energy-driven membrane systems.

Methods

SPVEK TFC membrane fabrication, characterization, and performance

The SPVEK thin-film composite membrane was fabricated by spin-coating a 1.0 wt.% solution of SPVEK in chloroform onto the polyimide support membrane (see Supplementary Information). The solution was applied at 2000 rpm to form the initial membrane, which was then dried at room temperature for 24 h and subsequently treated at 60 °C for 30 min to finalize the SPVEK thin-film composite membrane.

The surface and cross-sectional morphologies of SPVEK TFC membranes were observed by field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM; SU-8010, Japan).

Solvent permeation tests were conducted using a laboratory-scale cross-flow permeation system (Supplementary Fig. 19) at 25 ± 3 °C. All membranes used in the tests had an identical effective area of 7 cm2. The flow rate was set at 8 mL min-1, and the operating pressure was maintained between 4 and 8 MPa.

Computational methods

The all-atom polymer models were constructed through the following detailed steps:

1) Polymer Unit Construction: Repeat units of the polymer were constructed using the General Amber Force Field (GAFF)46, as detailed in Supplementary Fig. 13 and Supplementary Table 2. Electrostatic interactions of atoms were described using the Restrained Electrostatic Potential (RESP) charges47, calculated with Multiwfn48. These charges were obtained through charge fitting from Gaussian16 package49 calculations at the HF/6-31 G* level of theory (as shown in Supplementary Data 1-2).

2) Chain Assembly: A polymer chain was assembled from its repeat units. Considering the chiral spiro-carbons in PIM-1 and SPVEK, an equal number of dextral and sinistral units were included in each chain.

3) Bulk Construction: A polymer bulk was constructed with an initial density of 0.6 g cm-3 using several chains. Details regarding the number of repeat units per chain and the number of chains per bulk are provided in Supplementary Table 4. The polymer model was then built through a 21-step annealing process, as shown in Supplementary Table 3, Supplementary Data 3-6.

4) Membrane Formation: The polymer model was compressed and relaxed into a 2D thin membrane using a 7-step scheme (Supplementary Table 7). Organic molecules were subsequently added to both sides of the 2D thin membrane to form the initial membrane structure.

5) Swelling Process: The initial membranes underwent a swelling process, executed via an 8-step MD simulation protocol (Supplementary Table 8), to prepare them for subsequent simulations. The membrane process simulation for the swollen membranes was simulated by using non-equilibrium molecular dynamics (NEMD), as shown in Supplementary Data 7-8. Initially, energy minimization was performed using the steepest descent algorithm. Subsequently, a constant force was applied to graphene pistons to maintain a constant pressure differential across the membrane. Specifically, the high pressures on the feed side were set at and 100.1 MPa, while the low pressure on the permeate side was maintained at 0.1 MPa. To prevent membrane drifting, ten atoms of SPVEK were randomly selected and fixed. Periodic boundary conditions were enforced along the x- and y-directions throughout the simulation.

Lennard-Jones (LJ) interactions were calculated using a cut-off method within a 1.0 nm radius, while electrostatic interactions were computed using the Particle-Mesh Ewald (PME) method with a real-space cutoff of 1.0 nm. Temperature control was maintained using the v-rescale thermostat, and pressure was regulated by the Parrinello-Rahman barostat. The simulations utilized a time step of 2 fs, with atomic trajectories exported every 100 ns. Constraints were applied to hydrogen atom bonds and C ≡ N bonds to maintain structural rigidity during the simulations. All molecular dynamics simulations were conducted using the GROMACS (v2022.5) package40, with all models independently constructed three times to ensure the reliability and accuracy of the results.

The characterization of the polymer model obtained through the 21-step annealing process was conducted using the Zeo + + package50 based on Voronoi decomposition. The accessible and non-accessible surface areas within the structures were calculated using probe diameters of 2 Å, 2.2 Å, 3.30 Å (kinetic diameter of CO2), and 3.64 Å (kinetic diameter of N2). The calculations were performed with the high accuracy flag enabled. Surface areas were determined using 5000 samples, while pore size distributions were calculated using 50,000 samples per model. For the analysis of pore size distribution, 50,000 samples were used from the models. Pore surfaces for accessible and inaccessible areas were calculated using 800-1000 samples from the models and visualized with the assistance of Visual Molecular Dynamics (VMD, version 1.9.351). Simulated scattering vectors q for SPVEK and PIM-1 were calculated using ISAACS52 with structural data from the 21-step annealing model.

Density distributions in the x and y directions were analyzed throughout the NEMD simulations using the GROMACS package40, resulting in the formation of 2D number-density maps. The molecular coordination number was determined by counting the number of molecules within a 0.5 nm radius around each molecule over a 2.5 ns period during the NEMD simulation. Radial distribution functions for ethanol and cyclohexane were analyzed using the GROMACS package40, with SPVEK as the reference throughout the NEMD simulation.

Using the GROMACS package40, the mean squared displacement (MSD) of molecules over 500 ns was calculated, and the diffusion coefficient was derived using the Einstein relation. A repeat unit of the polymer was simulated. After energy minimization using the steepest descent method, an NVT ensemble simulation was performed at 300 K for 100 ns, with data sampled every 1 ps. We statistically analyzed the distance (d) between two benzene ring carbon atoms and the angle (θ) between their connecting vector and the C–C bond of the benzene ring, yielding the kernel density distributions for both parameters.

Ethanol and cyclohexane mixtures at varying mass ratios were introduced on one side of the 2D thin film, which was compressed and relaxed using the 7-step scheme described previously, as shown in Supplementary Data 9–18. Periodic boundary conditions were applied in the XY directions. Following energy minimization, an NPT ensemble simulation was performed at 1 bar and 300 K for 100 ps. Subsequently, an NVT ensemble simulation was conducted at 300 K for 10 ns, with data output every 20 ps. The number of ethanol and cyclohexane molecules within 1 nm of the polymer surface was quantified during the final 100 ps of the NVT trajectory.

The accurate interaction energies between molecules (ethanol and cyclohexane) and polymer chain segments (two repeating units of SPVEK) were calculated by the standard counterpoise-corrected method using the Gaussian16 package49. Firstly, perform geometry optimization on the polymer chain segment. Subsequently, fix the atomic positions of the polymer and place ethanol or cyclohexane molecules in proximity to the polymer segment. Conduct energy minimization for this system at the B3LYP/6-31 + G(d,p) level. Finally, the Basis Set Superposition Error (BSSE) correction method was employed to calculate accurate interaction energies from the optimized structure at the B3LYP/6-311 + + G(d,p) level, as shown in Supplementary Data 19, 20. Based on the computational data obtained from the above procedures, the Non-Covalent Interaction (NCI) map was subsequently generated53.

The kinetic dimensions of ethanol and cyclohexane were calculated based on iso-electronic density surfaces54. The molecular structures were first optimized at the B3LYP/6-311 G(d,p) level of theory using the Gaussian software. Subsequently, the iso-electronic density surfaces were generated in Multiwfn48 using an isovalue of 0.0015 a.u.
