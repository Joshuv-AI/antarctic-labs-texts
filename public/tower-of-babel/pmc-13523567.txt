Hybrid pentaspline pulsed-field and radiofrequency ablation for left atrial mitral isthmus block: Acute and long-term outcomes

The mitral isthmus (MI) represents an anatomically challenging region characterized by complex architecture and a significant epicardial component, often precluding durable transmural lesions. While achieving transmurality was formidable with radiofrequency (RF) energy, pulsed-field ablation (PFA) offers a promising alternative for this technically demanding substrate. Linear MI ablation is indicated for treating macroreentrant atrial tachycardias (ATs) and serves as an adjunctive strategy in persistent atrial fibrillation (AF). In this study, we report our experience with a hybrid MI ablation technique, combining RF energy via an irrigated-tip catheter with the use of the pentaspline PFA catheter.

The study cohort consisted of 77 patients (mean age 69.9 ± 9 years; 55.8% male) undergoing ablation for persistent AF or AT. The research reported in this article adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki, and the study protocol was approved by the authors’ institutional review board; all patients provided written informed consent. The objective was to evaluate a combined PFA and conventional RF approach for MI ablation. In all cases, 3-dimensional electroanatomic mapping was performed using the EnSite system (Abbott, Abbott Park, IL). Pulmonary vein isolation and posterior wall ablation were achieved using the pentaspline FARAWAVE PFA catheter (Boston Scientific, Marlborough, MA); in redo cases, PFA and/or RF energy was used to supplement prior lesion sets or perform further ablation as appropriate.

The primary MI ablation strategy involved initial endocardial RF delivery (TactiCath, Abbott) supported by a steerable sheath (FARADRIVE, Boston Scientific). RF lesions were applied point by point (35–45 W) with contact force >15 g, ensuring contiguity and targeting potential elimination across the line. If conduction persisted, epicardial RF was performed within the coronary sinus (CS) at the corresponding endocardial site. During CS ablation, power was limited to 25–35 W (contact force <20 g), with the catheter tip oriented atrially to ensure clear atrial electrogram recording. Residual conduction was addressed using the PFA catheter in “flower” configuration to achieve block. Stable contact was confirmed via 3-dimensional mapping and intracardiac echocardiography. PFA was also selectively applied to consolidate the line even if bidirectional block had already been attained via RF, especially in cases where achieving block required challenging or prolonged RF delivery. Nitrates were administered at the operator’s discretion before PFA application at the MI.

While the posterior MI was the standard target, an anterior line was preferred in cases of extensive anterior atrial fibrosis or significant conduction delay toward the left atrial appendage. Notably, an initial stand-alone PFA strategy was abandoned after the first 5 cases, as repeated acute conduction recovery was observed in the last 2. In these instances, supplementary RF applications were required to achieve block, prompting a shift to the dual-energy approach described above. The procedural end point was bidirectional block, verified at least 20 minutes postablation through differential pacing and high-density mapping along the line to identify residual conduction gaps.

MI ablation was performed for substrate modification in 20 patients as part of a rhythm control strategy for persistent AF, while the remaining 57 patients underwent ablation for AT management. Among the latter group, 30 patients presented with perimitral atrial flutter and 27 underwent MI ablation as a prophylactic measure against intraprocedurally identified reentrant ATs. Acute success was achieved in 100% of all attempted MI lines (65 posterior, 8 anterior, and 4 both). Overall, 75.3% of lines involved a hybrid approach, with 20% (15 of 75 MI lines) requiring PFA to reach final block after initial RF failure. At a mean follow-up of 19.5 ± 5.9 months, 71.4% of patients remained in sinus rhythm after the index procedure, increasing to 80.5% after the final intervention. Repeat procedures were performed in 11 patients (14.3%), including 8 for ablation due to AF/AT recurrence and 3 during left atrial appendage closure, where MI lines were assessed without further ablation. Redo findings demonstrated high lesion durability, with conduction recovery in only 2 of 13 evaluated lines (1 anterior and 1 posterior). Historically, achieving permanent bidirectional block at the MI using RF alone has been challenging because of suboptimal lesion durability. Factors limiting RF efficacy include the complex anatomy, the heat-sink effect of the adjacent CS, and the requirement for deep lesions. While PFA has emerged as a promising alternative, recent clinical data suggest that using the pentaspline PFA catheter as a stand-alone tool for MI ablation yields poor long-term durability.1 In our study, a strategic combination of both energy sources in the majority of our patients resulted in a 100% acute success rate and remarkable lesion durability. The pentaspline catheter is not primarily designed for linear lesion sets; however, even commercially available large-footprint PFA catheters often struggle with the demanding anatomy of the posterior MI, potentially because of an inability to adequately target its epicardial component.2 In our cohort, epicardial RF ablation within the CS was required in 46.4% of patients and was likely decisive for achieving complete block. Notably, this dual-energy approach addresses the vein of Marshall region for both AF substrate modification and MI block, potentially offering a safer, less demanding alternative to technically complex ethanol infusion.3 Furthermore, the synergy of dual-energy sources may offer superior outcomes, as suggested by recent experimental data.4 We hypothesize that initial RF delivery facilitates subsequent PFA efficacy by modifying tissue impedance or creating a localized substrate that renders residual conductive fibers more susceptible to electroporation. Supporting this, recent clinical evidence on anterior MI ablation shows that individual use of conventional RF or stand-alone pentaspline PFA leads to limited efficacy and suboptimal durability.5

In conclusion, integrating PFA via a pentaspline catheter with conventional RF ablation serves as a potent tool for consolidating permanent MI lesions. Despite the lack of a control group, these findings suggest that future dual-energy or advanced PFA designs may further enhance success in challenging anatomical regions such as the MI.
