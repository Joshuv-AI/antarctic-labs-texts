Introduction

Turán-type problems are some of the oldest and most fundamental in Combinatorics. They ask how dense must a graph be in order to force the presence of a particular substructure. The first results were those of Mantel
[
Man07
]
and Turán
[
Tur41
]
, who determined the maximum number of edges in an
n
-vertex graph without a triangle or complete graph of size
r
, respectively. In 1946, Erdős and Stone
[
ES46
]
generalised Turán’s theorem by showing that any
n
-vertex graph with at least
(1-\frac{1}{r-1}+o(1))\binom{n}{2}
edges contains a complete
r
-partite graph with
t
vertices in each part, denoted
K_{r}(t)
. The complete multipartite graphs are particularly important since the number of edges required to force the presence of these determines, up to lower order terms, the number of edges required to force the presence of any non-bipartite graph
[
ES66
]
.

An old class of Turán-type problems that are still not well-understood asks for density conditions which guarantee a
K_{r}(t)
inside a host graph that is itself
k
-partite (for some
k\geqslant r
). Questions of this type are often remarkably difficult, even for small values of
r
,
t
, and
k
. For instance, the notorious Zarankiewicz problem
[
Zar51
]
from 1951 asks for the maximum number of edges,
z(n;t)
, in a bipartite graph with parts of size
n
that does not contain
K_{t,t}=K_{2}(t)
. The celebrated Kővári-Sós-Turán theorem
[
KST54
]
states that
z(n;t)=\mathcal{O}(n^{2-1/t})
. Though this bound is believed to be tight for all
t\geqslant 2
, no matching lower bound is known for
t\geqslant 4
despite receiving considerable attention – we direct the reader to the comprehensive survey of Furëdi and Simonovits
[
FS13
]
.

For
k
-partite host graphs with
k\geqslant 3
, even less is known. Interest in these problems can be traced back to the seminal 1975 paper of Bollobás, Erdős, and Szemerédi
[
BES75
]
. They initiated the extensive study of multipartite Turán-type problems
[
Jin92
,
ST06
,
HS06
,
LTZ22
]
which has been central to Combinatorics with applications in graph arboricity
[
Alo88
]
, list colouring
[
Hax01
]
, defective colouring
[
HST03
]
, and strong chromatic numbers
[
Hax04
]
.

One of their main questions, which has so far remained elusive, concerned the first case,
k=r=3
. That is, the following tripartite version of the Zarankiewicz problem.

Problem 1.1

(Bollobás-Erdős-Szemerédi)
.

For each
t
, what is the smallest
\tau=\tau(n)
such that any
n\times n\times n
tripartite graph with minimum degree at least

In the above, an

tripartite graph
is a tripartite graph whose parts have size

is that there are

tripartite graphs with minimum degree

which are bipartite, and thus contain no triangles (consider the graph

with parts

,

, and

that contains all edges between

and

Extremal examples

In this section we describe an infinite family of extremal examples which show that
Theorem

1.2
is best possible, up to the value of the constant
K
, assuming the widely-believed conjecture that the Zarankiewicz numbers satisfy
z(n;t)=\Omega(n^{2-1/t})
.

Theorem 2.1
.

Suppose there are positive constants
c
and
C
such that
cn^{2-1/t}\leqslant z(n;t)\leqslant Cn^{2-1/t}
holds for all
n
. For every integer
k\geqslant\frac{2C}{3c}
and for every
n
that is a sufficiently large multiple of
3k
, there is a
K_{t,t,t}
-free
n\times n\times n
tripartite graph
G_{k,n}
with minimum degree
n+(c/4)\cdot n^{1-1/t}
.

Further, for
k\neq k^{\prime}
, at least
\Omega(n^{2})
edges of
G_{k,n}
need to be deleted to obtain a subgraph of
G_{k^{\prime},n}
.

is already known – this is the classical Kővári-Sós-Turán theorem
[
KST54
]
. They believed
[
KST54
, §6]
that

and so the hypothesis of
Theorem

2.1
holds. Many researchers also conjecture this to be the case; see, for example,
[
Für91
, p. 257]
,
[
Bol04
, §6, Conj. 15]
, and
[
FS13
, Conj. 2.24]
. The conjecture is known to hold for

[

]
and

.

In our pursuit of

, we will start with the dense triangle-free

tripartite graphs given by the following lemma.

Lemma 2.2
.

For all positive integers

with

satisfying the following properties

there are sets

and

such that

1.

\lvert U_{1}\rvert=\lvert U_{2}\rvert=\sigma

2.

every vertex

3.

Further, for
k\neq k^{\prime}

\Omega(n^{2})

We will prove

in

2.1

Note that every vertex of

Lemma 2.3
.

If there are positive constants

such that

Proof.

Let

\mathbb{Z}/(3k-1)\mathbb{Z}

\Gamma_{1}=K_{2}

The graph
\Gamma_{k+1}
has the following properties.

•

It is edge-maximal triangle-free: it is triangle-free and the addition of any edge results in a triangle.

•

It is

•

It is 3-partite with one possible partition being
\{0,1,\dotsc,k\}
,
\{k+1,k+2,\dotsc,2k+1\}
, and
\{2k+2,2k+3,\dotsc,3k+1\}

Notice that the first two parts of the partition given above contain

k

In order to obtain a balanced tripartite graph we will need to weight the vertices of
\Gamma_{k+1}
so that each part has an equal weight. To be precise, given a graph
G
and a
weighting

\omega\colon V(G)\to\mathbb{Z}^{+}
, the
weighted graph

(G,\omega)
is the graph obtained by replacing vertex
v
of
G
by an independent set
I_{v}
of size
\omega(v)
and each edge
uv
by a complete bipartite graph between classes
I_{u}
and
I_{v}
. Crucially, if
G
is triangle-free, then the graph
(G,\omega)
will be too. We will need the following lemma about weighted graph for our claim about the
G^{\prime}_{k,n}
being
\Omega(n^{2})

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
G
be an edge-maximal triangle-free graph in which all neighbourhoods are distinct and
H
be a triangle-free graph. Suppose that
G
is a subgraph of some weighted graph
(H,\omega)
. Then
G
is a subgraph of
H
.

Proof.

A
homomorphism
from a graph
G_{1}
to a graph
G_{2}
is a function
\phi\colon G_{1}\to G_{2}
such that, for every edge
uv\in G_{1}
, we have
\phi(u)\phi(v)\in G_{2}
(that is,
\phi
is edge-preserving). If
G
is a subgraph of
(H,\omega)
, then there is a homomorphism from
G
to
(H,\omega)
(the function mapping
G
to its embedding in
(H,\omega)
). Projecting this function down from
(H,\omega)
to
H
gives a homomorphism from
G
to
H
– denote this homomorphism by
\phi
.

First suppose that
\phi
is not injective. Then there are vertices
u,v\in V(G)
such that
\phi(u)=\phi(v)
. Since all neighbourhoods of
G
are distinct, we may and will assume there is a vertex
w
of
G
that is adjacent to
u
and not to
v
. Let
G^{\prime}=G+vw
. Now
\phi(v)\phi(w)=\phi(u)\phi(w)
and
u
is adjacent to
w
so, in fact,
\phi
is a homomorphism from
G^{\prime}
to
H
. But
G
is an edge-maximal triangle-free graph and so
G^{\prime}
contains a triangle. This triangle will map, under
\phi
, to a triangle in
H
. This contradicts the triangle-freeness of
H
. Thus
\phi
is injective. But then
\phi
is an embedding of
G
into
H
and so
G
is a subgraph of
H
.

\Gamma_{k+1}
. The graph
\Gamma_{k+1}
is an edge-maximal triangle-free graph in which all neighbourhoods are distinct. The graph
\Gamma_{k^{\prime}+1}
is triangle-free and
G^{\prime}_{k^{\prime},n}
is a weighted version of
\Gamma_{k^{\prime}+1}
. Since
k>k^{\prime}
,
\Gamma_{k+1}
is not a subgraph of
\Gamma_{k^{\prime}+1}
and so
Lemma

2.4
implies that
G^{\prime}_{k^{\prime},n}
does not contain
\Gamma_{k+1}
.

On the other hand, the graph
G^{\prime}_{k,n}
is a weighted version of
\Gamma_{k+1}
. A
canonical copy
of
\Gamma_{k+1}
in
G^{\prime}_{k,n}
is a copy of
\Gamma_{k+1}
in which each vertex is in the corresponding part of
G^{\prime}_{k,n}
. The number of such canonical copies is
(3\sigma)^{k}\cdot(3\sigma)^{k-1}(2\sigma)\sigma\cdot(3\sigma)^{k-1}(2\sigma)\sigma=3^{3k-2}\cdot 2^{2}\cdot\sigma^{3k+2}
. Each edge of
G^{\prime}_{k,n}
is in at most
(3\sigma)^{k}\cdot(3\sigma)^{k-1}(2\sigma)\cdot(3\sigma)^{k-1}(2\sigma)=3^{3k-2}\cdot 2^{2}\cdot\sigma^{3k}
canonical copies of
\Gamma_{k+1}
. Thus, to remove all copies of
\Gamma_{k+1}
from
G^{\prime}_{k,n}
(which is necessary to obtain a subgraph of
G^{\prime}_{k^{\prime},n}
) requires the deletion of at least
\sigma^{2}=\Omega(n^{2})
edges, as required.
∎

3
Counting tools

In this section we collect together some counting lemmas that guarantee the presence of
K_{t,t}
and
K_{t,t,t}
. Throughout the paper we use the following standard notation.
N_{G}(v_{1},\dots,v_{t})
denotes the common neighbourhood of a collection of vertices
v_{1},\dots,v_{t}
in graph
G
. For a pair of vertices
a
and
b
,
\deg_{G}(a,b)

\coloneqq\lvert N_{G}(a,b)\rvert
denotes the number of common neighbours of
a
and
b
. For a pair of disjoint sets

,

\coloneqq\lvert E(G[A,B])\rvert
denotes the number of edges of
G
with one end-vertex in
A
and the other in
B
. Finally, for a vertex
a
and vertex-set
B
,
e_{G}(a,B)

\coloneqq e_{G}(\{a\},B)
denotes the number of neighbours of
a
in
B
. We omit the subscript if
G
is clear from context.

We will frequently use the unbalanced version of the Kővári-Sós-Turán theorem
[
KST54
]
.

Theorem 3.1
.

For every positive integer
t
there is a constant
K
such that any
m\times n
bipartite graph with at least
K(mn^{1-1/t}+n)
edges contains
K_{t,t}
. In particular, if
m\geqslant n^{1/t}
, then
2Kmn^{1-1/t}
edges suffice to guarantee a
K_{t,t}
.

The following averaging argument converts many copies of
K_{1,1,t}
into a copy of
K_{t,t,t}
. Here and throughout the paper, when we write “there are at least
N

K_{1,1,t}
s in

” we mean there are at least
N
copies of
K_{1,1,t}
with one vertex in
A
, one in
B
, and
t
in
C
.

Lemma 3.2
.

For every positive integer
t
there is a constant
K\geqslant 1
such that if
G=(A,B,C;E)
is a tripartite graph with
\lvert A\rvert\geqslant\lvert B\rvert^{1/t}
and there are at least
K\cdot\lvert A\rvert\cdot\lvert B\rvert^{1-1/t}\cdot\lvert C\rvert^{t}

K_{1,1,t}
s in
A\times B\times C
, then

contains
K_{t,t,t}
.

Proof.

By averaging over
t
-tuples of distinct vertices of

there exist
c_{1},\dotsc,c_{t}\in C
such that
G[N(c_{1},\dotsc,c_{t})]
contains at least
K\cdot t!\cdot\lvert A\rvert\cdot\lvert B\rvert^{1-1/t}
edges. By
Theorem

3.1
, provided
K
is sufficiently large in terms of

, the edges of
G[N(c_{1},\dotsc,c_{t})]
contain a copy of

. Together with
c_{1},\dotsc,c_{t}
this gives a copy of
K_{t,t,t}
in

.
∎

We will frequently apply
Lemma

3.2
in the case where every edge between
A
and
B
has a linear-sized common neighbourhood in

. We package this into the following corollary.

Corollary 3.3
.

For every positive integer
t
and constant
\lambda>0
there is a constant
K
such that the following holds. Let
G=(A,B,C;E)
be a tripartite graph where

•

\deg(a,b)\geqslant\max\{\lambda\lvert C\rvert,t\}
for every edge
ab\in E(G[A,B])
,

•

\lvert A\rvert\geqslant\lvert B\rvert^{1/t}

•

e(G[A,B])\geqslant K\cdot\lvert A\rvert\cdot\lvert B\rvert^{1-1/t}
.

Then
G
contains a
K_{t,t,t}
.

Proof.

The number of
K_{1,1,t}
s in
A\times B\times C
is

\sum_{ab\in E(G[A,B])}\binom{\deg(a,b)}{t}\geqslant e(G[A,B])\cdot\binom{\max\{\lambda\lvert C\rvert,t\}}{t}\geqslant e(G[A,B])\cdot(\lambda/t)^{t}\cdot\lvert C\rvert^{t},

where the last inequality is immediate if
t\geqslant\lambda\lvert C\rvert
and follows from
\binom{x}{t}\geqslant(x/t)^{t}
for all
x\geqslant t
otherwise. The result follows from
Lemma

3.2
.
∎

We finish with a version of the dependent random choice lemma.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
G=(A,B;E)
be a bipartite graph with edge density
\lambda
. If
\lambda\lvert A\rvert\geqslant t
, then there are distinct vertices
a_{1},\dotsc,a_{t}\in A
and
B^{\prime}\subseteq N(a_{1},\dotsc,a_{t})
such that

•

\lvert B^{\prime}\rvert\geqslant\frac{1}{2}(\lambda/e)^{t}\lvert B\rvert
and

•

every vertex in
B^{\prime}
has at least
\frac{1}{2}\lambda\lvert A\rvert
neighbours in
A
.

Proof.

Call a vertex
b\in B

bad
if it has fewer than
\frac{1}{2}\lambda\lvert A\rvert
neighbours in
A
. Pick
t
distinct vertices
a_{1},\dotsc,a_{t}\in A
uniformly at random. Let
X
be the size of
N(a_{1},\dotsc,a_{t})
and
Y
be the number of bad vertices in
N(a_{1},\dotsc,a_{t})
. Using the convexity of
x\mapsto\binom{x}{t}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(X)

\displaystyle=\sum_{b\in B}\mathbb{P}(a_{1},\dotsc,a_{t}\in N(b))=\sum_{b\in B}\binom{\deg(b)}{t}\binom{\lvert A\rvert}{t}^{-1}

\displaystyle\geqslant\binom{\lvert A\rvert}{t}^{-1}\lvert B\rvert\cdot\binom{\lvert B\rvert^{-1}\sum_{b\in B}\deg(b)}{t}=\binom{\lvert A\rvert}{t}^{-1}\lvert B\rvert\binom{\lambda\lvert A\rvert}{t}.

On the other hand,

\mathbb{E}(Y)=\sum_{\text{bad }b}\binom{\deg(b)}{t}\binom{\lvert A\rvert}{t}^{-1}\leqslant\lvert B\rvert\binom{\frac{1}{2}\lambda\lvert A\rvert}{t}\binom{\lvert A\rvert}{t}^{-1},

and so, since
\binom{\frac{1}{2}\lambda\lvert A\rvert}{t}\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\binom{\lambda\lvert A\rvert}{t}
,

\mathbb{E}(X-Y)\geqslant\lvert B\rvert\binom{\lvert A\rvert}{t}^{-1}\biggl[\binom{\lambda\lvert A\rvert}{t}-\binom{\frac{1}{2}\lambda\lvert A\rvert}{t}\biggr]\geqslant\frac{\lvert B\rvert}{2}\binom{\lambda\lvert A\rvert}{t}\binom{\lvert A\rvert}{t}^{-1}\geqslant\frac{\lvert B\rvert}{2}\biggl(\frac{\lambda}{e}\biggr)^{t},

where the last inequality used the bounds
(x/t)^{t}\leqslant\binom{x}{t}\leqslant(ex/t)^{t}
for all
x\geqslant t
.
Pick distinct
a_{1},\dotsc,a_{t}\in A
for which
X-Y\geqslant\frac{1}{2}(\lambda/e)^{t}\lvert B\rvert
. Then take
B^{\prime}
to be the set of good vertices in
N(a_{1},\dotsc,a_{t})
. This has size
X-Y
, as required.
∎

4
Degrees, boosters, and some structural lemmas

We now come to the proof of
Theorem

1.2
. Recall that we start with an
n\times n\times n
tripartite graph
G
which has minimum degree at least
n+\tau
where
\tau=Kn^{1-1/t}
for some large constant
K
. We label the three parts of
G
as
V_{1},V_{2},V_{3}
. This invokes a natural direction of edges in
G
: for
u\in V_{i}
,
v\in V_{i+1}
(such indices will always be modulo 3) we say that
uv\in E(G)
is a
forward edge
while
vu\in E(G)
is a
backward edge
.

The
forward neighbourhood
of a vertex
v
, denoted
N^{+}(v)
, is the set of
u
such that
vu
is a forward edge. Note that if
u\in V_{i}
, then
N^{+}(v)\subseteq V_{i+1}
. The size of
N^{+}(v)
is the
forward degree
of
v
and is denoted
\deg^{+}(v)
. The
backward neighbourhood
and
backward degree
are defined similarly. Note that
\deg(v)=\deg^{+}(v)+\deg^{-}(v)
for each vertex
v
.

We now define certain edges that are particularly important in the proof as they are useful for creating
K_{t,t,t}
s.

Definition 4.1

(boosters)
.

Let
e
be an edge. Let the end-vertices of
e
be
u
and
v
where
uv
is in the forward direction.

•

e
is a
booster
if
\deg^{+}(v)\geqslant\deg^{+}(u)
,

•

e
is an
r
-booster
if
\deg^{+}(v)\geqslant\deg^{+}(u)+r
.

Note that boosters are exactly 0-boosters. Boosters are very useful as they give a lot of information about the neighbourhoods of their end-points.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
G
be an
n\times n\times n
tripartite graph with
\delta(G)\geqslant n+\tau
. Let
uv
be an
r
-booster and
V_{i}
be the part containing neither
u
nor
v
. Then

•

\deg(u,v)\geqslant\tau+r
,

•

\lvert V_{i}\setminus(N(u)\cup N(v))\rvert\leqslant\deg(u,v)-\tau-r
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality
u\in V_{i+1}
and
v\in V_{i+2}
(indices modulo 3) and so
\deg^{+}(v)\geqslant\deg^{+}(u)+r
.
The inclusion-exclusion principle gives

\displaystyle\deg(u,v)

\displaystyle=\lvert N(u,v)\cap V_{i}\rvert\geqslant\lvert N(u)\cap V_{i}\rvert+\lvert N(v)\cap V_{i}\rvert-n

\displaystyle=\deg^{+}(v)+\deg^{-}(u)-n

\displaystyle=(\deg^{+}(v)-\deg^{+}(u))+(\deg(u)-n)

\displaystyle\geqslant r+\tau,

and

\displaystyle\lvert V_{i}\setminus(N(u)\cup N(v))\rvert

\displaystyle=n-\lvert V_{i}\cap(N(u)\cup N(v))\rvert

\displaystyle=n-\deg^{-}(u)-\deg^{+}(v)+\deg(u,v)

\displaystyle=(n-\deg(u))+(\deg^{+}(u)-\deg^{+}(v))+\deg(u,v)

\displaystyle\leqslant-\tau-r+\deg(u,v).\qed

In this language it is easy to give a proof of Graver’s result that
\tau=1
suffices to guarantee the presence of a triangle. Let
u
be a vertex of
G
with
\deg^{+}(u)
minimal. Since
\delta(G)\geqslant n+1
,
u
has at least one forward neighbour
v
. Then, by minimality of
\deg^{+}(u)
, the edge
uv
is a booster and so, by
Lemma

4.2
,
\deg(u,v)\geqslant\tau=1
which gives a triangle in
G
.

The following is a useful structural lemma that we shall frequently apply. For the reader it may be helpful to think of
H
as being

– there is only one instance where we need to apply the lemma to a strict subgraph of

.

Lemma 4.3
.

For every positive integer
t
and constant
\lambda>0
there is a constant
K
such that the following holds. Let
G
be an
n\times n\times n
tripartite graph with parts
V_{1},V_{2},V_{3}
and
\delta(G)\geqslant n+\tau
. Let
A\subseteq V_{1}
,
B\subseteq V_{2}
,

, and

be such that the density of

is at least
\lambda
and

. Then either

or there exist

Applying

Let

-heavy, every vertex of
B^{\ast}
has at least
2tr
neighbours in
V_{3}
. Furthermore,
\deg^{-}(a_{i})=\deg(a_{i})-\deg^{+}(a_{i})\geqslant n-r
and so each
a_{i}
has at most
r
non-neighbours in
V_{3}
. Thus each vertex of
B^{\ast}
has at least

\max\{\lvert B_{m}\rvert/(3t^{2}2^{6t}),2tr\}-tr\geqslant\tfrac{1}{2}\max\{\lvert B_{m}\rvert/(3t^{2}2^{6t}),2tr\}\geqslant\lvert B_{m}\rvert/(6t^{2}2^{6t})

neighbours in
N(a_{1},\dotsc,a_{t})
. Thus the number of edges between
B^{\ast}
and
N(a_{1},\dotsc,a_{t})\cap V_{3}
is at least

\displaystyle\lvert B^{\ast}\rvert\cdot\lvert B_{m}\rvert/(6t^{2}2^{6t})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:sizeofBm}}}{{\geqslant}}\frac{\lvert B^{\ast}\rvert\cdot k}{2000\cdot 6t^{2}2^{6t}}+\frac{\lvert B^{\ast}\rvert\cdot\lvert B_{m}\rvert}{6t^{2}2^{6t+1}}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:sizeofBstar}}}{{\geqslant}}\frac{K}{10^{10t}}\Bigl(\lvert B^{\ast}\rvert\cdot n^{1-1/t}+n^{t-1}\cdot\lvert B^{\prime\prime}_{m}\rvert^{2-t}\Bigr)

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{K}{10^{10t}}(\lvert B^{\ast}\rvert\cdot n^{1-1/t}+n),

where the second inequality used
200000\cdot 6t^{2}2^{6t}\leqslant 10^{10t}
and the final inequality used
\lvert B^{\prime\prime}_{m}\rvert\leqslant n
. Thus, provided
K
is large enough,
Theorem

3.1
guarantees that there is a
K_{t,t}
in
G[B^{\ast},N(a_{1},\dotsc,a_{t})\cap V_{3}]
. Together with
a_{1},\dotsc,a_{t}
this gives a
K_{t,t,t}
in
G
.
∎

We are now ready to conclude the proof of our main result,
Theorem

1.2
. Let
G
be an
n\times n\times n
tripartite graph with minimum degree
n+Kn^{1-1/t}
.
Lemma

5.1
guarantees there is an integer
k\geqslant K/100\cdot n^{1-1/t}
such that
G
contains either a bipartite subgraph of booster edges with parts of size at least
k/200
and density at least
1/200
or
k/12
vertices in one part each having forward degree at most
100k
and each being incident to at least
k/200
forward edges that are
k/100
-heavy. If the former occurs, then
Lemma

5.2
implies that, provided
K
is large enough,
G
contains
K_{t,t,t}
. If the latter occurs, then
Lemma

5.5
implies that, provided
K
is large enough,
G
contains
K_{t,t,t}
. In either case
G
contains
K_{t,t,t}
, as required.

6
Concluding remarks

While
Theorem

1.2
provides a satisfactory answer to Bollobás, Erdős, and Szemerédi’s problem,
Problem

1.1
, one could ask an even more general question.

Problem 6.1
.

For each tripartite graph
H
, what is the smallest
\tau=\tau(n)
such than any
n\times n\times n
tripartite graph with minimum degree at least
n+\tau
contains
H
?

For arbitrary
H
this may be very hard indeed given our limited understanding of bipartite Turán numbers. One case that may be more approachable is
H=K_{t,s,r}
for
t\leqslant s\leqslant r
.

Conjecture 6.2
.

For all positive integers
t\leqslant s\leqslant r
, there is a constant
K
such that, if
G
is an
n\times n\times n
tripartite graph and
\delta(G)\geqslant n+Kn^{1-1/t}
, then
G
contains
K_{t,s,r}
.

If true, this would be best possible up to the constant
K
, owing to the extremal examples of
Section

2
. Much of our argument still works here (note that the Kővári-Sós-Turán theorem,
Theorem

3.1
, actually gives a
K_{t,s}
provided
K
is large enough), but when
s
is much bigger than
t
case (ii) of
Lemma

5.1
does not provide a high enough density of heavy edges to proceed.

Another possible direction would be to consider an
r
-partite version for
r\geqslant 4
. Bollobás, Erdős, and Szemerédi
[
BES75
]
asked what minimum degree
c_{r}n+1
guarantees the presence of
K_{r}
in an
n\times\dotsb\times n

r
-partite graph – that is, in an
r
-partite graph where all parts have size
n
. This spurred on a line of research
[
Alo88
,
Jin92
]
with the solution for even
r
given by Szabó and Tardos
[
ST06
]
and for odd
r
by Haxell and Szabó
[
HS06
]
. It is natural to ask the following.

Problem 6.3
.

For integers
r\geqslant 4
and
t\geqslant 2
, what is the smallest
\tau=\tau(n)
such that every
n\times\dotsb\times n

r
-partite graph with minimum degree at least
c_{r}n+\tau
contains
K_{r}(t)
?
