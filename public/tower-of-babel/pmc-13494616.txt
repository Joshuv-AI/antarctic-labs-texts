Associations between social and economic determinants of health and receiving infertility care among female Veterans: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Zoe H Pleasure, Michelle A Mengeling, Ginny L Ryan, AM Racila, Samantha K Benson, Siobhan S Mahorter, Anne G Sadler, Judy E Stern, Mark Vander Weg, Lisa S Callegari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo examine the associations between social and economic determinants of health with receipt of infertility diagnoses and care among Veterans. In the non-Veteran population, socially and economically marginalized individuals are less likely to receive infertility care. Little is known about these associations in the population of United States military Veterans, who may have unique fertility care needs and healthcare access experiences.DesignWe analyzed cross-sectional survey data collected from the Impact of Sexual Assault and Combat-Related Trauma on Fertility in Veterans study conducted from 2016 to 2020.SubjectsOur analytic sample includes the subset of Veterans aged 20–45 years who were assigned female sex at birth and experienced infertility, defined as a self-reported history of unprotected sex for 12 or more months without pregnancy.ExposureVariables of interest included social and economic determinants, including income, Veterans Health Administration (VA) benefits and insurance status, marital status, race and ethnicity, and sexual orientation.Main Outcome MeasuresOutcomes included self-reported receipt of an infertility diagnosis and fertility treatment. Associations between social and economic determinants and the outcomes were evaluated using multivariable logistic regression. Age and lifetime diagnoses of mental health conditions were included as potential confounders of these associations.ResultsAmong the 589 female Veterans with self-reported infertility, 22.8% reported receiving an infertility diagnosis and 19.5% reported receiving fertility treatment. Having a household income of greater than $110,000 (odds ratio [OR] = 5.33, 95% confidence interval [CI], 2.06–13.77) was positively associated with receipt of an infertility diagnosis in adjusted models. Being married or currently living with a partner (OR = 2.32, 95% CI, 1.22–4.41) and having a household income of greater than $110,000 (OR = 3.89, 95% CI, 1.38–10.98) were positively associated with receiving fertility treatment in adjusted models. Neither race nor VA benefits and insurance status were associated with either outcome.ConclusionSimilar to the non-Veteran population, income remains an important economic determinant of care receipt among Veterans. Our finding that only a minority of those with self-reported infertility received a diagnosis or treatment highlights the need for efforts to improve Veterans’ access to fertility care and thus their ability to achieve the families they desire.Key Words: Veteran, infertility diagnosis, income, fertility care, determinants of health

Despite substantial growth in research on Veterans’ reproductive health care over the past several decades (1), infertility care remains understudied in this population. Although infertility affects nearly one-fifth of women in the general US population (2, 3), infertility prevalence may be as high as 40% among female Veterans (4, 5). Female Veterans may need more support with fertility than their non-Veteran counterparts for several reasons, including their higher rates of medical and mental health conditions, as well as possible toxin exposures during their military service that may adversely affect fertility and repeated exposure to sexual violence (4, 6). In addition, the nature of military service often encourages service members to defer childbearing until later in life, when natural fertility rates are lower (7, 8). Veterans are in addition more likely than non-Veterans to identify as sexual and/or gender minorities, meaning many Veterans may in addition require family-building support for reasons beyond traditionally defined infertility (4, 9, 10).

Eligibility for health care in the Veterans Health Administration (VA) is determined by income, service-connected disability, exposure to toxins, location of service in certain combat theaters (e.g., Iraq or Afghanistan), and other criteria (11). Given high rates of socioeconomic, psychosocial, and health vulnerabilities among female Veterans, many are eligible for health benefits, although data indicate that only a minority use VA health care (12). The VA medical benefits cover fertility evaluation, management, and specific infertility treatments, including medications and intrauterine insemination. Other treatments, such as in vitro fertilization (IVF), have historically been available only to those with a service-connected disability affecting fertility and who are legally married to a partner of a different sex (13). This policy was amended in 2024 to include access to IVF for single or unmarried Veterans with qualifying injuries or illnesses, although costs for unmarried partners are not covered. The policy, in addition, expanded access to the use of donor gametes or embryos for IVF for those Veterans who qualify (14, 15, 16).

Veterans may face other barriers to accessing services or receiving an infertility diagnosis beyond coverage. In a prior study based on a national cohort of female Veterans using VA between 2001 and 2010, diagnosis and receipt of services were rare, with under 2% of Veterans having received an infertility diagnosis and only 22% of those diagnosed being assessed or treated for infertility (9). Although several studies have explored the associations between common exposures during service and infertility prevalence (4, 6), less is known about what inequities in access to fertility services exist for Veterans and how social and economic determinants of health (e.g., income, education, experiences of discrimination by race, and sexual orientation) may drive these inequities. Veterans may, in addition, experience challenges in seeking out or receiving fertility-supporting treatment even if not systematically excluded through VA directives, because prior research has documented disparities in access to sexual and reproductive health care among Veterans by race and ethnicity, sexual or gender identity, educational attainment, and income levels (6, 9, 17). Findings from outside the VA suggest that inequities in receipt of infertility services may be due to social and economic determinants of health and structural factors such as racism, sexual orientation, gender discrimination, ableism, and biases regarding individuals’ parenting fitness (18). Because of Veterans’ unique fertility care needs and healthcare access experiences, understanding the extent to which these inequities are also present for Veterans is a critical step in identifying opportunities to improve equitable access to fertility services and support their ability to achieve the families they desire.

The objectives of this study were to calculate the percentage of Veterans who self-reported receiving an infertility diagnosis or fertility treatment among those who met the International Committee for Monitoring Assisted Reproductive Technologies clinical definition of infertility (19), and 2) examine the associations between social and economic determinants of health and receipt of infertility diagnosis and fertility treatment for Veterans.

Methods

Data source

The data for this study were collected in the ‟Impact of Sexual Assault and Combat-Related Trauma on Fertility in Veterans” study, a cross-sectional telephone survey conducted between June 2016 and January 2020. Survey methods have been previously published (4). In brief, a national sampling frame of Veterans aged 20–45 years who had separated from military service within the prior 10 years was constructed using the Veterans Affairs/Department of Defense Identity Repository (VADIR), a data repository containing information of Veterans, servicemembers, and their dependents, regardless of VA enrollment status. From this frame, a simple random sample of 50,100 Veterans, stratified by sex (20,000 female and 30,100 male), was generated such that all eligible Veterans had an equal probability of selection. Of the female Veterans in the sample, 17,546 were mailed recruitment packets, and 1,410 completed the interview (response rate: 8.0%). After the mailing, the study team made up to eight contact attempts by telephone. Informed consent was reviewed verbally before participation, and questions were addressed by trained research staff. Veterans who declined participation were removed from further contact. Data were collected using computer-assisted telephone interviews (CATIs). The University of Iowa Institutional Review Board (#201410760) and the Iowa City Veterans Affairs Research and Development Committee approved this study.

Study sample

The analytic sample included all Veterans assigned female sex at birth who have ever had unprotected sexual intercourse with a sperm-producing partner and who have a history of infertility (Fig. 1). Infertility was defined using the International Committee for Monitoring Assisted Reproductive Technologies definition from 2017, which includes experiencing 12 consecutive months of unprotected sex with a sperm-producing partner without achieving pregnancy, or having a limitation in their capacity to achieve pregnancy with their partner or individually (19). On the basis of our available data, we defined infertility as a self-report of 12 months or greater of unprotected intercourse with a sperm-producing partner without becoming pregnant. We excluded participants who started an interview but did not complete it (n = 140), refused to answer questions about reproductive health and fertility (n = 3), or declined to answer whether they had had unprotected sex (n = 1).

Outcome measures

The primary outcomes were binary variables based on self-report of ever having received an infertility diagnosis and of receiving fertility treatment. Survey respondents were asked, “Have you alone, your partner alone, or you and a partner ever been diagnosed with infertility?” and “Have you or a partner ever been treated for infertility in the past?”

Covariates

The social and economic determinants of interest included self-reported measures of education level (high school or less, some college or technical, and college or higher), current marital status (married/living with partner and single/divorced/widowed), racial identity (Black, White, Multiple, and Other), Hispanic or Latino/a/e ethnicity (Yes and No), sexual orientation (heterosexual or gay, lesbian, and bisexual), current VA benefits and insurance status (see description below), current annual household income ($0–30,000, $31,000–50,000, $51,000–70,000, $71,000–90,000, $91,000–110,000, and $110,000+), and current employment status (employed or not employed by choice, not employed because of other reasons). Respondents were asked if they currently had health insurance that covered all or some of their medical bills and were provided with examples. Those who answered ‟yes” were asked about each type of insurance. We then categorized the current VA benefits and insurance status variable as follows: VA coverage (VA benefits for service-connected disability or indigency) and private insurance (TRICARE, Civilian Health and Medical Program of the Department of Veterans Affairs, and other private plans); VA coverage only or VA plus other public insurance (Medicare, Medicaid, or Medigap); private insurance only or private and public non-VA insurance; and no insurance or public insurance only. This categorization was selected to approximate access to fertility care coverage, with dually enrolled Veterans in VA coverage and private insurance as the most likely to receive coverage, and Veterans with no insurance or public insurance as the least likely to receive coverage for this care.

We, in addition, included variables hypothesized to be potential confounders of the associations between social and economic determinants and infertility diagnosis and fertility treatment. These confounders included age at interview (20–45, 20–29, 30–39, and 40–49), self-report of whether the respondent ever had a live birth or was ever diagnosed with posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD), depression, or anxiety, current potentially hazardous drinking behaviors (a score of 3 or higher on the alcohol use disorders identification test [AUDIT-C]), and lifetime smoking history. We hypothesized that Veterans diagnosed with a mental health condition would likely be more engaged with the health care system than individuals without these conditions and thus more likely to receive an infertility diagnosis and care related to fertility. Additionally, hazardous drinking behaviors and smoking have evidence-supported connections to infertility. Supplemental Table 1 (available online) contains the survey instrument items for each variable included in the analysis.

Statistical analysis

We conducted exploratory analyses to identify associations between social and economic determinants of health and the receipt of infertility diagnoses and fertility treatments. First, we examined the distribution of characteristics for the total sample. Second, we assessed bivariate associations between infertility diagnosis and fertility care, and each social and economic determinant of interest, as well as potential confounders, using χ2 tests of independence for categorical variables and t-tests for continuous variables. For t-tests, pooled or Satterthwaite estimators were used depending on whether the assumption of equal variances was met, as assessed by an F-test.

Separate multivariable logistic regression models were then constructed for each outcome (infertility diagnosis and fertility care). Key social and economic determinants of interest (race, ethnicity, current VA benefits and insurance status, and sexual orientation) were included in both models. We also included other determinants (marital status, income) and potential confounders (age, lifetime depression diagnosis) in both models if the bivariate association with either outcome had a P value <.1 (marital status, income). Veterans with missing values for any of the included variables were excluded from the logistic regression models.

We report OR, 95% CI, and P values. All analyses were conducted in SAS, using two-sided tests with an alpha level of 0.05.

Results

We assessed the representativeness of the full sample by comparing female Veterans who completed the interview with those who were invited but did not complete the interview (Supplemental Table 2). As shown, differences between groups were generally small, with most absolute standardized mean differences below 0.10, indicating minimal evidence of systematic differences between completers and noncompleters.

Among the 1,410 female Veterans who completed study interviews, 589 met the eligibility criteria for these analyses (Fig. 1). Within the analytic sample, 58.6% were 30–39 years (Table 1). Nearly half of the sample had completed a college degree or higher (45.2%). Most of our sample identified as heterosexual (89.2%), and most were married or living with a partner (68.8%). Nearly one-fifth of the sample identified as more than one race (19.7%). The rest identified with a single race: White (58.1%), Black (15.8%), or another racial identity (6.5%). More than 15% of the sample identified as Hispanic. Over one-third (43.3%) were covered by VA benefits and private health insurance, and one-fifth were covered by VA benefits only or in combination with public insurance (19.7%). The median household income was $65,000 (interquartile range [IQR] = $55,000). Approximately 80% of Veterans were either employed or not employed by choice (80.1%). Seventy-three percent of the sample previously had a live birth. Lifetime diagnoses of mental health conditions were common, including PTSD (35.6%), depression (56.5%), and anxiety (42.4%). Nearly 40% of Veterans had a positive AUDIT-C screen (39.2%).

Over one-fifth of our analytic sample reported they or their partner had ever received an infertility diagnosis (22.8%) (Table 2). Respondents who reported an infertility diagnosis were more likely to be married or living with a partner (79.9% vs. 65.5%, P = .002) compared with those without a diagnosis. The proportion of White Veterans was slightly higher among those who received an infertility diagnosis vs. those who did not, whereas the proportions of Veterans in all other racial groups were marginally higher among those who did not receive a diagnosis; however, these differences were not statistically significant (P = .75). Among Veterans who received an infertility diagnosis, a higher proportion had a household income of greater than $110,000 compared with those who did not receive a diagnosis (26.4% vs. 15.3%, respectively, P = .002). More Veterans who did not receive an infertility diagnosis were concentrated in the lowest household income category of $0–30,000 than those who did receive an infertility diagnosis (18.7% vs. 6.2%, P = .002). Depression diagnoses were more common among those diagnosed with infertility than those not diagnosed (64.2% vs. 54.2%, P = .04). Of those diagnosed (n = 134), 69.4% reported receiving fertility treatment (data not shown).

Almost 20% of the sample (n = 115) reported receiving fertility treatment for themselves or their partner (Table 2). Most of the 115 who received treatment (80.9%) reported having been previously diagnosed (data not shown). Veterans who received fertility treatment were more likely to be married or live with a partner than those who did not (86.1% vs. 64.6%, P<.0001). Among Veterans who received fertility treatment, there was a higher percentage of those with a household income of greater than $110,000 compared with Veterans who did not receive fertility treatment (28.7% vs. 15.7%, respectively, P = .0003). Nonsignificant differences were noted in the receipt of fertility treatment by age categories (P = .13), race (P = .26), sexual orientation (P = .15), and VA benefits and insurance status (P = .22).

Our regression models included marital status, race, sexual orientation, VA benefits and insurance status, and household income as social and economic determinants of health; age and depression diagnosis were included as confounders (Table 3). In the adjusted infertility diagnosis model, Veterans with a household income over $110,000 had more than five times the odds of receiving an infertility diagnosis compared with those with a household income of $0–$30,000 (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] = 5.33, 95% CI, 2.06–13.77). In the adjusted fertility treatment model, Veterans who were currently married or living with a partner had over twice the odds of having received fertility treatment compared with those who were single, divorced, or widowed (aOR = 2.32, 95% CI, 1.22–4.41). Veterans with a household income of greater than $110,000 had almost four times the odds of receiving fertility treatment compared with those with a household income of $0–30,000 (aOR = 3.89, 95% CI, 1.38–10.98).

Discussion

In this study of over 500 female Veterans, fewer than a quarter (22.8%) who self-reported that they met our clinical definition of infertility had ever received a diagnosis of infertility from a healthcare provider, and an even lower proportion (19.5%) had ever received fertility treatment. Low income was an important independent indicator of care receipt among Veterans, similar to the non-Veteran population. Importantly, we did not find any differences in infertility diagnosis or fertility care receipt by VA benefits and insurance status, despite many Veterans having access to infertility services through the VA. These findings suggest a significant unmet need for infertility evaluation and treatment among Veterans.

To our knowledge, our study is the first to investigate the proportion of Veterans who received an infertility diagnosis among those experiencing a minimum of 12 consecutive months of unprotected sex with a sperm-producing partner without achieving pregnancy. The high proportion of our sample who did not receive a diagnosis may reflect a lack of knowledge about available services, logistical barriers to care, or a lack of desire to pursue evaluation or treatment for infertility. Future research should examine what proportion of Veterans who experience infertility would have desired a fertility evaluation or treatment but did not receive it, as well as barriers to infertility evaluation and diagnosis within the VA healthcare system.

Of those who received an infertility diagnosis, over two-thirds (69.4%) went on to receive fertility treatment at some point in their lifetime. This estimate is higher than has been previously observed, including in a prior study, which found that 22% of VA users between 2001 and 2010 with an infertility diagnosis received fertility treatment or assessment (9). In the general population, a study using data from the National Survey of Family Growth, the primary data source for studying infertility for the US population, found that 41% of individuals aged 25–44 with current fertility problems received fertility medical services (20).

It is important to note that Veterans who are low-income, who may have enhanced access to fertility care compared with other low-income individuals if they qualify for VA benefits, are similarly at risk of not being diagnosed or receiving fertility treatment (21, 22, 23). In our multivariable models, Veterans who met our criteria for infertility with incomes of $0–$30,000 were less likely to have received an infertility diagnosis and fertility treatment compared with Veterans with a household income greater than $110,000. These findings align with previous research that has shown an association between fertility treatment and higher incomes (20, 22, 23, 24). Potential explanations for this association among Veterans specifically include persistent financial barriers, particularly among those who have not accessed VA benefits or do not qualify, and a lack of knowledge about available VA services for Veterans covered by these benefits. Additionally, financial barriers, such as the economic burden of parenting in the US (25), and bias or discrimination against those of lower socioeconomic status (26, 27), factors that prevent non-Veterans from accessing fertility care, may similarly affect Veterans. The current economic demands of fertility treatment in the US often limit family building to people with higher incomes and undercut reproductive autonomy for those who have lower incomes (28, 29).

Inequities in the receipt of fertility treatment among racial and ethnic minoritized populations are well documented for the non-Veteran population but less understood for Veterans (30, 31, 32). Among our sample, we did not find any statistically significant differences in the distribution of infertility diagnosis or fertility treatment by race or Hispanic ethnicity, although subgroup sample sizes were small. To our knowledge, only one prior study has examined racial inequities in infertility treatment among Veterans, finding that Veterans who identified as a minoritized race were less likely to report receiving fertility treatment (17). Unlike our study, which recruited Veterans from around the country, the prior study exclusively focused on Midwestern VA medical centers. The prior study, in addition, had a larger analytic sample and combined Veterans who identified as Native American or Alaskan Native, Asian or Pacific Islander, Hispanic or Latino, or Black or African American into one racial category, which differed from our study, which included separate racial categories to reflect White, Black, Hispanic, Unknown, and Other and Hispanic/Latinx ethnicity separately. More research is needed with a larger, national sample to further investigate potential differences in infertility diagnosis and fertility care by racialized identity.

Strengths of this study include a national sample of Veterans who both use and do not use VA healthcare and our focus on gaps in care among those who meet the clinical definition of infertility. Additionally, the use of CATI techniques for collecting data contributes to the robustness and completeness of the data source, allowing for rigorous investigations of the associations between social and economic determinants of health and fertility experiences. This study, in addition, has several limitations. Participation required access to a working telephone and up-to-date contact information, which may have introduced selection bias. Although participants and nonparticipants were similar across most measured demographic and military service characteristics, as shown in Supplemental Table 2, participants were slightly more likely to have higher educational attainment. Participants may, in addition, have differed from nonparticipants in unmeasured ways, including potential differences in healthcare engagement. Second, several variables in our model (e.g., marital status, current VA benefits and insurance status, and household income) were constructed from questions about a Veteran’s current situation, whereas outcomes related to fertility diagnosis and care were assessed by asking about the Veteran’s lifetime experiences. Thus, there may be a mismatch in temporality between variables of interest and outcomes, making it difficult to know what type of partnership they had, their VA and insurance coverage, or income during the period they experienced infertility. Third, our analyses relied on self-reports of infertility diagnoses, fertility treatment, and sexual activity over the life course, which may have led to underreporting of these outcomes and underestimation of the associations between socio-demographics and economic factors and the outcomes. Fourth, although we queried gender identity, the number of participants assigned female at birth who self-identified as not being a woman was too small to report separately. Fifth, despite a robust literature linking cigarette smoking to infertility (33, 34), we did not find statistically significant associations between smoking history and lifetime diagnosis of infertility or receipt of infertility treatment. This may be due, at least in part, to the fact that smoking status and infertility were not assessed contemporaneously. Finally, sample sizes for specific subgroups limited our ability to apply more in-depth intersectional analyses. More research is needed to examine how intersecting experiences of social and economic determinants (e.g., income, racialized identity, and sexual orientation) and the lived experiences of the Veterans who have them impact access and use of this care (19, 35).

Conclusion

This study identified a significant unmet need for fertility diagnosis and care among Veterans experiencing infertility, especially for those with the lowest incomes. Additional investigation is needed to understand underlying causes, such as potential knowledge gaps regarding VA’s coverage or barriers to accessing infertility care through the VA. Broader efforts by the VA to disseminate information about fertility services and to understand and address barriers faced by Veterans desiring fertility care could mitigate disparities and enhance all Veterans’ ability to achieve their reproductive goals.
