Multi-site distributed training with data protections for PET-based synthetic CT
Authors: Kasper Jørgensen, Lauren Partin, Raghavan Ashok, Vijay Shah, Anders Bertil Rodell, Martin Bazik, Bruce Spottiswoode, Flemming Littrup Andersen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAccurate PET quantification relies on attenuation correction (AC), commonly performed using a linear attenuation map derived from a CT acquisition. However, CT can introduce misregistration artifacts and adds radiation dose. Synthetic CT (sCT) from non-attenuation corrected (NAC) PET offers a CT-less alternative, but training robust models requires multi-site data that may be difficult to share under privacy regulations. We aim to enable PET-based sCT training across sites without exposing data. Methods We built a federated learning (FL) framework and trained two sCT generators–a paired conditional GAN and a CycleGAN. Models were pretrained on a single-site cohort (Site 1, n = 425) and fine-tuned via FL using additional data from Site 1 (n = 25) and a second site with different scanners and reconstruction parameters (Site 2, n = 25). Performance was assessed on an internal hold-out set (Site 1, n = 91) and two external cohorts (Sites 3,4; n = 11, 10) using region-wise relative mean error (rME) of SUV in AC PET. ResultsBoth models produced anatomically plausible sCT and AC PET with low errors when test data matched training distributions. FL fine-tuning improved robustness under distribution shift at Site 3, reducing errors across most regions, while maintaining comparable performance at Site 4 where protocols resembled the pretraining site. Conclusion Multi-site FL is a feasible path to increase the generalizability of PET-based sCT while preserving data privacy. The proposed framework offers a practical template for training and deploying CT-less AC models across heterogeneous clinical environments.Keywords: Federated learning, Privacy-preserving distributed training, Synthetic CT (sCT), PET attenuation correction (AC), Deep learning, Multi-site generalization and domain shift, Attenuation map generation, PET/CT

Introduction

Positron emission tomography (PET) is a vital tool for analyzing a range of conditions, including the entire treatment pathway across multiple oncologies, from tumor staging to treatment assessment. For accurate quantification, PET relies on attenuation correction (AC) implemented by several algorithms to report standardized uptake value (SUV). Clinicians use the AC PET image to support diagnosis. The classical approach for AC is to use an anatomical image such as CT. However, metal artifacts in CT and PET/CT misregistration due to patient motion introduce quantification errors.

To address misregistration and eliminate CT dose, especially for dose-sensitive populations such as pediatric patients or in repeat scans, several CT-less AC strategies have emerged. Maximum likelihood reconstruction of attenuation and activity (MLAA) jointly estimates activity and attenuation from emission data, but the attenuation sinogram is determined only up to a constant [1] and can degrade in low-count scenarios [2]. Deep learning approaches have also been proposed. Image-based methods generate synthetic CT (sCT) from non-attenuation-corrected (NAC) PET using convolutional neural networks (CNNs) or diffusion models, guided by pixel-wise losses, adversarial losses in generative adversarial networks (GANs), or physics-based constraints. Some approaches combine MLAA with image-to-image networks, using MLAA estimates as a prior for a CNN to reduce MLAA noise [3]. Population-based priors have also been used to guide sCT synthesis [4]. Direct generation of AC PET has been proposed as a fast alternative [5, 6], but this can limit reconstruction protocol flexibility and reduce transparency of error sources.

However, deep learning strategies rely on large training datasets to represent the target domain, and too few training cases or limited variability can introduce bias in AI applications [7]. Small sample size is a difficult obstacle to overcome in medical applications due to privacy restrictions for patient information, such as Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) and General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR). To address limited data access, federated learning (FL) was proposed to enable collaborative training across multiple sites without direct data sharing. A common approach is federated averaging, which trains a local model at each site and aggregates the weights into a global model [8]. FL is particularly advantageous in privacy restricted domains such as medical imaging, and it has been used in many applications across both medical and non-medical domains. Research has focused on challenges such as aggregation under data heterogeneity, defenses against client-side and model poisoning attacks, and communication bottlenecks. However, its adoption in real, non-simulated clinical environments remains limited. Many studies have central access to all data and only simulate distributed training, although open-source frameworks that enable FL, such as Flower [9] and NVIDIA FLARE [10], have gained traction.

In PET, FL has been applied to image artifact detection [11] and tumor segmentation [12, 13]. CT-less AC with FL has also been investigated [14], but evaluation was only performed with on-site data rather than external site data, which limits conclusions about generalizability. It also included a centralized comparison, implying data access to all sites. In contrast, we address CT-less AC in a physically distributed setting without cross site data access; to our knowledge, this is the first such demonstration.

Specifically, we study sCT generation from NAC PET. We compare two GAN architectures trained on a large single-site dataset and then fine-tuned via FL using data from a second site with different scanners and reconstruction parameters. We show that FL fine-tuning improves robustness and generalization across heterogeneous multi-site data while maintaining performance on the original pretraining domain. Our main contributions are threefold (i) an end to end, user friendly platform for multi-site FL in medical imaging covering decentralized storage, cohort curation, secure on-site training, central aggregation, inference, and on-site visualization, (ii) the first demonstration of improved generalization of sCT generation using FL in a non-simulated decentralized multi-site setting, and (iii) a direct comparison of CT based vs sCT based AC PET on internal and external cohorts using region wise SUV relative mean error and qualitative review.

Methods

We first describe our federated learning (FL) platform used for data handling, training, and inference. We then detail the experimental setup for training and comparing models for synthetic CT (sCT) generation.

Federated learning framework

We use the Teamplay Research platform for data storage, training, and model inference (Siemens Healthineers, Forchheim, Germany). Built on the Kubernetes Framework [15], it supports AI training workflows and clinical validation through an AI inference engine and research viewer applications. The NVFlare engine [10] and the MONAI framework [16] power the machine learning operations layer in Teamplay Research. A key capability is privacy-preserving learning, including federated learning (FL). The platform thus enables cross-site training by exchanging model parameters rather than raw data. To ensure reliable AI, it also provides data harmonization and profiling tools and adheres to GDPR. In summary, the platform integrates the following components to provide a robust and scalable AI infrastructure:

Data storage and management: A secure and reliable environment for large-scale structured and unstructured data (for example imaging, electronic health records, genomics, and wearable devices). It supports curation, annotation, and quality control, and it complies with data protection and privacy regulations.

Data

This study includes datasets from four sites (Site 1–4), each containing paired NAC PET/CT studies.

Training cohorts

Site 1: The largest cohort (n=541) was acquired on a Siemens Biograph Vision Quadra (Siemens Healthineers, Knoxville, TN). Of these, 425 studies were used to train the base models, 25 for FL fine-tuning, and the remaining 91 served as an independent internal test set. NAC PET images were reconstructed using OSEM (4 iterations, 5 subsets; 4i5s) with a 4 mm Gaussian post-filter and without PSF.

Two-stage training

Both networks were trained using a two-stage protocol with data from Sites 1 and 2. Stage 1: a base model was trained on volumes from Site 1. Stage 2: the model was fine-tuned via federated learning (FL) on an additional 25 volumes from Site 1 and 25 from Site 2. In the first round, models were initialized with weights determined by Stage 1. Each FL round comprised 25 local epochs, where a round would end with transfer of local model weights to central server and the next round would begin with transfer of global model weights to the local sites. 200 rounds were completed in total and client weights were aggregated using federated averaging (FedAvg [8]) after each round.

Inference and post-processing

Inference for both models was performed using a sliding-window scheme with 85% overlap and Gaussian-weighted patch blending. The predicted normalized CT values were transformed back to Hounsfield Units (HU) and resampled to the original CT resolution. To preserve the patient bed and background, these regions were composited from the measured CT into the synthetic CT (sCT) using a body mask estimated from the NAC PET by Gaussian smoothing, intensity thresholding, and hole filling. Voxels outside the body mask were taken from the measured CT, while voxels inside the mask were replaced by the synthetic prediction. This approach emulates a potential clinical workflow in which an empty-bed CT can be acquired prior to the patient scan acquisition.

Evaluation

Network validation was performed on a holdout set from Site 1 and on datasets from two external sites (Sites 3 and 4), as detailed in Table 1. For each case, AC PET images were reconstructed using PSF, OSEM 4i5s, and 2 mm Gaussian filtering, both with the synthetic CT and with the measured CT (reference AC PET) for comparison.

Qualitative analysis

Synthetic CTs and the corresponding AC PET images from both models were visually compared to assess image quality and anatomical fidelity.

Quantitative analysis

The GAN (pre- and post-FL) and CycleGAN (pre- and post-FL) models were compared using the relative mean error (rME) of SUV in AC PET. Metrics were computed within CT-derived regions of interest (lungs, liver, heart, aorta, spleen, brain, bones) obtained with TotalSegmentator [21]. The measured CT was used as a reference for the synthetic CT comparison.

Site 1 (in-distribution)

For the high-BMI subject from Site 1 (Fig. 2), both pre- and post-FL models capture the global anatomy. The post-FL GAN shows larger errors in the brain, left leg, and intestines, and the post-FL CycleGAN presents slightly reduced soft-tissue delineation relative to pre-FL. Despite these degradations, post-FL sCTs remain visually comparable to those pre-FL, indicating that the FL models still perform well on the original training domain.

Site 3 (OOD). For Site 3 (Fig. 3), which lies outside the pretraining distribution, the pre-FL GAN exhibits large artifacts near the bladder, abdomen, and lungs that are largely reduced post-FL. CycleGAN similarly shows a pre-FL lung artifact that is mitigated after FL. Both models distort skull shape pre-FL; this improves post-FL, although a residual brain artifact remains for the post-FL GAN. Notably, GAN reproduces the subject’s hip implant, whereas CycleGAN shows an air pocket pre-FL and bone-like material post-FL at the implant site. The corresponding AC PET example (Fig. 4) illustrates reduced SUV errors post-FL.

Site 4 (external in-distribution). Qualitative results for Site 4 (Fig. 5) are similar to Site 1 results. GAN tends to delineate soft tissues (e.g., abdomen) more clearly, and bone structures appear sharper with GAN than with CycleGAN. Neither model accurately captures abdominal air-filled cavities. The visible impact of FL for this subject is again limited, suggesting minimal degradation relative to the pre-FL models trained only on similar data.

In Fig. 6, a respiratory-phase mismatch at the liver dome in the reference AC PET causes a characteristic crescent-shaped misregistration artifact at the liver dome, commonly referred to as the ’banana artifact’, resulting from respiratory-phase mismatch between PET and CT acquisitions. This misregistration is corrected in the sCT-based reconstructions for all models except the GAN pre-FL, which retains the artifact.

Discussion

This study provides evidence that federated learning can significantly improve the generalizability of PET-based synthetic CT (sCT) generation across heterogeneous clinical sites without compromising patient data privacy. Our key finding is that FL fine-tuning consistently reduced region-wise SUV errors on the out-of-distribution (OOD) site, which used a different acquisition protocol. This was qualitatively confirmed by the visible reduction of artifacts around the bladder, lungs, and skull in the OOD images post-FL. Importantly, this improvement did not degrade performance at sites with protocols similar to the original pretraining data. By deploying a physically distributed, multi-site FL network, our work offers more compelling evidence of real-world clinical benefit than prior studies that relied on simulated federation or local-only evaluations. Furthermore, we extend the application of FL in medical imaging beyond artifact detection and segmentation to the critical task of PET attenuation correction, demonstrating its value in addressing protocol shifts.

The choice of generative architecture also played a distinct role in performance. The paired GAN, benefiting from direct supervision and an explicit bone Dice loss term, produced sCTs with crisper bone delineation and clearer soft-tissue contrast. In contrast, the CycleGAN, trained on unpaired data with a cycle-consistency objective, yielded smoother images but exhibited greater robustness to distribution shifts. This trade-off aligns with previous findings that paired objectives excel at voxel-level accuracy, whereas unpaired objectives prioritize learning the underlying data distribution, making them more resilient to domain variation r13121.

Successful real-world deployment, however, requires navigating several operational hurdles. A primary consideration is that the measured CT, used as our reference, is not a perfect ground truth due to potential metal artifacts and PET/CT misregistration. Indeed, some observed sCT “errors” may represent corrections of CT-specific issues, such as the resolution of the “banana artifact” at the liver dome. This is compounded by the inability to directly view remote training data in an FL setting, making robust monitoring essential. While loss curves are useful, they can be difficult to interpret without a visual reference, highlighting the need for more informative, automated metrics for quality control. Future work should integrate registration quality checks, automated artifact detection, and statistical validation of image headers to better guide model improvements. To further advance clinical autonomy and address these challenges, we propose a fully CT-free workflow where a single empty-bed scan, acquired once per scanner, is digitally composited with the sCT, eliminating the per-patient CT dependency. This could be enhanced with safeguards like physics-informed constraints and model-generated uncertainty maps to flag atypical cases for review.

Finally, this study has limitations that present avenues for future research. Our evaluation was conducted on relatively small external cohorts and was restricted to a single tracer (F-FDG) and scanner family (Siemens Biograph). We plan to expand the federated network to additional sites and scanner vendors to test scalability and robustness, with the ultimate goal of developing generalizable models that eliminate the need for hospital-specific model deployments. Although our two-stage training strategy handled site imbalance effectively, more advanced FL algorithms like FedProx or domain-weighted aggregation may be needed to ensure stability as scanner and protocol diversity increases [23, 24]. The primary endpoint was region-wise SUV error; future studies must expand the evaluation to include lesion-level quantification, task-based endpoints such as tumor detection, and formal reader assessments to confirm clinical non-inferiority. Exploring techniques for uncertainty calibration and explicit domain-shift detection will also be crucial for increasing the safety and reliability of sCT in routine clinical practice.

Conclusion

Single-site synthetic CT models generalize poorly across scanners, limiting feasibility of deploying single-scanner trained models to diverse scanner types. In contrast, we demonstrate that federated learning is feasible in a real-world deployment with physically distributed data and no shared data access. Cross-site fine-tuning, while keeping patient data local, improved out-of-distribution generalization and preserved in-distribution performance. Our proposed federated learning framework can readily be applied to other clinical applications to strengthen multi-site algorithm robustness in restricted environments. Future work will scale to more sites and protocols and include task-based endpoints to confirm clinical utility.
