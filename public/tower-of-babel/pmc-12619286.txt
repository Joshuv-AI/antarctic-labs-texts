Application of three-anatomical-plane dissection technique in the partial nephrectomy of renal cell carcinoma abutting the hilum gulf
Authors: Qi Wang, Yueming Chen, Guixin Zhang, Xiaohui Shen, Yi Gao, Haofei Wang, Le Xu, Guangliang Jiang, Chen Fang, Hai Huang, Danfeng Xu, Lu Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo describe the application of a three-anatomical-plane dissection (TAPD) technique for RCC-AHG and evaluate its feasibility and safety.Design, setting, and participantsBetween June 2019 and October 2023, 42 patients undergoing PN using the TAPD technique at Ruijin Hospital were included. Introduction

Partial nephrectomy (PN) and radical nephrectomy are used to treat localized renal carcinoma [1]. When technically feasible, robotic or laparoscopic PN is the surgical choice for treating stage I-III renal carcinoma. Robotic or laparoscopic PN is associated with better renal function preservation, lower incidence of chronic kidney disease, and lower incidence of cardiovascular events, and the oncological effect is similar to that of radical nephrectomy [2, 3].

Renal hilar tumors usually have higher RENAL scores (radius, exophytic/endophytic, nearness to collecting system or sinus, anterior/posterior, and location relative to polar lines) and greater surgical complexity than non-hilar tumors.

This study focused on renal cell carcinomas abutting the hilum gulf (RCC-AHG), a special type of renal hilar tumor. The endophytic tumor was confined to the renal lip, abutting or compressing the main hilar vessels, yet it demonstrated a clear demarcation by not invading the renal parenchyma located in the territory of the interlobar arteries (Fig. 1A). A number of suturing techniques have been put forth for tumors at this location, but no simple, safe, and reproducible universal resection technique has been established.

The first aim of this paper is to describe a new three-anatomical-plane dissection (TAPD) technique that we applied to PN for RCC-AHG. We also aimed to assess the feasibility and safety of this technique based on perioperative data, oncological results, renal function protection, and the occurrence of complications.

Patients and methods

Patient selection

From June 2019 to October 2023 in our center (Ruijin Hospital, Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine, China.), 42 patients undergoing PN using the TAPD technique were included.

The inclusion criteria were as follows:

Radiological evidence of distant metastasis or intraoperatively confirmed multifocal tumors;

History of ipsilateral renal surgery or radiotherapy;

History of ipsilateral renal surgery or radiotherapy;

Severe coagulopathy or inability to tolerate anesthesia;

Severe coagulopathy or inability to tolerate anesthesia;

Postoperative pathological diagnosis of benign disease.

Postoperative pathological diagnosis of benign disease.

In total, 44 patients were initially screened, of whom 2 were excluded because of benign postoperative pathology. Finally, 42 patients met the eligibility criteria and were included in the present analysis.

Imaging evaluation

All patients underwent standardized contrast-enhanced CT examinations. Imaging features were independently assessed in a blinded manner by two experienced radiologists. The evaluation focused on tumor size, RENAL score, and potential signs of vascular or perinephric fat invasion. Any discrepancies were resolved by consensus discussion.

Data collection

The following information was prospectively collected: patient age, sex, BMI, age-adjusted Charlson comorbidity index (aCCI), tumor diameter, location of the ipsilateral kidney, RENAL score, potential signs of vascular or perinephric fat invasion, TNM stage, surgical methods, surgical approaches, warm ischemia time, postoperative complications (Clavien‒Dindo complication classification), postoperative hospital stay, pathological features, positive surgical margin rate, preoperative creatinine, creatinine at 1 day, 3 months, 6 months, and 12 months after surgery, and recurrence or metastasis.

TAPD technique

The TAPD technique was based on the anatomical characteristics of RCC-AHG. We found that RCC-AHG and the outside world have three interface surfaces with different characteristics. During surgery, safe tumor resection can be achieved by sequentially isolating the three interface surfaces between the tumor and the outside world. The anatomical characteristics of the three interface surfaces were as follows: (1) Interface surface 1 (C1): The interface between the tumor and the perirenal fat was the convex part of the tumor, which could be fully exposed after removal of the perirenal fat around the tumor (Fig. 1B. (2) Interface surface 2 (C2): This was the interface between the tumor and the main large blood vessels in the renal hilum, which are usually in direct contact with or are oppressed on the renal vein (renal artery) trunk and its branches. There could be a thin layer of fat between the tumor and renal vessel tissues or renal branch vessels, but these vessels usually eventually entered the kidney and rarely entered the tumor. Therefore, the plane of the tumor capsule was usually the plane lacking blood vessels. By achieving separation via cold scissors and punctate electrocoagulation of small blood vessels, renal artery obstruction was not needed at this stage (Fig. 1B and C). (3) Interface surface 3 (C3): C3 was the contact plane between the tumor and kidney parenchyma. This plane was the part of the tumor blood vessels that entered and exited the tumor. In theory, most blood vessels supplying the tumor from the renal artery and its branches first enter the renal parenchyma and then enter the tumor through the C3 plane. C3 was dissected after the first two planes were adequately treated. Methods such as enucleation, with or without occlusion, could be used to reduce the wound area as much as possible (Fig. 1C). For the tissue connecting C2 to C3, i.e., the posterior incisional edge of the renal lip, many small blood vessels could be near this position that entered the renal parenchyma, which were easy to retract after damage, and might be missed during suturing. Excessive tissue resection should be minimized.

Surgical procedures

After general anesthesia, the patient was placed in the lateral position. Depending on the location of the tumor, the transabdominal approach or the retroperitoneal approach was taken, and robotic or laparoscopic surgery was performed. Taking robotic transabdominal surgery for ventral tumors as an example, the operating channel was arranged according to the classic robotic PN method. The lens hole was located outside and above the umbilicus, and the robotic camera channel was placed at the midaxillary line, on the medial side of the anterior superior iliac spine and under the costal arch. Two other assistant channels were established (exterior photographs of the surgery) [4]. The paracolic sulci was opened to expose the anterior surface of the kidney, the tumor and the ventral fat of the renal hilum were fully removed, and C1 was exposed (Fig. 2A). The renal artery or branches were isolated and not blocked yet. C2 was dissected at the plane between the renal vein and the tumor (Fig. 2B). The renal artery was occluded or selectively occluded, and C3 was dissected between the renal tumor capsule and renal parenchyma (Fig. 2C-D). The wound was closed continuously with a V-loc barbed suture (Fig. 2E-F). The renal blood flow was opened to check for possible bleeding points, and punctate electrocoagulation or suturing was performed if necessary.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were obtained as follows: for continuous variables, normally distributed data were expressed as mean ± SD, whereas non-normally distributed data were reported as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR). Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and proportions, as appropriate.

Statistical analyses were performed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS).

Results

A total of 42 patients were included in this study. Detailed characteristics of the patient population and intraoperative data are provided in Tables 1 and 2. The perioperative outcomes, postoperative outcomes and postoperative follow-up data are presented in Table 3. Postoperative complications of any grade occurred in 3 (7.14%) patients, including two Clavien-Dindo grade I events, and one grade II event. No high-grade postoperative complications occurred. Negative surgical margins were observed in all patients. At a mean follow-up of 12 months, neither local nor distant recurrences of the disease were observed.

Discussion

With the increasing application of PN, urological surgeons are facing an increasing number of PN surgeries for highly complex renal carcinoma, including renal hilar tumors, which require more precise techniques such as advanced image guide techniques including intraoperative fluorescence imaging and holographic imaging, advanced artery clamping technique including selective arterial occlusion, meticulous suturing techniques etc. However, the difficulty of PN of renal hilar tumors should not be underestimated, and a comprehensive understanding of individualized tumor anatomy and a standard resection strategy are needed.

In this study, we described a specific type of renal hilar tumor—RCC-AHG and the application of a three-anatomical-plane dissection (TAPD) technique for RCC-AHG. Patient characteristics, perioperative data, and postoperative outcomes were prospectively collected and retrospectively analyzed. All procedures were performed under institutional ethics approval (ethics approval number: 2024-94), and written informed consent was obtained from all patients. Inclusion criteria were solitary RCC located in the renal sinus bay area, extending beyond the polar lines, and closely associated with hilar vessels. Patients with multifocal tumors, cT3–4 disease, or incomplete perioperative data were excluded. No patients were lost to follow-up during the perioperative period, ensuring transparency and minimizing potential selection bias. The results suggest that TAPD may be a feasible and effective approach in terms of oncological outcomes, perioperative complications, and renal function preservation.

RCC-AHG was defined at our center based on clear, reproducible anatomical characteristics, including tumors originating from the renal lip, occupying the renal sinus bay, extending beyond the upper and lower polar lines, and being in close proximity to major hilar vessels. The endophytic tumor was confined to the renal lip, abutting or compressing the main hilar vessels, yet it demonstrated a clear demarcation by not invading the renal parenchyma located in the territory of the interlobar arteries (Fig. 1A). These features provided the anatomical basis for TAPD. The RENAL score, which evaluates the tumor’s radius (R), exophytic properties (E), nearness to the renal sinus or collecting system (N), anterior/posterior location (A), and the tumor’s position within the kidney (L), was systematically applied to further stratify tumor characteristics. In this study, patients included had a RENAL score ranging from 9 to 10. Preoperative CT scans were meticulously reviewed to exclude significant fat or venous invasion, and postoperative pathology confirmed that the majority of cases were staged as pT1-2, with no cases upstaged to pT3. These findings support the validity of our case selection. Furthermore, RCC-AHG in our center was characterized by a distinctive vascular pattern, where the tumor’s blood vessels entered the kidney before infiltrating the tumor itself. The interface between the tumor and the non-kidney parenchyma was identified as a relatively avascular plane, which also served as the anatomical foundation for TAPD.

Compared to other exophytic tumors, RCC-AHG surgery is characterized by a more complex perihilar anatomy, which includes dense vascular structures, thereby increasing the risk of unexpected vascular injury. Additionally, the resection site following tumor removal is arcuate rather than basin-shaped, and small vessels at the hilum-parenchyma junction may be retracted and hidden. If these vessels are inadequately sutured, they can predispose to postoperative bleeding. To address these challenges, the TAPD technique was developed, incorporating structured dissection along three anatomically defined planes: (1) the avascular interface at the renal lip (C1), (2) peritumoral dissection at the sinus bay (C2), and (3) limited parenchymal transection (C3). The selection of these planes is based on their anatomical characteristics: C1 and C2 are chosen to avoid major vascular structures, while C3 is specifically designed to minimize parenchymal loss by limiting the extent of renal tissue resection. Notably, only C3 requires reconstruction, which further reduces parenchymal loss.

This approach differentiates itself from conventional practices by prioritizing the preservation of both renal parenchyma and vasculature, making TAPD especially advantageous for the management of complex perihilar tumors. While vascular ligation in perihilar regions remains a standard surgical technique, TAPD enhances precision by utilizing anatomically guided dissection planes, with particular emphasis on the C3 plane. This strategy reduces the risk of inadvertent vascular injury and improves postoperative outcomes. Renorrhaphy was tailored to the curved geometry of the renal lip, allowing for continuous, tension-free suturing, which is critical for preserving vascular integrity and minimizing postoperative bleeding. While non-blocking or selective clamping was generally feasible for the C1 and C2 regions, the decision at C3 was primarily guided by tumor characteristics and the surgeon’s expertise. Emerging evidence suggests that parenchymal preservation, rather than ischemic time, alongside careful management of patient comorbidities, is a stronger predictor of long-term renal function. This further supports the rationale for limiting C3 intervention in the TAPD approach [5–10]. The core innovation of TAPD lies in defining RCC-AHG as a distinct anatomical entity, which facilitates the adoption of a standardized surgical technique. Our results demonstrate that this tailored, systematic approach leads to superior clinical outcomes in patients with this specific type of hilar tumor.

Our results suggest that TAPD may represent a safe and reproducible surgical approach for RCC-AHG, as evidenced by intraoperative blood loss, operative time, and postoperative complication rates that appear comparable to, or in some cases favorable relative to, those reported for other high-complexity PN procedures [11–16]. Although the mean length of postoperative hospitalization was 5 days, which might seem prolonged in light of the relatively low complication rate, this is likely attributable to local institutional protocols and patient management practices that favor extended observation after major urological surgery, rather than to complications themselves. It should be noted, however, that the retrospective, single-center, and non-comparative design of this study limits the strength of these conclusions. In the future, we plan to expand our cohort and conduct comparative studies with other surgical approaches, with the aim of obtaining more reliable and generalizable evidence. It is generally thought that the tumor’s location, size, and relationship with the surrounding tissue structures are key, complex factors in PN surgery. For more than 20 years, techniques such as tumor enucleation, selective clamping, and off-clamp PN have been developed to standardize PN and reduce surgical risks, thereby facilitating the widespread adoption of robotic and laparoscopic PN and promoting the evolution of high-complexity procedures [11]. For RCC-AHG, establishing a universal surgical strategy based on anatomical characteristics is equally important. The TAPD technique we describe offers such a structured, anatomy-driven three-plane framework, which distinguishes it from conventional PN. By providing a reproducible sequence of avascular-to-vascular dissection planes, TAPD serves as a systematic template tailored to the unique challenges of hilar tumors. To further contextualize our results, we compared perioperative and renal functional outcomes of our TAPD approach with those reported in previous studies of renal hilar or high-complexity tumors (Table 4). This comparison highlights that our technique provides favorable outcomes in terms of intraoperative safety, complication rates, warm ischemia time, and preservation of renal function.

Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. Although data collection was prospective, the analysis was retrospective and conducted at a single high-volume center, limiting generalizability. The sample size of 42 patients is relatively small, and all procedures were performed by highly experienced surgeons, which may not reflect broader clinical practice. In addition, detailed radiological descriptors and comprehensive pathological staging beyond pT1-2 were not systematically included, and long-term renal functional or oncological outcomes were not evaluated. These factors underscore the need for cautious interpretation of our results.

Moving forward, our team will focus on conducting larger multicenter studies and, if feasible, randomized controlled trials to further validate the safety, efficacy, and potential advantages of TAPD compared with conventional PN. In addition, we plan to investigate the learning curve and to establish standardized training pathways, integrating novel simulation tools and animal models, which will be essential to ensure reproducibility and facilitate the broader adoption of this technique.
