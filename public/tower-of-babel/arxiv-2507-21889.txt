When is the diagonal contractible?
Xi Chen, Frank Gounelas

Abstract
. For a smooth projective complex variety X X , we prove that there exists a birational morphism X × X → Y X\times X\to Y to a projective variety Y Y contracting the diagonal Δ X ⊂ X × X \Delta_{X}\subset X\times X to a point if and only if X X has maximal Albanese dimension and irregularity q ≥ 2 ​ dim X q\geq 2\dim X . We also give necessary and sufficient conditions for arbitrary contractions of the diagonal and for the existence of a birational morphism which is an isomorphism outside the diagonal.



1.
Introduction

Let
X
be a smooth projective variety over the complex numbers
{{\mathbb{C}}}
. The main purpose of this paper is to study the following problem.

Question 1.1
.

Let
\Delta_{X}\subset X\times X
be the diagonal. When does there
exist a birational projective morphism
\pi\colon X\times X\to Y
so that
\pi(\Delta_{X})
is a point?

If the above happens, we say that the diagonal
\Delta_{X}\subset X\times X
is
contractible to a point
.
In this paper, in addition to contractions of the diagonal to a point, we consider contractions
\pi
such that
\dim\pi(\Delta_{X})<\dim X
.

Definition 1.2
.

We say that the diagonal
\Delta_{X}\subset X\times X
is
contractible to a variety of dimension
m
or
has an
m
-dimensional contraction
if there exists a birational projective morphism
\pi:X\times X\to Y
such that
\dim\pi(\Delta_{X})=m
.

Remark 1.3
.

By taking the Stein factorisation, the existence of a birational
\pi
contracting
\Delta_{X}
is equivalent to the existence of a generically finite
\pi
contracting
\Delta_{X}
, so we rather treat the stronger assumption as our main question.

In view of the classical case of
\dim X=1
(see Section
2
), it would seem that contractibility of the diagonal might depend on subtle positivity properties of
X
. Instances of this can already be found in
[
LO18
]
, where the positivity of the cycle
[\Delta_{X}]\in\mathop{\mathrm{CH}}\nolimits_{\dim X}(X\times X)
is studied. However, we will see that the question of contractibility of the diagonal is rather simple and in fact equivalent to properties of the Albanese morphism (cf.
[
LO18
, §5.3]
).

We now state the main theorems of this paper. For a smooth projective variety
X
, we denote the Albanese morphism by

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{alb}:X}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{Alb}(X).}

Theorem 1.4
.

For a smooth projective variety
X
, the diagonal
\Delta_{X}\subset X\times X
is contractible to a point if and only if

(1.1)

\dim\operatorname{Alb}(X)\geq 2\dim X\text{ and }\dim X=\dim\operatorname{alb}(X).

To explain the conditions in the theorem, we say that a variety has
maximal Albanese dimension
if

\dim X=\dim\operatorname{alb}(X).

This holds if and only if
\Omega^{1}_{X}
is generically generated by global sections, for example if it is globally generated - examples of such varieties are subvarieties of abelian varieties. The first condition in (
1.1
) is about the
irregularity
of
X

\begin{split}q&=h^{1}(X,{\cal O}_{X})=h^{0}(X,\Omega^{1}_{X})\\
&=\dim\operatorname{Alb}(X)\geq 2\dim X.\end{split}

As it is rather simple, we give a quick summary of the method of proof of the above theorem in the most important case, namely when
q=0
. If
f:X\times X\to Y
is birational, then for any ample line bundle
L
on
Y
, we have that
f^{*}L
is big and nef. Given our assumption on
q
, we may write
f^{*}L=\pi_{1}^{*}D_{1}+\pi_{2}^{*}D_{2}
for
D_{i}\in{\rm Pic}X
and
\pi_{i}
the two projections (in general there is a contribution from the Picard group of the Albanese variety, see Lemma
3.3
). Restricting to fibres of
\pi_{i}
, we see that
D_{i}
must also be big and nef. If
f(\Delta_{X})
were a point, then
D_{1}+D_{2}=f^{*}L|_{\Delta_{X}}=0
which gives a contradiction.

It is worth noting that the image
Y
of the birational contraction is basically never smooth along the image of
\Delta_{X}
(see
[
KM98
, Corollary 2.63]
) - for example in the curve case (see Section
2.1
) the image is singular but with Gorenstein singularities
[
Băd01
, Corollary 2.5]
.

We also prove a necessary and sufficient condition for
\Delta_{X}
to be contractible to a variety of dimension
m<\dim X
, although it is less explicit than (
1.1
). The two statements could be combined but we preferred to give a cleaner statement to Theorem
1.4
.

Theorem 1.5
.

For a smooth projective variety
X
, the diagonal
\Delta_{X}\subset X\times X
is contractible to a variety of dimension
m
if and only if there exists a dominant morphism
\pi:X\to W
to a projective variety
W
with
\dim W=m
such that
\dim X_{p}=\dim\operatorname{alb}(X_{p})
and
\operatorname{alb}(X_{p})
is not contained in a translate of any abelian subvariety of
\operatorname{Alb}(X)
of dimension less than
2\dim X_{p}
for a general point
p\in W
and
X_{p}=\pi^{-1}(p)
.

For an abelian variety

, we may consider the

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces a_{1}-a_{2}.}

As we will see in the proofs of the above two theorems, the contraction of the diagonal

is derived from the map

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{alb}(x_{1})-\operatorname{alb}(x_{2}).}

When (

to a point.

It is reasonable to ask whether this map is essentially “the only” morphism which contracts

. To be more precise, let

be the Stein factorization of

Question 1.6
.

Is it true that every generically finite map
X\times X\to Y^{\prime}
contracting
\Delta_{X}
to a point factors through
\lambda_{\mathrm{St}}
?

We discuss this in Section

and in particular give a positive answer in some limited cases, see Lemma

There are other variants of Question

. One could additionally require that
\pi:X\times X\backslash\Delta_{X}\to Y\backslash\pi(\Delta_{X})
is an isomorphism.
Note that if
X
is a smooth projective variety which contains a uniruled subvariety

, e.g., a rational curve, then
\lambda

\Delta_{X}
, but also
Z\times Z
to a point, since an abelian variety does not contain any rational curves. We can nevertheless prove the following criterion for this variant of the problem.

Theorem 1.7
.

For a smooth projective variety
X
, there exists a contraction
f:X\times X\to Y
of the diagonal to a point such that

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X\times X\setminus\Delta_{X}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Y\backslash f(\Delta_{X})}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f}

is an isomorphism if and only if
\operatorname{alb}:X\to A=\operatorname{Alb}(X)
is finite and birational over its image such that

(1.4)

\dim(X\times_{A}X\backslash\Delta_{X})\leq 0

and for all
0\neq a\in A
we have

(1.5)

\dim\left(\operatorname{alb}(X)\cap(\operatorname{alb}(X)+a)\right)\leq 0.

We explain the conditions a bit. Noting that
\lambda^{-1}(0)=X\times_{A}X
, condition (
1.4
) is equivalent to the fact that
\operatorname{alb}
is finite and only has finitely many fibres which contain more than one point, so is in particular birational too (i.e.,
\operatorname{alb}
is an isomorphism outside finitely many points of

). The second condition (
1.5
) is equivalent to the fact that the difference map

is finite outside the diagonal, i.e., in combination with (
1.4
), that
\lambda^{-1}(a)
is finite for all
0\neq a\in A
.

These two conditions are true for subvarieties
X\subset A
of abelian varieties, satisfying
\dim X\cap(a+X)=0
for all
a\neq 0
, and we note that this last condition forces condition (
1.1
). It would be interesting to give some examples of varieties
X
with finite Albanese morphism
\operatorname{alb}:X\to A
so that only finitely many fibres contain more than one point yet
\operatorname{alb}
is not a closed embedding.

Notation:
For the remainder of the paper we will simplify notation and denote by
A=\operatorname{Alb}(X)
and
\alpha=\operatorname{alb}
. We work over the complex numbers
{{\mathbb{C}}}
throughout, and varieties are reduced and irreducible.

2.
Low dimensional examples

The primary examples of varieties whose diagonal is contractible to a point are non-degenerate subvarieties
X\subset A
of abelian varieties of codimension at least
\dim X
. Here
non-degenerate
means they are not contained in a translate of a proper abelian subvariety of
A
.

We note that the two conditions in Theorem
1.4
are invariant under birational morphisms from other smooth projective varieties, so we may as well try to classify minimal varieties with this property.

2.1.
Curves

Let
C
be a smooth projective curve. Then for
C=\Delta_{C}\subset C\times C
the diagonal with normal bundle
N_{C/C\times C}
we have

\deg N_{C/C\times C}=\deg T_{C}=2-2g.

In particular, if
g\leq 1
then
\Delta_{C}
cannot be contracted by Grauert’s contractibility criterion
[
BHPV04
, p.91, Theorem 2.1]
. If
g\geq 2
, then the difference map

\displaystyle\delta:C\times C

\displaystyle\to

\displaystyle J(C)

\displaystyle(p,q)

\displaystyle\mapsto

\displaystyle{\cal O}(p-q)

always contracts
\Delta_{C}
to a point, and outside
\Delta_{C}
will be an isomorphism onto its image if
C
is not hyperelliptic, whereas it will have degree 2 if
C
is hyperelliptic (see, e.g.,
[
ACGH85
, p.223, Exercise D]
). Hence, taking the Stein factorisation in the latter case, we see that the diagonal of a curve is contractible if and only if
g\geq 2
.

As we will see below in Lemma
2.5
, the difference map to the Jacobian is essentially the only morphism contracting the diagonal, in that any other birational morphism to a surface
f:C\times C\to S
where
f(\Delta_{C})
is a point factors through the Stein factorisation of the difference map.

2.2.
Surfaces

Let
S
be a smooth projective surface. From
Theorem
1.4
, the diagonal of
S\times S
is contractible to a point if and only if

\dim\operatorname{alb}(S)=2\hskip 12.0pt\text{and}\hskip 12.0ptq(S)\geq 4.

In particular this is not possible if the Kodaira dimension
k(X)\leq 0
. Examples where it does happen are products of curves of genus at least two or more generally non-degenerate 2-dimensional subvarieties of abelian varieties of dimension at least 4. Here is a small classification result for surfaces which leaves out surfaces of general type.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
S
be a minimal smooth projective surface with Kodaira dimension
\kappa(S)\leq 1
. Then the diagonal of
S
is contractible to a point if and only if
S\cong(C\times E)/G
for
G\subset E
a finite subgroup acting diagonally on the product, where
E,C
are smooth projective curves of genus
1
and
g\geq 3
respectively.

Proof.

One direction follows easily by computing

h^{1}((C\times E)/G),{\cal O}_{(C\times E)/G})=g+1.

For the other, note that from the classification of surfaces and Theorem
1.4
, we have already ruled out
\kappa(S)\leq 0
. If
\kappa(S)=1
, then
S
admits an elliptic fibration

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f:S}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces C}

to a smooth projective curve. From
[
KU85
, Lemma 3.4]
, we obtain that
\dim\operatorname{Alb}(S)=g+1
and that no fibre of
f
gets contracted under the Albanese morphism. In particular,
f
cannot contain any rational curves (which would necessarily be contracted to
\operatorname{Alb}(S)
) and has only multiple fibres. Consider

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces J}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces C}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{1,1}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces g}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\psi}

the corresponding Jacobian fibration of
f
and the induced moduli map
\psi
. From
[
CDL25
, Theorems 4.3.1, 4.3.20]
,
g
must be a smooth fibration. In particular
\psi
is a contraction and it follows that
g
must be isotrivial. This implies that all smooth fibres of
f
are isomorphic. It follows that there is an elliptic curve
E\subset\mathrm{Bir}(S)
. The result now follows from
[
Fon24
, Proposition 3.26]
or
[
Rv76
, §5]
.
∎

Remark 2.2
.

In view of results in
[
Art70
]
one could hope that contractibility of the diagonal to a point could be related to
N_{\Delta/X\times X}^{*}\cong\Omega^{1}_{X}
being ample. This is only true in the divisor case though, namely when
\dim X=1
as Section
2.1
shows. Indeed, as the above shows,
X=C\times E
for
E
an elliptic curve and
g(C)\geq 3
has contractible diagonal but
\Omega^{1}_{X}
is globally generated but not ample as it has a trivial quotient. Moreover, a sufficiently general, high degree and high codimension complete intersection in projective space will have
\Omega^{1}_{X}
ample yet
q=0
, so the diagonal cannot be contracted to a point.

Now we may ask the question when or whether it is possible at all that the diagonal of
S\times S
is contractible to a curve.
From Theorem
1.5
, the necessary and sufficient conditions for this to happen are

(1)

there exists a projective dominant morphism to a curve
S\to W
,

(2)

\dim\alpha(S_{p})=1
for
p\in W
general,

(3)

\alpha(S_{p})
is not an elliptic curve for
p\in W
general.

For example, if
C_{1},C_{2}
are two general curves of genus at least
2
, then the diagonal of
C_{1}\times C_{2}
is contractible to a curve.

We end with a remark in a slightly different direction. In
[
LO18
]
, positivity properties of the cycle
[\Delta_{X}]\in\mathop{\mathrm{CH}}\nolimits_{n}(X\times X)
are discussed for surfaces. In particular, they study when
[\Delta_{X}]
is big or nef. They prove for example that if
X
is a K3 surface, then
[\Delta_{X}]
is neither big nor nef, and that if
X
is very general, then
\Delta_{X}
is the unique effective cycle in its numerical class, so is extremal in the pseudo-effective cone of cycles. Nevertheless, by our theorem above, it cannot be contracted to a point.

2.3.
Higher dimension

Other than high-codimensional non-degenerate subvarieties of abelian varieties, the following is easy to see and is the prototypical example in all dimensions of interesting contraction behaviour for the diagonal.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
C_{1},\ldots,C_{n}
be general curves of genus

so that

for all

\Delta_{C_{1}\times\ldots\times C_{n}}\subset C_{1}\times\ldots\times C_{n}\times C_{1}\times\ldots\times C_{n}

Even though we do not use it, we leave the following well-known lemma here to highlight further the relevance of positivity properties of

, though as we pointed out in Remark

, the relation of positivity to contractibility of the diagonal is subtle.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
X
be a smooth projective variety. Then the Albanese morphism
\alpha:X\to A
is unramified (in particular finite) if and only if
\Omega^{1}_{X}
is globally generated.

3.
Proofs of the theorems

Lemma 3.1
.

Let

Proof.

If there exists a birational

with

, then for any ample divisor

) holds.

Conversely, if there exists a big and semi-ample divisor

satisfying (

), then

gives a birational morphism

to a variety of dimension
m

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\lambda:X\times X}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A\times A}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A,}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\alpha\times\alpha}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces d}

where
d:A\times A\to A
is
the difference map

(x,y)\mapsto x-y
, is such a map because
\lambda(\Delta_{X})=\{0\}
and
\lambda
is generically finite onto its image by Lemma
3.2
. In other words, as a contraction of the diagonal to a point we may take the Stein factorisation of
\lambda
.

The other direction will follow from Proposition
3.4
proved below.
∎

Proposition 3.4
.

For a smooth projective variety
X
and a birational morphism
f:X\times X\to Y
, we have

\dim(f(\Delta_{X}))\geq\dim X-\min(\dfrac{1}{2}\dim A,\dim\alpha(X)).

In particular, if the diagonal is contractible to a point, then
(
1.1
) must hold.

Proof.

Suppose that
\Delta_{X}\subset X\times X
is contractible to a variety of dimension
m
.
As pointed out in Lemma
3.1
, there exists a big and semi-ample divisor
D
on
X\times X
satisfying (
3.1
).
By Lemma
3.3
,

D=\pi_{1}^{*}D_{1}+\pi_{2}^{*}D_{2}-(\alpha\times\alpha)^{*}B

for some
D_{1},D_{2}\in{\rm Pic}_{{\mathbb{Q}}}(X)
and
B\in{\rm Pic}_{{\mathbb{Q}}}(A\times A)
satisfying (
3.3
).

Since
D
is big and nef and
(\pi_{i}^{*}D_{i})|_{\pi_{i}^{-1}(p)}=0=((\alpha\times\alpha)^{*}B)|_{\pi_{i}^{-1}(p)}
, we see that both
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
are big and nef on
X
. Furthermore, we can choose
D
such that
D_{1}=D_{2}
by applying the group action
\sigma:X\times X\to X\times X
sending
\sigma(x_{1},x_{2})=(x_{2},x_{1})
. More precisely, let

G=D+\sigma^{*}D=\pi_{1}^{*}(D_{1}+D_{2})+\pi_{2}^{*}(D_{1}+D_{2})-(\alpha\times\alpha)^{*}(B+\sigma^{*}B)

where we use
\sigma
for both maps
X\times X\to X\times X

and
A\times A\to A\times A
given by
\sigma(x_{1},x_{2})=(x_{2},x_{1})
.

As a sum of big and semi-ample divisors,
G
is also big and semi-ample on
X\times X
, and since

D\Big|_{\Delta_{X}}=\sigma^{*}(D)\Big|_{\Delta_{X}}

in
{\rm Pic}_{{\mathbb{Q}}}(\Delta_{X})={\rm Pic}_{{\mathbb{Q}}}(X)
, we still have

G^{m}\Delta_{X}\neq 0\hskip 12.0pt\text{and}\hskip 12.0ptG^{m+1}\Delta_{X}=0

or equivalently,

(3.7)

\delta_{X}^{*}(G)^{m}\neq 0\hskip 12.0pt\text{and}\hskip 12.0pt\delta_{X}^{*}(G)^{m+1}=0

for
\delta_{X}:X\to X\times X
given by
\delta_{X}(x)=(x,x)
.

Restricting
G
to
\Delta_{X}
, we have

(3.8)

\delta_{X}^{*}(G)=2(D_{1}+D_{2})-\alpha^{*}\delta_{A}^{*}(B+\sigma^{*}B)=2(D_{1}+D_{2}-\alpha^{*}M)

in
{\rm Pic}_{{\mathbb{Q}}}(X)
for
\delta_{A}:A\to A\times A
given by
\delta_{A}(a)=(a,a)
and

M=\dfrac{1}{2}\delta_{A}^{*}(B+\sigma^{*}B).

It follows from (
3.7
) and (
3.8
) that

(3.9)

(D_{1}+D_{2}-\alpha^{*}M)^{m+1}=0.

Let us consider

P=p_{1}^{*}M+p_{2}^{*}M-(B+\sigma^{*}B)

in
{\rm Pic}_{{\mathbb{Q}}}(A\times A)
, where
p_{i}:A\times A\to A
are the two projections.

Let
d:A\times A\to A
be the difference map
d(a,b)=a-b
. Then

P\Delta_{A}=Pd^{*}(0)=0.

Since
d^{*}(0)
and
d^{*}(a)
are algebraically equivalent, we conclude that

Pd^{*}(a)=0

for all
a\in A
. Then

(3.10)

(\alpha\times\alpha)^{*}P.\lambda^{*}(a)=(\alpha\times\alpha)^{*}(Pd^{*}(a))=0

for all
a\in A
, where
\lambda=d\circ(\alpha\times\alpha):X\times X\to A
.

We claim that

(3.11)

G^{2m+1}\lambda^{*}(a)=0

for all
a\in A
. This follows from

\displaystyle G^{2m+1}\lambda^{*}(a)

\displaystyle=\big((G-(\alpha\times\alpha)^{*}P)+(\alpha\times\alpha)^{*}P\big)^{2m+1}\lambda^{*}(a)

\displaystyle=(G-(\alpha\times\alpha)^{*}P)^{2m+1}\lambda^{*}(a)\hskip 24.0pt\text{(by \eqref{eq:intersection5})}

\displaystyle=\big(\pi_{1}^{*}(D_{1}+D_{2}-\alpha^{*}M)+\pi_{2}^{*}(D_{1}+D_{2}-\alpha^{*}M)\big)^{2m+1}\lambda^{*}(a)

\displaystyle=0\hskip 24.0pt\text{(by \eqref{eq:intersection4})}.

Since
G
is big and nef on
X\times X
, (
3.11
) implies that
\lambda:X\times X\to A
has relative dimension at most
2m
.
On the other hand, we have

(3.12)

\dim\lambda(X\times X)=\min(\dim A,2\dim\alpha(X))

from Lemma
3.2
. Hence

\displaystyle 2m

\displaystyle\geq\dim(X\times X)-\dim\lambda(X\times X)

\displaystyle=2\dim X-\min(\dim A,2\dim\alpha(X))

and the proposition follows.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.5
.

Suppose that there exists a generically finite projective morphism
f:X\times X\to Y
such that
\dim f(\Delta_{X})=m
. Then it is clear that we have a dominant morphism
X\cong\Delta_{X}\to W=f(\Delta_{X})
. Since
f
contracts
\Delta_{X}
to
W
, it contracts
\Delta_{X_{p}}\subset X_{p}\times X_{p}
to a point for all
p\in W
. By the same argument as for Proposition
3.4
, we see that there exists a big and nef divisor
G
on
X\times X
such that

(3.13)

G\lambda_{p}^{*}(a)=0

for
p\in W
general and all
a\in A
, where
\lambda_{p}:X_{p}\times X_{p}\to A
is the restriction

\lambda:X\times X\to A
to
X_{p}\times X_{p}
.

We claim that
G
is big and nef on
X_{p}\times X_{p}
for
p\in W
general. Otherwise,

G^{2n-2m}.(X_{p}\times X_{p})=0

for
n=\dim X
. Since
X_{p}\times X_{p}
and
X_{p}\times X_{q}
are algebraically equivalent for
p,q\in W
general, we have

G^{2n-2m}.(X_{p}\times X_{q})=0

for
p,q\in W
general.
This is impossible since
G
is big on
X\times X
and
X_{p}\times X_{q}
covers
X\times X
. So
G
is big and nef on
X_{p}\times X_{p}
for
p\in W
general. Combining this with (
3.13
), we see that
\lambda_{p}
maps
X_{p}\times X_{p}
generically finite onto its image. This implies that
\dim X_{p}=\dim\alpha(X_{p})
and
\alpha(X_{p})
is not contained in a translate of any abelian subvariety of
A
of dimension less than
2\dim X_{p}
. To see the latter, note that if
\alpha(X_{p})
were contained in a translate of an abelian subvariety
B\subset A
of dimension less than
2\dim X_{p}
, then the image of
\lambda_{p}
would be contained in the image of the difference map
d:B\times B\to A
, which has dimension at most
\dim B<2\dim X_{p}
.

On the other hand, suppose that
there is a dominant projective morphism
\pi:X\to W
such that

\dim W=m
,
\dim X_{p}=\dim\alpha(X_{p})
and
\alpha(X_{p})
is not contained in a translate of any abelian subvariety of
A
of dimension less than
2\dim X_{p}
for
p\in W
general. Then
\lambda
maps
X_{p}\times X_{p}
generically finite onto its image.

Let us consider
\eta:X\times X\to W\times W\times A
given by

\eta(x_{1},x_{2})=(\pi(x_{1}),\pi(x_{2}),\lambda(x_{1},x_{2})).

Clearly,
\eta
contracts
\Delta_{X}
to
\Delta_{W}\times\{0\}\cong W
. On the other hand, since
\eta
maps the fibre of
X\times X
over
(p,p)\in W\times W
generically finite onto its image, it is generically finite over its image.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.7
.

For the “if” part, we consider the map
\lambda:X\times X\to A
defined in the proof of Proposition
3.4
.

From (
1.5
), the map
d:\alpha(X)\times\alpha(X)\to A
is finite outside of the diagonal
\Delta_{\alpha(X)}
.
And since
\alpha:X\to A
is finite over
\alpha(X)
,
the map

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X\times X\backslash X\times_{A}X=X\times X\backslash\lambda^{-1}(0)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A\backslash\{0\}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\lambda}

is finite. On the other hand, from (
1.4
),

X\times_{A}X=\Delta_{X}\sqcup\Sigma

for a finite set
\Sigma
of points.
It suffices to take
X\times X\xrightarrow{f}Y\to A
to be the Stein factorization of
\lambda
.

For the “only if” part, let
f:X\times X\to Y
be a birational morphism which is an isomorphism outside the diagonal
\Delta_{X}
. We let
D=f^{*}L
for an ample divisor
L
on
Y
.
As in the proof of Proposition
3.4
,
we let

\displaystyle D

\displaystyle=\pi_{1}^{*}D_{1}+\pi_{2}^{*}D_{2}-(\alpha\times\alpha)^{*}B

\displaystyle G

\displaystyle=D+\sigma^{*}D

\displaystyle M

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2}\delta_{A}^{*}(B+\sigma^{*}B)

\displaystyle P

\displaystyle=p_{1}^{*}M+p_{2}^{*}M-(B+\sigma^{*}B).

From (
3.9
),

D_{1}+D_{2}-\alpha^{*}M=0

and hence

G-(\alpha\times\alpha)^{*}P=\pi_{1}^{*}(D_{1}+D_{2}-\alpha^{*}M)+\pi_{2}^{*}(D_{1}+D_{2}-\alpha^{*}M)=0.

In addition, since
f
does not contract any curve outside of
\Delta_{X}
, we have

G.C>0

for all irreducible curves
C\not\subset\Delta_{X}
.

If (
1.4
) fails, then there exists an irreducible curve
C\subset X\times_{A}X=\lambda^{-1}(0)
such that
C\not\subset\Delta_{X}
, which implies

(\alpha\times\alpha)^{*}P.C=G.C>0.

On the other hand, since
(\alpha\times\alpha)(C)\subset\Delta_{A}
and
P\Delta_{A}=0
,
we have

P.(\alpha\times\alpha)_{*}C=0,

which is a contradiction, giving (
1.4
). It is easy to see that this implies that
\alpha:X\to A
is finite and birational over its image.

Similarly, if (
1.5
) fails for some
a\neq 0
, then there exists an irreducible curve
C\subset\lambda^{-1}(a)
and so

(\alpha\times\alpha)^{*}P.C=G.C>0.

On the other hand, since
(\alpha\times\alpha)(C)\subset d^{-1}(a)
and
Pd^{*}(a)=0
,
we have

P.(\alpha\times\alpha)_{*}C=0,

which is a contradiction, giving (
1.5
).
∎
