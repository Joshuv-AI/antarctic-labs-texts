Climatic Influence on COVID‐19: Investigating the Role of Temperature and Humidity in the Spread of the Omicron Variant
Authors: Leonardo López, Alejandro Fontal, Brandon Alfaro, Xavier Rodó
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUnderstanding how climate modulates infectious disease dynamics is critical for anticipating epidemic patterns. After a hot debate on potential facilitation of population spread by climate factors during the first 2 years of the COVID‐19 pandemics, not much attention has been devoted to assess whether the ensuing SARS‐CoV‐2 waves also displayed similar climatic facilitation, or conversely, whether immunity dismissed any such forcing and potential signatures. This study examines the association between climatological variables—specifically temperature and absolute humidity (AH)—and the global incidence of the SARS‐CoV‐2 Omicron variant (B.1.1.529) during its massive epidemic wave (2021–2022). Using global‐scale epidemiological and climate data, we applied the scale‐dependent correlation analysis to identify transient, scale‐specific non‐linear associations between factors and the disease across regions. We found consistent significant local negative correlations between incidence and both temperature (rs=−0.4 to −0.6) and AH (rs=−0.3 to −0.5), particularly in mid‐latitudes during colder months. Threshold analyses revealed temperature and humidity ranges of 12°C–21°C and 8–12 g m−3, respectively, where transmission was most favored, fully in line with previous results obtained for the first COVID‐19 variants. A stochastic population‐based model incorporating a temperature‐modulated infection rate reproduced epidemic dynamics more accurately than constant or seasonal formulations, reducing normalized residuals by approximately 40%–50%. These results largely demonstrate a robust climatic influence also on Omicron transmission yet underscoring the value of integrating environmental drivers into epidemic modeling and early‐warning frameworks.Keywords: Omicron, COVID‐19, SARS‐CoV‐2, climate, temperature, absolute humidity, model, gobal

Introduction

Pandemics have historically shaped human societies, economies, and public health strategies. The Black Death in the 14th century and the Spanish Flu of 1918 are stark reminders of the devastating impact infectious diseases can have on global populations (Piret & Boivin, 2021). More recently, the H1N1 swine flu pandemic of 2009 and especially the COVID‐19 pandemic have further illustrated the profound societal and economic disruptions caused by such outbreaks (Sampath et al., 2021). These events, which claimed millions of lives, have also prompted significant advancements in epidemiology and public health infrastructure, underscoring the importance of preparedness and response strategies in mitigating the effects of future pandemics.

The transmission of respiratory pathogens is strongly shaped by environmental conditions, particularly temperature and humidity, which influence viral stability, aerosol dynamics, and human behavioral responses (Lam et al., 2020; Thomas, 2020). For respiratory viruses such as influenza, a well‐documented enhancement of transmission occurs under cold and dry conditions (Axelsen et al., 2014), and large‐scale atmospheric circulation patterns, such as the East Asian Monsoon, have been shown to shape the spatio‐temporal dynamics and synchronization of seasonal epidemics (Zhang et al., 2022). A similar climatic sensitivity was increasingly recognized during the COVID‐19 pandemic as the airborne nature of SARS‐CoV‐2 gained broad scientific acceptance (Wang et al., 2021).

Research conducted during the first pandemic waves revealed that decreases in temperature and absolute humidity (AH) were associated with increased SARS‐CoV‐2 transmission in midlatitudes (Fontal et al., 2021; Morawska et al., 2020; Morawska & Milton, 2020), while warm‐season surges in some regions were linked to behavioral responses to extreme heat, including the tendency to gather indoors under air conditioning. Although this evidence confirmed the climatic sensitivity of COVID‐19, most studies aggregated cases across different viral lineages and epidemiological phases, limiting the ability to isolate variant‐specific climatic effects.

The emergence of genetic variants of SARS‐CoV‐2, such as those with the D614G mutation, marked a turning point in the pandemic's trajectory (Tao et al., 2021). Variants of concern (VoCs) like Alpha, Beta, Gamma, Delta, and most recently, Omicron, have posed new challenges with their increased transmissibility, altered disease severity, and potential immune evasion capabilities (Choi & Smith, 2021).

The Omicron variant, first detected in Botswana and South Africa in November 2021, quickly spread globally, causing a large surge in cases (Karim & Karim, 2021). Epidemiological data indicated that Omicron exhibited significantly higher transmissibility than the Delta variant (Antonelli et al., 2022). Omicron's estimated basic reproduction number R0 ranges from 9.5 to 10.0, whereas Delta's R0 was around 5.08, and its effective reproduction number also remained systematically higher (Antonelli et al., 2022; Karim & Karim, 2021). This heightened transmissibility prompted the World Health Organization to designate Omicron as a Variant of Concern on 26 November 2021.

Figure S1 in Supporting Information S1 illustrates the phylogenetic relationships among various SARS‐CoV‐2 variants, highlighting the evolutionary paths and connections between different lineages. The Omicron variant (B.1.1.529), marked in yellow, stands out with many sub‐lineages, indicating its significant mutation rate and adaptability. Understanding these evolutionary relationships and the potential impact of climate on the dynamics of the Omicron strain appears crucial, as it helps underscore the role of environmental factors in the spread and evolution of this highly transmissible variant.

While prior global studies identified clear climatic signatures in COVID‐19 transmission (Fontal et al., 2021), they generally aggregated multiple pandemic waves, viral variants, and pre‐vaccination conditions, limiting the attribution of climate sensitivity to specific lineages. More recent analyses have reported nonlinear temperature–incidence relationships, including increased transmission at cold and hot extremes (Magers et al., 2023; Usmani et al., 2022), although they similarly lacked variant‐level resolution.

Building on this literature, the present study focuses exclusively on the global wave dominated by the Omicron variant (B.1.1.529) during 2021–2022, a period characterized by high transmissibility and widespread population immunity. During the study period (late 2021–2022), global genomic surveillance data indicate that Omicron (B.1.1.529) and its sublineages accounted for more than 90−95% of reported SARS‐CoV‐2 sequences worldwide, with Delta and other variants contributing only marginally at the beginning of the wave (Khare et al., 2021). We combine a scale‐dependent correlation (SDC) framework (Rodó & Rodríguez‐Arias, 2006) with a stochastic compartmental model incorporating temperature‐ and humidity‐dependent transmission rates. This integrated statistical–mechanistic approach allows us to identify transient, geographically specific climate–incidence associations and to directly evaluate the mechanistic contribution of climatic variability to Omicron transmission dynamics in the post‐vaccination phase of the pandemic.

Methods

Data

The circulation of strains was obtained from the GISAID hCoV‐19 data set. GISAID, a global scientific initiative established in 2008, provides open access to genomic data for influenza viruses and SARS‐CoV‐2. It has been instrumental in the COVID‐19 response, offering the first complete SARS‐CoV‐2 genome sequences on 10 January 2020. GISAID data includes virus genetic sequences, which are crucial for tracking variants like Omicron B.1.1.529.

As illustrated in Figure S2 in Supporting Information S1, the B.1.1.529 variant, first sequenced in November 2021, rapidly became dominant within a month (starting in the penultimate week of December 2021). Its peak prevalence occurred from January 2022 to November 2022, after which sequencing data indicated a decline in its predominance by the last week of January 2023 (Figure S2a in Supporting Information S1). The timeline marking the onset and subsequent decline of Omicron's prevalence closely aligns with global trends in daily COVID‐19 cases (Figure S2b in Supporting Information S1). This study will specifically examine this critical period. The gray shaded area in the figure denotes the standard deviation around the global mean of daily new cases per 100,000 individuals, providing insights into the variability among countries during the specified timeframe.

ERA5, the fifth generation of ECMWF atmospheric reanalysis, provides comprehensive global climate data from 1940 to the present through the Copernicus Climate Change Service (C3S) API. For this study, data for 2 m Dewpoint Temperature, 2m Temperature, Mean Sea Level Pressure, and Total Precipitation were extracted and processed into daily averages. Additionally, Relative Humidity and AH were derived, offering insights into climate impacts on virus capacity of transmission.

NASA's Socioeconomic Data and Applications Center (SEDAC) provides the Gridded Population of the World (GPW), version 4, which offers high‐resolution global population density data, including for the year 2020. This data is used to compute weighted averages of meteorological variables per regional unit based on population density, ensuring accurate reflection of the impact on COVID‐19 transmission at the population level.

Our World in Data (OWID) provides detailed data on COVID‐19, including confirmed cases, deaths, tests, and vaccinations, sourced from the WHO COVID‐19 Dashboard. Data preprocessing involves generating a complete daily series by assuming an equal distribution of weekly reported cases and calculating a centered 7‐day rolling average to smooth out daily variations, as the study addresses weekly‐scale variations.

Statistics and Mathematical Methods

Scale‐Dependent Correlation (SDC) Analysis

To identify transient associations between climatological variables and COVID‐19 incidence, we used the scale‐dependent correlation (SDC) analysis Rodó and Rodríguez‐Arias (2006); Fontal et al. (2021). SDC is a time series method designed to detect localized, short‐lived correlations at varying temporal scales. This allows us to uncover transient relationships that may be nonlinear and be embedded and unrecognizable by traditional correlation metrics.

The analysis involves an iterative extraction of fragments of fixed length s from two time series (e.g., temperature and incidence), and enables the computation of their correlation using Pearson's, Kendall or Spearman's method. A randomization test with m permutations is then used to determine the statistical significance of observed correlations. Results are displayed as time–scale correlation maps (SDC plots), where significant associations are visualized across different time windows and fragment sizes (See Text S2 in Supporting Information S1).

Key methodological parameters such as fragment length, correlation type, and the treatment of significance thresholds were calibrated based on sensitivity analyses. Full algorithmic details and mathematical formulations are provided in Supporting Information S1.

Population‐Based Model

To complement the statistical analysis, we implemented a population‐based stochastic compartmental model to simulate COVID‐19 dynamics under the influence of climatological variables. The model builds upon a modified SEIR framework, extending it into an SEIQRDPV structure to incorporate vaccination stages, protected populations, and a temperature‐modulated infection rate.

The infection rate is modeled as a time‐ and temperature‐dependent function, allowing for seasonal and climatic effects to influence disease spread. This formulation enables the evaluation of how fluctuations in ambient temperature modulate the force of infection over time. Model compartments include vaccinated individuals at various dose levels, detected and undetected infections, recovered, deceased, and protected populations.

Results

This section presents the findings from both the statistical (SDC) and mechanistic (SEIQRDPV) modeling analyses. Results are organized in three parts: (a) large‐scale spatiotemporal associations between climate variables and COVID‐19 incidence; (b) identification of temperature and humidity thresholds at finer scales; and (c) model validation under different infection rate assumptions.

Global and Regional Correlations Between Climate and Omicron Incidence

To assess the potential relationship between temperature (T) and AH (AH) and COVID‐19 incidence during the predominance of the Omicron B.1.1.529 variant, a two‐way SDC analysis was conducted. This analysis considered daily new COVID‐19 cases across different regions from 19 December 2021 to 31 January 2023.

The SDC method used a temporal window size s=105 days and varying lags between the COVID‐19 and climate variable time series (0–21 days). The resulting plots (Figures 1 and 2) display scale‐dependent correlations grouped by latitude and longitude for Temperature (T) and AH.

Negative correlations occur largely in synchrony across countries during the rise of Omicron, as T and AH fall, and revert as they rise during the waning phase. This synchrony across distinct climate regimes reinforces the consistency of the observed climatic signal.

To identify threshold ranges of T and AH associated with significant correlations, we analyzed all Italian regional data using a window size s=14 days. Figures 3b and 3c show the distributions of correlation magnitudes and signs across climate ranges.

The peaks in these distributions highlight thresholds where climate influence is strongest: 12−21°C for temperature and 8−12g/m3 for AH. These results identify environmental conditions most conducive to Omicron spread, consistent with prior waves of SARS‐CoV‐2.

Climate‐Modulated SEIQRDPV Model Validation

To evaluate a potential mechanistic consistency, we fitted the SEIQRDPV model under three infection‐rate assumptions: (a) constant, (b) seasonal sinusoidal, and (c) temperature‐modulated. Full model details and parameter calibration procedures are provided in Supporting Information S1.

The temperature‐modulated model (Figure 4a) achieved the best performance, reproducing observed dynamics more accurately at both global and continental scales. Compared to both the constant and seasonal models, it reduced normalized errors by approximately 75%, showcasing how temperature acts as a robust modulator of the Omicron transmission. Although the absolute‐humidity model also performed well (not shown), temperature yielded lower residuals and higher stability, confirming its stronger and more consistent influence.

Model performance under (a) temperature‐dependent, (b) constant, and (c) seasonal infection rates. Each panel (a–c) shows: global active cases (top; model: blue, data: orange), normalized error (middle), and regional dynamics (bottom; model: solid, data: dashed). The temperature‐dependent approach (a) clearly shows the best fit to the observed Omicron wave (2021 September–2022 September).

Quantitatively, the SDC analysis revealed consistent negative associations between daily COVID‐19 incidence and both temperature and AH across most regions. The median Spearman correlation coefficient ranged between −0.4 and −0.6 for temperature and between −0.3 and −0.5 for AH, with approximately 65% of the analyzed time windows exhibiting statistically significant negative relationships (Figures 1 and 2). At the country level, these patterns were synchronous across mid‐latitude European countries (Figure 3a). The analysis of Italian regional data further identified robust thresholds for climate sensitivity: temperature values between 12 and 21°C and AH levels between 8 and 12g/m3 were associated with the strongest negative correlations between climatic conditions and case incidence (Figures 3b and 3c). These ranges delineate environmental conditions that favor the propagation of the Omicron variant at the population level and confirm similar ranges to previous circulating strains. Finally, model performance metrics confirmed the relevance of temperature as a dominant external driver. The temperature‐dependent model achieved the best fit, reducing normalized residual errors by roughly 40–50% compared to the constant‐rate formulation and outperforming the seasonal model across all continents (Figures 4b and 4c). This quantitative improvement substantiates the observed statistical associations and provides mechanistic support for a temperature‐modulated transmission dynamic during the Omicron wave.

Discussion and Conclusions

Our findings reinforce the evidence that meteorological conditions, particularly temperature and humidity, played a measurable role in modulating SARS‐CoV‐2 transmission dynamics.

However, in contrast to previous studies that examined general COVID‐19 incidence across all variants (Magers et al., 2023; Usmani et al., 2022), our analysis isolates the Omicron wave and quantifies its distinct climatic sensitivity under the epidemiological context of widespread immunity and high viral transmissibility.

Unlike our previous global study (Fontal et al., 2021), which analyzed multiple pandemic waves to detect broad climatic signatures, this study focuses on a single dominant variant causing the largest pandemic wave in a context of acquired immunity and integrates both a SDC approach and a temperature‐modulated stochastic model. During this period, genomic surveillance maintained sufficiently high global coverage to resolve Omicron's emergence and predominance. After the aforementioned period, a marked decline in sequencing efforts has been documented globally, complicating the detection of emerging variants and limiting the reliability of variant‐resolved epidemiological analyses (Uraki et al., 2025).

This dual framework not only captures the transient nature of climatic effects but also mechanistically evaluates their contribution to epidemic trajectories, providing a more refined understanding of climate–virus interactions in the post‐vaccination phase of the pandemic.

By explicitly distinguishing between statistical correlations and mechanistic causation, the present work contributes to bridge the gap between empirical climate‐epidemiological observations and theoretical transmission modeling, helping to contextualize the seasonal behavior of emerging respiratory viruses under evolving climatic and social conditions.

The research into the relationship between climate variables, specifically temperature (T) and AH, and COVID‐19 incidence during the predominance of the Omicron B.1.1.529 variant reveals significant strong correlations and highlights the same role described to be exerted by physical conditions on the spread and viability of other viruses attached to aerosol particles as potentially crucial factors. The analysis conducted from 19 December 2021, to 31 January 2023 as well as the fitted COVID‐19 models, both indicate that climatic factors played again a crucial role in modulating COVID‐19 transmission at the population level.

In the Northern Hemisphere, strong transient negative correlations were observed during the initial rise of Omicron in late 2021, while positive correlations were detected during the warmer months. This pattern indicates a U‐shaped complex interaction between climate and virus transmission dynamics, which we describe below (Usmani et al., 2022). The synchrony in negative correlations across different European Union countries during the rise and fall of the Omicron wave reinforces the consistency of climatic influence on COVID‐19 spread across various spatial and temporal scales.

Furthermore, identifying specific thresholds for temperature and AH, where transmission effects are most pronounced, emphasizes the importance of these climatic parameters. These findings suggest that public health strategies should consider meteorological conditions, especially by improving indoor ventilation and adapting control measures according to climate variations.

Alongside the application of the SEIQRDPV model, which incorporates temperature as an infection rate modulator, effectively reproduced the real dynamics worldwide, even with the large heterogeneity observed in the data sets. The model demonstrated considerable stability within the fitted infection rate values, validating its robustness and reliability. This stability underscores the model's capability to accurately predict infection trends, even under varying climatic conditions and data uncertainties.

The observed climatic modulation of Omicron transmission can be explained by a combination of biological and behavioral mechanisms that operate across the temperature–humidity spectrum. Biologically, lower temperatures and reduced AH generally enhance the environmental stability and infectivity of respiratory viruses, including SARS‐CoV‐2, in aerosols and on surfaces, while certain warm extremes can also preserve infectivity via droplet‐mediated persistence under specific conditions. Experimental work supports these patterns: studies of viral persistence and aerosol physics show that cold, dry conditions favor droplet evaporation into small aerosols that remain airborne longer, and that viral lipid envelopes and infectivity persist for longer at lower temperatures and moderate relative humidities (Bushmaker et al., 2023; Hirose et al., 2022; Schuit et al., 2020; Smither et al., 2020; van Doremalen et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2023).

There is strong empirical support for airborne transmission of multiple respiratory viruses (SARS‐CoV, MERS‐CoV, influenza, rhinovirus, RSV), and controlled studies indicate that aerosols can be the dominant transmission route in many settings (Cowling et al., 2013; Kulkarni et al., 2016; Yan et al., 2018). Animal experiments and environmental sampling similarly document air‐borne spread of SARS‐CoV‐2 under conditions that exclude direct contact or large‐droplet transmission (Kutter et al., 2021; Lednicky et al., 2020, 2021; Shi et al., 2020; Sia et al., 2020). Aerodynamic measurements and particle sizing show that the majority of exhaled particles are in the respirable size range (<5μm, with a substantial fraction <1μm), and viral RNA and occasionally infectious virus have been recovered from aerosols across a wide size spectrum (0.25–>4 μm) in clinical and environmental studies (Chia et al., 2020; Guo et al., 2020; Lednicky et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2020; Santarpia et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2021). Laboratory aerosolization experiments also report aerosolized SARS‐CoV‐2 half‐lives on the order of 1−3hours under some conditions (Schuit et al., 2020; Smither et al., 2020; van Doremalen et al., 2020).

With respect to Omicron specifically, several investigations report increased environmental persistence of VoC and greater survival of some Omicron sublineages (e.g., BA.1, BA.5) at low temperatures and moderate relative humidity (40−60%), including on common packaging and surface materials, which is consistent with the climatic sensitivity we observe in population‐level incidence (Bushmaker et al., 2023; Hirose et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2023).

This is consistent with experimental evidence showing that SARS‐CoV‐2 VoC exhibit variable but generally enhanced persistence on different materials, including human skin and stainless steel, particularly under low temperature and moderate humidity conditions (Hirose et al., 2021; Onianwa et al., 2022; Pottage et al., 2021).

Behavioral mechanisms act in parallel. Cold weather promotes indoor congregation, prolonged close contacts, and reduced ventilation, amplifying aerosol accumulation and transmission risk; conversely, extreme heat may also push people indoors with air conditioning, increasing contact density though typically to a lesser extent than cold periods (Moriyama et al., 2020). Taken together, these biological and behavioral pathways, supported by experimental, animal, environmental, and epidemiological evidence, provide a coherent mechanistic explanation for the transient negative correlations between temperature/humidity and Omicron incidence observed in our SDC analyses and for the improved performance of the temperature‐modulated transmission model.

Overall, these results support the existence of strong external drivers of COVID‐19 transmission, similar to the seasonality observed in influenza and other coronaviruses. The significant ‐albeit locally transient‐ correlations between temperature, AH, and COVID‐19 incidence underscore the transient but influential role of climate in virus spread. These findings align with previous studies, despite methodological differences, and highlight the need for incorporating meteorological parameters into public health planning.

Future public health prevention strategies could highly benefit from tailored climate services and early‐warning systems to better manage current waves of COVID‐19 transmission and other similar viruses, especially as new variants with higher transmissibility and immune evasion capabilities emerge. The persistence of a climatic influence on COVID‐19 transmission, irrespective of the variant under investigation, further reinforces the necessity for ongoing public health interventions to include meteorological indicators as a promising way to anticipate impacts and help mitigate the impact of the virus effectively. These insights emphasize that public health strategies must remain adaptive and incorporate climatic factors to respond to the evolving challenges posed by COVID‐19 new variants and potentially also to the threats of new respiratory viruses. Strengthening genomic surveillance remains essential, as recent analyses warn that reduced sequencing efforts since 2023 have already hindered the detection of emerging variants and may challenge future efforts to integrate climatic, immunological, and viral evolutionary information in early‐warning systems (Uraki et al., 2025).

Our findings align with a growing body of evidence linking meteorological conditions to the seasonality of respiratory infections beyond COVID‐19. Recent studies on influenza dynamics have shown that variations in temperature and humidity modulate epidemic intensity and timing at global and regional scales. For instance, Ruan et al. (2025) demonstrated that gradual global warming can reshape the amplitude and frequency of influenza epidemics, while Liang et al. (2023) quantified how meteorological variability drives the timing of influenza peaks worldwide. Similarly, Zhang et al. (2022) identified the influence of the East Asian monsoon system on the spatiotemporal patterns of influenza activity in China. Together, these studies highlight that the climatic modulation of viral transmission is not unique to SARS‐CoV‐2 but part of a broader, climate‐sensitive framework of epidemic dynamics. Our analysis of the Omicron variant extends this knowledge by confirming that even highly transmissible variants remain influenced by temperature and humidity fluctuations in partially acquired immune settings, supporting the climatic imprint hypothesis across multiple respiratory pathogens.
