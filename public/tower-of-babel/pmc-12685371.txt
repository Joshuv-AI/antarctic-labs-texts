Socially focused intelligent assistive technologies for caregiving for homebound older adults with cognitive impairment: a scoping review
Authors: Juliet Chigozie Donatus Ezulike, Mohit Prashar, Kosisochukwu Anyaegbunam, Blessing Ugochi Ojembe, Shital Desai, Michael Ebe Kalu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectivesBeing homebound with cognitive impairment (CI) presents major public health challenges, increasing home healthcare costs and contributing to caregiver burden, social isolation, and reduced quality of life. As loneliness and social isolation rise among this population, socially focused intelligent assistive technologies (SFIATs) have emerged as increasingly viable solutions. This scoping review examined the current body of literature on SFIATs and the barriers and facilitators to their support of homebound older adults with CI and their caregivers.Research Design and MethodsUsing Arksey and O’Malley’s framework, we searched 12 databases with MeSH terms related to older adults, CI, SFIATs, and homebound status. Data were analyzed descriptively using themes, with findings mapped across the socioecological framework.ResultsNineteen studies conducted in 12 high-income countries were included. Robots, tablets, telephones, computers, virtual avenues, and other smart devices were among the SFIATs utilized in caregiving for older individuals who were homebound or had CI. SFIATs facilitated social interaction, engagement, and connectedness among older adults and caregivers. Challenges and benefits associated with their use were evident at individual, interpersonal, community, organizational, and policy levels.Discussion and ImplicationsResearch suggests significant potential in SFIATs, but their implementation faces multi-level challenges, often due to limited direct input from end-users, leading to concerns that impact their utilization. SFIAT development must adopt co-creation approaches to ensure its contextual appropriateness. Further research is needed, particularly in low- and middle-income countries, to understand the landscape, benefits, and challenges of SFIATs in diverse global settings.

This review explored the current body of literature on socially focused intelligent assistive technologies (SFIATs) (e.g., robots, tablets, telephones, and virtual avenues) utilized in caregiving for homebound older adults with cognitive impairment. Among many benefits, these technologies enhanced social engagement for older adults and their caregivers. However, barriers, including affordability and ethical concerns about deception and infantilization, present challenges that can limit the use of these technologies. Adopting participatory approaches and designing SFIATs through the lens of systems thinking, along with a human-centered perspective, is important. Further research is necessary in low- and middle-income countries.

The worldwide population of individuals aged 65 and above is projected to reach 1.6 billion by the year 2050 United Nations (2023). As the global population undergoes rapid aging, the prevalence of chronic health conditions and functional impairments is increasing significantly, leading to a growing population of older adults who are homebound. Homebound status has been defined in various ways, including the frequency of leaving the home (typically less than once per week) (Lee et al., 2022), the need for assistance to leave the home (Ko & Noh, 2021), mobility limitations indicating substantial restrictions (Negrón-Blanco et al., 2016), and social factors such as loneliness and social isolation (Qiu et al., 2010).

The prevalence of homebound status among older adults varies substantially depending on the specific definition used. In developed countries, the proportion of individuals aged 65 years and above classified as homebound ranges from approximately 3.5% to 39% when defined by the frequency of leaving the home, typically less than once per week (Lee et al., 2022). Globally, the prevalence of homebound status among older adults aged 65+ is estimated to be between 1.6% and 4.7%, rising to 60% for those aged 85 years and older, with higher rates observed in rural areas and among women (Negrón-Blanco et al., 2016). Similarly, in developed nations like the United States, the prevalence of homebound status ranges from 5.6% to 19.6% when defined by needing assistance to leave the home (Ko & Noh, 2021). Homebound older individuals frequently experience multiple chronic health conditions and an elevated risk of hospitalization and mortality (Soones et al., 2017; Vu et al., 2013). Additionally, systematic reviews have indicated that homebound older adults face a greater likelihood of developing functional limitations, depressive symptoms, and ­cognitive impairments (CIs) (Niculescu et al., 2021; Xiang et al., 2020).

CI, such as dementia, Alzheimer’s disease, and mild cognitive impairment (MCI), are prevalent among homebound older adults, affecting approximately 29% of the homebound older adult population (Qiu et al., 2010). Dementia is characterized by a progressive decline in cognitive functioning and behavioral abilities to a degree that significantly impairs an individual’s activities and daily life (National Institute on Aging, 2023). Its severity ranges from the initial stage, where it begins to affect a person’s functioning, to the most advanced stage when the individual becomes entirely dependent on others for assistance with fundamental daily activities (National Institute on Aging, 2023). Similarly, Alzheimer’s disease, characterized by the accumulation of amyloid plaques and neurofibrillary tangles in the brain, is a specific and most prevalent type of dementia (U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2020). However, MCI is a condition characterized by observable decrements in cognitive capacities, including memory and reasoning abilities, representing an intermediate state between the cognitive changes associated with typical aging and the onset of dementia (Anand & Schoo, 2024).

CI represents a significant and growing subset within the homebound older adult demographic. The average prevalence of Alzheimer’s disease and related dementias and MCI among homebound older adults aged 65 years and older is 80% and 18.9%, respectively, across various world regions, with individual study prevalence rates ranging from 7.7% to 42% (Ornstein et al., 2015; Petersen et al., 2014). CI can lead to reduced independence, decreased quality of life, and an increased risk of developing more severe forms of dementia, necessitating greater care and supervision for homebound older adults (Ren et al., 2018; Stites et al., 2018). As the global population ages, the incidence of CI and the number of homebound older adults are projected to rise, underscoring the need for comprehensive care strategies targeted at this vulnerable population.

The dual trends of homebound status and CI pose substantial public health challenges and escalate home healthcare costs, contributing to issues, such as caregiver burden, elevated rates of depression, social isolation, and diminished quality of life among older adults and their caregivers (Ornstein et al., 2015; Qiu et al., 2010). Caregivers of homebound individuals and persons with CI confront significant challenges, including physical strain, psychological stress, and financial costs associated with providing unpaid, home-based care (National Institute on Aging, 2023; Petersen et al., 2014; Qiu et al., 2010; U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2020). The Alzheimer’s Association (2024) reported that in 2023, caregivers of people with dementia, including MCI, provided an estimated 18.6 billion hours of unpaid care worth roughly $244 billion. This underscores the substantial economic and social burden placed on families and caregivers. Consequently, understanding how to effectively support homebound older adults with CI is crucial in improving their overall well-being and reducing the burden on caregivers and the healthcare system.

Following the growing loneliness and social isolation experienced among homebound older adults with CI, technologically focused strategies have emerged as increasingly viable solutions for support and to alleviate the burden on the social care system, both financially and in terms of human resources (Rossi et al., 2020). Socially focused intelligent assistive technologies (SFIATs), referred to as a class of technological solutions designed to facilitate and enhance social interaction, communication, and engagement for individuals with disabilities (Cano et al., 2021), are one of the promising emerging technologies to support this population and their caregivers. They include such a range of solutions as cognitive training tools, monitoring systems, social robots, and communication aids (Chan & Nejat, 2011; Hedman et al., 2016; Ke et al., 2020; Law et al., 2019; Tiersen et al., 2021; Wu et al., 2021). Their primary objectives are to enhance independence, safety, and social connectedness for homebound older adults with CI. For instance, while assistive robots can provide support with daily tasks, improve safety through monitoring, and offer therapeutic interventions targeting cognitive, physical, and social well-being (Law et al., 2019), cognitive intervention technologies, such as computer-based and robot-assisted programs, have demonstrated the potential to promote healthy aging and assist with daily activities for older adults experiencing CI (Hedman et al., 2016).

The field of SFIATs is broad and somewhat fragmented, posing challenges for caregivers and healthcare providers in identifying and recommending appropriate solutions for homebound older adults and older persons with CI. The existing literature reveals a significant gap in studies that focus specifically on the use of SFIATs in the care of homebound older adults with CI. Globally, the population of older individuals living with CI is projected to increase exponentially to 152 million cases by 2050, with a significant majority residing in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) (Nichols & Vos, 2021). Similarly, while there exists a research gap on the prevalence of homebound status among older adults in LMICs, evidence from studies in high-income countries (HICs) indicates that the proportion of homebound older persons is also rising due to chronic illness, cognitive decline, and frailty, among other factors (Ko & Noh, 2021; Lee et al., 2022; Negrón-Blanco et al., 2016). The demographic and epidemiological shift highlights the urgent need for innovative solutions such as SFIATs to support the growing population of older persons who are homebound and experiencing CI. While some research has examined related topics on SFIATs separately, that is, either among homebound older adults only or older adults with CI only (Collette et al., 2021; Gadbois et al., 2022; Jiménez et al., 2021; Zhan et al., 2024), comprehensive studies addressing both domains are scarce. Consequently, a thorough scoping review is warranted to map the available literature on SFIATs and their application in the care of homebound older adults with CI. This scoping review thus aimed to synthesize the existing evidence, identify key themes and gaps within the literature, and provide a comprehensive foundational framework to guide future research, clinical practice, and policy development in this area.

To guide this review, we used Bronfenbrenner’s (1979) Social Ecological Model, which sees social and health outcomes as being influenced by multiple levels, including individual, interpersonal, organizational, community, and policy contexts. This highlights the complex interaction between individuals and their environment. The framework is especially relevant for understanding SFIATs, as their impact and adoption depend on individual ability as well as mediating factors like caregiver relationships, healthcare systems, design features, and broader structural conditions. Situating our synthesis within this model facilitates a comprehensive mapping of the challenges and opportunities of SFIATs, allowing for an examination of how these technologies can be integrated into the daily lives of homebound older persons with CI.

Method

Scoping review design

This scoping review followed Arksey and O’Malley (2005)’s methodological framework and was reported following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses extension for Scoping Review flowchart (Tricco et al., 2018). This review was registered at https://osf.io/8jkps/resources.

Identifying the research questions

The primary research questions that guided this review were (a) What socially focused intelligent assistive technologies are currently available to support homebound older adults with CI? (b) What evidence exists regarding the impact of socially focused intelligent assistive technologies on the social engagement of homebound older adults with CI? (c) What are the key challenges and benefits associated with the use of socially focused intelligent assistive technologies for caring for homebound older adults with CI?

Identifying relevant studies

We developed a comprehensive search strategy in consultation with a social and health sciences librarian to identify relevant studies. The search utilized both keywords and standardized medical subject headings (MeSH terms) associated with the following concepts: older adults, CI, socially focused intelligent assistive technologies, and homebound status (see Table 1 for detailed terms). The search strategy was adapted for relevant electronic databases, including Ovid MEDLINE, Embase, APA PsycINFO, AMED (Allied and Complementary Medicine), Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, CINAHL, AgeLine, AgeInfo, Sociological Abstracts, Social Service Abstracts, and Web of Science. The aim was to gather a diverse array of publications relevant to the topics of older adults, CI, SFIATs, and homebound status.

Selecting studies

The study selection procedure was carried out in Covidence, comprising two stages, including title/abstract screening and full-text screening. Two independent reviewers screened the title and abstract in Covidence, applying predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria to each article.

Charting the data

The data were charted and organized into an Excel spreadsheet, adapted from a previous review (Kalu et al., 2021) but modified to align with the research questions of this current review. The relevant data extracted included the following key elements: study information (author name(s), publication year, country, research aim(s), study design, participant characteristics [e.g., gender, age, cognitive status, and living situation], sample size, and data collection methods); technology features (type of SFIATs, its design features, and technological capabilities); and outcomes assessed (social engagement, technology’s usability, user experience [including challenges and facilitators], impact on well-being and quality of life [we extracted mean scores, or mean difference, and effect size to determine impact], user satisfaction [presented in percentage]), and future research statement. Three reviewers independently conducted a pilot data extraction exercise, after which they convened to discuss and resolve any discrepancies. A fourth reviewer was consulted to provide input and facilitate consensus when there was a persistent disagreement.

Collocating, summarizing, and reporting the results

We provided a narrative synthesis of the key findings from the included studies, offering a descriptive summary of the available evidence relevant to the research questions. The social-ecological model (Bronfenbrenner, 1979) guided our analysis, emphasizing the dynamic interplay between individual, interpersonal, organizational, community, and policy-level factors that influence health behaviors and outcomes. We selected this model due to its relevance in understanding the complex interplay of factors that shape the use and impact of assistive technologies in home-based care settings. This framework enabled us to map the challenges and benefits associated with the use of SFIATs by homebound older adults and older persons with CI, as well as their caregivers, across multiple levels of influence. Two reviewers independently mapped the identified challenges and benefits from each of the included articles onto the different levels of the Social Ecological Model. Both met in a research meeting with three other authors to merge their mapping, and any discrepancies were discussed and resolved in the research meeting. The narrative synthesis encompassed key areas, including the nature and scope of SFIATs designed to support homebound older adults and persons with CI and their caregivers; the outcomes and impacts of these technologies as evaluated in the included studies; and the challenges and benefits associated with the use of SFIATs in home-based care settings. As this was a scoping review, the use of a critical appraisal tool and formal ethics approval was not required (Arksey & O’Malley, 2005).

Results

A total of 1,044 studies were initially identified through database searches. After removing 200 duplicate entries, 844 studies underwent title and abstract screening with 712 studies excluded, leaving 132 studies that underwent full-text screening. Of the 132 studies, 112 were excluded for several reasons listed in Figure 1, with only 19 studies included in the final analysis.

Study population

The 19 included studies targeted diverse older adult populations, with varying criteria related to homebound status and CI. Specifically, 13 studies focused on individuals with or without CI (Blekou et al., 2022; Boger et al., 2014; Damant et al., 2024; FakhrHosseini et al., 2020; Flynn et al., 2024a, 2024b; Gaber et al., 2020; Goodman-Casanova et al., 2020; Kim et al., 2025; Prophater et al., 2021; Shin et al., 2022; Wu et al., 2014; Zuschnegg et al., 2025), two targeted older adults with both limited mobility (homebound) and CI (Fowler et al., 2016; Khosla et al., 2021), while one focused on only homebound or semi-homebound older adults (Choi et al., 2024).

Furthermore, the data were collected across various populations including caregivers (Damant et al., 2024; Fowler et al., 2016; Schmitz & Becker, 2024), older adults (Choi et al., 2024; Gaber et al., 2020; Goodman-Casanova et al., 2020; Khosla et al., 2021; Kim et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2014; Zuschnegg et al., 2025), a combination of older adults and their caregivers (Blekou et al., 2022; FakhrHosseini et al., 2020; Flynn et al., 2024a, 2024b; Wangmo et al., 2024), across older adults, care partners, and other stakeholders (Felber et al., 2024; Prophater et al., 2021; Shin et al., 2022) and among caregivers and health professionals (Boger et al., 2014).

Study design

The 19 studies included in this review comprised eight qualitative studies (Boger et al., 2014; Damant et al., 2024; Felber et al., 2024; Flynn et al., 2024a, 2024b; Schmitz & Becker, 2024; Shin et al., 2022; Wangmo et al., 2024), six mixed-methods (FakhrHosseini et al., 2020; Fowler et al., 2016; Gaber et al., 2020; Khosla et al., 2021; Wu et al., 2014; Zuschnegg et al., 2025), and five quantitative studies (Blekou et al., 2022; Choi et al., 2024; Goodman-Casanova et al., 2020; Kim et al., 2025; Prophater et al., 2021). Among the qualitative studies, two stated a qualitative exploratory design (Schmitz & Becker, 2024; Wangmo et al., 2024), two adopted a participatory action research design (Flynn et al., 2024a, 2024b), while the four other studies did not state any specific research designs (Boger et al., 2014; Damant et al., 2024; Felber et al., 2024; Shin et al., 2022).

Of the six mixed-methods studies, three explicitly described the integration of qualitative and quantitative components (FakhrHosseini et al., 2020; Khosla et al., 2021; Zuschnegg et al., 2025), while the other three studies (Fowler et al., 2016; Gaber et al., 2020; Wu et al., 2014) did not explicitly elaborate on their integration strategies. Additionally, one mixed-method study was a randomized control trial (RCT). Among the five quantitative studies, three stated that they adopted a cross-sectional design (Blekou et al., 2022; Choi et al., 2024; Goodman-Casanova et al., 2020), one was an RCT (Kim et al., 2025), and one, a community-based evaluation (Prophater et al., 2021).

Types of SFIATs used in the included studies

Robots

Some articles (n = 8) identified social robots as assistive technologies used for caregiving (FakhrHosseini et al., 2020; Felber et al., 2024; Khosla et al., 2021; Kim et al., 2025; Shin et al., 2022; Wu et al., 2014; Zuschnegg et al., 2025). These were humanoid robots (Zuschnegg et al., 2025), mobile telepresence robots (FakhrHosseini et al., 2020; Shin et al., 2022), socially assistive robots -SAR (Felber et al., 2024; Khosla et al., 2021; Kim et al., 2025; Wangmo et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2014). The SAR had various shapes, such as those of a doll (Kim et al., 2025), an animal (Wangmo et al., 2024), as well as other unspecified forms. The identified studies highlighted the robots’ capacity to enhance social interaction, facilitate activities, provide companionship (FakhrHosseini et al., 2020; Felber et al., 2024; Khosla et al., 2021; Kim et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2014; Zuschnegg et al., 2025), and alleviate caregiver burden (Shin et al., 2022). Some specific robot features mentioned include providing cognitive task support, such as reminders and quizzes (Khosla et al., 2021) and enabling communication through video or voice commands to reduce caregiver burden (Shin et al., 2022).

Tablets

Six studies identified tablets as one of the groups of assistive technological solutions for caregiving that provide social engagement for their users. With voice and video call features, alongside other internet-based applications, this assistive technology is used in delivering interactive content that helps reduce social isolation, empowers older individuals to live more actively and independently, improves emotional well-being, and enhances social engagement between and among older adults, their caregivers, family members, and other important stakeholders (Blekou et al., 2022; Choi et al., 2024; Damant et al., 2024; Flynn et al., 2024a; Prophater et al., 2021; Zuschnegg et al., 2025).

Televisions, computers, telephones

Five articles listed these assistive technologies for caregiving (Boger et al., 2014; Choi et al., 2024; Damant et al., 2024; Flynn et al., 2024a; Gaber et al., 2020; Goodman-Casanova et al., 2020). One study identified the use of telephones and television in the context of telehealth to provide health and social support services to older adults (Goodman-Casanova et al., 2020). These SFIATs stimulated cognition through stimulus memory games, promoted physical activity through the visualization of a video of indoor home exercise (Damant et al., 2024; Goodman-Casanova et al., 2020), and facilitated communication and social connectedness with healthcare professionals and loved ones through calls (Damant et al., 2024; Flynn et al., 2024a).

Virtual avenues

We mapped four articles that identified virtual avenues to enhance social connectivity and facilitate information exchange for the target population (Damant et al., 2024; Flynn et al., 2024b; Fowler et al., 2016; Schmitz & Becker, 2024). In Fowler et al. (2016), virtual support avenues, including websites, were used to provide support to caregivers. Similarly, Schmitz and Becker (2024) highlighted the use of a chatbot in an information platform to provide support to dementia caregivers. Flynn et al. (2024b)’s study reported that head-mounted display and handheld controllers were used to provide a multi-user virtual reality environment, providing a social connecting space for caregivers and their care recipients living with dementia. Social media platforms such as Facebook, WhatsApp, and LinkedIn, among others, were also technology-informed strategies used to enhance social connectedness among older people and their carers in Damant et al. (2024).

Other information communication technologies (ICTs)

Four studies (Boger et al., 2014; Damant et al., 2024; Felber et al., 2024; Gaber et al., 2020) identified various other ICTs with communication and social connectedness features embedded in them. They include smart speakers and wrist watches, email, a DVD player, apps with video call features (Damant et al., 2024), ambient sensors (Felber et al., 2024), radio (Boger et al., 2014; Gaber et al., 2020), and other forms of everyday technology.

Impact of SFIATs on social engagement

Participants in 11 included articles reported SFIATs’ enhancement of their social engagement, interaction, and connectedness (Choi et al., 2024; Damant et al., 2024; FakhrHosseini et al., 2020; Felber et al., 2024; Flynn et al., 2024a, 2024b; Khosla et al., 2021; Prophater et al., 2021; Shin et al., 2022; Wangmo et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2014). Few studies (n = 5) went on to assess user satisfaction or perceived usefulness of the SFIATs (Flynn et al., 2024b; Khosla et al., 2021; Kim et al., 2025; Prophater et al., 2021; Shin et al., 2022; Wu et al., 2014), revealing varying levels of acceptance and engagement.

Shin et al. (2022) reported satisfaction ratings for robot use across caregivers (81%), clinicians (74%), and individuals with MCI (64%). Likewise, Prophater et al. (2021) evaluated the iN2L tablets, which received high ratings from staff members for reducing loneliness (mean rating: 4.28/5) and improving mood (mean rating: 4.31/5). Participants with Alzheimer’s disease also expressed enjoyment when using the tablets (mean rating: 4.30/5). In Kim et al. (2025)’s study, the depression score among older adults with very mild CI significantly decreased from 4.67 to 2.78 after 6 weeks of engagement with social robots. Similarly, among participants with MCI, cognitive function significantly increased from 15.33 at the baseline to 17.5 post-exposure to the social robot.

Furthermore, Khosla et al. (2021) demonstrated positive feedback through a post-trial survey of the social robot called Betty, with participants particularly enjoying activities like dancing (mean rating: 4.0/5) and finding Betty’s responses engaging (mean rating: 3.6/5). Most participants agreed that Betty made them smile (mean rating: 4.4/5) and helped them revisit happy memories (mean rating: 3.4/5). Emotional engagement during interactions with Betty was notable, with positive emotions observed in 8.44% of the interaction time and minimal negative responses (0.87%). Finally, Wu et al. (2014) explored user acceptance of the Kompaï robot using a 25-item questionnaire. Participants reported moderate ease of use (mean = 2.96/5 for cognitively intact healthy (CIH) adults, 2.47/5 for MCI) and perceived enjoyment (mean = 2.67/5 for CIH, 1.94/5 for MCI), alongside low negative intention to use (mean = 1.20/5 for CIH, 0.83/5 for MCI).

Challenges and benefits associated with using SFIATs in caregiving for homebound older adults with cognitive impairment

Although evidence from the included studies generally supports the potential benefits of SFIATs for homebound older adults with CI, several challenges and benefits were also identified (Figure 2). Challenges refer to the barriers or factors that make individuals hesitant to accept or adopt technological solutions for caregiving, while benefits refer to the factors that facilitate their acceptance or adoption. We mapped those challenges and benefits across individual, interpersonal, community, organizational, and policy factors of the Social Ecological Model developed by Bronfenbrenner (1979). Applying this multi-level perspective is crucial in elucidating how the adoption and effectiveness of SFIATs are impacted by a range of factors, from individual cognitive limitations to broader systemic and policy-related barriers.

Challenges associated with SFIAT use using the social ecological model

Individual level

At the individual level, the included articles reported that many older adults struggled to engage with new technologies due to reasons such as a lack of awareness about technological or digital innovations for caregiving (Felber et al., 2024), the lack of motivation and fear of losing independence (Wu et al., 2014), the cost of the device (Boger et al., 2014; Choi et al., 2024; Felber et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2014), cognitive decline (Boger et al., 2014; Choi et al., 2024; Khosla et al., 2021; Wu et al., 2014), unfamiliarity with digital devices (Boger et al., 2014; Flynn et al., 2024b; Shin et al., 2022; Wu et al., 2014), discomfort in learning new systems, maintenance of the device, and technical support (FakhrHosseini et al., 2020; Felber et al., 2024; Shin et al., 2022; Wu et al., 2014), and privacy and security concerns (FakhrHosseini et al., 2020; Shin et al., 2022; Wu et al., 2014).

Additionally, caregivers and their care recipients highlighted individual-level challenges, including digital literacy issues (Choi et al., 2024), lack of perceived effectiveness/usefulness of the technology (Boger et al., 2014; Choi et al., 2024; Wangmo et al., 2024), concern about the technology replacing human contact (Felber et al., 2024; Wangmo et al., 2024), and ethical concerns about deception, infantilization, and the dignity of older adults, as participants prefer to be treated as adults and have human contact (Wangmo et al., 2024). One article also highlighted internalized ageism, wherein older adults perceive assistive technologies, such as robots, as symbols of dependency, which they associate with functional decline (Wu et al., 2014). Hence, participants in some studies felt that the use of robots (e.g., pet-like and humanoid robots) was more appropriate in long-term care settings rather than at home, and only for people with dementia instead of those with MCI (Felber et al., 2024; Wangmo et al., 2024) or for lonely and dependent people (Wu et al., 2014).

Interpersonal level

We mapped four studies (Boger et al., 2014; FakhrHosseini et al., 2020; Felber et al., 2024; Fowler et al., 2016) across the interpersonal level. This level refers to interactions and relationships between individuals, such as those between older adults and their caregivers, and between individuals and their environment, which influence behaviors and outcomes (Bronfenbrenner, 1979). Such a challenge includes an increased caregiver burden (Boger et al., 2014; Felber et al., 2024; Fowler et al., 2016). Such a care burden was observed in situations wherein setting up and maintaining SFIATs often became caregivers’ responsibility, as they were frequently tasked with troubleshooting or providing support to older adults when using the technology (Boger et al., 2014; Felber et al., 2024; Fowler et al., 2016). Also, some SFIATs’ reliance on electricity (Felber et al., 2024) and Wi-Fi, in addition to concerns about potential issues that may arise if the connection fails (FakhrHosseini et al., 2020), were mapped as interpersonal challenges associated with SFIATs, given that interactions may be disrupted if there are technical difficulties.

Community level

We did not map any challenges for the included articles across the community level.

Organizational level

At the organizational level, we identified four articles (Boger et al., 2014; FakhrHosseini et al., 2020; Felber et al., 2024; Schmitz & Becker, 2024) that highlighted challenges related to the manufacturers’ design of technologies, and the usability issues associated with those technological innovations. Such challenges include SFIATs containing too much or irrelevant information and the lack of direct reference to individual needs (Schmitz & Becker, 2024), the assistive technology’s poor fit with the home environment (Boger et al., 2014), their reliance on electricity (Felber et al., 2024) and Wi-Fi, (FakhrHosseini et al., 2020). Such design features were thought to inherently cause potential challenges in the future, affecting the usability of those SFIATs.

Policy level

Mirroring the affordability challenge posed by the cost of SFIATs, a key policy-level challenge identified in four articles (Boger et al., 2014; Choi et al., 2024; Felber et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2014) is the lack of adequate funding mechanisms to support the adoption and utilization of SFIATs. Boger et al. (2014) raised critical questions about the affordability of assistive robots, such as whether pension funds could partially finance their purchase or whether renting these technologies might be a more cost-effective solution. Similarly, Wu et al. (2014), Choi et al. (2024), and Felber et al. (2024) highlighted cost as a significant barrier to the acquisition of assistive technologies, noting the absence of external funding from sources like government programs or third-party payment systems.

Benefits associated with SFIAT utilization

Individual level

At the individual level, seven articles (Blekou et al., 2022; Boger et al., 2014; Choi et al., 2024; Damant et al., 2024; Kim et al., 2025; Shin et al., 2022; Wangmo et al., 2024) revealed how SFIATs enabled older adults to sustain independence and reduce loneliness and social isolation. Personalization was also highlighted in four studies (Flynn et al., 2024b; Khosla et al., 2021; Prophater et al., 2021; Schmitz & Becker, 2024) as a crucial facilitator for sustained engagement with SFIATs. For instance, while Prophater et al. (2021) reported that Wi-Fi-enabled iN2L tablets were customized to include personalized content and activities, enhancing emotional and cognitive engagement, Khosla et al. (2021) described how the social robot Betty was tailored to meet individual needs through direct consultations with older adults and their caregivers.

Additionally, user-centered design was identified as essential for ensuring usability and effectiveness. Boger et al. (2014) noted that features such as emergency assistance tools, medication reminders, and intuitive interfaces fostered safety for older adults. The assistive technologies also allowed anonymity, supported mobile and micro learning with flexible access (Schmitz & Becker, 2024), improved older adults’ cognitive functioning and daily routine adherence (Kim et al., 2025), provided companionship and emotional comfort (Wangmo et al., 2024), and reduced caregiver burden (Damant et al., 2024; Felber et al., 2024; Wangmo et al., 2024).

Interpersonal level

Eleven articles demonstrated that SFIATs fostered enhanced communication and shared social connectedness/engagement/interaction among older adults, their caregivers, and other key stakeholders in their social networks (Choi et al., 2024; Damant et al., 2024; FakhrHosseini et al., 2020; Felber et al., 2024; Flynn et al., 2024a, 2024b; Khosla et al., 2021; Prophater et al., 2021; Shin et al., 2022; Wangmo et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2014). Also, increased access to telehealth to avail health and social support services to older adults (Choi et al., 2024; FakhrHosseini et al., 2020; Goodman-Casanova et al., 2020), and remote monitoring and safety of care recipients (Damant et al., 2024) were mapped as interpersonal level benefits associated with SFIAT utilization.

Community level

At the community level, there were promising developments highlighted in some included studies regarding leveraging SFIATs to better integrate homebound older adults and persons with CI into their communities. Three articles highlighted the potential for community-based support (Damant et al., 2024; Fowler et al., 2016) and training initiatives (Prophater et al., 2021) facilitated by local organizations and community centers. The virtual health neighborhood project described by Fowler et al. (2016) leveraged a dedicated website to connect caregivers of individuals with dementia in a specific geographic area with an interprofessional healthcare team. This not only fostered social connections but also provided training and skill development opportunities for caregivers who were unable to leave their homes due to their care recipients’ needs. Similarly, the availability of peer and staff support programs in the care communities examined in Prophater et al. (2021)’s study significantly enhanced the use of personalized tablets, enabling participants to maintain social connections and alleviate feelings of loneliness. In Damant et al. (2024), participants discussed using the internet and social media platforms to seek information and education regarding their caregiving situations.

Organizational level

We did not map any studies at the organizational level, as few studies that identified organizational-level benefits of using SFIATs discussed them in the context of long-term care or other clinical settings, which was not our study’s focus.

Policy level

We did not map any studies at this level.

Discussion

This review aimed to examine the current landscape of literature on SFIATs used in caregiving for homebound older adults with various levels of CI. To the best of our knowledge, our study is the first review that provides insight into the existing literature on this topic. Analysis of the included studies revealed that robots, telephones, tablets, televisions, computers, and virtual avenues were some of the primary SFIATs utilized in caregiving for homebound older individuals and those with CI. Employing the Social Ecological Model as a framework, some of the challenges and benefits associated with SFIATs were mapped across individual, interpersonal, community, organizational, and policy levels. While the studies demonstrated the potential of technologies to facilitate social connectedness, reduce social isolation, improve well-being, and enhance interaction among care recipients, caregivers, and health professionals, numerous challenges were identified at multiple levels of the Social Ecological Model.

Among some of the challenges identified were the lack of familiarity with SFIATs, the lack of their perceived usefulness/effectiveness, and SFIATs fitting poorly with the home environment. Observing the methodologies of included articles in our review, the identified challenges across multiple levels may have stemmed from the limited inclusion of end-user perspectives, especially older adults and their caregivers, during the pre-design research stages of SFIATs development, as only a small number of studies (Flynn et al., 2024b; Khosla et al., 2021) reported employing participatory design approaches. Despite robust evidence from human-computer interaction and gerontechnology research demonstrating that user involvement improves usability, acceptability, and overall outcomes (Fischer et al., 2020; Kim, 2015), co-creation approaches have yet to be widely adopted in technology design. This exclusion reflects a broader pattern of technocentric innovation, in which assistive technologies are often created for, rather than with, the target population, potentially resulting in misalignments between design features and real-world needs (Howard et al., 2022; Wherton et al., 2015).

From an equity perspective, the exclusion of technology end users, in this case, homebound older persons, those with CI, and informal caregivers, risks reproducing misalignments and digital exclusion and undermines the relevance and acceptability of these technologies. Instances of misalignment between design features and real-world needs of SFIAT end users were demonstrated in studies where participants were hesitant to accept technologies, for example, pet-like robots perceived as infantilizing and not upholding their dignity (Wangmo et al., 2024). This outcome aligns with critical gerontology research that critiques the deployment of assistive technologies, which intentionally or unintentionally reinforce deficit-based perspectives on aging (Neven, 2010; Peine & Neven, 2021). Therefore, culturally grounded, user-led co-creation processes are critical to ensure that SFIATs are well adopted or misaligned with users’ needs.

Additionally, loss of autonomy and ethical concern about deception were identified among the challenges associated with SFIATs. These outcomes are consistent with research identifying tensions between care, control, and surveillance in the use of technologies among older populations (Bertolazzi et al., 2024; Desai et al., 2022; Wangmo et al., 2019). The findings highlight the need for user-centered design approaches in SFIAT development, ensuring that technologies align with the values, preferences, and capabilities of homebound older adults, persons with CI, and their carers, while upholding their autonomy and dignity. Also, designing through the lens of systems thinking, along with a human-centered perspective, is important to ensure that ethical considerations are factored into the design process.

Furthermore, affordability and integration of assistive technologies into policy frameworks continue to be a critical concern, as the high cost of digital devices and the absence of third-party funding or public reimbursement programs were highlighted among the challenges associated with SFIATs utilization. Without systemic investment and policy frameworks that address the affordability issue, SFIATs risk exacerbating, rather than mitigating, disparities in caregiving support, leading to situations whereby only a subset of older adults and their caregivers gain the full benefits of SFIATs while others are left behind (Miller et al., 2022; Wang & Wilson, 2022). Therefore, forging strategic partnerships among governments, technology developers/designers, and communities is essential to ensure these technologies are affordable, sustainable, and responsive to local needs. A coordinated policy approach, including regulatory standards, reimbursement models, and strategic investment, is also critical to create an enabling environment for the effective integration of SFIATs into the continuum of care for homebound older adults and individuals with CI.

Study limitations

Notwithstanding the contributions of the included studies, several gaps in the literature warrant attention, calling for more research in these areas. An important but under-examined aspect is the gendered nature of caregiving. It was evident in the reviewed studies that the majority of caregivers and SFIAT users were women. Yet, none of the studies explored how gender shaped patterns of technology use, user expectations, or the digital labor involved. This oversight is problematic given the longstanding feminist scholarship critiquing the gendered distribution of caregiving responsibilities and the often invisible work required to manage assistive devices within home settings (Champagne-Holland, 2023; Klostermann et al., 2022). Future research must carefully investigate whether SFIATs redistribute or reinforce existing gender inequalities in caregiving.

It was also evident in the review that all the studies included were conducted in HICs. Therefore, translating the reported outcomes to LMICs is critical and requires careful consideration. In the HICs represented in this review, the challenges reported center on such issues as technologies’ acceptance, ethical concerns, and design misalignments, whereas in LMICs, unique structural barriers related to digital divide, encompassing disparities in affordability/access to devices, internet connectivity, and low digital literacy, are likely to pose more immediate challenges (Ekoh et al., 2021; Jongebloed et al., 2024). Moreover, cultural differences in informal care provision, some of which include reliance on community-based networks and intergenerational support (Agbawodikeizu et al., 2024; Ezulike et al., 2024, 2025) may influence the acceptability and utility of SFIATs. These contextual differences underscore the need for cross-cultural studies and pilot projects in LMICs to properly contextualize SFIATs within diverse caregiving ecologies and avoid imposing Western technocentric models onto structurally/culturally different environments.

Furthermore, we noted some inconsistencies in how various studies determined the clinical diagnosis of CI among their study participants. While some studies highlighted that their participants were selected based on an existing clinical diagnosis, others relied on self-reported or clinician/informal caregiver-noted impairments without any clear specifications. This heterogeneity somewhat complicates comparison across studies, highlighting the need for more consistency in how the diagnosis of CIs is determined and reported to strengthen the empirical evidence on SFIATs.

Additionally, the reviewed articles tended to convey an implicit perspective of technology alone as a panacea to social isolation, caregiver burden, and systemic inefficiencies. However, this techno-optimistic viewpoint must be controlled by recognizing that care deficits and lack of social connectedness/engagement are shaped by broader structural factors, some of which include intergenerational family dynamics, prevailing societal attitudes toward aging and disability, and community-based activities, which cannot be solely solved through technology (Suragarn et al., 2021; Vollmer Dahlke & Ory, 2020). As argued by scholars in critical gerontology and disability studies, assistive technologies must be understood not as neutral tools, but as socially situated artifacts that have the potential to either reproduce or challenge existing inequalities (Gibson et al., 2012; Peine & Neven, 2021).

Future research should critically examine the assumptions underlying SFIATs’ deployment and contextualize their use within the broader support and care infrastructure. They should also move beyond cross-sectional design toward longitudinal studies that explore the impact of sustained SFIAT use in daily life. Co-design approaches involving homebound older adults and those with CI, along with their caregivers across diverse socio-economic and cultural contexts, are essential to ensure equity and inclusivity. Furthermore, cross-cultural trials combined with policy and system-level integration are crucial to promote widespread and equitable adoption of SFIATs.

Conclusion

The integration of SFIATs into the caregiving of older adults who are homebound and those with clinically diagnosed CI has demonstrated the potential to contribute toward the enhancement of the social engagement and well-being of carers and their care recipients. From assistive robots that facilitate interactive companionship to tablet-based systems that support remote communication, these technologies offer promising avenues for reducing social isolation and supporting aging in place. However, persistent barriers ranging from ethical and affordability concerns to perceived non-usefulness, among other barriers, highlight the critical need for context-sensitive, co-creation approaches in SFIAT design and development. Moving forward, technological innovations must be informed by the target population’s lived experiences and supported by policies that promote inclusivity across socioeconomic and cultural contexts, including LMICs. Researchers should prioritize cross-cultural and longitudinal studies that assess the ongoing efficacy, equity impact, contextual adaptability, and ethical dimensions of SFIATs in various settings. Also, policymakers must develop frameworks for funding and reimbursement to make SFIATs accessible and affordable, particularly for vulnerable populations across HICs and LMICs. By aligning technological innovation with participatory, user-centered design and supportive policy frameworks, SFIATs can play a critical role in strategies for advancing healthy aging and aging-in-place, ensuring sustainable and inclusive models for home-based care.
