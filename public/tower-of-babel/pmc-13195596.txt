Popping FVIII concentrates into an updated classification
Authors: Stacy E. Croteau
Published: 2026

In this issue of Blood Advances, Hermans et al1 investigate how pharmacokinetic (PK) parameters could inform a clinically meaningful classification schema for factor VIII (FVIII) concentrates. Although the use of terms such as standard half-life (SHL) and extended half-life (EHL) factor concentrates has been commonplace for over a decade, no formal definitions exist.2 EHL concentrates typically comprise those that are specifically engineered, through Fc fusion or linking of polyethylene glycol chains, to reduce FVIII clearance and extend circulating half-life. The rapid expansion of FVIII concentrates since 2013, without a clear approach to classification, has made discussions with patients about their potential utility in various clinical contexts increasingly challenging. Additionally, facilitating payor understanding of how to consider these FVIII concentrates is critical to avoiding requirements for patients to “fail” a series of bioequivalent therapies before gaining access to PK-enhanced FVIII concentrates or other prophylaxis options.

To date, we have no hemophilia A therapies that reliably achieve sustained normal range FVIII activity (or an effective equivalent). We have amassed several therapies, specifically nonfactor therapies including FVIIIa mimetics and rebalancing therapies that maintain steady-state bleed prevention in the mild hemophilia A range without the benefit of even transient normalization of hemostasis. The dosing strategy for these therapies is fixed without a focus on dose reduction to try to identify the threshold below which breakthrough bleeding occurs (minimum effective dose). In contrast, the strategy with prophylactic FVIII concentrates has been to achieve trough levels just above the severe hemophilia threshold of 1% to 3%, with more recent recognition of the likely benefit of targeting FVIII trough activity of 3% to 5%.3

A substantial barrier to achieving more robust hemostatic levels with FVIII concentrates has been their PK characteristics, which necessitate frequent IV administration to maintain even modest trough levels (as well as the exorbitantly high cost of factor concentrate therapy). Further FVIII product engineering, effectively decoupling the interaction between FVIII and von Willebrand factor (VWF), has liberated this FVIII construct from VWF-dependent clearance. Efanesoctocog alfa, rFVIIIFc-VWF-XTEN, leverages the Fc recycling pathway, the VWF D'D3 region, and XTEN polypeptides to extend the half-life of FVIII, overcoming the barrier of endogenous VWF interaction, leading to more robust PK parameters, which the authors emphasize are not adequately captured using the current EHL label.

Integrating several published, manufacturer-derived or independent, and product-specific population PK models, the authors simulated profiles for 1000 virtual patients aged 12 to 63 years across 4 FVIII concentrates (octocog alfa [Advate], rurioctocog alfa pegol [Adynovate], efmoroctocog alfa [Eloctate/Elocta], and efanesoctocog alfa [Altuviiio/Altuvoct]) in an attempt to provide a framework for providers, patients, payors, and other stakeholders to conceptualize how the features of different categories of FVIII concentrates could be deployed in clinical situations such as routine prophylaxis, bleed treatment, or perioperative management. In addition to reporting traditional PK metrics, authors introduce time in therapeutic target (TTT) and segmented area under the curve (AUC) to help reframe the hemostatic impact of single dose and steady-state profiles of representative SHL, EHL, or the proposed high sustained activity (HSA) FVIII concentrates. Rather than focusing on only a global comparison of PK parameters and trough activity targets of 1% to 3% or 3% to 5%, the authors consider a range of potentially clinically relevant thresholds that reflect increasing expectation of effective hemostasis. They explore half-life, TTT>40%, and AUC>40% as the key metrics to illustrate the degree and duration of FVIII normalization achievable with weekly HSA product administration compared to the recommended administration frequency for SHL and EHL products.

Authors suggest HSA concentrates be classified by the ability to achieve an FVIII activity of >10% for at least 7 days and FVIII activity >40% for 3.5 days, but they do not otherwise propose cutoffs to delineate SHL, EHL, or HSA categories based on classical or their novel PK metrics. Some have previously suggested a half-life extension ratio metric of 1.34 or a composite criteria of intentional technology to extend circulating FVIII half-life, an AUC ratio of ≥1.25 (above US Food and Drug Administration/European Medicines Agency bioequivalence cutoff), and a half-life ratio of ≥1.35 to distinguish between SHL and EHL products, reflecting the modest half-life extension afforded by the initial attempts with engineered FVIII concentrates. Although the authors’ methodology shows promise, analyses of other FVIII concentrates are needed to establish whether this proof-of-concept approach of categorizing FVIII concentrates effectively discriminates products into 3 categories or whether the PK-based metrics ultimately demonstrate more of a continuum. Validation with available PK data sets and prospective PK studies is also needed.

Although all available FVIII concentrates are highly effective for bleed prevention and treatment, the varied engineering technologies and the resultant impacts on PK parameters influence the approach to dosing and infusion frequency needed to achieve a desired hemostatic impact. It is important to remember that these PK metrics summarize population-level features of these concentrates; there will be variability at the individual level to consider when developing specific care plans.6 The benefit of conceptualizing FVIII concentrate properties, as the authors have here, is the shift in focus toward achieving normal physiological FVIII activity over greater durations of time and the near elimination of time spent with FVIII activity <10%. This is consistent with the change in degree of hemostatic protection introduced by nonfactor therapies, which has facilitated a change in behavior (physical activity) and therapeutic expectations for individuals with access to these therapies. The PROPEL study demonstrated the incremental improvement in reduced bleed rates that could be achieved by targeting FVIII troughs of 8% to 12% rather than 1% to 3%, but it was achieved at the arduous cost of continuing every other day or daily infusion schedules with an EHL product.7 Authors highlight that investigation is required to elucidate whether PK properties of HSA concentrates can be associated with improved clinical outcomes; however, this will depend on how they are deployed. If used for their “ultralong half-life” to perpetuate a goal of low FVIII troughs (<5%), we will not harness the potential of HSA FVIII concentrates, particularly for our pediatric patients, to further improve hemostasis, bleed prevention, and promotion of long-term musculoskeletal health while facilitating greater physical activity, societal functionality, and movement toward a hemophilia-free mind.
