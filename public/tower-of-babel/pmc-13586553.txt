Life‐Saving Cryoextraction for Acute Severe Respiratory Failure Caused by Cheese‐Filled Fish Cake Aspiration: A Case Report
Authors: Mayu Goda, Yuki Takigawa, Ken Sato, Hitoshi Hara, Tomoya Osedo, Yuto Sasano, Toki Kitano, Keisuke Shiraha, Suzuka Matsuoka, Jun Nishimura, Hiromi Watanabe, Kenichiro Kudo, Keiichi Fujiwara
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTA 79‐year‐old man with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) aspirated cheese‐filled Kamaboko, a Japanese fish cake, accidentally while having his lunch. He developed acute respiratory failure and presented to a referral hospital. Chest computed tomography (CT) revealed an aspirated fish cake in the right middle bronchus. After endotracheal intubation in the intensive care unit, the fish cake was successfully removed using a cryoprobe. Although foreign body (FB) aspirations are rare in adults, they can cause life‐threatening respiratory dysfunction. Prompt removal of the FB using an appropriate removal device based on its characteristics is essential. Here in, we report the successful cryoextraction of an organic airway FB from a patient with severe respiratory failure.

Introduction

Foreign body (FB) aspiration is rare in adults and can cause severe respiratory failure requiring prompt removal of the FB using the most appropriate technique [1]. Organic FBs, such as food, account for the majority of aspirated FBs, whereas they were less common in our Japanese retrospective cohort study [2]. Organic FBs are often difficult to detect because they are radiolucent on chest radiographs. Computed tomography (CT) is useful for detecting radiolucent FBs [3] and facilitates planning of the removal procedure by evaluating airway FB size, location and characteristics. Cryotherapy is particularly useful for removing FBs with high water content, because it enables FB adherence to cryoprobe by freezing. Hence allowing airway FB to be extracted en bloc. We report a case of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) exacerbation caused by aspiration of an organic FB that improved dramatically after rapid bronchoscopic removal using cryotherapy. Our patient experienced safe and rapid removal of organic FB by cryoprobe.

Case Report

A 79‐year‐old man with a medical history of COPD receiving home oxygen therapy at 2 L/min accidentally aspirated cheese‐filled Kamaboko during his lunch. He developed sudden severe dyspnea and presented to a referral hospital. Though chest radiograph showed no evidence of FB (Figure 1A), chest CT revealed a 6‐mm × 14‐mm FB in the right middle bronchus (Figure 1B,C) and he was referred to our hospital. On arrival, his temperature was 36.4°C, blood pressure was 166/102 mmHg, pulse rate was 132 beats/min, respiratory rate was 32 breaths/min and oxygen saturation was 97% on 5 L/min oxygen via oxygen mask. He was cyanotic and auscultation revealed lung wheezing. Arterial blood gas revealed acidosis, deoxygenation and carbon dioxide retention (pH 7.216, PaO2 60.3 mmHg, PaCO2 66.2 mmHg on 5 L/min oxygen). He was urgently transferred to the intensive care unit and was emergently intubated via bronchoscopy with an 8.0‐mm endotracheal tube under intravenous anaesthesia with propofol and fentanyl. Immediately after initiating mechanical ventilation (SIMV+PS; PEEP 6 cm H2O, PS/PC 8 cm H2O, FiO2 40%), flexible bronchoscopy (BF‐1TQ290; Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) was performed. A white fish cake was identified in the right middle bronchus but could not be removed by bronchoscopic suction (Figure 2A). Given the acuity of the situation, a 1.7‐mm single‐use cryoprobe (20402‐401; ERBE, Tübingen, Germany) was selected to enable rapid cryoextraction of the FB. After ensuring adequate contact of cryoprobe with the FB under endoscopic visualisation, the cryoprobe was activated for 10 s and the fish cake was gradually withdrawn en‐bloc with bronchoscope through the endotracheal tube while freezing was maintained. After four cryoextraction passes (Figure 2B,C), the fish cake was completely removed from the right middle bronchus (Figure 2D,E). The patient was extubated immediately after the procedure approximately 48 min from time of arrival at the ICU. Bronchoscopy procedure time was 10 min. Following removal of the fish cake, his oxygen saturation remained consistently above 90% while receiving nasal oxygen therapy at 2 L/min. Arterial blood gas analysis performed 1 h after the procedure showed improvement in acidosis and carbon dioxide retention (pH 7.336, PaO2 58 mmHg, PaCO2 50.4 mmHg). The patient was discharged on the day after the procedure without complications.

Discussion

Patients with COPD have a higher risk of aspiration because swallowing dysfunction is common, and the long‐term use of inhaled corticosteroids or anticholinergic agents may further increase this risk [4]. Organic FBs account for only 13% of airway FB aspiration in Japanese cohort study [2], and they can be difficult to identify in patients with acute respiratory failure because they are often radiolucent on chest radiograph, as in this case [3]. Ng et al. [5] reported that radiographs were normal in 25% of patients with FB aspiration, whereas airway FBs were visible on chest CT in 81% of patients who underwent CT. Furthermore, diagnosis can be challenging because approximately 70% of patients with airway FBs are unaware of the aspiration event [2]. In the present case, the aspiration event was clearly recognised, which facilitated early suspicion of an airway FB despite the normal chest radiograph. Therefore, chest CT should be considered in patients with persistent cough or respiratory failure when airway FB aspiration is suspected, even when chest radiographs are unremarkable.

In Japan, a traditional Japanese rice cake known as Mochi, is a common cause of airway FB aspiration, particularly among older adults [6]. Though cheese‐filled Kamaboko is a popular Japanese food product made from fish paste filled with cheese. Cheese filled Kamaboko is uncommonly reported as airway FB in Japan. Igarashi et al. reported that the most common airway FBs in Japan are meat, bread and Mochi [7].

Flexible bronchoscopy is generally considered the first choice for FB removal because it is widely available, technically less demanding and can be performed without general anaesthesia. However, rigid bronchoscopy should be considered in patients with severe respiratory dysfunction or when the FB is large or sharp [1]. Regardless of the technique used, selection of the most appropriate removal device is essential for safe and effective removal. Flexible bronchoscopy could be initiated more rapidly in the ICU while it takes time to prepare the operation theatre and required resources for performance of rigid bronchoscopy. Therefore, the patient was intubated, and flexible bronchoscopy was initially performed through the endotracheal tube under mechanical ventilation, with rigid bronchoscopy available as a backup, if necessary.

Both cryoprobes and basket forceps have been used successfully for organic FBs removal. Cryoprobes function optimally when freezing causes adherence of FB which then facilitates en bloc removal [8]. In our patient's case, we assessed the fish cake FB to have enough water content to be frozen to sufficient degree for strong adherence to cryoprobe. We deemed that the cryoprobe was the most suitable device for rapid removal and completed FB removal procedure within 10 min. No complications, including bleeding, occurred and respiratory acidosis and carbon dioxide retention improved immediately after the procedure. In our multicenter retrospective cohort study of airway FBs, which included our institution and covered cases until March 2025, no airway FBs were removed using cryoprobes [2]. We previously reported the successful removal of aspirated soybeans using cryotherapy in late 2025 [8]. In the reported case, FB removal was completed within 20 min (from insertion of the rigid bronchoscope to extubate). When the patient's respiratory status or anaesthesia is poor and the FB is mobile, FB removal may require more than 1 h [1]. The cryoprobes were selected based on our clinical experience. At our institution, hands‐on training sessions on airway FB removal using cryoprobes are held regularly. We believe that prior training using simulation models such as beans, gummy candies and sausages may be beneficial, especially in preparing for emergency clinical situations.

In conclusion, aspirated cheese‐filled Kamaboko causing acute respiratory failure was successfully removed by cryoprobe from COPD patient with poor lung reserves. Careful assessment of the physical characteristics of the aspirated FB before the procedure is important for selecting the optimal retrieval devices. Cryoprobes may be particularly useful for the retrieval of organic FBs with a high‐water content because they can facilitate rapid and effective en bloc removal.
