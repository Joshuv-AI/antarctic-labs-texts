Computing the Reachability Value of Posterior-Deterministic POMDPs
Nathanaël Fijalkow, Arka Ghosh, Roman Kniazev, Guillermo A. Pérez, Pierre Vandenhove

Abstract
Partially observable Markov decision processes (POMDPs) are a fundamental model for sequential decision-making under uncertainty. However, many verification and synthesis problems for POMDPs are undecidable or intractable. Most prominently, the seminal result of Madani et al. (2003) states that there is no algorithm that, given a POMDP and a set of target states, can compute the maximal probability of reaching the target states, or even approximate it up to a non-trivial constant. This is in stark contrast to fully observable Markov decision processes (MDPs), where the reachability value can be computed in polynomial time. In this work, we introduce posterior-deterministic POMDPs , a novel class of POMDPs. Our main technical contribution is to show that for posterior-deterministic POMDPs, the maximal probability of reaching a given set of states can be approximated up to arbitrary precision. A POMDP is posterior-deterministic if the next state can be uniquely determined by the current state, the action taken, and the observation received. While the actual state is generally uncertain in POMDPs, the posterior-deterministic property tells us that once the true state is known it remains known forever. This simple and natural definition includes all MDPs and captures classical non-trivial examples such as the Tiger POMDP (Kaelbling et al. 1998), making it one of the largest known classes of POMDPs for which the reachability value can be approximated.

Introduction

Partially observable Markov decision processes.

Partially observable Markov decision processes (POMDPs) constitute the
canonical mathematical framework for sequential decision-making under
uncertainty. Introduced by Åström
[
2
]
and developed
algorithmically by Smallwood and Sondik
[
28
]
, POMDPs
were firmly established as a central paradigm in artificial intelligence through the
influential work of Kaelbling, Littman, and
Cassandra
[
19
]
. A POMDP models a
decision-making agent that must act in a stochastic environment whose true
state is hidden; the agent receives only noisy observations and must maintain
a
belief
(a probability distribution over possible states) which they
update as new observations arrive.

The ubiquity of POMDPs stems from their ability to naturally capture
real-world scenarios where agents must act on incomplete information. In

robotics
, POMDPs model navigation under sensor noise, manipulation with
uncertain object poses, and collision avoidance for autonomous
vehicles
[
21
,
18
,
29
]
.
In
healthcare
, they enable treatment planning when diagnoses are
uncertain and patient states evolve
stochastically
[
17
,
27
]
.

Dialogue systems
use POMDPs to model the inference of user intent from
noisy speech recognition
[
30
,
31
]
. In

verification and controller synthesis
, POMDPs arise naturally when
synthesizing controllers for systems with imperfect sensors or hidden
adversarial behavior
[
1
,
10
]
.
Across these domains, the core problem studied remains the same:
computing optimal or near-optimal policies in the face of an infinite belief
space.

The computational barrier.

Despite their ubiquity, POMDPs present formidable computational challenges.
The belief space is an infinite continuous simplex.
Optimal policies must map this infinite space to actions. Additionally, the value function, while piecewise-linear and convex for finite-horizon problems
[
28
]
, can have exponentially many pieces.

The complexity landscape is daunting. For finite-horizon POMDPs, even
determining whether there is a strategy that achieves a given sum of rewards is
PSPACE-complete
[
24
]
. For infinite-horizon
objectives, the situation is far worse: Madani, Hanks, and
Condon
[
23
]
established that the maximal
probability of reaching a given set of states (a.k.a. the reachability value)
cannot be approximated, even up to a fixed error
of
\frac{1}{3}
. More precisely, it follows from Condon and
Lipton
[
12
]
that deciding whether the reachability value exceeds

\frac{2}{3}
or is below
\frac{1}{3}
is undecidable. Almost-sure
reachability (determining whether a strategy reaches the target with
probability
1
) is decidable
[
3
]
, but this positive result does not
extend to approximating or computing the exact value.

This computational barrier motivates a fundamental question:
are there natural, expressive classes of POMDPs for which the reachability value can be approximated?

The landscape of tractable classes.

The search for tractable classes has resulted in several restricted models.
At one extreme,
fully observable Markov decision processes (MDPs)
admit
polynomial-time algorithms for reachability and discounted objectives.
Decidable classes with some actual partial observability
include
revealing POMDPs

[
4
]
, where the structure of information revelation is constrained.
On a related but distinct note,
multiple-environment MDPs

(MEMDPs)
[
26
,
11
]
allow to study robustness across uncertain
transition dynamics. Importantly, MEMDPs are not POMDPs: the agent knows the state but faces uncertainty about the environment’s parameters.

A notable class is that of
deterministic
POMDPs

[
22
,
7
]
. Here, transition and observation functions
are required to be deterministic functions of state-action pairs.
Deterministic POMDPs also admit decidable value approximation.

Quasi-deterministic POMDPs

[
6
]
weaken these restrictions
by only requiring the transition function to be deterministic.

The central open question has been:
what is the largest natural class of
POMDPs for which reachability value approximation is possible?
This paper
introduces

posterior-deterministic POMDPs
, a class that we
argue provides a compelling answer to this question.

Posterior-deterministic POMDPs.

Informal definition.
A POMDP is
posterior-deterministic
if, when the current state is known, the successor state after any action-observation pair is uniquely determined.

Equivalently, the posterior distribution over states, conditioned on knowing the current state and observing
(a,o)
, is always a point mass.
The key structural insight is the following:

Key property.
In posterior-deterministic POMDPs, the belief support
(the set of states with positive probability at any given moment) can only

decrease
in size.

This property distinguishes posterior-deterministic POMDPs from general
POMDPs, where a single observation can “spread” probability mass to new
states. It also clarifies the relationship to quasi-deterministic POMDPs:
posterior-determinism is a strictly weaker condition. Quasi-deterministic POMDPs
require that successor states be determined by the current state and the
action taken, yet they allow for stochastic observations.
Posterior-deterministic POMDPs further allow for a truly stochastic transition
function, requiring only that each observation resolves
the successor state uniquely given the current state and the action taken.
The class is natural
and encompasses important examples:

•

all MDPs (trivially, since observations reveal the state),

•

the
Tiger POMDP

[
19
]
, a
canonical benchmark in the POMDP literature, and

•

all quasi-deterministic POMDPs, hence all deterministic POMDPs.

Contributions.

Our main result is that posterior-deterministic POMDPs admit decidable value approximation for reachability objectives.

Main theorem.
For any posterior-deterministic POMDP
\mathcal{P}
, initial
belief
\mathbf{b}
, and a tolerance
\varepsilon>0
, one can compute a value
v\in[0,1]
such that
|\mathsf{Val}^{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbf{b})-v|\leq\varepsilon
.

To prove this result, we use an extension of the classical notion of
end components
from MDP theory to the POMDP setting, applications of martingale theory, and a belief tree unfolding that exploits the structural properties of posterior-deterministic POMDPs to ensure termination and correctness.

Outline.

The two subsequent sections contain all the technical arguments:
Section

is dedicated to the correctness and convergence of our approximation algorithm, and
Section

6
studies end components.

Preliminaries

If
X
is a finite set, we denote by
\mathsf{Dist}(X)
the set of all probability distributions over
X
.
The
support
of a distribution
\mu\in\mathsf{Dist}(X)
is the set
\mathsf{supp}(\mu)=\left\{x\in X\ \middle|\ \mu(x)>0\right\}
.

POMDPs.

A
partially observable Markov decision process
(POMDP) is a tuple
\mathcal{P}=(Q,A,O,\mathbf{T})
where
Q
is a finite set of
states
,
A
is a finite set of
actions
,
O
is a finite set of
observations
, and
\mathbf{T}\colon Q\times A\to\mathsf{Dist}(O\times Q)
is a
transition function
.
We frequently write
\mathbf{T}(o,q^{\prime}\mid q,a)
to denote the probability of receiving observation
o
and going to state
q^{\prime}
when taking action
a
in state
q
(formally,
\mathbf{T}(o,q^{\prime}\mid q,a)=\mathbf{T}(q,a)(o,q^{\prime})
).
We also write
\mathbf{T}(q^{\prime}\mid q,a)
to denote the marginal probability of going to state
q^{\prime}
when taking action
a
in state
q
(formally,
\mathbf{T}(q^{\prime}\mid q,a)=\sum_{o\in O}\mathbf{T}(o,q^{\prime}\mid q,a)
) and, similarly,
\mathbf{T}(o\mid q,a)
to denote the marginal probability of receiving observation
o
when taking action
a
in state
q
(formally,
\mathbf{T}(o\mid q,a)=\sum_{q^{\prime}\in Q}\mathbf{T}(o,q^{\prime}\mid q,a)
).

An agent interacting with a POMDP takes actions sequentially, which updates the state probabilistically based on function
\mathbf{T}
.
Yet, the agent only observes the observations, not the actual states (and of course, the agent knows which actions they have taken).
In particular, they do not know in general which state the POMDP is in at a given moment—which is why it is called
partially observable
.

Let
\mathcal{P}=(Q,A,O,\mathbf{T})
be a POMDP.
A
run
of
\mathcal{P}
is an infinite sequence of states, actions, and observations of the form
\rho=q_{0}a_{1}o_{1}q_{1}a_{2}o_{2}\ldots\in(Q\times A\times O)^{\omega}
such that
\mathbf{T}(q_{i},a_{i+1})(o_{i+1},q_{i+1})>0
for all
i\geq 0
.
We write
\mathsf{Runs}(\mathcal{P})
for the set of runs on
\mathcal{P}
.

A
history
is a finite prefix of a run ending in a state; it is an element of
Q\times(A\times O\times Q)^{*}
.
We write
\mathsf{last}(h)
for the last state of the history.
We also define an
observable history
(resp.
observable run
) as the projection of a history (resp. run) to
(A\times O)^{*}
(resp.
(A\times O)^{\omega}
), corresponding to the information an agent has access to while taking actions in the POMDP.
We write
\mathsf{obs}(\cdot)
for this projection.
The
length

\left\lvert h\right\rvert
of an (observable) history
h
is the number of
actions in it.
If
\rho
is an (observable) run, we write
\rho_{\leq n}
for the (observable) history consisting of the states, actions, and observations of
\rho
up to index
n
included.

Beliefs and belief supports.

A
belief
of
\mathcal{P}
is a distribution
\mathbf{b}\in\mathsf{Dist}(Q)
.
Beliefs are denoted as boldface letters.
The set of all beliefs of
\mathcal{P}
is denoted as
\mathsf{Beliefs}(\mathcal{P})
.
Beliefs are used to represent the most accurate information about the current state that the agent has.
We usually assume that the agent has an
initial belief
about its starting position in the POMDP.
This belief is updated as actions are taken and observations are received.
If
\mathbf{b}
is the current belief, after taking action
a
and observing
o
, the new belief
\mathbf{b}_{a,o}
is given, for
q\in Q
, by

\mathbf{b}_{a,o}(q)=\frac{\sum_{q^{\prime}\in Q}\mathbf{b}(q^{\prime})\cdot\mathbf{T}(o,q\mid q^{\prime},a)}{\sum_{q^{\prime\prime}\in Q}\sum_{q^{\prime}\in Q}\mathbf{b}(q^{\prime})\cdot\mathbf{T}(o,q^{\prime\prime}\mid q^{\prime},a)}\ .

(1)

In matrix notation, we have
\mathbf{b}_{a,o}=\frac{\mathbf{b}\cdot\mathbf{T}_{a,o}}{\lVert\mathbf{b}\cdot\mathbf{T}_{a,o}\rVert_{1}}
, where
\mathbf{T}_{a,o}
is the transition matrix defined as
\mathbf{T}_{a,o}(q,q^{\prime})=\mathbf{T}(q,a)(o,q^{\prime})
.

The
support
of a belief
\mathbf{b}
is the set
\mathsf{supp}(\mathbf{b})
.
We write
\mathsf{BeliefSups}(\mathcal{P})
for the set of all belief supports of
\mathcal{P}
(corresponding to the set
2^{Q}\setminus\{\emptyset\}
).
We will sometimes write a belief
\mathbf{b}
as

\sum_{q\in Q}\mathbf{b}(q)\cdot q
.

It will sometimes be convenient to restrict the support of a belief. This
gives rise to
sub-beliefs
, functions
\mathbf{b}
from
Q
to
[0,1]
such that

\sum_{q\in Q}\mathbf{b}(q)\leq 1
.
For a belief
\mathbf{b}
and subset of states
S\subseteq Q
,
the restriction
\left.\mathbf{b}\right|_{S}
of
\mathbf{b}
to
S
is
the sub-belief defined so that

\left.\mathbf{b}\right|_{S}(q)=\begin{cases}\mathbf{b}(q)&\text{if $q\in S$}\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

We frequently extend functions defined on states to beliefs in the natural way.
For instance, we write

\mathbf{T}(o\mid\mathbf{b},a)=\sum_{q\in\mathsf{supp}(\mathbf{b})}\mathbf{T}(o\mid q,a)\cdot\mathbf{b}(q)

for the probability of observing
o
after taking action
a
from belief
\mathbf{b}
.

Strategies.

A
strategy
in
\mathcal{P}
is a function
\pi\colon(A\times O)^{*}\to\mathsf{Dist}(A)
that sequentially takes an action (possibly in a randomised fashion) given the current
observable
history.
Such strategies are sometimes called
observation-based
in the literature because they only look at the sequence of observations (as well as the actions they have previously taken) to make their decisions.
In this paper, all strategies are assumed to be observation-based.

Probability measure on runs.

For a history
h
, the
cylinder
\mathsf{Cyl}(h)
is the set of all runs that start with
h
.
Let
\mathcal{F}
be the
\sigma
-algebra on the set
\mathsf{Runs}(\mathcal{P})
generated by the set
\left\{\mathsf{Cyl}(h)\ \middle|\ h\in Q\times(A\times O\times Q)^{*}\right\}
.
For an initial (sub-)belief
\mathbf{b}
and a strategy
\pi
in
\mathcal{P}
, we define a function
\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{\mathbf{b}}
such that for
h\in Q\times(A\times O\times Q)^{*}
,
\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{\mathbf{b}}(\mathsf{Cyl}(h))
is the probability of getting the history
h
starting from
\mathbf{b}
using strategy
\pi
.
We give a formal inductive definition: if
h=q
contains a single state, then
\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{\mathbf{b}}(\mathsf{Cyl}(h))=\mathbf{b}(q)
.
If
h=h^{\prime}aoq
, then

\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{\mathbf{b}}(\mathsf{Cyl}(h))=\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{\mathbf{b}}(\mathsf{Cyl}(h^{\prime}))\cdot\pi(\mathsf{obs}(h^{\prime}))(a)\cdot\mathbf{T}(\mathsf{last}(h^{\prime}),a)(o,q)\ .

The function
\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{\mathbf{b}}
can be uniquely extended to a probability distribution on the full measurable space
(\mathsf{Runs}(\mathcal{P}),\mathcal{F})
using Ionescu-Tulcea extension theorem
[
20
, Section 14.3]
.
We write
\mathbb{E}^{\pi}_{\mathbf{b}}\left[\cdot\right]
to denote the expectation with respect to the probability distribution
\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{\mathbf{b}}
.

Value of a POMDP.

A
reachability objective
on
\mathcal{P}
is specified by a set of target states
T\subseteq Q
.
Formally, it is the measurable set

\mathsf{Reach}_{T}=\left\{q_{0}a_{1}o_{1}q_{1}a_{2}o_{2}\ldots\in\mathsf{Runs}(\mathcal{P})\ \middle|\ \exists i\geq 0,q_{i}\in T\right\}
.
Given an initial (sub-)belief
\mathbf{b}
, the
(reachability) value
of
\mathcal{P}
with respect to

T
is

\mathsf{Val}^{\mathcal{P}}_{T}(\mathbf{b})=\sup_{\pi}\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{\mathbf{b}}(\mathsf{Reach}_{T})

where the supremum is taken over all (observation-based) strategies
\pi
in
\mathcal{P}
.
When
\mathcal{P}
and
T
are clear from the context, we simply write

\mathsf{Val}(\mathbf{b})
.
Observe that
\mathsf{Val}(\mathbf{b})
is upper-bounded by
\left\lVert\mathbf{b}\right\rVert_{1}=\sum_{q\in Q}\mathbf{b}(q)
, which gives a better upper bound than
1
when
\mathbf{b}
is a sub-belief.

We also define the value of a (sub-)belief
\mathbf{b}
and an action
a
as

\mathsf{Val}(\mathbf{b},a)=\sum_{o\in O}\mathbf{T}(o\mid\mathbf{b},a)\cdot\mathsf{Val}(\mathbf{b}_{a,o})\ ,

(2)

where
\mathbf{b}_{a,o}
is
obtained from
\mathbf{b}
after taking action
a
and observing
o

(see
Equation

1
).

Using that randomisation in strategies is not needed in POMDPs to approach the value for measurable objectives
[
9
, Lemma 1]
, we have that

\mathsf{Val}(\mathbf{b})=\max_{a\in A}\ \mathsf{Val}(\mathbf{b},a)\ .

(3)

Assumptions.

A state is
absorbing
if, whenever it is reached, the agent remains in that state forever, no matter what actions are taken.
A state is
observable
if, whenever it is reached, the agent always
receives a specific observation that uniquely identifies the state, thus
collapsing the current belief to a singleton set.
We may assume, without loss of generality, that
the target set
T
contains a single state
\top
and that there
is a unique state
\bot
with value
0
. Moreover, both
\top
and
\bot
are absorbing and observable.
We assume that all POMDPs we consider later in the paper satisfy these properties.
The statement appears to be folklore. For completeness, we include a proof of it in
Appendix

A
.

Lemma 1
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a POMDP with target set
T
and initial belief
\mathbf{b}
.
We can, in time linear in the size of
\mathcal{P}
, build a POMDP
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
with two new states
\top
and
\bot
, and a belief
\mathbf{b}^{\prime}

•

both
\top
and
\bot
are absorbing and observable,

•

\bot
is the only state with value
0
in
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
,

•

the reachability value is preserved, i.e.,
\mathsf{Val}^{\mathcal{P}}_{T}(\mathbf{b})=\mathsf{Val}^{\mathcal{P}^{\prime}}_{\{\top\}}(\mathbf{b}^{\prime})
.

Problem.

We focus on the following problem. It is undecidable
for general
POMDPs
[
23
,
15
]
.

Value approximation.

Given a POMDP
\mathcal{P}
, a belief
\mathbf{b}
, a
target state
\top
, and a tolerance

, compute a number
v\in[0,1]
such that

\left|\mathsf{Val}(\mathbf{b})-v\right|\leq\varepsilon.

now present the class of POMDPs we call

3.1
POMDPs and automata

A
finite automaton
is a tuple
\mathbb{A}=(Q,\Sigma,\Delta)
where
Q
is a finite set of
states
,
\Sigma
is a finite
alphabet
, and
\Delta\subseteq Q\times\Sigma\times Q
is a
transition relation
.
It is
deterministic
if for every
q\in Q
and

\sigma\in\Sigma
, there is at most one state
q^{\prime}\in Q
such that
(q,\sigma,q^{\prime})\in\Delta
.
For deterministic finite automata, we replace
\Delta
by a (partial)
transition function

\delta\colon Q\times\Sigma\rightharpoonup Q
defined as
\delta(q,\sigma)=q^{\prime}
if
(q,\sigma,q^{\prime})\in\Delta
.
We extend the transition function to words in the usual way and write
\delta^{*}(q,w)
for the state reachable from
q
by reading the word
w\in\Sigma^{*}
.

From a POMDP
\mathcal{P}=(Q,A,O,\mathbf{T})
, we construct a (possibly non-deterministic)
finite automaton
\mathbb{A}(\mathcal{P})
on the alphabet
A\times O
with

Q
as the set of states and the transition relation
\Delta
so that

(q,(a,o),q^{\prime})\in\Delta

if there is a non-zero probability to go from the state
q
to the state
q^{\prime}
after taking the action
a
and receiving the observation
o
, i.e., if
\mathbf{T}(o,q^{\prime}\mid q,a)>0
.

We extend the transition relation
\Delta
of
\mathbb{A}(\mathcal{P})
to
\mathsf{BeliefSups}(\mathcal{P})
in the natural way:

\delta(S,(a,o))=\left\{q^{\prime}\in Q\ \middle|\ q\in S,(q,(a,o),q^{\prime})\in\Delta\right\}=\left\{q^{\prime}\in Q\ \middle|\ q\in S,\mathbf{T}(o,q^{\prime}\mid q,a)>0\right\}\ .

For brevity, we write
\delta(S,a,o)
instead of
\delta(S,(a,o))
.
We can then define a
deterministic
finite automaton
\mathbb{B}(\mathcal{P})
with set of states
\mathsf{BeliefSups}(\mathcal{P})
, alphabet
A\times O
, and transition function
\delta
.

We also extend the transition function of
\mathcal{P}
to
\mathsf{Beliefs}(\mathcal{P})
in the natural way:

\delta(\mathbf{b},a,o)=\mathbf{b}_{a,o},

where
\mathbf{b}_{a,o}
is defined in
Equation

1
. Note
that this gives rise to a possibly infinite automaton
since the set of beliefs is infinite.

3.2
Posterior-deterministic POMDPs

Definition 2
.

A POMDP
\mathcal{P}
is called
posterior-deterministic
if
\mathbb{A}(\mathcal{P})
is deterministic.

In other words, a POMDP
\mathcal{P}=(Q,A,O,\mathbf{T})
is posterior-deterministic if for every state
q\in Q
, action
a\in A
, and observation
o\in O
, there exists at most one state
q^{\prime}\in Q
such that
\mathbf{T}(o,q^{\prime}\mid q,a)>0
.
In a posterior-deterministic POMDP, if the current state
q
is known, then taking an action
a
and receiving an observation
o
leads to at most one possible successor state
q^{\prime}
.
We use
\delta\colon Q\times A\times O\to Q
to denote the
transition function
of
\mathbb{A}(\mathcal{P})
.

A crucial property of posterior-deterministic POMDPs is that, once the current state is known, then it will remain known forever as every subsequent action-observation pair leads to a unique successor state.
More generally, the size of the current belief support can never increase when taking actions and receiving observations, as stated in the following lemma.

Lemma 3
.

Let
\mathcal{P}=(Q,A,O,\mathbf{T})
be a posterior-deterministic POMDP.
For every belief support
S
of
\mathcal{P}
and observable history

h\in(A\times O)^{*}
we have

|\delta^{*}(S,h)|\leq|S|\ .

In posterior-deterministic POMDPs, the origin of the partial observability is in the initial belief.
However, in contrast to the more strongly constrained (quasi-)deterministic POMDPs
[
22
,
7
,
6
]
,
even assuming knowledge of the current state, there may still be randomness in the observation received after taking an action and in the subsequent state.

Our main result is that the value-approximation problem is decidable for posterior-deterministic POMDPs with reachability objectives.

Theorem 4
.

There is an algorithm that solves the value-approximation problem for
posterior-deterministic POMDPs. Moreover, given a POMDP
\mathcal{P}
, a belief

\mathbf{b}
, a tolerance
\varepsilon>0
, and a threshold
v\in[0,1]
as
input, deciding which case
applies from (i)
\mathsf{Val}(\mathbf{b})\geq v+\varepsilon
or (ii)
\mathsf{Val}(\mathbf{b})<v

is in 3EXPTIME.

Remark 5

(Memory of
\varepsilon
-optimal strategies)

defines a partial order on the equivalence classes of
\sim
,
which is also denoted by
\preceq
.

Lemma 18
.

If
T\preceq S
, then
|T|\leq|S|
.

Proof.

Follows from
\mathcal{P}
being posterior-deterministic and
Lemma

3
.
∎

Keeping this lemma in mind, we extend the quasi-order
\preceq
as follows.

Definition 19
.

Define a quasi-order
\trianglelefteq
on
\mathsf{BeliefSups}(\mathcal{P})
as

S\trianglelefteq T\quad\iff\quad S\preceq T\text{ or }|S|<|T|\ .

Lemma

18
implies that, for every
S\sim T
, we have
|S|=|T|
.
Therefore,
\trianglelefteq
induces the same equivalence classes as
\preceq
.
Moreover, it induces a partial order on the equivalence classes of
\sim
that extends
\preceq
.

Definition 20
.

The
\mathsf{rank}
of an equivalence class of
\sim
is its height in the partial order induced by
\trianglelefteq
on the equivalence classes of
\sim
.
The
\mathsf{rank}
of a belief support is the rank of its equivalence class for
\sim
.
The
\mathsf{rank}
of a belief
\mathbf{b}
is defined as
\left\lVert\mathbf{b}\right\rVert_{1}\cdot\mathsf{rank}(\mathsf{supp}(\mathbf{b}))
, i.e., the rank of its support weighted by the total probability mass of the belief.

In particular, relying on the assumptions of
Lemma

1
,
\mathsf{rank}(\mathbf{b})=0
if and only if
\mathsf{supp}(\mathbf{b})
is either
\left\{\top\right\}
or
\left\{\bot\right\}
.
The rank is upper-bounded by the height of the partial order induced by
\trianglelefteq
, and is thus at most
2^{|Q|}-1
(which is the cardinality of
\mathsf{BeliefSups}(\mathcal{P})
).

Tree statistics.

We extend the definition of
\mathsf{Val}
and
\mathsf{rank}
to the nodes of
\widehat{\mathrm{{T}}}_{n}(\mathbf{b})
.
We define two statistics,
\textnormal{{tree}-}{\mathsf{val}{}}_{n}
and
\textnormal{{tree}-}{\mathsf{rank}{}}_{n}
, for all nodes of
\widehat{\mathrm{{T}}}_{n}(\mathbf{b})
.
These are used to lower and upper bound the value of the belief associated with the node (upcoming formal statement in
Theorem

21
).
These statistics are defined inductively on the structure of the tree as follows.
Recall that nodes in the tree are sometimes labelled by
sub-
beliefs due to the cut moves, hence the use of
\left\lVert\mathsf{belief}(x)\right\rVert_{1}
(which is not always
1
) in the definitions below.

For a leaf
x
in
\widehat{\mathrm{{T}}}_{n}(\mathbf{b})
,

\displaystyle\textnormal{{tree}-}{\mathsf{val}{}}_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\left\lVert\mathsf{belief}(x)\right\rVert_{1}&\text{if $\mathsf{supp}(\mathsf{belief}(x))=\left\{\top\right\}$}\\
0&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}\quad\text{ and }

\displaystyle\textnormal{{tree}-}{\mathsf{rank}{}}_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=\mathsf{rank}(\mathsf{belief}(x))\ .

Observe that
\textnormal{{tree}-}{\mathsf{rank}{}}_{n}(x)
is
0
if
\mathsf{supp}(\mathsf{belief}(x))
is either
\left\{\top\right\}
or
\left\{\bot\right\}
, and is at least
\left\lVert\mathsf{belief}(x)\right\rVert_{1}
otherwise.

For a sum node
x
,

\displaystyle\textnormal{{tree}-}{\mathsf{val}{}}_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\text{child $y$ of $x$}}p_{y}\cdot\textnormal{{tree}-}{\mathsf{val}{}}_{n}(y)\quad\text{ and }

\displaystyle\textnormal{{tree}-}{\mathsf{rank}{}}_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\text{child $y$ of $x$}}p_{y}\cdot\textnormal{{tree}-}{\mathsf{rank}{}}_{n}(y)\ ,

where
p_{y}
is the weight of the edge connecting the child
y
to its parent
x
.

For a cut node
x
with
y
being the only child of
x
,
\textnormal{{tree}-}{\mathsf{val}{}}_{n}(x)
(resp.
\textnormal{{tree}-}{\mathsf{rank}{}}_{n}(x)
) is equal to
\textnormal{{tree}-}{\mathsf{val}{}}_{n}(y)
(resp.
\textnormal{{tree}-}{\mathsf{rank}{}}_{n}(y)
).

For a max node
x
,

\displaystyle\textnormal{{tree}-}{\mathsf{val}{}}_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=\max_{\text{child $y$ of $x$}}\textnormal{{tree}-}{\mathsf{val}{}}_{n}(y)\quad\text{ and }

\displaystyle\textnormal{{tree}-}{\mathsf{rank}{}}_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=\max_{\text{child $y$ of $x$}}\textnormal{{tree}-}{\mathsf{rank}{}}_{n}(y)\ .

5.2
Correctness of the algorithm

We now prove that we can obtain lower and upper bounds on the value of the belief associated with any node of
\widehat{\mathrm{{T}}}_{n}(\mathbf{b})
using
\textnormal{{tree}-}{\mathsf{val}{}}_{n}
and
\textnormal{{tree}-}{\mathsf{rank}{}}_{n}
, which means our algorithm is sound.

Theorem 21

(Correctness)
.

For all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and all nodes
x
of
\widehat{\mathrm{{T}}}_{n}(\mathbf{b})
,

\textnormal{{tree}-}{\mathsf{val}{}}_{n}(x)\leq\mathsf{Val}(\mathsf{belief}(x))\leq\textnormal{{tree}-}{\mathsf{val}{}}_{n}(x)+\textnormal{{tree}-}{\mathsf{rank}{}}_{n}(x)+|\mathsf{supp}(\mathsf{belief}(x))|\cdot\theta\ .

Proof.

The proof of this theorem is a simple induction on the structure of the tree.

Let
x
be a leaf of
\widehat{\mathrm{{T}}}_{n}(\mathbf{b})
.
If
\mathsf{supp}(\mathsf{belief}(x))
is
\left\{\top\right\}
(resp.
\left\{\bot\right\}
), then
\textnormal{{tree}-}{\mathsf{val}{}}_{n}(x)=\mathsf{Val}(\mathsf{belief}(x))=\left\lVert\mathsf{belief}(x)\right\rVert_{1}
(resp.
0
), so the claim holds.
Otherwise, let
\mathbf{b}=\mathsf{belief}(x)
.
We have
\textnormal{{tree}-}{\mathsf{val}{}}_{n}(x)=0
and
0\leq\mathsf{Val}(\mathbf{b})\leq\left\lVert\mathbf{b}\right\rVert_{1}\leq\left\lVert\mathbf{b}\right\rVert_{1}\cdot\mathsf{rank}(\mathsf{supp}(\mathbf{b}))=\textnormal{{tree}-}{\mathsf{rank}{}}_{n}(x)
, so the claim holds as well.
This covers case
Rule -(ii)
of
Definition

16
.

Let
x
be an inner node of
\widehat{\mathrm{{T}}}_{n}(\mathbf{b})
.
Assume by induction that the claim holds for all children of
x
.
We now distinguish cases according to its type: sum, cut, or max node.

•

If
x
is a sum node, it is unfolded using
Rule -(i)
(resp.
Rule -(iv)
).
In this case,
\textnormal{{tree}-}{\mathsf{val}{}}_{n}(x)
,
\mathsf{Val}(\mathsf{belief}(x))
, and
\textnormal{{tree}-}{\mathsf{rank}{}}_{n}(x)
are obtained as weighted sums of the corresponding values of the children of
x
: by definition for
\textnormal{{tree}-}{\mathsf{val}{}}_{n}
and
\textnormal{{tree}-}{\mathsf{rank}{}}_{n}
, and by (
2
) (resp.
Theorem

11
) for
\mathsf{Val}
.
Moreover, for all children
y
of
x
, we have
|\mathsf{supp}(\mathsf{belief}(y))|\leq|\mathsf{supp}(\mathsf{belief}(x))|
by
Lemma

3
.
The claim then follows using the induction hypothesis on all children of
x
.

•

If
x
is a cut node, it is unfolded using
Rule -(iii)
.
Let
y
be its unique child. Let
k
be the number of states
q\in\mathsf{supp}(\mathsf{belief}(x))
such that
\mathsf{belief}(x)(q)<\theta
, i.e., the number of states removed by
\mathsf{Cut}_{\theta}
.
We have by definition that
\textnormal{{tree}-}{\mathsf{val}{}}_{n}(x)=\textnormal{{tree}-}{\mathsf{val}{}}_{n}(y)
and
\textnormal{{tree}-}{\mathsf{rank}{}}_{n}(x)=\textnormal{{tree}-}{\mathsf{rank}{}}_{n}(y)
.

For the left inequality, observe that
\mathsf{Val}(\mathsf{belief}(y))\leq\mathsf{Val}(\mathsf{belief}(x))
since
\mathsf{belief}(y)
is obtained from
\mathsf{belief}(x)
by removing some probability mass.
Hence, by induction hypothesis on
y
,

\textnormal{{tree}-}{\mathsf{val}{}}_{n}(x)=\textnormal{{tree}-}{\mathsf{val}{}}_{n}(y)\leq\mathsf{Val}(\mathsf{belief}(y))\leq\mathsf{Val}(\mathsf{belief}(x))\ .

For the right inequality, observe that, by definition of
\mathsf{Val}
and
\mathsf{Cut}_{\theta}
,

\mathsf{Val}(\mathsf{belief}(x))\leq\mathsf{Val}(\mathsf{belief}(y))+k\cdot\theta
.
Using the induction hypothesis on
y
, we thus obtain

\displaystyle\mathsf{Val}(\mathsf{belief}(x))

\displaystyle\leq\textnormal{{tree}-}{\mathsf{val}{}}_{n}(y)+\textnormal{{tree}-}{\mathsf{rank}{}}_{n}(y)+|\mathsf{supp}(\mathsf{belief}(y))|\cdot\theta+k\cdot\theta

\displaystyle=\textnormal{{tree}-}{\mathsf{val}{}}_{n}(x)+\textnormal{{tree}-}{\mathsf{rank}{}}_{n}(x)+|\mathsf{supp}(\mathsf{belief}(x))|\cdot\theta\ .

•

If
x
is a max node, it is unfolded using
Rule -(v)
or
Rule -(vi)
.
In this case,
\textnormal{{tree}-}{\mathsf{val}{}}_{n}(x)
,
\mathsf{Val}(\mathsf{belief}(x))
, and
\textnormal{{tree}-}{\mathsf{rank}{}}_{n}(x)
are defined as maxima of the corresponding values of the children of
x
: by definition for
\textnormal{{tree}-}{\mathsf{val}{}}_{n}
and
\textnormal{{tree}-}{\mathsf{rank}{}}_{n}
, and by
Theorem

13
(resp. (
3
)) for
\mathsf{Val}
.
Moreover, for all children
y
of
x
, we have
|\mathsf{supp}(\mathsf{belief}(y))|\leq|\mathsf{supp}(\mathsf{belief}(x))|
by
Lemma

3
.
The claim then follows using the induction hypothesis on all children of
x
.
∎

5.3
Termination of the algorithm

We now show that the upper and lower bounds provided by
Theorem

21
converge to an
\varepsilon
-approximation of the actual value of the belief as
n
goes to infinity.
Fix
N=2^{|Q|+1}
.
Let
p_{\min}^{\mathcal{P}}
be the smallest non-zero probability occurring in the syntactic description of
\mathcal{P}
, i.e.,
\min\left\{\mathbf{T}(o,q^{\prime}\mid q,a)\mid\mathbf{T}(o,q^{\prime}\mid q,a)>0\right\}
.
Take
c=\frac{(p_{\min}^{\mathcal{P}}\cdot\theta)^{N}}{2^{|Q|}}
.

Theorem 22

(Termination)
.

For all
\varepsilon>0
and natural number
n_{\varepsilon}
such that

n_{\varepsilon}\geq\frac{N}{c}\cdot\left((|Q|+1)\cdot\ln(2)+\ln\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)\right),

we have

\textnormal{{tree}-}{\mathsf{val}{}}_{n_{\varepsilon}}(\textnormal{{root}}(\widehat{\mathrm{{T}}}_{n_{\varepsilon}}(\mathbf{b})))\leq\mathsf{Val}(\mathbf{b})\leq\textnormal{{tree}-}{\mathsf{val}{}}_{n_{\varepsilon}}(\textnormal{{root}}(\widehat{\mathrm{{T}}}_{n_{\varepsilon}}(\mathbf{b})))+\varepsilon\ .

To prove this theorem, our main technical tool is the following lemma, which shows that the
\textnormal{{tree}-}{\mathsf{rank}{}}
of a node always decreases by the multiplicative factor
1-c
after
N
additional steps of the tree unfolding.

Lemma 23
.

Let
\mathbf{b}
be a sub-belief of
\mathcal{P}
.
Then,

\textnormal{{tree}-}{\mathsf{rank}{}}_{N}(\textnormal{{root}}(\widehat{\mathrm{{T}}}_{N}(\mathbf{b})))\leq(1-c)\cdot\mathsf{rank}(\mathbf{b})\ .

We show how to use this lemma to prove
Theorem

22
before proving the lemma itself.

Proof of
Theorem

22
using
Lemma

23
.

Let
r=\textnormal{{root}}(\widehat{\mathrm{{T}}}_{n_{\varepsilon}}(\mathbf{b}))
.
By
Theorem

21
, we know that

\textnormal{{tree}-}{\mathsf{val}{}}_{n_{\varepsilon}}(r)\leq\mathsf{Val}(\mathbf{b})\leq\textnormal{{tree}-}{\mathsf{val}{}}_{n_{\varepsilon}}(r)+\textnormal{{tree}-}{\mathsf{rank}{}}_{n_{\varepsilon}}(r)+|\mathsf{supp}(\mathbf{b})|\cdot\theta\ .

Recall that we defined
\theta=\frac{\varepsilon}{2\cdot|Q|}
.
Thus,

|\mathsf{supp}(\mathbf{b})|\cdot\theta\leq|Q|\cdot\theta\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\ .

To obtain
Theorem

22
, it now suffices to show that
\textnormal{{tree}-}{\mathsf{rank}{}}_{n_{\varepsilon}}(r)\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}
.

For
m\leq n
, we consider
\widehat{\mathrm{{T}}}_{m}(\mathbf{b})
to be a subtree of
\widehat{\mathrm{{T}}}_{n}(\mathbf{b})
with the same root.
Let
x
be a leaf of
\widehat{\mathrm{{T}}}_{n}(\mathbf{b})
.
Observe that by definition of
\widehat{\mathrm{{T}}}_{n}(\mathbf{b})
, expanding the tree by
N
additional steps from a leaf
x
of
\widehat{\mathrm{{T}}}_{n}(\mathbf{b})
is equivalent to building
\widehat{\mathrm{{T}}}_{N}(\mathsf{belief}(x))
, so

\textnormal{{tree}-}{\mathsf{rank}{}}_{n+N}(x)=\textnormal{{tree}-}{\mathsf{rank}{}}_{N}(\textnormal{{root}}(\widehat{\mathrm{{T}}}_{N}(\mathsf{belief}(x))))\ .

Lemma

23
therefore implies that for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and leaves
x
of
\widehat{\mathrm{{T}}}_{n}(\mathbf{b})
,

\textnormal{{tree}-}{\mathsf{rank}{}}_{n+N}(x)\leq(1-c)\cdot\mathsf{rank}(\mathsf{belief}(x))\ .

By definition of
\textnormal{{tree}-}{\mathsf{rank}{}}
for leaves, we have
\textnormal{{tree}-}{\mathsf{rank}{}}_{n}(x)=\mathsf{rank}(\mathsf{belief}(x))
,
so

\textnormal{{tree}-}{\mathsf{rank}{}}_{n+N}(x)\leq(1-c)\cdot\textnormal{{tree}-}{\mathsf{rank}{}}_{n}(x)\ .

By definition of
\textnormal{{tree}-}{\mathsf{rank}{}}
for inner nodes (obtained using
\max
or weighted sums of children), observe that if the
\textnormal{{tree}-}{\mathsf{rank}{}}
of all leaves is multiplied by a factor
1-c
(or smaller), then this change is propagated to all nodes in the tree.
Hence, one can prove by induction that for all nodes
x
of
\widehat{\mathrm{{T}}}_{n+N}(\mathbf{b})
, we have

\textnormal{{tree}-}{\mathsf{rank}{}}_{n+N}(x)\leq(1-c)\cdot\textnormal{{tree}-}{\mathsf{rank}{}}_{n}(x)\ .

Thus, for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

\textnormal{{tree}-}{\mathsf{rank}{}}_{kN}(\textnormal{{root}}(\widehat{\mathrm{{T}}}_{kN}(\mathbf{b})))\leq(1-c)^{k}\cdot\mathsf{rank}(\mathbf{b})\leq(1-c)^{k}\cdot 2^{|Q|}\ .

Using that
1-c\leq e^{-c}

[
16
, Inequality 142]
, we obtain that

(1-c)^{k}\cdot 2^{|Q|}\leq e^{-c\cdot k}\cdot 2^{|Q|}\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}

when
k\geq\frac{1}{c}\cdot\left((|Q|+1)\cdot\ln(2)+\ln(\frac{1}{\varepsilon})\right)
.
∎

, the only missing piece to prove the termination of our algorithm.

(5)

6.4
The posterior probability of starting in a given state

Let
\mathcal{P}=(Q,A,O,\mathbf{T})
be a POMDP.
1
1

1

Results of
Section

6.4
hold for general POMDPs, not only posterior-deterministic ones.
However, we will only use them in the posterior-deterministic case.

For every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we define a
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{F}_{n}
on
\mathsf{Runs}(\mathcal{P})
(which, we recall, is a subset of
(Q\times A\times O)^{\omega}
) that corresponds to the information available to the agent after the first
n
action-observation pairs.
We extend the notion of cylinders to

histories: for an observable history

\mathsf{Cyl}(h)
is the set of runs in
\mathsf{Runs}(\mathcal{P})
whose projection to
(A\times O)^{\left\lvert h\right\rvert}
matches

Formally, for
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we define

\mathcal{F}_{n}=\left\{\mathsf{Cyl}(h)\ \middle|\ \text{$h$ is observable and $\left\lvert h\right\rvert=n$}\right\}\ .

The sequence
\mathcal{F}_{0}\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{1}\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{2}\subseteq\dots
defines a
filtration
.

Let
\mathbf{b}
be an initial belief, and
\pi
be a strategy.
For every
q\in\mathsf{supp}(\mathbf{b})
, define a sequence of random variables
(X^{q}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
that track the probability to have started in
q
given the first
n
actions and observations, in addition to the initial belief
\mathbf{b}
.

Formally, consider the event
\mathsf{Cyl}(q)\in\mathcal{F}
(i.e., “the initial state is
q
”).
For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we define the random variable
X^{q}_{n}\colon(A\times O)^{\omega}\to[0,1]
as

X^{q}_{n}(\rho)=\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{\mathbf{b}}(\mathsf{Cyl}(q)\mid\rho_{\leq n})\ ,

representing the a posteriori probability to have started in
q
given that we have observed the first
n
actions and observations of run
\rho
.
In other words,

X^{q}_{n}=\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{\mathbf{b}}(\mathsf{Cyl}(q)\mid\mathcal{F}_{n})\ .

In particular,
X^{q}_{n}
is
\mathcal{F}_{n}
-measurable for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Notice that
X^{q}_{0}=\mathbf{b}(q)
.
This sequence of random variables is a
martingale

[
14
, Section 4.2]
for all possible initial states
q
.
Observe that this value can be computed inductively using Bayes’ rule, and depends on the initial belief
\mathbf{b}
but not on the strategy
\pi
(as long as
\pi
takes the actions along the history with non-zero probability).

Lemma 39
.

For every
q\in\mathsf{supp}(\mathbf{b})
, the sequence
(X^{q}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
defines a martingale with respect to the filtration
(\mathcal{F}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
.

Proof.

Let
q\in\mathsf{supp}(\mathbf{b})
.
First of all, for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
X^{q}_{n}
is
\mathcal{F}_{n}
-measurable by definition.
Moreover, for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, it holds that
0\leq X^{q}_{n}\leq 1
.
Hence,
\mathbb{E}^{\pi}_{\mathbf{b}}\left[|X^{q}_{n}|\right]\leq 1<\infty
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

To show that
(X^{q}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a martingale with respect to the filtration
(\mathcal{F}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
,
it remains to show that for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\mathbb{E}^{\pi}_{\mathbf{b}}\left[X^{q}_{n+1}\mid\mathcal{F}_{n}\right]=X^{q}_{n}
.

By definition, for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

X^{q}_{n}=\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{\mathbf{b}}(\mathsf{Cyl}(q)\mid\mathcal{F}_{n})=\mathbb{E}^{\pi}_{\mathbf{b}}\left[\mathbbm{1}_{\mathsf{Cyl}(q)}\mid\mathcal{F}_{n}\right]\ .

Hence, for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{\pi}_{\mathbf{b}}\left[X^{q}_{n+1}\mid\mathcal{F}_{n}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}^{\pi}_{\mathbf{b}}\left[\mathbb{E}^{\pi}_{\mathbf{b}}\left[\mathbbm{1}_{\mathsf{Cyl}(q)}\mid\mathcal{F}_{n+1}\right]\mid\mathcal{F}_{n}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}^{\pi}_{\mathbf{b}}\left[\mathbbm{1}_{\mathsf{Cyl}(q)}\mid\mathcal{F}_{n}\right]\text{ by the law of iterated expectations}

\displaystyle=X^{q}_{n}\ .\qed

The following is an obvious but useful corollary of
Lemma

39
.

Corollary 40
.

\mathbb{E}^{\pi}_{\mathbf{b}}\left[X^{q}_{n}\right]=\mathbf{b}(q)\leq 1
for all
q\in\mathsf{supp}(\mathbf{b})
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Using Doob’s convergence theorem
[
14
, Theorem 4.6.7]
, given that the martingales
(X^{q}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
are bounded and thus uniformly integrable, we get the following.

Corollary 41
.

For every
q\in\mathsf{supp}(\mathbf{b})
,
there exists a random variable
X^{q}_{\infty}
such that the sequence
(X^{q}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}

.

6.5
Distinguishing SECs

Theorem

is our main result about distinguishing SECs; it shows that in a distinguishing SEC, there is a way to distinguish between states that are distinguishable in practice, by following a strategy staying in the SEC that separates at the limit the belief into disjoint parts.

We fix for this section a posterior-deterministic POMDP

We first prove the “

Lemma 42

\mathsf{Val}(\mathbf{b})\leq\sum_{C\in S/{\equiv_{(f,S)}}}\mathsf{Val}(\left.\mathbf{b}\right|_{C})\ .

Proof.

By definition,

For a fixed strategy

We conclude that

\displaystyle\mathsf{Val}(\mathbf{b})

\displaystyle=\sup_{\pi}\sum_{C\in S/{\equiv_{(f,S)}}}\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{\left.\mathbf{b}\right|_{C}}(\mathsf{Reach}_{\top})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{C\in S/{\equiv_{(f,S)}}}\sup_{\pi}\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{\left.\mathbf{b}\right|_{C}}(\mathsf{Reach}_{\top})

\displaystyle=\sum_{C\in S/{\equiv_{(f,S)}}}\mathsf{Val}(\left.\mathbf{b}\right|_{C})\ ,

7
Other related works

Beyond the works discussed in the introduction, we mention connections to several related lines of research.

Point-based value-iteration methods

[
25
]
and their variants provide practical algorithms for approximate POMDP solving, but without formal approximation guarantees: they are heuristics that work well empirically but may not converge to the true value.
To the best of our knowledge, our result provides the first provable
\varepsilon
-approximation algorithm for a class strictly larger than deterministic POMDPs.

The decidability landscape for POMDPs with
\omega
-regular objectives
has been systematically studied
[
8
]
, establishing a detailed map of decidable and undecidable fragments.
Our work contributes to this landscape by identifying a new decidable fragment for reachability.

On the safety side, recent work
[
5
]
has shown that safety values (the complement of reachability) can be approximated in general POMDPs, contrasting with the undecidability of reachability.

Proof.

Our proof is in two steps: first, we show how to reduce to the case of a single target state, and then we show how to reduce to the case of a single state with value

These two steps can then be composed to obtain the desired result.

We construct a new POMDP

is a fresh state not in

is a fresh observation not in

for all

,

\mathbf{T}^{\prime}(o,q^{\prime}\mid q,a)=\mathbf{T}(o,q^{\prime}\mid q,a),

•

\mathbf{T}^{\prime}(o_{\top},\top\mid q,a)=\sum_{o\in O,q^{\prime}\in T}\mathbf{T}(o,q^{\prime}\mid q,a),

In particular, state
\top
is absorbing and observable in

.
Moreover, this construction can be done in linear time in the size of
\mathcal{P}
.

We also define the initial belief
\mathbf{b}^{\prime}
in
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}

\mathbf{b}^{\prime}(q)=\mathbf{b}(q)

\mathbf{b}^{\prime}(\top)=\sum_{q\in T}\mathbf{b}(q)

\pi

\mathcal{P}^{\prime}

from
\mathbf{b}^{\prime}

This implies in particular that the values of
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
with respect to the reachability objectives defined by

\{\top\}

Let
\pi
be a strategy in
\mathcal{P}
.
We define a strategy
\pi^{\prime}

as follows.
Let

Intuitively, we make strategy

\pi^{\prime}
as

\pi^{\prime}(h^{\prime})=\begin{cases}\pi(h^{\prime})&\text{if $h^{\prime}$ contains no occurrence of $o_{\top}$}\\
\delta_{a}&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

where
\delta_{a}
is the Dirac distribution on
a
.

Observe that
\mathsf{Reach}_{T}
is the disjoint union of the cylinders
\mathsf{Cyl}(h)
for all histories
h\in Q\times(A\times O\times Q)^{*}
that visit
T
a single time at their last state.
For such an
h
, let
h^{\prime}
be the corresponding history in
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
obtained by replacing the last observation with
o_{\top}
and the last state with
\top
.
By construction, both
\pi
and
\pi^{\prime}
coincide exactly up to the first visit of
T
or
\top
, hence

\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{\mathbf{b}}(\mathsf{Cyl}(h))=\mathbb{P}^{\pi^{\prime}}_{\mathbf{b}^{\prime}}(\mathsf{Cyl}(h^{\prime}))\ .

Summing over all such disjoint cylinders
\mathsf{Cyl}(h)
, we obtain

\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{\mathbf{b}}(\mathsf{Reach}_{T})=\mathbb{P}^{\pi^{\prime}}_{\mathbf{b}^{\prime}}(\mathsf{Reach}_{\{\top\}})\ .

Conversely, let
\pi^{\prime}
be a strategy in
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
.
We define a strategy
\pi
in
\mathcal{P}
as follows.
Let
a\in A
be an arbitrary action.
Let
h\in(A\times O)^{*}
be an observable history in
\mathcal{P}
.
We distinguish whether
h
is a possible observable history in
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
or not.
One can show that
h
is a possible observable history in
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
if and only if there is a history
\overline{h}\in Q\times(A\times O\times Q)^{*}
in
\mathcal{P}
such that its projection to
(A\times O)^{*}
is
h
and none of the states in
\overline{h}
belong to
T
.

We now define
\pi
on an observable history
h
as:

•

if
h
is a possible observable history in
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
from
\mathbf{b}^{\prime}
, we define
\pi(h)=\pi^{\prime}(h)
;

•

if not, it means that all histories
\overline{h}
with projection
h
have necessarily reached
T
in
\mathcal{P}
, so we may define
\pi(h)=\delta_{a}
.

Once again, for every history
h\in Q\times(A\times O\times Q)^{*}
that visits
T
a single time at its last state, let
h^{\prime}
be the corresponding history in
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
obtained by replacing the last observation with
o_{\top}
and the last state with
\top
.
By construction, both
\pi
and
\pi^{\prime}
coincide exactly up to the first visit of
T
or
\top
, hence

\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{\mathbf{b}}(\mathsf{Cyl}(h))=\mathbb{P}^{\pi^{\prime}}_{\mathbf{b}^{\prime}}(\mathsf{Cyl}(h^{\prime}))\ .

Summing over all such disjoint cylinders
\mathsf{Cyl}(h)
, we obtain

\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{\mathbf{b}}(\mathsf{Reach}_{T})=\mathbb{P}^{\pi^{\prime}}_{\mathbf{b}^{\prime}}(\mathsf{Reach}_{\{\top\}})\ .

We now turn to the second part of the lemma.
We prove that we can assume without loss of generality that there is a single state
\bot
with value
0
, which is absorbing and observable.
Let
Q_{\bot}=\left\{q\in Q\ \middle|\ \mathsf{Val}^{\mathcal{P}}_{T}(\delta_{q})=0\right\}
be the set of states with value
0
.
They can be easily identified by standard graph algorithms, since they are the states from which there is no path to
T
in the underlying graph.
We construct a new POMDP
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}=(Q^{\prime},A,O^{\prime},\mathbf{T}^{\prime})
with
Q^{\prime}=(Q\setminus Q_{\bot})\cup\{\bot\}
, where
\bot
is a fresh state not in
Q
,
O^{\prime}=O\cup\{o_{\bot}\}
, where
o_{\bot}
is a fresh observation not in
O
, and the transition function
\mathbf{T}^{\prime}
defined as follows:

•

for all
q,q^{\prime}\in Q\setminus Q_{\bot}
,
a\in A
, and
o\in O
,

\mathbf{T}^{\prime}(o,q^{\prime}\mid q,a)=\mathbf{T}(o,q^{\prime}\mid q,a),

•

for all
q\in Q\setminus Q_{\bot}
and
a\in A
,

\mathbf{T}^{\prime}(o_{\bot},\bot\mid q,a)=\sum_{o\in O,q^{\prime}\in Q_{\bot}}\mathbf{T}(o,q^{\prime}\mid q,a),

•

for all
a\in A
,

\mathbf{T}^{\prime}(o_{\bot},\bot\mid\bot,a)=1.

By construction, state
\bot
is absorbing, observable, and the only state with value
0
in
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
.
Moreover, this construction can be done in linear time in the size of
\mathcal{P}
since
Q_{\bot}
can be identified in linear time (they are the states that cannot reach
T
in the underlying graph).

The constructions are then the same as in the proof for the single target state: we build a correspondence between strategies in
\mathcal{P}
and strategies in
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
that makes them coincide on all observable histories that have not surely reached
Q_{\bot}
or
\bot
yet.
Since histories that reach
Q_{\bot}
or
\bot
have value
0
in both POMDPs, the probabilities of reaching the target sets are preserved.
This shows in particular that the values of
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
with respect to the reachability objectives defined by
T
and
\{\top\}
, respectively, are equal.
∎
