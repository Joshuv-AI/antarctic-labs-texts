Abruzzini procedure: transsternal bronchial closure for left bronchopleural fistula post-pneumonectomy
Authors: José de Sá Moraes Neto, Murilo Fernandes de Souza, Paulo Manuel Pego-Fernandes, Alessandro Wasum Mariani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBronchopleural fistula (BPF) is a rare but severe complication following pneumonectomy, associated with high morbidity and mortality, particularly when pleural contamination occurs. Several predisposing factors have been described, including preoperative corticosteroid use, right-sided pneumonectomy, neoadjuvant therapy, diabetes mellitus, and the presence of a long bronchial stump. Preventive strategies such as bronchial stump reinforcement have been widely employed, though the optimal technique and material remain controversial. Therapeutic options range from minimally invasive approaches, including endobronchial closure with cauterization or biological glue, to complex surgical procedures such as intrathoracic muscle transposition, thoracostomy, and thoracoplasty. In cases where conventional reclosure of the stump is hindered by infection or adhesions, the transsternal transpericardial approach described by Abruzzini in 1961 offers an alternative that avoids direct manipulation of the infected pleural cavity. This technique enables safe exposure of the mediastinal structures through a full median sternotomy, allowing direct closure of the bronchial stump. The present study provides a descriptive account and educational video of the Abruzzini technique, based on a real clinical case. The patient, a 72-year-old male with silicosis and endobronchial aspergilloma, underwent left pneumonectomy with radical mediastinal lymphadenectomy. Postoperative evolution was complicated by stump dehiscence and recurrent BPF, despite previous surgical attempts with manual suturing and thymic flap reinforcement. Definitive management was achieved through the transsternal transpericardial approach, involving careful mediastinal dissection, protection of major vascular structures, and closure of the bronchial stump with stapling under bronchoscopic guidance. This report emphasizes the technical nuances of the procedure, including the impact of mediastinal shift in left pneumonectomy, the risks of vascular injury, and the necessity of meticulous preoperative imaging. Differences between left- and right-sided fistulas are also highlighted, underscoring the importance of individualized surgical planning. BPF remains one of the most challenging post-pneumonectomy complications, with mortality exceeding 50% in uncontrolled cases. Although rarely performed, the Abruzzini technique remains a valuable tool in selected scenarios, with reported success rates above 80%. By documenting this surgical approach through video demonstration, the present work aims to enhance thoracic surgery training, preserve classical techniques, and promote safer management of complex BPF.Keywords: Bronchopleural fistula (BPF), pneumonectomy, Abruzzini

Highlight box

Surgical highlights

• The procedure followed established principles of median sternotomy for mediastinal access, with appropriate anesthetic preparation, selective intubation, and intraoperative bronchoscopy for airway control. Dissection of the great vessels and careful handling of the atrium and pulmonary arteries also adhered to conventional practices, ensuring safe retropericardial exposure.

What is conventional and what is novel/modified?

• The choice of the transsternal transpericardial approach for definitive closure of the bronchopleural fistula (BPF) represents a relevant modification compared to classic approaches (such as lateral thoracotomy or reinforcement with muscle/mediastinal flaps). The use of a stapler under direct endoscopic guidance, combined with repositioning of the great vessels to widen the surgical field, enabled safe closure even in the presence of fibrosis and dense adhesions. This technical adaptation was necessary due to mediastinal shift after left pneumonectomy and the large size of the fistula, which precluded less invasive alternatives.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• The technique described broadens treatment options for post-pneumonectomy BPFs, particularly in complex cases with large dehiscence, retracted mediastinum, and dense adhesions. This report suggests that the transpericardial approach may be a viable and safe alternative in selected patients, especially when conventional measures such as direct suture or local flap coverage have failed. Further studies, including prospective series, are required to validate reproducibility and better define its indications.

Introduction

Bronchopleural fistula (BPF) is a rare but potentially fatal complication that may occur after pneumonectomy, manifesting in the immediate or delayed postoperative period. Its management poses a significant clinical and surgical challenge due to the risk of pleural cavity contamination and high morbidity and mortality rates (1,2). The most important risk factors for developing BPF include preoperative steroid therapy, right-sided pneumonectomy, neoadjuvant therapy, diabetes, or leaving a long bronchial stump (3). To reduce the occurrence of BPF, several surgical methods may improve bronchial stump vascularization. Despite many cases of successful buttressing, the ideal tissue material has still not been identified, and the actual benefit of the bronchial stump coverage after pneumonectomy remains controversial (4).

Therapeutic options range from conservative procedures, such as endobronchial closure with cauterization or biological glue, to more invasive surgical interventions, including intrathoracic muscle transposition, thoracostomy, and thoracoplasty (1,2).

Immediate thoracotomy with new bronchial stump closure is recommended in cases of overt fistula; however, the management of a contaminated pleural cavity remains controversial. In 1961, Abruzzini described a transsternal transmediastinal approach for bronchial stump closure to avoid direct manipulation of an infected pleural cavity. This technique involves a full median sternotomy and exposure of mediastinal vascular structures, allowing the stump to be closed with interrupted sutures. The approach is relatively simpler when the fistula affects the left main bronchus, due to the shorter length of the right bronchial stump (3,4).

As the indications for pneumonectomy have decreased, so too have the frequency of complications such as BPF. Such as reducing practical learning opportunities for surgical trainees. Therefore, disseminating technical knowledge and promoting safe management methods are essential to improve patient care (4,5).

In this context, the present study aims to describe, through an illustrative video, the practical application of the Abruzzini technique for the treatment of BPF, contributing to thoracic surgery education and patient safety (6,7).

This study consists of a descriptive report and educational demonstration of the Abruzzini surgical procedure, illustrated through a video developed from a real clinical case. We present this article in accordance with the SUPER reporting checklist (available at https://jovs.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jovs-25-25/rc).

A 72-year-old male with a known diagnosis of silicosis under pulmonology follow-up was admitted for progressive dyspnea and hemoptysis sputum. Chest computed tomography (CT) revealed a left perihilar mass. Flexible bronchoscopy with biopsy confirmed an endobronchial aspergilloma.

Given the clinical findings and intraoperative suspicion of a concomitant sarcomatoid neoplasm, a left pneumonectomy with radical mediastinal lymphadenectomy was performed. The immediate postoperative course was uneventful, with clinical stability maintained for 7 days.

On postoperative day 8, the patient developed worsening ventilator parameters. Chest radiography revealed contralateral mediastinal shift, and bronchoscopy confirmed a bronchial stump fistula. Figure 1 demonstrates the CT chest of the patient.

Any significant pleural infection should be managed before surgery. In this case, the large size of the fistula precluded adequate cavity instillation, prompting fistula correction before debridement or irrigation.

Exploratory thoracotomy with manual stump suture and thymic tissue flap coverage was performed.

Operating room setup and team positioning should follow standard protocols for procedures requiring full median sternotomy. Monitor placement for rigid endoscopy and bronchoscopy should be verified.

A gel pad is placed beneath the shoulders to anteriorly displace the thorax, facilitating access to the tracheobronchial tree.

Intubation is usually performed using a long double-lumen endotracheal tube, inserted into the healthy bronchus. During the procedure, the tube is retracted to verify adequate bronchial stump closure. The contralateral bronchus should also be checked for unexpected distortion.

Surgical draping follows the usual protocol for median sternotomy.

Anesthesiologists should be instructed to aspirate airways regularly to avoid spillage into the healthy lung. Preoperative discussion regarding intubation strategy is recommended.

Step-by-step description

A key concern is careful handling of the great vessels, which must be gently retracted to access retropericardial structures and avoid injury during manipulation of the tracheobronchial tree, especially when using a stapler. The video demonstrates the surgical technique as seen in Video 3.

Transsternal bronchial closure for left bronchopleural fistula post-pneumonectomy.

A standard median sternotomy is performed to access the mediastinum (0:03'').

The aorta (Ao) and superior vena cava (SVC) are retracted to expose the right pulmonary artery (RPA), left pulmonary artery (LPA), and posterior pericardium. These structures are isolated with cardiac tape to improve maneuverability (0:15'').

After aortic traction, the RPA is dissected and secured. It lies anterior to the posterior pericardium and slightly below the carina. The SVC is retracted laterally with caution (0:22''). In this case, the LPA stump was restapled for improved visualization (0:25'').

Following vessel mobilization (0:55''), the posterior pericardium is opened with electrocautery to access the tracheobronchial tree. Adhesions and lymph nodes are removed (0:39'').

The carina and left main bronchus are identified (1:00'').

Blunt dissection and a Mixter clamp assist in separating the esophagus from the trachea. A nasogastric or orogastric tube aids esophageal identification.

With bronchoscopy, the stapler is introduced and positioned to close the stump (1:05''). Optimal positioning is critical to avoid injury to nearby structures such as the SVC. The bronchoscopy view should be available to the surgeon to ensure the staple line does not distort the contralateral bronchus or trachea (1:17'').

Once confirmed, the stump is stapled. An Endo GIA™ Ultra 12 mm stapler with a 30 mm green cartridge (for thick tissue) was used (1:37'').

Ideally, the residual distal stump should be removed to prevent recurrence or mediastinitis. In this case, dense adhesions prevented its removal (1:52'').

Saline irrigation over both stumps confirms airtight closure.

A 28-Fr chest tube is placed near the surgical site. Sternotomy is closed by the standard procedure.

The procedure lasted 190 minutes, and the bleeding was estimated at 330 mm.

Postoperative considerations and tasks

Twelve days after this second surgery, the patient again deteriorated clinically and radiologically. CT findings suggested stump dehiscence. A new surgical intervention was then performed via the transsternal transpericardial route for definitive closure of the BPF. The technique is described below.

Tips and pearls

This study presents a detailed account of the Abruzzini surgical procedure through an educational video based on a real clinical case. The technique includes full median sternotomy, transmediastinal dissection, and bronchial stump closure with interrupted sutures, as described in the literature. Technical aspects of mediastinal exposure and necessary adaptations depending on the side of the fistula are also addressed. The video was created for educational purposes, with a focus on teaching thoracic surgeons and residents.

Discussion

BPF remains one of the most challenging and potentially fatal post-pneumonectomy complications, with mortality exceeding 50% in cases of uncontrolled pleural infection. When the bronchial stump is inaccessible by conventional means due to extensive infection or adhesions, the Abruzzini technique offers an effective alternative.

The transsternal transpericardial approach allows direct visualization of the tracheobronchial tree, enabling precise stump closure even in hostile environments. Previous studies have shown that, when properly indicated and executed, this technique can achieve definitive fistula control with success rates over 80%, especially in left-sided cases with mediastinal shift (5,6). Some differences have been described between left and right BPF. In left pneumonectomy, the pronounced mediastinal shift frequently narrows the retropericardial space, making exposure of the carina and left main bronchus particularly challenging. In addition, the close relationship of the LPA stump and descending Ao requires careful dissection to avoid vascular injury. Conversely, in right-sided fistulas, exposure is generally more straightforward due to the wider retropericardial space; however, the proximity of the SVC and azygos vein creates a different set of technical risks. These differences imply that surgical planning must be individualized: left-sided closures demand meticulous preoperative imaging and delicate handling of mediastinal structures, whereas right-sided approaches emphasize protection of venous return and avoidance of airway distortion.

In this case, during the first approach, only a stapled closure was chosen, without muscle flap. Primary suturing and reinforcement with thymic tissue were chosen for the reapproach. An attempt was made to reduce the bronchial stump, but the anatomy was unfavorable—adhesions and inflammation. Then, the patient presented clinical and laboratory worsening with hemodynamic instability (need for low doses of vasoactive drug), increased inflammatory tests—such as leukocytes and C-reactive protein—in addition to a purulent appearance in the pleural drain. For the third approach, due to the hostile and infected pleural space, making another thoracotomy unsustainable, the transsternal approach was chosen.

Despite its efficacy, the technique remains underutilized, partly due to the anatomical complexity and rarity of indication. In this context, video documentation of the technical steps, as presented here, serves as an essential educational tool for training thoracic surgeons and preserving classical surgical knowledge.

Conclusions

This study therefore reinforces the importance of the Abruzzini technique as part of the therapeutic arsenal for complex BPFs and highlights the role of visual education in modern surgical training.
