On the continuity of phase transition of three-dimensional square-lattice XY models
Zhou Gang

Abstract




Department of Mathematics and Statistics, Binghamton University, Binghamton, NY, 13850

Abstract

We study the continuity of magnetization at the phase transition of the ferromagnetic XY model in the three-dimensional square lattice with the nearest neighborhood interaction. We assume that, at the critical temperature, with probability 1, for every edge in the infinite directed graph generated by the random path representation, finitely many edges exist so that they form a finite loop.
Then, we prove that the phase transition is continuous at the critical temperature. The main technical contribution is to find a switching lemma to establish a bijection between equally weighted graphs.

Introduction

In the present paper, we are interested in the phase transition at the critical inverse temperature of the XY model on the three-dimensional square lattice.

To formulate the problem we start by considering a finite subset of a three-dimensional square lattice

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=[-L,L]^{3}\bigcap\mathbb{Z}^{3}.

(1.1)

On this lattice the Hamiltonian is defined as

\displaystyle H_{L,\nu}:=-\sum_{k,l\in\mathcal{L}}J_{k,l}S_{k}\cdot S_{l}

(1.2)

and
\nu=+
or
0.

Here, depending on the location of the site
k
,
S_{k}\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
satisfies different conditions: the lattice is decomposed into two parts,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}^{o}\cup\partial\mathcal{L},

(1.3)

with
\mathcal{L}^{o}
being the interior, and
\partial\mathcal{L}=\{(z_{1},z_{2},z_{3})\ |\ \max\{|z_{1}|,\ |z_{2}|,\ |z_{3}|\}=L\}
the boundary;
for
k\in\mathcal{L}^{o}
, the only requirement for
S_{k}
is that
|S_{k}|=1;
when
k\in\partial\mathcal{L}
i.e. it is on the boundary, we consider two boundary conditions: when
\nu=0
, we use the free boundary condition, specifically

\displaystyle S_{k}=0,\ \text{if}\ k\in\partial\mathcal{L};

(1.4)

when
\nu=+
, the plus boundary condition,

\displaystyle S_{k}=(1,0)^{T},\ \text{if}\ k\in\partial\mathcal{L}.

(1.5)

Here we only consider the nearest neighborhood interaction, specifically,

\displaystyle J_{k,l}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\frac{1}{6}&\text{if}\ |k-l|=1;\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

Thus for any fixed
k
,

\sum_{l}J_{k,l}=1.
Also, we remark that all the analyses in the present papers work for finite-ranged, symmetric ferromagnetic potentials.

Next, we define a probabilistic measure.

For any given local function
F
of the spins, we define

\displaystyle\langle F\rangle_{\beta,\nu,L}:=\int\prod_{k\in\mathcal{L}^{o}}dA_{k}\ e^{-\beta H_{L,\nu}(S)}F(S)

(1.8)

where
A_{k}
is the area element for
S_{k}
;
\nu=+\ \text{or}\ 0;
and
H_{L,\nu}
denotes the Hamiltonian in (
1.2
).
By choosing
F=1,
we define

\displaystyle Z_{\beta,\nu,L}:=\langle 1\rangle_{\beta,\nu,L}.

(1.9)

We are interested in the following quantities:

\displaystyle\big\langle F\big\rangle_{\beta,\nu}:=\lim_{L\rightarrow+\infty}\frac{\big\langle F\big\rangle_{\beta,\nu,L}}{Z_{\beta,\nu,L}}.

(1.10)

And we are especially interested in the case
F=\cos(\theta_{0})
. The so-called magnetization
m^{*}:[0,\infty)\rightarrow[0,\infty)
is a function defined as

\displaystyle m^{*}(\beta):=\Big\langle\cos(\theta_{0})\Big\rangle_{\beta,+}.

(1.11)

It is well known that there exists a critical inverse temperature
\beta_{c}>0
such that
when
\beta<\beta_{c}
,
m^{*}(\beta)\equiv 0;

and when
\beta>\beta_{c}
,
m^{*}(\beta)>0.

A natural question is:

\displaystyle\text{at the critical inverse temperature}\ \beta=\beta_{c},\ \ m^{*}(\beta_{c})=0?

(1.12)

A positive answer implies the continuity of
m^{*}(\beta)
at
\beta=\beta_{c}
because (1)
\lim_{\beta\searrow\beta_{c}}m^{*}(\beta)=m^{*}(\beta_{c})
; and (2)
m^{*}(\beta)=0
if
\beta<\beta_{c}
.

We will consider the problem (
1.12
) in the present paper.
Here we restrict our consideration to the 3-dimensional square lattice and nearest neighbor interaction. We need to assume that Assumption
2.2
holds, which states that, at the critical inverse temperature, in the directed graphs generated by the random path representation, with probability 1, for every fixed edge, one can find finitely many edges so that together they form a finite loop.
Under these conditions, we will prove that

\displaystyle m^{*}(\beta_{c})=0.

(1.13)

Now we discuss some technicality. Perhaps the Ising model is the most well-studied classical spin model. A key tool is the so-called switching lemma, which Griffiths, Hurst, and Sherman invented

Given the significance of the switching lemma, it is important to find a similar technique for the other spin models, such as the XY model, which is to be considered in this paper. The present paper is centered around building a “switching lemma” for directed graphs generated by the random path representation of the XY model.

Before discussing the difficulties, we briefly review some key steps in applying the switching lemma for undirected graphs.

We consider two sets of pairs of (undirected) graphs on a lattice
\Big(\mathbb{Z}\cap[-N,N]\big)^{3}
for some large integer

, and a possible so-called ghost
\delta-
site, see e.g. the graphs generated by the random current representation of Ising model in
[

\Lambda_{x,y}

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Lambda_{x,y}&:=\Big\{\big(A_{\delta},\ B\big)\ \Big|\ A_{\delta}\ \text{and}\ B\ \text{satisfy the conditions in (1), see below}\Big\};\\
\Gamma_{x,y}&:=\Big\{\big(C_{\delta},\ D\big)\ \Big|\ C_{\delta}\ \text{and}\ D\ \text{satisfy the conditions in (2), see below}\Big\},\end{split}

(1.14)

where
x
and
y
are two sites on the lattice, and the two conditions are

(1)

A_{\delta}
and
B
only have finitely many edges;
A_{\delta}=A_{\delta}(x,y)
has sinks at

and

, and

has no sink,
A_{\delta}
might depend on the
\delta
-site. And their union

contains a

\delta
-site. —– In the rest of the paper we say this path is
\delta

(2)

C_{\delta}

has sinks at
x
and
y

might depend on the
\delta

contains a

The result is the following:

Lemma 1.1
.

Under the conditions above, there exists a bijection between
\Lambda_{x,y}
and
\Gamma_{x,y}
.

The proof is based on the so-called switching lemma, and we will sketch it.

Based on the information above, there exist countably many
x\longleftrightarrow y
-paths, denoted by
P_{1}
,
P_{2}
,
\cdots
, such that (1) they avoid the
\delta-
site, and (2)

and
\Gamma_{x,y}
can be partitioned into the following disjoint subsets:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Lambda_{x,y}=&\cup_{k=1}^{\infty}\Lambda_{x,y;P_{k}},\\
\Gamma_{x,y}=&\cup_{k=1}^{\infty}\Gamma_{x,y;P_{k}}\end{split}

(1.15)

where
\Lambda_{x,y;P_{k}}
and
\Gamma_{x,y;P_{k}}
are sets of graphs defined as

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Lambda_{x,y;P_{k}}:=&\Big\{\big(A_{\delta},\ B\big)\in\Lambda_{x,y}\ \Big|\ P_{k}\subset A_{\delta}\cup B;\ \text{and for any}\ l<k,\ P_{l}\not\subset A_{\delta}\cup B\Big\};\\
\Gamma_{x,y;P_{k}}:=&\Big\{\big(C_{\delta},\ D\big)\in\Gamma_{x,y}\ \Big|\ P_{k}\subset C_{\delta}\cup D;\ \text{and for any}\ l<k,\ P_{l}\not\subset C_{\delta}\cup D\Big\}.\end{split}

(1.16)

We can define a bijection

F_{P_{k}}:\ \Lambda_{x,y;P_{k}}\rightarrow\Gamma_{x,y;P_{k}},

as follows:

\displaystyle F_{P_{k}}(A_{\delta},B)=(C_{\delta},D)

(1.17)

where
C_{\delta}
and
D
are defined as

\displaystyle\begin{split}C_{\delta}:=&(A_{\delta}\backslash P_{k})\cup(B\cap P_{k}),\\
D:=&(B\backslash P_{k})\cup(A_{\delta}\cap P_{k}).\end{split}

It is easy to see that the map is a bijection. A very useful property is that there is a “conservation law”:

\displaystyle A_{\delta}\cup B=C_{\delta}\cup D,

(1.18)

which, together with (
1.16
), guarantees that

\displaystyle F_{P_{k}}(\Lambda_{x,y;P_{k}})\cap F_{P_{l}}(\Lambda_{x,y;P_{l}})=\emptyset,\text{if}\ k\not=l.

(1.19)

(
1.15
)-(
1.19
) establish a bijection between

and
\Gamma_{x,y}
. Therefore, Lemma

Next, we consider directed graphs generated by the random path representation of the XY model.

In brief, for directed graphs, a simple generalization of the technique above does not work, especially because we do not have a “conservation law” like (

Specifically, if

\displaystyle F_{p}\Big((A_{\delta},B)\Big)=\Big(C_{\delta},D\Big)

where

\displaystyle C_{\delta}:=

\displaystyle(A_{\delta}\backslash P)\cup(\overline{B\cap P}),

\displaystyle D:=

\displaystyle(B\backslash P)\cup(\overline{A_{\delta}\cap P}).

Here

is the reverse of the directed graph

Consequently, instead of the conservation law (
1.18

\displaystyle A_{\delta}\cup B\not=C_{\delta}\cup D.

(1.21)

Consequently, a simple generalization of switching lemma will not work for the XY model.

(1)

(3)

After refining the pairing, we prove that the switching is between graphs of equal weights. For this we use the notion of “surgical switching”, for the detail, we refer to Proposition

3.1
Free boundary condition

We rewrite Hamiltonian as

\displaystyle H_{L,0}=-\sum_{k,l\in\mathcal{L}}J_{k,l}S_{k}\cdot S_{l}=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k,l\in\mathcal{L}}J_{k,l}\Big(e^{i(\theta_{k}-\theta_{l})}+e^{-i(\theta_{k}-\theta_{l})}\Big),

(3.1)

where, in the second step we used
S_{k}=(\cos\theta_{k},\ \sin\theta_{k})
and

\displaystyle\begin{split}S_{k}\cdot S_{l}=&\cos\theta_{k}\cos\theta_{l}+\sin\theta_{k}\sin\theta_{l}\\
=&\cos(\theta_{k}-\theta_{l})\\
=&\frac{1}{2}\big(e^{i(\theta_{k}-\theta_{l})}+e^{-i(\theta_{k}-\theta_{l})}\big).\end{split}

(3.2)

Decompose the Hamiltonian
H
into two parts

\displaystyle H_{L,0}=H_{+}+H_{-},

(3.3)

where
H_{+}
and
H_{-}
are defined as

\displaystyle\begin{split}H_{+}:=&-\sum_{\sigma(k)>\sigma(l)}J_{k,l}e^{i(\theta_{k}-\theta_{l})},\\
H_{-}:=&-\sum_{\sigma(k)>\sigma(l)}J_{k,l}e^{-i(\theta_{k}-\theta_{l})},\end{split}

(3.4)

here
\sigma:\mathcal{L}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}
is an (arbitrary) injective map.
Recall that
J_{k,k}=0
and
J_{k,l}=J_{l,k}.

Taylor-expand the exponentials
e^{-\beta H_{+}}
and
e^{-\beta H_{-}}
to obtain

\displaystyle\begin{split}e^{-\beta H_{+}}=&\sum_{n_{k\rightarrow l}}\prod_{\sigma(k)>\sigma(l)}(\beta J_{k,l})^{n_{k\rightarrow l}}\frac{1}{n_{k\rightarrow l}!}e^{in_{k\rightarrow l}(\theta_{k}-\theta_{l})};\\
e^{-\beta H_{-}}=&\sum_{n_{k\leftarrow l}}\prod_{\sigma(k)>\sigma(l)}(\beta J_{k,l})^{n_{k\leftarrow l}}\frac{1}{n_{k\leftarrow l}!}e^{-in_{k\leftarrow l}(\theta_{k}-\theta_{l})}.\end{split}

(3.5)

Consequently,

\displaystyle\begin{split}e^{-\beta H_{L,0}}=&e^{-\beta H_{+}}e^{-\beta H_{-}}\\
=&\sum_{n_{k\rightarrow l},\ n_{k\leftarrow l}}\prod_{\sigma(k)>\sigma(l)}(\beta J_{k,l})^{n_{k\rightarrow l}+n_{k\leftarrow l}}\frac{1}{n_{k\rightarrow l}!}\frac{1}{n_{k\leftarrow l}!}e^{i(n_{k\rightarrow l}-n_{k\leftarrow l})(\theta_{k}-\theta_{l})}.\end{split}

(3.6)

We call the nonnegative integer
n_{k\rightarrow l}
the number of edges going from
k
to
l
; and
n_{k\leftarrow l}
the number of edges going from

to

Take the integral

for any

and find that,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2\pi}e^{-\beta H_{L,0}}d^{3}\theta_{k}\not=0

only when the numbers of incoming edges and outgoing ones are equal, equivalently:

\displaystyle\sum_{j:\ \sigma(k)>\sigma(j)}n_{k\rightarrow j}+\sum_{l:\ \sigma(k)>\sigma(l)}n_{l\leftarrow k}=\sum_{l:\ \sigma(l)>\sigma(k)}n_{l\leftarrow k}+\sum_{j:\ \sigma(j)>\sigma(k)}n_{j\rightarrow k}.

(3.8)

Therefore, the quantity

, defined in (
1.9

\displaystyle\begin{split}Z_{\beta,0,L}=&\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{\#\Lambda}}\int e^{-\beta H_{L,0}}\prod_{k\in L^{0}}d^{3}\theta_{k}\\
=&\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle\sum_{n_{k\rightarrow l},\ n_{k\leftarrow l}}\prod_{k,l}\Big[(\beta J_{k,l})^{n_{k\rightarrow l}+n_{k\leftarrow l}}\frac{1}{n_{k\rightarrow l}!}\frac{1}{n_{k\leftarrow l}!}\Big]\ \text{if}\ \eqref{eq:sumR}\ \text{holds for all}\ k,\\
\\
0\ \text{otherwise}.\end{array}\right.\end{split}

(3.9)

To simplify the notations, we use the following expression: provided that (

\displaystyle\begin{split}Z_{\beta,0,L}=&\sum_{\partial({\bf{n}_{0,\rightarrow}+\bf{n}_{0,\leftarrow}})=\varnothing}\big(\beta\bf{J}\big)^{{\bf{n}}_{0,\rightarrow}+{\bf{n}}_{0,\leftarrow}}\frac{1}{{\bf{n}_{0,\rightarrow}}!}\frac{1}{{\bf{n}_{0,\leftarrow}}!}\\

(3.10)

where

,

and

and

The result is the following:

Next, we increase

The detailed proof is straightforward but tedious. We choose to skip it.

Similarly, almost all terms
D_{\widehat{G_{0,N}}(y\rightarrow x)}
will be canceled.

Therefore, we proved that

\displaystyle\lim_{L\rightarrow\infty}\Big(\frac{\tilde{F}_{\delta,L}(x\rightarrow y)\ Z_{0,L}}{\tilde{Z}_{\delta,L}Z_{0,L}}-\frac{F_{0,L}(y\rightarrow x)\ \tilde{Z}_{\delta,L}}{\tilde{Z}_{\delta,L}Z_{0,L}}\Big)=0.

(6.38)

This and the definition of
D_{1,x,y}(L)
in (
4.2
) imply the desired
\displaystyle\lim_{L\rightarrow\infty}D_{1,x,y}(L)=0
.

Similarly, we prove that
\displaystyle\lim_{L\rightarrow\infty}D_{2,x,y}(L)=0
, and hence finish proving Proposition
4.1
.

6.4.2
Switching between graphs of equal weights: Proof of Proposition

For technical reasons, it is easier to prove a slightly more general result, which will be Lemma
6.14
below.

We start by defining a notion of “surgical switching by a graph
\mathcal{P}
” for some unpaired graph
\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{P}(x\rightarrow y)
.
Here we emphasize that
\mathcal{P}
is unpaired, and thus, in switching we do not follow the rule of pairing, and hence, we destroy and re-establish some of them.

Recall that
\Pi
, defined in (
6.2
), is the projective map so that
\Pi(G_{\#}(x\rightarrow y))
is the unpaired graph underlying the paired one
G_{\#}(x\rightarrow y)
.

Definition 6.12
.

Suppose that
G_{\delta,\mathcal{P}}=G_{\delta,\mathcal{P}}(x\rightarrow y)
is a paired infinite graph. And
\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{P}(x\rightarrow y)
is an unpaired subgraph in
\Pi(G_{\delta,\mathcal{P}})
.

We perform a surgical switching, using
\mathcal{P}
, on
G_{\delta,\mathcal{P}}
to obtain
G_{0,\overline{\mathcal{P}}}=G_{0,\overline{\mathcal{P}}}(y\rightarrow x)
as follows.

For
G_{0,\overline{\mathcal{P}}}
, its corresponding unpaired graph
\Pi(G_{0,\overline{\mathcal{P}}})
, is obtained from
\Pi(G_{\delta})
by switching
\mathcal{P}
with the procedure described in (
6.7

Next, we consider the pairing in the new graph
G_{0,\overline{\mathcal{P}}}
.

In brief, some pairings remain unchanged, and some pairings must be modified depending on whether they are paired with edges in
\mathcal{P}
.

(
I
)

We start with an easy case. Specifically, suppose that
\mathcal{P}
is a
x\rightarrow y
path that does not contain any loop and is of the form

\displaystyle\begin{split}x&=a_{0}\rightarrow a_{1}\rightarrow a_{2}\rightarrow\cdots\rightarrow a_{n}=y,\\
&\text{and}\ a_{k}\not=a_{l}\ \text{if}\ k\not=l.\end{split}

(6.39)

Fix a site
v\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}
, the pairing of an edge
e_{v\rightarrow w}
(or
e_{w\rightarrow v}
) at the site
v
will stay the same in the following two possibilities:

–

the edge is not paired with any edge of
\mathcal{P}
,

–

the edge
e_{v\rightarrow w}
(or
e_{w\rightarrow v}
) is part of
\mathcal{P}
and is already paired to another edge in
\mathcal{P}
at

Otherwise,
v=a_{k}
for some

there are two possible pairings of
e_{a_{k}\rightarrow w}
(or

e_{a_{k}\rightarrow w}
at the site
a_{k}
. That for
e_{w\rightarrow a_{k}}
is almost identical, and hence is omitted.

–

The edge

is part of
\mathcal{P}

w=a_{k+1}

a_{k}

e_{b_{k-1}\rightarrow a_{k}}\not\in\mathcal{P}

Then in the new graph

is not part of
\mathcal{P}
, and at
a_{k}
it is paired with
e_{a_{k-1}\rightarrow a_{k}}\in\mathcal{P},
and the edge
e_{a_{k}\rightarrow a_{k+1}}\in\mathcal{P}

e_{c\rightarrow a_{k}}
.

Then in the new graph
G_{0,\overline{\mathcal{P}}}
where we reverse the path
\mathcal{P}
, at
a_{k},

The next result is obvious.

Lemma 6.13
.

For any fixed finite graph
\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{P}(x\rightarrow y)
, the mapping
\xrightarrow[\text{surgical}]{\mathcal{P}}

for some graph

(6.42)

Similar to (

), it is implied by (

The next property implies the desired Proposition

.

Lemma 6.14
.

If
G_{\delta,N}(x\rightarrow y)
and
F_{0,N}(y\rightarrow x)
are two partially paired graphs in the region
|z|\leq N
, and
\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{P}(x\rightarrow y)\subset\Pi(G_{\delta,N}(x\rightarrow y))
is a unpaired graph contained in the region
|z|<N
, such that

G_{\delta,N}(x\rightarrow y)\xrightarrow[\text{surgical}]{\mathcal{P}}F_{0,N}(y\rightarrow x),

then

\displaystyle D_{\widehat{G_{\delta,N}}(x\rightarrow y)}=D_{\widehat{F_{0,N}}(y\rightarrow x)}.

(6.43)

Proof.

To simplify notations we use the same conventions as in (
6.3
).

We start from (
6.42
) and (
6.30
), which imply that

\displaystyle\sum_{\Pi(V_{\delta,N})=\Pi(G_{\delta,N})}D_{\widehat{V_{\delta,N}}}=\sum_{\Pi(K_{0,N})=\Pi(F_{0,N})}D_{\widehat{K_{0,N}}}.

(6.44)

Our strategy is to show that, (1) on both sides of (
6.44
), the summations are taken over terms of equal weights; (2) the number of elements in the two summations are equal. Thus, every term involved is of equal weight as desired.

For the left hand side, the rule set in (
6.31
) implies that
D_{\widehat{V_{\delta,N}}}=D_{\widehat{G_{\delta,N}}}
in (
6.31
). Thus,

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\sum_{\Pi(V_{\delta,N})=\Pi(G_{\delta,N})}D_{\widehat{V_{\delta,N}}}=\Upsilon_{G_{\delta,N}}\ D_{\widehat{G_{\delta,N}}},\end{split}

(6.45)

where
\Upsilon_{G_{\delta,N}}
is used to count the number of elements in the summation:

\displaystyle\Upsilon_{G_{\delta,N}}:=

\displaystyle\Big|\Big\{\widehat{V_{\delta,N}}\ \Big|\ \Pi(V_{\delta,N})=\Pi(G_{\delta,N})\Big\}\Big|.

Similarly, the right-hand side takes a new form

\displaystyle\begin{split}\sum_{\Pi(K_{0,N})=\Pi(F_{0,N})}D_{\widehat{K_{0,N}}}=\Upsilon_{F_{0,N}}D_{\widehat{F_{0,N}}}\end{split}

(6.46)

with

\displaystyle\Upsilon_{F_{0,N}}:=

\displaystyle\Big|\Big\{\widehat{K_{0,N}}\ \Big|\ \Pi(K_{0,N})=\Pi(F_{0,N})\Big\}\Big|.

The three identities (
6.44
)-(
6.46
) imply that

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Upsilon_{G_{\delta,N}}D_{\widehat{G_{\delta,N}}}=\Upsilon_{F_{0,N}}D_{\widehat{F_{0,N}}}.\end{split}

(6.47)

Now we claim that

\displaystyle\Upsilon_{G_{\delta,N}}=\Upsilon_{F_{0,N}}.

(6.48)

If this holds, then it implies the desired identity

D_{\widehat{G_{\delta,N}}}=D_{\widehat{F_{0,N}}}.

To prove (
6.48

\mathcal{P}
provided in the lemma, the surgical transformation
\xrightarrow[\text{surgical}]{\mathcal{P}}
, defined in (
6.41

\Big\{\widehat{V_{\delta,N}}\ \Big|\ \Pi(V_{\delta,N})=\Pi(G_{\delta,N})\Big\}
and
\Big\{\widehat{K_{0,N}}\ \Big|\ \Pi(K_{0,N})=\Pi(F_{0,N})\Big\}
. Therefore, these two finite sets have the same number of elements, and hence (
6.48
) holds.

The proof is complete.
