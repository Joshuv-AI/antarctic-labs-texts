First record of Prosogonotrema bilabiatum Vigueras, 1940 (digenea: Sclerodistomidae) in marine fishes from the Persian Gulf, Hormozgan Province, Southern Iran
Authors: Aida Vafae Eslahi, Majid Pirestani, Zahra Gharibi, Ioannis Adamopoulos, Amin Karampour, Faezeh Mohammadi, Fatemeh Samiee-Rad, Khadijeh Ahmadi, Milad Badri, Luís Madeira de Carvalho
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study documents the first occurrence of the digenean trematode Prosogonotrema bilabiatum in marine fish from the Persian Gulf, Iran.MethodsOne hundred eighty-two specimens of four ecologically and economically important marine fish species (Alectis indica, Rachycentron canadum, Seriola dumerili, and Solea elongata) were examined between January 2023 and December 2024 in Hormozgan Province using light microscopy and molecular techniques.ResultsAmong the sampled fish, the overall prevalence of P. bilabiatum was found to be 31.31% (57/182). Molecular analyses targeting the nuclear 28 S rRNA and mitochondrial cytochrome c oxidase subunit I (COI) genes corroborated the morphological identification of P. bilabiatum. Phylogenetic reconstruction based on 28 S sequences clustered the Iranian isolates with Brazilian P. bilabiatum with strong nodal support, confirming their taxonomic placement within Sclerodistomidae. In contrast, COI analyses placed the Iranian isolates in a distinct monophyletic clade, but no reference COI sequences of Prosogonotrema genus were available in GenBank for direct comparison. Accordingly, the present study provides the first COI sequences for the genus, establishing a novel molecular reference for future phylogenetic studies.ConclusionOur finding underscores the importance of integrative taxonomy in documenting parasite diversity in understudied marine ecosystems such as the Persian Gulf, where climate-related environmental changes may alter parasite distributions. Continued parasitological monitoring, complemented by molecular and phylogenetic approaches, will be essential for clarifying species boundaries within genus Prosogonotrema and for assessing the ecological and fisheries implications of trematode infections in the region.Keywords: Fish parasites, Digenea, Integrative taxonomy, COI barcode, 28S rRNA, Phylogeny, Marine biodiversity, Host range

Background

Prosogonotrema Vigueras, 1940 (family Sclerodistomidae) is a digenean trematode and the sole genus within the subfamily Prosogonotrematinae with currently 16 valid species [1]. It is distinguished by a ventral sucker positioned at or just behind the body’s midpoint, reproductive organs located in the forebody (with the ovary sometimes situated dorsally to the ventral sucker), and a single, median accessory excretory vesicle. They are predominantly found in the stomachs of marine teleost fishes [2].

Prosogonotrema bilabiatum, the type species of the genus, was first described from specimens collected in the Yellowtail snapper (Ocyurus chrysurus) from the northern coast of Cuba. It is chiefly characterized by the positioning of its reproductive organs in the preacetabular region and the presence of two distinct semicircular lips situated in the preoral area [3, 4]. Belonging to the family Sclerodistomidae, this digenean trematode has been recorded from various marine hosts, but is most frequently documented in the Atlantic spadefish (Chaetodipterus faber) along the Brazilian coast [1, 5].

The Persian Gulf is a semi-enclosed epicontinental sea situated in the northwestern extension of the Indian Ocean, renowned as one of the warmest marine environments globally [6], characterized by a mastoidal regime, high temperatures, salinity, and evaporation [7, 8]. It supports diverse habitats such as coral reefs, mangroves, seagrass beds, seaweeds, and muddy to rocky shores. Projections indicate its mean annual sea surface temperature may rise by about 2.2 °C by 2,100 exceeding the global average rate [9]. With severe environmental conditions, the Gulf harbours a unique species mix, defined by limited diversity but notable endemism when compared to the broader Indian Ocean [10]. Unique geological formations and oceanographic conditions in the Gulf foster high marine biodiversity and productivity [11].

As a hotspot for marine parasite biodiversity [12], the Persian Gulf sustains a diverse range of fish species. The fish diversity of the Persian Gulf consists of 744 verified species across 27 orders, 131 families and 445 genera [13]. Elongate sole (Solea elongata Day, 1877), Cobia (Rachycentron canadum Linnaeus, 1766), Greater amberjack (Seriola dumerili Risso, 1810), and Indian threadfish (Alectis indica Rüppell, 1830) are ecologically and economically significant fish species in Persian Gulf [13, 14].

The demersal fish S. elongata occupies sandy and muddy coastal bottoms at depths of 8–28 m across the western Indian Ocean, ranging from the Persian Gulf to India and Sri Lanka, and feeds chiefly on benthic invertebrates, particularly small crustaceans [14]. The diet of S. elongata is dominated by small benthic invertebrates, particularly amphipods, various crustaceans, bivalves, and copepods [15].

Known as cobia, R. canadum is the sole surviving member of the family Rachycentridae, exhibiting a broad, cosmopolitan distribution in tropical and subtropical oceans [16]. It is a predatory benthopelagic fish capable of feeding near the seabed, within mid-water zones, and close to the surface. Its biological characteristics such as rapid growth, adaptability to culture conditions, and desirable flesh qualitycombined with strong demand in Asian markets, position it as an excellent candidate for intensive aquaculture. The species is widely distributed in tropical and subtropical waters around the world, except for the eastern Pacific [17].

The greater amberjack (S. dumerili) is a sizable pelagic predator from the family Carangidae, found widely in temperate to tropical marine environments, and is an important species for both commercial fisheries and sport fishing [18]. Adults are known to feed opportunistically on both pelagic and benthic fishes, as well as cephalopods and crustaceans [19].

The Indian threadfish (A. indica), also known as diamond trevally, ranges throughout the tropical Indo–West Pacific, where adults occupy reef environments down to 100 m, and juveniles inhabit estuaries and seagrass beds; this species forms coastal shoals and has limited commercial significance [20]. It is a carnivorous marine fish with a diverse diet that reflects its role as a mid- to upper-level predator in coastal and offshore ecosystems. Stomach-content analyses from previous studies indicate that its primary prey consists of cephalopods, particularly squid, while small fishes and crustaceans also contribute substantially to its diet. The species’ feeding behavior is opportunistic, allowing it to forage both in pelagic mid-waters and near the seafloor. This trophic flexibility positions A. indica as an important predator in regulating prey populations and maintaining ecosystem balance [21].

Including both plankton-feeding pelagic species and larger predatory jacks in the host set allows the study to span a broad range of trophic levels. This approach enhances the ecological relevance of parasite monitoring, as parasite transmission and prevalence may differ depending on host ecology, diet, and habitat. Moreover, including species with economic importance in local and global fisheries underlines the potential implications of parasitism not only for the health and ecology of wild populations but also for fisheries sustainability and aquaculture practices.

Despite the ecological and economic importance of the examined fish hosts, information on their intestinal helminths in the Persian Gulf remains extremely limited, and no confirmed records of P. bilabiatum have previously been reported from Iranian waters. This lack of baseline data restricts our understanding of parasite biodiversity, host–parasite associations, and potential implications for local fisheries. Therefore, the present study aims to characterize P. bilabiatum collected from four marine fish species in the Persian Gulf (Hormozgan Province, southern Iran) using an integrative approach that combines detailed morphological observations with molecular analyses. By providing the first verified record of P. bilabiatum in this region, documenting new host and geographic range extensions, and generating the first mitochondrial cytochrome c oxidase subunit I (COI) sequence data for the genus Prosogonotrema, this research addresses critical gaps in the taxonomy and distribution of these poorly studied digeneans. The study contributes essential reference data for future ecological and phylogenetic investigations and highlights the importance of integrative taxonomy in improving parasite documentation in understudied marine ecosystems.

Materials and methods

Study area, hosts and sample collection

A cross sectional study was carried out during January 2023 to December 2024 in coastal zone of Hormozgan Province, Iran. Hormozgan Province, situated in southern and southeastern Iran, borders the northern coastline of the Persian Gulf and includes numerous islands and coastal areas [22]. The Persian Gulf, situated in a subtropical and extremely arid zone at the northwestern edge of the Indian Ocean, is a semi-enclosed marine basin linked to the Gulf of Oman and the Arabian Sea through the Strait of Hormuz [23]. The passage serves as an important route for exchanging water, nutrients, and marine species between the regions [10]. It spans between latitudes 23° and 30° and longitudes 45° to 56°, with an average depth of about 35 m. Its seabed deepens toward the east, reaching over 110 m at the Strait of Hormuz [23].

Sampling was conducted at four major coastal sites of Hormozgan province, including Abu Musa Island, Qeshm Island, Sirik, and Bandar-e-Jask (Fig. 1; Table 1). Features such as water depth and seasonal variations in maximum and minimum temperature and humidity were considered (Table 1).

A total of 182 fish specimens belonging to four different species (Solea elongate (n = 56), Rachycentron canadum (n = 45), Seriola dumerili (n = 42), and Alectis indica (n = 39) (Fig. 2A-D).

The biometric characteristics of the sampled hosts were as follows: Alectis indica had a mean total length of 107.5 ± 4.33 cm and a mean body weight of 19.5 ± 0.87 kg. Rachycentron canadum measured 114.0 ± 2.31 cm in length with a mean weight of 44.5 ± 0.87 kg. Seriola dumerili exhibited a mean total length of 106.5 ± 3.75 cm and a mean body weight of 54.5 ± 0.29 kg. Finally, S. elongata had a mean length of 17.5 ± 2.02 cm and a mean weight of 1.5 ± 0.29 kg. These biometric data provide essential context for interpreting parasite distribution across host species.

The present study did not involve any experimental manipulation or handling of live animals. All fish samples were obtained post-mortem from local fishermen through the fisheries section of Hormozgan Province as part of routine fishery activities.

Parasitological examination

All fish specimens were subjected to a standardized parasitological inspection following established helminthological protocols [24, 25]. Each fish was examined externally and internally. The skin, fins, gills, and oral cavity were screened under a stereomicroscope, and the gastrointestinal tract and associated organs were removed and examined in Petri dishes containing physiological saline. Parasites were isolated under a stereomicroscope, washed in distilled water, and transferred for further processing.

Preparation of trematode specimens

Recovered trematodes were relaxed in warm saline (≈ 40 °C) until body contraction ceased, then gently flattened between two slides to preserve morphological integrity. Specimens intended for morphological examination were immediately fixed either in 10% neutral-buffered formalin or in 70% ethanol, following standard procedures. All fixed samples used for staining were processed under the same protocol described below. A separate portion of each specimen was preserved at − 20 °C exclusively for molecular analysis and not used for morphological evaluation.

Staining and mounting protocol

The staining procedure followed the standard carmine-staining method commonly applied in trematode taxonomy [26, 27]. Fixed specimens were rinsed twice in tap water and then stained for 2–6 h using: herbal extract solutions at 5%, 10%, and 20% (w/v), or standard carmine dye (control). After staining, specimens were rinsed with tap water and differentiated in acid alcohol (1–2% HCl in 70% ethanol) for 20 s to 2 min until the desired contrast was achieved. Samples were then dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (50%, 70%, 85%, 96%, and 100%; 15 min each), cleared in alcohol–xylene (30 min), followed by pure xylene (30 min), and permanently mounted in Canada balsam.

Morphological assessment

Only fully prepared, stained, and mounted specimens were used for detailed morphological observation. No morphological evaluation was performed on partial tissue portions. Morphological terminology and diagnostic characters were defined following authoritative taxonomic references [2]. Measurements were obtained using an ocular micrometer from multiple adult specimens, and all key diagnostic structures (oral sucker, pharynx, testis, ovary, vitelline tubule, ventral sucker, and cecum) were documented based on a previous publication [28]. Representative structures were annotated in Fig. 2G.

Parasitological indices

Prevalence, mean intensity, and mean abundance were calculated according to the definitions proposed by Bush et al. (1997) [29] to provide standardized parasitological metrics for comparison across studies.

Seasonal-based analysis

The dataset was grouped into four seasonal categories (spring, summer, autumn, winter) to examine the association between parasite infection and season, A chi-square (χ²) test was used to evaluate whether infection prevalence differed significantly between seasons. In addition, a qualitative correlation analysis was performed to assess whether seasonal environmental variables (temperature and humidity) were associated with infection occurrence. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Molecular and phylogenetic analysis

Genomic DNA was extracted from individual ethanol-preserved specimens using a FAVORGEN DNA Tissue Kit (Favorgen Biotech Corp., Taiwan), following the manufacturer’s protocol. Two gene regions were amplified: a partial fragment of the large subunit ribosomal RNA gene (28 S rRNA) and the mitochondrial cytochrome c oxidase subunit I (COI) gene.

For amplification of the 28 S rRNA gene, primers BD1 (5′-GTC GAA TTC GTA GGT GAA CCT GCG GAA GGA TCA-3′) and BD2 (5′-GTC GAA TTC CTC CGC TTA TTG ATA TGC TTA AGT TC-3′) were used. For the COI gene, primers JB3 (5′-TTT TTT GGG CAT CCT GAG GTT TAT-3′) and JB4.5 (5′-TAA AGA AAG AAC ATA ATG AAA ATG-3′) were employed. These primers are widely used for Digenea phylogenetics and species-level discrimination [30, 31].

The expected product sizes were approximately 1300 bp for 28 S and 400 bp for COI. PCR amplifications were carried out in a 25 µl reaction mixture containing 1× PCR buffer, 1.5 mM MgCl₂, 0.2 mM of each dNTP, 0.1 µM of each primer, 1 U of Taq DNA polymerase, and approximately 50 ng of template DNA. The thermal cycling profile consisted of an initial denaturation at 94 °C for 3–5 min, followed by 35 cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 30 s, annealing for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 30 s, with a final elongation step at 72 °C for 7–10 min. The annealing temperature was set at 55 °C for the 28 S rRNA gene and 52 °C for the COI gene.

PCR products were checked by agarose gel electrophoresis, purified, and sequenced bidirectionally using an ABI automated sequencer. Raw chromatograms were edited in Chromas, and consensus sequences were generated in BioEdit. Sequences were aligned with related trematode sequences retrieved from GenBank using MUSCLE. Pairwise sequence similarities were calculated using MEGA X.

Phylogenetic analyses were performed using the Maximum Likelihood method (ML) and General Time Reversible model (GTR), applying the best-fit nucleotide substitution models determined by ModelTest. Nodal support was assessed with 1000 bootstrap replicates for ML and posterior probability values for BI.

Results

The overall prevalence of all parasites was 31.31% (57/182) in the examined fish. Infections were observed in 51.28% of A. indica (n = 20/39), 33.33% of R. canadum (n = 15/45), 28.57% of S. dumerili (n = 12/42), and 17.85% of S. elongata (n = 10/56).

During necropsy, we observed clusters of mature P. bilabiatum attached to the intestinal wall, together with occasional juvenile forms representing earlier developmental stages (Fig. 2E & F).

Parasites specimens matched P. bilabiatum descriptions by Vigueras (1940) and recent redescriptions [1]. The body is elongated, slightly flattened, fuse or cylindrical shaped. The body size was between 1.2 and 2.8 mm. There were two semicircular preoral lips in the anterior extremity, covering the anterior surface of the oral sucker partially. The suckers were developed and strongly muscular. An oral sucker present in the anterior extremity, which is subterminal, symmetrical, or slightly wider than longer; a symmetrical ventral sucker is present in the middle or third posterior portion of the body (Fig. 2G).

Across all examined fish, the total number of parasites was 147, yielding an overall mean intensity of 2.58 parasites per infected host and a mean abundance of 0.81 parasites per examined fish. Infection levels varied among host species. Alectis indica exhibited the highest parasite load, with a mean intensity of 2.95 and a mean abundance of 1.51. This was followed by R. canadum (mean intensity 2.53, mean abundance 0.84) and S. dumerili (mean intensity 2.50, mean abundance 0.71). The lowest infection values were observed in S. elongata, which showed a mean intensity of 2.00 and a mean abundance of 0.36. These patterns are consistent with the prevalence values previously reported, where A. indica exhibited the greatest infection rate (51.28%) and S. elongata the lowest (17.85%) (Table 2).

The Chi-square test indicated a significant difference in infection prevalence among the four host species (χ² = 14.56, df = 3, p < 0.01), suggesting that parasite infection was not evenly distributed among the examined species. Seasonal variation was also assessed for each species where data were available. Alectis indica showed no statistically significant seasonal variation (χ² = 0.32, df = 1, p > 0.05), whereas Rachycentron canadum and Seriola dumerili displayed decreasing prevalence from spring to summer, but these differences were not statistically significant (p > 0.05). Solea elongata exhibited stable prevalence across autumn and winter, showing no significant seasonal variation (Tables 1 and 2).

Molecular confirmation and phylogenetic analysis

The 28 S rRNA sequences obtained from four isolates (PV614122–PV614125) showed 99.2–100% similarity with reference sequences of P. bilabiatum from Brazil. In the 28 S phylogenetic tree (Fig. 3, left), the Iranian isolates (Rc21, Ai15, Sd36, Se68) clustered with Brazilian P. bilabiatum in a strongly supported clade (bootstrap = 100; posterior probability = 1.00), confirming their identity and placement within Sclerodistomidae.

The COI sequences (PV614117–PV614120) demonstrated 98.5–99.3% intra-group similarity. In the COI phylogenetic tree (Fig. 3, right), the Iranian isolates formed a distinct monophyletic clade within Sclerodistomidae, clearly separated from related families such as Lecithasteridae and Isoparorchiidae. However, no reference sequences of Prosogonotrema were available in GenBank for COI, making direct interspecific comparison impossible. These results therefore represent the first molecular record of COI for P. bilabiatum.

Discussion

Our study provides the first confirmed record of P. bilabiatum from the territorial waters of Iran in the Persian Gulf, extending both its known geographical distribution and host spectrum. Its occurrence in four phylogenetically diverse fish species A. indica (Carangidae), R. canadum (Rachycentridae), S. dumerili (Carangidae), and S. elongata (Soleidae).

The Persian Gulf is recognized as a biodiversity hotspot for various taxa [23], despite its extreme environmental conditions and relatively low overall species richness compared to the wider Indian Ocean [10]. The occurrence of P. bilabiatum in this region aligns with its previously reported distribution from a wide range of hosts and in distant localities including, Epinephelus areolatus from the Kuwaiti coast, Persian Gulf [28], Platax orbicularis from the Digha coast, West Bengal [32], Naso hexacanthus from Irabu-jima, Ryukyu Islands, Japan [33], Lutjanus synagris from the Caribbean Sea [34], Ocyurus chrysurus near Cat Cay, Caribbean (British West Indies) [35], Lutjanus griseus from the Cumaná coast in Sucre, Venezuela [36], and Lutjanus adetii and Platax pinnatus from Heron Island, Queensland, Australia [37].

In Brazil, P. bilabiatum was reported by Amato infecting the Atlantic spadefish (Chaetodipterus faber) along the southern coast of Santa Catarina State [38], by Cezar and Luque from the southeastern coast of Rio de Janeiro State [39], and from the coastal zone of the State of Rio de Janeiro by Machado and colleagues [1]. Moreover, it was observed in Rhomboplites aurorubens from states of Rio de Janeiro and Espírito Santo [40].

Such a broad geographic and host range suggests a circumtropical distribution, likely mediated by the migratory patterns and trade of marine fishes, particularly carangids such as A. indica.

Molecular analyses using 28 S rRNA and COI markers supported the morphological identification, clustering the Iranian specimens with Brazilian P. bilabiatum, thereby reinforcing its taxonomic placement. However, the genus Prosogonotrema is taxonomically complex, with multiple nominal species described across disparate regions and hosts. As suggested by Dronen et al., conducting molecular studies on these species to clarify the valid species and synonyms within Prosogonotrema and to better resolve the genus’s phylogenetic relationships [41].

Molecular analyses conducted in this study further corroborated the morphological identification of P. bilabiatum. The 28 S rRNA sequences of Iranian isolates clustered with Brazilian P. bilabiatum with strong bootstrap and posterior probability support, confirming their conspecific condition.

In addition to supporting species identification in the present study, the inclusion of the 28 S rRNA marker is particularly important for resolving taxonomic questions within Prosogonotrema and other members of the Sclerodistomidae. As demonstrated by recent research in the Mediterranean Sea [42], the 28 S gene provides robust phylogenetic signal for delineating digenean taxa, clarifying interspecific relationships, and confirming species boundaries when morphological traits are ambiguous or show overlapping ranges. This marker has proven especially valuable for differentiating closely related trematodes and for detecting hidden diversity within genera. In line with these findings, our 28 S sequences clustered the Iranian isolates with Brazilian P. bilabiatum with strong nodal support, reinforcing the reliability of this locus for comparative taxonomic work. The congruence between our morphological identification and 28 S-based phylogenetic placement underscores the essential role of nuclear ribosomal markers in integrative taxonomy, particularly for genera such as Prosogonotrema where historical descriptions are limited and molecular resources remain scarce.

In contrast, COI sequences formed a distinct and well-supported clade within Sclerodistomidae, but could not be directly compared to other Prosogonotrema taxa due to the absence of COI sequences in GenBank. This study therefore provides the first COI sequences for the genus Prosogonotrema, representing an important contribution to the molecular taxonomy of the family. The complementary use of nuclear (28 S) and mitochondrial (COI) markers underscores the value of integrative taxonomy, where 28 S allows comparison with global reference data while COI provides new genetic resources for future phylogenetic studies.

From an ecological perspective, the detection of P. bilabiatum in multiple host species from the Persian Gulf has several implications. Firstly, it highlights the Gulf as a reservoir of digenean diversity despite its relatively low overall species richness compared to the broader Indian Ocean [10, 12, 23]. Secondly, the parasite’s ability to infect both demersal and pelagic hosts suggests ecological versatility that may enhance its resilience to environmental variability. Given ongoing climate-driven changes in the Gulf, including rising sea surface temperatures and altered salinity regimes [9], parasites with broad host ranges and flexible life cycles may be particularly successful in persisting or even expanding their distribution.

Finally, the economic importance of host species such as R. canadum and S. dumerili emphasizes the need for ongoing parasitological surveillance [16, 18]. While P. bilabiatum has not been directly linked to significant fish morbidity, its presence adds to the overall parasite load and could contribute to cumulative stress in wild and cultured stocks. Future studies should therefore focus not only on documenting distribution and taxonomy, but also on evaluating potential impacts on fisheries and aquacultures in the region.

Conclusions

This study provides the first record of P. bilabiatum in Iran and new host associations in the Persian Gulf, thereby extending the known geographical and host range of the species. Morphological data were strongly supported by molecular findings: 28 S sequences confirmed a conspecific condition with Brazilian isolates, and COI sequences, reported here for the first time for the genus Prosogonotrema, provided novel reference data for future phylogenetic research. These results emphasize the importance of integrative taxonomy in marine parasitology, particularly in understudied ecosystems such as the Persian Gulf. Continued surveillance and multilocus phylogenetic analyses will be essential to clarify the evolutionary history, taxonomic relationships, and ecological implications of P. bilabiatum and related digeneans.
