Tunnelling amplitudes and Hawking radiation from worldline QFT
and Karthik Rajeev

Abstract
We compare Hawking radiation in a collapse background with Schwinger pair creation in an electric field. The comparison is driven by the presence of an analogue horizon in the Schwinger case, which causally divides spacetime for classical particles, but through which quantum fields can tunnel. Amplitudes for tunnelling processes are encoded in the asymptotic behaviour of solutions to the appropriate background-coupled wave equation. We construct these solutions, in both gravity and QED, using the worldline approach, where tunnelling and particle creation manifest as complex saddle points of a real-time path integral. For the Schwinger effect, these saddles correspond to complex worldlines, while for Hawking radiation the corresponding worldlines are real, but appear complex when extended beyond a certain coordinate patch.

2.1
Physics at effective horizons

The physics of interest, in particular the analogy with gravitational horizons, is made most explicit by working in lightfront coordinates,

\mathrm{d}s^{2}=2\mathrm{d}x^{{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\mathrm{d}x^{{\scriptscriptstyle-}}-\mathrm{d}x^{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}\mathrm{d}x^{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}\;.

(2.1)

We define null vectors
\{n_{\mu},{\bar{n}}_{\mu}\}
by
n\cdot x=x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}
,
{\bar{n}}\cdot x=x^{\scriptscriptstyle+}
,
{\bar{n}}\cdot n=1
.
Our electric field then has a field strength tensor with
F_{{\scriptscriptstyle-}{\scriptscriptstyle+}}=E
and all other components vanishing. The Lorentz force equations of motion for a particle in this field reduce to

m\ddot{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}=\pm eE{\dot{x}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}\;,\quad{\ddot{x}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}=0\;.

(2.2)

Writing
\pi_{\mu}=m\dot{x}_{\mu}
for the kinematic momenta, the first integral of (
2.2
) is

\pi_{\scriptscriptstyle+}=p_{\scriptscriptstyle+}-eEx^{\scriptscriptstyle-}\;,\quad\pi_{\scriptscriptstyle-}=p_{\scriptscriptstyle-}+eEx^{\scriptscriptstyle+}\;,\qquad\pi_{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}=p_{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}\;,

(2.3)

where for simplicity we have chosen initial conditions
\pi_{\mu}(0)=p_{\mu}
and
x^{\mu}(0)=0
. Example solutions are shown as part of Fig.
2
, below.

The statement that massive particles have speed less than
c
translates to
\pi_{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}>0
in lightfront coordinates. It is then apparent from (
2.3
) that particles with
eE>0
are accelerated to
c
in finite ‘lightfront time’
x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}
(but infinite ‘instant form’ time
x^{0}
); they cannot cross the surface

x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}=x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}_{h}:=\frac{p_{\scriptscriptstyle+}}{eE}\;.

(2.4)

This null surface can be thought of as an electromagnetic ‘horizon’
[
46
,
47
]
, as the particle is causally disconnected from the region
x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}>x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}_{h}
. The analogy with gravitational horizons is not exact, since particles of the opposite charge can pass the horizon without issue – however, these particles have an equivalent horizon at
x^{\scriptscriptstyle+}=-p_{\scriptscriptstyle-}/(eE)
.

Turning to the quantum theory, we solve the Klein-Gordon equation
(D^{2}+m^{2})\varphi=0
to construct particle wavefunctions
\varphi
, where
D_{\mu}=\partial_{\mu}+ieA_{\mu}
. A convenient gauge potential for the constant electric field which manifests the connection to the classical physics above is

\displaystyle eA_{\mu}={\bar{n}}_{\mu}eEx^{\scriptscriptstyle-}\;.

(2.5)

A natural ansatz for
incoming
particle wavefunctions is then

\displaystyle\varphi_{p}(x)=e^{-ip_{\scriptscriptstyle+}x^{\scriptscriptstyle+}-ip_{{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}}x^{{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}}}f_{p}(x^{{\scriptscriptstyle-}})\;,

(2.6)

on which the KG equation reduces to the first-order form

\displaystyle 2ip_{{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\Big(1-x^{{\scriptscriptstyle-}}/x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}_{h}\Big)f_{p}^{\prime}(x^{{\scriptscriptstyle-}})=\big(p_{{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}}^{2}+m^{2}+ieE\big)f_{p}(x^{\scriptscriptstyle-})\;.

(2.7)

This would be trivial to solve except that the solution is ambiguous exactly at the location of the classical horizon
x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}=x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}_{h}
. To be precise, we write down the naive solution of (
2.7
) in the region
x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}<x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}_{h}
, that is

\displaystyle\varphi_{p}(x)=e^{-ip_{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}x^{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}-ip_{\scriptscriptstyle+}x^{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\,e^{-\left(\frac{1}{2}-i\frac{p^{2}_{{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}}+m^{2}}{2eE}\right)\log\left(1-\frac{x^{{\scriptscriptstyle-}}}{x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}_{h}}\right)}\;.

(2.8)

The questions to answer are then ‘how do we continue this wavefunction past the horizon?’, and ‘what is the associated physics?’ It is here that the worldline approach is useful.

2.2
Worldline representation of particle wavefunctions

To construct particle wavefunctions in the worldline approach, we first recall the worldline representation of the scalar field propagator in a background
A_{\mu}
, that is

G(x,y)=\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\!\mathrm{d}T\,\int\limits_{z(0)=y}^{z(T)=x}\!\mathcal{D}z\,e^{iS}\;,\qquad S=-\int_{0}^{T}\!\mathrm{d}\tau\,\bigg(\frac{1}{4}{\dot{z}}^{2}+m^{2}+e{\dot{z}}\cdot A(z)\bigg)\;,

(2.9)

in which
T
is proper time. The path integral is taken over all worldlines
z^{\mu}(\tau)
with Dirichlet boundary conditions at
\tau=0
and
\tau=T
, and represents the quantum mechanical transition element between states
\ket{x}
and
\ket{y}
with Hamiltonian
H=D^{2}+m^{2}
, i.e.

G(x,y)\equiv\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\!\mathrm{d}T\,\bra{x}e^{-i(H-i\epsilon)T}\ket{y}\;.

(2.10)

We could use some other boundary condition at, say,
T=0
, or sew the propagator to some chosen state, replacing
\ket{y}\to\ket{\psi}
. The resulting path integral simply describes how information in
\ket{\psi}
is propagated to
x^{\mu}
.
Now, observe that by computing the same path integral, but extending the proper-time integral to the entire real line, we no longer propagate but rather
project
onto solutions of the Klein-Gordon equation since, formally,

\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\mathrm{d}T\bra{x}e^{-iHT}\ket{\psi}=\bra{x}2\pi\delta(H)\ket{\psi}\;.

(2.11)

Let us apply this to our system of a scalar particle in an electric field. To describe the incoming wavefunction (
2.8
), it is natural to impose initial conditions in momentum space, which translates to the choice
\braket{y|\psi}\propto e^{-ip\cdot y}
. With this, (
2.11
) motivates us to consider the path integral

\begin{split}\varphi_{p}(x)&:=\int\!\mathrm{d}^{4}y\,e^{-ip\cdot y}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\mathrm{d}T\!\int^{z(T)=x}_{z(0)=y}\mathcal{D}z\,e^{iS}\;,\end{split}

(2.12)

with
p_{\mu}
on-shell; attaching our chosen state amounts to taking a Fourier transform with respect to the initial worldline point
y^{\mu}
. We may convert (
2.12
) to the equivalent form

\begin{split}\varphi_{p}(x)&=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\mathrm{d}T\!\int^{z(T)=x}\mathcal{D}z\,e^{iS_{\text{WL}}}\;.\end{split}

(2.13)

in which we have free boundary conditions at
\tau=0
, but the worldline action acquires a boundary term, i.e.

\displaystyle{S}_{\rm WL}=-p\cdot z(0)-\int_{0}^{T}\!\mathrm{d}\tau\,\bigg(\frac{\dot{z}^{2}}{4}+m^{2}+eA\cdot\dot{z}\bigg)\;.

(2.14)

Practically, the simplest way to compute the path integral in (
2.13
) is to use Dirichlet boundary conditions on both ends of the line, and then Fourier transform to momentum space. Since the integral is Gaussian, and well-known in closed form (for a recent treatment see
[
90
]
) we simply quote results. The exact form of the kernel
\braket{x|e^{-iHT}|y}
in the gauge (
2.5
) is
[
46
]

\displaystyle\braket{x|e^{-iHT}|y}

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{-im^{2}T}}{16i\pi^{2}T}\frac{eE}{\sinh(eET)}e^{\frac{i(x^{{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}}-y^{{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}})^{2}}{4T}}e^{-\frac{ieE(x^{\scriptscriptstyle+}-y^{\scriptscriptstyle+})(x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}-y^{\scriptscriptstyle-}e^{-2eET})}{\left(1-e^{-2eET}\right)}}\;.

(2.15)

The Fourier transform of the above is again Gaussian, and we obtain

\displaystyle\varphi_{p}(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\mathrm{d}T\,e^{eET}e^{iS_{\rm cl}}\;,

(2.16)

in which

\displaystyle S_{\rm cl}=\frac{p_{\scriptscriptstyle-}p_{\scriptscriptstyle+}\left(e^{2eET}-1\right)(1-x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}/x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}_{h})}{eE}-p\cdot x-(p_{\perp}^{2}+m^{2})T\;.

(2.17)

Note that
S_{\rm cl}
is the classical action, i.e.
S_{\rm WL}
in (
2.14
) evaluated on the solution of the classical worldline equations of motion obeying the boundary conditions

z^{\mu}(T)=x^{\mu}\;,\qquad\tfrac{1}{2}\dot{z}^{\mu}(0)+eA^{\mu}(z(0))=p^{\mu}\;.

(2.18)

The solution is, explicitly,
z_{\rm cl}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}}(\tau)=x^{{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}}-2p_{{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}}(T-\tau)
and

\displaystyle z_{\rm cl}^{{\scriptscriptstyle-}}(\tau)

\displaystyle=x_{h}^{\scriptscriptstyle-}+\left(x^{{\scriptscriptstyle-}}-x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}_{h}\right)e^{2eE(T-\tau)}\;,\qquad z_{\rm cl}^{{\scriptscriptstyle+}}(\tau)=x^{{\scriptscriptstyle+}}+\left(\frac{e^{2eE\tau}-e^{2eET}}{eE}\right)p_{{\scriptscriptstyle-}}\,.

(2.19)

We note the appearance of the same factor
1-x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}/x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}_{h}
in (
2.17
) as in the wavefunctions of Sec.
2.1
. The action and integrand of (
2.16
) are, though, well-behaved everywhere, so resolution of what happens at the horizon must lie in evaluation of the
T
-integral, to which we now turn.

2.3
Saddle point analysis

We now analyse the
T
-integral (
2.16
), identifying the saddle points and steepest descent contours in the complex
T
–plane. We observe first that a change of variables
Y=e^{eET}
absorbs the leading exponential in (
2.16
) into the measure, hence we may focus on the behaviour of the classical action
S_{\text{cl}}
. We continue to use
T
for simplicity.

The integral (
2.16
) is still a sum over worldlines, but now over the
saddle point
worldlines
z_{\rm cl}(\tau)
, which we can think of as parametrised by
T
. These are not the classical trajectories discussed in (
2.1
), as they can be both timelike and spacelike, as seen by considering

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}\dot{z}_{\rm cl}^{2}=2p_{{\scriptscriptstyle-}}p_{{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\bigg(1-\frac{x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}}{x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}_{h}}\bigg)e^{2eET}-p_{{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}}^{2}\;.

(2.20)

In the classically allowed regime,
x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}<x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}_{h}
, the right hand side of (
2.20
) can be positive or negative. In the classically forbidden region
x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}>x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}_{h}
, though, contributions to (
2.16
), thus to the wavefunction
\varphi_{p}(x)
, come exclusively from spacelike worldlines, see Fig
2
. This suggests that the two cases
x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}\gtrless x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}_{h}
will need to be treated separately, as is immediately born out when we identify the saddle points
T_{s}
of (
2.16
). These obey
S_{\rm cl}^{\prime}(T_{s})=0
, or

\displaystyle e^{-2eET_{s}}=1-\frac{x^{{\scriptscriptstyle-}}}{x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}_{h}}\;,

(2.21)

(This saddle point condition is equivalent to
\dot{z}_{\rm cl}^{2}/4-m^{2}=0
for classical worldlines.) Outside the horizon,
x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}<x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}_{h}
, the right hand side of (
2.21
) is positive and the saddle points lie at

T_{s}=-\frac{1}{2eE}\log\bigg(1-\frac{x^{{\scriptscriptstyle-}}}{x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}_{h}}\bigg)-\frac{n\pi i}{eE}\;,\quad n\in\mathbb{Z}\;,

(2.22)

in particular the
n=0
saddle lies on the real axis. Beyond the horizon, on the other hand,
x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}>x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}_{h}
, the right hand side of (
2.21
) becomes negative and there is no real saddle. Instead all saddle points lie off the real axis at

T_{s}=\frac{-i\pi}{2eE}-\frac{1}{2eE}\log\bigg(\frac{x^{{\scriptscriptstyle-}}}{x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}_{h}}-1\bigg)-\frac{n\pi i}{eE}\;,\quad n\in\mathbb{Z}\;.

(2.23)

It is useful to now look at the explicit form of the corresponding saddle point trajectories.

Outside the horizon, substitution of the saddle point condition (
2.22
) into (
2.19
) yields a classical worldline (i.e. a solution of the Lorentz force equation) with the form

\displaystyle z^{{\scriptscriptstyle-}}_{\rm cl}(\tau)\Big\rvert_{T\rightarrow T_{s}}=x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}_{h}(1-e^{-2eE\tau})\,,

(2.24)

where we focus on the minus-component. This worldline always lies in the classically allowed region
z_{\rm cl}^{\scriptscriptstyle-}<x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}_{h}
, even if
\tau
varies over the whole real line, see Fig.
2(a)
.
For
x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}>x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}_{h}
on the other hand, the saddle point value of
T_{s}
is complex, implying that
z^{{\scriptscriptstyle-}}_{\rm cl}(\tau)
in (
2.24
) can be complex, as
\tau
itself is now allowed to take complex values.
The worldlines are thus only defined up to contour deformations. Nevertheless, some physical insight can still be obtained by choosing a representative contour in the complex
\tau
-plane, for example that shown in Fig.
1
.
The saddle point worldline is, explicitly, writing
r\equiv\text{Re}[T_{s}]\in\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle z^{{\scriptscriptstyle-}}_{\rm cl}(\tau)|_{T\to T_{s}}=\begin{cases}x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}_{h}(1-e^{-2eEt})\;,&\tau=t\in[r,\infty)\\[2.0pt]
x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}_{h}(1-e^{-i\pi s}e^{-2eE\,r})&\tau=\displaystyle\frac{i\pi}{2eE}s+r\;,\quad s\in(-1,0)\\[5.0pt]
x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}_{h}(1+e^{-2eEt})&\tau=\displaystyle\frac{-i\pi}{2eE}+t\;,\quad t\in(-\infty,r]\;.\end{cases}

(2.25)

When
\tau
lies on the positive real axis, see Fig.
1
, we recover precisely (
2.24
), which is again a particle worldline lying outside the horizon. Now, when
\tau
lies on the lower horizontal line in Fig.
1
, the trajectory remains real and describes an
antiparticle
worldline, parametrized by real
\tau_{r}
, which always lies
beyond
the horizon. Therefore, the complex worldline describing tunneling can be thought of the union of real particle and anti-particle trajectories bridged by a complex worldline, as shown in Fig.
2(b)
. These serve as proxies for correlations associated with the spacelike worldlines on which the path integral has support when
x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}>x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}_{h}
.

These saddle point solutions, or ‘worldline instantons’, are commonly encountered in the Schwinger effect –

T
-integral. We could do this by integrating along the steepest descent contours, which are shown along with the saddle points in Fig.

for
x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}<x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}_{h}
and in Fig.

for
x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}>x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}_{h}
. The steepest descent contours pick up contributions from all saddles, as we discuss later, but here we present a more direct route to the wavefunction of interest.

For
x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}<x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}_{h}
we shift the integration variable by the real (
n=0
) saddle,
T\to T+T_{s}
. This pulls all
x^{\mu}
-dependence out of the proper time integral which thus contributes a normalisation constant,
\mathcal{N}
. The integral is convergent, since we can simply displace the contour into the upper half plane, such that it runs to the green-shaded region of Fig.
3(a)
just above the real axis. For
x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}>x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}_{h}
, the singularities of
iS_{\text{cl}}
tell us, see Fig.

below
the real axis, i.e.
n=0
in (

). Again, this pulls all
x^{\mu}
-dependence out of the proper-time integral. One finds, combining both cases,

\displaystyle\varphi_{p}(x)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}e^{-ip_{\scriptscriptstyle+}x^{\scriptscriptstyle+}-ip_{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}x^{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}}\begin{cases}e^{-\big(\frac{1}{2}-i\frac{m^{2}+p_{\perp}^{2}}{2eE}\big)\log\left(1-\frac{x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}}{x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}_{h}}\right)}&x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}<x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}_{h}\;,\\
{e^{-\pi\frac{p_{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}^{2}+m^{2}}{2eE}}}e^{-i\frac{\pi}{2}}e^{-\big(\frac{1}{2}-i\frac{m^{2}+p_{\perp}^{2}}{2eE}\big)\log\left(\frac{x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}}{x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}_{h}}-1\right)}&x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}>x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}_{h}\;,\end{cases}

2.8
).

. The sign of the exponent (damping rather than an unphysical exponential increase) is fixed by the allowed contour deformations. The quantum wavefunction thus tunnels into the classically forbidden region, where it is exponentially damped.

The normalisation constant is

\begin{split}\mathcal{N}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\mathrm{d}T\,e^{eET}\exp\left[i(e^{2eE{T}}-1)\frac{p_{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}^{2}+m^{2}}{2eE}-i(p_{\perp}^{2}+m^{2}){T}\right]\;.\end{split}

(2.27)

The integral can be performed in terms of the Gamma function, but we will only need its modulus. Any other ambiguities can be fixed by the limit
eE\to 0
. We can ultimately write

\mathcal{N}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+e^{-\pi(p_{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}^{2}+m^{2})/(eE)}}}\;.

(2.28)

We may write a single expression for
\varphi_{p}(x)
valid for all
x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}
by noting that the functional form in (

) corresponds to using the principal value of log. With this understood, we have

\varphi_{p}(x)=\mathcal{N}\,e^{-ip_{\scriptscriptstyle+}x^{\scriptscriptstyle+}-ip_{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}x^{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}}e^{-\big(\frac{1}{2}-i\frac{m^{2}+p_{\perp}^{2}}{2eE}\big)\log\left(1-\frac{x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}}{x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}_{h}}\right)}\;,\quad\forall\,x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}\;.

(2.29)

The classical physics of this system suggests that the the 1-to-1 scattering amplitude should be encoded in the behaviour of
\varphi_{p}(x)
for
x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}<x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}_{h}
, while our quantum expectations are that the pair creation amplitude should be encoded in
\varphi_{p}(x)
in the classically forbidden region
x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}>x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}_{h}
. As we confirm in detail in Appendix.

,
\mathcal{N}
is essentialy the 1-to-1 amplitude. As we cross the horizon, this is multiplied by the Schwinger factor in (
2.26
), and this is the pair creation amplitude. In other words we can read off from

2.3.1
Properties of tunnelling and other modes

More properties of the tunnelling wavefunction (
2.29
) are highlighted by comparison with other possible solutions of the Klein-Gordon equation. These can be obtained by, for example, choosing a different contour for the proper time integral. The purple dashed curve in Fig.
3
is one such choice; for
x<x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}_{h}
the contour integral reproduces the same function (

x>x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}_{h}
the contour can be contracted to a point, and the integral vanishes. One therefore obtains

\displaystyle\int_{\infty}^{\infty-i\frac{\pi}{2eE}}\mathrm{d}T\!\int^{z(T)=x}\!\mathcal{D}[z]\,e^{i\mathcal{S}_{\rm WL}}\propto\Theta(x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}_{h}-x^{\scriptscriptstyle-})\,\varphi_{p}(x)=:\phi_{p}(x)\;.

(2.31)

The contour in (

2.1

\phi_{p}
, hence the normalisation differs from that of the tunnelling wavefunction (which we revisit in the next section), and so all contributing saddle point worldlines lie outside the horizon.

It looks from (

) that, in the chosen gauge, boundary conditions are naturally placed on surfaces of constant
x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}
.
This provides data on a characteristic, rather than Cauchy data – see

The choice of proper-time contour seems to be a way to impose extra conditions which leads to different solutions. The lack of good asymptotics obscures this, but if we imagine that the field is turned off at some finite
x^{\scriptscriptstyle+}

The relevant integrals are easily performed in terms of the gamma function. Writing out only the nontrivial structures, we find

\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\mathrm{d}x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}e^{i\omega x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}}\phi_{p}(x)\sim(i\omega x_{h}^{\scriptscriptstyle-})^{-i\frac{\lambda}{2}-\frac{1}{2}}x_{h}^{\scriptscriptstyle-}e^{i\omega x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}_{h}}\Gamma\big(i\frac{\lambda}{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}\big)\;,

(2.32)

which in particular has support on all frequencies
\omega

\phi_{p}(x)

87
]
, which we discuss in Sec.
4

\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\mathrm{d}x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}e^{i\omega x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}}\varphi_{p}(x)\sim(i\omega x_{h}^{\scriptscriptstyle-})^{-i\frac{\lambda}{2}-\frac{1}{2}}x_{h}^{\scriptscriptstyle-}e^{i\omega x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}_{h}}\Gamma\big(i\dfrac{\lambda}{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}\big)\big(1+{e^{-\pi\lambda}}\big)\Theta(\omega)\;,

which, in contrast to (

\varphi_{p}

3
Amplitudes from mode functions in background fields

Particle creation in a background, be it in gauge theory or gravity, is often described in terms of Bogoliubov coefficients relating incoming and outgoing modes of the corresponding field operator (assuming a quadratic Hamiltonian, as will be the case throughout). The ultimate objects of interest, though, are asymptotic observables, which should be expressible in terms of amplitudes. The purpose of this section is therefore to give some general results linking the Bogoliubov and amplitudes-based approaches, in any gauge or gravitational background with sufficiently good behaviour to admit a notion of asymptotic states. (For a recent discussion of the subtleties concerning asymptotic states when long-range forces are involved see
[
102
]
.) Combined with intuition from the Schwinger effect, this will set the stage for our later discussion of Hawking radiation.

Our starting point is the appropriate scalar field equation – the Klein-Gordon equation in QED or wave equation in gravity – and its ‘in’ and ‘out’ mode solutions.
These reduce to free solutions in the asymptotic past and future (as appropriately defined for massive or massless particles) respectively. For in, out and free wavefunctions parameterised by a set of quantum numbers ‘
{\mathbf{i}}
’ we write
\varphi_{\mathbf{i}}^{\pm\text{in}}
,
\varphi_{\mathbf{i}}^{\pm\text{out}}
,
\varphi_{\mathbf{i}}^{\pm\text{free}}
respectively, in which ‘
\pm
’ refers to positive and negative energy modes (particles and antiparticles in QED). The scalar field operator
\Phi(x)
can be expanded using either the in or out modes as

\displaystyle\Phi(x)

\displaystyle=b_{\mathbf{i}}\varphi^{+\text{in}}_{\mathbf{i}}(x)+d_{\mathbf{i}}^{\dagger}\varphi^{-\text{in}}_{\mathbf{i}}(x)=B_{\mathbf{i}}\varphi^{+\text{out}}_{\mathbf{i}}(x)+D_{\mathbf{i}}^{\dagger}\varphi^{-\text{out}}_{\mathbf{i}}(x)\;,

(3.1)

in which an appropriate sum/integral over repeated indices is implied. The set of ‘in’ operators defines the in-vacuum via
b_{\mathbf{i}}\ket{\rm in}=d_{\mathbf{i}}\ket{\rm in}=0
and, similarly, the out-vacuum
1
1

1

The ‘in’ vacuum is naturally taken to be the usual, empty vacuum in which our initial scattering states are prepared. The ‘out’ vacuum is just some other reference state,
not
the time-evolved ‘in’ vacuum.
is defined by
B_{\mathbf{i}}\ket{\rm out}=D_{\mathbf{i}}\ket{\rm out}=0
.

Linearity of the wave equation implies that in-modes can be expressed as linear combinations of out-modes, and vice versa. Equivalently, the form of the Hamiltonian implies that the in-operators in (
3.1
) are linear combinations of the out-operators. We may write

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}b_{\mathbf{i}}\\
d^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{i}}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha_{{\mathbf{i}}\,{\mathbf{j}}}&\beta_{{\mathbf{i}}\,{\mathbf{j}}}\\[3.0pt]
\beta^{\dagger}_{{\mathbf{i}}\,{\mathbf{j}}}&\alpha^{\dagger}_{{\mathbf{i}}\,{\mathbf{j}}}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}B_{\mathbf{j}}\\
D^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{j}}\end{pmatrix}\;,

(3.2)

in which we adopt a matrix notation for the
Bogoliubov coefficients
such that, e.g.
\alpha^{\dagger}_{{\mathbf{i}}\,{\mathbf{j}}}=\alpha^{\star}_{{\mathbf{j}}\,{\mathbf{i}}}
. The field operator in the asymptotic future is related to that in the past by the
S
-matrix, hence
B_{\mathbf{i}}=S^{\dagger}b_{\mathbf{i}}S
and so on, while unitarity of the transformation is expressed as usual by

(\alpha\alpha^{\dagger})_{{\mathbf{i}}\,{\mathbf{j}}}-(\beta\beta^{\dagger})_{{\mathbf{i}}\,{\mathbf{j}}}=\delta_{{\mathbf{i}}\,{\mathbf{j}}}\;,\qquad(\alpha\beta)_{{\mathbf{i}}\,{\mathbf{j}}}-(\beta\alpha)_{{\mathbf{i}}\,{\mathbf{j}}}=0\;.

(3.3)

The in and out modes themselves are related by

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\phi^{+\rm in}_{\mathbf{i}}&\phi^{-\rm in}_{\mathbf{i}}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\phi^{+\rm out}_{\mathbf{j}}&\phi^{-\rm out}_{\mathbf{j}}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\alpha^{\dagger}_{{\mathbf{j}}\,{\mathbf{i}}}&-\beta_{{\mathbf{j}}\,{\mathbf{i}}}\\[3.0pt]
-\beta^{\dagger}_{{\mathbf{j}}\,{\mathbf{i}}}&\alpha_{{\mathbf{j}}\,{\mathbf{i}}}\end{pmatrix}\;.

(3.4)

To illustrate the information content of the Bogoliubov coefficients, we can compute e.g. the expected number of particles in the asymptotic future, starting from the empty vacuum
\ket{\text{in}}
:

\displaystyle\braket{{\rm in}|S^{\dagger}{b^{\dagger}}_{\!{\mathbf{i}}}b_{\mathbf{i}}S|{\rm in}}=\beta^{\dagger}_{{\mathbf{i}}\,{\mathbf{j}}}{\beta}_{{\mathbf{j}}\,{\mathbf{i}}}\;,\quad\text{(no sum over ${\mathbf{i}}$)}\;.

(3.5)

This expression and equations (
3.4
) show that the out-modes evaluated at
past
asymptotic infinity encapsulate, via the Bogoliubov coefficients, information about particle creation at
future
asymptotic infinity. The Bogoluibov coefficients must also encode the amplitudes of the background QFT, namely the
1\rightarrow 1
scattering and
0\to 2
pair creation amplitudes,

\displaystyle\braket{{\rm in}|{b_{\mathbf{j}}}S{b^{\dagger}}_{\!{\mathbf{i}}}|{\rm in}}\;,\qquad\braket{{\rm in}|{d_{\mathbf{j}}}S{d^{\dagger}}_{\!{\mathbf{i}}}|{\rm in}}\;,\qquad\braket{{\rm in}|d_{\mathbf{j}}b_{\mathbf{i}}S|{\rm in}}\,.

(3.6)

In/out modes are naturally associated with retarded/advanced boundary conditions and propagators, whereas amplitudes are naturally associated with Feynman propagators. This prompts us to consider a different set of solutions of the wave equations – the Feynman modes. We will
define
these in terms of the in-modes as

\displaystyle\varphi^{+\text{F}}_{{\mathbf{i}}}(x):=\varphi^{+\text{in}}_{{\mathbf{j}}}(x)({\alpha^{\dagger-1}})_{{\mathbf{j}}\,{\mathbf{i}}}\qquad\varphi^{-\text{F}}_{{\mathbf{i}}}(x):=\varphi^{-\text{in}}_{{\mathbf{j}}}(x)(\alpha^{-1})_{{\mathbf{j}}\,{\mathbf{i}}}\,\;.

(3.9)

By construction, the
\varphi^{\pm\text{F}}_{\mathbf{i}}(x)
are positive and negative energy modes in the asymptotic past, like the in-modes. The difference is that, while the in/out-modes directly encode the Bogoliubov coefficients, the Feynman modes directly encode the scattering and pair production amplitudes
\mathcal{A}_{1\rightarrow 1}(\mathbf{i}\rightarrow\mathbf{j})
and
\mathcal{A}_{0\rightarrow 2}(\mathbf{i},\mathbf{j})
through their asymptotic behaviour. Specifically, it follows from (
3.4
) and the expressions (
3.7
)–(
3.8
) that

\displaystyle\varphi^{-\text{F}}_{\mathbf{i}}(x)\sim\begin{cases}\sum_{\mathbf{j}}\mathcal{A}_{1\rightarrow 1}(\mathbf{i}\rightarrow\mathbf{j})\varphi^{-\text{free}}_{\mathbf{j}}(x)\quad&;\quad x^{0}\rightarrow-\infty\,,\\
\varphi^{-\text{free}}_{\mathbf{i}}(x)+\sum_{\mathbf{j}}\mathcal{A}_{0\rightarrow 2}(\mathbf{i},\mathbf{j})\varphi^{+\text{free}}_{\mathbf{j}}(x)\quad&;\quad x^{0}\rightarrow+\infty\,.\end{cases}

(3.10)

Amplitudes are thus encoded in the
asymptotic behaviour
of the Feynman modes.

While we defined the Feynman modes from in-modes, one could alternatively obtain them by solving the appropriate wave equation with boundary conditions implied by (
3.10
). These are that
\varphi^{-\text{F}}_{\mathbf{i}}(x)
(1) approaches a negative energy function in the past (the first line of (
3.10
) is a sum over free, on-shell negative energy modes) and (2) is a mixture of positive and negative modes in the future, with the negative energy part being a free ‘
\mathbf{i}
’ mode. Practically, in a perturbative solution of the wave equation in powers of the coupling, the Feynman modes follow from iterating with the free anti-/Feynman propagator
[
106
]
.

We comment that in perturbative, amplitude-based approaches, the exponentiation of amplitudes – obtained by resumming diagrams at all orders but within a fixed order of the semiclassical expansion – occurs after performing an on-shell Fourier transform from momentum space to spacetime. The asymptotic behaviour of the Feynman modes, as we have just seen, corresponds precisely to this transform. This makes studying the Feynman modes a natural alternative to analysing amplitudes directly, in particular in semiclassical settings, such as Hawking radiation.

The evolution of trajectories from positive to negative energy echoes the changes in spectral properties of the wavefunctions of Sec.
3

2

4.2
Worldline representation of wavefunctions

We now translate the gauge theory framework of Sec.
2.2

2.2

Although we can sidestep many technical difficulties in our setup, the event horizon introduces further specific challenges, related to both the definition of the path-integral measure and the choice of ‘initial’ state

4.2.1
The out-modes

Working in the formalism of Sec.
3
, the appropriate past asymptotic boundary for massless scalars is
\mathcal{I}^{-}
, from which originate all radial null geodesics. The appropriate future asymptotic boundary is the union of the future event horizon
\mathcal{H}
and the endpoint
\mathcal{I}^{+}
of all radially null geodesics that escape the collapse. It is useful to make a separation of the out-modes
\varphi^{\pm\rm out}_{\mathbf{i}}

into two sets, depending on their asymptotic support. We write
\varphi^{\pm\mathcal{I}}_{\mathbf{i}}
for wavefunctions which reduce to positive and negative energy modes near
\mathcal{I}^{+}
, but have no support on
\mathcal{H}
. Similarly,
\varphi^{\pm\mathcal{H}}_{\mathbf{i}}
denotes wavefunctions which reduce to positive and negative energy modes near
\mathcal{H}
, with no support on
\mathcal{I}^{+}
.
While the
\varphi^{\pm\mathcal{I}}_{\mathbf{i}}
can be uniquely specified, the choice of
\varphi^{\pm\mathcal{H}}_{\mathbf{i}}
involves a degree of arbitrariness. For brevity, we shall now focus on the positive-energy modes, writing
\varphi_{\mathbf{i}}^{\mathcal{I}}\equiv\varphi_{\mathbf{i}}^{+\mathcal{I}}

The symmetry of the problem tells us to work in a basis of spherical modes, such that ‘
\mathbf{i}

\epsilon
is an energy. We will focus on

{\mathbf{i}}\to\epsilon

\displaystyle\varphi^{\mathcal{I}}_{\epsilon}(y)\sim\frac{e^{-i\epsilon U}}{\epsilon R}\;,\qquad y\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rightarrow}}{{\in}}\mathcal{I}^{+}\;.

(4.15)

\mathcal{I}^{+}
associated with a radial geodesic that initially starts at
V=v
on
\mathcal{I}^{-}
.

Finally, the spherical in-modes, on the other hand, take the form

\displaystyle\varphi^{\pm\rm in}_{\epsilon}(x)\sim\frac{e^{\mp i\epsilon v}}{\epsilon r}\;,\qquad x\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rightarrow}}{{\in}}\mathcal{I}^{-}\,,

(4.24)

and so the Bogoliubov coefficients can be found simply by Fourier transforming (
4.23
). The particle spectrum, recall by (
3.5
), then takes the well-known form of a thermal distribution

\displaystyle\braket{{\rm in}|a_{{\mathcal{I}}}^{\epsilon\,\dagger}a_{\mathcal{I}}^{\epsilon}|{\rm in}}\propto\frac{1}{e^{8\pi GM\epsilon}-1}\;.

(4.25)

4.3
The tunnelling modes and Hawking radiation

We finally turn the the tunnelling modes, from which we will read off the amplitude for Hawking radiation. The tunnelling modes have support on
\mathcal{H}
as well as
\mathcal{I}^{+}
. Their worldline representation should then allow for paths that cross the horizon, c.f. the discussion below (
2.29
). This implies that we also need a state
\psi
which can be defined beyond the horizon. If we want to attach the ‘same’ state as for the out modes, (
4.26
), then we need only to find a way to continue the coordinate
U
.

The way to do so is provided by Sec.
3
and Sec.
2
. The first line of (
3.10
) tells us that we need to continue
U
such that the resulting tunnelling mode is a positive energy function on
\mathcal{I}^{-}
. The intuition for
how
to do this is provided by the Schwinger-effect examples in (
2.32
) and (
2.33
) – we define
U
for the entire range of
0<R<\infty
by the same analytic continuation as used for the Schwinger effect, i.e. we have (
4.11
) but with log understood to be take its principal value.
Our initial state may then again be written

\displaystyle\psi(y)\propto\frac{e^{-i\epsilon U}}{\epsilon R}\;,\qquad\epsilon>0\,.

(4.26)

It follows that the appropriate worldline action
S_{\rm WL}
also has the same form as for the out-modes, see (
4.17
), and we can write

\displaystyle\varphi_{\epsilon}^{\text{F}}(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dT\int^{z(T/2)=x}\mathcal{D}[z]e^{iS_{\rm WL}}\;,

(4.27)

where the measure now allows for worldlines that may cross the horizon. The boundary conditions on the worldlines also remain the same as in (
4.21
) and (
4.20
). This is because the initial condition (
4.21
) specifies only the proper-time derivative
\dot{U}(-T/2)
and not
U(-T/2)
itself. It follows that the geometrical optics approximation to the (positive and negative energy) tunnelling modes will, as for the out-mode, take the form

\displaystyle\varphi_{\epsilon}^{\pm\text{F}}(x)\propto\frac{e^{\pm iS_{\rm WL}[z_{\rm cl.}]}}{\epsilon r}\;,

(4.28)

where
z_{\rm cl}(\tau)
is again the classical geodesic respecting the boundary conditions (
4.20
) and (
4.21
). This means that for
v<v_{0}
the tunnelling and out-modes have the same functional form (just as the (
2.29
) and in-modes (
2.31
) for the Schwinger effect agree outside the horizon). So, we have, from now on focussing on the
negative
energy mode which, see (
3.10
), most directly encode the pair production amplitude,

\displaystyle\varphi_{\epsilon}^{-\text{F}}(x)\propto\frac{e^{+i\epsilon\left[v-4GM\log\left(\frac{v-v_{0}}{v_{0}}\right)\right]}}{\epsilon r}=\varphi^{-\mathcal{I}}_{\epsilon}(x)\;,\qquad x\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rightarrow}}{{\in}}\mathcal{I}^{-}\quad\text{and}\quad v<v_{0}.

(4.29)

For
v>v_{0}
on the other hand, while no saddle points contribute to
\varphi^{\mathcal{I}}_{\epsilon}(x)
, the tunnelling mode receives contributions from saddle points (geodesics) that cross the horizon. The final result for the tunnelling wavefunction near
\mathcal{I}^{-}
is again determined by the value of
U(-T/2)
, but this is now complex. One finds

\displaystyle\varphi_{\epsilon}^{-\text{F}}(x)\propto e^{-4\pi GM\epsilon}\frac{e^{+i\epsilon\left[v-4GM\log\left(\frac{v_{0}-v}{v_{0}}\right)\right]}}{\epsilon r}\;,\qquad x\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rightarrow}}{{\in}}\mathcal{I}^{-}\;,\qquad v>v_{0}\;,

(4.30)

in which the leading exponential factor arises from the imaginary part of
U(-T/2)
. To interpret this result, we recall the discussion in Sec.
4.1
, from which one might expect the contributing saddle points to here describe geodesics that start from
\mathcal{I}^{-}
, cross the horizon, and ultimately hit the singularity. However, (
4.10
) tells us that for such a geodesic, with
v>v_{0}
, the outgoing energy would be negative, violating the boundary condition
\dot{R}(-T/2)=2\epsilon>0
in (
4.21
). The correct interpretation of the contributing saddle points is rather that they are the
time-reversed
versions of the horizon-crossing radial geodesics – they start from the singularity, cross the horizon, and ultimately reach
V=v
at
\mathcal{I}^{-}
, see Fig.
7(b)
. These time-reversed geodesics satisfy all the boundary conditions in (
4.20
) and (
4.21
), hence are valid saddle points of the path integral in (
4.28
).

For this reason we identify (
4.30
), aside from the leading real exponential, with the asymptotic form of the wavefunction
\varphi^{-\mathcal{H}}_{\epsilon}(x)
; this is the mode function having support only on
\mathcal{H}
in the future, and the minus sign appears in the superscript because the saddle point is a time-reversed geodesic, hence this function should be associated to a negative energy state.

We can summarise the situation as follows. For
x
approaching
\mathcal{I}^{-}
the tunnelling wavefunction is a negative energy function with the form

\displaystyle\varphi_{\epsilon}^{-\rm F}(x)\sim\frac{1}{\epsilon r}\begin{cases}\displaystyle{e^{+i\epsilon\left[v-4GM\log\left(\frac{v-v_{0}}{v_{0}}\right)\right]}}&v<v_{0}\\[10.0pt]
\displaystyle e^{-4\pi GM\epsilon}{e^{+i\epsilon\left[v-4GM\log\left(\frac{v_{0}-v}{v_{0}}\right)\right]}}&v>v_{0}\end{cases}\qquad x\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rightarrow}}{{\in}}\mathcal{I}^{-}\;.

(4.31)

Expressed as a function
throughout
spacetime, the tunnelling wavefunctions will take the form

\displaystyle\varphi_{\epsilon}^{\rm\pm F}(x)=\varphi_{\epsilon}^{\pm\mathcal{I}}(x)+e^{-4\pi GM\epsilon}\varphi^{\mp\mathcal{H}}_{\epsilon}(x)\;,

(4.32)

in which
\varphi^{\pm\mathcal{I}}_{\epsilon}
are postive/negative energy functions on
\mathcal{I}^{+}
while
\varphi_{\epsilon}^{\mp\mathcal{H}}
are negative/positive energy function on the horizon. We have not evaluated these functions for all arguments, nor should be need to, but we should verify that our
\varphi_{\epsilon}^{-\rm F}(x)
is indeed a valid Feynman mode. This we do by checking against the key properties discussed at the end of Sec.
3
below (
3.10
).

First, in the past,
\varphi^{-\text{F}}_{\epsilon}(x)
takes the form given in (
4.31
), the
v
-dependence of which closely resembles the
x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}
-dependence of the tunnelling wavefunction
\varphi_{p}(x)
in the Schwinger case. As for that example, see (
2.33
)), we find that
\varphi^{\pm\text{F}}_{\epsilon}(x)
is a positive/negative energy mode as
x
approaches
\mathcal{I}^{-}
. Next, to analyse the behaviour of
\varphi^{\pm\text{F}}_{\epsilon}(x)
near
\mathcal{I}^{+}\cup\mathcal{H}
, observe that the global form given in (
4.32
) makes it clear that the tunnelling wavefunction asymptotes to the free spherical out-mode near
\mathcal{I}^{+}
. Near the horizon, however, it asymptotes to a negative-energy horizon mode. This establishes that
\varphi^{\pm\text{F}}_{\epsilon}(x)
is indeed a Feynman mode.

We can now identify the amplitude for pair creation, which we identify with

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{0\rightarrow 2}(\epsilon,\epsilon^{\prime})\equiv\frac{\braket{{\rm in}|a_{\mathcal{H}}^{\epsilon^{\prime}}\,a_{\mathcal{I}}^{\epsilon}\mathcal{S}|{\rm in}}}{\braket{{\rm in}|\mathcal{S}|{\rm in}}}

(4.33)

where
a_{\mathcal{H}}^{\epsilon}
and
a_{\mathcal{I}}^{\epsilon}
are annihilation operators associated to the modes
\varphi_{\epsilon}^{\mathcal{I}}
and
\varphi_{\epsilon}^{\mathcal{H}}
respectively. The amplitude can now be read off from (
3.10
) and (
4.32
) as, up to a phase,

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{0\rightarrow 2}(\epsilon,\epsilon^{\prime})=2\pi{\delta}(\epsilon-\epsilon^{\prime})\,e^{-4\pi GM\epsilon}\;,

(4.34)

which exhibits both the expected conservation of energy and exponential dependence on the gravitational coupling.

Having drawn parallels with the Schwinger effect throughout this derivation of the amplitude for Hawking radiation, we finish by highlighting one notable difference between the gauge and gravitational results.
The difference lies in the nature of the worldlines associated with the tunneling processes.

Recall that in the Schwinger effect, the wavefunction in the classically forbidden region
x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}>x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}_{h}
is effectively described by a
complex
instanton (which takes the form of two real trajectories connected by a complex part). The analogous contribution to the Feynman wavefunction for Hawking radiation at
v>v_{0}
is, in contrast, described by a
real
radial null geodesic propagating backward in time.

In both scenarios the semiclassical exponential suppression associated with tunneling arises from the imaginary part of the classical action, although the details again differ. In the Schwinger case, the imaginary contribution stems from the term
(p_{\perp}^{2}+m^{2})T
in the worldline action. In the Hawking case, on the other hand, the imaginary part originates from the boundary term
\epsilon U
which becomes imaginary even though the saddle-point worldline itself remains real. Had we formulated the path integral in a coordinate system adapted to observers outside the horizon, using, say,
outgoing
Eddington-Finkelstein coordinates, the saddle-point radial null geodesic contributing to the tunneling process would naturally arise as a complex solution to the geodesic equation, since it would extend beyond the domain of the coordinate chart. A more covariant perspective is to observe that the boundary term possesses a branch cut across the horizon, hence why real worldlines crossing the horizon can yield imaginary parts. (In the Schwinger case the branch structure only appears after evaluating the
T
-integral.)

5
Conclusions

Classically, the dynamics of a charged particle in a constant electric field and those of a massless particle in a black hole spacetime are both influenced by the presence of an (in the former case effective) horizon. Quantum mechanically, there is tunneling at this horizon, reflecting the underlying process of particle creation.

We have applied the Lorentzian worldline path integral approach to describe particle creation via the Schwinger effect, and Hawking radiation
at the semiclassical level. Our approach was based on a worldline construction of solutions of the appropriate wave equation for a particle in an electric field or in a Vaidya (collapse) spacetime.

The Schwinger effect is nearly fully analytically tractable,
but a general analysis of the solutions of background-coupled wave equations shows that scattering amplitudes, in both gravity and gauge theory, are encoded in their asymptotic behaviour. (Specifically, the asymptotic forms of certain solutions correspond to on-shell Fourier transforms of the amplitudes.) Having only to evaluate our wavefunctions in asymptotic regions, and being guided by results from the Schwinger effect, allowed us to extract the pair-creation amplitude associated with Hawking radiation from
a Lorentzian worldline path integral.

In both cases, the boundary conditions on the worldlines contributing to the wavefunction admit a simple interpretation: one end satisfies a Dirichlet condition, aligning the worldline coordinate with the argument of the wavefunction, while the other end imposes a condition on the worldline’s energy-momentum, matching that of the wavefunction. The latter condition can be implemented by supplementing the worldline action with an appropriate boundary term. At the semiclassical level, our calculations reduced to the evaluation of the worldline action on classical trajectories satisfying these boundary conditions.

When the wavefunction is evaluated in classically forbidden regions, the worldline action acquires an imaginary part, giving rise to an exponentially suppressed factor akin to tunneling in quantum mechanics. Interestingly, while the relevant worldline trajectories are complex in the Schwinger effect, they remain real in the case of Hawking radiation, despite the action acquiring an imaginary part.

The horizon present already in the classical theory manifests as a branch cut in the wavefunction. We showed that the worldline formalism naturally bridges these classical and quantum features in both the Schwinger and Hawking cases. The wavefunctions in these two cases exhibit similar non-analytic behaviour (near critical values of their arguments associated with the horizons), but this arises in distinct ways in our analysis. In the Schwinger case, the branch cut emerges from the proper-time integral, whereas in the Hawking case, it can be traced back to the branch cut structure of the boundary term in the worldline action.

Our analysis opens up several directions for further research.
Our treatment of Hawking radiation relied on a semiclassical approximation; it would be interesting to see if our results could be derived from a more rigorous formulation of the worldline path integral in bounded spacetimes, or how exact solutions to the wave equation in Schwarzschild spacetime, discussed in
[
118
]
, emerge from the worldline formulation.
For the Schwinger effect, we showed that the complex worldline instanton responsible for tunneling effectively represents a family of spacelike worldlines contributing to the wavefunction.
An interesting question is whether a similar structure exists in the Hawking case — is there a set of real worldlines with real action can capture the tunneling behavior of the Feynman mode?
One avenue worth exploring is the potential role of non-radial geodesics in the computation of fixed-angular-momentum modes, and whether they provide the appropriate description.
Finally, another possible extension of our results is to cosmological pair creation, with de Sitter spacetime serving as an obvious starting point, due to the presence of a cosmological horizon. For very recent work on this topic see
[
119
]
.

Appendix A
Asymptotics and direct worldline computation of amplitudes in constant electric fields

One purpose of this appendix is to show that particle scattering and pair production amplitudes in a constant background are supported on spacetime region outside, respectively beyond, a classical horizon. In addition, however, this section also presents a direct computation of scattering amplitudes in a constant external background, employing the worldline path integral approach. In particular, we demonstrate how these amplitudes can be derived without any recourse to properties of complicated parabolic cylinder functions that commonly appear in treatments of this system.

In terms of in/out modes and the propagator
G(x,y)
, the pair creation amplitude has the LSZ expression
[
120
]

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{0\rightarrow 2}(q,p)

\displaystyle=\lim_{t_{b}=t_{a}\rightarrow\infty}\int\!\mathrm{d}^{3}\mathbf{y}\mathrm{d}^{3}\mathbf{x}\,\phi^{\dagger\rm out}_{q}(t_{a},\mathbf{x}){\overset{\leftrightarrow}{\partial}}_{t_{a}}G(x,y){\overset{\leftrightarrow}{\partial}}_{t_{b}}\phi^{\dagger\rm out}_{p}(t_{b},\mathbf{y})\,,

(A.1)

while the 1

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{1\rightarrow 1}(p\rightarrow q)

\displaystyle=\lim_{t_{b}=-t_{a}\rightarrow\infty}\int\!\mathrm{d}^{3}\mathbf{y}\mathrm{d}^{3}\mathbf{x}\,\phi^{\dagger\rm out}_{q}(t_{a},\mathbf{x}){\overset{\leftrightarrow}{\partial}}_{t_{a}}G(x,y){\overset{\leftrightarrow}{\partial}}_{t_{b}}\phi^{\rm in}_{p}(t_{b},\mathbf{y})\,,

(A.2)

both being naturally expressed in terms of ‘instant’ time
x^{0}=t
. We therefore switch here to the commonly-used time-dependent gauge
eA_{\mu}=(0,0,0,eEt)
(in Cartesian coordinates). Note that we only need the in/out modes in the large-time limit; these asymptotic solutions to the Klein-Gordon equation are easily found to be

\displaystyle\phi^{\rm in}_{p}(x)

\displaystyle\sim\frac{e^{ieEt^{2}}}{\sqrt{-eEt}}e^{-ip_{j}x^{j}}\qquad;\qquad t\rightarrow-\infty

(A.3)

\displaystyle\phi^{\rm out}_{p}(x)

\displaystyle\sim\frac{e^{-ieEt^{2}}}{\sqrt{eEt}}e^{-ip_{j}x^{j}}\qquad;\qquad t\rightarrow\infty

As a result, the amplitudes (

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\pm}

\displaystyle=\lim_{t_{a}\rightarrow\infty}\int\!\mathrm{d}^{3}\mathbf{x}\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\mathrm{d}T\,\mathcal{F}_{\pm}(T)\int^{z(T)=(t_{a},\mathbf{x})}_{z^{0}(0)=\pm t_{a}}\mathcal{D}[z]\,e^{iS_{\pm}}\,,

in which the relevant action is

with

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\pm}

\displaystyle=\lim_{t_{a}\rightarrow\infty}\int\!\mathrm{d}^{3}\mathbf{x}\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\mathrm{d}T\,\left(\frac{eE}{2\pi i\sinh(2eET)}\right)^{1/2}\mathcal{F}_{\pm}(T)\,e^{iS_{\pm}(z_{\text{cl}})}\;,

(A.8)

in which

\displaystyle S_{\pm}(z_{\text{cl}})=-(p_{\perp}^{2}+m^{2})T+(q\pm p)_{i}x^{i}-eE\,\bar{t}_{a}^{2}\tanh^{\pm 1}(eET)\;,

(A.9)

is indeed the classical action evaluated on the saddle point worldline satisyfing the boundary conditions

\displaystyle z^{\mu}(T)

\displaystyle=(t_{a},\mathbf{x})\,,

(A.10)

\displaystyle z^{0}(0)

\displaystyle=\pm t_{a}\quad;\quad\frac{1}{2}\dot{z}^{3}(0)-eEz^{0}(0)=\pm p^{3}\quad;\quad\frac{1}{2}\dot{z}^{\perp}(0)=\pm p^{\perp}\,.

(A.11)

From here it is notationally simpler to focus on the amplitudes separately.

A.1
The 0-to-2 amplitude

The explicit form of the saddle point worldline in the pair production amplitude is the same as that found for the computation of the tunnelling wavefunction in the text, up to some transformation between integration constants. Specifically, and reverting to lightfront coordinates, we have here

\displaystyle{z}_{\rm cl}^{-}(\tau)

\displaystyle=\hat{x}_{h}^{-}+\left({x}^{-}-\hat{x}^{-}_{h}\right)e^{2eE(T-\tau)}\;,\qquad{z}_{\rm cl}^{+}(\tau)={x}^{+}+\left(\frac{e^{2eE\tau}-e^{2eET}}{eE}\right)\hat{p}_{-}\,,

in which, note,
{x}^{\pm}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(t_{a}\pm x^{3})
, while we define
\hat{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle-}_{h}
and

\displaystyle{x}^{-}-\hat{x}_{h}^{-}=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{eE}\frac{(eEt_{a}-p_{3})}{1+e^{2eET}}=\frac{\hat{p}_{-}}{eE}\,.

Since

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{0\rightarrow 2}(q,p)

The 1-to-1 amplitude

Appendix B
Direct worldline computation of the semiclassical Hawking radiation amplitude

Paralleling the discussion in the previous appendix, we outline here a direct computation of the Hawking pair creation amplitude in the worldline formalism. This will make the connection with the Schwinger effect even more transparent.

The natural analogue of (
A.5
), i.e. the amplitude for Hawking radiation, is

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{0\rightarrow 2}(\epsilon,\epsilon^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}dT\mathcal{F}(T)\int_{\mathcal{E}(\tau_{i})=\epsilon^{\prime}}^{\mathcal{E}(\tau_{f})=\epsilon}\mathcal{D}[z]\exp\left[iS\right]\,,

(B.1)

where ‘
\mathcal{E}(\tau_{f})=\epsilon
’ is shorthand for the conditions

\dot{R}(\tau_{f})=2\epsilon\qquad\text{and}\qquad\dot{U}(\tau_{f})=0\;,

(B.2)

(analogously
\mathcal{E}(\tau_{i})=\epsilon^{\prime}
) which fix the energy, while
\{\tau_{i},\tau_{f}\}
are appropriate asymptotic values of the affine parameter
\tau
. In the semiclassical limit, where we take both the worldline and proper-time integrals in their saddle-point approximations, the amplitude becomes

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{0\rightarrow 2}(\epsilon,\epsilon^{\prime})\sim\exp\left[i\bar{S}_{\rm WL}\right]\Bigg\rvert_{z\rightarrow\bar{z}_{cl}}\;,

(B.3)

where
\bar{z}_{cl}
is the radial geodesic satisfying the boundary conditions (
B.1
), which in turn are implemented by adding appropriate boundary terms to the action as in the text, thus

\displaystyle\bar{S}_{\rm WL}=-\int_{\tau_{i}}^{\tau_{f}}\frac{1}{4}g_{\mu\nu}(z)\dot{z}^{\mu}\dot{z}^{\nu}d\tau+\epsilon U(\tau_{f})-\epsilon^{\prime}U(\tau_{i})\;.

(B.4)

Just as in our analysis of the wavefunction
\varphi^{\mathcal{I}}(x)
in Sec.

, the action receives contributions only from these boundary terms, which depend only on the asymptotic values of
U
.

In order to motivate the worldline instanton corresponding to the above boundary condition, we first rewrite the asymptotic form of the geodesic solution in (
4.8
) as follows:

\displaystyle V(\lambda)=4GM\left(e^{\frac{\lambda}{4GM}}+1\right)+\lambda+u\quad;\quad R(\lambda)=2GM\left(e^{\frac{\lambda}{4GM}}+1\right)\;,

(B.5)

in which we have defined a non-affine parameter
\lambda
by
\tau\equiv\frac{GM}{\epsilon}e^{\frac{\lambda(\tau)}{4GM}}
. Note the striking similarity between

and the instanton

appearing in the Schwinger effect, (

such that it runs from

\epsilon=\epsilon^{\prime}
, and its asymptotic

\displaystyle U(\tau_{f})-U(\tau_{i})=u-(u-4GM\pi i)=4\pi GMi\;.

(B.6)

Thus we recover the amplitude

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{0\rightarrow 2}(\epsilon,\epsilon^{\prime})\propto\delta(\epsilon-\epsilon^{\prime})e^{-4\pi GM\epsilon}\,,

(B.7)

which we previously read off from the Feynman modes. The delta function arises because the instanton exists when
\epsilon=\epsilon^{\prime}
.
