Smart contracts and homomorphic encryption for private P2P energy trading and demand response on blockchain
Authors: Dan Mitrea, Liana Toderean, Tudor Cioara, Ionut Anghel, Marcel Antal
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractBlockchain technology offers great value in terms of decentralization, data integrity, transparency, and traceability, however the transactional data is public, and accessible raising concerns about violating privacy regulations. For example, in the peer-to-peer energy trading and demand response use cases, the data stored in blockchain may allow a third party to infer the load profiles or even identify the behind the meter assets. In this paper, we employ homomorphic techniques to encrypt the energy transactional data stored on the blockchain allowing the smart contracts functions responsible for implementing the business logic of the energy flexibility trading and settlement to perform computations on encrypted data. As computations on smart contracts and public blockchains can be expensive, we have used the lighter version of the Partial Homomorphic Encryption scheme to obfuscate the energy data. To ensure the validity of the smart contracts' functions executed on encrypted data, we leverage on the consensus mechanism of the blockchain network, thus ensuring computation correctness. The solution was validated considering a micro-grid with 12 prosumers that trade their flexibility peer-to-peer (P2P). The results demonstrate the feasibility of maintaining encrypted energy data on the blockchain, executing smart contract functions on encrypted data, and preserving the privacy of computations. As anticipated, the trade-off for better privacy is the gas consumption overhead of the smart contracts’ functions which is higher compared to the non-encrypted case, depending on the length of the public-private keys pair. Nonetheless, our solution exhibits consistent execution times for smart contracts, making it suitable for private networks where gas costs are of minimal concern.Keywords: Blockchain, Privacy, Smart contracts, Encrypted data, P2P energy trading, Demand response

Introduction

Decentralization is an important factor in facilitating the green energy transition. However, as renewable energy sources are integrated at the edge of the smart grid, their volatile and intermittent nature generates technical challenges thus a shift in smart grid control becomes imperative. Unlike the control in the conventional grid, which adjusts the energy production to meet demand, in the smart grid, the energy demand is aligned with production by utilizing flexibility. The peer-to-peer (P2P) energy trading and demand response (DR) programs are good options for achieving decentralized control of the demand side [1]. They transform the energy prosumers into flexible assets and active participants to the management of local energy systems [2], motivating them to change their energy consumption patterns by using their flexibility to match a specific demand level and integrate larger shares of renewable energy [3]. In this context, the blockchain is a promising technology for smart grid decentralized control, allowing for implementing a distributed ledger of energy transactions over a P2P network that ensures data integrity, transaction traceability, and transparency [4]. On blockchain platforms, the prosumers can trade their energy in a decentralized manner without an intermediary, participate in demand response programs [5], or aggregate in virtual power plants [6,7] to address energy market barriers [73].

However, the advantage of the blockchain for smart grid control, the transactional ledger distribution to peer nodes, is also one of its weaknesses, as every network participant may access the data, raising concerns about violating privacy regulations [8,69]. The transactions on the distributed ledger are public and accessible, holding data related to energy consumption, prices, etc. Even if such data is associated with an anonymous account, machine learning techniques can extract relevant knowledge and private information, such as consumer load profiles [9]. Furthermore, techniques such as Non-Intrusive Load Monitoring (NILM) [10] can even infer the household appliances behind the meter, while other the citizens' behaviour from the consumption trends [11]. Asymmetric encryption algorithms can assure data privacy by encrypting it, however it might result in reduced traceability, which is a significant aspect in smart grid management solutions. The blockchain not only keeps track of energy data, but also the business logic linked with smart contracts for P2P energy trading and DR programs thus encrypting all this data is useless since the contracts won't be able to access and process it [13]. Novel mechanisms are required to allow blockchain data to be encrypted and to run smart contracts functions on it, handling operations such as comparison, addition, or multiplication.

In this paper, we propose a solution based on integrating homomorphic encryption to obfuscate the energy transactional data stored on the blockchain and smart contracts implementing the business logic of the P2P energy flexibility trading and DR settlement to support computations on encrypted data. Homomorphic encryption [12] techniques are a specific type of cryptographic algorithms that allow computations on data that is encrypted, without revealing the secret to the party performing the computation. The Partial Homomorphic Encryption (PHE) schemes, allows for executing either addition (e.g., Benaloh cryptosystem [14], Pallier cryptosystem [15]) or multiplication (e.g., RSA cryptosystem [16], ElGamal cryptosystem [17]) on encrypted data, and Fully Homomorphic Encryption (FHE) schemes based on lattice cryptography [18] allows for executing arbitrary operations on encrypted data. FHE algorithms are highly complex and impractical for implementation in smart contracts due to computational overhead and high gas consumption (i.e., a measure of computational effort on the Ethereum blockchain used to calculate transaction fees). Therefore, in our solution, we use a simpler PHE scheme that supports the few basic operations that are sufficient for implementing flexibility marketplace and DR settlement smart contracts while ensuring energy data privacy and integrity. However, homomorphic encryption used alone has a major drawback: one cannot validate that the computations on encrypted data are correct without verifying them on plain-text data, making the initial encryption useless. We address it by using the built-in consensus mechanism of the blockchain network to validate the correctness of computations performed on encrypted data. Because smart contract functions are validated through consensus, all operations on encrypted data is guaranteed to be correct, justifying the synergy between blockchain and homomorphic encryption to ensure data privacy, integrity, and computation correctness.

Related work

Authentication and anonymization mechanisms are proposed to ensure the energy data privacy by preventing attackers from impersonating honest participants [[19], [20], [21], [22]]. They rely on off-chain mutual authentication through a central, autonomous entity, which will hide the real identity of each prosumer by generating aliases based on encryption of address, a random UUID, or any other similar technique. Likewise, prosumers might encrypt their identification data on the blockchain and use coin mixing services such as Mixcoin [23] or Coinshuffle [24] to avoid data leakage. Solutions may combine multi-signatures and anonymous encrypted messaging to enable privacy-preserving energy trading and anonymous negotiation of prices over blockchain infrastructures [25].

Various cryptographic techniques are employed to ensure the privacy of energy data in blockchain infrastructures [[26], [27], [28]]. Commitment schemes rely on cryptography to allow one to commit a value and at the same time, keep it hidden from others. The committed value can be revealed later, and a party cannot change a value after they have committed to it [27]. Such schemes are used in various energy-related use cases such as decentralized energy trading or sharing of energy assets. Wang et al. [26] propose the use of Pedersen's commitment to assuring a privacy-preserving sharing of energy storage without requiring a trusted third party in a blockchain-based implementation. Secure multiparty computation enables prosumers to trade their excess electricity in a privacy-preserving manner [29]. Hiding the order quantities and prices from other participants is important as from them, an attacker may deduce sensitive information [28]. Others propose secure digital evidence solutions for blockchain combined with loose coupling schemes where the secret information is stored in trusted storage and its identifier in the blockchain [72]. Zero Knowledge Proofs (ZKPs) are one of the most used commitment schemes in energy blockchain, allowing to verify certain properties of that data, without decrypting it [30]. A peer can prove a statement without disclosing any other information regarding its private data [31]. Interactive ZKPs require interaction between the individual proving their knowledge and the entity validating the proof. Several implementations of this technology have been proposed, including zk-SNARK [32] and its more scalable version zk-STARK [33] with bigger-sized proofs generated quicker and faster, consuming less gas, BuletProof [34], Zerocash [35], and Zether [36] used to enable the confidential transfer of cryptocurrency. In blocking-enabled smart grid scenarios, the prosumers keep their energy data private and only validate it on the blockchain using ZKPs generated by the prosumer itself [37,38] and further used to validate potential deviations or malicious data. Others use ZKPs to ensure the transparency and privacy needed for blockchain adoption for energy labelling and trading green certificates [39] or for implementing energy aggregation schemes [40].

Homomorphic encryption techniques are relevant for smart grid management as they allow to perform computations on encrypted data without first decrypting it [41,42]. They are used to address privacy problems in smart grids, such as blockchain integration with IoT energy metering devices [43] or virtual aggregation of smart meter data to obtain average power usage in a neighbourhood [44]. In the energy trading context, the techniques are useful for adding encrypted order quantities and verifying the trading process [45]. Wang et al. [46] use homomorphic encryption with identity-based signature to assure anonymity in trading energy using off-chain cloud storage and the on-chain transaction. In Ref. [71], the authors propose a blockchain-based privacy-preserving scheme that can offer secure access through attribute-based encryption and transmission and verification of blockchain data through homomorphic encryption. Cheon-Kim-Kim-Song homomorphic encryption scheme is used for data privacy on blockchain networks [75]. Homomorphic encryption is integrated with multi-party computation methods [47,48] for smart grid peers who want to compute a function over multiple variables while keeping their values private. On-chain data protection methods based on homomorphic encryption and secure multi-party computation are researched to sort and clear encrypted orders in distributed energy trading platforms [74]. Mu et al. [49] proposed a circuit-based mechanism for comparing the price without revealing it to the participants using secure multi-party computation. Buyers generate the circuit, and the sellers perform the computation by applying logical circuits and sharing the result. The Paillier Cryptosystem is integrated with secure multi-party computation in Ref. [50] to enable entities to encrypt data and perform computations over encrypted information without sharing information regionally to reach consensus. Consensus on multiple channels without revealing private data can be achieved using homomorphic encryption and a multi-channel architecture [51] while homomorphic encryption can provide confidentiality of energy data transmission from smart meters, energy computation, and anonymous aggregation in smart grids [52].

Finally functional encryption and ring signatures have been studied and integrated with blockchain infrastructures. Ring signatures allows a member to sign a message, and any other member can decrypt it using a set of keys, being computationally unfeasible to determine which of the set's member keys was used to produce the signature [53]. The trade-off for integration with the blockchain is the increased transaction size due to the ring signatures. The message is signed anonymously using a signature set bringing correctness, anonymity, and unforgeability as advantages to smart grid and edge-based scenarios [54,55]. Entities participating in a P2P energy market could form a ring signature and ensure privacy of prosumers identities [56]. A more complex solution is multi-authority traceable ring signature [57] for distributed settings where there are multiple master entities generating keys, and an auditing node can distinguish an entity if it is a member of the ring signature set or not. The functional encryption is a generalization of the public/private key encryption method being a good fit for blockchain and energy grid scenarios [58,59]. It guarantees that the information is encrypted and protected in blockchain nodes and verifiable through smart contracts [58]. Encoding algorithms are proposed for energy bids and offers to reduce the computation, making it feasible for blockchain integration [59]. The Function Hiding Inner Product encryption has been used to encrypt data for an energy market, such as order amounts and prices [60,61]. Moreover, it can infer properties or compare two encoded numbers, a useful feature in energy markets [62]. By applying functional encryption, the energy data can be compared without knowing their decrypted values, and the result can be used for other functions on the market.

Our solution progresses the state of the art by integrating homomorphic encryption with blockchain to enable the on-chain privacy of energy transactions. We define smart contracts to work on encrypted data supporting P2P energy trading and decentralized DR programs. This type of asymmetric encryption allows the smart contracts to perform functions on encrypted data, and the obtained result, if it were to be decrypted using the private key, would result in the same value as if it were to apply the function on plain values. To maintain the computational overhead and gas consumption as low as possible, we use a simpler PHE scheme that offers operational support for the smart contracts functions while ensuring energy data privacy and integrity. To verify the validity of the functions' computations on encrypted data, we leverage on the consensus mechanism of the blockchain network, as they are implemented in smart contracts, thus ensuring computation correctness.

Homomorphic encryption for P2P trading and DR

The integration of homomorphic encryption for on chain privacy is done using a blockchain-based local flexibility market that enables the P2P trading of day-ahead flexibility among prosumers. The design of the core market functionality was presented in one of our previous papers [63]. Smart contracts are defined to manage decentralized DR programs in which the signals for changing energy-related behaviour are determined as results of self-assessment and P2P energy flexibility trading.

The Data-Acquisition and Storage module features an MQ Telemetry Transport (MQTT) [65] listener that collects monitored energy data from topics associated with devices. Smart contracts on encrypted data

We define an encrypted flexibility token to digitize and encrypt the energy flexibility provided by the energy assets of the smart grid. We have used the ERC721 to represent the energy flexibility as non-fungible token and ERC20 to account them in smart contracts and make payments. In this paper we emphasis the integration of homomorphic encryption, but more details about other features of this approach can be found in our previous papers [63,64].

P2P flexibility trading

The ERC721 based flexibility token handles functions on tokenized infrastructure with encrypted data. Adaptation have been made to ERC721 standard. We are still using the token index, which represents the current index of the token to be minted, and the last identification number which exists. But we have added use some auxiliary data structures for knowing which tokens are owned by who, how many tokens an address has, and what is the encrypted amount associated with a specific token. To keep the energy data private, we cannot mint ERC721 tokens proportional to the amount of energy transferred, because it would give out the amounts involved in trading. Thus, we have defined functions to encrypt the tokens mint by each energy asset and then used in the flexibility exchanges (see Algorithm 3).

The mintEncrypted function generates a new token index to store an encrypted number of tokens. We are using an internal function call that checks if the address that will own these tokens is legit and that the tokenID does not already exist. Then the energy asset owner of the tokens is attributed to the specific data structure, and the mapping which holds the token number to a specific address is updated with the encrypted amount.

For quantification of owned tokens in smart contracts, the standard ERC20 uses a mapping of addresses and determines the number of tokens owned without making any differences among individual tokens (fungible assets). The ERC20 token is used to deal payments in our solution. As each owner may hold more than one non-fungible energy tokens, we have identified tokens metadata to differentiate between them. We have defined a map-based structure that associates to each address several tokens. As a result, the energy token marked by the same metadata, once transferred, could have its quantity split among more than one flexibility assets that are buying.

The P2P flexibility trading on the market must be private. The tokens used for market orders and transactions are locked and transferred from one market participant to another using encrypted amounts. The orders are submitted at the beginning of the market session. When registering a trade, the tokens locked by the buyers corresponding to the commitment are transferred to the seller. The positive or negative values of the trade energy quantity are encrypted before. The commitment update step uses encrypted values. After matching the flexibility orders, pairs of public and private keys are generated for encryption and shared by the matched energy assets. The trade quantity obtained through matching is encrypted using the public key generated for that party before registering trade transaction.

The EncryptedLERC20 smart contract is responsible to hold the encrypted version of the flexibility tokens locked by an energy flexibility asset. The lock function is automatically done during the submission of a flexibility bid or offer to the marketplace by the corresponding smart contract associated with the asset. After the matching of flexibility orders in transactions, the pair of public and private keys are available, and the locks can be transferred during settlement. An _encryptedLocked map (see Algorithm 4) is defined to store for each unlocking address an encrypted lock containing the encrypted amount according to the submitted flexibility order, the coefficient which indicates the number of functions applied on the encrypted value (needed for validation purposes) and a Boolean variable indicating if the map entry is active.

DR programs settlement

The settlement process aims to monitor the delivery of energy, check the compliance with the values committed in flexibility transactions and transfer the tokens into the wallets of the assets involved. The monitored values are encrypted with the public key KeyFEpublic when they are collected and stored in the Mongo database. The hourly sum of the monitored values can be computed over the encrypted values using the public key due to the homomorphic encryption algorithm (see Fig. 4).

Aggregating and encrypting the monitored energy values.

The encrypted sum of the monitored energy values is determined as a product on the encrypted monitored values. Therefore, the hourly value is computed by multiplying the encrypted monitored values from the current hour and is stored on blockchain through the smart contract associated with the flexibility asset (see Algorithm 6) using a MonitoredValue event (line 6). A comparison between the hourly value and the value of the flexibility transaction commitments is needed to establish the settlement payment, but the encrypted values are not comparable. Thus, two differences are computed on encrypted values (lines 7–8). An external request is made to obtain the comparison result on decrypted values (line 9), and the settlement payment is computed (line 10).

We have also implemented a method to transfer tokens from the seller to the buyer (see Algorithm 8). Since we cannot perform subtraction functions with homomorphic properties, we pass as arguments not only the encrypted quantity, but also the encrypted amount for the negative of the quantity. As the trades can result between a buyer and multiple sellers, we must know exactly the payments associated with each trade. We will generate a new token ID for each new owned number associated with a transfer, and we will know the exact numbers transferred by an entity to multiple others. If we kept one ID and subtract/add there, we would have known only the total amounts, not the exact bits. So, each transfer generates a new token ID. We decrypt the balance of the owner's token ID that initiates the transfer, with the transferred amount, by summing the previous value with the negative of the transfer amount. Then, the encrypted transfer amount will be associated with a new token index assigned to the recipient.

In the encrypted token contract only the encrypted number of tokens for each locking address is kept, thus the token receiver is not known. The transfer is made through the address of the tokens' owner (tokens_owner) which is a manager account (lines 8–10). If the decrypted amount is larger than the initial locked number of tokens, then this locking address received tokens and the difference is transferred from the address of the tokens’ owner. The difference from the transfer method without encryption is that if some address receives tokens trough an encrypted transfer, the tokens are locked until the unlockFromEncrypted method is called.

Evaluation results

In this section, we evaluate the feasibility of our solution in the context of P2P flexibility trading and decentralized demand response. The objective is to check if the smart contracts on encrypted data and PHE functions integrated with the blockchain can support the submission of bids and offers, minting tokens, flexibility transacting, and settlement of DR.

We have used the energy data of prosumers from a microgrid. The monitored energy data is processed and aggregated hourly and forecasting algorithms are used to predict the day ahead flexibility [70]. The estimations are used to support the bids and offers submission process in the market session. In our evaluation, we have considered four prosumers as buyers of flexibility and eight prosumers acting as sellers (see Fig. 5). The buyers feature more local renewable generation than consumption, therefore they aim to sell the surplus of energy, while the sellers of flexibility are aiming to shift their flexibility loads to match the renewable generation.

Evaluation microgrid and blockchain network overlay.

The prosumers participate to the market session by submitting day-ahead orders for the energy providing estimates for each of the 24 h in the next day (see Fig. 6 for prosumers profiles). Initially, these orders are placed onto the blockchain in an unencrypted form and then the P2P trades generated between two prosumers are encrypted before being stored on the blockchain.

The Encrypted ERC721 smart contract is used to mint non-Fungible tokens representing the sellers’ traded quantities. These tokens symbolize a commitment of the seller to deliver a certain amount of energy flexibility as per the trade agreement. These tokens are transferred to the buyers during settlement based on the actual energy delivery. Each entity must fulfil its commitment, which means buyers must pay for the energy they have agreed to receive, and sellers must send non-fungible tokens equivalent to the amount of energy they promised to deliver the next day. Therefore, during DR settlement, the actual monitored energy values are compared to the committed values in transactions using the encrypted settlement logic. The encrypted committed values and the encrypted monitored values are stored for each hour of the market session in the FlexibilityAssetEncrypted smart contract. Encrypted commitments, monitored values and settlement payment of Prosumer1.

A buyer can be matched with one or more sellers in transactions. The commitment value is a sum of the encrypted transaction quantities for the specific hour. In the settlement phase, the encrypted monitored values are stored in the blockchain. The difference between the monitored and the commitment values is computed to establish the settlement payment that is proportional to the difference. In the case of buyers, if the actual energy consumed exceeds the initially agreed-upon commitment, then a payment computation is triggered. Only the prosumer part of the transaction has the private key required to decrypt the connected values, ensuring that the settlement and payment processes are done in a way that maintains the privacy of the involved parties.

Table 5 presents the encrypted number of locked tokens in different phases of the market session. In the place orders phase, quantity of tokens locked is equal to the orders energy amount multiplied by price (hour deposit established for the current market session). After the key pairs are generated for the prosumers matched in flexibility trades (highlighted in Table 5 with different colours), the locked tokens are encrypted. During the commitment and settlement phases, the transfers between prosumers are made using encrypted tokens. The buyers pay the energy to the matched sellers by transferring the locked tokens corresponding to the traded amount of each hour.

Discussion

The proposed solution has clear benefits on enabling the registration of encrypted energy transactions on the blockchain and providing smart contracts that run on encrypted data for P2P energy flexibility trading and DR settlement. However, one trade-off that needs to be analysed is the overhead brought by the integration of the homomorphic encryption with the blockchain and the smart contracts functions. Table 7 shows the total gas consumption of the P2P flexibility market in different phases for the whole trading scenario defined in Section 4 with and without homomorphic encryption.

As expected, using smart contract functions on encrypted data generates higher costs compared to scenarios where encryption is not employed and can be costly on public networks. However, the solution enforces the privacy because the energy transactions are stored encrypted on the blockchain, and the smart contracts functions executed on them can be verified for correctness. The smart contracts functions for placing flexibility orders and encrypted ERC721 minting are moderately intensive compared to other. In the latter case we only attribute the encrypted values to each energy seller address. The most expensive functions in terms of gas consumption are the trades registration on the chain and the DR settlement. Trade registration implies not only saving the trades in specific arrays, but also transferring encrypted ERC721 tokens equivalent to the traded amount, from sellers to buyers, and updating commitment data structures such that they can be later compared to the monitored values. The settlement computations also include the registration of energy data thus is the most expensive of all phases. It requires to compute both the sum and difference between the committed value and the monitored value and multiply them with constant represented by the trading price. API calls are used from smart contracts to decrypt both values with the prosumer's private key. Depending on which value is decrypted correctly, we know which value is bigger than the other, and enables the computation of penalties or rewards based on the relative sizes of the decrypted values.

One factor that influences the smart contracts functions execution on encrypted data and the gas consumption is the size of the public and private keys. We have run the same flexibility trading scenario twice halving the size of the keys pair making the encrypted numbers shorter and less complex, so all smart contracts functions involving them will be less expensive. The total gas consumption for all the functions executed in the two cases is shown in Table 8. Depending on the number of functions on encrypted numbers, compared to other functions in the respective methods, we have from 1.5 times less gas consumed each key variation, in the encrypted ERC721 mint, up to 1.73 times less gas consumed in the DR settlement phase.

In terms of function execution, the computational overhead brought by the homomorphic encryption integration is minimal, the execution time of individual operations being slightly higher than in the case when no encryption has been used.

Conclusions

In this paper, we have proposed a privacy-preserving energy blockchain by using homomorphic encryption to obfuscate the transactions stored on-chain and defining smart contracts that can make computations on encrypted data to implement the business logic of P2P energy flexibility trading and DR settlement. Because making computations on smart contracts and public blockchains can be expensive, we have used the lighter version of Partial Homomorphic Encryption scheme that offers data integrity and privacy. The smart contracts defined are performing functions on encrypted data, while the correctness is guaranteed by the consensus on blockchain state.

The solution was validated considering a micro-grid with 12 prosumers that can trade their flexibility in a P2P manner. The results show the feasibility of integrating homomorphic encryption with blockchain and smart contracts to make private the energy data stored on the chain and to support the implementation of flexibility market and DR settlement logic. Also, the smart contracts functions can run on encrypted data making their computation private.

The trade-off of the solution is the increased overhead brought by the functions on encrypted data in terms of gas consumption and transactional throughput. This is relevant for the public networks where the price of the gas can be high. The evaluation shows that the gas consumption depends on the length of the public private key used to encrypt data being 2 to 4 times higher than in the non-encrypted case. However, there are no high variations on the smart contracts’ execution time and the gas consumption values are smaller when distributed to individual peers. This make our solution more fit for private networks where the cost of the gas consumption price is not relevant and are more likely to be adopted in smart grid scenarios.
