Theory of enhanced-by-coincidence neural information transmission
Miguel Ibáñez-Berganza, Giulio Bondanelli, Stefano Panzeri

Abstract
The activity of neurons within brain circuits has been ubiquitously reported to be correlated. The impact of these correlations on brain function has been extensively investigated. Correlations can in principle increase or decrease the information that neural populations carry about sensory stimuli, but experiments in cortical areas have mostly reported information-limiting correlations, which decrease the information encoded in the population. However, a second stream of evidence suggests that temporal correlations between the spiking activity of different neurons may increase the impact of neural activity downstream, implying that temporal correlations affect both the encoding of information and its downstream readout. The principle of how encoding and readout combine are still unclear. Here, we consider a model of transmission of stimulus information encoded in pre-synaptic input spike trains with information-limiting time-correlations to the output firing of a post-synaptic biologicaly-plausible leaky integrate and fire (LIF) readout neuron. We derive an analytical solution of the model in the diffusion approximation, in which the encoding spiking activity is treated as a continuous-time stochastic variable. An ansatz based on a separation of timescales allows us compute the stimulus information transmitted to the readout over a broad range of parameters. Our analytical results reveal that, for sufficiently low input firing rates, large enough difference in input stimulus-specific activity, and moderately large input temporal correlations, the stimulus discriminability of the firing of the LIF readout neuron can be enhanced by the presence of input time correlations, despite they decrease the stimulus information encoded in its inputs.

Understanding the feasibility of such an enhanced-by-coincidence information transmission between pre- and post-synaptic activity requires models of neurons as physical systems that integrate both stimulus encoding and readout mechanisms. To understand how correlated input at the pre-synaptic stage enhance information transmission to the post-synaptic stage, we propose a biologically plausible Neural Encoding and Readout Model (NERM)
[
32
]
. The model consists of a population of pre-synaptic neurons encoding a binary stimulus, which subsequently drives a single post-synaptic readout neuron. The encoding neurons generate spike trains with a certain strength and time scales of correlation across neurons. These correlations are designed to be information-limiting, that is to decrease the information encoded in the input when the strength of correlation increases.
The readout neuron is modeled as a leaky integrate-and-fire (LIF) neuron and integrates afferent currents as a weighted sum of spike trains with a given membrane integration time constant.
Numerical solutions of this model reveal that the stimulus discriminability of the readout neuron (as quantified by the signal-to-noise ratio between the difference in stimulus-specific mean firing rates and its variance, SNR) can be, seemingly paradoxically, enhanced by stronger information-limiting correlations for sufficiently short readout time constants and sufficiently low input firing rate. In these conditions correlations amplify the stimulus information transmitted to the readout even when decreasing input information.

Our previous study left open important questions about the conditions needed for noise correlations to overcome their information-limiting impact at the readout stage. The study of the model was limited to only
N=2
input encoding neurons, while information transmission in the brain involves larger neural populations. Moreover, we did not provide an analytical understanding of the interplay between encoding and readout of information in neural populations. To overcome these issues and allow an understanding of these neural operations as a statistical physical system, here we develop an analytical framework to characterize the SNR enhancement effect beyond numerical simulations. This framework allows to study encoding in a population with an arbitrary number
N
of pre-synaptic input neurons with time-dependent correlations, and study how this information is transmitted to a readout LIF neuron. The ability to obtain solutions for arbitrary input population sizes allows us to study the interplay between the spatial dimension of population size (including the large
N
limit) and the time scales of input correlations and of readout integration.
Our approach employs a diffusion approximation, where the readout neuron membrane potential obeys a second-order stochastic differential equation. In this framework, the inter-spike interval (ISI) of the readout neuron corresponds to the first passage time of an
inertial
stochastic process. We derive analytical expressions for the mean and variance of the readout neuron firing rate, and its temporal correlation structure. Treating this problem within the theory of stochastic processes involves analyzing first-passage times of non-Markovian random walks, which typically requires solving coupled Fokker-Planck equations. By introducing a novel analytical approach —the
quenched-noise approximation
— we provide tractable expressions for the firing rate mean and variance under the assumption that the noise correlations timescale
{\tau_{\rm c}}
is much larger than the membrane time constant of the readout neuron
{\tau_{\rm m}}
. The quenched-noise approximation exploits classical expressions for the mean rate of the LIF neuron with white noise input
[
42
]
. Our results extend previous expressions for the mean rate of LIF neurons with colored noise input with
{\tau_{\rm m}}\ll{\tau_{\rm c}}

[
17
]
, which were recovered in the limit of weak correlation strength. Moreover, we are able to study analytically the opposite limit
{\tau_{\rm c}}\ll{\tau_{\rm m}}
using a Poisson approximation.
These results provide the necessary conditions for noise correlations to enhance the readout SNR. Notably, our analysis demonstrates that SNR enhancement can occur for a wide range of noise correlation timescales
{\tau_{\rm c}}
, regardless of whether they exceed or fall below the membrane time constant
{\tau_{\rm m}}
.

III.2
The quenched-noise approximation for the LIF neuron rate mean

In the following, we derive expressions for the mean and variance (hence, for the SNR, see Eq. (
4
)) of the readout neuron defined by Eq. (
5
) as a function of the parameters
{\mu_{\rm I}}^{(\pm)},{v_{\rm c}},{\tau_{\rm c}},w,{\tau_{\rm m}}
.

How to compute analytically the mean and variance of the firing rate of a LIF model with a coloured noise input has been extensively investigated. Approximate expressions for the mean
{r_{\rm R}}
have been derived in the
{\tau_{\rm c}}\ll{\tau_{\rm m}}
and
{\tau_{\rm c}}\gg{\tau_{\rm m}}
limits
[
10
,
16
,
17
,
19
,
42
,
45
]
. In particular, Moreno et al
[
17
]
derive an analytical approximation to the mean firing rate
{r_{\rm R}}
of the colored-input noise LIF neuron in the
{\tau_{\rm c}}\gg{\tau_{\rm m}}
limit (at first order in
{\tau_{\rm m}}/{\tau_{\rm c}}
), and under the further approximations of small
\alpha_{\rm M}:={\sigma_{\rm c}}^{2}/{\sigma_{\rm I}}^{2}
and small
{\mu_{\rm I}}/({\sigma_{\rm I}}^{2}+{\sigma_{\rm c}}^{2})^{1/2}
.

Here we present an approximated expression for
{r_{\rm R}}
in the
{\tau_{\rm c}}\gg{\tau_{\rm m}}
limit, in the
quenched-noise approximation
. The quenched-noise approximation generalizes the Moreno solution
[
17
]
to larger values of
\alpha_{\rm M}
. The approximation stands on a simple argument: whenever
{\tau_{\rm c}}\gg{\tau_{\rm m}}
,
in time windows of length
\ell
,
{\tau_{\rm m}}\ll\ell\ll{\tau_{\rm c}}
, the coloured noise
\xi(t)
is approximately constant and, consequently,
the rate of the LIF neuron behaves as that of a white-noise LIF neuron
with input current
I(t)=\tilde{\mu}+{\sigma_{\rm I}}\,\eta(t)
, where
\tilde{\mu}={\mu_{\rm I}}+{\sigma_{\rm c}}\,\xi
is an effectively constant drift. Under this assumption, the firing rate
{r_{\rm R}}
in time intervals of size
{T_{\rm c}}\gg{\tau_{\rm c}}
can be approximated as
the average of
{r_{\rm w}}(\tilde{\mu})
over the stationary distribution of values of
\tilde{\mu}\sim{\cal N}(\tilde{\mu};{\mu_{\rm I}},{v_{\rm c}})
with
{v_{\rm c}}={\sigma_{\rm c}}^{2}/(2{\tau_{\rm c}})
(see (Eq.
5b
)), where
{\cal N}(\cdot;m,v)
denotes the normal distribution with mean
m
and variance
v
. Thus, the readout firing rate in the quenched-noise approximation
{r_{\rm R}}({\mu_{\rm I}})\simeq{r_{\rm q}}({\mu_{\rm I}})
is given by:

\displaystyle{r_{\rm q}}({\mu_{\rm I}})

\displaystyle:=\left<{r_{\rm w}}(\tilde{\mu})\right>_{{\cal N}(\tilde{\mu};\mu_{I},{v_{\rm c}})}\qquad\text{(quenched-noise approximation for ${\tau_{\rm c}}\gg{\tau_{\rm m}}$)}

(9)

where
{r_{\rm w}}(\mu)
is the Siegert solution for the mean rate of a
white noise LIF neuron
(see Eq. (
42
) in Appendix
E
)
[
46
,
42
]
. In the saddle-point approximation for low enough
{v_{\rm c}}={\sigma_{\rm c}}^{2}/(2{\tau_{\rm c}})
, it can be proved (see Appendix
E
) that Eq. (
9
) takes the analytic form:

\displaystyle{r_{\rm q}}({\mu_{\rm I}})

\displaystyle={r_{\rm w}}({\mu_{\rm I}})\left[\frac{1}{1-{v_{\rm c}}g^{\prime}_{1}({\mu_{\rm I}})}\right]^{1/2}\exp\left(\frac{{v_{\rm c}}}{2}\frac{g_{1}^{2}({\mu_{\rm I}})}{1-{v_{\rm c}}g^{\prime}_{1}({\mu_{\rm I}})}\right),\qquad\text{${\tau_{\rm c}}\gg{\tau_{\rm m}}$, low ${v_{\rm c}}$}

(10)

where the function
g_{1}:={r_{\rm w}}^{\prime}/{r_{\rm w}}
and its derivative
g^{\prime}_{1}
with respect to
{\mu_{\rm I}}
depend on the white LIF neuron parameters only. Notice that, consistently with the quenched-noise hypothesis in Eq. (
9
), the emerging correction to the white noise case depends on the properties of the colored noise only through
{v_{\rm c}}
.

Interestingly, if we now expand
[1-{v_{\rm c}}g^{\prime}_{1}({\mu_{\rm I}})]^{-1/2}
in (Eq.
10
) to the first order in
\nu_{\rm W}=({\sigma_{\rm c}}^{2}{\tau_{\rm m}})/(\sigma_{I}^{2}{\tau_{\rm c}})
(W stands for Walter et al, who use the same perturbative parameter in reference
[
49
]
), and the exponential in (Eq.
10
) to the first order in
{\tau_{\rm m}}/{\tau_{\rm c}}
, we recover the Moreno et al expression for
{r_{\rm R}}
at first order in
{\tau_{\rm m}}/{\tau_{\rm c}}
in Eq. (
41
). We conclude that Eq. (
10
) is therefore a generalization of the Moreno solution for
{r_{\rm R}}
in the
{\tau_{\rm c}}\gg{\tau_{\rm m}}
limit
[
17
,
19
]
, expected to hold for larger values of
\alpha_{\rm M}
(Figs.
5
,
9
-A and Appendix
G
).

Comparing the Moreno and quenched-noise approximations with the numerical data in the regime
{\tau_{\rm c}}\gg{\tau_{\rm m}}
reveals that: (
i
) for low
{\sigma_{\rm c}}
both approximations
r_{\rm M}
and
{r_{\rm q}}
converge (see Eq. (
10
)) to the white-noise LIF activation function
{r_{\rm w}}
and for low enough
{\sigma_{\rm c}}
and hence
\alpha_{\rm M}
, both solutions
r_{\rm M}
and
{r_{\rm q}}
are consistent with each other (Fig.
5
-A red lines, Fig.
9
-A and Appendix
G
); (
ii
) for larger values of
{\sigma_{\rm c}}
the solutions differ, and the numerical estimation of
{r_{\rm R}}
(i.e.
\tilde{r_{\rm R}}
; see Appendix
C
), is
consistent only with the quenched-noise approximation
(Fig.
5
-B blue lines); (
iii
) to find full agreement between numerical simulations and
{r_{\rm q}}
, we need to apply the finite-Number of Integration Steps (NIS) correction (Fig.
5
-B, see Appendix
C
).

We stress that Eq. (
10
) is an analytical approximation of Eq. (
9
). As such, its validity limits are those of Eq. (
9
) (i.e.,
{\tau_{\rm c}}\gg{\tau_{\rm m}}
), plus the further condition that
{v_{\rm c}}
is low enough (see Appendix
E
). Throughout the article, with the exception of Appendix
F
, we always use our analytic expression for the rate mean, Eq. (
10
), and never the numerical evaluation of the integral in Eq. (
9
). As a matter of fact, our analytical expression for the rate mean is a good approximation, since the conditions for its derivation are satisfied in the range of parameters that we probe in our simulations. This is shown in Fig.
5
-B and in Appendix
E
. The same holds for the expressions of the rate variance in the quenched-noise approximation, that we show below in Sec.
III.4
. Finally, we probe, in a designated simulation in Appendix
F
for constant
{\tau_{\rm c}}
and increasing
{\sigma_{\rm c}}
(hence, increasing
{v_{\rm c}}
), the quenched noise approximation beyond the validity limits of Eq. (
10
). Since, in this simulation, it is
{\tau_{\rm c}}\gg{\tau_{\rm m}}
, we are within the validity limits of the quenched-noise approximation and, indeed, the readout firing rate is still well explained, net of the finite NIS effect described below, by Eq. (

III.3
Intuitive interpretation of the enhanced firing rate effect in the quenched-noise approximation

The quenched-noise approximation provides an intuitive interpretation of the enhancement of the firing rate in the presence of colored noise. Since
{r_{\rm q}}({\mu_{\rm I}})\simeq\left<{r_{\rm w}}({\tilde{\mu}})\right>_{\tilde{\mu}}
(Eq. (
9
)), we expect the introduction of a colored noise to increase the LIF firing rate,
{r_{\rm R}}({\mu_{\rm I}})>{r_{\rm w}}({\mu_{\rm I}})
, for those values of
{\mu_{\rm I}}
in which
{r_{\rm w}}
is convex, and vice-versa – since
\left<f(\mu)\right>_{\mu}>f(\left<\mu\right>_{\mu})
is the definition of convex function. Precisely, since
{v_{\rm c}}^{1/2}
is the standard deviation of
{\tilde{\mu}}
, we expect
{r_{\rm q}}({\mu_{\rm I}})>{r_{\rm w}}({\mu_{\rm I}})
to hold for values of
{\mu_{\rm I}}
and
{v_{\rm c}}
such that
{\mu_{\rm I}}
is lower, in units of
{v_{\rm c}}^{1/2}
, than the inflection point
\mu^{*}
of the white-noise LIF neuron (satisfying
{\rm d}^{2}{r_{\rm w}}(\mu)/{\rm d}\mu^{2}|_{\mu^{*}}=0
). Fig.
6
shows that this is the case for both the quenched-noise and the Moreno approximations.

The enhancement in firing rate therefore has a simple explanation in the large-correlation timescale regime. The input current signal
I(t)
fluctuates within a timescale
{\tau_{\rm c}}
. Although those fluctuations are symmetric around the mean
{\mu_{\rm I}}
, since the activation function of the white LIF model is non-linear,
and convex for low enough current
{\mu_{\rm I}}
,
the gain in firing rate for positive fluctuations,
{r_{\rm w}}({\mu_{\rm I}}+\delta)-{r_{\rm w}}({\mu_{\rm I}})
is larger than the loss for negative fluctuations
-{r_{\rm w}}({\mu_{\rm I}}-\delta)+{r_{\rm w}}({\mu_{\rm I}})
. Thus, for the firing rate to be enhanced by colored noise,
the LIF readout neuron must operate in a sufficiently low input current regime
(so that
{r_{\rm w}}({\mu_{\rm I}})
is convex, given the relevant parameters
{\sigma_{\rm I}},{\tau_{\rm m}}
, see Sec.
V
). This effect is known as
Jensen’s force

[
50
]
.

The rate variance
{v_{\rm R}}
of a LIF neuron with white+coloured noise input has been analytically computed only in the
{\tau_{\rm c}}\to 0
limit
[
12
]

{\tau_{\rm c}}\to\infty

[
21
]

[

18
]

[

[

[

1

. Here we derive an expression for the rate variance within the quenched-noise approximation.

We first provide an expression for the
temporal correlation function

{\sf C}(t):=\left<{\varrho}(t^{\prime}){\varrho}(t^{\prime}+t)\right>-\left<{\varrho}(t^{\prime})\right>\left<{\varrho}(t^{\prime}+t)\right>

\displaystyle{\rm Var}({\rm rate}):={v_{\rm R}}=\frac{{r_{\rm R}}}{{T_{\rm c}}}+\frac{2}{{T_{\rm c}}^{2}}\int_{0}^{{T_{\rm c}}}{\rm d}t^{\prime}(T-t^{\prime}){\sf C}_{-}(t^{\prime})

temporal correlations

\displaystyle{r_{\rm R}}{g_{\rm q}}^{(\text{lo-}\omega)}(t-t^{\prime})

IV
Noise correlations and SNR enhancement in the encoding-readout model

In this section, we study the behavior of the SNR, Eq. (
4
) as a function of the noise correlation stationary variance
{v_{\rm c}}
(parametrised by
{\alpha_{\rm V}}
) and timescale
{\tau_{\rm c}}
. Crucially, for the comparison between numerical estimations and theoretical approximations for different values of
{\tau_{\rm c}}
, we keep
the noise stationary variance
{v_{\rm c}}
constant
(where, throughout this section, by
constant
we mean
independent of
{\tau_{\rm c}}
), which amounts to consider
a
{\tau_{\rm c}}
-dependent count variance

{\sigma_{\rm c}}^{2}=2{\tau_{\rm c}}{v_{\rm c}}
(see
[
21
]
). Taking
{v_{\rm c}}
constant is the suitable scenario to explore the
{\tau_{\rm c}}\gg{\tau_{\rm m}}
limit without loss of generality (see Appendix
L
).

The fact that
{\sigma_{\rm c}}
depends on
{\tau_{\rm c}}
for constant
{v_{\rm c}}
is an arbitrary choice of the correlated noise amplitude that we probe in our simulations for each value of
{\tau_{\rm c}}
. Since
{\tau_{\rm c}},{\sigma_{\rm c}}
do not vary independently (in the so called
first simulation set
analyzed in this section, see Appendix
C
), we decided to set the spike count variance
{\sigma_{\rm c}}
to depend on the independent variable
{\tau_{\rm c}}
as
{\sigma_{\rm c}}=(2{\tau_{\rm c}}{v_{\rm c}})^{1/2}
(being
{v_{\rm c}}
a
{\tau_{\rm c}}
-independent proportionality constant), rather than taking the alternative option (i.e.,
{v_{\rm c}}
depending on
{\tau_{\rm c}}
as
{v_{\rm c}}={\sigma_{\rm c}}^{2}/2{\tau_{\rm c}}
, being
{\sigma_{\rm c}}
a
{\tau_{\rm c}}
-independent proportionality constant) because this scenario makes more sense when studying the large-
{\tau_{\rm c}}
limit (see Appendix
L
).

We provide an alternative analysis of the SNR in a complementary probing variables scheme, in which we fix
{\tau_{\rm c}}
and vary
{\sigma_{\rm c}}
(instead of fixing
{v_{\rm c}}
and varying
{\tau_{\rm c}}
), in Appendix
F
.

IV.1
Use of the analytical approximations

Below in subsection
IV.2
, we present a study of the behavior of the SNR as a function of
{\tau_{\rm c}}
by comparing the numerical simulations of
{r_{\rm R}}^{(s)}
,
{v_{\rm R}}^{(s)}
and of the SNR, with the following analytical approximations.

In the region
{\tau_{\rm c}}\ll{\tau_{\rm m}}
, the only analytical solution that we need in order to explain the numerical estimations of the SNR, is the Moreno et al solution
[
17
]
for the rate,
r_{\rm M}
, in the
{\tau_{\rm c}}\ll{\tau_{\rm m}}
limit (Eq. (10) of
[
17
]
). Two different conditions make possible to approximate the
{r_{\rm R}}
,
{v_{\rm R}}
and SNR, disposing only of this analytical expression. First, because in the limit
{\tau_{\rm c}}\ll{\tau_{\rm m}}
, and for fixed
{v_{\rm c}}
,
{\sigma_{\rm c}}
is small enough for our simulations to be in the validity regime of the Moreno solution (low
\alpha_{\rm M}={\sigma_{\rm c}}^{2}/{\sigma_{\rm I}}^{2}
) and, hence, the Moreno et al solution for
{\tau_{\rm c}}\ll{\tau_{\rm m}}
reproduces correctly the numerical simulations for
{r_{\rm R}}
(see Fig.
9
-A). Second, because, for low enough
{\tau_{\rm c}}
, hence low enough
{\sigma_{\rm c}}
, we can neglect the over-Poissonian corrections to the variance
{v_{\rm R}}
in Eq. (
11
) (see the
{\alpha_{\rm V}}=0
case in Fig.
8
). In sum, we simply take
{v_{\rm R}}{T_{\rm c}}\simeq{r_{\rm R}}\simeq r_{\rm M}
, where
r_{\rm M}
is the Moreno et al approximation (we will refer to this approximation for the readout rate variance as the
Poisson-Moreno approximation
). We construct our analytical expression for the SNR with these expressions for
{r_{\rm R}}
and
{v_{\rm R}}
.

For
{\tau_{\rm c}}\gg{\tau_{\rm m}}
, none of these conditions hold any more. Although in principle we dispose of the alternative Moreno et al solution for
r_{\rm M}
, valid for
{\tau_{\rm c}}\gg{\tau_{\rm m}}
(that we report in Eq.
41
), it is no longer possible to exploit this solution to explain the behavior of the SNR for large
{\tau_{\rm c}}
and constant
{v_{\rm c}}
. Indeed, for large enough
{\tau_{\rm c}}
, hence
{\sigma_{\rm c}}
, the simulation parameters lie outside the validity limit (low
\alpha_{\rm M}
) of the Moreno solution and, consequently,
r_{\rm M}
differs from the numerical estimation
\tilde{r_{\rm R}}
. Second, and more importantly, the over-Poissonian corrections of the rate variance cannot be neglected any longer. Therefore, we cannot approximate
{r_{\rm R}}
with
r_{\rm M}
, and we cannot use any longer the relation
{v_{\rm R}}{T_{\rm c}}\simeq{r_{\rm R}}\simeq r_{\rm M}
. The quenched-noise approximation for
{\tau_{\rm c}}\gg{\tau_{\rm m}}
provides us with a solution for both problems. First,
{r_{\rm q}}
captures well the behavior of
{r_{\rm R}}
also in this regime. Second, the quenched-noise approximation provides us with an equation for the rate variance as well (Eq. (
13
), or the simpler hand-waving quenched-noise approximations Eqs. (
109
,
110
)). We illustrate these facts in the next subsection, and particularly in Fig.
9
, in which we compare the Poisson-Moreno approximation with the quenched-noise approximation.

IV.2.2
Rate variance enhancement

We now consider the denominator of the SNR, i.e. the stimulus-averaged variance of the readout firing rate,
({v_{\rm R}}^{(+)}+{v_{\rm R}}^{(-)})/2
. First, for low enough
{\tau_{\rm c}}
we observe an agreement between
\tilde{v_{\rm R}}
and the Poisson-Moreno approximation (Fig.
9
-B). We attribute the small deviations to a numerical underestimation of the variance due to the finite-NIS effect (see Appendix
M
). We notice that in this case we do not employ the NIS-corrected estimation for the rate variance, since it exhibits larger statistical errors.
Second, the over-Poissonian increment of
\tilde{v_{\rm R}}
for large values of
{\tau_{\rm c}}
in the presence of noise correlations can only be explained, within the quenched-noise approximation, as the result of the integration of the temporal correlation function
{g_{\rm q}}
(see Eq. (
13
), and Appendix
I
for a further, intuitive explanation of the enhancement of
\tilde{v_{\rm R}}
for large
{\tau_{\rm c}}
).

Overall, both approximations, the Poisson-Moreno and the quenched-noise approximations describe the numerical behavior of
\tilde{v_{\rm R}}
in the
{\tau_{\rm c}}\ll{\tau_{\rm m}}
and
{\tau_{\rm c}}\gg{\tau_{\rm m}}
regimes, respectively. For large
{\tau_{\rm c}}
, the variance
{v_{\rm R}}
increases with
{\tau_{\rm c}}
(see the second term in Eq. (
11
) and Eq. (
13
)), while the mean rate
{r_{\rm R}}
stays constant with
{\tau_{\rm c}}
(as expected, see Eq. (
10
)).

IV.2.3
SNR enhancement

From the results of the previous subsections, it follows that the SNR exhibits a non-monotonic behavior with
{\tau_{\rm c}}
(Fig.
9
-C).
For large enough
{\tau_{\rm c}}
, noise correlations lower the readout SNR below its value in absence of noise correlations. For high values of
{\tau_{\rm c}}
, the increment of the SNR numerator,
\Delta{r_{\rm R}}
, depends only on
{v_{\rm c}}
, so that it stays constant in
{\tau_{\rm c}}
(see Fig.
9
-A and Sec.
III.2
). Instead, the rate variance increases with
{\tau_{\rm c}}
due to the over-Poissonian correction (which increases with
both
{\tau_{\rm c}}
and
{v_{\rm c}}
, see Eq. (
13a
) and Appendix
I
). Beyond a certain value of
{\tau_{\rm c}}
, the increment in the denominator becomes prevalent, thus leading to a decrement of the SNR.
For sufficiently small
{\tau_{\rm c}}
, when the enhancement in the rate variance
({v_{\rm R}}^{(+)}+{v_{\rm R}}^{(-)})/2
is not enough to overcome that of
\Delta{r_{\rm R}}
in the SNR numerator, we observe that the noise correlations induce an enhancement of the SNR. This constitutes the apparently paradoxical effect according to which encoding correlations that lower the encoding-SNR, enhance the readout SNR.

We have also compared the readout SNR with the
encoding SNR
(see Eq. (
8
)) (see Fig.
27
in Appendix
G
and Fig.
29
in Appendix
M
). By construction, the encoding SNR decreases with the noise correlation amplitude, and it may actually become lower than the readout SNR (please, see a discussion on this point in Appendix
K
).

For details regarding the small theoretical-numerical disagreement for low
{\tau_{\rm c}}
in Fig.
9
-C, we refer to the Appendix
M
, where we attribute it to the finite-NIS effect.

In sum, the SNR presents a non-monotonic behavior with
{\tau_{\rm c}}
, that is quantitatively explained by the quenched-noise approximation.

IV.3
The conditions for enhanced-by-consistency SNR to occur

In this section we address
for which general values
of the encoding and decoding parameters we expect the SNR enhancement to occur in the presence of noise correlations.
The quenched-noise approximation for the rate mean and variance allows us to draw necessary conditions on the NERM parameters, at least for sufficiently large
{\tau_{\rm c}}
. Our conclusions are drawn from the behavior of
{r_{\rm q}},{v_{\rm q}}
as a function of
{\mu_{\rm I}}
and
{\sigma_{\rm c}}
(Fig.
10
). In this section, we use the quenched-noise approximation and, in particular, the hand-waving expression for the rate variance, Eq. (
110
) in Appendix
H
, which is already a good approximation for the behavior of the rate variance (Fig.
8
).

Conditions on
{\mu_{\rm I}}^{(\pm)}
and dependence on
{\sigma_{\rm c}}
.
In Sec.
III.3
we said that, for the colored noise to enhance the firing rate
{r_{\rm R}}
, the value of
{\mu_{\rm I}}
should be safely lower than the inflection point of the LIF (circle in Fig.
10
-A). Given the parameters of the white LIF
{\tau_{\rm m}},{\sigma_{\rm I}}
, the inflection point occurs at the value of
{\mu_{\rm I}}
for which
g_{1}^{2}+g_{1}^{\prime}=0
, where
g_{1}={r_{\rm w}}^{\prime}/{r_{\rm w}}
. The inflection point
{\mu_{\rm I}}^{*}
of
{r_{\rm w}}({\mu_{\rm I}})
is also the value of
{\mu_{\rm I}}
below which
{r_{\rm q}}>{r_{\rm w}}
for low enough
{v_{\rm c}}
(indeed, expanding
{r_{\rm q}}
in (
10
) to
o({v_{\rm c}})
, we get that
{r_{\rm q}}\geq{r_{\rm w}}
for
{\mu_{\rm I}}\leq{\mu_{\rm I}}^{*}
, v. Fig.
10
). We can proof (see Appendix
J
) that the condition
{\mu_{\rm I}}^{(+)}<{\mu_{\rm I}}^{*}
is actually a necessary condition for the SNR to be enhanced in the presence of noise correlations in the NERM model, at least for large enough
{\tau_{\rm c}}\gtrsim{\tau_{\rm m}}
. In essence, for the SNR to increase in the presence of noise correlations, it is necessary that the numerator
\Delta{r_{\rm R}}
increases (which is not sufficient, since the denominator could increase too much). Furthermore, a necessary condition for
\Delta{r_{\rm R}}
to increase is, in its turn, that
{r_{\rm R}^{(+)}}
increases. Therefore, for the SNR to increase with
{\alpha_{\rm V}}>0
,
{r_{\rm R}^{(+)}}
must increase, which implies
{\mu_{\rm I}}<{\mu_{\rm I}}^{*}
.

In Appendix
J
we also show that this upper bound to
{\mu_{\rm I}^{(+)}}<{\mu_{\rm I}}^{*}
can be improved by taking into account the increase in the readout rate variance, i.e., by imposing that, beyond the numerator, the SNR itself should increase in the presence of noise correlations. With the analytic solution at hand, the condition
\text{SNR}_{\rm q}\geq\text{SNR}_{\rm w}
implies that (see Appendix
J
)
{\mu_{\rm I}}^{(+)}<\hat{\mu_{\rm I}}<{\mu_{\rm I}}^{*}
, where
\hat{\mu_{\rm I}}
is the solution of the transcendental equation
g_{1}^{\prime}+g_{1}^{2}=2{\tau_{\rm c}}{r_{\rm w}}g_{1}^{2}
. Although
\hat{\mu_{\rm I}}
has been derived for low enough values of
{v_{\rm c}}
and
{\mu_{\rm I}^{(-)}}
(see Appendix
J
), it actually works as an upper bound for the value of
\mu
below which we observe the SNR enhancement in all our simulations –see the green vertical line in Fig.
10
. See also Figs.
11
,
30
,
31
and Appendix
O
.

Again, this necessary condition is not sufficient to guarantee that
\text{SNR}_{\rm q}>\text{SNR}_{\rm w}
. Too high values of
{v_{\rm c}}
, or non-negligible values of
{\mu_{\rm I}}^{(-)}
, hence of
{r_{\rm R}}^{(-)}
, may make
\text{SNR}_{\rm q}<\text{SNR}_{\rm w}
. Let us, first, focus on the dependence on
{\mu_{\rm I}^{(-)}}
. Mind that, at least for low enough
{\sigma_{\rm c}}
,
{v_{\rm R}}^{(-)}
is an increasing function of
{\mu_{\rm I}}^{(-)}
(see Fig.
10
) and, therefore, the SNR denominator enhancement increases with
{\mu_{\rm I}^{(-)}}
. The larger the value of
{\sigma_{\rm c}}
, the lower the values of the (leftmost) inflection points of
{r_{\rm R}}
and
{v_{\rm R}}
(see Fig.
10
). This implies that the larger
{\sigma_{\rm c}}
, the lower the value that
{\mu_{\rm I}}^{(-)}
should take for the SNR to increase (as can be seen in Fig.
30
in Appendix
N
).

We now focus on the dependence on
{\sigma_{\rm c}}
. For large enough values of
{\sigma_{\rm c}}
(or
{v_{\rm c}}
), the SNR denominator increases faster with
{\sigma_{\rm c}}
than the numerator
\Delta{r_{\rm R}}
, because of the over-Poissonian correction to the variance, cf. the second term in Eq. (
13a
), or Eq. (
110
) in Appendix
I
. This is the origin of the non-monotonous dependence of the SNR on the noise amplitude (see as well Appendix
F
for an explicit analysis of this).

In sum, in order for the SNR to increase in the presence of noise correlations and for large enough
{\tau_{\rm c}}\gtrsim{\tau_{\rm m}}
, it is necessary that: (c1)
{\mu_{\rm I}}^{(+)}<\hat{\mu_{\rm I}}
is low enough, and (c2) both
{\sigma_{\rm c}}
and
{\mu_{\rm I}}^{(-)}<\hat{\mu_{\rm I}}^{(-)}
are low enough, with the upper bound
\hat{\mu_{\rm I}}^{(-)}
being a decreasing function of
{\sigma_{\rm c}}
.

V
Discussion

Here we used an Neural Encoding and Readout Model (NERM) of neural activity that combines information encoding in neural populations with a biophysically plausible model of neural readout, to investigate the conditions under which correlations between the temporal activity of input neurons can enhance the transmission of neural information downstream, even if they decrease the amount of information encoded in the input. Addressing this problem is highly relevant in neuroscience because it establishes theoretical grounds for the empirical and seemingly paradoxical discovery that higher correlations in neural activity aid performance at the behavioral level, even when they decrease the information at the encoding stage.

We formally analyzed a mechanism of enhanced-by-consistency readout
[
32
]
, in which the readout is implemented as a single neuron with an output coincidence-detection nonlinearity, and studied it as a function of the interplay between the encoding and readout timescales. Previous work
[
32
]
analyzed this NERM by numerical integration, with
{N}=2
encoding neurons. Here, we extend this work by providing an approximated analytic solution of a more general version of the model, which helps assess the conditions under which the apparently paradoxical effect of lower encoding but higher transmission of neural information occurs.
Previous works have addressed, with analytical approximations, the effect of temporal input correlations on the
mean
output rate of a LIF neuron
[
10
,
16
,
17
]
.
Here, we provided alternative, approximated analytical expressions not only for the mean output firing rates (that actually generalize those in
[
17
]
), but also for the SNR of information transmission. This constitutes a key extension to evaluate the implications of correlated activity on neural information processing.

We found that the non-linearity of the LIF activation function induces asymmetric fluctuations of the output firing rate, following symmetric
input
fluctuations. The quenched-noise approximation developed here, valid for large or moderate values of
{\tau_{\rm c}}\gtrsim{\tau_{\rm m}}
and for low stationary noise variance
{v_{\rm c}}
, provides us with the conditions under which these asymmetric fluctuations allow information-limiting noise correlations to enhance the readout SNR. In particular, the following necessary conditions for the mean input current must be satisfied (see Sec.
IV.3
): the readout neuron must operate in a fluctuation-driven regime for low enough
{\mu_{\rm I}}^{(+)}<\hat{\mu_{\rm I}}
, in agreement with the results of
[
32
]
, with
\hat{\mu_{\rm I}}({v_{\rm c}},{\tau_{\rm m}})
being a decreasing function of
{v_{\rm c}}
and
{\tau_{\rm m}}
; the stimulus-specific difference in
encoding
firing rates
{\mu_{\rm I}}^{(+)}-{\mu_{\rm I}}^{(-)}
is high enough (with
{\mu_{\rm I}}^{(-)}<\hat{\mu_{\rm I}}^{(-)}({v_{\rm c}},{\mu_{\rm I}})
, being
\hat{\mu_{\rm I}}^{(-)}({v_{\rm c}},{\tau_{\rm m}}
) a decreasing function of
{v_{\rm c}}
and
{\tau_{\rm m}}
). These conditions on the input firing rates confirm previous conclusions that input correlations enhance readout information from a postsynaptic neuron when the readout integration time constant is short enough so that the average excitatory inputs received during an integration window are much smaller than the gap between the spiking threshold and resting potential of the readout neuron, so that output firing is driven by input fluctuations
[
32
]
.

Beyond the conditions on the encoding firing rates
{\mu_{\rm I}}^{(\pm)}
, our analytical solutions reveal necessary conditions on other variables, which were not considered in previous work
[
32
]
. For instance, it reveals conditions on the amplitude of noise correlations,
{\sigma_{\rm c}}
: this quantity must be large enough for noise correlations to have an impact on the SNR numerator
\Delta{r_{\rm R}}
, but not too large for the SNR denominator
\left<{v_{\rm R}}^{(s)}\right>_{s=\pm}
to overcome the enhancement in the numerator (Fig.
10
and Sec.
IV.3
).
The non-monotonic dependence of the readout SNR on
{\sigma_{\rm c}}^{2}=2{v_{\rm c}}{\tau_{\rm c}}
and, hence,
on
{\tau_{\rm c}}
for fixed
{v_{\rm c}}
(Fig.
9
) can be clearly understood within the quenched-noise approximation. On the one hand, the larger
{\tau_{\rm c}}
, the larger the minimum value of the amplitude
{\sigma_{\rm c}}
of noise correlations that can elicit a rate enhancement
\Delta{r_{\rm R}}
(which depends on
{\sigma_{\rm c}}
only through
{v_{\rm c}}={\sigma_{\rm c}}^{2}/(2{\tau_{\rm c}})
). On the other hand, while
\Delta{r_{\rm R}}
depends on the stationary variance
{v_{\rm c}}
only, the readout variance
{v_{\rm R}}
increases with
both
{v_{\rm c}}
and
{\tau_{\rm c}}
(and
faster with
{v_{\rm c}}
, cf. Eq. (
13a
)). The dependence of the mean readout rate
{r_{\rm R}}
on the stationary variance
{v_{\rm c}}
follows from the quenched-noise approximation
ansatz
itself (Eq.
9
). In contrast, the readout rate variance
{v_{\rm R}}
increases with
{v_{\rm c}}
and
{\tau_{\rm c}}
, because it is the integration, Eq. (
11
), of the temporal correlation function (which, in the quenched-noise approximation, decays as an exponential with timescale
{\tau_{\rm c}}
and amplitude
Q({v_{\rm c}})
, see Eqs. (
12
) and (
110
) in Appendix
I
). As a result, the enhancement of the readout SNR can occur over a wide range of timescales
{\tau_{\rm c}}
(or
{\sigma_{\rm c}}
for fixed
{v_{\rm c}}
), while it decreases at sufficiently large noise amplitude
{\sigma_{\rm c}}
.

In sum, for the noise correlations to enhance the readout SNR,
{\mu_{\rm I}}^{(\pm)}
should be lower than the inflection point of the white-noise LIF activation function, their difference
{\mu_{\rm I}}^{(+)}-{\mu_{\rm I}}^{(-)}
should be large enough, and
{\sigma_{\rm c}}
should be large enough, but not too large.

It is of interest to consider the neuroscientific implications of the conditions and parameter regions we found to be able to support an enhanced-by-consistency information transmission regime. The first consideration regards how the numerical range of parameters in which this is possible compares with real biological parameters. Figure
11
explores this regime as a function of two parameters that have been extensively measured in the brain: the time constant of correlations
\tau_{c}
and the firing rate differences between the different stimuli to be detected. The correlation time constant increases systematically when moving from sensory to higher-order areas
[
53
]
, and it ranges from one or few milliseconds for the retina or sub-cortical structures
[
54
,
55
,
56
]
, to few tens of milliseconds for cortical sensory areas, and hundreds of milliseconds
for association or decision-making areas
[
34
,
57
,
58
]
. The firing rate differences between stimuli to be discriminated can vary from fractions of one Hz (spikes/s) to few tens of Hz
[
59
,
60
,
61
,
62
,
63
,
64
]
. Our results (Fig.
11
) show an enhanced-by-consistency regime for rate differences spanning few tens of Hz and correlation time constants r
\tau_{c}
larger than the membrane time constant
\tau_{m}
(which is typically in the range of 5–20 ms) and reaching several tens to few hundreds of millisecond, well within the range of sensory and association areas, suggesting that the enhanced-by-consistency regime can be sustained in these brain areas.
The second consideration is that the additional necessary conditions derived here have potential implications for the neural biological functions.
We have found that information-limiting correlations are useful to enhance information transmission only for intermediate values of the correlation strength
{\sigma_{\rm c}}
.
Across experiments and brain areas, correlation between neurons are reported consistently to be non-zero, but always of small or moderate value
[
23
,
8
]
. Our result suggests that non-null but moderate values may be useful to design neural codes that trade off effectively the information-limiting and information-enhancing effect of correlations and achieve good levels of information transmission.
We have also found that the SNR enhancement may occur for a wide range of time constants of input correlations
{\tau_{\rm c}}
, even at values of
{\tau_{\rm c}}
much larger than the readout membrane time constant
{\tau_{\rm m}}
.
This implies that neural circuits may be able to enjoy the benefits of information-encoding-limiting yet information-transmission-enriching correlations even when encoding information with activity correlated over long times. This is important because long encoding timescales are useful to combine temporally separate inputs and accumulate consistently information over time, or to implement or compute a goal-oriented navigation plan
[
34
]
.

From the methodological point of view, we have proposed a (to our knowledge) novel analytical approximation for the rate mean and variance of the LIF model with white+colored input noise or, equivalently, for the first passage time of a random walker with non-Markovian noise (the
active Browinan particle
, in the terminology of
[
49
,
65
]
). Despite its conceptual and methodological simplicity, this appears as one of the few existing results regarding the LIF model with temporally and spatially correlated input
[
22
,
49
]
.
The quenched-noise approximation reveals that the colored-noise LIF neuron exhibits a higher-curvature activation function with respect to the white-noise LIF activation function
{r_{\rm w}}({\mu_{\rm I}})
. Moreover, beyond the effect on the average firing rate, the quenched-noise approximation addresses as well how temporal auto-correlations in the input are transmitted into auto-correlations in the colored-LIF output firing rate and, finally, how such auto-correlations shape the rate variance below or above Poissonian fluctuations
{v_{\rm R}}={r_{\rm R}}/{T_{\rm c}}
.

Our work has considered a single readout neuron. While this simplification is useful to understand in detail the role of neural non-linearities in the transmission of information from temporally correlated inputs, information transmission in the brain happens from input to output populations. Other work
[
39
]
has considered the conditions for effective transmission of information between neural populations, although considering only time-averaged spike count responses and neglecting the temporal aspects of correlated activity and neural post-synaptic integration that we considered here. Extending this and previous works will be important to merge the principles of population readout with those of time-correlation and time-integration, to understand how the spatio-temporal structure of neural activity shapes information transmission.

The function of populations of sensory neurons includes encoding the time course of time-varying stimuli, beyond the encoding of the identity of static stimuli as considered in our study. It would be important in future studies to extend our work to consider how noise correlations and readout nonlinearities impact the encoding and readout of information about the time course of time-varying stimuli. Indeed, an empirical study
[
66
]
suggested that noise correlations limit the temporal precision of the information that the time course of the average firing in a neural population can convey about the time course of a stimulus. Other studies have proposed that readout nonlinearities can enhance the detection of very rapid stimulus variations in a way that is robust to the detrimental encoding effects of noise correlations
[
67
]
. While studying the time-varying encoding and readout is beyond the scope of the present article, we speculate that extending our formalism to this problem will give insights about the precise conditions in which the temporal parameters of stimulus dynamics, noise correlations and readout nonlinearities allow for a beneficial effect of time-dependent noise correlations on the transmission of information about dynamic stimuli.

A simplification of our work, shared by other previous attempts to characterize analytically how input correlations change the output firing characteristics of a LIF neuron
[
46
,
17
]
, is that all steps of post-synaptic time integration have been summarized through the membrane potential time constant. We considered synapses having instantaneous dynamics, neglecting both their finite rise time and decay time constants, and we neglected the timescales of dendritic integration
[
68
,
69
]
. Although our simple model still allows to study effectively some of the consequences of lengthening or shortening of integration time constants by non-membrane contributions (for example, the effect of slower synaptic decay time constants could be recapitulated by increasing the membrane time constant), these simplifications prevent us to study the effect of the interplay between membrane, synaptic and dendritic integration in shaping the input-output information of a neuron
[
40
]
. Studying these issues is beyond the scope of the present study and the reach of analytical calculations, and is left for further work.

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{N}w_{j}^{2}\mu_{j}

(24b)

\displaystyle{v_{\rm c}}

\displaystyle=\frac{{\sigma_{\rm c}}^{2}}{2{\tau_{\rm c}}}

(24c)

\displaystyle{\sigma_{\rm c}}^{2}

\displaystyle={\bf w}^{\dagger}\cdot C\cdot{\bf w}.

(24d)

B.3
The Valente et al 2021 model

The NERM analyzed in this article is actually the generalization, for an arbitrary number of encoding neurons
N
, of the encoding-and-readout model originally defined in section
“A model of enhanced-by-consistency information transmission”
in
[
32
]
). In reference
[
32
]
the encoding neurons are represented by inhomogeneous Poisson processes whose firing rate behaves, given the binary stimulus
s=\pm 1
as:

\displaystyle r_{i}^{(s)}(t)=\mu_{i}^{(s)}+{\sigma_{\rm V}}_{i}\left({\alpha_{\rm V}}{\xi_{\rm C}}(t)+(1-{\alpha_{\rm V}}^{2})^{1/2}\xi_{i}(t)\right)

(25)

where
\mu_{i}^{(s)}
is the tuning curve and
{\alpha_{\rm V}}
is a real number in
[0,1]
modulating the fraction of shared and private noise (in such a way that
\left<\xi_{i}(t)\xi_{j}(t^{\prime}+t)\right>=\delta_{ij}(2{\tau_{\rm c}})^{-1}\exp(-|t|/{\tau_{\rm c}})
,
\left<{\xi_{\rm C}}(t){\xi_{\rm C}}(t^{\prime}+t)\right>=(2{\tau_{\rm c}})^{-1}\exp(-|t|/{\tau_{\rm c}})
,
\left<\xi_{i}(t){\xi_{\rm C}}(t^{\prime}+t)\right>=0
). In this way, the noise variance of the
i
-th encoding neuron is independent of
{\alpha_{\rm V}}
and equal to
{v_{\rm V}}_{i}={\sigma_{\rm V}}_{i}^{2}/(2{\tau_{\rm c}})
. The spike rate standard deviation
{\sigma_{\rm V}}_{i}
is set to be proportional to the difference of the tuning curves
{\Delta_{\mu}}_{i}=\mu_{i}^{(+)}-\mu_{i}^{(-)}
:

\displaystyle{\sigma_{\rm V}}_{i}

\displaystyle={\nu_{\rm V}}\,{\Delta_{\mu}}_{i}

(26)

\displaystyle{\Delta_{\mu}}_{i}

\displaystyle:=\mu_{i}^{(+)}-\mu_{i}^{(-)}>0

(27)

and
{\nu_{\rm V}}\geq 0
is a positive noise-to-signal ratio. Taking the noise amplitude
{\sigma_{\rm V}}
proportional to
{\Delta_{\mu}}_{i}
, one ensures that for any
{\alpha_{\rm V}}>0
the noise correlations are information-limiting.

We make notice that Valente et al model is of the form as in the previous subsection, with:

\displaystyle\sigma_{i}={\sigma_{\rm V}}_{i}^{2}={\nu_{\rm V}}^{2}\,{\Delta_{\mu}}_{i}^{2}

(28)

\displaystyle\bar{C}_{ij}=\alpha_{V}^{2}\qquad i\neq j.

(29)

If we now suppose that all encoding neurons have equal tuning curves increments,
{\Delta_{\mu}}_{m}={\Delta_{\mu}}
, and that the vector
\bf w
has constant components
w_{j}=w
, we obtain that the current encoding
I(t)
satisfies Eq. (
23
) with (see (
24
)):

\displaystyle\mu_{I}^{(s)}

\displaystyle=wN\overline{\mu}^{(s)}

(30)

\displaystyle\left(\sigma_{I}^{(s)}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=Nw^{2}\overline{\mu}^{(s)}

(31)

\displaystyle{\sigma_{\rm c}}^{2}

\displaystyle=2{\tau_{\rm c}}{v}w^{2}N{\sigma_{\rm V}}^{2}\left(1+(N-1){\alpha_{\rm V}}^{2}\right)

(32)

where
\overline{\mu}^{(s)}
is the average of the encoding neuron tuning curves. From these last equations we see that the signal/noise ratio of the input current
{\rm snr}={\Delta_{\mu_{I}}}/({\sigma_{\rm c}}^{2}+\sigma_{I}^{2})^{1/2}
monotonically decreases with
{\alpha_{\rm V}}
. Eq. (
23
) has the equivalent form:

\displaystyle I(t)={\mu_{\rm I}}+{\sigma_{\rm I}}\,\eta(t)+{\sigma_{\rm c}}\,\xi(t)

(33)

\displaystyle\dot{\xi}(t)=-\xi(t)/{\tau_{\rm c}}+\frac{1}{{\tau_{\rm c}}}\eta^{\prime}(t)

(34)

(we omit the
\mu_{I}
-and
\sigma_{I}
-dependence on
s
), where
\eta,\eta^{\prime}
are two different continuous time white noises, and
\xi(t)
is an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process with timescale
{\tau_{\rm c}}
.

B.4
Analogy with the random walk problem

In the diffusion approximation, valid for
{\mu_{\rm I}}\gg({\sigma_{\rm I}}^{2}+{\sigma_{\rm c}}^{2})^{1/2}
(see
[
42
,
17
]
, the stimulus-readout probability distribution
\text{prob}({\vec{t}}|s)
(on which the SNR depends, through
{r_{\rm R}},{v_{\rm R}}
in Eq. (
4
)), becomes the distribution of sequences of first passage times of a continuous-time random walker
V(t)
(see Eq. (
5b
)), with damp
1/{\tau_{\rm m}}
, constant drift
{\mu_{\rm I}}
, white noise drift
{\sigma_{\rm I}}\eta(t)
, colored noise drift
{\sigma_{\rm c}}\xi(t)
, and the reset mechanism of the LIF neuron (that conditions the stationary distribution of the
\xi
’s
[
18
]
). The readout neuron Inter-Spike Interval (ISI) time is the first passage time of such a random walker (see
[
49
,
65
]
, and references therein).

B.5
A difference between the present notations and that of Valente et al

Please, mind the notation difference between
[
32
]
and the present article: there, it is assumed that
{\rm Cov}\left(x_{i}^{(s)}(t),x_{j}^{(s)}(t)\right)=\sigma^{2}{\tau_{\rm c}}\exp(-|t-t^{\prime}|/{\tau_{\rm c}})
, so that their parameter
\sigma^{2}
has a different dependence on
{\tau_{\rm c}}
than our
{\sigma_{\rm V}}_{i}^{2}
’s (and than our
{v_{\rm V}}_{i}
’s). Indeed, in their work, the instantaneous Poisson rate
r(u)
is converted into a time-correlated Poisson rate
r(t;{\tau_{\rm c}})
in the following way (mind the misprint in their low-pass filter, equation 19):

\displaystyle r(t,{\tau_{\rm c}})=\int_{0}^{t}{\rm d}u\,e^{-(t-u)}r(u)

(35)

so that
\left<(r(t;{\tau_{\rm c}})-\mu)(r(t^{\prime};{\tau_{\rm c}})-\mu)\right>=\sigma^{2}{\tau_{\rm c}}\exp(-|t-t^{\prime}|/{\tau_{\rm c}})
.

Appendix C
Numerical methods

C.1
Numerical integration of the stochastic differential equation for the readout neuron membrane potential

In the main text, we have presented a stochastic-processes theory of the NERM defined in section
II
. The diffusion approximation (see
III.1
) allows us to treat the membrane potential of the readout LIF neuron as the solution of a stochastic differential equation. In the article, we compare the approximated analytical solutions to such equations, with numerical simulations, that help us to asses the approximations’ validity limit. We have, in particular, and as in references
[
17
,
19
]
, numerically solved Eqs. (
5
) using the Euler-Maruyama method
[
74
,
75
]
, completed with a numerical protocol (see below) that corrects the bias induced by the finiteness of the Number of Integration Steps (NIS) per unit time,
{n_{\rm is}}
.

In brief, our numerical estimation for the rate mean and variance,
{r_{\rm R}},{v_{\rm R}}
respectively, are performed as follows. First, we employ the Euler-Maruyama method with
{T_{\rm c}}{n_{\rm is}}
integration steps to numerically integrate Eqs. (
5
) with the desired set of parameters
{\mu_{\rm I}},{\sigma_{\rm I}},{\sigma_{\rm c}},{\tau_{\rm c}},{\tau_{\rm m}}
in a time interval of length
{T_{\rm c}}
. We perform a number of
{n_{\rm tr}}
different realizations of such integration, differing in the independent random samples of the white noises
\eta,\eta^{\prime}
, and in the initial conditions for
\xi(0),v(0)
(that can either be independently sampled from their stationary distributions, or chosen, as for our numerical results, to coincide with those of the previous realization in the last instant value). For each realization, we store the corresponding sequence of readout neuron spike times
\vec{t}
. Afterwards, we compute the across-trials average and variance of the rate
{n_{\rm R}}({T_{\rm c}})/{T_{\rm c}}
, where
{n_{\rm R}}({T_{\rm c}})=\text{len}(\vec{t})
in single realizations, and where
{T_{\rm c}}=20
. These are, respectively, our finite-
{n_{\rm is}}
estimations for the rate mean and variance,
\tilde{r_{\rm R}},\tilde{v_{\rm R}}
(circles and squares in Figs.
5
,
8
,
9
-A,
9
-B,
9
-C). Importantly, and as already specified in the main article text,
all the rate variance estimations that we report are rate variances per unit time
:
{v_{\rm R}}{T_{\rm c}}
. Indeed, both the singular and the regular part of the temporal correlation function
\sf C
induce rate variances that decreases as
1/{T_{\rm c}}
: to eliminate this dependency, we report the variance in intervals of unit length. Consequenly, the SNR analised in Secs.
IV.2
,
IV.3
is the SNR per unit time window, or:
\text{SNR}={T_{\rm c}}^{-1/2}\times\text{SNR}({T_{\rm c}})
. The so defined standard deviation of the rate in the unit time interval,
({v_{\rm R}}{T_{\rm c}})^{1/2}
coincides with the value of the rate standard deviation,
{v_{\rm R}}^{1/2}
that we obtain if we divide each of our
{n_{\rm tr}}
realizations of the readout spike trains
\vec{t}_{r}
in
m={\rm int}({T_{\rm c}})
chunks, in such a way that we have a new set of
m\times{n_{\rm tr}}
shorter realizations, of length
1
each, and we compute the standard deviation of
\text{len}(\vec{t})
across this novel set of spike trains. The same is true for the SNR per unit time interval.

C.4
Algorithm of finite-NIS effect reduction

Essentially, our finite-NIS correction algorithm consists in the following simple procedure. Given a simulation for fixed
{\tau_{\rm c}}
, we first estimate
{r_{\rm R}}
it for all values of
{\mu_{\rm I}},{n_{\rm is}}
, obtaining
\tilde{r_{\rm R}}({\mu_{\rm I}},{n_{\rm is}})
and its statistical uncertainty. Afterwards, we perform a regression of this function as a function of
{n_{\rm is}}
, assuming a fitting function
\tilde{r_{\rm R}}({\mu_{\rm I}},{n_{\rm is}})\simeq r_{\infty}({\mu_{\rm I}})+b({\mu_{\rm I}})\,{n_{\rm is}}^{\beta}
, but
forcing the exponent
\beta
to assume a common value across the different data
\tilde{r_{\rm R}}({\mu_{\rm I}},{n_{\rm is}})
, i.e., performing a unique fit for all the
{\mu_{\rm I}}
datasets, in which
\beta
is
{\mu_{\rm I}}
-independent and the rest of the fit parameters
r_{\infty},b
are
{\mu_{\rm I}}
-dependent. The
{n_{\rm is}}=\infty
extrapolated regression parameter
r_{\infty}({\mu_{\rm I}})
is our estimation for the rate mean (for the given
{\tau_{\rm c}}
, and the rest of the parameters). Employing this regression protocol, we observe a lower
\chi^{2}
variable per degree of freedom, than fixing
\beta
, or fitting it for each
{\mu_{\rm I}}
separately (please, see the details of the regression protocol in
[
48
]
). We repeat this procedure for each
{\tau_{\rm c}}
. In Fig.
12
-A, we report the fitted values of
r_{\infty}
for a given value of
{\tau_{\rm c}}
. In Fig.
12
-B, we report the fitted values of
\beta
vs
{\tau_{\rm c}}
. Note the low values of
\beta
, that make the raw estimation of the first passage time so slowly dependent on
{n_{\rm is}}
and, hence, so inaccurate –and, consequently, the finite-NIS effect mitigation procedure so necessary.

Future completions of this work could include a comparison with alternative numerical schemes of integration of stochastic differential equations beyond Euler-Maruyama, as those based on the path integral
[
72
,
73
]
.

C.5
Numerical estimation of the temporal distribution function
g

In order for the numerical estimation of the temporal distribution function
g(t)
to be as clear as in Fig.
7
, it is necessary to account for the finite-
{T_{\rm c}}
bias of the estimation. In particular, we account for the finite-
{T_{\rm c}}
bias, by computing
the probability couples of spikes to be at a distance
d=|t-t^{\prime}|
, when the spikes are sampled with uniform probability in the
[0,T]
time interval
. This cumulative probability can be easily seen to be
\text{Prob}\left(d<D\right)=2D/T-D^{2}/T^{2}
, and the probability density is:
\text{prob}\left(d\right)=2/T-2d/T^{2}
. Consequently, if we choose arbitrary bins
b_{1}<\ldots<b_{B}<T
, it is
\text{Prob}\left(b_{j-1}<D<b_{j}\right)=2(b_{j}-b_{j-1})/T-({b_{j}^{2}-b_{j-1}^{2}})/T^{2}
.

Our
g
estimation algorithm works, consequently, as follows: (i) we initialize to zero an array
g
of length
B
. (ii) We put into
{\texttt{g}[i]}
the number of couples of spikes
t,t^{\prime}
belonging to the same spike train, across all the
{n_{\rm tr}}
spike trains in the simulation, whose distance
d=|t-t^{\prime}|
lies in the
i
-th bin:
b_{i-1}<d\leq b_{i}
. (iii)
\texttt{g}[i]
is redefined dividing it by
\text{Prob}\left(b_{i-1}<D<b_{i}\right)
. We expect that, for large enough
d
, the result is the expected value of the number of couples in an interval
{T_{\rm c}}
(since
\text{Prob}\left(b_{i-1}<D<b_{i}\right)=
(# couples in the interval
{T_{\rm c}}
whose distance lies in the
i
-th bin) divided by the (# couples in the interval
{T_{\rm c}}
)). (iv) We consequently divide by the expected value of the number of couples of (independent) spikes in
{T_{\rm c}}
,
\left<({n_{\rm R}}-1){n_{\rm R}}/2\right>=({r_{\rm R}}{T_{\rm c}})^{2}/2
, using our numerical estimation
\tilde{r_{\rm R}}
at the place of
{r_{\rm R}}
; (v) Finally, we multiply this quantity by
\tilde{r_{\rm R}}
, to obtain our estimation of
g
. In this way, the assymptotic value is, as required,
{r_{\rm R}}
. Summarising:

(i)

initialize
\texttt{g}[i]=0

\forall i=1,\ldots,B

(ii)

put in
g[j]
the number of couples of spikes such that
b_{j-1}<|t-t^{\prime}|\leq b_{j}
,
\forall j
and
\forall
trials

(iii)

\texttt{g[j] /= }2(b_{j}-b_{j-1})/{T_{\rm c}}-({b_{j}^{2}-b_{j-1}^{2}})/{T_{\rm c}}^{2}
,

Appendix D
Validity range of the approximations

D.1
Validity of the diffusion approximation for the encoding current

The membrane potential of the LIF readout neuron in the Valente model is described, in the diffusion approximation, by the system of equations (
5
). Such approximation consists in approximating the statistics of the number of encoding spikes in the time window
\Delta
(sampled from a Poisson distribution with average
\Lambda=\int_{\Delta}{\rm d}t\,{\bf w}\cdot{\bm{\rho}}(t)
) by the statistics of the normal distribution. For the diffusion approximation to the encoding current to be correct, the average encoding current in the diffusion approximation
{\mu_{\rm I}}
, must be large enough in units of the noise amplitude,
({\sigma_{\rm I}}^{2}+{\sigma_{\rm c}}^{2})^{1/2}
(the square root of the total spike count variance per unit time). If
{\mu_{\rm I}}
were not large enough in units of
({\sigma_{\rm I}}^{2}+{\sigma_{\rm c}}^{2})^{1/2}
, negative values of the spike count rate could exhibit non-negligible probability and, consequently, one would overestimate the count variance. We have assessed the validity of the diffusion approximation for the encoding current, which is illustrated for the 1st simulation in the inset of panel F of Fig.
3
: the numerical estimation of the standard deviation of the spike count variance (performed through the Jack-Knife method for increasingly large time window size
\Delta_{\rm J}
) is, indeed, statistically consistent with its theoretical value
({\sigma_{\rm I}}^{2}+{\sigma_{\rm c}}^{2})^{1/2}
.

For completeness, in Fig.

we report the parameters
\alpha_{\rm M},\nu_{\rm W}

{\tau_{\rm c}}
, for two values of
{v_{\rm V}}({\alpha_{\rm V}})
, and for
the lowest value of
{\mu_{\rm I}}
in the second simulation. The largest value of
\alpha_{\rm d}
is at most
\simeq 0.1
implies that, in the worst case, the mean encoding rate is roughly ten times larger than its standard deviation. This confirms that the parameters of the simulations considered in the article lie in the validity range of the diffusion approximation for the encoding current.

D.4
Validity of the diffusion approximation for the readout firing rate mean and variance

The analytical results presented in this article exploit the Siegert result for the firing rate of the LIF model with white-colored noise current, Eq. (
42
), that was derived in the diffusion approximation
[
42
]
. Consequently,
our analytical results refer to the LIF model with coloured input noise in the diffusion approximation, Eq. (
5
)
. In this article, we have numerically demonstrated that our analytical approximations explain quantitatively the full diffusion NERM in Eq. (
5
). It is a natural question to ask to what extent the diffusion approximation itself is accurate in the region of parameters that we explore in this article or, in other words, to what extent Eq. (
5
) is a good model of the
LIF model with spiking encoding current
in Eqs. (
2
,
3
). We will show in this section that, actually, the diffusion approximation is not accurate in the fluctuation-driven regime of the LIF model, in which the SNR enhancement phenomenon occurs. However, the SNR enhancement phenomenon, and its qualitative dependence with
{\mu_{\rm I}}^{(\pm)}
and
{\sigma_{\rm c}}
explained in Sec.
IV
are also present in the LIF model with spiking encoding current, albeit in range of parameters that are shifted with respect to those that apply for the diffusion LIF model in Eq. (
5
). Therefore, the SNR enhancement effect and its dependence on the relevant quantities are not an artifact of the diffusion approximation.

We have simulated (the
6th set of simulations
, see Table
9
) the LIF model with spiking encoding current with the same parameters
{\tau_{\rm c}},{\tau_{\rm m}},N,{\sigma_{\rm V}}
of the fourth simulation, in a finer and broader grid of values of
{\mu_{\rm I}}
and with three values of the gain
w
. The simulations of the LIF model with spiking encoding current are performed through an event-driven exact integration algorithm
[
77
,
78
,
48
]
. The comparison between the diffusion approximation for the mean readout firing rate
{r_{\rm w}}
(in the case of zero colored noise amplitude
{\sigma_{\rm V}}=0
) and the numerical firing rate of the LIF model with spiking encoding current is shown in Fig.
15
. Figure
16
is a variant of this analysis for nonzero encoding noise correlations,
{\sigma_{\rm V}}=4.4721
.
In the figures, the vertical lines signal the value of
\hat{\mu_{\rm I}}
for each value of
w
, or the upper bound derived in section
IV
for the value of
{\mu_{\rm I}}^{(+)}
below which the SNR enhancement can occur. We observe that, despite the absolute error decreases for decreasing
w
, as expected, the relative error (lower panels in Figs.
15
,
16
) actually decreases with
w
for given values of
{\mu_{\rm I}}
. In any case, we observe that in the interesting region of
{\mu_{\rm I}}<\hat{\mu_{\rm I}}
in which the phenomenon that we analyse occurs, the relative error of the diffusion approximation becomes higher than one (i.e., of the same order, or higher than the theoretical value itself)
for all the values of
w
. This picture holds unchanged in the presence of noise correlations, and also for wider ranges of
w
. Although we expect the relative error to decrease for LIF models with lower values of
{\tau_{\rm m}}
, we conclude that, in the regime in which we observe the SNR enhancement, the diffusion approximation is not accurate in general. These results are consistent with previous assessment of the accuracy of the diffusion approximation
[
79
,
80
]
, that reveal that it is only relatively accurate.

In this circumstance, a natural question is whether the behavior of the SNR that we described in Sec.
IV
is an artifact of the diffusion approximation. We checked whether the NERM with spiking encoding current actually exhibits the behavior predicted by the theory (Sec.
IV
) and by the NERM in diffusion approximation, Eq. (
5
)). In this section, we find that this is the case.

We have assessed this with the help of the
7th simulation
(see Table
10
), whose results are summarized in Fig.
17
. The figure serves as a guide for our numerical tests of the SNR of the NERM with spiking encoding current, that we present in Fig.
18
, where we show the SNR for the couple
({\mu_{\rm I}^{(+)}},{\mu_{\rm I}^{(-)}})=(50,60)

{\mu_{\rm I}}^{(\pm)}
are marked with vertical lines in the inset of Fig.
17

{\sigma_{\rm V}}
. In Fig.
18
, we show how, for the interval
({\mu_{\rm I}^{(+)}},{\mu_{\rm I}^{(-)}})=(50,60)
, the SNR significantly increases with
{\sigma_{\rm V}}
, in such a way that noise correlations indeed induce an enhancement of the SNR, as far as both
{\mu_{\rm I}}^{(\pm)}
are low enough. This result is also consistent with
[
32
]
.

Furthermore, also the non-monotonic behaviors of the SNR versus
{\mu_{\rm I}^{(+)}}
and
{\sigma_{\rm V}}
described in Sec.
IV
are observed in the spiking current NERM. We illustrate this in Figs.
19
,
20
, where we plot the excess of SNR,
\text{SNR}-\text{SNR}_{\text{w}}
versus
{\mu_{\rm I}^{(+)}}
, for two values of
{\mu_{\rm I}^{(-)}}=46.67,73.33
(in Figs.
19
,
20
respectively), and where of course
\text{SNR}_{\text{w}}
is the SNR for the white noise case with
{\sigma_{\rm c}}=0
(red points in Fig.
17
). First, Fig.
19
illustrates the non-monotonic dependence of the SNR on
{\mu_{\rm I}^{(+)}}
: when
{\mu_{\rm I}^{(+)}}
approaches the inflection point
{\mu_{\rm I}}^{*}
of the
{r_{\rm w}}

\Delta{r_{\rm R}}

{\mu_{\rm I}^{(+)}}

20

{\mu_{\rm I}^{(-)}}

{\mu_{\rm I}^{(-)}}
, the SNR decreases with
{\sigma_{\rm c}}
. This is consistent with the qualitative dependence on
{\sigma_{\rm c}}
that we described in Sec.
IV.3
. Given
{\mu_{\rm I}}^{(-)}

{\sigma_{\rm c}}
beyond which the SNR decreases with
{\sigma_{\rm c}}
. We expect, in this way, that for sufficiently low
{\mu_{\rm I}^{(-)}}

{\sigma_{\rm c}}

{\mu_{\rm I}^{(-)}}

{\sigma_{\rm c}}

{\mu_{\rm I}}^{(-)}

19
,
20

10
,
30
) that the behavior of the NERM with spiking encoding current exhibits the same qualitative behavior than its counterpart in the diffusion approximation.

We conclude that, despite the diffusion approximation does not accurately reproduce the behavior of the NERM with spiking encoding current in Eqs. (
1
,
2
,
3

5
)).

As a remark note, it is somehow natural that the SNR enhancement effect and its dependence on the relevant quantities are not an artifact of the diffusion approximation. This behavior can, indeed, be simply explained in terms of some essential features of the analytic solution, as the faster dependence on
{\sigma_{\rm c}}
of the over-Poissonian correction to the rate variance (the SNR denominator), with respect to the rate mean (the SNR numerator, see Sec.
IV
). As far as we expect such general features to apply for the LIF model with spiking encoding current, we also expect the SNR behavior in the NERM to stay qualitatively unchanged in the NERM with spiking encoding current.

An in-depth analysis of the spiking encoding current NERM (for instance, its dependence on the NIS), and its relation with the theory presented here is out of the scope of the present article.

E.2

The readout firing rate in the quenched-noise approximation is:

\displaystyle{r_{\rm q}}({\mu_{\rm I}})

\displaystyle:=\left<{r_{\rm w}}(\tilde{\mu})\right>_{{\cal N}(\tilde{\mu}|\mu_{I},{v_{\rm c}})}\qquad\text{quenched-noise app.}

\Delta=[0,\infty]
. More specifically, the integral we want to perform is:

\displaystyle I:=\left<r(y)\right>_{{\cal N}(y;y_{0},v)}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Delta}{\rm d}y\,{\cal N}(y;y_{0},v)r(y)=

(46)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi v)^{1/2}}\int_{\Delta}{\rm d}y\,e^{-f(y)}

(47)

\displaystyle f(y)

\displaystyle:=\frac{(y-y_{0})^{2}}{2v}-\ln(r(y))

(48)

\displaystyle M

\displaystyle:=v^{-1}.

(49)

Let us Taylor-expand around
y_{0}
:
f(y)=-\ln(r(y_{0}))-(y-y_{0})\,g_{1}(y_{0})+(1/2)(y-y_{0})^{2}[M-g^{\prime}_{1}(y_{0})]+O[(y-y_{0}^{3})]
, where we have defined
g_{1}(y):=r^{\prime}(y)/r(y)
and
M:=1/v
and where

is the big-o notation. Hence:

\displaystyle I

\displaystyle=r(y_{0})\int_{\Delta}{\rm d}y\,\exp{\left(-\frac{1}{2}(y-y_{0})^{2}[M-g_{1}^{\prime}(y_{0})]+(y-y_{0})g_{1}(y_{0})+O[(y-y_{0})^{3}]\right)}.

(50)

From now on, we will consider
M
to be large. Values of

such that

are exponentially suppressed in the integral. If
M
is large enough, we can consequently neglect the terms of order
(y-y_{0})^{3}
in front of those of order
(y-y_{0})
and
(y-y_{0})^{2}
(even those that are not proportional to
M
). Suppose that this is the case, that
O[(y-y_{0})^{3}]
can be neglected in the exponential of (
50
). This amounts to approximate
\ln r
by its Taylor series up to order two in the relevant interval of length
M^{-1/2}
. If, moreover,
y_{0}\gg M^{-1/2}
(or if
y_{0}>0
, and
M

[0,\infty]
by
[-\infty,\infty]
. Summarising:

\displaystyle I

\displaystyle\simeq r(y_{0})\frac{1}{(2\pi v)^{1/2}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}{\rm d}z\,\exp{\left(-\frac{1}{2}z^{2}[M-g_{1}^{\prime}(y_{0})]+zg_{1}(y_{0})]\right)}\qquad\text{large $M$ and $y_{0}>0$},

(51)

where we have changed the integration variable
z=y-y_{0}
.
Under these assumptions, we can use Gaussian formulae in (
51
) and get:

\displaystyle I

\displaystyle\simeq r(y_{0})\left(\frac{1}{1-M^{-1}g_{1}^{\prime}(y_{0})}\right)^{1/2}\exp{\left(\frac{1}{2}\frac{M^{-1}g_{1}^{2}(y_{0})}{1-M^{-1}g_{1}^{\prime}(y_{0})}\right)}.

(52)

Notice that, if we take the
M\to\infty
limit in eq.
52
, we obtain
I\simeq r(y_{0})
, which is what we obtain approximating our

in Eq. (
46
) using the standard saddle-point equation (where
\sf f
is a generic function):

\displaystyle\int\,r\,\exp(M{{\sf f}})\simeq r(y_{0})(2\pi)^{1/2}(M|{\sf f}^{\prime\prime}(y_{0})|)^{-1/2}\exp(M{\sf f}(y_{0})).

(53)

Now, substituting
r
by
{r_{\rm w}}
,
y_{0}
by
{\mu_{\rm I}}
, and
v
by
{v_{\rm c}}
in Eq. (

), we get the quenched-noise approximation for the mean rate:

\displaystyle{r_{\rm q}}({\mu_{\rm I}})

\displaystyle={r_{\rm w}}({\mu_{\rm I}})\left[\frac{1}{1-{v_{\rm c}}g^{\prime}_{1}({\mu_{\rm I}})}\right]^{1/2}\exp\left(\frac{{v_{\rm c}}}{2}\frac{g_{1}^{2}({\mu_{\rm I}})}{1-{v_{\rm c}}g^{\prime}_{1}({\mu_{\rm I}})}\right)

(54)

where the function
g_{1}(\mu)
and its derivative
g^{\prime}_{1}(\mu)
depend on the white LIF parameters only:

\displaystyle g_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{{r_{\rm w}}^{\prime}}{{r_{\rm w}}}=-\frac{{t_{\rm w}}^{\prime}}{{t_{\rm w}}}

(55)

\displaystyle g_{1}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=-\frac{{t_{\rm w}}^{\prime\prime}{t_{\rm w}}-{{t_{\rm w}}^{\prime}}^{2}}{{t_{\rm w}}^{2}}

(56)

We report the explicit expressions for the derivatives of the Siegert mean ISI:

\displaystyle{t_{\rm w}}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\pi^{1/2}{\tau_{\rm m}}^{3/2}}{{\sigma_{\rm I}}}\left[\phi(\Theta)-\phi(H)\right]

(58a)

\displaystyle{t_{\rm w}}^{\prime\prime}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\pi^{1/2}{\tau_{\rm m}}^{2}}{{\sigma_{\rm I}}^{2}}\left[\Theta\phi(\Theta)-H\phi(H)\right]

(58b)

where
\Theta,H
denote the functions in Eq. (

E.3
Relation with asymptotic integral expansions

Taylor-expanding
r
around
y_{0}
within the integrand in (

) would lead to the assymptotic integral expansion:

\displaystyle I

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{2\pi v^{2}}\right)^{1/2}\int_{\Delta}{\rm d}y\,\left(\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}(y-y_{0})^{m}\frac{r^{{}^{\prime}m}(y_{0})}{m!}\right){\cal N}(y;y_{0},v)=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{r^{{}^{\prime}m}(y_{0})}{m!}\left<(y-y_{0})^{m}\right>_{{\cal N}(y;y_{0},v)}=

(59)

\displaystyle\simeq\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{r^{{}^{\prime}2n}(y_{0})}{(2n)!}v^{n}

(60)

where

means the

-th order derivative. The last equation is an approximation, because we have approximated the integrating interval

by

-th order of the assymptotic expansion in

up to first order in

E.4
Formal mathematical proof

Let
g
and
h
be two functions, twice continuous and differentiable in an interval
\Delta
in the positive real line, that can also be unbounded. Now, suppose that
g
has a single absolute maximum at
x_{0}\in\Delta
, and it is
g^{\prime\prime}_{0}:=g^{\prime\prime}(x_{0})<0
. We want to evaluate the integral

\displaystyle I_{M}=\int_{\Delta}{\rm d}x\,e^{Mg(x)+h(x)}

(62)

The standard saddle point approximation is:

\displaystyle I^{\rm(std)}_{M}=e^{Mg_{0}+h_{0}}\left(\frac{2\pi}{M|g_{0}^{\prime\prime}|}\right)^{1/2}

(63)

and one can easily proof that

\displaystyle\lim_{M\to\infty}\frac{I_{M}}{I^{\rm(std)}_{M}}=1.

(64)

Let us consider the alternative approximation:

\displaystyle I^{\rm(alt)}_{M}=e^{Mg_{0}+h_{0}}\left(\frac{2\pi}{M|g_{0}^{\prime\prime}|-h_{0}^{\prime\prime}}\right)^{1/2}\exp{\left(\frac{1}{2}\frac{M^{-1}{h^{\prime}_{0}}^{2}}{|g_{0}^{\prime\prime}|-M^{-1}h_{0}^{\prime\prime}}\right)}.

(65)

It is easy to proof that it also satisfies:

\displaystyle\lim_{M\to\infty}\frac{I_{M}}{I^{\rm(alt)}_{M}}=1

(66)

but it is less immediate to see that it is actually a better approximation than
I^{\rm(std)}_{M}
. Indeed, it is:

\displaystyle\lim_{M\to\infty}\frac{I_{M}-I^{\rm(alt)}_{M}}{I_{M}-I^{\rm(std)}_{M}}=1

(67)

and not
<1
, as one could initially have expected! The reason is that the alternative approximation is a correction for large, but not infinite values of
M
. Rather, we want to proof that the quotient above is
\leq 1

for large enough
M
. The proposition to be demonstrated is the following. Given
g,h,\Delta,x_{0}
, there is some
\tilde{M}
above which it is

\displaystyle\left|\frac{I_{M}-I^{\rm(alt)}_{M}}{I_{M}-I^{\rm(std)}_{M}}\right|\leq 1\qquad\forall M>\tilde{M}.

(68)

Let us see how this could be proven. We need some preliminary propositions.

Lower and upper bounds.
We follow the demonstration in
[
81
]
. First, we are able to write analytical expressions for lower and upper bounds to
I_{M}
, that will be referred to as
I_{M}^{{\rm lb}(\epsilon)},I_{M}^{{\rm ub}(\epsilon)}
respectively, both depending on a real parameter
\epsilon>0
. For the lower bound, one increases by
\epsilon
the curvatures of
g
and
f
, in such a way that
\forall\epsilon>0,\exists\,\delta>0
such that
Mg(x)+h(x)\geq Mg_{0}-M(|g^{\prime\prime}(x)|+\epsilon)\delta_{x}^{2}/2+h_{0}+h^{\prime}_{0}\delta_{x}+(h^{\prime\prime}_{0}-\epsilon)\delta_{x}^{2}/2
for all
x
in the interval
|\delta_{x}|<\delta
. As a consequence:

\displaystyle I_{M}

\displaystyle\geq\int_{|\delta_{x}|<\delta}{\rm d}x\,e^{Mg(x)+h(x)}\geq I_{M}^{{\rm lb}(\epsilon)}

(69)

\displaystyle I_{M}^{{\rm lb}(\epsilon)}

\displaystyle:=e^{Mg_{0}+h_{0}}\int_{-\delta M^{1/2}}^{\delta M^{1/2}}{\rm d}y\,e^{-y^{2}(|g_{0}^{\prime\prime}|+\epsilon)/2+y^{2}M^{-1}(h^{\prime\prime}_{0}-\epsilon)/2+yM^{-1/2}h_{0}^{\prime}}.

(70)

For the upper bound, we decrease the curvature by a quantity
\epsilon>0
, which is small enough so that the increased curvature is still lower than zero,
-g^{\prime\prime}_{0}+\epsilon<0
. By continuity of
g^{\prime\prime}
and the Taylor theorem, for each of such
\epsilon
’s
\exists\,\delta>0
such that

\displaystyle Mg(x)+h(x)\leq M(g_{0}-(|g^{\prime\prime}_{0}|-\epsilon)\delta_{x}^{2}/2)+h_{0}+h^{\prime}_{0}\delta_{x}+(h^{\prime\prime}_{0}+\epsilon)\delta_{x}^{2}/2

(71)

for all
x
such that
|\delta_{x}|<\delta
. Now, let
M^{*}
be a value for which the integral

\displaystyle\tilde{I}:=\int_{\Delta}{\rm d}x\,e^{M^{*}g(x)+h(x)}<\infty

(72)

is finite. Hence, given
\epsilon
and
\delta
, there exist a
\eta>0
such that
Mg(x)\leq M(g_{0}-\eta)
in all the points
|\delta_{x}|>\delta
outside the interval
(-\delta+x_{0},x_{0}+\delta)
. Therefore,

\displaystyle I_{M}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{|\delta_{x}|>\delta}{\rm d}x\,e^{Mg(x)+h(x)}+

\displaystyle\int_{|\delta_{x}|<\delta}{\rm d}x\,e^{Mg(x)+h(x)}\leq

(73)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{|\delta_{x}|>\delta}{\rm d}x\,e^{M^{*}g(x)}e^{(M-M^{*})(g_{0}-\eta)+h(x)}+

\displaystyle\int_{|\delta_{x}|<\delta}{\rm d}x\,e^{Mg(x)+h(x)}\leq

(74)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle e^{(M-M^{*})(g_{0}-\eta)}\tilde{I}+

\displaystyle\int_{|\delta_{x}|<\delta}{\rm d}x\,e^{Mg(x)+h(x)}\leq

(75)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle e^{(M-M^{*})(g_{0}-\eta)}\tilde{I}+

\displaystyle e^{Mg_{0}+h_{0}}\int_{|\delta_{x}|<\delta}{\rm d}x\,e^{-M(|g^{\prime\prime}_{0}|-\epsilon)\delta_{x}^{2}/2+\delta_{x}h^{\prime}_{0}+(h^{\prime\prime}_{0}+\epsilon)\delta_{x}^{2}/2}\leq

(76)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle e^{(M-M^{*})(g_{0}-\eta)}\tilde{I}+

\displaystyle e^{Mg_{0}+h_{0}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}{\rm d}x\,e^{-M(|g^{\prime\prime}_{0}|-\epsilon)\delta_{x}^{2}/2+h_{0}+\delta_{x}h^{\prime}_{0}+(h^{\prime\prime}_{0}+\epsilon)\delta_{x}^{2}/2},

(77)

or, using Gaussian formulae:

\displaystyle I_{M}\leq I_{M}^{{\rm ub}(\epsilon)}=e^{(M-M^{*})(g_{0}-\eta)}\tilde{I}+e^{Mg_{0}+h_{0}}\left(\frac{2\pi}{M(|g^{\prime\prime}_{0}|-\epsilon)-(h_{0}^{\prime\prime}+\epsilon)}\right)^{1/2}\exp{\left(\frac{1}{2}\frac{{h^{\prime}_{0}}^{2}}{M(|g_{0}^{\prime\prime}|-\epsilon)-(h^{\prime\prime}_{0}+\epsilon)}\right)}.

(78)

The strategy to demonstrate the proposition in (
68
) is to examine, for large
M
, the following lower and upper bounds

\displaystyle q_{M}^{{\rm u}(\epsilon)}

\displaystyle:=\frac{I_{M}^{{\rm ub}(\epsilon)}-I^{\rm(alt)}_{M}}{I_{M}^{{\rm lb}(\epsilon)}-I^{\rm(std)}_{M}}

(79)

\displaystyle q_{M}^{{\rm l}(\epsilon)}

\displaystyle:=\frac{I_{M}^{{\rm lb}(\epsilon)}-I^{\rm(alt)}_{M}}{I_{M}^{{\rm ub}(\epsilon)}-I^{\rm(std)}_{M}}

(80)

for the ratio:

\displaystyle q_{M}^{{\rm l}(\epsilon)}\leq\frac{I_{M}-I^{\rm(alt)}_{M}}{I_{M}-I^{\rm(std)}_{M}}\leq q_{M}^{{\rm u}(\epsilon)}\qquad\forall\epsilon.

(81)

We demonstrate that
q_{M}^{{\rm u}(\epsilon)}\leq 1
and that
q_{M}^{{\rm l}(\epsilon)}\geq-1
for large enough
M
. Let us consider
q_{M}^{{\rm u}(\epsilon)}
. It is easy to see that:

\displaystyle q_{M}^{{\rm u}(\epsilon)}=\frac{e^{-\eta M}\,u\,\tilde{I}+\left(\frac{2\pi}{M(|g^{\prime\prime}_{0}|-\epsilon)-(h_{0}^{\prime\prime}+\epsilon)}\right)^{1/2}e^{\frac{1}{2}\frac{{h^{\prime}_{0}}^{2}}{M(|g_{0}^{\prime\prime}|-\epsilon)-(h_{0}^{\prime\prime}+\epsilon)}}-\left(\frac{2\pi}{M|g_{0}^{\prime\prime}|-h_{0}^{\prime\prime}}\right)^{1/2}e^{\frac{1}{2}\frac{{h^{\prime}_{0}}^{2}}{M|g_{0}^{\prime\prime}|-h_{0}^{\prime\prime}}}}{\int_{-\delta M^{1/2}}^{\delta M^{1/2}}{\rm d}y\,e^{-\frac{y^{2}}{2}(|g_{0}^{\prime\prime}|+\epsilon-M^{-1}(h_{0}^{\prime\prime}-\epsilon))+yM^{-1/2}h_{0}^{\prime}}-\left(\frac{2\pi}{M|g_{0}^{\prime\prime}|}\right)^{1/2}.}

(82)

where
u:=\exp[-M^{*}(g_{0}-\eta)-h_{0}]
. It is instructive to see what happens if we take first the
M\to\infty
limit of this expression:

\displaystyle\lim_{M\to\infty}\frac{I_{M}^{{\rm ub}(\epsilon)}-I^{\rm(alt)}_{M}}{I_{M}^{{\rm lb}(\epsilon)}-I^{\rm(std)}_{M}}=\frac{(|g_{0}^{\prime\prime}|-\epsilon)^{-1/2}-(|g_{0}^{\prime\prime}|)^{-1/2}}{(|g_{0}^{\prime\prime}|+\epsilon)^{-1/2}-(|g_{0}^{\prime\prime}|)^{-1/2}}.

(83)

Our best estimators for
I_{M}
are recovered in the
\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}
, which gives

\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\lim_{M\to\infty}\frac{I_{M}^{{\rm ub}(\epsilon)}-I^{\rm(alt)}_{M}}{I_{M}^{{\rm lb}(\epsilon)}-I^{\rm(std)}_{M}}=1.

(84)

Now, it is clear what is happening: in the
\lim_{M\to\infty}
our corrections to the standard Laplace method (i.e., the differences between
I^{({\rm alt})}
and
I^{({\rm std})}
) vanish. Let us now consider the ratio in (
82
), and suppose that
M
is so large that one can neglect the exponentially decaying term
\exp{(-\eta M)}
in the numerator, and that we can substitute the integral in the denominator by the same integral with the
-\infty,\infty
limits (the differences between both decay very fast as the complementary error function
\rm erfc
in
\delta M^{1/2}
, whose leading term is
\sim\delta M^{1/2}\exp(\delta^{2}M)
). Indeed, these are the only two terms decaying exponentially in
M
. We have:

\displaystyle\frac{I_{M}^{{\rm ub}(\epsilon)}-I^{\rm(alt)}_{M}}{I_{M}^{{\rm lb}(\epsilon)}-I^{\rm(std)}_{M}}\simeq\frac{\left(\frac{2\pi}{M(|g^{\prime\prime}_{0}|-\epsilon)-(h_{0}^{\prime\prime}+\epsilon)}\right)^{1/2}e^{\frac{1}{2}\frac{{h^{\prime}_{0}}^{2}}{M(|g_{0}^{\prime\prime}|-\epsilon)-(h_{0}^{\prime\prime}+\epsilon)}}-\left(\frac{2\pi}{M|g_{0}^{\prime\prime}|-h_{0}^{\prime\prime}}\right)^{1/2}e^{\frac{1}{2}\frac{{h^{\prime}_{0}}^{2}}{M|g_{0}^{\prime\prime}|-h_{0}^{\prime\prime}}}}{\left(\frac{2\pi}{M(|g^{\prime\prime}_{0}|+\epsilon)-(h_{0}^{\prime\prime}-\epsilon)}\right)^{1/2}e^{\frac{1}{2}\frac{{h^{\prime}_{0}}^{2}}{M(|g_{0}^{\prime\prime}|+\epsilon)-(h_{0}^{\prime\prime}-\epsilon)}}-\left(\frac{2\pi}{M|g_{0}^{\prime\prime}|}\right)^{1/2}}\qquad\text{large $M$}.

(85)

Mind that, as far as
h^{\prime}_{0}\neq 0
or
h^{\prime\prime}_{0}\neq 0
, this ratio is lower than one in absolute value, because the difference between both terms in the numerator is lower in absolute value than the difference between both terms in the denominator (and it would vanish for
\epsilon=0
). Notice that, given
M
,
\epsilon
cannot be arbitrary low for (
85
) to be a good approximation of (
82
): it has to be
\epsilon
(and, consequently,
\delta
and
\eta
in (
82
)) large enough so that we can neglect
\exp{(-\eta M)}
and substitute
\int_{-\delta M^{1/2}}^{\delta M^{1/2}}
by
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}
. The larger
M
, the lower we can take
\epsilon
, and the better is the estimation in (
85
). However, it is not necessary that
\epsilon
is very small. The only thing we need is
M
large enough for the upper bound (
85
) to be
\leq 1
. The same argument can be applied to
q_{M}^{{\rm l}(\epsilon)}\geq-1
, and this leads us to Eq. (
68
).

Analytical expression for the rate variance in the quenched-noise approximation

H.1
Sketch of the derivation of the temporal distribution function in the quenched-noise approximation

The quenched-noise approximation to
g
is as follows. Let
n_{j}(\ell)
be the number of LIF spikes in the time interval
[t_{j},t_{j+1}]
where
t_{j}:=j\ell
and
j
is an integer in
[0,L={\rm int}({T_{\rm c}}/\ell)]
. We define the
discrete-time temporal distribution function
in the quenched-noise approximation,
{g_{\rm q}}
, as:

\displaystyle{r_{\rm q}}\,{g_{\rm q}}\left(t_{j}-t_{j^{\prime}}\right)=\frac{1}{\ell^{2}}\left<\left<n_{j}(\ell)n_{j^{\prime}}(\ell)\right>_{{\bf n}|{\bm{\mu}}}\right>_{{\bm{\mu}}}

(86)

where the expected values are, respectively: with respect to the white-LIF neuron probability density
\text{prob}({\bf n}|{\bm{\tilde{\mu}}})
of sequences
(n_{1}(\ell),\ldots,n_{L}(\ell))
of
L
spike counts, given the sequence of

(\tilde{\mu}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{\mu}_{L})

\text{prob}({\bm{\tilde{\mu}}})

in resolution timescales

{\tau_{\rm m}}\ll\ell\ll{\tau_{\rm c}}\ll{T_{\rm c}}

The first hypothesis in our calculation consists in the factorisation of the inner correlation:

\xi

\displaystyle P(\mu,t|\mu^{\prime},t^{\prime})

\displaystyle={\cal N}\left(\mu;{\mu_{\rm I}}+\omega\mu^{\prime},{v_{\rm c}}(1-\omega^{2})\right)

\displaystyle\omega

\displaystyle:=e^{-|t-t^{\prime}|/{\tau_{\rm c}}}.

\displaystyle\left<{r_{\rm w}}(\mu_{t^{\prime}})\left<{r_{\rm w}}(\mu_{t})\right>_{P(\mu_{t}|\mu_{t^{\prime}})}\right>_{P(\mu_{t^{\prime}})},

Appendix I
Hand-waving estimation for the rate variance in the quenched-noise approximation

I.1
Hand-waving ansatz for the temporal distribution function

We here present a hand-waving argument, consistent with the quenched-noise approximation, that leads to an alternative analytic expression for the rate variance of the LIF model with white+colored noise input. Interestingly, the hand-waving approximation for
{v_{\rm R}}
and
g
captures relatively well the behavior of the numeric estimation of these quantities, but
worst than the full quenched-noise calculation in Sec.
H
, of which the hand-waving approximation is a further simplification
(please, see Figs.
7
,
8
).

The approximation stands on a simple ansatz for the form of the temporal distribution function
g(t)=\left<{\varrho}(t^{\prime}){\varrho}(t^{\prime}+t)\right>/{r_{\rm R}}
of the white+colored noise LIF. We suppose it to be of the same functional form
a+b\,e^{-|t|/{\tau_{\rm c}}}
of the
encoding temporal distribution function
(see Eq. (
23
) in Sec.
B
):
g_{\rm en}(t)=\left<I(t^{\prime})I(t^{\prime}+t)\right>/\left<I(t^{\prime})\right>=({v_{\rm c}}/{\mu_{\rm I}})e^{-|t|/{\tau_{\rm c}}}+{\mu_{\rm I}}
. The ansatz is:

\displaystyle{g_{\rm q}}^{(\text{hand})}(t)=\frac{{u_{\rm q}}}{{r_{\rm q}}}e^{-|t|/{\tau_{\rm c}}}+{r_{\rm q}},

(107)

where the coefficients
a={r_{\rm q}}
,
b={u_{\rm q}}/{r_{\rm q}}
are fixed so that the ansatz is consistent with the quenched-noise approximation. The emerging stationary variance
C_{-}(0)={r_{\rm q}}{g_{\rm q}}^{(\text{hand})}(0)-{r_{\rm q}}^{2}
is, indeed, the stationary variance according to the quenched-noise approximation (in analogy with
{r_{\rm q}}
in Eq. (
45
) in Sec.
E
):

\displaystyle{u_{\rm q}}({\mu_{\rm I}}):=

\displaystyle\left<{r_{\rm w}}(\tilde{\mu})^{2}\right>_{{\cal N}(\tilde{\mu}|\mu_{I},{v_{\rm c}})}-{r_{\rm q}}^{2}.

(108)

A straightforward calculation in the saddle-point approximation, completely analogous to that in Sec.
E
, leads to:

\displaystyle{u_{\rm q}}({\mu_{\rm I}})+{r_{\rm q}}^{2}({\mu_{\rm I}})

\displaystyle={r_{\rm w}}({\mu_{\rm I}})^{2}\left[\frac{1}{1-2{v_{\rm c}}g^{\prime}_{1}({\mu_{\rm I}})}\right]^{1/2}\exp\left(\frac{2{v_{\rm c}}g_{1}^{2}({\mu_{\rm I}})}{1-2{v_{\rm c}}g^{\prime}_{1}({\mu_{\rm I}})}\right).

(109)

We can now compute the
\text{Var}(\text{rate})
associated to our
{g_{\rm q}}^{(\text{hand})}
in Eq. (
107
), using the relation between
\text{Var}(\text{rate})
and
C_{-}
in Eq. (
11
), and taking
{r_{\rm q}}
as the amplitude of the singular part of
C(t)={r_{\rm q}}\delta(t)+C_{-}(t)
, with
C_{-}(t)={r_{\rm q}}{g_{\rm q}}^{(\text{hand})}(t)-{r_{\rm q}}^{2}
. The result is our hand-waving expression for the rate variance:

\displaystyle{\rm Var}\left[\frac{{n_{\rm R}}({T_{\rm c}})}{{T_{\rm c}}}\right]

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{{r_{\rm q}}}{{T_{\rm c}}}+\frac{2{\tau_{\rm c}}}{{T_{\rm c}}}\,{u_{\rm q}}.

(110)

I.2
The variance of step-inhomogeneous Poisson processes

We will now see that Eq. (
110
) can be found in an alternative, easily interpretable manner. In Eq. (
110
), the first term corresponds to the variance of the rate
of a homogeneous Poisson process whose firing rate is equal to the firing rate in the quenched-noise approximation
. The second term in Eq. (
110
) accounts for the inhomogeneity of the rate of the Poisson process. Overall, Eq. (
110
) corresponds to the variance of
an inhomogeneous Poisson process such that the firing rate is constant in time windows of length
2{\tau_{\rm c}}
and, within each window, it assumes a constant, stochastic value
r_{\mu}:={r_{\rm w}}(\mu)
, where
\mu
is independently sampled from the stationary probability distribution
{\cal N}(\mu;\mu_{I},v_{\rm c})
in the quenched approximation
.

In other words, our hand-waving approximation to the variance of our white+colored-noise input LIF neuron for
{\tau_{\rm c}}\gg{\tau_{\rm m}}
in Eq.
110
, can be seen to emerge as well under the following set of assumptions:

1.

The rate variance
{\rm Var}[{n_{\rm R}}({T_{\rm c}})/{T_{\rm c}}]

of the white-noise LIF neuron with
{\sigma_{\rm c}}=0
and input mean
\mu
can be well approximated with the variance of a Poisson process with the corresponding firing rate
{\rm Var}[{n_{\rm R}}({T_{\rm c}})/{T_{\rm c}}]\simeq{r_{\rm w}}(\mu)/{T_{\rm c}}
. This assumption (also adopted in the above derivation of Eq. (
110
)) is approximately satisfied in the parameter region considered in our analyses. Indeed, for null or low enough
{\sigma_{\rm c}}
, the variance deviations from Poisson are of the same order of the numerical estimation uncertainty of
{v_{\rm R}}
, or lower, see Fig.
8
.

2.

The dynamics of the input current
I
can be approximated with a process in which
I
is constant in time intervals of size
{T_{\rm s}}=2{\tau_{\rm c}}
, and independently sampled across different intervals, from its stationary distribution. We assume, in other words, that the encoding current correlation functions can be approximated as:
\text{Cov}\left(I(t^{\prime}),I(t^{\prime}+t)\right)={v_{\rm c}}\,\text{Heav}(2{\tau_{\rm c}}-t)
, where
Heav
is the Heaviside function.

3.

Within time windows of size as large as
2{\tau_{\rm c}}
, the LIF effectively behaves as a white-noise LIF with
constant input current

\mu
, sampled from its stationary distribution according to the quenched-noise approximation.

Let us derive Eq. (
110
) under these assumptions. Let the time interval of length
T
be divided into
M
small intervals of length
{T_{\rm s}}=T/M
so that
r_{\mu}
is constant in such smaller intervals. The number of spikes
n(T)
is, in the large interval of length
T
, a stochastic variable that can be expressed as
n(T)=\sum_{m=1}^{M}n_{m}({T_{\rm s}})
, where
n_{m}({T_{\rm s}}):=n\left(m{T_{\rm s}}\right)-n\left((m-1){T_{\rm s}}\right)
is the number of spikes in the
m
-th small interval. Now, let the
n_{m}({T_{\rm s}})
’s be independent and identically distributed, and that their average and variance be finite. Then, the Central Limit Theorem wants, for sufficiently large
M
, the average rate
n(T)/T
to exhibit mean and variance as follows:

\displaystyle\left<\frac{n(T)}{M}\right>

\displaystyle\simeq\left<n_{m}({T_{\rm s}})\right>

(111)

\displaystyle{\rm Var}\left[\frac{n(T)}{M}\right]

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{1}{M}{\rm Var}\left[n_{m}({T_{\rm s}})\right]

(112)

where expectation values and variances refer to the annealed probability distributions of both the realizations of the Poisson spikes given
r_{\mu}
, and of the different instances of
r_{\mu}
. This is equivalent to suppose that one first samples
M
independent
\mu\sim{\cal N}(\cdot;\mu_{I},v_{\rm c})
and, for each of such
\mu
’s, one samples
n_{m}({T_{\rm s}})\sim{\rm Poisson}(\cdot;{T_{\rm s}}r_{\mu})
.

The average of the single
n_{m}
’s is:

\displaystyle\left<n_{m}({T_{\rm s}})\right>

\displaystyle=\left<\left<n_{m}({T_{\rm s}})\right>_{{\rm P}|\mu}\right>_{\mu}={T_{\rm s}}\bar{r}

(113)

\displaystyle\bar{r}

\displaystyle:=\left<r_{\mu}\right>_{\mu}

(114)

where we have used that the average over the joint probabilities on
n_{m}
’s and
\mu
’s is
\left<\cdot\right>=\left<\left<\cdot\right>_{{{\rm P}|\mu}}\right>_{\mu}
, where
\left<\cdot\right>_{{\rm P}|\mu}
refers to the expectation over the conditional probability
{\rm Poisson}(\cdot;{T_{\rm s}}r_{\mu})
, and
\left<\cdot\right>_{\mu}
the expectation according to
{\cal N}(\cdot;\mu_{I},v_{\rm c})
. In the above online equation, the identity
\left<n_{m}({T_{\rm s}})\right>_{{\rm P}|\mu}=r_{\mu}
is just the equation for the average of
{\rm Poisson}(\cdot;{T_{\rm s}}r_{\mu})
.

The variance of the single
n_{m}
’s is:

\displaystyle{\rm Var}\left[n_{m}({T_{\rm s}})\right]

\displaystyle=\left<\left<\left(n_{m}({T_{\rm s}})-{T_{\rm s}}\bar{r}\right)^{2}\right>_{{\rm P}|\mu}\right>_{\mu}

(115)

where we have used Eq. (
113
)
\left<n_{m}({T_{\rm s}})\right>={T_{\rm s}}\bar{r}
. Now, sum and subtract
{T_{\rm s}}r_{\mu}
within the parentheses, to get:

\displaystyle{\rm Var}\left[n_{m}({T_{\rm s}})\right]

\displaystyle=\left<\left<\left(n_{m}({T_{\rm s}})-{T_{\rm s}}r_{\mu}\right)^{2}\right>_{{\rm P}|\mu}\right>_{\mu}+{T_{\rm s}}^{2}\left<\left(\bar{r}-r_{\mu}\right)^{2}\right>_{\mu}

(116)

where we have used again Eq. (
113
). Using the equation for the variance of the Poisson distribution
\left<\left(n_{m}({T_{\rm s}})-{T_{\rm s}}r_{\mu}\right)^{2}\right>_{{\rm P}|\mu}={T_{\rm s}}r_{\mu}
, we finally get:

\displaystyle{\rm Var}\left[n_{m}({T_{\rm s}})\right]

\displaystyle={T_{\rm s}}\bar{r}+{T_{\rm s}}^{2}\,{\rm Var}_{\mu}\left[r_{\mu}\right].

(117)

Now, we are interested in the variance of the rate
n(T)/T
. It is
{\rm Var}[n(T)/T]=(M^{2}/T^{2}){\rm Var}[n(T)/M]
and, using the Central Limit Theorem argument in Eq. (
112
):

\displaystyle{\rm Var}\left[\frac{n(T)}{T}\right]

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{\bar{r}}{T}+\frac{{T_{\rm s}}}{T}\,{\rm Var}_{\mu}\left[r_{\mu}\right]

(118)

Now, taking
{T_{\rm s}}=2{\tau_{\rm c}}
we get Eq. (
110
).

Appendix J
Necessary conditions for the SNR enhancement

An upper bound in
{\mu_{\rm I}^{(+)}}
for the SNR enhancement to occur.
We will now argue that, since the SNR numerator is
\Delta{r_{\rm R}}\leq{r_{\rm R}}({\mu_{\rm I}}^{(+)})
, a necessary condition for the SNR to increase with the noise correlation strength is
{\mu_{\rm I}}^{(+)}<{\mu_{\rm I}}^{*}
. We will adopt the following double delta notation:
\delta
for the increment when introducing the colored noise,
{\alpha_{\rm V}}>0
, while
\Delta
for the
s=\pm
change, with
\Delta{\mu_{\rm I}}={\mu_{\rm I}^{(+)}}-{\mu_{\rm I}^{(-)}}>0
. We focus in the regime when the white-noise LIF response curve
{r_{\rm w}}
is convex, for low enough
{\mu_{\rm I}}<{\mu_{\rm I}}^{*}
, since it is the only one in which one observes a
\delta({\rm SNR})>0
. As we have argued above, in this regime it is
\delta{r_{\rm R}}>0
(we can understand this fact thanks to the quenched-noise approximation, at least when
{\tau_{\rm c}}\gtrsim{\tau_{\rm m}}
). Moreover, we also know (again thanks to the quenched-noise hypothesis) that the increment in the SNR denominator in the presence of colored noise is positive
\delta\left<{v_{\rm R}}^{(s)}\right>_{s}
, since both terms of
{v_{\rm R}}
in Eq. (
11
) increase with
{\alpha_{\rm V}}
. We conclude that, in the regime where one numerically observes
\delta({\rm SNR})>0
(
\mu
sufficiently lower than
\mu^{*}
), the condition
\delta({\rm SNR})>0
implies
\delta\Delta{r_{\rm R}}>0
. Indeed,
\delta({\rm SNR})>0
can be written as:

\displaystyle\frac{\Delta{r_{\rm R}}}{\left<{v_{\rm R}}\right>}>\frac{\Delta{r_{\rm w}}}{\left<{v_{\rm w}}\right>}

(119)

where
{r_{\rm R}},{r_{\rm w}}
refer, again, to the readout rate in the presence and absence of noise correlations, respectively, and where
\left<{v_{\rm R}}\right>:=({v_{\rm R}}^{(+)}+{v_{\rm R}}^{(-)})/2
. This translates into
\Delta{r_{\rm R}}>\Delta{r_{\rm w}}\left<{v_{\rm R}}\right>/\left<{v_{\rm w}}\right>>\Delta{r_{\rm w}}
(because, as we said before,
\delta{v_{\rm R}}>0
). We conclude that
\delta\Delta{r_{\rm R}}>0
. Thus, having
\delta\Delta{r_{\rm R}}>0
is a necessary condition for
\delta({\rm SNR})>0
. Moreover, since also
\delta{r_{\rm R}^{(-)}}>0
(because, in general,
\delta{r_{\rm R}}>0
), we also have that
\delta{r_{\rm R}^{(+)}}>0
is a necessary condition (since
\delta{r_{\rm R}^{(+)}}=\delta{r_{\rm R}^{(-)}}+\delta\Delta{r_{\rm R}}\geq\delta\Delta{r_{\rm R}}\geq 0
). Therefore,
\delta\Delta{r_{\rm R}}>0
and
\delta{r_{\rm R}^{(+)}}>0
are necessary conditions for
\delta({\rm SNR})>0
(which are not sufficient, since the denominator could increase too much). As a matter of fact,
\delta{r_{\rm R}^{(+)}}>0
implies
{\mu_{\rm I}^{(+)}}<{\mu_{\rm I}}^{*}
. First, because, in the light of the quenched-noise approximation, it must be
{\mu_{\rm I}}<{\mu_{\rm I}}^{*}
for
\delta{r_{\rm R}}>0
. Second, if we expand
{r_{\rm q}}
in Eq. (
10
) up to first order in
{v_{\rm c}}
, and we impose
{r_{\rm q}}={r_{\rm w}}
, we obtain, again, that the solution is
\mu^{*}
satisfying
g_{1}^{2}+g_{1}^{\prime}=0
. For larger values of
{v_{\rm c}}
, for which the
O[{v_{\rm c}}^{2}]
is non-negligible, the solution of
{r_{\rm q}}={r_{\rm w}}
occurs for lower values of
{\mu_{\rm I}}<{\mu_{\rm I}}^{*}
. We conclude that
{\mu_{\rm I}}<{\mu_{\rm I}}^{*}
is a necessary condition for the SNR to be enhanced. Indeed, this is what we observe in all the simulations in the article: the SNR enhancement occurs for
{\mu_{\rm I}}<{\mu_{\rm I}}^{*}
.

A lower upper bound in
{\mu_{\rm I}^{(+)}}
for the SNR enhancement to occur.
We can make the condition
{\mu_{\rm I}^{(+)}}\leq{\mu_{\rm I}}^{*}
more stringent by imposing that, beyond the numerator, the SNR itself should increase. This lower upper bound for
{\mu_{\rm I}^{(+)}}
is found by imposing the further condition that not only it has to be
\delta\Delta{r_{\rm R}}>0
, but
\delta({\rm SNR})>0
. First, we assume firing rates so low that, in the white-noise case for
{\alpha_{\rm V}}=0
, we can neglect the over-Poissonian deviations of
{r_{\rm R}}
(mind that, for the white-noise model, this is verified in almost all cases probed in this article in which
{\alpha_{\rm V}}=0
, c.f. Fig.
8
). Neglecting
{r_{\rm R}^{(-)}}
, and taking
{v_{\rm R}}{T_{\rm c}}\simeq{r_{\rm w}}
, the white-noise SNR is (we omit the
{T_{\rm c}}
proportionality factors)
{\rm SNR}_{\rm w}\simeq(2{r_{\rm w}})^{1/2}
. Now, take
{\rm SNR}_{\rm q}\simeq{r_{\rm q}}^{(+)}({v_{\rm q}}^{(+)}/2)^{-1/2}
(again neglecting
{r_{\rm R}^{(-)}}
), Taylor-expand our expression for
{r_{\rm q}}^{(+)}/{({v_{\rm q}}^{(+)})}^{1/2}
(taking
{v_{\rm q}}
from Eq. (
109
) in Appendix
I
), and stop at first order in
{v_{\rm c}}
. Equating
\text{SNR}_{\rm q}=\text{SNR}_{\rm w}
results in the transcendental equation
g_{1}^{\prime}+g_{1}^{2}=2{\tau_{\rm c}}{r_{\rm w}}g_{1}^{2}
, whose solution is dubbed
\hat{\mu_{\rm I}}
. The numerator
{r_{\rm R}}^{(+)}
and the denominator
{{v_{\rm R}}^{(+)}}^{1/2}
both increase with
{\sigma_{\rm c}}
, so that both effects cancel at first order in
{v_{\rm c}}
. In fact, to order
o[{v_{\rm c}}]
, the condition
\text{SNR}_{\rm q}=\text{SNR}_{\rm w}
translates into
g_{1}^{\prime}+g_{1}^{2}=2{\tau_{\rm c}}{r_{\rm w}}g_{1}^{2}
, and equation that
does not depend on
{v_{\rm c}}
. The same happens to its solution
\hat{\mu_{\rm I}}
(green line in Fig.
10
).

We conclude that
\delta(\text{SNR})>0
implies
{\mu_{\rm I}}^{(+)}<\hat{\mu_{\rm I}}
for low enough
{v_{\rm c}}
and
{\mu_{\rm I}^{(-)}}
(since we have neglected
{r_{\rm R}^{(-)}}
). This condition can therefore be taken as a necessary condition on
{\mu_{\rm I}}^{(+)}
for the SNR to increase in the presence of noise correlations, in the NERM, and for large enough
{\tau_{\rm c}}\gtrsim{\tau_{\rm m}}
, and low enough
{v_{\rm c}}
and
{\mu_{\rm I}^{(-)}}
.

While we expect the upper bound
{\mu_{\rm I}}^{*}
to hold always, the lower upper bound
\hat{\mu_{\rm I}}
is therefore expected to hold for low enough values of
{\mu_{\rm I}^{(-)}}
and
{v_{\rm c}}
. In Sec.
IV.3
we have mentioned that not only
{\mu_{\rm I}}<{\mu_{\rm I}}^{*}
, but even the more stringent condition
{\mu_{\rm I}}<\hat{\mu_{\rm I}}
is respected in all the figures in which we have evaluated the SNR. This is not very surprising, in the light of the fact that, for high enough values of
{\mu_{\rm I}^{(-)}}
and of
{v_{\rm c}}
, also the condition
\delta(\text{SNR})>0
tends to not be satisfied any more (as explained in Sec.
IV.3
). In practice, there are no cases, among those probed here, in which
\delta(\text{SNR})>0
and, at the same time, the conditions low
{\mu_{\rm I}^{(-)}}
and low
{v_{\rm c}}
are not satisfied. Therefore, the upper bound
\hat{\mu_{\rm I}}
tends to hold in all cases.

Appendix K
The encoding SNR

In Fig.
9
we compared the readout SNR with the
encoding
SNR, and noticed that the later may become lower than the former. This fact is consistent with the data processing inequality
[
82
]
, stating that, whenever
X\to Y\to Z
is a Markov chain, or (overloading the symbol
P
) whenever
P(X,Y,Z)=P(X)P(Y|X)P(Z|Y)
, their mutual information satisfy
I(\hat{X};\hat{Z})\leq I(\hat{X};\hat{Y})
. Let
X
be the stimulus
s
,
Y
be
the number of encoding spikes
(from any encoding neuron)
in the time interval
[0,{T_{\rm c}}]
,
n({T_{\rm c}})
, and
Z
be the number of readout spikes in
[0,{T_{\rm c}}]
,
{n_{\rm R}}({T_{\rm c}})
. First, the quantity that we have chosen to quantify the information carried by
X
and
Y
or
Z
is not the mutual information, but the encoding and readout signal-to-noise ratios (that, indeed, depend on the statistics of
Y|X
and of
Z|X
, respectively). But, most importantly, the readout variable
Z={n_{\rm R}}({T_{\rm c}})
does not depend on the encoding number of spikes only, but on the whole history of encoding density of spikes,
{\cal R}=(\rho(t))_{t=0}^{T_{\rm c}}
. In other words, if as variables
X,Y,Z
we chose
s,n({T_{\rm c}}),{n_{\rm R}}({T_{\rm c}})
, the data processing inequality does not apply, since
P(Z)
is not a function of
Y
only:
p(Z|Y)
does depend on
X
. Some information regarding the stimulus is actually encoded in the temporal fluctuations of the current
(\rho(t))_{t=0}^{T_{\rm c}}
, not only through the number of spikes
n({T_{\rm c}})=\int_{0}^{T_{\rm c}}{\rm d}t\,\rho(t)
.

Appendix L
Dependence on
{\tau_{\rm c}}
of the colored noise amplitude

We are interested in the dependence of the readout rate mean and variance,
{r_{\rm R}}^{(s)},{v_{\rm R}}^{(s)}
, on the encoding and decoding properties in the Valente model. In order to study the dependence on the correlation time
{\tau_{\rm c}}
, there are two possibilities (see references in
[
21
]
). The first one is to consider that
the stationary noise amplitude
{v_{\rm c}}
is constant
(where, throughout this section, by
constant
we mean
independent of
{\tau_{\rm c}}
), being
{\sigma_{\rm c}}=(2{\tau_{\rm c}}{v_{\rm c}})^{1/2}
consequently a function of
{\tau_{\rm c}}
. The second one is taking
{\sigma_{\rm c}}^{2}
constant (and, hence,
{\tau_{\rm c}}
-dependent stationary noise amplitude
{v_{\rm c}}={\sigma_{\rm c}}^{2}/(2{\tau_{\rm c}})
). The first scenario is suitable to address the
{\tau_{\rm c}}\to\infty
limit of our problem since, if
{v_{\rm c}}
is constant, the colored noise does have an influence for infinitely large
{\tau_{\rm c}}
(indeed,
\text{Cov}\left(I(t),I(t^{\prime})\right)={v_{\rm c}}
, see Eq. (
7
)), while for
{\tau_{\rm c}}\to 0
the coloured noise has no influence (indeed,
\text{Cov}\left(I(t),I(t^{\prime})\right)=0
). Oppositely, the constant-
{\sigma_{\rm c}}
scenario is the suitable one to consider the
{\tau_{\rm c}}\to 0
regime of the encoding-readout model, since for
{\tau_{\rm c}}\to 0
the colored noise term
{\sigma_{\rm c}}\xi(t)
reduces to a delta-correlated noise (
\text{Cov}\left(I(t)I(t^{\prime})\right)=({\sigma_{\rm I}}^{2}+{\sigma_{\rm c}}^{2})\delta(t-t^{\prime})
, see Eq. (
7
)), while for infinite
{\tau_{\rm c}}
it vanishes (see Table
11
).

In this work, we analyze the behavior of the readout neuron SNR, both according to the analytical and numerical solutions of the stochastic differential Eq. (
5
), for several values of the parameters. For the
{\tau_{\rm c}}
-dependency,
we consider constant stationary variance
{v_{\rm c}}
, since we are specially interested in the behavior of the SNR in the moderate and large-
{\tau_{\rm c}}
regimes.
The
{\tau_{\rm c}}\to 0
limit is less interesting in what, even in the case of constant
{\sigma_{\rm c}}
, the colored-noise LIF neuron behaves for
{\tau_{\rm c}}=0

as a white-noise LIF neuron
with white noise amplitude equal to
{\sigma_{\rm I}}^{2}+{\sigma_{\rm c}}^{2}
. In this situation, the readout SNR could increase in the presence of small-
{\tau_{\rm c}}
noise correlations, but not if one chooses, as a reference, the white-LIF readout with white-noise amplitude
={\sigma_{\rm I}}^{2}+{\sigma_{\rm c}}^{2}
. Oppositely, and as show in this article, for constant
{v_{\rm c}}
and large values of
{\tau_{\rm c}}
, the behavior of the colored-noise LIF is non-trivially different from that of the white-noise LIF.

We stress that setting a constant value for
{v_{\rm c}}
does not lead to a loss of generality, as it only determines the value of
{\sigma_{\rm c}}
that we probe when varying
{\tau_{\rm c}}
. Equation (
7
) implies that, the larger the value of
{\tau_{\rm c}}
, the larger must be
{\sigma_{\rm c}}
in order for the colored noise term to have a significant impact in
I(t)
, hence in the readout neuron statistics. Setting a constant value for
{v_{\rm c}}
accounts for this issue. The constant-
{\sigma_{\rm c}}
scenario is left to future studies.

Appendix M
Underestimation of the rate variance

As already mentioned, we do not employ, in the main text, the finite-NIS correction algorithm to the variance, since it leads to large errors in the SNR. Consequently, our estimations for
{v_{\rm R}}
are slightly underestimated. This is, however, not a serious issue since, as we said, the overestimations in the numerator and denominator of the SNR in Fig.
9
partially cancel. In Fig.
29
we report the SNR but, differently from Fig.
9
, we use the NIS-corrected estimation for the numerator,
\Delta{r_{\rm R}}
. In this situation, for our approximations to work well in the
{\tau_{\rm c}}\ll{\tau_{\rm m}}

the numerical estimation for
{r_{\rm R}}
(not the Moreno theoretical one)

{v_{\rm R}}=\tilde{r_{\rm R}}/{T_{\rm c}}
. This works, because the Poisson hypothesis
\tilde{r_{\rm R}}\simeq\tilde{v_{\rm R}}/{T_{\rm c}}
still holds for finite
{n_{\rm is}}
, when both
{r_{\rm R}}
,
{v_{\rm R}}
are underestimated. We consequently expect that the SNR in Fig.
29
is an overestimation of the SNR (since the numerator is NIS-corrected, while the denominator is underestimated), while the one in Fig.
9
is a more accurate estimation.
