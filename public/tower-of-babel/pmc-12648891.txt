Exploring the mediating effect of burnout between fear of COVID-19, job-related stress and turnover intention among elderly care workers
Authors: Zsanett Németh, Petra Deák, Alexandra Makai, Márta Hock
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe turnover intention of elderly care workers affects the quality and costs of social care, worker overload and associated health risks. Preventing the turnover and job-related stress of elderly care workers is essential to effectively support the elderly and ensure high-quality care. This study investigated the relationship between fear of COVID-19 and turnover intention, and job-related stress through burnout.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted in Hungary with 269 elderly care workers. Data collection tools included a self-designed demographic questionnaire, the Turnover Intention Scale (TIS-6), Maslach Burnout Inventory (MBI-HSS), Effort-Reward Imbalance (ERI) and Fear of COVID-19 Scale (FCV-19).ResultsIndirect effects of fear of COVID-19 were observed on the turnover intention (B = 0.092, p = .008), ER ratio (B = 0.009, p = .007), effort (B = 0.070, p = .007), and overcommitment (B = 0.093, p = .006) via emotional exhaustion. Additionally, fear of COVID-19 negatively influenced overcommitment (B = − 0.043, p = .017) through depersonalization. The models explained 26.3% of the variance in effort (R² = 0.263), 3.4% in reward (R² = 0.034), 23.9% in overcommitment (R² = 0.239), and 25.8% in the ER ratio (R² = 0.258).ConclusionsThe mediating role of emotional exhaustion and depersonalization in the relationship between pandemic-related fear and turnover intention and job-related stress underscores the need for targeted interventions in Hungary’s elderly care sector. These should focus on early detection of burnout symptoms, especially emotional exhaustion, and implement systematic strategies such as regular monitoring, stress management training, supportive supervision, and team-based peer support to mitigate the impact of chronic occupational stress.Trial registrationThe study protocol was registered on ClinicalTrials.gov before the start of the study, on 02/08/2022 (identifier: NCT05487157).Keywords: COVID-19, Turnover intention, Job-related stress, Burnout, Emotional exhaustion, Indirect effects

Background

Turnover intention and burnout

In 2023, Hungary’s elderly population (65+) reached 20.5%, with an ageing index of 141.5% [1]. Although population decline continues, dementia cases are projected to rise by 2050 [2, 3]. The growing demand for elderly care is particularly concerning given the significant decline in caregiver numbers: from 171,200 employed in social care in 2020 to 158,800 in 2022, with an average of 14,948 professionals in elderly care between 2020 and 2023 [4]. Identifying determinants of turnover intention is crucial, as it predicts actual turnover [5, 6]. High turnover rates among care workers are a global concern; preventing turnover is essential to meet increasing care demands and maintain care quality [7–9]. Worker turnover elevates recruitment and training costs, and increases workloads for remaining staff, heightening health and safety risks and amplifying job-related stress, which further drives turnover intentions [7, 10–12]. Beyond the immediate organizational consequences, the intention to leave should therefore be understood not merely as an individual choice, but more deeply, as an institutional structural and psychosocial challenge and a systemic indicator of the policy sustainability of long-term care organizations [13]. Burnout is also extensively studied in nursing and elderly care [14–16]. Burnout significantly predicts turnover intention and is more prevalent among those who leave [8, 17–19]. Elderly care facility staff report higher burnout rates than community-based workers [20]. Despite variability in strength, prior research consistently identifies burnout- particularly emotional exhaustion and depersonalization- as key predictors of turnover intention [21–23]. Burnout as an outcome, reflects prolonged exposure to stressors such as effort–reward imbalance, job strain, or emotional labor, with studies showing that higher stress levels lead to greater burnout [14, 16]. As a mediator, it explains how stressors translate into other consequences [24–26]. Each role has distinct implications: outcomes point to the need for prevention, predictors help identify at-risk groups, and mediators clarify the mechanisms linking variables. Addressing burnout and turnover intention among elderly care workers is thus a critical public health and policy priority to ensure workforce stability and the long-term quality and sustainability of elderly care services in the post-pandemic context [27]. Persistent emotional and physical demands, limited outcome visibility, and lower recognition relative to other healthcare sectors increase burnout risk [28–31]. These challenges are compounded by the emotionally taxing nature of the work, as staff provide daily support to residents in declining health and face constant exposure to suffering and loss [32–35].

The psychological impact of the COVID-19 pandemic

The COVID-19 pandemic has exacerbated these pre-existing vulnerabilities: long-term care workers have experienced chronic staffing shortages, high risk of infection, fear of infection, and deep emotional exhaustion, while witnessing the residents’ isolation and death [29, 36, 39]. Such working conditions contribute not only to burnout and job dissatisfaction but also to the broader instability of the long-term care workforce [13, 39–42].

Empirical evidence links fear of COVID-19 with moderate to high burnout levels, particularly emotional exhaustion and depersonalization [43, 44]. Heightened fear also correlated with anxiety about career prospects, job insecurity, and greater intent to leave [38, 40, 45–49]. Multiple studies confirm burnout mediates the relationship between fear of COVID-19 and turnover intention, highlighting how pandemic-related strain undermines workforce stability [16, 17, 24, 50–53]. Elevated workplace stress due to pandemic-induced uncertainty and fear significantly predicted burnout and turnover intention [15, 54, 55], exacerbating the ongoing crisis of burnout, turnover and job stress [17, 39, 51]. Moreover, a reciprocal causal relationship exists between job-related stress and burnout: higher stress contributes to burnout, which in turn amplifies stress levels [56–58].

Theoretical framework: the use of MBI-HSS and ERI models

The Effort-Reward Imbalance (ERI) model developed by Siegrist (1996) offers a relevant framework for understanding job-related stress in high-demand, emotionally intensive professions such as elderly care [59–61]. According to the model, stress arises from imbalance between employees’ invested effort and received rewards [60, 61]. Its core components -effort, reward, and overcommitment- produce an effort-reward ratio reflecting this imbalance [60]. Complementing this, the Maslach Burnout Inventory-Human Services Survey (MBI-HSS) provides a validated model for examining burnout’s dimensions: emotional exhaustion, depersonalization, and personal accomplishment. In elderly care, characterized by emotional labor and limited recognition, the MBI-HSS framework captures the complex impacts of prolonged job stress [62]. During the COVID-19 pandemic, heightened fear of infection functioned as a primary psychological stressor, triggering emotional exhaustion and depersonalization, the core dimensions of burnout as conceptualized by the MBI-HSS [63, 64]. Burnout, in turn, mediates the impact of COVID-related fear on two critical outcomes: turnover intention and perceived effort–reward imbalance [17, 25, 64–66]. During the COVID-19 pandemic, increased workloads, psychological and physical strainexacerbated burnout risk and also occupational stress in elderly care settings [63, 64]. Studies confirm that burnout strongly correlates with high effort combined with low reward [60, 61], in this case pandemic-related risks were not matched by proportional rewards [61, 67]. Psychological anxiety and fear were linked to effort, reward, and overcommitment [68]. The integration of ERI and MBI-HSS thus offers a comprehensive theoretical framework linking individual psychological mechanisms or fear to both personal and institutional outcomes, highlighting the interconnected nature of psychological and organizational changes in long-term care. This dual pathway demonstrates that pandemic-induced psychological strain not only undermined individual well-being but also contributed to systemic stress, organizational inequity, and workforce instability. Accordingly, the conceptual pathway can be summarized in Fig. 1. as, fear of COVID-19 → burnout (MBI-HSS) → turnover intention and effort–reward imbalance (ERI).

Sample and settings

A cross-sectional quantitative study was conducted in Hungary between June 2022 and May 2023 among professionals employed in elderly care. The online questionnaire was completed voluntarily and anonymously, with their consent. Participants (aged 18 or older) were recruited using a combination of convenience and snowball sampling via direct outreach to institutional leaders, centralized email distribution and posts in professional online forums. The sampling frame included 577 institutions (from a total of 852), representing an estimated accessible population of approximately 11,540 workers, based on an average staff size per facility. According to national data, there was an average of 14,948 professionals in elderly care between 2020 and 2023. Snowball sampling was chosen due to the lack of a centralised registry of elderly care workers and the practical challenges of directly contacting staff across multiple institutions during the post-pandemic period. While this approach enabled access to participants, it may have introduced selection bias, as participants were recruited through social and professional networks. A total of 277 responses were received, of which 269 were retained after quality control procedures. Respondents represented various professional roles (e.g., nurses, social caregivers, managers) and were employed in diverse institutional settings (e.g., nursing homes, home assistance, support services) with heterogeneous ownership structures (public, non-profit, religious, private).

Measurements

Demographic variables were collected using a questionnaire developed by the research team, covering age, sex, type of residence, work experience, job position, tenure, monthly income, salary assessment, and commuting distance.

Job-related stress was assessed with the validated Hungarian version of the Effort-Reward Imbalance (ERI) questionnaire [59, 61] widely applied in studies on burnout and turnover [59–62]. The ERI model, developed by.

Siegrist, is a social exchange-based theoretical framework that posits stress arises when the efforts an individual.

invests in their job are not adequately reciprocated by rewards [60]. It assumes a reciprocal relationship between.

effort and reward, where imbalance, especially high effort combined with low reward, elicits sustained stress reactions and increases health risks. In addition to effort and reward, the model includes overcommitment as a personal coping style, referring to an individual’s intrinsic tendency to invest excessive effort and strive for approval, often leading to chronic strain. The short version contains 15 items across three dimensions: effort (ERI E; 3 items), reward (ERI R; 6 items), and overcommitment (ERI O; 6 items) rated on Likert scales with some reverse-coded items [61, 72]. Scores range from 3 to 15 for effort, 6–30 for reward, and 6–24 for overcommitment; higher scores reflect greater effort, reward or overcommitment. Internal consistency was satisfactory (Cronbach’s α = 0.78), consistent with previous studies reporting values above 0.77 [61]. Based on the framework of Siegrist et al., an effort–reward ratio was computed for each respondent according to the following formula: E/(R × c). In this equation, “E” denotes the sum score of the effort scale, “R” represents the sum score of the reward scale, and c refers to a correction factor applied to account for the unequal number of items across the effort and reward dimensions. Specifically, the correction factor was set at 0.5, reflecting the ratio between the number of items on the effort scale (3 items) and the reward scale (6 items), i.e., 3/6. By interpretation, values approaching zero signify a favorable psychosocial condition characterized by relatively low effort combined with relatively high reward. Conversely, values exceeding 1.0 indicate disproportionately high effort that is not adequately compensated by actual or anticipated rewards. It is important to note that within occupational groups defined more by underload than overload, a low level of reward in itself may constitute a risk factor for the development of work-related stress [60].

Burnout was assessed using the Maslach Burnout Inventory – Human Services Survey (MBI-HSS) [62], for which a license was purchased for authorized use [73–75]. It measures emotional exhaustion (MBI-ee), depersonalization (MBI-dp), and personal accomplishment (MBI-pa) on 7-point Likert scales (0–6). Emotional exhaustion and depersonalization reflect higher burnout, while personal accomplishment is reverse-coded [74, 76]. The scale demonstrated good internal reliability in this study (Cronbach’s α = 0.87), consistent with previous findings (α > 0.72) [73].These dimensions are essential for understanding how COVID-19-related emotional strain affects job stress and turnover intention, with fear of COVID-19 influencing turnover mainly via emotional exhaustion and depersonalization, highlighting the MBI-HSS’s relevance in examining this mediation [50, 51, 73–75].

Turnover intention was evaluated with the validated Hungarian version of the Turnover Intention Scale (TIS-6) [77, 78]. This 6-item scale uses a 5-point Likert scale [1–5]; total scores ≤ 18 indicate intention to stay, while scores > 18 indicate intention to leave [77]. The scale has been widely used by researchers in various fields to measure turnover intention [79, 80].

Fear of COVID-19 was assessed using the validated Hungarian Fear of COVID-19 Scale (FCV-19). The 7-item Likert scale (5-point) yields total scores from 7 to 35; higher scores reflect greater fear [81, 82]. Internal consistency was excellent in this study (Cronbach’s α = 0.89), consistent with previous findings (α = 0.84) [81, 82].

Data analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS 28.0 and JASP 0.18.3.0, with significance set at p < .05. Descriptive statistics summarized sample characteristics, and internal reliability was assessed by Cronbach’s alpha (α), where values near 1 indicate high consistency [83]. Variables with normal distributions (as indicated by Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests, p > .05) are presented as mean (SD), whereas non-normally distributed variables (p < .05) are presented as median (IQR) to provide a consistent and interpretable summary of the data. Spearman’s rank correlation assessed relationships among FCV-19, MBI, TIS-6, and ERI to test hypotheses H1–H3 [84]. Hypotheses H4–H5 were evaluated using multiple linear regression (enter method), examining the combined effects of MBI subscales on TIS-6 and ERI [85]. All key statistical assumptions were tested, including normality, homoscedasticity, multicollinearity, autocorrelation, and linearity. The primary objective was to assess burnout’s mediating role between fear of COVID-19, turnover intention, and job-related stress. Mediation analyses tested H6 and H7, examining how FCV-19 affects TIS-6 and ERI dimensions via MBI subscales as mediators. Analyses were performed in JASP 0.18.3.0, with relevant mediators specified per hypothesis. Direct, indirect, total, and total indirect effects were calculated and presented in tables, including unstandardized coefficients (B), standardized coefficients (Beta), standard errors (SE), and 95% confidence intervals (CI). A bias-corrected bootstrap with 5,000 resamples and percentile-type CI enhanced accuracy, reduced bias, and ensured reliability for small or non-normal samples [86–88]. Path coefficients determined the strength and direction of relationships within the structural model. Direct, indirect, total, and total indirect effects were calculated to capture complex variable interactions. The mediating role of MBI was assessed alongside the total impact of FCV-19 on TIS-6 and ERI. The R² effect size quantified the explanatory power of FCV-19 in accounting for variance in TIS-6 and ERI [88].

Linear regression analyses assessed the combined effects of MBI dimensions on TIS-6 and ERI (Table 4). All statistical assumptions for multiple linear regression were examined and satisfied. Visual inspection of histograms and P–P plots confirmed the normal distribution of residuals. Homoscedasticity was supported by the randomly scattered pattern of residuals across predicted values. No evidence of multicollinearity was observed, as variance inflation factor (VIF = 1.009–2.077) values were below 2.077 and Tolerance values exceeded 0.482 (Tolerance = 0.482–0.991). The Durbin–Watson statistics ranged from 1.799 to 2.070, indicating no evidence of autocorrelation. Linearity and independence of observations were also met. Higher levels of emotional exhaustion (β = 0.468, p < .001) and lower personal accomplishment (β = 0.175, p < .001) were significantly associated with increased turnover intention, explaining 28.2% of its variance (adj. R² = 0.292, p < .001). MBI also explained significant portions of variance in ERI E (adj. R² = 0.267, p < .001), ERI O (adj. R² = 0.251, p < .001), and ER-ratio (adj. R² = 0.258, p < .001), while no significant effects were observed on ERI R (adj. R² = 0.005, p = .081). Increases in MBI-ee were associated with higher ERI E (β = 0.576, p < .001), ERI O (β = 0.651, p < .001), and ER-ratio (β = 0.547, p < .001), whereas MBI-dp was linked to lower ERI O (β = 0.283, p < .001). Lower MBI-pa contributed to reduced ERI R (β = 0.127, p < .022) but higher ERI O (β = 0.128, p = .022) and ER-ratio (β = 0.143, p = .010).

Mediation analyses examined how burnout dimensions (emotional exhaustion, depersonalization, and personal accomplishment) mediated the associations between fear of COVID-19 (FCV-19), effort–reward imbalance (ERI) components, and turnover intention (TIS-6). Path coefficients are shown in Fig. 2, and detailed indirect effects are presented in Table 5.

Results indicated that FCV-19 was significantly and positively associated with emotional exhaustion (MBI-ee; B = 0.408, SE = 0.140, z = 2.919, p = .004) and depersonalization (MBI-dp; B = 0.225, SE = 0.068, z = 3.313, p < .001), but unrelated to personal accomplishment (MBI-pa; p > .05).

In turn, emotional exhaustion showed strong positive associations with ERI effort (B = 0.172, SE = 0.023, z = 7.493, p < .001), overcommitment (B = 0.228, SE = 0.028, z = 8.220, p < .001), and the ER ratio (B = 0.022, SE = 0.003, z = 6.994, p < .001), but not with ERI reward (p > .05). Depersonalization was significantly and negatively related to ERI overcommitment (B = − 0.190, SE = 0.056, z = − 3.419, p = .001), while personal accomplishment showed modest positive relations with ERI reward (B = 0.032, SE = 0.015, z = 2.091, p = .037) but negative relation with overcommitment (B = -0.048, SE = 0.023, z = -2.108, p = .035) and ER ratio (B = -0.006, (SE = 0.003), z= -2.526, p = .012). Beyond these direct paths, several indirect effects confirmed the mediating role of burnout, particularly emotional exhaustion. Significant indirect effects were observed in the links between FCV-19 and ERI effort (B = 0.070, SE = 0.026, z = 2.720, p = .007), ERI overcommitment (B = 0.093, SE = 0.034, z = 2.751, p = .006), and the ER ratio (B = 0.009, SE = 0.003, z = 2.694, p = .007).

The specific indirect pathways revealed significant mediation via emotional exhaustion for FCV-19 and ERI effort (B = 0.070, (SE = 0.026), z = 2.720, p = .007), ERI overcommitment (B = 0.093, (SE = 0.034), z = 2.751, p = .006), and the ER ratio (B = 0.009, (SE = 0.003), z = 2.694, p = .007), and via depersonalization for FCV-19 and ERI overcommitment (B = -0.043, (SE = 0.018), z= -2.379, p = .017). No significant indirect effects were observed through MBI-PA (p > .05). These findings indicate that although FCV-19 did not directly predict ERI, its effects were transmitted through MBI, particularly MBI-ee and MBI-dp. Direct effects of FCV-19 on ERI outcomes were not significant (p > .005). Similarly, total effects of FCV-19 on ERI were non-significant (p > .05), indicating that the relationship operated entirely through burnout symptoms rather than a direct link. However, total indirect effects were significant for ERI E (B = 0.058, SE = 0.023, z = 2.518, p = .012) and the ER ratio (B = 0.008, SE = 0.003, z = 2.561, p = .010), reinforcing the overall mediating pattern. The models accounted for 26.3% of the variance in ERI effort (R² = 0.263), 3.4% in ERI reward (R² = 0.034), 23.9% in ERI overcommitment (R² = 0.239), and 25.8% in ER ratio (R² = 0.258).

Discussion

This study examined turnover intention and job-related stress among Hungarian elderly care workers, focusing on burnout’s mediating role. The results highlight that burnout, particularly emotional exhaustion and depersonalization, significantly mediated the relationship between fear of COVID-19, turnover intention, and job-related stress. While earlier studies primarily identified burnout as an outcome of job stress or a predictor of negative work outcomes [14–16, 57, 58], growing evidence suggests its mediating role, especially in healthcare and social care settings [51, 56, 89]. Emotional exhaustion and depersonalization may serve as key pathways through which COVID-19-related anxiety affects job outcomes [24, 38, 40, 51], particularly in elderly care where emotional labor and physical demands are considerable [30, 31]. Exploring burnout’s mediating role offers valuable insights for mental health interventions and retention strategies.

The study partially confirmed the first hypothesized associations between fear of COVID-19 and burnout. Fear of COVID-19 showed significant positive associations with emotional exhaustion and depersonalization, consistent with prior studies [28, 29, 37, 69], confirming that pandemic-related anxiety contributes to emotional exhaustion and emotional distancing in healthcare professionals. However, no significant association with personal accomplishment was found, which differs from previous results, where reduced personal accomplishment was also significantly associated with fear of the pandemic [71]. The lack of connection to personal accomplishment reflects the relative stability of self-efficacy and may indicate its protective role against stress [90]. Additionally, individuals experiencing severe burnout may have already exited the workforce and thus were not captured in the sample, potentially attenuating associations.

Hypothesis 2

was mostly supported, fear of COVID-19 was positively associated with effort, overcommitment, and effort–reward imbalance, although not directly with perceived rewards. These results align with the theoretical model proposed by Siegrist, which emphasizes that perceived imbalance, rather than absolute reward levels, triggers stress [61]. This pattern is consistent with earlier findings, where pandemic-related fear was positively correlated with perceived effort and ER ratio [70]. In the context of elderly care, pandemic-related fear may have contributed to increased workload and heightened commitment, while rewards remained stable or unchanged, potentially leading to elevated effort–reward imbalance [60].

Hypothesis 3

was not supported by our data, no direct association was found between fear of COVID-19 and turnover intention. This finding contrasts with earlier pandemic-phase studies where elevated fear strongly predicted turnover intention [38, 52, 79]. This may reflect a temporal shift as the data collection occurred after the acute phases of the pandemic, when immediate fear-driven turnover pressures may have subsided [38, 40, 49]. Instead, these findings emphasize the indirect pathways through which pandemic-related fear exerts its effects, highlighting the mediating role of burnout.

H4 hypothesis was partially supported. Emotional exhaustion consistently emerged as the strongest predictor, positively associated with increased turnover intention, corroborating earlier research [26], which shows that emotionally depleted workers are more likely to consider turnover. Additionally, personal accomplishment was significantly negatively associated with turnover intention, workers feeling less effective were more likely to consider exit, consistent with studies in long-term care [91]. Emotional exhaustion and personal accomplishment was significantly associated with turnover intention in our sample, but not with depersonalization, diverging from some prior studies [26, 91–93]. This may suggest that depersonalization functions more as an adaptive coping mechanism, helping workers maintain performance under emotional strain, without necessarily increasing turnover intentions [94] .

The findings partially confirm Hypotheses 5, highlighting important differentiation among burnout dimensions. Emotional exhaustion was strongly linked to higher perceived effort, overcommitment, and effort-reward imbalance, aligning with previous findings [55]. Lower personal accomplishment was linked to higher overcommitment and effort-reward imbalance, while inversely related to perceived rewards. In contrast, depersonalization showed limited associations, demonstrating a negative relationship only with overcommitment, and had no significant associations with effort, reward or effort-reward imbalance. These patterns suggest that emotional exhaustion and reduced personal accomplishment represent the primary burnout components perceptions of job-related stress, while depersonalization may play a more peripheral role in our sample [17, 95–97].

The results partially support Hypotheses 6 and 7, consistent with previous findings, demonstrating differential mediating effects of burnout dimensions. Consistent with previous studies, emotional exhaustion significantly mediated the associations between fear of COVID-19 and turnover intention [17, 50, 51, 65], as well as between effort, overcommitment, or effort-reward imbalance [25, 37, 66, 98]. These findings confirm the central role of emotional exhaustion in translating pandemic-related anxiety into adverse occupational consequences. In contrast, depersonalization showed limited and selective mediating effects. In line with our findings, previous research has identified depersonalization as a significant mediator in the relationship between fear of COVID-19 and overcommitment [37, 99]. This suggest that emotional detachment may contribute to maladaptive coping under increased stress demands, serving as a psychological detachment mechanism that helps individuals buffer overwhelming emotional burdens while continuing to meet work demands [100, 101].

Importantly, the study was conducted during the post-acute phase of the COVID-19 pandemic, when immediate health threats had substantially declined due to vaccination coverage and adaptive institutional measures. Therefore, fear of COVID-19 during this period may have reflected more generalized anxiety and lingering psychological burden rather than acute infection concerns. This contextual factor may partly explain why personal accomplishment did not significantly mediate any of the observed associations. Similarly, depersonalization may have evolved into a longer-term adaptive coping mechanism, enabling continued functioning without directly driving turnover intentions or perceived effort-reward imbalance [102].

Taken together, the findings emphasize emotional exhaustion as the primary pathway through which pandemic-related fears contribute to turnover intentions and job-related imbalance, while illustrating the complex, temporally sensitive roles of depersonalization and personal accomplishment in modulating adaptive functioning and resilience in sustained high-stress professional environments.

Strengths and limitations

This study included 269 elderly care workers across Hungary, representing a relatively large sample. However, several limitations must be acknowledged. Selection bias may have occurred, as individuals who left their jobs during the pandemic were not included, potentially affecting the findings. While snowball sampling facilitated the recruitment of an adequate sample, it may also have introduced selection bias. This approach could have led to the overrepresentation of individuals with similar sociodemographic or professional characteristics, while those who had already exited the sector were likely excluded. Such limitations necessarily constrain the extent to which the findings can be generalized to the broader population of Hungarian elderly care workers. The cross-sectional design limits causal inferences between risk factors and outcomes. Unexamined variables may have influenced turnover intention, leaving some mediators or moderators unaccounted for. The study’s focus on Hungarian elderly care institutions limits generalizability to other social care sectors; broader contexts should be explored in future research. Self-reported measures may introduce response biases, as subjective assessments may not fully reflect objective conditions. The online survey format may have limited participation, contributing to low response rates and further limiting generalizability. Convenience sampling may have introduced socially desirable responses and excluded dissenting perspectives. Future studies should consider alternative data collection methods, including face-to-face questionnaires, workplace observations, peer reporting, and interviews, to enhance data validity. The statistical conclusions apply only to the study sample and do not represent the entire Hungarian elderly care workforce. Additionally, the timing of data collection - beginning three months after Hungary’s fifth COVID-19 wave - may have influenced turnover intention and burnout levels. Longitudinal studies are recommended to track these variables over time and provide insights into the evolving dynamics of turnover intention and actual turnover behavior throughout the pandemic.

Conclusions

This study underscores emotional exhaustion as the primary mechanism linking pandemic-related fears to turnover intention and job-related stress among elderly care workers in the post-acute phase of the COVID-19 pandemic. While depersonalization and personal accomplishment played more limited and temporally sensitive roles, the enduring emotional burden highlights burnout as a dynamic and ongoing process with implications for workforce well-being and retention. Given these findings, interventions should prioritize the early identification and management of emotional exhaustion to prevent escalation of psychological strain and turnover intentions. Organizational strategies may include regular monitoring of emotional exhaustion levels, provision of stress management training tailored to healthcare settings, promotion of supportive supervision, and fostering team-based peer support to mitigate emotional overload. Future research should continue to investigate the long-term trajectories of these burnout processes, particularly examining how emotional exhaustion, depersonalization, and personal accomplishment evolve over time in response to persistent occupational stressors beyond the pandemic context. Additionally, healthcare institutions should establish clear workload thresholds and enforce mandatory rest periods to prevent chronic strain accumulation. From a policy perspective, national healthcare authorities should consider developing sector-specific mental health support protocols for long-term care workers, including state-subsidized access to psychological services and burnout prevention programs tailored to high-risk roles. Funding mechanisms could also incentivize facilities that demonstrate proactive burnout mitigation strategies through quality metrics.
