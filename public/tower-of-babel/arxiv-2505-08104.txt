Recovery dynamics of a gap-engineered transmon after a quasiparticle burst Preprint: APS/123-QED
Heekun Nho, Thomas Connolly, Pavel D. Kurilovich, Spencer Diamond, Charlotte G. L. Bøttcher, Leonid I. Glazman, Michel H. Devoret

Abstract
Ionizing radiation impacts create bursts of quasiparticle density in superconducting qubits. These bursts severely degrade qubit coherence for a prolonged period of time and can be detrimental for quantum error correction. Here, we experimentally resolve quasiparticle bursts in 3D gap-engineered transmon qubits by continuously monitoring qubit transitions. Gap engineering allowed us to reduce the burst detection rate by a factor of a few. This modest reduction falls several orders of magnitude short of the reduction expected if the quasiparticles quickly thermalize to the cryostat temperature. We associate the limited effect of gap engineering with the slow thermalization of the phonons in our chips after the burst.



Introduction.—
Quantum error correction protocols redundantly encode a logical qubit in the entangled state of multiple physical qubits
Nielsen and Chuang 2010
. This redundancy makes the encoded information robust to errors occurring on individual physical qubits, as long as two assumptions hold. First, errors should be uncorrelated between different physical qubits. Second, errors occurring on the same qubit should not have temporal correlations. In superconducting processors, common errors – such as qubit relaxation due to material defects – generally satisfy these assumptions. It has been shown, however, that there are rare error burst events that violate both conditions. During these error bursts (hereby referred to as “bursts”), the qubit relaxation rates dramatically increase across multiple qubits simultaneously for a prolonged duration
Vepsäläinen et al. 2020
;
Martinis 2021
;
Wilen et al. 2021
;
McEwen et al. 2022
;
Thorbeck et al. 2023
;
Harrington et al. 2024
;
Li et al. 2024
. Mitigating bursts is therefore essential to realize large-scale quantum error correction.

The underlying mechanism of the bursts is the impact of ionizing radiation on the chip hosting the qubits
1

There is another mechanism for bursts, where a phonon shower is generated by the avalanche-like release of stress in the chip
Yelton et al. 2025
. Its experimental signatures and mitigation strategies should be similar to those for high-energy impacts.
. A high-energy particle striking the chip produces a cascade of high-energy phonons that rapidly propagate throughout the substrate. When the phonons collide with the superconducting films of the qubits, they break Cooper pairs, producing quasiparticle excitations (QPs). When the QPs tunnel across the Josephson junction, they can exchange energy with the qubit degree of freedom. This results in an elevated qubit relaxation rate. Only when the QPs recombine or get trapped does the error burst end and the performance of the processor recover. This prompts the search for strategies to either accelerate the phonon decay
Martinis 2021
;
Iaia et al. 2022
or suppress QP tunneling.

On the latter front, a promising way forward is engineering of the superconducting gap
Aumentado et al. 2004
;
Ferguson et al. 2006
;
Naaman and Aumentado 2007
;
Court et al. 2007
;
Shaw et al. 2008
;
Sun et al. 2012
;
Riwar and Catelani 2019
;
Serniak 2019
;
Diamond et al. 2022
;
Pan et al. 2022
;
Marchegiani et al. 2022
;
Connolly et al. 2024
;
McEwen et al. 2024
. By creating a non-uniform gap profile – which can be achieved by varying the thickness of superconducting films – it is possible to impede the QP tunneling across the qubit junction and suppress decoherence
Diamond et al. 2022
;
Marchegiani et al. 2022
;
Connolly et al. 2024
;
McEwen et al. 2024
.
For this approach to be effective, the difference in gaps at the two sides of the junction should exceed the qubit energy by an amount significantly larger than the QP energy.
This condition has been shown to hold for the “resident” non-equilibrium QPs typically present in the superconducting films in the steady state
Connolly et al. 2024
. By interacting with phonons, the energy of these QPs equilibrates with the base plate of the refrigerator even while the QP density remains strongly out of equilibrium. Yet, an important question for the suppression of QP-induced correlated errors remains unanswered: can gap engineering prevent QP tunneling following the impact of high-energy particles, when the phonon bath itself is moved away from equilibrium?

In this paper, we address this question by measuring QP bursts in 3D transmons with different pairs of junction film thicknesses and, consequently, with different gap profiles.
First, we benchmark our gap engineering by measuring the gap difference,
\delta\Delta
, for our devices with different film thicknesses. The gap difference is obtained by monitoring the tunneling rate of resident QPs at different fridge temperatures. By varying the film thickness, we fabricate the gap difference in the range of 0 to 10
\mathrm{GHz}
(in frequency units). We then evaluate the effect of gap difference on the detection rate of burst events and measure qubit relaxation rates during bursts.

We find that gap engineering reduces the rate of burst detection by a factor of a few. However, the reduction falls short of the four orders of magnitude suppression observed for the tunneling of the resident QPs. We believe that this discrepancy results from the elevation of the effective chip temperature during the burst to about 90 mK. The additional thermal energy allows the QPs to overcome the gap difference and tunnel across the junction, thus facilitating qubit relaxation. We directly confirm the persistence of elevated substrate temperature by comparing the qubit excitation and relaxation rates during the burst.

Our results should be contrasted with those of Ref.
McEwen et al. 2024
, where the gap difference – obtained by film thicknesses similar to our gap-engineered device – resulted in essentially no bursts being detected.
The difference may arise from a combination of several factors. First, unlike ours, the chip in Ref.
McEwen et al. 2024
has most of its area covered by the aluminum film forming the ground plane. Second, the
substrate of Ref.
McEwen et al. 2024
is silicon whereas ours is sapphire. Third, the chips are thermalized differently. Our chips are anchored to the package only by two isolated clamps each, whereas the chip of Ref.
McEwen et al. 2024
is presumably anchored by a large number of wirebonds. Finally, unlike in our measurement, Ref.
McEwen et al. 2024
probed the qubit only sparsely, once every 100
\mathrm{\mu s}
. Such a measurement may miss bursts with a recovery time of less than 100
\mathrm{\mu s}
.

Measuring the gap difference.—
As an initial step, we characterize the superconducting gap barriers,
\delta\Delta
, of the fabricated devices. To do this, we measure the parity-switching rates in the steady state, i.e., away from any rare QP bursts. In this case, the parity-switching rates are dominated by the tunneling of steady-state, resident non-equilibrium QPs, which populate our superconducting films. In the steady state, the energy distribution of these QPs equilibrates with the base stage of the refrigerator
Connolly et al. 2024
. The temperature dependence of the QP tunneling rate then follows the Arrhenius law, with the activation exponent controlled by
\>\delta\Delta
. Indeed,
\>\delta\Delta
impedes the QP across the junction, such that only few energetic QPs tunnel through the junction. Fitting the measured temperature dependence of the parity-switching rates to the Arrhenius law thus directly yields
\>\delta\Delta
, as we explain below. This measurement is conceptually similar to the one described in Ref.
Connolly et al. 2024
. The measured parity-switching rates as a function of temperature for our three devices are shown in Figure
1
(a).

To fit the measured rates, we use a detailed theoretical model of QP tunneling in the presence of a gap difference at the junction
Diamond et al. 2022
;
Marchegiani et al. 2022
;
Connolly et al. 2024
. According to this theory, when the qubit is in the ground state, the parity-switching rate reads
\>\Gamma^{\mathrm{QP}}_{0}=\>\gamma^{\mathrm{QP}}_{0}\>x_{\mathrm{QP}}^{\mathrm{ne}}
. Here,
\>x_{\mathrm{QP}}^{\mathrm{ne}}
is the temperature-independent density of resident QPs
measured in units of Cooper pair density, and
\>\gamma^{\mathrm{QP}}_{0}\propto E_{J}\exp\left(-\>\delta\Delta/\>k_{\mathrm{B}}T\right)

is proportional to the QP tunneling rate and the fraction of QPs with energy exceeding
\delta\Delta
. Here,
\>k_{\mathrm{B}}
is the Boltzmann constant,
T
is the temperature of QPs, and
E_{J}
is the Josephson energy of the junction.
When the qubit is excited, and if
\>\delta\Delta>\>hf_{q}
, this expression modifies to
\>\Gamma^{\mathrm{QP}}_{1}=\>\gamma^{\mathrm{QP}}_{1}\>x_{\mathrm{QP}}^{\mathrm{ne}}
with
\>\gamma^{\mathrm{QP}}_{1}\propto E_{J}\exp\left(-\left[\>\delta\Delta-\>hf_{q}\right]/\>k_{\mathrm{B}}T\right)
, where
h
is the Planck constant and
f_{q}
is the qubit frequency. The activation energy is reduced compared to that in
\>\gamma^{\mathrm{QP}}_{0}
because the QP can absorb the excitation from the qubit, making it easier to overcome the gap difference.
On contrary, if
\>\delta\Delta<\>hf_{q}
, then
\>\gamma^{\mathrm{QP}}_{1}
becomes roughly independent of temperature. For each device, we simultaneously fit the parity-switching rates
\Gamma_{0}
and
\Gamma_{1}
to the expressions outlined above, using
\>\delta\Delta
and
\>x_{\mathrm{QP}}^{\mathrm{ne}}
as fitting parameters.

There are two complications in this fitting protocol. First, at high temperatures,
T>100\>\mathrm{mK}
, the density of equilibrium QPs,
\>x_{\mathrm{QP}}^{\mathrm{th}}(T)=\sqrt{2\pi\>k_{\mathrm{B}}T/\Delta}\exp\left(-\Delta/\>k_{\mathrm{B}}T\right)
, becomes comparable to that of the resident non-equilibrium QPs,
\>x_{\mathrm{QP}}^{\mathrm{ne}}
. Therefore, at high temperatures, we need to account for the contribution of equilibrium QPs to the parity-switching rates. Second, there is a small background contribution
\Gamma^{\mathrm{ph}}
to parity-switching rates coming from the absorption of stray pair-breaking photons at the junction
Houzet et al. 2019
;
Diamond et al. 2022
;
Connolly et al. 2024
. In our theoretical fit, we add such contributions as a temperature-independent offset to the parity-switching rates. Notably, the contribution
\Gamma^{\mathrm{ph}}
of the photons might be different for the two qubit states
Houzet et al. 2019
;
Diamond et al. 2022
. We use notations
\Gamma_{0}^{\mathrm{ph}}
and
\Gamma_{1}^{\mathrm{ph}}
to differentiate this contribution between the respective qubit states.

Burst measurement.—
With the gap differences under control, we proceed to analyze the temporally correlated errors caused by QP bursts in the devices. To assess the effect of the gap difference on mitigating the high-energy impacts, we compare the QP bursts in the big- and small-
\>\delta\Delta
devices. The results for the medium-
\>\delta\Delta
device are discussed separately in supplemental materials, due to the complications arising from its gap difference being comparable to the qubit energy.

To detect the QP bursts, we attempt to stabilize the qubit in its excited state. To this end, we repeatedly readout the qubit state every 5.7
\mathrm{\mu s}
. If we find the qubit in its ground state
|0\rangle
, we apply a
\pi
-pulse to bring it to the excited state
|1\rangle
. The resulting pulse sequence is illustrated in Fig.
2
(b). During the QP burst events, the qubit relaxation rate significantly increases. This allows us to identify the bursts by the excess number of observed
|1\rangle\rightarrow|0\rangle
relaxation events, see e.g. Fig.
2
(d). We note that the measurement is post-selected for events which do not cause large changes to offset charge
noa
. However, we find that this post-selection is inconsequential for the statistics of our burst events.

To collect sufficient statistics, we repeat the measurement described above for about 10 hours for each device. We then chop the measurement traces into
1\>\mathrm{ms}
windows and tally the number of observed relaxation events in each window. The normalized histograms of the relaxation counts are shown in Fig.
2
(c). For each device, the histogram contains a large peak with a maximum of 10 to 15 relaxation events. This peak is unrelated to the QP bursts. It describes a Poissonian relaxation process consistent with the independently measured steady-state qubit relaxation time
T_{1}^{\rm steady}\sim 100\>\mu\mathrm{s}
. In addition to the Poissonian peak, however, there is an anomalous tail with an excess number of relaxation events. This tail stems from the QP burst events. The clear distinction between the Poissonian peak and the anomalous tail makes us confident that a burst has occurred within a
1\>\mathrm{ms}
window when the number of relaxation events exceeded a certain threshold

. Choosing a common threshold for both devices, we see that the burst detection rate in the big-
\delta\Delta
device (
0.57\>\mathrm{min}^{-1}

2.76\>\mathrm{min}^{-1}

Notably, the suppression of the burst detection rate by gap engineering is only about a factor of
5
. This observation is inconsistent with the QP energy being thermalized with the mixing chamber of the fridge immediately after the impact. If the QPs were thermalized – which is the case for resident QPs in Fig. 1 –
\>\delta\Delta
would suppress the burst detection rate by about a factor of
\exp(\left[\>\delta\Delta-hf_{q}\right]/\>k_{\mathrm{B}}T)\sim 10^{4}
in the big-
\>\delta\Delta
device. Here,
T=25\>\mathrm{mK}
is the base temperature of our fridge.

T_{1}

\Gamma_{10}

\Gamma_{10}(t)
after the burst event, we similarly measure the qubit

\Gamma_{01}(t)

\ket{1}
. From the two rates, one may extract the time dependence of the QP density,
\>x_{\mathrm{QP}}(t)
, and the qubit temperature
T_{q}(t)

\>k_{\mathrm{B}}T_{q}=hf_{q}/\mathrm{ln}\left(\Gamma_{10}/\Gamma_{01}\right)

\>x_{\mathrm{QP}}
is similar to that of the relaxation rate. In about a millisecond, the QP density decays to a level where the QPs no longer limit the qubit
T_{1}

The qubit temperature
T_{q}
surges to about 90 mK after the burst. This number significantly exceeds the temperature of the mixing chamber of our fridge. The qubit temperature returns to its steady state exponentially, with a time constant of about
6\>\mathrm{ms}
. This time scale is almost 10 times longer than the
0.7\>\mathrm{ms}
time constant for the decay of
\>x_{\mathrm{QP}}
. The temperature thus remains elevated even long after the generated by the impact non-equilibrium QPs are gone.
This points to a dominant role of the substrate properties in determining the evolution of
T_{q}(t)
.
Indeed, the increase in qubit temperature to
90\>\mathrm{mK}
that we see is consistent with the substrate phonon heat capacity for a typical radiation impact energy of
\sim 100\mathrm{keV}-1\>\mathrm{MeV}

;
noa
. The decay constant for the qubit temperature is determined by the rate of phonon escape from the system, likely through the chip clamp.

The elevated substrate temperature after the burst explains the weak sensitivity of burst occurrence to the gap difference. As previously described, the suppression of QP-induced qubit relaxation by the gap difference is controlled by the activation exponent
\exp(-[\delta\Delta-hf_{q}]/\>k_{\mathrm{B}}T)
. For the gap difference to be effective,
\delta\Delta-hf_{q}
must be much larger than
\>k_{\mathrm{B}}T
. For our big-
\delta\Delta
device,
(\delta\Delta-hf_{q})/\>k_{\mathrm{B}}\sim 200\>\mathrm{mK}
which only exceeds the lattice temperature during the QP burst by a factor of two. This is consistent with the observed decrease of the burst occurrence by about a factor of a few in the presence of gap difference.

Conclusion.—

In this work, we investigate the effect of superconducting gap engineering on QP bursts in transmon qubits. Our results confirm that the difference of superconducting gaps for the two sides of the Josephson junction exceeding the qubit energy,
\>\delta\Delta>hf_{q}
, can reduce both the detection rate of burst, see Fig.
2
(c), and the qubit relaxation rate during these events, see Fig.
2
(e).

However, we also uncover a critical limitation of the gap engineering method: during bursts, the substrate and the QPs retain high energy
\sim
100 mK which is only mildly lower than
\>\delta\Delta-hf_{q}
. We attribute this elevated temperature to the slow escape of hot phonons from our chip. Our findings suggest two potential strategies to further suppress the adverse effects of QP bursts. First, increasing the gap difference to
\>\delta\Delta/\>k_{\mathrm{B}}\gg
100 mK can prevent QPs from crossing the junction even when their energy is elevated. The increase of
\>\delta\Delta
can be achieved by employing materials with a higher gap, such as granular aluminum
Abeles et al. 1966
;
Cohen and Abeles 1968
;
Deutscher et al. 1973
;
Sun et al. 2012
. Second, the effectiveness of gap engineering can be enhanced by providing an efficient escape mechanism for hot phonons. This can be achieved by improving the thermal link between the chip and the mixing chamber plate of the refrigerator. Alternatively, the phonons can be absorbed by patches of normal metal placed directly on the chip
Martinis 2021
;
Iaia et al. 2022
;
Larson et al. 2025
.
Due to their high heat capacity, these patches can serve as efficient heat sinks.
The combination of these two strategies should suppress the detrimental effect of QP bursts on superconducting quantum processors.
