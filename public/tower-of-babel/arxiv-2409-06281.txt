Schwarzschild Lensing From Geodesic Deviation
Zhao Li, Xiao Guo, Tan Liu, Tao Zhu, Wen Zhao

Abstract
We revisit the gravitational lensing of light or gravitational waves by a Schwarzschild black hole in geometric optics. Rather than focusing on a single massless particle, we investigate the collective behavior of a congruence of light or gravitational rays, described by the geodesic deviation equation (GDE). By projecting GDE onto the Newman-Penrose tetrad, we decouple the equation and find an analytical Dyson-like series solution in the weak deflection and thin lens limits. Using this solution, we analyze the evolution of the cross-sectional area and axis ratio. Finally, we reproduce the magnification and axis ratio of the lensing images up to second-order weak deflection approximation, addressing some previously overlooked corrections.

III
Solution to deviation vector

In this section, we present the solution to the geodesic vector from Eq. (
15
). By transforming these two equations into a set of first-order differential equations, we obtain the equivalent matrix form

\bm{\mathcal{D}}\bm{\mathcal{Y}}=\bm{\mathcal{A}}\bm{\mathcal{Y}},\quad\text{with}\quad\bm{\mathcal{Y}}\equiv\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\xi_{\pm}\\
\bm{\mathcal{D}}\xi_{\pm}\end{array}\right),\quad\text{and}\quad\bm{\mathcal{A}}\equiv\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&1\\
\pm\Psi_{0}&0\end{array}\right).

(16)

In a typical lensing system, the impact parameter of the photon/graviton paths is much larger than the gravitational radius of the lens object. Meanwhile, the source and observer are far from the lens, and the background curvature approaches zero sufficiently at these two points. These two conditions are typically referred to as weak deflection and thin lens limits. We assume that the lensed wave is emitted by a point source and propagates freely near the source. Therefore, the initial conditions for the deviation vector can be set as
\xi_{\pm}=0
and
\bm{\mathcal{D}}\xi_{\pm}=c_{\pm}
. The initial derivative is proportional to the open angle of the congruence near the wave source. In these two limits, the right-hand side of Eq. (
16
) is always perturbation, and its 0-th solution can be set as

\bm{\mathcal{Y}}_{0}=c_{\pm}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\lambda\\
1\end{array}\right).

(17)

The Dyson-like series solution is then written as
[
37
,
40
,
42

\bm{\mathcal{Y}}_{1}=\int_{0}^{\lambda}d\lambda^{\prime}\bm{\mathcal{A}}(\lambda^{\prime})\bm{\mathcal{Y}}_{0}(\lambda^{\prime}),

(18)

\bm{\mathcal{Y}}_{2}=\int_{0}^{\lambda}d\lambda^{\prime}\bm{\mathcal{A}}(\lambda^{\prime})\bm{\mathcal{Y}}_{1}(\lambda^{\prime})=\int_{0}^{\lambda}d\lambda^{\prime}\int_{0}^{\lambda^{\prime}}d\lambda^{\prime\prime}\bm{\mathcal{A}}(\lambda^{\prime})\bm{\mathcal{A}}(\lambda^{\prime\prime})\bm{\mathcal{Y}}_{0}(\lambda^{\prime\prime}),

(19)

\cdots,

\bm{\mathcal{Y}}_{n+1}=\int_{0}^{\lambda}d\lambda^{\prime}\bm{\mathcal{A}}(\lambda^{\prime})\bm{\mathcal{Y}}_{n}(\lambda^{\prime}).

(20)

\xi_{\pm}
is finally iteratively given by,

(22)

and
\tilde{\xi}_{\pm}
are real. The

is purely imaginary, such that the integration constants

are real and purely imaginary, respectively.

The GDE (

Application: Schwarzschild lensing

IV.1
Null tetrad and Weyl scalars

In the coordinates
\{t,r,\theta,\varphi\}
, the line element of Schwarzschild black hole of mass
M

,

(28)

Because of the spherical symmetry of the Schwarzschild background, the orbits of the massless test particle lie in the equatorial plane of the black hole, with
\theta=\pi/2
.
L
is the angular momentum of the photon or graviton. The radial potential
U_{r}
is defined as

U_{r}\equiv\pm\sqrt{1-\frac{L^{2}}{r^{2}}f(r)},

(29)

where the overall sign is negative and positive when the particle moves toward or backward to the pericenter, respectively. In a general stationary spacetime, the spacelike basis
\bm{e}_{y}
is constructed using the Newton gauge
[
45
,
46
]
, where the time component

is set to
0
. Its spatial components

are determined by defining a local

-axis along the free-fall acceleration, i.e.,
\vec{\mathbf{r}}=(1,0,0)

to be perpendicular to the 3-wavevector,
\vec{\mathbf{k}}
, and the local
\vec{\mathbf{r}}
-axis, i.e.,

\bm{e}_{y}

\bm{e}_{y}=\left\{0,0,\frac{1}{r},0\right\}.

(30)

And then another basis vector

\bm{m}_{0}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\bm{e}_{x}+i\bm{e}_{y})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left\{0,-\frac{L}{E}\frac{f(r)}{r},\frac{i}{r},\frac{1}{E}\frac{U_{r}}{r}\right\},

and

\bm{k}_{0}\cdot\bm{n}_{0}=-1

\bm{n}_{0}\cdot\bm{n}_{0}=0

), and (

Note that

, represent the affine parameter
\lambda
evaluated at the source, pericenter, and observer positions, respectively. As a result, the integration takes on different forms in these two intervals. When
\lambda_{s}<\lambda<\lambda_{m}
, the integral is expressed as

\mathcal{I}_{n}(x)=\int_{x_{s}}^{x}[\lambda_{\rm I}(x)-\lambda_{\rm I}(x^{\prime})]\Psi_{0}[\lambda_{\rm I}(x^{\prime})]\mathcal{I}_{n-1}[\lambda_{\rm I}(x^{\prime})]\frac{d\lambda_{\rm I}(x)}{dx^{\prime}}dx^{\prime},\quad(n=1,2,3,\cdots).

(37)

When
\lambda_{m}<\lambda<\lambda_{o}
, the integral is split into two parts:

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{x_{s}}^{1}[\lambda_{\rm II}(x)-\lambda_{\rm I}(x^{\prime})]\Psi_{0}[\lambda_{\rm I}(x^{\prime})]\mathcal{I}_{n-1}[\lambda_{\rm I}(x^{\prime})]\frac{d\lambda_{\rm I}(x)}{dx^{\prime}}dx^{\prime}

(38)

\displaystyle+\int_{1}^{x}[\lambda_{\rm II}(x)-\lambda_{\rm II}(x^{\prime})]\Psi_{0}[\lambda_{\rm II}(x^{\prime})]\mathcal{I}_{n-1}[\lambda_{\rm II}(x^{\prime})]\frac{d\lambda_{\rm II}(x)}{dx^{\prime}}dx^{\prime},\quad(n=1,2,3,\cdots).

The analytical expressions for the integrations
\mathcal{I}_{n}
are presented in Appendix
D
. Due to the complexity of these expressions, we performed numerical calculations to better illustrate their behavior.

\tilde{\xi}_{-}

and

and

\tilde{\xi}_{-}
are set with the same initial conditions, such that
\sigma
remains zero until the photon/graviton approaches the lens object. Notably, in image 1, both optical scalars remain continuous, while in image 2, a discontinuity arises due to the caustic point.

IV.3
The magnification and axis ratio of the lensing images

In the last subsection, we explored the behaviors of the geodesic deviation vector, cross-sectional area/axis ratio, and spin coefficients along the photon/graviton trajectories. In this subsection, we turn to the observational imprints of these evolutions. The magnification of a lensing image is defined as the ratio of the lensed to the unlensed energy flux at the observer. In geometric optics, the energy flux of light and GWs is covariantly conserved within the congruence, making it proportional to
1/\mathcal{A}
. Therefore, the magnification can be written as

\mu=\frac{\widehat{\mathcal{A}}(\lambda_{o})}{\mathcal{A}(\lambda_{o})}.

(40)

where
\widehat{\mathcal{A}}
represents the cross-sectional area of a congruence propagating freely along the unlensed path (OS, see Fig.
1
). By setting the initial conditions
\widehat{\mathcal{A}}(\lambda=0)=0
and
\bm{\mathcal{D}}\widehat{\mathcal{A}}(\lambda=0)=1
, the solution is

\widehat{\mathcal{A}}(\lambda)=\frac{1}{\mathrm{OS}^{2}},\quad\text{with}\quad\text{OS}=D_{S}\sqrt{1+\tan^{2}\beta}=\frac{r_{o}}{\sqrt{1+\tan^{2}\beta}}\left[1+\sqrt{1-(1+\tan^{2}\beta)\left(1-\frac{r_{s}^{2}}{r_{o}^{2}}\right)}\right].

(41)

We recall that
r_{s}
and
r_{o}
are the radial Schwarzschild coordinates of the source and observer, which is related with
D_{L}
and
D_{S}
by

r_{o}=D_{L},\quad\text{and},\quad r_{s}=D_{S}^{2}(1+\tan^{2}\beta)-2D_{L}D_{S}+D_{L}^{2}

(42)

The area of the lensed congruence is computed using the Dyson-like series in Eq. (
22
). At the observer position, we obtain the following expressions for the first four terms in this series,

\mathcal{I}_{0}(\lambda_{o})=\lambda_{o}\approx\frac{M}{u_{m}}\left(\frac{1}{x_{s}}+\frac{1}{x_{o}}\right)\left[1-\frac{1}{2}x_{s}x_{o}\left(1-\frac{4u_{m}}{x_{s}+x_{o}}\right)\right],

(43)

\mathcal{I}_{1}(\lambda_{o})\approx\frac{2M}{x_{s}x_{o}}\left\{2+\left(\frac{45\pi}{16}-4\right)u_{m}+\left[\left(\frac{192}{5}-\frac{135\pi}{16}\right)u_{m}^{2}+2u_{m}(x_{s}+x_{o})-(x_{s}^{2}-x_{s}x_{o}-x_{o}^{2})\right]\right\},

(44)

\mathcal{I}_{2}(\lambda_{o})\approx\frac{M}{x_{s}x_{o}}\left\{\frac{15\pi}{8}u_{m}+u_{m}\left[\left(48-\frac{45\pi}{8}\right)u_{m}-4(x_{s}+x_{o})\right]\right\},

(45)

and

\mathcal{I}_{3}(\lambda_{o})\approx\frac{M}{x_{s}x_{o}}\frac{16}{5}u_{m}^{2}.

(46)

Under the weak deflection and thin-lens approximations,
u_{m}\ll 1
, and
x_{s,o}\equiv r_{m}/r_{s,o}\ll 1
with the same order of magnitude as
u_{m}
, we expand these expressions in terms of small parameters
u_{m}
,
x_{s}
, and
x_{o}
, up to the zeroth order, as shown in Eqs. (
43
-
46
). By inserting these into Eq. (
21
), and then into the definition in Eq. (
25
), we can compute the area
\mathcal{A}(\lambda_{o})
. Finally, combining this with the unlensed area in Eq. (
41
) and applying Eq. (
40
), we obtain the magnification.

Next, we transform the parameters used here into the commonly employed lensing parameters. This is done as follows: (1) Transform
u_{m}
into the impact parameter
b
via Eq. (
61
), where
b=\pm D_{L}\sin\theta/M
for image 1 and 2, respectively. (2) Transform
x_{s}
and
x_{o}
into
\beta
,
D_{L}
, and
D_{LS}
using their definition and Eq. (
42
). (3) Substitute the relation between
\beta
and
\theta
from lens equation in Eq. (
70
). (4) In the weak deflection and thin lens limit, we expand the image position as

\theta=\theta_{E}\left[\theta_{0}+\theta_{1}\epsilon+\theta_{2}\epsilon^{2}+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{3})\right],

(47)

where the bookkeeper
\epsilon
is defined as,

\epsilon\sim\frac{\theta_{E}}{4\mathcal{D}},\quad\text{with}\quad\mathcal{D}\equiv\frac{D_{LS}}{D_{S}}.

(48)

After these transformations, the magnification is expanded in powers of
\epsilon
as

\displaystyle\mu

\displaystyle=\frac{\theta_{0}^{4}}{\theta_{0}^{4}-1}\mp\frac{15\pi\theta_{0}^{3}}{16(1+\theta_{0}^{2})^{3}}\epsilon+\frac{\theta_{0}^{2}}{384(1-\theta_{0}^{2})^{2}(1+\theta_{0}^{2})^{5}}\Bigg\{-1024(3-2\mathcal{D}^{2})

(49)

\displaystyle+1024(9+12\mathcal{D}-14\mathcal{D}^{2})\theta_{0}^{2}+[4096(3-\mathcal{D}^{2})-2025\pi^{2}]\theta_{0}^{4}-\left[2048(6+15\mathcal{D}-17\mathcal{D}^{2})-2025\pi^{2}\right]\theta_{0}^{6}

\displaystyle-1024(9+6\mathcal{D}-8\mathcal{D}^{2})\theta_{0}^{8}+1024\mathcal{D}(3+18\mathcal{D}-20\mathcal{D}^{2})\theta_{0}^{10}+6144\mathcal{D}(1-\mathcal{D})\theta_{0}^{12}\Bigg\}\epsilon^{2}+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{3}),

The leading-order result is well-documented in standard textbooks, such as
[
4
]
. When
\theta=\theta_{E}
, the Einstein angle, and
\theta_{0}=1
, the magnification diverges to infinity. These positions, where the magnification becomes infinite, define the critical curve. When the image lies outside the critical curve, the magnification is positive, corresponding to an upright image with positive parity. Conversely, inside the critical curve, the magnification is negative, indicating an inverted image with negative parity, as summarized in Table
1
. In the second-order terms, the
\pm
sign distinguishes between the two images: the upper sign for image 1 and the lower for image 2. The higher-order corrections have been derived by Ref.
[
31
]
using the Jacobian matrix formalism.

Let us compare our result (
49
) with the previous work
[
31
]
[Eq. (76-79), with
A_{1}=4
,
A_{2}=15\pi/4
, and
A_{3}=128/3
] and highlight the differences. In
[
31
]
, the magnification is defined using the Jacobian matrix,

\mu=\left|\frac{\sin\beta}{\sin\theta}\frac{d\beta}{d\theta}\right|^{-1},

(50)

which is consistent with Eq. (
49
) at the leading order,
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{0})
. The inconsistency appears at the first and second-order corrections, due to some missing corrections when Ref.
[
31
]
derives the higher-order corrections.

(1): It is noted that the images formed at the same and opposite sides to the source correspond to positive and negative angular momentum. Therefore the lens equations for images 1 and 2 are slightly different if we require the impact parameters to be positive. This is shown in Eqs. (
70
,
71
). However, Ref.
[
31
]
defined the impact parameter as
b\equiv L/ME
, rather than
b\equiv|L|/ME
, thus missing an extra
\pm
symbol in the first-order correction.

(2): As mentioned before, the plane perpendicular to the optical axis and passing through the lens object is usually referred to as the lens plane. As shown in Fig.
1
, the deflection point (marked by A) is not at such plane, unless
\mathrm{SN}=0
and
D_{L}=D_{LS}
. However, Ref.
[
31
]
assumed that point A always lies on the lens plane, which leads to inconsistency in the second order. The above two corrections are raised from the lens equation derived by Ref.
[
31
]
, which is incompatible with high-order approximations of weak deflection limit. Once a more accurate lens equation is given, one can still calculate the magnification from the Jacobian matrix (
50
).

(3): The definition in Eq. (
50
) ignored the slight difference between paths SAO and SO, shown in Fig.
1
. In the unlensed case, the signal detected by the observer is emitted from the source in the direction of SO, while the lensed signal is emitted in the direction of SA. To calculate such corrections, we denote the energy flux along SO and SA as
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{SO}}
and
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{SA}}
, respectively. With the help of SZ, which is perpendicular to SN and shown as the red line in Fig.
1
, the ratio
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{SA}}/\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{SO}}
is calculated as

\frac{\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{SA}}}{\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{SO}}}=\frac{\cos\angle\mathrm{ZSA}}{\cos\angle\mathrm{ZSO}}=\frac{\cos(\alpha\mp\theta)}{\cos\beta},

(51)

where we have used
\angle\mathrm{ZSO}=\beta
and
\angle\mathrm{ZSA}=\pi/2-\angle\mathrm{ASI}=\pi/2-(\pi-\alpha-\angle\mathrm{SIA})=\alpha\mp\theta
, with
\mp
corresponding to the image 1 and image 2. Considering corrections (1) and (2), and multiplying the factor (
51
) onto Eq. (
50
), one obtains the improved magnification, which is consistent with Eq. (
49
).

Another direct observational quantity is the axis ratio of the images, which describes the shape distortion of the background wave source. Similar to the derivation of magnification, the axis ratio
\mathcal{R}(\lambda_{o})
is given by,

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}

\displaystyle=\frac{\theta_{0}^{2}-1}{\theta_{0}^{2}+1}\mp\frac{15\pi(\theta_{0}^{2}-1)(1+3\theta_{0}^{2})}{16\theta_{0}(1+\theta_{0}^{2})^{3}}\epsilon+\frac{(\theta_{0}^{2}-1)}{768\theta_{0}^{2}(1+\theta_{0}^{2})^{5}}\Bigg\{-9(2048-225\pi^{2})

(52)

\displaystyle-[4096(18+3\mathcal{D}-4\mathcal{D}^{2})-8100\pi^{2}]\theta_{0}^{2}-3[4096(6+\mathcal{D}-\mathcal{D}^{2})-3375\pi^{2}]\theta_{0}^{4}

\displaystyle-8193\mathcal{D}^{2}\theta_{0}^{6}+6144(3-2\mathcal{D}+2\mathcal{D}^{2})\theta_{0}^{8}-4096\mathcal{D}(3-4\mathcal{D})\theta_{0}^{10}\Bigg\}\epsilon^{2}+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{3}).

The axis ratio is zero when the lensing image is located at the critical curve, with
\theta_{0}=1
. This means the extended source is distorted into a line segment, with zero area and infinite amplitude. Similar to the magnification, the axis ratio is positive/negative for the images located outside/inside the critical curve, representing whether the image is upright (positive parity) or inverted (negative parity). The numerical results of
\mathcal{R}
are listed in Table
1
.

In the leading order of weak deflection limit, the magnification and axis ratio are related to the convergence (
\kappa
) and shear (
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
) by

\mu=\frac{1}{(1-\kappa)^{2}-\gamma^{2}},\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{R}=\frac{1-\kappa-\gamma}{1-\kappa+\gamma},

(53)

with
\gamma=\sqrt{\gamma_{1}^{2}+\gamma_{2}^{2}}
. The convergence depends on the lens mass density within the geodesic congruence. The shear
\gamma_{1}
characterizes the stretching of the lensing images and
\gamma_{2}
characterizes its orientation. For the Schwarzschild lens, which is vacuum and axisymmetric,
\kappa=0
,
\gamma_{2}=0
, and
\gamma=\gamma_{1}
. Through the Jacobian matrix
[
4
]
, the shear of the point-mass lens is calculated as
\gamma=1/\theta_{0}^{2}
at the leading order of weak deflection approximation, resulting in
\mu=\theta_{0}^{4}/(\theta_{0}^{4}-1)
and
\mathcal{R}=(\theta_{0}^{2}-1)/(\theta_{0}^{2}+1)
, as shown in the leading terms of Eqs. (
49
) and (
52
). However, higher-order correction has been rarely studied. As calculated by Ref.
[
47
]
,
\kappa
and
\gamma_{2}
are expected to be nonzero at the subleading order. More importantly, besides convergence and shear, another optical scalar, rotation, should be defined to represent the anti-symmetric component of the Jacobian matrix. We are developing this calculation, which goes beyond the scope of this work and will be investigated in our future work.

V
Conclusion

In this work, we revisited the Schwarzschild lensing of light or GWs from geodesic deviation. The detected lensed signal consists of a congruence of light/GW rays, and their collective behavior is described by the GDE. To solve it, we projected the GDE onto the NP tetrad constructed along the null geodesics and got a pair of decoupled equations for
\xi_{+}
and
\xi_{-}
(
15
) for real Weyls scalar
\Psi_{0}
. Similar to the Dyson series, we presented the solution to the transverse components
\bm{\xi}_{\pm}
of the deviation vector in Eqs. (
21
) and (
22
). In addition, the relationship between the deviation vector and the optical scalars,
\rho
and
\sigma
, were presented in Eq. (
11
). The physical meaning of these two scalars is the relative rate of change of the congruence cross section and the axis ratio [see Eq. (
24
)].

Subsequently, we considered the lensing process in the weak deflection limit, where the impact parameter is sufficiently large. The general signature is shown in Fig.
1
; two images are formed by Schwarzschild lensing. In this assumption, the Dyson-like series was calculated from Eq. (
37
,
38
) analytically. We depicted such results in Fig.
2
, and compared them with the numerical solution for a group of parameters listed in Table
1
. We also found that
\mathcal{I}_{0}
and
\mathcal{I}_{1}
are dominant, and
\mathcal{I}_{2},\,\mathcal{I}_{3}
are close to zero, implying the series (
21
) is convergent in the weak deflection limit. Fig.
3
shows the evolution of the deviation vector.
\tilde{\xi}_{+}
increases more rapidly after lensing and keeps positive. And
\tilde{\xi}_{-}
increases more slowly or even decreases after lensing, meeting the caustic point, and finally forms an inverted image at the observer. Correspondingly, Figs.
4
and
5
show the cross-sectional area, axis ratio, and optical scalars, defined in Eqs. (
25
,
26
).

In subsection
IV.3
, we reproduced the magnification from the geodesic deviation [see Eq. (
49
)], which is consistent with the one obtained using the Jacobian matrix in Ref.
[
31
]
. Meanwhile, we found some missing corrections in the previous calculation, e.g., the deflection point deviates from the lens plane and the lens equations are slightly different for the two images. Additionally, we presented the result of the axis ratio, equivalently, the shear, of lensing images up to the second-order weak deflection approximation [see Eq. (
52
)].

This work provides a solid foundation for future studies. As discussed in Ref.
[
35
,
44
]
, the calculation of spin coefficients is indispensable when investigating the lensing effects beyond geometric optics, in which richer information on light and GW polarization is presented
[
48
,
49
,
50
,
51
,
52
,
53
]
. In this work, we calculated
\rho
and
\sigma
following the procedure in Ref.
[
34
]
in the simplest case, the Schwarzschild lensing. Furthermore, the astrophysical lens objects are more complex than a Schwarzschild black hole
[
54
]
. Extending the computation to a more general lens model is beneficial for lensing observations and tests of gravitational theories in the future.

Appendix A
Derivation to Eq. (
5
) and (
8
)

From left-hand side of Eq. (
5
), we have

\displaystyle m_{\nu}\left(\xi^{\alpha}\nabla_{\alpha}k^{\nu}\right)

\displaystyle=m_{\nu}\left[\xi^{(a)}e^{\alpha}_{(a)}\right]\nabla_{\alpha}k^{\nu}=\xi^{(a)}\left\{e^{\alpha}_{(a)}(\nabla_{\alpha}k_{\nu})m^{\nu}\right\}

(54)

\displaystyle=\xi^{(3)}\left\{e^{\alpha}_{(3)}(\nabla_{\alpha}k_{\nu})m^{\nu}\right\}+\xi^{(4)}\left\{e^{\alpha}_{(4)}(\nabla_{\alpha}k_{\nu})m^{\nu}\right\}

\displaystyle=\xi^{m}\Big[m^{\alpha}(\nabla_{\alpha}k_{\nu})m^{\nu}\Big]+\xi^{\bar{m}}\Big[m^{\alpha}(\nabla_{\alpha}k_{\nu})\bar{m}^{\nu}\Big]=\sigma\xi^{m}+\rho\xi^{\bar{m}}.

And from the right-hand side of Eq. (
5
), we have

\displaystyle m_{\nu}\left(k^{\alpha}\nabla_{\alpha}\xi^{\nu}\right)

\displaystyle=m_{\nu}\left[k^{\alpha}(\nabla_{\alpha}\xi^{\nu})\right]=m_{\nu}\left[k^{\alpha}(\partial_{\alpha}\xi^{\nu}+\Gamma_{\alpha\lambda}^{\nu}\xi^{\lambda})\right]

(55)

\displaystyle=m_{\nu}\left\{k^{\alpha}\partial_{\alpha}\left[\xi^{(a)}e_{(a)}^{\nu}\right]+k^{\alpha}\Gamma_{\alpha\lambda}^{\nu}\left[\xi^{(a)}e_{(a)}^{\lambda}\right]\right\}

\displaystyle=m_{\nu}\left\{\left[k^{\alpha}\partial_{\alpha}\xi^{(a)}\right]e_{(a)}^{\nu}+\xi^{(a)}k^{\alpha}\left[\partial_{\alpha}e_{(a)}^{\nu}+\Gamma_{\alpha\lambda}^{\nu}e_{(a)}^{\lambda}\right]\right\}

\displaystyle=m_{\nu}\left\{\left[\bm{\mathcal{D}}\xi^{(a)}\right]e_{(a)}^{\nu}+\xi^{(a)}k^{\alpha}\nabla_{\alpha}e_{(a)}^{\nu}\right\}=m_{\nu}\left[\bm{\mathcal{D}}\xi^{(a)}\right]e_{(a)}^{\nu}=\bm{\mathcal{D}}\xi^{\bar{m}}.

Combining them gives
\bm{\mathcal{D}}\xi^{\bar{m}}=\sigma\xi^{m}+\rho\xi^{\bar{m}}
, with its complex conjugate being Eq. (
11
). Similar with the derivation in Eq. (
55
), the second derivative to
\xi^{\nu}
is

m_{\nu}\frac{D^{2}\xi^{\nu}}{D\lambda^{2}}=m_{\nu}\frac{Dz^{\nu}}{D\lambda}=m_{\nu}k^{\alpha}\nabla_{\alpha}z^{\nu}=\bm{\mathcal{D}}z^{\bar{m}}=\bm{\mathcal{D}}\left(m_{\nu}z^{\nu}\right)=\bm{\mathcal{D}}\left[m_{\nu}\left(k^{\alpha}\nabla_{\alpha}\xi^{\nu}\right)\right]=\bm{\mathcal{D}}^{2}\xi^{\bar{m}}.

(56)

And then the left-hand side of Eq. (
8
) is

-m_{\nu}R^{\nu}_{\rho\alpha\beta}k^{\rho}k^{\beta}\xi^{\alpha}=-C_{\nu\rho\alpha\beta}m^{\nu}k^{\rho}e_{(a)}^{\alpha}k^{\beta}\xi^{(a)}=-C_{\nu\rho\alpha\beta}m^{\nu}k^{\rho}m^{\alpha}k^{\beta}\xi^{m}=-C_{mkmk}\xi^{m}=\Psi_{0}\xi^{m},

(57)

where
C_{\nu\rho\alpha\beta}=R_{\nu\rho\alpha\beta}
is the Weyl tensor in vacuum, and we have used
\xi^{n}=0
and
R_{mk\bar{m}k}=0
. The latter can be proved as follows. Firstly, from
C_{mk\bar{m}k}=\overline{C_{\bar{m}kmk}}=\overline{C_{mk\bar{m}k}}
, we find
C_{mk\bar{m}k}
is real. And then

C_{mk\bar{m}k}=\frac{1}{2}(C_{mk\bar{m}k}+C_{\bar{m}kmk})=\frac{1}{2}C_{\nu\rho\alpha\beta}(m^{\nu}\bar{m}^{\alpha}+\bar{m}^{\nu}m^{\alpha})k^{\rho}k^{\beta}=\frac{1}{2}C_{\nu\rho\alpha\beta}(g^{\nu\alpha}+k^{\nu}n^{\alpha}+k^{\alpha}n^{\nu})k^{\rho}k^{\beta}=0,

(58)

where the background metric is
g^{\alpha\beta}=-k^{\alpha}n^{\beta}-k^{\beta}n^{\alpha}+m^{\alpha}\bar{m}^{\beta}+m^{\beta}\bar{m}^{\alpha}
. Combining Eqs. (
56
) and (
57
), we get
\bm{\mathcal{D}}^{2}\xi^{\bar{m}}=\Psi_{0}\xi^{m}
, with the complex conjugate being Eq. (
12
).

Appendix B
Schwarzschild lensing

One defines
u\equiv M/r
, and then the photon/graviton trajectory is dictated by
[
3
]

\left(\frac{du}{d\varphi}\right)^{2}=\frac{1}{b^{2}}-u^{2}(1-2u)\equiv f(u),

(59)

The impact parameter is
b=|L|/M
. When
b>3\sqrt{3}
, the function
f(u)
possesses two different zero points, denoted by
0<u_{2}<u_{3}
, the allowed orbits should be located at interval
[0,u_{2}]
and
[u_{3},+\infty]
, where
f(u)>0
. These first kinds of orbits are open, in which the massless particle moves between an infinite region and a pericenter, corresponding to the gravitational lensing situation. The radial range of allowed motion is
r_{0}<r<\max\{r_{o},r_{s}\}
, or equivalently,
\min\{u_{o},u_{s}\}<u<u_{m}\equiv M/r_{m}
.
r_{o}
and
r_{s}
denote the radial coordinates of the wave source and observer. correspondingly,
u_{o}\equiv M/r_{o}
and
u_{s}\equiv M/r_{s}
.

In this work, we only focus on the weak deflection limit and thin lens approximation, in which the impact distance (
Mb
) is much larger than the gravitational radius of the lens black hole, and the distances from emitter/observer to lens object are very larger than the impact distance. Using a bookkeeper,
\epsilon
, the order of magnitude impact parameter, source/observer distance are

u_{m}\sim\frac{1}{b}\sim\epsilon,\quad\frac{r_{m}}{r_{s}},\frac{r_{m}}{r_{o}}\sim\epsilon.

(60)

The following calculations are based on the Taylor expansion by the bookkeeper
\epsilon
. Additionally, the explicit relationship between
u_{m}
and
b
is given by
f(u_{m})=0
,

b=\frac{1}{u_{m}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-2u_{m}}}=\frac{1}{u_{m}}\left[1+u_{m}+\frac{3}{2}u_{m}^{2}+\frac{5}{2}u_{m}^{3}+\mathcal{O}(u_{m}^{4})\right].

(61)

Following the standard steps, the trajectory equation
u=u(\varphi)
is

u=u_{m}\cos\varphi+u_{m}^{2}\left(1-\cos\varphi+\sin^{2}\varphi\right)+u_{m}^{3}\left[\left(2-\frac{3}{4}\sin^{2}\varphi\right)\cos\varphi-2(1+\sin^{2}\varphi)+\frac{15}{4}\varphi\sin\varphi\right]+\mathcal{O}(u_{m}^{3}),

(62)

Up to order
\mathcal{O}(u_{m}^{3})
. The deflection angle,
\alpha
, is obtained from the asymptotic behavior of solution (
62
), given by

\alpha=4u_{m}+\left(-4+\frac{15}{4}\pi\right)u_{m}^{2}+\left(\frac{122}{3}-\frac{15\pi}{2}\right)u_{m}^{3}.

(63)

After giving the deflection angle (
63
), we re-drive the lens equation in this appendix. We first investigate image 1 shown in Fig.
1(a)
, in which the source and image lie on the same side of the optical axis. In
\triangle
ASI, applying the sine theorem gives,

\frac{\text{SI}}{\sin\alpha}=\frac{\text{AI}}{\sin\angle\text{ASI}}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\frac{\text{NI}-\text{SN}}{\sin\alpha}=\frac{\text{OI}-\text{OA}}{\sin[\pi/2-(\alpha-\theta)]}=\frac{\text{OI}-\text{OA}}{\cos(\alpha-\theta)},

(64)

where we use
\angle\text{ASI}=\pi-\alpha-\angle\text{AIS}=\pi-\alpha-(\pi/2-\theta)=\pi/2-(\alpha-\theta)

and then

\left(\frac{\text{NI}}{\text{OI}}-\frac{\text{SN}}{\text{OI}}\right)\cos(\alpha-\theta)=\left(1-\frac{\text{OA}}{\text{OI}}\right)\sin\alpha,

(65)

or, equivalently

\left(\frac{D_{S}\tan\theta-D_{S}\tan\beta}{D_{S}\sec\theta}\right)\cos(\alpha-\theta)=\left(1-\frac{\text{OA}}{D_{S}\sec\theta}\right)\sin\alpha.

(66)

Then the length of line OA can be expressed from the
\triangle
OAL by sine theorem,

\frac{\text{OL}}{\sin\angle\text{OAL}}=\frac{\text{OA}}{\sin\angle\text{OLA}}.

(67)

Here, the line AL divides
\angle
OAS equally, such that the above equation becomes

\frac{D_{L}}{\sin(\pi-\alpha)/2}=\frac{\text{OA}}{\sin[\pi-\theta-(\pi-\alpha)/2]}.

(68)

Using the expression of OA in terms of
D_{L}
,
\theta
, and
\alpha
,

\text{OA}=D_{L}\frac{\cos(\alpha/2-\theta)}{\cos(\alpha/2)}.

(69)

We finally arrived at

\left(\tan\theta-\tan\beta\right)\cos\theta\cos(\alpha-\theta)=\sin\alpha-\frac{2D_{L}}{D_{S}}\cos\left(\frac{\alpha}{2}-\theta\right)\cos\theta\sin\frac{\alpha}{2}.

(70)

By replacing
\theta
by
-\theta
, and
\beta
by
-\beta
(equivalently, replace
\mathrm{NI}-\mathrm{SN}
by
\mathrm{NI}+\mathrm{SN}
) in Eq. (
70
), we get the lens equation for image 2,

-\left(\tan\theta-\tan\beta\right)\cos\theta\cos(\alpha+\theta)=\sin\alpha-\frac{2D_{L}}{D_{S}}\cos\left(\frac{\alpha}{2}+\theta\right)\cos\theta\sin\frac{\alpha}{2},

(71)

in which the source and image lie on the different sides of the optical axis.

To solve the lens equation (
70
,
71
), we rescale the source position and image position as

\beta=\beta_{0}\theta_{E},\quad\theta=\theta_{E}\left[\theta_{0}+\theta_{1}\epsilon+\theta_{2}\epsilon^{2}+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{3})\right],

(72)

where the bookkeeper
\epsilon
is defined in Eq. (
48
). Then the lens equation at leading order is simplified as

\beta_{0}=\theta_{0}-\frac{1}{\theta_{0}},

(73)

at the leading order, whose solution is

\theta_{0}=\frac{1}{2}\left[\beta_{0}\pm\sqrt{\beta_{0}^{2}+4}\right].

(74)

In the second and third orders, the solutions to
\theta_{1}
and
\theta_{2}
are

\theta_{1}=\pm\frac{15\pi}{16(1+\theta_{0}^{2})},

(75)

and

\displaystyle\theta_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3\theta_{0}(1+\theta_{0}^{2})^{3}}\Bigg\{48-\frac{675}{256}\pi^{2}-16\mathcal{D}^{2}+\left[8(9-3\mathcal{D}+2\mathcal{D}^{2})-\frac{675}{128}\pi^{2}\right]\theta_{0}^{2}

(76)

\displaystyle+40\mathcal{D}^{2}\theta_{0}^{4}-8(3-9\mathcal{D}+4\mathcal{D}^{2})\theta_{0}^{6}+8\mathcal{D}(6-5\mathcal{D})\theta_{0}^{8}\Bigg\}.

The plus-minus symbol corresponds to the image 1 and 2, respectively. The leading-order result (

[

75

Appendix C
Solution to the radial motion

In weak deflection limit, the radial potential reduces to

U_{r}=\pm\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\left\{1-u_{m}\left(\frac{x^{2}}{1+x}\right)-u_{m}^{2}\left[\frac{x^{2}(2+x)^{2}}{2(1+x)^{2}}\right]\right\}+\mathcal{O}(u_{m}^{3}).

(77)

where
x\equiv u/u_{m}
,
u=M/r\leqslant u_{m}
,
u_{m}=M/r_{m}\ll 1
, with
r_{m}
is the radius of pericenter. When the particle moves toward the pericenter, the above equation is integrated by

\displaystyle M^{-1}\lambda_{\rm I}=M^{-1}\int_{r_{s}}^{r}U_{r}^{-1}dr

\displaystyle\approx-\frac{1}{u_{m}}\left(\frac{\nu}{x}-\frac{\nu_{s}}{x_{s}}\right)-\left(\frac{\nu}{1+x}-\frac{\nu_{s}}{1+x_{s}}\right)

(78)

\displaystyle+\frac{u_{m}}{2}\left[\frac{x\nu}{(1+x)^{2}}-\frac{x_{s}\nu_{s}}{(1+x_{s})^{2}}+3\arcsin x-3\arcsin x_{s}\right],

with initial condition
\lambda_{s}=0
. For simplicity, we define

\nu=\sqrt{1-x^{2}}
, and
\nu_{s}=\sqrt{1-x_{s}^{2}}
. When arriving the pericenter,
r=r_{m}
, where
x=1
, the affine parameter is

\lambda_{m}=\frac{1}{u_{m}}\frac{M}{x_{s}}\left[1+x_{s}\left(u_{m}-\frac{x_{s}}{2}\right)\right],

(79)

And then, when moving backward, the integration should be

\displaystyle M^{-1}\lambda_{\rm II}

\displaystyle=M^{-1}\left\{\lambda_{m}+\int_{r_{m}}^{r}U_{r}^{-1}dr\right\}

(80)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{u_{m}}\left(\frac{\nu}{x}+\frac{\nu_{s}}{x_{s}}\right)+\left(\frac{\nu}{1+x}+\frac{\nu_{s}}{1+x_{s}}\right)

\displaystyle-\frac{u_{m}}{2}\left[\frac{x\nu}{(1+x)^{2}}+\frac{x_{s}\nu_{s}}{(1+x_{s})^{2}}+3\arcsin x+3\arcsin x_{s}-3\pi\right].

And the affine parameter at the observer position has been shown in Eq. (
43
).
