Clinical usability and functional outcomes of a lightweight robotic orthosis (HKAFO) in chronic stroke patients with hemiplegic gait: a pilot study
Authors: Yeo Joon Yun, Bo Kyoung Kim, Hyeonseok Cho, Hyuk-Jae Choi, Dongbin Shin, Hyeyoun Jang, Mi Jung Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGait impairment after stroke limits independence. Powered orthoses may augment task-specific over-ground practice, although clinical uptake is constrained by usability. We evaluated a powered hip–knee–ankle–foot orthosis (HKAFO) for functional outcomes and usability in ambulatory adults with chronic post-stroke hemiparesis.MethodsIn a prospective multicenter single-arm pilot, 15 participants completed six supervised over-ground HKAFO sessions across three weeks (40 min per session). Pre-specified clinical outcomes were the Berg Balance Scale (BBS), Dynamic Gait Index (DGI), and Timed Up and Go (TUG). Short-distance walking (6-minute walk test [6MWT], 10-meter walk test [10MWT]) was assessed with and without the device. Analyses used paired tests and a two-way repeated-measures ANOVA with within-subject factors Time (pre, post) and Condition (with HKAFO, without HKAFO).ResultsBBS increased by 3 points (45 [36–49] to 48 [42–52], p = 0.002), DGI by 2 points (19 [13–21] to 21 [18–23], p = 0.004), and TUG decreased by 1.98 s (16.80 [11.35–21.65] to 14.82 [9.85–20.39], p = 0.014). With the HKAFO, 6MWT distance rose (194.1 to 220.0 m, p = 0.006) and 10MWT speed increased (0.57 to 0.71 m/s, p = 0.005), whereas unassisted walking changed little over three weeks. Two-way repeated-measures ANOVA confirmed a main effect of Time and a strong main effect of Condition for 6MWT (Time: F(1,14) = 10.34, p = 0.006, ηp²=0.425; Condition: F(1,14) = 33.93, p < 0.001, ηp²=0.708), with a non-significant Time×Condition interaction (p = 0.090). For 10MWT cadence the interaction approached significance (F(1,14) = 4.23, p = 0.059, ηp²=0.232); for 10MWT speed and time, interactions were not significant, while the Condition main effect remained significant (speed: F(1,14) = 17.15, p = 0.010; time: F(1,14) = 7.46, p = 0.016). Participants reported perceived stability benefits alongside burdens related to weight, donning and doffing, and operation.ConclusionsAcross six sessions, HKAFO-assisted over-ground practice was associated with improved balance and functional mobility and with faster short-distance gait during device-assisted walking, with limited short-term transfer to unassisted gait. The two-condition design delineated a device-dependent profile. Confirmatory randomized trials with longer follow-up and usability-focused refinement are warranted.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12984-025-01770-1.Keywords: Hip-knee-ankle-foot orthosis (HKAFO), Stroke, Hemiplegic gait

Introduction

Gait impairment after stroke reduces independence and quality of life and is characterized by limited walking ability, reduced functional mobility, and impaired balance. A large proportion of survivors exhibit persistent mobility limitations despite rehabilitation; approximately 60% show significant deficits immediately after the acute event, and up to 30% continue to have considerable walking limitations thereafter. Falls are common, with annual rates of 50–73%, and are associated with increased morbidity, mortality, and healthcare utilization [1–4]. Conventional rehabilitation—intensive physical therapy with orthoses or walking aids—can help, but outcomes are often inconsistent and do not always generalize to daily activities; adherence may be reduced by fatigue, insufficient repetition, discomfort, and psychological factors such as low motivation or depression [5–8]. These limitations motivate interest in technologies that can deliver high-quality, task-specific practice and support sustained gains in ambulation.

Robotic-assisted gait training provides controlled, repetitive, task-specific practice that promotes neuromuscular activation and supports intensive motor learning. Clinical studies have reported improvements in speed, symmetry, endurance, and balance across neurological populations, particularly among stroke and spinal cord injury survivors [9–15]. Examples include treadmill-tethered and over-ground exoskeletons, which assist lower-limb movements and supply real-time sensorimotor feedback to facilitate motor relearning [13–15]. Such robotic exoskeletons typically function by mechanically assisting lower limb movements, facilitating repetitive practice, and providing real-time sensory-motor feedback to patients, which can accelerate motor relearning and neuromuscular recovery [16–18]. However, uptake outside structured environments remains constrained by usability burdens: device mass in the 18–25 kg range, mechanical rigidity, limited portability, complex interfaces, and extended donning and doffing. These barriers reduce clinical acceptance, patient adherence, and practicality for routine over-ground use [19, 20].

Electromechanical, treadmill‑tethered systems and end‑effector robots deliver controlled, repetitive stepping and are supported by Cochrane evidence for improving walking after stroke, particularly when combined with conventional therapy; however, longer‑term generalization and community translation remain variable. Over‑ground powered exoskeletons enable therapist‑assisted ambulation off the treadmill. A growing clinical and technical literature, including a meta‑analysis of randomized trials in stroke, reports small‑to‑moderate improvements in walking ability and speed while noting heterogeneity, short exposures, and very low certainty of evidence [21]. Soft wearable exosuits and hip‑assist devices target distal or proximal joints to improve symmetry and energetic cost; in stroke, a soft exosuit improved paretic propulsion, ankle kinematics, and reduced metabolic burden during walking [22]. A systematic review of wearable lower‑limb exoskeletons reported substantial heterogeneity in study populations, training dose, and outcome measures, with limited randomized evidence [23]. Across device classes, usabilityremains a leading determinant of uptake and satisfaction, motivating lighter, simpler systems for over-ground practice [24].

To address these limitations, recent work has emphasized lighter, more compact, and easier-to-use orthoses for community-based ambulation, with lightweight structures, streamlined interfaces, and customizable assistance [25–27]. Many systems integrate inertial measurement units, EMG-informed feedback, and wireless connectivity to enable point-of-care monitoring and adjustment [28, 29].

Within this evolving landscape, we evaluated a powered hip–knee–ankle–foot orthosis (HKAFO) designed to deliver phase-appropriate assistance during over-ground walking while reducing setup burden and accommodating alignment, thereby supporting task-specific practice in routine therapy corridors. We therefore conducted a prospective multicenter single-arm pilot in ambulatory adults with chronic post-stroke hemiparesis. Pre-specified outcomes were balance, dynamic gait, and functional mobility. Short-distance walking was assessed with and without the device to distinguish device-dependent augmentation from unassisted function. We hypothesized that short-term improvements would be detectable on clinical scales and greater during device-assisted walking than unassisted endurance.

Methods

Study design

This study was designed as a prospective, multicenter, single-arm, pilot trial conducted at Hanyang University Medical Center (HYMC) and Seongdong Rehabilitation Clinic. The study protocol was approved Institutional Review Board (IRB) of HYMC (IRB No. 202407-001) and adhered to the ethical guidelines outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Participants were recruited between August 2024 and October 2024, with the final follow-up assessments completed by October 11, 2024. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Participants completed a total of seven visits. At the first visit (screening), written informed consent was obtained, and participants were evaluated for eligibility based on demographic characteristics, disease-related information (including primary diagnosis causing gait impairment, Functional Ambulation Category (FAC) scale, currently assistive devices, and ability to follow three-step verbal commands). During the second visit (baseline) and the final visit (post-intervention), participants underwent comprehensive assessments including gait evaluations conducted both with and without the HKAFO, measurements of general physical function, perceived exertion, and overall health status. After completing six sessions of gait training, participants were asked to complete a satisfaction survey regarding their experience with the orthosis. All training sessions and assessments were conducted in a controlled clinical environment with continuous monitoring and supervision by trained clinical staff. Participants

Initially, 21 participants were enrolled in the study. Five withdrew after the initial screening visit (Visit 1), and one withdrew after Visit 2, leaving 15 participants who completed all study procedures. Sample size calculations using G*Power 3.1 determined that 14 participants would provide 80% power with a two-tailed α of 0.05, assuming a mean difference of 0.20 m/s (SD 0.20 m/s) in the 10-meter Walk Test speed (effect size d = 0.80). To accommodate an anticipated 30% attrition rate, enrollment was increased to 20 participants.

Eligible participants were adults aged ≥ 20 years diagnosed with chronic stroke and demonstrating hemiplegic gait, who could follow at least two steps of a three-step verbal command, had sufficient upper limb function to use an assistive device such as a cane or crutches, and maintained adequate passive range of motion in the hip, knee, and ankle joints. Independent ambulation, with or without assistive devices, was also required (FAC score 2–4). Individuals eligible for powered HKAFO training included those with gait impairment due to prolonged bed rest, surgical complications, frailty, or neurological injuries sustained at least two months prior with persistent lower limb weakness. The contralateral limb was required to have a manual muscle testing (MMT) grade of at least 3, while the affected limb could have an MMT grade of 4 or lower. Spasticity, assessed by the Modified Ashworth Scale (MAS), had to be between grades 0–2. Participants also needed to be medically stable enough to engage in at least one hour of gait training per day.

Exclusion criteria included physical characteristics preventing proper HKAFO fitting, such as extreme height, lower limb amputation, or excessive limb girth; use of implantable cardiac devices such as pacemakers or defibrillators; medically or neurologically unstable conditions such as uncontrolled seizures or severe ataxia; significant cognitive or communicative impairments; uncontrolled comorbidities such as diabetes, hypertension, or renal disease; severe lower limb deformities or fractures; bilateral upper limb dysfunction preventing device use; and any condition considered unsuitable by the investigators.

Device

A powered HKAFO developed by Hexar Humancare Co., Ltd. (Ansan, Korea) was used in this study (Fig. 1). This wearable gait-assist robot weighs approximately 7.6 kg and is secured to the pelvis, thigh, and lower leg with adjustable harnesses. Custom thigh and lower-leg harnesses were provided as necessary to ensure proper fitting and alignment.

Lightweight hip–knee–ankle–foot orthosis (HKAFO): A Rear view, B Front view, C Lateral view with participant wearing the device

The orthosis provides assistance through electromechanical actuators at the hip and knee, delivering up to 12 Nm of flexion–extension torque at the hip and up to 20 Nm at the knee. Each actuator includes an electric motor, reducer, controller, inertial measurement unit (IMU), and a Bluetooth 5.0 dual module (FB301BC, Firmtech, Korea). These components monitor joint angles and thigh inclination in real time, enabling accurate gait-phase estimation and appropriate torque-assistance commands. The main controller relays gait commands to the motor controller, which delivers the required torque to each joint.

The high-level controller uses current-based control (Fig. 2). For the hip joint, a gait-phase estimation algorithm predicts the gait cycle (0–100%). According to the estimated phase, a predefined torque profile with friction compensation is generated and the desired torque is delivered to the hip actuator. For the knee joint, the control strategy uses the hip-derived gait-phase information: during stance the knee is controlled by an impedance scheme, and during swing a torque profile is applied. The final control input is combined with a friction-compensation term and sent to the knee actuator as the desired torque.

High-level controller of HKAFO device: Current control framework based on gait phase estimation for hip and knee torque generation

The HKAFO supports a range of motion from − 25° to 110° at the hip and from 0° to 120° at the knee. A self-aligning mechanism with six degrees of freedom allows passive abduction and adduction, ankle dorsiflexion and plantar flexion, and active flexion and extension, promoting a more natural gait pattern. A built-in Bluetooth module enables data exchange within approximately 10 m, allowing external devices such as tablets to receive real-time feedback on device status and gait parameters and to adjust control settings as needed. The battery supports approximately 2 h of continuous operation.

Intervention

Participants completed six over-ground gait-training sessions with the HKAFO, twice per week for three consecutive weeks (40 min per session; 240 min total). Each training session lasted 40 min and involved supervised flat-surface walking exercises. Gait-training sessions were structured as follows: (i) two trials of the ten-meter walk test (10MWT), (ii) one trial of the six-minute walk test (6MWT), (iii) a seated rest period of approximately 10 min, and (iv) a second 6MWT. Sessions were carried out in a controlled clinical environment under continuous monitoring and supervision by at least one trained researcher or physical therapist.

Before each session, participants completed a 5-minute familiarization to confirm orthosis fit and comfort. The HKAFO was worn throughout training. For safety, an adjustable suspension harness attached to a standing/walking lift was used, and additional rests were permitted ad libitum. Assistance at the hip and knee was phase-appropriate and adjustable. The supervising clinician titrated assistance and cadence to tolerance, using device readouts to support symmetrical stepping and safe turning. All parameter changes, rests, and symptoms were documented in case-report forms.

The within-session walking bouts above were used for practice and progression and were not used for outcome scoring. Formal outcome assessments were obtained at baseline and after the 3-week program, as detailed in the Outcomes and Statistical Analysis sections. No adverse events were observed, and concomitant therapies were maintained unchanged during the intervention period.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were calculated to characterize the study population, including demographic data, clinical characteristics, and baseline measurements. Continuous variables are presented as means and standard deviations (SD) or medians and interquartile ranges (IQR), depending on normality assessment results. Categorical variables are reported as frequencies and percentages.

Data normality was assessed visually using histograms and statistically confirmed with the Shapiro-Wilk test. Paired comparisons of continuous variables measured pre- and post-intervention were conducted using paired t-tests for normally distributed data, whereas the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was utilized for data that were not normally distributed. For outcomes assessed under both conditions at both time points, we additionally performed a two-way repeated-measures ANOVA with within-subject factors Time (pre, post) and Condition (with HKAFO, without HKAFO), testing the main effects and their interaction. We report F-statistics with df1 = 1 and df2 = 14 and provide partial eta squared (ηp²) as an effect size. Because each factor has two levels, a sphericity correction is not required.

Statistical analyses aimed to determine the intervention’s effectiveness by comparing baseline and post-training outcomes, including gait parameters, functional scales, and physical assessments. A two-tailed p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (version 22.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), R software (version 4.1.3; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), and Matlab (version 2020b; MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA).

Results

Fifteen participants were enrolled in this study, with a mean age of 58 ± 10.73 years (Table 2). The mean duration from stroke onset to study enrollment was 5.47 ± 5.57 years, confirming that all participants were in the chronic phase post-stroke. Of the participants, 12 were male and 3 were female. The average height and weight were 166.37 ± 7.34 cm and 69.07 ± 12.48 kg, respectively, and the mean BMI was 24.85 ± 3.77 kg/m². The paralysis side was left in 6 participants and right in 9 participants. Assistive devices were used by 8 participants, while 7 did not use any assistive devices.

Primary outcome

Primary outcomes consisted of gait-related assessments (Table 3). Following six sessions of HKAFO-assisted gait training, significant improvements were noted in all primary outcomes. Specifically, BBS scores increased significantly from 45 (36–49) to 48 (42–52) (p = 0.002), DGI scores rose from 19 [3–21] to 21 [18–23] (p = 0.004), and TUGT times decreased significantly from 16.80 s [11.35–21.65] to 14.82 s [9.85–20.39] (p = 0.014). For the 6mWT, distance walked without the orthosis showed a non-significant increase from 253.4 m [173.7–305.9] to 269.7 m [195.1–308.4] (p = 0.088), while the distance with HKAFO significantly improved from 194.1 m [120–219.1] to 220.0 m [160–261.4] (p = 0.006). Regarding the 10MWT, walking time significantly decreased from 8.69s [7.35–12.15] to 6.96s [6.07–11.25] (p = 0.008), and gait speed significantly increased from 0.57 m/s [0.41–0.68] to 0.71 m/s [0.44–0.82] (p = 0.005) with the HKAFO; changes in the without-HKAFO condition were not statistically significant. Cadence did not show significant changes in either condition (p = 0.497 without HKAFO; p = 0.201 with HKAFO).

Two-way repeated-measures analysis of short-distance outcomes

We used a two-way repeated-measures framework with within-subject factors Time (pre, post) and Condition (with HKAFO, without HKAFO) to test whether pre–post change differed between conditions (Time × Condition). For 6MWT distance, there was a significant main effect of Time (F(1,14) = 10.34, p = 0.006) and a strong main effect of Condition (F(1,14) = 33.93, p < 0.001). The Time × Condition interaction favored larger pre–post improvement with the device but did not reach statistical significance at α = 0.05 (F(1,14) = 3.32, p = 0.090; partial η²=0.192). For 10MWT cadence, the interaction was near the significance threshold (F(1,14) = 4.23, p = 0.059; partial η²=0.232). For 10MWT speed and time, interactions were not significant (speed: F(1,14) = 1.63, p = 0.222; time: F(1,14) = 0.54, p = 0.474), although the main effect of Condition remained robust (speed: F(1,14) = 17.15, p = 0.010; time: F(1,14) = 7.46, p = 0.016). Overall, these analyses indicate that short-term gains were more apparent during device-assisted walking, consistent with the device-dependent pattern summarized in Tables 3 and 4; the modest sample size likely limited interaction-level significance (Table 5).

Secondary outcomes and participant-reported usability

Secondary measures changed minimally: aside from small shifts in potassium (decrease), hematocrit (slight increase), and red blood cell count (slight decrease), laboratory values were stable; pulmonary function, body composition, and spasticity showed no meaningful change; passive range of motion improved only for left knee flexion and manual muscle testing only for right hip abduction (Supplementary Tables 1–5). Satisfaction was mixed (60% overall), yet 80% preferred their prior gait-assist methods, most often citing device weight, donning/doffing burden, and operational difficulty; perceived benefits included improved stability and posture (Supplementary Table 6). Together, these signals align with the device-dependent pattern in the primary outcomes and underscore usability as the main determinant of uptake.

Discussion

In this single-arm pilot of ambulatory adults with chronic post-stroke hemiparesis, six over-ground sessions with a powered HKAFO were associated with improvements in balance and functional mobility and with faster short-distance gait when walking with the device. Representative changes included a 3-point increase in BBS, a 2-point increase in DGI, and a 1.98-s reduction in TUG (Table 3). With the HKAFO, 6MWT distance and 10MWT speed increased, whereas unassisted walking changed little over three weeks; between-condition gaps narrowed after training but persisted for 6MWT distance and cadence (Table 4). A two-way repeated-measures analysis showed significant main effects of time and condition and an interaction trend for cadence, with a similar pattern for 6MWT (Table 5). Together, these findings indicate a short-term, device-dependent pattern of benefit. Secondary physiologic measures changed minimally, and participant-reported feedback highlighted perceived stability benefits alongside usability burdens.

Previous work indicates that robotic-assisted gait training can improve gait speed and endurance after stroke, particularly with treadmill-tethered exoskeletons [30], yet translation to everyday over-ground mobility is often limited [13–15]. Because ecological validity depends on the practice context, over-ground devices may better target real-world ambulation but can face usability constraints such as device weight, donning and doffing, and operational complexity, which can blunt short-term carry-over. Within this context, we explicitly compared performance with and without the device to separate device-dependent gains from generalized recovery and to acknowledge that transfer to unassisted function typically requires longer exposure.

In our study, balance and functional mobility improved after a short course of over-ground device-assisted practice, with concordant change across the BBS, DGI and TUG. Prior robotic rehabilitation reports show mixed balance effects, likely due to differences in dose, practice setting, outcome selection, and patient characteristics [31, 32]. A plausible explanation is that phase-appropriate assistance at the hip and knee improved weight acceptance and control of the stance-to-swing transition, reduced reliance on compensatory strategies such as vaulting or hip hiking, and enabled accurate rehearsal of anticipatory and reactive postural adjustments during task-specific over-ground practice. Early neuromotor adaptation in timing and coordination can then be expressed during unassisted clinical testing. This profile is clinically meaningful because better balance and functional mobility are linked to lower fall risk and greater independence in activities of daily living. More durable generalization to unassisted walking will likely require higher cumulative exposure and improved usability, including lower device weight, simpler interfaces, and easier donning and doffing.

In this pilot, device-assisted short-distance gait improved, whereas unassisted gait changed little over six supervised sessions. Both the between-condition contrasts and the two-way repeated-measures analysis indicated main effects of time and condition with only modest interactions. Taken together, these findings suggest that the HKAFO acutely augments supported ambulation by stabilizing step timing and facilitating limb advancement, while generalization to unassisted endurance remains limited over a brief exposure and likely depends on greater cumulative practice and streamlined operation. Compared with earlier treadmill-tethered studies, our over-ground protocol clarifies what improves acutely and under which condition [21, 30, 33]. By assessing each participant at both time points under with- and without-device conditions, the design separates device-dependent augmentation from generalized recovery. The six-session dose further suggests that pace normalizes first, whereas endurance equalizes only with additional exposure. This design difference, together with the over-ground setting, explains why our findings align with prior reports of short-distance speed gains yet al.so show limited immediate carry-over to unassisted walking.

Over the brief six-session exposure, the overall pattern is most consistent with early neuromotor adaptation elicited by phase-appropriate assistance at the hip and knee. By stabilizing weight acceptance and the stance-to-swing transition, assistance likely reduced compensatory strategies such as vaulting and hip hiking and enabled more consistent limb advancement and foot clearance, which are precisely the transitions probed by BBS, DGI, and TUG. This interpretation accords with neuromechanical accounts that task-specific practice can rapidly improve timing, coordination, and postural control through spinal and supraspinal mechanisms, without requiring structural hypertrophy over short intervals [10, 11, 27, 28]. The near-threshold interaction for cadence and the persistence of the between-condition gap for 6MWT distance suggest that step timing and pace normalize earlier than endurance, a sequence that is compatible with prior robotic-gait work in stroke in which short-cycle training improves speed and dynamic stability while longer exposure is typically required for endurance and transfer [13, 14, 17, 30]. Evidence from a hip-assist exoskeleton trial also supports the plausibility of phase-appropriate assistance improving locomotor efficiency and functional mobility in stroke populations [20]. Taken together, our two-condition design and short-dose results support a mechanistic hypothesis in which phase-appropriate torque and alignment accommodation enhance postural transitions and step regularity early, whereas energy economy and fatigue resistance emerge with greater cumulative practice and progressive reduction of assistance, explaining the main-effect pattern in the two-way analyses and the residual between-condition differences at post-training.

Several design features of the HKAFO likely enabled the clinical pattern described above. First, gait-phase estimation with current control delivered phase appropriate torque at the hip and knee. Assistance was smoother and more physiologically relevant than position- or velocity-driven schemes, and it reduced compensatory movements during over-ground practice. Second, a unilateral configuration on the paretic limb produced meaningful change while keeping device mass and mechanical complexity low. This supports a practical and potentially cost-effective alternative to heavier bilateral systems. Third, Bluetooth based real time monitoring allowed clinicians to track joint kinematics and phase timing, adjust assistance at the point of care, and deploy the device in routine therapy corridors rather than only in laboratories. Together these innovations address long-standing limitations of robotic gait devices. These features align with the device-dependent gains in short-distance gait and with the unassisted improvements in balance and functional mobility. Together, these observations support broader over-ground implementation in stroke rehabilitation.

An over-ground powered HKAFO can be integrated as a therapeutic adjunct within standard forty-minute sessions and routine corridor practice. In early inpatient care it supports safe weight acceptance and stepping while therapists control risk with a harness and lift. In outpatient programs it is suited to building pace and community ambulation through task-specific over-ground walking with adjustable assistance and progressive challenge. As usability improves it can transition to hybrid models that combine supervised sessions with structured home use and simple remote check-ins. The device offers practical advantages for clinical uptake. A unilateral configuration lowers mass and setup burden and fits high-throughput workflows. Real-time monitoring allows point-of-care titration and stepwise reduction of assistance as patients adapt. Candidate selection can focus on ambulatory chronic stroke who can don the device with minimal help and follow safety instructions. These features preserve ecological validity and provide a controllable bridge from assisted practice to unassisted walking, clarifying where the device fits in multidisciplinary rehabilitation and why it is useful.

This study has several important limitations that must be acknowledged. Firstly, the small sample size limits the generalizability of our findings and the statistical power to detect subtle yet clinically meaningful changes. Secondly, the absence of a control group prevents definitive attribution of observed improvements exclusively to the HKAFO intervention; although outcomes were assessed with and without the device at each time point to contextualize device-dependent changes, these within-subject contrasts do not substitute for randomization or a standard-of-care comparator. Thirdly, the short duration of the intervention (six sessions over three weeks) may not be sufficient to demonstrate long-term benefits or sustainable improvements. Finally, long-term adherence and sustained effects beyond the immediate intervention period were not assessed, potentially limiting the clinical relevance of these findings in chronic rehabilitation settings.

Future work should evaluate the HKAFO in adequately powered, multicenter randomized trials with pre-specified primary endpoints, blinded outcome assessment, and extended follow-up to test durability and transfer to unassisted mobility. Endpoints should include falls, health-related quality of life, adherence, and cost-effectiveness to inform scalable adoption. In parallel, iterative device refinement should prioritize weight reduction, simplified interfaces, and faster donning and doffing; implementation studies should assess home or hybrid use under standardized eligibility, dosing, adherence, and safety monitoring.

Conclusion

In this single-arm pilot of ambulatory adults with chronic post-stroke hemiparesis, six sessions of over-ground training with a powered HKAFO were associated with improvements in balance, dynamic gait, and functional mobility. Device-assisted short-distance gait also improved, whereas unassisted gait changed little over three weeks. Assessing outcomes with and without the device delineated a short-term pattern of benefit that coexisted with usability burdens, including weight, donning and doffing, and operational complexity. A powered HKAFO may therefore serve as a therapeutic adjunct for task-specific over-ground practice. Randomized controlled trials with longer follow-up are needed to establish clinical effectiveness, durability, and transfer to unassisted community ambulation.
