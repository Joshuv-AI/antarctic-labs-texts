Formal Integration of Electron Scattering Processes via Separation of Dynamical and Geometric Contributions
Lorenzo Bagnasacco, Fabio Taddei, Vittorio Giovannetti

Abstract
By decoupling the geometric from the dynamical contributions in the scattering processes, we develop a method to compute the scattering matrix of electrons in a one-dimensional coherent conductor connected to two electrodes. In particular, we demonstrate that, in the high-energy regime, the transmission matrix converges to the Berry operator of the system. We showcase the method through several examples featuring different in-plane magnetic field profiles. Notably, our results reveal the possibility of achieving near-perfect spin-flip transmission, highlighting potential applications in spintronics.

II
Physical model

We consider a minimal theoretical setup where electron dynamics are described within the
effective-mass approximation
[
29
]
. In this widely used framework, the kinetic
term takes a quadratic form, corresponding to a parabolic dispersion relation for the
propagating modes
[
30
]
. This approximation is standard in mesoscopic
transport theory and provides a reliable description over a broad range of energies
[
31
,
32
]
.
The Hamiltonian describing the propagation of electrons in a 1D wire under the action of a position-dependent magnetic field
\bm{B}(y)

writes

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{H}}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\frac{\hat{p}^{2}_{y}}{2m}-\bm{\mu}_{s}\cdot\bm{B}(y)\;,

(1)

with
p_{y}:=-(i/\hbar)\partial_{y}
denoting the electron momentum corresponding to the operator
\hat{p}_{y}
along the longitudinal axis
y
of the device and

\bm{\mu}_{s}
the electronic spin magnetic momentum. Indicating with

g(>0)
the Landé factor,
\mu_{B}(>0)
the Bohr magneton, and
\hat{\bm{\sigma}}:=(\hat{\sigma}_{x},\hat{\sigma}_{y},\hat{\sigma}_{z})
the vector of Pauli operators, the second contribution of
\hat{\mathcal{H}}
can be expressed as

\displaystyle\hat{h}_{y}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle-\bm{\mu}_{s}\cdot\bm{B}(y)=g\mu_{B}{B}(y)\hat{{\sigma}}_{\bm{n}(y)}\;,

(2)

where
B(y):=|\bm{B}(y)|
and
\bm{n}(y):=\bm{B}(y)/B(y)
indicate respectively the magnitude and the orientation of the magnetic field at position
y
, and
\hat{{\sigma}}_{\bm{n}(y)}:=\bm{n}(y)\cdot\hat{\bm{\sigma}}
is the spin operator component along

\bm{n}(y)
.
For fixed
y
, the eigenvectors of
\hat{h}_{y}
identify the
Zeeman spin-eigenstates,

\hat{h}_{y}\ket*{\phi^{(\ell)}_{{\bm n}_y}}={{E}_{{\rm Z}}}_{y}^{(\ell)}\ket*{\phi^{(\ell)}_{{\bm n}_y}},\quad{{E}_{{\rm Z}}}_{y}^{(\ell)}:=-(-1)^{\ell}g\mu_{B}|\bm{B}({y})|\;,

(3)

with
\ell=\{0,1\}
. In particular, we will refer to
\ket*{\phi^{(0)}_{{\bm n}(y)}}
as the (local)
lower-energy
Zeeman spin-eigenstate, while to
\ket*{\phi^{(1)}_{{\bm n}(y)}}
as the (local)
higher-energy
Zeeman spin-eigenstate (recall that
g\mu_{B}
is positive).

In our analysis, we partition the wire into three distinct regions: the left lead (
y\leq y_{\rm L}
), the right lead (
y_{\rm R}\leq y
), and the scattering region (
y_{\rm L}<y<y_{\rm R}
), see Fig.
1
. In the left and right leads, the magnetic field is uniform, assuming constant values
\bm{B}_{\rm L}:=\absolutevalue{\bm{B}_{\rm L}}\bm{n}_{\rm L}
and
\bm{B}_{\rm R}:=\absolutevalue{\bm{B}_{\rm R}}\bm{n}_{\rm R}
, respectively.
We assume the magnitudes of these fields are equal, such that
\absolutevalue{\bm{B}_{\rm L}}=\absolutevalue{\bm{B}_{\rm R}}=B_{0}

while allowing their orientations, denoted as
\bm{n}_{\rm L}
and
\bm{n}_{\rm R}
,
to differ.
In contrast, the magnetic field in the scattering region is not constant providing a somehow smooth transition between
\bm{B}_{\rm L}
and
\bm{B}_{\rm R}
(more precisely we shall require
\bm{B}(y)
be differentiable everywhere with first derivative that is continuous at the interface between the leads and the scattering region).
In virtue of these assumptions
in the leads the system exhibits spin-resolved energy bands

\displaystyle E^{(\ell)}(k):=\frac{\hbar^{2}k^{2}}{2m}+{{E}_{{\rm Z}}}^{(\ell)}\qquad\ell\in\{0,1\}\;,

(4)

where

{{E}_{{\rm Z}}}^{(\ell)}:=-(-1)^{\ell}g\mu_{B}B_{0}
are the eigenvalues (
3
) evaluated outside the scattering region, whose gap

\displaystyle E_{\rm Z}:=\frac{{{E}_{{\rm Z}}}^{(1)}-{{E}_{{\rm Z}}}^{(0)}}{2}=g\mu_{B}B_{0}\;,

(5)

defines the fundamental energy scale of the problem.
Electrons with sufficiently high energy
E
can occupy these bands. Their redistribution across the scattering region, as well as the current flowing through the device,
is governed by the transmission and reflection scattering matrices
t_{E}
and
r_{E}
of the model. Computing them accounts in solving the energy-resolved Schrödinger equation of the setup, i.e.

(\hat{\mathcal{H}}-E)\ket*{\psi^{(E)}_y}=-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\partial^{2}_{y}\ket*{ \psi^{(E)}_y}+(\hat{h}_{y}-E)\ket*{\psi^{(E)}_y}=0\;,

(6)

with
\ket*{ \psi^{(E)}_y}
the spinor wave-function associated with energy eigenvalue
E
.
In the leads the solution of Eq. (
6
) can be expressed as

\ket*{\psi^{(E)}_{y}}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\sum_{\ell\in\{0,1\}}\left(A_{E}^{(\ell)}e^{ik_{E}^{(\ell)}y}+R_{E}^{(\ell)}e^{-ik_{E}^{(\ell)}y}\right)\ket*{\phi^{(\ell)}_{\bm{n}_{\rm L}}},\\
\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\forall y\leq y_{\rm L}\\
\\
\sum_{\ell\in\{0,1\}}T_{E}^{(\ell)}e^{ik_{E}^{(\ell)}y}\ket*{\phi^{(\ell)}_{\bm{n}_{\rm R}}}\;,\qquad\forall y\geq y_{\rm R}\;,\end{array}\right.\\

(7)

with
\ket*{\phi^{(\ell)}_{\bm{n}_{{\rm L}/{\rm R}}}}
the Zeeman eigenvectors (
3
) evaluated for
y=y_{\rm L},y_{\rm R}
respectively,
and

\displaystyle k_{E}^{(\ell)}:=\sqrt{2m(E-{{E}_{{\rm Z}}}^{(\ell)})}/\hbar\;,

(8)

determined by the dispersion relation (
4
) by imposing the constraint
E^{(\ell)}(k)=E
.
We emphasize that for
E\leq{{E}_{{\rm Z}}}^{(\ell)}

the coefficients
A_{E}^{(\ell)}
,
R_{E}^{(\ell)}
and
T_{E}^{(\ell)}

describe evanescent waves. These waves decay exponentially with distance from the scattering region and, as a result, do not carry any current.
In contrast for

E>{{E}_{{\rm Z}}}^{(\ell)}
,
A_{E}^{(\ell)}
,
R_{E}^{(\ell)}
, and
T_{E}^{(\ell)}
characterize the asymptotic behavior of
\ket*{\psi^{(E)}_{y}}
far away
from the scattering region.
Specifically in this regime
A_{E}^{(\ell)}
represents right-going electrons injected into the
\ell
-th
energy band (
4
) of the left lead,
R_{E}^{(\ell)}

denotes the associated amplitude of left-going reflected electrons, and
T_{E}^{(\ell)}
the amplitude of right-going transmitted electrons.
The functional dependence among these terms, determined by explicitly solving
Eq. (
6
), define the transmission
t_{E}
and reflection
r_{E}
matrix of the model.

Consider for instance the
two-channel
regime where

E
exceeds the biggest Zeeman eigenvalues in the leads, i.e.

\displaystyle E>{{E}^{(1)}_{{\rm Z}}}={{E}_{{\rm Z}}}\;.

(9)

In this regime, both the energy bands of the system can be populated, and

t_{E}
and
r_{E}
correspond to

2\times 2
matrices. The elements of these matrices
[
29
]
are defined by the following relations:

[t_{E}]_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}}:=\tau_{E}^{(\ell,\ell^{\prime})}\sqrt{\tfrac{k_{E}^{(\ell)}}{k_{E}^{(\ell^{\prime})}}}\;,\quad[r_{E}]_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}}:=\rho_{E}^{(\ell,\ell^{\prime})}\sqrt{\tfrac{k_{E}^{(\ell)}}{k_{E}^{(\ell^{\prime})}}}\;,

(10)

where
\tau_{E}^{(\ell,\ell^{\prime})}
and
\rho_{E}^{(\ell,\ell^{\prime})}
) are, respectively, the values of amplitudes
T_{E}^{(\ell)}
and

R_{E}^{(\ell)}
appearing in Eq. (
7
)
that emerge when we take

A_{E}^{(\ell)}=\delta_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}}
.
In particular,
[t_{E}]_{\ell,0}
(resp.
[r_{E}]_{\ell,0}
) represents the probability amplitude for an electron starting in the
lower-energy
eigenstate in the left lead and arriving in the
lower-energy
right (left) lead eigenstate if
\ell=0
, or in the
higher-energy
right (left) lead eigenstate if
\ell=1
. Contrarily,
[t_{E}]_{\ell,1}
(resp.
[r_{E}]_{\ell,1}
) represents the probability amplitude for an electron starting in the
higher-energy
left lead eigenstate and arriving in the
lower-energy
right (left) lead eigenstate if
\ell=0
, or in the
higher-energy
right (left) lead eigenstate if
\ell=1
.
With these definitions, current conservation is ensured by the unitarity relation

r_{E}^{\dagger}r_{E}+t_{E}^{\dagger}t_{E}=\openone\;.

(11)

For the sake of completeness, we derive this result in Appx.
A
.

In the
single-channel
regime the injection energy
E
is larger than the lowest eigenvalue in the leads but smaller than their highest eigenvalue, i.e.

\displaystyle E^{(1)}_{\rm Z}=E_{\rm Z}>E>-E_{\rm Z}=E^{(0)}_{\rm Z}\;.

(12)

Under this condition only the lowest band supports electrons far from the scattering region. In this case
t_{E}
and
r_{E}
can still be defined as in (
10
), but the only elements
associated with the low-energy eigenstates contribute to the current. Specifically,
the term
[t_{E}]_{0,0}
which represents the probability amplitude for an electron starting in the
lower-energy
eigenstate in the left lead and arriving in the
lower-energy
right lead eigenstate, and the element
[r_{E}]_{0,0}
, which represents
the reflection amplitude for the same eigenstate.
In this case, the current conservation identity (
11
) simplifies to:

|[r_{E}]_{0,0}|^{2}+|[t_{E}]_{0,0}|^{2}=1\;.

(13)

III
Solving the scattering problem

To compute the values of the matrices
t_{E}
and
r_{E}
we need to explicitly find a spinor
wave-function
\ket*{\psi^{(E)}_y}
which solves
Eq. (
6
) for all
y
, and used it to identifying the coefficients
A_{E}^{(\ell)}
,

R_{E}^{(\ell)}
, and
T_{E}^{(\ell)}
that enters in Eq. (
7
).
For this purpose, following Ref.
[
21
]
, we expand

\ket*{\psi^{(E)}_y}
w.r.t. the local eigenstates (
3
) of the spin operator
\hat{h}_{y}
, i.e.

\ket*{\psi^{(E)}_y}=\sum_{\ell=0,1}C^{(\ell)}_{y}\ket*{\phi^{(\ell)}_{{\bm n}_y}}\;,

(14)

with
C^{(\ell)}_{y}:=\langle{\phi^{(\ell)}_{{\bm{n}}_{y}}}\ket*{\psi^{(E)}_y}
being (coordinate-dependent) complex probabilities amplitudes. With this choice Eq. (
6
) assumes the form

(\partial_{y}+K_{y})^{2}\bm{C}_{y}+\frac{2m}{\hbar^{2}}(E\mathbb{1}-\Omega_{y})\bm{C}_{y}=0\;,

(15)

where
\bm{C}_{y}:=(C^{(0)}_{y},C^{(1)}_{y})^{T}
is the column vector of coefficients
C^{(\ell)}_{y}
,

\Omega_{y}
is a
2\times 2
diagonal matrix whose elements are the instantaneous eigenvalues in Eq. (
3
),

\displaystyle[\Omega_{y}]_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}}:=E^{(\ell)}_{Z_{y}}\delta_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}}\;,

(16)

and finally
K_{y}
is the
2\times 2
Berry matrix
[
33
]
of the process, whose elements are defined as

[K_{y}]_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}}:=\innerproduct*{\phi^{(\ell)}_{{\bm n}_y}}{\partial_y \phi^{(\ell')}_{{\bm n}_y}}.

(17)

By construction
K_{y}
is a skew-matrix
which assumes zero value in the leads and varies continuously at the interface with the
scattering region, i.e.

\displaystyle K_{y}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-K^{\dagger}_{y}\;,

(18)

\displaystyle K_{y}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0\;,\qquad\forall y\notin\;]y_{\rm L},y_{\rm R}[\;.

(19)

We stress that the second request is less demanding than it might initially appear. This is due to the fact that, at large distances from the scattering region, the magnetic field becomes constant, taking the values
\bm{B}_{\rm L}
and
\bm{B}_{\rm R}
.
Consequently, we can enforce condition (
19
) by simply redefining the boundaries of the scattering region as

\displaystyle y_{\rm L}\rightarrow y_{\rm L}^{-}:=y_{\rm L}-\epsilon\;,\qquad y_{\rm R}\rightarrow y_{\rm R}^{+}:=y_{\rm R}+\epsilon\;,

(20)

with
\epsilon>0
is an
infinitesimal positive shift.
This regularization allows us to accommodate configurations in which
\bm{B}({y})
exhibits an explicit discontinuity at the borders of the scattering region.
The matrix
K_{y}
defines the Berry operator of the model via the following unitary transformation

\mathcal{U}_{y_{1}\to y_{2}}:=\overleftarrow{\exp}\left\{-\int_{y_{1}}^{y_{2}}dyK_{y}\right\}\;,

(21)

where
y_{1}\leq y_{2}
, and
\overleftarrow{\exp}[\cdots]
indicates path ordering of the operators product. In this sense,
\mathcal{U}_{y_{1}\to y_{2}}
plays the role of a Wilson line (the open-loop analog of a Wilson loop
[
34
]
) in gauge theory, representing the gauge-invariant holonomy of the Berry connection
K_{y}
along the path from
y_{1}
to
y_{2}
. As discussed in Sec.
III.1
, setting
\bm{D}_{y}:=\partial_{y}\bm{C}_{y}
the first derivative of the vector
\bm{C}_{y}
, the continuity
constraint (
19
) allows us to formally integrate
Eq. (
15
) producing the identity

\begin{bmatrix}\bm{C}_{y_{\rm R}}\\
\bm{D}_{y_{\rm R}}\end{bmatrix}=\Gamma^{(E)}_{y_{\rm L}\rightarrow y_{\rm R}}\begin{bmatrix}\bm{C}_{y_{\rm L}}\\
\bm{D}_{y_{\rm L}}\end{bmatrix}\;,

(22)

with
\Gamma^{(E)}_{y_{\rm L}\rightarrow y_{\rm R}}
the generalized transfer matrix
of the process. Factorizing out the Berry operator contribution, the matrix
\Gamma^{(E)}_{y_{\rm L}\to y_{\rm R}}
can be expressed as

\displaystyle\Gamma^{(E)}_{y_{\rm L}\rightarrow y_{\rm R}}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{U}_{y_{\rm L}\rightarrow y_{\rm R}}&0\\
0&\mathcal{U}_{y_{\rm L}\rightarrow y_{\rm R}}\end{bmatrix}\tilde{\Gamma}^{(E)}_{y_{\rm L}\rightarrow y_{\rm R}}\;,

(23)

with the energy dependent term

\displaystyle\tilde{\Gamma}^{(E)}_{y_{\rm L}\rightarrow y_{\rm R}}:=\overleftarrow{{\rm\exp}}\left\{\int_{y_{\rm L}}^{y_{\rm R}}dy\tilde{\mathcal{M}}^{(E)}_{y}\right\}\;,

(24)

generated by the
4\times 4
block matrix

\tilde{\mathcal{M}}^{(E)}_{y}:=\begin{bmatrix}0&\mathbb{1}\\
\frac{2m}{\hbar^{2}}(\tilde{\Omega}_{y}-E\mathbb{1})&0\end{bmatrix}\;,\quad\tilde{\Omega}_{y}:=\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}_{y_{\rm L}\rightarrow y}\Omega_{y}\mathcal{U}_{y_{\rm L}\rightarrow y}\;.

(25)

The matrix
\tilde{\mathcal{M}}_{y}^{{\dagger}(E)}

plays a crucial role in the scattering problem. Specifically, as demonstrated in Appx.
A
, it guarantees current conservation (
11
) through the symmetry relation:

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{M}}_{y}^{{\dagger}(E)}\begin{bmatrix}0&\mathbb{1}\\
-\mathbb{1}&0\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}0&\mathbb{1}\\
-\mathbb{1}&0\end{bmatrix}\tilde{\mathcal{M}}_{y}^{(E)}=0\;.

(26)

Recalling
Eq. (
7
) we can write

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\bm{C}_{y_{\rm L}}\\
\bm{D}_{y_{\rm L}}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}{\scriptstyle A_{E}^{(0)}e^{ik_{E}^{(0)}y_{\rm L}}+R_{E}^{(0)}e^{-ik_{E}^{(0)}y_{\rm L}}}\\
{\scriptstyle A_{E}^{(1)}e^{ik_{E}^{(1)}y_{\rm L}}+R_{E}^{(1)}e^{-ik_{E}^{(1)}y_{\rm L}}}\\
{\scriptstyle ik_{E}^{(0)}(A_{E}^{(0)}e^{ik_{E}^{(0)}y_{\rm L}}-R_{E}^{(0)}e^{-ik_{E}^{(0)}y_{\rm L}})}\\
{\scriptstyle ik_{E}^{(1)}(A_{E}^{(1)}e^{ik_{E}^{(1)}y_{\rm L}}-R_{E}^{(1)}e^{-ik_{E}^{(1)}y_{\rm L}})}\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\bm{C}_{y_{\rm R}}\\
\bm{D}_{y_{\rm R}}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}{\scriptstyle T_{E}^{(0)}e^{ik_{E}^{(0)}y_{\rm R}}}\\
{\scriptstyle T_{E}^{(1)}e^{ik_{E}^{(1)}y_{\rm R}}}\\
{\scriptstyle ik_{E}^{(0)}T_{E}^{(0)}e^{ik_{E}^{(0)}y_{\rm R}}}\\
{\scriptstyle ik_{E}^{(1)}T_{E}^{(1)}e^{ik_{E}^{(1)}y_{\rm R}}}\end{pmatrix}\;,

(27)

which replaced in Eq. (
22
), leads to a set of 4 linear identities that link the amplitudes
T_{E}^{(\ell)}
and
R_{E}^{(\ell)}
to
the input amplitudes
A_{E}^{(\ell)}
.
In conjunction with Eq. (
10
) this results in two linear
equations for
t_{E}
and
r_{E}
, i.e.

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}W^{-1}t_{E}W=\;F^{\dagger}_{\rm R}\mathcal{U}_{y_{\rm L}\to y_{\rm R}}\Big(X_{0,0}+iX_{0,1}V\Big)F_{\rm L}\\
\qquad\qquad+F^{\dagger}_{\rm R}\mathcal{U}_{y_{\rm L}\to y_{\rm R}}\Big(X_{0,0}-iX_{0,1}V\Big)F^{\dagger}_{\rm L}W^{-1}r_{E}W\;,\\
\\
iVW^{-1}t_{E}W=\;F^{\dagger}_{\rm R}\mathcal{U}_{y_{\rm L}\to y_{\rm R}}\Big(X_{1,0}+iX_{1,1}V\Big)F_{\rm L}\\
\qquad\qquad+F^{\dagger}_{\rm R}\mathcal{U}_{y_{\rm L}\to y_{\rm R}}\Big(X_{1,0}-iX_{1,1}V\Big)F^{\dagger}_{\rm L}W^{-1}r_{E}W\;,\end{array}\right.

(28)

where

W:=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&\sqrt{\tfrac{k_{E}^{(1)}}{k_{E}^{(0)}}}\end{pmatrix}\;,\;\;\;V:=\begin{pmatrix}k_{E}^{(0)}&0\\
0&k_{E}^{(1)}\end{pmatrix}\;

(29)

and

F_{{\rm L}}:=\begin{pmatrix}e^{ik_{E}^{(0)}y_{{\rm L}}}&0\\
0&e^{ik_{E}^{(1)}y_{{\rm L}}}\end{pmatrix}\;,\;\;\;F_{{\rm R}}:=\begin{pmatrix}e^{ik_{E}^{(0)}y_{{\rm R}}}&0\\
0&e^{ik_{E}^{(1)}y_{{\rm R}}}\end{pmatrix}\;,

(30)

are
2\times 2
diagonal matrices defined also in Eqs. (
96
) and (
101
) of
Appx.
A.1
. Moreover,
X_{i,j}
are the
2\times 2
blocks of
\tilde{\Gamma}^{(E)}_{y_{\rm L}\rightarrow y_{\rm R}}
, namely

\tilde{\Gamma}^{(E)}_{y_{\rm L}\rightarrow y_{\rm R}}=\overleftarrow{{\rm\exp}}\left\{\int_{y_{\rm L}}^{y_{\rm R}}dy\,\tilde{\mathcal{M}}^{(E)}_{y}\right\}=\begin{bmatrix}X_{0,0}&X_{0,1}\\
X_{1,0}&X_{1,1}\end{bmatrix},

(31)

defined also in Eq. (
118
). At odds with Ref.
[
21
]
, the scattering amplitudes are now calculated exactly at any given energy
E
.

With the exception of the Berry operator
\mathcal{U}_{y_{\rm L}\to y_{\rm R}}
and of the block matrices

, all quantities entering into Eq. (
28
) admit a closed form solution. Indeed, the main challenge lies in evaluating
\mathcal{U}_{y_{\rm L}\to y_{\rm R}}
and

, since both are defined as solutions of path-ordered exponentials that, in general, do not admit a closed form solution. As will be illustrated in the following sections through specific examples and special regimes, both
\mathcal{U}_{y_{\rm L}\to y_{\rm R}}
and

can admit a closed form; in such cases, the reflection and transmission matrices themselves admit a fully closed form. Most importantly, the system of Eq. (

) highlights the separation between the dynamical and geometric contributions to the scattering problem. On the one hand, the matrices
W
,
F_{\rm L}
,
F_{\rm R}
, and
V
are purely dynamical, as they depend solely on the wave vectors of the asymptotic states
k^{(0/1)}_{E}
, while the Berry operator
\mathcal{U}_{y_{\rm L}\to y_{\rm R}}
is purely geometric. On the other hand, the block matrices
X_{i,j}
of Eq. (
31
) generally cannot be separated into dynamical and geometric components. However, we anticipate that in special regimes the
X_{i,j}
matrices simplify and become fully dynamical.

III.1
Derivation of Eq. (
22
)

Define the vectors

\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{C}}_{y}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}_{y_{\rm L}\to y}\bm{C}_{y}\;,

(32)

\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{D}}_{y}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\partial_{y}\tilde{\bm{C}}_{y}=\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}_{y_{\rm L}\rightarrow y}\left(\bm{D}_{y}+K_{y}{\bm{C}}_{y}\right)\;,

(33)

where in the second identity of Eq. (
33
) we used

\partial_{y}\mathcal{U}_{y_{\rm L}\to y}=-K_{y}\;\mathcal{U}_{y_{\rm L}\to y}\Longrightarrow\partial_{y}\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}_{y_{\rm L}\to y}=\mathcal{U}^{{\dagger}}_{y_{\rm L}\to y}\;K_{y}\;.

(34)

This allows one to convert Eq. (
15
) in

\partial^{2}_{y}\tilde{\bm{C}}_{y}+\frac{2m}{\hbar^{2}}(E-\tilde{\Omega}_{y})\tilde{\bm{C}}_{y}=0\;,

(35)

and hence in the first order differential equation

\partial_{y}\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{\bm{C}}_{y}\\
\tilde{\bm{D}}_{y}\end{bmatrix}=\tilde{\mathcal{M}}^{(E)}_{y}\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{\bm{C}}_{y}\\
\tilde{\bm{D}}_{y}\end{bmatrix},

(36)

with
\tilde{\mathcal{M}}^{(E)}_{y}
and
\tilde{\Omega}_{y}
the matrices defined in Eq. (
25
).
It is worth noticing that the current conservation condition (
106
)
expressed in terms of
\tilde{\bm{C}}_{y}
and
\tilde{\bm{D}}_{y}
, becomes

\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{C}}^{\dagger}_{y}\cdot\tilde{\bm{D}}_{y}-\tilde{\bm{D}}^{\dagger}_{y}\cdot\tilde{\bm{C}}_{y}=\mbox{const.}

(37)

Alternatively we can write this by saying that the first derivative of

\tilde{\bm{C}}^{\dagger}_{y}\cdot\tilde{\bm{D}}_{y}-\tilde{\bm{D}}^{\dagger}_{y}\cdot\tilde{\bm{C}}_{y}
w.r.t.

y
must be zero, i.e.

\displaystyle\partial_{y}\left(\tilde{\bm{C}}^{\dagger}_{y}\cdot\tilde{\bm{D}}_{y}-\tilde{\bm{D}}^{\dagger}_{y}\cdot\tilde{\bm{C}}_{y}\right)=0\;.

(38)

Invoking (
36
) we can finally notice that (
38
) is guaranteed
by the symmetry (
26
) of the matrix
\tilde{\mathcal{M}}^{(E)}
. Indeed we can write

\partial_{y}\left(\tilde{\bm{C}}^{\dagger}_{y}\cdot\tilde{\bm{D}}_{y}-\tilde{\bm{D}}^{\dagger}_{y}\cdot\tilde{\bm{C}}_{y}\right)=\partial_{y}\left(\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{\bm{C}}^{\dagger}_{y},\tilde{\bm{D}}^{\dagger}_{y}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}0&\mathbb{1}\\
-\mathbb{1}&0\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{\bm{C}}_{y_{\rm R}}\\
\tilde{\bm{D}}_{y_{\rm R}}\end{bmatrix}\right)=\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{\bm{C}}^{\dagger}_{y},\tilde{\bm{D}}^{\dagger}_{y}\end{bmatrix}\left(\tilde{\mathcal{M}}_{y}^{{\dagger}(E)}\begin{bmatrix}0&\mathbb{1}\\
-\mathbb{1}&0\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}0&\mathbb{1}\\
-\mathbb{1}&0\end{bmatrix}\tilde{\mathcal{M}}_{y}^{(E)}\right)\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{\bm{C}}_{y_{\rm R}}\\
\tilde{\bm{D}}_{y_{\rm R}}\end{bmatrix}=0\;.

The derivation of Eq. (
22
) finally follows by integrating the system (
36
) over the scattering region, from
y_{\rm L}
to
y_{\rm R}
. This leads to

\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{\bm{C}}_{y_{\rm R}}\\
\tilde{\bm{D}}_{y_{\rm R}}\end{bmatrix}=\overleftarrow{\exp}\left\{\int_{y_{\rm L}}^{y_{\rm R}}dy\tilde{\mathcal{M}}^{(E)}_{y}\right\}\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{\bm{C}}_{y_{\rm L}}\\
\tilde{\bm{D}}_{y_{\rm L}}\end{bmatrix}\;,

(39)

which corresponds to Eq. (
15
) thanks to the identities

\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{C}}_{y_{\rm L}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bm{C}_{y_{\rm L}}\;,\qquad\tilde{\bm{C}}_{y_{\rm R}}=\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}_{y_{\rm L}\rightarrow y_{\rm R}}\bm{C}_{y_{\rm R}}\;,

(40)

\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{D}}_{y_{\rm L}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bm{D}_{y_{\rm L}}\;,\qquad\tilde{\bm{D}}_{y_{\rm R}}=\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}_{y_{\rm L}\rightarrow y_{\rm R}}\bm{D}_{y_{\rm R}}\;,

(41)

which follows from (
32
) and the continuity condition (
19
).

IV
Special regimes

The analysis allows for a fully analytical treatment in at least three distinct regimes. The first is the asymptotic limit where
E
is significantly larger than any other energy scale in the model. The second occurs when the variation of the magnetic field within the scattering region is minimal. The third regime arises when the magnetic field in the scattering region is piecewise constant.

IV.1
Large energy limit

Let us first consider the case where the particle energy is
much larger than the Zeeman energy splitting in the wire, i.e.

\displaystyle E\gg\Delta E_{\rm Z}^{(\max)}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\max_{y}({E_{\rm Z}}^{(1)}_{y}-{E_{\rm Z}}^{(0)}_{y})

(42)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2g\mu_{B}\max_{y}|\bm{B}({y})|\;.

In this regime one has that

\displaystyle k_{E}^{(0)}\simeq k_{E}^{(1)}\simeq k_{E}:=\sqrt{2mE}/\hbar\;,

(43)

and we can disregard the spatial modulation of
\tilde{\mathcal{M}}^{(E)}_{y}
induced by the instantaneous Zeeman eigen-energy term
2m\tilde{\Omega}_{y}/\hbar^{2}
,

\tilde{\mathcal{M}}^{(E)}_{y}\Big|_{E\gg\Delta E_{\rm Z}^{(\max)}}\simeq\mathcal{M}_{E}:=\begin{bmatrix}0&\mathbb{1}\\
-k^{2}_{E}\mathbb{1}&0\end{bmatrix}\;.

(44)

Accordingly the path-ordered exponent associated with such operator can be explicitly computed obtaining

\displaystyle\tilde{\Gamma}^{(E)}_{y_{\rm L}\rightarrow y_{\rm R}}\Big|_{E\gg\Delta E_{\rm Z}^{(\max)}}

\displaystyle\simeq

\displaystyle\exp\{\mathcal{M}_{E}\,L\}

(45)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}{\scriptstyle\cos(k_E L)\mathbb{1}}&{\scriptstyle k_{E}^{-1}\sin(k_E L)\mathbb{1}}\\
{\scriptstyle-k_{E}\sin(k_E L)\mathbb{1}}&{\scriptstyle\cos(k_E L)\mathbb{1}}\end{bmatrix},

where

L:={y_{\rm R}}-{y_{\rm L}}
represents the length of the scattering region. It is worth noticing that in this special regime the above matrix becomes fully dynamical, depending explicitly on the wave vectors
k_{E}
of the asymptotic states. By replacing the above identity in Eq. (
22
) and solving for
T_{E}^{(\ell)}
and
R_{E}^{(\ell)}
, this leads finally to

t_{E}\Big|_{E\gg\Delta E_{\rm Z}^{(\max)}}\simeq\mathcal{U}_{y_{\rm L}\rightarrow y_{\rm R}},\qquad r_{E}\Big|_{E\gg\Delta E_{\rm Z}^{(\max)}}\simeq 0\;,

(46)

i.e. the transmission matrix is equal to the Berry operator, with no reflection (see Appx.
C

for calculation breakdown). We conclude by noting that higher-order corrections can be computed by tracking the expansion of

\tilde{\Gamma}^{(E)}_{y_{\rm L}\rightarrow y_{\rm R}}
in terms of the difference
\tilde{\mathcal{M}}^{(E)}_{y}-\mathcal{M}^{(E)}
. For example, in Appx.
C.2
, we use this approach to determine the first-order correction to the reflection matrix.

Equation (
46
) has a significant implication when one considers that, in the large-energy limit, the scattering process should have a negligible effect on the spin of the particle. Physically, this can be understood through an analogy with dynamical quenches: a high-energy particle propagating from left to right along the wire encounters a sudden, abrupt change in the external magnetic field. This rapid variation leaves the spin invariant and induces no reflections.
If one adopts this ansatz, as detailed in Appx..
C.1
, Eq. (
46
) leads to the following key identity

\displaystyle[\mathcal{U}_{y_{\rm L}\rightarrow y_{\rm R}}]_{\ell^{\prime},\ell}=\bra*{\phi^{(\ell')}_{\bm{n}_{\rm R}}}\ket*{\phi^{(\ell)}_{\bm{n}_{\rm L}}}\;,

(47)

In Sec.
V
, we will provide a proof of Eq. (
47
)
for the special case where
the variation of the magnetic field along the 1D wire is constrained on a fixed plane. Additionally, we will present an argument that extends the result to the general case.
The identity (
47
) is a remarkable result: it reveals that the Berry operator
\mathcal{U}_{y_{\rm L}\rightarrow y_{\rm R}}
can be determined analytically and depends solely on the magnetic field’s components at the boundaries, hence reducing to a purely topological form. Crucially, it is independent of the magnetic field’s behavior in the scattering region or the length
L=y_{\rm R}-y_{\rm L}
. Notably, this result predicts that if the magnetic fields at the left and right leads are identical (i.e.
\bm{n}_{\rm R}=\bm{n}_{\rm L}
), the Berry operator transformation reduces to the identity
[
35
]
.

IV.2
Infinitesimal scattering region limit

When the variation of the magnetic field inside the scattering region is sufficiently small
the path-ordered exponent in Eq. (
22
) can be approximated by a regular exponential

\displaystyle\tilde{\Gamma}^{(E)}_{y_{\rm L}\rightarrow y_{\rm R}}\simeq{\exp}\left\{\int_{y_{\rm L}}^{y_{\rm R}}dy\tilde{\mathcal{M}}^{(E)}_{y}\right\}=\mathcal{D}_{\rm L}(Q_{E})\;,

(48)

where
Q_{E}
is the Hermitian matrix

\displaystyle Q_{E}:=\frac{2m}{\hbar^{2}}\left(E\mathbb{1}-\int_{y_{\rm L}}^{y_{\rm R}}dy\tilde{\Omega}_{y}/L\right)\;,

(49)

and

\mathcal{D}_{\rm L}(Q_{E}):=\begin{bmatrix}{\scriptstyle\cos(\sqrt{Q_E}L)}&{\scriptstyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{Q_{E}}}\sin(\sqrt{Q_E}L)}\\
{\scriptstyle-\sqrt{Q_{E}}\sin(\sqrt{Q_E}L)}&{\scriptstyle\cos(\sqrt{Q_E}L)}\end{bmatrix}\;,

(50)

(see Appx..
D
for details).
Observe in particular, that for infinitesimally short scattering regions (i.e.
L\rightarrow 0
),
the matrix

\mathcal{D}_{\rm L}(Q_{E})
, and hence
\tilde{\Gamma}^{(E)}_{y_{\rm L}\rightarrow y_{\rm R}}
,
reduces to the identity. However, this behavior does not necessarily extend to the Berry operator. Specifically, as long as the boundary conditions at the edges of the scattering region are preserved in the
L\rightarrow 0
limit,
Eq. (
47
) remains applicable and can assign a non-trivial value to

\mathcal{U}_{y_{\rm L}\rightarrow y_{\rm R}}
.
These special conditions are satisfied when the magnetic field
\bm{B}(y)

undergoes an abrupt transition from
\bm{B}_{\rm L}
to
\bm{B}_{\rm R}
at a single point

y_{\rm L}=y_{\rm R}=y_{\star}
of the 1D wire. Under such circumstances,
leveraging the preceding analysis, we can hence write

\Gamma^{(E)}_{y_{\rm L}\rightarrow y_{\rm R}}\Big|_{L=0}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{U}_{y_{\star}}&0\\
0&\mathcal{U}_{y_{\star}}\end{bmatrix}\;,

(51)

with
\mathcal{U}_{y_{\star}}:=\mathcal{U}_{y_{\star}^{-}\rightarrow y_{\star}^{+}}

the Berry operator acquired when crossing the discontinuity point. In this case, notice that the matrix
\Gamma^{(E)}_{y_{\rm L}\to y_{\rm R}}
matrix becomes fully dynamical, depending explicitly on the wave vectors
k^{(0/1)}_{E}
of the asymptotic states.
Replaced into Eq. (
28
) this
implies

\displaystyle r_{E}\Big|_{L=0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tfrac{W\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}_{y_{\star}}W^{-2}\mathcal{U}_{y_{\star}}W-\mathbb{1}}{W\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}_{y_{\star}}W^{-2}\mathcal{U}_{y_{\star}}W+\mathbb{1}}\;,

(52)

\displaystyle t_{E}\Big|_{L=0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2W^{-1}\mathcal{U}_{y_{\star}}W\tfrac{\mathbb{1}}{W\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}_{y_{\star}}W^{-2}\mathcal{U}_{y_{\star}}W+\mathbb{1}}\;,

which represents the reflection and transmission matrices through the discontinuity (as shown in
Appx..
D
for
E\rightarrow\infty
these solutions behave as predicted by Eq. (
46
)).

IV.3
Piecewise constant field regime

When the magnetic field is a piecewise constant function the operator
\Gamma^{(E)}_{y_{\rm L}\rightarrow y_{\rm R}}

can be expressed as an ordered product of simpler terms. This approach is structurally analogous to lattice gauge formulations of Wilson loops
[
36
,
37
]
, where continuous line integrals are replaced by ordered products of link variables. Specifically, let us assume that there exists a collection of ordered points

\displaystyle y_{0}=y_{\rm L}<y_{1}<y_{2}<\dots<y_{N-1}<y_{N}=y_{\rm R}\;,

(53)

that identify a collection of
N
non-overlapping intervals

I_{j,j+1}:=]y_{j},y_{j+1}[

over which the magnetic field (and hence

{\Omega}_{y}
), take constant values.
Under this condition we can write

\displaystyle\Gamma^{(E)}_{y_{\rm L}\rightarrow y_{\rm R}}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{U}_{N}&0\\
0&\mathcal{U}_{N}\end{bmatrix}\mathcal{D}_{L_{N-1}}(Q^{(N-1)}_{E})\cdots

(54)

\displaystyle\quad\cdots\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{U}_{2}&0\\
0&\mathcal{U}_{2}\end{bmatrix}\mathcal{D}_{L_{1}}(Q^{(1)}_{E})\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{U}_{1}&0\\
0&\mathcal{U}_{1}\end{bmatrix}\mathcal{D}_{L_{0}}(Q^{(0)}_{E})\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{U}_{0}&0\\
0&\mathcal{U}_{0}\end{bmatrix},

where for all
j\in\{0,1,\cdots,N\}
,

\mathcal{U}_{{j}}:=\mathcal{U}_{y^{-}_{j}\rightarrow y^{+}_{j}}
represents the Berry operator transformation
one gets when crossing the discontinuity point
y_{j}
,

\mathcal{D}_{L_{j}}(\cdots)
is the matrix function defined in Eq. (
50
), and finally

\displaystyle L_{j}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle y_{j+1}-y_{j}\;,

\displaystyle Q^{(j)}_{E}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\frac{2m}{\hbar^{2}}(E\mathbb{1}-{\Omega}_{j})\;,

(55)

with
{\Omega}_{j}
being the value of
{\Omega}_{y}
in the interval
I_{j,j+1}
.
The derivation of Eq. (
54
) is provided in
Appx..
E
.
Notably, by factoring out the total Berry operator contribution as defined in (
23
), the right-hand side of Eq. (
54
) can be equivalently rewritten in the form:

\displaystyle\Gamma^{(E)}_{y_{\rm L}\rightarrow y_{\rm R}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{U}_{y_{\rm L}\rightarrow y_{\rm R}}&0\\
0&\mathcal{U}_{y_{\rm L}\rightarrow y_{\rm R}}\end{bmatrix}\tilde{\mathcal{D}}_{L_{N-1}}(Q^{(N-1)}_{E})\cdots

(56)

\displaystyle\cdots\tilde{\mathcal{D}}_{L_{1}}(Q^{(1)}_{E})\tilde{\mathcal{D}}_{L_{0}}(Q^{(0)}_{E})\;,

where now

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{D}}_{L_{j}}(Q^{(j)}_{E}):=

(57)

\displaystyle\qquad\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}_{y_{\rm L}\rightarrow y_{j}}&0\\
0&\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}_{y_{\rm L}\rightarrow y_{j}}\end{bmatrix}\mathcal{D}_{L_{j}}(Q^{(j)}_{E})\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{U}_{y_{\rm L}\rightarrow y_{j}}&0\\
0&\mathcal{U}_{y_{\rm L}\rightarrow y_{j}}\end{bmatrix}\;,

with

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{y_{\rm L}\rightarrow y_{j}}=\mathcal{U}_{j}\cdots\mathcal{U}_{1}\mathcal{U}_{0}\;,

(58)

the Berry operator accumulated moving from
y_{\rm L}^{-}
to
y_{j}^{+}
.
As a particular application let us consider the special case of a piecewise constant
scattering region where

\displaystyle{\bm{B}}(y)=0\qquad\forall y\in]y_{\rm L},y_{\rm R}[\;.

(59)

Since in this case
Q^{(0)}_{E}=\frac{2m}{\hbar^{2}}E\mathbb{1}
, Eq. (
54
) reduces to

\Gamma^{(E)}_{y_{\rm L}\rightarrow y_{\rm R}}=\begin{bmatrix}{\scriptstyle\cos(k_E L)\mathcal{U}_{y_{\rm L}\rightarrow y_{\rm R}}}&{\scriptstyle k_{E}^{-1}\sin(k_E L)\mathcal{U}_{y_{\rm L}\rightarrow y_{\rm R}}}\\
{\scriptstyle-k_{E}\sin(k_E L)\mathcal{U}_{y_{\rm L}\rightarrow y_{\rm R}}}&{\scriptstyle\cos(k_E L)\mathcal{U}_{y_{\rm L}\rightarrow y_{\rm R}}}\end{bmatrix}\;.

(60)

We finally observe that the identity (
54
), together with Eq. (
48
), allows us to
derive approximate solutions for
\Gamma^{(E)}_{y_{\rm L}\rightarrow y_{\rm R}}

for arbitrary choices of
{\bm{B}}_{y}
. Specifically, a finite-length scattering region can be divided into
N
equally spaced segments
[y_{j},y_{j+1}[
(with
j\in\{0,1,...,N-1\}
,
y_{0}=y_{\rm L}
and
y_{N}=y_{\rm R}
) of length
\Delta y:=L/N
. Provided that
\Delta y
is sufficiently small to ensure the validity of Eq. (
48
) across all segments, Eq. (
54
) can then
be employed as a well-defined numerical procedure for solving the scattering problem.

V
Berry operator

To compute the Berry operator transformation (
21
)
we begin by considering the special case where the variation of the magnetic field along the 1D wire is restricted to a fixed plane. Under this assumption, the 3D vector
\bm{n}(y)
, which specifies the orientation of the magnetic field
\bm{B}(y)
, can be expressed in terms of a single polar angle
\theta_{y}
as:

\displaystyle\bm{n}(y):=\sin{(\theta_y)}\bm{n}_{1}+\cos{(\theta_y)}\bm{n}_{3}\;,

(61)

with
\bm{n}_{1}
and
\bm{n}_{3}
are orthonormal vectors.
The Zeeman eigenvectors (
3
) can hence be expressed as

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\ket*{\phi^{(0)}_{{\bm n}(y)}}=\cos{(\tfrac{\theta_y}{2})}\ket{\downarrow}-\sin{(\tfrac{\theta_y}{2})}\ket{\uparrow}=e^{-i(\theta_{y}/2)\hat{\sigma}_{{\bm{n}_{2}}}}\ket{\downarrow}\;,\\
\ket*{\phi^{(1)}_{{\bm n}(y)}}=\cos{(\tfrac{\theta_y}{2})}\ket{\uparrow}+\sin{(\tfrac{\theta_y}{2})}\ket{\downarrow}=e^{-i(\theta_{y}/2)\hat{\sigma}_{{\bm{n}_{2}}}}\ket{\uparrow}\;,\end{array}\right.

(62)

with
\ket{\downarrow}:=\ket*{\phi^{(0)}_{{\bm n}_3}}
and
\ket{\uparrow}:=\ket*{\phi^{(1)}_{{\bm n}_3}}
the eigenvectors of the Pauli operator
\hat{\sigma}_{{\bm{n}_{3}}}:={\bm{n}_{3}}\cdot\hat{\sigma}
associated to the eigenvalues
-1
and
1
respectively, and
\hat{\sigma}_{{\bm{n}_{2}}}:={\bm{n}_{2}}\cdot\hat{\bm{\sigma}}

the Pauli operator associated to the vector
\bm{n}_{2}:=\bm{n}_{3}\times\bm{n}_{1}
(expressed in matrix form in the
representation for which
\ket*{\uparrow}:=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1\\
0\end{smallmatrix}\right)
and

\ket*{\downarrow}:=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0\\
1\end{smallmatrix}\right)
,
\sigma_{{\bm{n}_{2}}}
is just the matrix

\begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\
i&0\end{pmatrix}
).
From Eq. (
62
) it follows

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\ket*{\partial_y \phi^{(0)}_{{\bm n}(y)}}=-(\partial_{y}\theta_{y})\ket*{\phi^{(1)}_{{\bm n}(y)}}/2\;,\\
\ket*{\partial_y \phi^{(1)}_{{\bm n}(y)}}=(\partial_{y}\theta_{y})\ket*{\phi^{(0)}_{{\bm n}(y)}}/2\;,\end{array}\right.

so that
the Berry matrix (
17
) becomes

K_{y}=(\partial_{y}\theta_{y})\begin{pmatrix}0&1/2\\
-1/2&0\end{pmatrix}=i(\partial_{y}\theta_{y})\sigma_{{\bm{n}_{2}}}/2\;.

(66)

Now note that Eq. (
66
) implies that the commutator
\left[K_{y},K_{y^{\prime}}\right]=0
for all
y
and
y^{\prime}
.
Since the ordered exponential simplifies to a standard exponential, the Berry operator admits a closed formula, resulting in the following expression

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{y_{\rm L}\rightarrow y}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp[-(i/2)\int_{\theta_{y_{\rm L}}}^{\theta_{y}}dy\;(\partial_{y}\theta_{y})\sigma_{{\bm{n}_{2}}}]

(67)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp[-i(\tfrac{{\theta_{y}}-{\theta_{y_{\rm L}}}}{2})\sigma_{{\bm{n}_{2}}}]\;,

which in particular, for
y=y_{\rm R}
yields

\mathcal{U}_{y_{\rm L}\rightarrow y_{\rm R}}=\exp[-i(\tfrac{\alpha}{2})\sigma_{{\bm{n}_{2}}}]=\begin{pmatrix}\cos(\alpha/2)&-\sin(\alpha/2)\\
\sin(\alpha/2)&\cos(\alpha/2)\end{pmatrix}\;,

(68)

with
\alpha:=\theta_{y_{\rm R}}-\theta_{y_{\rm L}}
.
Using Eq. (
62
) one can easily verify that the matrix elements of
Eq. (
68
)
matches exactly with the prediction of Eq. (
47
).
We can hence conclude that such an identity can be explicitly proved for the special cases where the
\bm{n}(y)
evolve an a 2D plane as in (
61
).
Extending this result for arbitrary trajectories of
\bm{n}(y)

can be achieved by employing the same strategy outlined at the end of
Sec.
IV.2
for the numerical evaluation of

\overleftarrow{{\exp}}\left\{\int_{y_{\rm L}}^{y_{\rm R}}dy\tilde{\mathcal{M}}^{(E)}_{y}\right\}
.
Specifically this time we decompose the scattering region into
N
equally spaced segments
[y_{j},y_{j+1}[
(with
j\in\{0,1,...,N-1\}
,
y_{0}=y_{\rm L}
and
y_{N}=y_{\rm R}
) of the length
\Delta y=L/N
so that

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{y_{\rm L}\rightarrow y_{\rm R}}=\mathcal{U}_{y_{N-1}\rightarrow y_{N}}\cdots\mathcal{U}_{y_{2}\rightarrow y_{3}}\mathcal{U}_{y_{1}\rightarrow y_{2}}\mathcal{U}_{y_{0}\rightarrow y_{1}}\;.

(69)

Now, assume that the length
\Delta y=L/N

is sufficiently small so that, within each interval, the evolution of

\bm{n}(y)
can be approximated as occurring on a 2D plane. More precisely, for each

j
, we assume that there exists
a couple of orthonormal axis
\bm{n}_{1}^{(j)}
and
\bm{n}_{3}^{(j)}
, along with a function
\theta_{y}^{(j)}
such that

\bm{n}(y)\simeq\sin{(\theta^{(j)}_y)}\bm{n}^{(j)}_{1}+\cos{(\theta^{(j)}_y)}\bm{n}^{(j)}_{3}\;,\;\forall y\in[y_{j},y_{j+1}[\;.

(70)

By invoking (
68
) we can hence evaluate the individual terms
in the product (
69
) via (
47
). Specifically for all
j
we have

[\mathcal{U}_{y_{j}\rightarrow y_{j+1}}]_{\ell^{\prime},\ell}=\bra*{\phi^{(\ell')}_{\bm{n}_{j+1}}}\ket*{\phi^{(\ell)}_{\bm{n}_{j}}}
.
Note then that this property implies that

\displaystyle[\mathcal{U}_{y_{j+1}\rightarrow y_{j+2}}\mathcal{U}_{y_{j}\rightarrow y_{j+1}}]_{\ell^{\prime},\ell}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=\sum_{\ell^{\prime\prime}=\{0,1\}}[\mathcal{U}_{y_{j+1}\rightarrow y_{j+2}}]_{\ell^{\prime}\ell^{\prime\prime}}[\mathcal{U}_{y_{j}\rightarrow y_{j+1}}]_{\ell^{\prime\prime},\ell}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=\sum_{\ell^{\prime\prime}=\{0,1\}}\bra*{\phi^{(\ell')}_{\bm{n}_{j+2}}}\ket*{\phi^{(\ell'')}_{\bm{n}_{j+1}}}\bra*{\phi^{(\ell'')}_{\bm{n}_{j+1}}}\ket*{\phi^{(\ell)}_{\bm{n}_{j}}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=\bra*{\phi^{(\ell')}_{\bm{n}_{j+2}}}\ket*{\phi^{(\ell)}_{\bm{n}_{j}}}\;.

(71)

which used in (
69
) allows us to conclude that

\displaystyle[\mathcal{U}_{y_{\rm L}\rightarrow y_{\rm R}}]_{\ell^{\prime},\ell}=\bra*{\phi^{(\ell')}_{\bm{n}_{N}}}\ket*{\phi^{(\ell)}_{\bm{n}_{0}}}=\bra*{\phi^{(\ell')}_{\bm{n}_{\rm R}}}\ket*{\phi^{(\ell)}_{\bm{n}_{\rm L}}}\;,

(72)

hence proving (
47
).

Examples

In this section we apply the method developed in Sec.
III
to
two different examples under the assumption that along the 1D wire
the Zeeman field varies continuously in a fixed 2D plane determined by the directions

\bm{n}_{1}
and
\bm{n}_{3}
as indicated in Eq. (
61
), i.e.

\displaystyle\bm{B}(y)=B_{1}(y)\bm{n}_{1}+B_{3}(y)\bm{n}_{3}\;.

(73)

VI.1
Scheme I

Consider the scenario depicted in panel
a)
of Fig.
1
,
where the Zeeman field in the two leads has the same magnitude, is directed along the some axis
\bm{n}_{3}
, but with opposite orientations, i.e.

\displaystyle\bm{B}_{\rm L}=B_{0}\,{\bm{n}}_{3}\;,\qquad\bm{B}_{\rm R}=-B_{0}\,{\bm{n}}_{3}\;,

(74)

corresponding to set
{\bm{n}}_{\rm L}={\bm{n}}_{3}
and
{\bm{n}}_{\rm R}=-{\bm{n}}_{3}
.
Adopting the notation of Eq. (
62
), this in particular implies

\displaystyle\ket*{\phi^{(0)}_{y_{\rm L}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ket*{\phi^{(0)}_{{\bm n}_3}}=\ket{\downarrow}=\ket*{\phi^{(1)}_{-{\bm n}_3}}=\ket*{\phi^{(1)}_{y_{\rm R}}}\;,

(75)

\displaystyle\ket*{\phi^{(1)}_{y_{\rm L}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ket*{\phi^{(1)}_{{\bm n}_3}}=\ket{\uparrow}=-\ket*{\phi^{(0)}_{-{\bm n}_3}}=-\ket*{\phi^{(0)}_{y_{\rm R}}}\;.

(76)

To interpolate among the values (
74
)
we define the components of the field (
73
) in the scattering region

VII
Conclusions

In summary, we have proposed a method to describe the coherent transport of electrons through a 1D wire subject to a magnetic field. We have derived a closed-form differential equation, which contains both geometric and dynamical contributions, that governs (spin-resolved) electron transport for a generic spatially varying magnetic field which interacts with the electrons spin via the Zeeman coupling.
We have shown that electrons are fully transmitted when the injection energy is much larger than the Zeeman splitting energy. In this regime, the quantum state of the transmitted electrons undergoes a topological transformation, i.e. the Berry operator of the system. Indeed, such Berry operator depends solely on the values of the magnetic field at the nanowire boundaries, regardless of the magnetic field’s behavior within the wire. We have then analyzed two specific examples. In the first one, when the injection energy is below the Zeeman energy, we have demonstrated that perfect spin-flip occurs in the transmission through the wire.
In the second example, for injection energies below the Zeeman splitting energy, we observed a balanced spin-mixing. Moreover, for an infinite injection energy (or an infinitesimal wire length), the quantum state of the transmitted electron emerges with a different wavevector in the first example. In the second example, the quantum state becomes entangled between its spin and wavevector degrees of freedom.

We mention that we have checked our results in Figs.
2
and
4
using an independent numerical method which uses a wavefunction matching technique (KWANT-toolkit
[
39
]
). We mention that the numerical probabilities plotted in all the figures coincide with the results obtained with KWANT-toolkit up to a relative error of the order of
10^{-5}
.

To conclude, our approach can be extended to realistic three dimensional nanowires (where multiple transverse modes are present). As long as the number of modes available for transport is restricted to two by choosing the injection energy of electrons within a reduced range of values, we have obtained similar results to the ones reported in Figs.
2
and
4
.
We mention that a possible implementation of a Zeeman field having the required behavior can be obtained by bending a nanowire in a uniform magnetic field, or by depositing a nanowire on a substrate containing a magnetic material with a suitable pattern of non-collinear magnetic moments. Examples are Heusler compounds
[
40
]
, low-dimensional systems which lack structural inversion symmetry, such as a single atomic layer of manganese on a tungsten substrate
[
41
]
, and other materials, such as insulating multiferroics
[
42
]
and van der Waals magnets
[
43
]
.

C.1.1

In this large energy regime, one might be tempted to conclude that the scattering process has a negligible impact on the particle’s propagation. 0&A^{n}\end{bmatrix}\;,\qquad{\mathcal{M}}^{2n+1}=\begin{bmatrix}0&A^{n}\\
A^{n+1}&0\end{bmatrix}\;.

(147)

Accordingly for
x
real, we can write

\displaystyle{{\rm\exp}}\left\{x{\mathcal{M}}\right\}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{x^{k}\mathcal{M}^{k}}{k!}=\begin{bmatrix}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{x^{2n}A^{n}}{(2n)!}&\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{x^{2n+1}A^{n}}{(2n+1)!}\\
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{x^{2n+1}A^{n+1}}{(2n+1)!}&\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{x^{2n}A^{n}}{(2n)!}\end{bmatrix}\;.

(148)

In case
A
is positive semidefinite one has

\displaystyle A=\left(\sqrt{A}\right)^{2}\;,

(149)

so that

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{x^{2n}A^{n}}{(2n)!}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\left(x\sqrt{A}\right)^{2n}}{(2n)!}=\cos\left(x\sqrt{A}\right)\;,

(150)

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{x^{2n+1}A^{n}}{(2n+1)!}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{A}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\left(x\sqrt{A}\right)^{2n+1}}{(2n+1)!}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{A}}\sin\left(x\sqrt{A}\right)\;,

(151)

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{x^{2n+1}A^{n+1}}{(2n+1)!}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\sqrt{A}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\left(x\sqrt{A}\right)^{2n+1}}{(2n+1)!}={\sqrt{A}}\sin\left(x\sqrt{A}\right)\;,

(152)

Accordingly we can write

\displaystyle{{\rm\exp}}\left\{x{\mathcal{M}}\right\}={\cal D}_{x}(A):=\begin{bmatrix}\cos(x\sqrt{A})&\frac{1}{\sqrt{A}}\sin(x\sqrt{A})\\
-\sqrt{A}\sin(x\sqrt{A})&\cos(x\sqrt{A})\end{bmatrix}\;.

(153)

A similar expression applies also when
A
is Hermitian but not necessarily positive semidefinite.
In such case it is useful to write

\displaystyle A=A_{+}-A_{-}\;,

(154)

with
A_{+},A_{-}\geq 0
,
A_{+}A_{-}=0
representing the positive and negative parts of
A
.
Accordingly we have

\displaystyle\sqrt{A}=\sqrt{A_{+}}+i\sqrt{A_{-}}\;,\qquad\qquad\frac{1}{A}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{A_{+}}}+\frac{-i}{\sqrt{A_{-}}}\;,

(155)

and
the cosine and sine operators appearing in Eq. (
153
) can be computed as

\displaystyle\cos(x\sqrt{A})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\cos(x\sqrt{A_+})\oplus\cosh(x\sqrt{A_-})\;,

(156)

\displaystyle\sin(x\sqrt{A})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sin(x\sqrt{A_+})\oplus i\sinh(x\sqrt{A_-})=\sin(x\sqrt{A_+})+i\sinh(x\sqrt{A_-})\;,

(157)

where the symbol
\oplus
has been introduced to indicate that the sum must be done
by projecting the functions
f({A_{\pm}})
on the support of
{A_{\pm}}
.
Therefore we can now write

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{A}}\sin(x\sqrt{A})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{A_{+}}}\sin(x\sqrt{A_+})+\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{A_{-}}}\ \sinh(x\sqrt{A_-})\;,

(158)

\displaystyle{\sqrt{A}}\sin(x\sqrt{A})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\sqrt{A_{+}}}\sin(x\sqrt{A_+})-{\sqrt{A_{-}}}\sinh(x\sqrt{A_-})\;.

(159)

Equations (
48
) and (
45
) follow finally by setting
x=L
and taking the operator
A
in Eq. (
153
) equal to
Q_{E}
and
-k_{E}^{2}\mathbb{1}
respectively.

Appendix E
Scattering problem for piecewise constant elements

As anticipated in the main text, when the magnetic field in the scattering region is piecewise constant, the transfer matrix
\Gamma^{(E)}_{y_{\rm L}\rightarrow y_{\rm R}}

can be formally integrated as in Eq. (
54
). To see this, given
\epsilon>0

an infinitesimal increment,
we find it useful to introduce two extra points
y_{j}^{\pm}:=y_{j}\pm\epsilon
for each of the
N+1
elements of the partition (
53
). Observe that the set of points

\displaystyle y_{0}^{-}<y_{0}^{+}<y^{-}_{1}<y^{+}_{1}<y^{-}_{2}<y^{+}_{2}<\dots<y^{-}_{N-1}<y^{+}_{N-1}<y^{-}_{N}<y^{+}_{N}\;,

(170)

identifies a new collection of non overlapping intervals

\displaystyle I_{j}^{(\epsilon)}:=]y_{j}^{-},y_{j}^{+}[\;,\qquad\qquad I_{j,j+1}^{(\epsilon)}:=]y_{j}^{+},y_{j+1}^{-}[\;,

(171)

that provide a covering of the scattering region. In particular,

I_{j}^{(\epsilon)}
are infinitesimal intervals over which the Berry matrix

K_{y}
experience an abrupt change; on the contrary from the properties of (
53
), it follows that
on
I_{j,j+1}^{(\epsilon)}
the matrix
\Omega_{y}

assumes constant value
\Omega_{j}
and the Berry matrix
K_{y}
is null, i.e.

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}K_{y}=0\;,\\
\Omega_{y}=\Omega_{j}\;,\end{array}\right.\qquad\forall y\in I_{j,j+1}^{(\epsilon)}\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\mathcal{U}_{y^{+}_{j}\rightarrow y}=\mathbb{1}\\
\tilde{\Omega}_{y}=\Omega_{j}\;,\\
\mathcal{M}_{y}^{(E)}=\mathcal{M}_{E}^{(j)}:=\begin{bmatrix}0&\mathbb{1}\\
\frac{2m}{\hbar^{2}}({\Omega}_{j}-E\mathbb{1})&0\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0&\mathbb{1}\\
-Q^{(j)}_{E}&0\end{bmatrix}\;,\end{array}\right.\qquad\forall y\in I_{j,j+1}^{(\epsilon)}\;,

(172)

with
Q^{(j)}_{E}
as in Eq. (
55
).
Most importantly each of the intervals (
171
)
fulfill the continuity condition (
19
) so that we can invoke the general formula Eq. (
22
) to propagate the vector
\begin{bmatrix}\bm{C}_{y}\\
\bm{D}_{y}\end{bmatrix}
across them.
Specifically, for all
j
we can write

\begin{bmatrix}\bm{C}_{y_{j}^{+}}\\
\bm{D}_{y_{j}^{+}}\end{bmatrix}=\Gamma^{(E)}_{y_{j}^{-}\rightarrow y_{j}^{+}}\begin{bmatrix}\bm{C}_{y_{j}^{-}}\\
\bm{D}_{y_{j}^{-}}\end{bmatrix}\;,\qquad\begin{bmatrix}\bm{C}_{y_{j+1}^{-}}\\
\bm{D}_{y_{j+1}^{-}}\end{bmatrix}=\Gamma^{(E)}_{y_{j}^{+}\rightarrow y_{j+1}^{-}}\begin{bmatrix}\bm{C}_{y_{j}^{+}}\\
\bm{D}_{y_{j}^{+}}\end{bmatrix}\;,

(173)

which by concatenation gives

\Gamma^{(E)}_{y^{-}_{\rm L}\rightarrow y^{+}_{\rm R}}=\Gamma^{(E)}_{y^{-}_{0}\rightarrow y^{+}_{N}}=\Gamma^{(E)}_{y^{-}_{N}\rightarrow y^{+}_{N}}\Gamma^{(E)}_{y^{+}_{N-1}\rightarrow y^{-}_{N}}\cdots\Gamma^{(E)}_{y^{+}_{1}\rightarrow y^{-}_{2}}\Gamma^{(E)}_{y^{-}_{1}\rightarrow y^{+}_{1}}\Gamma^{(E)}_{y^{+}_{0}\rightarrow y^{-}_{1}}\Gamma^{(E)}_{y^{-}_{0}\rightarrow y^{+}_{0}}\;.

(174)

The identity (
54

51

the transfer matrix over the infinitesimal intervals

\Gamma^{(E)}_{y^{-}_{j}\rightarrow y^{+}_{j}}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{U}_{j}&0\\

(175)

and using Eq. (
172
) to write

\Gamma^{(E)}_{y^{+}_{j}\rightarrow y^{-}_{j+1}}={{\rm\exp}}\left\{\int_{y^{+}_{j}}^{y^{-}_{j+1}}dy\mathcal{M}_{E}^{(j)}\right\}={{\rm\exp}}\left\{({y^{-}_{j+1}-y^{+}_{j})}\mathcal{M}_{E}^{(j)}\right\}=\mathcal{D}_{L_{j}}(Q^{(j)}_{E})\;,

(176)

where we used the identity (
153

A=Q^{(j)}_{E}
and

.

Appendix F
Property of the scattering matrix

The transmission and reflection matrices
t_{E}
and
r_{E}
computed for electrons injected from the left lead, contribute in defining the
scattering matrix
S_{E}
via the identity,

S_{E}=\begin{bmatrix}r_{E}&t^{\prime}_{E}\\
t_{E}&r^{\prime}_{E}\end{bmatrix}\;,

(177)

with
t^{\prime}_{E}
(
r^{\prime}_{E}
) being the transmission (reflection) matrix for electrons injected from the right lead.
As discussed in Ref.
[
29
]
, for a given position-dependent magnetic field
\bm{B}(y)
, the matrix

S_{E}
satisfies the reciprocity relation

S_{E}({\bm{B}(y)})=[S_{E}({-\bm{B}(y)})]^{T},

(178)

where the superscript
T
means transpose.
From Eq. (
178
) and (
177
) follow straightforwardly that

t_{E}({\bm{B}(y)})=[t^{\prime}_{E}({-\bm{B}(y)})]^{T},\qquad\quad r_{E}({\bm{B}(y)})=[r^{\prime}_{E}({-\bm{B}(y)})]^{T}\;.

(179)

Now we will show that, under two specific conditions, the scattering problem for electrons that are injected from the left lead with
\bm{B}(y)
is formally equivalent to the scattering problem for electrons that are injected from the right lead with the reverse field,
-\bm{B}(y)
.
More precisely, if these two conditions are satisfied, the system in Eq. (
22
) is identical for the two scattering problems, meaning that

t_{E}({\bm{B}(y)})=t^{\prime}_{E}({-\bm{B}(y)}).

(180)

The first condition is that

[\Omega_{y}]_{\bm{B}(y)}=[\Omega_{y^{\prime}}]_{\pm\bm{B}(y^{\prime})}

where
y\in[y_{\rm L},y_{\rm R}]
and
y^{\prime}=:y_{\rm L}+y_{\rm R}-y
so that
y^{\prime}\in[y_{\rm R},y_{\rm L}]
.
In other words, the Zeeman eigenvalues, contained in the matrix
\Omega_{y}
, must be symmetric with respect to the middle point of the scattering region. The second condition is that
\mathcal{U}_{y_{\rm L}\to y}|_{\bm{B}(y)}=\mathcal{U}_{y_{\rm R}\to y^{\prime}}|_{-\bm{B}(y^{\prime})}
, which holds true if
\theta_{y}|_{\bm{B}(y)}=\theta_{y^{\prime}}|_{-\bm{B}(y^{\prime})}
, see Eq. (
68
). Combining Eq. (
179
) with Eq. (
180
), it is straightforward to find

t_{E}({\bm{B}(y)})=[t_{E}(\bm{B}(y)]^{T}\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad[t_{E}]_{0,1}=[t_{E}]_{1,0}\;.

(181)

Notice that in the Example II (Sec.
VI.2
) both conditions are satisfied, leading to the property in Eq. (
181
). On the other hand, in the Example I (Sec.
VI.1
), the first condition is satisfied, while the second one is not. Indeed, we find
\absolutevalue{\theta_y}_{\bm{B}(y)}=\absolutevalue{\theta_{y'}}_{-\bm{B}(y^{\prime})}
, meaning that
\absolutevalue{\mathcal{U}_{y_{\rm L} \to y}}_{\bm{B}(y)}=\absolutevalue{\mathcal{U}_{y_{\rm R} \to y'}}_{-\bm{B}(y^{\prime})}
.
Although we are not able to prove it, numerically in this case we find

|[t_{E}]_{0,1}|^{2}=|[t_{E}]_{1,0}|^{2}
.
