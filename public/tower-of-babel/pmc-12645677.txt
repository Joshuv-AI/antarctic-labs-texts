The association of ZJU index with bowel health in US participants: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Jie An, Cheng Jiao, Jun Guo, Lipu Zhang, Zhi Li, Guangchao Liu, Yao Zhang, Wei Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study investigates the association between the Zhejiang University (ZJU) index, a composite metabolic indicator, and bowel health, specifically chronic diarrhea and constipation, using NHANES data (2005–2010).MethodsThis cross-sectional analysis included 11,180 participants. The ZJU index was used as the exposure variable, and bowel conditions were assessed via self-reported symptoms. Logistic regression analysis was employed to assess the association between the ZJU index and the prevalence of these conditions. Smooth curve fitting was utilized to evaluate potential non-linear relationships. Subgroup and sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of the findings.ResultsThe ZJU index was significantly associated with both diarrhea and constipation prevalence. Specifically, a 5-unit increase in the ZJU index was associated with a 13% higher likelihood of chronic diarrhea (OR = 1.13, P < 0.001) and a 12% lower likelihood of chronic constipation (OR = 0.88, P = 0.004). Findings were consistent across subgroups and robust in sensitivity analyses.ConclusionThis study demonstrates an association between the ZJU index and bowel health outcomes. The results underscore the importance of metabolic factors in gastrointestinal health and suggest the ZJU index is associated with the risk of bowel disorder. Future research should validate these findings and explore underlying mechanisms.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40001-025-03452-z.

Introduction

Bowel health is a significant global public health concern, with chronic constipation and chronic diarrhea being the most prevalent functional bowel disorders, affecting approximately 16% and 5% of the global population, respectively [1, 2]. These conditions substantially impair quality of life and are associated with various chronic diseases, including cardiovascular diseases [3], diabetes [4], and chronic kidney disease [5]. Recent advances in gut microbiota and gut–brain axis research have contributed to improved bowel health management strategies.

Chronic constipation is characterized by infrequent bowel movements, hard stools, and difficulty in defecation, often linked to gut microbiota imbalance, weakened gut motility, psychological stress, and suboptimal dietary habits [2, 6]. Conversely, chronic diarrhea, characterized by frequent, loose, or watery stools, has a multifactorial etiology that includes bowel infections, microbiota imbalance, inflammation, and food intolerances [7, 8]. Both conditions are influenced by metabolic factors, with insulin resistance and diabetes often associated with chronic diarrhea [9–11], and hypothyroidism and hyperglycemia recognized as risk factors for chronic constipation [12].

The Zhejiang University (ZJU) index, a composite metabolic marker that includes body mass index (BMI), fasting plasma glucose (FPG), triglycerides (TG), and the ratio of alanine aminotransferase (ALT) to aspartate aminotransferase (AST), has been extensively utilized in investigations of various metabolic disorders, such as non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) and type 2 diabetes [13–15]. However, the association between the ZJU index and bowel health, particularly chronic diarrhea and constipation, has not been fully explored. Given the potential value of the ZJU index in assessing metabolic disorders and related disease risks, further investigation into its link with gastrointestinal health is justified and could significantly enhance our understanding in this field.

The present investigation is designed to explore the association between the ZJU index and bowel health, with a particular focus on its association with chronic diarrhea and constipation. Utilizing data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), this study endeavors to reveal the potential value of the ZJU index in assessing bowel health, thereby offering valuable insights for subsequent research endeavors and clinical practice.

Materials and methods

Data source and study population

This cross-sectional study utilized de-identified data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), conducted by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) from 2005 to 2010. NHANES is a comprehensive program that assesses the health and nutritional status of the noninstitutionalized U.S. population using a stratified multistage probability sampling method. All participants provided written informed consent during the original data-collection visits, and the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) Ethics Review Board approved both the protocol and the written-consent procedure. Because the present analysis uses only publicly released, de-identified datasets and involves no participant contact or re-identification, no additional consent or IRB review was required.

The initial study sample consisted of 31,034 participants. After applying strict inclusion and exclusion criteria, the final analytic sample included 11,180 individuals. Specifically, 16,390 participants were excluded due to incomplete data on bowel health, and 11,704 were excluded due to incomplete ZJU index data. An additional 2573 participants were excluded due to incomplete data on fat intake (202), dietary inflammatory index (DII) (1691), household income (976), and other covariables (69). The final sample included 1095 participants with constipation and 974 with diarrhea. Calculation of ZJU index

The ZJU index, computed as FPG (mmol/L) + BMI (kg/m2) + 3 × (ALT/AST ratio) + 2 (if female) + TG (mmol/L) [14], served as the primary exposure variable in this study. BMI is calculated by dividing an individual's weight in kilograms by the square of their height in meters. This index combines several key metabolic parameters to offer a comprehensive evaluation of metabolic health.

Assessment of bowel health

Bowel health was assessed using the Bristol Stool Form Scale (BSFS) and bowel movement frequency data collected at the Mobile Examination Center (MEC) of NHANES. Chronic constipation and diarrhea were identified based on specific BSFS scores and bowel movement frequencies. Chronic constipation was defined by a BSFS score of 1 or 2, coupled with fewer than three bowel movements per week. Chronic diarrhea was characterized by a BSFS score of 6 or 7, with more than three bowel movements per day. BSFS scores of 3, 4, or 5, as well as other movement patterns, indicated normal bowel function and were not categorized as chronic conditions [16]. These classifications derive from participants’ self-reported usual bowel patterns, captured through two NHANES survey items: “How often do you usually have bowel movements?” and “Please look at this card and tell me the number that corresponds to your usual or most common stool type.” While the term 'usual' implies habitual symptoms rather than transient states, this single-assessment approach cannot confirm symptom chronicity per clinical standards (e.g., Rome IV criteria, which require ≥ 3 months of symptoms).

Assessment of covariables

The selection of covariables was based on clinical relevance and prior research findings. To investigate the association between the ZJU index and bowel health, we adjusted for a comprehensive set of covariables, including demographic factors (sex, age, race/ethnicity, education, marital status, and household income), lifestyle factors (physical activity, fat intake, smoking, alcohol consumption, and DII), and health status (hypertension and diabetes). Race/ethnicity was categorized into four groups: non-Hispanic white, non-Hispanic black, Mexican, and others [17]. Educational levels were divided into three categories: less than 9 years, 9–11 years, and 12 years or more [17]. Marital status was classified into two groups: married/cohabitating and living alone [18]. Household income was stratified into three levels based on the poverty income ratio (PIR) from the NHANES database: low income (PIR ≤ 1.3), medium income (PIR > 1.3 to 3.5), and high income (PIR > 3.5) [19].

Physical activity levels were assessed using the Global Physical Activity Questionnaire (GPAQ), which included questions on daily work and leisure activities. These activities were quantitatively evaluated using metabolic equivalents (METs) [17]. Smoking status was determined based on whether an individual had smoked at least 100 cigarettes in their lifetime and was classified into two categories: never smokers and current smokers [20]. Alcohol consumption was recorded based on an annual intake of at least 12 alcoholic drinks and was categorized as either drinkers or non-drinkers [21]. Information on hypertension and diabetes was obtained through self-reported questionnaires.

The DII was used to evaluate the inflammatory potential of participants’ diets. This index integrates 45 food parameters to quantify the impact of diet on systemic inflammation. The DII is calculated by determining correlation coefficients for each food parameter based on their associations with inflammatory biomarkers. These coefficients are then transformed and calculated to derive an overall DII score [22]. A higher DII score signifies a stronger pro-inflammatory effect of the diet, while a lower score indicates a more anti-inflammatory effect.

Statistical analysis

We conducted a secondary analysis of the NHANES data. Weighted analysis performed dietary two-day sample weight (WTDR2D), with sampling weights computed as 1/3 × WTDR2D. Categorical data were presented as unweighted frequencies and weighted percentages, while continuous variables were presented as weighted means (SD) or medians (IQR). Group differences were evaluated using one-way ANOVA for normally distributed data, Kruskal–Wallis tests for skewed data, and Chi-square tests for categorical data.

To investigate the association between the ZJU index and bowel health, we employed weighted multivariate logistic regression models. The ZJU index was analyzed both as a categorical variable (split into tertiles) and as a continuous variable (per 5-unit increment). We constructed three models with increasing levels of adjustment for potential confounders: Model 1 was adjusted for demographic factors (sex, age, race/ethnicity, education, marital status, and household income). Model 2 further adjusted for lifestyle factors (physical activity, fat intake, DII, smoking status, and alcohol consumption); and Model 3 additionally adjusted for health status factors (hypertension and diabetes). To evaluate the performance of our logistic regression models, we conducted receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis to calculate sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy. The area under curve (AUC) was used to measure the model’s overall performance.

To examine potential non-linear dose–response associations, we conducted a weighted restricted cubic spline analysis with knots positioned at the 10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles, excluding the extreme 5% of values to enhance accuracy. Non-linearity was tested using a likelihood ratio test comparing the model with only a linear term against the model with both linear and cubic spline terms. If a non-linear correlation was observed, the threshold effect of the ZJU index on the outcome was calculated from subgroup analyses that were conducted using relevant subgroup variables, with interactions evaluated using the likelihood ratio test. Missing data were managed by listwise deletion on an analysis-by-analysis basis. To address potential biases introduced by missing data and to ensure the robustness of our findings, we conducted a series of sensitivity analyses. One such analysis involved excluding participants who reported using specific medications known to affect bowel health, including metformin, opioids, and thyroid replacement therapies. We then performed logistic regression analysis on this subset of participants to determine if the observed associations between the ZJU index and bowel health outcomes remained consistent. Additionally, we utilized the R multiple imputation (MI) procedure with 5 replications and a chained equation approach to address missing covariate data. Weighted multivariate logistic regression analysis was conducted on the imputed data to further evaluate the robustness of our findings. To further assess the impact of unmeasured confounding factors, we calculated E-values to evaluate the potential impact of unmeasured confounding factors. An E-value greater than 1 indicates that the observed association is robust to unmeasured confounding.

Statistical analyses were conducted using R Statistical Software (Version 4.2.2, http://www.R-project.org, The R Foundation) and the Free Statistics analysis platform (Version 1.9.2, Beijing, China, http://www.clinicalscientists.cn/freestatistics). A two-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Result

Baseline characteristics

The study cohort comprised 11,180 participants with a mean age of 49.62 ± 17.80 years, of whom 47.37% were male and 52.63% were female. The overall prevalence of diarrhea and constipation in this population was 7.87% and 9.43%, respectively. Table 1 presents the general characteristics of the participants stratified by tertiles of the ZJU index. Compared with those in the lowest tertile (Q1), individuals in the highest tertile (Q3) of the ZJU index were more likely to be middle-aged or elderly, non-Hispanic Black or Mexican American, have 9–11 years of education, be of low income, have lower levels of physical activity, and have conditions such as hypertension, diabetes, and diarrhea (all P values < 0.01). Conversely, those in the Q3 tertile were less likely to be younger, non-Hispanic White, have higher education levels, be high-income earners, consume alcohol, engage in higher levels of physical activity, or have good bowel health (all P values < 0.01). No significant differences were observed among the three tertiles regarding fat intake and smoking status (all P values > 0.05). Association between ZJU index and bowel health

Table 2 presents the results of the weighted multivariate logistic regression analysis. After adjustment for potential confounding variables, each 5-unit increase in the ZJU index was associated with a 13% higher likelihood of chronic diarrhea (odds ratio (OR) = 1.13, 95% CI: 1.07–1.19, P < 0.001) and a 12% lower likelihood of chronic constipation (OR = 0.88, 95% CI: 0.80–0.96, P = 0.004) (Table 2, Model 3). These findings indicate that even a modest change in the ZJU index can significantly influence bowel health in the population. When comparing the highest tertile (Q3) with the lowest tertile (Q1) of the ZJU index, the adjusted OR for chronic diarrhea was 1.48 (95% CI: 1.16–1.87, P = 0.002), indicating a 48% higher likelihood of chronic diarrhea in Q3 compared to Q1. For chronic constipation, the adjusted OR was 0.65 (95% CI: 0.48–0.98, P = 0.009), indicating a 35% lower likelihood of chronic constipation in Q3 compared to Q1 (Table 2, Model 3).

To further evaluate the performance of our logistic regression model, we conducted ROC analysis. For constipation, the AUC was 0.691 (95% CI: 0.675–0.707). At a threshold of 0.107, the model's sensitivity was 0.685 and specificity was 0.607. For diarrhea, the AUC was 0.631 (95% CI: 0.612–0.650). At a threshold of 0.096, the model's sensitivity was 0.587 and specificity was 0.624. These results demonstrate an association between the ZJU index and bowel disorders (Figure S1 and Table S1).

Restricted cubic spline

The dose–response curves estimated in this study reveal a significant linear relationship between the ZJU index and the likelihood of both chronic diarrhea and constipation. The P-values for non-linearity were 0.673 for diarrhea (Fig. 2A) and 0.297 for constipation (Fig. 2B), respectively, suggesting that a linear model sufficiently describes the relationship. Subgroup, sensitivity and additional analyses

Subsequent subgroup analyses revealed that the associations between the ZJU index and both chronic diarrhea and constipation were consistent across various demographic, lifestyle, and disease status subgroups. Specifically, these relationships remained significant across different sexes, age groups, marital statuses, and among individuals with varying smoking and drinking habits, as well as within different categories of hypertension and diabetes status (Figure S2, S3).

Following multiple imputations of the missing covariate data, weighted multivariate logistic regression analysis was conducted, and the results were consistent with those of the primary analysis (Table S2). To further assess the robustness of the main findings, we conducted an additional sensitivity analysis by excluding participants who reported using metformin, opioids, or thyroid replacement therapies. The logistic regression analysis on this subset of participants yielded results consistent with our primary analysis, confirming the robustness of the observed associations (Table S4).To further evaluate the potential impact of unmeasured confounding factors, E-values were calculated to evaluate the potential impact of unmeasured confounding factors. The association between the ZJU index and bowel health (diarrhea and constipation) was found to be robust, except in scenarios where the OR for the likelihood of bowel health (diarrhea and constipation) attributable to an unmeasured confounder would need to be 1.51 and 1.53, respectively (Figure S4).

Discussion

This study represents the initial large-scale cross-sectional exploration of the association between the ZJU index and bowel health, specifically chronic diarrhea and constipation, within a representative sample of the US population. The findings reveal a significant linear association between the ZJU index and the prevalence of these conditions. Specifically, a 5-unit increase in the ZJU index is associated with a 13% higher likelihood of chronic diarrhea (OR = 1.13) and a 12% lower likelihood of chronic constipation (OR = 0.88). This 5-unit increment in the ZJU index was selected to highlight significant associations with bowel health outcomes, enhancing statistical power and effect size detection, thereby making the findings both statistically significant and clinically relevant. In subgroup analyses, the consistent associations across different demographic and clinical subgroups, coupled with the robustness confirmed by sensitivity analyses, lend further credence to the results. These findings emphasize the importance of metabolic factors in bowel health and highlight the association of the ZJU index with bowel disorder prevalence.

While direct patient reporting is key for assessing diarrhea or constipation symptoms, the ZJU index offers additional insights into the metabolic factors underlying these conditions. By integrating multiple metabolic parameters (BMI, FPG, TG, and the ALT/AST ratio), the ZJU index provides a comprehensive metabolic health assessment. Our study examines its association with bowel health outcomes to identify potential metabolic risk factors for bowel disorders. This not only deepens our understanding of the metabolic–gastrointestinal link, but also supports the development of targeted interventions.

The ZJU has demonstrated validated utility across various metabolic disorders. Its predictive capacity for NAFLD was evidenced by significant correlations with fibrosis severity and steatosis progression [23]. In Asian cohorts, elevated ZJU index values independently associated with diabetes incidence, particularly in specific subpopulations [15]. This metric further demonstrated multisystem relevance: a positive dose–response relationship with obstructive sleep apnea syndrome (OSAS) risk was observed in Chinese middle-aged and elderly populations, and it is positively associated with indicators of OSAS severity [24]; and significant sarcopenia risk associations were identified in U.S. adults [25]. Given the established systemic connections between metabolic dysregulation and gastrointestinal pathophysiology, exploring the role of the ZJU index in chronic constipation and diarrhea holds significant clinical importance.

Metabolic disorders significantly influence gastrointestinal pathophysiology, contributing to both chronic diarrhea and constipation while adversely affecting overall metabolic health. Emerging evidence highlights complex associations between metabolic dysregulation and bowel function. Notably, after adjusting for anti-diabetic medications and other confounders, metabolic syndrome has been shown to increase the likelihood of chronic diarrhea compared to normal bowel function (adjusted OR: 1.271) [26]. Furthermore, the body roundness index (BRI) has been found to positively correlate specifically with chronic diarrhea, but not constipation, revealing a distinct pathophysiological relationship [27]. Among diabetic patients, constipation is prevalent, with prolonged colonic transit time observed in approximately 45% of symptomatic individuals [28]. Additionally, the incidence of diarrhea is elevated in diabetic patients and often alternates with constipation [28]. Patients with hyperlipidemia exhibit significantly higher total gastrointestinal symptom scores, which include indigestion, diarrhea, abdominal pain, and reflux [29]. Complementary research based on the NHANES reveals that the prevalence of chronic diarrhea is independently increased in NAFLD patients compared to those with normal bowel patterns, regardless of BMI. Although the incidence of constipation is also elevated in NAFLD, this association is more dependent on BMI than the association observed with diarrhea [26]. These findings highlight distinct mechanisms linking metabolic dysfunction to gastrointestinal symptoms, emphasizing the need for tailored management strategies in metabolic disorders.

Existing literature indicates that metabolic factors, as reflected by the ZJU index, could potentially influence bowel health through various pathways. Metabolic disorders are associated with gastrointestinal disturbances, including diarrhea and constipation, which may be related to impacts on bile acid metabolism, gut microbiota balance, intestinal barrier function, and immune regulation. Bile acid imbalance, often seen in metabolic disorders, might overexcite colonic motility and secretion, potentially leading to diarrhea [30]. Additionally, disruptions in gut microbiota, which can occur due to conditions like NAFLD associated with high-fat diets, might impair intestinal function and trigger inflammation [30]. These disruptions can compromise the intestinal barrier, allowing microbes and their metabolites to enter systemic circulation, potentially leading to inflammation and gastrointestinal symptoms [31–33]. While our study does not allow for a direct examination of these mechanisms, our findings align with these established pathways and underscore the need for further investigation into the metabolic–gastrointestinal axis.

This study benefits from several strengths. Analyzing a nationally representative sample of 11,180 NHANES participants provides sufficient statistical power to examine clinically relevant associations between metabolic health and bowel function. The composite ZJU index, which integrates multiple parameters, enables a comprehensive metabolic assessment, offering advantages over single biomarker evaluations. Standardized inclusion and exclusion criteria, multiple imputations for missing data, and thorough adjustments for demographic, lifestyle, and clinical confounders ensure methodological rigor. The analytical approach incorporates restricted cubic spline regression to assess potential non-linear relationships. Additional sensitivity analyses across key demographic variables and clinical subgroups further support the robustness of the findings.

This study provides valuable insights but has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional nature of the study precludes the establishment of causality, as the temporal relationship between metabolic status and bowel health cannot be established. Despite adjusting for numerous potential confounders, residual confounding from unmeasured variables, such as medication use (e.g., laxatives) and gastrointestinal comorbidities, may still be present. Second, bowel symptom definitions were based on single-timepoint self-reports of “usual” stool patterns, which align with NHANES-based literature but cannot verify Rome IV-compliant chronicity. This approach may introduce outcome misclassification, potentially attenuating observed associations. Third, the exclusion of a large number of participants due to missing data on key variables could impact the representativeness of our sample, complicating the interpretation of our findings. Fourth, the ROC analysis revealed only modest discriminatory performance, with AUC values ranging from 0.63 to 0.69. While statistically significant, these values indicate limited discriminatory power for clinical application. The ZJU index, while associated with bowel health outcomes, should be used cautiously and in conjunction with other diagnostic tools. Lastly, the generalizability of our findings may be limited by the predominantly U.S.-based study population, potentially restricting applicability to other ethnic groups or healthcare systems. Additionally, our analysis did not investigate potential biological mechanisms underlying the observed associations, such as gut microbiota composition, intestinal permeability, or systemic inflammation pathways. Future prospective studies incorporating biomarker measurements and mechanistic experiments would help elucidate these relationships.

Conclusions

In summary, our findings reveal clinically significant associations between the ZJU index and bowel health outcomes, with particularly strong correlations observed for chronic diarrhea and constipation. These results suggest an association between the composite metabolic index and bowel disorder prevalence. Further research is warranted to explore its potential role as a biomarker. Future research should focus on prospectively validating these associations in longitudinal cohorts, elucidating the underlying mechanisms of metabolic–gut interactions, and conducting translational studies to assess the clinical utility of metabolic profiling for risk stratification of bowel disorders.
