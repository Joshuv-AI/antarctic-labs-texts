Motivation Outweighs Barriers in Predicting Perceived Effectiveness of Virtual Home Health Care for Chronic Disease Management in Saudi Arabia
Authors: Saleh Alzughaibi, Ahmed Alqheedan, Eman M Alanazi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAdoption of Virtual Home Health Care (VHHC) for chronic disease is shaped by competing motivations and barriers, yet these multi-select factors are rarely analyzed alongside effectiveness evaluations. Whether high trust in the clinician can coexist with doubt about the virtual format has not been examined in VHHC populations.ObjectiveTo characterize the motivation and barrier endorsement patterns of VHHC users and their demographic correlates, and to determine the relative contribution of motivations and barriers to perceived effectiveness. A secondary aim examined provider–modality trust discordance (simultaneously trusting one’s clinician while doubting the virtual modality) and its demographic correlates.MethodsThis was a secondary analysis of cross-sectional data from a previously published study of 517 chronic disease patients in Saudi Arabia. Four motivation items and four barrier items (multi-select) were recoded into binary variables. Each of the 8 items was cross-tabulated with 4 demographic variables (gender, age, education, disease duration), yielding 32 chi-square tests. Perceived effectiveness was operationalized as the mean of 11 Likert-scaled items (Cronbach’s α = 0.870). Hierarchical regression tested the incremental prediction of effectiveness by barrier count and motivation count, with non-parametric and ordinal sensitivity analyses. Trust discordance was operationalized as high provider trust (E2 ≥ 4) combined with accuracy-doubt endorsement.ResultsSpeed of access (69.4%) was the top motivation; accuracy doubt (63.4%) was the top barrier. Of the 32 chi-square tests, 11 reached statistical significance. The strongest association was between quality concern and education (Cramér’s V = 0.243, p < 0.001), with postgraduates endorsing quality concerns at double the rate of high-school patients (68.4% vs 33.3%). In hierarchical regression, barrier count added negligible variance (ΔR2 = 0.0004), whereas motivation count added 12.8% (B = 0.225, p < 0.001); the association was confirmed by bootstrap, rank-based and ordinal sensitivity analyses. Provider–modality trust discordance affected 41.2% of the sample.ConclusionVHHC adoption among chronic disease patients is associated with motivation intensity rather than barrier absence. The high prevalence of trust discordance (41.2%) suggests that provider trust and modality trust operate independently, with implications for targeted, multidisciplinary communication strategies.

Introduction

Chronic diseases represent a considerable public health burden in Saudi Arabia, affecting the healthcare system and requiring innovative care delivery solutions.1,2 Virtual Home Health Care (VHHC) has emerged as a promising approach for managing chronic conditions, enabling patients to receive healthcare services remotely through digital platforms.3,4 The global expansion of telehealth has been well documented, with systematic reviews consistently reporting high patient satisfaction and clinical outcomes comparable to in-person care.5–7

In Saudi Arabia, the Seha Virtual Hospital, launched in February 2022 as part of Vision 2030’s health sector transformation program, now connects 224 hospitals and serves over 480,000 patients annually.8,9 Patient satisfaction rates range from 82% to 86%.8 The Kingdom’s digital health market reached $4.4 billion in 2024, with over 31 million citizens using the Sehhaty platform.10 Recent studies have confirmed that telehealth improves healthcare access and chronic disease management outcomes globally.3,6,11

VHHC for chronic disease is rarely delivered by a single profession. Virtual chronic care in Saudi Arabia is typically provided by multidisciplinary teams in which physicians lead diagnosis and treatment, nurses coordinate monitoring and self-management support, pharmacists conduct medication review and reconciliation, nutritionists deliver dietary counselling, and patient educators reinforce adherence.8,9 Each discipline interacts with patients through the same virtual platform but addresses different facets of motivation and trust, so understanding the patient-level drivers of engagement is relevant across the whole team rather than to physicians alone.

However, most VHHC studies focus on effectiveness perceptions or satisfaction outcomes, treating motivations and barriers as background context.1,12 When motivations and barriers are collected as multi-select survey items, they are typically reported as simple frequencies without examining their demographic correlates or relative predictive power.13 This represents a missed analytical opportunity: understanding what attracts and what repels different patient subgroups is essential for targeted communication as VHHC programs scale.

The Health Belief Model (HBM) posits that health behavior is determined by the balance between perceived benefits and perceived barriers.14 Applied to VHHC, this predicts that both motivations and barriers should independently predict effectiveness perceptions. The Capability, Opportunity, Motivation–Behavior (COM-B) model, by contrast, suggests that motivation may compensate for constrained opportunity, operating as a distinct driver rather than simply balancing against barriers.15 Recent work applying the COM-B framework to digital health adoption has found that motivational factors often override opportunity constraints among chronic disease patients.16

Additionally, a pattern we term provider–modality trust discordance — the simultaneous presence of high trust in the healthcare provider and doubt about the virtual modality — has not been examined in VHHC populations. In plain terms, a patient may fully trust the clinician treating them while remaining skeptical that a virtual consultation can be as accurate as an in-person visit. Telehealth trust research has typically treated trust as unidimensional, measuring either provider trust or technology trust but rarely examining their joint distribution.5,17 The present study addresses this gap.

This study therefore had two aims: (1) to characterize motivation and barrier endorsement patterns among VHHC users with chronic diseases and their demographic correlates; and (2) to determine whether motivations or barriers more strongly predict perceived effectiveness. As a secondary aim, we examined the prevalence and correlates of provider–modality trust discordance.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

This was a secondary analysis of cross-sectional data from Elsayed et al.1 The original study examined VHHC effectiveness perceptions among 517 chronic disease patients in Saudi Arabia but did not analyze the multi-select motivation and barrier items. The present study examines these previously unanalyzed variables alongside the effectiveness items. Throughout, VHHC was evaluated by participants as a single service; the dataset does not distinguish which professional disciplines individual patients interacted with.

Sample Size and Participants

The sample comprised 517 adult patients (≥ 18 years) with at least one chronic disease who had used VHHC services. Data were collected between March and June 2025 via an online questionnaire. The sample was predominantly female (n = 360, 69.6%), with the largest age group being 25–35 years (n = 204, 39.5%). Education levels were high school or below (n = 69, 13.3%), bachelor’s degree (n = 315, 60.9%), and postgraduate degree (n = 133, 25.7%). The majority (n = 297, 57.4%) had lived with their chronic disease for more than 5 years.

Study Instrument

The questionnaire consisted of three parts: (1) demographic data (gender, age, education, chronic disease type, disease duration, residence); (2) effectiveness items — 11 Likert-scaled items (E1–E9, E11, E12; 5-point scale from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree”) assessing convenience, trust, access, communication, equivalence, and satisfaction; and (3) two open-ended multi-select items assessing motivations for using VHHC and barriers deterring use.

The original instrument contained 12 effectiveness items. One item measuring audio clarity (E10: “I can hear the doctor clearly during VHHC sessions”) was excluded from the analysis dataset owing to high redundancy with the visual equivalence item (E9–E10 r = 0.708), yielding an 11-item scale.

The four motivation options were speed of access, no transportation needed, avoiding waiting rooms, and no work absence required. The four barrier options were accuracy doubt, quality concern, privacy worry, and recording concern. Respondents could select any combination of items. Responses were originally in Arabic semicolon-delimited text and were recoded into binary variables (0 = not endorsed, 1 = endorsed) for analysis.

Instrument Validity and Reliability

The instrument was validated by Elsayed et al1 through expert-panel review for content validity Cronbach’s alpha was α = 0.889 for the original 12-item scale; for the 11-item scale used in the present analysis, α = 0.870, indicating good internal consistency.

Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed using SAS v9.4 (SAS OnDemand for Academics) with a significance level of α = 0.05, and key results were cross-checked in Python. Descriptive statistics (frequencies, means, standard deviations) were calculated for all variables. Chi-square tests of independence with Cramér’s V were used to examine all 32 associations between the 8 motivation/barrier items and the 4 demographic variables.18 Because each test cross-tabulated a binary item with a demographic variable, the degrees of freedom equaled the number of demographic categories minus one: df = 1 for the two-category variables (gender, disease duration), df = 2 for education (three categories: high school or below, bachelor’s, postgraduate), and df = 3 for age (four categories). A continuity correction was applied to 2×2 tables.

Perceived effectiveness was operationalized as the mean of the 11 effectiveness items, producing a near-continuous composite (36 distinct values; range 1.64–4.91) rather than a single ordinal response. Consistent with established guidance, multi-item summated scales of this kind are treated as approximately interval and analyzed with parametric methods, which are robust to the ordinal origin of their component items.19,20 Hierarchical linear regression entered demographics (Step 1), barrier count (Step 2), and motivation count (Step 3) to determine the incremental prediction of perceived effectiveness.

To confirm that the regression findings were not artefacts of the interval treatment of the composite, three sensitivity analyses were performed: (1) a non-parametric Spearman rank correlation between each count and effectiveness; (2) a bias-corrected bootstrap (5000 resamples) of the motivation-count coefficient; and (3) an ordinal proportional-odds logistic regression with effectiveness recoded into quartiles. One-way ANOVA with Tukey HSD post-hoc tests compared satisfaction (E12) and perceived effectiveness across trust groups; effect sizes (η2) followed Cohen’s (1988) conventions.18

Trust discordance was operationalized as the combination of high provider trust (E2 ≥ 4) and simultaneous endorsement of accuracy doubt, yielding four groups: Consistent Trust (high trust, no accuracy doubt), Discordant (high trust + accuracy doubt), Low Trust No Doubt, and Consistent Distrust (low trust + accuracy doubt). The four-group classification was used only for the ANOVA (between-groups df = 3); demographic correlates of discordance were tested by cross-tabulating each demographic variable with the binary discordant indicator.

Ethical Considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Ministry of Health Institutional Review Board (IRB log No. 25–86E). Informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to data collection. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Sensitivity Analyses

Because the outcome was a composite of Likert items, three sensitivity analyses tested whether the motivation–barrier asymmetry depended on the interval treatment of the scale. First, distribution-free Spearman correlations reproduced the asymmetry without any interval assumption: motivation count was moderately and significantly correlated with effectiveness (ρ = 0.40, p < 0.001), whereas barrier count was not (ρ = −0.01, p = 0.87). Second, a bias-corrected bootstrap (5000 resamples) of the motivation-count coefficient produced a 95% confidence interval of 0.181 to 0.269, closely matching the parametric interval (0.174 to 0.276) and excluding zero. Third, an ordinal proportional-odds logistic regression with effectiveness recoded into quartiles confirmed the pattern: each additional motivation endorsed nearly doubled the odds of a higher effectiveness category (OR = 1.98, p < 0.001), while barrier count had a small effect (OR = 0.77, p = 0.039). The substantive conclusion was therefore unchanged across parametric, rank-based and ordinal approaches.

Discussion

This study provides the first systematic analysis of motivation and barrier profiles among VHHC users with chronic diseases in Saudi Arabia. Three principal findings emerged: (1) motivation intensity was the primary correlate of perceived effectiveness, while barriers had virtually no predictive power; (2) quality concern was strongly linked to education level, with postgraduates showing the highest endorsement rate; and (3) 41.2% of patients demonstrated a provider–modality trust discordance, a novel observation with implications for VHHC communication strategies.

Motivation Dominance Over Barrier Reduction

The most practically relevant finding was the striking asymmetry between motivations and barriers as correlates of perceived effectiveness. Motivation count explained 12.8% of variance in effectiveness perceptions, while barrier count explained only 0.04% of the variance — a difference of more than two orders of magnitude in predictive contribution, and one that held across parametric, rank-based and ordinal analyses. This pattern is more consistent with the COM-B model’s emphasis on motivation than with the Health Belief Model’s barrier–benefit balance framework. The HBM predicts that perceived benefits and perceived barriers should contribute comparably to health-behavior decisions; our data indicate that this is not the case for VHHC effectiveness evaluations.14,15

This finding aligns with the updated systematic review by Hepburn et al,16 which applied the COM-B framework to digital health adoption among older adults with chronic diseases and found that motivational factors consistently override opportunity constraints. Similarly, Pong et al13 reported in their scoping review of digital health implementation studies that positive patient satisfaction was common regardless of reported barriers. Our results extend this literature by providing direct quantitative evidence of the motivation–barrier asymmetry within a single regression model.

The practical implication is counterintuitive but important: VHHC programs may achieve greater engagement returns by strengthening motivation messaging — emphasizing convenience, time savings, workplace flexibility, and reduced travel burden — than by investing primarily in barrier-reduction strategies. This does not mean barriers are unimportant; barrier reduction remains essential for access equity, particularly for populations facing technological or infrastructural constraints.7,21 However, our data suggest that once patients are using the service, barriers have minimal association with how they evaluate its effectiveness. The distinction between access barriers (which prevent use) and evaluative barriers (which reduce perceived quality) deserves further investigation.

The Speed–Accuracy Tension in VHHC Adoption

The simultaneous high endorsement of speed of access as a motivation (69.4%) and accuracy doubt as a barrier (63.4%) reveals a tension in VHHC adoption that has not previously been quantified at the item level. Patients appear to be drawn to VHHC primarily for its convenience while harboring concerns about its clinical accuracy. The co-occurrence of these two responses within the same patients suggests that convenience and quality evaluations operate on separate cognitive dimensions.

This is consistent with the J.D. Power (2024) US Telehealth Satisfaction Study,22 which reported that trust and whether the visit met patients’ needs ranked above convenience as drivers of satisfaction — suggesting that while convenience attracts patients, quality perceptions ultimately determine satisfaction.22 The Rabbani et al7 systematic review and meta-analysis similarly found that, although telehealth improved access and convenience across most settings, concerns about clinical equivalence persisted even when outcomes were objectively comparable to in-person care Our data suggest that speed of access functions as a near-universal motivator, while accuracy doubt represents a more targeted concern that varies significantly by age and education.

Education-Linked Quality Evaluation

The association between quality concern and education (V = 0.243) was the single strongest effect across all 32 tested demographic–item associations. Postgraduate patients endorsed quality concerns at more than double the rate of high-school patients (68.4% vs 33.3%). This finding should not be interpreted as indicating dissatisfaction among educated patients; rather, it reflects what Nutbeam23 described as critical health literacy — the capacity to critically analyze health information and healthcare services. Highly educated patients bring analytical skills developed through advanced education to their evaluation of VHHC, identifying potential quality limitations that less-educated patients may not question.

This interpretation is supported by the broader pattern of results: despite endorsing more barriers, postgraduate patients did not report significantly lower satisfaction or effectiveness perceptions than other education groups. Lee and Nam21 reported similar findings in their analysis of HINTS data, noting that education-linked telehealth disparities are not always in the expected direction. The implication for VHHC communication is that educated patients may benefit from evidence-based quality-assurance messaging rather than generic promotional content. Providing transparent data on clinical outcomes, diagnostic accuracy, and equivalence to in-person care may be more effective for this subgroup than emphasizing convenience benefits they already recognize.

Provider–Modality Trust Discordance: A Novel Risk Profile

The finding that 41.2% of the sample simultaneously endorsed high provider trust and accuracy doubt represents a novel contribution to the telehealth trust literature. Traditional trust research in healthcare has often treated trust as a unitary construct, measuring overall trust in the healthcare system or in the provider.5,17 Our results suggest that VHHC trust operates on at least two levels: trust in the provider (the person delivering care) and trust in the modality (the virtual medium through which care is delivered). These two dimensions can diverge substantially within the same patient.

Discordant patients represent a distinct communication challenge. They cannot readily be engaged through standard trust-building interventions, as their provider trust is already high (M = 4.38 on a 5-point scale). Nor can they be reached through generic barrier reduction, as their concern is specifically about modality accuracy — not provider competence, privacy, or recording. The age gradient (older patients showing higher discordance) and education gradient (postgraduates showing higher discordance) suggest that this profile is concentrated among patients with more experience of in-person care and greater capacity to critically evaluate the virtual alternative.

Communication strategies for discordant patients should acknowledge modality-specific concerns rather than dismissing them. Messages framed to bridge the provider–modality gap — for example, that the same clinical reasoning applies whether a patient is seen in person or virtually — may be more effective than generic telehealth promotion. The Seha Virtual Hospital’s expansion to 224 facilities will increasingly serve this discordant population as the Kingdom’s patient base becomes older and more educated under Vision 2030.8

Implications for Multidisciplinary VHHC Delivery

VHHC for chronic disease in Saudi Arabia is typically delivered by multidisciplinary teams — physicians, nurses, pharmacists, nutritionists, and patient educators working through virtual platforms.8,9 It is important to be explicit about a boundary of the present data: participants evaluated VHHC as a single service, and the dataset does not record which disciplines each patient interacted with or capture discipline-specific experiences. The recommendations that follow are therefore offered not as empirical findings but as a conceptual framework for future multidisciplinary research and intervention design, grounded in the motivation, barrier and trust patterns observed here.

Within that conceptual frame, the patient-level patterns map onto distinct disciplinary roles. Because motivation messaging was the strongest correlate of perceived effectiveness, nurses — who often coordinate ongoing monitoring — are well placed to reinforce the convenience, time-saving and reduced-travel benefits that drive engagement. Pharmacists conducting virtual medication review could pair the same convenience framing with concrete demonstrations of accuracy, such as confirming medication lists on screen, to address the dominant accuracy-doubt barrier. Physicians, who anchor the accuracy concern most directly, could routinely narrate their diagnostic reasoning during virtual consultations to bridge the provider–modality gap that defines the discordant group. Nutritionists and patient educators, whose work depends on sustained adherence, could tailor messaging to the education gradient observed here — offering evidence-based quality-assurance content to highly educated patients who scrutinize service quality, and clearer benefit-focused content to others.

The trust discordance pattern is particularly relevant for care coordination. Because discordant patients combine high provider trust with modality doubt, leveraging the existing clinician relationship — rather than relying on technology-focused messaging — may be the most effective pathway for sustaining engagement across disciplines. Future research should collect discipline-specific interaction data so that these hypotheses can be tested directly, and should examine whether discipline-tailored communication can reduce the discordance rate.

Limitations of the Study

Several limitations should be noted. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference about the relationships among motivations, barriers, and perceived effectiveness; the associations reported here are correlational and exploratory, and the analysis was not pre-registered. The convenience sampling method and geographic concentration in the Central Region (88%) limit generalizability to other Saudi regions. The four motivation and four barrier items were multi-select categories rather than validated scales, limiting psychometric evaluation. Social desirability bias may inflate motivation endorsement and suppress barrier reporting. The trust discordance classification uses a binary threshold (E2 ≥ 4) that may not capture the full continuum of trust. Importantly, VHHC was evaluated as a single service and the dataset does not capture discipline-specific interactions, so the multidisciplinary implications above are conceptual rather than empirical. Future longitudinal studies should track how motivation and barrier profiles evolve over time, collect discipline-level data, and examine whether discordance predicts actual service disengagement.

Conclusion

VHHC adoption among chronic disease patients is associated with motivation intensity rather than barrier absence. Barriers are common — 63.4% of patients doubt accuracy and 48.5% worry about quality — yet barrier count contributes negligibly (ΔR2 = 0.0004) to effectiveness prediction once motivations are accounted for, whereas motivation count alone explains 12.8% of variance, with each additional motivation endorsed associated with a 0.225-point increase in perceived effectiveness. In parallel, 41.2% of patients demonstrate a provider–modality trust discordance that is associated with age and education. As Saudi Arabia’s Seha Virtual Hospital continues to expand under Vision 2030, VHHC programs and their multidisciplinary teams may benefit from prioritizing motivation enhancement alongside barrier reduction and from developing modality-specific trust communication for the substantial discordant population.
